Effects of core training on physical performance in team-sport athletes: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yang Liu, Fengming Zhang, Shanshan Kong, Jiale Liu, Hanpeng Wang, Shijie Shi, Mykola Bezmylov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCore training has been widely explored as a way to enhance trunk function and improve sport-specific performance. Although previous reviews provide some evidence for improvements in core-related functions such as trunk strength, endurance, and balance, evidence in relation to physical performance is mixed. Team sports are characterized by repeated accelerations, decelerations, changes in direction and body orientation, and sequential explosive actions, all of which place high demands on trunk stability and neuromuscular control of the limbs. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have examined the effects of core or trunk training on athletic performance. However, evidence remains limited regarding direct comparisons between core training and non-core training controls in team-sport athletes. Therefore, this study conducts a systematic review and meta-analysis to examine these effects in this population.MethodsFour databases (Web of Science Core Collection, PubMed, EBSCOhost and the Cochrane Library) were systematically searched. Randomized controlled trials and controlled trials comparing core training with non-core training controls in team-sport athletes were included. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2 for randomized trials and ROBINS-I for non-randomized controlled studies, and certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE framework, with reporting conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Meta-analyses of balance, vertical jump, linear sprint time, and change-of-direction ability were performed using Stata 18, and effect sizes were calculated as standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test.ResultsA total of 22 studies involving 588 team-sport athletes were included. Compared with non-core training controls, core training resulted in a moderate improvement in balance (SMD = 0.67, 95% CI 0.30 to 1.04; I2 = 66.6%), along with small but statistically significant effects on vertical jump (VJ) (SMD = 0.35, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.62; I2 = 0.0%), linear sprint (SMD = − 0.52, 95% CI − 0.79 to − 0.25; I2 = 15.2%), and change-of-direction ability (CODA) (SMD = − 0.40, 95% CI − 0.66 to − 0.14; I2 = 13.9%). For balance, effects were larger with > 2 sessions per week than with ≤ 2 sessions per week (SMD = 0.97 vs. 0.20; P for subgroup difference = 0.015), while no subgroup differences were found for age, intervention duration, or intervention structure. Funnel plots and Egger’s regression tests showed no evidence of publication bias.ConclusionSome evidence suggests that, compared with non-core training controls, core training improves balance, VJ, linear sprint, and CODA in team-sport athletes, with the largest effects observed for balance. Higher training frequencies of more than two sessions per week were associated with greater balance gains. However, given the low to very low certainty of evidence and methodological limitations within the current literature, these results should be interpreted with caution, and further high-quality controlled trials are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-026-01807-5.Keywords: Core training, Team-sport athletes, Balance, Vertical jump, Linear sprint, Change-of-direction ability

Introduction

Team sports are characterized by intermittent, high-intensity, multidirectional efforts in dynamic and unpredictable environments. These demands require athletes to repeat explosive efforts throughout competition [1, 2]. Key efforts, including jumping, maximal linear sprinting, and rapid changes of direction, require integrated neuromuscular control and postural stability; together, these shape match-relevant performance characteristics [3, 4]. For this reason, physical performance, defined here as balance, vertical jump (VJ), linear sprint, and change-of-direction ability (CODA), is important for movement efficiency and remains a main goal of performance training [4, 5]. These outcomes were selected because they are commonly assessed in team-sport athletes and reflect important aspects of physical performance, including postural control, explosive lower-limb power, acceleration, and multidirectional actions. Other performance-related metrics may also be relevant, but they were not included because reporting across studies was too inconsistent for meaningful synthesis. Although these four domains—balance, VJ, linear sprint, and CODA—are closely linked in practice, each reflects a distinct aspect of physical performance and places different mechanical and neural demands on the athlete; they were therefore treated as separate outcomes [4].

Among the factors thought to affect the four performance domains, the trunk musculature, or core, is considered the biomechanical and neuromuscular center of whole-body movement [6–8]. In anatomical terms, the core includes the lumbopelvic–hip complex as well as the diaphragm, pelvic floor, and nearby axial muscles [9–12]. As a result, this system provides proximal stiffness, controls intersegmental motion, and supports efficient force transfer between the upper and lower limbs [9, 10, 13]. In addition, effective core function limits energy loss between body segments and supports anticipatory postural adjustments and intermuscular coordination through feedforward control, which is needed for rapid, high-power tasks [13–15]. When core function is reduced, athletes may show poorer postural control, greater energy loss, and less precise timing of force production [9, 10, 13, 16]. Together, these changes can reduce balance, VJ performance, sprint speed, and CODA [17, 18]. Therefore, different training programs are used to develop core capacity, although they vary in purpose and approach [13]. Traditional stable-surface training is commonly used to develop trunk force production and endurance [19, 20], whereas instability-based or task-specific training, such as Swiss-ball exercises, BOSU training, suspension training, or integrated joint-by-joint drills, may place greater demands on stabilizing function, proprioceptive control, and sensorimotor adaptability [19, 21–23]. However, these distinctions are not absolute and may vary according to the specific exercises and training context.

There have been a few meta-analyses recently conducted looking at core training and effects on athletic performance generally, and there seems to be a theme that they primarily enhance trunk strength, core endurance, and balance [6, 17]. In athletes especially, the transfer to VJ, linear sprint, and CODA is at the lower end, with more variability in results, suggesting distal performance outcome transferability of core training may not be as robust, though more balance-oriented measures tended to show more stable adaptations while speed- and agility-based outcomes were more confounded [6, 17]. In youth and competitive athletes trunk training is commonly related to postural control and local trunk endurance gains but transfer to high-velocity sport actions is inconsistent [7]. Recent evidence suggests that instability-based core training results in greater adaptations in trunk strength and, in some cases, sprint performance compared to traditional stable-surface training [24]. Sport-specific syntheses in adolescent cohorts report similar improvements in balance and explosive capacity but again highlight heterogeneous response in speed and agility based measures across these studies, underscoring the uncertainty regarding transferability of training interventions in the field [8, 25].

However, important caveats exist. Previous reviews blurred the lines between recreational athletes, competitive individuals and clinical samples in the same analyses. To our knowledge, evidence from controlled trials in team-sport athletes that directly compare core training with non-core training controls has not yet been synthesized in a focused meta-analysis. For these reasons, our present work seeks to synthesize controlled studies in team-sport athletes and compare the effects of core training with non-core training controls on balance, VJ, linear sprint, and CODA. In addition, we explore potential moderators such as age, training frequency, and intervention duration.

Methods

We followed the PRISMA 2020 statement for reporting systematic reviews and meta-analyses [26] and the methodological guidance in the Cochrane Handbook [27]. We registered the review in PROSPERO under registration number CRD420261309016. The review was registered prospectively before data extraction began.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were established using the PICOS framework covering Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study Design [28]. Original, peer-reviewed studies published in English were eligible. The literature was searched through 30 November 2025. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are given in Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Information sources and search strategy

Literature searches were conducted in the Web of Science Core Collection, PubMed, EBSCOhost and the Cochrane Library from database inception through 30 November 2025. Free-text keywords related to core training and team sports were used and the search string was tailored to the syntax of each database. No limits were applied during database searching; however, only English-language papers were included at screening. The search strategy was: (“core training” OR “core strength training” OR “core stability training” OR “core endurance training” OR “core muscle* training” OR “core exercises” OR “trunk train*” OR “trunk exercise*” OR “trunk strengthening” OR “trunk stability” OR “lumbopelvic train*” OR “lumbopelvic stability” OR “lumbar stabiliz*” OR “spinal stabiliz*” OR “motor control exercise*” OR “segmental stabiliz*” OR “deep trunk muscle training”) AND (soccer OR football* OR basketball* OR handball* OR rugby OR hockey OR “field hockey” OR futsal* OR volleyball OR “team sport*” OR “team-sport*”). Full search strategies are provided in Table S1.

Study selection

All records from the database searches were imported into EndNote for reference management. EndNote was used by two authors (YL and MB) for de-duplication using automated and manual methods, with limits applied according to the database searched. The same authors screened the titles and abstracts against the inclusion criteria independently. Articles were retrieved for full-text screening and assessed independently for final inclusion. If there was any disagreement between authors, the authors discussed the articles and reviewed the original reports until a decision was reached; if no consensus was reached, a third author (FZ) was consulted. Screening was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines.

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a predefined and standardized data collection form. Two reviewers (YL and MB) independently extracted all data pertinent to the study. Extracted variables included: author and publication year, sport and sex, group sample size and allocation, participant age (mean ± SD), intervention duration (weeks), the type of core training (static/dynamic/combined), session duration (minutes), training frequency (sessions per week), training environment (stable/unstable/mixed), season, competitive level, performance tests, and outcome measures. A separate reviewer (FZ) checked all data extractions to ensure accuracy. All data were organized in Microsoft Excel. For key missing or unclear information, the authors attempted to contact the original study authors via email or ResearchGate. If no reply was received, change-score SDs were derived from the available baseline and post-intervention SDs using the standard formula when possible; outcomes for which sufficient data still could not be obtained were excluded from quantitative synthesis.

Outcome measures

Outcomes were categorized into four domains: balance, VJ, linear sprint, and CODA.

In the included studies, VJ was assessed using countermovement jump or squat jump, and direct VJ height outcomes were also included. Linear sprint was evaluated using sprint time in tests over distances ranging from 10 m to 40 m. CODA was assessed with standardized agility tests, including the T-test, Illinois Agility Test, 5–10–5 Agility Test, Eurofit 10 × 5 m Shuttle Test, shuttle runs, and sport-specific COD tasks. Balance was evaluated using dynamic and static balance tests, including the Y-Balance Test, Star Excursion Balance Test, static balance tests, and instrumented postural control assessments.

To ensure consistent interpretation across outcomes, outcome direction harmonization was prespecified before the analyses. When necessary, direction reversal was applied at the effect-size level rather than by modifying the original raw data. When multiple outcomes were reported within the same domain, we selected the test with the largest sample size or the most commonly used protocol across studies to preserve statistical independence.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

Risk of bias was assessed according to study design. Randomized trials were assessed using the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [29]. Non-randomized controlled intervention studies were assessed using ROBINS-I [30]. Two reviewers (YL and MB) performed all assessments independently. When disagreements occurred, the reviewers discussed the judgments. A third reviewer (FZ) made the final decision if agreement was not reached.

Evidence certainty across all outcomes was appraised according to the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework. Two reviewers (YL and MB) conducted these evaluations independently, and the reviewers resolved any differences through discussion with a third reviewer (FZ).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative analyses were conducted using Stata/MP 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Meta-analyses were performed only when at least three studies reported pre-intervention and post-intervention data for the same outcome domain in both intervention and control groups [27]. Outcomes that differed in measurement approach, scale, or construct, or that belonged to different outcome domains, were not pooled and were instead summarized narratively [27].

Effect sizes were calculated by comparing pre–post changes between intervention and control groups [31]. When different tests were used within the same outcome domain, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were computed and expressed as Hedges’ g with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) [31, 32]. The SD associated with the change score was used, where available. Where change-score SDs were not directly reported, we derived the change-score SDs from the baseline and post-intervention SDs using the standard formula dependent on the within-group pre–post correlation coefficient; when this coefficient was not reported we assumed r = 0.50 [33]. Hedges’ small-sample correction factor was applied. Effect sizes were categorized as trivial (< 0.2), small (0.2–0.6), moderate (0.6–1.2), large (1.2–2.0), very large (2.0–4.0), or extremely large (> 4.0) [34]. For time-based outcomes, including linear sprint and CODA, lower values indicated better performance; therefore, negative SMDs favored the intervention group.

A random-effects inverse-variance model with the DerSimonian–Laird estimator for between-study variance was applied for all meta-analyses to account for expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity arising from differences in participant characteristics, intervention protocols, and assessment procedures [31]. Conventional 95% confidence intervals were used for the pooled effect estimates. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using both the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. The I² statistic was used as the primary index for interpreting the magnitude of heterogeneity, with thresholds of < 25%, 25–75%, and ≥ 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [35]. When heterogeneity was moderate or high (I² ≥ 25%), leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted, where appropriate, to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates [35, 36]. We also performed comparator-type sensitivity analyses to assess whether studies with atypical comparator conditions affected the pooled results. These analyses were separate from the leave-one-out analyses and were conducted irrespective of the I² value.

When studies reported multiple eligible intervention groups sharing a single control group, we selected one intervention arm based on its relevance to the predefined outcome domain to avoid double-counting [31].

Subgroup analyses were conducted only for outcomes showing at least moderate heterogeneity to explore potential sources of heterogeneity [31]. Studies were stratified by training frequency (≤ 2 vs. > 2 sessions per week), age (≤ 16.4 vs. > 16.4 years), intervention duration (≤ 8 vs. > 8 weeks), and intervention structure. Intervention structure was classified as standalone core training or additional core training plus regular sport-specific training. Cut-off values for age and intervention duration were set at the median values across the included studies, and training frequency was divided into ≤ 2 vs. > 2 sessions per week to ensure sufficient numbers of studies in each subgroup [31]. Subgroup differences were examined using random-effects meta-analysis. However, meta-regression was not performed because of the relatively small number of studies per outcome and substantial heterogeneity in intervention characteristics, which may yield unstable regression estimates. Therefore, subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and the Egger’s regression test for outcomes that included at least 10 studies [31].

Results

Study selection

The study selection process is shown in Fig. 1. We identified 445 records through database searches, including Web of Science Core Collection (n = 190), Cochrane Library (n = 117), PubMed (n = 101), and EBSCOhost (n = 37). After removing 181 duplicate records, 264 records remained for screening. We excluded 204 records after title and abstract screening. The remaining 60 reports were sought for retrieval and assessed for eligibility. A total of 38 reports were excluded after full-text assessment for eligibility. Ultimately, 22 studies were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis [19–21, 37–55].

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of the study selection process

Study characteristics

Participant characteristics from the 22 included studies are summarized in Table 2. Across these studies, 588 team-sport athletes were included, and individual study sample sizes ranged from 12 to 40 participants. Most participants were male: 14 studies included only male athletes, 5 included only female athletes, and 3 did not report participant sex; none of the included studies clearly reported a mixed-sex sample. Participant age ranged from 12.2 to 23.3 years, with a median age of 16.4 years. The studies covered several team sports, including soccer in 12 studies, basketball in 4, handball in 4, volleyball in 1, and field hockey in 1. Most studies involved Tier 2 athletes, with 19 studies classified as Tier 2 and 3 as Tier 3 [56]. Regarding training season, 6 studies were conducted during the in-season period, 2 during the pre-season, and 1 during the off-season, while 13 did not report the timing of the training season. Four main outcome domains were reported: balance outcomes were reported in 15 studies involving 392 participants, VJ in 8 studies with 217 participants, linear sprint in 10 studies involving 272 participants, and CODA in 10 studies involving 280 participants. These outcomes were assessed using several field-based tests. Balance was mainly evaluated using the Y-Balance Test, with additional assessments based on the Star Excursion Balance Test and related variants; VJ was primarily measured using the countermovement jump (CMJ) and countermovement jump with arm swing (CMJA); linear sprint was assessed over distances ranging from 10 m to 40 m; and CODA was most often evaluated using agility-based tests, such as the Illinois Agility Test, the 5–10–5 Agility Test, the shuttle run test, and sport-specific change-of-direction protocols. This sex distribution and variation in study context should be considered when interpreting the pooled results.

Methodological characteristics of the interventions

Across the included studies, intervention duration ranged from 4 to 12 weeks. Training frequency ranged from 2 to 7 sessions per week, and session duration varied from 14 to 60 min where reported, although several studies did not report session length. Most studies used combined core training protocols, whereas only 2 used static core training. Regarding the training environment, most interventions were conducted in stable settings, and fewer studies used unstable or mixed environments. The studies also differed in how core training was delivered. In some studies, it was used as a standalone program, while in others it was added to regular sport-specific training. The control conditions were not uniform either, ranging from regular sport-specific training to mobility training, strength training, small-sided games, or recreational activities. Accordingly, these control conditions were classified as non-core training controls for the purpose of evidence synthesis (Table 2).

Study risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed according to study design. Randomized trials were assessed using RoB 2, and most were judged as having some concerns overall. These concerns were mainly related to insufficient reporting of the randomization process and selection of the reported result. Deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, and outcome measurement were generally judged as low risk (Figs. 2 and 3). Four non-randomized controlled intervention studies were assessed using ROBINS-I and were judged as having serious overall risk of bias, mainly because of confounding related to non-randomized group allocation and, in one study, missing outcome data (Table 3).

Main effects

Balance

As shown in the meta-analysis that included 15 individual studies encompassing 392 total subjects, core training had a moderate statistical effect in relation to improving balance (SMD = 0.67, 95% CI 0.30 to 1.04). The overall degree of statistical variability among the 15 studies was moderate (I² = 66.6%; Cochran’s Q = 41.95, df = 14, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4).

Forest plot of core training on balance. Weights are from random-effects model

Vertical jump

The meta-analysis included 8 studies with 217 total participants and demonstrated that core training has a small but statistically significant impact (SMD = 0.35, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.62) on improving VJ. Statistically, the degree of variability between the studies was low (I² = 0.0%; Cochran’s Q = 5.97, df = 7, P = 0.543). Thus, when looking at the pooled effect of core training across the included studies, the effect remains relatively stable (Fig. 5).

Forest plot of core training on vertical jump. Weights are from random-effects model

Linear sprint

As shown in the present meta-analysis of 10 studies involving a total of 272 participants, core training has a small but statistically significant effect on improving linear sprint (SMD = − 0.52, 95% CI − 0.79 to − 0.25). The degree of statistical variability among the 10 studies was low (I² = 15.2%; Cochran’s Q = 10.62, df = 9, P = 0.303). Consequently, the overall effect of core training on linear sprint is stable across all 10 studies (Fig. 6).

Forest plot of core training on linear sprint. Weights are from random-effects model

Change-of-direction ability

As demonstrated by the present meta-analysis of 10 studies with 280 total participants regarding the effects of core training on CODA, it appears that there is also a small but statistically significant effect of core training on improving CODA (SMD = − 0.40, 95% CI − 0.66 to − 0.14). The overall statistical variability among the 10 studies is low (I² = 13.9%; Cochran’s Q = 10.45, df = 9, P = 0.315). The pooled results of all 10 studies show a stable overall effect of core training on CODA (Fig. 7).

Sensitivity analyses

Two sensitivity analyses were performed in this review. First, a leave-one-out analysis was conducted for balance, as this was the only outcome with at least moderate heterogeneity (I² = 66.6%). This analysis was not applied to VJ, linear sprint, or CODA, where heterogeneity was low.

Second, comparator-type sensitivity analyses were performed for the four outcomes to examine whether the pooled results were affected by studies with atypical comparator conditions. These analyses served a different purpose from the leave-one-out analysis. They were used to address variation in comparator type and were therefore not based on the I² value.

For the comparator-type sensitivity analyses, studies were excluded only from the outcome-specific analyses to which they contributed data. These were Amato et al. [38], Arslan et al. [39], Belli et al. [41], and Gong et al. [47]. After these exclusions, 11 studies remained for balance, 6 for VJ, 9 for linear sprint, and 7 for CODA. The results of these comparator-type sensitivity analyses were generally consistent with the main analyses, suggesting that the pooled findings were not driven by studies with atypical comparator conditions.

Subgroup analyses

Because only balance showed at least moderate heterogeneity (I² = 66.6%), subgroup analyses were conducted only for balance based on training frequency (sessions per week), age (years), and intervention duration (weeks). A significant difference between subgroups was observed for training frequency (P = 0.015). Studies using more than two sessions per week showed a larger effect (SMD = 0.97, 95% CI 0.45 to 1.49; I² = 72.9%) than studies using two or fewer sessions per week (SMD = 0.20, 95% CI − 0.14 to 0.54; I² = 0.0%) (Fig. 8).

No significant subgroup differences were observed for age (≤ 16.4 years vs. > 16.4 years; P = 0.175), intervention duration (≤ 8 weeks vs. > 8 weeks; P = 0.316) or intervention structure (standalone core training vs. additional core training plus regular sport-specific training; P = 0.211). Effects appeared larger in older athletes (Fig. 9), longer interventions (Fig. 10), and studies adding core training to regular sport-specific training (Fig. 11), but these differences were not statistically significant.

Discussion

Overall, this meta-analysis showed that, compared with non-core training controls, core training had statistically significant effects on balance, VJ, linear sprint, and CODA in team-sport athletes. However, the effect sizes differed across outcomes, as balance showed a moderate effect while the other domains showed small effects. At the same time, balance outcomes showed moderate heterogeneity, while heterogeneity remained low for VJ, linear sprint, and CODA measures. Because both the magnitude and consistency of the effects varied across domains, the following sections discuss each outcome separately.

Balance

Balance has previously been referred to as a key foundational motor skill that underpins athletic performance across sports and is therefore considered a key component of sporting success, particularly when competing in dynamic, multidirectional team environments which require frequent and rapid adjustments to posture [57]. In line with this, the present meta-analysis found that, in team-sport athletes, core training led to a moderate improvement in balance compared with non-core training controls, although moderate heterogeneity was observed. Subgroup analyses showed that training frequencies of > 2 sessions per week were associated with larger balance effects, while age and intervention duration showed no statistically significant subgroup differences.

While Ma et al. [25], summarising balance outcomes from three trials in adolescent badminton athletes, also reported mixed results, their limited evidence base precluded quantitative meta-analysis [25]. Previous reviews have generally reported positive effects of core-focused interventions on balance across different populations and training contexts [6, 8, 17, 58]. Training-related adaptations may simply improve the capacity for postural control in youth team-sport players. Even when there is some difference in youth and training modalities between studies and subjects, postural control exhibits consistent improvements across this broader body of evidence. In the present study, these benefits also extend to youth team-sport athletes who frequently perform accelerations, decelerations, single-limb support, and rapid changes in body orientation. Consequently, there are high demands for dynamic postural control and proximal stability. The training focus is directly informative in targeting these capacities.

These gains in balance may be partly explained by a couple of phenomena resulting from core training. One is the improved anticipatory postural adjustments and pre-activation of trunk musculature that help to pre-stabilize the body’s center of mass before limbs move [13, 59]. More recent meta-analyses report that core training generally leads to improvement in balance measures that may indirectly reflect a better neuromuscular control of the lumbopelvic–hip complex in the performance of unilateral and multidirectional tasks [6, 60]. Improved distal stability may lead to better proximal stability and force transmission down the kinetic chain, a phenomenon that has been linked to enhanced movement effectiveness during various athletic tasks [6, 17] and may be a mechanism for aiding an athlete in staying balanced and steady during accelerations, decelerations, and cuts.

From a practical perspective, our findings would suggest that core training programs, consistently administered for approximately 6–8 weeks, can enhance balance in team-sport athletes. The majority of interventions supplemented static stabilization with elements of dynamic trunk control; this suggests that mixed static–dynamic core stimuli may be more effective in enhancing postural mechanisms than are the static stimuli alone. Subgroup analyses indicate that frequency of more than 2 sessions per week might further enhance such balance performance gains, with little moderation from duration or age. This apparent relationship suggests that low or very non-specific core loading may in part explain the non-significant findings in some studies. Taken together, these findings imply that higher training frequency, rather than intervention duration or age-related factors, plays a major role in postural control, which was used here to reflect team-sport athletes’ core integrity. As such, these findings support the rationale for integrating core training into regular team practice, rather than delivering it as a stand-alone training session, thus allowing consistent exposure. Because training protocols currently remain heterogeneous from study to study, further work is needed to delineate optimal protocols.

Vertical jump

VJ is an important component of athletic performance in team sports [61], underpinning essential actions such as rebounding and blocking in basketball [62] and offensive and defensive jumping in volleyball and soccer [63, 64]. Sports scientists and strength and conditioning practitioners use VJ widely as a time-efficient and non-invasive means of indexing lower-body neuromuscular power, and performance on this task is closely aligned with success in sport-specific actions [65].

Considering the centrality of VJ to multi-skilled team sports, our meta-analysis showed that core training promoted a small but statistically significant improvement in VJ in team-sport athletes compared with non-core training controls, with low heterogeneity across studies. Thus, while not extensive, core training has contributed positively to VJ outcomes in that population.

Earlier meta-analyses have also suggested positive effects of core training on VJ or jump-related performance across broader athletic populations [7, 8, 17]. Therefore, indeed supporting the present results, core training may improve jump-related performance within relatively broad definitions. As in our results, it was couched as at least a general indicator of physical fitness, not specifically game or position performance outcomes.

Physiologically and biomechanically, several mechanisms may explain this. The trunk acts as a kinetic link that transmits force between the lower and upper extremities, enabling efficient center of mass translation in explosive actions [13], such that VJ is not only a direct consequence of lower-limb force generation but also depends on effective proximal stabilization that minimizes energy loss and allows force to be transmitted through the lumbopelvic region [65, 66]. On this basis, core-oriented training may enhance neuromuscular activation and control of the lumbopelvic–hip complex, thereby maintaining trunk stability during high-force production events [67]. This is particularly important during the stretch–shortening cycle, where smooth coupling between landing and subsequent take-off is fundamental to the successful execution of maximal VJ [68].

From an applied perspective, the VJ interventions included in this review were typically delivered as short-term core training programs (6–8 weeks, 2–3 sessions per week) embedded within regular team practice, most often using static or combined exercise formats. This consistency across studies suggests that relatively brief, time-efficient core programs can be feasibly incorporated into routine training schedules and may yield small but statistically meaningful improvements in VJ performance in team-sport athletes. These findings support the inclusion of core training as one component of a broader performance development framework. Although gains in proximal stability and neuromuscular control may contribute to more effective transmission of lower-limb force during jumping, larger improvements in jump height are likely to depend on concurrent lower-limb strength and power-oriented training. Furthermore, the limited number of available studies restricts detailed evaluation of VJ-specific training moderators. Future trials with larger sample sizes are therefore required to better characterize dose–response relationships and to refine integrated jump training strategies in team-sport populations.

Linear sprint

Linear sprint is a crucial physical determinant of performance within team sports and has been suggested to be inherently related to foundational characteristics of major competitive movements across a number of sports [69]. In basketball, for instance, sprint speed during fast-break transitions is linked to offensive scoring and overall performance [70]. In elite soccer, straight sprint efforts are associated with goal-scoring chances and match results [71]. In rugby union, straight-line sprint ability is associated with goal-line penetrations and defensive recovery during match play [72]. Given that linear sprint provides an indication of how rapidly an athlete can generate horizontal velocity from the outset of performance, it is commonly used as a measure in applied sport science and strength-conditioning assessments. In that light, our meta-analysis found that, relative to non-core training controls, core training had a small effect on linear sprint in team-sport athletes, with low heterogeneity across studies. Previous meta-analyses have also reported mixed findings regarding the effects of core training on linear sprint, although modest improvements have been observed in some athletic populations [6, 7, 24].

Overall, meta-analytic evidence on core training and linear sprint remains mixed; however, modest improvements have been reported in some team-sport athletes, likely underpinned by biomechanical adaptations related to trunk control and horizontal force application. Sprint acceleration requires the transient generation of large and effective horizontal ground reaction forces during the initial ground contact steps, and the force–velocity profile holds promise as a convenient means of quantifying this parameter of mechanical output [73]. The progressive forward lean adopted during early acceleration facilitates alignment of the center of mass in the intended direction of travel, and underpins greater production of horizontal force and rapid establishment of higher velocity profiles overall [74]. Favorable trunk muscle activation and stiffness can promote proximal stability, permitting forces generated in the lower limbs to be passed on more effectively as full-body motion overall, damping needless energy losses and improving the efficiency of torque and force transfer [13, 75]. Experimental work shows that trunk fatigue reduces the associated musculoskeletal stiffness and disrupts elastic energy storage and return during initial ground contacts during explosive lower-limb action, weakening force transfer overall [76]. For this reason, more targeted core training likely aids acceleration capacity by improving trunk–pelvis–lower limb coordination, reflecting neuromuscular control of the various segments rather than trunk strength alone [77, 78]. Because the neuromuscular and postural control systems are still maturing in youth athletes, coordination-based improvements from core training then have more beneficial transfer to speed, which supports adaptability of these types of training methods to the team-sport setting [79, 80].

Sprint interventions outlined in our analysis generally applied short-term core training approaches typically two–three times per week over approximately 6–12 weeks. Session duration ranged between about 15 and 60 min for most studies, and many used combined static–dynamic progressions for exercises, delivered as part of regular team practice. Most studies also assessed linear sprint over shorter distances (10–20 m), suggesting that results are primarily driven from relayed gains in acceleration-type sprinting. Notably, they were recorded mostly under stable training conditions, which implies that conventional floor-based work may be sufficient to support additional sprint improvements. Thus it appears that coaches may include some mixed core exercise type approaches into at least two such sessions a week, to correspondingly provide a noticeable benefit to linear sprint in team-sport athletes. At the same time, session content and completed training work varied across studies, so core training should be combined with sprint-specific and lower-limb power training as part of a broader speed-development program. This is also relevant because lower-limb asymmetry may partly influence sprint and agility performance; targeted pre-season training has been reported to improve 10-m sprint and agility performance while reducing lower-limb muscular asymmetries in elite soccer players [81].

Change-of-direction ability

CODA is a key physical performance measure in team sports as it underpins the ability of players to attack and defend via rapid transitions between offensive and defensive play, while also allowing athletes to slow down, reposition, and then re-accelerate under the pressure of competition [82, 83]. In basketball, CODA is important in gaining separation from opposition defenders and for rapid rotations in defense. Notably, in elite soccer, high-intensity change-of-direction actions are closely associated with subsequent goal-scoring situations, testifying to the tactical salience of CODA during the game [83, 84].

In this context, the current meta-analysis found that, compared with non-core training controls, core training elicited a small but statistically significant improvement in CODA in team-sport athletes, with low heterogeneity across studies. This finding should be applied cautiously to female athletes, given the small number of female-only studies. In female soccer players, lower-limb power is also linked with agility, linear speed, and change-of-direction deficit [85]. Previous reviews have also reported potentially beneficial effects of core or trunk muscle training on CODA or agility, although the available evidence has remained limited and somewhat heterogeneous [6, 7]. In contrast, in the current meta-analysis, 10 studies solely of team-sport athletes were synthesized with CODA regarded as a primary performance outcome, providing more context-specific and methodologically consistent evidence, building the practice-facilitating salience of core training for multidirectional performance.

CODA arises from the integrated processes of coordinated deceleration, re-acceleration and body reorientation under multidirectional demands, and this integrative process requires synchronized eccentric braking and concentric re-acceleration toward a new heading, with effective stabilization of the trunk and control of the center of mass [86, 87]. During intensive directional transitions, rapid force transfer through the lower limbs to the upper body is vital, and improved core stability augments intersegmental transmission by controlling excessive trunk motion and maintaining alignment through a minimal posture shift, thus reducing energy loss and improving mechanical efficiency [60, 88]. Neuromuscular adaptations to core training, characterized by refined timing and coordination of trunk muscle activation, may contribute to improved trunk control during cutting, which supports faster reorientation and shorter deceleration phases [21, 60]. Greater core stiffness and dynamic trunk control are associated with superior braking mechanics and enhanced production of horizontal ground reaction force during the deceleration–propulsion transition, which represents a key determinant of CODA [86, 89]. In youth athletes undergoing maturation of their neuromuscular and postural systems, targeted core training may yield greater gains in coordination and stability, thus facilitating more effective execution of change-of-direction tasks, or observed improvements may reflect greater adaptability of trunk control during development [79, 90].

In practical terms, our findings suggest that effective core training for enhancing CODA can be achieved over around 6–12 weeks, with at least 2 sessions per week in team-sport athletes. The majority of implementing studies applied a combination of static and dynamic core exercises, completed in combination with normal team training sessions and separate sessions. Therefore, effectively incorporating trunk work into common everyday activities may assist in improving COD performance. It is worth noting that all subjects improved in the COD test within 10 trials, and most interventions were for short duration each, 15–30 min sessions, suggesting more efficient core training can transfer to performance without significantly increasing total training volume. Together, these findings suggest potential value for including mixed core-specific exercises alongside sport-specific COD and speed drills, within a bandwidth of physical training. That said, as the exercise design and testing protocols differed between studies, further research should clarify the optimal choice of exercise and progressions; but regular embedded core training may represent a pragmatic way to improve CODA in team-sport athletes.

Limitations

There are a number of limitations to our study.

Firstly, a number of included trials had concerns around randomisation procedures and selective reporting, which may have reduced the precision of our pooled estimates, although effect directions were generally consistent across outcomes and benefits require confirmation in future high-quality RCTs. In particular, all included studies raised some concerns regarding selective outcome reporting, which may have led to some inflation of effect estimates, most likely in a positive direction.

Secondly, the included evidence was dominated by male-only samples. Only 4 studies included female athletes only, and 3 studies did not report participant sex. As a result, the pooled effects may mainly reflect responses in male team-sport athletes. Their applicability to female athletes remains uncertain, especially given possible sex-related differences in lower-limb mechanics, injury risk, and neuromuscular control.

Thirdly, core training protocols varied widely in type, frequency, length and training setting, and a number provided insufficient details on session length or training adherence; therefore, current evidence does not allow identification of the optimal training strain in terms of training volume and intensity or exercise dose. In addition, although all control groups were classified as non-core training controls, the specific comparator conditions varied across studies and included regular sport-specific training, mobility training, strength training, small-sided games, and recreational activities. These differences in intervention structure and comparator type may have contributed to clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Training season was not reported in 13 of the 22 included studies, which may have contributed to the heterogeneity observed for balance outcomes, as training responses may differ across in-season, pre-season, and off-season contexts.

Fourthly, while we reported standardized effect sizes, within outcome domains trials used different field-based tests which may have impacted both the ability to combine trials and the practical relevance of the findings. This was particularly relevant for the balance domain, where different tools assessed somewhat different constructs, such as dynamic versus static balance and unilateral versus bilateral control. This also applied to linear sprint, where sprint distances ranged from 10 m to 40 m and may have reflected different sprint capacities.

Finally, many interventions were short term and follow-up data were generally unavailable and owing to the limited number of controlled trials available for each outcome we were unable to attempt moderation analyses based upon variables such as age and level of competition. In addition, the small sample sizes of many included trials may have limited statistical power and increased the risk of small-study effects and inflated effect estimates. Therefore, the low I² values for VJ, linear sprint, and CODA should not be taken as clear evidence of homogeneity, since the small evidence base may have reduced the ability to detect between-study differences.

Conclusion

Core training is associated with a small and positive effect on some physical performance measures in team-sport athletes compared with non-core training controls, with balance showing the largest effect size. VJ, linear sprint, and CODA show small but statistically significant improvements. Given the variation in specific comparator conditions across studies, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Most interventions were delivered during regular team practice as a 15–30 min session comprising static and dynamic exercises, so their feasibility and short-term effectiveness are supported. We observed larger pooled effects in subgroup analyses when core training studies used more than two sessions per week. However, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously, and wider implementation science work will be needed in team sports. Age and intervention duration showed no significant subgroup differences within the present analyses. There remains an urgent need for more high-quality controlled trials, specifically with larger sample sizes, better standardized outcomes and clearer doses, particularly in female athletes and team-sport populations beyond soccer.
