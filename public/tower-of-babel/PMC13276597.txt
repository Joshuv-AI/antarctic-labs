Different modulation patterns of theta and gamma dual-site HD-tACS on cognitive impairment
Authors: Wensi Hao, Xiaohui Yu, Qilin Zhou, Yu Jia, Xin Su, Yueyi Yu, Yuping Wang, Jiaojian Wang, Chunyan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by impaired local network integration and long-range connections. Theta and gamma oscillations are critical for cognitive function, so this study used dual-site fronto-temporoparietal junction (TPJ) high-density transcranial alternating current stimulation (HD-tACS) to explore its frequency-specific effects on brain networks and cognition. Thirty-six AD patients were randomized 1:1:1 to 6Hz-tACS, 40Hz-tACS and sham stimulation targeting bilateral fronto-TPJ cortex for four weeks. Cognitive functions were assessed at baseline, post-treatment and 8-week follow-up. TMS-EEG and voxel-based distance-related functional connectivity analysis evaluated network changes. Both active stimulation groups showed sustained cognitive improvements for eight weeks compared to the sham stimulation group (all p < 0.017). 40Hz-tACS also enhanced language function (p < 0.025). 6Hz-tACS increased anterior functional connections and anterior-to-posterior information flow, while 40Hz-tACS increased posterior connections and posterior-to-anterior flow, closely linked to cognitive improvements. These effects are oscillation frequency-dependent, supporting cognitive improvement in AD.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is an irreversible, progressive neurodegenerative disease which typically manifests as a progressive impairment of memory, language, and communication abilities.1 The pathophysiology of AD can be attributed by a variety of variables, such as the amyloid-β (Aβ) and tau-protein deposition,2 abnormalities of neuronal and synaptic plasticity, and altered cortical oscillations both within and between brain networks.3 Currently commonly used drug treatments, such as acetylcholinesterase inhibitors,4 have limited efficacy.5 Non-invasive neuromodulation therapy which affects neuroplasticity,6 ultimately improving cognitive and behavioral processes, is developing rapidly.

Complex cognition frequently necessitates long-range integration throughout the entire brain networks, in addition to interactions within local regions.7,8 In large-scale topological network structures, AD is no longer merely regarded as a local lesion, but rather as a disconnection syndrome characterized by the collapse of long-range functional connections (e.g., 100–130 mm) between the main cognitive regions,9,10 thereby leading to disordered network integration. The main involved functional regions include the precentral gyrus, medial prefrontal cortex, temporal-parietal junction (TPJ), and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC).10 Among them, the DLPFC is crucial for top-down executive control and working memory, while the TPJ is a key hub for bottom-up stimulus processing, attention reorientation, and multisensory information integration.7 In AD patients, the white matter fiber bundles connecting the frontal and parietal lobes (such as the superior longitudinal fasciculus), as well as functional connections, are significantly impaired. Studies have shown that the gradual deterioration from mild cognitive impairment to mild AD mainly affects the synchrony of the attention system in the frontal-parietal region, rather than the coordination between hemispheres.11 Therefore, promoting long-range connections and brain network integration, as well as regulating neural plasticity, are the development trends of neuromodulation therapies for AD.

Neural oscillations, as rhythmic electrical events,12 serve as the foundation for the state regulation and information transmission in large-scale brain networks that are associated with memory and behavior.13 Slow oscillations are indicative of remote communication between brain regions, whereas fast oscillations reflect local communication between neuron groups.14 Research has demonstrated that theta oscillation15 and gamma oscillations16,17 are substantially correlated with enhanced cognitive performance. The frontal midline theta oscillation (4–8 Hz) is significantly correlated with cognitive and executive control.18 Declarative and associative memory, visual feature binding,19 and various higher-order cognitive functions20 have been demonstrated to be significantly influenced by gamma oscillation (30–80 Hz).

Hippocampal theta oscillation impairment is a neurological marker of memory impairment in AD.21,22 The prominent disruption of gamma oscillations, proportional to the progression of AD,23 is thought to be the result of the loss of inhibitory interneurons and cholinergic innervation, mediated by the accumulation of toxic amyloid-beta peptides and tau-associated pathology.24,25 In recent years, studies of theta and gamma-frequency stimulation therapy for AD have provided further evidence. Theta-frequency high density transcranial alternating current stimulation (HD-tACS) acting on DLPFC or posterior parietal lobe26 can significantly improve working memory performance. In animal models of AD, gamma oscillation induces a remarkable reduction in the pathological burden of amyloid and tau-protein levels by increasing microglia activity, resulting in a significant improvement in cognitive performance.27,28 40 Hz-tACS therapy targeting bilateral temporal lobes (over 4 weeks) in AD patients reduces tau-protein levels by restoring disrupted endogenous gamma oscillation in AD patients.29 Therefore, modulating theta and gamma brain oscillations at 6 Hz and 40 Hz may be a promising treatment for improving cognitive function in patients with AD.

TACS, acting on endogenous neural oscillations at a specific frequency, can influence the synchronous and desynchronous activity of neural oscillations in complex brain networks,30 thereby increasing cognition adjustment capabilities by promoting the recovery of local and global network functions. HD-tACS31 can improve the target accuracy, leading to improved therapeutic outcomes and a promising future advancement, which is beneficial to the implementation of multi-target synchronous modulation.

We applied dual-site fronto-TPJ HD-tACS to observe the improvements of local and long-range connectivity and cognitive dysfunction in AD patients by regulating the activities of the theta and gamma oscillations of the cognitive network. This study contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying various brain network oscillations in cognitive processing and offers an approach and foundation for effectively regulating and treating cognitive impairment in AD.

Results

Demographic and cognitive function assessments

In this study, we reported the outcomes of a four-week HD-tACS treatment which targeted the bilateral fronto-TPJ (Figure 1) in AD patients. 36 patients completed 4-week treatment and 8-week follow-up for efficacy analysis (Figure S1). Each group consisted of 10 patients who completed the TMS-EEG and fMRI data collection before and after the treatment. There were no statistically significant differences in gender, age, age of onset, education level, AD duration, drug use, and cognitive scale scores among the three groups at baseline (Table S1). Changes of time-varying EEG network patterns after HD-tACS treatments

Time-varying EEG networks showed significant differences in enhanced dynamic information outflow direction of the active stimulus groups before and after treatments, while this was rarely observed in the sham stimulus group. In the 6 Hz-tACS group, the increased information outflow was mainly from the medial prefrontal cortex (Fz) during the early period (at 76 ms and 356 ms) compared with baseline. The middle period (700-1000 ms) showed a pattern of enhanced connections with Fz and bilateral inferior parietal lobes (P3, P4) as hub nodes. In the late period (1880 ms), the enhanced connections between anterior and posterior midline brain areas still increased significantly. In the 40 Hz-tACS group, in the early period (76 ms, 356 ms), the increased information outflow was mainly from the medial parietal cortex (Pz), and showed an enhanced connection pattern that significantly increased from the posterior to anterior midline brain areas. In the middle and late period (700-2000 ms), it still showed an enhanced connection pattern with Pz as the hub node. In the sham stimulus group, local enhanced connections between the left middle-posterior temporal (T5) and central regions increased in the middle and late periods compared with baseline. Correlation of the changes on increased information outflow of hub nodes and scale improvements in AD patients before and after treatment

We found that the increase of MoCA score after treatment in the 6 Hz-tACS group was positively correlated with the increased number of enhanced connections edges of the Fz node at 1068 ms (r = 0.644, p = 0.045 < 0.05) and 1880 ms (r = 0.675, p = 0.032 < 0.05), respectively. In the 40 Hz-tACS group, the increase of the MMSE scale after treatment was positively correlated with the increased number of enhanced connections edges of the Pz node at 76 ms (r = 0.637, p = 0.047 < 0.05). The decreased score of the ADAS-Cog scale was positively correlated with the increased number of enhanced connections edges of the Pz node at 357 ms (r = 0.760, p = 0.010 < 0.05). The decreased score of the ADAS-Cog-memory scale was positively correlated with the increased number of enhanced connections edges of the Pz node at 76 ms and 357 ms (r = 0.678, p = 0.031; r = 0.662, p = 0.037, respectively). No correlation was found between the changes of dynamic time-varying network information flow and clinical changes in the sham stimulation group (Figure S2).

Changes of seed-based distance-related FC analyses after HD-tACS treatment

We performed a seed-based whole-brain resting state FC analysis to determine significant hub-seed alterations after HD-tACS treatment in long and short ranges functional interactions between brain regions of AD patients. After 6 Hz-tACS treatment, compared with baseline, the long-range functional connections between right frontal lobe and bilateral parietal lobe increased, while the functional connections between right parietal lobe and left lateral frontal decreased. In the short-range functional connections, the functional connections between bilateral dorsal prefrontal, medial frontal and central regions increased, the functional connections between the medial frontal and medial frontal limbic regions increased, and the functional connections between the brain regions of the bilateral temporal-occipital lobe decreased (Figure S3, Tables S7 and S8).

After 40 Hz-tACS treatment, there was an observed increase in long-range functional connections between the right parietal lobe and the left frontal-temporal lobe, as well as between the bilateral occipital lobes and the bilateral frontal-central regions, when compared with baseline. In contrast, functional connections between the left caudate and the right inferior temporal gyrus, bilateral occipital lobes decreased. In addition, the short-range functional connections between bilateral temporal-parietal regions and bilateral occipital regions increased, while the connections between bilateral frontal lobes and bilateral midline deep nuclei decreased (Figure S4, Tables S9 and S10).

Discussion

Our investigation revealed that the application of theta and gamma dual-site fronto-TPJ HD-tACS resulted in enhanced cognitive function, which was sustained for 8 weeks following the therapy. The cognitive function in AD patients did not exhibit any substantial improvement in the sham stimulation group. None of the patients experienced severe adverse reactions during the course of treatment or at 8-week follow-up, suggesting that theta and gamma dual-site fronto-TPJ HD-tACS may be a safe and effective treatment for AD patients.

Normal brain function requires interactions between spatially separated and functionally specialized macroscopic regions.32 The directionality of these interactions in large-scale functional networks determined by magnetoencephalography showed that well-organized posterior-to-anterior patterns of information flow in alpha1, alpha2, and beta bands were dominated by regions in the visual cortex and posterior default mode network, yet opposite patterns of anterior-to-posterior flow were found in the theta band, involving mainly regions in the frontal lobe which sends information to a more distributed network.33 This is consistent with our research results. Our findings indicated that theta-tACS led to an enhanced information outflow of dynamic time varying EEG network from the anterior to the posterior pattern with the medial prefrontal lobe (FZ) as the hub node, while gamma-tACS resulted in an increased information outflow of dynamic time varying EEG network from the posterior to the anterior pattern with the medial parietal lobe (PZ) as the hub node. It is proposed that the change of the information flow direction may be dependent on the modulating oscillation frequency.

Patients with AD have dysfunction in the cognitive processing network with the DLPFC,34 TPJ,35 and medial frontal-parietal lobe36 serving as the hub nodes. TACS enhances the coordinated activities between local and distant brain regions by modulating the intrinsic brain oscillation, thereby inducing the changes of spike-time-dependent synaptic plasticity.37 Our findings showed that different oscillation frequency recruitment causes changes in local functional integration of hub nodes in different brain regions. Enhanced local functional integration (short-range functional connections) by theta-tACS were mainly in DLPFC and medial prefrontal lobe, while mainly in parietal-occipital lobe by gamma-tACS. Reduced short-range functional connections by theta-tACS were mainly in bilateral occipital lobes and left middle temporal gyrus, while mainly in frontal-central subcortical regions such as thalamus, parahippocampus, and cingulum by gamma-tACS. Research has demonstrated that the phase synchronization between the PFC, temporal lobe, and hippocampus enhanced and the long-term memory was more comprehensive as the theta oscillation power in the frontal area increased.38 In a randomized, double-blind, sham controlled, crossover study,39 gamma-tACS acting on medial parietal lobe significantly enhanced memory performance and restored cholinergic neurotransmission in the posterior cortex. Thalamus is a key node for coordinating cognitive function. Thalamic architecture is related with the well-established low-dimensional patterns (such as resting-state networks and gradients in spatiotemporal organization) observed in cortico-cortical functional connectivity. Thalamic activity broadcasts gamma-frequency “up” states in the cerebral cortex,40 but then uses the inhibitory blanket of the RTN to protect the cortical state from otherwise distracting thalamic input.41 We speculate that the decrease of short-distance connections in subcortical thalamus by gamma-tACS may be beneficial to better cortico-cortical functional integration. Different frequency regulation may have different effects on the functional integration of local brain regions (anterior or posterior regions) and cortical-subcortical interaction patterns.

The interaction of remote brain regions is necessary for the cognitive processing.32 The reduction of long-range connections may disrupt the functional integration between distant brain regions.9 Research has indicated that patients with AD exhibited impairments in long-range functional connectivity, which leads to increased Aβ deposition, and is significantly correlated with cognitive performance as measured by MMSE and MoCA.9,10 We used resting state fMRI to analyze the improvements of different frequency tACS on long-range functional connections above 100 mm. The results showed that theta dual-site fronto-TPJ HD-tACS induced a long-range connection pattern with increased functional connections between the right frontal lobe and bilateral parietal lobe, and decreased functional connections between the right parietal lobe and the left lateral frontal lobe. It suggested that 6 Hz tACS enhanced connectivity in the prefrontal lobe and information flow from the anterior to the posterior regions, which is consistent with the theta phase acting as a carrier or time frame for organizing and scheduling gamma information. The DLPFC is considered the central hub of cognitive control, and by enhancing the theta power of the frontal lobe and its influence on downstream regions. 6 Hz tACS may improve the top-down, long-range coordination process required for integrating temporal information, a process crucial for working memory and complex cognitive tasks. Gamma dual-site fronto-TPJ HD-tACS caused a long-range connection pattern with increased functional connections between bilateral posterior parietal-occipital lobe and bilateral frontal-central regions, and decreased functional connections between left caudate and bilateral temporal-occipital regions, which is consistent with the role of gamma oscillations in local feature binding and sensory processing. It was suggested that the change of long-range functional connections caused by tACS was also oscillatory frequency dependent (Figure 8). Furthermore, research has demonstrated that the occipital lobe is the neurophysiological source of long-range cortical connections,33 suggesting that the posterior head regions in DMN are a strong sender and frequent receiver of high-frequency information, thereby maintaining internal cognitive psychology and attention, etc.42,43 Furthermore, 40 Hz-tACS demonstrated a substantial improvement in language function in AD patients when contrasted with 6 Hz-tACS, and its efficacy lasted for 8 weeks. This may be attributed to the high gamma coherence during speech production and speech perception in frontal and temporal brain regions.44 Gamma oscillations support local feature binding and semantic integration, while long-range gamma coherence between frontal and temporal regions enables cross-regional communication essential for language processing.45 Our bilateral 40 Hz-tACS protocol, targeting the frontal lobe and TPJ regions, is hypothesized to enhance frontal-temporal gamma synchrony, thereby facilitating more efficient language-related information transfer. In conclusion, different frequency HD-tACS promote network functional integration and cognitive function improvements by recruiting different frequency brain network activities, having distinct brain region preferences for enhancing local functional integration, long-range functional connections and cortical-subcortical communication. Additionally, tACS at different frequencies showed different effects in promoting the directionality of information flow. This study helps us understand the significance of theta and gamma oscillations in brain network activity for cognitive processing and establish a neurobiological foundation for exploring new therapeutic approaches.

Limitations of the study

Our study has limitations. First, this randomized double-blind trial has a small sample size of AD patients. To further support the research results, a larger sample size and multi-center randomized controlled trials are needed. Secondly, all AD patients used cholinesterase inhibitors throughout non-invasive tACS therapy, but the dose was constant during treatment and follow-up, and a sham stimulation control group was provided. Therefore, although the potential cumulative effect of tACS therapy and drugs may exist, this study can still demonstrate the effectiveness of non-invasive tACS on cognitive impairment. Third, due to the small sample size, we were unable to examine the potential influence of sex or gender on treatment outcomes, which should be explored in future larger-scale studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

We recruited 36 right-handed patients (19 men and 17 women) diagnosed with probable AD from December 2020 to December 2023 in the Department of Neurology of Xuanwu Hospital to participate in this prospective, randomized, sham-controlled trial. All the instruments used in the study were identical in terms of size, color, appearance, and weight. All the researchers and patients remained blinded to the group allocation, and were unable to make any guesses about the groups based on their appearance or experience. This study has been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No. LYS[2019]02). All patients provided written informed consent before enrollment. We followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT).46

All patients underwent detailed clinical evaluation by two or more neurologists, including history, physical examination, laboratory tests, and imaging. According to 2018 National Institute on Aging Alzheimer's Association (NIA-AA) guidelines, we recruited patients diagnosed with probable mild-to-moderate Alzheimer’s disease.47 Patients who have been taking cholinesterase inhibitors for at least 3 months need to continue taking the same drug dose during treatment and follow-up.

The dual-site fronto-TPJ HD-tACS was used for treatments. After evaluating 53 people identified as having probable AD for eligibility, 36 patients were randomised in a 1:1:1 ratio to receive sham stimulation, 6Hz-tACS, or 40Hz-tACS (12 in each group). The study flow chart was shown in Figure S1.

Method details

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

We recruited 36 right-handed patients (19 men and 17 women) diagnosed with probable AD according to 2018 National Institute on Aging AD (NIA-AA) guidelines from December 2020 to December 2023 in the Department of Neurology of Xuanwu Hospital. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) right-handed patients aged 50–85 years old; (2) Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) score≤24; (3) The Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score of 0.5 or 1.0; (4) Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) score≤8; (5) The Hachinski Ischemia Scale (HIS) score <4; (6) Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker evidence of Alzheimer’s disease amyloid and tau pathology or PET positive for amyloid. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) any neurological disease other than dementia, such as epilepsy, stroke, progressive neurological disease (such as multiple sclerosis), etc.; (2) contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging: patients with pacemakers, coronary stents and aneurysm clips; (3) diagnosed with major depression or other serious mental illness; (4) taking benzodiazepine or having a history of drug abuse. (5) patients with severe heart, liver, lung and kidney diseases.

Sample size and group allocation

The sample size was estimated using PASS 2020 software (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, Utah, USA) based on a repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) design. The study employed a 3 (Group: 6 Hz tACS, 40 Hz tACS, Sham) × 3 (Time: baseline, week 4, week 12) mixed factorial design with Group as the between-subjects factor and Time as the within-subjects factor. Based on pilot data (n = 12 per group), the following parameters were used: standard deviation (σ) = 7.5, autocorrelation between repeated measures (ρ) = 0.80, and Geisser-Greenhouse correction for violation of sphericity. With a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05, a sample size of 12 participants per group (N = 36 total) provided 84.6% power to detect the Group × Time interaction effect (effect size = 0.636) on ADAS-Cog scores, which was the primary outcome.

The cognitive scale assessments

Cognitive scale and neuropsychological scale assessments were performed by neurologists and neuropsychologists who were unaware of the treatment assignment. All subjects were assessed at baseline (W0), at the end of treatment (W4), and at the 8-week follow up (W12). The primary cognitive assessment was the Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale, Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-Cog). Other cognitive assessment scales included the MMSE, the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and the CDR scale. The occurrence and severity of adverse events were assessed using the Adverse Event Scale.

HD-tACS protocol

We used a multi-channel high-precision transcranial electrical stimulator (Soterix Medical, Inc., New York, United States), employing 8 electrodes in 8 channels to stimulate the target cortical areas of the patients and used the sim NIBS software to simulate the theoretical electric field and current intensity to ensure the efficiency of the electrode montage (Figure 1). One central electrode was placed at the DLPFC (64 conductive electrode cap F3/F4), and two surrounding return electrodes were placed at F5/F6 and FC3/FC4. The other central electrode was placed at the posterior TPJ region (64 conductive electrode cap TP7/TP8), and two surrounding return electrodes were placed at CP5/CP6 and P7/P8. The ground line was placed at the contralateral inferior temporal lobe (64 conductive electrode cap C5/C6). For the active stimulus group, the stimulation frequencies were 6Hz and 40Hz with a peak-to-peak current intensity of 2.0 mA. For the sham stimulus group, electrodes were also placed at the same site, with a 30 s ramp-up to 2.0 mA at the beginning and a 30 s ramp-down at the end. We applied stimulation simultaneously to the left DLPFC and the left posterior TPJ region followed by the right side. Each side received 20 min of stimulation for a daily total of 40 min, with treatments separated by one day. The course of therapy encompassed a duration of four weeks and comprised a total of twelve treatments. After completion of treatments, the patients were asked for adverse symptoms such as headache, skin burning sensation and dizziness, etc.

Data acquisition and analysis of TMS-EEG

In this study, single-pulse TMS-EEG was applied to collect AD patients dynamic time-varying EEG signals before and after treatments. TMS stimulation was performed using the Magstim Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation Instrument (Magstim Company Ltd., London, UK), with a maximum stimulus output intensity of 2.0 T. TMS-EEG data were obtained using a magnetic field-compatible EEG amplifier with a sampling rate of 1,024 Hz (Yunshen Ltd, Beijing, China) and an electrode cap with 32 TMS-compatible electrodes according to a 10–20 system (Greentek Ltd, Wuhan, China). The electrode impedance was maintained below 5 kΩ. Electrodes CPz and nose tip were used as ground and reference electrodes, respectively. A total of 120 sTMS stimuli were administered to the right temporoparietal lobe (corresponding to P4 points on the subject’s scalp, according to the international 10–20 system) through a figure-eight coil with a diameter of 70 mm. The stimulation intensity was 110% of the RMT. Each stimulus was delivered at an interval of 4s, lasting for 8 min. RMT was defined as the minimum stimulation intensity capable of evoking MEP amplitude of 50 μv at 5 out of 10 stimuli.9

We preprocessed EEG data before and after treatments in patients with AD using MATLAB (R2023a) and EEGLAB software. With 100 data segments kept for each patient, TMS served as the stimulus point, and EEG data were intercepted from 500 ms before to 2000 ms after each stimulus point. The filter bandwidth was adjusted to 1-45 Hz and we performed notch filtering on the data (48–52 Hz). Independent component analysis (ICA) was used to remove artifacts, including TMS-induced muscle artifacts, auditory artifacts, eye blinks, eye movements, and electrode noise.48

Resting-state MRI acquisition and analysis

During the scan, the subjects were in a resting state and we used the foam filler to reduce the patients’ head movement. The 3D T1-weighted magnetic-preparation rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) data and resting-state function data were obtained on the Siemens 3.0 T MRI system (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). Resting state functional image scanning were acquired using standard echo-planar imaging (EPI), main parameters: repetition time (TR): 2000 ms, time of echo (TE): 30 ms, acquisition matrix: 64x64, flip angle (FA): 90°, slice thickness: 4 mm, slice interval: 1 mm, voxel size= 3.75 × 3.75 × 4.00 mm3, 240 volumes. High resolution T1 weighted structure(T1WI) was obtained using the MPRAGE sequence, with specific parameters: TR: 1900 ms, TE: 2.2 ms, FA: 9°, acquisition matrix: 256x256, slice thickness:1 mm, slice interval: 0 mm, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1mm3, 176 slices were collected from the whole brain. Structural images were reviewed by two experienced attending physicians to exclude subjects with organic lesions in the brain parenchyma.

The resting state image data was preprocessed using MATLAB, dpabi V7.050 and Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12). Specific steps include: (1) format transformation, (2) removal of the first 10 volumes to achieve the magnetization equilibrium, (3) head motion correction: calculating the translation and rotation parameters of X/Y/z axis, excluding subjects with head motion translation >2 mm and rotation >2°; (4) covariate regression: regressing out the Friston 24 parameter model of interference signals (including head movement parameters, linear trends, cerebrospinal fluid and white matter signals); (5) spatial standardization: registering images to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) coordinate space to eliminate differences in brain structure between different subjects; (6) detrending the transformed image. Finally, all images were filtered (0.01–0.1Hz). In addition, interpolation was further removed on excessive malmotion images exceeding the preset criteria (frame displacement >0.2) by linear interpolation.

Long/short-range functional integration analysis

To determine the effect of tACS on the functional information integration, we used Nodal functional connectivity strength (FCS) analysis.

Nodal functional connectivity strength (FCS) analysis is called the “degree centrality” of weighted networks in graph theory.10 Voxels with higher FCS values in the gray matter template (>1 SD beyond the global mean) are defined as hub nodes in the network, which can be used to evaluate the effect of tACS on the long-range functional information integration, thus reflecting the topological characteristics of functional brain networks.

To calculate the FCS, firstly, the time series was extracted from the pre-processed resting-state fMRI data, and the functional connectivity of any two voxels in gray matter template was calculated using the Pearson’s correlation coefficient,51 and a threshold value of 0.2 was used to remove the weak connection caused by signal noise. This threshold has been shown to exclude spurious connections and maintain intrinsic connection patterns. Each voxel value was converted to a z-value using the Fisher-z transformation to improve normality.51,52,53 The whole brain functional connectivity matrix of each individual was obtained.

Previous studies suggested that the FCS metric had a strong association with connectivity distance (e.g., Euclidean distance) between regions. Therefore, it is important to investigate whether changes of treatment-related FCS in AD patients are dependent on distance. We first calculated the Euclidean distance. Dij was an approximate anatomical distance of functional connectivity between voxel i and voxel j.51,52,53 According to the previous studies, we set the Euclidean distance threshold as 100 mm, where functional connections between 100 mm and 180mm are long-range connections, and those from the distance of two adjacent voxels to 100 mm are short-range connections.51,53 For a given voxel, i, the FCS was computed as follows:FCS(i,k)=1Nvoxels−1∑j≠i,j∈DijNvoxelsZijrij>r0;where zij was the Fisher- Z transformed version of correlation coefficient of rij (between voxel i and voxel j); Nvoxels was the number of voxels in the GM mask, r0 was 0.2 as mentioned above, and Dij was the Euclidean distance between voxel i and voxel j. For each subject, an FCS plot was generated separately for long-range and short-range. Finally, the normalized functional images were spatially smoothed using a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel, and the FCS values were selected for statistical analysis.

Seed-based distance-related functional connectivity analyses

In order to determine which brain regions functional connectivity changes were induced by tACS treatment at long-range and short-range before and after tACS treatments, we conducted a seed-based distance-related whole-brain resting state FC analysis.10 For each region with significant differences in FCS values, we defined a region of interest (ROI) of a 6-mm-radius sphere centered on the maximal peak voxel of the cluster. In this study, we obtained a total of 27 ROIs. For each ROI, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient between its average time series and each voxel time series within the gray matter template, and set functional connections between 100 mm and 180 mm in Euclidian distance as long-range connections, and connections from the distance of two adjacent voxels to 100 mm as short-range connections.51,53 Fisher Z-transformation was further performed to improve the normality of the correlation coefficients.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Demographic and cognitive characteristics analysis

We used the SPSS 26 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and GraphPad Prism 8 for the statistical analysis. The normality of the continuous data was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. If the quantitative data conformed to the normal distribution, the data were described as mean ± standard error, otherwise described as median (interquartile range). The age, age of onset, education years and course of disease of the three groups at baseline were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. Gender and drug use of the three groups at baseline were determined by Chi-square test. Scale scores at three time points (W0, W4 and W12) were assessed by mixed repeated measures ANOVA and the interaction effect of Group X Time was further assessed. Mauchly sphericity test was used to evaluate whether the data met the spherical distribution. If not, Greenhouse-Geisser correction was used. Post-hoc analysis and Bonferroni correction were performed. Pairwise comparisons were performed three times for between-groups (6Hz-tACS vs. 40Hz-tACS, 6Hz -tACS vs. sham, 40Hz-tACS vs. sham, p < 0.017 (0.05/3)) and two times for within-groups (W4/W12 vs. Baseline, p < 0.025 (0.05/2)). The remaining data were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.

EEG data processing and statistics

MATLAB software was used for statistical analysis of the time-varying EEG network, and paired t test was performed on the baseline-corrected time-varying ADTF matrix of AD patients to compare whether there were statistical differences in the dynamic brain network before and after treatment. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, Pearson correlation coefficient was used to analyze the correlation between the changes in information outflow of hub nodes and the scale improvements of AD patients before and after treatments in each group.

Resting-state functional MRI statistics and analysis

MATLAB software and DPABI V7.0 software50 were used to conduct paired t test for the FCS values of the long-range and short-range connections at the voxel level before and after treatments. The cluster size corresponding to multiple comparison correction was 70 voxels.10 If p < 0.05, it was considered that the voxel had statistical difference in the comparison before and after treatments. Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) coordinates and automatic anatomical landmark (AAL) templates were used to locate and identify the standard coordinates of different brain regions. The results were visualized using the SurfIce (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/surfice/).
