Asymptotically Robust Learning-Augmented Algorithms for Preemptive FIFO Buffer Management
Wen-Han Hsieh, Ya-Chun Liang

Abstract
We present a learning-augmented online algorithm for the preemptive FIFO buffer management problem, where packets arrive online to a finite-capacity buffer, must be transmitted in FIFO order, and the algorithm may preemptively discard buffered packets to accommodate future arrivals. Our algorithm simultaneously achieves 1 1 -consistency, η \eta -smoothness, and asymptotic 3 \sqrt{3} -robustness, where η \eta denotes the prediction error. Specifically, it attains an optimal competitive ratio of 1 1 under perfect predictions, degrades smoothly as the prediction error increases, and maintains an asymptotic competitive ratio of 3 \sqrt{3} under arbitrarily inaccurate predictions, matching the best-known worst-case guarantee for the classical online problem [ 10 ] . A key technical contribution of our work is the introduction of an output-based prediction error metric . Because capacity constraints dictate that only a strictly bounded subset of arriving packets is ultimately transmitted, our metric assesses prediction quality over the resulting optimal schedules rather than the raw input sequences, avoiding artificial error penalties. To guarantee robustness, our algorithm dynamically monitors predictions and executes a buffer-clearing strategy upon transitioning to a worst-case fallback mechanism. We prove that the competitive loss incurred by this clearing operation is bounded by an additive capacity constant that vanishes asymptotically. Finally, we show that our algorithm provides a generalized framework for learning-augmented buffer management: substituting the fallback module with any β \beta -competitive online algorithm immediately yields asymptotic β \beta -robustness.

Introduction

Efficient network traffic management is essential for high-performance applications, such as video conferencing and cloud computing, which require high data throughput, low latency, and
strict
reliability. In such
architectures,
Quality of Service (QoS) switches play a central role by offering differentiated services
across
various traffic categories.
System
performance relies on two fundamental mechanisms:
buffer management
and
packet scheduling
. The former operates under spatial constraints:
packets arrive with associated values, and the system must make instantaneous admission
and eviction
decisions under a fixed buffer capacity to maximize the total transmitted value. In contrast, packet scheduling focuses on temporal constraints,
typically
modeled as scheduling on an unbounded buffer subject to
release times and deadlines.

We study the
preemptive FIFO buffer management problem
.
While
numerous
variants of packet scheduling and buffer management
have been extensively studied,
the competitive ratio for this
specific
problem has remained at
\sqrt{3}
since the
seminal
work of Englert and Westermann
[
10
]
,
leaving a persistent gap with the known lower bound of
1.419
.
Furthermore,
the design of learning-augmented algorithms for this setting remains entirely unexplored.
Our objective is to bridge this gap by integrating machine-learned predictions
into the decision-making process.
We aim to achieve optimal performance under
perfectly
accurate predictions while
strictly
maintaining the best-known
\sqrt{3}
-competitiveness as a worst-case guarantee, regardless of prediction quality.

Our Results

•

We design a learning-augmented online algorithm (Algorithm

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Problem Definition

The
preemptive FIFO buffer management problem
relaxes the
strict admission
constraints of the traditional FIFO model by allowing
the eviction of previously accepted packets.
The objective is to maximize the total transmitted value subject to a
strictly
fixed buffer capacity.
We model time as a sequence of discrete
steps
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,T\}
. Let
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{T})

denote
the complete input sequence, where each
\sigma_{i}=(p_{i_{1}},p_{i_{2}},\ldots,p_{i_{k}})

is
the sequence of packets arriving at time step
i
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
Each packet
p\in\sigma_{i}
carries a value
v(p)
.
Every
time step consists of two sequential phases:

1.

Arrival Phase:
Upon the arrival of each packet, the algorithm
must instantaneously decide
to
accept or drop it. The algorithm
may preempt (discard) any resident packet to free capacity.
Each accepted packet is
enqueued
at the tail of the buffer,
preserving FIFO semantics.

2.

Transmission Phase.
After processing all
arrivals
in
\sigma_{i}
, exactly one transmission occurs: the packet at the head of the buffer is transmitted, and the system
irrevocably accrues its value.

2.2
Notation

We
establish notation applicable
to any algorithm

\mathrm{ALG}
,
including the offline optimal schedule (
\mathrm{OPT}
) and the
Preemptive Greedy
algorithm (
\mathrm{PG}
).
We denote the
final schedule produced by

\mathrm{ALG}
on input
\sigma
as
\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma)
, and its total transmitted value as
v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma))=\sum_{p\in\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma)}v(p)
. For any time step
t
,

v(\mathrm{ALG}_{<t}(\sigma))
and
v(\mathrm{ALG}_{\leq t}(\sigma))

denote the total value transmitted strictly before and including
step
t
,
respectively.
Similarly,

v(\mathrm{ALG}_{\geq t}(\sigma))
and
v(\mathrm{ALG}_{>t}(\sigma))

denote
the value transmitted from
step
t
onward.
By definition:

v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma))=v(\mathrm{ALG}_{<t}(\sigma))+v(\mathrm{ALG}_{\geq t}(\sigma))=v(\mathrm{ALG}_{\leq t}(\sigma))+v(\mathrm{ALG}_{>t}(\sigma)).

For an algorithm
\mathrm{ALG}

initialized with
buffer state
B
, we write
\mathrm{ALG}(B,\sigma)
to denote its schedule.

B^{\mathrm{ALG}}_{t}

represents
the buffer state at the end of time step

t
, holding
total value
v(B^{\mathrm{ALG}}_{t})
.
For a learning-augmented algorithm
utilizing
prediction
\hat{\sigma}

alongside
the actual input
\sigma
, we denote
the schedule
as
\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})
and
the associated prediction error as

\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})

(formalized
in Section
2.4
). Similarly,
the additive property holds
for any time step
t
,
i.e.,

v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))=v(\mathrm{ALG}_{<t}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))+v(\mathrm{ALG}_{\geq t}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))=v(\mathrm{ALG}_{\leq t}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))+v(\mathrm{ALG}_{>t}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})).

2.3
Performance Metrics

2.3.1
Online Algorithms

Performance is evaluated
via competitive analysis
[
27
,
17
]
.

Definition 1

(Competitive Ratio)
.

An online algorithm
\mathrm{ALG}
for a maximization objective is
c
-competitive
if there exists a constant
k\geq 0
such that for all input sequences
\sigma
:

v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))\leq c\cdot v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma))+k,\quad\text{where }c=\sup_{\sigma}\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma))}.

An algorithm is
strictly
c
-competitive
if
k=0
, and weakly
c
-competitive otherwise.
We
also define
the asymptotic competitive ratio
to capture limiting behavior.

Definition 2

(Asymptotic Competitive Ratio)
.

The asymptotic competitive ratio
AC

is defined as:

AC=\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\left\{\frac{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))}\;\middle|\;v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))=n\right\}.

Remark 3
.

For a maximization objective, the traditional definition of the asymptotic competitive ratio is mathematically equivalent to the following formulation:

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left\{\frac{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))}\;\middle|\;v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))=n\right\}\geq\frac{1}{c}\iff\limsup_{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma))\to\infty}\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma))}\leq c

In our subsequent proofs, we adopt the
right-hand
formulation
(evaluating the limit as
v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma))\to\infty
).
This
maintains consistency with
both the standard structure of competitive analysis and the established conventions in prior work on this problem.

2.3.2
Learning-Augmented Online Algorithms

The performance of
learning-augmented algorithms is characterized by three criteria:

Definition 4

(Learning-Augmented Properties)
.

For a maximization problem, an algorithm
\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})
is:

•

\alpha
-consistent:

if

\sup_{\hat{\sigma}=\sigma}\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}\leq\alpha.\quad\text{(performance under perfect predictions)}

•

\beta
-robust:

if

\sup_{\hat{\sigma}}\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}\leq\beta.\quad\text{(worst-case guarantee)}

•

\eta
-smooth:

if there exists a non-decreasing
function
f(\eta)
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)}{\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})}\leq f(\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})),\text{satisfying $f(0)=\alpha$, and
bounded globally by $\beta$.}

(graceful degradation of the competitive ratio as prediction error
\eta
increases)

2.4
Output-Based Prediction Error

A fundamental challenge in
learning-augmented mechanism design
is
formulating an error metric that faithfully captures the relationship between prediction accuracy and algorithmic degradation.
Traditionally, many
online
problems adopt input-based error measures, such as the
\ell_{1}
-norm
distance
between the predicted and actual input sequences. While
mathematically intuitive for unconstrained environments, these metrics structurally overestimate prediction error in admission control paradigms like the preemptive FIFO buffer, where the system processes an entire arrival sequence but fundamentally admits and transmits only a strictly bounded subset.

To illustrate this structural mismatch,
consider a
severely capacity-constrained
scenario where
a massive volume of
packets
arrives
over time, but the buffer
size permits the transmission of only a tiny fraction (e.g., admitting
100
out of
1000
arriving packets).
If
a prediction mechanism perfectly anticipates the values and optimal schedules for the transmitted subset but wildly misestimates the values of the discarded majority, an input-based metric would accumulate a massive, yet entirely artificial, error penalty.
Since neither the offline optimal schedule nor the online algorithm ever transmits these discarded packets, such mispredictions exert zero influence on the final objective value. This reveals a critical flaw: penalizing discrepancies over untransmitted packets conflates irrelevant input noise with actual scheduling degradation.

Motivated by this observation, we argue that a well-defined error metric for buffer management must strictly isolate the discrepancies that explicitly alter the final transmitted sequence.
Consequently,
we introduce
a novel
output-based prediction error.
Instead of measuring the distance between input sequences, this metric is formulated over
the total value of the symmetric difference between the
offline
optimal schedules
generated
under the true and predicted inputs.
By shifting the evaluation from the input stream to the resulting optimal schedules, this metric precisely captures the true cost of prediction failures.
The following are the
formal
definitions of symmetric difference and our prediction error
metric.

Definition 5

(Symmetric Difference)
.

For sets
A
and
B
, the symmetric difference is

A\triangle B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
.

Definition 6

(Output-Based Prediction Error)
.

Let
\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)
and
\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma})
be the offline optimal schedules
for
the true sequence
\sigma
and prediction
\hat{\sigma}
, respectively. The prediction error
\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})
is:

\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})=\sum_{p\in\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma})\triangle\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)}v(p)=\underbrace{\sum_{q\in\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma})\setminus\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)}v(q)}_{D(\hat{\sigma},\sigma)}+\underbrace{\sum_{q^{\prime}\in\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)\setminus\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma})}v(q^{\prime})}_{D(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})}.

Remark 7
.

D(\hat{\sigma},\sigma)
captures

false positives
:
packets the predicted optimal transmits but the true optimal discards, causing the online algorithm to retain suboptimal packets.

D(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})
captures

false negatives
:
high-value packets the true optimal transmits but the predicted schedule ignores.

Theorem 8
.

Algorithm
1

Proof.

Since Algorithm
1

perfectly replicates

\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma})
throughout
the
execution, we have

\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})=\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma}).

Under perfect predictions (
\hat{\sigma}=\sigma

the competitive ratio evaluates to:

\sup\limits_{\hat{\sigma}=\sigma}\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}=\sup\limits_{\hat{\sigma}=\sigma}\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma}))}=1.

Hence, Algorithm
1
achieves 1-consistency.
∎

Theorem 9
.

Algorithm
1
exhibits unbounded robustness.

Proof.

To evaluate the robustness, we first establish the competitive ratio as a function of the prediction error.
We compute the difference in the total transmitted value by explicitly decomposing
\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma})
and
\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)
into their intersection and respective set differences. Letting
S^{\mathrm{COM}}=\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma})\cap\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)
denote the set of commonly transmitted packets, we expand the schedules as follows:

\begin{cases}\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma})=S^{\mathrm{COM}}\cup D(\hat{\sigma},\sigma),\\
\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)=S^{\mathrm{COM}}\cup D(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}).\end{cases}

Here, as established in Remark
7
,
D(\hat{\sigma},\sigma)
and
D(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})
represent the packets
transmitted exclusively by
\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma})
and
\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)
, respectively. The absolute difference evaluates to:

\displaystyle\left|v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))-v(\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma}))\right|

\displaystyle=\left|v(S^{\mathrm{COM}}\cup D(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))-v(S^{\mathrm{COM}}\cup D(\hat{\sigma},\sigma))\right|

\displaystyle=\left|v(D(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))-v(D(\hat{\sigma},\sigma))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|v(D(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))\right|+\left|v(D(\hat{\sigma},\sigma))\right|

\displaystyle=\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}).

Rearranging the absolute value inequality and
dividing by
v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))
yields:

\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}\leq\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma}))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}+\frac{\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}\triangleq f(\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})).

(1)

Equation (
1
) demonstrates that the competitive ratio is strictly bounded by a non-decreasing function
f(\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\to\infty}f(\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))=\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma}))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}+\lim\limits_{\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\to\infty}\frac{\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}=\infty.

1-Consistent, Smooth, and
\sqrt{3}
-Robust Algorithm

Algorithm
2
extends
the intuitive algorithm by embedding a real-time protective mechanism.
Upon detecting an untrustworthy prediction, it permanently switches to
\mathrm{PG}(2+\sqrt{3})
, the best-known
\sqrt{3}
-competitive
baseline
algorithm. The switch is
governed by two simultaneous thresholds:

•

Prediction-Consistency Check (
v(\mathrm{OPT}_{\leq i}(\hat{\sigma}))/v(\mathrm{ALG}_{\leq i}(\sigma))>\rho
):
Detects when the algorithm significantly lags behind the predicted optimal schedule. The parameter
\rho
regulates strictness. The lower bound
\rho=1
enforces absolute adherence to yield
1

\rho=\sqrt{3}
structurally matches the worst-case robustness limit.

•

PG-Robustness Check (
v(\mathrm{PG}_{\leq i}(\sigma))/v(\mathrm{ALG}_{\leq i}(\sigma))>1
):
Detects critical false negatives. If the prediction systematically ignores high-value packets, the Prediction-Consistency Check alone is insufficient, as both the predicted optimal and the algorithm would incorrectly discard them, keeping their ratio artificially low. To prevent this, a virtual instance of
\mathrm{PG}(2+\sqrt{3})
operates continuously on a separate buffer. It serves as a real-time lower bound of baseline performance, triggering an immediate switch if the prediction falls behind what a greedy heuristic would naturally achieve.

Upon violating either threshold, Algorithm
2

executes the
buffer-clearing strategy
and permanently
transitions to the baseline.

The Buffer-Clearing Strategy.

Switching to the baseline algorithm during the execution introduces a technical challenge. At the moment of the switch, the current buffer state was constructed based on inaccurate predictions. Continuing to process packets with this suboptimal state could violate the competitive bounds of the
\mathrm{PG}
algorithm.
Therefore, upon switching, the algorithm executes a buffer-clearing strategy that
instantaneously discards all resident packets.

While discarding the entire buffer may appear excessively aggressive, the theoretical insight is that the strict capacity constraint naturally bounds the total discarded value. Consequently, the resulting loss is strictly an additive constant that vanishes asymptotically.
Lemma
10

formally proves
that the offline optimal value differs by at most
the initial buffer value

\varepsilon

when comparing executions starting from an arbitrary buffer versus an empty buffer (the formal proof is deferred to Section
4.2
).

Lemma 10

(OPT Comparison Lemma)
.

Consider
an arbitrary initial buffer

B
and
an empty buffer

B^{\prime}=\emptyset
.
Let

v(\mathrm{OPT}(B,\sigma))

denote
the total value transmitted by the optimal schedule
operating on input
\sigma
starting from buffer
B
.
Then:

v(\mathrm{OPT}(B^{\prime},\sigma))-\varepsilon\leq v(\mathrm{OPT}(B,\sigma))\leq v(\mathrm{OPT}(B^{\prime},\sigma))+\varepsilon,

Analysis of Algorithm

We now prove that Algorithm

\eta

4.1
Analysis of Consistency and Smoothness

Theorem 11
.

Algorithm
2
is 1-consistent.

Proof.

Under perfect predictions,

v(\mathrm{OPT}_{\leq i}(\hat{\sigma}))=v(\mathrm{OPT}_{\leq i}(\sigma))

for all
i\in[T]
.
Furthermore, the optimal sequence trivially upper-bounds the greedy baseline, ensuring
v(\mathrm{PG}_{\leq i}(\sigma))\leq v(\mathrm{ALG}_{\leq i}(\sigma))
holds at every step
i
.
Therefore,
neither the Prediction-Consistency Check nor the PG-Robustness Check is ever triggered.
Algorithm
2

mirrors
\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)
precisely, and
1
-consistency follows directly
from Theorem
8
.
∎

Theorem 12
.

Algorithm
2
is
\eta
-smooth.

Proof.

Following the analytical steps established
in the proof of Theorem
9
,
we have the global upper bound:

v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))\leq v(\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma}))+\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}).

We focus on the execution where the algorithm relies on the prediction (i.e., the protective fallback is not triggered).
Under this condition,
the Prediction-Consistency Check
ensures that the performance strictly satisfies

v(\mathrm{OPT}(\hat{\sigma}))\leq\rho\cdot v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})).

Substituting this into the upper bound yields:

v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))\leq\rho\cdot v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))+\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}).

Dividing both sides by
v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))

yields our first bound on the competitive ratio:

\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}\leq\rho+\frac{\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}.

Simultaneously, because the PG-Robustness Check is never triggered, we guarantee that

v(\mathrm{PG}(\sigma))\leq v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})).

Combining this with the
\sqrt{3}
-competitiveness of
\mathrm{PG}
, we obtain a second bound:

\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}\cdot v(\mathrm{PG}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}\leq\sqrt{3}.

Taking the minimum of these two bounds, we conclude that the competitive ratio is globally bounded by the function, which we define as:

f(\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))\triangleq\min\left(\rho+\frac{\eta(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))},\,\sqrt{3}\right).

Since
\rho\geq 1
is a constant,
f(\eta)
is a non-decreasing function that degrades smoothly with the prediction error
\eta
, and is strictly capped at
\sqrt{3}
. Note that if an excessively large error forces a switch to the baseline, the performance is independently capped by the asymptotic
\sqrt{3}
-robustness (proved subsequently in Theorem
14
). Hence, Algorithm
2
is
\eta
-smooth.
∎

4.2
Analysis of the Buffer-Clearing Strategy

To prove Lemma
10
, we
partition
the transmitted packets into those
arriving in
the future input
\sigma
and those
originally residing in buffer
B
.

Proposition 13
.

Let
\mathrm{OPT}_{\emptyset}(\sigma)
and
\mathrm{OPT}_{B}(\sigma)

denote the optimal schedules constructed strictly from packets arriving in the future input sequence
\sigma
, starting from buffer states
\emptyset
and
B
, respectively.
Then,

v(\mathrm{OPT}_{\emptyset}(\sigma))\geq v(\mathrm{OPT}_{B}(\sigma)).

Proof.

We observe that any sequence of newly arriving packets that can be feasibly scheduled starting from a partially full buffer
B
also remains feasible when starting from an empty buffer
\emptyset
.
Let
\mathrm{OPT}_{B}(\sigma)=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n})
.
Since
\mathrm{OPT}_{\emptyset}(\sigma)
is the optimal schedule over
the superset of all such feasible schedules,
we have
v(\mathrm{OPT}_{\emptyset}(\sigma))\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}v(b_{i})=v(\mathrm{OPT}_{B}(\sigma))
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
10
.

We first prove the right-hand inequality.
Since
B^{\prime}=\emptyset
, we have
\varepsilon=|v(B)-v(B^{\prime})|=v(B)
. We decompose
\mathrm{OPT}(B,\sigma)
into two disjoint:
\mathrm{OPT}_{B}(\sigma)

(transmissions from future input)
and
\mathrm{OPT}(B,\sigma)\setminus\mathrm{OPT}_{B}(\sigma)

(transmissions from the initial buffer).
Because the latter is drawn entirely from
B
, its value cannot exceed
v(B)=\varepsilon
. Applying Proposition
13
:

\displaystyle v(\mathrm{OPT}(B,\sigma))

\displaystyle=v(\mathrm{OPT}_{B}(\sigma))+v(\mathrm{OPT}(B,\sigma)\setminus\mathrm{OPT}_{B}(\sigma))

\displaystyle\leq v(\mathrm{OPT}_{B^{\prime}}(\sigma))+v(\mathrm{OPT}(B,\sigma)\setminus\mathrm{OPT}_{B}(\sigma))

\displaystyle\leq v(\mathrm{OPT}_{B^{\prime}}(\sigma))+v(B)

\displaystyle=v(\mathrm{OPT}(B^{\prime},\sigma))+\varepsilon.

Next, we prove the left-hand inequality.
Consider
a suboptimal schedule that immediately preempts every packet in
B
at time step
1
, subsequently executing
\mathrm{OPT}(\emptyset,\sigma)
.
This schedule achieves
a value of

v(\mathrm{OPT}(\emptyset,\sigma))=v(\mathrm{OPT}(B^{\prime},\sigma))
. Since
\mathrm{OPT}(B,\sigma)

dominates all feasible schedules originating from
B
:

\mathrm{OPT}(B,\sigma)\geq\mathrm{OPT}(\emptyset,\sigma)=\mathrm{OPT}(B^{\prime},\sigma)\geq\mathrm{OPT}(B^{\prime},\sigma)-\varepsilon.

∎

4.3
Analysis of Robustness

Theorem 14
.

Algorithm
2
is asymptotically
\sqrt{3}
-robust.

Proof.

Assume the algorithm triggers a switch at time step
t^{*}
. We analyze the execution by partitioning the timeline into
[1,t^{*}-1]
and
[t^{*},T]
.
Prior to
t^{*}
, the PG-Robustness Check remained unviolated, guaranteeing
v(\mathrm{PG}_{\leq i}(\sigma))\leq v(\mathrm{ALG}_{\leq i}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))
for all
i<t^{*}
. Combining this with the established
\sqrt{3}
-competitiveness of the
\mathrm{PG}
algorithm:

v(\mathrm{OPT}_{<t^{*}}(\sigma))\leq\sqrt{3}\cdot v(\mathrm{PG}_{<t^{*}}(\sigma))\leq\sqrt{3}\cdot v(\mathrm{ALG}_{<t^{*}}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})).

From step
t^{*}
onward, the physical buffer is cleared. Applying Lemma
10
to relate the optimal schedule starting from
B_{t^{*}-1}^{\mathrm{OPT}}
to an empty buffer, and invoking the
\sqrt{3}
-competitiveness of
\mathrm{PG}
:

\displaystyle v(\mathrm{OPT}_{\geq t^{*}}(\sigma))

\displaystyle=v(\mathrm{OPT}(B_{t^{*}-1}^{\mathrm{OPT}},\sigma_{\geq t^{*}}))

\displaystyle\leq v(\mathrm{OPT}(\emptyset,\sigma_{\geq t^{*}}))+v(B_{t^{*}-1}^{\mathrm{OPT}})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3}\cdot v(\mathrm{PG}(\emptyset,\sigma_{\geq t^{*}}))+v(B_{t^{*}-1}^{\mathrm{OPT}})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{3}\cdot v(\mathrm{ALG}_{\geq t^{*}}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))+v(B_{t^{*}-1}^{\mathrm{OPT}}).

Summing the partitions to compute the global competitive ratio:

\displaystyle\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}

\displaystyle=\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}_{<t^{*}}(\sigma))+v(\mathrm{OPT}_{\geq t^{*}}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}_{<t^{*}}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))+v(\mathrm{ALG}_{\geq t^{*}}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}\cdot\left[v(\mathrm{ALG}_{<t^{*}}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))+v(\mathrm{ALG}_{\geq t^{*}}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))\right]+v(B_{t^{*}-1}^{\mathrm{OPT}})}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{3}+\frac{v(B_{t^{*}-1}^{\mathrm{OPT}})}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}.

Evaluating the limit as
v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))
approaches infinity:

\lim\limits_{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))\to\infty}\frac{v(\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma))}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}\leq\sqrt{3}+\lim\limits_{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))\to\infty}\frac{v(B_{t^{*}-1}^{\mathrm{OPT}})}{v(\mathrm{ALG}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}))}=\sqrt{3}.

The additive capacity constant vanishes, establishing that Algorithm
2
is asymptotically
\sqrt{3}
-robust.
∎

5
Conclusion and Open Problems

We bridge the gap between classical worst-case limits and modern machine-learned predictions for
the preemptive FIFO buffer management problem.
Specifically, we present a novel learning-augmented framework that simultaneously achieves

1
-consistency,
\eta
-smoothness, and asymptotic
\sqrt{3}
-robustness.
By continuously monitoring prediction quality against a virtual baseline, the algorithm dynamically executes a buffer-clearing fallback strategy when predictions prove unreliable. Crucially, we prove that the structural loss incurred by this clearing operation is strictly bounded by the buffer capacity, an additive constant that vanishes asymptotically, thereby preserving the worst-case
\sqrt{3}
-robustness.

A central technical innovation of this research is the formulation of an output-based prediction error metric. Recognizing that traditional input-based distances artificially penalize mispredictions on untransmitted packets in capacity-constrained environments, the proposed metric assesses prediction quality strictly over the resulting optimal schedules. By grounding the error in the symmetric difference of the outputs, this approach precisely captures the true cost of prediction failures, which directly enables the graceful
\eta
-smoothness guarantee. Furthermore, this mechanism naturally extends into a generalized framework: substituting the fallback module with any
\beta
-competitive online algorithm seamlessly yields an asymptotic
\beta
-robustness guarantee.

These results open several promising directions for future research. A fundamental open question is to establish the theoretical lower bound on the robustness of strictly
1
-consistent algorithms for this problem, characterizing the inherent tradeoff between perfect consistency and worst-case robustness. Additionally, designing practical machine learning models explicitly tailored to minimize the proposed output-based error metric, or exploring the theoretical limits of alternative prediction models (e.g., predicting packet values or arrival distributions), remains an exciting avenue for bridging theoretical guarantees with empirical network performance.
