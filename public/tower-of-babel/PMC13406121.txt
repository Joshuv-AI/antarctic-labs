Percutaneous Release of Trigger Finger: Long-Term Review of Outcomes Using 18-Gauge Needle
Authors: Akshai Mansingh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:This was a retrospective study of a large cohort of patients with trigger fingers treated by percutaneous release by a single surgeon who had a long-term follow-up. The study evaluates patient demographics, occupation, and comorbidities, as well as the long-term outcomes of the technique along with complications.Methods:This was a retrospective study of adult patients who underwent percutaneous release for Quinnell grade 2 to 4 trigger fingers over a 25-year period.Results:Percutaneous release was performed on 863 fingers in 551 patients. Women were affected three times more often than men. The thumb was most commonly affected. Approximately one-third of patients had multiple digit involvement. Hand dominance was markedly associated with triggering. No notable effects were observed related to jobs characterized by repetitive hand movements or to diabetes. No complications were reported after percutaneous release.Conclusion:Being an office desk procedure, percutaneous release is permanent, safe, and cost-effective and should be considered as first-line treatment for demonstrable trigger fingers.

Trigger finger (stenosing tenosynovitis) affects up to 2% of the United States' general population.1-3 It is characterized by pain, swelling, limited movement, and clicking, snapping or triggering of the affected digit.3-9 This results in restricted activities and interference with normal functioning. The thickening of the A1 pulley causes the entrapment of the flexor tendon, which impedes its natural gliding capability and results in the triggering mechanism.1,4,6,10-14

Clinical severity is graded by Quinnell classifications (Table 1). While nonsurgical treatments are often attempted for milder grades,2 surgical release of the A1 pulley is considered the most definitive treatment.2,11 Corticosteroid injections can provide symptomatic relief within 3 weeks15 but are associated with potential complications such as infection, skin atrophy, and recurrence, with variable success rates, particularly in diabetic patients.15,16 Percutaneous release, first described in 1958, offers a less invasive alternative to standard open surgery.4,9,13,17 Performed as an office-based procedure, it is associated with low complication rates, less postoperative pain, avoidance of palmar scarring, and faster recovery of motor function.5,7,8,11,14 Despite being a well-established technique, there is a paucity of large studies reporting its very long-term outcomes.

This study presents a single surgeon's 25-year experience with over 850 percutaneous trigger finger releases using a hypodermic needle. The primary objectives were to evaluate the long-term outcomes and complications of the technique and to establish a comprehensive demographic profile of affected patients, with a focus on age, occupation, digit involvement, hand dominance, and the influence of comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus.

Methods

This was a retrospective study of adult patients (older than 18 years), referred to the author, with Quinnell grade 2 to 4 trigger fingers between July 1, 2000, and June 30, 2024. Patients with grade 0 or 1 triggering were excluded because the absence of a consistently demonstrable trigger precluded intraoperative confirmation of a complete release. Patients with a history of previous surgery on the same digit, severely distorted hand anatomy (eg, severe Dupuytren contracture), or taking anticoagulation medication were also excluded. All included patients underwent percutaneous release and were followed up for a minimum of 6 months.

Data were collected on patient age, sex, occupation, coexisting diseases, hand dominance, affected fingers, duration of symptoms, and the date of their most recent follow-up examination.

The surgical technique performed was a blind release using an 18-gauge hypodermic needle, performed at the office desk. Once the patients could demonstrate triggering, the skin was cleaned with povidone-iodine and anesthetized with 1.5 mL of 2% lidocaine. This was administered proximal to the site of triggering, which was usually 2 to 3 mm proximal to the distal palmar crease over the metacarpophalangeal joint on the palmar surface (Figure 1). In some cases, patients were unable to demonstrate triggering due to pain. In these instances, local anesthesia was administered, and the procedure proceeded only if triggering could then be demonstrated.

The needle was then introduced through the site of the previous injection and advanced under the A1 pulley in a longitudinal fashion parallel to the tendon. Once the “give” was felt under the sheath, the beveled edge was used to incise the sheath. The needle was removed completely, and the extent of the release was assessed (Videos 1 and 2). If incomplete, the procedure was repeated until the release was successful and the finger moved freely. If an additional entry was needed distal to the original point, this was done to ensure a complete release. It is important that one remains in the line of the tendon on any finger (including the thumb), so as not to cause neurovascular damage. After securing hemostasis with digital pressure, a sticking plaster was applied. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medication was then given to patients for the following 24 hours.

Nonparametric analyses and the chi-square test of association were conducted to determine whether there were any statistically significant associations among the variables of interest. The results of these analyses were defined as statistically significant if their associated P values were <0.05.

Results

A total of 863 digits in 551 patients underwent percutaneous release, most of whom were women (396 patients, 71.8%). The peak incidence occurred in patients aged between 51 and 70 years, accounting for 549 digits (64.4%; Figure 2). The thumb was the most frequently affected digit (342 digits, 39.6%), followed by the middle finger (214 digits, 25%; Figure 3). The dominant hand was significantly more affected (57.5%; P = 0.0023). Multiple digits were released in 176 patients (31.9%; Table 2). One patient underwent nine releases, and four patients had seven releases. The mean duration of symptoms before presentation for the first finger was 4.1 months (range, 0.5 to 60 months) and 4.0 months for subsequent fingers. For the 18 patients who had undergone a previous open release on a different digit, all returned for percutaneous release of subsequently affected fingers.

Follow-up from the time of release ranged from 6 to 254 months, with a mean of 99 ± 72.3 months. One patient presented at the age of 44 with a congenital trigger thumb that was released. His record was not included in the calculations for the duration of symptoms.

No long-term complications were observed, including tendon ruptures, infections, or long-term nerve injuries. One patient reported digit hypersensitivity that resolved completely within 3 weeks. An incomplete release, defined as persistent triggering after the procedure, occurred in 10 digits (1.2%). All were successfully treated with a second percutaneous release performed 2 to 6 weeks later. Three patients (0.3% of fingers) returned with recurrences in the same digit after 5 to 6 years and underwent percutaneous re-release. Two patients (0.3%) ultimately underwent open release: one due to the presence of Dupuytren contracture requiring more extensive release and the other who declined a repeat percutaneous procedure.

Comorbidities were present in 193 patients (35%), with hypertension being the most common (131 patients, 23.8%). Diabetes mellitus was present in 77 patients (14.0%), accounting for 135 fingers (15.6%). The difference in incidence between diabetics and nondiabetics was not statistically significant (P = 0.61), nor was multidigit involvement in diabetic patients. The duration of symptoms before presentation among diabetic patients was not significantly different (P = 0.21) compared with nondiabetic patients.

Eleven patients had other connective tissue disorders (eight with mild Dupuytren contractures, two with neurofibromatosis, and one with Peyronie disease). Fifteen had previously had carpal tunnel syndrome (twelve) or De Quervain tenosynovitis (four). One patient had both. Most (358 patients [65%]) had no coexisting disease.

Occupation was categorized as repetitive hand use (eg, manual labor and housewife) in 190 patients (34.5%), desk-based (including computer use) in 267 patients (48.5%), and medical field in 40 patients (7.3%). Occupation could not be determined for 48 patients (8.7%).

Discussion

This level III study is the largest study we are aware of, documenting the long-term outcomes of percutaneous trigger finger release, with only one other study looking at more than 500 patients.5 The treatment of trigger fingers depends on the severity.2,17 It was suggested that Quinnell grade 1 to 3 fingers be initially treated nonsurgically, with surgery indicated only after conservative methods fail.4,7,13 Owing to a fixed deformity in the underlying structures of the hand,8 grade 4 trigger fingers are locked and, therefore, said not to be amenable to percutaneous release.7,11,12 For this study, once triggering could be established (grade 2 to 4), percutaneous release was offered as primary treatment. Grade 0 and 1 triggering present problems in ensuring full release because they are not consistently demonstrable before the procedure. Because percutaneous release requires visual cues of smooth finger movement to ensure complete release, this cannot be determined in the early grades. It was believed that the lack of complications and minimally invasive nature of this procedure superseded giving corticosteroid injections, which have some associated complications and have the same level of inconvenience (an injection) to the patient.

Nonsurgical treatment includes splinting the affected digit, anti-inflammatory medication, and corticosteroid injections.1,4,7,11,18-20 Extracorporeal shock wave treatment has had moderate success.12,21 Surgical releases can be done either percutaneously or openly.11,20 The success rate of the open approach is as high as 99%3,6 and is reported to have better overall long-term outcomes.6 However, the success rate of percutaneous release is also over 90%,5,6,22 with some reports citing 100% success.14,19,23 This study had an initial success rate of 98.5% with initial release and 100% with re-release.

Percutaneous release is associated with decreased pain and scarring in the short term, better satisfaction, and earlier return to work, although the long-term outcomes are the same as with open release.24,25 Patients with severe Dupuytren contractures or other conditions that distort finger anatomy and those with previous finger surgery are recommended to undergo open release to clearly visualize their structures.

The low complication profile is a key finding. With no tendon ruptures or permanent nerve injuries in over 850 procedures, this study provides strong evidence that the blind percutaneous technique, when performed meticulously, carries a minimal risk of iatrogenic injury. This challenges the theoretical concerns regarding neurovascular damage often associated with blind releases.4,7,8 The single case of transient hypersensitivity (which resolved) and the low rate of incomplete release (1.2%) further underscore the safety and reliability of the technique. The 0.4% recurrence rate, all successfully managed with repeat percutaneous release, suggests that even long-term failure can be addressed with the same minimally invasive approach. Although ultrasonography guidance has been advocated to improve the precision of the release,7,26 the exceedingly low complication and incomplete release rates in this large series suggest that its routine use may not be necessary for surgeons experienced in the technique.

Open release, on the contrary, is associated with a greater risk of complications, such as scarring and infection.3,11

The only other large study by Weiss et al2 also noted no complications in 596 fingers (429 patients), with only 9.9% (59 fingers) needing re-releases. Of these 59 failed releases, 17 underwent another percutaneous release (15 were successful) and 40 opted for open release (two chose no additional treatment), all of which were successful. This study likewise had a success rate of 98.5%, with 1.4% receiving a re-release percutaneously. Both large studies are single surgeon experiences and underscore that percutaneous release is a safe procedure with results similar to open release. In this study the incomplete releases did not occur at any particular time on the learning curve, these large series.

There is a cost advantage of percutaneous release5,23 compared with open surgery, which requires an operating theater or procedure room. Although initial corticosteroid injections have been recommended for diabetic trigger finger,2 percutaneous release is more economical and effective, without the complications of the injection. Overall, the highly successful outcomes with percutaneous release of mature trigger fingers suggest that it should be considered a primary treatment, being a safe, effective, and inexpensive treatment approach.

An 18-gauge needle, as previously described,27 had been used successfully by the author. Although use of 19-gauge to 21-gauge needles5,28 has been described, the sturdiness of the 18-gauge needle allows it to get through thick sheaths, which may not be possible with the thinner needles. By inserting the needle under the sheath and lifting, there is minimal risk of damage to the nerves, even at the thumb.

Trigger finger mostly affects patients in their fifth, sixth, and seventh4-6,11,12,14 decades of life and has been reported to be up to six times more common in women,4-6,8,14 although in this study, the ratio was 3:1. Fingers reported to be most affected are the thumb4-6,8,12,14 and ring finger,4-6 although the middle finger was more frequently involved than the latter in this study. Multiple digit involvement was 32% compared with 27% in the study by Weiss et al.5

Strain and accompanying degenerative issues induced by frequent hand use are likely implicated in the predominance of the dominant hand being affected,4 as seen in this study.

Trigger finger has been described to be more common among diabetics,2,4,5,11-13 with a prevalence of 10% to 20%,4,6,8,12 due to the increased risk of musculoskeletal disorders with chronicity of the disease.29 One study showed that 78% of diabetic patients presenting with trigger fingers had the disease for longer than 5 years.5 Although 14% of the cohort in this study were diabetic, this was not statistically significant compared with the nondiabetic group, nor was multidigit involvement.

Trigger finger is reported to be linked to occupations involving excessive gripping and hand flexion, although no previous studies provide conclusive evidence for this association.4,30 In this study, those with occupations extensively involving repetitive hand use were a minority compared with those with desk jobs, although most in the latter group did use a keyboard or computer, though not necessarily excessively.

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design. These include potential recall bias for long-term outcomes and the absence of validated patient-reported outcome measures for pain and function in the early postoperative period. The lack of a control group (eg, open release and corticosteroid injection) precludes a direct comparative analysis of effectiveness. Furthermore, as a single-surgeon series, the results may reflect a specific level of expertise and may not be generalizable to all surgeons, particularly those early in their learning curve. However, this design also provides consistency in patient selection, surgical technique, and postoperative assessment, which strengthens the internal validity of the findings.

Despite these limitations, the study's major strengths lie in its very large sample size and exceptionally long follow-up period, providing robust data on the long-term safety and durability of percutaneous release. The findings strongly suggest that for patients with a demonstrable trigger finger (Quinnell grade 2 to 4), percutaneous release should be considered as a first-line treatment.
