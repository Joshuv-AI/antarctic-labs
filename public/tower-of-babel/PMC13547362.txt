Multisource attribution and transfer dynamics of PTEs in soil–rice systems in Southwestern China
Authors: Li Meng, Wei Li, Jiahui Duan, Jinhua Luan, Yongchuan Yang, Ruigang Zhang, Lianglin Li, Cheng Fu, Mohuan Ning, Hai Deng, Feng Hu, Zixuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContamination by potentially toxic elements (PTEs) in soil–rice systems poses serious threats to food safety and human health. A total of 225 soil samples and 90 rice samples were collected from typical rice-growing areas in Chongqing, Southwestern China, to investigate PTE sources and transfer in the soil–rice system. The concentrations of 11 PTEs (As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Hg, Mn, Mo, Ni, Pb, Se, and Zn) were determined, and their sources and migration behaviors were investigated using a Positive Matrix Factorization (PMF) model combined with enrichment and transfer indices. Results showed that Zn and Mn dominated in soils, while As, Cd, and Hg exhibited pronounced spatial heterogeneity and signals of exogenous disturbance. PMF identified five pollution sources in soils: geological background (39.5%), agricultural sources (34.8%), light industrial activities & sewage irrigation (17.8%), arsenic-enriched urban disturbance mixed source (5.7%), and industrial emissions & atmospheric deposition (2.2%). Within rice plants, Cd, As, and Se were notably enriched in roots (BCF > 2.6), while grain translocation was limited, indicating a protective barrier. PMF source analysis for rice identified industrial activities & traffic (40.8%), agriculture & irrigation water (34.5%), and coal combustion & waste disposal (24.7%) as major contributors. Transfer coefficient patterns suggested element-specific translocation within rice plants, with Cd showing relatively stronger upward movement, Zn preferentially enriched in stems, and grain accumulation remaining low for most elements. This study provides insights into PTE source-associated patterns and transfer dynamics in soil–rice systems, providing a theoretical basis for paddy soil remediation and agricultural product safety management.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Potentially toxic elements (PTEs) contamination in soils is a global environmental issue that poses ecological risks and food-chain transfer risks, thereby threatening food safety and human health1–3. With accelerating industrialization and agricultural intensification, PTEs can enter farmland systems through industrial emissions, sewage irrigation, and fertilizer misuse, resulting in increasingly prominent contamination at the soil-crop interface2,4–6. Because crop uptake is a major pathway by which soil PTEs enter the human diet, understanding their migration from soil to edible crops is essential for agricultural product safety.

PTEs contamination in rice-growing systems is particularly important because rice is a staple food for a large population and can accumulate elements from paddy soils. A nationwide soil survey in China showed that 19% of agricultural soils exceeded the soil quality standards for PTEs, with key excess elements being metalloid arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), copper (Cu), and nickel (Ni). A study covering 27 European countries found that 28% of soil samples exceeded corresponding threshold values for PTEs7. In Asian rice samples, average concentrations of As, Cd, and Pb were 0.236 ± 0.317 mg/kg, 0.136 ± 0.150 mg/kg, and 0.255 ± 0.013 mg/kg, respectively8. As the staple food for over half the world’s population, rice’s unique flooded cultivation and rhizosphere microenvironment make it more prone to accumulation of Cd, As, and Pb compared to upland crops. This leads to frequent PTE exceedance events in rice globally—such as China’s southern “cadmium rice” incident and the arsenic-contaminated rice crisis in Bangladesh—highlighting the urgent need for pollution management in soil–rice systems9.

Both the external environment and internal physiological factors affect PTE accumulation in rice, including bioavailability, soil physicochemical properties, microbial diversity, and root exudates10,11. For example, soil pH influences the solubility, complexation, and adsorption of PTEs; under flooded conditions during rice growth, the redox potential decreases, increasing the proportion of As(III), which is more toxic and mobile12, Cd may form insoluble sulfides under reducing conditions, decreasing its availability. Numerous studies have explored the mechanisms of PTE transfer from soil to rice13. Generally, PTEs can enter rice roots from the soil solution and rhizosphere porewater, and their subsequent movement within plants is influenced by element bioavailability, root retention, and organ-specific partitioning. The rhizosphere microenvironment, by modulating root pH and redox potential (Eh), impacts the solubility and availability of PTEs14,15. PTEs are transported from roots to aerial parts (stems, leaves, grains) via the xylem and are subsequently redistributed, tending to accumulate in metabolically active tissues (such as leaves), while some (like Cd and As) more readily translocate to grains; the internal distribution varies greatly among different PTEs16,17. Although extensive research has been done on soil and rice PTE accumulation mechanisms, significant gaps remain in understanding the systemic drivers of metal transfer from soil to crops, especially under spatially heterogeneous environmental conditions18. Existing studies have mostly focused on soil geochemistry, often neglecting the interactive effects of natural and anthropogenic factors. Furthermore, regional hydrology and cultivation methods (such as irrigation) may jointly amplify or mitigate metal transfer19.

However, in rice-growing areas of Southwestern China, the multi-source coupled migration of PTEs in soil–rice systems remains insufficiently understood. This region is characterized by complex geological backgrounds, intensive agricultural activities, industrial emissions, irrigation-related inputs, and spatially heterogeneous paddy-field conditions. These factors may jointly affect PTE accumulation in soils and their subsequent transfer into different rice organs, but their combined effects have not been adequately clarified. Therefore, this study focuses on typical hilly rice-growing areas in Chongqing, Southwestern China, integrating elemental surveys, multivariate statistical analysis, and the Positive Matrix Factorization (PMF) model to investigate PTE contamination characteristics, source apportionment, and transfer dynamics in the soil–rice system. By using the bioaccumulation factor (BCF) and transfer factor (TF), the enrichment and transfer capacities of different PTEs among rice organs were quantified. The results provide a basis for understanding multi-source migration processes and for improving paddy soil contamination control and agricultural product safety management.

Materials and methods

Overview of the study area

The research was conducted in a typical rice-growing area in Chongqing, China, covering approximately 30 square kilometers and consisting of three subregions (Fig. 1): Yanwo Town in the northwest (5 km2), Diaoyucheng Subregion in the central part (6 km2), and Longshi Town in the northeast (19 km2). The area is characterized by Jurassic strata (Shaximiao and Suining Formations) and Holocene alluvium, with lithology mainly comprising purplish-red mudstone, siltstone, and interbedded sandstone, forming undulating low hills and shallow valleys. The region has a subtropical monsoon climate, with a multi-year average temperature of 18 °C and annual precipitation of 980 mm, concentrated mainly from May to September. The main soil types are paddy soils (including subtypes such as bleached soil, gravelly soil, and alluvial soil), reflecting intensive rice cultivation. Socioeconomically, the three townships have a population of approximately 178,000, with agriculture dominated by rice, maize, and animal husbandry, which are also potential anthropogenic sources of PTEs. High-energy and high-emission industries, such as cement, nonferrous metal smelting, glass products, and brick manufacturing, are concentrated around the study area, leading to significant emissions of atmospheric particulates. More than 2,600 open burning sites are present, indicating the prevalence of waste incineration issues.

Location of the Study Area and Distribution of Sampling Sites. The base-map boundaries were not modified. Sampling sites were added using field GPS coordinates collected during the 2022–2023 field campaign.

Sample collection and analytical methods

Systematic sample collection was carried out in the study area during 2022 and 2023, including soil and rice. Soil samples comprised both horizontal and vertical profiles: for horizontal profiles, samples were collected at 40 m intervals over a total length of 9 km, yielding 225 surface soil samples (0–20 cm depth) across Yanwo Town, Longshi Town, and Diaoyucheng Subregion; for vertical profiles, samples were collected by stratified trenching to the bedrock layer, with a total of four profiles set up. A total of 90 rice samples were collected from representative paddy fields during the rice maturity stage in 2022 and 2023. Each rice sample was separated into grain, root, stem, and leaf for subsequent analysis. Rhizosphere soil samples were collected from the corresponding fields during the same period at a depth of 0–20 cm. Field sampling was conducted with permission from the relevant local authorities and landowners/farmers. The field study did not involve protected species or protected areas, and all sampling activities complied with applicable local and institutional guidelines and regulations.

All samples followed standardized processing procedures: soil samples were air-dried, with gravel and organic debris removed, then ground to 200 mesh (0.074 mm) for total analysis; rice samples were washed with deionized water, oven-dried, and then ground by organ to pass through a 40-mesh sieve.

Total concentrations of As, Cr, Cu, Mn, Ni, Pb, Se, and Zn in soil were determined by X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF, S8 Tiger); trace elements such as Cd and Mo were analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, NexION 350X); Hg was determined by atomic fluorescence spectrometry (AFS-9750). Soil pH was measured using a glass electrode method with an FE-28 pH meter in a soil:water suspension at a ratio of 1:2.5 (w/v).

Multivariate statistical analysis

To preliminarily explore co-occurrence patterns and potential source-related signals of PTEs in rice, correlation analysis and principal component analysis (PCA) were used for multidimensional data mining20. Pearson correlation analysis was first employed to evaluate the co-occurrence relationships of elements such as As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Hg, Ni, Se, and Zn, identifying potential homologies or migration associations. Based on standardized concentration datasets, PCA was then conducted; the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were used to confirm data suitability, and principal components with eigenvalues > 1 were extracted. For rice samples, correlation analysis and PCA were used to identify elemental covariation patterns rather than to provide direct evidence of independent source contributions, because PTEs concentrations in rice are also affected by soil bioavailability, root uptake, and organ-specific partitioning.

Positive matrix factorization (PMF) model

To quantify source contribution rates, the US EPA PMF 5.0 model was employed for positive matrix factorization. The model calculates errors for each chemical component using weights, and then applies least squares to determine the main sources and their contribution rates22,23. Compared to other source apportionment methods, PMF does not require measuring source profiles, constrains factor loadings to non-negative values, can utilize data standard deviations for optimization, and is robust to missing or imprecise data24.

Results and discussion

Content characteristics and source analysis of PTEs in soil

PTEs content levels in soil

Descriptive statistics of PTEs concentrations in the study area soils were analyzed. As shown in Table 1 and Fig. 2, Mn (mean 503.01 mg/kg) and Zn (mean 85.22 mg/kg) were markedly higher than other elements, indicating their dominance in the soils of the region. Hg (mean 0.08 mg/kg) and Se (mean 0.20 mg/kg) had the lowest concentrations. The range for Mn was particularly wide (max 912.00 mg/kg, min 281.00 mg/kg, range 631 mg/kg), suggesting substantial spatial heterogeneity. Among the analyzed elements, As, Hg, and Cd showed high coefficients of variation, indicating strong spatial heterogeneity and likely significant anthropogenic disturbances such as fertilization, industrial emissions, or traffic25,26. In contrast, Cr showed a more spatially coherent distribution and was mainly associated with parent material, whereas Mo appeared to be more strongly controlled by parent material and soil properties27,28. The distributions of As, Hg, and Cd exhibited dense outliers and significant skewness, suggesting possible point-source contamination. By comparison, Cr and Pb distributions showed logarithmic symmetry, with standard deviations of 5.49 and 2.67, respectively, consistent with natural background homogenization. Notably, some samples of As and Cd approached the limit values of China’s Soil Environmental Quality Standards (GB 15618-2018), warranting further environmental risk attention.

PMF model source component profiles and source contributions.

In the PMF model, Factor 1 (F1) had the highest contributions from As (47.07%) and Mn (46.45%), both of which are typical geological background elements. These mainly originate from the weathering of parent bedrock and the dissolution of natural minerals. In the study area, the geological background comprises Middle–Upper Jurassic shale, carbonate rock, and interbedded sandstone, especially the Furong and Shaximiao formations, which are rich in arsenic-bearing minerals such as pyrite and arsenopyrite. These are prone to weathering and dissolution under redox conditions29,30, releasing Mn2+, As5+, and As3+ into the soil environment. In addition, the well-developed soil profiles in this region, with weathering crusts typically 50–150 cm thick, may facilitate the release and vertical redistribution of these elements during bedrock weathering. Some released elements can be retained or re-immobilized in the surface soil through adsorption onto clay minerals, Fe/Mn oxides, and organic matter, resulting in surface enrichment31. Therefore, F1 can be interpreted as the geological background source controlled by natural bedrock weathering.

Factor 2 (F2) was characterized by high contributions from Cu (35.53%), Ni (35.06%), Mn (35.03%), Zn (34.96%), and Hg (31.23%), indicating a composite agricultural source. Cu and Zn are typical metal additives in livestock manure, compound fertilizers, and micronutrient fertilizers, while Ni mainly derives from phosphate fertilizers and certain pesticide additives32. The moderate contribution of Hg (31.23%) is likely related to historical use of mercury-containing pesticides, such as mercuric chloride disinfectants. As an agricultural area, Hechuan commonly applies fertilizers and manure to rice paddies, while irrigation water may also introduce dissolved PTEs. In particular, changes in soil pH caused by phosphate fertilizers promote the activation and migration of metals33. Therefore, F2 is interpreted as an agricultural pollution source characterized by Cu, Ni, Mn, Zn, and Hg.

Factor 3 (F3) was dominated by Hg (64.38%), followed by Cd (18.81%), Zn (6.54%), and Cu (2.68%), showing a strong industrial source signature, especially led by Hg. Mercury is a typical atmospheric mobile PTEs, with main sources including coal-fired power plant emissions, mercury-related industries (such as trichloroethane and PVC plastics), and historical mercury mining34. Hg can be transported long distances through the atmosphere and deposited into the soil, where it readily binds with organic matter and clay particles, remaining in the cultivated layer for extended periods. The moderate contribution of Cd suggests the influence of light industrial pollution or fertilizer inputs. Cd is common in phosphate fertilizers (extracted from phosphate rock) and metallurgical emissions, and is highly enriched in atmospheric particulate deposition35. Studies indicate that the enrichment factor (EF) for Cd in industrial areas is generally > 10, qualifying it as a significant pollutant. Downwind of industrial zones in Hechuan (such as electronics and machinery manufacturing), atmospheric particulates may deposit Hg and Cd into paddy topsoil via wind transport and rainfall leaching. Both Hg and Cd exhibit high mobility and biotoxicity, and their long-term accumulation presents a latent ecological risk to farmland systems. Thus, F3 is clearly attributed to industrial emissions and atmospheric deposition sources.

Factor 4 (F4) was characterized by a distinct combination of As (48.01%), Cu (29.08%), Ni (25.06%), and Zn (23.19%). Unlike F1, which reflects the geological background with co-dominance of As and Mn, F4 exhibited strong As enrichment but nearly no Mn contribution, indicating that it cannot be simply attributed to natural bedrock weathering. Meanwhile, the very low contributions of Hg (0.14%) and Cd (6.96%) suggest that this factor is also different from the industrial atmospheric deposition source (F3) and the Cd-dominated light industrial/sewage irrigation source (F5). Therefore, F4 is more reasonably interpreted as an arsenic-enriched urban disturbance mixed source. In this factor, Cu, Ni, and Zn are likely associated with road dust, vehicle-related wear36, and diffuse urban surface inputs, whereas the elevated As may reflect the remobilization and redistribution of arsenic from arsenic-rich parent material or historically accumulated topsoil under strong human disturbance, such as road construction, surface runoff, and soil resuspension37. Under the complex land-use setting of the study area, this factor likely represents the superimposed effect of urban disturbance and geogenic arsenic enrichment rather than a single traffic source.

Factor 5 (F5) was dominated by Cd (53.32%), followed by Zn (22.57%), Ni (19.46%), and Cu (16.79%). The absolute predominance of Cd indicates a strong association with anthropogenic input sources, particularly light industrial emissions, domestic sewage irrigation, and fertilizer accumulation. Cd is well-known for its toxicity and high mobility, commonly found in phosphate fertilizers and various industrial products such as batteries, electroplating, and pigments38. Some farmland in the study area is located on the urban fringe, where irrigation with domestic or light industrial wastewater has historically been prevalent, particularly from sources such as phosphate fertilizer plants, metal finishing plants, and textile dyeing plants, where wastewater contains high concentrations of Cd, Zn, and Cu. These can easily enter soil via irrigation and bind to clay or organic matter particles39. The moderate contributions of Zn and Cu indicate complex pollution composition, potentially including mixed sources such as domestic waste dumps and small rural factories. The significant contribution of Ni further suggests potential pollution from metallurgy or hardware processing industries. Therefore, F5 is interpreted as a Cd-dominated light industrial activity and sewage irrigation pollution source.

Enrichment of PTEs in rice

As shown in Fig. 4, the overall concentration characteristics of PTEs in the rice sample dataset varied significantly among elements, providing a general overview of element-level accumulation in rice samples. Zn exhibited the highest concentration (16.68 μg/g), followed by Cu at 2.87 μg/g. The concentrations of Ni, Cr, and Cd ranged between 0.12 and 0.49 μg/g. Arsenic (As) and Se had concentrations of 0.035 μg/g and 0.018 μg/g, respectively, while Hg had the lowest concentration (0.003 μg/g). The overall order of PTEs concentrations in the rice sample dataset was: Zn > Cu > Ni > Cr > Cd > As > Se > Hg. Lead (Pb) was below the detection limit in the rice samples.

Overall concentration characteristics of PTEs in rice samples.

To further distinguish the organ-specific distribution underlying the overall pattern shown in Fig. 4, the concentrations of nine PTEs in roots, stems, leaves, and grains were analyzed separately (Fig. 5). The results showed that, for all PTEs, their concentrations in grains were significantly lower than in vegetative organs. For example, Cr concentrations were 23.17 μg/g in roots, 5.64 μg/g in stems, and 15.67 μg/g in leaves, while in grains it was only 0.019 μg/g—a difference of three orders of magnitude. This is mainly attributed to the physical barrier provided by the rice hull and the dilution effect of the endosperm, which restrict the translocation of PTEs to the edible parts9.

Organ-specific enrichment characteristics of PTEs in rice.

Moreover, most PTEs (such as Cr, Ni, and As) were most highly enriched in roots, indicating that the root system acts as a primary site for uptake and immobilization of PTEs40. In contrast, Zn was most concentrated in the stem (61.31 μg/g, significantly higher than the root concentration of 47.37 μg/g), likely due to Zn’s role as an essential element involved preferentially in stem physiological metabolism. Notably, the concentrations of Cu, Zn, and Cd in stems exceeded those in leaves, while other elements (such as As and Hg) tended to migrate more readily to the leaves. Such differentiation may be related to element-specific chemical properties, bioavailability, root retention, and organ-level partitioning within rice plants41,42.

Source apportionment of PTEs in rice

To explore potential source-associated patterns of PTEs in rice, correlation analysis and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed for eight PTEs (Cr, Ni, Cu, Zn, As, Se, Cd, Hg) in rice (Figs. 6 and 7). Strong positive correlations were observed between Ni and Cd (r = 0.89), Ni and Cu (r = 0.80), and Cu and Cd (r = 0.80). Moderate positive correlations were found between Cu and Zn (r = 0.79), Ni and Zn (r = 0.67), Se and Cd (r = 0.70), and Se and Ni (r = 0.63). Moderate negative correlations were identified for As with Cu (r = −0.57), As with Ni (r = −0.47), As with Zn (r = −0.44), and As with Cd (r = −0.45). In contrast, Hg and Cr exhibited weak correlations with the other elements.

Correlations of PTEs concentrations in rice.

Loadings of PTEs concentrations in rice.

Principal component analysis explained the primary sources of variation in rice PTEs concentrations. PC1 accounted for 51% of the total variance, with high loadings for Ni (0.46), Cu (0.46), Zn (0.39), and Cd (0.45). PC2 explained 15.1% of the variance, with As (0.5) and Se (0.42) as the principal contributors, while PC3 was dominated by Cr (0.75) and Hg (0.63).

The sources of PTEs in rice may differ considerably. According to the results of the correlation and principal component analyses, certain PTEs in rice—such as Ni, Cd, Cu, and Zn—displayed significant positive correlations, suggesting that these elements may share similar sources or migration pathways. The elements with high loadings on PC1 (Ni, Cu, Cd, and Zn) likely originate from agricultural activities, such as the application of pesticides, fertilizers, or other agricultural products containing metal additives. After application, these substances may be absorbed by rice through soil adsorption or water-mediated migration43. The characteristics of PC2 may be associated with irrigation water sources or the geological background, especially in soils or groundwater enriched in arsenic and selenium, which can readily lead to their accumulation in rice. Furthermore, the high loadings of Cr and Hg in PC3, along with their weak correlations with other elements, suggest independent sources such as industrial atmospheric deposition, domestic sewage irrigation, or localized soil enrichment. Therefore, the sources of PTEs in rice are likely to be complex and multifaceted, involving agricultural inputs and irrigation water, as well as regional soil background and environmental pollutant inputs. It should be noted that PCA mainly reflects covariance patterns among elements, whereas PMF resolves mixed-source profiles and relative source contributions; therefore, PCA components and PMF factors are not expected to correspond one-to-one. To further support the interpretation of source-associated PTEs profiles in rice, PMF analysis was conducted on PTEs concentrations in rice.

For PMF model calculations, the concentration units were set to mg/kg. After evaluation, Ni, Cu, Zn, As, Cd, and Hg were selected for the PMF analysis in this study. The model converged after 200 runs, and to determine the optimal number of factors, the software selected the seventh optimal result, ultimately identifying three pollution factors. Under this model, R2 values ranged from 0.53 to 0.96, indicating a good correlation between predicted and observed concentrations. PMF model source component profiles and source contributions.

Factor 1 (F1) primarily contributed to the accumulation of Cu and Zn in rice, accounting for 47.95% and 40.51%, respectively. Cu and Zn are not source-specific elements and may be introduced into paddy systems through both agricultural practices and non-agricultural anthropogenic inputs. Agricultural practices, particularly the long-term application of livestock manure, organic fertilizers, and compound fertilizers, can contribute Cu and Zn to agricultural soils44. However, in this study, the PMF profile of F1, together with the industrial and traffic-related activities around the sampling area, suggests that this factor more likely reflects a mixed industrial activity and traffic-related source rather than a purely agricultural source. Metal processing, smelting, electronics manufacturing, vehicle exhaust emissions, and road dust may release Cu- and Zn-bearing particles into the surrounding environment, which can subsequently enter paddy fields through atmospheric deposition, surface runoff, or irrigation return flow. Therefore, F1 was interpreted as a mixed industrial activity and traffic-related source.

Factor 2 (F2) was mainly characterized by high contributions from Cd (85.78%) and Ni (51.98%). These elements are widely present as a result of coal combustion, phosphate fertilizer application, and municipal solid waste incineration and residue disposal. Coal naturally contains both metals, which are emitted with fly ash during combustion and dispersed into surrounding areas. Impurities of Cd in phosphate and some compound fertilizers—since many phosphate rocks contain high Cd concentrations—can enter farmland directly if fertilizers are not adequately purified. Furthermore, fly ash and bottom ash from waste incineration, if randomly dumped or used for landfilling in farmlands, may release PTEs into the soil45. Under slightly acidic and reducing soil conditions, Cd can migrate to the root zone and be taken up by rice roots, then be translocated to the grain. Although the bioavailability of Ni is relatively lower, long-term or cumulative inputs can also lead to its accumulation in rice plants, affecting rice quality and food safety. In summary, F2 can be identified as a coal combustion & waste disposal source.

Factor 3 (F3) was overwhelmingly dominated by arsenic (As, 72.89%) and mercury (Hg, 74.63%). Unlike the previous two factors, this source is more closely associated with agricultural inputs, paddy field water management systems, and disturbance of historically accumulated sediments, exhibiting the characteristics of typical agricultural non-point source pollution, and is commonly found in regions with complex irrigation systems or substandard water sources. Firstly, As and Hg in agriculture mainly derive from the long-term use of certain organic pesticides and fungicides. Although the use of organoarsenic insecticides and organomercury grain dressings for rice pest control has been banned for many years, these compounds are extremely stable in soil and can still persist and slowly release, forming historical legacy pollution46. In addition, some low-cost fertilizers may also contain trace arsenic impurities that can accumulate in the fields after application. Under paddy field conditions, both As and Hg exhibit high bioavailability. Rice cultivation typically occurs in strongly reducing, low-oxygen environments, favoring the presence of As(III), which is much more toxic and more readily absorbed by rice than As(V)47,48. Under similar conditions, mercury is easily methylated to form methylmercury, which can rapidly accumulate in rice grains and even pose neurological health risks to consumers. In summary, F3 can be interpreted as an agricultural and irrigation water pollution source.

Migration patterns of PTEs in the soil–rice system

PTEs migration in the soil–rice system showed clear differences among rice organs. As shown in Table 4, roots were the main accumulation site for several elements, especially Cd, As, and Se, with BCF values of 5.19, 3.87, and 2.60, respectively. In contrast, BCF values in grains were much lower. For example, the BCF of Cd decreased from 5.19 in roots to 0.68 in grains, while that of As decreased from 3.87 to 0.02. Cr showed a similar trend, with a grain BCF of only 0.001. These results suggest that several PTEs were readily retained in roots after uptake, whereas their transfer to grains was limited.

Different elements showed different redistribution patterns within the plant. Zn had the highest BCF in the stem (0.79), which was higher than that in roots (0.58) and leaves (0.21). This is consistent with the concentration pattern shown in Fig. 5, where Zn reached 61.31 μg/g in stems and 47.37 μg/g in roots. Cd also showed relatively high accumulation in stems (BCF = 4.62), indicating that part of the absorbed Cd could still be transported upward after root uptake. By comparison, leaves showed relatively higher enrichment than stems for As, Cr, Hg, and Se, suggesting that these elements were more likely to be retained in leaves during further transport within the plant.

The transfer factor results in Fig. 9 further reflect these differences in internal translocation. Stem-to-leaf transfer was relatively stronger for As and Cr, whereas Cd and Zn showed comparatively stronger root-to-stem transfer. Together with the generally low BCF values in grains, these results indicate that PTEs transport in rice was selective rather than uniform, and that different elements followed different migration pathways after entering the plant. Overall, roots were the main accumulation site for Cd and As, stems played an important role in the redistribution of Zn and part of Cd, and leaves showed relatively stronger accumulation of As, Cr, Hg, and Se. Grain remained the least enriched organ for most elements, indicating that the movement of PTEs into edible tissues was restricted during internal transport.

Conclusions

This study systematically revealed the concentration characteristics, source attribution, and migration behaviors of PTEs in the soil–rice system of typical rice-growing areas in Chongqing. Using the PMF model, multivariate statistical analysis, and bioaccumulation parameters, the following main conclusions were drawn:

PTEs pollution in soils exhibits characteristics of mixed sources. Arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), and mercury (Hg) showed strong spatial variability and potential ecological risk. The PMF model identified five major sources: geological background (39.5%), agricultural pollution sources (34.8%), light industrial activities & sewage irrigation (17.8%), urban disturbance mixed source with As enrichment (5.7%), and industrial emissions & atmospheric deposition (2.2%).

PTEs accumulation in rice exhibits organ specificity. The roots showed significant enrichment of toxic metals such as Cd, As, and Se (BCF > 2.6), whereas the accumulation in grains was limited due to the hull barrier and internal dilution mechanisms, suggesting restricted translocation of PTEs into grains. Zn was most enriched in the stem, likely related to its role as a nutrient element.

Sources of metal contamination in rice vary significantly. PMF source apportionment indicated that the main sources of PTEs in rice included: industrial activity & traffic sources (40.8%), agricultural & irrigation water pollution sources (34.5%), and coal combustion & waste disposal sources (24.7%).
