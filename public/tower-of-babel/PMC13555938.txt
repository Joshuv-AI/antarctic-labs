Evaluating microbial network inference methods: moving beyond synthetic data with reproducibility-driven benchmarks
Authors: Zahra Ghaeli, Rosa Aghdam, Changiz Eslahchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMicrobial network inference is an essential approach for revealing complex interactions within microbial communities. However, the lack of experimentally validated gold standards presents a significant obstacle in evaluating the biological accuracy of inferred networks. This study delivers a comprehensive comparative assessment of six widely used microbial network inference algorithms on four diverse real-world microbiome datasets alongside computationally generated samples, including synthetic, noisy, and bootstrap-derived variants. Our evaluation framework extends beyond conventional synthetic benchmarking by emphasizing reproducibility-focused assessments grounded in biologically realistic perturbations.ResultsOur analysis reveals that bootstrap resampling and low-level noisy datasets (≤ 10% perturbation) effectively preserve the key statistical properties of real microbiome data, serving as reliable proxies for assessing algorithmic consistency. Conversely, synthetic datasets generated via the widely used SPIEC-EASI method exhibit substantial divergence from real data. Notably, several algorithms fail to distinguish between structured and random networks, highlighting a lack of structural sensitivity and the limitations of overreliance on synthetic benchmarks.ConclusionsThis study provides critical insights for the microbiome research community, emphasizing the need for more reliable and broadly applicable approaches to network evaluation. We propose a benchmarking framework that prioritizes real-data-derived perturbations and mandates rigorous statistical validation of synthetic datasets. Our findings highlight the importance of robustness and reproducibility analyses as complementary evaluation criteria for microbial network inference methods when validated biological ground truth is unavailable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12859-026-06510-3.

Introduction

The term microbiota refers to a community of microorganisms (including bacteria, archaea, protists, fungi, and viruses) that coexist within a shared environment. In contrast, the term microbiome includes not only these microorganisms but also their genetic material and the environmental context in which they reside [1]. With advances in sequencing technologies, comprehensive human microbiome datasets have become available, enabling extensive research into the microbiome’s role in human health and disease [2–4].

The microbiome plays a critical role in the development, progression, and treatment of various diseases, including cancer [5, 6]. Dysbiosis (an imbalance in the microbial community) has been linked to colorectal, breast, lung, and prostate cancers [7], often through its impact on metabolic, inflammatory, and immunological pathways. The gut microbiome is also implicated in neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, where it influences both metabolite production and genetic factors involved in disease progression [8]. Moreover, a bidirectional relationship has been observed between the gut microbiome and schizophrenia: not only does microbial composition influence the disorder’s onset and progression, but the disease itself alters the microbiome structure [9].

Microbial interactions are often classified broadly as positive, neutral, or negative, reflecting patterns such as co-occurrence, independence, or mutual exclusion among operational taxonomic units (OTUs) [10].

In microbiome studies, 16S rRNA gene sequencing and whole-genome sequencing are commonly used to group sequences sharing more than 97% similarity into OTUs. The resulting data are typically structured as abundance matrices, where rows represent samples and columns represent OTUs. These matrices often contain many zeros (due either to true biological absence or technical limitations such as insufficient sequencing depth) making interpretation difficult [11, 12].

Furthermore, due to variability in sequencing depth, microbiome data are inherently compositional that is, they represent relative rather than absolute abundances. This constant-sum constraint introduces dependencies among taxa, potentially leading to spurious correlations, particularly negative ones. To address these issues, log-ratio transformations (such as the centered log-ratio (CLR) method) are commonly employed, as they allow more valid statistical analyses by accounting for the compositional nature of the data [13, 14].

Inferring microbial interaction networks is a key task in microbiome research, as these networks provide insights into how different OTUs coexist, compete, and influence host health [10, 15, 16]. In such networks, nodes represent OTUs and edges denote potential biological associations, such as co-occurrence, competition, or mutual exclusion. These interactions are often inferred from co-occurrence patterns and occasionally supported by experimental validation [17]. Microbial networks have practical applications in identifying keystone OTUs, understanding community resilience, and guiding microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategies [18].

One of the key challenges in microbial network inference is the lack of a true biological ground truth for most real microbial communities, which limits the assessment of absolute accuracy and biological relevance of inferred networks [19, 20]. While a few datasets with experimentally validated interactions exist, such as Arabidopsis root communities [21] or known planktonic interaction networks [22], these benchmarks are rare and cover only a limited subset of natural microbial interactions. Although experimentally validated ground-truth microbial networks exist for controlled time-series systems, such as the Venturelli synthetic gut community, these datasets are specifically designed for fitting generalized Lotka–Volterra (gLV) dynamical models [23]. Given their longitudinal nature and experimental design (e.g., serial transfers), they are not directly comparable to the static cross-sectional OTU-level data used in our study. As a result, many studies rely on synthetic datasets generated from artificial network topologies (e.g., cluster, band, or scale-free networks) to benchmark the accuracy of inference algorithms [14, 24–27]. However, these artificial benchmarks often fail to capture the ecological complexity, nonlinearity, and context dependency of real microbial ecosystems, where interactions may be influenced by metabolic dependencies, environmental conditions, ecosystem-level processes, and microbial functional roles [10, 17, 28].

Accordingly, several previous studies have evaluated inference accuracy using synthetic or simulated data with known underlying networks. For example, Weiss et al. assessed the sensitivity and precision of correlation-based methods under sampling noise and rarefaction, and Hirano and Takemoto evaluated the ability of co-occurrence methods to recover interaction patterns generated by ecological simulation models [29, 30]. While such studies provide important insights into algorithmic accuracy under controlled conditions, their conclusions are inherently tied to the assumptions and simplifications of the underlying synthetic models.

In contrast, the interpretation of inferred networks from real microbiome data remains challenging, as network structure can be strongly influenced by methodological choices, environmental heterogeneity, and data perturbations [31]. Building on these observations, and in line with recent discussions on benchmarking challenges [19], our study focuses on evaluating the reproducibility and robustness of inferred microbial networks. We do this by comparing networks obtained from real data with those inferred from controlled noisy perturbations and bootstrap resampling, rather than assessing absolute accuracy against an assumed ground truth.

To reconstruct these networks from sequencing data, various computational algorithms have been developed, typically categorized into correlation-based and conditional dependence-based approaches. Early correlation-based methods include Pearson and Spearman correlations, followed by SparCC (Sparse Correlations for Compositional data) [32], which estimates linear Pearson correlations between log-transformed components using an approximation based on sparsity and log-ratio variances, with iterative exclusion of strong correlations for improved accuracy. This line of work has led to more advanced methods such as CCLasso (Correlation inference for Compositional data through Lasso) [33] and REBACCA (regularized estimation of the basis covariance based on compositional data) [34], which further refine network inference under compositional constraints.

Conditional dependence-based methods aim to capture direct associations between OTUs by accounting for indirect interactions. Prominent examples include SPIEC-EASI (SParse InversE Covariance Estimation for Ecological Association Inference) [14], which combines centered log-ratio transformation to address compositionality with sparse inverse covariance estimation using neighborhood selection or graphical lasso, enabling inference of conditional independence networks; and SPRING (Semi-Parametric Rank-based Inference in Graphical models) [24], which employs a semi-parametric rank-based estimator for correlations and partial correlations, handling excess zeros and extending to compositional data via modified clr transformation. More recent tools include CoNet [35] which integrates multiple inference approaches in an ensemble method to generate consensus association networks; meta-network [36], which employs meta-analysis across multiple datasets for optimized species-species co-occurrence networks; HARMONIES (Hybrid Approach foR MicrobiOme Network Inferences via Exploiting Sparsity) [37], a hybrid framework that first normalizes complex microbiome count data using a Bayesian ZINB model, and then infers a sparse, direct association network among taxa via a Gaussian Graphical Model with Graphical Lasso; FLASHWeave [25], a heuristic-optimized probabilistic graphical model that rapidly infers sparse, direct microbial interaction networks from large-scale heterogeneous sequencing data; and MicroNet-MIMRF (Microbial Network based on Mutual Information and Markov Random Fields) [26], which combines mutual information with Markov random fields for edge detection.

In this paper, we address the critical challenge of evaluating microbial network inference algorithms by conducting an extensive comparison of six widely adopted methods, with a central focus on assessing their reproducibility: gCoda [27], a graphical model tailored for compositional data based on a logistic normal distribution and sparse inverse covariance estimation via majorization-minimization; OIPCQ (Order Independent PC-based algorithm using Conditional Quantification), initially developed for gene regulatory networks [38, 39], which extracts direct dependencies between microorganisms based on conditional mutual information; S-E(glasso) (SPIEC-EASI with Graphical Lasso) [14], part of the SPIEC-EASI framework; S-E(mb) (SPIEC-EASI with Meinshausen-Bühlmann neighborhood selection) [14]; SPRING (Semi-Parametric Rank-based Inference in Graphical models) [24] and SparCC (Sparse Correlations for Compositional data) [32], a correlation-based method that infers associations by correcting for the compositional nature of the data using log-ratio transformations. These six methods were chosen for their widespread adoption in microbiome studies, coverage of key approaches (correlation-based, graphical models, and conditional mutual information), and ability to handle compositionality and sparsity, allowing a comprehensive reproducibility assessment.

An additional practical challenge in benchmarking microbial network inference methods is the comparability of inferred networks across datasets and inference runs. Some algorithms adaptively modify the set of included taxa based on the input data, for example by excluding nodes with weak or unstable signals. While such behavior can be advantageous for specific modeling goals, it complicates reproducibility analyses that rely on direct comparisons to a reference network inferred from the original data. In particular, when inferred networks do not retain a consistent set of nodes across the real dataset and its noisy or bootstrap-derived variants, edge-wise comparisons become ambiguous. To ensure fair and interpretable reproducibility assessment, we therefore focus on methods that produce networks with a consistent node set across datasets.

We evaluate these algorithms on four real-world microbiome datasets to rigorously test their reproducibility. Importantly, unlike traditional evaluations that primarily utilize synthetic data generated from predefined, idealized network topologies, our approach includes more statistically grounded and reproducibility-oriented benchmarks, such as bootstrap sampling and controlled noisy perturbations derived directly from real datasets.

Our evaluation framework provides deeper insight into the reliability of network inference methods by systematically comparing performance across noisy and bootstrap-generated datasets derived from real data, together with limited synthetic benchmarks. We highlight limitations of traditional synthetic data benchmarks, particularly in reflecting the statistical complexity and structural properties of real microbiome datasets. Our results support the use of reproducibility-focused benchmarks based on real-data-derived perturbations as complementary tools for assessing methodological robustness. This study provides a practical framework for future methodological evaluations, emphasizing the importance of realistic data scenarios for supporting a cautious and robust assessment of method performance.

Methods

Dataset sources

We selected four publicly available, preprocessed microbiome datasets that are commonly used for evaluating microbial interaction network inference methods. These datasets were chosen based on the following criteria: (i) public accessibility to ensure reproducibility; (ii) prior filtering in their original studies to remove low-prevalence and low-abundance OTUs, yielding a moderate number of taxa (p 90-138) relative to samples (n ≈ 195-296), which is essential for reliable sparse network inference in high-dimensional settings where raw microbiome data typically exhibit p ≫ n; (iii) representation of diverse body sites (gut and vaginal) to demonstrate method generalizability; and (iv) prior use as benchmarks in related studies.

We utilized four publicly available microbiome datasets in this study. The first two, amgut1 and amgut2, are provided in the SpiecEasi R package and correspond to two phases of the American Gut Project (AGP), a large-scale citizen science initiative studying the human gut microbiome. Both datasets are based on stool samples: amgut1 includes 289 samples and 127 OTUs, while amgut2 contains 296 samples and 138 OTUs [14]. The third dataset, denoted as GUT, was collected to examine the relationship between gut microbiome composition and susceptibility to Plasmodium falciparum infection [40]. It includes 195 stool samples and 128 OTUs [41]. The fourth dataset, referred to as MOMS-PI, originates from the NIH-funded Multi-Omic Microbiome Study: Pregnancy Initiative (MOMS-PI), which investigates the role of the microbiome in maternal and infant health. The dataset used in this study consists of 225 vaginal samples and 90 OTUs [42]. Data generation methods

To evaluate microbial network inference algorithms under varied conditions, we generated three types of datasets derived from the real data: synthetic data, noisy data, and bootstrap data.

Synthetic data : The SpiecEasi (SE) and SPRING (SP) methods generates synthetic data that mimics real data through the NORmal To Anything (NORTA) algorithm [43]. Both methods simulate count-like data by taking as input a predefined network topology and a real dataset, and producing synthetic samples that reflect the structure of the input network. However, the methods differ in how they handle the marginal distributions of the data:SPIEC-EASI (SE): SE uses parametric models (e.g., Negative Binomial (NB) or Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ziNB)) to assign marginal distributions to the synthetic data. These models are fitted to real data, but they may not always perfectly capture the distributional characteristics of the microbiome data, such as overdispersion, skewness, and zero-inflation.SPRING (SP): SP uses the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) of the real data to derive the marginal distributions. This approach does not impose any parametric assumption on the data and instead directly uses the distribution of the real data, making the synthetic data more faithful to the true statistical properties of the original data, including sparsity, overdispersion, and zero-inflation.We used a total of 11 input network topologies for data generation. These included three predefined structures scale-free, banded, and clustered graphs (from SpiecEasi R package) and two random networks generated using the Erdos-Renyi function from the igraph R package [44] (For evaluating the ability of algorithms to detect random structures), as well as six networks inferred from real data using the gCoda, OIPCQ, S-E(glasso), S-E(mb), SPRING, and SparCC algorithms. For each of the 11 topologies, we generated synthetic datasets using both the SPIEC-EASI (SE) and SPRING (SP) methods, resulting in a total of 22 synthetic datasets for each real dataset (11 topologies × 2 methods).

SPIEC-EASI (SE): SE uses parametric models (e.g., Negative Binomial (NB) or Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ziNB)) to assign marginal distributions to the synthetic data. These models are fitted to real data, but they may not always perfectly capture the distributional characteristics of the microbiome data, such as overdispersion, skewness, and zero-inflation.

SPIEC-EASI (SE): SE uses parametric models (e.g., Negative Binomial (NB) or Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ziNB)) to assign marginal distributions to the synthetic data. These models are fitted to real data, but they may not always perfectly capture the distributional characteristics of the microbiome data, such as overdispersion, skewness, and zero-inflation.

SPRING (SP): SP uses the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) of the real data to derive the marginal distributions. This approach does not impose any parametric assumption on the data and instead directly uses the distribution of the real data, making the synthetic data more faithful to the true statistical properties of the original data, including sparsity, overdispersion, and zero-inflation.

SPRING (SP): SP uses the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) of the real data to derive the marginal distributions. This approach does not impose any parametric assumption on the data and instead directly uses the distribution of the real data, making the synthetic data more faithful to the true statistical properties of the original data, including sparsity, overdispersion, and zero-inflation.

Noisy data (Dirichlet-Multinomial): To evaluate the sensitivity of network inference methods to perturbations while preserving compositional constraints and zero inflation, we generated noisy datasets using a Dirichlet-multinomial process. For each sample, the original library size was preserved, and counts were resampled across OTUs according to a Dirichlet-multinomial distribution with parameters derived from the original data. For a specified noise level (5%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, or 50%), a corresponding fraction of entries in each column were modified. This procedure introduces realistic variation in both abundance magnitudes and presence-absence patterns while maintaining the overall compositional structure.

To enable a more comprehensive assessment, we additionally generated noisy datasets based on Poisson and truncated normal perturbations (see Supplementary Methods: Noise Generation).

Bootstrap data: To evaluate the reproducibility of network inference in the presence of sample-level variability, we generated bootstrap datasets by resampling complete samples (i.e., rows) from each original dataset with replacement. Each bootstrap dataset had the same number of rows as the corresponding original dataset; for example, each bootstrap dataset generated from amgut1 consisted of 289 resampled rows. Because resampling was performed with replacement, these rows were drawn from the original samples, so some samples could appear multiple times, whereas others might not appear in a given bootstrap dataset. This procedure captures variability induced by heterogeneous sample compositions and allows us to assess the stability of inferred networks under realistic resampling fluctuations.

Thus, for each real dataset, we generated a total of 41 distinct dataset types across three categories: synthetic, noisy, and bootstrap. Specifically, this included 22 synthetic datasets generated from 11 network topologies using two data-generation methods, 18 noisy datasets spanning six noise levels across three noise models (Dirichlet-multinomial, Poisson, and truncated normal), and one bootstrap dataset category. To assess robustness, each synthetic and noisy dataset type was generated 100 times. For the bootstrap analysis, we generated 250 bootstrap datasets for each real dataset. This fixed number was chosen to provide consistent resampling across datasets while keeping the computational burden manageable, as each bootstrap replicate required reconstructing microbial networks using multiple inference algorithms followed by downstream network comparisons.

Comparing generated data with real data

It is crucial that the generated datasets, synthetic, noisy, and bootstrap, resemble the real microbiome data in meaningful ways, as this similarity ensures that downstream evaluations of network inference methods are both realistic and reliable. To assess the faithfulness of the generated data, we conducted three types of comparisons: diversity analysis, matrix entry similarity, and distributional alignment.

Diversity analysis: We computed three alpha diversity indices for each sample in both the real and generated datasets: Richness, the Shannon index, and the inverse Simpson index [45]. These metrics capture different aspects of microbial community diversity, including species richness and evenness. The Richness refers to the number of species present in a sample. However, examining diversity indices alone is not sufficiently precise, and one cannot conclude that two datasets lack a significant difference solely based on the absence of a statistically significant difference in their indices. It is possible that synthetic data may not show a statistically significant difference in a particular index, yet in reality, they may differ substantially due to variations in the matrix entries, thus masking the absence of a significant difference. In particular, microbiome abundance matrices are highly zero-inflated, and two datasets may exhibit similar diversity indices while differing substantially in the spatial distribution of zero and non-zero entries across samples and taxa. Therefore, since we are comparing real data with synthetic, noisy, and bootstrap data (not healthy versus diseased data) it is important that the results of diversity indices be interpreted in a way that considers the degree of agreement between the data entries and the real data.

Matrix entry similarity (F-score): In this analysis, we focus on the agreement between the presence and absence of taxa across datasets, as this directly reflects the zero-inflated structure characteristic of microbiome abundance data. While diversity indices provide useful summaries of community composition, they do not capture fine-grained differences at the matrix-entry level, particularly the spatial distribution of zeros and non-zeros, which plays a critical role in microbial network inference. To explicitly assess how well this structural property is preserved, we evaluate presence-absence agreement rather than abundance magnitude. Specifically, we binarize both real and generated abundance matrices by assigning a value of 1 to non-zero entries and 0 to zeros, and compute the F-score to quantify the overlap between the resulting binary matrices. This measure therefore captures how closely the generated datasets reproduce the sparsity pattern of the original data. For each generated matrix, we computed the number of true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) by comparing to the binarized real data. To evaluate the performance of the six microbial network inference algorithms described above, we employed a multi-level network comparison framework that combines edge-based measures with node-level structural similarity metrics.

For all network inference algorithms, we used the default parameter settings recommended by the authors. Different network inference algorithms produce a sequence of networks corresponding to method-specific regularization parameters, each yielding a different level of network sparsity. To ensure a fair and interpretable comparison, we evaluated inferred networks at matched sparsity levels, defined in terms of a fixed edge fraction. For an undirected network with p nodes, the maximum possible number of edges is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{\max } = \frac{p(p-1)}{2}.$$\end{document} For a given edge fraction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_f$$\end{document}, we selected, for each algorithm, the inferred network whose number of edges was closest to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_f \times E_{\max }$$\end{document}. In this way, all algorithms were compared using networks with the same proportion of possible edges, independent of their internal regularization mechanisms. Importantly, this sparsity matching was applied only at the evaluation stage and did not influence model fitting or parameter selection. This sparsity-controlled evaluation framework enables meaningful comparison of network structure across algorithms in the absence of a known ground-truth network density.

For synthetic datasets generated using the SE and SP methods, the known input network topology (11 different topologies described in Section Data generation methods) served as the reference network. Each algorithm inferred a network from the synthetic data, which was then compared to the original topology. For noisy and bootstrap datasets, no ground-truth interaction network is available. In these cases, we assessed reproducibility by using each algorithm’s inferred network from the original real dataset as its own baseline reference. Specifically, for a given method A, a network inferred from the real dataset using method A was used as the reference when evaluating networks inferred by method A from the corresponding noisy or bootstrap datasets. This approach ensures that reproducibility is evaluated consistently within each algorithm’s modeling assumptions.

Edge-based evaluation: Networks were represented as adjacency matrices, and edge-level similarity was quantified using Precision, Recall, and F-score:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & \text {Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & \text {Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & F\text {-score} = \frac{2 \cdot \text {Precision} \cdot \text {Recall}}{\text {Precision} + \text {Recall}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, TP is the number of edges present in both the inferred and reference networks, TN is the number of absent edges in both, FP are edges present only in the inferred network, and FN are edges present only in the reference network.

Node-level structural similarity: While edge-based metrics capture pairwise agreement, they do not fully characterize the preservation of higher-order or local network structure. To assess the stability of node-centered interaction patterns, we additionally computed a node-level Jaccard similarity index. For each node, the Jaccard index measures the overlap between its neighborhood in the inferred network and its neighborhood in the reference network:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} J_i = \frac{|N_i^{\text {ref}} \cap N_i^{\text {inf}}|}{|N_i^{\text {ref}} \cup N_i^{\text {inf}}|}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_i$$\end{document} denotes the node-level Jaccard similarity for node i. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_i^{\text {ref}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_i^{\text {inf}}$$\end{document} represent the sets of neighboring nodes (adjacent vertices) of node i in the reference and inferred networks, respectively. Thus, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_i$$\end{document} quantifies the proportion of shared neighbors relative to the total number of unique neighbors of node i across the two networks.

This measure captures the preservation of local network structure around each node, providing a mesoscale assessment of network similarity beyond individual edge recovery.

This metric quantifies the extent to which the local interaction context of each OTU is preserved, providing a complementary perspective on network similarity beyond individual edges.

To summarize node-level stability across the network, we defined a success criterion based on a Jaccard threshold (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_i \ge th_{\textrm{Jaccard}}$$\end{document}). The node-level success rate is reported as the proportion of nodes whose local neighborhoods meet or exceed this threshold. This measure reflects the preservation of mesoscale network structure in terms of consistent local connectivity patterns, without relying on explicit community detection algorithms.

Together, the combination of sparsity-controlled edge-based metrics and node-level structural similarity provides a more comprehensive evaluation of both the accuracy of network inference on synthetic datasets and the stability of algorithmic output across noisy and bootstrap replicates.

Comparison between real and generated datasets

We first characterized the baseline ecological properties of the four real-world datasets, which revealed substantial inherent variability in species richness and alpha-diversity indices across the different environments (Fig. 2). Specifically, amgut1 and amgut2 represent high-complexity environments with significantly higher richness, while GUT and MOMS-PI exhibit lower diversity and a more constrained species distribution. Following this, we assessed how well synthetic, noisy, and bootstrap datasets recapitulate these properties using three complementary criteria: diversity indices, matrix entry similarity (F-score), and distributional alignment via the KS test and JSD metric.Fig. 2Variation in ecological metrics across datasets. Diversity indices: Differences in diversity indices (Richness, Shannon, and Inverse Simpson) between the real and generated datasets were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, and statistical significance was assessed based on the adjusted p-values.

For all three diversity indices (Richness, Shannon, and Inverse Simpson), the synthetic datasets generated using the SE and SP methods, as well as the bootstrap datasets, exhibited consistent behavior across the four real-world datasets. In particular, SE-generated synthetic data showed statistically significant differences from the real data for all datasets and across all indices after FDR correction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\textrm{adj}} < 0.05$$\end{document}).

In contrast, the SP method displayed a more dataset-dependent pattern: statistically significant differences were observed for amgut1 and amgut2 across all indices, whereas no significant differences were detected for GUT and MOMS-PI. Bootstrap datasets showed no statistically significant differences from the real data for any of the diversity indices across all datasets.

For Dirichlet-multinomial noisy datasets, no statistically significant differences in the Shannon and Inverse Simpson indices were observed for amgut1 and MOMS-PI up to 5% noise, and for amgut2 and GUT up to 10% noise after FDR correction. Statistically significant differences emerged only at higher noise levels.

For the Richness index, no significant differences were detected for amgut1 and amgut2 up to 20% noise, while GUT and MOMS-PI showed no significant differences at any noise level.

Detailed results for the Poisson and truncated normal noise models are reported in the Supplementary Material (Figures S1–S12 and Tables S1–S3).

Matrix entry similarity (F-score): The binary F-score analysis demonstrated that all three noise models (Dirichlet-multinomial, Poisson, and truncated normal) preserved a high level of entry-wise agreement with the real data, particularly at low noise levels. At 5% noise, F-scores exceeded 95% across all datasets for all noise models, indicating near-complete preservation of the original presence-absence structure. As the noise intensity increased, F-scores declined gradually and consistently across datasets and noise models. At 50% noise, F-scores ranged from approximately 85-90% for amgut1 and amgut2, 73-78% for GUT, and 68-73% for MOMS-PI, reflecting the increasing difficulty of maintaining exact sparsity patterns under stronger perturbations.

In contrast, synthetic datasets generated by the SE method exhibited substantially lower F-scores, most notably for amgut2 (3.6%) and MOMS-PI (23%), indicating a pronounced disruption of the original presence-absence structure. By comparison, SP-generated synthetic datasets achieved markedly higher F-scores, closely matching those observed for bootstrap datasets across all datasets (e.g., 72% for amgut1 and 68% for amgut2). This similarity suggests that SP better preserves the sparsity structure of the real data than SE, albeit less effectively than noise-based perturbation schemes (Figures S13–S16 and Table S4).

Distributional similarity (KS test): The KS test was used to assess OTU-level distributional differences between the real and generated datasets, with significance determined after FDR correction. Bootstrap and Synthetic datasets generated by the SP method showed complete distributional agreement with the real data across all datasets, with no OTUs exhibiting significant differences.

In contrast, synthetic datasets generated using the SE method exhibited pronounced distributional deviations. Specifically, significant differences were detected for 92% of OTUs in amgut1 and MOMS-PI, 100% in amgut2, and 28% in GUT, highlighting substantial alterations of OTU abundance distributions under this generation scheme.

Among noise-based perturbations, Dirichlet-multinomial noise showed strong robustness: no significant OTU-level differences were observed up to 30% noise for any dataset, and even at the highest noise level (50%), the proportion of significantly affected OTUs remained low (e.g., 25% for amgut1, 8% for amgut2, and ≤ 1% for GUT and MOMS-PI).

By contrast, truncated normal and Poisson noise models led to markedly larger distributional disruptions at moderate to high noise levels. While both models preserved OTU distributions at low noise (5-10%), substantial divergence emerged from 20% noise onward, with up to 100% of OTUs showing significant differences in amgut1, amgut2, and MOMS-PI at higher noise levels. The GUT dataset was comparatively more robust, exhibiting lower proportions of affected OTUs under these noise models.

Overall, these results indicate that SP and bootstrap generation preserve OTU-level distributions most faithfully, Dirichlet-multinomial noise provides a controlled and robust perturbation scheme, whereas Poisson and truncated normal noise induce rapid distributional divergence as noise intensity increases (Figures S17–S20 and Table S5).

Distribution comparison JSD: The JSD was used to quantify differences between OTU abundance distributions in the generated and real datasets, with lower values indicating greater similarity.

Across all datasets, noise-based perturbations yielded substantially lower JSD values than synthetic and bootstrap generation, indicating closer distributional agreement with the real data. Among noise models, truncated normal and Poisson noise at low levels (5-10%) produced the smallest JSD values, reflecting minimal perturbation of OTU distributions. As noise intensity increased, JSD values rose gradually for both models, with truncated normal noise consistently yielding slightly lower divergence than Poisson noise across datasets.

Dirichlet-multinomial noise showed a stronger dependence on noise level, with JSD values increasing monotonically from low to high noise. Nevertheless, even at moderate noise levels, the divergence remained below that observed for synthetic and bootstrap datasets, indicating controlled but systematic distributional shifts.

In contrast, synthetic datasets generated by the SE and SP method and bootstrap datasets exhibited substantially larger JSD values across all datasets, highlighting pronounced distributional differences from the real data.

The magnitude of divergence varied across datasets: GUT consistently showed the lowest JSD values under all generation schemes, indicating greater robustness of its OTU distributions, whereas MOMS-PI exhibited the largest divergences, particularly for bootstrap and high-noise conditions. Overall, all generated datasets showed non-zero JSD values, indicating measurable distributional deviations from the real data under this metric (Figures S21–S24 and Table S6).

Algorithms performance on constructing microbiome networks based on generated datasets

We evaluated the performance of six microbial network inference algorithms (gCoda, OIPCQ, S-E(glasso), S-E(mb), SPRING, and SparCC) across four real microbiome datasets (amgut1, amgut2, GUT, and MOMS-PI) using three types of derived datasets: synthetic, noisy, and bootstrap (described in Section Data generation methods). All results reported in this section correspond to networks selected at a fixed edge fraction of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_f = 2\%$$\end{document}, ensuring that all algorithms are compared at the same level of network sparsity.

For the synthetic datasets, comparing the inferred networks with their known underlying topologies assessed both the accuracy of the algorithms in network retrieval and their robustness in the face of random variance due to data generation. The synthetic networks provided a controlled setting to evaluate the algorithms’ ability to recover the true structure, where we could directly compare the inferred networks to the known topologies. For the noisy and bootstrap datasets, the inferred networks were compared with the corresponding network derived from the original real dataset (as a baseline) to assess the reproducibility and robustness of the algorithms under perturbation conditions.

Performance on Bootstrap and Noisy Datasets: The performance of the algorithms was first evaluated on bootstrap replicates and under controlled noise perturbations. On bootstrap datasets, the top performer varied: SparCC excelled on amgut1 and GUT, gCoda on amgut2, and S-E(glasso) on MOMS-PI (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Tables S7-S10).

Discussion

In this study, we present a reproducibility-centered evaluation framework for microbial network inference algorithms, addressing the challenge posed by the lack of experimentally validated ground truth in most microbiome datasets. By benchmarking six widely used methods on real datasets and their perturbed variants (bootstrap resampling and controlled noise addition), alongside synthetic datasets, we demonstrate that reproducibility under realistic data perturbations can serve as a practical proxy for assessing algorithmic robustness. Importantly, while synthetic datasets allow assessment of inference accuracy against a predefined ground truth, real-world datasets only permit evaluation of reproducibility and robustness to perturbations, as the true underlying interaction network remains unknown.

We evaluated the similarity of generated datasets to real data by assessing diversity indices, matrix entry similarity, and OTU abundance distribution comparisons. This allowed us to assess how closely each generated dataset approximated the corresponding real dataset.

Although our study does not systematically isolate the causal impact of individual data properties (e.g., diversity or sparsity) on inference outcomes, the consistent association between measures of how closely the generated data match the real data and reproducibility scores suggests that preservation of these empirical characteristics is important for meaningful benchmarking.

Several limitations should be noted. First, because experimentally validated ground-truth networks are unavailable for the real microbiome datasets analyzed here, our bootstrap and noise-based evaluations should be interpreted as complementary assessments of algorithmic reproducibility and robustness, rather than as evidence of biological correctness or substitutes for accuracy-based validation. Second, although synthetic datasets provide known reference topologies, their conclusions depend on the assumptions of the data-generation model and may not fully generalize to real microbial ecosystems. Third, our analysis focused on six commonly used inference algorithms and selected sparsity levels; therefore, the observed rankings may vary with additional methods, parameter choices, or network densities. These limitations highlight the need to interpret reproducibility-based results as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, accuracy-based benchmarking when reliable ground truth is available.

As shown, SE-based synthetic datasets across all considered topologies and datasets exhibit clear differences from the real data according to most evaluated similarity metrics. Consistent with this observation, all inference algorithms generally achieve low accuracy when evaluated on these synthetic datasets. An exception is SPRING on the amgut1 dataset, where comparatively high accuracy is observed despite the limited similarity between the synthetic and real data. This discrepancy highlights that strong performance on synthetic benchmarks can be method and dataset specific and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Additionally, we observed that the algorithms showed similar performance across various synthetic topologies (such as banded, clustered, random, and real-data-inferred networks). Specifically, for the GUT dataset, the algorithms yielded comparable results for SE and SP data generation methods, despite their distinct statistical characteristics.

As shown in the bootstrap results, algorithm reproducibility is sensitive to sparsity assumptions, highlighting the importance of controlled edge selection when comparing inference methods and interpreting reproducibility outcomes.

Together, synthetic accuracy assessments and reproducibility analyses under real-data perturbations provide complementary perspectives for evaluating microbial network inference methods.

As microbiome research progresses toward integrative and predictive modeling, combining multiple benchmarking strategies (synthetic accuracy assessment where possible and reproducibility analysis on real-data-derived perturbations) may improve method selection and the reliability of downstream interpretations.

In summary, our reproducibility-driven framework offers a practical complement to existing benchmarks, providing insights into algorithmic stability under conditions that more closely mirror real microbiome datasets.

Conclusion

In this study, we examined the performance of several microbial network inference algorithms using a combination of synthetic datasets and real microbiome data subjected to bootstrap resampling and controlled noise perturbations. Our results show that, in the absence of experimentally validated ground truth, reproducibility under realistic data perturbations provides complementary information to accuracy assessments based on synthetic benchmarks.

We acknowledge that evaluations based solely on idealized synthetic data can lead to method and dataset-specific conclusions that may not generalize to real-world settings. Conversely, evaluations based solely on robustness (e.g., via bootstrap or noise perturbations) can also be misleading, as an algorithm may be stable yet produce biologically incorrect networks in the absence of ground truth. Therefore, neither approach alone is sufficient; rather, we advocate for a complementary evaluation strategy that considers both synthetic benchmarks and data-driven stability measures, while recognizing the limitations inherent to each.
