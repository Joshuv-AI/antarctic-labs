Structural dichotomy and mass criticality in indirect chemotaxis cascades: fourth-order ellipticity versus Volterra memory Thanks: These authors (J. Yu and L.S. Wang) contributed equally to this work. Corresponding author: L.S. Wang (wang.s41@northeastern.edu)
Jiguang Yu, Louis Shuo Wang

Abstract
. We investigate the structural and operator-theoretic foundations of indirect signal-generation mechanisms in Keller–Segel-type chemotaxis models. By analyzing a physically motivated multi-stage signaling cascade, we establish a precise mathematical dichotomy between instantaneous equilibration and transient kinetic memory. Specifically, we prove that the fully equilibrating parabolic–elliptic–elliptic (PES) cascade reduces to a static fourth-order elliptic interaction. In dimension four, an exact algebraic cancellation of the leading Newtonian singularity yields a purely logarithmic kernel, shifting the mass-critical dimension from N = 2 N=2 to N = 4 N=4 . Through an L 2 L^{2} -gradient flow formulation, we identify the corresponding concentration-scaling candidate critical mass M ∗ = 64 ​ π 2 ​ τ / χ M_{*}=64\pi^{2}\tau/\chi . In sharp contrast, we demonstrate that the mixed elliptic–parabolic (MEP) cascade retains a genuine Volterra memory effect that defies static reduction. Its interaction drift acts as a singular perturbation in time—exhibiting classical two-dimensional Keller–Segel principal order near the time diagonal, yet providing fourth-order smoothing in its frozen-time average, necessitating a mixed space–time threshold theory. These results isolate the physical origin of mass-critical dimensional shifts in multiscale biological systems and formulate the specific adapted Adams/logarithmic Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev (log-HLS) inequalities and mixed-norm criteria required to close the threshold problems.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

In the mathematical modelling of biological and physical self-organisation, chemotaxis—the directed movement of cellular or synthetic agents in response to chemical gradients—represents a fundamental mechanism for macroscopic pattern formation. Models of Keller–Segel type
[
1
,
2
]
exhibit critical behaviour through a delicate balance between random diffusion and chemotactic aggregation. In the classical parabolic–elliptic Keller–Segel system

(1)

\partial_{t}u=\Delta u-\chi\nabla\!\cdot(u\nabla v),\qquad-\Delta v=u,

the eliminated signal law is
v=(-\Delta)^{-1}u
. The corresponding drift operator
\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}
has principal pseudodifferential order
-1
, and under the parabolic scaling

u_{\lambda}(x,t)=\lambda^{\alpha}u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)
, diffusion and chemotactic aggregation balance precisely for
\alpha=2
. Hence, the mass-critical dimension is
N=2
and the scaling-critical Lebesgue exponent is
q_{c}=N/2
. In two dimensions, the logarithmic singularity of the Newtonian potential and the logarithmic Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev (log-HLS) inequality
[
3
,
4
]
underpin the sharp critical-mass theorem
M_{c}=8\pi/\chi
of Blanchet–Dolbeault–Perthame
[
5
]
; see also
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

In realistic biochemical networks, however, signalling is rarely instantaneous or direct. Many physically motivated chemotaxis models replace the direct production law
-\Delta v=u
by an indirect cascade, where the migration signal is not produced directly by the cells but is generated through an intermediate substance, reflecting multi-stage metabolic or kinetic processing
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. The simplest such cascades introduce a single intermediate variable
w
. We focus on two particular cascades that look formally similar but differ in one decisive physical aspect: whether the equation governing the final migration cue assumes instantaneous equilibration (elliptic) or retains its transient diffusion dynamics (parabolic).

1.2.
Two indirect cascades

Throughout the paper, let

(2)

A:=I-\Delta,\qquad A_{\tau}:=I-\tau\Delta,\qquad\tau>0,

be realised on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, on the flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{N}
, or on a bounded
C^{2}
domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions; in the bounded-domain setting all operators below are taken with this boundary condition. The chemotactic sensitivity
\chi>0
is fixed.

The first model considered is the
parabolic–elliptic–elliptic (PES) cascade
,

(PES)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=\Delta u-\chi\nabla\!\cdot(u\nabla c),\\[2.84526pt]
0=\Delta w-w+u,\\[2.84526pt]
0=\tau\Delta c-c+w.\end{cases}

The second is the
mixed elliptic–parabolic (MEP) cascade
,

(MEP)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=\Delta u-\chi\nabla\!\cdot(u\nabla c),\\[2.84526pt]
0=\Delta w-w+u,\\[2.84526pt]
\partial_{t}c=\Delta c-c+w.\end{cases}

In both systems
u
is the cell density,
w
is the intermediate substance, and
c
is the final chemotactic signal sensed by the cells.

In (
PES
), both signal equations are elliptic, meaning the intermediate kinetics equilibrate infinitely fast. The signal variables can thus be eliminated pointwise in time:

(3)

Aw=u,\qquad A_{\tau}c=w\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c=K_{\tau}u,\qquad K_{\tau}:=A_{\tau}^{-1}A^{-1}.

The operator
K_{\tau}
is positive and self-adjoint on
L^{2}
, with Fourier multiplier

(4)

\widehat{K_{\tau}f}(\xi)=\frac{\widehat{f}(\xi)}{(1+|\xi|^{2})(1+\tau|\xi|^{2})}.

The behaviour at high frequency is

(5)

\frac{1}{(1+|\xi|^{2})(1+\tau|\xi|^{2})}=\frac{1}{\tau|\xi|^{4}}+O(|\xi|^{-6})\quad\text{as }|\xi|\to\infty,

so
K_{\tau}
has principal symbol of order
-4
(we follow the symbol-decay convention; see Convention
1.1
below) and its principal homogeneous part is
\tau^{-1}(-\Delta)^{-2}
. The chemotactic drift
\nabla K_{\tau}
has symbol of order
-3
.

In (
MEP
), the first elimination still gives
w=A^{-1}u
, but the final signal equation reduces to

(6)

\partial_{t}c+Ac=A^{-1}u.

Duhamel’s formula then yields

(7)

c(t)=e^{-tA}c_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)A}A^{-1}u(s)\,ds.

Thus, instead of a static fourth-order elliptic map
u(t)\mapsto c(t)
, the MEP elimination produces a Volterra memory operator. The memory part of the drift,

(8)

\mathcal{V}u(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\nabla e^{-(t-s)A}A^{-1}u(s)\,ds,

has Fourier multiplier

(9)

m(\xi,\theta)=\frac{i\xi\,e^{-\theta(1+|\xi|^{2})}}{1+|\xi|^{2}},\qquad\theta=t-s>0.

For every fixed
\theta>0
the heat factor renders
m(\xi,\theta)
rapidly decaying in
\xi
. Near the time diagonal,

(10)

m(\xi,\theta)\longrightarrow\frac{i\xi}{1+|\xi|^{2}}\qquad\text{as }\theta\downarrow 0,

and the limiting multiplier has symbol of order
-1
, the same order as the classical Keller–Segel drift
\nabla(I-\Delta)^{-1}
.

On the other hand, if
u
is frozen in time then

(11)

\int_{0}^{t}\nabla e^{-\theta A}A^{-1}u\,d\theta=\nabla A^{-2}\bigl(I-e^{-tA}\bigr)u,

which has symbol of order
-3
. Therefore, the MEP drift is, in a precise sense, both Keller–Segel-like near the time diagonal and PES-like in its time average. Reconciling these two competing features is the criticality problem for MEP and lies outside the static elliptic framework that suffices for PES.

-3
, and the classical Keller–Segel drift
\nabla A^{-1}

Main structural theorem

The central result of the paper makes the PES/MEP dichotomy precise.

(Structural dichotomy)
.

Let
N\geq 1
,
\tau>0
,
\chi>0

(PES structure.)

1.4.
Free energy and concentration-scaling candidate mass

Because
K_{\tau}
is self-adjoint, PES admits the gradient-flow free energy

(12)

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u]:=\int_{\Omega}u\log u\,dx-\frac{\chi}{2}\int_{\Omega}u\,K_{\tau}u\,dx.

Smooth positive solutions of the eliminated PES equation satisfy the entropy-dissipation identity

(13)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u(t)]=-\int_{\Omega}u\,\bigl|\nabla(\log u-\chi K_{\tau}u)\bigr|^{2}\,dx\leq 0;

see Proposition
3.1
. In dimension
N=4
, the logarithmic local singularity of
K_{\tau}
produces the same kind of variational concentration scenario that, in the two-dimensional classical Keller–Segel problem, leads to the critical mass
8\pi/\chi
.

For a one-point concentrating sequence

(14)

u_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-4}U\!\left(\tfrac{x-x_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right),\qquad U\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{1}),\ U\geq 0,\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}U\,dx=M,

located at an interior point
x_{0}
, an explicit computation (Proposition
3.4
) gives

(15)

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u_{\varepsilon}]=\left(4M-\frac{\chi M^{2}}{16\pi^{2}\tau}\right)\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+O(1).

The leading coefficient vanishes at

(16)

M_{\!*}=\frac{64\pi^{2}\tau}{\chi}

which we identify as the
concentration-scaling candidate
for the four-dimensional PES critical mass. We emphasise that the computation (
15
)–(
16
) is a scaling identification, not a sharp threshold theorem. To promote
M_{\!*}
to a sharp threshold requires the corresponding sharp
K_{\tau}
-adapted logarithmic Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev / Adams inequality; we formulate this as Problem
5.1
in Section
5
.

2.
Operator structure of PES

We begin the analysis of (
PES
) by establishing the operator-theoretic properties of the eliminated interaction
K_{\tau}=A_{\tau}^{-1}A^{-1}
in (

).

2.1.
Spectral and Fourier representation

On
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
K_{\tau}
is the Fourier multiplier

(17)

\widehat{K_{\tau}f}(\xi)=m_{\tau}(\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi),\qquad m_{\tau}(\xi):=\frac{1}{(1+|\xi|^{2})(1+\tau|\xi|^{2})}.

Thus
0<m_{\tau}(\xi)\leq 1
and
m_{\tau}(\xi)\sim(\tau|\xi|^{4})^{-1}
as
|\xi|\to\infty
.

On
\mathbb{T}^{N}
with
f(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\widehat{f}_{k}e^{ik\cdot x}
,

(18)

K_{\tau}f=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\frac{\widehat{f}_{k}}{(1+|k|^{2})(1+\tau|k|^{2})}\,e^{ik\cdot x}.

On a bounded
C^{2}
Neumann domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, let
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j\geq 0}
be the
L^{2}
-orthonormal basis of Neumann eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
with eigenvalues
0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots
. Then

(19)

K_{\tau}f=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mu_{j}^{(\tau)}\langle f,\varphi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}}\varphi_{j},\qquad\mu_{j}^{(\tau)}:=\frac{1}{(1+\lambda_{j})(1+\tau\lambda_{j})}.

For
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{s}
the spectral Sobolev space associated with the self-adjoint operator
A=I-\Delta
on the relevant manifold; thus

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{A}^{s}}^{2}:=\langle A^{s}f,f\rangle=\sum_{j}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s}|\langle f,\varphi_{j}\rangle|^{2}

on
\Omega
(with the obvious modifications on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
or
\mathbb{T}^{N}
), which coincides with the usual Sobolev space
H^{s}
in the standard range; cf.
[
60
]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Positivity, self-adjointness, and fourth-order smoothing)
.

Let
\tau>0
. The operator
K_{\tau}=A_{\tau}^{-1}A^{-1}
is positive and self-adjoint on
L^{2}
. Moreover, for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

(20)

\|K_{\tau}f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{A}^{s+4}}\leq\max\{1,\tau^{-1}\}\,\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{A}^{s}}.

On
\mathbb{R}^{N}
this reduces to the standard Sobolev estimate
\|K_{\tau}f\|_{H^{s+4}}\leq\max\{1,\tau^{-1}\}\|f\|_{H^{s}}
.

Proof.

Positivity and self-adjointness follow from the spectral representation (
19
) (resp. from
0<m_{\tau}(\xi)\leq 1
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
or
\mathbb{T}^{N}
). For the smoothing estimate, observe

(1+\lambda_{j})^{2}\mu_{j}^{(\tau)}=\frac{1+\lambda_{j}}{1+\tau\lambda_{j}}\leq\max\{1,\tau^{-1}\}\qquad\text{for every }j\geq 0,

since
(1+\lambda)/(1+\tau\lambda)
is a Möbius function bounded between
1
and
\tau^{-1}
on
[0,\infty)
. Hence

\|K_{\tau}f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{A}^{s+4}}^{2}=\sum_{j}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s+4}\bigl(\mu_{j}^{(\tau)}\bigr)^{2}|\langle f,\varphi_{j}\rangle|^{2}\leq\max\{1,\tau^{-2}\}\,\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{A}^{s}}^{2}.

The Euclidean case follows identically from Plancherel and the multiplier bound
(1+|\xi|^{2})^{2}m_{\tau}(\xi)\leq\max\{1,\tau^{-1}\}
.
∎

The associated quadratic form is non-negative: on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

(21)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f\,K_{\tau}f\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}}{(1+|\xi|^{2})(1+\tau|\xi|^{2})}\,d\xi\geq 0,

and on
\Omega
,

(22)

\int_{\Omega}f\,K_{\tau}f\,dx=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mu_{j}^{(\tau)}|\langle f,\varphi_{j}\rangle|^{2}\geq 0.

2.2.
Principal symbol and drift order

The high-frequency expansion of the symbol is

(23)

m_{\tau}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\tau|\xi|^{4}}\left[1-\frac{1+\tau^{-1}}{|\xi|^{2}}+O(|\xi|^{-4})\right]\quad\text{as }|\xi|\to\infty.

Equivalently, microlocally at high frequency,

(24)

K_{\tau}=\tau^{-1}(-\Delta)^{-2}+\Op(S^{-6}),

where the remainder is a pseudodifferential operator whose symbol lies in the standard Hörmander class
S^{-6}
of symbols decaying at order
-6
.

Proposition 2.2

(Principal symbol of PES interaction)
.

The eliminated PES interaction
K_{\tau}
is a fourth-order elliptic smoothing operator. Its principal symbol is
\tau^{-1}|\xi|^{-4}
, and its principal homogeneous part is
\tau^{-1}(-\Delta)^{-2}
. Consequently, the chemotactic drift
\nabla K_{\tau}
has principal symbol
i\xi/(\tau|\xi|^{4})
and principal order
-3
.

Proof.

The asymptotic expansion (
23
) is obtained by writing

m_{\tau}(\xi)=(\tau|\xi|^{4})^{-1}(1+|\xi|^{-2})^{-1}(1+(\tau|\xi|^{2})^{-1})^{-1}

and expanding each factor in a geometric series for
|\xi|^{2}>\max\{1,\tau^{-1}\}
. The corresponding pseudodifferential decomposition (
24
) is then standard. Differentiation in
x
multiplies the symbol by
i\xi
, lowering the symbol-decay order from
4
to
3
.
∎

2.3.
Scaling and the critical Lebesgue exponent

The full Bessel-product operator
K_{\tau}
is not exactly scale-invariant because
A
and
A_{\tau}
contain zeroth-order terms; however, its principal homogeneous part
\tau^{-1}(-\Delta)^{-2}
is exactly invariant under dilations. The following scaling discussion concerns this principal homogeneous regime, which dictates the high-frequency dynamics.

For
\lambda>0
, define

(25)

u_{\lambda}(x,t):=\lambda^{\alpha}u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t).

Then
\partial_{t}u_{\lambda}=\lambda^{\alpha+2}(\partial_{t}u)(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)
and
\Delta u_{\lambda}=\lambda^{\alpha+2}(\Delta u)(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)
. Since the principal part of
K_{\tau}
has symbol of order
-4
, it transforms under dilations as

(26)

(K_{\tau}u_{\lambda})(x,t)\sim\lambda^{\alpha-4}(K_{\tau}u)(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t),

in the sense that the equality holds exactly with
K_{\tau}
replaced by its principal part
\tau^{-1}(-\Delta)^{-2}
, with lower-order terms suppressed by an additional factor
\lambda^{-2}
at large
\lambda
. Consequently
\nabla K_{\tau}u_{\lambda}\sim\lambda^{\alpha-3}(\nabla K_{\tau}u)(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)
and the nonlinear aggregation term scales as

(27)

\nabla\!\cdot(u_{\lambda}\nabla K_{\tau}u_{\lambda})\sim\lambda^{2\alpha-2}\bigl[\nabla\!\cdot(u\nabla K_{\tau}u)\bigr](\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t).

Balancing with
\partial_{t}u_{\lambda}
and
\Delta u_{\lambda}
requires
\alpha+2=2\alpha-2
, i.e.
\alpha=4
.

Proposition 2.3

(Scaling of PES)
.

The principal parabolic scaling of PES is

(28)

u_{\lambda}(x,t)=\lambda^{4}u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t).

Under this scaling, for
1\leq q<\infty
,

(29)

\|u_{\lambda}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}=\lambda^{4-N/q}\,\|u(\cdot,\lambda^{2}t)\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}.

Therefore the scaling-critical Lebesgue exponent is
q_{c}=N/4
, and the mass-critical dimension is
N=4
.

Proof.

The scaling exponent
\alpha=4
was derived above. For (
29
),

\|u_{\lambda}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{q}}^{q}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lambda^{4q}|u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)|^{q}\,dx=\lambda^{4q-N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u(y,\lambda^{2}t)|^{q}\,dy.

Taking the
q
-th root yields (
29
). The mass case
q=1
gives invariance precisely at
N=4
.
∎

Remark 2.4

(Comparison with classical Keller–Segel)
.

For the classical parabolic–elliptic Keller–Segel system, the drift
\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}
has principal order
-1
and the scaling balance gives
\alpha=2
, so the critical Lebesgue exponent is
q_{c}=N/2
, with mass criticality in
N=2
. The PES drift
\nabla K_{\tau}
has principal order
-3
, the scaling exponent is
\alpha=4
, and the critical exponent is
q_{c}=N/4
. In particular,
L^{N/2}
is
not
the scaling-critical space for PES.

2.4.
Logarithmic kernel in dimension four

We now identify the local singularity of the integral kernel of
K_{\tau}
in dimension four. The argument uses partial fractions together with the classical asymptotic expansion of the four-dimensional Bessel potential of order two.

Lemma 2.5

(Bessel kernel
G_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
)
.

Let
N=4
. The kernel

(30)

G_{2}(x):=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}\right](x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{4},

is a positive radial function in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus\{0\})
which decays exponentially at infinity and admits the local expansion

(31)

G_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|x|^{2}}-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\log\frac{1}{|x|}+\rho_{2}(x),

where
\rho_{2}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
and bounded in a neighbourhood of the origin.

Proof.

The smoothness of
G_{2}
away from
0
, positivity, and exponential decay are classical
[
61
]
. The function
G_{2}
solves

(32)

-\Delta G_{2}+G_{2}=\delta_{0}\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{4}).

Let
G_{0}(x):=1/(4\pi^{2}|x|^{2})
be the Newtonian fundamental solution of
-\Delta
on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, so
-\Delta G_{0}=\delta_{0}
. Then
R:=G_{2}-G_{0}
satisfies

(33)

-\Delta R=-G_{2}\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus\{0\},

with
R(x)=O(\log(1/|x|))
as
|x|\to 0
by the heat-kernel representation of
G_{2}

[
61
]
. A direct radial computation gives
\Delta(\log|x|)=2/|x|^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Hence the function
w(x):=-(8\pi^{2})^{-1}\log(1/|x|)
satisfies

-\Delta w=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|x|^{2}}=-G_{0}(x),

and consequently

-\Delta(R-w)=-G_{2}+G_{0}=-R.

The right-hand side has at most logarithmic singularity, so standard elliptic regularity gives
R-w\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
. Setting
\rho_{2}:=R-w\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
yields (
31
).
∎

Figure 2.
Comparison of radial singularity profiles in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
(
\tau=2
). The classical Newtonian potential and the single Bessel potential
G_{2}(r)
exhibit a steep
O(r^{-2})
singularity at the origin. In contrast, the eliminated PES interaction kernel
G_{\tau}(r)
demonstrates an exact algebraic cancellation of this leading singularity, resulting in a softened, purely logarithmic divergence. This structural softening is the geometric mechanism that shifts the mass-critical dimension of the cascade from

to
N=4
.

Proposition 2.6

(Logarithmic kernel of
K_{\tau}
in
N=4
)
.

Let
N=4
and
\tau>0
. The integral kernel
G_{\tau}
of
K_{\tau}
on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
admits the local expansion

(34)

G_{\tau}(x)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\tau}\log\frac{1}{|x|}+R_{\tau}(x),

where
R_{\tau}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
is bounded in a neighbourhood of the origin. The same expansion holds on
\mathbb{T}^{4}
for
x
in any fundamental domain. On a bounded
C^{\infty}
Neumann domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
, the integral kernel
G_{\tau}(x,y)
of the Neumann realisation of
K_{\tau}
admits, for every compact set
\mathcal{K}\Subset\Omega
,

(35)

G_{\tau}(x,y)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\tau}\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}+R_{\tau}(x,y),\qquad(x,y)\in\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K},

with
R_{\tau}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K})
. The same conclusion holds on bounded
C^{2}
Neumann domains under the corresponding interior Green-kernel regularity assumption
(
see Remark
2.7
)
.

Proof.

Step 1: Partial-fraction decomposition for
\tau\neq 1
.

For
\tau\neq 1
, the partial-fraction identity is

(36)

\frac{1}{(1+|\xi|^{2})(1+\tau|\xi|^{2})}=\frac{1}{1-\tau}\cdot\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}-\frac{\tau}{1-\tau}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\tau|\xi|^{2}}.

The inverse Fourier transform of
(1+\tau|\xi|^{2})^{-1}
is obtained by the dilation
\xi=\eta/\sqrt{\tau}
:

\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1}{1+\tau|\xi|^{2}}\right]\!(x)=\tau^{-2}G_{2}(x/\sqrt{\tau})\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{4}.

Therefore

(37)

G_{\tau}(x)=\frac{1}{1-\tau}\!\left[G_{2}(x)-\frac{1}{\tau}\,G_{2}(x/\sqrt{\tau})\right].

Step 2: Cancellation of the leading
|x|^{-2}
singularity.

By Lemma
2.5
,

G_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|x|^{2}}-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\log\frac{1}{|x|}+\rho_{2}(x),

and the same lemma applied at
x/\sqrt{\tau}
gives

G_{2}(x/\sqrt{\tau})=\frac{\tau}{4\pi^{2}|x|^{2}}-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\log\frac{\sqrt{\tau}}{|x|}+\rho_{2}(x/\sqrt{\tau}).

Hence

\frac{1}{\tau}\,G_{2}(x/\sqrt{\tau})=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|x|^{2}}-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\tau}\log\frac{1}{|x|}-\frac{\log\sqrt{\tau}}{8\pi^{2}\tau}+\tau^{-1}\rho_{2}(x/\sqrt{\tau}),

and therefore

G_{2}(x)-\frac{1}{\tau}\,G_{2}(x/\sqrt{\tau})=\Bigl(\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\tau}-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\Bigr)\log\frac{1}{|x|}+\tilde{\rho}(x)=\frac{1-\tau}{8\pi^{2}\tau}\log\frac{1}{|x|}+\tilde{\rho}(x),

with
\tilde{\rho}
continuous and bounded near the origin. Combining with (
37
),

G_{\tau}(x)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\tau}\log\frac{1}{|x|}+R_{\tau}(x),\qquad R_{\tau}(x):=\frac{\tilde{\rho}(x)}{1-\tau},

which proves (
34
) for
\tau\neq 1
. The crucial cancellation is that the
|x|^{-2}
coefficient in
\frac{1}{\tau}G_{2}(x/\sqrt{\tau})
matches that of
G_{2}(x)
identically, eliminating the leading singularity. (see Figure
2
)

Step 3: The case
\tau=1
.

If
\tau=1
, then
K_{1}=A^{-2}
has Fourier multiplier
(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-2}
, and its kernel is the Bessel potential of order four on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. By the heat-kernel representation
[
61
]
,

G_{1}(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1}{(1+|\xi|^{2})^{2}}\right]\!(x)=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{2}\Gamma(2)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-1}e^{-t-|x|^{2}/(4t)}\,dt=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}K_{0}(|x|),

where
K_{0}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order zero. The classical asymptotics

K_{0}(r)=\log\frac{1}{r}+(\log 2-\gamma_{E})+O(r^{2}\log r)\qquad\text{as }r\downarrow 0

(with
\gamma_{E}
Euler’s constant) give

G_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\log\frac{1}{|x|}+R_{1}(x),

with
R_{1}
continuous and bounded near
0
, in agreement with (
34
) at
\tau=1
. Continuity of
R_{\tau}
in
\tau
follows from continuity in
\tau
of the multiplier
m_{\tau}
on every set
\{|\xi|\geq\varepsilon\}
.

Step 4: Torus.

On
\mathbb{T}^{4}=\mathbb{R}^{4}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{4}
, the kernel is

G_{\tau}^{\mathbb{T}}(x)=\sum_{k\in(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{4}}G_{\tau}(x+k),

the sum over
k\neq 0
defining a
C^{\infty}
function on any fundamental domain. Hence (
34
) holds on
\mathbb{T}^{4}
with the same leading coefficient.

Step 5: Bounded
C^{\infty}
Neumann domain.

For a bounded
C^{\infty}
Neumann domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
, the standard interior parametrix construction for elliptic pseudodifferential operators (cf.
[
62
]
) decomposes the Neumann realisation of
K_{\tau}
on
\Omega
as a Euclidean parametrix with kernel
G_{\tau}
(from
\mathbb{R}^{4}
) plus a smoothing remainder of order
-\infty
on any interior compact
\mathcal{K}
. Inserting (
34
) into the parametrix gives (
35
).
∎

Remark 2.7

(Low-regularity domains)
.

The interior parametrix in Step 5 uses smooth-coefficient pseudodifferential calculus, which applies cleanly on
C^{\infty}
domains. For bounded
C^{2}
Neumann domains, the analogue of (

) should still hold with the same leading coefficient at any interior compact

, by a low-regularity Green-kernel argument for the operator
A_{\tau}A=(I-\tau\Delta)(I-\Delta)
; we do not record the details. Concentration computations later in the paper are stated for interior concentration points
x_{0}\in\Omega
with
\operatorname{dist}(x_{0},\partial\Omega)>0
, so only the interior expansion is used.

Remark 2.8

(Boundary effects on concentration)
.

The expansion (
35
) is an

expansion. Concentration near
\partial\Omega
for the Neumann problem requires additional boundary-parametrix analysis (with the precise coefficient of the local logarithmic singularity at boundary points typically modified by reflected-image contributions). All concentration computations below are stated for interior
x_{0}
.

3.
Free energy, dissipation, and concentration scaling

We now exploit the operator-theoretic structure of
K_{\tau}
to identify the gradient-flow structure of PES, derive the entropy-dissipation identity, and compute the concentration scaling that singles out
M_{\!*}=64\pi^{2}\tau/\chi
as the candidate critical mass in dimension four.

3.1.
Eliminated PES equation as a gradient flow

Using
c=K_{\tau}u
, the first equation of (
PES
) becomes

(38)

\partial_{t}u=\Delta u-\chi\nabla\!\cdot(u\nabla K_{\tau}u)=\nabla\!\cdot\!\bigl[u\,\nabla(\log u-\chi K_{\tau}u)\bigr],

where the second equality uses
\Delta u=\nabla\!\cdot(u\nabla\log u)
for positive smooth
u
. The natural free energy associated with (
38
) is

(39)

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u]=\int_{\Omega}u\log u\,dx-\frac{\chi}{2}\int_{\Omega}u\,K_{\tau}u\,dx.

Self-adjointness of
K_{\tau}
is essential: for any test function
\phi
,

(40)

\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(u+\varepsilon\phi)K_{\tau}(u+\varepsilon\phi)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\phi\,K_{\tau}u\,dx,

so that the
L^{2}
-gradient of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}
is

(41)

\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}}{\delta u}(u)=\log u+1-\chi K_{\tau}u,

and equation (
38
) is the formal
L^{2}
-gradient flow
\partial_{t}u=\nabla\!\cdot(u\nabla\tfrac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}}{\delta u})
in the Wasserstein sense; cf.
[
63
,
64
]
for the abstract setting.

Proposition 3.1

(Entropy dissipation)
.

Let
u\in C([0,T];L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega))\cap C^{1}((0,T);L^{1}(\Omega))
be a positive, sufficiently smooth solution of (
38
) satisfying one of the following: (a) periodic boundary conditions; (b) Schwartz decay at infinity on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
; (c) the no-flux boundary condition

(42)

\bigl(\nabla u-\chi u\,\nabla K_{\tau}u\bigr)\cdot\nu=0\qquad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

Then

(43)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u(t)]=-\int_{\Omega}u\,\bigl|\nabla(\log u-\chi K_{\tau}u)\bigr|^{2}\,dx\leq 0,

and in integrated form,

(44)

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u(T)]+\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\Omega}u\,\bigl|\nabla(\log u-\chi K_{\tau}u)\bigr|^{2}\,dx\,dt=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u_{0}].

In addition,
\int_{\Omega}u(\cdot,t)\,dx
is conserved.

Proof.

By (
41
),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u(t)]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\log u-\chi K_{\tau}u)\,\partial_{t}u\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\log u-\chi K_{\tau}u)\,\nabla\!\cdot\bigl[u\nabla(\log u-\chi K_{\tau}u)\bigr]\,dx,

the additive constant from (
41
) contributing nothing because
\partial_{t}\int u\,dx=0
(which follows from (
42
) or the analogous decay/periodicity hypothesis). Integration by parts gives (
43
) with vanishing boundary term. Integration in time gives (
44
). Mass conservation is immediate from
\partial_{t}u=\nabla\!\cdot(\cdots)
together with the boundary/decay hypothesis.
∎

3.2.
Entropy and interaction scaling of concentrating sequences

Throughout this subsection,
N=4
. Fix an interior point
x_{0}\in\Omega
with
\operatorname{dist}(x_{0},\partial\Omega)>0
(or
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
, resp.
x_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{4}
). Fix
U\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{1}(0))
with
U\geq 0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}U(z)\,dz=M
, and define

(45)

u_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon^{-4}U\!\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right),\qquad 0<\varepsilon\ll 1.

Then
\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\,dx=M
and
u_{\varepsilon}\,dx\rightharpoonup M\delta_{x_{0}}
weakly-
*
as measures.

Lemma 3.2

(Entropy scaling)
.

For
u_{\varepsilon}
as in (
45
),

(46)

\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\log u_{\varepsilon}\,dx=4M\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}U\log U\,dz=4M\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+O(1).

Proof.

Substitute
x=x_{0}+\varepsilon z
,
dx=\varepsilon^{4}dz
. Then
u_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-4}U(z)
and

\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\log u_{\varepsilon}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}U(z)\bigl(4\log\varepsilon^{-1}+\log U(z)\bigr)\,dz=4M\log\varepsilon^{-1}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}U\log U\,dz.\qed

Lemma 3.3

(Interaction scaling)
.

For
u_{\varepsilon}
as in (
45
),

(47)

\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\,K_{\tau}u_{\varepsilon}\,dx=\frac{M^{2}}{8\pi^{2}\tau}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+O(1).

Proof.

By Proposition
2.6
, for
x,y
in the compact support
\mathcal{K}\Subset\Omega
of
u_{\varepsilon}
(uniformly in
\varepsilon
small),

G_{\tau}(x,y)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\tau}\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}+R_{\tau}(x,y),\qquad R_{\tau}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K}).

Hence

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}K_{\tau}u_{\varepsilon}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\tau}\iint u_{\varepsilon}(x)u_{\varepsilon}(y)\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}\,dx\,dy+\iint u_{\varepsilon}(x)R_{\tau}(x,y)u_{\varepsilon}(y)\,dx\,dy.

The remainder integral is bounded by
\|R_{\tau}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K})}M^{2}=O(1)
. For the logarithmic part, substitute
x=x_{0}+\varepsilon z
,
y=x_{0}+\varepsilon\zeta
:

\displaystyle\iint u_{\varepsilon}(x)u_{\varepsilon}(y)\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}^{4}}U(z)U(\zeta)\Bigl(\log\varepsilon^{-1}+\log\frac{1}{|z-\zeta|}\Bigr)\,dz\,d\zeta

\displaystyle=M^{2}\log\varepsilon^{-1}+\iint U(z)U(\zeta)\log\frac{1}{|z-\zeta|}\,dz\,d\zeta.

The last integral is finite since
U
is smooth and compactly supported, so (
47
) follows.
∎

Proposition 3.4

(Concentration-scaling candidate mass)
.

Let
N=4
and let
u_{\varepsilon}
be as in (
45
). Then

(48)

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u_{\varepsilon}]=\left(4M-\frac{\chi M^{2}}{16\pi^{2}\tau}\right)\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+O(1).

The leading coefficient vanishes precisely at

(49)

M_{\!*}=\frac{64\pi^{2}\tau}{\chi}.

Consequently:

(i)

if
M<M_{\!*}
, then
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u_{\varepsilon}]\to+\infty
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
;

(ii)

if
M=M_{\!*}
, then
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u_{\varepsilon}]=O(1)
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
;

(iii)

if
M>M_{\!*}
, then
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u_{\varepsilon}]\to-\infty
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. (see the variational descent illustrated in Figure
3
).

Proof.

Substitute (
46
) and (
47
) into (
39
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u_{\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle=4M\log\varepsilon^{-1}-\frac{\chi}{2}\cdot\frac{M^{2}}{8\pi^{2}\tau}\log\varepsilon^{-1}+O(1)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(4M-\frac{\chi M^{2}}{16\pi^{2}\tau}\Bigr)\log\varepsilon^{-1}+O(1).

The leading coefficient
4M-\chi M^{2}/(16\pi^{2}\tau)=M\bigl(4-\chi M/(16\pi^{2}\tau)\bigr)
vanishes at
M=64\pi^{2}\tau/\chi
, and the three cases follow from the sign of the coefficient.
∎

Corollary 3.5

(Variational descent above the candidate mass)
.

If
M>64\pi^{2}\tau/\chi
, then there exist
u_{\varepsilon}
concentrating at any interior point
x_{0}
such that
u_{\varepsilon}\,dx\rightharpoonup M\delta_{x_{0}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u_{\varepsilon}]\to-\infty
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.∎

Remark 3.6

(The threshold value is variational, not dynamical)
.

Corollary
3.5
produces sequences along which the free energy is unbounded below. By itself this does
not
imply finite-time blow-up of the PES flow: the entropy-dissipation identity (
43
) only forces
t\mapsto\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u(t)]
to be non-increasing. Promoting variational descent to dynamical concentration requires further dynamical tools (virial identity, monotonicity formula, or a sharp
K_{\tau}
-adapted compactness theory). We formulate the resulting open problem in Section
5
.

4.
Volterra-memory structure of MEP

We turn to the mixed elliptic–parabolic cascade (
MEP
). The intermediate elliptic equation
0=\Delta w-w+u
gives
w=A^{-1}u
at each time, so that the signal equation reduces to

(50)

\partial_{t}c+Ac=A^{-1}u.

4.1.
Duhamel representation and Volterra drift

Let
\{e^{-tA}\}_{t\geq 0}
denote the analytic semigroup generated by
-A
.

Proposition 4.1

(Volterra representation)
.

Let
(u,c)
be sufficiently smooth so that all of the operations below are justified. Then

(51)

c(t)=e^{-tA}c_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)A}A^{-1}u(s)\,ds,

and consequently

(52)

\nabla c(t)=\nabla e^{-tA}c_{0}+\mathcal{V}u(t),\quad\mathcal{V}u(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\nabla e^{-(t-s)A}A^{-1}u(s)\,ds.

Proof.

Apply
e^{tA}
to (
50
) and integrate from
0
to
t
:

e^{tA}c(t)-c_{0}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{sA}A^{-1}u(s)\,ds.

Multiplication by
e^{-tA}
gives (
51
). Differentiation in
x
yields (
52
).
∎

4.2.
Drift multiplier: near-diagonal versus time-averaged orders

On
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\widehat{e^{-\theta A}f}(\xi)=e^{-\theta(1+|\xi|^{2})}\widehat{f}(\xi)
and
\widehat{A^{-1}f}(\xi)=(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-1}\widehat{f}(\xi)
, so the memory drift multiplier at time lag
\theta=t-s>0
is

(53)

m(\xi,\theta)=\frac{i\xi\,e^{-\theta(1+|\xi|^{2})}}{1+|\xi|^{2}}.

Proposition 4.2

(Near-diagonal Keller–Segel order)
.

For every fixed
\theta>0
,
|m(\xi,\theta)|\leq|\xi|(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-1}e^{-\theta|\xi|^{2}}
decays faster than any polynomial as
|\xi|\to\infty
, so
\mathcal{F}^{-1}m(\cdot,\theta)
is a smoothing operator of infinite order. However, the family
\{m(\cdot,\theta)\}_{\theta>0}
is
not
uniformly smoothing as
\theta\downarrow 0
:

(54)

m(\xi,\theta)\longrightarrow m_{0}(\xi):=\frac{i\xi}{1+|\xi|^{2}}\qquad\text{locally uniformly in }\xi\text{ as }\theta\downarrow 0,

and the limiting multiplier
m_{0}
has principal order
-1
, equal to that of the classical Keller–Segel drift
\nabla(I-\Delta)^{-1}
. Thus the symbol-class order of the Volterra-drift kernel at the time diagonal is Keller–Segel-like, even though no individual time-slice operator
\mathcal{F}^{-1}m(\cdot,\theta)
(with
\theta>0
) has order
-1
.

Proof.

The first claim follows from
e^{-\theta(1+|\xi|^{2})}\leq e^{-\theta|\xi|^{2}}
, and the limit (
54
) from
e^{-\theta(1+|\xi|^{2})}\to 1
as
\theta\downarrow 0
, uniformly on compact
\xi
-sets. For
|\xi|\to\infty
,
|m_{0}(\xi)|=|\xi|/(1+|\xi|^{2})\sim|\xi|^{-1}
, so
m_{0}
has principal order
-1
. The non-uniformity in
\theta
follows because the symbol estimate
|m(\xi,\theta)|\leq|\xi|^{-1}
at fixed
\theta=0^{+}
does not improve to higher decay even as
|\xi|\to\infty
.
∎

The following observation isolates the second competing feature of the MEP drift.

Proposition 4.3

(Frozen-time average is fourth-order smoothing)
.

If
u(s)\equiv u
is frozen in time, then

(55)

\int_{0}^{t}\nabla e^{-(t-s)A}A^{-1}u\,ds=\nabla A^{-2}\bigl(I-e^{-tA}\bigr)u.

The associated Fourier multiplier

M_{t}(\xi)=\frac{i\xi\bigl(1-e^{-t(1+|\xi|^{2})}\bigr)}{(1+|\xi|^{2})^{2}}

satisfies
M_{t}(\xi)\sim i\xi/|\xi|^{4}
as
|\xi|\to\infty
for each fixed
t>0
, and therefore has principal order
-3
.

Proof.

By change of variables
\theta=t-s
,

\int_{0}^{t}\nabla e^{-(t-s)A}A^{-1}u\,ds=\nabla A^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta A}u\,d\theta=\nabla A^{-1}\cdot A^{-1}(I-e^{-tA})u=\nabla A^{-2}(I-e^{-tA})u,

where we used
\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta A}\,d\theta=A^{-1}(I-e^{-tA})
. The Fourier multiplier follows by direct computation, and the high-frequency asymptotics yield principal order
-3
.
∎

Remark 4.4

(Time-averaged smoothing is not a static reduction)
.

For nonlinear MEP solutions,
u
is
not
frozen in time. The nonlinear memory term

\mathcal{V}u(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\nabla e^{-(t-s)A}A^{-1}u(s)\,ds

cannot be replaced by
\nabla A^{-2}(I-e^{-tA})u(t)
without an additional approximation argument controlling the time variation of
u(s)
on the interval
[0,t]
. Consequently, the MEP criticality problem is a genuinely mixed space–time problem: its near-diagonal component (Proposition
4.2
) has Keller–Segel order
-1
, while its frozen-time average (Proposition
4.3
) has fourth-order order
-3
. The static fourth-order elliptic scaling of PES cannot be transferred to MEP through the frozen-time average alone.

4.3.
Space–time Volterra estimates

Although the near-diagonal multiplier has Keller–Segel order
-1
, the Volterra structure provides quantitative smoothing measured in mixed space–time norms. We record the basic estimates.

Lemma 4.5

(Heat–Bessel estimate)
.

Let
1<q\leq s<\infty
and
\theta>0
. There exists
C=C(N,q,s)
such that

(56)

\|\nabla e^{-\theta A}A^{-1}f\|_{L^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq C\,e^{-\theta}\theta^{-\beta(q,s)}\|f\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})},

with

(57)

\beta(q,s):=\Bigl(\tfrac{N}{2}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{q}-\tfrac{1}{s}\bigr)-\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigr)_{+}.

The analogous estimate (with
e^{-\theta}
replaced by
1
) holds on
\mathbb{T}^{N}
and on bounded Neumann domains.

Proof.

The Fourier multiplier of
\nabla A^{-1}
is
i\xi/(1+|\xi|^{2})
, which satisfies the Mikhlin–Hörmander condition
|\xi|^{|\alpha|}|\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}(i\xi/(1+|\xi|^{2}))|\leq C_{\alpha}
for every multi-index
\alpha
. Hence
\nabla A^{-1}
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for
1<p<\infty
, and by Bessel-potential / Sobolev embedding it maps
L^{q}\to L^{r}
boundedly for any
1<q\leq r<\infty
satisfying

\frac{1}{r}\geq\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{N}.

The heat semigroup
\{e^{\theta\Delta}\}_{\theta>0}
satisfies the standard ultracontractive estimate

\|e^{\theta\Delta}g\|_{L^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq C\theta^{-\frac{N}{2}(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{s})}\|g\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})},\qquad 1\leq r\leq s\leq\infty.

Writing
e^{-\theta A}=e^{-\theta}e^{\theta\Delta}
, the Bessel mass term contributes the exponential factor
e^{-\theta}
, and the heat factor contributes the ultracontractive gain in integrability.

Case
1/q-1/s\leq 1/N
.

In this range,
\nabla A^{-1}:L^{q}\to L^{s}
is bounded directly, and
\|e^{-\theta A}h\|_{L^{s}}\leq e^{-\theta}\|h\|_{L^{s}}
. Hence

\|\nabla e^{-\theta A}A^{-1}f\|_{L^{s}}=\|e^{-\theta A}\nabla A^{-1}f\|_{L^{s}}\leq e^{-\theta}\|\nabla A^{-1}f\|_{L^{s}}\leq Ce^{-\theta}\|f\|_{L^{q}},

which is (
56
) with
\beta(q,s)=0
.

Case
1/q-1/s>1/N
.

Set
1/r:=1/q-1/N
, so that
\nabla A^{-1}:L^{q}\to L^{r}
boundedly. Then

\|\nabla e^{-\theta A}A^{-1}f\|_{L^{s}}\leq\|e^{-\theta A}\|_{L^{r}\to L^{s}}\,\|\nabla A^{-1}f\|_{L^{r}}\leq Ce^{-\theta}\theta^{-\frac{N}{2}(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{s})}\|f\|_{L^{q}}.

By the choice of
r
, the exponent is

\frac{N}{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{s}\right)=\frac{N}{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{N}-\frac{1}{s}\right)=\frac{N}{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{s}\right)-\frac{1}{2}=\beta(q,s),

yielding (
56
).

The torus and bounded Neumann domain cases proceed identically, using the Mikhlin–Hörmander theorem in those geometries (with the exponential decay
e^{-\theta}
absent on bounded domains, where it is absorbed into a constant on
[0,T]
).
∎

Lemma 4.6

(Pointwise-in-time Volterra bound)
.

Let
T>0
,
1<q\leq s<\infty
. For
0<t\leq T
,

(58)

\|\mathcal{V}u(t)\|_{L^{s}}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}(t-\sigma)^{-\beta(q,s)}\|u(\sigma)\|_{L^{q}}\,d\sigma.

Proof.

Apply Lemma
4.5
pointwise in
\sigma
to the integrand in
\mathcal{V}u(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\nabla e^{-(t-\sigma)A}A^{-1}u(\sigma)\,d\sigma
and integrate.
∎

Proposition 4.7

(Mixed-norm Volterra estimate)
.

Let
T>0
,
1<q\leq s<\infty
, and
\beta=\beta(q,s)
as in (
57
).

(i)

Suppose
\beta>0
. Let
1\leq p,r,a\leq\infty
satisfy

(59)

1+\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{p},\qquad a\beta<1.

Then

(60)

\|\mathcal{V}u\|_{L^{r}(0,T;L^{s})}\leq C\,T^{1/a-\beta}\,\|u\|_{L^{p}(0,T;L^{q})}.

(ii)

Suppose
\beta=0
. Then for every
1\leq p\leq\infty
,

(61)

\|\mathcal{V}u\|_{L^{p}(0,T;L^{s})}\leq C\,T\,\|u\|_{L^{p}(0,T;L^{q})}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.6
,
\|\mathcal{V}u(t)\|_{L^{s}}\leq C(k_{\beta}\star\|u\|_{L^{q}})(t)
, where
k_{\beta}(\theta)=\theta^{-\beta}\mathbf{1}_{(0,T)}(\theta)
and
\star
denotes convolution on the half-line (extending by zero outside
(0,T)
).

For
\beta>0
and
a\beta<1
,
k_{\beta}\in L^{a}(0,T)
with
\|k_{\beta}\|_{L^{a}(0,T)}=((1-a\beta)^{-1}T^{1-a\beta})^{1/a}\leq C_{\beta,a}T^{1/a-\beta}
. Young’s convolution inequality (
59
) gives

\|\mathcal{V}u\|_{L^{r}(0,T;L^{s})}\leq C\|k_{\beta}\|_{L^{a}(0,T)}\|u\|_{L^{p}(0,T;L^{q})},

yielding (
60
). For
\beta=0
, (
58
) gives
\|\mathcal{V}u(t)\|_{L^{s}}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|u(\sigma)\|_{L^{q}}\,d\sigma\leq C\,T^{1-1/p}\|u\|_{L^{p}(0,T;L^{q})}
(Hölder), and (
61
) follows.
∎

Theorem 4.8

(MEP drift estimate)
.

Let
T>0
,
1<q\leq s<\infty
, and let exponents satisfy the hypotheses of Proposition
4.7
. Suppose
c_{0}\in W^{1,s}(\Omega)
and
u\in L^{p}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))
. Then the MEP signal satisfies

(62)

\|\nabla c\|_{L^{r}(0,T;L^{s})}\leq CT^{1/r}\|\nabla c_{0}\|_{L^{s}}+CT^{1/a-\beta(q,s)}\|u\|_{L^{p}(0,T;L^{q})}

when
\beta(q,s)>0
. If
\beta(q,s)=0
, the second term is replaced by
CT\|u\|_{L^{p}(0,T;L^{q})}
with
r=p
.

Proof.

From (
52
),
\|\nabla c(t)\|_{L^{s}}\leq\|\nabla e^{-tA}c_{0}\|_{L^{s}}+\|\mathcal{V}u(t)\|_{L^{s}}
. The semigroup bound
\|\nabla e^{-tA}c_{0}\|_{L^{s}}\leq C\|\nabla c_{0}\|_{L^{s}}
gives the first term of (
62
); the second is Proposition
4.7
.
∎

Remark 4.9

(Space–time, not static, criticality)
.

Theorem
4.8
is the correct form of estimate for MEP: smoothing appears through a time convolution. Importing the PES critical exponent
q_{c}=N/4
from Proposition
2.3
is not legitimate without additional control on the time variation of
u
. Any threshold theory for MEP must be expressed in mixed norms.

4.4.
Mild formulation and continuation criterion

The density equation in MEP, written in divergence form
\partial_{t}u=\Delta u-\chi\nabla\!\cdot(u\nabla c)
, admits the mild formulation

(63)

u(t)=e^{t\Delta}u_{0}-\chi\int_{0}^{t}\nabla e^{(t-s)\Delta}\cdot\bigl(u(s)\nabla c(s)\bigr)\,ds,

coupled to
\nabla c=\nabla e^{-tA}c_{0}+\mathcal{V}u
via Proposition
4.1
.

Proposition 4.10

(Classical continuation criterion)
.

Let
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
u_{0}\geq 0
and
c_{0}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
. Let
(u,c)
be a classical solution of (
MEP
) on
[0,T_{\max})
,
T_{\max}\in(0,\infty]
. If
T<T_{\max}
and

(64)

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty})}+\|\nabla c\|_{L^{1}(0,T;W^{1,\infty})}<\infty,

then the solution extends past
T
. In particular, if
T_{\max}<\infty
, then

(65)

\limsup_{T\uparrow T_{\max}}\Bigl[\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty})}+\|\nabla c\|_{L^{1}(0,T;W^{1,\infty})}\Bigr]=\infty.

Proof.

The hypothesis (
64
) ensures that the drift
\nabla c\in L^{1}(0,T;W^{1,\infty})
and
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty})
, so the density equation
\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=-\chi\nabla\!\cdot(u\nabla c)
is a uniformly parabolic equation with controlled lower-order coefficients. Standard parabolic regularity (cf.
[
65
]
) yields Hölder estimates and then classical bounds for
u
up to time
T
, depending only on the quantities in (
64
) and the initial data. The signal equation
\partial_{t}c+Ac=A^{-1}u
is linear parabolic with
L^{\infty}
right-hand side, so
c
acquires corresponding regularity. The classical local existence theorem then extends the solution past
T
.
∎

Remark 4.11

(No three-dimensional mass-critical theorem)
.

The estimates above do
not
imply a three-dimensional mass-critical theorem for MEP. The near-diagonal multiplier has Keller–Segel order
-1
, whereas the frozen-time average has fourth-order order
-3
. Whether the Volterra memory produces a new effective critical threshold, distinct from both the Keller–Segel and PES thresholds, requires a scale-invariant mixed-norm theory of the nonlinear map

u\longmapsto\nabla\!\cdot\!\left(u\int_{0}^{t}\nabla e^{-(t-s)A}A^{-1}u(s)\,ds\right).

This question is formulated as Problem
5.4
in Section
5
.

5.
Open threshold problems and conditional statements

The structural results of the previous sections settle the operator classification but leave open the corresponding sharp threshold theory. We record here the precise problems whose resolution would complete the picture, together with conditional statements that follow immediately from the structural analysis.

5.1.
Sharp
K_{\tau}
-adapted Adams/log-HLS inequality for PES

In view of the four-dimensional logarithmic kernel (Proposition
2.6
) and the concentration scaling (Proposition
3.4
), the natural sharp inequality is the fourth-order Adams/log-HLS analogue of the two-dimensional Beckner log-HLS inequality
[
3
]
. Let

(66)

\mathcal{A}_{M}(\Omega):=\left\{u\geq 0:\ \int_{\Omega}u\,dx=M,\ \int_{\Omega}u(1+|\log u|)\,dx<\infty\right\}.

The condition
\int u|\log u|<\infty
ensures that the entropy
\int u\log u
is finite
and
that the inequality (
67
) below is non-vacuous:
\int u\log u
can be negative, so the inequality is meaningful only when the entropy side is bounded below. On a bounded domain
\Omega
this is automatic from the elementary bound
u\log u\geq-1/e
. On
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, finiteness must be supplemented by a tightness or moment condition such as
\int|x|^{2}u\,dx<\infty
to ensure that
\int u\log u
is bounded below on
\mathcal{A}_{M}
; this is standard in two-dimensional Keller–Segel theory
[
5
]
. We adopt this convention throughout.

Problem 5.1

(Sharp
K_{\tau}
-adapted log-HLS/Adams inequality)
.

Let
N=4
and let
\Omega
be either a bounded
C^{\infty}
Neumann domain,
\mathbb{T}^{4}
, or
\mathbb{R}^{4}
(in the last case with the moment normalisation above). Determine the sharp constants for which

(67)

\int_{\Omega}u\,K_{\tau}u\,dx\leq a_{\sharp}(M,\tau,\Omega)\int_{\Omega}u\log u\,dx+C(M,\tau,\Omega)

holds for all
u\in\mathcal{A}_{M}(\Omega)
, with
a_{\sharp}
and
C
depending only on
M
,
\tau
, and
\Omega
(and on the boundary condition). Since
\int u\log u
may take both signs, the meaningful subcritical condition is
\tfrac{\chi}{2}a_{\sharp}<1
, which by Proposition
5.5
converts the inequality into a bound on the free energy modulo a finite additive constant.

The one-point concentration computation forces any sharp inequality of the form (
67
) to satisfy the lower constraint

(68)

a_{\sharp}(M,\tau,\Omega)\geq\frac{M}{32\pi^{2}\tau}

at the logarithmic concentration scale. Indeed, for the concentrating family
u_{\varepsilon}
of (
45
), Lemmas
3.2
–
3.3
give

\frac{\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\,K_{\tau}u_{\varepsilon}\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\log u_{\varepsilon}\,dx}=\frac{M}{32\pi^{2}\tau}+o(1),\qquad\varepsilon\downarrow 0,

so any constant
a
for which (
67
) holds uniformly along this family must satisfy
a\geq M/(32\pi^{2}\tau)
, with equality forced if the inequality is to be saturated by one-point concentration. The natural conjectural sharp form is therefore

(69)

a_{\sharp}(M,\tau,\Omega)=\frac{M}{32\pi^{2}\tau},

up to lower-order domain-dependent corrections absorbed into
C(M,\tau,\Omega)
. With this value, the subcritical condition
\tfrac{\chi}{2}a_{\sharp}<1
becomes exactly

M<\frac{64\pi^{2}\tau}{\chi}=M_{\!*},

matching the candidate critical mass of Proposition
3.4
. This consistency is the heuristic basis for the following conjecture.

Conjecture 5.2

(Sharp threshold for PES free energy in
N=4
)
.

Let
N=4
and let
\Omega
be
\mathbb{R}^{4}
,
\mathbb{T}^{4}
, or a bounded
C^{2}
Neumann domain. The PES free energy
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}
satisfies the alternative:

(i)

if
0<M<M_{\!*}
, then
\inf_{u\in\mathcal{A}_{M}(\Omega)}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u]>-\infty

(
with the usual tightness or moment condition on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
to control the entropy from below
)
;

(ii)

if
M>M_{\!*}
, then
\inf_{u\in\mathcal{A}_{M}(\Omega)}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u]=-\infty
;

(iii)

at
M=M_{\!*}
, loss of compactness occurs through one-point mass concentration, with the precise compactness alternative depending on the domain and on the boundary conditions.

Item (ii) is established (Corollary
3.5
). The crux of the conjecture is item (i), which is equivalent to Problem
5.1
together with the appropriate Cauchy–Schwarz/Jensen analysis. The fourth-order Adams inequality of
[
47
]
and its sharp form in
\mathbb{R}^{4}

[
66
]
provide an exponential integrability framework but do not directly imply a sharp log-HLS form adapted to the specific kernel
K_{\tau}
; the natural conjectural form is dictated by (
69
).

5.2.
Supercritical PES dynamics

For
M>M_{\!*}
, Corollary
3.5
produces sequences with
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u_{\varepsilon}]\to-\infty
. The entropy-dissipation identity (
43
) implies that smooth PES solutions cannot increase their free energy, but this does not by itself yield dynamical concentration.

Problem 5.3

(Supercritical PES dynamics in
N=4
)
.

Let
N=4
and
M>M_{\!*}
. Determine:

(i)

whether negative free energy implies finite-time blow-up of PES;

(ii)

if blow-up occurs, whether the concentrating profile is governed by the fourth-order logarithmic kernel
(8\pi^{2}\tau)^{-1}\log(1/|x-y|)
, in the sense of a refined profile/quantisation theory analogous to
[
20
]
;

(iii)

whether, in the absence of finite-time blow-up, infinite-time aggregation can occur, in analogy with the indirect-production critical-mass phenomenon of
[
12
]
;

(iv)

at the threshold
M=M_{\!*}
, whether mass quantisation holds.

A complete answer requires more than the energy identity: it requires a dynamical mechanism (virial identity, monotonicity formula, or sharp compactness) linking the fourth-order logarithmic attraction to spatial concentration.

5.3.
Memory criticality for MEP

For MEP, the structural difficulty is that the drift cannot be replaced by a static operator: Remark
4.4
forbids transferring the PES exponent
q_{c}=N/4
via the frozen-time average alone. A proper threshold theory must operate at the level of the bilinear nonlinearity, in mixed space–time norms.

Problem 5.4

(MEP memory criticality)
.

Develop a critical theory for the MEP system (
MEP
) based on the Volterra drift
\mathcal{V}u(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\nabla e^{-(t-s)A}A^{-1}u(s)\,ds
. Specifically, determine whether there exists a Banach space
X
of densities satisfying:

(i)

X
is invariant (or critical) under the parabolic scaling of the density equation;

(ii)

the Volterra estimate
(
Theorem
4.8
)
closes the drift bound:
u\in X
implies
\nabla c\in Y
for a drift space
Y
controlling
\nabla\!\cdot(u\nabla c)
;

(iii)

the bilinear map

(u,v)\longmapsto\int_{0}^{t}\nabla e^{(t-s)\Delta}\!\cdot\!\left[u(s)\int_{0}^{s}\nabla e^{-(s-\sigma)A}A^{-1}v(\sigma)\,d\sigma\right]ds

is bounded on
X\times X\to X
;

(iv)

the continuation criterion of Proposition
4.10
can be replaced by a critical or subcritical condition in
X
;

(v)

the resulting threshold distinguishes whether MEP exhibits Keller–Segel-type criticality, PES-type averaged criticality, or a genuinely new memory-induced threshold.

The interaction between the near-diagonal Keller–Segel order
-1
and the frozen-time averaged order
-3
is the essential open question for MEP. We do not assert any specific critical exponent in advance of the resolution of Problem
5.4
.

5.4.
Conditional statements

We close with two conditional statements that follow immediately from the structural analysis. Both record what is implied by, respectively, an affirmative resolution of Problem
5.1
and a choice of admissible space
X
in Problem
5.4
.

Proposition 5.5

(Conditional PES lower bound)
.

Let
N=4
,
M>0
, and suppose Problem
5.1
admits a solution: there exist constants
a(M,\tau,\Omega)
and
C(M,\tau,\Omega)
such that

(70)

\int_{\Omega}u\,K_{\tau}u\,dx\leq a(M,\tau,\Omega)\int_{\Omega}u\log u\,dx+C(M,\tau,\Omega)

for all
u\in\mathcal{A}_{M}(\Omega)
, with
\tfrac{\chi}{2}a(M,\tau,\Omega)<1
. Then

(71)

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u]\geq\Bigl(1-\tfrac{\chi}{2}a(M,\tau,\Omega)\Bigr)\!\int_{\Omega}u\log u\,dx-\tfrac{\chi}{2}C(M,\tau,\Omega),

and consequently
\inf_{u\in\mathcal{A}_{M}(\Omega)}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u]>-\infty
(under the usual tightness assumption on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
).

Proof.

Insert (
70
) into the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}
:

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PES}}[u]=\int u\log u-\tfrac{\chi}{2}\int uK_{\tau}u\geq\Bigl(1-\tfrac{\chi}{2}a(M,\tau,\Omega)\Bigr)\!\int u\log u-\tfrac{\chi}{2}C(M,\tau,\Omega).

The coefficient is strictly positive by hypothesis, and
\int u\log u
is bounded below on
\mathcal{A}_{M}
at fixed mass (using Jensen’s inequality on bounded domains, or moment-Jensen on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
).
∎

Proposition 5.6

(Conditional MEP continuation)
.

Let
(u,c)
be a classical MEP solution on
[0,T_{\max})
. Suppose there exists a mixed-norm space
X
such that, for any
T<T_{\max}
,

(72)

\|u\|_{X([0,T])}<\infty\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\|\nabla c\|_{L^{1}(0,T;W^{1,\infty})}<\infty,

through the Volterra representation (
51
). If, in addition,
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{\max};L^{\infty})}<\infty
, then
T_{\max}=\infty
.

Proof.

Combine the hypothesis with Proposition
4.10
.
∎

5.5.
Conclusion

The mathematical analysis reveals that the physical assumption of instantaneous equilibration versus transient kinetic memory fundamentally alters the macroscopic pattern-formation thresholds. Specifically, we identify two distinct criticality classes for indirect signal-generation chemotaxis cascades:

(i)

PES
is a
static fourth-order elliptic
chemotaxis system. The eliminated interaction
c=K_{\tau}u
is positive, self-adjoint, and fourth-order smoothing; the drift has principal order
-3
; the critical scaling is
u_{\lambda}(x,t)=\lambda^{4}u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)
with
q_{c}=N/4
; mass is critical in
N=4
; and the four-dimensional kernel has logarithmic local coefficient
1/(8\pi^{2}\tau)
. The variational candidate critical mass is

M_{\!*}=\frac{64\pi^{2}\tau}{\chi}.

Promoting
M_{\!*}
to a sharp threshold reduces to the sharp
K_{\tau}
-adapted Adams/log-HLS inequality of Problem
5.1
.

(ii)

MEP
is a
Volterra-memory
chemotaxis system. Its signal law is
c(t)=e^{-tA}c_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)A}A^{-1}u(s)\,ds
. Its near-diagonal drift has Keller–Segel order
-1
; its frozen-time average has fourth-order order
-3
. The two competing features prevent a static fourth-order elliptic reduction. The corresponding threshold problem is a genuinely mixed parabolic–Volterra problem (Problem
5.4
).

The fundamental conclusion is that
PES and MEP belong to different criticality classes.
PES belongs to the fourth-order elliptic Adams/log-HLS class, while MEP belongs to a Volterra-memory class whose critical behaviour cannot be inferred from the PES scaling, nor from a static elliptic argument of any kind. Recognising this operator-theoretic separation is an essential first step for correctly formulating critical mass phenomena in realistic multiscale models, where the intermediate kinetics of microenvironments often retain critical temporal memory.
