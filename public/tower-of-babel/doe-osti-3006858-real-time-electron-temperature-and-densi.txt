Executive Summary

This project set out to design, build, and deploy a real-time multi-point Thomson scattering (rt-
MPTS) system for NSTX-U, capable of providing electron temperature and density profiles—and
their uncertainties—in real time for use in equilibrium reconstruction and plasma control. The
long-term goal was to move Thomson scattering from a purely post-shot diagnostic to a central
element of real-time situation awareness and feedback control on NSTX-U.

Over the course of the award, we completed the full end-to-end rt-MPTS system. All major
hardware has been purchased, installed, and integrated; the real-time analysis software has been
developed, optimized, and benchmarked; and the interface to the NSTX-U Plasma Control System
(PCS) has been designed and tested. The entire system has been demonstrated in a bench-top
configuration and with recorded NSTX-U data. Operationally, the rt-MPTS system is ready for
commissioning as soon as NSTX-U resumes routine plasma operation.

Because NSTX-U was not available for most of the project period, a large fraction of the effort
naturally shifted to non-NSTX-U activities that nevertheless directly advance the goals of this
grant. In particular, we:

 • Implemented and tested a real-time Thomson-based profile reconstruction and partial
 kinetic rt-EFIT workflow on DIII-D.
 • Built and deployed a version of the rt-MPTS system at the LHD stellarator in Japan, based
 on the same hardware and software concepts, and successfully demonstrated real-time
 electron temperature measurements there.
 • Developed and applied real-time and near-real-time equilibrium reconstruction, profile
 fitting, and control algorithms on DIII-D that are directly portable to NSTX-U once it
 returns to operation.

These activities, combined with the complete construction of the NSTX-U rt-MPTS system, fulfill
the main technical goals of the project that were under our control, and they demonstrate the
broader value of real-time Thomson-based profile information for disruption avoidance, pedestal
control, and advanced confinement scenario development.

1. Overview of the Project

The original motivation for this project was the recognition that feed-forward control alone cannot
access or sustain many of the high-performance regimes of interest on NSTX-U. Historically,
Thomson scattering has been treated almost exclusively as a post-shot diagnostic: the Nd:YAG
lasers fire during the discharge, the scattered light is collected and digitized, and a detailed analysis
is performed after the shot to reconstruct electron temperature Te and density ne profiles. The results
are then used to design the next discharge.

In the upgraded NSTX-U, the need for real-time profile information is especially acute. The device
aims to operate at high normalized beta and in advanced confinement regimes that are close to
stability boundaries. The real-time equilibrium reconstruction code (rt-EFIT) has already been
prepared to accept kinetic constraints, and a real-time MSE system is being developed to provide

current profile information. What is missing is a real-time Thomson scattering diagnostic capable
of supplying density and temperature profiles with quantified uncertainties fast enough for use in
feedback control and disruption avoidance.

The central objective of this grant was therefore to transform the existing MPTS system on NSTX-
U into a real-time diagnostic: to acquire the multiplexed Thomson signals, perform the full Selden-
based spectral analysis in real time, and deliver Te(r), ne (r), and their associated uncertainties shot-
by-shot and time-point-by-time-point to both the PCS and the data system.

In parallel, the project aimed to integrate these real-time profiles into a partial kinetic rt-EFIT
workflow and to demonstrate the benefits of such a system for pedestal control and disruption
avoidance, both on NSTX-U and, in practice, on other machines (DIII-D, LHD) during the NSTX-
U outage.

2. Development of the Real-time MPTS System on NSTX-U
2.1 Diagnostic context and measurement principle

The NSTX-U MPTS system provides 42 radial channels spanning the full plasma cross section,
from the low-field side to the high-field side. Nd:YAG lasers fire at 60 Hz, and the scattered
photons are collected by polychromators viewing the midplane. Each polychromator splits the
scattered light into several spectral bands, which are detected by avalanche photodiodes and pre-
amplifiers. The result is a multiplexed train of analog pulses at each radial location corresponding
to different wavelength bands and time windows; each train encodes the local Thomson spectrum
from which electron temperature (Te) profile and electron density (ne) profile can be recovered.

section of NSTX-U with the MPTS channel locations, (b) typical electron temperature (Te) profile,
(c) enlarged channel locations, and (d) a typical electron density (n e) profile. The profiles of Te and
ne are measured across the full plasma cross section from the LFS to the HFS. The horizontal bars
 on the profiles indicate the projection of the fiber bundle width on the laser path, mapped to the

In the pre-existing configuration, these signals were digitized with CAMAC hardware and
analyzed after the shot. The first task of this project was to replace that acquisition chain and build
a new dedicated rt-MPTS system capable of acquiring and processing all channels fast enough for
real-time use.

2.2 Real-time capable hardware

The hardware architecture that was ultimately implemented is sketched in Figure 2. Each
polychromator output is cabled to a modern high-speed analog-to-digital converter, and the digital
data are streamed to a dedicated real-time server, which in turn outputs the processed results both
to the PCS and to the MDSplus data system.

Figure 2. Sketch of the hardware setup: (left) polychromator with up to six interference filters (not
illustrated), APDs, and pre-amplifiers; (middle left) RT-analog digital converter (ADC); (middle
right) RT-MPTS PC; and (right) output to the NSTX-U PCS and MDSplus data storage. The
polychromator detects the scattered photons, which are digitized by the RT-ADC and analyzed in
the RT-MPTS PC. The evaluated Te and ne are outputted to the NSTX-U PCS and exported to the
MDSplus data storage [Lagner].

Concretely, we:

 • Replaced the legacy CAMAC digitizers with commercially available SIS3316-250-14
 ADCs from Struck Innovative Systeme. These digitizers sample at 250 Msamples/s with
 14-bit resolution and offer two switchable voltage ranges tailored to the small Thomson
 signals. They were selected not only for their speed and dynamic range but also for their
 ability to support a custom firmware upgrade that allows real-time Ethernet “push”
 streaming of data from all channels.
 • Procured enough SIS3316 modules to cover the existing 20 new spatial channels installed
 under the previous grant and added capacity for 22 additional channels, for a total of 42.
 This completes the radial coverage of the MPTS system in a single real-time framework
 and allows retirement of the older CAMAC hardware for this purpose.
 • Installed these digitizers in a new electronics crate and integrated them into a dedicated
 rack in the NSTX-U Thomson room. All cabling from polychromators to digitizers and
 from digitizers to the server has been installed and end-to-end tested at the hardware level.
 • Installed a SuperMicro real-time server with 20 CPU cores and ample memory, equipped
 with a low-latency Solarflare (now Xilinx) Ethernet card for communication with the

 digitizers and a General Standards analog output card with 64 channels for sending
 processed data to the PCS. The server is the heart of the rt-MPTS system: it receives the
 raw waveforms, analyzes the spectra, and outputs the multiplexed Te, ne, and error signals.

A key design choice was to make the rt-MPTS system largely independent of the NSTX-U PCS
timing. The ADCs stream their data to the rt-MPTS server; the server performs the analysis and
then uses its own analog output card to send the processed signals to the PCS. This architecture
minimizes synchronization requirements and allows us to test and validate the rt-MPTS system in
“piggyback” mode during routine operations without burdening the PCS servers. It also provides
a straightforward path for scaling the system to more channels or more complex analysis by
upgrading the dedicated server rather than modifying the PCS.

2.3 Real-time Thomson analysis software

In parallel with the hardware work, we implemented a new C++11-based real-time Thomson
analysis code specifically optimized for speed and robustness. The code architecture follows a
clear separation of tasks: reader threads handle data acquisition from the ADCs, analysis threads
perform the spectrum fitting for each radial channel, and an output thread encodes the final Te, ne
values and their uncertainties into analog voltages for the PCS.

which continuously stores data from the RT-ADCs. When the laser is pulsed (red arrow), the buffer
 is read out and transferred to the analysis module, which calculates Te and ne that are then
 outputted to the NSTX-U PCS [Lagner].

The real-time workflow can be summarized as follows:

 1. Pre-shot initialization: At the beginning of each discharge, the rt-MPTS server loads all
 required calibration data (filter transmission curves, APD gains, Rayleigh and Raman
 calibration factors, scattering geometry, etc.) from MDSplus. It then pre-computes an
 intensity lookup table by evaluating the Selden relativistic Thomson model for a wide range
 of Te values and convolving with the spectral response of each polychromator. This lookup
 table maps an assumed temperature to a set of expected intensities in each spectral channel.
 2. Real-time acquisition and buffering: During the discharge, the SIS3316 ADCs stream
 digitized waveforms into a circular buffer on the server. The buffer stores the laser trigger
 signals, integrating sphere (laser energy) signals, and all spectral channel signals for each
 radial position. The buffer is read in response to each laser pulse, ensuring that the full
 waveforms for all channels at that time point are captured.
 3. Spectrum analysis: For each radial channel and time point, the analysis module detects
 the photon pulses, subtracts offsets, and fits the measured intensities to the pre-computed
 lookup table via a \chi^2 minimization. The output of this fit is the local Te, its uncertainty,
 and a normalization corresponding to the scattered photon flux. The normalization is then
 converted into ne using Raman or Rayleigh calibration.
 4. Output and error handling: The resulting Te, ne, and their uncertainties are mapped to
 analog voltages on the output card and sent to the PCS. A simple but robust error-coding
 scheme is used: for each channel and time point, status bits indicate whether the fit
 converged, whether the result is within a reasonable physical range, and whether data were
 missing or saturated. In parallel, all raw waveforms and processed results are written to
 MDSplus for post-shot analysis.

The pre-computation of the intensity lookup table is central to achieving real-time performance.
Instead of evaluating the Selden model repeatedly during the discharge, we perform this heavy
computation once per shot and store the results in a hash table indexed by Te. In real time, the
fitting routine need only search this pre-computed table for the temperature value that best matches

the measured intensities, which is much faster than repeatedly computing the underlying physics
model.

2.4 Benchmarking and validation

Extensive benchmarking was carried out to ensure that the new real-time code reproduces the
results of the established post-shot Thomson analysis. To do this, we took post-shot PA (photo-
amplifier) data from NSTX-U, fed them through the rt-MPTS analysis pipeline, and compared the
resulting Te and ne time traces channel-by-channel with the standard offline analysis.

Figure 5. Fit comparisons of RT and post-shot TS evaluation: (a) electron temperature (Te) and (b)
 electron density (ne) values for a core (red) and an edge (blue) channel. Within the measurement
 uncertainties, the RT evaluation is capable of reproducing the post-shot evaluated Te and ne
 [Lagner].

Within measurement uncertainties, the agreement between the two codes is excellent: the real-time
code reproduces both core and edge temperatures and densities across the full time range of
representative discharges. The primary advantage of the real-time code is speed: the fitting of one
spectrum for one radial channel takes less than 2.5 ms, and multiple channels can be processed in
parallel. For a 60 Hz laser repetition rate and 42 channels, this satisfies the real-time requirement
with margin.

These tests demonstrate that the real-time analysis is accurate enough to replace the post-shot
analysis for the purposes of control and equilibrium reconstruction, and they validate the basic
architecture of the rt-MPTS system.

2.5 Status at the end of the award

By the end of the award period, the rt-MPTS system for NSTX-U is complete at the hardware and
software levels. The digitizers, server, network connections, and analog outputs have been
installed and tested. The real-time analysis code has been benchmarked against the offline analysis
and shown to be both accurate and fast. The system has been exercised with real NSTX-U data
and synthetic tests.

Because NSTX-U has not been in routine operation, full integration with the living PCS and on-
plasma commissioning have not yet occurred. However, no further hardware development is

required: from the perspective of this grant, “the whole system” has been built, tested, and prepared
for deployment.

3. Extension to Other Devices: LHD and International Impact

One of the clearest indicators of the success and utility of the rt-MPTS concept has been its
adoption by other facilities. During the project period, the LHD stellarator team in Japan
approached us and asked that we build a version of the rt-MPTS system for their Thomson
scattering diagnostic.

We accepted this request and, drawing directly on the architecture developed for NSTX-U,
implemented an rt-MPTS system at LHD using essentially the same SIS digitizers and a similar
real-time analysis server. The software was adapted to the specific geometry and calibration of the
LHD Thomson system, but the basic design—pre-computation of Selden-based lookup tables,
real-time fitting of spectra, and output to control—remained unchanged.

Figure 6: The digitizer cards and the real time server (right) as implemented at LHD. The rt-MPTS
Te result is compared with the offline TS system (bottom left), with the rt-MPTS system in red and
 reference in blue.

During the Spring 2020 LHD campaign, data and calibration from LHD’s Thomson channels were
made available to the rt-MPTS system, and we demonstrated real-time electron temperature
measurements over a range of discharges. For the initial implementation, the real-time
temperatures agreed with LHD’s offline analysis within uncertainties, confirming that the NSTX-
U rt-MPTS design is portable across devices and magnetic configurations.

Also a copy of this rt-MPTS system was proposed to W7-X and now being used there. These
international adoptions underscore the broader value of the real-time Thomson concept and reflect
favorably on the impact of this DOE investment.

4. Partial Kinetic rt-EFIT and Real-time Equilibrium Reconstruction

While the rt-MPTS system is itself a major hardware and software achievement, its real scientific
value lies in how the resulting profiles are used. A central goal of this project was therefore to feed
real-time Thomson-based profiles into rt-EFIT, enabling partial kinetic equilibrium reconstruction
in real time.

On NSTX-U, the plan is to use the rt-MPTS system to provide ne (r), Te(r), and their uncertainties,
which can be transformed into flux coordinates and fit with modified hyperbolic tangent functions
in the pedestal and polynomial functions in the core. These fits then yield an electron pressure
profile p_e(\psi). With simple assumptions or auxiliary diagnostics (e.g. CHERS for ion
temperature), total pressure profiles can also be estimated. These pressure constraints, alongside
magnetic and MSE data, are passed to rt-EFIT, which then solves the Grad–Shafranov equation
with both magnetic and kinetic constraints.

Because NSTX-U was not available, this approach was first implemented and tested on DIII-D,
which uses the same rt-EFIT code base as NSTX-U. On DIII-D, real-time Thomson data are
already available, and we deployed a real-time fitting routine for these data similar to what is
envisioned for NSTX-U. The resulting pressure profiles were then supplied to rt-EFIT together
with magnetic measurements, leading to “partial kinetic” real-time equilibria in which the current
profile and pressure pedestal are much better constrained than in magnetics-only reconstructions.

Experiments on DIII-D have shown that these kinetically constrained equilibria capture key
physical features—such as the bootstrap current peak in the pedestal and the detailed shape of the
pressure profile—that are essential for accurate stability calculations and control. These DIII-D
results are a proof-of-principle for the NSTX-U implementation: once the rt-MPTS system there
is commissioned, a very similar workflow can be used to construct partial kinetic rt-equilibria for
every discharge.

The project has also leveraged the CAKE (Consistent Automatic Kinetic Equilibrium) tool
developed by our group for offline kinetic equilibrium reconstruction. CAKE generates large
databases of high-quality kinetic equilibria with carefully controlled Grad–Shafranov error, and
these equilibria are used as a benchmark against which to test the real-time versions. Lessons
learned from CAKE about profile fitting, outlier rejection, and EFIT parameter choices directly
inform the design of the real-time algorithms and are especially helpful for ensuring robustness
across a wide range of plasma regimes.

5. Control Applications and Non-NSTX-U Work Enabled by NSTX-U
Downtime

The extended NSTX-U outage forced a rebalancing of effort toward other devices, but it did not
change the fundamental nature of the work. Instead, it shifted the focus to demonstrating, on DIII-
D and LHD, the kinds of real-time control capabilities that rt-MPTS is intended to unlock on
NSTX-U. Much of this work was carried out under other awards but built directly on the systems
and expertise developed in this project.

On DIII-D, we used Thomson and other diagnostics in real-time feedback loops to control pedestal
density and pressure, to regulate divertor detachment, and to access and sustain advanced regimes
such as Super-H mode. These control experiments rely on precisely the kind of rapidly updated
profile information that rt-MPTS can provide on NSTX-U. The same group has also demonstrated
edge density and pedestal control for ELM suppression and mitigation using gas puffing and 3D
magnetic perturbations, and has implemented feedback on divertor temperature and detachment
location based on divertor Thomson measurements.

The project has contributed to the development of real-time equilibrium and stability analysis tools
such as STRIDE and parallelized DCON, which can compute ideal and resistive MHD stability
metrics fast enough for use in feedback. These tools have been deployed on DIII-D and are natural
complements to real-time kinetic equilibria: once the pressure and current profiles are accurately
reconstructed, they can be fed into these codes to estimate stability margins in real time and trigger
control actions when margins are crossed.

In addition, the experience gained in developing rt-MPTS—both for NSTX-U and LHD—has fed
into a series of AI-based diagnostic and control tools. For example, neural-network-based profile
reconstruction and surrogate models for kinetic equilibrium have been developed that can
reproduce the results of full kinetic equilibrium calculations at real-time speeds. Multimodal
“Diag2Diag” techniques have been used to reconstruct synthetic Thomson-like signals from other
faster diagnostics, effectively providing temporal “super-resolution” for Te and ne without
increasing laser repetition rates. While these AI tools were developed primarily on DIII-D data,
they are conceptually and technically aligned with the rt-MPTS framework and are expected to be
directly useful for NSTX-U once it resumes operations.

From the perspective of this grant, the key point is that the downtime of NSTX-U did not halt
progress toward real-time, profile-based control. Instead, it prompted the group to demonstrate
these ideas more broadly on other machines, validate the algorithms and hardware architectures in
real operation, and build a toolkit that will be immediately available for NSTX-U.

6. Publications and Other Research Outputs

The work supported by this award has led to a substantial number of journal articles, conference
presentations, and technical reports. These outputs fall into three broad categories:

 1. Core rt-MPTS and Thomson analysis work: This includes publications describing the
 scalable real-time Thomson analysis framework, its application to NSTX-U, and the
 development of real-time Thomson software for NSTX-U and other machines. These
 papers document the architectural choices, benchmarks, and early test results that underpin
 the system described in this report.
 2. Real-time equilibrium, profile fitting, and control: Several publications report on the
 integration of kinetic constraints into real-time equilibrium reconstruction on DIII-D, the
 use of real-time profiles for pedestal and divertor control, and related control experiments.
 These works use Thomson-based profiles—either directly or as part of a broader diagnostic
 suite—to guide real-time actuator decisions and explore advanced confinement regimes.

 3. Extensions to AI-assisted diagnostics and stability control: More recent papers focus on
 machine-learning-based real-time kinetic profile reconstruction, multimodal diagnostic
 super-resolution, and interpretable AI for tearing-mode stability prediction. While some of
 these studies are funded partially by other awards, all of them build directly on the
 diagnostic, equilibrium, and control infrastructure that this project helped to establish.

In addition to formal publications, the project has produced software artifacts—most notably the
rt-MPTS code itself—that are in use by collaborators and that serve as a template for similar
systems being built on other devices.

Below we summarize the main papers that used this grant and briefly describe their contributions.

The NSTX-U overview paper “NSTX-U theory, modeling and analysis results”
[Guttenfelder2022] provides a comprehensive summary of recent analysis, theory, and modeling
activities for NSTX and NSTX-U. It integrates results on macrostability, energetic particle
transport, pedestal and core transport, and boundary physics, and documents how the modeling
and control capabilities—including real-time profile measurements and control tools developed
under this grant—support NSTX-U’s mission and inform next-step device design.

The Super H-mode paper “High fusion performance in Super H-mode experiments on Alcator C-
Mod and DIII-D” [Snyder2019] analyzes high-pedestal, high-performance regimes enabled by
EPED-guided pedestal optimization on Alcator C-Mod and DIII-D. It demonstrates that carefully
shaped edge profiles and pedestal control can substantially increase fusion performance, providing
much of the physics motivation for the real-time edge diagnostics and control algorithms further
developed in this project.

The paper “Initial operation and data processing on a system for real-time evaluation of Thomson
scattering signals on the Large Helical Device” [Hammond2021] reports the first deployment of
the real-time Thomson scattering (TS) evaluation framework, originally developed for NSTX-U,
on the Large Helical Device (LHD). It shows that the system can robustly acquire and process TS
signals shot-to-shot at the laser repetition rate, demonstrating portability of the software and
hardware architecture and delivering real-time Te and ne profiles in a stellarator environment—an
important non-NSTX-U deliverable during periods when NSTX-U was not operating.

In “Experimental evidence of ECCD-based NTM suppression threshold reduction during mode
locking on DIII-D” [Nelson2020], the team demonstrates that electron cyclotron current drive
(ECCD) applied during mode locking can substantially reduce the power threshold for neoclassical
tearing mode (NTM) suppression. This work combines targeted modeling with real-time control
to show how advanced feedback strategies can exploit locked-mode dynamics, directly informing
the control-oriented approach to profile and stability management developed under the present
grant.

The paper “Development of Real-Time Software for Thomson Scattering Analysis at NSTX-U”
[Rozenblat2019] focuses on the core software infrastructure of the real-time TS system. It
describes a multithreaded C++11 architecture that evaluates Te and ne within a 60 Hz cycle,
starting from digitized photon signals and delivering real-time outputs for the plasma control

system. This work constitutes a central deliverable of the grant, demonstrating that a full real-time
processing chain for multi-point TS can meet stringent latency requirements on NSTX-U.

In “Real-time pedestal optimization and ELM control with 3D fields and gas flows on DIII-D”
[Laggner2020], the authors implement and test multiple real-time control schemes that combine
ELM diagnostics, real-time edge profile measurements (including TS), 3D resonant magnetic
perturbations, and gas puffing. The paper shows that real-time control of the pedestal can both
access and sustain improved ELM suppression regimes, illustrating how the diagnostic and real-
time processing capabilities developed in this project translate directly into closed-loop control
strategies on a major tokamak.

The work “Interpretative SOL modeling throughout multiple ELM cycles in DIII-D”
[Nelson2021] uses interpretative scrape-off-layer (SOL) modeling to follow the evolution of SOL
conditions over repeated ELM cycles. By combining detailed SOL models with experimental data,
it quantifies how divertor conditions, recycling, and sheath physics interact with pedestal dynamics
and ELM behavior. This analysis supports the control-oriented objectives of the project by
clarifying how edge and divertor physics constrain achievable pedestal and ELM control
scenarios.

The article “Machine learning-based real-time kinetic profile reconstruction in DIII-D”
[Shousha2024] introduces a neural-network-based replacement for traditional CAKE-style kinetic
equilibrium reconstruction, designed for real-time deployment in the plasma control system.
Training on offline kinetic reconstructions, the model infers Te, Ti, ne, and related quantities in
milliseconds, enabling real-time kinetic EFIT and advanced control schemes that require full
profile information. This work is a direct realization of the project’s goal of real-time kinetic profile
reconstruction using modern machine learning tools.

Finally, “Unified ELM suppression on KSTAR and DIII-D via adaptive feedback control
strategies” [Shousha2025] demonstrates a unified, adaptive feedback controller for edge-localized
mode (ELM) suppression using resonant magnetic perturbations on both KSTAR and DIII-D. The
controller combines amplitude and phase control of RMP fields, multiple toroidal mode numbers,
and new “jump” and “probing” strategies to robustly maintain ELM suppression across devices.
This paper represents a culmination of the project’s control-oriented thrust: taking real-time
diagnostics, kinetic reconstructions, and control algorithms and deploying them in flexible,
machine-independent feedback strategies for ELM suppression.

Together, these publications document the full arc of the project: from the development and
deployment of real-time Thomson scattering hardware and software, through interpretative and
predictive modeling of edge and SOL physics, to machine-learning-enabled profile reconstruction
and advanced feedback control of NTMs and ELMs on multiple international fusion facilities.

7. Training, Personnel, and Workforce Development

A major outcome of this project has been the training of a new cohort of scientists and engineers
skilled in real-time diagnostics, high-performance computing, and control for fusion plasmas.
Graduate students and postdoctoral researchers have worked on all aspects of the rt-MPTS system:

hardware integration, firmware coordination with the digitizer vendor, real-time software design,
data pipelines to MDSplus, equilibrium reconstruction, and control algorithm development.

Ricardo Shousha and Oak Nelson completed their theses mostly funded by this grant and many
students are completing theses that rely heavily on the tools developed under this award—whether
in the context of NSTX-U, DIII-D, or LHD. Their work spans detailed Thomson analysis, kinetic
equilibrium reconstruction, control of edge and core instabilities, and the application of machine
learning to profile reconstruction and stability prediction.

From a broader workforce perspective, the project has helped to establish a culture of real-time
thinking within the diagnostic and control communities: diagnostics are no longer viewed solely
as post-shot analysis tools but as active participants in the control loop. This shift is essential for
the next generation of fusion devices, where operations near stability limits will be routine and
real-time situation awareness will be critical for both performance and machine protection.

8. Summary and Outlook

This final report summarizes the results of the DOE grant “Real-time Electron Temperature and
Density Profile Measurements for NSTX-U” (DE-SC0015480). The primary technical goal—to
design, build, and test a real-time Thomson scattering system for NSTX-U—has been achieved.
The rt-MPTS system comprises:

 • A complete set of modern high-speed digitizers for all 42 MPTS channels, installed and
 integrated at NSTX-U.
 • A dedicated real-time server with low-latency network interfaces and a multi-channel
 analog output card for communication with the PCS.
 • A fully implemented real-time Thomson analysis code, benchmarked against the standard
 offline analysis and demonstrated to meet accuracy and speed requirements.

In addition, the project has:

 • Developed and tested partial kinetic rt-EFIT workflows, using real-time Thomson-based
 profiles as pressure constraints in real-time equilibrium reconstruction.
 • Demonstrated the value of real-time profile information for control on DIII-D, including
 pedestal optimization, divertor detachment control, and advanced scenario access.
 • Exported the rt-MPTS concept to the LHD stellarator and initiated discussions with other
 international facilities.
 • Contributed to a broader ecosystem of AI-assisted diagnostics, equilibrium reconstruction
 tools, and stability analysis codes that are well aligned with the needs of future reactor-
 scale devices.

The main outstanding task is to commission the rt-MPTS system on NSTX-U itself once the device
returns to operation. At that point, the hardware and software completed under this grant, combined
with the algorithms and operational experience developed on DIII-D and LHD, will allow NSTX-
U to move quickly into a regime where real-time electron temperature and density profiles are

central to equilibrium reconstruction, disruption avoidance, and advanced confinement scenario
development.

In that sense, this project has not only delivered a working diagnostic system but has also helped
to define how real-time profile information will be used in practice on NSTX-U and other devices
in the ITER era and beyond.
