Associations of cumulative exposure and longitudinal change patterns of the triglyceride glucose–Chinese visceral adiposity index with incident cardiovascular disease in middle-aged and older adults: evidence from CHARLS
Authors: Huilin Liu, Ximin Zhang, Kangning Tang, Xiaojiao Zhang, Sijing Li, Ziru Li, Xiaoming Dong, Huili Liao, Wenjie Long, Zhenjie Zhuang, Shihao Ni, Zhongqi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe triglyceride glucose–Chinese visceral adiposity index (TyG-CVAI), an integrated marker of insulin resistance and visceral adiposity, has been associated with cardiovascular outcomes. However, the longitudinal cardiovascular implications of cumulative TyG-CVAI exposure and directional changes in TyG-CVAI remain insufficiently characterized. This study aimed to examine the associations of cumulative TyG-CVAI exposure and longitudinal TyG-CVAI change patterns with incident cardiovascular disease (CVD) among middle-aged and older Chinese adults.MethodsThis prospective study included 4,338 participants from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) who were free of CVD during the exposure assessment period from 2011 to 2015 and had complete TyG-CVAI data in 2011 and 2015. Participants were categorized into four TyG-CVAI change patterns (low–low, low–high, high–low, and high–high). Cumulative TyG-CVAI was calculated using repeated measurements from 2011 to 2015 and analyzed as both a standardized continuous variable and quartiles. Incident CVD was defined as new-onset heart disease or stroke during follow-up. Kaplan–Meier analysis, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis were performed.ResultsDuring a mean follow-up of 5 years, 845 incident CVD events occurred among 4,338 participants. Compared with the low–low group, the fully adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident CVD were 1.50 (1.21–1.88) for the low–high group, 1.64 (1.20–2.25) for the high–low group, and 1.77 (1.49–2.09) for the high–high group. When cumulative TyG-CVAI was analyzed as a standardized continuous variable, each 1-SD increase was associated with a higher risk of incident CVD after full adjustment (HR 1.10, 95% CI 1.06–1.14). When categorized into quartiles, participants in the highest quartile had a higher CVD risk than those in the lowest quartile (HR 1.91, 95% CI 1.56–2.33; P for trend < 0.001). RCS analysis revealed a significant nonlinear association between cumulative TyG-CVAI and incident CVD.ConclusionHigher cumulative TyG-CVAI burden and unfavorable longitudinal change patterns were associated with an increased risk of incident CVD among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. These findings suggest that repeated TyG-CVAI assessment may help identify individuals with sustained or worsening metabolic-visceral burden and support longitudinal cardiovascular risk stratification.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12933-026-03258-1.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death worldwide and continues to impose a substantial public health burden. According to the World Health Organization, CVD accounted for an estimated 19.8 million deaths globally in 2022, representing approximately 32% of all deaths [1, 2]. In China, CVD also remains one of the greatest threats to population health. Based on GBD 2023 data, the mortality rate of CVD in China reached 316.08 per 100,000, with a disability-adjusted life-year (DALY) rate of 6262.68 per 100,000 in 2023 [3]. Despite substantial advances in the management of traditional risk factors such as hypertension [4], dyslipidemia [5], and diabetes [6], considerable residual CVD risk persists, underscoring the need for practical and accessible markers that can improve early risk stratification and identify high-risk individuals before overt cardiovascular events occur.

Among the biological processes underlying this residual risk, insulin resistance (IR) [7–9] and visceral adiposity [10–12] have been recognized as two major pathophysiological drivers of CVD. Accordingly, increasing attention has been directed toward metabolic markers that reflect these abnormalities. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, calculated from fasting triglycerides (TG) and fasting blood glucose (FBG), has been proposed as a simple surrogate marker of IR and has been shown to be strongly associated with incident CVD [13, 14], stroke [13, 15], and other metabolic disorders [16–18]. The Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI), a sex-specific composite indicator derived from age, body mass index, waist circumference, triglycerides, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, was developed to estimate visceral adiposity in Chinese adults [19–21] and has also been associated with new-onset CVD [22, 23]. Given the synergistic interaction between IR and visceral fat accumulation, composite indices that capture both metabolic dysfunction and adiposity may provide additional information beyond single-domain markers [21, 24–26].

Consistent with this rationale, recent evidence supports the clinical relevance of TyG-CVAI as an integrated marker of metabolic-visceral burden. Zheng et al. [27] reported that higher baseline TyG-CVAI was independently associated with new-onset CVD in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Wang et al. [28] further showed that baseline TyG-CVAI, cumulative TyG-CVAI exposure, and dynamic changes in TyG-CVAI were all associated with new-onset stroke. In addition, Wang et al. [29] systematically compared longitudinal trajectories of the TyG index combined with multiple classical and novel obesity indices, including TyG-CVAI, and reported that these trajectories were associated with CVD risk. Nevertheless, prior studies have primarily focused on baseline levels, stroke-specific outcomes, or broad comparisons across multiple TyG-obesity derivatives. The cardiovascular implications of TyG-CVAI-specific cumulative burden and directional change patterns remain insufficiently clarified.

TyG-CVAI may not be adequately captured by a single baseline assessment. Because IR, lipid metabolism, and visceral adiposity can change over time, repeated measurements may better reflect the long-term metabolic burden relevant to cardiovascular risk. Increasing evidence indicates that cumulative exposure and dynamic changes in cardiometabolic markers may provide additional prognostic information beyond one-time measurements [28, 30, 31]. Thus, evaluating both cumulative burden and longitudinal change patterns of TyG-CVAI may help clarify the cardiovascular implications of repeated TyG-CVAI assessment.

Therefore, using data from CHARLS, the present study aimed to investigate the associations of longitudinal TyG-CVAI change patterns and cumulative TyG-CVAI exposure with incident CVD among middle-aged and older Chinese adults, and to evaluate the robustness of these associations using alternative longitudinal exposure definitions and sensitivity analyses. These findings may help clarify the long-term cardiovascular implications of TyG-CVAI and support its potential role as a simple and accessible marker for longitudinal cardiometabolic risk stratification.

Methods

Study population

This study was based on data from CHARLS, a nationally representative prospective cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults aged 45 years and above. CHARLS adopted a multistage, stratified probability sampling design and collected information through standardized questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory testing. Baseline data were collected in 2011, with subsequent follow-up surveys conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020 [32].

For the present analysis, we included participants who met the following criteria: (1) were aged 45 years or older at baseline (2011); (2) had complete data for TyG-CVAI calculation at both waves 1 (2011) and 3 (2015); (3) had no history of CVD and available CVD status data from waves 1 to 3; and (4) available follow-up CVD outcome data after wave 3. Individuals not meeting all these criteria were excluded from the analysis. The detailed participant selection process is shown in Fig. 1.

Ethical approval for CHARLS was granted by the Peking University Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Physical measurement and laboratory assays

Structured questionnaires were used to collect information on sex, age, educational attainment, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, and physician-diagnosed conditions including hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes. Anthropometric parameters were measured by trained interviewers according to standardized protocols. Height and weight were measured with participants dressed in light clothing and without footwear. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the ratio of weight in kilograms to the square of height in meters (kg/m²). Waist circumference (WC) was measured at the umbilical level using a measuring tape while participants maintained a standing posture. Blood pressure and heart rate were measured three times after a 5-minute rest using an electronic sphygmomanometer, and the average of the three readings was documented.

Fasting blood samples were obtained from all participants following an overnight fast of at least 8 h. Serum concentrations of triglycerides (TG), fasting blood glucose (FBG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and other lipid profiles were determined using standard enzymatic methods under strict quality-control procedures consistent with prior CHARLS protocols [33].

Assessment of TyG-CVAI, cumulative TyG-CVAI, and TyG-CVAI change patterns

The TyG index was computed as ln [TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2] [34]. The CVAI was calculated using sex-specific equations: CVAI=− 267.93 + 0.68 × age (years) + 0.03 × BMI (kg/m2) + 4.00 × WC (cm) + 22.00 × log10 (TG) (mmol/L) − 16.32 × HDL-C (mmol/L) for men, and CVAI=− 187.32 + 1.71 × age (years) + 4.23 × BMI (kg/m2) + 1.12 × WC (cm) + 39.76 × log10(TG) (mmol/L) − 11.66 × HDL-C (mmol/L) for women [35]. The TyG-CVAI was then derived as the product of the TyG index and CVAI: TyG-CVAI = TyG × CVAI [25, 28]. Cumulative TyG-CVAI exposure was calculated using the trapezoidal rule: (TyG-CVAI2011 + TyG-CVAI2015)/2 × (time2015−time2011). This measure approximates the area under the TyG-CVAI–time curve between 2011 and 2015 and represents the average TyG-CVAI burden over the exposure window multiplied by the time interval between the two measurements. This approach assumes approximately linear change in TyG-CVAI between the two waves.

TyG-CVAI change categories were defined according to TyG-CVAI levels in 2011 and 2015. Because no universally accepted clinical cut-off value for TyG-CVAI has been established for CVD risk prediction, the quartile-based low/high categories were used to represent relative TyG-CVAI burden within the study population rather than clinical diagnostic thresholds. Sex-specific quartile cut-off values were derived from the 2011 baseline TyG-CVAI distribution and then applied to both 2011 and 2015 TyG-CVAI values. The first and second quartiles (Q1–Q2) were combined and defined as the lower-burden group, whereas the third and fourth quartiles (Q3–Q4) were combined and defined as the higher-burden group. Participants were subsequently classified into four TyG-CVAI change categories: low–low, low–high, high–low, and high–high.

Ascertainment of incident CVD

Incident CVD was evaluated based on participants’ self-reported physician diagnoses of heart disease or stroke. At both baseline and each follow-up assessment, participants were asked: “Have you been diagnosed with heart disease (including myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, congestive heart failure, or other heart-related disorders) by a physician?” and “Have you been diagnosed with stroke by a physician?”. Participants were considered to have CVD if either condition was reported. During subsequent follow-up visits, participants were required to confirm the ongoing presence of heart disease or stroke. The definition of CVD used in this study is consistent with previous CHARLS-based studies [36, 37], and with criteria used in major international cohort investigations [38]. In the present analysis, incident CVD was not secondarily validated using adjudicated clinical records, linked claims data, or medication inventories.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, or as median and interquartile range (IQR) if non-normally distributed. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. Baseline characteristics were summarized according to TyG-CVAI change patterns. Differences among groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, as appropriate, and the chi-square test for categorical variables.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident CVD. Cumulative TyG-CVAI was standardized before Cox regression when analyzed as a continuous variable; therefore, the HR for continuous cumulative TyG-CVAI was interpreted per 1-SD increase. Cumulative TyG-CVAI was also categorized into quartiles, with the lowest quartile as the reference group. Tests for linear trend were performed by assigning the median value of each quartile and modeling this value as a continuous variable.

For TyG-CVAI change patterns, Kaplan–Meier curves were used to compare the cumulative incidence of incident CVD across change categories, and differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Associations between TyG-CVAI change categories and incident CVD were then evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models, with the low–low group as the reference category.

Three sequential models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3 was further adjusted for marital status, educational level, residence, smoking status, drinking status, cancer, liver disease and kidney disease. These covariates were selected to reduce potential overadjustment for cardiometabolic conditions that may lie on the pathway between TyG-CVAI and CVD.

Restricted cubic spline analyses were used to examine the dose-response association between cumulative TyG-CVAI and incident CVD. Three knots were placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the cumulative TyG-CVAI distribution, corresponding to 1555.99, 3262.62, and 5391.34, respectively. The 25th percentile of cumulative TyG-CVAI, corresponding to 2331.67, was used as the reference value. Overall and nonlinear associations were tested using Wald chi-square tests. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals.

Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate potential effect modification according to age (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years), sex, smoking status (never vs. ever), drinking status (never vs. ever), hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidemia. For age, sex, smoking status, and drinking status, the stratification variable itself was not included in the corresponding subgroup model. Hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia were used only as stratification variables and were not included as covariates in the primary model. Potential interactions were assessed using likelihood ratio tests.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the primary findings. First, missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations. With missing data, the numbers of missing values for the covariates were 3 (0.07%) for rural residence, 4 (0.09%) for drinking status, 13 (0.30%) for kidney disease, 16 (0.37%) for cancer, and 16 (0.37%) for liver disease. Follow-up time, outcome status, TyG-CVAI change patterns, and cumulative TyG-CVAI were not imputed. Second, to account for death as a competing event for incident CVD, Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models were fitted, and the results were reported as subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) with 95% CIs. Third, an extended model additionally adjusted for baseline hypertension and diabetes was fitted to evaluate whether the associations were robust under a more conservative adjustment strategy. Fourth, cumulative TyG-CVAI was reclassified into tertiles rather than quartiles, and the association with incident CVD was re-evaluated. Fifth, to reduce reliance on the predefined quartile-based low/high classification, absolute change in TyG-CVAI from 2011 to 2015 was modeled as a standardized continuous variable, with and without further adjustment for baseline TyG-CVAI. Sixth, sex-specific tertile-based transition patterns were constructed using tertile cut-off values derived from the 2011 baseline distribution and applied to both 2011 and 2015 TyG-CVAI values. A simplified five-category transition variable and a full nine-category tertile transition variable were both examined. Finally, exploratory K-means clustering was performed using winsorized and sex-standardized TyG-CVAI values measured in 2011 and 2015. The optimal number of clusters was evaluated using the elbow method and average silhouette width.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2), and a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics by TyG-CVAI change categories

Figure 1 illustrates the process of participant selection. A total of 17,708 participants were enrolled in CHARLS Wave 1. After sequential exclusion of participants with incomplete TyG-CVAI data at Wave 1 or Wave 3 (n = 11,280), those aged < 45 years (n = 181), those with prevalent CVD or missing CVD status during Waves 1–3 (n = 1,674), and those without follow-up CVD outcome information at Wave 4 or Wave 5 (n = 235), 4,338 participants were included in the final analysis. Among them, 1,506 were classified into the low–low group, 664 into the low–high group, 228 into the high–low group, and 1,940 into the high–high group.

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the 4,338 participants according to four TyG-CVAI change categories: low–low (n = 1,506), low–high (n = 664), high–low (n = 228), and high–high (n = 1,940). The median age of the study population was 57 years (IQR, 51–63), and 54% were female. Significant between-group differences were observed for most demographic, lifestyle, and cardiometabolic variables, including age, sex, marital status, residence, smoking and drinking status, waist circumference, blood pressure, lipid parameters, glucose, CRP, TyG index, CVAI, cumulative TyG-CVAI, and the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidemia (all P < 0.05).

Specifically, the high–high group displayed the least favorable baseline metabolic profile, with the highest median waist circumference [91.50 (87.00, 97.00) cm], triglycerides [139.83 (101.78, 204.43) mg/dL], TyG index [8.93 (8.57, 9.38)], CVAI [122.34 (106.14, 142.08)], and cumulative TyG-CVAI [4,531.73 (3,922.10, 5,276.52)], as well as the highest prevalence of hypertension (31%), diabetes (7.7%), and dyslipidemia (11%). By contrast, the low–low group showed the most favorable cardiometabolic pattern, with the lowest median waist circumference [76.80 (72.50, 80.30) cm], triglycerides [77.88 (61.06, 101.78) mg/dL], TyG index [8.26 (7.99, 8.56)], CVAI [59.16 (43.75, 72.04)], and cumulative TyG-CVAI [2,036.65 (1,517.12, 2,441.58)], together with the highest HDL-C level (59.70 ± 15.73 mg/dL) and the lowest prevalence of hypertension (9.7%), diabetes (2.4%), and dyslipidemia (3.1%). The low–high and high–low groups showed intermediate metabolic profiles between the low–low and high–high groups.

Figure 2 illustrates the trajectories of sex-standardized TyG-CVAI from 2011 to 2015 across the four predefined change categories. Participants in the low–low and high–high groups maintained relatively stable low and high TyG-CVAI levels over time, respectively, whereas those in the low–high and high–low groups showed clear upward and downward transitions. Figures S1 and S2 further show the movement across sex-specific quartiles and the distribution of sex-standardized TyG-CVAI within each change category.

Associations of TyG-CVAI change patterns and cumulative exposure with incident CVD

During a mean follow-up of 5 years, a total of 845 new-onset CVD events were recorded among the 4,338 participants included in the analysis. Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed to evaluate the cumulative incidence of incident CVD according to TyG-CVAI change categories. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated significant separation in cumulative CVD incidence across TyG-CVAI change patterns, with the highest incidence observed in the high-high group and the lowest in the low-low group (log-rank P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Table 2 presents the results of the multivariable Cox regression analyses. In the fully adjusted model, compared with the low-low group, HRs (95% CIs) for incident CVD were 1.50 (1.21–1.88) for the low-high group, 1.64 (1.20–2.25) for the high-low group, and 1.77 (1.49–2.09) for the high-high group. Event rates increased from 13.6% in the low-low group to 19.7%, 21.0%, and 23.8% in the low-high, high-low, and high-high groups, respectively.

When cumulative TyG-CVAI was analyzed as a standardized continuous variable, each 1-SD increment was associated with a 10% higher risk of incident CVD after full adjustment (HR 1.10, 95% CI 1.06–1.14; P < 0.001). When categorized into quartiles, participants in Q2, Q3 and Q4 had significantly higher risks than those in Q1, with fully adjusted HRs of 1.26 (95% CI 1.01–1.56; P = 0.036), 1.40 (95% CI 1.13–1.73; P = 0.002), and 1.91 (95% CI 1.56–2.33; P < 0.001), respectively. A significant positive trend was observed across quartiles (P for trend < 0.001).

RCS analysis further demonstrated a significant overall association between cumulative TyG-CVAI and incident CVD, with evidence of nonlinearity (P for overall < 0.001; P for nonlinearity = 0.00281) (Fig. 4). Kaplan-Meier curves for cumulative incidence of CVD across quartiles of cumulative TyG-CVAI are presented in Supplementary Figure S3.

The proportional hazards assumption was not violated in the main Cox models, as indicated by Schoenfeld residual tests for the TyG-CVAI change-pattern model, the continuous cumulative TyG-CVAI model, and the cumulative TyG-CVAI quartile model (all global P > 0.85; Supplementary Table S11).

Taken together, the Kaplan–Meier analysis, Cox regression models, RCS results and proportional hazards assessment consistently supported the association between higher longitudinal TyG-CVAI burden and increased risk of incident CVD.

Subgroup analyses of TyG-CVAI change patterns and CVD risk

Table 3 presents subgroup analyses of the associations between TyG-CVAI change patterns and incident CVD across strata of demographic factors (age and sex), behavioural factors (smoking and drinking status), and baseline cardiometabolic conditions (hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia). No statistically significant interaction was observed by age, sex, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, diabetes, or dyslipidemia (all P for interaction > 0.05), indicating that the associations between TyG-CVAI change categories and incident CVD were generally consistent across subgroups.

In the stratified analyses, compared with the low–low group, the high–high group was generally associated with a higher risk of incident CVD across most subgroups, whereas the estimates for the low–high and high–low groups were less stable in some strata, likely owing to smaller subgroup sample sizes. Additional subgroup analyses treating cumulative TyG-CVAI as a standardized continuous variable are presented in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S4.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses yielded results broadly consistent with the main findings. After multiple imputation for missing covariates, the associations of TyG-CVAI change patterns and cumulative TyG-CVAI with incident CVD remained similar to those in the complete-case analyses. Compared with the low–low group, the HRs (95% CIs) for incident CVD were 1.49 (1.20–1.86) for the low–high group, 1.62 (1.18–2.22) for the high–low group, and 1.77 (1.50–2.09) for the high–high group. Each 1-SD increase in cumulative TyG-CVAI remained associated with a higher risk of incident CVD (HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.06–1.14), and the highest quartile of cumulative TyG-CVAI remained associated with increased CVD risk (HR 1.91, 95% CI 1.56–2.33). The corresponding results after multiple imputation are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Fine-Gray competing-risk analyses treating death as a competing event produced materially similar estimates. Compared with the low–low group, the low–high, high–low, and high–high groups were associated with higher risks of incident CVD, with sHRs of 1.47 (1.19–1.82), 1.60 (1.19–2.15), and 1.71 (1.46–2.02), respectively. In addition, cumulative TyG-CVAI remained significantly associated with incident CVD as a standardized continuous variable (sHR 1.09, 95% CI 1.04–1.15), and participants in Q2, Q3, and Q4 had higher risks than those in Q1, with sHRs of 1.25 (95% CI 1.02–1.53), 1.38 (95% CI 1.13–1.68), and 1.84 (95% CI 1.52–2.22), respectively. The Fine–Gray competing-risk regression results are shown in Supplementary Table S3.

In the extended model additionally adjusted for baseline hypertension and diabetes, most associations were attenuated but remained directionally consistent with the primary findings. Compared with the low–low group, the adjusted HRs were 1.44 (95% CI 1.15–1.80) for the low–high group, 1.51 (95% CI 1.10–2.07) for the high–low group, and 1.56 (95% CI 1.31–1.86) for the high–high group. Cumulative TyG-CVAI also remained associated with incident CVD when analyzed as a standardized continuous variable (HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.04–1.13) and in the highest quartile (Q4 vs. Q1: HR 1.61, 95% CI 1.30–1.98) (Supplementary Table S4).

When cumulative TyG-CVAI was reclassified into tertiles, the association with incident CVD also remained robust. In the fully adjusted model, compared with the lowest tertile, the adjusted HRs (95% CIs) were 1.41 (1.18–1.69) for the second tertile and 1.77 (1.48–2.11) for the third tertile, with a significant dose–response trend across tertiles (P for trend < 0.001). The tertile-based analysis of cumulative TyG-CVAI is presented in Supplementary Table S5.

To reduce reliance on the predefined quartile-based low/high classification, absolute change in TyG-CVAI from 2011 to 2015 was modeled as a standardized continuous variable. In the model without adjustment for baseline TyG-CVAI, ΔTyG-CVAI was not significantly associated with incident CVD (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.93–1.06; P = 0.748). After further adjustment for baseline TyG-CVAI, each 1-SD increase in ΔTyG-CVAI was associated with a higher risk of incident CVD (HR 1.05, 95% CI 1.01–1.09; P = 0.010), suggesting that upward change in TyG-CVAI may provide additional information beyond baseline TyG-CVAI level (Supplementary Table S6).

In the simplified five-category tertile-based transition analysis, compared with the stable low group, the increasing, stable middle, and stable high groups were associated with higher risks of incident CVD, with adjusted HRs of 1.50 (95% CI 1.18–1.89), 1.50 (95% CI 1.17–1.92), and 1.95 (95% CI 1.56–2.43), respectively. The decreasing group showed an elevated but statistically non-significant risk estimate (HR 1.27, 95% CI 0.95–1.72) (Supplementary Table S8). The full nine-category tertile transition analysis showed generally consistent results, with elevated CVD risk estimates observed in the middle–middle, middle–high, high–low, and high–high groups. Estimates for some transition groups, particularly the high–low group, were based on small subgroup sizes and had wide confidence intervals; therefore, they should be interpreted cautiously. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table S9.

Exploratory K-means clustering based on winsorized and sex-standardized TyG-CVAI values supported a two-cluster solution representing low and high TyG-CVAI burden. The elbow plot and average silhouette width supported the selection of two clusters, and the mean trajectories of the two K-means-derived clusters are shown in Supplementary Figure S5. Compared with the low-burden cluster, the high-burden cluster had a higher crude CVD event rate (23.4% vs. 16.5%) and was significantly associated with incident CVD after multivariable adjustment (HR 1.41, 95% CI 1.22–1.61; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S10).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, higher longitudinal TyG-CVAI burden was associated with increased risk of incident CVD. Participants with low–high, high–low, and high–high TyG-CVAI change patterns had higher CVD risks than those with a low–low pattern. Cumulative TyG-CVAI was positively associated with incident CVD when analyzed as both a standardized continuous variable and quartiles, and a significant nonlinear dose-response relationship was observed. These findings suggest that TyG-CVAI may be informative not only as a single-time-point metabolic marker, but also as a longitudinal marker of sustained cardiometabolic burden.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of established cardiometabolic risk factors and recent studies on TyG-CVAI and related longitudinal cardiometabolic markers. In the China Kadoorie Biobank, each 10 mmHg higher usual systolic blood pressure was associated with higher risks of ischemic heart disease (HR 1.31, 95% CI 1.28–1.34), ischemic stroke (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.29–1.31), and intracerebral hemorrhage (HR 1.68, 95% CI 1.65–1.71) [39]. In the same nationwide cohort, self-reported diabetes was associated with increased risks of cardiovascular mortality (HR 2.07, 95% CI 1.90–2.26), major coronary events (HR 2.44, 95% CI 2.18–2.73), and ischemic stroke (HR 1.68, 95% CI 1.60–1.77) [40]. In the present study, compared with the low–low group, the multivariable-adjusted HRs were 1.50 (95% CI 1.21–1.88) for the low–high pattern, 1.64 (95% CI 1.20–2.25) for the high–low pattern, and 1.77 (95% CI 1.49–2.09) for the high–high pattern. Participants in the highest quartile of cumulative TyG-CVAI also had a higher risk of incident CVD than those in the lowest quartile (HR 1.91, 95% CI 1.56–2.33). Although direct numerical comparisons across studies should be made cautiously because of differences in exposure definitions, outcomes, statistical models, and covariate adjustment, these estimates suggest that the association of longitudinal TyG-CVAI burden with CVD risk is epidemiologically meaningful and broadly within the range reported for established cardiometabolic risk factors in Chinese populations.

Our results are also broadly consistent with recent evidence on TyG-CVAI and TyG-obesity derivative indices. A recent CHARLS-based study by Zheng et al. [27] showed that participants in the highest baseline TyG-CVAI quartile had a higher risk of new-onset CVD than those in the lowest quartile (HR 1.64, 95% CI 1.35–2.00). Wang et al. [28] further reported that high and increasing TyG-CVAI was associated with incident stroke (OR 3.16, 95% CI 1.94–5.22), and that the highest tertiles of cumulative and baseline TyG-CVAI were also associated with higher stroke risk (OR 2.53, 95% CI 1.60–4.02 and OR 2.49, 95% CI 1.57–3.95, respectively). In addition, a multi-index CHARLS analysis reported that poorly controlled trajectories and the highest cumulative exposure of TyG-CVAI were associated with increased CVD risk, with risk estimates of 2.00 and 1.72, respectively; the corresponding estimates were 2.00 and 1.78 for TyG-WC, 2.13 and 1.70 for TyG-BMI, and 1.76 and 1.56 for TyG-BRI [29]. Taken together, these comparisons place the observed TyG-CVAI effect sizes in a clinically interpretable range. Rather than presenting TyG-CVAI as a completely new cardiovascular risk marker or a replacement for established clinical risk assessment, our findings add incremental evidence that sustained, worsening, or apparently improving TyG-CVAI patterns may provide complementary information for longitudinal CVD risk stratification.

One important implication of our results is that TyG-CVAI may be more informative when assessed longitudinally rather than only at baseline. Because both insulin resistance and visceral adiposity fluctuate over time, a single measurement may not adequately represent the long-term metabolic burden relevant to later cardiovascular events. In our study, participants with persistently high TyG-CVAI had the highest CVD risk in the primary change-pattern analysis. In addition, individuals with a low–high pattern had increased risk, suggesting that worsening TyG-CVAI status may identify individuals with emerging metabolic-visceral risk. The high–low pattern was also associated with elevated CVD risk, which may reflect residual risk related to prior exposure to high metabolic-visceral burden, even after apparent improvement.

Because the quartile-based low/high classification may not fully capture the continuous nature of TyG-CVAI change, we further evaluated alternative definitions of longitudinal change. The association between TyG-CVAI dynamics and incident CVD was supported by analyses using standardized ΔTyG-CVAI, tertile-based transition patterns, and exploratory K-means clustering. These additional analyses suggest that the observed associations were not solely dependent on the predefined Q1–Q2/Q3–Q4 cut-off. Nevertheless, the quartile-based categories should be interpreted as relative exposure groups within this study population rather than clinically established thresholds.

Evidence from other longitudinal or noninvasive cardiometabolic markers also supports the value of repeated or accessible metabolic risk assessment. For example, Cai et al. [41] identified distinct plasma aldosterone concentration trajectories in patients with hypertension and found that moderate-stable and high-stable patterns were associated with increased CVD risk compared with the low-stable pattern (HR 2.19, 95% CI 1.59–3.01; and HR 2.56, 95% CI 1.68–3.91, respectively). Zhao et al. [42] reported a J-shaped association between the weight-adjusted-waist index and CVD risk in hypertensive patients with obstructive sleep apnea, whereas Wen et al. [43] reported a linear association between the hepatic steatosis index and major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events in a similar high-risk population. Together, these studies highlight the broader relevance of longitudinal biomarker patterns and noninvasive metabolic-adiposity indices for cardiovascular risk stratification.

Several biological mechanisms may help explain these findings. TyG-CVAI captures the combined burden of insulin resistance and visceral adiposity, which may act in a mutually reinforcing manner [21, 27]. Insulin resistance contributes to hyperglycaemia and atherogenic dyslipidemia [44]. In addition, it may impair endothelial function and vascular insulin signaling, thereby promoting vascular injury and atherosclerotic progression [45]. Chronic hyperglycaemia may further amplify oxidative stress, inflammation, and endothelial damage, ultimately accelerating cardiovascular dysfunction [46]. Visceral adipose tissue, as an active endocrine and inflammatory organ, releases pro-inflammatory cytokines and free fatty acids, which may further impair insulin signaling pathways, aggravate systemic inflammation, and exacerbate insulin resistance [21, 47]. Because triglycerides are included in both the TyG index and CVAI formulas [27, 28], TyG-CVAI partly weights lipid-related metabolic information twice. Therefore, TyG-CVAI should be interpreted as an integrated metabolic-visceral burden rather than as evidence of independent or additive effects of two fully distinct biological dimensions.

Beyond these pathways, chronic low-grade inflammation, vascular stiffness, renal dysfunction, and ectopic lipid deposition may also contribute to the link between sustained metabolic-visceral burden and CVD risk. Chronic low-grade inflammation may promote endothelial activation, oxidative stress, and atherosclerotic plaque progression, whereas vascular stiffness may increase pulsatile hemodynamic stress, cardiac afterload, and microvascular injury [48, 49]. Renal dysfunction may be involved as part of arterial stiffness-related end-organ damage [50], while ectopic lipid deposition and adipose tissue dysfunction may amplify systemic metabolic dysregulation, inflammation, and residual cardiovascular risk [47, 51, 52]. Future studies incorporating repeated mechanistic biomarkers, imaging-based adiposity assessment, and clinically adjudicated cardiovascular outcomes are warranted to further clarify these pathways.

From a clinical and public health perspective, TyG-CVAI has several practical advantages. It can be calculated from routine biochemical and anthropometric measurements, including triglycerides, fasting glucose, body mass index, and waist circumference, and requires no specialized testing. Therefore, it may be suitable for population-level risk stratification and longitudinal monitoring in settings where these measurements are routinely available. Our findings suggest that repeated assessment of TyG-CVAI may help identify individuals with sustained or worsening metabolic-visceral burden. A low–high pattern may indicate deterioration in cardiometabolic status over time and may warrant closer assessment of glucose metabolism, lipid profile, blood pressure, adiposity, and lifestyle-related risk factors. Conversely, a high–low pattern may suggest improvement in TyG-CVAI, but the elevated risk observed in this group indicates that residual cardiovascular risk may persist. Therefore, individuals with a high–low pattern should not be considered risk-free and may still require continued monitoring and maintenance of cardiometabolic risk management. A persistently high TyG-CVAI pattern may indicate sustained metabolic-visceral burden and may identify individuals who require more intensive risk evaluation. However, TyG-CVAI should be interpreted as a risk-stratification marker rather than a stand-alone clinical decision tool. Because TyG-CVAI was measured approximately four years apart in this study, our findings support the general concept of periodic reassessment but cannot determine the optimal clinical reassessment interval.

This study has several strengths. First, it was based on a nationally representative prospective cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, in whom CVAI is particularly applicable because it was developed for Chinese populations. Second, we evaluated both cumulative exposure and longitudinal change patterns of TyG-CVAI, which allowed a more comprehensive assessment of long-term metabolic-visceral burden. Third, the robustness of the findings was assessed using multiple approaches, including multiple imputation, competing-risk models, standardized ΔTyG-CVAI, tertile-based transition patterns, exploratory K-means clustering, and formal assessment of the proportional hazards assumption. Nevertheless, several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, incident CVD was identified by self-reported physician diagnosis, rather than adjudicated clinical records, linked claims data, or medication-based validation, which may have introduced recall-related misclassification; however, such misclassification was likely to be largely non-differential because TyG-CVAI was assessed before outcome ascertainment. Second, as an observational study, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded, and the distinction between confounders and intermediate cardiometabolic conditions cannot be fully established. Third, cumulative TyG-CVAI was calculated using only two repeated measurements in 2011 and 2015, and the quartile-based low/high classification reflected the relative distribution within this study population rather than clinically established thresholds, although alternative analyses supported the robustness of the findings. Fourth, because triglycerides are included in both the TyG index and CVAI formulas, TyG-CVAI should be interpreted as an integrated metabolic-visceral burden marker rather than as evidence of independent or additive effects of two fully distinct biological dimensions. Finally, because CVAI was developed in Chinese populations, the generalizability of TyG-CVAI to non-Chinese populations remains uncertain.

Conclusion

Higher cumulative TyG-CVAI burden and unfavorable longitudinal change patterns were associated with a higher risk of incident CVD among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. These findings suggest that repeated TyG-CVAI assessment may help identify individuals with sustained or worsening metabolic-visceral burden and support longitudinal cardiometabolic risk stratification. Further studies with clinically adjudicated outcomes and more frequent repeated measurements are warranted to validate these findings and determine clinically actionable monitoring intervals.
