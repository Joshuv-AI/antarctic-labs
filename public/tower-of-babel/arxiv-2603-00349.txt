EmCoop: A Framework and Benchmark for Embodied Cooperation Among LLM Agents
Hanqing Yang, Shiyu Chen, Narjes Nourzad, Marie Siew, Jingdi Chen, Carlee Joe-Wong

Abstract
Real-world scenarios increasingly require multiple embodied agents to collaborate in dynamic environments under embodied constraints, as many tasks exceed the capabilities of any single agent. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) enable high-level cognitive coordination through reasoning, planning, and natural language communication. However, fine-grained analyses of how such collaboration emerges, unfolds, and contributes to task success in embodied multi-agent systems are difficult to conduct with existing benchmarks. In this paper, we introduce EmCoop, a benchmark framework for studying cooperation in LLM-based embodied multi-agent systems. Our framework separates a high-level cognitive layer from a low-level embodied interaction layer, allowing us to characterize agent cooperation through their interleaved dynamics over time. Given a cooperation-constrained embodied task, we propose generalizable, process-level metrics that diagnose collaboration quality and failure modes, beyond final task success. We instantiate our framework in two embodied environments that scale to arbitrary numbers of agents and support diverse communication topologies, and use these instantiations to demonstrate how EmCoop enables systematic analysis of cooperation dynamics across team sizes and task settings. The project web page can be found at: https://happyeureka.github.io/emcoop .

This need for agent collaboration raises the
first core challenge
of this paper:
how can we systematically study and evaluate collaboration in embodied multi-agent systems (MAS)?
Existing benchmarks either focus on single-agent embodiment
(

18
;
11

or simplified multi-agent settings that fail to capture realistic collaboration dynamics
(

)
. Moreover, cooperation is often treated as an implicit factor in task success, rather than as an explicitly observable and analyzable

(

To address this gap, a benchmark for collaboration embodied MAS collaboration should (i) make collaboration transparent and human-readable, enabling visualization and auditing during the cooperation process, and (ii) support explicit communication topologies and interaction structures that can be analyzed and compared across environment settings.

Large language models (LLMs) provide powerful cognitive capabilities for embodied agents, enabling symbolic task understanding, high-level reasoning, and long-horizon planning
(
26
;
12
)
. Thus, LLMs are well-suited for open-ended, multi-modal, and complex embodied tasks that require abstraction and communication
(
2
;
15
)
.
However, real-world tasks require these
cognitive and communicative decisions to influence low-level embodied execution and subsequent environment transitions
under embodied constraints.
This cognitive–embodied gap becomes even more pronounced in multi-agent settings, where multiple agents must align their internal reasoning, share knowledge, and coordinate physical actions in a shared environment. Understanding how cognitive intelligence is translated into collective embodied behavior, therefore, constitutes a
second fundamental challenge
for LLM-based embodied multi-agent systems.

Motivated by the challenges of grounding cognitive intelligence into collective embodied behavior, we introduce EmCoop, a benchmark framework for studying cooperation in LLM-based multi-agent systems through embodied agents (Figure
1
). EmCoop explicitly connects a high-level
cognitive layer
, where LLM agents perform symbolic reasoning, planning, and communication, with a low-level
embodied layer
, where agents execute primitive actions under physical and temporal constraints. This dual-layer design directly addresses our second fundamental challenge and, together with a formal specification of agent interactions, makes the entire cooperation process transparent, interpretable, and human-readable, addressing our first fundamental challenge. Unlike prior work that focuses on single-agent grounding or model-based world dynamics, EmCoop is model-free and emphasizes observable interaction processes, allowing it to generalize across diverse environment scenarios.

After reviewing related work in Section
2
and formalizing embodied cooperation in Section
3
, we make the following
contributions
: (1)
Dual-layer cognitive–embodied interface
(Section
4.1
).
We formalize a dual-layer environment interface and multi-agent interaction loop for embodied LLM agents, which bridges high-level cognitive representations and low-level embodied interaction dynamics. By grounding LLM models into executable primitive actions, this interface provides a principled abstraction for translating cognitive decisions into embodied multi-agent behavior, thus allowing us to analyze agents’ cooperation process. (2)
Cooperation-constrained task design
(Section
4.2
). We define
cooperative tasks
as temporally extended embodied processes whose progress requires sustained multi-agent collaboration. These tasks incorporate generalizable cooperation constraints that reflect the embodied environment. By requiring agents to cooperate and scaling to accommodate multiple numbers of agents, they provide a vehicle to study agent cooperation. (3)
Process-level cooperation metrics and diagnostics
(Section
5
). We propose a suite of generalizable metrics that quantify cooperation quality beyond task success, including communication efficiency, coordination structure, temporal dependency, and failure modes. These metrics enable real-time diagnosis and visualization of cooperation dynamics in a reward-free and model-agnostic manner. (4)
A scalable benchmark for cooperative intelligence
(Section
6
). We release a benchmark with two embodied environment instantiations of EmCoop that support evaluation on tasks with arbitrary numbers of agents, scalable difficulty levels, and diverse communication topologies. (5)
Empirical analysis of cooperation dynamics
(Section
7
). We design controlled experiments to study how cooperation generalizes and evolves, addressing research questions (RQs) on the effect of communication topology on cooperation performance (
RQ1
), the stages at which cooperation typically breaks down (
RQ2
), and whether explicit cooperation feedback improves task performance (
RQ3
).
We
conclude
by discussing future work in Section
8
.
Together
, these contributions establish a unified and extensible benchmark for analyzing and designing collaborative LLM-based embodied multi-agent systems.

Cognitive–embodied grounding challenge.

Cognitive decisions and communication operate over symbolic action concepts

\Gamma\bigl(\hat{a}_{i,k}(\theta_{i,k}),\,s_{t}\bigr)\mapsto\{a_{i,\tau}\}_{\tau=t:t^{\prime}}.

4
EmCoop Methodology

We now present a methodological framework for analyzing cooperation in LLM-based embodied multi-agent systems by explicitly addressing the two challenges identified above:
(i) the
cognitive–embodied grounding challenge
, i.e., how to align high-level plans and communication with stepwise embodied execution; and
(ii) the
cooperative tasks and constraints challenge
, i.e., how to probe cooperation through the evolution of constraint satisfaction and final outcomes.

4.1
Solving the Cognitive–Embodied Grounding Challenge: Cognitive–Embodied Interface

We
operationalize
the cognitive–embodied grounding challenge by specifying the alignment between cognitive-time events and discrete environment transitions, so that cognitive activity can be attributed to stepwise embodied outcomes.

Clock alignment.

Let
\hat{\mathbb{T}}
denote cognitive time and
\mathbb{T}=\{0,\dots,T\}
environment steps.
Define an index map
\kappa:\hat{\mathbb{T}}\to\mathbb{T},
by
\kappa(\hat{t})=\max\{\,t\in\mathbb{T}:
env. step
t
has completed by
\hat{t}

\}
,
and let
\hat{t}_{t}
be the wall-clock time at which step
t
completes, so that
\kappa(\hat{t}_{t})=t
.
This induces the between-step cognitive interval

I_{t}=[\hat{t}_{t},\hat{t}_{t+1}),

during which agents may reason, communicate, and (re)commit plans whose effects are realized at the next environment transition.

Step-resolved grounding.

For each agent
i
and each cognitive-time plan commitment in interval
I_{t}
, let
\pi_{i}|_{I_{t}}
denote the plan active at the end of
I_{t}
(the most recent committed, not-yet-terminated plan).
Let
\hat{a}_{i,t}
denote the next action concept selected by this plan for execution after
\hat{t}_{t+1}
.
Under the grounding semantics in (
4
), this action concept corresponds to a step-resolved realized action segment

\Gamma\bigl(\hat{a}_{i,t},\,s_{t}\bigr)\;\Rightarrow\;\{a_{i,\tau}\}_{\tau=t:t_{i}^{\prime}},\qquad t_{i}^{\prime}\geq t,

(8)

resulting in a joint primitive evolution coupled across agents by the environment transition kernel.
Equation (
8
) does not redefine
\Gamma
; it specifies the measurable object recorded at runtime: the primitive action segment generated by the action concept active at step
t
.

Aligned episode log.

We align cognitive traces with stepwise embodied outcomes with an aligned per-step log

\mathcal{L}_{t}\triangleq\Bigl(X_{I_{t}},\;M_{I_{t}},\;s_{t},\;a_{t},\;s_{t+1},\;\{\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{g,t+1}\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{t+1}}\Bigr),

(9)

where
X_{I_{t}}\equiv X_{[\hat{t}_{t},\hat{t}_{t+1})}
and
M_{I_{t}}\equiv M_{[\hat{t}_{t},\hat{t}_{t+1})}
are the cognitive and message traces between consecutive environment transitions.
This log induces an attribution-ready dataset in which step
t+1

\{\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{g,t+1}\}_{g}
, is paired with the immediately preceding cognitive activity
\left(X_{I_{t}},\;M_{I_{t}}\right)
. In the next section, this relation will allow operators to act on

while evaluating effects in the cooperation trace

without re-specifying agent internals.

4.2
Solving the Cooperative Tasks and Constraints Challenge

We treat cooperative embodied multi-agent systems as a structured information-to-outcome system,
where our aim is not to optimize these embodied outcomes as a black-box performance function,
but to analyze the internal structure of cooperation through a set of operators that probe
predictability, intervention effects, temporal attribution, and feedback control.
Concretely, we study the cooperative mapping

\mathcal{F}:\bigl(X_{[\hat{t},\hat{t}^{\prime}]},\;M_{[\hat{t},\hat{t}^{\prime}]},\;\mathcal{H},\;F\bigr)\;\mapsto\;\bigl(\{\mathcal{C}_{g,t}^{\pm}\}_{t=1}^{T},\;Y_{g}\bigr),

(10)

which maps observable cognitive traces
X_{[\hat{t},\hat{t}^{\prime}]}
, communication trajectories
M_{[\hat{t},\hat{t}^{\prime}]}
,
communication topology
\mathcal{H}
, and feedback signals
F_{t}

to the cooperation trace
\Xi_{g}
of constraint evolution and final task outcome from (
7
).
This formulation makes cooperation

predictable
through cross-domain generalization of constraints
\{\mathcal{C}_{g,t}^{\pm}\}
(RQ1),

structurally analyzable
via interventions on communication topology
\mathcal{H}
(RQ2),

temporally diagnosable
through attribution over interaction-stage trajectories
\mathcal{Q}_{i,t}
(RQ3),
and
intervention-controllable
via feedback-modulated policies
\pi_{i}(a\mid h,F)
(RQ4)
, where
h=(x_{i},M_{i})
is the cognitive history.

Episode record and derived variables.

For
each task

let

RQ1: Cooperation and topology (topology intervention operator).

We study how communication topology affects cooperation performance and failure modes.

To isolate structural and causal effects, we treat the communication topology
\mathcal{H}
as an
explicit intervention
applied under matched conditions.
For fixed
(e,g,n)
and a fixed agent backbone, we vary
\mathcal{H}
over a predefined set of topologies, while holding all other experimental factors constant.

We represent the communication structure among agents as a directed graph

\mathcal{H}=(I,E),\qquad E\subseteq I\times I,

(12)

where nodes
I
correspond to agents and a (directed) edge

indicates that agent
i
can send messages to agent
j

For each communication topology
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, we evaluate

\mathbb{E}[Y_{g}\mid\mathcal{H}_{k}]
and the expected cooperative constraint violation patterns
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}_{g,t}^{\pm}\mid\mathcal{H}_{k}]

\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
Together, these quantities characterize not only how well agents perform (in terms of
Y_{g}
), but also

\mathcal{C}_{g,t}^{\pm}
). We can further compare both the task success rates and violated-constraint distributions under different topologies
\mathcal{H}_{j}
and
\mathcal{H}_{k}

on cooperative outcomes

and failure modes. Appendix

RQ2: Process-level cooperation dynamics (temporal attribution operator).

We ask at which stages of the cooperation process failures occur and which cooperative constraints are violated.
Let
\tau_{f}\in\{1,\dots,T\}
denote the first failure-relevant step for task
g
in an episode.
Let
\mathrm{Term}_{t}\in\{0,1\}
indicate whether the episode terminates at step
t
, and define

\tau_{f}=\min\Bigl\{t:\ \mathrm{Term}_{t}=1\ \wedge\ Y_{g}=0\Bigr\},

(13)

i.e., the first termination step of an unsuccessful episode.

With slight abuse of notation, we use
\hat{\tau}_{f}
to denote the most recent interaction stage before environment time
\tau_{f}

\kappa(\hat{\tau}_{f})=\tau_{f}
.
Let
\mathcal{Q}_{[\hat{\tau}_{f}-k:\hat{\tau}_{f}]}=\{\mathcal{Q}_{i,\hat{t}}\}_{i\in I,\;t\in[\hat{\tau}_{f}-k,\hat{\tau}_{f}]}
denote a

We study the conditional attribution model

P\bigl(\mathcal{C}_{g,\tau_{f}}^{-}\mid\mathcal{Q}_{[\hat{\tau}_{f}-k:\hat{\tau}_{f}]},\,M_{[\hat{\tau}_{f}-k:\hat{\tau}_{f}]}\bigr),

(14)

which maps agent-level interaction dynamics
\mathcal{Q}_{[\tau_{f}-k:\tau_{f}]}
and communication trajectories

to the violated cooperative constraints
\mathcal{C}_{g,\tau_{f}}^{-}
at the failure step.
This formulation defines a temporal attribution problem that localizes cooperative breakdowns to specific reasoning stages, interaction patterns, and embodied constraint categories.

RQ3: Cooperation feedback (feedback control operator).

We study whether explicit cooperation feedback improves collaborative performance

by exposing agents to, or withholding, structured signals derived from constraint evaluation, with all other experimental factors held constant.

Let
F_{t}
denote a feedback variable available to agents at step
t
, defined as a function of the cooperative constraint state.
In this work, we consider feedback of the form
F_{t}=\mathcal{C}_{g,t}^{-}
, i.e., the violated cooperative constraints for task
g
at step
t
.
Let
\pi_{i}(a\mid h)
denote the policy of agent
i
conditioned on its cognitive history
h=(x_{i},M_{i})
, and let
\pi_{i}(a\mid h,F)
denote a feedback-aware policy that additionally conditions on the feedback signal

.
We evaluate the effect of feedback through the performance gap

\mathbb{E}[Y_{g}^{\mathrm{with\ feedback}}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{g}^{\mathrm{without\ feedback}}],

(15)

which measures the expected improvement in task success when agents have access to explicit cooperation feedback.

Once all agents reach a

(16)

where
X_{[\hat{t}_{t},\hat{t}^{\prime}_{t}]}
and
M_{[\hat{t}_{t},\hat{t}^{\prime}_{t}]}
summarizes what is observable during the between-step cognitive phase, while the transition
s_{t}\!\to s_{t+1}
summarize what those cognitive activities achieve in embodied execution.

Each task
g\in\mathcal{G}_{t}
evolves according to a task-specific transition function
T_{g}
defined over the primitive environment dynamics, while
Z_{t}
represents agent capability states
that may change as a result of execution.

This separation enables attribution from agent-level MAEIL dynamics, plan events, and communication to environment-level task progress and constraint satisfaction.

5.2
Metrics

Decision overhead (
D
).

Quantifies the cognitive control effort induced by MAEIL dynamics. We define:

D^{\delta t}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i\in I}\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{i,[\hat{t}_{t},\hat{t}^{\prime}_{t}]}\rvert,

(17)

and the per-agent overhead

D_{i}=\sum_{t}\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{i,[\hat{t}_{t},\hat{t}^{\prime}_{t}]}\rvert.

From this, we report the average and variability across agents,

D^{\mathrm{avg}}=\mathbb{E}_{i\in I}[D_{i}],\qquad D^{\mathrm{std}}=\mathrm{Std}_{i\in I}(D_{i}).

(18)

where
\mathcal{Q}_{i,[\hat{t}_{t},\hat{t}^{\prime}_{t}]}
is the MAEIL control-state
trajectory of agent
i
.

Let the per-agent wait time be

W_{i}=\sum_{t}\mathsf{W}_{i,[\hat{t}_{t},\hat{t}^{\prime}_{t}]}
,
capturing delays from plan alignment before primitive execution.
We report the average and dispersion across agents,

W^{\mathrm{avg}}=\mathbb{E}_{i\in I}[W_{i}],\qquad W^{\mathrm{std}}=\mathrm{Std}_{i\in I}(W_{i}).

(19)

This metric supports
RQ2
by capturing how much cognitive processing and control switching precede cooperative failures, enabling diagnosis of whether breakdowns are associated with excessive reasoning, waiting, or interruptions.
It also contributes to
RQ3
by revealing whether feedback reduces cognitive overhead during recovery.

Communication load (
L
).

Communication metrics are defined over the message trace
M_{[\hat{t},\hat{t}^{\prime}]}
, with the total number of messages being
L^{\mathrm{tot}}=\lvert M_{[\hat{t},\hat{t}^{\prime}]}\rvert
. Define the per-agent communication load
L_{i}
as the number of messages sent by agent
i
in
M_{[\hat{t},\hat{t}^{\prime}]}
, and we report

L^{\mathrm{avg}}=\mathbb{E}_{i\in I}[L_{i}],\qquad L^{\mathrm{std}}=\mathrm{Std}_{i\in I}(L_{i}).

(20)

These metrics support
RQ1
by quantifying how communication structure and load vary under different topologies, and
RQ2
by identifying whether failures are preceded by communication bottlenecks or imbalances.
They also inform
RQ3
by measuring whether feedback induces more efficient information exchange.

Planning dynamics (
P
).

Planning stability is measured from plan trajectories
\{\pi_{i,\hat{t}}\}
using four metrics: inter-agent plan consistency (
P^{\mathrm{coh}}
), number of plan interruptions (
P^{\mathrm{int}}
), replanning rate (
P^{\mathrm{re}}
), and plan resumption rate (
P^{\mathrm{res}}
).
We additionally record the total number of plans (
P^{\mathrm{total}}
) as a planning activity measure.
These metrics directly support
RQ2
by revealing how cooperative plans evolve before failure, including whether breakdowns are associated with frequent interruptions or replanning.
They also support
RQ3
by measuring whether feedback stabilizes planning behavior and reduces disruptive plan events.

Embodied effects (
\mathcal{C},\Delta Z
).

Cooperative constraint satisfaction defined by the predicate

\displaystyle C^{\mathrm{coop}}_{g}(x_{g,t},s_{t},Z_{g,t})

\displaystyle=C^{\mathrm{d}}_{g}(x_{g,t},s_{t})\;\wedge\;

(21)

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{c\in\{\mathrm{\Delta\ell,\Delta t,n}\}}C^{c}_{g}(x_{g,t},Z_{g,t}).

with components
C^{\Delta\ell},C^{\Delta t},C^{d},C^{n}
, corresponding to spatial, temporal, dependency, and participation constraint improvement ratios.
Cooperation outcomes reflect what is observable at the environment level after execution, including both environment state transitions and capability changes (
\Delta Z_{t}=Z_{t+1}-Z_{t}
), corresponding to the realized effects of agents’ cognitive activities as mediated by the environment transition dynamics. For each task g, evaluating
C^{\mathrm{coop}}_{g}(x_{g,t},s_{t},Z_{g,t})
induces the sets of satisfied and violated cooperative constraints,
\mathcal{C}^{+}_{g,t}
and
\mathcal{C}^{-}_{g,t}
, which together characterize task progress and directly attribute failures to specific constraint types.

These metrics are central to
RQ1
by capturing how different topologies affect constraint satisfaction and to
RQ3
by quantifying whether feedback reduces violation frequency.
They form the primary link between cognitive processes and task-level outcomes.

Experiment Design and Results

We evaluate EmCoop in two embodied environments with distinct cooperation constraints, varying
communication topology
,
task difficulty
, and
agent count
using
GPT-5.2
and
DeepSeek-V3.2
. Figure 3 summarizes cooperation metrics (shown for MA-Crafter), with full results and run traces for both environments provided in Appendix
J
and
K
.

RQ1: How does communication topology affect cooperation?
LLM agents cooperate by forming plans at the cognitive layer and influencing one another’s decision-making through communication, which in turn shapes their behaviors. We instantiate four communication topologies (Individual, Debate, Centralized, and Decentralized) and use EmCoop to study how topology and team size (two vs. three agents) affect cooperative performance and failure modes across our two environments, each with three difficulty levels. Detailed descriptions of these topologies in Appendix
B
.

Centralized
topologies achieve the highest plan coherence in most settings, indicating stronger alignment of agents’ cognitive plans. An exception occurs in the
GPT-5.2, 2-agent, Easy
setting: because tasks require only single-agent effort, the leader frequently reallocates work, reducing sustained joint planning. In the 3-agent case, centralized control often assigns identical tasks to multiple workers, increasing redundancy but maintaining coherence.

Individual
agents exhibit the highest plan stability (PS) and the lowest decision overhead. Without communication, plans are not interrupted.

Decentralized
topologies consistently incur the highest communication load. Agents tend to exhaust their per-step communication budgets, resulting in the largest total message counts across all conditions.

Debate
topologies show reduced plan coherence, as agents frequently critique each other and commit to different plans.

RQ2: Where does cooperation break down in the process?
EmCoop provides three levels of visualization for the cooperation process within an episode run, enabling analysis of when cooperation breaks down during task execution and which cooperative constraints are violated.

Increasing task difficulty from
Easy
to
Hard
significantly reduces satisfaction of participation (PT) constraints across all topologies and agent counts. This confirms that our scalable task design meaningfully increases cooperative difficulty by tightening embodied constraints.

In Hard mode, agents also struggle to improve their capabilities (CG), such as acquiring resources or tools, because successful progress requires satisfying more cooperative constraints. The traces in Appendix
K
reveal when and which constraints are not satisfied over time, allowing us to attribute breakdowns to specific embodied requirements rather than abstract performance metrics.
These effects hold across both
GPT-5.2
and
DeepSeek-V3.2
, indicating that the observed failures persist across models.

RQ3: Case Study with Environment Feedback

Figure 4
:
Case study in MA-Crafter with three agents under Hard difficulty, using DeepSeek-V3.2 as the backend. The figure illustrates environment feedback over time, including task-state transitions, agent capability changes (CG), and cooperative constraint changes with respect to tasks.

We evaluate environment feedback in MA-Crafter with three agents under a centralized topology in the Hard setting using DeepSeek-V3.2. We define the environment feedback function
F_{t}(g)
to return feedback on task
g
, including participation requirement, current participation ratio, current participating agents, and the task’s dependencies. When the leader broadcasts a message, it queries
F_{t}
and attaches this feedback, so workers receive the task snapshot alongside the leader’s instruction. Without
F_{t}
, agents must infer feasibility implicitly and reason about whether a task requires their participation, especially when the leader mistakenly assigns agents to different tasks. With
F_{t}
, agents can collect multiple resources within 20 steps even in the Hard setting.

8
Conclusion

We introduced EmCoop, a benchmark framework for embodied cooperation among LLM agents that makes the cooperation process observable across
cognitive activity
,
environment execution
, and
cooperative constraints
. EmCoop shifts evaluation beyond end-task success toward interpretable cooperation dynamics. While we propose a concrete set of cooperation metrics, there is no single canonical metric for cooperation quality. Instead, EmCoop supports customized metrics over interaction traces by exposing cooperation signals at these three levels and their interplay.

To study how communication topology affects cooperative performance, we consider multiple communication topologies and analyze their impact. Nodes correspond to
agents
, and a directed edge indicates that one agent can send messages to another (with reciprocity in the bidirectional case). We consider the following communication topologies:

1.

Individual
: Agents do not communicate with one another.

2.

Debate
: During reasoning, each agent broadcasts messages to all other agents. Each agent observes all previously generated messages.

3.

Centralized
: For n agents, one agent acts as a leader and the remaining agents are followers. During reasoning, the leader broadcasts a message to all followers and waits for their responses. Each follower waits for the leader’s message, generates a response, and forms a plan. If a follower is interrupted by a new leader message during execution, it decides whether to resume the current plan or replan before responding to the leader.

4.

Decentralized
: At each decision-making step, each agent has a communication budget of n. An agent may send up to n messages and receive up to n messages per step.

Appendix C
MAEIL: Interaction Loop and Stage Transitions

In this section, we further explain the multi-agent environment interaction loop (MAEIL) and the events that trigger transitions between its stages. As introduced in Sec
3
, at any cognitive time
\hat{t}
, each agent
i
maintains an internal state
x_{i,\hat{t}}=(\mathcal{Q}_{i,\hat{t}},\pi_{i,\hat{t}})
,
where
\mathcal{Q}_{i,\hat{t}}
denotes the current MAEIL stage of agent
i
, and
\pi_{i,\hat{t}}
denotes its active plan. We begin with a single agent interacting with the environment, and then extend the discussion to multiple agents that reason asynchronously while acting in a shared, stepwise environment. Communication is subsequently introduced as an explicit interaction mechanism among agents.

Single-agent case.

When only one embodied agent is present, the agent alternates between a
reasoning stage

\mathsf{R}
and an
execution stage

\mathsf{X}
, such that
\mathcal{Q}_{i,\hat{t}}\in{\{\mathsf{R},\mathsf{X}\}}
.
During
\mathsf{R}
, the agent may reason in any style, invoke any available tools, and take an arbitrary amount of cognitive time to produce a plan
\pi_{i,\hat{t}}
.
The agent executes a plan in the environment and returns to reasoning once execution completes, following interaction pattern resembling ReAct
(
37
)
.

Multiple agents without communication.

When multiple embodied agents act in a shared environment without communication, an additional wait stage
\mathsf{W}
is required, so that
\mathcal{Q}_{i,\hat{t}}\in{\{\mathsf{R},\mathsf{X},\mathsf{W}\}}
.
Since the environment advances only when all agents maintain an active plan, an agent that has committed its plan but is waiting for others, transitions into
\mathsf{W}
.

i:\;\{\mathsf{R},\mathsf{X}\}\;\xrightarrow{\exists i\in I_{\mathrm{active}}:\ \neg\mathrm{Ready}}\;\mathsf{W}\;\xrightarrow{\forall i\in I_{\mathrm{active}}:\ \mathrm{Ready}}\;\mathsf{X}.

Multiple agents with communication.

When communication is enabled, agents may receive messages at any time, including during execution.
To model this, we introduce an interrupt stage
\mathsf{I}
, yielding
\mathcal{Q}_{i,\hat{t}}\in\{\mathsf{R},\mathsf{X},\mathsf{W},\mathsf{I}\}
.
Upon receiving a message, an agent transitions to
\mathsf{I}
, processes the incoming information, and decides whether to resume its current plan or replan. Agents in stages
\mathsf{R}
and
\mathsf{I}
may send messages, while agents in any stage may receive messages:

i_{2}:\{\mathsf{R},\mathsf{I}\}\;\xRightarrow{\textsc{Msg}_{i_{2}\to i_{1}}(\hat{t})}\;i_{1}:\{\mathsf{R},\mathsf{X},\mathsf{W},\mathsf{I}\}.

Message arrival interrupts waiting or execution and moves the agent to
\mathsf{I}
:

i_{1}:\;\{\mathsf{W},\mathsf{X}\}\;\xrightarrow{\textsc{Msg}_{i_{2}\to i_{1}}(\hat{t})}\;\mathsf{I}\xrightarrow{\textsc{Ready}(i_{1},\hat{t}^{\prime})}\mathsf{W},

where the transition is defined for the receiving agent
i
. After processing the message, the agent enters
\mathsf{W}
once it maintains an active plan, either by resuming the previous plan or by committing a new one. Figure
2
shows an instance of multiple communicating agents and their MAEIL transitions.

Appendix D
Cooperation Analysis: Algorithmic Details

Algorithm
1
specifies the threaded execution loop used to support cooperation analysis under asynchronous communication. At each environment step, agents run in parallel within live threads so they can continuously receive and process messages while forming plans. The system waits until all agents report readiness, which serves as a synchronization barrier that ends the current reasoning interval. Once all agents are ready, their threads are joined to ensure planning has completed, and the environment is advanced via a single joint step that returns observations, rewards, and termination signals. The episode ends when any agent terminates or is truncated, or when the maximum step budget is reached. Throughout this loop, the reasoning-phase interactions (e.g., internal state updates and message traffic) can be logged during the asynchronous period, and per-step environment outputs can be recorded after each call to

Appendix G
MA-Crafter

MA-Crafter is an embodied multi-agent environment that extends the Crafter world to support cooperative decision making among many agents in a shared, partially observable 2D grid world. Agents explore, gather resources, and craft tools through a technology tree, while maintaining individual inventories, health states, and local views. We apply EmCoop’s task design to instantiate scalable cooperative tasks by enforcing embodied participation constraints on execution, and provide a dual interface: a symbolic planning layer for high-level, interpretable multi-agent plans, and a low-level execution layer that grounds plan executions under these constraints.

H.1
Task Design

We denote the set of agents by I and the set of blocks available at the start of an episode by
\mathcal{B}
.
We treat each block’s four sides as distinct tasks, denoted by
g\in\mathcal{G}
, where each task corresponds to pushing block
b(g)
from side
s(g)\in\mathcal{S}(b(g))
. Each block has an integer weight
w_{b(g)}\geq 1
, indicating the number of agents needed to push it. Meaning, each task requires participation threshold
p(g)=w_{b(g)}
.
The block occupies a contiguous square of side length
w_{b(g)}
, so its physical size grows in direct proportion to its weight.
This proportionality ensures consistency, as larger blocks both span more grid cells and require a greater quorum of agents to move.
Each agent
i\in I
occupies a single grid cell, with position denoted
\mathcal{x}_{i}^{t}
at step
t
. Episodes
terminate
successfully when all blocks have been delivered to the goal region.
Episodes
truncate
if the

Block chain.

A block

of weight

. The chain behaves as a composite structure whose motion depends on whether the total applied force at its leading face is sufficient to move all blocks within the chain. Let
i

Agent chain.

Agents exert unit forces on blocks through pushing chains. A pushing chain forms when multiple agents align
collinearly
behind a block and all push in the direction of the block face.
The effective force at the contact face is the number of aligned agents in that direction.
If this total meets or exceeds the required block (or chain) weight, the structure advances.
Otherwise, the attempt fails and all participating agents remain in place, as shown in Fig.

Agent Movement and Collisions

While the environment supports a dual-layer action interface, primitive and symbolic (see Section
H.2.2
for details), each symbolic action is unrolled into a sequence of primitive actions, with all movements and collisions resolved at the primitive level.
At each timestep, each agent issues a primitive action
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
, which specifies a target cell on the grid.
A move is valid if the target cell lies within bounds and is not occupied by a block
(including newly moved blocks). If multiple agents attempt to move into the same unoccupied cell, the agent with the smallest index
i
successfully claims the cell, while all others involved in the conflict remain unmoved. If any agent attempts to move into a cell occupied by a stationary agent, the move fails and both agents stay in place. This rule prevents overlap and introduces a consistent tie-breaking mechanism for simultaneous movements.

H.2
Cognitive Layer Design

CUBE features a dual-layer interface that integrates symbolic reasoning with vector-based representations across
observation, action, and feedback channels
(see Sections
H.2.1
–
H.2.2
for details). The symbolic layer abstracts the dynamics of the environment into discrete entities and relations such as the distance between the agents and the quorum, allowing high-level reasoning, planning, and language interaction. Complementing this, the vector-based layer provides dense spatial and state features suitable for reinforcement learning and low-level control. Together, these layers allow agents to ground symbolic reasoning in embodied experience, supporting cooperative learning across reinforcement, language, and hybrid agent architectures.

H.2.1
Observation Space

CUBE provides two observation modalities: a symbolic observation and a multi-channel observation. This dual interface supports diverse agent architectures, enabling reinforcement learning agents to rely on grid-based encodings, LLM-based agents to operate over symbolic state descriptions, or novel approaches that combine both.

Symbolic Observation.

At each step
t
, every agent
i
receives a symbolic dictionary describing the current state.
This includes global environment information (grid size, positions of all agents) as well as a compact summary for each block (block ID, weight, position, and distance to the goal column).
The dictionary also records all symbolic actions taken so far in the episode, along with their corresponding primitive actions and the status (start, in progress, or end) at each timestep. This structured interface allows reasoning directly about concepts such as which blocks remain, how far they are from the goal, and where teammates are, supporting high-level planning and coordination.

Multi-channel Observation.

In addition, a five-channel grid encodes agent locations, block weights, the goal column, a channel marking which agent occupies each cell, and a channel marking which block occupies each cell.
This representation resembles standard reinforcement learning observations and is primarily included for compatibility with reinforcement learning pipelines and for visualization.

H.2.2
Action Space

CUBE supports two sets of action spaces.
The primitive action space provides low-level grid movements, enabling agents to interact directly with the environment through discrete directional moves.
The symbolic action space abstracts these primitives into higher-level cooperative strategies, such as aligning on a block face, synchronizing for a push, or waiting for teammates.
Together, these two levels allow experiments to target both reinforcement learning agents, which operate naturally over primitive actions, and LLM-based agents, which benefit from reasoning over symbolic actions.

Primitive Actions.

Each agent selects from a discrete 5-action set

\mathcal{A}=\{\texttt{STAY}=0,\ \texttt{UP}=1,\ \texttt{DOWN}=2,\ \texttt{LEFT}=3,\ \texttt{RIGHT}=4\}.

At time
t
, each agent
i
issues an action
a_{i,t}
specifying a movement direction, and all agents act in parallel to move one unit in their respective directions.
Moves succeed only if the target cell is free; collisions with walls, agents, or insufficiently supported blocks cause the agent to remain in place. A push succeeds if the aligned agents’ combined force exceeds the total weight of the aligned blocks and the destination cell is free, in which case both the blocks and agents advance one step.

Symbolic Actions.

Beyond primitive grid movements, CUBE provides a small library of
symbolic actions
that capture essential coordination behaviors while remaining easy to ground in the environment. Each symbolic action is executed by compiling it into a sequence of primitive moves until the specified condition is met. In our final setup, we use only three symbolic actions:
move
,
wait
, and
push
.
move
compiles into repeated primitive movements in a fixed direction,
wait
compiles into
STAY
actions for a fixed number of steps, and
push
compiles into repeated primitive pushes against the specified block until the push terminates.

H.3
Difficulty and Customization

CUBE is designed to reduce the burden of fine-grained environment engineering and to prevent unfair or inconsistent comparisons caused by unclear environment settings, ensuring fair benchmarking across algorithms. Each episode is automatically generated from a single governing variable
n
, which defines both the environment scale and cooperative difficulty. By default, the grid side length is
k=\max(20,n)
, with
n
agents placed on the board and a structured set of blocks whose weights range from
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
down to one. Lighter blocks appear in greater numbers, creating a balanced mix of large, coordination-intensive tasks and smaller, easily solvable ones. The block distribution is chosen to ensure that episodes remain neither trivial nor overcrowded across scales, with roughly half of the grid covered by blocks. Agents are initialized along the wall opposite the goal region, and blocks are placed under constraints that prevent adjacency to walls or other blocks, since only pushing is allowed and agents would have no way to move them if a block were placed in a corner.

The parameter
n
simultaneously determines the number of agents, grid size, and block weights, creating a transparent and controllable progression of task difficulty. As
n
increases, heavier blocks require larger agent quorums, and more-block configurations lead to greater congestion and synchronization demands, since each agent contributes only one unit of force. Although individual layouts vary, tasks at the same
n
exhibit comparable cooperative complexity, enabling systematic evaluation of coordination and planning strategies from minimal to large-scale embodied settings that involve negotiation, allocation, synchronization, and sub-team organization. In addition, we allow user-specified scenario design to override the default configuration, enabling control over grid size, number of agents, block weights, and their distributions for targeted case studies.

•

Easy Configuration (Level 1).

The environment contains 3 blocks of weight 1, where each block requires a single agent to push. This configuration allows agents to work independently without requiring coordination.

•

Hard Configuration (Level 3).

The environment contains 1 block each of weight 1, 2, and 3, requiring 1, 2, and 3 agents respectively to push simultaneously. This configuration enforces multi-agent coordination, as heavier blocks require multiple agents to push from the same side.

Appendix I
Step-Level Feedback and Logging

This section describes the step-level feedback that agents receive and how their decisions and outcomes are recorded within the
MA-Crafter
environment. At each environment timestep, agents receive feedback on their current plan and action—including violation types, specific violations, and error messages—which allows them to revise their plans or learn how to interact with the world correctly. This feedback, along with the agent’s plans and executed actions, is logged at every step for every agent. This logging framework enables detailed analysis of multi-agent behavior. Beyond simply indicating whether an agent failed the task, the logs also provide insight into when the failure occurred and why it happened.

I.1
Step-Level Feedback

At each environment timestep, agents receive feedback based on their observations, agent state, and validation of their proposed actions. This includes error information such as violation types (
PRE_CHECK
,
PLAN_CHECK
,
EXECUTION
) and error categories (e.g.,
COORDINATION_ERROR
,
WORLD_UNDERSTANDING
), along with descriptive error messages. This feedback is immediately available to agents and can be used for learning or adjusting their plans in real time. All of these details are also logged at every step for each agent.

Appendix K
Experiment Traces

The
top
row of each figure visualizes agents’ cognitive activities, capturing high-level planning, replanning, and communication events. The
middle
row reflects the embodied execution of these plans. The
bottom
row summarizes the effects of execution in terms of task-state progression and agent capability changes over time. These traces make explicit how agent reasoning and interaction patterns are shaped by the imposed communication topology. Across topologies, differences in when agents plan, wait, or interrupt execution reflect distinct coordination structures that influence downstream embodied behavior.

In the hard setting, the cooperative constraints exceed the effective capability of two-agent teams. Although agents adapt over time and individual task constraints may be satisfied intermittently, it is rare for all cooperative constraints to be satisfied simultaneously. In particular, while spatial and dependency constraints may occasionally align, the participation constraint becomes significantly harder to satisfy as task difficulty increases. Satisfying all constraints is a necessary precondition for meaningful task progress, but it does not by itself guarantee task completion. For example, in Fig.
, three individual agents may occasionally collect resources successfully due to initialization effects or chance co-occurrence; however, unlocking the full technology tree required to reach diamond cannot reliably be achieved by chance alone.

As task difficulty increases, agents frequently become spatio-temporally misaligned. This misalignment manifests as agents arriving at task locations at different times, committing incompatible plans, or executing actions that are locally valid but globally ineffective under cooperative constraints. These failures are reflected in the persistent fragmentation of constraint satisfaction in the task and capability timelines.

Across topologies, agents exhibit a strong preference for using the navigation tool to reach target locations. However, failures often arise from inaccurate specification of object identifiers during navigation, leading to execution errors or ineffective movement. Additionally, environment dynamics can alter reachability during execution, causing navigation actions that were initially feasible to become infeasible, further compounding coordination failures.

Overall, the traces illustrate that harder tasks amplify coordination bottlenecks not only at the cognitive level but also through embodied constraint coupling. As difficulty increases, successful cooperation requires increasingly precise temporal alignment, accurate shared references, and robust handling of dynamic environment changes, which remain challenging for small teams under all four topologies.
