Pre-COPD, PRISm, and COPD in young adults born preterm

Elizabeth F Smith, Callan Lavery, Shannon J Simpson

Introduction

Up to 50% of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) cases originate from impaired lung growth and development. In recognition, the 2023 Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) update introduced the COPD-Developmental (COPD-D) etiotype, formally recognizing the risk that preterm birth (<37 weeks’ gestation) poses on lifelong lung health. Preterm-born individuals may have low and/or declining lung function, structural airway abnormalities, and/or respiratory symptoms that persist into adulthood. Population-based studies report 32-fold higher odds of unspecified COPD diagnosis (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification code J44.9) in extremely preterm-born (<28 weeks gestation) young adults (18-29 years of age), compared to those born at term.

Like prediabetes or preeclampsia, “pre-COPD” identifies an especially at-risk population of patients who would benefit from closer follow-up and risk management, and provides a critical window for early intervention. “Pre-COPD” describes individuals who do not yet meet spirometric criteria for COPD (typically forced expiratory volume in 1 second [FEV1]/forced vital capacity [FVC] ratio <0.70, or below the lower limit of normal), but who exhibit symptoms, radiologic abnormalities, or early functional decline and who are thought to be on a trajectory toward COPD. Similarly, the preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm) phenotype, characterized by a normal FEV1/FVC ratio but reduced FEV1, is also frequently associated with transition toward COPD and higher all-cause mortality. Despite growing recognition of preterm birth as a risk factor for COPD, the incidence of pre-COPD, COPD-D, or PRISm remains unknown in young adulthood survivors of preterm birth.

Here we describe the presence of functional markers of pre-COPD, as well as incidence of PRISM and COPD, in a well-characterized cohort of young adults born very preterm (≤32 weeks’ gestation).

Methods

Young adults from the longitudinal Western Australian Lung Health in Prematurity (WALHIP) cohort (born 1998-2003, 74% received surfactant, 79% received antenatal steroids) underwent lung function testing (spirometry, plethysmography, and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide [DlCO]) according to American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society international guidelines, under ethical approval of the Child and Adolescent Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee (RGS815). Z-scores for all tests were calculated using the relevant Global Lung Function Initiative reference equations. Respiratory symptoms were collected via a modified ISAAC questionnaire. Participants were included only if they had valid FEV1 and FVC measurements (N = 151).

Spirometry outcomes were used to classify participants meeting COPD criteria, defined by an FEV1/FVC ratio below the lower limit of normal. COPD severity was categorized as mild (FEV1 z-score ≥ −2.51), moderate (−4.1 ≤ FEV1 z score < −2.51), or severe (FEV1 z score < −4.1). PRISm was defined by a FEV1/FVC z-score > −1.64 and FEV1 z-score < −1.64. No COPD was defined by both FEV1 and FEV1/FVC z-scores > −1.64.

We defined pre-COPD by normal spirometry in the presence of (1) hyperinflation, defined by a residual volume/total lung capacity (RV/TLC) z-score >1.64; (2) low DlCO, defined by a DlCO z-score < −1.64; or (3) FEV1 decline, defined by a z-score reduction of at least 0.5 between childhood (4-12 years) and 19-year visits. For participants with multiple childhood visits, the first observation with valid spirometry outcomes was taken.

Clinical factors associated with membership in each group (preterm no COPD, pre-COPD, PRISm, mild COPD, moderate-severe COPD) was assessed via Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. All analysis was performed using R version 4.5.0.

Results

Data from 113 very prematurely born participants (mean age, 19.3 ± 1.4 years; gestational age 27.3 ± 1.4 weeks) showed that 42 (37%) met diagnostic criteria for COPD, of whom 35 (83%) were considered to have mild COPD and 7 (17%) moderate COPD (). A further 7 (6%) participants was classified with PRISm and 14 participants (12%) met criteria for pre-COPD. Among those in the pre-COPD group, 6 (43%) had hyperinflation, 6 had FEV1 decline, and 2 (14%) had low DlCO (). There was no overlap between indicators in the pre-COPD group.

Discussion

More than 55% of survivors of very preterm birth within this cohort were classified as having COPD-D or COPD precursors (pre-COPD or PRISm) by early adulthood. Despite this high burden of lung disease, coordinated respiratory follow-up programs over the lifespan and published models of care are absent, and clinical management guidelines for prematurity-associated lung disease are based on low or very low levels of evidence. Furthermore, 85% of adult pulmonologists perceive only moderate to very low awareness of preterm lung disease among their colleagues—highlighting a population that continues to fly under the radar.

The GOLD executive summary advocates for screening spirometry for COPD case finding in those with symptoms and/or risk factors for COPD (eg, >20 pack-years of smoking, recurrent chest infections, or prematurity), where the diagnostic yield for COPD is relatively high. However, with >2 million babies surviving very preterm birth annually, we urgently need to understand which survivors are at risk of COPD in young adulthood to prioritize those participating in case-finding screening programs. Similarly, once identified, there is no evidence to support appropriate treatment of this population, and randomized controlled trials in patients with “pre-COPD” and in young people with COPD are also urgently required.

The data presented herein should be considered in the context that nearly two-thirds of the WALHIP preterm cohort had a neonatal classification of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD; defined by at least 28 days of supplemental oxygen as assessed at 36 weeks postmenstrual age) due to a deliberate BPD enrichment during recruitment. BPD is a risk factor for lower lung function. Further, although some individuals with mild spirometry impairments move within normal limits following the bronchodilator administration, those with post-bronchodilator spirometry within the normal range still demonstrated a high burden of pre-COPD indicators, and their values remained lower than those of healthy term-born individuals.

There is a high prevalence of asthma diagnoses across both the pre-COPD and COPD groups reflecting the well-recognized overlap between asthma-like symptoms and prematurity-associated obstructive lung disease, although the mechanisms of prematurity-associated lung disease remain poorly understood. Because these classifications are based on physiological markers linked to future fixed airflow limitation, identifying these patterns remains clinically relevant and highlights a spectrum of early airway abnormalities that may progress to chronic obstructive physiology in adulthood. Given the limited mechanistic understanding of preterm-related lung disease and its overlap with both asthma- and COPD-like features, these findings should be interpreted as reflecting a COPD-like obstructive pattern rather than a definitive diagnostic label. Regardless, these data highlight the substantial and still underrecognized impending burden of COPD-like lung disease in those born very prematurely in the postsurfactant era.
