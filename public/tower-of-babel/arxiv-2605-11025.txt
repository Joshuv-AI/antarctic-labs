State Canonization and Early Pruning in Width-Based Automated Theorem Proving
Mateus de Oliveira Oliveira, Sam Urmian

Abstract
Width-based automated theorem proving is a framework where counterexamples to graph-theoretic conjectures are searched width-wise relative to some graph width measure, such as treewidth or pathwidth. In a recent work it has been shown that dynamic programming algorithms operating on tree decompositions can be combined together with the purpose of width-based theorem proving. This approach can be used to show that several long-standing conjectures in graph theory can be tested in time 2 2 k O ⁡ ( 1 ) 2^{2^{k^{O(1)}}} on the class of graphs of treewidth at most k k . In this work, we give the first steps towards evaluating the viability of this framework from a practical standpoint. At the same time, we advance the framework in two directions. First, we introduce a state-canonization technique that significantly reduces the number of states evaluated during the search for a counterexample of the conjecture. Second, we introduce an early-pruning technique that can be applied in the study of conjectures of the form ℙ 1 → ℙ 2 \mathbb{P}_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}_{2} , for graph properties ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}_{1} and ℙ 2 \mathbb{P}_{2} , where ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}_{1} is a property closed under subgraphs. As a concrete application, we use our framework in the study of graph-theoretic conjectures related to coloring triangle-free graphs. In particular, our algorithm is able to show that Reed’s conjecture for triangle-free graphs is valid on the class of graphs of pathwidth at most 5, and on graphs of treewidth at most 3. Perhaps more interestingly, our algorithm is able to construct in a completely automated way counterexamples to invalid strengthenings of Reed’s conjecture. These are the first results showing that width-based automated theorem proving is a promising avenue in the study of graph-theoretic conjectures.



1
Introduction

Width-based automated theorem proving is a framework where parameterized algorithms are employed to search for counterexamples to graph-theoretic conjectures.
Within this framework, the search for counterexamples is conducted width-wise, relative to some specific width measure for graphs, such as treewidth or pathwidth. More specifically, given a conjecture
C
and a positive integer
k
, the objective is to determine whether
C
holds on the class of graphs of width at most
k
. If
C
does not hold on this class of graphs, a counterexample of width at most
k
that invalidates the conjecture should be produced.

This approach is relevant for two main reasons. First, many interesting classes of graphs have small width with respect to some graph width measure. For example, trees and forests have treewidth at most
1
, series-parallel graphs have treewidth at most
2
and outerplanar graphs have treewidth at most
2

[
25
,
7
,
10
]
.
It has also been shown that
k
-outerplanar graphs have treewidth
O(k)

[
4
,
24
]
, and that
k
-caterpillars have pathwidth at most
k

[
30
]
. Second, many important conjectures in graph theory are not known to hold on classes of graphs of small treewidth or pathwidth, and it is therefore natural to try to determine whether such conjectures hold when restricted to such classes of graphs.
In many cases, structural properties of graphs of small treewidth or pathwidth have been used to produce analytic proofs of special cases of several well studied conjectures
[
20
,
12
,
21
,
22
,
29
,
34
,
27
]
. The framework of width-based automated theorem proving is an avenue for automating this approach for certain classes of conjectures.

In a recent work
[
16
]
, a new approach for width-based automated theorem proving was introduced. Within this approach, instead of specifying graph properties using logical formulas, such properties are specified using dynamic programming algorithms operating on graph decompositions. More specifically, it was shown in
[
16
]
that given dynamic programming cores
\mathsf{D}_{1},\mathsf{D}_{2},\dots,\mathsf{D}_{r}
specifying graph properties
\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2},\dots,\mathbb{P}_{r}
, and given a Boolean combination
\mathbb{P}
of these properties, there is an algorithm
\mathfrak{A}
that takes a number
k
as input and decides whether all graphs of treewidth at most
k
belong to
\mathbb{P}
. Furthermore, if not all graphs of treewidth at most
k
belong to
\mathbb{P}
, then the algorithm outputs a certificate that can be used to extract a counterexample.
This approach takes double-exponential time with respect to the number of bits necessary to represent local witnesses used by the dynamic programming algorithms. Given that many interesting graph properties have DP-algorithms that use local witnesses of size
k^{O(1)}
when processing tree decompositions of width at most
k
, the dynamic programming approach developed in
[
16
]
implies that many interesting conjectures can be tested in time double-exponential in
k^{O(1)}
on the class of graphs of treewidth at most
k
. This includes long-standing conjectures such as Hadwiger’s conjecture (for a fixed number of colors)
[
9
]
, Tutte’s flow conjectures
[
23
,
28
]
, and Reed’s conjecture for triangle-free graphs
[
31
]
.

1.1
Our Results

In this work, we take the first steps towards evaluating the viability of the width-based automated theorem proving framework introduced in
[
16
]
from a practical perspective. At the same time, we advance this framework by introducing a new width-based deduction algorithm that produces significantly fewer states than the algorithm originally introduced in
[
16
]
. Our new algorithm leverages two techniques. First, we introduce a suitable notion of state canonization. Second, we introduce an early pruning technique that can be used in the study of conjectures of the form
\mathbb{P}_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}_{2}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{1}
is a graph property closed under subgraphs. As a concrete case study, we use our implementation to
study graph-theoretic statements related to colorings of graphs. Our algorithm produced nontrivial counterexamples for false statements and confirmed in a completely automated way a well-known conjecture due to Reed
[
31
]
on the class of graphs of pathwidth at most
5
, and on the class of graphs of treewidth at most
3
.
Together, our results provide the first evidence that the width-based ATP framework introduced in
[
16
]
may be a promising avenue in the study of graph-theoretic conjectures.

The approach for width-based automated theorem proving introduced in
[
16
]
, and further developed in the present work, is heavily based on dynamic programming algorithms deciding graph-theoretic properties. Another suitable approach for the study of width-based automated theorem proving is based on logic
[
14
,
32
,
13
]
. In this context, instead of using dynamic-programming algorithms to define graph properties, such graph properties are defined using logical formulas. For example, it can be shown that for each formula
\varphi
in the monadic second-order logic of graphs, there is an algorithm that takes a positive integer
k
as input and determines whether every graph of treewidth at most
k
satisfies
\varphi

[
32
,
14
]
. The drawback with this approach is that the running time of the deduction algorithm is governed by a function that grows as a tower of exponentials on the quantifier depth of the formula
\varphi
. It is worth noting that several interesting graph-theoretic properties have dynamic programming algorithms that produce local witnesses of size
k^{O(1)}
when processing tree decompositions of width at most
k
, while at the same time requiring large quantifier depth to be expressed in MSO logic
[
16
]
. For such properties, while the time complexity implied by the logic approach is upper bounded by a tower of exponentials on
k
, the time complexity implied by the dynamic-programming approach is upper bounded by a double exponential in
k^{O(1)}
.

Over the years, a large variety of general formalisms have been developed with the aim of solving combinatorial problems on graphs of bounded treewidth. This includes combinatorial approaches based on dynamic programming
[
8
,
6
]
, approaches based on answer set programming
[
18
]
, logic-based model checking
[
14
,
32
,
13
]
, etc.

A complementary line of research studies decomposition-first dynamic programming frameworks and declarative systems
that exploit supplied decompositions, often achieving strong instance-level performance for
satisfaction/optimization tasks
[
18
,
2
]
. Related declarative toolchains
(e.g., ASP systems such as clingo) provide extensible backends that can host decomposition-based methods
in practice
[
19
]
.
Additionally, first-order (FO) model checking has well-studied tractability frontiers tied to locality and structural restrictions, and in practice it is frequently supported by SAT/SMT and first-order ATP backends
[
15
,
3
,
26
,
33
]
.
Nevertheless, the primary focus of these approaches remains per-instance evaluation rather than the evaluation of a given property on a whole class of graphs of bounded width.

Preliminaries

Basic notation.

We denote by
\mathbb{N}~\dot{=}~\{0,1,\dots\}
the set of natural numbers, and by
\mathbb{N}_{+}~\dot{=}~\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
the set of positive natural numbers.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the interval
[n]~\dot{=}~\{1,\dots,n\}
, noting in particular that
[0]=\emptyset
.
Given a set
S
, we write
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(S)
for the set of all
finite
subsets of
S
, and
\mathcal{P}(S)
for the
power set
of
S
, i.e., the set of all subsets of
S
.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
\mathcal{P}(S,k)
for the set of all
k
-element subsets of
S
.
Let
f:X_{1}\times X_{2}\times\dots\times X_{r}\rightarrow X
be a function, and let
S_{i}\subseteq X_{i}
for each
i=1,\dots,r
. We define the image of
f
over the product
S_{1}\times S_{2}\times\dots\times S_{r}
as

f(S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{r})~\dot{=}~\{f(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{r})\mid s_{i}\in S_{i}\text{ for }i=1,\dots,r\}.

A quick reference for the notation used in this paper is provided in Appendix
Summary of Notation
.

Graphs.

In this work, an
undirected

graph
is defined as a triple

G=(V,E,\rho)
,
where
V\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is a
finite
set of
vertices
,

E\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is a finite set of
edges
, and

\rho\subseteq E\times V
is an
incidence relation

with the property that each edge is incident to exactly two vertices.
For each edge
e\in E
, we define its
endpoints
as
\mathrm{endpts}(e)=\{v\in V\mid(e,v)\in\rho\}
.
In what follows, we may write
V(G)
,
E(G)
, and

\rho_{G}
to denote
V
,
E
, and

\rho
, respectively. The
size
of a graph
G
is defined as
|G|=|V(G)|+|E(G)|
.
We denote by
Graphs
the set of all graphs. The
empty graph
is the graph

(\emptyset,\emptyset,\emptyset)
with no vertices and no edges.
Note that vertices and edges are treated as distinct sorts, even though both are encoded as natural numbers; thus it is harmless if the same integer occurs in both
V(G)
and
E(G)
.

Loops, multi-edges, and simplicity.

We consider loopless graphs; multiedges are allowed.
We say that a graph is
simple
if it has no multiedge, i.e., whenever
e\neq e^{\prime}
are distinct edges then
\mathrm{endpts}(e)\neq\mathrm{endpts}(e^{\prime})
.

Disjoint union.

Let
G
be a graph and let
i\in\{0,1\}
. We define the renamed copy
G^{(i)}
by

V(G^{(i)})~\dot{=}~\{2v+i:v\in V(G)\},\qquad E(G^{(i)})~\dot{=}~\{2e+i:e\in E(G)\},

and

\rho_{G^{(i)}}~\dot{=}~\{(2e+i,2v+i):(e,v)\in\rho_{G}\}.

For two graphs
G
and
H
, we define their disjoint union by

G\uplus H~\dot{=}~G^{(0)}\cup H^{(1)}.

That is,
V(G\uplus H)=V(G^{(0)})\cup V(H^{(1)})
,

E(G\uplus H)=E(G^{(0)})\cup E(H^{(1)})
, and

\rho_{G\uplus H}=\rho_{G^{(0)}}\cup\rho_{H^{(1)}}
.
Whenever we say “disjoint union” of two graphs, we mean this fixed construction.

Graph isomorphisms.

An
isomorphism
from a graph
G
to a graph
H
is a pair

\varphi=(\phi,\nu)
where

\phi:V(G)\rightarrow V(H)
is a
bijection from the vertices of
G
to the vertices of
H
and

\nu:E(G)\rightarrow E(H)
is a
bijection from the edges of
G
to the edges of
H
with the
property that for each vertex
v\in V(G)
and each edge

e\in E(G)
,
(e,v)\in\rho_{G}
if and only if

(\nu(e),\phi(v))\in\rho_{H}
.
If such a bijection exists, we say that
G

and
H
are
isomorphic
, and denote this fact by
G\sim H
.

Graph embeddings.

Let
H=(V_{H},E_{H},\rho_{H})
and
G=(V_{G},E_{G},\rho_{G})
be graphs.
An
embedding
of
H
into
G
is a pair
(\phi,\nu)
where
\phi:V_{H}\to V_{G}
and
\nu:E_{H}\to E_{G}
are injective functions such that for every
v\in V_{H}
and every
e\in E_{H}
,

(e,v)\in\rho_{H}\iff(\nu(e),\phi(v))\in\rho_{G}.

We say that
H

embeds as a subgraph
of
G
if such an embedding exists.

Graph properties.

A
graph property
is any subset
\mathbb{P}\subseteq\textsc{Graphs}

closed under isomorphisms. That is to say, for each two isomorphic graphs

G
and
H
in
Graphs
,
G\in\mathbb{P}
if and
only if
H\in\mathbb{P}
. Note that the sets
\emptyset
and

Graphs
are graph properties. Given a set
S
of graphs, the

isomorphism closure
of
S
is defined as the set

\mathsf{ISO}(S)=\{G\in\textsc{Graphs}\;:\;\exists H\in S,G\sim H\}.

We use the standard Boolean operations on graph properties: for graph properties
P,Q\subseteq\textsc{Graphs}
, we write

\neg P~\dot{=}~\textsc{Graphs}\setminus P
,

P\wedge Q~\dot{=}~P\cap Q
,

P\vee Q~\dot{=}~P\cup Q
,
and
P\rightarrow Q~\dot{=}~\neg P\vee Q
.
All of these are again graph properties (they are closed under isomorphism).

Ranked alphabets and terms.

A
ranked alphabet
is a finite set
\Sigma
equipped with a function
\mathfrak{a}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{N}
, which assigns to each symbol in
\Sigma
a non-negative integer called its
arity
.
A
term
over a ranked alphabet
\Sigma
is a pair
\tau=(T,\lambda)
, where
T
is a finite rooted tree and
\lambda:\mathsf{Nodes}(T)\to\Sigma
is a labeling function that assigns to each node
p\in\mathsf{Nodes}(T)
a symbol from
\Sigma
. The set
\mathsf{Nodes}(T)
denotes the set of nodes in the tree
T
, with the root node included, and where each node may have ordered children.
The labeling must respect arities: if
\lambda(p)
has arity
r
, then the node
p
must have exactly
r
children
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
. In particular, leaf nodes are labeled with symbols of arity
0
. We assume that the children of each node are ordered from left to right, so it makes sense to speak of the
i
-th child of a node.
We may write
\mathsf{Nodes}(\tau)
to refer to
\mathsf{Nodes}(T)
, and we use
|\tau|
to denote the number of nodes in the term, i.e.,
|\mathsf{Nodes}(T)|
. The
height
of
\tau
is defined as the height of the tree
T
. The set of all terms over
\Sigma
is denoted by
\mathrm{Terms}(\Sigma)
.
If
\tau_{1}=(T_{1},\lambda_{1}),\dots,\tau_{r}=(T_{r},\lambda_{r})
are terms in
\mathrm{Terms}(\Sigma)
, and
a\in\Sigma
is a symbol of arity
r
, then the term
a(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{r})
is defined after replacing the child terms by isomorphic copies with pairwise disjoint node sets. Thus reusing the same written term in two child positions means taking two fresh copies. The resulting pair is
\tau=(T,\lambda)
where
u
is a fresh node not appearing in any of the copied trees, and the node set of
T
is
\{u\}\cup\mathsf{Nodes}(T_{1})\cup\dots\cup\mathsf{Nodes}(T_{r})
. The root of
T
is
u
, and we define
\lambda(u)=a
. For each
j\in[r]
, the labeling
\lambda
agrees with
\lambda_{j}
on the nodes of
T_{j}
, that is,
\lambda|_{\mathsf{Nodes}(T_{j})}=\lambda_{j}
.

3
Instructive Dynamic Programming Cores

In this work, we represent graphs of treewidth at most
k
using
k
-instructive tree decompositions (
k
-ITDs)
[
16
]
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the following alphabet

\begin{array}[]{l}\Sigma_{k}\;=\Big\{\mathtt{Leaf},\;\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\},\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\},\\
\hskip 142.26378pt\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\},\;\mathtt{Join}\;:\;u,v\in[k+1],u\neq v\Big\},\end{array}

(1)

where
\mathtt{Leaf}
is a symbol of arity
0
,
\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}
,
\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}

and
\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}
are symbols of arity
1
, and
\mathtt{Join}
is a symbol of arity
2
. We call
\Sigma_{k}
the
k
-instructive alphabet
. Intuitively, elements of
\Sigma_{k}
represent instructions that can be used for the construction of a graph of treewidth at most
k
, together with a set
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq[k+1]
of
active labels
, where each active label labels exactly one vertex of the graph.

1.

In the base case, the instruction
\mathtt{Leaf}
creates an empty graph with an empty set of active labels.

2.

Now, let
G
be a graph with set of active labels
\mathfrak{b}
.

(a)

For each
u\in[k+1]\backslash\mathfrak{b}
, the instruction
\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}
adds a new vertex
to
G
, labels this vertex with
u
, and adds
u
to
\mathfrak{b}
.

(b)

For each
u\in\mathfrak{b}
, the
instruction
\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}
erases the label from the current vertex labeled with
u
, and
removes
u
from
\mathfrak{b}
. The intuition is that the label
u
is now free and may be used later in the
creation of another vertex.

(c)

For each two distinct
u,v\in\mathfrak{b}
, the instruction
\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}

introduces a new edge between the current vertex labeled with
u
and the current vertex labeled with
v
.
We note that multiedges are allowed in our graphs.

3.

Finally, if
G
and
G^{\prime}
are two graphs, each having
\mathfrak{b}
as the set of active labels, then the instruction

\mathtt{Join}
creates a new graph by identifying, for each
u\in\mathfrak{b}
, the vertex of
G
labeled
with
u
with the vertex of
G^{\prime}
labeled with
u
.

Such a construction process can be formalized using a term
\tau
over
\Sigma_{k}
, which specifies an inductive construction from the leaves towards the root. More specifically, leaves are labeled with the
\mathtt{Leaf}
instruction, nodes with a single child are labeled with an instruction of type
\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}
,

\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}
, or

\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}
, and nodes with two children are labeled with the
\mathtt{Join}
instruction. We let
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
be the graph associated with the root of
\tau
. We let
B(\tau)
be the set of active labels after processing all operations in
\tau
, and let

\theta[\tau]:B(\tau)\rightarrow V(\mathcal{G}(\tau))
be the map that sends each label in

B(\tau)
to its corresponding vertex in
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
.
We refer to Appendix
B
for a formal inductive definition of
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
.
We note that not all terms
over
\Sigma_{k}
give rise to legal graphs. For instance, if the set
\mathfrak{b}
does not contain a label
u
then the instruction
\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}
is not well defined. Similarly, if
u
is already in
\mathfrak{b}
, then the instruction
\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}
is not well defined. In order to specify the set of all terms over
\Sigma_{k}

that do correspond to graphs, we may use a tree automaton
A_{k}
.
More specifically, we let
A_{k}=(\Sigma_{k},Q_{k},F_{k},\Delta_{k})
be the tree automaton where
Q_{k}=F_{k}=\mathcal{P}([k+1])
, and

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\Delta_{k}&=&\{\mathtt{Leaf}\rightarrow\emptyset\}\\
&\cup&\{\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}(\mathfrak{b})\rightarrow\mathfrak{b}\cup\{u\}\;|\;u\in[k+1]\backslash\mathfrak{b}\}\\
&\cup&\{\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}(\mathfrak{b})\rightarrow\mathfrak{b}\backslash\{u\}\;|\;u\in\mathfrak{b}\}\\
&\cup&\{\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}(\mathfrak{b})\rightarrow\mathfrak{b}\;|\;u,v\in\mathfrak{b},u\neq v\}\\
&\cup&\{\mathtt{Join}(\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{b}^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathfrak{b}\;|\;\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\}.\end{array}

We let
\mathsf{ITD}_{k}=\mathcal{L}(A_{k})
where
\mathcal{L}(A_{k})
is the set of terms accepted by

A_{k}
. The terms in
\mathsf{ITD}_{k}
are called
k
-instructive decompositions
. It turns out that graphs that can be represented by

k
-instructive tree decompositions are precisely the graphs of treewidth at most
k
.

Lemma 1

(
[
16
]
)
.

Let
G\in\textsc{Graphs}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
G
has
treewidth at most
k
if and only if there exists a
k
-instructive tree decomposition
\tau
such that
\mathcal{G}(\tau)\simeq G
.

We also use the join-free specialization of this representation for pathwidth. Let
\mathsf{IPD}_{k}
be the subset of
\mathsf{ITD}_{k}
consisting of terms with no occurrence of the join symbol
\mathtt{Join}
. These are the
k
-instructive path decompositions. As in the standard nice-decomposition representation, a graph has pathwidth at most
k
if and only if it is represented by some term in
\mathsf{IPD}_{k}
. All search algorithms below specialize to pathwidth by using this join-free transition system, i.e., by omitting join successors.

Dynamic programming algorithms operating on tree decompositions can be formalized using the notion of an
instructive dynamic programming core
(instructive DP-core), as defined below.

Definition 2

(Instructive DP-Cores)
.

An
instructive dynamic programming core
is a sequence of
6
-tuples

\mathsf{D}=\{(\Sigma_{k},\mathcal{W}_{k},\mathtt{Final}_{k},\Delta_{k},\mathtt{Clean}_{k},\mathtt{Inv}_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
where for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

1.

\Sigma_{k}
is the
k
-instructive alphabet;

2.

\mathcal{W}_{k}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}
is a decidable subset of
\{0,1\}^{*}
;

3.

\mathtt{Final}_{k}:\mathcal{W}_{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
is a function;

4.

\Delta_{k}
is a set containing

•

A finite subset
\mathtt{Leaf}\subseteq\mathcal{W}_{k}
.

•

A function
\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}:\mathcal{W}_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{W}_{k})
for each
u\in[k+1]
.

•

A function
\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}:\mathcal{W}_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{W}_{k})
for each

u\in[k+1]
.

•

A function
\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}:\mathcal{W}_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{W}_{k})
for each
\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{P}([k+1],2)
.

•

A function
\mathtt{Join}:\mathcal{W}_{k}\times\mathcal{W}_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{W}_{k})
.

5.

\mathtt{Clean}_{k}:\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{W}_{k})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{W}_{k})
is a function;

6.

\mathtt{Inv}_{k}:\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{W}_{k})\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{*}
is a function.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we let

\mathsf{D}[k]=(\Sigma_{k},\mathcal{W}_{k},\mathtt{Final}_{k},\Delta_{k},\mathtt{Clean}_{k},\mathtt{Inv}_{k})

denote the

k
-th tuple of
\mathsf{D}
.
We may write
\mathsf{D}[k].\Sigma
to denote the set

\Sigma_{k}
,
\mathsf{D}[k].\mathcal{W}
to denote the set
\mathcal{W}_{k}
,
and so on.
Intuitively, for each
k
,
\mathsf{D}[k]
is a description of a dynamic programming algorithm that operates on
k
-instructive tree decompositions. Such an algorithm processes a
k
-instructive tree decomposition
\tau
from the leaves
towards the root, and assigns a set of local witnesses to each node of
\tau
, depending on which instruction labels the node and on the sets assigned to the children of the node.
The transition functions above are defined on individual witnesses. Whenever we apply a transition to finite witness sets, we use the following union liftings:

\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}(S)~\dot{=}~\bigcup_{\mathsf{w}\in S}\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}(\mathsf{w}),

\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}(S)~\dot{=}~\bigcup_{\mathsf{w}\in S}\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}(\mathsf{w}),

\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}(S)~\dot{=}~\bigcup_{\mathsf{w}\in S}\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}(\mathsf{w}),

5
Width-Based ATP with Symmetry Breaking

In this section, we introduce our main technical result. More specifically, we introduce a width-based automated deduction algorithm endowed with a symmetry-breaking procedure that allows us to remove redundant states during the search for a counterexample to a given conjecture. At the core of our technique lies the notion of a
witness action
.
Intuitively, functions that satisfy the axioms of a witness action can be used to define permuted versions of local witnesses generated by a DP-core. This allows us to define the notion of a canonical form of a state generated during the search process. Instead of keeping track of all inferred states, we only keep their canonical forms. This leads to a significant reduction of the search space because states with the same canonical form are identified. Our main theorem (Theorem
27
) states that this process preserves provability.

5.1
Relabelings and Witness Actions

A DP-state is indexed by an interface bag
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq[k+1]
and a set of local witnesses
S
. The labels in
[k+1]
are abstract names: permuting them does not change the underlying partial graph represented by an instructive tree decomposition prefix. Consequently, a naive search explores many states that are identical up to relabeling. State canonization quotients the search space by these relabelings by mapping each state to a canonical representative, so that each equivalence class is explored once.

A
k
-relabeling function
is an injective function

, where
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq[k+1]
. We let

denote the set of all such functions.
We use injections (rather than permutations) because the active bag size varies across the decomposition; thus relabelings behave like partial permutations whose domain is the current bag.
Given a

-relabeling function

, and a subset
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq[k+1]
, we let

be the image of
\mathfrak{b}
under
f
. Next, we define the notion of a witness action.

For relabeling functions

, we write

for the partial function with domain

\{u\in dom(g):g(u)\in dom(f)\}

given by

each

(a)

5.2
Relabeled Refutations

A standard
(k,\mathsf{D})
-refutation (Definition
5
) shows that an inconsistent state is reachable from the initial state by DP-operations. In the presence of symmetry, we also want to allow intermediate states to be renamed (canonized) on the fly. Relabeled refutations formalize exactly this: each inference step applies a DP-operation and then (optionally, via the identity relabeling) applies an admissible relabeling transported to witnesses by the action.

We next define relabeled refutations (Definition
21
), which allow intermediate states to be renamed via relabelings transported to witnesses by the action.
In the join clause, we additionally allow an internal permutation of the shared interface bag to align the second witness set with the labeling used by the first before applying

For a bag

Introduce edge.

4.

Join.

Assume

5.3
Canonical Pairs and Canonized Refutations

Let
U
be an ordered set of elements, and let
X\subseteq U
be finite. We let
\mathrm{vec}(X)
be the vector obtained by
ordering the elements of
X
from the smallest
value to the largest value. For instance, if
X=\{2,3,5\}
, then
\mathrm{vec}(X)=(2,3,5)
.
Given two such subsets
X,X^{\prime}\subseteq U
, we say
that
X<X^{\prime}
if
\mathrm{vec}(X)
is
lexicographically smaller than
\mathrm{vec}(X^{\prime})
.
We use the standard lexicographic order on finite sequences: compare the first position where they differ, and if one sequence is a strict prefix of the other then the shorter sequence is smaller.

Let
\mathsf{D}
be a DP-core and fix an arbitrary
order for the set
\mathsf{D}[k].\mathcal{W}
. For instance,
this order can be simply the lexicographic order on strings. We say that a pair
(\mathfrak{b},S)

is smaller than
(\mathfrak{b}^{\prime},S^{\prime})
if the pair

(\mathrm{vec}(\mathfrak{b}),\mathrm{vec}(S))
is lexicographically smaller than the pair

(\mathrm{vec}(\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}),\mathrm{vec}(S^{\prime}))
.
Any fixed total order yields a correct canonization procedure; the specific order only affects which representative is chosen.

Definition 24

(Canonical Pair)
.

Let
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq[k+1]
and let
S\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathsf{D}[k].\mathcal{W})
be such that
(\mathfrak{b},S)
is well-formed, i.e.,
\mathrm{Lbl}_{k}(S)\subseteq\mathfrak{b}
.

\mathrm{CAN}_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(\mathfrak{b},S)=\min\{(f(\mathfrak{b}),\rho_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(f,S))\;|\;f\in\mathcal{F}_{k},\mathit{dom(f)=\mathfrak{b}}\}.

We call any function
f:\mathfrak{b}\rightarrow[k+1]
where the minimum in the above equation is achieved a
canonical relabeling of
(\mathfrak{b},S)
.
We say that
(\mathfrak{b},S)
is
canonical
if
\mathrm{CAN}_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(\mathfrak{b},S)=(\mathfrak{b},S)
.

Lemma 25

(Canonical representatives are orbit-invariant)
.

Let
\mathsf{D}[k]
be equipped with a witness action. Let
(\mathfrak{b},S)
be well-formed and let
f:\mathfrak{b}\to[k+1]
be injective. If

(\mathfrak{b}^{\prime},S^{\prime})=(f(\mathfrak{b}),\rho_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(f,S)),

then

\mathrm{CAN}_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(\mathfrak{b}^{\prime},S^{\prime})=\mathrm{CAN}_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(\mathfrak{b},S).

Proof.

The two minima in Definition
24
are taken over the same relabeling orbit. Indeed, for every relabeling
h:\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\to[k+1]
, the composition
h\circ f:\mathfrak{b}\to[k+1]
is a relabeling and, by the composition axiom,

(h(\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}),\rho_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(h,S^{\prime}))=((h\circ f)(\mathfrak{b}),\rho_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(h\circ f,S)).

Conversely, for every relabeling
g:\mathfrak{b}\to[k+1]
, the map
g\circ f^{-1}:\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\to[k+1]
is a relabeling and inverse relabeling plus composition give

((g\circ f^{-1})(\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}),\rho_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(g\circ f^{-1},S^{\prime}))=(g(\mathfrak{b}),\rho_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(g,S)).

Thus the two sets of candidate representatives are equal, and their lexicographic minima are equal.
∎

By Lemma
16
, if
\mathsf{D}
is interface-respecting then all pairs arising in refutations and in dynamization are well-formed, and therefore
\mathrm{CAN}_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}
is defined for all pairs considered in this section. Moreover, since the bag component is compared before the witness-set component, canonization normalizes bags: any two canonical pairs with bags of the same size have the same bag component.

Intuitively, given a fixed action
\rho_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}
the canonical form

\mathrm{CAN}_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(\mathfrak{b},S)

of a pair
(\mathfrak{b},S)
is the lexicographically smallest pair obtained by relabeling the elements of
\mathfrak{b}
and each string
\mathsf{w}\in S
according to some relabeling function
f:\mathfrak{b}\rightarrow[k+1]
. A
canonical relabeling
of
(\mathfrak{b},S)
is any function
f:\mathfrak{b}\rightarrow[k+1]

with the property that
(f(\mathfrak{b}),\rho_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(f,S))=\mathrm{CAN}_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(\mathfrak{b},S)
.

For example, consider
k=3
, bag
\mathfrak{b}=\{2,4\}
, and a singleton witness set
S=\{\gamma\}
where (for example, in the max-degree core)
\gamma
is a map with
\gamma(2)=0
and
\gamma(4)=1
.
Let
f_{1}(2)=1,f_{1}(4)=2
and
f_{2}(2)=2,f_{2}(4)=1
.
Then
\rho_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(f_{1},S)=\{\gamma_{1}\}
where
\gamma_{1}(1)=0,\gamma_{1}(2)=1
, while
\rho_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(f_{2},S)=\{\gamma_{2}\}
where
\gamma_{2}(1)=1,\gamma_{2}(2)=0
.
Canonization selects the lexicographically smallest among these relabeled pairs.

Next, we define the notion of a canonized refutation. Intuitively, such a refutation is obtained by canonizing the
(k,\mathsf{D})
-pairs occurring in a

(k,\mathsf{D})
-refutation.

Definition 26

(Canonized Refutation)
.

Let
\mathsf{D}
be a coherent DP-core, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
F=(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})
be a sequence of relabelings, and

R\equiv(\mathfrak{b}_{0},S_{0})(\mathfrak{b}_{1},S_{1})\dots(\mathfrak{b}_{m},S_{m})

be an
F
-relabeled
(k,\mathsf{D})
-refutation. We say that
R
is canonized if for each
i\in\{0,1,\dots,m\}
, the pair
(\mathfrak{b}_{i},S_{i})
is canonical.

Canonized refutations formalize quotienting the state space by interface relabelings: after each DP step we replace the resulting state by its canonical representative. Witness actions are the key requirement that makes this sound: they ensure that relabelings commute with the DP transitions, so canonizing “on the fly” preserves reachability of inconsistent states.

Our main theorem (Theorem
27
) states that if
\mathsf{D}
is a coherent interface-respecting DP-core with identity cleaning and equipped with a witness action, then the existence of a canonized

(k,\mathsf{D})
-refutation is equivalent to the existence of a graph of treewidth
at most
k
that does not belong to the property

\mathbb{G}(\mathsf{D})
defined by
\mathsf{D}
.

Theorem 27
.

Let
\mathsf{D}
be a coherent interface-respecting DP-core with identity cleaning and equipped with a witness action and

.
Then there is a canonized
(k,\mathsf{D})
-refutation if and only if some graph of treewidth at most

k
does not belong to
\mathbb{G}(\mathsf{D})

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
)
If there is a canonized
(k,\mathsf{D})
-refutation, then in particular there is an
F
-relabeled
(k,\mathsf{D})
-refutation. By Theorem
23
, there exists a
k
-instructive tree decomposition
\tau
with
\mathcal{G}(\tau)\notin\mathbb{G}(\mathsf{D})
.

(
\Leftarrow
)
Assume that some graph of treewidth at most
k
does not belong to
\mathbb{G}(\mathsf{D})
. By Theorem
6
, there exists a
(k,\mathsf{D})
-refutation

R=(\mathfrak{b}_{0},S_{0})(\mathfrak{b}_{1},S_{1})\dots(\mathfrak{b}_{m},S_{m})

in the sense of Definition

.
Since
\mathsf{D}
is interface-respecting, Lemma

implies that

is well-formed, and therefore each application of

below is well-defined.
For each

Fix

1.

Introduce vertex.

Forget vertex.

If

5.4
Symmetry-Aware Search

We now give a symmetry-aware state-space search procedure for a fixed width parameter
k
.
Here
\mathsf{D}
denotes a
k
-abstract DP-core; in general,
\mathsf{D}
may itself be obtained as a
k
-abstract DP-combination
(i.e., by combining several DP-cores according to a Boolean formula) as in
[
16
]
.
Here “
k
-abstract” means that the DP-core (or combination) is specialized to the fixed interface label universe
[k+1]
.

We explore the implicit transition system of
(k,\mathsf{D})
-states by a standard breadth-first search, starting from the initial state.
Each generated successor is immediately canonized, so the search runs directly on canonical representatives in the quotient space induced by relabelings.
Algorithm
1
specifies the exploration and returns a refutation trace as soon as an inconsistent state is reached (otherwise it reports that inclusion holds).
In the join case, the current state is combined with previously processed states with the same bag (in both argument orders), considering all permutations of the shared interface.

input :
A finite, coherent, interface-respecting
k
-abstract DP-combination
\mathsf{D}
with identity cleaning and equipped with a witness action

output :
A canonized relabeled
(k,\mathsf{D})
-refutation trace
R
if there exists
G\in\textsc{GraphsTw}[k]
with
G\notin\mathbb{G}(\mathsf{D})
, and ”Inclusion Holds”, otherwise.

R\leftarrow[]
;

1

Seen\leftarrow\emptyset
;

2

Init\leftarrow\mathrm{CAN}_{\mathsf{D}}^{k}(\emptyset,\mathsf{D}[k].\mathtt{Leaf})
;

3

Y\leftarrow[Init]
;

4

Seen\leftarrow Seen\cup\{Init\}
;

5

6
Early Pruning

Our second main contribution is an early-pruning procedure that can be applied in the study of conjectures of the form
\mathbb{P}_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}_{2}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{1}
is closed under subgraphs. This reduces the search space even more because it allows us to avoid the computation of states that do not contribute to the search for a counterexample.

A graph property
\mathbb{P}
is said to be closed under subgraphs if
whenever a graph
G
belongs to
\mathbb{P}
, we have that
every subgraph of
G
also belongs to
\mathbb{P}
.
For example, the property of being
3
-colorable is closed under subgraphs.
In contrast, the property of having minimum degree at least
d
is not closed under subgraphs.
If the graph property
\mathbb{G}(\mathsf{D})
specified by a given coherent DP-core
\mathsf{D}
is of the form
\mathbb{G}(\mathsf{D}_{1})\rightarrow\mathbb{G}(\mathsf{D}_{2})
for coherent DP-cores
\mathsf{D}_{1}
and
\mathsf{D}_{2}
, such that
\mathbb{G}(\mathsf{D}_{1})
is closed under subgraphs, then when running our inference algorithm to
determine whether some graph of treewidth at most
k
is not contained in
\mathbb{G}(\mathsf{D})
we may prune the search earlier.
The following simple, but crucial observation is the basis of our specialized search.

Observation 30
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a graph property closed under subgraphs. Let
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
be
k
-instructive tree decompositions such that
\tau
is a subterm of
\tau^{\prime}
. Then, if
\mathcal{G}(\tau)\notin\mathbb{P}
, then
\mathcal{G}(\tau^{\prime})\notin\mathbb{P}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathcal{G}(\tau)\notin\mathbb{P}
. Since
\tau
is a subterm of
\tau^{\prime}
, Lemma
46
yields that
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is isomorphic to a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(\tau^{\prime})
. Suppose
\mathcal{G}(\tau^{\prime})\in\mathbb{P}
. Since
\mathbb{P}
is closed under subgraphs, that subgraph belongs to
\mathbb{P}
; because graph properties are taken up to isomorphism,
\mathcal{G}(\tau)\in\mathbb{P}
. This contradicts the assumption that
\mathcal{G}(\tau)\notin\mathbb{P}
.
∎

yields that
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
is isomorphic to a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
. Since
P_{1}
is closed under subgraphs, graph properties are taken up to isomorphism, and
\mathcal{G}(\tau)\in P_{1}
, we have
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)\in P_{1}
. Equivalently,
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)\in\mathbb{G}(\mathsf{D}_{1})
, so coherence of
\mathsf{D}_{1}
implies that
\sigma
is accepted by
\mathsf{D}_{1}[k]
. Hence
\Gamma[{\mathsf{D}_{1},k}](\sigma)
contains a final witness, i.e., the premise holds at every intermediate state.
Algorithm
2
enumerates all successors of every dequeued premise-satisfying state and records each generated state at most once. Therefore the premise-satisfying derivation above is eventually generated, and the algorithm returns an inconsistent trace. Conversely, if the algorithm returns such a trace, the soundness argument gives a counterexample. If the finite queue is exhausted, no reachable inconsistent state exists, and by completeness no counterexample exists.
∎

6.2
Canonized Premise Search

The implementation used in the experiments combines the premise-pruned transition system above with the canonization procedure of Section
5.4
. We formalize this combined version as follows. Assume that
\mathsf{D}_{1}
and
\mathsf{D}_{2}
are interface-respecting DP-cores with identity cleaning and with witness actions. For a relabeling function
f:\mathfrak{b}\to[k+1]
, define the product action on triples by

f\cdot(\mathfrak{b},S_{1},S_{2})~\dot{=}~\bigl(f(\mathfrak{b}),\rho_{\mathsf{D}_{1}}^{k}(f,S_{1}),\rho_{\mathsf{D}_{2}}^{k}(f,S_{2})\bigr).

Fix total orders on
\mathsf{D}_{1}[k].\mathcal{W}
and
\mathsf{D}_{2}[k].\mathcal{W}
, and order triples lexicographically by their bag vector and the two witness-set vectors. For every well-formed triple, define

\mathrm{CAN}_{\mathsf{D}_{1},\mathsf{D}_{2}}^{k}(\mathfrak{b},S_{1},S_{2})~\dot{=}~\min\{f\cdot(\mathfrak{b},S_{1},S_{2})\mid f\in\mathcal{F}_{k},\ dom(f)=\mathfrak{b}\}.

Because the two component actions preserve final witnesses, both premise satisfaction and inconsistency are invariant under this canonical replacement.

Lemma 32

(Product canonical representatives are orbit-invariant)
.

Let
(\mathfrak{b},S_{1},S_{2})
be a well-formed triple and let
f:\mathfrak{b}\to[k+1]
be injective. If

(\mathfrak{b}^{\prime},S^{\prime}_{1},S^{\prime}_{2})=f\cdot(\mathfrak{b},S_{1},S_{2}),

then

\mathrm{CAN}_{\mathsf{D}_{1},\mathsf{D}_{2}}^{k}(\mathfrak{b}^{\prime},S^{\prime}_{1},S^{\prime}_{2})=\mathrm{CAN}_{\mathsf{D}_{1},\mathsf{D}_{2}}^{k}(\mathfrak{b},S_{1},S_{2}).

Proof.

This is the componentwise version of Lemma
25
. For every relabeling
h:\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\to[k+1]
, the product action of
h
on the primed triple equals the product action of
h\circ f
on the original triple, by composition in both component witness actions. Conversely, every relabeling
g:\mathfrak{b}\to[k+1]
is obtained from the primed side as
g\circ f^{-1}
, using inverse relabeling componentwise. Hence the two sets of candidate triples in the definition of
\mathrm{CAN}_{\mathsf{D}_{1},\mathsf{D}_{2}}^{k}
are equal, and so their minima are equal.
∎

Lemma 33

(Product relabeled traces are realizable)
.

Let
\mathsf{D}_{1}
and
\mathsf{D}_{2}
be DP-cores with identity cleaning and witness actions.
Consider a finite well-formed product trace

(\mathfrak{b}_{0},S_{1,0},S_{2,0}),\dots,(\mathfrak{b}_{m},S_{1,m},S_{2,m})

obtained by the componentwise relabeled rules of Definition
21
, using the same constructor, indices, relabeling, and join-bag permutation in both components at each step.
Then, for every relabeling
g:\mathfrak{b}_{m}\to[k+1]
, there is a
k
-instructive tree decomposition
\tau
such that

B(\tau)=g(\mathfrak{b}_{m}),

and, for each
q\in\{1,2\}
,

\Gamma[{\mathsf{D}_{q},k}](\tau)=\rho_{\mathsf{D}_{q}}^{k}(g,S_{q,m}).

Proof.

This is Lemma
22
applied simultaneously to the two components.
The product trace uses one underlying constructor at each step, so the induction in that lemma constructs the same instructive decomposition for both components.
The well-definedness checks for relabelings and join permutations are componentwise: support containment follows in each component exactly as in Lemma
22
, and the same relabeling
g
is applied to the common final bag.
Identity cleaning ensures that the constructed subterm witness sets coincide with the dynamizations of
\mathsf{D}_{1}
and
\mathsf{D}_{2}
.
∎

The
ISO-BFS-premise
search is Algorithm
2
with the following two modifications. First, the initial triple and every generated successor triple are replaced by their canonical representative under
\mathrm{CAN}_{\mathsf{D}_{1},\mathsf{D}_{2}}^{k}
before lookup in
Seen
and insertion in
Y
. Second, in the join case the algorithm ranges over all earlier processed triples with the same bag, all permutations of the shared bag, and both argument orders, exactly as in Algorithm
1
, applying the corresponding relabeling to the second join argument before the componentwise join. The pathwidth version is obtained by omitting join successors.

Theorem 34

(Correctness of ISO-BFS-premise)
.

Assume that
\mathsf{D}_{1}
and
\mathsf{D}_{2}
are finite, coherent, interface-respecting DP-cores with identity cleaning and witness actions, and that
P_{1}=\mathbb{G}(\mathsf{D}_{1})
is closed under subgraphs. Then ISO-BFS-premise terminates. Moreover, it returns “Inclusion Holds” if and only if there is no graph
G
of treewidth at most
k
such that
G\in P_{1}
and
G\notin P_{2}
; otherwise it returns a canonized premise-pruned trace ending in an inconsistent triple.

Proof.

Finiteness gives only finitely many canonical triples, and the algorithm inserts each of them into
Seen
at most once, so termination follows.

Soundness is the componentwise analogue of Lemma
28
. Every returned trace is obtained from the initial triple by applying one of the lifted DP-transitions to the two components and then applying a product-action relabeling; in the join case, the same bag permutation is used in both components. Thus the returned trace is a product relabeled trace in the sense of Lemma
33
. Applying that lemma to the identity relabeling on the final bag gives one
k
-instructive tree decomposition whose two dynamizations are the two witness sets in the final triple. Since the final triple is inconsistent, this decomposition is accepted by
\mathsf{D}_{1}
and rejected by
\mathsf{D}_{2}
. Coherence then gives a graph
G
of treewidth at most
k
with
G\in P_{1}
and
G\notin P_{2}
.

For completeness, suppose such a graph exists and choose a
k
-instructive tree decomposition
\tau
representing it. The proof of Theorem
31
gives a premise-satisfying derivation of an inconsistent triple from the subterms of
\tau
. Canonizing each triple in that derivation preserves premise satisfaction and inconsistency, and the product-action commutation laws ensure that every canonized successor is among the successors enumerated by ISO-BFS-premise. Whenever the enumerated successor differs from the canonized derivation only by a relabeling of the interface, Lemma
32
identifies their canonical representatives. Thus the breadth-first exploration must eventually generate an inconsistent canonical triple unless it has already returned one. Therefore, if the algorithm exhausts the search and returns “Inclusion Holds”, no such graph exists.
∎

7
Reed’s Conjecture Parameterized by Treewidth

In this section, we provide a concrete example of how dynamic programming algorithms can be used to provide asymptotic upper bounds on the time complexity of verifying whether a given graph-theoretic conjecture is valid on the class of graphs of width at most
k
. More specifically, we analyze the following well-known conjecture due to Reed
[
31
]
, which establishes an upper bound on the chromatic number
\chi(G)
of a triangle-free graph
G
in terms of the maximum degree
\Delta(G)
of
G
.

Conjecture 35
.

For any simple, triangle-free, undirected graph
G
,

\chi(G)\leq\left\lceil\frac{\Delta(G)+3}{2}\right\rceil.

Note that graphs of treewidth at most
k
are
(k+1)
-colorable
[
5
]
. The following theorem due to Dvořák and Kawarabayashi establishes a better upper bound for the chromatic number of triangle-free graphs in terms of treewidth.

Theorem 36

(
[
17
]
)
.

For any triangle-free graph
G
of treewidth
\leq k
,
\chi(G)\leq\left\lceil\frac{k+3}{2}\right\rceil
.

Therefore, in order to prove that every graph of treewidth at most
k
satisfies
Conjecture
35
, it is enough to consider graphs whose maximum degree is at most
k-1
: if
\Delta(G)\geq k
, then Theorem
36
implies the conjecture. More precisely, it suffices to show that for each
s\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}
, every graph of treewidth at most
k
and maximum degree at most
s
has chromatic number at most
\lceil\frac{s+3}{2}\rceil
. For particular values of
k
this range can sometimes be shortened further; for example, when
k=5
, the case
s=4
is already discharged by Theorem
36
, since both bounds are equal to
4
.

Now, let
\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{ChromaticNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtMost}(r)
denote the graph property consisting of all graphs that are
r
-colorable,
\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)

denote the graph property consisting of all graphs that have maximum degree at least
d
,
\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)
be the property consisting of all graphs that have clique number at least
\omega
, and
\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}
be the property consisting of all graphs that have some multiple edges. Let

\textsc{Reed}(s)
be the graph property

\displaystyle\textsc{Reed}(s)\equiv{}

\displaystyle\bigl(\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}\wedge\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(3)

(2)

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\wedge\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(s+1)\bigr)

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\rightarrow\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{ChromaticNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtMost}(\lceil(s+3)/2\rceil).

Here,
\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}
enforces simplicity and
\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(3)
enforces triangle-freeness.
Moreover,
\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(s+1)
is equivalent to
\Delta(G)\leq s
, and the conclusion asserts
\lceil(s+3)/2\rceil
-colorability.
The premise
\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}\wedge\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(3)\wedge\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(s+1)
is closed under subgraphs, so the early-pruning procedure from Section
6
applies when searching for counterexamples to
\textsc{Reed}(s)
.
Indeed, subgraphs of simple graphs are simple, subgraphs cannot create a triangle, and taking subgraphs cannot increase the maximum degree.

Then determining whether all graphs of treewidth at most
k
satisfy Reed’s conjecture is equivalent to determining whether for each
s\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
,
the set of all graphs of treewidth at most
k

is contained in property
\textsc{Reed}(s)
. Now,

\textsc{Reed}(s)
is a Boolean
combination of four properties
represented by finite DP-cores of polynomial bit-length in
k
(see Theorem
37
below). The simple-clique core is used only together with the simplicity mask
\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}
, which is the setting in which its correctness statement applies. The specification of each of the four DP-cores can be found in the appendix. Such implementations are of independent interest, because each such core may be viewed as an algorithm that takes a
k
-instructive tree decomposition
\tau
as input and decides whether the graph
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
associated with
\tau
satisfies the corresponding property represented by the core.

Theorem 37

(DP-Cores for Reed’s Conjecture)
.

For each
d,r\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and each
\omega\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
with
\omega\geq 2
, there exist finite, interface-respecting instructive DP-cores with the cleaning functions specified in their definitions and witness actions, denoted

\begin{gathered}\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d),\quad\mbox{ChromaticNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtMost}(r),\\
\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega),\quad\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},\end{gathered}

satisfying the following properties.

1.

\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)
has bit-length
O(k\cdot\log(d+1))
and

\mathbb{G}(\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d))=\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)
.

2.

\mbox{ChromaticNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtMost}(r)
has bit-length
O(k\cdot\log(r+1))
and

\mathbb{G}(\mbox{ChromaticNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtMost}(r))=\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{ChromaticNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtMost}(r).

3.

\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)
has bit-length
O(k\cdot\log\omega)
and its defined graph property agrees with
\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)
after intersecting both sides with
\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}
.

4.

HasMultipleEdges
has bit-length
O(k^{2})
and
\mathbb{G}(\mbox{HasMultipleEdges})=\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}

The cores in items 1, 2, and 4 are coherent. The simple-clique core is used below only under the mask
\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}
, and item 3 is the coherent simple-graph property needed for Reed’s formula.

Proof.

Take the four cores defined in Section
4
and in Appendix
C
. Each definition is an instructive DP-core; the cleaning function is the identity except for the simple-clique core, whose cleaning function keeps only the found witness once such a witness is present. The transition rules mention only the current active labels, remove forgotten labels from the local data, and use label sets as the support of local witnesses; hence the cores are interface-respecting.

For
\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)
, Corollary
50
gives

\mathbb{G}(\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d))=\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)
, and Observation
51
gives bit-length
O(k\cdot\log(d+1))
. Lemma
52
gives the witness action. Coherence follows from the correctness corollary because maximum degree is invariant under graph isomorphism.

For
\mbox{ChromaticNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtMost}(r)
, Corollary
11
gives

\mathbb{G}(\mbox{ChromaticNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtMost}(r))=\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{ChromaticNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtMost}(r).

Observation
12
gives bit-length
O(k\cdot\log(r+1))
. Lemma
20
gives the witness action. Coherence follows because
r
-colorability is invariant under graph isomorphism.

For
\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)
, Corollary
56
proves correctness on simple graphs, and this is exactly the equality after intersecting both sides with
\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}
. Observation
57
gives bit-length
O(k\cdot\log\omega)
, and Lemma
58
gives the witness action. The simple-graph restriction is essential here: the Reed formula uses this core only together with
\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}
, so the stated property equality is the one needed in Equation (
2
). Coherence on this restricted use follows because clique existence in simple graphs is invariant under graph isomorphism.

For
HasMultipleEdges
, Corollary
62
gives

\mathbb{G}(\mbox{HasMultipleEdges})=\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}
, Observation
63
gives bit-length
O(k^{2})
, and Lemma
64
gives the witness action. Coherence follows because the existence of multiple edges is invariant under graph isomorphism. Finiteness of all four cores follows from the displayed bit-length bounds for fixed
k
and fixed parameters.
∎

Lemma 38

(Identity-cleaning simple-clique variant)
.

Let
\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)^{\mathrm{id}}
be obtained from
\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)
by replacing its cleaning function by the identity function and leaving all transition rules unchanged.
For every
k
-instructive tree decomposition
\tau
whose graph is simple,

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle\in\mathsf{Accepted}(\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)[k])

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\tau\in\mathsf{Accepted}(\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)^{\mathrm{id}}[k]).

Proof.

In the simple-clique core the only final witness is
\mathfrak{b}
, and every transition preserves
\mathfrak{b}
once it appears.
The displayed cleaning function is the identity on witness sets not containing
\mathfrak{b}
and maps every witness set containing
\mathfrak{b}
to
\{\mathfrak{b}\}
.
Therefore, by induction over the term, the cleaned and identity-cleaned computations contain
\mathfrak{b}
at exactly the same subterms.
Acceptance depends only on the presence of
\mathfrak{b}
, so the two variants accept the same simple graphs.
∎

The simple-clique cleaning function above is the one used by the TreeWidzard implementation. For applications of the search theorems that are stated under identity cleaning, we use the identity-cleaning variant from Lemma
38
.

Lemma 39

(Masked simple-clique coherence for Reed)
.

For each
s\in\mathbb{N}
, the Boolean combination in Equation (
2
) is coherent when the triangle-freeness conjunct is represented by
\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(3)
only under the simultaneous mask
\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}
.

Proof.

Let two instructive decompositions represent isomorphic graphs. The properties

\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}
,
\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(s+1)
, and

\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{ChromaticNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtMost}(\lceil(s+3)/2\rceil)
are invariant under graph
isomorphism by Corollaries
62
,

50
, and
11
.
If the represented graph has a multiple edge, then the premise of
Equation (
2
) is false because of the conjunct

\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}
, so the value of the simple-clique component is
irrelevant. If the represented graph has no multiple edge, then it is simple,
and Corollary
56
identifies
the simple-clique core with the ordinary clique property on both isomorphic

∎

For the Reed searches, the four component cores are combined by the standard DP-combination construction of
[

, applied to the identity-cleaning variants when a search theorem assumes identity cleaning. The construction uses product witness sets and componentwise witness actions, so it preserves finiteness, interface-respectingness, identity cleaning, and the stated bit-length bound up to the sum of the component bit-lengths. Coherence of the resulting combination follows from the component correctness statements above, Lemma

Therefore, as a consequence of Theorem

Corollary 40
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, Reed’s conjecture for triangle-free graphs can be tested in time double-exponential in

Proof.

For a fixed

O(k\log(s+2))+O(k\log(r+1))+O(k\log 4)+O(k^{2})=O(k^{2}).

Hence the combined witness universe has size at most
2^{O(k^{2})}
, and a search state is a finite subset of this universe, so the number of states is at most
2^{2^{O(k^{2})}}
.
The finite alphabet operations, relabeling choices, and the factor of
k
choices for

Theorem
29
and Theorem

therefore give a decision procedure within time
2^{2^{O(k^{2})}}
.
∎

8.2
Automatically Validating Reed’s Triangle-Free Conjecture on Graphs of Small Pathwidth and Treewidth

When testing a graph-theoretic conjecture on the class of graphs of pathwidth/treewidth at most
k
, our deduction algorithm either produces a counterexample or exhausts the search space; in the latter case, the conjecture is valid on that class.

When combining both the symmetry-breaking technique introduced in Section
5
with the early-pruning technique introduced in Section
6
, our software targets Conjecture
35
in the class of graphs of pathwidth at most
5

5
implies treewidth at most
5

36
gives
\chi(G)\leq 4
for triangle-free graphs; for
\Delta(G)\geq 4

\Delta(G)\leq 3

\chi(G)\leq 3

[
11
]
. Thus we use the pathwidth-

For the automated search in this case, it therefore suffices to consider graphs of maximum degree at most
3

8.3
Multiplicity

The multiplicity of a DP-core with respect to a search strategy measures the maximum number of local witnesses produced by the core during the construction of states. Formally, for each explored state and each successor-generation step, we record the size of that component core’s lifted transition output on the current witness set (or pair of witness sets for joins), and the table entry is the maximum of this quantity over the run. Thus the reported values are state-level lifted-output maxima, not merely
\max_{w}|\delta(w)|
over single witnesses. Table
5
reports these multiplicities. Each entry has four components corresponding to the multiplicities of the cores
ChromaticNumber_AtMost
,
MaximumDegree_AtLeast
,
SimpleCliqueNumber_AtLeast
, and
HasMultipleEdges
, respectively. Note that
MaximumDegree_AtLeast
and
HasMultipleEdges
are deterministic DP-cores, and therefore the multiplicity of these cores is
1
regardless of the value of treewidth/pathwidth.
Higher multiplicity directly increases the branching factor in the state graph (more successor witnesses per step), which amplifies the benefit of canonization and premise pruning.

9
Conclusion

In this work, we took initial steps toward evaluating the width-based automated theorem proving approach introduced in
[
16
]
in practice. We implemented this approach in
TreeWidzard
and introduced two complementary search reductions: state canonization and early pruning for implications with subgraph-closed premises. Together, these techniques drastically reduce the explored state space in practice. Our experiments on coloring statements for triangle-free graphs demonstrate orders-of-magnitude reductions, automatically generate counterexamples to false strengthenings, and enable automated bounded-width validation of Reed’s triangle-free conjecture on graphs of pathwidth at most
5
and treewidth at most
3
.

It is worth highlighting the modularity of our approach. While the implementation of instructive dynamic programming cores requires specialized knowledge on the part of the programmer, the use of such cores once they have been implemented is straightforward. For instance, in our framework, the critical case for testing Reed’s conjecture on graphs of pathwidth at most
5
is the case where the degree is at most
3
. More precisely, we test the conjecture that all simple, triangle-free graphs of maximum degree at most
3
have chromatic number at most
3
. This conjecture is stated in TreeWidzard property-file syntax as follows, where the first four lines each correspond to a graph property, and the last line corresponds to the conjecture being tested.

x := MaximumDegree_AtLeast(4)
y := SimpleCliqueNumber_AtLeast(3)
z := HasMultipleEdges()
w := ChromaticNumber_AtMost(3)
Formula
NOT x AND NOT y AND NOT z IMPLIES w

The idea is that dynamic programming cores deciding graph properties are implemented as plugins that need to be implemented only once by a specialist and then used without difficulty by graph theorists. We believe that our approach has the potential to create a productive exchange of ideas between the community of researchers working on parameterized complexity theory and researchers working in automated theorem proving. In essence, our framework shows that three decades of accumulated knowledge obtained in the development of faster width-based parameterized algorithms for model checking may be put into use in the context of automated theorem proving.

Appendix B
Graph Associated with a
k
-Instructive Tree Decomposition

This appendix makes explicit the semantics of the constructors in the instructive alphabet by defining, for each
k
-instructive decomposition
\tau
, the associated graph
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
, the current interface bag
B(\tau)
, and the interface map
\theta[\tau]
.

Definition 41

(Constructor semantics for
\mathcal{G}(\cdot)
,
B(\cdot)
, and
\theta[\cdot]
)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\tau\in\mathsf{ITD}_{k}
.

(a)

If
\tau=\mathtt{Leaf}
, then
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is the empty graph,
B(\tau)=\emptyset
, and
\theta[\tau]
is the empty function.

(b)

If
\tau=\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}(\sigma)
, then
u\notin B(\sigma)
,
B(\tau)=B(\sigma)\cup\{u\}
, and
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is obtained from
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
by adding one new isolated vertex
x~\dot{=}~\min(\mathbb{N}_{+}\setminus V(\mathcal{G}(\sigma)))
. Moreover,
\theta[\tau]|_{B(\sigma)}=\theta[\sigma]
and
\theta[\tau](u)=x
.

(c)

If
\tau=\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}(\sigma)
, then
u\in B(\sigma)
,
B(\tau)=B(\sigma)\setminus\{u\}
,
\mathcal{G}(\tau)=\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
, and
\theta[\tau]=\theta[\sigma]|_{B(\tau)}
.

(d)

If
\tau=\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}(\sigma)
, then
u\neq v
,
\{u,v\}\subseteq B(\sigma)
,
B(\tau)=B(\sigma)
,
\theta[\tau]=\theta[\sigma]
, and
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is obtained from
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
by adding one new edge
e~\dot{=}~\min(\mathbb{N}_{+}\setminus E(\mathcal{G}(\sigma)))
with endpoints
\{\theta[\sigma](u),\theta[\sigma](v)\}
.

(e)

If
\tau=\mathtt{Join}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
, then
B(\sigma_{1})=B(\sigma_{2})=B(\tau)~\dot{=}~\mathfrak{b}
. Let
G_{i}~\dot{=}~\mathcal{G}(\sigma_{i})
and
\theta_{i}~\dot{=}~\theta[\sigma_{i}]
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, and let
H~\dot{=}~G_{1}\uplus G_{2}
be their disjoint union (Section
2
). Define
\mu:V(H)\to\mathbb{N}
by

\mu(x)~\dot{=}~\begin{cases}2\theta_{1}(u)&\text{if }x=2\theta_{2}(u)+1\text{ for some }u\in\mathfrak{b},\\
x&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

By Lemma
42
below,
\theta_{2}
is injective on
\mathfrak{b}
, hence the label
u
is unique whenever the first case applies and
\mu
is well-defined.
We define
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
as the graph with vertex set
\mu(V(H))
, edge set
E(H)
, and incidence relation
\{(e,\mu(v)):(e,v)\in\rho_{H}\}
, and set
\theta[\tau](u)~\dot{=}~2\theta_{1}(u)
for each
u\in\mathfrak{b}
. In particular, each child graph embeds as a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
(Lemma
44
), and for each
u\in\mathfrak{b}
the identified vertex is
\theta[\tau](u)
.
In other words,
\mathtt{Join}
glues the two child graphs along the shared interface labels
\mathfrak{b}
, while keeping all non-interface vertices disjoint and preserving all edges from both children.

Lemma 42

(Interface map injectivity)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\tau\in\mathsf{ITD}_{k}
.
Then the interface map
\theta[\tau]:B(\tau)\to V(\mathcal{G}(\tau))
is injective.

Proof.

We proceed by structural induction on
\tau
.
The claim is trivial for
\mathtt{Leaf}
, since the domain is empty.
For
\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}(\sigma)
, the map
\theta[\tau]
extends
\theta[\sigma]
and maps the fresh label
u
to the fresh vertex
x\notin V(\mathcal{G}(\sigma))
; since
\theta[\sigma](B(\sigma))\subseteq V(\mathcal{G}(\sigma))
, we have
x\notin\mathrm{im}(\theta[\sigma])
, hence injectivity is preserved.
For
\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}(\sigma)
, the map is a restriction of
\theta[\sigma]
, so injectivity is preserved.
For
\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}(\sigma)
, the interface map is unchanged.
Finally, for
\mathtt{Join}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
we have
\theta[\tau](u)=2\theta_{1}(u)
for
u\in B(\tau)
, where
\theta_{1}=\theta[\sigma_{1}]
is injective by the induction hypothesis; multiplying by
2
preserves injectivity, so
\theta[\tau]
is injective.
∎

Lemma 43

(Join preserves the graph axioms)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\tau=\mathtt{Join}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\in\mathsf{ITD}_{k}
.
Let
G_{i}~\dot{=}~\mathcal{G}(\sigma_{i})
and
\theta_{i}~\dot{=}~\theta[\sigma_{i}]
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, and let
H=G_{1}\uplus G_{2}
and
\mu
be as in Definition
41
(e).
Then for every edge
e\in E(H)
, the set

\{\,\mu(v):(e,v)\in\rho_{H}\,\}

has cardinality
2
. In particular,
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is a graph in the sense of Section
2
(no loops are created by the identification).

Proof.

Let
e\in E(H)
. Since
H
is the disjoint union of two renamed copies, the edge
e
belongs to exactly one child copy.
If
e\in E(G_{1}^{(0)})
, then both endpoints of
e
are even vertices and
\mu
fixes all even vertices, so the two endpoints remain distinct.
Otherwise
e\in E(G_{2}^{(1)})
, hence both endpoints are odd vertices.
If neither endpoint is of the form
2\theta_{2}(u)+1
with
u\in B(\tau)
, then
\mu
fixes both endpoints and they remain distinct.
If exactly one endpoint is of that form, then one endpoint maps to an even vertex and the other remains odd, so they are distinct.
If both endpoints are of that form, say
2\theta_{2}(u)+1
and
2\theta_{2}(v)+1
, then
u\neq v
(otherwise the endpoints would be equal), and
\mu
maps them to
2\theta_{1}(u)
and
2\theta_{1}(v)
.
By Lemma
42
,
\theta_{1}
is injective on the bag, hence
\theta_{1}(u)\neq\theta_{1}(v)
and therefore
2\theta_{1}(u)\neq 2\theta_{1}(v)
.
Thus in all cases the two endpoints of

remain distinct under
\mu

∎

We choose the smallest unused vertex/edge identifiers in (b) and (d) to make the construction canonical (i.e., deterministic rather than only defined up to isomorphism).

The join constructor glues two independently constructed graphs along a shared interface bag.
Formally, we (i) take the disjoint union
H=G_{1}\uplus G_{2}
, and then (ii) identify, for each interface label
u
, the two copies of the interface vertex
\theta_{1}(u)
and
\theta_{2}(u)
via the map
\mu
.
Only interface vertices are identified, and edges are never merged; consequently, if both children contain an edge between the same pair of interface vertices then the join creates a multi-edge, while vertices outside the interface remain disjoint across children (Lemma

).

Lemma 44

(Child embeddings in joins)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\tau=\mathtt{Join}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\in\mathsf{ITD}_{k}
.
Write
G~\dot{=}~\mathcal{G}(\tau)
and
G_{i}~\dot{=}~\mathcal{G}(\sigma_{i})
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, and let
\mu
be as in Definition
41
(e).
Then each child graph embeds as a subgraph of
G
:

1.

The maps
\phi_{1}:V(G_{1})\to V(G)
and
\nu_{1}:E(G_{1})\to E(G)
defined by
\phi_{1}(v)~\dot{=}~2v
and
\nu_{1}(e)~\dot{=}~2e
form an embedding of
G_{1}
into
G
.

2.

The maps
\phi_{2}:V(G_{2})\to V(G)
and
\nu_{2}:E(G_{2})\to E(G)
defined by
\phi_{2}(v)~\dot{=}~\mu(2v+1)
and
\nu_{2}(e)~\dot{=}~2e+1
form an embedding of
G_{2}
into
G
.

Moreover, for each
u\in B(\tau)
we have
\phi_{1}(\theta[\sigma_{1}](u))=\phi_{2}(\theta[\sigma_{2}](u))=\theta[\tau](u)
.

Proof.

Let
H~\dot{=}~G_{1}\uplus G_{2}
be the disjoint union. By the disjoint union construction (Section
2
), the left copy
G_{1}^{(0)}
uses even vertex/edge identifiers and the right copy
G_{2}^{(1)}
uses odd ones. By Definition
41
(e), the graph
G
has edge set
E(H)
and incidence relation

\rho_{G}=\{(e,\mu(v)):(e,v)\in\rho_{H}\}.

(1)
The maps
\phi_{1}
and
\nu_{1}
are injective. Moreover, since
\mu
fixes all even vertices, for each
(e,v)\in\rho_{G_{1}}
we have
(2e,2v)\in\rho_{H}
and hence
(2e,\mu(2v))=(\nu_{1}(e),\phi_{1}(v))\in\rho_{G}
. Conversely, if
(\nu_{1}(e),\phi_{1}(v))=(2e,2v)\in\rho_{G}
, then
(2e,2v)\in\rho_{H}
, which implies

(2)
The map
\nu_{2}
is injective. For

fixes odd vertices that are not interface vertices, and maps each odd interface vertex

to the even vertex

-images, so

is injective. Now, for each

(2e+1,2v+1)\in\rho_{H}

(2e+1,\mu(2v+1))=(\nu_{2}(e),\phi_{2}(v))\in\rho_{G}

(\nu_{2}(e),\phi_{2}(v))=(2e+1,\mu(2v+1))\in\rho_{G}

For the final claim, let

. Then
\phi_{1}(\theta[\sigma_{1}](u))=2\theta[\sigma_{1}](u)=\theta[\tau](u)
by Definition

(e). Also
\phi_{2}(\theta[\sigma_{2}](u))=\mu(2\theta[\sigma_{2}](u)+1)=2\theta[\sigma_{1}](u)=\theta[\tau](u)

.
∎

Lemma 45

(No cross-edges in joins)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\tau=\mathtt{Join}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\in\mathsf{ITD}_{k}
.
Write
G~\dot{=}~\mathcal{G}(\tau)
and
G_{i}~\dot{=}~\mathcal{G}(\sigma_{i})
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, and let
\mathfrak{b}~\dot{=}~B(\tau)
.
Let
\phi_{i}
and
\nu_{i}
be the vertex and edge maps of the embedding of
G_{i}
into
G
from Lemma
44
.
Then every edge identifier of
G
originates in exactly one child: for each
e\in E(G)
, there is a unique
i\in\{1,2\}
and a unique
e_{i}\in E(G_{i})
such that
e=\nu_{i}(e_{i})
.
For this child

, the endpoints
\mathrm{endpts}(e)
in
G
are contained in
\phi_{i}(V(G_{i}))
.
In particular, there is no edge of
G
whose endpoints lie respectively in

\phi_{1}(V(G_{1})\setminus\theta[\sigma_{1}](\mathfrak{b}))\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{2}(V(G_{2})\setminus\theta[\sigma_{2}](\mathfrak{b})).

Proof.

In the join construction of Definition
41
(e), the edge set of
G
is the edge set of the disjoint union
H=G_{1}\uplus G_{2}
, and vertices are identified only by the map
\mu
on interface vertices.
The disjoint union uses disjoint edge identifiers, and the join does not identify edge identifiers. Hence every edge

is uniquely of the form
\nu_{i}(e_{i})
for one child edge

.
Its incident vertices in

are precisely the

lie in the image of the corresponding child embedding. Since

only identifies interface vertices, no edge can connect a non-interface vertex from the first child image to a non-interface vertex from the second child image.
∎

Lemma 46

(Subterms induce subgraphs)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\tau\in\mathsf{ITD}_{k}
, and let
\sigma
be a subterm of
\tau
. Then
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
is isomorphic to a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on the distance from the root of
\sigma
to the root of
\tau
in the term tree. If
\sigma=\tau
, the claim is immediate. Otherwise, let
\pi
be the parent of
\sigma
in
\tau
. By Definition
41
,
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
embeds as a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(\pi)
for each constructor case (and in the join case, the required embeddings are given explicitly by Lemma

embeds as a subgraph of

∎

Appendix C
DP-Cores for Graph Properties

In this appendix, we define DP-cores for the graph properties used throughout the paper that are not already specified in the main text.
For each such graph property
\mathbb{P}
, we specify a DP-core and prove its correctness on instructive decompositions.
The concrete cores used for Reed’s conjecture are denoted

\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)
,

\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)
, and

HasMultipleEdges
, matching the corresponding TreeWidzard property-file names.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\tau\in\mathsf{ITD}_{k}
be a
k
-instructive decomposition (Section
3
).
Each DP-core is specified via a predicate of the form
\mathrm{Pred}_{\mathbb{P}}
relating decompositions and local witnesses, and we prove predicate soundness.
As a consequence, for every
\tau\in\mathsf{ITD}_{k}
, acceptance by the corresponding width-
k
core is equivalent to
\mathcal{G}(\tau)\in\mathbb{P}
(DP-core soundness).

C.1
Maximum Degree

Let
G
be a graph. The maximum degree
\Delta(G)
of
G
is the maximum degree among its vertices, i.e.,

\Delta(G)=\max_{x\in V(G)}d_{x},

where
d_{x}
is the degree of the vertex
x
in
G
. For the empty graph, we use the convention that its maximum degree is
0
. We denote the maximum degree of
G
by
\Delta
if
G
is clear from the context. Let
\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)
be the graph property containing all graphs that have a maximum degree of at least
d
. In the following, we define a DP-core
\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)
that decides
\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)
. More specifically, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k]
decides the graph property
\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)\cap\textsc{GraphsTw}[k]
.

Local Witness.
We begin by defining the structure of local witnesses for
\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k]
. The main idea is to track the degrees of active labels, which represent the active vertices of a graph, until we encounter a label with a degree of at least
d
. Inactive vertices do not need to be tracked since no edges will be connected to them.

To implement this idea, we define a local witness to be either a map or a Boolean value. We define the map to record the degrees of active labels for each node of a
k
-instructive tree decomposition. If a local witness is the Boolean value
b
, it indicates that a label with a degree of at least
d
has been found.

Definition 47
.

A
\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k]
local witness is either a map
\mathsf{w}:\mathfrak{b}\to\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}
, where
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq[k+1]
, or the Boolean value
\mathsf{w}=b
.

Intuitively,
\mathfrak{b}
represents the set of labels that are active in some node of a
k
-instructive tree decomposition, and the Boolean value
b
is a Boolean flag specifying that a vertex of maximum degree at least
d
was found.

All Witnesses
. We let
\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k].\mathcal{W}
be the set of all
\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k]
-local witnesses:

\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k].\mathcal{W}=\{\mathsf{w}:\mathsf{w}\text{ is a }\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k]\text{ local witness}\}.

Initial Witnesses
. When the instruction
\mathtt{Leaf}
is called, it creates an empty graph. The only possible local witness is the empty map,
\mathsf{w}_{0}:\emptyset\rightarrow\emptyset
, since the empty graph has no vertices.

\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k].\mathtt{Leaf}=\{\mathsf{w}_{0}\}.

Introduce Vertex
. Let
\mathsf{w}
be a
\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k]
-local witness, and let
u
represent a label in
[k+1]
. When a new vertex is introduced and labeled with
u
, we evaluate the situation based on the state of the local witness.

If a vertex with a degree of at least
d
has already been identified, i.e.,
\mathsf{w}=b
, there is no need to modify the local witness, as the desired condition has already been met. However, if no such vertex has been found, i.e.,
\mathsf{w}
is a map, the label
u
is added to the map
\mathsf{w}
with an initial value of 0. This reflects the fact that the vertex represented by
u
currently has a degree of 0, as no edges are connected to it at this stage.

\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k].\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}(\mathsf{w})=\begin{cases}\{\mathsf{w}\}&\quad\text{if }\mathsf{w}=b,\\[6.0pt]
\emptyset&\quad\begin{array}[t]{@{}l@{}}\text{if }\mathsf{w}\neq b\\
\text{and }u\in dom(\mathsf{w}),\end{array}\\[6.0pt]
\{\mathsf{w}\cup\{u\to 0\}\}&\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Forget Vertex
. Let
\mathsf{w}
be a local witness, and let
u
represent an active label. When the vertex labeled
u
is forgotten, the label
u
is freed, making it available for reuse when introducing a future vertex. If
\mathsf{w}=b
, it implies that a vertex meeting the required degree condition has already been identified. In this case, the local witness
\mathsf{w}
remains unchanged, as no further modifications are needed. On the other hand, if
\mathsf{w}
is a map, this process involves removing the label
u
from the map
\mathsf{w}
, since the degree of the vertex with label
u
will not increase anymore.

\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k].\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}(\mathsf{w})=\begin{cases}\{\mathsf{w}\}&\quad\text{if }\mathsf{w}=b,\\[6.0pt]
\{\mathsf{w}|_{dom(\mathsf{w})\setminus\{u\}}\}&\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Introduce Edge
. When introducing an edge between two vertices, the first step is to determine whether a vertex with a degree of at least
d
has already been found. Let
\mathsf{w}
be a local witness, and let
u
and
v
be distinct active labels representing two vertices. If
\mathsf{w}=b
, it indicates that a qualifying vertex has already been identified, and no further modifications to the local witness
\mathsf{w}
are needed.

Otherwise (i.e., if
\mathsf{w}
is a map), we proceed by incrementing the degrees of the vertices represented by
u
and
v
in the map
\mathsf{w}
. If either endpoint is missing from the map domain, the local witness is incompatible with introducing an edge on these active labels and the transition returns no witness. We then check whether either
\mathsf{w}(u)
or
\mathsf{w}(v)
reaches
d
after the increment. If this condition is met, it signifies that a vertex with the required degree has been found. In this case, the local witness will change to the Boolean value
b
, as further tracking of degrees is unnecessary.

If neither vertex reaches
d
, the degrees of
u
and
v
are updated in
\mathsf{w}
, and the local witness is modified accordingly.

Formally, the operation
\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k].\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}(\mathsf{w})
produces one of the following outcomes:

1.

If
\mathsf{w}=b
, return
\{\mathsf{w}\}
.

2.

If
\mathsf{w}\neq b
and
\{u,v\}\nsubseteq dom(\mathsf{w})
, return
\emptyset
.

3.

If either
\mathsf{w}(u)=d-1
or
\mathsf{w}(v)=d-1
, return
\{b\}
.

4.

Otherwise, return

\{(\mathsf{w}|_{dom(\mathsf{w})\setminus\{u,v\}}\cup\{u\to\mathsf{w}(u)+1,v\to\mathsf{w}(v)+1\})\}.

Join
.
Let
\mathsf{w}
and
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
be local witnesses associated with the left and right children of a given node, respectively. The goal of the
\mathtt{Join}
operation is to merge the graphs corresponding to these children along the vertices labeled with active labels.

The process begins by checking whether either child has already identified a vertex with a degree of at least
d
. Specifically, if
\mathsf{w}=b
for the left child or
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}=b
for the right child, the join operation immediately returns the default local witness
b
, as no further processing is needed.

If neither child has identified such a vertex (i.e., both
\mathsf{w}
and
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
are maps), we first check that the two maps have the same domain. If the domains differ, the two implementation witnesses are incompatible and the join returns the empty set. Otherwise, we proceed by checking the sum of the values of
\mathsf{w}
and
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
for each active label. If the sum for any active label is at least
d
, this indicates that a vertex with the required degree has been found. In this case, we again return the local witness
b
.

If no such vertex is found, we construct a new map
\mathsf{w}^{\prime\prime}
by summing the values of
\mathsf{w}
and
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
for each active label. This new map
\mathsf{w}^{\prime\prime}
represents the combined state of the left and right children.

Formally, the operation
\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k].\mathtt{Join}(\mathsf{w},\mathsf{w}^{\prime})
produces one of the following outcomes:

1.

If
\mathsf{w}=b
or
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}=b
, return
\{b\}
.

2.

If
dom(\mathsf{w})\neq dom(\mathsf{w}^{\prime})
, return
\emptyset
.

3.

If there exists
u\in dom(\mathsf{w})
such that
\mathsf{w}(u)+\mathsf{w}^{\prime}(u)\geq d
, return
\{b\}
.

4.

Otherwise, return
\{\mathsf{w}^{\prime\prime}\}
, where for each
u\in dom(\mathsf{w})
,
\mathsf{w}^{\prime\prime}(u)=\mathsf{w}(u)+\mathsf{w}^{\prime}(u)
.

Final Witness Function.

The final witness function is used to determine whether the condition of finding a vertex with a degree of at least
d
has been satisfied. This function checks if
\mathsf{w}=b
.
If
\mathsf{w}=b
, it confirms that a vertex with the required degree has been found, and the function outputs
1
. Otherwise, the function outputs
0
, indicating that the condition has not yet been met.

Formally:

\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k].\mathtt{Final}(\mathsf{w})=1\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\mathsf{w}=b.

Cleaning Function
. In our example, the cleaning function acts as the identity:

\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k].\mathtt{Clean}(S)=S\text{ for every }S\subseteq\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k].\mathcal{W}.

Invariant Function
. In our example, the invariant function is trivial. It sends a set of local witnesses
S
to
1
if and only if it contains a final local witness.

\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k].\mathtt{Inv}(S)=\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{ if }S\text{ has a final local witness,}\\[6.0pt]
0&\quad\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The next step is to prove the correctness of the DP-core we just defined. For this, we define the following predicate relating
k
-instructive tree decompositions with local witnesses.

Definition 48
.

We let
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d,k)
be the predicate that is true on a pair
(\tau,\mathsf{w})\in\mathsf{ITD}_{k}\times\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k].\mathcal{W}
if and only if one of the following happens:

1.

\mathsf{w}=b
and
\Delta(\mathcal{G}(\tau))\geq d
.

2.

\mathsf{w}\neq b
,
dom(\mathsf{w})=B(\tau)
,
\Delta(\mathcal{G}(\tau))<d
, and for each
u\in B(\tau)
,
\mathsf{w}(u)=d_{\mathcal{G}(\tau)}(\theta[\tau](u))
.

The first condition requires that if the witness
\mathsf{w}=b
, the graph associated with
\tau
should have a maximum degree of at least
d
. Otherwise, the maximum degree of the graph
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
should be less than
d
, the map domain should be exactly the active bag, and for each active label in
B(\tau)
, say
u
, the vertex associated with that label in the graph
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
should have the degree
\mathsf{w}(u)
.

Proposition 49
.

For each
\tau\in\mathsf{ITD}_{k}
, a local witness
\mathsf{w}
belongs to

\Gamma[{\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d),k}](\tau)

if and only if
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d,k)(\tau,\mathsf{w})=1
.

Proof.

We proceed by structural induction on
\tau
. Throughout, we use that the cleaning function of
\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k]
is the identity.

Base case:

\tau=\mathtt{Leaf}
. By Definition
3
,
\Gamma[{\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d),k}](\mathtt{Leaf})=\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d)[k].\mathtt{Leaf}=\{\mathsf{w}_{0}\}
, where
\mathsf{w}_{0}
is the empty map. Hence
\mathsf{w}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d),k}](\mathtt{Leaf})
if and only if
\mathsf{w}=\mathsf{w}_{0}
. Since
\mathcal{G}(\mathtt{Leaf})
is the empty graph and
B(\mathtt{Leaf})=\emptyset
, we have
\Delta(\mathcal{G}(\mathtt{Leaf}))=0<d
, so
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d,k)(\mathtt{Leaf},\mathsf{w}_{0})=1
and
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d,k)(\mathtt{Leaf},b)=0
. Conversely, if
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d,k)(\mathtt{Leaf},\mathsf{w})=1
and
\mathsf{w}\neq b
, then Condition 2 forces
dom(\mathsf{w})=\emptyset
, so
\mathsf{w}=\mathsf{w}_{0}
.

Induction step:
assume the claim holds for the immediate subterm(s) of
\tau
.

Case 1:

\tau=\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}(\sigma)
.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose
\mathsf{w}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d),k}]\allowbreak(\tau)
. Then
\mathsf{w}\in\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}(\Gamma[{\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d),k}]\allowbreak(\sigma))
. Hence there exists
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d),k}]\allowbreak(\sigma)
such that
\mathsf{w}\in\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}(\mathsf{w}^{\prime})
. If
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}=b
, then
\mathsf{w}=b
and by the induction hypothesis
\Delta(\mathcal{G}(\sigma))\geq d
. By Definition
41
, introducing a vertex adds an isolated vertex, so
\Delta(\mathcal{G}(\tau))\geq d
and
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d,k)(\tau,\mathsf{w})=1
. Otherwise
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
is a map and
\mathsf{w}=\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\cup\{u\to 0\}
. By the induction hypothesis,
\Delta(\mathcal{G}(\sigma))<d
and
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
records the degrees of all active vertices. By Definition
41
,
u
maps to a new isolated vertex, so
\mathsf{w}
records the degrees of all active vertices in
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
and
\Delta(\mathcal{G}(\tau))<d
, hence
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d,k)(\tau,\mathsf{w})=1
.

(\Leftarrow)
Suppose
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{MaximumDegree}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(d,k)(\tau,\mathsf{w})=1
. If
\mathsf{w}=b
, then
\Delta(\mathcal{G}(\tau))\geq d
, and since introducing an isolated vertex does not decrease maximum degree, we have
\Delta(\mathcal{G}(\sigma))\geq d
. By the induction hypothesis,

C.2
Clique Number

Let
G
be a graph. We say
G
has a clique of size
\omega
if
G
has a clique of size
\omega
as a subgraph. Let
\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)
be the graph property consisting of all graphs that have a clique of size
\omega
. Next, we give the specification of a DP-core
\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)[k]
for the property
\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)\cap\neg\mbox{Prop}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}\cap\textsc{GraphsTw}[k]
.
Throughout this subsection we assume
\omega\geq 2
.

Local Witness.
We begin by defining the structure of a local witness for
\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)[k]
. During the processing of a
k
-instructive tree decomposition, we do not know at the root which set of vertices forms a clique of size
\omega
. Therefore, we need to guess possible sets of vertices that have been introduced during the process and could form a clique. As we process edges, we update these guesses. We define a local witness to be a “potential clique,” or rather, a “partial clique” since it is not yet a complete clique. During the process of the
k
-instructive tree decomposition, we update this local witness.

To implement this idea, we consider a local witness to be either the Boolean flag
\mathfrak{b}
or a pair consisting of a map and an integer,
(\gamma,s)
. If the local witness
\mathsf{w}=\mathfrak{b}
, it indicates that we have found a clique of size
\omega
. Otherwise, if
\mathsf{w}=(\gamma,s)
, the map
\gamma
is used to track the current degrees of each active vertex included in the partial clique, reflecting its contribution to the potential clique. The integer
s
represents the count of vertices currently included in the partial clique, including selected vertices that may already have been forgotten, ensuring it does not exceed the target size
\omega
.

Definition 53
.

A
\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)[k]
local witness
\mathsf{w}
is one of the following:

•

The Boolean flag
\mathfrak{b}
, or

•

A pair
(\gamma,s)
where
\gamma:B^{\prime}\to\{0,1,\dots,\omega-1\}
is a map for some
B^{\prime}\subseteq[k+1]
, and
s
is an integer in
\{0,1,\dots,\omega\}
with
dom(\gamma)=B^{\prime}
and
|dom(\gamma)|\leq s
.

In Definition
53
,
dom(\gamma)
is the set of active labels that are currently selected into the potential clique, and
\gamma
records their current degrees inside this selected set. The value
s
is the size of the selected set (including any selected vertices that may already have been forgotten), so
|dom(\gamma)|\leq s
. The flag
\mathfrak{b}
is the normalized form of the implementation witness whose found flag is true. It is important to note that this DP-core is correct only for simple graphs, i.e., graphs with at most one edge between each pair of vertices. The following definitions would fail for multi-edge graphs.

All Witnesses
. Let
\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)[k].\mathcal{W}
be the set of all
\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)[k]
-local witnesses:

\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)[k].\mathcal{W}=\{\mathsf{w}:\mathsf{w}\text{ is a }\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)[k]\\
\text{ local witness}\}.

Initial Witnesses
. The initial state of the graph is empty, meaning there are no vertices or edges. Consequently, the only possible local witness at this stage is the empty map
\gamma_{0}
with the clique size set to
0
, indicating that no clique has been found yet. This ensures the DP-core starts with a minimal and consistent initial configuration.

\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)[k].\mathtt{Leaf}=\{(\gamma_{0},0)\}.

Introduce Vertex
.
Let
\mathsf{w}
be a
\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)[k]
-local witness, and let
u
represent a label in
[k+1]
. When a new vertex is introduced and labeled with
u
, we evaluate the situation based on the state of the local witness.

If a clique of size
\omega
has already been found (i.e.,
\mathsf{w}=\mathfrak{b}
), there is no need to modify the local witness. The function simply returns the current local witness unchanged, as the condition of finding the required clique has already been satisfied.

Otherwise, let
\mathsf{w}=(\gamma,s)
. If
u
is already in
dom(\gamma)
, the witness is incompatible with introducing a fresh vertex at label
u
, and the transition returns no witness. If the number of vertices currently included in the potential clique equals
\omega
(i.e.,
s=\omega
), no further vertices can be added to the clique. In this case, the local witness is also left unchanged, reflecting that no additional possibilities need to be explored.

If neither of the above conditions applies, the function always keeps the option of not including the new vertex
u
in the potential clique. The implementation adds
u
as a selected clique vertex only when no selected clique vertex has already been forgotten, that is, when
s=|dom(\gamma)|
. In that case,
u
’s degree is initialized to 0 in the map
\gamma
, and the size of the potential clique is incremented by 1.

This function is crucial for systematically exploring all configurations of vertices that might form a clique, while respecting the constraint of not exceeding the target clique size
\omega
.

The transition
\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)[k].\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}(\mathsf{w})
is defined by the following cases.

1.

If
\mathsf{w}=\mathfrak{b}
, then return
\{\mathsf{w}\}
.

2.

If
\mathsf{w}=(\gamma,s)
and
u\in dom(\gamma)
, then return
\emptyset
.

3.

If
\mathsf{w}=(\gamma,s)
,
u\notin dom(\gamma)
, and
s=\omega
, then return
\{\mathsf{w}\}
.

4.

If
\mathsf{w}=(\gamma,s)
,
u\notin dom(\gamma)
,
s<\omega
, and
s=|dom(\gamma)|
, then return

\{(\gamma,s),(\gamma\cup\{u\to 0\},s+1)\}.

5.

If
\mathsf{w}=(\gamma,s)
,
u\notin dom(\gamma)
,
s<\omega
, and
s>|dom(\gamma)|
, then return
\{(\gamma,s)\}
.

Forget Vertex
. Let
\mathsf{w}
be a
\mbox{SimpleCliqueNumber}\_\allowbreak\mbox{AtLeast}(\omega)[k]
-local witness, and let

u
represent a label in
[k+1]
. When the vertex labeled
u
is forgotten, the label
u
is freed, making it available for reuse when introducing a future vertex. The behavior of this function depends on the current state of
\mathsf{w}
.

If
\mathsf{w}=\mathfrak{b}
, this means a clique of size
\omega
has already been found, and as a consequence, the same local witness would be returned.

Let
\mathsf{w}=(\gamma,s)
. If
u\notin dom(\gamma)
, then

Let

Local Witness.

We begin by describing the structure of local witnesses for

\mathrm{b}

k
-instructive tree decomposition. The Boolean flag indicates that multiple edges have been found, so no further checks are necessary.

Definition 59
.

is one of the following:

•

The Boolean flag
\mathrm{b}

•

All Witnesses.

We define
\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k].\mathcal{W}
as the set of all

\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k].\mathcal{W}=\{\mathsf{w}:\mathsf{w}\text{ is a }\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k]\text{ local witness}\}.

Initial Witnesses.

When the instruction
\mathtt{Leaf}
is invoked, it initializes an empty graph. For the empty graph, the only valid local witness is the empty set since no edge has been introduced.

\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k].\mathtt{Leaf}=\{\emptyset\}.

Let
\mathsf{w}

Let
\mathsf{w}
be a local witness, and let

\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k].\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}(\mathsf{w})=\begin{cases}\{\mathsf{w}\}&\quad\text{if }\mathsf{w}=\mathrm{b},\\
\Bigl\{\bigl\{\{v,v^{\prime}\}:\begin{subarray}{c}v,v^{\prime}\neq u\\
\{v,v^{\prime}\}\in\mathsf{w}\end{subarray}\bigr\}\Bigr\}&\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Let
\mathsf{w}
be a local witness. When introducing an edge between two distinct vertices labeled

1.

.

3.

Otherwise, if
\{u,v\}\notin\mathsf{w}
, the edge pair
\{u,v\}
is added to
\mathsf{w}
.

\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k].\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}(\mathsf{w})=\begin{cases}\{\mathrm{b}\}&\quad\text{if }\mathsf{w}=\mathrm{b},\\
\{\mathsf{w}\cup\{\{u,v\}\}\}&\quad\text{if }\mathsf{w}\neq\mathrm{b}\text{ and }\{u,v\}\notin\mathsf{w},\\
\{\mathrm{b}\}&\quad\text{if }\mathsf{w}\neq\mathrm{b}\text{ and }\{u,v\}\in\mathsf{w}.\end{cases}

Join.

Let
\mathsf{w}
and
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
be local witnesses associated with the left and right children of a given node. The purpose of the
\mathtt{Join}
operation is to combine the graphs corresponding to these children, focusing on the vertices labeled with active labels.

If multiple edges have already been found in either child (
\mathsf{w}=\mathrm{b}
or
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}=\mathrm{b}
), the join operation immediately returns
\{\mathrm{b}\}
. Otherwise, if the intersection
\mathsf{w}\cap\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
is non-empty, this indicates the presence of multiple edges spanning both children, and the operation returns
\{\mathrm{b}\}
. If neither of these conditions is met, meaning no multiple edges exist, the join operation returns a combined local witness
\mathsf{w}\cup\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
.

\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k].\mathtt{Join}(\mathsf{w},\mathsf{w}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\{\mathrm{b}\}&\quad\text{if }\mathsf{w}=\mathrm{b}\text{ or }\mathsf{w}^{\prime}=\mathrm{b},\\
\{\mathrm{b}\}&\quad\text{if }\mathsf{w},\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\neq\mathrm{b}\text{ and }\mathsf{w}\cap\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset,\\
\{\mathsf{w}\cup\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\}&\quad\text{if }\mathsf{w},\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\neq\mathrm{b}\text{ and }\mathsf{w}\cap\mathsf{w}^{\prime}=\emptyset.\end{cases}

Final Witness Function.

The final witness function is used to determine whether the condition of finding multiple edges has been satisfied.

\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k].\mathtt{Final}(\mathsf{w})=1\iff\mathsf{w}=\mathrm{b}.

Cleaning Function.

In our example, the cleaning function acts as the identity.

\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k].\mathtt{Clean}(S)=S\text{ for every }S\subseteq\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k].\mathcal{W}.

Invariant Function.

In our example, the invariant function is the trivial one, which sends a set of local witnesses
S
to
1
if and only if it contains a final witness.

\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k].\mathtt{Inv}(S)=\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{ if }S\text{ contains a final witness},\\
0&\quad\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

The next step is to prove the correctness of the DP-core we just defined. For this, we define the following predicate relating
k
-instructive tree decompositions with local witnesses.

Definition 60
.

We let
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)
be the predicate that is true on a pair
(\tau,\mathsf{w})\in\mathsf{ITD}_{k}\times\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k].\mathcal{W}
if and only if one of the following is satisfied:

1.

If
\mathsf{w}=\mathrm{b}
,
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is a multigraph.

2.

If
\mathsf{w}\neq\mathrm{b}
,
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is a simple graph and

\mathsf{w}=\Bigl\{\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{P}(B(\tau),2):\exists e\in E(\mathcal{G}(\tau))\text{ with }\mathrm{endpts}(e)=\{\theta[\tau](u),\theta[\tau](v)\}\Bigr\}.

The first condition requires that if the witness is the Boolean flag
\mathrm{b}
, the graph associated with
\tau
should have multiple edges. The second condition ensures that if the witness is a set of label pairs, the graph is simple and
\mathsf{w}
encodes
exactly
those edges whose endpoints are currently active.

Proposition 61
.

For each
\tau\in\mathsf{ITD}_{k}
,
a local witness
\mathsf{w}
belongs to
\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\tau)

if and only if
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\tau,\mathsf{w})=1
.

Proof.

We proceed by structural induction on
\tau
. Throughout, we use that the cleaning function of
\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k]
is the identity.

Base case:

\tau=\mathtt{Leaf}
. Then
\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\mathtt{Leaf})=\{\emptyset\}
. Since
\mathcal{G}(\mathtt{Leaf})
is the empty (simple) graph,
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\mathtt{Leaf},\emptyset)=1
and
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\mathtt{Leaf},\mathrm{b})=0
.

Induction step:
assume the claim holds for the immediate subterm(s) of
\tau
.

Case 1:

\tau=\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}(\sigma)
.

By Definition
3
, a witness
\mathsf{w}
belongs to
\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\tau)
if and only if there exists
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\sigma)
with
\mathsf{w}\in\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}(\mathsf{w}^{\prime})
. Since
\mathtt{IntroVertex}\{u\}(\mathsf{w}^{\prime})=\{\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\}
for this core, we have
\mathsf{w}=\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
. Moreover, introducing a vertex does not change the edge set (Definition
41
), and the introduced vertex is isolated at this point. Hence
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
has multiple edges if and only if
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
has multiple edges, and the set of edges with both endpoints active is unchanged. The equivalence then follows directly from the induction hypothesis.

Case 2:

\tau=\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}(\sigma)
.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose
\mathsf{w}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\tau)
. Then there exists
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\sigma)
with
\mathsf{w}\in\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}(\mathsf{w}^{\prime})
. If
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}=\mathrm{b}
, then
\mathsf{w}=\mathrm{b}
, and by the induction hypothesis
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
is a multigraph. Since forgetting does not change the graph (Definition
41
), also
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is a multigraph, so
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\tau,\mathsf{w})=1
. Otherwise
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
is a set of label pairs, and
\mathsf{w}
is obtained from
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
by removing all pairs that contain
u
. By the induction hypothesis,
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
is simple and
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
is exactly the set of label pairs corresponding to edges between active vertices. Since forgetting only removes
u
from the interface and does not change the graph, the remaining pairs in
\mathsf{w}
are exactly those edges whose endpoints remain active in
\tau
. Hence
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\tau,\mathsf{w})=1
.

(\Leftarrow)
Suppose
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\tau,\mathsf{w})=1
.
If
\mathsf{w}=\mathrm{b}
, then
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is a multigraph, hence so is
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
.
By the induction hypothesis,

\mathrm{b}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\sigma).

Since
\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}(\mathrm{b})=\{\mathrm{b}\}
, we get

\mathsf{w}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\tau).

Otherwise
\mathsf{w}
is a set of pairs and
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is simple.
Let
G~\dot{=}~\mathcal{G}(\sigma)=\mathcal{G}(\tau)
and
\theta~\dot{=}~\theta[\sigma]
.
Define
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
to be
\mathsf{w}
together with all pairs
\{u,v\}
such that there is an edge between
\theta(u)
and
\theta(v)
in
G
.
Then
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\sigma,\mathsf{w}^{\prime})=1
, so by the induction hypothesis
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}]\allowbreak(\sigma)
.
By definition of the forget transition,
\mathtt{ForgetVertex}\{u\}(\mathsf{w}^{\prime})=\{\mathsf{w}\}
, hence
\mathsf{w}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}]\allowbreak(\tau)
.

Case 3:

\tau=\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}(\sigma)
.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose
\mathsf{w}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\tau)
. Then there exists
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\sigma)
with
\mathsf{w}\in\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}(\mathsf{w}^{\prime})
. If
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}=\mathrm{b}
, then
\mathsf{w}=\mathrm{b}
and the claim follows by induction. Otherwise
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
is a set. If
\{u,v\}\in\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
, then the transition returns
\mathrm{b}
, and by the induction hypothesis
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
already has an edge between the corresponding vertices. Since
\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}
adds another edge between the same endpoints (Definition
41
),
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is a multigraph and
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\tau,\mathrm{b})=1
. If
\{u,v\}\notin\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
, then
\mathsf{w}=\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\cup\{\{u,v\}\}
. In this case,
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
is simple by the induction hypothesis and does not contain an edge between the corresponding active vertices. Therefore
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is still simple, and the set of label pairs corresponding to edges between active vertices is exactly
\mathsf{w}
. Hence
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\tau,\mathsf{w})=1
.

(\Leftarrow)
Suppose
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\tau,\mathsf{w})=1
. If
\mathsf{w}=\mathrm{b}
, then
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is a multigraph. Removing the last introduced edge yields
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
, which is either already a multigraph or has an edge between the same endpoints as the removed edge. In either case, by the induction hypothesis there exists
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\sigma)
such that
\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}(\mathsf{w}^{\prime})
produces
\mathrm{b}
, and hence
\mathrm{b}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\tau)
. Otherwise
\mathsf{w}
is a set and
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is simple. Since
\mathtt{IntroEdge}\{u,v\}
introduces an edge between the active vertices labeled
u
and
v
, we have
\{u,v\}\in\mathsf{w}
. Let
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}~\dot{=}~\mathsf{w}\setminus\{\{u,v\}\}
. Then
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
is obtained from
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
by deleting this edge, hence it is simple and
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
is exactly the set of label pairs corresponding to edges between active vertices in
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
. Therefore
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\sigma,\mathsf{w}^{\prime})=1
, so by the induction hypothesis
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\sigma)
. Since
\{u,v\}\notin\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
, the intro-edge rule returns
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\cup\{\{u,v\}\}=\mathsf{w}
, hence
\mathsf{w}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\tau)
.

Case 4:

\tau=\mathtt{Join}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose
\mathsf{w}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\tau)
. Then there exist
\mathsf{w}_{1}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\sigma_{1})
and
\mathsf{w}_{2}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\sigma_{2})
such that
\mathsf{w}\in\mathtt{Join}(\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})
. If
\mathsf{w}=\mathrm{b}
, then either
\mathsf{w}_{1}=\mathrm{b}
or
\mathsf{w}_{2}=\mathrm{b}
or
\mathsf{w}_{1}\cap\mathsf{w}_{2}\neq\emptyset
. In the first two cases, by the induction hypothesis one child graph already has multiple edges, hence so does the join graph. In the third case, there is a label pair
\{u,v\}
that corresponds to an edge in both children; by Definition
41
(e), joining identifies the corresponding endpoints, producing two distinct edges between the same pair of vertices in
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
. Thus
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is a multigraph and
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\tau,\mathrm{b})=1
. If
\mathsf{w}\neq\mathrm{b}
, then
\mathsf{w}=\mathsf{w}_{1}\cup\mathsf{w}_{2}
and
\mathsf{w}_{1}\cap\mathsf{w}_{2}=\emptyset
. By the induction hypothesis, both child graphs are simple and
\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2}
encode exactly the edges between active vertices in the respective child graphs. By Lemma
45
, every edge between active vertices in
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
comes from exactly one child, and therefore
\mathsf{w}
is exactly the set of label pairs corresponding to edges between active vertices in the join graph. Hence
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\tau,\mathsf{w})=1
.

(\Leftarrow)
Suppose
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\tau,\mathsf{w})=1
. If
\mathsf{w}=\mathrm{b}
, then
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is a multigraph. This can happen either because one child already has multiple edges or because the join identifies two distinct edges between the same labeled endpoints from the two children. In both cases, by the induction hypothesis there exist witnesses
\mathsf{w}_{1}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}]\allowbreak(\sigma_{1})
and
\mathsf{w}_{2}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}]\allowbreak(\sigma_{2})
such that
\mathrm{b}\in\mathtt{Join}(\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})
, hence
\mathrm{b}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}]\allowbreak(\tau)
. Otherwise
\mathsf{w}
is a set and
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is simple. Let
\mathsf{w}_{i}
be the set of label pairs that correspond to edges between active vertices in
\mathcal{G}(\sigma_{i})
. Then
\mbox{Pred}\_\allowbreak\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}(k)(\sigma_{i},\mathsf{w}_{i})=1
, so by induction
\mathsf{w}_{i}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}]\allowbreak(\sigma_{i})
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Since
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is simple, no label pair can correspond to edges in both children, so
\mathsf{w}_{1}\cap\mathsf{w}_{2}=\emptyset
. Moreover, every edge between active vertices in the join graph belongs to exactly one child, so
\mathsf{w}=\mathsf{w}_{1}\cup\mathsf{w}_{2}
. Therefore
\mathtt{Join}(\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})=\{\mathsf{w}\}
, and hence
\mathsf{w}\in\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}]\allowbreak(\tau)
.
∎

Corollary 62
.

Let
\tau
be a
k
-instructive tree decomposition. Then
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
has multiple edges
if and only if
\tau\in\mathsf{Accepted}(\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k])
.

Proof.

By definition,
\tau
is accepted by
\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k]
if and only if
\Gamma[{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges},k}](\tau)
contains a final witness, i.e.,
\mathrm{b}
. By Proposition
61
, this holds if and only if
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
has multiple edges (equivalently, is not simple).
∎

Observation 63
.

A
\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k]
local witness has a bit-length

O(k^{2})
and timing
O(k^{2})
.

Proof.

A
\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k]
witness is either the Boolean flag
\mathrm{b}
or a set
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}([k+1],2)
of label pairs.
Since
|\mathcal{P}([k+1],2)|=\binom{k+1}{2}=O(k^{2})
, we can encode such a set
S
as a bitvector indexed by
\mathcal{P}([k+1],2)
, using
O(k^{2})
bits.
The operations of the core perform set insertions/deletions and unions/intersections over this universe, which can be implemented in
O(k^{2})
time.
∎

Lemma 64

(Witness action for
HasMultipleEdges
)
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Define
\rho_{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}}^{k}
on local witnesses by

\rho_{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}}^{k}(f,\mathrm{b})~\dot{=}~\mathrm{b}

and for a set witness
\mathsf{w}\subseteq\mathcal{P}([k+1],2)
with
\mathrm{Lbl}_{k}(\mathsf{w})\subseteq dom(f)
, let

\rho_{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}}^{k}(f,\mathsf{w})~\dot{=}~\bigl\{\{f(u),f(v)\}:\{u,v\}\in\mathsf{w}\bigr\}.

Then
\rho_{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}}^{k}
is a witness action for
\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}[k]
in the sense of Definition
17
.

Proof.

We verify the conditions of Definition
17
.

Finality invariance.

The only final witness is
\mathrm{b}
, and it is fixed by the action.

Label support transport.

The label support of
\mathrm{b}
is empty. For a set witness
\mathsf{w}\subseteq\mathcal{P}([k+1],2)
, the only labels appearing in
\mathsf{w}
are those occurring in one of its pairs, so

\mathrm{Lbl}_{k}(\mathsf{w})=\bigcup_{\{u,v\}\in\mathsf{w}}\{u,v\}.

By definition of the action, each pair
\{u,v\}
is renamed to
\{f(u),f(v)\}
, and therefore

\mathrm{Lbl}_{k}(\rho_{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}}^{k}(f,\mathsf{w}))=f(\mathrm{Lbl}_{k}(\mathsf{w})).

Inverse relabeling, composition, and extension invariance.

Since
f
is injective, applying
f^{-1}
maps each renamed pair back to the original pair, giving inverse relabeling; composition is immediate from
f(f^{\prime}(u))=(f\circ f^{\prime})(u)
applied elementwise to pairs.
If
f^{\prime}
extends
f
, then
f^{\prime}
agrees with
f
on
\mathrm{Lbl}_{k}(\mathsf{w})
, so the action produces the same set of renamed pairs.

DP-core consistency.

If
\mathsf{w}=\mathrm{b}
, then every transition returns
\{\mathrm{b}\}
and the commutation identities are trivial.
Otherwise, each transition is defined by set membership and by inserting/removing a single pair or taking union/intersection, and these operations commute with renaming pairs:
for instance, for
u,v\in dom(f)
,

\{u,v\}\in\mathsf{w}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\{f(u),f(v)\}\in\rho_{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}}^{k}(f,\mathsf{w}),

and renaming distributes over set difference and union.
Thus
\rho_{\mbox{HasMultipleEdges}}^{k}
satisfies Conditions
6a
–
6d
.
∎
