Executive Summary
Accurate wind field predictions are essential for atmospheric dispersion modeling support-
ing nuclear nonproliferation, treaty verification, and emergency response. However, no
universally agreed-upon set of evaluation metrics currently exists, leading to inconsistent
reporting and difficulty comparing models across studies.
 This report provides a comprehensive framework by reviewing recommendations, sys-
tematically categorizing metrics (error, bias, correlation, and vector metrics), and demon-
strating their application through a QUIC model case study against EMRTC 2019 field
data.
 Key findings show that different metrics reveal different performance aspects, e.g., mod-
els excelling in wind speed prediction may fail in capturing the correct wind direction.
Single metrics can be misleading and therefore, comprehensive model evaluation requires
considering multiple metrics.
 Essential recommended metrics are:

 • Root mean square error (RMSE), which provides overall error magnitude.

 • Normalized root mean square error (NRMSE), which enables cross-regime compari-
 son.

 • Mean bias, which captures systematic over/underpredictions.

 • Pearson correlation coefficient, which captures correlation between the modeled and
 measured winds.

 • Number of modeled wind speed within a factor of X, e.g., factor of 2 (FAC2), and
 number of modeled wind directions within X-degrees, e.g., FAC 15 degrees. This
 metric captures the closeness of a model to data.

 Supplemental metrics include vector RMSE (VRMSE), when both wind speed and di-
rection fidelity are needed, and scaled average angle (direction error weighted by speed).
 Consistent adoption of these metrics across evaluations against data will enable mean-
ingful inter-model comparisons, identify best-performing approaches, and advance model
development.

1 Introduction
Atmospheric transport and dispersion modeling plays an essential role in nuclear non-
proliferation by assisting in the discrimination between civilian uses and undeclared nu-
clear programs, as well as supporting robust attribution and treaty compliance verification.
Atmospheric transport and dispersion models use wind fields to predict the fate of ra-
dionuclides released in the atmosphere as gasses and/or aerosols from for instance medical
isotopes production facilities, enrichment facilities, reprocessing operations, nuclear plant
accidents, unreported nuclear weapon’s tests. The accuracy of atmospheric dispersion mod-
els depends on several related factors: the ability of the meteorological model to reproduce
realistic wind fields, which govern the transport of radioactive material in the atmosphere;
the assimilation of representative weather conditions during model (re-)initialization; the
suitability of the dispersion scheme for the atmospheric constituent being modeled (gasses,
aerosols, reactive or decaying species); and the realism of the source term (injection height,
release profile). Consequently, a robust set of performance metrics is needed to evaluate
predicted wind fields against observations or higher fidelity models to quantify model un-
certainty, identify the meteorological and topographical conditions associated with poor
performance, and compare the skill of different modeling approaches.
 A universally agreed-upon set of metrics for wind model performance does not currently
exist, primarily because no single metric is sufficient to capture all relevant sources of er-
ror. Model performance must be assessed across widely different applications, atmospheric
conditions, and topographies. Different research groups prioritize different metrics based
on their specific objectives, leading to inconsistent reporting practices. For instance, at-
mospheric dispersion applications place greater emphasis on wind direction accuracy, as
directional errors directly translate to errors in plume trajectory and the spatial location of
peak concentrations [14], while general weather forecasting emphasizes overall wind speed
magnitude and temporal evolution to capture synoptic patterns and improve forecast skill
across various time scales. The diversity of priorities and contexts has led to myriad evalua-
tion approaches, which sometimes emphasize either error magnitude (e.g., root mean square
error), or bias (e.g., fractional bias), or correlation (e.g., Pearson correlation coefficient),
making direct inter-model comparisons difficult [6, 42]. The lack of standardization not
only complicates efforts to identify the best-performing model(s) for a given application
but also obscures understanding of which specific aspects of the model physics or param-
eterizations contribute to success or failure. Consequently, greater discussion within the
atmospheric modeling community is needed regarding which metrics are most informative
for specific applications, how different metrics weigh various aspects of model performance,
and what constitutes a minimum essential set of metrics that should be reported to facilitate
meaningful model evaluation and comparison.
 While atmospheric model validation has employed a variety of statistical metrics to
assess performance, these metrics are often distributed across different specialized studies
and application domains. Wind field validation and atmospheric dispersion validation each
utilize distinct suites of metrics suited to their specific evaluation needs, yet a consolidated
reference documenting these metrics—including their mathematical formulations, appropri-
ate applications, and interpretive guidance—would benefit both research and operational
communities. Wind field metrics must assess model performance in predicting magnitude,
direction, and temporal variability.

 This report reviews commonly used metrics for wind model evaluation, with a focus on
comparing model predictions against observations. We describe the benefits and limitations
of each metric and apply a set of metrics to a case study examining wind predictions from
the Quick Urban and Industrial Complex (QUIC) model against experimental results from
the 2019 field campaign at the Energetic Materials Research and Testing Center (EMRTC)
[2]. Finally, we provide recommendations for the best set of metrics that can deliver the
greatest insights into model performance.

2 Review of prior recommendations
This section reviews recommendations of metrics made in prior works, highlighting the
evolution of thought on model evaluation.
 Fox [8] focuses on model evaluation for atmospheric dispersion models and distinguishes
between difference metrics and correlation metrics. Specifically, they recommend a number
of difference metrics including mean bias, variance (or standard deviation) of the error
distribution, and mean absolute error, while for correlation metrics they recommend the
correlation coefficient in both time and space. In a series of papers, Willmott [42, 43]
responded to Fox with their own recommendation. They advocated reporting: (1) means
and standard deviations of observations and predictions, (2) the slope and intercept of a
least-squares regression between observed and predicted values, (3) total, systematic, and
unsystematic root mean square error, and (4) index of agreement. Specifically, Willmott
argues that a number of standard metrics can be computed from these reported statistics,
and argues against the use of correlation coefficient in favor of index of agreement and slope
and intercept of regression.
 More recent recommendations include those given by Chang and Hanna [6]. They review
methods to evaluate air quality models and recommend a set of performance metrics, as well
as suggestions for magnitudes of metrics for ‘good’ models. The set of recommended metrics
includes fractional bias, geometric mean bias, normalized mean square error (normalized by
the product of the mean of the observed and the mean of the predicted values), geometric
variance, correlation coefficient, and fraction of predictions within a factor of two. They
note that the fraction of predictions within a factor of two is the metric most robust to
outliers.
 Beyond air quality applications, several papers have addressed metrics for wind power
forecasting. For example, Optis et al. [28] suggest the use of mean bias, unbiased root mean
square error, earth mover distance, and correlation coefficient. They stress the importance
of separating systematic and random error in model predictions. However, it is important to
note that validating wind models for wind energy application differs from plume transport
and dispersion applications. For wind energy applications, wind speed distribution is critical
since power scales with the cube of wind speed—making small distribution errors lead
to large forecast errors [12]. This cubic relationship makes earth mover distance, which
quantifies differences between modeled and measured wind distributions, a particularly
appealing metric [28], though it remains uncommon in air quality wind model evaluation.
 These varying recommendations across applications highlight the need to select evalu-
ation metrics that are appropriate for the intended modeling objectives.

3 Definition of performance metrics for wind models
Performance metrics are here classified into the following categories:

 • Error metrics, which quantify the magnitude of error between predictions and obser-
 vations. The most common examples are root mean square error and mean absolute
 error, as well as their normalized versions.

 • Bias metrics, which quantify systematic errors between predictions and observations.
 The most common examples are mean bias and fractional bias.

 • Correlation metrics, which measure the strength and direction of the linear rela-
 tionship between observed and predicted values, regardless of whether the actual
 magnitudes are correct. The most commonly used correlation metric is the Pearson
 correlation coefficient, often reported as its squared value or the coefficient of deter-
 mination. An alternative metric is the index of agreement proposed by Willmott [42]
 in a number of papers.

 • Additional metrics will also be discussed which provide useful information that is not
 captured with the metrics listed above. Some of these include vector metrics [45],
 distribution metrics such as earth mover distance [28], and others.

The following sections discuss each performance metric category in more detail, including
advantages and disadvantages of each metric, before providing suggestions of which metrics
to report. Selected metrics are summarized in Table 3-1.
 This report focuses specifically on performance metrics for wind speed and wind direc-
tion, each defined in Section 3.1.

M AE are each best suited to different error distributions, with RM SE being optimal for

information about the distribution of error than either metric alone [21, 29], with smaller
ratios representing uniform errors with few outliers, and large ratios indicating the greater
presence of outliers.

RM SE squared, and therefore behaves similarly to this metric. However, as a performance
metric, M SE is generally less used than RM SE because it can be harder to interpret due
to the fact that it uses squared units rather than the original units.

3.3 Bias metrics
While error metrics quantify the total error of a model, they do not provide any information
about systematic error in the model, in order words, whether the model consistently over-
predicts or underpredicts the values of interest. This is the benefit of bias metrics, which
will be presented in this section.

simple bias, or mean bias, defined as:

Bias is therefore defined as the difference between the mean predicted value and the mean

For example, a wind speed bias of 2 m s−1 is much more significant with a low mean wind
speed than with a high mean wind speed. However, the simple bias can be useful when the
absolute value of bias is an important metric, such as in wind energy modeling [28].

The fractional bias is equivalent to the mean bias relative to the average of the means of the
predicted and observed values. Under this definition, the fractional bias lies in the range
[−2, +2]. Occasionally it is defined with absolute values such that it has a minimum value
of zero [11, 23]. The advantage of fractional bias over simple bias is the fact that it is a
dimensionless quantity, and therefore can be easily compared across different sites, different
ranges of mean wind speed, different times of day, etc. However, since it depends on the
mean of the predicted values at the denominator, it is not model independent. For wind
direction, fractional bias should be normalized by 180◦ , representing the maximum possible
angular difference. By this definition, fractional bias is bounded by a maximum value of 2,
and is comparable to the definition of fractional bias for wind speed.
 Simple bias and fractional bias are the two most commonly used bias metrics in the wind
modeling and plume dispersion literature. However, several considerations regarding the
use of bias metrics merit attention. Bias metrics are ensemble-averaged measures and their
reliability depends on having a large enough dataset with well-defined means. Therefore,
when using bias, it should be ensured that the means accurately represent the distributions
of the predictions and observations. For example, Figure 3-1a shows the wind rose for
daytime winds over five days from the EMRTC 2019 field experiment [2], with the mean

 Another method to measure bias is using the slope and intercept of a linear regression
between predicted and observed values [42]. Similar to mean and fractional bias, performing
a linear regression gives an aggregate picture of the agreement between predicted and
observed values. However, the separate slope and intercept quantify the multiplicative
bias and additive bias respectively. Assuming a linear regression of the form o = ap + b,
a perfect model yields a slope a = 1 and an intercept b = 0, whereas slope greater than 1
indicates the model systemically underpredicts relative to observed values, and vice versa
for slope less than 1. However, this differs from the over/underprediction represented by
mean bias. For example, a mean bias of 2 m/s indicates that on average the model predicts
wind speeds 2 m/s larger than observed, whereas a slope of 0.5 indicates that on average
the model predicts wind speeds that are twice the value of those observed. The intercept
on the other hand is more difficult to interpret. For a slope of 1, the intercept quantifies
the additive bias, and is equivalent to the negative of mean bias, with b > 0 indicating
underestimation and b < 0 indicating overestimation. However, when slope is not equal
to 1, the intercept represents the additive bias at p = 0, but this bias can change across
the range. For example, the combination of a negative intercept and a slope greater than
1 could indicate overprediction at low p but underprediction at large p. While slope and
intercept of the linear regression provide additional information over mean or fractional
bias, it can also complicate interpretation.

3.4 Correlation metrics
While bias metrics provide a measure of the systematic error in a model, and error metrics
provide information about total error, neither provides any information on whether the
model is able to capture the overall trends observed in the measurements. This is where
correlation metrics prove useful. Correlation metrics provide information about how well a
model captures the temporal or spatial variability observed in the data: if a model closely
follows this variability, this will yield a high correlation.

3.5.1 Vector metrics
The majority of studies evaluating wind models use some combination of the metrics listed
above separately for both wind speed and wind direction. However, it can also be useful to
consider vector metrics, which consider both wind magnitude and vector direction together.

depending on only u and v components. Here, we also consider the vertical w component
of velocity (perpendicular to the plane described by u and v), which can be of considerable
magnitude in areas of complex terrain. However, all vector metrics can be defined just in
terms of u and v components.

Similar to bias, skewness requires a sufficient number of measurements to be reliable, and

a definition for circular EM D which determines the minimum area between the two CDFs
starting from each possible point in the circular data, which allows EM D to be computed
for wind direction data as well.

4 Case study
We evaluate nighttime wind predictions from the Quick Urban and Industrial Complex
(QUIC) model against EMRTC 2019 experimental data [2] using multiple metrics to demon-
strate their insights to assessing wind speed and direction forecasts. Recently, a new sub-
model was developed within QUIC to account for slope winds. Since the model is still under
development, it will not be described here. What is presented is a first validation exercise
using different ways to initialize the model: (1) initialization from closest Mesowest station
using the temperature deficit from WRF simulations to drive the new submodel (referred
to as MW), (2) Mesowest initialization with a set temperature deficit of 1 K (MW 1K), (3)
initialization with WRF ARW model at 1-km resolution (WRF 1km), (4) and WRF ARW
with 200-m resolution (WRF 200m).
 For simplicity, the focus of this section will be on the results at only one sonic anemome-
ter. Figure 4-1 shows the time series of the observed wind speed and direction as well as
results of each QUIC simulation for the nighttime winds (from midnight to 6 AM local
time). The selected metrics include error metrics (RM SE, M AE, cRM SE, N RM SE for
wind speed, and scaled average angle, SAA, for wind direction), bias metrics (mean bias
and fractional bias, F B), fraction of predicted values with a factor of two (or 15◦ ) of mea-
surements (F AC2/F AC15◦ ), correlation metrics (Pearson correlation coefficient, R, and
index of agreement, IA), and vector metrics (V RM SE, u and v velocity bias, and vector
correlation coefficient).
 The results are shown in Tables 4-1-4-3. For each metric, the QUIC initialization
strategy with the best performance is shown in green, whereas the QUIC initialization
strategy with the worst performance is shown in red.
 The results for wind speed in Table 4-1 show how different metrics can reveal different
aspects of model performance. Based on the error metrics, the WRF 200m option gives
the best results for wind speed, whereas the WRF 1km is the worst performing. The
same trend holds for both correlation metrics as well. However, the bias metrics reveal
that the MW model yields the lowest systematic error, but suffers from large random
error. Also, interestingly, mean bias agrees with the error metrics that the WRF 1km case
performs worst, but the fractional bias of the MW 1K case is larger. This makes sense
in consideration of the definition of fractional bias and the results of the WRF 1km case
in Figure 4-1. Fractional bias is normalized by the mean of the average observations and
average predictions, and Figure 4-1 shows the the WRF 1km case vastly overpredicts the
wind speed on the final two nights. This error in the model increases the mean of the
predicted values, and thus the denominator of F B, leading to a smaller relative F B for
WRF 1km vs. the MW 1K, which instead tends to underpredict the wind speed. This
illustrates one drawback of F B, namely that it can be influenced by large errors in the
mean of the predicted values.
 It is also interesting to note that the fraction of predicted values within factor of 2
(F AC2) indicates the opposite relationship as that of the error metrics, namely that the
WRF 1km shows the highest F AC2 and WRF 200m shows the lowest. This can be ex-
plained by the nature of the error in each case. From the bias, we see that the error in
the WRF 1km case comes from a large overestimation of wind speed, whereas the WRF
200m case shows a notable underestimation in wind speed. Given the definition of F AC2,
models which overestimate wind speed are more likely to fall within the factor of two than

ones which underestimate. In other words, two models may have the same absolute error
but very different F AC2 ratios if one consistently overpredicts and one consistently under-

5 Recommendations
This report reviewed the most commonly used metrics for performance evaluation of wind
models, discussed the benefits and limitation of each metric, and showed a case study of
applying a subset of metrics to different wind predictions from the QUIC model. Based
on this, this section provides recommended metrics for wind model evaluation, along with
justifications for each recommendation.
 The recommended essential metrics that should be included for all evaluations of model
performance regardless of the specifics of individual study are as follows:

 • The use of RM SE is recommended as an overall error metric. RM SE and M AE
 are the two most commonly used error metrics, and the case study has shown that
 disagreement in determining the best model between RM SE and M AE is rare. The
 determining factor in the choice between RM SE and M AE is in the weight placed
 on outliers, where RM SE more heavily penalizes outliers. For this reason, RM SE
 is recommended to more heavily penalize models with large errors.

 • In addition to a dimensional error metric, the use of a normalized error metric is
 also recommend for wind speed. This study focused on N RM SE, normalized by
 the mean of the observations. This provides the benefit of quantifying the error
 relative to the mean values of wind speed, such that the N RM SE of sites with
 large difference in average wind speed can be compared. Normalization by the mean
 of observations is recommended such that large errors in predicted values will not
 influence the normalized error. This normalized quantity is only useful for wind
 speed errors. While wind direction errors could be normalized by 180◦ , representing
 the error as a fraction of the maximum possible error, this does not provide any
 additional information.

 • The mean bias is recommended as bias metric. This quantifies the systematic error
 in the model in dimensional units. As the case study has shown, the commonly used
 definition of fractional bias is susceptible to the influence of large positive biases in
 the predictions lowering the normalized value relative to the dimensional value. If a
 normalized value of bias is needed, a better definition would be normalized only by
 the mean of the observations.

 • As a correlation metric, the Pearson correlation coefficient is the standard. The index
 of agreement, by mixing correlation and error, leads to harder to interpret results,
 whereas the Pearson correlation coefficient measures solely correlation, and therefore
 is useful when provided along with appropriate error and bias metrics.

 • Reporting of the fraction of predictions within a factor of two (or 15◦ for wind di-
 rection) is also recommended. While this metric can occasionally lead to misleading
 results, such as shown in Table 4-1, it is a standard metric used in the field and
 provides another view of model performance.

 In addition to the above essential metrics, a number of supplemental metrics are also
recommended. These can provide additional details of model performance in specific cases,
but are not consider essential to evaluate model performance. These are as follows:

Los Alamos National Laboratory 5-1

 • V RM SE is an especially useful metric when results from wind speed and wind direc-
 tion contradict one another, as shown in the case study. In these cases, it can provide
 a good overall view of model performance, when both wind speed and wind direction
 are equally important.

 • SAA can provide additional information on wind direction error while accounting for
 wind speed magnitude. It is especially useful when periods of low wind speed are
 present, which may lead to misleading RM SE values.

 • cRM SE can be useful when quantifying the random error in the model is important.
 This metric can be inferred from the overall RM SE and mean bias, but reporting it
 directly leads to easier comparison between models.

 The set of metrics outlined above provides a robust picture of model performance.
Consistent use of these recommended metrics will provide greater ability to compare models
across different studies, and therefore greater ability to assess the performance of models
against one another.

5.1 Acceptance criteria
In wind model evaluation, there has been little discussion of what values of specific met-
rics can be considered as representing a “good” model. This was done for instance for
atmospheric transport and dispersion modeling by Chang and Hanna [6], where a set of
acceptance criteria were defined:

   • F AC2 ≥ 50%.

 • Mean bias is within ±30% of the mean.

 • The random error is within a factor of two to three of the mean.

Hanna and Chang [13] revised these recommendations for dispersion in urban areas. Specif-
ically, they soften the acceptance criteria due to the added complexity of buildings and
differing land uses. They recommended:

   • F AC2 ≥ 30%.

   • |F B| ≤ 0.67, or relative mean bias less than a factor of about 2.

 • N M SE ≤ 6, or random error less than about 2.4 times the mean.

 While similar acceptance criteria could be defined for wind speed or direction, there
are a number of limitations with defining acceptance criteria. First, defining general ac-
ceptance criteria is arbitrary by nature. For example, the prior studies define the criteria
based on typical values obtained by various models at the time. However, this estab-
lishes benchmarks based on current capabilities rather than establishing criteria based on
outcome-specific scenarios. Second, general acceptance criteria does not account for dif-
fering complexity. This is highlighted by the fact that separate criteria must be defined
for urban areas, and this does not account for countless other possible complexities such
as complex terrain, atmospheric stability, or low versus high winds. It would be expected

that even a “good” model would perform significantly worse in an area of extreme terrain
complexity, for instance. Instead, it is the opinion of the authors that acceptance criteria
should be based on theoretical goals, to capture specific phenomena of operational value.
However, it remains unclear how to define these specific operational goals, and these goals
will vary greatly between applications. We do not propose any specific goals here, but this
is a possibility for a future direction of research.
