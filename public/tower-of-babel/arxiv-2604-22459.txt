Reasoning About Probabilities, Actions, and Knowledge in Fuzzy Modal Logic
Daniil Kozhemiachenko, Igor Sedlár

Abstract
We explore a fuzzy modal logic that can formalise probabilistic reasoning about actions and knowledge. In particular, we deal with contexts involving statements about events expressed via modal formulas, e.g., ‘after doing a a , the probability of A A knowing that p p holds increases / decreases / is equal to 0.25 0.25 ’, ‘according to A A , p p is equally likely to happen after doing a a or b b ’, etc. We define the semantics of the logic on Kripke frames equipped with probability measures. We analyse the complexity of deciding the satisfiability of formulas of our logic over finitely branching models, for the full language and its fragments of varying expressivity. In particular, we identify several fragments of our logic where satisfiability is decidable in polynomial time.

Introduction

Probabilistic reasoning is a well-established field in artificial intelligence and knowledge representation and reasoning. It has found numerous applications in logic programming
[

,

, ontologies

In this paper, we consider a fuzzy modal logic that can formalise probabilistic reasoning about actions and knowledge. We study the complexity of the full language and its fragments, including those for which satisfiability is decidable in polynomial time. This presents a perspective on the computational properties of formal verification and automated reasoning with probabilistic specifications that describe agents’ knowledge and actions.

Probabilistic Logics

Probabilistic logics were first proposed by Nilsson (

Since then, even more expressive systems of probabilistic logics have been extensively studied. In particular, Ognjanovic et al. (

Probabilistic Reasoning in Fuzzy Logics

An important alternative approach to probabilistic logics is to use so-called

?

6

Reasoning about uncertainty in fuzzy logics is usually formalised with

Modal expansions of fuzzy probabilistic logics have also been extensively studied. In particular, Godo et al. (

Contributions

Although probabilistic fuzzy modal logics have received much attention, to the best of our knowledge, the framework proposed by Corsi et al. (
?
) is the only one where modalities can appear on both layers. It also seems that the computational properties of such logics are considerably less studied. Thus, in this paper, we bring together the frameworks of
[
14
]
and
[
28
]
and explore a logic that we call
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
, whose language is based upon a modal expansion of the combined Łukasiewicz and Product logics where epistemic and action modalities appear both in inner- and outer-layer formulas. As we will see in Section
3
, this enables us to reason with a wide range of events and express various relations between their probabilities.

We show that the language can (i) express probabilistic statements about modal events involving knowledge or effects of actions (e.g. statements about the probability of an agent knowing a proposition or a proposition holding after a specific action is performed) and (ii) represent lower and upper probabilities of events arising from epistemic uncertainty or from uncertainty about outcomes of actions. As we discuss in more detail below, system specifications of these forms are important in areas such as multi-agent systems, cybersecurity, and robotics.

The main technical result in the paper is an extensive study of the complexity of
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
. We establish that the validity (and satisfiability) problem for arbitrary formulas over finitely-branching models is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete. We also consider several fragments of our language where
validity is decidable in polynomial time
. Our contributions shed light on the computational features of formal verification and automated reasoning for probabilistic action-epistemic specifications within our expressive framework.

Language and Semantics

We begin with the definition of the
event language
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathtt{Act}
and
\mathtt{Agt}
be two countable sets of labels, representing the set of basic actions and the set of agents, respectively. Let
\mathtt{Var}
be a countable set of propositional variables, expressing basic events. The set
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{evt}}

is defined using the following grammar:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{evt}}\ni\alpha

\displaystyle\coloneqq p\mid\mathord{\sim}\alpha\mid\alpha\land\alpha\mid\mathsf{K}_{A}\alpha\mid[a]\alpha

where
p\in\mathtt{Var}
,
A\in\mathtt{Agt}
and
a\in\mathtt{Act}
.

As one can see,

is a variant of the classical multi-modal language containing knowledge modalities
\mathsf{K}_{A}
for agents
A\in\mathtt{Agt}
(where
\mathsf{K}_{A}\alpha
means that ‘the agent
A
knows that
\alpha
’) in combination with action modalities
[a]
for actions
a\in\mathtt{Act}
(where
[a]\alpha
means that ‘
\alpha
holds in all states that can be obtained by doing
a
’).

Definition 2

(Event frames and models)
.

•

An
event frame
is a tuple
\mathfrak{F}=\langle W,\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R}\rangle
where
W\neq\varnothing
,
\mathsf{E}
is a function from
\mathtt{Agt}
to the set of equivalence relations on
W
, and
\mathsf{R}:\mathtt{Act}\rightarrow 2^{W\times W}
.

•

An
event model
is a tuple
\mathfrak{E}=\langle W,\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R},V\rangle
with
\langle W,\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R}\rangle
being an event frame and
V:\mathtt{Var}\rightarrow 2^{W}
.

Given a model
\mathfrak{E}
, we will write
W_{\mathfrak{E}}
,
\mathsf{E}_{\mathfrak{E}}
,
\mathsf{R}_{\mathfrak{E}}
, and
V_{\mathfrak{E}}
to denote, respectively, the set of states of
\mathfrak{E}
, its epistemic and action relations, and its valuation.

Informally,
W
is a set of possible states of the environment (or possible worlds). The equivalence relation
\mathsf{E}_{A}
for
A\in\mathtt{Agt}
represents the epistemic state of agent
A
:
\langle w,u\rangle\in\mathsf{E}_{A}
(written also as
\mathsf{E}_{A}wu
or
w\mathsf{E}_{A}u
) means that, in state
w
, the agent
A
lacks sufficient information to distinguish state
w
from state
u
. Similarly, the relation
\mathsf{R}_{a}
represents possible outcomes of performing action
a\in\mathtt{Act}
:
\langle w,u\rangle\in\mathsf{R}_{a}
(written also as
\mathsf{R}_{a}wu
or
w\mathsf{R}_{a}u
) means that
u
is a possible outcome of performing
a
in state
w
. That is, we do not assume that actions are deterministic. We define
\mathsf{R}_{a}(w)\coloneqq\{w^{\prime}\mid w\mathsf{R}_{a}w^{\prime}\}
and
\mathsf{E}_{A}(w)\coloneq\{w^{\prime}\mid w\mathsf{E}_{A}w^{\prime}\}
. Finally, the valuation function
V
assigns to each propositional variable
p\in\mathtt{Var}
an
event
, that is, a subset of
W
. This event will also be denoted as
\|p\|_{\mathfrak{E}}
. Events
\|\alpha\|_{\mathfrak{E}}
for complex event formulas are defined as usual in modal logic:

\displaystyle\|\mathord{\sim}\alpha\|_{\mathfrak{E}}

\displaystyle=W\setminus\|\alpha\|_{\mathfrak{E}}

\displaystyle\|\alpha_{1}\land\alpha_{2}\|_{\mathfrak{E}}

\displaystyle=\|\alpha_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}}\cap\|\alpha_{2}\|_{\mathfrak{E}}

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{K}_{A}\alpha\|_{\mathfrak{E}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{A}\|\alpha\|_{\mathfrak{E}}\coloneqq\{w\mid\mathsf{E}_{A}(w)\subseteq\|\alpha\|_{\mathfrak{E}}\}

\displaystyle\|[a]\alpha\|_{\mathfrak{E}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{a}\|\alpha\|_{\mathfrak{E}}\coloneqq\{w\mid\mathsf{R}_{a}(w)\subseteq\|\alpha\|_{\mathfrak{E}}\}

Definition 3

(Probability space)
.

A
probability space
over an event model
\mathfrak{E}{=}\langle W,\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R},V\rangle
is a pair
\mathbb{S}{=}\langle\mathcal{F},\mu\rangle
s.t.:

•

\mathcal{F}
is a collection of subsets of
W
which contains
W
and
V(p)
for all
p\in\mathtt{Var}
; and is closed under complements, finite unions and the operators
\mathcal{K}_{A}
,
\mathcal{R}_{a}
for all
A\in\mathtt{Agt}
and
a\in\mathtt{Act}
;

•

\mu
is a finitely additive probability measure on
\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 4

(Probabilistic models)
.

A
probabilistic model
is a tuple
\mathfrak{F}=\langle W,\mathbf{F},\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R},\mu,V\rangle
s.t.
\langle W,\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R},V\rangle
is an event model and
\langle\mathbf{F},\mu\rangle=\langle\mathcal{F}_{w,A},\mu_{w,A}\rangle_{w\in W,A\in\mathtt{Agt}}
is a tuple of probability spaces over
\langle W,\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R},V\rangle
for each
w\in W
and
A\in\mathtt{Agt}
.

Intuitively,
\mu_{w,A}(X)
is the subjective probability of
X
for agent
A
in state
w
. To achieve generality, we do not assume that the same events are measurable for each agent in each state. This design choice gives rise to the plurality of probability spaces
\langle\mathbf{F},\mu\rangle
. To express
reasoning
about events and their probabilities, we will use a language based on the fuzzy modal logic. We thus undertake the approach proposed by Hájek et al. (
?
;
?
;
?
). The propositional fragment of our language is based on that of
{{\textbf{{\L}}}}\bm{\Pi}\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}}
— the logic that combines connectives of Łukasiewicz and Product logics. We further expand it with epistemic and action modalities corresponding to those in the event language.

Definition 5
.

The
full probabilistic language

\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{Pr}}_{\mathsf{EA}}
is defined using the following grammar:

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(\alpha)\mid\neg\phi\mid\phi{\to}\phi\mid\phi{\bullet}\phi\mid\phi{\to_{\Pi}}\phi\mid\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}}\mid\mathsf{K}_{A}\phi\mid[a]\phi

where
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{evt}}
,
A\in\mathtt{Agt}
and
a\in\mathtt{Act}
.

Definition 6
.

Given a probabilistic model
{\mathfrak{M}}
, we define
{\mathfrak{M}}
-interpretation
as a function
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}:\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{Pr}}_{\mathsf{EA}}\times W_{\mathfrak{M}}\to[0,1]
s.t.:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(\alpha),w)

\displaystyle=\mu_{w,A}(\|\alpha\|_{\mathfrak{M}})

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\neg\phi,w)

\displaystyle=1-\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi{\rightarrow}\chi,w)

\displaystyle=\min(1,1-\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w)+\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\chi,w))

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi\bullet\chi,w)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w)\cdot\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\chi,w)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi{\rightarrow_{\Pi}}\chi,w)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w)\leq\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\chi,w)\\
\dfrac{\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\chi,w)}{\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w)}\text{ otherwise;}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}},w)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{K}_{A}\phi,w)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w^{\prime})\mid w\mathsf{E}_{A}w^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}([a]\phi,w)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w^{\prime})\mid w\mathsf{R}_{a}w^{\prime}\}

Definition 7
.

Let

be finite. We say that

is

-satisfiable

(on

•

\Gamma

\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\chi,w)=1
for every probabilistic model

and

s.t.

.

Convention 1
.

We will further use

,
\mathsf{K}{A}\mathsf{K}{B}[c]
, etc. Similarly,
\blacklozenge
stands for (sequences) of diamond-like modalities. In addition, we define

\displaystyle\overline{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}}{\rightarrow}\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\phi{\oplus}\chi

\displaystyle\coloneqq\neg\phi{\rightarrow}\chi

\displaystyle\triangle\phi

\displaystyle\coloneqq\neg(\phi{\rightarrow_{\Pi}}\overline{0})

\displaystyle\overline{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\neg\overline{1}

\displaystyle\phi{\odot}\chi

\displaystyle\coloneq\neg(\phi{\rightarrow}\neg\chi)

The semantics of these connectives is derived from Definition

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{A}\phi,w)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w^{\prime})\mid w\mathsf{E}_{A}w^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\langle a\rangle\phi,w)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w^{\prime})\mid w\mathsf{R}_{a}w^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi\oplus\chi,w)

\displaystyle=\min(1,\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w)+\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\chi,w))

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi\odot\chi,w)

\displaystyle=\max(0,\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w)+\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\chi,w)-1)

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\overline{1},w)=1\\
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\overline{0},w)=0\end{matrix}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\triangle\phi,w)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w)=1\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

,

,

.

3
Expressivity

The key feature of our language is its use of modal operators both in the event layer and in the probabilistic layer. Importantly, the modal operators are not limited to
\mathbf{S5}
-style epistemic operators expressing ‘agent
A
knows that…’, but they include general
\mathbf{K}
-style action operators expressing ‘…holds after action
a
is performed’. This combined language is rather expressive, as we show in this section.

We first point out that the event layer of our language can express various kinds of
modal events
.

Example 1

(Some modal events)
.

•

‘After action
a
is performed,
p
will hold’:

[a]p
.

•

‘
A
knows that
p
’:

\mathsf{K}_{A}p
.

•

‘After
a
is performed,
A
will know that
p
’:

[a]\mathsf{K}_{A}p
.

•

‘
A
knows that after
a
is performed,
p
will hold’:

\mathsf{K}_{A}[a]p
.

Our probabilistic operator
\mathsf{Pr}_{A}
that expresses
subjective probability
of agent
A
can then be used to represent probabilities of these events. We will treat objective probability as a particular case of subjective probability. Formally, we will assume that
\mathtt{ob}\in\mathtt{Agt}
and write
\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}
in our examples when dealing with objective probability.

Reasoning about probabilities of such modal events is crucial in various areas. In multi-agent systems, e.g., card games
[
11
]
, agents need to reason about what other agents know, and this reasoning is often based on probabilistic information. In cybersecurity, a security system needs to be able to reason probabilistically about what the attacker might know
[
7
]
. Probabilistic robotics
[
32
]
is well-studied. In particular, in human-robot interaction scenarios, human controllers often need to take into account what robots with possibly faulty sensors are likely to know in a given situation
[
1
]
.

Using propositional connectives of the fuzzy logic
{{\textbf{{\L}}}}\Pi\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}}
, we can express comparisons between probabilistic statements, including threshold comparisons.

•

‘According to
A
, it’s not much more likely that
p
is going to happen if action
a
is performed than if action
b
is performed’:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}_{A}([a]p)\to\mathsf{Pr}_{A}([b]p)

•

‘Agent
A
likely knows that
p
’:

\displaystyle\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}}\to\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}(\mathsf{K}_{A}p)

•

‘The probability that
A
knows that
p
is at least
0.7
’:

\displaystyle\triangle(\overline{0.7}\to\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}(\mathsf{K}_{A}p))

•

‘According to agent
A
, it’s twice as likely for
p
to hold after
a
is performed that it is for
p
to fail’:

\displaystyle\triangle(\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}}\bullet\mathsf{Pr}_{A}([a]p)\leftrightarrow\mathsf{Pr}_{A}([a]\neg p))

The following examples illustrate the expressivity of our logic in the setting of automated bidding agents.

Example 2
.

Consider two competing automated bidding agents

’s subjective probability of

The subjective probability is represented by the atomic probabilistic formula

. The truth degree of the implicational formula

is

if

’s subjective probability of

\mathtt{rare}
is no less that
0.7
and is the difference between

’s subjective probability of

knowing
\mathtt{rare}
and
0.7
otherwise (cf. Definition
6
).

Using the delta operator, we can express the crisp statement that

’s subjective probability of

\mathtt{rare}
is at least
0.7
. This is expressed by
\triangle(\overline{0.7}\to\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(\mathsf{K}_{B}\mathtt{rare}))

’s subjective probability of

.

.

Assume that there is an auction with two participants:

and

. One of the things

(

). Now

We finish the section with the following observation. It is known from

w\in W
. This gives us the following statement.

Proposition 1
.

A formula
\phi\in\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{Pr}}_{\mathsf{EA}}
is
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
-valid iff it is valid on SI-frames.

The idea of the sample-independent semantics is to decouple the event model from the states over which probabilistic formulas are evaluated. Relying on the equivalence between sample-independent semantics and standard semantics, we can prove the
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-completeness of the satisfiability of
\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{Pr}}_{\mathsf{EA}}
formulas over finitely branching frames.

Theorem 2

Given
\phi\in\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{Pr}}_{\mathsf{EA}}

\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete to decide whether it is satisfiable over finitely branching frames.

Proof sketch.

We begin with
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hardness. For that, we consider a polynomial reduction from the satisfiability of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{evt}}
-formulas to
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}_{fb}}
-satisfiability. Namely,
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{evt}}
is satisfiable iff
\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(\alpha)
is
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}_{fb}}
-satisfiable.

Let us now consider
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-membership. Our proof combines the ideas of
[
17

and
[
21

. First of all, we note that all tableaux terminate: all rules have the branching factor of at most
2
and decompose formulas into their subformulas.

Now, we proceed as follows. Using a standard tableaux procedure akin to the one by Blackburn et al. (

by building a tableau for

Now, for each

\displaystyle\mathsf{At}_{A}(w)=

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{X}}_{w,A}

\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{\Xi\,\subseteq\,\mathsf{SF}^{\sim}_{A}(w)}u^{A}_{\Xi}=1\right\}

(

\displaystyle\cup

(

Remark 2
.

Recall from

Theorem 3

Given a

5
Polynomial Fragments of
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}

As is well-known, classical logic has many polynomially decidable fragments. One of the most important such fragments is
Horn logic
. A
Horn theory
can be represented as a set of
rules
of two kinds:
\bigwedge^{n}_{i=1}p_{i}{\rightarrow}q
and
\bigwedge^{n}_{i=1}p_{i}{\rightarrow}\bot
. In Łukasiewicz logic, there is a similar polynomially decidable fragment studied by Bofill et al. (
?
). It is called
simple-clause fragment
: a simple clause theory is a theory that contains clauses of the form
\bigoplus^{m}_{i=1}p_{i}\oplus\bigoplus^{n}_{j=1}\neg q_{j}
. Using Definition
6
and Convention
1
, simple clauses can also be represented in the rule form.

In this section, we will consider two modal probabilistic expansions of the simple clause fragment. The difference is that instead of propositional literals, our clauses (rules) will be built over
uniform probabilistic literals
.

Definition 16

(Uniform modal literals (
\mathsf{UML}
’s))
.

Consider the following grammar that defines a fragment of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{evt}}
:

\displaystyle\pi

\displaystyle\coloneqq p\mid\neg p\mid\langle a\rangle\pi\mid\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{A}\pi

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle\coloneqq p\mid\neg p\mid[a]\nu\mid\mathsf{K}_{A}\nu

Formulas of the type
\pi
and
\nu
are called
\blacklozenge
-literals
and
\blacksquare
-literals
, respectively. A literal is
proper
if it contains at least one modality and
propositional
otherwise.

Definition 17

(Uniform probabilistic literals (
\mathsf{UPL}
’s))
.

Consider the following grammar (
\lambda
is a
\neg
-free
\mathsf{UML}
):

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(\lambda)\mid\neg\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(\lambda)\mid\langle a\rangle\zeta\mid\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{A}\zeta

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(\lambda)\mid\neg\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(\lambda)\mid[a]\eta\mid\mathsf{K}_{A}\eta

Formulas of the type
\zeta
and
\eta
are called
\blacklozenge^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literals
and
\blacksquare^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literals
, respectively. A literal is
proper
if it contains at least one modality
on the outer layer
and
propositional
otherwise.

Observe that all modal events from Example
1
are expressed via
\mathsf{UML}
’s and can occur in
\mathsf{UPL}
’s. Furthermore, it is important to note that
\blacksquare^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literals can contain
\blacklozenge
, and
\blacklozenge^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literals can contain
\blacksquare
on the inner layer. Moreover, the fragments of
\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{Pr}}_{\mathsf{EA}}
that we are considering in this section will not use connectives
\bullet
and
\rightarrow_{\Pi}
. Thus, again (cf. Remark
2
), by
[
22
, Lemma 3]
and
[
33
, Lemma 4.6]
, it follows that their
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
-satisfiability can be w.l.o.g. established w.r.t.
witnessed models
and using rules from Fig.
1
.

5.1
Universal Probabilistic Rules

Let us now define our first fragment —
universal probabilistic rules
. As the name suggests, we can only use box-like modalities on the outer layer. Note, however, that
inner formulas
can use both types of modalities.

Definition 18

(Universal probabilistic rules)
.

A
universal probabilistic rule
(
\mathsf{UPR}
) is

•

either a formula of the form
\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}\eta_{i}\rightarrow\eta^{\prime}
s.t.
\eta
’s are
proper

\blacksquare^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literals;

•

or a formula of the form
\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}\eta_{i}\rightarrow\overline{0}
s.t.
\eta
’s are
\blacksquare^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literals.

A
\mathsf{UPR}
theory
is a set of formulas containing
\mathsf{UPR}
’s.

For simplicity, we will write rules
\eta\rightarrow\overline{0}
as
\blacklozenge^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literals. E.g.,
\langle a\rangle\neg\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(p)
instead of
[a]\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(p)\rightarrow\overline{0}
.

Let us briefly illustrate the expressiveness of universal probabilistic rules. We recall several statements considered in Section
3
. One can see we can
compare
two probabilities:
\mathsf{Pr}_{A}([a]p)\to\mathsf{Pr}_{A}([b]p)
is a
\mathsf{UPR}
. We can also express that performing
a
does not
increase
the probability with
[a]\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}(\mathsf{K}_{A}p)\to\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}(\mathsf{K}_{A}p)
. Moreover,
\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}}\to\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}(\mathsf{K}_{A}p)
can be rewritten as follows:
\{\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}(q)\rightarrow\neg\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}(q),\neg\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}(q)\rightarrow\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}(q),\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}(q)\rightarrow\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}(\mathsf{K}_{A}p)\}
.

On the other hand, we cannot express that an action does not
decrease
a given probability:
\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}([a]p)\to[b]\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}([a]p)
is not a
\mathsf{UPR}
since
\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathsf{ob}}([a]p)
is not a proper
\blacksquare^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literal. Likewise, qualitative uncertainty —
\mathsf{K}_{A}\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}(p)\leftrightarrow\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{A}\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathtt{ob}}(p)
— is not expressible as a
\mathsf{UPR}
.

Furthermore, crisp statements with
\triangle
rational constants other than
\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}}
(cf. Examples
2
and
3
) are not expressible either. On the other hand, all formulas in Example
4
belong to the
\mathsf{UPR}
fragment.

The next statement establishes the polynomial decidability of
\mathsf{UPR}
theories. Observe from Remark
2
that since
\mathsf{UPR}
theories
do not contain

\bullet
and
\rightarrow_{\Pi}
, our decidability result holds for
all
probabilistic models.

Theorem 4
.

Given a
\mathsf{UPR}
theory
\Gamma
, it takes polynomial time to verify whether it is
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
-satisfiable.

Let us sketch the proof. We first show that it takes polynomial time to decide whether a set of
\mathsf{UPL}
’s is satisfiable. Then, we consider
irreducible theories
, i.e.,
\mathsf{UPR}
theories
\Gamma=\Theta\cup\Xi
where
\Theta
is a set of
\mathsf{UPL}
’s and
\Xi
is a set of
\mathsf{UPR}
’s with at least two literals each s.t.
\Xi
does not contain any literal
\theta
s.t.
\Theta\models_{\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}}\neg\theta
. We show that it takes polynomial time to verify whether a given
\mathsf{UPR}
theory is irreducible.

We then prove that every irreducible theory is
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
-satisfiable. First, note that
\Theta
is satisfiable. Second, we pick one
proper

\blacksquare^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literal from the left-hand side of each rule in
\Xi
(that contains proper
\blacksquare^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literals) and show that there is a pointed probabilistic model
\langle{\mathfrak{M}},w\rangle
s.t.
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\theta,w)=1
for all
\theta\in\Theta
, and every literal we picked has value
0
. Third, we show that the propositional literals in the rules that are not satisfied by that model can be evaluated at
\tfrac{1}{2}
in
w
(whence all rules
without
proper
\blacksquare^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literals have value
1
at
w
). This guarantees us that every formula in
\Gamma
has value
1
at
w
.

Let us now sketch a polynomial decision procedure for
\mathsf{UPR}
theories. First, we transform the theory into the clause form, using that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi\rightarrow\chi,w)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\neg\phi\oplus\chi,w)
in each probabilistic model
{\mathfrak{M}}
. Second, we apply the following rule:

\displaystyle\text{if }\Theta\models_{\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}}\neg\theta,

\displaystyle\text{ then }\Theta,\theta\oplus\kappa\models_{\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}}\kappa

Using this rule, we need polynomial time to either transform
\Gamma
into an irreducible theory or infer a literal
\theta^{\prime}
s.t.
\Theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}\models_{\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}}\overline{0}
with
\Theta^{\prime}
being (possibly) augmented with other
\mathsf{UPL}
’s inferred via the above transformation. In the first case,
\Gamma
is
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
-satisfiable, and in the second, it is not.

5.2
Existential Probabilistic Rules

Our second fragment is
existential
probabilistic rules. One can think of them as a probabilistic version of the Łukasiewicz description logic
\mathcal{EL}_{\bot}
(cf., e.g.,
[
3
, Ch. 6]
for a presentation of
classical

\mathcal{EL}_{\bot}
). The main difference, of course, is that we will consider the complexity of
local
satisfiability (whether a formula is true in some state of a model) of theories containing existential probabilistic rules. This is because
global
satifiability (whether a formula is true in all states of a model) of Łukasiewicz
\mathcal{EL}_{\bot}
is undecidable
[
4
,
12
,
9
,
10
,
2
,
8
]
.

Definition 19
.

An
existential positive probabilistic rule
(
\mathsf{EPR}^{+}
) is a formula of one of the following kinds:

•

a
\mathsf{UPL}
;

•

\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}\zeta_{i}{\rightarrow}\overline{0}
with
\zeta
’s being propositional literals or
\neg
-free
\blacklozenge^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literals;

•

\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}\zeta_{i}\rightarrow\zeta
with
\zeta
’s being
proper

\neg
-free
\blacklozenge^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literals.

There is also a syntactic difference between
\mathsf{EPR}^{+}
and
\mathcal{EL}_{\bot}
. Namely, we can freely use negation over propositional atoms. E.g., a rule
\neg\mathsf{Pr}_{A}([a]p){\odot}\neg\mathsf{Pr}_{B}(\mathsf{K}_{A}\mathsf{K}_{B}q){\rightarrow}\overline{0}
is allowed, despite the negation in the left-hand side.

On the other hand,
\mathsf{EPR}^{+}
’s and
\mathsf{UPR}
’s are not completely dual. In particular, in contrast to
\mathsf{UPR}
theories, we cannot use negation in
\blacklozenge^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literals when they occur in implications. Thus, existential rules such as
\langle a\rangle\neg\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(p)\rightarrow\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{A}\mathsf{Pr}_{B}(q)
are not allowed (while
[a]\neg\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(p)\rightarrow\mathsf{K}_{A}\mathsf{Pr}_{B}(q)
is a
\mathsf{UPR}
). Hence, no formula from Examples
2
–
4
, except
\mathsf{Pr}_{A}([m]B\,\texttt{folds})\rightarrow\mathsf{Pr}_{A}([a]B\,\texttt{folds})
, belongs to the
\mathsf{EPR}^{+}
fragment.

The next statement shows that
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
-satisfiability of
\mathsf{EPR}^{+}
theories is also decidable in polynomial time. The proof is similar to that of Theorem
4
.

Theorem 5
.

Given an
\mathsf{EPR}^{+}
theory
\Gamma
, it takes polynomial time to verify whether it is
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
-satisfiable.

We conclude the section with a short sketch of the proof. Observe first from Theorem
4
that it takes polynomial time to check the
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
-satisfiability of a given set of
\mathsf{UPL}
’s or whether an
\mathsf{UPL}
is entailed from a set of
\mathsf{UPL}
’s.

Now, as was the case in Theorem
4
, it remains to show that irreducible
\mathsf{EPR}^{+}
theories are
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
-satisfiable. The difference is that instead of picking
\blacksquare^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literals from each rule and assigning them value
0
at
w
, we show that there is a pointed probabilistic model
\langle{\mathfrak{M}},w\rangle
where one proper
\blacklozenge^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-literal
from the left-hand side of each rule has value
0
at
w
. Thus, given an
\mathsf{EPR}^{+}
theory, we can use the same procedure as in Theorem
4
to either transform it into an irreducible (and hence, satisfiable) theory or infer a contradiction.

6
Related Work

As we mentioned in the introduction, the first family of logics that combined modal (epistemic) and probabilistic reasoning was proposed by Fagin and Halpern (
?
). There are several important differences between their logic and ours. First, probabilistic formulas in
[
17
]
are expressed via linear inequalities that are
two-valued
, and thus, are not well suited to express imprecise statements such as ‘the probability of
\alpha

is not much
lower (higher) than the probability of
\beta
’. Second, the expressivity of the modal probabilistic language proposed by Fagin and Halpern is incomparable with that of
\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{Pr}}_{\mathsf{EA}}
. On the one hand, there are no separate
inner
and
outer
formulas. Thus, e.g., the following formula (adapted to the notation in this paper) is permitted:
(\mathsf{Pr}_{B}(\mathsf{K}_{A}(\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(a){\geq}\overline{\tfrac{2}{3}})){\leq}\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}}){\rightarrow}\mathsf{K}_{B}q
. On the other hand, inequality formulas can contain only one agent label. Thus, statements such as ‘
A
and
B
consider
\alpha
equally likely’ (
\triangle(\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(\alpha){\leftrightarrow}\mathsf{Pr}_{B}(\alpha))
in
\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{Pr}}_{\mathsf{EA}}
) cannot be expressed, unless one specifies the degree of likelihood. Furthermore, the validity of some probabilistic logics is
\mathsf{EXPTIME}
-complete
[
17
, Theorem 4.5]
. Observe, however, that all logics in
[
17
]
have the finite model property, while in our case, this holds only for the
\{\bullet,\rightarrow_{\Pi}\}
-free fragment of
\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{Pr}}_{\mathsf{EA}}
(recall Remark
2
).

Our work, however, continues the study of
fuzzy
modal probabilistic logics. In particular, we expand the fuzzy
epistemic
probabilistic logic proposed by Corsi et al. (
?
) with
action
modalities as well as with additional connectives
\bullet
and
\rightarrow_{\Pi}
. These allow us to express (subjective)
conditional probability
of
\alpha
given
\beta
via
\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(\beta)\to_{\Pi}\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(\alpha\land\beta)
as well as
degrees of independence
of
\alpha
and
\beta
:
\mathsf{Pr}_{A}(\alpha\wedge\beta)\leftrightarrow(\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)\bullet\mathsf{Pr}(\beta))
. Moreover, Corsi et al. study the axiomatisation and expressivity of their epistemic probabilistic logic, while we are concentrating on complexity. We also note that the logic in
[
14
]
is the fragment of
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
without
\bullet
,
\rightarrow_{\Pi}
, and action modalities.

Another closely related work is by Majer and Sedlár (
?
) and Kozhemiachenko and Sedlár (
?
). There, a logic
{\textbf{K{\L}}}\bm{\Pi}\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}^{\mathsf{Pr}}
with action modalities and propositional event formulas is considered. Note, however, that even though
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
is more expressive than
{\textbf{K{\L}}}\bm{\Pi}\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}^{\mathsf{Pr}}
, the satisfiability problem in both logics is
\mathsf{PSPACE}

Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced and studied
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
, a modal probabilistic logic that unifies and expands the frameworks of Corsi et al. (
?
) on one hand and Majer and Sedlár (
?
) and Kozhemiachenko and Sedlár (
?
), on the other hand. A distinguishing feature of our framework is the presence of epistemic and action modalities in both logical layers.

We showed that
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
can express probabilistic statements about modal events involving knowledge or the effects of actions and lower and upper probabilities of events arising from epistemic uncertainty or from uncertainty about outcomes of actions. Such specifications are important in domains such as robotics and cybersecurity. We established that the satisfiability problem for the full language over finitely-branching frames is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete. We also identified specific syntactic fragments for which validity is decidable in polynomial time.

There are several promising avenues for future research. A primary objective is to provide a sound and complete axiomatisation for
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
. To enrich the framework and improve its applicability to reasoning about programs and multi-agent systems, we also intend to extend the current language to incorporate Propositional Dynamic Logic constructs and operators for group knowledge.

Finally, we plan to explore the semantic boundaries and other computational aspects of the logic. Our current complexity results in Section
4
for the full
\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{Pr}}_{\mathsf{EA}}
rely on a restriction to finitely branching frames. Investigating the complexity of the logic without this constraint is a necessary step towards generalising the framework. We also intend to expand the scope of uncertainty representation by incorporating fuzzy events and exploring other uncertainty measures alongside their qualitative counterparts. Identifying additional polynomially-decidable fragments of
\mathsf{EA^{Pr}}
remains a priority for practical implementation.

contains only clauses with at least two literals each, then it is satisfiable. Indeed, it suffices to consider a model with one state

\{\langle w,w\rangle\}
and all variables are evaluated at
\tfrac{1}{2}
. Each literal in every clause will also have value
\tfrac{1}{2}
. Since there are at least two literals per clause, this satisfies
\Gamma
.

Now let
\Gamma=\Xi\cup\Lambda
with
\Xi=\{\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{m}\}
and
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
and
\kappa
’s having at least two literals each. We proceed as follows. First, we check that
\Lambda
is satisfiable. By Lemma
3
, this takes us polynomial time. Then for every literal
\lambda
occurring in
\Xi
, we check whether (1)
\Lambda\models_{\mathsf{EA}^{{{\textbf{{\L}}}}\mathbf{\Pi}}}\neg\lambda
or (2)
\Lambda\models_{\mathsf{EA}^{{{\textbf{{\L}}}}\mathbf{\Pi}}}\neg\lambda
. In the first case, we remove from
\Xi
all
\kappa
’s that contain
\lambda
. In the second case, we remove all occurrences of
\lambda
from
\Xi
. Again, this takes us polynomial time to check all literals. If at some stage we have a clause
\lambda^{\prime}\oplus\lambda^{\prime\prime}
s.t.
\Lambda\models_{\mathsf{EA}^{{{\textbf{{\L}}}}\mathbf{\Pi}}}\neg\lambda^{\prime\prime}
, then we remove occurrences of
\lambda^{\prime\prime}
and check that
\Lambda\cup\{\lambda^{\prime}\}
is satisfiable. We then use
\Lambda^{\prime}=\Lambda\cup\{\lambda^{\prime}\}
to check the remaining literals. We proceed in this way until either (i) we encounter a clause
\lambda_{1}\oplus\lambda_{2}
s.t.
\Lambda^{\prime}\models_{\mathsf{EA}^{{{\textbf{{\L}}}}\mathbf{\Pi}}}\neg\lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda^{\prime},\lambda_{2}\models_{\mathsf{EA}^{{{\textbf{{\L}}}}\mathbf{\Pi}}}\overline{0}

-irreducible theory. In the first case,

is unsatisfiable, in the second case,

Lemma 8

\lambda

\mathcal{B}
