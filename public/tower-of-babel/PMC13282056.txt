Hairy Cell Leukemia: A Rare Differential for an Infiltrative Pancreatic Mass
Authors: Sarah Ibrahim, Sana Rabeeah, Nishant Aggarwal, Neud Kiros, Laith Jamil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hairy cell leukemia (HCL) is an uncommon B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder, which rarely presents as a mass lesion. We report a 62-year-old man with a history of HCL (now in remission) who was incidentally noted to have a pancreatic tail irregularity on imaging. Interval follow-up magnetic resonance imaging showed significant interval progression of infiltrative lesion, which now appeared as a pancreatic mass encasing the splenic vessels. Thereafter, endoscopic ultrasound with fine needle biopsy confirmed recurrent HCL. This case highlights a mass-like pancreatic manifestation of recurrent HCL, which may mimic pancreatic malignancy.

INTRODUCTION

Hairy cell leukemia (HCL) is a rare, indolent B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder, accounting for approximately 2% of all leukemias, with an estimated incidence of 0.3 per 100,000 person-years.1 It is characterized by the presence of atypical B-cells with distinctive cytoplasmic projections (hairy cells). These cells typically infiltrate the peripheral blood, bone marrow, and spleen, while less common involvement has been described in the liver and lymph nodes.2,3 Although HCL is highly responsive to initial therapy, relapse is not uncommon, particularly with longer durations of follow-up. We report a patient with recurrent HCL presenting as a pancreatic mass lesion with encasement of the splenic vessels.

CASE REPORT

A 62-year-old man with a history of HCL (in remission) presented with right lower extremity sciatic pain. He had a known history of HCL diagnosed 9 years before presentation that was treated with 6 cycles of cladribine after which he had been in remission. He underwent lumbar spinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) which demonstrated degenerative disc disease but incidentally showed a renal lesion. An abdominal CT scan with and without contrast confirmed multiple left renal cysts and noted a hypoechoic change with irregular margins involving the tail of pancreas (Figure 1). For further characterization, an MRI of the abdomen with and without contrast was obtained, which confirmed the presence of irregularity with signal alteration at the tail of pancreas but did not show a discrete mass (Figure 2). Follow-up MRI after 9 months showed significant interval progression with a infiltrative process along the external contour of body and tail of pancreas, inseparable from the splenic hilum, which was partially encasing the splenic vessels with a normal caliber pancreatic duct (Figure 3). Complete blood count and liver function tests were unremarkable. Serum carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (12 U/mL) and carcinoembryonic antigen (1.8 ng/mL) were not elevated. Peripheral blood flow cytometry did not show any evidence of lymphoproliferative disorder.

Follow-up magnetic resonance imaging of the pancreas. (A) and (B) Interval progression of infiltrative soft tissue involving the pancreatic body and tail with encasement of the splenic vasculature (arrows).

Endoscopic ultrasound revealed a poorly defined 6.3 cm, heterogeneous, solid mass in the pancreatic tail with encasement of the splenic vessels and abutment of the spleen in addition to enlarged lymph nodes at the porta hepatis (Figure 4). Fine needle biopsy (FNB) of the pancreatic lesion demonstrated small lymphocytes with characteristic hairy cytoplasmic projections. Flow cytometry performed on the FNB specimen confirmed a clonal B-cell population expressing pan-B cell markers (CD19, CD20), as well as CD11c, CD25, CD103, and kappa light chain restriction, consistent with recurrent HCL.

Endoscopic ultrasound of the pancreas. (A) Heterogeneous hypoechoic mass in the pancreatic tail with poorly defined margins. (B) Color Doppler demonstrating encasement of the splenic vasculature.

During the same evaluation, an incidental 2-cm fungating mass at the esophagogastric junction was identified and biopsy confirmed esophageal adenocarcinoma, which was subsequently resected endoscopically.

A positron emission tomography (PET)/CT scan demonstrated fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-avid infiltrative lesions in the pancreas and the esophagogastric junction with multiple FDG-avid mediastinal, peripancreatic and porta hepatis lymph nodes (Figure 5). Subsequent endoscopic ultrasound with fine needle aspiration of subcarinal, porta hepatis, and peripancreatic lymph nodes showed monotonous population of atypical lymphocytes without any metastatic carcinoma. An ultrasound-guided left supraclavicular lymph node biopsy and CT guided right paracaval lymph node biopsy also confirmed recurrent HCL without metastatic carcinoma.

For his HCL recurrence, he received 1 cycle of cladribine in combination with rituximab, with excellent response. Follow-up PET scan showed improvement with most recent PET scan 3 years after completion of chemotherapy demonstrated no abnormal tracer uptake, consistent with complete radiographic remission. The patient remains under ongoing surveillance with no evidence of recurrent disease.

DISCUSSION

HCL typically involves the peripheral blood, bone marrow, and spleen, with less common involvement reported in the liver and lymph nodes.2,3 Pancreatic involvement is exceptionally rare.4 To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of HCL presenting as a pancreatic mass with encasement of the splenic artery and vein.

Presentation of HCL as a pancreatic mass—especially one that involves the surrounding structures (such as in our patient)—can mimic that of pancreatic adenocarcinoma. Importantly, patients with HCL are also at increased risk of secondary malignancies, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma must remain a key diagnostic consideration when a pancreatic lesion is identified in this population.5 Diagnosis of HCL in such a rare presentation requires a high index of suspicion and integration of histopathology, immunophenotyping, and advanced imaging techniques.6 In our case, FNB of the pancreatic lesion revealed characteristic lymphocytes with cytoplasmic projections. The immunophenotypic profile, including positive pan-B cell markers and CD11c, CD25, CD103, and kappa light chains, confirmed HCL according to established criteria.6

The standard treatment of HCL involves purine analogs such as cladribine, which achieve durable remission in most patients.7 However, relapse is not uncommon, as illustrated in our case. For relapsed or refractory disease, the addition of rituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, to cladribine has been shown to improve response rates and depth of remission.8

Our patient's coexistence of esophageal adenocarcinoma and HCL emphasizes the significance of long-term surveillance in hematologic cancer survivors. Patients with HCL, particularly those treated with purine analogues, are at increased risk of secondary malignancies including gastrointestinal, skin, lung, and prostate cancers. The necessity for proactive and thorough surveillance strategies in this population is highlighted by previous research showing that secondary malignancies significantly contribute to morbidity and mortality in long-term HCL survivors.9,10

This case underscores the importance of considering HCL in the differential diagnosis of pancreatic masses, particularly in patients with a history of hematological malignancy.
