Retrospective analysis of real-world clinical use of comprehensive genomic profiling in solid tumors in Finland 2017-2020
Authors: M. Myllynen, J. Kononen, E. Alanne, J. Koivunen, A. Anttalainen, L. Ukkola-Vuoti, S. Tuominen, M. Mäkinen, J. Laine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrecision oncology has developed significantly during the last decade. To understand and describe the use of genomic profiling for cancer patients, we carried out a retrospective study in Finland.Materials and methodsThe study included 297 patients who underwent comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) as part of their clinical care during 2017-2020. In addition to the genomic data, relevant background and clinical information were gathered from the patient records. Additionally, the actionability of the test results, clinical response and survival were evaluated.ResultsMost patients had either prostate (16.8%), breast (14.8%), or pancreatic cancer (11.8%). The majority of the patients had metastatic disease (85.8%) and had median age of 61 years. Patients were heavily pretreated, with 51.3% having received three or more treatment lines before CGP testing. Of the patients, 65.2% had at least one actionable alteration in their samples. The treatment decision was based on the CGP test result for 25.6% (n = 76) of the patients. The main reason for not giving CGP-guided treatment was the lack of clinically significant alterations based on the clinician’s judgment. Explorative survival analysis suggested variation across different ESMO Scale for Clinical Actionability of Molecular Targets tiers. It also indicated that a higher number of treatment lines before CGP testing and decreased performance status at testing were associated with poor survival.ConclusionsThis study provides a descriptive analysis of the clinical and genomic characteristics of the Finnish CGP-cohort and explores the clinical outcomes. The results align with recent findings from other European countries and provide a context for ongoing prospective studies.

Introduction

During the past decade, precision oncology has taken leaps forward with the approval of numerous targeted therapies. Concurrently, the demand for advanced diagnostics such as comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) has increased.

After the first prospective clinical trial for molecular treatment matching based on tumor profiling (SHIVA trial) was negative for progression-free survival (PFS) improvement,1 the debate on the benefit of genomic profiling has been ongoing. Since then, many molecularly targeted trials such as MyPathway,2 Targeted Agent and Profiling Utilization Registry (TAPUR),3 and Drug Rediscovery Protocol (DRUP) trial4 have provided growing evidence on molecularly guided treatment options.5 However, the use of genomic profiling has moved beyond clinical trials into the clinic, particularly for rare and hard-to-treat cancer types or cancers involving multiple targetable markers such as non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC). The increasing number of tumor-agnostic approvals further increases the need to expand multigene next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels to other cancer types.6 In 2020, the ESMO guideline recommended using multigene NGS panels in advanced NSCLC, cholangiocarcinoma, and prostate and ovarian cancers.7 In the 2024 update, several cancer types, such as advanced breast cancer and colorectal cancer, were added to the guideline, making routine NGS testing a realistic option for a significant number of patients.8 Integrating the ever-growing amount of testing and treatment options into clinical routine might pose challenges, as access to testing or treatment options might be restricted in certain geographical areas.9, 10, 11 Retrospective characterization of this real-world use of CGP has been lacking in the European context. Only recently, European results have been published from France12 and Germany.13,14

In many publicly funded health care systems, the routine use of genomic profiling is limited by the availability of drug access.11 Recently, new avenues have opened because different versions of the DRUP trial have enabled access to molecularly guided treatment options that are also outside of their approved indications.4,15 This study investigates the use of the Foundation Medicine (FMI) CGP service in Finland during 2017-2020 before the start of the Finnish DRUP-like trial FINPROVE (NCT05159245) and during a period when CGP services such as FMI first became widely available in Europe.16 We aim to provide a retrospective context to these prospective trials by characterizing the patient population, the actionability of the profiling, and clinical outcomes in the Finnish health care context. These findings can help in establishing a real-world benchmark for the Nordic region and the rest of Europe when evaluating the impact of evolving precision oncology programs.

Materials and methods

Study objective

The primary objective of this study is to describe the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients receiving FMI genomic profiling in Finland during 2017-2020. The secondary objectives include analysis of detected alterations and their implications (actionability, treatment decisions, and hereditary counseling). Exploratory objectives include analysis of the clinical outcomes in terms of response, overall survival (OS), and time to next treatment ratio (TTNT2:TTNT1).

Study design, population, and data source

This retrospective study was conducted with permission from the Finnish Social and Health Data Permit Authority (Findata; data permit no. THL/1370/14.02.00/2021) and by the provision of the Act on the Secondary Use of Health and Social Data (Finland’s Ministry of Justice 552/2019). Therefore, no informed consent from the patients was required.

This study included adult patients who were FMI (CGP) tested at Docrates, Turku University Hospital (TYKS), or Oulu University Hospital (OYS) in Finland between February 2017 and December 2020. CGP testing was performed at the discretion of hospital/treating physician as part of routine care, without restrictions on disease type or stage. Docrates is a private health care provider while TYKS and OYS are public health care providers. Data were collected as a manual chart review, where clinical experts collected data of CGP-tested patients according to a list of variables agreed upon beforehand. Additionally, the CGP test results were collected from the hospitals, and the test result-related data were extracted from the test reports. Dates of death were collected from the Digital and Population Data Services Agency, while treatment-related data were supplemented with data from Hospital District of Helsinki and Uusimaa data lake. The data were combined, pseudonymized, and released for analysis into a secure analysis environment by Findata. The data collection permission was granted by Findata on 4 February 2022, and the final data collection from medical records occurred on 31 July 2023. For further details on the cohort formation, see Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100724.

FMI genomic testing

The hybrid capture-based NGS tests have been validated as described elsewhere.17, 18, 19 The patients in this study underwent at least one of the following tests: FoundationOne CDx (tissue) (or earlier versions), FoundationOne Liquid CDx (blood) (or earlier versions), or FoundationOne HEME (tissue).

Patient characteristics and outcomes

Characteristics were reported as the number and proportion of patients for categorial variables and median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables. Proportion of missing values was reported for each applicable variable.

Actionability of genomic findings (on-label/off-label) was evaluated as reported in CGP reports by FMI. Clinical trials indicated in the CGP report were excluded because of their limited availability in Finland.

Response to treatment was evaluated in any of the following ways based on availability in patient records: Diagnostic imaging with formal measurement (e.g. RECIST), diagnostic imaging without formal measurement, laboratory testing, diagnostic scoping, clinical impression, other, unknown, or not reported.

OS was defined as time from the CGP testing date until death (all-cause; event). The length of the treatment line (TTNT) was used as a surrogate for PFS. TTNT was calculated for the treatment lines that began immediately before (TTNT1) and after (TTNT2) the date of the CGP testing based on the recorded start and end dates. TTNT included the beginning of next treatment line and death (all-cause) as events. OS, TTNT1, and TTNT2 were censored at the end of the study (31 December 2021) and were analyzed with Kaplan–Meier estimates. Median times with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated when reached. Factors associated with OS were assessed with a Cox proportional hazards model. The hazard ratios with 95% CIs and P values were calculated. ESMO Scale for Clinical Actionability of Molecular Targets (ESCAT) tier status in OS analysis was assessed based on the most recent literature. To maintain high validity, ESCAT Tier information was sourced primarily from ESMO NGS or cancer type-specific guidelines. In the absence of information in these guidelines, additional literature search was performed.

Statistical analyses

P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant, and multiple testing correction was not applied. P values less than 0.001 are reported as ‘<0.001’. Missing values were not imputed. Listwise deletion was used for missing values. Sensitivity analysis was not performed. According to the Act on the Secondary Use of Health and Social Data, Findata must ensure anonymity. Therefore, to protect study participants’ anonymity, only frequencies more than five are reported. Furthermore, to prevent inferring the exact number of censored patients based on other subcategories or from the total number of patients, some patient numbers over four have been replaced with ‘Censored’. This is in line with the guidelines by Findata. Analyses were performed in R version 4.0.3.

Results

Patient characteristics

The cohort for descriptive characteristics consisted of 297 adult patients diagnosed with a solid tumor who successfully underwent CGP testing during 2017-2020 (all CGP-tested cohort). CGP results were available for 287 patients. Of the entire cohort (n = 297), 74 patients were excluded from the downstream analysis because their date of death was not retrievable from the Digital and Population Data Services Agency; the majority of these patients were not Finnish citizens (analysis cohort n = 223; Table 1). The median age at the time of the CGP test was 60.84 years (IQR 50.68-70.24; Table 1). However, 25.3% of the patients were aged 70 years or older. Approximately half of the patients were female (51.2%). The majority of the patients had metastatic disease (85.8%), and the median time from the diagnosis of metastasis to testing was 11.14 months (IQR 2.87-29.26; Table 1). The most common cancer types in the cohort were prostate, breast, and pancreatic cancers, accounting for 16.8%, 14.8%, and 11.8% of cases, respectively (Table 1). Most of the patients (77.8%) had been offered CGP testing at the private cancer hospital Docrates, while the remaining patients underwent testing in public hospitals in Finland. Overall, 81% of the patients had their results reviewed by a molecular tumor board. Most patients had a good performance status [85% with Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) PS 0-1] at the time of CGP testing (Table 1). The most often altered genes are shown in Table 2. The CGP analysis was performed using tissue samples in 53.3% (153 of 287) of the patients and liquid biopsies in 46.7% (134 of 287; Table 3). Genomic findings

The genomic findings were investigated. KRAS, PIK3CA, TP53, and PTEN were identified as the most frequently altered genes (Table 2). In the all CGP-tested cohort, 85.4% of the patients had at least one mutation found (Table 3). There were minor differences in findings between solid tumor samples and liquid biopsies (e.g. KRAS mutation was found in 28.8% of tumor samples whereas it was 16.4% in liquid biopsies; Table 2). The median tumor mutational burden (TMB) was 4.00 mut/Mb (IQR 1.00-5.00) and TMB-high was seen only in 5.6% of the patients (16 mut/Mb threshold). Most patients were microsatellite stable (44.3%; considerable proportion not evaluable for microsatellite instability status; Table 3). Gene fusions and rearrangements were analyzed separately, revealing that 5.9% of patients harbored a rearrangement in their tumor tissue or circulating tumor DNA (Table 3).

Clinical actionability and outcomes

The actionability of the CGP test results was evaluated based on the suggested on- and off-label treatments in the FMI reports [initially, the reports were based on Food and Drug Administration indications but were updated to European Medicines Agency indications in 2017]. In the all CGP-tested cohort, 65.2% of the patients had an actionable genomic alteration in their sample (65.9% in the analysis cohort). Representing more real-world actionability, 27.9% of the patients had an on-label alteration in the all CGP-tested cohort and 42.7% among patients receiving treatment based on the test result (Table 3). Clinical trials indicated in the CGP report were not considered due to their limited availability in Finland. Based on the findings, the initial diagnosis was changed in fewer than five patients, and hereditary testing was suggested for 9% of the patients (Table 1). The most common genes with actionable alterations (≥1% frequency) are shown in Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100724, with KRAS and PIK3CA being the most frequent.

Of the patients, 25.6% (n = 76) received treatment based on the CGP results (Table 4). The main reason for not receiving treatment was that the test did not find clinically significant mutations based on clinician evaluation (64.3%). For 11.3% of the patients, it was indicated in the patient records that the recommended treatment was not reimbursed in Finland, and 5.4% of the patients were deemed unsuitable for the treatment (Table 4). In the all CGP-tested cohort, 51.3% had three or more treatment lines before CGP testing (Table 4). After CGP testing, the majority of the patients (58.2%) received at least one treatment line before death or loss to follow-up (the all CGP-tested cohort). However, half of the patients (49.1%) who did not receive treatment based on the CGP results did not receive any treatment lines after the test. In contrast, among the patients who had been given treatment based on the CGP result, the number of post-CGP treatment lines was higher, with 82.9% of patients receiving at least one post-CGP treatment line, and 29.3% of the patients receiving three or more treatment lines after the testing (Table 4).

The response to the treatments after the CGP test was evaluated (mostly by imaging, 80.8%, Supplementary Table S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100724). Patients with treatment not based on the CGP result achieved partial response in 21.5% of the cases. There were no complete responses. Among patients whose treatment was chosen based on the CGP result, 29.2% achieved partial response and fewer than five patients had complete response (Table 4). The treatment modalities that the patients were given pre- and post-CGP testing are described in Supplementary Table S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100724. In the CGP-based treatment group the patients were more often prescribed immunotherapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI)/multi-TKI and monoclonal antibody treatments and notably, less chemotherapy (Supplementary Table S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100724).

OS was evaluated for patients receiving CGP-based treatments with different ESCAT tiers20 and for patients who did not receive treatment based on the CGP results (Table 3). The median OS for patients with ESCAT Tier I/II alterations was 10.5 months (95% CI 5.6-18.2). For those with ESCAT Tier III or lower, it was 6.7 months (95% CI 3.9-12.0) and for patients with no CGP-based treatment, 7.4 months (95% CI 5.9-9.4; Figure 1A). It should be noted that the ESCAT tiers were assigned retrospectively and not at the time of the treatment. This may influence the results as certain treatments were not necessarily available in 2017-2020 (Supplementary Table S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100724). Additionally, we evaluated the OS more closely for the patients who did not receive treatment based on the CGP results, distinguishing between those who received at least one and those who did not receive any subsequent treatment line. Among this group, for patients without any post-CGP treatments, the median OS was 3.7 months (95% CI 1.4-6.2) and for patients with at least one treatment line, it was 12.3 months (95% CI 9.0-19.4; Figure 1B). The OS for the entire analysis cohort and different tumor types is presented in Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100724. The association of patient characteristics with OS was evaluated with the Cox model. A higher number of treatment lines before CGP testing and worse performance status at testing were associated with poor survival. Finally, we evaluated the intra-patient TTNT ratio between the pre-CGP (TTNT1) and post-CGP (TTNT2) treatment lines (TTNT2:TTNT1), similar to the PFS2:PFS1 ratio. In the literature, a PFS2:PFS1 ratio of ≥1.3 is considered a sign of benefit from the investigated modality.21 In our data, the median duration of the latter treatment line was shorter irrespective of whether the treatment was chosen based on the CGP result or not. However, the decrease in the latter treatment line duration was smaller when treatment was given based on the CGP result with a median ratio of 0.598 for patients without CGP-based treatment and 0.840 for CGP-based treatments (Supplementary Table S8 and Figure S3A and B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100724). The difference was not statistically significant.

Discussion

Real-world data (RWD) on the use of CGP in the European context remain scarce, with recent exceptions including the French REALM study and German cohorts.12, 13, 14 To our knowledge, this study is among the first pan-cancer studies in the Nordic countries to (retrospectively) investigate the use of CGP.22 Previously, tumor type-specific investigations and prospective pan-cancer analyses in the phase I setting have been published, most notably the Danish CoPPO trial, which included more than 2000 patients.22, 23, 24 Notably, our study included both tissue and liquid biopsy samples.

This study demonstrates that it is feasible to retrospectively analyze CGP use using Finnish electronic health records with a manageable amount of missing data. The study was performed as a patient chart review including multiple nonstructured and interpreted variables, reducing the amount of standardization. Additionally, the sample size for the survival analysis was reduced due to unavailable death dates for foreign patients treated at the Docrates Cancer Centre.

In general, patients were tested when the disease was metastatic, and they were slightly younger than cancer patients in general. The median age in the study was 60.84 years, whereas the median age for cancer diagnosis in general was 66 years (National Cancer Institute, https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/age). During the early years of CGP, testing was possibly more frequently offered to younger patients and not carried out routinely at the diagnostic stage in any tumor type, which may have influenced this. This is aligned with the observed median duration of 11 months from the diagnosis of metastatic disease to testing. This delay should be monitored in future studies to assess the integration of CGP into routine clinical use. This delay might have been influenced by patients moving between private and public hospitals. Most patients were relatively fit (85% ECOG PS 0-1), demonstrating a certain degree of targeting of this diagnostic modality. Outsourced CGP analysis may also raise concerns regarding potentially longer turnaround times and the risk of clinical deterioration in patients. However, this does not appear to be the case in our cohort because for only 10.8% of patients (5.4% for each category), the reason for not receiving treatment was documented as ‘patient not suitable’ or ‘noncompliance’, both of which might reflect clinical deterioration in some cases. Additionally, between 2017 and 2020, in-house NGS panels were not routinely utilized in Finland for many tumor types, and consequently, the in-house NGS turnaround times were not always optimal either.25

In our study, 25.6% of the patients were prescribed a treatment based on the CGP test result. This is somewhat higher than previously published rates in the European context in retrospective analyses, where approximately 17% of patients received recommended treatments,12,13 but is comparable to the 24% treatment rate observed in the prospective CoPPO trial.22 The fact that the majority of the patients in our study were tested in a private center might have contributed to this finding, as off-label treatment possibilities in Finnish public hospitals are limited. This is also supported by the finding that only for 11.3% of the patients, the reason for not receiving the treatment was the lack of reimbursement. However, this finding includes some degree of uncertainty as the non-reimbursed status was only noted when it was explicitly written in the patient records. The majority of patients had an actionable (on- or off-label) alteration (65.2%), which is in line with what has been published earlier (e.g. the French REALM study with 75%, including 17% of trial drugs).12,23,26

The median OS was longer for patients treated based on CGP findings with ESCAT Tier I/II alterations compared with those with lower tiers or those with no CGP-based treatment. A similar finding reached statistical significance in the Danish CoPPO trial, which likely reflects that trial’s larger cohort size and potentially broader access to phase I treatments compared with our study.22 However, strong conclusions from ESCAT Tier comparison cannot be made due to potential differences in these patient groups in our study. Retrospective analysis of the ESCAT Tiers has some limitations, as the study data were collected from patients tested during 2017-2020, when possibly not all treatments were available. In addition, for patients without CGP-based treatment but with at least one line of treatment post-CGP, the median OS was comparable with that of the ESCAT I/II group. This latter analysis, however, is hampered by immortality bias. In general, there was a trend toward improved responses, less deterioration of TTNT2, and improved OS in the Cox model when treatments were assigned based on CGP results but without statistical significance. In addition to the possible confounding variables and the heterogeneous population in this study, this lack of significance might be due to the fact that the patients had already received multiple treatment lines before CGP testing. The treatment benefit may be minor in this late-line setting, which is a recurring problem in CGP studies and has led to inconsistent results of the precision oncology approach in pan-cancer setting.1,5,27,28 Our analysis showing the number of treatment lines and performance status being significantly associated with the OS underscores this fact. Additionally, one of the largest cancer types in this study was poorly treatable pancreatic cancer. From the study period 2017-2020 toward the current year 2026, CGP has been seen to move into earlier treatment lines, possibly enhancing cost-effectiveness and patient outcomes.8

The DRUP-like trials in Norway (IMPRESS, NCT04817956), Sweden (MEGALiT, NCT04185831), and the Netherlands have reported disease control rates (complete response, partial response, or stable disease at 16 weeks) of 40%, 25%, and 34%, respectively, in standard treatment-exhausted populations.4,29,30 These results are similar to our finding with patients treated based on the CGP results (29.2% partial response and additional ‘less than five’ patients with complete response). However, it should be noted that response evaluation in a real-world setting is more heterogeneous in terms of methods and criteria, which can potentially lead to an overestimation of response rates.31 The MEGALiT trial also investigated the PFS2:PFS1 ratio and found that the physician’s choice treatment provided a better outcome than genomics-guided treatment (only 6% had ≥1.3 ratio).30 In contrast, in the German INFINITY registry and Danish CoPPO trials, ≥1.3 ratio was observed in approximately 30% of the patients.14,32 The Danish DRUP-like trial, ProTarget (NCT04341181), recently reported a clinical benefit rate of 30% and a median PFS of 2.49 months.33 The Finnish FINPROVE trial reported its first results at ESMO 2024, demonstrating a 4.5-month median PFS when choosing patients with ESCAT I/II Tier alterations.34 This slightly exceeds the median TTNT2 of 2.9 months observed in our study for CGP-based treatments. However, although many of these results are promising for treatment-exhausted patients, the benefit should ideally be confirmed in a randomized trial. Recently, the ROME Trial (NCT04591431), which is a randomized multi-basket phase II trial evaluating targeted treatment against standard of care, demonstrated a statistically significant increase in the objective response rate (17.5% versus 10%) and median PFS (3.5 months versus 2.8 months).35,36 However, significant caution is advised when comparing the aforementioned studies due to potential differences in inclusion criteria, genomic assays used, cancer types, and available treatments.

Our study has several limitations. First, the small and highly heterogeneous patient cohort, compounded by the lack of a matched control group, undermines the strength of our findings, despite the observed trend toward improved outcomes with CGP. Additionally, the manual data collection process limits scalability. The specific post-CGP therapeutic agents were not collected (alteration for decision and given treatment modality were collected), which restricted our ability to perform certain analyses, such as on- versus off-label comparisons. Finally, our analysis of treatment line length is limited, as it does not capture true PFS or account for the reasons for treatment discontinuation. Nevertheless, it is an often-used endpoint in real-world oncology analyses and has shown a correlation with PFS in clinical trials.22,32,37, 38, 39 However, in a real-world setting, the actual disease progression information is often less systematically documented.

Conclusion

Prospective use of CGP and biologically guided treatments have become more accessible with the emergence of the DRUP-like studies in the Nordic region and elsewhere in Europe.4,15 As one of the first pan-cancer CGP RWD studies in the Nordic region, our results provide a retrospective context from the pre-DRUP era for current prospective studies15,29,30,40 and align well with other reports in the European context. Given the significant increase in precision cancer therapeutics since our study’s enrollment ended in late 2020, continued real-world evaluation remains essential.41,42
