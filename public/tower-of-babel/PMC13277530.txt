Interpol review of forensic science management, 2022-2025
Authors: William P McAndrew, Paul J Speaker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper reviews and summarizes the forensic management literature from mid-2022 to mid-2025, covering laboratory strategic, tactical, and operational decision-making, benchmarking, quality assurance, human resource management, and other managerial impacts from technological developments.

Introduction

The 20th INTERPOL International Forensic Science Managers Symposium report for 2019-2022 highlighted the National Institute of Justice report on the status and needs of forensic science laboratories in the United States [1]. The European Network of Forensic Science Institutes (ENFSI) offered a similar perspective on the needs of the forensic science community moving forward (European Network of Forensic Science Institutes, 2022). Each report offered suggestions on how to maintain the path forward proscribed by the National Academy of Sciences [2] and the public funding and educational investment required to stay on that path.

Themes across research projects over the past three years reflect some issues that arose during the COVID-19 pandemic. Data trends in laboratory costs, productivity, and turnaround times reflect the supply chain interruptions and resulting inflation across the global economy. During this post-pandemic period, laboratories had to do more with fewer resources. Laboratory personnel had to deal with increased pressure to perform.

The research output over the past three years continues to refine that path forward with a focus on the research literature since the 20th INTERPOL International Forensic Science Managers Symposium report [3]. Much of that literature continued to focus on issues surrounding the maintenance of quality with research on quality systems, risk management, certification, and accreditation [4]. Additional attention was paid to workforce development in forensic sciences with a careful view of the stressors that were magnified during the pandemic. Manuscripts continue to explore issues regarding the most efficient use of limited resources, from developments in forensic intelligence to introspection of micro issues via frugal forensics.1 This includes considerations of the economic efficiency related to new technological developments and covers publications beyond the outlets highlighted by Jones [5].

Our review begins with the focus of several studies on quality management systems. The quality review proceeds with topics on accreditation, certification, and the role of oversight bodies. The quality topics then move to ethical considerations, bias, and risk management. Next, the review considers the personnel impacts from heightened job stressors. Developments in up-front forensics are discussed next and followed by creative managerial decisions via frugal forensics. The final three sections cover issues on the workforce, technology, and general developments that impact managerial decision-making.

Quality management systems, oversight, certification, and accreditation

Forensic science contributions to the justice system depend upon the reliability of analyses and the public trust in those analyses. Quality management systems (QMS), along with certification and accreditation, help build trust [6,7]. A strong QMS supports forensic science services through competence and impartiality, which increases confidence in the justice system (European Network of Forensic Science Institutes [[8], [9], [10]]. International efforts, like the Sydney Declaration for instance, advocate for establishing core QMS principles for forensic practice to foster a global culture of quality [[11], [12], [13]].

Quality management systems

Amongst the many publications that contribute to the literature on QMS are some broad overview manuals, reports, and publications. Browne et al. [14] offer suggestions for broad quality improvements for the interest of forensic science [8,9]. details what it means for quality in crime scene investigation (CSI) with characteristics of a QMS that are applicable to other areas of forensic investigation. The consideration of accreditation within CSI and the associated process of achieving accreditation are provided by Jones & Forry [10]. Two additional research outputs offer a broad consideration of the role of regulatory oversight in support of quality [15,16].

While these aforementioned publications offer a macro perspective on quality, other studies concentrate on more micro issues. These include examinations of justice system-wide quality enhancement [[17], [18], [19], [20]] and examinations of attributes within specific scientific disciplines [[21], [22], [23]].

Chu [15] describes a QMS as a formal framework that documents the structure, processes, roles, responsibilities, and procedures that an organization undertakes to produce its work product, while preventing nonconformities. The goal is to provide consistent services that follow regulatory requirements to the satisfaction of stakeholders. In the case of forensic sciences, quality is the hallmark of a successful enterprise [8,9,15].

Jones & Forry [10] outline characteristics that are key features of a QMS. These characteristics include the documentation of policy and procedures. A QMS manual describes the overall policy and objectives with details on the who, what, where, and when work processes are to be performed. A QMS emphasizes confidentiality and impartiality [7,10]. It embraces continuous improvement and offers continual feedback for managerial decisions on the efficient use of limited resources [[8], [9], [10]].

Key features of a QMS include effective risk management with proactive systems to alert management of quality issues [7,13]. Plans for maintaining and monitoring QMS include internal audits, management reviews, quality assurance programs, and proficiency testing [8,9]. Quality assurance programs work in conjunction with the realities of laboratory funding, with quality assurance a key component of capital investment decisions [24].

Consider the internal resources necessary to maintain a QMS. How much staffing is necessary to maintain the desired level of quality? How should human resources be deployed? Some initial inquiries into these and other questions were posed by the Association of Forensic Quality Assurance Managers (AFQAM), and the initial questions on quality management staffing were posed to participants in Project FORESIGHT [25]. In a voluntary survey, Project FORESIGHT participants responded to a series of questions on their allocation of personnel and associated duties for quality assurance. This convenience sampling provided a very interesting result that the average laboratory staffing included approximately 5.4% of the full-time equivalent employees (FTE) in quality roles, with only modest variability [26]. Future studies will address the connection between quality assurance staffing and laboratory performance.

Accreditation

The role of accreditation is discussed in several manuscripts. Formal recognition by an independent third party via an accreditation standard for a forensic service provider is the key external mechanism for monitoring compliance with quality standards [10,15,27]. The external validation provides additional support that the laboratory consistently provides reliable analysis in support of the justice system.

Specific standards developed by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and jointly by ISO and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) are addressed by various authors. While not an accreditation standard for technical competence, ISO 9001 specifies general requirements for a QMS and focuses on the effectiveness of a QMS in addressing customer requirements [[8], [9], [10]]. The two standards, ISO/IEC 17020 and ISO/IEC 17025, represent the key quality standards for forensic laboratories [8,9,[28], [29], [30], [31], [32]].

The accreditation standards offer several quality benefits, including formal independent support of quality, increased public confidence in the laboratory, safety and risk mitigation, and the facilitation of international standardization of methods [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. These standards should also lead to sustained process optimization and development, while reducing nonconformities [6,8,9].

Gestring [33] describes the process involved with achieving accreditation, from the internal commitment by the forensic laboratory management to the development of processes to proficiency testing. The accreditation process continues with both internal and external audits [6,33]. Beyond application, the laboratory must provide support to maintain accreditation [[8], [9], [10],33].

Despite the positive aspects of accreditation’s role in a QMS, Amoako & McCartney [28] emphasize potential limitations of accreditation as a guarantee of quality. Although their focus is on England and Wales, their caution can be applied globally. Does the oversight body have enough statutory authority to ensure standards? Can the forensic science provider afford to uphold those standards? Accreditation mainly addresses the symptoms of quality issues through non-compliance, rather than the root causes like limited budgets. Ross & Neuteboom [34] address limitations and provide some caution with sole reliance on accreditation as an assurance of quality. Additionally, as Chu [15] notes, accreditation is not a cure-all and does not ensure mistakes will be eliminated or that laboratories will always follow best practices.

Heavey et al. [35] also caution about near-miss events, which do not get as much attention as observed non-conformities. For forensic labs, the general ISO/IEC standards, designed for a wider audience, might lack the depth needed for forensic laboratories [7,36].

Certification

In addition to accreditation of the investigative unit or laboratory, individual analysts’ certification offers another part of a QMS [37]. Certification provides formal recognition of individual forensic science professionals and supports the efforts for standardization in a QMS [27,28,36]. There isn't a single universally accepted certification across all areas of expertise in forensic science [10,16]. Nonetheless, certification can provide a level of trust in the reliability of an examiner’s work.

Oversight

State forensic science commissions and regulators play a valuable role, in addition to accreditation and certification, to enhance reliability, quality, transparency, and trust in forensic science [15,16]. An examination of the workings of the Texas Forensic Science Commission (TFSC) shows that a commission can act more quickly and investigate disclosures more comprehensively than accreditation bodies [15]. The TFSC receives self-disclosures from laboratories regarding quality incidents impacting the integrity of forensic products or involving allegations of negligence or misconduct. The TFSC supplements accreditation bodies like the American National Standards Institute National Accreditation Board (ANAB) by asking key questions and encouraging further follow-up or retroactive case review. Overall, while accreditation provides a critical quality management function for forensic laboratories by setting a framework for documenting, reviewing, and responding to nonconformities, accreditation alone is insufficient for providing the oversight and accountability that a statutory state forensic science commission can offer [15].

Similarly, the United Kingdom’s Forensic Science Regulator (FSR) is charged with the task of ensuring that forensic science services meet scientific quality standards, primarily through adherence to its Code of Practice and Conduct [28]. The FSR Code mandates that forensic units inform the Regulator of non-conforming work that could lead to adverse public comment, be against public interest, or result in a miscarriage of justice [35]. In Africa, Zambia has established a National Forensic Authority with the regulatory power to enforce standards and guidelines [13].

Ropero-Miller & Jones [16] show that many state jurisdictions, in addition to Texas, have established forensic science oversight bodies. These regulatory bodies take various forms and have a wide variety of mandates. In general, they promote communication and collaboration, coordinate resources, seek laboratory improvements, expand accreditation, and investigate misconduct. This regulatory oversight offers independent reviews to help identify the source of problems. From these findings, the oversight commissions suggest recommendations for improvements.

Bias, ethics, and risk mitigation strategies

The National Academy of Science [2] report on strengthening forensic science emphasized the importance, but lack of research, in cognitive bias as a source for error found within the criminal justice system, in both its effects and potential mitigation strategies. Published over a decade and a half ago, the report has inspired an outpouring of research on where cognitive bias can be found in the forensic science landscape, its sources, the ethical imperative of addressing bias, and potential bias risk mitigation strategies. Given its weight, bias has always been included in previous editions of this review, and we continue to include it here.

Bias

Forensic science regularly factors into a decision on an individual’s guilt or innocence. Decisions live in the world of human reasoning and cognition, which, although powerful, are prone to systematic errors known as cognitive bias. Contrary to the storylines that television may implant in our collective consciousness, forensic science is indeed fallible [38]. Errors from cognitive bias are distinct from explicit discrimination, intentional misconduct, or incompetence resulting in random errors. Rather, cognitive biases are often unconscious yet predictable systematic deviations from rationality that even experts may be unaware of, creating a blind spot [38,39]. Some experts may dismiss the existence or the severity of bias within their sub-domain, even when bias is objectively measured and shared with them. This form of resistance to corrective information is known as cognitive impenetrability or, more informally, as tunnel vision [38]. Experts, even with advanced degrees, are not immune to how human minds evolved to process information, making recognition of cognitive bias not only at the systemic level but at the individual level of paramount importance [38,40,41]. This is why objective data-driven studies on bias are so important, so the forensic community can uncover, measure, and mitigate its effects.

Cognitive bias manifests and impacts forensic outcomes across any stage along the forensic investigative process, from evidence collection to expert testimony in a courtroom [38]. Broadly, the human mind evolved to see patterns that create casual stories, scripts, or schemas about typical situations to survive. If large cats with stripes have been found to be dangerous in previous human interactions, humans may sense danger momentarily when they see stripes regardless of the existence of a cat, a phenomenon known as pareidolia. Moreover, when we receive several pieces of disjointed information, often our minds create coherent and consistent messages based on our life experiences, the media, and our environment in a top-down processing approach, even when investigations ought to start from individual pieces of information in a bottom-up approach [38]. Anchoring bias, an attachment to first impressions, is a cognitive shortcut that can influence subsequent choices [38,40]. For example, suppose a forensic pathologist determined a death to be a suicide, a more expected outcome than a homicide. Given this initial determination, investigators may collect less evidence and not preserve the crime scene under the belief that the first impression of a suicide was correct, limiting any potential future homicide investigation [42]. Expectations of base rates of certain population characteristic metrics such as suicide, gleaned from industry or the personal experience of a forensic science practitioner, may lead to an expectation that evidence conforms to these base rates, which biases forensic work [40].

These expectations on base rates (possibly stereotypes) can be further biased by demographic information. For instance, it is more likely that a black child’s death under the age of six is ruled as a homicide rather than an accident by forensic pathologists, and caregiver demographics can statistically influence cause of death determination as well [39]. Further complicating matters, the determination of demographics themselves can be biased by those recording them in medical examiner records, compared to those reported by next of kin in medicolegal system reports. Hispanic individuals are often classified as White in homicide investigations, resulting in an undercounting of Hispanic victims of violence and an overcount of white victimization. This, in turn, can lead to misperceptions of specific communities and incomplete understanding of public health data, leading to a disproportionate public health response [39].

Bias is evident across the wide spectrum of forensic science areas of investigation. Bitemark comparison (not to be conflated with forensic odontology) is one of the most heavily criticized forensic science techniques. Historically, bitemark comparison has been reliant on subjective determinations by independent consultants who operate outside the oversight and governing structures of public crime laboratories. The lack of oversight and quality control mechanisms has led to known errors and wrongful convictions [43]. Fire debris investigation is another domain vulnerable to cognitive bias, caused in part by professionals relying on outdated training and approaches [42]. Additionally, when evidence encompasses an entire building, the convenience of bringing an analyst physically to the crime scene may outweigh the potentially biasing contextual information introduced to the analyst, such as observing bloodstains, interviewing witnesses, etc. [38,42]. Other forensic disciplines, such as latent fingerprint identification, forensic pathology, and hair microscopy, are also susceptible to task-irrelevant and contextual information [38,41,42]. For example, without blind verification, an analyst may unknowingly echo the erroneous findings of previous analysts, as in the misidentified fingerprints of Brandon Mayfield in the 2003 Madrid train bombing case [41,42]. In the case of hair microscopy, modern DNA techniques have alleviated some concerns as hair comparisons have become relegated to a screening tool [42]. Yet modern DNA techniques, often portrayed in the media as infallible, can still have their conclusions subject to misinterpretation and error. Confirmation bias, laboratory mismanagement, sample mix-ups, erroneously uploaded blind case DNA samples to the Combined DNA Index System (CODIS), or administrative mistakes such as a misplaced report can lead to error and potential bias [41,42,44].

There are several common sources of bias in forensic science. Contextual information, or case-specific information, can be either task-relevant or task-irrelevant depending upon what forensic analysis is conducted, and relevance may change depending upon the stage in the investigative process. A suspect’s prior criminal history, race/ethnicity, confessions, media reports, content of message during handwriting analysis, knowledge of who hired the analyst, information revealed on package labeling, etc. as examples should likely be shielded from analysts, as it can bias their interpretations of evidence [38,40,41,44,45]. Even if analysts are aware of the biasing effects of certain information, it can be difficult to forget what has been seen or heard, and its influence will persist in the subconscious [40]. Organizational or institutional environments comprised of cultures, procedural processes, and external pressures can also have a biasing influence on an analyst [38,41]. One expression of this is the allegiance effect bias, where a forensic expert favors the side that they work for [40,41]. For example, an analyst who works within a police department may find results more consistent with the narratives proposed by the police [38]. An analyst may be more likely to confirm the results of a friend or superior within a laboratory when not following blind verification procedures [38]. Additional methodological shortcomings, such as a lack of evidence, line-ups or fillers and decoys, as well as prejudiced messaging on inconclusive findings, can influence legal stakeholders and contribute to errors [38,40,42,44]. Finally, personal factors such as an analyst’s beliefs and life experiences, particularly related to crime victimization, can shape their approach and interpretation of evidence [38,40].

The consequences of cognitive bias in forensic science and the potential subsequent errors described are profound, often contributing to wrongful convictions. Not only may innocent victims be found guilty, but the true perpetrators may remain undetected in society and continue to offend [38,39,42,43]. According to the National Registry of Exonerations [46], since 1989 there have been 3698 exonerations, with 1060 having false or misleading evidence as a contributing factor. Even if bias does not create errors in one step along the forensic science workflow, it can lead to downstream problems [38]. Bias cascades or trickle-down bias can occur when a bias in one earlier stage of forensic science spreads throughout a case as it moves through the system. The original bias can compound, leading to inaccurate findings and distorted case outcomes [39,42].

Ethics

Understanding the cause and sources of cognitive bias, recognizing it as a problem, and proactively managing its effects is an ethical imperative for forensic sciences to retain public trust in its reliability [43]. Regardless of the conscious or subconscious personal thoughts of a forensic expert, they must communicate impartially with their objective findings, specifically in their area of expertise. Communication, both orally in the courtroom and in written reports should include messaging on the limits of testing procedures and their accuracy. That way, non-expert stakeholders may have sufficient understanding for appropriate decision making, and forensic results are not inappropriately overvalued by the criminal justice system. Failure to do so may lead to miscarriages of justice, including wrongful convictions [42,47]. By embracing continuous improvement in the reliability of forensic science and how it is communicated, credibility and public trust in the industry may be improved [48,49].

Mandatory and continuous training in ethics and cognitive bias is one step towards improving the reliability and trustworthiness of forensic evidence within the criminal justice system [40,48]. Forensic science training should extend beyond discipline-specific methods to skill in communication with criminal justice stakeholders [47]. Unfortunately, this training is lacking both in a broad foundational understanding of cognitive bias and in ways to mitigate its impacts. [40]. Effective and new scientific standards can only be useful if both new and seasoned practitioners go through retraining and regular refresher courses. Furthermore, some forensic experts disregard new scientific knowledge and standards and are under the illusion that their current methods are acceptable [40,42]. Indeed, there is ample evidence that individuals with advanced degrees are more susceptible to cognitive bias, although the size of its measured effects in comparison to those with bachelor’s degrees varies based on the forensic science areas of investigation [42].

Cultivating a culture of transparency, where a shift from traditional practices towards one of open communication and humble acknowledgement of the limits of science as they exist can also improve reliability and trust [48]. When errors do occur, they ought to be discussed as learning opportunities rather than suggesting they do not exist [48]. Cutting-edge techniques, such as DNA technology, have improved relative to older techniques, such as serology or hair comparison, yet they are still imperfect. Historically, some purported cutting-edge techniques (bitemark comparison, early DNA testing, etc.) have lacked scientific validity, and contained challenges and nuances that were not always properly communicated. This further highlights the need for continuous improvement, transparency, and interdisciplinary communication both within forensic science and broadly [42].

Risk management

Given the significant impact of cognitive bias and error on forensic science and society, developing and implementing a comprehensive risk analysis and mitigation strategy is essential for improving accuracy and strengthening public trust in the criminal justice system [42,48,50]. Error is likely unavoidable in complex systems such as forensic science. Therefore, fostering a positive error management culture where wrongful convictions are treated as sentinel events for learning will help the industry understand and proactively manage sources of error [42]. Cognitive bias and error are multidimensional, spanning forensic domains, stages along the forensic science workflow, and extend into the criminal justice system broadly. Addressing this complexity requires detailed studies of the types and sources of error to develop both targeted and comprehensive strategies for mitigating error [[40], [41], [42], [43],48,51]. Quality management systems (QMS) coupled with objective standards such as ISO/IEC 17025 is one such error mitigation strategy. Accredited laboratories following ISO 17025 are required to have a QMS to detect and manage the inevitability of error [42,44,48,51].

With the sophistication and advancement of many forensic science techniques, proactive communication and transparent reporting standards, where critical information is disclosed for proper scrutiny of results, are needed. Key disclosures include what fundamental principles and methodologies are involved, validity and error rates, assumptions, known limitations, competency testing and quality assurance standards, and cognitive factors that may have influenced conclusions, such as disclosures on interactions with police. The Victoria Police Forensic Science Services department exemplifies transitioning to transparent reporting across 17 disciplines, providing necessary scientific context and ensuring consistency in information delivery [47]. Despite efforts, communicating forensic findings and the proper interpretation of error rates with audiences outside of the forensic science ecosystem remains challenging. Given the diverse range of understanding across jurors, attorneys, and judges, using mainstream scientific methods as a norm for communication has been encouraged when definitive empirical guidance is difficult [47]. One example of the importance of language and how stakeholders respond is with the word “inconclusive.” An inconclusive test result is not inculpatory or exculpatory evidence, yet through courtroom examination by the prosecution, this can be inappropriately implied, even if unintentionally. This highlights the need for further debate and developed standards on best practices when communicating forensic results [44]. It may also be prudent to gatekeep forensic genomic data to avoid non-expert third parties or even bad actors from misrepresenting or misinterpreting results outside of a forensic purpose [50].

The adoption of structured information management systems, such as linear sequential unmasking (LSU) and blinding protocols, are error mitigation techniques used to limit contextual information. With LSU and its expanded version, LSU-Expanded (LSU-E), a procedural framework is used that sequences when information is released to analysts. This sequence is made as an a priori assumption on when information is relevant and when it is unlikely to contain biasing power [40,41]. Blinding procedures, such as blind verification where reviewing analysts are unaware of an initial examiner’s conclusions, is another strategy that can reduce conformation bias and provide a sample measurement of error [38,40,41]. Case managers and centralized case management systems, where the screening and filtering of information for its relevancy before it reaches an analyst are critical [38,40,41]. A standout example of robust blind testing is the Houston Forensic Science Center program, where the aim is to assess laboratory performance to reveal and mitigate any weaknesses [44].

Effective risk management extends well beyond cognitive bias and quality assurance programs and should account for other large-scale challenges that can affect business operations and reliability. External factors, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, impacted supply chains, local laboratory processes, and employees’ lives and morale. Widespread disruptions such as these demonstrated the need for creating operational plans to accommodate surges in demand as well. [51]. The modern world also presents technological risks, particularly for laboratories that operate in conjunction with critical infrastructures. From cybersecurity threats, skills gaps within complex domains, massive data velocity and volumes to contend with, and the need for interoperability of systems, other risk management tools are needed [52]. Within the context of genomic data, Budowle & Sanjantila [50] suggest several risk management protocols, including training, appropriate assignment of access rights, physical security of data storage, password protection, destruction of personal data, pseudonymization of personal data, the use of cryptographic techniques for data encryption, and database penetration audit tests. The importance of documented informed consent and data privacy practices was also explored in the literature [50,53], with electronic consent or e-consent exhibiting benefits for the forensic community beyond traditional paper-based approaches [50]. D’Anna et al. [54] further suggest the use of blockchain technology or non-fungible tokens to protect evidence chain of custody, where digital evidence integrity and authenticity can be verified. Patil et al. [55] reiterate the importance of preserving confidence in the chain of custody with digital evidence. Henriques et al. [52] proffered specific cybersecurity tools such as the Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) or Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) systems as ways to detect and manage threats, as well as outlining general ISO/IEC 270000 series or NIST standards. Ethics is a critical component of forensic science culture, yet unfortunately, there also still exist internal threats and “bad actors”, creating the need for forensic-ready risk management systems [56]. Traditional security measures are often insufficient given insiders’ privileged access to digital systems, so implementing software and procedures with a forensic-ready risk management design will improve a laboratory’s risk posture [57].

Since the NAS [2] report was released, outlining needed improvement in how cognitive bias and resulting error rates are managed, improvements have been made. Yet, several challenges and hurdles still exist in the implementation of error mitigation strategies. Investing in new technologies and implementing new quality management systems, such as those using blind verification, can be resource-intensive and expensive [58]. Without government or private sector investment, laboratory managers can struggle with best-case implementation of a QMS given available budgets [40,44]. Forensic science itself is a group of interconnected domains, which, when combined are themselves interconnected with the criminal justice system and society broadly. Evidence collection problems, communication errors, and regulatory burdens imposed externally on forensic science in this system can also undermine its effectiveness [42,44]. Thus, interdisciplinary cooperation in a system-wide approach is needed for effective bias mitigation [48]. The journey towards improvement of bias mitigation systems and enhancing ethical practices in forensic science is a continuous process. As science advances, the challenges faced will undoubtedly change as well. It is the culture of continuous improvement that must remain constant for forensic science to maintain public trust and continue to provide value to the criminal justice system [42].

Psychosocial impacts in forensic sciences

Working in forensic science is an occupational choice that is accompanied by potentially high stress levels for forensic analysts. The high-stress environment may easily impact the decision-making process of analysts, which can lead to additional stressors on the justice system [59]. Some of the potential stressors that examiners face include ambiguous decision thresholds, vicarious trauma, and organizational challenges [60]. These factors can influence thought processes and potentially result in errors or inconsistencies in analyses. While moderate stress can enhance performance through greater attentiveness, chronic stress can damage performance. Addressing psychosocial issues requires a variety of approaches, including fostering an environment of operational independence for laboratories, implementing checks on the system via risk management strategies, and embracing a work culture that supports analysts and de-stigmatizes mental health support.

A Brazilian study examined the impact of the forensic work environment and occupational factors on the mental health of forensic examiners [60]. Given the nature of forensic work, which involves constant exposure to death, violence, and human suffering, it creates an inherently highly stressful environment. Workers face ethical dilemmas, emotional burdens from victim stories, and difficulties separating professional and personal lives. Add to that stress, work overload due to staff shortages, long hours, and excessive demands, which contribute significantly to stress and burnout.

Busey et al. [59] emphasize that forensic science is a profession with immense responsibility and direct societal impact. The work conducted by forensic experts has a profound impact on society at large and a direct effect on victims, survivors, defendants, and their families. This underscores the critical need to understand and mitigate factors that could compromise the integrity and reliability of their work. The implementation of social policies focused on mental health within forensic institutions is crucial to address and mitigate the risks of occupational illness [60]. It is important for laboratories to provide psychological support, counseling, and regular mental health evaluations for forensic workers.

Almazrouei et al. [61] emphasize that the stress extends beyond the laboratory to the courtroom. The impact of this stressor is complex and influenced by several factors, including the nature of the decision, the work environment, and the inherent characteristics of the examiner. However, not all stress factors have a negative impact. Moderate levels of stress may improve performance.

Several factors are cited as key stressors in forensic science. These include exposure to trauma, ambiguous decision thresholds, workloads, organizational culture, lack of independence, and the stigma of mental health issues. Direct exposure to violent crime scenes and autopsies can lead to vicarious trauma, with symptoms mimicking Post Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), including fear, irritability, hypertension, helplessness, sleep difficulties, and troublesome images [59,62].

Many forensic disciplines, such as pattern comparison fields, have ambiguous thresholds for decisions [59]. Unlike toxicology with its statute-mandated thresholds or single-source DNA with a genetics-based foundation, pattern recognition lacks such firm footholds.

The work environment and culture can exacerbate stress. High workloads and work pace are factors that increase stress [63]. The real or perceived pressure from law enforcement on forensic laboratories is a significant contributor [59]. The Houston Forensic Science Center (HFSC) offers a model where true independence is a foundational principle [44]. Current forensic science cultures often treat errors as unacceptable, contrasting with risk management approaches seen in fields like aviation. This creates a stressful environment. A shift towards a risk management approach, categorizing risks by likelihood and severity, is suggested [59].

A significant barrier to forensic professionals seeking mental health treatment is the stigma attached to mental health and a fear of termination, particularly within law enforcement-associated units [64]. There's a perception that seeking help can lower resilience. While some newer, younger employees may have a different mentality, older investigators find it harder to accept the need for help [64]. The gender dynamic between the female-dominated forensic science versus the male-dominated sworn law enforcement is also considered a potential factor in varying stigma levels [64].

Further, women forensic scientists working in laboratories associated with law enforcement often experience harassment. While there are higher harassment levels for women in policing and Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) professions in general, a specific investigation into forensic scientists in law enforcement-associated labs is suggested [65]. There was no statistically significant difference in harassment levels for male or female targets, based on whether harassers were sworn or unsworn. However, there were disproportionately more male and sworn harassers compared to their representation, suggesting further investigation is warranted [65]. Higher authority harassers were associated with more person-related harassment.

Dealing directly with stressors offers some hope for improved work environments. Addressing these challenges requires systemic changes, including fostering laboratory independence, adopting robust risk management and blind testing protocols, and cultivating a supportive, de-stigmatized environment for mental well-being. Developing specific databases (e.g., FADAMA, Forensic Anthropology Data Bank) can facilitate sharing anonymized casework data, improving method accuracy assessment, and potentially reducing ambiguity in certain analyses [66]. Continuous monitoring of stress levels and promotion of compassion satisfaction are also vital for supporting the forensic workforce and ensuring the integrity of the criminal justice system. Various psychological tools are available to assess occupational stress, burnout, and emotional intelligence in forensic practitioners [59].

Forensic intelligence

Publications in forensic intelligence emphasize the underutilization of forensic science as an up-front benefit to investigations. Several studies over the most recent three years focused on the evolving role of forensic science in criminal justice, its organizational aspects, technological advancements, and the push for greater efficiency and intelligence integration. Wickenheiser & Knutson [67] emphasize that the need for speed in investigations via up-front forensics is a situation that has persisted and intensified over time.

Traditionally, forensic science has been a key factor in the administration of justice through the analysis of evidence to strengthen prosecutions (Police1, 2025). However, its value is increasingly recognized for broader societal benefits, including the assistance to investigations, crime prevention, and public safety [[68], [69], [70]]. There's a growing emphasis on front-end forensics, making use of forensic intelligence to provide early leads to investigators and prevent future crimes [[69], [70], [71]]. Up-front forensics involves the collection, processing, and analysis of forensic data to generate insights for decision-making and hopefully, crime prevention [71]. The effectiveness of forensic intelligence is enhanced through the integration of publicly available social data sources with proprietary internal large data sources to assist early laboratory analysis [69,70].

This proactive application of forensic science, particularly in intelligence gathering, demonstrates significant economic returns. Examples of testing previously unsubmitted sexual assault kits (SAKs) have shown a dramatic return on investment (ROI) by identifying serial offenders and preventing future crimes [[68], [69], [70]]. For newly collected SAKs, this ROI can be even higher [69,70]. Specific applications of forensic intelligence include Miami-Dade with the creation of forensic analyst positions, the Phoenix Police Department’s Crime Gun Intelligence Center (CGIC), and the Arkansas State Police Crime Laboratory Lean Six Sigma program’s identification of toxicological trends and the resulting public health applications to save lives [69,70]. Each sentinel impact extends the application of up-front forensics as an intelligence contribution towards additional cases [72].

Several other applications of forensic intelligence offer suggestions as to how the forensic community may expand contributions to stakeholders. Gibbon et al. [73] emphasize the use of cross-disciplinary teams that may offer improved approaches to finding answers for forensic investigations. These applications allow forensic science to increasingly employ interdisciplinary collaboration in cases of unidentified decedents. The South African example in forensic pathology offers a non-monetary, humanitarian-based return on investment using the skills of forensic pathologists, geneticists, anthropologists, and others [73].

Chornous & Leliuk [74] highlight the importance of all parts of the justice system to effectively bring up-front forensics into play. Using the Ukraine environment as an example, the legal framework permits many stakeholders to initiate a forensic examination. This includes the court, prosecutors, defense attorneys, and investigators to develop intelligence early in potential criminal proceedings.

Some of the manuscripts on forensic intelligence concentrated on specific areas of investigation. The advancement of forensic science embraces new technologies and employs new methodologies to handle increasing data complexity. Dhulawagol et al. [75] highlight the use of blockchain technology as a means to handle increasing needs for secure management of evidence as part of the large databases at the heart of intelligence development. Dunsin et al. [76] emphasize the development of neural networks to identify linkages in the development of intelligence. In the area of forensic ontology, the development of knowledge management systems may provide a structured framework to integrate diverse data sources (e.g., biological samples, digital evidence, witness testimonies) into a unified platform, facilitating intelligence development [77].

Forensic science is undergoing a significant transformation, moving beyond its traditional role as a confirmatory tool to becoming a probative, intelligence-driven discipline. This evolution is supported by economic justifications demonstrating high ROI for some probative strategies, and by continuous technological advancements in areas like digital forensics (neural networks), secure data management (blockchain), and interdisciplinary approaches [78]. Effective integration of these advancements within robust legal and organizational frameworks, as seen in Ukraine, is crucial for enhancing the efficiency and impact of criminal investigations and streamlining the administration of justice.

Frugal forensics

Forensic science services worldwide face significant funding and infrastructure limitations, requiring focused efforts on cost-effectiveness and efficient resource use [69,79,80]. Monitoring performance and resource use through benchmarking can greatly help optimize the use of allocated budgets (ENFSI, 2003; [25,81]). Research on economic efficiency examines advancements and challenges within forensic science, emphasizing the need for quality and ethical considerations in addition to economic viability. Discussion centers on innovative techniques like digital simulations for training, machine learning for network forensics, and improved DNA analysis, alongside cloud-based investigations and the application of cost-conscious forensics for resource-constrained forensic laboratories. A recurring theme across these sources is the importance of standardization, robust infrastructure, international collaboration, and ethical frameworks to ensure the integrity and admissibility of evidence. This ultimately contributes to a more just and equitable criminal justice system. Economic benefits derive from investing in forensic capabilities and from addressing issues such as case backlogs and supply chain disruptions.

Frugal Forensics is the term used to describe the development of a cost-effective approach to the strategic provision of forensic science services under extreme budget constraints [82]. This approach creates an economically driven forensic science service model that meets societal needs while prioritizing quality and safety. It is especially relevant for developing countries in the southern hemisphere, where jurisdictions face significant resource and technological challenges [79,80,82]. Frugal forensics involves a holistic assessment of jurisdictional vulnerabilities, with a focus on risk mitigation. This holistic emphasis promotes the use of simple, low-cost, environmentally-friendly, and easily accessible protocols [82].

The principles behind frugal forensics are based on six attributes: performance, accessibility, availability, cost, simplicity, and safety [82]. These attributes help evaluate different forensic methods to determine their suitability and sustainability. For instance, using reagents made from alternative, inexpensive solvents that are less vulnerable to supply chain disruptions can offer a more resilient and sustainable long-term solution in environments with tight budgets. This approach provides an ideal answer to the economic challenge of limited resources that still need to meet demand [79,80,82].

Forensic services worldwide face significant challenges related to funding and infrastructure. Smaller jurisdictions, or areas with scarce forensic resources, face complex choices that often involve trade-offs between cost, time, and data quality [79]. There is a common shared economic problem faced by forensic laboratories globally, where limited resources must be allocated without compromising the quality of results, despite increasing demand. Initial setup costs for a laboratory are high, as are the associated expenses surrounding accreditation, staffing, education and training, oversight, and administration.

Economies of scale play a critical role, as larger laboratories can spread costs over higher caseloads, resulting in lower per-sample costs [83]. At the same time, smaller labs struggle with the same high setup and operational costs but process fewer cases. As a result, the smaller volume jurisdictions are unable to achieve perfect economies of scale [69]. A strong business case development is essential for identifying optimal strategies, including reviewing current workflows, future goals, and conducting a financial analysis to identify the most beneficial options [79]. This includes performing a cost-benefit analysis of alternatives and considering the return on investment (ROI) associated with the options [25,26].

When budgets are limited and resources are scarce, the choice of forensic science technologies and methodologies is critical for achieving cost-effectiveness, economic efficiency, and optimal use of resources [84]. Alternative strategies and technological considerations are highlighted across the research output reviewed. In a resource-constrained environment, less-expensive alternatives must be considered in lieu of more expensive technologies [85].

Bouzin et al. [86] highlight several examples of cost-effective choices of technologies and techniques when resource limitations are pronounced. For example, in the processing of latent fingerprints, alternative solvents may be less expensive and more widely available, and are nearly as sensitive as the more expensive alternatives. Simple and cost-effective methods, such as heat presses, can be used for processing. For extreme resource limitations, frugal forensic approaches using natural products that are readily available offer viable alternatives, even if less effective in pure performance terms [82].

The analysis of digital evidence is another area where cost-effective alternatives are needed for underfunded jurisdictions. This includes the high cost of capital expenditures for new technologies, data acquisition, and the cost of storage [[87], [88], [89]]. Mazhar et al. [88] propose the use of third-party servers as a means to lower costs while maintaining quality of services. Deploying lightweight artificial intelligence devices in resource-constrained jurisdictions is suggested which can reduce power and memory usage, thus relieving storage limitations [89].

Wickenheiser et al. [90] present detailed guidelines for laboratories undertaking a forensic investigative genealogy (FIGG) program. The consideration of such a program demands that supporting policies follow forensic science best practices, which can be expensive.

De La Chica et al. [79] consider the adaptability of FIGG programs to smaller jurisdictions. For smaller jurisdictions, where economies of scale are less achievable, several alternatives are considered. Among the alternatives entertained is a preliminary analysis internally and outsourcing for a more detailed analysis. Also considered are relationships with university laboratories and Rapid DNA technology. The potential benefits of FIGG, countered by the severe budget restrictions of smaller jurisdictions, may make this technology prohibitive without some sharing via these alternatives [80].

Smaller jurisdictions often outsource evidence to large forensic laboratories in different countries, which can offer lower setup costs and the cost per sample. However, these cross-border relationships may suffer from a sense of less responsibility for the in-sourcing laboratory. An alternative to relying completely upon the out-of-country source would be to conduct preliminary analyses domestically, limiting outsourcing to the more complex tests [79]. This approach maintains more local control, and the sample triage prevents unnecessary and costly analysis of samples with no DNA. For smaller countries, existing laboratory facilities (e.g., hospitals) might be used as an additional cost-saving. Another cost-saving approach is using non-accredited university laboratories for screening. This offers benefits including greater flexibility, faster turnaround times, and increased control over sample prioritization. Rapid DNA technology can quicken DNA analysis produces producing results within hours with minimally trained personnel. Its application in disaster victim identification has great value via timely analysis.

Frugal forensics benefits from the research and development efforts of the more industrialized countries. Developing faster, less expensive technologies provides the methodologies that smaller jurisdictions with fewer resources can most effectively offer forensic science services [80]. Standardized tools and techniques can make investigations more efficient and cost-effective while decreasing turnaround time [30,87,91,92]. Partnerships between developed and less-developed countries are critical for the enhancement of forensic science in developing countries [93].

One example of the research and development contributions from more industrialized countries to smaller countries is the work of the U.S. National Institute of Justice’s Forensic Laboratory Needs Technology Working Group (FLN-TWG). Among the projects undertaken by FLN-TWG was the production of a report on the current and future generation laboratory information management system (LIMS) [94]. The detailed report includes a laboratory checklist of functionality in the design of an ideal LIMS.

Benchmarking reports offer insight into what is possible across the size range of jurisdictions, including these smaller countries (Speaker, 2003; Speaker, 2004; Speaker, 2005). These annual FORESIGHT reports offer details on cost-effective delivery of forensic science services by area of investigation and include caseload-specific efficiency levels for expected costs and corresponding productivity. Beyond the economic performance metrics, shared data on reproducible results enables smaller laboratories to more quickly and economically take advantage of new knowledge [95].

The benchmarking studies provide insight into trends in the management of forensic science laboratories, including the post-COVID-19 impacts ([96]; Speaker, 2003; Speaker, 2004b; Speaker, 2005). The pre- and post-COVID-19 trends suggest that the demand for forensic science services is growing faster than productivity growth and efficiencies can respond. Requests for service outpaced resources, and the responses to frugal forensic solutions are apparent to all jurisdictions. This growth in demand for services extends to coroners and medical examiners [26,97].

Workforce developments

Impacts on the forensic science workforce

Despite the attention that media and television shows such as the CSI: Crime Scene Investigation franchise have given to forensic science and its potentially exciting career opportunities [98], there is evidence that its effect on the number of applicants for forensic positions is in decline [99]. While a four percent decline in law enforcement employment was observed during the COVID-19 pandemic across the U.S., there are many factors at work predating the pandemic [99]. Salary competition with the private sector, lengthy and costly background checks for both full-time positions and internships, as well as the substantial training costs laboratories face when investing in new recruits, have all contributed to barriers in growing laboratory employment [98,99]. Further complicating matters, the breadth and depth of forensic science sub-disciplines and a lack of standardized job descriptions and validation instruments across laboratories to determine proper selection of recruits has led to suboptimal hiring practices and resultantly higher employee turnover [98].

Adopting principles from industrial organization (I/O) psychology can improve workforce selection and retention. By robustly analyzing job positions that look at the full range of tasks involved, as well as their frequency, importance, and context in which tasks are performed, better position descriptions that include the relevant knowledge, skills, and abilities needed for success can be written. Ideally an I/O psychologist or human resource expert can guide the creation of these job descriptions, which can guide both the recruiter and applicant. These job descriptions can aid in the development of interview questions, tests, and exams, as well as validation studies that use the gathered information to determine what applicant characteristics lead to higher performance and retention levels [98].

Effective workforce development can also improve recruitment and retention. It is multifaceted and requires recruitment initiatives that incorporate early engagement, feedback on the hiring process, and public outreach. Wickenheiser et al. [99] suggest that by engaging with students in high school and college early in their academic career, misconceptions about the practice of forensic science can be corrected, so a realistic expectation about average workdays in the field can be formed. This, in turn, may reduce disappointment and turnover if students’ image of a forensic science career is aligned with industry reality rather than popular television shows. Working with human resources to improve communication with applicants during the recruitment process, especially during lengthy background checks, could also maintain the applicant pool and avoid rescinded applications. Human resources can consider sending out anonymous surveys to determine the primary reasons why applicants prematurely drop out and also create mailing lists for newly opened positions directed at prospective employees [99]. To engage the public and cast a wide and diverse recruitment net, uncommon strategies such as the New York State Police’s 2023 participation in the New York State Fair could also be considered in workforce development initiatives [99].

Training and education

With the evolving landscape of forensic science, knowledge gaps have developed, necessitating enhanced training and education to better align with the practical needs of today’s criminal justice system [[98], [99], [100]]. Forensic anthropology, for example, lacks coursework dedicated to the potential demographic differences in human skeletal remains, delegating this to a continuing education topic only [101]. The fast-paced changes seen in digital forensics have also created a disconnect between practitioners and researchers in the field where training cases are not aligned with real investigation practices [100]. Even among FEPAC-accredited undergraduate programs, there still exists a gap between the curriculum deployed and the reality of working in a forensic laboratory [99].

To bridge the gap between cutting-edge research and academic programs, curriculum development and pedagogical innovations are actively being explored. Experiential learning or real-world service projects, hands-on analysis of digital or physical specimens, and increasing the realism of student case studies of cybercrime have been suggested [100,101]. The New York State Police Crime Laboratory System has created the Forensic Investigative Curriculum (FIC) School, where local colleges can host events that directly fill the gap between academia and actual forensic laboratories. Through presentations, mentor mixer sessions, question and answer sessions, and demonstrations, the program goal is to improve the pipeline of strong and qualified future forensic scientists within a welcoming and supportive environment [99].

Internships are perhaps one of the best bridges between academia and professional practice, where students gain real-world experience, and laboratories can potentially benefit from additional labor hours. Challenges exist, including taking a scientist away from their primary duties to work with a student who may not create any immediate benefit for the laboratory. Background checks for students also can be time-consuming and costly and may even take longer than the internship itself, making it impractical for many students. Funding for internships is another potential hurdle that can be hard to overcome, especially if the students do not immediately add net value to the laboratory. One proposal to overcome this challenge is the concept of a remote internship. This type of internship involves a partner university with school-owned equipment and instrumentation, where students are guided by a university professor on a research topic that aligns with laboratory priorities. This reduces laboratory strain, while still providing student experiences and potential valuable information that laboratories can independently confirm with or without students on the laboratory’s site [99].

Technology and digitalization within forensic science management

The adoption of new technology and digital practices has had significant impacts on the forensic science industry, profoundly impacting the management of forensic science laboratories. This section details how these advancements can be leveraged as an opportunity for increased efficiency, as well as the challenges they create, such as the maintenance of evidence integrity and its security.

Advancements in blockchain technology, the Internet of Things (IOT), cloud computing, and artificial intelligence and machine learning have and will continue to present opportunities to improve the way the forensic industry is managed. How data is stored, how technologies interact with one another, and how the very decisions the forensic science industry is tasked to make are being altered in ways never seen before [87]. This highlights the importance of continuous improvement and evolution in the practice of managing forensic science laboratories in order to adapt to the dynamic world [102]. Camello et al. [103] offer a systematic approach to the consideration and implementation of new technologies. Weinstein et al. [104] examine the adoption of technological solutions from the perspective of medical examiners and coroners.

Blockchain

Blockchain technology operates as a decentralized, distributed, and immutable ledger that can record information and transactions securely, creating a foundation of transparency [105]. This transparency and immutability offer forensic science a secure method for recording the flow of digital evidence and maintaining chain of custody, ensuring evidence is legally admissible in court [105,106]. New tailor-made and private blockchain solutions for digital forensics, such as Forensiblock, have been developed, improving on the security of previous public blockchains that lacked privacy and did not restrict access [105]. With Forensiblock, investigative steps can be automated, access can be controlled based on roles or with staged authorization set by a case manager, and evidence can be tracked, all in a way that is intended to maintain quality standards and pass any audits [105]. Since blockchain technologies are often unalterable and public, as in the case of many cryptocurrencies, it can also be used to investigate financial crimes by tracking money origins and illegal behavior. In a similar fashion, blockchain can be used to track shipments of goods, verify their origin, and help in verifying their authenticity [107]. The Federal Bureau of Investigation’s National Cryptocurrency Enforcement Team (NCET) was developed to help address the growing impact of crimes using blockchain technology, yet many laboratories and countries lack the infrastructure, personnel, and resources to similarly respond to digital crimes [107].

Internet of Things

From smart watches to doorbell cameras, cell phones to your smart refrigerator, the internet is everywhere and across billions of connected devices not traditionally thought of as computers. This is known as the Internet of Things (IoT) [87,108,109]. The quality of life and efficiency benefits of this networking of information are vast, extending beyond consumer applications and include industrial, medical, and military applications [87,109]. Within forensic science, IoT and the data it collects provide valuable information and resources for investigations, including audio or visual evidence [110,111], remote access to capabilities [91], or automation of investigation processes (i.e., ambient intelligence) [112,113]. IoT’s rapid introduction has created significant challenges for digital forensics. Massive amounts of data are being created across billions of heterogeneous devices with varying operating systems, sensors, protocols, and data formats, which challenges standard investigation procedures [87,91,108,109,114,115]. Lack of device and data format homogeneity has also created privacy and security concerns regarding sensitive information that is stored and transmitted across IoT devices. The distributed nature of IoT devices, which can be connected to multiple networks, shared servers, and potentially across multiple geographic regions, can also create tracking and jurisdictional problems for investigators, especially for cross-border international crime [87,91,116].

Alam & Kabir [87] offer several suggestions for a future direction in IoT forensics. Developing application-specific investigation models (ASIM) created for specific systems, for example, smart home crime scenarios, provides a step-by-step investigative process for identifying, collecting, interpreting, presenting, and preserving evidence. Integration of artificial intelligence and machine learning, synchronization of time across devices to develop accurate timelines, and a forensic readiness approach to device design to proactively facilitate future investigations were also suggested as important to IoT forensics.

Cloud computing

Cloud computing is the process of delivering various computer services and resources over the internet [[116], [117], [118]]. By outsourcing some computer capabilities, forensic laboratories can take advantage of the scale of service providers to reduce cost, while adding flexibility since the nature of the cloud limits the need for physical hardware [91]. There are specific challenges that cloud computing presents to digital forensics. If laboratories outsource physical hardware investments and expert analysis to service providers through forensics as a service models [114,116], their access to digital evidence may be limited by their cloud service providers, especially in cases of cybersecurity attacks on those service providers [91]. In the case of digital evidence collection, data stored on the cloud rather than on physical devices creates other investigation issues. From cloud service providers privacy and security policies, evidence integrity and chain of custody maintenance when data is managed by a third party, or jurisdictional boundary constraints, how evidence is managed in the cloud has become key issue in cloud digital forensics [91,116,117].

Several structured frameworks for managing cloud digital forensics have been offered to address the unique needs within this environment beyond traditional digital forensic approaches. Forensics as a service, previously mentioned, is one concept where third-party service providers’ computation power, storage, and speed are likely much greater than individual laboratories, helping investigators deal with growing volumes of data and backlogs [91]. Cloud computing readiness is another approach, where laboratories can prepare in advance for problems that cloud computing may create. The Amazon Elastic Computer Cloud (EC2), in conjunction with the Amazon Elastic Block Storage (EBS) is one example where snapshots of users’ stored data can be viewed offline without affecting the original stored evidence [116]. Modularization of forensic science within cloud environments, where investigation steps are broken down into smaller component parts more easily managed, is another option [91,119]. Blockchain technology has been shown to help facilitate modularization within certain forensic contexts [112].

Artificial intelligence and machine learning

Previous editions of this triennial review touched on artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning; however, this current review is the first one written after the public launch of the now ubiquitous ChatGPT. AI’s ability to recognize patterns and automate routine tasks has shown to increase the efficiency and quality of results while also reducing human error. [87,119]. Coupled with other technologies such as wireless video sensors, AI has also been shown to proactively identify abnormal behavior, increasing the accuracy of video monitoring while reducing the need for human monitoring (Khan et al., 2022). Practitioners with formal digital evidence strategies [120], such as a User and Entity Behavior Analytics system, can now leverage data to see very subtle pattern anomalies that may have otherwise been overlooked by human analysis [87,113,119]. Machine learning’s ability to classify and cluster data can also automate the prioritization of data and evidence, allowing investigators to focus on the most relevant information for investigations [119].

The black box nature of AI has some questioning its transparency and has created the need for explainable AI (XAI) that is developed with input-output validation to retain evidence legitimacy and admissibility in court [91,119]. Other challenges exist, including dealing with massive amounts of data volume and fragmentation as well as the funding resources required to do so [91,109], integrating AI securely into preexisting forensic software tools not designed for AI and without application programing interfaces (APIs) [91,119], and training current forensic analysts how to use AI effectively while simultaneously not allowing AI use to cause atrophy of traditional human forensic analysis skills [119].

To effectively integrate AI and machine learning into forensic investigations and overcome these challenges, the forensic community will need to continue embracing adaptability. Developing standardized tools, techniques and terminology will be needed for a unified understanding of the forensic/AI landscape [87,91,119]. Modularization, potentially through on-device AI rather than cloud solutions, could also be considered to protect privacy and chain of custody of evidence [91,119]. Knowing when and where AI can automate, and when it can’t, will also be needed with an acceptance of dynamic change to facilitate a hybrid AI-assisted model that aids rather than replaces the human investigator [119].

Laboratory Information Management Systems

Laboratory Information Management Systems (LIMS), especially in an era of digital evidence, provides a structured framework to manage laboratory processes, procedures, and the pathway evidence takes through the investigative process. Sometimes realized as an Integrated Management System (IMS) which unifies disparate management systems and standards under a common framework, IMS work to achieve continuous improvement, reduce duplication across systems, and increase operational efficiency [102]. ISO Guide 72 and 83 can serve as principles to follow for writing quality management system standards capable of supporting internal and external audits, performance reviews, and helping to ensure regulatory compliance [102]. By taking a holistic approach that creates an overhead view of operations for management, costs are reduced and time is saved, helping reduce bureaucracy and waste [94,102] provide guidance along with a checklist of functions to meet present and future LIMS needs. Vernon et al. [121] consider the data management needs for coroners and medical examiners.

Proper structured document management and record keeping throughout an investigation is essential [102,105]. How files are named, determining who controls various versions of files, watermarking files to mark drafts, and defining retention periods all need to be considered [102]. A LIMS must also be designed with proper segregation of employee duties where employees have been screened and continuously trained for proper use of the system [102]. All of this takes time, computer power, and financial resources, yet by embracing quality LIMS and IMS systems, laboratories will have a powerful tool that addresses operational and regulatory challenges [102,105,120].

Digital evidence

The widespread adoption of technology across the human experience has made digital data an ever-present factor of modern life. As humans live more of their lives in a digital space, digital evidence has and will be increasingly relied upon in criminal investigations and legal proceedings [114,120]. Broad in scope, digital evidence sources can originate from traditional computers, cell phones, GPS systems, smart homes, and networked IOT devices previously discussed [87,105,109,110]. File types can also vary widely, from email, spreadsheets, video files, picture files, chat logs, etc. [110,114].

Given the unique nature of digital files, maintaining their credibility throughout an investigative process is needed [105]. Recording every digital transaction and maintaining proper and comprehensive storage history is critical to maintaining chain of custody and integrity of digital evidence, and ensuring evidence tampering is prevented [105]. A blockchain, as previously discussed, is one tamper-resistant innovation that can solve the complex challenges digital evidence creates, given its potentially distributed cloud environment and non-physical nature [105,106,116].

There exist numerous other unique challenges in the digital space, requiring complex solutions. The rise of encryption in criminal communication [91], difficulty in verifying the user who sent an email [114], verifying that files have not been distorted or misinterpreted [114], preserving online content as well as privacy especially in social media environments [112,114], and acquiring lawful access to distributed cloud systems [114] all require novel solutions that meet regulatory and quality assurance standards. A digital evidence strategy (DES) plan has been offered as one transparent solution framework, where a defensible and dynamic plan guides digital forensic practitioners in their investigations within a given timeframe. The DES plan outlines what actions to take, their scope, and justifications for actions while also incorporating legal and ethical considerations in a way that maximizes evidence recovery, reduces risk or error, and improves efficiency [120].

General developments

Foundational challenges

The forensic science community has long faced criticism and challenges to the way it operates, notably from the 2009 NAS Report that inspired an outpouring of research on the topic. Macroeconomic effects from the 2009 Financial Crisis and, more recently, COVID-19 have put increased financial strain on forensic laboratory budgets and their ability to properly staff and train employees, and acquire needed equipment, at levels far exceeding any broad inflationary pressures. This, in turn, has resulted in increased backlogs and the potential for deviations in quality and effectiveness of forensic science outputs across jurisdictions [19,20]. The opioid epidemic and growth of emerging drugs, coupled with sexual assault kit (SAK) backlogs, have put further strain on resources [3,19,20,72]. A historical lack of scientific rigor or shared culture, and a fragmented consistency across laboratories and sub-disciplines have also been pointed to as contributing factors of inefficiencies and regrettably wrongful convictions [12,19,20]. This has eroded public trust and the magic-like quality or the CSI Effect that the average citizen had in their mind regarding forensic science, necessitating greater transparency and openness on the accuracy and reliability of forensic science as a criminal justice tool [19,20]. Although there is nuance to the topic of public trust when looking across countries and sub-disciplines [122], the Gallup Organization polling Americans shows low and declining perceptions of the criminal justice system since 1993 [19,20].

Evolution of quality management and standards

The challenges the forensic science community face have served as significant catalysts for ongoing continuous improvement, improved transparency, and accountability across the discipline [3,12,19,20,23,49,95,103,123]. Much of the focus has been on internal policies and oversight, including management approaches, such as Total Quality Management and Lean Six Sigma [3], and the push for common standards often through accreditation [19,20]. These include general assessment standards as in ISO/IEC 17025 and ISO/IEC 17020, and more focused discipline-specific standards such as those provided by the Organization of Scientific Area Committees (OSAC) for Forensic Science, established by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) [5,19,20]. Technologies such as an electronic case management system is one approach shown to contribute to a quality management system, with an ability to enhance workflow efficiency [104,121]. Roux et al. [12], however, warn against an overreliance and excessive focus on operational standards, accreditation, and efficiency as they may distract from a more fundamental focus on mission relevant scientific research [4] and the creation of a culture of forensic science rather than laboratory bureaucracy.

Shifting operational models and service delivery

The traditional model of completing all forensic analysis of submitted evidence to courtroom ready levels of completeness in an era if increasingly scarce resources has become unsustainable [67]. To manage this increasing level of submitted evidence while also providing useful real-time information to investigators, new approaches using technology need to be adopted. The Lead-with-Speed model and Forensic Lead Programs (FLPs) are one suggested approach. In this model, a sort of short-order cook department within laboratories can offer a limited menu of forensic services that provide quick information to investigators that aid in criminal apprehension, while also reducing resource conflicts with other less time sensitive testing [67]. Junior forensic scientists managed by more experienced investigators can work within a department, making new employees quickly value-added labor in evidence screening, triage, and preparation [67]. One example of this is the improvement of the National Integrated Ballistic Information Network (NIBIN) that now provides quicker leads by training additional technicians and external partners to reduce turnaround times [67]. Portable field tests, such as rapid DNA and near-infrared technology drug tests, have also been shown to enable faster analysis in a lead-with-speed fashion [3]. Improved information sharing through streamlined forensic reporting (SFR) seen in England and Wales, which adopts standardized reporting strategies, can also improve reaction time of investigators. If time is not wasted looking through and understanding non-standardized reports, actionable information can be seen more easily and used by investigators more effectively [67]. More broadly, coordination and data sharing between laboratories, coroners’ offices, and other public safety stakeholders can provide the forensic intelligence that then reduces future resource needs for forensic analysis if crimes are proactively reduced [3,12,67].

In several publications, process improvement in a single area of investigation is highlighted. Gibb & Riemen [124] recommend improvements in practices for latent fingerprints. Elsewhere in DNA casework, backlog reduction prioritization is discussed [125].

More broadly, the impacts of climate change and the impact on general operations is a concern addressed by Thompson [126]. As scientists, taking an objective approach, coming up with a plan to address the impact of climate change on forensic science is imperative.

Research and development priorities

Despite the importance of forensic science as a critical aspect of the criminal justice system, funding for forensic science research and development remains limited and inconsistent, especially when compared to other science disciplines [19,20]. The primary forensic science research funding source in the United States is the Department of Justice (DOJ) through the National Institute of Justice (NIJ), yet when navigating to the NIJ website page covering funding opportunities, one is met with a message that “at this time, NIJ has removed all previously posted Notices of Funding Opportunity and associated webinars” (National Institute of Justice, 2025). As of this writing, there are no listed current or forthcoming funding opportunities from the NIJ. Larger and more consistent funding, with coordination across federal agencies including the NIJ, National Science Foundation, and NIST, will be needed to establish any meaningful research community that goes beyond proof -of-concept studies, and addresses the validity and reliability of test results as evidence [4,12,19,20,22].

The publish-or-perish general academic climate found within research universities has also impacted forensic science. Research has prioritized quantity and citation count, rather than quality and real-world impacts. The increasing number of journals has also resulted in increasingly specialized scholars operating in silos, further isolating knowledge and preventing communication of information that could be meaningfully useful [4]. Some in the literature have suggested the need for fewer but higher-quality forensic science journals aligned with practical impact, including making research outputs open source to help bridge the gap between research and forensic science laboratory operations [3,4].
