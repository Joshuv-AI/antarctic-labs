Effect of reciprocal Corona × Consort mating direction on hatchability and Corona/Consort phenotype distribution in Gloster canaries under ad libitum commercial feeding: a retrospective breeder registry analysis
Authors: Ercan Mevliyaoğulları
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCorona (crested) × Consort (non-crested) mating is a routine and biologically important pairing strategy in Gloster canary breeding. Although reciprocal use of the same phenotypes is common in practice, the extent to which mating direction is associated with hatchability and offspring Corona/Consort distribution has received limited formal evaluation under breeder conditions.MethodsA retrospective breeder-registry dataset was analysed without alteration of the recorded values. Records originated from a single hobby Gloster canary breeding facility in Çukurova, Adana Province, Türkiye. The dataset comprised 20 Gloster canary pairs/cages, including 10 Corona male × Consort female pairs and 10 Consort male × Corona female pairs. Four consecutive breeding cycles were recorded for each pair, giving 80 pair-cycle observations, 254 eggs set and 204 live offspring with phenotype records. Birds were maintained under routine management with ad libitum imported mixed canary seed and imported commercial egg food; a 14-h daily photoperiod was reported. Breeding cycle was significantly associated with hatchability (p < 0.001). Mean hatchability declined from 91.2% in cycle 1 to 67.5% in cycle 4, with a significant negative linear trend across cycles (slope: -8.3% points per cycle; p < 0.001). Offspring phenotype distribution was not significantly associated with mating direction (χ² = 3.373; df = 1; p = 0.066). The decline in hatchability across repeated breeding cycles appears more relevant for breeder management than the sex direction of the Corona and Consort parents. Because no molecular genotyping, feed analysis, body-condition scoring or calendar-year information was available, the findings should be interpreted as practical phenotypic evidence rather than genetic confirmation. Background

The Gloster Fancy canary is a selected type breed in which the Corona phenotype is characterized by a head crest, whereas the Consort phenotype lacks the crest but is selected for the same general type and quality standards [1–3]. In practical Gloster breeding, Corona × Consort pairing is used to maintain the crested phenotype while avoiding crested × crested matings, which are generally discouraged because of reduced viability associated with a double-factor crested condition described in classical breeder genetics [3–5].

The routine nature of Corona × Consort pairing does not eliminate a practical question faced by breeders: whether the direction of the reciprocal mating, Corona male × Consort female or Consort male × Corona female, is associated with hatching output or the proportion of Corona and Consort offspring. Classical expectations do not provide a strong reason to expect a parent-of-origin effect for the phenotypic segregation itself. However, reciprocal mating direction could still be relevant to breeders if it were associated with egg production, hatchability, parental behaviour or chick survival under field conditions.

Breeding output is also influenced by management conditions, particularly repeated breeding cycles, parental condition, photoperiod and diet. Canary breeding requires sufficient energy, protein, minerals and vitamins during egg formation, incubation and chick rearing [2]. Commercial mixed seed and egg-food products are widely used, but their nutrient composition can vary between products [6]. Canary breeder studies comparing commercial egg foods or describing first-clutch reproductive and early phenotypic outcomes under defined feeding regimens similarly illustrate why feeding conditions should be explicitly documented [7, 8]. Broader companion-animal and avian feed-technology studies further show that ingredient substitution, processing method, particle size, water-holding capacity and microstructure can influence physicochemical and in vitro digestibility characteristics of feed products [9, 10]. Therefore, documenting the feeding context is important when interpreting reproductive performance, even when feeding is not experimentally manipulated.

The present study used retrospective breeder records to evaluate reciprocal Corona × Consort mating direction under routine ad libitum commercial feeding. The objectives were to determine whether mating direction was associated with: (i) hatchability across four consecutive breeding cycles and (ii) Corona/Consort phenotype distribution among live offspring. The study was framed as a practical registry analysis; it was not designed to validate the molecular genotype underlying crest inheritance.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

This study was a retrospective observational analysis of breeder-registry records from routine Gloster canary production. No experimental intervention, invasive procedure, treatment allocation or change in feeding protocol was performed for this study. The source registry file was analysed as recorded, and no raw data values were changed. The records were obtained from Dr. Ercan Mevliyaoğulları’s hobby canary breeding facility located in the Çukurova district of Adana Province, Türkiye. All chicks whose records were included in the dataset were produced by Dr. Ercan Mevliyaoğulları, a member of the Seyhan Canary and Songbird Breeders Association (breeder ring no. 070), and all breeding was carried out in the same facility. Dr. Mevliyaoğulları is a Turkish Gloster canary champion and one of the founding members of the Turkish Gloster Club. The official 2008 Turkish National Championship results published by the Turkish Canary and Cage Birds Federation record the relevant Adana breeder entry with ring code 1.070.041.002 as first in the Gloster Corona Solid Colour/Crested class and as Best of Breed [11]. These details are reported to document the breeder context of the registry; the statistical unit of analysis remained the pair/cage and breeding cycle.

The dataset included 20 breeding pairs/cages, treated as 20 families for the purposes of analysis. Ten pairs were Corona male × Consort female (T×K in the breeder code), and ten pairs were Consort male × Corona female (K×T). Four consecutive breeding cycles were recorded for every pair, giving 80 pair-cycle observations. The registry contained 254 eggs set and 204 hatched/live offspring with recorded Corona/Consort phenotype classification. The analysed registry file did not include calendar-year identifiers or exact laying dates; therefore, year and season effects could not be modelled and are reported as study limitations.

Birds, management and feeding

The animal material consisted of Gloster Fancy canaries maintained under routine breeder conditions. Parental phenotypes were recorded as Corona (crested) or Consort (non-crested) according to external head-feather morphology and Gloster type definitions. The breeder registry described feeding as ad libitum imported mixed canary seed and imported commercial canary egg food, and reported a 14-h daily photoperiod. The registry file did not record feed brand, feed intake, chemical feed composition, mineral supplementation, water intake, the relative contribution of natural daylight and artificial lighting, temperature, humidity or body-condition measurements.

Variable definitions and registry coding

Egg number and hatched-chick number were recorded using four-digit codes in which each digit represented one breeding cycle. For example, the code “3342” indicated 3, 3, 4 and 2 eggs in breeding cycles 1, 2, 3 and 4, respectively. The same coding structure was used for hatched chicks. Total live offspring phenotype was recorded using a T/K code. In the breeder registry, T represented Corona/crested offspring and K represented Consort/non-crested offspring. For example, “4T3K” indicated seven live offspring, including four Corona/crested and three Consort/non-crested chicks. The registry file recorded live-offspring phenotype as T/K, but it did not record the exact age at phenotype scoring.

Statistical analysis

Because four consecutive breeding cycles were recorded for the same pairs, hatchability was analysed using a linear mixed-effects model rather than a conventional factorial ANOVA. Pair identity was included as a random intercept to account for repeated observations within pair. Mating direction, breeding cycle and their interaction were included as fixed effects in the full model. The interaction was evaluated first; because it was not significant, the main effects were interpreted using an additive mixed-effects model. Breeding cycle was also evaluated as an ordered linear term to test the direction of trend across cycles. Results are reported as descriptive means with standard deviations or standard errors where appropriate, together with model-based p-values.

The association between mating direction and offspring phenotype distribution was tested using Pearson’s chi-square test. The overall deviation from a 1:1 Corona/Consort distribution was examined using an exact binomial test. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. Given the retrospective design and small number of pairs, inferential results were interpreted cautiously.

Results

Dataset summary

The analysed dataset contained 20 breeding pairs/cages and 80 pair-cycle records. Across all pairs and cycles, 254 eggs were set and 204 chicks hatched, giving an aggregate hatchability of 80.3%. The Corona male × Consort female group produced 97 hatched/live chicks from 126 eggs, whereas the Consort male × Corona female group produced 107 hatched/live chicks from 128 eggs (Table 1).

Hatchability by mating direction and breeding cycle

Mean pair-cycle hatchability was 76.3 ± 25.3% in Corona male × Consort female matings and 83.1 ± 26.4% in Consort male × Corona female matings (Fig. 1). In the mixed-effects model, mating direction was not significantly associated with hatchability (p = 0.334). The mating direction × breeding cycle interaction was also not significant (p = 0.838), indicating that the pattern across cycles did not differ detectably between reciprocal mating groups.

Breeding cycle was significantly associated with hatchability in the additive mixed-effects model (p < 0.001). Mean hatchability decreased across consecutive cycles: 91.2% in cycle 1, 85.8% in cycle 2, 74.2% in cycle 3 and 67.5% in cycle 4 (Fig. 2). The ordered-cycle model showed a significant negative linear trend, corresponding to an estimated decline of 8.3% points per additional breeding cycle (p < 0.001).

Offspring Corona/Consort phenotype distribution

Phenotype distribution among live offspring is shown in Table 2; Fig. 3. In Corona male × Consort female matings, 56 of 97 offspring (57.7%) were Corona/crested and 41 (42.3%) were Consort/non-crested. In Consort male × Corona female matings, 48 of 107 offspring (44.9%) were Corona/crested and 59 (55.1%) were Consort/non-crested. Pearson’s chi-square test did not show a significant association between mating direction and offspring phenotype (χ² = 3.373; df = 1; p = 0.066). Overall, 104 of 204 offspring (51.0%) were Corona/crested and 100 (49.0%) were Consort/non-crested; this overall proportion did not differ significantly from 50:50 in the exact binomial test (p = 0.834).

Discussion

Principal findings

This retrospective breeder-registry analysis found no statistically significant evidence that reciprocal Corona × Consort mating direction affected hatchability or the Corona/Consort distribution of live offspring. The finding was consistent across the mixed-effects analysis of hatchability, which accounted for repeated cycles within the same pair, and the chi-square analysis of phenotype counts. In contrast, hatchability decreased across consecutive breeding cycles, suggesting that cycle progression and associated management or physiological factors may be more relevant to reproductive output than the sex direction of the Corona and Consort parents.

Interpretation of phenotype distribution

The overall Corona/Consort distribution was close to the theoretical 1:1 expectation often used by breeders when planning Corona × Consort matings. However, the present findings should be interpreted as phenotypic registry evidence rather than molecular proof of inheritance. The study did not include DNA sampling, marker testing or parentage/genotype confirmation. Therefore, statements in the revised manuscript have been limited to observed phenotype distribution and are described as compatible with classical expectations rather than as confirmation of a specific genetic mechanism.

Molecular studies in other avian crest phenotypes illustrate why this distinction is important. For example, crest in domestic chicken has been associated with regulatory variation involving the HOXC region [12]. The present study did not test whether comparable molecular mechanisms are relevant to Gloster canaries. Future prospective work combining breeder records with molecular genotyping would be required to evaluate genotype, penetrance, parent-of-origin effects and possible embryonic losses directly. Such work could use minimally invasive avian DNA sampling approaches, because DNA suitable for PCR-based bird genetic studies can be obtained from plucked feathers [13]. More broadly, avian parentage and relatedness studies show that molecular markers can affect inference about mating and genetic compatibility, underscoring why phenotypic registry data alone should not be treated as genotype confirmation [14].

Breeding cycle, management and welfare considerations

The most management-relevant pattern was the decline in hatchability from the first to the fourth breeding cycle. In the original analysis this was discussed in relation to parental fatigue, nutrition, micronutrients and calcium metabolism. These remain biologically plausible explanations, but the current dataset did not measure body condition, feed intake, feed nutrient composition, serum or egg micronutrient status, calcium balance, eggshell thickness or embryo mortality stage. Accordingly, the revised interpretation treats these mechanisms as hypotheses rather than demonstrated causes.

The decline observed in later cycles also has welfare implications. Continuing a pair through four breeding cycles may increase reproductive burden if parental body condition, feather quality, feeding behaviour, eggshell quality or chick-rearing performance deteriorates. Under practical breeding conditions, repeated cycles should therefore be accompanied by regular monitoring of adult condition and chick outcomes, and breeders should be prepared to stop breeding or provide recovery periods when declining performance or welfare concerns are observed. The present data support attention to conditioning and recovery management, but they do not define an optimal maximum number of cycles.

Role of feeding and photoperiod

All records were produced under ad libitum imported mixed seed and imported commercial egg-food feeding. This feeding regime should be understood as a study condition rather than an experimental treatment. Because the feeds were not sampled or chemically analysed during the recorded production period, the study cannot attribute differences in hatchability to specific nutrients. This is relevant because previous canary-feed analyses and broader pet-food/feed-processing studies show that feed products can differ in nutrient composition, physicochemical structure and digestibility-related characteristics [6, 9, 10]. Accordingly, future prospective Gloster studies should consider chemical analysis of the actual seed and egg-food products used during the breeding period and, where processed feed products are used, physicochemical characterization approaches similar to those applied in companion-animal and pigeon-feed research [9, 10]. Similarly, the registry reported a 14-h daily photoperiod, but did not record exact dates or the natural/artificial lighting composition. Day length, season and artificial-light management may influence breeding output and could not be separated from breeding-cycle effects in this retrospective dataset.

Limitations

The main limitations are the small sample size, retrospective design, single-facility data source, absence of calendar-year and exact seasonal information, absence of molecular genotyping, lack of feed composition analyses, absence of the exact age at phenotype scoring, and lack of direct measures of parent condition, egg quality, embryo mortality, chick sex or environmental variables. Because all birds were produced in one hobby Gloster canary breeding facility, external validity to other breeders, regions and management systems is limited. The pair was the statistical unit for repeated hatchability observations, but phenotype totals were available only as cumulative live-offspring counts over four cycles. Because externally visible avian traits, including eye/iris colour, may have complex mechanistic, genetic and evolutionary bases, future Gloster studies would benefit from standardized head-region photography and, where possible, independent phenotype scoring [15]. Health status was also not screened; subclinical infectious or parasitic status could not be excluded. Molecular epidemiologic work in pet birds has identified genetically diverse Cryptosporidium species/genotypes and potential public-health relevance, illustrating the value of veterinary health monitoring in future breeder-registry studies [16]. Therefore, conclusions should be limited to the practical association between recorded mating direction, hatchability and observed phenotype distribution in this registry. The study does not establish causality and should be considered hypothesis-generating for larger prospective studies.

Conclusions

Under the single-facility conditions recorded in this retrospective Gloster canary breeder registry, reciprocal Corona × Consort mating direction was not significantly associated with hatchability or the Corona/Consort distribution of live offspring. Hatchability declined across consecutive breeding cycles, and this pattern appears more relevant for practical breeder management than whether the Corona parent was male or female. Because the study was based on phenotypic records and did not include genotyping, feed analysis, body-condition measurements, veterinary health screening or calendar-year data, conclusions should be stated cautiously. Prospective studies with molecular confirmation, standardized phenotype documentation, and direct nutritional, environmental, health and welfare measurements are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying reduced hatchability in later cycles.
