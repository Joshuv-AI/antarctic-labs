 ABSTRACT
High-resolution population modeling is important for supporting effective decision-making across
diverse sectors. LandScan Mosaic generates population estimates at the level of individual buildings and
aggregates them to 3 arc second grids. This approach performs well in regions where building footprint
data are comprehensive and reliable. However, building stock datasets are still incomplete, sparse, or
entirely missing for large portions of the globe, and this situation presents a structural limitation for strictly
building-based population models. To address this research gap, we propose a computer vision–based
framework that employs Google Earth Engine satellite embeddings and UNet, enabling us to directly
impute grid-level population estimates in building data–deficient areas. The framework is applied to
Taiwan as a case study, and it demonstrates strong predictive performance, with an R2 of 0.89, root
mean square error of 18.70, and mean absolute error of 8.41, outperforming traditional machine learning
approaches. Notably, the proposed framework effectively addresses building false-positive errors inherent
in Global Human Settlement Layer data, correctly identifying uninhabited areas that are erroneously
classified as populated. The framework also offers significant advantages for global population mapping,
particularly in terms of scalability and temporal consistency, thereby extending the coverage and accuracy
of high-resolution population products in data-scarce regions worldwide. Urban planners, decision-makers,
and related stakeholders can obtain granular population distributions to support more accurate and targeted
infrastructure investment, service delivery, resource allocation, and risk assessment decisions.

 1. INTRODUCTION
High-resolution population modeling has historically been dominated by top-down approaches that infer
human presence indirectly from environmental and remotely sensed covariates. In these frameworks,
population is distributed based on relationships between census counts and proxies of human habitation,
such as built-environment indicators, nighttime lights, land cover, accessibility, and physical geography
(Lebakula et al. 2025). Although these models are effective for broad-scale mapping, they treat the human
environment implicitly, modeling population as a continuous surface conditioned on habitat suitability
rather than as an outcome of discrete, observable structures. Recent advancements in population modeling
have shifted this paradigm toward explicitly representing components of the human environment—most
notably, buildings—as the fundamental units by which population is estimated and spatially organized.
LandScan HD (Tuccillo, Moehl, Adams, et al. 2025) and its successor, LandScan Mosaic (Adams, Woody,
and Urban 2025), embody this shift by generating population estimates at the level of individual buildings
and aggregating them to 3 arc second grids. This building-centric formulation performs well in regions
where building footprint data are comprehensive and reliable.
However, building stock datasets are still incomplete, sparse, or entirely missing for large portions of the
globe, and this situation presents a structural limitation for strictly building-based population models.
In such regions, the absence of explicit building geometries prevents direct application of the LandScan
Mosaic methodology and introduces discontinuities in otherwise global high-resolution population
products. Previous studies have addressed this limitation by leveraging built-up surface proxies, such as
Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL)-derived built-up areas, to heuristically approximate the presence
of buildings and distribute population in areas where building footprints are unavailable. Although
this approach preserves spatial coverage, it departs from the inferential foundations of building-level
population modeling and relies on assumptions that are not explicitly tied to observed structures.
Consequently, data-scarce regions continue to represent a critical methodological gap in achieving globally
consistent, building-informed population estimates.

 1

To overcome this limitation, in this study we propose a framework that learns spatial patterns from
grid-level population estimates (3 arc second) and Google Earth Engine satellite embeddings in building
data–rich regions and then imputes these relationships to areas that lack building data to directly predict
grid-level population. Using this approach, we aim to extend LandScan Mosaic’s existing population
estimates continuously to building data–deficient regions.
Notably, this study employed a computer vision–based method (UNet) that implicitly considers spatial
relationships between pixels within the model structure, unlike traditional machine learning approaches.
Traditional models process each grid independently and thus fail to capture spatial dependencies among
adjacent regions. However, UNet’s convolutional operations and skip connections propagate spatial
patterns and context across near pixels at multiple spatial scales, enabling the model to effectively learn
these spatial dependencies (Jeong and Lee 2025; Ronneberger, Fischer, and Brox 2015). Consequently, our
approach generates spatially coherent imputation results that effectively bridge the data gap and enhance
the global coverage and accuracy of LandScan Mosaic.

 2. DATA AND METHOD
2.1 STUDY AREA AND DATA
Taiwan was selected as the study area because of its varied urban–rural composition as well as its diverse
urban densities. For ground truth data (target variable), we used LandScan Mosaic data, which provide 3
arc second grid-level population distributions. The noticeable characteristic of these data is to first estimate
building-level population and then generate grid-level population by spatial aggregation.
Predictors (input features) used in this study include 64-band satellite embeddings derived from the
Google Satellite Embedding V1 annual datasets (Google embeddings) from 2022 to 2024 (Brown
et al. 2025), which provide recent and comprehensive representations of earth’s surface characteristics.
These embeddings are generated by the AlphaEarth Foundations model, which integrates multisensor data,
including optical, radar, and LiDAR data, to provide semantically rich geospatial representations (Brown
et al. 2025). Notably, to enhance ensemble diversity, embeddings corresponding to the 25th, 50th, and
75th percentiles were downloaded separately and used. To address limitations in urban areas dominated
by high-rise buildings, where the Google embeddings lack elevation information and prediction accuracy
decreases, we integrated additional surface elevation data sourced from the Copernicus digital elevation
model (DEM)1 (European Space Agency 2023) and DEM data from the Shuttle Radar Topography
Mission global 30 m dataset (NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory 2013). Because the satellite datasets
had different resolutions, we matched their resolutions to the LandScan Mosaic 3 arc second grid.
Additionally, none, infinite, and negative values in the DEM and DSM datasets were replaced with 0 to
avoid invalid elevation data inputs in the model. Because the Google embeddings are already standardized,
the additional standardization was applied only to the DEM and DSM data to ensure consistency across the
input features.
A mask was created corresponding to regions where population presence was estimated using GHSL data.
Considering the modest uncertainties associated with GHSL data, we excluded the population estimates
derived from GHSL data for these regions and substituted estimates predicted from the combined raster
dataset, including the Google embeddings and topology data. These masked areas represent the regions
ultimately targeted for population imputation in our study.

2.2 METHOD
Figure 1 presents an overview of the framework for imputation developed in this study. This framework
employs multisource geospatial data and a computer vision model to systematically impute population
estimates in areas where GHSL-based predictions are uncertain. The framework is organized into two
main steps: (1) the generation of the training dataset and (2) the training and imputation process.
2.2.1 Creation of Training Set
Training data are constructed from multisource raster inputs, including LandScan Mosaic, the GHSL
mask, percentile-specific Google embeddings (25th, 50th, and 75th), digital elevation models (DEMs,
DSMs), and other relevant geospatial layers. Initially, valid pixels with population values greater than 0 are
filtered from the LandScan Mosaic raster mask, and pixels identified by the GHSL mask are subsequently
excluded. A random sample of 10,000 pixels is then selected from the remaining valid areas for patch
extraction. For each sampled pixel, square raster patches (64 × 64) are extracted across all feature layers.
This process yields a spatially aligned training dataset in which each sample consists of stacked input
feature patches and a corresponding ground truth patch from the population mosaic (see “Training Data
Generation” in Figure 1).

2.2.2 Training and Imputation
2.2.2.1 Introduction of Main Model
We employed the UNet architecture (Ronneberger, Fischer, and Brox 2015), a widely recognized deep
learning framework in computer vision known for its effectiveness in pixelwise prediction tasks. The
distinguishing feature of UNet lies in its symmetric encoder–decoder structure built on convolutional
neural networks (CNNs). In the encoder (contracting path), sequential convolutions coupled with max
pooling progressively reduce the spatial dimensions of the input, enabling the extraction of hierarchical
and multiscale regional features from the input array. These reduced representations, often called context
maps, retain essential spatial and attribute information. The decoder (expansive path) reconstructs the
output via upsampling and convolutions, gradually restoring the original spatial resolution. Furthermore,
skip connections are employed at each resolution stage to transfer high-resolution features from the
encoder directly to the decoder, helping to preserve fine-grained spatial details and contextual information
throughout the reconstruction process. This mechanism allows the model to combine global and localized
context, resulting in highly accurate predictions (Jeong and Lee 2025; Ronneberger, Fischer, and
Brox 2015). UNet has been widely adopted in domains such as medical imaging, remote sensing, and
population modeling for tasks that require pixel-level predictions (Jeong and Lee 2025; Pascarella
et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2022; Ronneberger, Fischer, and Brox 2015).
In addition, the UNet architecture employed in this study uses ResNet50, proposed by He et al. (2016),
as the encoder backbone instead of a standard CNN. ResNet50 enables efficient training of very deep
networks via its deep residual learning framework and skip connection mechanism. Although traditional
very deep learning models often experience degradation problems in which performance deteriorates
as depth increases, ResNet50 demonstrates improved performance with deeper network layers. This
characteristic allows our UNet model to effectively capture features ranging from low-level patterns to
high-level abstractions from the input three-dimensional array. The model was implemented using the
segmentation models PyTorch library (Iakubovskii 2019). We did not use pretrained weights for the
encoder; thus, we enabled the model to learn features specific to the population estimation task.
The model’s last step is to apply a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function to the final output layer
to ensure nonnegative predictions, because population counts cannot be negative.
The model accepts 66 input channels, including 64 from the Google embeddings and 2 from the elevation
datasets (DEM and DSM), and outputs a single channel representing predicted grid population.

2.2.2.2 Model Training
To accommodate ensemble prediction, the entire process of patch extraction and training data generation
was repeated for each of the Google embedding raster percentiles. Subsequently, three modified UNet
models were independently trained—one per embedding percentile—using the resampled and standardized
inputs.
In the training process, we employed Bayesian optimization (instead of a grid-search approach) and
k-fold cross-validation (k = 5) to prevent overfitting and to perform fine-tuning efficiently (Shahriari
et al. 2016; Frazier 2018). Because the grid-search approach requires the models to be trained for all
possible hyperparameter combinations, it is impractical. The search space included learning rate (range:
0.00001–0.001) and number of training epochs (range: 5–25). The model was trained using the Adam
optimizer with mean squared error (MSE) as the loss function.

 4

2.2.2.3 Prediction and Imputation
For entire regions predictions, each trained model was applied to the corresponding embedding dataset
using a sliding window technique to generate predicted population maps. Final model outputs were
combined and evaluated using standard metrics, including coefficient of determination (R2 ), root mean
square error, and mean absolute error. These evaluations were computed exclusively on the filtered valid
pixels (population > 0, excluding GHSL-masked areas) in the ground truth and their corresponding
locations in the ensemble predictions. Finally, areas where population presence was estimated by GHSL
were excluded and replaced via imputation with predictions from the ensemble; this approach enhances
the spatial consistency and reliability of gridded population estimations see “Overall flow” in Figure 1).
The overall process, including training, validation, and prediction, was performed using an NVIDIA A100
GPU.

 3. RESULTS
3.1 COMPARISON OF MODEL ARCHITECTURES
To determine the optimal modeling approach and target variable representation for population estimation,
we conducted a comprehensive comparison across two dimensions: (1) model architecture (the proposed
UNet ensemble-based framework versus traditional machine learning models—random forest (RF) and
extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)—and (2) target variable transformation (original, log-transformed,
and density-transformed counts).

3.1.1 Traditional Machine Learning Baseline
Traditional machine learning models (RF and XGBoost) require flattened feature vectors at the individual
pixel level, whereas UNet operates on multidimensional spatial arrays. Therefore, training datasets for
traditional models were constructed by randomly selecting 10,000 valid pixels; input features consisted of
Google embeddings and topological values at each selected pixel. Additionally, to enable the traditional
models to incorporate spatial context, we created an augmented feature set by appending the mean values
of each feature from neighboring pixels (based on queen contiguity) to the original pixel-level features.
This enabled us to evaluate whether traditional models can benefit from explicit spatial information and
compare their performance against UNet’s implicit spatial learning.
To ensure a fair comparison with the UNet ensemble-based approach, we applied the same modeling
pipeline to the traditional machine learning models: for each of the three Google embedding percentiles
(25th, 50th, 75th), separate training datasets were generated, and independent models were trained.
The final predictions were obtained by ensemble averaging across the three percentile-specific models,
following the UNet ensemble strategy. The key difference was in the input representation—traditional
models use pixel-level feature vectors (with or without neighborhood augmentation), whereas UNet
processes spatially structured multidimensional arrays that implicitly encode spatial relationships through
convolutional operations.
All traditional models (with or without neighborhood augmentation) were trained using Bayesian
optimization and k-fold cross-validation, following the same hyperparameter tuning strategy we used for
our framework. For each model configuration, we evaluated performance across all three target variable
transformations.

3.1.2 Performance Results
Table 1 presents a comprehensive comparison of all modeling approaches for the three target variable
transformations. The UNet ensemble substantially outperformed traditional models across all metrics
and transformations. For the density transformation, the UNet ensemble achieved an R2 of 0.89, RMSE
of 18.70, and MAE of 8.41, whereas RF and XGBoost achieved R2 values of only 0.37 and 0.39, RMSE
values of 43.32 and 42.37, and MAE values of 19.45 and 18.63, respectively.
Among target transformations, the log transformation yielded the poorest performance across all models.
Although the UNet ensemble only reached an R2 of 0.76, RMSE of 27.11, and MAE of 9.24, it still
substantially outperformed RF and XGBoost, which had R2 values from 0.19 to 0.26, RMSE values
from 46.32 to 49.60, and MAE values from 16.67 to 20.56. Incorporating neighborhood features into
the traditional models led to modest improvements across all metrics. For example, for XGBoost, MAE
decreased from 20.37 to 18.63 for original counts and from 21.04 to 18.63 for the density transformation.
However, the traditional models remained substantially inferior to the UNet ensemble, which achieved
MAE values of 8.43 and 8.41 for original counts and the density transformation, respectively.

 7

The superior performance of the UNet ensemble can be attributed to its ability to learn multiscale spatial
features through convolutional operations and skip connections, which implicitly encode neighborhood
relationships without requiring explicit feature engineering. In contrast, traditional models struggle to
capture complex spatial dependencies even when neighborhood features are manually incorporated.
Among the target variable transformations, density normalization consistently yielded the best performance
across all model types, likely because of its ability to standardize variation in grid sizes and reduce the
dynamic range of target values.

3.2 ADMINISTRATIVE-LEVEL PERFORMANCE EVALUATION
In the previous section, we determined that the proposed UNet-based framework and density transformation
for the target variable resulted in the best performance. To assess spatial variation in model performance,
we calculated error metrics for the first-level administrative divisions of Taiwan—Taiwan Province, Taipei
City, Tainan City, Taichung City, New Taipei City, Kaohsiung City, Fuchien Province.
Figure 2 shows performance metrics for each administrative division. Overall, the model achieved
consistently high performance across all regions. The R2 values ranged from 0.79 to 0.90, which
demonstrates robust spatial generalization. For most regions, the model achieved R2 values greater than
the overall mean (indicated by the dotted red line). The model showed particularly strong performance for
Kaohsiung City, New Taipei City, and Tainan City, with R2 values from approximately 0.88 to 0.90.
Metropolitan areas exhibited notable differences in error magnitudes. Taipei City and New Taipei City
had substantially higher RMSE and MAE values compared to other regions, likely reflecting the higher
population density and greater spatial heterogeneity characteristic of these metropolitan areas. In contrast,
Taiwan Province, Tainan City, and Kaohsiung City, in which urban and rural zones are mixed and
population density is moderate compared to metropolitan areas, had low RMSE and MAE values. This
pattern indicates that although the model is less accurate in extremely densely populated metropolitan
areas, it effectively captures population distributions in moderately urbanized areas and rural zones.
Although Fuchien Province had the lowest R2 value of 0.79, its RMSE and MAE values were less than the
regional mean. These results suggest that although the explained variance is lower, the absolute prediction
errors are still reasonable. The lower R2 is likely a result of the province’s smaller sample size and distinct
geographic characteristics compared to the main island regions. Overall, these results indicate that the
UNet-based framework generalizes well across diverse administrative regions with varying population
densities and urban characteristics.

3.3 FINAL PREDICTION
Using the trained UNet model with density-transformed target variables, we generated full-coverage
population predictions for the entire Taiwan study area. Figure 3 presents the predicted population density
distribution across Taiwan.
The raw predicted population distribution aligns reasonably well with the ground truth LandScan Mosaic
data. The predicted mean of 20.39 persons per grid with a standard deviation of 44.23 is comparable to the
ground truth mean of 22.41 persons per grid with a standard deviation of 55.28. However, the predicted
maximum value of 720.89 is significantly less than the ground truth maximum of 1,544.89.
This discrepancy is likely caused by methodological differences in post-processing. The LandScan Mosaic
normalizes grid predictions using the total known population counts from official statistics to ensure
that the sum of all predicted grids matches the actual population total. When the same normalization
procedure is applied to the UNet predictions, the statistical properties converge closely to the ground
truth: the normalized predictions yield a mean of 22.41, standard deviation of 53.30, and maximum
of 1,216.37—values that closely match the LandScan Mosaic values of 22.41, 55.28, and 1,544.89,
respectively.
An advantage of the ensemble approach employed in this study is its ability to quantify the uncertainty
associated with the predictions. As a measure of uncertainty, we calculated the coefficient of variation,
which is defined as the ratio of the standard deviation (σ) to the mean (µ) of the ensemble predictions for
each grid. Unlike standard deviation, which tends to scale with the mean population size, coefficient of
variation provides a standardized, relative measure of dispersion, enabling more meaningful comparisons
of uncertainty across areas with different population densities.
Figure 4 maps the spatial distribution of prediction uncertainty across Taiwan. The map reveals that
prediction uncertainty is generally low throughout the study area. In particular, urban centers and densely
populated regions exhibit very low coefficient of variation values, indicating high model confidence in
these areas. Although a few rural areas show higher levels of uncertainty, the overall results demonstrate
the stability and reliability of the ensemble predictions.

 4. DISCUSSION
4.1 ADDRESSING BUILDING FALSE POSITIVES VIA GOOGLE EMBEDDINGS
The most notable finding of this study is that Google embedding–based predictions effectively deal with
building false positive problems that appear in GHSL-based estimates. The case presented in Figure 5
clearly demonstrates this difference. In a region characterized by riverine sand deposits and bare ground
surfaces, GHSL-based estimates assigned nonzero population values to multiple grids due to the presence
of GHSL building footprints in these locations. In contrast, the Google embedding–based approach
correctly assigned zero population to the same locations, accurately identifying these areas as uninhabited.
This difference likely stems from the distinct characteristics of the two data sources. GHSL primarily
detects buildings based on multispectral imagery from Sentinel-2 and Landsat (Pesaresi et al. 2024).
A previous study discovered that GHSL building detection is affected by mixed pixel problems, in
which pixels containing signals from roads, bare ground, and buildings can lead to commission errors
(Liu et al. 2020). Consequently, the GHSL dataset contains false positive building footprints in these
nonbuilding areas, which subsequently leads to erroneous population assignments.
In contrast, the UNet model trained on Google embeddings data assigned no population to riverine sand
deposit areas in which GHSL building footprints led to erroneous population assignments; the UNet model
correctly identified these locations as uninhabited. These results indicate that Google embeddings encode
information that enables the model to avoid false positive population allocations in areas in which GHSL
building detection produces errors.
These findings have broader implications beyond population imputation. The building detection capability
of Google embeddings can serve as a foundation for developing building presence/absence models.
In particular, combining recently released GHSL data (Riffler et al. 2025) with Google embeddings
would make it possible to construct a hybrid building detection framework that simultaneously leverages
GHSL’s extensive spatiotemporal coverage and the high semantic discrimination capability of the Google
embeddings. Such an approach could provide a more reliable foundation for population estimation in
regions that lack building footprint data.

4.2 BUILDING FOOTPRINT–FREE POPULATION MODELING
The current LandScan Mosaic framework relies fundamentally on a building-centric approach, in which
building footprints serve as the primary spatial units for population estimation (Adams, Woody, and
Urban 2025). Individual buildings are assigned population values, which are subsequently aggregated
to grid-level estimates. Although this approach has proven effective in data-rich regions, it inherently
assumes the availability of comprehensive and up-to-date building footprint datasets. This study shows
that comparable population estimation accuracy can be achieved without building footprint data, providing
an alternative for population modeling.
The proposed building footprint–free approach provides significant advantages for global population
mapping, particularly in terms of scalability and temporal consistency. Unlike building footprint datasets,
which require substantial computational resources and time-intensive processing pipelines to generate
and update, data-driven surface representations are increasingly readily available as preprocessed global
products with standardized coverage. Furthermore, satellite embeddings offer scalable, temporally
consistent, and semantically rich inputs for population modeling. Therefore, embedding-based population
estimation enables a more resilient and adaptive framework for population modeling.

 13

4.3 LIMITATIONS
Although this study provides strong evidence for the applicability of embedded satellite imagery for
population estimation, several limitations remain. The building footprint–free approach may perform
differently across regions with distinct geographic or socioeconomic characteristics because the model’s
effectiveness depends on discernible surface features that correlate with human settlements. Moreover,
although this study demonstrates the potential of Google embeddings for population modeling, a reliance
on proprietary data products raises concerns regarding long-term data accessibility and reproducibility.

 14

 5. CONCLUSION
In this study, we developed a UNet-based framework that used Google Earth Engine satellite embeddings
to impute population estimates in regions in which there are quality issues with building footprint
data. Using high-capacity satellite embeddings, our model correctly distinguishes inhabited areas from
uninhabited areas, thereby avoiding false-positive population allocations.
Future research should aim to enhance the framework’s capability to capture vertical and temporal
dimensions of population distribution. The recent development of global building height datasets presents
an opportunity to replace the outdated DSM data used in this study with accurate, building-specific height
information. Integrating these datasets with satellite embeddings could substantially improve prediction
accuracy in high-rise dominated, dense urban cores. Additionally, future work could extend the framework
to generate temporally explicit population estimates, producing a time series of gridded population outputs
that captures year-to-year or even seasonal changes in human settlements. Such dynamic population
products would enable more detailed monitoring of urban growth, displacement, and other demographic
processes over time.

 6. OPERATIONAL IMPLEMENTATION
6.1 CURRENT PIPELINE
The current Google embedding imputation pipeline uses three UNet models trained on the 25th, 50th,
and 75th percentile embeddings; hyperparameters are optimized via Bayesian optimization and fivefold
cross-validation. Each model requires approximately 443.87 min for hyperparameter tuning on a single
NVIDIA A100 GPU. Once the optimal parameters are identified, model training takes approximately 20
min. Thus, the complete tuning and training process takes approximately 8 h per model. Because three
models are trained (using the the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentile embeddings), the full pipeline requires
approximately 24 h on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU.
