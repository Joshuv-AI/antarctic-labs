 ABSTRACT

The Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User Facility provides one of the world's largest
openly accessible repositories of atmospheric observations through the ARM Data Discovery
platform. Although the repository contains more than three decades of measurements collected
from permanent observatories, mobile facilities, aircraft campaigns, and field experiments,
identifying appropriate datasets can be challenging, particularly for new users unfamiliar with
ARM instrumentation and datastream organization. To improve data accessibility, the ARM Data
Center developed the ARM Data Advisor (ADA), a domain-specific LLM-powered conversational
scientific assistant designed to facilitate scientific data discovery, dataset interpretation, and user
guidance. This report evaluates ADA's performance as a domain-specific scientific assistant using
realistic atmospheric science workflows. The evaluation examines five key capabilities: data
retrieval and curation efficiency, hallucination resistance, scientific reasoning, response to
ambiguous queries, and conversational memory. Representative prompts were developed to
simulate typical interactions between researchers and the ARM Data Discovery platform, and
ADA's responses were assessed for retrieval efficiency, scientific accuracy, consistency, and
practical usefulness. In these representative tests, ADA reduced the complexity of discovering and
accessing ARM datasets by recommending appropriate datastreams, explaining instrumentation,
interpreting metadata, and assisting with data processing workflows. ADA also exhibits strong
domain knowledge of atmospheric science terminology and generally resists hallucination by
acknowledging unavailable datasets and requesting clarification when appropriate. Overall, the
results indicate that ADA represents a promising advancement in scientific data discovery within
the ARM User Facility and has considerable potential to improve researcher productivity,
particularly for new users and interdisciplinary scientists seeking efficient access to ARM
observations.

Introduction

The Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User Facility Data Center is one of the world's
most advanced repositories for atmospheric science data. Operated by Oak Ridge National
Laboratory for the U.S. Department of Energy Office of Science, it serves as far more than a
conventional data archive. The ARM Data Center is a comprehensive scientific data ecosystem
that acquires, processes, quality-controls, archives, distributes, and preserves atmospheric
observations for researchers worldwide¹. It manages observations collected from ARM's
permanent observatories, mobile facilities, aircraft campaigns, and special field experiments
conducted across diverse environments. Over the past three decades, the ARM User Facility has
significantly advanced atmospheric science by providing long-term, high-quality observations that
are freely accessible to the global research community.

 Researchers can access more than seven petabytes of atmospheric observations collected
over 30 years through the ARM Data Discovery portal at no cost. This web-based platform enables
users to efficiently search for datasets based on location, atmospheric variable, instrument,
observation period, and field campaign. Each datastream is accompanied by comprehensive
metadata, including instrument descriptions, calibration histories, site information, references,
principal investigators, and data processing details. By organizing observations within a
standardized and searchable framework, the Data Discovery portal enables researchers to rapidly
identify datasets relevant to their scientific objectives, reducing the inefficient and error-prone
process of manually locating observations.

 To further enhance the user experience, the ARM Data Advisor (ADA) was developed to
streamline the discovery, retrieval, and interpretation of meteorological observations and
atmospheric composition measurements. ADA is a domain-specific LLM-powered conversational
scientific assistant developed by the ARM Data Center at Oak Ridge National Laboratory to
facilitate efficient access to ARM datasets, documentation, web resources, and user services. Users
can interact with ADA using natural language to request datasets based on criteria such as
geographic location, observation period, measured variables, or instrumentation. In response,
ADA recommends appropriate datastreams, generates data visualizations, explains dataset quality
and metadata, and can even initiate data orders on behalf of the user. Beyond data retrieval, ADA
functions as a scientific assistant by providing recommendations on dataset selection, data
curation, and analysis strategies that help researchers address specific scientific questions more
effectively.

 As part of pre-release testing, ADA was evaluated as an information retrieval system, a
scientific assistant, and a domain-specific reasoning model. This report examines its performance
in terms of retrieval efficiency, hallucination resistance, scientific appropriateness, handling of
ambiguous prompts, and conversational memory. Each evaluation consisted of representative
prompts designed to simulate the typical workflows of researchers using the ARM Data Discovery
platform. The primary objective of this report is to assess ADA's effectiveness as a scientific
advisor and research assistant rather than its underlying technical implementation. Each section
concludes with an overall assessment of ADA's performance based on its responses to the
corresponding evaluation prompts.

Impression: ADA effectively guided the simulated user in downloading the required
meteorological data, even when they had little to no prior experience with ARM data discovery. It
provided clear, step-by-step instructions, from locating and accessing the appropriate datasets to
opening and working with NetCDF (.nc) files. In addition, ADA suggested the use of Atmospheric
Community Toolkit (ACT), an open-source python toolkit for analyzing of atmospheric time-
series. It also provided Python code that successfully extracted the required data, making the
process accessible even to users with limited programming experience. The Sources section was
particularly valuable for new users, as it provided helpful information about metadata, data
discovery links, instrumentation, datastreams, and ARM sites, enabling a better understanding of
the datasets and their context.

 Two of ADA's most valuable capabilities, particularly for users with limited experience
navigating the ARM Data Discovery platform, are the Quick Look Plot and Order functionalities.
The Quick Look Plot provides an immediate visualization of atmospheric variables, allowing users
to make an initial assessment of data behavior and suitability before initiating a download.
Complementing this feature, the integrated Order functionality enables users to directly request
the datasets recommended by ADA, streamlining the data acquisition process and reducing the
need for manual navigation through the ARM Data Discovery interface.

 Overall, in this test, ADA substantially reduced the learning curve associated with accessing
and utilizing the extensive ARM data archive. By streamlining data discovery and processing, it
allowed users to devote more time to addressing important scientific questions rather than
navigating data access challenges.

Prompt: With the available data, how can I investigate new particle formation in BNF?
 Response: To investigate New Particle Formation (NPF) at the Bankhead National
 Forest (BNF) site using the available data, you can look for the characteristic "banana
 plot" signature—a rapid increase in the concentration of small particles that grow over
 time.
 Since NPF involves the transition from gas-phase precursors to stable clusters and then
 to larger particles, you can use a combination of size distribution and chemical data to

 Step-by-Step Investigation Strategy
 1. Identify NPF Events (The "Banana Plot")
 Use the Merged SMPS/APS datastream (`bnfmergedsmpsapsM1.c1`). By plotting the
 particle size distribution (concentration vs. diameter) over time for the month of May,
 you can look for events where a high concentration of particles appears at the smallest
 detectable size (typically <10 nm) and gradually shifts toward larger diameters over
 several hours or days.
 2. Analyze Precursor and Oxidant Influence
 NPF is often driven by the oxidation of precursor gases. You can use the Ozone (O₃) data
 (`bnfaoso3M1.b1`) as a proxy for the oxidative capacity of the atmosphere. High ozone
 levels often correlate with the production of hydroxyl radicals (OH), which are critical

 19

 for oxidizing sulfur dioxide (SO₂) or volatile organic compounds (VOCs) into low-
 volatility species that trigger nucleation.
 3. Determine Chemical Composition
 Once a growth event is identified, use the ACSM data (`bnfaosacsmM1.b2`) to analyze
 the chemical makeup of the resulting particles. Specifically, look for:
 Sulfate: A primary driver of nucleation in many environments.
 Organics: These often contribute to the subsequent growth of the newly formed
 particles.
 4. Evaluate the "Condensational Sink"
 NPF is suppressed when there are already many large particles present, as these "sink"
 the precursor gases before they can nucleate. You can use the Merged SMPS/APS data to
 calculate the total aerosol surface area; NPF events are more likely to occur when this
 surface area is low.

Conclusion and Recommendations

ADA was evaluated across five capabilities relevant to the needs of users of the ARM Data
Discovery platform through a series of representative scenarios and prompts. Typical user
workflows were simulated to assess ADA's effectiveness as a data retrieval system, scientific
advisor, and research assistant. These scenarios demonstrated that ADA effectively guided users
in identifying and retrieving the datasets required for specific research objectives. In particular, the
Quick Look Plot and Order functionalities enabled users to rapidly assess the suitability of datasets
before initiating downloads, substantially reducing the time required to identify appropriate data
products. Furthermore, ADA successfully passed a series of hallucination tests by accurately
recognizing unavailable datasets and transparently communicating their absence rather than
generating fictitious information. ADA also performed well as a scientific advisor by
recommending relevant datasets, suggesting analytical workflows, and directing users to
appropriate resources for specific research topics. Ambiguous user queries were handled
effectively, as ADA requested additional context and encouraged users to refine their prompts
before recommending datasets. Finally, ADA demonstrated strong session continuity by retaining
conversational context across extended periods of inactivity, allowing users to resume previous
discussions without repeating earlier information. Overall, ADA substantially enhanced the
usability of the ARM Data Discovery platform by streamlining data discovery and improving
access to relevant scientific resources.

 To further enhance the user experience, it would be beneficial for ADA to provide greater
transparency regarding its conversation retention policies, including the duration for which
conversation history is maintained and any limitations on the number of interactions that can be
retained. Clear guidance on these aspects would help users better manage project information and
establish appropriate expectations for long-term continuity across research activities.
Nevertheless, users are encouraged to provide clear and comprehensive prompts to maximize the
relevance and quality of ADA's responses.
