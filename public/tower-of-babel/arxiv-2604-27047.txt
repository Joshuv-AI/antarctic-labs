Beyond Cloud-9: The case for discovering more HI-rich failed halos yt ( 51 ) , Amiga Halo Finder ( 29 ) , PlotDigitizer ( https://plotdigitizer.com/app) ).
Jorge Moreno, Coral Wheeler, Francisco J. Mercado, M. Katy Rodriguez Wimberly, Pratik J. Gandhi, Jenna Samuel, Robert Feldmann, James S. Bullock, Andrew Wetzel, Michael Boylan-Kolchin

Abstract
HI-rich starless halos, should they exist, hold great promise for elucidating properties of dark matter halos. This Letter examines the properties of HI-rich failed halos at redshift zero across state-of-the-art cosmological simulations (FIREbox, NIVARIA-LG and Recal-EAGLE). First we compare two numerical analogs with Cloud-9, purported to be the first discovery of a starless HI-rich halo. We argue that differences may be driven by environmental factors, and/or the treatment of gas self-shielding – which might further limit existing analytic schemes aimed at inferring dark matter halo information from 21 cm HI observations. We also find that the failed halo samples in the three simulations span different regions of the HI-gas-halo mass ( M HI − M gas − M 200 M_{\rm HI}-M_{\rm gas}-M_{\rm 200} ) plane. FIREbox objects occupy a very narrow regime, while NIVARIA-LG extends to a wider range of M 200 M_{\rm 200} values - and achieves higher M HI M_{\rm HI} and M gas M_{\rm gas} values. Recal-EAGLE M HI M_{\rm HI} values are similar to FIREbox, albeit with lower gas and halo masses. Lastly, we predict that more HI-rich starless halos can be discovered by exploring the HI-poor regime in the local universe, rather than HI-rich populations at high redshift. Overall, we advocate for the allocation of resources to detect and characterize other HI-rich (and HI-poor) failed halos in the local universe, plus dedicated follow-up spectroscopic observations that scrutinize claims to the absence of a faint stellar component, and that assess their isolation status in detail.

III
Discussion and Conclusions

The recent detection and characterization of Cloud-9, a candidate HI-rich failed halo in the local universe (
M_{\star}\lesssim 10^{3.5}M_{\odot}
,
M_{\rm HI}\simeq 10^{6}M_{\odot}
and
M_{\rm HI}/M_{\star}\gtrsim 443
) was no small feat
(
1
)
. As is often the case with exotic/extreme new findings – e.g., thin satellite planes, galaxies lacking dark matter, the tiniest galaxies, etc.
(
25
;
52
;
35
;
16
)
– this particular discovery poses challenges to simulations. However, bestowing too much constraining power upon a single object might be imprudent.

As a first step, in this Letter we compare the aforementioned Cloud-9 observations against two simulated HI-rich starless analogs, one from FIREbox
(
37
,
M_{\rm HI}\simeq 1.9\times 10^{6}M_{\odot}
,)
and one from NIVARIA-LG
(
20
,
M_{\rm HI}\simeq 6.2\simeq 1.9\times 10^{6}M_{\odot}
,)
. Both simulated objects disagree with Cloud-9’s 21-cm column density profiles in the innermost region (Figure
2
) – though the NIVARIA-LG object aligns with these observations at larger radii (
0.8-2
arcmin). This could be driven by resolution. For the NIVARIA-LG analog, divergence occurs at approximately twice their softening length. Meanwhile, the FIREbox analog contains 47 gas particles in this region with mean softening length of 80 pc (3.6 arcsec).

Another possible culprit is environment. A recent study by
50
points out that estimating
M_{\rm 200}
from observed HI column density profiles is far from trivial, highlighting the important role played by environment. Indeed, the highly asymmetric nature of the outermost isocontours measured by
5
with the Very Large Array (VLA) suggest that Cloud-9’s structure has been affected by environment, possibly by ram-pressure stripping. Notably, Cloud-9 is located
\sim
104 kpc away
(
55
, in projection,)
from M94 (NGC 4736), a nearby post-starburst spiral with stellar mass
M_{\star}\sim 5.4\times 10^{10}M_{\odot}

(
55
)
. In comparison, M94’s stellar halo extends out
\sim 30
kpc
(
21
)
and its HI-disk out to
\sim 47
kpc
(
55
)
. Furthermore, this galaxy also contains a diffuse HI-filament and seven high velocity clouds (HVCs) within
\sim 50
kpc of its center, plus two classical satellites at
\sim 38
kpc and
\sim 69
kpc
(
48
, in projection,)
. From abundance-matching considerations
(
3
;
38
)
, we expect M94’s virial radius to be
\sim 250-300
kpc. In other words, the possibility that Cloud-9 is in fact a satellite of M94, or at least a backsplash neighbor
(
14
;
4
;
35
)
, has not been completely ruled out. Indeed,
55
suggests that M94 might have experienced recent merging, which are believed to be responsible for tidal debris
(
36
)
. This, along with its highly asymmetric shape
(
5
)
, might not fully extricate Cloud-9 from a tidal-debris origin.

Consequently, the above considerations preclude us from placing too much constraining power on a single object. On a population-wide level, however, the three simulations considered here make different predictions for HI-rich failed objects (Figure
1
). FIREbox objects occupy very narrow ranges:
M_{\rm HI}\simeq(1.9-15)\times 10^{6}M_{\odot}
,
M_{\rm gas}\simeq(1.1-1.6)\times 10^{8}M_{\odot}
, and
M_{\rm 200}\simeq(7.8-8.6)\times 10^{9}M_{\odot}
. Their Recal-EAGLE counterparts cover a similar range in HI:
M_{\rm HI}\simeq(1.9-8.6)\times 10^{6}M_{\odot}
; but lower values on the other two axes:
M_{\rm gas}\simeq(1.6-14)\times 10^{8}M_{\odot}
, and
M_{\rm 200}\simeq(2.4-7.7)\times 10^{9}M_{\odot}
. On the
M_{\rm 200}-M_{\rm HI}
plane, the NIVARIA-LG population surrounds the other two samples, with 75% of their objects harboring over
10^{7}M_{\odot}
in HI and 50% with
M_{\rm gas}>2\times 10^{8}M_{\odot}
, exceeding the most gaseous systems in the other two simulations. Lastly, 25% of the NIVARIA-LG sample have
M_{\rm 200}<2\times 10^{9}M_{\odot}
, slightly below the Recal-EAGLE population, and substantially lower than FIREbox.

Notably, FIREbox predicts the lowest abundance of HI-rich failed halos in the Local Universe. This might be driven by our more detailed local Sobolev/Jeans-length self-shielding treatment, which foments HI-formation/retention and, ultimately, galaxy ignition
(
37
, thus promoting other viable HI-rich systems ‘away’ from the failed regime,)
. The other two simulations, on the other hand, rely on an analytic scheme by
40
. NIVARIA-LG and Recal-EAGLE exhibit excellent agreement with each other – and with the analytic model by
8
– on the temperature-density regime, especially at high densities. However, our FIREbox analog deviates from this behavior (Figure
3
). We note that our other three HI-rich failed objects in FIREbox (not shown) also display broad disagreement in the
T-n_{H}
plane. Indeed, it would be interesting to explore how the nine objects excluded by
8
– with
M_{\rm 200}\gtrsim 10^{9.7}M_{\odot}
and high central HI-densities – behave under a self-shielding treatment similar to ours
(
46
, e.g., with COLIBRE,)
. Meanwhile, the aforementioned limitations raise questions about the applicability of analytic schemes like the one proposed by
8
to extract dark matter halo properties from observed HI profiles -- warranting further studies and formulations with more sophisticated self-shielding prescriptions

We close with a few words on the detection of failed halos beyond the HI-rich regime at
z=0
. Overall, at high redshift, failed halos tend to have more HI gas
(
37
)
. Potentially, one could detect such systems with strong-lensing, though accomplishing this below
M_{\rm HI}\simeq 10^{10}M_{\odot}
has proven to be quite challenging
(
13
)
. Figure
4
shows the cumulative co-moving number density of HI-rich (blue) and HI-rich plus HI-poor (black) failed halos in FIREbox versus redshift. This suggests that, if we constrain ourselves to HI-rich objects (
M_{\rm HI}\geq 10^{6}M_{\odot})
, we harvest little gain by exploring early epochs. Namely, one would need to observe beyond
z=2
for the number of such object to increase by
1
dex. On the other hand, the HI-poor regime (at
z=0
) offers more fertile ground for discovery. In particular, FIREbox predicts that failed halos with
M_{\rm HI}\simeq 5\times 10^{4}-10^{6}M_{\odot}
are scarce. Such ‘zone of avoidance’ is not present in the NIVARIA-LG and Recal-EAGLE simulations. Likewise, NIVARIA-LG predicts that HI-poor failed halos do not supersede
M_{\rm 200}
values beyond
\sim 5\times 10^{9}M_{\odot}
, while Recal-EAGLE predicts a slightly higher cut off. Meanwhile, unlike the other two simulations, FIREbox predicts the existence of HI-poor starless halos with high halo masses: one object with
M_{\rm HI}\simeq 8.6\times 10^{3}M_{\odot}
and
M_{\rm 200}\simeq 1.2\times 10^{10}M_{\odot}
; plus another one with
M_{\rm HI}\simeq 81M_{\odot}
and
M_{\rm 200}\simeq 8.5\times 10^{9}M_{\odot}
. Confirmation of the existence of such extreme objects in the real universe could, in principle, serve to discriminate amongst galaxy formation models.

In summary, we demonstrate that HI-rich starless halos akin to Cloud-9 have the potential to constrain baryonic processes across cosmological simulations, which currently predict rather distinct HI-rich failed halo populations in the Local Universe. Indeed, observing such objects in large numbers
(
33
, e.g.,)
can guide the next generation of cosmological simulations – the type that capture the multi-phase turbulent structure of the ISM plus the physics of self-shielding adequately – such as FIREbox and its successors, BonFIRE and CampFIRE (Samuel et al., in prep). Despite the aforementioned complications, HI-rich failed halos are simple because they do not suffer from the effects of star formation and its associated feedback, allowing us in principle to marginalize over baryonic processes to constrain the nature of dark matter. However, in order to achieve this, it is imperative to detect and characterize more such systems. For this reason, we urge the community to prioritize investigations of HI-rich objects in the Local Universe, especially those that scrutinize the purported absence (or presence) of a stellar component – as well as their environment – via detailed spectroscopic observations and kinematic measurements.
