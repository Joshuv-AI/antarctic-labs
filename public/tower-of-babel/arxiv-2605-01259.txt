The Normal Domination Partizan Game in Stars
Rudini Sampaio, Edileudo Maciel M. Filho, Jefter G. Maciel Paz, João Marcos Brito

Abstract
The Domination game is an impartial game on graphs, introduced in 2010, and proved PSPACE-complete in the normal variant in 2026. In this game, Alice and Bob alternately select playable vertices, where a vertex is playable if it dominates at least one vertex not dominated by the vertices selected before in the game. The game ends when the selected vertices form a dominating set. In the normal variant, the player unable to move loses. In contrast to the impartial game, the partizan game has the vertices already colored with A A , B B , or C C , in such a way that Alice (resp. Bob) can only select vertices colored with A A (resp. B B ) or C C . The partizan game was proved PSPACE-hard in 2026. In this paper, we determine the winner of the Normal Partizan Domination game in graphs whose components are complete split graphs, including star forests, for any initial coloring of its vertices. We also obtain partial results for complete bipartite graphs.

Introduction

Given a graph
G
, we say that a subset
D\subseteq V(G)
of vertices of
G
is a
dominating set
if every vertex has a neighbor in
D
or belongs to
D
. We say that a vertex

dominates
a vertex

if
u
and
v
are neighbors or
u=v
.

In the Domination game on a graph
G
, Alice and Bob alternately select one playable vertex, where a vertex is
playable
if it dominates at least one vertex not dominated by the vertices selected before in the game. The game ends when the set of selected vertices is a dominating set of
G
.

In the normal variant, the player unable to move loses. In the misère variant, the player unable to move wins. In the optimization variant, it is also given an integer
k
and Alice wins if and only if at most
k
vertices are selected. The smallest
k
such that Alice wins this variant is the
game domination number

\gamma_{g}(G)
. It is not difficult to check that
\gamma(G)\leq\gamma_{g}(G)\leq 2\gamma(G)-1
, where the
domination number

\gamma(G)
is the size of a minimum dominating set of
G
.

The Domination game was introduced in 2010 by Brešar et al.
[
4
]
in the optimization variant, which was proved PSPACE-complete in 2016 by Brešar et al.
[
3
]
. The normal variant was introduced by Brito et al.
[
5
]
, where it was proved PSPACE-complete. They also proved that Alice wins the normal variant in a path
P_{n}
(resp. cycle
C_{n}
) if and only if the remainder of the division of
n
by
4
is different from
0
(resp. equal to
3
).

The Domination game is classified as an
impartial game
, since there are no special moves for each player, that is, in any position of the game in a graph, if Alice can make certain move, Bob could also make it if it were his turn (and vice-versa). We say that a game is
partizan
if it is not impartial.

In the Normal Partizan Domination game, the vertices of the graph
G
are already colored with colors
A
,
B
or
C
, in such a way that Alice (resp. Bob) can only select vertices colored with
A
(resp.
B
) or
C
. Except for this difference, the game proceeds as in the impartial game. If all vertices are colored
C
, we are in the impartial game. Brito et al.
[
5
]
also proved that the partizan game is PSPACE-complete and obtained a polynomial time algorithm to determine the winner of the partizan game in paths and cycles, for every initial coloring of the vertices with colors
A
and
B
.

In this paper, we use the Combinatorial Game Theory to determine in polynomial time the winner of the Normal Partizan Domination game for every disjoint union of complete split graphs, including star forests, for any initial coloring of the vertices with colors
A
,
B
and
C
.

In the rest of the paper, we consider the Normal Partizan Domination game in a graph
G
whose vertex set is partitioned in 3 subsets
A
,
B
and
C
, representing the colors of each vertex, in such a way that Alice (resp. Bob) can only select vertices with color
A
(resp.
B
) or
C
. In the impartial variant, every vertex has color
C
.

Theory of Normal Partizan Games in a Nutshell

Books of Combinatorial Game Theory, such as
[
1
,
2
,
7
]
, present the established theory on normal games, which associates to each position of a game a surreal number, which belongs to a numerical system created by Conway

in the 1970s, containing rational numbers, but also different numbers such as

(which is incomparable to

),
\uparrow
and

It is known that a rational number is surreal if and only if it is

(its denominator is a power of 2, including all integers).
Roughly speaking, let

be the surreal number of a game. It is known that
s>0
(resp.
s<0
) if Alice (resp. Bob) always wins, playing first or not, that
s=0
if the first player loses and that
s||0
(incomparable to
0
) if the first player wins.

The notation
\{X|Y\}
describes surreal numbers associated to games, where
X
(resp.
Y
) is the list of options (possible moves) of Alice (resp. Bob).
For example, in the empty game
0=\{|\}
, there is no option for Alice and Bob and then the first player loses.
In the game
1=\{0|\}
(resp.
-1=\{|0\}
), Alice (resp. Bob) wins obtaining the game
0
(the opponent has no move).
It is known that the game
1/2^{n+1}
is equal to
\{0|1/2^{n}\}
and that
-1/2^{n+1}=\{-1/2^{n}|0\}
.
In the game star
*=\{0|0\}
, the first player always wins, obtaining the game
0
, and then
*||0
.

The
sum
of games
J_{1}+J_{2}
is the game in which the next player must make a move in only one of the games
J_{1}
or
J_{2}
. It is easy to see that the sum of games is commutative and associative.
Given a game
J
, let
nJ=n\cdot J
be the sum of
n
disjoint copies of
J
, let
nJ*=*+nJ
and let
-J
be the game in which the roles of Alice and Bob are changed.
It is known that
J+(-J)=0
(the first player always loses in the sum of

and
-J
).

Let
\uparrow=\{0|*\}
and
\downarrow=\{*|0\}
.
The game
\uparrow^{[n]}
is recursively defined as
\uparrow^{[1]}=\uparrow
and
\uparrow^{[n]}=\{\uparrow^{[n-1]}|*\}
for

.
Analogously,
\downarrow_{[1]}=\downarrow
and
\downarrow_{[n]}=\{*|\downarrow_{[n-1]}\}
for

.
It is known that
\uparrow^{[n]}=-\downarrow_{[n]}
,
\uparrow^{[n+1]}>\uparrow^{[n]}>0

\downarrow_{[n+1]}<\downarrow_{[n]}<0
,
\uparrow^{[n]}||*
and
\downarrow_{[n]}||*
.

When

is impartial, then

. In those games, in addition to

and

, recursively define

From the Sprague-Grundy Theory, it is known that

and then the first player loses. Furthermore, if

5
Complete Split Graphs

A
complete split graph
is any graph
G
such that
V(G)
has a partition
(K,S)
such that
K
induces a clique,
S
induces an independent set and there are all possible edges between
K
and
S
.

Theorem 8
.

Consider the game in a complete split graph
G
with partition
(K,S)
. If every vertex of the clique
K
has color
A
(resp.
B
), the game is equivalent to the game in the star with center colored
A
(resp.
B
). Otherwise, the game is equivalent to the game in the star with center colored
C
.

Proof.

Note that any move in
K
ends the game and that, if a vertex of
K
is not playable, then no vertex is playable and the game ended. Therefore, if every vertex of
K
has color
A
, then only Alice can play in
K
(and just once), which is equivalent to a single vertex colored
A
in
K
. Analogous for Bob if every vertex of
K
has color
B
. Otherwise, both Alice and Bob can play (and just once) in
K
, which is equivalent to a single vertex with color
C
in
K
.
∎
