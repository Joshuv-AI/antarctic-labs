A Diffusion Analysis of Policy Gradient for Stochastic Bandits
Tor Lattimore

Abstract
We study a continuous-time diffusion approximation of policy gradient for k k -armed stochastic bandits. We prove that with a learning rate η = O ⁡ ( Δ 2 / log ⁡ ( n ) ) \eta=O(\Delta^{2}/\log(n)) the regret is O ⁡ ( k ​ log ⁡ ( k ) ​ log ⁡ ( n ) / η ) O(k\log(k)\log(n)/\eta) where n n is the horizon and Δ \Delta the minimum gap. Moreover, we construct an instance with only logarithmically many arms for which the regret is linear unless η = O ⁡ ( Δ 2 ) \eta=O(\Delta^{2}) .

Introduction

Policy gradient is a classical and widely used reinforcement learning algorithm
(
7
)
.
Our focus is on the dynamics of policy gradient with the softmax policy class on Gaussian bandits.
Even in this simple setup, only the two-armed setting is really properly understood.
We opt for the unusual simplification of studying a continuous-time diffusion approximation
of the policy gradient algorithm.
We believe (but do not prove) that this is a high-quality approximation. This approach comes with a number of advantages.
Most straightforwardly, the randomness arising from the sampling of actions is removed, which slightly simplifies the analysis.
More significantly, by switching to continuous time we can exploit the vast literature on stochastic differential equations.
Needless to say, we hope the proof ideas can be generalised to discrete time eventually, but for now we are happy to settle with simpler analysis
and new means for understanding a delicate problem.

We let

Bandit notation

There are

We will consider Gaussian rewards with mean

We assume that

In discrete time the learner sequentially selects actions

Policy gradient in continuous time

In discrete time the classical policy gradient algorithm specified to
k
-armed bandits
is derived as follows.
The value function is
v(\theta)=\langle\pi(\theta),\mu\rangle
and its gradient is

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(\theta)=(\operatorname{diag}(\pi)-\pi\pi^{\top})\mu\,.

(1)

This quantity is not observed, but is classically estimated in round
t
using Monte-Carlo by

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla}_{t}=(\operatorname{Id}-\pi_{t}\bm{1}^{\top})e_{A_{t}}Y_{t}=(\operatorname{Id}-\pi_{t}\bm{1}^{\top})e_{A_{t}}Y_{t}\,.

The policy gradient update with learning rate
\eta
is then

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}+\eta\widehat{\nabla}_{t}\,.

In continuous time there are two ways to proceed.
One option is to approximate the discrete time stochastic process
(\theta_{t})
by a continuous process with matching variance
and drift. Alternatively, the mechanism for deriving the policy gradient method can be repeated from first principles in continuous
time.
Eq.

1
remains the same in continuous time. Because the drift of the continuous-time process
(X_{t})
is

\operatorname{diag}(\pi_{t})\mu
, an unbiased continuous-time estimator of
v^{\prime}(\theta_{t})
is

(\operatorname{Id}-\pi_{t}\bm{1}^{\top})\operatorname{d}\!X_{t}
.
Then the continuous-time policy gradient algorithm is naturally defined by the stochastic process

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}\!\theta_{t}

\displaystyle=\eta(\operatorname{diag}(\pi_{t})-\pi_{t}\pi_{t}^{\top})\mu\operatorname{d}\!t+\eta\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\pi_{t}})\Sigma^{1/2}\operatorname{d}\!B_{t}-\eta\pi_{t}\langle\sqrt{\pi_{t}},\Sigma^{1/2}\operatorname{d}\!B_{t}\rangle\,.

This is equivalent to approximating the discrete time policy gradient algorithm by matching the mean and variance.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
\eta\leq 1
. Then, for all actions

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\inf_{t\leq n}\theta_{t,a}\leq-\log\left(n/\delta\right)\right)\leq\delta\,.

Suppose that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi_{t,1}}\leq\frac{6k\log(n/\delta)}{\theta_{t,1}+1+\log(n/\delta)}\,.

Upper bounds

To avoid clutter we assume that the variance matrix is the identity:

As a warmup, we start with the two-armed setting, which is straightforward and also well-understood in the discrete time setting
(

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that
a=\Delta_{2}/\eta>1
. Then with
k=2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Reg}_{n}]\leq\frac{a}{2\Delta_{2}}\log\left(1+\frac{2(a+1)n\Delta_{2}^{2}}{a^{2}}\right)+\frac{a^{2}}{2(a-1)\Delta_{2}}\,.

Proof.

Abbreviate
\pi_{t}=\pi_{t,1}
.
Starting with the same regret decomposition as proposed by
1
, the regret is

\displaystyle\operatorname{Reg}_{n}=\Delta_{2}\int_{0}^{n}(1-\pi_{t})\operatorname{d}\!t=\Delta_{2}\int_{0}^{n}\pi_{t}(1-\pi_{t})\operatorname{d}\!t+\Delta_{2}\int_{0}^{n}(1-\pi_{t})^{2}\operatorname{d}\!t\,.

(2)

Let
Z_{t}=\theta_{t,1}-\theta_{t,2}
and

M_{t}=\exp(bZ_{t})
. By Itô’s rule,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{n}]-1

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{n}\pi_{t}(1-\pi_{t})M_{t}(2b\eta\Delta_{2}+2b^{2}\eta^{2})\operatorname{d}\!t\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{n}\pi_{t}(1-\pi_{t})M_{t}\Delta_{2}^{2}(2b/a+2b^{2}/a^{2})\operatorname{d}\!t\right]\,.

When
b=1
, then
M_{t}=\pi_{t}/(1-\pi_{t})
and in this case

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{n}]=1+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{n}\pi_{t}^{2}\Delta_{2}^{2}(2/a+2/a^{2})\right]\leq 1+\left(2/a+2/a^{2}\right)n\Delta_{2}^{2}\,.

(3)

Therefore, since
(Z_{t})
has drift
2\eta\Delta_{2}\pi_{t}(1-\pi_{t})
and
Z_{0}=0
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{n}\pi_{t}(1-\pi_{t})\operatorname{d}\!t\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}]}{2\eta}\leq\frac{\log(\mathbb{E}[M_{n}])}{2\eta}\leq\frac{a}{2\Delta_{2}}\log\left(1+\frac{2(a+1)n\Delta_{2}^{2}}{a^{2}}\right)\,,

(4)

where the inequalities follow from Jensen,
Eq.

3
and because
a=\Delta_{2}/\eta
.
For the other term in the decomposition, let
b=-1
so that
M_{t}=(1-\pi_{t})/\pi_{t}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{n}]-1=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{n}(1-\pi_{t})^{2}\Delta_{2}^{2}(2/a^{2}-2/a)\right]\,.

Since
M_{n}
is non-negative and
a>1
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{n}(1-\pi_{t})^{2}\operatorname{d}\!t\right]\leq\frac{a^{2}}{2(a-1)\Delta_{2}}\,.

(5)

Combining
Eqs.

4
and
5
with
Eq.

2
yields the bound.
∎

Remark 5
.

As
n\to\infty
and by choosing
a\to 1
very slowly one obtains the regret for the (non-anytime) algorithm
of
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Reg}_{n}]\sim\frac{\log(n)}{2\Delta_{2}}
, which appears to contradict the lower bound by
3
.
The reason there is no contradiction is that the learning rate depends on
\Delta_{2}
and in this scenario the
classical lower bound does not apply and the
\frac{\log(n)}{2\Delta_{2}}
regret is optimal
(
2
)
.

The argument above does not generalise to the situation where there are more than two actions.
Not only is the problem more difficult to analyse, the learning rate needs to be considerably smaller and the regret is correspondingly much worse.
We will prove the following:

Theorem 6
.

Suppose that
\eta\leq\frac{\Delta_{2}^{2}}{8\log(2n^{2})}
, then
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Reg}_{n}]=O\left(\frac{k\log(k)\log(n)}{\eta}\right)
.

We start with a straightforward lemma governing the dynamics of
\log(\frac{\pi_{t,1}}{\pi_{t,a}})
that reveals the main challenge when
k>2
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
Z_{t,a}=\theta_{t,1}-\theta_{t,a}
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}\!Z_{t,a}

\displaystyle=\eta\left[\pi_{t,a}\Delta_{a}+(\pi_{t,1}-\pi_{t,a})R_{t}\right]\operatorname{d}\!t+\eta\sqrt{\pi_{t,1}+\pi_{t,a}-(\pi_{t,1}-\pi_{t,a})^{2}}\operatorname{d}\!W_{t}\,,

where
(W_{t})
is a standard Brownian motion.

The expression in
Lemma

7
is revealing. If we are to prove the algorithm is learning, then we must show that
Z_{t,a}
increases with time for
a\neq 1
.
Naturally this will be easiest to prove if the SDE associated with
Z_{t,a}=\theta_{t,1}-\theta_{t,a}
has positive drift and well-controlled noise.
Regrettably, however, this is not the case. The drift
\eta\pi_{t,a}\Delta_{a}+\eta(\pi_{t,1}-\pi_{t,a})R_{t}
can be negative if
\pi_{t,1}<\pi_{t,a}

and the regret is large. If there were no noise, then an elementary analysis of the differential equation would show that the drift is always positive
because at initialisation
\pi_{t,1}\geq\pi_{t,a}
and the positive drift ensures this stays true throughout the trajectory.
The following lemma shows that when
\eta
is suitably small, then
Z_{t,a}
can be lower-bounded with high probability.

Lemma 8
.

Suppose that
\eta\leq\frac{\Delta_{2}^{2}}{8\log(2n/\delta)}
, then
\mathbb{P}(\inf_{t\leq n}Z_{t,a}\leq-\Delta_{2}/2)\leq\delta
.

Proof.

Abbreviate
Z_{t}=Z_{t,a}
and let
\tau=\inf\{t:Z_{t}=-\Delta_{2}/2\}
and

s(t)=\eta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}(\pi_{u,1}+\pi_{u,a}-(\pi_{u,1}-\pi_{u,a})^{2})\operatorname{d}\!u
and
t(\cdot)=s^{-1}(\cdot)
be its inverse.
Lastly, let
U_{s(t)}=Z_{t}
.
By definition,
\inf_{t\leq n}Z_{t}
has the same law as
\inf_{s\leq s(n)}U_{s}
. Moreover, when
U_{s}\geq-\Delta_{2}/2
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}\!U_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta}\left[\frac{\pi_{t(s),a}\Delta_{a}+\pi_{t(s),a}(\exp(U_{s})-1)R_{t(s)}}{\pi_{t(s),1}+\pi_{t(s),a}-(\pi_{t(s),1}-\pi_{t(s),a})^{2}}\right]\operatorname{d}\!s+\operatorname{d}\!W_{s}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\eta}\left[\frac{\pi_{t(s),a}\Delta_{a}}{\pi_{t(s),1}+\pi_{t(s),a}}\right]\operatorname{d}\!s+\operatorname{d}\!W_{s}=\frac{\Delta_{a}}{2\eta(\exp(U_{s})+1)}\operatorname{d}\!s+\operatorname{d}\!W_{s}\,.

where the inequality holds because
R_{t(s)}\leq 1
and
\exp(U_{s})-1\geq\exp(-\Delta_{2}/2)-1\geq-\Delta_{2}/2\geq-\Delta_{a}/2
, which means the numerator is positive.
Looking at this relation, when the learning rate is small relative to
\Delta_{2}
, the process has considerable positive drift when
U_{s}\in[-\Delta_{2}/2,1]
.
Formally, since
s(n)\leq n
and by a comparison to the SDE in
Proposition

16
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\inf\{U_{s}:0\leq s\leq s(n)\}\leq-\Delta_{2}/2\right)\leq\delta
.
∎

4
Lower bound

Proposition

4
shows that when there are only two actions, then setting
\eta
just slightly smaller than
\Delta_{2}
leads to near-optimality of policy gradient.
The next construction shows that when there are more than two actions the correct choice of
\eta
can be as small as
\Delta_{2}^{2}
and in general
no choice leads to near-optimal regret in the sense of being
remotely close to the lower bound by
3
.
The fundamental difference is that when
k=2
, the drift of
\theta_{t,1}-\theta_{t,2}
is always positive. When there are more arms, this only holds on trajectories
that are well-behaved in the sense that
\theta_{t,1}-\theta_{t,a}
never gets too negative for
a\neq 1
.

Lower bound construction

The poor behaviour of policy gradient is most dramatic when
k>2
and
\mu
is such that
\Delta=(0,\Delta_{2},1,\ldots,1)
with
\Delta_{2}
close to zero.
The following arguments can be made to work with
\Sigma=\operatorname{Id}
but are simplified considerably by instead letting
\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(e_{1}+e_{2})
so that the only noise arises from the first two actions.
What happens in this scenario is that for a long time actions
1
and
2
are nearly statistically indistinguishable.
Since actions
a>2
are very suboptimal,
\theta_{t,a}
for
a>2
decreases rapidly and
\theta_{t,1}
and
\theta_{t,2}
increase.
But they do not increase together at the same rate. Rather, unless the learning rate is very small, the dynamics of policy gradient effectively ‘picks a winner’ in
\{1,2\}
at random
and by the time
\pi_{t,a}
is negligible for
a>2
, it turns out that either
\pi_{t,1}
or
\pi_{t,2}
is nearly
1
.
From then on policy gradient behaves as if the bandit has two actions, but with a potentially very disadvantageous initialisation.

Theorem 10

The SDE in

In particular, provided that

5
Discussion

Continuous time vs discrete time

When the learning rate is small, the continuous time and discrete time processes are extremely similar.
Even for larger learning rates, the noise terms contract asymptotically in both cases, suggesting that the diffusion approximation may be good
for any learning rate once the algorithm reaches the asymptotic regime.
We believe our proof technique for the upper bound is likely to translate to the discrete time setting, probably with only a little tedium.
Proving the lower bound in discrete time is probably more challenging and arguably less worthwhile.

Refining the lower bound

Our upper bound is
\tilde{O}(k/\Delta_{2}^{2})
.
The lower bound shows that this is not in general improvable when
k
is logarithmic.
An interesting direction is to extend the lower bound construction to introduce a linear dependence on
k
.

\bm{k}
-dependence

1
constructed a lower bound showing that the learning rate needs to be
O(1/k)
and speculated that the correct learning rate might be around
\Delta_{2}/k
.
Our upper bound holds without assuming that
\eta
is less than
O(1/k)
, suggesting that this is a transient requirement where the continuous time and discrete time setups are
not comparable. Moreover, our lower bound shows that in certain cases the learning rate needs to be
O(\Delta_{2}^{2})
in order to achieve sublinear regret.

Logarithmic factors

The upper bound in
Theorem

6
relies on the assumption that
\eta\leq c\Delta_{2}^{2}/\log(n)
. One may wonder if this logarithmic term in the denominator can be dropped.
The argument in
Lemma

8
is quite crude, so there may be room for improvement there.

Bounding the quadratic variation

Note that by definition, if
s\leq\kappa
, then
V_{s}<1
and by
Eq.

14

\displaystyle V_{s}^{2}\leq\max\left(1,4\eta e^{(1-\epsilon)s}\right)\,,

(15)

where we used the fact that when
s\leq\kappa
, then
[M]_{s}\leq 3/2
and hence
W_{[M]_{s}}\geq-2
.
By definition the quadratic variation is

\displaystyle[M]_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-(1-\epsilon)u}\gamma_{u}^{2}\operatorname{d}\!u\,.

(16)

Since all the terms are positive and
\gamma_{u}\geq 1
, it immediately follows that
[M]_{s}\geq(1-e^{-s})/4
.
Upper bounding is more delicate. Since
\gamma_{t}\in[0,4]
,

\displaystyle[M]_{s}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(a)}}}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-(1-\epsilon)u}(1+4G_{t(u)})(1+V_{u}^{2})\operatorname{d}\!u

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(b)}}}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-(1-\epsilon)u}(1+4G_{t(u)})(2+4\eta e^{(1-\epsilon)u})\operatorname{d}\!u

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(c)}}}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-(1-\epsilon)u}\left(1+\frac{800}{m}\min\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}},e^{u/2}\right)\right)\left(2+4\eta e^{(1-\epsilon)u}\right)\operatorname{d}\!u

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(d)}}}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1-e^{-(1-\epsilon)s}}{2(1-\epsilon)}+1/8

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(e)}}}}}{{\leq}}3/4\,.

where
(a)
follows from
Eq.

16
,

(b)
from
Eq.

15
,

(c)
from
Eq.

12
and
Eq.

13
,

(d)
by computing the integral and naive bounding and
(e)
is immediate.
Therefore
(1-e^{-s})/2\leq[M]_{s}\leq 3/2
for all

Bounding the SDE

Our exact solution is

\displaystyle V_{s}

\displaystyle=e^{(1-\epsilon)s/2}\left[\frac{\sqrt{\eta}}{96}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-(1-\epsilon)u/2}(1+G_{t(u)})\operatorname{d}\!u+\sqrt{\eta}W_{[M]_{s}}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(a)}}}}}{{\leq}}e^{(1-\epsilon)s/2}\left[\frac{\sqrt{\eta}}{96}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-(1-\epsilon)u/2}\left(1+\frac{8}{m}e^{u/2}\right)\operatorname{d}\!u+\sqrt{\eta}W_{[M]_{s}}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(b)}}}}}{{\leq}}e^{(1-\epsilon)s/2}\left[\frac{\sqrt{\eta}}{96}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-u/2+\epsilon s_{\max}/2}\left(1+\frac{8}{m}e^{u/2}\right)\operatorname{d}\!u+\sqrt{\eta}W_{[M]_{s}}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(c)}}}}}{{\leq}}e^{(1-\epsilon)s/2}\left[\frac{\sqrt{\eta}}{48}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-u/2}\left(1+\frac{8}{m}e^{u/2}\right)\operatorname{d}\!u+\sqrt{\eta}W_{[M]_{s}}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(d)}}}}}{{\leq}}e^{(1-\epsilon)s/2}\left[\frac{\sqrt{\eta}}{48}\left(2+\frac{8s_{\max}}{m}\right)+\sqrt{\eta}W_{[M]_{s}}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(e)}}}}}{{\leq}}e^{(1-\epsilon)s/2}\left[\frac{\sqrt{\eta}}{16}+\sqrt{\eta}W_{[M]_{s}}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(f)}}}}}{{\leq}}e^{(1-\epsilon)s/2}\left[\frac{\sqrt{\eta}}{16}+\sqrt{\eta}(e^{-s}/4-1/8)\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(g)}}}}}{{<}}e^{(1-\epsilon)s/2}\sqrt{\eta}(e^{-s}/4-1/16)\,.

where
(a)
follows from
Eq.

13
.

(b)
since
s\leq s_{\max}
.

(c)
since
\exp(\epsilon s_{\max}/2)\leq 2
.

(d)
by crudely bounding the integral.

(e)
since
m\geq 8s_{\max}
.

(f)
since
W_{u}\leq 1/8-u
and
[M]_{s}\geq(1-e^{-s})/4
.

(g)
since
\eta<c
and
\sqrt{\eta}\geq C\Delta_{2}

Hence, with constant probability, whenever

\displaystyle Z_{t}=2\operatorname{arcsinh}(U_{t})\leq 2\operatorname{arcsinh}(V_{s(t)})<2\operatorname{arcsinh}\left(e^{(1-\epsilon)s/2}\sqrt{\eta}(e^{-s}/4-1/16)\right)\,,

which completes the proof.
