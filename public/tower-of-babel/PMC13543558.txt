Atlantoaxial subluxation treated with exercise therapy combined with occipito-maxillary band traction: a case report
Authors: Jingjing Gao, Bin Jiang, He Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAtlantoaxial subluxation (AAS) is a potentially serious condition of the cervical spine. Traditional management often involves a choice between conservative methods, which can have high recurrence rates, and surgical fusion, which may lead to a loss of cervical mobility. This case report demonstrates a novel combined approach to treat intractable dizziness caused by AAS.This approach involves the use of combined exercise therapy and occipito-maxillary band traction therapy.Case presentationA 10-year-old male patient of Chinese ethnicity presented with persistent dizziness attributed to atlantoaxial subluxation. The treatment regimen consisted of exercise therapy combined with occipito-maxillary band traction. The therapeutic protocol involved initial joint reduction via traction, followed by a comprehensive exercise therapy designed to restore dynamic neuromuscular stability. Following this combined treatment, the patient's symptoms were completely resolved.ConclusionThis case illustrates that a combination of occipito-maxillary band traction and comprehensive exercise therapy, which integrates mechanical reduction with functional neuromuscular remodeling, can be an effective treatment for mild to moderate AAS. This approach provides a viable therapeutic option, particularly for early intervention, when conventional conservative treatments are insufficient but surgery is not yet indicated.

Introduction

Atlantoaxial subluxation (AAS) refers to the situation where the joints between the first and second cervical vertebrae lose their normal alignment but do not completely disconnect. This part of the dislocation may cause the atlas to shift forward, backward or rotational relative to the axis. An open-mouth radiograph or a CT scan typically shows deviation of the odontoid process with slippage of the C1 on the C2 is often caused by factors such as loose transverse ligaments, abnormal development of the odontoid process, or trauma [1]. Atlantoaxial subluxation has a relatively high incidence rate. If not treated promptly, it can lead to adverse effects such as pain, reduced neck movement and damage to the nervous system [2]. At present, the treatment of atlantoaxial subluxation is divided into conservative treatment and surgical treatment. The methods of conservative treatment include observation, the use of cervical braces and analgesics, skin traction or bone traction. The main surgical treatment is C1-C2 posterior fusion surgery, but both have certain limitations. Exercise therapy in combination with the occipito-maxillary band traction, as a kind of optimization strategy, conservative treatment by traction + movement stability of double path, considering long-term and short-term anatomical correction function reconstruction, is a conservative optimal selection for the treatment of atlantoaxial subluxation. In this article, we present a patient with atlantoaxial subluxation and discuss the effectiveness of the combined exercise therapy with occipito-maxillary band traction treatment for this case.

Key clinical message

For patients with atlantoaxial subluxation who do not respond to traditional conservative treatment and meet surgical indications, the innovative application of exercise therapy targeting the neck muscle groups (stretching and relaxation + stability training) combined with occipito-maxillary band traction can effectively restore joint alignment, enhance neuromuscular control, achieve complete symptom relief, and prevent recurrence for a long time (3 years). This plan provides a safe alternative strategy for avoiding surgical risks and postoperative joint stiffness.

Case presentation

Case history and first visit

A 10-year-old male table tennis player of Chinese ethnicity sought medical attention due to paroxysmal dizziness for one year. The clinical manifestations include a feeling of dizziness (2–3 times per week, each session lasting from half a day to one day) accompanied by nausea and blurred vision, without tinnitus or limb movement disorders. He has visited multiple departments such as neurology and orthopedics. No abnormalities were found in the cranial CT/MRI/MRA, electroencephalogram, and vestibular function tests. After oral administration of flunarizine and 8 cervical manual reduction treatments, the symptoms were partially relieved (residual average 2 episodes of vertigo per month, 8–10 h each time), and there was no further improvement after continuing the above treatment. There was no previous history of neck trauma. Two weeks before the onset of the disease, there was a history of throat infection (blood routine test from another hospital showed a white blood cell count of 13.9 × 109/L and a neutrophil percentage of 82.5%). The blood routine test was normal after three months.

Specialized physical examination revealed a Cock-Robin position with the head tilted to one side and with the chin rotated to the side opposite the facet subluxation: the head was tilted to the right with a rotation to the left in the sitting position. Meanwhile, physical examination revealed asymmetry of the bilateral earlobes (the right side was lower), acromions and subscapular angles (the left side was lower), head forward movement, rounded shoulders and a Grisel's Syndrome with torticollis, stiff neck and limited mobility. The physiological curvature of the cervical vertebrae was reduced, with tenderness in the spinous processes of C5-6 ( +), increased tension in the suboccipital muscles, scalene muscles and sternocleidomastoid muscles accompanied by tenderness (+ + +), and tenderness in the bilateral pectoralis major, teres minor and interscapular regions ( +). No positive signs were found in the neurological examination. The intervertebral foramen compression test (-). The patient's weight is 36 kg. The CT scan of the neck confirmed subluxation of the atlantoaxial joint. Based on the clinical manifestations and CT scan, it can be determined that the patient's subluxation was atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation (AARS). The Fielding and Hawkins classification was Type II. Before the treatment, the distance of the left lateral atlantodental spaces (LLADS) was 2.0 mm, while the right one (RLADS) was 7.8 mm, the variance between them was 5.8 mm. The differential diagnosis excludes vestibular migraine (without headache/photophobia and photophobia), benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (without induction by changes in body position), and supports cercal-origin dizziness (imaging subluxation + abnormal muscle group tone). The surgeon recommended surgical treatment. His parents, considering the risks of the operation and postoperative complications, turned to us for help. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient and his guardian for the publication of this case report and all related images. All personal identifiable information has been fully anonymized to protect patient privacy.

Methods

An innovative combined intervention (twice a week) was implemented for 8 weeks, with initial joint reduction via traction, followed by a comprehensive exercise therapy designed to restore dynamic neuromuscular stability. The exercise therapy plan consists of three core modules: (1) Manual stretching and relaxation: To relieve the tension of the suboccipital muscle group and the lateral cervical flexor muscle group; (2) Resistance stability training: Strengthen the rotational stability of the suboccipital muscle group and the static endurance of the posterior/lateral cervical muscle group; (3) Suspension training: Activates the deep cervical flexors and the neuromuscular control of the core muscle groups in the back. Outcome and follow-up

After treatment, the patient's dizziness symptoms (presented by the Dizziness Rating Scale) and the accompanying visual impairments showed a stepwise relief. The symptoms disappeared after 8 weeks of treatment, and no recurrence occurred after 3 years of observation. The sequence of symptom improvement is shown in Table 2. After the treatment, specialized physical examination showed improvement. The physiological curvature of the cervical vertebrae was still decreased, but the left and right tilting of the head when sitting disappeared. The bilateral earlobes, acromions and subscapular angles were at the same height. The forward head movement, rounded shoulders, and symptoms of Grisel's Syndrome were all improved. The tenderness of the spinous processes at the 5th and 6th cervical vertebrae has disappeared, the tension and tenderness of the suboccipital muscles, scalene muscles and sternocleidomastoid muscles have vanished, the tenderness of the bilateral pectoralis major and teres minor muscles has been alleviated, and the tenderness in the bilateral interscapular region has disappeared. The intervertebral foramen compression test (-). Imaging assessment showed that the atlantoaxial subluxation of the patient had significantly improved. Conclusion

This case confirms that the comprehensive exercise therapy focusing on the neck muscle groups combined with occipito-maxillary band traction can successfully reverse refractory atlantoaxial subluxation, achieve biomechanical reduction, symptom elimination and long-term stability maintenance, providing evidence-based support for avoiding surgical risks.

Discussion

The suboccipital muscle group, as the deepest muscle group of the upper cervical vertebrae, attaches to the occipital bone, atlas and axis, and is crucial for maintaining the stability of the atlantoaxial joint (AAJ) and the atlantooccipital joint (AOJ). Anatomical studies have shown that these muscles are rich in muscle spindles (the density of muscle spindles per gram of muscle is higher than that of other neck muscle groups), making them play a key role in posture control and proprioceptive feedback [3–5]. However, due to its small size and proximity to the craniovertebral joints, the suboccipital muscle group is at a mechanical disadvantage and is prone to functional disorders caused by long-term poor posture (such as using electronic devices with the head down) or chronic strain, which in turn can lead to atlantoaxial subluxation and cervicogenic dizziness [6]. In this case, the patient's joint stability was significantly improved through the combined exercise therapy and traction regimen. This plan not only corrects the abnormal alignment of the atlantoaxial joint, but also reduces the need to rely solely on external fixation (such as cervical braces) or surgical fusion by enhancing the functional compensation mechanism of deep muscle groups.At present, the limitation of traditional conservative treatments (such as cervical brace fixation and analgesics) lies in their passivity and inability to address muscle weakness or neural control defects. While surgical treatment (such as C1-C2 fusion) can provide stability, it sacrifices 50% of the rotational function of the cervical vertebrae and may accelerate the degeneration of adjacent segments [7]. In contrast, the combination of exercise therapy and occipito-maxillary band traction avoids permanent movement limitations caused by surgery, and is particularly suitable for adolescents or patients who need to preserve cervical spine range of motion. The potential mechanism of exercise therapy combined with occipito-maxillary band traction treatment, on the one hand, forms a dynamic stability system by activating the suboccipital muscle groups and superficial muscle groups (such as trapezius and sternocleidomastoid muscles) [8]. On the other hand, axial tension is used to reduce the pressure exerted by the atlas on the odontoid process, promoting joint repositioning. The combination of exercise therapy and occipito-maxillary band traction treatment also plays a role in neuromuscular remodeling. By applying multiple elements, it maximizes its therapeutic effect in motor control training, including rope height, vibration and posture [6]. When performing suspension training, one or more areas of the patient's body are fully supported by specially designed ropes, allowing the patient to use their own body weight as resistance for training and freeing the therapist's hands for manual guidance. Therapists can apply controlled vibrations to selected body areas by manually tapping the rope or using a vibration device. It will create a persistent unstable state, exerting greater pressure on the neuromuscular system, thereby triggering posture correction strategies and inducing high levels of neuromuscular stimulation, which may enhance balance ability [9].

Existing literature generally supports the reduction effect of occipito-maxillary band traction on atlantoaxial subluxation, but it mostly focuses on short-term anatomical improvement. This study innovatively confirmed that the combination of exercise therapy and occipito-maxillary band traction can maintain the reduction effect for a longer time.The reason lies in dealing with the suboccipital muscle group that has the greatest impact on the atlantoaxial joint. Manual stretching and relaxation are carried out on it, followed by resistance training to enhance muscle strength. Finally, stability training and proprioceptive stimulation are conducted to improve neuromuscular control. The difference between this and simple manual reduction lies in that it not only restores the alignment and alignment of the joint, but also further strengthens the muscle strength and further improves the neuromuscular control of the joint. Thus, the patient avoided surgical treatment, as well as the surgical risks and possible adverse effects such as joint stiffness after the operation. However, this protocol should be used with caution in patients with acute traumatic dislocation or severe ligament laxity. The present study is limited by the retrospective nature of the data available. Moreover, the research type affects the power and significance of the findings. In the future, it is necessary to further verify its efficacy through multi-center RCT studies (such as comparing surgery with combination therapy), and deeply explore the treatment mechanism to establish a more complete clinical application guideline.

From a neurosurgical perspective, the distinction between true atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation (AARS) due to ligamentous laxity and functional torticollis secondary to suboccipital muscle dysfunction is critical, as it directly determines whether non-operative management is appropriate. In similar cases of AARS (particularly Type I or II in the Fielding and Hawkins classification), successful non-surgical reduction has been reported; however, recurrence remains high when passive reduction alone is employed without addressing neuromuscular control. The present case highlights a key clinical differentiator: instability is characterized by pathological hypermobility of the atlantoaxial joint due to laxity of the transverse and alar ligaments and joint capsule, leading to recurrent rotatory subluxation that is difficult to maintain in a reduced position, with dynamic X-rays confirming instability. In contrast, muscle dysfunction presents with postural rotational torticollis arising from neck muscle spasms and imbalance, yet the atlantoaxial joint structure remains stable with no pathological ligamentous laxity, and the deformity improves significantly after muscle relaxation. Our protocol—combining manual stretching, resistance training, and proprioceptive stimulation—specifically targets the latter mechanism, thereby avoiding unnecessary surgical fusion while achieving durable reduction. This differentiation is especially relevant in neurosurgical practice, where over-reliance on imaging without functional assessment of muscle recruitment may lead to overtreatment.

Clinical inspiration In daily life: people's use of electronic products such as mobile phones, ipads and computers is gradually increasing. Some people do not pay attention to maintaining a good posture, such as lying down to look at their mobile phones, which can lead to instability of the atlanto-axial joint. Although it has not reached the degree of subluxation, symptoms such as dizziness and headache still occur. X-ray examination of the open-mouth position of the cervical vertebrae will find that the axons of the axial vertebrae and the bilateral lumps are not at equal distance. The treatment plan can also be carried out in reference to this case. We have treated many such patients according to the treatment ideas discussed in this article, and the results have been satisfactory. If one is unaware of the commonality of this situation and focuses on the many other causes that lead to headaches and dizziness, it will result in a waste of a large amount of medical resources and delays in treatment. Therefore, the patient's visit and treatment experience in this case are very inspiring for clinical work and can be promoted and applied.

Conclusion

We report a case of a patient with persistent dizziness caused by atlantoaxial sublation successfully treated with exercise therapy combined with occipito-maxillary band traction. This treatment, through occipito-maxillary band traction reduction and suboccipital muscle group reinforcement training, not only improved joint alignment but also restored dynamic stability, completely alleviating the patient's symptoms and avoiding surgery. This case suggests that for patients with atlantoaxial subluxation who meet the indications, this combined therapy can be used as an alternative to surgery. Its core value lies in the integration of biomechanical correction and neuromuscular function remodeling. In the future, more cases need to be accumulated to identify the optimal suitable population, optimize treatment parameters, and establish a standardized evaluation system.
