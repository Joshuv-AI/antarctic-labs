Development of eco-friendly HPC using advanced hybrid multi-scale basalt fibers and ground mining materials
Authors: Mohamed A. Daghash, Osama Youssf, Mohamed M. Yousry Elshikh, Mahmoud Elshahawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent decades, high performance concrete (HPC) has gained the attention of concrete industry due to its high strength and durability. However, the high consumption of manufactured fibers and Portland cement in HPC raised a high concern about its suitability. Accordingly, this research has been designed to investigate the effect of using hybrid multi-scale basalt fibers as natural fibers, in addition to ground mining materials as Portland cement partial replacement in the production of eco-friendly HPC. The basalt fibers were used at different contents of 1, 2, and 3% by weight of cementitious materials, with fiber length gradation examined across three hybridization levels incorporating four fiber lengths ranging from macro (30 mm) to micro (3 mm). The partial replacement of cement was conducted using eco-friendly ground mining materials namely, metakaolin (MK), silica flour (SF), and ground feldspar (FLs) at various replacement ratios. Several mechanical properties were measured such as; compressive strength, splitting tensile strength, flexural strength, and elasticity modulus. The results demonstrate that increasing the level of fiber hybridization and combining multiple basalt fiber lengths significantly enhanced the mechanical properties compared to mono-size fiber systems, resulting in HPC compressive, tensile, and flexural strengths increases of over 4%, 8.5%, and 6%, respectively, at the highest hybridization level. Moreover, the usage of eco-friendly supplementary cementitious materials in HPC helped in achieving better mechanical properties, denser microstructures, and better ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) values, which in turn aided in the development of eco-friendly HPC with better overall performance. The optimal content of MK and SF was 10%, while that of FLs was 5%, which was based on the mechanical performance, UPV, microstructure, and both environmental and economic assessment of HPC. This study has provided a more sustainable HPC with better mechanical performance, lower material cost with favorable environmental impact, and better eco-efficiency.

Introduction

Overview

High Performance Concrete (HPC) is an advanced concrete with superior properties. During the past few decades, HPC has been found to be a major breakthrough with high strength, durability, and resistance to aggressive environments1,2. Due to these superior characteristics, HPC is employed in critical structures such as high-rise buildings, highway pavements, infrastructures, and airport runway under extremely high loads or demanding environments3,4. However, despite its excellent compressive strength of approximately (100 MPa), HPC is still brittle under tension, which is a serious problem in tensile and cracking performance5–7. Because of its brittleness, the cracking process in this concrete type is very quick, resulting in an instantaneous failure. Thus, the reinforcement of this concrete material with fibers is necessary to improve its tensile behavior and toughness capacity. Different types of fibers, including steel, polypropylene, and glass fibers, have been widely studied in the literature. Although these categories of fibers are very effective in improving tensile strength and arresting cracks, they have some serious limitations and drawbacks8,9, including low fire resistance, corrosion, rust, and poor alkali resistance. Additionally, because of their synthetic nature, they do not meet the sustainability criteria. Accordingly, numerous studies have been carried out on the usage of basalt fibers (BF) as a durable, sustainable, and natural fibers type10.

BF have proved to be a sustainable and eco-friendly alternative for concrete reinforcement. The natural basalt rock, with no need for chemical treatment, provides these fibers with superior tensile strength and good thermal stability. Its nature also enables it to provide an excellent resistance to chemicals and corrosion11. Additionally, BF have been proven to offer better environmental and electrical resistance properties than other types of synthetic fibers12. Moreover, BF also can be distributed very well within the concrete matrix. Therefore, they offer a good solution to improve tensile and bending behavior besides sustainability goals. Fibers increase the tensile strength of concrete and effectively bridge the developing cracks. Cracks originate from microdefects and grow due to tension, leading to reduced concrete integrity. Microfibers can control early microcracks, while macrofibres can improve ductility in larger cracks; thus, the combination of both can improve resistance to cracks of various sizes13.

At the Recent time, many studies have been conducted on HPC reinforced with BF due to their superior properties. The results show that the addition of basalt fibers to HPC not only improved the mechanical properties but also the energy absorption capacity under high impact loading14,15. Some authors also showed that the hybridization of BF with other types can improve the overall performance of concrete16–18. Therefore, the addition of BF, whether in mono or hybrid forms, has been reported to be effective in improving both the sustainability and mechanical properties of HPC. There have been studies on the use of multiple fiber lengths in concrete. They all show that multi length hybridization is more effective than mono size fibers configuration. These is because of the formation of a multilevel fibers network that can effectively resist the propagation of cracks19,20. Most of the previous studies were carried out on the hybridization of two different fiber lengths, while studies on more than two lengths of the same fiber type are very rare, especially for BF21,22. Moreover, besides the fact that the number of existing studies on multiscale BF is very limited, they only focus on low fiber volume fractions without considering the hybridization for medium or high fractions, which indicates a research gap in this scope of study. This emphasizes that the hybridization of multiscale fibers is a developing area and has a promising future, especially in HPC.

The concept of sustainability in structural engineering has attracted the interest of using recycled and industrial wasted materials, such as crumb rubber23,24, granite and ceramic powders25, biochar26, as well as mining powders such as metakaolin (MK)27, silica flour (SF), and ground feldspar (FLs)28. These materials contain a certain amount of pozzolanic compounds that contribute to strengthening the concrete matrix, making them excellent supplementary materials. These supplementary materials not only serve as partial substitutes for cement but also serve as eco-friendly admixtures, thereby contributing to substantial reductions in emissions of carbon29, given that a ton of CO2 is emitted due to the production of one ton of cement, while also improving concrete microstructure, enhancing durability, reducing permeability, and mitigating costs30,31. Of these supplementary materials, MK, which is obtained by the calcination of kaolin clay at 650–800 °C, is an amorphous aluminosilicate with high silica 50–55% and alumina 40–45% contents32. The pozzolanic reaction of MK with calcium hydroxide in concrete results in the production of supplementary calcium silicate hydrates, thereby making the concrete microstructure denser, reducing porosity, and improving durability and strength33,34. Elwardany et al.35 have reported that MK can be used as a partial substitute for cement up to 20% to enhance the concrete behavior without negative effect. A recent study conducted by Imoh et al.36 showed that the most significant improvements in all mechanical performance and concrete microstructure occurred at approximately 10% MK replacement. Likewise, SF, an ultrafine powder produced through the grinding of high-purity silica sand with a composition of about 99% SiO2 and a microstructure of crystalline particles37, acts as a partially pozzolanic material and micro-filler. Its addition to the concrete paste makes the matrix more refined, improves compressive strength, and also takes part in pozzolanic reactions that further enhance durability. A study conducted by Ramdani et al.38 illustrated that 15% SF could effectively enhance the mechanical performance and microstructure of concrete. Li et al.39 investigated the synergetic effect of SF and silica fume (Sfu), and observed that incorporating Sfu and SF at a 10% replacement ratio significantly improved the permeability resistance and enhanced the early-age compressive performance of HPC. Apart from the above-mentioned silica-based materials, FLs also provides an alternate source of aluminosilicate material with a composition of about 65–72% silica and 15–20% alumina. When used as a fine filler or as a partial substitute for cement, feldspar improves particle packing, concrete microstructure, and compressive strength28,40,41. Nevertheless, unlike MK and SF, its higher alkali content may necessitate pre-treatment to avoid the possibility of alkali-silica reactions42,43. Recently, FLs has attracted the attention from researchers for its application in concrete due to its low carbon footprint and low cost compared to cement. Qu et al.44 found that the best performance of mechanical properties, concrete density, and water absorption rate has been achieved at 6% FLs dosage. Rashad et al.45 evaluated the influence of FLs usage and found that the optimal cement replacement ratio was 10%, providing the best performance of mechanical properties, abrasion wear, water permeability, and drying shrinkage of concrete.

Despite their advantages, systematic research on the integration of these materials in HPC is still very limited. Hence, the development of sustainable concrete mixtures is a high priority not only for the reduction of environmental and material costs but also for the improvement of long-term performance. It is worth noting that the combination of cement replacement materials in a matrix already reinforced with BF is a very promising approach for the simultaneous improvement of mechanical properties and durability. In fact, synergistic influences between BF and supplementary materials such as MK and industrial mineral powders have been observed, which have a significant effect on the improvement of structural properties of the composite10,46–48.

Research gap

Despite extensive research on BF-reinforced HPC, most studies have primarily focused on either mono-sized fibers or limited hybridization using at most two fiber lengths, without implementing a truly engineered hierarchical system based on three to four systematically distributed length scales from macro to micro. Moreover, studies on supplementary cementitious materials, such as MK, SF, and FLs, remain significantly limited in HPC, with each material typically investigated in isolation rather than as part of an integrated system. Furthermore, to the authors’ knowledge, previous research has treated the hybridization of multi-scale BF and cement replacement independently without a unified engineering framework combining hierarchical fiber systems and partial cement replacement within the same concrete matrix. This represents a clear research gap that calls for an integrated, design-oriented approach to investigate the effect of the interaction between graded BF and matrix modification for enhanced mechanical and sustainable performance. Accordingly, the objectives of this research are to: (1) explore the effect of BF addition to HPC by analyzing the fiber ratio of 1%, 2%, and 3% by weight of cementitious materials, (2) investigate the impact of BF length gradation starting from macro mono-sized fibers to a compilation of four fiber lengths across three levels of hybridization on the mechanical properties of HPC, and (3) investigate the sustainability improvement of HPC by replacing cement with three mining-based materials (MK, SF, and FLs) as green supplementary materials, and assess their combined impact with hybrid BF systems on the mechanical properties of HPC. By combining the hybrid BF systems with sustainable approaches to cement replacement, this research aims to produce eco-friendly HPC with improved mechanical behavior, environmental impact, and sustainability.

Experimental program

Research framework

The main objective of this research is to develop HPC with highly sustainable system through enhancing its overall mechanical behavior, microstructure, and environmental and economic impact using sustainable materials such as BF and eco-friendly ground mining materials, namely SF, FLs, and MK. This study is designed as two-stages interconnected research framework. In the first stage, the research focuses on studying the influence of hierarchical BF hybridization approach with multiple lengths, starting from a macro mono-size configuration and progressively moving through several hybridization levels with increasing the incorporated fiber lengths till reaching the micro-size BF, to produce a concrete matrix reinforced with fully hybrid multi-scale BF. The rationale is to integrate the distinct roles of each fiber scale. Larger fibers primarily enhance crack-bridging and load transfer, while smaller fibers are more effective in controlling micro-crack initiation and propagation. Subsequently, the hybrid fiber configuration that exhibits the best mechanical performance is then selected to be the control mix of the second stage. In the second stage, the cement is partially replaced with the selected supplementary materials at specified ratios of replacement. These materials are introduced to enhance the microstructure, improve matrix densification, and produce a more sustainable and cost-effective concrete system. The mechanical properties, ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV), microstructure, as well as environmental and economic performance of concrete are then evaluated. Through this framework, this study integrates multi-scale BF with sustainable binder modification to create HPC mixtures which are not only mechanically superior but also environmentally-friendly and economically viable.

Materials and mix design

To study the combined effect of graded BF and partial cement replacement with chosen eco-friendly ground mining materials on the performance of HPC, a total of 22 mix designs were prepared. The mix designs were systematically classified into two different categories. Group A was planned to study the effect of BF gradation at 1%, 2%, and 3% by weight of the cementitious materials, while Group B was planned to study the effect of cement replacement materials at 5, 10, and 15% by volume of the cement content. Previous research demonstrated that high fiber content can significantly enhance tensile and flexural strengths, while excessive dosages may decrease the compressive strength due to voids induced by fiber agglomeration. Therefore, the selected BF contents were chosen to achieve a balanced mechanical performance between compressive, tensile, and flexural characteristics. Regarding cement replacement materials, numerous studies reported that optimal mechanical performance can be achieved within a replacement ratio range of 10–20%. Excessively high dosages may improve economic and environmental efficiency of concrete; however, the mechanical behavior may decrease. Accordingly, the selected replacement ratios were carefully chosen to balance the mechanical performance, cost efficiency, and environmental sustainability. The notation of Group A mixes is defined according to the BF content and the level of fiber hybridization. The levels of hybridization (HY) were categorized in the following manner: HY0 is a mono-size system with a single fiber length and there is no hybridization, HY1 is a single level of hybridization with two fiber sizes, HY2 is two levels of hybridization with a combination of three fiber sizes, and HY3 is three levels of hybridization with the incorporation of four different fiber sizes. For example, the notation BF15-HY3 stands for a mixture of 15 kg/m3 of BF with the third level of hybridization (HY3) that incorporates four different fiber sizes. In contrast, the notation of Group B mixes is defined according to the type of cement replacement material and its corresponding replacement percentage. For example, 5%SF, 10%FLs, and 15%MK refer to 5%, 10%, and 15% cement replacement levels using SF, FLs, and MK, respectively. For Group A mixtures, CM was adopted as the control mix, whereas BF5–HY3 (CM2) was selected as the control mix for Group B. Accordingly, Group B mixtures were developed from BF5–HY3 (CM2) mix, which contains 5 kg/m3 of BF at the third hybridization level incorporating four different fiber sizes.

In this study, Portland cement (CEM I 52.5N) and silica fume (Sfu), with specific gravities of 3.15 and 2.2, respectively, were employed as the primary binders in all HPC mixes. The Sfu content was kept constant at 50 kg/m3 for all mixtures. Cement content was kept constant at 450 kg/m3 in the control and 12 fiber-reinforced mixtures, with fiber dosage and hybrid configuration as variables, and adjusted only in the mixes incorporating partial cement replacement. Three mining-derived powders (SF, MK, and potassic FLs) were incorporated, with specific gravities of 2.65, 2.60, and 2.58, respectively. The potassic FLs was selected due to its comparatively low alkali content to minimize the risk of alkali–silica reaction. Both the FLs and SF were sourced from local quarries and companies in Egypt. In contrast, MK was produced through high-temperature calcination of raw kaolin. A high-performance superplasticizer-SP (Vesocrete 3425), produced by Sika company, was incorporated in all HPC mixtures. This admixture conforms to ASTM C494/C494M49 Types F and G and BS EN 934-2:200950. The SP, with a specific gravity of 1.08, was used at a constant dosage of 2% by weight of the primary cementitious materials (10 kg/m3), and a constant amount of potable mixing water (150 kg/m3) was maintained across all mixes.

Natural sand was employed as the fine aggregate and crushed dolomite as the coarse aggregate in this study. The sand had a fineness modulus of 2.85 and a specific gravity of 2.6, while the dolomite had a nominal size of 4–12.5 mm and a specific gravity of 2.7. Both aggregates were clean and free of chlorides or other harmful substances. In the control mix CM, the sand content was 625 kg/m3, adjusted in other mixes to account for different fiber dosages added at the expense of an equivalent sand volume. The dolomite content was kept constant at 1075 kg/m3, providing a stable aggregate framework to support the targeted mechanical properties of the HPC mixtures. Both the coarse and fine aggregates were verified to conform to the requirements of ASTM C33/C33M51 for particle size distribution. Mixing procedures and curing method

The casting procedure of the concrete mixtures was carefully designed to ensure uniformity and optimal performance. The aggregates and all of the cementitious materials were initially mixed in a dry case for 4 min in a pan mixer. After that, half of the used water and all of the dosage of SP were added, and the mix was mixed for an additional 3 min to obtain initial homogeneity. Finally, the remaining water quantity as well as BF were added gradually. For concrete mixes with multiple lengths of BF, the fibers were added one by one, starting from the shortest length to the longest one, with complete mixing after each fiber addition to avoid fiber agglomeration and achieve optimal distribution of all fiber lengths within the concrete mix. The concrete mix was further mixed for an additional 3 min after adding all fibers content to further improve the homogeneity of the mix. The concrete mix was carefully monitored during the mixing and the addition of fibers to avoid clumping and to maintain a homogeneous mix. The fresh concrete mix was then poured in molds and compacted using a vibrating table to achieve optimal consolidation. Subsequently, the specimens were left in the molds for 24 h prior to demolding and cured in water at ambient temperature around of 20 °C and humidity of 95% until the testing date. Tests procedures

To evaluate the performance of HPC mixtures effectively, a number of tests were carried out to determine their properties in both the fresh and hardened states. The workability of the HPC mixtures was first determined by the slump test, carried out in accordance with ASTM C14352, using a standard slump cone with a base diameter of 200 mm, top diameter of 100 mm, and a height of 300 mm as shown in Fig. 5. After determining the properties in the fresh state, the mechanical properties of the hardened concrete were determined. The compressive strength test was carried out on cubic specimens of 100 × 100 × 100 mm3 at 7, 28, and 56 days of age, using a compression machine in accordance with BS EN 12,390-353. To further determine the tensile properties, the splitting tensile strength test was carried out on cylindrical specimens of 100 × 200 mm (diameter × height) at 28 days, in accordance with ASTM C496/C496M54. Moreover, the flexural strength was also measured on prismatic specimens of dimensions 100 × 100 × 500 mm3 at 28 days by the three-point loading method, as per ASTM C78/C78M55. Finally, the elasticity modulus was also measured on cylindrical specimens of dimensions 150 × 300 mm (diameter × height) at 28 days by the hydraulic compression machine at a loading rate of 0.25 MPa/s, as per ASTM C46956, giving insight into the stiffness properties of HPC mixes.Fig. 5Slump values: (a) control mix (CM) without fibers; (b) BF5-HY0 mix incorporating 5 kg/m3 mono-sized BF.

Slump values: (a) control mix (CM) without fibers; (b) BF5-HY0 mix incorporating 5 kg/m3 mono-sized BF.

Apart from the above-mentioned mechanical performance tests, other tests were also carried out to complement the analysis of the developed HPC mixes. UPV and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) tests were conducted to gain more information about the internal integrity and microstructure of the concrete. UPV test was carried out on the dried cylindrical specimens of size 150 × 300 mm and 100 × 200 mm, which were the same specimens used for splitting tensile and modulus of elasticity tests, as per ASTM C59757. SEM analysis was carried out on samples taken from selected mixes to support and validate the results of the previous mechanical tests. Prior to SEM examination, each sample was coated with a thin gold layer of 12 nm, specifically applied to prepare the samples for this type of analysis. The mixes here were selected based on their optimal and critical results from both compressive strength and UPV tests. The testing forms of the hardened concrete are illustrated in Fig. 6. Experimental results.

Fresh concrete

The slump test results clearly demonstrate that both BF content and length gradation have a pronounced effect on the workability of HPC, as illustrated in Fig. 7. For mixtures reinforced with mono-size macro BF without hybridization (HY0), workability decreased steeply as fiber content increased. Compared with the control mix CM, slump reductions of 20%, 80%, and 88% were reported at fiber contents of 5, 10, and 15 kg/m3, respectively. For fibers at higher contents of 10 and 15 kg/m3, the matrix of HPC was found to be highly crowded and saturated with fibers. This resulted in substantial loss of workability, while at 5 kg/m3 dosage, the matrix was unsaturated relatively. This loss can be attributed to the micrometric diameter of used BF and its large length, which result in increased water attraction to the fiber surface, increased mixture viscosity, and consequent slump loss58. At low BF content of 5 kg/m3, there was a limited impact of the hybridization of fibers, see Fig. 8a. There was only 5%, 10%, and 5% slump loss for HY1, HY2, and HY3, respectively, compared to the mono-size configuration (HY0). These small differences show that at low fiber doses, fiber interlocking and matrix saturation are very limited, and hence fiber length distribution has a marginal effect on workability results. Conversely, the effect of fiber hybridization became more decisive with higher fiber contents. For mixtures with 10 kg/m3 of BF, the slump values showed an increase of 70%, 80%, and 180% for HY1, HY2, and HY3, respectively, compared to HY0. Similar observations were made at 15 kg/m3 dosage, where the workability improvement was 100%, 133%, and 150%. The reason for this enhancement is the better distribution and packing of the fibers with different lengths, which results in less agglomeration of excessive fibers and better rheological properties of the fresh concrete. In general, these results confirm that the hybridization of BF is a crucial factor in maintaining acceptable workability at high fiber contents, while its effect can be neglected at low fiber contents.Fig. 7The slump values of HPC mixes.Fig. 8The effect of: (a) BF hybridization; and (b) cement replacement materials on HPC slump values.

The slump values of HPC mixes.

The effect of: (a) BF hybridization; and (b) cement replacement materials on HPC slump values.

In the case of cement replacement materials, the effect of these materials on workability was highly dependent on the type of fine powder as depicted in Fig. 8b. For MK, the 5% replacement showed that there wasn’t noticeable effect, while the replacement of 10% and 15% showed a decrease in slump of 2.63% and 7.89%, respectively, compared to the second control mix BF5-HY3 (CM2). This is primarily due to the high fineness, angular shape of particles, and high specific surface area of MK, which results in increased water demand and decreased flowability33. For SF, the workability was consistently improved, and the rate of improvement increased with the increase in replacement levels. Compared to CM2 mix, the increase in slump was 5.26%, 7.89%, and 15.79% for 5%, 10%, and 15% replacement levels, respectively. These improvements are primarily due to the filler effect and the uniformity of particle size distribution as reported in Fig. 1. In addition, the close-to-spherical shape of SF particles is expected to function similarly to a ball bearing, making it easier for particles to slide, thus further improving workability. Potassic FLs showed a moderate performance. There was no apparent effect at the 5% replacement level, while moderate improvements of 5.26% and 7.89% were achieved at replacement levels of 10% and 15%, respectively, relative to the CM2 mix. This can be attributed to its low pozzolanic activity and inert filler properties, which improve flow while requiring no significant increase in water demand. Its particle shape, being relatively close to that of SF, could also help improve particle sliding in the mix. Based on the above results, it can be concluded that the workability of fine cement replacement materials is primarily dependent on their physical properties. While MK showed a negative effect at higher levels of replacement, SF showed the most positive effect, and potassic FLs showed a moderate improvement in the flowability of fresh concrete.

Hardened concrete

Compressive strength

The compressive strength of HPC mixtures was determined at 7, 28, and 56 days to investigate the influence of BF content, level of fiber hybridization, and type and dosage of cement replacement materials using mining powders. The compressive strength results are shown in Table 5 and Fig. 9. For the mono-size BF (HY0), the addition of 5, 10, and 15 kg/m3 contents showed strength gain of 5.31%, 7.38%, and 2.07%, respectively, at 28 days compared to the first control mixture (CM). These findings are in line with the previous study15. The higher enhancements at 5 and 10 kg/m3 contents of BF addition show the effective fiber reinforcement, whereas the lower enhancement at 15 kg/m3 is attributed to the saturation of the matrix, high interlocking of fibers, void formation, and substantial slump loss, which resulted in limited compaction59.Fig. 9Compressive strength of HPC mixes (Groups A and B).

Compressive strength of HPC mixes (Groups A and B).

Figure 10a–c indicate that the effect of fiber hybridization with varying lengths further contributed to the compressive strength, which showed similar trends at 7, 28, and 56 days. At the lowest BF content (5 kg/m3), HY1 showed a slight decrease in strength by 0.62% at 28 days relative to the mono-size condition (HY0), which can be attributed to the lack of fiber dispersion. However, HY2 and HY3 showed a gradual increase in strength reaching 1.48% for HY3 compared to HY0. At higher BF dosages of 10 and 15 kg/m3, the compressive strength showed a continuous increase with the level of hybridization, from 1.45% to 3.38% and from 2.54% to 4.44% for 10 and 15 kg/m3 contents, respectively, from HY1 to HY3. This progressive enhancement is in the same line with the reported findings in the previous literature21. The compressive strength enhancement is related to the developed multi-scale system of crack arresting within the concrete matrix. Compression failure is initiated by distributed microcracks that slowly grow and merge into unstable macrocracks. Systematically graded BF were incorporated to control cracking at different scales. Short fibers inhibited microcrack initiation, intermediate fibers restricted crack propagation, and long fibers bridged larger cracks and delayed their localization. The integration of multi-scale fibers reduced crack connectivity, improved stress redistribution and matrix confinement, resulting in a denser and more stable internal structure22. Therefore, the hybrid system HY3 showed the best compressive performance with its excellent ability of suppressing the damage evolution from micro to macro scale. Overall, the hybridization of BF is most effective at higher contents, while lower contents require a well-developed network of fiber hybridization to improve the strength significantly.Fig. 10The effect of: (a–c) BF hybridization; and (d–f) cement replacement materials on the compressive strength of HPC at 7, 28, and 56 days, respectively.

The influence of partial replacement of cement depends on the type of material used, level of replacement, and age of curing, as depicted in Fig. 10d–f. At the early age (7 days), the compressive strength is mainly influenced by the physical effects of replacement materials, see Fig. 10d. SF increased the early-age strength at 5%, 10%, and 15% replacement levels by 1.99%, 4.89%, and 4.74%, respectively, relative to the second control mix (BF5-HY3 (CM2)). This can be ascribed to its crystalline nature, micro-filler effect, and relatively uniform particle size, which improve the packing of particles and decrease the initial capillary porosity38. MK showed a slight increase in early-age strength, as its pozzolanic reaction has not yet been fully activated. FLs decreased the early-age strength at all levels of cement replacement because of its inert property and the dilution effect.

At 28 days, as illustrated in Fig. 10e, a combination of both physical and chemical effects is responsible for strength gain. SF recorded the highest degree of improvement (by 11%) at replacement level of 10% compared to CM2, which can be attributed to the optimal packing of particles, pore refinement, and enhanced matrix continuity. At SF replacement level of 15%, the degree of improvement slightly reduced, indicating that the limit of dilution was being reached. MK recorded positive effects at 28 days, especially at replacement level of 10% (by 8.85%) relative to the CM2 mix, due to the progressive pozzolanic reaction, which resulted in the production of more C–S–H and refinement of pores60. At 15% replacement level of MK, the degree of improvement was slightly reduced, indicating that beyond a certain level of replacement, calcium hydroxide is not available in adequate quantities. FLs recorded a very low degree of improvement at 5% replacement level (by 3.15%) compared to CM2, but reduced further with increased replacement levels. This is due to its low pozzolanic activity.

At 56 days, Fig. 10f presents that the long-term behavior is dominated by the continuous process of chemical reactions and microstructural evolution. MK showed the highest degree of improvement of 10.13% at 10% replacement over CM2, indicating continuous pozzolanic activity and enhanced matrix density. Even at 15% replacement, MK showed positive improvement of 5.76% over CM2, indicating its effectiveness in the long term at optimal levels. SF showed continuous improvement, reaching a maximum of 9.59% at 10% replacement compared to the CM2 mix, due to physical densification. Splitting tensile strength

The outcome of the 28-day tensile strength, as presented in Fig. 11 and Table 5 clearly indicates the combined impact of BF dosage, fiber length distribution, and partial replacement of cement on the tensile performance of HPC. In the case of mono-sized fiber mixtures (HY0), the increase in BF content from 5 to 10 kg/m3 resulted in a substantial improvement in tensile strength from 12.7% to 25.1% compared to the control mix (CM). This can be due to the increased number of effective fiber bridges, which improve the transfer of stresses and resist crack opening14. However, further increase in BF dosage to 15 kg/m3 led to a slight decrease in the improvement to 22.9%, primarily due to fiber entanglement and reduced workability.Fig. 11The tensile & flexural strengths of HPC mixes.

The tensile & flexural strengths of HPC mixes.

Introducing fiber gradation (HY1, HY2, and HY3), as depicted in Fig. 12a, resulted in an overall supplementary increase in tensile strength compared to the mono-sized case (HY0). At a relatively low content of BF of 5 kg/m3 dosage, the effect of fiber gradation was relatively insignificant (0.49% to 2.95%) relative to HY0. This is because of the relatively low fiber density, which hinders the effective utilization of multi-scale crack-bridging process. In contrast, the HPC mixes containing relatively higher dosages of BF (10 and 15 kg/m3 contents) showed a relatively stronger response to the gradation of fiber. For mixes containing 10 kg/m3 dosage of BF, the effect of fiber gradation was more significant, with increments increasing from 2.1% at HY1 to 5.5% at HY3, whereas mixes containing 15 kg/m3 BF showed the strongest response, with increments increasing from 3.7% to 8.9% compared to HY0. This progressive trend in tensile behavior is in agree with that of previous study20. This consistent enhancement is mainly due to the integrated crack-bridging effect of multi-length fiber network. Controlling cracks at multiple scales is critical since the occurrence of tensile failure because of the initiation and rapid growth of a crack. Microfibers reduce stress concentration and delay microcracks generation, while intermediate and long fibers bridge the growing cracks and resist the widening of cracks. Moreover, the fiber pull-out mechanism plays an important role in energy dissipation and in delaying the debonding between fibers and the matrix. Multi-scale interaction improved fiber engagement efficiency, delayed crack localization, and promoted more uniform stress distribution within the matrix. Therefore, HY3 hybrid system has controlled the whole crack evolution process to achieve the best tensile performance.Fig. 12The effect of: (a) BF hybridization; and (b) cement replacement materials on HPC tensile strength.

The effect of: (a) BF hybridization; and (b) cement replacement materials on HPC tensile strength.

The effect of partial cement replacements with supplementary fine materials was studied at 5%, 10%, and 15% replacement ratios compared to the second control mix (CM2), see Fig. 12b. At a 5% replacement ratio, all materials showed a small increase in tensile strength, with SF showing the greatest increase of 5.9%, followed by MK with an increase of 4.6%, whereas FLs reached its most optimal performance at this percentage with an increase of 4.1% compared to the CM2 mix. The increasing in replacement level to 10% resulted in the optimal performance of SF with an increase of 11.3%, primarily because of its high density of packing and pore-filling capacity, which resulted in an improvement in the continuity of the matrix and the enhancement of the interfacial bond between HPC matrix and BF surface. MK also showed a significant increase of 9.5%, which can be attributed to its pozzolanic properties.

On the other hand, increasing the level of replacement to 15% tended to decrease the tensile strength in general, especially for FLs, which was lower than the CM2 mix. This is due to the over dilution of cement content and lack of reactivity to make up for the reduced cement content. In general, these findings support that the success of fine material substitution is highly dependent on the replacement ratio, where silica flour and MK are best at 10%, while FLs is better at lower replacement ratios.

Flexural strength

The results of flexural strength at 28 days shown in Fig. 11 clearly demonstrate the combined impacts of BF dosage, hybridization of fiber length, and the partial replacement of cement with fine materials on the bending behavior of HPC. The mono-sized BF (HY0) addition resulted in an improvement in flexural strength, and the mix containing 5 kg/m3 BF dosage demonstrated an increase of 14.7% compared to the control mix (CM). The maximum strength was attained at dosage of 10 kg/m3, with a 27.7% improvement over the CM mix. However, with further increase in BF dosage to 15 kg/m3, the improvement was slightly reduced to 26.4% compared to CM, which can be attributed to the reduced workability and increased porosity at the fiber surface, thus affecting the efficiency of load transfer across the cracks62.

The implementation of BF hybridization (HY1–HY3), as depicted in Fig. 13a, resulted in a consistent improvement in flexural strength for all fiber contents compared to their respective mono-sized mixes (HY0). Although the effect of fiber gradation was not very significant at lower fiber dosages of 5 kg/m3, where the improvement was in the range of 0.76% to 3.18% compared to HY0, this is due to the low fiber concentration, which is insufficient to effectively trigger multi-scale crack-bridging actions. On the other hand, for mixes with higher BF contents of 10 and 15 kg/m3, the effect was more pronounced. For the fiber dosage of 10 kg/m3, the improvement in flexural strength showed an increasing trend from 1.5% in HY1 to 4.7% in HY3, whereas the maximum improvement in flexural strength was observed for BF content of 15 kg/m3, which increased from 3.12% to 6.35% compared to HY0. The increase in fiber hybridization improves the flexural performance because of the effective multi-scale crack control. The combination of short, intermediate and long BF reduces microcracking, stabilizes crack growth and bridges larger cracks, and improves crack distribution and stress redistribution. In general, the flexural performance shows the same trend as the tensile performance. The HY3 hybrid system gives the best results due to its better crack-bridging efficiency. Even though the initial flexural strength of 10 kg/m3 mono-sized BF (HY0) was higher than that of 15 kg/m3, the application of fiber hybridization (HY1-HY3) resulted in all 15 kg/m3 mixtures performing better than the 10 kg/m3 mixtures, as depicted in Fig. 11. This further verifies that fiber hybridization overcomes the drawbacks of macro fibers by appropriately allocating graded fibers to withstand the applied load63.Fig. 13The effect of: (a) BF hybridization; and (b) cement replacement materials on HPC flexural strength.

The effect of: (a) BF hybridization; and (b) cement replacement materials on HPC flexural strength.

On the other hand, as shown in Fig. 13b, the impact of partial replacement of cement with supplementary materials was evaluated at 5%, 10%, and 15% compared to the second control mix (CM2). At cement replacement level of 5%, all materials showed a significant increase in flexural strength. SF showed the greatest increase by 7% compared to CM2, followed by MK (6.3%), while FLs showed its optimal contribution at this low replacement percentage, increasing the flexural strength by 5.2%. The increase in the replacement ratio to 10% led to the optimal performance of the reactive and micro-filler materials. SF showed the greatest increase by 13.2% relative to CM2, mainly because of its high particle packing and the capability of pore-filling, which improved the continuity of the HPC matrix and enhanced the bond between the fibers and the matrix. MK showed a substantial increase at this percentage by 10.7%, and this is due to its high pozzolanic activity and the production of additional cementitious products that fill the pores in the matrix.

Further increasing the replacement to 15% generally reduced flexural strength. FLs, in particular, performed below the reference mixture CM2, which is related to excessive cement dilution and its limited reactivity, insufficient to compensate for the loss of cementitious phases. Overall, these results indicate that the effectiveness of fine material substitution strongly depends on the replacement ratio, with SF and MK achieving optimal flexural performance at 10%, whereas FLs is most effective at the lower 5% replacement level exactly like the tensile strength. These flexural strength results are consistent with the previously discussed tensile strength findings, indicating that the observed improvements in both tensile and flexural behavior due to fiber gradation and partial cement replacement are closely related.

Elasticity modulus

Figure 14 and Table 5 present the 28-day elastic modulus results, providing insights into the combined effects of BF incorporation, fiber length hybridization, and partial cement replacement with fine materials on the stiffness of high-performance concrete. Initially, the influence of mono-sized macro-BF (HY0) at dosages of 5, 10, and 15 kg/m3 was evaluated against the reference control mixture (CM). Moderate improvements in elastic modulus were observed at low to medium fiber contents, with increases of approximately 4.8% at 5 kg/m3 and 8.1% at 10 kg/m3 relative to CM. However, further increasing the fiber content to 15 kg/m3 resulted in a smaller enhancement of around 1.6%, which is attributed to reduced workability and increased void formation, whereas moderate fiber content contributes to improved matrix cohesion. The decrease in elasticity modulus with a similar fiber ratio was also found in previous studies64. The higher BF content (BF15) significantly reduced workability, increased fiber interlocking, and formed internal voids, thereby decreasing the compressive performance. Therefore, the intermediate dosage (BF10) provides the best balance between matrix integrity and crack control. In contrast, the optimum fibrous-HPC mixture for both tensile and flexural strengths is (BF15-HY3) with the highest fiber content of 15 kg/m3, reflecting the main role of BF in resisting crack propagation. On the other hand, the optimal hybridization configuration for all mechanical properties was HY3 containing the ultimate packing of multi-size fibers using four lengths. This multi-scale fiber network enabled efficient control of cracks from micro to macro scales and improves overall fiber–matrix interaction and stress redistribution.

As shown in Fig. 15b, the effect of partial replacement of cement with fine materials, namely MK, SF, and FLs, was assessed compared to the second control mix BF5-HY3 (CM2). At replacement level of 10%, the highly reactive pozzolans like SF and MK showed significant improvements, with maximum values of 9.7% and 8.3%, respectively, over CM2. This is due to their high chemical reactivity and ability to produce a refined microstructure. On the other hand, FLs showed a slight improvement by 3.4% over the elasticity modulus of CM2 at replacement level of 5%, while higher replacement levels showed decreases by 2.3% at 10% replacement and 6.85% at 15% replacement levels, below the CM2 mix. This can be related to its relatively inert nature and the ability to increase the porosity of the matrix at higher contents. Based on the results, it can be concluded that the maximum replacement level for the elastic modulus is 10% for both SF and MK, while FLs should be used at lower replacement ratios ≤ 5% to prevent the detrimental effects on the stiffness of concrete.

Ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) test

In order to assess the efficiency of the concrete matrix and to support the results of the mechanical results, the UPV test was carried out on all HPC mixes. The UPV test provides a direct indication of the quality of the concrete matrix, allowing for a clear interpretation of the mechanical results. The results of UPV test summarized in Table 5 and Fig. 16 show that the dosage of BF, the level of hybridization, as well as the type and amount of the replacement materials of cement, have a significant effect on the results of UPV test measured at 28 days. For the mixtures containing mono-size BF (HY0), the addition of 5 kg/m3 of BF showed a slight increase in UPV of about 0.76% over the primary control mix (CM). The slight improvement can be related to the poor crack-bridging and moderate fiber distribution at low fiber dosage. But with the increase in fiber content to 10 and 15 kg/m3, the UPV results showed a gradual reduction of about 1.1% and 1.65%, respectively, below the CM value. The reduction in UPV is primarily due to the increased porosity of the matrix because of fiber interlocking65, mainly because of the longer fibers used in the mono-size mixture (30 mm).Fig. 16The ultrasonic pulse velocity UPV of HPC various mixes.

The ultrasonic pulse velocity UPV of HPC various mixes.

With the advent of the hybridization of BF length, a new trend emerged, see Fig. 17a. At fiber content of 5 kg/m3, the systems of hybridization (HY1, HY2, and HY3) exhibited a meager UPV enhancement of 0.10%, 0.20%, and 0.06%, respectively, over the mono size case (HY0). This is consistent with the fact that at lower fiber concentrations, the effect of hybridization is negligible, as the interaction between the fibers is minimal. However, at higher fiber concentrations of 10 and 15 kg/m3, the effect of hybridization became more significant. At BF content of 10 kg/m3, the UPV increased by 0.14%, 0.28%, and 0.93% for HY1, HY2, and HY3, respectively, over HY0. On the other hand, the increment of BF content to 15 kg/m3 enhanced the UPV up to 0.20%, 0.53%, and 0.80%. These values clearly establish that the hybridization of fibers enhances the uniformity of various fiber lengths. With regard to the eco-friendly cement substitutes, the impact on UPV was highly sensitive to the type of material as well as the level of substitution. For SF and MK, Fig. 17b illustrates that the UPV increased steadily at 5% and 10% contents of substitution. UPV increase of 1.87% and 3.31% for SF, and similar increases of 1.7% and 3.1% for MK, respectively, were observed relative to the secondary control mix BF5-HY3 (CM2). In both instances, the optimal level of substitution for UPV was found to be 10%. This can be ascribed to the combined filler action of their ultra fine particles and their pozzolanic action, which increases the formation of C–S–H gel38,66.

However, an increase in the replacement level to 15% resulted in a slight decrease in the enhancement of UPV (2.85% for SF and 2.7% for MK) above CM2. This slight decrement in the UPV improvement is believed to be related to the active cement content dilution and the partial saturation of the pozzolanic reaction. On the other hand, the potassic FLs presented a clear and different trend. The best results of UPV were achieved at a low replacement level of 5% with an improvement of about 1.18% compared to CM2. Further increases in the FLs content to 10% and 15% replacement levels resulted in UPV decreases of 0.18% and 0.74%, respectively, below CM2. This trend is primarily attributed to the relatively low pozzolanic activity of FLs, which restricts the formation of C–S–H gel and consequently leads to a less dense microstructure at higher ratios of cement replacement. Based on the UPV results, it can be confirmed that the hybridization of fiber length is most effective at higher dosages of BF, while reactive replacement materials of cement such as MK and SF are highly effective in enhancing the density of the concrete matrix at optimal levels, unlike FLs, which is most effective at low ratios of cement replacement.

Building on all mechanical properties and UPV findings, the optimal cement replacement ratio for both SF and MK, consistently exhibiting the best performance, is 10%, while that of FLs doesn’t exceed 5%. The optimal dosage for MK in this study is similar to that reported in studies36,67, but contrasts with that reported in35. For SF, the optimal ratio is in line with the literature39, but lower than that reported in38 and higher than that reported in68,69. On the other hand, the optimal ratio related to FLs is consistent with that reported in studies44,70, but lower than that reported in45. These distinctions among numerous studies are attributed to the grinding fineness degree of substitution materials, their reactive compounds percentage, water to cement ratio (w/c), conditions of curing, and the type of primary existing cementitious binders in addition to their content within the concrete mixture.

Figure 18a, with a magnification of × 500, represents the sample of the first control mix (CM), which does not have any content of BF. The figure reveals a highly homogeneous concrete matrix with a rapidly widespread propagation of macro-cracks. The visible cracks are attributed to the brittle natural of the HPC and the lack of fiber reinforcement to restrain the propagation of cracks. On the other hand, Fig. 18b represents the BF15-HY0 mix, which has a content of 15 kg/m3 mono-size macro-BF of 30 mm length. A high degree of fiber agglomeration and interlocking is noticed due to the high fiber content and large length, along with a clearly visible porosity due to poor bonding at the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) between the fibers and the concrete matrix. This observation is consistent with the lower mechanical properties and UPV findings. Figure 18c represents the second control mix BF5-HY3 (CM2), which contains a 5 kg/m3 content of BF with a hybridization of four graded lengths. It is noticed that the fibers are bonded very well to the matrix at the ITZ, clearly arresting the cracks and preventing them from propagating through the concrete matrix. This indicates the beneficial role of fiber reinforcement gradation in length to improve all mechanical properties.

Figure 19a illustrates the (10% SF) mix at magnification of × 200. It can be observed from the micrograph that there is an almost ideal packing of silica flour particles at cement replacement of 10%. It’s also shown that there are an excellent bonding and cohesion at ITZ between the fibers and the HPC matrix. The almost ideal packing of particles results in a substantial reduction in porosity, which in turn contributes to the enhanced mechanical properties and UPV values. The high content of SiO2 in silica flour (99.45%, as presented in Table 2) also contributes to the enhanced C–S–H gel formation and matrix density. Figure 19b presents the (10% MK) mix at magnification of × 500. The micrograph clearly indicates the development of a substantial amount of C–S–H gel, along with the good packing of particles and reduced porosity. These aspects are related to the high pozzolanic activity and the well-graded distribution of MK particle size, see Fig. 1, which had a positive influence on the results of the mechanical properties and UPV.

Lastly, Fig. 19c represents the (15% FLs) mix at × 800 magnification. From this image, there is a decrease in the formation of C–S–H gel and an increase in the concrete matrix porosity. The absence of C–S–H gel, which is responsible for the densification and strengthening of the matrix, results in an increase in porosity, hence the reduction in UPV and mechanical properties of this mixture.

Image-based pore analysis

To evaluate the relative surface porosity of HPC mixtures, image-based pore analysis was conducted on the SEM micrographs of mixes using ImageJ software. Figure 20 illustrates the workflow for assessing the relative surface porosity mainly through two sequential stages. In the first stage, a thresholding analysis was performed on the original SEM images to quantitatively assess the surface compactness and pore distribution of the concrete matrix. During this process, grayscale intensity levels were adjusted to distinguish pore regions from the solid matrix phase. The pores were then highlighted in red to be more clarified. This step facilitated the differentiation between dense regions and porous zones within the microstructural scope of HPC matrix. In the second stage, binary conversion was conducted on the thresholded images, visualizing the pores in white, while the dense regions were kept in black. The white regions were then easily counted as a percentage of the entire micrograph, representing the total value of the relative surface porosity. Figure 20 clearly shows that a 10% replacement of SF and MK led to higher densification, having relative surface porosity values of 6.35% and 7.59%, respectively compared to 9.47% for the secondary control mix (CM2), reflecting the improvements in mechanical properties and UPV results. In contrast, a 15% replacement of FLs led to a less dense matrix, having a relative surface porosity of 16.4%, compared to the CM2 mixture, reflecting the decrees in mechanical properties and UPV results. These findings confirm that image-based pore analysis can serve as an effective tool for evaluating microstructural densification, making the mechanical properties and UPV results of HPC more reliable.Fig. 20SEM image-based pore analysis for relative surface porosity evaluation of different HPC mixtures.

Sustainability and environmental impact

Carbon emissions and materials cost

The cement industry significantly contributes to environmental pollution and high energy consumption due to Portland cement production71,72. Recent research has focused on reducing cement content through sustainable replacement materials73, or developing eco-friendly alternatives such as geopolymer concrete74,75. Therefore, current trends aim to achieve a balance between mechanical performance, environmental sustainability, and economic efficiency to produce greener and more cost-effective concrete76–81.

The carbon emission of each material (EC) used in concrete mainly depends on its nature of origin, manufacturing process, transportation requirements, and necessary energy of grinding, which affect the overall carbon footprint of concrete. For instance, cement is known as the material with the highest EC value among all binders due to the extreme temperature required for clinker production and burning process. In contrast, BF have a higher EC value compared to Portland cement because of the extremely required heat that is necessary for melting basalt rocks and transforming them into fibers. Moreover, the greenhouse-gas emission of MK is approximately 55% lower than that of Portland cement82, while SF and FLs have significantly lower EC values compared to Portland cement. This is due to that the carbon emission of SF and FLs completely depend on grinding and transportation only, whereas MK needs high temperature for calcining the raw material of Kaolin to produce Metakaolin. Furthermore, water and aggregates used have the lowest EC values which are often neglected in most studies. In this study, the approximate EC values of all materials used were collected from the previous literature along with Inventory of Carbon and Energy (ICE) database83–86. Finally, the cost of each raw material (Pi) was determined according to the local market and expressed in (EGP/kg). Discussion

Figure 21a illustrates the total cost (CM) of 1 m3 of concrete. The CM values followed a monotonic decreasing trend with increasing the replacement ratios of both SF and FLs, showing their effectiveness in producing more economical concrete. In contrast, increasing the MK dosage showed an opposite trend with a consistent increase in CM values, reflecting the high cost of MK reaching four times the cost of cement. Due to their low carbon footprint compared to Portland cement, the gradual increase in SF, FLs, and MK dosages showed a similar progressive trend in the total carbon emission (CCO2), as shown in Fig. 21b. The partial cement replacement with SF, FLs, and MK produced more sustainable HPC with lower values of CCO2, demonstrating the effectiveness of these materials in enhancing the environmental impact.

On the other hand, the cost efficiency index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{M}})$$\end{document} and environmental efficiency index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${(\mathrm{U}}_{{\mathrm{C}\mathrm{O}}_{2}})$$\end{document} are affected by the type and replacement ratio of the utilized alternative materials. As shown in Fig. 21c, SF and MK exhibited the lowest values of UM at 10% replacement ratio, while FLs showed the lowest UM value at 5% replacement ratio. Similarly, for both SF and MK, the 10% and 15% replacement levels exhibited the lowest UCO2 values, with nearly identical results for both ratios, see Fig. 21d. In contrast, a 5% replacement level of FLs led to the lowest value of UCO2.

Throughout all results presented in Figs. 21a–d, the optimal cement replacement ratio for FLs is 5%, while the optimal ratio for both SF and MK is 10%. Interestingly, the aforementioned ratios, selected based on environmental and economic evaluations, align with those that achieved the best mechanical performance of HPC. This demonstrates the ability of these alternative materials, at the specified ratios, to provide the best-balanced solution for producing concrete with high mechanical properties, lower cost, and greater environmental friendliness.

The purpose of this research is to explore the mechanical properties, UPV, microstructure, and both economic and environmental impacts of proposed HPC mixes with highly hybridized graded BF and ground mining materials, namely MK, Potassic FLs, and SF as partial substitutes for cement. Future works

Although the current study provided comprehensive insights into the mechanical properties, microstructure, and economic and environmental impact of SF, FLs, and MK in HPC, it is highly recommended that future research should: (1) study the long-term durability and freeze–thaw resistance of HPC containing SF, FLs, and MK; (2) assess the synergetic effect of (SF + FLs), (SF + MK), and (MK + FLs) at different replacement ratios on mechanical properties and microstructure of HPC under elevated temperatures; (3) investigate the effect of thermally and chemically treated FLs on ASR and volumetric stability of HPC.
