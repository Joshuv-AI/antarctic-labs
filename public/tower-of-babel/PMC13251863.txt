Chiral nanoparticles drive enantiomer-specific osteogenic differentiation of stem cells and accelerate bone regeneration
Authors: Yuwen Wang, Zheng Zhong, Zeqing Li, Yuecong Guo, Christina Sin U. Ieong, Tao Yao, Boguang Yang, Ning Zhang, Songlin He, Zhilong Zhou, Jun Liu, Runxuan Cai, Yaling Wang, Chung Hang Jonathan Choi, Chuanbin Mao, Denghui Xie, Zhifeng Huang, Bo Liu, Maobin Xie, Chunying Chen, Zhong Alan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise control over stem cell differentiation is of crucial importance in regenerative medicine, such as stem cell–based bone repair. Chiral nanoparticles (NPs) exhibit enantiomer-dependent interactions with stem cells, providing a promising strategy for guiding cell behaviors. Here, we investigated the role of chiral NPs in modulating osteogenic differentiation of stem cells. L-CF-NPs, D-CF-NPs, and A-CF-NPs with controllable nanoscale chirality were synthesized to investigate the effect of enantioselectivity on stem cell fate. In vitro, L-CF-NPs resulted in the highest cellular uptake through clathrin-mediated, integrin-involved endocytosis. This led to the most pronounced up-regulation of osteogenic marker expression, mineralization (via MAPK/JNK/ERK), and angiogenic marker expression. In vivo, volumetric 3D-bioprinted scaffolds incorporating L-CF-NPs resulted in the fastest bone regeneration in a rat model of critical-size bone defects. This work establishes nanoscale chirality as a design parameter for biomaterials, offering a promising approach to regenerating bone and other tissues.

INTRODUCTION

With multilineage differentiation potential, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are a highly promising cell source for regenerative medicine (1–4). Despite the broad use of biomaterials to direct MSC differentiation and enhance therapeutic outcomes, MSC-based tissue regeneration remains constrained by two key challenges: (i) the lack of biomaterial platforms that enable precise spatiotemporal control over MSC fate (5) and (ii) an incomplete understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing MSC-biomaterial interactions (6).

Chirality, a fundamental organizing principle in biology, plays a critical role in various biological processes, including protein folding and receptor-ligand binding (7, 8). The inherent enantiomeric selectivity in biological processes, such as the L-amino acid preference in proteins and the different pharmacological activities of chiral drugs, underscores the importance of chirality in cellular communication (9, 10). Leveraging chirality as a design parameter, chiral biomaterials hold the potential to open new strategies to promote tissue regeneration (8).

While several previous studies have used chiral biomaterials to promote tissue regeneration, there is a paucity of research into the regulation of cell fate by biomaterials with geometrical asymmetry. For example, D-peptide–crosslinked hydrogels were used to realize accelerated wound healing (11), and L-peptide supramolecular scaffolds were used to promote anti-inflammatory M2 polarization of macrophages (12). In addition, L-type cysteine-modified gold nanoparticles (NPs), which resulted in higher cellular uptake than their D-type counterparts, were used to accelerate periodontal regeneration (13). However, these chiral systems do not demonstrate geometric asymmetry. While D-nanoarrays with structural chirality have been fabricated to modulate immune responses (14), the generation of such nanoarrays was limited to two-dimensional (2D) surfaces. Recent advancements in nanomanufacturing technologies have enabled the precise engineering of chiral NPs with programmable spatial geometries (15, 16). Despite these capabilities, the potential of nanoscale geometric asymmetry in guiding MSC differentiation remains largely unexplored (17), representing a substantial knowledge gap in regenerative chiral NP design.

Among various unmet medical needs for effective tissue regeneration, critical-sized bone defects (CSBDs) affect more than 2 million patients annually (18–23). It is estimated that CSBD treatment incurs a medical cost of $2.5 billion in the US alone (24). Given the known indispensable role of MSCs in bone regeneration, it is critical to realize precise guidance of MSCs toward osteogenesis for effective CSBD regeneration. In addition, sufficient angiogenesis is required to support bone formation and sustain the regenerated tissue (25, 26). Current biomaterial-based strategies (e.g., growth factor delivery and conducive topographical cues) often lack the specificity to direct the necessary MSC differentiation programs (27–29). Chiral nanomaterials offer molecular-level control of MSC fate, presenting opportunities for targeted bone regeneration (17, 30).

Here, we explored the interactions between human bone marrow MSCs and gold NPs with geometric asymmetry and elucidated the underlying mechanisms (Fig. 1). We synthesized L-CF-NPs (NPs synthesized using L-cysteine-L-phenylalanine dipeptide), D-CF-NPs (NPs synthesized using D-cysteine-D-phenylalanine dipeptide), and A-CF-NPs (NPs synthesized using L-cysteine-L-phenylalanine dipeptide and D-cysteine-D-phenylalanine dipeptide) with identical surface chemistry and observed the highest bioactivity of L-CF-NPs. Specifically, the L-CF-NPs resulted in the most robust enhancement of osteogenic marker expression [e.g., Runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) and Osteocalcin (OCN)] and mineralization in MSCs and effectively stimulated hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) signaling–mediated vascularization. Mechanistically, the L-CF-NPs were found to drive clathrin-mediated endocytosis and integrin activation, consequently triggering the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)/extracellular signal–regulated kinase (ERK) signaling axis—a pathway essential for osteogenesis. Inspired by this observation, we leveraged our chiral NPs to accelerate CSBD regeneration. In vivo, volumetrically bioprinted scaffolds (31) incorporating L-CF-NPs and MSCs showed the highest efficacy in repairing CSBDs in a rat model. Our findings demonstrate that nanoscale chirality is a key determinant of biomaterial functionality, providing a blueprint for designing enantiomer-specific therapies for advancing skeletal repair.

RESULTS

Characterization and enantiomer-dependent cellular uptake of chiral NPs

Chiral Au NPs were synthesized via a two-step reaction (32): (i) hexadecyltrimethylammonium chloride (CTAC)–stabilized seed formation via ascorbic acid reduction, followed by (ii) growth guided by chiral dipeptides. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectroscopy confirmed high purity and yield of triangular seed particles, which served as the precursors for growing chiral Au NPs (Fig. 2A and fig. S1A). The dipeptide-directed growth resulted in the formation of Au NPs exhibiting chiral morphologies and uniform size distribution (Fig. 2B). On resonance with localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) in a wavelength range of 500 to 900 nm (Fig. 2C), chiral Au NPs exhibited bisignate circular dichroism (CD) signals; changing the handedness of dipeptides from the L- to D-configuration made the CD spectrum flip around the zero-CD axis (Fig. 2D). These results illustrate that the dipeptide-induced chiral NPs have chiroptical activity, whereby the L- and D-dipeptides give rise to the fabrication of L- and D-CF-NPs (33, 34). L- and D-dipeptides showed chiroptical activity at a wavelength <230 nm (fig. S1B), which was barely detected in the chiral Au NPs (fig. S1C). It illuminates that the chiroptical activity of chiral Au NPs does not directly originate from the dipeptides attached to the Au NPs but from chirality transfer from the dipeptides. The dipeptides induce chiral LSPR and direct the formation of chiral nano-morphologies, accounting for their chiroptical activity. The chiroptical activity of individual chiral Au NPs was quantified by calculating the anisotropy g-factor, given by (35)g=CD16,500Abswhere CD and “Abs” represent the ellipticity (units: medg) and the absorbance (or extinction), respectively. Chiral Au NPs have g ≅ 0.02 in the visible region, indicating their enantioselective light-matter interactions (Fig. 2E). Dynamic light scattering confirmed uniform hydrodynamic diameters for all NPs, with polydispersity indices (PDIs) below 0.3 indicating monodisperse distributions (fig. S1, D and E) (36). NP tracking analysis results corroborated the colloidal stability of L-CF-NPs in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (fig. S1F). Atomic-resolution, high-angle annular dark-field imaging and energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy confirmed the successful conjugation of dipeptides to methoxy polyethylene glycol thiol (mPEG-SH) on L-CF-NP surfaces (fig. S1G). The formation of Au─S bonds between mPEG-SH and gold NPs depends on pH: Coordination bonds dominate at lower pH, while covalent bonds form preferentially at higher pH (37). Previous studies have shown that the formation of Au─S covalent bonds requires a minimum interaction time of ~3.0 s (38). The coating with mPEG-SH has been demonstrated to enhance NP biocompatibility, which is critical for clinical applications (39).

The biocompatibility of chiral NPs was evaluated using the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Following a 24-hour culturing period with varying NP concentrations, no toxicity on human bone marrow MSCs was observed when the NP concentrations were 50 μg/ml or lower (fig. S2). The chiral NPs tended to show a more pronounced effect on human MSC proliferation after 5 days of treatment than nonchiral NPs (fig. S3). The subsequent stage of the research was to investigate the mechanism of cell uptake of NPs (Fig. 2F). Bio-TEM (Fig. 2G) and nanocomputer tomography (nano-CT) (Fig. 2H, fig. S4, and movies S1 to S3) results showed that L-CF-NPs were more readily absorbed by cells in comparison to D-CF-NPs and A-CF-NPs. Besides, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) quantitative analysis revealed that the gold content in cells treated with L-CF-NPs was significantly higher than that in cells treated with D-CF-NPs and A-CF-NPs (Fig. 2I), with the gold content for the L-CF-NP–treated group being 2.76 and 1.54 times higher, respectively, than the D-CF-NP– and A-CF-NP–treated groups. Inhibition studies using Pitstop 2 abolished these uptake differences, demonstrating that enantiomer-selective internalization occurs through clathrin-mediated endocytosis (40, 41). This pathway involves the encapsulation of NPs within intracellular vesicles ranging from 100 to 500 nm in size (42). To validate the preferential cellular uptake of L-CF-NPs over the other two types, MSCs were cotreated with Cyanine 3 (Cy3)-labeled L-CF-NPs and either Cy5-labeled A-CF-NPs or D-CF-NPs to assess competitive NP uptake. After 6 hours, L-CF-NP internalization was found to be predominant in the presence of D-CF-NPs or A-CF-NPs (Fig. 2, J and K).

Mechanisms for enantiomer-dependent endocytosis of chiral NPs

To quantitatively assess the stereoselective affinity of key proteins involved in clathrin-mediated endocytosis of the chiral NPs, microscale thermophoresis was carried out (43, 44). This analysis revealed distinct binding preferences. Clathrin light chain (CLT), low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1) proteins all exhibited significantly stronger affinity (lower thermodynamic dissociation constant, KD) for the L-CF-NPs than D-CF-NPs and A-CF-NPs (Fig. 3A). Among these markers, CLT is a self-aggregating protein composed of three heavy chains and three light chains, which can aggregate into flat lattice or grid-like structures (45). TFR1 binds to transferrin on the cell surface, thereby initiating a clathrin-dependent process that results in the internalization of the complex (41). CLT and TFR1 have been identified as integral components of the clathrin-mediated endosome (46). Moreover, studies have demonstrated that LDLR can undergo constitutive transport via clathrin-mediated endocytosis (47). These results directly confirm chiral specificity for NP-protein interactions at the single-biomolecule level.

An analysis of the protein atomic configurations on the NP surface provides further insights into the NP’s effect on adsorption (Fig. 3B). Among the three protein types, clathrin heavy chain and CLT complex (3LVG) uniquely interacts with both the top platform and the skewed edges of the NP (fig. S5), while the other two are largely confined to the platform. This more extensive adsorption behavior explains the significantly larger interaction energy observed for the 3LVG protein. The chirality of the NPs further modulates this interaction. The 3LVG protein conformation is better matched with the L-CF-NP, as its three-residue branches align well with the NP’s skewed edges. In contrast, a D-CF-NP has opposing chirality, which creates a geometric mismatch. This significantly suppresses the conformation of the protein to the edges, particularly for the terminal parts of its branches, as highlighted by the dashed circles in Fig. 3B. Unlike 3LVG, the ectodomain of the human TFR (1CXB) and extracellular domain of the LDLR (1N7D) proteins exhibit no significant conformational difference between their adsorptions on L-CF-NPs and D-CF-NPs. This is due to their small size, which confines their interaction primarily to the flat platform of the NP, a region less affected by chiral asymmetry. Nevertheless, the chirality of the NP does influence the adsorption through the peptide coating. The chiral peptides on the NP surface adopt different configurations depending on the NP’s chirality, which in turn modulates the protein-NP interaction. The peptides on the L-CF-NP platform are packed more loosely than those on the D-CF-NP. This difference in packing density is particularly evident for the 1CXB protein and contributes to the observed variations in interaction energy.

The evolution of protein-NP interaction energy [Coulomb, van der Waals (vdW), and total interaction energy] over time for all six simulation cases is presented in Fig. 3C. A decomposition of this energy reveals that vdW interactions constitute the dominant component of the total nonbonded interaction, a trend consistent across all systems. Because of its larger size, the 3VLG protein exhibits an interaction energy with the NP an order of magnitude greater than the other two proteins and also saturates more slowly. Notably, for all proteins, the interaction energy is consistently higher with the L-CF-NP than with the D-CF-NP. This chiral dependence is most pronounced for 3VLG, where the interaction energy with the L-CF-NP is ~2000 kJ/mol higher than with the D-CF-NP at 20 ns—a disparity far greater than that observed for the other proteins (fig. S6A).

The critical role of NP chirality in 3LVG protein adsorption was further tested by flipping the protein upside down (a 180° vertical inversion). This inversion reverses the chiral preference: The conformation of the flipped 3LVG protein becomes superior on the D-CF-NP compared to the L-CF-NP. The terminal parts of two protein branches tend to detach from the L-CF-NP in this inverted state (fig. S6B). This observation is confirmed by the time evolution of the interaction energy, which becomes significantly higher for the D-CF-NP than for the L-CF-NP after the flip, as indicated in fig. S6C. However, this inverted configuration is inherently less stable. When flipped, several short residues at the protein’s center act as a scaffold, propping up the entire structure and increasing its separation from the NP surface. This increased distance directly leads to a largely weakened interaction, as evidenced by the much smaller interaction energy. Consequently, the most stable and experimentally observed binding mode is the native, unflipped orientation on the L-CF-NP. In this preferred state, the short residues point outward, allowing the protein to approach the surface more closely and achieve a stronger, more favorable interaction.

The above findings collectively confirm that chiral NPs, particularly L-CF-NPs, are preferentially internalized via clathrin-mediated pathways. To further elucidate the mechanisms for cellular uptake of the NPs, we also used reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) to test the expression of genes associated with clathrin-mediated cellular uptake. Following a 6-hour NP treatment period, a marked higher expression level for CLTA was observed for cells exposed to L-CF-NPs than those to D-CF-NPs or A-CF-NPs. Concurrently, the expression levels of LDLR, TFR1, and fibronectin 1 (FN1) were 2.29-, 3.04-, and 2.28-fold higher, respectively, in the L-CF-NP group than the control group (Fig. 3D and fig. S7). In addition, L-CF-NP–treated cells exhibited augmented expression of FN1 (fibronectin), suggesting that this may enhance cell extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions, thereby potentiating the uptake of chiral NPs through ECM-mediated adhesion. Consistent with the canonical endocytic trafficking pathway (41), internalized chiral NPs were observed to predominantly localize to lysosomes, as confirmed by colocalization of Cy3-labeled L-CF-NPs with Lysotracker staining after 12 hours of incubation (fig. S8). These results indicate that, following clathrin-mediated endocytosis, the NPs are transported through endosomal compartments and ultimately accumulate within lysosomes. Critically, Pitstop 2 treatment eliminated both the enantioselective uptake differences and NP-induced gene expression changes (Fig. 3E). This further demonstrates that clathrin-dependent mechanisms govern stereoselective internalization at both the functional, transcriptional, and subcellular localization levels.

Transcriptome sequencing reveals up-regulated expression of osteogenic markers in L-NP–treated MSCs

We performed RNA sequencing to understand how the enantiomer-dependent uptake of chiral NPs affects the transcriptome of human bone marrow MSCs, one of the most commonly used cell types in regenerative medicine (Fig. 4A). The MSCs were cultured with different L-, D-, or A-CF-NPs in a growth factor–free osteoinductive medium. Transcriptomic analysis revealed 1154 up-regulated genes (Fig. 4B) in L-CF-NP–treated cells compared to the control group. Among these up-regulated gene, the expression levels of ephrin B2 (EFNB2), bone marrow stromal cell antigen 2 (BST2), S100 calcium binding protein A4 (S100A4), and integrin α2 (ITGA2) were significantly increased (Fig. 4C). EFNB2 encodes Ephrin-B2, a ligand for the EphB4 receptor that is critical for vascular remodeling and osteogenic coupling (48, 49). BST2 has been shown to regulate bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2)-mediated osteoblast differentiation (50), while S100A4 has been demonstrated to modulate calcium channel activity, thereby facilitating mineral deposition (51). ITGA2 plays a crucial role in bone mechanotransduction (52), and under mechanical forces, ITGA2 activate downstream MAPK pathways to regulate osteoblast differentiation and bone matrix synthesis.

Subsequent analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment further yielded important findings (fig. S9A). GO enrichment analysis of biological processes revealed significant enrichment in angiogenesis, bone mineralization, MAPK pathway regulation, and ossification, suggesting that L-CF-NPs have the capacity to stimulate bone regeneration and angiogenesis (Fig. 4D). In addition, the L-CF-NPs were found to stimulate the synthesis of type I collagen, as indicated by the enrichment in the collagen-containing ECM under the GO enrichment analysis of the cellular components (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, molecular function enrichment analysis demonstrated enhanced calcium ion binding, ECM formation, and BMP receptor binding, thereby substantiating the role of L-CF-NPs in facilitating calcium ion influx and bone formation (Fig. 4F). Moreover, Kyoto Gene and Gene Encyclopedia (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed enrichment in pathways such as the calcium signaling pathway, HIF-1α signaling pathway, and MAPK signaling pathway, which are known to support osteogenesis (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, genome-wide gene set enrichment analysis revealed the enrichment of processes such as collagen trimer up-regulation, bone mineralization, mineralized tissue development, and collagen binding (Fig. 4, H and I, and fig. S9B). Together, these findings suggest that L-CF-NPs promoted metabolic processes and signaling pathways linked to osteogenesis in human MSCs.

L-CF-NPs most effectively promoted osteogenic differentiation of MSCs

The osteoinductive property of the NPs was systematically evaluated through biochemical and molecular analyses. At the transcription level, L-CF-NPs significantly increased COL1A1 (2.14-fold), BSP (15.96-fold), BMP-2 (13.70-fold), and ALP (3.32-fold) expression relative to untreated cells (Fig. 5A). Fluorescence staining of RUNX2 and OCN further confirmed that L-CF-NPs effectively promoted the osteogenic differentiation of human MSCs (Fig. 5B and figs. S10 and S11). The activity of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), an early osteogenic marker, was 1.20-fold and 1.30-fold higher in MSCs treated with L-CF-NPs compared to D-CF-NPs and A-CF-NPs, respectively, after 7 days. Alizarin red S (ARS) staining confirmed the enhanced mineralization in the L-CF-NP group, and quantitative image analysis revealed 1.42-fold higher ARS staining intensity for the L-CF-NP group than the control (Fig. 5, C and D). Western blot results revealed a 1.49-, 1.70-, 1.71-, and 1.58-fold increase in OCN, COL1A1, ALP, and RUNX2 protein levels in L-CF-NP–treated cells, respectively, compared to the control (Fig. 5E and fig. S12). These results collectively suggest that L-CF-NPs effectively promote early- and late-stage osteogenic differentiation of MSCs.

KEGG pathway analysis revealed a correlation between L-CF-NP bioactivity and MAPK signaling, a pivotal regulator of osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 4G). Western blot results showed that the level of phosphorylated JNK1/2/3 in L-CF-NP–treated MSCs was 1.54-fold and 1.38-fold higher compared with the A-CF-NP and D-CF-NP groups, respectively. In addition, the level of phosphorylated ERK1/2 showed a 1.35-fold and 1.28-fold increase in L-CF-NP–treated MSCs compared with cells treated with the D-CF-NP and A-CF-NP groups, respectively (Fig. 5F and fig. S13). Besides, no difference in p38 phosphorylation was observed in cells treated with the three types of NPs (fig. S14). These findings are consistent with the results of previous studies on the involvement of MAPK/JNK/ERK in bone metabolism, confirming the important role of this signaling pathway in L-CF-NP–driven osteogenesis and mineralization (53–55).

To ascertain the proangiogenic effects of chiral NPs, a transwell coculture system was established (Fig. 5G). Human MSCs were seeded in NP-containing scaffolds, consisting of 2.5% silk fibroin and 2.5% gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA), in the upper chamber (the same scaffolds were used for in vivo testing). Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were 2D cultured in the lower chamber. Tube formation assay revealed that HUVECs cocultured with L-CF-NP–treated MSCs exhibited 1.56- and 1.63-fold more branching nodes than D-CF-NP and A-CF-NP groups, respectively, and the corresponding fold changes in the number of junctions were both 1.55 (Fig. 5, H and I, and fig. S15), indicating enhanced angiogenic potential of MSCs driven by L-CF-NP–primed paracrine signaling. Immunofluorescence staining and RT-qPCR analysis collectively showed that L-CF-NP–treated MSCs had significantly higher expression of angiogenic markers (vascular endothelial growth factor) than the control (fig. S16). Transcriptional profiling further revealed the highest expression level of HIF-1α, ANGPT1, ANGPT2, VEGFC, and FGF in L-CF-NP–treated MSCs (fig. S17), indicating the sustained promotion of vascular maturation and remodeling.

The assessment of intracellular calcium flux, a pivotal regulator of osteogenesis-angiogenesis coupling (56), was conducted using fluorescent probes. L-CF-NP–treated MSCs demonstrated elevated calcium ion concentrations in comparison to the other groups (Fig. 5J). KEGG pathway analysis linked this effect to HIF-1α and calcium signaling (Fig. 4G), with L-CF-NPs inducing higher HIF-1α protein expression (fig. S18). These findings are consistent with prior in vivo data (57), which demonstrated that L-CF-NP–up-regulated HIF-1α expression promoted angiogenic differentiation and femur defect repair.

Volumetric 3D-bioprinted, L-CF-NP–containing scaffolds induced most robust bone regeneration in vivo

The use of volumetric bioprinting was predicated on its ability to expeditiously fabricate complex, patient-specific scaffolds exhibiting elevated structural fidelity and minimal mechanical stress on encapsulated cells (31, 58). This is of paramount importance for the preservation of MSC viability and function in bone regeneration. The composition of the bioink was optimized at a ratio of 2.5% silk fibroin, with the objective of balancing mechanical resilience, bioactivity, and printability. To enable efficient photo-crosslinking, the bioink incorporated NPs (100 μg/ml) and 1 × 106/ml of MSCs, combined with 2.5% GelMA. This formulation demonstrated exceptional printability, producing intricate anatomical constructs (e.g., ear, tooth, and screws) (Fig. 6A and fig. S19). The subsequent in vivo evaluation protocol detailed the implantation process and schedule of scaffolds in rat CSBDs, as shown in Fig. 6B. Live/dead staining demonstrated >90% live cells within NP-laden scaffolds postprinting, confirming the bioink’s high biocompatibility (Fig. 6C and fig. S20). Mechanical testing confirmed that NP incorporation did not compromise hydrogel integrity, with negligible differences observed in the mechanical properties between NP-free and NP-loaded hydrogels (Fig. 6D and fig. S21A). Rheological characterization of the bioink revealed higher storage modulus (G′) than loss modulus (G″) in the frequency range of 0.1 to 10 Hz, along with self-healing behavior that is highly desirable for surgical application (Fig. 6E and fig. S21B). Furthermore, the gel forming ability of the inks with different GelMA concentrations was investigated (fig. S21C).

The ability of chiral NPs to regenerate CSBDs was assessed in a rat model of femoral defect. The animals were divided into five groups: the control group (without treatment), cell-laden but NP-free scaffold group, and three groups of cell-laden scaffolds containing A-CF-NPs, D-CF-NPs, or L-CF-NPs. These scaffolds were prepared by volumetric 3D bioprinting and implanted into CSBDs in Sprague-Dawley rats (Fig. 6F and fig. S22). Micro-CT analysis at 4 and 8 weeks postimplantation revealed progressive bone formation across all groups, with the L-CF-NP group exhibiting the most pronounced defect bridging and trabecular organization. Quantitative analysis of micro-CT data revealed that the L-CF-NP–containing scaffolds induced significantly higher increment in bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), trabecular thickness, and trabecular number (Tb.N), as well as the smaller trabecular separation compared to the control group (Fig. 6, G and H, and fig. S23). These results highlight the enantiomer-dependent capacity of chiral NPs to promote bone maturation and structural organization.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining were conducted to histologically analyze the regenerated bone. The largest area of new bone appeared to be formed for the L-CF-NP group, suggesting the most pronounced enhancement of mineralization and structural maturity (Fig. 7A). In addition, the L-CF-NP group showed superior relative collagen content to the control and scaffold only groups at 4 and 8 weeks postimplantation (Fig. 7, B and C). Furthermore, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining, an indicator of osteoclast activity, revealed a lower number of TRAP-positive cells for the L-CF-NP group compared to the control group (Fig. 7, D and E), suggesting a lower level of bone resorption for the L-CF-NP group. These findings are consistent with the highest BV/TV and BMD observed for the L-CF-NP group.

We further used immunostaining to confirm the effectiveness of L-CF-NPs in promoting osteogenesis in vivo. Immunohistochemical staining showed that the number of OCN- and RUNX2-positive cells was found to be significantly higher in the L-CF-NP group than the scaffold and control groups (figs. Immunofluorescence analysis further revealed the highest expression level for CD31, HIF-1α, and alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) in the defect area for the L-NP–treated group (Fig. 7, F to I), indicating that HIF-1α signaling–mediated vascularized bone regeneration. H&E staining of major organs, including heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, revealed no histopathological abnormalities in any animals treated with NP-containing scaffolds (fig. S26), confirming the high biocompatibility of the NPs.

DISCUSSION

The present study unraveled the molecular mechanisms for enantiomer-dependent uptake of chiral NP by MSCs and showed that L-CF-NPs could most effectively augment MSC osteogenesis in vitro and accelerate bone regeneration in vivo. The enantiomeric NPs with geometric asymmetry demonstrated higher cellular uptake, via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, than their achiral counterparts, with L-CF-NPs inducing the most pronounced cellular uptake. Using both experiments and computational simulations, we proved the higher affinity of L-CF-NPs for CLT, TFR, and LDLR than D-CF-NPs and A-CF-NPs. In addition, the L-CF-NPs most effectively drove the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs in vitro by activating the MAPK/JNK/ERK signaling cascade. In a rat CBSD model, L-CF-NP–containing, volumetric 3D-bioprinted silk fibroin (SF)/GelMA scaffolds resulted in the most robust bone regeneration. These results position chirality as a highly promising yet underexplored design criterion for regenerative biomaterials capable of precisely guiding stem cell fate.

The bio-TEM, nano-CT, and ICP-MS results consistently showed a higher level of L-CF-NP uptake by MSCs. To validate the preferential cellular uptake of L-CF-NPs, we cultured MSCs in the presence of both L- and D- or A-CF-NPs. The results from the competitive NP uptake tests further confirmed receptor-mediated selected endocytosis of L-enantiomers. Furthermore, both computer simulations and Pitstop 2 treatment clearly supported clathrin-mediated enantiomer selection that favors L-CF-NP uptake. These mechanistic insights enabled us to leverage nanoscale geometrical asymmetry to modulate clathrin-mediated, integrin-involved NP-MSC interactions, activate MAPK cascade, and significantly augment osteogenesis and angiogenesis. This design paradigm has not been reported previously for regenerative nanobiomaterials.

A number of studies have previously tuned the NP size, charge, or surface chemistry to modulate cellular uptake (59–62), yet the role of chirality remains underexplored. Studies on cysteine-modified Au/Se NPs have documented the preferential uptake of L-enantiomers, but the underlying mechanisms remained to be elucidated (13, 63). Similarly, L-enantiomers of tartaric acid–modified mesoporous silica NPs exhibited higher oral absorption (64). However, the observed enantiomer-specific cellular uptake was exclusively ascribed to the surface chemistry, i.e., the molecular ligands used, and the role of nanoscale geometric asymmetry was not investigated. In the study by Xu et al. (32), chiral gold NPs with geometric asymmetry demonstrated immune modulation via CD97/EGF-like module-containing mucin-like receptor 1 (EMR1) binding. The present study definitively correlated enantioselective uptake of chiral NPs to clathrin-mediated, integrin-involved endocytosis and MAPK cascade activation.

D-enantiomers of lipid NPs were previously reported to exhibit stronger affinity with cells, thereby enhancing mRNA delivery (65). However, the lipid NPs used had only molecular chirality but not geometrically chiral nanostructures, and the study did not decipher the mechanisms underlying cellular update of different enantiomers. In addition, while we did not observe differential expression of p38 induced by our chiral NPs, a previous study reported pan-MAPK/P38 activation induced by gold NPs during mouse bone marrow MSC osteogenesis (66). Furthermore, a study has demonstrated that a sub-10-nm, folic acid–conjugated gold NP can inhibit p38α MAPK (67). Such different findings highlight the distinct enantiomer-dependent signaling pathways associated with osteogenesis.

A notable aspect of NP-cell interactions is the formation of a protein corona, which has been demonstrated to mediate the biological properties and fate of engineered NPs in physiological environments. Baimanov and colleagues (44) previously demonstrated that the chirality of NPs influences both the composition and spatiotemporal evolution of their protein corona. Specifically, they established a real-time monitoring platform to observe the dynamic assembly of hard and soft protein coronas on chiral Cu2S NPs. This enabled direct identification of corona proteins and revealed subtle yet critical differences in interaction kinetics and composition between enantiomeric NPs. Furthermore, the team found that chiral AuNPs with distinct surface configurations recruit specific protein subpopulations from plasma, including lipoproteins, complement components, and acute phase proteins (43). These NPs form a chirality-specific protein composition that influences cellular uptake of the NPs as well as their circulation and biodistribution. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that nanoscale chirality modulates direct cell-NP recognition and reshapes the molecular landscape at the nanobio interface through selective protein adsorption. The resulting composition of the corona may subsequently drive the observed variations in cellular uptake and osteogenic effects. Thus, a comprehensive understanding and precise modulation of protein corona layers on chiral NPs hold considerable promise for the development of highly effective, targeted nanomaterials for regenerative medicine.

Several limitations remain to be addressed for future clinical translation of our chiral NPs. First, while our study confirms that chiral NPs can effectively drive the robust osteogenic differentiation of MSCs, further research is required into their interactions with other relevant cell populations, such as osteoclasts and endothelial cells. In addition, a systematic assessment of dynamic immune responses during bone regeneration is necessary, particularly regarding long-term biocompatibility studies. The fate of NPs within implanted scaffolds and their long-term effects, including biodegradation, retention, clearance, and potential accumulation in targeted versus nontargeted tissues, are meaningful future research directions. Although previous studies indicate that gold NPs generally exhibit favorable biocompatibility and multiple clearance pathways (68–71), a comprehensive investigation of these processes, especially under repeated or prolonged exposure scenarios, remains crucial for subsequent evaluation.

Second, the chiral NP-induced therapeutic outcomes require validation in larger animal models of CSBDs. Third, to gain deeper molecular insights into the chiral NP-cell interactions, it is beneficial to decouple molecular chirality from intrinsic structural chirality. To address these gaps, spatial multiomic mapping of cell type–specific responses to chiral NPs should be carried out for different regeneration phases. Quantitative evaluation of bone regeneration will need to be conducted in large animals, preferably using chiral biomaterial-loaded, clinical-grade implants. In addition, enantiomerically pure, ligand-free NPs can be synthesized through chiral field–assisted lithography to study the effects of geometric asymmetry alone on cell behaviors. Moreover, it is highly desirable to develop biodegradable chiral biomaterials with optimized enantioselectivity and degradability to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes.

In summary, this work established a direct relationship between nanoscale geometric asymmetry and important biological processes, such as MSC osteogenesis. Geometric asymmetry has been shown to directly drive clathrin-mediated, integrin-involved endocytosis. Activation of the MAPK pathway has been demonstrated to link stereoselective uptake of the NPs to mechanotransductive signaling. Notably, the L-CF-NPs could actively engage CLT and integrin receptors to promote stem cell–based osteogenesis. Moving forward, we envision that the biomaterial design principles and biological mechanisms elucidated herein can be extended to diverse chiral architectures, such as topological metamaterials and injectable hydrogels (8). The expansion of material platforms to encompass polymeric and ceramic chiral systems will serve to evaluate the universality of our mechanistic findings from this study (8). In addition, as an emerging preclinical testing platform, organ-on-a-chip platforms (72, 73) created from human cells hold promising potential to decipher nanotechnology-biology interface and accelerate the clinical translation of chiral nanotherapeutics for tackling CSBD and other disorders.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The animal experiments were authorized by the Animal Experimental Committee of the Seyotin (Guangdong) Co. Ltd. (approval SYT2024130) and carried out in accordance with the regulations outlined in the National Law on Experimental Animal Utilization (P.R. China).

Chemicals

Gold (III) chloride trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O), hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB; purity ≥ 99%), CTAC (purity ≥ 99%), L-ascorbic acid (purity ≥ 99.9%), potassium iodide (KI; purity ≥ 99%), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH; purity ≥ 95%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Hydrochloric acid (HCl; 37%) and nitric acid (HNO3; 70%) were purchased from Duksan. L/D-cysteine phenylalanine dipeptide (purity ≥ 70%) was purchased from Sangon Biotechnology. Thiol functionalized mPEG-SH was purchased from ChinaPeptides. All aqueous solutions were prepared with deionized water (18.2 MΩ; Millipore). Silkworm cocoons were purchased from Jiangsu Xinyuan Silk Technology Co., China. Gelatin from porcine skin, lithium bromide (LiBr), and sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. Methacrylic anhydride (MA) was purchased from JSENB, China. Tris (2,2′-bipyridyl) dichlororuthenium (ii) hexahydrate (Ru) and sodium persulfate (SPS) were purchased from Advanced Biomatrix, USA.

Synthesis of chiral NPs

In a typical synthesis as reported (74), 1.7 ml of 100 mM cetrimonium chloride (CTAC) was injected into 8 ml of deionized water, followed by the addition of 80 μl of 10 mM KI and 101 μl of sodium tetrachloroaurate solution (made by mixing HAuCl4 and NaOH in a 1:1 ratio). Then, 85 μl of 0.064 M ascorbic acid was rapidly injected to reduce Au3+; at the same time, the color of the solution changed from yellow to colorless. Last, 10 μl of 100 mM NaOH was rapidly injected into the solution to reduce of Au+ for 15 min. As the reaction was completed, the color of the solution changed from colorless to blue. The growth solution was formed by adding 800 μl of 0.01 M CTAB and 200 μl of 0.01 M HAuCl4 to 4 ml of deionized water (stirring, 10 s). After 10 min, 0.48 ml of 40 mM ascorbic acid was injected into the growth solution (mix well). Then, 50 μl of 4 mM L/D-cysteine-phenylalanine dipeptide and 50 μl of seeds were added into the growth solution with stirring of 30 s. Last, 100 μl of mPEG-SH (molecular weight = 2000; 50 mM) was added to 1 ml of NPs. After 12 hours, the supernatant was discarded by centrifugation, and the sediment was resuspended in culture medium/PBS.

Characterization of chiral AuNPs

NPs were characterized using UV-Vis spectroscopy (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices) and CD (Chirascan V100, Applied Photophysics). The anisotropy factor (g-factor) is calculated using the following equationg−factor=CD/(16,500∗Abs)where CD (in medg) and absorbance (Abs) values were acquired from CD spectra.

Morphological and elemental analyses were conducted via TEM (Hitachi H7700), scanning electron microscopy (ZEISS Sigma 300), and scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM; Talos F200X). Hydrodynamic diameters and PDIs were measured using a Zetasizer (Malvern).

Cell culture

Human bone marrow MSCs and rat bone marrow MSCs were purchased from Oricell (Guangzhou, China). Cells from passages four to six were used in all experiments. The cell culture medium used for MSCs was low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM). Medium were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (antibiotic-antimycotic). All reagents were obtained from Gibco. The osteogenic differentiation medium consisted of high-glucose DMEM with 100 nM dexamethasone, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, and L-ascorbic acid (50 μg/ml). During differentiation, the culture medium was replaced three times a week. All chemicals used in osteogenic differentiation were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.

Cell sample preparation for soft x-ray nanotomography

MSCs were seeded at a density of 104 cells/ml onto 100-nm-thick silicon nitride frames. After incubating the cells with NPs (1 μg/ml) for 12 hours, they were rinsed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. The cells were then dehydrated using an ethanol gradient (30 to 100%), air-dried, and stored in clear imaging capsules to preserve structural integrity before synchrotron imaging.

Soft x-ray nanotomography imaging and analysis

Imaging experiments were conducted at the BL07W beamline of the National Synchrotron Radiation Laboratory. Samples were mounted on a motorized stage, and projection images were acquired at an energy of 520 eV with a pixel size of 13.2 nm. The field of view was 12.5 μm by 12.5 μm. For 3D reconstruction, each sample was rotated from −65° to +65° at 0.5° intervals, yielding 261 projections. Image processing involved background correction, jitter correction, ring artifact removal, and alignment of the rotation axis. Slice images were reconstructed using the simultaneous algebraic reconstruction technique. Avizo software generated a preliminary 3D volume, segmented intracellular NPs and cellular structures, and performed 3D volume rendering.

Cell viability and proliferation assay

CCK-8 (APExBIO, USA) was used to determine the viability and proliferation of MSCs. Cell viability and proliferation were determined using cell densities of 1 × 104 and 5.0 × 103 cells per well seeded at the bottom of 96-well plates. After the cells reached 70 to 80% fusion, NPs (1 μg/ml) was added. For proliferation experiments at 1, 3, 5, and 7 days, 10% of the total volume of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. After 120 min of incubation, the optical density of each well was measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 microplate spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, USA).

Cellular uptake

Chiral NPs were observed being internalized by MSCs using TEM (Hitachi H-7650; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Cells were cultured in growth medium at an initial density of 2.0 × 105 cells per well in a six-well plate. After 3 days of culture, cells were fixed, dehydrated, embedded, sectioned, and observed using TEM. The quantity of AuNPs in MSCs was detected using ICP-MS (Agilent Technologies). Cells were cultured in growth medium at an initial density of 8 × 104 cells per well in a 12-well plate. When cells reached 70% confluence, the growth medium was replaced with osteogenic differentiation medium containing L/A/D-AuNPs. After 3 days, the culture dishes were rinsed four times with PBS to remove free-floating AuNPs. The harvested cells were lysed in aqua regia and diluted, and the gold concentration was determined by ICP-MS.

For the NP uptake competition experiment, Cy3-labeled L-CF-NPs and Cy5-labeled A/D-CF-NPs were used. MSCs were cultured in confocal dishes and allowed to adhere for 24 hours. NPs (L/A/D) were added at a concentration of 1 μg/ml to the differentiation medium and incubated with MSCs for 6 hours. Images were captured using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

Simulation of protein binding affinity

The NPs were modeled from a cubic Au particle by first extracting a regular tetrahedron with a side length of ~33.9 nm (LAMMPS software). The tetrahedral NP was then reoriented such that the (111) lattice direction aligned with the z axis. A skew displacement was applied to the Au atoms along the z axis, which passes through the geometric center of the tetrahedron’s base plane, serving as the axis of rotation. The skew angle increased linearly from 0° at the base plane to 45° at the apex of the tetrahedron. To impart L-CF-NP, the skewing rotation was applied counterclockwise; for D-CF-NP, it was applied clockwise. Following the skewing process, the top of the Au NP was truncated at a distance of 4 nm from the tetrahedral apex. This truncation was performed along the (111) lattice plane—the most densely packed and stable atomic plane—to enhance adsorption with protein molecules, and thus this facet was deliberately exposed.

It is important to note that, unlike the flat (111) lattice plane truncated at the top, the three side surfaces of the tetrahedral NP are skewed. These skewed surfaces exhibit weaker adsorption with protein molecules, as larger molecular deformation would be required for the proteins to conform to the distorted atomic arrangement. Therefore, in our simulations, three protein molecules were placed atop the flat platform to ensure contact with this stable facet. Before placement, the skewed NP was coated with chiral peptides—L-Cys-L-Phe for the L-CF-NP and D-Cys-D-Phe for the D-CF-NP. The peptide-coated NP and associated proteins were then immersed and centered in a water box of sufficient size to allow for unconstrained protein relaxation. The simulation box edge length was set to 36 nm, containing approximately 2 × 106 atoms in total, including water molecules.

Six independent molecular dynamics simulations were performed for each of the three protein types (3LVG, 1CXB, and 1N7D), interacting with both L-CF-NPs and D-CF-NPs. The system initially underwent energy minimization to eliminate any steric clashes or unfavorable atomic contacts. The minimized structure was then equilibrated each for 2 ns in the NVT and NPT ensemble using a 1-fs time step, followed by a 20-ns production run in the NPT ensemble. During the production phase, the interaction energy between the protein and the NP was recorded every 50 ps. Throughout the simulations, all Au atoms were held fixed in space, while all other atoms were allowed to move freely. All simulations were conducted using the GROMACS package (version 2022.5). Protein atoms were described by the CHARMM36 force field, while the chiral peptides were modeled using the CGenFF force field. Water molecules were represented with the transferable intermolecular potential with 3 points (TIP3P) model. Non-bonded interactions were treated as a combination of vdW and Columbic potentials. All protein information is sourced from the Protein Data Bank: https://rcsb.org/.

ALP activity and staining

MSCs were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in a six-well plate. Once the cells reach 70% confluence, the osteogenic medium was switched. NPs were added simultaneously. On day 7, ALP activity was detected using an ALP assay kit (Beyotime). One hundred microliters of Western and Immunoprecipitation (IP) cell lysis buffer was added to each well. Fifty microliters of the supernatant was diluted and mixed with 50 μl of the color development substrate in a 96-well plate and incubated at 37°C in the dark for 5 to 10 min. Then, 100 μl of stop solution was added, and the absorbance was measured at 405 nm. MSCs were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well in a 24-well plate. On day 7, the cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 10% formalin. Then, the BCIP/NBT ALP staining kit (Beyotime) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The ALP staining results were observed using an inverted optical microscope.

ARS staining and quantification

The cells were cultured and treated in the same way as ALP staining. Mineralization was assessed on day 21. The cells were fixed and stained with 2% ARS (Sigma-Aldrich) and then imaged. The deposits were then dissolved in 10% cetylpyridinium chloride, and the absorbance at 562 nm was measured. ImageJ 1.54 [National Institutes of Health (NIH), USA] was used to quantify the staining intensity.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from the samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Purity and concentration were verified using a NanoDrop ND-1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and integrity was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies). Polyadenylated mRNA was enriched with poly-dT magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific), fragmented at 94°C for 5 min, and reverse transcribed into cDNA with SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Second-strand synthesis was performed using ribonuclease H and DNA polymerase I (New England Biolabs). Libraries were constructed via end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation. This was followed by Uracil-DNA Glycosylase (UDG)-mediated digestion of uracil-containing strands. PCR amplification (8 cycles of 98°C denaturation, 60°C annealing, and 72°C extension) generated fragments of 300 ± 50 bp. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Lianchuang Biotechnology, China).

Real-time RT-PCR

MSCs (2 × 105 cells per well) were cultured in osteogenic medium with L-, A-, or D-CF-NPs for 7 days. Total RNA was extracted using a Zymo RNA kit, reverse transcribed using a Yeasen cDNA kit, and amplified using primers targeting markers. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in table S1, obtained from the Primer Library (https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/), and synthesized by BGI Genomics. Transcript levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde phosphate dehydrogenase and quantified via the ΔΔCt method. Untreated cells served as controls.

Western blotting

The MSCs were lysed posttreatment in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the proteins were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Bio-Rad). The membranes were probed with primary antibodies (table S2), followed by horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies. Visualization was achieved using chemiluminescence (SuperSignal West Femto, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ 1.54 (NIH, USA).

Tube formation assay

To assess angiogenesis, MSCs were encapsulated in GelMA/SF hydrogels containing NPs and cultured for 1 week. Next, HUVECs were cultured in Matrigel-coated 24-well plates. HUVECs (2 × 105 cells per well) were seeded beneath transwell inserts containing NP-treated MSCs. After 6 hours, tubular networks were imaged, and parameters (nodes, branches, and total length) were quantified using ImageJ 1.54 (NIH, USA).

Intracellular calcium detection

MSCs (5 × 104 cells/ml) were cultured with NPs in osteogenic medium for 72 hours and then incubated with Fluo-4 acetoxymethyl ester (Fluo-4 AM) (Beyotime; 1 μM in PBS) for 30 min. Last, the cells were imaged using an EVOS Imaging System. Fluorescence intensity was analyzed to quantify calcium levels.

SF/GelMA/NP (bio)ink preparations

The SF solution was prepared according to our previous protocols (31). Briefly, silkworm cocoons were cut into small pieces and boiled for 30 min in 0.02 M aqueous Na2CO3 solution for complete degumming. Then, the degummed SF was sufficiently rinsed in distilled water for two times, placed on a flat surface in a fume hood, and dried at room temperature overnight. The dried SF was dissolved in LiBr at a ratio of 1:4 (w/v) at 60°C for 4 hours. The SF/LiBr solution was dialyzed with distilled water for 3 days, and the dialyzed solution was centrifuged twice at 3000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. Next, the precipitate was discarded, and the supernatant was filtered with 0.22-μm filters. The SF solution was packed into a dialysis bag and placed in an ultraclean for concentration. The final concentration of the SF solution was calculated by weighting the remaining mass of the SF solution after evaporation. The high concentration SF solution was diluted to 2.5, 5, and 10% (v/v) with a sterile 1× PBS solution.

The GelMA solution was synthesized as previously reported (75). For GelMA, gelatin was completely dissolved in a 50°C water bath, and then Methacrylic anhydride (MA) was added at a ratio of 10 g/8 ml (substitution of about 90%), and the chemical reaction between gelatin and MA lasted for 2 hours in a 50°C water bath. After dialyzing the GelMA solution with distilled water in a 40°C water bath for a week, the solution was pumped and filtered twice. Next, the GelMA dry spongy was obtained after lyophilization for a week. The GelMA spongy was dissolved in PBS at 2.5, 5, and 10% (w/v) for 1 hour in a 37°C water bath, and the GelMA solutions were filtered with 0.22-μm filters.

A total of 2.5, 5, and 10% SF and 2.5, 5, and 10% GelMA were mixed in a 1:1 ratio to obtain 1.25/1.25, 1.25/2.5, 1.25/5, 2.5/1.25, 2.5/2.5, 2.5/5, 5/1.25, 5/2.5, and 5/5% (v/v) SF/GelMA ink, which was mixed homogeneously according to the total amount of ink. The SF/GelMA (bio)inks were prepared by adding Ru/SPS (Ru and SPS have been dissolved to a reserve concentration and mixed at a ratio of 1:10) to SF/GelMA (bio)inks and mixing thoroughly. The SF/GelMA (bio)ink was stored in the dark and ready for light crosslinking. The appropriate number of cells was digested and centrifuged, the supernatant was taken, and then the photoinitiator-containing ink was added, which could be photocrosslinked. The cell concentration is 1 million/ml, and the NP concentration is 100 μg/ml.

VBP procedure

SF/GelMA/chiral NP objects were volumetrically printed with a volumetric bioprinter VBP-T200 (Green Key Biotechnology, China). The VBP-T200 printer was loaded a light-emitting diode (λ = 525 nm) projector which had the resolution of 912 by 1140 pixels and 50-μm pixel size. The Haomiao (Green Key Biotechnology, China) software was used for printing. All computer-aided design (CAD) models are reconstructed in software, and a print slice file group is created, which is programmed to dynamically project the slice image into the vial. In addition, the (bio)inks were contained in a transparent glass vial with a diameter of 1.2 cm and a height of 2.2 cm, placed in the center of a rotating platform at a rotational speed of 6° s−1. After the printing parameters were adjusted according to the concentration of bioink and cell density, the (bio)ink was irradiated with patterned light for 30 to 60 s to build a construct in the vial.

Jaccard similarity index calculation

Jaccard similarity index is a measure of similarity for two samples, with a range of 0 to 100%. The higher percentage means better similarity. The Jaccard similarity indices between the CADs and the resulting prints were calculated by a customized MATLAB (version R2021b) program.

Live/dead imaging

The Sprague-Dawley rat bone marrow MSCs were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Guangzhou, China) for this study. The viability of MSCs within the SF/GelMA/chiral NP constructs was measured by the live/dead viability/cytotoxicity kit (Beyotime, China) after volumetric bioprinting. The volumetrically printed MSC-laden SF/GelMA/chiral NP hydrogels were washed with PBS twice and placed individually in a 5-ml sterile centrifuge tube. Then, calcein-AM/propidium iodide solution was routinely prepared according to the instructions of the supplier [the working solution containing calcein-AM (1 μl ml−1) and propidium iodide (1 μl ml−1) in PBS] and added into the tube. Next, the MSC-laden scaffolds were incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Last, the samples were examined using a fluorescence microscope after washed with PBS twice. The numbers of live and dead cells were counted using ImageJ 1.54 (NIH, USA).

Mechanical property measurement

Mechanical properties of MSC-laden SF/GelMA/chiral NP hydrogels with a diameter of 3 mm and a thickness of 3 mm were characterized. Briefly, the volumetric printed bioscaffolds were removed at the time points of 1, 4, 7, and 14 days of culturing, and after washed with PBS twice, they were tested for mechanical properties. The measurements were conducted using a texture analyzer (TA.XTC-18, Shanghai Baosheng Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China). Each testing group contained three replicates (n = 3), and the strain rate was at 2 mm/min to test the maximum anticompression capacity at a strain of 50% at room temperature. The mechanical testing data were analyzed using Origin (2021, OriginLab, USA). The compression modulus was calculated from the slope of each stress-strain curve in the linear region where the strain is between 5 and 20%.

Gelation time measurement

To monitor physical (bio)ink gelation before printing, the gelation time was recorded at 4°C for different concentrations of GelMA. All GelMA samples were equilibrated at 37°C for at least 10 min before each gelation test to maintain the liquid state. During the experiments, the GelMA samples were transferred from 37°C to an ice bath, and the timer was started. At the designed time points, such as 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 min, the timer was stopped, and the GelMA samples were removed from the ice bath and photographed using a digital camera.

Therapeutic efficacy of scaffolds on CSBD rat model

Eight- to 12-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats (200 to 220 g; Guangzhou Shunyuntian Laboratory Animal Co.) were randomly assigned to five groups: (i) empty defect (control); (ii) SF/GelMA scaffolds alone; (iii) L-CF-NPs + SF/GelMA scaffolds; (iv) A-CF-NPs + SF/GelMA scaffolds; and (v) D-CF-NPs + SF/GelMA scaffolds. Under anesthesia (4% isoflurane in oxygen), a 3-mm-diameter × 3-mm-deep femoral defect was created using a precision drill. The scaffolds were implanted into the defect site. After 4 or 8 weeks, the femora were harvested for analysis via gross observation, micro-CT, and histology.

Micro-CT analysis

Bone regeneration was assessed using a Quantum GX2 micro-CT system at 4 and 8 weeks postsurgery. Scans were conducted at 50 kV and 100 μA with a resolution of 30 μm/pixel. A cylindrical volume of interest (VOI) centered on the defect site was analyzed to quantify BV/TV, BMD, and Tb.N. The VOI had a diameter of 3 mm and a depth of 0.27 mm.

Histological and immunostaining procedures

Harvested bone samples were decalcified in 10% EDTA-2Na (Solarbio, China) at 4°C for 50 days, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5-μm slices. The sections were then stained with H&E, Masson’s trichrome, and TRAP using commercial kits. For immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence, antigen retrieval was performed, followed by incubation with primary antibodies (table S3) and the corresponding secondary antibodies. Fluorescence signals were quantified using ImageJ 1.44 (NIH, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Intergroup differences were analyzed using one-tailed unpaired t test for two groups and a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple groups in GraphPad Prism 10. Significance was set at P < 0.05. All experiments included ≥3 biological replicates.
