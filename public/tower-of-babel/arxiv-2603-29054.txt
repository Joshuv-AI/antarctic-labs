Likelihood-Free Inference via Structured Score Matching
Haoyu Jiang, Yuexi Wang, Yun Yang

Abstract
In many statistical problems, the data distribution is specified through a generative process for which the likelihood function is analytically intractable, yet inference on the associated model parameters remains of primary interest. We develop a likelihood-free inference framework that combines score matching with gradient-based optimization and bootstrap procedures to facilitate parameter estimation together with uncertainty quantification. The proposed methodology introduces tailored score-matching estimators for approximating likelihood score functions, and incorporates an architectural regularization scheme that embeds the statistical structure of log-likelihood scores to improve both accuracy and scalability. We provide theoretical guarantees and demonstrate the practical utility of the method through numerical experiments, where it performs favorably compared to existing approaches.

Introduction

In many scientific domains, including astrophysics
(
38
)
, epidemiology
(
28
;
7
)
, ecology
(
59
;
4
)
, and genetics
(
3
)
, researchers model complex phenomena using forward simulators. These simulators implicitly define a likelihood function but rarely provide it in closed form, rendering traditional statistical methods that rely on explicit likelihood evaluation inapplicable. This framework is commonly referred to as
likelihood-free inference
(LFI) or
simulation-based inference
(SBI)
(
10
)
.

A large body of work in LFI has focused on the Bayesian setting, where a prior over the parameter space is specified and the goal is to approximate the posterior distribution. The most established approach is Approximate Bayesian Computation
(
3
, ABC,)
, which assigns weights to parameter draws based on the similarity between simulated and observed datasets. Much of the research in this area has focused on developing more effective similarity measures, including distances on summary statistics
(
3
;
16
;
41
)

and discrepancies between empirical distributions
(
31
;
5
;
46
;
57
;
9
)
. Another major direction is Bayesian synthetic likelihood
(
49
;
19
)
, which constructs a surrogate likelihood based on summary statistics that are asymptotically normally distributed. More recently, advances in deep learning have enabled direct approximation of the posterior distribution via generative models, such as autoregressive flows
(
43
;
44
;
45
)
, conditional generative adversarial networks
(
58
)
, and conditional diffusion models
(
50
)
.
However, a key limitation of these approaches is that their performance depends heavily on the choice of training objectives and simulation budgets. As a result, the learned posteriors may be biased or poorly calibrated, particularly in regions of the parameter space with limited training coverage
(
18
;
17
)
.

In the frequentist setting, LFI is often referred to as “indirect inference”
(
23
;
30
)
in the econometrics literature, with a focus on parameter estimation, confidence sets and hypothesis testing
(
12
;
13
)
. One major line of work develops estimators by matching simulated and observed datasets
(
36
;
21
;
20
;
32
)
, where the discrepancy functions play a role analogous to the similarity measures in ABC. A second line introduces a tractable auxiliary model and compares simulated and observed datasets through auxiliary estimates, with parameters chosen to minimize their difference
(
23
)
. The more recent direction, similar to the Bayesian setting, utilizes deep neural network or deep generative models to directly approximate the odds ratio
(
13
)
or the Fisher score
(
33
)
from simulated datasets.

In this work, we adopt the frequentist perspective on likelihood-free inference and propose a new score-based estimator for the model parameter, defined as the root of an estimated likelihood score obtained via score matching, together with associated confidence sets. Our approach is motivated by the fact that the MLE can be characterized as the root of the likelihood score. To improve the quality of the score approximation, we use structured score matching, which incorporates key statistical properties of the true likelihood score — such as additivity across samples, the Fisher information identity, and the mean-zero property — directly into the network architecture and training objective (c.f. Section
2.1
). This ensures that the estimated score respects the fundamental structure of the likelihood score and leads to more accurate inference. In addition, we develop a computation procedure for this estimator based on an iterative algorithm that mimics the gradient dynamics of maximizing the log-likelihood function. For uncertainty quantification, we consider three options for constructing confidence sets: using the estimated Fisher information matrix, the Huber sandwich covariance matrix, and multiplier bootstrap methods. We provide theoretical guarantees on the consistency and asymptotic behavior of our estimators, establish bootstrap consistency, and show that the algorithm converges locally at an exponential rate. Empirically, our methods demonstrate strong performance compared to existing frequentist LFI approaches.

2
Problem Setup and Methods

Let
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast}=(X_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,X_{n}^{\ast})
denote a collection of
n
observations drawn from a distribution
\mathbb{P}^{(n)}_{\theta^{\ast}}
in the parametric model family
\big\{\mathbb{P}^{(n)}_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\big\}
,
where each
X^{\ast}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
and
\theta^{\ast}
denotes the true parameter lying in the parameter space
\Theta\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\theta}}
. For simplicity, we assume that for every
\theta\in\Theta
, the distribution
\mathbb{P}^{(n)}_{\theta}
admits a density
p^{(n)}_{\theta}
. In this work, we focus on the i.i.d. setting where
p^{(n)}_{\theta}({\mathbf{X}}_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{\theta}(X_{i})
.

In the likelihood-free inference framework, the likelihood function
\log p^{(n)}_{\theta}({\mathbf{X}}_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{\theta}(X_{i})
may not be directly available. Instead,
\mathbb{P}^{(n)}_{\theta}
is implicitly specified through a data-generating process
{\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}
parameterized by
\theta
, in the following sense: for any
\theta\in\Theta
, if one draws a random vector
{\mathbf{Z}}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})
with i.i.d. components
Z_{i}\sim\mu
for some known distribution
\mu
(e.g.,
{\mathcal{N}}(\bf{0},\,{\mathbf{I}}_{d})
), then
\big({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}(Z_{1}),\ldots,{\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}(Z_{n})\big)\sim\mathbb{P}^{(n)}_{\theta}
. In other words, while the likelihood cannot be directly evaluated, simulation is feasible: for any parameter
\theta\in\Theta
, one can readily use the simulator
{\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}
to generate pseudo-datasets
{\bf X}_{m}\sim\mathbb{P}^{(m)}_{\theta}
of arbitrary size
m
.

Example 1

(A toy example)
.

As a simple illustration, consider data generated as
X=\exp(Z_{1})+Z_{2}
, where

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}Z_{1}\\
Z_{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{1}\\
\theta_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0.2\\
0.2&1\end{pmatrix}\right).

Here
\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
is the parameter of interest. Despite the simplicity of this model, its likelihood
p(X|\,\theta)
is analytically intractable, even though the model is regular in the sense that it satisfies classical asymptotic properties such as consistency, asymptotic normality, and efficiency of the maximum likelihood estimator
(
54
)
.

Other problems involving intractable likelihoods, such as complex or highly nonlinear latent variable models
(
35
;
47
)
, can likewise be addressed using the likelihood-free inference framework, as they permit efficient sampling despite the difficulty of direct likelihood evaluation.

One line of work in the likelihood-free inference literature proposes replacing the intractable likelihood with a surrogate objective that facilitates parameter estimation
(
24
;
3
, e.g.,)
. While these surrogate-based methods offer computational advantages, they may lead to a loss in statistical efficiency. In this work, we aim to directly approximate the maximum likelihood estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{\text{\rm MLE}}_{n}
, defined as

\widehat{\theta}^{\text{\rm MLE}}_{n}=\arg\max_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{\theta}(X_{i}).

The MLE is known to be asymptotically efficient, in the sense that its asymptotic variance attains the Cramér–Rao lower bound
(
54
)
. Moreover, to enable uncertainty quantification, we aim to construct a confidence set
R(\mathcal{X}_{n}^{*})
(centered around
\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}}
) for
\theta^{*}
with asymptotic
(1-\alpha)
nominal coverage, that is,

\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{*}}^{(n)}\big(\theta^{*}\in R({\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{*})\big)\to 1-\alpha,\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Before delving into the technical details, we first introduce some notation. We use the shorthand
s^{\ast}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})=\nabla_{\theta}\log p^{(n)}_{\theta}({\mathbf{X}}_{n})
to denote the true likelihood score.
We denote the Fisher information matrix at parameter value
\theta
by
I(\theta)={\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim p_{\theta}}\big[-\nabla_{\theta}s^{\ast}(\theta,X)\big]={\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim p_{\theta}}\big[-\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\log p_{\theta}(X)\big]
.

In the remainder of this section, we first introduce a structured score matching procedure, which tailors the generic score matching approach
(
27
)
to the estimation of the likelihood score, and then describe how we construct the score-based estimator and confidence sets based on it.

2.1
Structured score matching

Our estimator relies on the likelihood score. Since a likelihood-free framework does not assume the availability of a tractable likelihood, we employ the score matching technique proposed by
27
,
51
and
37
to approximate the likelihood score
\nabla_{\theta}\log p^{(n)}_{\theta}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})
with a score model
s_{\phi}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}):{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\theta}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{np}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\theta}}
, parametrized by some parameter
\phi
(e.g., a neural network). When the sample size
n
is large, learning or approximating a generic nonlinear function with input dimension
d_{\theta}+np
may require an enormous number of training samples and parameters in
\phi
. This motivates us to later impose additional structure to simplify the score network architecture.

Specifically, to estimate the likelihood score, we minimize the expected squared distance
between the true score
s^{\ast}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
and its estimator
s_{\phi}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
. This yields the following optimization procedure:

\min_{\phi}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})\sim p(\theta)p^{(n)}_{\theta}({\mathbf{X}}_{n})}\Big[\big\|s_{\phi}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})-s^{*}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})\big\|^{2}\Big],

where
p(\theta)
is a sampling distribution that controls the weighting of the score estimation loss across the parameter space
\Theta
.
The sampling distribution
p(\theta)
is assumed to have full support over
\Theta
. For example, one may choose it as a uniform distribution over an expert-specified range or as a Gaussian distribution centered at a rough estimate
\theta^{(0)}

(
33
)
.

Remark 1

(A two-round procedure)
.

The quality of our estimators depends on how well
s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
approximates the true score
s^{*}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
in a neighborhood of
\theta^{\ast}
(see
1
). In high-dimensional settings where
p
is large, however, the weighting distribution
p(\theta)
may place little mass in this neighborhood, yielding a poor estimate of
s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{*})
. To address this, one may adopt a two-round procedure: in the first round, simulations are drawn from the uninformative proposal
p(\theta)
to obtain an initial estimator and its confidence set; in the second round, the sampling distribution is refined to concentrate around the initial estimate, and
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
is re-estimated, leading to improved score approximation and more accurate inference. We implement the two-round procedure in our empirical analysis in
Section

4
and include a comparison with the estimators from the first round in Appendix.

Under mild conditions (see
26
;
29
for details), the score matching optimization problem is equivalent to

\begin{split}&\min_{\phi}\;{\mathbb{E}}_{(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})\sim p(\theta)p^{(n)}_{\theta}({\mathbf{X}}_{n})}\Big[\left\lVert s_{\phi}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})\right\rVert^{2}+2s_{\phi}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}\log p(\theta)+2\,\text{tr}\!\big(\nabla_{\theta}s_{\phi}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})\big)\Big],\end{split}

(1)

where
\text{tr}(A)
denotes the matrix trace. We include a proof in the Appendix, adapted from
(
26
;
29
)
, to keep our presentation self-contained.
This equivalent formulation enables score matching without explicitly computing
s^{\ast}
, by instead solving an empirical risk minimization problem over
\phi
.

However, as previously noted, the vanilla score matching strategy may require large training samples and may fail to capture the statistical structure of the true score, which is essential for our estimators (for reasons clarified later). To address this, we adopt the structured score matching approach, where a similar setting under the Bayesian framework was considered in
29
. We incorporate three universal properties of the likelihood score into both the network architecture and the training objective.

Here we briefly review three key statistical properties of
s^{*}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
and how they are incorporated into
s_{\phi}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
. From classical statistical theory, under suitable regularity conditions, the true likelihood score satisfies the following properties
(
54
, see, e.g., Chapter 5.5 of)
:
1.
(additive structure)

s^{\ast}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s^{\ast}(\theta,X_{i})
.
2.
(curvature structure)

{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[s^{\ast}(\theta,X)s^{\ast}(\theta,X)^{\top}+\nabla_{\theta}s^{\ast}(\theta,X)\right]=0
.
3.
(mean-zero structure)

{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim p_{\theta}}[s^{\ast}(\theta,X)]=0
.
Accordingly, the estimated score function
s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
should inherit these properties. In particular, the curvature property implies two equivalent definitions of the Fisher information matrix:

I(\theta)={\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim p_{\theta}}\big[-\nabla_{\theta}s^{\ast}(\theta,X)\big]={\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim p_{\theta}}\big[s^{\ast}(\theta,X)\,s^{\ast}(\theta,X)^{\top}\big]
.
For the additive structure, we impose
s_{\phi}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{\phi}(\theta,X_{i})
.
For the curvature structure, we require
{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{\theta}}[s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,X)s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,X)^{\top}+\nabla_{\theta}s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,X)]\approx 0
, which is enforced through a regularization penalty.
For the mean-zero structure,
{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{\theta}}[s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,X)]\approx 0
, which is achieved via a second-step debiasing regression.
We also include the detailed description of the algorithm in Appendix. For notational simplicity, we henceforth denote the estimated score function as
\widehat{s}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})=s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
.

Remark 2

(Choice of score matching scheme)
.

While denoising score matching
(
56
)
is often preferred in diffusion models, we instead adopt direct score matching in (
1
). This choice allows a more transparent incorporation of the underlying statistical structure and is well suited to SBI settings, which typically involve low- to moderate-dimensional parameters. In particular, the resulting additive structure reduces network complexity, while the mean-zero and curvature conditions enable precise analysis and control of error accumulation over
n
observations (see
Section

3
). In contrast, although denoising score matching avoids evaluating the Jacobian trace
\nabla_{\theta}s_{\phi}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}_{n}})
, the Gaussian convolution step destroys the conditional i.i.d. structure of
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}
given the perturbed parameter
\tilde{\theta}
. As a result, the statistical structure exploited in our analysis no longer applies. Recent work
(
22
;
1
)
incorporates additive structures within denoising score matching, but the nonzero Gaussian perturbation introduces bias, and how this bias accumulates with
n
has not yet been fully analyzed.
To mitigate the computational cost associated with Jacobian evaluation, one may also consider sliced score matching variants
(
52
;
41
)
.

2.2
Score-based estimator

Under mild conditions, the likelihood score function
s^{\ast}(\theta,\,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s^{\ast}(\theta,X_{i}^{\ast})
, viewed as a function of the parameter
\theta
, admits a unique solution
(
54
, see, e.g., Section 5.6 of)
within a neighborhood
\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};r_{0})=\big\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}:\,\|\theta-\theta^{\ast}\|\leq r_{0}\big\}
of
\theta^{\ast}
, where
r_{0}>0
is a fixed radius such that the Fisher information matrix
I(\theta)
is locally positive definite. The MLE
\widehat{\theta}^{\text{\rm MLE}}_{n}
can be characterized as the root of the score function in this neighborhood, i.e.,

\widehat{\theta}^{\text{\rm MLE}}_{n}=\Big\{\theta\in\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};r_{0}):\sum_{i=1}^{n}s^{\ast}(\theta,X_{i}^{\ast})=0\Big\}.

(2)

Motivated by this, we define our score-based estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
for approximating
\widehat{\theta}^{\text{\rm MLE}}_{n}
as the root of the estimated score
\widehat{s}
, that is,

\widehat{\theta}_{n}=\Big\{\theta\in\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};r_{0}):\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{s}(\theta,X_{i}^{\ast})=0\Big\}.

(3)

Our theoretical results in Section
3
establish that the estimated score
\widehat{s}
admits at least one root in
\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};r_{0})
, and moreover that this root is unique under suitable assumptions on the quality of the estimated score, which ensures the estimator is well-defined. Showing the existence of such a root is not trivial (see the discussion after Theorem
1
), since the estimated score
\widehat{s}
may not be the gradient of a function; therefore, one cannot simply invoke the existence of a critical point (or more precisely, a local minimum) of a continuous function.

In practical implementation, we propose to numerically compute
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
as the limit of the following iterative scheme:

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}+\alpha H_{t}\,\widehat{s}(\theta^{(t)},\,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast}),\quad t\geq 0,

(4)

where
\alpha
is the step size and
H_{t}
is a preconditioning matrix. In practice, one may either use a random initialization or apply a computationally inexpensive method such as the simulated method of moments
(
36
;
42
)
to set
\theta^{(0)}
. Our numerical experiments suggest that random initialization alone is already sufficient.

Note that procedure (
4
) coincides with a preconditioned gradient ascent algorithm for maximizing the log-likelihood
\log p_{\theta}^{(n)}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})
when
\widehat{s}
is replaced by the true likelihood score
s^{\ast}
.
Moreover, any stationary point of this recursion is a root of
\widehat{s}(\,\cdot\,,\,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})
. In Section
3
, we further show that Algorithm (
4
) converges exponentially fast to
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
when initialized within the neighborhood
\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};r_{0})
. This is because our score-matching method enforces curvature matching, ensuring that the Jacobian
\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}(\theta^{(t)},\,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})
remains close to the true likelihood-score Jacobian
\nabla_{\theta}s^{\ast}(\theta^{(t)},\,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})
(i.e., the Hessian of
\log p_{\theta}^{(n)}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})
). As a result, the iterative scheme (
4
) inherits the (local) strong convexity and smoothness properties and thus effectively mimics the gradient dynamics of maximizing the log-likelihood function (c.f. Theorem
5
).

Regarding the preconditioning matrix
H_{t}
, a simple choice is to set
H_{t}\equiv\bm{I}_{d_{\theta}}
, which corresponds to the vanilla gradient ascent algorithm. However, thanks to the curvature matching property of our score-matching method, a more algorithmically efficient choice is
H_{t}=\big[\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}(\theta^{(t)},\,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})\big]^{-1}
, which corresponds to the Newton–Raphson algorithm
(
6
, see, e.g.,)
for maximizing the log-likelihood, whose exact counterpart enjoys super-exponential convergence. In particular, since we approximate the score function
\widehat{s}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
with a neural network, the derivative of the score network can be efficiently computed via automatic differentiation
(
2
)
.

When the parameter dimension
d_{\theta}
is large, repeatedly computing a full matrix inverse at each iteration may be computationally expensive. In such cases, one may instead set
H_{t}\equiv\big[\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}(\theta^{(0)},\,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})\big]^{-1}
, provided a good initialization
\theta^{(0)}
is available (possibly obtained via a short pilot run). This corresponds to a quasi-Newton–type algorithm
(
39
, see, e.g.,)
for maximizing the log-likelihood. In addition, classical statistical theory on one-step MLE
(
54
, see, e.g., Section 5.7 of)
shows that once
\theta^{(t)}
enters an
o_{p}(1)
neighborhood of
\theta^{\ast}
, a single Newton–Raphson update is sufficient to obtain an estimator that is asymptotically equivalent to the MLE. In our case, although we only have access to an approximate score curvature
\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}
, the curvature-matching penalty ensures that
\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}
remains close to the true curvature
\nabla_{\theta}s^{\ast}
of the log-likelihood, so only a few iterations are sufficient to obtain a statistically optimal estimator. See Fig.
1
in Section
4
for a numerical demonstration.

2.3
Confidence interval construction

According to Theorem
2
in Section
3
, the score-based estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
is asymptotically indistinguishable from the MLE
\widehat{\theta}^{\text{\rm MLE}}_{n}
. From classical asymptotic normality of the MLE, we know

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}^{\text{\rm MLE}}_{n}-\theta^{*})\to{\mathcal{N}}(0,[I(\theta^{*})]^{-1}),\ \ n\to\infty,

where recall that
I(\theta^{*})={\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim p_{\theta^{\ast}}}[-\nabla_{\theta}s^{*}(\theta^{\ast},X)]
is the Fisher information matrix evaluated at
\theta^{\ast}
.
Motivated by this result, we present three approaches for constructing confidence intervals based on the estimated score
\widehat{s}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
. The first two rely on plug-in methods that replace
I(\theta^{\ast})
with a consistent estimator, while the third is based on the (multiplier) bootstrap.

Option 1: Information matrix.

The first approach is based on directly approximating the information matrix
I(\theta^{\ast})
by taking an empirical average of the negative Jacobian of the estimated score
\widehat{s}
. Specifically, we consider the following simple plug-in estimator:

\widehat{I}(\widehat{\theta}_{n})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\Big(\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}(\theta,X_{i}^{\ast})+\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}(\theta,X_{i}^{\ast})^{\top}\Big)\Big|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}_{n}},

(5)

where the average of the Jacobian and its transpose is taken to ensure that
\widehat{I}(\widehat{\theta}_{n})
is symmetric.

The corresponding confidence set
R({\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{*})
for
\theta^{\ast}
with significance level
1-\alpha\in(0,1)
is then constructed as

R({\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{*})=\big\{\theta:n(\theta-\widehat{\theta}_{n})^{T}\widehat{I}(\widehat{\theta}_{n})(\theta-\widehat{\theta}_{n})\leq\chi^{2}_{d_{\theta},1-\alpha}\big\}.

where
\chi^{2}_{d_{\theta},1-\alpha}
is the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the chi-square distribution with
d_{\theta}
degree of freedom. In addition, marginal confidence intervals for each coordinate
\theta_{j}^{\ast}
with level
1-\alpha\in(0,1)
can be constructed as

R_{j}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{*})=\Big\{\,\theta_{j}:\big|\theta_{j}-[\widehat{\theta}_{n}]_{j}\big|\leq z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\big[[\widehat{I}(\widehat{\theta}_{n})]^{-1}\big]_{jj}}\,\Big\},

where
[\bm{a}]_{j}
denotes the
j
-th coordinate of a generic vector
\bm{a}
,
[A]_{ij}
denotes the
(i,j)
-th entry of a generic matrix
A
, and
z_{1-\alpha}
is the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the standard normal distribution.

Another plug-in estimate for the Fisher information matrix, based on the curvature property, can be specified as

\widehat{I}(\widehat{\theta}_{n})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{*})\,\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{*})^{T}\Big)

(6)

Our empirical results in
Section

4
show that the (
5
) consistently outperform (
6
).

Option 2: Sandwich covariance matrix.

Because our estimator is defined as the root of the estimated score rather than that of the true score, it may be viewed as an
M
-estimator under a potentially misspecified model, since the curvature constraint
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\theta}}\!\big[s^{\ast}(\theta,X)s^{\ast}(\theta,X)^{\top}+\nabla_{\theta}s^{\ast}(\theta,X)\big]=0
that holds for the true score
s^{\ast}
may not be satisfied by
\widehat{s}
. In this case, a more robust and accurate alternative is the Huber sandwich covariance estimator
(
25
)
, defined as

\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}=\big(\widehat{I}(\widehat{\theta}_{n})\big)^{-1}\Bigg[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{\ast})\,\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{\ast})^{\top}\Bigg]\big(\widehat{I}(\widehat{\theta}_{n})\big)^{-1}

with
\widehat{I}(\widehat{\theta}_{n})
being given in (
5
). Note that when a sample version of the curvature constraint holds for
\widehat{s}
, then
\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}
reduces to
\big(\widehat{I}(\widehat{\theta}_{n})\big)^{-1}
.

Analogous to Option 1, we construct the confidence set for
\theta^{\ast}
with level
1-\alpha\in(0,1)
using the sandwich covariance matrix as

R({\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})=\Big\{\theta:n(\theta-\widehat{\theta}_{n})^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1}(\theta-\widehat{\theta}_{n})\leq\chi^{2}_{d_{\theta},1-\alpha}\Big\},

together with the corresponding marginal confidence intervals.

The sandwich covariance matrix formula was also considered by
19
in the context of Bayesian synthetic likelihood. We provide a detailed discussion about our differences in the Appendix.

Option 3: Bootstrap.

Our final option for uncertainty quantification is the bootstrap
(
15
)
.
In particular, we consider the multiplier bootstrap
(
55
, see, e.g., Section 2.9 of)
, where for each bootstrap replicate
b=1,2,\ldots,B
, we obtain
\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{(b)}
as the root of the weighted estimated score equation

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}^{(b)}\,\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{(b)},X_{i}^{*})=0,

(7)

with
\{\omega_{i}^{(b)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
i.i.d. random multipliers satisfying
{\mathbb{E}}[\omega_{i}^{(b)}]=1
and
{\rm Var}(\omega_{i}^{(b)})=1
.
A common choice is
\omega_{i}^{(b)}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\text{Exp}(1)
. Following the same argument as earlier, one can show that the bootstrap estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{(b)}
is well defined (exists and is locally unique).

Under mild conditions, the conditional distribution of

\sqrt{n}\,(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{(b)}-\widehat{\theta}_{n})
given
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast}
(with randomness arising from the multipliers) approximates the distribution of
\sqrt{n}\,(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta^{\ast})
(cf. Theorem
4
).
Consequently, a confidence set for
\theta^{\ast}
at significance level
1-\alpha\in(0,1)
can be constructed as

R({\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{*})=\Big\{\theta:(\theta-\widehat{\theta}_{n})^{\top}H(\theta-\widehat{\theta}_{n})\leq\widehat{q}_{H,1-\alpha}\Big\},

for any positive definite matrix
H
, where
\widehat{q}_{H,1-\alpha}
denotes the empirical
(1-\alpha)
-th quantile of the bootstrap quantities

\big\{(\theta^{(b)}-\widehat{\theta}_{n})^{\top}H(\theta^{(b)}-\widehat{\theta}_{n})\big\}_{b=1}^{B}
.
Furthermore, we can directly construct marginal confidence intervals for each coordinate
\theta_{j}
as

R_{j}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{*})=\Big[[\widehat{\theta}_{n}]_{j}+\widehat{a}_{n},\;[\widehat{\theta}_{n}]_{j}+\widehat{b}_{n}\Big],

where
\widehat{a}_{n}
and
\widehat{b}_{n}
denote the empirical
(\alpha/2)
-th and
(1-\alpha/2)
-th quantiles of

\big\{[\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{(b)}]_{j}-[\widehat{\theta}_{n}]_{j}\big\}_{b=1}^{B}
, respectively.

Remark 3

(Comparison of three options)
.

Our numerical results in Section
4
suggest that the sandwich covariance plug-in method and the bootstrap method perform better than the information matrix plug-in method, with the former two showing very similar performance. This is consistent with our expectation that the sandwich method provides a more robust and accurate approximation to the asymptotic covariance matrix, while under model misspecification, the multiplier bootstrap is known to also approximate the asymptotic normal limiting distribution with the correct sandwich covariance structure
(
55
)
.

3
Theoretical Results

Our estimator and its confidence sets are built from the estimated score function
\widehat{s}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
, and their accuracy depends on how closely
\widehat{s}(\cdot,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{*})
approximates the true score
s^{*}(\cdot,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{*})
. We begin by introducing conditions that characterize this approximation. Let
\|A\|_{\rm F}=\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}^{2}}
denote the matrix Frobenius norm.

Assumption 1

(Uniform score-matching error)
.

In the neighborhood
\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};r_{0})
for some
r_{0}>0
, there exist sequences
\varepsilon_{i,n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for
i=1,2,3
, such that the score-matching error, curvature-matching error, and mean-matching error are uniformly bounded as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1,n}^{2}:=\sup_{\theta\in B(\theta^{\ast};r_{0})}{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim p_{\theta^{\ast}}}\big[\left\lVert\widehat{s}(\theta,X)-s^{\ast}(\theta,X)\right\rVert^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2,n}^{2}:=\sup_{\theta\in B(\theta^{\ast};r_{0})}\left\lVert{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim p_{\theta}}\big[\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}(\theta,X)+\widehat{s}(\theta,X)\widehat{s}(\theta,X)^{T}\big]\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{3,n}^{2}:=\sup_{\theta\in B(\theta^{\ast};r_{0})}\left\lVert{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim p_{\theta}}\big[\widehat{s}(\theta,X)\big]\right\rVert^{2}.

Since the structured score matching procedure targets the single-data score
s^{\ast}(\theta,X_{i})
rather than the full-data score
s^{\ast}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
, the estimation errors do not explicitly depend on the sample size
n
. However, for the overall estimation error to vanish as
n
grows, these errors themselves must decay to zero. Fortunately, with sufficiently many simulated datasets, the three errors can be made arbitrarily small. Specifically, the score-matching error
\varepsilon_{1,n}
can be characterized using generalization results for score estimation
(
40
)
. The curvature-matching error
\varepsilon_{2,n}
can be controlled through an appropriate choice of regularization. The mean-matching error
\varepsilon_{3,n}
stems from Monte Carlo approximation of the expected score and is of order
O(m^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, where
m
is the Monte Carlo sample size.

Theorem 1

(Existence, Uniqueness and Consistency)
.

Under Assumption
1
and other mild regularity assumptions in the Appendix, it holds with probability converging to
1
as
n\to\infty
that
\widehat{s}(\cdot,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})
has a unique root
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
within
\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};r_{0})
, and
\widehat{\theta}_{n}\to\theta^{\ast}
in probability as
n\to\infty
.

It is worth highlighting that our proof of the existence of a root of
\widehat{s}(\cdot,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})=0
does not follow the standard approach, because
\widehat{s}(\cdot,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})
is not necessarily the gradient of any function. To this end, we construct an auxiliary objective function
g(\theta)=\|\widehat{s}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})\|^{2}
and show that
g(\theta)
admits at least one local minimum
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
in
B(\theta^{\ast};r_{0})
. The first-order optimality condition, together with the nonsingularity of
\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
over
B(\theta^{\ast};r_{0})
, then implies
\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})=\bm{0}
.

Theorem 2

(
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
is close to
\widehat{\theta}^{\text{\rm MLE}}_{n}
)
.

Under the same assumptions as
Theorem

1
, it holds with probability converging to
1
as
n\to\infty
that the difference between our estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
in (
3
) and MLE
\widehat{\theta}^{\text{\rm MLE}}_{n}
defined in (
2
) can be bounded as

\displaystyle\big\|\,\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\widehat{\theta}^{\text{\rm MLE}}_{n}\big\|\leq C\,(n^{-1/2}\varepsilon_{1,n}+n^{-1/2}\varepsilon_{2,n}+\varepsilon_{3,n}\big).

Theorem 3

(Asymptotic normality of
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
)
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

1
,
if the mean-matching error satisfies
\varepsilon_{3,n}=o(n^{-1/2})
as
n\to\infty
, then our estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
satisfies

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta^{\ast})\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}\big(0,\big[I(\theta^{\ast})\big]^{-1}\big),\text{ as }n\to\infty.

To characterize bootstrap consistency, we adopt the standard notion of convergence in distribution in probability
(
55
)
, which we denote by
\overset{d^{\ast}}{\to}
. Concretely, for random probability measures
Q_{n}^{\ast}
(e.g., bootstrap laws) and a fixed law
Q
, we write
Q_{n}^{\ast}\overset{d^{\ast}}{\to}Q
if
d_{\mathrm{BL}}(Q_{n}^{\ast},Q)\overset{p}{\to}0
,
n\to\infty
, where
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
is the bounded Lipschitz distance.

Theorem 4

(Bootstrap consistency)
.

Under the same assumptions as
Theorem

3
, the multiplier bootstrap estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{(b)}
defined in (
7
) satisfies

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}^{(b)}-\widehat{\theta}_{n})\overset{d^{\ast}}{\to}\mathcal{N}\big(0,\big[I(\theta^{\ast})\big]^{-1}\big),\ \ \mbox{as }n\to\infty.

Since the limiting distribution of
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}^{(b)}-\widehat{\theta}_{n})
coincides with that of
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta^{\ast})
, this justifies the use of bootstrap empirical quantiles to construct asymptotically valid confidence sets.

Theorem 5

(Algorithmic convergence)
.

Under the same conditions as
Theorem

3
, if the initial value
\theta^{(0)}
is drawn from the neighborhood
\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};r_{0})
in
1
,
and additionally assume
h_{\min}\,\bm{I}_{d_{\theta}}\preceq H_{t}\preceq h_{\max}\,\bm{I}_{d_{\theta}}
for positive constants
(m,L,h_{\min},h_{\max})>0
. Then the iterates of Algorithm in (
4
) satisfy

\displaystyle\big\|\theta^{(t)}-\widehat{\theta}_{n}\big\|\leq c\,\rho^{t}\,\big\|\theta^{(0)}-\widehat{\theta}_{n}\big\|,\quad t\geq 0,

for some constant
c>0
and
\rho=\max\{|1-c_{1}\alpha h_{\min}|,\,|1-c_{2}\alpha h_{\max}|\}
,
where the constants
(c_{1},c_{2})
only depend on the condition number of
I(\theta^{\ast})
.

According to this theorem, Algorithm (
4
) mimics the gradient ascent algorithm for maximizing the log-likelihood function
\log p_{\theta}^{(n)}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})
, and achieves exponential convergence for any stepsize
\alpha\in\big(0,(c_{2}h_{\max})^{-1}\big)
.

4.3
The g-and-k Model

The g-and-k model is widely used in robust statistical modeling because of its ability to flexibly capture non-normal features such as skewness and heavy tails while remaining relatively simple to simulate from. It is commonly applied in fields such as finance
(
14
)
, insurance
(
48
)
, and risk modeling, where data often exhibit asymmetry and extreme values that violate Gaussian assumptions. It is defined implicitly through its quantile function
P^{-1}_{\theta}(u)=Q(z(u);\theta)
where
z(\cdot)
is the quantile of
{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
and
\theta=(A,B,g,k)
. The
Q
function is defined as

Q(z;A,B,g,k)=A+B\Big[1+c\cdot\text{tanh}(gz/2)\Big]z(1+z^{2})^{k}.

where parameters
A,B,g,k
control location, scale, skewness, and tail heaviness, respectively. Despite its flexibility and interpretability, the likelihood function of the g-and-k model is intractable. The simulation study on the g-and-k model is provided in
Section

B.2
.

Here we illustrate the performance of our method to the
Garch
exchange rate dataset from the Ecdat package
(
11
)
. It consists of 1967 daily US dollar exchange rates against other currencies from 1980 to 1987. We focus on the exchange rate with Canadian Dollars.
We use a g-and-k distribution to model the log return.
We fit the dataset using our two-round score matching procedure, with more details provided in
Appendix

D
.
We evaluate the model fit using a Q–Q plot that compares the observed data with samples generated from the fitted g-and-k model and from a fitted normal baseline in
Figure

2
.

Figure 2
:
Q–Q plot comparing the fitted g-and-k/normal distribution with the observed data

As shown in
Figure

2
, the g-and-k distribution provides a much better fit to this heavy-tailed log-return data than the normal distribution. Furthermore, the second-round estimates show a noticeable improvement over the first round, confirming the effectiveness of the two-round procedure.

5
Discussion

This work proposes a new framework for likelihood-free inference by using the basic fact that the MLE is the root of the likelihood score. By approximating the score rather than the likelihood itself, we build estimators and confidence sets that remain closely connected to likelihood-based inference. In particular, by adding statistical structure into the score network, we improve score estimation and enhance uncertainty quantification.

For future work, one promising direction is to add additional structural constraints such as sparsity or symmetry to the score Jacobian, which could further improve estimation. We are also interested in extending the method beyond the i.i.d. setting, for example to dependent time-series or spatial data, and in adapting the approach to regression problems.

Appendix A
Proofs

Before presenting the proofs of the theorems in the main paper, we introduce some common notation and regularity assumptions required for the results.

Notations

We use
\sigma_{\min}(A)
to denote the smallest singular value of a matrix
A
.
In the proofs, we frequently apply Taylor expansions to each coordinate of

\widehat{s}(\cdot,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})
and
\nabla\widehat{s}(\cdot,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})
,
that is, to
\widehat{s}_{j}(\cdot,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast}):{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\theta}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and

\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}_{ij}(\cdot,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast}):{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\theta}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
both viewed as scalar-valued functions of
\theta
.
Note that when performing element-wise Taylor expansions,
the remainder term may be evaluated at different intermediate parameter values
for different components of the function.
Let
\theta_{A},\theta_{B}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\theta}}
denote two generic parameter vectors. We will consider three types of Taylor expansions:

•

Scenario 1:

\widehat{s}(\theta_{B},{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})=\widehat{s}(\theta_{A},{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})+H_{n}(\widehat{s},\theta_{A},\theta_{B})(\theta_{B}-\theta_{A}),

where

\displaystyle H_{n}(\widehat{s},\theta_{A},\theta_{B}):=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla(\widehat{s}-s^{\ast})_{1}(\theta_{1}^{\prime},{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})\\
\vdots\\
\nabla(\widehat{s}-s^{\ast})_{d_{\theta}}(\theta_{d_{\theta}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\theta}\times d_{\theta}},

(9)

and each
\theta_{j}^{\prime}=c_{j}\theta_{A}+(1-c_{j})\theta_{B}
for some
c_{j}\in[0,1]
.

•

Scenario 2:

\displaystyle\widehat{s}(\theta_{B},{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})=\widehat{s}(\theta_{A},{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})+\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}(\theta_{A},{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})(\theta_{B}-\theta_{A})+\frac{1}{2}T_{n}(\widehat{s},\theta_{A},\theta_{B})[\theta_{B}-\theta_{A},\theta_{B}-\theta_{A}],

(10)

where
T_{n}(\widehat{s},\theta_{A},\theta_{B})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\theta}\times d_{\theta}\times d_{\theta}}
,
and its
i
-th slice is the Hessian matrix
\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\widehat{s}_{i}(\theta_{i}^{\prime},{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})

evaluated at
\theta_{i}^{\prime}=c_{i}\theta_{A}+(1-c_{i})\theta_{B}
for some
c_{i}\in[0,1]
.
Here, for
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and a tensor
A=[A_{1},\dots,A_{p}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times d\times d}

with
A_{1},\dots,A_{p}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
, we denote

A[z,z]:=(z^{\top}A_{1}z,\dots,z^{\top}A_{p}z)^{\top},\quad A[z]:=(A_{1}^{\top}z,\dots,A_{p}^{\top}z)^{\top}.

•

Scenario 3:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}(\theta_{B},{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})=\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}(\theta_{A},{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})+T_{n}(\widehat{s},\theta_{A},\theta_{B})[\theta_{B}-\theta_{A}],

(11)

where
T_{n}(\widehat{s},\theta_{A},\theta_{B})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\theta}\times d_{\theta}\times d_{\theta}}
,
and its
(i,j)
-th element is

\nabla_{\theta}\!\left\{\frac{\partial\widehat{s}_{j}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})}{\partial\theta_{[i]}}\right\}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\theta}},

evaluated at
\theta_{i,j}^{\prime}=c_{i,j}\theta_{A}+(1-c_{i,j})\theta_{B}
for some
c_{i,j}\in[0,1]
.
Here,
\theta_{[i]}
denotes the
i
-th coordinate of
\theta
.
The operation
T_{n}(\widehat{s},\theta_{A},\theta_{B})[\theta_{B}-\theta_{A}]

computes the inner product between
(\theta_{B}-\theta_{A})
and each
(i,j)
-th element of

T_{n}(\widehat{s},\theta_{A},\theta_{B})
.

Now we impose additional regularity assumptions on the model and the likelihood score functions.

Assumption 2

(Nonsingular Fisher information)
.

\sigma_{\min}\big(I(\theta^{\ast})\big)\geq m
for some
m>0
.

Since the true Fisher information matrix
I(\theta^{\ast})
is always positive semi-definite, this condition ensures that
I(\theta^{*})\succeq mI
, i.e.,
\big(I(\theta^{*})-mI\big)
is positive semi-definite. Such a condition is commonly imposed to guarantee that the true model parameter is identifiable.

Assumption 3

(Regularity conditions on Score)
.

For some
\delta>0
and any
x

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};\delta)}\lVert\widehat{s}(\theta,x)\rVert^{2}\leq G(x),\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};\delta)}\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}(\theta,x)\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq G(x),\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};\delta)}\lVert\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\widehat{s}_{j}(\theta,x)\rVert_{F}\leq G(x)

\displaystyle\lVert\widehat{s}(\theta,X)-\widehat{s}(\theta^{\ast},X)\rVert\leq L(X)\lVert\theta-\theta^{\ast}\rVert,\quad{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim P_{\theta^{\ast}}}[L(X)^{2}]<\infty,\forall\theta\in\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};\delta).

The first part of this assumption consists of standard regularity conditions on the likelihood score for parametric models, ensuring that the MLE (or in our context,

The following lemma shows that the difference between the estimated Fisher information matrix

Lemma 2
.

Under Assumptions

We have the following inequalities,

\displaystyle\lVert\widehat{I}(\theta^{\ast})y\rVert

\displaystyle\geq(m-2C_{3}\varepsilon_{1,n}-\varepsilon_{2,n})\lVert y\rVert

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}^{(b)}-\widehat{\theta}_{n})

in the second step.

For term (III), we have

\displaystyle\text{(III)}=\underbrace{-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\omega_{i}-1)\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{\ast})}_{\text{(III.1)}}\underbrace{-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{\ast})}_{\text{(III.2)}}

For term (III.1), we have
{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{\omega}}[\text{(III.1)}\mid{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast}]=\bm{0}
and

\displaystyle\text{Cov}_{P_{\omega}}[\text{(III.1)}\mid{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast}]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{\ast})\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{\ast})^{T}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\ \

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{\omega}}[\lVert\text{(III.1)}\rVert^{2}\mid{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast}]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{\ast})\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{2}(X_{i}^{\ast})\overset{P_{\theta^{\ast}}}{\longrightarrow}0,

as
n\to\infty
,
where the last step follows from
3
. We then invoke Markov’s inequality to obtain that (III.1)
=o_{P_{\omega}}(1)
in
P_{\theta^{\ast}}
-probability. Moreover, by
3
and the uniform law of large numbers, we have (III.2)
=\widehat{I}(\theta^{\ast})+o_{P_{\theta^{\ast}}}(1)
. Therefore, it follows that (III)
=\widehat{I}(\theta^{\ast})+o_{P_{\omega}}(1)
in
P_{\theta^{\ast}}
-probability.

For term (IV), we have
\lVert\widehat{\theta}^{(b)}-\widehat{\theta}_{n}\rVert=o_{P_{\omega}}(1)
in
P_{\theta^{\ast}}
-probability by
Equation

17
. Moreover, since

is bounded using
3
, we have that (IV)
=o_{P_{\omega}}(1)
in
P_{\theta^{\ast}}
-probability.

For term (V), we define

\displaystyle\Delta:=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\omega_{i}-1)\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{\ast})-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\omega_{i}-1)\widehat{s}(\theta^{\ast},X_{i}^{\ast})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\omega_{i}-1)\big[\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{\ast})-\widehat{s}(\theta^{\ast},X_{i}^{\ast})\big].

Then we have
{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{\omega}}[\Delta\mid{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast}]=\bm{0}
, and

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(\Delta\mid{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{\ast})-\widehat{s}(\theta^{\ast},X_{i}^{\ast})\big]\big[\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{\ast})-\widehat{s}(\theta^{\ast},X_{i}^{\ast})\big]^{T}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\ \

\displaystyle E[\lVert\Delta\rVert^{2}\mid{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\widehat{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{n},X_{i}^{\ast})-\widehat{s}(\theta^{\ast},X_{i}^{\ast})\rVert^{2}\leq\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(X_{i}^{\ast})^{2}\overset{P_{\theta^{\ast}}}{\longrightarrow}0,

as
n\to\infty
,
where the second line is by

and the consistency of
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
. Then, invoking Markov’s inequality yields that
\Delta=o_{P_{\omega}}(1)
in
P_{\theta^{\ast}}
-probability.

Combining the bounds for terms (III), (IV) and (V), we can finally conclude

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}^{(b)}-\widehat{\theta}_{n})=\Big\{\big[\widehat{I}(\theta^{\ast})\big]^{-1}+o_{P_{\omega}}(1)\Big\}\bigg\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\omega_{i}-1)\widehat{s}(\theta^{\ast},X_{i}^{\ast})+o_{P_{\omega}}(1)\bigg\},

(18)

with
P_{\theta^{\ast}}
-probability going to

. Then, by the last bounded

in
P_{\theta^{\ast}}
-probability.
Therefore, we apply Lindeberg’s central limit theorem conditional on

and obtain

\displaystyle d_{\text{BL}}\Big(\mathcal{L}\Big(\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{s}(\theta^{\ast},X_{i}^{\ast})\widehat{s}(\theta^{\ast},X_{i}^{\ast})^{T}\Big]^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\omega_{i}-1)\widehat{s}(\theta^{\ast},X_{i}^{\ast})\Big|{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast}\Big),\,\mathcal{N}(0,I)\Big)\overset{P_{\theta^{\ast}}}{\longrightarrow}0

where recall that

, for two generic random variables

and

.

and

A.5
Proof of Theorem
5

Write
g(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{s}(\theta,X_{i}^{\ast})
and recall that
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
is the (locally unique) root of
g
in
\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};r_{0})
, whose existence and uniqueness follow from Theorem
1
. Define the error
e_{t}=\theta^{(t)}-\widehat{\theta}_{n}
. A single iteration of (
4
) yields

e_{t+1}\;=\;e_{t}+\alpha H_{t}\big(g(\theta^{(t)})-g(\widehat{\theta}_{n})\big)\,.

(20)

By the mean value theorem,

g(\theta^{(t)})-g(\widehat{\theta}_{n})\;=\;J_{g}(\tilde{\theta}_{t})\,(\theta^{(t)}-\widehat{\theta}_{n})\;=\;J_{g}(\tilde{\theta}_{t})\,e_{t},

for some
\tilde{\theta}_{t}
on the segment joining
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
and
\theta^{(t)}
, where
J_{g}(\theta)=\nabla_{\theta}g(\theta)
.
Combining above displays gives

e_{t+1}\;=\;\Big(I+\alpha H_{t}J_{g}(\tilde{\theta}_{t})\Big)\,e_{t}.

Let
M_{t}=-H_{t}^{1/2}J_{g}(\tilde{\theta}_{t})H_{t}^{1/2}
.

From Assumption
1
, the estimated score Jacobian
J_{g}(\theta)
is uniformly close to the true likelihood-score Jacobian
\nabla_{\theta}s^{\ast}_{n}(\theta)
over
\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};r_{0})
, and hence inherits its local spectral bounds made in Assumption
2
. Concretely, there exists constants
0<m^{\prime}<L<\infty
such that

m^{\prime}\,\bm{I}_{d_{\theta}}\;\preceq\;-\,J_{g}(\theta)\;\preceq\;L\,\bm{I}_{d_{\theta}},\ \ \text{for all }\theta\in\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\ast};r_{0}).

(21)

Moreover, recall from the conditions of the theorem that we have
0<h_{\min}\,\bm{I}_{d_{\theta}}\preceq H_{t}\preceq h_{\max}\,\bm{I}_{d_{\theta}}
. Therefore, we have the bound

m^{\prime}\,h_{\min}\,\bm{I}_{d_{\theta}}\;\preceq\;M_{t}\;\preceq\;L\,h_{\max}\,\bm{I}_{d_{\theta}},

and the spectrum of
I-\alpha M_{t}
is contained in

\big\{\,1-\alpha\lambda:\ \lambda\in[m^{\prime}h_{\min},\,Lh_{\max}]\,\big\}
. It then follows that

\|e_{t+1}\|_{H_{t}^{-1}}\;=\;\|\big(I-\alpha M_{t}\big)H_{t}^{1/2}e_{t}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\rho\,\|e_{t}\|_{H_{t}^{-1}},

where
\rho=\max\{\,|1-\alpha m^{\prime}h_{\min}|,\ |1-\alpha Lh_{\max}|\,\}
and for a generic matrix
H
, the
\|\cdot\|_{H}
is defined through
\|x\|_{H}^{2}=x^{T}H^{-1}x
.
Since the norms
\|\cdot\|
and
\|\cdot\|_{H_{t}^{-1}}
are equivalent under our condition on
H_{t}
, there is a constant
c\geq 1
(independent of
t
) such that

\|e_{t+1}\|\;\leq\;c\,\rho\,\|e_{t}\|\,.

Finally, we obtain by induction that

\|e_{t}\|\;\leq\;\rho^{t}\,\|e_{0}\|,\qquad t\geq 0,

which is the claimed bound.

A.6
Proof of the score matching objective

We include the proof from
26
;
27
;
29
to keep the presentation self-contained. For notational simplicity, we use the shorthand
X
to denote the data
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast}
, and follow the notations in
29
.
We first introduce two additional regularity conditions required to analyze the score-matching objective.

Assumption 4

(Boundary Condition)
.

For any
X\in{\mathcal{X}}
and score network parameter
\phi
, it holds that

p(\theta)\,p_{\theta}(X)\,s_{\phi}(\theta,X)\to 0

as
\theta
approaches the boundary of the support
\partial\Omega_{\theta\mid X}
of the conditional distribution
p(X|\theta)
(as a function with respect to
\theta
).

Assumption 5

(Finite Moments)
.

For any
\phi
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{(\theta,X)\sim p(\theta)\,p_{\theta}(X)}\big[\|\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(X)\|^{2}\big]

and

{\mathbb{E}}_{(\theta,X)\sim p(\theta)\,p_{\theta}(X)}\big[\|s_{\phi}(\theta,X)\|^{2}\big]

are both finite.

We first rewrite the score-matching objective as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{(\theta,X)\sim p(\theta)p(X\mid\theta)}\left\lVert s_{\phi}(\theta,X)-\nabla_{\theta}\log p(X\mid\theta)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{p(\theta,X)}\left\lVert s_{\phi}(\theta,X)\right\rVert^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}_{p(\theta,X)}\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\log p(X\mid\theta)\right\rVert^{2}-2{\mathbb{E}}_{p(\theta,X)}\Big[s_{\phi}(\theta,X)^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log p(X\mid\theta)\Big].

Here the first two terms are finite under
5
and the last term is also finite due to the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Additionally, the second term
{\mathbb{E}}_{p(\theta,X)}\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(X)\right\rVert^{2}
is a constant in
\phi
can thus can be ignored in the optimization program. The first term does not depend on unknown quantity
p(X\mid\theta)
, so we only need to address the last term.

Denote the joint support of
(\theta,X)
as
\Omega:=\{(\theta,X)\in\Theta\times{\mathcal{X}}:p(\theta)p(X\mid\theta)>0\}
. We denote
\theta_{-j}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{j-1},\theta_{j+1},\theta_{d_{\theta}})^{T}
and the marginal support of
(\theta_{-j},X)
as
\Omega_{(\theta_{-j},X)}:=\{(\theta_{-j},X):(\theta,X)\in\Omega\text{ for some }\theta_{j}\}
. We denote the boundary segments orthogonal to the
j
-th axis at
(\theta_{-j},X)
as
\text{Sec}(\Omega;\theta_{-j},X):=\{\theta_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}:(\theta,X)\in\Omega\}
.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{p(\theta,X)}\Big[s_{\phi}(\theta,X)^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log p(X\mid\theta)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\mathrm{d}X\int_{\Omega(X)}p(\theta)p(X\mid\theta)s_{\phi}(\theta,X)^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log p(X\mid\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\mathrm{d}X\int_{\Omega(X)}p(\theta)\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\theta}}s_{\phi,j}(\theta,X)\nabla_{\theta_{j}}p(X\mid\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\theta}}\int_{\Omega_{(\theta_{j},X)}}\mathrm{d}X\mathrm{d}\theta_{-j}\int_{\text{Sec}(\Omega;\theta_{-j},X)}p(\theta)s_{\phi,j}(\theta,X)\frac{\partial p(X\mid\theta)}{\partial\theta_{j}}\mathrm{d}\theta_{j}.

For each coordinate
j
and the inside integral, assuming
\text{Sec}(\Omega;X,\theta_{-j})
is an interval and denote it as
(a_{j},b_{j})
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\text{Sec}(\Omega;\theta_{-j},X)}p(\theta)s_{\phi,j}(\theta,X)\frac{\partial p(X\mid\theta)}{\partial\theta_{j}}\mathrm{d}\theta_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(\theta)s_{\phi,j}(\theta,X)p(X\mid\theta)\big|_{a_{j}}^{b_{j}}-\int_{\text{Sec}(\Omega;\theta_{-j},X)}\frac{\partial p(\theta)s_{\phi,j}(\theta,X)}{\partial\theta_{j}}p(X\mid\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\text{Sec}(\Omega;\theta_{-j},X)}\Big[\frac{\partial p(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{j}}s_{\phi,j}(\theta,X)+p(\theta)\frac{\partial s_{\phi,j}(\theta,X)}{\partial\theta_{j}}\Big]p(X\mid\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta_{j}\qquad\text{(\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{ass:boundary})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\text{Sec}(\Omega;\theta_{-j},X)}\Big[\frac{\partial p(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{j}}s_{\phi,j}(\theta,X)+\frac{\partial s_{\phi,j}(\theta,X)}{\partial\theta_{j}}\Big]p(\theta)p(X\mid\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta_{j},

which concludes our proof.

Appendix C
Details on Structured Score Matching

Here we provide algorithmic details on how we enforcing the three statistical properties, additive structure, curvature structure and mean-zero structure, on our score networks. A similar procedure under the Bayesian setting was considered in
29
.

We begin with the
additive structure
, which allows us to simplify the score network to
s_{\phi}(\theta,X)
, so that the estimated full-data score becomes
\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{\phi}(\theta,X_{i})
. This structure enables estimation of the common individual-level score function
s^{*}(\theta,X)
via single-data score matching, rathar than directly learning the full-data score
s^{*}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{n})
. Consequently, we train the network
s_{\phi}(\theta,X)
on a reference table
{\mathcal{D}}^{S}=\{(\theta^{(k)},X^{(k)})\}_{k=1}^{N}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}p(\theta)\,p_{\theta}(X)
, which reduces the overall simulation cost from
O(Nn)
to
O(N)
.

To further enhance estimation accuracy, we incorporate the curvature structure by introducing a curvature-matching regularization term into the training objective:

\min_{\phi}{\mathbb{E}}_{p(\theta)}\bigg[{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\theta}}\Big[\underbrace{\|s_{\phi}(\theta,X)-s^{\ast}(\theta,X)\|^{2}}_{\text{score-matching loss on a single $X$}}\Big]+\lambda_{1}\,\underbrace{\Big\|{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\theta}}\!\big[s_{\phi}(\theta,X)s_{\phi}(\theta,X)^{T}+\nabla_{\theta}s_{\phi}(\theta,X)\big]\Big\|_{F}^{2}}_{\text{curvature-matching loss}}\bigg],

(22)

where the hyperparameter
\lambda_{1}>0
controls the curvature penalty and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm. In practice, the expectation are replaced by empirical averages. As shown in our theoretical analysis in
Section

3
, the curvature structure ensures that the distribution of our estimator preserves the local geometry of the true MLE.

Finally, we impose the
mean-zero
structure through a post-processing debiasing step. Specifically, we center the estimated score network by subtracting its mean under the model
\widehat{h}(\theta):={\mathbb{E}}_{X_{1}\sim p_{\theta}}[s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,X_{1})]
from

s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,x)
. To estimate
\widehat{h}(\theta)
, we fit a regression model
h_{\psi}(\theta):{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\theta}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\theta}}
parameterized by
\phi
, minimizing the mean-matching loss

\begin{split}\widehat{\psi}=&\arg\min_{\psi}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta)}\bigg[\big\lVert h_{\psi}(\theta)-{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\theta}}\big[s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,X)\big]\big\rVert^{2}\\
&+\lambda_{2}\big\lVert h_{\psi}(\theta)h_{\psi}(\theta)^{T}-\nabla_{\theta}h_{\psi}(\theta)-{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\theta}}\big[s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,X)\big]h_{\psi}(\theta)^{T}-h_{\psi}(\theta){\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\theta}}\big[s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,X)^{T}\big]\big\rVert_{F}^{2}\bigg]\end{split}

(23)

where
\lambda_{2}
controls the curvature penalty. This second penalty guarantees that the debiased score,
\widetilde{s}(\theta,X)=s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,X)-h_{\widehat{\psi}}(\theta)
, continues to satisfy the curvature structure.

To implement (
23
), we generate an additional regression
reference table
{\mathcal{D}}^{R}=\{(\theta^{(l)},{\mathbf{X}}^{(l)}_{m_{R}})\}_{l=1}^{N_{R}}
, where each dataset

{\mathbf{X}}^{(l)}_{m_{R}}
of size
m_{R}
is simulated from
p_{\theta^{(l)}}^{(m_{R})}
.
The expectations in (
23
) are also approximated by an empirical averages. A complete algorithmic summary of the structure score matching procedure is provided below.

Algorithm 1

Structured Score Matching

Input:
Sampling distribution
p(\theta)
, observed dataset
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\ast}
, networks
s_{\phi}(\theta,X)
and
h_{\psi}(\theta)
.

1. Reference Table:
Generate
{\mathcal{D}}^{S}=\{(\theta^{(k)},X^{(k)})\}_{k=1}^{N}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}p(\theta)p_{\theta}(\cdot)
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{R}=\{\theta^{(l)},{\mathbf{X}}^{(l)}_{m_{R}}\}_{l=1}^{N_{R}}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}q(\theta)p^{(m_{R})}_{\theta}(\cdot)
.

2. Network Training:
Train
s_{\phi}(\theta,X)
on
{\mathcal{D}}^{S}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{R}
using loss in (
22
) and obtain
\widehat{\phi}
.

3. Mean Regression:
Estimate the mean of
s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,X)
on
{\mathcal{D}}^{R}
using (
23
) and obtain
\widehat{\psi}
.

Return
Debiased structured score function
\widetilde{s}(\theta,X)=s_{\widehat{\phi}}(\theta,X)-h_{\widehat{\psi}}(\theta)

44
)
with 5 autoregressive transforms, each of which has 2 hidden
layers of 50 units each. We generate
n_{\text{NLE}}
single data points, where
n_{\text{NLE}}
matches the simulation cost of our method, to train the likelihood density network. We have batch size
b_{\text{NLE}}
and learning rate
5\times 10^{-4}
to train the network until convergence. With the trained likelihood density network, we use autograd in Pytorch to obtain the gradient and Hessian of the estimated log-likelihood. We use the obtained gradient to do gradient ascent to get the point estimate, and then use the sandwich formula to get the confidence intervals.

For the SL method, summary statistics are constructed for each example. Using the summary statistic, we follow
(
59
)
to implement the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm to search the maximizer of the synthetic likelihood. After the markov chain converges, we follow
(
59
)
to fit a quadratic regression to get the point estimate. The confidence intervals are simply obtained by taking sample quantiles of the converged markov chain.

The code for our experiments is available at
https://github.com/Haoyu-Jiang/Structured_Score_Matching/tree/main/MLE
.

D.4.2
Real data

Implementation details

We note that the log-return data has a very small magnitude, indicating a small scale parameter and potential numerical instability during the network training. To address this, we standardized the data by dividing by its standard deviation. This transformation is valid because the g-and-k family is closed under linear transformations, allowing the parameters on the original scale to be recovered by rescaling afterward. For the network training, we adopt a uniform proposal distribution over
[-1,1]\times[-2,1]\times[-5,5]\times[0,0.5]
for
(A,\log B,g,k)
. We have reference table sizes
(N,N_{R},m_{R})=(120\;000,6\;000,2\;000)
, and the batch size for score matching is
500
.
