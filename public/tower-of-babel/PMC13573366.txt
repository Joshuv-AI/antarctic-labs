Traditional phytopharmaceutical practices and integrative healthcare perspectives among herbal practitioners: a cross-sectional analysis in Tigray, Ethiopia
Authors: Yemane Tadesse Desta, Tesfay Godifey Tsegay, Haftom Kahsay Abraha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditional medicine remains a crucial component of primary healthcare in Ethiopia. This study assessed phytopharmaceutical practices and integrative health perspectives among traditional herbal practitioners in Tigray, Ethiopia. A community-based mixed-method cross-sectional study was carried out from March to May 2024 among 58 purposively selected traditional herbal practitioners across five zones in Tigray. A semi-structured interviews, observational assessments and medicinal plant specimen documentation were employed. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and binary logistic regression, while qualitative data were analyzed using thematic content. The findings revealed that the mean age of practitioners was 56.5 years, with males constituting 62.07% participants. Most of the practitioners’ experience exceeded 20 years and practiced herbal remedy on a part-time basis. A total of 22 medicinal plant species were documented for the treatment of infectious, physical and spiritual illnesses. Leaves (47.80%), roots (23.80%) and stem barks (16.41%) were the most extensively used plant parts. Greater informant consensus was observed for gastrointestinal disorders (ICF = 0.95) and respiratory diseases (ICF = 0.86). Fidelity level analysis indicated strong agreement on the utilization of Osimum lamifolium for respiratory disorders (93.1%), Ruta chalepensis for gastrointestinal ailments (92%) and Aloe vera for skin infections (90%). Formal education (OR = 6.00, p = 0,046), both family influence and experiential source of knowledge (OR = 11.67, p = 0.032), and prior training (OR = 3.82, p = 0.015) were significantly associated with openness towards integration with modern healthcare systems, whereas older age was reversibly associated with integration willingness (OR = 0.17, p = 0.05). Environmental degradation (98.28%), knowledge erosion (96.55%), lack of formal recognition (89.66%), and inadequate documentation (87.93%) were cited as major challenges to the sustainable practice of traditional medicine. In conclusion, the findings underscore the significance of traditional herbal medicine in community healthcare of Tigray. However, inadequate documentation, environmental factors and poor regulatory framework threatens sustainability of the knowledge. Improving documentation systems, regulatory frameworks and culturally sensitive integration strategies may enhance the sustainability and integration of traditional herbal medicine with the modern healthcare system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-026-05444-w.

Introduction

Traditional phytopharmaceutical practices have been traced back to ancient time and contributed a critical role in the healthcare system of Ethiopia, specifically in regions including Tigray [1]. An estimated 80% of Ethiopian society depends on traditional herbal medicine for primary healthcare [2–4]. This is a testament to the deep-rooted historical and cultural significance of traditional medicinal practice in the country [5]. This is due to its affordability and accessibility, particularly to the poor communities; to easily control several human and animal ailments [3, 6]. The practice is orchestrated by a affluent tapestry of indigenous knowledge where the huge part of population and indigenous healers utilize a wide range of medicinal plants, animal-derived parts, and mineral substances to detect and treat numerous ailments [7]. The traditionalknowledge is passed orally across generations, usually acquired by some spiritual procedures where religious texts and knowledgeable elders serving as a guardians of ethnomedical knowledge [5].

In Tigray, traditional healers obtain their drugs from natural substances mainly plant components for therapeutic function [6]. Traditional herbal drugs are prepared in various dosage forms including liquids, ointments, powders and pills [3]. Ethnomedicinal studies carried in the region have highlighted the utilization of a wide range of medicinal plant species from diversified plant families with leaves occupied the dominant plant part used most and oral administration is the main route of intake [8]. Despite the prevalent use and significance of these practices, there is a remarkable shortage of standardized documentation and modernization, underlying the need for vigorous research and management initiatives [1, 8].

Integration of traditional pharmaceutical practices and contemporary healthcare system raises significant institutional and regulatory obstacles in Ethiopia [9]. The existing national regulatory structures are believed to be unsatisfactory with loose enforcement, low governmental attention and support and insufficient resources and organizational structures for traditional herbal medicine [5]. Even though some regulatory mechanisms are in place at regional levels of the country, irregularities and gaps exist living a room for unregistered and unrecognized traditional practitioners to work out of control restraining the capacity of guaranteeing the efficacy, safety and quality of the indigenous knowledge [5, 9]. Although the Ethiopian Drug Administration and Control Authority (DACA) have been doing efforts to devise more inclusive working guidelines and regulatory mechanisms on the use of traditional medicine, but so far no safety standard regulations have been developed and no traditional herbal products have been given formal market approval [5].

Despite these obstacles, the widespread utilization and reliance on herbal medicine in Tigray highlights the long-lasting adaptability and customization of traditional healthcare approaches. Previous findings have indicated that knowledge towards traditional herbal practice varies based on several factors. Those are age of traditional healers, education status, and level of seniority or year of experience [1, 10]. Integrative healthcare systems are nowadays gaining an attention due to their effectiveness in controlling a wide range of ailments [11]. To harness the optimum potential of Ethiopia’s unattached phytomarmaceutical heritage promoting collaboration betweentraditional healers and the concurrent modern healthcare system is paramount significant. Therefore, this study was aimed to investigate the phytopharmaceutical practices and integrative health perspectives among herbal practitioners in Tigray, Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design

A community-based mixed-method cross-sectional study, comprising both quantitative and qualitative componentswas conducted from March 1 to May 30, 2024, in five administrative zones of the Tigray region. A multi-site design was preferred to capture cultural and geographical variations in traditional phytopharmaceutical practices across the five zones.

Study area

The study was conducted in Tigray, a region in northern Ethiopia comprising seven zones, including the capital city Mekelle. Fifthly eight traditional herbal practitioners were identified from five zones, namely Southwest, Central, Eastern, Southeastern and Sothern Tigray. These five zones are known for their ethnomedicinal diversity and intensive prevalence of traditional herbal practices. Western zone was excluded due to security issues associated with the 2020 Tigray-Ethiopia war. Mekelle was also excluded due its limited ethnomedicinal diversity and traditional healing practices compared to the other zones (Fig. 1).

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population consists of traditional herbal practitioners living in the selected zones of Tigray. In this study, the term traditional herbal practitioners, refers to persons recognized by the surrounding communities as healthcare providers employing plant-based remedies acquired from indigenous knowledge. This term is applied consistently throughout the paper. They were included based on the following criteria: (i), had been practicing actively in herbal medicine. (ii), currently using herbal-based remedies for healing purpose (iii), were accepted by the local community for their therapeutic practices. Individuals unwilling to participate or refused to provide informed consent were excluded from the study.

Sampling strategy and sample size determination

A purposive sampling method complemented by snowball sampling technique was used to select experienced and knowledgeable traditional practitioners across the five zones. First, key informants identified purposively with the help of health extension workers and community leaders based on their community acceptance, experience and reputation. Consequently, using snowball sampling, identified practitioners referred other recognized practitioners based on their professional networks. This strategy is more important and widely used in ethnobotanical studies conducted in Ethiopia and similar settings to reach out traditional knowledgeable people who are mostly not accessed through conventional sampling methods [1, 4, 12]. A total of 58 traditional herbal practitioners were included in this study. The sample size was determined based on saturation of data, a standard approach in ethnobotanical and qualitative study, in line with other ethnobotanical researches [4, 12].

Data collection methods

Data were collected using semi-structured interviews, field observations, and plant specimen collection. The data collection instrument was developed following a comprehensive review of similar published etnobatanical studies to ensure contextual relevance and content validity. Prior to the survey, the questionnaire was pre-tested on seven traditional herbal practitioners to ensure content clarity and relevance. Minor modifications were done based on the pre-test results, and these participants were not included in the final dataset to avoid potential bias.

The final questionnaire (see Supplementary File 1) was prepared addressing socio-demographic characteristics, sources and transmission of knowledge, common ailments handled by the practitioners, medicinal plants used, prescription methods and traditional herbal practitioners’ perspectives on integration with the modern healthcare system.

Prior to the main interview, the questionnaire was translated from English into a local language (Tigrigna), subsequently back-translated into English and reviewed for accuracy. Data collection was conducted in the local language by trained native-speaking data collectors. Informed consent was obtained from individuals who participated in the study prior to the interviews.

Observational data were collected through field visits to document natural habitats of medicinal herbs, harvesting practices, and preparation and administration techniques. These observations were used as a complement of interview data and were helped to validate reported practices. Herbal specimens were collected by obtaining an appropriate permission to the land owners of the herbs. The following herbal samples were collected: Achyranthes aspera roots, Acokanthera schimperi roots & barks, Aloe vera leaves, Artemisia abyssinic leaves, Artemisia afra leaves, Boscia salicifolia bark & leaves, Cordia Africana bark & leaves, Croton macrostachyus Del. leaves, Cucumis ficifolius A. leaves, Echinops kebericho roots, Foeniculum vulgare seeds, Kalancho equartiniana leaves, Lepidium sativum seeds & leaves, Myrica salicifolia bark & leaves, Ocimum lamifolium leaves, Rumex abyssinicus Jacq leaves, stem & flowers, Ruta chalepensis L. leaves, stem & flowers, Solanum marginatum leaves & fruits, Verbena officinalis roots & stems, Withania somnifera roots, Zehneria scabra leaves, Zingiber offcinale rhizome.

Botanical identification and documentation of the sample specimens were made and samples were stored at Aksum University, Tigray, Ethiopia, in May, 2024. The plant specimens were identified at the herbarium section of Department of Biology, Aksum University by Dr. Haftu Woldu (Botanist).

The samples were authenticated with voucher specimens, which were deposited in the plant herbarium section of the institute with their voucher numbers (Table 1).

Data analysis

Quantitative data were exported to IBM SPSS® software (version 22.0, IBM, USA) after they were entered into Excel 2019, cleaned for completeness and consistency. Descriptive analysis including frequencies, percentages were used to summarize socio-demographic characteristics of the traditional herbal practitioners, medicinal plant utilization patterns, plant parts used by practitioners, preparation methods, and perspectives towards integrative healthcare systems. Binary logistic regression was conducted to identify factors correlated with openness to integration with main-stream healthcare. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95 confidence interval (CI) were calculated. Moreover, Chi-square test of independence were used to compare the response of THP groups who were willing towards integration and who were not, across the different variables. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethnobotanical indices

Informant consensus factor

Informant consensus factor (ICF) is used to test homogeneity of informant’s knowledge in choosing certain medicinal plants against a given ailment group. It ranges from 0 to 1, where a high value acts as a good indicator for a high rate of informant consensus. ICF is used to select ailment categories where there is consensus on the use of plants among the informants and to identify species with particular importance in a culture. Human ailments will be classified into broad ailment categories (several ailments based on the organ systems in one category) following Ermias Lulekal and others [13].

ICF=Nur-Nt /nur-1

Where, ICF: Informants Consensus Factor.

Nur: Number of citations in each category.

Nt: Number of species used.

Fidelity level index

According to Alexiades [14], the fidelity level index (FL) is utilized to prove that certain plant species are used for the same main reason. As a result, the fidelity level for medicinal plants used to prevent the communicable and non-communicable diseases will be determined.

For qualitative data, practitioners’ interviews and field observation notes were reviewed, translated into English and analyzed using thematic content analysis approach. Open coding was carried out manually to identify frequent concepts and outlines. Related codes were subsequently grouped into broad thematic groups related to traditional knowledge transmission, medicinal plant utilization practice, practitioners’ perspectives toward integration, perceived barriers to integration and sustainability challenges of traditional herbal medicine. Themes were interpreted and triangulated with quantitative data findings to ensure validity and improve contextual understanding of the findings. Citation frequencies were derived from the number of practitioners who independently answered a specific medicinal plant species, therapeutic use, challenges, or perception category during interviews and presented in figure.

Results

Sociodemographic and professional characteristics of traditional herbal practitioners

In this study 58 traditional herbal practitioners included in this study, with a mean age of 56.5 years. Male participants accounted the majority of respondents (36, 62.07%), while females constituted 22 (37.93%). About half of the practitioners were older than 59 years, implying that traditional herbal practices in the study area are predominantly held by aged individuals. Regarding religion of practitioners, majority of them were adherents of Ethiopian Orthodox Church (86.7%), while Muslims constituted 13.79%.

With respect to level of education, 22 (37.93%) % of the healers had no any education, 19 (32.76%) had religious-based education, 11 (18.97%) attended formal education, and 6 (10.34%) attended both religious and formal education. Most practitioners had considerable professional experience, with 27 reporting exceeding 20 years of service.

Traditional herbal healing was predominantly performed as a part-time job by 44 (75.86%) participants. More than half of the practitioners (62.07%) expressed openness towards integration with the conventional healthcare system. With respect to source of traditional medicinal knowledge religious institutions (41.38%), and family inheritance (31.03%) were the major reported sources. Prior training related to traditional medicinal practice was reported by 37 (63.79%) respondents (Table 2). In contrast, willingness to transfer indigenous knowledge remained limited, as only 25.86 respondents were willing to share knowledge, while 60.34% preferred conveying knowledge only to relatives.

Traditional phytopharmaceutical practices

This study documented 22 medicinal plant species commonly used by traditional herbal practitioners for the treatment of infectious, physical and spiritual ailments. Medicinal plant preparations were predominantly administered in the form of decoction, infusions, powders, or extracts mixed either with tea, honey, milk or water. for oral administration or else topically applied for skin and external wound-related problems.

Several medicinal plant species reported to demonstrate multipurpose treatment applications. For example, Ocimum lamifolium was commonly utilized for cold, cough, headache, and spiritual healing functions, while Withania somnifera was primarily applied for stress, weakness, impotence, and joint-associated ailments. Likewise, Echinops kebericho and Artemisia afra, were reported to use for both therapeutic and spiritual purposes, including headache, malaria and against evil eye cleansing protection (Table 3).

Fidelity Level (FL) values of commonly reported medicinal plants

Fidelity Level (FL) analysis indicated varying degrees of informant consensus about the therapeutic use of medicinal plants. High FL were documented for Ocimum lamiifolium for treating respiratory ailments (93.1%), Ruta chalepensis for gastrointestinal disorders (92%), and Aloe vera for skin infections (90%), showing strong agreement among informants on their medicinal potency. Likewise, Zingiber officinale, and Lepidium sativum demonstrated relatively high FL values for gastrointestinal and respiratory disorders, respectively, showing their intensive traditional application. Conversely, the lower FL values recorded for some ailments, such as Verbena officinalis in the treatment of skin infection (39.6%). This may indicate low informant consensus or the diversified use of the species. Over all, medicinal plants with higher FL value may reveal culturally prioritized remedies and potential medicinal plant species for further pharmacological study (Table 6).

Integrative healthcare perspectives of traditional herbal practitioners

Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors associated with openness of traditional herbal practitioners towards intercession with the modern healthcare system. Traditional herbal practitioners aged above 59 years were less likely to be ready to support integration compared to practitioners younger than 40 years (OR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.03–0.99, p = 0.05). Participants with formal education had 6 times increased odds of expressing openness towards integration compared with those without education (OR = 6.00, 95% CI: 1.03–34.86, p = 0.046).

Similarly, respondents who reported acquiring the traditional medicinal knowledge from both family influence and presiding sickness experiences indicated significantly higher odds of openness towards integration (OR = 11.67, 95% CI: 1.23–110.1, p = 0.032). Respondents who had received prior training were also more likely to support integration with the modern healthcare system (OR = 3.82, CI: 1.30-11.21, p = 0.015).

In contrast, variables including gender, years of service, and religious educational background did not indicate statistically significant association with openness to wards integration of traditional herbal medicine practice with modern healthcare system. (Table 7).

Perceived benefits of integrative healthcare

Among practitioners who reported willingness to integrate with modern healthcare system, the most frequently perceived was formal herbal medicinal practice recognition (95.45%). Other frequently cited benefits included improvement of patient care (90.91) and enhancement of therapeutic efficacy (90.91%).

In contrast, economic benefit was less emphasized benefit by respondents compared to the others. Overall, these findings highlight that collaboration of traditional herbal practices with modern healthcare system is highly valued with regard to gaining recognition of the therapeutic practice, improvement of patient care and synergistic healthcare outcomes (Fig. 4).

Perceived challenges to integration

Traditional herbal practitioners were asked for their insights towards the challenges of integrative health that they perceived. They identified several concerns regarding integration with modern healthcare system. The most predominant barriers among traditional herbal practitioners resistant to integration included fear of losing therapeutic efficacy, cultural resistance, religious concerns and lack of clear government regulatory frame works.

Although scientific validation and intellectual property concerns were raised by most of the practitioners of both groups (those who supported integration and those who did not); these variables did not significantly differ between the two groups (Fig. 5).

Major challenges in traditional herbal medicine

The findings in Table 7 indicate the major challenges that traditional herbal practitioners in the study are facing. These challenges are ranked with regard to their frequency and perceived severity (high, medium, low), implicating insight into the most critical issues affecting the traditional health practice.

Environmental degradation was identified as the most critical challenge affecting traditional herbal medicine practice, reported by 57 (98.28%) participants. Remarkably, most participants indicated that degradation of natural vegetation and forests threatens the availability and accessibility of medicinal plants. Consequently, it affects the sustainability of traditional herbal medicine practice.

Indigenous knowledge erosion (96.55%), lack of formal recognition (89.66%), inadequate documentation (87.93%), and the raising presence of false or unqualified practitioner (84.48%) were also reported by the respondents as major concerns. Similarly, inadequate storage facilities for herbal preparations (74.14%), inadequate training and integration opportunities (53.45%), government regulatory gaps (43.10%), and limited market access (29.31%) were also perceptible concerns reported by respondents (Table 8). These findings indicated that both environmental and institutional factors threaten the continuity, authority and sustainability of traditional herbal medicine practices in our study area.

Discussion

The study highlights important insights into traditional phytopharmaceutical practices and perspectives of traditional herbal practitioners towards healthcare integration in Tigray, Ethiopia. The findings suggest that traditional herbal medicine continues to play a substantial role in community overall healthcare despite growing social ecological and regulatory challenges.

The predominance of elderly male practitioners observed in this research shows that indigenous medicinal knowledge is largely possessed by elderly male individuals within the community. The limited involvement of younger generations may suggest low interest in the traditional practice and the knowledge is at risk of gradual lose over time due to inadequate inter-generational transmission and limited long-lasting preservation. Comparable patterns have been reported in other studies carried out in Ethiopia, where traditional knowledge system is mostly retained in older individuals and transferred through religious institutions or family [15].

This finding indicates the solid cultural and religious foundations of indigenous medicinal knowledge in Tigray. The dominant transmission mechanism is oral for preserving ethnomedicinal knowledge despite undocumented oral traditions remain the risk of gradual knowledge erosion over years.

The study also documented extensive use medicinal plants for managing diverse physical and spiritual ailments. Leaves were the most predominantly utilized plant parts, which is in line with the report of other ethnobotanical investigations in Ethiopia and other African countries [12, 16]. This may be associated with easy accessibility of leaves, limited harvesting effect on the survival of the plant, and increased availability of bioactive secondary metabolites they contain [17, 18].

Medicinal plants, including Withania somnifera, Osimum lamifolium and Lepidium sativum, identified in this study were frequently cited for multiple treatment applications. The widespread utilization of these plant species reflects robust community trust and sustained empirical knowledge regarding their therapeutic value. The utilization of certain medicinal plant species for both physical and spiritual ailment healing purpose further reveals the holistic nature of indigenous medicinal knowledge system in the region.

The findings further illustrated substantial interest among traditional herbal practitioners towards integration with modern healthcare system. Educational status and previous training of practitioners considerably improved openness towards integration of healthcare services, demonstrating that exposure to biomedical concepts through formal education and associated training programs may enhance confidence and interest for collaboration. This accords with previous ethnomedicinal researches investigating integrative healthcare system in similar setting of African countries [19, 20].

Conversely, older traditional herbal practitioners showed relatively low acceptance of integration of traditional healthcare practices with biomedical systems, possibly due to concerns regarding cultural preservation, loss of practitioner’s autonomy, and skepticism towards modern medical science. Among the most predominantly cited barriers to integration were the perceived loss of therapeutic potency and erosion of cultural identity uniqueness. These concerns showcase that efforts towards integration should considerate culturally sensitive and holistic approaches that value and preserve indigenous knowledge. Similarly, other researchers have reported that traditional herbal practitioners are concerned that collaboration may undermine the perceived treatment efficacy or the spiritual value of traditional herbal practices [21, 22].

The study further identified critical concerns impacting the sustainability of traditional herbal medicinal practices. Environmental degradation appeared as the most significant challenge due to its direct impact on the availability of medicinal plants. Ecological imbalance, habitat loss and deforestation may severely compromise the continuing accessibility of medicinal plants. Similar findings have been recorded corroborating our results from Ethiopia and other places that indicate ecological imbalance and thinly regulated interventions impaired the continuance of traditional herbal practice [23].

Lack of documentation of the indigenous knowledge was another critical concern. Since traditional medicinal knowledge is mainly transmitted verbally, failure of systematically documenting the skill, protocol and prescription may result in irreversible loss of the knowledge, particularly as the older knowledgeable generation die. Moreover, the rise of unqualified healers can weaken public trust in traditional healing practice and compromise the credibility of the indigenous knowledge, calling for a desperate need for institutional regulatory frame works and certification processes.

In conclusion, the findings underscore the significance of traditional herbal medicine in community healthcare of Tigray. Practitioners use diverse plants species for their practice. Moreover, the results revealed intensive but with weakly founded structure of indigenous medicinal knowledge largely transferred in an untailored way. Therefore, the need for comprehensive interventions targeted at preserving efforts are demanded to ensure sustainability of indigenous knowledge while promoting integration with modern healthcare systems. These interventions should focus on documentation, initiatives of developing regulatory frameworks and safeguarding of intellectual property rights of traditional herbal practitioners.
