Two decades of resurrection studies: What have we learned about contemporary evolution of plant species?
Authors: Lillie K. Pennington, Seema N. Sheth, Steven J. Franks, Jill T. Anderson, Elena Hamann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPremiseGlobal climate change has altered the eco‐evolutionary trajectories of plant species, leading to observed shifts in phenotypes, such as earlier flowering. However, disentangling the contributions of plasticity and adaptation to trait changes remains challenging. The resurrection approach is a powerful method to study genetic and plastic responses by contrasting ancestral and descendant lineages from the same populations under common conditions.MethodsWe compiled a database of resurrection studies to examine plant evolutionary responses to global change using a meta‐analysis (46 studies) and quantitative and qualitative review (61 studies). Studies varied widely in the number of focal populations and intervening years separating generations, yet most involved annual plant species in North America and Europe.ResultsWe found evidence for rapid, contemporary evolution in over half of the cases. Moreover, evolution was often adaptive. As hypothesized, annuals showed greater magnitudes of evolutionary change than perennials. Across studies, annual descendants evolved earlier phenology and more resource‐acquisitive leaf traits relative to ancestors. Under drought stress, annual and perennial species both evolved earlier phenology. Furthermore, in response to drought, annuals evolved increased plasticity in phenological and physiological traits but reduced plasticity in leaf traits, whereas perennials showed no evidence for evolution in plasticity.ConclusionsOur study reveals that rapid evolution is common but not ubiquitous and highlights the key role of drought escape in plant responses to a warming world. Our review also suggests promising avenues for future resurrection research.

Research within the last 40 years has substantially deepened our understanding of contemporary evolution in natural populations (Endler, 1986; Grant and Grant, 2002; Thompson, 2013; Kingsolver and Buckley, 2017). This work has revealed that rapid evolutionary changes can occur within just a few generations (Grant and Grant, 2002; Franks et al., 2007; Schoener, 2011) and that evolutionary processes have the potential to influence population persistence on relatively short timescales (Carroll et al., 2007; DeMarche et al., 2019; Anderson et al., 2025). Despite these enormous advances, demonstrating that adaptive evolution has occurred in any given natural population remains challenging. This task has become more pressing in an era of rapid environmental change, as adaptive responses to novel selection could enable species to persist in their current ranges (Ellner et al., 2011; Chevin and Bridle, 2025). One methodological advancement to evaluate adaptive evolution in natural populations is the direct comparison of traits expressed by ancestor and descendant lineages from the same location under common conditions (i.e., a resurrection study; Figure 1, Table 1 [Franks et al., 2007, 2018]). This powerful approach quantifies the extent and directionality of contemporary evolution (Franks et al., 2007, 2018). Here, we performed a quantitative and qualitative literature review and a meta‐analysis of resurrection studies to characterize insights into evolution in wild populations gained by this approach.

The meta‐analysis framework can illuminate major patterns that have emerged across resurrection studies that examine species that differ in life histories and mating systems, populations that differ in genetic diversity and connectivity, traits that differ in heritability and plasticity, and regions that differ in the drivers and rate of environmental change. For example, species with short generation times and high standing genetic variation may adapt more rapidly to selection imposed by contemporary climate change (Hamann et al., 2018), whereas long generation times, low genetic diversity and genetic trade‐offs across traits could constrain adaptation in others (Etterson and Shaw, 2001; Jarrold et al., 2019; Moran, 2020). As such, it follows that due to their short generation times, annual species may have faster evolutionary rates, in absolute time, than perennial species. Ultimately, resurrection studies hold great promise for evaluating how variation in life‐history traits, ecological strategies, population size, mating systems, standing genetic variation, and intraspecific genetic differentiation (Thompson et al., 2023) contribute to differences in the adaptive potential of populations confronting novel environmental conditions (Etterson and Shaw, 2001).

Many published resurrection studies have examined evolution under increasingly frequent or severe drought stress (Appendix S1: Table S1) because climate change is intensifying aridity in many ecosystems (IPCC et al., 2023; Kornhuber et al., 2024). The direction and type of evolutionary responses to drought could be driven by resource acquisition strategies, reflecting several classical theoretical frameworks (Figure 2). Integrated suites of traits often evolve in response to drought stress, competition, resource limitation, and periodic biomass destruction via environmental disturbance (Grime, 1974, 1977; Reich, 2014; Pierce et al., 2017). These trait syndromes have been captured elegantly by the leaf economics spectrum (LES) (Wright et al., 2004). Specifically, plants that have evolved drought resistance tend to display slow growth, long life‐spans, and a resource‐conservative strategy, characterized by high leaf dry matter content, low leaf area, low specific leaf area and reduced leaf nitrogen content (Figure 2). Other species may express resource‐acquisitive traits at the fast end of the leaf economics spectrum (Figure 2). Frequent and severe droughts can favor the evolution of drought‐escape strategies, characterized by low leaf dry matter content, high leaf area, short lifespans, high specific leaf area and nitrogen content, that promote rapid growth and development (Wright et al., 2004). This strategy has been documented in several annual species via earlier flowering (Franks et al., 2007; Vigouroux et al., 2011; Nevo et al., 2012; Thomann et al., 2015; Hamann et al., 2018; Rauschkolb et al., 2022), earlier seed emergence (Dickman et al., 2019), and accelerated growth (Franks, 2011; Rauschkolb et al., 2022). In contrast, perennials, which often display a stress‐resistant and resource‐conservative strategy, could evolve toward suites of traits enabling drought tolerance or avoidance (Kooyers, 2015), including delayed flowering due to slow growth, low carbon assimilation rate, low stomatal conductance, and high water‐use efficiency (Anstett et al., 2021) (Figure 2). We emphasize that the framework that we have developed here could extend to other elements of global change, with annuals evolving stress escape and perennials evolving stress tolerance or avoidance strategies.

Resurrection studies can be used to examine and quantify the rate of evolution, to distinguish evolution from plasticity, and to examine the evolution of plasticity, which are all useful for understanding and predicting climate change responses (Franks et al., 2014). When ancestors and descendants are raised in the same environment in a resurrection study (Figure 1), phenotypic and genomic differences between ancestors and descendants would be due to evolution rather than plasticity (Franks et al., 2007). Because the degree of plasticity can be considered a trait (Schlichting and Pigliucci, 1995), plasticity can be measured in both ancestors and descendants when both are grown under two or more different conditions (Figure 1, Table 1). The difference in degree of plasticity between ancestors and descendants indicates evolutionary change in plasticity (Franks, 2011; Franks et al., 2014; Hamann et al., 2018). Finally, resurrection studies can quantify the rate of evolution when the difference in trait values between ancestors and descendants and time in generations are both known (Table 1; Franks et al., 2007, 2014).

Resurrection studies conducted in multiple ecologically relevant environments are uniquely situated to investigate genetic shifts in plasticity in response to factors associated with global change, and when paired with selection analyses, these studies can evaluate the adaptive nature of plasticity (Sultan et al., 2013; Hamann et al., 2018; Lambrecht et al., 2020; Johnson et al., 2022). Spatial and temporal variation in environmental conditions can favor the evolution of trait plasticity (Moran, 1992; Alpert and Simms, 2002; Baythavong, 2011; Anderson et al., 2012; Murren et al., 2014). Increasing environmental variability under climate change (IPCC et al., 2023; Kornhuber et al., 2024) could impose selection for steeper norms of reaction for various traits (Jentsch et al., 2007; Matesanz et al., 2010; Lázaro‐Nogal et al., 2015; Kingsolver and Buckley, 2017; Stotz et al., 2025), and adaptive plasticity could mitigate the immediate negative fitness effects of climate change (Matesanz et al., 2010; Nicotra et al., 2010; Shaw and Etterson, 2012; Franks et al., 2014). However, not all populations maintain genetic variation for plasticity, not all traits are plastic, genetic correlations could constrain the evolution of increased plasticity (Murren et al., 2014; Oostra et al., 2018), and climate change is not uniform across the globe (IPCC et al., 2023). Thus, it remains unclear whether changing climates will favor the evolution of plasticity or whether plasticity will be sufficient to maintain fitness especially under novel climatic extremes (Duputié et al., 2015; Chevin and Hoffmann, 2017; Kingsolver and Buckley, 2017; Arnold et al., 2019; Stotz et al., 2025).

In a 2018 review, Franks et al. identified 12 published studies that used the resurrection approach, all of which reported rapid evolution in at least one trait and, in most cases, increased fitness in descendant lineages—reflecting evidence of contemporary adaptation. The review also highlighted methodological challenges that could bias evolutionary inference, including maternal effects (Roach and Wulff, 1987), storage effects on seed viability or performance, and the “invisible fraction” arising from nonrandom mortality during storage (Weis, 2018; Franks et al., 2019). Franks et al. (2018) emphasized the importance of using refresher generations to mitigate the effects of maternal and storage effects (Table 1). Bias from the “invisible fraction” can be mitigated at the initial time of collection with a random, unbiased sample of populations and after collection with storage conditions that optimize seed viability and a high germination rate of all seeds (Franks et al., 2019). However, because few studies were available at the time, it was not yet possible to evaluate broader evolutionary patterns—such as whether species with different life histories evolve distinct trait syndromes in response to shared environmental drivers—or to formally assess how common best practices (e.g., refresher generations, tests of plasticity evolution) were being implemented. Since then, the number and diversity of resurrection studies have expanded substantially, providing the opportunity for a comprehensive, quantitative assessment of evolutionary patterns and methodological rigor across taxa, traits, and environments.

Here, we present the first formal meta‐analysis of resurrection studies to evaluate four key hypotheses about plant evolutionary responses to novel environments. With this meta‐analysis, we first aimed to test whether annual plants have a greater magnitude of evolutionary response to global change relative to perennial plants (H1). Second, we evaluated whether annuals and perennials have different magnitudes or directions of evolutionary responses across broad categories of traits, for example those captured by the leaf economics spectrum (H2). Third, we focused on one dimension of global change that many resurrection studies have considered: drought. In the context of increasing drought stress, we hypothesized that annual plants will evolve traits associated with drought escape, whereas perennial plants will develop traits linked to drought avoidance or tolerance (H3). Finally, we examined the hypothesis that, in response to environmental variability associated with climate change, plant populations could evolve increased plasticity (H4). We had stringent selection criteria for our formal meta‐analysis, but we also conducted a quantitative literature review to provide a broader view of resurrection studies and to highlight general trends in the published literature, including studies not retained in our meta‐analysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Meta‐analysis search criteria

To examine evolutionary responses to contemporary climate change, we conducted a phylogenetically corrected meta‐analysis of resurrection experiments in accordance with the PRISMA‐EcoEvo guidelines for systematic reviews in ecology and evolution (O'Dea et al., 2021). In October 2023, January 2024, April 2025, and October 2025, we searched for resurrection studies on Web of Science using the terms [“resurrection approach” AND “plant”], [“resurrection study” AND “plant”], [“resurrection ecology” AND “plant], [“contemporary evolution” and “plant”], [“back in time” AND “plant”], [“rapid adaptation” AND “plant”], and [“seed banks” AND “evolution” AND “plant”] with the aim of extracting phenotypic data and fitness components from all plant resurrection experiments published to date.

We supplemented this search with a Google Scholar query using the terms [“resurrection study” AND “plant”], [“resurrection approach” AND “plant”], and [“resurrection ecology” AND “plant”] and used the “search within citing articles” feature to identify publications citing foundational resurrection studies (Franks et al., 2007, 2008) and the initial literature review (Franks et al., 2018). We focused on studies published in English‐language, peer‐reviewed journals. One author performed the literature search. We screened the titles and abstracts of 1846 studies identified through these searches to identify suitable publications based on these inclusion criteria: (1) The study contrasted ancestral and descendant lineages in an experimental framework, either in the field or controlled conditions (e.g., greenhouse or growth chamber) and measured traits and/or fitness for at least one ancestral and descendant generation from at least one natural population; (2) efforts were made to sample seeds or propagules from the same natural populations in both ancestral and descendant generations; (3) data were available for both generations, either within the publication (in figures or tables), through online repositories, communications with authors, or in supplemental material such that we could download sample size, means, and either standard deviations or standard errors or extract these metrics from figures with WebPlotDigitizer version 4.8 (Rohatgi, 2024); and (4) the study directly measured phenotypes and/or fitness (Appendix S2: Figure S1). Of the 61 studies that we identified for the literature review, five were excluded from the meta‐analysis because they reported exclusively genomic data, four were excluded for studying ancestral and descendant populations that were not geographically the same, three were excluded because the data were unavailable and the authors did not reply to our inquiries, two were excluded for having unclear sample sizes, and one for reporting change in statistical metrics rather than raw phenotypes.

Literature review

Qualitative data extraction

To gather information from all published resurrection experiments that reported phenotypic traits, fitness components, and genomic sequence data (Appendix S1: Table S1), we also conducted a comprehensive literature review. For each study, we recorded the research questions, aims, and hypotheses, along with key elements of the work such as the geographic extent of seed/propagule sampling for ancestral and descendant genotypes; the experimental design and treatments applied; and the traits and fitness components measured (Appendix S1: Table S1). We summarized key results, noting which traits were reported to have shown evidence of evolution, whether evolutionary changes were discussed or tested as adaptive (by linking traits to fitness under ancestral vs. descendant conditions), what the adaptive response was, if evolution in plasticity was observed or discussed, whether heritability was assessed, the reported rate of evolution (if calculated), and the putative driver of evolutionary change (Appendix S1: Table S1).

Quantitative data extraction

From each publication that met our inclusion criteria, we extracted trait and fitness data for the ancestor and descendent generations for our meta‐analysis. We obtained complete data sets for almost all studies from data repositories and communication with authors. From these raw data, we calculated the mean, standard deviation, and sample size using the summarySE function in the R package Rmisc (Hope, 2022) for each trait in each sampled population. We used WebPlotDigitizer version 4.8 (Rohatgi, 2024) to extract data from figures in Sultan et al. (2013), and we gathered data from a supplementary table for three others (Nevo et al., 2012; Blum et al., 2021; Everingham et al., 2024). For the four studies that contrasted ancestral seeds with descendant seeds collected across multiple years (Hamann et al., 2018; Anstett et al., 2021; Benning et al., 2023; White et al., 2023), we included only the earliest and latest years in which more than one individual was measured. For studies that measured traits in multiple environments, we extracted data for ancestors and descendants in each of the environments included in the study.

To examine trends across studies that quantified different sets of phenotypes, we divided traits into eight categories: resource allocation, fitness components, floral traits, leaf economic spectrum (LES) traits, leaf traits other than LES traits, phenology, physiology, and plant size (Appendix S2: Table S2; see also Siefert et al., 2015; Griffin‐Nolan et al., 2018; Lemmen et al., 2019; Pennington et al., 2021; Boyd et al., 2022). Very few studies reported binary fitness components (e.g., survival, or flowering success; Appendix S1: Table S1); therefore, we excluded these binary data from our meta‐analysis because odds‐ratios effect sizes for binary data cannot easily be combined with Hedge's g, which is an effect size for continuous data. We recorded details of the experimental design, such as the number of years that intervened between the ancestor and descendant generations, whether a refresher generation was grown before the experiment, where the experiment occurred (field, greenhouse, growth chamber), and which manipulations (if any) were applied. We also gathered information about each focal species, including: the mating system (selfing, mixed mating, outcrossing), the life history strategy (annual or perennial), and the status of the species in the region where seeds were collected (native, non‐native and naturalized, or non‐native and invasive). Five studies did not indicate whether the species was native to the location where source populations were identified—when this information was not supplied, we used reputable sources to add this information to our table (e.g., Calflora, USDA Plants). Furthermore, for perennial species, data on generation times were rarely available, and the mating system was often challenging to categorize correctly. For example, many species in the data set operated along a mating system spectrum rather than being strictly selfing or obligately outcrossing. Therefore, we focused our analyses on trait category and life history strategy, which had the most comprehensive representation across the data set.

Rate of evolution in haldanes

To assess the speed of evolution detected in resurrection experiments of annual species—for which we know that the generation time is 1 year—we calculated rates of evolution, in haldanes, using the formula given by Hendry and Kinnison (1999), which gives evolutionary rates in standard deviation units: h=[(x2/sp)–(x1/sp)]/Ng, where h = haldanes (the rate of evolution), x 2 = the mean trait value of descendants, x 1 = the mean trait value of ancestors, s p = the pooled standard deviation, Ng = the number of generations.

We used the number of intervening years between ancestors and descendants as an approximation of the number of generations (Appendix S1: Table S1). We excluded perennials from this calculation as we did not have data on their generation times.

Phylogenetic correlation matrix

To correct for relatedness of focal species in the data set, we included a phylogenetic correlation matrix, based on data from the Open Tree of Life (Hinchliff et al., 2015), as a random effect in our meta‐analysis. We produced our tree using updated taxonomic names with the function tol_induced_subtree in the R package rotl ver. 3.1.0 (Michonneau et al., 2016) and calculated branch lengths using the function compute.brlen in the R package ape version 5.7.1 (Paradis and Schliep, 2019). Finally, we computed a phylogenetic correlation matrix for use in our meta‐analysis using the function vcv in the ape package (Paradis and Schliep, 2019).

Formal meta‐analysis

For the meta‐analysis, we first contrasted ancestral and descendant trait values by computing the standardized mean difference effect size (Hedges’ g) (Hedges, 1981) and variance with the function escalc in the R package metafor version 4.8‐0 (Viechtbauer, 2010). Positive values of this effect size indicate that the descendant generation had larger trait or fitness values than the ancestral generation.

Our data set combines LES traits that reflect different drought responses. For example, high specific leaf area (SLA) and low leaf dry matter content (LDMC) have been linked to a drought escape strategy, whereas low SLA and high LDMC are linked to a drought avoidance or tolerance strategy (Kooyers, 2015). Therefore, an increase in SLA and decrease in LDMC in the descendant generation would indicate an evolution of drought escape, whereas lower SLA and higher LDMC in the descendant generation would indicate drought avoidance or tolerance. Calculations of effect sizes based on these variables would have contrasting signs. For example, the evolution of drought escape would result in a positive effect size for SLA but a negative effect size for LDMC. To ensure concordance in the sign of the effect sizes, we converted SLA to its inverse (leaf mass area) by multiplying the effect size for SLA by negative one (–1), such that the directionality of this trait matched expectations for drought escape (i.e., low leaf mass area and LDMC signal drought escape). Further, for three studies that measured “relative water content” or “foliar water content” as leaf wet mass/dry mass, we categorized these traits as LDMC but again multiplied by –1 for the sign to match directionality of LDMC, which is measured as leaf dry mass/wet mass.

To account for substantial variation among studies in the number of years that elapsed between the ancestral and descendant generations, we divided each effect size by the number of intervening years between the generations, resulting in effect sizes scaled by time elapsed between the ancestor and descendent generations. To preserve variance in the rescaled units, we adjusted variance in the effect sizes by dividing the variance in the overall effect size by the square of the number of intervening years, resulting in appropriately scaled effect sizes and variances following the standard property of variance under linear transformation. We used the function rma.mv in metafor 4.8‐0 (Viechtbauer, 2010) to analyze the scaled effect size as a function of these moderators: life history strategy (perennial vs. annual), trait category, and the two‐way interaction. All analyses included study identifier, species name, population identifier, and the phylogenetic correction as random effects. We conducted three separate statistical tests using our data set to address our hypotheses. To account for testing multiple hypotheses with one data set, we set our alpha to 0.017 (=0.05/3 tests).

Analysis 1

To examine whether annual plants showed greater magnitudes of evolutionary response to environmental change than perennials (H1) and if annual and perennial plants had different magnitudes and directions of evolutionary response for different traits (H2), we analyzed the magnitude of evolutionary change (effect size scaled by number of intervening years) as a function of life history, trait category, and their interaction with all random effects listed above.

Analysis 2

To test whether annual species evolved a drought escape strategy and perennials evolved drought tolerance (H3), we used the subset of studies that imposed a drought treatment (N = 19) and included only observations from the drought treatment. We then analyzed the magnitude of evolutionary change as a function of life history, trait category, and their interactions, and with all random effects listed above.

Analysis 3

To test whether descendants evolved higher plasticity than ancestors (H4), we again focused on studies that imposed a drought treatment (N = 19) to enable sufficient replication. The studies included in this analysis were conducted in the context of drought specifically (N = 13), climate change more broadly (N = 5), and focal species invasion into a new habitat (N = 1); thus, they are studies that included experimental treatments and studied population response to environmental variability. We calculated the relative distance plasticity index (RDPI; Valladares et al., 2006) for each generation and trait as the absolute difference between the means of the control and experimental treatment divided by the sum of those means. We then calculated Hedges’ g using the RDPI values for each generation and trait as the descendant RDPI minus the ancestor RDPI, resulting in an effect size (scaled by the number of intervening years) that relates to the evolution of plasticity. We analyzed this rate as a function of life history, trait category, and their interactions, and with all random effects listed above.

RESULTS

Quantitative literature review

The resurrection studies in our review included flowering plants from 21 orders, 28 families, and 87 species (Appendix S2: Figure S2) across five continents, with most studies from Europe and the United States (Figure 3A). Most studies detected phenotypic evolution, with significant differences between ancestors and descendants in at least one trait found in 56 of 61 studies (Appendix S1: Table S1). Evidence for evolution was found in about half of all traits measured across studies. Only five studies reported no significant evolutionary changes between generations in any of the traits measured (Appendix S1: Table S1). The average time between the ancestor and descendant collections was 21.5 years (SD = 13.9; mode = 7; range = 2–90 years) (Appendix S2: Figure S3A). Following best practices, 65% of studies grew a refresher generation before the experimental generation, but there was uneven reporting on seed germination rates in ancestral and descendant generations. Resurrection studies to date are biased toward native, annual plants in laboratory settings (Appendix 1; Appendix S2: Figure S4). About two thirds of resurrection studies used annual species (Figure 3B). Many studies did not specifically report the mating system or native status of their study species. Most studies grew plants under benign conditions, but when plants were grown under multiple environments (N = 22), drought stress was the most common treatment (N = 19). Fewer than half of the studies measured plasticity, with many of these reporting the evolution of increased trait plasticity (Appendix 1: Table S1). Most studies included only one source population, but the number of populations ranged from 1–60 (mean = 6 populations per study; SD = 10.5, mode = 1) (Appendix S2: Figure S3B). For those studies that included >2 populations, few spanned the full geographic range of the species (but see Kooyers, 2015; Dickman et al., 2019; Vtipil and Sheth, 2020; Wooliver et al., 2020; Anstett et al., 2021; Branch et al., 2024; Sheth et al., 2026) or environmental gradients (but see Nevo et al., 2012). Reporting on maternal lines included in each study varied, but of those that reported, around 33% of studies included 30 or more maternal lines as recommended to capture population diversity (Franks et al., 2018). We found eight published resurrection studies with genomic or genetic data (Appendix S1: Table S1). Of these, four experiments paired genomic and phenotypic data to examine genetic changes between the ancestral and descendant generations. Studies have found changes in allele frequencies in genes related to drought stress and flowering time (Rhoné et al., 2010; Nevo et al., 2012; Franks et al., 2016) and upregulation of those genes (Hamann et al., 2021).

Resurrection studies revealed rapid evolution

In calculating rates of evolution in the annual plants in the data set, we found evidence for rapid evolution, but also substantial variation within and between trait categories. An evolutionary rate of 0.1 haldane is a change in the average phenotype of 0.1 SD in 1 generation. Across all traits and species, the average absolute value of the evolutionary rate was 0.090 haldanes (N = 1181, SE = 0.0038). However, this average rate also varied substantially, with cases ranging up to 1.65 haldanes. When we examined evolutionary rate by trait category, LES traits and phenology had the fastest rates and allocation, floral traits and physiology the slowest (Figure 4; Appendix S2: Table S3). In terms of directionality, we found several trends, including, for example, that fitness evolved to be greater (for a list of fitness components, see Appendix S2: Table S2), while phenology evolved to be earlier (Appendix S2: Table S3).

Formal meta‐analysis

Overview

Across all extracted data sets (46 retained studies), we calculated 2151 effect size observations, including 916 observations from perennial species and 1251 from annual species (Figure 3C). In the full data set, 647 observations were from experimental manipulations of factors such as drought and herbivory, while 1504 were from benign, well‐watered conditions that were designed to avoid a stress response (Appendix S1).

Annual and perennial species differ in the magnitude and directionality of evolution

The meta‐analysis supported the hypothesis (H1) that annual plants would have faster rates of evolution than perennials. We found significant effects of trait category (N = 2151, QM = 123.48, df = 8, P < 0.0001) and the interaction between trait category and life history (N = 2133, QM = 111.94, df = 6, P < 0.0001; Appendix S2: Table S4), indicating that the magnitude and directionality of evolutionary shifts differed for annual and perennial plant species across traits. For annual species, the descendant lineages had lower values for traits related to the LES, reflecting the evolution of a resource‐acquisitive, drought‐escape strategy (low LDMC, high SLA, low leaf mass area) and earlier phenology than annual ancestral lineages, with floral traits and plant size trending lower, meaning that, while this relationship is not significant, descendants tended toward smaller flowers and smaller overall size. Floral traits were not quantified in perennial species in our data set. Perennial descendants did not significantly differ from ancestors in the magnitude of evolutionary change, though phenology also trended earlier, while trait values like physiology, LES traits, fitness, and allocation trended higher (Figure 5; Appendix S2: Table S5, Figure S5).

Drought stress imposes selection for earlier phenology in annual and perennial species

We found mixed support for our second hypothesis (H2) that drought stress leads to the evolution of drought escape in annuals and drought tolerance in perennials. Focusing on drought studies (N = 19, n = 397), we found significant effects of trait category and the interaction between trait category and life history (QM = 23.06, df = 2, P < 0.001; Appendix S2: Table S4). As expected, we detected evidence for the evolution of drought escape in annual descendants through earlier phenology (Figure 6; Appendix S2: Table S5, Figure S6). Surprisingly, we also found evidence of earlier phenology in perennial plants. This finding is in opposition to our initial hypothesis that perennials would evolve delayed flowering as a resource conservation strategy associated with drought avoidance or tolerance.

To test whether global change favors the evolution of increased phenotypic plasticity (H3), we analyzed the subset of studies that imposed an experimental treatment (N = 19, n = 271). This analysis revealed that plasticity (the relative distance plasticity index, Valladares et al., 2006) varied with trait category (QM = 2235.79, df = 7, P < 0.0001) and the interaction between trait category and life history (QM = 984.45, df = 4, P < 0.0001; Appendix S2: Table S4). For annual plants, we found an increase in plasticity for phenology and physiology traits, and a decrease in plasticity for traits from the LES (Figure 7; Appendix S2: Table S5, Figure S7). DISCUSSION

Our meta‐analysis of resurrection studies is the first to address key hypotheses about evolutionary responses to recent global change and enables us to make the following major conclusions. First, our results support the hypothesis that annuals had greater magnitudes of evolutionary response than perennials (H1), but the magnitude and direction of evolutionary response varied with trait category (H2). Specifically, annuals shifted toward fast, resource‐acquisitive leaf and phenology traits, including a more rapid initiation of flowering and production of thinner, presumably more cheaply made leaves, whereas perennials had limited evolutionary shifts in any trait category. Second, when focusing on drought as one potential stressor associated with global change, we found support for evolutionary shifts toward traits associated with drought escape in annuals (earlier onset of flowering, higher SLA, and lower LDMC), but perennials did not have any consistent evolutionary shifts toward drought avoidance or tolerance (H3). Third, although we did not find evidence for the evolution of increased plasticity (H4) for all life histories and trait categories, annuals evolved increased plasticity in phenological and physiological traits and decreased plasticity in LES traits. Overall, our review of resurrection studies revealed significant evolutionary changes in over 50% of observed traits, adding to a growing body of work showing that rapid evolution in natural populations is common (Sultan et al., 2013; Franks et al., 2016; Christie et al., 2023; White et al., 2023; Karitter et al., 2025). However, rapid evolution was not ubiquitous; nearly half of the traits we examined did not differ between ancestral and descendant generations. Furthermore, we found limited evidence for consistent differences between ancestors and descendants across many trait categories, likely because trait evolution proceeded in different directions depending on species, populations and context. Below, we highlight opportunities for future research to clarify when and where rapid evolution is most likely.

Annuals had greater magnitudes of trait evolution than perennials

Possibly due to their shorter generation times (Friedman, 2020), annuals had greater magnitudes of evolutionary change relative to perennials. Specifically, annuals evolved resource‐acquisitive traits and advanced phenology from ancestors to descendants in both the full analysis of all studies (Figure 5) and the analysis of studies that included a drought treatment (Figure 6), consistent with the evolution of drought escape. In contrast, evolutionary shifts were less evident in perennials, with descendants having earlier phenology than ancestors only in response to drought (Figure 6). Thus, our results did not provide evidence for the evolution of drought avoidance or tolerance in perennials, but we note that the statistical power to detect trends in perennials was limited due to the modest number of studies (N = 20). Moreover, temporal and spatial patterns may play a greater role than life history in shaping adaptive responses. For example, repeated late‐season droughts could favor drought escape in annuals and perennials (Heschel and Riginos, 2005). Furthermore, in certain environments such as mediterranean climates with a growing season that often ends in a terminal drought, the evolution of flowering time may be decoupled from resource‐conservative traits that are favored under drought avoidance or tolerance (Austen et al., 2017).

Our results are consistent with prior studies showing shifts toward earlier reproduction in many spring‐flowering species (Parmesan and Yohe, 2003; Thackeray et al., 2016; Inouye, 2022). Phenology was the most commonly measured trait across resurrection studies (Figure 3B, C), with 76% of studies assessing reproductive timing, likely contributing to the strong signal detected in our meta‐analysis. For example, a series of resurrection studies in the annual plant Brassica rapa revealed adaptive evolution of earlier flowering following a multi‐year late‐season drought, consistent with stress escape (Franks et al., 2007; Weis et al., 2014). Other sets of traits associated with a resource‐acquisitive strategy also evolved rapidly, including developmental timing and water‐use efficiency (Franks and Weis, 2008; Franks, 2011), with similar patterns across populations, drought episodes, and in experimental evolution (Hamann et al., 2018; Johnson et al., 2022). Thus, global advancements in spring phenology could be associated with evolutionary shifts in a suite of traits. However, resurrection experiments with B. rapa illustrate that extreme drought can limit escape strategies (Hamann et al., 2018), underscoring the need to examine the boundaries of adaptation. Genomic analyses further revealed the genetic basis of these responses (Franks et al., 2016; Hamann et al., 2021), highlighting the value of integrating genomic tools in future resurrection studies.

Trait plasticity has evolved in annuals, but has not always increased

In regions where climatic variability has increased, climate change could impose novel selection on reaction norms (Nicotra et al., 2010; Chevin and Hoffmann, 2017). Our analysis detected increased plasticity in annual plants for phenology and physiology and decreased plasticity for LES traits (Figure 7). While plasticity can promote short‐term persistence (Richardson et al., 2017), it may not suffice in the long term (Vinton et al., 2022) and is not always adaptive (Ghalambor et al., 2007; Duputié et al., 2015). Future resurrection studies should link reaction norms to fitness via selection analyses to assess adaptive value. Physiological traits like water‐use efficiency (WUE) and photosynthesis, which respond quickly and reversibly to environmental change (Grime and Mackey, 2002), may be especially prone to evolve plasticity under variability (Stotz et al., 2021). Yet few resurrection studies have quantified traits associated with physiology and resource allocation (Figure 3B, C). Nevertheless, drought can drive shifts in root traits and biomass allocation to enhance water acquisition (Padilla and Pugnaire, 2007; Comas et al., 2013; Lynch, 2013). CO2 enrichment, warming, and drought interact to exert selection on physiology, including WUE, stomatal conductance, and photosynthesis (Ainsworth and Rogers, 2007; Reich et al., 2018; Sendall et al., 2024). Future resurrection studies in variable environments could simultaneously assess evolutionary shifts in these traits and test for adaptive plasticity.

Reduced plasticity in LES traits may reflect declining responsiveness to environmental variation (Stotz et al., 2022). Stress may promote stronger trait integration (Schlichting, 1986; Chapin et al., 1993; Pigliucci, 2005), constraining the evolution of plasticity. Geographic differences in climate change exposure (IPCC et al., 2023) may also matter: Plasticity might not evolve if climatic variability remains unchanged. For example, Wooliver et al. (2020) found reduced thermal breadth in a southern Mimulus cardinalis population, likely due to decreased temperature seasonality, and Branch et al. (2024) found plasticity loss in southern and gain in northern populations, aligning with spatial drought gradients. These findings emphasize the need to consider both mean climate shifts and variability over time. Experiments that expose ancestors and descendants from multiple populations to diverse environments can reveal how evolution, plasticity, and their interaction shape trait change (Anstett et al., 2021; Sheth et al., 2026), and can uncover the genetic basis of these responses (Franks et al., 2018).

Evolution is rapid but varies across traits and studies

We found extensive variation across trait categories in the rate of evolution, with the fastes evolution for LES and phenological traits, moderate rates of evolution for fitness and plant size, and slower rates for traits associated with resource allocation, floral characteristics and physiology. This result accords with prior studies that indicate that flowering time can evolve very rapidly and often, as we found, to earlier onset (Austen et al., 2017), while plant physiological traits are often highly plastic but may be somewhat less evolutionarily labile (Arnold et al., 2024). It would be premature to draw conclusions from the seven reports of evolution of allocation‐based traits, and the differences among trait categories should be interpreted with caution given the small sample sizes of some of these categories. However, these results highlight some potentially interesting trends and indicate that future resurrection experiments should attempt to quantify a greater breadth of phenotypes.

We found rapid rates of evolution (in haldanes) for annual species (Figure 4). For some trait categories, the evolutionary rates were as high as 1.65 haldanes, which is substantially higher than in previous reviews (Hendry and Kinnison, 1999; Bone and Farres, 2001). These calculations add to a growing body of evidence that evolution in natural populations can be extremely rapid, particularly under strong selection. It is important to note, however, that rapid evolution can also be caused by drift, especially in small populations, and it can be maladaptive, particularly under stress (Battlay et al., 2023; Fussmann and Kopp, 2023). The resurrection approach can distinguish adaptive from maladaptive or neutral evolution by analyzing the fitness consequences of trait shifts (Hamann et al., 2018). Future studies that expose lineages to ancestral and contemporary environments can test whether selection favors ancestral trait values in environments that reflect historical climates and descendant trait values in environments that reflect contemporary climates (Figure 1). Furthermore, quantitative genetic experiments could be designed to contrast the adaptive potential of ancestral and descendant lines through estimates of genetic variance in fitness and traits under different climate scenarios.

Notably, in nearly half of the cases included in our meta‐analysis, significant trait differences between ancestral and descendant generations were not documented, and most studies did not include demographic data or links between trait evolution and changes in population size. This ambiguity makes it highly challenging to predict the likelihood of evolutionary rescue, which occurs when adaptive evolution leads to population recovery (reviewed by Carlson et al., 2014). A key area for future research is further disentangling when, why, and where rapid evolution occurs and how this evolution influences population growth. Theory and empirical results from microcosm experiments suggest that evolutionary rescue is most likely when population sizes are large, standing genetic variation is high, and the rate of environmental change is gradual (Carlson et al., 2014). Integrating resurrection studies with data on demography (Sheth and Angert, 2018), quantitative genetic variation, climate anomalies, and knowledge of the agents and targets of selection (Wadgymar et al., 2022) will inform predictions of when and how evolution could enable or hinder long‐term population persistence with climate change. As we continue to broaden the taxonomic and geographic breadth of resurrection research and expand the spatial and temporal sampling within these studies, our understanding of the contexts in which populations are most likely to adapt to climate change will improve.

Recommendations and considerations for future studies

Our literature review revealed challenges, recommendations, and suggestions for future work (Table 2, Figure 8). We found that studies in our literature review and meta‐analysis often failed to report key attributes that could shed light on the patterns we observed (Table 2). When available, information about the range of possible generation times for perennial plants could generate novel insights into the differences in evolutionary rates between annuals and perennials. Further, mating system could also influence evolutionary rates, but we were unable to investigate its role due to inconsistent reporting. Thus, to maximize the ability to synthesize data across resurrection studies, future studies should provide more comprehensive information on mating system, generation time, native status, and sample sizes, along with geographic coordinates and climate data for source populations (Table 2). Further, we encourage studies to report all traits investigated rather than only those with statistically significant differences between ancestors and descendants. Moreover, few resurrection studies have examined biotic interactions, invasive species, mating system evolution, multiple environmental factors and evolutionary changes along environmental gradients in field settings, or rangewide variation (Figure 8); Appendix S3). We encourage researchers to design future studies to test how complex suites of changing abiotic and biotic conditions influence plant evolution.

Our literature review revealed numerous additional opportunities for continued application of the resurrection approach. One key resource available for studies in North America is Project Baseline (Franks et al., 2008; Etterson et al., 2016), an established long‐term seed bank of a set of focal species, supporting research projects that use these collections. Further, conservation seed bank collections have been used in resurrection studies (Everingham et al., 2021; Rauschkolb et al., 2022; Karitter et al., 2025) and can be considered for future experiments (Mattana et al., 2025). In addition, we recommend that resurrection studies include locations outside of North America and Europe, which are currently overrepresented. Additionally, woody species have been examined in only two studies (Everingham et al., 2021, 2024). This underrepresentation is notable given the ecological importance of long‐lived plants in carbon storage, ecosystem stability, and biodiversity support (Pan et al., 2011; Betts et al., 2017) and their potential for adaptive genetic variation (Bisbing et al., 2021; Caignard et al., 2024). While such studies are challenging due to seed availability and timescales, resurrection studies in woody perennials are feasible with foresight and planning. Finally, genomic studies thus far have largely confirmed the genetic basis of phenotypic changes. Challenges remain, such as the often‐limited ability to link genomic changes to phenotypic outcomes (Hoban et al., 2016). Combining multigenerational and resurrection studies with whole‐genome sequencing and other ‐omics techniques has the potential to greatly enhance our understanding of plant adaptation (Brown and Koenig, 2022; Anstett et al., 2026).

CONCLUSIONS

From a meta‐analysis of 46 studies from the last two decades, we detected widespread and rapid evolution in natural plant populations, including the evolution of drought escape in annual plants, which matches evolutionary theory. However, in nearly half of the examined cases, no significant trait differences between ancestral and descendant generations were documented, highlighting the challenges of accurately predicting the probability of evolutionary rescue. One critical area for future research is assessing whether rapid, directional selection across generations results in a reduction of genetic variance or an increase in genetic correlations opposing selection, potentially constraining future adaptive potential (Kopp and Matuszewski, 2014). Additionally, most resurrection studies have focused on single stressors, yet natural environments are inherently complex. Investigating potential fitness trade‐offs—such as whether adaptation to drought escape compromises competitive ability or reduces resistance to herbivory—will provide a more realistic picture of how plants evolve under simultaneous pressures. By moving toward more integrative, multivariate approaches and including a broader range of life histories, resurrection studies can play a key role in predicting and supporting species persistence under accelerating global change.
