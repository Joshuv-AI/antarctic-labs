Intercontinental VA-ECMO transfer from Reunion Island for access to heart transplantation and durable mechanical support: a 15-year experience
Authors: Charles Vidal, Théophile Harle, Nicolas Allou, Thomas Bradier, Arthur Neuschwander, Margot Caron, Eric Braunberger, Audrey Maillot, Jerôme Corré, Julien Jabot, Adrien Bouglé, Charles-Edouard Luyt, Pascal Leprince, Pascal Battistella
Published: 2026

Reunion Island is a French overseas territory located more than 10,000 km from mainland France. For most of the study period, heart transplantation and durable ventricular assist device (VAD) implantation were unavailable locally. Patients with refractory cardiogenic shock supported by veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) who were considered potential candidates for advanced heart failure therapies therefore required intercontinental transfer to mainland France [1, 2]. Such transfers mobilize substantial logistical, human, and financial resources while exposing critically ill patients and their families to the consequences of prolonged geographical separation. Data regarding long-distance VA-ECMO transfer for access to advanced therapies remain scarce [3]. We report a 15-year experience of intercontinental VA-ECMO transfer from Reunion Island.

We retrospectively reviewed all consecutive adult patients transferred under VA-ECMO from Reunion Island to mainland France between January 2011 and January 2026 for consideration of heart transplantation or durable ventricular assist device (VAD) implantation. Among 324 VA-ECMO implantations performed during the study period, 43 patients underwent intercontinental transfer and 39 were included after exclusion of four patients transferred for other indications (heart–lung transplantation, cardiac surgery unavailable locally, pediatric transfer, and transfer to another country). Median age was 50 years (IQR 33–57), and the leading causes of cardiogenic shock were ischemic and dilated cardiomyopathies (Supplementary Table S1). Transfer was performed a median of 5 days (IQR 3–8) after VA-ECMO initiation. Patients were transported under deep sedation on a portable Cardiohelp® system by a dedicated ECMO retrieval team during scheduled commercial flights lasting approximately 11 h (Supplementary Fig. S1) [4]. Intra-aortic balloon pumps, when present, were systematically removed before transport. All patients reached the receiving advanced heart failure center alive. Four patients (10%) experienced major transport-related complications requiring medical intervention, including three episodes of septic shock requiring vasopressor escalation and one ventricular arrhythmia successfully treated by electrical cardioversion. No patient experienced cardiac arrest, major bleeding, ECMO circuit failure, or accidental decannulation during transport (Supplementary Table S2).

Overall, 22 patients (56%) ultimately accessed advanced heart failure therapies, including 14 heart transplants and 8 durable VAD implantations (Fig. 1). Nineteen patients underwent urgent transplant listing after transfer. Although only a subset of patients were considered immediate candidates for transplantation upon arrival, several initially ineligible patients ultimately accessed advanced therapies following reassessment and escalation to alternative temporary mechanical circulatory support strategies. Patients who ultimately reached transplantation or durable mechanical support generally presented with less severe disease at VA-ECMO initiation, characterized by lower lactate concentrations, fewer episodes of cardiac arrest, and a lower incidence of major ECMO-related complications, particularly vascular events (Supplementary Table S3). Overall, in-hospital mortality was 49%, but was markedly lower among patients who accessed advanced therapies than among those who did not (18% vs. 88%, p < 0.001). Durable VAD implantation proved an effective bridge-to-transplant strategy when immediate transplantation was not feasible. Among the six patients surviving VAD implantation, five subsequently underwent successful heart transplantation after a median of nine months, while the remaining patient was still active on the transplant waiting list at last follow-up. After exclusion of two patients with less than one year of follow-up, one-year mortality was 51% in the overall cohort and remained substantially lower among patients who accessed advanced therapies than among those who did not (20% vs. 88%, p < 0.001).

Our findings support the feasibility and safety of intercontinental VA-ECMO transfer over extreme distances when performed by a dedicated retrieval team using standardized transport protocols. All patients reached the receiving advanced heart failure center alive, and major transport-related complications were infrequent and successfully managed without ECMO circuit failure, accidental decannulation, or cardiac arrest. More importantly, our experience suggests that the major challenge lies not in the transfer itself but in selecting patients most likely to benefit from advanced heart failure therapies. Only a minority of patients receiving VA-ECMO ultimately required intercontinental transfer, as many recovered on temporary support or were not considered suitable candidates for advanced therapies. Within this selected population, successful bridging was associated with less severe disease at VA-ECMO initiation and fewer ECMO-related complications, particularly vascular complications, highlighting the importance of preserving end-organ function and minimizing ECMO-related morbidity [5]. Importantly, candidacy for transplantation often remained dynamic: several patients initially considered unsuitable ultimately accessed definitive therapies following reassessment and escalation to alternative temporary mechanical circulatory support. Furthermore, durable VAD implantation proved an effective bridge-to-transplant strategy, with five of the six surviving VAD recipients subsequently undergoing successful heart transplantation. Together, these findings support intercontinental transfer as a bridge-to-decision strategy, providing access to specialized reassessment and advanced circulatory support rather than transplantation alone. Although limited by its retrospective design, small sample size, and inherent selection bias, this study represents one of the longest reported experiences of intercontinental VA-ECMO transfer for access to advanced heart failure therapies.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This retrospective observational study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the STROBE reporting guidelines. In accordance with French regulations governing retrospective non-interventional research, written informed consent was not required. The study was conducted under the French MR-004 data protection framework for research not involving human intervention (Health Data Hub registration No. 31238545).
