Comparative clinical evaluation of Icon resin infiltration and Vanish XT varnish for managing white spot lesions: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Deem Al-Blaihed, Doaa Jamal, Khlood Baghlaf, Shahad N Abudawood, Rabab Aljawi, Omar Abd El Sadek El Meligy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhite spot lesions (WSLs) are a common esthetic concern. This study compared the clinical efficacy of Icon® resin infiltration (RI) and Vanish™ XT varnish (Vanish XT) in improving WSL appearance. A single-blinded randomized controlled trial was conducted on 138 WSLs in 33 children aged 7–14 years. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive Icon® RI (n = 75) or Vanish XT (n = 63). Lesions were evaluated at baseline (T0), immediately post-treatment (T1), and after 12 weeks (T2). Objective color changes were measured using the CIE L*a*b* system, while subjective esthetic improvement and opacity reduction were assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS). The Icon® group demonstrated significantly greater reduction in color mismatch relative to adjacent sound enamel at T1 (ΔE = 2.34 ± 0.5) compared with the Vanish XT group (ΔE = 6.35 ± 1.2, P < 0.05). However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as measurements at T1 may have been influenced by transient enamel dehydration. At 12 weeks, the Icon® group continued to demonstrate lower ΔE values than the Vanish XT group (ΔE = 4.69 ± 0.7 vs. 8.50 ± 1.3, P < 0.001). VAS scores were significantly higher for the Icon® group in both esthetic satisfaction and opacity reduction (P < 0.01). Minor surface staining occurred in two Icon® cases at follow up but did not affect overall esthetic perception. Within the short follow-up period, Icon® RI demonstrated improved esthetic outcomes compared with Vanish™ XT, with differences observed in color parameters and subjective assessments. Introduction

White spot lesions (WSLs) are characterized by subsurface enamel demineralization that increases porosity within the enamel structure, allowing water or organic fluids to infiltrate the lesion body (Denis et al. 2013). These structural changes alter the optical properties of enamel because the refractive index of porous enamel differs from that of sound enamel. Consequently, light scattering increases within the lesion body, resulting in the characteristic opaque, chalky-white appearance and loss of natural enamel translucency (Meyer-Lueckel and Paris 2008; Ritter et al. 2019). The clinical visibility of WSLs is therefore closely related to changes in enamel optical behavior, including alterations in lightness, translucency, and opacity (Guerra et al. 2015).

These lesions are categorized into pre-eruptive and post-eruptive WSLs based on their timing relative to tooth eruption (Gholami et al. 2023). WSLs are either due developmental disturbances during enamel formation or enamel decalcification following the caries process (Dziaruddin and Zakaria 2022; Gholami et al. 2023). Fluorosis and molar-incisor hypomineralization (MIH) are the two most common developmental anomalies associated with WSL formation (Dziaruddin and Zakaria 2022; Gholami et al. 2023). Dental fluorosis is a disorder that affects teeth and results from excessive fluoride exposure during amelogenesis, often from birth to approximately 8 years of age (Bhagavatula et al. 2016; DenBesten et al. 2002; Niazi and Pepper 2023) while MIH is hypomineralization of one to four first permanent molars, which may also impact the permanent incisors (Weerheijm et al. 2001) and clinically identified by asymmetric and well-defined opacities (Cabral et al. 2020). The color of the defects ranges from white/creamy to yellow/brown, and they frequently progress to post-eruptive breakdown (PEB) (Da Costa-Silva et al. 2011).

The psychological effects of enamel abnormalities on children are an established topic supported by data from both clinical and social science experts (Marshman and Rodd 2015). Unsatisfactory esthetic appearance, low self-esteem, and adverse psychosocial effects have been documented regarding the occurrence of WSLs in the anterior teeth of children (Benson et al. 2015).Oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) and well-being of children with MIH, as a distinct condition, is a relatively new field of investigation (Dantas-Neta et al. 2016; Gutiérrez et al. 2019; Portella et al. 2019).

Contemporary management of WSLs includes several minimally invasive strategies that aim either to remineralize the lesion or to improve its optical appearance. Among these approaches, resin infiltration (RI) and fluoride-releasing resin-modified glass ionomer varnishes represent two conceptually different therapeutic strategies. Resin infiltration primarily acts by penetrating the porous enamel structure and altering its refractive index, thereby masking the lesion and restoring enamel translucency. In contrast, fluoride-releasing varnishes promote remineralization through sustained release of fluoride and other ions, enhancing mineral deposition within the enamel surface.

Despite their different mechanisms of action, limited clinical evidence directly compares the esthetic outcomes of these two treatment modalities within the same clinical setting. Moreover, most previous studies have focused on either post-eruptive demineralization or developmental enamel defects independently. Evaluating both therapeutic strategies within a single randomized clinical framework and across lesions of both developmental and environmental origin may provide valuable insight into their relative clinical performance.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to clinically compare the efficacy of Icon® resin infiltration and Vanish™ XT fluoride-releasing varnish in improving the esthetic appearance and color parameters of WSLs. The null hypothesis proposed that there would be no significant difference in esthetic improvement between the two treatment modalities.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of King Abdulaziz University (KAU), Faculty of Dentistry (proposal number: 140-07-23). This clinical trial was registered at www.Clinicaltrials.gov, with the identification number NCT06696105.

Study design and subjects

This was a parallel-group, single-blinded, randomized controlled trial. Subjects were assigned into two groups: RI assigned as Group I and Vanish XT as Group II. The unit of analysis in the present study was the white spot lesion (WSL), as multiple lesions could be present within the same participant. Each lesion was treated and evaluated independently for the purposes of colorimetric and clinical assessment. The samples were screened and recruited from the Pediatric Dentistry Clinics at King Abdulaziz University Dental Hospital (KAUDH), Jeddah, Saudi Arabia between July 2024 and February 2025. Children were screened for demineralized or hypo-mineralized WSLs, affecting at least one of the labial surfaces of their permanent anterior teeth. Teeth were visually evaluated using ICDAS codes (Pitts and Ekstrand 2013). Only teeth with codes 1 and 2, displayed clinically as chalky white, dull, non-cavitated enamel opacities were included. Regarding MIH lesions, only mild lesions were included according to the EAPD policy document (Lygidakis et al. 2022). Lesions were characterized by well-demarcated enamel opacities in the absence of enamel breakdown, including lesions that exhibit hypersensitivity to external stimuli, such as air and water, but not to mechanical stimuli, such as brushing. Before any treatment, a detailed explanation of the nature of the study, treatment procedure, along with its possible outcomes, risks, benefits, and discomfort were explained to the parents of the children who met the inclusion criteria. All the parents who agreed on the participation of their children and agreed to commit to the follow-up appointments, were included in the study after signing a written informed consent form.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Children were assessed for eligibility according to the following patient and teeth inclusion criteria. Healthy children aged 7–14 years, classified as ASA I according to the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification (ASA, 2020), cooperative based on the Frankl Behavior Rating Scale, and not known to have any allergies to the components of the dental materials used in the study.

Teeth inclusion criteria were a minimum of one WSL on the labial surface of the maxillary or mandibular permanent anterior teeth, the presence of a clinically non-cavitated color change due to enamel demineralization or hypomineralization (Lygidakis et al. 2022; Pitts and Ekstrand 2013) and no history of previous bleaching, restorations, or root canal treatment of the selected tooth. Primary teeth were excluded as were permanent teeth with periodontal disease, dental caries, direct or indirect restorations, or yellow-brown opacities with post-eruptive enamel breakdown.

Sample size

The sample size for the clinical trial was determined using G Power software (Kang 2021). A significance level of 0.05 and a power of 85% were used as a base for calculations, based on two similar studies (Kannan and Padmanabhan 2019; Rohym et al. 2020). The outcomes were the variance in color between the treatment groups over the follow-up period on WSLs before and after treatment in the study by (Kannan and Padmanabhan 2019). It was estimated that 88 WSLs (44 WSLs in each arm) were adequate. To compensate for loss to follow-up or other attrition causes, the sample size was recalculated with an additional 10%; accordingly, a total of 96 WSLs were required at the beginning of the clinical trial.

Sample groups, randomization and blinding

Randomization was performed to assign individual WSLs, in a 1:1 ratio, to one of the two treatment groups using computer-generated randomization software available at www.randomization.com. Therefore, allocation occurred at the lesion level rather than at the patient level, allowing each eligible lesion to be independently assigned to either the RI group or the Vanish XT group. Study reporting followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trails (CONSORT) guidelines (Moher et al. 2010). The materials and devices used for each treatment method are described along with their manufacturer’s information in Table 1. Only patients were blinded to the treatment groups as blinding of examiners was not possible.

Procedure

Prior to the examination, a comprehensive review of the participants’ medical history, demographic characteristics, such as gender, age, and nationality, as well as contact details were documented in a standardized form. Oral hygiene status was assessed using the debris index-simplified (DI-S) (Greene and Vermillion 1960).

All participants received professional dental prophylaxis, which included ultrasonic scaling when calculus deposits were present, followed by mechanical polishing using a prophylaxis paste (Ultrapro TX prophy paste) applied with a nylon brush mounted on a slow-speed handpiece. After prophylaxis, comprehensive clinical examination was performed under optimal lighting. The teeth surfaces were air-dried and systematically assessed for the presence of opacities using the ICDAS II criteria for environmental WSLs and the EAPD criteria for developmental WSLs (Lygidakis et al. 2022; Pitts and Ekstrand 2013). All findings were documented accordingly.

The shade of the WSLs and the adjacent healthy enamel was measured using the VITA EasyShade Advance® 4.0 spectrophotometer (Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Sackingen, Germany). A protective shield was applied to the probe tip, and the instrument was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Color changes in the treatment groups were evaluated at three time points: baseline (T0), immediately post-treatment (T1), and after 12 weeks (T2). To ensure consistency and reproducibility, all measurements were conducted under standardized conditions at the same dental clinic, between 10:00 AM and 12:00 PM. Additionally, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, the lesion area was measured until two successive measurements reported the same shade. Color measurements were obtained using the VITA EasyShade Advance® 4.0 spectrophotometer (Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany), which records tooth color using standardized CIE L*a*b* color coordinates. The Commission Internationale de l’Eclairage (CIE) color parameters, namely ΔE, encompass variables L, a, and b. ΔE= (ΔL*2 +Δa*2+ Δb*2) (1/2). L* represents the lightness coordinate, with values ranging from 0 (perfect black) to 100 (perfect white), while a* and b* represent the red-green and yellow-blue chromaticity coordinates, respectively (Jiang and Guo 2021). Among these parameters, L* was considered particularly relevant in the present study because white spot lesions are primarily perceived clinically as alterations in enamel brightness and opacity. Therefore, changes in L* values provide a clinically meaningful indicator of improvements in lesion visibility and esthetic appearance as perceived by the human eye (Fig. 1).

Color measurement procedure. (A) Spectrophotometric assessment of white spot lesions (WSLs) using the CIE Lab* color system. (B) Recorded spectrophotometric readings for WSLs at different evaluation time points (T0, T1, T2)

ΔE is a quantitative metric used to assess the difference between two colors. A ΔE value of 3.7 is commonly accepted as the clinical threshold for perceptible color mismatch or change (Johnston and Kao 1989). Specifically, WSLs are considered clinically undetectable when the ΔE value between the lesion and adjacent sound enamel is below 3.7, whereas values exceeding this threshold indicate that the lesion is visibly distinct (Kannan and Padmanabhan 2019).

Standardized clinical photographs were acquired using a DSLR camera (Nikon D5100, Nikon Corporation, Thailand) equipped with an AF-S Nikon 18–55 mm f/3.5-5.6G VR II macro lens (Nikon Corporation, Thailand) was used for all cases. The camera was configured with the following settings: shutter speed, 1/125 s; aperture, F22; ISO 200; and auto white balance. The external MF-R76 ring flash (Godox, China) was operated in the manual mode at 1/16 power without exposure compensation. Using disinfected photo retractors, intraoral photographs were obtained before, immediately after, and 12 weeks after application of the intervention methods.

A cross-polarization filter compatible with a ring flash (Polarisa, Egypt) was prepared and mounted securely onto a camera lens. The purpose of this filter was to eliminate all light reflections and glare, to observe the lesion borders more clearly. To minimize inter-operator variability, all photographic acquisitions were performed by a single trained examiner, following a predefined standardized protocol. To ensure consistency in the imaging conditions, the camera was mounted on a fixed tripod, maintaining a constant distance from the subject. Additionally, the dental unit was stabilized in a standardized resting position to minimize variability in photographic parameters. Standardized intraoral photographs were obtained at each assessment interval (T0, T1, and T2). The dimensions and locations of the WSLs were mapped to ensure accurate shade measurements.

All the procedures were performed by a single dentist who was calibrated to the use of the spectrophotometer. All participants in both study groups were provided with appropriate eye protection. For individuals reporting pre-operative discomfort or hypersensitivity, a standardized anesthetic regimen was administered. This involved the application of 20% benzocaine topical anesthesia (Prime-Gel, Topical Anesthetic Gel, Cherry, Prime Dental, Saudi Arabia) to the dried mucosal surface, followed by infiltration with a few drops of local anesthesia using lidocaine HCl 2% with epinephrine 1:100.000 (Octocaine® 100, Novocol Healthcare Inc. Cambridge, Ontario, Canada) administered to the buccal vestibule adjacent to the treated teeth.

The teeth in all treatment groups were isolated from the lip and gingival tissues using OptraDam® Plus (Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein). Dental floss ligatures (Dental Floss, non-waxed, Oral-B Laboratories, Iowa City, IA, USA) were placed. To further enhance the marginal seal and prevent leakage at the gingival sulcular level, OpalDam® (Ultradent Products, South Jordan, UT, USA) was applied.

To eliminate surface discoloration, smoothen the superficial enamel layer, and acquire a highly polished surface of mineralized tissue, mild microabrasion was performed using Opalustre™ (Ultradent Products, South Jordan, UT, USA) as recommended in the literature (AAPD 2024; Bahadir et al. 2022; Gençer and Kirzioğlu 2019; Lygidakis et al. 2022; Segura et al. 1997). A single application of a slurry layer was applied to the affected tooth surfaces and Opal prophy cups were used with a slow-speed handpiece under light pressure (< 20 s per surface) after which the teeth were thoroughly rinsed with water and air-dried.

Icon® resin infiltration protocol

RI (Icon®; DMG, Hamburg, Germany) was applied following the manufacturer’s instructions. A 15% HCl solution (Icon® Etch, DMG, Hamburg, Germany) was applied to the lesion in a sufficient quantity using the provided etch syringe and was periodically massaged onto the lesion surface using the syringe tip in circular motions and allowed to remain in contact with the surface for 2 min. Then, the area was thoroughly rinsed with water for 30 s, followed by drying using an air spray. Upon observation of the chalky appearance of the enamel surface, a drying step using ethanol applied to the lesion using a designated dry syringe and left for 30 s, after which the surface was again dried with an air spray. The etching and drying steps were repeated for three cycles. Then, an adequate amount of resin infiltrant was dispensed onto the lesion using an infiltrant syringe. The material was left in place for 3 min to ensure thorough penetration of the porous enamel. Lastly, the excess resin was removed with gentle air spray and flossed and then light-cured for 40 s using a light-curing device (Acteon® Mini LED™, Acteon Group, Mérignac, France). Subsequently, a second application of the infiltrant was performed, allowing 1 min for the material to infiltrate the enamel pores. This was followed by an additional 40-s light-curing phase to complete polymerization. Upon completion of the procedure, the floss ligatures and rubber dam were carefully removed. The final surface finishing was performed using fine-grit polishing burs.

Vanish XT varnish protocol

Vanish™ XT Extended Contact Varnish (3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) was applied following the manufacturer’s instructions. Acid etching was performed using 35% phosphoric acid (Scotchbond Universal Etchant, 3 M ESPE, USA) for 15 s, followed by thorough rinsing with water and the excess pooled water was gently removed by air drying. The two-component Vanish XT material (liquid and paste) was dispensed onto a mixing pad and blended using a small spatula for 10–15 s until a smooth, homogenous, and glossy consistency was achieved. The working time of the mixed material was approximately 2 min and 30 s. The material was applied to the tooth surface in a uniform thin layer, not exceeding 0.5 mm in thickness, using a modelling brush applicator and polymerized using a light-curing unit for 20s (Acteon® Mini LED™, Acteon Group, Mérignac, France). One coat was applied, as recommended by the manufacturer. Finally, a moistened cotton applicator was used to gently wipe the coated surface to remove the superficial film residue.

Statistical analysis

Data collection, tabulation, and statistical analysis were conducted using SPSS program version 25.5 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The significance level was set at a P-value of 0.05. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to present outcomes and independent variables. Comparative analyses between the two groups were performed using chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, and independent samples t-test. Intraclass correlation coefficient was calculated to assess intra- and inter-examiner reliability. Because multiple lesions could occur within the same participant, the WSL was considered the unit of analysis for statistical comparisons.

Although the WSL was considered the unit of analysis, multiple lesions could be present within the same participant, which may introduce intra-subject correlation. This potential clustering effect was considered during interpretation of the results and is acknowledged as a limitation of the study.

Analysis of teeth characteristics

A total of 138 teeth were evaluated across both treatment groups. The teeth were categorized based on the type of WSL, arch type, and tooth type. Out of the total lesions, 113 (81.9%) were developmental and 25 (18.1%) were environmental. This distribution showed a statistically significant difference between the lesion types (P = 0.000).

Error assessment

The L*a*b* values of one WSL each in the upper and lower arch of one patient were recorded in vivo three times on three consecutive days. The ranges of variances due to measurement error for L value were between 0.1 and 0.30 units for the WSL on the maxillary tooth and 0.20 units for the WSL on the mandibular tooth. This indicates precision in measurements recorded with the spectrophotometer as conducted by other studies (Kannan and Padmanabhan 2019; Sluzker et al. 2011). All measurements were carried out under normalized conditions to ensure their accuracy and reproducibility (Table 1S).

Intergroup comparison

Table 2S outlines the differences in the lightness (L*) values between group I (RI) and group II (Vanish XT) across three time intervals. Intergroup analysis demonstrated no significant difference in L* values between the two groups at baseline. Following treatment, the RI group showed significantly higher L* values than the Vanish XT group at both evaluation time points, indicating greater improvement in enamel brightness. No significant difference was observed between the groups for the adjacent sound enamel (P = 0.448), confirming that both groups started from a comparable baseline and that the observed differences were treatment-related.

Intragroup comparison

Intragroup analysis revealed that group I (RI) demonstrated a statistically significant increase in the L* values from T0 to T1 (P = 0.003). However, because the T1 measurement was obtained immediately after treatment under rubber dam isolation, part of this increase may be influenced by transient enamel dehydration caused by tooth drying. Although the follow-up measurements revealed changes from (T1-T2) and (T0-T2), they were not statistically significant (P = 0.055 and 0.08, respectively). In contrast, group II (Vanish XT) demonstrated no statistically significant changes in the L* values across any of the evaluated time intervals (T0-T1: P = 0.490; T1-T2, P = 0.322; T0-T2 P = 0.851).

Comparative analysis of red-green and yellow-blue color parameters

Following treatment and at follow-up, group I (RI) demonstrated significantly better correction along the red-green (a*) axis, compared with group II (Vanish XT) (P < 0.05), reflecting superior esthetic improvement in terms of reducing red and green discolorations. A shift toward 0 on the a* axis is considered better esthetically because it removes unnatural hues, such as the ones present in WSLs (Gómez et al. 2018; Youssef et al. 2020). In contrast, no significant differences were found between the groups at any time point along the yellow-blue (b*) axis (P > 0.05), suggesting that both treatments produced similar outcomes in terms of yellow or blue color components (Table 3S).

Analysis of overall color difference (ΔE) between the groups

Two distinct ΔE analyses were performed in the present study: (1) comparison between WSLs and adjacent sound enamel, and (2) assessment of color changes across the follow-up intervals (T0-T1, T1-T2, and T0-T2). These analyses are presented and interpreted separately below.

Comparison of WSLs color changes and sound enamel

ΔE values were calculated individually for each lesion using the corresponding L*, a*, and b* coordinates at the specified comparison time points, after which the resulting ΔE values were averaged within each group. Therefore, the mean ΔE values discussed in this section cannot be directly derived from the mean L*, a*, and b* values reported in Tables 2S and 3S.

At baseline (sound-T0), both treatment groups demonstrated comparable levels of color mismatch relative to adjacent sound enamel (Group I (RI): ΔE = 7.84; Group II (Vanish XT): ΔE = 8.33; P = 0.5524), confirming similar baseline conditions. Both values were well above the clinical perceptibility threshold (ΔE = 3.7), indicating that the WSLs were highly visible and clinically distinguishable from the surrounding sound enamel prior to treatment.

Following treatment, group I (RI) demonstrated significantly greater color correction than group II (Vanish XT) at both the immediate post-treatment and follow-up assessments. At T1, group I showed a ΔE value of 2.34 compared with 6.35 in group II (P = 0.0493). Although the mean ΔE value in group I fell below the clinical perceptibility threshold at T1, this measurement was obtained under rubber dam isolation and may have been partially influenced by transient enamel dehydration. At T2, group I demonstrated a ΔE value of 4.69 compared with 8.50 in group II (P = 0.0002). Despite the increase in ΔE values at follow-up, the RI group continued to demonstrate greater color improvement than the Vanish XT group.

Color changes of WSLs at different time points

Although group I (RI) exhibited a greater mean color change than group II (Vanish XT) between baseline and immediately post-treatment (T0-T1), the difference did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05). Nevertheless, the magnitude of change in group I exceeded the clinical perceptibility threshold (ΔE = 3.7), indicating that the improvement may still be visually perceptible in a clinical setting. In contrast, the mean ΔE value in group II remained below this threshold, suggesting that the color improvement achieved during the T0-T1 interval was not clinically perceptible and therefore not visually distinguishable from the original WSL.

Both groups demonstrated similar color stability between T1 and T2, with no significant intergroup differences observed during the follow-up period (P = 0.9738). Although the overall long-term color change from baseline to follow-up was greater in group I than in group II, this difference also did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05). Overall, while both treatments resulted in color improvement, the intergroup differences in interval-based ΔE changes were not statistically significant (Table 3).

Esthetic changes and surface change

Intra-examiner reliability demonstrated a highly significant correlation, with intraclass correlation coefficients ranging from 0.90 to 0.98. Similarly, inter-examiner reliability demonstrated a highly significant correlation, with intraclass correlation coefficients ranging from 0.90 to 0.93.

Subjective esthetic assessment using VAS scores demonstrated consistently higher ratings for the RI group compared with the Vanish XT group at both evaluation time points, reflecting greater perceived improvement in lesion appearance and surface characteristics. For EC, group I achieved mean scores of 4.71 ± 1.73 at T1 and 5.06 ± 1.90 at T2, whereas group II recorded lower means of 3.55 ± 2.17 and 3.82 ± 2.39, respectively. These differences were statistically significant at both time points (T1: P = 0.0004; T2: P = 0.0006). Similarly, for SC, group I reported mean scores of 4.60 ± 1.73 at T1 and 4.95 ± 1.95 at T2, compared with 3.54 ± 2.11 and 3.85 ± 2.32 in group II. These differences were also statistically significant (T1: P = 0.0010; T2: P = 0.0009), indicating superior performance of the RI group in both esthetic and surface evaluations (Table 4).

Given that multiple lesions were analyzed per participant, these findings should be interpreted with consideration of potential intra-subject correlation. While each lesion was treated as an independent unit of analysis, clustering within participants may have influenced variance estimates and the precision of the observed effects, and may therefore affect the generalizability of the findings.

Discussion

The present study compared the esthetic efficacy of two minimally invasive approaches, Icon® and Vanish™ XT, in managing WSLs in permanent anterior teeth. RI demonstrated greater improvement in both objective (CIE L*a*b* color analysis) and subjective (VAS) measures compared with Vanish XT within the evaluated time frame; however, these findings should be interpreted in the context of the study design and short follow-up duration. Specifically, the ΔE and L* values in the RI group showed perceptible improvement that was generally maintained over the short-term follow-up period, reflecting improved masking performance. However, the relatively short follow-up period limits conclusions regarding long-term color stability. Therefore, the null hypothesis stating that no difference exists between the two treatment modalities was rejected.

WSLs are characterized by increased opacity and loss of normal translucency of the enamel, owing to absorption and light scattering (at the roughened surface and within the porous lesion), which is the main esthetic problem (Youssef et al. 2020). The reduced L* values observed in WSLs can be explained by the optical properties of demineralized enamel (Kannan and Padmanabhan 2019). The difference in the refractive index between healthy enamel and porous crystals within the lesion leads to increased scattering and absorption of light, resulting in the characteristic whitish appearance of WSLs (Benson 2008). This light-scattering phenomenon occurs primarily within the microporosities of the lesion body, limiting light transmission and contributing to a reduction in perceived brightness (Darling et al. 2006; Fondriest 2003; Ko et al. 2000). Therefore, changes in the L* values are particularly significant as they provide a quantifiable measure of the visual impact of WSLs and the effectiveness of interventions aimed at masking or reversing them.

The superior improvement in the L* values observed with RI compared with Vanish™ XT may be attributed to the fundamental differences in their mechanisms of action and material properties. RI works by deeply penetrating the porous enamel lesion with a low-viscosity resin, which replaces air within the lesion body (Dziaruddin and Zakaria 2022). It can improve color, even in deeper lesions, because it penetrates deeper lesions and the effect appears immediately after treatment (Meyer-Lueckel and Paris 2008). This process alters the refractive index of the lesion, making it more similar to that of sound enamel (approximately 1.52), thereby masking the white opaque appearance caused by subsurface demineralization (Paris et al. 2013). This results in an immediate and perceptible improvement in brightness and translucency that mimics healthy enamel (Saccucci et al. 2022; Zaazou et al. 2024). The immediate improvement observed following treatment should be interpreted cautiously, as dehydration of enamel during isolation may temporarily enhance lesion masking. Optical blending leads to immediate and visually perceptible esthetic improvement (Mosallam et al. 2018).

Vanish™ XT is a resin-modified glass ionomer varnish designed primarily to release fluoride, calcium, and phosphate ions and promote surface-level remineralization (ESPE 2016). Upon application, fluoride is immediately released from the surface of the material, whereas additional fluoride embedded within the matrix acts as a reservoir, enabling sustained release over time (Reddy et al. 2019). Although it enhances the mineral content of enamel over time, its penetration depth is limited and it lacks the ability to significantly alter the refractive properties of the lesion (Duminis et al. 2018; Edunoori et al. 2022; Virupaxi et al. 2016). Consequently, Vanish XT may improve enamel integrity and provide preventive benefits, but its esthetic impact is minimal compared with the deeper and more durable masking effect of RI (Abang et al. 2023). These differences in light-scattering behavior and depth of action likely account for the more pronounced increase in the L* values observed in group I (RI).

Intragroup comparisons revealed that group I (RI) showed a statistically significant increase in the L* values immediately post-treatment. This aligns with findings from previous clinical studies that demonstrated that RI can rapidly improve the esthetic appearance of WSLs (Çiftçi et al. 2018; Gençer and Kirzioğlu 2019; Kannan and Padmanabhan 2019). Although subsequent changes between T1-T2 and T0-T2 did not reach statistical significance, the sustained elevation in the L* values suggested that the treatment effect was maintained over the follow-up period. This finding is consistent with results reported by Gholami et al., who observed that the masking effect of RI remained stable over time, without evidence of relapse (Gholami et al. 2023). These findings carry important clinical implications. The immediate and durable esthetic improvement observed following a single application of RI suggests it may be an effective and efficient approach for managing WSLs, particularly when rapid cosmetic results are desired. This interpretation is further supported by evidence from recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses identifying RI as the most effective modality for improving the esthetic appearance of WSLs, particularly in anterior teeth where optical blending plays a critical role in patient-perceived esthetic outcomes (Mistry and Rodriguez 2024; Puleio et al. 2021).

In contrast, group II (Vanish XT) did not exhibit statistically significant intragroup changes in the L* values across any of the measured intervals, reflecting limited esthetic efficacy. This outcome is consistent with prior research, which has shown that while fluoride-releasing varnishes can support enamel remineralization, they typically do not produce clinically noticeable improvements in lesion appearance (Rohym et al. 2020). Therefore, while both materials have a role in WSL management, the improved optical performance and sustained effect observed with RI suggest it may be a suitable option, particularly when short-term esthetic improvement is a primary clinical objective. However, fluoride-releasing varnishes such as Vanish XT may still play a valuable role in clinical practice, particularly in preventive strategies and in cases where esthetic demands are secondary, as these materials primarily support enamel remineralization and the preventive management of early enamel lesions. Nevertheless, Kannan and Padmanabhan reported a significant improvement in enamel lightness following Clinpro XT application both immediately after treatment and at later follow-up intervals (Kannan and Padmanabhan 2019).

The present study demonstrated a marked difference in the esthetic performance of RI and Vanish XT in masking WSLs. At baseline, both treatment groups exhibited comparable color mismatches relative to adjacent sound enamel, with ΔE values exceeding the clinical perceptibility threshold of 3.7, confirming the clear visual detectability of WSLs prior to intervention. Following treatment, group I (RI) showed a significant improvement in color matching, with ΔE values decreasing toward the clinical perceptibility threshold, indicating improved masking of WSLs. These findings are consistent with those of a randomized clinical trial that evaluated ΔE values following treatment with RI and other remineralizing agents, including fluoridated hydroxyapatite and herbal toothpaste (Youssef et al. 2020). In the current study, a slight increase in ΔE values was observed at follow-up, indicating some loss of the initial masking effect. Although some differences in ΔE values between the treatment groups did not reach statistical significance, the magnitude of change in certain cases approached or exceeded the commonly accepted clinical perceptibility threshold (ΔE = 3.7), suggesting that these changes may still be visually detectable to patients or clinicians in a clinical setting. This observation highlights the importance of considering both statistical significance and clinical relevance when interpreting esthetic outcomes. The increase in ΔE values over time may be attributed to extrinsic staining, which has been reported as a potential limitation of resin-infiltrated surfaces, particularly in individuals who frequently consume stain-forming foods and beverages (Leland et al. 2016). Polishing or bleaching of the infiltrated area can be performed (Saccucci et al. 2022). Studies have shown that polishing infiltrated lesions increases their resistance to staining, thereby minimizing the staining effect (Araújo et al. 2015; Paris et al. 2013). This also applies to group II (Vanish XT), where a slight increase was observed in the ΔE value at follow-up. This can also be explained by the influence of dietary pigments and low-pH media, such as coffee (Chen et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2023), therefore disagreeing with the study that concluded that Clinpro XT offered protection against tooth discoloration (Xu et al. 2023).

Group II (Vanish XT) maintained a visible mismatch across all evaluated time points, reflecting minimal color correction. These results are consistent with the findings of El-Monem who compared RI with ProSeal, a surface sealant (El-Monem, 2021). The study also began with similar baseline conditions, but over time, the Icon® group achieved and maintained a significantly better color match, whereas ProSeal-treated lesions remained visibly different This further supports the idea that RI has a stronger and longer-lasting esthetic impact. Compared with the study by Kannan et al., who reported that RI produced a significantly greater immediate reduction in color discrepancy, compared with Clinpro XT varnish, likely because of its ability to modify the optical properties of WSLs (Kannan and Padmanabhan 2019). However, at later follow-up intervals, Clinpro XT varnish demonstrated superior improvements, suggesting a more gradual but sustained esthetic effect (Kannan and Padmanabhan 2019).

This randomized controlled design ensured unbiased allocation and minimized selection bias. Moreover, the inclusion of controlled comparison groups allowed a direct evaluation between RI and Vanish XT, providing clinically relevant insights into two commonly used yet distinct treatment modalities. Furthermore, the study was sufficiently powered to detect significant differences owing to its adequate sample size. In addition, the low dropout rate enhanced the reliability and internal validity of the findings. Moreover, the follow-up period facilitated the assessment of both immediate and short-term treatment stability, strengthening the robustness of the results. Finally, the use of a multimodal (triangulated) assessment approach combining objective spectrophotometric color measurements, subjective VAS ratings, and standardized digital and cross-polarized photography further improved the validity and depth of the esthetic evaluation. Despite the strengths of the present study, certain limitations should be acknowledged. One limitation of the present study is the relatively short follow-up period, which may not fully capture the long-term color stability of the treated lesions. Over time, resin-infiltrated surfaces may be susceptible to extrinsic staining, particularly in individuals who frequently consume pigmented foods and beverages. Therefore, longer follow-up periods are required to better evaluate the durability of the esthetic outcomes achieved with these treatment modalities. Another limitation relates to the analytical approach, in which individual lesions were treated as independent observations despite potential clustering within participants. This may have introduced intra-subject correlation, potentially affecting variance estimation and confidence in effect size precision. Consequently, the reported findings should be interpreted with caution in terms of generalizability. Future studies employing hierarchical or mixed-effects models are recommended to more appropriately account for within-participant clustering. Some children with MIH experienced hypersensitivity during air drying or water rinsing, necessitating adjustments in isolation techniques and treatment delivery. Moreover, achieving proper isolation was particularly challenging and time-consuming in partially erupted teeth, despite careful efforts to maintain moisture control. In addition, cervical lesions posed further difficulties in achieving adequate isolation, potentially influencing material adaptation and procedural efficiency. Collectively, these clinical challenges may have introduced minor procedural variability, although standardization efforts were made to minimize their impact on the study outcomes. Future research should include longer-term randomized clinical trials to assess the stability of esthetic improvements over time. Additionally, incorporating patient-reported outcome measures, such as oral health-related quality of life and patient satisfaction, would provide valuable insight into the broader clinical impact of these treatment approaches.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, resin infiltration demonstrated improved short-term esthetic outcomes compared with Vanish™ XT in the management of WSLs. These findings should be interpreted within the context of the short follow-up duration and lesion-level analytical approach. Although RI achieved greater immediate color improvement, interpretation of post-treatment measurements should consider the potential influence of enamel dehydration. Improvements were maintained over the 12-week follow-up; however, complete masking below the clinical perceptibility threshold was not consistently sustained. Clinically, resin infiltration represents an effective minimally invasive option for esthetic enhancement, while fluoride-releasing varnishes may remain valuable in preventive strategies. Longer-term studies are required to confirm the durability of these outcomes.
