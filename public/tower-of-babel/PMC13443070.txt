Burst deep brain stimulation for Parkinson's disease: A systematic review of efficacy and safety
Authors: Martijn Hendriks, Matej Lokar, Hisse Arnts, Rob MC Mestrom, Dejan Georgiev, R Saman Vinke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurst deep brain stimulation (DBS) is a promising alternative to conventional deep brain stimulation (cDBS), incorporating paradigms such as theta burst stimulation (TBS), burst cycling (BC), and coordinated reset (CR). Findings from transcranial magnetic stimulation, spinal cord stimulation and focused ultrasound neuromodulation inspire its potential application in Parkinson's disease (PD).ObjectiveThis review evaluates the evidence on the efficacy and safety of burst DBS in PD.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science. Inclusion criteria encompassed peer-reviewed primary preclinical or clinical studies in English, reporting motor outcomes and burst stimulation parameters. Methodological quality was assessed using SYRCLE and a modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Data extraction was performed systematically, and findings were synthesized narratively.Results19 studies met inclusion criteria: nine preclinical and ten clinical studies. Eight focused on BC (three including safety), four examined TBS (two including safety), and seven addressed CR (one including safety). Burst DBS showed mostly similar acute efficacy to cDBS, with distinct post-stimulation effects. No major adverse events were reported. However, stimulation settings varied widely, with no consensus on optimal parameters.ConclusionEfficacy and safety of burst DBS appear to be comparable to cDBS, with potential benefits such as post-stimulation effects. However, the risk of bias, variability in stimulation settings, inconsistent terminology, and other methodological considerations limit the interpretation of previous research. Further randomized studies are crucial to better refine the stimulation parameters and establish standardized clinical protocols that can be used to compare burst DBS with cDBS.Keywords: deep brain stimulation, Parkinson's disease, burst, theta burst, burst cycling, coordinated reset

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder with an increasing incidence. 1 PD is characterized by hallmark motor symptoms, including bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, and postural instability. 2 In advanced PD, when levodopa-induced dyskinesias, wearing-off and other complications emerge, deep brain stimulation (DBS) serves as an effective therapeutic option. 3 Efforts to optimize DBS have explored various strategies, broadly categorized as: (1) modification of the stimulation target or site, including directional stimulation, and (2) modification of stimulation parameters and paradigms. 4 As a result of ongoing optimization efforts, burst stimulation has emerged as a promising DBS approach to enhance therapeutic efficacy.

Unlike conventional DBS (cDBS), which is characterized by continuous stimulation, burst stimulation consists of trains of electrical pulses interspersed with quiescent intervals. Each active stimulation period is known as the burst interval, while the non-stimulation phase is referred to as the pause interval. Within each burst interval, pulses are delivered at a defined intraburst frequency, whereas the interburst frequency determines the rate at which burst intervals occur (Figure 1). These combined intervals can be delivered continuously (most burst paradigms) or intermittently (intermittent burst DBS). Unlike adaptive DBS, which continuously adjusts stimulation based on neural feedback, or variable frequency stimulation, which continuously changes pulse timing, burst stimulation delivers pulses at structured burst and pause intervals with specific intraburst frequency.5,6

Different burst patterns. An overview of the different burst patterns described in the literature is presented. Burst interval represents the period of stimulation of a single burst and pause interval represents the period of quiescence in between bursts. Intraburst frequency is defined as the frequency of stimulation pulses within a single burst and interburst frequency presents the repetition frequency of bursts. In coordinated reset stimulation shuffle time is the period after which the order of stimulated contacts is changed. BI: burst interval; PI: pause interval; intraF: intraburst frequency; interF: interburst frequency; cDBS: conventional, continuous deep brain stimulation; TBS: theta burst stimulation; BC: burst cycling stimulation; iBS: intermittent burst stimulation; C0/1/2: stimulation contacts; CR: coordinated reset; sCR: shuffled coordinated reset.

Burst stimulation has been widely studied in transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and spinal cord stimulation (SCS), and more recently implemented in focused ultrasound (FUS) studies.7–10 Evolving from repetitive TMS, theta burst stimulation (TBS) was introduced in 2005, delivering bursts at 50 Hz intraburst and 5 Hz interburst frequencies within the theta range (4–7 Hz). Applied continuously for 20 s or intermittently with 2 s stimulation and 8 s pauses,8,11 it was inspired by hippocampal studies linking theta-range burst discharges to learning8,12 and plasticity induction.13,14 Evidence suggests TBS TMS induces lasting neuroplastic changes. 8 Meanwhile, burst SCS, introduced in 2010, has been characterized by bursts at 500 Hz intraburst and 40 Hz interburst frequency. 7 It enhances cortical activation in areas involved in pain processing and may induce neuroplastic changes in nociceptive networks, thereby increasing neuropathic pain suppression.15–17 In addition, TBS paradigms have been adapted from TMS and implemented in burst-based FUS neuromodulation, including in patients with PD. 9 FUS burst stimulation has been associated with plasticity and metaplasticity effects. 10 These converging findings across modalities have motivated the proposals to incorporate burst stimulation principles in DBS.

Several distinct forms of burst DBS exist, including burst cycling (BC), TBS, and coordinated reset (CR) (Figure 1). TBS delivers bursts with interburst frequencies within the theta range. BC usually involves switching the device ON and OFF to introduce stimulation gaps due to technical limitations. 18 Some TBS paradigms also incorporate this approach,19,20 but BC is defined here as burst DBS with interburst frequencies beyond the theta range. CR is characterized by sequential bursts of high-frequency stimulation applied across different electrode contacts.21,22 A modified version, shuffled CR (sCR), periodically alters the sequence of electrode activation. 23 Burst DBS may serve as a promising alternative to cDBS, potentially leading to less side effects, lower current usage, and prolonged post-stimulation effects.24–26

Despite this promising evidence, a comprehensive overview of burst DBS in PD is lacking. This systematic review aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of burst DBS, involving both PD patients and animal models. Animal studies offer mechanistic insights that support translation to human applications.

Methods

The systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines (Supplementary Material). 27 This review was not registered, and no protocol was established beforehand.

Search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase and Web of Science databases up until 29.9.2025, using a comprehensive search strategy (Supplementary Material) that was developed in consultation with a medical librarian. It consisted of the following search terms and their synonyms: Parkinson's disease, deep brain stimulation, subthalamic nucleus, globus pallidus internus, ventral intermediate nucleus, burst, coordinated reset and cycling.

Study selection

The following inclusion criteria were used: PD or Parkinsonian, burst DBS, English, peer reviewed primary studies, preclinical or clinical studies, motor outcome (i.e., any motor related outcome parameter, such as Movement Disorders Society Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale [MDS-UPDRS] or movement time) and burst stimulation information (i.e., any burst stimulation parameter, such as intraburst frequency). All articles were screened independently by two researchers (MH and ML), any conflicts were discussed and resolved.

Data extraction

Data was extracted independently by the two researchers based on a predetermined results table. Data was compared for differences; any differences were resolved by mutual agreement. The following parameters were included: reference, publication year, country, study design, population type, number of participants, inclusion criteria, DBS system, stimulation effect measured in the ON or OFF medication state (ON-MED or OFF-MED) and stimulation ON or OFF state (STIM-ON or STIM-OFF), DBS target, unilateral or bilateral stimulation, stimulation type, time of stimulation, stimulation settings, efficacy outcomes and type, adverse events with follow-up time.

Quality assessment

Quality assessment was performed independently by two researchers by using SYRCLE's risk of bias (RoB) tool for animal studies and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) RoB tool for non-randomised clinical studies.28,29 The NOS was modified to better evaluate methodological quality given the specific study designs (Supplementary Material). Formal assessment of RoB from missing results (reporting bias) and certainty of evidence (e.g., using GRADE) were not performed.

Synthesis methods

Due to few clinical studies and both heterogeneous and small sample sizes in human and animal research, no meta-analysis was conducted. All studies were included in a narrative synthesis, grouped by population (animal or human) and burst stimulation type. Effect measures varied across studies and were summarized individually. No data conversions or imputations were performed; all reported effect measures and outcomes were extracted as presented in the original studies. Given the limited data and heterogeneity, no subgroup or sensitivity analyses were conducted. This paper provides a narrative summary of burst DBS in PD, supported by tables highlighting key findings.

Results

Search results

Initially, 1851 records were retrieved. Subsequently, 824 duplicates were excluded, 1027 articles screened and 983 excluded at title/abstract stage. 44 articles were assessed by full text screening. Finally, 19 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in this review (Figure 2).

Quality assessment results

The quality assessment of included studies using SYRCLE and NOS RoB tools is shown in Figures 3 and 4, respectively. Nine studies were conducted in animals, of which three investigated BC, three CR, two sCR and one both CR and sCR.23,24,30–36 Most employed within-subject designs, with stimulation applied mostly unilaterally to the subthalamic nucleus (STN) and globus pallidus internus (GPi) or its rodent homolog, the entopeduncular nucleus (EPN), for several hours per day across multiple days. Assessments were performed in OFF-MED state, with STIM-ON or STIM-OFF.

Ten studies were conducted in humans, including three on BC, four on TBS, two on both BC and TBS, and one on CR.19,20,25,26,37,39–43 Clinical study designs varied and included case series, randomized controlled trials, and within-subject or crossover designs. Stimulation was applied to the GPi, STN, posterior subthalamic area, or ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM), either unilaterally or bilaterally, for durations ranging from minutes to days. Assessments were primarily conducted in the OFF-MED state with STIM-ON or STIM-OFF.

Animal

Three studies evaluated the efficacy of BC, reporting similar acute improvement to cDBS, but with post-stimulation effects (changes in motor symptoms after stimulation cessation).30–32 The first study examining BC efficacy on a single parkinsonian non-human primate (NHP) revealed that BC improved movement time (3.94 s) compared to STIM-OFF (6.10 s) and cDBS at 80 Hz (4.72 s), but showed similar improvement to cDBS at 135 Hz (3.97 s). 31 Another similar study showed acute motor improvement compared to STIM-OFF, with largest differences in motor scores observed when stimulating the substantia nigra pars reticulata-STN border, ventral STN and region mediodorsal to GPi. 32 Additionally, in a mouse model, BC significantly and cDBS nearly significantly improved velocity (BC: 0.5 to 1.0 cm/s; cDBS: 0.5 to 0.9 cm/s) and immobility (BC: 82% to 48%; cDBS: 75% to 51%). However, only BC showed post-stimulation effects, with velocity trending higher (1.0 to 1.8 cm/s) and immobility stable (48% to 45%), whereas cDBS worsened (velocity: 0.9 to 0.4 cm/s; immobility: 51% to 87%). Return to baseline was longer for BC (124 vs. 37.5 min). The most effective burst setting was a 175 Hz intraburst frequency with a 200 ms burst interval and 800 ms pause interval. 30

Four studies evaluated non-shuffled CR efficacy,23,24,33,34 reporting motor improvement comparable to cDBS, but longer post-stimulation effects. In the first study, low-intensity CR improved akinesia post-stimulation, confirmed by a 2-point reduction in video disability scores, with effects lasting 35 days. In contrast, cDBS improved akinesia only within the first 30 min but had no effect afterwards. 24 Another study confirmed that CR led to immediate post-stimulation movement time improvement similar to cDBS, but with post-stimulation effects lasting up to a week after stimulation ceased. 33 A CR study in two NHPs examined motor effects of CR duration (2 h vs. 4 h) during stimulation and at 90 min post-stimulation, next-day post-stimulation (30 min before next stimulation), and extended post-stimulation. During stimulation, improvement was statistically significant for 2 h CR (modified UPDRS for NHPs [mUPDRS] improvement for each animal 81.1%, 5.7%), 4 h CR (23.6%, 45.7%), and cDBS (34.0%, 32.9%), though scores varied more between animals for 2 h CR. 90 min post-stimulation improvement was significant for 4 h CR in both animals (21.9%, 51.4%) and for 2 h CR in the second animal (72.2%), but absent in cDBS. Similarly, next-day post-stimulation effects were observed for 4 h CR in both animals (23.1%, 44.8%) and for 2 h CR in the second animal (41.1%), whereas cDBS showed none. This trend continued with extended post-treatment effects lasting for days. 34

Three studies evaluated the efficacy of sCR, reporting motor improvement with post-stimulation benefits.23,35,36 Comparing sCR and non-shuffled CR in two NHPs, sCR resulted in greater 60 min post-stimulation mUPDRS improvement from baseline (shuffled vs. non-shuffled, animal one: 21% vs. −12–4%; animal two: ∼30% vs. ∼20%) and next-day (morning after stimulation day) post-stimulation improvement (peak animal one: 17,5%; animal two: 33%/36% [optimized settings]), while cDBS did not. During stimulation, effects of sCR were similar to cDBS, but optimized interburst frequency (27 Hz) and stimulation intensity (0.16 mA) led to greater motor improvement with sCR in one animal on the last stimulation day. 23 Another study examined post-stimulation motor effects (measurements between sessions) with sCR in three animals, with one animal undergoing a comparison between 21 Hz and 27 Hz interburst frequency. Post-stimulation improvement (peak mUPDRS improvement from baseline in animal one: 29.4%, animal two: >50%, animal three 21Hz: 25.6%, 27Hz: 32%) and swing speed improvement were observed in all animals, both lasting several days after stimulation. Specifically, 27 Hz led to more motor improvement, while 21 Hz led to swing speed and stride length improvement. 35 The effects of sCR at different interburst frequencies (27 Hz, 24 Hz, 21 Hz, and 15 Hz) is further supported by a study showing significant immediate post-stimulation improvement, which accumulated over the five stimulation days, and next-day post-stimulation motor improvement (mUDPRS improvement not reported). 36

Human

Five studies evaluated the efficacy of BC, resulting in mixed results.26,37–40 In the first clinical trial BC movement times for a wrist flexion/extension task were longer than cDBS (0.13 s). 40 Another clinical study found no significant differences in gait measures or motor scores between BC (4, 10 and 15 Hz interburst frequencies), cDBS and STIM-OFF. Notably, compared to cDBS, BC reduced battery consumption by 53–88%. 26 However, a different study found motor improvement in three patients with DBS-resistant tremor symptoms, without tremor rebound at a 1-year follow-up. 38 Others found that BC improved motor symptoms compared to STIM-OFF (MDS-UPDRS III BC 31.2 vs. STIM-OFF 39.8) but showed no differences from cDBS (BC 31.2 vs. cDBS 31.0). 37 Finally, in a case series, one patient tolerated BC for a year with slight worsening before reverting to cDBS, while the other unenrolled due to tremor. 39

Four TBS studies reported efficacy,19,20,41,42 with most showing at least some motor improvement similar to cDBS.19,20,41 In an initial case series with patients refractory to cDBS, only three showed motor improvement, with two experiencing persistent gait festination improvements. 42 A later clinical trial by Horn et al. 19 found that 50 Hz intraburst frequency with adaptive intensity (MDS-UPDRS III 12.3) and 200 Hz intraburst frequency TBS (12.5) improved motor symptoms compared to STIM-OFF (16.4), while 50 Hz TBS without adaptive intensity did not (14.5), but all differed from cDBS (10.5). Specifically, rigidity score improved with all burst settings similar to cDBS, while akinesia score improved only with 50 Hz adaptive intensity and tremor score only with 200 Hz TBS, both similar to cDBS. Conversely, on a standardized tapping test, akinesia showed the greatest improvement with 200 Hz and cDBS. Notably, therapeutic window widened with 50 Hz TBS but required a higher amplitude for full rigidity resolution and was not achieved in all patients. No lasting motor improvements were seen 30 min post-stimulation. Another clinical trial comparing bilateral TBS with Horn et al.'s unilateral study, found similar motor improvement with bilateral TBS (relative change of lateralized MDS-UPDRS III, 26.1%) and cDBS (29.9%), while unilateral cDBS (31.6%) improved motor scores more than unilateral TBS (16.0%). In addition, when compared to unilateral TBS, bilateral TBS significantly improved lower limb rigidity and showed a trend toward better full motor score. 20 The latest trial revealed greater effect of cDBS in a special Freezing of gait (FOG)-COURSE task (6.3) compared to TBS (11.1) and equal motor score effect of cDBS (MDS-UPDRS III 28.1) and TBS (33.9). Further analysis in a subgroup of patients who subjectively preferred TBS revealed equal improvement of gait and motor scores with TBS (FoG-COURSE 4.1, MDS-UPDRS III 27.4) compared to cDBS (3.9, 27.9). The TBS preference could be predicted by the MDS-UPDRS II scores. 41 Additionally, two of the BC studies also investigated stimulation with interburst frequency within TBS range.26,40 The first study found TBS inferior to cDBS. Simultaneously, it also showed a trend toward shorter movement times with lower interburst frequency 1 Hz (outside theta range) when compared to 5 Hz (movement time improvement by 0.08 s). 40 Conversely, another study found theta-range stimulation as effective as cDBS and BC in alleviating motor and gait abnormalities and noted a trend toward better gait metrics with lower interburst frequency (4 Hz vs. 10 Hz/15 Hz). 26 The only clinical CR study investigated motor effects at immediate post-stimulation and next-day post-stimulation (morning after stimulation day). Peak improvement occurred on day three, with a mean MDS-UPDRS III reduction from baseline of 24% at next-day post-stimulation and 58% immediate post-stimulation. Immediate post-stimulation tremor improved in four out of six patients (60% reduction), while rigidity and bradykinesia improved in all (63% reduction). Overall, motor effects assessed at immediate post-stimulation across the three days were significant, averaging a 27% reduction. 25

Safety

Safety outcomes were reported in six studies, as summarized in Table 3.

Three BC studies assessed safety, reporting no major, nor persistent adverse events.26,37,40 In one study, two patients needed a lower voltage, because of a lower threshold for side effects, such as paresthesia. 40 Others observed during each BC setting transient paresthesia in two patients, worsening of balance in one and, specifically with 10 Hz interburst frequency, transient concentration change in one. 26 Another study reported tongue rolling in one patient and facial vibration in another, both alleviated by stimulation adjustments. 37

Two TBS studies assessed safety, reporting no major persistent adverse events.19,20 In one study, one patient endured severe akinesia and could not tolerate 50 Hz TBS with adaptive intensity adjustment. 19 Furthermore, there was no difference in adverse events between unilateral and bilateral TBS. 20

One non-shuffled CR study assessed safety, reporting no adverse events. 25

Discussion

The findings from this review confirm that burst DBS could potentially provide motor improvements comparable to cDBS, while producing additional sustained post-stimulation effects. Furthermore, reported adverse events were infrequent and mostly transient, but systematic comparisons with cDBS were scarce. Hence, these results suggest that burst DBS has the potential for efficacy and an acceptable preliminary safety profile in PD, warranting further investigation.

Although most of the included studies showed outcomes similar to cDBS, some studies reported inferior outcomes.19,40–42 Few studies were underpowered40,42 and the case series by Sáenz-Farret et al. 42 only included patients with refractory axial symptoms, possibly leading to sampling bias. Moreover, low intraburst frequency (50 Hz) in the case series 42 along with insufficient increase in amplitude might explaining the negative results.19,44 Additionally, study by Horn et al. 19 reported improvement comparable to cDBS only in specific subsections of MDS-UPDRS III. Lastly, Dutke et al. 41 showed that TBS produces similar improvement to cDBS in a subgroup of patients who preferred TBS. Inconsistent patient selection might therefore explain the variability in the reported results.

Burst DBS is hypothesised to induce neuroplastic changes.19,20 The mechanisms of targeted neuroplasticity were previously studied in the field of TBS TMS, which is known to produce stimulation outlasting effects, likely mediated by N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors, Ca2+ signalling, gamma-aminobutyric acid receptor, growth factors and gene expression.11,45 While these findings cannot be directly translated to DBS, some of the preclinical studies have shown DBS produces sustained stimulation effects.24,25,30,34,36 One of the mechanisms behind the proposed inhibitory effect of DBS, referred to as inhibitory postsynaptic potentiation, was shown to persist even after cessation of stimulation. 46 Additionally, in mice, optogenetic burst stimulation of the external globus pallidus selectively excited some neurons while inhibiting others. Applying these findings through burst DBS resulted in therapeutic effects extending beyond the period of stimulation. 30 The described effects lay foundation for the possibility of neuroplastic changes in DBS which could lead to sustained post-stimulation effects. While such effects have been demonstrated for CR,24,25,34,36 they have been scarcely described for other types of burst stimulation. 30

Most of the studies on CR showed motor improvement and post-stimulation effects.23,24,33–36 CR is proposed to reset the firing phase of several neural populations adjacent to each contact, thus leading to persistent beta oscillatory band desynchronisation.21,22 As basal ganglia beta band coherence correlates with PD motor symptoms, the desynchronisation could lead to acute and long-term motor improvement.21,22,47 This is supported by animal studies showing CR-induced motor improvement correlating with reduction of beta oscillations in the STN and reduced cortico-basal ganglia-thalamic network coherence.33,36 The question emerges, whether similar results can be replicated in human subjects. The only clinical CR study to date 25 reported beta oscillation reduction with associated motor improvement and carry-over effects, but the study lacked comparison to cDBS. Furthermore, similarly to many other CR studies, due to technical constrains (lack of appropriate neurostimulators) the study only assessed the outcomes after stimulation was stopped. Lastly, while in CR computational models ON:OFF cycling at the frequency of the underlying pathological oscillation was shown to provide greater degree of neural desynchronisation than continuous ON patterns, 48 there are no available NHP or human studies directly comparing ON:OFF cycling to continuous ON CR.

Currently there is no consensus on the most optimal burst stimulation settings, therefore, the settings used in different studies are quite heterogeneous. It is speculated that low-frequency TBS shows greater effect on akinesia, while higher frequencies are more suitable for tremor control. 19 Additionally, with low intraburst frequency (around 50 Hz), simultaneous increase in intensity is needed to effectively alleviate motor symptoms. 19 In TBS DBS an interburst frequency of 5 Hz was most often utilized, which is in accordance with BC DBS studies, where superior motor outcomes were observed with lower interburst frequencies.19,20,26,37,38,40 Still the burst intervals and pause intervals in these studies differ greatly (e.g., the shortest burst interval being 0.1 s, and the longest ranging 30 s) and therefore the results must be interpreted carefully. Furthermore, in CR higher stimulation intensities (2.0–4.0 mA) and interburst frequencies (up to 60 Hz) were implemented in human than the NHP studies. 25 As speculated CR benefits (fewer side effects and optimised energy consumption) are thought to results from lower required stimulation intensities, this finding seems contradictory.21,34 With the introduction of burst DBS greater flexibility in stimulation parameters has become available. However, these parameters have been investigated only to a limited extent, which likely contributes to the variability observed across studies. In this context BC paradigms can facilitate block ON/OFF fMRI designs, providing insights into the network-level effects of stimulation and their potential relevance for optimizing DBS programming. 49 Understanding how the stimulation settings influence clinical outcomes remains an important next step, and systematic evaluation is essential.

The first limitation of our study is that due to inconsistent naming of burst stimulation paradigms our search string could have missed some potentially relevant studies. Furthermore, many of the included articles could either be classified as case reports/series or feature small sample sizes. Therefore, the current literature on the topic cannot provide a definitive conclusion on the efficacy and safety of burst stimulation. Moreover, because of the lack of better tools, RoB assessment was performed using NOS that was modified to suit the type of studies included, although this could have changed its sensitivity for detection of bias. Additionally, the complex neurophysiological background of the burst stimulation paradigms makes it difficult to compare among different burst stimulation setting (e.g., low and high frequency burst stimulation) and stimulation patterns (e.g., CR and BC/TBS). Therefore, these different stimulation modalities should be reviewed separately in the future. Lastly, in most non-shuffled CR studies contact activation follows a random sequence combined with ON:OFF cycling.24,25,33,34 The contact activation sequence in non-shuffled CR is therefore even more randomized than in sCR and should, in theory, produce even great amount of neural desynchronisation. The delineation into non-shuffled and sCR is hence obsolete and should be more precisely defined in the future.

Bridging these gaps through well-designed clinical trials is essential for advancing DBS strategies in PD. Randomised controlled trials are needed to optimize burst stimulation parameters, to more rigorously assess its safety and efficacy compared to cDBS, and to evaluate energy consumption on the longer term. Studies should determine whether burst stimulation induces neuroplastic changes and lasting post-stimulation effects. Additionally, unconventional waveforms such as variable-frequency, interleaving, paired-pulse, and temporally optimized DBS, may provide lower energy consumption, broader symptom relief, and fewer stimulation-related side effects, and should therefore be further explored. 18

In conclusion, burst stimulation appears to be a viable DBS option for PD, offering substantial flexibility and unexplored potential. Many implanted systems already support burst functionality, though it remains inactive. Future research should systematically investigate different burst patterns and optimal stimulation settings in humans with PD through rigorous, well-controlled studies.
