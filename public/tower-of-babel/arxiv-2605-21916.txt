A2QTGN: Adaptive Amplitude Quantum-Integrated Temporal Graph Network for Dynamic Link Prediction
Nouhaila Innan 1,2 , M. Murali Karthick 3 , Simeon Kandan Sonar 3 , Vivek Chaturvedi 3 , Muhammad Shafique 1,2

Abstract
Dynamic link prediction is important for modeling evolving interactions in complex systems, including social, communication, financial, and transportation networks. Classical temporal graph models capture sequential dependencies, but they may struggle to represent concurrent and rapidly changing node-edge interactions in large dynamic graphs. We propose A2QTGN (Adaptive Amplitude Quantum-Integrated Temporal Graph Network), a hybrid quantum-classical framework that combines adaptive amplitude encoding with a Temporal Graph Network backbone. The proposed mechanism represents node interaction features as quantum states and selectively refreshes amplitude embeddings based on temporal activity, preserving stable node states while emphasizing meaningful structural changes. This design reduces unnecessary quantum re-encoding and improves temporal representation for link prediction. Experiments on five Temporal Graph Benchmark datasets show that A2QTGN achieves strong predictive and ranking performance across diverse dynamic graphs. Ablation studies confirm the importance of both the quantum embedding module and the adaptive update strategy, while hardware-aware inference using a noisy backend and limited real-device execution supports the feasibility of near-term quantum-assisted temporal graph learning.

Instead of relying on claims of exponential advantage, quantum-inspired feature maps offer alternative geometric representations by transforming classical inputs into structured quantum states whose inner-product relationships may reveal patterns not easily captured by standard encoders. These embeddings modify the underlying decision boundary in feature space, potentially enabling models to distinguish subtle structural variations in evolving graphs.

Prior hybrid studies have demonstrated that augmenting classical architectures with parametrized quantum circuits can improve representation quality, introduce non-linear feature transformations, and stabilize optimization dynamics

II
Related Work

Prior studies on link prediction can be systematically categorized into three major methodological strands: those that capture time-evolving relationships through
temporal graph networks
, those employing established techniques such as
classical link predictors and meta-learning
approaches, and those exploring the potential of quantum computation with
quantum link prediction methods
.

II-A

Temporal Graph Networks

The evolution of temporal graph networks began with models like JODIE
[
12
]
, which used recurrent neural networks to model continuous-time dynamics, but its sequential dependency incurred high computational cost and limited scalability. This led to the foundational TGN
[
17
]
, which introduced an efficient, event-driven message passing mechanism coupled with a node memory module for inductive learning on streaming graphs. However, TGN’s design suffered from two primary shortcomings which were limited expressive power due to simple aggregation, and poor generalization across diverse networks. Post-TGN advancements addressed these issues through architectural diversification: TGAT
[
31
]
utilized self-attention for better aggregation but faced quadratic complexity; PINT
[
22
]
enforced provably injective message passing to enhance expressivity; and lightweight models like GraphMixer
[
2
]
favored efficiency using MLPs but often sacrificed performance on complex dynamics. The latest trend, exemplified by research into TGS
[
19
]
, aims to overcome the generalization barrier by using large-scale pre-training to establish transferable knowledge and neural scaling laws for dynamic graphs.

II-B

Classical Link Predictors, Embeddings, and Meta-Learning

Link prediction in static and dynamic graphs remains a challenge, as extensively studied by Ghasemian
et al.

[
4
]
. Their evaluation across hundreds of real-world networks demonstrated that no single algorithm dominates, leading to the consensus that near-optimal performance relies on meta-learning ensembles combining multiple structural and embedding-based methods. While models that integrate node attributes, such as AGEE
[
5
]
, successfully push performance yielding a 3% improvement over conventional embedding methods, the struggle for efficiency and expressivity persists in increasingly complex scenarios. For instance, methods designed for highly expressive knowledge graph link prediction, such as ComplEx
[
26
]
or RotatE
[
23
]
, achieved high scores but are fundamentally constrained by their transductive nature and high computational demands, struggling to scale to the billions of edges found in massive industrial graphs. Similarly, while deep GNN architectures like
GraIL

[
24
]
enable inductive link prediction, they require expensive local sub-graph extraction for every link prediction query, drastically increasing the inference latency and posing a clear, quantitative trade-off between structural expressivity and the necessity of real-time computational efficiency. This ongoing friction necessitates continued research into scalable yet highly expressive models.

II-C

Quantum Link Prediction and Embeddings

Recent efforts have explored quantum models for link prediction and representation learning. Fundamental to this area are quantum encoding schemes, including basic strategies such as angle encoding
[
27
]
and Amplitude encoding
[
27
]
, as well as more complex circuit-based methods, including Instantaneous Quantum Polynomial (IQP) circuits and Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)–inspired circuits, all of which map classical features into expressive quantum states.
Building upon these foundational encoding techniques, specialized quantum models have been developed for relational tasks. PQKELP
[
11
]
, for instance, introduced a projected quantum kernel embedding approach tailored for dynamic networks, demonstrating that quantum feature mappings can significantly improve relational reasoning capabilities. Concurrently, Moutinho
et al.

[
15
]
proposed a quantum-walk-based link prediction model, effectively showing how quantum interference effects can be harnessed to uncover latent connectivity patterns.

Despite these critical advances, existing quantum approaches predominantly operate on static graphs. They fundamentally lack dedicated mechanisms for temporal memory or continuous-time node representation, limiting their ability to effectively capture long-term dependencies in evolving graph structures.

To address these gaps, we introduce the
A2QTGN
, which integrates adaptive quantum encoding within a temporal graph framework to enable dynamic node-state modeling and efficient temporal information propagation.

III
Methodology

The proposed framework,
A2QTGN
, integrates quantum feature encoding within a temporal graph learning architecture to enable dynamic link prediction on evolving graphs. The model combines two complementary modules:
(1) the
AAE
, which encodes temporal variations of node features into quantum states, and
(2) a memory-based
TGN
, which propagates temporal dependencies across events for link prediction.

III-A

Discrete Temporal Graph Formulation

To support the proposed methodology, the temporal learning pipeline first requires a structured representation of how the graph evolves over time. In this work, we adopt a discrete temporal graph formulation that aligns with the event-driven processing of A2QTGN.

A discrete temporal graph is modeled as a sequence of graph snapshots indexed by discrete time steps:

\mathcal{G}=\{\mathbf{G}_{t}=(\mathbf{V}_{t},\mathbf{E}_{t},\mathbf{X}_{t},\mathbf{F}_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T},

(1)

where
\mathbf{V}_{t}
denotes the set of nodes at time
t
,
\mathbf{E}_{t}\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{t}\times\mathbf{V}_{t}
is the set of observed edges,
\mathbf{X}_{t}
represents the node feature matrix, and
\mathbf{F}_{t}
represents the corresponding edge feature matrix. Each incoming interaction event, typically a user–item pair
(u,v)
at time
t
, updates the graph with temporally varying features encoded in
\mathbf{X}_{t}
and
\mathbf{F}_{t}
.

The dynamic link prediction objective is to infer future interactions by using all historical snapshots up to time
t
. Formally, given
\{\mathbf{G}_{\tau}\}_{\tau\leq t}
, the model learns a mapping

f:\{\mathbf{G}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{G}_{t}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\!\left((u,v,f_{uv})\in\mathbf{E}_{t^{\prime}}\right),\quad t^{\prime}>t,

(2)

which estimates the probability that an edge
(u,v)
with attributes
f_{uv}
will appear at a future time
t^{\prime}
. This formulation ensures that both structural evolution and temporal variations in node and edge features are explicitly captured, forming the foundation for the quantum–temporal processing pipeline of A2QTGN.

III-B

Adaptive Amplitude Encoding

The AAE module represents the core novelty of our approach, addressing a fundamental efficiency challenge in temporal quantum graph processing. This module efficiently translates changes in node features over time into the quantum amplitude space. Rather than re-encoding every node at every timestep, AAE measures the magnitude of feature change and adjusts the encoding accordingly. Nodes that exhibit meaningful temporal variation receive fresh amplitude embeddings, while nodes with minimal change simply retain their previous quantum state. This adaptive mechanism allows the model to focus computational effort where the graph is evolving most, significantly improving efficiency while preserving the expressiveness of the learned representations.

III-C

Temporal Memory Update

The temporal memory update module is responsible for tracking historical dynamics and maintaining a long-term profile for each node (users and items). It builds on the core principles of the TGN architecture. Specifically, TGN introduces a continuous-time memory mechanism in which each node maintains a dedicated memory vector that is updated only when new interaction events occur. This event-driven paradigm enables efficient processing of large-scale temporal graphs without recomputing entire node histories at every time step.

In our framework, this principle is adopted by assigning every user and item a persistent memory state
m_{i}
together with an associated timestamp
t_{\text{last}}
. When a new interaction involving node
i
is observed at time
t
, its memory is retrieved and combined with the freshly generated quantum-temporal embedding
z_{i,t}
to produce an updated node representation. The resulting high-dimensional embedding
h_{i,t}
is then written back into the node’s memory, mirroring TGN’s message-based memory update rule. By decoupling the persistent memory (long-term state) from the transient embedding (task-specific state), the module preserves the temporal evolution of each node while enabling efficient real-time adaptation, consistent with the design principles of TGN.

Memory Storage

Each node
i
maintains a dedicated memory vector
m_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{\text{mem}}}
, and a record of the last time it was updated,
t_{\text{last}}
. These are managed by the
MemoryModule
(i.e.,
self.user_mem
and
self.item_mem
): The memory state
m_{i}
encapsulates the node’s long-term history and evolving behavioral patterns.

Temporal Integration

When a new interaction occurs at time
t
, the node’s current memory
m_{i,t_{\text{prev}}}
is retrieved. This memory is then used, along with the newly computed quantum-encoded feature representation
z_{i,t}
(derived from the AAE), to calculate a new, high-dimensional temporal embedding
h_{i,t}
:

h_{i,t}=\text{EmbeddingModule}(m_{i,t_{\text{prev}}}\parallel z_{i,t}).

(3)

After
h_{i,t}
is used for the link prediction task, it is immediately used to update the memory. The new memory state
m_{i,t}
is set to the current temporal embedding (detached from the computation graph to prevent gradients from flowing backward through time), and the update timestamp is set to
t
:

m_{i,t}\leftarrow h_{i,t},\quad t_{\text{last}}\leftarrow t.

This process ensures that the memory is incrementally updated in real-time with the most recent, relevant temporal information, allowing the model to adapt its predictions dynamically.

III-D

Node Representation and Link Prediction:

The formation of the node-level embedding and its subsequent use for link prediction is a two-step process, combining adaptive quantum encoding with a classical neural network for final representation and scoring.

Adaptive Quantum Encoding (
x\to z
)

The AAE is used to create an intermediate quantum-derived feature
z_{i,t}
for a node
i
based on the change in its features between interactions.

1.

Change Detection:
The temporal feature change
\Delta x_{i,t}
is computed:
\Delta x_{i,t}=X_{\text{curr}}-X_{\text{prev}}.

2.

Activity Factor:
An activity factor
\alpha
is calculated using a sigmoid function
\sigma
applied to the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the change, weighted by a hyperparameter
\beta
:

\alpha=\sigma(\beta\lVert\Delta x_{i,t}\rVert).

3.

Adaptive Update (
x^{\prime}
):
The encoder checks if
\alpha
exceeds a threshold
\tau
. If the change is significant (
\alpha>\tau
), a new feature vector is calculated as
X_{\text{prev}}+\alpha\Delta x_{i,t}
and normalized to form
x^{\prime}
. If the change is insignificant, the previous state
X_{\text{prev}}
is retained. This ensures only salient temporal changes are propagated, reducing the encoding of noise.

4.

Quantum Embedding (
z
):
The vector
x^{\prime}
is then padded and encoded into a quantum state
|\psi\rangle
using
qml.AmplitudeEmbedding
. The intermediate feature
z
is then extracted by measuring the expectation value of Pauli-Z operators (
\hat{Z}
) on all qubits:

z=\left(\langle\psi|\hat{Z}_{1}|\psi\rangle,\dots,\langle\psi|\hat{Z}_{N_{\text{qubit}}}|\psi\rangle\right).

Node Embedding Formation (
z\to h
)

The final node-level temporal embedding
h_{i,t}
is generated by the
EmbeddingModule
. This module acts as a feature fusion layer, concatenating the node’s long-term memory
m_{i,t_{\text{prev}}}
with the freshly generated quantum-encoded feature
z_{i,t}
:

h_{i,t}=\text{NN}([m_{i,t_{\text{prev}}}\parallel z_{i,t}])

This process creates a comprehensive node representation that captures both the long-term history (via
m
) and the immediate, relevant quantum-compressed temporal change (via
z
).

Link Prediction

For a given potential interaction between a user
u
and an item
i
, their respective final temporal embeddings
h_{u,t}
and
h_{i,t}
are obtained. These two embeddings are concatenated and passed through a scoring Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), denoted as
Scorer
, followed by a sigmoid activation
\sigma
:

P(\text{Link})=\sigma(\text{Scorer}([h_{u,t}\parallel h_{i,t}])).

(4)

The output is a scalar score representing the probability of a link forming, which is optimized against the ground truth labels using Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss during training.

IV-D

Ablation Study

The ablation study evaluates the contribution of the two main components of A2QTGN: the quantum embedding module and the adaptive quantum embedding update mechanism. We consider three update strategies. First, the proposed adaptive update strategy recomputes the quantum embedding only when the adaptive decision rule is activated. Second, the always-update variant removes the adaptive decision rule and recomputes the quantum embedding at every time step. Third, the no-update baseline disables quantum embedding updates entirely and reuses a fixed embedding throughout the temporal sequence. This design allows us to separate the benefit of using quantum embeddings from the benefit of updating them conditionally.

IV-F

Hardware Inference Evaluation

To assess the feasibility of quantum-assisted inference under hardware-aware conditions, we evaluate A2QTGN using the
FakeTorino
backend with 2048 shots. This backend provides a noisy simulation based on the topology and noise model of the corresponding IBM quantum device, allowing controlled and repeatable inference experiments while accounting for realistic quantum noise effects. Unlike ideal statevector simulation, this setting introduces finite-shot estimation and backend noise, making it suitable for evaluating whether the learned quantum representations remain usable under practical execution constraints. In addition to the
FakeTorino
noisy-backend evaluation, we also execute A2QTGN on an actual IBM quantum device under a constrained 100-shot setting. Since this real-device run uses a small measurement budget, it is reported only as supporting evidence rather than as the main hardware benchmark.

IV-G

Discussion

The results provide three main insights into the behavior of A2QTGN. First, the model shows strong predictive and ranking performance across datasets with different temporal structures, but the source of difficulty differs by dataset. For example,
tgbl-flight
achieves the highest accuracy and AUC, suggesting that its temporal interaction patterns are highly learnable despite its large edge volume. In contrast,
tgbl-review
presents a more difficult generalization setting, with lower accuracy, precision, AUC, and MRR. This indicates that dataset difficulty is not determined only by graph size, but also by temporal regularity, interaction variability, and the predictability of future links.

Second, the ablation results confirm that both the quantum embedding and the adaptive update rule are important to the final performance. Removing the quantum embedding substantially weakens the representation, while replacing the adaptive rule with always-update or no-update strategies reduces generalization. This suggests that the benefit of A2QTGN does not come only from adding a quantum layer to TGN, but from controlling when quantum representations are refreshed. Selective updating appears to preserve useful temporal information while reducing unnecessary recomputation and limiting unstable representation changes.

Third, the hardware-aware inference results show that the learned quantum representations remain usable under noisy quantum execution. The
FakeTorino
backend results indicate that A2QTGN can preserve strong ranking behavior under finite-shot noisy simulation, especially on
tgbl-wiki
,
tgbl-coin
, and
tgbl-flight
. The limited real-hardware run further supports executable feasibility, although its results should be interpreted cautiously because of the restricted 100-shot budget. These findings suggest that the proposed architecture is compatible with near-term quantum execution, while also showing that performance remains sensitive to shot budget, backend noise, and dataset-specific separability.

Despite these strengths, several limitations remain. The adaptive threshold introduces a trade-off between responsiveness and computational savings: conservative thresholds may miss meaningful temporal changes, while aggressive thresholds may trigger excessive quantum updates. In addition, the quantum embedding capacity may become limiting for high-dimensional or highly heterogeneous temporal graphs unless paired with more effective feature compression or more efficient circuit designs. Future work should therefore study threshold selection, circuit scaling, and hardware-aware optimization strategies to improve robustness on larger and more irregular dynamic graphs.

V
Conclusion

This work presented A2QTGN, a hybrid quantum–classical framework for temporal link prediction that combines adaptive amplitude encoding with a Temporal Graph Network backbone. The proposed design enables quantum embeddings to be updated selectively based on temporal feature changes, reducing redundant re-encoding while preserving informative node-state evolution.

Evaluation on five TGBL datasets shows that A2QTGN achieves strong predictive and ranking performance across diverse dynamic graphs. The results also show that the adaptive update mechanism improves generalization compared with always-update and no-update alternatives, while hardware-aware inference supports the feasibility of executing the model under noisy quantum settings.

Future work will focus on improving scalability for larger and more heterogeneous temporal graphs, refining the adaptive threshold selection, and developing more hardware-efficient quantum circuits for dynamic network analysis.
