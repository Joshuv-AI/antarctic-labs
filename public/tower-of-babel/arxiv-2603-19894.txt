Stochastic behavior along mean motion resonances in the restricted planar 3-body problem
M. Guardia, V. Kaloshin, P. Martín, P. Roldan

Abstract
One of the most remarkable instability zones in the Solar system are Kirkwood gaps in the asteroid belt. In this paper we analyze instabilities in the famous Kirkwood gap 3 : 1 3:1 in the regime of small eccentricity of Jupiter. Mathematically speaking, we study the evolution of asteroids under the influence of the Sun and Jupiter using the restricted planar elliptic 3-body problem (RPE3BP) for initial conditions near a mean motion resonance 3:1. The main result exhibits stochastic diffusing behavior of the eccentricity of the asteroid for a rich set of initial conditions. Roughly speaking, for small eccentricity e 0 e_{0} of Jupiter, the evolution of the eccentricity of the asteroid 𝐞 ⁡ ( t ⋅ e 0 − 2 ) \mathbf{e}(t\cdot e_{0}^{-2}) at the Kirkwood gap 3 : 1 3:1 behaves like a diffusion process on the line, where the randomness comes from the initial conditions. Along with KAM theory, we have mixed behavior in the asteroid belt, that is coexistence of quasiperiodic (deterministic) and stochastic (diffusive) behavior. Our proof consists of four main steps: 1. (Separatrix map) Compute the first and second orders of the perturbation theory of the separatrix map for the RPE3BP near 3:1 resonance with respect to small eccentricity of Jupiter. 2. (Normally Hyperbolic Invariant Lamination) Establish the existence of a normally hyperbolic invariant lamination Λ \Lambda for the time one map of RPE3BP, which is a weakly invariant object locally given as the product of a Cantor set and a 2-dimensional cylinder. 3. (Skew-shift model) The dynamics restricted to Λ \Lambda , after a change of coordinates, is given by a skew-shift. In the simplified form we have f : ( ω , I , θ ) → ( σ ​ ω , I + e 0 ​ A ω ​ ( I ) ​ cos ⁡ ( θ + ψ ω ) + 𝒪 ⁡ ( e 0 2 ) , θ + Ω ω ​ ( I ) + 𝒪 ⁡ ( e 0 ) ) , \qquad f:(\omega,I,\theta)\to(\sigma\omega,I+e_{0}A_{\omega}(I)\cos(\theta+\psi_{\omega})+\mathcal{O}(e^{2}_{0}),\theta+\Omega_{\omega}(I)+\mathcal{O}(e_{0})), where I ∈ [ a , b ] , θ ∈ 𝕋 , ω ∈ Σ = { 0 , 1 } ℤ I\in[a,b],\ \theta\in\mathbb{T},\ \omega\in\Sigma=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}} , the space of sequences of 0 , 1 0,1 ’s, σ : Σ → Σ \sigma:\Sigma\to\Sigma is the shift in this space, Ω ω \Omega_{\omega} is the shear, A ω A_{\omega} is an amplitude, and e 0 e_{0} is the eccentricity of Jupiter, which is taken as a small parameter, 4. (Martingale analysis) Study the evolution of a probability measure μ \mu supported on Λ \Lambda and given as a product of a Bernoulli measure on Σ \Sigma and either the Lebesgue measure or an atomic measure on 𝕋 \mathbb{T} . A delicate martingale analysis shows that the dynamics restricted to Λ \Lambda exhibits diffusing behavior of the eccentricity of the orbit of the asteroid. This martingale analysis strongly relies on decay of correlations of compact Lie group extensions of hyperbolic maps and a special Central Limit Theorem for sums of dependent random variables.

Introduction

The stability of the Solar System is a longstanding problem.
Over the centuries, mathematicians and astronomers have
spent an inordinate amount of energy proving stronger and
stronger stability theorems for dynamical systems closely
related to the Solar System, generally within the framework of
the Newtonian
N
-body problem:

\ddot{q}_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}m_{j}\frac{q_{j}-q_{i}}{\|q_{j}-q_{i}\|^{3}},\qquad q_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad m_{i}>0,\qquad i=0,1,\dots,N-1,

and its planetary subproblem, where the mass
m_{0}
(modeling
the Sun) is much larger than the other masses
m_{i}
(the planets).

Letting the masses
m_{i}
tend to
0

i=2,\dots,N-1
, we
obtain a collection of
N-2
independent
restricted problems
:

\ddot{q}_{i}=m_{0}\frac{q_{0}-q_{i}}{\|q_{0}-q_{i}\|^{3}}+m_{1}\frac{q_{0}-q_{1}}{\|q_{0}-q_{1}\|^{3}},\qquad q_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad i=2,\dots,N-1.

where the massless bodies are influenced by, without
themselves influencing, the primaries of masses
m_{0}
and

m_{1}
. For
N=3
, this model is often used to approximate
the dynamics of the Sun–Jupiter–asteroid or other
Sun-planet-object problems, and it is the simplest
one conjectured to have a wide range of instabilities.

1.1
An example of relevance in astronomy:
The asteroid belt and the restricted planar 3-body problem

One place in the Solar System where the dynamics is well

1.2
The Wisdom mechanism

The first explanation of formation of Kirkwood gaps was

Then the dynamics in the mean motion resonances of the restricted

fast, intermediate and slow. In reality, this ratio is approximately
\approx 0.632
and
not small.

According to Wisdom in the main approximation, on a carefully chosen energy surface, the secular evolution of the asteroid motion near 3:1 resonance with Jupiter is described
by a particular “slow” Hamiltonian system of two degrees of freedom. This system exhibits

1.3
The restricted planar elliptic 3-body problem

Consider the restricted 3-body problem and assume that the massless
body moves in the same plane as the other bodies,
called primaries. We normalize the total mass to one, and we call
the three bodies the Sun (mass
1-\mu
), Jupiter (mass
\mu

with
0<\mu<1
) and the asteroid (zero mass). If the energy of
the primaries is negative, their orbits describe two ellipses with
the same eccentricity, say
e_{0}\in[0,1)
. For convenience, we denote
by
q_{0}(t)
the normalized position of the primaries (or “fictitious body”)
at time
t
, so that the Sun and Jupiter have respective positions

-\mu q_{0}(t)
and
(1-\mu)q_{0}(t)
. The Hamiltonian of
the asteroid is

\mathcal{H}(p,q,t)=\frac{\|p\|^{2}}{2}-\frac{1-\mu}{\|q+\mu q_{0}(t)\|}-\frac{\mu}{\|q-(1-\mu)q_{0}(t)\|},

(1)

where
q,p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean distance.
Without loss of generality one can assume that
q_{0}(t)
has
semi-major axis 1 and period
2\pi
. In what follows we
abbreviate the name of this problem as RPE3BP.

For
e_{0}\in[0,1)
, the RPE3BP has two and a half degrees of freedom. When
e_{0}=0
,
the primaries describe uniform circular motions around their center of mass. This system is often called

the restricted planar circular 3-body problem
and, in what follows, is abbreviated RPC3BP.

Thus, in a frame rotating with the primaries, the system becomes autonomous and hence
has only two degrees of freedom. Its energy in the rotating frame is a first integral, called

the Jacobi integral or the Jacobi constant
, defined by

\mathbf{J}(q,p,t)=\frac{\|p\|^{2}}{2}-\frac{1-\mu}{\|q+\mu q_{0}(t)\|}-\frac{\mu}{\|q-(1-\mu)q_{0}(t)\|}-(q_{1}p_{2}-q_{2}p_{1}).

(2)

The aforementioned KAM theory applies to both the circular
and the elliptic problem
[
1
,
39
]
and asserts that
if the mass of Jupiter is small enough, there is a set of initial
conditions of positive Lebesgue measure leading to quasiperiodic
motions, in the neighborhood of circular motions of the asteroid (see also
[
14
]
).
If Jupiter has a circular motion, since the system has only two
degrees of freedom, KAM invariant tori are
2
-dimensional
and separate the
3
-dimensional energy surfaces. But in
the elliptic problem,
3
-dimensional KAM tori do not prevent
orbits from wandering on a
5
-dimensional phase space.
In this paper we prove the existence of a wide enough set of
orbits in the RPE3BP whose eccentricity has
chaotic behavior
and can be approximated by a 1-dimensional stochastic
diffusion process.

Let us write the Hamiltonian (
1
) as follows

\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(p,q,t)=\mathcal{H}_{0}(p,q)+\mathcal{H}_{1}(p,q,t,\mu),

where

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{0}(p,q)&=\frac{\|p\|^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{\|q\|}\\
\mathcal{H}_{1}(p,q,t,\mu)&=\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1-\mu}{\|q+\mu q_{0}(t)\|}-\frac{\mu}{\|q-(1-\mu)q_{0}(t)\|}.\end{split}

(3)

The Keplerian part
\mathcal{H}_{0}
allows us to associate elliptical elements
to every point
(p,q)
of the phase space of negative energy
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
We are interested in the fluctuations of the Jacobi constant
\mathbf{J}
and
the osculating eccentricity
\mathbf{e}
under the
t
-evolution.

To analyze this evolution, in the following two sections, we introduce two main ingredients
that will be used in our main results, Theorems
1.1
and

1.2
below. These are diffusion processes and the kind
of measures that we consider.

1.4
Diffusion processes

To state the main result about diffusive behavior, we need to recall some basic
probabilistic notions. A random process
\{B_{t},t\geq 0\}
is called
the Wiener process
or

a Brownian motion
if the following conditions hold:

•

B_{0}=0
,
B_{t}
is almost surely continuous and

B_{t}-B_{s}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,t-s)
for any
0\leq s\leq t
,
where
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma}^{2})
denotes the normal
distribution with expected value
\bm{\mu}
and variance
\bm{\sigma}^{2}
.

•

B_{t}
has independent increments. That is, if

0\leq s_{1}\leq t_{1}\leq s_{2}\leq t_{2}
, then
B_{t_{1}}-B_{s_{1}}

and
B_{t_{2}}-B_{s_{2}}
are independent random variables.

A Brownian motion is a properly chosen limit of the standard
random walk. A generalization of a Brownian motion is

a diffusion process
or
an Itô (or Itô-Döblin) diffusion
. To define it, let
(\Omega,\Sigma,P)
be a probability space. Let

X_{t}:[0,+\infty)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. It is called an Itô diffusion if
it satisfies
a stochastic differential equation
of the form

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\bm{b}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\sigma}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},

(4)

where
B_{t}
is a Brownian motion and
\bm{b}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

and
\bm{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are the drift and the variance respectively.
For a point
x\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\mathbf{P}^{x}
denote the law of
X_{t}

given initial data
X_{0}=x
, and let
\mathbb{E}^{x}
denote the
expectation with respect to
\mathbf{P}^{x}
.

The
infinitesimal generator
of
X_{t}
is the operator
A
,
which is defined to act on suitable functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

Af(x)=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}^{x}[f(X_{t})]-f(x)}{t}.

The set of all functions
f
for which this limit exists at
a point
x
is denoted
D_{A}(x)
, while
D_{A}
denotes
the set of
f
’s for which
the limit exists for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. One can show that any
compactly-supported
\mathcal{C}^{2}
function
f

lies in
D_{A}
and that

Af(x)=\bm{b}(x)\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}+\dfrac{1}{2}\bm{\sigma}^{2}(x)\dfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x\partial x}.

(5)

The distribution of a diffusion process is characterized by
the drift
\bm{b}(x)
and the variance
\bm{\sigma}(x)
.

1.5
Invariant measures supported on normally hyperbolic invariant laminations

The main results stated in the next section will show how the pushforward of a certain
class of measures along the flow associated to the Hamiltonian (
1

an Itô process at certain time scales. Before giving precise statements, let us explain
how are the measures that we consider.

The Hamiltonian (
1

a priori chaotic setting in Arnold diffusion. A simplified model of such Hamiltonians is
a small perturbation of a perturbed pendulum like dynamics (i.e. a saddle with invariant
manifolds intersecting transversally) times an integrable twist map on a cylinder (see
Figure
4
below). For such models, one can build a normally hyperbolic (weakly) invariant lamination
(NHIL for short, see Definition
4.3
below). This NHIL is homeomorphic to

\Sigma\times\mathbb{T}\times\mathcal{I}
where
\Sigma=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is the space of sequences
of two symbols and
\mathcal{I}\subset\mathbb{R}
is an interval.

The measures whose pushforward we analyze are of two following types:

1

We consider measures which are a Bernouilli measure on
\Sigma
times an atomic measure at
any given values
of angle
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
and
I\in\mathcal{I}
.

2

We consider measures of the form Bernouilli measure on
\Sigma
times Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
times an atomic measure for any given value
I\in\mathcal{I}

Roughly speaking Theorem
1.1
below will show diffusive behavior for the pushforward of such measures projected onto the action coordinate
I

\theta
) or the type 2 measures.

The recent paper

The normally hyperbolic invariant lamination

In this section we construct the Normally Hyperbolic Invariant Lamination in two steps.
First we analyze the Poincaré map associated to the circular problem (

This system is autonomous and hence
I

is reduced to proving the existence of a horseshoe at each level

As a second step, we show the existence of the NHIL for (the Poincaré map of) the elliptic problem
(
11

0<e_{0}\ll 1
. This is a regular perturbation problem and, therefore,
the existence of the lamination is a consequence of normal hyperbolicity.

A normally hyperbolic invariant lamination generalizes the concepts of hyperbolic sets and normally
hyperbolic invariant manifolds (see
[
23
]
). Here we consider a less general definition than the one in
[
23
]
,
which is be enough for our purposes. It follows the ideas in
[
25
]
, where normally
hyperbolic invariant laminations for a priori unstable nearly integrable Hamiltonian systems are constructed.

First, we define a normally hyperbolic invariant manifold.

Definition 4.1
.

Given a
C^{1}
map
F
on a manifold
M
, we say that that a submanifold

N\subset M
is normally hyperbolic for the map
F
if it is
F

one can split

into three invariant subbundles,

T_{x}M=T_{x}N\oplus E_{x}^{s}\oplus E_{x}^{u},\qquad\text{for all }\quad x\in N

which satisfy

\begin{split}\|DF^{n}(x)|_{E^{s}}\|\leq C\lambda^{n}\qquad\text{ for }n\geq 0\\
\|DF^{n}(x)|_{E^{u}}\|\leq C\lambda^{|n|}\qquad\text{ for }n\leq 0\\
\|DF^{n}(x)|_{TN}\|\leq C\eta^{|n|}\qquad\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{N}\end{split}

for some
C>0
,
\lambda<1
and
\eta\geq 1
such that
\eta\lambda<1
.

The concept of normally hyperbolic invariant lamination is a generalization of
this definition. To this end, we define first the following.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\Sigma=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. We define the Bernoulli

\sigma:\Sigma\to\Sigma
as
(\sigma\omega)_{k}=\omega_{k+1}
.

Definition 4.3
.

Consider a
C^{1}
map
F
defined on a manifold
M
. We say that
a closed set
\Xi
is a normally hyperbolic invariant lamination if

\Xi=\bigcup_{\omega\in\Sigma}\Xi_{\omega}

where
\Xi_{\omega}
are
C^{1}
manifolds which satisfy

F(\Xi_{\omega})=\Xi_{\sigma\omega}
and is normally hyperbolic in the following
sense. There exist constants
C>0
,
0<\lambda<1

Since

Define the operator

. Then, if

is an eigenfunction of this operator of eigenvalue

. Evaluating this equality at
\omega=\omega^{+},\omega^{-}
and using

,
\sigma^{2P+1}\omega^{-}=\omega^{-}
, we have

e^{\mathrm{i}m\sum_{j=0}^{2P}\beta_{\sigma^{j}\omega^{+}}}A_{\omega^{+}}=\nu^{2P+1}A_{\omega^{+}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad e^{\mathrm{i}m\sum_{j=0}^{2P}\beta_{\sigma^{j}\omega^{-}}}A_{\omega^{-}}=\nu^{2P+1}A_{\omega^{-}}.

Since
A_{\omega^{+}},A_{\omega^{-}}\neq 0
,

e^{\mathrm{i}m\sum_{j=0}^{2P}\beta_{\sigma^{j}\omega^{+}}}=\nu^{2P+1}=e^{\mathrm{i}m\sum_{j=0}^{2P}\beta_{\sigma^{j}\omega^{-}}},

which is equivalent to

(53)

We show that this is not possible. We split the sum in the exponent into two sums. The first one for
j\in[P-|\log\rho|,P+|\log\rho|]
and the second one for the complement. For the first one, note that the number of terms in the sum is of order
\log\rho
. Then, using the expansion for
\beta_{\omega}
given in Lemma
5.1
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\in[P-|\log\rho|,P+|\log\rho|]\end{subarray}}^{2P}\left(\beta_{\sigma^{j}\omega^{+}}-\beta_{\sigma^{j}\omega^{-}}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\in[P-|\log\rho|,P+|\log\rho|]\end{subarray}}^{2P}\left(\beta^{0}_{\omega_{j}^{+}}-\beta^{0}_{\omega^{-}_{j}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\rho\log^{2}\rho\right).

Since the two periodic orbits
\omega^{+}
,
\omega^{-}
only differ in the
P
-symbol in the interval

, one has

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\

&=\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\rho\log^{2}\rho\right).\end{split}

(54)

Now we consider the second sum
j=0,\ldots,2P
with

. Since
\omega^{+}
and
\omega^{-}
in the period

only differ by the symbol

, one has that

for some

and

. Note that here the metric on

is the one induced by the horseshoe obtained in Theorem
4.4
. Then, since
\beta_{\omega}
is Hölder continuous. One has that for

Thus, the second sum satisfies

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\

}\quad\zeta>0.

(55)

Then, applying the estimates obtained in (
54
) and (

Taking
\rho>0
small enough and using condition (
52
) we reach a contradiction.

|n_{j+1}-n_{j}|\geq ce_{0}^{\gamma-1}\quad\text{ for }j=0,\ldots,j^{*}-1.

Therefore, since
n\leq\kappa e_{0}^{-2}
,

j^{*}\lesssim e_{0}^{-1-\gamma}.

(82)

Then, by Lemma
5.9
, for any small

\delta>0
,

\mathrm{Prob}\left(\exists k\,\text{ s. t. }\Delta_{j}<e_{0}^{-2(1-\gamma)+\delta}\text{ or }\Delta_{j}>e_{0}^{-2(1-\gamma)-\delta}\right)\leq e_{0}^{-1-\gamma}e^{-Ce_{0}^{-\delta}}.

This implies,

\begin{split}\Bigg|\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{j^{*}}\eta_{j}\Bigg|&\,\,\exists j\,\text{ s.
t. }\Delta_{j}<e_{0}^{-2(1-\gamma)+\delta}\text{ or }\Delta_{j}>e_{0}^{-2(1-\gamma)-\delta}\Bigg)\Bigg|\\
&\times\mathrm{Prob}\left(\exists j\,\text{ s. t. }\Delta_{j}<e_{0}^{-2(1-\gamma)+\delta}\text{ or }\Delta_{j}>e_{0}^{-2(1-\gamma)-\delta}\right)\\
&\leq e_{0}^{-1-\gamma}\cdot e_{0}^{2\gamma+\zeta}\cdot e_{0}^{-1-\gamma}e^{-Ce_{0}^{-\delta}}.\end{split}

Now we bound the first term in (
81
). The assumptions on the exit times
\Delta_{j}
when conditioning the probability imply

e_{0}^{-2\gamma+\delta}\leq j^{*}\leq e_{0}^{-2\gamma-\delta}.

(83)

Now we are ready to prove that the first term in (
81
)
tends to zero with
e_{0}
. We bound the probability by one. To prove
that
the conditioned expectation in the first line tends to zero with
e_{0}
,
it is enough to take into account (
80
) and
(
83
), to obtain

\left|\mathbb{E}\left(\left.\sum_{j=0}^{j^{*}}\eta_{j}\right|e_{0}^{-2(1-\gamma)+\delta}\leq\Delta_{j}\leq e_{0}^{-2(1-\gamma)-\delta}\,\,\forall j\right)\right|\lesssim e_{0}^{2\gamma+\zeta}\cdot e_{0}^{-2\gamma-\delta}\leq e_{0}^{\zeta-\delta}.

Therefore, taking
\delta>0
small enough we obtain (
63
).
In conclusion, we obtain that the
J
behaves as a diffusion process

d\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}=\bm{b}(\mathcal{J})d\kappa+\bm{\sigma}^{2}(\mathcal{J})dB_{\kappa},

where the drift
\bm{b}
and the variance
\bm{\sigma}^{2}
are those defined in Lemma
5.8
.

Since the change of coordinates considered in Proposition
5.7

is
e_{0}
-close to the identity, we obtain that the original variable
I

behaves following the same diffusion process. Finally it is enough to recall that the Jacobi constant
\mathbf{J}
in (
2
) satisfies
\mathbf{J}=-I+\mathcal{O}(e_{0})
(see (
11
)). This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

5.4
Stochastic evolution of the eccentricity (Theorem
1.2
)

To deduce Theorem
1.2
from Theorem
1.1
, we proceed as follows.
First, note that one can deduce the angular momentum
G
from the other variables and the Jacobi constant. Indeed,

\mathbf{J}=-\frac{1}{2L^{2}}-G-\Delta H_{\mathrm{circ}}+\mathcal{O}(\mu e_{0}),

which, implies that one can write the angular momentum
G
as

G=\mathbf{G}(L,\ell,g,\mathbf{J},t)
,
where
\mathbf{G}
is a function satisfying

\mathbf{G}(L,\ell,g,\mathbf{J},t)=-\mathbf{J}-\frac{1}{2L^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\mu).

Then, if we define

\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J},L)=\sqrt{1-\frac{(\mathbf{J}+\frac{1}{2L^{2}})^{2}}{L^{2}}},

(see (
8
)),
we can write the eccentricity

\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J},L_{0})+E,\qquad L_{0}=3^{-1/3}

where

E=\sqrt{1-\frac{\mathbf{G}(L,\ell,g,\mathbf{J},t)^{2}}{L^{2}}}-\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J},L_{0}),

which is
\mathcal{O}(\mu)
. Now, to prove Theorem
1.2
, it only remains to:

(a)

Show that if
\mathbf{J}_{s}
is a diffusion process with drift
\bm{b}(\mathbf{J}_{s})
and variance
\bm{\sigma}^{2}(\mathbf{J}_{s})
(as stated in Theorem
1.1
), then
\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{s}=\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J}_{s},L_{0})
is also a diffusion process for suitable
\widetilde{\bm{b}}(\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{s})
and
\widetilde{\bm{\sigma}}^{2}(\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{s})
.

(b)

Show that
E
can be split into the two correction terms provided by Theorem
1.2
.

Item
(a)
is a direct consequence of Itô Lemma. Indeed, if we denote by
\mathbf{J}=g(\widetilde{\mathbf{e}},L_{0})
the inverse of the function
\mathbf{J}\mapsto\tilde{\mathbf{e}}=\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J},L_{0}
), it ensures that
\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{s}
also satisfies a diffusion process with drift and variance given by

\begin{split}\widetilde{\bm{b}}(\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{s})&=\partial_{\mathbf{J}}\mathbf{E}(g(\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{s},L_{0}),L_{0})\bm{b}(g(\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{s},L_{0}))+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{\mathbf{J}}\mathbf{E}(g(\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{s},L_{0}),L_{0})\bm{\sigma}^{2}(g(\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{s},L_{0})),\\
\widetilde{\bm{\sigma}}(\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{s})&=\partial_{\mathbf{J}}\mathbf{E}(g(\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{s},L_{0}),L_{0})\bm{\sigma}(g(\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{s},L_{0})).\end{split}

Now we prove Item
(b)
. To this end, let us consider a section
\Pi=\{q=\rho\}
, which is transverse to the unstable manifold of the normally hyperbolic invariant cylinder (
q
is the coordinate introduced in Lemma
3.4
). Note that the orbits in the normally hyperbolic invariant lamination keep crossing this section at each loop (until hitting the stoping time in the boundary). For each trajectory
\widetilde{\Phi}_{t}(X)
with
X
in the support of the measure (i.e in the normally hyperbolic invariant lamination), we denote by
t_{j}
,
j\geq 1
, the sequence of times these orbits hit the section
\Pi
. Since the lamination is
\rho
-close to the normally hyperbolic invariant cylinder, these times satisfy
t_{j+1}-t_{j}\sim|\log\rho|
for any
X\in\mathrm{supp}\nu_{\mathbf{J}^{*},e_{0}}
.

Now, for
e_{0}>0
small enough, each of the points
\widetilde{\Phi}_{t}(X)
are
\rho
-close to a homoclinic orbit of the RPC3BP (
e_{0}=0
) at the same Jacobi constant as
\widetilde{\Phi}_{t}(X)
(provided by Ansatz
1
). Let us denote by
(L_{j},\ell_{j},g_{j})(t,\mathbf{J})
,
j=1,2
, the time parameterization of these homoclinics. Then, one can define the two corrections as follows. Note that we have to analyze

\mathcal{E}(X,t)=E(\widetilde{\Phi}_{t}(X)),

which we split it as
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{2}
as

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{1}(X,t)&=\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J}_{t}(X),L_{t}(X))-\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J}_{t}(X),L_{0})\\
\mathcal{E}_{2}(X,t)&=E_{2}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{t}(X)),\end{split}

where
z_{t}(X)=\pi_{z}\widetilde{\Phi}_{t}(X)
, for
z=L,\ell,g,\mathbf{J},t
, and

E_{2}(X)=\sqrt{1-\frac{\mathbf{G}(L,\ell,g,\mathbf{J},t)^{2}}{L^{2}}}-\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J},L).

We analyze each of them separately for
t\in(t_{j},t_{j+1})
and we denote
\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{J}_{t_{j}}(X)
. We write the first one as
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\mathcal{E}_{1}^{1}+\mathcal{E}_{1}^{2}
with

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{1}^{1}(X,t)&=\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J},L_{j}(t,\mathbf{J}))-\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J},L_{0})\\
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{2}(X,t)&=\left[\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J}_{t}(X),L_{t}(X))-\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J},L_{j}(t,\mathbf{J}))\right]+\left[\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J},L_{0})-\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{J}_{t}(X),L_{0})\right].\end{split}

Note that since we are considering the regime
0<e_{0}\ll\rho\ll 1
,
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{2}
satisfies
|\mathcal{E}_{1}^{2}|\lesssim\rho^{\zeta^{*}}
since the orbits in the lamination are
\rho^{\zeta^{*}}
-close to the homoclinic orbit (see Theorem
4.5
). Therefore this term is of the form of the correction
E
in Theorem
1.2
. The term
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{1}
only depends on the choice of homoclinic orbit, the Jacobi constant at time
t_{j}
and on the time
t\in(t_{j},t_{j+1})
, and therefore it is of the form of the correction
\delta\mathbf{e}
. Moreover, by Ansatz
1
,

|\mathcal{E}_{1}^{1}(X,t)|\leq 126\mu.

Moreover, one can easily check that, by Ansatz
1
and Theorem
4.5
,
E_{2}
satisfies

|E_{2}(X)|\leq 126\mu.

We split
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\mathcal{E}_{2}^{1}+\mathcal{E}_{2}^{2}
as

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{2}^{1}(t,\mathbf{J})&=E_{2}(L_{j}(t,\mathbf{J}),\ell_{j}(t,\mathbf{J}),g_{j}(t,\mathbf{J}),\mathbf{J},t)\\
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{2}(X,t)&=E_{2}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{t}(X))-E_{2}(L_{j}(t,\mathbf{J}),\ell_{j}(t,\mathbf{J}),g_{j}(t,\mathbf{J}),\mathbf{J},t).\end{split}

Then, proceeding analogously as for
\mathcal{E}_{1}
, one has that
|\mathcal{E}_{2}^{2}(X,t)|\lesssim\rho^{\zeta^{*}}
and contributes to the correction term
E
in Theorem
1.2
and that
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{1}
is of the form of the correction term
\delta\mathbf{e}
in the theorem.

and Lemma

. This is explained in Section

6.1
Decay of correlations and a CLT for type 2 measures (Theorem
5.2
)

We start by explaining the setting of
[
16
]
and the results that we use. Consider
\Sigma
an aperiodic subshift of finite type and a compact Lie group
G
. Assume that
G
acts (orthogonally) on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by identifying
G
with a subgroup of
O(n)
(for some
n
).

Fix
\vartheta\in(0,1)
and consider a map
h:\Sigma\to G
with
\|h\|_{\vartheta}<\infty
(see (
45
)). We define the skew product
\sigma_{h}:\Sigma\times G\to\Sigma\times G
as

\sigma_{h}(\omega,g)=\left(\sigma\omega,gh(\omega)\right),

(84)

where the second component is just the (right) Lie group product by
h
. Consider the
\sigma
-equilibrium state
\mu
and the Haar measure
\nu
on
G
. Then, the product measure
\mathtt{m}=\mu\times\nu
is invariant under
\sigma_{h}
.

We consider
G
-equivariant observables
\phi:\Sigma\times G\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that is observables
\phi
satisfying

\phi(\omega,ag)=a\phi(\omega,g)\quad\text{ for all }\quad a\in G.

(85)

Equivalently,
\phi(\omega,g)=gV(\omega)
where
V:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We assume that
V
is Hölder and satisfies
\|V\|_{\vartheta}<\infty
(see (
45
)). We consider the space of those observables

\mathcal{F}_{\vartheta}^{G}=\left\{\phi:\Sigma\times G\to\mathbb{R}^{n}:\phi(\omega,g)=gV(\omega),\|V\|_{\vartheta}<\infty\right\}.

Consider the space
L^{2}(\Sigma\times G,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Note that
\mathcal{F}_{\vartheta}^{G}\subset L^{2}(\Sigma\times G,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. It is easy to see that one can scale the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\vartheta}
so that it satisfies
\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\phi\|_{\vartheta}
.

We also define the Koopman operator

U:L^{2}(\Sigma\times G,\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\Sigma\times G,\mathbb{R}^{n}),\qquad U\phi=\phi\circ\sigma_{h}.

(86)

Assume that

\begin{gathered}\text{The only solutions of \, $U\phi=\alpha\phi$ \, of the form \, $\phi(\omega,g)=gV(\omega)$}\\
\text{are $\alpha=1$ and $V=\text{constant}$.}\end{gathered}

(87)

Note that this is implied by weak mixing. However, it is a weaker statement since only rules out the existence of equivariant eigenfunctions of the Koopman operator
U
.

Under the condition (
87
), in
[
16
]
, it is shown that
there exists
r\in(0,1)
and
C>0
such that for any
j\geq 1
and
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{F}_{\vartheta}^{G}
,

\left|\int\phi\circ\sigma_{h}^{j}\psi^{T}d\mathtt{m}-\int\phi\,d\mathtt{m}\int\psi^{T}d\mathtt{m}\right|\leq C\|\phi\|_{\vartheta^{1/2}}\|\psi\|_{\vartheta^{1/2}}\,r^{j}.

(88)

The paper
[
16
]
also provides a Central Limit Theorem under assumption (
87
). To state it, we first need to introduce the covariance matrix. For a
G
-equivariant observable
\phi:\Sigma\times G\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define

\phi_{N}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\phi\circ\sigma_{h}^{j}.

Assume that
\phi
has zero mean, that is
\int\phi d\mathtt{m}=0
. Then, one can easily check that (
88
) implies that the limit
\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\int\phi_{N}\phi_{N}^{T}d\mathtt{m}
exists, which allows to define the covariance matrix

\Sigma_{\phi}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\int\phi_{N}\phi_{N}^{T}\,d\mathtt{m}.

Lemma 6.1

(
[
16
]
)
.

Assume that the matrix
\Sigma_{\phi}
is non-singular. Then, for each
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the sequence
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}c^{T}\phi_{N}
,
N\geq 1
, converges in distribution to a normal distribution with zero mean and variance
\sigma^{2}=c^{T}\Sigma_{\phi}c
.

Note that the setting of Theorem
5.2
fits the one in
[
16
]
. Indeed, it is enough to consider
the Lie Groups

G_{m}=\left\{g_{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(m\alpha)&\sin(m\alpha)\\
-\sin(m\alpha)&\cos(m\alpha)\end{pmatrix}:\alpha\in\mathbb{R}/(2\pi/m)\mathbb{Z}\right\}\qquad\text{with}\qquad m=1,2.

(89)

Then, for a given
\vartheta
-Hölder function
\beta:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
, one can define
g_{\beta}\in\mathcal{F}_{\vartheta}(\Sigma,G_{m})
and the associated map

\sigma_{\beta}(\omega,g_{\alpha})=g_{\alpha+\beta(\omega)}V(\sigma\omega).

For this map, the absence of non-trivial eigenvalues of the associated operator
U
in the unit circle is given by assumption (
48
). Then, we can consider the associated
G_{m}
-equivariant observables,

\phi(\omega,\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(m\alpha)&\sin(m\alpha)\\
-\sin(m\alpha)&\cos(m\alpha)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}P_{1}(\omega)\\
P_{2}(\omega)\end{pmatrix}

(90)

for
\vartheta
-Hölder functions
P_{1},P_{2}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
. Note that all equivariant observables over
G_{m}
have zero mean with respect to the measure
\mathtt{m}
.

In this setting, the decay of correlations statement (
88
) and the Central Limit Theorem (Lemma
6.1
) imply Theorem
5.2
.

6.2
Decay of correlations and a CLT for type 1 measures (Theorem
5.4
)

The statements on decay of correlations and Central Limit Theorem in Theorem
5.2
consider as measure the Gibbs measure on
\Sigma
times the Haar measure on the Lie group
G
. Now we prove Theorem
5.4
, which gives the same statements as Theorem
5.2
but for a different measure, which is not invariant: fixing any initial condition
s_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
and considering the Gibbs measure on
\Sigma
, that is
\mathtt{m}_{s_{0}}=\mu\times\delta_{s_{0}}
.

We start by proving that the first item of the theorem is a direct consequence of Corollary
5.3
and the second item of Theorem
5.4
. Below, in Sections
6.2.1
and
6.2.2
we prove the second item of the theorem.

If we write
F
and
G
as

F(\omega,s)=F^{+}(\omega)e^{\mathrm{i}ms}+\overline{F^{+}}(\omega)e^{-\mathrm{i}ms},\quad G(\omega,s)=G^{+}(\omega)e^{\mathrm{i}ms}+\overline{G^{+}}(\omega)e^{-\mathrm{i}ms},\quad m=1,2,

we have that, for
n>k
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(G(\sigma^{n}\omega,s_{n})F(\sigma^{k}\omega,s_{k})\right)=&2\mathrm{Re\,}\left(F^{+}(\sigma^{n}\omega)\overline{G^{+}}(\sigma^{k}\omega)e^{\mathrm{i}m\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}\beta^{\sigma^{j}\omega}}\right)\\
&+2\mathrm{Re\,}\left(F^{+}(\sigma^{n}\omega)G^{+}(\sigma^{k}\omega)e^{\mathrm{i}m\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\beta^{\sigma^{j}\omega}}e^{\mathrm{i}m\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\beta^{\sigma^{j}\omega}}\right).\end{split}

Then, the term in the first line can be bounded applying Corollary
5.3
. The term in the second line is estimated by the second item of Theorem
5.4
.

We prove now the second item of Theorem
5.4
. We strongly rely on the tools developed in
[
16
]
. This is done in two steps. First, in Section
6.2.1
we prove it under the assumption that
\beta
,
F
and
G
only depend on future symbols
\omega_{k}
,
k\geq 0
. That is, we prove decay of correlations for skew-products over the one-sided shift. Then, in Section
6.2.2
, we explain how to adapt the proof to the general case.

6.2.1
Decay of correlations and Lemma
5.5
for one-sided shifts

We first prove Item 2 of Theorem
5.4
for skew-products over the one-sided shift. To simplify the notation we consider
m=1
. The same proof applies to
m=2
.

Let us define
\Sigma^{+}=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, call
\sigma
the shift on
\Sigma^{+}
and
\mu
the Gibbs measure on
\Sigma^{+}
. For a function
P:\Sigma^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
, we can define the Koopman and transfer operators associated to
\sigma
as

\mathcal{U}:L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{+})\to L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{+})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{L}:L^{1}(\Sigma^{+})\to L^{1}(\Sigma^{+}),

by

\mathcal{U}P=P\circ\sigma\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\mathcal{L}P\,Q\,d\mu=\int_{\Sigma^{+}}P\,\mathcal{U}Q\,d\mu.

Now, we consider the Lie group extension of the shift
\sigma
,
\sigma_{\beta}:\Sigma^{+}\times\mathbb{T}\to\Sigma^{+}\times\mathbb{T}
defined as

\sigma_{\beta}(\omega,s)=(\sigma\omega,s+\beta(\omega))

(91)

where
\beta:\Sigma^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\vartheta
-Hölder function (see (
45
)) and, following
[
16
]
, we consider equivariant observables

F(\omega,s)=P(\omega)e^{\mathrm{i}s},\qquad G(\omega,s)=Q(\omega)e^{\mathrm{i}s},

(92)

where
P,Q:\Sigma^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
are also
\vartheta
-Hölder functions. We denote by
F_{\vartheta}(\Sigma^{+})
the space of
\vartheta
-Hölder functions
P:\Sigma^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
.

Associated to this skew-shift and the equivariant observables, we define the twisted transfer operator

\mathcal{L}_{\beta}P=\mathcal{L}(e^{\mathrm{i}\beta}P),

which satisfies that, for
n\geq 1
,

\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\mathcal{L}_{\beta}^{n}P\,Qd\mu=\int_{\Sigma^{+}}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}}P\,\mathcal{U}^{n}Qd\mu\qquad\text{where}\qquad\beta_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\beta\circ\sigma^{j}.

(93)

Note that the right hand side above can be seen as a Koopman operator for equivariant observables (
92
),

F\circ\sigma_{\beta}=e^{\mathrm{i}\beta}\mathcal{U}Pe^{\mathrm{i}s}.

The following proposition is proven in
[
16
, Corollary 4.1]
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Fix
\vartheta\in(0,1)
. Consider the map
\sigma_{\beta}
in (
91
) with
\beta:\Sigma^{+}\to\mathbb{R}

\vartheta
-Hölder and assume that the equation
e^{\mathrm{i}\beta(\omega)}A(\sigma\omega)=\nu A(\omega)
has at most one solution in
F_{\vartheta}(\Sigma_{+})
: the trivial solution
\nu=1
,
A_{\omega}=\text{constant}
. Then, there exists
C>0
and
r\in(0,1)
such that

\left\|\mathcal{L}_{\beta}^{n}P\right\|_{\vartheta}\leq Cr^{n}\|P\|_{\vartheta}\qquad\text{for all}\quad P\in F_{\vartheta}(\Sigma_{+}).

Note that the assumption (
48
) implies that there exists
C>0
and
r\in(0,1)
such that
\mathcal{L}_{\beta}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2\beta}
satisfy the proposition.

Assumption (
48
) and this proposition will imply Item 2 of Theorem
5.4
for the one-sided shift, that is

\left|\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(P\left(\sigma^{n}\omega\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}(\omega)}Q\left(\sigma^{k}\omega\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{k}(\omega)}\right)\right|\leq C\,\|P\|_{\vartheta}\,\|Q\|_{\vartheta}\ r^{n},

for any
n>k
and
P,Q\in F_{\vartheta}(\Sigma_{+})
.

Note that the left hand side can be written as

J_{n,k}=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(P\left(\sigma^{n}\omega\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}(\omega)}Q\left(\sigma^{k}\omega\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{k}(\omega)}\right)=\int_{\Sigma^{+}}(\mathcal{U}^{n}P)e^{i\beta_{n}}(\mathcal{U}^{k}Q)e^{i\beta_{k}}d\mu.

Now, since
\beta_{n}=\beta_{k}+\mathcal{U}^{k}\beta_{n-k}
, one has that

e^{i\beta_{n}}=e^{i\beta_{k}}\mathcal{U}^{k}e^{i\beta_{n-k}}.

(94)

Then, using also (
93
),

\begin{split}J_{n,k}&=\int_{\Sigma^{+}}e^{2\mathrm{i}\beta_{k}}\mathcal{U}^{k}\left(e^{i\beta_{n-k}}Q\,\mathcal{U}^{n-k}P\right)d\mu=\int_{\Sigma^{+}}Q\mathcal{L}_{2\beta}^{k}1e^{i\beta_{n-k}}\mathcal{U}^{n-k}Pd\mu\\
&=\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\mathcal{L}_{\beta}^{n-k}\left(Q\mathcal{L}_{2\beta}^{k}1\right)Pd\mu.\end{split}

Therefore, denoting by
|\cdot|_{\infty}
the norm associated to
L^{\infty}(\Sigma_{+})
and recalling that
|\cdot|_{\infty}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\vartheta}
, Proposition
6.2
and (
46
) imply

\begin{split}|J_{n,k}|&\leq\left|\mathcal{L}_{\beta}^{n-k}\left(Q\mathcal{L}_{2\beta}^{k}1\right)\right|_{\infty}|P|_{\infty}\leq Cr^{n-k}\|Q\mathcal{L}_{2\beta}^{k}1\|_{\vartheta}|P|_{\infty}\\
&\leq Cr^{n-k}\|Q\|_{\vartheta}\|\mathcal{L}_{2\beta}^{k}1\|_{\vartheta}|P|_{\infty}\leq C^{2}r^{n}\|Q\|_{\vartheta}\|1\|_{\vartheta}|P|_{\infty}\leq C^{2}r^{n}\|Q\|_{\vartheta}|P|_{\infty}.\end{split}

This completes the proof of Item 2 of Theorem
5.4
for one-sided shifts.

Actually, to generalize this result to two-sided shifts we need a more involved decay of correlations statement which involves more observables. This is stated in the following lemma, which will be also used to prove Lemma
5.5
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Fix
\vartheta\in(0,1)
. Then, there exist constants
r\in(0,1)
and

C>0
such that, for any
n,k,i\in\mathbb{N}
,
n>k>i
and any
\vartheta
-Hölder functions
P,Q,S,T:\Sigma^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
, the following is satisfied,

\begin{split}\left|\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}(\omega)}P\left(\sigma^{n}\omega\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{k}(\omega)}Q\left(\sigma^{k}\omega\right)S\left(\omega\right)\right)\right|&\leq C\,|P|_{\infty}\,\|Q\|_{\vartheta}\,\|S\|_{\vartheta}\ r^{n}\\
\left|\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}(\omega)}P\left(\sigma^{n}\omega\right)Q\left(\sigma^{k}\omega\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}(\omega)}S\left(\sigma^{i}\omega\right)T(\omega)\right)\right|&\leq C\,|P|_{\infty}\,\|Q\|_{\vartheta}\,\|S\|_{\vartheta}\,\|T\|_{\vartheta}\ r^{n}.\end{split}

Proof.

Note that the first statement in Lemma
6.3
is implied by the second one just taking
Q=1
. Therefore, it is enough to prove the second one.

We denote

J_{nki}=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}(\omega)}P\left(\sigma^{n}\omega\right)Q\left(\sigma^{k}\omega\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}(\omega)}S\left(\sigma^{i}\omega\right)T(\omega)\right)\\
=\int_{\Sigma^{+}}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}}\mathcal{U}^{n}P\,\mathcal{U}^{k}Q\,e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}}\mathcal{U}^{i}S\,Td\mu.

Then, using (
94
),

\begin{split}J_{nki}=&\int_{\Sigma^{+}}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{k}}\mathcal{U}^{k}\left(Qe^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n-k}}\mathcal{U}^{n-k}P\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}}\mathcal{U}^{i}S\,Td\mu\\
=&\int_{\Sigma^{+}}e^{2\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}}\mathcal{U}^{i}\left[Se^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{k-i}}\mathcal{U}^{k-i}\left(Qe^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n-k}}\mathcal{U}^{n-k}P\right)\right]Td\mu\end{split}

and, by (
93
),

\begin{split}J_{nki}=&\int_{\Sigma^{+}}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{k-i}}\mathcal{U}^{k-i}\left(Qe^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n-k}}\mathcal{U}^{n-k}P\right)S\mathcal{L}_{2\beta}^{i}Td\mu\\
=&\int_{\Sigma^{+}}Qe^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n-k}}\mathcal{U}^{n-k}P\mathcal{L}_{\beta}^{k-i}\left(S\mathcal{L}_{2\beta}^{i}T\right)d\mu\\
=&\int_{\Sigma^{+}}P\mathcal{L}_{\beta}^{n-k}\left[Q\mathcal{L}_{\beta}^{k-i}\left(S\mathcal{L}_{2\beta}^{i}T\right)\right]d\mu.\end{split}

Then, Proposition
6.2
and (
46
) imply

\begin{split}|J_{nki}|\leq&|P|_{\infty}\left|\mathcal{L}_{\beta}^{n-k}\left[Q\mathcal{L}_{\beta}^{k-i}\left(S\mathcal{L}_{2\beta}^{i}T\right)\right]\right|_{\infty}\leq Cr^{n-k}|P|_{\infty}\left\|Q\right\|_{\vartheta}\left\|\mathcal{L}_{\beta}^{k-i}\left(S\mathcal{L}_{2\beta}^{i}T\right)\right\|_{\vartheta}\\
\leq&C^{2}r^{n-i}|P|_{\infty}\left\|Q\right\|_{\vartheta}\left\|S\right\|_{\vartheta}\left\|\mathcal{L}_{2\beta}^{i}T\right\|_{\vartheta}\leq C^{3}r^{n}|P|_{\infty}\left\|Q\right\|_{\vartheta}\left\|S\right\|_{\vartheta}\|T\|_{\vartheta}.\end{split}

∎

6.2.2
Decay of correlations and Lemma
5.5
for two-sided shifts

To go from the one-sided shift to the two-sided shift, we proceed in two steps. We first consider observables defined in
\Sigma=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
but
\beta
still defined in
\Sigma^{+}
(i.e depending only on future symbols). Then, in the second step we consider also a general
\beta:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Fix
\vartheta\in(0,1)
and consider the map
\sigma_{\beta}
in (
91
) where
\beta:\Sigma^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\vartheta
-Hölder function. Assume that
\sigma_{\beta}
satisfies (
48
). Then, there exist constants
r\in(0,1)
and

C>0
such that, for any
n,k,i\in\mathbb{N}
,
n>k>i
and any
\vartheta
-Hölder observables
P,Q,S,T:\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
, the following is satisfied,

\begin{split}\left|\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}(\omega)}P\left(\sigma^{n}\omega\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{k}(\omega)}Q\left(\sigma^{k}\omega\right)S\left(\omega\right)\right)\right|&\leq C\,\|P\|_{\vartheta}\,\|Q\|_{\vartheta}\,\|S\|_{\vartheta}\ r^{n}\\
\left|\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}(\omega)}P\left(\sigma^{n}\omega\right)Q\left(\sigma^{k}\omega\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}(\omega)}S\left(\sigma^{i}\omega\right)T(\omega)\right)\right|&\leq C\,\|P\|_{\vartheta}\,\|Q\|_{\vartheta}\,\|S\|_{\vartheta}\,\|T\|_{\vartheta}\ r^{n}.\end{split}

Proof.

As already mentioned in the proof of Lemma
6.3
, the first statement is a direct consequence of the second one taking
Q=1
. Therefore, we just have to prove the second one.

To prove the second statement, we proceed as in the proof of Lemma 4.9 in
[
16
]
(see also
[
36
]
). The first step is to consider slightly modified observables
P_{j}
,
Q_{j}
,
S_{j}
,
T_{j}
which depend only on coordinates
\omega_{l}
with
|l|\leq j
and satisfy

|P_{j}|_{\infty}\leq|P|_{\infty}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|P-P_{j}|_{\infty}\leq\|P\|_{\vartheta}\,\vartheta^{k}

(95)

(and analogously for the other observables, its construction is explained in
[
16
]
).

Then, we write

J_{nki}=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}(\omega)}P\left(\sigma^{n}\omega\right)Q\left(\sigma^{k}\omega\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}(\omega)}S\left(\sigma^{i}\omega\right)T(\omega)\right)=\int_{\Sigma}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}}P\circ\sigma^{n}Q\circ\sigma^{k}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}}S\circ\sigma^{i}Td\mu

(96)

as

\begin{split}J_{nki}=&\int_{\Sigma}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}}(P-P_{j})\circ\sigma^{n}Q\circ\sigma^{k}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}}S\circ\sigma^{i}Td\mu\\
&+\int_{\Sigma}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}}P_{j}\circ\sigma^{n}(Q-Q_{j})\circ\sigma^{k}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}}S\circ\sigma^{i}Td\mu\\
&+\int_{\Sigma}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}}P_{j}\circ\sigma^{n}Q_{j}\circ\sigma^{k}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}}(S-S_{j})\circ\sigma^{i}Td\mu\\
&+\int_{\Sigma}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}}P_{j}\circ\sigma^{n}Q_{j}\circ\sigma^{k}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}}S_{j}\circ\sigma^{i}(T-T_{j})d\mu\\
&+\int_{\Sigma}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}}P_{j}\circ\sigma^{n}Q_{j}\circ\sigma^{k}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}}S_{j}\circ\sigma^{i}T_{j}d\mu.\end{split}

Taking into account (
95
), the term in the first line can be estimated as

\begin{split}\left|\int_{\Sigma}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}}(P-P_{j})\circ\sigma^{n}Q\circ\sigma^{k}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}}S\circ\sigma^{i}Td\mu\right|&\leq\left|(P-P_{j})\circ\sigma^{n}\right|_{\infty}\left|Q\circ\sigma^{k}\right|_{\infty}\left|S\circ\sigma^{i}\right|_{\infty}\left|T\right|_{\infty}\\
&\leq\left\|P\right\|_{\vartheta}\left|Q\right|_{\infty}\left|S\right|_{\infty}\left|T\right|_{\infty}\vartheta^{j}\\
&\leq\left\|P\right\|_{\vartheta}\left\|Q\right\|_{\vartheta}\left\|S\right\|_{\vartheta}\left\|T\right\|_{\vartheta}\vartheta^{j}.\end{split}

Proceeding analogously, one can estimate also the terms in the second, third and fourth lines and one obtains the same upper bound. Therefore

\left|J_{nki}\right|\leq 4\left\|P\right\|_{\vartheta}\left\|Q\right\|_{\vartheta}\left\|S\right\|_{\vartheta}\left\|T\right\|_{\vartheta}\vartheta^{j}+\left|\widehat{J}_{nki}\right|,

(97)

where

\widehat{J}_{nki}=\int_{\Sigma}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}}P_{j}\circ\sigma^{n}Q_{j}\circ\sigma^{k}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}}S_{j}\circ\sigma^{i}T_{j}d\mu.

We now estimate this term. Since the measure
\mu
is
\sigma
-invariant,

\widehat{J}_{nki}=\int_{\Sigma}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}\circ\sigma^{j}}P_{j}\circ\sigma^{n+j}Q_{j}\circ\sigma^{k+j}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}\circ\sigma^{j}}S_{j}\circ\sigma^{i+j}T_{j}\circ\sigma^{j}d\mu.

Note that

e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n+j}}=e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}\circ\sigma^{j}}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{j}}=e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{j}\circ\sigma^{n}}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}},

which implies that

e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}\circ\sigma^{j}}=e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{j}\circ\sigma^{n}}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\beta_{j}}.

We use this identity to rewrite
\widehat{J}_{nki}
as

\widehat{J}_{nki}=\int_{\Sigma}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}}\widehat{P}_{j}\circ\sigma^{n}\widehat{Q}_{j}\circ\sigma^{k}e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{i}}\widehat{S}_{j}\circ\sigma^{i}\widehat{T}_{j}d\mu,

where

\widehat{P}_{j}=e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{j}}P_{j}\circ\sigma^{j},\quad\widehat{Q}_{j}=Q_{j}\circ\sigma^{j},\quad\widehat{S}_{j}=e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{j}}S_{j}\circ\sigma^{j},\quad\widehat{T}_{j}=e^{-2\mathrm{i}\beta_{j}}T_{j}\circ\sigma^{j}.

Note that, by construction, these observables only depend on future variables. That is, they are defined on
\Sigma^{+}
. Then, we can apply Lemma
6.3
, which implies

\left|\widehat{J}_{nki}\right|\leq C\,|\widehat{P}_{j}|_{\infty}\,\|\widehat{Q}_{j}\|_{\vartheta}\,\|\widehat{S}_{j}\|_{\vartheta}\,\|\widehat{T}_{j}\|_{\vartheta}\ r^{n}\leq C\,|P_{j}|_{\infty}\,\|\widehat{Q}_{j}\|_{\vartheta}\,\|\widehat{S}_{j}\|_{\vartheta}\,\|\widehat{T}_{j}\|_{\vartheta}\ r^{n}.

Now we bound each term in the right hand side. We have that
|P_{j}|_{\infty}\leq|P|_{\infty}
by (
95
). For the other terms, proceeding as in
[
16
]
, one can see that

\|\widehat{Q}_{j}\|_{\vartheta}\leq C_{2}|Q|_{\infty}\vartheta^{-2j}

for some
C_{2}\geq 1
which only depends on
\vartheta
and
\beta
(but is independent of the observable).

Then, one obtains

\left|\widehat{J}_{nki}\right|\leq CC_{2}^{3}\,|P|_{\infty}\,|Q|_{\infty}\,|S|_{\infty}\,|T|_{\infty}\ r^{n}\vartheta^{-6j}.

This estimate and (
97
) lead to the following bound for
J_{nki}
(see (
96
)),

\left|J_{nki}\right|\leq C\left\|P\right\|_{\vartheta}\left\|Q\right\|_{\vartheta}\left\|S\right\|_{\vartheta}\left\|T\right\|_{\vartheta}\left(\vartheta^{j}+r^{n}\vartheta^{-6j}\right)

for some
C>0
independent of the observables. Therefore to complete the proof it is enough to define
\nu>1
such that
r^{\nu}=\vartheta
and choose
j=\lfloor n/7\nu\rfloor
.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the second statement in Theorem
5.4
. We now consider the map
\sigma_{\beta}
in (
91
) satisfying (
48
), where now
\beta:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\vartheta
-Hölder function (see (
45
)) defined on
\Sigma=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. The first step is to perform a change of coordinates so that
\beta
depends only on future symbols (that is, defined on
\Sigma^{+}
). The following lemma is proven in
[
36
]
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Fix
\vartheta\in(0,1)
and consider a
\vartheta
-Hölder function

. Then, there exists
\vartheta^{1/2}
-Hölder functions

We use this lemma to rewrite the left hand side of (

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{nk}&=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{n}(\omega)}P\left(\sigma^{n}\omega\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\beta_{k}(\omega)}Q\left(\sigma^{k}\omega\right)\right)\\

6.2.3
Central Limit Theorem

To prove Item 3 of Theorem
5.4
, we rely again on the Lie Group extension setting of
[
16
]
and also on the approach in
[
24
]
to prove Central Limit Theorems for “weakly dependent” random variables.

For
\beta:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
, we consider the associated map

\sigma_{\beta}(\omega,g)=(\sigma\omega,gh(\omega)),

(98)

where
h:\Sigma\to G
is

h(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\beta(\omega)&\sin\beta(\omega)\\
-\sin\beta(\omega)&\cos\beta(\omega)\end{pmatrix},

(99)

and
G=G_{1}
is the Lie group introduced in (
89
).

We consider equivariant observables in
F_{\theta}(\Sigma\times\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R}^{n})
, that is observables of the form

\phi(\omega,g)=gV(\omega).

We fix
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and consider the sequence of random variables

X_{j}=c^{T}gh_{j}V\circ\sigma^{j},\qquad h_{j}(\omega)=h(\omega)h(\sigma\omega)\ldots h(\sigma^{j-1}\omega).

(100)

Without loss of generality, we can take
c=(1,0)^{T}
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Assume that the map
\sigma_{\beta}
in (
98
) satisfies (
48
). Assume also that, for any
g\in G
, the random variables
X_{j}
satisfy that

\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}X_{j}=0.

Then,

•

The variance

\begin{split}\bm{\sigma}^{2}=&\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(V_{1}^{2}(\omega)+V_{2}^{2}(\omega)\right)\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re\,}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left((V_{1}(\sigma^{j}\omega)-\mathrm{i}V_{2}(\sigma^{j}\omega)(V_{1}(\omega)+\mathrm{i}V_{2}(\omega)))e^{\mathrm{i}\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\beta_{\sigma^{k}\omega}}\right)\end{split}

(101)

is well defined.

•

Assume that
\bm{\sigma}^{2}\neq 0
. Then, for any fixed
g\in G
, the sequence

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{j}

converges weakly to a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{\sigma}^{2})
as
N\to+\infty
.

Note that the first item of the proposition is a direct consequence of Item 2 of Theorem
5.4
. We devote the rest of this section to prove the second item of
the proposition. The first step is to apply a transformation so that the observable and the matrix
h
only depend on the past symbols. To this end, we define

\Sigma^{-}=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{-}}.

Lemma 6.7
.

Fix
\vartheta\in(0,1)
and consider
\vartheta
-Hölder functions
h:\Sigma\to G
of the form (
99
) and
V:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and an equivariant observable
\phi(\omega,g)=gV(\omega)
. Then, there exists
\vartheta^{1/2}
-Hölder functions
\widetilde{h},\widehat{M}:\Sigma^{-}\to G
(with
\widetilde{h}
given by a function
\widetilde{\beta}:\Sigma^{-}\to\mathbb{R}
as in (
99
)) and
W,\chi:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
such that

h=\widehat{M}\widetilde{h}(\widehat{M}\circ\sigma)^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\widehat{M}}^{-1}V=W+\chi-\widetilde{h}\chi\circ\sigma.

Moreover,
\sigma_{\beta}
satisfies (
48
) if and only if
\sigma_{\widetilde{\beta}}
satisfies (
48
).

The proof of this lemma can be found in
[
36
]
.

If we define the observable

\psi(\omega,g)=gW(\omega),

it can be easily seen (see
[
16
]
) that the Birkhoff sums

\phi_{N}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\phi\circ\sigma_{h}^{j},\qquad\psi_{N}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\psi\circ\sigma_{\widetilde{h}}^{j},

satisfy

\phi_{N}=M\psi_{N}+\mathcal{O}(1)\qquad\text{where}\quad M(\omega,g)=g\widehat{M}(\omega)g^{-1}.

Moreover, since
M\in G
, the covariant matrices

\Sigma_{\phi}=\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(\phi_{N}\phi_{N}^{T}),\qquad\Sigma_{\psi}=\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(\psi_{N}\phi_{N}^{T})

satisfy

\Sigma_{\phi}=\Sigma_{\psi}.

Note that the variance
\bm{\sigma}^{2}
in (
101
) is just
\bm{\sigma}^{2}=c^{T}\Sigma_{\phi}c
with
c=(1,0)^{T}
.

In
[
16
]
it is also proven the following lemma.

Lemma 6.8
.

The sequences
\{M\phi_{N},N\geq 1\}
and
\{\phi_{N},N\geq 1\}
have the same joint probability distribution.

The results above imply that proving a central limit theorem for the sequence
X_{j}=c^{T}\phi_{j}
in (
100
) with convergence to a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{\sigma}^{2})
with the variance in (
101
) is equivalent to proving it for the sequence
\widetilde{X}_{j}=c^{T}\psi_{j}
with the same limiting distribution.

From now on, we just deal with
\{\widetilde{X}_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
. Note that the random variables
\widetilde{X}_{j}
only depend on the symbols
\{\omega_{k}\}_{k\leq j}
. To simplify the notation, we drop the tildes in
X_{j}
.

To prove the central limit theorem for this sequence, we split the sum
S_{n}=X_{1}+\ldots+X_{n}
as follows. For fixed
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
S_{n}
can be written
as

S_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\xi_{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\eta_{j},

where

\displaystyle\xi_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=jp+jq+1}^{(j+1)p+jq}X_{m},

for

\displaystyle\,\,0\leq j\leq k-1

(102)

\displaystyle\eta_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=(j+1)p+jq+1}^{(j+1)p+(j+1)q}X_{m},

for

\displaystyle\,\,0\leq j\leq k-1

(103)

\displaystyle\eta_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=kp+kq+1}^{n}X_{m}.

(104)

That is, any two random variables
\xi_{i},\xi_{j},i\neq j
, are “separated” by
one variable
\eta_{j}
containing
q
terms.
We choose the parameters
p
and
q
as

p\sim n^{\tau}\qquad\text{and}\qquad q\sim\log^{2}n,

(105)

for
\tau\in(0,1)
to be chosen later. Note that
k
satisfies

k\sim\frac{n}{p}\sim n^{1-\tau}.

We first compute the variance of the random variable
S_{n}
. Recall that
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}S_{n}=0
. Then,

\bm{\sigma}_{n}^{2}=V(S_{n})=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(S_{n}^{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(X_{i}^{2})+2\sum_{i<j}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(X_{i}X_{j}).

(106)

Note that, by Theorem
5.4
, there exists
0<\bm{\sigma}_{-}<\bm{\sigma}_{+}
such that

\bm{\sigma}_{-}^{2}n\leq V(S_{n})\leq\bm{\sigma}_{+}^{2}n

Moreover,

\lim_{n\to\infty}V(S_{n})=\bm{\sigma}^{2},

where
\bm{\sigma}^{2}
is the variance introduced in (
101
).

We prove the Central Limit Theorem by the characteristic functions method. To this end, we have to show that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}tS_{n}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}=e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}.

We prove that in several steps. We first state several technical lemmas (Lemmas
6.9
and
6.10
) analyzing
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}tS_{n}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}
. To this end, we define

\widetilde{S}_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\xi_{j}.

Note that
\widetilde{S}_{k}
is the sum of some of the terms in
S_{n}
.

Lemma 6.9
.

Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,

\left|\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}S_{n}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}-\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\widetilde{S}_{k}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}\right|=o(1)\text{ as }n\to+\infty.

Proof.

Since
k
satisfies
k\sim n^{1-\tau}
and
\eta_{j}
satisfies
|\eta_{j}|\lesssim\log^{2}n
, one has that

\left|e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}tS_{n}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}-e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\widetilde{S}_{k}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}\right|\leq\left|e^{\frac{it\widetilde{S}_{k}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}\left(e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}}-1\right)\right|\lesssim\frac{\log^{2}n}{n^{\tau}},

which implies the claim.
∎

Lemma 6.10
.

Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,

\left|\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\widetilde{S}_{k}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}\right)-\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\xi_{j}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}\right)\right|=o(1)\text{ as }n\to+\infty.

Proof.

We split

\mathcal{I}=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\widetilde{S}_{k}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}-\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\xi_{j}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}

as
\mathcal{I}=\sum_{j=2}^{k}\mathcal{I}_{j}
with

\mathcal{I}_{j}=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\widetilde{S}_{j}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}\prod_{\ell=j+1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\xi_{\ell}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}-\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\widetilde{S}_{j-1}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}\prod_{\ell=j}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\xi_{\ell}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}.

Note that

|\mathcal{I}_{j}|\leq\left|\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\widetilde{S}_{j}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}-\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\widetilde{S}_{j-1}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\xi_{j}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}\right|.

(107)

We estimate the right hand side by using conditioned
expectations. We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{j}
the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{\omega_{k}\}_{k\leq jp+jq}
. By Lemma
6.7
, both
\widetilde{h}
and the considered observables only depend on the past symbols. Therefore, the iterates

X_{1},\ldots X_{jp+jq}
are functions of
\{\omega_{k}\}_{k\leq jp+jq}
.
This implies

\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(f(\xi_{\ell})|\mathcal{F}_{j-1})=f(\xi_{\ell})\qquad\text{for}\qquad\ell\leq j-1.

Then, recalling
\widetilde{S}_{j}=\widetilde{S}_{j-1}+\xi_{j}
, we rewrite the right hand side
in (
107
) as

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\widetilde{S}_{j}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}-\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\widetilde{S}_{j-1}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\xi_{j}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}=&\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(\left.e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\widetilde{S}_{j}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}\right|\mathcal{F}_{j-1}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\widetilde{S}_{j-1}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t\xi_{j}}{\bm{\sigma}_{n}}}\right)\\

Appendix A
Wisdom mechanism

The first explanation of formation of the Kirkwood gaps was
proposed by Wisdom
[
41
]
and
Neishtadt
[
33
]
(see also
[
32
]
). Suppose that the mass ratio between Jupiter and the Sun and
eccentricity of Jupiter satisfy

\frac{\sqrt{\mu}}{e_{0}}\ \ll\ 1,

then the dynamics in the mean motion resonances of the restricted
planar 3-body problem can be described by a system with
three time scales: fast, intermediate and slow. According to Wisdom
in the main approximation, the secular evolution of the asteroid motion
near the 3:1 mean motion resonance with Jupiter is described by the canonical equations

\dot{\varphi}=\{\varphi,H_{P}\},\qquad\dot{\Phi}=\{\Phi,H_{P}\},\qquad\dot{x}=\{y,H_{P}\},\qquad\dot{y}=\{x,H_{P}\}.

Here
\varphi
is the critical angle (three times the mean longitude
of Jupiter minus the mean longitude of the asteroid),
\Phi
is some
function of the asteroid’s semimajor axis
a
,
x
and
y
are
proportional to the Laplace vector components

x\approx a^{1/4}\left(\frac{\mathbf{e}}{e_{0}}\right)\cos\hat{g},\qquad x\approx a^{1/4}\left(\frac{\mathbf{e}}{e_{0}}\right)\sin\hat{g},

where
\hat{g}
is the argument of the asteroid perihelion.

The Poisson brackets
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
in the above Hamiltonian
equations are defined in a way such that they split the components into slow and fast,

\{\varphi,\Phi\}=1,\qquad\{x,y\}=\varepsilon,\qquad\{\varphi,x\}=\{\varphi,y\}=\{\Phi,x\}=\{\Phi,y\}=0.

The averaged Hamiltonian
H_{P}
, given by Wisdom
[
41
]
,
can be rewritten as

H_{P}(\Phi,\varphi,x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\alpha\Phi^{2}+A(x,y)\cos\varphi+B(x,y)\sin\varphi+C(x,y),

where
A(x,y),B(x,y),C(x,y)
are quadratic polynomials in
x,y
(
see
[
32
]
).

Observe that the Hamiltonian
H_{P}
has two fast components

(\Phi,\varphi)
and two slow components
(x,y)
. If the fast
components are on a level set
E_{f}
not passing through its
equilibrium there is a second averaged system, giving a slow
Hamiltonian
H_{\text{slow}}(x,y,E_{f})
and describing an evolution
of the slow component.

It is, however, possible that the fast components
(\Phi,\varphi)
,
which are a pendulum, approach the critical level set and, as a result, approach
the saddle equilibrium. The set of values of
(x,y)
which correspond to this
phenomenon forms the so-called
uncertainty curve
\Gamma
(see the curve
in red in Figure
5
). Each time the slow component crosses

\Gamma
, there is a redistribution of slow and fast energy. See Figure
2

for the associate evolution of the eccentricity.

Figure 5
:
Slow evolution and uncertainty curve (see e.g.
[
30
]
).

On the plane of the slow component in Figure
5
, by definition,
the radial component corresponds to the rescaled eccentricity
of the asteroid. Crossing the “red” uncertainty curve can
lead to the slow component getting outside of the “central”
island and evolve in the exterior of it. This leads to a drastic
increase in eccentricity and to crossings with the orbit of Mars.

Even assuming that
\sqrt{\mu}/e_{0}
is small, numerical simulations indicate that the resonances 4:1, 5:2 and 7:3 are have mixed behavior, which includes chaotic regions (see
[
30
,
28
]
). In the chaotic regions, the asteroids in these mean motion resonances can reach
very large eccentricities on a “short” timescale:

0.6\cdot 10^{6}
and
19\cdot 10^{6}
for the 5:2 and 7:3 resonances respectively (see also
[
19
]
). However, it is hard to agree that these resonances are dominantly chaotic
as 7:3 has "plenty" of asteroids left (see Figure
1
). It is believed that in different gaps there are different
mechanisms of instabilities of the eccentricity (see
[
29
]
).

\nu_{i}=\prod_{k\leq n_{i}+\widetilde{n}_{\gamma}-1}b_{k}

defined on the space of sequences

X_{i}=\left\{\omega_{i}^{\leq}=\{\omega_{k}\}_{k\leq n_{i}+\widetilde{n}_{\gamma}-1},\omega_{k}\in\{0,1\}\right\}.

Note that

\nu_{i+1}=b_{n_{i+1}+\widetilde{n}_{\gamma}-1}\times\ldots b_{n_{i}+\widetilde{n}_{\gamma}}\times\nu_{i}.

We first prove the inductive statement for
j=0
. Note that

P_{0}=\mathrm{Prob}\left\{\left|M_{0}\right|\leq e_{0}^{\gamma-1}\right\},

which, relying on the random variable
\xi_{n}
introduced in (
112
), satisfies

P_{0}=\mathrm{Prob}\left\{\left|\xi_{\widetilde{n}_{\gamma}}\right|\leq 3\right\}.

Then, proceeding as in the proof of the first statement of Proposition
5.9
and using the Central Limit Theorems provided in Theorems
5.2
and
5.4
, there exists a constant
C\in(0,1)
such that, for
e_{0}>0
small enough,

P_{0}\leq C.

Now we assume that we have proven (
116
) for some
j=0,\ldots,n_{\gamma}-2
and we prove it for
j+1
.

Then, by conditioning the probability,

P_{j+1}=\int_{X_{i}}\mathrm{Prob}\left\{\left.\left|M_{i}\right|\leq e_{0}^{\gamma-1}\,\textrm{ for all }i=0,\dots,j+1\right|\omega_{i}^{\leq}\right\}d\nu_{j}.

Now, since
M_{i}
for
i=0,\dots,j
are independent of
\omega_{k}\in[n_{i}+\widetilde{n}_{\gamma},n_{i+1}+\widetilde{n}_{\gamma}-1]
, this probability can be written as

\begin{split}P_{j+1}=\int_{X_{i}}&\Bigg[\mathrm{Prob}\left\{\left.\left|M_{j+1}\right|\leq e_{0}^{\gamma-1}\,\textrm{ for all }i=0,\dots,j+1\right|\omega_{i}^{\leq}\right\}\\
&\times\mathrm{Prob}\left\{\left.\left|M_{i}\right|\leq e_{0}^{\gamma-1}\,\textrm{ for all }i=0,\dots,j\right|\omega_{i}^{\leq}\right\}\Bigg]d\nu_{j}.\end{split}

Now, proceeding as in the case
j=0
,

\mathrm{Prob}\left\{\left.\left|M_{j+1}\right|\leq e_{0}^{\gamma-1}\,\textrm{ for all }i=0,\dots,j+1|\right|\omega_{i}^{\leq}\right\}\leq C

and therefore

P_{j+1}=CP_{j},

which completes the proof of the inductive statement (
116
).

Now, since
n_{\delta}=[e_{0}^{-\delta}]
, (
116
) implies the second statement of Proposition
5.9
.
