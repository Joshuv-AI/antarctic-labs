A versatile method for accurately predicting electronic absorption spectra of tetrapyrrole macrocycles
Authors: Guofan Zhang, Xuanzhi Wang, Qirui Sun, Hanchi Zhao, Jiaao Han, Haoyuan Yang, Hanyu Wang, Chengshu Tian, Aizhen Kuang, Chulong Zhang, Tianhao Li, Zijing Wang, Pengzhan Guo, Rui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the UV-Vis-NIR electronic spectra of tetrapyrrole macrocycles (porphyrins, chlorins, and bacteriochlorins) is crucial to rational designs of novel functional molecules and materials for energy conversion, catalysis, bio-imaging, and medical applications. Despite this, no methods have been defined to enable high throughput screening for a large body of tetrapyrrole molecules (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$>10^3$$\end{document}) in a short-time period and accurately predict their electronic absorption spectra. Traditional time dependent-density functional theory (TD-DFT) method is cheap for single molecule simulation, but it can be expensive and time-consuming for massive numbers of molecules . Using a combination of computational chemistry, Gouterman four-orbital model, and machine learning methods, we show accurate predictions of the electronic absorption spectra of tetrapyrroles bearing various peripheral substituents across the UV-Vis-NIR spectral regions. Optimized AutoGluon pipelines trained on the full data set achieve high predictive fidelity (R2 = 0.985 validation, 0.936 test; mean/median errors < 0.1 eV) and generalized well across the metal-free porphyrin, chlorin, and bacteriochlorin scaffolds represented in this study. Together, this work establishes an efficient (cost and time), scalable, and mechanistically grounded framework for predicting tetrapyrrole electronic absorption spectra, offering broad utilities in rational designs of tetrapyrrole-based functional materials for real-world applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55436-6.

Introduction

Natural occurring tetrapyrroles are vital to everyday life1,2. For example, Chlorophyll a and Bacteriochlorophyll a are responsible for native oxygenic and anoxygenic photosynthesis, respectively3,4. Heme b involves in oxygen-storage/transport, electron transport and various catalytic activities5. Due to their unique spectral features (broad spectral coverage across UV-Vis-NIR with exceptionally high molar absorptivity, 50,000 - 400,000 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M^{-1}cm^{-1}$$\end{document} for native and synthetic tetrapyrrole Soret bands6) and highly tunable excited-state properties7, tetrapyrroles especially porphyrins have been widely used in solar energy conversion8,9, photoredox catalysis10,11, imaging12,13 material sciences14,15, and photodynamic therapy16,17. These native tetrapyrroles share similar structural and conjugation patterns as shown in Fig. 1. Synthetic analogues of these tetrapyrroles, namely porphyrin, chlorin, and bacteriochlorin, stand for heme, chlorophyll, and bacteriochlorophyll benchmark compounds, respectively6.Fig. 1Representative structures of native tetrapyrroles and synthetic benchmarks.

Electronic absorption properties are central to tetrapyrrole macrocycles because their biological functions and other applications are governed by how efficiently they harvest and transfer absorbed solar energy. For example, chlorophylls and bacteriochlorophylls that play key roles in oxygenic and anoxygenic photosynthesis differ in their electronic absorption energies: chlorophylls absorb strongly in the red spectrum region, whereas bacteriochlorophylls cover more into the NIR region. Chlorophyll-based oxygenic photosynthesis uses higher-energy visible photons to generate strong oxidizing equivalents capable of water oxidation, whereas bacteriochlorophyll-based anoxygenic photosynthetic organisms utilize lower-energy red/NIR photons and therefore relies on more easily oxidized electron donors.Fig. 2A highlights the UV-Vis-NIR electronic absorption spectra of native hematin, chlorophyll, and bacteriochlorophyll molecules. The electronic spectra consist of strong transitions (high oscillator strength) in the blue-violet domain that represent the B (Soret) bands and weaker transitions at lower energy region(longer wavelengths), which are the Q bands that feature transitions originating from the ground state (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_0$$\end{document}) to the lowest energy singlet excited state (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_1$$\end{document}). Note that, compared to porphyrin (macrocycle core for heme), chlorin and bacteriochlorin exhibit diminished oscillator strength for the B bands, while the Q bands become intense and show significant bathochromic shift18,19. The peripheral substituents on the macrocycles will give rise to substantial alterations in the electronic spectra20,21. For example, different R groups (methyl vs. formyl) that distinguish Chlorophyll a from Chlorophyll b (Figure 1) afford a substantial difference in their electronic spectra in terms of transition energies and intensities (Fig. 2). Therefore, understanding and predicting how substituents affect the spectral features as well as the excited-state properties (lifetimes, quantum yields of luminescence, and redox potentials) of these tetrapyrroles are important, as these properties lay foundations for rational designs of novel tetrapyrrole-based functional materials for real-world applications. Experimental synthesis and characterization libraries of new tetrapyrroles are time-consuming and costive, while conventional excited-state calculations can also become computationally expensive when dealing with large numbers of complex tetrapyrroles. Therefore, reliable pre-screening and high-throughput spectral prediction enable an efficient route to target and identify promising candidates before actual synthesis. Such approach prioritizes structures with target absorption windows, and guide molecular design for application-specific photophysical performance of the molecules. In order to perform a pre-screening on the spectral properties of a large body of novel designed tetrapyrrole molecules with distinct peripheral substituents and functional groups for the aforementioned applications, a method that is capable of high throughput screening and predicting the electronic absorption spectra with high-accuracy at a low cost is critical. Traditional computational methods based on density functional theory (DFT) and time dependent-density functional theory (TD-DFT) have been routinely applied to calculate the spectral and excited-state properties of molecules. However, TD-DFT calculations for massive numbers of complicated molecules like tetrapyrrole macrocycles can be expensive and time-consuming, therefore the scalability based on TD-DFT is limited for high-throughput property predictions. In recent years, there have been pioneering work and significant advances in data-driven methodologies for predicting molecular spectra and properties22–33. However, to our best knowledge, there are only a few reports on predicting the HOMO-LUMO gaps for porphyrins34, and there is no such direct prediction methods for electronic absorption spectra of tetrapyrrole macrocycles with high accuracy at low cost. Gouterman pioneered a four orbital model35,38–41(the Gouterman model) to understand the electronic absorption spectra of porphyrins; the Gouterman model enables understandings of the energetics and intensities of the electronic transitions of tetrapyrrole macrocycles (vide infra). Based on the model, with the energies of the four specific frontier molecular orbitals calculated from DFT, Du et al.37 simulated the electronic absorption spectra of tetrapyrroles from the linear combinations of one-electron excitations from HOMO-1 (H-1) and HOMO (H) to LUMO (L) and LUMO+1 (L+1) (Fig. 2B). This method gives simulated spectra that are comparable to the experimental spectra, often outperforming those calculated from TD-DFT37. This important work with the elegant mathemetical transformations of numbers to spectra paved the way for data-driven method for predicting tetrapyrrole spectra based on the energies of the four orbitals.

In this work, based on the pioneering study by Gouterman38,39,42, Michl36,43,44, Lindsey, Bocian and Holten et al.37,44–54, we construct the Tetrapyrrole MO Database (TMODB), that includes tetrapyrrole compounds combining structurally distinct porphyrins, chlorins, and bacteriochlorins and represent the molecules in computationally interpretable chemical formats. With extensive DFT calculations, we gathered the energies of the four orbitals described in the Gouterman model for each molecule within the database. Building on this resource, we then introduced an integrated ML framework designed to predict the energies of the four orbitals (H-1, H, L, and L+1) and reconstruct the electronic absorption spectra of the tetrapyrrole molecules of interest. The TMODB pipeline is designed to process molecular data by generating fingerprints from SMILES and combining DFT optimized geometry and orbital energy properties to predict the electronic absorption spectra of tetrapyrrole molecules (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Workflow of the proposed method for predicting electronic spectra of tetrapyrroles.

Method

DFT calculation methods

DFT calculations were conducted using the polarizable continuum model (PCM) with toluene as solvent implemented in Gaussian 16 A.0355 The MMFF9456 pre-optimized tetrapyrrole molecules were subjected to full optimization with the B3LYP/6-31G(d) level of theory57–62. The molecular orbital (MO) energy and isosurface were were derived from the optimized structures.

The present TMODB training and prediction datasets are restricted to metal-free tetrapyrrole macrocycles. Metal-coordinated porphyrin, chlorin, and bacteriochlorin complexes were not included in the model training, validation, testing, or spectral prediction analyses reported in this study. Accordingly, the DFT protocol and the subsequent Gouterman based machine learning framework are designed to describe ligand centered \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\pi }$$\end{document} orbital energetics in metal-free tetrapyrrole systems. The resulting model should therefore not be regarded as directly applicable to metallotetrapyrroles, for which metal-centered d orbitals, metal-to-ligand and ligand to metal charge transfer states, spin state effects, and metal-dependent relativistic or dispersion contributions may play significant roles. Extending this framework to metal-coordinated tetrapyrroles will require the construction of a dedicated metallotetrapyrrole dataset, the use of metal-appropriate basis sets or effective core potentials, and the incorporation of additional descriptors or correction terms that explicitly capture metal-ligand electronic interactions. This extension constitutes an important direction for future development.

Training protocols

The dataset was randomly partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets in a 70:15:15 ratio using a fixed random seed (42) to ensure reproducibility. Within the training pool, primary model selection was conducted under a 5-fold cross-validation regime, with stratification applied through target binning to preserve the distribution of orbital energy values. Fold assignment was performed at the molecular identity level to prevent data leakage among conformers or tautomers of the same compound.

Molecular structures were represented as extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFP6; radius = 3, 2,048 bits) generated with RDKit, with sanitization, valence checks, and aromaticity perception enforced. Molecules that failed parsing were excluded from further analysis.

Hyperparameter optimization was performed automatically using AutoGluon’s internal Bayesian scheduler under the best quality preset, with training budgets capped at 1 h per run. Early stopping was applied for iterative learners to prevent overfitting. Bagging (five folds, one set) and up to two levels of stacking were enabled to construct ensemble predictors.

Model performance was evaluated with mean absolute error (MAE) as the primary regression metric, and coefficient of determination (R2) reported as a secondary indicator of goodness of fit. Final predictors were retrained on the combined training and validation sets, and their generalization performance was assessed on the untouched 15% test set.

All experiments were conducted on a Linux cluster (Ubuntu 20.04, CUDA 11.7) equipped with NVIDIA L20 GPUs and Intel Xeon CPUs. Random seeds were fixed across preprocessing, model training, and evaluation stages to ensure reproducibility.

Results and discussion

Construction of the TMODB

A curated dataset of 1,437 tetrapyrrole molecules with diverse substituents was compiled to enable ML prediction of electronic absorption spectra with the associated H-1, H, L, and L+1 orbital energies defined within the framework of the Gouterman model35. Each molecule is represented by its canonical SMILES63 notation and is annotated with the energies of the H-1, H, L, and L+1 orbitals computed using DFT. The dataset integrates 396 characterized tetrapyrrole molecules extracted from the PhotochemCAD database (with molecular orbital energies computed at the same level of theory and solvent compared to TMODB)2,46,64–66, alongside 161 porphyrins, 500 chlorins, and 380 bacteriochlorins generated via a fully automated Python-based molecular design pipeline to further enhance structural diversity.The PhotochemCAD derived component is referred to as the PC subset in the following analyses. This subset was constructed by including all PhotochemCAD MO database entries for which B3LYP computed Gouterman four-orbital energies were available. These four orbital-energy fields correspond to the two occupied and two unoccupied tetrapyrrole π orbitals required for the Gouterman spectral reconstruction. The PC subset therefore serves as an external benchmark like reference set derived from curated PhotochemCAD molecular-orbital data, rather than as a manually selected family of a single tetrapyrrole subclass37. To evaluate the effects of various substituents on the electronic absorption spectra of the tetrapyrrole molecules, all generated molecules were classified into three categories according to the electronic characteristics of their peripheral substituting R groups: (1) electron-donating groups (EDGs), (2) electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs), and (3) extended π-conjugated systems (Conjs). Representative chemical structures for each category consist of common substituents such as alkyl, amino, nitro, cyano, and aromatic moieties (Figure S1).

Mapping and stratification of chemical space via dimensionality reduction

The database was divided into training, validation, and test subsets in a 70/15/15 (%) ratio to ensure an unbiased representation of molecular diversity. To assess the spatial uniformity of the database and explore the intrinsic distribution of the molecular features, the uniform aggregation and projection of the multiplicity (UMAP) method was used to project all molecules into a latent two-dimensional space (Fig. 4)67. The projection arrives at a distinct separation value x = 5, which is then used to define three major clusters: Cluster A(beige, x łe 5), Cluster B (blue-gray, x > 5), and a unified cluster TMODB. UMAP organizes these high-dimensional descriptors such that structurally simple, compact molecules cluster fall within the x < 5 region, while more complexed molecules are mapped to the x > 5 region. Notably, molecules from the training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) sets occupy the overlapping regions between Cluster A and Cluster B. This overlap indicates that structurally similar molecules are distributed across all three partitions, rather than being confined to a single subset.Fig. 4UMAP projection of the molecular chemical space: training (70%, dusty-rose circles), validation (15%, pink squares), and test (15%, teal triangles).

Statistical analysis of orbital energy and substituent correlations

Cluster A is composed primarily of molecules bearing small substituents (average MW ≈ 494 Da), fewer aromatic substituents (≈ 3.2 per molecule), and limited substituent diversity, typically featuring substituents such as thiomethyl and alkynyl groups. In contrast, Cluster B contains molecules with larger substituents (average MW ≈ 692 Da), characterized by a higher number of aromatic substituents (e.g., phenyl, styryl, pyridyl ≈ 5.6 per molecule) and enriched in groups including halogen atoms (Cl, Br), hydroxyl, and amino groups. This distinction stems from how ECFP6 fingerprints68 capture molecular features depending on parameters such as atom connectivity, types and sizes of the different substituents. In addition to the global partitioning into Clusters A and Cluster B, localized regions with distinct density distributions are also identified (Figure S2). A densely populated subregion Cluster_1_Dense is found to be enriched in sulfur-containing heterocycles and alkynes. A sparsely distributed region Cluster_2_Sparse is dominated by halogen substituents and substituents bearing oxygen and nitrogen atoms. Despite the pronounced structural divergence between these subsets, analyses of frontier molecular orbital energies show a high degree of overlap in their H–L distributions (Figure S3). This observation indicates that the observed structural heterogeneity does not perturb the overall distributions of the frontier molecular orbital energies of the tetrapyrrole molecules (vide infra), underpinning the robustness and diversity of the database for ML prediction performance.

Fig. 5 describes the network representation of the correlations between substituent classes (EDGs, EWGs, and Conjs) and frontier orbital energies, including their energy gaps. The majority of the statistically significant relationships (p < 0.05) exhibit absolute Pearson correlation coefficients (PCCs) greater than 0.25, with several exceeding 0.5. The high PCCs demonstrate a strong correlation between the nature of the substituents and the corresponding molecular orbital energies, providing physically interpretable substituent–property relationships that permit successful predictive modeling. Fig. 5 right panel shows a correlation matrix, where the size and color of the circles indicate the magnitude and sign of PCCs. The network representation on the left highlights statistically significant relationships (p < 0.05), with edge colors and thickness encoding p-value ranges and correlation strengths. Correlation analysis reveals distinct relationships between substituent classes and orbital energy descriptors. EWGs exhibit stabilization of both the H–1 and H orbitals, accompanied by increases in the H–L and H–1–L gaps69,70. In contrast, EDGs are correlated with destabilization of the occupied orbitals, leading to a concomitant narrowing of these two energy gaps71,72. Conj substituents show complicated effects on the orbital energies where different substituents can alter orbital energetics in both directions. Model optimization through cluster-aware learning and multi-orbital regression

ML models were trained separately on Cluster A, Cluster B, and TMODB using the AutoGluon framework to ensure that the impact of different chemical clusterings on predictive performance can be assessed75. Model evaluation was conducted on both validation and test sets using five widely adopted performance metrics: (1) coefficient of determination (R2), (2) mean absolute error (MAE), (3) mean squared error (MSE), (4) root mean squared error (RMSE), and (5) mean relative error (MRE). As illustrated in Fig. 6 and Table 1, incorporating broader structural diversities improves predictive robustness and enhances transferability across the chemical space. The model trained on TMODB consistently outperforms those trained on individual clusters, achieving an R2 of 0.985 on the validation set and 0.936 on the test set. All models were derived from optimized AutoGluon pipelines that are specifically configured to minimize training time while reducing prediction error. Although AutoGluon is not inherently designed to handle multi-output regression tasks76, we modified the pipeline to enable single-orbital energy prediction for the four target orbitals by training separate models for each. A systematic model selection was then carried out to identify the optimal algorithm for each orbital based on both training time and predictive performance (Figure S3). To evaluate the physical interpretability of the ML model beyond numerical accuracy, we used the predicted frontier molecular orbital energy values to simulate the electronic absorption spectra of various tetrapyrrole molecules following the reported method37. Representative molecules were selected based on their R2 values between ML-predicted and DFT-computed orbital energies. Four molecules from the PC subset (Figure S5) are chosen to represent the top (highest R2), average, median, and lowest predictive performances. The simulated spectra derived from ML predictions are overlaid directly on those orbital energies computed using DFT (Fig. 10). For top-performing molecules, the predicted spectra are indistinguishable from their DFT counterparts, both in wavelength of the absorption maximums and in their corresponding oscillator strengths. For molecules with lower R2 scores, spectral deviations are observed, particularly in transition energy (wavelength) and intensities, though major absorptive features are preserved. Spectra simulations are also applied to Chl and BChl scaffolds (Fig. 10 and Figure S6) to assess group-specific spectral prediction fidelity. For each substituent class (EDGs, EWGs, and Conjs), we selected the molecules with the highest R2 value together with average, median, and low values of R2 within each family. With high-R2 values, the ML-derived spectra accurately capture the characteristic Q-band and B-band features of the tetrapyrrole molecules. However, in low-R2 examples, spectral deviations become noticeable, particularly in higher-energy transitions. Conclusion

In summary, this paper delineates the development of the TMODB, a large-scale and structurally diverse dataset comprising 1,437 porphyrins, chlorins, and bacteriochlorins annotated with DFT-calculated frontier molecular orbital energy. By categorizing molecules into electron-donating, electron-withdrawing, and π-conjugated substituent classes, we show that substituent types and patterns can be leveraged to regulate the structure–property relationships, leading to distinct interpretations of the electronic spectra of various tetrapyrrole macrocycles. Building on this dataset, we introduce a cluster-aware machine learning framework optimized through AutoGluon ensemble pipelines for multi-orbital regression that achieves high predictive fidelity (R2 = 0.985 for validation and 0.936 for test sets). The model underscores robust transferability across vast molecular scaffolds, with mean and median errors consistently below 0.1 eV. Electronic absorption spectra constructed from ML-predicted orbital energies using the Gouterman model are in close agreement with both experimental spectra and those simulated from DFT-calculated orbital energies using the same model, confirming that this framework achieves high quantitative predictive accuracy. The spectra of various tetrapyrroles simulated from this paper also outperform those from TD-DFT computations in terms of both accuracy and computational efficiency. Together, this work establishes a scalable, accurate, and mechanistically grounded strategy for predicting electronic properties of tetrapyrroles, offering broad utility in the rational design and high throughput screening of tetrapyrrole-based functional materials for photochemistry, imaging, catalysis, energy conversion, and medical applications.
