Genome instability is increased during DNA replication in the presence of the CPD UV photoproduct in transcriptionally active genes
Authors: Rika Kusumoto-Matsuo, Yusuke Matsuno, Naoki Akiba, Yuya Manaka, Jiro Toshima, Fumio Hanaoka, Ken-ichi Yoshioka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUltraviolet (UV) light primarily induces cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) and pyrimidine-pyrimidone (6-4) photoproducts (6-4 PPs) and increases the risk of melanoma developing with genome instability. Here, we show that DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) associated with genome instability arise during DNA replication in the presence of CPDs. Whereas 6-4PPs, which induce γH2AX expression and cell-cycle arrest, are repaired before the onset of DNA replication, CPDs persist into S phase. We also show that these DSBs are induced in association with transcription-coupled nucleotide excision repair (TC-NER). These DSBs decrease after CPDs were partially repaired in active chromatin regions, but persist in inactive chromatin regions. Structure variants (SVs) and single nucleotide variants (SNVs) associated with the UV-related single-base substitution 7 (SBS7) signature are induced in UV-irradiated cells. Our results point to a pathway that may underlie the genomic alterations observed in human melanoma.Subject areas: molecular biology, cell biology, cancer

Introduction

Exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light induces the cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer (CPD) and 6–4 PP photoproducts (PPs) and increases the risk of melanoma.1,2,3 Among the three types of UV irradiation (UV-A, -B, and -C), CPDs and 6-4 PPs are induced by short UV wavelength light (UV-B and -C).1 Conversely, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated by long UV wavelength light (UV-A and -B).4 UV PPs are usually repaired by either global genome nucleotide excision repair (GG-NER) or transcription-coupled nucleotide excision repair (TC-NER).5,6,7 The former targets helix distortions caused by the UV PPs and the latter is specifically initiated when these lesions block the progression of RNA polymerase II. Despite these repair systems, UV irradiation is still associated with an increased risk of melanoma that develops with genome instability. It is currently unclear how UV PPs contribute to genome instability.

Genome instability is a hallmark of cancer.8,9,10 As shown in an in vitro study using mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), genome instability is induced by replication stress-associated double-strand breaks (DSBs) and increases the risk of clonal evolution of cells with mutations in key components of tumor suppressor pathways, such as the ARF/p53 pathway.11,12,13 Structure variants (SVs) are massively induced in association with genome instability, and their formation correlates with the induction of single nucleotide variants (SNVs).11,12 Currently, it is unclear which mechanism is responsible for the correlation between SVs and SNVs. In fact, their induction pathways are mechanistically separated: while SVs are caused by erroneous repair of DSBs, SNVs are primarily induced by DNA polymerase errors. Importantly, however, a correlation between SVs and SNVs is widely observed in cancer cells.14 Supporting this argument, single-base substitution (SBS) signatures15 associated with genome instability are highly observed in regions close to SV sites, such as SBS3 (homologous recombination deficiency) and SBS2 and 13 (APOBEC mutational signatures).14 This indicates the importance of genome instability triggered by replication stress for the formation of both SVs and SNVs.

Genome instability is often induced by replication stress-associated DSBs that occur as a result of endogenous and exogenous factors. In the case of γ-ray irradiation, which causes multiple types of DNA damage including DSBs, DNA single-strand breaks, and ROS-associated nucleotide adducts,16,17,18,19,20 it has recently been shown that DSBs that lead to genomic instability are caused by replication stress during the following S phase after the DSBs directly induced by the irradiation have been repaired.12,21 By contrast, it remains unclear how UV damage causes genome instability. While NGS analyses revealed the UV-associated single base substitution 7 (SBS7) signature, which matches the SNVs induced by translesion DNA polymerase η at sites of UV PPs,22,23,24 it is still unclear how DSBs associated with SV induction are caused by PPs and which PPs are associated with SNV induction and DSB formation. In addition, although a previous study used CPD/6–4 PP photolyase technology showed that skin cancer is caused by CPDs but not by 6-4 PPs,25 it is still unclear what makes the difference in the effects of CPDs and 6-4 PPs.

The current study revealed that, although 6-4 PPs induce γH2AX foci formation and cell-cycle arrest and hence are repaired before the onset of DNA replication, CPDs do not. DSBs that contribute to genome instability arise during DNA replication in the presence of CPDs in association with TC-NER. These DSBs decrease after CPDs in transcriptionally active chromatin regions are repaired. UV-associated SNVs, identified as SBS7, can be induced in association with SVs. Our results suggest a mechanistic pathway for the induction of SVs and SNVs.

Results

Induction of genome instability and clonal evolution by UV-C irradiation

To study the effect of UV-induced damage on genome instability, we first tested the effects of UV on primary MEFs, because when these cells are cultured by the 3T3 protocol they undergo ARF/p53 pathway-dependent growth arrest,26 but subsequently immortalize in the presence of genome instability through the clonal evolution of immortalized cells mutated in components of the ARF/p53 pathway.11 In fact, such immortalization was previously shown by UV irradiation.27,28 After testing the effects of different UV irradiation conditions, we observed the generation of 6-4 PPs and CPDs (Figures S1A–S1C) and growth retardation with a dose of 8 J/m2 UV-C, resulting in MEF immortalization (Figure 1A). Importantly, the immortalization was significantly facilitated by the irradiation (Figure 1B). Chromosome number alteration and massive SV and SNV inductions were observed in the resulting MEFs regardless of UV irradiation (Figures 1C–1E and S1D). Within these SVs, translocation and inversion were the major events, with inversions increasing after irradiation (Figure 1E). To estimate the clonality status, we analyzed variant allele frequency (VAF) and compared the expected values of clonal evolution of single diploid and tetraploid MEFs and also for cases of clonal evolutions of multiple MEFs (Figure 1F). The VAF data matched the expected values for clonal evolutions of multiple MEFs. Together with chromosome number analyses showing two peaks (around 40 and 80), implying the presence of diploid and tetraploid cells regardless of irradiation (Figure 1C), these results suggest UV irradiation is not critical for determining ploidy status, but importantly indicate that UV irradiation facilitates the clonal evolution of cells with genome instability.

We next analyzed SBS signatures induced in the immortalized MEFs, in which those at around SV sites (<1 M base) were compared with those at other sites, because SNV induction is often affected by SVs. The UV-associated SBS7a signature was observed within 1 Mb of SV sites (Figure 1G), especially around translocation and inversion sites (Figure 1H), implying irradiation-associated induction of translocations as well as inversions. Thus, these results suggest that UV irradiation increases the risk of chromosomal translocation and inversion and of associated SBS7a-related SNVs.

UV-associated DSB formation increases the risk of genome instability

SVs are caused by erroneous end-joining of DSBs. To clarify the UV PPs that induce DSBs and genome instability, we next tested the statuses of 6–4 PP, CPD, γH2AX, and 53BP1 after UV-C irradiation (8 J/m2) in MEFs at passage 3 (Figures 2A and 2B). As previously reported, 6-4 PPs are repaired more rapidly than CPDs.29,30,31,32 Indeed, we observed that the 6–4 PP signal level decreased to baseline within 24 h, whereas approximately 50% of the CPD signal still remained even at 72 h after irradiation. We observed two types of γH2AX foci, i.e., 53BP1-independent γH2AX foci resulting probably from the generation of single-strand DNA (ssDNA), and merged γH2AX/53BP1 foci indicating DSB formation. The former were observed at 6 h after irradiation. The results suggested that 6-4 PPs are a major cause of the observed γH2AX foci, because these foci decayed at 24–48 h after irradiation, the same period as that of 6-4 PP-signal decay (Figure 2A). By contrast, the latter increased at 24 h after irradiation, especially in EdU-positive replicating cells (Figure 2C), the time at which CPDs were still high and the 6-4 PP signal had decayed (Figures 2A and S1B). Thus, these results indicate that UV-irradiated cells form DSBs during the following S phase in the presence of CPDs. In addition, such DSB formation was associated with phosphorylated RPA foci formation (Figure S2A) and R-loop (RNA-DNA hybrid) accumulation (Figure S2B), suggesting that the DSBs were associated with replication stress and transcription. However, such DSBs were decreased even in EdU-positive cells at 48–72 h after irradiation (Figures 2B and 2C), the period during which the CPD level was still high (Figure 2A), suggesting that only some CPDs were associated with DSB formation.

To examine the effect of UV-associated damage on genome instability, we analyzed micronuclei status (Figures 2D and S2C), because micronuclei initially arise with genome instability triggered by replication stress-associated DSBs33,34 and after UV irradiation.35 As expected, the micronuclei level was increased 24–48 h after irradiation. In addition, cGAS- and γH2AX-positive micronuclei, which initially appear in association with genome instability,12,33,34,36 were significantly increased 48 h after irradiation (Figure 2D). Together, the results suggest that genome instability is induced by UV irradiation in association with the DSB formation that occurs during DNA replication in the presence of CPDs.

DSB formation in the presence of CPDs after the repair of 6-4 PPs

The above results demonstrated that DSBs that can cause genome instability are induced in the presence of CPDs but not 6-4 PPs, raising the question of why CPDs induce these DSBs but not 6-4 PPs. Since the DSBs that can cause genome instability were induced in replicating cells (Figure 2C), we next tested the effects of UV PPs on the S phase entry. To this end, we used MCF7 cells as a model because they can be synchronized in M phase, enabling cell-cycle progression to be monitored from early G1 phase to S-phase entry. Cells were irradiated with UV-C (8 J/m2) at 2 h after release from M phase and DNA lesions were monitored from early G1 phase to S-phase entry, i.e., according to EdU-incorporation status (Figures 3A–3C and S3A–S3C). Cell-cycle progression was confirmed by determining chromosome copy number and the level of phosphorylated H3 at Ser10 (i.e., an M-phase marker) (Figure S3A). γH2AX foci independent of 53BP1 foci were observed in EdU-negative cells at 3 h after UV irradiation (Figure 3A: see a cell shown with a white arrowhead), as well as minor pre-existing γH2AX/53BP1 foci (Figure 3A: see red arrowheads). The γH2AX foci that appeared at 3 h after the irradiation were suppressed by an ATR inhibitor (Figure S3D). Importantly, S-phase entry was delayed in the presence of γH2AX foci (Figure 3C). By contrast, 53BP1 foci were observed in replicating EdU-positive cells 6–18 h after UV irradiation (Figure S3C), a period during which the 6-4 PP signal decreased (Figure 3B). Together, these results suggest that 6-4 PPs are repaired before S-phase entry due to γH2AX expression-associated cell-cycle arrest. DSBs appeared during DNA replication after 6-4 PPs were repaired.

We next analyzed the CPD and γH2AX statuses of replicating cells. As expected, EdU-positive replicating cells were observed even among CPD-positive cells (Figures 3D and S4A). The γH2AX level was especially high in the CPD-positive replicating cells. In addition, γH2AX foci overlapped tightly with 53BP1-foci in UV-irradiated replicating cells (Figures 3E, 3F, and S4B–S4D). Together, these results show that DSBs that potentially cause genome instability occur when cells start DNA replication in the presence of CPDs.

Preferential repair of CPDs in active chromatin regions

Although DSBs that lead to genome instability are induced during DNA replication in the presence of CPDs, it is unclear how these DSBs are reduced even in the presence of CPDs at later timepoints (Figure 2). After DSB formation decreased, we observed localized CPD-signal decay in nuclei, suggesting that biased CPD repair had occurred in certain chromatin regions (Figure 4A: see the area indicated by yellow dotted lines and puncta indicated by white arrowheads). This localized CPD decay was significantly higher at later timepoints than at earlier timepoint (Figures 4B and S5A). In addition, persistent CPDs significantly accumulated in bright DAPI puncta associated with heterochromatin regions (Figures 4C and S5B). In fact, persistent CPDs, observed at 48 h after UV irradiation, were predominantly present in heterochromatin regions (Figure 4D: see HP1 and H3K9me3 signals). This was in contrast to active chromatin regions (Figure 4D: see H3K9ac signal). Consistently, merged γH2AX/53BP1 foci that appeared at 24 h after UV irradiation were strongly present outside of bright DAPI puncta (Figure 4E). Together, these results support our hypothesis that UV-associated DSBs are induced when CPDs occur in active chromatin regions.

Replication stress-associated DSB formation in association with TC-NER

Given that CPDs in transcriptionally active genes can be specifically targeted by TC-NER,5,37 DSB formation by CPDs could be induced in association with TC-NER. We next tested the effects of 5,6-dichlorobenzimidazole riboside (DRB), an RNA polymerase II inhibitor and knockdown (KD) of Cockayne syndrome type B (CSB), because TC-NER mediated by CSB is initiated by stalling of RNA polymerase II (polII) at CPD sites.5,6 We observed significant suppression of 53BP1/γH2AX foci by DRB and also by CSB-KD using multiple siRNAs (Figures 5A, 5B, and S6A). The level of RPA phosphorylated at Ser33 was also reduced after CSB-KD. These results indicate that the CPD-induced DSBS were produced in association with TC-NER. The results showing that CSB-KD decreases DSBs suggest that many DSBs are induced with the onset of TC-NER, possibly through TC-NER intermediates. However, this decrease in DSBs mediated by CSB-KD was only partial. In fact, a previous study reported that RNA polII can stall continuously at CPD sites in the absence of CSB38; therefore, stalled RNA polII might also cause replication stress by blocking replication fork progression. Thus, although DSBs might be caused by multiple pathways, those DSBs arise during DNA replication in the presence of CPDs in association with TC-NER. Importantly, the resulting micronuclei level was significantly reduced by DRB (Figure 5C). Together, those results suggest that formation of these DSBs is further associated with the risk of genome instability.

The above results indicated that DSBs that lead to genome instability are induced during DNA replication in association with CPD repair by TC-NER. This motivated us to test whether DSB formation can be decreased if there is enough time to repair such CPDs before S-phase entry. We compared the effects of UV irradiation 3 and 6 h after M-phase release (Figure S6B: see workflow) because CPD carryover into S phase is expected to occur more when UV is irradiated at 6 h than that at 3 h. As expected, γH2AX/53BP1 foci were significantly higher after UV irradiation at 6 h than that at 3 h (Figure S6B). In addition, the number of micronuclei was significantly higher in cells irradiated with UV at 6 h after M-phase release (Figure S6C). Together, our results indicated that UV-associated genome instability is induced when there is not enough time to repair CPDs in active genes before S-phase entry.

Relevance to the human melanoma genome

To test the relevance of our in vitro findings to human melanoma, SVs and SNVs in the human melanoma genome39 were analyzed. Based on SBS signatures, the human melanoma can be classified into cluster 1 (i.e., SNV high) and 2 (i.e., SV high) (Figures 6A and 6B). While the UV-associated SBS7a–d were the major signatures in cluster 1 and were rarely affected by SVs, SBS7a–d fractions in cluster 2 were minor and were often affected by SVs. Based on the SV dependence and SBS7 fractions, our in vitro model was similar to cluster 2. Since cells with genome instability often show a correlation between SNV induction and SVs,12,14 we next analyzed the association between SVs and SNVs in human melanoma and compared these associations with those in our in vitro model. We first counted the numbers of SNVs around SV sites and compared the number with that expected for a random distribution in human melanoma: SNVs around SV sites in cluster 2 were significantly higher than the random distribution, unlike those in cluster 1 (Figure 6C). Consistently with this, SNV induction was correlated with SVs in some instances in cluster 2, but not in cluster 1 (Figure S7). Correlations between SV and SNV formation were also observed in the MEF model (Figure 6D), supporting the similarity of our UV-irradiated MEF model with cluster 2.

Replication stress-associated DSBs can occur for multiple reasons, including transcription-replication conflicts and secondary DNA structures.40,41 Our results revealed that DSBs arise during DNA replication in the presence of CPDs in association with TC-NER. By contrast, pathways of UV-associated SNV formation can be multiple, including (1) SV-associated formation in active genes and (2) persistent CPD-associated formation by low-fidelity translesion synthesis (TLS) polymerases42,43 in inactive chromatin regions. Therefore, SVs/SNVs induced in transcriptional regions and others were analyzed (Figure 6E). While the ratios of SVs in both clusters 1 and 2 were similar (close to 1), SNVs largely accumulated in non-transcriptional regions, indicating biased SNV formation in regions where CPDs cannot be repaired by TC-NER. Such biased SNV formation in non-transcriptional regions was significantly higher in cluster 1 than in cluster 2, probably because some SNV formation in cluster 2 was associated with SVs (Figure 6C). The level of induced SNVs in transcriptional regions in the MEF model was similar to that in cluster 2, supporting the similarity of our in vitro model with cluster 2.

Discussion

Our results revealed that among the two major UV PPs, CPDs and 6-4 PPs, only some CPDs are associated with DSB formation that increases the risk of genome instability. UV-irradiated cells form γH2AX foci in response to 6-4 PPs, which delay S-phase entry to allow their repair before the onset of DNA replication. By contrast, CPDs do not induce γH2AX foci and are repaired more slowly. As a result, cells with CPDs can enter S phase, causing replication stress-associated DSBs during DNA replication in the presence of CPDs in association with TC-NER and increasing genome instability and clonal evolution of immortalized cells. This risk of DSB formation decreases once CPDs in active chromatin regions are repaired, although CPDs can remain unrepaired in inactive chromatin regions.

Genome instability induced by UV irradiation further facilitates the clonal evolution of immortalized cells (Figure 1). Our results clarify some of the pathways associated with melanoma development, including UV-associated SV- and SNV-induction pathways. The induction pathway of genome instability after UV irradiation is similar to that observed after γ-ray irradiation.12,21 In the case of UV light, CPDs in active genes are the primary cause of those DSBs.

Difference between CPDs and 6-4 PPs

Our results demonstrate that CPDs are associated with both SV and SNV formation, while 6-4 PPs are not. This poses the question of what is responsible for this difference between CPDs and 6-4 PPs? The answer likely lies in the degree of DNA helix distortion caused by the PPs. In fact, 6-4 PPs cause greater helix distortion than CPDs.44,45 Such helix distortion likely impacts both cell-cycle arrest and repair because γH2AX foci can be induced in response to ssDNA exposed as a result of helix distortion. Importantly, this allows 6-4 PP repair before S-phase entry through cell-cycle arrest. By contrast, CPDs rarely form γH2AX foci unless the cells are subjected to replication stress. In addition, 6-4 PPs appear to be repaired equally well at active and inactive chromatin loci, which is a characteristic of repair by the GG-NER pathway. In fact, GG-NER targets DNA helical distortion.6,46

The repair efficiencies of CPDs and 6-4 PPs differ greatly. In MEFs, while 6-4 PPs are repaired within a few hours, CPDs are repaired over a much longer time period. Although CPDs in active genes are still repairable within 48 h, CPDs in heterochromatin regions are rarely repaired even after 72 h in MEFs. The difference in the repair efficiencies of CPDs/6-4 PPs were also observed between MCF7 cells and MEFs. In fact, this is expected since GG-NER activity in human cells is much higher than in rodent.47

CPD-associated risk of SVs and SNVs

CPDs increase the risk both SVs and SNVs. However, the induction hotspots of SVs and SNVs differ in human melanoma; while SV levels are almost identical in transcriptional regions and others, SNV levels are significantly higher outside transcriptional regions (Figure 6E). This suggests that SVs and SNVs are induced by different CPDs. Based on our results, SV formation is associated with TC-NER and is thus more likely to occur in transcriptionally active genes. Although SNVs tend to occur in the presence of SVs, this is likely not the major SNV-induction pathway in human melanoma. In fact, SNVs are predominantly accumulated in transcriptionally inactive regions where CPDs can persist. These SNVs are likely induced by low-fidelity TLS polymerases during translesion DNA synthesis at CPD sites.48,49 Compared with cluster 1 in human melanoma, SNV levels in non-transcriptional regions are lower in cluster 2, possibly because some SNVs are associated with SVs (Figure 6E). SV-associated SNVs are also observed in the MEF model, suggesting a similarity between SNV formation in this model and that in cluster 2.

UV-associated SNV numbers and loci could be affected by differences in UV exposure. In fact, while MEFs in this study were exposed to a single UV-C dose, i.e., 8 J/m2 typically for less than 10 s, many individuals who go on to develop melanoma have been exposed to lower doses of UV light over much longer time periods throughout their lives. CPDs outside of genes are hardly repairable and therefore their number can increase steadily with accumulating exposures to UV light; therefore, the SNV formation might largely depend on lifestyle-related differences in UV exposure.

CPD-associated risk of clonal evolution

Our results revealed that UV light-induced CPDs increase SV and SNV levels in MEFs. A critical question is what facilitates the clonal evolution of cancer cells? Based on our results, we suggest that it might be due to CPDs that induce SVs. In fact, persistent CPDs can lead to the accumulation of SNVs in noncoding regions, where their direct impact on gene expression is likely limited, although they may still influence gene regulation through effects on the regulatory cistrome. In contrast, SVs induced by CPDs occur mainly in active genes, including cancer-driver genes. In addition, although immortalized MEFs mutated in ARF/p53 pathway components usually have mutations in both alleles of the Cdkn2a gene (or the TP53 gene), mutations in both alleles cannot be attributed easily to SNVs produced by simple polymerase errors; however, it is possible that the mutations are induced in association with SVs, such as through loss of heterozygosity and chromosomal deletion.

Among various cancer types, melanoma exhibits a high mutation burden,15,50 which is prominently represented in cluster 1 in our analyses. However, these SNVs predominantly accumulate outside of genes (Figure 6E) and are therefore unlikely to significantly contribute to functional genetic alterations. In contrast, DSBs that give rise to SVs occur in the presence of CPDs associated with TC-NER, suggesting the potential for mutation induction within gene regions. Together with the aforementioned finding that SVs are associated with dysfunction of the ARF/p53 pathway, these results underscore the importance of TC-NER in preventing genetic alterations.

Limitations of the study

To investigate the effects of UV-induced damage on genome instability, primary MEFs were used. UV-induced damage was monitored by confocal microscopy, and the resulting SVs and SNVs were analyzed by whole-genome sequencing following MEF immortalization. This approach enables the examination of the relationship between UV-induced damage and the generation of SVs and SNVs. However, this study has limitations related to the irradiation dose and exposure duration in in vitro cell culture. Specifically, the culture medium must be removed to deliver the intended UV energy to the cells, which restricts the duration of exposure. As a result, the current study reflects the effects of short-term, high-energy UV exposure. In contrast, UV exposure in many melanoma patients is typically characterized by lower energy but prolonged duration over a lifetime. Therefore, future studies should aim to improve UV irradiation methods to better replicate these physiological conditions.

Experimental model and study participant details

MEFs were prepared from C57BL/6 mouse embryos and cultured using a 3T3 passage protocol.55 For immortalization of MEFs, MEFs that reached the growth-arrested state were maintained with a medium change every 3 days until they exhibited immortalized cell growth (IP1). All MEFs were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal calf serum (FCS; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). MCF7 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FCS.

Method details

M-phase synchronization

M-phase synchronization was performed by incubating cells with 150 ng/mL nocodazole (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 4 h, and shaking the culture dish to obtain M-phase synchronized cells.

Chromosome number analysis

Cells cultivated in the presence of 12.5–25 ng/mL colcemid for 1.5–5 h were trypsinized, collected by centrifugation (1,500 rpm for 5 min), suspended in 75 mM KCl (5 mL), and incubated for 20 min. Carnoy’s solution (acetic acid:methanol = 1:3) (6 mL) was added to the cell suspension before centrifugation (1,500 rpm for 5 min). The pellet was washed twice by suspending cells in Carnoy’s solution (5 mL), followed by centrifugation (1,500 rpm for 5 min). The resultant chromosome samples were again suspended in Carnoy’s solution (0.1–1 mL) and spread onto glass slides. The slides were incubated at 80°C for 48 h, soaked in 0.025% trypsin in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (37°C) for a few seconds, and then washed once in PBS containing 10% FCS and once in PBS containing 5% FCS. For chromosome counting, the slides were stained by soaking for 30 min in 10 ng/mL bisBenzimide H33258 in McIlvaine buffer (pH 4.4) and subsequent washing with water. After another round of soaking in McIlvaine buffer and washing with water, the slides were dried, exposed to fluorescent light for more than 48 h, and then embedded in 50% glycerol–McIlvaine buffer. Chromosomes were monitored by microscopy (Zeiss Axio Imager Z2).

UV irradiation and assays

Cells were irradiated with defined doses of UV-C (254 nm) using a GL15 lamp (Panasonic, Tokyo, Japan). The energy output of the lamp was measured with a UVR-300 UV intensity meter equipped with UD-250 (Topcon Technohouse Corp., Tokyo, Japan).

Survival rates were determined by the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium, inner salt (MTS) assay. Cells were plated in 6-well culture plates, irradiated with UV-C, and cultured for 4 days. Cellular viability was estimated using the CellTiter 96 Aqueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Immunofluorescence imaging with a confocal microscope

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, blocked with 2% goat serum prepared in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100, and then subjected to immunofluorescence using the primary and secondary antibodies indicated below. Immunofluorescence analysis was performed using a confocal laser microscope11 (Olympus FV10i and FV3000 and Leica SP8). γH2AX and 53BP1 foci in each nucleus were automatically counted using the “Find Maxima” function of ImageJ51 after manual identification of nuclei, which were visualized by DAPI staining. Foci were counted under the same conditions in each experiment. Cells were stained with 10 μM BrdU for 24 h, washed with PBS, and pre-extracted (25 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 3 mM MgCl2, 300 mM sucrose, and 0.5% Triton X-100) for 20 min on ice before being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and immunostained. EdU staining was performed with a Click-iT Plus EdU Imaging Kit (Life Technologies, Waltham, MA). To avoid NER of the lesions created by the incorporated EdU,56 the cells were treated with a 30 min pulse of EdU.

To evaluate the local CPD signal intensity in nuclei, images of each nucleus were divided into 2.09 μm2 (1.44 × 1.44 μm, i.e., 4 × 4 pixels) sections and the fluorescence intensity in each section was quantified with ImageJ.51 Biased CPD repair-associated local CPD signal intensity decay was evaluated by determining the relative local CPD intensity (maximum-to-minimum of the average CPD intensity) and the normalized average deviation of the CPD signal intensity. The CPD signal intensities in DAPI-high puncta and other regions were separately determined and then the average CPD signal intensity in each region was calculated in each nucleus. The persistent CPD accumulation rate was determined based on the relative CPD signal intensities. Correlations between the local CPD signal intensity and local HP1α, H3K9me3, and H3K9ac signal intensities was analyzed after co-immunostaining.

Antibodies, siRNA, and inhibitors

Antibodies against the following proteins and nucleotides were obtained from the indicated suppliers: γH2AX (9718, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; 613402, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), 53BP1 (PC712, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), CPD (NM-DND-001, Cosmo Bio, Tokyo, Japan), 6–4 PP (NM-DND-002, Cosmo Bio), cGAS (D1D3G, Cell Signaling Technology), phosphorylated histone H3 at Ser 10 (ab5176, abcam, Cambridge, UK), HP1α (2616, Cell Signaling Technology), tri-methylated histone H3 at Lys 9 (D4W1U, Cell Signaling Technology), acetylated H3 at Lys 9 (ab10812, abcam), and phosphorylated RPA32 at Ser 33 (NB100-544, Novus Biologicals, Centennial, Colorado, USA). The following secondary antibodies were obtained from the indicated suppliers: anti-mouse IgG-Alexa Fluor 488 (A-11001, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA; 1/1000), anti-rabbit IgG-Alexa Fluor 594 (A-11012, Thermo Fisher; 1/1000), anti-mouse IgG-Alexa Fluor 594 (ab150116, Abcam; 1/1000), and anti-rabbit IgG-Alexa Fluor 647 (ab150075, Abcam; 1/1000).

Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Life Technologies) was used to transfect siRNAs. The sequence of the top-strand siRNA targeting CSBs were CCAAGAAUCUCAAAGGUCU[dT][dT] (siCSB#1) and GGACUUAUAGCCCUAAGAA[dT][dT] (siCSB#2).

The following inhibitors were obtained from the indicated suppliers: ATM inhibitor KU-55933 (S1092, Selleck Biotech, Houston, TX), ATR inhibitor VE-822 (S7102, Selleck Biotech), DNA-PK inhibitor NU7441 (S2638, Selleck Biotech), and RNA polymerase II inhibitor DRB (D1916, Sigma).

Cell cycle analysis

Cell cycle status was determined by flow cytometry analysis (Guava easyCyte HT, Merck) after fixation with 70% ethanol and incubation in PBS containing propidium iodide (10 μg/mL) and 100 μg/mL RNase A. The effect of release from M phase was determined by p-H3 statuses.

Whole-genome sequencing analyses

Next-generation sequencing libraries were constructed following the manufacturer’s protocol. For each sample, 100 ng of genomic DNA was randomly fragmented into <500 bp fragments by sonication (Covaris S220). The fragments were treated with End Prep Enzyme Mix for end repair, 5′ phosphorylated, and dA-tailed in one reaction, followed by T-A ligation to add adaptors to both ends. Size selection of adaptor-ligated DNA was then performed using beads, and fragments of ∼470 bp (with an approximate insert size of 350 bp) were recovered. Each sample was then amplified by PCR using P5 and P7 primers, with both primers carrying sequences that anneal with Flowcell to perform bridge PCR and the P5/P7 primer carrying index sequences that allow multiplexing. The PCR products were cleaned up using beads, validated using a Qsep100 instrument (Bioptic, Taiwan, China), and quantified using a Qubit3.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Then, libraries with different indices were multiplexed and loaded on an Illumina NovaSeq instrument according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed using a 2 × 150 paired-end configuration. Image analysis and base calling were conducted using NovaSeq Control Software (NCS) + OLB + GAPipeline-1.6 (Illumina).

Cutadapt (V1.9.1) was used to remove adaptor sequences, PCR primers, content with more than 10% N bases, and bases with a quality lower than 20. BWA52 (V0.7.12) was used to map clean data to the reference genome. Mapping results were processed by Picard (V1.119) to remove duplication. Unified Genotyper was used to call and filter SNVs/insertions/deletions (InDels) with GATK (V3.4.6) software. SNV/InDels were annotated by Annovar (V21 Feb 2013). SVs were detected with Manta (V1.6.0). CNVnator (V0.3.1) was used to analyze genomic structure variation. Somatic variation was analyzed by VarScan (V2.3.8). Enrichment for target genes was completed with the GO and KEGG databases.

Analyses of mutational signature, circos plots, variant allele frequency and correlations of SVs and SNVs

The ICGC PCAWG study was searched for validated data of SVs, SNVs, InDels, mutational signatures, and clinical data (sex, age at diagnosis, and survival time) in patients with melanoma (MELA-AU).39 Mutational signatures were evaluated with SigProfilerExtractor53 (https://github.com/AlexandrovLab/SigProfilerExtractor, version 1.1.23), which was employed to analyze SNVs using default settings.

Circular representations of genomic alterations (SVs and SNVs) were visualized with BioCircos.57 Moving averages of 10 Mb were determined after counting the number of alterations every 1 Mb. To assess the correlation between SVs and SNVs, each point was plotted and Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated. The Student’s t test was used for statistical analysis.

To assess the clonality status of immortalized MEFs, variant allele frequencies (VAFs) were determined for each SNV and compared with the expected values for the cases of single clonal evolution of diploid (2 N) and tetraploid (4 N) and clonal evolution of multiple cells, i.e., 6 N (diploid and tetraploid MEFs) and 8 N (two diploid and one tetraploid MEFs). The expected values of VAFs were estimated from the simulated detection frequency of mutations. For those estimations, chromosome number and depth (=50) were used in each. This process was repeated until the estimated number reached the SNV number detected by whole-genome sequencing analyses.

DNA-RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (DRIP-seq) analyses

DRIP was performed as previously described.58,59 For each sample, genomic DNA was extracted from cultured cells with a QIAamp Blood Mini Kit (51104, QIAGEN, Venlo, Nederland) and fragmented with the following cocktail of restriction enzymes in 300 μL of rCutSmart Buffer (B6004S, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) containing the following restriction enzymes at 37°C for 4 h: 1 μL of BsrGI-HF (20 U/μL) (R3575S, New England Biolabs), 1 μL of EcoRI-HF (20 U/μL) (R3101S, New England Biolabs), 1 μL of HindIII-HF (20 U/μL) (R3104S, New England Biolabs), 1 μL of SspI-HF (20 U/μL) (R3132S, New England Biolabs), and 1 μL of XbaI (20 U/μL) (R0145S, New England Biolabs). Fragmented DNA was purified with a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (28104, QIAGEN). As a control, half of each sample was treated with 0.8 μL of RNase H (5 U/μL) (M0297S, New England Biolabs) in 80 μL of RNase H reaction buffer (B0297S, New England Biolabs) at 37°C overnight.

10× DRIP binding buffer (100 mM sodium phosphate pH 7.0, 1.4 M NaCl, and 0.5% (vol/vol) Triton X-100) was diluted with TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 and 1 mM EDTA). For immunoprecipitation, 5 μg of fragmented DNA and 5 μg of anti-DNA-RNA antibody (S9.6) (MABE1095, Merck) were incubated in 250 μL of 1× DRIP binding buffer at 4°C overnight and then 15 μL of Protein An FG beads (TAS8848N1172, Tamagawa Seiki, Iida, Japan) that had been washed with PBS were added to the immunoprecipitation reaction mix and incubated at 4°C for 2 h. After incubation, beads were separated from the supernatant with a magnetic stand and washed with DRIP binding buffer three times. After washing, DNA was eluted with DRIP elution buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, and 0.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate) and purified using a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit.

DRIP samples were quantified using a QuantiFluor fluorometer (Promega) and qualified using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 instrument (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Next-generation sequencing libraries were constructed with a NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (E7103S, New England Biolabs) and NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (E7335S, New England Biolabs) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Then, libraries with different indices were multiplexed and loaded on an Illumina Novaseq instrument according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina). Sequencing was performed using a 2 × 150 paired-end configuration. Image analysis and base calling were conducted using NovaSeq Control Software (NCS) + OLB + GAPipeline-1.6 (Illumina).

To remove technical sequences, including adapters, PCR primers, and fragments and bases with a quality lower than 20, PF (pass filter) data in fastq format were processed using Cutadapt (version 1.9.1) to obtain high-quality clean data. Reference genome (GRCh38.p13) sequences and gene model annotation files of appropriate species were downloaded from genome websites such as UCSC, NCBI, and ENSEMBL. Next, bowtie2-build was used to index the reference genome sequence. Finally, clean data were aligned to the reference genome using Bowtie2 (version 2.2.6). MACS254 (version 2.2.7.1) was used to analyze peak quality control and peak calling. Peaks were annotated by ChIPseeker (version 1.36.0).60

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel. The data are presented as mean values ±SD. Significant differences between groups were determined by Welch’s t test or Student’s t test or Dunnett’s multiple comparisons. Significant difference between Kaplan-Meier curves was determined by log rank test followed by the Holm-Sidak test.
