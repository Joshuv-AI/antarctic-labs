Preliminary Validation of the Epileptic Seizure Trigger Questionnaire for Perceived Seizure Triggers in Adults with Epilepsy
Authors: Lucas Lima Najar, Marleide da Mota Gomes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeSeizure triggers-such as stress, sleep deprivation, and missed medication-are commonly reported by patients with epilepsy. Understanding these perceived triggers is critical for patient counseling and preventive strategies. However, no validated self-report instrument exists to systematically assess them. To develop and conduct a preliminary psychometric evaluation of the epileptic seizure trigger questionnaire (EST-Q).MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study with 100 adults with active epilepsy recruited from two public hospitals in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. The initial 12-item questionnaire was refined into an 11-item instrument after removing one poorly performing item. Psychometric evaluation was conducted exclusively on the seven-item core module, after exclusion of four items with high non-response rates. Analyses included exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, internal consistency assessment, and item response theory (IRT). Given the modest sample size, confirmatory factor analysis and IRT findings were interpreted as exploratory and preliminary.ResultsThe seven-item core suggested a predominantly unidimensional structure (Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin=0.578; variance explained=29.7%), with borderline internal consistency (α=0.699; ω=0.681), which is adequate for group-level research but insufficient for individual clinical decisions, and moderate to high item discrimination. The four excluded items remain clinically relevant but were unsuitable for psychometric evaluation due to limited applicability.ConclusionsThe EST-Q shows promise as a standardized tool for assessing perceived seizure triggers. These preliminary findings support its potential utility, but validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is needed to confirm its factor structure, reliability, and generalizability before routine clinical use.Keywords: Epilepsy, Epileptic seizures, Surveys and questionnaires, Psychometrics, Brazil

Introduction

Seizure triggers-such as stress, sleep deprivation, and missed medication-are reported by a significant proportion of patients with epilepsy.1,2 Identifying these perceived precipitants is clinically relevant, as recognizing and modifying triggers can support seizure prevention and guide individualized counseling.2 However, most studies assessing seizure triggers rely on unstructured interviews or heterogeneous lists, limiting reproducibility and comparability.1–3

Standardized instruments in other chronic conditions, such as the asthma trigger inventory, have demonstrated the value of treating "perceived trigger susceptibility" as a quantifiable latent construct.4 This approach, also used in migraine research, enhances consistency and clinical precision. Although seizure triggers are diverse, they can be conceptualized as factors that transiently lower an individual’s seizure threshold. Under this framework, the perception of susceptibility to triggers may represent a clinically relevant latent construct-analogous to perceived trigger susceptibility in asthma-and may be amenable to psychometric evaluation.

We hypothesize that an individual’s propensity to perceive various stimuli as triggers reflects an overarching and potentially quantifiable trait of perceived seizure susceptibility. We posit that this overarching trait may reflect a patient's generalized level of somatic awareness or perceived vulnerability to internal and external state changes, which manifests in the endorsement of specific triggers. This theoretical mechanism unifies seemingly disparate triggers (e.g., stress, sleep deprivation) under a single latent construct, as they all contribute to a patient’s subjective model of vulnerability. A standardized scale offers significant advantages over a simple checklist, including the possibility of generating a summary indicator for research and educational purposes, tracking changes over time, evaluating interventions, and exploring associations with outcomes such as seizure frequency, anxiety, and quality of life. To date, no validated self-report instrument exists to systematically assess perceived seizure triggers in epilepsy.

To address this gap, we developed the epileptic seizure trigger questionnaire (EST-Q), a brief self-report instrument designed to standardize the assessment of perceived seizure precipitants. This study reports its preliminary psychometric evaluation in a sample of 100 adults with active epilepsy.

Methods

Design and setting

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Instituto de Neurologia Deolindo Couto, Federal University of Rio de Janeiro (CAAE 78065224.0.0000.5261; approval No. 6,809,811), and by the Hospital Municipal Ronaldo Gazolla Ethics Committee (approval No. 7,130,522).

Participants

We consecutively recruited 100 adults with active epilepsy (last seizure ≤5 years). Exclusion criteria included psychogenic non-epileptic seizures, illiteracy, severe cognitive or visual impairment, or intellectual disability preventing independent questionnaire completion. While a sample of 100 is adequate for preliminary exploratory factor analysis (EFA), it is at the lower end of recommended thresholds for stable factor solutions. This sample size is a key limitation for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and item response theory (IRT) models, which require larger samples for stable parameter estimation. Thus, these analyses are presented as initial, illustrative findings.

Instrument

The initial EST-Q consisted of 12 items representing commonly reported seizure triggers (Table 1). Responses used a 4-point Likert scale (0=never; 3=always), with a "not applicable" option for items referring to contexts not universally experienced by all patients. The Likert scale was selected to capture the frequency of trigger perception, enabling quantitative analysis for psychometric evaluation.

Procedure

We assessed content validity through expert review by epileptologists, followed by semantic testing in 32 patients to evaluate clarity and comprehension. An independent pilot application was then conducted in an additional group of 20 patients. For this preliminary psychometric evaluation, we analyzed data from the full sample of 100 patients.

Handling of non-applicable items

Four items-menstruation (52% non-response), alcohol use (43%), tobacco use (61%), and illicit drug use (71%)-showed high frequencies of "not applicable" responses. These missing responses likely reflected the limited applicability of these contexts within the study population, rather than misunderstanding of the items. Because these factors apply only to specific subgroups, the reduced number of valid cases prevented robust psychometric estimation. Thus, we excluded these items from EFA, CFA, and reliability analyses. This decision improved the psychometric performance of the core scale but narrowed the instrument’s scope regarding subgroup-specific triggers.

To balance psychometric rigor and clinical utility, we propose a two-part structure: a seven-item core module for psychometric assessment and a four-item clinical supplement for comprehensive patient profiling. The core module includes items on sleep deprivation, missed medication, stress, fasting, medication change, fatigue, and fever/infection.

Data analysis

We performed psychometric analyses in R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), including exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, internal consistency indices (Cronbach’s α, McDonald’s ω, and composite reliability),5 and IRT modeling.6 Our approach followed established guidelines for health measurement instruments, including expert content validation, semantic testing, pilot administration, and successive psychometric analyses (EFA, CFA, IRT).6,7

Results

Sample characteristics

The sample included 56 women and 44 men, with a mean age of 44.4±14.7 years. Most patients had focal epilepsy (n=75), followed by generalized (n=20), unknown (n=4), and mixed focal/generalized (n=1). The mean duration of epilepsy was 25.4 years (median, 24; range, 2–62). Median seizure frequency was 12 episodes per year, ranging from rare events to daily seizures.

Item exclusion and final structure of the EST-Q

The initial 12-item pool was reduced as follows. One item (food consumption) was removed due to poor psychometric performance. Four items (menstruation, alcohol, tobacco, and illicit drug use) were designated as a clinical supplement due to high rates of “not applicable” responses. The remaining seven items formed the core module for psychometric evaluation. Thus, the final EST-Q comprises 11 items: a seven-item psychometric core and four additional clinically relevant items.

Factor analysis

EFA was consistent with a tentatively unidimensional structure for the EST-Q core in this preliminary sample (Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin [KMO]= 0.578; Bartlett’s p<0.001), explaining 29.7% of the variance. While the KMO value is modest and the explained variance is below conventional thresholds, these findings underscore the exploratory nature of the present results. Internal consistency

The seven-item core module yielded Cronbach’s α=0.699, McDonald’s ω=0.681, and composite reliability=0.765. These values are considered borderline for individual clinical interpretation, but acceptable for exploratory and group-based research at this preliminary stage. Importantly, psychometric analyses were conducted solely on the seven-item core scale, and no reliability estimates are claimed for the 4 supplemental items.

CFA

Fit indices for the unidimensional model were consistent with an initially adequate model fit (χ2(20)=21.85; χ2/df=1.09; comparative fit index=0.990; Tucker-Lewis index=0.987; root mean square error of approximation=0.031), although these values should be interpreted cautiously given the modest sample size and potential overfitting. These results are presented as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, requiring confirmation in larger samples.

IRT

IRT analyses indicated discrimination parameters ranging from moderate (0.65–1.34) to very high (>1.70). Difficulty thresholds spanned from −1 to 6, suggesting the EST-Q core may be sensitive, in this preliminary dataset, across a broad range of the latent continuum of perceived susceptibility. These IRT parameters, while promising, should be considered preliminary due to the sample size.

Discussion

This preliminary study provides initial evidence for the reliability and structural validity of the EST-Q, a novel tool designed to assess the latent construct of perceived seizure trigger susceptibility. The EST-Q showed borderline but potentially adequate reliability for a preliminary study, and CFA indices were consistent with an initially adequate and exploratory model fit. IRT results indicated that the items discriminated well across levels of perceived susceptibility.

Importantly, the present findings suggest that patients’ perceptions of seizure triggers may be meaningfully structured and amenable to systematic assessment, although this interpretation remains tentative and requires further empirical confirmation. The fact that a single factor emerged, explaining nearly a third of the variance, provides preliminary evidence for a shared latent trait. The remaining variance likely captures the unique, item-specific nature of each trigger, which is consistent with clinical reality. This pattern is compatible with the interpretation that the EST-Q may capture a general dimension of perceived trigger susceptibility, while individual items retain specific clinical relevance. Although a unidimensional model was supported in this preliminary analysis, the complexity of the construct suggests that alternative structural models, such as bifactor or hierarchical solutions, could be explored in future studies with larger and more diverse samples.

From a theoretical perspective, perceived susceptibility to seizure triggers may plausibly operate as a latent trait reflecting interindividual differences in bodily awareness, attentional focus, and interpretation of internal states. Previous research has shown that constructs such as somatosensory amplification represent stable individual tendencies to perceive and intensify bodily sensations, and are associated with health-related perceptions and behaviors.8 Moreover, cognitive and perceptual models of symptom perception emphasize the role of expectation, attention, and memory in shaping how bodily changes are perceived and interpreted.9 Together, these models support the conceptualization of perceived trigger susceptibility as a continuous, psychologically meaningful dimension rather than as a set of unrelated beliefs.

Limitations

These findings must be interpreted within the context of a small, single-country, exploratory validation. The modest sample size restricts the stability of the CFA and IRT results and likely contributed to the modest KMO (0.578) and explained variance (29.7%). These values, while acceptable for an initial study, indicate the complexity of the construct and the preliminary nature of this model.

The high non-response rates for four items (menstruation, alcohol, tobacco, and illicit drugs) necessitated their exclusion from the psychometric core, narrowing the instrument’s scope. These high “not applicable” rates may reflect characteristics specific to the present sample, such as low prevalence of substance use in the population served, socioeconomic profile, or cultural norms related to reporting such behaviors, rather than problems with item clarity or comprehension. This context may limit the generalizability of the clinical supplement to other populations and settings. This exclusion may have influenced the factorial structure and reliability estimates, highlighting the need for careful re-evaluation of these items in future studies.

The internal consistency of the seven-item core (α=0.699; ω=0.681) should be interpreted as borderline. Such values are insufficient for individual clinical decision-making, yet acceptable for group-level research purposes in a preliminary validation context. Accordingly, the EST-Q core is currently better suited for research and educational applications rather than for high-stakes individualized use. Future studies should aim to improve reliability through item refinement or expansion of the item pool. Importantly, psychometric validation was performed exclusively on the seven-item core module, and the four supplemental items were not included in any reliability or factor analyses.

Clinical implications

The final 11-item EST-Q emphasizes seizure triggers that are common and broadly applicable across patients. Subgroup-specific triggers-such as alcohol use, smoking, or menstrual factors-remain clinically relevant but are better addressed through complementary assessment. As a result, the total EST-Q score does not fully capture perceived susceptibility to all clinically relevant triggers; however, this decision preserves the comparability and reliability of the core measure in its current developmental stage. In future studies with larger and more diverse samples, expanded versions of the EST-Q including these conditional items could be tested and psychometrically evaluated, allowing a broader assessment while maintaining measurement rigor.

The EST-Q emerges from this preliminary study as a potentially useful instrument for research and preliminary clinical assessment of perceived seizure triggers. Its initial psychometric profile suggests that it may contribute to a more standardized approach to trigger evaluation; however, further validation is required before any routine or individual-level clinical use. It should be emphasized that psychometric evidence currently pertains exclusively to the seven-item core module, whereas the four supplemental items remain to be formally evaluated.

To advance the EST-Q toward broader application, future studies should prioritize: 1) confirming the factor structure and generalizability in a larger, multicenter Brazilian cohort; 2) establishing temporal stability through test-retest reliability; and 3) examining convergent validity with measures of self-efficacy and quality of life.

Further research should also explore discriminant validity and, crucially, the predictive validity of the EST-Q score regarding subsequent seizure frequency, treatment adherence, and patient-reported outcomes. These steps are essential to consolidate the psychometric foundation of the instrument and determine its appropriate scope of use.
