Association of parental death and separation with child mortality in India
Authors: Ujjwal Das, Sanjay K Mohanty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParental death and separation in early life can have profound effects on child health, increasing the risk of mortality. Using data from the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey (2019–21), this study examined the association of parental death and separation on mortality among children aged 0–59 months in India. A total of 232,577 children were included in the analysis, with infant and under-five mortality rates estimated using the life table technique. Cox proportional hazards models with 95% confidence intervals were applied to assess the effect of parental death and separation on all-cause mortality. Results showed that child mortality was significantly associated with both parental death and parental separation. Male children experienced higher mortality rates than female children when either parent had died (IMR/U5MR: 39 and 65 vs. 26 and 29 per thousand, respectively). Children whose parents had died showed higher hazards of infant and under-five mortality, although these associations were not statistically significant after adjustment (HR = 1.45, 95% CI: 0.96–2.19, p = 0.08; HR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.01–2.63, p = 0.04). In contrast, children residing in rural areas had significantly higher hazards of infant mortality (HR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.22–1.53, p < 0.01) and under-five mortality (HR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.25–1.52, p < 0.01). Among children of higher educated parents, those from wealthier households showed significantly lower mortality risks compared with children of separated parents. The findings indicate that parental death and separation have substantial adverse effects on child survival, underscoring the need for strengthened healthcare access, targeted public health interventions, and policies that support vulnerable families to reduce child mortality in India.Keywords: Life table, Parental death, Cox proportional, Infant, Under-fiveSubject terms: Epidemiology, Paediatric research

Introduction

The impact of parental death and separation on child mortality is a significant concern, especially in low- and middle-income countries where children are already exposed to increased health risks1. Studies from China, Italy, and the Netherlands indicate that between one-fifth and one-third of all children experience the loss of a parent by the age of 152–4. In contrast, reports from high-income countries suggest that 3–4% of children lose a parent before turning 10 years old5,6.The loss of a parent disrupts a child’s access to care, emotional support, and essential resources, increasing their vulnerability to illness and death7. Parental presence plays a crucial role in ensuring child survival, particularly during infancy and early childhood when children are highly dependent on caregiving, nutrition, and timely healthcare8. Disruption caused by parental death or separation may compromise these protective mechanisms through reduced supervision, weakened emotional support, economic hardship, and limited access to healthcare services. These vulnerabilities may increase the likelihood of illness, delayed treatment, and mortality among children under five years of age6,8. Additionally, a cross-sectional study conducted in Nepal revealed a strong association between maternal death and infant survival during the first 24 weeks of life9. Children whose mothers die during childbirth or in the early years of life are more likely to die before reaching the age of five, as they lose access to breastfeeding, proper nutrition, and essential care10. The mother’s role in ensuring the child’s nutrition, hygiene, and health is vital for survival, and her absence often leads to neglect, poor health supervision, and increased susceptibility to diseases11. The loss of a mother is a strong indicator of poor educational outcomes for a child12. Children who have lost their mothers are significantly less likely to be enrolled in school and tend to complete fewer years of education, compared to those whose mothers are still alive, even when accounting for age13–15. In addition, children who have lost fathers have been found to live in significantly poorer households, on average16,17. Previous study shows the children experiencing the trauma of losing a parent may face psychological issues such as anxiety and depression, which can manifest physically through weakened resistance to disease7,18. In some cases, children left behind by deceased parents are subject to neglect or even abuse, further endangering their health and survival19. While the loss of either parent increases a child’s risk of mortality, research consistently shows that the loss of a mother has more severe consequences than the loss of a father20. Additionally, growing evidence suggests that the health, education, and socioeconomic status of both mothers and fathers play a crucial role in determining their children’s health and survival outcomes21,22.

Research has consistently shown a strong link between parental death and increased child mortality. For example, studies in sub-Saharan Africa have demonstrated that maternal death significantly raises the risk of child death, particularly in regions where healthcare access is limited23. In South Africa, approximately 2.3 million children under the age of 18 have lost one or both parents24. Findings from Danish national registries indicate that mortality rates are higher among bereaved parents compared to those who have not experienced such a loss, especially for deaths resulting from unnatural causes (e.g., accidents and suicide) within the first three years following their child’s death25. Additionally, children who have lost a parent face an increased risk of developing psychiatric issues in the early years after the loss26–28.

Although the impact of parental separation on child mortality has been less extensively studied, evidence suggests that separation due to factors such as migration or conflict29,30. Separation frequently leads to economic hardship, particularly in cases where the custodial parent struggles financially31. The lack of financial support from the non-custodial parent may mean that children’s healthcare needs are neglected, resulting in a failure to seek timely medical care when children fall ill and contributing to higher mortality risks32. Children learn social behaviors and communication patterns from their parents. In cases where parents remain together, this learning is typically shaped by parental guidance and role modeling. However, when parents are divorced, separated, or frequently engage in conflicts—such as the use of abusive language—children are more likely to adopt these negative behaviors themselves33. In the Indian context, where family cohesion and parental authority play a central role in early childhood socialization, disruptions in parental relationships may have especially profound consequences for child well-being. Some researchers have compared the effects of parental absence due to death with those resulting from divorce, suggesting that parental death is a more exogenous factor in relation to child welfare than divorce34,35. Fronstin et al. (2001) found that parental absence during ages 11–15 is linked to lower educational attainment for both males and females36. For males, the negative impact is more pronounced when the absence is due to death rather than divorce, whereas for females, the effect is similar in magnitude regardless of the cause.

Although several studies from high-income and African settings have documented the adverse consequences of parental disruption, nationally representative evidence from India remains limited. Given changing family structures and persistent child mortality inequalities, examining this association in the Indian context is important. Emerging research indicates that children from higher socioeconomic backgrounds may experience greater effects from parental separation37–40, with similar patterns observed in cases of parental death41. In India, a middle-income country, rising trends in divorce and separation coincide with persistently high childhood mortality. This study investigates the association of parental death and separation on mortality among children aged 0–59 months.

Data and methods

Data

This study used data from the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019–21), which is part of the global Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) program. NFHS-5 employed a stratified, two-stage sampling design in rural areas and a three-stage design in urban areas to obtain a nationally representative sample of households and individuals. The overall household response rate was 98%, and the eligible women’s response rate was 97%, ensuring data reliability. Detailed sampling procedures are documented in the NFHS-5 report42.

For this analysis, the sample was restricted to 232,920 children born in the five years preceding the survey to women aged 15–49 years. After excluding 334 cases with incomplete survival information and omitting multiple births (twins and higher-order), the final analytical sample comprised 232,577 singleton children (Fig. 1). Within this sample, 1,696 children had parents who were divorced or separated, and 1,470 children had experienced the death of at least one parent, while the remaining children were living with both parents.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome variables in this study are infant mortality and under-five mortality, measured as the duration of survival from birth in months. Infant mortality is defined as the probability of a child dying between birth and 11 months of age, while under-five mortality refers to deaths occurring between birth and 59 months. The event variable was coded as 1 if the child had died before the age cutoff and 0 otherwise. Separate analyses were conducted for infant mortality (death before the completion of 12 months of age, i.e., 0–11 months) and under-five mortality (death before the completion of 60 months of age, i.e., 0–59 months). Children who were alive at the end of the observation period were treated as right-censored.

Time variables

The time-to-event variable was constructed using each child’s age at death (in completed months) for those who had died before age five. For children who were alive at the time of the survey, survival time was defined as their age in completed months at the date of the interview. Thus, the time variable captured the duration from birth until either death (event) or censoring (alive at interview or survival beyond age five).

Exposure variables

The key exposure variable was parental status at the time of survey, categorized as currently married, divorced/separated, and parental death. The NFHS-5 dataset does not provide event-history information on the timing of widowhood, separation, or divorce relative to child death. Therefore, maternal marital status at the time of survey was used as a proxy indicator of parental disruption. The childhood parental death and divorced were defined a death or divorce of a parent before child age of 12 and 59 months. As highlighted in the reviewed literature, several socio-economic and demographic variables have also been considered for their significant impact on infant and under-five mortality in relation to parental divorce and death. These are age of the respondents (15–19; 20–24; 25–29; 30–34; 35–39; 40–44 and 45–49 years), mother education (no education; primary; secondary and higher secondary), place of residence (urban and rural), Social groups (schedule cast; schedule tribe; other backward class and others), religion (Hindu; Muslim; Christian and others), wealth status (poorest; poorer; middle; richer and richest), sex of the child (male and female), birth order (1; 2; and 3+), birth interval (< 24 months; >24 months), place of delivery (home and institutional) and antenatal care visit (No ANC visit; Up to 4; and More than 4 ANC visits).

Statistical analysis

All analyses accounted for the complex multistage sampling design of NFHS-5. Sampling weights were applied to obtain nationally representative estimates, while clustering at the primary sampling unit (PSU) level and stratification variables were incorporated using the survey (svy) design in STATA. The life table technique was used for estimating the infant and under-five mortality rates both currently married parent, divorced or separated parent, and death of parent, either father death or mother. Before conducting the regression analysis, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). All covariates had VIF values well below the commonly accepted threshold of 10, indicating no serious multicollinearity problems. The Cox proportional hazards regression models were fitted under this survey framework to generate robust standard errors and 95% confidence intervals in relation to childhood parental death and divorce. Results

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the analytical sample by maternal marital status. The majority of respondents across all groups were aged 25–29 years, with currently married mothers contributing the largest share of the total sample. Secondary education was the most common educational level in all marital groups, although widowed mothers had a relatively higher proportion with no formal education. Most children belonged to rural households, reflecting the overall NFHS population structure. Notable differences were observed across social and economic characteristics. Widowed and divorced/separated mothers were relatively more concentrated in the poorest wealth quintiles, rural areas, and higher birth orders compared with currently married mothers. Institutional delivery and adequate ANC utilization were high across all groups, although slightly lower among widowed and divorced/separated mothers. These baseline differences suggest that socioeconomic disadvantage and maternal healthcare utilization vary by marital status and may partly explain observed differences in child survival outcomes.

Table 4 shows, both parental death and parental separation were significantly associated with higher infant mortality, with risks 57 to 58% (HR = 1.57, 95%CI: 1.06–2.33, p = 0.03; HR = 1.58, 95%CI: 1.05–2.38, p = 0.03) greater than among children of currently married parents. In the unadjusted model, children experiencing parental death had significantly higher hazards of infant and under-five mortality compared with children living with currently married parents. However, after adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and maternal healthcare factors, the association was attenuated and, in some models, did not remain statistically significant. This suggests that part of the observed excess mortality risk may be explained by underlying socioeconomic and healthcare-related disadvantages associated with parental disruption. Children whose mothers had higher education experienced a 46% lower hazard of infant mortality compared with children of mothers with no formal education (HR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.38–0.76). Caste differences were less consistent, with Other Backward Classes showing a lower risk compared to Scheduled Castes, while the advantage of the General caste group diminished after adjustment. A clear wealth gradient persisted, with children from the richest households having a 50% (HR = 0.50, 95%CI: 0.35–0.71, p = < 0.001) lower risk of infant mortality compared to the poorest, even after adjustment.

Discussion

Reducing childhood mortality is one of the most important public health priorities worldwide. Parents play a critical role in improving child care and reducing illness and death. Conversely, the death or divorce of a parent significantly impacts a child’s life, regardless of the child’s age. Each day of a parent’s absence has a detrimental effect on the well-being of the child. Parental illness, death, divorce, or separation may reduce the capacity of caregivers to provide consistent protection, supervision, and care for young children, thereby increasing their vulnerability to adverse health outcomes20,22.

The present study found that children of divorced or separated parents consistently face higher infant and under-five mortality rates compared to those from currently married households. The study reveal that parental marriage has a beneficial long term association on child survival, with presence of a stepparent being associated with a survival advantage of under- five children43. This trend is observed across various socio-demographic groups, although wealthier families experience much lower overall mortality rates. The results suggested that children from poorer, less educated, rural households—particularly those belonging to Scheduled Castes and Tribes—experience higher mortality rates when either parent dies. The loss or prolonged absence of one or both parents because of death, marital disruption, or severe illness may weaken caregiving support and increase the risk of poor health and survival outcomes among children44. Existing studies have mentioned that when a mother dies, her children face a significantly increased risk of death, particularly under-five children who depend on maternal care, nutrition, and protection45. The prevalence was higher among under-five children (U5MR) compared to infants under the age of one. The effect of a mother and father death on childhood mortality did not vary with child sex. The greater association of a death of fathers on girls than a boys in existing study indicate that the discrimination against girl at that time46,47. Several studies have shown that in contexts of economic or social stress, girls are more likely than boys to experience disadvantages in food allocation, healthcare seeking, and48–50. The loss of a father may therefore exacerbate these vulnerabilities by reducing household income and weakening bargaining power for girls’ well-being.

Maternal education plays a dominant role in reducing the risks of childhood mortality. The prevalence of infant and under-five mortality was lower among children of highly educated parents. Previous studies have shown that long-term child survival is closely linked to maternal education, as higher educational attainment improves knowledge of effective methods for preventing, identifying, and treating childhood illnesses51. Cleland et al. reported that each additional year of maternal education reduces the risk of under-five mortality by 7–9%, while 1–3 years of schooling is associated with a 20% decrease in childhood mortality risk52. Furthermore, country-level studies have demonstrated that lower childhood mortality rates are more prevalent among children of highly educated parents53.

The results from the regression analysis suggested that children from wealthier households and higher caste groups, particularly those with currently married parents, had significantly lower risks of infant and under-five mortality. Children from poorer households are more exposed to health risks and have less resistance to disease due to malnutrition and other risk factors typical of poorer communities54. A study in New Zealand found that children from the lowest socio-economic households had twice the risk of childhood mortality compared to those from the highest socio-economic households55. The adjusted regression models indicated slightly higher hazards of infant and under-five mortality among female children compared with males. Although the magnitude of the association was modest, the findings may reflect persistent gender-related inequalities in nutrition, healthcare utilization, and caregiving practices in some socio-cultural settings in India. The exploratory stratified analysis suggested some variation in mortality risks according to the type of parental loss; however, several estimates were unstable because of small subgroup sample sizes. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require validation using longitudinal data with more detailed parental event histories.

The findings further highlight the importance of proximate maternal and healthcare factors in improving child survival. Children born after a birth interval of more than 24 months, delivered in health institutions, and whose mothers received adequate ANC visits experienced significantly lower under-five mortality hazards, consistent with evidence that maternal healthcare utilization improves early detection of pregnancy-related complications and promotes safer neonatal care. In addition, the strong protective gradient observed across maternal education and household wealth underscores the role of socioeconomic advantage in ensuring better nutrition, timely healthcare access, and improved caregiving practices, all of which contribute to enhanced child survival. For children of divorced or separated parents, the results were more variable, with higher uncertainty due to wider confidence intervals. Although children residing in rural areas showed higher infant and under-five mortality in the unadjusted analysis, the association did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for socioeconomic and maternal healthcare factors. This finding suggests that disparities in education, household wealth, and healthcare utilization may partly explain the higher mortality observed among rural children. Existing studies have mentioned that the father’s occupation and working status are major factors in determining childhood mortality56. The death of a father can significantly impact a child’s economic well-being, educational attainment, and overall socioeconomic trajectory throughout their life course47. While maternal care is crucial for a child’s short-term survival, paternal care also plays a vital role in the long run, both in direct child-rearing and in providing economic and emotional support33,53. Previous studies have reported that children under five who lost their mother faced a mortality risk more than four times higher than those whose mother survived8. Our findings indicate that the hazard risk of childhood mortality following maternal death is substantially higher for infants (< 1 year) compared to older children (0–5 years). The increased risk of neonatal and infant mortality after a mother’s death is largely attributed to the sudden cessation of breastfeeding, a key determinant of infant survival57. When a mother dies before her child’s first birthday, breastfeeding is abruptly discontinued, contributing to nearly half of child deaths due to malnutrition58–60. Overall, the results highlight that the impact of parental death on infant mortality rate (IMR) and under-five mortality rate (U5MR) varies significantly across different background characteristics, with notable disparities based on sex, caste, and wealth index. Future research should make use of longitudinal data to better establish temporal and causal links between parental death, separation, and child survival. Studies incorporating more detailed information on maternal health, healthcare availability, and household support systems would provide a deeper understanding of mechanisms linking parental loss to child mortality.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. The cross-sectional and retrospective nature of NFHS-5 limits temporal sequencing between parental disruption and child death; therefore, causal inference cannot be established. Second, potential confounders such as maternal health status, access to healthcare services, and previous pregnancy and delivery history were either unavailable or only partially measured in the dataset, which may have introduced residual confounding. Finally, the relatively small number of cases in certain subgroups (e.g., parental death among wealthier households) resulted in wide confidence intervals, and findings for these groups should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

The findings suggest that parental death and divorce or separation are associated with higher infant and under-five mortality hazards compared with children living with currently married parents. In the adjusted models, children whose parents had died showed approximately 45% higher infant mortality hazard and 35% higher under-five mortality hazard, although these associations did not remain statistically significant after full adjustment. Children of divorced or separated parents also demonstrated elevated mortality hazards, particularly in socioeconomically disadvantaged households.

Parental education—especially at the secondary and higher levels—was strongly protective against child mortality, underscoring the role of human capital in improving child health outcomes. The effects of parental death were more pronounced among children from poorer and middle-income households, while those from wealthier households had comparatively lower risks. These findings highlight the need for targeted health and social support programs for bereaved and separated children, including sustained psychosocial and economic assistance. Policies that strengthen women’s health and education, expand social safety nets, and ensure equitable access to child healthcare are critical. Such interventions are essential for achieving further reductions in under-five mortality, in line with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG-3).
