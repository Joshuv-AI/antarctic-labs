Overview of the Clinical Course and Management of Paclitaxel-Associated Coronary Vasospasm Presenting With a Transient ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction Pattern

ECG = electrocardiogram; STEMI = ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.

History of Presentation

A 58-year-old woman with recurrent villoglandular cervical adenocarcinoma developed abrupt chest discomfort within minutes of initiating her first paclitaxel infusion at an outpatient chemotherapy center. On standing, she experienced a witnessed syncopal episode and was transferred to the emergency department, where she was found to be hypotensive and in respiratory distress, requiring bilevel positive airway pressure support for sustained hypoxia and vasopressor support with norepinephrine.

Electrocardiography (ECG) demonstrated sinus rhythm with marked ST-segment elevations in the inferior leads, accompanied by reciprocal changes in leads V1-V3, concerning for an acute inferior and posterior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) (Figure 1). An ECG obtained shortly after demonstrated near-complete resolution of the ST-segment abnormalities (Figure 2). Given the profound ECG changes in conjunction with syncope, respiratory distress, and shock physiology, emergent coronary angiography was pursued.

Electrocardiographic Findings at Presentation With ST-Segment Elevations

Initial 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrating sinus rhythm with marked ST-segment elevations in the inferior leads (II, III, and aVF) accompanied by reciprocal ST-segment depression in the anterior precordial leads (V1-V3), consistent with an acute inferior and posterior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction pattern.

Follow-Up Electrocardiogram Demonstrating Resolution of ST-Segment Elevation With Residual Inferior T-Wave Inversion

Repeat 12-lead electrocardiogram obtained approximately 30 minutes after the initial tracing demonstrates sinus rhythm with resolution of the previously noted inferior ST-segment elevations and reciprocal changes. New T-wave inversions are present in the inferior leads (II, III, and aVF), consistent with evolving ischemic repolarization abnormalities in the setting of transient coronary vasospasm.

Past Medical History

The patient was diagnosed with villoglandular cervical adenocarcinoma in January 2022 and underwent a total hysterectomy with subsequent radiation therapy. Recurrent disease was discovered in February 2025, prompting exploratory laparotomy with adhesiolysis and tumor debulking in June 2025. Paclitaxel chemotherapy was initiated in July 2025. She had no known history of coronary artery disease, diabetes mellitus, tobacco use, or alcohol use.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial differential diagnosis prioritized life-threatening causes of ST-segment elevation associated with hemodynamic compromise. Acute plaque rupture with coronary thrombosis was strongly considered given the STEMI-pattern ECG. Pulmonary embolism could also have been considered given the patient’s acute hypoxia and cancer history. Other considerations included coronary embolism, spontaneous coronary artery dissection, and acute coronary vasospasm. Given the temporal relationship to chemotherapy infusion, infusion-related hypersensitivity with secondary coronary ischemia and direct taxane-associated vasospasm were also entertained. Stress (Takotsubo) cardiomyopathy was considered less likely given the absence of characteristic wall motion abnormalities on subsequent imaging. Rapid ECG resolution and the absence of obstructive coronary disease ultimately supported a diagnosis of severe chemotherapy-associated coronary vasospasm mimicking STEMI.

Investigations

Initial troponin I was reassuringly low at 11 ng/L. Emergent left heart catheterization with coronary angiography was performed. Hemodynamic assessment revealed an aortic pressure of 119/75 mm Hg, left ventricular pressure of 124/0 mm Hg, a normal left ventricular end-diastolic pressure of 7 mm Hg, and no evidence of aortic stenosis. Fluoroscopy demonstrated minimal coronary calcification.

Coronary angiography revealed severe diffuse coronary vasospasm involving the left main and left coronary system. After intracoronary nitroglycerin administration, the left main caliber increased substantially, with no significant obstructive disease identified. The left anterior descending artery was found to be a moderate-sized vessel with a mid-intramuscular bridge and no significant atherosclerotic disease aside from vasospasm. The left circumflex artery demonstrated vasospasm with a possible ostial lesion estimated at 25% to 50%. Notably, the initial ECG suggested inferior ischemia consistent with right coronary artery (RCA) territory; however (Figure 3), the RCA was a large dominant vessel with luminal irregularities but no obstructive disease. This ECG-angiographic discordance is most consistent with spontaneous resolution of an RCA vasospastic event before angiographic assessment, with unmasking of a concurrent or sequential left coronary system spasm.

Right Coronary Artery Angiography Demonstrating the Absence of Vasospasm

Coronary angiography of the right coronary artery in the LAO projection demonstrating a large, dominant vessel with mild luminal irregularities throughout its course. No focal obstructive lesions, filling defects, or smooth concentric luminal narrowing consistent with active vasospasm are identified. The preserved caliber and absence of a diffuse smooth luminal narrowing support spontaneous resolution of the inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction seen on the initial electrocardiogram, consistent with the dynamic and transient nature of taxane-induced coronary vasomotor instability.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) performed the following day demonstrated normal biventricular size and systolic function with an ejection fraction of 60% to 65% and mild tricuspid regurgitation. The estimated right ventricular systolic pressure was 27 mm Hg without pericardial effusion. A follow-up TTE 2 months later demonstrated similar findings with an ejection fraction of 55% to 60%, normal chamber sizes, trivial mitral regurgitation, mild tricuspid regurgitation, and an estimated right ventricular systolic pressure of 24 mm Hg.

Management

In the setting of a STEMI-pattern ECG accompanied by syncope and shock physiology, the patient underwent emergent coronary angiography identifying severe diffuse coronary vasospasm of the left main and left coronary system (Figure 4A). Administration of 200 μg of intracoronary nitroglycerin resulted in prompt vasodilation and complete angiographic resolution of the spasm (Figure 4B). After the procedure, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit on continuous telemetry. Vasopressor support was discontinued after hemodynamic stabilization.

Coronary Angiography Before and After Intracoronary Nitroglycerin

(A) Coronary angiography demonstrating severe diffuse vasospasm involving the left main coronary artery and left coronary system, with smooth luminal narrowing and no evidence of obstructive coronary artery disease. (B) Repeat angiography after intracoronary nitroglycerin administration demonstrating prompt vasodilation and normalization of vessel caliber.

Serial troponin measurements and TTE were obtained to evaluate for myocardial injury and ventricular function. Long-acting nitrate therapy with isosorbide mononitrate was initiated to reduce recurrence risk. A multidisciplinary discussion with oncology was used to address chemotherapy-induced coronary vasospasm and to guide future treatment plans.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient improved rapidly after angiographic resolution of coronary vasospasm and required no further vasopressor support. Echocardiography demonstrated preserved biventricular function without regional wall motion abnormalities. During hospitalization, she developed transient leukocytosis and lactic acidosis, which naturally improved after resolution of her acute event. Secondary infectious evaluation, including blood and urine cultures, demonstrated no growth. She was discharged with plans for close oncology follow-up and continuation of antispasm therapy.

Subsequent outpatient echocardiography demonstrated sustained preservation of ventricular function without recurrent ischemic symptoms.

Discussion

Taxane-associated coronary vasospasm is an uncommon but clinically important complication of chemotherapy-related cardiovascular toxicity that can closely mimic acute coronary syndrome. Regulatory data estimate the incidence of significant cardiovascular events from single-agent paclitaxel at approximately 1%, with higher rates in combination regimens and albumin-bound formulations.1,2 Although most reported events involve asymptomatic bradycardia or conduction abnormalities, severe ischemic presentations including STEMI have been described during infusion or shortly afterward.3, 4, 5

This case illustrates a high-risk presentation in which abrupt symptom onset during paclitaxel infusion was accompanied by transient STEMI-pattern electrocardiographic changes, hemodynamic collapse, and diffuse left main vasospasm on angiography. Of particular importance, the near-complete resolution of ST-segment elevation before angiography could have falsely reassured clinicians and prompted premature de-escalation. Instead, syncope, hypotension, and respiratory distress appropriately triggered emergent invasive evaluation. This highlights a critical teaching point in which clinical risk and not electrocardiographic improvement alone must guide decision-making.6

Similar reports describe rapid ECG normalization and absence of myocardial necrosis, supporting vasospasm over plaque rupture as the dominant mechanism.3,4 However, fatal myocardial infarction has also been reported with paclitaxel exposure, emphasizing the diagnostic uncertainty at presentation and the necessity of definitive coronary assessment.5 Despite the outcome of the reported case, it is still crucial to remember that even brief luminal compromise can result in extensive myocardial ischemia and profound hemodynamic collapse.

The pathophysiology of taxane-induced coronary vasospasm is multifactorial. Paclitaxel induces endothelial dysfunction through oxidative stress and impaired nitric oxide bioavailability, leading to heightened vascular smooth muscle reactivity and vasoconstriction.7 In addition, inflammation, autonomic mechanisms, and direct myocardial effects may contribute to arrhythmogenesis and ischemia during infusion.7 These processes can produce diffuse, multivessel vasospasm that is angiographically reversible with intracoronary nitrates, as observed in this case.

A central procedural teaching point highlighted by this case is the critical role of intracoronary nitroglycerin. When administered, intracoronary nitroglycerin provides simultaneous diagnostic clarity and definitive treatment. Therefore, intracoronary nitroglycerin should be routinely considered when diffuse or dynamic coronary narrowing is encountered during emergent angiography.7, 8, 9

The electrocardiographic-angiographic discordance in this case further emphasizes the potential role of intracoronary intervention. Acetylcholine provocation, for example, can unmask vasospasm in angiographically normal-appearing vessels proving useful in the evaluation of vasospastic angina and myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries.8,10 Had hemodynamic stability been achieved before procedure completion, acetylcholine provocation of the RCA may have elicited inducible vasospasm, providing direct angiographic confirmation of multivessel coronary hyperreactivity and further corroborating the proposed sequential vasospasm mechanism. Incorporation of provocation testing when ECG-angiographic discordance is present may improve mechanistic characterization and inform long-term vasospasm management.

Despite the transient ECG changes, emergent invasive evaluation was appropriate and consistent with contemporary STEMI systems-of-care recommendations, given syncope, shock physiology, and ST-segment elevation.6 Premature attribution of symptoms to chemotherapy-related toxicity without exclusion of obstructive coronary disease could delay life-saving reperfusion therapy. Once obstruction is excluded, the myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary artery framework provides a useful diagnostic construct, with coronary vasospasm recognized as a key underlying mechanism.8

Management focuses on vasodilator therapy and multidisciplinary coordination. Intracoronary and systemic nitrates are effective for acute spasm relief, while long-acting nitrates or calcium channel blockers may reduce recurrence risk.7,8 Close collaboration between cardiology and oncology teams is essential to guide future chemotherapy decisions, weighing oncologic benefit against cardiovascular risk. Expert consensus emphasizes individualized, shared decision-making in cardio-oncology patients undergoing invasive evaluation, particularly when vascular toxicity is suspected.7,9

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that paclitaxel-associated coronary vasospasm can present with transient STEMI-pattern electrocardiographic changes and shock physiology, where initial ECG resolution may be misleading. Emergent angiography is essential in said presentations, and intracoronary nitroglycerin plays a pivotal role in confirming the diagnosis, excluding alternative nonobstructive STEMI mimics, and providing immediate therapy. This case also demonstrates that taxane-induced vasomotor instability may involve multiple coronary territories in a dynamic and potentially sequential fashion, such that angiographic findings at any single point in time may not fully capture the breadth of the vasospastic process. In cases where electrocardiographic and angiographic findings are discordant and clinical suspicion for multivessel vasospasm remains high, intracoronary acetylcholine provocation testing of angiographically normal-appearing vessels represents a valuable adjunctive strategy to unmask latent coronary hyperreactivity and should be considered when hemodynamic stability permits. Heightened awareness of this clinical nuance is critical to optimize acute management and inform subsequent oncologic treatment strategies.
