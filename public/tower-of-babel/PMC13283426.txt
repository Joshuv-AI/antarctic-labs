Central serous chorioretinopathy following intramuscular testosterone therapy in prolactinoma
Authors: Vivek Jha, Pinaki Dutta, Ashley B Grossman, Byanjana Bashyal
Published: 2026

A man in his early 40s presented with decreased libido and erectile dysfunction and was diagnosed with prolactinoma [serum prolactin 886 ng/ml (reference range, 4–15 ng/mL)] with secondary hypogonadism [serum testosterone 2.3 nmol/l (9–28 nmol/L); LH 1.2 IU/L; FSH 1.5 IU/L]. MRI revealed a pituitary microadenoma (9 × 8 mm, Knosp grade II). Other pituitary axes were intact. He was started on cabergoline 2 mg weekly and intramuscular testosterone enanthate 100 mg every 2 weeks for symptomatic hypogonadism.

After 3 months, he developed painless blurred vision in the left eye. Visual acuity was 6/6 in the right eye and 6/24 in the left. Serum testosterone at that time was 27.6 nmol/l (~795 ng/dl). He denied corticosteroid exposure, hypertension, diabetes, or other known risk factors. Fundus photography of the left eye showed subretinal fluid at the macula (Fig. 1A). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the left eye demonstrated neurosensory retinal detachment with subretinal fluid (Fig. 1B), while the right eye showed normal retinal architecture (Fig. 1D). Fluorescein angiography of the left eye confirmed a focal macular leak with a classic ‘smoke-stack’ pattern (Fig. 1C).

Multimodal imaging demonstrating central serous chorioretinopathy. (A) Fundus photograph of the left eye showing subretinal fluid at the macula (arrow). (B) Optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the left eye showing neurosensory retinal detachment with subretinal fluid (arrow). (C) Fluorescein angiography of the left eye demonstrating a focal macular leak with the characteristic ‘smoke-stack’ pattern (arrow). (D) OCT of the right eye demonstrating normal retinal architecture.

Intramuscular testosterone was switched to transdermal therapy, while cabergoline was continued. At 8-week follow-up, the patient reported significant symptomatic improvement. Best-corrected visual acuity improved from 6/24 to 6/9 in the left eye. Follow-up OCT demonstrated marked reduction in subretinal fluid, with central macular thickness decreasing from 412 μm to 268 μm, and a corresponding decrease in the height of neurosensory detachment from 186 μm to 52 μm, consistent with partial resolution.

These findings are consistent with central serous chorioretinopathy (CSC), a multifactorial disorder associated with corticosteroids, stress, and hormonal influences [1]. Exogenous testosterone has been associated with CSC in case reports and small series [2, 3]. Proposed mechanisms include androgen receptor–mediated effects on choroidal vascular permeability and neurovascular dysregulation [4]. Intramuscular testosterone produces greater peak–trough fluctuations than transdermal formulations, which may be relevant to CSC pathophysiology [5]. Notably, transdermal testosterone has also been implicated in CSC, suggesting a possible idiosyncratic response independent of the route of administration [6].

In addition, cabergoline, a dopaminergic agonist, may influence choroidal blood flow through modulation of sympathetic tone, circadian rhythm, and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activity. While a causal relationship cannot be established, the potential contribution of concomitant dopaminergic therapy should be acknowledged. Cabergoline was continued throughout the course of treatment.

However, CSC is inherently multifactorial with a variable natural history, including spontaneous resolution and recurrence. Therefore, causality cannot be definitively established in this case. Clinicians should consider CSC in patients receiving testosterone therapy who present with visual symptoms and ensure prompt ophthalmologic evaluation.
