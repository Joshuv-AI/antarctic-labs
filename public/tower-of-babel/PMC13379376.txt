M polynomial and degree based topological indices of six common synthetic polymers
Authors: Noel George, Usha Arcot, Bruntha Devi Palanisamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The M-polynomial is a powerful algebraic tool that encodes structural information of molecular graphs and enables the derivation of numerous degree-based topological indices through algebraic operations. In this paper, we compute the general forms of M-polynomials for six widely used synthetic polymers modeled as linear chains of n repeating monomer units: Polyethylene (PE), Polypropylene (PP), Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC), Polystyrene (PS), Nylon-6,6, and Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET). Using hydrogen-suppressed molecular graphs, we derive the edge partitions based on vertex degrees for each polymer chain and express the M-polynomials as explicit functions of the chain length parameter n. From these M-polynomials, eleven degree-based topological indices are computed, including the first and second Zagreb indices, the modified second Zagreb index, the general Randić index, the symmetric division degree index, the harmonic index, the inverse sum index, the augmented Zagreb index, the atom-bond connectivity index, and the geometric-arithmetic index. Three-dimensional surface plots of the M-polynomials and comparative analyses of the topological indices across all six polymers are presented. The results reveal distinctive structural characteristics of each polymer class and demonstrate the utility of M-polynomials in the systematic study of polymer molecular topology. Furthermore, we discuss the relevance of these computations to quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR) studies for predicting physicochemical properties of polymers.

Introduction

Chemical graph theory provides a framework for modeling molecular structures as graphs, where atoms are represented as vertices and chemical bonds as edges. A molecular graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G = (V, E)$$\end{document} consists of a vertex set V(G) and an edge set E(G). The degree \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_v$$\end{document} of a vertex v is the number of edges incident to v. Two vertices u and v are adjacent if they share an edge. The minimum and maximum degrees of G are denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta (G)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta (G)$$\end{document}, respectively1,2.

Topological indices are numerical invariants of molecular graphs that correlate with physicochemical properties of chemical compounds. They play a crucial role in quantitative structure–property relationships (QSPR) and quantitative structure–activity relationships (QSAR) studies2–5. The first Zagreb index, introduced by Gutman and Trinajstić1, was shown to be related to the total \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}-electron energy of molecules. Since then, numerous degree-based topological indices have been proposed, including the Randić index6, which correlates strongly with boiling points, surface areas, and chromatography retention times of alkanes, and the atom-bond connectivity index7, which models the enthalpy of formation of alkanes with high accuracy.

Literature review

The concept of M-polynomial was introduced by Deutsch and Klavžar8 as a unifying algebraic framework from which multiple degree-based topological indices can be derived through differential and integral operators. This elegant approach provides a single polynomial from which a wide family of indices can be extracted, thereby avoiding the need for separate derivations for each index.

Since its introduction, the M-polynomial framework has been extensively applied to various molecular structures. Munir et al9. computed the M-polynomial and degree-based topological indices of polyhex nanotubes, establishing the utility of this approach for nanotubular structures. Kwun et al10. extended this work to V-phenylenic nanotubes and nanotori. Raza and Sukaiti11derived M-polynomials for two classes of dendrimer nanostars and four types of nanotubes, providing a detailed comparison of the resulting indices. Ali et al12. applied M-polynomials to zigzag and rhombic benzenoid systems.

In the context of nanostructures and chemical networks, Yu et al13. investigated the M-polynomial and topological indices of several transformed networks, demonstrating the versatility of this framework for complex network topologies. Liu et al14. computed topological properties of concealed non-Kekuléan benzenoid hydrocarbons, extending the applicability of degree-based indices to polycyclic aromatic compounds. Gao et al15. studied the redefined Zagreb indices of titania nanotubes.

Beyond the M-polynomial framework, several studies have demonstrated the strong predictive power of degree-based topological indices in QSPR modeling. Hayat et al4. studied graphs with given connectivity and their minimum Sombor index, demonstrating applications to QSPR studies of monocarboxylic acids through regression analysis. In a related study, Hayat et al5. investigated the extremal hyper-Zagreb index of trees with given segments and showed strong applications to regression modeling in QSPR studies. Dearden3 provided a comprehensive review of the use of topological indices in QSAR and QSPR modeling, establishing their reliability for predicting molecular properties.

The bond additive modeling approach by Vukičević and Gašperov16introduced the Adriatic indices, while Furtula et al17. proposed the augmented Zagreb index. Vukičević and Furtula18 also studied indices based on the ratios of geometrical and arithmetical means of end-vertex degrees.

More recently, several studies have introduced novel variants of Zagreb-type indices and demonstrated their effectiveness in QSPR modeling of physicochemical properties. Çiftçi19 introduced the Zagreb \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document} indices for benzene derivatives and established strong QSPR correlations with experimentally measured properties. In related work, Çiftçi20 proposed the Zagreb omicron indices and Çiftçi21 developed the Zagreb \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document} indices, both with applications to QSPR modeling. Yamaç22 introduced Zagreb \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} indices for benzene derivatives, and Çiftçi, Yamaç and Denizler23 proposed the Zagreb \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upsilon$$\end{document} indices with demonstrated QSPR applications. These studies collectively illustrate the growing applicability of degree-based descriptors in quantitative property prediction and cheminformatics, further motivating the present work.

In the context of extremal graph theory and topological indices, Das and Mondal24 investigated Zagreb connection indices in structure–property modelling, establishing bounds and extremal results that underpin the theoretical foundations of molecular descriptor research.

In the domain of polymer and biochemical structures, Maragadam et al25. computed the M-polynomial and NM-polynomial for selected anticancer drugs, highlighting the practical utility of molecular descriptors in pharmaceutical research. Lokesha et al26. investigated polysaccharides such as amylose and the blue starch–iodine complex using edge-dividing methods, deriving several topological indices and demonstrating the applicability of graph-theoretic descriptors to biochemical structures. Shanmukha et al27. computed M-polynomials and topological indices for Styrene-Butadiene Rubber (SBR), providing insights into the structural characterization of synthetic polymers using mathematical descriptors. In a related study, Shanmukha et al28. conducted a comparative study of multilayered graphene structures using M-polynomials, deriving eleven degree-based topological indices with 3D visualizations to analyze variations among monolayer, bilayer, and trilayer configurations.

Research gap and motivation

Despite the extensive application of M-polynomials to nanostructures, dendrimers, and chemical networks, a notable gap exists in the literature: the systematic computation of M-polynomials and degree-based topological indices for common synthetic polymer chains remains largely unexplored. Polymers such as polyethylene, polypropylene, polyvinyl chloride, polystyrene, nylon, and polyester constitute the most commercially significant class of chemical compounds, with annual global production exceeding 400 million tonnes. Their physicochemical properties, including melting point, glass transition temperature, tensile strength, and solubility, are of immense industrial and environmental importance.

While previous studies have focused on nanotubes9,11, dendrimers11, benzenoid systems12, and transformed networks13, comparatively few systematic studies have derived closed-form M-polynomial expressions for linear polymer chains as functions of the number of repeating units n. This represents a notable gap because: (i)Polymers represent the most widely produced class of synthetic chemical compounds.(ii)The linear chain structure of polymers, with its characteristic terminal effects and periodic interior, offers rich mathematical structure for graph-theoretic analysis.(iii)Establishing topological characterizations of polymer molecular graphs is essential for developing QSPR models3–5 that can predict polymer properties from structural descriptors.The current study addresses this gap by providing, to the best of our knowledge, among the first systematic M-polynomial analyses of six representative commodity polymers.

Molecular graph models of polymer chains

We use hydrogen-suppressed (heavy-atom) molecular graphs where each vertex represents a non-hydrogen atom (C, N, O, Cl) and each edge represents a covalent bond. For a polymer chain of n repeating units, the terminal effects at chain ends produce vertices of reduced degree compared to interior positions. The edge set is partitioned as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E(G) = \bigcup _{i \le j} E_{\{i,j\}}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{\{i,j\}} = \{uv \in E(G): \{d_u, d_v\} = \{i,j\}\}$$\end{document}.

Remark 2.4

The graph-theoretic isomorphism between PP and PVC molecular graphs implies that all degree-based topological indices derived from the M-polynomial are identical for these two polymers at the same chain length. This reflects a well-known limitation of degree-based topological descriptors: they depend solely on vertex degrees and edge connectivity, not on the chemical identity or atomic mass of individual atoms. In the hydrogen-suppressed representation, the pendant methyl carbon in PP and the pendant chlorine atom in PVC both appear as degree-1 vertices, rendering the two graphs indistinguishable. To differentiate such chemically distinct but topologically equivalent structures, one must employ alternative approaches such as weighted molecular graphs, atom-type-specific descriptors, or distance-based indices2.

Nylon-6,6 is a polyamide with repeating unit –[NH–(CH\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document})\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_6$$\end{document}–NH–CO–(CH\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document})\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_4$$\end{document}–CO]–, containing 16 heavy atoms per monomer (2N + 12 C + 2O). Theorem 2.6

Let G be the molecular graph of a Nylon-6,6 chain with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n \ge 1$$\end{document} repeating units. Proof

The linear chain of n repeating units contains 16n vertices and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$16n - 1$$\end{document} edges. Discussion

The results obtained in this study reveal several important structural and topological relationships among the six polymer classes investigated.

Structural complexity ordering

For all computed topological indices and all values of n, we observe a consistent ordering:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {PE} \;<\; \text {PP} = \text {PVC} \;<\; \text {PS} \;<\; \text {Nylon-6,6} \;\lesssim \; \text {PET},$$\end{document}with minor variations for some indices. This ordering reflects the increasing structural complexity of the monomer units, from the simple two-carbon backbone of PE to the aromatic ring-containing PET with its ester linkages and multiple heteroatoms.

Graph-theoretic equivalence of PP and PVC

A notable result is the exact equality of all topological indices for PP and PVC at every chain length (Remark 2.4). This arises from the topological equivalence of their hydrogen-suppressed molecular graphs: both have a pendant atom of degree 1 at each alternate backbone carbon, regardless of whether it is a methyl carbon or a chlorine atom. This demonstrates that degree-based topological indices, by construction, cannot distinguish between chemically distinct but topologically equivalent structures. To differentiate such polymers, one would need to employ weighted molecular graphs or atom-type-specific descriptors2.

Linear growth behavior

All topological indices grow linearly with the number of repeating units n (Corollary 4.3), which is expected for linear polymer chains. The slope of this linear relationship is determined by the structural complexity of the monomer and the distribution of vertex degrees, as confirmed by the comparative plots in Figure 3. PET exhibits the steepest growth rate for most indices due to its large monomer unit (14 heavy atoms) containing an aromatic ring, ester linkages, and carbonyl groups.

Role of aromatic rings

Polymers containing aromatic rings (PS and PET) exhibit significantly higher topological index values compared to their aliphatic counterparts. The phenyl ring in PS contributes 6 additional heavy atoms and 7 additional bonds per monomer, creating a substantial increase in all indices. The para-disubstituted benzene ring in PET introduces degree-3 vertices that further enhance the topological complexity.

Sensitivity of different indices

The augmented Zagreb index (AZ) shows the greatest sensitivity to structural changes, exhibiting the largest numerical range across the polymer series. This is due to its cubic dependence on the edge-weight function. In contrast, the harmonic index (H) and the modified second Zagreb index (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^mM_2$$\end{document}) show relatively modest variations, making them less discriminating for structural comparisons.

M-polynomial surface analysis

The 3D surface plots of the M-polynomials (Figure 2) reveal distinct topographic features for each polymer. Polymers with edges connecting higher-degree vertices (e.g., PET with its (3, 3) edges) show steeper surfaces at higher x and y values. The surface complexity increases with the number of distinct edge types in the partition, with PET showing the most complex surface due to its five distinct edge types.

Basis for systematic topological characterization

Our claim of providing a systematic topological characterization of common polymer structures is grounded in the following observations. First, the M-polynomial encodes the complete degree-based structural information of the molecular graph, and any degree-based topological index can be extracted from it. Second, the eleven indices computed span a diverse set of mathematical operations (sums, products, ratios, roots, powers) applied to vertex degrees, thereby capturing different aspects of the molecular graph topology2. Third, the consistent ordering across all eleven indices and all chain lengths suggests that this ordering reflects genuine structural differences rather than artifacts of any particular index definition. Finally, the linear formulas we provide (Table 7 and Corollaries 4.1–4.2) enable direct computation of index values for any chain length, and could serve as candidate descriptors in subsequent QSPR regression studies3–5 once paired with experimental polymer property datasets.

Potential applications and societal relevance

The six polymers studied in this work collectively account for a substantial fraction of global plastic production. Within the graph-theoretic scope of this study, the topological characterizations we provide may offer the following potential applications, which we present as directions for future empirical work rather than as established outcomes of the present analysis: (i) the computed indices could, in principle, serve as candidate structural descriptors in subsequent regression or machine-learning studies aimed at predicting polymer properties, subject to validation against experimental data; (ii) the structural complexity ordering provides a graph-theoretic reference that may inform comparative studies of polymer alternatives; (iii) the topological signatures of commodity plastics may complement other descriptors used in environmental science research on polymer degradation, though such connections require independent verification; and (iv) the closed-form expressions in n enable straightforward computation of index values for any chain length, which could support future screening or correlation studies. We emphasize that the present work is purely graph-theoretic, and any property-prediction, polymer-design, or machine-learning application would require dedicated experimental and statistical validation beyond the scope of this paper.
