Comparative green synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles using millet extracts and their physicochemical characterization and in vitro antidiabetic activity
Authors: Naveen Pathivada, Gopi Mamidi, Amancharla Indira Priyadarsini, Gunduluru Swathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZinc plays an essential role in glucose metabolism, insulin storage, and antioxidant defense, and its deficiency has been linked to impaired pancreatic β-cell function and increased oxidative stress. In this study, zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) were synthesized through a green, plant-mediated approach using aqueous extracts of three commonly consumed millets (Eleusine coracana (finger millet), Pennisetum glaucum (pearl millet), and Panicum sumatrense (little millet)). The millet extracts acted as natural reducing agents during nanoparticle formation, leading to the production of crystalline ZnO nanoparticles under alkaline conditions.The synthesized ZnO NPs were characterized using UV–visible spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning and transmission electron microscopy (SEM/TEM), dynamic light scattering (DLS), and zeta potential analysis to confirm their phase purity, morphology, crystallinity, and colloidal stability. The nanoparticles exhibited a hexagonal wurtzite ZnO structure with crystallite sizes in the nanometer range, while the hydrodynamic sizes were larger due to surface hydration and aggregation effects.In vitro biological evaluation showed that millet-derived ZnO NPs exhibited dose-dependent inhibition of carbohydrate-digesting enzymes (α-amylase and α-glucosidase) along with moderate inhibition of dipeptidyl peptidase-IV (DPP-IV). Cellular studies using INS-1 pancreatic β-cells and 3T3-L1 adipocytes demonstrated enhanced glucose-stimulated insulin secretion and increased glucose uptake at non-cytotoxic concentrations. In addition, the nanoparticles showed antioxidant activity, as indicated by reduced intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and increased activities of endogenous antioxidant enzymes.Overall, this study demonstrates that millet extracts can be effectively used for the green synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles, producing nanomaterials with promising in vitro antidiabetic and antioxidant properties. The comparative evaluation across different millets also provides useful insight into how phytochemical composition influences nanoparticle characteristics and biological performance.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia resulting from impaired insulin secretion, insulin resistance, or both. The global prevalence of diabetes continues to rise, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, creating an urgent need for complementary and sustainable therapeutic strategies [1]. Oxidative stress and progressive pancreatic β-cell dysfunction are widely recognized as central contributors to the development and progression of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [2]. Zinc is an essential trace element involved in multiple aspects of glucose metabolism, including insulin synthesis, storage, and secretion within pancreatic β-cells. It also contributes to antioxidant defense by regulating redox-sensitive enzymes and maintaining cellular stability [3, 4]. Clinical and experimental studies have demonstrated that zinc deficiency is associated with impaired insulin action, increased oxidative stress, and poor glycemic control, highlighting the importance of zinc homeostasis in diabetes management [5].Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) have emerged as promising zinc delivery systems due to their high surface area, physicochemical stability, and gradual dissolution under physiological conditions [6]. Several in vitro studies have reported that ZnO nanoparticles can modulate carbohydrate-digesting enzymes, enhance insulin secretion, and attenuate oxidative stress in pancreatic and peripheral cell models [7, 8]. However, conventional synthesis routes often involve chemical reducing agents, surfactants, or organic solvents, which may limit their suitability for nutraceutical or food-related applications. Green synthesis using plant-derived extracts offers an environmentally benign alternative for nanoparticle production. In such systems, naturally occurring phytochemicals, including polyphenols, flavonoids, organic acids, and proteins, participate in metal ion reduction and influence nanoparticle nucleation, growth, and stabilization [9, 10]. Compared to chemically synthesized nanoparticles, plant-mediated ZnO nanoparticles are frequently reported to exhibit improved biocompatibility and reduced cytotoxicity [11].

Millets are nutritionally rich cereal grains widely consumed across Asia and Africa. Varieties such as finger millet (Eleusine coracana), pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum), and little millet (Panicum sumatrense) are recognized for their low glycemic index, high dietary fiber content, and appreciable levels of phenolic compounds and essential minerals [12, 13]. Dietary consumption of millets has been associated with delayed carbohydrate digestion, improved antioxidant status, and better glycemic control in diabetic and pre-diabetic populations [14]. The phytochemical composition of millets, particularly their phenolic acids and flavonoids, makes them suitable candidates for green nanoparticle synthesis. The selection of these three millets in the present study is based on their distinct phytochemical profiles, mineral composition, and established relevance in glycemic regulation, which provides a suitable basis for comparative evaluation under controlled synthesis conditions. While plant-mediated synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles using medicinal plants and leaves has been widely reported, comparatively fewer studies have explored cereal grains, particularly millets, as reducing agents for nanoparticle synthesis [15]. Moreover, systematic comparative studies evaluating how different millet species influence ZnO nanoparticle characteristics and biological responses remain limited. In this context, the present study aims to comparatively evaluate whether phytochemical differences among selected millet extracts influence the physicochemical characteristics and in vitro biological responses of ZnO nanoparticles synthesized under identical conditions. The synthesized nanoparticles were characterized to determine their structural, morphological, and colloidal properties, and their in vitro antidiabetic and antioxidant activities were evaluated using enzyme inhibition assays and established cellular models. By adopting a controlled comparative approach, this work seeks to clarify the role of millet-derived extracts in nanoparticle formation while maintaining a reproducible and nutraceutical-oriented experimental framework. Unlike routine green synthesis studies, the present work emphasizes methodological consistency, cautious interpretation of biological responses, and the role of post-synthesis calcination in determining final nanoparticle properties.

Materials and methods

Materials

Finger millet (Eleusine coracana), pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum), and little millet (Panicum sumatrense) grains were procured from cultivated agricultural fields in Chandragiri Mandal, Tirupati District, Andhra Pradesh, India. The millets were grown as seasonal food crops, and prior permission was obtained from local farmers before sample collection. No wild, endangered, or protected plant species were involved in this study [12, 13]. Zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(CH3COO)2·2 H2O, ≥ 99% purity), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, aluminium chloride, α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (pNPG) were purchased from Merck (India) and Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. All aqueous solutions were prepared using ultrapure water [9, 16].INS-1 rat pancreatic β-cells (established rat insulinoma cell line) and 3T3-L1 mouse preadipocytes were obtained from the National Centre for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune, India, and were maintained according to the culture protocols recommended by the supplier [17, 18]. The millet samples, including finger millet (Eleusine coracana), pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum), and little millet (Panicum sumatrense), were botanically authenticated by Dr. A. Indira Priyadarsini, Lecturer in Botany, Government Degree College (Autonomous), Nagari, Andhra Pradesh, India. Voucher specimens of each millet species were prepared and deposited in the departmental herbarium for future reference, with identification carried out based on standard morphological and taxonomic characteristics of cultivated millet varieties [19].

Preparation of millet extracts

Millet grains were thoroughly washed with distilled water, shade-dried, and finely powdered. Ten grams of the powdered millet sample were mixed with 100 mL of distilled water and heated at 60 °C for 1 h under continuous stirring. The resulting extract was allowed to cool, followed by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min, and then filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter. The filtrate was stored at 4 °C and used within 72 h.Total phenolic content (TPC) was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method and expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per gram of extract [16]. Total flavonoid content (TFC) was measured using the aluminium chloride colorimetric assay and expressed as mg quercetin equivalents per gram of extract [20].

Green synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles

Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) were synthesized using a green precipitation method with millet extracts acting as reducing and stabilizing agents. For the synthesis, 100 mL of 1 mM zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(CH3COO)2·2 H2O) solution was prepared in distilled water under constant magnetic stirring at room temperature [9, 10].Subsequently, 20 mL of freshly prepared millet extract was added dropwise to the zinc acetate solution under continuous stirring. The reaction mixture was maintained under stirring for 30 min to ensure uniform interaction between the precursor and phytochemicals [9].The pH of the reaction mixture was then gradually adjusted to approximately 10–11 by the dropwise addition of 1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution. Upon increasing the pH, a white precipitate formed, indicating the formation of zinc hydroxide intermediates [10, 16]. The reaction was continued under constant stirring at 60 °C for 90 min to facilitate complete conversion. The resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min and washed repeatedly with distilled water and ethanol to remove unreacted residues and soluble impurities. The washed product was then dried at 60 °C overnight [16].Finally, the dried material was calcined in a muffle furnace at 300 °C for 2 h to obtain crystalline ZnO nanoparticles. The calcined powder was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature and stored in airtight containers for further characterization and biological evaluation [6, 10].

Characterization of ZnO nanoparticles

UV–visible absorption spectra were recorded in the wavelength range of 200–800 nm to monitor nanoparticle formation. FTIR spectra were obtained in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 to identify functional groups associated with millet extracts and Zn–O bond formation. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) over a 2θ range of 20–80° to determine the crystal structure and phase purity of the nanoparticles. The average crystallite size was estimated using the Scherrer equation [21].Surface morphology and particle size were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Hydrodynamic particle size distribution and zeta potential were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) to assess colloidal stability in aqueous dispersion. Selected analyses were performed using shared or outsourced facilities, and representative data are presented.

In vitro antidiabetic and antioxidant assays

α-Amylase and α-glucosidase inhibition assays were performed using standard colorimetric methods. ZnO nanoparticles were tested at concentrations ranging from 10 to 100 µg/mL. Acarbose was used as a positive control, while enzyme solution without inhibitor served as a negative control [22, 23]. DPP-IV inhibition was evaluated using a commercial fluorometric assay kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and the results are expressed as percentage inhibition at selected concentrations.INS-1 pancreatic β-cells were used for glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) studies. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.3T3-L1 preadipocytes were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and differentiated into adipocytes using a standard hormonal induction protocol involving insulin, dexamethasone, and isobutylmethylxanthine (IBMX), as reported previously [24]. Glucose uptake was assessed using a fluorescent glucose analog.Nanoparticle concentrations were restricted to non-cytotoxic levels (≤ 25 µg/mL) for all cell-based assays. Intracellular ROS levels were measured using a DCFH-DA assay. Activities of antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), were determined using standard colorimetric kits. Cytotoxicity was evaluated using the MTT assay after 24 and 48 h of exposure.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and the data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical comparisons were carried out using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test where applicable. Prior to ANOVA, data normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Although n = 3 is commonly used in in vitro studies, statistical interpretations are presented with appropriate caution [25]. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

UV–visible spectroscopy

The formation of zinc oxide nanoparticles was initially monitored by UV–visible spectroscopy in the wavelength range of 200–800 nm. Corresponding millet extract solutions without zinc precursor were used as blanks to eliminate background absorbance arising from soluble phytochemicals. Zinc acetate solution without extract showed no characteristic absorption in the examined region. Upon addition of millet extracts to the zinc acetate solution under alkaline conditions, the reaction mixture gradually developed a milky white appearance, indicating nanoparticle formation. The UV–visible spectra of the reaction mixtures exhibited a characteristic absorption band in the range of 370–375 nm, which is commonly associated with the intrinsic band-gap absorption of ZnO nanoparticles [6, 19]. To examine the progression of nanoparticle formation, spectra were recorded at different reaction times (0–120 min). The absorbance intensity at ~ 372 nm increased steadily from 0 to 90 min, indicating continuous nucleation and growth of ZnO nanoparticles. Beyond 90 min, no significant increase in absorbance intensity or peak shift was observed up to 120 min, suggesting that the reaction had reached completion. This stabilization of absorbance supports the selection of 90 min as the optimal reaction time for nanoparticle synthesis (Fig. 1). A comparative analysis of ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using different millet extracts revealed minor variations in absorbance intensity, while the peak positions remained nearly unchanged (Fig. 1). ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using pearl millet extract showed slightly higher absorbance intensity compared to finger millet and little millet. This variation is attributed to differences in extract composition, particularly phenolic and flavonoid content, which can influence reduction kinetics and nucleation density [9, 10, 15]. However, since the peak positions were comparable across all samples, the fundamental ZnO band-gap characteristics remained unaffected by the millet source. Overall, the UV–visible analysis confirms the successful formation of ZnO nanoparticles through millet-mediated green synthesis and indicates that millet phytochemicals primarily influence reaction kinetics rather than altering the intrinsic optical properties of ZnO.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to identify functional groups present in the millet extracts and to examine changes associated with ZnO nanoparticle formation. FTIR spectra of the millet extracts exhibited broad absorption bands around 3300–3400 cm−1, corresponding to O–H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups commonly present in phenolic compounds and carbohydrates [15, 20]. Bands observed near 2920–2850 cm−1 are attributed to aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations, while absorption around 1630–1650 cm−1 corresponds to C=O stretching of carbonyl groups or amide I vibrations arising from proteins and amino acid residues present in the extracts [20].After nanoparticle synthesis and subsequent calcination at 300 °C, notable changes were observed in the FTIR spectra of the ZnO nanoparticles. The intensity of bands in the 3000–2500 cm−1 region showed a marked reduction or complete disappearance, indicating thermal degradation or removal of most organic constituents during calcination. This observation is consistent with previous reports on calcined biogenic metal oxide nanoparticles synthesized via plant-mediated routes [9, 10].Despite calcination, weak residual bands corresponding to O–H and C=O vibrations were occasionally observed, which may arise from surface-adsorbed hydroxyl groups or trace carbonaceous residues formed during thermal decomposition. The presence of such residual surface functionalities has been commonly reported for green-synthesized metal oxide nanoparticles and does not imply the retention of intact phytochemical capping layers [10].A distinct absorption band observed in the region of 430–480 cm−1 in all nanoparticle samples is attributed to Zn–O stretching vibrations, confirming the formation of zinc oxide [19, 20]. The appearance of this band, along with the attenuation of organic functional group signals, indicates successful conversion of the zinc precursor into crystalline ZnO (Fig. 2).Importantly, the FTIR analysis suggests that millet-derived phytochemicals primarily function as reducing and complexing agents during the nanoparticle formation stage, facilitating nucleation and growth of ZnO nanoparticles. Following calcination, the nanoparticles are predominantly inorganic ZnO with minimal residual organic signatures. Therefore, the biological effects observed in subsequent assays are more likely associated with ZnO nanoparticle properties and trace surface functionalities rather than intact phytochemical coatings.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The crystalline structure and phase purity of the millet-mediated ZnO nanoparticles were examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. The XRD patterns of all synthesized samples exhibited well-defined diffraction peaks at 2θ values around 31.7°, 34.4°, 36.2°, 47.5°, 56.6°, 62.8°, and 67.9°, corresponding to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), and (112) lattice planes of hexagonal wurtzite ZnO, respectively. These reflections are in good agreement with the standard Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) card no. 36-1451, confirming the formation of crystalline ZnO [21, 22] (Fig. 3). No additional diffraction peaks corresponding to zinc hydroxide, zinc carbonate, or other impurity phases were observed, indicating high phase purity of the synthesized nanoparticles. The sharpness and intensity of the diffraction peaks further suggest good crystallinity, consistent with the calcination treatment applied during synthesis. The average crystallite size of the ZnO nanoparticles was estimated using the Scherrer equation, applied to the most intense (101) diffraction peak. The calculated crystallite sizes were found to be in the range of approximately 22–38 nm across the different millet-derived samples. These values represent the size of coherently diffracting crystalline domains rather than the overall particle dimensions. Minor variations in crystallite size were observed among ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using different millet extracts, which may be attributed to differences in reaction kinetics and nucleation behavior influenced by extract composition during the precipitation stage. It is important to note that XRD analysis does not provide direct information on particle morphology or aggregate size; therefore, crystallite size values should not be directly equated with particle sizes observed in electron microscopy. Importantly, the term “hexagonal” used in the context of XRD refers to the crystal structure of ZnO (wurtzite phase) rather than the external shape of the nanoparticles. Particle morphology is more accurately assessed using SEM and TEM analyses, as discussed in the subsequent section. Overall, the XRD results confirm the successful formation of phase-pure, crystalline ZnO nanoparticles via millet-mediated green synthesis, with crystallite sizes in the nanometer range suitable for further physicochemical and biological evaluation.

Scanning and transmission electron microscopy (SEM and TEM) analysis

The surface morphology and particle size distribution of the millet-mediated ZnO nanoparticles were investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). SEM micrographs revealed that the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles consisted of aggregated nanostructures with relatively uniform grayscale contrast and a rough surface texture (Fig. 4). The observed agglomeration is attributed to the inherently high surface energy of ZnO nanoparticles and the absence of strong organic stabilizing agents following calcination at elevated temperature. Such aggregation behavior is commonly reported for metal oxide nanoparticles synthesized via green and plant-mediated routes, particularly after thermal treatment [26].TEM analysis provided higher-resolution insight into the individual particle morphology and size distribution [27]. The ZnO nanoparticles were predominantly near-spherical to irregular in shape, with particle sizes distributed approximately in the range of 20–80 nm (Fig. 5a). While a limited number of smaller particles in the range of 20–30 nm were observed, the majority of particles were larger, resulting in an average particle size of approximately 40–50 nm. This suggests that the smaller particles represent individual crystallites or less-aggregated domains rather than the dominant particle population. The particle sizes determined from TEM were consistently larger than the crystallite sizes estimated from XRD analysis. This discrepancy is expected, as XRD provides information on the size of coherently diffracting crystalline domains, whereas TEM reflects the overall physical dimensions of particles, which may consist of multiple crystallites fused together. Therefore, the SEM and TEM observations are consistent with the XRD results when the fundamental differences between these characterization techniques are considered [28, 27]. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images further confirmed the crystalline nature of the ZnO nanoparticles, revealing clear lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of approximately 0.26 nm, corresponding to the (002) plane of hexagonal wurtzite ZnO (Fig. 5b). Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns exhibited concentric diffraction rings rather than discrete spots, indicating the polycrystalline nature of the nanoparticles [29].Importantly, although the ZnO crystal structure is hexagonal (wurtzite phase), the external morphology of the nanoparticles is predominantly near-spherical or irregular rather than well-defined hexagonal shapes. This distinction between crystallographic structure and particle geometry is emphasized to avoid misinterpretation. Overall, SEM and TEM analyses confirm the successful formation of crystalline ZnO nanoparticles with nanoscale dimensions and moderate aggregation, consistent with a calcined, millet-mediated green synthesis route.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential analysis

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis was performed to evaluate the hydrodynamic size distribution of ZnO nanoparticles dispersed in aqueous medium. The nanoparticle suspensions exhibited hydrodynamic diameters in the range of approximately 80–110 nm (Fig. 6a). These values are larger than the particle sizes observed in TEM analysis and the crystallite sizes estimated from XRD.This difference is expected and arises from the fundamental distinctions between the measurement techniques. DLS measures the hydrodynamic diameter of particles in suspension, which includes contributions from surface hydration layers as well as nanoparticle aggregation in liquid media. In contrast, TEM provides direct visualization of dried particles under high vacuum, while XRD estimates the size of coherently diffracting crystalline domains. Therefore, the larger size values obtained from DLS do not contradict the SEM/TEM or XRD results, but instead reflect the dispersion behavior of ZnO nanoparticles in aqueous media [26, 28].Zeta potential measurements were carried out to assess the surface charge and colloidal stability of the nanoparticle dispersions. The ZnO nanoparticles exhibited negative zeta potential values in the range of approximately − 24 to − 32 mV, indicating moderate electrostatic stability in aqueous suspension (Fig. 6b). Negative surface charge is commonly observed for ZnO nanoparticles synthesized via aqueous precipitation and green synthesis routes, and it contributes to reduced particle agglomeration through electrostatic repulsion [19].While the observed zeta potential values suggest reasonable short-term dispersion stability, it is important to note that ZnO nanoparticles tend to aggregate over time, particularly in electrolyte-rich or biological media. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as indicative of dispersion behavior under controlled laboratory conditions, rather than as evidence of long-term colloidal stability in complex biological environments. Overall, the DLS and zeta potential analyses provide complementary information on the dispersion characteristics of millet-mediated ZnO nanoparticles in aqueous media. These findings support the suitability of the nanoparticles for in vitro studies, while emphasizing the need to consider aggregation effects when interpreting biological or nutraceutical applications (Fig. 7).

In vitro antidiabetic activity

The in vitro antidiabetic potential of millet-mediated ZnO nanoparticles was evaluated using a combination of enzyme inhibition assays and cell-based functional studies. All experiments were conducted within non-cytotoxic concentration ranges established from preliminary cytocompatibility assessments. Such multifunctional biological responses of green-synthesized nanomaterials have also been highlighted in recent studies focusing on plant-mediated nanocomposites and bioactive systems [30, 31]. The effect of ZnO nanoparticles on glucose-stimulated insulin secretion was examined using INS-1 pancreatic β-cells. Cells were exposed to ZnO nanoparticles at concentrations ≤ 25 µg/mL under basal and high-glucose conditions. Untreated and vehicle-treated cells served as controls. ZnO nanoparticle treatment resulted in increased insulin secretion under high-glucose conditions relative to controls (Fig. 8). This response occurred without a corresponding reduction in cell viability, indicating that the observed effect was not associated with cytotoxic stress. These results are consistent with previous in vitro studies employing INS-1 cells and suggest a supportive effect on insulin secretion under stimulated conditions, without implying the involvement of specific intracellular signaling pathways [32]. Similar functional responses have also been reported for bioactive nanomaterials designed for metabolic regulation [30].

However, it is important to note that the magnitude of increase in insulin secretion observed in this study is moderate. Therefore, the results are interpreted as indicative of preliminary biological interaction rather than strong pharmacological efficacy, and comparisons with potent secretagogues such as GLP-1 analogs were beyond the scope of the present study.

α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibition

ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using different millet extracts were assessed for their ability to inhibit α-amylase and α-glucosidase enzymes at concentrations ranging from 10 to 100 µg/mL. Acarbose was used as a positive control, while enzyme solutions without inhibitors served as negative controls. The ZnO nanoparticles exhibited concentration-dependent inhibition of both enzymes, with partial inhibition observed at higher concentrations (Fig. 7). Complete enzyme suppression was not observed, indicating moderate inhibitory activity.The inhibition is attributed to nonspecific interactions between ZnO nanoparticles and enzyme surfaces or active sites, which may hinder substrate accessibility. Differences in inhibition efficiency among millet-derived ZnO nanoparticles were modest, suggesting broadly comparable enzymatic responses across samples. These findings are consistent with earlier reports on biogenic ZnO nanoparticles evaluated using in vitro enzyme inhibition assays [7, 8, 33], as well as recent studies emphasizing the role of phytochemical-assisted nanomaterials in modulating enzymatic activity [31].

Enzyme activity in the absence of nanoparticles was taken as the control.No kinetic analysis or IC₅₀ determination was performed; therefore, the results are presented as preliminary screening data to assess inhibitory trends rather than definitive enzymatic potency [7, 8]. Such preliminary screening approaches are commonly adopted in recent nanomaterial-based bioactivity studies [30, 34].

Glucose uptake in 3T3-L1 adipocytes

Glucose uptake was evaluated in differentiated 3T3-L1 adipocytes using a fluorescent glucose analog (2-NBDG). ZnO nanoparticles increased glucose uptake relative to untreated controls in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 10). Insulin-treated cells served as positive controls. The enhancement in glucose uptake was moderate and reached a plateau at higher concentrations, consistent with functional responses typically observed in in vitro adipocyte models [24]. These findings indicate that ZnO nanoparticles can influence glucose utilization under controlled in vitro conditions; however, no conclusions regarding molecular mechanisms or in vivo relevance are drawn [35]. Recent studies on plant-derived bioactive systems and sustainable nanomaterials have similarly emphasized cautious interpretation of in vitro metabolic responses prior to translational validation [34, 36] (Fig. 11).

Antioxidant and cytoprotective activity

The antioxidant response of millet-mediated ZnO nanoparticles was evaluated using intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurements and antioxidant enzyme activity assays to assess their influence on cellular oxidative status under in vitro conditions. Intracellular ROS levels were quantified using a DCFH-DA fluorescence assay. Cells treated with ZnO nanoparticles at non-cytotoxic concentrations (≤ 25 µg/mL) exhibited a moderate reduction in ROS levels compared to untreated controls (Fig. 12a). This decrease suggests that ZnO nanoparticles can attenuate oxidative stress under the experimental conditions employed; however, the assay does not distinguish between direct radical scavenging and indirect modulation of cellular redox balance [37]. Such modulation of oxidative stress by green-synthesized nanomaterials has also been reported in recent studies involving plant-derived systems [30, 34].To further assess the antioxidant response, the activities of endogenous antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), were measured using standard colorimetric assays. ZnO nanoparticle treatment resulted in modest but statistically significant increases in enzyme activities relative to control cells (Fig. 12b–d). These observations indicate a limited activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms, potentially associated with alterations in cellular redox homeostasis rather than strong antioxidative protection [38, 26]. Similar trends have been observed in recent nanomaterial-based studies, where moderate enhancement of enzymatic antioxidant responses is reported without direct evidence of long-term cytoprotective effects [34].Cytocompatibility of the ZnO nanoparticles was evaluated using the MTT assay following 24 and 48 h exposure. Cell viability remained above 90% at concentrations up to 25 µg/mL, while a gradual reduction in viability was observed at higher concentrations (Fig. 13). Based on these results, concentrations ≤ 25 µg/mL were considered suitable for subsequent in vitro functional assays. No evidence of acute cytotoxicity was observed within this concentration range [39, 28].It is important to note that the observed antioxidant modulation and cytocompatibility are derived from short-term in vitro assays and do not imply long-term cytoprotection or therapeutic efficacy. Overall, the results indicate that millet-mediated ZnO nanoparticles exhibit acceptable biocompatibility and can moderately influence oxidative stress parameters under controlled experimental conditions. Recent studies on sustainable nanomaterials and plant-derived bioactive systems similarly emphasize the need for cautious interpretation of in vitro findings prior to mechanistic validation and in vivo translation [36]. Therefore, further detailed mechanistic investigations and in vivo studies are required to fully establish their therapeutic potential.

Overall discussion and comparative interpretation

The present study demonstrates that aqueous extracts of finger millet, pearl millet, and little millet can be effectively employed as green reaction media for the synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles. Across all three millet sources, ZnO nanoparticles with comparable crystalline phase, morphology, and colloidal characteristics were obtained, indicating that the synthesis route is robust and reproducible (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6). Comparative analysis revealed that differences among millet-derived ZnO nanoparticles were modest rather than pronounced. Minor variations observed in UV–visible absorbance intensity, crystallite size, and dispersion behavior can be attributed to differences in extract composition, which influence nucleation and growth kinetics during the precipitation stage [9–11].

Importantly, it is recognized that calcination at 300 °C removes most organic phytochemical constituents, resulting in predominantly inorganic ZnO nanoparticles. Therefore, the role of millet extracts is primarily associated with influencing precursor complexation, nucleation behavior, and intermediate gel formation prior to calcination, rather than contributing persistent surface-bound phytochemical functionality. Following calcination, the resulting nanoparticles exhibited broadly similar physicochemical properties, indicating that the inorganic ZnO phase predominantly governs the final material characteristics [6, 19]. This distinction clarifies that the comparative framework reflects synthesis-stage influences rather than retained phytochemical effects in the final product.The in vitro antidiabetic evaluations consistently showed concentration-dependent inhibition of carbohydrate-digesting enzymes and functional modulation of glucose handling in pancreatic β-cells and adipocytes (Figs. 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11). These effects were observed at non-cytotoxic concentrations and were reproducible across ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using different millet extracts. Importantly, the study does not attribute these biological responses to specific intracellular signaling pathways or to the presence of intact phytochemical coatings. Instead, the observed responses are interpreted as functional outcomes of ZnO nanoparticle exposure under controlled in vitro conditions, consistent with previous screening-level studies on biogenic ZnO nanoparticles [7, 8, 35], as well as recent reports on plant-mediated nanomaterials exhibiting comparable biological trends [30, 31].The antioxidant and cytocompatibility assessments further support the biological compatibility of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles, showing reduced intracellular oxidative stress, moderate enhancement of endogenous antioxidant enzyme activities, and maintenance of cell viability at lower concentrations (Figs. 12 and 13). These effects are interpreted as short-term cellular responses rather than definitive evidence of long-term cytoprotection or therapeutic efficacy. Such redox modulation and concentration-dependent cytocompatibility are commonly reported for metal oxide nanoparticles interacting with cellular systems and should be interpreted with caution in the absence of mechanistic or in vivo validation [26, 28]. Similar cautious interpretations have been emphasized in recent studies on sustainable nanomaterials and bioactive systems [34, 36].Overall, the key outcome of this study is that, despite compositional differences among millet extracts, the resulting ZnO nanoparticles exhibit broadly comparable physicochemical and biological characteristics after calcination. This highlights the robustness and reproducibility of cereal-based green synthesis routes and suggests that precursor variability has a limited influence on final ZnO properties under controlled processing conditions. The comparative approach adopted in this study provides insight into the use of nutritionally relevant cereal extracts as green synthesis media for ZnO nanoparticles, while also emphasizing that post-synthesis processing steps, particularly calcination, play a dominant role in determining nanoparticle behavior. Conclusion

In conclusion, zinc oxide nanoparticles were successfully synthesized using aqueous extracts of finger millet, pearl millet, and little millet through a green, phytochemical-assisted route followed by controlled calcination. Structural and morphological analyses confirmed the formation of phase-pure, crystalline ZnO nanoparticles with nanoscale dimensions and moderate aggregation. Comparative characterization demonstrated that, although minor variations in reaction kinetics and dispersion behavior were observed among millet-derived samples, the final physicochemical properties were largely governed by the inorganic ZnO phase. In vitro biological evaluations revealed concentration-dependent enzyme inhibition, functional modulation of glucose handling in pancreatic β-cells and adipocytes, and moderate regulation of oxidative stress parameters at non-cytotoxic concentrations. These effects were reproducible across millet-derived nanoparticles and are interpreted as short-term functional responses under controlled experimental conditions, without invoking specific molecular mechanisms or long-term therapeutic claims. This study provides a systematic comparative evaluation of ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using three nutritionally distinct millet species under identical experimental conditions. The significance of this work lies in its comparative design, the use of edible cereal grains as green synthesis media, and the methodologically conservative interpretation of physicochemical and biological data that explicitly accounts for post-synthesis calcination effects. Rather than attributing biological responses to intact phytochemical coatings or proposing speculative molecular pathways, the findings offer reproducible insight into how millet-derived extracts influence nanoparticle formation, crystallinity, dispersion characteristics, and in vitro functional responses.

However, a limitation of the present study is the absence of comparative evaluation using soluble zinc ions (Zn²⁺), which would help distinguish nanoparticle-specific effects from ionic zinc-mediated responses. Future studies will address this aspect, along with detailed mechanistic investigations and in vivo validation, to better establish the translational relevance of these findings.Overall, this work establishes a reproducible and comparative framework for the green synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles using edible cereal extracts and highlights the importance of synthesis control and post-processing conditions in determining nanoparticle behavior. While the results provide a useful basis for nutraceutical-oriented and green nanomaterial research, further validation is required before practical applications can be realized.
