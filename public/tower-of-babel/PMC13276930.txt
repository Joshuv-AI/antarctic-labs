1.1. Keynote one

Trends in non-communicable diseases pattern in Africa: the paradigm shift

Kaushik L. Ramaiya1,2,3

1Shree Hindu Mandal Hospital, Dar-Es-Salaam, Tanzania; 2Tanzania Diabetes Association, Dar-Es-Salaam, Tanzania; 3Tanzania NCD Alliance (TANCDA), Dar-Es-Salaam, Tanzania

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):1.1.

Abstract

Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs), Injuries and Mental Health conditions (NCDI/MH) are leading causes of disability and premature deaths worldwide and represent a growing proportion of disease burden in African countries. It is estimated that 70% of all global deaths are from NCDs, of which 85% of the premature deaths (30 – 70 years) in 2019 were in low- and middle-income countries.

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) accounted for 17.9 million deaths in 2019, making up 32% of all global deaths. In sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries, mortality due to CVDs is rapidly increasing. CVD-related deaths in SSA reached about 470,000 in 2019 and led to 22.9 million Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALYs). While traditionally infectious diseases such as malaria, tuberculosis and Human Immuno-deficiency Virus (HIV) caused predominant morbidity and mortality, most SSA countries have undergone an epidemiologic transition that is associated with a rapid rise in non-communicable diseases. Metabolic risk factors include elevated blood glucose, hyperlipidemia, central obesity and hypertension. Important socio-demographic challenges such as rural to urban migration, and changes in diet and lifestyle patterns contribute to a rapid rise in the prevalence of these risk factors in SSA.

Every second, 28 lives between the ages of 30 and 70 are cut short because countries have not taken policy, legislative and regulatory measures to respond to the needs of people living with or at risk of cardiovascular diseases, cancers, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases, or mental health conditions, including preventive, curative, palliative, and specialized care.

1.2. Keynote two

How human immune responses catch-up with SARS-COV-2 variants and other coronaviruses

Takamasa Ueno

Joint Research Center for Human Retrovirus infection, Kumamoto University, Kumamoto, Japan

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):1.2.

Abstract

In the recent Corona Virus Disease of 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, official data suggest a relatively milder impact in terms of infection and mortality rates in Sub-Saharan Africa including Tanzania. This might be due to the substantial proportion of cross-reactive immunity against Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome-Corona Virus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) in Africa, attributable by the circulation of various coronaviruses among different bat species in this region. However, on the other side, its impact may be potentially underestimated, because coronavirus infections and the nature of immune responses in the African population remain largely understudied. To address this issue, we looked at the frontline health care workers (HCWs) as this population group is at high risk of exposure to SARS-CoV-2 and other coronaviruses circulating within the community. Based on the collaboration with Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences, we recruited 200 frontline HCWs at the two tertiary hospitals in Dar es Salaam two years into the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, and investigated the heterologous nature of the neutralizing immunity against coronaviruses including not only various SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern but also SARS-CoV-1, Middle East Respiratory Syndrome-Corona Virus (MERS-CoV), HCoV-229E, and HCoV-NL63. Despite low vaccination rates (<20%), we observed a broad range of neutralizing activity in HCWs, but no neutralization activity detected against MERS-CoV. We also observed a strong correlation between neutralizing antibody titers for SARS-CoV-2 and SARS-CoV-1, but not between other coronaviruses. Cross-neutralization titers against the newer Omicron subvariants, BQ.1.1 and XBB.1.5, were significantly reduced compared to BA.1 and BA.2 subvariants. The results highlighted a broad range of neutralizing potency against coronaviruses in Tanzanian HCWs.

In addition, we have made progress in elucidating how human cytotoxic T cell responses can retain responsiveness to SARS-CoV-2 variants in vaccine recipients.

1.3. Keynote three

Tanzania’s journey to universal health coverage: overcoming challenges, embracing opportunities, and ensuring healthy lives for all

Ntuli Kapologwe1,2

1East, Central and Southern Africa Health Community (ECSA-HC), Arusha, Tanzania; 2School of Nursing and Public Health, The University of Dodoma, Dodoma, Tanzania

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):1.3.

Abstract

Introduction: Tanzania’s pursuit of Universal Health Coverage (UHC) is a pivotal component of its broader health system reforms, aiming to provide all citizens with access to quality health services without financial hardship. UHC encompasses a wide range of services, from preventive to curative care, ensuring that no one is left behind due to socioeconomic factors. The key elements driving this transformation include sufficient and equitable funding, improved healthcare access for all income groups, and a focus on the quality of health services. Tanzania’s efforts are shaped by international health frameworks such as the WHO’s intrinsic goals for health systems, which emphasize improving health outcomes, fairness in financial contributions, and system responsiveness.

Progress and Challenges: Over recent years, Tanzania has made notable strides in improving healthcare access and quality. The availability of essential health commodities at health facilities has increased, and vaccination coverage has risen significantly, with immunization rates reaching 94% by 2022 while UHC service coverage index is 43. Institutional deliveries have also improved, contributing to a reduction in maternal mortality. However, significant disparities remain in some regions, such as Mwanza and Kilimanjaro, where further interventions are needed. Additionally, challenges persist in healthcare quality, particularly regarding early antenatal care, which remains underutilized. The Health Basket Fund (HBF), which supports primary healthcare services, continues to be a major source of financing, although issues such as fund mismanagement and delayed disbursements have been reported.

The introduction of the Universal Health Insurance Act in 2023 marks a significant milestone, providing a legal framework for mandatory health insurance aimed at expanding coverage and ensuring equitable access to healthcare. In parallel, the government has launched the Integrated Community Health Program, which involves over 137,294 community health workers (CHWs) to address gaps in rural and underserved areas.

Conclusion: Despite significant progress, Tanzania faces ongoing challenges in its path toward achieving UHC. Key areas for improvement include ensuring effective fund management, addressing regional disparities, and enhancing the responsiveness and quality of healthcare services. Moving forward, Tanzania’s strategy will focus on optimizing resource allocation, promoting strategic health purchasing, and expanding evidence-based interventions to meet the health needs of its population. By continuing to strengthen both community-based and facility-based services, Tanzania aims to realize the goals of UHC and improve overall health system performance, ensuring that all citizens have access to the healthcare they need to live healthy lives with well -being.

Harnessing the power of implementation science and innovation in addressing local health challenges

Stephen Kibusi1,3, Emmanuel Sindato2, Mwajuma Mdoe1

1School of Nursing and Public Health, The University of Dodoma, Dodoma, Tanzania; 2School of Medicine and Dentistry, The University of Dodoma, Dodoma, Tanzania; 3Directorate of Research, Publications and Consultancy, The University of Dodoma, Dodoma, Tanzania

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):2.1.

Abstract

Implementation science aims to bridge the gap between research findings and practical applications, exploring to ensure that interventions are implemented successfully and yield intended outcomes. The plenary lecture will explore a range of factors influencing implementation research, including identifying the contextual factors that influence implementation, identifying and addressing barriers and facilitators, and utilizing strategies to promote sustainable and effective implementation. Drawing lessons from disciplines, such as behavioral science, organizational science, and health systems research. The lectures will explore the philosophical paradigms, theories and established frameworks, monitoring and evaluation strategies, stakeholders’ engagement and scale up and spread strategies that inform and guide the implementation process. Implementation strategies will be explored in line with the conventional research designs to help the audience gain insight on the topic. Drawing lessons from locally implemented projects, the lecture through the panelists will discuss lessons learned and the way forward in applying implementation science and innovation in our local health challenges. By understanding the principles of implementation science, researchers, policymakers, and practitioners can enhance the successful adoption and integration of evidence-based interventions, leading to improved outcomes in healthcare.

2.2. Plenary two – reproductive maternal newborn child and adolescent health

Moderator: Prof. Ipyana Mwampagatwa

Progress in reduction of maternal and child deaths in Tanzania: ingredients for success.

Ahmad Mohamed Makuwani1, Pius Kagoma2, Mwajuma Mdoe3

1Department of Reproductive, Maternal and Child Health, Ministry of Health, Dodoma, Tanzania; 2Prime Minister’s Office – Regional authorities and Local Governments, Dodoma, Tanzania; 3School of Nursing and Public Health, The University of Dodoma, Dodoma, Tanzania

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):2.2.

Abstract

Introduction: Tanzania has made progress in meeting the third Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) targets. Reports from Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey - TDHS (2022) show remarkable decline of Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR) to 104 from 556 in 2015/2016. Also, the under-five mortality ratio (U5MR) declined from 67 in 2016 to 43 in 2022. Whereas, the newborn mortality ratio has not improved as much, standing at 26, 25, 24 in 2010, 2016 and 2022 respectively.

Design: A quantitative desk review of the health system data from 2015 - 2023. No conflict of interest and ethical issues as cited data are already public.

Results: Leadership has been a pacemaker; through from President Kikwete’s Primary Health Services Development Program (PHSDP) (2007) and Sharpened One Plan (2014 - 2016), President Magufuli’s construction of Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (EmONC) health facilities (2017 - 2021), President Samia’s launch of ‘Jiongeze Tuwavushe Salama’ campaign (2018) and signing of performance contracts on nutrition by the Regional Commissioners (2022). These initiatives improved United Nations (UN) EmONC Process Indicators by increasing service access within 5 km to 75% - 85%, institutional delivery to 81%, caesarean section rate to 11% and reduced case fatality to 0.7%.

Other ingredients to the gain include use of telemedicine through WhatsApp groups by primary healthcare workers to access specialized care remotely. Telemedicine enabled virtual review of maternal and perinatal deaths identifying suboptimal skills, practice, attitude and leadership as leading causes of maternal and newborn deaths hence providing targeted solutions. Growth of the network of paved roads and better means of transport; clean and safe water supply in towns (86%) and villages (72%); electricity coverage to all districts, 3,225 out of 3,956 wards (82%) and 10,294 out of 12,268 villages (84%); and improved mobile phone and internet connectivity; have proven to be valuable factors in the reduction of maternal and child mortality.

Conclusion: Tanzania is on track to SDG 3 targets of MMR < 70 per 100,000 live births and U5MR < 25 per 1000 live births by 2030. However, neonatal care is lagging hence calling for investment. Achieving SDG 3 requires political will, health system strengthening and investment on other services beyond health.

2.3. Plenary three – navigating the nexus of NCDs and organ transplantation in Tanzania: insights from UDOM and Benjamin Mkapa Hospital (BMH) joint initiatives

Moderator: Dr. Mohamed Zahir

2.3.1. Presentation one

End-stage kidney disease and renal transplantation program: medical perspectives

Alfred Meremo

Department of Internal Medicine, School of Medicine, the University of Dodoma, Dodoma, Tanzania

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):2.3.1.

Abstract

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is estimated to affect more than 850 million people worldwide and resulted in over 3.1 million deaths in 2019. Kidney disease ranks as the 8th leading cause of death, and if left unaddressed, is projected to become the 5th leading cause of years of life lost by 2040. The onset of CKD is often undetected, with less than 10% of the patients found with CKD stage 3, or lower who are aware of their disease. Chronic kidney disease diagnosis is frequently made in CKD stage 4, when the symptoms become more evident. CKD can rapidly and quietly progress to late CKD stages in impoverished environments culminating into end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). Metabolic risk factors that contribute to increased risk for CKD include; raised blood pressure, overweight/obesity, high blood glucose levels and high levels of fat in the blood. Tobacco use, physical inactivity, the harmful use of alcohol, unhealthy diets and air pollution all increase the risk of dying from CKD. Chronic kidney disease is a common disorder associated with significant health care expenses, resulting in cardiovascular events, ESKD and eventually death. Many people worldwide, and mostly in low and lower-middle-income countries (LIC/LMIC) where health care resources are severely limited, in need of kidney replacement therapy (KRT) as a life-sustaining treatment do not receive it. Dialysis resources in sub-Saharan Africa, if available, are generally limited but in high demand. Clinicians at the bedside are faced with the dilemma of deciding who lives and who dies; to most patients, dialysis services are very expensive and are not affordable. In Tanzania, it is estimated that there are about 5,800 – 8,500 people with ESKD requiring dialysis or kidney transplant. Until 31st January 2022; a total of 2,750 patients were on dialysis (32% of those requiring dialysis) and 325 received kidney transplants. Kidney transplantation (KT) is the getaway from chronic dialysis; it is cheaper and has better outcomes in terms of improved quality of life when compared to dialysis. The University of Dodoma (UDOM) took initiatives in April 2016 to request the Tokushukai Medical Group to assist in establishing kidney transplant services in Dodoma at the Benjamin Mkapa Hospital (BMH), a UDOM teaching hospital. The collaboration between the Ministry of Health, Tokushukai Medical Group, UDOM and BMH has enabled 40 kidney transplant operations since 23rd March 2018.

2.3.2. Presentation two

End-stage kidney disease and renal transplantation program: surgical perspectives

Masumbuko Mwashambwa1,2

1Department of Surgery, School of Medicine and Dentistry, The University of Dodoma, Dodoma, Tanzania; 2Department of Surgery, Benjamin William Mkapa Hospital, Dodoma, Tanzania

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):2.3.2.

Abstract

Introduction: Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects almost a billion (850M) people which is 10% of the global population. It is 20 times the HIV and cancer prevalence and twice as much as tuberculosis. In 2019 CKD contributed 2·4% of all causes of death and caused 41.5 million DALYs. Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) affects 7%-18% of hospitalized patients and 20-200 per million persons in the community. Spending is very high for both governments and persons. Tanzania is among countries which offer both dialysis, with 3327 patients on dialysis in 11 regional referral hospitals, and kidney transplant (KT), with 128 cases done so far. The University of Dodoma (UDOM) and Benjamin Mkapa Hospital (BMH) have pioneered renal replacement therapies in Dodoma.

Brief History of Kidney transplant at UDOM- BMH: The BMH is a brain-child of UDOM. Renal replacement therapy was initiated by H.E. The Late Benjamin William Mkapa, the Retired President of the United Republic of Tanzania and Chancellor of UDOM, and the then Chairman of the Tokushukai General Incorporated Association (TGIA), Dr. Tarao Tokuda in Tokyo, Japan. The meeting culminated in signing of a memorandum of understanding between the Ministry of Health (MoH) and TGIA, the latter paving a way for strategic cooperation, donation of 10 dialysis machines, training and initiation of dialysis (2013) and later to KT at BMH in 2018.

Our team: One of the secrets of KT achievements at UDOM and BMH is teamwork and partnership. The team is composed of nephrologists, surgeons, anesthesiologists, nurses, laboratory scientists, social workers and pharmacists from both institutions, but increasingly majority from BMH. This team is led by a kidney transplant committee, which is led by two co-chairpersons and holds regular meetings. The deliberations are channeled to the hospital management team.

Patients’ characteristics and outcomes: BMH-UDOM has operated 40 patients so far, the male to female ratio is 2.6:1, with mean; age of 43.7 years (R: 11-63, SD 11.6) and BMI of 24.7 (R:17.7-65, SD 7.8), hemoglobin of 11.8 (R; 6.07-15.7, SD, 2.3), eGFR of 8.3 (R:2.71-21, SD, 4.9), serum creatinine of 812 mmol/L (R: 237-1761.87, SD, 352.8), and BUN of 18.02 mg/dl (R: 5.2-45, SD, 8.6) and albumin of 39.7 g/dl (R;26.6-52, SD,1706).

Two thirds (68%) were either employed or business persons; almost all had formal education (97%), one was a foreigner and all except one were on dialysis. The mean duration on dialysis of 23.2 months (R 1-72, SD 21.9), 27.8%, 38.9% and 33.3% were using temporary catheters, permanent catheters and arteriovenous fistulas respectively. One quarter of the patients reported access related complications. Half of the patients were blood group O+, 22.2% A+, 16.7% B+, 8.3% AB and one case O-. At BMH, 20.5% of donors were spouses, all of them wives to husbands. Immunologically, 70% of the donations were of intermediate to high risk, with approximately 40% needing plasma exchange to remove antibodies. Most donors had a single renal artery (84.6%), 11% had double and only one had three arteries. Induction immunotherapy was done using MMF, Tacrolimus, Rituximab and Basiliximab in almost all cases. An anti-thymocyte Immunoglobulin was used in only one case.

All recipients were approached via modified Gibson curvilinear incision, vascular exposure and dissection were done in similar manner in all recipients. In 51% of cases end-to-side was the vascular technique, and in those with accessory arteries (5 cases) a common channel was the most common technique used. An extravesical Lich-Gregory uretero-neo-cystomy with a double J-stent was used in all cases.

Regarding outcomes; 92% had a favorable outcome (although two got cardiac arrest and one had delayed kidney function) and three kidneys were lost (one possibly due to thrombotic rejection or skill mix). Wound complications occurred in six patients (2 had superficial surgical site infection and 4 sterile dehiscence), and one patient had prolonged lymphorrhea. In-hospital mortality occurred in one patient (2.6%), three (7.7%) patients died due other causes (two died of COVID-19 and one due to rejection a few years after transplant). The overall mortality is therefore 10.3%. The median hospital stay is 22.5 with 75 percentile of 28.7 days.

Conclusion: Strategic partnership between UDOM, Tokushukai Medical Corporation Inc and BMH, team work, dedication and hard work have enabled a successful initiation of sustainable KT service in Dodoma. The pace is slow, steady and faced by different challenges, but transplant continues. KT at BMH has given chances to high-risk patients, some who would have needed referral to other countries. The general results are promising, and the future of transplant is guaranteed.

2.3.3. Presentation three

Sickle cell disease and bone marrow transplantation program

Shakilu Jumanne1, Stella Malangahe2, David Mashala2, Fabio Giglio3, Cornelio Uderzo3, Alphonce Chandika2

1Department of Pediatrics, School of Medicine and Dentistry, University of Dodoma, Dodoma, Tanzania; 2Department of Pediatrics, Benjamin Mkapa Hospital, Dodoma, Tanzania; 3Help3 Onlus, Monza, Italy

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):2.3.3.

Abstract

Introduction: Sickle cell disease (SCD) is the commonest inherited monogenic hemoglobin disorder globally. Tanzania carries one of the highest global birth incidences of SCD estimated at 6 per 1000 live births, and about 11,000-14,000 children are born with the disease annually. About 50%-90% of children with SCD are bound to die before 5 years of age without access to proper treatments. Using the conventional supportive therapies such as Hydroxyurea and transfusions, the life expectancy of people with SCD rarely exceeds 45 years. Access to bone marrow transplantation (BMT) and other curative options for children with SCD in lower-middle-income countries (LMICs) has been limited for many years due to a lack of expertise and infrastructures required to safely offer these treatments. We present results of a collaborative project to establish the BMT program at BMH in Dodoma, Tanzania.

Project implementation: In 2018, the project was launched to establish a BMT center involving an Italian Charity of BMT experts, HELP3-ODV based in Monza, Italy with experts from BMH, and UDOM. Areas of focus included upgrading the existing infrastructures (BMT ward, diagnostic and blood bank services) to meet the standards for safe BMT and to train a dedicated multidisciplinary team of experts required to run the service. A special BMT unit was renovated and installed with required facilities: Seven restricted access isolation rooms, portable (PlasmaAir Sentinel) air filtration machines and equipment such as x-ray blood irradiator and Terumo apheresis machine for exchange transfusion. Training of staff was done onsite by visiting experts from Italy, virtual teleconference and short-term clinical placements in Italy. Trained team included 2 specialist doctors, 4 nurses, 2 laboratory scientists, one interventional radiologist and 1 biomedical engineer. This was followed by developing standard operating procedures according to local circumstances to guide various aspects of BMT care. The first 3 patients were admitted for transplant in January 2023 using Busulfan, Rabbit Antithymocyte Globulin (ATG), and Cyclophosphamide (Cytoxan) as a conditioning regimen. An Anti-CD-20, Rituximab was given at day -1 for prevention of post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease. Prophylaxis of graft versus host disease (GVHD) consisted of methotrexate and cyclosporine. Bone marrow was used as stem cells source from HLA-identical siblings for all patients.

Project progress: From January 2023 to May 2024, a total of 10 patients have been transplanted. Eight patients were males and two females with median age 8 (range 5-12 years). Median Total Nucleated Cells (TNC) infused is 4.665x108/kg (range 2.6-6.1x108). All patients engrafted with a median Absolute Neutrophil Count (ANC) and platelet count engraftment at day + 27 (range 19-41) and at day + 25 (range 15-35), respectively. All patients experienced febrile neutropenia, responding to broad-spectrum antibiotics. One patient had Plasmodium falciparum infection post-transplant, and the bacteria isolated from blood culture included Coagulase-negative Staphylococci and Gram-positive rods and Pseudomonas aeruginosa in one patient. Grade II skin acute Graft versus Host Disease (aGVHD) was diagnosed in three patients and treated with Methylprednisolone and Mycophenolate Mofetil with complete remission. At a median follow-up of +163.5 (range 18-478) days from transplantation, no severe GVHD has been diagnosed. One patient was diagnosed with oral Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) lesions treated with cryotherapy. All patients remain transfusion-free, without SCD events after BMT.

Conclusion: Bone marrow transplantation is a highly needed lifesaving therapy in LMICs and can be offered without major adjustments in human and financial resources. Conventional routine SCD care is in the long run equally expensive compared to BMT which alleviates the disease and its associated clinical, social and economic impact to the patient, family and the community at large.

2.4.1. Presentation one

Fostering global one health resilience and sustainability: mobilizing global resources and expertise to meet the 7-1-7 target for outbreak detection, notification, and response in Tanzania

Gerald Misinzo

SACIDS Foundation for One Health, Sokoine University of Agriculture, Morogoro, Tanzania

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):2.4.1.

Abstract

In order to end pandemics, it is essential to detect the earliest signs of a potential outbreak fast enough and take local actions to prevent global spread. Early detection and pathogen identification is by far the most achievable and cost-effective way to prevent local threats from becoming global threats. The timely identification and genetic characterization of pathogens of animal and public health importance during outbreaks is now possible using next-generation sequencing technologies. Technology is now available for using fit-for-purpose field deployable genomic-based technologies using battery-powered nucleotide amplification thermocyclers, electrophoresis machines and next-generation sequencers for outbreak confirmation and pathogen identification at community level in humans and animals. This setup has enabled the detection and genetic characterization of pathogens in reservoirs, vectors, animals and humans proving its application in One Health and contribution towards achieving the 7-1-7 target to make the world safer from pandemics. A community of practice mobilizing global and local (glocal) resources and expertise is highly recommended to foster global health resilience and sustainability.

Keywords: One Health, next-generation sequencing, pandemics

2.4.3. Presentation three

Successes and challenges in antimicrobial stewardship in a developing country setting: implications and opportunities

Jeremiah Seni1,2,3

1Catholic University of Health and Allied Sciences, Mwanza, Tanzania; 2Bugando Medical Centre, Mwanza, Tanzania; 3National Antimicrobial Stewardship Technical Working Group – AMR MCC, Tanzania

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):2.4.3.

Abstract

Introduction: Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the global calamities with socio-economic, clinical and economic adverse impacts. Existence of multidrug resistant bacteria continues to challenge responsive measures and threatens limited antimicrobial therapeutic options. Hence, a critical need to capitalize on antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) programs.

Methodology: This review evaluated AMS implementation status since launching of the first National Action Plan on AMR (NAP-AMR) in 2017 through review of published research, guidelines, standard operating procedures and protocols to highlight challenges and opportunities.

Results: The AMS implementation is on-going in 29 hospitals in Tanzania (9 tertiary, 13 secondary, and 7 primary level hospitals). These include 3 private hospitals. Three key guidelines were developed namely National AMS Policy Guidelines (2020), AWaRe classification of antibiotics (2020), and Standard Treatment Guidelines and National Essential Medicines List (STG/NEMLIT, 2021). The country has consistently submitted AMR and antimicrobial consumption data to the WHO-GLASS; and conducted AMS performance using standardized tools like WHO-PPS, and WHO-AMS Core Element Indicators (at national and facility levels). Remarkable AMS performance is attested by the 2nd WHO Joint External Evaluation (score: level 3 - developed capacity), and the national core elements for AMS in the WHO African Region (advanced level score:106/132 and Tanzania ranked 1st among 31 countries). Of interest, STG/NEMLIT and AWaRe guidelines are currently under review, and will incorporate AMR surveillance data to reflect evidence-based local context. Finally, the National AMS curriculum was developed, and is accredited by all professional bodies in the human sector to foster human resource capacity building. The AMS in-country experts continue to build capacity in other African countries. Limited involvement of primary level health care facilities and private sectors, financial constraints and unappealing utilization of laboratory services to guide rational antimicrobial therapies are key challenges.

Conclusions: AMS implementation in Tanzania is progressing well with remarkable strides locally and regionally. Alignment of AMS implementation activities to ensure congruence across public and private hospitals is reiterated. Translating generated AMS evidence to guide rational antimicrobial therapies, and subsequently improve patients’ outcomes should be fostered.

Keywords: Antimicrobial stewardship, Tanzania, World Health Organisation

Advocating adolescents’ cognition, affection, livelihood, and life skills: exploring the acceptability and feasibility of a 5-step afrocentric pedagogical framework for developing integrated sexual and reproductive health materials in a problem-based pedagogy in Tanzania

Walter Millanzi

Department of Nursing Management and Education, School of Nursing and Public Health, The University of Dodoma, Dodoma, Tanzania

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):3.1.

Abstract

Introduction: The majority of adolescents are currently becoming sexually active before their 18th birthday having to battle with gender-based violence, early sexual debuts, teenage pregnancies, sexually transmitted infections (STIs), early parenthood, and school dropouts. This study designed and prototyped the 5-step Afrocentric pedagogical framework for developing integrated sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR) materials in a problem-based pedagogy (PBP) in Tanzania

Methods: The study employed a descriptive qualitative design using a constructivist research approach among 36 research trainers and 647 adolescents in Tanzania. The principal investigator benchmarked tools from previous studies to develop the Experts Appraisal Inventory (EAI), Trainers Experience Inventory (TEI), and Adolescent Experience Inventory (AEI) for data collection. An inductive and deductive qualitative data analysis approach were used to manage and analyze data. Findings were presented in quotations and concepts.

Results: There was a felt need to develop integrated sexual and reproductive health materials in a Problem-Based Pedagogy using a 5-step Afrocentric pedagogical framework. Participants appreciated the framework reporting that it was relevant, age-appropriate, contextualized, simple, friendly and easy to use to run a session. Others appreciated the use of puzzled real-life initiators and opportunities to engage in learning activities, share their lived experiences and develop pedagogical mini-projects to be implemented in their communities.

Conclusion: The 5-step Afrocentric pedagogical framework for developing integrated sexual and reproductive health materials in a Problem-Based Pedagogy was feasible and acceptable as reported by the studied population. Its adoption to large-scale interventions may function as a multidisciplinary pedagogical tool to assist peer educators, and caregivers in providing adolescents with relevant, age-appropriate, and comprehensive Sexual and Reproductive Health and Rights in a contextualized manner.

Keywords: Adolescents; problem-based-pedagogy; sexual and reproductive health; sexually transmitted infections; pregnancy; livelihood; life skills

3.2. Session two – health system strengthening

Moderator: Dr. Stephen Kibusi

Innovative health technologies in improving quality of care

Issa Sabi

Mbeya Medical Research Center, National Institute for Medical Research, Mbeya, Tanzania

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):3.2.

Abstract

Innovative health technologies are playing a pivotal role in enhancing the quality of care across healthcare systems globally. As a result, the Tanzania Health Sector Strategic Plan V (2021-2026) has prioritized the development of innovative health technologies for increased productivity, efficiency and quality of healthcare in the country. Here, we highlight the influence of several cutting-edge technologies in place including telemedicine, electronic medical records (EMR), mobile health (mHealth) applications, wearable devices, artificial intelligence (AI), and robotics.

Telemedicine is expanding access to medical services in remote and underserved areas, reducing travel burdens, and enabling timely consultation. EMRs enhance information sharing and better coordination among health care providers, leading to better patient management and continuity of care. mHealth apps empower patients to manage their health through reminders, health education, and remote monitoring, improving adherence to treatment and preventive measures. Wearable devices facilitate continuous monitoring of vital signs, allowing early detection and timely intervention for chronic conditions. AI-driven diagnostic tools support healthcare workers in making accurate diagnosis, particularly in resource limited settings. Robotics improves surgical precision and operation efficiency. Additionally, solar powered health technologies ensure reliable power supply for medical equipment in rural areas, enhancing the functionality of health facilities.

Tanzania has witnessed major advances in introduction and utilization of digital health technologies in routine healthcare with over 180 apps/systems currently in use. However, the challenges remain on the fragmentation of the existing systems due to the various standalone health programs; therefore, more emphasis should be put on interoperability and exchange of information for these systems to meet full potential in delivery of healthcare.

3.4. Session four – food security, environmental sustainability and climate change

Moderator: Mr. Jacktan Ruhighira

Safeguarding health: grain storage practices and public health risks

Rashid Suleiman

Department of Food Science and Agro-processing, School of Engineering and Technology, Sokoine University of Agriculture, Morogoro, Tanzania

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):3.4.

Agricultural grains food production is seasonal normally once per year. However, the demand for grains is evenly spread throughout the year. Thus, grain storage is important to ensure a constant supply throughout the year. Generally, grains are stored for three main purposes; as food from one harvest to the next, for seed, and to sell when prices are very high during the lean season. Majority (85%) of the smallholder farmers store their grains in traditional structures. It is estimated that about 25% - 40% of food grains produced in developing countries are stored in households in traditional storage. However, most of the traditional grain storage structures are rudimentary and vulnerable to attack from several stored-product insect pests and mold. Mold growth in grain is associated with the production of mycotoxins. Mycotoxin contamination has attracted worldwide attention because of its greatest public health risk and its impact on the economy and trade. The most important groups of mycotoxin contamination in cereal grain are aflatoxins (AF), ochratoxins (OT), trichothecenes, zearalenone (ZEN), and fumonisins (F). Among these AFs are the most notorious mycotoxins. AFs are secondary metabolites produced by fungi, particularly Aspergillus flavus. The most common types of AFs are AFB1, AFB2, AFG1, AFG1, AFM1, and AFM2. Aflatoxin B1 is among the most potent ones and is classified by the (International Agency for Research on Cancer) IARC as a group 1 carcinogen due to its toxic, carcinogenic, teratogenic, mutagenic, and immunotoxic effects. It is thought to be the primary cause of human liver cirrhosis as well as liver cancers. Liver cancer is one of the most common and deadly types of cancer diseases, whose occurrence has been strongly correlated with dietary exposure to aflatoxins, and individuals who are chronically infected with the Hepatitis B or C virus. To reduce the problems of mycotoxins contamination and safeguard the health of consumers in developing countries, the grain should be properly dried, sorted and cleaned to remove any contaminated grain, and stored in airtight (hermetic) storage containers to control moisture migration, insects and mold.

Multivariate logistic regression identified moderate to severe motor dysfunction (AOR 10.18(2.33 –57.61), p= 0.004), intellectual disability (AOR 7.61(1.99 – 29.09), p=0.002), increased urination frequency/incontinence (AOR 12.18(2.33 –63.61), p= 0.003), history of abdominal/flank pain (AOR 5.25(1.07 – 25.76), p=0.041), positive leukocyte esterase (AOR 1.56(1.13 – 5.86), p=0.003) and positive nitrate (AOR 3.59(2.43 – 5.86), p<0.001) as strong predictors of positive urine culture.

Conclusion and Recommendations: UTI prevalence among CP children was significantly high, with E. coli as the primary causative agent displaying concerning antibiotic resistance patterns. This underscores the necessity for guideline reviews on antibiotic selection. Scheduled urine cultures and sensitivity analyses are recommended for CP children exhibiting predictive factors for UTI.

Keywords: Cerebral palsy, urinary tract infection, antibiotic resistance, culture sensitivity, Dodoma

4.5.6. Prevalence and determinants of urogenital schistosomiasis among adolescents in Mtama district Lindi - 2023

Baraka Mbuba1, Kennedy Ngowi2, Edward Lwidiko3, Huda Omary3

1Benjamin Mkapa Hospital, Dodoma, Tanzania; 2Kairuki Memorial Hospital, Dar-Es-Salaam, Tanzania; 3Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Science, Dar-Es-Salaam, Tanzania

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):4.5.6.

Introduction: Approximately 250 million people are believed to be afflicted with schistosomiasis worldwide, with 80% of those cases occurring in Sub-Saharan Africa. Tanzania ranks as the second most burdened country after Nigeria, with recorded overall prevalence over 50%. The 2022 report states that regional prevalence across the country ranges from 12.5% to 87.6%. The most effective public health strategy to combat schistosomiasis globally continues to be preventive chemotherapy. This intervention in Tanzania has mostly targeted school-aged children, and excluded children of other age groups who are also at risk. The study aimed to determine the prevalence of urogenital schistosomiasis among adolescents at Mtama district Lindi.

Methodology: A quantitative cross-sectional study was carried out with 712 participants in the Mtama district from May to August 2023. A urine sample was collected from all participants and was transported for examination and investigation at MUHAS laboratory, membrane filtration technique was used to examine presence and intensity of eggs in the urine whereas eyes observations and dipstick were used to determine macro and micro-haematuria respectively. Knowledge and awareness of urogenital schistosomiasis was assessed through a questionnaire given to each participant. Data was analysed using SPSS software where multivariable logistic regression was employed to test for associations and a P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, ethical clearance was obtained from MUHAS.

Results: In this study the overall prevalence of urinary schistosomiasis among adolescents, determined based on microscopic examination of S. haematobium eggs in urine was found to be 17.5%. A significant association was identified between hematuria and urogenital schistosomiasis (p-value<0.000), where 93.3% of adolescents with hematuria were infected. The level of knowledge among adolescents concerning urogenital schistosomiasis was intermediate, where 64% of the study population had knowledge concerning urogenital schistosomiasis.

Conclusion and recommendations: The prevalence of urogenital schistosomiasis was associated with low levels of education, frequent contact with contaminated water among adolescents, poor attitude, and poverty. It is recommended to have community mass chemotherapy since there are other age groups who are at risk to be infected by urogenital schistosomiasis.

4.5.7. The spatiotemporal analysis of malaria-related school absenteeism in Tanzania

Emanuel Peter1, Onduru Gervas Onduru1, Susan Rumisha2, Hamisi Japhari1, Jackline Nkoma1

1National Institute for Medical Research, Dar es Salaam, Tanzania; 2Malaria Atlas Project, Geospatial Health and Development, Telethon Kids Institute, Perth, Australia

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):4.5.7.

Introduction: School-age children (6-15) remain the major reservoirs of the malaria parasite among malaria-endemic countries. Little is known about the impact of higher rates of parasitemia and asymptomatic infections in this age group on school outcomes such as absenteeism in Tanzania. This study aimed to determine the impact of malaria on school absenteeism in Tanzania using national school malaria parasitaemia surveys (SMPS) data.

Methodology: This is a retrospective secondary data analysis. The study utilized School Malaria Parasitemia Surveys (SMPSs) data collected between 2015 and 2021. STATA software version 17.0 (Stata Corp LP, College Station, USA) was used to analyze data. Initially, descriptive analysis was performed to summarize data, then, Logistic regression was applied to model the relationship between school absenteeism and predictor variables. Furthermore, the QGIS software was used to display the spatiotemporal variation of malaria-related absenteeism in schoolchildren.

Results: About 230,713 pupils were included whereby 42 548 (18.44%) were absent from school the last two weeks before the survey of whom 9,534 (22.58%) students were absent due to malaria. The prevalence of malaria absenteeism has shown a decreasing trend from 28% in 2015 to 18% in 2021. However, Geita consistently had the highest proportion of malaria-related school absenteeism over four years (2015-2021). Furthermore, malaria absenteeism was higher in rural areas (26.76%) than in urban areas (8.95%).

Conclusion and Recommendations: Malaria-related school absenteeism is 22.58%, implying that almost a quarter of school absenteeism is due to malaria and has shown a marked variation in zones and regional levels over the years. The findings could be used to guide targeted malaria intervention and setting priorities on resource allocation to address the negative effect of malaria on school attendance.

Keywords: Malaria, secondary data analysis, school absenteeism

4.6.2. Climate conscious healthcare: assessing healthcare worker knowledge and behaviors to foster climate advocacy and accountability in the healthcare sector

Sophia Kara, Ahmed M. Jusabani, Sisawo Konteh

Aga Khan Health Services Tanzania (AKHST), Dar-es-Salaam, Tanzania

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(22):4.6.2.

Introduction: The healthcare sector is one of the top contributors to climate change, a crisis labeled as the biggest global health threat of this century. Alarmingly, the rise in climate-related diseases creates a positive feedback loop whereby higher healthcare demands lead to even more sector emissions. Research demonstrates that healthcare workers (HCWs) are deemed as trustworthy sources of information and can inspire climate action at the individual and broader societal levels by addressing misinformation and leading through example. However, for patient-targeted educational initiatives to be effective, understanding and bridging gaps in the climate knowledge and practices of HCWs is needed to build their capacity to act as environmental advocates.

Methodology: A cross-sectional, online survey was administered to all AKHST HCWs and included questions to assess their awareness of climate change’s impact on health, understand their participation in workplace climate action, and identify barriers encountered towards promoting environmental sustainability in their healthcare practice. Convenience sampling was utilized to ensure maximum participation of all HCWs for the generalizability of findings.

Results: Preliminary results indicate that, while a considerable proportion acknowledges the link between healthcare activities and climate change, barriers such as resource constraints and lack of training exist. Positive correlations emerge between sustainable practices and perceived improvements in patient outcomes, reinforcing the interconnectedness of healthcare and environmental stewardship.

Conclusion: Much is being spoken about the consequences of climate change to health. However, the integration of the health sector into addressing climate change is still at an infancy stage. In striving for system change, this study underscores the pivotal role of training HCWs as environmental advocates to leverage the compounding effect that behavior change can have. To transform healthcare ecosystems toward a sustainable future, interventions should target knowledge gaps, integrate climate change curriculum into medical education, and foster a culture that promotes climate change adaptation and mitigation.

Keywords: Healthcare Workers, Climate Change Knowledge, Environmental Advocacy, Healthcare Sector
