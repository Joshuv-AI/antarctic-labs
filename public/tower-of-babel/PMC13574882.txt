An assessment of the effectiveness of hydroxyurea therapy in children and adolescents with sickle cell anaemia at Kenyatta National Hospital
Authors: Madline Yacoub-Kenyi Leju, Amsalu Degu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sickle cell anaemia (SCA) is a major public health concern in Kenya, particularly affecting children and adolescents. Despite the inclusion of hydroxyurea in national treatment guidelines, its real-world use and effectiveness in Kenya remain under-documented, especially in tertiary care settings such as Kenyatta National Hospital (KNH). The drug is known to reduce vaso-occlusive crises (VOCs), transfusions, and hospitalizations, but its impact in resource-limited environments requires further investigation. Hence, this study aimed to assess the clinical and hematological effectiveness of hydroxyurea therapy in children and adolescents with sickle cell anaemia at Kenyatta National Hospital. A retrospective one-arm cohort design was used to review medical records of pediatric patients with sickle cell anaemia (≤ 16 years) who were treated with hydroxyurea at Kenyatta National Hospital between January 2020 and December 2023. Eligible patients had received hydroxyurea therapy for a minimum duration of six months and had at least 12 months of documented follow-up data. Clinical outcomes, including the frequency of vaso-occlusive crises, hospitalizations, acute chest syndrome, stroke events, and transfusion requirements, were assessed alongside hematological markers such as hemoglobin level, mean corpuscular volume, and reticulocyte count. Data were collected using a structured data abstraction tool adapted from previously published studies. Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29, with one-way analysis of variance used to examine changes in clinical and hematological parameters across multiple follow-up time points. A total of 54 children and adolescents with sickle cell anaemia (mean age: 6.8 ± 4.4 years) were included, all of whom had a substantial burden of disease at baseline, marked by frequent vaso-occlusive crises (2.6 ± 1.9), hospital admissions (2.4 ± 2.0), blood transfusions (2.9 ± 1.7), and episodes of acute chest syndrome (1.3 ± 0.9). Over the 3, 6, 12, and 24-month follow-up period after initiation of hydroxyurea therapy, overall clinical outcomes showed a general decline in the proportion of patients experiencing vaso-occlusive crises, hospitalizations, transfusion needs, and infections compared with baseline. Hematological parameters demonstrated progressive improvement, with mean hemoglobin rising from 7.8 ± 1.1 g/dL at baseline to 10.5 ± 2.0 g/dL at 24 months, mean corpuscular volume increasing from 77.0 ± 21.1 fL to 96.7 ± 13.2 fL, and reticulocyte counts decreasing from 16.3 ± 11.9% to 6.0 ± 6.9%. Hydroxyurea therapy was associated with sustained improvements in hematological indices, characterized by increased hemoglobin levels and mean corpuscular volume, alongside a reduction in reticulocyte counts over the follow-up period. These changes were accompanied by reductions in key clinical complications, including vaso-occlusive crises and hospitalizations.

Background

Sickle cell anaemia is an inherited blood disorder marked by chronic hemolytic anemia and frequent vaso-occlusive episodes. This condition arises from a mutation in the β-globin gene, which leads to the production of abnormal hemoglobin S (HbS) [1, 2]. Under low oxygen levels, HbS polymerizes, causing red blood cells to take on a rigid, sickle shape [3]. These deformed cells are susceptible to hemolysis and vascular blockage, resulting in complications like pain crises, acute chest syndrome, stroke, and long-term organ damage [4]. It is usually diagnosed in infancy or early childhood and requires lifelong management to mitigate severe morbidity and mortality.

Worldwide, millions are impacted by the disease, with Sub-Saharan Africa representing approximately 75% of all SCA cases [5, 6]. The World Health Organization (WHO) regards SCA as a significant public health issue in Africa due to its prevalence, early onset, and lack of adequate healthcare access [7].

Sickle cell anaemia remains a significant public health concern in Kenya, contributing to substantial morbidity and mortality among children and adolescents. The condition leads to recurrent hospital admissions, frequent pain crises, transfusion dependence, and long-term organ complications, all of which impose a heavy burden on affected families and the healthcare system [8, 9]. Despite this escalating public health challenge, the Kenyan health system struggles with several obstacles in delivering comprehensive care, such as insufficient provider training, high treatment costs, and a lack of specialized services.

Hydroxyurea was initially developed as a cytotoxic agent, but it has now emerged as a vital disease-modifying treatment for sickle cell anemia. The drug enhances the production of fetal hemoglobin (HbF), which helps to prevent the sickling of red blood cells and promotes better cell survival. Research, including the BABY HUG trial [10], the HUSOFT extension [11], and the LaSHS trial [12], has shown its effectiveness in decreasing painful episodes, acute chest syndrome, and the need for blood transfusions, while also improving survival rates and clinical stability for both children and adults. Hydroxyurea has been demonstrated to be safe and effective in infants as young as nine months [10].

In low-income settings, hydroxyurea has similarly beneficial effects in reducing hospitalizations and complications [13, 14]. Nevertheless, its effectiveness is hindered by challenges such as inadequate drug availability, weak monitoring systems, low adherence due to concerns about side effects or caregiver exhaustion, and a lack of provider experience [15, 16]. Variations in how patients respond because of pharmacokinetic differences further complicate standard dosing regimens, emphasizing the need for personalized treatment approaches [17].

Hydroxyurea, a well-studied and cost-effective therapeutic agent, has been shown globally to reduce the frequency of vaso-occlusive crises, hospitalizations, transfusions, and the risk of acute chest syndrome and stroke in both pediatric and adult populations with SCA [4, 10]. It achieves this by increasing fetal hemoglobin (HbF) levels, reducing hemolysis, and improving red blood cell function. Its long-term use is also associated with improved survival and quality of life [13]. Despite these proven benefits and its inclusion in Kenya’s national guidelines for SCA management [18], the uptake and utilization of hydroxyurea therapy in clinical practice remain remarkably low, largely due to concerns about drug safety, inadequate healthcare provider training, lack of routine monitoring infrastructure, and limited awareness among caregivers [19, 20].

At Kenyatta National Hospital, the leading referral center in the country, there is insufficient evidence concerning the real-world effectiveness of hydroxyurea and the factors influencing its success for children and adolescents with sickle cell anemia. Research has concentrated mainly on awareness and usage rather than clinical outcomes. This creates a significant evidence gap that limits efforts to refine treatment protocols and enhance patient outcomes. This study seeks to assess the clinical effectiveness of hydroxyurea treatment in children and adolescents with sickle cell anemia at Kenyatta National Hospital.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a retrospective one-arm cohort design to evaluate the effectiveness of hydroxyurea therapy in children and adolescents with Sickle cell anaemia. The study involved reviewing medical records of pediatric patients who had been on hydroxyurea therapy at Kenyatta National Hospital from 1 st January 2020 to 31 st December 2023. As a single-arm observational study, it assessed changes in clinical and hematological outcomes within the cohort before and after the initiation of hydroxyurea treatment.

Study setting and period

The data collection was conducted over three months, from 1 st August 2025 to 29th November 2025, in the Pediatric Hematology Department at Kenyatta National Hospital. Kenyatta National Hospital is a level 6 hospital located in Upper Hill, approximately 5 km southwest of Nairobi’s city center. The hospital has a substantial bed capacity of over 2,100 beds and functions as a national referral center, offering dedicated facilities for patient care, teaching, and research.

Study population

The study population included children and adolescents aged 16 years and below who had been diagnosed with sickle cell anaemia and received outpatient or inpatient care at Kenyatta National Hospital between 1 st January 2020 and 31 st December 2023.

Sample size

The sample size was determined using a census approach. A total of 120 medical records of children and adolescents diagnosed with sickle cell anaemia and managed at Kenyatta National Hospital between 1 st January 2020 and 31 st December 2023 were reviewed. Out of 120 medical records, 66 of them were excluded from the study. Thirteen (n = 13) were excluded because the duration of hydroxyurea therapy was less than six months, while fifty-three (n = 53) were excluded due to incomplete documentation of key clinical outcomes, including history of hospitalization (n = 20), blood transfusion (n = 15), and vaso-occlusive crises (n = 18). Hence, 54 medical records met the inclusion criteria and were included in the study.

Research instruments

Data for this study were collected using a structured data abstraction form that was specifically designed to extract relevant information from patients’ medical records. The tool captured data on sociodemographic variables such as age, sex, and date of diagnosis; clinical parameters including frequency of vaso-occlusive crises, hospitalization rates, transfusion history, incidence of acute chest syndrome, and stroke; and hematological markers such as hemoglobin levels, mean corpuscular volume, and reticulocyte count. The data collection tool was adapted from previously validated instruments used in similar retrospective studies [21–25], with modifications made to align with the specific objectives and context of the present study.

Pretesting

To enhance the reliability and validity of the data collection tool, it was pretested on 5% of the estimated study sample drawn from a similar setting that was not included in the final study population. Feedback obtained from the pretest informed necessary adjustments to the format, terminology, and layout of the tool before the main data collection.

Data collection techniques

Prior to data collection, ethical approval was obtained from the Kenyatta National Hospital–University of Nairobi Ethics and Research Committee to ensure compliance with ethical standards. The study was also registered with the Kenyatta National Hospital Medical Research Department. Following approval, formal authorization was sought from the Health Information Department to access medical records. A list of pediatric and adolescent patients diagnosed with sickle cell anaemia between 2020 and 2023 was extracted using unique hospital identification numbers, forming the sampling frame for the study. Longitudinal data were collected at multiple time points—baseline (before hydroxyurea initiation), 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, and 24 months—to assess both short-term and sustained treatment effects. The researcher used a structured data abstraction tool to extract relevant information from eligible patient records that met the inclusion criteria, including confirmed sickle cell anaemia diagnosis, age ≤ 16 years, and a minimum of six months of hydroxyurea therapy. Collected data included sociodemographic characteristics, clinical history, details of hydroxyurea treatment, and treatment outcomes such as hospitalization rates and hematologic responses. All data were managed confidentially, with patient identifiers removed to protect privacy.

Data analysis

Collected data were entered, cleaned, and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.01. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and baseline clinical characteristics. Continuous variables such as age, hemoglobin levels, mean corpuscular volume, and reticulocyte count were summarized using means ± standard deviation and medians, while categorical variables such as sex and comorbidity status were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Clinical and hematological outcomes were analyzed at baseline, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, and 24 months after initiation of hydroxyurea therapy. Results were presented using means, standard deviations, percentages and frequency tables. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to compare the difference in the clinical parameters, such as vasooclusive crises, hospitalization rate, transfusion requirements and infection over the time period.

Operational definition of terms

Acute chest syndrome

A serious complication of sickle cell disease characterized by chest pain, fever, cough, and pulmonary infiltrates on chest X-ray.

Hematological response

Describes the measurable improvements in blood parameters following hydroxyurea therapy, such as increased hemoglobin concentration and decreased reticulocyte counts.

Infection

It is defined based on documented clinical assessment of fever, elevated neutrophil count or positive microbiological blood culture results.

Stroke

It is defined as an onset of neurological deficit characterised by unilateral weakness, based on clinical assessment and supported by computed tomography (CT) brain imaging.

Vaso-occlusive crises

Painful episodes caused by sickled red cells obstructing blood vessels that require hospitalisation and treatment with opioid analgesics.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

The majority of participants were male 31 (57.4%), while females accounted for [23] 42.6%. The mean age of the participants was 6.8 ± 4.4 years, indicating that most of the cohort consisted of young children. Regarding genotype distribution, HbSS was the predominant genotype, accounting for 49 (90.7%) of participants, followed by HbSC 2 (3.7%) and Sβ⁺ thalassemia 3 (5.6%). Most participants (37, 68.5%) had no documented comorbidities, while respiratory and infectious conditions were the most commonly reported among those with comorbidities (Table 1).

Clinical outcomes following hydroxyurea therapy

At baseline, patients experienced a high burden of disease, with a mean of 2.6 ± 1.9 vaso-occlusive crises (VOCs), 2.4 ± 1.9 hospital admissions, and 2.9 ± 1.7 blood transfusions. Following initiation of hydroxyurea therapy, improvements were observed in most clinical outcomes, particularly during the first 12 months. The proportion of patients with no vaso-occlusive crises increased from 48.1% at 3 months to 55.6% at 12 months, although a decline was observed at 24 months (40.7%). Hospitalization-free participants increased from 55.6% at 3 months to 72.2% at 12 months, with a slight reduction at 24 months (63.0%). Blood transfusion requirements also declined over time, with over two-thirds of participants remaining transfusion-free at all follow-up points. Half of the participants (50.0%) experienced no acute chest syndrome during follow-up. Stroke events were rare, with 85.2% of participants having no documented stroke over 24 months. Other infections remained consistently low across follow-up periods (Table 3).

Mean corpuscular volume increased steadily from 90.7 ± 12.2 fL to 96.7 ± 13.2 fL, consistent with expected hydroxyurea-induced macrocytosis. Discussion

This study provides comprehensive evidence on the clinical and hematological impact of hydroxyurea therapy among children and adolescents with sickle cell anaemia managed at Kenyatta National Hospital. The demographic profile of the patients characterized by a predominance of male participants and a high frequency of the HbSS genotype, is consistent with patterns reported in both African and global sickle cell populations. HbSS remains the most severe and clinically burdensome genotype, often associated with frequent vaso-occlusive episodes, chronic hemolysis, and increased healthcare utilization, which explains its dominance among patients initiated on hydroxyurea therapy [26].

The observed reduction in vaso-occlusive crises within the first 12 months of therapy reflects the well-established clinical benefits of hydroxyurea. In this study, the proportion of participants with no VOCs increased from 48.1% at 3 months to 55.6% at 12 months. Similar trends have been reported in a Ugandan study where a reduction in mean VOC episodes from 3.4 to 1.6 episodes per year (approximately a 53% reduction) among SCA children [27], while a Nigerian study reported a 50–60% reduction in pain crises within the first year of treatment [28]. In high-income settings, a 44% reduction in annual VOC rates was observed, while the BABY HUG trial documented a reduction from 2.3 to 1.4 episodes per year [4, 29]. These findings support the biological plausibility of the improvements observed in this cohort, as hydroxyurea increases fetal hemoglobin production, thereby reducing hemoglobin S polymerization, red cell sickling, and vascular obstruction.

Similarly, hospitalization-free status increased from 55.6% at 3 months to 72.2% at 12 months. Comparable reductions in hospitalization have been reported in sub-Saharan Africa, which ranged from 1.8 to 0.6 admissions per patient peryear following hydroxyurea initiation [13]. In contrast, studies from the United States reported a 60–70% reduction in hospital admissions over two years of follow-up [15]. The difference in magnitude likely reflects earlier treatment initiation, consistent drug availability, and structured adherence support in high-income settings.

The slight decline in clinical improvement observed at 24 months was reflected by a reduction in hospitalization-free patients to 63.0% in the study setting. This contrasts with the more sustained benefits reported in developed countries, where hospitalization reductions remain stable beyond two years [15]. Similar attenuation of benefit has, however, been reported in African settings, where adherence challenges, treatment interruptions, and stock-outs are common [16, 24]. In routine clinical practice, a simplified flat-dose regimen (commonly 500 mg once daily) was also used in some patients, at initiation or during follow-up in the study setting. Limited dose escalation in some patients may also have contributed to the observed reduction in clinical outcomes with continuous use of hydroxurea therapy.

Blood transfusion requirements remained relatively low throughout the follow-up period. This aligns with evidence that hydroxyurea reduces severe anemia and acute complications that necessitate transfusion, thereby lowering the risk of transfusion-related complications. In a Kenyan study, a reduction in transfusion frequency from 2.1 to 0.9 transfusions per year was reported following hydroxyurea initiation [30]. Similar findings have been reported in Uganda and Nigeria, where transfusion reductions of 40–60% have been documented [27, 28]. In contrast, studies from Europe and North America reported transfusion-free proportions exceeding 80–90%, likely due to earlier disease control and better supportive care [4].

Acute chest syndrome and stroke were infrequent during follow-up, with over 94% of patients being stroke-free at 24 months, suggesting a low incidence of cerebrovascular complications. This is comparable to findings from African studies reporting stroke-free rates of 92–96% among children on hydroxyurea [13, 31]. In high-income countries, hydroxyurea has been associated with stroke risk reductions of up to 70% [4], suggesting that the protective effect observed in this study is consistent, though potentially limited by later initiation and less frequent neurovascular monitoring.

In this study, infection prevalence declined from 22.2% at 12 months to 16.7% at 24 months. Similar delayed benefits have been reported in an African setting, where a 35% reduction was observed in infectious episodes after two years of continuous therapy [13]. In contrast, studies from developed countries often reported earlier reductions in infection rates, sometimes within the first year, likely reflecting better immunization coverage, prophylactic antibiotic use, and prompt access to treatment [15].

Baseline data demonstrated a high burden of vaso-occlusive complications (mean of 2.56 ± 1.91 episodes), reflecting the severity of disease before hydroxyurea initiation. Comparable baseline burdens have been reported in African pediatric patients, with mean VOC frequencies ranging from 2.5 to 4 episodes per year [13, 27]. Lower baseline complication rates reported in some high-income settings likely reflect earlier diagnosis and preventive care through newborn screening programs [32].

The hematological findings provide strong objective evidence of treatment effectiveness. In this study, mean hemoglobin increased from 9.55 ± 1.73 g/dL at 3 months to 10.49 ± 2.02 g/dL at 24 months, representing an increase of approximately 1.7 g/dL from baseline. Similar improvement of hemoglobin have been reported in other African studies [13, 27] and in high-income settings [4, 15]. The steady increase in mean corpuscular volume across follow-up intervals is a well-recognized pharmacodynamic marker of hydroxyurea exposure and adherence. Macrocytosis reflects the production of fetal hemoglobin–rich erythrocytes, which are larger and more resistant to sickling. African studies report MCV increases of 15–20 fL within the first year of therapy, while developed-country studies report similar magnitudes, confirming macrocytosis as a reliable marker of hydroxyurea exposure and adherence [27]. The consistent rise in MCV observed in this study supports sustained biological response to therapy.

The marked reduction in reticulocyte counts from baseline (16.3 ± 11.9%) to 12 months (4.4 ± 6.0%) indicates reduced bone marrow stress and decreased compensatory erythropoiesis secondary to hemolysis. The slight increase observed at 24 months may reflect intercurrent illness, adherence variability, or physiological fluctuations common in chronic hemolytic disorders. Importantly, reticulocyte levels remained substantially lower than baseline, indicating ongoing therapeutic benefit. Comparable reductions have been reported in Uganda (from 12.5% to 5.1%) and in United States pediatric patients (from approximately 10–12% to 4–6%) [13, 15].

Taken together, the clinical stabilization and hematological improvements observed in this study reinforce hydroxyurea’s role as an effective disease-modifying therapy for children and adolescents with sickle cell anaemia. The findings are consistent with both controlled trials and real-world evidence, while also highlighting contextual challenges related to long-term adherence and follow-up in resource-limited settings.

Study limitations

This study was limited by its retrospective design, which depended on routinely documented medical records and restricted control over data completeness, accuracy, and consistency. Key variables such as medication adherence, precise hydroxyurea dosing adjustments, and reasons for treatment interruptions were not consistently recorded, which may have influenced the observed clinical and hematological outcomes. In addition, the absence of a control group of untreated patients restricted the ability to attribute observed changes exclusively to hydroxyurea therapy, while missing data at some follow-up points constrained more robust longitudinal analyses. Even though fetal haemoglobin level is important in assessing the efficacy of hydroxyurea, it was not routinely performed in the study setting. Hence, treatment response was assessed using the available clinical outcomes, including frequency of vaso-occlusive crises, hospitalizations, and transfusion requirement. The study also did not assess the safety profile of hydroxyurea therapy using haematological parameters. Hence, future studies should also address the safety profile of this treatment among sickle-cell patients.

Conclusion

The findings of this study showed that hydroxyurea therapy was associated with notable clinical and hematological improvements among children and adolescents with sickle cell anaemia managed at Kenyatta National Hospital. Reductions in vaso-occlusive crises, hospital admissions, transfusion requirements, and infectious complications were observed, particularly within the first year of treatment, alongside objective hematological improvements including increased hemoglobin levels, rising mean corpuscular volume, and reduced reticulocyte counts. Although some attenuation of clinical benefits was observed at 24 months, the overall results support hydroxyurea as an effective disease-modifying therapy in this population and reinforce its role in improving disease control and reducing morbidity in pediatric sickle cell anaemia. Enhanced caregiver education and adherence monitoring strategies are recommended to sustain clinical benefits and minimize treatment interruptions. Future studies should adopt prospective, multicenter designs and appropriate comparison groups to improve generalizability, strengthen causal inference, and further evaluate long-term outcomes, quality of life, and health system impacts of hydroxyurea therapy in the Kenyan context.
