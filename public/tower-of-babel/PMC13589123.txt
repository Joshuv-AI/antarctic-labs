Quality attributes of chicken breast proteins following sequential enzymatic hydrolysis and polyphenol conjugation
Authors: W.Y. Son, C. Arjin, J. Hwang, J.H. Youn, K.S. Kim, K. Sringarm, N. Chaiwang, P. Setthaya, H.W. Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present study evaluated the effects of sequential enzymatic hydrolysis and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) treatment on the physicochemical characteristics, techno-functional properties, antioxidant capacity, and in vitro digestibility of chicken breast proteins using a 2 × 2 factorial design consisting of intact protein (CB), EGCG-treated protein (CB-EGCG), hydrolyzed protein (CBH), and EGCG-treated hydrolyzed protein (CBH-EGCG). Chicken breast proteins were hydrolyzed using Alcalase (50 °C, 2 h), followed by EGCG treatment under alkaline conditions (pH 9.0, 24 h). Enzymatic hydrolysis increased sulfhydryl content and turbidity, whereas EGCG treatment reduced sulfhydryl content and further increased turbidity. FTIR spectra showed noticeable changes in the amide regions and phenolic-associated spectral regions following hydrolysis and EGCG treatment, particularly in CBH-EGCG. Bound polyphenol content was higher than free polyphenol content in both EGCG-treated groups and was significantly greater in CBH-EGCG than in CB-EGCG (P < 0.05). Water-holding capacity, oil absorption capacity, and emulsifying activity index increased following hydrolysis and EGCG treatment, whereas emulsion stability decreased after hydrolysis alone but improved following EGCG treatment. DPPH radical scavenging activity and ferric-reducing antioxidant power were significantly increased by both hydrolysis and EGCG treatment, with CBH-EGCG exhibiting the highest antioxidant activity (P < 0.05). In vitro digestibility increased following hydrolysis, whereas EGCG treatment reduced digestibility in intact proteins. However, CBH-EGCG maintained digestibility comparable to CBH. Overall, controlled enzymatic hydrolysis followed by EGCG treatment may provide a practical processing strategy for developing chicken breast protein ingredients with improved functional and antioxidant properties while maintaining desirable digestibility.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of protein-enriched food markets has increased the demand for protein ingredients with not only high nutritional value but also improved functionality and technological properties (Day, 2013; Loveday, 2019). Chicken breast protein, characterized by its favorable amino acid composition and high nutritional value, has recently gained attention as a potential ingredient for protein powders and reconstituted food systems (Dalle Zotte et al., 2020). However, its broader application remains limited by poor solubility and weak interfacial functionality, which hinder its performance in complex food systems (Qiu et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2023).

Enzymatic hydrolysis has been widely applied as a processing strategy to improve the technological properties of food proteins. Proteolytic enzymes such as Alcalase cleave peptide bonds, resulting in reduced molecular size and increased exposure of hydrophilic groups, which generally improve dispersibility and solubility (Korhonen and Pihlanto, 2006; Udenigwe and Aluko, 2012). However, the extent of hydrolysis critically influences the resulting functional properties; excessive hydrolysis may weaken the structural integrity required for interfacial film formation, thereby negatively affecting emulsifying stability and other techno-functional properties (Ma et al., 2024).

Protein–polyphenol interactions, particularly those involving (−)-epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), have been investigated as an approach to improve antioxidant activity and interfacial functionality in food protein systems (Wu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021, 2023). However, these interactions may also reduce protein digestibility by limiting enzyme accessibility or masking proteolytic cleavage sites (Cirkovic Velickovic and Stanic-Vucinic, 2018; He et al., 2019). Because enzymatic hydrolysis alters protein molecular size and conformation, it may influence subsequent EGCG interactions and the resulting functional and digestive properties (Ma et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2024). Thus, the sequential application of controlled enzymatic hydrolysis and EGCG treatment may provide an effective strategy for improving chicken breast protein functionality while minimizing undesirable effects on digestibility.

In our previous study (Son et al., 2025), EGCG conjugation was shown to enhance the functional properties of chicken breast protein. However, the effects of the structural state of the protein prior to conjugation were not considered. In particular, limited information is available regarding how prior enzymatic hydrolysis influences subsequent EGCG conjugation and the resulting quality-related functional and digestive properties of chicken breast protein systems. Therefore, this study examines how enzymatic hydrolysis modulates EGCG conjugation and its impact on the functional and digestive properties of chicken breast protein. The findings will support the development of protein ingredients with enhanced solubility and interfacial performance, thereby expanding the applicability of chicken protein in formulated food systems without compromising nutritional quality and technological attributes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design

This study was designed to evaluate the individual and combined effects of enzymatic hydrolysis and EGCG conjugation on the quality attributes of chicken breast protein. A 2 × 2 factorial arrangement was applied, with enzymatic hydrolysis and EGCG conjugation as the two main factors. Four treatment groups were prepared: intact chicken breast protein (CB), Alcalase-hydrolyzed chicken breast protein (CBH), EGCG-conjugated chicken breast protein (CB-EGCG), and EGCG-conjugated chicken breast hydrolysate (CBH-EGCG).

Sample preparation

Commercial microwave-assisted vacuum-dried broiler chicken breast powder (CB, moisture content ≤ 7%) was obtained from a domestic food manufacturer (Seobifood Co. Ltd., Namyangju-si, Republic of Korea). The powder was dispersed in deionized distilled water (DDW) to a concentration of 20% (w/v) and homogenized using a high-speed homogenizer (HG-15A, Daihan Scientific, Korea) at 10,000 rpm for 2 min. The pH of the suspension was adjusted to 8.0 using 0.1 M NaOH prior to enzymatic hydrolysis.

Enzymatic hydrolysis was performed according to the procedure of Kurozawa et al. (2008) with minor modification, using Alcalase 2.4 L (EC 3.4.21.62; declared activity 2.4 AU/kg; density 1.18 g/mL; Novozymes, Denmark). The enzyme-to-substrate ratio was set at 1% (w/w, based on protein concentration). Hydrolysis was conducted at 50 °C for 2 h in a temperature-controlled water bath (JSIB-22T, JS Research Inc., Korea). The reaction was terminated by heating the mixture at 85 °C for 20 min to inactivate the enzyme. After cooling at room temperature for 60 min, the hydrolysate was filtered through a test sieve (35 mesh; wire diameter 315 μm) to remove insoluble fractions. The chicken breast hydrolysates (CBH) were collected and stored at 4 °C until subsequent conjugation treatment.

For preparation of protein-polyphenol conjugates, CB or CBH samples were dispersed at a concentration of 20 mg/mL in 10 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), homogenized, and equilibrated at 4 °C for 24 h to ensure complete hydration. The pH of the solution was then adjusted to 9.0 using 2 M NaOH. Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MI, USA) was added at a final concentration of 0.1 mg/mL, which was selected based on preliminary optimization experiments and previous studies (Han et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2021). The mixtures were stirred at room temperature for 24 h under ambient atmospheric conditions to promote protein–polyphenol interaction. Following conjugation treatment, the samples were frozen at −70 °C and freeze-dried under vacuum conditions (80 × 10-3 Torr; PVTFD10R, Ilshin Lab, Republic of Korea). The resulting freeze-dried powders were vacuum-packaged in PA/PE film and stored at −20 °C until further analysis.

Structural and physicochemical characterization

Determination of sulfhydryl groups

The sulfhydryl (–SH) content of the protein–polyphenol conjugates was determined in duplicate using a colorimetric assay based on Ellman’s reagent, with minor modifications from Fu et al. (2020). Ellman’s reagent was freshly prepared by dissolving 0.2 g of 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) in 50 mL of Tris–glycine buffer (0.086 M Tris, 0.09 M glycine, and 4 mM EDTA, pH 8.0). A 1 mL aliquot of protein sample (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 4 mL of Tris–glycine buffer, followed by the addition of 125 μL of Ellman’s reagent. The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Absorbance was measured at 412 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Biochrom Libra S22, Cambridge, UK). The sulfhydryl content was calculated using the following equation: Sulfhydryl content (μmol/g protein) = (73.53 × A412) / protein concentration (mg/mL).

Turbidity measurement

Turbidity of the samples was determined according to the method of Han et al. (2023) with minor modifications. Freeze-dried samples were dispersed in DDW at a concentration of 5 mg/mL and homogenized prior to analysis. The absorbance of the dispersions was measured at 600 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Biochrom Libra S22, Cambridge, UK). DDW was used as the blank.

Protein electrophoresis

Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) was performed according to Laemmli (1970) with minor modifications. Samples were adjusted to a protein concentration of 100 mg/mL, homogenized, filtered through Whatman No. 4 filter paper, mixed with reducing sample buffer at a ratio of 4:1 (v/v), and heated at 100 °C for 5 min. A 20 μL aliquot was loaded onto a gel consisting of a 5% stacking gel and a 10% separating gel. Electrophoresis was conducted at 80 V through the stacking gel and 120 V through the separating gel. The gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 and destained prior to visualization. A pre-stained protein marker (Zen-Pre Stained Protein Marker, ZV-26619, Zenvia, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) was used to estimate molecular weights.

Tricine SDS–PAGE was performed according to Schägger (2006) with slight modifications. Samples were mixed with Tricine sample buffer at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio, heated at 100 °C for 5 min, and briefly centrifuged before loading onto a gel consisting of a 5% stacking gel and a 10% separating gel. Electrophoresis was conducted at 30 V until the samples entered the separating gel and subsequently at 150 V. The gels were fixed, stained with Serva Blue G, and destained with 10% (v/v) acetic acid. Molecular weights were estimated using a low-range protein marker (PageRuler Low Range Unstained Protein Ladder, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania).

Zeta potential

The zeta potential of the samples was determined according to the method of Wang et al. (2019) using a nanoparticle analyzer (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK) at 25 °C. Freeze-dried samples were dispersed in DDW at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and diluted to an appropriate concentration prior to measurement. The dispersions were equilibrated at room temperature before analysis.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectra of freeze-dried samples were recorded according to the method of Gautam et al. (2024) using a Spectrum Two FT-IR spectrometer (L160000A, PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT, USA). Spectra were collected over the wavenumber range of 4,000–500 cm⁻¹ at room temperature. Each spectrum was obtained from 32 scans with a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹.

Polyphenol concentration

Bound and free polyphenol contents of the four treatments were determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric method (Imeh and Khokhar, 2002). Each sample was dispersed in distilled water at a concentration of 2 mg/mL. For free polyphenol content, the sample dispersion was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. For bound polyphenol content, the whole sample dispersion was directly used. Briefly, 100 μL of each prepared sample solution was mixed with 500 μL of 10% (v/v) Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and allowed to react for 5 min at room temperature. Then, 400 μL of 7.5% (w/v) sodium carbonate solution was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. Gallic acid was used as the external standard, and the results were calculated from the calibration curve and expressed as μg gallic acid equivalents per mg of sample (μg GAE/mg sample).

Technological properties

Water-holding capacity (WHC) and oil absorption capacity (OAC)

The water-holding capacity (WHC) and oil absorption capacity (OAC) of the samples were determined in duplicate according to the method of Acosta-Domínguez et al. (2021). One gram of sample powder was placed in a 15 mL centrifuge tube and mixed with 10 mL of either DDW or soybean oil (Beksul Co., Seoul, Republic of Korea). The mixtures were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the weight of the residue was recorded. WHC and OAC were expressed as grams of absorbed water or oil per gram of sample (g/g).

Emulsifying properties

The emulsifying properties of the samples were determined according to Han et al. (2023). Briefly, 30 mL of sample solution (5 mg/mL in DDW) was mixed with 10 mL of soybean oil and homogenized at 12,000 rpm for 3 min using a homogenizer (HG-15A, Daihan Scientific, Wonju, Republic of Korea). An aliquot of the emulsion (50 μL) was diluted with 10 mL of 0.1% (w/v) SDS solution, and absorbance was measured at 500 nm. The emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsion stability index (ESI) were calculated using the following equations:EAI(m2/g)=(2×2.303×A0×dilutionfactor)/(C×φ×104)ESI(min)=(A0/(A0−A30))×30where C is the protein concentration (g/mL), φ is the oil volume fraction, and A₀ and A₃₀ are the absorbance values measured at 0 and 30 min, respectively.

Antioxidant capacities

2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity

The DPPH radical scavenging activity of the protein-polyphenol conjugates was measured in triplicate using the modified method by Chen et al. (2012). One hundred microliters of the 0.1 mM DPPH solution in 99.9% (v/v) ethanol were mixed with an equal volume of sample solution (1 mg/mL in DDW) or DDW (as control). After the reaction at 25 °C for 30 min, the absorbance of the reactant (Asample) was measured at 515 nm using a microplate reader (Infinite M200 PRO, Tecan, Grödig, Austria). l-Ascorbic acid (0.5 mg/mL in DDW, PHR1008, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MI, USA) was used as a positive control. The DPPH radical scavenging activity was calculated using the following equation:DPPHradicalscavengingactivity(%)=[1−(Asample−Ablank)/Acontrol]×100

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

The ferric-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) of the conjugates was assessed in triplicate using the method of Othman et al. (2007). The FRAP reagent was prepared by mixing 300 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 10 mM TPTZ, and 20 mM FeCl3·6H2O at 37 °C in a 10:1:1 ratio. One hundred microliters of the sample solution (5 mg/mL) were mixed with 3 mL of the FRAP reagent and 300 μL of DDW, and the mixture was kept at 37 °C for 4 min. The absorbance of the reactant was read at 593 nm. l-Ascorbic acid (5 mg/mL) was used as a positive control.

In vitro digestibility assessment

In vitro digestibility was assessed in triplicate following the modified method of Bornhorst and Paul Singh (2014). For oral digestion, 1 g of the sample powder was mixed with 1 mL of amylase (TB0920, Pickering Laboratories, Mountain View, CA, USA) containing 1.5 mM CaCl2. The pH of the sample was adjusted to 7 using 0.1 N NaOH, and the mixture was shaken in a 37 °C water bath for 2 min. For gastric digestion, the oral digest was mixed with simulated gastric fluid (0.03 M NaCl, pH 1.2, 3.2 mg/mL pepsin, 0.15 mM CaCl2) at a 1:1 volume ratio, adjusted to pH 3.0, and incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 2 h. For intestinal digestion, the gastric digest was mixed with simulated intestinal fluid (0.05 M KH2PO4, pH 7.5, 10 mg/mL pancreatin, 0.6 mM CaCl2) at a 1:1 ratio, adjusted to pH 7.0, and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The final reactant was centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min, and the residue was collected and dried at 65 °C for 12 h to obtain undigested fractions. The in vitro digestibility was calculated based on the weight of the undigested fraction using the following equation.Invitrodigestibility(%)=(100−(undigestedfractionweight(g)×100)/sampleweight(g))

Statistical analysis

A completely randomized design with a 2 × 2 factorial arrangement was applied in this study. The two main processing factors were enzymatic hydrolysis and EGCG conjugation, each at two levels (without and with treatment). Three independent experimental batches were prepared on separate days, and all four treatments were independently produced within each batch. Each batch was considered an experimental replicate for statistical analysis (n = 3), whereas duplicate or triplicate analytical measurements within each batch were treated as technical measurements. The results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics software (Version 18.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate the main and interaction effects of enzymatic hydrolysis and EGCG conjugation. Significant differences among means were determined using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test at P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Sulfhydryl content and turbidity

The sulfhydryl content of chicken breast proteins differed significantly among treatments (Fig. 1A) (P < 0.05). A significant interaction between enzymatic hydrolysis and EGCG treatment was observed for sulfhydryl content (P < 0.001), with a greater EGCG-induced reduction in the hydrolyzed protein. Enzymatic hydrolysis increased sulfhydryl levels, with CBH exhibiting the highest value, which may be associated with structural disruption and the exposure of previously buried thiol-containing regions. In contrast, EGCG treatment significantly decreased sulfhydryl content in both native and hydrolyzed chicken protein samples. Similar reductions in sulfhydryl content have been reported in protein–polyphenol systems and are generally associated with interactions between oxidized polyphenols and thiol groups, as well as thiol oxidation during processing (Iimure et al., 2013; Skočibušić et al., 2006). Turbidity was evaluated to assess changes in aggregation behavior and intermolecular interactions induced by enzymatic hydrolysis and subsequent EGCG treatment. Turbidity differed significantly among treatments (Fig. 1B) (P < 0.05), increasing in the order of CB < CB-EGCG < CBH < CBH-EGCG. A significant interaction was observed for turbidity (P < 0.001), with a greater EGCG-induced increase in the intact protein. Enzymatic hydrolysis markedly increased turbidity, which may be related to structural modification and enhanced intermolecular association resulting from the exposure of hydrophobic and reactive regions. Similar increases in turbidity following hydrolysis have been reported by Liu et al. (2014), who indicated that partial unfolding promotes aggregation and intermolecular interactions. EGCG treatment further elevated turbidity in both native and hydrolyzed samples, consistent with enhanced protein–polyphenol association and aggregate formation. Previous studies have demonstrated that polyphenols such as EGCG can induce protein aggregation by cross-linking protein molecules and enhancing hydrophobic interactions, leading to increased light scattering (Tan et al., 2022). Notably, the highest turbidity observed in CBH-EGCG suggests that hydrolysis-induced structural flexibility and increased availability of reactive sites facilitate more extensive intermolecular association during conjugation. This combined effect between hydrolysis and polyphenol binding has been similarly reported in other protein systems, where controlled hydrolysis enhances the extent of polyphenol-induced aggregation (Zhao et al., 2022).

Protein electrophoresis

SDS–PAGE (Fig. 1C) and Tricine SDS–PAGE (Fig. 1D) showed that enzymatic hydrolysis markedly reduced the intensity of the major protein bands and increased the proportion of lower-molecular-weight fragments and peptides. In the intact chicken breast protein system, CB-EGCG showed reduced intensity of several major protein bands, particularly around 15 kDa. This change indicates that EGCG treatment affected the electrophoretic band pattern, although it does not directly confirm protein–polyphenol conjugation. In contrast, the electrophoretic profiles of CBH and CBH-EGCG were largely comparable, with no distinct EGCG-induced changes observed. Therefore, the electrophoretic results provide limited evidence regarding structural interactions in the hydrolyzed samples. Peptide–polyphenol associations may be difficult to detect using conventional electrophoretic techniques because of the low molecular weight and reduced staining sensitivity of peptide fractions (Ma et al., 2024; Ozdal et al., 2013).

Bound and free polyphenol content

Bound and free polyphenol contents were determined to evaluate the extent of EGCG incorporation and to distinguish between free and protein-bound phenolic fractions. Bound and free polyphenol contents were significantly higher in CBH-EGCG than in CB-EGCG (Fig. 2) (P < 0.05). Notably, bound polyphenol content exceeded free polyphenol content in both systems, indicating the presence of a bound phenolic fraction. The substantial increase in bound polyphenol content in the hydrolyzed system suggests that enzymatic hydrolysis enhanced the accessibility of reactive amino acid residues, thereby promoting more efficient incorporation of EGCG. Moreover, the higher bound polyphenol fraction, together with the previously observed sulfhydryl depletion and turbidity increase, supports the occurrence of interactions between EGCG and protein molecules. However, the analyses performed in the present study do not distinguish covalent binding from strong non-covalent association. Therefore, direct structural analyses are required to confirm the precise interaction mechanism. Under alkaline conditions (pH 9.0) and in the presence of oxygen, EGCG can undergo autoxidation to form quinone intermediates, which subsequently react with nucleophilic side chains such as thiol and ε-amino groups, leading to covalent conjugation (Ozdal et al., 2013). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectra of chicken breast proteins exhibited characteristic absorption bands of protein systems, including a broad band around 3,300 cm-1 and amide I and amide II bands near 1,650 and 1,540 cm-1, respectively (Fig. 3). Compared with CB, the hydrolyzed and EGCG-treated samples showed changes in band intensity and spectral profiles in these regions. The alterations observed in the amide I and II regions may reflect changes in protein backbone conformation associated with peptide bond cleavage and EGCG treatment. Changes in the broad region around 3,300 cm-1 may be related to modifications in hydrogen-bonding interactions involving O–H and N–H groups (Kroll et al., 2003). In addition, spectral differences were observed in the 1,200–1,000 cm-1 region, which may be associated with C–O stretching vibrations of phenolic groups and thus suggest the presence of EGCG in the treated protein systems (Ozdal et al., 2013). Among the treatments, CBH-EGCG showed the greatest spectral deviation from CB. This observation suggests that prior hydrolysis may have increased the susceptibility of chicken breast proteins to structural modification during subsequent EGCG treatment. However, the FTIR spectra alone do not establish the precise molecular interaction or specific conformational changes involved.Fig. 3FTIR spectra of CB, CB-EGCG, CBH, and CBH-EGCG over the range of 4,000–500 cm-1. Technological Properties

Water-holding capacity and Oil absorption capacity

Water-holding capacity (WHC) and oil absorption capacity (OAC) differed significantly among treatments (Fig. 4) (P < 0.05). WHC increased in the order of CB < CB-EGCG < CBH < CBH-EGCG, indicating that both enzymatic hydrolysis and EGCG conjugation enhanced water retention. No significant interaction was observed for WHC (P = 0.102), with a greater EGCG-induced increase after hydrolysis. The increase in WHC following hydrolysis may be associated with structural unfolding and the exposure of hydrophilic groups (Nuñez et al., 2021). In addition, EGCG conjugation further improved WHC, likely due to enhanced protein–polyphenol interactions and hydrogen bonding involving phenolic hydroxyl groups (Cao et al., 2023). OAC (Fig. 4B) exhibited a similar trend, with CBH-EGCG showing the highest value. A significant interaction was observed for OAC (P = 0.012), indicating that hydrolysis enhanced the effect of EGCG treatment. EGCG conjugation improved oil absorption, which may be associated with increased hydrophobic interactions resulting from structural modification. Zeta potential

All chicken breast protein samples exhibited negative zeta potentials, indicating a net negative surface charge under the measurement conditions (Fig. 4C). CB treatment showed the most negative value (−21.63 mV), whereas EGCG conjugation shifted the zeta potential toward less negative values (−18.87 mV). Enzymatic hydrolysis resulted in a more remarkable increase toward charge neutralization, with CBH exhibiting the least negative value (−14.5 mV). In the hydrolyzed treatments, no significant difference was observed between CBH and CBH–EGCG, indicating a limited additional effect of EGCG on surface charge after hydrolysis (P < 0.05). A significant interaction was observed for zeta potential (P = 0.007), as EGCG treatment produced opposite changes in intact and hydrolyzed proteins. The shift toward less negative zeta potential suggests that both hydrolysis and EGCG conjugation altered interfacial charge characteristics (Dickinson, 2003). The greater effect of hydrolysis is likely associated with structural disassembly and changes in the distribution of ionizable groups following peptide bond cleavage (Kristinsson and Rasco, 2000). The reduced magnitude of zeta potential indicates weakened electrostatic repulsion, implying that colloidal stability in hydrolyzed systems may be governed more by non-electrostatic interactions (McClements, 2004). In contrast, the modest change induced by EGCG conjugation in the native protein suggests partial masking or rearrangement of surface charges through protein–polyphenol interactions (Hasni et al., 2011; Jakobek, 2015), while its effect becomes negligible in peptide-rich systems. Overall, enzymatic hydrolysis appears to be the dominant factor influencing zeta potential, whereas the impact of EGCG is more evident in the intact protein system.

Emulsifying properties

Emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsion stability index (ESI) differed significantly among treatments (Fig. 4D) (P < 0.05). EAI increased following enzymatic hydrolysis and EGCG conjugation, with CBH-EGCG exhibiting the highest value (P < 0.05). No significant interaction was observed for EAI (P = 0.198), indicating largely independent effects of hydrolysis and EGCG treatment. The improvement in EAI after hydrolysis can be attributed to enhanced diffusion and adsorption of smaller peptides at the oil–water interface, while EGCG conjugation likely contributed to improved interfacial interactions (Fan et al., 2018; Padial-Domínguez et al., 2020). In contrast, ESI decreased in CBH compared to CB, indicating reduced interfacial stability due to peptide fragmentation and the formation of weaker interfacial films (Rajendhran et al., 2024). However, EGCG conjugation significantly improved emulsion stability, particularly in CB-EGCG. No significant interaction was observed for ESI (P = 0.330). Although EGCG conjugation did not increase the magnitude of zeta potential, the enhanced stability suggests that emulsion stabilization was governed primarily by non-electrostatic mechanisms. Specifically, protein–polyphenol interactions may enhance interfacial film formation and steric stabilization, thereby compensating for the reduced electrostatic repulsion (Feng et al., 2019). These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that protein–polyphenol interactions improve interfacial properties and emulsifying performance (Capar et al., 2024).

Antioxidant capacities

2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity

DPPH radical scavenging activity differed significantly among treatments (Fig. 5A) (P < 0.05). Enzymatic hydrolysis increased antioxidant activity compared to the native protein, which can be attributed to the generation of low-molecular-weight peptides with enhanced electron-donating capacity (Setthaya et al., 2021). EGCG conjugation further enhanced radical scavenging activity in both native and hydrolyzed systems (Wu et al., 2023), with CBH-EGCG exhibiting the highest value (P < 0.05). A significant interaction was observed for DPPH radical scavenging activity (P < 0.001), with a greater EGCG-induced increase in the intact protein. The increased activity in CBH-EGCG reflects the combined contribution of peptide-derived antioxidant properties and the radical-scavenging capacity of EGCG. Ferric-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

FRAP values differed significantly among treatments (Fig. 5B) (P < 0.05). Enzymatic hydrolysis increased reducing power compared to the native protein, which can be attributed to the exposure of electron-donating amino acid residues following peptide bond cleavage (Tejpal et al., 2017). EGCG conjugation further enhanced ferric-reducing capacity, reflecting the strong electron-donating ability of phenolic hydroxyl groups (Sadowska-Krępa et al., 2019), with CBH-EGCG exhibiting the highest value. A significant interaction was observed for FRAP (P = 0.020), indicating that the effect of EGCG treatment depended on hydrolysis status. The elevated FRAP value in CBH-EGCG indicates the combined contribution of peptide-derived reducing activity and EGCG-mediated electron transfer. Similar improvements in reducing power have been reported in protein–polyphenol systems, where conjugation enhances overall redox capacity (Chotphruethipong et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024). From a poultry-processing perspective, the treated chicken breast protein could be utilized as a value-added ingredient in emulsified poultry products, restructured meat products, and powdered high-protein formulations. Such applications may broaden the use of chicken breast protein beyond conventional whole-muscle products and provide additional opportunities for the development of poultry-derived protein products. However, the antioxidant activity observed in the present ingredient-level assays should be further validated in actual poultry food matrices, particularly with respect to processing compatibility, color, sensory quality, and storage stability.

In Vitro Digestibility Assessment

In vitro digestibility showed clear differences among treatments (Fig. 6) (P < 0.05). Enzymatic hydrolysis markedly increased digestibility compared to the native protein, reflecting enhanced protease accessibility following peptide bond cleavage (Beaubier et al., 2023). In contrast, EGCG treatment reduced digestibility in the native protein system, which may be attributed to interactions between polyphenols and proteins that limit enzyme accessibility or mask proteolytic cleavage sites, consistent with previous reports (Shi et al., 2024). From a nutritional perspective, the lower measured digestibility of CB-EGCG may represent a potential limitation because reduced proteolysis could decrease the release of absorbable peptides and amino acids. Notably, CBH-EGCG exhibited digestibility comparable to CBH, indicating that prior hydrolysis mitigated the inhibitory effects associated with EGCG treatment (Chen et al., 2025). A significant interaction was observed for in vitro digestibility (P = 0.001), as EGCG treatment reduced digestibility in the intact protein but had little effect after hydrolysis. These results suggest that controlled enzymatic hydrolysis may help maintain the measured in vitro digestibility following EGCG treatment. Conclusion

This study demonstrated that enzymatic hydrolysis and EGCG treatment differentially influenced the quality attributes of chicken breast proteins. Enzymatic hydrolysis improved interfacial functionality and digestibility, whereas EGCG treatment enhanced antioxidant capacity and technological properties associated with protein–polyphenol interaction. In addition, hydrolyzed chicken breast proteins exhibited greater physicochemical changes following EGCG treatment than intact proteins. Although EGCG treatment reduced digestibility in intact chicken breast proteins, prior hydrolysis mitigated this effect, allowing CBH-EGCG to maintain digestibility comparable to the hydrolyzed treatment. Overall, controlled enzymatic hydrolysis followed by EGCG treatment may provide a potential processing strategy for developing value-added chicken protein ingredients with improved functional and antioxidant properties while maintaining desirable digestibility, thereby diversifying poultry-derived protein products and potentially increasing consumer interest in their applications. However, the present analyses do not fully resolve the molecular-level characteristics of EGCG-associated proteins and peptides, and further studies using mass spectrometric and complementary structural analyses are needed to identify specific interaction sites and conformational modifications.
