Optimizing Travel Time and Regenerative Energy for Periodic Timetables
Sarah Roth, Sven Jäger, Niels Lindner, Anita Schöbel

Abstract
Regenerating braking energy is one major pathway to make rail traffic energy-efficient. It is therefore desirable to design timetables that exploit this feature. However, timetables that allow to regenerate energy are often bad for the passengers. We hence formulate and analyze a bicriteria optimization problem (PESP-Passenger-Energy) to find periodic railway timetables that maximize the regenerated energy in terms of the brake-traction overlap time and minimize the travel time of the passengers. Our model extends the Periodic Event Scheduling Problem (PESP) and offers a rich combinatorial theory. We investigate its computational complexity on one-station networks, building on matchings and Hamiltonian paths. Besides showing its NP-hardness even for a single objective, we identify several polynomial-time solvable special cases. Finally, we provide two case studies, underlining the practicability of our model, and analyzing the Pareto front.



Keywords:

Energy-efficient timetabling, Periodic timetabling, Regenerative braking, Sustainable planning, Optimization

Introduction

In order to reach the climate goals, it is necessary to strengthen the role of public transportation in passenger transport. However, also public transport systems themselves consume a large amount of energy. In rail traffic, traction energy for electric vehicles accounts for up to 80% of the total consumption
[
10
]
.
This motivates to design energy-efficient train trajectories. In this paper, we exploit that modern electric motors are able to regenerate energy while braking. For example, Deutsche Bahn reports that 18.7% of the traction energy could be recovered in the year 2024
[
8
]
. For trains, especially in low voltage direct current (DC) systems, the most efficient way to use the regained energy is a transmission to a close-by accelerating train via the catenary
[
32
]
. Such systems are for example used in many metro and suburban rail systems, but also for long-distance railways in several countries such as the Netherlands, Russia, Italy, or Spain.

From the perspective of planning, it is therefore sensible to schedule train timetables in a way that synchronizes braking and acceleration processes of nearby trains. Such a schedule has two advantages concerning the energy usage: First, it enables a maximum usage of the regenerated energy and, hence, reduces the total amount of energy that needs to be purchased by the public transport operator. Second, it prevents power peaks that might surcharge the transportation system’s power supply.

However, from a passenger perspective, this synchronization of braking and acceleration is undesirable, as it prevents a passenger transfer from the braking to the accelerating train. Narrowly missing a train leads to frustration of the passengers, and systematically long waiting times might cause them to prefer the car over public transport.

As an illustration of this trade-off, we consider an example motivated by the Swiss railways. The trains are scheduled by means of an integrated fixed-interval timetable
[
19
]
, which is designed to prevent narrowly missed connections. At each station, the timetable prescribes a fixed time such that shortly before this time, all trains stopping at the station arrive, and the trains depart shortly after that time, see
Fig.

1(a)
. This enables short transfer times from and to all directions. On the other hand, a transfer of regenerative braking energy from one train to another is impossible. For this objective, an efficient timetable would schedule the trains one after another such that the braking and acceleration phases overlap pairwise, see

While it is beneficial to the environment to use as much of the regenerative braking energy as possible, it is also of utmost importance to provide attractive public transportation to the passengers. Our contribution.

We summarize our key contributions as follows:

1.

We propose a mathematical framework and a mixed-integer programming formulation for the bicriteria discrete optimization problem of finding a feasible periodic timetable maximizing the brake-traction overlap and minimizing the passengers’ travel time on an arbitrary event-activity network (PESP-Passenger-Energy), extending the Periodic Event Scheduling Problem (PESP).

2.

For timetables minimizing travel time (PESP-Passenger) or maximizing the brake-traction overlap (PESP-Energy) individually, we investigate the complexity status of different problem variants on networks modeling a single station. We show that the (classic) PESP-Passenger is already NP-hard in the case of one station. Moreover, we characterize the structure of optimal solutions and link them to matchings and Hamiltonian paths, resulting in a polynomial-time algorithm with additive performance guarantee. We further identify several classes of polynomially solvable cases.

3.

On two case studies, one being a major hub of the Berlin S-Bahn network, we demonstrate that our bicriteria model is able to handle realistic scenarios, and we compute and analyze the Pareto front.

This paper is based on a previous conference proceedings paper
[
18
]
for which it significantly extends the complexity analysis and algorithms. The Berlin S-Bahn case study is new.

2
Literature

The task of designing efficient railway timetables by means of the Periodic Event Scheduling Problem (PESP) has been subject to study at least since the seminal work by Serafini and Ukovich in 1989
[
34
]
. An overview can be found in Liebchen’s dissertation
[
22
]
, and more recent approaches are illustrated in, e.g.,
[
3
,
14
,
4
]
. Traditionally, the literature on timetabling has focused on minimizing the passengers’ travel time. More recently, however, the growing importance of energy efficiency has led to significant research attention on reducing energy consumption, particularly within the engineering sciences. A comprehensive review of these works goes far beyond the scope of this paper. Instead, we only mention the most relevant papers in our context and refer to the survey by Scheepmaker, Goverde, and Kroon
[
32
]
and the exemplary recent papers
[
26
,
20
,
17
]
, which contain more extensive literature reviews.

There are two ways in which the timetable can affect the trains’ energy consumption. On the one hand, there is the idea of saving energy by the implementation of energy-efficient driving strategies, see, e.g., Howlett, Milroy, and Pudney
[
16
]
. These depend on the time scheduled for each driving section; typically longer travel times require less energy. Ghoseiri, Szidarovszky, and Asgharpour
[
11
]
considered a biobjective train scheduling model, combining the objectives of minimizing energy and minimizing travel time, and approximate the Pareto frontier using the
\varepsilon
-constraint method.

On the other hand, the timetable can influence the usage of regenerative energy in train systems. This was first researched by Ramos, Pena, Fernández, and Cucala
[
31
]
, who allow a modification of the dwell times to increase the brake-traction overlap. A more detailed modeling of the energy consumption that combines the driving strategies and the brake-traction overlaps has been studied by Yin, Yang, Tang, Gao, and Ran
[
41
]
, who devised a Lagrangian relaxation-based heuristic for this problem. In contrast, Gupta, Tobin, and Pavel
[
6
]
considered a very simplified linear programming model to synchronize the start times of braking phases and the end times of acceleration phases. Bärmann, Gemander, Martin, and Merkert
[
1
]
report on implementing an energy-efficient timetable by optimizing both speed profiles and brake-traction overlaps for the Nuremberg subway.
Finally, Sun, Yao, Dong, and Clarke
[
35
]
proposed a method to adjust speed profiles during operation in order to achieve brake-traction overlaps even in the case of delays.

The bicriteria problem of minimizing the passenger travel times and maximizing the brake-traction overlap has been investigated by Yang, Ning, Li, and Tang
[
40
]
, who developed a genetic algorithm. Moreover, Yang, Liao, Wu, Timmermans, Sun, and Gao
[
39
]
applied the NSGA-II algorithm for approximating the Pareto frontier. Gong, Luan, Yang, Qi, and Corman
[
13
]
presented a non-linear integer programming formulation and an adaptive large-neighborhood search for the bicriteria problem integrating rolling stock circulations.

All these works are based on a given aperiodic timetable that can be modified. Only recently, the study of the periodic version of this problem has been initiated by Wang, Zhu, and Corman
[
37
]
. They assume a given nominal periodic timetable and develop a model that can be used for local adjustments. On the one hand, their aim is to maximize the brake-traction overlap to enable the usage of regenerative energy on a fixed set of synchronized arrival and departure events. On the other hand, they include passenger-related objectives such as the minimization of the generalized average travel time of all passengers and the minimization of the maximum individual increase in generalized travel time. Wang et al. also provide a visualization of the Pareto frontier for these objectives on an instance of Dutch railways.

Problem Description

We consider an extension of the Periodic Event Scheduling Problem (PESP), whose basic notions are recalled in
Section

3.1
, including our construction of event-activity networks. We introduce our model of brake-traction overlaps in
Section

3.2
. This allows us to define the baseline problem of this paper,
PESP-Passenger-Energy
, as a bicriteria optimization problem combining periodic timetabling and matching of braking and accelerating trains in
Section

3.3

Section

3.4
.

3.1
Preliminaries

In the timetabling problem, we are given a public transport network and a set of lines
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, which are defined as sequences of served stations
v\in\mathcal{V}
. Every line is operated periodically with a given period
T
. Hence, we search for a periodic timetable that assigns an integer number (time) between
0
and
T-1
to each departure and each arrival of a line at a station. This timetable needs to respect the duration of activities such as travel times between stations, dwell times at stations, and transfer times for passengers. In contrast to the usual setting, e.g., in
[
22
]
, we also consider
energy
transfers between a braking and an accelerating train.

The standard model used for such periodic timetabling problems is the periodic event scheduling problem (PESP) developed in
[
34
]
, which is based on an
event-activity-network
(EAN)
\mathcal{E}=(E,A)
, whose nodes are periodically repeated
events
and whose arcs are
activities
that take place between events. For timetabling, the network is derived from a set of directed lines
\mathcal{L}
serving stations
v\in\mathcal{V}
, see for example
[
27
,
30
]
. The node set is given by arrival and departure events
E=E_{\mathrm{arr}}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}E_{\mathrm{dep}}
, constructed from the lines and stations as follows:

E_{\mathrm{arr}}\coloneqq\bigl\{(v,\ell,\mathrm{arr})\bigm|\ell\in\mathcal{L}\text{ arrives at }v\in\mathcal{V}\bigr\},\ E_{\mathrm{dep}}\coloneqq\bigl\{(v,\ell,\mathrm{dep})\bigm|\ell\in\mathcal{L}\text{ departs at }v\in\mathcal{V}\bigr\}.

The activities
A\coloneqq A_{\mathrm{drive}}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{trans}}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{energy}}
connect the events as follows:

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{drive}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigl\{((v_{1},\ell,\mathrm{dep}),(v_{2},\ell,\mathrm{arr}))\in E_{\mathrm{dep}}\times E_{\mathrm{arr}}\bigm|\ell\text{ serves }v_{2}\text{ directly after }v_{1}\bigr\},

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigl\{((v,\ell,\mathrm{arr}),(v,\ell,\mathrm{dep}))\in E_{\mathrm{arr}}\times E_{\mathrm{dep}}\bigr\},

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{trans}}

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigl\{((v,\ell_{1},\mathrm{arr}),(v,\ell_{2},\mathrm{dep}))\in E_{\mathrm{arr}}\times E_{\mathrm{dep}}\bigm|\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}\bigr\},

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{energy}}

3.2
Modeling the Brake-Traction Overlap

Let us consider one energy activity
ji\in A_{\mathrm{energy}}
. We denote the minimum of the acceleration and braking times associated with energy arc
ji
by
t_{ji}^{\min}\coloneqq\min\{t_{j}^{\mathrm{ac}},t_{i}^{\mathrm{br}}\}
and the maximum by
t_{ji}^{\max}\coloneqq\max\{t_{j}^{\mathrm{ac}},t_{i}^{\mathrm{br}}\}
.
As for the definition of a timetable above, we represent all periodically repeating times by their occurrence in the interval
[0,T)
. With this convention, and in contrast to
[
34
]
, we define a periodic interval as

[a,b]_{T}\coloneqq\begin{cases}[a\bmod T,b\bmod T]&\text{if }a\bmod T\leq b\bmod T,\\
[0,b\bmod T]\cup[a\bmod T,T)&\text{else.}\end{cases}

The length of a periodic interval is then
\operatorname{length}([a,b]_{T})=(b-a)\bmod T
.
The periodic interval of the acceleration phase after the departure event
j
is then
[\pi_{j},\pi_{j}+t_{j}^{\mathrm{ac}}]_{T}
. Analogously,
[\pi_{i}-t_{i}^{\mathrm{br}},\pi_{i}]_{T}
describes the braking phase before the arrival event
i
. Due to the assumption that
t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{j}+t^{\mathrm{br}}_{i}<T
, the intersection of both intervals is again a periodic interval, whose length equals the overlap of the braking and acceleration phases:

Definition 3.1

(Brake-Traction Overlap)
.

For
ji\in A_{\mathrm{energy}}

we define the
(brake-traction) overlap
resulting from a periodic timetable
\pi
as

o_{ji}\coloneqq\operatorname{length}\bigl([\pi_{j},\pi_{j}+t_{j}^{\mathrm{ac}}]_{T}\cap[\pi_{i}-t_{i}^{\mathrm{br}},\pi_{i}]_{T}\bigr)
.

Clearly, the overlap does not depend on the exact times
\pi_{j}
and
\pi_{i}
but only on their difference, i.e., on the periodic tension
x_{ji}=(\pi_{i}-\pi_{j})\bmod{T}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\pi
be a periodic timetable. The overlap on an energy activity
a\in A_{\mathrm{energy}}
with periodic tension
x_{a}

o_{a}=\max\bigl\{\min\{x_{a},\,t^{\min}_{a},\,t^{\max}_{a}+t^{\min}_{a}-x_{a}\},\ 0\bigr\}

Having presented the input data in
Section

3.1

[

38

4
Complexity Overview

Even without an objective function, the Periodic Event Scheduling Problem (PESP), which consists of deciding whether a feasible timetable exists, is NP-complete for any fixed
T\geq 3

[
29
]
. When all activities are free, so that feasibility is guaranteed, finding a periodic timetable with minimum total travel time is NP-hard
[
28
,
24
]
.
Therefore, also the problem of deciding whether there is a feasible timetable with a total travel time of the passengers smaller than some constant
K_{1}
and a total overlap of brake and traction phases that is bigger than some constant
K_{2}
is NP-complete.

To obtain a better understanding, we investigate the complexity of
PESP-Passenger-Energy
on a class of simple but meaningful instances, and for each individual objective.

4.1
One-Station Networks

From the application context, it makes sense to consider energy transmission locally at a single station.
Our upcoming complexity investigations are based on a small network of a single transfer station called a
one-station network
. Among others, we will show that the (classic) PESP-Passenger is already NP-hard on one-station networks.

5
PESP-Passenger on a One-Station Network

In this section, we will consider PESP-Passenger minimizing the passengers’ travel time, i.e., the “classic” PESP. We strengthen the results of
[
28
]
and
[
29
]
by showing that the problem of finding a timetable minimizing the total transfer time is already hard on one-station networks where all waiting activities are present but do not restrict feasibility.

Theorem 5.1
.

The problem PESP-Passenger is NP-hard on a one-station network, even if all possible transfer activities are present, the period time is fixed to
T=2
, and
u_{a}=l_{a}+1
for all
a\in A_{\mathrm{wait}}
.

Proof.

We show NP-hardness by a reduction from the Max-Cut problem. Let
I
be an arbitrary Max-Cut instance, consisting of a graph
G=(V,R)
and a weight function
\upsilon\colon R\to\mathbb{R}
. We search a bipartition
(S,T)
of the vertex set
V
such that the sum of the weights on the edges between the sets
S
and
T
,
\sum_{\{i,j\}\in R:i\in S,j\in T}\upsilon(\{i,j\})
, is maximum.

Based on
I
we define an instance
I^{\prime}
of the PESP-Passenger problem on a one-station network: Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{pass}}=(E_{\mathrm{arr}}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}E_{\mathrm{dep}},\,A_{\mathrm{wait}}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{trans}})
be a one-station network with
n\coloneqq|V|
lines, inducing
n
arrival events and
n
departure events, and
A_{\mathrm{wait}}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{trans}}=E_{\mathrm{arr}}\times E_{\mathrm{dep}}
. This means that
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{pass}}
is the complete bipartite graph
K_{n,n}
with all edges directed from
E_{\mathrm{arr}}
to
E_{\mathrm{dep}}
. We consider the period time
T\coloneqq 2
. The waiting activities
a\in A_{\mathrm{wait}}
have
l_{a}\coloneqq 0
and
u_{a}\coloneqq 1
, and their weights are
w_{a}\coloneqq\sum_{r\in R}\upsilon(r)+1
. For the transfer activities
((i,\mathrm{arr}),(j,\mathrm{dep}))\in A_{\mathrm{trans}}
, define the bounds
l_{(i,\mathrm{arr}),(j,\mathrm{dep})}\coloneqq 1
and
u_{(i,\mathrm{arr}),(j,\mathrm{dep})}\coloneqq 2
and weights

w_{(i,\mathrm{arr}),(j,\mathrm{dep})}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\upsilon(\{i,j\})&\text{if }\{i,j\}\in R,\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

We want to show that any optimal solution to
I^{\prime}
can be transformed to an optimal solution of
I
.
Let
\pi
be an optimal timetable for
I^{\prime}
. Then for every
i\in V
we have
\pi_{(i,\mathrm{dep})}=\pi_{(i,\mathrm{arr})}
: Suppose, to the contrary, that there exists some
i\in V
with
\pi_{(i,\mathrm{dep})}=\pi_{(i,\mathrm{arr})}+1
. Then the waiting arc
((i,\mathrm{arr}),(i,\mathrm{dep}))
contributes
\sum_{r\in R}\upsilon(r)+1
to the total travel time. By moving the departure time earlier to
\pi_{(i,\mathrm{arr})}
, these costs vanish, while the transfer times from each other line to line
i
may increase by at most
1
. However, this increase of transfer time is clearly bounded by
\sum_{r\in R}\upsilon(r)
. Thus, the timetable
\pi
cannot minimize the total travel time.

We define
S\coloneqq\{i\in V\mid\pi_{(i,\mathrm{arr})}=\pi_{(i,\mathrm{dep})}=0\}
and
T\coloneqq\{i\in V\mid\pi_{(i,\mathrm{arr})}=\pi_{(i,\mathrm{dep})}=1\}
.
To see that
(S,T)
is an optimal solution to the Max-Cut problem,
note that
\pi
minimizes the sum of the weights multiplied with the periodic tensions in
I^{\prime}
. As every transfer arc has periodic tension
1
or
2
, this is the same as maximizing the sum of the weights of arcs with periodic tension
1
, which are exactly those between the sets
S
and
T
. Hence,
(S,T)
is a maximum-weight cut.
∎

Remark
.

It is well-known that finding a feasible timetable in the sense of PESP on arbitrary EANs is NP-hard for any fixed period time
T
with
T\geq 3

[
29
]
. This result does not carry over to
T=2
, as any instance can be preprocessed in such a way that every activity
a
is either fixed (
l_{a}=u_{a}
) or free (
u_{a}-l_{a}=T-1=1
). As fixed arcs can always be contracted (cf.
[
22
]
), no feasibility restrictions remain: Any PESP instance with
T=2
is trivially feasible.
Theorem

5.1
shows however that the hardness persists when asking for an optimal timetable. It is not surprising that simple networks suffice: It has been shown in
[
2
]
that the separation of a maximally violated cycle or change-cycle inequality for PESP-Passenger is already NP-hard on a star network with turnaround loops.

Now we establish a special case in which we know the structure of an optimal solution.

Definition 5.2

(Basel Solution Structure)
.

A timetable
\pi
for a one-station network
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{pass}}
has a
Basel solution structure
if all arrival events are scheduled at the same time
\pi^{\mathrm{arr}}
and all departure events at time
\pi^{\mathrm{dep}}
such that

(\pi^{\mathrm{dep}}-\pi^{\mathrm{arr}})\bmod T=l^{\max}\coloneqq\max\{l_{a}\mid a\in A\}.

The name “Basel solution structure” has been chosen since such a structure occurs in integrated interval-fixed interval timetables as they are, e.g., common in Basel, Switzerland. In these timetables, all trains arrive and depart roughly at the same time, respectively, as is also illustrated in
Fig.

1(a)
. The following result confirms that such a solution is highly attractive for passengers.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{pass}}=(E,A)
be a one-station network with lower and upper bounds
l_{a},u_{a}
on the arcs such that
u_{a}=l_{a}+T-1
for all transfer arcs
a\in A_{\mathrm{trans}}
. Then any timetable
\pi
with the Basel solution structure minimizes the total travel time independently of the weights if and only if

l_{a}=l_{a^{\prime}}
for all
a,a^{\prime}\in A_{\mathrm{trans}}\cup A_{\mathrm{wait}}
.

Proof.

First, let us assume that
l_{a}=l_{a^{\prime}}
for all
a,a^{\prime}\in A_{\mathrm{trans}}\cup A_{\mathrm{wait}}
. Let
\pi
be a timetable with the Basel solution structure. Then the periodic tensions induced by
\pi
are

x_{ij}=(\pi_{j}-\pi_{i}-l_{ij})\bmod T+l_{ij}=(l^{\max}-l^{\max})\bmod T+l^{\max}=l^{\max}

for all
ij\in A_{\mathrm{trans}}\cup A_{\mathrm{wait}}
. As we cannot do better than attaining the lower bounds on the tensions,
\pi
must be optimal.

Let us now assume that
\pi
is optimal and that there is an arc
a^{\prime}\in A_{\mathrm{trans}}\cup A_{\mathrm{wait}}
with
l_{a^{\prime}}<l^{\max}
. In the following we find a weight vector
w
for which
\pi
is not optimal. Let
a^{\prime}=i^{\prime}j^{\prime}
. Then the following timetable
\pi^{\prime}
achieves a lower objective value than
\pi
for the following weight vector
w
:

w_{ij}\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }ij=i^{\prime}j^{\prime},\\
0&\text{else,}\end{cases}\qquad\quad\pi_{i}^{\prime}\coloneqq\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i=i^{\prime},\\
l_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}&\text{if }i=j^{\prime},\\
\text{arbitrary feasible values}&\text{else.}\end{cases}

This is possible as only the waiting activities impose feasibility constraints.
The weighted sum of the periodic tensions w.r.t.
\pi
is
l^{\max}
, and it is
l_{a^{\prime}}
w.r.t.
\pi^{\prime}
. By assumption,
l_{a^{\prime}}<l^{\max}
, hence
\pi
is not optimal.
∎

While a timetable with the Basel solution as in
Proposition

5.3
is optimal for PESP-Passenger, the overlap of the energy activities is forced to be small (see also
Fig.

1
), as typically transfer, acceleration and braking times are relatively short:

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}=(E,A)
be a one-station network and let
\pi
be a timetable with the Basel solution structure. If
l^{\max}+\max\{t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{j}+t^{\mathrm{br}}_{i}\mid ji\in A_{\mathrm{energy}}\}<T
, then any solution to PESP-Energy using
\pi
has overlap
0
.

Proof.

Let
(M,\pi,x,o)
be a solution to PESP-Energy with
\pi
having the Basel solution structure. For
ji\in A_{\mathrm{energy}}
then holds
x_{ji}=T-l^{\max}
, which by assumption is strictly larger than
t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{j}+t^{\mathrm{br}}_{i}
. By
Lemma

3.2
, we find
o_{ji}=0
.
∎

6
PESP-Energy on a One-Station Network

Similar to the previous section where PESP-Passenger on a one-station network was analyzed with respect to its complexity and the structure of an optimal solution has been described for a simple special case, we now investigate the same questions for PESP-Energy on a one-station network. We distinguish between the two cases where an arbitrary set
A_{\mathrm{energy}}
of of event pairs is given between which an energy activity is possible and where an energy activity can be selected between every departure and arrival. While, in the first case, we are able to show the problem’s NP-hardness, in the second case, we provide a polynomial-time algorithm with additive performance bound.

We start with a simple upper bound for the objective value of the maximization of the brake-traction overlap on an arbitrary network.

Proposition 6.1
.

For an instance of PESP-Energy on an EAN
\mathcal{E}=(E,A)
, consider a feasible solution
S=(M,\pi,x,o)
. Let
W
be the maximum weight of a (perfect) matching in the graph
G=(E,A_{\mathrm{energy}})
with respect to the weights
\rho_{a}\coloneqq t^{\min}_{a}
for all
a\in A_{\mathrm{energy}}
. Then
\sum_{a\in M}o_{a}\leq W
.

Proof.

Each overlap is bounded from above by both the corresponding acceleration and the corresponding braking time:
o_{ji}\leq t_{j}^{\mathrm{ac}}
and
o_{ji}\leq t_{i}^{\mathrm{br}}
.
∎

The problem can be divided into two stages: first, select a matching of the energy arcs, i.e., determine which trains can transfer their energy to which trains, and second, find a timetable for the selected energy activities. Of course, the timetable affects what constitutes a good matching in the first stage. We first study the second stage of finding a timetable for a given matching.

6.1
The Timetable for a Given Matching of Energy Activities

In this subsection, we investigate for a one-station network how to obtain an optimal timetable for a given matching of energy activities, and how close its achieved overlap is to the weight of the matching.
The input
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{energy}}
is a bipartite graph with partition classes
E_{\mathrm{arr}}
and
E_{\mathrm{dep}}
. The set of waiting activities constitutes a perfect matching from
E_{\mathrm{arr}}
to
E_{\mathrm{dep}}
. These activities impose the only feasibility constraints on the timetable
\pi
. In contrast, the energy activities solely influence the objective value. Hence, there are no restrictions between arrival and departure times of different lines.

For any matching
M\subseteq A_{\mathrm{energy}}=E_{\mathrm{dep}}\times E_{\mathrm{arr}}
, the graph
(E,\,M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}})
is a union of node-disjoint directed cycles and directed paths. By
Corollary

3.3
, we achieve full overlap on a cycle
C
if and only if the periodic tension
x
satisfies
t_{a}^{\min}\leq x_{a}\leq t_{a}^{\max}
for all energy activities
a\in C\cap M
. On the other hand, for any feasible periodic tension, it has to hold that
l_{a}\leq x_{a}\leq u_{a}
for all
a\in A_{\mathrm{wait}}
and
\sum_{a\in C}x_{a}\in T\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 6.2
.

For a matching
M\subseteq A_{\mathrm{energy}}
and a cycle
C\subseteq M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
, we define the distance from the interval that allows full overlap to the nearest multiple of the period time by

\delta_{C}\coloneqq d([L_{C},U_{C}],T\mathbb{Z})=\min\{|x-\gamma T|\mid x\in[L_{C},U_{C}],\ \gamma\in\mathbb{Z}\},

where
L_{C}\coloneqq\sum_{a\in C\cap A_{\mathrm{wait}}}l_{a}+\sum_{a\in C\cap M}t_{a}^{\min}
and
U_{C}\coloneqq\sum_{a\in C\cap A_{\mathrm{wait}}}u_{a}+\sum_{a\in C\cap M}t_{a}^{\max}
.

A timetable with maximum brake-traction overlap on the selected energy activities can be determined for each connected component of
(E,M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}})
separately. The following
Theorem
describes the structure of an optimal timetable for a directed cycle. Recall that
\rho_{a}=t_{a}^{\min}
for
a\in A_{\mathrm{energy}}
. For a set
B\subseteq A
of activities, we write
\rho(B)\coloneqq\sum_{a\in B\cap A_{\mathrm{energy}}}\rho_{a}
and
o(B)\coloneqq\sum_{a\in S\cap A_{\mathrm{energy}}}o_{a}
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
M\subseteq A_{\mathrm{energy}}
be a matching, let
C\subseteq M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
be a cycle, and let
a_{0}\in C
with
\rho_{a_{0}}\leq\rho_{a}
for all
a\in C\cap M
. Then there is a solution
(M,\pi,x,o)
to PESP-Energy such that

(a)

o_{a}=\rho_{a}
for all
a\in C\cap M\setminus\{a_{0}\}
,

(b)

o(C)=\rho(C)-\min\{t^{\min}_{a_{0}},\,\delta_{C}\}
,

(c)

o(C)
is maximum among all solutions that fix the energy activities
M
.

Proof.

Suppose first
\delta_{C}=0
. Then there is a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
such that
l_{a}\leq x_{a}\leq u_{a}
for all
a\in C\cap A_{\mathrm{wait}}
and
t_{a}^{\min}\leq x_{a}\leq t_{a}^{\max}
for all
a\in C\cap M
such that
x(C)=\sum_{a\in C}x_{a}
is an integer multiple of
T
. In particular,
x
is a periodic tension on
C
, and by
Corollary

3.3
, we can realize full overlap
o_{a}=t^{\min}_{a}=\rho_{a}
on all energy activities in
C
.

Now assume that
\delta_{C}>0
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\delta_{C}
is the distance from
L_{C}
to the next integer multiple of
T
, i.e., there is a
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
L_{C}-\delta_{C}=\gamma T
and
U_{C}+\delta_{C}\leq\gamma T+T
. Define
x\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
via

x_{a}\coloneqq\begin{cases}l_{a}&\text{ if }a\in C\cap A_{\mathrm{wait}},\\
t_{a}^{\min}&\text{ if }a\in C\cap M\setminus\{a_{0}\},\\
(t_{a_{0}}^{\min}-\delta_{C})\bmod T&\text{ if }a=a_{0}.\end{cases}

Then
x(C)\equiv L_{C}-\delta_{C}\equiv\gamma T\equiv 0\pmod{T}
. We infer that
x
is a periodic tension on
C
that defines full overlap
o_{a}=t^{\min}_{a}=\rho_{a}
on all
a\in C\cap M\setminus\{a_{0}\}
. In the case that
\delta_{C}\leq t_{a_{0}}^{\min}
, we have
x_{a_{0}}=t_{a_{0}}^{\min}-\delta_{C}
and therefore
o_{a_{0}}=t_{a_{0}}^{\min}-\delta_{C}
, so that

o(C)=\sum_{a\in C\cap M\setminus\{a_{0}\}}\rho_{a}+t_{a_{0}}^{\min}-\delta_{C}=\rho(C)-\delta_{C}=\rho(C)-\min\{t_{a_{0}}^{\min},\,\delta_{C}\}.

Otherwise, if
\delta_{C}>t_{a_{0}}^{\min}
, we have
x_{a}=t_{a_{0}}^{\min}-\delta_{C}+T
, as
0<\delta_{C}<T
. Since then

\displaystyle x_{a_{0}}

\displaystyle=t_{a_{0}}^{\min}-\delta_{C}+T

\displaystyle\geq t_{a_{0}}^{\min}+U_{C}-\gamma T

\displaystyle=t_{a_{0}}^{\min}+(U_{C}-L_{C})+\delta_{C}

\displaystyle>t_{a_{0}}^{\min}+(t_{a_{0}}^{\max}-t_{a_{0}}^{\min})+t_{a_{0}}^{\min}

\displaystyle=t_{a_{0}}^{\min}+t_{a_{0}}^{\max},

we find
o_{a_{0}}=0
. This yields

o(C)=\sum_{a\in C\cap M\setminus\{a_{0}\}}\rho_{a}=\rho(C)-t_{a_{0}}^{\min}=\rho(C)-\min\{t_{a_{0}}^{\min},\,\delta_{C}\}.

The argument when
\delta_{C}
is the distance from
U_{C}
to the nearest integer multiple of
T
, is analogous, so that we have established (a) and (b).

It remains to show (c), i.e., that no feasible periodic tension can induce larger overlap on
C
. To this end, consider an optimal solution
(M,\pi^{*},x^{*},o^{*})
. Let

\delta^{*}\coloneqq d([L_{C},U_{C}],x^{*}(C))=\min\{|x-x^{*}(C)|\mid x\in[L_{C},U_{C}]\}.

Since
x^{*}(C)
is an integer multiple of
T
, we have
\delta^{*}\geq\delta_{C}
. The periodic tension
x^{*}
hence misses the full overlap interval
[L_{C},U_{C}]
by
\delta
. By definition of the overlap function (cf.
Fig.

5
), we lose an overlap of at least
\min\{t_{a_{0}}^{\min},\delta\}
. We obtain

o^{*}(C)\leq\rho(C)-\min\{t_{a_{0}}^{\min},\delta^{*}\}\leq\rho(C)-\min\{t_{a_{0}}^{\min},\delta_{C}\}=o(C),

showing that the periodic tension
x
constructed above realizes the maximum possible overlap on
C
.
∎

We can interpret directed paths that arise as components of
(E,M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}})
as cycles with a missing edge of overlap 0.
Hence, by
Theorem

6.3
, there is an optimal timetable for such a path such that all energy edges have full overlap.

6.2
Complexity Results for PESP-Energy

We have seen that finding an optimal timetable for given energy activities is an easy task. The complexity of PESP-Energy thus comes from the task of selecting the energy activities that admit an optimal timetable with a large brake-traction overlap. We start with the simple case where we have no restrictions on the waiting activities.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{energy}}
be a one-station network such that all waiting activities are free. Then a maximum-weight matching w.r.t.
\rho\coloneqq t^{\min}
yields an optimal solution to PESP-Energy whose overlap equals the matching’s weight. In particular, PESP-Energy is solvable in polynomial time.

Proof.

We first compute a maximum-weight matching
M
within the set
A_{\mathrm{energy}}
of possible energy activities as in
Proposition

6.1
. By
Theorem

6.3
, we find a timetable
\pi
that realizes the maximum possible overlap
o_{a}=t_{a}^{\min}
for all energy arcs except possibly for one arc per cycle component
C
of
A_{\mathrm{wait}}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}M
. Since every such cycle
C
necessarily contains a free waiting activity, we can add (modulo
T
) any number
\delta
to the periodic tension of the critical arc, and subtract (modulo
T
) the same number
\delta
from the periodic tension of an arbitrary waiting activity in
C
, while maintaining feasibility, and not altering the overlap of the other components of
A_{\mathrm{wait}}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}M
. This way, we can achieve the maximum overlap on all energy arcs of
C
. By
Proposition

6.1
, this solution is optimal.
∎

Let us now consider the more general case of PESP-Energy with restricted waiting times and a restricted set of compatible arrivals and departures between which an energy transfer is possible.

Theorem 6.5
.

The problem PESP-Energy on a one-station network
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{energy}}
with an arbitrary set of possible energy activities and arbitrary bounds on the waiting activities is NP-hard.

Proof.

We prove the statement through a reduction from the Hamiltonian Path Problem on a directed graph
D=(V,R)
with
|V|=n
. Let us define the following instance of PESP-Energy on a one-station network. Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{energy}}=(E,A)
be a one-station network with
n
lines, where a pair
((j,\mathrm{dep}),(i,\mathrm{arr}))
is contained in
A_{\mathrm{energy}}
if and only if there is an arc
ji\in R
. Let the bounds on the waiting activities be given by
l_{a}=u_{a}=0
for all
a\in A_{\mathrm{wait}}
, hence, the departure time of a line will be its arrival time. The braking and acceleration times of all lines are set to
t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{i}=t^{\mathrm{br}}_{i}=1
for all
i\in[n]
. The period time is chosen to be equal to the number of lines plus one:
T=n+1
.

Now, there is a Hamiltonian path in
D
if and only if there is a solution to our PESP-Energy instance with a total overlap of at least
n-1
. Assuming there is a Hamiltonian path from
s
to
t
in
D
, we select the energy activities corresponding to the arcs of the path. We can then start at the departure node of the line associated with
s
and set its departure time to
0
. Then, given that we have visited node
i
, for node
i+1
of the Hamiltonian path we fix the arrival and departure times of the corresponding line to
\pi((i+1,\mathrm{arr}))=\pi((i+1,\mathrm{dep}))=\pi((i,\mathrm{arr}))+1
. Like this, we obtain full overlap on the energy arcs given by the Hamiltonian path. As the Hamiltonian path consists of
n-1
arcs, we achieve an overlap of
n-1
.

Consider now a solution
S=(M,\pi,x,o)
to PESP-Energy with overlap of at least
n-1
. Then there must be full overlap on at least
n-1
energy arcs. Let
A_{\text{full}}
be the se of these arcs. Then
A_{\text{full}}\cup A_{\mathrm{wait}}
induces a union of paths and cycles in
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. If there is a cycle of full overlap arcs, then every arc on that cycle must have a periodic tension (duration) of
1
, which is not possible because
T>n
. Therefore, there are only paths. Since
A_{\text{full}}
consists of
n-1
arcs, there can only be a single path, which is thus a Hamiltonian path in
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{energy}}
. This corresponds to a Hamiltonian path in the original graph
D
.
∎

The cases that are not covered by
Theorems

6.4
and
6.5
, i.e., when waiting activities can have arbitrary bounds and
\mathcal{E}_{n}
is complete are studied in the following subsections.

6.3
Perfect and Greedy Matchings of Energy Arcs

While
Theorem

6.3
describes the structure of an optimal timetable for each connected component resulting from a fixed set
M
of energy activities, we will now investigate the problem of selecting the energy activities.
In the following, we assume that an energy activity is possible between any departure and arrival event (
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{energy}}=K_{n,n}
). The next result provides a lower bound on the number of energy activities in a globally optimal solution.

Theorem 6.6
.

In every optimal solution
S=(M,\pi,x,o)
to PESP-Energy on a one-station network
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{energy}}
there are at least
n-1
energy activities in
M
.

Proof.

Let
S=(M,\pi,x,o)
be an optimal solution to PESP-Energy with
|M|<n-1
, so at least two connected components of
(E,A_{\mathrm{wait}}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}M)
are directed paths
P_{1},P_{2}
. For
k\in\{1,2\}
let
i_{k}\in E_{\mathrm{arr}}
be the start and
j_{k}\in E_{\mathrm{dep}}
be the end node of
P_{k}
.

Let
M^{\prime}\coloneqq M\cup\{j_{1}i_{2}\}
,
c\coloneqq\pi_{j_{1}}+t^{\min}_{j_{1}i_{2}}-\pi_{i_{2}}
, and let us define the following timetable

\pi^{\prime}_{v}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\pi_{v}&\text{if }v\in E\setminus V(P_{2}),\\
(\pi_{v}+c)\bmod T&\text{if }v\in V(P_{2}).\end{cases}

We note that
\pi^{\prime}
is feasible.
For the new energy activities
M^{\prime}
and the new timetable
\pi^{\prime}
, we obtain the periodic tensions

x^{\prime}_{ji}=\begin{cases}(\pi^{\prime}_{i}-\pi^{\prime}_{j})\bmod T=(\pi_{i}-\pi_{j})\bmod T=x_{ji}&\text{for }ji\in M,\\
(\pi^{\prime}_{i_{2}}-\pi^{\prime}_{j_{1}})\bmod T=t_{j_{1}i_{2}}^{\min}&\text{for }j=j_{1},i=i_{2},\end{cases}

and hence the brake-traction overlaps

o^{\prime}_{ji}=\begin{cases}o_{ji}&\text{if }ji\in M,\\
t^{\min}_{j_{1}i_{2}}&\text{if }ji=j_{1}i_{2}.\end{cases}

Consequently, as
t^{\min}_{j_{1}i_{2}}>0
,
S^{\prime}\coloneqq(M^{\prime},\pi^{\prime},x^{\prime},o^{\prime})
yields a better objective value than
S
, so
S
cannot be optimal.
∎

Corollary 6.7
.

If
M
is a matching that maximizes the achievable overlap and is not perfect, then there is a unique perfect matching
M^{p}
extending
M
, and this leads to the same overlap.

This yields an alternative way of looking at optimal solutions to PESP-Energy on a one-station network. A perfect matching
M^{p}\subseteq A_{\mathrm{energy}}
corresponds one-to-one to a permutation
\varphi\colon[n]\to[n]
of the trains (lines) in a one-station network. This is given by
\varphi(\ell)=k
if and only if
((\ell,\mathrm{dep}),(k,\mathrm{arr}))\in M^{p}
. The directed cycles in
M^{p}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
then correspond to the cycles of the permutation.

Recall that we can find an upper bound for the objective value of a PESP-Energy instance by calculating the maximum-weight perfect matching within the set of possible energy activities
a\in A_{\mathrm{energy}}
w.r.t. the weights
\rho\colon A_{\mathrm{energy}}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\rho_{ji}=\min\{t_{j}^{\mathrm{ac}},t_{i}^{\mathrm{br}}\}
(cf.
Proposition

6.1
). It is known that for such weight structure, the maximum-weight perfect matching can be found easily by a greedy algorithm for the weights in our problem. Sorting both
t^{\mathrm{br}}_{i}
,
i\in E_{\mathrm{arr}}
, and
t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{j}
,
j\in E_{\mathrm{dep}}
, according to their sizes, we obtain permutations
\sigma
and
\varphi
with
t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\sigma(1)}\leq\dots\leq t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\sigma(n)}
and
t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi(1)}\leq\dots\leq t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi(n)}
.

Lemma 6.8

(e.g.
[
36
, Lemma 1]
)
.

M_{\mathrm{greedy}}\coloneqq\bigl\{(\sigma(i),\varphi(i))\in E_{\mathrm{dep}}\times E_{\mathrm{arr}}\bigm|i\in[n]\bigr\}
is a maximum-weight perfect matching w.r.t.
\rho
in the graph
(E,A_{\mathrm{energy}})
.

.
By applying

Although Hamiltonian paths might sound impractical at first glance, we show now that in our case they can be computed efficiently.

Theorem 6.10
.

A Hamiltonian path
in
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{energy}}
traversing all waiting activities that has maximum weight w.r.t.
\rho
can be found in polynomial time.

In order to prove this theorem, we show that this problem can be transformed to the so-called
Large TSP
,
for which a polynomial-time algorithm is known, see
[
36
]
. To simplify the notation, in this section we write
t_{\ell}^{\mathrm{ac}}\coloneqq t_{(\ell,\mathrm{dep})}^{\mathrm{ac}}
and
t_{\ell}^{\mathrm{br}}\coloneqq t_{(\ell,\mathrm{arr})}^{\mathrm{br}}
for
\ell\in[n]
.
Moreover, we can contract the waiting edges because they must appear in any allowed Hamiltonian path. This results in the complete bi-directed graph
G=([n],\mathcal{A})
on the set of all lines and weights
\rho_{G}\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\rho^{G}(k\ell)\coloneqq\min\{t_{k}^{\mathrm{ac}},t_{\ell}^{\mathrm{br}}\}
. This is formalized in the following
Lemma
.

Lemma 6.11
.

If
P_{G}
is a maximum-weight Hamiltonian path in
G
w.r.t.
\rho^{G}
, then

P\coloneqq\{((k,\mathrm{dep}),(\ell,\mathrm{arr}))\mid k\ell\in P_{G}\}\cup\{((\ell,\mathrm{arr}),(\ell,\mathrm{dep}))\mid\ell\in[n]\}

is a maximum-weight Hamiltonian path in
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{energy}}
w.r.t.
\rho
traversing all waiting activities, where we set
\rho((\ell,\mathrm{arr}),(\ell,\mathrm{dep}))\coloneqq 0
for all
\ell\in[n]
.

Proof.

First,
P
is the arc set of a Hamiltonian path and contains all waiting activities. Consider a maximum-weight Hamiltonian path
P^{\mathrm{opt}}
that visits all waiting activities. Then

(P^{\mathrm{opt}})_{G}\coloneqq\{k\ell\mid((k,\mathrm{dep}),(\ell,\mathrm{arr}))\in P^{\mathrm{opt}}\}

is a Hamiltonian path in
G
. In particular,
\rho^{G}((P^{\mathrm{opt}})_{G})\leq\rho^{G}(P_{G})
, so that

\rho(P^{\mathrm{opt}})=\rho^{G}((P^{\mathrm{opt}})_{G})\leq\rho^{G}(P_{G})=\rho(P),

and we conclude
\rho(P)=\rho(P^{\mathrm{opt}})
.
∎

The next two lemmata deal with Hamiltonian cycles.

Lemma 6.12
.

Any maximum-weight Hamiltonian cycle in
G
w.r.t.
\rho^{G}
contains a maximum-weight Hamiltonian path. Conversely, any maximum-weight Hamiltonian path can be closed to a maximum-weight Hamiltonian cycle.

Proof.

Let
k\coloneqq\operatorname{arg\,min}\{\min\{t_{\ell}^{\mathrm{ac}},t_{\ell}^{\mathrm{br}}\}\mid\ell\in[n]\}
. W.l.o.g. let us assume
t_{k}^{\mathrm{br}}\leq t_{k}^{\mathrm{ac}}
. We know that all incoming arcs of
k
have weight
\rho^{G}_{\min}\coloneqq t_{k}^{\mathrm{br}}
.
Let
C^{\mathrm{opt}}
be a maximum-weight Hamiltonian cycle. Since
C^{\mathrm{opt}}
must visit
k
, it must contain an arc
a
of weight
\rho^{G}_{\min}
. Then
P\coloneqq C^{\mathrm{opt}}\setminus\{a\}
is a Hamiltonian path with weight
\rho^{G}(P)=\rho^{G}(C^{\mathrm{opt}})-\rho^{G}_{\min}
.

Let now
P^{\mathrm{opt}}
be a maximum-weight Hamiltonian path, and let
v
be the first and
u
be the last vertex in
P^{\mathrm{opt}}
. Then
C\coloneqq P^{\mathrm{opt}}\cup\{uv\}
is a Hamiltonian cycle with weight

\rho^{G}(C)=\rho^{G}(P^{\mathrm{opt}})+\rho^{G}(uv)\geq\rho^{G}(P^{\mathrm{opt}})+\rho^{G}_{\min}\geq\rho^{G}(P)+\rho^{G}_{\min}=\rho^{G}(C^{\mathrm{opt}}).

Therefore,
\rho^{G}(C)=\rho^{G}(C^{\mathrm{opt}})
and
\rho^{G}(P)=\rho^{G}(P^{\mathrm{opt}})
, so that

P
and
C
must be optimal.
∎

This shows that a Hamiltonian path as in
Theorem

6.10
can be found by computing a maximum-weight Hamiltonian cycle in
G
w.r.t.
\rho^{G}
. This is obviously equivalent to computing a minimum-weight Hamiltonian cycle for the weights
-\rho^{G}_{k\ell}=\max\{-t_{k}^{\mathrm{ac}},-t_{\ell}^{\mathrm{br}}\}
. This is an instance of the Large TSP, which is well known to be solvable in polynomial time, see e.g.
[
36
]
.

The maximum-weight Hamiltonian path yields a feasible solution to PESP-Energy on a one-station network. We can guarantee that the objective value of this solution is not further away from the optimal objective value than the smaller of the largest acceleration and the largest braking time. This follows from the following
Theorem
, which bounds the difference between the lower bound from
Proposition

6.9
and the upper bound from the maximum-weight matching (
Proposition

6.1
).

Theorem 6.13
.

Let
P
be a Hamiltonian path traversing all waiting activities in
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{energy}}
of maximum weight. Then it holds

\rho(M_{\mathrm{greedy}})-\rho(P\cap A_{\mathrm{energy}})\leq\min\bigl\{\max\{t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\ell}\mid\ell\in[n]\},\ \max\{t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell}\mid\ell\in[n]\}\bigr\}.

Proof.

We iteratively convert the greedy matching
M^{0}_{\text{greedy}}
into a matching inducing a Hamiltonian cycle and bound the total reduction of weight in this process. Finally, we delete one edge to obtain a Hamiltonian path.

Let us assume that
t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{1}\leq\dots\leq t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{n}
holds, and let
\varphi^{0}
denote the permutation obtained by
M^{0}_{\text{greedy}}
such that
t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(1)}\leq\dots\leq t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(n)}
. Then
M^{0}_{\text{greedy}}=\{((\ell,\mathrm{dep}),(\varphi^{0}(\ell),\mathrm{arr}))\mid\ell\in[n]\}
.
The permutation
\varphi^{i}
corresponds to the perfect matching
M^{i}
obtained in iteration
i
. In each iteration, we obtain the matching
M^{i}
as follows from the matching
M^{i-1}
.
Let
C\subseteq M^{i-1}\cup A_{\mathrm{wait}}
be the cycle containing
(1,\mathrm{dep})
. If
C
is a Hamiltonian cycle, we are done. Otherwise, there is a smallest
\ell_{i}
in iteration
i
such that
(\ell_{i},\mathrm{dep})\in C
but
(\ell_{i}+1,\mathrm{dep})\not\in C
.
We define the new permutation
\varphi^{i}
as follows:

\varphi^{i}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i}+1)&\text{if }x=\ell_{i},\\
\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i})&\text{if }x=\ell_{i}+1,\\
\varphi^{i-1}(x)&\text{else.}\end{cases}

For the new matching
M^{i}
we have:

\displaystyle M^{i}=M^{i-1}

\displaystyle\cup\bigl\{((\ell_{i},\mathrm{dep}),(\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i}+1),\mathrm{arr})),((\ell_{i}+1,\mathrm{dep}),(\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i}),\mathrm{arr}))\bigr\}

\displaystyle\setminus\bigl\{((\ell_{i},\mathrm{dep}),(\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i}),\mathrm{arr})),((\ell_{i}+1,\mathrm{dep}),(\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i}+1),\mathrm{arr}))\bigr\}.

The cycle’s length increases by this operation and for the weight of the new matching
M^{i}
, we get

\displaystyle\rho(M^{i})

\displaystyle=\rho(M^{i-1})+\min\{t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i}+1)}\}+\min\{t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}+1},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i})}\}

\displaystyle\hskip 62.0pt-\min\{t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i})}\}-\min\{t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}+1},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i}+1)}\}

\displaystyle=\rho(M^{i-1})-|[t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}},t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}+1}]\cap[t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i})},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i}+1)}]|.

Note that in each iteration
i
we have
t_{\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i})}^{\mathrm{br}}\leq t_{\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i}+1)}^{\mathrm{br}}
, as the inequality holds for
\varphi^{0}
, and the image of
\ell_{i}+1
has not been changed in iterations
1,\dotsc,i-1
, while the image of
\ell_{i}
can only have decreased.
Therefore, we have
\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i})\leq\varphi^{0}(\ell_{i})\leq\varphi^{0}(\ell_{i}+1)=\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i}+1)
.

Further, we know that
[t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i})},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i}+1)}]\subseteq[t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(1)},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(n)}]
.
Hence, the length of the intersection can be bounded by

|[t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}},t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}+1}]\cap[t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i})},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{i-1}(\ell_{i}+1)}]|\leq|[t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}},t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}+1}]\cap[t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(1)},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(n)}]|

and, therefore

\rho(M^{i})\geq\rho(M^{i-1})-|[t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}},t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}+1}]\cap[t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(1)},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(n)}]|.

Let
k
be the number of iterations until we obtain a matching
M^{k}
such that
M^{k}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
corresponds to a Hamiltonian cycle. It holds
k\leq n-1
as there are
n
different trains.

\displaystyle\rho(M^{k})

\displaystyle\geq\rho(M^{0}_{\text{greedy}})-\sum_{i=1}^{k}|[t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}},t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}+1}]\cap[t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(1)},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(n)}]|

We can bound the sum of the intersections as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}|[t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}},t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}+1}]\cap[t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(1)},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(n)}]|

\displaystyle\leq\biggl\lvert\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}[t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{j},t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{j+1}]\biggr\rvert=t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{n}-t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{1},

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}|[t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}},t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\ell_{i}+1}]\cap[t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(1)},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(n)}]|

\displaystyle\leq|[t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(1)},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(n)}]|=t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(n)}-t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(1)}.

Thus,
\rho(M^{k})\geq\rho(M^{0}_{\text{greedy}})-\min\{t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{n}-t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{1},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(n)}-t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(1)}\}
. In order to receive a Hamiltonian path
P\subseteq M^{k}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
, we delete one edge from the matching
M^{k}
. As we want to maximize the path’s weight, we choose the edge with the lowest weight. Due to the weight structure, this weight is
\min\{t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{1},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(1)}\}
. For the difference of the weights of the greedy matching
M_{\text{greedy}}
and the weight of the energy arcs in
H
, we get:

\displaystyle\rho(M_{\text{greedy}})-\rho(P\cap A_{\mathrm{energy}})

\displaystyle\leq\min\{t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{n}-t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{1},\,t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(n)}-t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(1)}\}+\min\{t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{1},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(1)}\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\{t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{n},t^{\mathrm{br}}_{\varphi^{0}(n)}\}.

The weight of the path
P
is a lower bound for the weight of an optimal Hamiltonian path.
∎

7
Polynomial Cases of PESP-Energy

In this section, we identify special cases of PESP-Energy on a one-station network
K_{n,n}
with arbitrary bounds on waiting activities that can be solved in polynomial time.
Each special case is based on a particular choice of the acceleration and braking times.

7.1
Special Case: Large Period Time
T

In the first case, we consider a period time that is so large that no cycle
C\subseteq M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
can have full overlap on all energy arcs. This corresponds to an aperiodic timetabling problem.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
u^{\max}\coloneqq\max\{u_{a}\mid a\in A_{\mathrm{wait}}\}+\max\{t^{\min}_{a}+t^{\max}_{a}\mid a\in A_{\mathrm{energy}}\}
, and let

T\geq n\cdot u^{\max}
. Then, any matching
M^{H}
inducing a Hamiltonian path of maximum weight w.r.t.
\rho\coloneqq t^{\min}
defines an optimal solution
S=(M^{H},\pi^{H},x^{H},o^{H})
.

Proof.

Let
S=(M,\pi,x,o)
be an optimal solution and let
M^{H}
be any matching that induces a maximum-weight Hamiltonian path
P^{H}=M^{H}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
w.r.t.
\rho
. Applying
Theorem

6.3
to the cycle obtained by adding the missing arc to
P^{H}
, we find a timetable
\pi^{H}
with overlap
o^{H}
such that
o^{H}_{a}=t^{\min}_{a}
for all
a\in P^{H}\cap A_{\mathrm{energy}}M^{H}
. Then
\sum_{a\in M^{H}}o^{H}_{a}=\sum_{a\in P^{H}\cap A_{\mathrm{energy}}}\rho_{a}
.

Now consider an arbitrary matching
M\subseteq A_{\mathrm{energy}}
.
By
Corollary

6.7
, we can assume that
M
is perfect. Let
C
be an arbitrary directed cycle in
M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
. By
Theorem

6.3
(a), we can assume that
\pi
induces full overlap on all but the arc of
C
with smallest
t^{\min}
, say
a_{0}
. Recalling
Definition

6.2
, due to
l\geq 0
and
t>0
, we have
L_{C}\geq t_{a_{0}}^{\min}
. As also
U_{C}+t_{a_{0}}^{\min}\leq n\cdot u^{\max}\leq T
, we conclude
\delta_{C}\geq t_{a_{0}}^{\min}
, and hence
o_{a_{0}}=0
and
o(C)=\rho(C)-t_{a_{0}}^{\min}
by the formula in
Theorem

6.3
(b).

Removing
a_{0}
from
M
, we still maintain a matching with maximum overlap. We can delete the no-overlap arcs
a_{0}^{1},\dotsc,a_{0}^{m}
in all cycles
C_{1},\dotsc,C_{m}
of
M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
and obtain

o(M)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\rho(C_{k})-t_{a_{0}^{k}}^{\min})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\rho(C_{k}\setminus\{a_{0}\}).

By adding additional arcs, we can connect the paths
C_{k}\setminus\{a_{0}\}
to a Hamiltonian path
P
. Therefore,
o(M)\leq\rho(P)\leq\rho(P^{H})
.
∎

7.2
Special Case: Equal Acceleration or Equal Braking Times

In the second case, all trains need the same time for the acceleration (or the braking) process, i.e.,

t_{i}^{\mathrm{ac}}=t^{\mathrm{ac}}
for all
i\in E^{\mathrm{dep}}
or
t_{i}^{\mathrm{br}}=t^{\mathrm{br}}
for all
i\in E^{\mathrm{arr}}
. W.l.o.g., let us assume that all acceleration times are equal, i.e.,
t^{\mathrm{ac}}=t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{j}
for all
i,j\in[n]
. Then the possible overlap between one braking train and any accelerating train is the same. Hence, it becomes irrelevant for which two trains the departure and the arrival are synchronized. Therefore, the solution of one big cycle of trains in arbitrary order together with a timetable maximizing the overlap on this cycle is optimal.

Proposition 7.2
.

Suppose that all acceleration times are equal, i.e.,
t^{\mathrm{ac}}=t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{j}
for all
i,j\in[n]
, or all braking times are equal, i.e.,
t^{\mathrm{br}}=t^{\mathrm{br}}_{i}
for all
i,j\in[n]
. Then, there is an optimal solution consisting of one cycle of all lines in arbitrary order. This can be found in polynomial time.

Proof.

Let us assume that there is no optimal solution consisting of a single cycle, and consider an optimal solution
(M,\pi,x,o)
with the minimum number of cycles. Let
C_{1},C_{2}
be two different cycles in
M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
, and let
a_{k}=(j_{k},i_{k})\in A_{\mathrm{energy}}\cap C_{k}
for
k=1,2

Consider the alternative solution with
M^{\prime}\coloneqq(M\setminus\{a_{1},a_{2}\})\cup\{j_{1}i_{2},j_{2}i_{1}\}
. Then
M^{\prime}
induces a big cycle on the node set
V(C_{1})\cup V(C_{2})
. Let
c\coloneqq\pi_{j_{1}}+x_{a_{1}}-\pi_{i_{2}}
, and set

\pi^{\prime}_{v}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\pi_{v}&\text{if }v\in E\setminus V(C_{2}),\\
(\pi_{v}+c)\bmod T&\text{if }v\in V(C_{2}).\end{cases}

Then the arc
j_{1}i_{2}
with braking time
t^{\mathrm{br}}_{i_{2}}
has the new periodic tension
x_{j_{1}i_{2}}^{\prime}=(\pi^{\prime}_{i_{2}}-\pi^{\prime}_{j_{1}})\bmod T=(\pi_{i_{2}}+c-\pi_{j_{1}})\bmod T=x_{a_{1}}
, i.e., it also has the same overlap as there was on arc
a_{1}
because
t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{i_{1}}=t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{i_{2}}
.
Moreover, the arc
j_{2}i_{1}
has periodic tension

x^{\prime}_{j_{2}i_{1}}=(\pi_{i_{1}}^{\prime}-\pi_{j_{2}}^{\prime})\bmod T=(\pi_{i_{1}}-\pi_{j_{2}}-c)\bmod T=(\pi_{i_{1}}-\pi_{j_{1}}+\pi_{i_{2}}-\pi_{j_{2}}-x_{a_{1}})\bmod T=x_{a_{2}}
, i.e., the overlap is also equal to the one on arc
a_{2}
as
t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{i_{1}}=t^{\mathrm{ac}}_{i_{2}}
. Therefore, together the overlap on the two new arcs is the same as on the two old arcs. So we have found an optimal solution with fewer cycles, which constitutes a contradiction.
∎

7.3
Special Case: Acceleration Time Equals Braking Time

In the following, we investigate the case that each train accelerates as long as it brakes, i.e.,
t_{i}^{\mathrm{ac}}=t_{i}^{\mathrm{br}}
for all
i\in[n]
. Hence, we can denote the time that train
i
needs for accelerating/braking simply by
t_{i}
. We assume that the
n
trains in a one-station network are ordered such that
t_{i}\leq t_{i+1}
for all
i\in[n-1]
.
First we describe the structure of the matching induced by a maximum-weight Hamiltonian cycle.

Lemma 7.3
.

The matching
M_{H}\coloneqq\{((i,\mathrm{dep}),(i-1,\mathrm{arr}))\mid i\in\{2,\dots,n\}\}\cup\{((1,\mathrm{dep}),(n,\mathrm{arr}))\}
is of maximum weight w.r.t.
\rho
among the matchings
M
that result in exactly one cycle
C=M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
.

Proof.

Let
M^{*}
be a matching of maximum weight such that
M^{*}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
is a cycle. Now, we know that for the arcs in
M^{*}
incident to the nodes
(1,\mathrm{dep})
and
(1,\mathrm{arr})
, we obtain the minimum weight
t_{1}
. Hence, for the second smallest train, we also know that the weight
t_{2}
must be obtained on at least one of the arcs in
M^{*}
incident to
(2,\mathrm{dep})
or
(2,\mathrm{arr})
. Otherwise, they would both be matched to nodes with a smaller weight, but such a matching would result in a closed cycle and contradict the assumption that
M^{*}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
forms one cycle. Similar, for any
0<i<n
, we can argue that
M^{*}
must contain at least
i+1
arcs with a weight smaller or equal to the one of the
i^{\text{th}}
smallest train. Finally, we know that, in case it holds
t_{n}>t_{n-1}
, there is no arc in
M^{*}
with the weight
t_{n}
. However, if it holds
t_{n}=t_{n-1}=\dots=t_{n-k}
, then there can be at most
k-1
arcs in
M^{*}
with weight
t_{n}
.
This means that we cannot do better than obtaining the weights of the matching
M_{H}
, i.e.
t_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}
.
∎

Next, we want to calculate the optimal objective value of the overlap under the condition that the given trains are supposed to be matched into one cycle.

Lemma 7.4
.

The matching
M_{H}
from
Lemma

7.3
yields a cycle
C=M_{H}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
with maximum overlap

o(C)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{n}+\max\{t_{1}-\delta_{C},\,0\},

where
t_{1}
is the smallest acceleration/braking time of all
i\in C
and
t_{n}
is the largest.

Proof.

We apply the formula in
Theorem

6.3
(b). Computing
\rho(C)
as in the proof of
Lemma

7.3
,

o(C)=\rho(C)-\min\{t_{1},\delta_{C}\}=t_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}-\min\{t_{1},\delta_{C}\}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{n}-\min\{0,t_{1}-\delta_{C}\}.

∎

Now, we go back to the original problem and consider a matching
M
that maximizes the total overlap. Then for each connected component (cycle) of
M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
, we can assume that the cycle follows the order described above. Therefore, it suffices to specify which trains are contained in each cycle.
Let us now investigate the relation of the cycles of
M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
to each other.

Definition 7.5
.

Let
C_{1},C_{2}\subseteq M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
be two cycles obtained from a matching of energy arcs. We denote by
s_{1}=\text{arg}\min\{t_{i}\mid i\in C_{1}\}
the train with smallest acceleration/braking time in
C_{1}
and by
s_{2}=\text{arg}\min\{t_{i}\mid i\in C_{2}\}
the train with smallest acceleration/braking time in
C_{2}
. Without loss of generality, let
t_{s_{1}}\leq t_{s_{2}}
. Let
l_{1}=\text{arg}\max\{t_{i}\mid i\in C_{1}\}
denote the train with largest acceleration/braking time in
C_{1}
.

C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are called
crossing cycles
if
t_{l_{1}}\geq t_{s_{2}}
.

Note that for crossing cycles, we have
\min\{t_{l_{1}},t_{l_{2}}\}\geq t_{s_{2}}
.

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
C_{1}\cup C_{2}=M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
be two crossing cycles obtained from a matching of energy arcs on a one-station network with
n
trains. Then, the maximum overlap obtained by
M
is smaller or equal to the maximum overlap obtained by a maximum-weight Hamiltonian path matching
M_{H}
, i.e.
o(M)\leq o(M_{H})
.

Proof.

For the overlaps we obtain by
Lemma

7.4

\displaystyle o(C_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in C_{1}}t_{i}-t_{l_{1}}+\max\{t_{s_{1}}-\delta_{C_{1}},\,0\},

\displaystyle o(C_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in C_{2}}t_{i}-t_{l_{2}}+\max\{t_{s_{2}}-\delta_{C_{2}},\,0\},

\displaystyle o(C)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{n}+\max\{t_{1}-\delta_{C},\,0\}.

This means

\displaystyle o(C_{1})+o(C_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{l_{1}}-t_{l_{2}}+\max\{t_{s_{1}}-\delta_{C_{1}},\,0\}+\max\{t_{s_{2}}-\delta_{C_{2}},\,0\}.

We want to show that
o(C_{1})+o(C_{2})\leq o(C)
.

•

If
t_{s_{1}}-\delta_{C_{1}}\leq 0
and
t_{s_{2}}-\delta_{C_{2}}\leq 0
, then

\displaystyle o(C_{1})+o(C_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{l_{1}}-t_{l_{2}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{n}\leq o(C),

as one of
t_{l_{1}}
and
t_{l_{2}}
equals
t_{n}
.

•

If
t_{s_{1}}-\delta_{C_{1}}\geq 0
and
t_{s_{2}}-\delta_{C_{2}}\leq 0
, then

\displaystyle o(C_{1})+o(C_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{l_{1}}-t_{l_{2}}+t_{s_{1}}-\delta_{C_{1}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{n}\leq o(C),

because
\max\{t_{l_{1}},t_{l_{2}}\}=t_{n}
,
\min\{t_{l_{1}},t_{l_{2}}\}\geq t_{s_{1}}
, and
\delta_{C_{1}}\geq 0
.

•

If
t_{s_{1}}-\delta_{C_{1}}\leq 0
and
t_{s_{2}}-\delta_{C_{2}}\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle o(C_{1})+o(C_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{l_{1}}-t_{l_{2}}+t_{s_{2}}-\delta_{C_{2}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{n}\leq o(C),

because
\max\{t_{l_{1}},t_{l_{2}}\}=t_{n}
,
\min\{t_{l_{1}},t_{l_{2}}\}\geq t_{s_{2}}
as
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are crossing cycles, and
\delta_{C_{2}}\geq 0
.

•

Finally, if
t_{s_{1}}-\delta_{C_{1}}\geq 0
and
t_{s_{2}}-\delta_{C_{2}}\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle o(C_{1})+o(C_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{l_{1}}-t_{l_{2}}+t_{s_{1}}-\delta_{C_{1}}+t_{s_{2}}-\delta_{C_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{n}+t_{s_{1}}-(t^{\min}_{l}-t_{s_{2}})-\delta_{C_{1}}-\delta_{C_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{n}+t_{1}-k-\delta_{C_{1}}-\delta_{C_{2}},

as it holds
t_{s_{1}}=t_{1}
and
\max\{t_{l_{1}},t_{l_{2}}\}=t_{n}
. Further, we define
t^{\min}_{l}:=\min\{t_{l_{1}},t_{l_{2}}\}\geq t_{s_{2}}
, which holds as
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are crossing cycles, and set
k\coloneqq t^{\min}_{l}-t_{s_{2}}
.
If we consider the sum of the lower bounds and minima of the departure/arrival times on the energy arcs on cycles, we obtain

\displaystyle L_{C}

\displaystyle=L_{C_{1}}+L_{C_{2}}+k.

Further, we know that that the sum of all lower and all upper bounds is independent of the matching. Hence, it holds

\displaystyle L_{C}+U_{C}

\displaystyle=L_{C_{1}}+U_{C_{1}}+L_{C_{2}}+U_{C_{2}}.

This yields

\displaystyle U_{C}

\displaystyle=U_{C_{1}}+U_{C_{2}}-k.

Hence, we get the following estimation for
\delta_{C}
:

\displaystyle\delta_{C}=d([L_{C},U_{C}],T\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle=d([L_{C_{1}}+L_{C_{2}}+k,U_{C_{1}}+U_{C_{2}}-k],T\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\leq d([L_{C_{1}}+L_{C_{2}},U_{C_{1}}+U_{C_{2}}],T\mathbb{Z})+k

\displaystyle\leq d([L_{C_{1}},\,U_{C_{1}}],T\mathbb{Z})+d([L_{C_{2}},U_{C_{2}}],T\mathbb{Z})+k

\displaystyle=\delta_{C_{1}}+\delta_{C_{2}}+k.

Therefore, we obtain the following result for the overlap of
C
:

\displaystyle o(C)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{n}+t_{1}-\min\{t_{1},\delta_{C}\}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{n}+t_{1}-\min\{t_{1},\delta_{C_{1}}+\delta_{C_{2}}+k\}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-t_{n}+t_{1}-k-\delta_{C_{1}}-\delta_{C_{2}}

\displaystyle=o(C_{1})+o(C_{2}).

∎

Corollary 7.7
.

There is an optimal matching of energy activities that does not induce any crossing cycles.

We can exploit this knowledge in order to obtain an efficient algorithm. Any cycle in such a solution consists of subsequent trains according to the acceleration and braking times. Let
C_{s}^{l}=\{s,s+1,\dots,l-1,l\}
denote the cycle containing all trains from
s
to
l
with

. Then
o(C_{s}^{l})=\sum_{i=s}^{l-1}t_{i}+\max\{t_{s}-\delta_{C_{s}}^{l},\,0\}
.

Dynamic Program

8
Cycle-Restricted Variants of PESP-Energy

While we were able to show that PESP-Passenger is NP-hard on a one-station network, for PESP-Energy it is still open whether solutions can be found in polynomial time for one-station networks.
In this section, we determine the complexity status of two variants of PESP-Energy. These variants of the original problem demand a certain structure of the matching of energy arcs in the solution. They differ in the amount of cycles that are allowed to be formed by the (perfect) matching together with the waiting activities
M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
in a feasible solution.

8.1
Exactly One Cycle of Trains Induced by
M

In the following we will show that the cycle obtained by a maximum-weight Hamiltonian path has maximum overlap among all solutions whose matching induces a single cycle.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
M^{H}
be a matching specifying the energy activities in a PESP-Energy instance
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{energy}}
on a one-station network such that
C^{H}=M^{H}\cup A_{\mathrm{wait}}
is a Hamiltonian cycle in
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{energy}}
of maximum weight w.r.t.
\rho\coloneqq t^{\min}
. Then, there is a timetable
\pi^{H}
such that the solution
(M^{H},\pi^{H},x^{H},o^{H})
results in the maximum overlap among all solutions
(M,\pi,x,o)
that yield a single cycle
M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
.

Proof.

Let
(M,\pi,x,o)
be optimal such that
C\coloneqq M\cup A_{\mathrm{wait}}
is a single cycle. Since
M
and
M^{H}
are both perfect matchings,

L_{C}+U_{C}=\sum_{a\in A_{\mathrm{wait}}}(l_{a}+u_{a})+\sum_{i\in E_{\mathrm{arr}}}t_{i}^{\mathrm{br}}+\sum_{j\in E_{\mathrm{dep}}}t_{j}^{\mathrm{ac}}=L_{C^{H}}+U_{C^{H}}.

Moreover,

\min\{t_{a}^{\min}\mid a\in M\}=\min\{\min\{t_{i}^{\mathrm{br}}\mid i\in E_{\mathrm{arr}}\},\min\{t_{j}^{\mathrm{ac}}\mid j\in E_{\mathrm{dep}}\}\}=\min\{t_{a}^{\min}\mid a\in M^{H}\},

and we call this quantity
\vartheta
.
Since
C^{H}
is of maximum weight, we have
k\coloneqq L_{C^{H}}-L_{C}=\rho(C^{H})-\rho(C)\geq 0
, and therefore
U_{C^{H}}=L_{C}+U_{C}-L_{C^{H}}=U_{C}-k
, so that
[L_{C^{H}},U_{C^{H}}]=[L_{C}+k,U_{C}-k]=[L_{C},U_{C}]
. We derive that
\delta_{C^{H}}\leq\delta_{C}+k
and, by
Theorem

6.3
(b),

o(C^{H})=\rho(C^{H})-\min\{\vartheta,\delta_{C_{H}}\}\geq\rho(C)+k-\min\{\vartheta,\delta_{C}+k\}\geq\rho(C)-\min\{\vartheta,\delta_{C}\}=o(C),

so that
(M^{H},\pi^{H},x^{H},o^{H})
is optimal.
∎

By
Theorem

6.10
it follows that this variant of PESP-Energy can be solved to optimality in polynomial time.

8.2
At Least Two Cycles of Trains Induced by
M

Let us now consider the other case. We require every solution
S=(M,\pi,x,o)
to PESP-Energy to contain at least two cycles in the union of
M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
. The following lemma shows that PESP-Energy with this additional constraint on the solution structure is NP-complete on a one-station network.

Theorem 8.2
.

It is NP-complete to decide whether there is a solution
S=(M,\pi,x,o)
to PESP-Energy on a one-station network with a total overlap bigger or equal to a given
O\in\mathbb{R}

such that
M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
consists of at least two cycles.

Proof.

The proof works by reduction from the partition problem. For the partition problem we are given a set of natural numbers
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
. Now, we want to find a subset
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}
. Therefore, we construct a PESP-Energy instance on a one-station network of size
n
with
t^{\mathrm{ac}}=t^{\mathrm{br}}=1
and
l_{a_{i}}=u_{a_{i}}=c_{i}-1
for all
a_{i}\in A_{\mathrm{wait}}
. We choose the period time to be
T=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}
. There is a solution to the partition problem if and only if there is a solution to the defined two-cycle PESP-Energy instance with an overlap greater or equal to
n
. Given a solution
I
to the partition problem, we can choose an arbitrary matching
M
such that
M\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}A_{\mathrm{wait}}
contains a cycle
C_{I}
with exactly the waiting activities
a_{i}
for
i\in I
and a cycle
C_{\overline{I}}
containing exactly all the other waiting activities. Due to the choice of
T
, it is possible to achieve full overlap on
C_{I}
by choosing
x_{a}=1
for all energy activities
a\in A_{\mathrm{energy}}
because

\sum_{a\in C_{I}}x_{a}=\sum_{a_{i}\in C_{I}\cap A_{\mathrm{wait}}}(c_{i}-1)+\sum_{a\in C_{I}\cap A_{\mathrm{energy}}}1=\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}=T.

The same can be done with the cycle
C_{\overline{I}}
. Hence, we have found a solution to PESP-Energy with an overlap of at least
n
. On the other hand, we know that for each cycle
C
in a solution to PESP-Energy with overlap
n
we must have
\sum_{a\in C}x_{a}=kT
for a
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. As we are required to have at least two cycles, we have
k=1
. Hence, we have found a valid partition.
∎

9.2
Case Study 2: Berlin Ostkreuz with 14 Trains

We now turn to a realistic larger case study. The Berlin Ostkreuz station is a major hub for local and regional trains, and has been the station with the fourth-highest passenger volume in Germany in 2024
[
9
]
. During weekend nights, six suburban railway lines of S-Bahn Berlin stop at Ostkreuz, offering convenient passenger transfers between trains using the circle line (S41/S42, S8) and the main west-east corridor (S3, S5, S7, S75), see
Fig.

10
. These use a 750 V DC system
[
7
]
, meaning that synchronizing the braking and acceleration times can lead to significant energy savings. Although the timetable has certain symmetries with several trains meeting every 15 minutes, it is not a pure integrated fixed-interval timetable – which cannot be realized with the available infrastructure anyway, see
Fig.

9
.

Basic instance parameters.

Out of the six lines, five are operated with a period time of 30 minutes. Only the circle line S41/S42 runs every 15 minutes. For planning purposes, the S-Bahn Berlin timetable is computed with a resolution of 0.1 minutes, so that we consider a PESP-Passenger-Energy instance on a one-station network
\mathcal{E}_{14}
with 14 trains and a period time of
T=300
for the sake of working with integer timestamps. As bounds for the waiting activities, we set a uniform lower bound of 5 time units (0.5 minutes), and for each train, we round the waiting time of the annual timetable up to the next multiple of 5 for the upper bound.
Around Ostkreuz, the maximum allowed speed is 80 km/h. Rounding up the times found with an interactive calculator tool
[
12
]
, we set a uniform acceleration time of 0.4 minutes (
t^{\mathrm{ac}}=4)
, and a braking time of 0.5 minutes (
t^{\mathrm{br}}=5
). As an upper bound
W
for the possible brake-traction overlap, we obtain
W=14\cdot 4=56
time units (cf.
Proposition

6.1
). As all acceleration times are equal, we expect in view of
Proposition

7.2
a timetable that realizes an overlap of 56.

Railway timetabling requirements.

However, we need to deal with additional railway timetabling constraints: For the lines S41 and S42, we impose a strict headway of 15.0 minutes between two subsequent arrivals and departures, respectively. This can be dealt with by additional headway activities, see, e.g., Liebchen and Möhring
[
21
]
. Furthermore, we want to ensure that no track is occupied by two trains at the same time, and demand that a time of 1.7 minutes has to pass between a departure and the next arrival for any piece of infrastructure. Finally, while the four-track layout extends further west, the lines S5, S7, S75 need to share a standard double-track section towards the east, and we require every pair of two distinct trains to be at least 2.5 minutes apart here. These values are in accordance with the actual planning parameters at the infrastructure manager, and the track occupation constraints can be integrated into PESP using the methods of Masing et al.
[
25
]
. To incorporate the flexibility of the two platforms in the lower level, we use the IPESPC constraints introduced by Bortoletto et al.
[
5
]
.

Transfer weights.

Concerning the passenger flow, we introduce transfer activities with a minimum transfer time of 2.0 minutes only between the upper and the lower level, and between S3 and S5/S7/S75 from and to eastbound services. Other transfer relations can be realized at other stops in the network as well, and we will disregard them here. To obtain meaningful activity weights
w
in the absence of data, we proceed as follows. Starting from the overall passenger load on a weekday
[
33
]
, we obtain demands of 147, 187, 184, 163 thousand passengers (kpax) for the directions north, east, south, west, respectively. We stick to kpax as unit, being well aware that the demand in our after-midnight scenario is much lower, but still believing in similarly distributed passenger flows. As an adjustment, we assume that the demand splits 80:20 in favor of the outbound east to west direction, while we assume a 50:50 split for the circle line in the north and south directions. E.g, we assume that
187\cdot 80\,\%=149.6
kpax want to travel eastbound, and only
163\cdot 20\%=32.6
kpax westbound. Moreover, we suppose that the demand on the lines in the same direction is distributed as on their branching points in
[
33
]
. E.g., as further south of Ostkreuz, 57 kpax passengers use the direction of Grünau (S8), and 124 kpax use the circle line (S41/S42) we derive that a share of
\frac{57}{57+124}\approx 31.5\,\%
of the total southbound demand is attributed to line S8. For simplicity, we assume that the transfers from or to a specific direction of a line are distributed in the same way, e.g., from all the passengers that transfer from eastbound line S5,
31.5\%
will choose southbound S8. As it is unrealistic to assume that all passengers change, we require that 50% of passengers in each train will stay on board. Lastly, to ensure feasibility, but also noting that the Ostkreuz area is a popular nightlife spot, we allow passengers to enter the system here. All these requirements can be modeled in a straightforward manner as a network-flow-type linear program. To obtain somewhat balanced activity weights, we choose to maximize the minimum number of passengers on a transfer activity. The linear program can be solved instantly with an optimal value of approximately 0.4 kpax. Out of the 347.7 total kpax, only 14.4 kpax enter at Ostkreuz. The highest weight of 7.2 kpax is associated to the transfer from eastbound S5, the strongest west-east line, to each of the two trains of southbound S41.

Computational setup.

Having set up the details of the PESP-Passenger-Energy Ostkreuz instance, we solve the program (
1
)-(
13
) with an
\varepsilon
-constraint method. We use Gurobi 12
[
15
]
as a MIP solver on a high-throughput cluster with Intel Xeon Gold 6342 CPUs and 512 GB RAM, allowing Gurobi to run exclusively and in parallel on 32 threads. To further accelerate the solution process, we invoke Gurobi with best bound emphasis and aggressive cut generation, and additionally separate flip inequalities as user cuts (cf. Lindner and Masing
[
23
]
).

Results.

It turns out that, also with all additional constraints, there is indeed a feasible timetable that realizes the maximum possible overlap of 56 time units. For this overlap, the minimum total travel time of the passengers is 25287.14 (see
Fig.

12(a)
), while neglecting energy considerations brings down the travel time to 21475.17 (see
Fig.

12(b)
), which is approximately 15 % less. For comparison, the timetable in operation (cf.
Fig.

10
) offers a travel time of 29840.55 with an overlap of 3, but is of course not targeted towards our choice of activity weights, and reflects constraints coming from other parts of the network. The full Pareto front is depicted in
Fig.

11
, along with computation times. The Pareto-efficient points are arranged in groups of four, which can be explained by the relatively short acceleration/braking times: Shifting the overlap on an energy arc by up to 4 time units still seems to allow to maintain the transfers, so that the picture is governed by the number of energy arcs with positive overlap rather than the actual value. For example, moving from overlap 52 to 53 means to have 14 energy arcs with positive overlap instead of 13, and causes a travel time increase of 1164.48, while going from overlap 53 to 54 requires only 30.39 more travel time. On the other hand, following the Pareto principle, significant overlaps can already be obtained with relatively small increases in travel time. E.g., 50 % of the maximum possible overlap need less than 4 % more travel time. Concerning computation times, the computational effort for the travel time minimization with an overlap of 32 or less is very manageable with wall times less than 1 hour, and even less than 10 minutes for an overlap less than 20. This changes dramatically with higher overlaps, the longest time needed being almost 17 hours to compute an optimal timetable with an overlap of 42.

Conclusion and Outlook

We have introduced the PESP-Passenger-Energy problem that integrates the maximization of the brake-traction overlap time into the context of periodic railway timetabling by means of the Periodic Event Scheduling Problem. Apart from giving a MIP formulation, we characterized the structure of optimal solutions for both single objective problems on a one-station network, and investigated the complexity landscape. On such networks, we obtained a polynomial-time algorithm with an additive performance guarantee for the PESP-Energy problem, and exact polynomial-time algorithms in several special cases. Finally, we have presented two case studies. With our model, the computation of the Pareto front has been feasible for a real-world 14 train instance. Besides closing the remaining gap in the complexity status, the next step is to apply our model to network-scale instances. An integration with the task of finding timetables that allow energy-efficient driving is also a topic for future research.
