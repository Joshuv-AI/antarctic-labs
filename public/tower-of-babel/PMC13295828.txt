Prognostic value of baseline clinical features in SLE: a cluster analysis
Authors: Betül Dikkanoğlu Demirok, Sibel Balci, Andaç Komaç, Duygu Temiz Karadağ, Ayşe Çefle, Ayten Yazici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSLE is a heterogeneous systemic autoimmune disease with diverse clinical manifestations. We aimed to identify clinical subgroups of SLE patients using cluster analysis at diagnosis and at the last follow-up and to evaluate their prognostic implications for disease activity, organ damage and mortality.MethodsIn this single-centre retrospective cohort study, 199 patients with SLE who fulfilled the 1997 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria and were regularly followed at the Rheumatology Clinic of Kocaeli University Hospital were included. Patients were divided into clusters based on their clinical involvement at diagnosis and the last visit using the Gower distance and the Partitioning Around Medoids algorithm. Differences between clusters were assessed using one-way analysis of variance, Kruskal-Wallis and χ2 analyses. Analyses were performed using SPSS V.29.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA) and R V.4.3.0.ResultsAt diagnosis, three clusters were identified: Cluster 1 (n=108) with predominant arthritis; Cluster 2 (n=49) with renal and haematological involvement and highest disease activity (SLE Disease Activity Index 2000, p=0.004); Cluster 3 (n=42) with mucocutaneous manifestations, often accompanied by haematological involvement. After a median follow-up of 168 months, four clusters were identified. Cluster 4′, a heterogeneous group with mucocutaneous, articular and renal involvement, had significantly higher Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC)/ACR Damage Index (SDI) scores (median 4.0 (0–8), p<0.001), indicating poor prognosis. During follow-up, 29.6% of Cluster 1, 22.4% of Cluster 2 and 42.9% of Cluster 3 transitioned to Cluster 4′. Patients with mucocutaneous-predominant disease at diagnosis had a twofold increased risk of transitioning to the poor-prognosis cluster (OR 2.05, 95% CI 1.01 to 4.16, p=0.046). Mortality did not differ significantly between clusters.ConclusionCluster analysis based on clinical involvement in SLE can identify homogeneous patient subgroups and predict long-term outcomes. Patients with mucocutaneous-predominant onset, often receiving less intensive treatment, may have a worse prognosis, highlighting the importance of individualised monitoring and management strategies.

Introduction

SLE is a heterogeneous systemic autoimmune disease that can affect many organs. Although its aetiopathogenesis is not fully understood, it predominantly affects women of childbearing age and is characterised by a relapsing-remitting clinical course.1 2 SLE is associated with autoantibody production and immune complex deposition, leading to multiorgan involvement; however, it remains difficult to predict which organs will be affected in individual patients.3 Due to the influence of multiple genetic and environmental factors, SLE exhibits a wide spectrum of clinical phenotypes, and treatment strategies should therefore be individualised according to disease manifestations and severity.46 If inflammation cannot be adequately controlled and disease activity persists, irreversible organ damage may develop, resulting in increased morbidity and mortality.7 8 Previous studies have demonstrated that cumulative organ damage is a strong predictor of future mortality in patients with SLE.9

Cluster analysis refers to an unsupervised statistical approach designed to identify homogeneous subgroups within heterogeneous populations based on measures of similarity and distance, without predefined classes. By grouping individuals with similar characteristics, this method enables the identification of distinct clinical phenotypes or subgroups that may be associated with prognosis and long-term outcomes.1012

SLE is a dynamic disease in which clinical manifestations may emerge, regress or evolve over time, leading to changes in disease phenotype. Therefore, in a multifaceted and unpredictable condition such as SLE, clustering approaches provide a valuable framework for identifying disease subgroups and exploring potential relationships between clinical patterns and outcomes.1315 Several studies have identified SLE subgroups using cluster analyses based on disease activity indices, such as the SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K), and damage indices, such as the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC)/American College of Rheumatology (ACR) Damage Index (SDI), incorporating clinical and laboratory features. These studies have highlighted the potential value of such subtypes in guiding disease management and treatment decisions.1422 However, relatively few studies have performed cluster analyses based primarily on clinical involvement, independent of immunological factors.14 15 23 24 In the present study, we performed cluster analysis using clinical involvement features derived from the ACR classification criteria at the time of diagnosis and at the last follow-up visit in patients with SLE, with the aim of evaluating disease evolution over time and assessing the impact of initial organ involvement on prognosis, disease activity, organ damage and mortality.

Methods

Study design and patients

This single-centre retrospective study included 199 patients with SLE who were regularly followed up in the rheumatology clinic between January 2004 and January 2025 and who fulfilled the 1997 ACR classification criteria for SLE.25 Patients were excluded if they did not fulfil the 1997 ACR classification criteria, had insufficient follow-up data or had incomplete clinical records. Between October 2024 and March 2025, clinical and laboratory data at the time of diagnosis, cumulative clinical and laboratory findings up to the last follow-up visit, and treatment modalities were retrospectively collected from outpatient medical records and the hospital information system. Cluster analysis was performed twice based on clinical involvement defined by the ACR classification criteria: first, according to clinical involvement at the time of diagnosis and second, according to clinical involvement at the last follow-up visit.

Definitions

Disease activity was assessed using the SLEDAI-2K, calculated both at the time of diagnosis and at the last follow-up visit. SLEDAI-2K activity levels were categorised as follows: no activity (SLEDAI=0), mild activity (SLEDAI 1–5), moderate activity (SLEDAI 6–10), high activity (SLEDAI 11–19) and very high activity (SLEDAI ≥20).26 Changes in disease activity were defined as follows: an increase of more than three points indicated disease exacerbation, a decrease of more than three points indicated improvement, a change of 1–3 points indicated persistently active disease, and a score of 0 indicated remission.27 28 Organ damage was evaluated using the SDI.29 The SDI was calculated at the last follow-up visit to assess cumulative organ damage. As defined in the original SLICC/ACR Damage Index, only irreversible damage present for at least 6 months was considered.

Statistical analysis

A total of 199 patients with SLE were clustered based on clinical involvement parameters assessed at diagnosis and at the last follow-up visit. Similarity between patients was quantified using the Gower distance metric,30 which is appropriate for binary clinical variables. Clustering was performed using the Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM) algorithm,31 a robust clustering method that is less sensitive to outliers than K-means clustering. The optimal number of clusters was evaluated using silhouette width, a distance-based measure of clustering quality.32 Clustering analyses were conducted in R V.4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the cluster package. The ACR clinical classification criteria were used as input variables for the clustering procedure.

All other statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.29.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Continuous variables were summarised as mean±SD or as median with IQR, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test or one-way analysis of variance for normally distributed variables and the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. Dunn’s test was applied for post-hoc multiple comparisons. Associations between continuous variables were evaluated using Spearman’s correlation analysis, while associations between categorical variables were examined using the χ2 test with Bonferroni correction. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to calculate the OR. Cox proportional hazards regression was used for multivariate analyses. Survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and comparisons between groups were performed using the log-rank test. A two-tailed p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 199 patients with SLE were included in the study, of whom 174 were women and 25 were men. The mean age at symptom onset was 33.90±13.17 years, and the mean age at diagnosis was 35.52±13.34 years. Arthritis (56.3%) was the most frequent clinical involvement in patients, followed by haematological involvement (42.7%), malar rash (36.7%) and photosensitivity (33.2%). Anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies were detected in 67.8% of patients, anti-Sm antibodies in 12.1%, antiphospholipid antibodies in 21.6% and hypocomplementaemia in 65.8% (table 1).

Based on clinical involvement at the time of diagnosis, cluster analysis using the Gower distance matrix identified three distinct subgroups. Cluster 1 (n=108) was the largest subgroup and was predominantly characterised by arthritis (87.0%, p<0.001), while haematological involvement was the least frequent manifestation in this cluster (p<0.001). Cluster 2 (n=49) was mainly characterised by renal involvement (p<0.001), with haematological involvement also being a dominant feature (p<0.001). This cluster exhibited the highest baseline SLEDAI-2K scores and represented the most active disease subgroup (p=0.004). Cluster 3 (n=42) was predominantly characterised by mucocutaneous involvement, including malar rash, photosensitivity and discoid rash (p<0.001), with haematological involvement also being frequent (p<0.001). No significant differences were observed among clusters with respect to age at diagnosis; however, patients in Cluster 3 tended to be diagnosed at a later age (mean ages for Clusters 1, 2 and 3: 35, 34 and 38 years, respectively) (table 1).

After a median follow-up duration of 168 (48–516) months, reanalysis based on cumulative clinical involvement identified four distinct clusters. In Cluster 1′, arthritis was present in all patients, while no renal involvement was observed (p<0.001). Renal involvement was significantly more frequent in Cluster 2′ (p<0.001), and this cluster had a higher proportion of male patients compared with the other clusters (24.5%, p=0.015). Cluster 3′ was characterised predominantly by mucocutaneous involvement, including malar rash, discoid rash and photosensitivity (p<0.001, p=0.003 and p<0.001, respectively), with no patients exhibiting renal involvement or arthritis. Cluster 4′ demonstrated a heterogeneous pattern, including malar rash, photosensitivity, arthritis and renal involvement (p<0.001). The SDI was significantly higher in Cluster 4′ (median 4.00 (0–8), p<0.001), identifying this subgroup as having the poorest prognosis. No significant differences were observed among clusters regarding haematological, neurological or immunological involvement based on cumulative findings (p=0.605, p=0.761 and p=0.224, respectively) (table 2).

Treatment patterns differed significantly among clusters. Rituximab, cyclophosphamide and mycophenolate mofetil were more frequently used in Clusters 2′ and 4′ (p<0.001). In contrast, patients in Cluster 3′ received rituximab, corticosteroids and azathioprine less frequently than those in other clusters (p<0.001, p=0.046 and p=0.043, respectively) (table 2).

During follow-up, 49.1% of patients in Cluster 1, 67.3% in Cluster 2 and 52.4% in Cluster 3 remained within their initial clusters. Progression to Cluster 4′, the subgroup associated with poor prognosis, occurred in 29.6% of patients from Cluster 1, 22.4% from Cluster 2 and 42.9% from Cluster 3. Patients in Cluster 3, characterised by predominant mucocutaneous involvement at diagnosis, had a twofold increased risk of progression to Cluster 4′ compared with patients in the other clusters (OR 2.05, 95% CI 1.01 to 4.16, p=0.046) (figure 1).

The mean survival time of the study population was 39.74±0.95 years, with no significant differences observed between clusters. Patients who died had significantly higher SDI scores compared with survivors (p=0.002). Mortality rates did not differ significantly between clusters (p=0.813); however, mortality was 7.23 times higher in women than in men (p=0.012).

Correlation analysis based on cumulative clinical involvement demonstrated a positive correlation between renal involvement and malar rash as well as serositis (r=0.18, p<0.01 and r=0.18, p<0.05, respectively). In contrast, renal involvement was negatively correlated with discoid rash and photosensitivity (r=–0.16, p<0.05 for both) (figure 2).

Discussion

Cluster analysis, which groups objects with high similarity and identifies homogeneous clusters, has recently become popular in many diseases and, naturally, in SLE, which has a heterogeneous clinical presentation. In this study, cluster analysis was performed using clinical involvement features derived from the ACR classification criteria at the time of diagnosis and at the last visit in patients with SLE, and the effect of initial clinical involvement on prognosis over time was investigated. As a result of the analysis, it was observed that the cluster with predominantly mucocutaneous involvement at the beginning had a two times higher probability of transitioning to a heterogeneous cluster with a poor prognosis in follow-ups compared with other clusters. At the same time, a correlation was found between renal involvement and malar rash and serositis; the presence of discoid rash and photosensitivity was found to be protective against renal involvement.

Cluster analysis has been used in previous studies on patients with SLE with varying clinical manifestations. In the first large-scale, multicentre study, Jacobsen et al (n=513), three clinical clusters were defined based on the initial clinical findings of patients with SLE using the K-means cluster analysis method. These were those with discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE) findings without malar rash and nephropathy, the renal-dominant cluster and the malar rash and photosensitivity-dominant cluster. It was shown that the cluster with renal involvement had higher rates of renal complications; proteinuria was found in 100% of the renal cluster, 33% in the DLE-dominant cluster and 34% in the malar rash and photosensitivity-dominant cluster (p<0.01); azotaemia in 41%, 20% and 13%, respectively (p<0.01); hypertension in 39%, 20% and 19%, respectively (p=0.0002) and myositis in 9%, 2% and 5%, respectively (p=0.007). Based on these findings, the authors concluded that the initial clinical phenotype may provide valuable information regarding disease course.23

In the study of 1082 patients by To et al, three clusters were defined in SLE according to cumulative final clinical involvement using K-means cluster analysis, and it was shown that these groups were prognostically distinct from each other. These clusters were defined as a mucocutaneous and articular involvement cluster, a renal and haematological involvement cluster, and a heterogeneous cluster. In these clusters, the standardised mortality rate was reported as 7.23 (6.7–7.7, p<0.001) in those with renal and haematological involvement; 1.27 (1.1–1.5, p=0.005) in the heterogeneous cluster and 0.95 (0.5–1.7, p=0.86) in the mucocutaneous and articular cluster defined as the benign cluster, and it was found that mortality was significantly higher in the cluster with renal and haematological involvement.24

More recently, Coit et al (n=724) identified three clinical clusters based on the 1997 ACR classification criteria using Gower’s distance method. Similar to the findings of To et al, a cluster characterised by predominant renal and haematological involvement was defined as a high-risk cluster. The other two clusters were characterised mainly by malar rash and photosensitivity and by oral ulcers (the benign cluster), respectively. The high-risk cluster demonstrated a 6.25-fold higher probability of developing renal involvement compared with the benign cluster and a 3.7-fold higher probability compared with the remaining cluster. Additionally, the risk of developing haematological involvement was 25-fold and 12.5-fold higher, respectively, compared with the other clusters. The authors emphasised that the presence of photosensitivity or oral ulcers appeared to be protective against renal, haematological and immunological involvement.33

In a multicentre French cohort study (n=440), clustering based on cumulative findings over a 10-year follow-up period was performed using K-means analysis with 29 clinical and laboratory parameters according to the 2012 SLICC classification criteria. Three distinct clusters were identified: a cluster with prominent antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS)-related features, a cluster dominated by arthritis and Sjögren’s syndrome and a heterogeneous cluster. Unlike other cluster studies, this analysis identified a cluster with distinct APS characteristics, suggesting that a significant proportion of patients with primary APS could be classified as having SLE under the 2012 SLICC criteria.15 34

In a recent cluster study published in 2025, baseline and final clinical involvement were analysed separately using the 2019 ACR criteria in a cohort of 278 patients. The stability of clusters over time and their relationship with prognosis were evaluated. It was reported that in Cluster 1, where 66.9% of patients clustered at the time of diagnosis, arthralgia (86.6%) and photosensitivity (51.6%) were frequent (p≤0.0001 compared with other groups) and after an average follow-up of 13±10 years, arthralgia was detected in 95.3% of patients and photosensitivity in 58.7% (Cluster 1′), generally stable and had the best survival rate. It was stated that the Cluster 2/2′ cluster, where renal involvement was significant, was the most severe cluster (renal involvement: 50% for Cluster 2, 62.2% for Cluster 2′; p<0.0001 compared with other groups) and was associated with a worse prognosis compared with others. When comparing Cluster 1′, which had the best prognosis, with the most severe group (Cluster 2′), mortality was observed to be 2.49 times higher, and in Cluster 3′ it was observed to be 1.84 times higher. With these results, it was emphasised that the clusters remained largely stable over time and that the initial clinical phenotypes may have lasting prognostic value.14

Le Tallec et al14 identified three distinct clinical clusters using hierarchical clustering with the Ward method based on 23 clinical manifestations derived from the 2019 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR)/ACR classification criteria. Similar to this longitudinal design, our study also performed cluster analysis by evaluating cumulative clinical findings at baseline and after a median follow-up of 14 years (IQR 4–43). The difference is that the findings were evaluated based on the 1997 ACR criteria, not the 2019 ACR criteria.14 The analysis revealed that three different clusters were separated based on baseline findings (Cluster 1 arthritis, Cluster 2 renal and haematological and Cluster 3 mucocutaneous). As in previous studies, the largest cluster was the one dominated by joint findings.14 15 24 In the post-follow-up cluster analysis, four different clusters were separated, including a heterogeneous cluster (Cluster 4′). Unlike the only study in the literature that examined baseline and post-follow-up findings, our study showed transitions between clusters. The percentage of those remaining stable was found to be 49.1% in Cluster 1, 67.3% in Cluster 2 and 52.4% in Cluster 3. Although no difference was found between the clusters in terms of mortality (3.6%, 7.5%, 3.4% and 6.6%, respectively; p=0.813), Cluster 4′ cluster was observed to have a worse damage index, and the rate of transition to this cluster was higher in Cluster 3. The transition rate to the Cluster 4′ subgroup was found to be 29.6% for Cluster 1, 22.4% for Cluster 2 and 42.9% for Cluster 3. Tallec et al used the EULAR/ACR criteria score due to its positive correlation with SDI and reported that clusters with renal involvement have a poor prognosis. In our study, the SDI score was used, and it was found to be significantly higher in the Cluster 4′ group, defined as a heterogeneous group, meaning it was the group with the worst prognosis. Patients in Cluster 3 were twice as likely to progress to a heterogeneous cluster with poor prognosis (Cluster 4′) compared with the other two clusters (OR 2.05, 95% CI 1.01 to 4.16, p=0.046). Furthermore, renal involvement was positively correlated with malar rash and serositis (r=0.18, p<0.01 and r=0.18, p<0.05, respectively), while discoid rash and photosensitivity showed negative correlations (r=−0.16, p<0.05 and r=−0.16, p<0.05, respectively). All these studies demonstrate that initial clinical phenotypes can be prognostically significant.

Our study found that the mucocutaneous cluster may have a poor prognosis over time, unlike what has been reported in the literature. This may be due to the less aggressive treatment given to patients with mucocutaneous SLE and the fact that they are not followed up as closely as patients with other involvements. Therefore, mucocutaneous predominant onset may not always be benign; the current findings suggest that patients with mucocutaneous predominance should also be monitored more carefully in the long term.

Mortality numbers were low in the clusters, and no significant difference was observed between them. SDI was significantly higher in patients who died (p=0.002). This finding supports the fact that damage accumulation is a strong predictor of mortality, as shown in the meta-analysis of Worstell et al. In this meta-analysis, it was shown that each unit increase in SDI score significantly increased mortality (HR 1.34, 95% CI 1.24 to 1.44, p<0.001; Q: p=0.027, I2=52.1%).35

Although different clustering methodologies have been used across SLE studies, including K-means, hierarchical clustering and PAM-based approaches, broadly similar clinical phenotypes have been reported. Jacobsen et al and To et al used K-means clustering; Coit et al applied Gower distance with PAM clustering, and Le Tallec et al performed hierarchical clustering using the Ward method. Despite methodological differences, these studies consistently identified severe organ-involving phenotypes as well as milder mucocutaneous or articular-predominant subgroups. In our study, we preferred the Gower distance combined with the PAM algorithm because the clustering variables consisted of binary clinical features derived from ACR classification criteria. Although alternative clustering methods may lead to some variation in cluster composition, the overall similarity of the main clinical phenotypes across studies suggests consistency between clustering approaches.

The retrospective design of this study represents a limitation and may have resulted in incomplete or inaccurate data. Additionally, the single-centre nature of the study and the relatively modest sample size may limit generalisability. On the other hand, cluster analysis was performed not only based on baseline but also on cumulative involvement at the last visit in a cohort with long-term follow-up. In this way, clinical changes over time could be monitored; prognosis could be predicted with SDI. We believe this is valuable in capturing the dynamic nature of SLE.

In conclusion, cluster analyses based on clinical involvement in SLE can provide long-term prognosis prediction. The fact that patients with a mucocutaneous phenotype, who likely receive less immunosuppressive treatment, have a worse long-term prognosis highlights the need for careful monitoring of this cluster. SLE is a heterogeneous disease, and personalised monitoring and treatment planning are essential. Early cluster-based risk stratification to identify homogeneous subgroups of SLE can allow for individualisation of both monitoring strategies and treatment intensity.
