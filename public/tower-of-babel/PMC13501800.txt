One- versus two-sample fecal immunochemical testing for colorectal cancer screening: a systematic review and meta-analysis of participation, diagnostic performance, and cost-effectiveness
Authors: Li Ye, Qiu Ling Hou, Haijun Peng, Qiu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhether collecting one or two fecal samples for immunochemical testing (FIT) optimizes colorectal cancer screening outcomes remains unclear.MethodsWe systematically searched multiple databases until October 2025 for studies directly comparing 1-FIT and 2-FIT strategies. Pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for dichotomous outcomes.ResultsFive studies (n = 45,888) and three economic evaluations were included. Participation was higher with 1-FIT (RR = 1.05, P < 0.0001), while 2-FIT had a higher positivity rate (RR = 0.65, P < 0.0001). Although 2-FIT detected more CRC (RR = 0.62, P = 0.02), advanced neoplasia and advanced adenoma detection were similar. The 2-FIT strategy had higher sensitivity (RR = 0.64, P = 0.04) but lower specificity (RR = 1.70, P < 0.001) than 1-FIT. A pivotal finding was that the 1-FIT strategy demonstrated a superior positive predictive value (PPV) for both advanced neoplasia (RR = 1.21, P = 0.004) and advanced adenoma (RR = 1.41, P < 0.0001), which translated into a substantially lower demand for colonoscopy (RR = 0.64, P < 0.0001) Economic evaluations consistently favored 1-FIT across different healthcare systems.ConclusionAlthough the 2-FIT strategy improves test sensitivity, it fails to enhance advanced neoplasia detection while substantially increasing colonoscopy demand due to its reduced positive predictive value. In contrast, the 1-FIT strategy preserves screening efficacy with superior participation rates, resource utilization, and cost-effectiveness. We recommend 1-FIT as the preferred strategy in settings with constrained colonoscopy resources, while reserving 2-FIT for resource-abundant environments where maximizing sensitivity justifies the additional economic and operational costs (Scheme 1).Trial registrationThe research protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO registry, bearing the registration identifier CRD420251153202.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04954-8.Keywords: Fecal immunochemical test, Colorectal cancer screening, Cost-effectiveness, Diagnostic accuracy

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents a major global public health challenge [1–3]. According to the 2022 GLOBOCAN estimates [4], CRC ranks third in incidence among all malignancies, with annual mortality exceeding 930,000 cases worldwide. A particularly concerning trend is the annual increase of approximately 3% in the age-standardized incidence rate among adults aged 40–49 years, underscoring the need for preventive strategies targeting younger populations [5, 6] Fortunately, more than 80% of CRCs develop through a well-characterized adenoma-carcinoma sequence, typically progressing over 10–15 years from detectable precancerous lesions to invasive cancer [7, 8]. This natural history provides a critical window of opportunity for effective screening interventions [9]. Organized screening of average-risk individuals can effectively identify precancerous lesions and early-stage cancers, thereby reducing both CRC incidence and mortality [10, 11].

Among various screening modalities, the fecal immunochemical test (FIT) has gained widespread adoption as the primary screening tool in many organized programs worldwide since approximately 2015, replacing traditional guaiac-based fecal occult blood tests (gFOBT) [12, 13]. This transition is largely attributable to FIT’s operational simplicity, absence of dietary restrictions, and favorable diagnostic accuracy [14, 15].

Most current FIT-based screening programs utilize a single fecal sample (1-FIT) per screening round. However, given the potential for intermittent bleeding from advanced colorectal neoplasia, it has been hypothesized that collecting two fecal samples (2-FIT), typically using an “any-positive” referral rule for colonoscopy, might reduce false-negative results and improve lesion detection rates [16]. Several independent studies have demonstrated that the 2-FIT strategy increases test sensitivity, though this advantage is accompanied by a higher positivity rate and a corresponding decrease in positive predictive value (PPV) [17, 18]. This trade-off has significant implications for colonoscopy resource allocation, participant experience, and the overall cost-effectiveness of screening programs.

To date, no systematic review has comprehensively synthesized the evidence comparing 1-FIT and 2-FIT strategies across multiple dimensions, including participation rate, diagnostic performance, and economic outcomes. To address this evidence gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to compare 1-FIT and 2-FIT strategies across participation, diagnostic accuracy, and cost-effectiveness, providing evidence-based guidance for policymakers and clinicians.

Methods

This meta-analytic investigation was conducted in strict adherence to the methodological standards delineated within the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework [19].

Search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from their inception until October 2025. The search strategy was developed in collaboration with an experienced medical librarian and incorporated both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms pertaining to “fecal immunochemical test,” “single sample,” “two samples,” and “colorectal cancer screening.” Prior to implementation, the search syntax was pilot-tested and optimized. Two independent reviewers (Ye and Hou) conducted the literature search and screening process. The complete search strategy is detailed in Supplementary Table S1. To ensure comprehensive coverage, we manually examined the reference lists of all included studies to identify any additional relevant publications not captured through the electronic search. Any discrepancies during study selection were resolved through consultation with a third independent reviewer (Peng). The study selection process is summarized in Fig. 1.

A PRISMA flow chart of study selection

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria encompassed randomized controlled trials, prospective cohort studies, or retrospective studies that directly compared single-sample and two-sample FIT strategies in asymptomatic, average-risk adults undergoing CRC screening, and which reported at least one relevant outcome such as participation rate, positivity rate, positive predictive value for advanced neoplasia, advanced adenoma, or colorectal cancer, lesion detection rate, or cost-effectiveness measures. Conversely, exclusion criteria involved studies focusing on high-risk populations, employing non-prospective designs, reporting only cancer detection rates without data on advanced neoplasia or participation, or being abstracts, conference proceedings, reviews, or animal studies.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two investigators independently performed study selection, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment. Extracted data included: first author, publication year, study design, population characteristics, sample size, FIT brand and cutoff values, and all relevant outcome measures described above in Table 1.

The risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (ROB 2.0) for RCTs [20] and the Risk of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool for non-randomized studies [21]. The analysis revealed that all studies exhibited low risk of bias, thereby rendering the study results highly credible (Supplementary Table S2,3).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software. For dichotomous outcomes (e.g., participation rate, positivity rate, detection rate), risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated as effect measures. RRs were also used to compare differences in PPV, sensitivity, and specificity between the two strategies. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic. A random-effects model was applied when I² > 50%; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Of the eight included studies, five were comparative clinical studies eligible for meta-analysis, and three were economic evaluations analyzed using a narrative synthesis approach. All FIT thresholds were converted to the recommended unit of µg hemoglobin per gram of feces (µg Hb/g feces) using the conversion 1 ng/mL ≈ 0.2 µg/g. For qualitative FITs (e.g., InSure®), positivity was defined as the presence of hemoglobin in any sample.

Results

Search outcomes

Our comprehensive search strategy identified 368 unique publications. Following abstract screening, 121 titles were selected for comprehensive review of the full texts. Subsequently, 8 studies that satisfied the inclusion criteria were incorporated into the ultimate analysis. A flowchart delineating the literature search and selection process is provided (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of studies

Five primary studies, comprising a total of 45,888 invitees, were included in the meta-analysis [18, 22–25]. The key characteristics of these studies are summarized in Table 1. All studies directly compared the 1-FIT and 2-FIT strategies within asymptomatic, average-risk adult populations. The overall risk of bias was assessed as acceptable, with most studies rated as having low to moderate risk. Three additional economic evaluations were included for narrative synthesis [26–28] (Table 2).

Meta-analysis results

Participation rate

Three studies reported participation rates [18, 22, 25]. The pooled analysis demonstrated that the participation rate for the 1-FIT strategy was significantly higher than that for the 2-FIT strategy (RR: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.029–1.064, P < 0.0001), with low heterogeneity among studies (I² = 27%) (Fig. 2a).

Forest plots showing individual study and summary estimates for the comparison between the single-sample FIT (1-FIT) and the two-sample FIT (2-FIT) strategies. a Risk Ratio (RR) and 95% Confidence Interval (CI) for participation rate. b RR and 95% CI for FIT positivity rate

FIT positivity rate

Two studies reported FIT positivity rates [18, 22]. The 2-FIT strategy yielded a significantly higher positivity rate than the 1-FIT strategy (RR: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.597–0.712, P < 0.0001), indicating a 53% relative increase in positivity rate. No heterogeneity was observed (I² = 0%) (Fig. 2b).

Lesion detection rate

Advanced Neoplasia (AN)

No significant difference was observed between the two strategies (RR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.801–1.092, P = 0.40), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Fig. 3a).

Comparison of lesion detection rates. Forest plots showing individual study and summary estimates for the comparison between the 1-FIT and 2-FIT strategies. a RR and 95% CI for Advanced Neoplasia (AN) Detection Rate. b RR and 95% CI for Advanced Adenoma (Aa) Detection Rate. c RR and 95% CI for Colorectal Cancer (CRC) Detection Rate

Advanced Adenoma (AA)

Similarly, the detection rates for advanced adenoma were comparable between groups (RR = 1.005, 95% CI: 0.847–1.193; P = 0.952), with low heterogeneity (I² = 7%) (Fig. 3b).

Colorectal Cancer (CRC)

A statistically significant difference was observed, favoring the 2-FIT strategy (RR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.413–0.938, P = 0.02), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Fig. 3c).

Positive Predictive Value (PPV)

For Advanced Neoplasia (AN)

The PPV for AN was significantly higher in the 1-FIT group than in the 2-FIT group (RR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.062–1.380, P = 0.004), though heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 70%) (Fig. 4a). This heterogeneity may be attributable to variations in FIT thresholds and population characteristics across studies. A sensitivity analysis excluding one outlier study confirmed the robustness of this finding (RR: 1.36, 95% CI: 1.166–1.592, P < 0.0001; I² = 0%) (Supplementary Figure S1).

For Advanced Adenoma (AA)

The PPV for Aa was significantly higher with the 1-FIT strategy (RR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.210–1.651, P < 0.0001), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Fig. 4b).

For Colorectal Cancer (CRC)

No significant difference in PPV for CRC was detected between the strategies (RR: 0.90, 95% CI: 0.606–1.347, P = 0.62), and heterogeneity was absent (I² = 0%) (Fig. 4c).

Diagnostic performance (sensitivity & specificity)

Sensitivity

The 2-FIT strategy exhibited significantly higher sensitivity than the 1-FIT strategy (RR: 0.64, 95% CI: 0.42–0.98, P = 0.04), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Fig. 5a).

Comparison of Colonoscopy Compliance and Diagnostic Performance. Forest plots showing individual study and summary estimates for the comparison between the 1-FIT and 2-FIT strategies. a RR and 95% CI for Colonoscopy Screening Completion Rate. b RR and 95% CI for Test Sensitivity. c RR and 95% CI for Test Specificity

Specificity

The 1-FIT strategy demonstrated significantly higher specificity (RR: 1.70, 95% CI: 1.39–2.08, P < 0.001), albeit with high heterogeneity (I² = 71%), potentially due to differences in the definition of a positive test between strategies and across studies (Fig. 5b).

Colonoscopy referral

A significant difference was observed in colonoscopy referral rates between the two strategies, with the 2-FIT strategy leading to a higher referral rate (RR: 0.64, 95% CI: 0.584–0.701, P < 0.0001). No heterogeneity was detected (I² = 0%) (Fig. 5c).

Sensitivity analysis (restricted to first screening round)

A sensitivity analysis was performed by restricting the data to the first screening round of all studies (including only the first round from Schreuders et al., [18]) (Supplementary Figure S2-S10). The results revealed important differences from the primary analysis. Most notably, the statistically significant advantage of the 2-FIT strategy in colorectal cancer detection observed in the primary analysis was no longer present (RR: 0.76, 95% CI: 0.468–1.231, P = 0.26) (Supplementary Figure S6). Additionally, the magnitude of difference in the FIT positivity rate between strategies was attenuated, though the direction of effect remained consistent.

Crucially, the absence of significant differences in advanced neoplasia and advanced adenoma detection rates between the two strategies remained unchanged in this sensitivity analysis. Similarly, the superior positive predictive value of the 1-FIT strategy for advanced neoplasia and advanced adenoma persisted. These findings suggest that the inclusion of subsequent screening rounds in the primary analysis, particularly the enhanced sensitivity of 2-FIT for detecting intermittently bleeding cancers over multiple rounds, contributed to the observed CRC detection advantage. However, the core conclusions regarding the detection of clinically significant precancerous lesions and screening efficiency remain robust when considering only initial screening participation.

Economic evaluation

Three economic evaluations from the Netherlands, the United States, and China were included [26–28], each employing microsimulation or decision-analytic models to systematically compare the cost-effectiveness of 1-FIT versus 2-FIT strategies within their respective healthcare contexts (Table 2). These studies were synthesized narratively due to heterogeneity in model structures and outcome measures.

A model based on the Dutch healthcare system indicated that while the 2-FIT strategy (using a “one-positive” criterion) yielded additional life-years gained (LYG) in a biennial screening program for individuals aged 55–75, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) ranged from €4,024 to €8,041 per LYG—within a generally acceptable range [26]. However, a critical insight emerged when all potential screening strategies (varying start/stop ages and intervals) were considered: increasing the intensity of 1-FIT screening (e.g., by shortening intervals or expanding the age range) produced greater health gains than switching to a two-sample approach per round. Thus, allocating limited resources to enhance 1-FIT frequency is a more cost-effective means of improving outcomes than investing in two-sample collection.

A U.S.-based study [27], focusing on a single screening round within an integrated health system, found that when differential participation rates were accounted for, the 1-FIT strategy was more cost-effective across most willingness-to-pay thresholds. Cost-effectiveness acceptability curve analysis revealed that below approximately $28,000 per detected AN, the 1-FIT strategy (particularly at a 50 ng/mL cut off) had the highest probability of being optimal. Only above this threshold did the 2-FIT strategy (50 ng/mL) become a potential option, though without a clear advantage, underscoring the economic preference for 1-FIT under realistic budget constraints.

A study from Eastern China [28], using real-world data, evaluated multiple strategies including FIT combined with questionnaires. Although annual two-sample FIT with a questionnaire yielded the greatest health benefits (e.g., greater reductions in CRC incidence and mortality, more QALYs gained), the authors emphasized that all strategies—including various 1-FIT approaches—had cost-effectiveness ratios well below World Bank thresholds for middle-income countries. This implies that in resource-limited settings, adopting a 1-FIT strategy—which offers slightly lower health gains but substantially lower costs—can achieve very high cost-effectiveness, enabling broader population coverage under fixed public health budgets and aligning with efficiency-oriented priorities.

In summary, these economic evaluations, despite differing settings and perspectives, consistently conclude that the 1-FIT strategy achieves comparable or superior health outcomes at a significantly lower cost than the 2-FIT strategy. The marginal health benefit offered by the 2-FIT approach comes at a disproportionately high economic cost, rendering it difficult to justify under typical budget constraints. From a health economics standpoint, prioritizing and optimizing a single-sample FIT strategy represents a more efficient allocation of healthcare resources.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide the first comprehensive global assessment of the multifaceted impact of 1-FIT versus 2-FIT strategies on participation rates, diagnostic performance, and cost-effectiveness in CRC screening. Our findings demonstrate that in an average-risk population, while the 2-FIT strategy offers higher detection sensitivity due to increased sampling, manifested as a significantly higher positivity rate, this theoretical advantage does not translate into a substantial improvement in the detection rate of AN. More critically, the 2-FIT strategy is accompanied by significantly reduced specificity, leading to a lower PPV and serving as the primary driver for a 56% increase in colonoscopy referral requirements. In contrast, the 1-FIT strategy achieves higher screening participation rates and superior PPV while maintaining AN detection rates comparable to the 2-FIT strategy, and demonstrates better cost-effectiveness. These conclusions provide important guidance for optimizing resource allocation in CRC screening programs globally.

Although AN combines CRC and AA, its inclusion as a composite endpoint is clinically relevant because it reflects the primary target of screening—detecting lesions with malignant potential. Therefore, we retained this outcome in our analysis.

Regarding participation rates, the 1-FIT strategy was significantly superior to the 2-FIT strategy. This result is highly consistent with the findings of Mosen et al., whose research directly indicated that switching from a single-sample to a double-sample protocol reduces participant compliance, primarily attributable to the increased complexity of the sample collection process [25]. The overall effectiveness of any screening program is highly dependent on population coverage [29]. A screening test with slightly higher theoretical sensitivity will see its public health value greatly diminished if its complexity hinders broad participation [30, 31]. Strategies such as mailed outreach [32] and patient navigation [33] have been proven to effectively enhance participation rates, but their effects may be offset by the cumbersome sampling requirements of the double-sample strategy. Therefore, the significant advantage of 1-FIT in participation rates provides strong initial support for its adoption as the preferred strategy, particularly in populations with limited healthcare resources or lower trust in the healthcare system [34]. This highlights the necessity of balancing the pursuit of technical perfection with the achievement of broad population coverage.

Furthermore, regarding diagnostic performance, our study moves beyond simply comparing lesion detection rates to reveal essential differences between the two strategies. Although the 2-FIT strategy has a higher positivity rate due to the collection of more samples, this does not translate into a significant increase in the detection of AN, which holds clear public health significance. This phenomenon resonates with the findings of Auge et al. [30], whose research showed that the additional detection value from the second sample largely comes from low-risk adenomas rather than AN. This directly leads to a significantly lower PPV for the 2-FIT strategy [24, 35]. In other words, the 2-FIT strategy increases the number of colonoscopies without proportionally identifying more crucial precancerous lesions. This finding aligns with the conclusions of van der Selby et al. regarding the variability in FIT performance [36].

In contrast, the 1-FIT strategy demonstrates higher screening efficiency. Its higher PPV for advanced neoplasia and high-grade adenomas means that for every colonoscopy performed due to a positive FIT result, the probability of finding a clinically significant lesion is greater. This not only improves the cost-effectiveness of screening but also reduces unnecessary invasive examinations and associated risks for healthy individuals, thereby optimizing the utilization of colonoscopy, a scarce resource. This is particularly crucial for systems with constrained colonoscopy resources [37]. Our results are consistent with the conclusions of Chiu et al. and Moosavi et al., namely that single-day or single-sample strategies can utilize colonoscopy resources more efficiently while maintaining a reasonable AN detection rate [38, 39].

It must be acknowledged that the 2-FIT strategy holds a statistical advantage in the CRC detection rate, reflecting its higher sensitivity for intermittently bleeding cancers. However, the clinical significance of this advantage requires prudent evaluation. Firstly, the effect size is relatively small; secondly, and more critically, this advantage does not extend to the more preventable precancerous stage. The marginal gain in CRC detection with 2-FIT must be weighed against its lower PPV and higher colonoscopy demand. From a population health perspective, 1-FIT offers a more efficient balance between accuracy and resource utilization.

Our findings raise a fundamental question: Is increasing the number of samples per screening round the optimal path to improving cancer sensitivity? Evidence from economic evaluations provides a negative answer. Studies represented by Goede et al. and Smith et al. show that, compared to adopting a 2-FIT strategy, maintaining a 1-FIT strategy but appropriately shortening the screening interval (e.g., changing from once every two years to annually) can prevent more life-years lost at the same cost, demonstrating superior cost-effectiveness [26, 27]. This strategy compensates for the sensitivity loss of a single test by increasing the screening frequency. In the long term, its preventive effect on interval cancers may be superior to increasing the number of samples at a single time point. This forms a complementary insight with the research of Haug et al. on the risks of extending screening intervals [40]. Therefore, for programs pursuing higher sensitivity, decision-makers should consider adjusting the screening frequency rather than increasing sample complexity. As suggested by Young & Symonds, program optimization should focus on the screening interval and cut-off value, rather than the number of samples [41]. Young et al. similarly demonstrated that adjusting both the number of samples and the positivity threshold can influence colonoscopy workload and detection rates, reinforcing the importance of balancing test performance with resource efficiency [42]. Furthermore, combining the modeling studies of Cenin et al. and Li et al. have shown that risk-stratified screening strategies may offer greater efficiency and benefits than a singular “increasing samples” strategy [28, 43].

Limitations and future research directions

The interpretation of our findings should be considered in light of several limitations. First, the modest number of primary studies available for meta-analysis may limit statistical power for certain endpoints. In particular, the large sample size of the Schreuders et al. study contributed substantially to the pooled estimates, potentially introducing bias. Second, heterogeneity in FIT assay brands and diagnostic thresholds across studies represents a potential source of variation in the pooled effect estimates. Third, the observed statistical heterogeneity in some analyses warrants cautious interpretation. Furthermore, the current evidence is largely confined to short-term screening outcomes, with limited long-term follow-up data on interval cancer incidence and disease-specific mortality. We also acknowledge that we did not examine more nuanced strategies for duplicate FIT testing, such as applying a higher threshold for the second sample, which might improve specificity while preserving sensitivity; this represents an important direction for future research.

Finally, one of the included major studies incorporated data from multiple screening rounds. In sensitivity analyses restricted to the first screening round, the results for certain outcomes differed notably from the primary analysis. Most notably, the significant advantage of the 2‑FIT strategy in detecting colorectal cancer was no longer statistically significant, although the direction of effect remained consistent, suggesting a potential cumulative benefit of 2‑FIT over subsequent rounds. This pattern indicates that the performance characteristics of the two strategies may evolve over the course of a screening program, reflecting a trade‑off between the short‑term efficiency of 1‑FIT and the potential long‑term cumulative sensitivity of 2‑FIT. These observations do not invalidate our primary findings but highlight the need for further studies with sufficient data from multiple rounds to confirm this hypothesis.

Despite these limitations, the principal conclusions regarding participation rates, positive predictive value, and resource utilization remained consistent. Future research should prioritize larger comparative studies, establish individual participant data meta‑analysis platforms to explore subgroup‑specific strategies, conduct long‑term follow‑up to assess ultimate clinical outcomes, and perform comprehensive economic evaluations across diverse healthcare settings to inform the development of precision screening approaches.

Furthermore, our analysis is grounded in the conventional use of FIT as a dichotomous (positive/negative) test, a simplification that discards the substantial risk-predictive information contained within the quantitative fecal hemoglobin (f-Hb) concentration. There is robust evidence that the magnitude of f-Hb is directly proportional to the risk of colorectal cancer and advanced neoplasia, often outweighing traditional demographic risk factors in multivariate models. This underscores the potential of more nuanced, risk-stratified screening pathways. A notable example is the Colofit model [44], which integrates f-Hb concentration with other clinical variables to calculate an individual’s cancer risk, enabling a more personalized approach to colonoscopy referral. Future research should explore how such risk-adaptive strategies might further optimize the balance between sensitivity and resource utilization, potentially superseding the simple 1-sample versus 2-sample debate.”

Conclusion

Based on the limited available evidence, the single-sample FIT strategy appears to be the more efficient approach for population-based colorectal cancer screening. While the two-sample method increases sensitivity and may enhance cancer detection over multiple rounds, it fails to improve the detection of advanced neoplasia—the primary goal of screening. This comes at the cost of significantly lower specificity, a poorer positive predictive value, and a substantially increased demand for colonoscopy. Balancing diagnostic performance, participant adherence, and resource utilization, we recommend the single-sample FIT as the preferred strategy for organized screening programs, particularly in settings with constrained resources. The two-sample strategy should be reserved for unique circumstances where maximizing long-term sensitivity is the paramount objective, and the requisite colonoscopy capacity is sustainably available (Scheme 1).
