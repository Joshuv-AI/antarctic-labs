Abortion—A brief description of the evolution of methods
Authors: Laura Slade, Andrea Deussen, Jodie Dodd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThroughout history, women across the world have undergone abortion. This is despite a variety of religious and legal oppositions that remain present in many countries today. Procedures for abortion have evolved considerably and now include safe and effective methods, and access has improved in many settings. There are however many individuals across the world living in environments with restricted access to safe methods because of legal, logistical and social barriers. We discuss a brief history of methods used to terminate pregnancy in the first and second trimesters of pregnancy.

Key messageMethods of abortion have evolved, increasing the availability, effectiveness and safety, while substantially reducing associated morbidity. Efforts across the world must continue to ensure that all women have safe access to this fundamental service.

ABORTION IN THE PRE‐MODERN ERA

In the premodern era, women ingested various herbs and substances with variable efficacy, almost all of which were associated with a range of adverse effects. 1 , 2 These procedures were sufficiently common that some of the substances used in Ancient Greece and Rome for contraception and abortion were so popular that the plants were driven to extinction. Silphium, one of these plants, was so important to the economy in the ancient Greek city of Cyrene that it's picture was imprinted on the coin. 3 Often these substances reported mostly in ancient Greek and Roman literature were toxic and caused a spectrum of both morbidity and mortality, which were reported or monitored poorly in early times. Reported adverse effects included vomiting, seizures and abdominal pains, with “successful” abortion occurring inconsistently and unpredictably as another effect. 4

Many different practices were advised in the premodern era for their apparent ability to initiate an abortion. These varied by local culture and geographic area, and included practices such as wearing a tight pelvic girdle, jumping from a height or lying on a heated coconut shell. 3 , 5 Early English medical texts from the 17th century report surgical procedures for abortion very infrequently. It is therefore commonly accepted that surgical methods were not developed until hundreds of years later. 6 Ancient Jewish texts mention a procedure for inserting the root of the mallow tree fashioned as a probe for treatment of “semen (that have) become lodged.” 5 The oldest visual representations of abortion are at the temples in Ankor Wat in Cambodia dating to ~800 AD. Here depictions of pregnant women being pounded on the abdomen by a mallet reflect a procedure referred to as “massage abortion” which was described throughout South Eastern Asia. 5 Despite their variable efficacy and relatively high mortality, drastic procedures such as these continued for many years.

There are few reports of punishment for abortion in early texts from the 16th and 17th Century. 4 In early English texts, the death penalty was only inflicted for someone who performed or assisted with an abortion, in settings where it was procured an abortion against the wishes of the woman's husband. 5 , 6 In Ancient Rome, the acceptance of abortion was designed to protect the rights of the father, rather than conferring any rights or concerns about the wellbeing of the pregnant woman. 4 By description, these procedures involved various medicinal substances and remedies, which were often prescribed to “restore the menses” or “relieve menstrual blockages,” terminology that was subsequently carried across many centuries. 6 These abortifacient products included substances such as Hellebore, sage and pennyroyal 3 , 6 that were readily available and unregulated, requiring no medical advice or supervision for procurement or use.

Between 1000 and 1500, there was a general societal acceptance towards abortion until “the quickening” across many countries in Europe and America which referred to the time when fetal movements could first be perceived, and is now recognized to occur between 18 and 20 weeks' gestation. 7 , 8 It was not until 1588 that Pope Marcellus II declared all abortions to be murder. However, this pronouncement was quickly reversed by his successor, Pope Paul IV, just 3 years later with the realization this ruling on the common but secretive practice was unenforceable. 4 Abortion continued to be practiced in the first half of pregnancy throughout most areas of the world without significant opposition until the early 19th century. 4 Seemingly, abortion was available but was not considered an issue by religious or community leaders of the time (Table 1). 4

THE NINETEENTH AND TWENTIETH CENTURIES

In 1861 the Offenses Against the Person Act was introduced in England which formally prohibited abortion with a punishment of life imprisonment for those who performed an abortion or tried to self‐abort a pregnancy. 9 Despite this, rates of abortion were noted to be increasing, with the rise most marked amongst married women. 6 This was mostly attributed to failed attempts at contraceptive practices. 10 This, along with changes in medical record keeping and reporting, led to several developments in the surgical procedures used to perform an abortion. In the mid‐1840s in France, the process of cervical dilatation and endometrial curettage (D&C) was developed, initially with high morbidity and mortality, largely secondary to infectious and hemorrhagic complications. 11 , 12 The procedure involved inserting a device into the uterus that was designed to “scrape” the uterine lining, 13 which was performed without anesthetic or aseptic technique. Deaths were reported from “hysterical tetanus” a description of peritonitis resulting from uterine perforation during this procedure. 14

The safety of the D&C procedure was somewhat improved during the 1870s when Alfred Hegar invented metal dilators of varying diameter to open the cervix. 11 The cervical dilators used today have changed very little and are still represented by his name. These were used to bluntly and progressively open the cervix so that the instrument used for removal of the products of conception could be safely inserted into the uterine cavity. This reduced the risk of perforation and damage to the surrounding organs when the sharper instrument, or later with the development of instruments with suction attached, were used to remove pregnancy tissue. At a similar time, changes in personal hygiene and sanitation further improved the safety of surgical procedures, although infection remained a significant cause of morbidity and mortality throughout the nineteenth century. 12

By the 1860s, advertisements for abortifacients were broadly disseminated and claimed to improve menstrual regularity and restore “obstruction.” 15 These pills were usually not described in the advertising and in the literature it is difficult to know what they contained and instead were referred to as a “female pill” or “regulating pills” only. 4 In 1899, the Lancet medical journal reported an investigation of commercial suppliers of abortifacient drugs warning readers of their danger, but the same journal issue ran advertisements for other similar medications that were apparently trustworthy. 16 Although the euphemisms used to describe these medications were broadly similar, the difference seemed to reflect the origin of the advertisements—that is, if advertisements originated from one of the many underground and secretive abortion services, or from the medical profession who wanted increasingly to break into the lucrative market of selling abortifacients. 17 , 18

Significant changes occurred after the first World War, with increasing societal importance placed on women's rights generally. 5 Women in Britain were granted the right to vote in 1918, provided they were aged 30 or above and had qualifications or owned property. This requirement was changed again just 10 years later when the voting rights of women were equaled to men. 19 In 1921, Marie Stopes, a British scientist, opened a clinic in London to provide free advice and support to married women seeking contraception and family planning advice. 20 This enabled the development of various methods of contraception that were both safe and effective, including the use of olive oil–soaked sponges and cervical caps. 21 Her work in the introduction of safe and suitable contraception and abortion provision was so important and revolutionary that in the 1970s, an international foundation was established in her name providing and promoting safe reproductive healthcare for women throughout the world. This foundation continues to be the most widely known provider of reproductive healthcare today.

DEVELOPMENT OF SURGICAL ABORTION

In the 1930s, significant developments were made in surgical abortion services with the advent of mechanical suction devices. 11 The history of using suction to effect an abortion can be traced to 1849 when a dry cupping technique was used by American physician Frederick Hollick, followed by the exhausting syringe in the 1860s developed by Scottish physician Sir James Simpson. 22 Mechanical suction devices followed in the 1930s 22 developed by various pioneers, though initially used for endometrial biopsies and incomplete abortions. 22 Two Chinese physicians have since been retrospectively credited with advancing this technique to initiate and complete an abortion when their paper from 1958 was translated and published in the BMJ. 23 It is reported that two Russian physicians observed the technique in China and further developed the technique. It was not used in the United States and the United Kingdom until the late 1960s. 22

What followed was the widespread use of a blunt device to be inserted into the uterus after cervical dilation, with the contents suctioned via a vacuum aspirator. This had the benefit of further reducing the risk of uterine perforation from the procedure, while minimizing trauma to the uterus caused by movements to try and remove the pregnancy tissue manually. At around this time, antibiotics were being developed and first introduced in maternity services in 1936 with a drastic decline in maternal mortality following. Reliable record keeping in England is available from the early 1930s demonstrating a maternal mortality rate of approximately 400 per 100 000 births, with a sharp fall to below 50 by the 1950s. 24

Today there are a variety of devices used to effect surgical abortion once the cervix is dilated, depending upon the gestation. In early pregnancy, many low‐ and middle‐income and even some high‐income countries use a manual vacuum aspirator. 25 This is usually a single‐use small device which includes a part that enters through the cervix to the uterus, and a manual vacuum aspirator that is activated by the operator to remove the pregnancy tissue. A range of electronic suction devices also exist that can be attached to a cannula of varied size and inserted to remove products of conception. 26 These devices, either manual or electronic, are now preferred over the traditional curettage procedures with lower blood loss, pain, and procedure time. 27 Now using these devices, surgical abortion is associated with very low risks of serious complications with an efficacy of 99.8% in the first trimester. 28 It also has the advantages of being quick and predictable, having fewer side effects, less pain, and less time required away from normal activities when compared to medical abortion. 29

The surgical procedure of abortion becomes more technically complex with increasing gestation. 30 From approximately 12 weeks gestation, the size and development of the conceptus increases requiring a destructive procedure known as dilation and evacuation (D&E). This procedure requires more time to prepare the cervix, followed by the use of instruments as well as suction to grasp and remove fetal parts through the cervix. After approximately 18 weeks, the amount of cervical dilation required usually is above what can be achieved with medication in a short period of time without inducing labor. Instead, osmotic or mechanical dilators are placed into the cervix and left, often overnight, to allow gradual cervical dilation, followed by the removal of fetal tissues the next day. 31 Although it is possible to remove the fetus without dismemberment, a longer period of cervical dilation is required, and is thus more time consuming and damage to the fetus may occur, in particular the calvarium. For these reasons, D&E is generally performed before 24 weeks gestation. 32

DEVELOPMENT OF MEDICAL ABORTION

The initial abortifacient substances reported from the premodern era varied enormously in their compounds and levels of toxicity. The first trials of an abortifacient medication were in aminopterin, a folic acid antagonist which was developed and used initially for chemotherapy in the 1940s. 33 The efficacy was variable, and reported teratogenicity if an abortion was not affected limited the popularity of this method. 34 Abortifacient medications were revolutionized by the discovery and then artificial synthesis of prostaglandins by a Swedish group in the 1960s and 1970s, a feat which earnt them the Nobel Prize in Physiology and Medicine in 1982. 35 Prostaglandins induce uterine contractions and are now used to effect abortion throughout gestation. They are also commonly used for induction of labor in pregnancies at term. Prostaglandins were noted to be much more effective at inducing abortion than previous medications, albeit at the cost of significant gastrointestinal side effects and pain. 36

Two such prostaglandins are gemeprost and sulprostone. Gemeprost was developed in the 1980s, and an injectable preparation called sulprostone was also developed and trialed around this time. 37 These medications had similar efficacy to a surgical abortion but their use remained limited by the occurrence of gastrointestinal side effects. In the early 1980s, scientists in France developed mifepristone, a glucocorticoid receptor antagonist, recognizing that its action also included antiprogestogenic effects. 38 It was approved for use in 1988. 38 This was combined with gemeprost and sulprostone with high efficacy in early pregnancy, and overall, relatively low rates of complications in comparison with previous treatments. 39 , 40 , 41

In the early 1980s, French endocrinologists recognized misoprostol, a synthetic prostaglandin E1 medication used at the time for prevention or treatment of stomach ulcers, as a potential abortifacient. 42 , 43 , 44 Available in Brazil from the mid‐1980s and produced there from 1988, it was recommended by Brazilian pharmacists for abortion to circumvent laws criminalizing abortion. 44 Misoprostol is a more stable version of gemeprost that does not require refrigeration and was seen to effectively induce abortion in early pregnancy in combination with mifepristone. 37

Mifepristone, developed in 1982, 38 is a progesterone antagonist which functions to block the quiescent action of progesterone on uterine smooth muscle and significantly increases the efficacy of misoprostol in completing an abortion. 45 Because it is an antisteroid medication, it is contraindicated in women on chronic long term corticosteroid therapy and in women with porphyria. 46 The success rates of early medical abortion were significantly improved with the development and adjunct use of mifepristone. 38 , 47 The combination of mifepristone and misoprostol is well tolerated, allowing its prescription for use with tele‐medicine abortion services. 48 , 49 , 50 The importance of the safety of the use of these medications was recognized by the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics who released a statement in 1999 affirming the right of women to access safe medical or surgical abortion services, 41 followed closely by the World Health Organization which released guidance on safe abortion in 2003. 42

Many current regimens for early pregnancy outpatient medical abortion use a combination of mifepristone and misoprostol. This regimen has been shown to be safe, with a low overall risk of complications. 46 A systematic review incorporating data on 45 000 women undergoing a variety of medical abortion protocols reported fewer than 5% required surgery to complete the abortion, with only 1.1% of women reported to have an ongoing pregnancy. 51 A second review conducted for the purpose of risk prediction reports data on 126 000 abortions and found a failure rate of 6.7%, though it did not report the number of ongoing pregnancies. 52 The failure rate is most significantly influenced by pregnancy gestation, with earlier abortion more likely to be effective than those approaching the ninth week. 51 Other risk factors for medical abortion failure include maternal age, parity, previous abortion, marital status, type of residence, and differences between gestational age calculated using the last menstrual period and that measured via ultrasound. 52 Varying gestational age limits apply to the eligibility for early outpatient medical abortion depending on the country of use; however, WHO recommendations use safely up to 12 weeks of pregnancy. 53

Medical abortion can be performed at any stage of pregnancy, depending on the legal framework in place, and may be preferred in certain situations including to allow autopsy in the setting of termination of pregnancy for fetal anomaly. Mifepristone has also improved the efficacy of later pregnancy medical abortion. The risks of complications are higher with increasing gestation beyond 63 days, with the process usually of longer duration. The majority of second and third trimester medical abortions are achieved by using mifepristone followed by adjusted doses of misoprostol that are lower than the doses used in early pregnancy, but usually require a longer course. 54 Second and third trimester medical abortions are performed within a hospital service in most countries. 55

CONCLUSION

Abortion of pregnancy is not a “modern” societal issue, but one that has been afforded to women, albeit with varying degrees of success, over many centuries. Methods have evolved which have increased the availability, effectiveness and safety of abortion, while substantially reducing associated morbidity. It is important that there are continued efforts across the world towards safe, efficacious, and available abortion access for all women.
