Exploring the optimal GDM management in twin pregnancies—Comment on Obstetric and neonatal outcomes in the management of twin pregnancies with gestational diabetes using the IADPSG criteria for singleton pregnancies
Authors: Xinyu Shu, Mi Yao, Chenglong Li, Huixia Yang
Published: 2026

We read with keen interest the recently published article, which analyzed a three-year retrospective dataset of over a thousand twin pregnancies. The authors concluded that under the current diagnostic criteria for GDM, which are the same as those for singletons, GDM is strongly associated with an increased risk of small for gestational age (SGA) infants and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admissions, while it decreases the risk of large for gestational age (LGA) infants. Furthermore, by examining the weekly gestational weight gain (GWG) in relation to the risks of these adverse perinatal outcomes, the study suggests that tailored diagnostic criteria and management approaches for twin pregnancies warrant further investigation [1].

We greatly appreciate the scientific rigor of this study, which included adjustments for significant confounders such as pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) and GWG (the authors utilized BMI change as a proxy), employed generalized estimated equation (GEE) to account for the intra-twin correlation in the data, and selected twin-specific growth charts for reference [2]. With these robust designs, and considering the majority of literature indicating that GDM is associated with fetal growth acceleration even in twin pregnancies [3], we believe that the unusual findings of this study may largely be attributed to stricter glycemic management.

As the authors mentioned, only two studies to date have shown an association between GDM and an increased risk of SGA in twin pregnancies, both focusing on glycemic control. In a retrospective observational study, Berezowsky et al. [4] demonstrated that an optimal glucose value (defined as > 50% of values measured within target during the last three visits before delivery) was associated with a greater risk of SGA (aOR: 2.09, 95% CI: 1.10–3.96). Fox et al. [5], using a case-control approach, reported that twin pregnancies with SGA had significantly lower levels of maternal fasting glucose and a lower proportion of abnormal fasting and postprandial glucose levels compared to those without SGA (all P < 0.05). Notably, no studies have reported a significant relationship between GDM and a decreased risk of LGA in twin pregnancies. Although it is generally acknowledged that all GDM pregnancies receive corresponding treatments, whether through lifestyle interventions or medical therapy. Moreover, our own study also proved that GDM is associated with a mild increase in fetal weight (approximately 45 g for each fetus at birth) even with significant lower GWG after GDM diagnosis (manuscript under review in BMC Pregnancy and Childbirth). Moreover, given the significantly lower GWG observed in the GDM group of this study, it is reasonable to infer that an overall stringent surveillance was the major cause for this conclusion.

We could not agree more with the authors’ stance on the need to explore optimal diagnostic criteria and management approaches for GDM in twin pregnancies. Based on current evidence, clinical trials focusing on the impact of different diagnostic standards or management approaches (especially glycemic control) on perinatal outcomes are highly recommended. Such trials would help to elucidate the causal relationship between clinical interventions and outcomes, thereby facilitating the search for the most effective strategies. Considerable effort is still required in this endeavor.
