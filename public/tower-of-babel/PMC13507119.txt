Anti-CD4 monoclonal antibody treatment enhances CD8+ T cell antitumor immunity and suppresses tumor growth in pulmonary fibrosis-associated lung cancer in mice
Authors: Tatsuki Nishioka, Takehiro Sakabe, Masahiro Kitabatake, Noriko Ouji-Sageshima, Atsushi Hara, Ichiro Sato, Nobuhiro Fujioka, Ryutaro Furukawa, Tomoko Kasamatsu-Nishimura, Shigeto Hontsu, Shigeo Muro, Toshihiro Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung cancer (LC) as a complication worsens the prognosis of patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF); however, established therapeutic stragegies remain elusive. This study aimed to investigate whether modulation of the immune microenvironment could provide a potential therapeutic strategy in the setting of IPF-associated LC. We created a murine IPF-LC complication model by intravenous administration of Lewis lung carcinoma cells into bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis model mice. We administered anti-CD4 monoclonal antibody (mAb) to the mice and evaluated the effects on tumor growth, T-cell phenotype in the lungs and lymph nodes, and survival rate. T-cell phenotype was analyzed by flow cytometry and survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method. Tumor number and size were both significantly increased in this model. CD8+ T cells were significantly decreased, while regulatory T cells (Tregs) were significantly increased in the lungs and lymph nodes. Anti-CD4 mAb treatment significantly increased the number and antitumor gene expression of CD8+ T cells, suppressed tumor growth in fibrotic lungs, reduced the number of Tregs, and improved survival. These results indicate that anti-CD4 mAb treatment inhibits tumor growth by enhancing the number and antitumor activity of CD8+ T cells in IPF-LC mice. Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is one of the most common interstitial lung diseases, characterized by chronic, progressive fibrosing interstitial pneumonia of unknown cause1. The disease is associated with progressively worsening pulmonary function and a poor prognosis, with an average survival similar to or even worse than many neoplastic diseases2. IPF is also associated with various comorbidities, including pulmonary hypertension, emphysema, lung cancer (LC), cardiovascular disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and sleep disorders, some of which occur as a consequence of or have common risk factors with IPF, while the connections of other diseases with IPF remain unclear. Among these comorbidities, LC has been shown to have an important impact on mortality3. Patients with IPF have 7- to 14-fold increased risk of LC compared with the general population4. Pulmonary fibrosis is itself a risk factor for LC, while aging, male sex, smoking history, and coexisting emphysema are also strong risk factors. IPF and LC also share many pathogenic mechanisms, including genetics, epigenetics, signaling pathways, epithelial–mesenchymal transformation, and senescence5–7. Some drugs targeting molecules and processes in IPF and LC have thus already been approved or are undergoing clinical trials for the treatment of both diseases5. The treatment of LC with pulmonary fibrosis, however, is still intractable because of the increased risk of acute exacerbations after surgery, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy8.

Cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes (CTLs) in the tumor microenvironment play an important role in tumor immunity, with their antitumor effect primarily mediated by perforin, granzyme B, interferon (IFN)-γ, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) can also be induced and differentiated in the tumor microenvironment and suppress antitumor immunity by inhibiting the activation and differentiation of CD4+ helper T cells and CTLs9–11. Tregs are also strongly associated with the pathogenesis of IPF, and previous studies found a correlation between the number of Tregs and the severity of IPF12,13. A recent study reported a reduced number of CD8+ T cells in a mouse model of IPF-LC14. The detailed mechanism of IPF complicated by LC, however, remains unclear.

In this study, we created an IPF-LC model and aimed to clarify the contribution of CTLs and Tregs to the development of LC with pulmonary fibrosis. Notably, our study raised the therapeutic potential of anti-CD4 monoclonal antibody (mAb) for IPF-LC.

Materials and methods

IPF–LC mouse model

C57BL/6J mice (7-week-old females) purchased from CLEA Japan Inc. (Tokyo, Japan) were maintained under specific-pathogen-free conditions at the Animal Research Center of Nara Medical University. Bleomycin (BLM) was purchased from Enzo Life Science (Farmingdale, NY, USA). Lewis lung carcinoma cells (LLC; JCRB Cell Bank, Osaka, Japan) were inoculated via the tail vein to create a murine model of LC15. Mice were administered saline or 0.75 mg/kg BLM via the intratracheal route under anesthesia induced by an intraperitoneal injection of 100 mg/kg pentobarbital (Nacali tesque, Kyoto, Japan), and PBS or LLC (3 × 10⁵ cells/mouse) were injected into the tail vein 14 days later. Under the anesthesia by pentobarbital (100 mg/kg), mice were euthanized 7 or 14 days after injection by exsanguination via cardiac puncture followed by cervical dislocation. The animal experimental protocols were approved by The Animal Care and Use Committee at Nara Medical University (Approval Number: 13308) and all procedures were carried out according to the Policy on the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, Nara Medical University and Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines. Furthermore, all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Histological analysis

Left lung tissue isolated from euthanized mice was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sliced at a thickness of 1–2 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE), and Masson’s trichrome staining. Tumor number, tumor size, and the tumor area percentage in the lung were determined, as described previously16. Fibrosis was assessed according to the Ashcroft score17. To compare tumor size between groups, we selected the 10 largest tumors per sample, to reduce data variability. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval using 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 105 °C for 10 min, followed by double immunohistochemical staining for CD8 and Foxp3. The sections were first stained with anti-FoxP3 antibody (1:100; #12653; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) using a Histofine Simple Stain AP® kit (Nichirei, Tokyo, Japan), and visualized with Fast Red II (Nichirei). For CD8 staining, the slides were re-treated with citrate buffer under the same conditions to retrieve antigens and denature prior antibodies, and were then incubated with anti-CD8 antibody (1:2000; ab217344; Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom), followed by treatment with Histofine Mouse Stain Kit and DAB substrate (Nichirei). Foxp3-positive cells appeared red and CD8-positive cells appeared brown.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

Right lung lobes were isolated and dissociated using a Lung Dissociation Kit with a gentleMACS dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec GmbH, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells from lungs and draining lymph nodes (LNs) were stained with Zombie-Aqua (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA) to exclude dead cells. Fc receptors were then blocked using anti-CD16/32 mAb (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The cells were subsequently stained with mAbs against surface antigens: CD3 (clone 145-2C11), CD4 (GK1.5), CD8a (53 − 6.7), CD25 (PC61), CD44 (IM7), CD45 (30-F11), and PD-1 (29 F.1A12) (all Biolegend). For intracellular staining, the cells were fixed and permeabilized using a Mouse Foxp3 Buffer Set (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and then stained for Foxp3 (MF23). Stained cells were analyzed using a MACSQuant Analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec) and the data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA). Gating was performed sequentially for lymphocytes (forward/side scatter) and live cells. CD8+ T cells were identified as CD8+CD3+ cells and Tregs were identified as CD25+Foxp3+CD4+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 2). For gene expression analysis, CD8+ T cells were purified using a BD FACSAria IIu cell sorter (BD Bioscienc es).

In vivo antibody treatment

For CD4+ T cell or Treg depletion, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 100 µg of anti-CD4 (GK1.5) or anti-CD25 (PC-61.5.3) mAb, respectively, or control IgG (LTF-2 or HRPN) (Bio X Cell, Lebanon, NH, USA) in successive doses on days 3 and 1 before tumor injection. For survival studies, the antibody was administered once a week after tumor injection until 28 days after tumor injection.

Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was purified from lung tissue using NucleoSpin RNA (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) and then reverse transcribed to cDNA using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative PCR was performed with TaqMan gene expression assays using a StepOne system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the default cycling conditions, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The following TaqMan gene expression assays were used: Gapdh (Mm99999915), Ifng (Mm01168134), Prf1 (Mm00812512), GzmB (Mm00442837), Foxp3 (Mm00475162), Tnf (Mm00443258), and Il10 (Mm01288386). Gene expression was analyzed by the relative ΔΔCT method and normalized to Gapdh expression.

RNA sequencing

RNA sequence analysis was performed by Rhelixa Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). Extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed using a SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input Kit (Takara-Bio, Osaka, Japan) and RNA-seq libraries were prepared using a NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and 150 bp paired-end reads were acquired. Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were removed using fastp (v0.23.4)18 and the trimmed reads were mapped to the mouse reference genome GRCm39 (mm39) using STAR (v2.7.11a)19. Transcript abundance was quantified using RSEM (v1.3.3)20. The quality of the sequence data and mapped data was checked using MultiQC (v1.25.2)21. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed for Gene Ontology terms and hallmark gene sets using GSEA v4.4.022,23.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SEM). Statistical analysis was performed using Student’s t-tests with GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Survival rates were analyzed using log-rank tests after Kaplan-Meier analysis.

Results

BLM-induced lung fibrosis promoted tumor expansion

We assessed the influence of lung fibrosis on tumor growth by creating an IPF–LC mouse model. Mice received 0.75 mg/kg BLM intratracheally to induce lung fibrosis, followed by intravenous injection of LLC 14 days later. The mice were then analyzed 7 or 14 days after LLC injection (Fig. 1A). The number of tumors, size of each tumor, and percentage of tumor area in the lungs were quantitated in HE-stained lung sections. Tumor number, tumor size, and the percentage of tumor area were all significantly increased in the BLM + LLC group at Day 14, whereas there were no significant differences between the LLC and BLM + LLC groups at Day 7 (Fig. 1B-E). These results indicated that pulmonary fibrosis induced by BLM promoted the background of tumor progression. On the other hand, a pathological analysis using Masson’s trichrome staining and the Ashcroft score revealed no significant difference in lung fibrosis between the BLM-only and BLM + LLC groups at either Day 7 or Day 14. (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B).

C57BL/6J mice received intratracheal BLM 0.75 mg/kg, followed by intravenous injection of LLC via the tail vein 14 days later. Mice were analyzed 7 or 14 days after LLC injection. (B) Lung sections from LLC and BLM + LLC mice were stained with HE at Day 7 and Day 14 (scale bars = 500 μm). BLM-induced lung fibrosis changed the antitumor immunological environment

We compared the immunological environments in the LLC and BLM + LLC groups by analyzing the T cell phenotype in the lungs and draining LNs by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 2). The proportions of Tregs (CD25+Foxp3+CD4+) among CD3+ cells in the lungs were significantly increased at both Day 7 and Day 14, while CD8+ T cells were significantly decreased in the BLM + LLC group compared with the LLC group. In addition, the CD8+ T cell/Treg ratio in the lungs was significantly decreased in the BLM + LLC group (Fig. 2A). Similar results were also observed in the LNs (Fig. 2B). We also compared the tumor microenvironment in the LLC and BLM + LLC groups. Immunohistochemical staining for CD8 and Foxp3 in lung tissue showed a lower number of CD8⁺ cells and a higher number of Foxp3⁺ cells, resulting in a significantly decreased CD8⁺/Foxp3⁺ cell ratio in the BLM + LLC group (Fig. 2C).

Percentages of Tregs (CD25+Foxp3+CD4+ T cells) among CD3+ cells, CD8+ T cells among CD3+ cells, and CD8+ T cell/Treg ratio. (C) Immunohistochemical analysis of CD8 (brown) and Foxp3 (red) expression in tumors at Day 14 following LLC injection (left panel, scale bars = 100 μm and 50 μm), and CD8+/Foxp3+ cell ratio in tumors (right panel). (D) Gene expression levels of Foxp3, Il10, Tnf, and Ifng in lungs at Days 7 and 14. Day 7: n = 10 per group; Day 14: n = 5 in LLC and n = 7 in BLM + LLC groups. Values presented as mean ± SEM. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.

Regarding the expression profiles of genes associated with Tregs and CTL function in the lungs, Foxp3 and Il10 expression levels were significantly increased, whereas Tnf and Ifng were significantly decreased in the BLM + LLC group at Day 7 (Fig. 2D). Ifng expression was significantly decreased in the BLM + LLC group even at Day 14 (Fig. 2D). These results indicate that the tumor microenvironment was affected in BLM + LLC model mice, with reduced CD8+ T cells and enhanced Tregs.

BLM-induced lung fibrosis abrogated the antitumor CD8+ T cell immunological environment

We investigated functional changes in CD8+ T cells in the lung by analyzing their gene expression profile at Day 14 by RNA sequencing (Fig. 3A). Expression levels of genes associated with activation, antitumor cytokines, and intracellular signals, including Tnf, Il2, and Gzmb, were reduced in lung CD8+ T cells in the BLM + LLC group, while gene expression levels of naïve T cell markers were not decreased (Fig. 3B). In addition, GSEA showed a decrease in gene sets related to CD8⁺ T cell activation, proliferation, and cell cycle pathways in lungs in the BLM + LLC group (Fig. 3C), as well as a decrease in gene sets related to cell signaling, such as interleukin-2/signal transducer and activator of transcription 5, TNF-α/nuclear factor-κB (Fig. 3D). These results suggest that CD8+ T cells are functionally impaired in fibrotic lung tissue.

Anti-CD4 but not anti-CD25 antibodies inhibited tumor expansion in fibrotic lung tissue

We determined if Tregs contributed to enhanced tumor development in the BLM + LLC model using anti-CD25 mAb (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Histologically, there was no significant difference in the number of tumors, tumor size, or percentage of tumor area between BLM + LLC mice treated with control IgG and anti-CD25 mAb, indicating that tumor growth was unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 3B-E). Flow cytometric analysis of T lymphocytes from LNs showed that Tregs were significantly decreased, but there was no significant difference in the percentage of CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 3F). Anti-CD4 mAb treatment was previously suggested to deplete immunosuppressive populations in a mouse model more efficiently than anti-CD25 mAb treatment24. We therefore treated BLM + LLC model mice with anti-CD4 mAb 1 and 3 days before LLC injection and analyzed the results 14 days after LLC injection (Fig. 4A). Histologically, there was no significant difference in the number of lung tumors between the groups in BLM + LLC mice, but both tumor size and the percentage of tumor area were significantly smaller in the anti-CD4 mAb group compared with the control IgG group (Fig. 4B-E). On the other hand, there was no significant difference in lung fibrosis between the control Ab and anti-CD4 mAb groups at Day 14, as assessed by Masson’s trichrome staining and the Ashcroft score. (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B).

Anti-CD4 mAb treatment increased CD8+ T cells and decreased Tregs

To determine the mechanism by which anti-CD4 mAb inhibits tumor expansion, we examined the effects of anti-CD4 mAb administration on T cell populations potentially involved in antitumor immunity in BLM + LLC mice. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that the percentage of CD8+ T cells was significantly increased by anti-CD4 mAb treatment, and among these cells, the percentage of CD44highPD-1+CD8+ T cells, as a major effector population in the antitumor response, was also increased (Fig. 5A). In addition, the percentage of Tregs was significantly decreased and the CD8+ T cell/Treg ratio in the lungs was significantly increased by anti-CD4 mAb treatment (Fig. 5A). CD8 and Foxp3 immunohistochemical staining also showed a significant increase in the CD8+/Foxp3+ ratio in tumor areas, consistent with the flow cytometry results (Fig. 5B). Gene expression analysis of CD8+ T cells in lung tissue from BLM + LLC mice at Day 14 showed that anti-CD4 mAb increased the expression of antitumor factors, including Ifng, Prf1, and Gzmb (Fig. 5C). These results suggest that anti-CD4 mAb treatment successfully inhibited Tregs and enhanced antitumor immunity by activating CTLs in fibrotic lungs.

Anti-CD4 mAb reduced mortality in BLM + LLC model mice

We also evaluated the effects of anti-CD4 mAb treatment on survival in LLC and BLM + LLC mice. Mice were treated with anti-CD4 mAb once a week after LLC injection (Fig. 6A). BLM + LLC model showed significantly lower survival rates compared with LLC mice (Fig. 6B). Notably, anti-CD4 mAb treatment significantly increased survival in BLM + LLC mice, but not in LLC mice. These results indicate that anti-CD4 mAb has a strong antitumor effect in fibrotic lungs, leading to increased survival.

Discussion

LC complications are known to significantly worsen the prognosis of patients with IPF3; however, IPF-LC currently lacks established therapeutic strategies and the detailed mechanism of this effect is unknown. CD8+ T cells play an important role in tumor immunity, and immunosuppressive Tregs are involved in tumor-induced immune escape9–11 and in the severity of IPF12. Here, we aimed to elucidate the immunological characteristics of the IPF-LC mouse model by focusing on CD8+ T cells and Tregs. This model was characterized by an increase in the percentage of Tregs and a decrease in the percentage of CD8+ T cells in the fibrotic lung environment. To address this immunological feature specific to IPF, we examined the effects of different antibodies and showed that anti-CD4 mAb exerted a remarkable antitumor effect in the IPF-LC model, resulting in increases in the percentage of and expression of antitumor factors by CD8+ T cells and a decrease in the percentage of Tregs.

We established a murine model of IPF-LC, which represented the clinical features of pulmonary fibrosis in several respects. First, the model could recapitulate an immunological environment similar to that observed in human IPF-LC. The number and proportion of Tregs were increased in patients with IPF compared with healthy controls25,26. In addition, the number of Foxp3+ T cells was increased and the ratio of CD8+/Foxp3+ T cells was decreased in human lung squamous cell carcinomas exhibiting a pathological usual interstitial pneumonia pattern, a feature associated with IPF, compared with those without this pattern27. These results were consistent with the elevated Tregs and decreased CD8+ T cell/Treg ratio observed in BLM + LLC mice in the current study. Our results were also consistent with the decreased number of CD8+ T cells observed in a previous IPF-LC model14. Second, our model reflected the tumor growth trends in IPF-LC. The severity of fibrosis was associated with a higher lung cancer stage at diagnosis among patients with IPF-LC28, in accord with the significant increase in the number and size of tumors observed in the present study. Third, the survival rate is significantly lower in patients with IPF-LC compared with patients with LC alone29,30, and this trend was also confirmed in our model.

The CD8+ T cell phenotype is a key player in antitumor immunity. CD8+ T cells were significantly decreased in our IPF–LC model. Previous studies have reported the involvement of numerous immune checkpoint molecules and immunosuppressive T cells, including Tregs, in CD8+ T cell exhaustion31–33, and exhausted CD8+ T cells then cease to produce IL-2 and antitumor factors such as TNF-α, IFN-γ, and granzyme B34,35. The current model demonstrated a significant increase in Tregs along with a decreased proportion of CD8+ T cells among T cells and reduced IFN-γ expression in the lung. In addition, gene sets related to T cell activation/proliferation and related signaling pathways, such as interleukin-2/signal transducer and activator of transcription 5, were much less activated in CD8+ T cells. These results suggest that Tregs are partly involved in the inhibition of CD8+ T cells in our model. We also found a significant decrease in antitumor cytokines, such as TNF-α and IFN-γ, which might also contribute to the downregulation of CD8+ T cells. Pulmonary fibrosis is known to profoundly reshape immune regulation, including alterations in CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations and immune suppression5,36,37. Previous studies have been reported that fibrosis-associated signaling pathways such as TGF-β–mediated signaling, chronic inflammatory cytokine networks, and stromal remodeling influence T cell differentiation, immune suppression, and immune cell recruitment in fibrotic lungs38,39. In addition, tumors arising in fibrotic lungs develop within a markedly different inflammatory and fibrotic microenvironment compared with conventional lung cancer models36,37. These mechanisms may partly explain the immune alterations observed in our model. Therefore, examining our therapeutic approach in an IPF-associated tumor setting provides biologically and clinically relevant insight that cannot be directly inferred from standard tumor models.

Tregs play a crucial regulatory role in limiting the excessive inflammatory response and maintaining immune homeostasis40, as well as having an important role in various diseases, including IPF. The results of many studies, however, have shown vague or even contradictory results regarding the role of Tregs in IPF. Tregs can contribute to the progression of pulmonary fibrosis by producing TGF-β and other related factors and by promoting the epithelial–mesenchymal transition40. However, Tregs can inhibit the progression of pulmonary fibrosis by promoting the repair of epithelial cell damage, inhibiting the accumulation of fibroblasts, and suppressing the production and function of relevant pro-inflammatory factors and pro-inflammatory cells41,42. For example, the T-cell subsets regulated by Tregs differed depending on the stage of pulmonary fibrosis in some animal experiments43. Notably, Tregs have demonstrated a pro-fibrotic role during the early disease stages but hinder fibrosis development in the later stages44. Tregs are also involved in tumor development and progression by inhibiting antitumor immunity45. We confirmed that a dysregulated immune response, including enhanced Treg involvement, plays a critical role in the pathogenesis and development of LC under fibrotic conditions; however, further studies are needed to clarify the role of Tregs, which are indispensable for maintaining immune homeostasis, in IPF-LC.

The present results demonstrated that anti-CD4 mAb could be an effective treatment option for IPF-LC. Its strong antitumor effect in the IPF-LC mouse model was most likely due to the activation of CD8+ T cells. Anti-CD4 mAb also significantly enhanced the gene expression of antitumor factors such as IFN-γ, perforin, and granzyme B in CD8+ T cells and increased the proportion of CD44highPD1+CD8+ T cells among CD8+ T cells. This augmentation of CD8+ T cells by anti-CD4 mAb, in terms of both quantity and quality, may have been responsible for its antitumor effect in fibrotic lungs. Anti-CD25 mAb did not have a similar CD8+ T cell enhancing effect, which was the likely reason for the difference in tumor shrinkage effects between anti-CD4 mAb and anti-CD25 mAb in fibrotic lungs. In addition, histological examination showed that both tumor size and the percentage of tumor area were significantly decreased by anti-CD4 mAb, whereas anti-CD25 mAb had no significant effect on tumor development in BLM + LLC mice. Although both anti-CD4 mAb and anti-CD25 mAb contributed to Treg inhibition, only anti-CD4 mAb significantly upregulated CD8+ T cells. The nonspecific T-cell-suppressive effect of anti-CD25 mAb may partly explain the difference in antitumor effects between the two mAbs. Overall, the present results showed that anti-CD4 mAb has a stronger antitumor effect than anti-CD25 mAb. Similar results have been reported in mice with systemic neuroblastoma or subcutaneous B16F10, Colon 26, or LLC tumors25,46. In a murine model of systemic neuroblastoma, most Tregs were depleted by anti-CD4 mAb, with an average depletion of 93% compared with 48% depletion following treatment with anti-CD25 mAb47. Previous studies suggested that a fraction of CD4+Foxp3+ Tregs may escape anti-CD25 mAb depletion due to Ab-mediated downregulation of CD25 antigen expression on CD25+Foxp3+ cells, or the existence of a CD25−Foxp3+ subset46,48. In the present study, it is possible that some Tregs escaped depletion by anti-CD25 mAb due to decreased CD25 antigen expression. It is also possible that anti-CD4 mAb showed more effective tumor reduction because anti-CD25 mAb not only decreased Tregs, but also activated T cells expressing CD25, which may counteract the antitumor immune effect of Treg depletion48,49. We found no significant increase in CD8+ T cells after anti-CD25 mAb treatment, possibly because this mAb caused a nonspecific decrease in T cells, including CD8+ T cells as well as Tregs. Anti-CD4 mAb affects a broader population of CD4⁺ T cells, including multiple functional subsets that may influence anti-tumor immunity50,51. In addition, depletion of CD4⁺ T cells could create the homeostatic space for clonal expansion of tumor specific CD8⁺ T cells52–54. These nonspecific suppression of T cells may also explain why anti-CD25 mAb treatment did not significantly reduce tumor growth in mice with pulmonary fibrosis.

This study had several limitations. First, BLM induces lung fibrosis via tissue remodeling after acute inflammation, rather than chronic inflammation, which does not match the actual pathogenic process of IPF. The development of fibrosis in the BLM model is rapid and reversible, and does not replicate the slow and irreversible progression of IPF55. In addition, because fibrosis was relatively mild in this study, neither tumor progression nor anti-CD4 mAb administration had an obvious effect on the fibrotic pathology. Further studies using more advanced or persistent fibrotic models may be required to clarify the impact of tumor growth on fibrosis progression. Second, LCs induced by intravenous injection of LLC are metastatic, rather than primary cancers, which again does not correspond to the clinical pathophysiology of IPF-LC in terms of tumor origin and multiplicity. Third, the detailed mechanism by which anti-CD4 mAb increases the number and activation of CD8+ T cells remains unknown. Furthermore, the present study was not designed to dissect the precise mechanisms by which pulmonary fibrosis reshapes the immune microenvironment, elucidating these processes remains crucial for further enhancing the biological interpretation of our findings. Fourth, we have not directly compared the effects of anti-CD4 mAb between LC and IPF-LC. Such a comparison would be valuable to further define disease-specific mechanisms and optimize therapeutic approaches.

Despite these limitations, our results demonstrate that both CD8+ T cells and Tregs play a vital role in tumor growth in the fibrotic lung. Furthermore, Tregs may contribute to tumor growth under fibrotic conditions by suppressing the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells. The results suggest that anti-CD4 mAb treatment not only enhanced tumor-specific CD8+ T cell responses but also suppressed Tregs. These findings indicate that anti-CD4 mAb may exert a superior antitumor effect by augmenting CD8+ T cells and suppressing Tregs, and may thus represent a potential treatment option for IPF-LC.
