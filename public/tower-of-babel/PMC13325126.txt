Magnetic Alginate-Based Composite Beads Incorporating Cu/TiO2: Synthesis, Structure–Property Relationships, and Catalytic Applicability
Authors: Supanit Chungyampin, Kathawut Rawangkay, Kulwadee Ponanunrirk, Nattawut Rodtuk, Wissanu Charerntanom, Sasiradee Jantasee, Paiboon Sreearunothai, Preeyaporn Chaiyasat, Amorn Chaiyasat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Magnetic alginate-based composite beads integrating Cu/TiO2 and magnetite (Fe3O4) were fabricated as polymer–inorganic hybrid systems for magnetic recovery. These composite beads were generated via ionotropic gelation, a method that enabled efficient encapsulation of inorganic constituents within the sodium alginate matrix while preserving the crystalline structures of Fe3O4 and anatase TiO2. The impact of Fe3O4 loading on the beads’ structural and physicochemical attributes was systematically investigated, revealing specific structure–property relationships that dictated encapsulation efficacy, swelling behavior, bulk density, and internal morphology. Among the formulations examined, the S-1.0Fe3O4–SA beads demonstrated a favorable balance between structural integrity and mass-transfer accessibility. The efficacy of the composite beads was evaluated via their employment in the hydrogenation of glacial acetic acid, a representative liquid-phase reaction. Examination of the reaction products indicated the formation of ethanol and ethyl acetate; the precise product distribution depended on the Cu loading in the Cu/TiO2 component. A moderate Cu content was observed to facilitate product formation, concurrently maintaining the structural stability of the beads. The S-1.0Fe3O4/SA-1.8Cu/TiO2-0.4 catalyst exhibited the highest ethanol production (18.69 mg L–1). Lower Cu loading resulted in insufficient active sites, whereas higher loading led to aggregation and reduced catalytic efficiency. Recycling tests revealed a gradual decrease in ethanol formation upon reuse, indicating partial catalyst deactivation, likely due to surface fouling and Cu leaching. Therefore, this study highlights the promise of alginate-based magnetic composite beads as a versatile polymer-supported platform, in which the macromolecular structure is crucial for dictating functional performance.

Introduction

Polymer-based composite materials incorporating functional inorganic components are increasingly being studied because they can combine structural adaptability, functional versatility, and ease of processing. − Polymer-inorganic hybrid systems, in particular, offer unique opportunities to tailor physicochemical properties. These properties include swelling behavior, density, internal structure, and mass-transfer characteristics, which are crucial for applications in liquid-phase environments. − Therefore, the performance of these hybrid materials is mainly determined by the relationships between their structures and properties, rather than by the inherent activities of their individual components. In recent years, alginate-based composite materials, particularly magnetic alginate systems, have attracted increasing attention due to their versatility in catalytic and environmental applications ,,

Sodium alginate, a biopolymer, is widely studied among various polymer matrices. This is due to its renewability, environmental compatibility, and its ability to form hydrogel networks through ionotropic gelation with divalent cations. , This mild, easily controlled gelation process allows alginate to encapsulate various inorganic fillers. This results in composite beads whose size, internal structure, and mechanical properties can be adjusted. , As a result, alginate-based composite beads have been widely studied as polymeric hosts for functional materials. In these materials, the macromolecular network creates a confined microenvironment that affects transport processes and interactions at the interface. , Incorporating magnetic nanoparticles, particularly magnetite (Fe3O4), into alginate matrices further enhances the functionality of these composite systems. This allows for the quick, solvent-free recovery of the material using an external magnetic field. , Magnetically recoverable polymer composites offer advantages in liquid-phase applications, particularly in addressing separation, reuse, and process sustainability challenges. , Nevertheless, the incorporation of magnetic fillers substantially influences the physicochemical characteristics of the polymer matrix. Specifically, parameters such as encapsulation efficiency, swelling behavior, bulk density, and internal morphology depend on Fe3O4 loading and bead architecture, underscoring the need for systematic investigations that correlate composition with material properties. ,,

In addition to providing structural and recovery functions, inorganic fillers can impart functional activity to polymer-based composite systems. Copper-based materials supported on metal oxides, such as TiO2, represent a class of inorganic components that have been widely investigated for reactions involving oxygenated molecules due to their moderate activity and favorable selectivity. − Strong metal–support interactions in Cu/TiO2 systems have been reported to enhance Cu dispersion and stabilize catalytically relevant species. − When immobilized within polymeric matrices, such inorganic phases offer an opportunity to investigate how polymer network structure, filler dispersion, and mass-transfer pathways collectively influence functional behavior, rather than focusing exclusively on maximizing reaction rates. Carboxylic acids derived from biomass processing, including acetic acid obtained from fermentation, syngas conversion, and industrial waste streams, provide convenient model substrates for evaluating functional applicability in liquid-phase composite systems owing to their simple molecular structure and high polarity. Acetic acid is particularly suitable for probing transport phenomena, interfacial interactions, and reaction pathways within polymer-supported architectures. − In this study, acetic acid is used as a model probe molecule. This allows us to examine how the composite structure and interactions between the polymer and inorganic components affect the system under realistic reaction conditions. While there has been considerable research on alginate-based composites and magnetically recoverable materials, recent studies have further highlighted their potential in catalytic and liquid-phase applications. ,, Specifically, few studies have investigated how the combination of Cu/TiO2 and Fe3O4 affects the structure–property relationships of magnetic alginate-based composite beads.

In this work, we prepared magnetic alginate-based composite beads incorporating Cu/TiO2 and Fe3O4 via ionotropic gelation. These beads are polymer-inorganic hybrid systems designed for magnetic recovery. The influence of inorganic loading on bead properties, including structure, encapsulation efficiency, swelling behavior, bulk density, and morphology, was thoroughly examined. Structural and morphological characteristics were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD), field-emission scanning electron microscopy combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (FESEM–EDS), and particle size analysis. The functional applicability of the composite beads was assessed by hydrogenating glacial acetic acid, a model reaction, and the products were subsequently analyzed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). This study provides important insights into the informed design of polymer-based magnetic composite materials, highlighting the critical importance of structure–property relationships in determining functional efficacy.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Sodium alginate powder (commercial grade, Chemipan Corporation CO., Ltd., Thailand) was used as a bead matrix. Magnetite (commercial grade, Cernic International Co., Ltd., Thailand) was used to render the material magnetically recoverable and reusable. Calcium chloride (food-grade, Chem Rich CO., Ltd., Thailand) was used as a cross-linker. Degussa p25 titanium dioxide (TiO2) nanopowder (rutile: anatase/85:15, 99.9%, 20 nm, Nanoshel UK Limited, UK), copper­(II) sulfate (AR, Techno Pharm Chem, India), polyvinylpyrrolidone (commercial grade, Chemipan Corporation CO., Ltd., Thailand) and vitamin C (commercial grade, Chemipan Corporation CO., Ltd., Thailand) were used as synthesis copper nanoparticles. Acetic acid, glacial (AR, RCI Labscan Limited, Ireland), was used as the solvent in the hydrogenation reaction.

Preparation of Cu/TiO2 Components

Cu nanoparticles were synthesized via the chemical reduction method. A copper­(II) sulfate (CuSO4) solution was formulated by dissolving 0.30, 0.45, 0.60, and 0.75 g of CuSO4·5H2O in 350 mL of deionized (DI) water, which corresponded to 1.20, 1.80, 2.40, and 3.00 mmol of Cu, respectively. Subsequently, 0.75 g of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) was incorporated and stirred until complete dissolution. In a subsequent procedure, 0.20 g of TiO2 was dispersed in 100 mL of DI water and sonicated for 20 min to achieve uniform dispersion. The TiO2 suspension was then added to the Cu-PVP solution and continuously stirred. The pH was adjusted to 9 to facilitate the formation of a homogeneous mixture. Vitamin C (1.00 g) was dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water and added to the prepared solution in three equal portions, with 10 min intervals between each addition, while maintaining the temperature at 60 °C and stirring continuously. This caused the suspension to turn purplish-gray. The resulting nanoparticles were washed three times with deionized water, dried at 60 °C for 2 h, and then kept in an airtight container.

The initial amounts of the copper precursor and TiO2 used in the synthesis were used to calculate the expected copper loadings. This was based on the assumption that all the copper was incorporated into the Cu/TiO2 composites. These expected values corresponded to theoretical copper contents of about 27.60, 36.40, 43.30, and 48.80 wt % for 1.20, 1.80, 2.40, and 3.00 mmol of copper, respectively. The expected copper loading was used to identify the samples and for comparison, while the surface copper content was measured separately using FESEM-EDS analysis.

Preparation of Fe3O4–SA and Cu/TiO2–Fe3O4–SA Beads

Preparation of Fe3O4–SA Beads

Fe3O4-sodium alginate (SA) beads were created using ionotropic gelation, as described in ref . Sodium alginate (1.00 g) was gradually dissolved in 100 mL of deionized (DI) water while stirring continuously at 80 °C for 1 h. The solution was then allowed to sit at room temperature for 24 h to ensure complete hydration. Fe3O4 nanoparticles (0.50, 1.00, and 1.50 g, respectively) were separately dispersed in 20 mL of DI water by sonication for 20 min, then added to the alginate solution. The resulting suspension was mixed at 1000 rpm for 45 min to achieve uniformity. The Fe3O4–SA suspension was added dropwise to a 1% w/v CaCl2 solution. This was done using either an uncontrolled or a controlled dropping system. This process created beads with diameters of about 3 mm (large beads, B) and 150–300 μm (small beads, S), as shown in Figure a,b. The resulting beads were then cured in the CaCl2 solution for 1 h. They were then collected by filtration, washed two to three times with deionized water, and dried at 60 °C for 2 h before further analysis.

Preparation of alginate beads by manual dropping (a) and automated dropping (b).

Incorporation of Cu/TiO2 into Fe3O4–SA Beads

Based on encapsulation efficiency and bead size, the S–1.0Fe3O4–SA formulation was selected as the support matrix for the catalyst. Cu/TiO2 nanoparticles were added to the Fe3O4–SA beads using a pregelation mixing method. Specific amounts of Cu/TiO2 (0.20, 0.40, and 0.60 g, respectively) were dispersed in deionized (DI) water and sonicated for 20 min to create uniform suspensions. The Cu/TiO2 suspensions were then combined with the Fe3O4–SA precursor mixture, as described in Section . The resulting Cu/TiO2–Fe3O4–SA suspensions were mixed at 1000 rpm for 45 min to ensure the Cu/TiO2 was evenly distributed in the polymer matrix. After that, the mixtures were added dropwise to a CaCl2 solution (1% w/v) using a controlled dropping system to create composite beads. These beads were then allowed to cure in the CaCl2 solution for 1 h, collected by filtration, washed 3 times with DI water, and dried at 60 °C for 2 h. The resulting composite beads were named Cu/TiO2–Fe3O4–SA-0.20, Cu/TiO2–Fe3O4–SA-0.40, and Cu/TiO2–Fe3O4–SA-0.60, based on the amount of Cu/TiO2 used. The prepared beads were then analyzed structurally and tested for their catalytic properties.

Characterization of CuNPs, Fe3O4–SA Beads and Cu/TiO2–Fe3O4–SA Composites

The structural, morphological, and thermal characteristics of the CuNPs, Fe3O4–SA beads, and Cu/TiO2–Fe3O4–SA composites were analyzed. X-ray diffraction (XRD; PANalytical, X’Pert PRO MRD) was utilized to ascertain the crystalline structures employing Cu Kα radiation across a 2θ range of 10–80°. Optical microscopy (OM; SK-100EB and SK-100ET, Seek) was employed to examine the overall morphology, bead diameter distribution, and surface texture of the synthesized beads. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy integrated with focused ion beam and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (FESEM–FIB/EDS; Carl Zeiss, AURIGA) was employed to analyze the surface morphology and elemental composition, specifically the distributions of Fe, Ti, Cu, and O, at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. Laser diffraction (LD; SALD-2300, Shimadzu) was used to assess the particle size distribution of the beads across the micron-to-millimeter range, thereby complementing the microscopic observations.

The specific surface area of the produced CuNPs was evaluated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method, using N2 adsorption at 77 K (Bel Sorp Mini II) after 6 h of degassing at 150 °C. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA; TGA/DSC 3+, METTLER TOLEDO) was performed under nitrogen, from 25 to 600 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min–1, to evaluate the thermal stability and inorganic residue content of the beads. The organic alginate matrix undergoes thermal degradation within this temperature range, leaving the residual material primarily attributable to the inorganic Fe3O4 component enclosed within the beads.

Based on the TGA findings, the encapsulation efficiency (%EE) of Fe3O4 within the sodium alginate beads was estimated by comparing the experimentally measured Fe3O4 content with the theoretical Fe3O4 loading incorporated during bead fabrication. Furthermore, the swelling degree and bulk density of the Fe3O4–SA beads were determined using gravimetric methods.

Catalytic Properties Evaluation

The catalytic properties of the Cu/TiO2–Fe3O4–SA composites were investigated using the model hydrogenation of glacial acetic acid with molecular hydrogen (H2), which is commonly employed to evaluate Cu-based hydrogenation catalysts. − 2.0 g of the Cu/TiO2–Fe3O4–SA composites and 30 mL of glacial acetic acid were loaded into a stainless–steel autoclave (quartz liner). The reactor was sealed, purged three times with hydrogen to displace atmospheric air, and then pressurized with H2 to 1 bar at ambient temperature.The hydrogenation reaction was conducted at 200 °C for 4 h with continuous stirring. After the reaction, the mixture was cooled to room temperature, and the catalyst was magnetically recovered, rinsed several times with deionized water, dried at 60 °C, and reused under the same conditions for five consecutive cycles, in accordance with commonly reported procedures for magnetically separable catalysts. , The catalytic performance was evaluated based on product formation determined by GC–MS analysis. Ethanol was identified as the primary hydrogenation product and used as an indicator of catalytic activity, while the presence of byproducts such as ethyl acetate was considered in assessing selectivity.

This reaction system serves as a representative model for liquid-phase hydrogenation of biomass-derived oxygenated compounds. The Cu/TiO2–Fe3O4–SA composites are therefore expected to be applicable as magnetically recoverable catalysts in sustainable chemical processes, particularly for biomass conversion and catalytic upgrading reactions.

Results and Discussion

Structural and Properties of Cu/TiO2 Components

Cu/TiO2 components, distinguished by varying nominal Cu loadings (1.2Cu/TiO2, 1.8Cu/TiO2, 2.4Cu/TiO2, and 3.0Cu/TiO2), were synthesized and then integrated into magnetic alginate-based composite beads. The generation of CuNPs on the TiO2 support depends on the equilibrium between the Cu precursor concentration and the reducing agent; specifically, suitable molar ratios facilitate uniform nucleation and controlled growth of Cu species. , Conversely, at excessively elevated precursor concentrations, the rapid growth and aggregation of CuNPs may ensue, thereby diminishing structural uniformity.

The crystalline structures of both pristine TiO2 and Cu/TiO2 components were examined via X-ray diffraction (XRD), as depicted in Figure . The distinctive diffraction peaks corresponding to anatase TiO2 were identified at 2θ: 25.3° (101), 37.8° (004), and 48.0° (200). Following the incorporation of Cu, new diffraction peaks emerged at 2θ ≈ 43.3° (111) and 50.4° (200), which are attributable to metallic Cu, thereby validating the successful reduction of Cu species and their deposition onto the TiO2 surface. A progressive reduction in the intensity of the anatase TiO2 peaks was observed with increasing Cu loading, suggesting partial lattice disruption due to Cu deposition. Concurrently, the Cu-related reflections became more distinct, especially at elevated loadings (2.4 and 3.0), indicating enhanced Cu crystallinity. Conversely, excessive Cu deposition is also associated with a greater propensity for particle aggregation, potentially diminishing surface accessibility.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of pristine TiO2 and Cu/TiO2 components with different nominal Cu loadings: (a) 1.2Cu/TiO2, (b) 1.8Cu/TiO2, (c) 2.4Cu/TiO2, and (d) 3.0Cu/TiO2.

The textural properties of the Cu/TiO2 components were evaluated using nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements, following the BET method, as illustrated in Figure . The specific surface area and pore characteristics are critical factors influencing surface accessibility and mass-transfer dynamics within composite systems. The 1.2Cu/TiO2 sample (Figure a,b) exhibited a type IV adsorption–desorption isotherm and an H3/H4-type hysteresis loop, indicating a mesoporous structure. A considerable adsorption capacity (∼180 cm3 g–1 STP) was recorded, which correlated with a BET surface area of 47.02 m2 g–1 and a pore volume of 0.29 cm3 g–1. In addition, the BJH pore-size distribution analysis showed a significant peak around 25 nm, indicating a relatively consistent mesoporous structure. The adsorption–desorption isotherm for the 1.8Cu/TiO2 sample is classified as type IV, characterized by a smaller hysteresis loop and a decrease in nitrogen adsorption, as shown in Figure c,d. The observed decrease in surface area (39.19 m2 g–1) and pore volume (0.20 cm3 g–1) can be attributed to partial pore filling with Cu nanoparticles. The BJH pore-size distribution shows both broadening and a shift toward 35–36 nm, which suggests that Cu deposition changes the pore shape while keeping the mesoporous structure. Further increases in Cu loading, specifically to 2.4 and 3.0Cu/TiO2, lead to additional reductions in surface area (37.97 → 28.37 m2 g–1) and pore volume (0.18 → 0.12 cm3 g–1). This indicates greater surface coverage and partial pore blockage by aggregated Cu species. At the highest loading, a broad pore-size distribution with a tail extending beyond 100 nm suggests the presence of partially blocked mesopores and spaces between particles.

FESEM and EDS were employed to examine the surface morphology and elemental distribution of the Cu/TiO2 components, as illustrated in Figures and . The TiO2 support, characterized by aggregated nanoparticles with irregular morphology, largely retained its structure after Cu incorporation. The nanoscale morphology of the synthesized Cu/TiO2 components was further confirmed by FESEM analysis (see Figure S1 in Supporting Information). The EDS spectra validate the presence of Cu, in addition to Ti and O, thus confirming the successful integration of Cu species. Elemental mapping images reveal that Cu is distributed across the TiO2 surface; however, localized Cu-enriched regions become more pronounced with increased loadings. To quantitatively evaluate the surface Cu content, FESEM–EDS spot analyses were performed at five distinct surface locations for each sample, and the averaged values, along with their standard deviations, are presented in Figure e.

The findings indicate a general increase in surface Cu content with increasing nominal Cu loading; however, larger standard deviations are observed at elevated loadings, suggesting greater surface heterogeneity. Notably, the slightly lower surface Cu content observed for the 3.0Cu/TiO2 sample compared to the 2.4Cu/TiO2 sample can be attributed to increased Cu aggregation at higher loading levels, leading to nonuniform surface distribution. As FESEM–EDS spot analysis is surface-sensitive and semiquantitative, such aggregation may result in lower averaged surface Cu values despite higher nominal loading. This behavior suggests that excessive Cu loading leads to a nonuniform surface distribution rather than a direct proportional increase in available Cu sites. Among the compositions examined, 1.8Cu/TiO2 demonstrates a beneficial equilibrium between Cu dispersion, surface accessibility, and the maintenance of the mesoporous TiO2 structure. This is corroborated by the XRD data, which reveals distinct metallic Cu reflections without significant suppression of anatase TiO2 peaks, alongside BET and BJH analyses that indicate moderate pore modification without substantial blockage. FESEM–EDS analysis (Figure ) further revealed that 1.8Cu/TiO2 exhibited a more uniform dispersion of Cu without significant aggregation compared to other loadings. In contrast, the lower loading (1.2Cu/TiO2) showed limited Cu distribution, while higher loadings (2.4 and 3.0Cu/TiO2) resulted in noticeable aggregation. Importantly, the relatively uniform elemental distribution observed for the 1.8Cu/TiO2 sample suggests the presence of well-dispersed Cu active sites, which are beneficial for hydrogen activation. Conversely, the increased heterogeneity at higher Cu loadings implies the formation of localized Cu aggregates, which may reduce effective surface accessibility. Therefore, the structural information from Figure provides direct support for selecting 1.8Cu/TiO2 and helps explain the catalytic trends discussed in Section .

Formation and Physicochemical Properties of Fe3O4–SA Beads

The systematic investigation of Fe3O4-sodium alginate (Fe3O4–SA) bead formation served as an initial phase in the development of a robust magnetic polymer support matrix, intended for the later integration of Cu/TiO2. To clarify the structure–property correlations governing magnetic alginate bead performance, the effects of Fe3O4 loading and bead dimensions on encapsulation efficiency, swelling behavior, bulk density, and bead morphology were assessed. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was used to determine the encapsulation efficiency (%EE) of Fe3O4 in the alginate beads; the results are presented in Table . All formulations demonstrated high encapsulation efficiencies, with %EE values spanning from 89.96 to 97.03%. Specifically, for the larger beads (B, approximately 3 mm), an encapsulation efficiency of 95.84 ± 0.21% was attained with a Fe3O4 loading of 0.5 g. Conversely, increasing the Fe3O4 content to 1.0 and 1.5 g yielded slightly reduced %EE values of 90.22 ± 8.21% and 89.96 ± 3.93%, respectively. A comparable pattern was evident for the smaller beads (S, 150–300 μm); the highest %EE (97.03 ± 8.76%) was observed at 0.5 g Fe3O4, succeeded by 93.61 ± 3.41% and 90.56 ± 3.15% at elevated loadings.

The higher encapsulation efficiency observed at lower Fe3O4 loading can be attributed to a more homogeneous dispersion of Fe3O4 nanoparticles within the alginate matrix, which promotes effective ionic cross-linking with Ca2+ ions and minimizes particle loss during bead formation and washing steps. In contrast, increasing the Fe3O4 content to 1.0–1.5 g is likely to promote nanoparticle aggregation within the polymer network, leading to the formation of locally dense domains that are less effectively cross-linked and more susceptible to partial leaching. Bead size also influenced Fe3O4 retention behavior. As shown in Table , smaller beads generally exhibited higher encapsulation efficiency, which can be attributed to their more homogeneous internal structure and improved particle entrapment during gelation. In contrast, larger beads tended to show slightly lower or comparable encapsulation efficiencies, likely due to less uniform internal cross-linking. Nevertheless, smaller beads may exhibit a marginally increased susceptibility to Fe3O4 leaching due to their higher surface exposure. Conversely, small beads offer a higher external surface area and a more uniform particle distribution, which are advantageous for mass-transfer-related applications, although they may exhibit a marginally increased susceptibility to Fe3O4 leaching. Although the highest encapsulation efficiency was achieved at a Fe3O4 loading of 0.5 g, formulation S-1.0Fe3O4–SA was selected for subsequent studies. This formulation maintains a high %EE (93.61 ± 3.41%) while providing increased Fe3O4 content, thereby enhancing magnetic responsiveness and facilitating efficient recovery. Furthermore, the reduced bead size enhances both surface accessibility and mass-transfer properties. Consequently, S-1.0Fe3O4–SA represents a thoughtful balance among encapsulation efficiency, structural integrity, and functional appropriateness.

The swelling characteristics of Fe3O4–SA beads, differentiated by varying Fe3O4 loadings, are presented in Figure a. All bead formulations demonstrated swift swelling during the initial immersion phase, followed by gradual convergence toward equilibrium; equilibrium swelling was achieved within roughly 200 min across all samples, suggesting similar swelling kinetics. The degree of swelling, however, diminished systematically as the Fe3O4 content increased. The equilibrium swelling percentages, as depicted in Figure b, were 226.88 ± 4.23% for S-0.5Fe3O4–SA, 185.32 ± 5.11% for S-1.0Fe3O4–SA, and 138.24 ± 4.85% for S-1.5Fe3O4–SA. This observation suggests that a higher Fe3O4 loading reduces the alginate network’s expansion, thereby impeding water infiltration within the bead’s architecture. Conversely, the bulk density of the Fe3O4–SA beads exhibited a progressive increase with increasing Fe3O4 content, as illustrated in Figure c. The determined bulk densities were 0.77, 0.91, and 1.01 g cm–3 for S-0.5Fe3O4–SA, S-1.0Fe3O4–SA, and S-1.5Fe3O4–SA, respectively. This inverse correlation between swelling capacity and bulk density implies that greater Fe3O4 incorporation results in a more compact internal structure, thereby decreasing the available free volume for water absorption.

These observations further reinforce the structure–property relationships discussed above, highlighting that Fe3O4 loading governs both internal bead structure and physicochemical behavior, which are critical for their performance as magnetic catalyst supports.

The particle size distribution of Fe3O4–sodium alginate (Fe3O4–SA) beads with different Fe3O4 loadings is shown in Figure a,c,e. All bead formulations exhibited a dominant size fraction of approximately 200–600 μm, indicating that bead size was primarily determined by the droplet formation process rather than by the Fe3O4 content. No significant shift in the average particle size was observed with increasing Fe3O4 loading. Any apparent size differences observed in the optical images are attributed to variations in internal structure and contrast, rather than actual changes in bead diameter.

Representative optical microscopy images of the beads are presented in Figure b,d,f. All samples exhibited a generally spherical morphology, which is characteristic of alginate beads formed via ionotropic gelation. The S–0.5Fe3O4–SA beads (Figure b) showed a relatively uniform appearance, indicating homogeneous Fe3O4 dispersion within the alginate matrix. In contrast, the S–1.0Fe3O4–SA beads (Figure d) displayed a denser internal texture, consistent with increased filler incorporation. At the highest loading, the S–1.5Fe3O4–SA beads (Figure f) exhibited darker and less uniform regions, which are indicative of localized Fe3O4 aggregation. These morphological observations are consistent with the particle size distribution results and suggest that increasing Fe3O4 loading promotes internal structural densification and heterogeneity without significantly altering the overall bead size. Importantly, these changes reflect internal structural variations and aggregation behavior, rather than a systematic increase in overall bead size.

Based on the combined results of encapsulation efficiency, swelling behavior, bulk density, particle size distribution, and morphological analysis, the S-1.0Fe3O4–SA formulation was identified as the most suitable magnetic alginate support. Compared to formulations with lower or higher Fe3O4 loadings, this formulation provides a favorable balance between even particle distribution, structural integrity, and swelling capacity, while maintaining encapsulation efficiency above 90%. Therefore, S-1.0Fe3O4–SA beads were selected as the best support matrix for subsequent addition of Cu/TiO2 components and the formation of composite beads. These characteristics make S-1.0Fe3O4–SA beads particularly suitable as magnetic supports for catalytic applications, enabling efficient magnetic recovery and reusability while maintaining structural stability, and facilitating efficient mass transfer in liquid-phase catalytic systems, which is critical for hydrogenation reactions involving polar substrates such as acetic acid.

Structural and Morphological Characterization of the Cu/TiO2–Fe3O4–SA Composite Beads

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to examine the crystalline configurations of the S-1.0Fe3O4–SA support and the S-1.0Fe3O4–SA-1.8Cu/TiO2 composite beads, which were fabricated with different Cu/TiO2 loadings (0.2, 0.4, and 0.6), as illustrated in Figure . The S-1.0Fe3O4–SA support displayed distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ ≈ 30.2°, 35.5°, 43.1°, 53.5°, 57.0°, and 62.6°, thereby validating the successful incorporation of the inverse spinel structure of magnetite (Fe3O4) within the alginate matrix.

XRD patterns of the S-1.0Fe3O4–SA support and the S-1.0Fe3O4–SA −1.8Cu/TiO2 composite beads prepared with different Cu/TiO2 loadings (0.2, 0.4, and 0.6).

Moreover, the addition of Cu/TiO2 led to the appearance of supplementary diffraction peaks linked to anatase TiO2 at 2θ ≈ 25.3°, 37.8°, 48.0°, 54.0°, 55.1°, and 62.7°. This finding suggests that the crystalline structure of TiO2 was preserved after its immobilization within the Fe3O4–SA beads. The detection of diffraction peaks from Fe3O4 and TiO2 in all composite samples substantiates the successful synthesis of the Cu/TiO2–Fe3O4–SA hybrid system. Conversely, the composite beads did not exhibit evident diffraction peaks corresponding to metallic Cu or Cu oxide phases. This observation is attributed to the comparatively low Cu content, the extensive dispersion of Cu across the TiO2 surface, or the potential existence of nanosized or amorphous Cu structures, which are likely below the detection threshold of X-ray diffraction. No significant peak shifts, intensity reductions, or phase alterations were observed in either Fe3O4 or TiO2 as the Cu/TiO2 loading was increased from 0.2 to 0.6. This suggests that the crystal structures of the support components remain stable throughout composite bead synthesis, with little change across different Cu amounts. Therefore, these findings confirm that adding Cu does not alter the crystalline structure of the Fe3O4–SA support or the TiO2 phase, thereby creating a structurally sound composite that can be evaluated for its functionality.

Based on the XRD results, the S-1.0Fe3O4/SA-1.8Cu/TiO2-0.4 composite was chosen for further microstructural and elemental analyses. This composition has a moderate Cu loading while maintaining structural stability, making it suitable for evaluating Cu dispersion and surface morphology. The surface morphology and elemental composition of the selected composite bead were examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), as shown in Figure . Catalytic Performance in the Hydrogenation of Glacial Acetic Acid

The catalytic activity of the Cu/TiO2–Fe3O4–SA composite beads was evaluated in the hydrogenation of glacial acetic acid, and product analysis was conducted by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). External calibration curves were used to determine product concentrations, and acetone was used as an internal standard to ensure analytical precision. Under the defined experimental conditions, the reaction products included ethanol, ethyl acetate, and trace amounts of propionic acid. Control experiments conducted in the absence of the catalyst showed no detectable ethanol production, confirming that product formation was catalytic rather than thermal or noncatalytic. The S-1.0Fe3O4/SA-1.8Cu/TiO2–0.4 catalyst exhibited the highest ethanol concentration (approximately 18.69 mg L–1) among the composite materials studied, while catalysts with Cu/TiO2 loadings of 0.2 and 0.6 produced lower ethanol concentrations (Table ). This observation implies that an intermediate Cu loading facilitates a more advantageous equilibrium between the availability and accessibility of active Cu sites; conversely, excessive Cu incorporation does not further improve hydrogenation performance, potentially due to aggregation or partial obstruction of surface-active sites. All catalytic experiments were performed in triplicate, demonstrating good reproducibility with minimal variation among repeated measurements.

The superior ethanol formation observed over the S–1.0Fe3O4/SA–1.8Cu/TiO2–0.4 catalyst can be attributed to an optimal balance between accessible metallic Cu0 sites and preserved support structure. Structural characterization (Sections –) confirmed well-maintained crystalline phases (XRD) and relatively homogeneous Cu dispersion without significant agglomeration (FESEM–EDS), which are expected to maximize hydrogen activation while maintaining sufficient adsorption sites for controlled hydrogenation. At lower Cu loading, insufficient metallic Cu sites limit hydrogenation activity, whereas at higher loading, partial aggregation or surface coverage effects may reduce effective site accessibility, altering the hydrogenation–esterification balance.

It should also be emphasized that the present reaction was conducted at 200 °C and 1 bar H2, which is considerably milder than the conditions reported in ref (623 K and elevated hydrogen pressure). Under such conditions, hydrogen activation and acyl hydrogenation kinetics are reduced, making product distribution more sensitive to Cu dispersion and surface properties. This explains the incomplete acetic acid conversion observed in this work, while still demonstrating a clear dependence of product selectivity on Cu loading.

Overall, the results indicate that ethanol selectivity in this composite system is governed by the relative density of metallic Cu hydrogenation sites (M*) and oxide-related adsorption/acidic sites (S*), as well as by the reaction temperature and hydrogen availability.

It is crucial to emphasize that this study focuses on catalyst formulation and the relationship between structure and performance, rather than reaction optimization. Consequently, the product concentrations were relatively low under the experimental conditions, and no attempts were made to improve conversion or selectivity. Within this context, the observed trends provide a qualitative understanding of how Cu loading and composite structure influence catalytic performance in liquid-phase hydrogenation processes.

The durability of the catalyst under the applied reaction conditions (200 °C, 1 bar H2) was evaluated through repeated catalytic cycles. The reusability of the S-1.0Fe3O4/SA-1.8Cu/TiO2-0.4 catalyst was examined over five reaction cycles. GC-MS analysis conducted after the first, third, and fifth cycles showed a gradual decrease in ethanol formation with increasing cycles, indicating partial catalyst deactivation. This behavior is attributed to surface fouling, partial Cu leaching, or blockage of active sites during repeated operation, phenomena that are commonly reported for supported Cu-based catalysts. Although detailed cycle-by-cycle quantitative data are not presented, the observed trend is consistent and reproducible across repeated experiments, confirming its practical reusability. Further investigation of regeneration and long-term stability will be considered in future work.

Conclusions

Magnetic alginate-based composite beads using Cu/TiO2 and Fe3O4 were successfully fabricated. These beads are polymer-inorganic hybrid systems that can be recovered magnetically. The ionotropic gelation process enabled effective encapsulation of inorganic components within the sodium alginate matrix while preserving the crystalline phases of Fe3O4 and anatase TiO2. Systematic variation of Fe3O4 loading showed clear structure–property relationships that affected encapsulation efficiency, swelling behavior, bulk density, and bead morphology. The S–1.0Fe3O4/SA–1.8Cu/TiO2–0.4 formulation provided a good balance between structural stability and mass-transfer accessibility. The composite beads were useful for hydrogenating glacial acetic acid as a model liquid-phase reaction. This showed how the amount of Cu in the Cu/TiO2 component affects the trends in product production without damaging the composite. In general, this study shows that alginate-based magnetic composite beads are a flexible, recoverable polymer-supported platform and that the macromolecular structure is fundamental to their operation. The results offer valuable insights for the systematic design of polymer-based composite materials intended for catalytic and analogous applications that need structural reliability and facility of recovery.
