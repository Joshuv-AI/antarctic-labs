Why do adolescent girls become pregnant? Contextual pathways to adolescent pregnancy in rural Tanzania: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Philipo Joshua Loishiye, Neema C Magoti, Shadhir Said, Joseph Frank, Miriam B Cheche, Japhet Simeo, Victor Katiti, William Nkenguye, Sia E Msuya, Edwin Joseph Shewiyo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdolescent pregnancy remains a significant public health and social challenge in sub-Saharan Africa, including Tanzania. The study aimed to explore contextual reasons and determinants of adolescent pregnancy among girls using a mixed-methods approach.DesignA convergent mixed-methods study integrating a community-based cross-sectional survey with qualitative in-depth interviews and focus group discussions. Quantitative and qualitative data were analysed separately and integrated during interpretation using triangulation and comparative joint analysis.SettingCommunity-based study conducted in Korogwe District, Tanga Region, northeastern Tanzania.ParticipantsA total of 418 adolescent girls aged 15–19 years were selected through multistage sampling for the quantitative survey. The qualitative component purposively recruited pregnant and parenting adolescents, parents/guardians and community stakeholders.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was adolescent pregnancy, defined as ever having been pregnant, including current pregnancy. Secondary variables included sociodemographic, educational, economic, relational and contraceptive factors associated with adolescent pregnancy.ResultsThe prevalence of adolescent pregnancy in Korogwe District was 16%. In multivariable analysis, older age (18–19 years), school discontinuation, marital union and economic vulnerability were independently associated with adolescent pregnancy. Current contraceptive use was more commonly reported among adolescents who had experienced pregnancy, a finding likely reflecting post pregnancy uptake rather than a protective effect, given the cross-sectional design. Qualitative findings revealed that poverty, transactional relationships, early unions, gendered power imbalances and misconceptions about contraception constrained adolescents’ reproductive autonomy. Integrated analysis demonstrated convergence between statistical associations and lived experiences, showing that economic precarity and educational limitations operated as structural pathways influencing early union formation and limiting contraceptive decision making.ConclusionsAdolescent pregnancy among girls aged 15–19 years in Korogwe District is shaped by interrelated socioeconomic, educational and gendered factors. The identified factors and contextual reasons suggest that adolescent pregnancy is a socially patterned outcome rather than solely an individual behavioural issue. Multisectoral interventions addressing school retention, economic insecurity and gender power relations are essential to reduce vulnerability.

Background

Globally, the adolescent birth rate has declined over recent decades; however, progress has been uneven across regions, and adolescent pregnancy remains a major public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.1 An estimated 21 million girls aged 15–19 years in developing regions become pregnant each year and approximately 12 million give birth, with a substantial proportion of these pregnancies unintended.2 Sub-Saharan Africa continues to experience some of the highest adolescent fertility rates worldwide, driven by early sexual initiation, limited contraceptive access, gender disparities and socioeconomic disadvantage.3 4

Adolescent pregnancy is prioritised within the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3.7, which aims to ensure universal access to sexual and reproductive health services by 2030.5 6 However, progress in many countries, including Tanzania, remains slow. Data from the Tanzania Demographic and Health Surveys (TDHS) indicate that adolescent fertility increased from 116 to 132 births per 1 000 girls aged 15–19 years between 2010 and 2015/2016.7

National estimates show that 27% of girls aged 15–19 years in mainland Tanzania have begun childbearing, compared with 8% in Zanzibar, underscoring persistent regional disparities.7 Recent multilevel analysis of Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data from 2004 to 2022 shows persistent teenage pregnancy over time and identifies both individual and community level predictors, including education, wealth status and rural residence.8 These disparities highlight the need for subnational analyses to understand local variation that may be obscured in national aggregates.

Adolescent pregnancy is shaped by intersecting individual, interpersonal and structural determinants, including early sexual debut, limited educational attainment, poverty and limited contraceptive access.379 Adolescents are disproportionately affected by unsafe abortion due to financial, legal and sociocultural barriers to safe services, with global estimates suggesting up to three million unsafe abortions annually among girls aged 15–19 years.21012 Pregnant adolescents are also more likely than adult women to seek unsafe abortions due to stigma and fear of disclosure.11 The consequences extend beyond obstetric risks to include school discontinuation, economic disadvantage and social stigma.2 11 12

In response to these challenges, Tanzania has developed national strategies under the Health Sector Strategic Plan V and related reproductive, maternal, newborn, child and adolescent health frameworks to improve adolescent-friendly health services, expand family planning access and promote school retention.13 Despite these policy initiatives, adolescent pregnancy rates remain high in many districts, including Korogwe District in Tanga Region, suggesting potential contextual, sociocultural or service-delivery gaps that are not fully captured by national analysis. In the absence of district level evidence, interventions risk remaining insufficiently aligned with locally specific drivers of adolescent pregnancy.

Although nationally representative surveys and multilevel analyses provide important prevalence estimates and identify broad predictors, they aggregate heterogeneous populations and may obscure district-specific variation and sociocultural dynamics.7 8 Studies in African settings with comparable rural and periurban profiles have highlighted the influence of transactional sex, peer norms, household dynamics and parental communication on adolescent reproductive outcomes.1417 Few studies in Tanzania integrate quantitative and qualitative evidence within a socio-ecological framework to simultaneously assess prevalence and examine multilevel contextual drivers at the district level. Without such locally grounded evidence, interventions risk remaining insufficiently aligned with contextual realities.

Korogwe District in Tanga Region represents a predominantly rural and periurban setting where adolescent pregnancy remains persistently high despite ongoing national policy efforts. Guided by a socioecological framework that considers individual, interpersonal, community and societal influences on adolescent reproductive behaviour.18 The study aimed to explore contextual reasons and determinants of adolescent pregnancy among girls using a mixed-methods approach. By combining magnitude estimation with contextual analysis, the study provides district level, context-specific, implementation relevant insights to inform more targeted and responsive adolescent sexual and reproductive health interventions.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a convergent mixed-methods cross-sectional study integrating quantitative and qualitative approaches. The quantitative component examined the prevalence and determinants of adolescent pregnancy, while the qualitative component explored contextual, interpersonal and structural influences shaping adolescent reproductive behaviour. The two strands were implemented concurrently and integrated during interpretation to provide a comprehensive understanding of findings.

The study was guided by a socioecological framework, recognising that adolescent pregnancy is shaped by multilevel influences including individual (knowledge, behaviour), interpersonal (family and peer relationships), community (norms, school environment) and structural factors (access to services, policy context).18 This framework informed variable selection in the quantitative phase and development of the qualitative topic guides.

Study site

The study was conducted in Korogwe District Council (DC), Tanga Region, northern Tanzania, among adolescent girls aged 15–19 years. Korogwe DC covers 6534 km² with 29 wards and a population of 272,870.19 In 2022, Korogwe DC reported 1154 adolescent pregnancies, the highest in the region, compared with 370 in Korogwe Town Council.20 The district economy is primarily agricultural and livestock-based, including maize, cassava, paddy cultivation and cattle and goat rearing. Tanga Region has 262 553 adolescents aged 14–19 years (130 036 girls, 132 517 boys), of whom 35 389 reside in Korogwe District.19 20

Study population

Quantitative: Adolescent girls aged 15–19 years residing in Korogwe DC at the time of the study.

Qualitative: Purposively selected participants with knowledge or experience related to adolescent pregnancy, including adolescent boys and girls, parents, teachers, village leaders, social welfare officers and the district education officer.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Quantitative

Inclusion

Girls aged 15–19 years; provided assent (15–17 years) or consent (18–19 years).

Exclusion: Adolescents whose parents/guardians did not provide consent (15–17 years) or who had not resided in the district for ≥6 months

Qualitative

Inclusion: Individuals with experience or knowledge of adolescent pregnancy.

Exclusion: Those who declined to participate.

Sample size

Quantitative: The minimum sample size was calculated using the single-population proportion formula at a 95% CI and a 5% margin of error, using a prior prevalence estimate of 21% from the UNFPA teenage pregnancy factsheet for Korogwe District.20 21 The formula applied was:

n=Z²p(1−p)/d²

Where Z = 1.96, p = 0.21, d = 0.04. This yielded a minimum sample size of 398; adding 5% for non-response gave a final sample size of 418.

Qualitative

24 participants were included to ensure diversity of perspectives. Sample size was guided by data saturation, defined as the point at which no new themes emerged across the in-depth interviews (IDIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs).22 Participants were selected purposively to capture perspectives from adolescents, parents, teachers and community leaders (table 1).

Data collection tools and procedures

Quantitative

A structured questionnaire was adapted from DHS instruments and pretested in three non-study wards (Kwagunda, Kerenge, Mombo) among five adolescents per ward to refine clarity and flow. The questionnaire (online supplemental appendix 1).

Data were collected face to face using KoBoCollect on tablets by a trained team of four investigators (PJL, NCM, SS and JF).

Qualitative

Data were collected through FGDs, IDIs, and key informant interviews (KIIs) using a single semistructured interview guide adapted from previous Tanzanian studies.23 24 The same guide was used across all qualitative data collection methods to ensure consistency across explored domains. Minor adaptations in wording and probing were made according to participant category and interview context while preserving the core thematic structure. The interview guide is provided in online supplemental appendix 2. A total of two FGDs were conducted, one with adolescents (eight participants) and one with parents and teachers combined (eight participants), each lasting 60–90 min. Four IDIs were conducted with adolescent girls who had experienced pregnancy or were currently pregnant. Four KIIs were conducted with a social welfare officer, two village executive officers and a district education officer. Data saturation guided sample adequacy. Participants were recruited through community health workers and ward/village leaders, who also helped arrange interview venues.

The interviews and FGDs were conducted by trained members of the research team (PJL, NCM, SS and VK). PJL, NCM and SS were medical doctors, while VK was a senior public health trainee with prior experience in qualitative research. Before data collection, the team received training in qualitative interviewing techniques, research ethics, confidentiality, conducting discussions on sensitive reproductive health topics and minimising interviewer bias. The researchers were familiar with the local sociocultural context of rural Tanzania, which facilitated rapport building with participants. Reflexivity was maintained through regular team discussions regarding researcher assumptions, positionality and potential influence on data collection and interpretation.

Data analysis

Quantitative

Data were analysed using SPSS for window V.27. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise data. Continuous variables were summarised using mean and SD. Categorical variables were summarised using frequency and percentages. χ2 or Fisher’s exact test was used to determine the significant differences in proportions of the outcome (adolescent pregnancy) between different groups of exposure variables. Multivariable logistic regression was used to determine the factors associated with adolescent pregnancy. ORs with their 95% CIs were used to assess the associations between adolescent pregnancy and independent variables. All statistical tests were done at a significance level of 0.05 (p<0.05).

Qualitative

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and translated into English. Thematic framework analysis was applied, following the steps of familiarisation, coding, theme development and interpretation. Coding was conducted independently by four authors (PJL, SS, MBC and VK) and reviewed by two authors (SEM and EJS) with iterative discussion to resolve discrepancies, enhancing trustworthiness and inter-coder reliability. Reflexivity was maintained through regular team discussions regarding potential biases and prior assumptions.

Trustworthiness of qualitative data

Trustworthiness was ensured using established qualitative criteria. Credibility was enhanced through triangulation of FGDs, IDIs and KIIs, supported by regular team discussions of emerging interpretations. Dependability was ensured through a detailed audit trail covering data collection, transcription, translation and analysis. Confirmability was strengthened through independent coding by multiple researchers, followed by consensus discussions to resolve discrepancies and agree on final themes. Transferability was supported through thick description of the study context, participants and procedures.

Variables

Dependent variable: Adolescent pregnancy was coded into 1 (Yes) if the participant reported to have ever been pregnant, currently pregnant or ever having given birth at the time of survey and 0 if no. This definition reflects cumulative pregnancy experience rather than incidence. Given the cross-sectional design, associations identified should not be interpreted as causal or temporally directional.

Independent variables: Several demographic characteristics of the sample population were included in the analysis as the predictors or factors of adolescent pregnancies. Age (years) modelled as a numerical variable in years and categorised further into two groups; young adolescent (15–17 years) and older adolescent (18–19 years) girls. Ever been to school was coded into (Yes) if ever attended school and (No) for those who have never gone to school. For those who had attended school, they were further categorised into (Yes) for participants still attending school and (No) for those who had left. Education level was categorised as primary and secondary/above to indicate the highest level of formal education attained. Participants were classified by their primary caregiver: both parents, single parent, and ‘others’ which could include other family members or guardians. Marital status was categorised into union if married/cohabiting and not in union for those who are not married/cohabiting. Participants’ occupations were grouped into three categories: self-employed/employed if they were employed either by government/non-governmental organisations/any other institution or established their own business/activity that enabled them to earn income, student if they were still studying, and no formal work if they had no specific source of income. On the part of who makes decisions on health; this variable was categorised based on who had the most influence on the participant’s health decisions: myself, parents, and others which could include spouses or other family members.

Reproductive characteristics include: sexually active, which was coded into (Yes) if the participant has ever engaged in sexual activity and (No) if not. Among the sexually active participants, their age at first sexual intercourse was categorised into three groups: less than 15 years, 15–17 years and 18+ years. Participants were grouped based on whether they had received family planning education at school (Yes) and (No) if never received. Currently using any family planning methods was coded into (Yes) if participants were currently using any family planning method and (No).

Consent procedures

For the quantitative phase, written informed consent was obtained from participants aged 18–19 years. Adolescents aged 15–17 years provided written assent, with written parental or guardian consent obtained prior to participation.

For the qualitative phase (IDIs and FGDs), written informed consent was obtained from adult participants. For adolescents aged 15–17 years, written parental consent and adolescent assent were secured. For participants unable to write, the consent form was read aloud in Swahili and a right thumbprint was provided in the presence of a witness.

Participation was voluntary, and participants could decline any question or withdraw at any time without consequence.

Privacy, confidentiality and safeguarding

Given the sensitive nature of sexual and reproductive health topics, interviews and discussions were conducted in private settings. Questionnaires and transcripts were anonymised using unique identifiers. Audio files and electronic data were password-protected and accessible only to the research team. In FGDs, participants were reminded to maintain confidentiality of shared information.

Interviewers received training on ethical engagement with adolescents and managing sensitive disclosures. If participants exhibited distress or disclosed coercion or abuse, interviews were paused and appropriate referral was made to nearby health facilities or district social welfare services. No financial compensation was provided.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or the dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Background characteristics of the participants

A total of 438 adolescent girls aged 15–19 years in Korogwe DC were invited to participate, and 418 agreed, giving a response rate of 95.4%. The mean age of the participants was 17 years (SD=1.4 years). The majority of the 418 participants had ever attended school (91.1%), nearly 7 out of 10 (69%) had a primary level of education, and 13% reported being married or cohabiting at the time of interview. More than half (52%) reported being raised by both parents. Other characteristics are shown in table 2.

Reproductive characteristics

A third (33%) of the 418 adolescents were sexually active. The median age of sexual debut was 15 years (IQR 14-17). Knowledge of the menstrual cycle and the fertile period was low: only 38% of participants correctly identified that unprotected sex during the fertile period can result in pregnancy (table 3). The most widely known family planning methods are condoms (88.1%), pills (88%), and injectables/DEPO (82.2%). Less recognised methods included female sterilisation (27.9%), male sterilisation (14.1%), with only 5.6% of respondents aware of other methods (figure 1).

Prevalence of adolescent pregnancy

The prevalence of adolescent pregnancies was 16% (68 participants). Among the adolescents, 15 (22%) girls were pregnant at the time of the study, of which 7 of them reported the pregnancy was unwanted. 8 (12%) of the adolescents reported being able to continue with school. When the adolescents who had ever given birth or were pregnant were asked about their parents’/guardians’ reactions, 41 (60%) reported that they just accepted the pregnancy and said nothing, 21 (31%) reported that the parents/guardians were angry and scolded them and 6 (9%) reported that the parents/guardians forced them to marry or live with a person who was responsible for the pregnancy.

Factors associated with adolescent pregnancy

Based on the multivariable logistic regression, age, never attended school, school discontinuation, marital status, occupation and family planning use were significantly associated with adolescent pregnancies (table 4). Older adolescents (18–19 years) had 6.5 times higher odds of pregnancy compared with younger adolescents (15–17 years) (adjusted OR (aOR) 6.5, 95% CI 2.2 to 19.8, p<0.001). Adolescent girls who had never attended school had 6.7 times higher odds of pregnancy compared with those who had ever attended school (aOR 6.72, 95% CI 2.13 to 21.16, p<0.001). Girls who had dropped out of school had 18.7 times higher odds of pregnancy compared with those still in school (aOR 18.7, 95% CI 3.2 to 109.1, p<0.001). Girls not in a marital union had 96% lower odds of pregnancy compared with those in union (aOR 0.04, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.13, p<0.001). Girls who were students had 97% lower odds of pregnancy compared with employed adolescents (aOR 0.03, 95% CI 0.004 to 0.23, p<0.001). Girls not currently using any family planning method had 79% lower odds of pregnancy compared with those currently using any method (aOR 0.21, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.66, p=0.007).

Qualitative findings

Participant characteristics

A total of 24 participants were included in the qualitative component: 12 adolescents (aged 15–19 years) and 12 adult stakeholders, including parents, teachers, village leaders, social welfare officers and district education officials. Adolescents were evenly represented by sex. All adolescents were single, adult participants were married. Most adolescents reported having no personal income, whereas all adult participants reported a regular source of income.

Themes influencing adolescent pregnancy

Five major themes were identified through thematic analysis: (1) poverty and economic vulnerability, (2) perceptions of family planning, (3) traditions and social norms, (4) education and knowledge gaps and (5) parental communication and guidance.

Poverty and Economic Vulnerability

Across IDIs, KIIs and FGDs, participants consistently identified poverty as a major contributing factor to adolescent pregnancy, reflected in the following subthemes:

lack of basic needs

Economic hardship was consistently described as a key contextual factor associated with adolescent pregnancy. Participants reported that inability to meet basic needs particularly food, clothing and school related expenses exposed adolescents to transactional relationships.

“When parents cannot provide basic needs, girls look for other ways to get support. Sometimes the people who help them expect sexual relationships.” (Male adolescent, FGD)

School related expenses, including meals and transport, were frequently mentioned as contributing pressures.

“Some parents do not have even small money to give their daughter for food at school. Someone else gives her money, and later she is asked for sex.” (Male parent, FGD)

school access and transport barriers

Long travel distances to school were also perceived to increase vulnerability. Participants described how reliance on motorcycle transport created opportunities for coercive or exploitative relationships.

“Students travel long distances to school. Some drivers take advantage of that and start relationships with them.” (Female key informant)

adolescents as economic assets

Some adult participants described early marriage as a strategy used by families facing financial hardship.

“In some families, girls are married early so the family can receive wealth.” (Female teacher, FGD)

These findings suggest that structural poverty operates at household and community levels to shape adolescent vulnerability.

Perceptions of Family Planning

Negative perceptions and misconceptions regarding contraception emerged as a major theme.

contraception as inappropriate for adolescents

Many participants believed that contraceptives are intended only for married women or those who have already given birth.

“Birth control is for married people. If someone has not given birth, why should she use it?” (Male community member, FGD)

Adolescents similarly expressed reluctance:

“I have never used any method. I am not interested in them.” (Female adolescent, IDI)

fear of side effects

Fear of infertility and reproductive harm was widely reported.

“People say contraceptives destroy eggs and later you cannot give birth.” (Female adolescent, IDI)

These beliefs appeared to contribute to low uptake of modern contraception.

religious influences

While participants did not explicitly attribute adolescent pregnancy to religion, some described contraceptive use as conflicting with religious teachings.

“God created us to reproduce. Using family planning can feel like going against His will.” (Female adolescent, IDI)

Overall, misconceptions and sociocultural resistance toward contraception were perceived to limit preventive behaviours.

Traditions and Social Norms

Participants described traditional practices and prevailing community norms as shaping expectations around sexuality and marriage.

“Traditional teachings prepare girls for marriage. After that, some believe they are ready.” (Female key informant)

initiation rites

Initiation ceremonies were described as marking transition into perceived adulthood, sometimes reinforcing readiness for sexual relationships.

“After initiation, some girls feel they are mature and can start relationships.” (Female adolescent, IDI)

cultural ceremonies and dances

Traditional dances were described as environments where adolescents interact with limited supervision. The majority of participants declared a famous traditional dance style ‘Vigodoro’ having themes that make adolescents involved in sexual intercourse which is often unprotected and sometimes without consent.

“During traditional dances (Vigodoro), themes encourage relationships between boys and girls.” (Male adolescent, FGD)

These cultural contexts were perceived as normalising early sexual engagement.

Education and Knowledge Gaps

low community education levels

Participants linked limited formal education to reduced awareness of reproductive consequences.

“Low education in the community means many do not understand the risks of early pregnancy.” (Male community leader, FGD)

limited sexual and reproductive health knowledge

Although some health education efforts were reported, adolescents described limited understanding of contraceptive methods and fertility awareness.

“I do not know much about contraceptives.” (Female adolescent, IDI)

others highlighted gaps in understanding menstrual cycles

“If someone does not understand her cycle, she may become pregnant unintentionally.” (Male adolescent, FGD)

These findings suggest that educational limitations operate at both individual and community levels.

Parental Communication and Guidance

Limited open communication between parents and adolescents regarding sexuality was described as a barrier to informed decision-making. Cultural discomfort surrounding discussions of sexual health contributed to adolescents relying on peers for information, which was often perceived as inaccurate.

“Most of our parents are not friendly to us… once they hear about family planning methods, they tell you, ‘Go with your family planning methods because you don’t want to have children at all.” (Pregnant adolescent, IDI)

a key informant similarly observed

“Many parents do not have time with their children… These children then get information from outside, from non-official people, which leads them into situations like pregnancy.” (Female key informant, KI).

These accounts suggest that restricted parental guidance may increase adolescents’ vulnerability to early pregnancy.

Discussion

This mixed-methods study demonstrates that adolescent pregnancy in Korogwe District is shaped by structural factors rather than individual behaviour alone. Poverty, school discontinuation, early union formation and limited contraceptive autonomy were statistically associated with pregnancy, while qualitative evidence revealed how these factors interact within constrained opportunity structures shaped by economic precarity, gendered power dynamics and normative transitions to adulthood. Integrated analysis showed that economic insecurity and educational disruption operated as structural pathways that sequentially narrowed reproductive choice, a pattern consistent with the socioecological framework guiding the study.

Interpreting structural gradients

Large scale analyses from the DHS consistently demonstrate wealth and education gradients in adolescent fertility across sub-Saharan Africa.2527 Multilevel modelling studies further show that community-level deprivation and normative environments independently predict early pregnancy.8 28 Our integrated findings suggest that poverty functions not merely as a background characteristic but as an active constraint. Economic insecurity constrained educational opportunities; reduced engagement with schooling limited exposure to institutional protective structures; diminished opportunity increased susceptibility to transactional partnerships; and entrenched gender norms constrained contraceptive negotiation. In this sequence, adolescent pregnancy emerges as a socially patterned outcome of cumulative disadvantage.

The attenuation of school-based family planning education after adjustment likely reflects structural mediation rather than absence of effect. Adolescents most at risk were often those already disconnected from formal education systems, underscoring the limitation of interventions delivered primarily through schools. This pattern aligns with evidence that education operates as a structural determinant of reproductive agency rather than solely as an information platform.29

Predictors such as marriage and occupation require cautious interpretation. In cross sectional analyses, these may reflect adaptive responses following pregnancy rather than antecedent causes.30 Failure to recognise this sequencing risks overstating behavioural causality and underestimating structural drivers.

Culture, gender and normative governance

Culture in this setting operates as a normative governance system shaping acceptable transitions to adulthood. Fertility confers social legitimacy, particularly once formal educational trajectories are interrupted. Early unions were frequently described as protective or stabilising responses to economic uncertainty rather than solely coercive arrangements.

Gendered power asymmetries further constrained contraceptive autonomy, particularly in age disparate relationships where decision-making power was often skewed towards male partners.31 32 These norms did not operate in isolation but were reinforced by socioeconomic deprivation, which limited adolescents’ capacity to negotiate safe reproductive choices.

This framing cautions against reductionist cultural explanations of adolescent pregnancy. Norms are neither static nor isolated, they are embedded within broader political economy conditions that shape constrained choice.

Comparison with other studies

The prevalence of 16% observed in Korogwe District is lower than the 27% national estimate for mainland Tanzania,7 but consistent with district level variation reported in other Tanzanian and East African settings.8 14 The association between school discontinuation and pregnancy has been widely documented in sub-Saharan Africa,3 27 28 and the qualitative findings from this study extend existing evidence by describing the mechanisms through which school discontinuation and adolescent pregnancy are interconnected through economic vulnerability, reduced future opportunities and social transitions into early adulthood, rather than through individual decision-making alone. The role of transactional relationships, identified qualitatively in this study, is consistent with systematic evidence from the region.16 31

The observed association between current contraceptive use and adolescent pregnancy should be interpreted with caution. Given the cross-sectional design, temporality cannot be established. The higher likelihood of contraceptive use among adolescents who had experienced pregnancy does not suggest reduced contraceptive effectiveness or a causal relationship between contraceptive use and pregnancy. Instead, it most plausibly reflects post pregnancy initiation of contraception following contact with reproductive health services, including postpartum family planning uptake, as reported in other cross-sectional studies in the region.8 15 This pattern is best explained by reverse causality rather than contraceptive failure.

Implications for global health systems and policy

The findings highlight the limitations of policy approaches focused solely on contraceptive access or health education. Adolescents most vulnerable to pregnancy are often excluded from the institutional platforms that deliver these interventions. Prevention strategies therefore need to be upstream, intersectoral and structural. First, school reentry and retention policies should include financial support mechanisms and district level enforcement to prevent informal exclusion. Second, integration of social protection measures such as conditional cash transfers or household economic support may reduce adolescents’ reliance on transactional relationships. Third, adolescent-responsive health services should explicitly address power asymmetries within relationships rather than assuming autonomous contraceptive negotiation.

These strategies align with SDG 3.7 (universal access to sexual and reproductive health) and SDG 5 (gender equality and empowerment of women and girls).33 Coordination across health, education and social welfare sectors is critical; without structural alignment, behaviourally focused interventions will yield limited impact.

Strengths and limitations

This study benefits from a convergent mixed methods design that enabled triangulation of quantitative associations with qualitative insights, strengthening the validity and interpretative depth of the findings. The use of multistage sampling enhanced representativeness within the district. Inclusion of both in-school and out-of-school adolescents provided a broader community perspective, reducing the risk of underestimating pregnancy-related vulnerabilities.

However, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and assessment of temporality; some factors identified as associated with pregnancy may reflect consequences rather than antecedent determinants. Additionally, reliance on self-reported pregnancy history and contraceptive use may be affected by recall and social desirability bias, particularly given the sensitivity of reproductive health topics among adolescents. The study was conducted in a single district, which limits generalisability to other settings.

Future research

Longitudinal designs are required to disentangle causal sequencing between school discontinuation, partnership formation, and pregnancy. Multilevel analyses incorporating partner characteristics and community normative indices would further clarify structural drivers. Implementation research is needed to evaluate how national adolescent reproductive health strategies function at district level and to identify bottlenecks in intersectoral coordination.

Conclusions

Adolescent pregnancy in Korogwe District remains a significant public health and social concern, affecting nearly one in six adolescent girls aged 15–19 years, and is driven by intersecting structural and social vulnerabilities. Older age, school discontinuation, marital union, and economic hardship were independently associated with adolescent pregnancy. Current contraceptive use was more commonly reported among adolescents who had experienced pregnancy. However, this association should be interpreted cautiously given the cross-sectional design, which precludes assessment of temporality. The finding likely reflects postpregnancy uptake of contraception following contact with reproductive or postpartum care services, rather than a causal or protective effect of contraceptive use.

Qualitative findings further revealed how poverty, gendered power asymmetries, cultural expectations and misconceptions about contraceptives constrain adolescents’ reproductive autonomy.

These findings indicate that adolescent pregnancy is not merely a consequence of individual behaviour, but a socially patterned outcome shaped by structural disadvantage. Interventions limited to information provision and contraceptive access are unlikely to be sufficient without parallel investments in educational retention, economic empowerment, and transformation of harmful gender norms. Progress toward SDG 3.7 and SDG 5 will require integrated, multisectoral strategies that strengthen adolescent agency while addressing the socioeconomic conditions that shape reproductive decision-making.
