Dataset on student engagement in teaching evaluations at a private university in Vietnam
Authors: Tran Trong Huynh, Nguyen Thi Kieu An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article presents an openly accessible survey dataset on student engagement in teaching evaluations collected from undergraduate students at a private university in Vietnam. The dataset was collected through an online questionnaire administered to 362 undergraduate students enrolled in software engineering and business programs at a private university. The original instrument contained 30 items designed to measure organizational identification, institutional trust, perceived usefulness of student evaluations, process clarity, constructive evaluation behavior, and self-perceived evaluation bias. Following reliability and validity assessment, a validated five-factor measurement structure comprising 15 retained indicators was established. Data quality was evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis, reliability assessment, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and robustness checks. The resulting measurement model demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties, with standardized factor loadings ranging from 0.814 to 0.945, Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.866 to 0.931, and average variance extracted values exceeding recommended thresholds. The repository includes the anonymized dataset, questionnaire, codebook, reproducibility code, and public documentation. The dataset may support research on student evaluation of teaching, student engagement in higher education, institutional trust, organizational identification, educational measurement, and structural equation modeling applications. It may also facilitate replication studies and comparative research across higher education systems.

Background

Student evaluation of teaching (SET) is widely used in higher education to support instructional improvement, quality assurance, and academic decision-making [1,2]. Despite its widespread adoption, concerns remain regarding the quality and usefulness of evaluation data. Previous studies have primarily focused on potential biases in student evaluations, including the effects of expected grades, course characteristics, instructor attributes, and demographic factors [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Comparatively less attention has been devoted to understanding the conditions under which students engage meaningfully in evaluation processes.

The usefulness of SET depends not only on evaluation instruments but also on students’ willingness to provide thoughtful and constructive feedback [7,8]. Prior research suggests that students are more likely to participate seriously when they perceive evaluations as meaningful and believe their feedback will contribute to teaching improvement [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Institutional and psychological factors may therefore play an important role in shaping evaluation-related behaviors. In particular, organizational identification and institutional trust have been identified as factors that may influence students’ perceptions of institutional processes and their engagement with feedback systems [[13], [14], [15], [16]].

The present dataset was compiled to support research on these issues and to provide a validated survey instrument for future educational and behavioral research.

Data Description

The dataset contains survey responses collected from 362 undergraduate students enrolled in software engineering and business programs at a private university in Vietnam. Data were collected through an online questionnaire administered via Google Forms between February and March 2026. The survey was designed to examine institutional and psychological factors associated with student engagement in teaching evaluation processes. The original questionnaire consisted of 30 items distributed across six conceptual domains: organizational identification, institutional trust, perceived usefulness of student evaluations, process clarity, constructive evaluation behavior, and self-perceived evaluation bias. All items were measured using five-point Likert scales ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The repository includes the anonymized dataset, questionnaire, codebook, and reproducibility code. Although the original questionnaire included a process clarity domain, its indicators were not retained in the final validated measurement structure following reliability and validity assessment. The complete 30-item instrument remains available in the repository to support future replication and measurement development studies.

Following reliability and validity assessment, a validated five-factor measurement structure comprising 15 retained indicators was established. The final measurement model includes organizational identification, institutional trust, perceived usefulness of student evaluations, constructive evaluation behavior, and self-perceived evaluation bias. The retained indicators demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties and were subsequently used for measurement validation and structural analyses. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Research design. This dataset was generated through a cross-sectional survey designed to examine student engagement in teaching evaluations within a higher education context. The survey focused on institutional and psychological factors associated with students' participation in Student Evaluation of Teaching systems. The original questionnaire comprised six conceptual domains: organizational identification, institutional trust, perceived usefulness of student evaluations, process clarity, constructive evaluation behavior, and self-perceived evaluation bias. All survey items were measured using five-point Likert scales ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

Sample and data collection. Data were collected between February and March 2026 using an anonymous online questionnaire administered through Google Forms. The target population comprised undergraduate students enrolled in the Software Engineering and Business programs at a private university in Vietnam, representing approximately 1,000 students. The survey adopted an attempted census of the target population, in which all students attending scheduled classes during the data collection period were invited to participate. Students accessed the questionnaire by scanning a QR code displayed in class and completed the survey voluntarily using their own devices. Students who were absent or declined participation were not included in the sample.

A total of 362 valid questionnaires were retained after screening for completeness and duplicate submissions. No personally identifiable information was collected, and all responses were anonymized before analysis. Fig. 1 presents the demographic profile of the respondents.

Questionnaire development. The questionnaire was developed by adapting measurement items from established studies on organizational identification, institutional trust, student evaluation of teaching, and evaluation-related behavior. To facilitate respondents and preserve consistency with the original scales, the questionnaire was presented in both English and Vietnamese.

The draft questionnaire was reviewed by faculty members and the research team to evaluate the clarity, wording, and contextual appropriateness of the items for the Vietnamese higher education environment. A pilot survey involving approximately 60 undergraduate students from two classes was subsequently conducted. Feedback from the pilot test was used to improve item wording and questionnaire clarity before the main survey. Responses collected during the pilot phase were retained in the final dataset because the measurement instrument remained substantively unchanged.

Data preprocessing. Raw questionnaire responses were exported from Google Forms and screened before analysis. Records were checked for duplicate submissions, response consistency, and coding accuracy. Questionnaire responses were then anonymized and exported to Microsoft Excel before being imported into R for reliability assessment, measurement validation, and subsequent statistical analyses. The final dataset contained 362 valid observations.

Measurement instrument. The questionnaire was developed to measure six conceptual domains related to student engagement in Student Evaluation of Teaching. Organizational identification measures students' sense of belonging and attachment to their university (e.g., “I feel proud to be a student at this university.”). Institutional trust reflects students' confidence in how the university manages and utilizes teaching evaluation results (e.g., “I trust that the university uses student evaluations responsibly.”). Perceived usefulness of student evaluations measures students' beliefs that teaching evaluations contribute to improving teaching quality and educational outcomes (e.g., “Completing course evaluations helps improve teaching quality.”). Process clarity assesses students' perceptions of whether the evaluation process is transparent, understandable, and easy to follow. Constructive evaluation behavior measures the extent to which students provide careful and thoughtful evaluation responses (e.g., “I carefully read each question before responding.”). Self-perceived evaluation bias captures students' awareness that their evaluation responses may be influenced by subjective factors such as expected grades, personal preferences, or course difficulty (e.g., “My expected grade influences how I evaluate an instructor.”).

The original questionnaire consisted of 30 items adapted from established literature. Following reliability and validity assessment, the final validated measurement model retained 15 indicators representing five latent constructs. The process clarity construct was excluded because its indicators did not satisfy the predefined reliability and validity criteria. The public repository includes the bilingual questionnaire (PDF and editable formats) together with a variable codebook documenting variable names, coding schemes, and construct definitions.

Data validation and reproducibility. Data preparation and statistical analyses were conducted using R. The file analysis_reproducibility.R contains procedures for data cleaning, reliability assessment, confirmatory factor analysis, validity assessment, robustness checks, and structural equation modeling. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability. Convergent validity was assessed using average variance extracted and standardized factor loadings obtained from confirmatory factor analysis. Discriminant validity was examined using the Fornell–Larcker criterion. Additional procedures included sensitivity analyses that excluded potential straight-line responses and a single-factor confirmatory factor analysis to assess potential common method bias. Limitations

The dataset was collected from undergraduate students at a single private university in Vietnam. Consequently, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other higher education institutions or national contexts. In addition, the data are based on self-reported survey responses and were collected using a cross-sectional design. Although the dataset includes validated measures and extensive documentation, causal inferences cannot be drawn from the data alone.

Ethics Statement

This study involved human participants (undergraduate students aged 18 years or older). Participation in the survey was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to completing the online questionnaire. The survey was conducted anonymously, and no personally identifiable information was collected. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All responses were collected for academic research purposes only and were analyzed in aggregated form. The authors’ institution does not maintain a formal Institutional Review Board (IRB) or ethics committee for social science research. Under institutional guidelines, anonymous voluntary surveys that do not involve sensitive personal information, clinical procedures, or experimental interventions do not require formal ethical approval. Consequently, no ethics approval number or protocol number was assigned to this study.
