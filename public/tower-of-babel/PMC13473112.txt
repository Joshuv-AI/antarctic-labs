Research on the decision-making of high-tech manufacturing enterprises for energy conservation and emission reduction driven by industrial green microgrids under the green credit regulatory system
Authors: Hao Qin, Xiao Zhong, Maidina Aihemaiti, Haiwei Zhang, Zhongping Cui, Yuanlei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnder the circumstances where the green credit regulatory system has been implemented and the large-scale application of industrial green microgrids has occurred, the institutional constraints and clean energy technologies jointly reshape the emission reduction choices of high-tech manufacturing enterprises. This study constructs an evolutionary game model involving the government, research and development institutions, and high-tech manufacturing enterprises. Through this model, the stable equilibrium of the system’s evolution is sought, and the evolution patterns of key parameters are explored through numerical simulation. The conclusions are as follows: (1) The various input costs of the three parties constitute the threshold for emission reduction costs. The increase in costs significantly hinders green behaviors such as supervision, research and development, and active emission reduction. (2) Market-based benefits serve as the intrinsic driving force for collaborative emission reduction. The increase in technology transfer benefits and the improvement in enterprises’ green operation benefits can lead to a spontaneous shift towards a low-carbon economy. (3) The government’s policy tools exhibit differentiated effects, and research subsidies facilitate the breakthroughs in green microgrids technology. Excessive rewards can lead to a policy-dependent situation within the enterprise. Appropriate fines can rely on green credit constraints to compel enterprises to comply with emission reduction regulations. This study fills the analytical gap regarding the technical supply side in multi-party collaborative emission reduction. The conclusions provide a theoretical basis and practical reference for improving the collaborative emission reduction system, promoting the green transformation of high-tech manufacturing enterprises, and facilitating regional green and low-carbon development.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental social sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

With global warming, resource depletion, and environmental degradation, a low-carbon transition has become a global consensus1. The green transformation of the industrial sector, particularly high-tech manufacturing enterprises, is crucial for countries to achieve their goals of efficient energy conservation and emission reduction2. According to the International Energy Agency, the industrial sector accounts for over one-third of global final energy consumption. In the industrial sector, compared with other manufacturing enterprises, high-tech manufacturing enterprises are the fastest-growing sector and also the one with the most significant increase in energy consumption and emissions. During the production process, such enterprises have higher requirements for power supply quality and a clean environment. And their products are deeply integrated into the global supply chain. The carbon footprint directly affects export competitiveness and the cost of complying with international regulations. This underscores the critical importance of fostering green development among high-tech manufacturing enterprises3. Although high-tech manufacturing enterprises are often labeled "low-pollution, high-value-added", the industry’s scale expansion and deepening division of labor within the global industrial chain have increasingly highlighted energy consumption and emissions issues. Consequently, the strategic urgency of energy conservation and emission reduction continues to rise4. Against this backdrop, the government is compelling enterprises to reduce emissions through policies such as green credit regulation. The Guidelines for the Construction and Application of Industrial Green Microgrids (2026–2030) also explicitly designate industrial green microgrids as a key vehicle for energy conservation and carbon reduction. High-tech manufacturing enterprises serve as the core implementers of this technology, whose deployment directly supports companies in reducing carbon emissions at the production end and represents a key lever for achieving corporate emission reduction targets. No single entity can successfully implement such technologies or achieve emission-reduction outcomes. Tripartite collaborative decision-making among governments, research institutions, and high-tech manufacturing enterprises has become the core mechanism. For instance, BYD, as a typical high-tech manufacturing enterprise, has accelerated the achievement of its energy conservation and emission reduction goals through green innovation. Leveraging distributed photovoltaic and new energy storage technologies to build industrial green microgrids, the company has benefited from local government initiatives such as vehicle purchase subsidies and dual-credit policies, as well as technological support from R&D institutions5.

The green credit regulatory system represents an iterative upgrade of the government’s green information disclosure framework, serving as an incentive-compatible institutional innovation grounded in a relational contract network involving multiple stakeholders. Its core function lies in linking the environmental performance of high-tech manufacturing enterprises to financing costs and policy support6, imposing hard constraints, such as financing restrictions on energy-intensive enterprises, while offering soft incentives, such as policy preferences for green, emission-reducing enterprises7. The green credit regulatory system aims to guide enterprises from passive “regulatory compliance” toward proactive "green value creation", while also serving as a crucial tool for governments to regulate the environmental conduct of market entities. Industrial green microgrids serve as core supporting infrastructure for corporate green emissions reduction. Their value lies in integrating clean energy to lower corporate carbon emissions, reduce reliance on high-carbon energy sources, and enhance energy supply stability and utilization efficiency8. Industrial green microgrids provides a critical practical pathway for enterprises to gain green credit advantages, thereby fully realizing the incentive and regulatory effectiveness of the green credit regulatory system. The synergistic effects of the green credit regulatory system and industrial green microgrids are jointly driving energy conservation and emission reduction in high-tech manufacturing.

The green credit regulatory system mainly influences technological research decisions through policy guidance and expected returns. Su et al. conducted an analysis of the optimal contract design for green credit regulation from the perspective of the bilateral relationship between the government and enterprises9. Gao et al. explored the mechanism by which the credit system enables the green transformation of the manufacturing industry through a dual-subject research framework10. Wu et al. studied the challenges in the construction of the integrated energy market and policy adaptation through focusing on the market systems design11. In conclusion, the existing studies are mostly limited to the bilateral game between the government and enterprises, lacking the analysis of the three-party interaction involving research and development institutions, and ignoring the intermediary transmission effect of research and development transformation. Previous studies have explored the multiple effects of various measures on the construction and operation of industrial green microgrids from aspects such as technical architecture, scheduling optimization, and benefit calculation. Gutiérrez-Oliva et al. defined the microgrids technical architecture from the perspective of technical characteristics12. Chen et al. designed a low-carbon dispatching strategy for microgrids13, but they disconnected the connection between policy systems and technological applications. Rajamand modeled and calculated the cost reduction effect of demand response through technical and economic analysis14. In conclusion, the current studies fail to clarify the transmission path through which policy promotes research innovation and enables the implementation of results to enterprises empower microgrids for emission reduction, and it also lacks the analysis of multi-party interest linkage and the coordination of institutional and technical aspects. High-tech manufacturing enterprises possess the characteristics of energy conservation and emission reduction as well as resource utilization efficiency, and are also influenced by various complex factors. Zhang et al. empirically examined the emission reduction effect of high-tech industrial clusters15. Sun et al. explored the path of enterprise innovation16. Wang et al. calculated the contribution of innovation efficiency to emission reduction17. All three adopted static measurement models, neither integrating the dual variables of green credit and microgrid, nor capable of depicting the long-term dynamic evolution of the parties. In conclusion, the existing research mainly focuses on static analysis, lacking the adjustment processes of each party over time as well as the influence of key parameters. Based on the existing studies results, the innovation points of this study lie in: First, this study expands the scope of the game participants and constructs an evolutionary game model involving the government, R&D institutions, and high-tech manufacturing enterprises. This addition provides a strategy interaction analysis for the technical supply parties. Second, this study conducts a study on the coupling of systems and technologies, integrating green credit regulation and industrial green microgrids into the same analytical framework, and revealing the internal mechanism of enterprises’ emission reduction under the collaborative effect of the two. Third, this study conducts multi-parameter sensitivity analysis through numerical simulation, dynamically depicting the influence patterns of variables such as cost, benefit, rewards, and punishments on the evolution of the three-party decision-making process.

Based on this, this study explores the following question: How do green credit regulations and industrial green microgrids influence energy conservation and emission-reduction decisions in high-tech manufacturing enterprises? What are the dynamic evolutionary patterns of strategic interactions among these three elements? Can the introduction of industrial green microgrids optimize this synergistic effect? To address these issues, this study constructs a tripartite evolutionary game model involving governments, R&D institutions, and high-tech manufacturing enterprises. By analyzing the stable equilibrium conditions for strategic choices among entities and validating the impact effects of key parameters through numerical simulations, it systematically reveals the intrinsic logic of corporate energy conservation and emissions reduction under the green credit regulatory system and R&D support. This study provides theoretical foundations and practical references for improving collaboration among innovation entities, advancing the green transformation of high-tech manufacturing enterprises, and facilitating regional green and low-carbon emissions reduction.

Literature review

Research on the interaction between green credit regulatory system and multi-entity strategies

The green credit regulatory system is a mechanism whereby governments evaluate the creditworthiness of green behaviors based on entity-specific green conduct information, using prescribed indicators, methodologies, and procedures. It assigns credit ratings and publicly discloses them for use by relevant departments, institutions, and organizations, with multi-entity participation18. Su et al. argued that the green credit regulatory system aims to create an environment conducive to incentives and disincentives under low information thresholds, thereby seeking optimal contractual arrangements9. Building upon this, Gao and Ma further clarified that the system integrates market entities’ environmental behaviors into the credit evaluation system, combining incentive and constraint mechanisms to guide entities toward decisions aligned with energy conservation and emission reduction objectives10. Furthermore, Lv et al. contended that this system is essentially a Chinese-style green institution grounded in laws and regulations, focused on information sharing and application19, and aimed at consolidating the foundations of a modern market. The green credit regulatory system represents an incentive-compatible institutional innovation based on a relational contractual network involving multiple entities.

For R&D institutions, green credit regulation primarily influences their technological R&D decisions through two key aspects: policy guidance and profit expectations. On the one hand, Chen et al. noted that governments promote technological innovation by incorporating the green technology conversion efficiency of R&D institutions into credit evaluations20. Simultaneously, Tang et al. noted that governments also provide subsidies, rewards, and credit bonuses to R&D institutions that contribute to corporate energy conservation and emissions reduction21. These incentive policies guides R&D institutions to focus on areas such as low-carbon technologies and energy-saving processes urgently needed by high-tech manufacturing enterprises. On the other hand, R&D institutions must make decisions based on market demand and the costs of technological development. With the intensification of green credit regulation and the growing demand for emissions reduction among high-tech manufacturing enterprises, Peng and Zhang argued that R&D institutions should increase investment in industry-academia-research collaboration and shorten technology transfer cycles22.

For high-tech manufacturing enterprises, the core of decision-making lies in balancing compliance costs against credit gains. Multiple studies by Tian et al. indicated that the intensity of green credit regulation exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with corporate emission reduction costs23. Moderate regulatory pressure encourages voluntary emission reductions by enterprises. However, Lin et al. found that overly stringent regulation causes firms to prioritize short-term compliance over long-term technological innovation24. Meanwhile, the transparency of credit evaluations can also influence corporate decision-making. Lin and Pan proposed that a transparent evaluation system can reduce firms’ information costs associated with compliance and enhance their willingness to participate in green transformation25. For instance, synergies between digital finance and green credit can increase corporate transparency of environmental information by over 30%, significantly improving decision-making efficiency.

Research on the technical characteristics and emission reduction mechanisms of industrial green microgrids

As a key energy supply model supporting the green transformation of industry, the definition and technical characteristics of industrial green microgrids have become a focal point of academic attention. Gutiérrez-Oliva et al. explicitly stated that industrial green microgrids are microgrids within industrial sites or parks whose primary power supply originates from low-carbon sources, constituting a vital component of developing industrial green infrastructure12. Mehrabadi and Sathiakumar emphasized that industrial green microgrid technology, by integrating distributed power sources, energy storage devices, and energy conversion equipment into compact generation and distribution system, enables both grid-connected and standalone operation. It facilitates the development of green, low-carbon, and high-quality energy, playing a crucial role in advancing the green and low-carbon transition26. Green microgrids simultaneously fulfill multiple demands, including power supply, heating, cooling, humidity control, and domestic water use, thereby enhancing energy utilization efficiency. Furthermore, Kaur et al. noted that integrating energy storage units facilitates internal energy regulation within the system, enabling efficient and rational energy utilization27.

Industrial green microgrids serve as key vehicles for reducing industrial emissions, and their mechanisms have drawn significant academic attention. To achieve carbon peak and carbon neutrality goals28, Chen et al. conducted extensive research on low-carbon dispatch and operation of the power system. Existing scholars found that applying low-carbon integrated energy microgrid models in the power sector not only reduces system carbon emissions more effectively but also enhances energy utilization efficiency13. Bustamante et al. indicated that microgrids integrate distributed generation, energy storage systems, controllable loads, and, often, combined heat and power units. Microgrids can operate in both grid-connected and islanded modes, enhancing resilience, energy autonomy, and operational flexibility29. Xi et al. further noted that, driven by the global carbon neutrality goal, industrial green microgrids (IGMs) have become a key approach to accelerating the low-carbon transformation of the industrial sector30.

Research on driving factors and dynamic evolution of energy conservation and emission reduction in high-tech manufacturing enterprises

High-tech manufacturing enterprises are companies that rely on advanced technology as their core driver, engaging in research, development, design, and production of high-tech products. Energy conservation, reduced emissions, and efficient resource use characterize them. Zhang et al. constructed a panel threshold econometric model. Existing scholars found that high-tech enterprises exhibit green-industry characteristics in terms of resource conservation and environmental protection, and that their clustering has a significant impact on the development of the green economy15. Wang et al. proposed that high-tech manufacturing enterprises utilize advanced technologies for development and production. Due to their substantial R&D investments, high value-added nature, favorable international market prospects, and profound influence on other industries, high-tech manufacturing enterprises have become a critical domain in international economic and technological competition31. Meanwhile, Sun et al. found that, as key drivers of corporate transformation and upgrading, high-tech enterprises possess strong innovation capabilities and high levels of innovation. High-tech manufacturing enterprises serve as vital pillars of national economic development and can lead to national innovation and progress16. Additionally, Chen et al. noted that high-tech manufacturing enterprises possess robust research and development capabilities and technological reserves, enabling them to achieve innovations that enhance resource utilization efficiency and reduce environmental pollution32.

A complex interplay of multiple factors is influencing Energy conservation and emission reduction efforts in high-tech manufacturing enterprises, encompassing both internal technological and managerial capabilities and external regional characteristics and their positions within the global value chain. Wang et al. employed the DEA-Malmquist index model and panel econometric models to analyze that innovation efficiency in high-tech industries significantly contributes to carbon emission reduction, with one mechanism being the optimization of energy consumption structure17. Wei et al. argued that optimizing the energy consumption structure is a crucial mechanism for high-tech manufacturing enterprises to achieve carbon emission reductions, with the energy-saving bias of technological progress being key to lowering energy intensity33. Meanwhile, Wang et al. established an evaluation framework based on the DEA method. Existing scholars found that China’s high-tech manufacturing enterprises exhibit significant regional disparities in green capacity utilization rates, with higher rates in the west and lower rates in the east. This phenomenon indicated that regional heterogeneity leads to differences in emission-reduction performance34. Furthermore, Huang and Zhang employed a high-dimensional fixed-effects model to demonstrate further that digitalization exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with the embedded carbon emissions of exports, and that advancing one’s position in global value chains can significantly reduce carbon emissions35.

Insights and limitations of existing research

Existing research findings provide crucial insights for advancing this study: First, at the research perspective level, existing paradigms focusing on single subjects or elements offer a reference for multi-entity, multi-factor correlation analysis from a holistic viewpoint. Second, at the methodological level, the application experience with various quantitative empirical methods guides the optimization of research approaches and enhances the scientific rigor of conclusions. Third, at the theoretical level, Existing research clarifies the core logic linking green credit regulatory systems, industrial green microgrid technologies, and emissions reduction in high-tech manufacturing enterprises, laying a solid foundation for constructing a multi-entity interactive analysis framework.

While drawing extensively on the value of existing research, the current literatures still have the following shortcomings: First, the limitations of the game-theoretic perspective, which primarily focuses on the two-way interaction between government and enterprises, fail to incorporate R&D institutions adequately. Existing literature lack of a systematic analysis of the dynamic three-party interaction makes it difficult to reveal the mediating role of technological R&D and transformation in policy transmission and corporate emissions reduction. Second, the dynamic transmission logic among multiple entities remains inadequately explained. Existing literature fails to clarify the pathways through which policy instruments guide research institutions in tackling technological challenges and how technological achievements empower enterprises to reduce emissions. Consequently, the intrinsic logic of multi-entity collaborative emission reduction remains insufficiently elucidated. Third, research methods primarily rely on static analysis, lacking simulations of the dynamic evolution of multi-party strategies, technologies, and institutions. Static approaches struggle to capture the adjustment processes of entity’s strategies over time and the marginal effects of key parameters, thereby failing to comprehensively evaluate long-term emission reduction outcomes and policy optimization pathways.

Problem description and model construction

Problem description

During the green and low-carbon transformation process of high-tech manufacturing enterprises, the industrial green microgrids, as the core technology carrier, can not only reduce the consumption of fossil energy and carbon emissions at the source, but also promote the total factor productivity improvement of high-tech manufacturing enterprises. The application of this technology can achieve the coordinated development of green benefits and production efficiency. For instance, an integrated microgrid system combining power generation, distribution, load and storage specifically designed for industrial scenarios can achieve coordinated scheduling of photovoltaic power, energy storage and flexible loads. The system ensures the reliability of production, achieves an increase in the utilization rate of renewable energy, a significant reduction in carbon emission intensity, and provides a replicable practical path for the green transformation of high-tech manufacturing enterprises36. This study focuses on three parties: the government, R&D institutions, and high-tech manufacturing enterprises. And this study forms a dynamic interaction system centered around the common goal of "reducing emissions and carbon, and green development". Furthermore, this study constructs a mechanism through which the three parties can promote the industry green transformation in a closed-loop process of "policy guidance—technical support—practical implementation", as shown in Fig. 1.

Energy conservation and emission reduction mechanism for high-tech manufacturing.

Governments, R&D institutions, and high-tech manufacturing enterprises each play distinct roles and are closely interconnected in the green and low-carbon transition. As the regional environmental regulator and policy maker, the government’s core role is to establish institutional frameworks and provide policy safeguards. The green credit regulatory system serves as the primary vehicle for fulfilling this role. Through a dual mechanism of incentives and constraints, this system links corporate environmental performance to financing costs and policy support. The green credit regulatory system also guides research institutions to focus on low-carbon technology fields through targeted subsidies and other measures. R&D institutions serve as independent third-party technical entities separate from government and enterprises. Their core responsibility is to tackle and promote key technological breakthroughs. Industrial green microgrids, as core supporting infrastructure for corporate emissions reduction, represent the primary focus of these R&D institutions. By overcoming challenges in distributed photovoltaics, novel energy storage, and coordinated control of generation, the grid, load, and storage, they provide enterprises with technical solutions that simultaneously achieve energy conservation, reduced consumption, enhanced energy efficiency, and a stable power supply. High-tech manufacturing enterprises, as the primary implementers of energy conservation and emission reduction, serve as the core vehicles for policy implementation and technological application. Their emission-reduction outcomes directly determine the achievement of transformation goals and serve as the pivotal link between policy requirements and technological value. The three parties have formed a close strategic linkage in the research and application of industrial green microgrids, with mutual influence and restraint. How to fully leverage the carbon reduction enabling effect of industrial green microgrids through tripartite strategy collaboration, avoid falling into the governance predicament of "weak government regulation, R&D institutions low investment, and enterprises negative compliance", and achieve the active evolution of the tripartite strategy, are the core issues that this study urgently needs to address. Model assumptions

Hypothesis 1: This model has three parties, which are denoted by Ω to represent the set of game participants. Ω = {Government, R&D institutions, high-tech manufacturing enterprises}. The three parties together constitute a complete game system. Based on the fundamental difference between evolutionary game theory and traditional game theory, this study assumes that all the participating parties are of limited rationality. The participating parties have differences in their analytical, judgmental and learning abilities. At the initial stage, none of the parties can predict the optimal strategy. They learn and imitate during the game process, thereby making the most appropriate behavioral decisions in the current environment. The equilibrium state of the system is the result of the long-term interaction and evolution of various strategies, rather than the outcome of a one-time static game. This study separates R&D institutions from high-tech manufacturing enterprises as separate parties, and separates technological research from production and emission reduction activities37. This is a classic simplified assumption used in the research on the three-party evolution game of government, enterprise and R&D institution. This assumption is in line with the division of labor model in reality where R&D institutions focus on tackling green technology issues, while manufacturing enterprises concentrate on implementing production38. This helps to clearly depict the interaction mechanism among policy incentives, technological supply and emission reduction implementation.

Scenario 1: When , and , the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix corresponding to E4 (0,1,1) are all less than 0, indicating an evolutionarily stable point. At this point, the evolutionarily stable strategy for the three parties is {lenient supervision, high investment R&D, and green value-driven emissions reduction}. In this situation, the incremental benefits from the high investment in R&D by the R&D institutions exceed the incremental costs. Even without the pressure of mandatory regulation, they still have the motivation to continuously carry out green technology research. After high-tech manufacturing enterprises adopt industrial green microgrids technology, the incremental benefits from green emission reduction can cover the cost difference. Choosing green value-driven emission reduction is more economically reasonable. The government can reduce administrative costs by implementing more lenient supervision. Moreover, the mutually beneficial strategies between R&D institutions and high-tech manufacturing enterprises can already release the positive externalities of emission reduction through the market mechanism, and the system stability can be maintained without additional government intervention. This equilibrium indicates that industrial green microgrids technology is the core technical support for achieving market-driven low-carbon emission reduction, and it is also a key condition for achieving the dual goals of enterprises green transformation and high-quality development. In conclusion, the government should establish a market-oriented governance framework, and R&D institutions should enhance their efforts in technological research and the transformation of research outcomes. This can jointly encourage high-tech manufacturing enterprises to incorporate green microgrids technology into their long-term development strategies. All parties can achieve mutual benefits and win–win results, ultimately achieving coordinated development.

At this point, the evolutionarily stable strategy for all three parties is {strict supervision, high investment R&D, and supervision compliance-driven emission reduction}. In this situation, the government implements strict supervision. The additional supervision costs and the environmental monitoring costs are lower than the fines imposed on enterprises that fail to meet the emission reduction targets. Therefore, the government implements strict supervision is economically feasible. Meanwhile, the total sum of the government’s special subsidies and the difference between the benefit and cost obtained by the R&D institutions through high investment R&D is higher than the total cost of their investment. Even if the technology is not adopted by enterprises, it can still obtain stable subsidy income. Therefore, R&D institutions have abundant innovation motivation under the policy incentives. For enterprises, the supervision compliance-driven emission reduction measures they adopt represent a negative and reactive approach to reducing emissions. They merely meet the minimum environmental protection standards and do not undertake in-depth green transformation. Under the intense pressure of strict government supervision, enterprises will be confronted with hefty fines and rising financing costs. However, the high cost of green value-driven emission reduction and the market benefit uncertainty make its net benefit lower than that of negative compliance-based emission reduction. At this point, enterprises, due to the fact that the overall benefits of supervision compliance cover the costs of non-compliance , tend to opt for supervision compliance-driven emission reduction. In conclusion, the government should establish a sound green credit supervision and reward-punishment linkage mechanism, and strengthen precise subsidies and technological transformation incentives for R&D institutions with high investment R&D. R&D institutions should focus on conducting research on low-cost and easily adaptable industrial green microgrids technologies, providing technical support for enterprises to achieve green emissions reduction. The three parties jointly guide high-tech manufacturing enterprises to shift from negative compliance to proactive green value creation, and systematically enhance the carbon reduction and emission reduction efficiency.

Simulation analysis

To verify the stability of the equilibrium points in the aforementioned theoretical model, and to further explore the influence of each key parameter on the evolution path of the system. Based on the research results of Liu et al.41, this study adopts the numerical simulation method to conduct a dynamic simulation of the evolutionary game among the government, R&D institutions, and high-tech manufacturing enterprises. All simulations were carried out in the MATLAB environment. The simulation process adopts the method of controlling variables, that is, only a single core variable is changed to analyze its impact on the system’s stability strategy, while the values of the other variables remain constant. The parameter values in this study are determined based on the proportional allocation method commonly used in authoritative literature, and the real data are converted to a dimensionless form for hierarchical determination. The benchmark parameters for government supervision costs, rewards, penalties, etc. are based on the official statistics and policy standards in the "China Environmental Statistical Yearbook", "Industrial Green Microgrid Construction and Application Guidelines (2026–2030)", and "2024 Green Development Report". This study extracts the original data related to environmental protection supervision budget, green special subsidies, and industrial pollution control investment, converts them into relative proportions, and performs dimensionless scaling to complete the initial assignment. The parameters such as R&D investment, technological benefits, and enterprise emission reduction costs and benefits were combined with the data on green technology transformation, R&D investment, and operating income in BYD’s annual reports and sustainable development reports. Furthermore, this study uses the average input–output levels of the high-end manufacturing industry as a reference for calibration, ensuring that all parameters are in line with reality. Conclusions and implications

Main conclusions

By constructing a three-party evolutionary game model involving the government, R&D institutions, and high-tech manufacturing enterprises, this study systematically analyzed the strategic interactions and evolutionary paths of the three parties under the collaborative effect of the green credit regulatory system and industrial green microgrid technology. Then, this study conducted numerical simulations on the influence of changes in parameters such as costs, benefits, rewards, and punishments on the evolutionary path of the game system. The main conclusions are as follows:

First, cost barriers significantly constrain third parties’ strategic choices. When governments impose strict cost controls, research institutions incur high investment costs, and enterprises face excessively high costs for green emission reductions, these factors suppress stakeholders’ willingness to adopt proactive strategies. Particularly when a company’s green emission reduction costs exceed the combined value of its market gains and policy incentives, the system tends to converge toward a passive state driven solely by regulatory compliance. This makes it difficult to achieve genuine green-value-driven emission reductions.

Second, the revenue mechanism serves as the core endogenous driver propelling the system’s green and collaborative evolution. High-level returns can generate com-prehensive positive incentive effects. The direct benefits from adopting R&D institutions’ technologies and the market gains from corporate green emissions reductions can effectively incentivize their proactive R&D investments and voluntary emissions reductions. When the benefits of technology transfer and market returns become sufficiently significant, even with lenient government regulation, the system will naturally gravitate toward a virtuous cycle of high R&D investment and substantial emissions reductions, making a decisive contribution to sustained green emissions reduction.

Third, the design of government reward and punishment tools directly determines the effectiveness of the system’s regulatory measures. Specialized R&D subsidies effectively incentivize research institutions to pursue high-investment innovation, with subsidy levels positively correlated to innovation enthusiasm. Conversely, substantial rewards for enterprises may foster incentive dependency, thereby dampening their intrinsic motivation to reduce emissions. Furthermore, increasing fines for violations by a reasonable amount can significantly strengthen the constraints on corporate emissions reduction. However, this must be balanced with regulatory costs and corporate affordability to avoid disproportionate penalties.

Theoretical implications

Based on the model construction, evolutionary analysis, and simulation validation results of this study, the following three theoretical insights can be drawn:

First, this study fills the analytical gap regarding technology providers in multi-entity emissions reduction governance. Existing research on energy conservation and emissions reduction in manufacturing has primarily focused on the interaction between government and enterprises, without sufficiently incorporating research institutions as the core technology providers. This study constructs a tripartite evolutionary game framework involving "government-R&D institutions-high-tech manufacturing enterprises", addressing oversight in traditional dual-subject analyses of the technology supply chain. It responds to the research call by Ding et al. (2025) to "strengthen the theoretical positioning of technology entities within low-carbon governance system42 ".

Second, this study responds to the theoretical call for institutional and technological synergies to drive green transformation. Existing research has primarily examined the isolated effects of either the green regulatory system or the industrial green microgrid technologies. This study reveals the interactive mechanism through which these two elements mutually reinforce each other-policy guiding technological R&D while technology supporting policy implementation-demonstrating that the synergistic alignment of institutional frameworks and technological innovations is crucial for driving deep corporate emissions reductions. This conclusion aligns with the United Nations Environment Program’s theoretical advocacy that "integrating institutional tools and technological innovation is essential for achieving low-carbon industrial transformation", thereby enriching the theoretical framework of cross-dimensional collaborative emission reduction.

Third, this study expands the scope of application of evolutionary game theory in the field of low-carbon technologies. Existing applications of evolutionary game theory in energy conservation and emissions reduction primarily focus on static strategic equilibria. This study employs dynamic simulation to analyze the three-party strategic interactions across the entire chain of industrial green microgrid technology R&D, transformation, and application. It quantitatively assesses the marginal impact of various parameters on the evolutionary trajectory of the game system, thereby over-coming the limitations of simplified variables and static equilibrium inherent in traditional evolutionary game theory research.

Practical implications

Based on the aforementioned theoretical breakthroughs, this study has drawn the following practical implications:

First, rely on the green credit grading supervision to optimize management costs and implement a tiered disciplinary mechanism. Based on the current situation of the environmental protection credit platform in our country and the cross-departmental credit information linkage, promote the interconnection of ecological environment, industry and information technology data, and use digital rating to reduce regulatory expenditures such as on-site inspections by the government. A tiered penalty system based on the environmental credit rating of enterprises is implemented. The fines are collected and allocated to the local green industry special fund, which is used to subsidize the implementation of micro-grid projects. By increasing the cost of non-compliance, the problem of enterprises’ passive compliance is solved. At the same time, the cost barriers for the collaborative efforts of the three parties are reduced from the institutional perspective.

Second, implement differentiated and precise subsidies based on different targets, optimize the incentive structure to avoid incentive distortion. In combination with the domestic special support policies for industry-university-research cooperation, targeted research and development subsidies will be provided for R&D institutions focusing on core technologies of microgrids such as energy storage, power grid, load and storage. For projects whose technologies cannot be industrialized and implemented in the short term, transitional period guarantee subsidies will be set up. Link the green credit ratings of high-tech manufacturing enterprises with the dynamic adjustment of the reward amounts for actual emission reduction achievements, and eliminate the generous subsidies for general enterprises. At the same time, prevent enterprises from overly relying on fiscal subsidies and losing their own internal motivation for emission reduction, in order to adapt to the refined reform orientation of domestic fiscal subsidies.

Third, improve the market-based system for green electricity and carbon trading within the country, and rely on the generated revenue to encourage enterprises to actively build micro-grids. Based on the actual implementation of green electricity market trading, the national carbon market, and green supply chain policies in our country, we will connect multiple revenue channels such as green electricity export, carbon quota monetization, and green loan interest subsidies, thereby increasing the operational income of enterprises in setting up green micro-grids in their factory areas. Enterprises can further reduce the financing costs of their projects by leveraging the green credit premium. By achieving market-oriented benefits such as cost reduction and revenue increase, they can be encouraged to break away from passive compliance and voluntarily adopt green value-based emission reduction solutions.

Research limitations and future directions

To facilitate analysis, the model constructed in this study includes only the government, R&D institutions, and high-tech manufacturing enterprises, with certain simplifications applied to relevant parameters. Therefore, subsequent research can be enhanced in the following four aspects: (1) Incorporate diverse entities such as financial institutions and industry associations to expand the analytical dimensions of multi-party collaborative games. (2) Refine parameter settings by considering the heterogeneous characteristics and dynamic adjustment patterns of enterprises across different regions and scales. (3) Incorporate external variables such as carbon market volatility and technology iteration risks to enhance the model’s adaptability to complex real-world scenarios. (4) Conduct empirical tests using field survey data and panel data to strengthen the model’s practical applicability and persuasiveness.
