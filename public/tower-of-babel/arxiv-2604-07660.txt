Universal, sample-optimal algorithms for recovery of anisotropic functions from i.i.d. samples
Ben Adcock, Avi Gupta

Abstract
A key problem in approximation theory is the recovery of high-dimensional functions from samples. In many cases, the functions of interest exhibit anisotropic smoothness, and, in many practical settings, the nature of this anisotropy may be unknown a priori. Therefore, an important question involves the development of universal algorithms , namely, algorithms that simultaneously achieve optimal or near-optimal rates of convergence across a range of different anisotropic smoothness classes. In this work, we consider universal approximation of periodic functions that belong to anisotropic Sobolev spaces and anisotropic dominating mixed smoothness Sobolev spaces. Our first result is the construction of a universal algorithm. This recasts function recovery as a sparse recovery problem for Fourier coefficients and then exploits compressed sensing to yield the desired approximation rates. Note that this algorithm is nonadaptive , as it does not seek to learn the anisotropic smoothness of the target function. We then demonstrate optimality of this algorithm up to a dimension-independent polylogarithmic factor. We do this by presenting a lower bound for the adaptive m m -width for the unit balls of such function classes. Finally, we demonstrate the necessity of nonlinear algorithms. We show that universal linear algorithms can achieve rates that are at best suboptimal by a dimension-dependent polylogarithmic factor. In other words, they suffer from a curse of dimensionality in the rate – a phenomenon which justifies the necessity of nonlinear algorithms for universal recovery.



Keywords and phrases:
High-dimensional approximation, Anisotropic sobolev spaces, Nonlinear approximation, Universal algorithms

1
Introduction

Many problems require the approximation of functions of
d\gg 1
that are
anisotropic
, i.e., they exhibit differing degrees of smoothness with respect to different variables. Approximation of high-dimensional, anisotropic functions has been intensively studied over the last several decades. However, the vast majority of existing results consider situations where the anisotropic smoothness is known and strive to design algorithms using this information. This assumption may be unreasonable in practice, particularly in simulation settings where the underlying function is accessible only via a black-box numerical routine. In this paper, we study the significantly more challenging
unknown anisotropy
setting, where algorithms are required to be performant for all possible anisotropic behaviours. We refer to such algorithms as
universal
. Our main contributions are concrete algorithms that achieve near-optimal rates for universal recovery of anisotropic functions. Furthermore, we establish the necessity of nonlinear approximation for universal recovery, by showing that the best rates that can be achieved by linear algorithms are necessarily suboptimal.

Problem setting

We consider

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(x)\phi_{-n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\hskip 20.00003ptn\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

(1.2)

denote the Fourier coefficients of

Definition 1.1

(Dominating Mixed Smoothness Sobolev Spaces)
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in[0,\infty)^{d}

\displaystyle H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):\|f\|^{2}_{H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}}:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+|n_{j}|)^{2\alpha_{j}}|\hat{f}_{n}|^{2}<\infty\right\}.

Definition 1.2

(Anisotropic Sobolev Spaces)

Let

\displaystyle H^{\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):\|f\|^{2}_{H^{\beta}}:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{d}|n_{j}|^{\beta_{j}}\right)^{2}|\hat{f}_{n}|^{2}<\infty\right\}.

We refer to

h(\alpha)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[d]\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i},\hskip 10.00002ptp(\alpha)=|\{j\in[d]:\alpha_{j}=h(\alpha)\}|,\hskip 20.00003pt\alpha\in[0,\infty)^{d},

(1.3)

(1.4)

For either class of spaces, our aim is to design algorithms that achieve optimal approximation rates for
any

\alpha

m

(x_{1},f(x_{1})),\ldots,(x_{m},f(x_{m})).

We generally consider

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}(B(H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}),L^{2})\asymp_{\alpha,d}\left(\frac{\log^{p(\alpha)-1}(m)}{m}\right)^{h(\alpha)},\hskip 20.00003pt\varepsilon_{m}(B(H^{\beta},L^{2}))\asymp_{\beta,d}\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)^{g(\beta)}.

, Corollaries

As noted, the reconstruction maps established in (A) are nonlinear. In our final contribution, we confirm that nonlinearity is necessary. Specifically, in Corollary

we show the following. Let
\mathcal{A}
be any arbitrary subset of
(1/2,\infty)^{d}
with nonempty interior and for which
(\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{0})\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{A})
for some
\alpha_{0}>0
. Now let
G:C(\mathbb{T}^{d})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
be a linear operator of rank at most
m
for which

\sup_{f\in B(H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}})}{\|f-G(f)\|}_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{\mathcal{A},d}n^{-h(\alpha)}(\log(n))^{h(\alpha)(p(\alpha)-1)},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\alpha\in\mathcal{A},

(1.10)

for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In other words, using
m
linear (but otherwise arbitrary) samples

achieves the optimal error rate based on
n

B(H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}})

Then, necessarily,

n\lesssim_{\mathcal{A},d}\frac{m}{(\log m)^{d-1}}.

Conversely, picking one of the nonlinear algorithms

\sup_{f\in B(H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}})}{\|f-H(f)\|}_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{\alpha,d}n^{-h(\alpha)}(\log(n))^{h(\alpha)(p(\alpha)-1)},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\alpha\in\mathcal{A},

Thus, universal linear algorithms incur a curse of dimensionality in the factor

d>4

In Corollary
5.2
we show the analogous result for the
H^{\beta}

1.3
Related literature and discussion

Isotropic and anisotropic spaces have been studied extensively in approximation theory and numerical analysis. Anisotropic Sobolev spaces arise in various applications, including stochastic and parametric PDEs arising in uncertainty quantification, high-dimensional PDEs arising in quantum mechanics, kinetic theory, finance, fluid dynamics and beyond, operator learning and numerous other fields.
Many works have analyzed various widths corresponding to isotropic and anisotropic Sobolev spaces. See
[
51
,
23
,
39
,
32
,
18
]
for a modern overview, and, e.g.,
[
35
,
16
,
15
,
36
]
for related results on tractability. The more classical results on sampling widths and linear widths in the periodic setting concern the widths of classical isotropic and anisotropic Sobolev spaces. Extending the isotropic results of Höllig
[
25
]
to the anisotropic setting, Temlyakov
[
51
]
established sharp asymptotic decay rates for the linear widths of anisotropic Sobolev classes.
Temlyakov
[
51
]
also derives sharp asymptotic decay rates for the linear sampling widths of anisotropic Sobolev classes for all
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
, showing that under appropriate smoothness assumptions they decay at the same order as the corresponding linear widths. Here,
q
characterizes the integrability of the partial derivatives (or, in the case of fractional smoothness, the summability of the Fourier coefficients) and
p
characterizes the error norm (
L^{p}
). It is well known (see
[
45
, Remark 4.21(c)]
)
that for classical isotropic Sobolev spaces, nonlinear sampling algorithms do not improve the asymptotic rate compared to linear ones. The asymptotic rates they achieve are listed in
[
45
, Remark 4.21(c)]
.

We now turn to anisotropic Sobolev spaces of mixed smoothness.
For the case
p=q=2
,
[
50
]
and
[
38
]
established matching asymptotic upper and lower bounds for the
L^{2}
linear widths of these classes, yielding sharp asymptotic estimates. Further tractability results in this setting were obtained in
[
36
]
.
More generally, for
1\leq q<p<\infty
with
p\geq 2
, Byrenheid et al.
[
14
]
established sharp asymptotic estimates for the linear widths, sampling widths, and linear sampling widths of anisotropic Sobolev classes of mixed smoothness.
Specific attention has been given to
L^{2}
linear sampling widths of functions from Hilbert spaces (
p=q=2
). A breakthrough result by Krieg and M. Ullrich
[
33
]
showed that sampling recovery for reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces in

is asymptotically as powerful as linear approximation. Building on their idea, Dolbeault et al.
[
22
]
provided further refinements that can be applied to obtain sharp upper bounds for linear sampling widths of isotropic mixed smoothness Sobolev spaces in

(see
[

Moeller et al.
[

provide upper bounds for nonlinear sampling rates for many regimes of

Jahn et al.
[

provide upper bounds for
L^{2}

p=2
, corresponding to
L^{2}

[

. In contrast, for
1<q<2

[
20

investigated sampling recovery for anisotropic Sobolev classes in the
L^{2}
norm. In the regime
1<q<2
(with

More generally, Kosov and Temlyakov
[

extended these results to the

L^{p}

Many other kinds of widths such as Kolmogorov widths,

for an overview). Many of these widths lower bound sampling widths and linear widths and are hence useful tools to show sharpness of the asymptotic upper bounds for linear widths and sampling widths.

Our work differs from this literature in that we consider universal algorithms, delivering near-optimal rates for arbitrary values of the anisotropy parameters. As noted, our algorithms employ compressed sensing. This has been used for over a decade to develop efficient algorithms for high-dimensional function approximation
[

47
,

,

. The majority of results focus on isotropic spaces, such as isotropic mixed smoothness Sobolev spaces and mixed Wiener spaces

Some works have applied an iterative perspective to approximating functions from high-dimensional periodic Sobolev spaces with unknown anisotropy, where they learn the unknown anisotropy of the function and adapt their approximation procedure accordingly. Bartel and Schröter
[
9
]
develop one such approach. Their algorithm alternates between approximating the function and estimating its smoothness from the current approximation and sampling information, with each step feeding into the next to progressively improve both. Our algorithm is, by contrast,

The notion of universality that we use has also appeared in statistical learning theory, where
it refers to algorithms that achieve optimal rates without a priori knowledge of the
smoothness of the target function. Binev et al.

construct such estimators for regression functions from i.i.d. samples, achieving

First, their algorithm constructs the

Our focus on unknown anisotropy continues a line of work initiated in

\mathrm{supp}(w)=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:w_{\lambda}\neq 0\}
. If
z^{*}=(z_{i}^{*})^{N}_{i=1}
denotes a non-increasing rearrangement of
z
, then we can write this as

\sigma_{s}(z)_{q}=\Bigg(\sum_{i>s}|z_{i}^{*}|^{q}\Bigg)^{1/q}.

2.2
Stechkin’s inequalities

The following result is standard in the case
a=0
and
q<\infty
. See, e.g.,
[
3
, Lem. SM2.1]
.
Our lemma below is inspired by this reference. It extends the case
a=0
to general
a\geq 0
, and we also include the case
q=\infty
.
We also allow
s=0
. It should be noted that
results such as the one below
were never published in this form by Stechkin. See
[
23
, §7.4]
for further details. Nevertheless, we continue to refer to it as
Stechkin’s inequality
, as this terminology is widely used in the high-dimensional approximation and compressed sensing communities.

Lemma 2.5

(Stechkin’s inequality in
w\ell^{p,a}
)
.

Let
0<p<q\leq\infty
,
a\geq 0
and
c=(c_{i})_{i\in\Lambda}\in\mathbb{C}^{|\Lambda|}
, where
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}
is an index set and
N=|\Lambda|\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Then
c\in w\ell^{p,a}(\Lambda)
if and only if there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on
p,q,a,
and
c
such that

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{q}\leq C\bigl(\max\{1,s\}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(\max\{1,s\})\}\bigr)^{\frac{a}{p}}

for all
s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
s<N
when
N<\infty
.
Specifically, if
c\in w\ell^{p,a}(\Lambda)
and
q<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{q}\lesssim_{p,q,a}\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}\bigl(\max\{1,s\}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(\max\{1,s\})\}\bigr)^{\frac{a}{p}}

for all
s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
s<N
when
N<\infty
.
If
c\in w\ell^{p,a}(\Lambda)
and
q=\infty
, then

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{\infty}\lesssim_{p,a}\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}\bigl(\max\{1,s\}\bigr)^{-1/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(\max\{1,s\})\}\bigr)^{a/p}

for all
s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
s<N
when
N<\infty
.
Conversely, if
q<\infty
and

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{q}\leq C\bigl(\max\{1,s\}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(\max\{1,s\})\}\bigr)^{\frac{a}{p}}

for all
s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
s<N
when
N<\infty
, then

\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}\leq 2^{\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}}C.

If
q=\infty
and

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{\infty}\leq C\bigl(\max\{1,s\}\bigr)^{-1/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(\max\{1,s\})\}\bigr)^{a/p}

for all
s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
s<N
when
N<\infty
, then

\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}\leq 2^{1/p}C.

Proof.

Suppose first that
c\in w\ell^{p,a}(\Lambda)
and let
(c_{i}^{*})_{i=1}^{N}
be a nonincreasing rearrangement of
c
.
For
i\in\mathbb{N}
with
i\leq N
when
N<\infty
, we have

\displaystyle|c_{i}^{*}|\leq\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}\,i^{-1/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log i\}\bigr)^{a/p}.

We first consider the case
q<\infty
.
Let
s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
s<N
when
N<\infty
.
We set
s_{0}=\max\{3,\left\lceil\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2aq}{q-p}}\right\rceil\}
.
Suppose first that
s\geq s_{0}
. Then
s\geq 3
and

\displaystyle(\sigma_{s}(c)_{q})^{q}=\sum_{i>s}|c_{i}^{*}|^{q}\leq\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}^{q}\sum_{i>s}i^{-q/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log i\}\bigr)^{aq/p}=\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}^{q}\sum_{i>s}i^{-q/p}(\log i)^{aq/p}.

Since
s\geq 3
and
s\geq\mathrm{e}^{a}
, the function
x\mapsto x^{-q/p}(\log x)^{aq/p}
is decreasing for
x\geq s
. Hence

\displaystyle(\sigma_{s}(c)_{q})^{q}\leq\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}^{q}\int_{s}^{\infty}x^{-q/p}(\log x)^{aq/p}\,\mathrm{d}x.

With the changes of variables
x=\mathrm{e}^{t}
and
u=\frac{q-p}{p}t
, the integral becomes

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{\infty}x^{-q/p}(\log x)^{aq/p}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\log s}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{(p-q)t/p}t^{aq/p}\,\mathrm{d}t=\left(\frac{p}{q-p}\right)^{\frac{aq}{p}+1}\int_{\frac{q-p}{p}\log s}^{\infty}u^{\frac{aq}{p}}\mathrm{e}^{-u}\,\mathrm{d}u.

Recall that the
upper incomplete Gamma function
is defined as
\Gamma(r,x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}u^{r-1}\mathrm{e}^{-u}\,\mathrm{d}u
for
r>0
,
x\geq 0
.
We deduce that

\displaystyle(\sigma_{s}(c)_{q})^{q}\leq\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}^{q}\left(\frac{p}{q-p}\right)^{\frac{aq}{p}+1}\Gamma\left(\frac{aq}{p}+1,\frac{q-p}{p}\log s\right).

We shall use the bound
\Gamma(r,x)\leq 2x^{r-1}\mathrm{e}^{-x}
for
r\geq 1
and
x\geq 2(r-1)
. To see why this holds, let
r\geq 1
and
x>0
. Integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\Gamma(r,x)=x^{r-1}\mathrm{e}^{-x}+(r-1)\int_{x}^{\infty}u^{r-2}\mathrm{e}^{-u}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq x^{r-1}\mathrm{e}^{-x}+\frac{r-1}{x}\int_{x}^{\infty}u^{r-1}\mathrm{e}^{-u}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=x^{r-1}\mathrm{e}^{-x}+\frac{r-1}{x}\Gamma(r,x).

If
x\geq 2(r-1)
, then
1-\frac{r-1}{x}\geq\frac{1}{2}
, which implies that
\Gamma(r,x)\leq 2x^{r-1}\mathrm{e}^{-x}
, as claimed.
Applying this with
r=\frac{aq}{p}+1
and
x=\frac{q-p}{p}\log s
, and using the fact that
s\geq s_{0}
, we get

\displaystyle(\sigma_{s}(c)_{q})^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}^{q}\left(\frac{p}{q-p}\right)^{\frac{aq}{p}+1}2\left(\frac{q-p}{p}\log s\right)^{\frac{aq}{p}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{q-p}{p}\log s}

\displaystyle=\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}^{q}2\left(\frac{p}{q-p}\right)(\log s)^{\frac{aq}{p}}s^{1-\frac{q}{p}}.

Taking the
q
th root gives

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{q}\lesssim_{p,q,a}\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}s^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}(\log s)^{\frac{a}{p}},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall s\geq s_{0}.

We now consider the case
s<s_{0}
.We have

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)^{q}_{q}\leq\sigma_{0}(c)^{q}_{q}=\|c\|_{q}^{q}=\sum_{i\geq 1}|c_{i}^{*}|^{q}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq s_{0}}|c_{i}^{*}|^{q}+\sum_{i>s_{0}}|c_{i}^{*}|^{q}.

For the finite sum, we use the fact that
|c_{i}^{*}|\leq\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}i^{-1/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log i\}\bigr)^{a/p}
to get

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq s_{0}}|c_{i}^{*}|^{q}\leq\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}^{q}\sum_{1\leq i\leq s_{0}}i^{-q/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log i\}\bigr)^{aq/p}\lesssim_{p,q,a}\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}^{q}.

Here, we also used the fact that
s_{0}
depends on
p
,
q
and
a
only.
For the tail, we have
\sum_{i>s_{0}}|c_{i}^{*}|^{q}=(\sigma_{s_{0}}(c)_{q})^{q}
.
The bound proved above for
s\geq s_{0}
applies when
s=s_{0}
. This gives
(\sigma_{s_{0}}(c)_{q})^{q}\lesssim_{p,q,a}\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}^{q}
.
Combining these estimates yields
\|c\|_{q}\lesssim_{p,q,a}\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}
. Hence

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{q}\leq\|c\|_{q}\lesssim_{p,q,a}\|c\|_{p,a,\infty},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall s<s_{0}.

Moreover, for
s<s_{0}
we have
1\leq\max\{1,s\}\leq s_{0}
and
1\leq\max\{1,\log(\max\{1,s\})\}\leq\max\{1,\log s_{0}\}
.
Since
\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}<0
, it follows that
(\max\{1,s\})^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}\geq s_{0}^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}
,
and therefore

\displaystyle s_{0}^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\bigl(\max\{1,s\}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(\max\{1,s\})\}\bigr)^{a/p}\geq 1.

And since
s_{0}
only depends on
p,q
and
a
, after adjusting constants, we may write

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{q}\lesssim_{p,q,a}\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}\bigl(\max\{1,s\}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(\max\{1,s\})\}\bigr)^{a/p},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall s<s_{0}.

Combining this with the case
s\geq s_{0}
gives

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{q}\lesssim_{p,q,a}\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}\bigl(\max\{1,s\}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(\max\{1,s\})\}\bigr)^{\frac{a}{p}},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},

as required.
We now consider the case
q=\infty
.
Let
s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
s<N
when
N<\infty
and set
t=\max\{1,s\}
.
Then

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{\infty}=\sup_{i>s}|c_{i}^{*}|\ =|c_{s+1}^{*}|\leq\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}(s+1)^{-1/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(s+1)\}\bigr)^{a/p}.

Since
(s+1)^{-1/p}\leq t^{-1/p}
and
\max\{1,\log(s+1)\}\leq 2\max\{1,\log t\}
, we get

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{\infty}\leq 2^{a/p}\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}t^{-1/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log t\}\bigr)^{a/p},

as required.

We now prove the converse.
Assume first that
q<\infty
and that there exists
C>0
such that for all
s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
s<N
when
N<\infty
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{q}\leq C\bigl(\max\{1,s\}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(\max\{1,s\})\}\bigr)^{\frac{a}{p}}.

Taking
s=0
gives
\sigma_{0}(c)_{q}=\|c\|_{q}\leq C
. Hence
|c_{1}^{*}|\leq C
.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
2n\leq N
when
N<\infty
.
Since
(c_{i}^{*})
is nonincreasing, we have
|c_{i}^{*}|\geq|c_{2n}^{*}|
for
n+1\leq i\leq 2n
. Therefore

\displaystyle n|c_{2n}^{*}|^{q}\leq\sum_{i=n+1}^{2n}|c_{i}^{*}|^{q}\leq\sum_{i>n}|c_{i}^{*}|^{q}=(\sigma_{n}(c)_{q})^{q}.

Using the assumed bound for
s=n
, we get

\displaystyle|c_{2n}^{*}|\leq C\,n^{-1/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log n\}\bigr)^{a/p}.

Now let
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
2n+1\leq N
when
N<\infty
.
Since
|c_{i}^{*}|\geq|c_{2n+1}^{*}|
for
n+2\leq i\leq 2n+1
, we have

\displaystyle n|c_{2n+1}^{*}|^{q}\leq\sum_{i=n+2}^{2n+1}|c_{i}^{*}|^{q}\leq\sum_{i>n+1}|c_{i}^{*}|^{q}=(\sigma_{n+1}(c)_{q})^{q}.

Using the assumed bound for
s=n+1
and the fact that
(n+1)/n\leq 2
, we obtain

\displaystyle|c_{2n+1}^{*}|\leq 2^{1/q}C\,(n+1)^{-1/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(n+1)\}\bigr)^{a/p}.

Combining these bounds and using the fact that
(2n)^{1/p}\leq 2^{1/p}n^{1/p}
and
(2n+1)^{1/p}\leq 2^{1/p}(n+1)^{1/p}
gives

\displaystyle\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{n\geq 1}\big\{(2n)^{1/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(2n)\}\bigr)^{-a/p}|c_{2n}^{*}|,(2n+1)^{1/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(2n+1)\}\bigr)^{-a/p}|c_{2n+1}^{*}|\big\}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}}C.

Finally, assume that
q=\infty
and that there exists
C>0
such that for all
s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
s<N
when
N<\infty
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{\infty}\leq C\bigl(\max\{1,s\}\bigr)^{-1/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(\max\{1,s\})\}\bigr)^{a/p}.

Taking
s=0
gives
|c_{1}^{*}|\leq C
.
Let
i\in\mathbb{N}
with
2\leq i\leq N
when
N<\infty
and take
s=i-1
.
Then
\sigma_{i-1}(c)_{\infty}=\sup_{j\geq i}|c_{j}^{*}|=|c_{i}^{*}|
, so

\displaystyle|c_{i}^{*}|\leq C(i-1)^{-1/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log(i-1)\}\bigr)^{a/p}.

Since
(i-1)^{-1/p}\leq 2^{1/p}i^{-1/p}
and
\max\{1,\log(i-1)\}\leq\max\{1,\log i\}
for
i\geq 2
, we get

\displaystyle|c_{i}^{*}|\leq 2^{1/p}Ci^{-1/p}\bigl(\max\{1,\log i\}\bigr)^{a/p}.

Therefore
\|c\|_{p,a,\infty}\leq 2^{1/p}C
, as required.
∎

2.3
Best
s
-term approximation rates in
H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}

We now introduce some notation that will be used in the subsequent results. Given
r>1
and
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in(0,\infty)^{d}
, let

\displaystyle A(d,r,\alpha)=\left|\left\{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+|n_{j}|)^{\alpha_{j}}<r\right\}\right|,\hskip 20.00003pta(d,r,\alpha)=\left|\left\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}:\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+n_{j})^{\alpha_{j}}<r\right\}\right|.

Observe that

\displaystyle a(d,r,\alpha)\leq A(d,r,\alpha)\leq 2^{d}\times a(d,r,\alpha).

(2.1)

This lemma, as well as Lemma
2.8
later, is well-established in the literature. See, for example,
[
15
,
36
]
. We offer short proofs for completeness.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in(0,\infty)^{d}
and
h(\alpha)
and
p(\alpha)
be as in (
1.3
).
Then

\displaystyle a(d,r,\alpha)\asymp_{d,\alpha}r^{\frac{1}{h(\alpha)}}(\log r)^{p(\alpha)-1},

(2.2)

and

\displaystyle A(d,r,\alpha)\asymp_{d,\alpha}r^{\frac{1}{h(\alpha)}}(\log r)^{p(\alpha)-1}.

(2.3)

Proof.

Notice that (
2.3
) follows immediately from (
2.2
) and (
2.1
). Hence, we only need to show (
2.2
).
To do this,
we fix
p(\alpha)=p\in\mathbb{N}
for arbitrary
\alpha\in(0,\infty)^{d}
, where
d\in\mathbb{N}
is unspecified and proceed by induction on
d\geq p(\alpha)
.
First, for the base case
d=p(\alpha)
, we use the fact that
a(d,r,\alpha)=a(d,r^{\frac{1}{h(\alpha)}},1_{d})
, where
1_{d}
is the vector of ones of length
d
.
[
2
, Lemma B.3]
gives that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(d-1)!}\frac{(\lceil r\rceil-1)(\log(\lceil r\rceil-1))^{d}}{\log(\lceil r\rceil-1)+d}<a(d,r,1_{d})<(\lceil r\rceil-1)\frac{(\log(\lceil r\rceil-1)+d\log 2)^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}

where the lower bound holds for
r>1
and the upper bound holds for
r\geq r^{*}
, where
r^{*}
depends on
d
only.
Thus
a(d,r,1_{d})\asymp_{d}r(\log r)^{d-1}
,
and hence

\displaystyle a(d,r,\alpha)=a(d,r^{\frac{1}{h(\alpha)}},1_{d})\asymp_{d}r^{\frac{1}{h(\alpha)}}(\log r)^{p(\alpha)-1},\hskip 10.00002ptd=p(\alpha),

as required. Now assume that
a(d-1,r,\alpha)\asymp_{d,\alpha}\frac{1}{(p-1)!}r^{\frac{1}{h(\alpha)}}(\log r)^{p-1}
for some
d>p(\alpha)
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\alpha
is nondecreasing. Now observe that

\displaystyle a(d,r,\alpha)=\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor r^{1/\alpha_{d}}\rfloor}a(d-1,r/j^{\alpha_{d}},\alpha^{\prime}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where }\alpha^{\prime}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d-1}).

This implies that

\displaystyle a(d,r,\alpha)\asymp_{d,\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor r^{1/\alpha_{d}}\rfloor}(r/j^{\alpha_{d}})^{1/h(\alpha)}(\log(r/j^{\alpha_{d}}))^{p-1}.

We now establish upper and lower bounds separately. For the former, observe that, by assumption,
p(\alpha)<d
, and hence
\alpha_{d}>h(\alpha)
. Therefore

\displaystyle a(d,r,\alpha)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,\alpha}r^{1/h(\alpha)}(\log r)^{p(\alpha)-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor r^{1/\alpha_{d}}\rfloor}j^{-\alpha_{d}/h(\alpha)}\lesssim_{d,\alpha}r^{1/h(\alpha)}(\log r)^{p(\alpha)-1},

since the series
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{-\alpha_{d}/h(\alpha)}
converges for
\alpha_{d}>h(\alpha)
.
Hence the upper bound in (
2.2
) holds for the given
d>p(\alpha)
.

For the lower bound, we consider the
j=1
term only, which gives

\displaystyle a(d,r,\alpha)

\displaystyle\gtrsim_{d,\alpha}(r/1^{\alpha_{d}})^{1/h(\alpha)}(\log(r/1^{\alpha_{d}}))^{p(\alpha)-1}\gtrsim_{d,\alpha}r^{1/h(\alpha)}(\log r)^{p(\alpha)-1},

as required.
∎

We are now ready to present a result on best
s
-term approximation in
H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}
.

Theorem 2.7

(Best s-term approximation rate in
H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}
)
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d})
and
h(\alpha)
and
p(\alpha)
be as in (
1.3
).
Let
f\in H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}
and write
c=(\hat{f}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, where
\hat{f}_{n}
is as in (
1.2
). Then

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{q}\lesssim_{\alpha,q}s^{\frac{1}{q}-h(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}}(\log s)^{h(\alpha)(p(\alpha)-1)}\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall s\in\mathbb{N},

for all
q>\frac{1}{h(\alpha)+1/2}
. Moreover, for
q=\infty
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{\infty}\lesssim_{\alpha}s^{-h(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}}(\log s)^{h(\alpha)(p(\alpha)-1)}\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall s\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

We first prove the result for
q=2
.
Given
s\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\varepsilon>0
be such that
A(d,1/\varepsilon,\alpha)\leq s
, where
A(d,1/\varepsilon,\alpha)
is as in (
2.3
) with
r=1/\varepsilon
. Then

\sigma_{s}(c)^{2}_{2}\leq\sum_{n:\prod^{d}_{j=1}(1+|n_{j}|)^{\alpha_{j}}\geq 1/\varepsilon}|\hat{f}_{n}|^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\prod^{d}_{j=1}(1+|n_{j}|)^{2\alpha_{j}}|\hat{f}_{n}|^{2}=\varepsilon^{2}{\|f\|}^{2}_{H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}}.

(2.4)

By Lemma
2.6
, there are constants
c_{1}=c_{1}(d,\alpha),c_{2}=c_{2}(d,\alpha)>0
with
c_{2}>(p(\alpha)-1)!
such that

\displaystyle\frac{c_{1}}{(p(\alpha)-1)!}r^{1/h(\alpha)}(\log(r))^{p(\alpha)-1}\leq A(d,r,\alpha)\leq\frac{c_{2}}{(p(\alpha)-1)!}r^{1/h(\alpha)}(\log(r))^{p(\alpha)-1}.

There exists, for a large enough
s
, a positive real number
\varepsilon<1/\mathrm{e}
such that

\displaystyle s\leq\frac{c_{2}}{(p(\alpha)-1)!}\varepsilon^{-1/h(\alpha)}(\log(1/\varepsilon))^{p(\alpha)-1}<s+1,

(2.5)

which immediately implies that
A(d,1/\varepsilon,\alpha)\leq s
, and therefore (
2.4
) holds. We now upper bound
\varepsilon
.
Taking natural logarithms on both sides of (
2.5
) gives

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{c_{2}}{(p(\alpha)-1)!}\right)+\frac{1}{h(\alpha)}\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)+(p(\alpha)-1)\log\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)<\log(s+1).

Since
\log\left(\frac{c_{2}}{(p(\alpha)-1)!}\right)\geq 0
and
(p(\alpha)-1)\log\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\geq 0
, we get
\frac{1}{h(\alpha)}\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)<\log(s+1)
.
Substituting this bound into (
2.5
) leads to

\displaystyle s\leq\frac{c_{2}}{(p(\alpha)-1)!}\varepsilon^{-1/h(\alpha)}h(\alpha)^{p(\alpha)-1}\big(\log(s+1)\big)^{p(\alpha)-1}.

Rearranging,
we obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\left(h(\alpha)^{p(\alpha)-1}\frac{c_{2}}{(p(\alpha)-1)!}\right)^{h(\alpha)}s^{-h(\alpha)}\big(\log(s+1)\big)^{h(\alpha)(p(\alpha)-1)}.

(2.6)

Combining this with (
2.4
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{2}\lesssim_{\alpha}s^{-h(\alpha)}(\log s)^{h(\alpha)(p(\alpha)-1)}\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}},

which gives the result for
q=2
.

Next we consider
q\geq 2
. The previous result and
Lemma

2.5
imply that
c\in w\ell^{\frac{1}{h(\alpha)+1/2},\frac{h(\alpha)(p(\alpha)-1)}{h(\alpha)+1/2}}
.
Reapplying
Lemma

2.5
for
q>\frac{1}{h(\alpha)+1/2}
now gives

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{q}\lesssim_{\alpha,q}s^{\frac{1}{q}-h(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}}(\log s)^{h(\alpha)(p(\alpha)-1)}\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}},

as required.
For
q=\infty
, we apply
Lemma

2.5
with
q=\infty
and obtain

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(c)_{\infty}\lesssim_{\alpha}s^{-h(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}}(\log s)^{h(\alpha)(p(\alpha)-1)}\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}},

which completes the proof.
∎

2.4
Best
s
-term approximation rates in
H^{\beta}

We once more introduce some notation that will be used later. Given
\beta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{d})\in(0,\infty)^{d}
and
r>0
, let

C(d,r,\beta)=\left|\left\{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\sum_{j=1}^{d}|n_{j}|^{\beta_{j}}<r\right\}\right|.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\beta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{d})\in(0,\infty)^{d}
and
r>0
. Then

\displaystyle C(d,r,\beta)\asymp_{d,\beta}r^{\frac{1}{g(\beta)}},

(2.7)

where
g(\beta)
is as in (
1.4
).

Proof.

Let
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})\in[0,\infty)^{d}
,

3
Universal algorithms for unknown anisotropy

We now construct universal algorithms for recovering functions in the setting of unknown anisotropy, thus establishing contribution (A) of §

.
In this section,

is an arbitrary nondecreasing function with

as

3.1

Consider the spaces introduced in Definition

Theorem 3.1

(Probability bound)

3.3
Reduction to a sparse recovery problem

We now turn our attention to the proofs of Theorems

–
3.5
,
3.6
–
3.8
, commencing with the construction of the algorithms. The main results in the previous section, Theorems

, show that the best

-term approximation achieves the desired convergence rates in terms of

. Motivated by this, we pursue an approach based on compressed sensing, where we first convert the problem of approximating an unknown
f
from its samples (
1.5
) to a finite-dimensional sparse recovery problem.

Given an unknown function
f
and sample points
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
drawn i.i.d. from the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, we define the normalized sample vector

\displaystyle b=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\big(f(x_{i})\big)_{i=1}^{m}.

(3.1)

We seek to approximate
f
by recovering its Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}_{\Lambda}=(\hat{f}_{n})_{n\in\Lambda}
corresponding to an index set
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that will be chosen later. Write

and let
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times N}
denote the matrix formed by evaluating the truncated Fourier basis
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\Lambda}
at the sample points, divided by
\sqrt{m}
to normalize:

\displaystyle A=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left(\phi_{n}(x_{i})\right)_{i\in[m],n\in\Lambda}.

(3.2)

In addition, we define the truncation error
v\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
as

\displaystyle v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left(f(x_{i})-\sum_{n\in\Lambda}\hat{f}_{n}\phi_{n}(x_{i})\right)_{i\in[m]}.

(3.3)

Substituting
\hat{f}_{\Lambda}
,
A
and
v
into (
3.1
), we arrive at the following:

\displaystyle b=A\hat{f}_{\Lambda}+v.

(3.4)

Since
\hat{f}_{\Lambda}
is approximately sparse, this is a sparse recovery problem. Following
[
2
,
1
,
6
,
42
,
39
,
41
]
, we solve it using the SR-LASSO decoder
[
10
]
.

Definition 3.10

(SR-LASSO)
.

Given a regularization parameter
\lambda>0
, a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times N}
and vector
b\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, the
(unconstrained) Square Root (SR)-LASSO problem
is the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{N}}\lambda\|z\|_{1}+\|Az-b\|_{2}.

(3.5)

We now present several key compressed sensing tools. See, e.g.,
[

Definition 3.11

(

-rNSP)

Given

, a matrix

and all index sets

\displaystyle\|z_{S}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{s}}\|z_{S^{c}}\|_{1}+\tau\|Az\|_{2}.

(3.6)

Lemma 3.12

(rNSP implies stable and accurate recovery for the SR-LASSO problem)

Let

satisfy the rNSP of order

Let

\displaystyle\|z^{*}-z^{\sharp}\|_{2}

where the constants

Proof.

Lemma

Now let

be as in (

(3.12)

(3.14)

\displaystyle\|c-c_{\Lambda}\|_{1}\leq\Biggl(\sum_{n\notin\Lambda}|c_{n}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\max\{1,|n_{j}|\}^{2h(\alpha)}\Biggr)^{1/2}\Biggl(\sum_{n\notin\Lambda}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\max\{1,|n_{j}|\}^{-2h(\alpha)}\Biggr)^{1/2}.

For the second factor, we have

Theorem 3.15

(Error bound for the reconstruction map for anisotropic Sobolev spaces)

The proof follows the same structure as that of

3.14

\|f-f^{\sharp}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{d}\frac{\sigma_{s}(z)_{1}}{\sqrt{s}}+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}\lambda}+1\right){\|c-c_{\Lambda}\|}_{1}.

(3.17)

with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
for

3.10

We first estimate the term

using

2.9
with

.
Since

, this gives

\frac{\sigma_{s}(z)_{1}}{\sqrt{s}}=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\frac{\sigma_{s}(c)_{1}}{\sqrt{s}}\lesssim_{d,\beta}s^{-g(\beta)}\|f\|_{H^{\beta}}.

We now estimate the term
\|c-c_{\Lambda}\|_{1}

As in the proof of

Theorem

3.14
,
we write

\|c-c_{\Lambda}\|_{1}=\sum_{n\notin\Lambda}|c_{n}|=\sum_{n\notin\Lambda}|c_{n}|\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{d}\max\{1,|n_{j}|\}^{g(\beta)}}{\prod_{j=1}^{d}\max\{1,|n_{j}|\}^{g(\beta)}},

and apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\|c-c_{\Lambda}\|_{1}\leq\Biggl(\sum_{n\notin\Lambda}|c_{n}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\max\{1,|n_{j}|\}^{2g(\beta)}\Biggr)^{1/2}\Biggl(\sum_{n\notin\Lambda}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\max\{1,|n_{j}|\}^{-2g(\beta)}\Biggr)^{1/2}.

Here we used that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{j}(1+|n_{j}|)^{\beta_{j}}\leq 2^{\max_{k\in[d]}\beta_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{j}(1+|n_{j}|^{\beta_{j}})\leq 2^{\max_{k\in[d]}\beta_{k}}\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{d}|n_{j}|^{\beta_{j}}\right).

\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+|n_{j}|)^{g(\beta)}\lesssim_{d,\beta}1+\sum_{j=1}^{d}|n_{j}|^{\beta_{j}}

For the second factor in (

\left\{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\prod_{j=1}^{d}\max\{1,|n_{j}|\}\leq\frac{r}{2^{d}}\right\}\subseteq\Lambda,

[
8

2g(\beta)>1

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\prod_{j=1}^{d}\max\{1,|n_{j}|\}>\frac{r}{2^{d}}\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\max\{1,|n_{j}|\}^{-2g(\beta)}\lesssim_{d,\beta}r^{-2g(\beta)+1}\log(r)^{d-1}.

Substituting this and (

Theorem
3.14

Proof of Theorem

4
Lower bounds on nonlinear recovery widths

In this section, we present lower bounds for approximation in the spaces
H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}
and
H^{\beta}
. The main purpose of these results is to confirm that the algorithms established in Theorems
3.1
–
3.5
,
3.6
–
3.8
are optimal, up to the polylogarithmic factor arising in
\widetilde{m}
.
The following two theorems estimate the adaptive
m
-width (
1.8
)

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})
with
\alpha>1/2
and
B(H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}})
be the unit ball of
H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.
Then, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the width
\varepsilon_{m}
of
B(H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}})
in
L^{2}
satisfies

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}(B(H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}),L^{2})\asymp_{\alpha,d}\left(\frac{(\log m)^{p(\alpha)-1}}{m}\right)^{h(\alpha)}.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})
with
g(\beta)>1/2
and
B(H^{\beta})
be the unit ball in
H^{\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.
Then, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the width
\varepsilon_{m}
of
B(H^{\beta})
in
L^{2}
satisfies

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}(B(H^{\beta}),L^{2})\asymp_{\beta,d}\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)^{g(\beta)}.

Overall, these results confirm optimality of the algorithms developed in §
3
, up to a polylogarithmic factor.
Furthermore, by establishing a lower bound on
\varepsilon_{m}
, which allows for arbitrary adaptive linear measurements, they confirm that not only do pointwise samples constitute near-optimal information for recovery in
H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}}
and
H^{\beta}
, but also that i.i.d. sampling from the underlying uniform measure is also near-optimal. In particular, adaptive sampling, or even a change of the probability measure employed for i.i.d. sampling, is unnecessary to achieve near-optimal rates.
We remark in passing that the lower bound in Theorem
4.1
was shown in
[
14
, Cor. 8.2]
for general

and
L^{q}
-spaces (our result corresponds to

). However, the upper bound found in
[
14
, Thm. 8.4]
only holds for
p\neq q
. Our proof is specific to
p=q=2
and follows different arguments.

As commented, the algorithms developed in §
3
are universal, in that they achieve near-optimal rates simultaneously for different values of
\alpha
and
\beta
. It is notable that the algorithms implied in these theorems only need work for a fixed value of
\alpha
or
\beta
. It is an open problem whether a tighter lower bound can be established for universal algorithms, thereby narrowing the polylogarithmic gap between the current upper and lower bounds.

The remainder of this section establishes these results. We commence with the following, which demonstrates in an abstract sense that the adaptive
m
-width of an ellipse in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is lower bounded by the worst-case best
s
-term approximation error.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
w=(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
be a sequence of positive weights, and let

F=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w_{n}^{-2}|\hat{f}_{n}|^{2}\leq 1\right\}.

Then, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the width
\varepsilon_{m}(F,L^{2})
satisfies

w^{*}_{2m+1}\leq\varepsilon_{m}(F,L^{2})\leq w^{*}_{m+1},

where
(w^{*}_{i})^{\infty}_{i=1}
is a non-increasing rearrangement of
w
.

Proof.

The proof follows along the lines of the proof of

[
7
, Theorem 5.4]
.
We first require some notation.
For an index set
I
, sequence of positive weights
w=(w_{i})_{i\in I}
and
p
such that
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we write
\ell^{p}_{w}(I)
for the set of real valued sequences
c=(c_{i})_{i\in I}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I|}
such that

\|c\|_{p,w}:=\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{n\in I}w_{n}^{-p}|c_{n}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}&1\leq p<\infty\\
\sup_{n\in I}w_{n}^{-1}|c_{n}|&p=\infty\end{cases}<\infty.

Let
B({\ell^{p}_{w}}(I))
denote the unit ball in
\ell^{p}_{w}(I)
.

Now let
S:C(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
be an adaptive linear operator and

R:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
be a reconstruction map.
Define
J:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{2m}
and
K:\mathbb{R}^{2m}\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
by

J(z)=(\mathrm{Re}\,z_{1},\mathrm{Im}\,z_{1},\dots,\mathrm{Re}\,z_{m},\mathrm{Im}\,z_{m})
and

K(a_{1},b_{1},\dots,a_{m},b_{m})=(a_{1}+\mathrm{i}b_{1},\dots,a_{m}+\mathrm{i}b_{m})
.
For a finite index set
I\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
define the adaptive linear operator

M_{I}:\mathbb{R}^{|I|}\to\mathbb{R}^{2m}
by

M_{I}(c)=J\left(S\Bigl(\sum_{n\in I}c_{n}\phi_{n}\Bigr)\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall c=(c_{n})_{n\in I}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I|}.

Fix
c=(c_{n})_{n\in I}\in B(\ell^{2}_{w}(I))
and let
f=\sum_{n\in I}c_{n}\phi_{n}
.
Then

\displaystyle\|f-R(S(f))\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left|\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\bigl(f-R(S(f))\bigr)(x)\phi_{-n}(x)dx\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n\in I}\left|c_{n}-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}R(K(J(S(f))))(x)\phi_{-n}(x)dx\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in I}\left|c_{n}-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}R(K(M_{I}(c)))(x)\phi_{-n}(x)dx\right|^{2}

Thus, defining

L:\mathbb{R}^{2m}\to\mathbb{R}^{|I|}
as

L(z)=\mathrm{Re}\,\left(\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}R(K(z))(x)\phi_{-n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)_{n\in I},

we obtain

\displaystyle\|f-R(S(f))\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq\sum_{n\in I}\left|c_{n}-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}R(K(M_{I}(c)))(x)\phi_{-n}(x)dx\right|^{2}\geq\|c-L(M_{I}(c))\|_{2}^{2}.

We deduce that
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\|f-R(S(f))\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq\sup_{c\in B({\ell^{2}_{w}}(I))}\|c-L(M_{I}(c))\|_{2}^{2}
.
Since
M_{I}
is an adaptive linear operator, it follows that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}(\mathcal{F},L^{2})

\displaystyle=\inf\Bigl\{\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\|f-R(S(f))\|_{L^{2}}:S:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{C}^{m}\text{ adaptive},R:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to L^{2}\Bigr\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf\Bigl\{\sup_{c\in B({\ell^{2}_{w}}(I))}\|c-L(M_{I}(c))\|_{2}:M_{I}:\mathbb{R}^{|I|}\to\mathbb{R}^{2m}\text{ adaptive},L:\mathbb{R}^{2m}\to\mathbb{R}^{|I|}\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=:E_{2m}(B({\ell^{2}_{w}}(I)),\ell^{2}(I)).

In the next step we invoke a standard result, which states that if
F
, subset of a normed space
(Z,{\|\cdot\|}_{Z})
, is symmetric with respect to the origin, i.e.,
F=-F
, then
E_{m}(F,Z)\geq d^{m}(F,Z)
,
where

d^{m}(F,Z)=\inf\Bigl\{\sup_{z\in F\cap S}\|z\|_{Z}:S\subset Z\text{ with }\operatorname{codim}(S)\leq m\Bigr\}

is the Gelfand width.
With this, and the fact that
B({\ell^{2}_{w}}(I))
is symmetric,
we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2m}(F,L^{2})\geq E_{2m}(B({\ell^{2}_{w}}(I)),\ell^{2}(I))\geq d^{2m}(B({\ell^{2}_{w}}(I)),\ell^{2}(I)).

(4.1)

For the next step of our proof, we make use of a duality result. Consider the Kolmogorov width of a subset
F
of a normed space
(Z,{\|\cdot\|}_{Z})
:

d_{m}(F,Z)=\inf\Bigl\{\sup_{f\in F}\inf_{z\in Z_{m}}\|f-z\|_{Z}:Z_{m}\subset Z\text{ with }\dim(Z_{m})\leq m\Bigr\}.

Then

[
4
, Theorem B.3]
says that, given
real numbers
p
and
q
such that
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
and
a finite sequence of positive weights
w=(w_{n})_{n\in I}
, we have

\displaystyle d^{m}(B({\ell^{q^{*}}_{w}}(I)),\ell^{p^{*}}(I))=d_{m}(B({\ell^{p}}(I)),\ell_{1/w}^{q}(I)).

where
p^{*}
and
q^{*}
satisfy
1/p^{*}+1/p=1
and
1/q^{*}+1/q=1
respectively. With this, we have

\displaystyle d^{2m}(B({\ell^{2}_{w}}(I)),\ell^{2}(I))=d_{2m}(B({\ell^{2}}(I)),\ell_{1/w}^{2}(I)).

(4.2)

We combine this with another technical result.

[
4
, Lemma B.4]
states that given a finite sequence of positive weights
w=(w_{n})_{n\in I}
and
p,q
such that
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
, we have

\displaystyle d_{m}(B({\ell^{p}}(I)),\ell^{q}_{1/w}(I))=d_{m}(B(\ell^{p}_{w}(I)),\ell^{q}(I)).

With this, we have

\displaystyle d_{2m}(B({\ell^{2}}(I)),\ell^{2}_{1/w}(I))=d_{2m}(B({\ell^{2}_{w}}(I)),\ell^{2}(I)).

(4.3)

Combining (
4.1
), (

) and (

.
This holds for any

.
Now, we make an explicit choice for
I
. Choose
N>2m
and let
I=\{n_{1},\dots,n_{N}\}

th largest entry of the sequence

. Fix

By Hölder’s inequality,

which implies that

\displaystyle d_{2m}(B({\ell^{2}_{w}}(I)),\ell^{2}(I))\geq N^{-\frac{p-2}{2p}}d_{2m}(B({\ell^{p}_{w}}(I)),\ell^{2}(I)).

(4.4)

In this next step, we invoke
[

, which states that
for
N\in\mathbb{N}
with

, a finite sequence

of positive weights such that

and

such that

,
we have

d_{m}(B({\ell^{p}_{w}}(I)),\ell^{q}(I))=\left(\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset I\\
|S|=N-m\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\in S}\left({w_{n}}\right)^{\frac{qp}{p-q}}\right)^{\frac{p-q}{qp}}.

Combining this with (

|S|=N-2m\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\in S}\left({w_{n}}\right)^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}\right)^{\frac{p-2}{2p}}\geq N^{-\frac{p-2}{2p}}w^{*}_{2m+1}.

Letting

gives

.

To complete the proof, it remains to show the upper bound. For

, let

be such that

. Define the sampling-recovery pair

and

Then, for any

Lower bounds for universal linear recovery

We now expose the gap between the performance of linear algorithms for universal recovery of anisotropic Sobolev functions and the nonlinear algorithms constructed in this work. We do this by following and modifying arguments found in
[

, §5.4]
, which are based on
[

]
. These works consider the spaces

, and therefore part of our effort is to extend them to the spaces

, while
[

considers arbitrary

5.1
Lower bounds for universal linear recovery

We present the main results of this section. In the theorem below, we use the notation
\mathcal{L}_{m}(B)
to denote the set of linear operators

whose domain contains all trigonometric polynomials, whose range is an
m
-dimensional subspace of

and for which
{\|G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\cdot x})\|}_{L^{2}}\leq B
,

Theorem 5.1

(Lower bounds for universal linear recovery)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\subset(0,\infty)^{d}
have nonempty interior, and let

, for

and

(5.2)

Similarly, let
\mathcal{A}\subset(0,\infty)^{d}
have nonempty interior. If
G\in\mathcal{L}_{m}(B)
, for
m>m^{*}
for some fixed natural number
m^{*}
depending on
\mathcal{A}
and
d
only, satisfies

\sup_{f\in B(H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}})}{\|f-G(f)\|}_{L^{2}}\leq Cn^{-h(\alpha)}(\log(n))^{h(\alpha)(p(\alpha)-1)},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\alpha\in\mathcal{A},

(5.3)

where

does not depend on
\alpha
or

, then

(5.4)

The result above states that the loss of efficiency of the rate of universal linear recovery for either the

or

This loss of efficiency of the universal recovery rate is connected to the concept of index of universality from

Proof of Theorem
5.1

The proof follows from the following lemma, which is a modification of
[
51
, Thm. 5.4.3]
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
0<A_{j}<B_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
,
P=\prod^{d}_{j=1}[A_{j},B_{j}]
and suppose that
G\in\mathcal{L}_{m}(B)
, for
m>m^{*}
for some fixed natural number
m^{*}
depending on
P
and
d
only, satisfies either

\sup_{f\in B(H^{\beta})}{\|f-G(f)\|}_{L^{2}}\leq Cn^{-g(\beta)},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\beta\in P,

(5.7)

or

\sup_{f\in B(H^{\alpha}_{\mathsf{mix}})}{\|f-G(f)\|}_{L^{2}}\leq Cn^{-h(\alpha)}(\log(n))^{h(\alpha)(p(\alpha)-1)},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\alpha\in P,

(5.8)

where
C
does not depend on
\beta
(respectively,
\alpha
) or
n
. In the latter case, suppose also that
\cap^{d}_{j=1}[A_{j},B_{j})\neq\emptyset
.
Then

m\gtrsim_{d,P,B,C}n(\log(n))^{d-1}.

Note that (
5.7
) is precisely the case studied in
[
51
, Thm. 5.4.3]
. That the result also holds under condition (
5.8
) is, to the best of our knowledge, new.

Proof.

We follow the construction in the proof of
[
51
, Thm. 5.4.3]
. This asserts the existence of a
\beta\in P
(determined explicitly from
P
), natural numbers
k,s^{*}_{1},\ldots,s^{*}_{d}
and trigonometric polynomial
\nu
with the following properties:

(i)

2^{k}k^{d-1}\lesssim_{d,P,B}m
;

(ii)

{\|\nu\|}_{L^{1}}=1
;

(iii)

\hat{\nu}_{n}=0
if
|n_{j}|\geq 2^{s^{*}_{j}}
for some
j
, where
s^{*}_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
with
\sum^{d}_{j=1}s^{*}_{j}=k
;

(iv)

{\|\nu-G(\nu)\|}_{L^{2}}\gtrsim_{d,P,B}2^{k/2}
.

(v)

The vector
\beta^{*}
, defined explicitly by setting
\beta^{*}_{j}=\frac{g(\beta)k}{s^{*}_{j}}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
belongs to
P
.

Consider the anisotropic Sobolev spaces. By construction

g(\beta^{*})=g(\beta)
. Define the function

f(x)=2^{-k(g(\beta^{*})+1/2)}\nu(x).

Then (iii) implies that
{\|f\|}_{H^{\beta^{\star}}}\lesssim_{d,\beta^{*}}2^{-k/2}{\|\nu\|}_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{d,P}2^{-k/2}{\|\nu\|}_{L^{2}}
,
where the last inequality follows from the fact that
\beta
is explicitly constructed from
P
. Further, Nikolskii’s inequality (see, e.g.,
[
51
, Thm. 3.3.2]
), (ii) and (iii) imply that

{\|f\|}_{H^{\beta^{\star}}}\lesssim_{d,P}2^{-k/2}\prod^{d}_{j=1}(2^{s^{*}_{j}})^{1/2}{\|\nu\|}_{L^{1}}=1.

Hence
{\|f\|}_{H^{\beta^{*}}}\lesssim_{d,P}1
. Using (iv) and (
5.7
), we see that

2^{-kg(\beta^{*})}\lesssim_{d,P,B}{\|f-G(f)\|}_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{d,P}Cn^{-g(\beta^{*})}

Thus
n\lesssim_{d,P,B,C}2^{k}
, which implies that
n(\log(n))^{d-1}\lesssim_{d,P,B}2^{k}k^{d-1}
. The result for the anisotropic Sobolev spaces now follows from (i).

Now consider the dominating mixed smoothness spaces. Since
\cap^{d}_{j=1}[A_{j},B_{j})\neq\emptyset
by assumption, there exists a point
\alpha_{0}
such that
(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{0})\in P
with
\alpha_{0}<B_{j}
,
\forall j
. Now let

\alpha_{\delta}=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{0}+\delta,\ldots,\alpha_{0}+\delta).

For all sufficiently small
\delta
, we have
\alpha_{\delta}\in P
. Now define

f(x)=2^{-s^{*}_{1}\alpha_{0}-\sum^{d}_{j=2}s^{*}_{j}(\alpha_{0}+\delta)-k/2}\nu(x).

Then (ii), (iii) and Nikolskii’s inequality once more imply that

{\|f\|}_{H^{\alpha_{\delta}}_{\mathsf{mix}}}\lesssim_{d,P}2^{-k/2}{\|\nu\|}_{L^{2}}\lesssim 1.

We deduce from (iv) and (
5.8
) and the fact that
p(\alpha_{\delta})=1
,
h(\alpha_{\delta})=\alpha_{0}
that

2^{-s^{*}_{1}\alpha_{0}-\sum^{d}_{j=1}s^{*}_{j}(\alpha_{0}+\delta)}\lesssim_{d,P,B}{\|f-G(f)\|}_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{d,P}Cn^{-\alpha_{0}}.

Letting
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
and applying (iii) once more, we get
n\lesssim_{d,P,B,C}2^{k}
. The result follows from (i).
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Since it has nonempty interior by assumption,

,

The argument for the anisotropic dominating mixed smoothness Sobolev spaces proceeds by following a dimension-reduction argument. Let
p=p(\mathcal{A})
and choose
\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{A})
such that
p(\alpha^{\prime})=p
.
Let
J=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{p}\}\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
be the set of indices where
\alpha^{\prime}
attains its minimum, i.e.,
\alpha^{\prime}_{j_{1}}=\cdots=\alpha^{\prime}_{j_{p}}=h(\alpha^{\prime})=\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\alpha^{\prime}_{j}
,
and define the parameter set
\mathcal{A}_{J}=\{\alpha_{\mathsf{trunc}}=(\alpha_{j_{1}},\dots,\alpha_{j_{p}}):\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\}\subset(0,\infty)^{p}
.
Because
\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{A})
,
\mathcal{A}
must contain a rectangle
P=\prod_{j=1}^{d}[A_{j},B_{j})

We now work in dimension
p
using three linear maps

Define

Hf=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\,:\,k_{j}=0,\forall j\notin J}\langle f,\phi_{k}\rangle\phi_{k}.

Define

(Qf)(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=f(x_{j_{1}},\dots,x_{j_{p}})
.
Let
V_{J}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
be the span of
\{\phi_{k}:k_{j}=0,\ \forall j\notin J\}

Define
R:V_{J}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{p})

,
where
y_{j_{i}}=x_{i}
for

and
y_{j}\in\mathbb{T}
is some arbitrary fixed value for

.
Since
f\in V_{J}
, the value of

does not depend on

for
j\notin J
.
Now define

\|Hf\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

QRf=f
for all

,

\|Qf\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=(2\pi)^{\frac{d-p}{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{p})},\hskip 10.00002ptf\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{p}),

and

\|g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=(2\pi)^{\frac{d-p}{2}}\|Rg\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{p})},\hskip 10.00002ptg\in V_{J}.

Finally, we notice that

\displaystyle\widehat{Qf}_{k}=\begin{cases}(2\pi)^{(d-p)/2}\widehat{f}_{k_{J}},&k_{j}=0,\forall j\notin J,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002pt\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

(5.9)

where, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
k_{J}
represents the vector
(k_{j_{1}},\dots,k_{j_{p}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
.

Now, since the range of
G
has dimension at most
m
, the range of
G^{\prime}
also has dimension at most
m
.
Moreover, for
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
we have

\displaystyle\|G^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\cdot x})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{p})}=(2\pi)^{\frac{p-d}{2}}\|HGQ(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\cdot x})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq(2\pi)^{\frac{p-d}{2}}\|GQ(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\cdot x})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq(2\pi)^{\frac{p-d}{2}}B,

where we used the fact that
\|Hf\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}}
for the second last inequality and the facts

and
Q(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\cdot x})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\cdot x}
for the last inequality.
Thus, we conclude that
G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{m}(B)
in dimension
p
.

Next we bound
\sup_{f\in B(H^{\alpha_{\mathsf{trunc}}}_{\mathsf{mix}})(\mathbb{T}^{p})}{\|f-G^{\prime}(f)\|}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{p})}
over all
\alpha_{\mathsf{trunc}}\in P_{J}
.
Specifically, we show that there exists a constant
C
, independent of
\alpha_{\mathsf{trunc}}
and
n
, such that

\sup_{f\in B(H^{\alpha_{\mathsf{trunc}}}_{\mathsf{mix}})(\mathbb{T}^{p})}{\|f-G^{\prime}(f)\|}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{p})}\leq Cn^{-h(\alpha_{\mathsf{trunc}})}(\log(n))^{h(\alpha_{\mathsf{trunc}})(p(\alpha_{\mathsf{trunc}})-1)},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\alpha_{\mathsf{trunc}}\in P_{J}.

Pick any
\alpha\in P
and consider
\alpha_{\mathsf{trunc}}\in P_{J}
. What follows will be true for all
\alpha\in P
as
\alpha
was picked arbitrarily. Let
f\in B(H^{\alpha_{\mathsf{trunc}}}_{\mathsf{mix}})(\mathbb{T}^{p})
and set

By definition of

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}}\prod_{i=1}^{p}(1+|k_{j_{i}}|)^{2\alpha_{j_{i}}}|\hat{f}_{k}|^{2}\leq 1.

Applying (
5.9
), this is equivalent to

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+|k_{j}|)^{2\alpha_{j}}|\hat{g}_{k}|^{2}\leq 1.

Now, looking at the term
