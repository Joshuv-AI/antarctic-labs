Evaluation of systemic immune-inflammatory biomarkers in eclampsia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Aylin Yılmaz, Didar Kurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate systemic immune-inflammatory biomarkers in patients diagnosed with eclampsia and to investigate their potential association with inflammation.MethodsThis retrospective study included 31 patients with eclampsia and 51 healthy pregnant women matched for age and parity. Hematological biomarkers—including NLR, MLR, PLR, SII, SIRI, PNR, dNLR, and AISI—were calculated using complete blood count data collected prior to delivery. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using appropriate tests based on data distribution.ResultsThere were no statistically significant differences in NLR, MLR, SII, SIRI, dNLR, or AISI between groups (p > 0.05). The PLR and PNR levels were significantly lower in the eclampsia group (p = 0.021 and p = 0.044). Additionally, the eclampsia group had significantly lower gestational age and neonatal birth weight, and higher rates of cesarean section, ICU admission, and maternal complications (all p < 0.001).ConclusionThis study found no significant association between eclampsia and most systemic immune-inflammatory biomarkers. PLR and PNR values were significantly lower in the eclampsia group. These markers may serve as potential negative predictive biomarkers, but their diagnostic value remains limited. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify their clinical utility in eclampsia.

Introduction

Eclampsia is a significant cause of maternal mortality and morbidity [1]. The incidence of the condition is reported to range from 1 in 100 to 1 in 2,000 [2]. Existing data reveal significant disparities in both incidence and pregnancy outcomes between developed and developing countries [3]. This is associated with the level of healthcare provided, such as the early diagnosis of preeclampsia and the effective management of complications arising from eclampsia in pregnant women [2].

Eclampsia is typically characterised by the onset of seizures, which can be classified as tonic-clonic, focal, or multifocal. These seizures can occur in the prenatal, intrapartum, or postpartum period [4]. Prodromal symptoms, including headaches, blurred vision, epigastric or upper abdominal pain, have been reported prior to the onset of seizures [2, 4]. Postictal complications may include hypoxia, aspiration, trauma, pneumonia, and, in severe cases, neurological sequelae or maternal death [5]. It is acknowledged that a pregnant woman may have been previously diagnosed with preeclampsia, or may develop the condition without a prior diagnosis [6].

The etiopathogenesis of eclampsia remains poorly understood. Proposed mechanisms include uteroplacental ischemia leading to the release of toxic mediators into maternal circulation and subsequent breakdown of the blood–brain barrier [6]. Inflammation and maternal immune dysregulation have also been implicated as potential contributors [7].

In recent years, hematological parameters obtained from routine complete blood count (CBC) have gained attention as cost-effective, widely available, and reliable markers of systemic inflammation. These include the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI), delta neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (dNLR), platelet-to-neutrophil ratio (PNR), and the aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI). These markers have been shown to reflect the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory responses, and their utility has been demonstrated in various inflammatory, cardiovascular, and obstetric conditions. Given the inflammatory nature of eclampsia, these markers may also have relevance in understanding its underlying pathophysiology. While many studies have investigated the role of these inflammatory indices in preeclampsia and HELLP syndrome, there is a notable lack of studies focusing specifically on eclampsia [8]. This highlights a gap in the literature that warrants further investigation.

Accordingly, this study aims to investigate the association between various immune-inflammatory indices and eclampsia, with the goal of identifying biomarkers that may reflect its underlying inflammatory mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective study was conducted at Kartal Dr. Lütfi Kırdar City Hospital in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Kartal Koşuyolu High Specialization Training and Research Hospital (Approval No: 2025.05.1077). The medical records of all pregnant women who delivered at our institution between January 2015 and January 2025 were reviewed. Eclamptic patients were identified and included in the study group.

Participant selection and ethical considerations

Patients were contacted via telephone to obtain verbal informed consent after being informed about the study’s scope and data usage. Patients who declined participation or could not be reached were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included maternal age < 18 or > 45 years, presence of fetal chromosomal or structural anomalies, and maternal chronic illnesses (except for diabetes and hypertension).

A control group of pregnant women without any known systemic or obstetric complications was selected retrospectively from the same institutional database. The control participants were carefully matched to the eclampsia group in terms of maternal age and parity to minimise confounding variables.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from inpatient hospital records, including maternal age, gravidity, parity, systolic and diastolic blood pressure at admission, previous history of preeclampsia, gestational week at hospitalization and at delivery, fetal sex, mode of delivery, birth weight, neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission, and length of NICU stay. Data on the timing of eclampsia (antepartum, intrapartum, or postpartum), total hospitalization duration, adult intensive care unit (AICU) admission, and maternal complications (e.g., intracranial hemorrhage, maternal death) were also recorded.

Laboratory parameters and inflammatory indices

Complete blood count parameters obtained within 0–3 h before delivery were evaluated, including white blood cell (WBC), hemoglobin (Hgb), hematocrit (Hct), monocyte (Mo), platelet (Plt), neutrophil (Neu), and lymphocyte (Lym) counts. Additional laboratory parameters recorded were serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), creatinine, and spot urine protein levels.

The following hematological indices were calculated: neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) as NLR = Neu / Lym, monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) as MLR = Mo / Lym, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) as PLR = Plt / Lym, systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) as SII = (Neu × Plt) / Lym, systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI) as SIRI = (Neu × Mo) / Lym, platelet-to-neutrophil ratio (PNR) as PNR = Plt / Neu, derived neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (dNLR) as dNLR = Neu / (WBC – Lym), and the aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI) as AISI = (Neu × Mo × Plt) / Lym.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac version 24.0 was used for all statistical analyses. After checking whether the numerical data were normally distributed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, the parametric test was used for normally distributed data. For non-normally distributed data, a logarithmic transformation was applied. The PNR variable was already normally distributed and analyzed using the independent samples t-test. After logarithmic transformation, log(NLR), log(SII), log(SIRI), and log(AISI) showed normal distribution (Kolmogorov–Smirnov p > 0.05) and were also analyzed using the independent samples t-test. However, MLR, PLR, and dNLR did not show normal distribution even after transformation; therefore, they were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Descriptive values were expressed as mean and standard deviation. Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables, depending on the cell count and categorical data were expressed as numbers and percentages. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

A total of 23,570 women gave birth at our hospital between January 2015 and January 2025. Of these, 31 patients (0.13%) were diagnosed with eclampsia and included in the study group. A control group of 51 healthy pregnant women, matched by age and parity, was selected from the same institutional database.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

There were no statistically significant differences between the groups in terms of maternal age (28.2 ± 5.7 vs. 29.9 ± 6.0, p = 0.213) or parity (0.8 ± 1 vs. 1.2 ± 1.2, p = 0.700). There was a statistically significant difference in gravidity, with lower values observed in the eclampsia group compared to the control group (1.9 ± 1.1 vs. 2.5 ± 1.4, p = 0.049). Gestational age at birth and neonatal birth weight were significantly lower in the eclampsia group compared with the control group (33.7 ± 4.0 vs. 38.4 ± 1.8 weeks; 1994.7 ± 850.6 g vs. 3250.3 ± 455.0 g; both p < 0.001). Cesarean section was performed in all patients with eclampsia, compared to 41.2% of controls (p < 0.001).

Patients in the eclampsia group had longer total hospital stays (5.6 ± 3.2 vs. 2.8 ± 1.6 days, p < 0.001), higher AICU admission rates (54.8% vs. 0%, p < 0.001), and longer AICU stays (3.1 ± 4.2 days vs. none, p < 0.001). NICU admission was required for 64.5% of neonates in the eclampsia group, while none were admitted to the control group (p < 0.001). One maternal and one neonatal death occurred in the eclampsia group (3.2% each), although these differences were not statistically significant (Table 1).

Preeclampsia- and eclampsia-related findings

Blood pressure at hospital admission was significantly higher in the eclampsia group (SBP: 155.3±38.3 vs. 114.3±11.7 mmHg; DBP: 99.5±23.1 vs. 73.1±7.0 mmHg; both p < 0.001). Proteinuria (≥2+) was present in 87.1% of eclamptic patients but absent in controls (p < 0.001). Liver enzymes (AST and ALT) and serum creatinine levels were significantly elevated in the eclampsia group (p < 0.001 for AST and ALT; p = 0.007 for creatinine) (Table 2).

Most eclamptic seizures occurred during the antenatal period (90.3%), while 9.7% occurred postpartum. Notably, 74.2% of eclamptic patients were admitted directly to the emergency department. A prior diagnosis of preeclampsia in the current pregnancy was present in 4 patients (12.9%) (Table 2).

Inflammatory biomarker analysis

Among the inflammatory indices, only PLR and PNR differed significantly between groups, both being higher in the control group (PLR: 153.1±147.2 vs. 99.9±45.0, p = 0.021; PNR: 28.3±10.0 vs. 23.3±11.9, p = 0.044). No statistically significant differences were observed for NLR, MLR, SII, SIRI, dNLR, or AISI (p = 0.630, 0.695, 0.128, 0.501, 0.522, and 0.980) (Table 3).

Discussion

Eclampsia is a condition characterised by convulsions that develop against a background of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. Eclampsia may be preceded by preeclampsia or may develop without prior diagnosis. In a national analysis of eclampsia cases in the United Kingdom, it was found that 38% of eclamptic cases presented to hospital with no history of hypertension or proteinuria [9]. In the present study, the diagnosis of preeclampsia prior to eclampsia was observed in only a small proportion of patients.

Although eclampsia often presents abruptly, it may be preceded by prodromal symptoms such as headache, visual disturbances, epigastric pain, abdominal discomfort, and confusion. In our study, these symptoms were reported by only a few patients, with most presenting to the emergency department at the time of their first seizure. This may be attributed to limited awareness of prodromal signs among patients or the absence of such symptoms. As a tertiary care center, our institution receives a considerable number of cases that are not followed antenatally and present directly with eclampsia.

Magnesium sulfate is widely used in patients with severe preeclampsia to prevent the onset of seizures. Similarly, the administration of low-dose acetylsalicylic acid during early pregnancy in high-risk women has been shown to reduce the incidence of preeclampsia. These strategies indirectly contribute to the prevention of eclampsia. The incidence of eclampsia is notably lower in developed countries, where such measures are routinely implemented and healthcare systems are well-established. In contrast, the incidence remains higher in developing countries due to limited access to preventive and antenatal care. The low incidence and mortality observed in our study likely reflect the availability of effective antenatal surveillance and timely obstetric interventions.

Complete blood count (CBC) is a simple yet valuable tool frequently used in the evaluation of patients presenting for labor. Numerous studies have examined the predictive value of systemic inflammatory markers derived from CBC parameters. These indices have been investigated across a range of medical conditions and are commonly used as indicators of systemic inflammation [10–12]. Inflammation is a key component of the body’s innate immune response, facilitating the recognition and elimination of harmful stimuli and initiating tissue repair [13–15]. Although inflammation is a normal physiological process in pregnancy, dysregulated inflammatory responses have been implicated in complications such as preeclampsia [16].

Studies investigating the predictive value of inflammatory markers in hypertensive disorders of pregnancy have yielded inconsistent results. While some reported no association between NLR and hypertensive conditions [17, 18], others observed significantly elevated NLR levels in preeclamptic patients [19, 20]. Çintesun et al. found no significant differences in NLR, PLR, PDV, RDW, MPV, and PCT values in preeclamptic pregnancies [21]. Similarly, a meta-analysis by Zheng et al. showed that NLR has high sensitivity but low specificity for diagnosing preeclampsia [22]. Tobing et al. reported higher NLR and RDW levels and lower platelet counts in severe preeclampsia, suggesting potential value in assessing disease severity [23]. Conflicting results were also observed regarding PLR; while a meta-analysis of 25 studies found it ineffective for distinguishing preeclampsia or its severity [24], another review indicated limited sensitivity but acceptable specificity [25]. Additionally, Cui et al. suggested that NLR may serve as a predictor of hepatic and coagulation abnormalities in preeclampsia [26].

Early-trimester predictive models incorporating NLR, MLR, PLR, and SII have shown potential, yet results remain inconclusive [27–30]. Collectively, these inconsistencies suggest that while inflammation plays a role in preeclampsia pathophysiology, the clinical utility of hematological indices remains limited.

The findings of this study demonstrate that PLR and PNR values were significantly lower in cases of eclampsia compared to the control group. This condition may be associated with thrombocytopenia, which is part of the natural course of eclampsia or severe preeclampsia, or may result from hematological alterations related to inflammatory processes. In eclampsia, endothelial dysfunction and platelet consumption resulting from microangiopathic processes can lead to reduced platelet counts, which may partly explain these findings. Moreover, systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, which are central to the pathophysiology of eclampsia, can promote neutrophil activation and platelet aggregation, further contributing to these alterations. Nevertheless, these hematological indices are influenced by multiple physiological and clinical factors, including the timing of sampling, disease severity, and therapeutic interventions. Meta-analyses evaluating the diagnostic performance of PLR in preeclampsia have also reported limited specificity and modest predictive value [24]. Therefore, the diagnostic and prognostic value of PLR and PNR remains limited in clinical practice, and further prospective studies with standardized methodologies are needed to clarify their clinical utility.

Several studies have reported that physiological inflammation contributes to the onset of labor at term, involving mechanisms such as neutrophil activation and immune signaling pathways [31–33]. These inflammatory responses are considered part of the normal labor process and may explain the relatively higher PLR and PNR values observed in the control group. In particular, prenatal stress and the physiological demands of late-term labor may lead to increased neutrophil activity and inflammatory markers. However, several confounding factors may have also contributed to these findings. Inflammatory markers were measured at a single time point prior to delivery, which may have resulted in missing the inflammatory peak in the eclampsia group. In addition, treatments commonly administered in the management of eclampsia, such as corticosteroids and magnesium sulfate, may have suppressed the inflammatory response and altered hematological parameters. These factors may partially explain the differences in PLR and PNR values between the groups.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, its retrospective, single-center design may have introduced selection bias and reduced the external validity of the results. Third, inflammatory biomarkers were assessed at a single time point prior to delivery, which may have failed to reflect the dynamic nature of systemic inflammation in eclampsia. Moreover, laboratory analyses were performed using data from different time periods, and potential variations in measurement techniques over the years could not be completely excluded. Finally, potential confounding factors such as antenatal care status, timing of blood sampling, and pre-delivery treatments (e.g., corticosteroids or magnesium sulfate) could have influenced hematological parameters and were not fully controlled for in the analysis.

Conclusion

This study found no significant association between eclampsia and systemic inflammatory markers such as NLR, MLR, SII, SIRI, dNLR, and AISI. PLR and PNR values were found to be lower in the eclampsia group, possibly reflecting thrombocytopenia associated with the disease process. However, their clinical relevance in the context of eclampsia remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Large-scale, prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and to identify more robust and clinically relevant biomarkers.
