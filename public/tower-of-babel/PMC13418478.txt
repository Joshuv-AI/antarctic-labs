Emerging infection and nosocomial transmission of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae harboring blaNDM-21 in a neonatal intensive care unit
Authors: Song Hu, Hua Tan, Rong Ding, Qi Chen, Jing Yang, Mingxiao Han, Yi Wang, Hongbing Chen, Wenli Ji, Mengqiu Wu, Liang Chen, Hong Du, Yi-Wei Tang, Huacheng Tong, Haifang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCarbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) represents a significant threat in neonatal intensive care units (NICU) because of its high antimicrobial resistance and association with increased mortality rates. In our study, we identified three blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates from three critically ill pediatric inpatients; these isolates underwent comprehensive analysis. Our investigations included assessments of their clinical spatiotemporal distribution, antimicrobial susceptibilities, whole-genome sequencing (WGS), biological properties, and genomic and plasmid replicon typing. The three blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP strains, which belong to sequence type 35 (ST35), harbored the gene on IncX3 plasmids. Clinical spatiotemporal and WGS analyses indicated that the third case resulted from transmission from the second case within the NICU, whereas the first case remained epidemiologically unrelated. All three isolates displayed robust growth, formed strong biofilms, exhibited low mucoviscosity, possessed distinct capsule structures observable under an electron microscope, and demonstrated pathogenicity and virulence via in vitro experiments. Moreover, in mouse models, blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP showed significant multi-organ invasiveness and pathologic damage at an inoculation level of 106 CFU, leading to the death of all infected mice. This study provides evidence of the emergence of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP with enhanced virulence-associated features in pediatric/NICU-associated infections, thereby highlighting the convergence of rare carbapenemase-mediated resistance, virulence potential, and a probable risk of nosocomial transmission in a vulnerable patient population.KEYWORDS: Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae, whole-genome sequencing, phylogenetic analysis, bla NDM-21 , IncX3 plasmid

Introduction

Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) are characterized by high mortality rates and potent epidemic potential and have emerged as a significant global public health threat over the past decade. This poses severe challenges to public healthcare safety [1]. This threat is particularly pronounced in pediatric populations, where the incidence of CRE-induced infections continues to rise, leading to significantly increased morbidity and mortality. Notably, intensive care units (ICUs) serve as hotspots for CRE infections. Since 2005, there has been a significant surge in resistance to carbapenem antibiotics among clinically relevant Gram-negative Enterobacterales, particularly Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae). This surge has resulted in persistently high treatment failures and has directly contributed to elevated pediatric mortality [2]. The National Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network in China has reported a rise in the detection rate of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) from 2.9% to 26% between 2005 and 2023, highlighting the urgency of the situation in China [3].

The development of CRE resistance is a complex process involving primarily enzymatic antibiotic inactivation, alterations in cell membrane permeability (e.g. porin defects/mutations), changes in drug target sites, efflux pump overexpression, and biofilm formation [1]. Among these, carbapenemase production is the principal mechanism mediating high-level resistance. According to the Ambler classification, carbapenemases are categorized into Class A (e.g. KPC-type, serine-based), Class B (metallo-β-lactamases, MBLs), and Class D (e.g. OXA-48-type) [4]. Notably, genes such as blaKPC, blaNDM, blaIMP, and blaOXA-48 that encode these enzymes are often located on mobile genetic elements, facilitating horizontal gene transfer [5].

Among the MBLs, New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM) was first identified in K. pneumoniae in 2008 [6], and its variants have since been detected globally. NDM enzymes can hydrolyze nearly all clinical β-lactam antibiotics except monobactams (e.g. aztreonam). However, in NDM-producing strains that co-harbor other β-lactamases (such as ESBLs, AmpC, or OXA-48-like enzymes), there is often a compromised activity of aztreonam [7]. In recent years, the emergence of novel NDM variants has further complicated treatment strategies. The novel blaNDM-21 gene was first identified in 2018 in a urine isolate of carbapenem-resistant Escherichia coli (E. coli) from Sichuan Province in China, and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) revealed that the gene blaNDM-21 is located on a 46.1 kb IncX3 plasmid [8]. More recently, Gao et al. isolated a second E. coli strain harboring blaNDM-21 in Jiangsu Province of China, conveying that the gene blaNDM-21 was carried on a similar 46.16-kb IncX3 plasmid (designated pEC1020-NDM-21) [9]. Both isolates were obtained from urine specimens [8], [9]. Notably, Gao et al. did not determine whether these two E. coli strains harboring blaNDM-21 were part of colonization or if transmission had occurred in the clinical setting.

While NDM-producing K. pneumoniae and hypervirulent K. pneumoniae have been increasingly documented, the co-occurrence of rare NDM variants with CRKP strains exhibiting enhanced virulence remains undercharacterized, particularly within neonatal and pediatric settings [10,11]. The blaNDM-21 variant, closely related to blaNDM-5, does not appear to confer a distinct carbapenem resistance phenotype when compared to NDM-5 [8]. This study marks the important identification of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP in the critically ill neonates. We systematically analyzed clinical data and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) results from these patients to elucidate the resistance profiles and clinical manifestations associated with this novel CRKP variant. Furthermore, WGS was conducted to comprehensively characterize the molecular features of these blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates. This study aims to provide new evidence of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP with enhanced virulence-associated features in neonatal infections and probable nosocomial transmission, and underscore the need for ongoing genomic surveillance in high-risk hospital environments.

Materials and methods

Clinical data and AST

In 2023, CRE strains isolated at Nanjing Children’s Hospital were screened. Three CRKP strains (HS898, HS910, and HS915) were identified from respiratory secretion cultures, and comprehensive clinical data were collected. This study integrated clinical and epidemiological data, encompassing patient origin, date of birth, diagnosis, microbiological culture results, timing of infection, antibiotic treatment, clinical outcomes, ward structure, and bed distribution, to perform a spatiotemporal association analysis. E.coli ATCC 25,922 served as the quality control (QC) strain for all AST procedures. AST was conducted using the Vitek-2 Compact system GN334+XN04 susceptibility card. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for polymyxin E were determined using the broth microdilution method. Apart from polymyxin E and tigecycline, which were guided by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) version 10.0 guidelines, the breakpoint standards for all other drugs conformed to the M100-S32 standards from the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University Children’s Hospital, observing all ethical and regulatory standards.

WGS and plasmid analysis of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP

Three blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP strains were isolated as single colonies and stored at −80°C in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with 30% glycerol. Subsequent to their revival and purification at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, the strains underwent secondary identification employing matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Bruker Daltonik GmbH, Bremen, Germany).

Genomic DNA was extracted using the Omega Bio-Tek Bacterial DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Doraville, GA, USA). Short-read WGS was conducted on all three blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates, designated as HS898, HS910, and HS915. For comprehensive genomic and plasmid resolution, isolate HS898 was further subjected to long-read sequencing on the Nanopore PromethION platform. A hybrid assembly utilizing both short-read and long-read sequence data was performed for HS898. Comparative analyses of the short-read sequencing data from HS910 and HS915 with the complete genome of HS898 were conducted to evaluate their genomic relatedness and plasmid profiles.

The WGS was executed at Novogene on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), featuring 350-bp insert paired-end libraries, and the raw reads were assembled using SPAdes v3.15.4. A preliminary genomic screening was conducted using the local Kleborate database (v2.0.0), followed by uploading the assembled genomes to the PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org/) for sequence typing. To elucidate the genetic relationships among HS898, HS910, and HS915, a phylogenetic analysis was performed based on WGS, incorporating core-genome single-nucleotide polymorphism (cg-SNP) analysis and core-genome multi-locus sequence typing (cgMLST), with K. pneumoniae ATCC 43,816 serving as the outgroup [12]. The relatedness of these isolates was further affirmed by comparing their sequence types (ST), serotypes, and profiles of resistance and virulence genes. The construction of a pairwise genetic distance matrix, based on core-genome SNPs, was leveraged to quantify the genetic divergence among the isolates.

The WGS of the blaNDM-21-harboring strain HS898 was conducted on the Nanopore PromethION platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK), utilizing 10-kb fragment libraries. The hybrid assembly of Illumina short-reads and Nanopore long-reads was executed using Unicycler v0.4.9 (https://github.com/rrwick/Unicycler). The complete genomes were deposited in GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), followed by the identification of resistance genes using CARD (https://card.mcmaster.ca/) analysis and visualization through Proksee (https://proksee.ca/). The blaNDM-21 gene from HS898 was compared to the blaNDM-5 gene (CARD reference) using SnapGene. Plasmid replicon typing was performed based on the DANMEL v1.1 database (http://221.194.188.186/danmel_V1.1/). The plasmid pHS898-3 was annotated and visualized using the custom script plasmid_circular_plot.v3.pl in a Linux environment and subsequently edited in Inkscape. Comparative analyses between pHS898-3 and the IncX3 reference plasmid pNDM-HN380 [13] were conducted using custom scripts (locus_compare_plot.test.pl and locus_compare_plot.v2). Additionally, the strains HS910 and HS915 were assembled using the hybrid approach based on HS898.

Phylogenetic relationships and characteristics of NDM

Phylogenetic analysis of NDM variants was conducted based on their amino acid sequences. All variant sequences (blaNDM-1 to blaNDM-21) were retrieved from the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) and aligned using Clustal Omega (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo/). A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method with MEGA version 10 (https://www.megasoftware.net/). Evolutionary distances were computed using the Poisson correction method, and the tree topology was evaluated with bootstrap analysis (1000 replicates). The resulting tree was utilized to infer phylogenetic relationships and to highlight sequence features among the NDM variants.

Conjugation assay

Strains HS898, HS910, and HS915 demonstrated susceptibility to rifampicin in clinical AST, while rifampicin-resistant E. coli EC600 served as the recipient strain. After co-culture, we mixed the harvested overnight cultures of donor (blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP) and recipient strains in defined ratios and spotted them onto sterile filter papers placed on Mueller-Hinton (MH) agar. Following incubation at 37°C for 4 hours, we suspended the filter papers in 1 mL of LB broth, and 100 µL aliquots were plated on meropenem (2 µg/mL) + rifampicin (100 µg/mL) plates. After 24 hours of incubation at 37°C, the transconjugants were identified via MALDI-TOF MS. The blaNDM-21 gene in transconjugants was confirmed by PCR amplification using specific primers, followed by Sanger sequencing (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai).

Bacterial growth curves

A single colony of each K. pneumoniae strain, HS898, HS910, HS915, ΔcueOΔcopA [14] (a low-virulent control strain stored in our laboratory), and ATCC 43,816, was inoculated into 3 mL of LB broth and incubated for 12–14 hours at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm. Subsequently, the overnight cultures were diluted at a ratio of 1:10 in fresh LB broth (10 µL culture +90 µL medium per well) in a 96-well plate. Three replicate wells per strain and three LB-only blank control wells were prepared. The sealed plate was incubated at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm in a FLUOstar Omega microplate reader, with OD600 measurements taken every 0.5 hours for 24 hours. Concurrently with the export of the blank-corrected data, corresponding growth curves were generated using GraphPad Prism, and significant differences were assessed using multiple comparison tests. All assays were conducted in triplicate.

Mucoviscosity assay

After inoculating a single colony of each K. pneumoniae strain (HS898, HS910, HS915, ΔcueOΔcopA, and ATCC 43,816) into 2 mL LB broth, the cultures were incubated for 16–18 hours at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm. The OD600 of each culture was measured (ODculture). One milliliter of each culture was centrifuged (1,000 × g for 5 minutes), and the supernatant OD600 (ODsupernatant) was also measured. The sedimentation ratio (SR = ODsupernatant / ODculture) was then calculated. Based on the SR values, mucoviscosity was categorized as high (SR > 0.4), medium (0.2 ≤ SR ≤ 0.4), or low (SR < 0.2). Assays were performed in triplicate, and the experiments were repeated three or more times. Data were plotted as bar charts using GraphPad Prism, and significant differences were assessed using t-tests.

Biofilm formation assay

Our experiments aimed to quantify the biofilm formation by K. pneumoniae strains HS898, HS910, HS915, ΔcueOΔcopA, and ATCC 43,816. Overnight cultures (12–14 hours, 37°C, 200 rpm in LB broth) were diluted at a ratio of 1:19 in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth to approximately 1 × 106 CFU/mL. We then added aliquots (190 µL BHI +10 µL inoculum) to a 96-well polystyrene plate, with triplicate wells for each strain, and incubated these statically at 37°C for 48 hours. Following incubation, we aspirated the supernatants and washed the wells three times with 200 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The biofilms were then fixed with 200 µL of formaldehyde (room temperature, 30 minutes). After air-drying, the biofilms were stained with 100 µL of 1% crystal violet for 20 minutes, washed three times with PBS, and finally dissolved in 100 µL of anhydrous ethanol (37°C, 20 minutes). We measured the absorbance at 595 nm and analyzed the blank-corrected data using GraphPad Prism. Significant differences were assessed using one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s multiple comparison test. These assays were repeated at least three times.

Capsule visualization by electron microscopy

Bacterial capsules were visualized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; HT-7800, 120 kV) with negative staining. The capsules appeared as loose material exterior to the distinct electron-dense cell wall, referred to as the “black border.”

Serum killing assay

The serum resistance of the blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP strains (HS898, HS910, and HS915) was determined using a serum killing assay. Strains ATCC 43,816 and 13,883 were included as reference strains representing high and low virulence, respectively. Bacterial suspensions were prepared and adjusted to the specified concentration, mixed with normal serum, and incubated at 37°C. At 0, 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 hours, aliquots were collected, serially diluted, plated on agar plates, and incubated overnight to count CFUs. Serum survival kinetics were plotted as log10 CFU/mL using GraphPad Prism. Changes in bacterial load after serum exposure were quantified as Δlog10 CFU/mL = log10(CFU at the specified time point) – log10(CFU at 0 hours). Differences among groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by a multiple-comparison test. Assays were performed independently at least three times.

Galleria mellonella (G. mellonella) killing assay

The pathogenicity of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP strains (HS898, HS910, and HS915) was assessed in G. mellonella. In this assay, K. pneumoniae ΔcueOΔcopA and ATCC 43,816 served as models of low and high virulence, respectively. After a 10-fold dilution of the bacterial suspensions (~108 CFU/mL; OD600 ≈0.3), larvae (n = 20/group) were injected in the last proleg with 10 µL of the suspension (final ~105 CFU/larva). The injected larvae were incubated at 37°C, and their survival status was monitored every 6 hours for 72 hours. Survival curves were generated using GraphPad Prism and compared using the Log-rank test. Assays were independently repeated at least three times.

Mouse survival assay

The in vivo virulence of HS898, HS910, HS915, ΔcueOΔcopA, and ATCC 43,816 was further assessed in mice. Female C57BL/6 J mice (6–8 weeks old) were used in this study. Prior to the experiment, the mice were acclimatized to laboratory conditions for one week, with water and food provided ad libitum. The mice were purchased from SPF (Suzhou) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). The bacteria were cultured overnight and then diluted 10-fold (~108 CFU/mL; OD600 ≈0.3). Mice (n = 5/group) were given 100 µL intraperitoneal injections (final ~106 CFU/mouse) and subsequently raised at a constant temperature. Their survival status was monitored every 6 hours for 72 hours. Survival curves, generated using GraphPad Prism, were compared using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. At the experimental endpoint (72 hours), all surviving mice were humanely euthanized.

Mouse infection assay

The in vivo virulence of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP strains (HS898, HS910, and HS915) was evaluated using 6–8-week-old C57BL/6 mice. Bacterial suspensions were prepared and quantified through plate counting. The experiment comprised two batches. Specifically, in Batch 1, mice (n = 3/group) received intraperitoneal injections of 104 CFU of HS898, HS910, HS915, ATCC 43,816, or PBS. In Batch 2, mice (n = 3/group) received intraperitoneal injections of 106 CFU of HS898, HS910, HS915, ΔcueOΔcopA, or PBS. At 24 hours post-infection, mice were euthanized, and tissues from the spleen, kidney, liver, and lung were collected for bacterial load analysis. The data were visualized as bar charts using GraphPad Prism, and significant differences were evaluated using t-tests. Additionally, in Batch 2, tissues were harvested post-infection and subsequently processed for pathological examination, which included fixation, embedding, sectioning, and staining with hematoxylin and eosin. Prior to tissue collection, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of a 1.25% solution of tribromoethanol (Avertin; 0.2 mL per 20 g body weight), prepared by diluting a 100% stock solution in 2-methyl-2-butanol and stored at 4°C in the dark. The efficacy of anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of pedal and corneal reflexes. Subsequently, mice were euthanized using cervical dislocation, and death was verified by the cessation of respiration and cardiac arrest. All animal experiments were authorized by the Animal Care and Use Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University and adhered strictly to ethical regulations and standards of animal husbandry. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Results

Clinical characteristics and spatiotemporal distribution of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates

Three blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP strains were isolated from three critically ill pediatric inpatients. Strain HS898 was isolated from a patient admitted at five days old, HS910 from a patient admitted at one month and twenty days old, and HS915 from a patient admitted at 11 days old. Prior to transfer, HS910 had been hospitalized for over one month at a local hospital. Upon admission, all three patients underwent systematic clinical evaluations and pathogen testing.

Figure 1 shows that these pediatric inpatients were admitted on March 14, May 11, and 5 May 2023, respectively. Each patient, admitted to the NICU, presented with severe congenital dysplasia and was diagnosed with neonatal pneumonia, respiratory distress syndrome, and jaundice. During their hospital stays, they underwent bacterial culture and C-reactive protein (CRP) testing as required clinically. The bacterial cultures confirmed the presence of CRKP, while CRP levels served to differentiate between infection and colonization. Responses to antibiotic treatment provided indirect evidence of infection control and bacterial persistence.

Clinical timeline and spatiotemporal distribution of patients HS898, HS910, and HS915.

The NICU hospitalization periods for the patients are as follows: HS898 from March 14 to March 16, 2023; HS910 from May 11 to July 3, 2023; and HS915 from May 5 to June 7, 2023. Patient HS898 was transferred from the NICU to the CCU on March 16, 2023. The x-axis represents actual calendar dates beginning March 14, 2023, and the y-axis displays the serum C-reactive protein (CRP) concentrations measured during the hospitalizations. Filled symbols represent culture-positive results, while open symbols represent culture-negative results. Details such as culture results, CRP dynamics, and antimicrobial treatments are illustrated along the clinical timeline. The simultaneous presence of HS910 and HS915 in the NICU, combined with their genomic similarities, suggests probable nosocomial transmission. In contrast, HS898 is less evidently linked epidemiologically.

Patient HS898 was transferred from the NICU to the CCU on March 16 and tested positive for CRKP on March 24. For HS910, CRKP infection was first identified from a respiratory secretion culture on May 12. Concurrent metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of cerebrospinal fluid and blood samples also revealed the pathogen, and CRP levels were notably elevated at 195.16 mg/L, suggesting dissemination to the central nervous system and a possible concurrent bloodstream infection.

Significantly, patient HS915 developed a blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP infection one week after HS910’s admission and tested positive from respiratory secretions on May 19. Bed occupancy records confirmed that all three patients were housed in the same high-capacity NICU ward: HS898 in bed 29, HS910 in bed 52, and HS915 sequentially in beds 19 and 29. These combined clinical and spatiotemporal analyses support the probable nosocomial transmission between HS910 and HS915 within the NICU, although HS898 appeared to be epidemiologically independent.

In summary, these clinical and spatiotemporal findings indicate potential nosocomial transmission between HS910 and HS915. However, the limited number of cases precludes a definitive reconstruction of the complete transmission chain.

Antimicrobial resistance profiles and clinical treatment outcomes

The three blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP strains exhibited resistance to several antibiotics, including piperacillin, piperacillin-tazobactam, cefuroxime, cefuroxime axetil, cefotetan, ceftazidime, imipenem, amoxicillin-clavulanate, cefalotin, cefpodoxime, ceftizoxime, cefotaxime, cefoperazone-sulbactam, doripenem, meropenem, cefepime, nalidixic acid, doxycycline, and tetracycline. However, these strains remained susceptible to aztreonam, ciprofloxacin, sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, norfloxacin, tobramycin, amikacin, and tigecycline. Additionally, they demonstrated intermediate susceptibility to minocycline and colistin (Supplementary Table S1).

As depicted in Figure 1, three pediatric patients received different antimicrobial regimens: HS898 was treated from March 24 to April 10, 2023, with meropenem, combined with cefoperazone from April 3 to April 13, which resulted in bacterial eradication and the patient’s recovery by the time of discharge. HS910 underwent treatment with meropenem from May 11 to May 31, coupled with aztreonam from May 15 to June 6, leading to bacterial clearance and subsequent discharge. In contrast, HS915, who received aztreonam monotherapy from May 22 to June 5, continued to show bacterial colonization after treatment, although the patient was discharged in stable condition.

These observations indicate that, despite extensive drug resistance, infections caused by blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP strains can be managed clinically with timely and appropriate antimicrobial treatment. However, given that the study included only three patients and persistent bacterial colonization occurred in one case, these clinical outcomes should be interpreted with caution.

Genomic analysis reveals high relatedness among blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that strains HS898, HS910, and HS915 belonged to the same sequence type ST35 and shared the serotype KL108-O1/O2v2. They also displayed highly similar resistance and virulence gene profiles (Figure 2(A)). Additionally, a heatmap of the pairwise SNP distance matrix indicated fewer than 25 core-genome SNP differences among the three strains (Figure 2(B)), demonstrating their high genetic relatedness.

Phylogenetic analysis of HS898, HS910 and HS915.

(B) Heatmap of the pairwise SNP distance matrix: The three isolates (HS898, HS910, and HS915) exhibit fewer than 25 pairwise core-genome SNP differences, suggesting their recent clonal expansion and implicating them in a potential outbreak of bacterial transmission.

These findings broaden the scope of knowledge regarding the clinical and bacterial host background of the blaNDM-21, identifying this rare NDM variant in ST35 CRKP isolates associated with pediatric/NICU infections.

Genomic features and plasmid profiles of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates

WGS was conducted on three blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates, namely HS898, HS910, and HS915. These isolates were initially characterized using next-generation short-read sequencing. Additionally, isolate HS898 underwent third-generation long-read sequencing to obtain a complete reference genome and to fully resolve the plasmid structures. The hybrid assembly of HS898’s short-read and long-read sequencing data revealed one complete chromosome and three complete plasmids (Table 1). The short-read sequencing data for HS910 and HS915 were further analyzed and compared with the complete genome of HS898, serving as a reference framework. Both HS910 and HS915 exhibited highly similar chromosomal and plasmid profiles to HS898, with an average nucleotide identity of 99.9%, indicating an extremely close genomic relatedness among the three isolates. The relevant genomic data have been submitted to the NCBI. PubMLST analysis classified isolates HS898, HS910, and HS915 as ST35, KL108, and O1, respectively. CARD analysis identified major antimicrobial resistance genes, including blaCTX-M-14, blaLAP-2, blaSHV-33, and blaNDM-21, in all three isolates (Figure 3(A–D)). Kleborate detected virulence-associated loci, including wzi194, ybt2, iro3, iroN, rmpA, and iroB.

Phylogenetic analysis revealed a well-supported clade comprising blaNDM-5, blaNDM-17, blaNDM-20, and blaNDM-21, indicating their close evolutionary relatedness. blaNDM-21 showed the strongest phylogenetic affinity to blaNDM-5, suggesting an evolutionary origin from a blaNDM-5-like ancestor. The amino acid substitutions Val88Leu and Met154Leu, shared by NDM-5 and closely related variants, may represent a common evolutionary background for this lineage. Subsequent variants, including blaNDM-17, blaNDM-20, and blaNDM-21, acquired additional mutations, namely Glu170Lys, Arg270His, and Gly69Ser, respectively. The tree topology further suggested that horizontal gene transfer could contribute to the dissemination of NDM variants across diverse bacterial hosts, including E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and Acinetobacter baumannii (Figure 4(D)).

A conjugation assay confirmed the transfer of blaNDM-21 to E. coli EC600. Using primers blaNDM-21-F, 5′-ATGGAATTGCCCAATATTATGCAC-3′, and blaNDM-21-R, 5′-TCAGCGCAGCTTGTCGG-3′, PCR amplification produced the expected 813-bp product, which was verified by Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Figure 1).

To further contextualize the blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates identified in this study, we compared them with previously reported blaNDM-21-positive strains and representative IncX3-blaNDM plasmids (Supplementary Table S2). Although previously reported blaNDM-21-positive strains were predominantly E. coli, the isolates of the current study were K. pneumoniae ST35-KL108-O1/O2v2. Despite variations in bacterial species and clonal backgrounds, the association of blaNDM-21 with IncX3 plasmids remained consistent. These analyses underscore the potential role of IncX3 plasmids in the dissemination of blaNDM-21-like variants among Enterobacterales.

Virulence-associated genetic determinants of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates

Analysis of WGS-based virulence genes revealed that isolates HS898, HS910, and HS915 harbor multiple virulence-associated determinants, including rmpA, wzi194, ybt2, iroN, iroB, among others. The profiles of virulence genes for these three isolates are detailed in Supplementary Table S3).

The identification of these determinants implies that blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates possess a genomic background that enhances their virulence potential.

Integrated in vitro virulence-associated phenotypes of the isolates

In this study, K. pneumoniae ATCC 43,816, sourced from the American Type Culture Collection, served as a classical hypervirulent reference strain. In contrast, the ΔcueOΔcopA mutant, derived from ATCC 43,816 and maintained in our laboratory, functioned as an attenuated control strain.

Isolates HS898, HS910, and HS915 demonstrated growth curves similar to ATCC 43,816 and ΔcueOΔcopA control (Figure 5(A)), suggesting that the discrepancies in virulence-associated phenotypes are not attributable merely to differences in bacterial growth.

No significant differences in growth rates were observed among these strains (p > 0.05). (B) Sedimentation assay. HS898, HS910, HS915, and ΔcueOΔcopA exhibited significantly lower OD600(supernatant)/OD600(culture) ratios than ATCC 43,816, indicating enhanced sedimentation compared to ATCC 43,816 (****p < 0.0001). (C) Biofilm formation assay. Biofilm biomass was quantified by measuring absorbance at 595 nm. (D) Transmission electron microscopy images showing bacterial capsule morphology. Images were taken at magnifications of 20,000– 30,000×; scale bar, 500 nm. According to their sedimentation ratios, HS898, HS910, HS915, and ΔcueOΔcopA were categorized as low-viscosity strains, which significantly contrasted with the hypermucoviscous ATCC 43,816 strain. However, HS898, HS910, and HS915 exhibited higher viscosity than ΔcueOΔcopA (Figure 5(B)). Therefore, the virulence potential of these isolates appears not to rely solely on a hypermucoviscous phenotype.

The three blaNDM-21-harboring strains formed biofilms at levels comparable to those of ATCC 43,816 and significantly higher than ΔcueOΔcopA (Figure 5(C)), thus confirming their ability to form biofilms, a crucial virulence-associated phenotypic characteristic.

TEM revealed distinct capsules in HS898, HS910, and HS915, similar to those observed in ATCC 43,816 (Figure 5(D)). These structural features provide further evidence of virulence-associated phenotypic traits in the blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates.

To assess the serum survival capacities of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP strains, the low-virulence reference strain ATCC 13,883 was included alongside the high-virulence reference strain ATCC 43,816. HS898, HS910, and HS915 maintained relatively high survival levels during exposure to serum, with CFU/mL increasing over time, in contrast to ATCC 13,883, which exhibited a lesser increase in bacterial burden (Figure 5(E)). After normalization to the initial time point (0 hours), HS898, HS910, and HS915 demonstrated significantly higher Δlog10 CFU/mL values than ATCC 13,883 at both 1 hour and 3 hours, indicating stronger resistance to serum-mediated killing compared to the low-virulence reference strain (Figure 5(F,G)). Overall, the combined in vitro phenotypic assays suggest that the blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates possess multiple virulence-associated characteristics, including biofilm formation, capsule-related structural characteristics, and resistance to serum killing.

In vivo infection models confirmed enhanced pathogenic potential

In the G. mellonella infection model, the blaNDM-21-harboring strains demonstrated significant lethality. When compared with the ATCC 43,816 strain, which exhibited a survival rate of 15%, strains HS898, HS910, and HS915 each showed a significantly higher survival rate of 40%. However, these three strains exhibited greater virulence than ΔcueOΔcopA (Figure 6(A)).

(B) Mouse survival curve, showing significantly higher survival rates for HS898, HS910, and HS915 compared to ATCC 43,816 (**p < 0.01); but significantly lower survival rates for HS898 (*p < 0.05), HS910 (**p < 0.01), and HS915 (*p < 0.05) compared to ΔcueOΔcopA. In the mouse survival model, inoculation with any of HS898, HS910, or HS915 at 106 CFU resulted in the death of all mice within 72 hours. This outcome contrasts with that of the ΔcueOΔcopA group, in which 3 out of 5 mice survived, and the PBS control group, where all mice survived. Furthermore, the mortality kinetics of HS898, HS910, and HS915 were slower compared to ATCC 43,816, which caused 100% mortality within 12 hours (Figure 6(B)).

In a bacterial dissemination model at 104 CFU, mice infected with ATCC 43,816 displayed robust organ colonization, whereas those infected with blaNDM-21-harboring strains showed reduced yet detectable dissemination (Figure 6(C)). At a higher inoculum of 106 CFU, HS898, HS910, and HS915 produced significantly higher bacterial loads in organs than the ΔcueOΔcopA controls (Figure 6(D)).

Histopathological analysis following intraperitoneal injection with 106 CFU revealed no significant lesions in the spleen. However, mice infected with blaNDM-21-harboring strains exhibited pathological changes, including renal necrosis, lung injury, and hepatic congestion, which were absent in theΔcueOΔcopA-infected animals (Figure 7).

Overall, both the G. mellonella and mouse infection models demonstrated that HS898, HS910, and HS915 exhibit greater pathogenicity than the attenuated ΔcueOΔcopA control, but display lower virulence than the classical hypervirulent reference strain ATCC 43,816. These findings support the characterization of an intermediate-to-enhanced virulence-associated phenotype, rather than unequivocal classical hypervirulence.

Discussion

In this study, we identified blaNDM-21-harboring ST35-KL108 CRKP isolates from pediatric/NICU-associated infections. These isolates exhibited carbapenem resistance and carried the IncX3 plasmid-associated blaNDM-21, displayed close genomic relatedness suggestive of probable nosocomial transmission, and possessed several virulence-associated genomic and phenotypic characteristics. Crucially, based on available evidence, we do not categorize these isolates as classical hypervirulent or unequivocally highly pathogenic K. pneumoniae strains. Instead, we describe them as blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates with enhanced virulence-associated features.

The scientific contribution of this study extends beyond documenting blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP in pediatric/NICU-associated infections; it also demonstrates the convergence of carbapenem resistance, enhanced virulence-associated features, and potential nosocomial transmission. Previous studies have identified NDM-producing K. pneumoniae and hypervirulent K. pneumoniae as significant clinical threats. However, the emergence of rare NDM variants such as blaNDM-21 in CRKP isolates with virulence-associated characteristics is not well understood. In this study, blaNDM-21 was found in ST35 K. pneumoniae isolates carrying multiple virulence-associated determinants and exhibiting virulence-related phenotypes. Importantly, our data do not confirm that blaNDM-21 confers a distinct resistance phenotype compared to blaNDM-5. Rather, the concern arises from the genetic and clinical context in which blaNDM-21 was detected: a CRKP background with virulence-associated features, an IncX3 plasmid-associated resistance determinant, and infections occurring in a vulnerable pediatric/NICU setting. This combination may complicate treatment, infection control, and surveillance strategies.

CRE continues to be a significant pathogen causing healthcare-associated infections and poses an ongoing global public health threat. The World Health Organization has classified CRE as a priority pathogen, necessitating the development of new antimicrobials due to its association with high mortality rates [15]. For instance, a prospective study in the US reported a 30-day mortality rate of 27% [16]. Additionally, regional variations in CRE resistance gene distribution have been reported globally. For example, OXA-48-type carbapenemases are prevalent in Spanish K. pneumoniae [17], while research from Iran has highlighted horizontal gene transfer as a mechanism for disseminating OXA-48 and blaNDM-1 [18]. Similar challenges exist in China, where carbapenem resistance in K. pneumoniae ranges from 15.8% to 37.0% in a tertiary children’s hospital in Shandong [19]. Furthermore, there is an age stratification in CRE resistance genotypes in China, with NDM dominating in children (under 18 years; 47.8%), while KPC-2 predominates in adults (18–64 years; 60.2%) and elderly patients (65 years and older; 66.9%). Notably, CRKP carries a 41.2% mortality rate in elderly patients with brain injuries [20,21].

Extensive documentation of CRE genotypes exists worldwide. Clinical isolates carrying blaNDM-5, blaNDM-1 (ST11, IncX3 plasmid), and co-occurring blaKPC-2/NDM-1 have been reported in Italy, Pakistan, and Brazil [22–24]. Since its initial identification in 2007 in a Swedish patient [24], NDM has spread rapidly, particularly across South Asia and the Middle East. In China, NDM is the second most common CRE carbapenemase [25], occupying a dominant position in pediatric populations [1].

It was reported that the first incidence of E. coli and K. pneumoniae concurrently producing blaNDM-5 and OXA-181, facilitated by the highly transmissible IncX3 plasmid (blaNDM-5). This emergence has posed significant challenges for clinical treatment in young children in Jiangsu Province [26]. With the increasing prevalence of NDM, blaNDM-1 and blaNDM-5 have become the predominant subtypes in CRKP and CRE, respectively [1]. This study has shown that the blaNDM-21 gene, examined herein, evolved from blaNDM-5. The close phylogenetic relationship between NDM-21 and NDM-5, distinguishable by a single amino acid substitution (Gly69Ser), has significant functional and evolutionary implications [8]. These findings suggest that NDM-5 may act as a genetic platform for new variant emergence. The characteristic genetic background of NDM-5 (Val88Leu + Met154Leu) not only persists but also spreads across multiple variants, including NDM-17, NDM-20, and NDM-21 [8,27–29]. The detection of these variants in various regions and hosts within China indicates efficient dissemination by plasmids carrying blaNDM, particularly epidemic IncX3-type plasmids associated with NDM-5 and its derivatives. Consequently, enhanced molecular surveillance and epidemiological investigations of the NDM-5 lineage are imperative; this lineage represents both a persistent source of antimicrobial resistance dissemination and a potential reservoir for further evolution, posing a substantial threat to clinical infection control.

This study reports the global pediatric infections caused by blaNDM-21 (a rare carbapenemase gene)-harboring CRKP (n = 3 strains). The first three cases of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP were isolated from critically ill neonates. All strains originated from preterm neonates transferred from other hospitals. Dynamic CRP profiles indicated that HS898 and HS915 acquired CRKP during hospitalization, whereas HS910 was apparently infected prior to admission. The HS898 infection was managed in the CCU. HS910 was diagnosed with CRKP at multiple anatomical sites, including the central nervous system (CNS), a rare occurrence in infections caused by K. pneumoniae, indicative of severe systemic infection.

HS915 became infected one week after HS910’s admission into the NICU, evidencing partially overlapping infection timelines among the three neonates. Initially, all patients were treated in the main NICU area, but HS910 and HS915 were subsequently moved to isolated rooms following infection confirmation. The main NICU area, featuring shared clinical spaces such as a centralized nursing station, communal handwashing stations, and mobile medication carts, likely increased the risk of cross-infection among pediatric patients. The common time and space in bed occupancy highlighted shared exposure risks to CRKP. Spatiotemporal analysis supports the likelihood of nosocomial transmission, although an introduction of the strain from community or other healthcare sources cannot be entirely ruled out.

The clinical and public health significance of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP is particularly crucial in NICU settings, where critically ill neonates are highly vulnerable to invasive infections and nosocomial transmission. The probable transmission between HS910 and HS915 underscores the need for early identification of CRE-positive patients, strict contact precautions, cohorting or isolation when feasible, reinforcement of hand hygiene, environmental disinfection, and careful management of shared medical equipment [30]. Additionally, the presence of blaNDM-21 on a transferable IncX3 plasmid raises concerns about horizontal dissemination among Enterobacterales, potentially enabling this rare carbapenemase gene to spread beyond the original ST35 K. pneumoniae clone [31], [32]. Such plasmid-mediated dissemination could further limit therapeutic options, particularly for neonates, whose antimicrobial choices are already limited [33]. Consequently, susceptibility-guided therapy, antimicrobial stewardship, routine CRE screening, and molecular surveillance integrating strain-level and plasmid-level analysis are essential for early detection and containment of emerging blaNDM-21-producing CRKP in high-risk pediatric settings.

In this study, the three isolates had multidrug resistance to carbapenems, β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, and cephalosporins, consistent with a high-resistance phenotype. The successful treatment of all patients with high-level antibiotics provides indirect evidence of the considerable pathogenicity of this CRKP strain. As revealed by genomic analysis, strains HS898, HS910, and HS915 exhibited high genetic homogeneity, with minimal core-genome SNP differences (<25). Their identical resistance and virulence gene profiles may contribute to the fitness and dissemination potential of this clone in healthcare settings. The above-mentioned findings underscore the critical importance of integrating genomic surveillance into routine infection control to promptly identify and interrupt transmission chains. Notably, this resistance profile was closely linked to a mobile genetic element carrying the blaNDM-21gene. WGS localized blaNDM-21 to an IncX3 plasmid, a vector confirmed to exhibit high conjugative potential [34]. As our subsequent conjugation assay demonstrated, this blaNDM-21-bearing plasmid revealed efficient transfer, significantly increasing the risk of intra-hospital horizontal transmission and complicating infection control efforts.

Moreover, the phenotypic analysis of three ST35 (KL108, O1/O2v2) blaNDM-21-harboring strains revealed several characteristics. Broad-spectrum resistance was observed: Multiple resistance genes (blaCTX-M-14, blaSHV-33, blaLAP-2, and blaNDM-21) were identified through WGS. Clinical MICs confirmed resistance to penicillins and cephalosporins, mediated by blaCTX-M-14/SHV-33 [35,36], and to carbapenems, mediated by blaNDM-21. Additionally, the presence of LAP-2 suggests potential roles in resistance and virulence, although further studies are necessary to elucidate its functions [37]. Concerning virulence potential, notable virulence factors included wzi194 (associated with capsule synthesis [38]), ybt2 (yersiniabactin), truncated iro3, iroN (salmochelin receptor), and rmpA (associated with hypermucoviscosity) [39–41]. In terms of physiological phenotypes, robust metabolic activity was observed in growth curves, with these strains classified as exhibiting low mucoviscosity/adherence, as indicated by mucoviscosity assays. Previous studies have suggested that strong biofilm formation may contribute significantly to antibiotic resistance and evasion of immune responses [42]. TEM confirmed the presence of capsules, which aid in resisting phagocytosis [38]. The virulence-associated phenotype of these isolates should be interpreted with caution. The three blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates possessed several virulence-associated determinants, including rmpA, ybt2, iroB, and iroN, exhibited strong biofilm formation, displayed capsule-related structural characteristics, and demonstrated marked resistance to serum-mediated killing. In the serum killing assay, strains HS898, HS910, and HS915 exhibited significantly higher bacterial survival than ATCC 13,883 and showed a serum resistance phenotype comparable to that of ATCC 43,816. These results suggest that the isolates are capable of resisting complement-mediated killing, which represents an important virulence-associated phenotype. However, the evidence currently available does not support classifying these isolates as definitively classical hypervirulent or unequivocally highly pathogenic K. pneumoniae strains. In both the Galleria mellonella and mouse infection models, HS898, HS910, and HS915 displayed greater pathogenicity than the attenuated ΔcueOΔcopA control but lower virulence than the classical hypervirulent reference strain ATCC 43,816 [42]. Consequently, these isolates are considered to exhibit an intermediate-to-enhanced virulence-associated phenotype rather than being categorized as definitively highly pathogenic or classical hypervirulent.

In murine bacteremia models: At a low-dose infection (104 CFU), significantly higher organ colonization was observed in ATCC 43,816 compared to the blaNDM-21 strains (p < 0.05) [43]. At a high-dose infection (106 CFU), blaNDM-21 strains surpassed ΔcueOΔcopA in organ colonization across all observed sites (p < 0.01). Significant histopathological damage to organs was also noted, including renal tubular necrosis, hepatic sinusoidal congestion with disrupted cord architecture, and pulmonary alveolar wall thickening with inflammatory infiltration. Collectively, the blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP strains, while less virulent than classical hypervirulent strains, demonstrated substantial multi-organ invasiveness and pathological potential. In this study, the blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP strains demonstrated a lethal dose in mice of approximately 106 CFU, indicating a level of virulence that exceeds that of typical K. pneumoniae strains, which generally require ≥ 107 CFU to precipitate lethal infection [10].

Several limitations of this study ought to be acknowledged. Firstly, the inclusion of only three blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP isolates from three pediatric patients at a single center significantly limits the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Consequently, the results should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of the emergence of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP with enhanced virulence-associated features in pediatric/NICU-associated infections, rather than as evidence of broader prevalence or regional dissemination. Secondly, although the spatiotemporal overlap and close genomic relatedness support probable nosocomial transmission between isolates HS910 and HS915, the limited number of isolates and the absence of extensive environmental or colonization screening prevent a definitive reconstruction of the complete transmission chain. Thirdly, comparison with previously reported blaNDM-21-positive strains revealed that blaNDM-21 has predominantly been described in E. coli. In contrast, the isolates in our study were identified as K. pneumoniae ST35-KL108-O1/O2v2 (Supplementary Table S2). Notably, blaNDM-21 was consistently linked to IncX3 plasmids, suggesting that IncX3 plasmids may play a crucial role in the dissemination of blaNDM-21-like variants across different Enterobacterales hosts. However, given that publicly available blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP genomes remain scarce, future multicenter genomic surveillance involving a broader spectrum of clinical, colonizing, and environmental isolates is essential to elucidate the prevalence, evolutionary trajectory, and transmission dynamics of this emerging CRKP clone.

The emergence of blaNDM-21-harboring CRKP presents a significant concern due to its triad of attributes: high resistance, characterized by broad-spectrum resistance including to carbapenems; potential for horizontal transferability via IncX3 plasmid-mediated mechanisms; and an enhanced virulence-associated phenotype, demonstrated by the presence of multiple virulence factors with confirmed pathogenicity. This triad has the potential to exacerbate clinical management challenges, increase risks of nosocomial transmission, and elevate the likelihood of adverse pediatric outcomes. It is imperative to implement stringent infection control measures, such as enhanced contact isolation, environmental disinfection, hand hygiene, and antimicrobial stewardship, alongside active CRE surveillance and molecular tracking, particularly for novel resistance genotypes. These measures are crucial for limiting clinical transmission, reducing pediatric infection risks, and mitigating disease burden. Moreover, continuous monitoring is essential for the early detection of emerging resistance variants like blaNDM-21.
