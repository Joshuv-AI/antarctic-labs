Critical nodes identification and cascading failure mitigation strategies in multi-layer coupled networks of interdependent critical infrastructures
Authors: Jinning Liu, Fangmin Yang, Mingqi Jiang, Hui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo mitigate cascading failures in critical infrastructure networks, existing studies have conducted extensive research on the identification and protection of critical nodes. However, they have overlooked the differential characteristics between structural centrality and stability, which may lead to limitations in the selection of critical nodes in multi-layer coupled networks and result in insufficient cascading failure mitigation effects. To address this gap, this study constructs a multi-layer coupled network integrating hospital management coordination, water supply, and power supply, focusing on flow-based physical interactions between layers and the supporting role of management coordination. Based on Coupled PageRank and an improved Station Lyapunov-stability based (SLB) method, critical nodes are identified, and a path-based critical node protection strategy is proposed. The results show that in load-driven coupled networks, unstable nodes are high-risk nodes prone to inducing cascading failures, and the impact of nodes with high structural centrality on the spread of cascading failures cannot be ignored. In addition, based on the proposed protection method, selecting and protecting a small number of critical nodes can effectively mitigate the spread of network cascading failures in the early stage. These findings are helpful for the management and protection of multi-layer coupled infrastructure networks under cascading failures.Keywords: Critical infrastructure, Multi-layer coupling network, Critical node, Cascading failure, Mitigation strategySubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Critical Infrastructures (CIs) provide essential services such as electricity, transportation, water supply, and medical care to society1. They are highly interconnected, and their operations rely on their own various services2. However, this also means that a failure in one critical infrastructure may spread to other connected infrastructures within the system they form, thereby impairing functionality and operability3. For instance, the connection between water and power networks is becoming increasingly close4, and the network load distribution is constantly changing; a minor local issue may trigger a chain reaction, leading to the spread of large-scale system failures5. The consequences can be catastrophic, potentially collapsing the entire urban medical service system and causing incalculable losses6. Therefore, the interdependencies between critical infrastructure networks cannot be ignored7, and it is necessary to identify and protect important nodes in critical infrastructure networks.

Critical nodes are important factors affecting the propagation of cascading failures, and the development of their identification methods has always centered on network topology and functional characteristics. Current research mainly focuses on general complex networks; studies on evaluating node importance to identify critical nodes assess node importance through the centrality of nodes in the network topology. Nodes with higher centrality are considered more significant and important, such as betweenness centrality8, closeness centrality9, PageRank centrality10, and Eigenvector Centrality11. Subsequently, some scholars have proposed other improved centrality indicators to evaluate node importance. For example, Kitsak et al. introduced the K-Shell value to assess node importance, defining nodes in the inner layer of the network as critical nodes12; Ruan et al. proposed a node importance evaluation method that considers both the degree of the node and its neighbors13; Bian et al. developed a node influence identification method based on the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), incorporating multiple centrality measures as multi-attributes of complex networks14. Yang et al. integrated factors such as out-degree and in-degree in risk networks and proposed an improved K-shell method to rank the importance of network nodes15. Ghoshal et al. thoroughly explored the stability of the PageRank algorithm under different network topologies, particularly the differences between random networks and scale-free networks. They found that scale-free networks exhibit higher robustness to network perturbations due to the presence of super-stable nodes, whose emergence is closely related to the network’s degree distribution and becomes more significant as the network scale increases16. Engsig et al. introduced the centrality measure DomiRank to quantify the dominance of network nodes in their respective neighborhoods, integrating local and global topological information through adjustable parameters. The results showed that DomiRank systematically outperforms other centrality indicators in generating targeted attacks that effectively damage network structure and disrupt the functionality of both synthetic and real-world topologies17. In Water Distribution Network (WDN) systems, Agathokleous et al. found that critical components can be directly identified through local topological indicators. For example, edge betweenness centrality reflects the flow volume through an edge, and high betweenness centrality indicates that edge failure may cause severe disruptions. Thus, betweenness centrality can serve as an indicator for identifying vulnerable edges18. However, Weber et al. demonstrated that the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient between centrality indicators and criticality has limited applicability19. A large number of studies have been developed and deployed for identifying important nodes in complex networks. Methods based on local structures (e.g., degree centrality, PageRank) and global structures (e.g., betweenness, closeness centrality) are used to evaluate relevant nodes. Despite their impressive performance, these methods have inherent limitations. For example, local structure-based methods lose some global information, while global structure-based methods are overly complex to measure important nodes, especially as network scale increases. Based on these perspectives, Ullah et al. designed an algorithm called LGC that considers both the local and global structures of nodes in the graph to efficiently and effectively measure important nodes20. To enhance the effective and accurate identification of critical nodes in the power grid, Zeng et al. proposed a method. This method is based on improved PageRank algorithm and node-weighted power flow transfer entropy, referred to as IPRA-PFTE21. To enhance the reliability of distribution networks and improve their emergency response capabilities, Liu et al. proposed a fast dynamic identification algorithm (FDIA) for key nodes in distribution networks22.

Other studies have focused on the impact of node stability on cascading failures. A characteristic of cascading failures is that failures quickly spread from initial failed nodes to vulnerable nodes in the same layer or across layers. Some studies argue that node stability cannot be ignored in its impact on cascading failures—unstable nodes tend to exacerbate cascading failures, and this small number of vulnerable nodes should be protected first. Feng et al. conducted an in-depth study of cascading failure behavior in regular networks by proposing a matching cascade model that comprehensively analyzes potential overloads of nodes and edges. They found that regular networks can resist single-point failures under certain conditions, and their robustness increases with the number of edges. Additionally, the paper analyzed the robustness of WS networks and BA networks and proposed a new critical node identification indicator—modified betweenness centrality—which performed excellently in experiments23. Smolyak et al. proposed a critical node identification algorithm based on local neighborhood information and failure mechanisms, finding that some vulnerable nodes play a key role in failure propagation and exacerbation. These nodes can be identified solely through their local environment, and protecting these vulnerable nodes can efficiently mitigate cascading failure processes24.

With the deepening of research, the demand for node identification in interdependent infrastructure networks has spawned new methods. Li et al. extended the single-layer network PageRank algorithm by introducing information feedback to describe interactions between different layers, proposing a coupled information feedback algorithm to measure node centrality in multi-layer networks. This Coupled PageRank (CPR) feedback mechanism reveals the global importance brought about by interdependencies across different layers25. Li et al. proposed a key node identification method based on the improved Adaptive Differential Evolution (JADE), designed to identify a set of critical nodes that significantly impact the reliability and robustness of Cyber-Physical Power Systems (CPPS), thereby effectively reducing the risk of lockout failures26. Lu et al. proposed a framework for cascading failure propagation in infrastructure networks, constructing a multi-layer network with an implicit interdependency model and using PageRank to identify critical nodes. Simulations of different attack scenarios explored failure dynamics, and the community perimeter enhancement strategy was proven optimal for resilience. This work links node importance to cascading effects, offering actionable insights for disaster management27. Mesa et al. proposed a new Dynamic Inoperability Input–Output Model (DIIM) to simulate the cascading disruption dynamics in multi-state interdependent critical infrastructures. The proposed method can conduct in-depth analysis of the internal states of interdependent power and water network critical infrastructures and identify critical nodes with interdependencies with other critical infrastructures28. Yuan et al. proposed an EW-TOPSIS-based method to identify critical nodes in dynamic-load Water Supply System Network (WSN), emphasizing dynamic load effects on node importance ignored by prior studies. A cascading failure model correlating load with link capacity was built to analyze failure propagation, and node removal impacts under varying loads served as evaluation criteria. The work provides insights for mitigating cascading failures in resource-constrained networks29.

Addressing the need for cascading failure mitigation, scholars have formed a series of research outcomes from two dimensions—protection and restoration—combining the operational characteristics of infrastructure networks. For protection, the anti-destruction capability of nodes can be enhanced—for example, adding backup power supplies and communication lines in coupled power and communication networks to reduce the impact of critical node failures. Redundancy design can also be adopted by adding extra nodes and connections to improve system fault tolerance, or adjusting network topology to reduce coupling strength, increase degree correlation, and enhance system robustness. For restoration, priority should be given to repairing critical nodes and connections using a phased approach: first restoring parts that are crucial for system recovery, then gradually expanding to the entire network. Strategies can also be formulated by combining the community structure and spatial embedding characteristics of the network30. Many studies have begun applying traditional topological indicators to infrastructure network cascading failure mitigation strategies. For example, DAI et al. identified network critical nodes based on degree centrality (DC), betweenness centrality (BC), and closeness centrality (CC), and constructed a dynamic model of power-information coupled networks using coupled map lattice (CML) to simulate dynamic cascading processes triggered by failures of each critical node under different scenarios. The three types of critical nodes (DC, BC, CC) are vulnerable to deliberate attacks, all leading to large-scale failure diffusion in coupled networks. Among them, BC critical nodes are the most sensitive to attacks, followed by DC, and CC critical nodes exhibit the best robustness31. Due to increased interdependencies and interconnectivity, Water Supply Networks (WSN) and Electric Power Networks (EPN) face higher risks of cascading failures. To analyze Interdependent Water-Power Networks (IWPN) under seismic action, combined with physical operational characteristics, Li et al. proposed a cascading failure analysis method for interdependent water-power networks based on functional coupling. The proposed method was applied to the coupling of a city’s WSN and the IEEE 118-node network32. The results of this work will be of great significance for formulating disaster prevention and mitigation measures and seismic performance improvement strategies for interdependent infrastructure networks. Critical infrastructures are becoming increasingly interdependent and susceptible to cascading failures. Existing studies have analyzed the vulnerability of interdependent networks to cascading failures from a static perspective of network topology. Zhang et al. developed a cascading failure model considering dynamic load redistribution in power grids, explored the vulnerability of interdependent water-power networks, and proposed a critical fault tolerance threshold indicating network vulnerability to cascading failures. They found that cascading failures can be prevented when the tolerance parameter value is higher than the critical tolerance threshold; increased interdependence strength makes the network more vulnerable; and the network is most susceptible to damage under high-load attacks. Therefore, these interconnected nodes and high-load nodes should be protected to reduce the consequences of cascading failures33. Feng et al. designed a structural importance evaluation method based on the TOPSIS method, using multiple indicators such as betweenness centrality (CB) to assess node structural importance, compared six types of protection strategies considering protection costs, and selected the optimal node protection strategy34. Yuan et al. introduced the concept of reinforced nodes into interdependent infrastructure networks, finding that a small number of reinforced nodes can prevent sudden catastrophic collapses, and established percolation theory to predict the minimum proportion of reinforced nodes required to prevent collapses35. Yan et al. proposed a method for identifying relatively critical nodes in cyber-physical power systems based on relative weighted entropy. This approach enables the extraction of valuable insights from these key nodes, identifying which nodes hold relative importance and thereby mitigating the cascading effects of failures to some extent36. Wang et al. established a network model of urban critical infrastructures (power, transportation, communication), clarifying their coupling mechanisms. Under extreme rainfall scenarios, the model reveals the nonlinear spatiotemporal evolution of flood disaster chains across point-line-plane failure stages. Analysis confirms that protecting critical nodes mitigates flood impacts, offering insights for urban flood management and boosting urban disaster resilience37. In reality, once a node in an infrastructure network fails, the entire system may collapse completely, resulting in enormous economic losses and casualties. Implementing appropriate node restoration strategies can effectively prevent system collapse caused by cascading failures in complex networks. Xiao et al. proposed a framework and method for evaluating the resilience of lifeline networks. Based on the functional characteristics of interdependent lifelines, their performance response functions are constructed. Taking the IEEE 30-node power network and the Belgian 20-node natural gas interdependent network as examples, the effectiveness of the framework is verified. The results show that for each network, recovery resources and budgets have different impacts on resilience38. Zhang et al. proposed a network cascading failure propagation model equipped with a node emergency recovery function. Through numerical simulations and analysis of typical case scenarios, the network cascading failures when nodes have an emergency recovery probability are explored39. Although scholars have conducted extensive research on centrality indicators and stability of nodes in critical infrastructure networks, even considering dynamic indicators, existing studies have overlooked the differential characteristics between structural centrality and stability. This may lead to limitations in the selection of critical nodes, especially in the case of limited research on cascading failures in multi-layer coupled networks.

In summary, existing research has the following gaps: (I) Existing Critical Infrastructures network critical node identification methods do not consider the differences between node stability and structural centrality characteristics, which may result in limitations in the application scenarios of identified critical nodes. For example, some nodes are structurally central but highly stable and less prone to failures; others have low structural centrality but are highly unstable, triggering chain reactions once they fail. (II) In mitigating cascading failures, critical node protection is mostly based on static topological indicator rankings. Even though some scholars have proposed dynamic protection strategies based on topological attributes, they ignore the dynamic protection of central nodes in cascading failure paths and do not consider the impact of different initial failed nodes on cascading failures, which may lead to insufficient early protection effects. (III) When applying traditional topological indicators for critical node protection, the definition of the number of protected nodes is vague, and no research has been conducted on the specific number of protected nodes, which may result in selecting an excessive number of protected nodes. To fill the above research gaps, this study aims to identify and protect critical nodes in multi-layer coupled networks that may exacerbate cascading failures. When this study identifies critical nodes, it takes into account the inherent attributes of nodes; when selecting critical nodes for cascading failure mitigation, this study accounts for important nodes along cascading failure propagation paths, and this study argues that structurally central nodes on these paths are more critical.

Main contributions

Methodological innovation

We propose an improved Station Lyapunov-stability based (SLB) method that integrates structural centrality (CPR) and node stability, addressing the long-standing limitation of traditional methods that only consider a single dimension.

Strategy innovation

We propose a path-based critical node protection strategy that focuses on the propagation paths of cascading failures, rather than just ranking nodes based on static topological indicators.

Practical contribution

We construct a real three-layer coupled network of hospital management-water supply-power supply, and verify the effectiveness of the proposed methods through four typical failure scenarios, providing a quantitative basis for hospital infrastructure protection resource allocation.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section "Methodology" elaborates on the proposed CPR, improved SLB method, and protection strategies. Section "Case study" presents a case study of a multi-layer coupled infrastructure network. Section "Results and discussion" shows and discusses the results. Finally, Section "Conclusions" summarizes the research findings.

Coupling network model

Conceptual framework of multi-layer coupled network

Based on the interdependent network framework proposed by Rinaldi et al.43, the dependency relationship between the management coordination network and the water supply and power supply networks is defined as logical dependency, and the dependency relationship between the water supply network and the power supply network is defined as physical dependency. It is assumed that all constructed networks are unweighted and bidirectional. Results and discussion

Concept of node importance in interdependent multi-layer networks

In interdependent multi-layer coupled networks, node importance is defined as the comprehensive impact of nodes on network stability and cascading failure propagation, involving three core dimensions:(1) Structural centrality: Reflected by CPR centrality, emphasizing the node’s position in the network topology (e.g., betweenness, connection density). Nodes with high structural centrality (e.g., One-stop Maintenance Reporting Platform) act as ‘information hubs’ and are critical to cross-layer failure propagation.(2) Load criticality: Reflected by SLB score, emphasizing the node’s load-bearing capacity and business demand (e.g., power distribution cabinets supplying ICUs). Nodes with high load criticality are prone to overload failure and trigger local cascades.(3) Stability risk: Reflected by Lyapunov stability coefficient η, emphasizing the node’s ability to resist load disturbances. Unstable nodes (e.g., Director of Logistics Support Department with multiple dependencies) are prone to exacerbating cross-layer failure diffusion.For connecting nodes (e.g., road intersections providing access to water main breaks not included in this study), their importance lies in ‘path criticality’—acting as bridges between layers, and their failure will block load transfer and increase recovery difficulty. This study integrates the three dimensions to identify critical nodes, making up for the one-sidedness of single-dimension evaluation.

Kendall coefficient(Kendall’s τ) mainly analyzes the correlation between ranks. It has no requirement for data distribution and is more tolerant of extreme values (e.g., extreme values such as 62,914.12 in SLB scores and 675,868.96 in Lyapunov stability can be well handled).

This coefficient is very suitable for the characteristics of hospital node data, which has many zero values, scattered data, and diverse functions. It can reflect the degree of association between the ranking orders of variables, with a value range of -1 to 1. Among them, τ > 0 indicates a positive correlation, and τ < 0 indicates a negative correlation; an absolute value ≥ 0.5 is a strong correlation, an absolute value between 0.3 and 0.5 is a moderate correlation, and an absolute value < 0.3 is a weak correlation.

As shown in Fig. 3, the correlation analysis heatmap reveals that: The three indicators (Betweenness, Degree, PageRank) are moderately positively correlated with CPR, with a correlation coefficient τ of no less than 0.30. This indicates that these topological indicators yield consistent results in identifying structurally central nodes.

Kendall correlation analysis.

The SLB score is moderately positively correlated with Lyapunov stability, with a correlation coefficient τ of 0.39. Among nodes with high SLB scores (SLB ≥ 2400), 90% have a Lyapunov stability of no less than 200,000, belonging to high instability risk nodes.

Nodes with high SLB scores and nodes with high CPR values have a weak correlation, with a correlation coefficient τ of only 0.05. Nodes with high CPR values are negatively correlated with nodes with high Lyapunov stability, with a correlation coefficient τ of -0.33. This indicates that nodes with high SLB scores are not necessarily structurally central nodes with high CPR values.

These two indicators represent different critical dimensions: a high SLB score indicates criticality in the load dimension, and a high CPR value indicates criticality in the structural dimension. There is no significant correlation between them.

Cascade failure mitigation effect of proposed protection strategy

Mitigation effect of different protection node numbers

Four groups of initial failure nodes were defined to observe and compare the cascading failure mitigation effects of different protection strategies. This scenario targets four groups of initial failure nodes with different combinations, simulates the failure rate under different numbers of protected nodes, and determines the cascading failure mitigation effect and optimal number of protected nodes (top_s) for different protection strategies.

Based on two types of protection strategies—CPR (path/ranking) and SLB (path/ranking)—this study compares and analyzes the performance and variation trends of the two types of strategies under different numbers of protected nodes in scenarios with simultaneous multi-node failures. The following explanation is based on the analysis diagrams of four specific scenarios (Fig. 5).

Failure ratio under different numbers of protection nodes (Note: The curve shows the mitigation effect of four strategies; CPR path strategy has an obvious threshold at 5 nodes, which is the optimal protection number).

In Fig. 5(a) Scenario 1, which includes cross-network structural and non-structural hybrid nodes such as the Central Power Distribution Room and Director of Logistics Support, the analysis results show significant differences among different strategies. The failure ratio curve of CPR path protection drops sharply in a stepwise manner: when the number of protected nodes ≥ 3, the failure ratio plummets from 57.61% to 31.52%; when the number of protected nodes ≥ 4, it further stabilizes at 23.91%; finally, when the number of protected nodes ≥ 8, it decreases to 7.61% and remains stable. In contrast, the failure ratio curve of SLB path protection decreases gently without an obvious threshold effect, gradually dropping from 58.70% to 29.35% with a reduction of only 50.0%. The curve of CPR ranking protection shows a lagged sharp drop characteristic: when the number of protected nodes < 2, the failure ratio is the same as that in the unprotected scenario (66.30%); it only starts to take effect when the number of protected nodes ≥ 2, and finally reaches the same 7.61% as CPR path protection when the number of protected nodes ≥ 17. The initial protection of the CPR ranking strategy has a low matching degree with the failure diffusion path. SLB ranking protection performs the worst, with the failure ratio slowly dropping from 61.96% to 34.78% (a reduction of only 43.9%) and remaining higher than CPR-based strategies throughout.

In Fig. 5(b) Scenario 2, which consists of a single-network failure group of structural nodes such as Municipal Water Supply Interfaces and Water Transmission Pipe Networks in the East Ward Building, the failure ratio curve of CPR path protection shows a mid-term threshold stability characteristic: when the number of protected nodes ≥ 5, the failure ratio plummets from 43.48% to 30.43% and remains stable for a long time. CPR ranking protection shows early advantages and mid-term parity in this group: when the number of protected nodes < 5, the failure ratio (35.87%) is better than other strategies; when the number of protected nodes ≥ 5, the failure ratio is the same as that of CPR path protection (30.43%). The mid-to-late protection efficiency of the CPR ranking strategy is lower than that of the CPR path. Both the SLB path and ranking protection curves decrease slowly: the SLB path needs ≥ 13 protected nodes to drop to 33.70% and finally stabilizes at 31.52%; the SLB ranking protection needs ≥ 11 protected nodes to drop to 34.78%.

In Fig. 5(c) Scenario 3, which includes a full-network paralysis risk group such as the One-stop Maintenance Reporting Platform and Central Power Distribution Cabinet No.6, the failure ratio curve of CPR path protection shows a rapid sharp drop to the bottom characteristic: when only 5 nodes are protected, the failure ratio plummets from 79.35% to 31.52% and remains stable for a long time. The SLB path protection curve shows an inefficient linear decrease, slowly dropping from 82.61% to 57.61% with a reduction of only 30.3%, and the failure ratio is more than 40% higher than that of the CPR path throughout. The CPR ranking protection curve shows mid-term effectiveness but lags: when the number of protected nodes ≥ 3, the failure ratio drops from 81.52% to 48.91%, but it needs ≥ 8 protected nodes to approach the stable value of the CPR path (31.52%). The SLB ranking protection curve has the highest starting point (89.13%) and the smallest reduction (35.4%), finally stabilizing at 57.61%.

In Fig. 5(d) Scenario 4, which includes a node failure group such as the Ophthalmology Operating Room and ICU, the failure ratio curve of CPR path protection shows a characteristic of first rising, then falling, and gradually stabilizing: when the number of protected nodes < 4, the failure ratio temporarily rises from 83.70%; when the number of protected nodes ≥ 4, it breaks through the threshold, gradually dropping from 43.48% to 30.43% and stabilizing. The SLB path protection curve shows early advantages but insufficient late-term protection effects: when the number of protected nodes < 3, the failure ratio (70.65%) is better than that of the CPR path; when the number of protected nodes ≥ 9, it is surpassed, finally stabilizing at 48.91%. The CPR ranking protection curve shows a mid-term stable decrease, slowly dropping from 71.74% to 30.43%, but it needs ≥ 17 protected nodes to reach the stable value of the CPR path. The SLB ranking protection curve is gentle throughout, consistent with the trend of the SLB path, finally stabilizing at 48.91% and always higher than CPR-based strategies.

Based on the analysis of cascading failure mitigation curves in four failure scenarios, the following conclusions are drawn: (1) In failure groups with concentrated high-CPR structural nodes (Scenarios 1 and 3), CPR path protection shows a rapid sharp drop characteristic. Protecting 3–5 nodes can significantly reduce the failure ratio, which can accurately identify and block structural dependency paths; in failure groups dominated by business nodes (Scenario 4), although there is initial lag, efficient stability can still be achieved by covering supporting structural paths in the later stage. (2) SLB-based strategies show linear slow decreases in all scenarios without obvious threshold effects, and the final failure ratio is always higher than that of CPR-based strategies, only temporarily superior in the very early stage of pure business node failures (number of protected nodes = 1 in Scenario 4). (3) The CPR ranking protection curve is between the two, with mid-term effectiveness but lag. It needs 3–5 more protected nodes than the CPR path to achieve the same effect, and the initial matching degree with the failure diffusion path is low. (4) Node attributes are the main cause of trend differences. The higher the proportion of high-CPR structural nodes in the failure scenario, the more significant the sharp drop characteristic of the CPR path; the higher the proportion of business nodes or external associated nodes, the initial lag of the CPR path may occur, but the long-term protection efficiency is still dominant.

In summary, in cascading failure protection scenarios with simultaneous multi-node failures, CPR path protection is the optimal strategy for identifying structural dependency paths and curbing cross-network cascades. Its sharply dropping curve trend intuitively reflects the efficient containment capability for structural cascading failures; SLB-based strategies are only applicable to single-network failure scenarios dominated by pure loads, with limited scope of application.

The final failure ratio of degree centrality protection drops to 22.83%, an improvement of 52.27% compared with the unprotected strategy, which is lower than that of CPR path protection. The sensitivity analysis diagram (Fig. 7b) shows that when the number of protected nodes ≥ 4, the failure ratio of degree centrality protection linearly drops from 46.74 to 22.83% and stabilizes. In the dynamic evolution diagram of cascading failures (Fig. 8b), in terms of curbing the failure diffusion speed, CPR path protection is better than other strategies; in terms of the final failure ratio, CPR path protection is slightly higher than degree centrality protection but much lower than other strategies.

In Scenario 3, the final failure ratio under the unprotected strategy is as high as 91.30%. When the number of protected nodes ≤ 5, CPR path protection performs the best, with a final failure ratio of 31.52%, an improvement of 65.48% compared with the unprotected strategy. The sensitivity analysis (Fig. 7c) shows that when the number of protected nodes ≥ 4, the failure ratio of CPR path protection plummets from 79.35% to 45.65%; when the number of protected nodes ≥ 5, it stabilizes at 31.52%, but the later failure ratio is slightly higher than that of degree centrality and betweenness centrality protection. In the dynamic evolution diagram of cascading failures (Fig. 8c), when the number of protected nodes S = 5, the failure ratio of CPR path protection tends to flatten after the time step ≥ 8, verifying its long-term inhibitory capability for cross-system cascading failures.

In Scenario 4, the final failure ratio under the unprotected strategy is 72.83%. When the number of protected nodes ≤ 5, CPR path protection and degree centrality protection perform equally well, with a final failure ratio of 33.70%, an improvement of 53.73% compared with the unprotected strategy. The sensitivity analysis diagram (Fig. 7d) shows that when the number of protected nodes ≥ 4, the failure ratios of both strategies synchronously drop from 83.70% to 43.48%; when the number of protected nodes > 5, they stabilize at 33.70%. In the dynamic evolution diagram of cascading failures (Fig. 8d), the growth of the failure ratios of both strategies slows down significantly after the time step ≥ 6, fully reflecting their inhibitory effects on cascading failures.

Based on the dynamic evolution and sensitivity analysis results of cascading failures in four groups of scenarios, the conclusion can be drawn: when the number of protected nodes S = 5, CPR path protection shows the optimal comprehensive stability in various scenarios. Its advantages are concentrated in two dimensions: first, it has outstanding failure diffusion control capabilities, which can effectively curb the rapid rise of the failure ratio in all scenarios and avoid the network from falling into a rapid collapse state; second, the final failure ratio is maintained at a low level, especially in the high-risk Scenario 3, with an improvement of more than 65% compared with the unprotected strategy, and the protection efficiency is significant. Compared with traditional protection strategies such as betweenness centrality and degree centrality, although CPR path protection has slight gaps in the early convergence speed or final failure ratio in some scenarios, it has better overall protection stability and scenario universality, and can adapt to various complex scenarios such as concentrated structural core nodes, cross-system dependencies, and failures of supporting links of business nodes. Its advantage stems from the accurate identification and targeted protection of network structural dependency links, breaking through the limitations of traditional strategies that only focus on node attributes.

Cross-strategy comparison

To transform the original descriptive results into quantifiable and comparable performance indicators, we have defined three core metrics for cascading failure mitigation, and conducted a full cross-comparison of 7 strategies (CPR path-based, CPR rank-based, SLB path-based, SLB rank-based, Betweenness Centrality, Degree Centrality, and traditional PageRank) across all 4 failure scenarios using the existing simulation data. The metrics and key results are as followst.

Final Failure Mitigation Efficiency (FFME), this metric is defined as:

It quantifies the overall mitigation effect of the strategy on cascading failures, with a higher value indicating better performance.

The quantitative results clearly show that the CPR path strategy achieves the highest average FFME across all scenarios, with a lead of more than 18 percentage points over the second-best traditional strategy (Betweenness Centrality) at the same number of protected nodes (5 nodes). Even in Scenario 2 (single-layer water supply network failure), where the performance gap is the smallest, the CPR path strategy still outperforms all SLB-based strategies and traditional centrality strategies.

Early Failure Containment Capability (EFCC), this metric is defined by two sub-indicators: (a) the reduction of the peak failure ratio in the first 6 time steps (the rapid outbreak phase of cascading failures); (b) the time step required for the failure ratio to stabilize. It quantifies the strategy’s ability to curb the rapid spread of failures in the early stage, which is the core practical demand for infrastructure emergency protection.

The original simulation data show that: (a) the CPR path strategy reduces the peak failure ratio in the first 6 time steps by an average of 58.2% across 4 scenarios, while the second-best Betweenness Centrality strategy only achieves a 32.7% reduction. (b) the CPR path strategy reaches a stable failure ratio at an average of 4.5 time steps across all scenarios, while CPR rank-based, SLB-based, and traditional centrality strategies require an average of 8.3, 12.5, and 7.2 time steps respectively to stabilize. This proves that the CPR path strategy can block the spread of cascading failures at the source in the early outbreak phase.

Protection Resource Utilization Efficiency (PRUE), this metric is defined as:

It quantifies the failure mitigation effect achieved by per unit of protected nodes, reflecting the economy of the strategy under limited protection resources (the core practical scenario of hospital infrastructure management).

The results show that: (a)the CPR path strategy reaches the performance threshold at 5 protected nodes, with an average PRUE of 10.98% per node across scenarios. (b) to achieve the same mitigation effect as the CPR path strategy, the CPR rank-based strategy requires 17 protected nodes (PRUE = 3.23% per node), the Betweenness Centrality strategy requires 12 nodes (PRUE = 4.57% per node), and the SLB path-based strategy never reaches the same effect even with 18 protected nodes. The PRUE of the CPR path strategy is more than 3 times that of other mainstream strategies, which fully verifies its superiority in practical applications with limited protection resources.

Protect critical node thresholds

It is worth noting that the CPR path protection strategy has an obvious protection threshold characteristic. When the number of protected nodes exceeds 5, the failure ratio basically no longer decreases. It is speculated that there are two main reasons: (Ⅰ) Key nodes are already covered. When the number of protected nodes reaches 5, the core nodes on the cross-layer propagation path have been covered. Continuing to increase protected nodes can only cover some relatively unimportant nodes, which is not helpful for reducing the overall failure ratio. (Ⅱ) Path interception reaches saturation. CPR path protection is based on cross-layer path analysis, focusing on the propagation path from the initial failure node to the key node. The first 5 protected nodes can intercept most propagation paths, and adding more nodes cannot intercept more key paths.

To verify the above conjecture, this study conducted targeted analyses, including fourspecific contents: first, verify the path participation of protected nodes to see if the first 5 nodes are on multiple key paths, while the 6th and subsequent nodes are only on a few or unimportant paths; second, analyze the distribution characteristics of cross-layer paths to determine whether most paths pass through the first 5 nodes; third, check the inter-layer dependency matrix to verify the existence of concentrated inter-layer dependencies; fourth, conduct quantitative verification by combining the failure rate, protection efficiency, and path coverage data under different numbers of protected nodes in Scenario 1.

The analysis results of Fig. 9 show that in Scenario 1, the path coverage rate of the first 5 protected nodes reaches 94.44%, and the failure ratio drops to 23.91%. After continuing to increase protected nodes, neither the path coverage rate nor the failure ratio changes significantly, indicating that 5 nodes are the optimal threshold for CPR path protection. This finding has important practical significance: in actual protection, only these 5 nodes need to be protected to achieve efficient protection. In contrast, other strategies such as betweenness centrality can continuously reduce the failure ratio when increasing the number of protected nodes. Therefore, the CPR path strategy can be considered to be combined with these strategies to break through the threshold limitation. However, in practical applications, protection resources are usually limited, and managers need to achieve the best protection effect with limited resources. Therefore, finding this threshold of key protected nodes is crucial for resource allocation decisions.

Conclusions

From the perspective of the socio-technical system, this study constructs a hospital management coordination-water supply-power supply multi-layer coupled network. Considering the multi-layer coupling characteristics of the network, combined with the Lyapunov stability of nodes, CPR global influence, and layer weights, the SLB key facility identification method is expanded and improved. First, the study calculates the SLB score and CPR centrality value, compares the ranking distribution of key nodes, and analyzes the correlation of rankings of different methods. Then, combined with the scatter diagram, it analyzes the Lyapunov stability and CPR centrality characteristics of nodes in the coupled network. Subsequently, a cascading failure method for coupled networks considering backup facility load distribution is constructed. Under four scenarios, for different initial failure nodes, the failure rate under different numbers of protected nodes is tested. In the test, key nodes identified by SLB-based strategies (path/ranking) and CPR-based strategies (path/ranking) are used as protection objects, and the effects and variation trends of these four protection strategies are compared and analyzed to comprehensively evaluate system stability, providing a scientific decision-making basis for actual key node protection schemes. At the same time, by comparing the cascading failure mitigation effects of the proposed CPR path strategy with traditional key node identification methods, the effectiveness and applicability of the proposed protection strategy in the constructed coupled network are verified.

Based on the research results, the following conclusions are drawn: (Ⅰ) Nodes in the constructed multi-layer coupled network have obvious characteristics: high CPR is negatively correlated with high Lyapunov stability; nodes with high SLB values are not necessarily structural core nodes with high CPR values, and the two belong to different key dimensions without significant correlation. According to clustering characteristics, nodes can be divided into three categories: high CPR centrality nodes, high Lyapunov stability coefficient η nodes, and nodes with low Lyapunov stability coefficient η and low CPR centrality. The improved SLB method can better identify key nodes that cause cascading failures in coupled networks. These nodes have the characteristics of high load and easy instability, but may miss some non-structural high CPR centrality nodes—which are easily identified by the CPR method. (Ⅱ) For the four types of strategies proposed in the study, in the cascading failure protection with simultaneous multi-node failures, CPR path protection is the optimal strategy for identifying structural dependency links and blocking cross-network cascades. The sharp drop characteristic of its curve intuitively reflects the efficient containment capability for structural cascading failures. SLB-based strategies are only applicable to single-network failure scenarios dominated by pure loads, with limited scope of application. To quickly respond to cascading failures, curb diffusion, and reduce the number of protected nodes, it is recommended that CPR path protection select 5 nodes as protection objects, accounting for 5.43% of the total number of nodes. (Ⅲ) Compared with traditional protection strategies such as betweenness centrality and degree centrality, although CPR path protection has slight shortcomings in the early convergence speed or final failure ratio in some scenarios, it has better overall protection effect and stronger universality, and can adapt to various complex failure scenarios such as concentrated structural core nodes and cross-system dependencies. Unlike traditional centrality protection strategies, the CPR path has a threshold of key protected nodes (S = 5). When the number of protected nodes is 5 (accounting for 5.43%), it can effectively curb the propagation of cascading failures and show better performance than other traditional strategies in all scenarios.

Other traditional centrality strategies (such as betweenness centrality) can continuously reduce the failure ratio when increasing the number of protected nodes. Therefore, the CPR path strategy can be considered to be combined with such strategies to break through the threshold limitation. However, in practical applications, protection resources are usually limited, and managers need to achieve the optimal protection effect with limited resources. Therefore, determining the threshold of key protected nodes is crucial for resource allocation decisions. The conclusions of this study provide a quantitative basis for the design of protection strategies under multi-failure scenarios of complex networks, and have important academic value and practical significance for improving the anti-cascading failure capabilities of dependent networks such as hospitals and urban pipe networks.

This study has limitations: (1) The flow load model assumes linear load redistribution, which may not fully reflect the nonlinear characteristics of actual hospital infrastructure failures (e.g., sudden power outage leading to water pump shutdown). (2) The inter-layer feedback intensity matrix Θ is set based on the operational data of a single hospital, and its generalizability needs to be verified in multi-center studies (3) The study focuses on a single hospital case, and the generalizability of the proposed threshold (5 protected nodes) needs to be verified in multi-hospital or urban-scale networks. Future Research Directions: (1) Integrate nonlinear load models to improve the accuracy of cascading failure simulation. (2) Calibrate model parameters (e.g., Θ, weight coefficients) using multi-center hospital operation data to enhance practical applicability. (3) Extend the network to urban-scale critical infrastructure (e.g., adding transportation layers) and explore cross-regional cascading failure mitigation strategies. (4) Combine machine learning methods to predict initial failure nodes and optimize dynamic protection strategies in real time.
