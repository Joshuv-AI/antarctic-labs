Sex- and menopause-related differences in immune and gastrointestinal symptom architecture in ME/CFS: evidence from factor analysis and structural equation modeling
Authors: Lotte Habermann-Horstmeier, Lukas M. Horstmeier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a multisystem disorder characterized by neuroimmune, autonomic, and gastrointestinal dysfunction. Previous studies identified coherent symptom domains involving Brain, Autonomic, Gut, and Immune manifestations and demonstrated pronounced sex-specific differences in neurocognitive–sensory and autonomic symptom organization. However, whether immune-related and gastrointestinal symptoms form distinct or integrated latent structures in women and men with ME/CFS remains unclear. In addition, the potential influence of menopausal status on these symptom domains has not been systematically investigated.MethodsData from 748 adults with a medical diagnosis of ME/CFS were included in this cross-sectional analysis (608 women, 137 men, and 3 non-binary participants). Sex-stratified analyses were restricted to women and men. Symptoms were coded dichotomously. Sex-stratified analyses included cross-tabulations, Cramér’s V, tetrachoric correlations, logistic and linear regression models, exploratory factor analysis, and structural equation modeling (SEM). Additional subgroup analyses compared pre- and postmenopausal women. Model robustness was evaluated using a stratified training dataset.ResultsIn women, Immune (flu-like symptoms, susceptibility to infections) and Gut (gastrointestinal complaints, food intolerances) symptoms formed two distinct but related clusters, characterized by moderate within-cluster correlations (tetrachoric ρ = 0.47–0.60) and weaker cross-cluster associations (ρ = 0.30–0.35). Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) supported a two-factor solution. Consistent with this result, the alternative one-factor SEM showed comparatively poor fit (CFI = 0.913; RMSEA = 0.119), whereas the specified two-factor model was just-identified and therefore not suitable for global fit evaluation. In men, all four symptoms loaded onto a single integrated Gut/Immune factor, with within- and cross-domain tetrachoric correlations ranging from 0.43 to 0.68. SEM confirmed excellent fit for a one-factor model (CFI = 0.981; RMSEA = 0.074). Susceptibility to infections emerged as the dominant predictor in regression analyses. Among women, menopausal status selectively affected immune-related symptoms. Women classified as premenopausal reported flu-like symptoms more frequently than women classified as postmenopausal, whereas gastrointestinal symptoms and food intolerances remained stable across groups. These findings suggest differential hormonal sensitivity of immune versus gastrointestinal symptom trajectories.ConclusionsImmune and gastrointestinal symptoms in ME/CFS exhibit sex-specific latent structures. Women demonstrate two partially separable but related symptom domains, whereas men show a more integrated immune–gastrointestinal architecture. Furthermore, menopausal status appears to selectively modulate immune-related symptom expression while leaving gastrointestinal symptom patterns comparatively stable. Taken together, these results provide an exploratory framework that supports the concept of sex-dependent neuroimmune-autonomic mechanisms in ME/CFS and aligns with a dynamic, hormonally modulated immune component as well as a more persistent, gut-related process. The results highlight the importance of sex- and hormone-sensitive approaches to phenotyping, mechanistic research, and therapeutic stratification in ME/CFS.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08699-6.

Introduction

Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a debilitating multisystem disease [1] characterized by post-exertional malaise (PEM) [2], neurocognitive impairment [3], autonomic dysfunction [4], immunological abnormalities [5, 6], and a broad spectrum of fluctuating somatic symptoms [7]. The central symptom is post-exertional malaise (PEM). PEM is a pronounced and persistent worsening of symptoms following minimal physical or mental exertion; it usually occurs after a delay and typically lasts for more than 24 hours. It is accompanied by a high degree of physical and mental exhaustion and a rapid deterioration in overall well-being (fatigue) [2]. The disorder is typically triggered by infectious events and involves disturbances across multiple regulatory systems—including the nervous, endocrine, and immune systems—as well as impaired cellular energy metabolism and chronic low-grade inflammation, potentially contributing to disturbed physiological homeostasis [6, 7]. These findings are consistent with recent multi-omics and immunometabolic research reporting evidence for exaggerated innate immune responses, chronic inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and gut dysbiosis in ME/CFS patients.

Despite the multisystemic nature of ME/CFS, relatively little is known about how symptoms co-occur, cluster, or interact across biological domains. In a previous population-based study (Study 1), we showed that many symptoms appear in parallel across the disease course and that gastrointestinal and immunological symptoms frequently emerge early, particularly in women [8]. Building on these observations, Study 2 statistically validated four hypothesis-driven symptom clusters—Brain, Autonomic, Gut, and Immune—using exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses and structural equation modeling (SEM). These clusters demonstrated strong internal coherence and distinctiveness, supporting the hypothesis that ME/CFS symptom architecture may reflect underlying neuroimmune and autonomic processes [9].

Study 3 extended this work by examining sex-specific differences in the Brain/Brain21 and Autonomic clusters2. The results revealed marked sex-related divergence: women exhibited more homogeneous neurocognitive-sensory and autonomic clusters, whereas men showed more heterogeneous patterns suggesting vascular–metabolic and sympathetic involvement [10]. These findings align with established sex differences in immune function, where women typically show stronger adaptive immune responses and higher autoimmune activation, while men may exhibit weaker humoral immunity and greater susceptibility to certain infections [11].

Gastrointestinal symptoms are particularly relevant in ME/CFS. Multiple studies have documented alterations in the gut microbiota, reduced microbial diversity, and shifts toward pro-inflammatory taxa, suggesting a role for dysbiosis in disease pathophysiology. Recent reviews highlight that dysbiosis may contribute to intestinal barrier dysfunction (“Leaky Gut”), microbial translocation, oxidative stress, and systemic immune activation, potentially exacerbating ME/CFS symptoms [12, 13]. Although causality remains unproven, these mechanisms are biologically plausible and consistent with broader evidence linking gut dysbiosis to neuroinflammation and blood–brain barrier alterations [14]. A 2024 review further emphasizes that gastrointestinal microbiota changes may influence immunological and inflammatory pathways relevant to ME/CFS, even though current evidence is largely associative [15].

Taken together, these findings suggest that immune and gastrointestinal symptoms may form interconnected clusters, potentially mediated by gut–immune–brain interactions. However, whether these clusters manifest similarly in women and men remains unclear. Preliminary evidence from Study 2 and Study 3 suggests that women may exhibit distinct Immune and Gut clusters, whereas in men these symptoms may converge into a single integrated cluster. Such sex-specific differences would be consistent with known biological patterns: women generally exhibit stronger adaptive immune responses and may show greater vulnerability to immune–gut interactions, whereas men may display stronger sympathetic and vascular–metabolic involvement as well as greater susceptibility to certain infections [11, 16–18].

The present study (Study 4) therefore aims to: (1) determine whether Immune and Gut symptoms form two distinct clusters in women but a single integrated cluster (Combined Gut/Immune) in men; (2) evaluate the statistical coherence, distinctiveness, and latent structure of these clusters using bivariate associations, factor analysis, and SEM; and (3) situate these findings within a broader mechanistic framework linking immune–gut interactions to autonomic and neurocognitive symptom domains.

By integrating statistical modeling with emerging biological insights, this study seeks to clarify sex-specific symptom architectures in ME/CFS and contribute to a hypothesis-generating model that reflects divergent neuroimmune–autonomic pathways in women and men.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study (Study 4) is part of a multi-phase research program investigating the symptom architecture of myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS). It builds on the results of the following studies: (1) a population-based descriptive study on symptom prevalence (Study 1), (2) a factor-analytic validation of mechanistic symptom clusters (Study 2), and (3) an analysis of gender-specific differences in neurocognitive, sensory, and autonomic clusters (Study 3). Now, study 4 focuses on immune-related and gastrointestinal symptom domains. All analyses were conducted using data from the APAV-ME/CFS study, a predominantly qualitative investigation of the doctor-patient relationship, which also collected quantitative symptom data. The four symptoms were selected because they represented the “immune system” and “gut” symptom domains previously validated in Study 2 and were therefore examined here to assess their gender-specific latent organization (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Chronological placement of study 4 within the sequence of studies

Participants

A total of 1,238 individuals participated in the APAV-ME/CFS study. All participants were from Germany. Participants were recruited between June and October 2022. For the present analyses, we included only adult participants (>20 years) with a medically confirmed ME/CFS diagnosis (n = 748; 608 women, 137 men, 3 non-binary). The criteria used to establish the medically confirmed ME/CFS-diagnosis were not specifically queried. However, it can be assumed that the diagnosis was primarily based on the CCC3 or the ICC4, given that these diagnostic methods are widely used in Germany and participants in the qualitative part of the APAV-ME/CFS study repeatedly stated that their diagnoses were made using these criteria.

Symptom assessment and coding

Participants reported whether each symptom had occurred regularly in recent months. Because the original questionnaire assessed symptom presence as binary responses (yes/no), all symptom variables were analyzed as dichotomous indicators (0 = absent, 1 = present). Dichotomous coding is associated with reduced variance and may attenuate factor loadings and SEM fit indices. However, the use of tetrachoric correlations, cross-validation, and consistent findings across complementary statistical approaches suggested that the overall latent structure remained reasonably robust despite this limitation.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed separately for women and men to examine sex-specific symptom structures. The latent variables identified in the present analyses represent simplified statistical representations of symptom co-occurrence rather than comprehensive biological domains.

Logistic regression

Binary logistic regression models were estimated to assess the predictive relationships between symptoms. Depending on the analysis, either flu-like symptoms or susceptibility to infections served as the dependent variable. Model fit was evaluated using pseudo-R2 statistics.

Linear regression

Composite symptom scores were calculated by summing dichotomous symptom indicators to approximate continuous variation in symptom burden. Scores represented the sum of present symptom indicators and were treated as approximately continuous variables. The Immune score consisted of two dichotomous items (flu‑like symptoms, increased infection susceptibility), yielding a possible range of 0–2. The Gut score likewise comprised two dichotomous items (gastrointestinal complaints, food intolerances), also ranging from 0 to 2. The Combined Gut/Immune score represented the sum of all four indicators and therefore ranged from 0 to 4. No standardization or z‑transformation was applied. All analyses were conducted using raw sum scores to preserve interpretability.

Factor analysis

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with Varimax rotation was performed to evaluate the latent structure of the four symptoms. In women, we examined whether Immune and Gut symptoms separated into two distinct factors, whereas in men we tested a single-factor solution representing a Combined Gut/Immune construct. Given that each domain comprises only two indicators and the overall model includes four items, the factor analyses are considered exploratory and hypothesis-driven. Accordingly, we do not interpret the factor loadings in detail but use them to assess whether the observed symptom patterns align with the proposed latent structure.

Structural equation modeling (SEM)

Confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) and SEM were conducted to test the fit of one-factor vs. two-factor models. All models were estimated using maximum likelihood (ML) in Stata 12.0. Because Stata 12.0 did not support categorical estimators such as WLSMV, we were unable to compare ML and WLSMV solutions. However, ML can skew standard errors for dichotomous items, distort the RMSEA, and affect factor loadings. Results should therefore be interpreted cautiously, particularly the absolute fit indices. Stratified training dataset

To assess robustness, all analyses were repeated in a stratified, randomized training dataset derived from the full sample. Stratification was based on sex and illness duration to ensure proportional representation and minimize sampling bias. Results were directionally consistent across datasets.

Software

All analyses were conducted using Stata 12.0. Graphical models were created using Microsoft PowerPoint.

Results

Sample characteristics

Analyses were based on 748 adults with a medically confirmed ME/CFS diagnosis (608 women, 137 men, 3 non-binary). Because the non-binary group was too small for statistical modeling, results are reported for women and men only. Symptom frequencies for the Immune and Gut domains were consistent with previous studies: flu-like symptoms were reported by 76.8% of women and 73.0% of men; gastrointestinal complaints by 71.9% of women and 64.5% of men; food intolerances by 63.2% of women and 47.8% of men; and increased susceptibility to infections by 48.0% of women and 43.8% of men.

Structural equation modeling (SEM)

To examine the dimensional structure of Gut and Immune symptoms, three SEMs were estimated: separate one-factor models for the Gut and Immune domains, respectively, and a combined one-factor model including all four symptom indicators. In the overall sample (n = 745), the combined one-factor model showed poor fit (χ2 (2) = 23.98, p < 0.001; RMSEA = 0.121; CFI = 0.922), indicating that a single latent factor did not adequately represent the observed symptom structure.

Sex-stratified analyses revealed marked differences. In women, the corresponding one-factor model likewise showed poor fit (χ2 (2) = 19.30, p < 0.001; RMSEA = 0.119; CFI = 0.913), whereas in men the one-factor model fit the data well (χ2 (2) = 3.50, p = 0.174; RMSEA = 0.074; CFI = 0.981).

Taken together with the exploratory factor analysis, these findings support the interpretation that immune-related and gastrointestinal symptoms are better represented as two related latent domains in women than as a single common construct. However, because the specified two-factor model was just-identified (df = 0), this interpretation is based on converging evidence from the EFA and the poor fit of the alternative one-factor model rather than on a direct statistical comparison between competing CFA models.

Overall, the findings suggest sex-specific differences in the organization of gastrointestinal and immune-related symptoms (Tables 4 and 5). Communalities and uniquenesses

The analysis of communalities (h2) and uniquenesses (u2) revealed clear sex-specific differences in the underlying factor structure. Among women, the two immune-related indicators Susceptibility to infections and Flu-like symptoms showed very low communalities (h2 = 0.15 and 0.12, respectively), indicating that these items were only weakly accounted for by a common latent factor. In contrast, the gastrointestinal indicators Gastrointestinal complaints and Food intolerances demonstrated moderate communalities (h2 = 0.36 and 0.44), consistent with a stronger association with a gastrointestinal-related latent factor. This pattern is compatible with the identification of a two-factor solution in women, comprising distinct immune and gastrointestinal dimensions.

In men, a more homogeneous pattern emerged: all four indicators showed moderate communalities (h2 = 0.26–0.46), suggesting that each item contributed meaningfully to a single latent construct. Correspondingly, uniqueness values were in the mid-range (u2 = 0.54–0.74), with no indicator standing out as poorly represented. This profile is consistent with a unified, overarching factor integrating both immune-related and gastrointestinal symptoms. Prevalence and latent factor scores

Despite structural differences, women and men do not differ in the frequency of immune-related symptoms or in the mean Immune symptom score (all p > 0.05). These findings suggest that sex differences are more apparent in the structural organization of these symptoms than in overall symptom burden.

Impact of hormonal transitions on immune and autonomic regulation

Given the known impact of hormonal transitions on immune and autonomic regulation, we additionally explored whether the observed sex-specific factor structures differed between pre- and postmenopausal women. These analyses were considered exploratory due to limited subgroup sample sizes and because we could only roughly estimate which women were pre- and postmenopausal based on their age, since that information was not collected in the study.

Analyses stratified by illness duration revealed distinct patterns for immune- and gut-related symptoms. Across the total sample, flu-like symptoms did not differ significantly between duration categories. However, when stratified by menopausal status, a significant association emerged only in the postmenopausal group, with flu-like symptoms decreasing as illness duration increased (χ2 (2)=7.01, p = 0.030, V = 0.15). A similar pattern was observed for susceptibility to infections, which also declined with longer illness duration in the postmenopausal group (χ2 (2)=7.99, p = 0.018, V = 0.16), whereas no significant association was found in women classified as premenopausal.

In contrast, gastrointestinal symptoms and food intolerances increased with illness duration in both groups. Gastrointestinal complaints showed a significant duration effect in the premenopausal group (χ2 (2)=8.92, p = 0.012, V = 0.15) and a trend in the postmenopausal group. Food intolerances demonstrated strong associations with illness duration in both groups (postmenopausal: χ2 (2)=17.72, p < 0.001, V = 0.23; premenopausal: χ2 (2)=8.73, p = 0.013, V = 0.14).

Symptom burden

In the following calculations, sex was coded as female = 0, male = 1. Consistent with the group difference observed in the t-test (t = 2.59, p = 0.010), linear regression showed that male sex (coded as 1) was associated with a lower composite symptom score (β = −0.307, p = 0.010), indicating a higher overall symptom burden in women. When examining the two symptom clusters separately, a clear pattern emerged: the Gut symptom score was significantly higher in women (β = −0.226, p = 0.002), whereas the Immune symptom score did not differ between sexes (β = −0.080, p = 0.255). Thus, the observed sex difference in overall symptom burden (Combined Gut/Immune score) was entirely attributable to the Gut symptom cluster.

Among women, menopausal status was associated with distinct symptom patterns. Women classified as postmenopausal showed a significantly lower overall symptom burden than women classified as premenopausal (β = −0.236, p = 0.018). This effect was specific to the Immune cluster, where women classified as postmenopausal reported markedly fewer symptoms (β = −0.177, p = 0.003). In contrast, Gut symptoms did not differ by menopausal status (β = −0.059, p = 0.358). Interaction models including age (menopausal status × age) showed no evidence that age modified the association between menopausal status and either Immune or Gut symptoms (all p > 0.65). This may indicate that the observed menopausal effect is not attributable to age differences, as the strength and direction of the association remained stable across the age range.

Because fewer women were represented at older ages, the corresponding estimates show larger standard errors and wider confidence intervals. Together, these results demonstrate that sex differences in symptom burden are cluster-specific and that menopausal status selectively influences Immune-related symptoms.

Summary of sex-specific patterns

Taken together, the analyses reveal a consistent pattern of sex-specific differences in the organization of immune-related and gastrointestinal symptoms.

Symptom clusters across sexes. Flu-like symptoms and susceptibility to infections formed a coherent Immune cluster in both women and men, although associations were stronger in men, as reflected in higher tetrachoric correlations, higher factor loadings, and larger pseudo-R2 values, pointing to a more tightly integrated immune symptom structure. Gastrointestinal complaints and (food) intolerances likewise formed a robust Gut cluster in both sexes, with comparable effect sizes but slightly higher bivariate correlations in men.

Combined Gut/Immune structure. When the four symptoms were modeled jointly, the analyses suggested a sex difference. In men, a one-factor solution fit the data well (χ2 (2) = 3.50, p = 0.174; RMSEA = 0.074; CFI = 0.981), consistent with a common immune–gastrointestinal dimension. In women, the corresponding one-factor model showed comparatively poor fit (χ2 (2) = 19.30, p < 0.001; RMSEA = 0.119; CFI = 0.913), indicating that a single latent factor did not adequately represent the observed symptom structure. Together with the exploratory factor analysis, these findings support the interpretation that immune and gastrointestinal symptoms are better represented as two related latent domains in women. Discussion

This study provides converging evidence for sex-specific differences in the structure of immune-related and gastrointestinal symptoms in ME/CFS. Across all statistical approaches—including tetrachoric correlations, logistic and linear regression, factor analysis, and structural equation modeling—the overall pattern of findings suggested two related symptom domains in women (Immune and Gut), whereas men showed a single integrated cluster (Combined Gut/Immune) encompassing all four symptoms. Importantly, these structural differences do not coincide with differences in symptom prevalence or mean Immune factor scores. Although women exhibited a significantly higher overall Gut/Immune symptom burden, this difference was entirely attributable to Gut symptoms, whereas Immune symptom burden did not differ between sexes. Furthermore, the latent organization of immune and gastrointestinal symptoms differed substantially between women and men.

These findings extend the results of Study 2, which validated the existence of Immune and Gut clusters in the overall sample [9], and Study 3, which demonstrated sex-specific differences in neurocognitive sensory and autonomic symptom domains [10]. Together, these studies suggest that the symptom architecture of ME/CFS is not uniform but reflects sex-dependent neuroimmune–autonomic organization (Fig. 3).

However, the proposed neuroimmune-autonomic mechanisms should be interpreted as biologically informed models derived from symptom architecture rather than direct mechanistic proof. Because microbiome composition, autonomic physiology, cytokine profiles, and intestinal permeability markers—to name but a few—were not directly measured, the present findings remain hypothesis-generating and require multimodal biological validation.

Sex-specific clustering patterns

Women: distinct Immune and Gut clusters

In women, immune-related and gastrointestinal symptoms formed two statistically distinct but related domains. The EFA and the poor fit of the alternative one-factor SEM were consistent with this two-domain representation, suggesting that immune and gastrointestinal symptoms are more differentiated in women than in men.

This pattern aligns with known sex differences in immune function [21]. Women exhibit stronger adaptive immune responses, higher rates of autoimmune activation, and more pronounced cytokine reactivity than men [11]. They also show greater susceptibility to dysbiosis-related gastrointestinal symptoms and higher prevalence of food intolerances, consistent with the stronger Gut cluster observed here. Study 1 similarly found that gastrointestinal symptoms and intolerances increased markedly during the early disease course in women, supporting the notion that these symptoms represent a distinct and clinically meaningful domain [8].

Men: a Combined Gut/Immune cluster

In contrast, the analyses in men were consistent with a single integrated Gut/Immune factor characterized by stronger cross-domain associations. Although this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the smaller male sample, it suggests a more tightly coupled immune-gastrointestinal symptom organization.

This Combined Gut/Immune cluster is consistent with biological evidence that men exhibit weaker humoral immunity, higher susceptibility to infections, and stronger sympathetic activation than women. Sympathetic overactivation can amplify inflammatory responses, impair gut barrier function, and promote gastrointestinal dysmotility—mechanisms that may couple immune and gut symptoms more tightly in men. Study 3 similarly found that men’s autonomic symptom structure was dominated by respiratory and cardiovascular dysregulation, which may reflect a sympathetic–metabolic axis rather than the vagal–inflammatory axis observed in women [10].

Mechanistic interpretation: dysbiosis, intestinal permeability, and neuroimmune signaling

The sex-specific clustering patterns observed here may be interpreted within a mechanistic framework involving gut dysbiosis, intestinal barrier dysfunction (often reffered to as ‘Leaky Gut’), and neuroimmune–autonomic signaling.

Dysbiosis and intestinal barrier dysfunction

Multiple studies have documented reduced microbial diversity, loss of butyrate-producing species, and increased abundance of pro-inflammatory taxa in ME/CFS [22, 23]. Dysbiosis can impair epithelial tight junctions, increase intestinal permeability, and facilitate translocation of microbial products such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS). These processes can trigger systemic immune activation, oxidative stress, and cytokine release—mechanisms that plausibly contribute to both immune and gastrointestinal symptoms [24].

Sex-specific pathways

The findings are compatible with a model in which women may exhibit a vagal–hormonal–inflammatory pathway: In women estrogen modulates and may enhance immune responses, epithelial barrier integrity, and vagal tone [25]. Dysbiosis-induced permeability may lead to LPS translocation and activation of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory reflex. This could contribute to why Immune and Gut symptoms remain distinct but interconnected in women [24].

The findings are consistent with a model in which men may exhibit a sympathetic–metabolic–vascular pathway: In men sympathetic dominance can impair gut motility, reduce mucosal perfusion, and promote inflammatory signaling [26, 27]. Vascular–metabolic stress may amplify immune vulnerability and gastrointestinal symptoms simultaneously [28]. This is compatible with the Combined Gut/Immune cluster observed in men.

These mechanisms are compatible with findings from Study 3, where women showed stronger vagal involvement in autonomic clusters, while men showed stronger sympathetic involvement [10].

The role of menopause

In this study, flu-like symptoms were more prevalent among women classified as premenopausal, whereas gastrointestinal symptoms and food intolerances did not differ between the age-defined pre- and postmenopausal groups. These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that menopausal status may differentially influence specific symptom domains, although menopausal status was approximated using age rather than direct hormonal assessment. The observed pattern is also compatible with current hypotheses regarding estrogen-dependent immune modulation, including enhanced antiviral and inflammatory immune responses [11, 18, 29–32], increased mast-cell reactivity, and heightened central sensitivity to peripheral cytokine signals [33–35]. As a result, premenopausal women may exhibit a lower threshold for mounting a pronounced sickness response, which is characterized by fatigue, myalgia, and flu-like malaise [36]. After menopause, declining estrogen levels attenuate these immune-driven responses, which is compatible with the lower frequency of flu-like symptoms in older women.

Importantly, disease duration likely contributes to these differences. In our sample, postmenopausal women were older and had been ill for a longer period of time. Early disease stages in ME/CFS are typically marked by stronger antiviral and inflammatory signatures, including interferon activity, cytokine peaks, and mast-cell activation—all of which drive flu-like symptoms [37–41].

One possible interpretation is that, over time, these acute immune features diminish in at least a subset of patients and are replaced by more chronic metabolic, autonomic, and vascular dysfunctions [42–44]. Thus, the combination of higher estrogen-dependent immune reactivity and shorter disease duration may predispose premenopausal women to more pronounced flu-like symptoms, whereas postmenopausal women may show attenuated immune-driven symptom expression due to both hormonal decline and longer disease chronicity.

In contrast, gastrointestinal symptoms and food intolerances were remarkably stable across age defined menopausal status. This stability suggests that gut-related pathology may represent a more persistent and less hormonally responsive process. Current evidence indicates that once gut-barrier dysfunction is established—characterized by epithelial impairment, dysbiosis, and microbial translocation—it may become self-sustaining through ongoing immune activation and microbiome alterations. Menopause has been associated with long-lasting shifts in gut microbiome composition, reduced microbial diversity, and persistent markers of barrier dysfunction and microbial translocation [42, 45–48]. These findings support the possibility that gut dysfunction may persist beyond menopausal transition and may be less sensitive to hormonal modulation than immune-related symptoms. This may help explain why gastrointestinal symptoms did not differ between women defined as pre- and postmenopausal in our cohort despite clear hormonal modulation of immune-related symptoms.

Our additional analyses revealed that disease duration modulated immune‑related symptoms only in the postmenopausal group. In these women, both flu‑like symptoms and susceptibility to infections decreased with longer illness duration, whereas no such pattern was observed in the premenopausal group. This potentially aligns with the previously described notion that immune‑driven symptoms are strongest in early disease stages and attenuate over time, particularly once estrogen‑dependent immune amplification is lost. In contrast, gastrointestinal symptoms and food intolerances increased with disease duration in both groups, indicating a progressive and hormonally independent process consistent with persistent gut‑barrier dysfunction. Together, these findings reinforce a dual‑process model in which immune‑related symptoms are shaped by both hormonal status and disease stage, whereas Gut‑related symptoms may reflect a stable, chronic pathology that progresses irrespective of menopausal transition.

Taken together, our findings suggest a dual-process model: immune-related symptoms are potentially dynamic and hormonally modulated, whereas Gut-related symptoms reflect a stable, structural pathology that persists independently of menopausal status. This distinction may have implications for personalized treatment strategies, highlighting the need to consider both hormonal context and disease stage when interpreting symptom profiles in women with ME/CFS.

The present findings also align with the broader symptom architecture identified in Studies 2 and 3 [9, 10].

Women: a multi-axis, interconnected system

Women show distinct Immune and Gut clusters, a coherent Autonomic cluster, a homogeneous Brain/Brain2 cluster, and moderate correlations across clusters.

This pattern suggests a vagal–hormonal–inflammatory network, in which immune, gastrointestinal, autonomic, and neurocognitive symptoms are interconnected but retain distinct latent structures. Women exhibit a pattern of partially segregated but interconnected symptom clusters, compatible with recent systems‑immunology and neuroendocrine research. Contemporary work shows that women display more coherent yet distinctly modular immune and gut signatures, shaped by estrogen‑dependent transcriptional programs and X‑linked immune regulation [25, 49]. At the same time, multimodal analyses are consistent with moderately correlated but structurally separable immune, metabolic, autonomic, and neurocognitive modules, which may reflect sex‑specific integration across inflammatory, hormonal, and neurovisceral axes [50]. Neurocognitive domains in women also tend to form more homogeneous clusters, compatible with hormone‑sensitive neuroimmune coupling [51]. Together, these findings support the interpretation of a vagal–hormonal–inflammatory network in which immune, gastrointestinal, autonomic, and brain‑related symptoms remain distinct in their latent structure while being functionally linked through shared regulatory pathways.

Men: a more integrated, sympathetic-driven system

Men show a Combined Gut/Immune cluster, an Autonomic cluster dominated by respiratory and cardiovascular symptoms, and a more heterogeneous Brain/Brain2 cluster.

Recent neuroimmune and autonomic research is consistent with the interpretation that men exhibit a more integrated sympathetic–metabolic–vascular network, consistent with a unified Combined Gut/Immune cluster and stronger coupling between immune and gastrointestinal symptoms. Contemporary work demonstrates that sympathetic activation tightly links metabolic stress, vascular tone, and inflammatory signaling, producing coherent immune–gastrointestinal modules that interact strongly with autonomic dysregulation [52, 53].

Findings in gastrointestinal neuroimmune disorders show that immune and gut pathways become increasingly synchronized under sympathetic dominance, reinforcing the emergence of a single immune–gastrointestinal cluster [54]. Moreover, studies of the gut–immune–brain axis indicate that men display more heterogeneous neurocognitive patterns alongside stronger immune–autonomic coupling, aligning with a more variable Brain/Brain2 cluster and a distinct autonomic cluster dominated by respiratory and cardiovascular symptoms [55]. Clinical and methodological implications

Taken together, these results underscore the importance of sex-specific modeling in ME/CFS research (Fig. 3). Because symptom prevalence and mean factor scores are comparable between sexes while the latent symptom architecture differs fundamentally, analyses that pool men and women risk obscuring clinically relevant structural patterns. The findings therefore provide a foundation for more nuanced diagnostic approaches and sex-tailored therapeutic strategies that target the distinct neuroimmune–autonomic configurations observed in women and men.

Clinical and translational implications

These findings have several implications:

Sex-specific diagnostic patterns

Pre- and postmenopausal women may benefit from separate assessment of immune and gastrointestinal symptoms. In men, these symptoms may represent a single underlying process.

Sex‑specific differences in symptomatology and immune function suggest that tailored diagnostic assessment may increase diagnostic precision. In cardiology, sex‑specific high‑sensitivity troponin thresholds have been shown to reduce underdiagnosis of myocardial infarction in women and to identify high‑risk patients who would otherwise be missed [56–58]. Up to now, sex‑specific differences in symptom patterns, immune and hormonal profiles in ME/CFS are increasingly well documented [6, 59–62], but prospective studies demonstrating a direct clinical benefit of sex‑stratified diagnostic pathways are still lacking.

Potential biomarkers

The sex-specific symptom architectures identified in this study may also have implications for biomarker research in ME/CFS. The distinct separation of immune and gastrointestinal symptom clusters in women, compared with the integrated Combined Gut/Immune cluster observed in men, suggests that different biological pathways may underlie symptom organization across sexes and may therefore require sex-stratified biomarker approaches.

In women, biomarkers related to intestinal barrier dysfunction, microbiome composition, and vagal–immune regulation may be particularly relevant. Potential candidates include short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) profiles, markers of microbial translocation and intestinal permeability such as zonulin, lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP), and soluble CD14 (sCD14), as well as estrogen-sensitive cytokine signatures and mast-cell–associated mediators. Because the female symptom architecture was characterized by partially distinct but interconnected immune and gastrointestinal domains, autonomic measures reflecting vagal regulation—particularly heart rate variability (HRV)—may also provide useful indicators of neuroimmune integration.

In men, the tightly coupled Combined Gut/Immune cluster and stronger association with autonomic dysregulation suggest that biomarkers related to sympathetic activation, vascular stress, and metabolic dysfunction may be more informative. Potential candidates include catecholamine-associated autonomic markers, sympathetic HRV indices, endothelial dysfunction markers, inflammatory–metabolic stress markers, and immune profiles associated with infection susceptibility or chronic low-grade inflammation, consistent with the more integrated neuroimmune–metabolic response pattern suggested by the stronger convergence of immune and gastrointestinal symptoms observed in men.

Importantly, these proposed biomarkers should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than disease-specific indicators. Because the present study did not directly assess microbiome composition, autonomic physiology, cytokine profiles, or intestinal permeability, the suggested biomarker pathways remain inferential and require validation in multimodal longitudinal studies using standardized measures of diagnostic performance, including sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), as recommended in recent systematic reviews of ME/CFS biomarkers. Future research integrating symptom-network analysis with immune, autonomic, microbiome, metabolic, and vascular biomarkers may help identify biologically meaningful sex-specific endophenotypes in ME/CFS.

Therapeutic strategies

The present findings suggest that sex-specific symptom architectures in ME/CFS may also have implications for the development of more individualized therapeutic approaches. Because women and men showed fundamentally different patterns of immune–gastrointestinal coupling, therapeutic strategies targeting neuroimmune, autonomic, and gastrointestinal pathways may need to be adapted according to sex-specific biological organization.

In women, interventions targeting gut barrier integrity, neuroimmune regulation, and autonomic function may warrant further investigation. Because immune-related symptoms in women are consistent with a model in which these are influenced by menopausal status and disease duration, hormonal context and disease stage may be important considerations when evaluating therapeutic responses.

In men, approaches targeting autonomic imbalance, vascular function, and metabolic stress may be of particular interest The tighter coupling between immune and gastrointestinal symptoms observed in men may indicate that combined neuroimmune–metabolic interventions are particularly relevant in this subgroup.

Importantly, these proposed therapeutic implications remain exploratory and should not be interpreted as evidence-based treatment recommendations. The present study did not directly assess autonomic physiology, microbiome composition, inflammatory biomarkers, or treatment responses. Consequently, the suggested therapeutic pathways are derived from symptom-network architecture and mechanistic interpretation rather than direct interventional evidence. Nevertheless, the findings support the broader concept that ME/CFS may comprise biologically distinct, sex-dependent neuroimmune–autonomic phenotypes that could benefit from more stratified and personalized treatment approaches.

Future prospective studies combining symptom-network analysis with biological profiling and longitudinal treatment outcomes may help determine whether sex-specific therapeutic strategies improve clinical response, symptom burden, or disease progression in ME/CFS.

Limitations

Several measures were taken to reduce potential bias. All participants completed the same standardized questionnaire using identical symptom definitions and response options. Analyses were restricted to adults with a physician-confirmed diagnosis of ME/CFS, and participants with missing information on key demographic variables were excluded. Sex-stratified analyses were performed to reduce confounding by sex, and robustness of the findings was assessed using a stratified training dataset. Furthermore, multiple complementary statistical approaches (tetrachoric correlations, regression analyses, exploratory factor analysis, and SEM) were used to evaluate the consistency of the observed symptom structure.

However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, menopausal status was operationalized using an age-based cutoff (≤50 vs. >50 years), which provides only a proxy for biological menopausal transition and does not capture perimenopausal fluctuations, hormonal variability, or the potential influence of hormone replacement therapy. Because detailed menopausal information was not collected, classification into pre- and postmenopausal groups remained approximate.

Second, the analyses relied on cross-sectional data, precluding conclusions about temporal dynamics or causal relationships between hormonal status and symptom expression. The identified latent variables therefore represent simplified statistical representations of symptom co-occurrence rather than comprehensive biological domains

Third, all symptoms were assessed via self-report and dichotomized prior to analysis. Dichotomization reduces variance and may attenuate factor loadings, while self-report measures are additionally susceptible to recall and reporting biases. Because each latent domain was represented by only two indicators and the overall model included four binary items, the factor analyses should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-driven. Furthermore, because the specified two-factor model in women was just-identified, it could not be formally compared with the alternative one-factor model using global goodness-of-fit statistics. Consequently, support for the two-domain representation is based on converging evidence from exploratory factor analysis, tetrachoric correlations, and the poor fit of the alternative one-factor model rather than on a direct statistical comparison of competing CFA models. Accordingly, the factor loadings are interpreted primarily as indicators of whether the observed symptom patterns are consistent with the proposed latent structure, rather than as precise estimates of underlying biological processes.

Fourth, confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) and SEM were estimated using maximum likelihood (ML) in Stata 12.0 because categorical estimators such as WLSMV were not available in the software version used. Replication with WLSMV or comparable robust estimators in future studies would strengthen confidence in the reported latent structures. Given the binary nature of the indicators, ML estimation may produce biased standard errors and fit indices. However, supplementary analyses based on tetrachoric correlations and cross-validation suggested that the latent structure was reasonably robust.

Fifth, subgroup sample sizes, while adequate for the primary analyses, limited statistical power to detect subtle structural differences between pre- and postmenopausal women and prevented formal tests of measurement invariance across groups. Consequently, the observed sex differences in latent structure should be interpreted as exploratory until measurement invariance has been established in independent datasets.

Sixth, because the APAV-ME/CFS study was not designed primarily for multivariable risk-factor analyses, information on several potentially relevant confounders, including BMI, medication use, autoimmune comorbidities, infection history, socioeconomic status, and hormone replacement therapy, was not systematically collected. Consequently, adjustment for these variables was not possible in the present analyses. Age, sex, illness duration, and age-defined menopausal status were examined in separate analyses within the present research program, including the current study and subsequent studies; however, these variables were not incorporated as covariates in the present latent variable models.

No formal adjustment for multiple testing was applied because the analyses were hypothesis-driven and aimed primarily at evaluating the consistency of predefined symptom structures across complementary statistical approaches. Accordingly, individual p-values should be interpreted cautiously.

Finally, no biological markers were collected. Consequently, proposed mechanisms involving autonomic regulation, microbiome alterations, inflammatory pathways, or related physiological processes remain inferential and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. In addition, the analyses focused on a relatively restricted set of immune-related and gastrointestinal symptoms; broader symptom domains such as autonomic, neurological, or musculoskeletal symptoms may reveal additional menopausal effects not captured in the present study.

Conclusions

Immune and gastrointestinal symptoms in ME/CFS suggest fundamentally different clustering patterns in women and men. Women exhibit two partially separable symptom clusters, whereas men show a more integrated symptom architecture. These sex-specific patterns may reflect divergent neuroimmune–autonomic mechanisms and support the assumption that biological sex substantially shapes symptom organization in ME/CFS. Accordingly, the present findings provide further support for the development of sex-sensitive diagnostic frameworks, mechanistic models, and therapeutic approaches. In combination with Studies 1–3, the current results contribute to an emerging translational model of ME/CFS as a sex-modulated neuroimmune–autonomic disorder characterized by distinct immunological and gastrointestinal symptom patterns.

Furthermore, our findings are consistent with the hypothesis that age-defined menopausal status selectively modulates immune-related symptom expression, whereas gastrointestinal symptoms remain remarkably stable across the pre- and postmenopausal groups. Flu-like symptoms were more prevalent in women classified as premenopausal, compatible with evidence for estrogen-dependent amplification of antiviral and pro-inflammatory immune pathways as well as the heightened neuroimmune reactivity typically associated with earlier or more active disease stages. By contrast, food intolerances and related gastrointestinal symptom patterns did not differ significantly between pre- and postmenopausal groups. This observation may support the hypothesis that gastrointestinal manifestations in ME/CFS are linked to persistent alterations of intestinal barrier integrity and gut–immune interactions that, once established, become chronic and less dependent on hormonal status.

Taken together, these findings support a dual-process model in which immune-related symptoms may appear comparatively dynamic and hormonally modulated, whereas gastrointestinal symptoms may represent a more stable and self-perpetuating pathophysiological process. The apparent dissociation between immune and gastrointestinal symptom patterns further suggests that partially distinct biological mechanisms contribute to symptom persistence in ME/CFS. Recognizing these differential pattern may improve clinical phenotyping, facilitate stratification in future research, and support the development of more targeted and individualized therapeutic interventions, particularly for women across different hormonal stages.

Several limitations should nevertheless be considered (see Limitations). Future longitudinal studies integrating endocrine, immunological, autonomic, and microbiome-related biomarkers will be important to further clarify the mechanistic interplay between sex hormones, neuroimmune regulation, and gastrointestinal dysfunction in ME/CFS. The findings may primarily generalize to adults with physician-diagnosed ME/CFS recruited through specialized patient organizations and tertiary care centers. Replication in population-based cohorts and in international samples is warranted.
