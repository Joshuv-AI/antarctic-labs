Survival and predictors of major adverse cardiovascular events among patients with acute coronary syndrome in Ethiopia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Gebremariam Wulie Geremew, Abaynesh Fentahun Bekalu, Gebresilassie Tadesse, Setegn Fentahun, Tirsit Ketsela Zeleke, Zemenu Wube Bayleyegn, Gashaw Sisay Chanie, Sisay Sitotaw Anberbr, Habtamu Semagne Ayele, Assefa Kebad Mengesha, Demis Getachew, Leul Dejene Abate, Abera Girma Beyena, Yonatan Tsetadirgachew Legesse, Gelgela Gadisa Atomsa, Mulat Alemu Beshada, Tesfaye Birhanu Abebe, Tekletsadik Tekleslassie Alemayehu

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo determine the time to major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and identify its predictors among patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) discharged on secondary prevention medications in Ethiopia.DesignInstitution-based retrospective cohort study.SettingUniversity of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Northwest, Ethiopia.ParticipantsA total of 400 adult patients diagnosed with ACS and discharged with secondary prevention medications between January 2020 and December 2024.Outcome measuresThe primary outcome was time to first MACE, defined as reinfarction, stroke or heart failure, measured in years from hospital discharge. A Cox proportional hazards regression model was fitted to identify predictors of time to develop MACE. Data collected from patient medical charts were exported to STATA V.17 for analysis. The log-rank test was used to determine the survival difference between subgroups of participants.ResultsDuring follow-up, 33.8% of patients experienced at least one MACE. The median time to MACE was 1.5 years. Female sex (adjusted HR (AHR)=2.08; 95% CI 1.24 to 3.49), presence of chronic comorbidities (AHR=2.09; 95% CI 1.42 to 4.41) and higher Killip class (IV vs I: AHR=1.57; 95% CI 1.22 to 1.78) were independently associated with a shorter time to MACE. Patients aged ≤55 years had a lower risk of MACE compared with those aged ≥66 years (AHR=0.77; 95% CI 0.54 to 0.98).ConclusionsAmong patients with ACS discharged on secondary prevention medications, one-third experienced MACE within a median of 1.5 years. Older age, female sex, presence of comorbidities and higher Killip class were significant predictors of shorter time to MACE. These findings highlight the need for targeted postdischarge monitoring and secondary prevention strategies, particularly in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Ethiopia, Angina Pectoris, Coronary heart disease, Myocardial infarction

Introduction

Coronary heart disease remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide.1 As the number of individuals surviving an acute coronary syndrome (ACS) continues to rise, these patients are increasingly recognised globally as being at high risk for future cardiovascular (CV) events.2 Patients with ACS have an adjusted 5-year risk of experiencing subsequent ischaemic events that is approximately 20% higher in absolute terms compared with individuals without coronary heart disease.3 Secondary prevention strategies, such as lifestyle modifications (including dietary changes, regular exercise and smoking cessation) and cardioprotective medications (such as aspirin or other antiplatelet, beta-blockers, statins and either angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers), have been shown to effectively reduce the risk of recurrent ischaemic events in patients with ACS.4–6 Studies indicate that a significant number of eligible patients are not prescribed all the recommended medications, even at the time of hospital discharge.7–10 Moreover, among those who do receive the full range of guideline-recommended secondary prevention therapies, a subset still experiences CV events. Additionally, the timing of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in real-world settings remains poorly understood.

Secondary prevention in post-ACS patients remains a significant public health challenge.11 12 Despite substantial advancements in lifestyle interventions, pharmacologic therapies and invasive procedures, these patients continue to face a high risk of recurrent atherothrombotic events and mortality. This residual risk is driven by a complex interplay of intrinsic predisposing factors and external triggers within a multifaceted pathophysiological framework. Given the complexity of these biological processes, a one-size-fits-all approach is insufficient. Instead, a personalised clinical strategy is essential, one that includes the identification of novel risk factors and therapeutic targets.12–14

Various risk indicators for MACE following ACS, including angiographic findings and left ventricular function, have been previously reported.15 16 The short-term risk of MACE after ACS is also well established.17–20 In terms of long-term outcomes, several factors have been significantly associated with an increased risk of MACE within a 3-year follow-up period. These include the presence of diabetes, heart failure during the initial ACS hospitalisation, a higher Charlson comorbidity index and older age. Conversely, undergoing revascularisation was associated with a reduced risk.21 However, the long-term risk of MACE among patients with ACS in Ethiopia remains largely unknown. This population represents a distinct ethnic and genetic mix, often characterised by different risk factor profiles and limited access to advanced interventions like revascularisation compared with the cohorts that inform international guidelines.22 Understanding the temporal patterns of MACE in this specific setting is essential for adapting evidence-based strategies to local contexts.

The aim of this study was to determine the time to occurrence of MACE and to identify associated risk factors within a 5-year period following a first ACS event. In particular, we focused on identifying modifiable risk factors that could serve as potential targets for improving long-term prognosis after an initial ACS. Additionally, we assessed the prevalence of key CV risk factors, such as smoking, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes and hypertension, among a population-based sample of post-ACS patients. We investigated whether established global predictors hold the same weight in a resource-limited setting with different access to invasive cardiac care. The study was conducted at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (UoGCSH), located in Northwest Ethiopia.

Method and materials

Study area, period and design

An institution-based retrospective cohort study was conducted at the UoGCSH in Northwest Ethiopia. UoGCSH is a tertiary teaching hospital with 580 beds, serving a catchment population of approximately 5–7 million people. The facility provides comprehensive CV care through its emergency, inpatient and specialised outpatient units. The study included patients treated between 1 January 2020 and 31 December 2024, with data collection performed from March to May 2025.

Population

The source population included all adult patients (aged ≥18 years) diagnosed with ACS and receiving follow-up care at the UoGCSH. From this group, we specifically included those followed between 1 January 2020 and 31 December 2024, who met the predefined inclusion criteria. Standard patient medical charts at our institution do not record race or ethnicity, as nearly all patients seeking care at UoGCSH belong to the same broader racial background and regional ethnic groups.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Adult patients aged ≥18 years who were admitted with a diagnosis of ACS, as defined by the Third Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction,23 and were discharged on secondary prevention medications between 1 January 2020 and 31 December 2024, were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they refused secondary prevention medications, had missing time-related variables or prescribing information, were transfer-in cases, lacked at least one follow-up visit or had a history of reinfarction, congestive heart failure or stroke at the start of the study.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated to detect a significant difference in the occurrence of MACE between patients receiving optimal medical therapy (OMT) and those receiving non-OMT. Assuming a 50% baseline incidence of MACE in the non-OMT group (to maximise the required sample size) and a HR24 of 0.65 (representing a 35% risk reduction in the OMT group), the sample size was determined using the following parameters:

Two-tailed type I error alpha of 5%, statistical power (1-beta) of 80% and an equal allocation ratio between OMT and non-OMT groups. Based on these parameters, a minimum sample size of 366 participants was required. After accounting for a 10% contingency for potential missing clinical data or loss to follow-up in the retrospective records, the final target sample size was 404 patients. This sample size also ensures a sufficient number of events to maintain at least 10 events per variable for the multivariable Cox regression model.

Sampling technique and procedure

A retrospective cohort review was conducted using data from the UoGCSH, a tertiary government teaching hospital located in Northwest Ethiopia. The hospital serves as a major referral centre for a catchment population of approximately 5 million to 7 million people across the Central Gondar Zone and neighbouring regions. The facility operates within a mixed public health economy; while it is a government-funded institution providing subsidised care, the health system is characterised by a fee-for-service model for those without insurance. Although a Community-Based Health Insurance scheme has expanded coverage, supporting 57.5% of the study cohort, and patients often face out-of-pocket expenses for specific diagnostic tests, specialised cardiac medications and supplies not available in the hospital pharmacy. Access is further constrained by geographic distance for rural inhabitants and the limited availability of advanced CV interventions, such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).

According to the hospital’s Health Management Information System (HMIS) registries, a total of 1425 ACS patients were treated during this period, averaging approximately one case per day—reflects the unique systemic challenges within the Ethiopian healthcare landscape rather than a low regional prevalence of coronary artery disease. This volume is primarily influenced by significant prehospital barriers, including a lack of organised emergency medical services and substantial delays in seeking care, which often result in prehospital mortality. Furthermore, diagnostic limitations at primary and secondary healthcare levels, such as the inconsistent availability of electrocardiography and high-sensitivity troponin assays, lead to frequent underdiagnosis and a reduced referral rate to tertiary centres. A systematic random sampling technique was used to select the study participants. The sampling interval was calculated as K=1425/404 ≈ 3.53 and every fourth patient was selected. The first medical chart was chosen using a lottery method. If a selected chart was unavailable or the patient did not meet the inclusion criteria, the next eligible chart was included. Patient charts were retrieved from the hospital’s record room using their corresponding medical registration numbers.

Operational definitions

MACE: in this study, MACE was defined as the composite occurrence of reinfarction, stroke or heart failure.10 25

OMT was defined as the use of five recommended medications—beta-blockers, statins, dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) and either ACE inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs)—in accordance with ACC/AHA guidelines, provided there were no contraindications.26 27 Patients were considered to have received OMT if they were prescribed at least 75% of these indicated medications at discharge, which served as the threshold for defining OMT use.28

Statin intensity was defined according to the ACC/AHA guidelines on cholesterol management.29 High-intensity statins included atorvastatin at 40 or 80 mg/day and rosuvastatin at 20 or 40 mg/day, while all other statin regimens were classified as low to moderate intensity.10 30 31

Event: the occurrence of a MACE following an ACS was considered the event of interest.

Outcome measurements

The primary outcome was time to MACE, defined as a composite of reinfarction, stroke or new-onset heart failure. This duration was calculated in years from the date of hospital discharge to the date of the first documented event.

Event adjudication and data quality

To ensure rigorous event adjudication, an MACE was recorded only when a clinical diagnosis by a consultant physician was supported by objective diagnostic evidence. This included:

Reinfarction: new Electrocardiogram changes or significant elevation of cardiac biomarkers (eg, Troponin).

Stroke: confirmed via neuroimaging (CT or MRI).

Heart failure: confirmed by echocardiographic evidence or clinical signs requiring hospitalisation.

To maintain the integrity of the dataset, a complete case analysis approach was adopted. During the systematic sampling process, any medical chart missing more than 20% of the required baseline or outcome variables was excluded and replaced by the next eligible chart to ensure the target sample size of 404 was maintained.

Censoring and follow-up

Patients who did not experience an MACE by the end of the study period (31 December 2024) were censored at the date of their last recorded follow-up. To minimise attrition bias, medical charts were cross-referenced with the hospital’s mortality registry. In cases where patients were lost to follow-up, data were censored at the last known clinic contact. We acknowledge that, consistent with resource-limited settings, out-of-hospital events not reported to the facility were censored at the last documented encounter.

Data collection procedures and quality control technique

Three experienced data collectors, a physician, a nurse and a pharmacist, were involved in data collection. Prior to data collection, they received 2 days of training on the data collection instrument (Kobo Toolbox) and study procedures. Medical records of ACS patients attending the chronic follow-up clinic from January 2020 to December 2024 were reviewed. Patient logbooks were first compiled and re-entered into Microsoft Excel 2013 to check for duplicates. Using these verified lists, relevant clinical data, including baseline troponin levels, lipid profiles, blood glucose levels, blood pressure and other variables, were retrospectively extracted from medical archives.

The data abstraction tool was developed based on a review of relevant literature and adapted to suit the local clinical context. A pretest was conducted on 5% of the sample within the study setting to ensure the completeness and clarity of the abstraction format. Records used during the pretest were excluded from the final analysis. Necessary revisions were made to the tool based on the pretest findings. The data collection process was supervised closely, with the supervisor providing clear instructions on the study objectives and proper use of the abstraction tool.

Laboratory data, including lipid profiles, serum creatinine and cardiac troponin I (cTnI), were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic and paper-based records. All tests were conducted at the facility’s centralised laboratory using standardised automated analysers and enzymatic assays. For the purpose of this study, all values were converted from mass units to SI units. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, specific reagent manufacturers and intra-assay coefficients of variation were not available for the 4-year study period.

Data processing and analysis

Data were exported from KoboToolbox to Microsoft Excel for cleaning and then analysed using STATA V.17. Descriptive statistics were computed, and χ2 tests were used to compare categorical proportions. The primary outcome, time to MACE, was analysed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and log-rank tests. Incidence rates were calculated per person-years of observation. For multivariable analysis, a Cox proportional hazards model was fitted. Variables were selected based on a bivariable threshold of p<0.25 or established clinical relevance (eg, age, Killip class and comorbidities). The proportional hazards assumption was verified via Schoenfeld residuals (p=0.8297) and log-log plots. Model fit was assessed using Cox–Snell residuals, and multicollinearity was ruled out using variance inflation factors. To prevent overfitting, the final model was restricted to approximately one covariate per 10 events. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Statistical significance was declared at p<0.05.

Person-time of follow-up was calculated for each participant from the date of hospital discharge to the occurrence of the first MACE or censoring. Total person-years of observation were obtained by summing the individual follow-up times, and the incidence rate was calculated as the number of events divided by the total person-years of follow-up. Participants were censored if they were lost to follow-up or did not experience the outcome by the end of the study period. We evaluated the patterns of missingness and believe that the assumption of missing at random is reasonable. Analysis of the baseline characteristics showed that missingness for time-related variables and prescribing information was primarily associated with specific data collection sites and admission years rather than the clinical severity or outcomes of the patients themselves.

Ethical principle and consent to participate

Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the Research Ethics Review Committee of the Department of Clinical Pharmacy, School of Pharmacy, College of Medicine and Health Sciences, University of Gondar with the approval number of SOPS/298/2024. Following approval, an official letter of cooperation was provided to the UoGCSH to facilitate data collection. Since the study tracked outcomes for patients admitted between January 2020 and December 2024, informed consent was obtained at the point of data collection.

For patients who were reachable during the study period, both verbal and written informed consent were obtained after a comprehensive explanation of the study’s purpose. Participants were informed that their participation was voluntary and that they maintained the right to withdraw at any stage. To maintain confidentiality, patient names and unique identification numbers were excluded from the data collection tool. All data were coded, securely stored and accessed only by the principal investigator. Information obtained from patient charts was treated with the utmost confidentiality throughout the study. The study adhered to the ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki.32

Results

Sociodemographics and behavioural characteristics

A total of 400 participants were included in the study, giving a response rate of 99%. We can confirm that there was a minor amount of missing data during the final stage of our study. While our target sample size was 404 patients (which included the 10% contingency buffer), 4 patients were found to have missing critical data during the final extraction and cleaning process. Consequently, these four patients were excluded, and the final analysis was conducted using a complete case approach with a total of 400 patients. Because this minor loss represents only 1% of our target sample, the study retains its required statistical power (which originally necessitated 366 participants). More than half were male (57%) and urban residents (60.5%). Participants aged ≥65 years accounted for 38%, while 57.5% had health insurance coverage. Regarding behavioural characteristics, alcohol use (10.25%) was more common than smoking (6.5%), khat chewing (Khat; Catha edulis, a natural stimulant plant containing cathinone) (5.25%) and other substance use (specifically cannabis and non-prescribed benzodiazepines) (0.75%) (table 1).

Symptoms on admission, clinical characteristics, Killip class and comorbidities of ACS patients

Chest pain alone was the most common presenting symptom (51%), followed by chest pain with nausea/vomiting (31.25%) and chest pain with shortness of breath (12%). Most patients stayed in hospital for 7–14 days (39.25%). The majority had normal heart rate (92.5%), systolic blood pressure (66%) and diastolic blood pressure (69.25%) on admission. According to Killip classification, most patients were in class II (30.25%) or class III (27.5%), indicating moderate heart failure symptoms. Only 3.5% underwent cardiac catheterisation.

Regarding ACS type, NSTEMI was the most common diagnosis (56.75%), followed by STEMI (30.5%) and unstable angina (12.75%). Hypertension (42.75%) and diabetes mellitus (32.5%) were the most prevalent comorbidities, while dyslipidaemia, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, obesity and cardiogenic shock were less common (table 2).

Medication given during discharge

Regarding discharge medications, the majority of patients were prescribed evidence-based secondary prevention therapies. Statins were given to 392 patients (98%), and aspirin was prescribed to 391 patients (97.75%). Beta-blockers were used in 351 patients (87.75%), while 306 patients (76.5%) received DAPT. Additionally, 285 patients (71.25%) were discharged on either ACEIs or ARBs, and 84 patients (21%) received calcium channel blockers (figure 1). Overall, 243 of the 400 patients (60.75%) received OMT at discharge, indicating that just over three-fifths of the cohort was prescribed the recommended secondary prevention regimen.

Median survival time to develop MACE

Among the 400 patients with ACS, 135 (33.8%) experienced an MACE during the follow-up period; from this 15.5%, 11.0% and 7.25% were stroke, heart failure and myocardial reinfarction, respectively, while 265 (66.3%) were censored. The median follow-up time was 2.4 years (IQR: 1.82–2.76), while the median time to MACE was 1.5 years (95% CI 1.0 to 2.4). Over the 5-year follow-up, the cohort contributed a total of 280.08 person-years of observation. This corresponds to an incidence rate of 0.48 events per person-year (48 per 100 person-years) (figure 2).

Overall survival function

The Kaplan-Meier survival curve demonstrates a progressive decline in MACE-free survival among ACS patients over the 5-year follow-up period. Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a precipitous decline in MACE-free survival during the first 24 months postdischarge, followed by a gradual stabilisation (figure 3).

Kaplan-Meier survival curve showing time to major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) among patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) after hospital discharge while receiving secondary prevention therapy.

Comparison of survival experience

The Kaplan-Meier survival curves illustrate the estimated time to develop MACE across the different categories of each variable. The log-rank test was applied to determine whether there were statistically significant differences in time to MACE among these groups in ACS patients discharged on secondary prevention medications. In general, groups with lower Kaplan-Meier curves experienced a shorter time to develop MACE compared with those whose curves remained higher over the follow-up period.

The median time to develop MACE was shorter among male patients (1 year) compared with female patients (1.5±0.065 years, 95% CI 1.373 to 1.62). This difference was statistically significant based on the log-rank test (χ2=8.729, p=0.003) (figure 4). Similarly, ACS patients with comorbidities experienced a shorter median time to MACE (1.5 years) compared with those without comorbidities (2.5 years), with the log-rank test indicating a significant difference between the groups (χ2=6.107, p=0.013) (figure 5). Furthermore, elderly patients (≥66 years) had the shortest median time to MACE (1±0.422 year, 95% CI 0.365 to 1.83), compared with those aged ≤55 years (3.5 years) and 56–65 years (2.5 years), with the log-rank test indicating a significant difference between the groups (χ2=8.626, p=0.013) (figure 6).

Determinants of time to develop MACEs

The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox model was evaluated using the Schoenfeld residuals test. The assumption was further assessed using log-minus-log plots, which demonstrated that the covariates in the model produced parallel curves (online supplemental figure 1-3).

In the final multivariable model, age, comorbidity, killip class and gender remained statistically significant predictors of time to MACE (p<0.05). After adjusting of other covariates, the hazard of developing MACE was 2.081-fold increase in female participants as compared with male (adjusted HR (AHR)=2.081, 95% CI 1.241 to 3.490). Discussion

This study aimed to determine the time to development of MACE and to identify its predictors among patients with ACS discharged on secondary prevention medications at the UoGCSH. A total of 400 participants were enrolled, representing a 99% response rate of the target sample. This high response rate was achieved through a hybrid data collection approach: baseline clinical and laboratory data were extracted from medical charts, while follow-up data regarding MACE and survival status were collected prospectively through telephone interviews and clinic visit reviews. Participants were followed for up to 5 years to accurately determine the time to event, with those unreachable after multiple phone attempts being the primary reason for the 1% non-response. The median time to development of MACE was 1.5 years. During the follow-up period, 33.8% of the 400 ACS patients experienced at least one MACE. Multivariable Cox regression analysis demonstrated that age, comorbidity status, sex and Killip class were independent predictors of time to MACE within this cohort.

The present study observed high physician adherence to individual therapies like statins (98%) and aspirin (97.75%), yet the composite OMT rate was significantly lower at 60.75%. This prescription gap underscores a potential challenge: while single agents are easily initiated, integrating a full five-drug regimen may be hindered by physician inertia, polypharmacy concerns or clinical caution regarding renal function and bleeding risks.

According to Geremew et al,7 and Awashra et al,33 achieving composite OMT appears to be a meaningful determinant of postdischarge outcomes, with even marginal improvements linked to reduced long-term mortality. This is particularly vital during the vulnerable window of the first 12–24 months post-ACS, where MACE clustering is most frequent. In regions with limited revascularisation, the synergistic protection of OMT, including beta-blockers and DAPT, is often considered a key defence against early recurrence.34 Given that Killip class and comorbidities were strong predictors in our cohort, maximising ACEI/ARB and DAPT use warrants close clinical consideration.

While our recent literature has examined the postdischarge landscape for ACS patients in Ethiopia, such as the study by Geremew,7 which primarily focused on the prevalence of OMT and its direct impact on mortality. However, the current study addresses this gap by shifting the focus from a binary outcome (mortality) and treatment adherence to the temporal dynamics of MACE. By analysing the ‘time-to-event’, this research provides a more nuanced perspective when patients are most vulnerable following discharge in this setting. Furthermore, while the previous study covered the period up to early 2023, this ongoing project extends the observation window through December 2024. This allows for an evaluation of CV outcomes reflecting recent clinical guidelines and the postpandemic healthcare environment in Ethiopia.

The median time for the development of MACE in the present study was 1.5 years, which is in line with other studies done in South Korea35 and Australia.36 For example, a South Korean PCI cohort with ACS demonstrated that a substantial proportion of MACE occurred within the first few years after intervention, with a median follow-up of 3.8 years, showing nearly 20% experiencing events within that period, supporting relatively early event clustering postindex procedure. Consistent with these observations, large observational data from a US nationwide PCI sample reported that among patients who did experience MACE, the mean time to first event was about 1.37±1.28 years after index PCI, again underscoring a clustering of events within the first couple of years after coronary intervention.37 Collectively, these findings support the concept that while the absolute timing to MACE can vary by underlying condition and population, there is a recurring pattern of most events occurring within 1–2 years following the index CV insult or intervention, with the median time in many cohorts clustering near or under 2 years. This consistency with the present result strengthens the comparability of outcomes across diverse settings and emphasises the importance of vigilant follow-up during this high-risk period.

In the present cohort of 400 patients with ACS, 33.8% experienced at least one MACE over the follow-up period, indicating a substantial burden of recurrent CV morbidity in this high-risk population. This incidence is comparable with several prior observational studies of ACS and coronary artery disease cohorts. For example, a large registry of patients with coronary disease reported an overall MACE incidence of 36.7% over a similar median follow-up duration, underscoring a broadly similar range of event rates in real-world practice.38 Moreover, population-based data from long-term follow-up of first ACS survivors found that about 34%–48% experienced recurrent MACE within 1–3 years, with nearly a third occurring within the first year after the initial event.21 This further affirms that a substantial proportion of ACS patients remains vulnerable to subsequent adverse events despite contemporary management. However, reported MACE rates can vary substantially depending on study design, follow-up duration and patient characteristics. For instance, registry data from Brazilian ACS patients showed lower 1-year MACE rates (~13.6%), likely reflecting differences in event definitions, follow-up intensity and regional care practices.39 Conversely, studies with more extended follow-ups or higher risk populations sometimes report even higher cumulative MACE burdens. Collectively, the consistency with other cohorts highlights the ongoing risk of recurrent CV events after an ACS episode and supports the need for optimised secondary prevention and risk stratification strategies to potentially reduce subsequent adverse outcomes.

The age of the patient was a significant predictor of time to develop MACE among ACS patients in this study. This study showed that younger age (≤55) had lower risk of developing MACE as compared with elderly (≥66) (AHR=0.766, 95% CI 0.544 to 0.983; p value=0.008). This is consistent with studies done in China,40 Iran,41 Finland21 and Germany.42 Mechanistically, the increased MACE risk seen in older patients may be attributed to age-related vascular changes, higher prevalence of comorbidities, more extensive atherosclerosis and reduced physiological reserve, all of which may predispose elderly individuals to recurrent ischaemic events and worse recovery trajectories.40 43–45 This highlights the need for careful risk assessment in older patients after ACS and suggests that they may benefit from closer follow-up and optimised secondary prevention strategies to reduce the likelihood of recurrent CV events.

This study also found that chronic comorbidities were a significant predictor of time to develop MACE among ACS patients discharged with secondary preventive medications. The hazard of developing MACE among patients with chronic comorbidity was more than two times as compared with those without comorbidity (AHR=2.094, 95% CI 1.420 to 4.407; p value=0.042). This is in line with a study done in Spain,46 in a multinational ACS cohort; diabetes has been shown to confer significantly elevated MACE risk even after adjusting for other confounders and remains one of the most important prognostic factors in long-term follow-up analyses. Similarly, population-based research in Finland has shown that higher comorbidity burden, often quantified by indices such as the Charlson comorbidity index, correlates with an increased incidence of recurrent MACE and mortality after the first ACS event. For instance, studies demonstrate that patients with multiple coexisting chronic conditions including diabetes, renal dysfunction and prior heart failure have worse outcomes than those without these comorbidities, and that risk escalates with the number and severity of conditions present.21 The underlying mechanisms linking comorbidity to worse CV outcomes are multifactorial. Chronic conditions often contribute to systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction and accelerated atherosclerosis, which predispose patients to plaque instability and thrombosis. In addition, the presence of comorbidity may complicate management, limit the tolerance of aggressive therapies and reflect more advanced underlying disease, all of which can compromise recovery and increase vulnerability to subsequent ischaemic events.47–49

Being female was found to be a significant predictor of time to develop MACE among ACS patients discharged with secondary prevention medications, with women experiencing a higher hazard of adverse events compared with men (AHR=2.081, 95% CI 1.241 to 3.490; p=0.005). This finding is consistent with studies conducted in diverse settings, including large registry-based and cohort studies from Portugal,50 Australia,51 North America52 53 and Asia,54 which have reported higher rates of recurrent CV events and worse post-ACS outcomes among women compared with men. Previous studies have shown that women with ACS tend to present at an older age, have a higher burden of comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension54 55 and more frequently experience atypical symptoms,56 all of which may delay diagnosis and initiation of optimal treatment. Additionally, evidence suggests that women are less likely to receive invasive procedures and guideline-recommended secondary preventive therapies, which may further contribute to their increased risk of MACE.57 58 Finally, sociocultural barriers to healthcare access and disparities in the intensity of secondary prevention, where women may be less likely to receive the full OMT package due to perceived risk or cost, likely contribute to this increased hazard. Collectively, these factors may explain the observed sex-based disparity in outcomes and highlight the importance of sex-specific risk assessment and tailored secondary prevention strategies in female ACS survivors. However, evidence is mixed, and some studies report no increased or even lower adjusted risk of MACE in women when comprehensive confounding factors are controlled.51 59 The study highlights the gender disparity as a specific area for future intervention or qualitative research to understand why women in this region are at such significantly higher risk.

Killip class was also an independent predictor of time to development of MACE among patients with ACS discharged on secondary prevention medications. Patients classified as Killip class IV had a 56.6% higher hazard of developing MACE compared with those in Killip class I (AHR=1.566; 95% CI 1.221 to 1.780; p=0.044). This is supported by a number of studies. A large multicentre registry in the USA demonstrated that higher Killip classes are associated with progressively higher mortality, with classes III/IV showing substantially increased risk compared with class I, independent of other clinical variables. In this study, Killip class was one of the strongest predictors of both short-term and medium-term mortality in non-ST elevation ACS, with adjusted HRs significantly elevated for higher Killip categories.60 Another prospective cohort of STEMI patients in Italy, Killip class III–IV was independently associated with a higher risk of MACEs at follow-up, indicating its value not only for in-hospital risk but also for long-term outcomes, including recurrent ischaemic events and heart failure hospitalisations.61 Similarly, a study conducted in Israel and six Middle Eastern countries demonstrated that Killip class remains a significant independent predictor of mortality and MACE in the contemporary management of ACS. Despite advances in reperfusion strategies and the widespread use of guideline-directed secondary prevention therapies, patients presenting with higher Killip classes continued to experience substantially higher 1-year mortality and MACE rates compared with those in lower Killip classes. The confirmation of Killip class as a predictor, despite limited access to advanced haemodynamic monitoring, suggests it remains a robust, low-cost triaging tool for clinicians in Ethiopia. These findings highlight that the prognostic impact of initial heart failure severity is not fully mitigated by modern therapeutic interventions and underscore the persistent vulnerability of patients with advanced Killip class.

The findings of this study have important clinical and policy implications. Risk-stratified follow-up, closer monitoring for older adults, women and patients with comorbidities, as well as the careful titration of secondary prevention therapies, could potentially support early detection and management of recurrent events. Strengthening multidisciplinary care teams, enhancing adherence support, and ensuring access to guideline-directed therapies are essential to help reduce MACE risk. Moreover, system-level interventions, including structured post-ACS follow-up protocols, performance monitoring and ongoing provider training, may help mitigate therapeutic inertia and improve long-term outcomes in similar healthcare settings.

In summary, this study highlights the presence of residual risk of recurrent CV events following ACS within the study population and identifies associated predictors—age, sex, comorbidity burden and Killip class—that could help guide individualised and policy-level strategies to improve patient outcomes. Future prospective multicentre studies incorporating detailed clinical, procedural and adherence-related variables are warranted to further refine risk stratification and intervention strategies in resource-limited settings.

Strength and limitation of study

A primary strength of this study lies in its robust retrospective cohort design, which utilised an extensive follow-up period of up to 5 years. This longitudinal approach provided a granular, long-term look into the patterns of MACE over time. Furthermore, the study maintains high methodological rigour by employing advanced time-to-event analyses, specifically Kaplan-Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards regression models, with all proportional hazards assumptions strictly verified. Internal validity, consistency and external comparability were further enhanced through the integration of standardised, guideline-based clinical definitions for all measured variables.

Conversely, several limitations must be acknowledged. Because the study relied on historical medical documentation, its retrospective nature introduces a potential risk of information bias, which is further compounded by the absence of long-term medication adherence monitoring. Additionally, the findings have limited generalisability; as a single-centre study conducted within a healthcare infrastructure lacking advanced revascularisation interventions, these outcomes may not fully apply to settings with widespread access to invasive cardiac care.

Conclusion

In the sample of patients with ACS discharged on secondary prevention medications at our centre, the median time to MACE (MA was 1.5 years), with approximately one-third experiencing at least one event during follow-up. Older age, female sex, presence of comorbidities and higher Killip class independently predicted shorter time to MACE within this cohort. These findings highlight the persistent risk of recurrent CV events observed in this patient group and underscore the potential benefit of individualised, risk-stratified postdischarge care. Targeted clinical strategies, including closer observation of high-risk subgroups, optimisation of secondary prevention within available resources and enhanced adherence support, are warranted to help mitigate the burden of MACE in this setting.
