In vivo dosimetry using an electronic portal imaging device for pediatric myeloablative Total body irradiation with volumetric modulated arc therapy☆
Authors: Francesca Greco, Matteo Galetto, Maria Vaccaro, Marco De Spirito, Andrea Fidanzio, Elisa Placidi, Enrico Rosa, Gerardina Stimato, Stefania Teodoli, Silvia Mariani, Nicola Dinapoli, Vincenzo Frascino, Maria Antonietta Gambacorta, Silvia Chiesa, Elisa Meldolesi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeVolumetric Modulated Arc Therapy (VMAT) has enabled highly conformal Total Body Irradiation (TBI), improving dose homogeneity and sparing of organs-of-interest. However, the increased complexity of VMAT-based TBI requires robust quality assurance strategies. This study evaluated the feasibility, accuracy, and reproducibility of Electronic Portal Imaging Device (EPID)-based in-vivo dosimetry (IVD) for VMAT TBI treatments.Materials and MethodsForty pediatric patients treated with VMAT-based TBI were retrospectively analyzed with two prescription schemes: 12 Gy/six fractions and 9.99 Gy/three fractions. EPID transmission images were acquired during each VMAT arc and analyzed using a gamma passing rate (Pγ) of 5%/2 mm, 10% threshold. IVD was evaluated across different anatomical regions. The impact of inter-fraction anatomical variations (weight loss, abdominal swelling, gastrointestinal air) was assessed through dose recalculations on modified CT datasets. Phantom measurements were performed to validate the sensitivity of EPID-based IVD to clinically relevant perturbations.ResultsIVD measurements showed median Pγ of 97.3 ± 3.2%. Across the forty patients, a mean Pγ of 99.3 ± 0.8% and 99.0 ± 0.4% was observed in the head and thoracic regions respectively, lower values (96.5 ± 3.4% and 94.3 ± 3.6%) were found in the lumbar and pelvic regions respectively. Simulated abdominal swelling ≥1.5 cm reduced target coverage, while weight loss and gastrointestinal air had limited dosimetric impact. Phantom tests confirmed IVD sensitivity to significant anatomical changes.ConclusionsEPID-based IVD was a feasible tool for verifying VMAT-based TBI, enabling detection of clinically relevant inter-fraction anatomical variations and supporting safe treatment delivery.Keywords: Total Body Irradiation, In-vivo dosimetry, EPID, Quality assurance, Pediatric radiotherapy

Introduction

Total Body Irradiation (TBI) is a key component of the conditioning regimen prior to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], aiming to eradicate residual malignant cells and achieve immunosuppression of the recipient. Traditionally, TBI was delivered using extended static fields to achieve a uniform dose distribution. More recently, advances in intensity-modulated radiotherapy have improved dose homogeneity by reducing overdose and underdose regions, while enhancing organ-of-interest (OOI) sparing, particularly for the lungs and kidneys [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. The introduction of Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy (VMAT) has enabled a highly personalized and effective approach to TBI [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]. VMAT permits continuous modulation of dose rate, gantry speed, and multileaf collimator positions, enabling highly personalized treatments over very large target volumes. Despite these advantages, this VMAT-based technique introduces a high level of complexity in both treatment planning and delivery. The use of multiple isocenters, prolonged treatment times, and the strong dependence on patient positioning and anatomy increases the risk of dosimetric uncertainties. Consequently, robust quality assurance strategies are essential to ensure that the planned dose is accurately delivered. In this context, in-vivo dosimetry (IVD) plays a central role, providing direct verification of patient anatomy, beam delivery, and absorbed dose during treatment [8], [18], [19], [20]. IVD enables the identification of discrepancies between planned and delivered dose distributions, including those related to anatomical variations, setup inaccuracies, treatment planning system (TPS) calculation uncertainties, and machine-related errors [19], [20].

Among the available IVD approaches, Electronic Portal Imaging Devices (EPIDs) have gained increasing attention for in vivo dose reconstruction based on EPID images acquired during treatment delivery [19], [21], [22], [23]. EPID-based IVD is non-invasive, provides high spatial resolution, and is fully integrated into modern linear accelerators, allowing routine clinical implementation. By analyzing transit images acquired during irradiation, it is possible to reconstruct the dose delivered to the patient and directly compare it with the planned dose distribution. Several studies demonstrated the feasibility and accuracy of EPID-based IVD for IMRT and VMAT treatments, including large and highly modulated fields [18], [22], [24]. However, VMAT-TBI introduces specific challenges that are not fully addressed by standard VMAT applications, including whole-body target extension, multi-isocenter delivery, irradiation fields that can exceed the EPID active area, prolonged treatment times, and clinically relevant inter-fraction anatomical variations. These aspects may affect EPID image acquisition, fluence comparison, and the interpretation of IVD results.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the feasibility, accuracy, and reproducibility of EPID-based IVD in TBI treatments performed with the VMAT technique and to assess its potential to improve treatment safety and quality assurance for patients undergoing hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

Material and methods

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective design and the use of anonymized data collected from routine clinical practice, formal ethics committee approval was not required.

Patient characteristics

Forty pediatric patients were treated with a myeloablative VMAT TBI regimen at our institution, “Fondazione Policlinico Universitario Agostino Gemelli IRCCS”, from May 2024 to December 2025. Two different dose prescriptions were used depending on patient compliance and age: 12 Gy delivered in six fractions twice a day with an interval of at least 6 h between fractions and 9.99 Gy in three fractions delivered once a day for patients treated under anesthesia. The mean patient age was 11 years ranging from 5 to 19 years, with a height between 106 and 180 cm.

Imaging and planning

The VMAT-TBI technique required a multi-isocenter approach to ensure full target dose coverage over the entire body length [16]. Patients were treated from the head to the lower pelvis in a head-first supine (HF) position, whereas irradiation of the legs was performed in a feet-first supine (FF) configuration to avoid gantry–couch collisions. Patients were immobilized on a rotatable tabletop (TBI-Step, IT-V) using customized thermoplastic masks and a vacuum cushion to ensure setup reproducibility and patient comfort [10], [13], [14], [15], [25]. Legs and feet were positioned as close as possible without contact, and hands were placed against the body inside the vacuum bed to avoid high-dose hot spots and improve reproducibility.

Two CT scans were acquired using a SOMATOM go.Open Pro scanner (Siemens Healthineers): one HF scan from above the head to below the pelvis and one FF scan from below the feet to above the pelvis. Slice thickness was 5 mm for patients taller than 120 cm and 3 mm for smaller patients. [9], [10], [13], [14], [17]. The pelvic overlap region and the rotatable tabletop were included in the scans to enable image registration and improve dose calculation accuracy. HF and FF scans were rigidly registered in the treatment planning system. Contouring was performed according to the previous international recommendations [9].

Treatments were planned in Eclipse TPS (v18.0.1) and delivered on a TrueBeam™ linear accelerator (Varian Medical Systems, A Siemens Healthineers Company) using 6 MV photons. Plans included an HF VMAT section and an FF static AP/PA section for the legs. Junctions between HF isocenters were optimized using Auto-Feathering with at least 2 cm overlap [10], [26], while gradually reduced AP/PA field sizes were used in the FF section to ensure smooth dose gradients [14], [15], [27].

Plan quality was evaluated using DVH metrics (V90% > 98%, V95% > 90%, V110% < 5%, V120% < 1%) [9] and homogeneity was assessed according to ICRU-83 [28]:

Mean dose constraints were applied to a 1cm isotropic contraction of lungs (Lungs-1) and 5mm isotropic contraction of kidneys (Kidneys-0.5) according to the prescribed total dose: < 8 Gy and < 10 Gy, respectively, for the 12 Gy prescription, and < 6.7 Gy and < 8.3 Gy, respectively, for the 9.99 Gy regimen [29].

Treatment delivery workflow

The linac was equipped with a six-degrees-of-freedom robotic treatment couch that allowed for both translational and rotational corrections, thus enabling sub-millimetric alignment accuracy relative to the planned treatment isocenters. For each HF isocenter, a daily Cone Beam CT (CBCT) acquisition was performed immediately before beam delivery to verify and correct the patient setup [30]. This approach was adopted to mitigate the effects of potential intra-fraction motion occurring during the extended treatment time, particularly between successive isocenters, and to account for the limited compliance often observed in pediatric patients.

For each FF isocenter, setup verification was performed using orthogonal kV images acquired before irradiation of the lower extremities, and the corresponding couch shifts were applied when necessary.

In-vivo dosimetry

IVD was performed during each treatment fraction by acquiring EPID transmission images for every VMAT arc. EPID images were compared with the corresponding predicted fluence maps derived from the TPS. The analysis and dose reconstruction were performed using the SunCheck® PerFRACTION™ software (Sun Nuclear, a Mirion Medical company), which enabled quantitative comparison between the planned and delivered fluence through a global gamma analysis (5%/2 mm criteria, 10% threshold). The IVD results were reported in terms of gamma passing rates (γPR). Tolerance and action levels were set at γPR 95% and 90% respectively.

IVD results below the tolerance level prompted review of EPID acquisition, delivery parameters, patient setup, and daily CBCT images. Results below the action level required multidisciplinary evaluation before subsequent treatment delivery. Depending on the cause and clinical relevance of the discrepancy, possible actions included closer monitoring, additional image review, dose recalculation on updated anatomy, or plan re-evaluation when clinically indicated.

Simulation of anatomical variations

To evaluate the dosimetric impact of anatomical changes on dose distribution, a dedicated analysis was performed in all patients by simulating abdominal-region variations, including weight loss, swelling, air bubbles, and patient rotation of 2° and 5°. Starting from the original patient CTs, abdominal swelling was simulated by expanding the body at the abdominal level by adding electronic bolus layers of different thicknesses. Conversely, weight loss was simulated by progressively reducing the body contour in the abdominal region by different thicknesses. In both cases, the PTV was modified to reflect the changes in the body contour.

The gastrointestinal air was simulated on the original patient CTs by introducing an artificial structure in the abdominal region and assigning it an air-equivalent density, without modifying the PTV and the body contours.

Rotational shift of the patients was simulated in the Eclipse TPS using the Robust Dose tool.

Treatment plans were recalculated using the same monitor units as the original plans to assess the resulting changes in PTV and OOIs dose distributions as a function of the magnitude of the applied variation.

Phantom measurements for in-vivo dosimetry validation

Phantom measurements were performed to define clinically meaningful gamma-analysis criteria for EPID-based IVD, which enabled the detection and interpretation of dosimetric variations related to patient anatomy and setup. The selection of tested conditions was guided by the anatomical and positioning differences observed in the treated patients, which resulted in IVD outcomes lower than the action level. Two VMAT arcs corresponding to a pelvic isocenter from a representative TBI patient were recalculated on a CT scan of the ArcCheckMR phantom (Sun Nuclear, a Mirion Medical company) equipped with the MultiPlug™ system and tissue-equivalent inserts to reproduce a heterogeneous tissue configuration (Fig. 1). The VMAT arcs were delivered to the phantom while acquiring EPID-based IVD measurements. A reference acquisition without introduced errors was first performed to establish a baseline EPID measurement. Subsequently, controlled perturbations were introduced to simulate clinically relevant scenarios and to evaluate the sensitivity of the IVD system. These included placement of a 0.5, 1 and 1.5 cm bolus on the phantom to mimic changes in external body contour, rotations of the MultiPlug™ insert by 2° and 5° to reproduce residual setup rotational errors, and replacement of air cavities (the two empty slots and inserts C and D for the ionization chamber (Fig. 1)) with solid water inserts to simulate variations in gastrointestinal gas volume.

ArcCheckMR phantom equipped with the MultiPlug™ insert and tissue-equivalent inserts. Two slots were left empty to reproduce air gaps, while the C and D inserts were used for positioning the ionization chambers. Inserts A, B, E, and F mimic muscle, lung, adipose, and cortical bone tissues, respectively. The goniometer on the phantom was used to apply rotational shifts to the insert.

Results

Forty patients were treated at our institution using a VMAT-based TBI technique with two different dose prescriptions: 12 Gy delivered in six fractions twice daily (21 patients) and 9.99 Gy delivered in three fractions once daily (19 patients).

Mean V90 values were 99.3 ± 0.8% for the 12 Gy protocol and 99.6 ± 0.5% for the 9.99 Gy protocol, while homogeneity indices were 0.13 ± 0.03 and 0.12 ± 0.02, respectively. Mean lungs-1 and kidneys-0.5 doses were 8.0 ± 0.7 Gy and 9.2 ± 0.5 Gy (12 Gy prescribed dose), and 6.4 ± 0.5 Gy and 8.0 ± 0.2 Gy (9.99 Gy prescribed dose). Discussion

This study reports the clinical implementation of EPID-based IVD for pediatric VMAT-TBI for the systematic evaluation of overall treatment quality. Measurements showed good agreement between planned and delivered doses, with an overall mean γPR of 97.3 ± 3.2%.

VMAT-based TBI plans met the planning objectives for both prescription schemes (Table 1), with dosimetric results consistent with previous studies [31], [32]. Higher γPR values were observed in the head and thorax regions, which could reasonably be attributed to the effectiveness of patient immobilization achieved through the combined use of a thermoplastic mask and a vacuum mattress, ensuring stable positioning of the head, shoulders, and upper thorax.

In contrast, lumbar and pelvic isocenters exhibited lower γPR values compared with the cranial and thoracic regions. This finding likely reflected the impact of inter-fraction anatomical variations and the lower reproducibility of immobilization in these regions, particularly with respect to residual rotations and variability in arm positioning.

In this workflow, EPID-based IVD provided post-delivery verification of the agreement between planned and delivered fluence for each arc, rather than direct real-time monitoring of intra-fraction motion. Therefore, intra-fraction motion may have contributed to changes in IVD results when it produced measurable variations in the transmitted fluence.

Offline review of CBCT images from patients showing reduced γPR values at the lumbar and pelvic isocenters revealed the presence of small residual patient rotations, as well as variations in gastrointestinal air volumes and patient thickness. These changes were observed during treatment delivery and were plausibly related to concomitant pharmacological therapies affecting the patient's hydration status.

Dose recalculations on modified CT datasets provided additional insight into the dosimetric impact of these anatomical variations, enabling a controlled assessment of the sensitivity of the dose distribution to changes in patient thickness and internal heterogeneities.

The results shown in Fig. 3 suggest that PTV coverage is more robust under weight loss, remaining within clinical acceptance across all Δs values for both prescription groups, whereas abdominal swelling progressively compromised target coverage. In the 12 Gy prescription group, V90 dropped below the 98% threshold when Δs reached 1.5 cm or more, while in the 9.99 Gy prescription group V90 remained above the threshold up to Δs = 1.5 cm and fell below it only at the largest simulated body contour change.

To date, the maximum abdominal swelling observed in our cohort has been 0.8 cm, and no patient has exhibited variations exceeding 1.5 cm. Accordingly, the 1.5 cm threshold should be interpreted as a simulated dosimetric benchmark rather than an automatic trigger for clinical intervention. If relevant abdominal swelling were detected during treatment, the need for plan re-evaluation would be assessed on a case-by-case basis together with the radiation oncologist, considering the magnitude of the anatomical variation, the timing within the treatment course, and the overall clinical context.

For the lungs, constraints were consistently respected in both scenarios and for both prescription groups. This finding is expected because lungs dose was only marginally affected, as the simulated body contour changes were limited to the abdominal region, which is anatomically distant from the lungs. Conversely, kidneys showed a clearer trend due to their proximity to the modified region: mean dose decreased with abdominal swelling and increased with weight loss. However, all OOI values remained below the corresponding clinical limits, confirming maintained OOI sparing.

Similarly, the presence of a simulated gastrointestinal air bubble and rotational shifts did not introduce any appreciable dosimetric variations.

The use of IVD for treatment assessment in VMAT-based TBI treatments was validated through phantom measurements and dose recalculations, simulating potential errors observed clinically in patients. However, both methods had limitations, including the reduced tissue heterogeneity complexity of the phantom compared with a real patient and the approximations introduced when simulating patient variations for dose recalculations.

Phantom measurements highlighted the capability of IVD to detect setup or inter-fraction variations affecting treatment delivery, such as changes in patient thickness or large air volumes. IVD was unable to detect small variations such as minor rotations, even with strict γ-analysis criteria; however, these variations were not clinically relevant by dose recalculations. Therefore, IVD measurements should always be validated by offline CBCT review to determine the cause of IVD results below tolerance. Different gamma-analysis parameters were evaluated to identify the most appropriate criteria for detecting dosimetrically relevant errors. Using gamma-analysis criteria looser than 5%/2 mm reduced the system's sensitivity to detecting clinically relevant errors that could affect the dose distribution, as demonstrated by phantom measurements (Table 2), while the use of 3%/2 mm was too sensitive to air variations inside the patient.

This work demonstrates the effectiveness of IVD in detecting treatment variations arising from inter-fraction anatomical changes and setup uncertainties and reported the IVD results for the first forty patients treated with VMAT-based TBI at our institution. IVD also represented a valuable tool for treatment verification, supporting a multidisciplinary evaluation of the treatment process.

By enabling the systematic analysis of measured data, IVD contributed to the identification of potential sources of variability and fostered continuous improvements throughout the treatment planning and delivery workflow.

Future studies will be needed to refine and optimize the criteria for IVD analysis to improve the sensitivity and accuracy of the method in detecting only clinically relevant errors.
