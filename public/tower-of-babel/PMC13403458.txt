Seminal fluid enhances monkeypox virus infection via amyloidogenic fibrils
Authors: Emma Torbica, Matthias Denkewitz, Tamara Quick, Nadja Zöller, Katja Steinhorst, Stefan Kippenberger, Florian Rothweiler, Jindrich Cinatl, Pia Veratti, Florian Full, Undraga Schagdarsurengin, Sandra Ciesek, Jan Münch, Janis A Müller, Denisa Bojkova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMpox is a re-emerging zoonosis caused by monkeypox virus (MPXV), with recent outbreaks driven by sexual transmission. How semen contributes to MPXV infection, however, remains unclear. Here, we show that human seminal fluid potently enhances MPXV infection in susceptible target cells. Pre-exposure of MPXV to seminal fluid from multiple donors markedly increased infection of primary human fibroblasts and keratinocytes. Semen-derived peptide fragments that assemble into cationic amyloid fibrils (SEVI and SEM) enhanced infection of epithelial cells, primary monocytes, and ex vivo human skin, as well as clinical MPXV isolates and other orthopoxviruses. SEVI increased virion attachment and uptake, and fibril-targeting molecular tweezer (CLR01) disrupted seminal amyloids and abrogated enhancement. Notably, tecovirimat and brincidofovir retained activity under SEVI-enhanced conditions. These findings identify seminal amyloids as host factors that amplify MPXV infectivity and broaden cell tropism, suggesting that semen actively promotes sexual transmission of mpox.KEYWORDS: Mpox, MPXV, sexual transmission, semen, SEVI, molecular tweezer

Introduction

Monkeypox virus (MPXV), a member of the Orthopoxviruses, has historically caused sporadic cases, primarily through zoonotic transmission and confined to West and Central Africa [1]. However, a significant increase in cases within endemic regions in the last two decades followed by a global outbreak in 2022 prompted health organizations to recognize mpox as a re-emerging infectious disease of public health concern [2,3]. Currently, MPXV is causing an outbreak on the African continent, with more than 46,000 reported cases and 1,492 deaths (case fatality rate 3.3%) across 11 African countries recorded between 2022 and 2024 [4]. In 2025 52,845 mpox cases have been reported globally [5].

Mpox clinically resembles smallpox, typically presenting with a characteristic skin rash that, in severe cases, is followed by systemic organ involvement [6]. Phylogenetic analyses classify MPXV into two major clades – clade I and clade II – with further subdivision into sub-lineages a and b [7]. Clade Ia is considered the most virulent, with case fatality rates approaching 12%, in contrast to clades IIa, IIb, and Ib, which are associated with fatality rates ranging from 0.1% to 3.6% [8]. The 2022 global outbreak was driven by clade IIb, which has demonstrated efficient and sustained human-to-human transmission [9,10]. In 2023, clade Ib, was identified as the cause of an outbreak in previously non-endemic regions of Africa [11]. While clades Ia and IIa are associated with zoonotic transmission, clades Ib and IIb have shown patterns of ongoing human-to-human spread independent of animal reservoirs [12,13].

Transmission of MPXV between humans occurs through direct contact with infected biological fluids or skin lesions, during close or intimate contact that compromise mucosal or skin barriers [3,8]. During the 2022 outbreak, the rash frequently appeared in the genital and anorectal regions, and 95% of infected individuals reported a history of sexual contact [3,14]. Similarly, approximately 83% of clade Ib cases in the outbreak in the Democratic republic of Congo (DRC) have been linked to sexual activity [15]. Recently, five clade Ib cases without travel history have been reported in European countries [16]. Several of these affected individuals disclosed sexual contact, suggesting that clade Ib may also be spreading internationally through sexual networks.

The frequent presentation of initial lesions on the genitalia, rectum, and oropharynx in people infected with clade Ib/IIb supports a model of transmission via direct mucocutaneous inoculation during sexual contact [3,13–15]. This clinical pattern differs from earlier African outbreaks, where non-zoonotic transmission primarily occurred through intrafamilial or nosocomial exposure and the rash followed a centrifugal distribution over the body [17]. Notably, MPXV clade IIb DNA has been frequently detected in the seminal fluid of infected individuals [18,19], and infectious virus could be isolated from semen [20] suggesting that semen may potentially serve as a vehicle for viral transmission.

In this study, we show that human seminal fluid markedly enhances MPXV infection by increasing virion attachment to the cell surface. We identify naturally occurring semen-derived peptides that form cationic amyloid fibrils as key mediators of this effect. Finally, we demonstrate that fibril-targeting molecular tweezer CLR01 [21,22] disrupts these amyloids and abolishes seminal fluid–mediated enhancement of MPXV infection, revealing a host-derived mechanism that may be amenable to therapeutic intervention.

Materials & methods

Seminal fluid

Semen collection and analysis were approved by the Ethics Committee of Medical Faculty of Justus-Liebig-University Giessen (AZ 55/13 with amendment from October 13, 2022) and Marburg University (24-317), and samples were obtained from healthy volunteers after written informed consent. Semen analysis confirmed that all donors are healthy fertile individuals according to WHO definition (Suppl. Table 1).

Peptides and amyloid formation

Peptides were synthesized using standard Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis, purified via preparative reverse-phase HPLC, and characterized by analytical HPLC and mass spectrometry. The lyophilized peptides were then reconstituted in serum-free PBS at concentrations ranging from 2 to 10 mg/mL. All peptides of semen-derived sequences were chemically synthesized by Celtek Peptides (Nashville, TN) and dissolved in PBS (pH 7.0) at concentrations ranging from 2 - 10 mg/ml. Amyloid formation of synthesized PAP (248-286) and SEM1 (86-107) was induced by agitating 2 mg/mL solutions in PBS in an Eppendorf Thermomixer over night at 37°C at 1.400 rpm.

Cell culture

Primary human foreskin fibroblasts (HF) and kidney epithelial cells extracted from an African green monkey (Vero cells) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 IU/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin and 2 mM glutamine. Primary human keratinocytes (HK) were cultured in DermaLife K (Cellsystems) medium supplemented with 100 IU/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Immortalized human corneal epithelial cells (HCE-T) were cultured in DMEM/Ham’s F12 (Bio&Sell) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 IU/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, 2% HEPES and 0.5% DMSO. Human epithelial cell line derived from nasal septum carcinoma (RPMI 2650) was cultured in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM) (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 IU/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin and 2 mM glutamine. Infection experiments were carried out in the corresponding media containing only 1% FBS.

Isolation and culture of monocytes

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from buffy coats obtained from healthy donors (provided by RK-Blutspendedienst Baden-Württemberg-Hessen, Institute for Transfusion Medicine and Immunohematology, Frankfurt am Main, Germany). The isolation was performed using density gradient centrifugation with Ficoll (Pancoll, PAN-Biotech). Mononuclear cells were collected from the interface, washed with PBS, and seeded onto Cell + culture dishes (Sarstedt) in RPMI1640 medium (Gibco, ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with 100 IU/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Following a 90-minute incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO₂, non-adherent cells were removed. The medium was then replaced with fresh RPMI1640 supplemented with 100 IU/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 3% human serum (provided by RK-Blutspendedienst Baden-Württemberg-Hessen, Institute for Transfusion Medicine and Immunohematology, Frankfurt am Main, Germany).

Virus stock preparation

All used MPXV isolates were prepared using HF cells as previously described [23]. Briefly, confluent T175 flasks were infected at MOI of 0.1 for 72 h. After one freeze/thawing cycle, the cell debris was pelleted by centrifugation 800 rpm for 10 min and purified stocks were stored at −80°C. The virus titer was determined as TCID50/ml in confluent HF in 96-well microtiter plates. The following MPXV strains have been used in this study: Clade Ia (MPXV-Sudan, RKI), Clade Ib (MpxV/PHAS-506/Passage-03/SWE/2024_09_11), Clade IIa (MPXV-TNP, RKI), Clade IIb (MPXV1, EPI_ISL_15706238; MPXV2, EPI_ISL_15704688; MPXV3, EPI_ISL_15705315; MPXV4, EPI_ISL_15705640).

Cowpox (CPXV, Hum/Gri 07/01) and Camelpox (CMLV, CP-1) were prepared using Vero E6 cells. About 80% confluent T175 flasks were infected at a MOI of 0.01 for 5 days (Cowpox) and 6 days (Camelpox). After three freeze/thaw cycles, the cells were collected by scratching and pelleted by centrifugation at 3400 rpm for 10 min. Purified stocks were stored at −80°C. The virus titer was determined as pfu/mL in confluent Vero E6 cells in 12 well plates.

Detection and quantification of amyloid fibrils in SEVI and SEM samples

The ProteoStat Protein aggregation assay (ENZ-51023, Enzo Life Science, Germany) was used as recommended by the manufacturer. 10 µg/mL agitated and non-agitated PAP (248-286) as well as 25 µg/mL SEM (86-107) diluted in PBS was stained with ProteoStat dye at room temperature for 20 min. As a negative control, PBS was simultaneously stained. For fluorescence imaging and analysis, the Operetta CLS High Content Analysis System with Harmony software 5.2 (Revvity) was used. To quantify microscopy data, a threshold of fluorescence intensity was set to define ProteoStat stained structures which excludes background fluorescence. Further analysis by the Operetta included measuring the area of the previously set fluorescence threshold of ProteoStat-stained structures. Frequency distribution was performed using GraphPad Prism.

Infection assays with seminal fluid or semen-derived amyloid fibrils

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and were used for experiments after reaching confluency. Virus was pre-incubated with either seminal fluid or semen-derived amyloid fibrils (SEVI, SEM) at different concentrations for 5 min at room temperature or let untreated. For experiments involving seminal fluid, the virus was pre-incubated with semen at initial concentrations of 50, 10, 2, 0.4 and 0% (v/v). Once the mixture was applied on the cells, it underwent a 1:15 dilution, yielding final semen concentrations of 3.34, 0.67, 0.134, 0.027 and 0% on the cells. For experiments involving SEVI or SEM, the virus was pre-incubated with either SEVI or SEM in a 1:2 dilution, resulting in final concentrations of 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25 and 0 µg/ml for SEVI and 25, 12.5, 6.25, 3.125 and 0 µg/ml for SEM, together with a viral MOI of 0.04. Infection was carried out for 2 h. Afterwards, cells were washed 3 times with PBS and supplemented with fresh medium containing 1% FCS. The number of infected cells was quantified 24 h post infection using immunofluorescent staining of virus antigen.

Immunofluorescent staining of MPXV-infected cells

Cells were fixed and permeabilized with methanol/acetone (60:40) followed by blocking with solution containing 5% goat serum and 2% BSA. Primary antibody against MPXV, Anti-Vaccinia Virus antibody (Abcam; 1:4000) and secondary fluorescent antibody AlexaFluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific;1:4000) were used to capture virus infection. DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:1000) was utilized to visualize cell nuclei. Cells were imaged by Operetta CLS High Content Analysis System (Revvity) at 10x magnification capturing and analyzing the whole well (9 fields) with Harmony software 5.2 (Revvity). Following nucleus segmentation, cells were classified as infected if a surrounding Alexa Fluor 647-positive signal (MPXV staining) was detected. The intensity threshold for Alexa Fluor 647 was manually adjusted to accurately distinguish MPXV-positive staining. The infection rate was then calculated using the following formula: (Number of infected cells)/(Total number of nuclei) × 100.

MPXV infection of ex vivo human skin tissue

All experiments were performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki principles and in agreement with the institutional review board (112/06; 386/14). Ex vivo human skin tissue samples were obtained from split-thickness skin grafts not used for trans-implantation. Directly after skin removal, the sample was placed in PBS and immediately processed. The tissue was first perforated by microneedle (Segminismart®) from epidermal site to facilitate virus infection. Subsequently, 3 x 3 mm skin pieces were infected with 106 TCID50/mL of MPXV (clade IIb) pre-incubated with SEVI in 1% FCS containing MEM for 72 h. Afterwards, tissue samples were washed with PBS and fixed 4.5% Histofix over-night at 4°C. The fixed tissue samples were then paraffin-embedded and cut into 4 µm sections. After deparaffinisation and heat-induced epitope retrieval (Target Retrieval Solution pH9, Agilent-Dako, S2367), sections were incubated with a primary anti-vaccinia virus antibody (1:10.000, Abcam, ab35219), followed by incubation with secondary anti-rabbit IgG-horseradish peroxidase conjugates (ZytoChem HRP Kit, HRP-125, Zytomed Systems), and visualization using HistoGreen (Histo Green Kit, Linaris, LIN-E109) as peroxidase substrate. Images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse Ci microscope at 4× magnification. The infected area was quantified using ImageJ/Fiji by defining the total skin area (ROItotal) and the stained area (ROIstain) with the Threshold function. The same threshold settings were applied consistently across all samples. The percentage of infected area was calculated using the following formula: Infected area (%) = ⁣(ROIstain)/(ROItotal) × 100.

Preparation of purified mature viral particles (MVs)

HF cells in confluent T175 flasks were infected with MPXV (Clade IIb) and incubated for 48 h. Afterwards, cells were scraped into a falcon tube for centrifugation at 300 x g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 9.0). A Freeze-thaw cycle of the virus material was done 3 times, with 10 s of vortexing between each cycle. Afterwards, the virus material was homogenized by sonicating 4 times for 15 s at maximal power. For further purification of the virus material centrifugation at high speed for 5-10 min was done at 4°C. The supernatant was collected in 50 mL falcon tubes and kept on ice. The pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 9.0). The sonication and centrifugation steps were repeated, and the supernatants were pooled together. DNase I treatment was done with 0.1 mg/mL for 1 h. Subsequently, sterile 36% sucrose cushions were prepared by filling half a volume of an ultracentrifuge tube (SW28) with 36% sucrose. The sucrose cushion was overlayed with an equal volume of virus suspension and centrifuged for 90 min at 33000 g at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pelleted virus material was resuspended in 1 mM Tris-HCl (pH 9.0) and stored at −80°C.

Attachment assay

HF cells (0.5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well Phenoplates (Revvity) and subsequently infected with purified MVs (clade IIb) together with 10 µg/mL SEVI as described before. The virus-SEVI-mixture was incubated on the cells for 2 h at 4°C, followed by 3 washing steps with PBS and fixation with 4.5% Histofix for 20 min. For removal of surface-bound virus, a 0.05% trypsin treatment was performed for 10 min at 4°C prior to fixation. After fixation, the cells were incubated with a 50 mM quenching solution (Ammoniumchloride in PBS) for 5 min at room temperature. Fluorescent labelling of MPXV was done using a primary antibody against the envelope protein E8 (Sino Biological, 40890-T62; 1:1000) followed by secondary antibody treatment with a highly cross adsorbed Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-31573; 1:1000). Permeabilization with 0.1% triton X 100 was done after E8 primary and secondary antibody staining followed by additional antibody labelling of actin with a conjugated Phalloidin Alexa Fluor 488 antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific; A12379; 1:1000) as well as DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:1000). Fluorescent images were acquired on an Operetta CLS High-Content Analysis System (Revvity) using a 40× objective. Quantification of E8 puncta per cell was performed in Harmony software (v5.2, Revvity). Nuclei were segmented from the DAPI channel using the Find Nuclei module with setting B, corresponding to HF-cell nuclear size. Cell boundaries were then delineated with the Find Surrounding Region tool based on Phalloidin-488 staining. Alexa Fluor 647–positive puncta were identified using the Find Spots algorithm and restricted to the previously defined cellular region. Spot-detection thresholds for E8-positive puncta were optimized to ensure accurate cytoplasmic detection. The number of analysed cells is reported in the corresponding figure legends.

Internalization assay

HF cells (0.5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well Phenoplates (Revvity) and subsequently infected with purified MVs (clade IIb) together with 10 µg/mL SEVI as described before. In case of using molecular tweezers (CLR01), SEVI was preincubated with the respective molecular tweezer for 10 min at room temperature, followed by another 5 min incubation with the purified MVs (Clade IIb). The virus-SEVI-mixture or virus-SEVI-Tweezer-mixture was incubated on the cells for 2 h at 4°C, followed by 3 washing steps with PBS. After washing, the cells were incubated for an additional 1 h at 37°C. In case of removing surface-bound virus, a 0.05% trypsin treatment was performed for 10 min at 4°C. Subsequently, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4.5% Histofix for 20 min, followed with an incubation of a 50 mM quenching solution (Ammoniumchloride in PBS) for 5 min at room temperature. Permeabilization with 0.1% triton X 100 was done prior to fluorescent labelling. Fluorescent labelling of MPXV was done using a primary antibody against the envelope protein E8 (Sino Biological, 40890-T62; 1:1000) followed by secondary antibody treatment with a highly cross adsorbed Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-31573; 1:1000). Additional antibody labelling was done with a conjugated Phalloidin Alexa Fluor 488 antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific; A12379; 1:1000) as well as DAPI (1:1000). Fluorescent images were captured using Operetta CLS High Content Analysis System at 40x magnification. Number of E8 spots per cell was analysed with Harmony software 5.2 (Revvity) as previously described for attachment assay. The number of analysed cells is depicted in figures.

Antiviral assay

Confluent layers of HF cells were infected with MPXV with an MOI of 0.04 with and without SEVI (10 µg/mL) or with MOI 0.4. After 2 h of infection, the cells were washed and treated with decreasing concentrations of tecovirimat and brincidofovir. Antiviral effects were determined by immunostaining for MPXV (Anti-Vaccinia Virus antibody (abcam; 1:4000)) after 48 h. Fluorescent imaging and analysis were done using Operetta CLS High Content Analysis System with Harmony software 5.2 (Revvity).

Statistics

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise specified in the figure legend. The number of biological replicates, independent experiments, analyzed cells, or tissue sections is indicated in the corresponding figure legends, as applicable. Statistical significance is reported by displaying the corresponding p values directly in the figures. Each figure legend specifies the statistical test used for the respective analysis. Where multiple comparisons were performed, the applied multiple-comparison correction and the compared groups are stated in the figure legend. Curve fitting, IC50 determination, and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.

Results

Seminal fluid enhances MPXV infection

To investigate the impact of semen on MPXV infection, we pre-incubated the virus with 10% (v/v) seminal fluid (SF) from one donor for 5 min at room temperature. The virus–SF mixture was then diluted 15-fold upon inoculation of primary human fibroblasts (HF) and keratinocytes (HK), resulting in a final SF concentration of ∼0.7% (v/v) in the cell culture, i.e. below the >1% range at which SF becomes cytotoxic [24]. Under these conditions, immunofluorescence staining for MPXV at 24 h post infection showed a pronounced increase in the number of MPXV-positive cells in the presence of SF compared with the untreated control in both cell types (Figure 1(a)).

Having established that SF enhances MPXV infection, we next characterized the concentration dependence and donor-to-donor variability of this effect. For this, we tested SF from six individual healthy donors (Suppl. Table 1) at 50, 10, 2, 0.4 or 0% (v/v) during virion treatment. Under these conditions, SF from all donors increased the number of MPXV-positive cells in both HF and HK, with greater inter-donor variability observed in HK (Figure 1(b)). When data from all donors were averaged, SF concentrations of 50% and 10% significantly enhanced MPXV infection in HF (2.2- and 2.6-fold increases, respectively) and HK (4.1- and 3.5-fold increases, respectively) (Figure 1(c)). In HF, SF also promoted MPXV infection at 2% (v/v) of SF during virion treatment (2.4-fold increase; Figure 1(b)) and at a low virus dose (MOI 0.002; 3.7-fold increase; Suppl. Fig. 1), indicating that even sub-percent SF concentrations are sufficient to potentiate infection. To exclude the possibility that this effect was specific to a single MPXV strain, we pre-incubated SF with four different MPXV isolates, all belonging to clade IIb and collected during the 2022 outbreak. All isolates displayed increased infectivity after pre-incubation with SF (Figure 1(d)). Taken together, these findings indicate that seminal fluid components broadly enhance MPXV infection in human skin and stromal cells across contemporary outbreak isolates.

Semen-derived amyloid fibrils enhance MPXV infection

Seminal fluid contains protein fragments known to form amyloid fibrils, which have previously been shown to enhance HIV-1 infection [24–26]. We investigated whether such fibrils similarly potentiate MPXV infection. Two well-characterized semen-derived fibrils were tested: SEVI, formed from the prostatic acid phosphatase fragment PAP (248–286) [26], and SEM, formed from the semenogelin-1 fragment SEM1 (86–107) [25]. Immunofluorescence staining, using red fluorescent molecular rotor dye (Proteostat, Protein aggregation assay), confirmed that both peptides self-assembled into amyloid fibrils after overnight agitation (Figure 2(a)), producing structures 10–300 µm² in size (Suppl. Fig. 2). Pre-incubation of MPXV with SEVI (10 µg/ml) or SEM (25 µg/ml) significantly increased infection rates (Figure 2(b)), with dose–response assays revealing that even lower concentrations showed enhancing activity (Figure 2(c)). Critically, non-agitated peptides lacking fibrillar structures failed to augment MPXV infection (Suppl. Fig. 3), demonstrating that amyloid fibril formation is indispensable for the enhancement. Furthermore, cytotoxicity assays in HF cells showed no toxicity of SEVI and SEM at the concentrations used (Suppl. Fig. 4), indicating, that the observed increase in infection is attributable to an enhancing effect of the amyloid fibrils rather than toxicity.

Since the potential transmission routes for MPXV differ between clades with sexual transmission frequently documented for clades IIb and Ib [8], we investigated whether this phenotype may correlate with heightened sensitivity to semen-derived amyloid fibrils. We compared SEVI’s effect on clinical isolates from clades IIb, Ib, and earlier lineages (Figure 2(d)). While all isolates exhibited increased infection in the presence of 10 µg/ml SEVI, only clades IIb and Ib showed significant enhancement at lower concentrations (5 and 2.5 µg/ml, respectively). The similar pattern was observed for SEM (Suppl Figure 5A-C). To evaluate whether this enhancement extends beyond MPXV, we tested the effect of SEVI and SEM on two additional Orthopoxviruses: Cowpox and Camelpox (Suppl Figure 5D-E). Both viruses showed increased infection in the presence of these peptides, suggesting that semen-mediated enhancement may be a broader phenomenon within the Orthopoxvirus genus.

Because transmission efficiency is strongly influenced by infectious dose [26], we assessed SEVI’s effect at low MOIs (2.5 × 10-³, 6.3 × 10−4, and 1.6 × 10−4). SEVI enhanced MPXV infection at all doses, with fold increases of 2.4, 4.0, and 12.7, respectively (Figure 2(e)). These results show that amyloid fibrils can substantially boost infection even at minimal viral loads, potentially facilitating transmission under conditions of limited exposure. To further investigate whether the enhanced infection observed in the presence of SEVI is associated with increased viral production and release of infectious progeny virus, we next quantified extracellular viral titers in supernatants collected from HF cells at 72 h post infection. Supernatants derived from SEVI-enhanced MPXV infections showed increased infectious viral titers compared to MPXV infection alone, indicating that SEVI-mediated enhancement during early stages of infection translates into increased viral output (Suppl. Fig. 6).

SEVI potentiates MPXV infection across epithelial cells, immune cells, and skin tissue

Next, we examined epithelial cell types present at potential entry points during close-contact transmission of MPXV: primary human keratinocytes (HK), nasal epithelial cells (RPMI 2650), and corneal epithelial cells (HCE-T). All three exhibited increased MPXV infection in the presence of SEVI (Figure 3(a)) and no observed toxicity (Suppl. Fig. 7). HCE-T cells, poorly permissive to MPXV (4.6% infection versus 99% in HF, 96.5% in HK, and 33.5% in RPMI 2650; Suppl. Fig. 8), showed a dramatic response: pre-exposure of MPXV to SEVI increased infection from 3.8% to 54%, representing a 14.2-fold enhancement. This was accompanied by a marked increase in the virus induced cytopathic effect (Figure 3(b)).

Given the importance of early immune engagement, we also tested monocytes, which are among the first responders at mucosal and dermal sites and contribute to viral dissemination and immunopathology [27]. SEVI pre-treatment increased the number of MPXV-infected monocytes from two healthy donors at both day 3 and day 5 post-infection (Figure 3(c, d)).

To assess the impact of semen-derived amyloid fibrils in a physiologically relevant skin model, we used ex vivo human skin tissue. Skin grafts were infected with 10⁶ TCID₅₀/mL of MPXV pre-exposed to SEVI, and infection was visualized by immunohistochemical staining for viral antigen after 72 h (Figure 3(e)). SEVI treatment markedly increased the infected area from 6.3–16.1% (Figure 3(f, g)). These findings demonstrate that semen-derived amyloid fibrils can amplify MPXV infection even in complex human tissue, underscoring their potential to enhance viral spread under physiological conditions.

SEVI enhances attachment of MPXV to host cells

Given that pre-treatment of MPXV with SEVI enhances infection, we sought to determine at which stage of the viral life cycle the addition of SEVI exerts a significant effect. To address this, we conducted a time-of-addition experiment with three treatment conditions: MPXV was pre-incubated with SEVI prior to inoculation of the cells (A), SEVI was pre-incubated with cells prior infection (B) or SEVI was added post- infection (C) (Suppl Figure 9). The addition of SEVI after MPXV infection had no impact on number of infected cells, whereas pre-treatment of either cells or virus increased number of infected cells, suggesting that SEVI plays a role during the viral entry.

To determine the specific step in viral entry affected by SEVI, we quantified attachment and internalization of purified mature virions (MVs) using confocal microscopy. MVs are highly stable viral particles utilized by poxviruses for host to host transmission [28]. For detection of MVs, E8 was used as a marker for MPXV particles because it is a conserved MV-surface protein and the MPXV homologue of VACV D8L. VACV D8L has been extensively used for immunofluorescence/confocal microscopy-based visualization and quantification of virion attachment and entry [29], and it contributes to virion adsorption through binding to cell-surface chondroitin sulphate [30]. Thus, E8 immunostaining represents an appropriate approach to detect MPXV particles during early attachment and internalization experiments.

Attachment of MVs was assessed by incubating the virus at 4°C for 2 h (Figure 4(a)), followed by E8 staining under non-permeabilized conditions to detect surface-associated viral particles only (Figure 4(b)). SEVI markedly increased the number of attached MVs per host cell (Figure 4(c)) and the percentage of virus-bound cells (73% E8 + cells in SEVI-pretreated cultures vs. 54% in untreated cultures) (Figure 4(d)). To determine whether increased attachment also translated into enhanced internalization of MPXV particles, infection was synchronized at 4°C for 2 h, followed by incubation at 37°C for 1 h to allow viral uptake (Figure 4(e)). Internalized particles were then evaluated by E8 staining under permeabilized conditions to visualize intracellular virus (Figure 4(f)). Consistently, SEVI also increased MPXV internalization, as reflected by a higher number of intracellular viral particles (Figure 4(g)) and a greater proportion of virus-positive cells (88% vs. 75%) (Figure 4(h)).

To confirm that the signal detected in the internalization assay predominantly represented internalized rather than residual surface-bound virions, we included a trypsin-based stripping step prior to fixation and staining (Figure 4(i)). Trypsin treatment markedly reduced both the number of attached MV particles per cell and the percentage of E8 + cells in the attachment assay (Figure 4(j. k)). In contrast, after internalization, trypsin only marginally reduced the number of detected viral particles and did not affect the number of infected cells (Figure 4(l, m)). Although removal of surface-bound virions was not complete, consistent with previous reports [31], these findings support that the E8 signal detected after the internalization protocol mainly represents internalized MPXV particles. Interestingly, SEVI-pretreated virus was more resistant to trypsin-mediated removal in the attachment assay, potentially suggesting that SEVI stabilizes virus–cell interactions or shields cell-associated virions from enzymatic detachment. This may contribute to enhanced MPXV attachment and subsequent internalization in the presence of SEVI.

To determine whether the SEVI-mediated enhancement of MVs entry can be antagonized, we employed molecular tweezer, previously shown to disrupt pre-formed fibrils of seminal amyloids [21]. SEVI (10 µg/mL) was pre-incubated with CLR01 [21] for 10 min prior to MPXV exposure and assessed by internalization assay (Figure 5(a)). CLR01 significantly reduced both MPXV and SEVI-enhanced MPXV internalization (Figure 5(b, c)) and decreased the number of infected cells (Figure 5(d)). Dose-response analyses confirmed efficient inhibition of SEVI-mediated enhancement as well as MPXV infection at low concentrations (Figure 5(e)).

Efficacy of antiviral therapy against SEVI-enhanced MPXV infection

Two antiviral compounds, tecovirimat and brincidofovir, are currently available for treatment of mpox patients with complications or progressive disease. Tecovirimat is a VP37 inhibitor, that blocks maturation and release of extracellular envelope virions (EVs), while brincidofovir is a nucleoside analogue that prevents viral DNA replication. We investigated whether these compounds retain their antiviral efficacy during SEVI-enhanced MPXV infection.

HF cells were infected with MPXV at MOI of 0.04 with or without pre-incubation with SEVI. In parallel, cells were infected with MPXV at an MOI of 0.4 to mimic increase in the number of infected cells as observed by SEVI treatment. After one hour of infection, cells were washed and supplemented with fresh medium containing serial dilutions of the antiviral compounds. The number of infected cells was quantified 48 h post-infection.

Pre-incubation with SEVI increased the percentage of infected cells to a level comparable to that observed with a tenfold higher viral dose (MOI 0.4) (Figure 6(a, d)). Dose–response analyses revealed that tecovirimat exhibited slightly reduced efficacy under both SEVI pre-treatment and higher-MOI conditions (Figure 6(b, c)). In contrast, brincidofovir maintained consistent antiviral activity against both SEVI-treated and untreated MPXV infections (Figure 6(e, f)).

Discussion

Although mpox is not exclusively sexually transmitted [32], it has been observed during recent mpox outbreaks that transmission has frequently occurred during sexual activity [3,33] and MPXV DNA was often detected in semen for prolonged periods of time [18]. Consistent with this Yinda et al. [34] reported that MPXV remains infectious in different body fluids, especially in semen, for up to 13 days. Previously, 27 different viruses have been identified in semen [35], though not all are sexually transmissible. For example, high viral loads of Zika virus (ZIKV) are present in semen of infected individuals [36], however semen markedly suppresses ZIKV and other flavivirus infections [37] and sexual transmission is not commonly observed [38]. In contrast, seminal fluid enhances HIV-1 infection of cell types relevant to sexual transmission with several orders of magnitude [24] possibly explaining why HIV-1 is predominantly transmitted through sexual contact. These findings highlight the importance of understanding the biological role of semen in either facilitating or limiting viral infection. Here we provide evidence that components of seminal fluid (SF) increase MPXV infection in primary skin cells and ex vivo skin tissue and provide a mechanistic basis for how semen may promote MPXV infectivity and contribute to viral transmission of MPXV during sexual contact.

We demonstrate that SF from different donors increases infection in both primary fibroblasts and keratinocytes. Additionally, SF mediated infection of keratinocytes, the main MPXV-target cell type in skin [39,40], at very low virus concentrations. SF has been previously shown to enhance HIV-1 [24], HSV-2 [41] and HCMV infection [42]. This enhancing activity has been correlated with presence of amyloid fibrils derived from seminal proteins, which naturally occur at high concentrations in healthy semen [24,43]. We tested two such amyloid fibrils, SEVI and SEM, and both markedly enhanced MPXV infection in primary cells. Semen-derived fibrils not only amplified infection in ex vivo skin tissue but also augmented MPXV infection in monocytes, key early responders at mucosal and dermal sites that contribute to systemic viral dissemination [44]. Importantly, SEVI pre-exposure enabled a 14-fold increase in infection of corneal epithelial cells, which are poorly permissive, effectively broadening MPXV cell tropism to susceptible cells that are rarely infected.

Understanding the mode of human-to-human transmission substantially differs across MPXV clades and remains poor for some of them. Sexual activity as a main transmission route of clades IIb and Ib is supported by their high prevalence within sexual networks, skin lesions mainly localized in anogenital areas and often presence of concurrent sexually transmitted infections (17-29%) [3, 14, 19]. In contrast, zoonotic spillover is suggested as the main transmission route of clade Ia and IIa due to absence of genetic signatures of prolonged human-to-human transmission and low secondary attacks observed in outbreaks [45–47]. Whether these differences are driven by virus biology or reflect the networks within which transmission took place remains unclear. Our results indicate that infection by all MPXV clades is enhanced by SEVI and SEM fibrils with slightly higher enhancement observed for clade IIb and Ib. Though MPXV may not have evolved specifically to exploit semen, those capable of leveraging fibrils are likely to gain a transmission advantage. This mechanism may partly explain why sexual exposure, particularly at anal or genital mucosal sites, leads to higher attack rates than nonsexual skin-to-skin contact [48], and why mpox outbreaks disproportionately affect populations with dense sexual networks [49]. Furthermore, cases of clade Ia transmission linked to sexual activity have been described in current outbreak in highly populated area of DRC [50], indicating that sexual transmission may extend beyond clade IIb and Ib.

Consistent with MPXV findings, semen-derived fibrils enhanced infection of other orthopoxviruses, including cowpox and camelpox, suggesting that semen-mediated potentiation may be a conserved phenomenon within poxviruses. Interestingly, molluscum contagiosum, a human-restricted poxvirus, spreads through close contact, often involving sexual activity and lesions in the genital area [48–52]. Together, these parallels suggest that semen-mediated enhancement may extend beyond mpox and could represent a broader, underrecognized factor in the transmission biology of human poxviruses.

Poxviruses produce two major infectious forms: the mature virion (MV), which possesses a single lipid membrane, and the extracellular virion (EV), which is surrounded by an additional outer membrane [53]. EVs are primarily responsible for viral spread within a host, whereas MVs are the main form transmitted between hosts [54]. Our findings revealed that pre-exposure of purified MVs to SEVI increased both number of viral particles attached to host cells leading to higher number of internalized viral particles as well as number of infected cells. This aligns with prior work showing that semen-derived amyloid fibrils are positively charged, allowing them to bridge negatively charged viral membranes and negatively charged host cell surfaces, thereby enhancing viral attachment and entry [55–57]. It has been postulated that accessibility of the viral lipid membrane to large factors such as seminal fibrils might be a determinant of the fibril-mediated enhancement [38]. The MV membrane originates from the endoplasmic reticulum and is rich in anionic phospholipids, including phosphatidylserine, which contributes to the negative surface charge of the virion [58,59]. The membrane also incorporates ∼25 viral membrane proteins, including receptor-binding proteins [60] that form irregular protrusions on the viral surface [61]. This non-periodic arrangement likely leaves accessible patches of exposed lipid membrane, creating physical contact zones where positively charged seminal fibrils can engage and stabilize virion–cell interactions.

Crucially, freshly dissolved PAP(248-286) and SEM peptide monomers do not promote infection. Furthermore, fibril-disrupting agents such as CLR01 abolished SEVI’s effect, confirming that fibril presence is necessary for promoting MPXV infection. Notably, CLR01 inhibited MPXV infection even in the absence of amyloid fibrils. In addition to its anti-amyloid activity, CLR01 can also directly disrupt the lipid membranes of broad-range of enveloped viruses, such as HIV-1, HSV-2, ZIKV, IAV, Measles, Ebola virus, and SARS-CoV-2, highlighting its potential as a broad-spectrum antiviral agent [21,22,62]. Since MPXV has been reported to be co-transmitted together with other sexually transmissible viruses, such as HSV-2 and HIV-1, fibril-targeting agents could serve as adjunctive therapeutics for groups at risk, not only by neutralizing the pro-viral effects of semen-derived amyloid fibrils, but also by directly disrupting viral envelopes and limiting infectivity, thereby providing a dual mechanism of antiviral action that could reduce sexual transmission risk.

The clinically approved antivirals brincidofovir and tecovirimat retained efficacy against SEVI-enhanced MPXV infection, with only minor shifts in IC₅₀ values when adjusted to increased viral burden. This contrasts with HIV-1, where reverse transcription- and integration-targeting drugs show reduced effectiveness under semen-/SEVI-enhanced conditions [63], however the underlying mechanism is not fully understood.

This study highlights important insights while acknowledging some limitations. Using primary cells and ex vivo tissues, we provide a mechanistic foundation for understanding the role of semen in MPXV transmission, though further in vivo studies are needed. Semen is a complex fluid, and future work dissecting the contributions of fibrils and other components could reveal additional pathways influencing MPXV infection. Similarly, investigating the relevance of fibril-mediated enhancement to other poxviruses offers an opportunity to expand our understanding of orthopoxvirus biology and inform targeted public health strategies. Additionally, it remains unclear to what extent other bodily fluids contribute to MPXV transmission during sexual activity, and addressing this question will be important for a comprehensive understanding of viral spread.

Collectively, our results suggest that semen is not simply a passive carrier of MPXV but semen-derived fibrils may both increase the efficiency of viral transmission and enhance infection in hardly susceptible cells. Additionally, the molecular tweezers CLR01, which prevent both enhanced and non-enhanced infection, may represent potential antiviral candidates for MPXV infection.
