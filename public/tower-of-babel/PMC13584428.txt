Human microbiome alterations in Epstein–Barr Virus infection: a systematic review
Authors: Arghavan Zebardast, Kasra Javadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpstein–Barr virus (EBV) is associated with several malignancies and immune-mediated conditions, but its relationship with human microbial communities remains incompletely understood. We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and Scopus for human studies evaluating EBV-associated alterations in the microbiome. Because of substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, findings were synthesized narratively, and study quality was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool. Nine observational studies published between 2017 and 2025 were included, covering the oral cavity, nasopharynx, gut, gastric tissue, and subgingival plaque. EBV positivity or EBV-related clinical status was associated with niche-specific microbial shifts, including altered gut bacterial profiles, distinct microbial patterns in EBV-associated gastric cancer tissue, and enrichment of oral-associated pathobionts in nasopharyngeal carcinoma compartments. Alpha- and beta-diversity findings were inconsistent across studies. Overall, the evidence suggests context-dependent alterations in the microbiome in EBV-positive or EBV-related disease settings. However, these findings should be interpreted as EBV-associated rather than EBV-specific, particularly when EBV status overlaps with malignancy. The small observational evidence base, heterogeneous EBV-status definitions, methodological variability, and residual confounding limit causal inference. Background

The human microbiome, the assemblage of bacteria, viruses, fungi, and other microbes inhabiting the body, is now recognized as integral to human physiology [1]. These commensal communities (in the gut, oral cavity, respiratory tract, skin, etc.) perform essential functions in nutrition, barrier protection, and immune system development. Indeed, the gut microbiota has been called an essential organ for its metabolic, enzymatic, and immune roles. Perturbations in microbiome composition, known as dysbiosis, have been associated with various diseases, including allergic, inflammatory, metabolic, neuropsychiatric, and neoplastic conditions [1–3]. Importantly, recent advances highlight the human virome, the viral component of the microbiome, including bacteriophages, as a fundamental, yet underappreciated, part of this ecosystem [4]. Viruses present in mucosal tissues and the gut can influence bacterial community structure and interact directly with host cells, indicating that virus-microbiome-host interactions are crucial for understanding health and disease [5].

The Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), also known as human herpesvirus 4 (HHV-4), is a common virus that infects B lymphocytes and epithelial cells, establishing lifelong latency in the host. After the primary infection, which often occurs during childhood, EBV persists in memory B cells and can reactivate periodically via the oropharynx [6]. By the time they reach adulthood, approximately 90–95% of people worldwide are seropositive for EBV [7]. In most individuals, primary EBV infection is subclinical or causes infectious mononucleosis, but EBV is also a potent oncogenic virus. EBV is etiologically linked to a spectrum of malignancies of lymphoid and epithelial origin, including nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), endemic Burkitt lymphoma, a subset of Hodgkin lymphoma, and a subset of gastric carcinomas. These pathogenic roles reflect EBV’s ability to modulate cell growth and to evade immune responses [8, 9]. EBV-infected cells express viral genes that block apoptosis and interfere with immune recognition, so that a lifelong carrier state can predispose to disease under cofactors such as immunodeficiency or co-infection [9, 10].

Given the widespread presence and potential harmful effects of EBV, the relationship between EBV infection and the commensal microbiome is attracting increasing attention. Both experimental and clinical studies indicate that these interactions may have bidirectional effects. For instance, exposure to EBV has been shown to change gut microbial communities. In a study using a tree shrew model, acute EBV infection led to significant shifts in gut bacterial composition and changes in metabolite profiles [11]. In a mouse model of arthritis, EBV DNA administration altered gut microbiome alpha-diversity, increasing Chao1 richness while decreasing Shannon diversity, thereby exacerbating inflammation [12].

In the human oropharyngeal niche, EBV coexists with unique microbial communities. Recent analyses of patients with EBV-positive NPC have shown a distinct oral microbiota characterized by reduced overall diversity and specific shifts in microbial taxa compared with healthy individuals. Notably, Streptococcus sanguinis, which is enriched in patients with EBV-positive NPC, was found to promote the lytic reactivation of EBV in vitro by producing hydrogen peroxide [13].

Research has shown that commensal bacteria can play a significant role in influencing the latent-lytic switch of EBV and the immune response to it. The commensal microbiota can also modulate the host’s antiviral immunity. Recent reviews have highlighted that interactions between the microbiome and the host, such as co-infections or microbial metabolites, can regulate immune responses that affect both EBV persistence and reactivation. This creates a complex relationship in which EBV can alter microbial communities across various areas, including the oral cavity, gut, and nasopharynx. At the same time, the resident microbiota can impact EBV’s behavior through immune and metabolic pathways [5, 14].

Despite these insights, the full extent of crosstalk between EBV and the microbiome remains unclear. To date, most investigations have been limited and fragmented, focusing primarily on single-analyte studies, such as those examining gut or oral microbiomes, or model-based research. Comprehensive, multi-niche and longitudinal human studies, and systematic syntheses integrating these compartments, remain limited [11]. Likewise, while EBV is a key pathogen in the nasopharynx and salivary tract, systematic profiling of EBV-related dysbiosis in these sites remains limited. In summary, there is a clear knowledge gap regarding how latent or active EBV infection affects microbiome structure, diversity, and function across human body sites.

Materials and methods

Data sources and search strategy

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement [15]. No protocol for this systematic review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO or any other systematic review registry. However, the eligibility criteria, search strategy, screening process, data extraction approach, and risk-of-bias assessment were defined before study selection and are reported transparently in the Methods section.

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, and Scopus from database inception to 9 December 2025 to identify human studies investigating microbiome alterations in the context of EBV infection or EBV-related clinical status [16]. The search strategy was designed to maximize sensitivity by combining two core concept blocks: [1] EBV-related terms, including “Epstein–Barr virus,” EBV, human herpesvirus 4, and HHV-4; and [2] microbiome-related terms, including microbiome, microbiota, dysbiosis, microbial community, bacterial diversity, and site-specific terms such as gut, oral, and intestinal microbiome. Where available, controlled vocabulary terms, such as MeSH in PubMed and Emtree in Embase, were combined with free-text keywords searched in titles and abstracts. The complete database-specific search strategies, including the exact syntax used for PubMed, Embase, and Scopus, are provided in Supplementary Table S1 to ensure reproducibility.

All records retrieved from PubMed, Embase, and Scopus were imported into EndNote v21.5 and merged into a single reference library. Duplicate records were first identified using EndNote’s automated duplicate-detection function based on matching bibliographic fields, including DOI, title, author names, journal, and publication year. The automatically identified duplicates were then carefully reviewed by the reviewers, and confirmed duplicates were removed before title and abstract screening.

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts and subsequently assessed the full texts of potentially eligible articles according to the prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria; disagreements were resolved by consensus [17]. During title and abstract screening, records were excluded only when they did not meet one or more prespecified eligibility domains, including human study design, relevance to EBV infection or EBV status, availability of microbiome-related outcomes, eligible article type, English-language publication, and the presence of an EBV-negative comparator group or stratified analysis. No automated or AI-assisted screening tool was used in the present review; all screening and eligibility decisions were made independently by the two reviewers, with disagreements resolved by consensus.

In addition, the reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles were manually screened to identify any additional eligible publications that the database searches may have missed; however, this manual screening did not yield any additional eligible studies.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they involved human populations of any age or gender with EBV infection, encompassing both primary infections and reactivations. Eligible studies had to assess EBV-related changes in the microbiome, focusing on alterations in microbial diversity, composition, or function across one or more body sites (e.g., the gut, oral cavity, nasopharynx). Only observational studies, clinical trials, or interventional studies were considered, provided they reported outcomes that correlated EBV status with changes in the microbiome and included an EBV-negative comparator group or stratified analysis. Exclusion criteria included animal studies, review articles, studies lacking microbiome data, and studies not published in English.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized form [17]. The following information was extracted from each included study: study characteristics (author, year of publication, study design, sample size, population details), microbiome analysis methods (e.g., 16S rRNA sequencing, Shotgun metagenomic sequencing, and DNA–DNA hybridization), body site(s) sampled (gut, oral, nasopharyngeal, etc.), and key microbiome outcomes (diversity indices, taxonomic composition). Any disagreements during data extraction were resolved through discussion between the two authors. Risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool for non-randomised studies [18].

Data synthesis and analysis

Data were synthesized narratively because of substantial heterogeneity across the included studies in terms of study design, clinical population, anatomical niche, sample type, EBV-status definition, microbiome-profiling method, and reported outcome measures. Quantitative meta-analysis was not performed because the studies differed markedly in their populations, microbiome methods, diversity metrics, taxonomic resolution, and comparator groups.

For studies reporting microbial diversity, alpha-diversity and beta-diversity findings were extracted and summarized qualitatively. Alpha-diversity outcomes included measures of richness and evenness, whereas beta-diversity outcomes included ordination-based or distance-based comparisons of microbial community structure. Taxonomic alterations were grouped by anatomical niche, including gut, oral cavity, nasopharynx, gastric tissue, and subgingival plaque, and key microbial taxa associated with EBV positivity or EBV-related clinical status were summarized descriptively. Functional or metabolomic microbiome findings, where available, were discussed in relation to EBV status, inflammatory context, or disease phenotype.

During synthesis, the method used to define EBV positivity was considered qualitatively because EBV status was operationalized differently across studies. Definitions included serological markers, detection of circulating or local EBV DNA, salivary viral shedding, tissue-based viral detection, and EBV-associated tumor classification. These categories were not treated as biologically equivalent; instead, findings were interpreted in relation to each study’s clinical and virological context.

The synthesis, therefore, focused on identifying recurring patterns, context-specific findings, methodological limitations, and knowledge gaps rather than generating pooled effect estimates.

Results

Study selection

The database search identified 1,345 records from PubMed, Embase, and Scopus. After removing 493 duplicate records, 852 unique records remained for title and abstract screening. At this stage, 836 records were excluded because they did not meet the prespecified eligibility criteria based on title and/or abstract review. These exclusions were made independently by two reviewers, based on the screening domains described in the Methods section.

The remaining 16 reports were retrieved for full-text assessment. Following full-text review, seven reports were excluded for the following reasons: duplicate publication (n = 3), lack of relevant microbiome outcomes (n = 2), or absence of an appropriate comparator group (n = 2). Manual screening of the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews did not identify any additional eligible studies. Ultimately, nine studies met the predefined eligibility criteria and were included in the systematic review (Fig. 1).

Quality of studies

Risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool and is summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Overall, the evidence base showed predominantly moderate risk of bias. At the same time, one small pilot study was judged to have a serious overall risk of bias, mainly due to concerns regarding confounding and participant selection.

Across the included studies, confounding and participant selection were the most common sources of bias. These concerns were consistent with the observational nature of the evidence base, in which important covariates were not uniformly measured or controlled. Potential confounders varied by study population and clinical context and included demographic factors such as age and sex, dietary habits, recent exposure to antibiotics or other microbiota-modulating agents, immune status, HIV infection, transplant-related immunosuppression, cancer-related clinical characteristics, oral hygiene or periodontal inflammatory status, and co-infections with other viruses or bacteria, where applicable. Participant-selection concerns mainly reflected small sample sizes, convenience or highly selected clinical populations, disease-specific comparator groups, and heterogeneity in sampling sites and clinical conditions.

Other ROBINS-I domains were generally judged to be at lower risk. Classification of EBV exposure and missing data was mostly rated as low risk, reflecting the use of laboratory-based EBV assays and largely complete sampling or analysis pipelines. Deviations from intended exposure were also generally rated as low risk, with one study rated as moderate. Measurement of microbiome outcomes was usually rated as low risk. However, a minority of studies were rated as moderate, mainly because of differences in sample type, profiling method, or outcome assessment. Selection of reported results was commonly rated as moderate risk, indicating potential vulnerability to analytic flexibility or selective reporting. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating.

Main study characteristics

Nine observational studies published between 2017 and 2025 were included, collectively covering a broad spectrum of EBV-related clinical contexts and anatomical niches [13, 19–26]. The evidence base was heterogeneous in both design and population. Most studies used case–control or cross-sectional designs, while one study used a repeated-measures longitudinal design in astronauts. The included populations comprised pediatric cohorts, adult clinical cohorts, immunocompromised participants, patients with EBV-related malignancies, individuals with periodontal disease, and astronauts evaluated for viral reactivation during spaceflight. Because the astronaut study was conducted under unique environmental and physiological conditions, its findings were interpreted separately from terrestrial clinical cohorts.

Microbiome sampling covered multiple anatomical compartments, including the gastrointestinal tract, oral cavity, nasopharynx, periodontal niche, and tumor-associated tissues. Sample types included feces, intestinal mucosal biopsies, saliva or oral samples, nasopharyngeal swabs or brushings, subgingival plaque, gastric tumor tissue, and NPC-associated tissue samples. Most studies used 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing to characterize bacterial community structure. However, selected studies used other approaches, including shotgun metagenomic sequencing of fecal samples after pediatric liver transplantation, RNA-seq–derived microbial profiling in gastric tumor tissue, and checkerboard DNA–DNA hybridization in subgingival samples. Tumor tissue and mucosal biopsy specimens were considered relatively low in microbial biomass compared with fecal, saliva, or plaque samples; therefore, microbial signals from these specimens were interpreted cautiously due to possible susceptibility to reagent contamination, host-DNA background, sequencing artifacts, and taxonomic misclassification.

EBV status was defined heterogeneously across the included studies and likely reflected different biological states. Some studies used serological markers and/or circulating EBV DNA to identify acute or recent EBV infection or systemic viral burden, particularly in pediatric infectious mononucleosis, EBV-associated liver dysfunction, and post-transplant settings [19, 20]. Other studies used local EBV DNA detection, such as salivary EBV shedding during spaceflight or EBV DNA in intestinal mucosal biopsies, which may reflect local viral presence, shedding, or reactivation rather than systemic infection alone [25, 26]. In cancer-related studies, EBV status was defined using tumor-based assays, including EBER-ISH, viral transcripts, or qPCR/pathology-based tumor classification, reflecting EBV-associated malignant disease contexts rather than uncomplicated EBV infection [21–23]. In periodontal samples, EBV was detected locally by PCR in subgingival plaque [24]. Therefore, throughout this review, “EBV positivity” should be understood as an operational umbrella term rather than a single uniform biological state.

Collectively, the included studies were designed to identify EBV-associated dysbiosis signatures, characterize compartment-specific microbial shifts, and explore associations between microbial features and EBV burden, immune or inflammatory indices, or clinical phenotypes. Changes in human microbial composition in EBV infection

Across the included studies, EBV positivity or EBV-related clinical status was associated with niche-specific shifts in microbial composition. However, in disease-based settings such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma and EBV-associated gastric cancer, these shifts should be interpreted as EBV-associated or disease-context-associated rather than EBV-specific effects.

In gut microbiome studies, EBV positivity was generally associated with relative expansion of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria and reduced abundance of selected commensal taxa. In pediatric liver-transplant recipients, EBV-positive patients showed higher relative abundance of Firmicutes and Lactobacillus and lower abundance of Clostridium compared with EBV-negative controls [19]. Similarly, in EBV-associated gastrointestinal disease, several genera, including Granulicatella, Enterococcus, Atopobium, Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas, and Blautia, were enriched, whereas Prevotella, Sutterella, Collinsella, Shigella, Helicobacter, and Desulfovibrio were depleted [20]. In a pilot study of EBV-associated gastric adenocarcinoma, Bacteroides and members of the Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae families were enriched, whereas Prevotellaceae, including Prevotella_9, was markedly reduced in EBV-positive compared with EBV-negative patients [21]. Overall, gut studies suggested EBV-associated microbial patterns characterized by enrichment of selected Firmicutes or Proteobacteria and depletion of some commensal anaerobes, particularly Prevotella-related taxa [19–21].

In gastric tissue, EBV-positive gastric cancers showed distinct taxonomic differences [22]. However, these findings require cautious interpretation because they were derived from tumor tissue, a relatively low-microbial-biomass sample type, using tissue-based RNA-seq/metatranscriptomic microbial profiling. Differential abundance analysis indicated that only a limited subset of bacterial genera differed between EBV-positive and EBV-negative individuals. After review of the taxonomic assignments, Choristoneura was excluded from the bacterial microbiome summary because it represents a non-bacterial, eukaryotic classification and may reflect taxonomic misclassification or an artifact of the underlying dataset. Accordingly, EBV-positive gastric tissue was summarized as enriched in Bartonella, whereas Citrobacter and Acidithiobacillus were more abundant in EBV-negative tumors [22]. These findings should not be interpreted as broad bacterial enrichment in EBV-positive gastric tissue, and the observed differences may also reflect tumor biology, the gastric tumor microenvironment, inflammation, surgical or clinical exposures, or other cancer-related factors rather than EBV status alone.

In nasopharyngeal carcinoma tissue, EBV-positive cases showed enrichment of oral-associated anaerobic taxa, including Fusobacterium nucleatum, Prevotella intermedia, and Peptostreptococcus stomatis [23]. In contrast, commensal taxa such as Corynebacterium accolens, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Cutibacterium acnes were depleted. These findings suggest enrichment of oral-associated taxa, including members of Prevotellaceae, Fusobacteriaceae, and Peptostreptococcaceae, in nasopharyngeal samples from NPC patients compared with controls. However, because the available evidence does not establish strain-level relatedness or source tracking, these findings should be interpreted as compositional similarity or possible microbial sharing rather than confirmed migration between oral and nasopharyngeal niches. In addition, because EBV status is closely intertwined with the malignant disease context in NPC, the observed microbial patterns may reflect EBV-related tumor biology, local inflammation, altered tissue microenvironment, or disease-associated dysbiosis rather than an isolated effect of EBV infection.

In the oral cavity of NPC patients, Liao et al. reported EBV-related microbial differences [13]. Streptococcus sanguinis was significantly enriched in NPC cases, and other enriched genera included Lautropia, Oribacterium, Capnocytophaga, Actinomyces, and Peptostreptococcus. Several commensal taxa, including Leptotrichia, were reduced in abundance. Notably, S. sanguinis abundance correlated with EBV serological markers, suggesting an association between this oral taxon and EBV-related viral activity; however, this finding should be interpreted as associative rather than evidence of in vivo causality.

In subgingival plaque, Passariello et al. reported that EBV detection was associated with shifts in periodontopathogenic microbial communities [24]. Sites coinfected with EBV showed a higher prevalence of Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and a lower prevalence of Porphyromonas gingivalis, Tannerella forsythia, and Fusobacterium periodonticum compared with EBV-negative sites. Thus, EBV-positive periodontal lesions were characterized by enrichment of A. actinomycetemcomitans and depletion of selected periodontal taxa, including P. gingivalis, T. forsythia, and F. periodonticum. Microbial diversity in EBV infection

Microbial diversity findings were evaluated by separating within-sample diversity, referred to as alpha diversity, from between-sample community differences, referred to as beta diversity. Alpha-diversity metrics included measures of richness and evenness, such as Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, and observed OTUs. Beta-diversity findings were based on ordination or distance-based approaches, including UniFrac, Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, principal coordinates analysis, PERMANOVA, and Adonis tests. Overall, diversity findings varied substantially across studies, anatomical niches, clinical contexts, and analytical methods.

Alpha diversity

Alpha-diversity findings were inconsistent across the included studies. In gastric tissue, Carneiro et al. reported that EBV-positive gastric cancer cases had a significantly higher Shannon index than EBV-negative cases (p = 0.038) [22]. In contrast, the Simpson index indicated lower bacterial diversity in EBV-positive cases (p = 0.024). The microbiota of EBV-positive tumors was dominated by a limited number of Enterobacterales genera, including Shigella, Klebsiella, and Salmonella, without evidence of broad enrichment across Enterobacteriaceae.

In gut and intestinal studies, alpha-diversity results also differed by clinical context. Zhan et al. reported that Chao1 and Simpson indices were significantly higher in children with EBV-associated liver dysfunction than in those with infectious mononucleosis (p < 0.05), suggesting increased richness and diversity in the EBV-associated liver dysfunction group [20]. In contrast, Wang et al. found no significant differences in Shannon or Simpson diversity between EBV-positive and EBV-negative pediatric liver-transplant recipients [19]. Similarly, Gianella et al. reported no difference in observed OTU richness by EBV detection status in intestinal mucosal biopsies [25].

In the oral cavity, Liao et al. found that patients with NPC, all of whom were EBV-positive, had significantly lower oral alpha diversity than control subjects [13]. Faith’s phylogenetic diversity and Shannon diversity were both reduced in NPC patients (P = 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ and P = 5.5 × 10⁻⁵, respectively). By contrast, Urbaniak et al. reported no significant difference in salivary Shannon diversity according to EBV shedding status in astronauts (P = 0.35), although microbiome richness, defined as the number of detected taxa, was positively correlated with EBV viral load (P = 0.005) [26]. Because this study was conducted during spaceflight, its findings should be interpreted separately from terrestrial clinical cohorts. In the nasopharyngeal study by Liao et al., alpha diversity was not specifically compared by EBV status; the authors reported only that nasopharyngeal samples had lower diversity than oral samples [23].

Beta diversity

Beta-diversity findings were similarly context-dependent. In gastric cancer tissue, Carneiro et al. found no significant overall separation between EBV-positive and EBV-negative gastric cancer microbiomes using Bray–Curtis PERMANOVA (p = 0.141), indicating no statistically significant global compositional difference between the two groups [22].

In gut and intestinal studies, Zhan et al. reported visual separation of EBV-positive groups, including infectious mononucleosis and EBV-associated liver dysfunction, from EBV-negative groups in unweighted UniFrac principal coordinates analysis [20]. Wang et al. reported significant differences in gut microbiome composition by EBV status but did not provide formal beta-diversity metrics [19]. In contrast, Gianella et al. found no EBV-associated clustering in intestinal mucosal samples; community separation was observed by HIV status rather than EBV detection, and no significant beta-diversity difference was attributed to EBV [25].

In oral and nasopharyngeal compartments, beta-diversity differences were more consistently reported. Liao et al. found significant differences in oral microbiota composition between NPC patients and control subjects using both unweighted and weighted UniFrac Adonis tests [13]. However, the effect sizes were modest (R² ≈ 0.014–0.019, p = 0.001). Urbaniak et al. did not report formal beta-diversity analyses according to EBV shedding status in saliva [26]. In the nasopharyngeal study by Liao et al., Bray–Curtis PERMANOVA showed a significant compositional difference between NPC patients and controls (R² = 0.029, p = 0.001) [23].

Taken together, the available studies suggest that EBV positivity or EBV-related clinical status may be associated with changes in alpha and/or beta diversity in selected contexts, but the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of these findings varied by niche, disease setting, sample type, and analytical approach. Therefore, microbial diversity findings should be interpreted as context-dependent rather than as evidence of a uniform EBV-associated diversity signature. Discussion

This systematic review indicates that EBV positivity or EBV-related clinical status is associated with niche-specific alterations in the microbiome rather than a single, uniform microbial signature. Across the included studies, the recurring pattern was not the consistent enrichment of a single bacterial group across all compartments, but rather context-dependent disruption of local microbial ecology. In several settings, EBV-positive or EBV-related disease states were accompanied by enrichment of opportunistic, inflammation-associated, or oral-associated taxa and depletion of selected commensal anaerobes. However, the biological meaning of these patterns varied substantially across anatomical niches, host conditions, and disease contexts [13, 19–26].

The cancer-associated studies require particularly cautious interpretation. In nasopharyngeal carcinoma and EBV-associated gastric cancer, microbial profiles may reflect the tumor microenvironment, local inflammation, altered epithelial barriers, sampling site, and disease-related clinical exposures in addition to EBV status itself. Therefore, these microbial signatures are best interpreted as EBV-associated or disease-context-associated rather than EBV-specific [21–23].

Findings from non-malignant and immunologically distinct settings were more variable. Studies involving pediatric liver transplantation, EBV-associated liver dysfunction, HIV-associated intestinal biopsy cohorts, periodontal disease, and spaceflight-related viral reactivation differed substantially in their clinical context and sampling niche. This variability suggests that EBV-related microbial alterations are shaped by host immune status, local mucosal environment, and disease context rather than by EBV positivity alone [19, 20, 24–26].

Alpha- and beta-diversity findings were also inconsistent across studies. Reduced diversity or significant community separation was observed in some oral and nasopharyngeal comparisons, whereas gut, intestinal, and gastric findings were less uniform. These results are best interpreted as context-dependent indicators of community perturbation rather than reproducible biomarkers of EBV status across all body sites [13, 19–26].

The biological plausibility of EBV–microbiome interactions is supported by known links among EBV biology, mucosal immunity, microbial metabolism, and inflammatory signaling. EBV can influence the host immune environment through latent proteins and non-coding regulatory molecules, whereas commensal and pathobiont-derived signals may shape antiviral immune responses and mucosal inflammatory tone [10, 14, 27–29]. However, the human evidence summarized in this review remains largely associative. Experimental observations involving oral taxa such as S. sanguinis and selected anaerobes provide mechanistic hypotheses for microbiome–EBV crosstalk, but they do not establish that these bacteria drive EBV activity in vivo [13, 14]. Similarly, correlations between microbial richness and EBV shedding, or between specific gut taxa and inflammatory markers, should be interpreted as signals of possible interaction rather than proof of directionality or causality [20, 26].

These findings may have translational relevance, particularly for identifying exploratory microbial signatures associated with EBV-positive or EBV-related disease contexts. However, their clinical utility remains uncertain because the available studies were small, heterogeneous, and mostly observational. At present, microbiome profiles should be considered exploratory biomarkers rather than validated diagnostic, prognostic, or therapeutic targets. Future translational work should prioritize longitudinal cohorts, paired host–microbiome–virome analyses, EBV transcriptional profiling, metabolomics, and independent validation before microbiome-based prediction or intervention strategies can be proposed with confidence [13, 19–26, 30].

Limitations

These findings should be interpreted in light of substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity. The included studies differed in population characteristics, disease contexts, anatomical niches, sample types, EBV-status definitions, microbiome-profiling methods, sequencing depth, bioinformatic pipelines, taxonomic resolution, and diversity metrics. Most studies were small observational case–control or cross-sectional studies; therefore, causality and directionality cannot be inferred.

A major limitation is the heterogeneity in EBV-status definitions. EBV positivity was assessed using various approaches, including serological markers, circulating EBV DNA, salivary viral shedding, tissue-based EBV DNA or transcript detection, local PCR positivity, and EBV-associated tumor classification. These approaches may represent distinct biological states, including acute or recent infection, local viral persistence, viral shedding or reactivation, latent tumor-associated EBV, or EBV-related malignant disease contexts. Because EBV transcriptional activity and latent versus lytic markers were not consistently assessed, microbiome findings could not be stratified by EBV phase.

Several sample types require cautious interpretation. Tumor tissue and mucosal biopsy specimens may contain relatively low microbial biomass compared with fecal, saliva, or plaque samples, making them more susceptible to reagent contamination, sequencing artifacts, host-DNA background, and taxonomic misclassification, particularly when negative controls or independent validation are not clearly reported [31, 32]. Accordingly, non-bacterial or low-confidence assignments, such as Choristoneura, were not interpreted as EBV-associated bacterial microbiome signals.

Methodological constraints also limit inference. Most included microbiome studies relied on 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which provides limited taxonomic resolution and cannot confirm strain-level relatedness or directional microbial migration between anatomical niches. Therefore, findings suggesting oral–nasopharyngeal microbial sharing should be interpreted as compositional similarity or association rather than confirmed translocation.

Residual confounding remains an important concern. Factors such as age, sex, diet, antibiotic or microbiota-modulating medication exposure, immune status, HIV infection, transplant-related immunosuppression, cancer-related clinical characteristics, oral or periodontal status, co-infections, and sampling niche were variably controlled across studies. In cancer-based studies, particularly nasopharyngeal carcinoma and EBV-associated gastric cancer, microbiome differences may reflect tumor microenvironment, inflammation, treatment, or clinical exposures, or disease-associated dysbiosis rather than EBV status alone. In addition, the spaceflight study was interpreted separately because spaceflight-related physiological and environmental exposures may independently influence herpesvirus reactivation and salivary microbial composition.

Finally, only English-language publications were included; the review protocol was not prospectively registered, virome profiling and EBV transcriptional data were limited, and many analyses were exploratory. Therefore, the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger, longitudinal, standardized, multi-omic cohorts.

Future directions

Future studies should prioritize well-powered longitudinal cohorts with harmonized sampling across relevant anatomical niches to clarify temporal relationships between EBV status and microbiome changes. Integrated multi-omic designs combining shotgun metagenomics, virome analysis, metabolomics, EBV transcriptional profiling, and host immune or cytokine measurements will be essential to determine whether observed microbial signatures reflect EBV activity, disease context, inflammation, or shared environmental and clinical exposures.

Methodological standardization is also needed. Future studies should use consistent sample-collection protocols, adequate sequencing depth, negative-control reporting, standardized bioinformatic pipelines, and prespecified alpha- and beta-diversity metrics. Strain-resolved metagenomic and source-tracking approaches using paired samples from multiple body sites will be required to distinguish true microbial migration from compositional similarity between niches. Future systematic reviews may also benefit from validated AI-assisted or machine-learning tools to prioritize records or audit a subset of exclusions, while retaining independent human reviewer oversight for final eligibility decisions.

Overall, future work should move beyond descriptive taxonomic comparisons toward mechanistic and clinically validated models that account for EBV infection state, host immune status, cancer or transplant context, co-infections, medication exposure, and other confounders. Until such evidence is available, EBV-associated microbiome signatures should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than established biomarkers or therapeutic targets.

Conclusions

This systematic review suggests that EBV positivity or EBV-related clinical status is associated with niche-specific alterations in bacterial community composition across the gut, oral cavity, nasopharynx, gastric tissue, and subgingival plaque. Reported patterns included enrichment of selected Firmicutes and Proteobacteria with depletion of some commensal anaerobes in gut-related studies, as well as distinct microbial profiles in EBV-associated gastric cancer and nasopharyngeal carcinoma contexts. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because EBV status was often closely linked to the underlying disease state, particularly in cancer-based studies. Therefore, the observed microbial changes are best considered EBV-associated or disease-context-associated signatures rather than EBV-specific effects.

Overall, the current evidence remains hypothesis-generating. The included studies were few in number, mostly observational, heterogeneous in sampling and analytical methods, and affected by residual confounding, with a predominantly moderate risk of bias. Although EBV-associated microbiome signatures may have future biomarker or mechanistic relevance, stronger inference will require well-powered longitudinal studies using standardized sampling, harmonized microbiome analysis, clear EBV-status definitions, EBV transcriptional profiling, and integrated multi-omic approaches to clarify directionality, biological mechanisms, and clinical significance.
