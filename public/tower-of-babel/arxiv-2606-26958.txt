Spectral expansion of LQG heat trace and KPZ scaling
Nathanaël Berestycki, Jakob Klein

Abstract
Let h h be a whole plane Gaussian free field, and let Ω \Omega be a bounded domain in two dimensions. We study the asymptotics as t → 0 t\to 0 of the Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) heat trace, defined as the integral over Ω \Omega of the on-diagonal LQG heat kernel. Our main result is to show that the second term in the spectral expansion as t → 0 t\to 0 of the expected heat trace is governed by a nontrivial exponent, given by the KPZ (Knizhnik–Polyakov–Zamolodchikov) relation. A similar but stronger (almost sure) result applies to the related notion of heat content. Along the way we obtain various results on the short-term behaviour of the heat kernel, notably solving a conjecture of [ 12 ] concerning its annealed asymptotics, and showing the finiteness of all moments of the properly rescaled heat kernel.



1
Introduction, main results and conjectures

1.1
Background

In classical Riemannian geometry, the analysis of the
heat trace
(the space integral of the on-diagonal heat kernel, see definitions below) is a key way of identifying spectral invariants, i.e., quantities that are spectrally determined, such as the volume or the length of the boundary. Its short term asymptotics are closely related to some fundamental parameters of the underlying manifold, including its Euler characteristic, eigenvalue counting function, and the zeta-regularised determinant of its Laplacian.

In this paper we consider short time asymptotics of the heat trace in the context of
Liouville quantum gravity
(LQG), a certain canonical random geometry initially proposed by Polyakov
[
35
]
for non-critical bosonic string theory in two dimensions. We are motivated not only by the programme initiated in
[
12
]
(which initiated the study of many questions in the spectral geometry of LQG, see also
[
15
]
) but also by the fact that spectral geometry should provide an alternative way of defining and studying LQG; for instance, reweighing the law of an LQG surface corresponding to a certain coupling constant
\gamma\in(0,2)
by an appropriate power – the central charge – of its zeta-regularised determinant (if it can be well-defined) should lead to an LQG surface with a different coupling constant
\gamma^{\prime}
; see for instance
[
2
]
and
[
15
]
.

The leading order behaviour of the LQG heat trace
\mathbf{H}_{\Omega}(t)
as
t\to 0
was described in
[
12
]
and was found to be proportional to
(1/t)
times the LQG area of the surface, leading to an (almost sure) Weyl law for the eigenvalue counting function, albeit with a nontrivial,
\gamma
-dependent proportionality constant
c_{\gamma}
. In this paper we consider the next term in this expansion, which, if the Riemannian template is to be believed, should be governed by boundary effects and should be of order
1/\sqrt{t}
. In fact, our main finding is that this next term is determined by boundary effects which are considerably more subtle in the LQG case than in the Riemannian situation. In particular, its behaviour is described by a nontrivial exponent which is related to the so-called
KPZ equation
(after Knizhnik, Polyakov and Zamolodchikov), the relation which can be used to transform Euclidean scaling exponents of a set into its LQG analogue
[
31
,
19
,
37
,
8
,
4
,
24
]
; see also
[
10
, Chapter3]
for an overview.

Let
h
denote a whole plane Gaussian free field, normalised so its unit circle average vanishes (see
[
10
, Section 6.4.1]
for a definition, and see also (
1.18
) for some basic properties and reminders about this field). Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be a bounded, simply connected domain, and consider the restriction
h|_{\Omega}
of
h
to
\Omega
.
Let
\mu
denote the corresponding GMC (or Liouville area measure), which corresponds informally to the exponential of the field
h
:
\mu(dx)=\exp(\gamma h(x))dx
. However, this definition is not rigorous as the GFF is not pointwise defined, and requires a regularisation procedure:

\mu(dx)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{\gamma^{2}/2}e^{\gamma h_{\varepsilon}(x)}dx

as
\varepsilon\to 0
in probability with respect to the topology of vague convergence; see e.g.,
[
14
]
,
[
39
]
or
[
10
]
for the existence of this limit.

Let
(\mathbf{Z}_{t},0\leq t\leq\sigma_{\Omega})
denote the associated Liouville Brownian motion (
[
23
,
13
]
, killed upon leaving
\Omega
(so
\sigma_{\Omega}=\sigma_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Z})=\inf\{t>0:\mathbf{Z}_{t}\notin\Omega\}
). We review its construction in Section
1.8
, but for now let us simply mention that Liouville Brownian motion can be viewed as the canonical diffusion in the geometry of LQG, and is for instance conjectured to describe the scaling limits of random walks on corresponding models of random planar maps (see e.g.
[
9
]
for results in this direction).

Let
{\mathbf{p}}^{\Omega}_{t}(x,y)
denote the corresponding
heat kernel
, so that, by definition
[
22
]
,
{\mathbf{p}}^{\Omega}_{t}(x,y)
is the Radon–Nikodym derivative of the law of
\mathbf{Z}_{t}
, restricted to the event
\{\sigma_{\Omega}>t\}
, with respect to
\mu
, when
\mathbf{Z}
is started from
x\in\Omega
. A jointly continuous modification in
\Omega^{2}\times(0,t)
is known to exist (
[
32
]
) and we will only refer to this from now on. (Strictly speaking, these results were stated for Gaussian free fields with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
rather than the whole plane GFF restricted to
\Omega
which we consider here, but by local absolute continuity these results can easily be transported to this framework).

The main object of investigation in this paper is the
LQG heat trace
:

\mathbf{H}_{\Omega}(t)=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{p}^{\Omega}_{t}(x,x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x).

(1.1)

Although this won’t be needed in the paper, we recall that by the trace formula,
\mathbf{H}_{\Omega}(t)
is the Laplace transform of the nonnegative measure associated to the increasing eigenvalue counting function: if

\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
denotes the sequence of eigenvalues associated to the corresponding Liouville Brownian motion in nondecreasing order, and
\mathbf{N}(\lambda)=\sum_{n}1_{\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}\leq\lambda\}}
is the eigenvalue counting function, then
\mathbf{H}_{\Omega}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{N}(\lambda)
.

It was proved in
[
12
]
that the leading order asymptotics of the heat trace is of order
(1/t)
and is proportional to the LQG area of
\Omega
:

t\mathbf{H}_{\Omega}(t)\to c_{\gamma}\mu(\Omega)

(1.2)

in probability as
t\to 0
, where
c_{\gamma}=1/[\pi(2-\gamma^{2}/2)]
. Using a small extension of Tauberian theory and the above trace formula, this implied the following result for the eigenvalue counting function:

\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{N}(\lambda)\to c_{\gamma}\mu(\Omega),

(1.3)

(Once again, strictly speaking these results were established for a Gaussian free field with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
instead of a quantum cone restricted to
\Omega
as we do here, but there is no difficulty in extending that result to the setup of quantum cones considered here; see Section
3
for more details.)
Thus, the LQG eigenvalues satisfy a (quenched) Weyl law, with spectral dimension
d_{s}=2
but with anomalous proportionality constant
c_{\gamma}
instead of the constant
c_{0}=1/(2\pi)
observed in the standard Riemannian framework.

1.2
Heat trace results

Our goal in this paper is on the one hand, to go further in the expansion of the heat trace near
t=0
compared to (
1.2
), and on the other hand, to discuss the asymptotics of related quantities such as the
heat content
, discussed below. In the Riemannian setting, it is well known (see e.g.,
[
30
]
) that

H_{\Omega}(t):=\int_{\Omega}p^{\Omega}_{t}(x,x)dx=\frac{1}{t}c_{0}\text{Leb}(\Omega)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}c^{\prime}_{0}|\partial\Omega|,

(1.4)

at least when
\Omega
is a smooth domain. (Here
H_{\Omega}(t)
is the heat trace of standard Brownian motion in
\Omega
, and
p^{\Omega}_{t}(x,y)
is the transition probability of standard Brownian motion, killed upon leaving
\Omega
, and
|\partial\Omega|
denotes the boundary length of
\Omega
.) While the first term above matches with
(
1.2
) (replacing
c_{0}
by
c_{\gamma}
), we will see that the order of magnitude of the next term, averaged over the realisations of the field, is very different and is instead of order
t^{-(1-\Delta)}
, where
\Delta
is the
KPZ exponent
of the boundary, as will be defined below.

This appears to confirm numerical simulations performed in
[
12
]
, where it was observed numerically that the leading order exponent for the second term in the expansion of
\mathbf{H}_{\Omega}(t)
near
t=0
is anomalous, i.e., deviates (substantially) from
1/2
.

To state our results precisely, we first introduce carefully the exponents which play a role. Suppose that
\Omega
is a bounded domain, and let
\partial\Omega
be its boundary. We will need to consider the fractal dimension (more precisely, Euclidean scaling exponent) of
\partial\Omega
,

as viewed from the inside
of
\Omega
. (This corresponds to what is sometimes called the
inner
Minkowski dimension
[
20
]
, more precisely the inner Minkowski dimension would be given by
d=2-2\mathsf{x}
in the notations below.)
As the domain above need not be simply connected (so that
\partial\Omega
need not be connected, and could include Cantor-like sets of nontrivial dimensions), we also fix a connected component, denoted by
\partial^{*}\Omega
, of
\partial\Omega
, and which we assume is of positive diameter.

Assumption 1.1
.

Suppose there exists
\mathsf{x}\in[0,1]
such that

\Omega_{\varepsilon}
denotes the inner
\varepsilon-
neighbourhood of
\partial\Omega
(i.e.,
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{z\in\Omega:d(z,\partial\Omega)\leq\varepsilon\}
), then

\mathrm{Leb}\left(\Omega_{\varepsilon}\right)=\varepsilon^{2\mathsf{x}+o(1)}.

(1.5)

We also suppose that if
\Omega^{*}_{\varepsilon}=\{z\in\Omega:d(z,\partial^{*}\Omega)\leq\varepsilon\}
, then also
\text{Leb}(\Omega^{*}_{\varepsilon})=\varepsilon^{2\mathsf{x}+o(1)}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

We call
\mathsf{x}
defined by (
1.5
) the
inner
scaling exponent of
\partial\Omega
. Since
\partial^{*}\Omega
is connected, note that
\mathsf{x}\in[0,1/2]
necessarily, with
\mathsf{x}=1/2
if
\partial\Omega
is smooth.

Remark 1.1
.

The reader may wonder if the inner Minkowski dimension (or equivalently inner scaling exponent) ever differs from its global version. Equivalently, can the outer dimension of a domain
\Omega
ever be strictly larger than its inner dimension?
It is in fact possible to construct examples of such sets. We will provide in Appendix
C
an example of such a set.

Thus, suppose that (
1.5
) holds.
Let

\Delta\in[0,1]
be the unique solution of the KPZ equation

\mathsf{x}=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}\Delta+(1-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4})\Delta^{2},

(1.6)

where
x
is the inner scaling exponent associated to
\partial\Omega
, as in
(
1.5
).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
h
be as above. Then as
t\to 0
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{H}_{\Omega}(t)]=\frac{1}{t}c_{\gamma}\mathbb{E}[\mu(\Omega)]-t^{-1+\Delta+o(1)}.

(1.7)

Again, we emphasise that this result gives information only at the level of exponents for the second term in
\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{H}}_{\Omega}(t)]
. Concretely, it says

\frac{\log\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{H}_{\Omega}(t)]-\frac{1}{t}c_{\gamma}\mathbb{E}[\mu(\Omega)]\right)}{\log t}\to-1+\Delta

as
t\to 0
.
It is unclear at this stage whether one should expect the same result in an almost sure sense, i.e., without taking the expectation (over the realisations of the field
h
).

Beyond Theorem
1.2
, it would be particularly natural and interesting to be able to analyse where
(\Omega,h)
is a so-called unit
quantum disc
(of some particular weight, and conditioned to have unit boundary length, say).
Informally, this corresponds to the above case where
\Omega
is bounded by an SLE
κ
-type curve. Using the well known theorem of Beffara
[
6
]
for the value of the dimension of SLE (and a recent strengthening of this result by Powell and Sepulveda
[
36
]
, which shows that the dimensions of this curve as computed on each side separately agree), and solving the KPZ equation we would find
\Delta=1/2
. From Theorem
1.2
it is thus natural to conjecture:

Conjecture 1
.

Let
(\Omega,h)
be a quantum disc as above. Then

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{H}(t)]=\frac{1}{t}c_{\gamma}\mathbb{E}[\mu(\Omega)]-t^{-1/2+o(1)}

Thus for quantum discs one should recover (at least the level of exponents) the same behaviour as in the Riemannian case, (
1.4
). Although such a fact is heavily suggested by our results, we are currently unable to prove this at the moment, for reasons which we believe to be only technical. We are nevertheless able to prove a statement which is “morally equivalent” and which concerns the
heat content
, as we now explain.

1.3
Heat content

By definition, the
heat content

\mathbf{K}_{\Omega}(t)
is given by

\mathbf{K}_{\Omega}(t)=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{P}_{x}[\sigma_{\Omega}<t]\mu(\mathrm{d}x),

where we recall that
\sigma_{\Omega}=\inf\{t>0:\mathbf{Z}(t)\notin\Omega\}

is the exit time of
\Omega
by our Liouville Brownian motion, and
\mathbf{P}_{x}
denotes its (quenched) law starting from
x\in\Omega
.

The heat content has the following physical interpretation: imagine that at time
t=0
the domain
\Omega
has temperature
T=0
everywhere. Then starting from time
t=0^{+}
, heat up the boundary with a constant temperature
T=1
. The quantity
\mathbf{P}_{x}(\tau_{\Omega}>t)
then measures the temperature at a point
x
and time
t
in
\Omega
, so the heat content measures the integrated temperature (“total heat”) in
\Omega
at time
t>0
. Now, as time increases, the temperature inside
\Omega
gradually increases from
T=0
at time
t=0
to
T=1
at time
t=\infty
, and it is of particular interest to describe the asymptotics near
t=0
of this behaviour. Beyond its physical interpretation and connection to heat trace (which we will detail below), the heat content is also a quantity of interest from the point of view of spectral geometry of Euclidean domains and more generally Riemannian manifolds; see, e.g.,
[
33
,
42
,
40
,
41
]
, and references therein (note however that the heat content is not a spectral invariant).

Clearly, from the above description one might expect that the short time behaviour of
\mathbf{K}_{\Omega}(t)
is dominated by boundary effects, and this may lead the reader to wonder if there is a connection between the second term asymptotics of
\mathbf{H}_{\Omega}(t)
and the leading order of
\mathbf{K}_{\Omega}(t)
. The answer turns out to be yes: informally, for reasons that will be clarified later, one should expect that the second term in the expansion of
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{H}_{\Omega}(t)]
near
t=0
is approximately
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{K}_{\Omega}(t)]/t
. For this reason, a key step in our proof of Theorem
1.2
is to establish the asymptotic behaviour of the heat content
\mathbf{K}_{\Omega}(t)
near
t=0
.

Our results establish convergence in probability for
\mathbf{K}_{\Omega}(t)
. For this we require a stronger version of (
1.5
), where we control the size of the annuli centered at points on the boundary intersected with
\Omega
, uniformly over all scales and over the boundary. In the language of fractal geometry
[
20
]
, this corresponds to an
inner
and slightly weaker version of the notion of
local Assouad dimension
:

Assumption 1.2
.

There is a constant
C_{\partial}>0
such that for all
0<r<R\leq R_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\partial\Omega}\Big|\Omega_{r}\cap B_{R}(z)\Big|\leq C_{\partial}r^{2\mathsf{x}}R^{2(1-\mathsf{x})}.

(1.8)

The above gives an upper bound on the size of
r
-neighbourhood of the boundary, uniformly across the boundary, and at all mesoscopic scales. In particular, this implies

\displaystyle|\Omega_{r}|\leq\tilde{C}_{\partial}r^{2\mathsf{x}},

(1.9)

for some constant
\tilde{C}_{\partial}
. We also require a complementary lower bound, which says that for every choice of scale
0<R<1
, we have a polynomial number of good points in
\partial^{*}\Omega
next to which the volume growth of the boundary of
\Omega
grows in a controlled way (recall that
\partial^{*}\Omega
is some fixed component of
\partial\Omega
of positive diameter).

Assumption 1.3
.

For all sufficiently small
\zeta>0
there exists a constant
c_{\partial}=c_{\partial}(\zeta)>0
satisfying the following properties.
For every choice of
0<R<1
there exists integer
N\geq 1\vee R^{-2(1-\mathsf{x})+\zeta}
and points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\in\partial^{*}\Omega
with
B_{R}(z_{i})\cap B_{R}(z_{j})=\emptyset
if
1\leq i\neq j\leq N
, and for all
0<r<R
,

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\Big|\Omega^{*}_{r}\cap B_{R}(z_{i})\Big|\geq c_{\partial}r^{2\mathsf{x}}R^{2(1-\mathsf{x})}.

(1.10)

We call
\partial_{R}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\}
this set of points.

Assumption
1.2
can be viewed as an upper bound on the dimension, while Assumption
1.3
is a lower bound; we require here not only that there is a single point where the local behaviour matches the global upper bound (
1.8
) but an abundance of such points, which of course is typically the case for most natural random fractals. The requirement that
N\geq R^{-2(1-\mathsf{x})+\zeta}
is added at little cost, and corresponds to the fact that the dimension of
\partial\Omega
is assumed to be
2-2\mathsf{x}
. (Since
R
is typically small, the presence of the factor
R^{\zeta}
in the assumption makes the result below stronger, since it means that any polylogarithmic terms in the Minkowski gauge can be ignored).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
h
be as in Theorem
1.2
, i.e.,
h
is a full plane GFF normalized to have
0
average on the unit circle. Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain satisfying Assumptions
1.2
and
1.3
. Then

\mathbf{K}_{\Omega}(t)=t^{\Delta+o(1)}
where
o(1)
converges to 0 in probability. That is,

\frac{\log\mathbf{K}_{\Omega}(t)}{\log t}\to\Delta

as
t\to 0
in probability.

Note that for this result there is no need to average over the realisations of the GFF; the convergence holds in probability. However, the assumption on the boundary of
\partial\Omega
can be relaxed to (
1.5
) if all one desires is a control over
\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t)]
: indeed, as our results show, the estimate
\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t)]=t^{\Delta+o(1)}
holds as soon as Assumption
1.1
holds.

As already discussed above, the case of quantum discs (
[
28
,
18
,
3
]
) is particularly interesting. In that setting it should be possible based on the same ideas used in the proof of Theorem
1.3
to show the following result.

Conjecture 2
.

Let
(\Omega,h)
be a quantum disc, as defined in
[
28
]
.
Then

\frac{\log\mathbf{K}(t)}{\log t}\to\Delta=1/2

in probability.

1.4
Results pertaining to the heat kernel

A key aspect of the work to prove Theorem
1.2
is to analyse the short-term behaviour of the heat kernel under the
rooted
measure
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
, which may be thought of as the law of the field viewed from a quantum-typical point
x
(formally this is the Peyrière rooted measure arising from the Girsanov lemma and which may be described as the law of
h(\cdot)+\gamma G(x,\cdot)
, where
G(x,\cdot)
denotes the covariance of the field
h
, see (
1.18
)). In particular, we resolve a conjecture from
[
12
]
. We summarise our results as follows:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Omega
be any domain and let
x\in\Omega
. Consider the on-diagonal heat kernel
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\Omega}(x,x)
, viewed as a random variable under the law
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
. Then, as
t\to 0
we have the annealed convergence in law:

t{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\Omega}(x,x)\Rightarrow{\mathbf{p}}_{1}^{{\mathcal{C}}}(0,0)

(1.11)

where the limiting random variable describes the heat kernel at time one on the
\gamma
-quantum cone. Furthermore, this random variable satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{p}}_{1}^{{\mathcal{C}}}(0,0)]

\displaystyle=c_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\pi(2-\gamma^{2}/2)}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[({\mathbf{p}}_{1}^{{\mathcal{C}}}(0,0))^{n}]

\displaystyle<\infty,\text{ for all }n\geq 1.

See Section
3
for a precise definition of the
\gamma
-quantum cone. It is an interesting question as to whether one can compute explicitly the law or the moments of the limiting random variable,
{\mathbf{p}}_{1}^{{\mathcal{C}}}(0,0)
. We note that given (
1.11
), it follows that the Laplace transform of this random variable is given by a somewhat explicit formula, described in Lemma 4.4 of
[
12
]
.

1.5
The
in-out
decomposition and heuristic connection to KPZ

Here we describe informally a key idea of our proof or Theorem
1.2
for the heat trace; we call this the
in-out
decomposition. We then discuss how this decomposition can be used to give a conceptual explanation for how the KPZ scaling emerges naturally.

Reminders about KPZ.

We start by some reminders on the KPZ equation. Informally, the Euclidean scaling exponent of a Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is such that
\dim(A)=2(1-\mathsf{x})
, where
\dim(A)
is an appropriate notion of dimension for
A
(i.e., typically, Hausdorff or Minkowski dimension, with respect to Euclidean distance, respectively Lebesgue measure). In other words,
1-\mathsf{x}
is the
relative dimension
of
A
compared to that of the entire space.

The quantum scaling exponent
\Delta
is (at least informally) defined similarly, by the property that
1-\Delta
is the dimension of
A
in the intrinsic Liouville metric, relative to that of the entire space:

\dim_{\gamma}(A)=D_{\gamma}(1-\Delta),

(1.12)

where
D_{\gamma}
is the Hausdorff dimension of the whole space (see
[
27
]
for a rigorous formulation of the above informal identity). On the other hand, the value of
D_{\gamma}
is currently unknown (even nonrigorously), except in the case
\gamma=\sqrt{8/3}
where it is known that we have
D_{\gamma}=4
. See
[
10
, Chapter 3]
for a discussion.

While the proofs of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
are based on a “bare-hands” analysis of the heat content and heat kernel, which does not explicitly use the connection to KPZ, we provide here a heuristic explanation for why such an exponent is in fact natural.

The “in-out” decomposition.

Fix
x\in\Omega
. The starting point of our argumentation is the following trivial but essential decomposition:

\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\Omega}(x,x)=\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}(x,x)-\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)

(1.13)

where the first term indicates that there is no restriction on the trajectory (other than it must start and end at the same point
x
, but is otherwise not constrained to stay in the domain
\Omega
), and the second term corresponds to subtracting all trajectories that leave
\Omega
. Integrating (with respect to the GMC measure
\mu
) over
x\in\Omega
we obtain

\mathbf{H}(t)=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}(x,x)\mu(dx)-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\mu(dx).

(1.14)

This identity is what we refer to in the following as the in-out decomposition.

Let us explain how KPZ scaling emerges naturally from such a decomposition as well as basic scaling considerations. When
t
is small,
\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}(x,x)
depends only on the local geometry of the field
h
near
x
, which by results of
[
18
]
(see also Theorem 7.11 in
[
10
]
) is close (under the rooted law
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
) to that of a
\gamma
-quantum cone rerooted at
x
(let us call such a cone
{\mathcal{C}}_{x}
for now); see Section
3
for definitions and reminders. It is thus natural to expect
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}(x,x)\approx{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{{\mathcal{C}}_{x}}(x,x)
.

Now, a defining property of quantum cones is their invariance under scaling by a fixed amount of quantum (Liouville) area.
Since in two dimensions “volume=time” (for Brownian motion) it is not hard to verify the following elementary but crucial scaling relation for the heat kernel on the quantum cone:

\mathbf{p}^{{\mathcal{C}}}_{t}(0,0)\overset{d}{=}\frac{1}{t}\mathbf{p}_{1}^{{\mathcal{C}}}(0,0).

(1.15)

A proof of this scaling relation will be given in Proposition
3.1
.
As a consequence, plugging into (
3.1
) and using ergodicity of the quantum cone, it is not hard to guess that the expectation of the first term on the right hand side of (
3.1
) scales like
(1/t)\times\mathbb{E}[\mu(\Omega)]\times c_{\gamma}
where the constant
c_{\gamma}
corresponds to
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{p}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}}(0,0))
. This first term thus corresponds to the volume scaling in the Weyl law. Comparing with
[
12
]
, it must be the case
c_{\gamma}=1/[\pi(2-\gamma^{2}/2)]
; this explains Theorem
1.4
.

Given the above, the corrections to the Weyl law are thus captured by the second term in the in-out decomposition (
3.1
). Theorem
1.2
therefore boils down to estimating the “out term”, namely it suffices to show:

\mathbb{E}[\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\mu(dx)]=t^{\Delta-1+o(1)}.

(1.16)

Intuitively, the dominant contribution to the integral in the left hand side comes from points close to the boundary, since otherwise the probability to leave the domain is negligible.

Let
(\mathbf{Z}_{t},t\geq 0)=(\mathbf{Z}_{t}(x),t\geq 0)
denote the trajectory of a Liouville Brownian motion starting from
x\in\Omega
, and let
\sigma_{\Omega}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\mathbf{Z}_{t}\notin\Omega\}

be the first hitting time of the boundary of
\Omega
.
Let
\mathbf{P}_{x}
denote the law (conditional on the Gaussian free field, i.e., quenched) of
\mathbf{Z}
, given that
\mathbf{Z}_{0}=x
. Let
\mathbf{d}
denote the
\gamma
-intrinsic distance associated to our quantum cone, as constructed in the series of papers
[
26
,
25
,
17
]
.

It is widely expected that when
t\to 0
, uniformly over
x\in\mathbb{C}
,

\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{Z}_{t},x)\approx t^{1/D_{\gamma}}.

(1.17)

This generalises the well known fact that when
\gamma=0
, standard Brownian motion is diffusive: that is, if
B
denotes a standard Brownian motion starting from
x
, then
\|B_{t}-x\|\approx t^{1/2}
as
t\to 0
. (In fact, (
1.17
) can be inferred from the diffusivity of standard Brownian motion and the KPZ equation itself, see Exercise 4.6 in
[
10
]
.) In other words, in terms of intrinsic distance, the behaviour of the Liouville Brownian motion is very regular (whereas in terms of Euclidean distance, its behaviour is wildly dependent on the local geometry of
h
near
x
and in particular its thickness, see
[
29
]
).

Let us cover
\partial\Omega
with balls
\mathbf{B}_{i}
(with respect to the intrinsic metric
\mathbf{d}(\cdot,\cdot)
) of radius
r=t^{1/D_{\gamma}}
, so
\partial\Omega\subset\cup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}_{i}.

By definition, the number
N
of balls required for such a covering is approximately

N\approx r^{-\dim_{\gamma}(A)}=t^{-\dim_{\gamma}(A)/D_{\gamma}}=t^{-(1-\Delta)},

by definition of the quantum scaling exponent (see Section 3.12.1 in
[
10
]
), and where
\Delta=\Delta(\partial\Omega)
satisfies (
1.6
). From (
1.17
) it is natural to expect that if the starting point
x
of Liouville Brownian motion is such that
x\notin\cup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}_{i}
then
\mathbf{P}_{x}(\sigma_{\Omega}<t)\approx 0
and we should also have
\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\approx 0
. On the other hand, if
x\in\cup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}_{i}
then
\mathbf{P}_{x}(\sigma_{\Omega}<t)\approx 1
, and thus, one should have (in view of (
1.15
)):

\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\asymp\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}(x,x)\approx\frac{1}{t}.

We deduce that the left hand side of (
1.16
) satisfies:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{t}\mu(\cup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}_{i})

Now, by definition of the Hausdorff dimension of the whole space, one should also expect that for each ball
\mathbf{B}_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,N
) one has
\mu(\mathbf{B}_{i})\approx r^{D_{\gamma}}=t
.
(This is because the Hausdorff dimension
D_{\gamma}
corresponds to the volume growth exponent). Therefore, since the balls
\mathbf{B}_{i}
form an optimal cover of
\partial\Omega
and are thus essentially disjoint, we get altogether:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle\approx Nr^{D_{\gamma}}\frac{1}{t}

\displaystyle\approx t^{-(1-\Delta)}\times t\times t^{-1}=t^{-(1-\Delta)}.

This justifies (at least heuristically) the exponent obtained in the right hand side of (
1.16
) and thus the scaling in Theorem
1.2
.

Remark 1.5
.

The Euclidean analogue of the decomposition (
1.13
) and (
3.1
) were already noticed by Kac in his seminal paper
[
30
]
. A reasoning not too dissimilar to what is described above was also employed in the book by Berry
[
16
]
to predict (still in the deterministic case) that in
d\geq 2
dimensions and for a potentially rough bounded domain
\Omega
, the eigenvalue counting functions (with Dirichlet boundary conditions, say) has the following asymptotic behaviour:

N(\lambda)=c_{0}(d)\lambda^{d/2}\text{Leb}(\Omega)-c^{\prime}(d,h)\lambda^{h/2}|\partial\Omega|+o(\lambda^{h/2})

where
h
denotes the Hausdorff dimension of
\partial\Omega
,
|\partial\Omega|
its
h
-dimensional Hausdorff measure, and
c^{\prime}(d,h)
a certain constant. This conjecture (and a number of natural variants of it, such as ones in which Hausdorff dimension and measure are replaced by Minkowski dimension and content) have now been disproved in their strictest sense, although they may remain broadly true in a sense that remains somewhat unclear (at least to us) at this stage. We recommend the article of Molchanov and Vainberg
[
34
]
for a discussion of these results. In a sense, results such as our Theorem
1.2
can be viewed as providing some justification for these predictions, at least in the LQG setting.

1.8
Preliminaries and notations

Whole plane Gaussian free field.

A definition of the Gaussian free field
h
on the whole plane may be found in
[
10
, Chapter 6.4]
. Often, one sees this field as being defined modulo a global additive constant, but in this paper we choose a concrete normalisation (i.e., additive constant) by requiring that the average of this
field on the unit circle is zero. Thus
h
is a centered Gaussian field with covariance
G(x,y),(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
, in the sense that for arbitrary
f,g\in H^{-1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{C})
we have

\mathbb{E}[(h,f)(h,g)]=\int_{\mathbb{C}}f(x)g(y)G(x,y)dxdy,

with a slight abuse of notations when
f,g
are not pointwise defined.

An explicit formula for
G
is given by

\displaystyle G(x,y)=\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}+\log^{+}|x|+\log^{+}|y|,\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{C},

(1.18)

where
\log^{+}:=\max\{0,\log\}
; see, e.g., Lemma 5.30 in
[
10
]
. Note that for
x,y\in B_{1}(0)
this gives
G(x,y)=-\log|x-y|
. One can check that that the field
h
is invariant under all Möbius transforms of the extended complex plane
modulo constants
(see, e.g., Corollary 5.7 in
[
10
]
or combine Corollary 6.5 and Theorem 1.57 in
[
10
]
).

Liouville Brownian motion.

Given the random field
h
defined above, Liouville Brownian motion (
[
23
,
13
]
) is the canonical diffusion in the geometry defined by
h
. Its definition starting from a given point
x
proceeds in two steps. First, take an independent, standard Brownian motion
(B_{t},t\geq 0)
starting from
B_{0}=x
. Then consider the quantum clock process associated to
h
and
B
, namely:

\displaystyle\nu(s)

\displaystyle=\nu^{(h,B)}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}e^{\gamma h(B_{u})}du

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{\gamma^{2}/2}\int_{0}^{s}e^{\gamma h_{\varepsilon}(B_{u})}du,

where
h_{\varepsilon}
is a smooth regularisation of
h
at scale
\varepsilon
. (The first line is a formal identity and is given meaning by the second line. The existence of the limit in the second line, and independence of this limit with respect to the choice of regularising convolution kernel, is justified by GMC theory, see
[
14
]
and
[
39
]
or
[
10
, Chapter 3]
for a systematic presentation). By definition, the Liouville Brownian motion
(\mathbf{Z}_{t},t\geq 0)
starting from
x
is obtained by changing the time-parametrisation of
B
through the quantum clock process:

\mathbf{Z}_{t}=B_{\nu^{-1}(t)};\quad\text{ where }\nu^{-1}(t)=\inf\{s>0:\nu(s)>t\}.

(1.19)

It is known that, with this time-parameterisation the process is continuous and has no interval of constancy, however it is highly singular with respect to the original parameterisation of
B
(in particular,
d\nu
, viewed as a Stieltjès measure associated to the increasing process
\nu
, is mutually singular with respect to Lebesgue measure). Note also that if

\begin{cases}\tau_{\Omega}&=\inf\{t>0:B_{t}\notin\Omega\}\\
\sigma_{\Omega}&=\inf\{t>0:\mathbf{Z}_{t}\notin\Omega\}\end{cases}

(1.20)

then
\sigma_{\Omega}=\nu(\tau_{\Omega}).
The notations (
1.20
) will be enforced throughout the article as much as possible; however, in Section
3
it becomes necessary to consider the path space
C([0,T];\mathbb{C})
, endowed with various measures (which may be the law of ordinary Brownian motion, Liouville Brownian motion, Brownian bridge, Liouville Brownian bridge, etc.). We then use generically
\sigma_{\Omega}
and view it as a (continuous) functional defined on path space, which may lead to it being used for other processes than Liouville Brownian motion strictly speaking. To distinguish these laws, we will write
P_{x}
for the law of ordinary Brownian motion starting from
x
, and
\mathbf{P}_{x}
the law of Liouville Brownian motion starting from
x
. For instance, we have

{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(\sigma_{\Omega}>t)=P_{x}(\nu(\tau_{\Omega})>t),

and both sides of the identity are actually random variables under the law
\mathbb{P}
of the whole plane Gaussian free field
h
, say.

Other notations.

If
x\in\mathbb{C}
and
r>0
we call
B_{r}(x)
the Euclidean ball of radius
r
around
x
. We also let
Q=(\gamma/2)+(2/\gamma)
be the
background charge
of LQG. If
x\in\mathbb{C}
and
h
has the law
\mathbb{P}
of a whole plane GFF with zero unit circle average, we call
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
the corresponding Peyrière or
rooted law
, which is simply the law of
h(\cdot)+\gamma G(x,\cdot)
, where
h
has law
\mathbb{P}
and
G
is as in (
1.18
).

In order to conveniently keep track of errors that can be safely neglected when we care about exponents (as in Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
) we introduce the notion of convergence to zero
faster than polynomial
(FTP): we say that
f(t)\to 0
FTP if for any
k>0
,
|f(t)|\leq t^{k}
for
t
sufficiently small. In that case we also write
f(t)=o_{\mathrm{FTP}}(1)
.

We will also sometimes need the related (but slightly more subtle) notion of convergence to zero
slower than polynomial
(STP): say that
f(t)\to 0
STP if
f(t)\to 0
as
t\to 0
, but
f(t)t^{-\eta}\to\infty
for any
\eta>0
. In that case we also write
f(t)=o_{\mathrm{STP}}(1)
.

2
Heat content asymptotics

This section is devoted to a proof of Theorem
1.3
. We start by giving an overview of the proof, which is divided into three parts.

Overall the proof follows a familiar first and truncated second moment argument. Although the KPZ heuristics discussed in Section
1.5
strongly suggests breaking up the integral as a function of the intrinsic distance to the boundary, it turns out to be technically easier to analyse things if we break it up in terms of Euclidean distance to the boundary. We then estimate the probability of leaving the domain by time
t>0
by taking into account the thickness of the point
z
, and optimise over this thickness. It is implicit in the proof of the KPZ relation that the dominant contribution to the Liouville area of the neighbourhood of a fractal set
A
(measured in an essentially intrinsic way) comes from points with atypical thickness, even with respect to ordinary LQG where the points are typically
\gamma
-thick. Here we find a similar phenomenon: for instance, if
\Omega
is
smooth
the dominant contribution comes from points of thickness
\alpha=Q-\sqrt{Q^{2}-2}<\gamma
. (More generally the value of
\alpha_{0}
is specified in (
2.12
).)

We will first consider the case where
h
is a full plane GFF normalized to have zero average on the unit circle, and explain at the end how to deduce the result for quantum cones. We start in Section
2.1
to establish the first-moment asymptotics

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t)]=t^{\Delta+o(1)}\quad\text{as }t\to 0,

see Proposition
2.1
. We apply Girsanov’s theorem to swap the expectation with the integral against the Liouville measure which adds a
\gamma\log
singularity to the field. The problem is then reduced to understanding how the expected exit time from the domain scales with
t
, as a function of the starting point. A scaling argument (Lemma
2.2
) shows that, starting from a Liouville-typical (i.e.,
\gamma
-thick point), an LBM travels a Euclidean distance of order
t^{2-\gamma^{2}/2}
in time
t
.

However, as mentioned above, the dominant contribution comes from atypical
\alpha
-thick points; more precisely points with circle average
h_{r}(z)\approx\alpha\log(1/r)
, where
r=d(z,\partial\Omega)
and
\alpha<\gamma
is the result of an optimisation problem (which turns out to match the exponent
\alpha_{0}=\gamma(1-\Delta)
arising in the proof of the KPZ relation, see
[
19
]
). Lemma
2.5
gives the relevant time scale under the assumption that the point has a given thickness. Combined with an estimate on the size of the neighbourhood of the boundary (at a given Euclidean distance) using Assumption
1.9
and the probability that a given point is of a given thickness we are led to an explicit optimisation problem whose solution gives us the expectation of the heat content.

A key input in this chapter is to obtain good concentration bounds for the exit time of the unit disc by a Liouville Brownian motion from a given open set, say the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
. We obtain the following log-normal tail:

\mathbb{P}^{*}_{0}\otimes{P}_{0}[\nu(\tau_{\mathbb{D}})\leq\epsilon]\leq ce^{-c^{\prime}(\log\epsilon)^{2}},

(2.1)

for some constants
c,c^{\prime}>0
, and where
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{0}
indicates that the field may have an additional logarithmic singularity at the origin, and
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{0}\otimes\mathbf{P}_{0}
indicates we take the expectation both with respect to Brownian motion starting at the origin and GFF (i.e., it is an
annealed
probability). In particular the right hand side of
(
2.1
) converges to zero FTP as
\epsilon\to 0
.
This is the content of Lemma
2.3
, whose proof is technical and inspired by Duplantier–Sheffield
[
19
]
(or, more precisely, by a version of this argument discussed in
[
5
]
), and which may be of independent interest.

Section
2.3
concerns second-moment estimates for the heat content. Jensen’s inequality provides a lower bound on the second moment, so we only need an upper bound on the second moment. One would hope for

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t)^{2}]\leq t^{2\Delta+o(1)}\quad\text{as }t\to 0,

but this fails outside of the
L^{2}
-phase (i.e., when
\gamma\in[\sqrt{2},2]
), since already the second moment of the Liouville measure diverges. We need to identify a good event which carries most of the mass in an
L^{1}
sense, and prevents the second moment from blowing up. The problem is nonstandard, owing to the fact that on the one hand, the points we consider are
\gamma
-thick at an infinitesimal level (an unavoidable consequence of the use of Girsanov’s lemma) but are also conditioned to have a different thickness, namely
\alpha_{0}
, at scale
r
.

Finally, in Section
2.4
we invoke the Paley–Zygmund inequality to obtain convergence in probability. Because our first- and second-moment estimates carry
o(1)
errors, they initially yield a statement only on an event whose probability may tend to
0
. To amplify this to probability tending to
1
, we use a
0
–
1
-type argument: we partition the boundary into many small pieces, establish the local first- and second-moment bounds on each piece, and exploit their approximate independence. For this step we also need Assumption
1.3
, in order to control the
o(1)
terms uniformly over all small pieces. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

2.1
First moment computation

Let us now start with the first moment estimate of the heat content. We assume throughout Section
2.1
that
h
is a full plane GFF normalized to have
0
average on the unit circle and, without loss of generality,
\Omega\subseteq B_{1/2}(0)
. In particular, for any

,
G(x,y)=-\log|x-y|
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let

\Omega
satisfy Assumption
1.1
, i.e.,
\Omega
is a domain whose boundary has inner Euclidean scaling exponent
x\in[0,1/2]
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t)]=t^{\Delta+o(1)}\text{ as }t\to 0,

where

1.6
).

First, using Girsanov’s lemma (see Lemma 2.5 in
[
10
]
) we can rewrite the expected heat content as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{P}}_{z}[\sigma_{\Omega}\leq t]\mu_{\gamma}(dz)\Big]=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes{P}_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})\leq t]dz,

where, under
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}
the field can be written as
h=h^{\prime}+\gamma G(\cdot,z)
, with
h^{\prime}
being a whole plane GFF normalised to have zero unit circle average, and
G
being as in (

).

We need to understand the quantum clock

under a GFF with a
\gamma-

in this ball, to get by (

h=h^{2r}+u^{2r}+\gamma\frac{1}{|\cdot-z|},

where

has the law of a Gaussian free field in
B_{2r}(z)
with Dirichlet boundary conditions, and these are independent. Let

denote the exit time of the planar Brownian motion

from

, and let
\sigma_{r}=\nu(\tau_{r})
the associated exit time of the ball of radius

Lemma 2.2

(Scaling the exit time of a ball)
.

Suppose
B_{2r}(z)\subset\Omega
. Then, we have the almost sure inequalities:

\displaystyle r^{2-\gamma^{2}/2}e^{\gamma\underline{u}_{r}}\hat{\sigma}_{1}\leq\sigma_{r}\leq r^{2-\gamma^{2}/2}e^{\gamma\bar{u}_{r}}\hat{\sigma}_{1},

(2.2)

where
\bar{u}_{r}=\sup_{B_{r}(z)}u^{2r}
(resp.
\underline{u}_{r}=\inf_{B_{r}(z)}u^{2r}

\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}

\hat{\sigma}_{1}
is independent of
h|_{B_{2r}(z)^{c}}
and has the same law as that of the exit time
\sigma_{1}
from a unit ball by a Liouville Brownian motion starting at its center, associated to a Dirichlet Gaussian free field on
B_{2}(0)

Proof.

Denote
\hat{B}_{t}=(1/r)B_{tr^{2}}
, then
(\hat{B}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
has the law of a Brownian motion starting from
z

h
. Let
\hat{h}
denote the image of

B_{2}(0)
). Note that, by (

w\in B_{2r}(z)

G(z,w)=-\log|z-w|.

Hence

\displaystyle\nu(\tau_{r})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\ \int_{0}^{\tau_{r}}e^{\gamma h_{\epsilon}(B_{s})}\epsilon^{\gamma^{2}/2}ds

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\epsilon\to 0}\ \int_{0}^{\tau_{r}}e^{\gamma(h^{2r}_{r\epsilon}(B_{s})+u^{2r}_{r\epsilon}(B_{s}))}(r\epsilon)^{\gamma^{2}/2}e^{\gamma^{2}G(0,B_{s})}ds

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{r^{2}\hat{\tau}_{1}}e^{\gamma(h^{2r}_{r\epsilon}(r\hat{B}_{s/r^{2}})+\bar{u}_{r})}(r\epsilon)^{\gamma^{2}/2}|rB_{s/r^{2}}|^{-\gamma^{2}}ds

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}e^{\gamma\bar{u}_{r}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}r^{2}e^{\gamma h^{2r}_{r\epsilon}(r\hat{B}_{t})}(r\epsilon^{\gamma^{2}/2})|r\hat{B}_{t}|^{-\gamma^{2}}dt

\displaystyle=e^{\gamma\bar{u}_{r}}\ r^{2-\gamma^{2}/2}\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{\hat{\tau}_{1}}e^{\gamma\hat{h}_{\epsilon}(\hat{B}_{t})}\epsilon^{\gamma^{2}/2}|\hat{B}_{t}|^{-\gamma^{2}}dt

\displaystyle=e^{\gamma\overline{u}_{r}}r^{2-\gamma^{2}/2}\hat{\nu}(\hat{\tau}_{1}),

where
\hat{\nu}(\cdot)
denotes the clock process associated to
\hat{B}
in the environment
\hat{h}

∎

From this computation, it is natural to expect that, starting from a
\gamma
-typical point, a Liouville Brownian motion typically travels to a Euclidean distance of order

in time

Concerning

Concretely, we further decompose

\displaystyle\underline{u}_{r}=u(z)+\bigl(\underline{u}_{r}-u(z)\bigr).

Lemma 2.3
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\otimes P_{0}[\nu(\tau_{\mathbb{D}})\leq\epsilon]\leq c_{1}e^{-c_{2}(\log\epsilon)^{2}}.

(2.3)

a variation on classical concentration results for Gaussian processes.

Lemma 2.4

Let

where

B_{Nr}(z_{0})
, and
u
is harmonic on
B_{Nr}(z_{0})
(so
u(z_{0})
coincides with the circle average
h_{Nr}(z_{0})
). Then, we have that
h^{\prime},u(0)
and
u(\cdot)-u(z_{0})
are independent. Furthermore, fix
\lambda_{0}>0
. Then there are constants
c,c^{\prime}>0
,
c
only depending on
N
and
\lambda_{0}
and
c^{\prime}
depending only on
N
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\sup_{z\in B_{r}(z_{0})}|u(z)-u(z_{0})|\geq\lambda]\leq ce^{-c^{\prime}\lambda^{2}},\qquad\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}.

(2.4)

Furthermore, the constant
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(N)
can be chosen arbitrarily large by choosing
N
to be large enough.

We emphasise that aspects of this lemma remain true for a GFF with Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions on an arbitrary domain
U
such that
B_{Nr}(z_{0})\subset U
. In this case
u
and
h
are of course still independent, while
u(z_{0})
and
u(z)-u(z_{0})
are not in general. One can easily check that the proof of (
2.4
) remains valid for this case.

When analyzing the exit time of a Liouville Brownian motion started at
z
from the ball
B_{r}(z)
, we condition on the value of
u(z)
and rescale time accordingly. This changes the effective exponent. The next corollary identifies the resulting scaling exponent. In particular, the thickness of
z
(more precisely, the circle average at scale
r
) alters the typical Euclidean distance a Liouville Brownian motion started at
z
can cover, as follows:

Corollary 2.5

(Scaling with conditioning)
.

Recall the notations of Lemma
2.2
. Suppose
z\in\Omega
and
B_{2r}(z)\subset\mathbb{D}
.
For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, consider the event

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha):=\{h_{2r}(z)=\alpha\log 1/2r\}.

Then a.s. under
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}(\cdot|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha))
, we have by Lemma
2.2

\displaystyle r^{2+\gamma^{2}/2-\gamma\alpha}e^{\gamma(\underline{u}_{r}-u(z))}\hat{\sigma}_{1}\leq\sigma_{r}\leq r^{2+\gamma^{2}/2-\gamma\alpha}e^{\gamma(\bar{u}_{r}-u(z))}\hat{\sigma}_{1},

(2.5)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{1}
is as in Lemma
2.2
, and in particular,
\hat{\sigma}_{1}
and
\underline{u}_{r}-u(z)
are independent of
{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha)
.
As a consequence we obtain the following bound:
let

\displaystyle\kappa(\alpha):=\frac{1}{2+\gamma^{2}/2-\gamma\alpha}.

(2.6)

If
\alpha<Q
(so that
\kappa(\alpha))>0
), then for any
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\ \mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r})\leq t|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha)]=\begin{cases}0\qquad&\textnormal{if }r>t^{\kappa(\alpha)(1-\delta)}\\
1&\textnormal{if }r<t^{\kappa(\alpha)(1+\delta)}\end{cases}\qquad\textnormal{FTP},

(2.7)

where in both of these situations, the convergence to the limit holds FTP and, crucially,
uniformly
in
z
and
\alpha<Q
. On the other hand, if
\alpha\geq Q
and
r\leq 1
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r})\leq t|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha)]\to 0,\qquad\textnormal{FTP}.

Proof.

Under
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}
, we have
h=h^{\prime}+\gamma\log|z-\cdot|
, so conditioned on
{\mathcal{E}}_{r,z}(\alpha)
, we get by (

)

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{2r}(z)=(\alpha-\gamma)\log 1/2r.

Plugging that into (
2.2
), we obtain (
2.5
).

Now suppose that
\delta>0
and
\alpha<Q
so that
\kappa(\alpha)>0
. Then, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r})\leq t|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[t^{\kappa(\alpha)(1-\delta)(2+\gamma^{2}/2-\gamma\alpha)}e^{\gamma(\underline{u}_{r}-u(z))}\hat{\sigma}_{1}\leq t|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[t^{(1-\delta)}e^{\gamma(\underline{u}_{r}-u(z))}\hat{\sigma}_{1}\leq t|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[e^{\gamma(\underline{u}_{r}-u(z))}\hat{\sigma}_{1}\leq t^{\delta}|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\hat{\sigma}_{1}\leq t^{\delta/2}|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha)]+\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}[e^{\gamma(\underline{u}_{r}-u(z))}\leq t^{\delta/2}|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha)].

Now,
\hat{\sigma}_{1}
is independent of
{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha)
so the first term vanishes FTP by Lemma
2.3
, uniformly in
\alpha<Q
. The exact same is true for the second term, as by Lemma
2.4

u-u(z)
and
u(z)
are independent and the tail can be bounded by (
2.4
). The second case
r<t^{\kappa(\alpha)(1+\delta)}
follows in an analogous manner.

Now suppose
\alpha\geq Q
so
\kappa(\alpha)\leq 0
, and let
\delta>0
and
r\leq 1
. Then
r^{2+\gamma^{2}/2-\gamma\alpha}\geq 1
so we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r})\leq t|{\mathcal{E}}_{r,z}(\alpha)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[e^{\gamma(\underline{u}_{r}-u(z))}\hat{\sigma}_{1}\leq t|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\hat{\sigma}_{1}\leq t^{1/2}|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha)]+\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}[e^{\gamma(\underline{u}_{r}-u(z))}\leq t^{1/2}|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha)].

Again, we conclude by Lemma
2.3
and Lemma
2.4
that the right hand side tends to zero FTP in
t
, uniform in
\alpha\geq Q
.
∎

assuming Lemmas

and
2.4
.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

As already indicated at the beginning of this section, we start applying Girsanov’s theorem, to exchange the expectation and the integral. For every point

, under
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}
our field can be written as

where

(see (

We will show that
\gamma

). The contribution of a point is governed by the circle average of

at the scale of its distance to the boundary. Writing

, we therefore condition on the value of

We have to show that

\displaystyle I=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t]dz=t^{\Delta+o(1)}.

We start with the upper bound. We condition with respect to the value of

\alpha

\displaystyle\frac{h_{r}(z)}{\log(1/r)}=\gamma+\frac{h^{\prime}_{r}(z)}{\log(1/r)}

(2.8)

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{z\in\Omega}\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}\Bigl[\nu(\tau_{r})<t\Big|h_{2r}(z)=\alpha\log 1/r\Bigr]f_{r,z}(\alpha)dzd\alpha;

(2.9)

\displaystyle\text{ with }f_{r,z}(\alpha)=r^{(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}/2+o(1)};\text{ and }\int_{\alpha}f_{r,z}(\alpha)d\alpha=1\text{ for all }z\in\Omega

As we will see, the dominant contribution (even for a given
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
) to the second integral on the right hand side turns out to come from a thin “strip” of points at a certain distance from the boundary of
\Omega

, where
\kappa(\alpha)
is given by (

Let us first comment on the case
\alpha\geq Q
(i.e.
\kappa(\alpha)\leq 0
), and show that the portion of the first integral coming from such values of

2.5
, the probability in (

(in particular any

\displaystyle\int_{Q}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\int_{z\in\Omega}\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}\Bigl[\nu(\tau_{r})<t\Big|h_{2r}(z)=\alpha\log 1/r\Bigr]f_{r,z}(\alpha)d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq\int_{Q}^{\infty}\int_{z\in\Omega}Ct^{m}f_{r,z}(\alpha)dzd\alpha

\displaystyle\leq Ct^{m}\int_{z\in\Omega}\int_{Q}^{\infty}f_{r,z}(\alpha)d\alpha dz

\displaystyle\leq C|\Omega|t^{m}

and obtain that this case contributes less than
t^{\Delta+o(1)}
to the integral.

For
\alpha<Q
, we pick a small parameter
\delta>0
, let
\rho=t^{\kappa(\alpha)(1-\delta)}
and decompose the domain
\Omega
by setting

\displaystyle\Omega_{\alpha,n}^{\delta}

\displaystyle:=\{z\in\Omega:\rho^{n+1}<d(z,\partial\Omega)\leq\rho^{n}\},\qquad n\geq 1

\displaystyle\Omega^{\delta}_{\alpha,\mathrm{in}}

\displaystyle:=\{z\in\Omega:d(z,\partial\Omega)>\rho\}

\Omega=\Omega_{\alpha,\mathrm{in}}^{\delta}\cup\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\Omega_{\alpha,n}^{\delta}.

Let us first show that
\Omega_{\alpha,\mathrm{in}}^{\delta}
only contributes a negligible amount to the integral. Note that for
z\in\Omega_{\alpha,\mathrm{in}}^{\delta}
, we have
r=d(z,\partial\Omega)\geq\rho=t^{\kappa(\alpha)(1-\delta)}
. So by (
2.7
) in Corollary

, we see that for any
m>0
, there is a constant
C=C(m,\delta,\gamma)
(but independent of
\alpha<Q
) such that
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t]\leq Ct^{m}
. Hence

\displaystyle\int_{\alpha<Q}\int_{\Omega_{\alpha,\mathrm{in}}^{\delta}}\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r})<t]f_{r,z}(\alpha)dzd\alpha\leq

\displaystyle Ct^{m}\int_{\Omega_{\alpha,\mathrm{in}}^{\delta}}\int_{\alpha<Q}f_{r,z}(\alpha)d\alpha\leq Ct^{m}|\Omega|.

(2.10)

Since
m>0
is arbitrary this shows the left hand side is in particular less than
t^{\Delta+o(1)}
by picking
m>\Delta
.

Now to the case
\alpha<Q
and
z\in\Omega^{\delta}_{\alpha,n}
for some
n\geq 1
. It will turn out to be convenient to first show that the values of
\alpha
outside of a compact set (say

) contribute a negligible amount overall to the integral. To see this, we bound crudely
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r})<t]\leq 1
, and recall that

\int_{\alpha<-2}f_{r,z}(\alpha)d\alpha\leq C\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma+2)^{2}\log(1/r)\Bigr)

using standard results for the tail of a standard Gaussian random variable (where
C
is a universal constant). Furthermore, if
\alpha<-2
,

\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\Omega_{\alpha,n}^{\delta}\subseteq\{z\in\Omega:d(z,\partial\Omega)\leq t^{\eta}\},\text{ with }\eta=\kappa(-2)/2

so that uniformly for
z\in\cup_{n\geq 1}\Omega_{\alpha,n}^{\delta}
, we have
r\leq t^{\eta}
. Therefore, using Fubini’s theorem,

\int_{\alpha<-2}\int_{z\in\cup_{n\geq 0}\Omega_{\alpha,n}^{\delta}}f_{r,z}(\alpha)d\alpha\leq|\Omega|\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma+2)^{2}\eta\log(1/t)\Bigr).

One can easily check that
\eta(\gamma+2)^{2}/2>\Delta
, so that the integral on the left hand side is
t^{\Delta+o(1)}
.

We will thus henceforth be able to assume without loss of generality that
-2\leq\alpha<Q
. Let us call
I_{n}(\alpha)
the portion of the integral in the right hand side of (
2.9
) on
\Omega_{\alpha,n}^{\delta}
.
By the assumption on the inner Minkowski dimension of the boundary
1.2
, we have

\displaystyle|\Omega^{\delta}_{\alpha,n}|=(\rho^{n})^{2\mathsf{x}(1+o(1))}

Since
\kappa(\alpha)\geq\kappa(-2)
uniformly over
-2\leq\alpha\leq Q
, and since
n\geq 1
we deduce that

\displaystyle|\Omega^{\delta}_{\alpha,n}|\leq t^{2\mathsf{x}n\kappa(\alpha)(1-\delta)+o(1)},

(2.11)

where now

is uniform in
\alpha\in[-2,Q)
and
0<\delta<1/2
and only depends on

. Again, on
\Omega^{\delta}_{\alpha,n}

\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}\Bigl[\nu(\tau_{r})<t\Big|h_{r}(z)=\alpha\log 1/r\Bigr]\leq 1
, and we estimate

by

r^{(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}/2}\leq\rho^{(n+1)(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}/2}=t^{(n+1)\kappa(\alpha)(1-\delta)(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}/2}.

Thus, we get the following upper bound for

when

:

I_{n}(\alpha)\leq t^{2\kappa(\alpha)(1-\delta)(n+1)\mathsf{x}}t^{(n+1)\kappa(\alpha)(1-\delta)(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}/2+o(1)},

Where the
o(1)
-term is uniform in
z,\alpha,\delta
and
n
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\alpha<Q}\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\alpha)d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq t^{\Delta+o(1)}+\int_{-2}^{Q}\sum_{n\geq 0}\int_{\Omega_{\alpha,n}^{\delta}}t^{(n+1)\kappa(\alpha)(1-\delta)(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}/2+o(1)}dzd\alpha

\displaystyle\leq t^{\Delta+o(1)}+\int_{-2}^{Q}\sum_{n\geq 0}t^{2\kappa(\alpha)(1-\delta)(n+1)\mathsf{x}}t^{(n+1)\kappa(\alpha)(1-\delta)(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}/2+o(1)}d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq t^{\Delta+o(1)}+\int_{-2}^{Q}t^{o(1)}\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(t^{(1-\delta)f(\alpha)}\right)^{n}d\alpha,

where

f(\alpha)=\kappa(\alpha)(2\mathsf{x}+(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}/2).

Some elementary but tedious calculations show that the minimum of
\alpha\mapsto f(\alpha)

is attained at

\alpha_{0}=Q-\sqrt{Q^{2}-4(1-\mathsf{x})}.

(2.12)

The corresponding minimum is the value

\displaystyle f(\alpha_{0})=\frac{2\mathsf{x}+(\alpha_{0}-\gamma)^{2}/2}{2+\gamma^{2}-\alpha_{0}\gamma}=\Delta,

(2.13)

where one can check that

agrees with (

As a consequence, the sum in the integral above can be bounded (uniformly over

) by

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\Bigl(t^{(1-\delta)\Delta}\Bigr)^{n}\leq\frac{t^{(1-\delta)\Delta}}{1-t^{\Delta/2}}.

if we assume without loss of generality that
\delta<1/2
and
0<t<1
.
Integrating, we thus obtain

I\leq Ct^{(1-\delta)\Delta+o(1)}

with
C=2(Q+2)
and
t
sufficiently small. Since

is arbitrarily small, this shows

, as desired.

The lower bound is easier. We want to restrict to an event and a region which together carry the main contribution. Let
\alpha_{0}
be as in (
2.12
)
be the optimal value,
\kappa(\alpha_{0})
given by (
2.6
) (note that
\alpha_{0}<Q
, so
\kappa(\alpha_{0})>0
) and let
\delta>0
. Under the rooted law
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}
we have by (

),

, where
h^{\prime}
is again a full plane GFF normalized to have zero average on the unit circle. Let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha_{0},\delta)

\displaystyle:=\{h_{2r}(z)\in[\alpha_{0}\log 1/r,(\alpha_{0}+\delta)\log 1/r]\}

\displaystyle\Omega^{*}_{\delta,t}

\displaystyle:=\Bigl\{z\in\Omega\Big|t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})(1+2\delta)}\leq d(z,\partial^{*}\Omega)\leq t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})(1+\delta)}\Bigr\}.

(We recall that

. Let

\displaystyle P_{z}[\tau_{\Omega}<\tau_{r\log 1/t}]\geq\frac{n-1}{n}.

On

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r\log 1/t})<t|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha_{0},\delta)]\geq\frac{n-1}{n},

for

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha_{0},\delta)]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<\nu(\tau_{Cr})<t|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha_{0},\delta)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<\nu(\tau_{Cr})|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha_{0},\delta)]

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<\nu(\tau_{Cr}),\nu(\tau_{Cr})\geq t|{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha_{0},\delta)]

\displaystyle|\Omega^{*}_{\delta,t}|\geq t^{2\mathsf{x}\kappa(\alpha_{0})(1+\delta)+o(1)}-t^{2\mathsf{x}\kappa(\alpha_{0})(1+2\delta)+o(1)}=t^{2\mathsf{x}\kappa(\alpha_{0})(1+\delta)+o(1)},

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega^{*}_{\delta,t}}\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t\cap{\mathcal{E}}_{z,r}(\alpha_{0},\epsilon)]dz

\displaystyle\geq t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})(2\mathsf{x}+(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}/2)+o(1)}=t^{\Delta+o(1)}.

which completes the proof of Proposition

2.2
Proof of Lemmas
2.3
and
2.4

To complete the proof of Proposition
2.1
, it thus remains to verify the estimates for
\hat{\sigma}_{1}
and for

that were invoked above, namely Lemma
2.3
and Lemma
2.4
. These proofs are somewhat technical, and the rest of this subsection is devoted to developing them. We also note that the arguments are inspired by Duplantier and Sheffield (see Lemma 4.5 in
[

]
). We start by proving Lemma

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

The independence of
h^{\prime}
and
u
is just given by the spatial Markov property of the GFF. As
u
is harmonic on
B_{Nr}(z_{0})
, we can write for all
z,w\in B_{r}(z_{0})

\displaystyle u(z)-u(w)=(h,\nu_{z})-(h,\nu_{w})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(z_{0}+Nre^{i\phi})(\underbrace{f_{z}(\phi)-f_{w}(\phi)}_{=:f_{z,w}(\phi)})d\phi,

where
\nu_{z},\nu_{w}
are the harmonic measures on
\partial B_{Nr}(z_{0})
of
z,w
respectively and
f_{z},f_{w}
are the densities of
\nu_{z},\nu_{w}
to the uniform measure on
\partial B_{Nr}(z_{0})
. In particular
f_{z,w}
induces a signed measure on
\partial B_{Nr}(z_{0})
with total mass
0
. By estimating the first derivative of
f_{z,w}
for
z,w\in B_{r}(z_{0})
, it can be easily shown that

\displaystyle|f_{z,w}(\phi)|\leq L\frac{|z-w|}{(N-1)r},

(2.14)

where
L>0
is a universal constant (note that for this bound
z,w\in B_{r}(z_{0})
is crucial); this corresponds to
Harnack’s inequality
. We will write
L_{N}=L/(N-1)
; the only property we will need is that
L_{N}
depends only on
N
and tends to 0 as
N\to\infty
. By (
1.18
), and since
\bar{B}_{Nr}(z_{0})\subset\mathbb{D}
, we have

G(z^{\prime},w^{\prime})=\log(1/{|z^{\prime}-w^{\prime}|})
for any
z^{\prime},w^{\prime}\in\bar{B}_{Nr}(z_{0})
.
Since for any fixed
x\in\mathbb{C}
the function
z\mapsto G(z,x)
is harmonic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{x\}
, we get for
z,w\in B_{r}(z_{0})
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(u(z)-u(w),u(z_{0}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}G(z_{0}+Nre^{i\phi},z_{0}+Nre^{i\psi})f_{z,w}(\phi)d\psi d\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}G(z_{0},z_{0}+Nre^{i\phi})f_{z,w}(\phi)d\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\frac{1}{Nr}f_{z,w}(\phi)d\phi=0.

As
u
is centered Gaussian, we obtain independence of
u(z)-u(w)
and
u(z_{0})
. We also want to estimate the variance of
u(z)-u(w)
. Using that
f_{z,w}
integrates to
0
and (
2.14
), we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(u(z)-u(w))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{z,w}(\phi)G(z_{0}+Nre^{i\phi},z_{0}+Nre^{i\psi})f_{z,w}(\psi)d\psi d\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{z,w}(\phi)\log\frac{1}{Nr|e^{i\phi}-e^{i\psi}|}f_{z,w}(\psi)d\psi d\phi

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f_{z,w}(\phi)|\Big|\log\frac{1}{|e^{i\phi}-e^{i\psi}|}\Big||f_{z,w}(\psi)|d\psi d\phi

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\frac{1}{Nr}f_{z,w}(\phi)f_{z,w}(\psi)d\psi d\phi

\displaystyle\leq L_{N}^{2}\frac{|z-v|}{4\pi^{2}r^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big|\log\frac{1}{|e^{i\phi}-e^{i\psi}|}\Big|d\psi d\phi

\displaystyle\leq CL_{N}^{2}\frac{|z-w|^{2}}{r^{2}},

where
C>0
is a universal constant.

Let us use this to deduce the desired Gaussian tail for the supremum of
|u(z)-u(z_{0})|
over
z\in B_{r}(0)
. For
n\geq 1
, we define the lattice

\displaystyle A_{n}:=\{z\in B_{r}(z_{0}):z=(r2^{-n}k,r2^{-n}l),k,l\in\mathbb{Z}\}

Then we have
|A_{n}|\leq 4r^{2}2^{2n}
. Furthermore, for any given
z\in B_{r}(z_{0})
we define a sequence
(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, by setting
z_{n}
to being the closest point in
A_{n}
to
z

Then note that
|z_{n}-z_{n-1}|\leq\sqrt{2}r2^{-(n-1)}
and thus

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(u(z_{n})-u(z_{n-1}))\leq CL_{N}^{2}2^{-2n},

(2.15)

for some universal constant
C>0
. As
u
is harmonic and therefore also continuous, we can write

\displaystyle u(z)-u(z_{0})=\sum_{n\geq 1}u(z_{n})-u(z_{n-1}).

Let
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
for some fixed
\lambda_{0}>0
. Then using (
2.15
) and the estimate on the variance of
u(z)-u(w)
above, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\sup_{z\in B_{r}(z_{0})}|u(z)-u(z_{0})|\geq\lambda]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[\sup_{z\in B_{r}(z_{0})}|\sum_{n\geq 1}u(z_{n})-u(z_{n-1})|\geq\frac{6\lambda}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{-2}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}[\sup_{z\in B_{r}(z_{0})}|u(z_{n})-u(z_{n-1})|\geq\frac{6\lambda}{\pi^{2}}n^{-2}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}8\sum_{z_{n}\in A_{n}}\mathbb{P}[|u(z_{n})-u(z_{n-1})|\geq\frac{6\lambda}{\pi^{2}}n^{-2}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sum_{n\geq 1}2^{2n}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{C_{2}L_{N}^{-2}\lambda^{2}2^{2n}}{n^{4}}\Bigr),

(2.16)

for some universal constant
C_{1},C_{2}>0
. To conclude, we first notice that there is a universal constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{C_{2}L_{N}^{-2}\lambda^{2}2^{2n}}{n^{4}}

\displaystyle\geq CL_{N}^{-2}\lambda^{2}(n+2^{n})

\displaystyle\geq CL_{N}^{-2}\lambda^{2}n+C\lambda^{2}2^{2n}.

Using this, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}2^{2n}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{C_{2}\lambda^{2}2^{2n}}{n^{4}}\Bigr)\leq\max_{n\geq 1}\exp\Bigl(2\log(2)n-C\lambda^{2}2^{n}\Bigr)\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-CL_{N}^{-2}\lambda^{2}n}.

By maximizing over any
n\in[1,\infty)
, we get that the maximum can be estimated by

\displaystyle\max_{n\geq 1}\exp\Bigl(2\log(2)n-C\lambda^{2}2^{n}\Bigr)\leq\Bigl(\frac{2}{C\lambda^{2}}\Bigr)^{2}e^{-2}\leq C^{\prime}<\infty,

(2.17)

assuming
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
, for some constant
C^{\prime}
only depending on
\lambda_{0}
. Furthermore, the sum gives

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-C\lambda^{2}n}=\frac{e^{-CL_{N}^{-2}\lambda^{2}}}{1-e^{-CL_{N}^{-2}\lambda^{2}}}<\frac{1}{1-e^{-C}}e^{-CL_{N}^{-2}\lambda^{2}}.

(2.18)

By putting (
2.17
) and (
2.18
) together, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\sup_{z\in B_{r}(z_{0})}|u(z)-u(z_{0})|\geq\lambda]\leq ce^{-c^{\prime}\lambda^{2}},\qquad\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}

for constants
c,c^{\prime}>0
, where
c
depends only on
N
and
\lambda_{0}
and where
c^{\prime}=CL_{N}^{-2}
uniform in
\lambda_{0}
can be made arbitrarily big by choosing
N
large enough since
L_{N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. This completes the proof of Lemma
2.4
.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Lemma
2.3
and complete this section.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

First we note that we can assume without loss of generality that
\alpha=0
, as when
\alpha>0
the exit time becomes larger (since
\log(1/|z|)\geq 0
in
\mathbb{D}
). We therefore assume
\alpha=0
in the rest of the proof. The strategy is inspired by Duplantier and Sheffield’s approach in
[
19
]
(where the Liouville area measure of a ball is used in place of the quantum clock accumulated by an independent Brownian motion until its exit time from the unit disc). Roughly, the idea is as follows. We estimate the exit time from below by two smaller copies of itself, by considering two balls of a slightly smaller radius (but still same scale overall). For each of them, by the spatial Markov property, the GFF splits into an independent part plus a harmonic remainder, and Lemma
2.4
controls the harmonic contribution. This yields a lower bound on the original exit time by the maximum of two independent, smaller copies of itself, up to a small error term. By the change of coordinate formula of the Liouville measure, these smaller exit times get transported back to the original scale, resulting in a recursive inequality for the distribution function of the exit time. Estimating this recursion gives the desired bound.

Setup.
To make things rigorous, fix a path of the underlying (ordinary) Brownian motion
(B_{t},t\geq 0)
up to its exit from
B_{2}(0)
. Let

\displaystyle\tau_{1/2}=\inf\{t>0:|B_{t}|=1/2\},\qquad\text{ and let }z_{0}=B_{\tau_{1/2}}

be the first point where the (Euclidean) Brownian motion attains modulus 1/2. By Lemma
2.4
, by choosing
N
sufficiently large we suppose without loss of generality that the constant
c^{\prime}
in this Gaussian bound is such that
c^{\prime}>\log 2
.

For notational convenience, set

\displaystyle D^{+}

\displaystyle=B_{1/4}(0),\qquad D^{-}=B_{1/4}(z_{0}),\qquad D=D^{+}\cup D^{-},

\displaystyle B^{+}

\displaystyle=B_{1/(4N)}(0)\subseteq D^{+},\qquad B^{-}=B_{1/(4N)}(z_{0})\subseteq D^{-}.

The argument runs as follows. Given the path of the ordinary Brownian motion
(B_{t},t\geq 0)
, we bound
\sigma_{\mathbb{D}}=\nu(\tau_{\mathbb{D}})
from below by two ingredients:
– The exit time
\nu(\tau_{B^{+}})
of the smaller ball
B^{+}
– and the clock accumulated by
B
between
\tau_{1/2}
and
\tau_{B^{-}}
, defined as the first time after
\tau_{1/2}
when
B
leaves the ball
B^{-}
. Equivalently, this can be viewed as the clock
\nu^{(h,\tilde{B})}(\tilde{\tau}_{B^{-}})
where the underlying planar Brownian motion is
\tilde{B}_{s}=B_{s+\tau_{1/2}},s\geq 0
, and
\tilde{\tau}_{B^{-}}=\inf\{s>0:\tilde{B}_{s}\notin B^{-}\}
.

To make them independent, we use the spatial Markov property of the GFF
h^{2\mathbb{D}}
to get
h^{2\mathbb{D}}=h^{D^{+}}+h^{D^{-}}+u
, where
h^{D^{+}},h^{D^{-}}
are independent Dirichlet GFFs in
D^{+}
and
D^{-}
respectively and
u
is independent of the two other terms and harmonic in
D^{+}\cup D^{-}
. We define

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\log\nu^{2\mathbb{D}}(\tau_{\mathbb{D}}),

\displaystyle A^{+}

\displaystyle=\log\nu^{(h^{D^{+}},B)}(\tau_{B^{+}}),

\displaystyle A^{-}

\displaystyle=\log\nu^{(h^{D^{-}},\tilde{B})}(\tilde{\tau}_{B^{-}}),

where
\nu^{D^{+}},\nu^{D^{-}}
are the quantum clocks associated to
h^{D^{+}}
and
h^{D^{-}}
respectively. Now, by the strong Markov property of planar Brownian motion, given
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{1/2}}=\sigma(B_{s}:0\leq s\leq\tau_{1/2})
,
(\tilde{B}_{s},s\geq 0)
is a Brownian motion starting in
z_{0}
. Since
h^{D^{+}}
and
h^{D^{-}}
are also independent, we deduce that
A^{+}
and
A^{-}
are independent.

Construction of the recursion.
By the change of coordinate formula of LQG, we have

\displaystyle A^{+},A^{-}\overset{(d)}{=}A-(2+\gamma^{2}/2)\log(8N).

(2.19)

We will write
b=(2+\gamma^{2}/2)\log(8N)
. Its value for the rest of the argument is of little relevance but we note that
b>0
. Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle\nu^{2\mathbb{D}}(\tau_{\mathbb{D}})\geq\nu^{2\mathbb{D}}(\tau_{B^{+}})\geq e^{\gamma\underline{u}}\nu^{D^{+}}(\tau_{B^{+}}),

and similarly for
A^{-}
, so we conclude that

\displaystyle A\geq\max\{A^{-},A^{+}\}+\gamma\underline{u},

(2.20)

with
\underline{u}=\inf_{z\in B^{+}\cup B^{-}}u(z)
. Our goal is to show that, for sufficiently negative
x
,

\displaystyle p_{A}(x):=\mathbb{P}\otimes P_{0}[A\leq x]\leq e^{-c^{\prime}x^{2}}.

For
\rho\in(0,1)
that we will specify later, we split this probability into two terms,

\displaystyle p_{A}(x)=\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\otimes P_{0}[A\leq x,\underline{u}\leq\rho x/\gamma]}_{=:p_{1}(x)}+\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\otimes P_{0}[A\leq x,\underline{u}>\rho x/\gamma]}_{=:p_{2}(x)}.

Now, Lemma
2.4
allows us to immediately bound the first term, so that for sufficiently negative
x
,

\displaystyle p_{1}(x)\leq e^{-c^{\prime}x^{2}},

(2.21)

where we made
c^{\prime}
smaller to get rid of the factor
\rho^{2}/\gamma^{2}
in the exponent and also the constant
c>0
. Using (
2.20
), and the independence of
A^{+}
and
A^{-}
, we get

\displaystyle p_{2}(x)\leq\mathbb{P}(\max(A^{-},A^{+})\leq(1-\rho)x)\leq p_{A}\Bigl((1-\rho)x+\underbrace{(2+\gamma^{2}/2)\log 8N}_{=b}\Bigr)^{2},

which in total gives

\displaystyle p_{A}(x)\leq p_{A}\Bigl((1-\rho)x+b\Bigr)^{2}+e^{-c^{\prime}x^{2}},

for some constant
c^{\prime}>0
. From here the idea is to use a recursive argument to obtain the desired Gaussian bound. Such an idea is sketched in
[
19
]
; however, we could not follow the details of their argument, and indeed our numerical simulations suggest that the induction alone as used in
[
19
]
is not enough to guarantee a Gaussian bound without additional nontrivial information on the start of this recursion. The same question is also studied by Aru in
[
5
, Lemma 4.3]
, following a similar idea but with a cleaner approach, and we follow this instead. While some of the details of the argument there were not entirely written (and indeed the crucial iteration step cannot be carried out all the way up to
\nu_{0}
in Aru’s notations) we were nevertheless able to carry out the argument and provide a complete proof following his outline. The details are as follows.

Note that if we choose
N=2
so
b=(2+\gamma^{2}/2)\log(8N)
then
b\leq 16\log 2
, so if
x\leq-1000
and
\rho=0.15
then
x^{\prime}:=((1-\rho)x+b)\leq 0.8x=(4/5)x
. Using monotonicity of
p_{A}
we deduce

p_{A}(x)\leq e^{-c^{\prime}x^{2}}+p_{A}(4x/5)^{2};\quad\text{ for all }x\leq-1000.

(2.22)

Fix
a=c^{\prime}/2
and let
x_{0}\leq-1000
be such that if
x\leq x_{0}
then (
2.21
) holds true and also

e^{-ax^{2}}-e^{-c^{\prime}x^{2}}\geq e^{-0.9ax^{2}}.

(2.23)

Consider
x_{m}=-x_{0}\cdot(5/4)^{m}
. By monotonicity of
p_{A}
again it suffices to show that
p_{A}(x_{m})\leq e^{-ax_{m}^{2}}
for all sufficiently large
m\geq 0
. Suppose for contradiction that:

p_{A}(x_{m})>e^{-ax_{m}^{2}},\quad\text{ for infinitely many values of $m$.}

(2.24)

If
m\geq 1
is any such value we also have from (
2.22
)

e^{-ax_{m}^{2}}\leq p_{A}(x_{m})\leq e^{-c^{\prime}x_{m}^{2}}+p_{A}(x_{m-1})^{2}

so by (
2.23
) (since
x_{m}\leq x_{0}
),

p_{A}(x_{m-1})^{2}\geq e^{-ax_{m}^{2}}-e^{-c^{\prime}x_{m}^{2}}\geq e^{-0.9ax_{m}^{2}}.

This implies (since
0.45\cdot(5/4)^{2}\approx 0.7\leq 0.9
)

p_{A}(x_{m-1})\geq e^{-0.45ax_{m}^{2}}\geq e^{-0.9ax_{m-1}^{2}}.

(2.25)

This can be iterated by induction: for
0\leq k\leq m
, let

y_{k}=0.9^{k/2}x_{m-k}.

Thus
y_{0}=x_{m}
, and as
k
increases,
y_{k}
gradually shrinks closer to 0 at a geometric rate which is faster than
x_{m-k}
itself; in particular
y_{k}\geq x_{m-k}
. As a consequence, if

k_{m}=\min\{k\geq 0:y_{k}\geq x_{0}\}

is the first time for which

y_{k}
gets above the value
x_{0}
, then
k_{m}<m
, and in fact
m-k_{m}\to\infty
as
m\to\infty
(linearly with
m
, in fact).
We will show by induction on
k\geq 0
that for all
0\leq k<k_{m}
,

p_{A}(x_{m-k})\geq e^{-0.9^{k}ax_{m-k}^{2}}=e^{-ay_{k}^{2}}.

(2.26)

For completeness we write out the induction carefully. The case
k=0
was the assumption on
m
, and we have just done by hand the case
k=1
in (
2.25
). Let
k\geq 1
and suppose that (
2.26
) holds. Then from (
2.22
),

\displaystyle p_{A}(x_{m-k-1})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-ay_{k}^{2}}-e^{-c^{\prime}x_{m-k}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-ay_{k}^{2}}-e^{-c^{\prime}y_{k}^{2}}\quad\text{ (since $x_{m-k}\leq y_{k}$)}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-0.9ay_{k}^{2}}=e^{-0.9^{k+1}ax_{m-k}^{2}},

where we used (
2.23
), thanks to the fact that
k<k_{m}
and thus
y_{k}\leq x_{0}
. Therefore, taking square roots,

\displaystyle p_{A}(x_{m-k-1})

\displaystyle\geq e^{-(1/2)0.9^{k+1}ax_{m-k}^{2}}

\displaystyle=e^{-0.45\cdot(5/4)^{2}\cdot 0.9^{k}ax_{m-k-1}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-0.9^{k+1}ax_{m-k-1}^{2}}=e^{-0.9^{k+1}y_{k+1}^{2}}.

This completes the induction.

Now let us evaluate (
2.26
) at
k=k_{m}-1
. This reads:

p_{A}(x_{m-k_{m}+1})\geq e^{-ay_{k_{m}-1}^{2}}\geq\rho:=e^{-a\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{0.9(4/5)^{2}}}.

(2.27)

Note that (
2.27
) holds for the infinitely many values of
m
for which (
2.24
) holds. In particular, since
m-k_{m}\to\infty
as
m\to\infty
we have a subsequence
n_{m}\to\infty
such that
p_{A}(x_{n_{m}})\geq\rho
. This contradicts the fact that
p_{A}(x)\to 0
as
x\to-\infty
(which follows from the fact that
\nu^{2\mathbb{D}}(\tau_{\mathbb{D}})>0
a.s., see, e.g., Theorem 3.42 in
[
10
]
). Therefore, (
2.24
) cannot hold, and this concludes the proof of Lemma
2.3
.

∎

As an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.3
we obtain finiteness of all negative moments for the exit time. We write this below as this may be of independent interest.

Corollary 2.6
.

In the same setting as Lemma
2.3
, we have for all
p>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\otimes P_{0}\Bigl[\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathbb{D}}^{p}}\Bigr]<\infty.

Note that Theorem 3.42 in
[
10
]
shows finiteness of
all
negative moments for Gaussian multiplicative chaos. It seems initially tempting to want to deduce Corollary
2.6
from this result: indeed, when we condition on the trajectory of the Brownian path, then
\sigma_{\mathbb{D}}=\nu(\tau_{\mathbb{D}})
can be viewed as a GMC with respect to a reference measure given by the occupation measure of the Brownian path. Then Theorem 3.42 in
[
10
]
would apply and give finiteness of all negative moments but only conditionally given the Brownian path; one would still need to take expectation with respect to the Brownian path. Unfortunately, this last step is far from obvious, even if we keep track of the explicit dependence of the moments on the Brownian path itself, which, following the work of
[
11
]
(which itself relies on
[
21
]
), boils down to the Riesz energies of the stopped Brownian path, i.e., quantities of the form

\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathbb{D}}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathbb{D}}}1/|B_{t}-B_{s}|^{q}dsdt
for some
q>0
.

2.3
Second moment computation

In this section we complement our estimates on
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{K}_{\Omega}(t))
(which, in particular, gives an upper bound valid with high probability on the heat content itself via Markov’s inequality) with an upper bound on the second moment. Ultimately, the goal is to show that the heat content is concentrated so that we get the lower bound also with high probability.

However, in common with many problems involving Gaussian multiplicative chaos, a straightforward bound on the second moment of the type

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t)^{2}]\leq t^{2\Delta+o(1)}

is too much to hope for. Indeed, the total mass
\mu(\Omega)
itself does not even have finite second moment when
\gamma\notin[0,\sqrt{2})
. We will therefore need to apply a truncation argument in the spirit of
[
14
]
(see also
[
10
, Chapter 3]
). In essence, we have identified in the previous section an optimal distance from the boundary (roughly
r=t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})}
, where
\alpha_{0}=Q-\sqrt{Q^{2}-4(1-\mathsf{x})}=\gamma(1-\Delta)
, see (
2.13
)) which carries most of the contribution to the heat content. In addition, we know that the dominant contribution comes from points whose circle average is of order
h_{r}(z)\approx\alpha_{0}\log(1/r)
, where
r=\text{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)
, consistent with the KPZ heuristics. At the same time, we know that points which contribute to the heat content are themselves by definition sampled from Liouville measure and are thus
\gamma
-thick (
[
10
, Chapter 3]
), not
\alpha_{0}
-thick, i.e., at scales
\varepsilon\ll r
, one expects
h_{\varepsilon}(z)\approx\gamma\log(1/\varepsilon)
. Finding the good event which carries all the mass in an
L^{1}
sense, and does not make the second moment explode, is therefore more subtle than in the standard case discussed, say, in
[
14
]
.

Our solution is as follows. Recall the set
\partial_{R}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\}
from Assumption
1.3
, associated to a scale
R>0
and a small error
\zeta>0
. For each
t>0
, choose a radius
R(t)>0
depending only on
t>0
, such that
R(t)\to 0
when
t\to 0
but slower than any polynomial (STP), i.e.
R(t)=t^{o(1)}
or equivalently,
t^{-\varepsilon}R(t)\to\infty
for all
\varepsilon>0
when
t\to 0
. We also
fix
\zeta>0
arbitrarily (later
\zeta
and
R(t)
will be fixed by (
2.63
)). Given
R(t)
and
\zeta
, we consider the set
\partial_{R(t)}
coming from Assumption
1.3
. For
z_{i}\in\partial_{R(t)}
, we define the truncated heat content as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t):=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})}{\mathbf{P}}_{z}[\sigma_{\Omega}<t]\textbf{1}_{G_{t}}\mu_{\gamma}(dz),

(2.28)

where the good region
\Omega^{t}
is defined by

\displaystyle\Omega^{t}:=\Bigl\{z\in\Omega:\Bigl(\frac{C_{\partial}}{2c_{\partial}}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2\mathsf{x}}}t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})}\leq d(z,\partial^{*}\Omega)\leq t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})}\Bigr\}.

(2.29)

(This should not be confused with our notation
\Omega_{r}=\{z\in\Omega:d(z,\partial\Omega)\leq r\}
.) Here, we recall that the constants
c_{\partial},C_{\partial}
are those appearing in the boundary assumptions (
1.2
) and (
1.3
)). The event
G_{t}=G_{t}(z)
is a good event defined for
z\in\Omega^{t}
, defined as follows. Let us fix
\beta>\gamma
arbitrarily (later it will be taken suitably close to
\gamma
). Fix also
N>0
large enough such that (by scaling for ordinary Brownian motion and Beurling’s estimate)

\displaystyle P_{z}[\tau_{\Omega}>\tau_{Nd(z,\partial\Omega)}]\leq p_{1}=0.1,

(2.30)

uniformly over all
z\in\Omega^{t}
. Writing
r=Nd(z,\partial^{*}\Omega)
, we impose the following conditions:

\displaystyle G_{t}=G_{t}(z):=\Bigl\{(C1)\text{ and }(C2)\text{ are fulfilled}\Bigr\}

\displaystyle(C1)

\displaystyle\qquad h_{r}(z)\in[\alpha_{0}\log(1/r)-2M,\alpha_{0}\log(1/r)-M]

(2.31)

\displaystyle(C2)

\displaystyle\qquad h_{r^{1+q}}(z)\leq(\alpha_{0}+q\beta)\log(1/r),\qquad q\geq 0

(2.32)

for some
\beta>\gamma
sufficiently close to
\gamma
, and where
M
is a sufficiently large constant which will be fixed below depending solely on
\beta
and
\gamma
. Roughly speaking, condition (C1) imposes the correct order of magnitude for the circle average at the scale corresponding to the distance to the boundary (which is the correct intrinsic thickness of points on the boundary which actually contribute to the heat content), while condition (C2) interpolates between the thickness at scale
r
, which is
\alpha_{0}
, and that at infinitesimal scales, which is
\gamma
.

Note that because of our assumptions on the boundary and by (
1.9
), we have

\displaystyle|\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq|\Omega^{t}|\leq|\Omega_{t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})}}|\leq\tilde{C}_{\partial}t^{2\mathsf{x}\kappa(\alpha_{0})},

\displaystyle|\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})|

\displaystyle\geq|\Omega^{*}_{t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})}}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})|-|\Omega_{(c_{\partial}/2C_{\partial})^{1/2\mathsf{x}}t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})}}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})|

\displaystyle\geq c_{\partial}t^{2\mathsf{x}\kappa(\alpha_{0})}R(t)^{2(1-\mathsf{x})}-C_{\partial}\frac{c_{\partial}}{2C_{\partial}}t^{2\mathsf{x}\kappa(\alpha_{0})}R(t)^{2(1-\mathsf{x})}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{\partial}}{2}t^{2\mathsf{x}\kappa(\alpha_{0})}R(t)^{2(1-\mathsf{x})},

for all
t>0
and for all
z_{i}\in\partial_{R(t)}
.
In total we get

\displaystyle|\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})|=t^{\mathsf{x}\kappa(\alpha_{0})+o(1)},

(2.33)

where
o(1)\to 0
when
t\to 0
is an error term whose magnitude is bounded by a quantity tending to 0 uniformly in
z_{i}\in\partial_{R(t)}
.

The plan for this section is as follows. First, we show that the truncation does not remove a significant contribution:

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)]\geq t^{o(1)}\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t)].

Second, we estimate the second moment of
{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)
and show that

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)]^{2+o(1)}.

This then shows (via a standard Paley–Zygmund argument) that
{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)
is not much smaller than its expectation with reasonable probability, i.e., with a probability at least of order
t^{o(1)}
. This could however still tend to zero, and we use a decoupling argument to boost this probability from
t^{o(1)}
to close to 1. This last step is the subject of Section
2.4
.

Before starting with the proofs, we describe the two conditions
(C1),(C2)
under Girsanov’s transform. Recall that
X_{s}:=h_{e^{-s}}(z)
is a Brownian motion in
s
, so the conditions constrain the long-time behavior of
X
, when
t
gets small. By the Girsanov–Cameron–Martin lemma,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)]=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})}\mathbb{E}^{*}\Bigl[{\mathbf{P}}_{z}[\sigma_{\Omega}<t]\textbf{1}_{G_{t}}\Bigr]dz,

(2.34)

where under
\mathbb{P}^{*}
, the field decomposes as
h=h^{\prime}+\gamma G(z,\cdot)
with
h^{\prime}
being a whole plane GFF (normalised to have zero unit circle average). Consequently, by (
1.18
)
X_{s}
becomes a Brownian motion with drift
\gamma s
. Writing
X^{\prime}_{s}:=h^{\prime}_{e^{-s}}(z)
(which is a Brownian motion under
\mathbb{P}^{*}
), the conditions become

\displaystyle(C1)^{\prime}

\displaystyle\qquad X^{\prime}_{s}\in\bigl[(\alpha_{0}-\gamma)s-2M,(\alpha_{0}-\gamma)s-M\bigr],

(2.35)

\displaystyle(C2)^{\prime}

\displaystyle\qquad X^{\prime}_{s(1+q)}\leq\bigl(\alpha_{0}-\gamma+(\beta-\gamma)q\bigr)s,\qquad q\geq 0,

(2.36)

where
s=\log(1/r)
,
M
is still to be fixed, and as before,
r=Nd(z,\partial\Omega)
. In this form, the conditions are well suited for the first-moment analysis.

Lemma 2.7
.

For
{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)
defined as above, we have for all
z_{i}\in\partial_{R(t)}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)]=t^{\Delta+o(1)}\ \

where the
o(1)
-term is bounded by a quantity which tends to zero as
t\to 0
, uniformly in
z_{i}\in\partial_{R(t)}
. In fact, there exists
\epsilon(t)\to 0
such that the following holds. For any choice of
R(t)\to 0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)]\geq t^{\Delta+\epsilon(t)}R(t)^{2(1-\mathsf{x})},

(2.37)

where
\Delta
is as in Proposition
2.1
, uniformly in
z_{i}\in\partial_{R(t)}
.

Proof.

Clearly, we have
\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)]\leq\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t)]
, so it suffices to establish the lower bound. Our goal will be to establish that

\mathbb{P}^{*}\otimes P_{z}(\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t;G_{t})\geq c\mathbb{P}^{*}(G_{t})

(2.38)

for some constant
c>0
, uniformly in
z\in\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})
and uniformly in
z_{i}\in\partial_{R(t)}
. Note that this implies the strong lower bound (
2.37
).
Indeed, given this, we can write by the Girsanov–Cameron–Martin lemma (more specifically (
2.34
)):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)]

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})}\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t;G_{t}]dz\geq c\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})}\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}[G_{t}]dz

Recall that
G_{t}=(C1)^{\prime}\cap(C2)^{\prime}
. We can estimate the probability of
G_{t}
simply by that of
(C1)^{\prime}
: more precisely, there exists
p_{0}
depending solely on
\beta
,
\gamma
and
M
such that

\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}((C2)^{\prime}(z)|(C1)^{\prime}(z))\geq\mathbb{P}[B_{t}\leq M+(\beta-\gamma)t,\forall t\geq 0]\geq p_{0},

(2.39)

where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion starting from
B_{0}=0
. Furthermore, if
\beta
and
\gamma
are fixed, by choosing
M
large enough we can ensure that
p_{0}\geq 0.9
.

Thus,

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)]\geq cp_{0}\int_{\Omega^{t}}\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}[(C1)^{\prime}(z)]dz.

As
X^{\prime}_{s}
is a centered Gaussian random variable with variance
\log 1/r
and by using
r=Nd(z,\partial\Omega)
and (
2.29
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}[(C1)^{\prime}(z)]\geq C\frac{t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})(\alpha_{0}-\gamma)^{2}/2}}{\log 1/t},

where
C>0
is a constant which is uniform in
R(t),z
and
z_{i}
.
We deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)]

\displaystyle\geq cp_{0}\left|\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{0})\right|\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}[(C1)^{\prime}(z)]

\displaystyle\geq c_{\partial}t^{2\mathsf{x}\kappa(\alpha_{0})}R(t)^{2(1-\mathsf{x})}\frac{C}{\log 1/t}t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})(\alpha_{0}-\gamma)^{2}/2}

\displaystyle\geq c\frac{t^{\Delta}R(t)^{2(1-\mathsf{x})}}{\log 1/t}=t^{\Delta+\epsilon(t)}R(t)^{2(1-\mathsf{x})}.

where
c>0
is a constant which is uniform in
R(t)
and
z_{i}
and
\epsilon(t)
is tending to 0 as
t\to 0
, uniformly in
z_{i}\in\partial_{R(t)}
, and uniformly in the choice of
R(t)
.

It therefore suffices to prove (
2.38
). Now, observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t;G_{t}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t;(C1)^{\prime}]-\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t;(C1)^{\prime};\overline{(C2)^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t;(C1)^{\prime}]-\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[(C1)^{\prime};\overline{(C2)^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t;(C1)^{\prime}]-(1-p_{0})\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}[(C1)^{\prime}]

(2.40)

where
\overline{(C2)^{\prime}}
denotes the complement of the event
(C2)^{\prime}
, and
p_{0}
is as in (
2.39
).

To estimate the first term in the right hand side, we will use a Beurling estimate to compare
\nu(\tau_{\Omega})
with
\nu(\tau_{r})
(cf. (
2.30
)) as well as a scaling argument, where we recall that
r=Nd(z,\partial\Omega)
.
More precisely,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t;(C1)^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r})<t;(C1)^{\prime}]-\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r})<\nu(\tau_{\Omega});(C1)^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r})<t;(C1)^{\prime}]-\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\tau_{r}<\tau_{\Omega};(C1)^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r})<t;(C1)^{\prime}]-p_{1}\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}[(C1)^{\prime}].

(2.41)

Now, by Lemma
2.2
,
we have for
z\in\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})
,

\displaystyle\nu(\tau_{r})\leq r^{2-\gamma^{2}/2}e^{\gamma\bar{u}_{r}}\hat{\sigma}_{1}=r^{2-\gamma^{2}/2}e^{\gamma u(z)}e^{\gamma(\bar{u}_{r}-u(z))}\hat{\sigma}_{1},

where all three factors
e^{\gamma u(z)}
,
e^{\gamma(\bar{u}_{r}-u(z))}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{1}
are mutually independent.
When
(C1)^{\prime}
holds, we therefore deduce (after some algebra) that

\displaystyle\nu(\tau_{r})

\displaystyle\leq r^{1/\kappa(\alpha_{0})}e^{-\gamma M}e^{\gamma(\bar{u}_{r}-u(z))}\hat{\sigma}_{1}

\displaystyle=N^{1/\kappa(\alpha_{0})}te^{-\gamma M}e^{\gamma(\bar{u}_{r}-u(z))}\hat{\sigma}_{1}.

We may assume without loss of generality that

N^{1/\kappa(\alpha_{0})}e^{-\gamma M/2}\leq 1,

(2.42)

so that in total, canceling the factors of
t
on either side of the inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r})<t;(C1)^{\prime}]\geq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[e^{\gamma(\bar{u}_{r}-u(z))}\hat{\sigma}_{1}<e^{\gamma M/2};(C1)^{\prime}].

Now, observe that
(C1)^{\prime}
depends only on
u(z)
and is thus independent of both random variables appearing in the right hand side. We deduce

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r})<t;(C1)^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}[(C1)^{\prime}]\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[e^{\gamma(\bar{u}_{r}-u(z))}\hat{\sigma}_{1}<e^{\gamma M/2}].

The two random variables above are furthermore independent of each other, and their law does not depend on
r
or
z
(by scaling invariance of the Gaussian free field in the plane modulo constants). By choosing
M
sufficiently large and using the monotone convergence theorem, we can therefore ensure that the second term in the right hand side is as close to 1 as desired, say at least
0.9
. Hence

\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{r})<t;(C1)^{\prime}]\geq 0.9\ \mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}[(C1)^{\prime}]

Plugging this into (
2.41
) and recalling that
p_{1}=0.1
, we deduce

\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t;(C1)^{\prime}]\geq 0.8\ \mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}[(C1)^{\prime}].

In turn, plugging this into (
2.40
), and recalling that
p_{0}\geq 0.9
, we get

\mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}\otimes P_{z}[\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t;G_{t}]\geq 0.7\ \mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}[(C1)^{\prime}]\geq 0.7\ \mathbb{P}^{*}_{z}[G_{t}].

Let us comment quickly on the order in which we need to choose constants in order to make this argument work: we first pick
N
large enough that (
2.30
) holds with
p_{1}=0.1
. Then we choose
M
large enough so that (
2.42
) holds and (
2.39
) holds with
p_{0}=0.9
.

Altogether, this proves (
2.38
) with
c=0.7
, and thus concludes the proof of Lemma
2.7
.
∎

We remark that the constants
M
and
N
were chosen in a specific way in order to make the above proof work; the choice of these constants to define
G_{t}
(and thus the restricted heat content
{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)
) is now fixed and will not be changed when computing the second moment of the restricted heat content in Proposition
2.8
below.

Proposition 2.8
.

There exists
\epsilon(t)\to 0
such that the following holds for all
R(t)\to 0
. Consider the restricted heat contents
{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t),(1\leq i\leq N(t))
, defined above. Then for all
z_{i}\in\partial_{R(t)}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)^{2}]\leq t^{2\Delta+\epsilon(t)}R(t)^{4(1-\mathsf{x})-\alpha_{0}^{2}/2}\ \ \text{as}\ \ t\to 0,

uniformly in
z_{i}\in\partial_{R(t)}
, and where
\Delta
is as in Proposition
2.1
,

The dependence on
R(t)
, in particular the presence of the power
-\alpha_{0}^{2}/2
in the exponent (which represents a loss compared to the square of the first moment in (
2.37
)), plays an important role in subsequent aspects of the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof.

The lower bound is obvious from Jensen’s inequality and Lemma
2.7
. The hard part is to prove the corresponding upper bound. Using Girsanov again, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)^{2}]=\displaystyle\iint_{(\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i}))^{2}}\mathbb{E}^{**}\Bigl[{\mathbf{P}}_{z}[\sigma_{\Omega}<t]{\mathbf{P}}_{w}[\sigma_{\Omega}<t]\textbf{1}_{G_{t}(z)}\textbf{1}_{G_{t}(w)}\Bigr]|z-w|^{-\gamma^{2}}dzdw,

where under the tilted measure
\mathbb{P}^{**}
we have
h=h^{\prime}+\gamma G(z,\cdot)+\gamma G(w,\cdot)
with
h^{\prime}
being a whole plane Gaussian free field, normalised to have zero unit circle average. (Indeed, recall that on
B_{1}(0)
we have
G(z,w)=-\log|z-w|
and
\Omega\subset B(0,1/2)
). To make the computation easier, we split the integral in the variable
w
in the following way: we fix
z\in\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})
and define for
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle A_{n}(z):=\Bigl\{w\in\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R}(z_{i}):|z-w|\in[e^{-(n+1)},e^{-n})\Bigr\}.

(2.43)

Thus, we get, bounding both
{\mathbf{P}}_{z}[\sigma_{\Omega}<t],{\mathbf{P}}_{w}[\sigma_{\Omega}<t]
crudely by
1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)^{2}]\lesssim\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)(z_{i})}}\sum_{n\geq n(t)}\int_{A_{n}(z)}\mathbb{E}^{**}\Bigl[\textbf{1}_{G_{t}(z)}\textbf{1}_{G_{t}(w)}\Bigr]e^{n\gamma^{2}}dwdz

(2.44)

where
n(t)
is such that
e^{-n(t)}\asymp R(t)
.
The key step is to control the probability of the good events. To this end, we reformulate the conditions (
2.3
) once more; now in the presence of two logarithmic insertions rather than a single one.

The approach is as follows. Let us write
r_{0}:=t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})}>0
. We decompose the double integral into two regimes.

•

First we look at the
mesoscopic case
, where
|z-w|\geq 100r_{0}
. In this regime the two balls
B_{r_{0}}(z),B_{r_{0}}(w)
are disjoint. The objective is to show that, at bigger separations, the restriction of the GFF to the two balls are roughly independent, so the
\mathbb{P}^{**}
-probability of
G_{t}(z)
and
G_{t}(w)
(which concern the behaviour of the GFF inside balls of radius roughly
r_{0}
around both points) factorises, yielding the correct exponent. As the distance between the two points gets closer to the bound
100r_{0}
, the contribution to the integral coming from points at a given distance depends in a delicate way on the geometry of the domain near the boundary, in particular the size of balls of a given radius. This can be controlled by using Assumption
1.3
.

•

The second case is the
microscopic
one, where
|z-w|<100r_{0}
. Here the two balls
B_{r_{0}}(z),B_{r_{0}}(w)
may overlap, and the two logarithmic insertions from the Girsanov transform are close, yielding (almost) a
2\gamma
-log singularity. This proximity makes it very unlikely for both events
G_{t}(z)
and
G_{t}(w)
to hold - this is for similar reasons as in the GMC case (
[
14
]
).

This mesoscopic/microscopic splitting corresponds to partitioning the sum over
n
into
n\leq N_{t}:=\lfloor\log(1/r_{0})-\log(100)\rfloor-1
and
n>N_{t}
. Let us first comment on the size of
A_{n}(z)
. If
z\in\Omega^{t}
, there exists
\tilde{z}\in\partial\Omega
with
d(\tilde{z},z)<e^{-n}
. So we can estimate
A_{n}(z)
using Assumption
1.2
:

\displaystyle|A_{n}(z)|\leq|\Omega_{t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})}}\cap B_{2e^{-n}}(\tilde{z})|\leq 4C_{\partial}t^{2\mathsf{x}\kappa(\alpha_{0})}e^{-2n(1-\mathsf{x})}.

(2.45)

This will be useful in the mesoscopic case. On the other hand, in the microscopic case we will simply use the trivial bound
|A_{n}(z)|\leq|B_{e^{-n}}(z)|=\pi e^{-2n}
.

Now let us start with the estimate of (
2.44
). We begin with the mesoscopic case.

Mesoscopic case.

Let
n\leq N_{t}
, and suppose
w\in A_{n}(z)
. Under
\mathbb{P}^{**}
, we may write by the Girsanov–Cameron–Martin lemma:

\displaystyle h=h^{\prime}+\gamma G(z,\cdot)+\gamma G(w,\cdot),

(2.46)

where
h^{\prime}
is a full plane GFF, normalised to have zero unit circle average.
Let
s_{z}=\log(1/Nd(z,\partial\Omega))
and
s_{w}=\log(1/Nd(w,\partial\Omega))
(where
N
was chosen in (
2.30
)), and let
X_{s_{z}}^{\prime}:=h^{\prime}_{e^{-s_{z}}}(z)
and
Y_{s_{w}}^{\prime}:=h^{\prime}_{e^{-s_{w}}}(w)
.
So
(X_{s_{z}}^{\prime},Y^{\prime}_{s_{w}})
is a centered Gaussian vector, with respective variances and covariance given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}[(X_{s_{z}}^{\prime})^{2}]=s_{z}+O(1),\quad\sigma_{w}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}[(Y_{s_{w}}^{\prime})^{2}]=s_{w}+O(1),\quad\rho:=\mathbb{E}[X_{s_{z}}^{\prime}Y_{s_{w}}^{\prime}]=n+O(1),

(2.47)

where all
O(1)
terms are bounded by a constant
C>0
which is uniform in
z,w,n,t
and
z_{i}
. (Note that the estimate on the covariance relies on the fact that we are in the mesoscopic regime).

Because of (
2.44
), our goal is to estimate
\mathbb{P}^{**}[G_{t}(z),G_{t}(w)].
In fact we will ignore the condition
(C2)
and simply estimate these events using the conditions
(C1)
. Note that the conditions
(C1)(z)\cap(C1)(w)
imply

\displaystyle X_{s_{z}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\in\Bigl[(\alpha_{0}-\gamma)s_{z}-\gamma n-C,(\alpha_{0}-\gamma)s_{z}-\gamma n+C\Bigr],

\displaystyle Y_{s_{w}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\in\Bigl[(\alpha_{0}-\gamma)s_{w}-\gamma n-C,(\alpha_{0}-\gamma)s_{w}-\gamma n+C\Bigr],

where
C=2M
(and where
M
was chosen in the proof of Lemma
2.7
, and hence is uniform in
z,w,n
and
t
). Note that
s_{z}=\log(1/r_{0})+O(1)
, and analogously for
s_{w}
. For simplicity, we write
\lambda=\lambda_{n}:=n/(\log 1/r_{0})
(note that
\lambda\in[0,1]
since
n\leq N_{t}
). Thus, it suffices to compute the joint density of
(X^{\prime}_{s_{z}},Y^{\prime}_{s_{w}})
in the box

\displaystyle\Bigl[(\alpha_{0}-\gamma(1+\lambda))\log(1/r_{0})-C,(\alpha_{0}-\gamma(1+\lambda))\log(1/r_{0})+C\Bigr]^{2},

(2.48)

for some
C>0
which is uniform in
z,w
and
t
. To describe the joint law of
(X^{\prime}_{s_{z}},Y^{\prime}_{s_{w}})
we use the fact that we can explicitly describe the conditional law of
Y^{\prime}_{s_{w}}
given
X^{\prime}_{s_{w}}
, from which it is not hard to see that there exists an independent centered normal random variable
Z
such that

\displaystyle\left(X^{\prime}_{s_{z}},Y^{\prime}_{s_{w}}\right)\overset{d}{=}\left(X^{\prime}_{s_{z}},\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{z}^{2}}X^{\prime}_{s_{z}}+Z\right).

To make this work, by (
2.47
) we have to choose

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Z)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{w}^{2}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{\sigma_{z}^{2}}=\log\frac{1}{r_{0}}-\frac{n^{2}}{\log 1/r_{0}}+O(1)=\log\frac{1}{r_{0}}(1-\lambda^{2}+o(1))

\displaystyle\in\Bigl[\log\frac{1}{r_{0}}\Bigl(1-\lambda^{2}\frac{\log 1/r_{0}}{\log 1/r_{0}-C}\Bigr)-C,\log\frac{1}{r_{0}}\Bigl(1-\lambda^{2}\frac{\log 1/r_{0}}{\log 1/r_{0}+C}\Bigr)+C\Bigr]

(2.49)

For some
C>0
, which is uniform in
z,w,n
and
z_{i}
. Observe also that

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{z}^{2}}=\lambda+o(1)\in\Bigl[\lambda\frac{\log 1/r_{0}}{\log 1/r_{0}+C}-C,\lambda\frac{\log 1/r_{0}}{\log 1/r_{0}-C}+C\Bigr],

(2.50)

again for some
C>0
, which is uniform in
z,w,n
and
z_{i}
. Hence, conditionally given

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{s_{z}}\in\Bigl[(\alpha_{0}-\gamma(1+\lambda))\log(1/r_{0})-C,(\alpha_{0}-\gamma(1+\lambda))\log(1/r_{0})+C\Bigr],

(2.51)

by (
2.48
), the condition for
Y^{\prime}_{s_{w}}
can be equivalently reformulated in terms of
Z
as

\displaystyle Z\in(1-\lambda(1+o(1)))\biggl[(\alpha_{0}-\gamma\Bigl(1+\lambda(1+o(1))\Bigr)\log(1/r_{0}),(\alpha_{0}-\gamma\Bigl(1+\lambda(1+o(1))\Bigr)\log(1/r_{0})\biggr],

for an
o(1)
-term, which is of the same form as in (
2.49
) and (
2.50
). In particular the
o(1)
-term is uniform in
z_{i}\in\partial_{R(t)}
and uniform in the choice of
R(t)
. As
(X^{\prime}_{s_{z}},Z)
is a Gaussian vector with independent entries, we get by the estimates before

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{**}\Bigl[{G_{t}(z)};{G_{t}(w)}\Bigr]\leq r_{0}^{\frac{(\gamma(1+\lambda)-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}}r_{0}^{\frac{(1-\lambda)^{2}(\gamma(1+\lambda)-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2(1-\lambda^{2})}+\epsilon(t)},

where the
\epsilon(t)
is uniform in
z,w,n
and
z_{i}
and
\epsilon(t)\to 0
when
t\to 0
. Using (
2.44
), (
2.45
) and
e^{-n}=r_{0}^{\lambda}
, we conclude

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=n(t)}^{N_{t}}\int_{A_{n}(z)}\mathbb{P}^{**}\Bigl[{G_{t}(z)};{G_{t}(w)}\Bigr]e^{n\gamma^{2}}dwdz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})}\sum_{n=n(t)}^{N_{t}}\int_{A_{n}(z)}\mathbb{P}^{**}\Bigl[G_{t}(z)\cap G_{t}(w)\Bigr]e^{n\gamma^{2}}dwdz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})}\sum_{n=n(t)}^{N_{t}}\int_{A_{n}(z)}r_{0}^{\frac{(\gamma(1+\lambda)-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}}r_{0}^{\frac{(1-\lambda)^{2}(\gamma(1+\lambda)-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2(1-\lambda^{2})}+\epsilon(t)}e^{n\gamma^{2}}dwdz

\displaystyle\leq C_{\partial}\sum_{n\geq n(t)}\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})}r_{0}^{\frac{(\gamma(1+\lambda)-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}+\frac{(1-\lambda)^{2}(\gamma(1+\lambda)-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2(1-\lambda^{2})}+\epsilon(t)}e^{n\gamma^{2}}e^{-2n(1-\mathsf{x})}r_{0}^{2\mathsf{x}}dz

\displaystyle\leq(C_{\partial})^{2}\sum_{n\geq n(t)}r_{0}^{\frac{(\gamma(1+\lambda)-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}+\frac{(1-\lambda)^{2}(\gamma(1+\lambda)-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2(1-\lambda^{2})}+\epsilon(t)}e^{n\gamma^{2}}e^{-2n(1-\mathsf{x})}r_{0}^{4\mathsf{x}}R(t)^{2(1-\mathsf{x})}

\displaystyle=r_{0}^{4\mathsf{x}+\epsilon(t)}R(t)^{2(1-\mathsf{x})}\sum_{n\geq n(t)}r_{0}^{f(\lambda_{n})},

where the
o(1)
-term is uniform in
z,w
and
n
and

\displaystyle f(\lambda):=\frac{(\gamma(1+\lambda)-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}+\frac{(1-\lambda)^{2}(\gamma(1+\lambda)-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2(1-\lambda^{2})}+\lambda(2(1-\mathsf{x})-\gamma^{2}).

We estimate
f(\lambda)
naively:

\displaystyle f(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\gamma(1+\lambda)-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{1+\lambda}+\lambda(2(1-\mathsf{x})-\gamma^{2})

\displaystyle=(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}+\lambda\gamma^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\alpha_{0}^{2}+\lambda(2(1-\mathsf{x})-\gamma^{2})

\displaystyle=(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}+2\lambda(1-\mathsf{x})-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\alpha_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}+\lambda\Bigl(2(1-\mathsf{x})-{\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}}{2}}\Bigr).

Plugging that into our estimate of the mesoscopic part and using
\lambda_{n}=n/(\log 1/r_{0})
results in

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})}\sum_{n=n(t)}^{N_{t}}\int_{A_{n}(z)}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{**}\Bigl[{G_{t}(z)};{G_{t}(w)}\Bigr]e^{n\gamma^{2}}dwdz

\displaystyle\leq t^{4\mathsf{x}\kappa(\alpha_{0})+\epsilon(t)}R(t)^{2(1-\mathsf{x})}\sum_{n\geq n(t)}t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})f(\lambda_{n})}

\displaystyle\leq t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})(4\mathsf{x}+(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2})+\epsilon(t)}R(t)^{2(1-\mathsf{x})}\sum_{n\geq n(t)}t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})\lambda_{n}(2(1-\mathsf{x})-\alpha_{0}^{2}/2)}

\displaystyle=t^{2\Delta+\epsilon(t)}R(t)^{2(1-\mathsf{x})}\sum_{n\geq n(t)}e^{-n[(2(1-\mathsf{x})-\alpha_{0}^{2}/2]}

\displaystyle=t^{2\Delta+\epsilon(t)}R(t)^{4(1-\mathsf{x})-\alpha_{0}^{2}/2}.

This concludes the first part of the argument.

Microscopic case.
When
B_{r_{z}}(z)
and
B_{r_{w}}(w)
may overlap, the joint covariance structure of the circle-average processes
X^{\prime}_{s_{z}}
and
Y^{\prime}_{s_{w}}
becomes pointless. We therefore discard the constraint at
w
and impose only a single good event for
z
. Heuristically, as
z
and
w
approach each other faster than the radius shrinks, the configuration behaves like a single ball with a double logarithmic insertion at (nearly) the same center. This substantially increases the cost of enforcing the circle average to be no more than
\alpha_{0}\log(1/r_{0})
(in fact what will be useful here more specifically will be condition
(C2)
).
This can be used to balance the Girsanov factor
|z-w|^{-\gamma^{2}}
which otherwise leads to a blowup on the diagonal when
\gamma\in[\sqrt{2},2)
.

Let us start commenting on the event
(C1)\cap(C2)
for some
z\in\Omega^{t}
and
w\in A_{n}(z)
such that
|z-w|<100t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})}
, i.e.
n>N_{t}
. As before, we can rephrase this in terms of the Brownian motion
X^{\prime}_{s}:=h^{\prime}_{e^{-s}}(z)
. Since
|z-w|\asymp e^{-n}
, this means that
s\mapsto h_{e^{-s}}(z)
is a Brownian motion with two additional drifts. The first one is given by
\gamma s
and comes from the
\gamma-\log
-singularity at
z
. The second one is coming from the
\gamma-\log
-singularity at
w
, and is thus given by
\gamma s
so long as
s<n
, whereas it is roughly zero when
s>n
.

Our strategy will thus be to estimate from above
G_{t}(z)\cap G_{t}(w)
by
(C1)(z)
and
(C2)(z)
where we only enforce the condition
(C2)(z)
at a scale corresponding to the separation between
z
and
w
.

In fact, we will distinguish an additional “intermediate regime”, where
r_{0}/2\leq|z-w|\leq 100r_{0}
.
In this case, essentially
X^{\prime}_{s}
has drift
2\gamma
until roughly
s=s_{z}
, so it will in fact suffice to only use
(C1)(z)
.
Reasoning as in the mesoscopic case (see (
2.51
) with
\lambda=1
), but using only the condition
(C1)(z)
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{**}\Bigl[{G_{t}(z)};{G_{t}(w)}\Bigr]\leq Cr_{0}^{\frac{(2\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}},

(2.52)

where as before
r_{0}=t^{\kappa(\alpha_{0})}
and
C>0
is uniform in
z,w
and
z_{i}
. Thus, we estimate the contribution of the part
r_{0}/2\leq|z-w|\leq 100r_{0}
by

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{0})}

\displaystyle\int_{r_{0}/2\leq|z-w|\leq 100r_{0}}\mathbb{E}^{**}\Bigl[\textbf{1}_{G_{t}(z)}\textbf{1}_{G_{t}(w)}\Bigr]|z-w|^{-\gamma^{2}}dwdz

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{0})}\int_{r_{0}/2\leq|z-w|\leq 100r_{0}}r_{0}^{\frac{(2\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}}r_{0}^{-\gamma^{2}}dwdz

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{0})}r_{0}^{\frac{(2\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}+2-\gamma^{2}}dz

(2.53)

\displaystyle\leq Cr_{0}^{\frac{(2\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}+2-\gamma^{2}+2\mathsf{x}}.

(2.54)

We want to show that this gives a larger exponent than
2\Delta/\kappa(\alpha_{0})
. To prove this we rely on the following two elementary inequalities:

\begin{cases}\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}}{2}<2(1-\sqrt{\mathsf{x}})^{2}\\
2(1-\sqrt{\mathsf{x}})^{2}<2(1-\mathsf{x}).\end{cases}

(2.55)

The second inequality is easier to check: we cancel the factors of two and take the square root of both sides (since they are positive) and we need to check that
1-\sqrt{x}<\sqrt{1-x}
, or, equivalently,

, which is straightforward to check after squaring both sides.

For the first of the inequalities we observe that since
\alpha_{0}=Q-\sqrt{Q^{2}-4(1-\mathsf{x})}
from (
2.12
),

\alpha_{0}
is maximized at
\gamma=2
by monotonicity
(as it is monotone decreasing in

).
Hence

. Using that, we get

\displaystyle\frac{(2\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}+2-\gamma^{2}+2\mathsf{x}>4\mathsf{x}+(\alpha_{0}-\gamma)^{2}=2\Delta/\kappa(\alpha_{0}).

Therefore, the contribution of the ”intermediate regime”
r_{0}/2\leq|z-w|\leq 100r_{0}
is negligible: that is, the right hand side of (
2.54
) is negligible compared to

.

Thus, it remains to treat the case
|z-w|\leq r_{0}/2
. Fix

such that
|z-w|\leq r_{0}/2
,

. Then if

, we have
by (

)

\displaystyle\Big|h_{e^{-s}}(z)-h^{\prime}_{e^{-s}}(z)-2\gamma s\Big|\leq C,

(2.56)

for some explicit constant

. In other words, for points

at distance

from

,

, and

. In particular, applying this to

, the condition
(C1)(z)
implies

\displaystyle(C1)^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\qquad X^{\prime}_{s_{z}}\in[(\alpha_{0}-2\gamma+o(1))s_{z}-C,(\alpha_{0}-2\gamma+o(1))s_{z}+C],

(2.57)

where the

-terms vanishes when

and are uniform in
z,w,n
.

We now take
s=n-1
in (
2.56
) and in condition
(C2)
from (
2.3
) to obtain a condition on the Brownian motion

. However, it is more natural to phrase that condition in terms of the parameter

defining Condition
(C2)
in (

(1+q)s_{z}=s
.

\displaystyle(C2)^{\prime\prime}\qquad X^{\prime}_{s}

\displaystyle\leq(\alpha_{0}-\beta)\log(1/r_{0})+s(\beta-2\gamma)+C

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{0}-2\gamma+o(1))s_{z}+(\beta-2\gamma+o(1))qs_{z}+C,

(2.58)

where
C>0
is uniform in
z,w,n
and

and the

-terms vanishes when

and is also uniform in

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{(1+q)s_{z}}-X^{\prime}_{s_{z}}\leq(\beta-2\gamma+o(1))qs_{z}+C,

. As

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{**}[(C2)|(C1)]\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(\beta-2\gamma+o(1))^{2}q^{2}(\log 1/r_{0})^{2}+o(1)}{2q\log 1/r_{0}}\Bigr)=r_{0}^{\frac{(\beta-2\gamma)^{2}q}{2}+o(1)},

where all

-terms are uniform in

. As the probability of

can be estimated as in (

), we get in total

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{**}\Bigl[(C1)\cap(C2)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq r_{0}^{\frac{(2\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}+o(1)}r_{0}^{\frac{(2\gamma-\beta)^{2}q}{2}+o(1)}.

Now we are able to compute the contribution of the microscopic part. Using

|A_{n}(z)|\leq|B_{e^{-n}(z)}|\leq\pi s_{z}^{2(1+q)}\leq Cr_{0}^{2(1+q)},

(recall that

we get:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iint_{z,w\in\Omega^{t}:|z-w|<r_{0}/2}\mathbb{P}^{**}\Bigl[{G_{t}(z)};{G_{t}(w)}\Bigr]|z-w|^{-\gamma^{2}}dzdw

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega^{t}}\sum_{n=\log 1/r_{0}-O(1)}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}^{**}\Bigl[(C1)(z)\cap(C2)(z)\Bigr]|A_{n}(z)|r_{0}^{-(1+q)\gamma^{2}}dz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega^{t}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r_{0}^{\frac{(2\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}+o(1)}r_{0}^{\frac{(2\gamma-\beta)^{2}q}{2}+o(1)}r_{0}^{(2-\gamma^{2})(1+q)}dz

where all

and

\displaystyle f(q):=2\mathsf{x}+\frac{(2\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}+\frac{(2\gamma-\beta)^{2}q}{2}+(2-\gamma^{2})(1+q).

One can easily check that

\alpha_{0}

so close to

\displaystyle 2-\gamma^{2}+\frac{(2\gamma-\beta)^{2}}{2}>2-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}-\varepsilon,\qquad 2-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}-2\varepsilon>0,\qquad 2-3\varepsilon

\displaystyle\geq 2\mathsf{x}+\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}}{2}.

Then, we get

\displaystyle 2\mathsf{x}+

\displaystyle\frac{(2\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}+\frac{(2\gamma-\beta)^{2}q}{2}+(2-\gamma^{2})(1+q)

\displaystyle=2\mathsf{x}+\frac{(2\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}-\frac{(2\gamma-\beta)^{2}}{2}+\Bigl(2-\gamma^{2}+\frac{(2\gamma-\beta)^{2}}{2}\Bigr)(q+1)

\displaystyle\geq 2\mathsf{x}+\frac{(2\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}-\frac{(2\gamma-\beta)^{2}}{2}+2-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}-2\varepsilon+(1+q)\varepsilon

\displaystyle\geq 4\mathsf{x}+\gamma^{2}-2\gamma\alpha_{0}+\varepsilon+\alpha_{0}^{2}+(1+q)\varepsilon

\displaystyle=4\mathsf{x}+(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}+\varepsilon+(1+q)\varepsilon.

As

, where the

-term is uniform in

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r_{0}^{f(q_{n})+o(1)}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r_{0}^{4\mathsf{x}+(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}+\varepsilon}e^{-\varepsilon n(1+o(1))}=t^{2\Delta+\varepsilon}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\varepsilon n(1+o(1))}\leq t^{2\Delta+\varepsilon/2},

which is negligible compared to the contribution coming from the mesoscopic part and thus finishes the proof.

More precisely, by Lemma

(2.59)

where

Let us write

Almost independence.
First, we show that all the
{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)
are almost independent, by comparing their values to those associated with fields

where we turn off the common harmonic parts in balls of radius
4R(t)
around each

More precisely, we use the Markov property on the union of the

disjoint balls

\displaystyle h=h^{0}+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i},

where these terms are independent of one another,
h^{0}
is harmonic on
\cup_{i=1}^{N(t)}B_{4R(t)}(z_{i})

is a Dirichlet GFF on

We first control the fluctuations and the overall value of the harmonic parts in each ball, showing it is not too large.

Lemma 2.9
.

We define the good event
G:=G_{1}\cap G_{2}
, where

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle:=\Bigl\{\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq N(t)}\displaystyle\sup_{z\in B_{2R(t)}(z_{i})}|h^{0}(z)-h^{0}(z_{i})|\leq\sqrt{\log(1/t)}\Bigr\},

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle:=\Bigl\{\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq N(t)}|h^{0}_{4R(t)}(z_{i})|\leq 10\log(N(t))\Bigr\}.

Then for some constant
C>0

\mathbb{P}[G^{c}]\leq CN(t)^{-49}\to 0
as
t\to 0
.

Proof.

Let us start showing that
G_{1}
has probability tending to
1
. By Lemma
2.4
we get uniformly on all balls

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl[\displaystyle\sup_{z\in B_{2R(t)}(z_{i})}|h^{0}(z)-h^{0}(z_{i})|\geq\lambda\Bigr]\leq ce^{-c^{\prime}\lambda^{2}},

for
\lambda>1
and constants

. Using that, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[G^{c}]=\mathbb{P}\Bigl[\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq N(t)}\displaystyle\sup_{z\in B_{2R(t)}(z_{i})}|h^{0}(z)-h^{0}(z_{i})|>\sqrt{\log(1/t)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N(t)}\mathbb{P}\Bigl[\displaystyle\sup_{z\in B_{2R(t)}(z_{i})}|h^{0}(z)-h^{0}(z_{i})|>\sqrt{\log(1/t)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq cN(t)e^{-c^{\prime}\log(1/t)}=N(t)t^{c^{\prime}}\rightarrow 0,

as

diverges to infinity slower than any polynomial in

\mathbb{P}(G_{1})\to 1
.

Next, we need to control the circle averages

is a centered Gaussian with variance

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[G_{2}^{c}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Bigl[\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq N(t)}|h^{0}(z)_{4R(t)}(\omega)|>10\log(N(t))\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N(t)}\mathbb{P}\Bigl[|h^{0}_{4R(t)}(z_{i})|>10\log(N(t))\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq CN(t)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{100\log(N(t))^{2}}{2\log(\frac{1}{4R(t)})}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq CN(t)\exp\Bigl(-50\log(N(t))\Bigr)

\displaystyle=CN(t)^{-49}\to 0,\qquad\textnormal{ as }t\to 0.

Thus,

and the lemma is proved.
∎

We now want to compare the restricted heat content

\displaystyle{\mathbf{K}}_{i}^{h_{i}}(t):=\int_{\Omega^{t}\cap B_{R(t)}(z_{i})}{\mathbf{P}}_{z}[\sigma^{i}_{\Omega}<t]\textbf{1}_{G_{t}}\mu_{\gamma}^{h_{i}}(dz),

and

on

. As

are all independent, also the

are independent of each other.

.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

The upper bound on
{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t)
follows from the first moment computation (Proposition
2.1
) and Markov’s inequality. We therefore concentrate on the lower bound. Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Note that by Lemma
2.10

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t)\leq t^{\Delta+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}({\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t)\leq t^{\Delta+\varepsilon},\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq N(t))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(G^{c})+\mathbb{P}[t^{o(1)}{\mathbf{K}}^{h_{i}}_{i}(t^{1+o(1)})-X_{i}\leq t^{\Delta+\varepsilon}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq N(t);G].

\mathbb{P}[\max_{1\leq i\leq N(t)}X_{i}>t^{\Delta+\varepsilon};G]\to 0.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t)\leq t^{\Delta+\varepsilon})

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\max_{1\leq i\leq N(t)}{\mathbf{K}}^{h_{i}}_{i}(t)\leq t^{\Delta+\varepsilon}]\to 0.

(2.61)

As all
{\mathbf{K}}_{i}^{h_{i}}
are independent, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\max_{1\leq i\leq N(t)}{\mathbf{K}}^{h_{i}}_{i}(t)\leq t^{\Delta+\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[{\mathbf{K}}^{h_{1}}_{1}(t)\leq t^{\Delta+\varepsilon}]^{N(t)}.

(2.62)

On the other hand, by Lemma
2.10
again, for each
1\leq i\leq N(t)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[{\mathbf{K}}^{h_{i}}_{i}(t)\leq t^{\Delta+\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(G^{c})+\mathbb{P}({\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega,z_{i}}(t^{1+o(1)})\leq t^{\Delta+\varepsilon+o(1)})

\displaystyle\leq N(t)^{-49}+1-t^{\epsilon(t)}R(t)^{\alpha_{0}^{2}/2}

by (
2.59
) and Lemma
2.9
. Plugging back into (
2.62
), and using that
1-x\leq e^{-x}
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\max_{1\leq i\leq N(t)}{\mathbf{K}}^{h_{i}}_{i}(t)\leq t^{\Delta+\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle\leq[1-R(t)^{\alpha_{0}^{2}/2}t^{\epsilon(t)}+N(t)^{-49}]^{N(t)}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-N(t)t^{\epsilon(t)}R(t)^{\alpha_{0}^{2}/2}+N(t)^{-48}\right)\to 0.

In the last line, to conclude that the right hand side tends to zero, we used the fact that

R(t)^{\alpha_{0}^{2}/2}t^{\epsilon(t)}N(t)\to\infty.

(2.63)

Let us justify why this holds. Indeed, given (
2.55
), we have

R(t)^{\alpha_{0}^{2}/2}N(t)t^{\epsilon(t)}\geq R(t)^{-\eta}\cdot R(t)^{2(1-\mathsf{x})}N(t)t^{\epsilon(t)}=R(t)^{-\eta/2}t^{\varepsilon(t)}\to\infty

for
\eta=2(1-\mathsf{x})-\alpha_{0}^{2}/2>0
, and for the conclusion we used the fact that, in Assumption
1.3
,
N(t)\geq R(t)^{-2(1-\mathsf{x})+\zeta}
with
\zeta=\eta/2
. (Note that this implicitly fixes the value of
\zeta
to be used in the definition of
\partial_{R(t)}
in Assumption
1.3
, as well as the choice of
R(t)
, taken so that
R(t)^{-\eta/2}\gg t^{\epsilon(t)}
; this is possible as
\epsilon(t)
does not depend on
R(t)
.) This completes the proof of (
2.63
). Altogether, the proof of Theorem
1.3
is therefore complete.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

The argument shows that it would have been sufficient to replace Assumption
1.10
by the following weaker bound

N\geq R^{-2(1-\sqrt{\mathsf{x}})^{2}-\delta}
for some
\delta>0
, or indeed the even weaker bound
N\geq R^{-\alpha_{0}^{2}/2-\delta}
for some
\delta>0
. However, as this seems unnatural (compared to the assumption stated in (
1.10
), which just corresponds to the dimension of
\partial\Omega
) we left the assumption as such in Theorem
1.3
.

3
Heat trace asymptotics

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
, which deals with asymptotics of the (expected) heat trace

\mathbf{H}(t)=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{p}^{\Omega}_{t}(x,x)\mu(dz).

As already mentioned in the introduction, our starting point is the in-out decomposition (
1.13
), which we recall here for convenience:

\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\Omega}(x,x)=\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}(x,x)-\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x),

(3.1)

where
\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}(x,x)
is the heat kernel in the full plane, and
\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)
can informally be viewed as the heat kernel from
x
to
x
in time
t
, restricted to trajectories that leave
\Omega
before time
t
. In other words,

\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)=\mathbf{p}^{\mathbb{C}}_{t}(x,x){\mathbf{P}}^{\gamma}_{x\overset{t}{\to}x}(\sigma_{\Omega}<t)

{\mathbf{P}}^{\gamma}_{x\overset{t}{\to}x}
is the law of a
(\gamma-)
Liouville Brownian loop
of duration
t>0
. By definition, the latter (and more generally the
\gamma
-Liouville Brownian bridge of duration
t
from
x
to
y
) is the unique reversible law on path space
C([0,t];\mathbb{C})
such that, for any
0\leq s<t
,

\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{P}}^{\gamma}_{x\overset{t}{\to}y}}{\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{P}}^{\gamma}_{x}}\right)|_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}=\frac{{\mathbf{p}}_{t-s}^{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{X}_{s},y)}{{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}(x,y)}.

(3.2)

(Here, as before,
{\mathbf{P}}^{\gamma}_{x}
is the law of a Liouville Brownian motion starting from
x
, and
\mathcal{F}_{s}=\sigma(\mathbf{X}_{u},u\leq s))
is the canonical filtration on path space, and
\mathbf{X}_{s}(\omega)=\omega_{s}
is the standard evaluation, or coordinate, map.) As already mentioned, the law of a Liouville loop or Liouville Brownian bridge can
not
be simply described as the time-change of an ordinary Brownian bridge from
x
to
y
.

Theorem
1.3
concerns the expected value of the heat trace. When we take the expectation in (
3.1
), we find

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{H}}(t)]=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}(x,x)]dx-\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)]dx.

(3.3)

Our strategy will be to estimate these two terms (which we refer to as respectively the
bulk
and the
boundary
terms) separately:

•

(Bulk term). It is not hard to see that, for any
x\in\Omega
, (e.g., by adapting the proof of
[
12
]
) that
\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}(x,x)]\sim\tfrac{c_{\gamma}}{t}
. In fact we will need something more quantitative, and show that the difference
t\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}(x,x)]-c_{\gamma}
decays to 0 faster than any polynomial (FTP) as
t\to 0
.

•

(Boundary term). On the other hand, the second term is (at least intuitively) related to the behaviour of the heat content. Indeed, roughly speaking, we can expect that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)]

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[\mathbf{p}^{\mathbb{C}}_{t}(x,x){\mathbf{P}}_{x}(\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t)]

\displaystyle\approx\text{const.}(1/t)\times\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(\nu(\tau_{\Omega})<t)

so that this second term should be roughly
(1/t)\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t)]=t^{-(1-\Delta)+o(1)}
, by Theorem
1.3
. In making the above approximation, we identified the probability that a Liouville Brownian
bridge
of duration
t
leaves the domain
\Omega
, with the probability that a Liouville Brownian
motion
leaves that domain in the same time, and we also pretended that the two terms in the expectation are independent.

Combined together, these two asymptotics establish Theorem
1.2
. Although the above is quite convincing, making this precise requires some nontrivial arguments; in fact, we have to argue in a very indirect manner.

Before starting the proof, we recall for future reference the “bridge decomposition”, initially proved in
[
38
]
, and which plays a crucial role in the analysis of the LQG heat kernel (playing a crucial role e.g. in
[
8
]
and
[
12
]
). Indeed, while the heat kernel
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,y)
is difficult to handle directly, the bridge decomposition gives a handle on
time-integrals
of the LQG heat kernel, relating it to the ordinary heat kernel. More precisely, the bridge decomposition is the following statement (see Theorem 3.4 in
[
38
]
): if
G:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty]
is a nonnegative measurable function, then

\int_{0}^{\infty}G(t){\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,y)dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbf{E}}_{x\overset{u}{\to}y}[G(\nu(u))]p_{u}(x,y)du,

(3.4)

where
p_{u}(x,y)=e^{-|x-y|^{2}/(2u)}(2\pi u)^{-1}
is the ordinary (Gaussian) heat kernel.

This is both natural (as Liouville Brownian motion is a time-change of ordinary Brownian motion, a fact which expresses the conformally invariant nature of Brownian motion in dimension two) and surprising, in that a Liouville bridge is
not
the time-change of an ordinary Brownian bridge.

3.1
Scale-invariance of the heat kernel on the quantum cone

We start with some estimates which give a control on the
n
th moment of the (on-diagonal) heat kernel, showing in particular finiteness of this
n
th moment. The arguments will hinge on a delicate comparison between the heat kernel with respect to an environment given by a whole plane GFF (with a
\gamma
-log singularity at the origin), and a
\gamma
-quantum cone, respectively. Roughly speaking, the advantage of the former is that Gaussian computations are possible, while in the latter we can take advantage of some exact scale invariance properties, as will be seen later.

We fix some notations. Let
h
denote a realisation (under the law
\mathbb{P}
) of a GFF on the whole plane, normalised so that it has zero unit circle average. For
x\in B_{0}(1/2)
, let
h^{*}_{x}=h+\gamma G(x,\cdot)
. Thus
h^{*}_{x}
has law
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
. When the reference point
x
is clear we will simply write
h^{*}
. Associated to
h^{*}
there is a heat-kernel, which we write as
{\mathbf{p}}^{*}_{t}(z,w)
. To ease comparison with the case of the quantum cone below we recall the radial decomposition of this field (see Lemma 7.5 in
[
10
]
). Let
H_{\mathrm{rad}}(\mathbb{C})
denote the completion, with respect to the ordinary Dirichlet inner product, of smooth functions with compact support in
\mathbb{C}
, which are constant on each circle centered at the origin. Let
H_{\mathrm{circ}}(\mathbb{C})
denote the completion of those functions whose circle average on each circle centered at the origin is zero. We recall that we have the orthogonal decomposition
H^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{C})=H_{\mathrm{rad}}(\mathbb{C})\oplus H_{\mathrm{circ}}(\mathbb{C})
. This leads to the independent decomposition of
h
:

h=h_{\mathrm{rad}}+h_{\mathrm{circ}}

where
h_{\mathrm{rad}}
is a continuous function on
\mathbb{C}
which is radially symmetric, having the property that if
|z|=r=e^{-s}
for some
s\in\mathbb{R}
(thus
r>0
), then

h_{\mathrm{rad}}(z)=X_{s};

with
(X_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{R}}
a two-sided Brownian motion; that is,
X_{0}=0
, and
(X(s))_{s\in[0,\infty)}
and
(X(-s))_{s\in[0,\infty)}
two independent standard Brownian motions. Note that if
x=0
then
h^{*}=h^{*}_{x}
has a similar description
h^{*}=h^{*}_{\mathrm{rad}}+h^{*}_{\mathrm{circ}}
, with radial part given by
h^{*}_{\mathrm{rad}}(z)=X^{*}_{s}
whenever
|z|=e^{-s}
,

X^{*}_{s}=X_{s}+\gamma(s\vee 0);\quad s\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.5)

In order to make the comparison with the quantum cone easier, it is easier to parametrise the punctured plane
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
by the cylinder
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R}\times(0,2\pi)
via the map
z\in{\mathcal{S}}\mapsto e^{-z}
(thereby identifying the lines
\{\Im(z)=0\}
with
\{\Im(z)=2\pi\}
). Applying the LQG change of coordinates, the fields
h
and
h^{*}
become, respectively,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}h_{\mathrm{rad}}(z)&=X_{s}-Qs\\
h_{\mathrm{rad}}^{*}(z)&=X^{*}_{s}-Qs=X_{s}-(Q-\gamma 1_{s\geq 0})s,\end{cases}\quad z\in{\mathcal{S}},\Re(z)=s.

(3.6)

where, with a slight abuse of notation, we employ the same notation in for
h_{\mathrm{rad}}
in
{\mathcal{S}}
and in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, and
X_{s}
is a two-sided Brownian motion. Their circular part
h_{\mathrm{circ}}=h_{\mathrm{circ}}^{*}
is independent of their radial part and can be chosen to be identical in both cases.

Now we carefully introduce the
\gamma
-quantum cone, and more generally the
\alpha
-quantum cone (for
0\leq\alpha<Q
, corresponding to the “thick” regime), a field which we will denote by
\tilde{h}
, or
\tilde{h}^{\alpha}
if we want to emphasise the dependence on
\alpha
. We again parameterise the punctured plane by
{\mathcal{S}}
, and decompose it into a radial and angular components,
\tilde{h}={\tilde{h}}_{\mathrm{rad}}+{\tilde{h}}_{\mathrm{circ}}
. By definition of
\tilde{h}
,
{\tilde{h}}_{\mathrm{rad}}
and
{\tilde{h}}_{\mathrm{circ}}
are independent, with
{\tilde{h}}_{\mathrm{circ}}=h_{\mathrm{circ}}
in law. As for the radial part,

\displaystyle{\tilde{h}}_{\mathrm{rad}}(z)=:\tilde{X}_{s}-(Q-\alpha)s,\quad z\in{\mathcal{S}};\Re(z)=s

(3.7)

where
(\tilde{X}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
and
(\tilde{X}_{s})_{s\leq 0}
are independent, with laws given respectively by a Brownian motion motion, and another Brownian motion conditioned so that
\tilde{X}_{|s|}+(Q-\alpha)|s|\geq 0
for all
s\leq 0
.

Notations.
It will sometimes be useful to distinguish the different underlying environments for the heat kernel (be it the quantum cone
\tilde{h}
or the whole-plane GFF
h^{*}
) either by emphasising this at the level of random variables (leading to heat kernels written respectively as
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)
and
{\mathbf{p}}^{*}_{t}(x,x)
) or at the level of the underlying laws for the field, which are denoted by
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
and
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
respectively. In the latter case (i.e., when the law is explicitly specified, say in an expectation) we simply write
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)
.

Our first observation is that, on the quantum cone, there is an
exact
form of scale-invariance for the heat kernel, which leads to extremely useful scaling relations. For instance, we will see that we have the
exact
relation:

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{p}_{t}^{{\mathcal{C}}}(x,x)]=\frac{c_{\gamma}}{t},

Although scale-invariance for the area and boundary measures of the quantum cone is well known (see
[
18
,
10
]
) the remarkable consequences that this has for the heat kernel seem to not have been noticed before. We believe this technique will have other uses in the future, so this step is interesting in its own right. More generally, the result below is valid for the notion of
\alpha
-quantum cone, although we will need it in this paper only for the case
\alpha=\gamma
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,Q)
and let
\tilde{h}=\tilde{h}^{\alpha}
be an
\alpha
-quantum cone, with associated heat kernel
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t}(z,w)=\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{\alpha}_{t}(z,w)
. Let
x=0
. Then

\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{\alpha}_{t}(x,x)\overset{d}{=}\frac{1}{t}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{\alpha}_{1}(x,x).

(3.8)

Proof.

The idea is simply to exploit the scale-invariance of the quantum cone, which we first recall. To do so it is useful to indicate the explicit dependence of the area measure
\mu=\mu_{\tilde{h}}
with respect to a specific realisation of the quantum cone,
\tilde{h}
. The scale-invariance of the quantum cone (Theorem 7.18 in
[
10
]
) states that the quantum surface described by
(\tilde{h}+C,\mathbb{C},0,\infty)
is identical in law to that described by
(\tilde{h},\mathbb{C},0,\infty)
, for any
C\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus
\tilde{h}+C
is another quantum cone (in an embedding which is not the usual “unit circle embedding”, but embeddings are irrelevant as all quantities of interest here are LQG invariants). Note that

\mu_{\tilde{h}}(A)=e^{-\gamma C}\mu_{\tilde{h}+C}(A)

for any Borel set
A
. Note also that if
(B_{t},t\geq 0)
is a fixed two-dimensional standard Brownian motion, then the quantum clocks with respect to
\tilde{h}
and
\tilde{h}+C
satisfy a similar relation:

\nu_{\tilde{h}+C,B}(s)=e^{\gamma C}\nu_{\tilde{h},B}(s);\quad s\geq 0.

Thus the corrsponding Liouville Brownian motions
Z=Z^{\tilde{h},B}(s)
associated to
\tilde{h}
and Brownian path
B
satisfy:

Z^{\tilde{h},B}(t)=Z^{\tilde{h}+C,B}(te^{\gamma C}),\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

Let
t>0
, and let us fix
C=\log(1/t)/\gamma
, so
e^{\gamma C}=1/t
.
It follows that (using continuity of the heat kernel with respect to the space variable and the dominated convergence theorem)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}^{\tilde{h}}_{t}(x,x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(Z^{\tilde{h},B}(t)\in B_{x}(\varepsilon))}{\mu_{\tilde{h}}(B_{x}(\varepsilon))}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(Z^{\tilde{h}+C,B}(1)\in B_{x}(\varepsilon))}{t\mu_{\tilde{h}+C}(B_{x}(\varepsilon))}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}{\mathbf{p}}^{\tilde{h}+C}_{1}(x,x),

as desired.
∎

Before continuing with the rest of the proof of Theorem
1.3
we illustrate the interest of this idea by resolving a conjecture, stated in
[
12
]
, about the annealed convergence of the heat kernel in
\Omega
,
{\mathbf{p}}^{\Omega}_{t}(x,x)
(i.e., with Dirichlet boundary conditions in
\Omega
) under
\mathbb{P}^{*}
. The conjecture was in fact stated for a Dirichlet GFF in
\Omega
under the measure
\mathbb{P}^{*}
, rather than a full plane GFF under the measure
\mathbb{P}^{*}
, but as will be clear below this difference in boundary conditions of the field has no impact on the result and its proof.

Corollary 3.2
.

Consider the random variable
{\mathbf{p}}^{\Omega}_{t}(x,x)
under the law
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
, where
x\in\Omega
is arbitrary. Then

t{\mathbf{p}}^{\Omega}_{t}(x,x)\to X

(3.9)

in distribution as
t\to 0
. The random variable
X
has the law of
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{1}(0,0)
.

Proof.

This follows directly from Proposition
3.1
, the local convergence of the pointed quantum surface
(h+C,x)
as
C\to\infty
to a
\gamma-
quantum cone as
C\to\infty
(in the sense of total variation, say – see Theorem 7.18 in
[
10
]
), and the fact that
\mathbf{p}_{t}(x,x)
is measurable with respect to the local environment near
x
when
t\to 0
. Details are left to the reader; alternatively, we provide a complete argument (which yields quantitative bounds on the error) in Lemma
3.6
.
∎

3.2
Heat kernel moments

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
x\in B_{0}(1/2)
. For all
n\geq 1
and for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
c_{n}
such that,
under the rooted law
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
we have, for all
0<t<1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}\bigl[\bigl({\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)\bigr)^{n}\bigr]\leq c_{n}(1/t^{1+\varepsilon})^{n}.

In particular,
\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}\bigl[\bigl({\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)\bigr)^{n}\bigr]^{1/n}\leq t^{-1+o(1)}
as
t\to 0
(and is finite).

A similar, more precise estimate holds in the case of a quantum cone: there exists
\tilde{c}_{n}\in(0,\infty)
such that, for
x=0
, we have the identity

\tilde{\mathbb{E}}[({\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x))^{n}]=c_{n}(1/t)^{n}.

(3.10)

In particular,
\tilde{\mathbb{E}}[({\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x))^{n}]<\infty
for all
n\geq 0
and for all
t>0
, and
x=0
.

Proof.

The proof is similar in both cases (of
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
and
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
) and we do them in parallel. We will first treat the case
x=0
and then explain how to adapt the argument to the case of
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
with general
x\in B_{0}(1/2)
.

As the heat kernel is an LQG invariant (i.e., invariant under the LQG change of coordinates) we are free to use whichever parametrisation of the surface we wish; it will be convenient to switch between the whole plane model and the strip model, for the calculations below. As mentioned above, we will do so without indicating this in the notations.

Let
n\geq 1
. We recall that by the general theory of Dirichlet forms,
t\mapsto\mathbf{p}_{t}(x,x)
is monotone decreasing in
t>0
, and for each fixed
t>0
,
y\in\mathbb{C}\mapsto\mathbf{p}_{t}(x,y)
is maximised at
y=x
. Thus

\mathbf{p}_{t}(x,x)\leq\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\mathbf{p}_{u}(x,x)du.

Thus raising to the power
n\geq 1
and
bridge decomposition
(
3.4
), as well as Fubini’s theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{*}[\mathbf{p}_{t}(x,x)^{n}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2^{n}}{t^{n}}\mathbb{E}^{*}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\ldots\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{u_{1}\in[t/2,t]}\ldots\mathbf{1}_{u_{n}\in[t/2,t]}\mathbf{p}_{u_{1}}(x,x)\ldots\mathbf{p}_{u_{n}}(x,x)du_{1}\ldots du_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2^{n}}{t^{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ldots\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}^{*}\otimes{\mathbf{E}}_{x\overset{u_{1}}{\rightarrow}x,\ldots x\overset{u_{n}}{\rightarrow}x}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\nu^{1}(u_{1})\in[t/2,t]}\ldots\mathbf{1}_{\nu^{n}(u_{n})\in[t/2,t]}\right]\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}u_{1}\ldots u_{n}}du_{1}\ldots du_{n}

where
{\mathbf{E}}_{x\overset{u_{1}}{\rightarrow}x,\ldots x\overset{u_{n}}{\rightarrow}x}
is the law of
n

independent
ordinary Brownian bridges from
x
to
x
, of respective durations
u_{1},\ldots u_{n}
; and
\nu^{1}(\cdot),\ldots,\nu^{n}(\cdot)
are the quantum clocks accumulated by each of these bridges in the
common
environment
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
.

Applying Hölder’s inequality, we get

\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[\mathbf{p}_{t}(x,x)^{n}]\leq\frac{1}{\pi^{n}t^{n}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{u}\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x\overset{u}{\rightarrow}x}\bigl[\nu(u)\in[t/2,t]\bigr]^{\frac{1}{n}}du\right)^{n}.

(3.11)

In the case of the quantum cone, we recall that by scaling (Proposition
3.1
) it suffices to take
t=1
. The bound (
3.11
) also holds with
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
replacing
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
.

Thus it suffices to control the integral on the right hand side of (
3.11
) (and its analogue for the quantum cone in the case
t=1
), which we break into
u\in(0,t)
,
u\in(t,1)
, and
u\in(1,\infty)
. When
u\in(t,1)
we simply bound the probability by 1 so the integral is at most
\log(1/t)
.

So let us assume
u>1
, and observe that, by monotonicity of
\nu
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x\overset{u}{\rightarrow}x}\bigl[\nu(u)\in[t/2,t]\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x\overset{u}{\rightarrow}x}\bigl[\nu(u)\leq t\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x\overset{u}{\rightarrow}x}\bigl[\nu(u/2)\leq t\bigr].

Now, the law of a Brownian bridge of duration
u
, restricted to
[0,u/2]
, is absolutely continuous with respect to that of an ordinary Brownian motion over the same interval of time, with Radon–Nikodym derivative bounded by a universal constant
C>0
. Thus for any
q>0
, using also the monotonicity of
\nu
and Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x\overset{u}{\rightarrow}x}\bigl[\nu(u)\in[t/2,t]\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x}\bigl[\nu(u/2)\leq t\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq Ct^{q}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{E}}_{x}[\nu(u/2)^{-q}].

(3.12)

We will thus need to control the negative moments of
\nu(u)
. We defer this to slightly later, explaining first how we take care of the contribution to the integral coming from
u<t
.
In that case,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x\overset{u}{\rightarrow}x}\bigl[t/2\leq\nu(u)\leq t\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x\overset{u}{\to}x}[\nu(u)\geq t/2]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x\overset{u}{\to}x}[\nu(u/2)\geq t/4]+\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x\overset{u}{\to}x}[\nu(u/2,u)\geq t/4]

where
\nu(u/2,u)
is the quantum clock accumulated by the Brownian bridge during the interval
[u/2,u]
(i.e.,
\nu(u/2,u)=\nu(u)-\nu(u/2)
). By reversibility of the Brownian bridge, and by the same absolute continuity of Brownian bridge with respect to ordinary Brownian motion which was already used in (
3.12
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x\overset{u}{\rightarrow}x}\bigl[t/2\leq\nu(u)\leq t\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq 2C\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x}[\nu(u/2)\geq t/4]

\displaystyle\leq 2C\big(\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x}[\nu(u/2\wedge\tau_{1})\geq t/4]+{\mathbf{P}}_{x}[\tau_{1}<u/2]\big),

where we recall that
\tau_{1}=\inf\{t>0:|X_{t}-x|>1\}
is the first time
X
(the Brownian motion, here) leaves a ball of radius 1 around its starting point. Note that
{\mathbf{P}}_{x}[\tau_{1}<u/2]\to 0
as
u\to 0
FTP (in fact, exponentially fast in
(-1/u)
, by the explicit form of the Gaussian density function), so it suffices to concentrate on the first term. By Markov’s inequality, we again have for all
q>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x}[\nu(u/2\wedge\tau_{1})\geq t/4]

\displaystyle\leq(t/4)^{-q}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{E}}_{x}[\nu(u/2\wedge\tau_{1})^{q}].

(3.13)

Altogether, we get, for any choice of
q,q^{\prime}>0
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{u}\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x\overset{u}{\rightarrow}x}\bigl[\nu(u)\in[t/2,t]\bigr]^{\frac{1}{n}}du

\displaystyle\leq\log(1/t)+C

\displaystyle\quad+C_{q,n}t^{q/n}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{u}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{E}}_{x}[\nu(u/2)^{-q}]^{1/n}du

\displaystyle\quad+t^{-q^{\prime}/n}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{u}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{E}}_{x}[\nu(u/2\wedge\tau_{1})^{q^{\prime}}]^{1/n}du

(3.14)

We now state the relevant controls over the moments needed to complete this proof, starting with the control at “large” scales (
u>1
):

Lemma 3.4
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, for all
q>0
there exists a constant
C=C(\gamma,q,\varepsilon)
such that for
all

u>0
,

\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{E}}_{x}[\nu(u)^{-q}]\leq Cu^{\xi^{*}(-q)/2+\varepsilon},

where
\xi^{*}(-q)=-q(2-\gamma^{2}/2)+\gamma^{2}q^{2}/2
. The same estimate applies to a quantum cone: for all
u>0
,

\tilde{\mathbb{E}}\otimes{\mathbf{E}}_{x}[\nu(u)^{-q}]\leq Cu^{\xi^{*}(-q)/2+\varepsilon}

The interest and potential difficulty here lies in the fact that we want a result valid for
all

u>0
, i.e., including at large scales. The function
\xi^{*}
can be viewed as the
\gamma
-quantum cone multifractal spectrum (or that of a whole plane GFF with
\gamma
-log singularity at
all
scales – including large scales, unlike (
3.5
) where the logarithmic singularity is only present at scales less than unity).

Proof.

See Appendix
A
. ∎

Thus, applying Lemma
3.4
with
q>0
small enough that
\xi^{*}(-q)>0
(which is possible when
\gamma<2
), and plugging into (
3.2
) we see that the integral in the second line is bounded by a constant (say
C
), so this entire term is bounded by
Ct^{1/n}\leq C
.

The control at small scales (
u<1
) is provided by the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

For all
q>0
there exists a constant
C=C(\gamma,q)
such that for all
0<u<1
,

\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{E}}_{x}[\nu(u/2\wedge\tau_{1})^{q}]\leq Cu^{\xi^{*}(q)/2}

where as before,
\xi^{*}(q)=(2-\gamma^{2}/2)q-\gamma^{2}q^{2}/2
. The same estimate applies to a
\gamma
-quantum cone.

Again, the proof is deferred to the appendix. Consequently, the integral in the third line of (
3.2
) is convergent and thus (crudely) bounded for any
q^{\prime}>0
, by some constant
C_{q^{\prime},\gamma,n}
.
Altogether, this shows that the integral in (
3.2
) is bounded by
C(1+\log(1/t)+t^{-q^{\prime}/n})\leq Ct^{-q^{\prime}/n}
for
t
small enough.
Thus, combining our estimates and recalling (
3.11
),
we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[\mathbf{p}_{t}(x,x)^{n}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{t^{n}}t^{-q^{\prime}}

for
t
sufficiently small. Since
q^{\prime}>0
is arbitrary, this concludes the proof of Proposition
3.3
.
∎

3.3
Quantitative bulk convergence

Overview.
In this section we are concerned with the (expected) “bulk” term in the in-out decomposition (
3.1
), namely
\mathbb{E}[\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{p}^{\mathbb{C}}_{t}(x,x)\mu(dx)]
. Recall that we already know (by an easy adaptation of the results in
[
12
]
) that

\mathbb{E}[\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{p}^{\mathbb{C}}_{t}(x,x)\mu(dx)]\sim\frac{c_{\gamma}}{t}|\Omega|,

(3.15)

and our goal in this subsection is to show a quantitative form of convergence, namely, that the difference of the two terms above decays to zero faster than any polynomial (FTP) as
t\to 0
:

\left|\mathbb{E}[\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{p}^{\mathbb{C}}_{t}(x,x)\mu(dx)]-\frac{c_{\gamma}}{t}|\Omega|\right|\to 0,\quad\text{FTP}.

(3.16)

Rather than refining the techniques of
[
12
]
to obtain quantitative estimates that are sufficiently strong (which we feel would be quite difficult) we take an orthogonal approach, showing that the heat kernel associated to
h^{*}_{x}
can be compared with very good quantitative error bound (FTP) to that associated with
\tilde{h}
. Using the scale invariance of heat kernel on the quantum cone of Proposition
3.1
, this then gives quantitative (FTP) bound on the “in” term in the in-out decomposition. We start with the lemma allowing us to compare the two heat kernels.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
x\in B(0,1/2)
. Consider the fields
h^{*}_{x}
(whose law is that of a GFF on the whole plane, normalised to have zero unit circle average, plus
\gamma G^{\mathbb{C}}(x,\cdot)
) and the
\gamma
-quantum cone
\tilde{h}
centered at
x
(i.e.,
\tilde{h}(\cdot-x)
is a
\gamma
-quantum cone).
Let
\mathbf{p}^{*}_{t}(x,x)
and
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)
denote the respective associated heat kernels. We can couple
h^{*}_{x}
and
\tilde{h}
in such a way that, uniformly in
x\in\Omega
,

\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{p}^{*}_{t}(x,x)-\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)|]\to 0,\quad\mathrm{FTP}.

Proof.

We note that, by construction of the
\gamma
-quantum cone, the fields
\tilde{h}
and
h^{*}_{x}
can be made to agree exactly on the ball of radius 1/2, which we denote by
B
in this proof. We assume that
h
and
\tilde{h}
are coupled in this way (for instance, start by sampling
h
under
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
, then sample
\tilde{h}
according to the conditional law of
\tilde{h}
given
\tilde{h}|_{B}
). Similar to the in-out decomposition, note that

\mathbf{p}^{*}_{t}(x,x)=\mathbf{p}_{t}^{B}(x,x)+\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus B}(x,x),

(3.17)

where the first term consists of the heat kernel restricted to trajectories staying entirely in
B
, while the second denotes the heat kernel restricted to trajectories leaving
B
before time
t
(e.g.,
\mathbf{p}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus B}(x,x)=\mathbf{p}_{t}(x,x){\mathbf{P}}^{\gamma}_{x\overset{t}{\to}x}(\sigma_{B}<t)
). For notational ease we have removed the star from the two heat kernels on the right hand side.

Likewise, we may apply the same decomposition to
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)
, so that

\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)=\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{B}(x,x)+\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus B}(x,x),

(3.18)

Since
\tilde{h}
and
h^{*}_{x}
agree in
B
, we have

\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{B}(x,x)=\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{B}(x,x)

so that

|\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)-\mathbf{p}^{*}_{t}(x,x)|\leq\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus B}(x,x)+{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus B}(x,x).

We will bound both terms separately, showing that they converge to 0 in
L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
, FTP. The argument is the same for both terms, so let us start with the second one, say.
Note that, by reversibility of the Liouville Brownian bridge (which is to say, by reversibility of Liouville Brownian motion with respect to
L^{2}(\mu_{h})
), letting
\sigma_{B}
the first exit time of a given trajectory (which will either be Liouville Brownian motion or bridge below – note that in the latter case we cannot express the Liouville bridge as a time-change of an ordinary Brownian bridge, so cannot write
\sigma_{B}=\nu(\tau_{B})
as we would for a Liouville Brownian motion):

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus B}(x,x)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{P}}^{\gamma}_{x\overset{t}{\to}x}(\sigma_{B}<t){\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)

\displaystyle=2{\mathbf{P}}^{\gamma}_{x\overset{t}{\to}x}(\sigma_{B}<t/2){\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)

\displaystyle=2{\mathbf{E}}^{\gamma}_{x}\left[1_{\{\sigma_{B}<t/2\}}{\mathbf{p}}_{t/2}(\mathbf{Z}_{t/2},x)\right]

where in the last line we used the explicit construction of the law of Liouville Brownian bridge on
[0,t]
(or rather, its restriction to
[0,t/2]
as having the density
{\mathbf{p}}_{t/2}(\cdot,x)/{\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)
with respect to the law of Liouville Brownian motion
(\mathbf{Z}_{t},t\geq 0)
.

Now, by the general theory of Dirichlet forms,
{\mathbf{p}}_{s}(\cdot,x)
is maximised on the diagonal for any given
s>0
; thus

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus B}(x,x)\leq 2{\mathbf{P}}_{x}^{\gamma}(\sigma_{B}<t/2){\mathbf{p}}_{t/2}(x,x).

(3.19)

Let us take expectation with respect to the field
h^{*}_{x}
, and use Cauchy-Schwarz:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus B}(x,x)]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{P}}_{x}^{\gamma}(\sigma_{B}<t/2)^{2}]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t/2}(x,x)^{2}]^{1/2}.

Concerning the first expectation,
{\mathbf{P}}_{x}^{\gamma}(\sigma_{B}<t/2)\leq 1
so
{\mathbf{P}}_{x}^{\gamma}(\sigma_{B}<t/2)^{2}\leq{\mathbf{P}}_{x}^{\gamma}(\sigma_{B}<t/2)
, hence by Fubini’s theorem

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus B}(x,x)]

\displaystyle\leq 2\left[\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}^{\gamma}_{x}(\sigma_{B}<t/2)\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t/2}(x,x)^{2}]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=2\left[\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(\nu(\tau_{B})<t/2)\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t/2}(x,x)^{2}]^{1/2}.

The first term tends to zero FTP by Lemma
2.3
. The second tends to zero no faster than
t^{-1+o(1)}
by Proposition
3.3
. Altogether, this shows that
\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus B}(x,x)]\to 0
, FTP. The same reasoning applies to
\tilde{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus B}(x,x)]
(note that Lemma
2.3
applies also here to show that
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(\nu(\tau_{B})<t/2)
, since this estimate concerns only the behaviour of the field
inside

B
, where
\tilde{h}
and
h^{*}_{x}
agree).
This concludes the proof of Lemma
3.6
.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

For a fixed
x\in\Omega
, the same argument can be used to compare
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{*}(x,x)
(which is the heat kernel for
h^{x}
in the full plane) with
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\Omega}(x,x)
(which is the heat kernel for
h^{x}
, but restricted to trajectories not leaving
\Omega
, and under the measure
\mathbb{P}_{x}^{*}
): we have
\mathbb{E}[|{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{*}(x,x)-{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\Omega}(x,x)|]\to 0
, FTP.

An immediate consequence of this Lemma, together with the scale invariance of the heat kernel on the quantum cone is a strong (quantitative) estimate for the (expected) bulk term in the in-out decomposition (
3.1
), which proves our stated goal (
3.16
).

Lemma 3.8
.

We have

\left|\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}(x,x)]dx-\frac{|\Omega|}{t}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{p}}_{1}(0,0)]\right|\to 0,\quad\text{\emph{ FTP.}}

(3.20)

As a consequence,

\tilde{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{p}}_{1}(0,0)]=c_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\pi(2-\gamma^{2}/2)}

(3.21)

Proof.

The estimate (
3.20
) follows directly from Lemma
3.6
together with the scale invariance of the heat kernel on the quantum cone (with
\alpha=\gamma
) proved in Proposition
3.1
(and the fact that the expectation of
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{1}(0,0)
is finite by Proposition
3.3
).
As for (
3.21
), this follows by comparing with (
3.15
).
∎

We actually have all the tools to prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We have already seen in Corollary
3.2
that
t{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\Omega}(x,x)\to X
in law as
t\to 0
, where
X=\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{1}(0,0)
. In fact, from Remark
3.7
and Proposition
3.1
there is a coupling such that
\mathbb{E}[|t{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\Omega}(x,x)-X|]\to 0
, FTP.

From Lemma
3.8
we furthermore have
\mathbb{E}[X]=c_{\gamma}
, and from Proposition
3.3
we have that
\mathbb{E}[X^{n}]<\infty
for all
n\geq 0
.
∎

3.4
Boundary term

Taking into account the in-out decomposition (
3.1
), the quantitative control on the bulk (or “in”) term in Lemma
3.8
, in order to prove Theorem
1.2
, it remains to show:

Proposition 3.9
.

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\mu_{h}(dx)\right]=t^{\Delta-1+o(1)}.

(3.22)

We divide the proof in an upper bound and a lower bound. We start with the upper bound, which is a bit easier.

Proof of upper bound of (
3.22
).

We start by applying Fubini’s theorem and Girsanov’s lemma, so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\mu_{h}(dx)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}\left[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\right]dx.

Arguing as in (
3.19
) (but with
\Omega
instead of
B
), we have

{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\leq{\mathbf{p}}_{t/2}(x,x){\mathbf{P}}^{\gamma}_{x}(\sigma_{\Omega}<t/2)

(3.23)

Therefore, taking expectation
\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}
and applying Hölder’s inequality, we have for some arbitrary
n\geq 1
and some constant
c_{n}
depending only on
n\geq 1
and
\gamma
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\mu_{h}(dx)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t/2}(x,x)^{n+1}]^{\tfrac{1}{n+1}}\left(\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x}^{\gamma}[\sigma_{\Omega}<t/2]\right)^{\tfrac{n}{n+1}}dx

\displaystyle\leq c_{n}t^{-1+o(1)}\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x}^{\gamma}[\sigma_{\Omega}<t/2]\right)^{\tfrac{n}{n+1}}dx

\displaystyle\leq c_{n}t^{-1+o(1)}\left(\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}\otimes{\mathbf{P}}_{x}^{\gamma}[\sigma_{\Omega}<t/2]dx\right)^{\tfrac{n}{n+1}}

where in the second line we used Proposition
3.3
, and in the last line we used Jensen’s inequality, since
x\mapsto x^{n/(n+1)}
is concave.
The integral in the last line is nothing but
\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{K}}_{\Omega}(t/2)]
. Therefore, applying Proposition
2.1
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\mu_{h}(dx)\right]\leq c_{n}t^{-1+o(1)}(t^{\Delta+o(1)})^{\tfrac{n}{n+1}}.

Since
n\geq 1
can be chosen arbitrarily large, this proves

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\mu_{h}(dx)\right]\leq t^{-1+\Delta+o(1)},

which is the desired upper bound.
∎

Proof of lower bound of (
3.22
).

As in the proof of Proposition
2.1
, we get a lower bound on the expectation by restricting to points
x
at the right distance to the boundary, and to an event
{\mathcal{E}}={\mathcal{E}}_{x}
concerning the behaviour of the GFF near
x
similar to those already appearing in the analysis of the heat content, and which ought to provide the dominant contribution. However, the reason that the lower bound is harder is that there is no independence between the
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)
and the above event
{\mathcal{E}}_{x}
which determines the behaviour of the GFF near
x
; on the contrary, these events may be
a priori
very correlated. Since we want a lower bound, we cannot (as in the above upper bound) rely on Hölder’s inequality. Thus, an argument is needed, to show that the conditional expectation of
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)
remains of order
(1/t)
, even when we condition on the event
{\mathcal{E}}_{x}
.

Let
\delta>0
, and introduce the following region (our notation here differs slightly from that used in the proof of Proposition
2.1
):

\displaystyle\Omega^{*}_{\delta}

\displaystyle:=\Bigl\{x\in\Omega\Big|t^{\kappa_{0}(1+\delta)}\leq d(x,\partial^{*}\Omega)\leq 2t^{\kappa_{0}(1+\delta)}\Bigr\},

where we recall that
\partial^{*}\Omega
is a connected component of the boundary of
\Omega
of positive diameter, and we write
\kappa_{0}=\kappa(\alpha_{0})
.

We let
r=t^{\kappa_{0}(1+\delta)}
and note that
\text{dist}(z,\partial^{*}\Omega)\asymp r
whenever
z\in\Omega^{*}_{\delta}
. We choose a larger scale
r^{\prime}=t^{\kappa_{0}(1-\delta)}
, so that
r^{\prime}/r=t^{-2\kappa_{0}\delta}\to\infty
as
t\to 0
. (More generally the proof will depend on a number of choices of scales which have not been optimised, but are sufficient for the proof). In particular, for
x\in\Omega_{\delta}
, the ball
B_{r}(x)\subset\Omega
but a substantial portion of
B_{r^{\prime}}(x)
lies outside
\Omega
. We will condition the field on its circle average distance
r^{\prime}
, as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{x}

\displaystyle:=\{|h_{r^{\prime}}(x)-\alpha_{0}\log(1/r^{\prime})|\leq 1\}

Then, obviously,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)]dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega^{*}_{\delta}}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)\textbf{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{x}}]dx=\int_{\Omega^{*}_{\delta}}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)|{\mathcal{E}}_{x}]\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}[{\mathcal{E}}_{x}]dx.

As in (
2.8
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}[{\mathcal{E}}_{x}]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\tfrac{(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2}\log(1/r^{\prime})(1+o(1))\right)

\displaystyle=t^{\kappa_{0}(1-\delta)(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}/2+o(1)},

(3.24)

so it remains to estimate
\mathbb{E}^{*}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)|{\mathcal{E}}_{x}]
for
x\in\Omega^{*}_{\delta}
, and more precisely we claim that it suffices to
show

\mathbb{E}^{*}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)|{\mathcal{E}}_{x}]\geq t^{-1+o(1)},

(3.25)

uniformly over
x\in\Omega^{*}_{\delta}
. Indeed, if this holds, then

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)]dx

\displaystyle\geq t^{\kappa_{0}(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}/2-1}|\Omega^{*}_{\delta}|\times t^{\delta[\kappa_{0}(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}/2]+o(1)}

\displaystyle\geq t^{2\mathsf{x}\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{0}(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}/2-1+o(1)}\times t^{\delta[\kappa_{0}(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}/2]+o(1)},

where we used our assumption (
1.5
) to lower bound
|\Omega^{*}_{\delta}|
. Recalling from (
2.13
) that
\kappa_{0}(2\mathsf{x}+(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}/2)=\Delta
, we get

\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)]dx\geq t^{\Delta-1}\times t^{\delta[\kappa_{0}(\gamma-\alpha_{0})^{2}/2]+o(1)},

which proves the lower bound of (
3.22
) since
\delta
is arbitrary.

We thus focus on (
3.25
).
Even though
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)
is far from independent from the event
{\mathcal{E}}_{x}
, it is nevertheless reasonable to hope for such a lower bound in view of the a.s.
uniform
(over the entire domain) lower bound on the on-diagonal heat kernel
\mathbf{p}_{t}(x,x)
of the form
t^{-1+o(1)}
obtained in
[
1
]
. Of course, the latter is an a.s. bound, rather than a bound in expectation, so it cannot be directly used, but this suggests it is indeed reasonable to hope for such a conditional bound.

To see (
3.25
) we first change the field
h^{*}_{x}
to the
\gamma
-quantum cone. Arguing as in Lemma
3.6
, the error we make in doing so tends to zero FTP:

\mathbb{E}^{*}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)|{\mathcal{E}}_{x}]=\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)|{\mathcal{E}}_{x}]+o_{\text{FTP}}(1),

where
\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x}
denotes the law of a
\gamma
-quantum cone translated so
x
is at the origin. (Briefly, the only difference with the setting of Lemma
3.6
is that here we are conditioning on
{\mathcal{E}}_{x}
. Recall that Lemma
3.6
relies on coupling
h^{*}_{x}
and the translated quantum cone in such a way that they agree exactly in a ball of radius at least
1/2
around
x
, hence the events
{\mathcal{E}}_{x}
are the same for both fields and thus the argument from that Lemma applies
verbatim
.) It thus suffices to prove

\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)|{\mathcal{E}}_{x}]\geq ct^{-1}

(3.26)

for
t
small enough, uniformly in
x\in\Omega^{*}_{\delta}
. Let us apply a scaling to the quantum cone, as in Proposition
3.1
, i.e., consider the “unit circle embedding”
\bar{h}_{x}
of
\tilde{h}_{x}+C
, where
\tilde{h}_{x}
has the law
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
, of the translated
\gamma
-quantum cone and
C=(1/\gamma)\log(1/t)
. In other words, we apply the LQG change of coordinate:

\bar{h}_{x}(\cdot)=\tilde{h}_{x}(r_{C}\cdot)+C+Q\log r_{C},

(3.27)

where
r_{C}=e^{-s_{C}}
is defined by

s_{C}=\inf\{s\in\mathbb{R}:\tilde{X}_{s}-(Q-\gamma)s=-C\}

and where
\tilde{X}_{s}
describes the radial part of the
\gamma
-quantum cone, as in (
3.7
) (i.e., the circle average at distance
\rho
from the origin of
\tilde{h}
is
\tilde{X}_{\log(1/\rho)}+\gamma\log(1/\rho)
).

As explained in Proposition
3.1
, we have
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t}(x,x)=(1/t)\bar{\mathbf{p}}_{1}(x,x)
, where
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{p}}
denote the heat kernels associated respectively to
\tilde{h}_{x}
and to
\bar{h}_{x}
. Furthermore,

{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)=\frac{1}{t}\bar{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus r_{C}^{-1}\Omega}_{1}(x,x)

where
r_{C}^{-1}\Omega
denotes, with a slight abuse of notation, the image of
\Omega
under
z\mapsto(z-x)r_{C}^{-1}+x
.
Furthermore, when we phrase the event
{\mathcal{E}}_{x}
in terms of
\bar{h}_{x}
instead of in terms of
\tilde{h}_{x}
,

\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{x}=\Bigl\{\Big|(\bar{h}_{x})_{r^{\prime}/r_{C}}(x)-\alpha_{0}\log(1/r^{\prime})+C+Q\log r_{C}\Big|\leq 1\Bigr\},

where

\rho

\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)|{\mathcal{E}}_{x}]=\frac{1}{t}\bar{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus r_{C}^{-1}\Omega}(x,x)|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{x}].

We claim that:

•

(Large scales.) The effect of the conditioning becomes negligible as

given

converges (say in the sense of total variation locally on any compact) to that of the unconditional

•

(Small scales.) The requirement that trajectories leave the scaled domain

{\mathbf{p}}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus r_{C}^{-1}\Omega}(x,x)\to{\mathbf{p}}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}}(x,x)

Assuming these two facts, one sees that, using (

) and Fatou’s lemma,

\displaystyle\nopagebreak\liminf_{t\to 0}t\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{t}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(x,x)|{\mathcal{E}}_{x}]

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{t\to 0}\bar{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus r_{C}^{-1}\Omega}(x,x)|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{x}]\nopagebreak

\displaystyle\geq\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(x,x)]=c_{\gamma}

where in the last line we used (

Lemma 3.10

Given

Proof.

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
3.4

Here we provide a proof of Lemma
3.4
; the easier case of positive moments in the unit ball (Lemma
3.5
) is left for the reader to check and in fact can be deduced directly from existing estimates such as Proposition 3.37 in
[
10
]
. The reason why Lemma
3.4
requires a new argument is that it pertains to negative moments
at large times
for Brownian motion, corresponding to negative moments at large scales for the Liouville area measures. Furthermore, at large scales, both the quantum cone and the whole plane GFF under
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
have a behaviour which could cause the “standard” arguments (say in
[
10
]
) to break: indeed, on the one hand, beyond the unit ball the
\gamma
-quantum cone does not coincide with
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{0}
and in particular is not Gaussian; while on the other hand, the change in behaviour of the Green function
G(x,y)
beyond
|y|=1
(see (
1.18
)) causes a change in drift which breaks scale invariance for the whole plane GFF under
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{x}
; see (
3.5
). Thus careful arguments are needed.

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

Our first observation is that it suffices to consider the case
x=0
. In the case of the quantum cone this is directly assumed. In the case of the whole-plane GFF, recall that the whole plane GFF is invariant under all Möbius transforms up to additive constant (see, e.g., Lemma 6.26 in
[
10
]
). This implies that
h^{*}_{x}=h^{*}_{0}-\Omega
in law, where
\Omega
is a Gaussian random variable with fixed variance (corresponding to the average value of
h^{*}_{0}
on the circle of radius 1 around
-x
). The two random variables on the right hand side of this identity in law are not independent, but
\mathbb{E}[(e^{\gamma\Omega})^{p}]<\infty
for all
p>0
, so by Hölder’s inequality it suffices to prove the result with
h^{*}_{0}
in place of
h^{*}_{x}
, as announced.

The key observation is that both fields
h^{*}_{0}
and
\tilde{h}
can be coupled to a third field, which we will denote by
\hat{h}
, in such a way that

h^{*}_{0}\geq\hat{h},\quad\tilde{h}\geq\hat{h}

(A.1)

(in the sense that the difference of the distributions is a nonnegative function), and where the field
\hat{h}
can be simply written as

\hat{h}(\cdot)=h+\gamma\log(\tfrac{1}{|z|});\quad\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.

(A.2)

This is essentially obvious in the case of
h^{*}_{0}
, and amounts to the observation that

h^{*}_{0}(z)=h(z)+\gamma\log G(0,z)\geq h+\gamma\log(1/|z|),\quad\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.

There is in fact equality in the unit disc
B_{0}(1)
, and outside the unit by definition of
G
(see (
1.18
)) we have
G(0,\cdot)=0
while
\log(1/|z|)\leq 0
.

In the case of
\tilde{h}
this requires a bit more arguments and boils down to the fact that if
(X_{t},t\geq 0)
is a Brownian motion conditioned so that
X_{t}-(Q-\gamma)t\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
, then
X_{t}
stochastically dominates a Brownian motion. This can be seen either from an explicit computation of the generator, which has a positive drift (see, e.g., the discussion after Definition 7.7 in
[
10
]
where the generator is computed) or, more conceptually, can be seen from the fact that the conditioned process is a certain Doob
h
-transform, where the associated harmonic function, which is here very explicit as it is just an exponential function, is monotone increasing (see e.g.
[
7
]
for related arguments).

It thus suffices to prove the result with
\hat{h}
instead of
h^{*}_{x}
or
\tilde{h}
, and we will denote the corresponding measure by
\hat{\mu}
and quantum clock by
\hat{\nu}(u),u\geq 0
.

Now, note that by conformal invariance of the whole plane GFF,
(h(zR)_{z\in\mathbb{C}})
is a full plane GFF, with a different additive normalisation. Thus
h(zR)-h_{R}(0)
has the same law as
h
(where
h_{r}(x)
denote the circle average of
h
at radius
r
around
x
), and hence

\hat{h}(zR){=}\hat{h}^{\prime}(z)+\gamma\log(\frac{1}{R})+h_{R}(0)

(A.3)

where
\hat{h}^{\prime}
has the same law as
\hat{h}
. Fix a Brownian motion
(B_{t},t\geq 0)
and for
t\geq 0
, let
B^{\prime}_{t}=RB_{t/R^{2}}
. Then
(B^{\prime}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is also a standard Brownian motion. Let
\hat{\nu}(u)
denote the quantum clock of
B
with respect to
\hat{h}
and let
\hat{\nu}^{\prime}(u)
denote the quantum clock of
B^{\prime}
with respect to
\hat{h}^{\prime}
. Fix
u>0
. Then writing down explicitly the definition of the quantum clock, and noting that

\hat{h}_{\varepsilon}(zR)=(\hat{h}^{\prime})_{\varepsilon/R}(z)+\gamma\log(1/R)+h_{R}(0),

we see that

\hat{\nu}(u)=e^{\gamma h_{R}(0)+\gamma^{2}\log(1/R)}\hat{\nu}^{\prime}(u/R^{2})R^{2+\gamma^{2}/2}.

(A.4)

Thus if we suppose
u=R^{2}
we find

\hat{\nu}(u)=e^{\gamma h_{R}(0)}\hat{\nu}^{\prime}(1)R^{2-\gamma^{2}/2}.

Raising to the power
-q
and taking expectations on both sides,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\nu}(u)^{-q}]

\displaystyle=R^{-q(2-\gamma^{2}/2)}\mathbb{E}[e^{-q\gamma h_{R}(0)}\hat{\nu}^{\prime}(1)^{-q}].

Unlike in the exactly scale invariant case discussed e.g. in
[
10
, Theorem 3.27]
, the two random variables inside the expectation in the right hand side above are not independent. Nevertheless, we can use Hölder’s inequality: for every
m>1
, letting
m^{\prime}
be its Hölder conjugate (so
1/m+1/m^{\prime}=1
) we have

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\nu}(u)^{-q}]\leq u^{-q(1-\gamma^{2}/4)}\mathbb{E}[e^{-q\gamma mh_{R}(0)}]^{1/m}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\nu}^{\prime}(1)^{-qm^{\prime}}]^{1/m^{\prime}}

The first expectation can be computed exactly (and is equal to
e^{\gamma^{2}m^{2}q^{2}\mathrm{Var}(h_{R}(0))/2}
). Since GMC admits negative moments of all order (Theorem 3.42 in
[
10
]
), the second expectation is some fixed number
C=C_{q,m,\gamma}
which depends only on
q,m
and of course
\gamma
. Since
\mathrm{Var}(h_{R}(0))=\log R+O(1)=(1/2)\log u+O(1)
, we see that for any
m>1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\nu}(u)^{-q}]

\displaystyle\leq Cu^{-q(1-\gamma^{2}/4)}(u^{1/2})^{q^{2}m^{2}\gamma^{2}/2}.

By choosing
m>1
as close to 1 as needed, we see that the exponent of
u
above can be taken to be as close to

-q(1-\gamma^{2}/4)+q^{2}\gamma^{2}/4=\xi^{*}(-q)/2

as desired. This concludes the proof of Lemma
3.4
.
∎

Appendix B
The heat content is intrinsic to LQG

We verify that, as announced, the heat content is a quantity which is intrinsic to LQG (i.e., satisfies the LQG change of coordinate formula), even though it is not expected to be spectrally determined. Given a domain
\Omega
and a field
h
on
\Omega
, let us write
{\mathbf{K}}^{(\Omega,h)}(t)
for the heat content associated to the field
h
on
\Omega
.

Proposition B.1

(Heat content is intrinsic)
.

Let
(\Omega,h)\sim(\Omega^{\prime},h^{\prime})
be equivalent as quantum surfaces (i.e., in the sense of
[
19
]
). Then

\displaystyle{\mathbf{K}}^{(\Omega,h)}(t)={\mathbf{K}}^{(\Omega^{\prime},h^{\prime})}(t)\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq 0.

(B.1)

Proof.

Let
\phi:\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
be a conformal isomorphism and let
(B_{s})_{s\geq 0}
be a planar Brownian motion starting at some
z\in\Omega
. Then the Liouville Brownian motion associated to
(\Omega,h)
is given by
\mathbf{Z}_{t}=B_{\nu^{-1}(t)}
, where
\nu
is the quantum clock,

\nu(t)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{\gamma^{2}/2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma h_{\varepsilon}(B_{s})}\mathrm{d}s.

(B.2)

By Theorem 1.3 in
[
13
]
the process
(\mathbf{Z}_{t},t\geq 0)
satisfies the change of coordinate formulae and so is itself intrinsic to LQG:

\phi(\mathbf{Z}_{t})
is a Liouville Brownian motion in
\Omega^{\prime}
associated to the field
h\circ\phi^{-1}+Q\log|(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}|
, where
Q=\tfrac{2}{\gamma}+\tfrac{\gamma}{2}
. This is precisely
h^{\prime}
since
(\Omega,h)
and
(\Omega^{\prime},h^{\prime})
are equivalent as quantum surfaces.

Furthermore,
\phi(\mathbf{Z}_{t})=:\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}_{t}
starts at
\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}_{0}=\phi(z)
. Thus, if
\tau_{\Omega}
is the exit time by
B
of the domain
\Omega
, and
\sigma_{\Omega}
(resp.
\sigma^{\prime}_{\Omega^{\prime}}
) is the exit time of
\Omega
(resp.
\Omega^{\prime}
) by
\mathbf{Z}
(resp.
\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}
)
\sigma_{\Omega}=\nu(\tau_{\Omega})
and
\sigma_{\Omega}=\sigma^{\prime}_{\Omega^{\prime}}
. Thus we find
\mathbf{P}_{z}(\sigma_{\Omega}<t)=\mathbf{P}_{\phi(z)}(\sigma^{\prime}_{\Omega^{\prime}}<t)
. It follows that the heat content on
\Omega
associated to

is equal to that on
\Omega^{\prime}
associated to

, as desired.
∎

Appendix C
Example of different one-sided Minkowski dimensions

As we have not easily found an example of a domain with different one-sided Minkowski dimensions, we will give one here, which in fact is even a Jordan domain.

We start by describing the construction, which is a variation on the classical “comb” domain in which one removes an infinite sequence of parallel slits from a fixed nice domain, such as a square. Simply, we fatten the slits and add them above the square (rather than remove them from it), and let them have a height which decreases to zero as they accumulate towards a boundary point (taken to be a corner of the unit square). It will be important to assign precise rates to the various quantities involved (thickness of rectangles, height, spacing between rectangles).

Figure 2:
Example of a curve with different one-sided Minkowski dimensions on each side: the inner Minkowski dimension is
4/3
whereas the outer one is
3/2
.

We start by describing the domain in words. Let
S=(0,1)^{2}
denote the unit square. We will add to the top edge of the square a sequence of rectangles
R_{n},n\geq 1
(i.e., the lower edge of each rectangle will be contained in the segment
[\mathbf{i},\mathbf{i}+1]
, where
\mathbf{i}^{2}=-1
), sequentially from left to right, as follows. The
n
th rectangle has height
b_{n}:=(1/\log(n+1))>0
, and thickness or width
a_{n}:=n^{-3}/2
. The spacing (i.e., distance) between
R_{n}
and
R_{n+1}
is
c_{n}:=6/(\pi n^{2})-a_{n}
. In other words, the difference between the bottom-left corners of
R_{n}
and
R_{n+1}
is
6/(\pi n^{2})
.

We let
\ell_{n}
denote the lower edge of
R_{n}
, which is horizontal segment of the form
[\lambda_{n}+\mathbf{i},\lambda_{n}+a_{n}+\mathbf{i}]
, where

\lambda_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}6/(\pi i^{2})

is the real part of the bottom-left corner of
R_{n}
.
We then define the domain
\Omega
to be

\Omega=S\cup\left(\bigcup_{n\geq 1}R_{n}\right)\setminus\left(\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\ell_{n}\right)

Note that the rectangles
R_{n}
(say, the lower left corner,
\lambda_{n}+\bf i
) accumulate towards the point
\mathbf{i}+1
, since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}=1
.

We claim that
\Omega
may be viewed as a Jordan domain. To do this, it will be useful to denote by
\tilde{R}_{n}
the portion of
\partial\Omega
which consists of
R_{n}\setminus\ell_{n}
together with the portion of
\partial S
connecting
R_{n}
and
R_{n+1}
, thus in total
\tilde{R}_{n}
consists of four segments, three from
R_{n}
and one from
\partial S
.

We consider the curve
\eta
that starts at time 0 from the point
\eta(0)=(0,1)=\mathbf{i}
, and traces
\partial\Omega
counterclockwise as follows: for each
n\geq 1
, the curve traces
\tilde{R}_{1}
, followed by
\tilde{R}_{2}
, and so on. After visiting all rectangles,
\eta
continues from
\bf i+1
and visits the remaining three sides of
S
, in counterclockwise order. We need to fix a parameterisation for
\eta:[0,4]\to\partial\Omega
. To this end we fix a positive summable series
\varepsilon_{n}
such that
\sum_{n\geq 1}\varepsilon_{n}=1
, and require
\eta
to trace
\tilde{R}_{n}
in time
\varepsilon_{n}
, at constant speed.
We set
\eta(1)=\mathbf{i}+1
and let
\eta
traverse the rest the remaining three sides of
\partial S
at unit speed during the interval
[1,4]
.

The above prescription assigns uniquely a value
\eta(t)\in\partial\Omega
for each
0\leq t\leq 4
. The key observation is that
\eta
is continuous and simple.
To check continuity, note that only the continuity from the left at value
t=1
is potentially problematic. However, if
t<1
, then
\eta(t)
belongs to a uniquely defined
\tilde{R}_{n}
, say
\eta(t)\in\tilde{R}_{n(t)}
, with
n(t)
uniquely determined by the fact

\sum_{i=1}^{n(t)}\varepsilon_{i}\leq t<\sum_{i=1}^{n(t)+1}\varepsilon_{i}.

Note that
n(t)\uparrow\infty
as
t\to 1^{-}
. Hence, as
\text{diam}(\tilde{R}_{n})\to 0
and
d(\eta(1),\tilde{R}_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we see that
\eta(t)\to\eta(1)
as
t\to 1^{-}
. Thus
\eta
is a continuous curve.

Furthermore,
\eta
is simple. Indeed let
0\leq s<t\leq 4
. There are various cases to treat, but again the only potentially problematic case is when
t=1
and
0\leq s<1
. However, if
s<1
then
\eta(s)\in\tilde{R}_{n(s)}
so
\eta(s)\neq\eta(1)
. We deduce that
\eta
is a Jordan curve, with
\partial\Omega=\eta([0,4])
.

The claim is now the following:

Lemma C.1

(example of different one-sided Minkowski dimensions)
.

The Jordan domain
\Omega
has inner Minkowski dimension
4/3
and outer Minkowski dimension
3/2
.

Proof.

We have to estimate the outer and inner

-neighborhood for
\varepsilon>0

Inner neighborhood.
Without the rectangles, the segment from
(0,1)
to
(1,1)
would contribute an
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of area exactly
\varepsilon
. We now quantify the additional area contributed by each attached rectangle, distinguishing two regimes:

Consider the area of the
\varepsilon-
(inner) neighbourhood of
\tilde{R}_{n}

(as shown in the first two rectangles in Figure

). Compared to the flat case of

(their

is the rectangle’s height and the term with

Now suppose instead that

where
\tilde{c}\in(0,2)
can be computed explicitly and again comes from the rounded corners.

Note that
2\varepsilon\leq a_{n}
holds precisely when
n\leq\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1/3}\rfloor
. Thus, the total area of the inner
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of this part of
\partial\Omega

can be obtained by summing the contribution of all rectangles:

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon+\sum_{n=1}^{\varepsilon^{-1/3+o(1)}}(2\varepsilon b_{n}-2\varepsilon^{2}c)+\sum_{n=\epsilon^{-1/3+o(1)}}^{\infty}a_{n}(b_{n}-a_{n}\tilde{c})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon+\sum_{n=1}^{\varepsilon^{-1/3+o(1)}}\Bigl(\frac{2\varepsilon}{\log(n)}-2\varepsilon^{2}c\Bigr)+\sum_{n=\varepsilon^{-1/3+o(1)}}^{\infty}\frac{2}{n^{3}\log(n)}-\frac{4\tilde{c}}{n^{6}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{1-1/3+o(1)}+(\varepsilon^{-1/3})^{-(2-1)+o(1)}=\varepsilon^{2/3+o(1)},

where
o(1)
vanishes when
\varepsilon\to 0
. In conclusion, the inner Minkowski dimension is
2-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\log(I_{\varepsilon})/\log(\varepsilon^{-1})=2-2/3=4/3
.

Outer neighborhood.
Similar considerations apply, estimating the contribution of each rectangle. Now we must also account for interactions between neighboring rectangles, depending on whether they are sufficiently separated, corresponding to whether
2\varepsilon\leq c_{n}
or not.

If
2\varepsilon\leq c_{n}
, the gap between
R_{n}
and
R_{n+1}
is large enough that their outer
\varepsilon
-neighborhoods do not overlap. The
n
-th rectangle then contributes an additional area
2\varepsilon b_{n}-2c\varepsilon^{2}
, with the negative term arising from the rounded corners (for some
c\in(0,1)
).

If
2\varepsilon>c_{n}
, the
\varepsilon
-neighborhoods overlap in the gap. In this case, the extra area placed between the two rectangles is at most, say,
2b_{n}c_{n}\leq Cn^{-2}/\log(n)
.

Combining these two regimes, we obtain the stated decomposition of the total contribution:

\displaystyle O_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon+\sum_{n=1}^{\varepsilon^{-1/2+o(1)}}(\frac{2\varepsilon}{\log(n)}-2\varepsilon^{2}c)+C\sum_{n=\varepsilon^{-1/2+o(1)}}^{\infty}\frac{n^{-2+o(1)}}{\log(n)}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{1-1/2+o(1)}+\varepsilon^{-1(-1/2)+o(1)}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{1/2+o(1)}

so the outer Minkowski dimension is
2-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\log(O_{\varepsilon})/\log(\varepsilon)=2-1/2=3/2
. This completes the proof of Lemma
C.1
.
∎
