LPA Kringle IV Type‐2 Genetic Variants Are Associated With Apolipoprotein (a) Size, Hypertension, and Nonfasting Glucose Levels
Authors: Yihao Li, Florian Kronenberg, Stefan Coassin, Badri Vardarajan, Gissette Reyes‐Soffer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh plasma lipoprotein (a) (Lp[a]) levels, largely regulated by variation in the LPA gene, are a causal risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. LPA Kringle IV type 2 (KIV‐2) copy number (CN) inversely associates with Lp(a) levels, with Black and Hispanic individuals exhibiting higher Lp(a) levels than White individuals. These populations are underrepresented in genetic studies. We evaluated the ancestry‐specific LPA KIV‐2 variants and their associations with cardiometabolic and neurologic traits.MethodsWhole‐exome sequencing data from 3817 participants in the Washington Heights Inwood Columbia Aging Project were analyzed, including 886 non‐Hispanic White individuals (23%), 1811 Caribbean Hispanic individuals (47%), and 1120 Black individuals (29%). LPA KIV‐2 CN estimation and variant calling were performed using validated pipelines. Associations with clinical outcomes were evaluated using regression models adjusted for age, sex, and population substructure.ResultsA total of 1421 variants were identified in the LPA KIV‐2 region, including 267 exonic variants, 35% shared across ancestry groups. LPA KIV‐2 CN correlated with 314 variants, 65.6% associated with lower KIV‐2 CN and higher Lp(a) levels. Three novel exonic variants (4785‐C/A, 727‐T/C, and 723‐A/G) positively associated with hypertension across ancestry groups. Variant 412‐C/T was associated to lower glucose levels, independently of KIV‐2 CN; 4785‐C/A was negatively associated with KIV‐CN (P=2.3e‐159) and hypertension (P=0.032); and in a small cohort, it was negatively associated with weighted isoform size (P=1.5e‐4) and positively associated with Lp(a) levels (P=0.012).ConclusionsNovel LPA KIV‐2 variants are enriched in individuals with low KIV‐2 CN and are associated with hypertension and glucose levels. These findings support further investigation into Lp(a)‐mediated mechanisms underlying atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.Keywords: ancestry‐specific genetic variation, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, hypertension, lipoprotein(a), LPA Kringle IV type 2 copy number

Lipoprotein (a) (Lp[a]) is an apolipoprotein B100–containing lipoprotein, unique by its additional protein component, apolipoprotein (a) (apo[a]). Plasma Lp(a) levels are regulated by genetic factors (through the LPA gene locus that codes for apo[a]). 1 , 2 This strong genetic regulation of protein levels allowed large population studies to describe the role of Lp(a) as a causal risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. 3 , 4 Published studies have established high levels of Lp(a) as a top driver for aortic valve stenosis 5 and peripheral arterial disease. 6 An inverse relationship between levels and type 2 diabetes (T2D) 7 and associations with venous thrombosis 8 , 9 have also been observed.

The LPA gene evolved from the plasminogen (PLG) gene, 10 and the lack of expression in murine models has limited the translation of seminal basic research advances to humans. Significant differences exist in the LPA gene compared with PLG, one being the expansion of a highly polymorphic region, the Kringle IV type 2 (KIV‐2). 11 , 12 Human LPA is composed of 10 distinct types of Kringle IV repeats (KIV‐1 to KIV‐10), 1 Kringle V (KV), and a protease‐like domain. The KIV‐1 and KIV‐3 to KIV‐10 domains are each present in a single copy, whereas KIV‐2 shows an extensive copy number (CN) variation, ranging from 1 to >40 copies. Each KIV‐2 copy is 5.5 kb in size and consists of 2 exons of 160 and 182 bp, which are linked by a large intron (4 kb) and a short intron (1.2 kb) that joins it to the next copy. The CN variations in populations of diverse ancestry have been previously studied. 13 All the other KIV regions (KIV‐1 and KIV‐3 to KIV‐10) do not exhibit this variable CN distinction. The KIV repeat is transcribed into mRNA and translated into apo(a) isoforms, whose size is determined by the KIV‐2 CN. Most individuals express 2 isoforms, and 1, generally the smaller size isoform, is dominant. 14 Individuals expressing a low number of KIV‐2 repeats (up to 22 KIV repeats) produce small apo(a) isoforms, and this is associated with higher Lp(a) concentrations, whereas individuals carrying large apo(a) isoforms (>22 KIV repeats) have lower Lp(a) concentrations. 15 Mendelian randomization and large meta‐analysis studies showed that small isoforms are linked to increased risk of cardiovascular disease. 14 The association of apo(a) isoform size or KIV2 CN with disease is largely mediated through their effect on Lp(a) concentration. 16 , 17

Several LPA gene variants that affect Lp(a) plasma levels have been reported and recently reviewed, 18 , 19 and variant frequencies vary by ancestry. 20 , 21 , 22 New platforms have facilitated the analysis of the LPA gene region by incorporating mathematical estimation of both single‐nucleotide variants 23 and variable number tandem repeats 24 in the KIV region. These studies have also examined links to cardiovascular disease. Given the heterogeneity observed in the LPA KIV‐2 variants across different populations and the limited data available from diverse cohorts, along with the increased risk of cardiovascular disease and higher Lp(a) levels in Black and Hispanic populations, we used 2 recently published platforms 23 , 24 to identify common and rare variants within the LPA KIV‐2 repeat region, and to estimate KIV‐2 CN using exome‐sequencing data in a multi‐ancestry cohort. 25

METHODS

Study Population

We analyzed data from 3817 individuals (ie, all individuals who had exome‐sequencing data available) enrolled in the Washington Heights Inwood Columbia Aging Project (WHICAP), encompassing individuals with various disease conditions and healthy controls (Data S1). This cohort has not been previously assessed for lipid phenotypes. The cohort has 3 genetically identified ancestries: non‐Hispanic White (NHW; 23%), African American (29%) (we will refer to this cohort as Black individuals), Caribbean Hispanic (CH; 47%), mostly from the Dominican Republic and Puerto Rico. Sixty‐eight percent self‐identified as women. All study procedures, including use of the WHICAP cohort data, were approved by the institutional review board of Columbia University (institutional review board numbers AAAO9804 and AAAV0773). All participants provided written informed consent. The WHICAP stored data included standardized medical and neurologic examinations, antecedent risk factors, cognitive assessments, genome‐wide association study for Alzheimer disease (AD), whole‐exome sequencing (WES), whole‐genome sequencing, and fasting plasma lipid levels. The details of the WES and data processing are published. 25 We performed a pilot validation study by measuring Lp(a) concentrations and apo(a) isoform size in 87 individuals with stored serum samples.

Estimation of LPA KIV‐2 Copy Number

We used Dynamic Read Analysis for GENomics to detect the variable number tandem repeat. 24 The hg19 reference estimates the diploid length (ie, the sum of CN in both alleles) of LPA KIV‐2 variable number tandem repeat, and we used the mean of CN (ie, the estimate divided by 2) as our variable number tandem repeat (VNTR) estimate. Code and details of other modules involved are shared via GitHub repository (https://github.com/alexliyihao/vntrwrap). This pipeline estimates the read depth of KIV‐2 types 1A and 1B and gives normalizations from: (1) the mean depth extracted for all the regions labeled “extracted” from an individual and (2) 200 individuals with the closest sequencing profile. The KIV‐2 CN is then predicted by a scaling factor of 34.9 and 5.2, respectively, and an additional adjustment of −1 to account for the shared KIV‐2 reads from KIV‐1 and KIV‐3 reads. We evaluated the association of this estimate with all WHICAP phenotypes and LPA KIV‐2 variants. Recent studies have reported the distribution of KIV‐2 types (ie, A, B, or C). 18 , 23 In this study, we did not adjust for these subtypes as their effect has not been studied in large diverse cohorts and using WES data may not provide reliable estimation of the subtypes in our data set.

Identification of LPA KIV‐2 Variants by Ancestry

LPA KIV‐2 variant calling in WES: Previous studies have estimated that the LPA gene can explain 70% to 90% of the variance of Lp(a) plasma protein levels. 26 As previously mentioned, the gene contains a large CN variation that expresses >40 protein isoforms and causes large apo(a) size polymorphism. 11 Because this region is hypervariable and difficult to map using standard sequence analysis protocols, variants within the KIV‐2 region were usually missed in common sequencing projects, leaving up to 70% of the LPA coding region unaddressed.

WHICAP WES

This data set has been aligned to human genome hg19 (GRCh37) using the BWA‐mem pipeline. 27 We used a published pipeline to determine the genetic variation in the LPA gene KIV2. 23 Briefly, for each participant, we aligned exome‐wide reads to an LPA‐KIV2 resolved reference (namely, the PCR5104 amplicon), which contains the LPA KIV‐2 (sixth repeat) reference sequence (hg19 6:161033785‐161038888) to call potential variant sites using an ultradeep sequencing protocol. Following realignment to LPA KIV‐2, reads with high base, mapping, and alignment quality metrics, we called high‐quality variants using a method originally developed for detecting mitochondrial DNA heteroplasmy. 9 For each variant, the pipeline produces a present/absent call, but the information about genotype dosage is not provided. The identified variants were annotated with the location of genetic elements (exons and introns) and exon reading frame. Variants were excluded/dropped if: (1) they were missing in >10% of individuals, (2) the individual had a total coverage <50 reads at this variant, or (3) the variant was monomorphic. Furthermore, we defined variants found in >1% of the participants as common polymorphisms. The resulting variant profiles for all WHICAP participants are reported.

WHICAP Cohort

In 3817 unrelated individuals, we tested the association of LPA KIV‐2 variants and CN with the following phenotypes: self‐ reported history of heart disease, stroke, T2D, hypertension, clinical diagnosis of AD, 12‐hour fasting lipid levels (total cholesterol [TC], calculated low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, and high‐density lipoprotein; mg/dL), nonfasting glucose levels (mg/dL), hemoglobin (HbA1C), insulin (MIU/ml), and C‐peptide (ng/dL). These assays have been previously described. 25 , 28 , 29 The above variables were not available for all participants in whom we obtained exome sequencing data. Table 1 contains the specific number of volunteers for whom the data were available. Details and validation of the self‐reported variables capturing history of heart disease and stroke are provided elsewhere, 30 and the reliability of self‐reported hypertension, diabetes, and heart disease was excellent. Agreement between self‐reports and clinical measures was moderate for hypertension (κ: 0.58), good for diabetes (κ: 0.76–0.79), and moderate for heart disease (κ: 0.45), differing slightly by age, sex, education, and ancestry or ethnic group. Sensitivity and specificity for hypertension was 88.6% and 78.1%; for diabetes, 87.7% and 92.0% (hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5%) or 92.7% and 92.8% (hemoglobin A1c ≥7%); and for heart disease, was 85.8% and 75.5%. Diagnosis of clinical AD was established based on the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and the Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Association (now known as the Alzheimer's Association) criteria. 31 , 32

Statistical Analysis

Associations were tested using independent regression models within each ancestry (ordinary linear for quantitative traits and logistic for binary traits), adjusting for age at blood draw, sex, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 status (see section below), and first 3 principal components (computed from genome‐wide single‐nucleotide polymorphisms data) to account for population substructure. None of the principal components was associated with the study outcomes. Insulin, C‐peptide, triglycerides, and serum Lp(a) levels were natural log transformed as they were not normally distributed (right skewed). All data were analyzed using Statsmodels 0.13.1 running on Python 3.8. The results were summarized using an inverse variance meta‐analysis approach implemented in (meta‐analysis tool) METAL 11.3.25 33 with the exception of the pilot study, as we could not run ancestry‐wise regression in this small cohort. In those scenarios, 0/1 binary covariate for each genetic ancestries was applied as the NHW is used as the reference. Variant association in each ancestry group was included only when at least 5 individuals (no relatives included in WHICAP data set) carried the minor allele of the specific variant. Statistical significance was established using a Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR)–adjusted P=0.05 threshold. 34 For the meta‐analysis, the FDR adjustment P values are computed independently for each outcome on all the variants after the cross‐ancestry meta‐analysis P values are obtained for each single‐nucleotide variant–outcome combination. All the P values reported in the article are FDR adjusted unless specified. For implementation details and other modules involved, please check the GitHub repository (https://github.com/alexliyihao/lpa‐analysis). We detected haplotypes among variants that had strong associations to the same clinical phenotype using Haploview. 35 Because the LPA variant calling pipeline outputs presence/absence calls for variants and does not genotype those calls, we used 0/1 status (instead of variant dosage) for computing linkage disequilibrium metrics (D′ and r 2).

Plasma Lp(a) Levels and Isoform Size Measurements (n=87)

Lp(a) plasma concentration was measured using the isoform‐independent sandwich ELISA developed by the Northwest Lipid Metabolism and Diabetes Research Laboratory. 36 Apo(a) isoform size estimates were performed by the same laboratory using gel electrophoresis. We calculated weighted isoform size (wIS) 37 for all individuals and examined the correlation of Lp(a) levels and wIS with identified KIV‐2 variants and KIV‐2 CN estimate, respectively. Correlations were only tested for variants observed in at least 5 of the 87 participants.

APOE E4/E2 Genotype Phenotype

APOE ε4 status was generated using either isoelectric focusing described by Hixon‐Vernier approach 38 or TaqMan SNP genotyping assays (Applied Biosystems, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) of rs429358 (APOE*4) and rs7412 (APOE*2) SNPs. For analysis, we used the dosage of the APOE‐ε4 allele (0, 1, or 2 alleles per participant). ApoE genotypes have been shown to affect plasma lipid levels, 39 and hence we controlled for ApoE genotype in all regression models.

Data for all study outcomes have been provided, and a public code repository has been generated and includes all study data (https://github.com/alexliyihao/lpa‐analysis, https://github.com/alexliyihao/vntrwrap).

RESULTS

WES of 3817 WHICAP participants (886 NHW, 1120 Black, and, 1811 CH) were analyzed. The WES data include a higher number of women (N=2606) compared with men (N=1209), and the population mean±SD age is 80.6±7 years, appropriate for an aging study cohort. Two individuals were dropped for incomplete demographic data. The age of this data cohort (subset of WHICAP that had available exome sequencing) is representative of the full WHICAP data set, a population originally enrolled to study risk of AD and dementia. Baseline characteristics of the 3815 study participants are presented in Table 1. Additional details on the WHICAP population, whole‐exome sequencing, and preprocessing have been previously published. 27

LPA KIV‐2 Variant Presentation in 3 Ancestry Groups (WHICAP Cohort)

We identified 1421 variants: 267 exonic and 1154 intronic (Table S1, Figure S2). Variant location, carrier frequencies by ancestry, and sex are illustrated on Figure S3. The distribution of variants across all ancestry groups is presented on Figure 2. There was 36% overlap of LPA KIV‐2 variants within the 3 ancestry groups (Figure 2A). CH and Black individuals had similar percentage of unique variants (20% and 16%, respectively) (Figure 2A) and shared 15% of the variants. Of the 267 exonic variants identified (Figure 2B), 103 (38.6%) were published in the work of Coassin et al, 18 , 23 yet more than half of the variants (N=164, 61.4%) were not previously described (Figure 2C). A considerable proportion of these novel exonic variants were present in the 3 ancestry groups (35%), 26% were unique to CH individuals, and 21% were unique to Black individuals (2C). Importantly, although our cohort is not enriched in NHW, we did identify unique novel variants (8%) in this group. Figure 2D lists all identified intronic variants that, to our knowledge, have not been previously described. Previously reported variants (nonnovel) that were found in our cohort and their ancestry appearance frequency compared with the 1000 Genomes Project cohort are listed in Table S2. A substantial proportion of our identified variants (n=1007, 70.9%) were rare (the minor allele was observed in <1% of the population).

Association of LPA KIV‐2 Variants With KIV‐2 CN

It is well established that the number of KIV‐2 CN is inversely correlated to Lp(a) plasma concentration. Therefore, we combined the KIV‐2 CN (ie, we did not have Lp[a] levels in full cohort) and variant data to assist in identifying the drivers of metabolic phenotypes. Of the 1421 identified variants, 589 passed the frequency requirements (ie, minor allele appears in at least 5 individuals in at least 1 ancestry group), and 314 were significantly associated with KIV‐2 CN (Table S3). Most of the significant variants, 206 of 314 (65.6%), were associated with a lower number of KIV‐2 CN (i.e., higher levels of Lp[a]; eg, 4894‐G/A [β=−4.01 {number count of CN}, FDR‐adjusted P=1.17e‐284] and 690‐A/C [β=−3.95 FDR‐adjusted P=2.48e‐273]). Figure 3 highlights the top 9 variants that were negatively associated (blue is lower than red) with KIV‐2 CN. For each variant, individuals carrying the reference and alternative alleles show consistent differences in KIV‐2 CN estimates across ancestry groups. As Lp(a) levels are largely determined by the KIV‐2 CN, we examined its relationship to our available study phenotypes. The KIV‐2 CN was negatively associated with plasma cholesterol levels (P=0.02) and trends for low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels (P=0.053). These associations were driven by the NHW population and may be related to the contamination of low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol measurements and Lp(a), which has been previously described 40 (Table S4).

Association of LPA KIV‐2 Variants With WHICAP Phenotypes

We identified 10 LPA KIV‐2 variants significantly associated with our WHICAP phenotypes (Table 2), and Figure 4 highlights phenotypes with significant associations and gene locus positions. Three exonic variants, shared by all ancestry groups, showed positive associations with hypertension; 4785‐C/A (overall carrier frequency [CF]=78%; odds ratio [OR]=1.45; P=0.032), 727‐T/C (CF=96%; OR=2.11; P=0.032), and 723‐A/G (CF=96%; OR=1.97; P=0.038). Six intronic variants were also positively associated with hypertension (166‐G/A, 387‐G/C, 402‐G/A, 4527‐A/T, 4541‐G/A, and 4653‐A/T). One additional intronic variant, 412‐C/T (CF=76%; β=−14.52 mg/dL; P=0.02) was negatively associated with blood glucose level. Full data for all phenotypes are presented in Table S5. Effect of KIV‐2 CN on LPA KIV‐2 Association With Phenotypes

As we lacked Lp(a) plasma levels in the complete cohort, we used our KIV‐CN data to estimate the effect of CN on the observed associations with phenotypes. Of the 10 variants, 3 intronic variant associations (412‐C/T [β=−14.2; P=0.03] [glucose {mg/dL}], 166‐G/A [OR=1.41; P=0.05] [hypertension], and 387‐G/C [OR=1.40; P=0.05) [hypertension]) remained significant after adjusting for KIV‐2 CN (Table 2; Table S6).

The carrier frequency of most variants that were identified had broad ranges within each ancestry group (Table S1); however, the CFs of these 10 variants are consistent (Table 2) and mostly common (CF between 0.03 and 0.97) across the 3 ancestry groups (Table S7). To understand if these variants were associated with one another, we calculated linkage disequilibrium in the full study population. Among the top 9 variants associated with hypertension, we observed modest to high linkage disequilibrium between 723‐A/G and 727‐T/C (D′=0.846, r 2=0.655), 4527‐A/T and 4541‐G/A (D′=0.787, r 2=0.49), and 387‐T/C and 402‐G/A (D′=0.923, r 2=0.283) (Figure S15; Table S8).

Effects of Ancestry on LPA KIV‐2 Variants and KIV‐2 CN Associations Findings

We discovered unique exonic and intronic variants in each ancestry group. We decided to examine the associations of these variants with KIV‐2 CN. In CH (26%), none of the unique exonic variants was associated with KIV‐2 CN, or any of our study phenotypes. However, 8 intronic variants were significantly associated with KIV‐CN (4 positively associated: 4460‐C/G, 4691‐G/T, 1296‐G/A, and 4952‐C/T; and 4 negatively associated: 4587‐C/T, 395‐A/G, 4734‐G/T, and 4598‐T/C), but none was associated with other phenotypes. In Black individuals, we had 1 novel exonic variant (4798‐T/C) positively associated with KIV‐2 CN and not with reported phenotypes. Four intronic variants were associated with KIV‐2 CN (1 positive, 3 negative), and no associations were found with phenotypes. Although the unique variants are less in our NHW, we find that 2 intronic variants (5056‐G/T and 422‐T/C) are associated to KIV‐2 CN and not with phenotypes (Table 3; Table S3). Overall, there are many variants that are shared by all 3 ancestry groups. There are specific carrier frequency differences that we present on Tables S1 and S7. These differences highlight the heterogeneity observed in the variants of the KIV‐2 region.

Association of LPA KIV‐2 variants with plasma Lp(a) levels and apo(a) isoform size: pilot study

In a small number of stored serum samples (N=87; CH: N=14, Black: N=48, NHW: N=25) from the WHICAP cohort (Tables S9 and S10) we were able to measure Lp(a) levels and isoform size via gel electrophoresis. We evaluated the association of Lp(a) levels and isoform size (ie, weighted isoform size) with LPA KIV‐2 CN and variants (controlling for age, sex, genetic ancestry principle components, and APOE‐ε4 genotype). The VNTR was significantly associated with lipid levels reported in the WHICAP study 28 (Table S11). The median Lp(a) level for this population was 40.18 nmol/L, and the distribution of LPA KIV‐2 CN estimation by ancestry group is presented in Figure S16. As expected, there was a positive association between our estimated isoform size (wIS) and KIV‐2 CN (β=0.72 [wIS per CN], P=4.46e‐16). Additionally, KIV‐2 CN was negatively associated with Lp(a) levels (β=−0.125 nmol/L per CN, P=8.28E‐07).

In this small subgroup of participants, we found 265 of the previously identified LPA KIV‐2 variants (for carrier frequencies, see Table S12) that we observed in the larger cohort. Fifty‐four of the variants are exonic, 20 had been previously reported, 23 and 34 are part of the novel variants that we have found. There were 24 variants uniquely associated with wIS, 5 with serum Lp(a) levels, and 44 were associated with both wIS and serum Lp(a) levels (Figure 5 ; Tables S13–S15).

DISCUSSION

Lp(a) levels are higher in Black and Hispanic ancestry, 41 , 42 even at similar isoform (KIV‐2) sizes, compared with White individuals. The pathophysiology behind these higher absolute Lp(a) levels in Black and Hispanic individuals remains unclear and could be modulated by ancestry‐specific genetic variants. In this study, we used 2 previously published protocols aimed at estimating LPA KIV‐2 CN, the main driver of Lp(a) levels, and identifying genetic variants in the KIV‐2 region in 3 ancestry groups. We report novel variants within the KIV‐2 region. Although several of these variants were expressed in all ancestries, they varied in their carrier frequency, highlighting heterogeneity previously observed in this region. 18 , 43 These findings highlight the importance of including ancestry diversity when trying to define genetic variations in the LPA gene and the implications this may have for identifying variants linked to disease. A recently published study, that used the Dynamic Read Analysis for GENomics LPA caller, also examined LPA KIV‐2 variants and KIV‐2 CN in the ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities) study, a diverse population cohort. 44 However, the authors’ focus was on using the combination of KIV‐2 variants and CN provides to estimate Lp(a) levels, not necessarily linking the genetic variants to associations with phenotypes. In our data set, we include CH, which show similar distribution of KIV‐2 CN and confirm the known inverse correlation of KIV‐2 CN to plasma Lp(a) levels. In a study by Enkhmaa et al, 21 which included a large population of NHW and Black individuals, they also observed racial differences in LPA allele presentation, particularly in heterozygotes with detectable apo(a) isoforms. Similarly, Rubin et al found that Lp(a) level and LPA genotype explained more of the apo(a) isoform dominance pattern in Black individuals/African Americans (29%) than in White individuals (13%). 45 This region strongly regulates Lp(a) levels, and our study adds to the body of evidence supporting this ancestry‐specific variability.

The WHICAP cohort has been extensively studied for risk factors and mechanisms that drive AD. Because the WHICAP study has a high prevalence of AD (Table 2), we investigated the association of LPA variants and KIV‐2 CN with AD (Table S5; Figure S5). Although we identified some nominal associations, further validation in additional studies is required to make a definitive claim about the role of LPA variants in AD. In the context of our work on Lp(a), an apolipoprotein B–containing lipoprotein, it is important to highlight that epidemiologic and mechanistic data support shared pathologic pathways between cardiovascular dysfunction and neurodegeneration (ie, AD). Cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, smoking, and dyslipidemia, not only predispose individuals to atherosclerotic disease but also independently raise the risk of developing AD, with the aggregation of such factors further amplifying this risk and accelerating cognitive decline. 46 , 47 A recently published study included 2 large populations and found that Lp(a) levels were not associated with the risk of developing AD or vascular‐related dementia. 48 However, when accounting for competing risk of death, absolute risks of vascular‐related dementia increased with higher Lp(a) levels in the UK Biobank cohort (n=452 989; events=5132; P=0.01); this was not found in the Copenhagen studies (n=80 313; events=2734; P=0.42), which used KIV‐2 CN to estimate Lp(a) levels.

Relevant to our variant associations, hypertension has been shown to exacerbate AD pathology by promoting cerebrovascular damage, reducing cerebral microvascular density, and impairing nitric oxide signaling, which collectively enhance amyloid‐β accumulation and τ‐related neurodegeneration. 49 The multifactorial relationship between cardiovascular risk and AD underscores the potential of early cardiovascular risk management, 50 especially in those in whom Lp(a) may be driving cardiovascular risk.

The WHICAP cohort is a community‐based aging population study, and the high prevalence of various clinical conditions and cardiovascular phenotypes allowed us to evaluate their association with Lp(a). Previous studies of LPA KIV‐2 variants lacked association with clinical outcomes. In the WHICAP cohort, we found several variants significantly associated with hypertension and one with glucose levels. These findings are of scientific interest as previous studies report high Lp(a) concentrations in hypertensive individuals compared with normotensive subjects; however, these data are not consistent. 4 , 14 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 These inconsistencies may be because of the large genetic diversity in the LPA KIV‐2 region, including differences in carrier frequencies that our data captures. Our findings suggest that further work should focus on identifying the unifying pathologic pathway that links high Lp(a) levels to hypertension, the latter in line with more recent publications showing this relationship. 55 Interestingly, recent evidence from the multiethnic MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) cohort revealed that high Lp(a) levels enhanced the association of hypertension with incident cardiovascular disease, with the strongest association observed among Black participants. 56 This ethnic‐specific observation could also be supported by our study, which shows that the association between LPA variants and hypertension may be strongest in the Black ancestry group compared with CH and White individuals (Table S5).

Various studies have linked lower levels of Lp(a) to the incidence of T2D 7 , 57 ; however, no conclusive data exist on the mechanism or pathology driving this observation. 58 Our study showed a significant negative association of 1 variant with glucose levels, which could be linked to the observed clinical phenotypes connecting Lp(a) to T2D. Importantly, the novel variant association with glucose levels was not affected by the KIV‐2 CN. However, a previous Danish cohort reported that the highest quintile of the sum of the KIV‐2 repeats from the 2 apo(a) alleles was associated with T2D. 58 Our findings, along with these reports, will need further validation in other cohorts that include subjects with T2D and glucose data. There are various phase‐3 studies developing targeted therapies that have up to 90% lowering of plasma Lp(a) levels 59 ; the cardiovascular benefits have not been reported, and the incidence of T2D in those programs is not known. 60

Our main efforts were to describe novel variants, define their association to KIV‐2 CN, and importantly link to available metabolic phenotypes available in the WHICAP cohort. We found that only 4 of our variants significantly associated with metabolic phenotypes were significantly affected by the KIV‐2 CN. A small cohort of WHICAP participants allowed us to measure Lp(a) levels and isoform size. We identified 3 novel LPA KIV‐2 variants associated with isoform size: 2 positively associated and 1 negatively associated. Additionally, 1 variant was negatively associated with weighted isoform size. Overall, our novel findings of associations between LPA KIV‐2 variants with hypertension and glucose need further investigation and could be linked to the potential genetic control of Lp(a)'s role in driving vascular disease. It is well established that endothelial dysfunction is at the core of hypertension and tightly linked to glucose control (ie, diabetes). 61 , 62 In vitro, studies have shown that elevated Lp(a) can aid in the process of atherogenesis and cause endothelial dysfunction, mainly driven by the apo(a) component of the particle, which is the product of the LPA gene. The latter highlights pathways that can link Lp(a) to its role in disease development.

Study Limitations

Our current findings must also be reviewed in the context that a genetic variant can be in strong linkage disequilibrium with the true causal variant, and without functional studies, it will be difficult to determine which variant is responsible for Lp(a) concentration variability. It is unclear how the newly identified variants regulate Lp(a) levels and by which mechanisms. In addition to KIV‐2–specific variants, several groups have described LPA variants outside of this region. These non–KIV‐2 variants may play a role in Lp(a)‐related diseases. This variant is not in the LPA‐KIV2 region but outside. Lp(a) transcription may be affected by these variants, and some of the regulators of Lp(a) transcription have been previously described. 63 One such variant, rs3798220, is infrequent in individuals of European (NHW) and African American ancestry (CF=0.03 and 0.02, respectively) but is common in CH individuals (CF=0.12). 4 , 64 rs3798220 is a coding variant located in the protease‐like domain of LPA and is associated with plasma Lp(a) levels and cardiovascular risk, but not in all populations. 65

We wanted to confirm the effects of this common variant in the current cohort and found that it was associated with baseline insulin C‐peptide levels (β=3.4, Wilcoxon P=0.007), with variant carriers having higher C‐peptide levels. These data may support the recent observed association of hepatic insulin resistance with Lp(a). 66 Previous studies have shown that splice site variants 4925G>A and 4733G>A are frequent in some populations and significantly lower Lp(a) concentrations, reducing cardiovascular risk. 19 However, these variants were absent in the WHICAP cohort, underscoring the importance of studying diverse populations.

The WHICAP cohort is an aging population study with collected exome sequencing data but also characterized for various clinical conditions. We could not validate our data in a larger diverse cohort; however, we have used pipelines that have been described in large cohorts. There remains a need to replicate our study findings to be certain that the associations found apply to larger diverse data sets. In addition, we used exome‐wide reads to realign to the KIV‐2 reference, 18 to ensure all unaligned reads with potential matches to the repeat are aligned in the resulting BAM file; it is conceivable that reads aligning to regions of high similarity in other Kringle repeats could result in false‐positive variant calls. To partially overcome this limitation, we only used variants observed in ≥5 individuals within each ancestry group, assuming that recurrent variants are likely true calls. In work performed in the ARIC study, 44 the authors concluded that the KIV‐2 CN distributions were similar to our current observations. Additionally, in our study, we believe combining the effects of variants and CN provides the most comprehensive data. Of course, the populations enrolled in those studies do not mimic the WHICAP participants, as data on CH individuals are lacking. Our study concentrated on the analysis of variants within the LPA KIV‐2 regions; however, there is still a wide variability in Lp(a) concentrations within each KIV‐2 repeat group that could be explained by nongenetic factors and other genetic outside KIV‐2 region. Although WES primarily focuses on exons, it also captures some off‐target reads from intronic regions, including those near exon‐intron boundaries. These intronic variants can be informative, as they may affect gene splicing and expression, and in some cases, even be pathogenic populations. 3 , 67

Differences in carrier frequency and prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors between and within ancestral groups could confound and result in false‐positive associations. To overcome this, we analyzed each ancestral group independently and included principle components that capture latent population substructure to account for carrier frequency differences. Importantly, the disease relevance of these variants and their links to KIV‐2 CN have not been previously reported. Our pilot data cohort, which had Lp(a) plasma measurement, is small and hence the effect sizes and correlations with Lp(a) levels in this small cohort may not extrapolate to larger populations and need further validation. However, we report the significance of these relationships with KIV‐2 in the larger cohort.

The biomarkers and clinical diagnosis reported in this study were not available in all individuals. HbA1C data were available in 464 individuals, glucose levels were available in 837 individuals, and, importantly, the diagnosis of T2D was 30% (n=1137) compared with hypertension, which was present in 82% of the population. Importantly, previous publications in the WHICAP cohort report high reliability for hemoglobin A1c and reported T2D. 30 Despite these limitations, our statistical model considers the percentage of each outcome analyzed. In addition, the power for analysis of some associations may be weak and, hence, we only report significant associations.

CONCLUSIONS

The discovery of ancestry‐specific common and rare variants underscores the importance of diverse population studies in understanding genetic contributors to disease risk. The KIV‐2 region, a key regulator of circulating Lp(a) levels, has numerous novel variants that are significantly associated with hypertension and glucose levels. Our study highlights the challenge of linking genetic variants to causal mechanisms. Variants in strong linkage disequilibrium with causal variants may require functional studies to confirm their role. Lp(a) transcription may be influenced by these variants, necessitating further exploration. Recent studies using haplotypes for risk prediction and advanced methods, like quantitative polymerase chain reaction, for KIV‐2 repeat quantification provide avenues for future research.
