Architecture and function of holocentric CENP-A–independent inner kinetochores
Authors: Christine Yu, Sundar Ram Sankaranarayanan, Gaetan Cornilleau, Anna C. Howes, Caleigh M. Azumaya, Eric S. Day, Inna Zilberleyb, Bobby Brillantes, Tommy K. Cheung, Leonie Dec, Damarys Loew, Phong Tran, Christopher M. Rose, Ines Anna Drinnenberg, Claudio Ciferri, Stanislau Yatskevich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kinetochores are essential macromolecular complexes anchoring chromosomes to the mitotic spindle, ensuring faithful cell division. Despite their critical role, the structural organization of kinetochores across diverse species remains poorly understood. We present the inner kinetochore constitutive centromere-associated network (CCAN) structure of the silkmoth Bombyx mori, an insect that lacks the canonical centromere-specifying histone variant CENP-A and exhibits chromosome-wide centromeric activity (holocentric). The B. mori CCAN incorporates four previously uncharacterized centromeric subunit proteins that are structurally related to the Dam1/DASH complex but function in scaffolding the inner kinetochore rather than in microtubule binding. Similar to the yeast and human systems, the B. mori CCAN also entraps DNA within its central closed chamber. However, unlike these systems, the B. mori CCAN can also assemble in vitro into a self-contained head-to-head dimer via atypical histone-fold protein dimerization. On the basis of our findings, we propose that the holocentric organization may emerge from the modular arrangement of discrete kinetochore units.

INTRODUCTION

Centromeres are specialized chromosomal regions responsible for kinetochore assembly and spindle attachment during chromosome segregation (1, 2). In most species, centromeres incorporate a specialized histone H3 variant called CENP-A, which assembles into a nucleosome (CENP-ANuc) (3, 4). The CENP-ANuc lays the foundation for kinetochore assembly by recruiting the essential inner kinetochore known as the constitutive centromere-associated network (CCAN) (5–7). In the case of point centromeres found in a few unicellular yeast species, a single CENP-ANuc is recruited to the centromere in a DNA sequence–specific manner and recognized directly and specifically by the CCAN and its associated proteins (8–13). In the case of regional centromeres present in many other eukaryotes, including humans, many copies of CENP-ANuc are distributed over several hundred kilobases of DNA to recruit numerous CCAN complexes (14, 15). The CENP-C protein is the main scaffolding component that directly decodes the CENP-ANuc and recruits the rest of the CCAN to the centromere (16–23). The human CCAN primarily recognizes and binds the linker DNA between nucleosomes via a central CENP-LN DNA binding channel (18). Across eukaryotes, the CCAN recruits and directly binds to the Knl1-Mis12-Ndc80 (KMN) network, an outer kinetochore complex that mediates microtubule attachment (24).

Centromere organization and kinetochore composition exhibit considerable diversity across eukaryotes, highlighting their evolutionary plasticity despite their universally essential role in chromosome segregation (25–28). This diversity even extends to the loss of components such as CENP-A and CENP-C, which are thought to act as cornerstones for kinetochore assembly, including in humans (29, 30). How kinetochores assemble and function in the absence of these factors therefore remains a fundamental question. Despite the loss of CENP-A/C, many of these species, including several insect orders, retain sequence-divergent CCAN subunits, suggesting the existence of alternative, yet functionally robust, pathways to sustain kinetochore-spindle interactions.

Intriguingly, CENP-A/C–lacking insects also evolved a holocentric chromosome organization, where centromeric activity and kinetochore assembly are dispersed along the chromosomal length (30). In the holocentric silkmoth Bombyx mori (Lepidoptera order), a previous study of inner (CENP-T) and outer (Dsn1) kinetochore components in asynchronous cell lines revealed that kinetochores assemble over large chromosomal domains scattered along entire chromosomes (31). In mitosis, microscopy analyses showed that kinetochore staining appears as longitudinal bands along B. mori chromosomes, consistent with the holocentric architecture (31). These observations raise questions about the architecture of the kinetochore building blocks in these evolutionarily diverse eukaryotes.

To address these questions, and following up on previous work, we functionally, biochemically, and structurally characterized the B. mori kinetochore (29–31). Our work reveals how evolutionarily repurposed and conserved B. mori CCAN subunits can assemble in vitro into a unique dimeric structure that folds DNA into a configuration poised for chromosome segregation. This dimeric arrangement mirrors point-centromere architecture, suggesting that kinetochore building blocks can be redeployed as modular units, independently of CENP-A, to support diverse centromere architectures across eukaryotes.

RESULTS

Conserved and divergent structural features of B. mori CCAN

We recombinantly expressed and fully purified individual B. mori CCAN (bmCCAN) modules from Spodoptera frugiperda cells (Sf9), including the bmCENP-LN and bmCENP-HIKM modules, a unique bmCENP-T protein with an altered histone-fold domain (HFD) (32), and a module we term bmCENP-OP, predicted to contain an RWD domain similar to its human and yeast counterparts (fig. S1A) (29). For these experiments, each module was fully purified independently, including a final size exclusion chromatography (SEC) step, and subsequently mixed for in vitro reconstitution. Under these conditions, reconstitution using fully purified subcomplexes resulted in only partial bmCCAN assembly (fig. S1B).

We hypothesized that incomplete assembly reflected either the loss of weakly associated components or the absence of relevant posttranslational modifications during full purification. To address these possibilities, we adopted an alternative reconstitution strategy in which each bmCCAN module was affinity purified in Sf9 cells and the resulting eluates were combined directly and subjected to reconstitution on a heparin column (fig. S1C). Given the high sequence similarity between the two lepidopteran species B. mori and S. frugiperda, we anticipated that this approach would permit complementation by endogenous factors that facilitate CCAN assembly. Consistent with this expectation, the resulting bmCCAN complex exhibited subunit abundances closer to the predicted composition, although a small number of additional bands remained detectable (fig. S1, D and E). This seminative bmCCAN assembly was subsequently used for structural analysis and for identifying factors that promote CCAN assembly.

Using cryo–electron microscopy (cryo-EM), we determined three distinct structures from this sample: (i) bmCENP-LN:HIKM:T subcomplex (bmCCANLN:HIKM:T), (ii) bmCENP-LN:HIKM:T:OP complex (bmCCANLN:HIKM:T:OP), and (iii) bmCENP-LN:HIKM:T:OP complex with additional pentameric coiled-coil density bound across bmCENP-LN:HIKM:OP interfaces (apo-bmCCAN) (Fig. 1, A to C; figs. S2 and S3; and table S1).

The global architecture of apo-bmCCAN mirrors the homologous complexes from human and yeast despite limited sequence conservation and distinct predicted folds between the homologous subunits (Fig. 1D). apo-bmCCAN forms a ring-like structure, with the conserved CENP-LN channel serving as the central architectural feature (Fig. 1, B to D). The bmCENP-HIKM module reinforces and stabilizes bmCENP-L, similar to the human and yeast complexes. In contrast, the bmCENP-OP RWD domains (bmCENP-OPRWD) adopt a unique configuration, binding directly atop bmCENP-N and adding bulk density to the central channel (Fig. 1, B to D). This channel is sealed by the altered HFD of bmCENP-T (bmCENP-THFD) that connects bmCENP-HIKHead with the bmCENP-NPyrin (head group and pyrin domains of bmCENP-HIK and bmCENP-N, respectively; Fig. 1, B and C). The bmCENP-THFD, structurally distinct from canonical HFDs, likely compensates for the absence of the CENP-W protein found in most other species that encode CENP-T (Fig. 1D) (28, 32). bmCENP-T also makes an additional unique interaction with bmCENP-HIKBody using an α helix (bmCENP-Tα-helix) that precedes the bmCENP-THFD (Fig. 1, A to C).

In the apo-bmCCAN structure, the bmCENP-N:T interaction is poorly resolved, suggesting a dynamic interface that may serve as the primary DNA-entry gate into the central binding channel. Consistent with this, the bmCENP-LN channel is fully open in the bmCCANLN:HIKM:T subcomplex reconstruction (Fig. 1A). Last, in the DNA-bound structure described later, the bmCENP-N:T interaction is rigidified, and an extended density is observed for the CENP-T N-terminal extension that folds along bmCENP-NPyrin.

Centromeric subunit proteins form a central bmCCAN coiled coil

The salient feature in our reconstruction is the presence of a pentameric coiled coil nestled across most bmCCAN subunits. As this coiled coil did not belong to any overexpressed components it must have been copurified from the native source (Figs. 1, B and C, and 2, A and B). The high resolution of our reconstruction permitted the sequencing of these proteins directly from the cryo-EM maps using ModelAngelo (Materials and Methods and fig. S4A) (33). One helix of the pentameric coiled coil was predicted to belong to the N terminus of the bmCENP-H protein (bmCENP-HN). Consistent with this assignment, continuous density was observed between the bmCENP-H protein and the bmCENP-HN coiled-coil helix, validating our method (Fig. 1, B and C). Sequences for the other four proteins of the heteromeric coiled coil belonged to previously uncharacterized short proteins, which we termed centromeric subunit 1 (CS-1) to CS-4 proteins (Materials and Methods and Figs. 1, B and C, and 2, A and B). AlphaFold2 prediction of the complex between all identified CS proteins resulted in a high-confidence model that globally fitted the experimental cryo-EM map (fig. S4, B and C). Modeling these sequences into the experimental cryo-EM map resulted in a good side-chain fit for all CS-1 to CS-4 proteins (fig. S4D).

The discovered CS-1 to CS-4 subunits, together with bmCENP-HN, form the central pentameric coiled coil that binds along bmCENP-OPRWD. The N-terminal extensions of bmCENP-O (bmCENP-ON) and bmCENP-P (bmCENP-PN) wrap around the CS module, securing its position next to the bmCENP-N and bmCENP-M subunits (Figs. 1 and 2, A and B). In the fully assembled structure, bmCENP-HN threads beneath the bmCENP-ON and bmCENP-PN extensions (Fig. 2B). In addition, CS-4 inserts its C terminus at the pocket formed by the bmCENP-IK interface (fig. S4E). Notably, the CS-4:bmCENP-IK interaction is identical to the human CENP-O:CENP-IK interaction (fig. S4E) (18–20). At key contact sites, CS-4 and human CENP-O share chemically similar amino acids, suggesting that these unrelated proteins accomplish a conserved function.

CS module completes and augments bmCCAN architecture

We used the S. frugiperda CS module sequences to identify, clone, and reconstitute B. mori CS-1 to CS-4 orthologs. CS-1 to CS-4 proteins formed a single soluble complex in solution with 1:1:1:1 stoichiometry as determined by SEC coupled with multiangle light scattering (SEC-MALS) (fig. S5, A and B). Using an on-column reconstitution approach, we observed that the CS module stably associates only with the bmCENP-HIKM module (fig. S5, C to F). We reasoned that this is likely because bmCENP-HN contributes an α helix to the pentameric coiled-coil structure (Fig. 2, A and B). Consistent with this hypothesis, the interaction between the CS module and the bmCENP-HIKM module was abolished when bmCENP-HN was deleted (bmCENP-HΔNIKM) (fig. S6A). On the basis of our observation that bmCENP-ON and bmCENP-PN extensions wrap around the CS module and that the bmCENP-OP module was substoichiometric in our initial bmCCAN purification (fig. S1D), we reasoned that the CS module might be an integral component of the bmCCAN, which helps to link and stabilize the bmCCAN structure, and in particular important in recruiting the bmCENP-OP. Consistent with our hypothesis, all distinct bmCCAN components were enriched on bmCENP-HIKM–immobilized beads, specifically in the presence of the CS module, with the most marked enhancement observed for the bmCENP-OP module (Fig. 2, C to E). Deletion of the bmCENP-HN abolished CS module recruitment and CS-mediated bmCCAN stabilization effects, confirming that specific interactions are necessary for correct bmCCAN assembly (Fig. 2, D and E). We also observed that the CS module readily became incorporated into the bmCCAN during the on-column reconstitutions and improved the stoichiometry of all other components in the main bmCCAN peak (fig. S6B). Last, including the CS module in parallel expression and purification of the bmCCAN notably improved the stoichiometry of the bmCENP-OP module (figs. S1 and S6C). Collectively, our structural and biochemical analyses suggest that the discovered CS module is an important component of the bmCCAN architecture that links multiple modules together and appears to be necessary for bmCENP-OP recruitment.

Unexpectedly, bmCENP-OP associated only weakly with the bmCCAN in the absence of the CS module despite bmCENP-OP sharing a large binding interface with the bmCENP-N protein (Fig. 1, A to C). This is different from the human or yeast systems, where CENP-N:CENP-O interactions are mediated primarily by an extension in the CENP-N protein that folds along CENP-O (fig. S7A) (8, 10, 25). The difference is likely due to the presence of CENP-QU in human and yeast that pulls CENP-OP away from the CENP-LN module, reducing the CENP-N:CENP-O interface. bmCENP-O is moved 25 Å toward bmCENP-LN, and bmCENP-P is rotated by 60° compared to the human CENP-O/P (fig. S7B) (18). A bmCENP-OΔN mutant, lacking the first 76 amino acids that interact with the CS module (Fig. 2, A and B), and a bmCENP-OCENP-Nm mutant (Leu141→Ala (L141A), Q190A, F192A, L195A, and E201A substitutions disrupting the bmCENP-N interaction; fig. S7C) both failed to associate with the bmCCAN in pull-down assays, even when the CS module was present (fig. S7, D and E). This suggests that multiple interactions are required for complete bmCENP-OP association with the bmCCAN. This is similar to the CCAN assembly principles in the human system, where disrupting individual subunit interfaces was sufficient to abolish the formation of the entire human CCAN (18).

The CS module is a bona fide inner kinetochore component in cells

To understand whether the CS module is a kinetochore component in cells, we initially performed immunoprecipitation (IP) experiments in Sf9 cells, targeting CS-1, CS-2, CENP-O, and CENP-P to profile their interactions by mass spectrometry (MS). MS revealed that CS-1/2 proteins could pull down all other known CCAN components (data S1). We could also detect CS proteins in reciprocal CENP-O/P pull-down experiments (data S1). In addition, we used MS to analyze overexpressed and heparin-purified bmCCAN (fig. S1D and data S2). Using these and previously published results, we identified other proteins that are also found at human centromeres, such as FACT, topoisomerases, cohesin, and condensin complexes, to be enriched at the bmCCAN (7, 29, 31, 34–36). This suggests that despite the holocentric architecture and the absence of CENP-A and CENP-C, similar factors likely govern centromere function across evolutionarily distant organisms.

We targeted the CS module in immunofluorescence (IF) microscopy experiments using a custom-made antibody against the complex. The CS module localized to mitotic chromosomes through all stages of mitosis, similar to other known bmCCAN components (Fig. 2F) (29). Furthermore, the CS module also colocalized with bmCENP-T and bmDsn1 proteins, established markers of inner and outer kinetochore in Lepidoptera, further supporting its integration into functional kinetochore complexes (Fig. 2G).

To assign CS module localization at the chromatin level, we performed chromatin IP and sequencing (ChIP-seq) targeting the CS module in unsynchronized B. mori cells (Fig. 2, H and I). We observed that the CS module profile correlated strongly with previously established bmCENP-T profiles, with the CS module assembling over large domains found along the entire length of the B. mori chromosome (31). The similarity between both CS and bmCENP-T ChIP-seq profiles is consistent with these two modules forming part of the same complex on chromatin in cells.

We took advantage of our specific antibodies to gain insights into localization dependencies between different modules of the bmCCAN. We found that CS module chromatin localization in cells was dependent on bmCENP-T, bmCENP-I, and bmCENP-H, components essential for bmCCAN assembly in cells (Fig. 3, A and B) (29). In contrast, depletion of the CS module did not affect bmCENP-T or bmCENP-HIKM localization, consistent with CS module localization depending on bmCENP-T and bmCENP-HIKM, but not vice versa (fig. S8, A to D). Depletion of the bmCENP-OP complex had minor effects on CS module levels on chromatin, while depletion of CS abolished bmCENP-OP complex recruitment (Fig. 3, C and D). These data are consistent with the CS module’s role in recruiting bmCENP-OP but not vice versa. Likewise, depletion of the bmCENP-OP complex did not affect chromatin levels of bmCENP-T, while depleting bmCENP-K reduced bmCENP-T localization to chromosomes, consistent with previous localization dependencies between bmCENP-T and the bmCENP-HIKM complex (fig. S8, A and B) (29). Together with previously published analysis of bmCENP-LN module localization dependencies, our work suggests that bmCENP-LN, bmCENP-HIKM, and bmCENP-T are interdependent and required for minimal bmCCAN assembly on chromatin (Fig. 3E). bmCENP-LN-HIKM-T then recruits the CS module, likely via bmCENP-HN, and the CS module in turn recruits bmCENP-OP.

RNA interference (RNAi)–mediated depletion of the CS module in cells resulted in an increased number of cells delayed or arrested in mitosis (Fig. 3F), although to a lesser extent than the depletion of other essential CCAN components, such as bmCENP-T (29). To understand the cause of the mitotic arrest, we performed live-cell imaging on cells depleted of the CS module (Fig. 3, G and H, and fig. S8E). We observed that cells arrested specifically in the metaphase, a defect consistent with the impairment of the kinetochore function. In addition, quantification of the defects upon CS depletion revealed an increased number of mitotic cells with unaligned chromosomes and congression defects (Fig. 3I). Although the observed phenotypes were less severe than the depletion of bmCENP-T, which, in contrast to the depletion of CS (fig. S8, F and G), completely abolishes the outer kinetochore recruitment (29), our results show that the CS module plays a role in high-fidelity chromosome segregation and correct cell cycle progression.

The CS module is evolutionarily related to the Dam1/DASH complex

We used homology prediction tools to understand the evolutionary origin and conservation of the CS module. PSI-BLAST identified a clear sequence homology between CS-3 and a subunit of the Dam1/DASH complex (KAK6332828.1; E value = 4 × 10−4), an established component of the fungal outer kinetochore. In addition, HHpred analyses revealed more distant similarities between three CS proteins and Dam1/DASH subunits (CS-2 with Dad1, CS-3 with Duo1, and CS-4 with Dam1), supporting a potential evolutionary relationship between these complexes (fig. S9).

The Dam1/DASH complex is a 10-subunit assembly that oligomerizes into a ring-like structure around microtubules and plays a key role in sustaining kinetochore-microtubule attachments under tension (37–40). These interactions are mediated in part by the C-terminal regions of Dam1/DASH subunits, including Dam1, Duo1, Spc19, and Spc34, which also serve as major targets of the error-correction pathway that ensures proper kinetochore-microtubule attachment (40, 41).

Structural comparisons revealed a close architectural similarity between the CS module and one half of the Dam1/DASH complex (Fig. 3J) (37, 38). Within this framework, CS-1 corresponds structurally to Dad3, CS-2 to Dad1, CS-3 to Duo1, and CS-4 to Dam1. This assignment is consistent with phylogenetic analyses showing that CS-2, CS-3, and CS-4 cluster with Dad1, Duo1, and Dam1, respectively, rather than with their corresponding paralogs within the Dam1/DASH complex (43). In addition, the N-terminal region of bmCENP-H exhibits structural similarity to Spc34, a Dam1/DASH subunit involved in Ndc80 binding (38), but sequence alignment does not detect any similarities.

Despite these sequence and structural similarities to the α-helical core of the Dam1/DASH monomer, the functional roles of the CS module and Dam1/DASH complex are likely distinct. Sequence alignments indicate that homology is restricted to the N-terminal helical regions that form the structural core of the Dam1/DASH complex, whereas the C-terminal extensions of Dam1/DASH subunits, such as Dam1 and Duo1, that mediate microtubule binding and error-correction signaling are not conserved in the CS proteins (fig. S10). Moreover, in the bmCCAN structure, the CS module is capped by the remainder of the bmCCAN complex, precluding the oligomerization characteristic of Dam1/DASH. Consistent with this observation, SEC-MALS analyses show that the CS module is monomeric in solution (Fig. 1 and fig. S5, A and B), and IP-MS analyses in lepidopteran cell lines targeting CS-1 and CS-2 did not identify additional putative Dam1/DASH homologs (data S1).

Together, these findings argue against conservation of Dam1/DASH–like functions in the CS module, such as oligomerization, microtubule binding, and error correction. Instead, on the basis of the requirement of the CS module for bmCCAN assembly in vitro and the chromosome localization of the CENP-OP complex in vivo, we propose that the CS module primarily serves a scaffolding role in the assembly of the B. mori inner kinetochore. In this context, and drawing parallels to the repeated reuse of structural protein domains across kinetochore components (such as the RWD fold) (28), we suggest that the five-helix core characteristic of the Dam1/DASH complex has been evolutionarily repurposed in Lepidoptera to support CCAN architecture rather than microtubule attachment.

bmCCAN entraps DNA in monomeric and dimeric states

To understand how the bmCCAN interacts with DNA, we determined the bmCCAN structure bound to linear double-stranded DNA (Fig. 4, figs. S11 and S12, table S1, and movie S1). We used the 147–base pair (bp) Widom 601 DNA sequence for our structural work, which has an AT content comparable to the B. mori genome (39). We obtained several reconstructions from this sample: apo-bmCCAN structure similar to the one described above; 2.8-Å reconstruction of the monomeric bmCCAN bound to ~30 bp of DNA (66% of bmCCAN particles; Fig. 4, A to C); and 3.9- to 4.5-Å multibody reconstructions of a head-to-head bmCCAN dimer bound to ~55 bp of DNA (19.2% of CCAN particles; Fig. 4, D and E).

In the monomeric bmCCAN-DNA structure, the DNA is fully entrapped by bmCENP-LN:T:HIKHead modules that form a closed chamber via protein-protein interactions to augment the central bmCENP-LN DNA binding channel (Fig. 4C). The DNA in this state bends slightly at the bmCENP-THFD:HIKHead interface but generally assumes a straight conformation. Overall, the DNA binding mode via the CENP-LN channel is similar to those reported for human and yeast CCAN complexes (8–10, 18, 20). However, in the absence of CENP-QU proteins, the DNA binding chamber is closed directly by prominent bmCENP-N:T interactions involving both bmCENP-THFD and bmCENP-T N-terminal extension (bmCENP-TExtension) that interacts with the bmCENP-NPyrin domain surface (Fig. 4, A to C).

In the dimeric state, two CCANs associate in a head-to-head manner (Fig. 4, D and E). The unique bmCCAN dimer conformation is achieved by dimerization between bmCENP-NPyrin:THFD:HIKHead groups of bmCCAN-1 and bmCCAN-2 that form a composite interface to bind the DNA duplex, with DNA itself further linking and stabilizing the dimer state. The DNA is first fully entrapped by bmCENP-LN:T:HIKHead modules of bmCCAN-1, as described for the monomeric CCAN:DNA complex (Fig. 5, A and B). The DNA is then wrapped around a deep electropositive groove formed by the dimerized bmCENP-NPyrin:THFD:HIKHead modules before passing through a pseudosymmetrically positioned bmCENP-LN channel in bmCCAN-2 (Fig. 5, A and B). Overall, ~50 bp of DNA are directly bound by the bmCCAN dimer, and the dimer formation induces an ~120° bend in the DNA (Figs. 4, C and D, and 5C). The DNA bending is likely achieved by the dimer formation itself, considering that DNA assumes a largely straight trajectory in the monomeric bmCCAN-DNA complex (movie S1). bmCENP-THFD, having an altered HFD, forms an atypical dimer along the outer surface of the central helix of its HFD (Fig. 5D). We observe a distinct density for a metal ion at the bmCENP-T:CENP-T dimer interface coordinated by H938 and E934 residues from both bmCCANs (Fig. 5E). The identity of the metal is unknown, but the coordination geometry and distances are consistent with unassigned tetrahedrally coordinated metal ions. Overall, the bmCCAN dimer forms via noncanonical interfaces to tightly grip and bend the DNA into a loop-like structure.

To correlate our structural observations with the behavior of the bmCCAN in solution, we measured the affinity between the bmCCAN and DNA constructs of different lengths using a fluorescence polarization (FP) assay (Fig. 5, F and G). We observed that the affinity of the bmCCAN for DNA increased as the DNA length increased from 26 to 53 bp, with the affinity of the bmCCAN for DNA of 53, 76, and 90 bp being very similar. This is consistent with our structural observations where ~50 bp of DNA are engaged by the bmCCAN directly (Fig. 5C). We also observed that mutating either the bmCENP-LN channel or bmCENP-T residues involved in DNA binding (in both monomeric and dimeric states) did not affect bmCCAN assembly but reduced bmCCAN binding to the DNA, consistent with the structure (fig. S13, A to F).

We also evaluated the role of bmCCAN assembly intermediates in DNA binding. We could detect weak DNA binding by the bmCENP-HIKM:T modules together but not by bmCENP-LN:T or bmCENP-HIKM:LN modules (fig. S13, G and H). Combining the bmCENP-LN:T:HIKM modules resulted in increased binding, consistent with these modules containing all DNA-interacting residues. Adding bmCENP-OP or the CS module separately to the bmCENP-LN:T:HIKM assembly only modestly changed the affinity for DNA, but combining all modules into a complete bmCCAN resulted in tight DNA binding. This is consistent with our observations that the CS module is required to recruit bmCENP-OP to the bmCCAN and that the bmCENP-OP module further stabilizes the bmCENP-LN structurally into a closed DNA binding channel conformation.

In the dimeric bmCCAN-DNA structure, DNA becomes an integral component of the complex, threading through two bmCCANs and linking them in a head-to-head configuration (Fig. 4, D and E). We used SEC-MALS to measure the bmCCAN molecular weight in the absence (apo-bmCCAN) or the presence of 147 bp of DNA. We observed that the addition of DNA converted the bmCCAN from a monomeric state to an exclusively dimeric state, with no higher oligomeric assemblies observed (Fig. 5H). This is consistent with cryo-EM observations where only monomeric apo-bmCCAN was observed in the absence of DNA (Fig. 1) and the dimeric state was detected when DNA was present (Fig. 4, D and E). Analytical ultracentrifugation further confirmed these observations in solution and in the absence of any cross-linking reagents, with the enrichment of the dimeric bmCCAN specifically observed in the presence of DNA (fig. S13I).

To evaluate the role of bmCENP-T in bmCCAN dimerization and assess the functional relevance of the dimeric bmCCAN, we introduced targeted mutations at the bmCCAN dimerization interface within bmCENP-T (bmCENP-TDimerMut). These residues are spatially separated from the DNA binding surface and are therefore predicted to specifically perturb dimerization without directly affecting DNA binding (fig. S14, A and B). Consistent with this prediction, bmCENP-TDimerMut did not impair the assembly of the apo-bmCCAN complex (fig. S14C). However, in the presence of DNA, mutations caused a modest reduction in bmCCAN dimer formation and led to a decreased affinity of the bmCCAN for DNA in vitro (fig. S14, D to F). Together, these results indicate that dimerization enhances bmCCAN-DNA interactions. The incomplete loss of dimerization suggests that disrupting the CENP-T dimerization interface alone is insufficient to fully prevent bmCCAN dimer formation, likely due to the extensive dimerization interface contributed by additional subunits, including bmCENP-N and the bmCENP-HIK modules. Consistently, ectopically expressed bmCENP-TDimerMut could complement the depletion of endogenous bmCENP-T as efficiently as ectopically expressed wild-type bmCENP-T, with no apparent defects in mitotic progression (fig. S15, A to C). Further studies, including the introduction of additional mutations across multiple subunits at the dimerization interface, will be required to more fully define the functional role of bmCCAN dimerization. At present, the cellular requirement for dimerization remains unresolved.

Last, we asked whether the bmCCAN, in addition to binding linear DNA, could also interact with canonical octameric H3 nucleosomes (H3Nuc), given that the B. mori genome lacks the centromere-specific CENP-A histone. We found that the bmCCAN did not bind human H3-containing octameric nucleosomes (>90% sequence similarity between human and B. mori histones) reconstituted with either 147 bp of DNA (lacking linker DNA) or 199 bp of DNA (containing ~30 bp of linker DNA on each side) (fig. S15D). These observations suggest that bmCCAN binding likely requires an extended stretch of free DNA and that the bmCCAN is unable to readily unwrap nucleosomal DNA termini. Considering previous findings that the bmCCAN is enriched along transcriptionally silent chromatin domains in B. mori, it is possible that additional factors, such as chromatin remodelers, govern the assembly of the bmCCAN into chromatin in these regions.

DISCUSSION

Our characterization of the bmCCAN reveals conserved and unique principles of kinetochore organization. Our structural analysis shows how the evolutionarily divergent bmCCAN entraps DNA using the CENP-LN channel, which emerges as the conserved cornerstone feature of all CCAN complexes irrespective of centromere type (Fig. 1D). We identify previously uncharacterized CS-1 to CS-4 proteins and show that they are evolutionarily related to the Dam1/DASH complex, an outer kinetochore component that stabilizes and regulates spindle microtubule attachments in various fungal species (38, 40, 41). The presence of the CS module as part of the bmCCAN suggests a functional repurposing of these proteins in stabilizing inner kinetochore formation instead of contributing to the outer kinetochore function. This function might have evolved to compensate for the absence of CENP-C and/or CENP-QU proteins, which play similar roles in other eukaryotes (17, 42, 44). In addition, the presence of distant homologs of Dam1/DASH complex components in other eukaryotes, particularly in those missing CENP-C and/or CENP-QU (45), raises the possibility that these homologs may also have been repurposed to function at the inner kinetochore rather than their well-established role at the outer kinetochore (43). Our study also emphasizes the value of experimental approaches in complementing the limitations of homology searches in identifying distant orthologs (43).

Beyond the innovations at the inner kinetochore, the bmCCAN can also assemble in vitro into a symmetric, head-to-head dimer in the presence of DNA. This configuration is stabilized by the dimerization of the atypical bmCENP-T histone-fold and bmCENP-N proteins. We also observed that dimerization induces a 120° turn in the DNA. A head-to-head dimeric structure has also been observed in an in vitro–reconstituted Saccharomyces cerevisiae CCAN-CENP-ANuc complex, where two yeast CCAN modules bind the unwrapped DNA ends of yeast CENP-ANuc from either side (9). However, unlike the CCAN-driven dimerization in B. mori, the yeast complex is dimerized through its association with the CENP-ANuc (fig. S16, A and B). These dimeric architectures contrast with the human CCAN-CENP-ANuc complex, which has been characterized so far only in a monomeric state (fig. S16C) (18).

The cellular requirement for bmCCAN dimerization remains unresolved, particularly as the CENP-T dimerization mutant did not result in detectable mitotic defects. However, the dimerization observed in vitro offers a plausible model for how kinetochores in holocentric chromosomes might be organized from discrete, self-contained modular units (Fig. 6, A to D). In this model, holocentric kinetochore assembly could be achieved by repeating bmCCAN dimer or monomer units along chromatin at discrete genomic loci. This organization may extend to mitosis, wherein the longitudinal bands observed along the chromosome length consist of multiple, discrete kinetochore units that individually recapitulate some or all fundamental functions of kinetochores, such as robust microtubule attachment, error-correction pathway, and biorientation. However, we cannot exclude other conformations of the bmCCAN that could be driven by mitotic conditions and factors associated with it, nor can we exclude additional proteins that could link multiple bmCCAN dimers together.

Overall, our results reveal that kinetochore assembly is governed by conserved, modular principles regardless of centromere type. The same kinetochore units, related in structure to either monomeric or dimeric CCAN organizations, may be modularly repeated to support chromosome segregation in organisms with vastly different centromeric organizations, including holocentric insects.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cloning of the proteins for purification

Genes encoding B. mori CENP proteins (CENP-H, CENP-I, CENP-K, CENP-M, CENP-O, CENP-P, CENP-L, CENP-N, and CENP-T) including mutants and the CS module were synthesized by GenScript with codon optimization for insect cells. Mutants involved in this study are as follows: CENP-OCENP-Nm (L141A, Q190A, F192A, L195A, and E201A), CENP-OΔN (M1-N76 deletion), CENP-HΔN (M1-T33 deletion), CENP-LNmut (K281E, K263E, K285E, and K197E mutations in CENP-N and K202E, K204E, R195E, and K112E mutations in CENP-L), CENP-Tmut (K890E, K892E, K895E, and R896E), and CENP-TDimerMut (H938A, E934A, K931A, E960A, K945A, and K944A).

Genes for the CENP-HIKM, CENP-OP, CENP-LN, and CS module and their corresponding mutants as indicated in the text and in the figure legends were subsequently cloned into the pAcGP67-Dual expression vector. Tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease cleavable octahistidine (His8)-tag followed by SNAP-tag was added to the N terminus of CENP-T protein and CENP-T mutants. TEV protease cleavable double streptag-II (DS) tags were added to the C termini of CENP-I. TEV protease cleavable SNAP-tag followed by DS tag was added to the C terminus of CENP-P. CENP-LN was subcloned with C-terminal TEV-cleavable SNAP-DS tags on CENP-N and an N-terminal TEV-cleavable GST-tag on CENP-L.

Expression and purification of B. mori proteins from insect cells

The baculoviruses for the expression of all protein complexes and mutants were generated using standard protocols on the basis of the pAcGP67 vector. All proteins were expressed in Sf9 cells, which were not tested for mycoplasma contamination and were not authenticated. Typically, 4 liters of Sf9 cells were infected 2.5% (v/v) with the P3 cell culture, and the cells were harvested 48 to 72 hours after infection by centrifugation.

CENP-HIKM

Cells for CENP-HIKM and CENP-HIKM mutant modules containing Twin-Strep-tag were lysed by sonication in 50 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), and 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail tablets (Roche), phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), benzamidine, and benzonase. Cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 40,000 rpm for 1 hour at 4°C. Supernatants were passed through a 1.5-μm syringe filter and applied to three 5-ml Strep-Tactin Superflow Plus Cartridge columns (Qiagen) preequilibrated with lysis buffer. The Strep-Tactin column was washed with lysis buffer, and the protein was eluted in 50 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP with 5 mM desthiobiotin (Sigma-Aldrich). The Strep-Tactin–eluted fractions containing the CENP-HIKM module were diluted to a final salt concentration of 300 mM NaCl and directly loaded onto the HiTrap Heparin HP (Cytiva) affinity column preequilibrated with 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP. CENP-HIKM was eluted in 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP with a NaCl gradient from 300 to 2000 mM NaCl. Peak fractions were concentrated and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

CENP-LN

For CENP-LN and CENP-LN mutants, cells were lysed by sonication in 50 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM TCEP, and 1 mM EDTA supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail tablets (Roche), PMSF, benzamidine, and benzonase. Cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 40,000 rpm for 1 hour at 4°C. Supernatants were passed through a 1.5-μm syringe filter and applied to three 5-ml Strep-Tactin Superflow Plus Cartridge columns (Qiagen) preequilibrated with lysis buffer. The Strep-Tactin column was washed with lysis buffer, and the protein was eluted in 50 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP, with 5 mM desthiobiotin (Sigma-Aldrich). TEV protease was added to the Strep-Tactin–eluted fraction for tag removal at 4°C for overnight incubation. The untagged CENP-LN sample was collected in the flow-through from the Ni-NTA affinity column in the buffer containing 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 0.3 M NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM TCEP, and 20 mM imidazole and was directly loaded onto the HiTrap Heparin HP (Cytiva) affinity column preequilibrated with 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP. Untagged CENP-LN was eluted in 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP with a NaCl gradient from 300 to 2000 mM NaCl. Peak fractions were concentrated and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

CENP-OP

For CENP-OP and CENP-OP mutants, cells were lysed by sonication in 50 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM TCEP, and 1 mM EDTA supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail tablets (Roche), PMSF, benzamidine, and benzonase. Cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 40,000 rpm for 1 hour at 4°C. The filtered lysate was applied to three 5-ml Strep-Tactin Superflow Plus Cartridge columns (Qiagen) preequilibrated with lysis buffer. The Strep-Tactin column was washed with lysis buffer, and the protein was eluted in 50 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP, with 5 mM desthiobiotin (Sigma-Aldrich). The Strep-Tactin eluate was dialyzed in 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 0.3 M NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP at 4°C overnight. The dialyzed CENP-OP was loaded onto the HiTrap Heparin HP (Cytiva) affinity column preequilibrated with 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP. CENP-OP was eluted in 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP with a NaCl gradient from 300 to 2000 mM NaCl. Peak fractions were concentrated and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

CENP-T

For CENP-T and CENP-T mutants, cell pellets were resuspended in 50 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP without EDTA and supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail EDTA-free tablets, PMSF, benzamidine, and benzonase. Cells were lysed on ice by sonication, and the cell lysate was clarified by centrifugation for 1 hour at 40,000 rpm. The supernatant was incubated with nickel-nitriloacetic acid (Ni-NTA) agarose beads (Qiagen) for 3 hours with gentle rotation, followed by washing the beads in 25 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 1 M NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP with 20 mM imidazole. CENP-T proteins were eluted from the beads in 25 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 0.3 M NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP, with 300 mM imidazole. Ni-NTA–eluted CENP-T was dialyzed in 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP at 4°C overnight. Dialyzed CENP-T was further purified using a 5-ml HiTrap Heparin HP column (Cytiva) preequilibrated in 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP. CENP-T was eluted with 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP, with a NaCl gradient of 300 to 2000 mM. Peak fractions were concentrated and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

CS module

For the CS module, cells were lysed by sonication in lysis buffer containing 50 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP, and 10% glycerol supplemented with benzamidine, PMSF, EDTA-free protease inhibitor tablets (Roche), and benzonase. The cell lysate was clarified using a 1-hour ultracentrifuge spin at 40,000 rpm. The clarified lysate was loaded onto a Strep-Tactin column (Qiagen), immobilized protein was washed with lysis buffer, and the complex was eluted in a buffer containing 50 mM tris, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, and 10% glycerol, with the addition of 5 mM desthiobiotin (Sigma-Aldrich). TEV protease was added to cleave the Twin-Strep tag at 4°C overnight. Cleaved protein was diluted to 100 mM NaCl and applied to a 5-ml HiTrap HP Q column (Cytiva) equilibrated with 20 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP. CS was eluted with a gradient of 100 to 600 mM NaCl over 20 column volumes. The peak fractions were concentrated and applied onto a Superdex 200 16/600 column (Cytiva) equilibrated in 20 mM Hepes, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, and 10% glycerol buffer. Peak fractions were concentrated and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Large-scale CCAN assembly for proteomics and structural studies

CENP-HIKM, CENP-OP, and CENP-LN, with and without the CS module, were purified via the Strep-Tactin column (Qiagen) individually, as described above. CENP-T was purified using Ni-NTA agarose beads (Qiagen), as described above. CENP-LN was incubated with TEV protease to remove tags overnight, while CENP-T was dialyzed overnight to remove imidazole. CENP-LN was passed through a Ni-NTA affinity column to remove tags and TEV protease. All components were then combined, and salt was adjusted to 250 mM NaCl using a 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, buffer. The complex was loaded onto a HiTrap Heparin HP (Cytiva) affinity column preequilibrated with 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 250 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP. The CCAN complex was eluted in 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP with a NaCl gradient from 250 to 1000 mM. Peak fractions of the CCAN complex from the HiTrap Heparin HP column (Cytiva) were concentrated and dialyzed overnight to reduce the salt concentration in the buffer containing 20 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP. For CCAN-DNA structural work, a 147-bp Widom 601 DNA sequence (EpiCypher) was added at an equimolar ratio to the CCAN complex before dialysis. After dialysis, the CCAN or CCAN-DNA complex was ultracentrifuged at 50,000 rpm to remove any larger aggregates. For the apo-bmCCAN structure, the CCAN complex was on-column cross-linked using a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 column (Cytiva) by injecting 0.25% glutaraldehyde at a flow rate set to 0.3 ml/min for 7 ml before injecting the CCAN complex. For the CCAN-DNA structure, a 5 mM BS3-PEG5 chemical cross-linker was used instead of glutaraldehyde, but an identical procedure was followed. Last, the peak apo-bmCCAN or CCAN-DNA fractions were further purified using a Superose 6 Increase 3.2/300 column (Cytiva) in buffer containing 20 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP.

Cryo-EM grid preparation

Three microliters of apo-bmCCAN sample or CCAN-DNA sample at A280 (absorbance at 280 nm) = 1.0 (as measured by NanoDrop, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was applied to a Quantifoil 1.2/1.3 (300 Cu mesh) grid previously glow discharged using a Solarus plasma-cleaner for 60 s. Grids were blotted for 3 s at 7 force before being plunge vitrified in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The blotting chamber was maintained at 4°C and 100% humidity during freezing.

Cryo-EM data collection

Movies were collected using a Titan Krios G3i (Thermo Fisher Scientific) outfitted with a K3 camera and Bioquantum energy filter (Gatan). The K3 detector was operated in noncorrelated double-sampling counting mode, and the energy filter slit width was set to 20 eV. Movies were collected at a nominal magnification of 105,000× and a physical pixel size of 0.838 Å pixel−1, with a 50-μm C2 aperture and 100-μm objective aperture at a dose rate of 15 e− pixel−1 s−1. A total dose of 60 to 62 e− Å−2 was collected as a 59-frame movie, resulting in a 3-s movie with 1.02 to 1.05 e− Å−2 dose per frame.

Cryo-EM data processing

Both apo-bmCCAN and CCAN-DNA datasets were preprocessed on the fly using cryoSPARC version 4.0 (apo-bmCCAN) and version 4.2.1 (CCAN-DNA) (46). Movies were motion corrected using patch motion correction, contrast transfer function (CTF) information was estimated using patch CTF estimation, and micrographs were curated on the basis of CTF resolution fit. Default settings were used for all of the preprocessing steps.

For both datasets, similar initial processing steps were followed in cryoSPARC. Two datasets were processed independently. Initially, blob picking was used to overpick all micrographs. These picked particles were extracted in a 384-pixel box and binned to 128 pixels. Two-dimensional (2D) classification was run with 100 classes, 40 iterations, and 400 particles per class to curate the particles. “Good” (showing clear secondary structural features) class averages were selected, and three ab initio 3D models were generated using default parameters. The best ab initio class was further refined via a nonuniform refinement job type. The “good” classes were also used as templates to repick particles in all the apo-bmCCAN micrographs. Two rounds of heterogeneous refinement were used to curate all particles with sorting against one good CCAN volume derived above and four decoy noise volumes, which were generated via ab initio jobs using “bad” (clear noise or fragmented particles from the initial 2D classification jobs) particles from each dataset. The CCAN particles subsequently underwent nonuniform refinement and were then reextracted with a 400-pixel box without binning. Nonuniform refinement was used to generate high-resolution CCAN maps, which were used for per-particle motion correction as implemented in cryoSPARC to further polish the particle stack. Subsequently, different steps were used for the two datasets.

For apo-bmCCAN, 3D classification without alignment was used as implemented in cryoSPARC using principal component analysis initialization mode, 20 classes, and limiting resolution to 7 Å. This resulted in several distinct structural classes split over the set of 20 computational classes. The structural classes are bmCENP-LN-HIKM-T class, bmCCANLN-HIKM-T-OP class, and the complete apo-bmCCAN. Each class underwent nonuniform refinement to generate high-resolution maps. To improve the resolution around the mobile bmCENP-HIKHead domain, a mask was generated around the density map in this region to exclude all density outside of the bmCENP-HIKHead domain. Local refinement as implemented in cryoSPARC was used to improve the density map using pose/shift Gaussian prior set to 5° and 3 Å for rotations and shifts, respectively. The local resolution map was computationally aligned on the consensus map and then combined [using Phenix 1.20.1 (47)] with the consensus map into a single composite map suitable for modeling building.

For monomeric bmCCAN-DNA, 3D classification without alignment was used as implemented in cryoSPARC using simple initialization mode, 10 classes, and limiting resolution to 10 Å. apo-bmCCAN and flexible CCAN-DNA classes were excluded, while rigid monomeric CCAN-DNA particles underwent further nonuniform refinement. Local refinement with a mask around the mobile bmCENP-HIKHead domain was used (pose/shift Gaussian prior method with 5° and 3 Å for rotations and shifts, respectively) to improve the resolution around this region. The bmCENP-HIKHead domain local resolution map was computationally aligned on the consensus map of monomeric bmCCAN-DNA and then combined [using Phenix 1.20.1 (47)] with the consensus map into a single composite map suitable for modeling building.

For dimeric bmCCAN-DNA, particles were reextracted using a 768-pixel box and 3× bin. 2D classification with 50 classes was used to detect larger bmCCAN-DNA dimer classes, which were selected, and ab initio models were generated. Nonuniform refinement was used to improve the dimeric bmCCAN-DNA reconstruction. Subsequently, heterogeneous refinement was used to sort all good bmCCAN particles against either the monomeric bmCCAN-DNA class, dimeric bmCCAN-DNA class, and decoy noise volume. Dimeric bmCCAN-DNA particles were then reextracted in a 600-pixel box and binned to a 400-pixel box, giving an effective pixel size of 1.0875 Å. Particles were moved to RELION 5.0 (48), where 3D refinement produced a consensus reconstruction. During these steps, the CCAN-1 body was always well resolved, while the CCAN-2 body appeared to be more flexible. A mask around CCAN-2 was applied, and classification without alignment in RELION 5.0 was used to classify bmCCAN-DNA dimers into three classes. One of the classes showed strong features for the CCAN-2 body, and that class was further refined, giving a consensus bmCCAN-DNA dimer map used as the final map in the figure presentation and data deposition. Masks around CCAN-1, CENP-HIKHead-T-NPyrin dimers, and CCAN-2 were used for MultiBody reconstruction with Blush regularization (49) of the composite bmCCAN-DNA dimer structure. The CENP-HIKHead-T-NPyrin dimer body was used as the main body around which two CCAN bodies were allowed to move. The three bodies were aligned using the bmCCAN-dimer consensus map and then combined using Phenix 1.20.1 (47) to generate maps suitable for model building.

Model building

AlphaFold2 (50) was used to generate initial models for subcomplexes: CENP-OP, CENP-LN, CENP-HIKMHead and CENP-HIKMBody, CS module, and CENP-THFD. The models were rigid body fitted into the cryo-EM density maps using Chimera. Subsequently, Coot (51) was used to manually correct and rebuild the complete atomic models. The complete models were then refined in Phenix (version 1.20.1) (47) using real-space refinement algorithms using standard parameters.

CS module identification from cryo-EM maps

A 3.0-Å map of the apo-bmCCAN was used as input into ModelAngelo software (33) in sequence-less mode (build_no_seq), and standard settings were used to generate a homology profile for each of the chains that form the CS module. The homology profile for each CS module chain was then used in the HHBlits algorithm (52) to query the UniRef100 database. The top hits were then manually inspected, and the top hit for each CS protein was used in the BLAST algorithm against the S. frugiperda–annotated genome to find the sfCS proteins. SfCS protein sequences were then used to query the B. mori database to find the bmCS module components.

For example, the top hit from HHblits for CS-4 protein was UniRef100_A0A2A4IU08 Uncharacterized with a P value of 100.0. This corresponds to an uncharacterized protein in Heliothis virescens (tobacco budworm moth). The sequence of this protein is MSALGNMVQSTQQLQQDLQNELTTSNQLLRLISIELQQIKYLTSSGGEFESNLTKNSSLVKTLANMQQVDIENLPTFTRKQIDMSMNLTQNASYEEHNTFRNESEMEESQ. This sequence was used to find S. frugiperda CS-4 protein (original protein in the cryo-EM density map) with the sequence MSALGNLSQGNQQQDLQNELTTANQLLRLISLELEQIKNLTNPAGDFAANIKKNAELVKKLANIQQVDLTNLPPFTRKYIDLNENANQNVSYEEHNTFRNESEMDESQ. The sequence identity between the two proteins is 70%.

The following B. mori CS module sequences were found in this study: >CS-1 (KWMTBOMO01279): MAQLQQLQQDLQNELITTNQLLHLIGNELQQIICLTSSGGEIEKNIKKNASLMSILANLQHIDIENIPLVTRNQVTSNQNTEELNLQRNVLDETD; >CS-2 (KWMTBOMO05467): MTSVAPSDSESNVSGFVRYSDKSFKHLYTSVLEKLELINQDILQIENNLKNLIQHAGPLESQLSAVLHSLPKPNTNTAMETE; >CS-3 (KWMTBOMO00944): MSNNSETSLSQRDKAAELPMELESIRALNACLQAYLEHIRNVKRNLLAMNDNYRDLEDVNRQWQEVVEFRRE; >CS-4: MNSTVSDDLTRENSASEISKCISRLSANNRNLSLLHKELLEINLSVKAYVQESKKRLQGLRLDLAHEARSSHQVVELLTEK. Note that the B. mori CS-4 protein was not annotated in the current B. mori genome assembly but instead inferred from full-length cDNA clones.

Modeling of the complete B. mori holo-kinetochore

We used our experimentally determined bmCCAN structure and AlphaFold2-predicted bmKMN structure (50) to model the complete B. mori kinetochore at the molecular level (Fig. 6). First, we had to assign the orientation to the bmCCAN dimer with respect to the chromosome and the mitotic spindle. A unique feature of the bmCCAN is that bmCENP-T, apart from binding to the bmCENP-HIKHead via cryptic HFD, also binds to the bmCENP-HIKBody via an additional α helix (CENP-Tα-helix). Because of this additional bmCENP-Tα-helix, the N terminus of CENP-T that binds bmKMN faces away from the bmCCAN from the top of the loop structure, as shown in Fig. 6. Thus, the unique bmCENP-T binding mode to the bmCCAN defines the polarity of the inner kinetochore. Second, we had to connect the inner and outer kinetochores and determine the stoichiometry of the bmKMN. The disordered bmCENP-T N terminus is the only known bmKMN-binding region of the bmCCAN, and bmCENP-T is sufficient to recruit the outer kinetochore in cells (29). AlphaFold2, however, could not predict a consistent high-confidence model for the bmCENP-T:KMN interaction, potentially suggesting that multiple CENP-T regions could bind separate bmKMN modules, similar to the human CENP-T. This would also be consistent with recent findings that multiple human KMN molecules are necessary for stable kinetochore-microtubule attachments (53). Therefore, we modeled four KMN complexes bound per bmCCAN dimer connected to the bmCENP-T in an unknown manner. We also suggest that alternative stoichiometries are possible if more than two bmCENP-T are recruited to the bmCCAN or if other uncharacterized factors contribute to outer kinetochore recruitment (Fig. 6B).

In vitro SEC reconstitutions

Purified CENP-HIKM, CENP-OP, CENP-LN, CENP-T, and CS module were analyzed in a buffer containing 25 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP using the microAKTA system and the Superose 6 Increase 3.2/300 column (Cytiva). FP assay

All FP measurements were conducted on an EnVision 2103 Multilabel Reader (PerkinElmer), with excitation filter at 480 nm and emission filter at 535 nm, and carried out in a ProxiPlate 384-well, round, black-bottom plate (PerkinElmer, 6008260). FP assays were performed in binding buffer of 20 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, and 0.01% IGEPAL CA-630. 5′ fluorescein–labeled DNA of different lengths, 15-, 26-, 36-, 46-, 53-, 76-, and 90-bp sequences (IDT), was evaluated for the optimal binding by the CCAN complex. The CCAN complex was serially diluted in the binding buffer containing 2.5 nM labeled DNA. The binding reaction was analyzed after overnight incubation at 4°C. The data for each condition were collected at least in biological triplicate. All data points were displayed on the graphs with standard deviation (SD) displayed as error bars. DNA sequences used for the FP assay were as follows: 15 bp: 5′-6-FAM-TGGATATTTGGACCG-3′; 26 bp: 5′-6-FAM-TGTGGATATTTGGACCGCTTTGAGGC-3′; 36 bp: 5′-6-FAM-TAATCTGCAAGTGGATATTTGGACCGCTTTGAGGCC-3′; 46 bp: 5′-6-FAM-TAATCTGCAAGTGGATATTTGGACCGCTTTGAGGCCTTCGTTGGAA-3′; 53 bp: 5′-6-FAM-TAATCTGCAAGTGGATATTTGGACCGCTTTGAGGCCTTCGTTGGAAACGGGAAT-3′; 76 bp: 5′-6-FAM-TAATCTGCAAGTGGATATTTGGACCGCTTTGAGGCCTTCGTTGGAAACGGGAATATCTTCACATAAAAACTAAACA-3′; 90 bp: 5′-6-FAM-TAATCTGCAAGTGGATATTTGGACCGCTTTGAGGCCTTCGTTGGAAACGGGAATATCTTCACATAAAAACTAAACAGAAGCATTCTCAGA-3′.

Analytical ultracentrifugation

Sedimentation velocity experiments were performed in an Optima XL-I analytical ultracentrifuge (Beckman-Coulter, Indianapolis, IN) using an An60-Ti 4-hole rotor. Data were acquired using the ultraviolet/visible absorbance system on the centrifuge. Experiments were run at 20°C and 20,000 rpm using cells assembled with 12-mm charcoal-filled EPON centerpieces (Spin Analytical, Berwick, ME) and quartz windows. Samples included (i) apo-bmCCAN at 1.5 μM, (ii) 147-bp Widom 601 DNA (EpiCypher, 18-0006) alone at 0.8 μM, and (iii) bmCCAN-DNA mixture at 1.5 μM and DNA at 0.8 μM. All samples were prepared in 20 mM Hepes, pH 8.0, with 300 mM NaCl and 1 mM TCEP. Samples were equilibrated to 20°C for 1.5 hours before the run was started. Sedimentation was monitored by absorbance at 280 nm in continuous mode with a radial step size of 0.003 cm. A total of 330 scans were collected for each sample over ~7 hours.

The differential distribution of the sedimentation coefficient, c(s), for each sample was calculated using Sedfit version 18.1 (54). Sedimentation coefficients were corrected to standard conditions, S20,w, i.e., sedimentation in water at 20°C.

Mass photometry measurements

Mass photometry measurements were performed on a OneMP instrument (Refeyn) using silicone gaskets mounted on clean glass coverslips. Coverslips were cleaned immediately before use and dried under clean airflow/nitrogen, after which a silicone gasket was applied to form individual wells. In each well, buffer was first added to obtain focus using the droplet-dilution/autofocus routine, and the sample was then introduced into the same droplet, mixed by gentle pipetting, and measured immediately.

For fully purified wild-type apo-bmCCAN or bmCCAN and bmCCANDimerMut (using CENP-TDimerMut) containing 147-bp DNA, 10 μl of sample was loaded at a final protein concentration of 10 nM. Movies (60 s) were recorded in AcquireMP (Refeyn) and processed in DiscoverMP (Refeyn). Mass calibration was generated in DiscoverMP from measurements of protein standards of known mass acquired in the same buffer, applying the resulting linear contrast-to-mass calibration to all datasets. Mass distributions were obtained from detected landing events and fitted with Gaussian functions in DiscoverMP to estimate the mean mass and relative abundances.

Analysis and quantification of the FP assay

GraphPad Prism 10 was used for all FP data analysis and fitting. Nonlinear regression analysis of the response curve (change in fluorescence anisotropy) against protein concentration produced the best fit of curves to the data from which mean half effective concentration (EC50) values were calculated with the standard error of the mean (SEM), as implemented in Prism 10. These values are stated in the text and figures for each condition. The binding equations and curves used to estimate KD (dissociation constant) values did not produce perfect fits, probably because of some degree of cooperativity during DNA binding that was difficult to mathematically approximate. Therefore, we state the obtained EC50 values for which we have greater confidence.

Streptavidin bead pull-down assay

Strep-Tactin XT 4Flow resin (IBA Lifesciences) was washed with binding buffer containing 20 mM Hepes, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.01% IGEPAL CA-630, and BSA (bovine serum albumin; 0.1 mg/ml). Different CENP protein complexes, as indicated in the figures and figure legends, were incubated with streptavidin bead slurry at 30°C for 1 hour in binding buffer with gentle shaking on a thermomixer (Eppendorf). The final protein concentration for all components was 0.5 μM. The beads were pelleted and washed three times with 20 mM Hepes, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 0.01% IGEPAL CA-630 to remove the unbound protein, and the immobilized protein was incubated for 10 min at 30°C with elution buffer containing 20 mM Hepes, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.01% IGEPAL CA-630, and 50 mM biotin. The pretreated and biotin-eluted samples were analyzed by NuPAGE 4 to 12% Bis-Tris protein gels (Invitrogen) and InstantBlue Coomassie staining. All reactions were performed at least in triplicate for quantification.

Quantification of the pull-down assays

Coomassie-stained NuPAGE 4 to 12% Bis-Tris protein gels were analyzed using FIJI 2.9.0 software (55). Standard protocols were used to select each lane on the gel and to quantify the protein band intensities in each lane, obtaining arbitrary intensity values. These values were normalized using band intensity for a given protein in the input lane. For example, the CENP-T band intensity in the input and in the elution was estimated using this method, and the fraction of CENP-T protein bound was obtained by dividing the CENP-T band intensity in the elution by the CENP-T band intensity in the input. Some proteins, such as CENP-O and CENP-N, run at a very similar position on the SDS-PAGE gel. Therefore, combined values of CENP-O/N intensity were reported, given that we could not unambiguously separate these two proteins. The quantification measurements were performed across at least three biological replicates, and all values were plotted. The columns represent mean values with error bars representing SD.

SEC with multiangle light scattering (SEC-MALS)

Molar mass analysis was performed using an Agilent HPLC 1200/1220/1260/1290 series system connected to a DAWN MALS detector (Wyatt Technology) and an Optilab differential refractive index (RI) detector (Wyatt Technology). Protein gel band identification and confirmation

To identify unknown proteins using 2D liquid chromatography–tandem MS (LC-MS/MS), we used a 6545XT AdvanceBio LC/Q-TOF system equipped with an Agilent PLRP-S column (2.1 by 50 mm, 5 μm, 1000 Å). Insect kinetochore heparin column fraction MS analysis

Heparin peak fractions corresponding to assembled complete bmCCAN were cleaned up using acetone precipitation. The air-dried pellet was reconstituted with 50 μl of 8 M urea and 50 mM Hepes, pH 8.0; reduced (5 mM dithiothreitol, 45 min at 37°C); alkylated (15 mM iodoacetamide, 20 min at room temperature in the dark); and quenched [5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 15 min at room temperature in the dark]. The sample was diluted to 2 M urea and digested overnight at 37°C with sequencing grade trypsin (Promega, 1:50 enzyme:protein ratio). The digested peptides were acidified with 20% trifluoroacetic acid (1% TFA), centrifuged at 18,000g for 15 min, and desalted using C18 stage-tips. Resultant peptides were dried and resolubilized in 0.15% TFA for high pH reversed-phase fractionation using a commercially available kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fractionation was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol with a modified elution scheme where 16 fractions were collected [F1: 6.25% acetonitrile (ACN); F2: 7.5% ACN; F3: 8.75% ACN; F4: 10% ACN; F5: 11.25% ACN; F6: 12.5% ACN; F7: 13.75% ACN; F8: 15% ACN; F9: 16.25% ACN; F10: 17.5% ACN; F11: 18.75% ACN; F12: 20% ACN; F13: 21.25% ACN; F14: 22.5% ACN; F15: 23.75% ACN; F16: 40% ACN] and then combined into 8 fractions (F1 + F9, F2 + F10, F3 + F11, F4 + F12, F5 + F13, F6 + F14, F7 + F15, and F8 + F16). The samples were analyzed with a total run time of 95 min; FAIMS Pro DUO of −40 and −60 compensation voltage collected FTMS1 scans at a 120,000 resolution with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 1 × 106 and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. FTMS2 scans on precursors with charge states of 3 to 6 were collected at a 15,000 resolution with collision-induced dissociation fragmentation at a normalized collision energy of 30%, an AGC target of 2 × 104, and a maximum injection time of 11 ms.

Offline search was performed using comet version 2019.01 with the following parameters: a UniProt Trichoplusia ni and S. frugiperda database, 2025 version; static modification of Cys carbamidomethylation (+57.0215); and variable modification of Met oxidation (+15.9949). The peptide false discovery rate was filtered to <1% using a linear discriminator algorithm.

Affinity co-IPs

Cultures of the Sf9 cells expressing full-length S. frugiperda kinetochore proteins fused to a C-terminal 3xFLAG tag or control wild-type Sf9 cells were grown to the exponential phase in Sf-900 II SFM medium (Gibco, cat. no. 10902-088). The pellets were resuspended in 3 ml of HDG150 buffer (20 mM Hepes, pH 7.0, 150 mM KCl, 10% glycerol, 0.5 mM DTT, and one tablet cOmplete Protease Inhibitor), and then the cells were disrupted with 50 strokes using a Dounce homogenizer at 4°C. N3755-500UN) for 1 hour at 4°C on a roller, and 3 mM CaCl2 was added to the digestions. The MNase digestions were stopped by adding 50 μl of 0.2 M EGTA. To solubilize the digested chromatin, 4 ml of HDG400 buffer (20 mM Hepes, pH 7.0, 400 mM KCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM DTT, 0.05% NP-40, and one tablet cOmplete Protease Inhibitor) was added to the samples and incubated for 2 hours at 4°C on a roller. The M2 magnetic beads were prepared according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The digested chromatin samples were incubated with 30 μl of anti-FLAG M2 beads. Proteomics and MS analysis of native samples

In LC-MS/MS analysis, data-independent acquisition (DIA) analyses were performed with an RSLCnano system (Ultimate 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer, interfaced by a Nanospray Flex ion source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were trapped on a 2-cm nanoViper precolumn (75-μm inner diameter by 2 cm, C18 Acclaim PepMap 100, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a flow rate of 2.5 μl/min in 98% buffer A (2/98 CH3CN/H2O and 0.1% formic acid) for 4 min to desalt and concentrate the samples. Peptides were then injected onto a C18 column (75-μm inner diameter by 50-cm double nanoViper PepMap Neo, 2 μm, 100 Å, Thermo Fisher Scientific) regulated at a temperature of 50°C and separated with a linear gradient from 98% buffer A (2/98 CH3CN/H2O and 0.1% formic acid) to 30% buffer B (98/2 CH3CN/H2O and 0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 300 nl/min over 91 min. Peptides were analyzed in the mass spectrometer, applying a 2400-V spray voltage, funnel radio-frequency level at 40%, and a heated capillary temperature set to 280°C. MS full scans were recorded in centroid mode for ranges of 375 to 1500 mass/charge ratio (m/z) with a resolution of 120,000 at m/z 200, a normalized AGC target set at 300%, and a maximum injection time set to auto. The MS2 acquisitions were performed in centroid mode and on an auto range scan mode after fragmentation using higher-energy collision-induced dissociation with 25% normalized collision energy, a normalized AGC target of 3000%, and a resolution of 15,000. A window width of 15 Th (Thomsons) with 1 Th overlap, 40 scan events, and a maximum injection time set to auto were chosen for DIA on a 400 to 1000 m/z precursor mass range.

In data processing, for identification, the data were searched against the S. frugiperda corn annotation OGS6.1 database (https://bipaa.genouest.org/sp/spodoptera_frugiperda_pub/) manually curated to include the sequences of S. frugiperda CENP-K and CENP-N using Pulsar search engine through Spectronaut version 18.7 (Biognosys) by directDIA+ analysis using default search settings. Enzyme specificity was set to trypsin, and a maximum of two missed cleavage sites was allowed. N-terminal acetylation and oxidation of methionine were set as variable modifications. The resulting files were further processed using myProMS version 3.10 (https://github.com/bioinfo-pf-curie/myproms) (58).

For protein quantification, MS2 extracted ion chromatograms from proteotypic peptides shared between compared conditions (TopN matching) were used with missed cleavages allowed. Median and scale normalization at the peptide level were applied on the total signal to correct the extracted ion chromatograms for each biological replicate (N = 2 or 3). To evaluate the statistical significance of the change in protein abundance, a linear model (adjusted on peptides and biological replicates) was performed, and a two-sided t test was applied on the fold change estimated by the model. The P values were then adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. The MS proteomics raw data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE (59) partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD063391.

Analyses of MS data

The hits for each IP-MS were generated by applying the following filters in the dataset: fold change over control: 2 across the replicates; number of unique peptides: >2 in all three replicates. From the individual IP hits, those that are common between any sets of proteins and CENP-O, CENP-P, CS-1, and CS-2 (refer to sheet “consolidated”) were identified and listed in the additional sheets in data S1. The description of protein function was added on the basis of either known function at the kinetochore or on the basis of BLAST searches of the human or Drosophila melanogaster proteomes.

Microscopy

Images for quantification of CS complex localization, costained with bmCENP-T and bmDsn1 and upon depletion of other kinetochore subunits, for bmCENP-OP localization upon depletion of the CS complex, and for CENP-T, CENP-HIKM and Spc24/25 localization upon depletion of other subunits were acquired in a Leica Thunder microscope using a 63× objective (oil immersion; numerical aperture, 1.4). The acquisition parameters are as follows: DAPI—390 nm: excitation filter, 375 to 405 nm; emission filter, 420 to 450 nm; GFP (green fluorescent protein) (H3S10p)—475 nm: excitation filter, 462 to 496 nm; emission filter, 506 to 532; red (CS proteins)—555 nm: excitation filter, 542 to 566 nm; emission filter, 578 to 610 nm. Signals were detected using a Leica k8 CMOS back-thinned camera (2048 by 2048 pixels; pixel size: 6.5 by 6.5 μm) with a sensor size (diagonal) of 18.8 mm. Quantification of fluorescence intensity was performed using Fiji software (55) on unprocessed TIFF images. Mitotic cells (H3S10ph positive) were quantified. For RNAi-depleted cells, H3S10p signals were then used as markers to manually select, using the freehand selection tool, the nuclear area. The mean fluorescence intensity of each nucleus was measured and corrected for background. For background correction, the average of mean intensities of three random circular regions of fixed size (30 by 30 pixels) placed outside nuclear areas was determined and subtracted from the IF signal of each nucleus. For statistical analysis, Student’s t test (unpaired, unequal variance) was used. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Quantification of defects in bmCENP-TDimerMut

To quantify the mitotic index, all the cells in a given field were classified into untransfected interphase cells (DAPI-stained nuclei, α-phosphoH3-S10P negative, α-FLAG negative), untransfected mitotic cells (α-phosphoH3-S10P positive, α-FLAG negative), transfected interphase cells (α-phosphoH3-S10P negative, α-FLAG positive), and transfected mitotic cells (α-phosphoH3-S10P positive, α-FLAG positive). The percentage of mitotic cells in the untransfected and transfected populations was then calculated and plotted using GraphPad Prism.

To estimate the frequency of defective mitosis, untransfected mitotic cells (α-phosphoH3-S10P positive) or transfected mitotic cells (α-phosphoH3-S10P and α-FLAG positive) from the above experiment were classified into three categories: with no apparent mitotic defects, with alignment defects, or having congression defects. The percentage of these defects was calculated and plotted using GraphPad Prism.

Data from two independent experiments were used for these analyses. More than 650 cells were counted in each experiment to calculate the mitotic index, and more than 100 mitotic cells were counted for each transfection to classify the mitotic defects.

Cross-linked ChIP using the in-house protocol

Cross-linked ChIP was performed as previously described (63) with some modifications. Two confluent T75 flasks (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 156499) of BmN4 cells were used for each ChIP. Cells were cross-linked in freshly prepared 1% MeOH-free formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 28906) for 10 min at room temperature. Cross-linking was quenched by adding glycine to 125 mM for 2 min at room temperature. Cells were then washed in ice-cold PBS and incubated for 10 min with 150 μl of ice-cold lysis buffer (1% SDS, 10 mM EDTA, 50 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.1, and 1× cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche, cat. no. 11697498001). To the cell lysates, 1350 μl of ChIP buffer (1% Triton X-100, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.1, and 1× Protease inhibitor cocktail) was added along with 4.5 μl of 1 M CaCl2 (3 mM final) and then prewarmed for 2 min at 37°C. Nuclei were then treated with 1 or 2 units of MNase (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. N3755-500UN) for 15 min at 37°C. The MNase reaction was stopped by adding a mix of 30 μl of EDTA (0.5 M stock) and 60 μl of EGTA (0.5 M stock). Each MNase-treated nucleus sample was then sonicated using a Covaris E220 sonicator under the following parameters: 150 s; duty, 10%; power, 75 W; cycles/burst, 200; 7°C. Sonicated chromatin was centrifuged for 3 min at 16,000g, and a clear supernatant containing the solubilized chromatin was saved either as input or for ChIP. Anti–CENP-T serum (rabbit polyclonal) and anti–CS1–4 serum (mouse polyclonal) were incubated with protein A dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 10001D) for 10 min at room temperature to allow for bead-antibody binding. Antibody-bound beads were washed with ChIP buffer and mixed with input chromatin. All samples were incubated overnight at 4°C. Chromatin-bound beads were collected the next day on a magnetic rack and washed with the following ice-cold buffers: once with low-salt TSE 1 (0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, and 20 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.1); four times with high-salt TSE II (0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 500 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, and 20 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.1); and three times with 1× tris-EDTA. DNA was directly extracted from chromatin beads or input by adding DNA extraction buffer (20 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.1, 10 mM EDTA, 5 mM EGTA, 300 mM NaCl, and 1% SDS) and incubating at 37°C followed by reversing cross-links by addition of proteinase K (Qiagen, cat. no. 19131) and incubation overnight at 65°C. DNA was extracted with phenol:chloroform and precipitated with NaOAc and 100% EtOH in the presence of 1 μl of Glycoblue (15 mg/ml; Ambion, cat. no. AM9515). DNA was lastly resuspended in 1× tris-EDTA containing ribonuclease A (1 μg/μl) (Qiagen, cat. no. 19101) and incubated for 15 min at 37°C. Nucleosome profiles for input and ChIP DNA were analyzed using an Agilent 4200 Tapestation with a DNA high-sensitivity kit.

Next-generation sequencing and ChIP-seq data analysis

All steps of Illumina library preparation and sequencing were carried out at the Curie Institute’s sequencing platform. Adapter-trimmed, single-end Illumina reads of 100 bp in length were mapped using Bowtie2 (64) with default parameters to the B. mori genome assembly downloaded from Silkbase (http://silkbase.ab.a.u-tokyo.ac.jp), which was modified to extract only assembled chromosomes 1 to 28. After the removal of duplicates using Picard tools (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/), Deeptools bamCompare function (65) was used to generate ChIP-seq signal tracks represented as histograms of the average log2 ratio of RPKM (reads per kilobase per million mapped reads)–normalized read counts in IP over input in genome-wide 1-kb windows that were visualized in IGV (66). Deeptools multiBigWigSummary function was used to compute the average log2 ratio of RPKM-normalized read count in IP over input in genome-wide 10-kb windows for making scatterplots [H. Wickham (2016), https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org] of the correlation between different ChIP-seq targets. Pearson correlation was calculated in RStudio [RStudio Team (2016), RStudio: Integrated Development for R. RStudio, Inc., Boston, MA, www.rstudio.com/] after filtering out those 10-kb windows with zero mapped reads in both IP and input.

Homology predictions, alignments, and phylogenetic analyses

To test for the homology of the identified CS proteins to components of the Dam1 complex or to any other protein, HHpred version 3.2.0 searches (67) were performed against PDB_mmCIF70_3_Aug. The protein sequences of Dam1 complex members published by van Rooijen et al. (43) were used for phylogenetic analyses, including the identified putative homologs. The sequences of each orthologous group were aligned using MAFFT E-INS-I (MAFFT version 7) (68). For inferring the phylogenetic tree, the multiple sequence alignments of the subunits of Dam1/DASH complex were aligned using MAFFT merge E-INS-i. IQ-TREE (69) was then used to select a substitution model as advised by ModelFinder (70) and to infer the phylogeny using 1000 ultrafast bootstraps. The tree was visualized using iTOL (71). In the case of CS-3, an initial PSI BLAST search with default settings picked up other CS-3 homologs within Lepidoptera. However, the search also picked up Dad2, a Dam1/DASH subunit from an ascomycetous yeast (KAK6332828.1; E value = 4 × 10−4). Reciprocal searches with this sequence picked up lepidopteran CS-3 homologs as well. However, HHpred analysis suggests orthology with Duo1, a paralog of Dad2. In the phylogenetic analysis mentioned above, we observed CS-3 sequences to branch independently within the Duo1 cluster, suggesting marked sequence divergence from other Duo1 sequences.
