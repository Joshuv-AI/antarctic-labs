Aristotle’s intuitionist approach to morality guides a new direction in moral psychology
Authors: Paige Amormino, Kyle Fiore Law, Yoel Inbar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterdisciplinarity is widely endorsed yet difficult to enact, particularly when concepts travel between the humanities and empirical sciences. Moral psychology, positioned between moral philosophy and psychological science, offers a revealing case study of both the benefits and challenges of work that straddles these traditions. Past research in moral psychology has been organized largely around utilitarian and deontological templates from moral philosophy, which have shaped influential laboratory paradigms in the early 2000s. Yet more recent findings suggest that everyday moral evaluations more often hinge on character inference, relationships, and contextual meaning than on rule application or outcome calculation. These developments point to virtue ethics as a more compatible framework for describing how people judge moral situations. Accordingly, an Aristotelian approach is introduced as a concrete example of the “nuts-and-bolts” of humanities–science integration. The paper argues that Aristotle’s intuitionist virtue ethics—centered on practical wisdom, perception of particulars, and habituated character—can be translated into empirically tractable hypotheses that illuminate everyday moral judgment. A translational pathway is sketched for integrating concepts from virtue ethics into computational moral psychology by representing virtues as latent dispositions that guide attention, interpretation, and action across situations. This illustrates how philosophical constructs can be specified and formalized in models that remain sensitive to real-world context. Effective interdisciplinarity requires translation steps, shared vocabularies, and humility about disciplinary limits. Treated in this way, virtue ethics can inform moral psychology and help bridge science and the humanities to better understand real-world moral judgment.Subject terms: Philosophy; Psychology; Psychology; Science, technology and society

Introduction

Aristotle was famously wrong about many aspects of the natural world. For instance, he believed heavier objects fell faster and that life could arise from non-living matter (Barnes, 2000; DiSessa, 1982). For psychologists, these intuitive errors go beyond historical curiosities and illustrate how even brilliant thinkers rely on plausible “naïve theories” (Gelman and Noles, 2011) when data are incomplete and formal measurement tools are unavailable. Aristotle’s approach may have misfired in physics and biology, but it remains informative for psychologists investigating how humans form impressions, judgments, and decisions in everyday life.

This is especially true in the moral domain. Aristotle’s virtue ethics treats moral judgment as an exercised skill rather than the application of explicit rules or calculations. Central to this view is phronēsis (practical wisdom): an experienced, context-sensitive capacity to notice what matters in a situation and to choose actions that express virtue appropriately and for the right reasons (Aristotle, trans. 2000). Virtues (e.g., courage, generosity, honesty) shape what people are motivated to do; for the phronimos (the practically wise person), phronēsis guides how those motivations are expressed “in the right way, to the right degree, at the right time” (Aristotle, trans. 2000).

This intuitionist approach to morality contrasts with the theories that shaped post-Aristotelian moral philosophy, ranging from Bentham’s utilitarianism to Kant’s deontology (Allison, 1990; Bentham, 1879; Kant, 1964). These later philosophies informed moral psychology’s revitalization in the early 2000s, but as moral psychology continues to develop as a field, it has started to reexamine Aristotle’s overlooked insights with renewed appreciation. Here, we argue that Aristotle’s approach to morality can serve as a roadmap toward more fully integrating the role of intuition, character, values, and context in moral judgment and decision-making.

More broadly, this reframing illustrates a productive bridge between “non-science” and science. We intend this bridge in a specific, methodological sense: not a claim to contribute new philosophical scholarship, but a translational integration in which the psychological commitments embedded in a normative theory are made empirically tractable. Recent philosophical work argues that this kind of empirical engagement with normative concepts is itself part of philosophical inquiry, not merely preparatory to it (Lindauer, 2025). We wish to be explicit about the methodological premise underlying this bridge. Following Hume’s is-ought distinction (Hume, 1751), we do not claim that empirical evidence settles normative questions about how people should reason morally. Our claim is a narrower one: that certain philosophical frameworks, particularly those that make claims about psychological processes such as attention, habituation, and character inference, can be interpreted as generating testable predictions about observable behavior. Aristotle’s virtue ethics is unusual among normative theories precisely because it is also a rich account of psychological development, perception, and motivation. This dual status is the core of the paper’s argument. Treating Aristotelian ethics as “psychologically plausible” does not mean treating it as descriptively correct in all respects; it means taking seriously that its account of how moral judgment works maps onto empirical findings in ways that generate testable hypotheses.

Moral psychology’s disconnect

At the start of the 21st century, moral psychology experienced a revitalization when Joshua Greene and colleagues first used fMRI to explore sacrificial dilemmas, including the now-famous trolley problem (Greene et al., 2001). Their findings supported a dual-process model. Emotion-based impulses often led to deontological (rule-based) judgments and decisions, whereas deliberative cognition tended to support utilitarian (consequentialist) ones (Greene, 2007; Greene et al., 2001, 2009). Importantly, Greene’s framework carried an implicit normative dimension. Deliberative, utilitarian reasoning was framed as more reliable and less susceptible to systematic bias than its emotional, deontological counterpart (Greene, 2007). However, this framework has faced significant empirical scrutiny. Empirical psychologists pushed back on the theoretical interpretation of these findings, arguing that an objective psychological science of human morality should limit itself to describing human nature rather than prescribing which mode of moral reasoning is superior (Uhlmann et al., 2013).

More recently, deeper questions have emerged about whether the dual-process model accurately characterizes human morality in the first place. Dual-process theories such as Greene’s do not typically claim that people consciously apply the categorical imperative or explicitly perform utilitarian calculations. Rather, they claim that emotional and deliberative mechanisms produce patterns of moral judgment that happen to align with deontological and consequentialist predictions, respectively. The empirical question is therefore not whether people reason in terms of explicit philosophical principles (they clearly don’t; Awad et al., 2020; Nichols and Mallon, 2006), but whether the emotional-versus-deliberative distinction is the right mechanistic description of everyday moral cognition. Recent work suggests this framing mis-predicts important aspects of moral judgment. If the dual-process framework’s implicit normative logic holds—that deliberative, consequentialist reasoning represents the morally superior mode—one might expect moral exemplars to show more consistently utilitarian patterns in their self-reported moral beliefs. Yet even extraordinary altruists, such as people who donated a kidney to a stranger, show no such tendency (Amormino et al., 2022). Rather than reasoning more deliberatively, such individuals may instead be more emotionally responsive to others’ suffering—a pattern that, within Greene’s framework, would align with deontological rather than utilitarian tendencies. This suggests that the dual-process framework does not cleanly track the most prosocial behavior in everyday life. It is worth noting, however, that more recent “second-wave” dual-process theories have substantially refined the framework by incorporating richer accounts of affective processing and have begun to integrate insights from virtue theory (Railton, 2009, 2011, 2014; Zarpentine, 2025). Our argument is therefore not that dual-process accounts should be abandoned. Rather, we argue that a character-based Aristotelian framework can complement and extend them by foregrounding what they leave relatively underspecified: the role of character inference and trait attribution in everyday moral judgment.

In fact, the limitations of a purely affect-versus-deliberation framing are particularly evident in the phenomenon of moral dumbfounding (i.e., when people judge an action as wrong yet struggle to justify why; Haidt, 2001). Haidt’s (2001) influential social intuitionist model took dumbfounding as evidence that moral judgments are often driven by affect rather than deliberation. We do not dispute this, but we argue that an Aristotelian character-based framework explains what is driving the initial intuition that an action is wrong even when harm-based justifications are unavailable. Recent evidence suggests that such judgments stem from concerns about an agent’s character rather than the consequences of their actions (Royzman et al., 2015; Stanley et al., 2019; Uhlmann and Zhu, 2014). That is, people use behavior, and even seemingly harmless actions, as a window into underlying character. They perceive that a person’s actions, though not overtly harmful, reveal a dearth of moral virtue, and it is this perception of deficient character that drives the dumbfounded intuition. A dual-process account can explain why the judgment is affect-driven; an Aristotelian account explains what the affect is tracking.

The approach of examining whether people’s judgments align more closely with utilitarian or deontological ethics (Bartels, 2008; Cushman, 2013; Greene, 2007; Greene et al., 2001; Heir, 2023) has deep historical roots in philosophy and has generated a rich body of work, including widely cited findings about how people respond to sacrificial dilemmas (Cushman and Young, 2009; Greene et al., 2001). Yet everyday appraisals rarely fit these frameworks, instead focusing on what actions reveal about a person’s character (Everett et al., 2018; Hofmann et al., 2014; McManus et al., 2020). Every day, moral inquiry often asks, “What does this behavior say about the kind of person who did it?”, emphasizing patterns of past behavior, relationships, and intentions. These factors are less prominent in rule-based or outcome-based ethics (Fowler et al., 2021; Levine et al., 2024), but align well with Aristotle’s virtue ethics, which privileges habituation and practical wisdom (phronēsis) over universal moral rules, proposing that stable virtues like courage or compassion emerge from repeated practice and nuanced, context-sensitive judgment (Hursthouse and Pettigrove, 2022). For an Aristotelian virtue ethicist, moral judgment is largely a matter of asking, “What would the phronimos do in this situation?”. Empirical work increasingly supports this view, showing that moral intuitions hinge on identifying “good person” traits (e.g., honesty, generosity) rather than strictly following rules or maximizing outcomes (Uhlmann et al., 2015). Thus, we argue that moral psychology is undergoing a shift, integrating an Aristotelian perspective to better address the gaps in understanding moral cognition left by the dual-process model.

Aristotle’s moral psychology: virtues, perception of particulars, and practical wisdom

A key reason Aristotle’s framework maps well onto moral psychology is that it is built around the premise that moral judgments are formed through lived experience, not around a single explicit principle. In Aristotle’s view, moral evaluation depends on the perception of particulars—the concrete features of a case that make an action admirable, blameworthy, generous, cruel, loyal, or dishonest. What counts as “the right thing to do” is not reducible to a universal rule detached from context; rather, it depends on features such as relationships, roles, intentions, and the meaning of an act in its setting. This emphasis aligns with evidence that third-party judgments often rest on what an action reveals about an agent’s character (Royzman et al., 2015; Stanley et al., 2019; Uhlmann and Zhu, 2014) and with the broader person-centered approach to moral judgment (Uhlmann et al., 2015).

Aristotle also provides a developmental account of why moral intuitions feel immediate. Virtues are not merely endorsed beliefs; they are habits built through repeated practice, which gradually shape attention, affective reactions, and default interpretations (Foot, 2002). Over time, observers come to experience a “trained” moral sensibility: some actions simply look dishonest or feel cruel because the perceiver has learned which cues typically signal vice. On this view, moral dumbfounding is not merely a failure of reasoning; it can reflect a mismatch between (a) stable, experience-shaped intuitions about character and (b) the limited availability of post-hoc verbal justification (Haidt, 2001). This helps explain why everyday judgments often diverge from the utilitarian/deontological lens that dominates laboratory dilemmas (Awad et al., 2020; Nichols and Mallon, 2006).

A serious objection to virtue-ethical frameworks in psychology concerns situationism: the empirical finding that behavior is less cross-situationally consistent than robust trait attributions would predict (J. Doris, 2002; Merritt et al., 2010). If people’s behavior is strongly shaped by situational factors—and classic social psychology experiments suggest it often is—then positing stable virtuous dispositions as explanatory constructs may be empirically unwarranted. This is a genuine challenge that deserves direct engagement. We note, however, that situationist findings bear on two separable questions: (a) whether people possess robust cross-situational character traits, and (b) whether character inference plays a central role in moral judgment. Our Aristotelian proposal depends primarily on (b), which has strong empirical support (Uhlmann et al., 2015), and is less committed to a strong version of (a). Miller’s (2013, 2014) work on “mixed traits” offers a productive middle path: people may possess genuine character traits that shape behavior in probabilistic rather than deterministic ways, and these mixed traits are still morally and psychologically significant. Moreover, in the computational framework we sketch below, virtues are modeled as probabilistic priors rather than rigid behavioral rules, which is precisely the kind of disposition that can coexist with situational variability while still influencing the expected distribution of behavior across contexts.

Finally, Aristotle’s framework suggests an empirical shift in what we treat as the “targets” of moral cognition. Rather than modeling judgment as a choice between rule adherence and outcome maximization, an Aristotelian approach places character inference and trait attribution at the center: people evaluate actions as evidence about who someone is and how they are likely to behave across situations. This approach suggests that real-world moral behavior and judgment may emerge from prosocial dispositions and relational cognition rather than abstract philosophical consistency.

Toward a computational character-based moral psychology

Recently, computational moral psychology has offered new ways to model how people make moral judgments (Cushman, 2013; Mikhail, 2007). These methods often use formal models drawn from cognitive science or Bayesian inference to represent the relationships among beliefs, desires, and dispositions in moral decision-making. While earlier computational approaches emphasized deontological “rules” or utilitarian “utilities” as explicit parameters, more recent work has begun to represent the stable dispositions and value-based learning processes that underlie moral judgment (Crockett, 2013; Cushman, 2013), suggesting an opportunity to integrate virtue-ethical constructs into formal models of moral reasoning.

An Aristotelian contribution here is not to treat virtue as a slogan or a self-report label, but as a structured set of expectations about perception, motivation, and action. In computational terms, virtues can be represented as latent dispositions that shape (a) which features of a situation receive attention, (b) how ambiguous evidence is interpreted, and (c) what actions are treated as reasonable defaults. To make these claims concrete: (a) a justice-sensitive agent would differentially weight relational context (noticing, for instance, whether a resource allocation treats similar cases similarly), while an agent with weaker justice-sensitivity would not prioritize this feature; (b) when someone receives an unexpected gift from a relative stranger, a compassion-prior increases the probability of interpreting the act as genuinely generous rather than self-interested; and (c) an agent whose honesty disposition is strong treats truth-telling as the default in ambiguous communicative situations where deception might also be available. This makes virtue ethics unusually compatible with computational frameworks that already rely on latent states, inference, and learning, including Bayesian models of belief updating (Kleiman-Weiner et al., 2017) and reinforcement learning models of habit formation (Crockett, 2013).

These dispositions can be updated over time. Reinforcement learning or Bayesian updating can simulate how repeated decisions reinforce or modify them, directly mirroring the habituation process Aristotle describes. This connection between habituation and reinforcement learning is not merely analogical. Reinforcement learning models that update action policies based on reward signals, and Bayesian models that update priors based on observed evidence, are the natural computational homes for virtue-as-disposition: both describe how stable response tendencies emerge from experience and generalize across novel contexts. By integrating contextual features (social relationships, cultural norms, prior acts of kindness or harm), these models can approximate practical wisdom (phronēsis)—selecting actions that express virtues appropriately across situations rather than following a single rigid principle.

Importantly, phronēsis can be treated as a computationally expressible competence; it is the capacity to map situational features onto action policies that are sensitive to context. Rather than encoding a single global rule (“maximize utility” or “never violate rights”), a phronēsis-like model would learn when different virtues are relevant, how to trade them off, and how to calibrate intensity to the circumstances (e.g., generosity toward a stranger versus loyalty to a friend). This reframes “context sensitivity” from a philosophical slogan into a testable modeling target. Specifically, when do models that infer character and context better predict human moral judgments—how people actually evaluate agents and actions—than models that rely on rules or outcomes alone?

Thus, computational methods can complement virtue ethics by providing formal, testable accounts of how traits, emotions, and contexts jointly produce moral judgments. This merging of empirical data, emerging methods, and virtue-ethical insights promises a richer, more nuanced understanding of moral cognition.

Conclusion

Moral psychology’s early reliance on deontological and utilitarian theories helped uncover crucial insights about how emotional and deliberative processes shape moral judgment. Yet, recent evidence suggests that deontology and utilitarianism are rarely the driving forces behind everyday moral cognition. Instead, people rely on intuitions tied to character and virtue, akin to Aristotle’s vision of morality as habitual, context-sensitive, and deeply personal. Although Aristotle’s intuitive methods failed him in the natural sciences, his virtue ethics remains a compelling, empirically supported model of how individuals think, feel, and act in real moral situations. Moreover, computational approaches that capture stable dispositions and contextual sensitivity provide a promising avenue for further integrating character-based perspectives into testable, formal models of moral cognition.

Re-engaging, Aristotle, therefore, does not require treating philosophy as a substitute for science. Rather, it treats philosophy as a historically rich source of hypotheses about the structure of moral cognition—hypotheses that moral psychology and computational modeling are increasingly equipped to evaluate. In this spirit, it is worth recalling the warning of Bernard Williams (1929–2003), who argued that modern philosophy had “over-theorized” ethics by searching for decision procedures where ethical life offers none. Williams wrote that “moral philosophy cannot deliver … a theory to guide ethical reasoning. What it can do is to assist the self-understanding of those whose ethical reasoning already has guidance from elsewhere” (Williams, 1985, p. 217). Several concrete steps could further develop these connections between the human and natural sciences. Computationally, the framework invites direct model comparison—testing when models that infer character and context predict human moral judgments better than models built on rules or outcomes alone—and the translation of further virtue-ethical constructs, such as practical wisdom modeled as context-sensitive policy selection, into parameters that can be estimated from behavior. More broadly, such a transfer will require shared vocabularies for identifying which features of a philosophical theory are psychologically committal, explicit translation steps that preserve those commitments without importing normative claims, and humility about the limits of what such a transfer can establish. By using virtue ethics not to dictate but to inform moral psychology, we can strengthen the bridge between humanistic and scientific disciplines and better understand the beautiful mess of people’s moral lives.
