Strong nonlocality with more imaginarity and less entanglement
Subrata Bera, Indranil Biswas, Atanu Bhunia, Indrani Chattopadhyay, Debasis Sarkar

Abstract
Complex numbers are central to the formulation of quantum mechanics, yet their role as a genuine resource is only beginning to be understood. In this work, we demonstrate that quantum states with intrinsically complex amplitudes provide a fundamental advantage in state discrimination. We construct a set of five orthogonal three qubit pure states and show that the set is strongly nonlocal if and only if it includes imaginary components. Such a set becomes locally indistinguishable not only under local measurements but also against bipartite joint measurements. This enhanced robustness makes imaginarity a valuable resource for quantum cryptography since information encoded in these states remains secure against collaborative group attacks. Our results highlight a new operational role of complex numbers in quantum theory and establish imaginarity as a key enabler of cryptographic security. However, we reconstruct the set by replacing the only product state with a biseparable state whose shared entanglement between two parties nullifies the effect of imaginarity in exhibiting strong nonlocality. In fact, we show how entangling correlations between two distant parties can dilute the effect of imaginarity, and conversely, how imaginarity itself can mimic the role of entanglement. Nevertheless, the set spans a locally indistinguishable subspace, while its complement, in turn, produces distillable genuine entanglement. Notably, this is the smallest possible Unextendible Biseparable Basis (UBB) that resolves the open problem regarding the existence of a UBB of cardinality d 2 + d − 1 d^{2}+d-1 in d ⊗ 3 d^{\otimes 3} . Our construction yields a highly powerful set, rich in resources from multiple perspectives of quantum information theory, including many-copy discrimination, unambiguous identification, entanglement creation from product state, and non-entangling perturbations.

In perfect discrimination tasks, the states under consideration must be mutually orthogonal, since even a global measurement cannot perfectly distinguish nonorthogonal states. Consequently, the measurement operators must be chosen such that the post measurement states also remain mutually orthogonal. When such measurements act locally, especially under LOCC protocols, they are referred to as orthogonality preserving local measurements (OPLMs).

Beyond cryptographic implications, this result provides a theoretical and operational justification for regarding imaginarity as a fundamental quantum resource, placing it on par with entanglement and coherence in the hierarchy of quantum advantages. In particular, we show how entangling correlations between two distant parties can dilute the effect of imaginarity, and conversely, how imaginarity itself can mimic the role of entanglement. These insights suggest that the presence of complex phases should be understood as a nonclassical resource in its own right, with far-reaching consequences for the study of quantum nonlocality and secure information processing.

\sum_{k=1}^{5}Sch_{\perp}\{\rho_{k}\}\geqslant 4\cdot 2+1=9
, which violates the Lemma in
33
. Hence, the proposition follows.

In conclusion, we have shown that the presence of imaginary components in quantum states provides a distinct operational advantage in multipartite systems. In particular, this advantage manifests as a strengthening of nonlocality within sets of orthogonal pure states from the perspective of state discrimination. We construct a family of orthogonal three-qubit states, which exhibit strong nonlocality if and only if the set involves imaginary components. This establishes imaginarity as a genuine quantum resource with direct cryptographic applications, since information encoded in such states remains secure against a broader class of attacks than previously recognized.

Our results underscore the indispensable role of complex numbers in quantum theory and open new directions for exploring how imaginarity can be harnessed in quantum communication, nonlocality-based protocols, and resource-theoretic frameworks. However, we construct an unextendible biseparable basis by replacing the product states with a biseparable state whose shared entanglement between two parties nullifies the effect of imaginarity in exhibiting strong nonlocality. This yields the smallest possible set that resolves the open problem regarding the existence of a UBB of cardinality
d^{2}+d-1
in
d^{\otimes 3}

3
.

Our construction produces a highly powerful set, rich in resources from multiple perspectives of quantum information theory, and manifesting several fundamental forms of nonlocality. Firstly, any subset of four states splits the Hilbert space into two completely entangled subspaces, each of which remains robust under non-entangling perturbations. Such splits are particularly useful for generating global projective measurements capable of creating entanglement from any product states. Additionally, no ensemble of two full-rank mixed states supported on either half of the split can be unambiguously identified under LOCC. Moreover, both the span of the UBB and its complement remain stable under nontrivial perturbations. Finally, while the UBB spans a locally indistinguishable subspace, its complement, on the other hand, spans a genuinely entangled space which is distilable across every bipartition. This “all-in-one” construction, therefore, offers a versatile and valuable framework with significant potential for applications in quantum information research.

Lemma:

In a discrimination task of orthogonal bipartite pure states, a party with local dimension

IV. Proof of Lemma 1

Lemma:

Let
S=\{\ket{\psi\down{1}{i}}\}_{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle i=0$}}^{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle n-1$}}
be a set of
n
bipartite pure states, with
\{P_{m}\}
denoting the collection of all corresponding product forming matrices. The set spans a CES if and only if the system of quadratic equations
X^{t}P_{m}X=0
(for all
m
) admits only the trivial solution
X=0
, where
X\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Proof:
An arbitrary superposition of
n
pure states can be expressed as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\ket{\chi}_{\hskip-1.5pt\scriptscriptstyle AB}&=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}\ket{\psi\down{1}{i}}_{\hskip-1.5pt\scriptscriptstyle AB},\quad x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}\in\mathbb{C}\\
&=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}(\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle k}^{d_{\scriptscriptstyle A}{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}\;\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle l=0}^{d_{\scriptscriptstyle B}{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}a_{\scriptscriptstyle kl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\ket{kl}_{\hskip-1.5pt\scriptscriptstyle AB})\quad\text{[by (\ref{general_state})]}\\
&=\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle k=0}^{d_{\scriptscriptstyle A}{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}\ket{k}_{\hskip-1.5pt\scriptscriptstyle A}\otimes(\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\;\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle l=0}^{d_{\scriptscriptstyle B}{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle l}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\ket{kl}_{\hskip-1.5pt\scriptscriptstyle B})\\
&=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle d_{\scriptscriptstyle A}-1}\ket{k}_{\hskip-1.5pt\scriptscriptstyle A}\ket{\phi\down{1}{k}}_{\hskip-1.5pt\scriptscriptstyle B}\end{array}

where
\ket{\phi\down{1}{k}}=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle il}x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle kl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\ket{l}\in\mathcal{H}^{\scriptscriptstyle B}
for
k\in[d_{\scriptscriptstyle A}]
.

The state
\ket{\chi}
is a product state if and only if any pair of the collection of vectors
\{\ket{\phi\down{1}{k}}\}_{k=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle d_{\scriptscriptstyle A}-1}
are linearly dependent, i.e.,
\ket{\phi\down{1}{k}}
and
\ket{\phi\down{1}{p}}
are linearly dependent for each
k,p(>k)\in[d_{\scriptscriptstyle A}]
. This is equivalent to collinearity between two vectors
(\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle i}x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle kl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})\ket{l}+(\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle i}x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle kr}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})\ket{ r}
and
(\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle i}x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle pl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})\ket{ l}+(\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle i}x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle pr}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})\ket{r}
for each
l,r(>l)\in[d_{\scriptscriptstyle B}]
. Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\begin{vmatrix}\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle kl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}&\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle kr}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\\[10.0pt]
\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle pl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}&\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle pr}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\end{vmatrix}=0\\
\Rightarrow&(\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle kl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})(\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle pr}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})-(\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle kr}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})(\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle pl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})=0\\
\Rightarrow&(\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle kl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})(\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle j=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,x_{\scriptscriptstyle j}a_{\scriptscriptstyle pr}^{\scriptscriptstyle(j)})-(\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}a_{\scriptscriptstyle kr}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})(\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle j=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,x_{\scriptscriptstyle j}a_{\scriptscriptstyle pl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(j)})=0\\
\Rightarrow&\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle j=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}x_{\scriptscriptstyle j}a_{\scriptscriptstyle kl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}a_{\scriptscriptstyle pr}^{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}-\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle j=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}x_{\scriptscriptstyle j}a_{\scriptscriptstyle kr}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}a_{\scriptscriptstyle pl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}=0\\
\Rightarrow&\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle j=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}x_{\scriptscriptstyle j}(a_{\scriptscriptstyle kl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}a_{\scriptscriptstyle pr}^{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}-a_{\scriptscriptstyle kr}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}a_{\scriptscriptstyle pl}^{\scriptscriptstyle(j)})=0\\
\Rightarrow&\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle i=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}\,\sum\limits_{\scriptscriptstyle j=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle n-1}x_{\scriptscriptstyle i}x_{\scriptscriptstyle j}(\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle kp|lr}^{\scriptscriptstyle A|B})_{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle i,j$}}=0,\quad\text{[by (\ref{product_forming_matrices})]}\\
\Rightarrow&X^{t}\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle kp|lr}^{\scriptscriptstyle A|B}\,X=0\\
\Rightarrow&X^{t}P_{m}X=0\end{array}

(26)

where
X=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})^{t}
is the column vector with
x_{i}
denoting its
i
-th entry, and
\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle kp|lr}^{\scriptscriptstyle AB}=P_{m}
with
m
running over all choices of the indices
k,p,l,r
.

Every nonzero solution of eqs. (
26
) corresponds to a product state within the span of
S
. In other words, the system of homogeneous equations
X^{t}P_{m}X=0
(for all
m
) admits no nonzero solution if and only if the span of
S
consists entirely of entangled states.
