The Epidemiology of Catastrophic Head and Neck Injuries in High School and College Non-Football Sports: A 4-Decade Review
Authors: Courtney M Meyer, Kristen L Kucera, Samantha E Scarneo-Miller, Zachary K Winkelmann, Susan W Yeargin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Catastrophic head and neck injuries (CHNIs) in sport are rare but can have disabling or fatal outcomes. Prior studies have focused on football, equipment-laden sports, or different injury severities, not CHNI in nonfootball sports.Purpose:To describe the epidemiology of CHNI across levels (high school vs collegiate) in nonfootball sports and evaluate differences in injury rates (IRs) by sport, sex, and decade.Study Design:Descriptive epidemiological study.Methods:This study utilized 40 academic years (1982-1983 to 2021-2022) of surveillance data from the National Center for Catastrophic Sports Injury Research. Descriptive statistics summarized athlete, event, and injury characteristics. IRs were calculated from available institutional participation data for high school and collegiate levels. Athlete-exposure was defined as 1 athlete participating in 1 sport season. IRs per 100,000 athlete seasons (AS), IR ratios (IRRs), and 95% CIs were computed using negative binomial regression.Results:A total of 470 eligible CHNI events were included, capturing 17 unique sports. Most injuries transpired in those who were male (69.4%; n = 326), ranged in age from 12 to 25, and competed at the high school level (76.2%; n = 358). Of injuries, 12.6% were fatal (n = 59), 38.7% were permanently disabling (n = 182), and 44.9% were temporarily disabling (n = 211). Of all male patients (n = 326), traumatic brain injury (33.4%; n = 109) and spinal fractures (34.1%; n = 111/326) were reported most frequently, while traumatic injuries (n = 144) including brain (34.0%; n = 49) and other traumatic injuries (ie, nerve injury, facial fractures, etc [30.6%; n = 44]) occurred most frequently in female patients. CHNI occurred at a lower rate in female than male patients (IR, 0.12 vs 0.48 per 100,000 AS; IRR, 0.25; 95% CI, 0.18-0.33; P < .001) and in collegiate than high school athletes (IR, 0.11 vs 0.53 per 100,000 AS; IRR, 0.20; 95% CI, 0.15-0.27; P < .001). Sports with the highest CHNI rates included gymnastics (IR, 1.66) and ice hockey (IR, 1.52). Rates declined each decade, with the largest difference in rate occurring between 1982 and 1992 (IR, 0.59; IRR, 8.93; 95% CI, 2.23-35.68) and between 2012 and 2022 (IR, 0.06).Conclusion:CHNI rates in nonfootball sports declined over 4 decades and were higher among athletes who were male, competing at the high school level, or participating in gymnastics or ice hockey. Over half of the athletes sustaining CHNI experienced outcomes of permanent disability or fatality.Keywords: facial injuries, cervical spine, general sports trauma, medical aspects of sports

It is estimated that 5.9 million sport-related head, neck, and face injuries were treated in US emergency departments from 2010 to 2022. 32 These injuries occur through direct contact mechanisms, including collision with another person, surface, or object, resulting in hyperflexion, axial loading, hyperextension, rotation, and torsion.14,26 In college and high school athletes, injuries to the head or face required emergency transport the most frequently, followed by neck injuries. 18 The emergent care and attention given to these injuries is not surprising, as injuries to the head and neck can result in potentially catastrophic and fatal outcomes.

The National Center for Catastrophic Sports Injury Research (NCCSIR) defines catastrophic injuries as “fatalities, injuries that result in permanent functional disability, and serious injuries that result in temporary functional disability with full recovery.” 44 The NCCSIR surveys and classifies injuries of this nature. In their most recent all-sports injury report, head and spinal cord injuries accounted for 37% of all catastrophic injuries. 26 Examples of catastrophic head and neck injuries (CHNIs) include traumatic/structural brain injuries, 3 skull fractures, 44 mandibular fractures, 49 neck lacerations,13,49 spinal cord injuries,1,4,14 nerve root involvement,1,4,14 and vertebral fractures/dislocations.1,4,14 These injuries are monitored and reported annually; however, a retrospective analysis of aggregate data allows for the reporting of differences in injury rates (IRs) and the observation of trends over time. Previous studies on CHNI have focused on male patients, were specific to football13,31 and contact/collision sports,3,20 or included those that were not catastrophic, 16 with the most recent of these occurring in 2016, 20 2017, 31 and 2020. 16 Differences in IRs have been reported by sex 49 and across competition levels. 30 However, due to data source(s) or delimitations, not all studies have been able to stratify IRs by severity 49 or for noncontact sports. 20 As a result of the age and focus of previously published data on catastrophic head and neck injuries, there is a need for updated research. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to describe the epidemiology of CHNI at the high school and college levels for nonfootball sports. Secondarily, we aimed to examine differences in rates of CHNI by level, sex, sport, and decade.

Methods

This study was of retrospective epidemiologic design and conducted through secondary data analysis from an ongoing injury surveillance system, operating at a national level since 1982.28,41,56 Sport-related catastrophic injuries occurring in athletes are captured through publicly available sources (online media, news articles, etc); or reported directly to the NCCSIR by affiliates (athletes, parents, athletic trainers, staff, physicians, etc) via the addition of the online reporting portal in 2015 (https://www.sportinjuryreport.org). All events captured and reported are monitored and verified by NCCSIR and its consortium partners (https://nccsir.unc.edu/about-2/consortia-and-partners/).28,41,56 The NCCSIR categorizes eligible catastrophic injury events as associated with active participation in training/conditioning, practice, or competition for an enrolled sport to meet ≥1 criterion from 4 categories (the eligibility matrix can be found at https://nccsir.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5614/2023/09/NCCSIR-Case-Definitions-8.26.23.pdf). Based on NCCSIR’s eligibility matrix, concussions do not qualify for inclusion alone; however, they may be captured if sustained in correspondence with a reported catastrophic injury (eg, an athlete sustains a cervical spinal cord injury and concussion simultaneously). The inclusion criteria of this study were events of CHNI, including their encompassed structures (ie, eyes, brain, cervical vertebrae, spinal cord), resulting from participation in a sponsored high school or college sport. This study was limited to events occurring from 1982 to 1983 through the 2021 to 2022 academic years. The exclusion criteria included events involving athletes who experienced injuries from football, injuries from non–school sponsored sporting activity, or not as a result of sports participation (ie, motor vehicle collision or suicide). The University of South Carolina’s institutional review board reviewed and approved this study prior to data transfer and analysis.

The NCCSIR defines “athlete exposure” as 1 athlete participating in 1 sports season. 29 The NCCSIR uses annual participation statistics from the National Federation of State High School Associations (NFHS) and National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA).21,29 Missing data for annual sport participation counts were replaced when it was unlikely that the missing value was a true zero, based on participation rates observed before and after the missing instance (Appendix Table A1).

Although most NCAA institutions have a cheer team or spirit squad, the sport is not NCAA sponsored; therefore, participation statistics from Sports Marketing Analytics (SMA, 2011-2012 to 2020-2021) were used to analyze college and high school IRs. 2 Therefore, IRs for college and high school cheerleading were calculated separately and not included in the overall, level, sex, or sport calculations using NFHS and NCAA participation statistics.

The outcome of interest was the frequency of traumatic head and neck injuries. The factors of interest were competition level, decade, sport, and sex. The descriptive variables included all other athlete characteristics, event characteristics, institutional/location characteristics, and injury characteristics.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive frequencies and percentages were computed for the athlete’s age, event, and injury characteristics and stratified by sport level (high school, college). Select attributes of the athlete, event, and injury were described and stratified by sex and sport. IRs with 95% CIs were calculated per 100,000 athlete seasons (AS) by level, sex, and sport with frequencies of >10 events. IRs were analyzed in four 10-year academic periods (decades) to describe patterns over time. For cheerleading, IR was presented across the 10 years of accessible SMA participation data. Comparisons for cheerleading utilizing IR ratios (IRRs) were not included because of injury frequencies of <10 events when stratified.

Negative binomial regression was used to estimate IRRs and compute rate comparisons over each decade because of overdispersion (α < .05). Type 3 analysis of effects was employed to assess the direction and significance of rate changes. Because of the nonhomogeneity of the groups, the subvariables with the largest samples were used as referent levels for sex and level, and the smallest sample was used to compare decades. Statistical analysis was completed using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Results

NCCSIR captured 504 events; upon review and application of inclusion-exclusion criteria, 34 events (n = 15 non–school sponsored activity; n = 19 incorrect or missing body part), were excluded, resulting in a total of 470 events utilized for analysis.

Athlete Characteristics

Athletes were, on average, aged 17 ± 2 years at the time of injury (range, 12-25 years). Race was documented as unknown for most athletes (n = 418; 88.9%) in the data set. Male patients (n = 326) comprised 69.4% of injuries. The highest frequency of CHNI by decade occurred during the years 2002-2003 to 2011-2012 (34.5%; n = 162). This finding was consistent across level and sex and can be visualized in Figure 1. Event Characteristics

Over half of the events (58.9%; n = 277) occurred during competitions or games. A total of 17 unique sports were captured in this study, with the 3 most frequently reported being cheerleading (17.4%; n = 82), baseball (16.4%; n = 77), and wrestling (15.3%; n = 72). When stratified by sex, the most frequently reported men’s sports for CHNI were baseball (23.6%; n = 77/326), wrestling (21.8%; n = 71/326), and track and field (18.4%; n = 60/326). When stratified by level, the most frequently reported high school sports were wrestling (19.6%; n = 70/358) and baseball (17.0%; n = 61/358). Of college sports, cheerleading (25.0%; n = 28/112) and baseball (14.3%; n = 16/112) accounted for the highest frequency of injuries. Injury Characteristics

Among all studied events, the mechanism of injury was frequently recorded as unknown (35.5%; n = 167). When coded, the 2 most frequent mechanisms were contact with the ground/surface (24.3%; n = 114) and contact with an apparatus/object (21.7%; n = 102). Traumatic brain injury (33.6%; n = 158) and spinal fracture (30.6%; n = 144) were the highest recorded injury categories. The category of other traumatic injuries (n = 107) (Table 3) comprised skull fractures (63.6%; n = 68), other throat, facial, neck injuries/disorders (eg, nerve injury, vascular injury, larynx fracture) (15.9%; n = 17), orbital fractures (10.3%; n = 11), other facial fractures (eg, nonspecific, nasal, jaw) (4.7%; n = 5), other brain/head injuries (eg, second impact syndrome, brain aneurysm) (2.8%; n = 3), and eye injuries (eg, nonspecific, eye globe rupture) (2.8%; n = 3).

Stratified by Sex

When stratified by sex, the most common injury mechanism for male patients was contact with the ground/surface (29.4%; n = 96/326), while contact with another player (16.0%; n = 23/144) or apparatus/object (14.6%; n = 21/144) was most common for female patients. In male patients, traumatic brain injuries (33.4%; n = 109/326) and spinal fractures (34.1%; n = 111/326) were the most frequently reported injuries. In contrast, injuries with the highest frequency for female patients were traumatic brain injuries (34.0%; n = 49/144) and other traumatic injuries (ie, nerve injury, facial fractures, etc) (30.6%; n = 44/144). Of injury outcomes for male patients, over half were permanently disabling (40.5%; n = 132/326) or fatal (16.3%; n = 53/326), with the remainder temporarily disabling with recovery (40.2%; n = 131/326) or unknown/uncertain (3.1%; n = 10/326). For female patients, over half of injuries were temporarily disabling with recovery (55.6%; n = 80/144), and almost half were permanently disabling (34.7%; n = 50/144), fatal (4.2%; n = 6/144), or unknown/uncertain (5.6%; n = 8/144). Additional injury characteristics stratified by sex are presented in Table 3.

Stratified by Level

When stratified by level, the most frequently reported mechanisms of injury were contact with the ground/surface (26.3%; n = 94/358) for high school athletes or contact with another player (22.3%; n = 25/112) in college athletes. Spinal fractures were the most frequently reported injury in college athletes (33.9%; n = 38/112), and traumatic brain injuries (34.9%; n = 125/358) comprised the greatest proportion recorded in high school athletes. Temporary disability with recovery was the most frequently recorded injury outcome at both the high school (44.1%; n = 158/358) and the college level (47.3%; n = 53/112). A total of 45 fatalities occurred at the high school level and 14 at the college level. Additional injury characteristics stratified by level are presented in Table 3.

IRs and IRRs: Utilizing NFHS and NCAA Participation Data

Total IRs were lower in female patients (IR, 0.12; 95% CI, 0.09-0.15) compared with male patients (IR, 0.48 per 100,000 AS; 95% CI, 0.42-0.57) (IRR, 0.25; 95% CI, 0.18-0.33; P < .001). Total IRs were lower for college athletes (IR, 0.11; 95% CI, 0.09-0.13) compared with high school athletes (IR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.42-0.57) (IRR, 0.20; 95% CI, 0.15-0.27; P < .001). IRs decreased across each decade (Table 4), with 2012-2013 to 2021-2022 (IR, 0.06; 95% CI, 0.03-0.14) recording the lowest IR. The IR was significantly higher in each of the previous decades, compared with 2012-2013 to 2021-2022 (Table 4).

IRs: Utilizing SMA Participation Data

The overall IR for cheerleading ages 13 to 24 from 2011-2012 to 2020-2021 was 0.04 CHNIs per 100,000 AS (95% CI, 0.01-0.07). There were no reported CHNIs for male participants in high school or college cheerleading within the 2011-2012 to 2020-2021 decade. Frequencies of <5 CHNIs occurred for high school and college when stratified by level; therefore, no IRs or IRRs were presented.

Discussion

Across the 40-year study period, a total of 470 CHNIs occurred in high school and college nonfootball sports, for a mean annual frequency of almost 13 per academic year, ranging from 1 to 23. Traumatic brain injuries and spinal fractures accounted for ~65% of all CHNIs in the data set. Most injuries occurred to male athletes, with a mean age of 17 (range, 12-25 years), participating at the high school level, and competing during the 2002-2003 to 2011-2012 decade. Almost 60% of CHNIs resulted from competitions or games and almost half (46%) of the coded injury mechanisms occurred due to contact with the ground/surface (24%) or an apparatus/object (22%). We identified injuries across 17 unique sports, with cheerleading, baseball, and wrestling representing the 3 most frequently reported.

Athlete and Event Characteristics

Although female athletes represented under a third of the catastrophic injuries captured, they averaged 3.6 CHNIs/year. Cheerleading accounted for >50% of the reported sports for female athletes over the 40-year period, with the following 3 sports (soccer, gymnastics, and softball) each accounting for approximately 8% of the injuries. Cheerleading also represented a quarter of college-level injuries; however, <10 CHNIs occurred across both levels in the past decade, with none involving male patients during that time. The frequency of injuries in female and collegiate athletes attributed to cheerleading is not surprising, given previously reported frequencies of catastrophic 56 and overall injuries.12,42,55 Although prevention strategies (ie, elimination of the basket toss) have effectively reduced the rate of catastrophic injuries in cheerleading, 56 further exploration of strategies related to skill development and partner stunts (ie, tosses, pyramids, and other vertical team formations) may be warranted to address the large proportion of injuries attributed to contact with the ground.7,56 Of additional concern are the disparities in care (ie, medical access and coverage) for cheerleaders and variations in institutional policies stemming from differing sport sponsorship. 43 With the NCAA’s recent sponsorship of acrobatics and tumbling and the Committee on Women’s Athletics’ recommendation for a national collegiate stunt championship,45,46,52 team affiliates must evaluate resource and facility allocation, as well as preparation and updates to policies focused on the addition of these emerging sports. Although NCAA-sponsored teams offer athletes standard medical care, traditional high school and college cheerleaders may still face barriers to accessible and equitable care because of sponsorship differences.

Almost a quarter of CHNIs in male athletes occurred during baseball, which also had the second-highest frequency of injuries overall at both the college and the high school levels. A study of NCCSIR data focusing on baseball from 1981 to 2002 found a rate of 0.43 traumatic catastrophic injuries per 100,000 participants, with 33 cases of head and neck trauma documented. 8 Most injuries arose from player-to-player contact or contact from a ball/bat. 8 The present study included 77 CHNIs from baseball, indicating that the annual frequency has remained similar to that previously documented. This is unexpected, as rule changes to reduce ball exit speeds, such as the Batted Ball Coefficient of Restitution for metal bats, have been implemented at the high school (2011-2012 season) and college (2010-2011 season) levels. However, during the development of this publication, we did not discover studies examining the impact of this rule change on injury reduction. Researchers previously interviewed baseball affiliates to identify injury prevention solutions. 8 They suggested pitchers' helmets and screens, discontinuing head-first sliding, and improving training and communication to reduce collisions. Of these strategies, helmets with faceguards notably reduce facial injuries in adolescents. 35 High school and college baseball teams should continue implementing pitching/safety screens in practice and dugouts to mitigate the risk of injury from ball and bat strikes.

Injury Characteristics

Traumatic brain injuries and spinal fractures were the leading CHNI categories overall, among the male sex, and in high school athletes. Fatalities and permanent disabilities accounted for 51% of outcomes, highlighting that more athletes experienced life-ending or life-altering injuries than those who recovered. Of the 59 fatalities captured, 68% were due to traumatic brain injuries. It is imperative that prompt recognition and immediate management of these injuries occur, guided by clinical decision-making tools such as the Sport Concussion Assessment Tool 6 Immediate Assessment/Neuro Screen tool, 47 Glasgow Coma Scale, 53 and/or Canadian C-Spine Rules. 39 Once identified, emergency action plans must be implemented in accordance with policy and procedures and in coordination with local/on-site emergency management system teams.

When observing injuries to female athletes, the highest frequency categories were traumatic brain injuries and other traumatic injuries (ie, facial fractures, eye globe, neurovascular, and tracheal injuries). Almost 60% of CHNIs in female athletes had an outcome of temporary disability. Although injuries classified in the category of other traumatic head and face injuries occur infrequently, resulting in their inclusion in the group, they still have catastrophic and disabling outcomes. An analysis of National Electronic Injury Surveillance System (NEISS) data on craniofacial injuries in female athletes revealed that softball, basketball, and soccer were the most frequently reported sports, manifesting as contusions, abrasions, and lacerations. 23 Previous studies found higher IRs in female athletes for maxillofacial injuries in NCAA Division I athletes, 37 head, face, and eye injuries in lacrosse players, 33 and axial skeleton fractures in cheerleaders and gymnasts. 54 Conversely, some report more frequent eye, dental, and neck/cervical spine injuries in male athletes. 19 The rarity of these events may lead to a lack of detailed management plans in existing policy and procedure documents. Medical professionals providing care for female athletes and women’s sports should review policies to ensure proper planning for rare yet catastrophic injuries such as facial fractures, eye injuries, neurovascular injuries, and tracheal injuries.

IRs by Level

Total IRs were higher among high school athletes than among college athletes (0.5 vs 0.1 per 100,000 AS). Although participation is higher at the high school level, the statistically significant IRR of 0.2 between levels underscores the increased risk high school athletes face. Previous evidence suggests that poor officiating (delayed calls) 5 and problematic coaching practices lead to catastrophic injury, which may be amplified at the high school level, given athletes’ novice skills and sometimes improper techniques. 8 High schools have also been identified as lacking comprehensive policies on cervical spine injuries despite >10 years of published recommendations. 38 With new recommendations on spinal injury management published in 2020 40 and lacrosse-specific recommendations in 2025, 9 a larger percentage of documents may now contain outdated prevention, recognition, and management strategies. The NFHS, in coordination with medical staff, must work to improve the adoption and implementation of the policies and procedures outlining these strategies, while also mandating and enforcing sport-specific safety rules.

IRs by Decade

Despite the frequency of injuries and sports participation increasing across the first 3 decades, IRs decreased each decade, with 2012-2013 to 2021-2022 recording the lowest (IR, 0.06). The difference observed between the years of 2002-2003 to 2011-2012 (IR, 0.22) and 2011-2012 to 2021-2022 decades may be partially influenced by a decrease in sponsored athletic events/opportunities due to the COVID-19 pandemic. 27 However, a reduction in the rate of CHNIs over time has been observed in individual sports through examination of prevention strategy effectiveness. Examples include decreased IRs over time during the period of the current study for pole vault, 2 cheerleading, 56 hockey, 24 field hockey,6,25 and, anecdotally, in swimming. 10 Similarly, although football is not included in this study, traumatic brain injury rates among these athletes have also dropped considerably over the past 20 years, due to the implementation of safety regulations (pre- vs post-rule change: IRR, 0.82). 50 Although determining the cause of the reduction in the IR over time is outside the scope of this analysis, this finding, viewed through the lens of these effective prevention strategies, is promising. It should encourage the sports community partners to continue enforcing and adopting current CHNI prevention strategies.

IRs by Sex and Sport

Total IRs were significantly higher in male compared with female patients (0.5 vs 0.1 per 100,000 AS; IRR, 0.25). However, cheerleading was responsible for most injuries to female participants, and they were not represented in this rate comparison. When utilizing NCAA and NFHS participation data to observe IRs across all 40 years, the 5 highest rates were recorded by gymnastics (IR, 1.7), ice hockey (IR, 1.5), wrestling (IR, 0.4), lacrosse (IR, 0.4), and baseball (IR, 0.2). When using SMA data, the IR for cheerleading was 0.4 per 100,000 AS. Regarding risk, several of the sports mentioned above are often overlooked because of their higher IRs, such as football (IR, 1.1 per 100,000 athletes), 50 highlighting the need for sport-based analysis or subanalysis of IRs to remain a priority for researchers to provide sports medicine clinicians with setting-specific data.

When stratified by level or sex, gymnastics (high school/women’s) and ice hockey (college/men) represented the highest rates of injury for both categories. Prevention strategies have been implemented for ice hockey (penalty rule change), 24 lacrosse (checking rule change [men’s] and headgear mandate [women’s]),15,17 baseball (facemasks), 35 and cheerleading (basket toss elimination), 56 with significant reductions in IR. Though gaps exist in these prevention efforts, some methods are adopted only by specific regions or levels. Examples can be seen in lacrosse (high school 15 and Florida high schools 17 ), baseball (youth leagues), 35 and hockey (Rhode Island high schools). 24 Expanding data analysis over time or location could enhance understanding of these rules’ impact on CHNI and evaluate policy effectiveness and participation changes.

Despite having the highest IR overall, for women, and for college athletes in this analysis, we found no studies specifically examining catastrophic injury in gymnastics. While several publications exist on the epidemiology of injury in gymnastics,11,22,34,36,48,57 they often focus on less severe injuries that occur at a higher frequency.11,22,34,48 Many report gymnastic event frequency (bars, beam, vault, etc), though often not specifying the mechanism/action leading to injury. A deeper investigation of the risk factors for catastrophic injuries inherent to these events, such as action/sport-specific mechanisms (specific stunts) or skill familiarity (ie, novice/proficient/expert), can enhance injury prevention for gymnasts. Similarly, wrestling-related catastrophic injuries have been explored, 5 yet primary prevention measures such as rule changes to address head contact for takedowns have not. Institutions and governing bodies should develop and adopt additional prevention strategies to reduce CHNI in sports, particularly in gymnastics and wrestling, which first require further investigation into sport-specific causes to inform prevention efforts.

Limitations

This study has a few notable limitations. As previously mentioned, participation data were utilized from 3 sources and may not accurately represent national participation numbers. Injury surveillance by NCCSIR relies primarily on media searches followed by direct reports from parents, athletes, sport affiliates, sport organizations, and so forth. NCCSIR's data collection methods may underestimate catastrophic event frequency and may be incomplete. One possible solution to address these limitations is to compare NCCSIR and NEISS data in future analyses, which may enable greater generalizability of the results. Finally, due to the retrospective nature of this study, we (1) were limited by gaps in the breadth of previous and current data collected, which contributed to the missingness of data for some variables (ie, race, sport-specific mechanism/action), and we (2) could not establish a causal relationship between the reduction in IRs and participation declines, especially considering the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. Future studies would benefit from further examination of sex, race, and sport-based differences over time, especially where rule, sponsorship, and policy changes have occurred.

Conclusion

An analysis of 40 years of NCCSIR data on high school and college nonfootball sports revealed that half of the CHNI resulted in permanent disability or death. Although rare, differences in CHNI event and injury characteristics exist by level and sex. While IRs in nonfootball sports have declined, disparities in rates persist, particularly among male and high school athletes. Those participating in cheerleading, baseball, gymnastics, wrestling, and ice hockey have incurred the highest IRs. Although CHNI occur infrequently in the sports examined, our results emphasize the debilitating and life-altering effects, which may inform future research, policy, and rule changes at the NFHS and NCAA levels. Deeper investigation of sport-specific injury mechanisms by sex in these participants, combined with ongoing surveillance, is necessary to develop targeted CHNI prevention strategies.
