Thermality Breakdown in Null-Shifted Rindler Wedges.
Rakesh K Jha

Abstract
We investigate the behaviour of quantum fields in null-shifted Rindler wedges and analyse the particle spectra perceived by accelerated observers associated with these null deformations. Unlike the standard Unruh effect, our analysis compares two accelerated frames connected by a null displacement. We consider both massive scalar and Dirac fields, constructing their corresponding mode solutions in Rindler coordinates. Using normalised field expansions, we compute the Bogoliubov transformations between modes defined in the two null-shifted wedges. Our results demonstrate a fundamental breakdown of thermality: the presence of mass modifies the mode structure, rendering the characteristic exponential mixing of frequencies absent. This suggests that the massive field remains unexcited on this background, leading to a manifestly nonthermal response. These findings highlight that thermality in accelerated frames depends sensitively on the conformal symmetry of the field, which is broken by the introduction of a mass term.



I
Introduction

Quantum field theory in non-inertial frames provides a natural framework for exploring the observer dependence of particle concepts. A prominent example is the Unruh effect, which states that a uniformly accelerated observer perceives the inertial vacuum as a thermal state. This phenomenon arises from the nontrivial relation between field modes defined in Minkowski spacetime and those associated with Rindler coordinates
26
;
27
. As a consequence, the particle content of a quantum field depends not only on the state of the field but also on the observer and the spacetime region used in defining the mode decomposition
13
.

Rindler spacetime therefore plays a central role in understanding horizon physics and observer-dependent thermality
23
. In the conventional setting, the Minkowski vacuum appears thermal when expressed in terms of modes confined to a single Rindler wedge
12
;
26
. This thermal behaviour is typically characterised by the Planck distribution for bosonic fields and the Fermi–Dirac distribution for fermionic fields
20
. However, the emergence of thermality depends crucially on the geometric relation between the observers and the regions of spacetime to which their modes are restricted. It is therefore natural to ask how the particle spectrum changes when the relation between the associated wedges is modified.

A particularly interesting situation arises when one considers Rindler wedges related by shifts along null directions. Such null shifts preserve the causal structure of the horizon while altering the relation between the corresponding coordinate systems
20
;
13
;
10
. The resulting wedges describe observers that remain accelerated but are displaced relative to one another along a null direction. This setup naturally raises the question of how quantum field modes transform between such null-shifted wedges and whether the particle spectra perceived by these observers retain thermal characteristics.

Another conceptual issue concerns the role of mass in relativistic field theory. Massless fields propagate strictly along null directions, naturally transmitting information through spacetime along lightlike trajectories. In situations involving horizons and causal boundaries, these massless modes are therefore crucial for encoding and conveying information. In contrast, massive fields introduce additional structure into the field equations through mass terms that modify the propagation of field modes and couple different components of the field. Consequently, the presence of mass can alter the information-carrying behaviour of the underlying modes and may influence how accelerated observers perceive particle spectra.

Motivated by these considerations, we investigate the behaviour of quantum fields in null-shifted Rindler wedges, with particular emphasis on the role of mass. We analyse both massive scalar fields and Dirac fields and study the corresponding mode structures in Rindler spacetime. By constructing the relevant solutions and evaluating Bogoliubov transformations between null-shifted wedges, we examine the particle spectra perceived by observers associated with these wedges and explore how mass affects the emergence of thermal features.

The paper is organised as follows. In Sec .
II
, we review the Rindler coordinate system and introduce the null-shifted wedges relevant to our analysis. In Sec .
III
we study the massive scalar field and derive the corresponding mode solutions and Bogoliubov coefficients. Sec .
IV
extends the analysis to the Dirac field. In this section, we analyse the resulting particle spectra and discuss their physical implications. Technical details, including normalisation of modes and integral evaluations, are presented in the appendices.

II.2
Bogoliubov Transformation and the Number Operator

In this section, we derive the Bogoliubov transformation between two complete sets of mode functions and use it to evaluate the expectation value of the number operator in different Rindler frames.

Let
\{f_{i}(\omega)\}
and
\{f_{j}(\Omega)\}
be two complete sets of positive-norm, orthonormal modes to the inner Klein-Gordon product. When two such complete sets of modes are available, one set can be expressed in terms of the other using the Bogoliubov transformation
25
;
4

f_{j}(\Omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\;\bigg(\alpha_{ji}(\Omega,\omega)\;f_{i}(\omega)+\beta_{ji}(\Omega,\omega)\;f^{*}_{i}(\omega)\bigg),

(21)

Or, conversely,

f_{i}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\Omega\bigg(\alpha^{*}_{ji}(\Omega,\omega)\;f_{j}(\Omega)-\beta_{ji}(\Omega,\omega)\;f^{*}_{j}(\Omega)\bigg),

(22)

Here, the quantities
\alpha_{ji}
and
\beta_{ji}
are called the Bogoliubov coefficients.

A real quantum scalar field
\hat{\Phi}
, can be decomposed in terms of these sets of modes
\{f_{i}(\omega)\}
and
\{f_{j}(\Omega)\}
,

\hat{\Phi}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\;\bigg(\hat{a}_{i}\;f_{i}(\omega)+\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\;f^{*}_{i}(\omega)\bigg),

(23)

\hat{a_{i}}
and
\hat{a_{i}}^{\dagger}
are the annihilation and creation operators for the Rindler mode
i
. These operators follow the ETCR (equal time commutation relations) as

\begin{split}&\big[\hat{a}_{i},\hat{a}_{i^{\prime}}\big]=0\\
&\big[\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger},\hat{a}_{i^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\big]=0\\
&\big[\hat{a}_{i},\hat{a}_{i^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\big]=\delta(i-i^{\prime}),\end{split}

(24)

These commutation relations apply to free scalar fields quantised on equal-time hypersurfaces in curved spacetime.

The Rindler vacuum
\mid 0_{R}\rangle
is then defined as the state annihilated by
\hat{a}_{i}

\hat{a}_{i}\mid 0_{R_{i}}\rangle=0,\;\forall i

(25)

Also:

\hat{\Phi}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\Omega\;\bigg(\hat{a}_{j}\;f_{j}(\Omega)+\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\;f^{*}_{j}(\Omega)\bigg),

(26)

\hat{a}_{j}
and
\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}
are the annihilation and creation operators for the Rindler mode

\hat{a}_{i}
and
\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}
in Eq. (
24

The vacuum state can analogously be defined as

\hat{a}_{j}\mid 0_{\mathrm{R}_{j}}\rangle=0,\qquad\forall j

(27)

Using Eqs. (
23

26

\hat{a}_{j}(\Omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;\bigg(\alpha^{*}_{ji}(\Omega,\omega)\;\hat{a}_{i}(\omega)-\beta^{*}_{ji}(\Omega,\omega)\;\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)\bigg),

(28)

\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\Omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;\bigg(\alpha_{ji}(\Omega,\omega)\;\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)-\beta_{ji}(\Omega,\omega)\;\hat{a}_{i}(\omega)\bigg),

(29)

When

in the vacuum state annihilated by the operator

\begin{split}\langle 0_{R_{i}}\mid\hat{N}_{ji}(\Omega)\mid 0_{R_{i}}\rangle=\langle 0_{R_{i}}\mid\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\Omega)\;\hat{a}_{j}(\Omega)\mid 0_{R_{i}}\rangle\\

II.3
Choice of vacuum and symmetry considerations

The notion of particles in quantum field theory depends on the choice
of positive-frequency modes used to define the vacuum state. In Minkowski spacetime, the natural global vacuum is the Minkowski vacuum
|0_{M}\rangle
, defined with respect to the timelike Killing vector
\partial_{T}
. The field may be expanded in terms of positive-frequency Minkowski modes
u(\omega)^{(M)}
as

\hat{\Phi}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\left(\hat{a}(\omega)^{(M)}u(\omega)^{(M)}+\hat{a}(\omega)^{(M)\dagger}u(\omega)^{(M)*}\right),

(31)

and the Minkowski vacuum is defined by

\hat{a}(\omega)^{(M)}|0_{M}\rangle=0,\qquad\forall\omega>0.

(32)

This state is invariant under the full Poincaré group and is
regular across the entire spacetime.

For uniformly accelerated observers, it is natural to expand the
field in Rindler modes adapted to the Killing vector

\partial_{t_{i}}
associated with the Rindler time in wedge
R_{i}
.
One may then formally introduce the Rindler vacuum
|0_{R_{i}}\rangle

through the conditions

\overrightarrow{\hat{a}}_{i}(\omega)\,|0_{R_{i}}\rangle=0,\qquad\overleftarrow{\hat{b}}_{i}(\omega)\,|0_{R_{i}}\rangle=0,\qquad\forall\,\omega>0.

(33)

Unlike the Minkowski vacuum, the Rindler vacuum is not a globally
defined state. The corresponding mode functions are supported only
within a single Rindler wedge and are singular at the Rindler
horizons. Consequently,
|0_{R_{i}}\rangle
cannot be extended as a
regular state across the full Minkowski spacetime. For this reason, the Rindler vacuum is often regarded as unphysical when considered as a
global state
1
;
25
. Nevertheless, for observers whose worldlines remain confined to the wedge
R_{i}
, the Killing vector
\partial_{t_{i}}
generates time translations along their trajectories, providing a natural notion of positive frequency and a consistent particle interpretation. In this sense, the Rindler vacuum defines a consistent particle interpretation within the wedge.

The transformation between Minkowski and Rindler modes, therefore,
leads to nontrivial Bogoliubov coefficients. As a result, the
Minkowski vacuum appears as a thermal state when expressed in terms
of Rindler modes, which underlie the Unruh effect. In the present work, we adopt the Rindler vacuum associated with the wedge
R_{1}
as the reference state for our mode analysis and investigate how this state appears when
described in terms of modes associated with other null-shifted wedges. That is, the state
|0_{R_{1}}\rangle
is defined by the annihilation conditions:

\overrightarrow{\hat{a}}_{1}(\omega)\,|0_{R_{1}}\rangle=0,\qquad\overleftarrow{\hat{b}}_{1}(\omega)\,|0_{R_{1}}\rangle=0,\qquad\forall\,\omega>0.

This choice corresponds to the natural vacuum for observers whose
trajectories remain confined to the wedge
R_{1}
, with positive
frequency defined with respect to the Rindler time
t_{1}
. Although
This state is not globally regular across the Rindler horizons; it
provides a consistent particle interpretation for accelerated
observers in
R_{1}
and serves as the reference vacuum for the mode
analysis carried out in the following sections.

III.1
The Field Equation

We begin with a real scalar field

\nabla^{\mu}\;\nabla_{\mu}\phi(t,x)-m^{2}\;\phi(t,x)=0,

g^{\mu\nu}\;\nabla_{\mu}\;\nabla_{\nu}\phi(t,x)-m^{2}\;\phi(t,x)=0,

(35)

The line element in

-dimensional Rindler coordinates takes the conformally flat form:

ds^{2}=e^{2\;a\;x}\;(dt^{2}-dx^{2}),

(36)

From which the metric and its inverse are given by:

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\;2\;a\;x}&&0\\
0&&-e^{\;2\;a\;x}\end{pmatrix};g^{\mu\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\;-2\;a\;x}&&0\\

e^{\;-2\;a\;x}\;\frac{\partial^{2}\phi(t,x)}{\partial t^{2}}-e^{\;-2\;a\;x}\;\frac{\partial^{2}\phi(t,x)}{\partial x^{2}}-m^{2}\;\phi(t,x)=0,

(38)

Which simplifies to:

\bigg(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}-m^{2}\;e^{\;2\;a\;x}\bigg)\;\phi(t,x)=0,

Substituting into Eq. (

\begin{split}z&=\frac{m}{a}\;e^{\;a\;x}\\
\frac{d}{dx}&=a\;z\;\frac{d}{dz}\\

V
Conclusion

In this work, we have investigated the behaviour of quantum fields in a set of null-shifted Rindler wedges and examined the relation between the associated vacuum states. Starting from the Klein-Gordon equation for a massive scalar field in
(1+1)
-dimensional Rindler spacetime, we constructed the complete set of mode solutions. We analysed the Bogoliubov transformation between the field expansions defined in different null-shifted wedges.

Our analysis shows that, unlike the massless case, the presence of a mass
term significantly alters the structure of the field modes. In particular,
the solutions of the massive Klein-Gordon equation are expressed in terms
of modified Bessel functions rather than simple exponential null modes.
As a consequence, the field no longer admits a decomposition into purely
left-moving and right-moving components, reflecting the breaking of the
conformal symmetry that characterises the massless theory in two dimensions.

The Bogoliubov coefficients relating the mode expansions in the
null-shifted wedges were computed explicitly. We find that the
transformation does not lead to the characteristic Gamma function
structure that is responsible for the appearance of the Planck factor
in thermal spectra. Consequently, the expected exponential factor

e^{-2\pi\omega/a}
does not arise in the particle number expectation
value. The absence of this structure implies that the spectrum does
not exhibit thermality in the sense of a Planck distribution.

Our results, therefore, indicate that the mechanism responsible for the
thermal spectrum in the massless case is closely tied to the conformal
structure
14
of the underlying field equation. Our results indicate that thermality is not a universal consequence of acceleration but is intrinsically linked to the conformal structure of the field. The mass term introduces a physical scale that makes the observer’s perceived vacuum state invariant under the null-shifts considered here, thereby suppressing the Unruh-like thermal response.

These findings highlight the important role played by the symmetry
structure of the field equations in determining the thermodynamic
properties perceived by accelerated observers. The analysis presented
here suggests that the emergence of thermality in Rindler-type settings
is not solely a consequence of acceleration, but is also intimately
linked to the conformal properties of the underlying quantum field theory.

Future investigations into whether this nonthermal behaviour persists in higher dimensions or under non-linear deformations could provide deeper insights into the interplay between mass, horizons, and observer-dependent physics.

\langle\psi^{\prime}|\psi\rangle=-N_{\omega^{\prime}}N_{\omega}\;e^{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}\;I(\omega,\omega^{\prime}),

(138)

Where:

I(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\;=\;\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{m}\;K_{\frac{i\omega}{a}-\tfrac{1}{2}}(p)\;K_{\frac{i\omega^{\prime}}{a}+\tfrac{1}{2}}(p),

(139)

and
K_{\nu}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind.

We evaluate
I(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
using the standard Bessel-product identity:

K_{\mu}(z)K_{\nu}(z)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\mu+\nu}(2z\cosh t)\,\cosh[(\mu-\nu)t]\,dt,

(140)

with

\mu=\frac{i\omega}{a}-\frac{1}{2},\qquad\nu=\frac{i\omega^{\prime}}{a}+\frac{1}{2}.

Interchanging the order of integration gives:

\begin{split}I(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\frac{2}{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;\cosh\!\Big(\Big[\frac{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})}{a}-1\Big]t\Big)\\
\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\;K_{\frac{i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})}{a}}\!\big(2p\cosh t\big),\end{split}

(141)

Next, we evaluate the inner integral concerning
p
. Using the standard integral
21
:

\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\;K_{\nu}(\beta p)=\frac{\pi}{2\beta\cosh\left(\frac{\pi\nu}{2}\right)},\quad\text{for }|\Re\nu|<1,\quad\Re\beta>0,

(142)

and setting

\nu=\frac{i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})}{a},\quad\beta=2\cosh t,

we find

\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\;K_{\frac{i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})}{a}}(2p\cosh t)=\frac{\pi}{4\cosh t\;\cosh\left(\frac{\pi(\omega+\omega^{\prime})}{2a}\right)},

(143)

Substituting back, we obtain:

\begin{split}I(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\frac{\pi}{2m\;\cosh\left(\frac{\pi(\omega+\omega^{\prime})}{2a}\right)}\\
\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;\frac{\cosh\left(\left[\frac{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})}{a}-1\right]t\right)}{\cosh t},\end{split}

(144)

We want to evaluate the integral:

J(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;\frac{\cosh\left(\left[\frac{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})}{a}-1\right]t\right)}{\cosh t},

(145)

Let us define:
k\equiv\frac{\omega-\omega^{\prime}}{a}

The numerator can be expanded as:

\begin{split}\cosh\bigg((i\;k-1)t\bigg)=\cosh\left(i\;k\;t\right)\cosh t-\sinh(i\;k\;t)\sinh t,\end{split}

(146)

Thus the
t
-integral reduces to:

J(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\bigg[\cosh(k\;t)-i\;\tanh t\;\sinh\;(k\;t)\bigg],

(147)

The integral diverges at large
t
,so introduce a convergence factor
e^{-\epsilon\;t}
with
\epsilon\;>0
11
;
28

J_{\epsilon}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;e^{-\epsilon\;t}\bigg[\cosh(k\;t)-i\;\tanh t\;\sinh\;(k\;t)\bigg],

(148)

We will take
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
at the end.The above integral can be written in real and imaginary parts:

J_{\epsilon}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=J_{\epsilon}^{(Re)}+J_{\epsilon}^{(Im)},

(149)

Analyzing the real part of Eq. (
148
)
17
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;e^{-\epsilon\;t}\cosh(k\;t)=\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^{2}+k^{2}},

(150)

As
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
,this becomes sharply peaked at
\omega^{\prime}=\omega
and converges to a delta function
7
:

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}=\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^{2}+\left(\frac{\omega-\omega^{\prime}}{a}\right)^{2}}=\pi\;a\;\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}),

(151)

We focus on the imaginary part of the integral:

J_{\epsilon}^{(\text{Im})}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-\epsilon t}\tanh(t)\,\sinh(kt),

(152)

where
k=\frac{\omega-\omega^{\prime}}{a}
. Using the identity
\sinh(kt)=\frac{1}{2}(e^{kt}-e^{-kt})
, we write:

\displaystyle J_{\epsilon}^{(\text{Im})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\tanh(t)\left(e^{-(\epsilon-k)t}-e^{-(\epsilon+k)t}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left[I(\epsilon-k)-I(\epsilon+k)\right],

(153)

where we define the auxiliary function:

I(\alpha):=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\tanh(t)\,e^{-\alpha t}.

(154)

To evaluate
I(\alpha)
, we use the series expansion:

\tanh(t)=1-2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-2nt},\quad t>0.

(155)

Substituting into
I(\alpha)
and integrating term-by-term yields:

\displaystyle I(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-\alpha t}-2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-(\alpha+2n)t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}-2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha+2n}.

(156)

This sum can be expressed in terms of the digamma function
\psi(z)
29
:

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha+2n}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\psi\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}+1\right)-\psi\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\right],

(157)

So that:

I(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\alpha}-\left[\psi\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}+1\right)-\psi\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\right].

(158)

Substituting Eq. (
158
) into Eq. (
153
), we obtain:

\begin{split}J_{\epsilon}^{Im}=-\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{\epsilon-k}-\Delta\psi(\epsilon-k)\bigg)\\
-\bigg(\frac{1}{\epsilon+k}-\Delta\psi(\epsilon+k)\bigg)\bigg]\end{split}

After rearranging the above expression, we obtain:

\begin{split}J_{\epsilon}^{Im}=-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{\epsilon-k}-\frac{1}{\epsilon+k}\bigg)+\\
\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\Delta\psi(\epsilon-k)-\Delta\psi(\epsilon+k)\bigg),\end{split}

(159)

where we define:

\Delta\psi(x):=\psi\left(\frac{x}{2}+1\right)-\psi\left(\frac{x}{2}\right).

(160)

For large
|k|
, the digamma difference behaves as:

\Delta\psi(x)\sim\frac{2}{x},\quad\text{as }|x|\to\infty.

(161)

Thus:

\Delta\psi(\epsilon-k)-\Delta\psi(\epsilon+k)\sim\frac{2}{\epsilon-k}-\frac{2}{\epsilon+k}=\frac{4k}{\epsilon^{2}-k^{2}}.

(162)

The first term in Eq. (
159
) also gives:

\frac{1}{\epsilon-k}-\frac{1}{\epsilon+k}=\frac{2k}{\epsilon^{2}-k^{2}}.

(163)

Therefore, the imaginary part becomes:

J_{\epsilon}^{(\text{Im})}\sim-\frac{k}{\epsilon^{2}-k^{2}}+\frac{2k}{\epsilon^{2}-k^{2}}=\frac{k}{\epsilon^{2}-k^{2}},

(164)

Which is an odd function in
k
and vanishes in the sense of distributions when integrated against smooth test functions. It does not introduce a delta-function singularity.

In the distributional limit
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
,

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}J_{\epsilon}^{(\text{Im})}=0

(165)

This confirms Eq. (
165
), shows that the imaginary part of
J_{\epsilon}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
vanishes in the distributional limit
17
;
24
.
So only the real part contributes to the delta function.

Since both the numerator and denominator of the integrand in Eq. (
145
) are even functions of
t
, we can extend the integral over
\bigg[0,\infty\bigg]
to the full real line
\bigg[-\infty,\infty\bigg]
as discussed in Lighthill
(
18
, Sec. 2.6)
and Gel’fand & Shilov
(
8
, Ch. I, §3)
. This extension is justified because the evenness ensures the extended integrand remains well-defined and integrable over
\mathbb{R}
.From Eqs. (
145
), (
150
) and (
151
), we write:

J(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;\frac{\cosh\left(\left[\frac{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})}{a}-1\right]t\right)}{\cosh t}=2\;\pi\;a\;\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}),

(166)

Substituting Eq. (
166
) back into Eq. (
144
), we get:

\begin{split}I(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\frac{\pi}{2m\cosh\bigg(\frac{\pi(\omega+\omega^{\prime})}{2a}\bigg)}\times 2\;\pi\;a\;\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\\
=\frac{\pi^{2}\;a}{m\;\cosh\bigg(\frac{\pi\omega}{a}\bigg)}\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}),\end{split}

(167)

Recall the Dirac inner product condition:

\langle\psi_{\omega^{\prime}}|\psi_{\omega}\rangle=|N_{\omega}|^{2}I(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}),

(168)

Which implies:

|N_{\omega}|^{2}\frac{\pi^{2}a}{m\cosh\left(\frac{\pi\omega}{a}\right)}=1,

(169)

Solving for the normalisation constant yields:

|N_{\omega}|=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{m}{a}\cosh\left(\frac{\pi\omega}{a}\right)}.
