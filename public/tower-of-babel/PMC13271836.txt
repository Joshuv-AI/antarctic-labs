Fogarty Catheter Removal of External Iliac Artery Thrombus Caused by Sheath-Related Thrombus Entrapment Following Mechanical Thrombectomy: A Case Report
Authors: Ai Mizukami, Ichiro Suzuki, Keisuke Sasaki, Yoshihiko Morisue, Naoya Iwabuchi, Atsushi Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe transfemoral approach is commonly used in neuroendovascular procedures. However, access-site complications, although infrequent, can have serious clinical consequences. Acute limb ischemia (ALI) is a particularly serious condition, carrying a significant risk of limb loss and death and therefore requires rapid diagnosis and urgent management.Case PresentationWe report a rare case of acute external iliac artery occlusion due to a thromboembolism at the distal end of a femoral sheath after mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke. During the procedure, reflux from both the guiding catheter and the femoral sheath disappeared, raising suspicion of an access-site complication; a contralateral femoral access angiography was performed. This evaluation revealed complete occlusion of the external iliac artery. Immediate thrombectomy was performed via a cut-down approach using a Fogarty arterial embolectomy catheter (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA), resulting in the rapid restoration of arterial blood flow. The patient experienced neither limb ischemia nor neurological deterioration and achieved a favorable outcome.ConclusionThrombotic occlusion at the tip of a femoral sheath is a rare but potentially devastating complication of neuroendovascular procedures. The loss of reflux from a femoral sheath should alert clinicians to the possibility of access-vessel thrombosis and prompt immediate vascular evaluation and multidisciplinary intervention.

Introduction

The transfemoral approach is widely used as the primary access route in neuroendovascular procedures. The potential complications of this approach include groin hematoma, femoral pseudoaneurysm, and femoral or iliofemoral arterial occlusion. Although thrombus retention within femoral sheath introducers has been reported during mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke,1) progression to downstream arterial occlusion is extremely rare. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a thrombus at the distal end of a femoral sheath that progressed to external iliac artery occlusion and required thrombectomy. We report this case and discuss its clinical implications.

Case Presentation

A man in his 70s with a history of atrial fibrillation, who was receiving oral anticoagulation therapy, underwent carotid endarterectomy for symptomatic left internal carotid artery (ICA) stenosis. Anticoagulation was discontinued 2 days before surgery and restarted on postoperative day 7; the patient was discharged on postoperative day 11. The following day, he presented to our hospital with transient monocular blindness. MRI revealed acute ischemic lesions in the bilateral anterior cerebral artery–posterior cerebral artery (PCA) watershed zones and in the left middle cerebral artery–PCA watershed zone, rather than in the core PCA territory. MRA demonstrated poor visualization of the right common carotid artery (CCA) (Fig. 1). These findings suggested that emboli originating from the right ICA may have reached the contralateral hemisphere via the anterior communicating artery. DSA revealed acute occlusion extending from the right CCA to the ICA (Fig. 2A). Collateral circulation via the anterior communicating artery and the posterior communicating artery was observed (Fig. 2B and 2C). Mechanical thrombectomy was performed under local anesthesia with systemic heparinization, achieving an activated clotting time of approximately 280 s. The right femoral artery was accessed using a micropuncture technique, and an 8-Fr-long sheath was inserted. An 8-Fr OPTIMO FLEX guiding catheter (Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) was advanced into the right ICA. Two aspiration passes were performed using the OPTIMO guiding catheter (Fig. 2D and 2E). Thrombi were retrieved in both passes (Fig. 2H-① and 2H-②); however, angiographic recanalization was not achieved. Subsequently, another pass was performed using an EmboTrap III stent retriever (6.5 × 45 mm; Cerenovus, Johnson & Johnson, Irvine, CA, USA) (Fig. 2F), and a 4th pass was performed using a combination of the EmboTrap III stent retriever and a Trevo NXT stent retriever (6 × 37 mm; Stryker Neurovascular, Fremont, CA, USA) (Fig. 2G). Thrombi were retrieved by both stent retrievers (Fig. 2H-③ and 2H-④); however, recanalization was still not achieved. After a 5th aspiration pass using the OPTIMO guiding catheter, reflux from the guiding catheter was no longer observed. The catheter was removed under continuous negative pressure, and only a small amount of thrombus was aspirated (Fig. 2H-⑤). These findings suggested a substantial thrombus burden within the access system. Subsequently, aspiration from the femoral sheath was attempted; however, no reflux was observed. Although the sheath was slightly withdrawn and partially cut at its midportion, no reflux was observed, suggesting that thrombotic occlusion had occurred at the distal end of the sheath. Subsequent angiography of the contralateral femoral artery demonstrated successful intracranial recanalization with Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (TICI) grade 3 reperfusion (Fig. 2I and 2J). After confirming intracranial recanalization, abdominal aortography demonstrated interruption of contrast flow in the right external iliac artery distal to the sheath tip (Fig. 3A). In collaboration with a vascular surgeon, a surgical cut-down was performed to expose the femoral artery, and the sheath was removed; however, no reflux was observed. Thrombectomy was therefore performed using a Fogarty arterial embolectomy catheter (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA). The catheter was advanced retrogradely beyond the filling defect into the right common iliac artery and withdrawn after balloon inflation (Fig. 3B), resulting in retrieval of a thrombus approximately 17 mm in length (Fig. 3C). The thrombus was firm, and histopathological examination revealed a fibrin-rich clot. The time from recognition of the external iliac artery occlusion to successful revascularization was 110 min. No lower-limb ischemic symptoms appeared, and the right dorsalis pedis artery pulse was readily palpable after thrombus removal. The patient experienced no new neurological deficits or limb ischemia and was discharged with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0.

Discussion

The transfemoral approach remains the most commonly used access route for neuroendovascular procedures. However, access-site–related complications, although infrequent, can be clinically significant. Acute limb ischemia (ALI) is a particularly serious condition, with reported amputation rates of 10%–30% and mortality rates of up to 15%–25%.2,3) This highlights the need for rapid diagnosis and urgent intervention. Although thrombus retention within femoral sheath introducers during mechanical thrombectomy has been reported, progression to external iliac artery occlusion is extremely rare. In most reported cases, the thrombus remained within the sheath lumen and was managed endovascularly without causing limb ischemia. In contrast, the present case demonstrates a clear and clinically significant mechanism in which thrombus occlusion at the distal end of a femoral sheath may lead to acute external iliac artery occlusion, requiring prompt surgical intervention. This distinction is important because distal embolization carries a significant risk of ALI and requires a different treatment strategy. The treatment options for ALI include catheter-directed thrombolysis, endovascular thrombectomy, surgical thrombectomy, and hybrid approaches.3,4) As catheter-directed thrombolysis requires time to achieve reperfusion and carries a risk of bleeding complications, its application is limited in patients with progressive or rapidly progressive ischemia.4,5) In contrast, mechanical and surgical thrombectomy allow for rapid and reliable revascularization, complete removal of a large thrombus burden, and direct assessment of the inflow and outflow vessels.3,4) The Fogarty arterial embolectomy catheter (Edwards Lifesciences) has long been used as one of the standard tools for surgical thrombectomy in ALI, with reported limb-salvage rates of approximately 70%–90%.6–8) When sheath occlusion is suspected, contralateral femoral access should be considered as the first-line method for evaluating the access vessel. This approach allows safe and accurate visualization of the external iliac and common femoral arteries without the risk of dislodging thrombus by retrograde contrast injection from the occluded side. This case also raises an important consideration regarding access selection. When a large thrombus burden or long-segment occlusion from the CCA to the ICA is anticipated, transfemoral access may be preferable. The radial artery has a smaller diameter, and sheath occlusion during thrombectomy may result in significant upper-limb dysfunction. In contrast, the femoral artery provides a larger access route with richer collateral circulation, which may reduce the risk of severe ischemia. In this case, the loss of reflux from both the guiding catheter and the femoral sheath was an important warning sign of access-site thrombosis. To reduce the risk of a distal embolism and device-related complications, we avoided further aggressive endovascular manipulation. In collaboration with a vascular surgeon, early conversion to thrombectomy using a Fogarty arterial embolectomy catheter enabled rapid restoration of arterial blood flow and prevented progression to critical limb ischemia. This case highlights the importance of vigilance for thrombotic complications at the access site during neuroendovascular procedures. If sheath reflux disappears, the access vessel must be immediately evaluated, and timely multidisciplinary intervention is essential for preventing catastrophic outcomes. As access-site complications are recognized complications, neuroendovascular surgeons should possess basic vascular surgical skills for prompt initial management in such cases.

Conclusion

Large thrombi can embolize or become lodged within sheath introducers. In cases of external iliac artery occlusion due to thrombotic occlusion at the distal end of a sheath, prompt thrombectomy using a Fogarty arterial embolectomy catheter (Edwards Lifesciences) is a rational and effective life-saving strategy for preventing ALI.
