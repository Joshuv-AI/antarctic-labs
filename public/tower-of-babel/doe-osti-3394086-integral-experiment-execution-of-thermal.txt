Executive Summary
This report documents the execution of experiments and measurements for IER 519, Thermal/Epithermal
eXperiments (TEX) for Hanford applications, using plutonium Zero Power Physics Reactor (ZPPR) plates
moderated by interstitial polyethylene and iron (Fe) absorber plates. Initial hand stack, mass, and
dimensional measurements were performed in July 2025. The experiments were completed over three
weeks from December 2025 to January 2026 at the National Criticality Experiments Research Center
(NCERC) at the Nevada National Security Sites (NNSS). All photos and critical data were provided by
NCERC and experimenters in LANL’s NEN-2.

Planet, a vertical-lift assembly machine, was used for the TEX-Fe experiments. Each experiment used
plutonium ZPPR plates with varying thicknesses of iron absorber plates between polyethylene moderator
plates. As shown in Table 1-1, a total of three experiments were executed, using either 0.500-inch or 0.375-
inch iron absorber plates in conjunction with 0.125-inch, 0.3125-inch, or 0.4375-inch polyethylene
moderator plates. Each fuel layer consists of a thin polyethylene tray riveted to a thin aluminum heat sink
fin holding a maximum of 24 plutonium ZPPR plates in a 6 by 4 plate array. Multiple fuel and
absorber/moderator units were stacked together and reflected by a 1-inch bottom reflector and 1-inch to 1-
3/4-inch top reflector to create critical configurations. The total top reflector thickness was adjusted as
needed for fine reactivity control. One of the three configurations, Fe-11, consisted of a partial fuel layer,
which contained 8 of 24 ZPPR plates in the upper most fuel layer in the stack.

Section 1 Detailed Description
1.1 Experimental Overview
This report documents the TEX-Fe critical experiments, which extend the TEX-Pu benchmark series to
support criticality safety validation for Hanford waste applications. The original TEX-Pu experiments,
documented in PU-MET-MIXED-002 [1], first evaluated the five TEX-Pu baseline configurations with
plutonium/aluminum metal alloy Zero Power Physics Reactor (ZPPR) Plutonium-Aluminum No-Nickel
(PANN) plates moderated by polyethylene, covering five different fission energy regimes, with varying
fractions of thermal, intermediate, and fast fissions. PU-MET-MIXED-002 provided a reference for
evaluating plutonium systems with hydrogenous moderation with various diluents.

TEX-Fe builds directly on that foundation by retaining the same ZPPR fuel, polyethylene moderation, and
Planet vertical-lift assembly machine, while introducing iron absorber plates as a diluent. This continuity
with the earlier benchmark series allows several sections of this work to reference prior work, including
PU-MET-MIXED-002 [1], PU-MET-MIXED-003 [2], PU-MET-THERM-004 [3]. The validation need is
motivated by Hanford’s criticality safety concerns with the Hanford tank farms, where waste solids contain
an estimated 670 kg of plutonium across 177 tanks holding about 56 million gallons of waste. Current
criticality safety analysis credits absorption and dilution from waste constituents including aluminum,
chromium, iron, manganese, nickel, silicon, sodium, and zirconium, with iron and manganese serving as
the primary neutron absorbers in the most limiting waste compositions [4].

Three critical configurations were successfully executed across 15 critical measurements in December of
2025 and January of 2026 at the National Criticality Experiments Research Center (NCERC) at the Nevada
National Security Site (NNSS). The general-purpose, vertical-lift assembly machine, Planet, was used for
the TEX-Fe experiments [6]. Figure 1-1 shows a picture of the Planet machine during the original TEX-Pu
experiments in 2018, with the lower platen withdrawn. Figure 1-2 shows a schematic of the TEX-Pu
configurations, with all components labeled. It is important to note that the diluent layers also exist in the
lower stack of Figure 1-2, which was simplified for readability. Detailed descriptions of all components are
provided in the following sections.

1.2 Description of Experimental Configuration
1.2.1 Plutonium Aluminum and Aluminum Surrogate ZPPR Plates
1.2.1.1 Plutonium-Aluminum Plates
The plutonium ZPPR plates were initially procured and used by Argonne National Laboratory’s (ANL)
ZPPR program. While ZPPR plates vary in fuel type and form, the ones used throughout the many TEX
plutonium experiments were of the Plutonium Aluminum No Nickel (PANN) types. These plates were
initially fabricated by the Dow Chemical Company at Rocky Flats in the 1960s and composed of delta-
phase plutonium stabilized with 1.1% mass fraction of aluminum. The PANN plates have a nominal outer
cladding dimensions of 0.125-inch by 2-inch by 3-inch. Through the TEX plutonium experiments,
Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) committed substantial effort to characterize the PANN
plates, including dimensional measurements, radiochemistry, and destructive testing.

The following discussion was most recently described in PU-MET-THERM-004 and will be repeated here
for context [3]. The plutonium cores of the plates were fabricated by rolling cast slabs of Pu-Al to the
desired thickness of 0.084-inches to 0.086 inches, and strips were sheared and machined to a width of 1.763-
inches to 1.767-inches. The cores were cut to a length of 2.923-inches to 2.937-inches and the edges were
radiused. Figure 1-3 presents this plutonium core with the specified dimensions1, 2, 3.

As detailed in the original specification, the 239Pu content of each plate had the most restrictive tolerance,
with a target mass of 98.98 g ± 0.5 g. To meet this tight tolerance, the specification allowed the mass of the
core to be adjusted by drilling 3/32-inch to 1/4-inch diameter holes, with a maximum of five holes in scatter
locations in a single core. Alternatively, the edges and corners were allowed to be shaved as long as the
dimensions did not fall below the minimum specifications. It is believed that the PANN cores were shaved
to meet the mass tolerances instead of having holes drilled, based on measurements from a disassembled
plate by LLNL in 2018 and review of a radiographic record completed by ANL in 1962, which did not note
any drilled holes in the plates [3].

The construction and assembly of the ZPPR plate is shown in Figure 1-4 and Figure 1-5. This discussion
was largely taken from ANL-7313, Part 15. Figure 1-6 presents the outer dimensions of the stainless steel
cladding from the original specifications and drawings. The cladding was fabricated by folding 12-mil,
Type 304L stainless steel strips around a rectangular mandrel and welding one edge (Steps A through D of
Figure 1-4). A flat plug was then welded into the bottom (Step E of Figure 1-5). The dimensions of the plug
are shown in Figure 1-6. The cladding was made to a relatively loose fit to facilitate core loading. A flat
spring was placed in one end of the cladding to hold the core plate against the opposite end of the cladding.
The uncompressed spring dimensions are shown in Figure 1-8. According to the specification, the plates
are marked with the core batch letter and core batch serial number along the short edge of the plate closest
to the spring.

As detailed in the original specification for the Pu-Al plates, each plate was to be coated with nickel
deposited from carbonyl to allow loading and welding without cladding contamination and to form a
secondary oxidation barrier. The carbonyl-nickel coating was found to be impractical to apply and
prohibitively costly. Dow Chemical Company personnel developed a method of loading that protected the
cladding from contamination by means of a disposable, shim-stock loading funnel and sheath. This method
was used for the PANN plates, which is why they do not have the nickel coating.

The side of the element through which the cladding was loaded was closed by a stainless steel plug strip
(Step G in Figure 1-6). The welds were made at a sub-atmospheric pressure of helium-argon gas mixture
by means of a TIG welder developed at ANL. The reduced-pressure welds were required to prevent inflation
of the cladding at lower atmospheric pressure and by thermally expanded gas. The external atmospheric
pressure forced the sides of the cladding against the core plate.

The final, fully assembled plate had to pass through a go-no-go gage, which effectively set the upper bound
for the outer dimensions of the clad plate. Three gages were used: a 0.125-inch gage to check height
(thickness), a 3.01-inch gage to check the length of the plate, and a 2.00-inch gage to check the width of
the plate. Each plate was required to pass through the gages by the force of their own weight.

1.2.1.2 Aluminum Surrogate ZPPR Plates

as blanks or surrogates, with the same outer dimensions of the PANN plates were used to maintain the
PANN plate location and spacing. As discussed later in Section 2.5, these aluminum surrogate plates were
only used in the top layer of the Fe-11 configuration. The surrogates were fabricated to outer dimensions
of height, width, and length of 0.122-inches (0.3099 cm) by 2.00-inches (5.08 cm) by 3.00-inches (7.62
cm).

1.2.2 TEX Fuel Trays
The plutonium layers were arranged on trays to facilitate stacking of the experimental assembly. Multiple
layers were stacked together with varying thicknesses of interspersed diluent layers placed between the
layers to tune the neutron spectrum of the assembly. The trays in this series of experiments were the same
trays used for the TEX-Pu baseline configurations, evaluated as PU-MET-MIXED-002 [1].

The TEX fuel trays were also first procured and used in PU-MET-MIXED-002 and consist of an aluminum
heat dispersal fin sandwiched between an upper polyethylene (PE) tray frame and a lower polyethylene
moderator sheet as shown in Figure 1-10 through Figure 1-12. Eight aluminum rivets are used to hold the
fuel tray assembly together and are further described in the following subsections.

There were two kinds of trays used for the polyethylene experiments. The first tray, TEX-TRAY-PE-1/8,
had a 0.0625-inch (0.159 cm) thick high-density PE bottom with an integrated 0.011-inch (0.028 cm) Al-
6061 heat dispersal plate that extended beyond the edge of the tray. Design dimensions of this tray are
shown in Figure 1-10. The second tray type was designated TEX-TRAY-PE-1/8-TC, which was the same
as TEX-TRAY-PE-1/8 except that it had a 0.078-inch (0.198 cm) diameter channel milled out of the
polyethylene bottom to accommodate a resistance temperature detector (RTD) to monitor the temperature
in the assembly. The channel extended from the center of the polyethylene sheet to the edge, as shown in
the drawing in Figure 1-11. Figure 1-12 shows an upside-down picture of a tray with an RTD inserted. All
trays incorporated 1-inch (2.54 cm) of lateral reflection around the edge of the plutonium layer, provided
by a 0.1875-inch (0.47625 cm) thick PE frame. The PE and aluminum parts of the trays were held together
with aluminum rivets in eight locations around the perimeter of the tray. For TEX-TRAY-PE-1/8-TC, due
to the channel location in the middle of the polyethylene plate, the central aluminum rivet on one side was
relocated by 0.5-inch (1.27 cm).

1.2.2.1 Polyethylene Tray Parts
All parts for the PE trays were procured from Plastic Machining Company, a division of Interstate Plastics,
in Portland, Oregon, in 2016. The material is Quadrant EPP Proteus High Density Polyethylene, with a
reported density of 0.960 g/cm3. Upon receipt, the parts were inspected and measured in the LLNL machine
inspection shops and the data are shown in Table 1-6 and Table 1-7. The rivet holes were predrilled in the
PE frames and 30 parts of a total of 65 were measured. Twenty trays without RTD wells and 20 trays with
RTD wells were fabricated for a total of 40 trays. PE part densities were determined via high precision
measurement of a subsample of each part type. The volumes of the samples were determined with a gas
pycnometer, a Micromeritics AccuPyc II 1340 Gas Pycnometer. The pycnometer uses helium gas under
pressure to determine the volume of the sample placed in a sample cup. The pycnometer was calibrated

1.3 Description of Material Data
1.3.1 Plutonium and Aluminum Surrogate ZPPR Plates
The plutonium aluminum plates and aluminum surrogate plates have been used in several TEX experiments,
including PU-MET-MIXED-002, PU-MET-MIXED-003, and PU-MET-THERM-004. The discussion in
this section was largely taken from these benchmarks as the fuel remains unchanged.

1.3.1.1 Plutonium-Aluminum Plates
As detailed in the original specification, the 239Pu content of each plate had the most restrictive tolerance,
with a target mass of 98.98 ± 0.5 g. As such, the specification allowed the weight of the core to be adjusted
by drilling 3/32-inch to 1/4-inch diameter holes, with a maximum of five holes in scatter locations in a
single core. Alternatively, the edges and corners were allowed to be shaved as long as the dimensions did
not fall below the minimum specifications. The original specification called for plutonium with 93.5 wt. %
239
 Pu to be used to make the plates. However, the 239Pu content of the plates was actually 95.02 wt.%. This
discrepancy is explained in a letter dated November 29, 1960 from Dow Chemical to ANL11, which stated
that the available plutonium for the plates was 95.02% 239Pu. Since higher 239Pu content was used for the
plates, the size of the cores will likely be closer to the minimums required by the specification to achieve
the tight tolerances on the 239Pu target mass.

Composition data presented here was taken from an Argonne database that contained plate-by-plate detailed
information on overall plate mass, Pu-Al core mass, and major isotopic composition masses. Average fuel
and cladding masses and compositions for the Pu-Al plates are given in Table 1-21. Each Pu-Al fuel core
was weighed before and after cladding. The required accuracy of these original measurements was 0.01
grams on a balance having a precision of 0.005 grams and the entire population of 1266 3-inch PANN plates
was measured. Similarly, samples from each melt were analyzed when the fuel was cast, and the data was
used to derive the aluminum, impurity, and plutonium isotopic mass distributions in Table 1-21. Each melt
constituted approximately 30 plates, and the melt number was included as a letter stamped on the plates,
for example “A” or “B”. These original measurements form the basis of the “hot constants memo” a
summary of information in the ANL electronic plate material library (ADEN library). The last and most
recently issued hot constants memo is dated April 200112. The mass of the stainless steel cladding was
calculated for each plate based on the difference between the mass of the final, welded plate, and the
plutonium core mass. The composition of the 304-L stainless steel cladding of the PANN plates was found
in the 2001 hot constants memo and is reported in Table 1-22. From the original specification for the PANN
plate, the steel spring was required to be made of flat, SAE 1060 spring steel.

2.6 Fe-16
Five critical measurements were performed for Fe-16, measurements 3A through 3E, which had a
moderator thickness of 0.125-inches and iron absorber thickness of 0.5-inches. Measurement 3B was the
first critical measurement and preliminarily selected as the benchmark measurement. Other measurements
performed included reproducibility, reactivity worth studies for diluent and fuel layer plate swaps. These
measurements are detailed further in Sections 2.6.1 through 2.6.5. Complete location information for all
PANN or aluminum surrogate plates are provided in Appendix B by experiment.

2.6.1 Measurement 3A
Measurement 3A contained 20 full layers of 24 PANN plates, with 10 full layers and 9 full layers on the
lower and upper half of Planet, respectively. The upper stack was reflected by a 1 and 3/4-inch top reflector
thickness and the lower stack was reflected by a 1-inch bottom reflector. 3A was performed at the close of
operations in the afternoon of January 13, and criticality was achieved by adding top reflector to the upper
stack. However, there were discussions held to assess if a asymmetric thick top reflector was desired over
a partial fuel layer in what would be the 21st layer. Between 3A and 3B, LLNL performed calculations to
assess if there was a change in neutron spectra by adding 3/4-inch to the top reflector. The results of the
calculations demonstrated that there was no significant change in the neutron spectrum by including
additional top reflector, and upon resuming operations on January 14, measurement 3B was performed.

2.6.5 Measurement 3E
The final measurement in Fe-16 and the TEX-Fe campaign, is measurement 3E, which studied the reactivity
worth of a fuel tray moving from an area of least reactivity importance to an area of higher importance
within the upper stack. This measurement could only be done in the upper stack because alignment between
the upper and lower stacks cannot be guaranteed without restacking the experiment. Additionally, the upper
most fuel layer could not be moved to the center of the stack because it contains an RTD. Therefore, the
fuel layer in unit 11, containing PANN ZPPR plates M1-20 and N1-4, were switched with the fuel tray in
unit 20, which contained PANN ZPPR plates W17-18, FFF2-3, and X1-20. Measurement details are
included in Table 2-62 through Table 2-64. Reactor period and estimated excess reactivity values, which
were calculated using the plutonium Inhour parameters in the LC program software, are presented as they
are shown in NCERC logbooks. Note that the diluent layer in measurement 3D was replaced with the
original diluent layer used in measurements 3A through 3C. Additionally, the stack details did not change
as described in earlier tables and only the PANN ZPPR plates in the trays changed, therefore stack details
are not restated here.

Section 3 Future Work
While this report documents the successful execution of the TEX-Fe experiments across 15 critical
measurements, additional work is required to complete the benchmark and reduce the overall benchmark
uncertainty. Remaining efforts include obtaining or measuring dimensional information for the
polyethylene moderators, top and bottom reflector parts, aluminum positioning plate, and the aluminum
membrane. Density and impurity information or additional information from the vendor on the certificate
of analysis on the polyethylene moderators and reflectors are needed to reduce uncertainty in variations of
the polyethylene composition. Lastly, mass measurements of bottom reflector and aluminum membrane
and positioning plate will assist in analytically determining density of these components and reduce the
associated uncertainty in these areas.
