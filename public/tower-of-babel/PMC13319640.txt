Tuberculosis death prediction calculator for prospective use at diagnosis in resource-constrained programme settings: a statewide cohort study

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop predictive models for early and overall tuberculosis (TB) deaths for prospective use at TB diagnosis in resource-constrained TB programme settings.DesignStatewide cohort study using routinely captured secondary data.SettingWith the majority of TB deaths being early (within 2 months), India’s TB programme’s information management system (Ni-kshay)-dependent death prediction models (using age, gender, TB site, previous treatment, microbiological confirmation, HIV, diabetes and bank account availability) are not feasible for prospective use, as few variables are captured at diagnosis. Utilising routinely captured triage variables for severe illness at diagnosis (body mass index, pedal oedema, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and ability to stand without support) from an ongoing statewide and state-specific differentiated TB care initiative to reduce TB deaths in Tamil Nadu state (southern India, 80 million population with 0.1 million annual notifications), robust models for prospective use were developed.ParticipantsAdults (aged ≥15 years) with TB (not known to be drug-resistant at diagnosis) that were notified from public facilities of Tamil Nadu from July 2022 to June 2023.Outcome measuresEarly and overall (within 12 months of notification) TB deaths. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used to assess accuracy of models built using modified Poisson regression.ResultsAmong 55 971 adults, the overall death rate was 7.4%, and 67.9% of the deaths were early. In predicting overall deaths, accuracy of the model using all Ni-kshay variables (AUC 0.716 (95% CI 0.707 to 0.725)) was as good as the model using triage variables for severe illness only (AUC 0.701 (95% CI 0.691 to 0.711)). To the latter, adding potentially capturable Ni-kshay variables at diagnosis (age, gender, TB site, previous treatment and microbiological confirmation) significantly improved model accuracy (AUC 0.754 (95% CI 0.745 to 0.763)). Further addition of remaining Ni-kshay variables did not improve accuracy significantly. Death prediction equations were generated for these models.ConclusionSimple and easily measurable triage variables for severe illness should be routinely captured at TB diagnosis. A death prediction calculator (http://44.208.93.99/) based on these variables (specifically triage variables for severe illness combined with age, gender, TB site, previous treatment and microbiological confirmation) may be used by Indian states and high TB burden countries seeking scalable, data-driven interventions to reduce TB deaths.Keywords: Differentiated TB care, Risk stratification, TB mortality, Predictive models, Triaging for severe illness, Operational Research

Introduction

WHO’s End TB Strategy provides a flexible framework for countries to reduce tuberculosis (TB)-related deaths (henceforth, referred to as TB deaths) by 95% by 2035.1 The progress towards reducing TB deaths could be accelerated by reducing TB incidence, reducing deaths among incident TB (early initiation of appropriate regimen, treatment adherence, severity assessment and comorbidity management), addressing social determinants of health and ensuring universal health coverage.2

In high TB burden countries, considering majority deaths are early (within 2 months of diagnosis, within 8 weeks of intensive phase),3–5 it is imperative to decide on the need for inpatient care (severity assessment) at TB diagnosis before linking the person with TB to a peripheral health facility for ambulatory directly observed treatment (differentiated TB care to reduce TB deaths). Clinical, laboratory and radiological capacities vary across facilities that diagnose TB. Thus, there is a need for death prediction models that are flexible in line with the available resources and capacity, using variables available at diagnosis in resource-constrained programme settings.3

Most TB death prediction models are either from clinical resourceful settings or focused on drug-resistant TB or HIV-associated TB, and they do not cater to drug-susceptible TB in non-HIV settings.6–10 Additionally, models using programme data utilise retrospective data on baseline characteristics, many of which are not capturable at TB diagnosis to take appropriate action.6 Hence, they are not prospectively implementable.

Tamil Nadu is a southern India state with ≈80 million population, ≈0.1 million annual TB notifications and 6.6% TB death rate among all people with TB notified from public facilities in 2021.11 Between April 2022 and December 2025, the state implemented a differentiated TB care initiative to reduce TB deaths called Tamil Nadu Kasanoi Erappila Thittam (TN-KET, meaning TB death-free project in Tamil).12–14 At TB diagnosis, a paper-based TN-KET triage tool captured five variables (body mass index (BMI), pedal oedema, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and ability to stand without support) to measure five indicators for identification of three conditions (very severe undernutrition, respiratory insufficiency and poor performance status) (Box 1).3 15 These variables are strong predictors of TB deaths,16–19 easy to collect at diagnosis in routine programme settings from primary to tertiary healthcare facilities (TB diagnosis centres)12–14 and were not captured in any other national TB programmes. These were captured in Severe TB Web Application (TB SeWA), the state-specific e-tool of TNKET.12–14 20 As a public health guidance, those with any of the three conditions (triage-positive) were prioritised for inpatient care. This is probably the first such model implemented statewide in routine healthcare settings.12–14

Ni-kshay (India TB programme’s information management system)-dependent death prediction models may not be feasible for prospective use, as few variables are captured at diagnosis. Availability of triage variables for severe illness at diagnosis in Tamil Nadu provided the opportunity to develop robust and useful death prediction models.14

This study aimed to develop predictive models (applicable at TB diagnosis) for early and overall TB deaths using triage variables from TB SeWA and routinely capturable baseline variables from Ni-kshay. Specific objectives were to (i) assess the improvement in the accuracy of Ni-kshay variables-based death prediction model after incorporating the triage variables and (ii) compare four prediction models (along with their accuracy estimates) in four hypothetical scenarios in line with the availability of resources and capacity at TB diagnosis centres in routine programme settings.

Methods

Study design

This study utilised a cohort design, drawing on secondary data from Ni-kshay and TB SeWA.

Setting

Tamil Nadu national TB elimination programme infrastructure includes 35 districts (30 excluding Chennai), ≈460 subdistrict-level TB units and ≈5250 peripheral health institutions (PHIs), of which ≈2780 are in public sector. Paper-based registers maintained at TB units record notifications of people with TB, management details and treatment outcomes. These details are updated in Ni-kshay by the senior TB treatment supervisor of each TB unit. It is standard practice for the diagnosing TB unit to notify at diagnosis and refer the person with TB to the PHI closest to the residence for ambulatory directly observed treatment. In the current TB unit (where the person is taking treatment), the senior TB treatment supervisor transcribes most of the baseline variables into Ni-kshay, resulting in an induced/ unavoidable delay in capturing baseline variables.

Participants

TN-KET focused on adult (≥15 years) public-notified TB (not known to be drug-resistant at diagnosis) in 30 TB districts of Tamil Nadu (all districts except Chennai, the capital city).14 21 In this study, those notified between July 2022 and June 2023 were included. As per programme guidelines, children (<15 years) with presumptive TB are referred to paediatrician for further workup. People with TB who are found to be drug-resistant are routinely referred for further pre-treatment evaluation to pulmonologists in tertiary care centres. Therefore, these two categories of people with TB were not included under TN-KET.14 21

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Stata (V.12.1, StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA) for the descriptive and analytical components, while R (V.2024.12.1+563) was used for constructing the receiver operating characteristic plots. The authors had access to all the variables from Ni-kshay and TB SeWA that were required for analysis.

Programme-reported early (within 2 months) and overall (routinely reported maximum within 12 months in Ni-kshay) TB deaths were the outcomes. Death was defined as TB-related death (TB may or may not be the underlying cause of death) occurring anytime from notification to outcome declaration (maximum 12 months from notification).22

Given the binary nature of the outcome variable, modified Poisson regression (enter method) with robust variance estimates was used.23 Variables with crude p<0.20 were included in the regression models. Continuous variables (age, BMI, respiratory rate and oxygen saturation) were retained as such in the models without categorisation. For missing continuous variables or implausible outliers in continuous variables, imputation was performed using mean values from the dataset. For handling missing data in categorical variables, separate ‘missing’ categories were retained if the extent of missingness was relatively high (Ni-kshay variables) and imputed as mode if the extent of missingness was relatively low (triage variables).

Modified Poisson regression models were built for each of the four scenarios (models 1–4). Within each scenario, four outcomes were considered: early death, overall death and their respective sensitivity analyses. Sensitivity analysis assumed all programme-reported pre-treatment and during-treatment loss to follow-up (not taking treatment consecutively for 2 months) as TB deaths.22 The predictive performance of each model was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). To ensure statistical rigour beyond overlapping 95% CIs, DeLong’s test was employed for formal pairwise comparisons of the AUCs. The primary analysis employed modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimates to determine the factors associated with mortality, reporting results as risk ratios (RR) with 95% CIs. Although modified Poisson regression was used to estimate RR, individual predicted probabilities were derived using the logit transformation. This approach was adopted under the assumption that for outcomes with relatively low frequency, the RR provides a robust approximation for risk estimation, whereas the logit link ensures clinically interpretable probabilities constrained between 0 and 1. Beta coefficients were utilised for creating prediction equations, with the linear predictor (η) as the output. The probability of TB deaths was then calculated using the formula exp(η) / [1+exp(η)].

To ensure the prediction equations remain applicable for prospective use in routine programmatic settings, we adopted a pragmatic approach to handling missing input values. If one or more predictor variables are missing at the time of prospective use at TB diagnosis, continuous variables will get imputed using their mean values and categorical variables using their mode. The pattern of missingness was assessed using Little’s missing completely at random test for the variables respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and BMI. The test yielded χ²(7) = 95.46, p<0.001, indicating that the data were not missing completely at random. The proportion of missing data was low across these variables, <10% for each (for those who were triaged). After correcting data entry errors (replacing 0 and 99), the remaining missing data were handled using mean imputation. This strategy avoids excluding variables from the prediction process and helps maintain the accuracy and reliability of the prediction when deployed by health staff in real-world conditions.24 For model 1, missing programmatic data were retained as a separate category, and no imputation was performed. For models 2–4, a sensitivity analysis using complete case analysis (CCA) was conducted to assess the robustness of the mean/mode imputation strategy. Model performance (AUC) from the CCA was compared against the primary models to ensure consistency (online supplemental table S19).

Results

Of a total of 55 971 study participants notified, 82.9% (n=46 417) were triaged at diagnosis. The overall death rate was 7.4% (n=4141), with an early death rate of 5.0% (n=2810, 67.9% of overall deaths). The overall death rate based on triage results was as follows: 19.8% (1124/5681) among triage-positive (severely ill), 5.1% (2074/40,736) among triage-negative (not severely ill based on triaging) and 8.8% (843/9554) among not triaged.

Improvement in model 1 after incorporating variables used for triaging

For early deaths, model 1 demonstrated AUC of 0.732 (95% CI 0.721 to 0.742), which significantly increased to 0.787 (95% CI 0.778 to 0.797) when triage variables were added. For overall deaths, model 1 demonstrated AUC of 0.716 (95% CI 0.707 to 0.725), which also significantly increased to 0.767 (95% CI 0.759 to 0.776) when triage variables were added (online supplemental figure S1). The results were consistent after the sensitivity analyses (table 3).

Comparing all four models

Model 2 achieved an AUC of 0.715 (95% CI 0.703 to 0.726) for early deaths and 0.701 (95% CI 0.691 to 0.711) for overall deaths. Its predictive performance was not significantly different from model 1. When compared model 2, model 3 had significantly better predictive performance: AUC of 0.772 (95% CI 0.762 to 0.783) for early deaths and 0.754 (95% CI 0.745 to 0.763) for overall deaths. When compared with model 3, model 4 did not have significantly better predictive performance: AUC of 0.787 (95% CI 0.778 to 0.797) for early deaths and 0.767 (95% CI 0.759 to 0.776) for overall deaths (online supplemental figure S1). Pairwise comparisons using DeLong’s test demonstrated that the discriminative accuracy (AUC) of models 1–4 differed significantly from one another (p<0.001).

Furthermore, sensitivity analysis using CCA confirmed the robustness of our missing data handling strategy; the predictive accuracy across all models remained consistent with the primary findings (online supplemental table S19), suggesting that the pragmatic mean/mode imputation did not introduce significant bias. Beyond AUC, comprehensive performance metrics including sensitivity, specificity and accuracy for all models across both early and overall mortality outcomes are detailed in online supplemental table S20).

Discussion

Key findings

This is the first study to assess the predictive utility of BMI, pedal oedema, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and ability to stand without support (recorded at diagnosis in Tamil Nadu, southern India) for TB deaths in routine programmatic settings. There were three key findings. First, the addition of these triage variables to a TB death prediction model based on all baseline variables captured by India’s TB programme (Ni-kshay) significantly improved the prediction accuracy. Second, by themselves, the accuracy of TB death prediction based on triage variables was similar to that based on all baseline variables captured by India’s TB programme. Third, the addition of potentially capturable variables at diagnosis from Ni-kshay significantly improved the death prediction based on triage variables only. However, further addition of variables from Ni-kshay did not improve accuracy.

Strengths and limitations

The analysed cohort is highly representative of the adult drug-susceptible TB population seeking care in the public sector across Tamil Nadu. This is supported by the statewide coverage of 30 districts, a large sample size of 55 971 participants and a high triaging rate of 82.9% within routine programme settings.

Considering 70% of deaths were early, assessing three scenarios (models 2–4) at diagnosis in line with the availability of resources and capacity makes it suitable for real-world application. A recent systematic review highlighted that most TB outcome prediction models were developed using retrospective clinical data, with limited external validity and a lack of integration into routine public health programmes.6 The AUC values in this study were also comparable with those reported in the review, demonstrating strong discriminatory power and suggesting that programme-linked prediction models can be both scalable and effective in real-world settings.6

There were four limitations. First, 17% of adults with TB who were not triaged were excluded from the prediction models. When compared with the triaged group, previous analysis has shown that those not triaged were more likely to have extrapulmonary TB, diagnosed in high notification districts, diagnosed in teaching hospitals and transferred out of the district.14 Second, these prediction models can be used only at the time of diagnosis and cannot be used later for predicting TB deaths. Third, the use of routine programme data introduces potential errors or misclassifications. We see no reasons for differential errors in data capture of triage and Ni-kshay variables among those who experience and do not experience death. The results were consistent after the sensitivity analysis assuming all programme-reported pre-treatment and during-treatment losses (not taking treatment consecutively for 2 months) as TB deaths. Hence, we do not expect the misclassification of any of the independent variables to be differential.

Finally, lack of external validation is another limitation. As this is the first statewide programme to capture these specific triage variables at diagnosis, no independent dataset was available for comparison. Future studies should evaluate the performance of these models in different regional contexts to ensure wider generalisability.

Policy and practice relevance

The prediction equations are intended to be integrated into digital TB surveillance systems (such as Ni-kshay or TB SeWA), and it has been designed for use by frontline health staff, such as senior treatment supervisors, without requiring advanced clinical or statistical expertise. In addition, a web-based death prediction calculator has been developed to allow any health staff to easily access all 4 models and estimate the predicted probability of TB death (http://44.208.93.99/). It is free to use after appropriate training.25

Even with triage variables used in TN-KET, the death predictive performance was good and comparable to all baseline variables in Ni-kshay. In Tamil Nadu, triage variables are routinely captured (and ongoing) at TB diagnosis within a day.14 These are easy to measure and infer in routine programme settings.14 15 26 Conversely, all baseline variables of Ni-kshay are not feasible to capture at diagnosis. The five triage variables, namely BMI, pedal oedema, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and ability to stand without support, should be included as baseline variables at TB diagnosis by national TB programmes and acted on before sending the person to a PHI for ambulatory treatment (as done in TN-KET).12–14

Even though the predictive performance of triage variables was good, the addition of potentially capturable baseline variables from Ni-kshay at diagnosis significantly improved the accuracy of prediction. However, incorporating other Ni-kshay variables that are potentially not capturable at the time of diagnosis did not substantially improve the model. The addition of variables not immediately capturable at diagnosis adds complexity without meaningful benefit; these may be deprioritised for triaging and prediction purposes. A simple, triage variables-based model supported by some variables from Ni-kshay may thus be sufficient for prospective real-time implementation. If there is administrative will to capture potentially capturable variables from Ni-kshay at diagnosis (and not in PHI where treatment is provided), then model 3 can be considered in Ni-kshay if it also captures triage variables or in TB SeWA if both e-platforms ‘talk’ to each other through application programming interface integration. Incorporating a machine learning model to transform these static models into a dynamic one will facilitate continuous refinement of the prediction on a regular basis as new cohort data becomes available. Until this happens, the web-based death prediction calculator (http://44.208.93.99/) may be used.25

Conclusion

BMI, pedal oedema, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and ability to stand without support are not routinely captured by TB programmes. The availability of these five triage variables related to severe illness at TB diagnosis in Tamil Nadu allowed us to generate robust and feasible TB death prediction models. Other states in India and high TB burden countries should also consider including these variables in their existing TB information management systems. These triage variables with (model 3) or without (model 2) other potentially capturable variables at diagnosis (age, gender, TB site, previous treatment and microbiological confirmation) should be used to predict deaths. These models are flexible in line with the available resources and capacity and can therefore be applied in programme settings. The integration of model 2 or 3 into existing digital platforms like TB SeWA or Ni-kshay hold the potential for real-time use at diagnosis, enabling faster decision-making (referral of severely ill for inpatient care) to reduce TB deaths. Until the integration happens, model 3 in web-based death prediction calculator may be used by health staff after appropriate training.
