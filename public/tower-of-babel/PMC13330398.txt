Effect of pH and viscosity of two different iron supplements on enamel discoloration in permanent teeth: an in vitro study
Authors: Konain Khalid, Maham Muneeb Lone, Syed Yawar Ali Abidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIron supplements are used as a common remedy to treat iron deficiency for years. Despite widespread research of iron on primary teeth discoloration, their esthetic side effect on permanent teeth remains underexplored. The objective of this study was to assess the effect of different viscosity and pH of two commonly used iron supplements on enamel discoloration of extracted permanent teeth.MethodsSixty extracted single-rooted permanent teeth were divided into three groups: Ferosoft (pH-5.25, viscosity 0.44 cP), Maltofer (pH- 4.47, viscosity 0.07 cP), and control (artificial saliva). Teeth were immersed in the respective solutions for 2 weeks, with the solutions replaced after every 48 h to replenish iron. Tooth shade was recorded at baseline, after one week, and after two weeks using the VITA classical shade guide. Mean color change was calculated by comparing baseline shade with shade after one and two weeks of exposure for each group. ANOVA and Tukey tests were used for statistical analysis after data entry into SPSS v.23.ResultsBoth Ferosoft and Maltofer caused significant enamel discoloration compared to the control group (p < 0.05). Maltofer showed the highest mean color change over time, though the difference between Ferosoft and Maltofer was not statistically significant.ConclusionsIron supplements cause significant enamel discoloration that can be esthetically displeasing. The greater staining potential of Maltofer is likely due to its lower pH and viscosity. Results of this study reiterate the importance of limiting the use of iron supplements to minimize the esthetic complications as a result of their use.

Background

Iron deficiency is considered as one of the most common nutritional deficiency in the world, affecting about 30% of people worldwide; including children, women of reproductive age, individuals with chronic illnesses, elderly and patients with increased physiological or pathological iron demands [1–3]. Primary causes of iron deficiency include inadequate dietary intake, impaired absorption, increased requirements, heavy menstrual bleeding, and chronic gastrointestinal blood loss. Clinically, patients can present with fatigue, lethargy, dyspnea, headache and cognitive dysfunction [4]. Due to its high prevalence and systemic effects, prevention of iron deficiency remains a major public health concern, usually managed through dietary modifications, food fortification, and iron supplementation [5, 6].

Extrinsic tooth discoloration mainly results from the deposition of chromogenic substances on the enamel surface, influenced by chemical and physical properties of oral liquids being ingested. Alteration in the color of external tooth surface is a common issue with intake of iron drops [7, 8]. Discoloration due to iron occurs as a result of iron ions reacting with the organic and inorganic components of enamel. Interaction of oxidized ferric ions with bacterially produced sulphide ions results in a blackish surface discoloration due to formation of ferric sulphide. Additionally, these ions bind to the acquired pellicle and enamel matrix proteins, contributing to the extrinsic staining. These mechanisms highlight the chemical reactions and surface interactions that result in the development of iron-induced tooth discoloration [9].

Dental erosion is a process that causes abrasion and attrition by softening of the enamel surface by non-bacterial acids. Major sources of acid include diet, certain medications and some occupational exposure [10]. Medications with acidic pH directly interact with the enamel surface, making it susceptible to erosion [11]. Decrease in pH caused by exposure to these medications can unfavorably erode and increase the staining potential of the external and internal tooth surfaces [12]. A low pH not only promotes enamel demineralization, but also increases the surface roughness, thereby escalating adsorption and retention of chromogenic iron compounds. This chemical modification of enamel contributes significantly to the initiation and progression of tooth discoloration. Thus, pH plays a critical role in altering enamel integrity and facilitating stain adherence. There have been a number of studies that have also highlighted the role of viscosity of liquids on their absorption. The rate of oral clearance depends on the relative consistency of the medicine [13, 14]. Liquids with higher viscosity have a slower rate of clearance and will therefore remain in contact with the enamel for a longer period of time, increasing their potential to interact with the tooth surface.

A study conducted by Babaei et al. on four different groups of iron drops reported increased enamel discoloration with reduction of pH of iron drops. The mean color change of iron supplement with higher pH was 8.58 ± 4.15 while iron supplement with a lower pH showed a color change of 12.43 ± 4.51 [13]. Another study conducted in 2019 on primary teeth reported that color change of the primary teeth following immersion in iron supplements with closer pH values did not have a significantly different color change (12.679 ± 3.20 and 12.90 ± 2.23) in low and high pH respectively [8].

There is considerable evidence in the literature regarding the effect of iron supplements on primary tooth discoloration. However, a gap in knowledge exists in published literature of how variations in pH and viscosity of iron supplements influence the color of permanent tooth surfaces. Results of this study will serve as foundation to establish the effect of different iron supplements on the color stability of permanent teeth. It will help to identify which iron supplement causes less discoloration, and thus can then be prescribed in cases of iron deficiency safely.

Methods

This in-vitro experimental study was conducted in the Department of Operative Dentistry and Endodontics at SIOHS, Jinnah Sindh Medical University, over a duration of six months (January- June 2025) after the approval of the institutional IRB (Ref # JSMU/IRB/2024/857). The sample size was calculated using OpenEpi v. 3.01. Based on a study by Babaei et al., the mean color change of enamel in iron supplement with higher pH was 8.58 ± 4.15, while enamel exposed to iron supplement with a lower pH showed a mean color change of 12.43 ± 4.51. Using these values as reference, confidence level of 95% and power of 80%, the calculated sample size was 20 per group. Since the study involved two experimental groups and one control group, the total sample size was 60 teeth.

Data collection was initiated after the institutional ethical review board approval. To confirm that the two iron supplements selected for the study had distinct pH levels and viscosities, these properties were measured in advance. The viscosity of the supplements was assessed at 25 °C using Ostwald’s U-tube viscometer, while the pH was determined using a pen pH meter. The results are shown in Table 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were established prior to sample selection. Human permanent single-rooted teeth with intact enamel and no visible structural defects were included in the study. Teeth with developmental defects, cracks, fractures, previous root canal treatment, or enamel abnormalities were excluded. Sixty teeth meeting the inclusion criteria were selected through non-probability convenience sampling from the tooth bank maintained at Jinnah Sindh Medical University. All teeth included in this study were already indicated for extraction for orthodontic or periodontal purposes. No tooth was extracted solely for the purpose of this study.

The teeth were then divided by simple random sampling into three groups as follows:Group A: 250 ml artificial saliva and 100 mg of Ferosoft syrup (Hilton Pharma (Pvt.) Ltd., Korangi Industrial Area, Karachi, Pakistan).Group B: 250 ml of artificial saliva and 100 mg of Maltofer syrup (Searle Company Ltd., Multan Road, Lahore, Pakistan).Group C: 250 ml of artificial saliva.

Group A: 250 ml artificial saliva and 100 mg of Ferosoft syrup (Hilton Pharma (Pvt.) Ltd., Korangi Industrial Area, Karachi, Pakistan).

Group B: 250 ml of artificial saliva and 100 mg of Maltofer syrup (Searle Company Ltd., Multan Road, Lahore, Pakistan).

Group C: 250 ml of artificial saliva.

The teeth were mounted in dental gypsum, leaving the crown portions exposed and numbered from 1 to 60. They were then stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h.

After 24 h, the teeth were immersed in their respective solutions. Solutions in each group were changed after every 48 h to replenish the iron. Three readings were recorded using the VITA classic shade guide for each sample. The enamel shade of each sample was measured and recorded in the data collection form using a VITA classic shade guide (VITAPAN classical, Germany). All shade assessments were conducted in natural daylight at a fixed time of the day under similar lightning conditions with observation limited to no more than 3–4 s per reading to reduce eye fatigue and any variability in shade evaluation. All other shade matching guidelines were adhered to so as to record the correct shade. Shade was measured by arranging the VITA shade guide (Fig. 1) in order from the lightest to darkest color. The lightest color ( value ) was numbered 01 and the darkest was numbered 16. The first reading was recorded at baseline- before the teeth were exposed to any supplement. The 2nd reading was recorded at 7th day after the teeth were immersed in the solutions. The 3rd reading was recorded at 14th day after immersion in the solutions. A single operator (KK) performed all the measurements.

As the lightest shade was numbered 01 and the darkest numbered 16, the mean color change was recorded as the difference of shade from the baseline to the change in shade after one week and two weeks of exposure for all the groups.

Data was entered and analyzed using SPSS v. 23. Frequency (%) of type of tooth and color change were recorded. The tooth color at baseline, 1 week and 2 weeks and the color change of tooth after exposure were recorded as mean ± SD. Kolmogorov Smirnov test was applied to check for normality of data. Repeated measures ANOVA test was applied to assess the difference in progression of discoloration in the control and experimental groups. To assess the difference in color change among the study groups, one-way ANOVA and post-hoc Tukey test was applied. Level of significance was set at p-value ≤ 0.05.

Results

This study was conducted on 60 single rooted permanent teeth to evaluate the enamel discoloration caused by two different iron supplements with different pH and viscosities. Out of the total 60 single rooted-teeth used in the study, there were 30 (50%) mandibular and 30 (50%) maxillary teeth. Teeth included in the study were 29 (48.3%) premolars, 12 (20.0%) canines, 13 (21.7%) lateral incisors, and 6 (10.0%) central incisors. Enamel discoloration was seen in all the 40 (66.6%) teeth that were immersed in iron supplements, while 20 (33.3%) teeth immersed in the control group showed no color change.

The baseline color of teeth in the three groups before any intervention was similar (p > 0.05). Teeth exposed to Ferosoft (group A) showed significant color change after one week (p = 0.002) and two weeks of immersion (p = 0.000) on both the buccal and lingual surfaces. Teeth exposed to Maltofer (Group B) showed a higher and a statistically significant discoloration after both one week and two week of supplement exposure (p = 0.000) on the buccal as well as the lingual surfaces (Tables 2 and 3). Discussion

Iron deficiency is a major public health concern, especially in developing countries like Pakistan. The steadily increasing number of cases, particularly among children and individuals with certain conditions such as pregnancy and blood disorders, highlights the need for effective management through iron supplementation [3]. Iron deficiency often leads to various systemic and oral symptoms. Systemically, it can lead to mental retardation, cardiac issues, chronic fatigue, emotional disturbance and mood swings. Intra orally, it may present as mucosal pallor, glossodynia, angular cheilitis, ulcers, fungal infections or persistent burning sensation in the oral cavity [17]. Timely diagnosis and treatment of iron deficiency is therefore necessary to prevent iron deficiency anaemia and its associated signs and symptoms [18].

Iron salts are often prescribed in the form of tablets, suspensions or intravenous preparations. While intravenous iron therapy is most commonly preferred to cure iron deficiency anaemia in cancer patients [19], long-term, high-dose iron suspension syrups are recommended to improve anaemia in other conditions [16]. These syrups typically contain ferrous sulphate or ferric hydroxide polymaltose complex along with other essential multivitamins and minerals. One major concern when using these supplements is their adverse effect on dental esthetics. Beyond simple adsorption, iron also undergoes chelation with enamel protein and the acquired pellicle, forming iron protein complexes that have strong retentive ability onto the enamel surface. Moreover, the redox behavior of iron enhances its surface deposition within micro porosities of demineralized enamel. These chemical interactions are modulated by environmental factors including pH changes and bacterial metabolites, that ultimately result in persistent and clinically evident discoloration [20]. It is important that medical practitioners recognize this side effect and counsel patients regarding the risk of tooth discoloration, especially with long term use of liquid iron formulations. Where clinically feasible, tablet or capsule forms should be preferred, as they minimize direct contact of enamel to staining agents. While considerable research has been conducted on the impact of iron supplements on primary teeth, there are only a limited number of studies that have studied their effect on permanent teeth [8].

This in-vitro study was conducted to evaluate the color change of single rooted permanent teeth when immersed in two different iron supplements i.e. Ferosoft and Maltofer. These iron supplements differ on the basis of their pH and viscosity. Maltofer has a lower pH and viscosity compared to Ferosoft, despite having a similar composition of iron i.e. iron polymaltose. The results demonstrate that both supplements significantly discolored the buccal and lingual surfaces of teeth over time as compared to artificial saliva, which confirms the hypothesis that iron containing supplements contribute to tooth staining. From a clinical perspective, patients taking such supplements should be advised to avoid direct contact with teeth, for example by using a dropper and rinsing their mouth immediately after intake to minimize the risk of staining.

Even though no significant difference in discoloration was found between the two experimental groups after two weeks, Maltofer displayed a consistently higher mean difference in tooth discoloration. This can be explained due to the lower pH and viscosity of Maltofer. These results are similar to the study by Babaei et al. that reported an increase in enamel discoloration when exposed to four different iron drops with varying pH [13]. In comparison, a study on primary teeth by Tayebi et al. reported that color change of teeth following immersion in iron supplements with differing pH values was not significantly different [8]. The pH of a solution can be a major contributor to its erosive potential on enamel surfaces. Medication with lower pH, especially those with extended-release formulation significantly increase the risk of enamel demineralization, subsequently increasing the surface roughness and ultimately tooth discoloration [21].

Chemically, acidic environments promote partial dissolution of hydroxyapatite, exposing interprismatic regions that facilitate penetration and binding of iron ions, thereby enhancing discoloration. As iron supplements with lower pH tend to cause more severe discoloration, it indicates a pH dependent staining mechanism, highlighting the importance of selecting less acidic formulations when clinically feasible [22], along with adopting preventive strategies such as maintaining good oral hygiene and scheduling periodic professional polishing may help in controlling extrinsic staining.

In a study by Tayebi et al. [8], the effect of four iron preparations on color stability of primary teeth was analyzed. All four iron supplements caused noticeable discoloration at 96 h, however the extent of discoloration with vitamin C-fortified supplements, Ferrodrop (ΔE = 13.41) and Feroglobin (ΔE = 13.30) was highest, whereas Ferbolin, lacking vitamin C, showed less discoloration (ΔE = 12.68). Notably, there was no significant difference in between the discoloration potential based on differing pH values of these supplements. In contrast, our study conducted on permanent teeth found a statistically significant difference in discoloration with change in pH of supplements.

A systematic review conducted by Asgari I et al. in 2020 emphasized that the level of acidic nature of supplements contributes most to increased enamel porosity and staining [7]. Acidic suspension forms such as ferrous sulfate and ferric chloride were found to have the highest staining potential. These formulations enhance ionic availability of iron, thereby increasing the likelihood of chemical reactions with enamel constituents and accelerating the staining process. The present study also reports similar results and highlights the impact of viscosity on the staining potential of iron supplements on permanent teeth.

Similar to our study, Babaei et al. [13], also assessed discoloration by five pediatric iron supplements of varying pH and viscosities. In their study, Ferbolin, with the lowest pH but highest viscosity caused the greatest discoloration after two weeks (ΔE- 22.11 ± 5.34). In the present study on permanent teeth, supplement with lower viscosity and pH caused more discoloration after 2 weeks (ΔE- 6.35 ± 1.134), posing the highest risk to enamel esthetics. The present study confirmed that lower pH and higher viscosity act as key contributors to tooth discoloration even in permanent teeth.

While prescribing iron supplements to manage iron deficiency, clinicians should be aware of the potential esthetic side effects of certain formulations. This awareness might affect the decision of recommending less discoloring alternatives, supplements in tablet form as well as reinforcement and maintenance of effective oral hygiene practices. Applying surface enamel coating, rinsing or brushing more often with proper education and guidance along with professional dental polishing at follow-up appointments are recommended measures to control extrinsic staining [20, 23].

Even though shade matching was carried out using VITA shade guide that is considered subjective, measures were taken to minimize its subjectivity by minimizing observer’s eye fatigue, using controlled similar light conditions and all readings done by the same observer. However, advanced colorimeters that are able to quantify small, incremental changes in L*, a*, and b* values that occur during the early stages of staining could not be used to objectively quantify color change because of lack of availability. The present study also did not simulate the oral environment i.e. salivary flow, diet, and brushing as it was conducted in in-vitro settings. Future studies can be done to explore the effects of iron supplements under real life settings through in-vivo trials, and with objective measurements using spectrophotometers. Additional studies may also be planned to visualize structural alteration and discolorations at the microscopic level using scanning electron microscopy.

Conclusion

Irrespective of the physicochemical constitution of iron supplements, significant enamel discoloration is observed by their use. Iron supplement with lower pH and viscosity exhibits more tooth discoloration than those with higher pH. These results emphasize the importance of product selection and patient counselling during iron therapy to minimize their esthetic effects on the tooth structure.
