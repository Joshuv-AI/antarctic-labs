H3K18 lactylation promotes allergic airway inflammation in asthma via a CSF1/CSF1R/MAPK autocrine axis
Authors: Minxuan Hu, Yixin Chen, Haohua Huang, Jinzhong Zhuo, Haishan Zhong, Dongyu Liu, Qi Yu, Yuhan Du, Jinming Zhang, Shaoxi Cai, Hangming Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic reprogramming of airway epithelial cells is a hallmark of asthma, yet the mechanisms by which altered metabolism drives inflammation remain largely unknown. Introduction

Asthma is a heterogeneous chronic inflammatory airway disease characterized by airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) and persistent airway inflammation [1, 2]. Accumulating evidence suggests that the airway epithelium, composed of ciliated, goblet, and basal cells, serves as a key regulator of immune responses upon allergen exposure [3, 4]. As the first line of defense, epithelial cells sense environmental insults and releases alarmins such as TSLP, IL-25, and IL-33 to initiate type 2 inflammation [5–7]. Given their pivotal role in initiating and sustaining airway inflammation, targeting epithelial-driven inflammatory signaling has emerged as a potential anti-inflammatory strategy.

Cellular metabolism dynamically adapts to environmental changes to meet bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands [8]. Enhanced glycolysis, commonly referred to as the Warburg effect, is increasingly recognized in allergic and inflammatory diseases [9]. In asthma, elevated serum and sputum lactate levels were negatively correlate with %FEV [10, 11]. Inhibition of glycolysis effectively attenuates airway inflammation in asthmatic murine models [10]. Consistently, enhanced glycolysis in airway epithelial cells is closely associated with the secretion of IL-25 and IL-33 [12]. Despite these insights, the precise mechanisms by which glycolysis modulates inflammatory responses in epithelial cells remain to be fully explored.

Recent studies have revealed that metabolic intermediates serve as substrates for histone modification, establishing a direct connection between cellular metabolism and epigenetic regulation [13]. Histone lactylation, a newly identified post-translational modification derived from lactate, has drawn increasing attention for its role in disease and potential as a therapeutic target [14, 15]. Although Chen et al. [16] reported that the HIF-1α–glycolysis–lactylation axis in macrophages contributes to allergic airway inflammation, whether histone lactylation occurs in airway epithelial cells and how it influences epithelial-driven inflammatory responses remain unknown.

Colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R), a receptor tyrosine kinase primarily expressed in myeloid cells [17], plays essential roles in macrophage activation and alveolar DCs recruitment during allergic inflammation [18, 19]. Notably, airway epithelial cells secrete the CSF1R ligand CSF1 following allergen exposure, thereby recruiting CSF1R⁺ immune cells and subsequently exacerbates allergic airway inflammation [20]. However, whether epithelial cells express functional CSF1R themselves and engage in CSF1-mediated inflammatory signaling remains to be elucidated.

Here, we identify histone lactylation as a critical epigenetic mechanism linking glycolysis to airway inflammation. We demonstrate that enhanced glycolysis in airway epithelial cells elevates lactate production, which induces histone lactylation and subsequently promotes the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes, including CSF1R. The resulting epithelial CSF1/CSF1R signaling further activates MAPK pathway and amplifies inflammatory cytokine expression. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized metabolic–epigenetic mechanism underlying asthma pathogenesis and highlight the glycolysis–H3K18la–CSF1R axis as a potential therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

The following antibodies were used : Pan-Kla (PTM-1401, PTM Bio), H3K18la (PTM-1406, PTM Bio), histone H3 (9715 S, Cell Signaling Technology), HK2 (22029-1-AP, Proteintech), PKM2 (15822-1-AP, Proteintech), LDHA (19987-1-AP, Proteintech), β-Actin (60008-1-Ig, Proteintech), EP300 (20695-1-AP, Proteintech), ERK1/2 (11257-1-AP, Proteintech), phospho-ERK1/2 (28733-1-AP, Proteintech), P38 MAPK (14064-1-AP, Proteintech), phospho-p38 MAPK (28796-1-AP, Proteintech), α-Tubulin (11224-1-AP, Proteintech), and CSF1R (A3019, ABclonal).

The reagents used included the glycolysis inhibitor 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) (HY-13966, MCE) and sodium oxamate (S6871, Selleck), lactate (HY-B2227B, MCE), CSF1R inhibitor GW2580 (HY-10917, MCE) and recombinant human M-CSF (CSF1) (HY-P7050, MCE). House dust mite (HDM) extracts were purchased from ALK-Abello A/S.

Human participants

Asthma patients (n = 12) and healthy controls (n = 12) were recruited from Nanfang Hospital (Guangzhou, China) between February 2023 and February 2025. Asthma was diagnosed according to the 2024 Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) and the Guidelines for Bronchial Asthma Prevention and Management (2024 edition). Exclusion criteria included: (1) coexisting other respiratory diseases, such as interstitial lung disease or bronchiectasis; (2) use of oral or systemic corticosteroids or immunotherapy within the past week; (3) severe comorbidities involving major organ systems, including severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases, or malignancies; (4) pregnancy or lactation; and (5) severe mental disorders or critical illness impairing cooperation with pulmonary function testing. Clinical information is presented in Supplementary Table S1. Peripheral blood was collected from asthma patients and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The serum supernatant was carefully separated, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent measurement of lactate and CSF1 levels. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital (NFEC-2023-215), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Cell culture and treatment

The human bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B (ATCC, USA) was cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Procell, China) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. For experimental treatments, cells were subjected to the following conditions: (1) exposure to HDM (200, 400, or 800 U/L) for 12, 24, or 48 h; (2) pretreatment with 2-DG (10 mM, 2 h) or sodium oxamate (10 mM, 12 h) before HDM (800 U/L, 24 h) stimulation; (3) transfection with P300-targeting siRNA for 12 h, followed by HDM (800 U/L, 24 h) treatment; and (4) treatment with lactate (10 mM), GW2580 (1 µM) or recombinant human M-CSF (CSF1) (50 ng/mL) for 24 h, alone or in combination with HDM.

Animals and experiment design

C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks) were maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility and provided with sterilized water and food. In the control group, the mice were exposed to PBS. In the asthma group, mice were sensitized with an intraperitoneal injection of 0.1 mL PBS containing HDM (4000 U/mouse) on days 0 and 7, followed by intranasal HDM challenge (0.02 mL PBS, 800 U/mouse) every other day from days 8 to 21. In the HDM + 2-DG group, mice received intraperitoneal 2-DG (1 g/kg in 0.01 mL PBS) 1 h prior to each HDM challenge. In the HDM + GW2580 group, mice were administered GW2580 (10 mg/kg in 0.01 mL PBS) intraperitoneally before each HDM challenge. Mice were sacrificed 24 h after the final HDM challenge. All animal experiments followed previously published protocols [21].

Single‑cell sequencing data acquisition, integration, and processing

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were obtained from the GEO public database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), including the GSE164015 for asthma patient, the GSE130148 and GSE136831 datasets for healthy lung tissues. A total of 107,969 cells from 8 asthma patients and 38 healthy individuals were included for analysis. Cells were filtered based on quality-control criteria (nFeature_RNA > 200, nFeature_RNA < 2,500, and percent.mt < 25), retaining only high-quality cells. To minimize batch effects across different datasets and sequencing platforms, the Harmony (version 1.2.0) integration algorithm was applied. Clustering analysis was performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions in Seurat (version 5.0.3), and no significant batch-associated differences were observed in UMAP plots. Cell populations were annotated based on marker gene expression.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between asthmatic and healthy airway epithelial cells were identified using the “FindMarkers” function in the Seurat R package. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.10.1). The pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed using Monocle (version 2.30.1). Metabolic pathway activation scores were calculated using the “AddModuleScore” function in Seurat.

Lactate content measurement

Lactate levels were determined using a lactate colorimetric assay kit (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For extracellular lactate measurement, cell culture media were collected and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were used for lactate analysis. To assess intracellular lactate, cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (KeyGEN Biotech, China) and the resulting lysates were subjected to lactate measurement.

Protein extraction and Western Blot

Cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer containing 1% PMSF and 1% phosphatase inhibitor (KeyGEN Biotech, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, German). After blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies and then fluorescent secondary antibodies. Fluorescence signals were analyzed using the Odyssey Infrared Image System (LI-COR Biosciences, USA).

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent and reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the Evo M-MLV RT premix (Accurate Biology, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was conducted on a QuantStudio 6 Flex system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR kit (Accurate Biology, China). Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCT method, with appropriate internal controls.

RNA interference

BEAS-2B cells were transfected with siRNA using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 12 h, followed by stimulation with HDM for subsequent experiments. For assessment of transfection efficiency, cells were harvested after 12 h of siRNA transfection and subjected to Western blot analysis. The siRNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Pathological staining and Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Lung tissues were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning into 4 μm-thick slices. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), periodic acid–Schiff (PAS), or Masson’s trichrome to evaluate inflammatory cell infiltration, mucus production, and airway remodeling, respectively. For immunohistochemistry, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval using a Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.9). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using hydrogen peroxide for 20 min. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against Pan-Kla, then incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 min. Signals were visualized using a DAB substrate kit (Zhong Shan Jin Qiao, China).

Immunofluorescence staining (IF)

Cells were cultured on confocal-compatible dishes and treated as indicated. Cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and permeabilized with methanol at -20℃ for 10 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 488- or Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, USA) for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (FV1000, Olympus, Japan) at 100× magnification.

For lung tissue sections, deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.9) were performed. Blocking and antibody incubation steps were conducted as described for cells. Imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (FV1000, Olympus).

ELISA

Concentrations of IL-25, IL-33, TSLP, IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, CSF1, and HDM-specific IgE were quantified using ELISA kits (Gelatins, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

RNA sequencing

RNA-seq was performed on control and HDM-stimulated cells. Library preparation and sequencing were conducted by Beijing Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. DEGs were identified using the criteria |log2foldchange| > 0.5 and p < .05. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was then performed to identify biological pathways associated with these DEGs.

ChIP assay and CUT&Tag-qPCR

BEAS-2B cells were harvested and fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min, followed by terminating of cross-linking with 1.25 M glycine. ChIP-seq was performed using an anti-H3K18la antibody (PTM Bio, China) by Hangzhou LC-Bio Technologies Co., Ltd. CUT&Tag assays were conducted using a CUT&Tag Detection Kit (Novoprotein, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primer sequences used for CUT&Tag-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Statistical analysis

All experimental results are expressed as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range. Statistical analysis and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2). Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired Student’s t-tests, while one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test was employed for multiple group comparisons. Correlation analyses were conducted using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. P-values < 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant.

Result

Enhanced aerobic glycolysis and lactate production in asthma

Given the potential involvement of glycolysis in asthma, we first assessed serum lactate levels in 12 patients with asthma and 12 healthy controls. Consistent with previous studies, serum lactate levels were significantly elevated in asthma patients compared with controls (Fig. 1A). Moreover, serum lactate levels were positively correlated with eosinophil counts, total IgE, and FeNO in asthma patients (Fig. 1B, C and D). Negative associations with FEV₁/FVC and %FEV₁ were also observed but did not reach statistical significance (Figure S1A, S1B). These findings suggest that circulating lactate is markedly increased in asthma patients and is associated with the severity of eosinophilic inflammation.

To further map the metabolic landscape in both healthy and asthmatic lungs, we integrated scRNA-seq data from 107,969 cells derived from 8 asthma patients and 38 healthy individuals. Cell annotation based on established lineage markers identified major cellular populations, including immune, endothelial, stromal, and airway epithelial cells. Among these, airway epithelial cells were further resolved into basal, ciliated, and club cell populations (Figs. 2 A, 2B). Next, we assessed glycolysis module scores and observed significant increases across multiple cell types in asthma, notably in airway epithelial cells (Fig. 2C, S2A). Consistently, pathway enrichment analysis of airway epithelial cells further supported enhanced glycolytic activity, accompanied by reduced oxidative phosphorylation in asthmatic samples (Fig. 2D). Pseudotime analysis also suggested that glycolytic activity remained elevated across the inferred transcriptional states of airway epithelial cells (Fig. 2E).

Prompted by the observed metabolic alterations, we next examined glycolytic activity in airway epithelial cells in asthma. Differential expression analysis showed upregulation of multiple glycolysis-related genes in asthmatic airway epithelial cells compared with control subjects (Figure S2B). To validate these findings, BEAS-2B cells were stimulated with varying concentrations of HDM for different durations. Both supernatant and intracellular lactate levels increased significantly after HDM exposure (Fig. 2F). qPCR and Western blot analyses further showed that HDM treatment increased the mRNA and protein expression of key glycolytic enzymes, including HK2, PKM2, and LDHA, with significant upregulation observed at 800 U HDM stimulation (Fig. 2G and H). Together, these results support enhanced glycolysis as a characteristic metabolic alteration in airway epithelial cells in asthma.

Histone lactylation contributes to HDM-induced inflammatory responses in airway epithelial cells

We then investigated whether enhanced glycolysis was associated with histone lactylation in airway epithelial cells. Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses revealed significantly elevated histone lactylation levels in HDM-treated BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 3A and B). Among the reported histone lactylation sites, H3K18la has been extensively studied because of its important roles in gene regulation [22] and disease pathogenesis, including cancer [23] and inflammatory disorders [24]. We therefore focused on H3K18la alterations and its functional relevance in airway epithelial cells. Consistently, H3K18la levels were markedly increased following HDM treatment (Fig. 3A). To further examine the relationship among glycolysis, histone lactylation, and epithelial inflammatory responses, BEAS-2B cells were pretreated with glycolysis inhibitors 2-DG or oxamate (Fig. 3C). Inhibitor treatment significantly reduced both supernatant and intracellular lactate levels, indicating suppression of HDM-induced glycolytic activity (Fig. 3D). Moreover, glycolysis inhibition markedly reduced histone lactylation levels in HDM-treated cells (Fig. 3E and F). qPCR analysis showed that HDM stimulation significantly upregulated IL-33, TSLP, IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 expression, which was attenuated by glycolysis inhibition (Fig. 3G and H).

To further assess the relationship between lactate-associated histone lactylation and inflammatory responses, BEAS-2B cells were co-stimulated with lactate and HDM. Additional lactate exposure further enhanced HDM-induced histone lactylation (Fig. 3I) and increased inflammatory cytokine expression compared with HDM treatment alone (Fig. 3J). Given that P300, a histone acetyltransferase with broad acyltransferase activity, has been implicated in mediating histone lactylation [14], we knocked down P300 using siRNA (si-P300) to inhibit this modification (Figure S3A). Notably, P300 knockdown significantly reduced histone lactylation (Fig. 3K) and was accompanied by decreased inflammatory cytokine expression (Fig. 3L).

In summary, these findings support a role for lactate-dependent histone lactylation in linking glycolytic metabolism to inflammatory cytokine expression in airway epithelial cells. Modulation of histone lactylation may therefore represent a potential strategy for alleviating airway inflammation in asthma.

Glycolysis inhibition suppresses histone lactylation and attenuates HDM-induced allergic airway inflammation

To further explore the involvement of histone lactylation in allergic airway inflammation in vivo, we administered the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG in an HDM-induced asthma model using C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 4A). Compared with the HDM group, 2-DG-treated mice exhibited significant reductions in total cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils in BALF (Fig. 4B). Consistently, 2-DG administration also suppressed HDM-induced inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4C), mucus overproduction (Fig. 4D) and airway remodeling (Fig. 4E). In addition, 2-DG treatment reduced HDM-induced increases in IL-25, TSLP, and type 2 inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13) in BALF (Fig. 4F), as well as serum levels of HDM-specific IgE (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these results indicate that glycolysis inhibition effectively attenuates HDM-induced allergic airway inflammation in vivo.

To assess whether histone lactylation contributes to the anti-inflammatory effects of 2-DG, lactate production and histone lactylation levels were assessed in the murine asthma model. As expected, 2-DG treatment significantly reduced lactate levels in BALF (Fig. 4H). Immunohistochemistry revealed that HDM exposure markedly increased Pan-Kla levels, particularly within the airway epithelium, whereas 2-DG administration effectively suppressed this upregulation (Fig. 4I). Western blot analysis further demonstrated reduced Pan-Kla and H3K18la levels following 2-DG treatment (Fig. 4J). Overall, these findings suggest that glycolysis inhibition alleviates allergic airway inflammation and is associated with suppression of histone lactylation in the airway epithelium.

H3K18la activates CSF1R transcription in airway epithelial cells

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which histone lactylation contributes to allergic airway inflammation, we first performed ChIP-seq using an anti-H3K18la antibody in HDM-treated BEAS-2B cells. HDM exposure markedly increased H3K18la peak enrichment around transcription start sites (TSSs) (Fig. 5A, S4A). Genomic distribution analysis further revealed that approximately 30.5% of differentially modified H3K18la peaks were located in promoter regions, with around 20% specifically enriched within ± 1 kb of the TSS (Fig. 5B, S4C). Overall, 6,654 genes exhibited differential H3K18la modification following HDM treatment (p < .05, |log₂FoldChange| > 0.5), among which 1,354 genes showed increased H3K18la enrichment in their promoter regions (Figure S4D). KEGG analysis of the genes with increased H3K18la peaks in promoter regions revealed significant enrichment in pathways associated with epithelial barrier integrity such as focal adhesion and tight junction signaling, as well as inflammation-related pathways, including MAPK and PI3K-Akt signaling (Fig. 5C). We then performed RNA-seq analysis to identify genes transcriptionally regulated by histone lactylation. Differential expression analysis revealed 395 upregulated and 204 downregulated genes in response to HDM stimulation (Fig. 5D). KEGG analysis of the upregulated genes demonstrated significant enrichment of the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway, further supporting enhanced glycolytic activity in airway epithelial cells during asthma (Fig. 5E). Enrichment of the MAPK signaling pathway was also observed in HDM-treated cells (Fig. 5E). Through integrating the ChIP-seq and RNA-seq datasets, we identified 10 genes that exhibited both increased mRNA expression and enhanced H3K18la enrichment in their promoter regions following HDM exposure (Fig. 5F).

Given its established relevance in allergic airway inflammation, CSF1R was investigated as a candidate transcriptional target of H3K18la. ChIP-seq analysis revealed enhanced H3K18la enrichment in the promoter region of CSF1R following HDM exposure (Fig. 5G), which was further supported by CUT&Tag-qPCR (Fig. 5H). Consistently, HDM stimulation significantly upregulated CSF1R mRNA levels in BEAS-2B cells, whereas 2-DG treatment or P300 knockdown markedly suppressed this induction. Conversely, lactate supplementation further augmented CSF1R expression (Fig. 5I). Immunofluorescence staining further showed increased CSF1R protein expression in HDM-treated BEAS-2B cells and in the airway epithelium of HDM-induced asthmatic mice. This increase was attenuated by 2-DG treatment (Fig. 5J and K). Together, these findings support a role for H3K18la in promoting CSF1R expression during allergic airway inflammation.

CSF1R inhibition attenuates HDM-induced allergic airway inflammation

To assess the functional role of CSF1R in histone lactylation-associated allergic airway inflammation, the selective CSF1R inhibitor GW2580 [25] was administered in an HDM-induced asthma model (Fig. 6A). Compared with the HDM group, GW2580 treatment markedly attenuated HDM-induced inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 6B), mucus overproduction (Fig. 6C), and airway remodeling (Fig. 6D). Consistent with these changes, BALF analysis demonstrated significant reductions in total inflammatory cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils (Fig. 6E), together with decreased levels of inflammatory cytokines following GW2580 administration (Fig. 6F). Serum HDM-specific IgE levels were also notably decreased in GW2580-treated mice compared with the HDM group (Fig. 6G). Overall, these findings support a functional contribution of CSF1R signaling to HDM-induced airway inflammation.

Histone lactylation promotes CSF1 production and promotes CSF1/CSF1R signaling in airway epithelial cells

Growing evidence suggests that CSF1R expression is not exclusively restricted to myeloid immune cells but is also expressed on the membranes of various cancer cell types. CSF1R+ cancer cells could produce CSF1 themselves to sustain an autocrine positive feedback loop, thereby promoting tumor progression and modulating immune responses [26]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that a similar axis may also be present in airway epithelial cells during asthma.

We first examined whether airway epithelial cells could produce CSF1 in response to allergen stimulation. Compared with the control group, HDM stimulation significantly increased both CSF1 mRNA expression and secretion. Notably, inhibition of histone lactylation by 2-DG or siP300 markedly reduced CSF1 expression and secretion, whereas lactate supplementation enhanced both (Fig. 7A and B). Next, we examined the role of CSF1R in this process by treating BEAS-2B cells with GW2580. CSF1 secretion was significantly reduced following CSF1R inhibition (Fig. 7C). Additionally, GW2580 treatment not only suppressed CSF1 mRNA expression but also downregulated the mRNA levels of IL-33, TSLP, and type 2 inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 7D). To further examine whether airway epithelial cells are directly responsive to CSF1, we stimulated BEAS-2B cells with CSF1. CSF1 treatment increased the mRNA expression of IL-33 and TSLP, elevated H3K18la levels, and enhanced CSF1R expression. Moreover, compared with HDM stimulation alone, co-stimulation with CSF1 and HDM induced stronger responses, indicating that CSF1 further amplified HDM-induced epithelial inflammatory responses (Fig 7E, F and G). Finally, we assessed the clinical relevance of this axis by measuring serum CSF1 levels in asthma patients. Although serum CSF1 levels did not significantly differ between asthma patients and healthy individuals, a positive correlation was observed between serum CSF1 and lactate levels within the asthma group (Figure S5A, S5B). Collectively, these findings indicate that airway epithelial cells are directly responsive to CSF1 and support a potential CSF1/CSF1R-mediated epithelial amplification loop.

Histone lactylation-regulated CSF1/CSF1R axis activates MAPK signaling in airway epithelial cells

Previous studies have demonstrated that CSF1R activation triggers autophosphorylation of its intracellular tyrosine kinase domain and thereby activates multiple downstream pathways, including PI3K, ERK1/2, and JAK signaling [27]. The MAPK signaling pathway plays a critical role in inflammatory responses by promoting transcription of inflammation-associated genes upon external stimulation [28]. In our study, KEGG analysis of upregulated genes from both ChIP-seq and RNA-seq datasets revealed significant enrichment of the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 5C and E). Consistently, GSEA demonstrated significant activation of the MAPK signaling pathway in HDM-stimulated cells (Figure S6A). Supporting these findings, Western blot analysis showed that HDM treatment markedly increased phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and p38 in BEAS-2B cells compared with controls. Inhibition of histone lactylation with 2-DG or siP300 markedly suppressed this response, whereas lactate supplementation enhanced their phosphorylation (Fig. 8A and B). Moreover, inhibition of CSF1R with GW2580 attenuated HDM-induced MAPK activation (Fig. 8C and D). Immunofluorescence staining of p-ERK1/2 and p-p38 in lung sections further showed reduced ERK1/2 and p38 phosphorylation in the airway epithelium following 2-DG or GW2580 treatment (Fig. 8E). Taken together, these results support activation of the CSF1/CSF1R/MAPK signaling axis downstream of histone lactylation in airway epithelial cells.

Discussion

Metabolic reprogramming has increasingly been recognized as an active driver of inflammatory signaling in chronic airway diseases [29]. In asthma, airway epithelial cells are known to undergo substantial metabolic alterations after allergen exposure [11], yet the mechanisms that translate these changes into sustained inflammatory responses remain incompletely understood. In this study, our findings support a mechanistic framework linking HDM-induced glycolytic remodeling to epithelial inflammatory activation through histone lactylation and the downstream CSF1/CSF1R/MAPK autocrine axis, thereby highlighting histone lactylation as a potential therapeutic target in allergic airway inflammation.

Lactate, traditionally regarded as a metabolic byproduct of glucose metabolism, is increasingly recognized as a signaling metabolite with broad physiological and pathological functions [30]. Although its immunomodulatory effects have been widely documented, particularly in immune cells [31], its role in airway epithelial cells remains less well defined. Our study shows that HDM stimulation significantly enhanced glycolytic activity in airway epithelial cells, leading to increased lactate accumulation. Elevated lactate not only reflects enhanced metabolic flux but also contributes to epigenetic regulation. We observed a pronounced increase in lactate-dependent histone lactylation in airway epithelial cells following HDM exposure, which was associated with allergic airway inflammation both in vitro and in vivo. These findings highlight a novel mechanism in which lactate reshapes the epigenetic landscape of airway epithelial cells, thereby linking metabolic reprogramming to inflammatory gene activation. Nevertheless, an important question that remains is whether H3K18la acts as a primary regulatory determinant or instead functions within a broader epigenetic network. The lack of strategies for selectively modulating H3K18la, together with the absence of a comprehensive assessment of alterations across all histone lactylation sites, limits our ability to define its precise contribution and to determine whether multiple lactylation sites cooperate in regulating gene expression. These challenges underscore the complexity of defining the role of histone modifications in disease pathogenesis. Further development of tools for selective modulation of histone lactylation, combined with more comprehensive profiling of the lactylation landscape, may facilitate deeper mechanistic insights and therapeutic exploration in asthma.

Epigenetic modifications are dynamically regulated by “writers” and “erasers” that add or remove chemical groups on histones, thereby shaping chromatin accessibility and gene transcription [32, 33]. To date, several histone lactylation writers have been identified, including P300 [14], HBO1 [34], and GCN5 [35], while HDAC1-3 and SIRT1-3 have been reported to function as potential erasers [36]. In our study, knockdown of P300 reduced H3K18la and attenuated the HDM-induced inflammatory response in airway epithelial cells, supporting the role of P300 in mediating histone lactylation in this context. However, given that P300 also catalyzes multiple other histone acylations, such as acetylation [37], crotonylation [38], and isobutyrylation [39], its global inhibition may lead to broad epigenomic alterations with unpredictable consequences. Thus, P300 may not represent an optimal therapeutic target. Moving forward, the development of more selective lactylation modulators will be critical for enabling precise regulation of this pathway in allergic airway inflammation.

Histone lactylation at promoter regions has been shown to directly modulate gene transcription from chromatin. Here, ChIP-seq analysis identified increased H3K18la enrichment at the CSF1R promoter region, supporting H3K18la as an important regulator of CSF1R transcription in airway epithelial cells during allergic airway inflammation. Pharmacological inhibition of CSF1R using GW2580 suppressed epithelial inflammatory responses in vitro and alleviated allergic airway inflammation in vivo. Moreover, our data support a CSF1/CSF1R/MAPK signaling axis in airway epithelial cells, suggesting that H3K18la-regulated CSF1R activation may amplify inflammatory signaling. While the present study focused on airway epithelial cells, previous studies have established a pro-inflammatory role for CSF1R signaling in macrophages during asthma [18, 19]. Consistently, our additional F4/80 and CD68 staining showed that GW2580 reduced the HDM-induced accumulation of macrophage-associated cells in lung tissues, suggesting that the in vivo effect of CSF1R inhibition may involve pulmonary macrophage responses (Figure S7). Further investigation of lactylation-related CSF1R signaling in macrophage populations will be important for clarifying the broader cellular context of CSF1R-associated airway inflammation.

Clinical observations in asthma patients further supported the link between histone lactylation and eosinophilic inflammation. Consistent with previous reports [10, 11], we detected increased serum lactate levels in asthma patients. Furthermore, lactate concentrations were positively correlated with multiple markers of eosinophilic inflammation, suggesting that lactate-associated metabolic alterations may be linked to type 2 inflammatory activity in asthma. However, no significant correlation was observed between lactate levels and lung function parameters, which may be attributable to the relatively small sample size and heterogeneity among study participants. Importantly, serum lactate is a systemic metabolic marker, and lactate alone may not fully reflect glycolytic activity in asthma. Circulating lactate can be influenced by multiple factors beyond asthma itself, including tissue oxygenation/hypoperfusion, hepatic or renal clearance, and metabolic status [40, 41]. In addition, β2-agonists such as salbutamol have been reported to increase serum lactate, particularly during acute exacerbations or intensive treatment [42]. Therefore, future studies in larger clinical cohorts with more comprehensive metabolic and clinical parameters, such as blood pyruvate and the lactate-to-pyruvate ratio [43], will be important for reducing potential bias, better defining glycolytic status in asthma, and evaluating its potential as a biomarker of eosinophilic inflammation.

In summary, our findings extend current understanding of how epithelial metabolic remodeling contributes to allergic airway inflammation. By linking glycolytic activation to histone lactylation and downstream inflammatory signaling, this study highlights histone lactylation as a potential therapeutic target in asthma.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identifies a metabolic–epigenetic link between glycolytic remodeling and epithelial inflammatory activation in asthma. In this context, HDM-induced glycolytic remodeling in airway epithelial cells promotes histone lactylation, which enhances CSF1R transcription and activates the CSF1/CSF1R/MAPK autocrine axis. These findings provide new insight into asthma pathogenesis and suggest that histone lactylation may represent a potential target for therapeutic intervention.
