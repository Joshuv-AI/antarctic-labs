Bridge-RAG: An Abstract Bridge Tree Based Retrieval Augmented Generation Algorithm With Cuckoo Filter
Zihang Li Wenjun Liu Yikun Zong Jiawen Tao Siying Dai, Songcheng Ren Zirui Liu Yanbing Jiang Tong Yang

Abstract
As an important paradigm for enhancing the generation quality of Large Language Models (LLMs), retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) faces the two challenges regarding retrieval accuracy and computational efficiency. This paper presents a novel RAG framework called Bridge-RAG. To overcome the accuracy challenge, we introduce the concept of abstract to bridge query entities and document chunks, providing robust semantic understanding. We organize the abstracts into a tree structure and design a multi-level retrieval strategy to ensure the inclusion of sufficient contextual information. To overcome the efficiency challenge, we introduce the improved Cuckoo Filter, an efficient data structure supporting rapid membership queries and updates, to accelerate entity location during the retrieval process. We design a block linked list structure and an entity temperature-based sorting mechanism to improve efficiency from the aspects of spatial and temporal locality. Extensive experiments show that Bridge-RAG achieves around 15.65% accuracy improvement and reduces 10 × \times to 500 × \times retrieval time compared to other RAG frameworks.

Introduction

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has become an important paradigm for enhancing the generation quality of Large Language Models (LLMs) by integrating external knowledge
(
11
)
.
Existing RAG frameworks are primarily of three types: text-based, graph-based, and tree-based.
Text-based ones store information as raw text.
In contrast, graph-based and tree-based RAG represent knowledge through structured schemas to provide richer context and capture complex relationships among entities.

RAG systems face two critical challenges regarding retrieval accuracy and computational efficiency.
First, in terms of retrieval accuracy, standard RAG frameworks usually rely on vector similarity search, which tends to retrieve passages that are semantically related but contextually fragmented or noisy. This misalignment often results in the generator receiving insufficient or irrelevant evidence, inevitably propagating errors to the final response.
Second, a severe efficiency bottleneck persists in structured RAG frameworks, especially for Tree-RAG.
As the dataset scale and tree depth grow, the time required to traverse the hierarchical structure and locate relevant entities significantly increases.

the retrieval time of standard RAG framework like Tree-RAG and Graph-RAG are still quite long. These two challenges hinder the deployment of RAG systems in real-world scenarios that demand high precision and low-latency responses.

Our first key design focuses on enhancing retrieval accuracy from the aspects of semantic depth and contextual breadth.
First, we introduce the concept of
abstract
to facilitate robust semantic understanding.
Specifically, an
abstract
groups several consecutive document chunks into a high-level knowledge unit.
We organize these abstracts into tree structures where upper-level abstracts represent more general concepts and lower-level ones capture more detailed information.
This hierarchical organization facilitates a three-tier retrieval path where
abstract
serves as the bridge between
query entity
and
document chunk
.
In this way, Bridge-RAG ensures the captured content is semantically relevant.
Second, we design a multi-level retrieval strategy to ensure the inclusion of sufficient contextual information.
Specifically, Bridge-RAG systematically traces the hierarchical lineage of the abstract tree, retrieving document chunks associated with both parent and child abstracts alongside the target node.
This strategy ensures that the retrieved context spans multiple abstraction levels, enabling Bridge-RAG to to maintain a comprehensive contextual scope and further improve the answer accuracy.

Our second key design introduces an
Improved Cuckoo Filter
to accelerate the retrieval process by facilitating
O(1)
lookups from query entities to their corresponding abstracts.
This design incorporates two primary optimizations aimed at enhancing efficiency from the perspectives of spatial and temporal locality.
First, to optimize spatial locality, we introduce a block linked list structure for entity-abstract mappings. Unlike traditional linked lists, each node aggregates multiple addresses, which significantly reduces memory fragmentation and optimizes cache performance for large-scale hierarchies. Second, to leverage temporal locality, we propose an entity temperature-based sorting mechanism. By maintaining a temperature variable that records access frequency, the Cuckoo Filter dynamically reorders entities within each bucket.
By placing high-temperature entities at the front, we effectively optimize the efficiency of linear search within buckets.
To maximize storage efficiency, entities are represented within the Cuckoo Filter as 12-bit fingerprints, significantly reducing the memory footprint compared to storing raw strings.
To ensure scalability, we also design a dynamic resizing mechanism that maintains the load factor within an optimal range. This strategy ensures high memory utilization while effectively mitigating hash collisions as the dataset expands.

Related Work

Cuckoo Filter
Cuckoo Filter is an efficient data structure for supporting fast element lookup and deletion operations, which is mainly used for collection operations and data stream processing
(
6
)
. It is developed based on the idea of Cuckoo Hashing
(
13
)
. Cuckoo Filter outperforms traditional Bloom filters in terms of storage efficiency and query performance, especially in scenarios where frequent insertion and deletion of elements are required. The main advantage of Cuckoo Filter is its support for dynamic updates, which enables it to efficiently handle element changes in a collection. Unlike Bloom Filter, Cuckoo Filter can not only query whether an element exists or not, but also support the deletion operation of an element, a feature that is important in many practical applications
(
8
)
. The working principle of Cuckoo Filter is based on multiple hash functions and a bucket structure, which is utilized by storing the elements in fixed-size buckets and using the hash conflicts to achieve fast lookup of elements. Recent research has further explored the integration of such probabilistic data structures into high-throughput retrieval systems to minimize index memory footprints while maintaining low latency
(
14
)
. In 2025, researchers have taken this further by proposing Hybrid-Index RAG
(
2
)
, which combines learned indexes with probabilistic filters like Cuckoo Filters to handle billion-scale vector datasets.

Graph-RAG
Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation (Graph-RAG) is an extension of RAG, where the information retrieval process is augmented by leveraging graph-based structures to organize and retrieve information
(
11
)
. The key difference between traditional RAG and Graph-RAG is the use of a graph, which can improve the relevance and contextuality of the retrieved information
(
9
)
. The algorithmic process of Graph-RAG enhances the understanding of relationships and provides more precise information retrieval than traditional RAG
(
3
)
. Notably, recent approaches like Microsoft’s GraphRAG have introduced community detection mechanisms to generate community summaries, enabling "global" answers
(
3
)
. Furthermore, EMG-RAG
(
19
)
integrates an Editable Memory Graph to manage partitions and capture dynamic relations. To address the limitations of simple binary relations in complex reasoning, Hyper-RAG
(
20
)
utilizes hypergraphs to model multi-entity relationships, showing superior performance in multi-hop reasoning tasks compared to standard graph methods. However, the complexity in graph construction and maintenance will be a trouble, and the quality of the graph can also affect the accuracy of responses. Therefore, in comparison to Tree-RAG, Graph-RAG still has certain disadvantages.

Tree-RAG
Tree-RAG (T-RAG) is an emerging method that combines tree structure and large language models to improve the effectiveness of knowledge retrieval and generation tasks. This hierarchical approach is increasingly vital as indicated by recent benchmarks like AA-LCR (2025)
(
1
)
. The necessity of structural retrieval is further supported by the RAG-Bench-2025
(
16
)
, which reveals that flat retrieval systems fail significantly on questions requiring synthesis of information across more than five document clusters. A prominent recent advancement is RAPTOR
(
15
)
, which employs recursive clustering. Building on this, Dynamic-Tree RAG
(
12
)
introduces an adaptive pruning mechanism that dynamically adjusts the tree depth based on query complexity, optimizing the trade-off between accuracy and speed. Similarly, Reasoning-RAG
(
18
)
integrates Chain-of-Thought processes directly into the tree traversal, allowing the model to refine its retrieval path iteratively. In our proposed framework, we extend these concepts by utilizing entities as explicit bridges to access these hierarchical abstracts. The system traverses through the hierarchical structure of the tree to obtain the abstract nodes related to the query. This process effectively combines knowledge retrieval and generation and improves the accuracy and contextual relevance.

Data Pre-processing

It is important to identify key concepts and construct hierarchical abstract tree structures from datasets, where each abstract groups multiple document chunks and abstracts are organized hierarchically based on their semantic relationships. It mainly involves the steps of concept identification, relationship extraction, abstract construction, and filtering.
For existing hierarchical data, binary pairs representing parent-child relationships are directly extracted. For raw textual data, text cleansing is first performed manually to remove irrelevant information.

3.1
Concept Identification

SpaCy is a Python library, and its entity recognition function is based on deep learning models (e.g., CNN and Transformers). It captures information by transforming the text into word vectors and feature vectors. The models are trained on a labeled corpus to recognize named entities in the text, such as names of people and places. We use the spaCy library to recognize and extract key concepts from a user’s query
(
7
)
. Note that entity recognition serves to identify key concepts in queries, which are then used to locate corresponding abstract nodes in the abstract tree, where each abstract represents a higher-level semantic concept that groups multiple document chunks.

3.2
Relationship Extraction

Various relationships are identified from the data, including organizational, categorization, temporal, geographic, inclusion, functional, and attribute relationships. The relationships manifest through grammatical structures such as noun phrases, prepositional phrases, relative clauses, and appositive structures
(
17
;
4
)
. We focus on extracting relationships that express dependency, such as "belongs to," "contains," and "is dependent on." The process is presented in Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
The process of relationship extraction

We apply several dependency parsing models to analyze the grammatical structure of the data. This helps identify relationships between words, such as subject-verb-object or modifier relationships.

We define rules to identify hierarchical relationships. If a word modifies another noun, it can be interpreted as a child-parent relationship; If there are conjunctions (e.g., "and", "or"), handle them to group concepts under the same parent. As a result, there is a list of tuples representing the hierarchical structure.

3.3
Relationship Filtering

After extracting relations, certain relationships are filtered out to ensure maintain the tree structure:

•

Transitive Relations: If transitive relations are detected (e.g., "A belongs to B", "B belongs to C" and "A belongs to C"), remove distant relations.

•

Cycle Relations: If cycles are detected (e.g., "A belongs to B" and "B belongs to A"), only the closest relationship is retained.

•

Self-Pointing Edges: Any relation where a node points to itself is removed.

•

Duplicate Edges: Multiple edges between the same nodes are pruned, leaving only one edge.

Methodology

In this section, we propose Bridge-RAG, which integrates an improved Cuckoo Filter into Tree-RAG to boost efficiency in knowledge retrieval, while enhancing the accuracy at the same time via bridging the abstracts with entity chunks.

4.1
Hierarchical Abstract Tree Structure

The foundation of our approach lies in the hierarchical abstract tree structure, which addresses the accuracy limitations of traditional RAG systems. Abstracts are built by semantically grouping five consecutive document chunks into higher-level knowledge units. Each abstract receives a pair identifier
i
and subsumes the chunk IDs
5i…5i+4
. The five constituent chunks are fused with embedding aggregation or summarization to produce a concise semantic summary, enabling efficient abstraction-layer retrieval.

Abstracts are organized hierarchically in tree structures, where upper-level abstracts represent more general and abstract concepts, while lower-level abstracts capture more specific and detailed information. This hierarchical organization enables multi-level semantic retrieval: when a query entity is identified, we not only retrieve the corresponding abstract and its associated document chunks, but also trace the abstract’s parent-child relationships in the tree. Formally, given an initial abstract set
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\{a_{i}\}
retrieved from the Cuckoo Filter, we expand it through hierarchical traversal:

\mathcal{A}_{\text{expanded}}=\mathcal{A}_{0}\cup\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{0}}\left(\mathcal{P}_{d}(a)\cup\mathcal{C}_{d}(a)\right)

(1)

where
\mathcal{P}_{d}(a)
denotes the set of parent abstracts up to
d
levels upward, and
\mathcal{C}_{d}(a)
denotes the set of child abstracts up to
d
levels downward. By retrieving these related abstracts along with their corresponding document chunks, we construct a comprehensive context spanning multiple abstraction levels, significantly improving answer accuracy by providing both high-level semantic understanding and granular textual details.

4.2
Cuckoo Filter with Block Linked List and Temperature Sorting

While the hierarchical abstract tree structure substantially improves accuracy, it introduces computational overhead. To address this efficiency challenge, we integrate an improved Cuckoo Filter that stores entity-abstract mappings, enabling fast lookup to locate relevant abstracts containing query entities. The lookup operation for entity
e
achieves constant time complexity:

T_{\text{lookup}}(e)=O(1)

(2)

Our Cuckoo Filter design incorporates two key innovations for acceleration.

Block Linked List Structure
: Unlike traditional Cuckoo Filter implementations that store single values, we introduce a block linked list structure where each block node stores abstract node addresses related to this entity. Since each abstract corresponds to exactly five consecutive document chunks, chunk addresses are implicitly determined: for abstract with pair_id
i
, the chunks have chunk_id
5i
through
5i+4
. Formally, for an entity
e
associated with
m
abstracts, the space complexity is
O(\lceil m/3\rceil)
, compared to
O(m)
for traditional linked lists, achieving approximately 3x space reduction when
m\geq 3
. This design significantly reduces memory fragmentation and improves space utilization, particularly effective for entities appearing in multiple locations within the abstract forest.

Entity Temperature-Based Sorting
: To further optimize retrieval performance, we propose an entity temperature-based adaptive sorting strategy. Each entity-abstract mapping maintains a temperature variable
T(e)
recording access frequency. During retrieval, when entity
e
is successfully located, its temperature is incremented:
T(e)\leftarrow T(e)+1
. The Cuckoo Filter dynamically sorts entities within each bucket by temperature, placing frequently accessed entities at the front. Formally, for bucket
B
containing entities
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
, we maintain
T(e_{i})\geq T(e_{j})
for
i<j
, ensuring high-frequency entities are searched first. Since Cuckoo Filter performs linear search within buckets, this temperature-based ordering leverages access locality to accelerate retrieval.

In summary, in each entry of the Cuckoo Filter bucket, we store: (1) the entity’s fingerprint (12-bit), (2) the temperature variable (stored at the head of the block linked list), and (3) the head pointer of the block linked list (containing abstract node addresses, from which chunk addresses are derived). The storage mode is illustrated in Figure

, and details about the process of insertion and eviction are provided in Appendix
A
.

4.3
Context Generation with Entity-Abstract-Chunk Bridge

Our context generation process leverages the hierarchical abstract structure and the Cuckoo Filter to achieve both high accuracy and efficiency. The workflow demonstrates a bridging mechanism that connects query entities to abstracts and then to specific document chunks, where abstracts serve as the crucial intermediate layer.

The workflow consists of five steps: (1)
Entity Recognition
: Key entities are identified using entity recognition techniques (e.g., SpaCy). (2)
Entity-to-Abstract Lookup
: The Cuckoo Filter locates abstracts containing each entity via O(1) lookup, returns the block linked list pointer, and increments the entity’s temperature. (3)
Hierarchical Context Enrichment
: We trace parent-child relationships to expand the abstract set based on max hierarchy depth, retrieving parent and child abstracts up to several levels. (4)
Abstract-to-Chunk Bridge
: We retrieve all chunks associated with the expanded abstract set, where each abstract is linked to 5 chunks. (5)
Similarity-based Selection
: We compute cosine similarity between query embedding
\mathbf{q}
and chunk embeddings
\mathbf{c}_{j}
:

\text{sim}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{c}_{j})=\frac{\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{c}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{q}\|\|\mathbf{c}_{j}\|}

(3)

We then select the top-
k
chunks
\mathcal{C}_{\text{top-k}}
with the highest similarity scores:

\mathcal{C}_{\text{top-k}}=\arg\max_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C},|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|=k}\sum_{c_{j}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\text{sim}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{c}_{j})

(4)

where
\mathcal{C}
is the set of all chunks associated with the expanded abstract set. The top-
k
chunks are then selected, and their corresponding abstracts are retrieved. Finally, this multi-level contextual information (selected chunks and abstracts) is combined with the system prompt and query to form the final prompt for the LLM. The complete workflow is illustrated in Figure

Experiments

In this section, we describe our experimental setup, baseline methods, Bridge-RAG, and comprehensively evaluate the results. Our experiments aim to assess the efficiency of Bridge-RAG approach under diverse experimental conditions, particularly in terms of retrieval speed and accuracy.

5.1
Baseline

To benchmark the effectiveness of the proposed Cuckoo Filter Bridge-RAG, we compare it against several baseline models. Our baselines include the naive Tree RAG, the naive RAG and Graph-RAG. These baselines allow us to quantify the improvements introduced by our Cuckoo Filter Bridge RAG.

Naive RAG

This baseline follows the standard retrieve-then-generate pipeline without any hierarchical or filtering enhancement. The top-k chunks returned by cosine similarity are directly concatenated with the user query and fed to the LLM. No abstract layer, graph structure, or approximate acceleration is employed, providing a clean reference for measuring the gains introduced by later optimizations.

Graph RAG

This model integrates approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search with a graph-based structure to accelerate retrieval while maintaining semantic relevance. Entities and their relationships are organized into a directed graph, enabling multi-hop traversal and contextual inference. During retrieval, ANN is used to identify the closest matching abstracts efficiently, and the graph structure guides the expansion to related entities for enriched context.

Tree RAG

In this variant, Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) search is employed to accelerate document retrieval in the entity tree structure. Instead of performing exact similarity search, the model leverages efficient ANN indexing techniques (e.g., FAISS or HNSW) to retrieve top-K candidates. It provides a straightforward baseline for evaluating the benefits of replacing the entities with abstracts and the efficiency of the improved Cuckoo Filter.

5.2
Bridge-RAG

Bridge-RAG integrates the improved Cuckoo Filter with block linked list structure and entity temperature-based sorting (described in Section
4.2
) and hierarchical abstract tree structures for multi-level context retrieval (described in Section
4.1
). The implementation details are provided in the Methodology section.

5.4.1
Comparison Experiment

We conduct the experiments by randomly selecting 1000 questions on each dataset. Then we record the average retrieval time and average response accuracy using ROUGE, BLEU, and BERTScore. Table
1
presents the retrieval time and accuracy of various RAG-based models across the MedQA and AALCR datasets.

Bridge-RAG achieves the best performance across both datasets. On the AALCR dataset, Bridge-RAG improves accuracy by around 22.1% in BLEU compared to Tree RAG, while being 500 times faster. Compared to Graph RAG, Bridge-RAG improves accuracy by 38.9% in BLEU, while being 10 times faster. Compared to Naive RAG, Bridge-RAG improves accuracy by 68.1% in ROUGE-L and 104.5% in BLEU, while being 2.4 times faster.

On the MedQA dataset, Bridge-RAG improves accuracy by around 15% in ROUGE-L and BLEU compared to Tree RAG, while being 226 times faster. Compared to Graph RAG, Bridge-RAG improves accuracy by 9.4% in ROUGE-L and 17.1% in BLEU, while being around 10 times faster. Compared to Naive RAG, Bridge-RAG improves accuracy by 24.8% in ROUGE-L and 20.5% in BLEU, while being 2.8 times faster.

The speed advantage comes from Bridge-RAG’s Cuckoo Filter-based direct entity lookup (O(1) complexity), compared to Tree RAG’s exhaustive BFS traversal. Moreover, our method only needs to calculate the similarity between the query and the candidate chunk pool, rather than the full chunks, which greatly speeds up the process. The accuracy improvement stems from Bridge-RAG’s explicit hierarchical abstraction levels (parent, target, child) that capture multi-level semantic relationships, providing more structured and semantically coherent context than graph-based traversal or direct chunk retrieval. The multi-level context retrieval strategy traces parent-child relationships in the abstract tree, retrieving context from multiple abstraction levels to enable more accurate and contextually coherent answers.

Comparing different depth settings reveals a trade-off between accuracy and efficiency. The accuracy improvement with increasing depth comes from retrieving context from more abstraction levels: Depth=1 only uses target abstracts, while Depth=2 and Depth=3 additionally retrieve parent and child abstracts, providing broader semantic context and more granular details. The increased retrieval time is due to the additional tree traversal operations required to access parent and child nodes, but remains efficient due to the Cuckoo Filter’s fast entity-to-abstract mapping that minimizes the overhead of hierarchical expansion.

Moreover, the error rate of our method in the process of searching abstracts is very low. After building the trees based on the dataset, the Cuckoo Filter includes 1024 buckets, each of which can hold up to 4 fingerprints and block linked list head pointers. The Cuckoo Filter’s own memory expansion strategy is to increase the number of buckets by a power of two. In the experimental datasets, there are thousands of abstracts that can be constructed, and the space load factor for the Cuckoo Filter is more than 70%. Because the space load factor is not too high and searching errors are mainly caused by hash collisions, the error rate is almost 0, showing that the number of abstracts causing the lookup error is 0 to 1 out of 1024 buckets for thousands of abstracts.

5.4.2
Ablation Experiment

Sorting the fingerprints and head pointers of the block linked lists by temperature can optimize the retrieval time without occupying any extra space, which is eminently useful when the query given by the user requires retrieving a large number of abstracts.
We design experiments to measure the effect of having or not having the sorting design on the result. In figure
5
, we can observe that the retrieval time after the first round is significantly shorter than that of the first round. This is because the temperatures are updated according to the access frequency in each round and after each query, the Cuckoo Filter sorts the entities according to the entities’ temperatures. This sorting design allows the ’hot’ entities to be found more quickly in subsequent queries.

6
Conclusion

We presented Bridge-RAG, a retrieval-augmented generation framework that marries a hierarchical abstract tree with an improved Cuckoo Filter to simultaneously raise accuracy and cut latency. By tracing parent–child relationships across multiple abstraction levels, the model acquires both high-level concepts and fine-grained evidence, while the block-linked-list Cuckoo Filter guarantees O(1) entity-to-abstract lookup and automatically re-orders hot entries for sustained speed. Experiments on MedQA and AALCR show up to 15 % higher answer quality, as well as 3 times faster than Naive RAG, around 10 times faster than retrieval time against Graph RAG, and 500 times faster than Tree RAG,which representing a 2-order-of-magnitude speedup.

Limitations

Bridge-RAG assumes that five-chunk fixed-length abstracts offer an optimal trade-off between semantic coverage and retrieval granularity—longer or variable-length groupings may behave differently. Hierarchical depth is constrained to
\leq{3}
levels to contain latency; deeper trees or dynamic depth selection could further affect accuracy-speed balance. Finally, the system inherits the hallucination risk of the frozen backbone LLM: when retrieved chunks themselves contain conflicting or outdated facts, Bridge-RAG can propagate rather than correct the error.

Entity to Abstract Mapping Strategy

Entity Temperature-Based Sorting
: To optimize retrieval performance, we propose an
entity temperature-based sorting mechanism
. Each entity maintains a temperature variable
T(e)
that records its access frequency. During retrieval, when entity
e
is found, its temperature is incremented:
T(e)\leftarrow T(e)+1
. The Cuckoo Filter dynamically sorts entities within each bucket by temperature in descending order, ensuring frequently accessed entities are searched first. Since Cuckoo Filter performs linear search within buckets, this ordering leverages access locality to accelerate retrieval.
