A palmitoylation signature for prognosis immune infiltration and drug sensitivity in esophageal carcinoma
Authors: Chuan Xu, Zhuo Lu, Shijun Wu, Yuanyuan He, Xiaoyue Peng, Quanjin Li, Wei Gan, Gang Wu, Guangxia Wei, Xi Liu, Chunlin Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEsophageal carcinoma (ESCA) has a poor prognosis. This study aimed to develop a palmitoylation-related signature (PRS) for ESCA prognosis and therapy prediction. Using scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data from public databases, we constructed a signature via Cox and LASSO regression. It was validated internally and externally. Associations with clinical features, immune infiltration, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and drug sensitivity were analyzed. A 7-gene PRS was established, stratifying patients into risk groups with distinct outcomes (1–3 years AUCs: 0.698–0.805). High-risk patients showed altered immune infiltration, TMB, and drug sensitivity. The gene PAH was highly expressed in ESCA and promoted proliferation, migration, and invasion via PI3K/AKT pathway activation. Furthermore, this tumorigenic effect depends on its palmitoylation modification. In conclusion, the PRS can serve as an independent prognostic biomarker for ESCA, effectively predict prognosis and treatment response, and may represent a potential therapeutic target.Subject areas: Health sciences, medicine, internal medicine, oncology

Introduction

Esophageal carcinoma (ESCA) is one of the most common malignant tumors of the digestive system, and the sixth most common cause of cancer death worldwide.1 Among these types, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) are the two main types of cancer.2,3 At present, although significant advancements have been made in treatment through surgical procedures, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, the prognosis of ESCA remains very poor.4,5,6 This is because the differences in treatment response caused by tumor heterogeneity remain a core issue that hinders prognosis. For example, patients with the same TNM stage may have varying risks of postoperative recurrence.7 Moreover, there are no specific markers for the early diagnosis of ESCA. Most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, which further increases the difficulty of treatment.8 These current situations highlight the urgent need for precise prognostic prediction tools and treatment sensitivity markers in patients with ESCA.

The current prognostic assessment systems (such as TNM stage and degree of differentiation) are unable to capture the molecular heterogeneity of tumors. The TNM staging system is based solely on anatomical features and cannot reflect molecular-level differences, such as differences in tumor metabolism and signaling pathways.9 Although histological grading can indicate differentiation status, it lacks a direct correlation with the immune microenvironment and drug sensitivity.10 In recent years, although there have been explorations of prediction models based on mRNA or methylation-related signatures, most of them have focused on a single biological process. For example, the metabolic gene model is unable to distinguish the population benefiting from immunotherapy,11 and the association between the immune checkpoint gene-related signature and chemotherapy sensitivity is relatively weak.12 This limitation has led to the urgent need in clinical practice to integrate multidimensional information to identify molecular signatures that can jointly predict patient prognosis, immune status, and drug sensitivity.

Abnormally regulating the posttranslational modifications of proteins is a key driving factor for the malignant phenotype and heterogeneity of tumors. Among them, palmitoylation, the only reversible lipid modification, is involved in core processes such as cell proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, and immune evasion by dynamically regulating protein localization, signal transduction, and protein interactions.13,14,15 Abnormal expression of palmitoyltransferases (such as the ZDHHC family) and depalmitoylases (such as APT1) has been confirmed to be associated with metastasis and drug resistance in breast cancer and lung cancer.16,17,18 ZDHHC11 activates the PI3K/AKT pathway through palmitoylation, thereby promoting the progression of glioblastoma.19 PD-L1 modified by S-palmitoylation can enhance membrane stability to mediate immune evasion.20 However, there is a significant lack of systematic research on palmitoylation in ESCA. Whether it can serve as a molecular characteristic for analyzing tumor heterogeneity and whether it can be associated with prognosis and treatment response remains to be explored.

In this groundbreaking study, we first proposed the concept of a palmitoylation-related signature (PRS), revealing the complex correlation between palmitoylation and the occurrence and development of ESCA. We established it as an independent prognostic factor and emphasized its potential as a tool for predicting prognosis, immune infiltration, and drug sensitivity in patients. Furthermore, the hub gene PAH was confirmed to be significantly correlated with palmitoylation activity. Moreover, we preliminarily revealed the molecular mechanism by which PAH contributes to the progression of ESCA. In summary, this study systematically explored the prognostic value of the PRS and its correlation with clinical features, revealing its potential as a prognostic biomarker and new therapeutic target for patients with ESCA. These findings provide promising data for accurate prognosis assessment and personalized treatment of patients with ESCA.

Results

Identification of palmitoylation-related genes (PRGs) in ESCA

To identify the relevant genes that best reflect the signature of palmitoylation for the system, we conducted a detailed analysis of the single-cell transcriptome data GSE188900 from ESCA. After rigorous quality control, a total of 51,306 high-quality cells were selected for subsequent analysis. The quality control process and visualization of various single-cell data samples are shown in Figures S1A–S1F. The distribution of the quality-controlled samples was uniform, and the sequencing depth and mitochondrial ratio were within the appropriate range. The single-cell data were subsequently reduced in dimension via the UMAP technique, resulting in 24 clusters (Figure 1A). On the basis of the classical markers (Figure 1B), these 24 clusters were ultimately classified into 11 types of cells (Figure 1D). Notably, the control group samples had the greatest number of epithelial cells, which was different from that of the tumor group (Figure 1C). On the basis of the AUCell algorithm, we assigned 23 palmitoylation genes as a gene set to each cell for scoring, thereby evaluating the overall level of palmitoylation in ESCA. Based on the scoring results, we generated a UMAP graph (Figure 1E). Additionally, based on the median score of AUCell, we divided these cells into two groups: those with high palmitoylation scores and those with low palmitoylation scores (Figure 1F). The results revealed that PRGs tended to be highly expressed in immune cells, especially NK cells, T cells, and epithelial cells. To further study the PRGs, we conducted a correlation analysis between all the genes and the AUCell score in the tumor samples and obtained 791 positively correlated genes (Figure 1G). Additionally, we performed a differential analysis between the high-score group and the low-score group, using a p value <0.05 as the threshold. Eventually, we obtained 15 genes whose expression was upregulated in the high-score group (Figure 1H). By combining these key genes, we obtained a total of 793 candidate genes for palmitoylation.

The PRG signature reveals strong predictive power for ESCA prognosis

To further identify the PRGs that are significantly associated with prognosis from the candidate genes, we conducted a univariate Cox regression analysis using the transcriptome data from the TCGA. Among the 793 candidate genes, 11 genes were significantly associated with the OS of patients (p < 0.05) (Figure 2A). To optimize feature selection and reduce the risk of model overfitting, LASSO regression analysis was further employed to screen these 11 genes (Figure 2B). Through multivariate Cox regression analysis, 7 PRGs with independent prognostic value were ultimately selected, namely, SLC6A7, PAH, MYPN, GPR82, CCDC88C, UGT8, and HMX2. On the basis of the expression levels of these 7 genes and their regression coefficients, the coefficients of 4 genes (SLC6A7, PAH, GPR82, and CCDC88C) were positive and were classified as high-risk factors (i.e., high expression was associated with a poorer prognosis); the coefficients of the remaining 3 genes (MYPN, UGT8, and HMX2) were negative and protective factors (i.e., high expression was associated with a better prognosis) (Figure 2C). We calculated risk scores for each sample, visually presenting the differences in gene expression among different risk groups. To further explore the relationship between the risk score and patient prognosis, we plotted the risk distribution corresponding to the risk score distribution (Figures 2D and 2E). The results shown in the heatmap were subsequently used to display the gene expression characteristics of patients in the high-risk and low-risk groups (Figure 2F). Next, we used the KM survival curve and the ROC curve in the TCGA training set to evaluate the survival differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups. The analysis results indicated that the prognosis of patients in the high-risk group was significantly worse than that of patients in the low-risk group (p < 0.001). Furthermore, this model performed exceptionally well in predicting the 1-year, 2-year, and 3-year survival rates, with corresponding AUC values of 0.772, 0.757, and 0.705, respectively (Figure 2G). To verify the applicability of the model, we used the TCGA test set to test the KM curve, which indicated that the prognosis of patients in the high-risk group was significantly worse than that of patients in the low-risk group (p < 0.001). The areas under the ROC curves were 0.741, 0.746, and 0.698, respectively (Figure 2H). An external test of the GSE130078 test set revealed that the prognosis of patients in the high-risk group was significantly worse than that of patients in the low-risk group (p < 0.001). The areas under the ROC curves were 0.712 and 0.805, respectively (Figure 2I). We further validated the PRG signature composed of these 7 core genes in the GSE53622 dataset, and found that the prognosis of patients in the high-risk group was significantly worse than that of the low-risk group (p < 0.0001). Additionally, this model performed exceptionally well in predicting 1-year, 2-year, and 3-year survival rates, with corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.695, 0.646, and 0.730, respectively (Figure 2J). In conclusion, the prognostic model constructed on the basis of the PRGs not only demonstrated strong predictive ability in the training set but also received good validation in the external validation set, confirming that it has broad application prospects as an accurate and reliable prognostic tool.

Prediction of the biological mechanisms associated with the PGR signature

To elucidate the potential biological mechanisms underlying the adverse prognosis in high-risk and low-risk patients and to clarify heterogeneity at the molecular level, we systematically evaluated the differences in biological pathways and genomic characteristics reflected by the PGR signature in the TCGA-ESCA cohort. GO enrichment analysis revealed significant differences between the high- and low-variance groups in aspects such as T cell activation (Figure 3A). The KEGG enrichment analysis also revealed that the differentially expressed pathways were enriched in immune inflammatory pathways such as “neutrophil extracellular trap formation” and “NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity” (Figure 3B). To further investigate the specific biological differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups, GSEA was used to analyze the significantly upregulated HALLMARK pathways in different risk groups. The results revealed that in the high-risk group, the pathways enriched were “allograft rejection” (p < 0.001) “inflammatory response” (p < 0.001), and “pancreatic β-cells” (p = 0.0012) (Figure 3C), whereas in the low-risk group, they were “E2F targets” (p < 0.001), “G2/M checkpoint” (p < 0.001), and “mitotic spindle” (p < 0.001) (Figure 3D). Additionally, we conducted a correlation analysis between the activity of the HALLMARK pathways and the PGR risk score, revealing significant differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups. We found that in the high-risk group, the PGR risk score was significantly positively correlated with the inhibitory effect on the coagulation pathway, and the PGR risk score was also related to the inhibitory state of the KRAS signaling pathway activity (Figure 3E). Furthermore, we investigated the correlation between the PGR risk score and the sample mutation landscape and generated a waterfall plot to display the 10 genes with the highest mutation rates in each group (Figures 3F and 3G). We subsequently compared the differences in mutations between the two groups and reported that KDM3B, L3MBTL4, FIGN, and PKHD1L1 presented greater numbers of mutations in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group, whereas RGS12 and GPR98 presented greater numbers of mutations in the low-risk group than in the high-risk group (Figure 3H).

PRG score reshapes the immune cell infiltration landscape

Previous studies have shown that palmitoylation profoundly affects tumor immunity by regulating the stability of immune checkpoint proteins and modulating the functions of immune cells.21 However, in ESCA, the effects of palmitoylation levels on tumor immunity have not yet been studied. To comprehensively demonstrate the impact of the degree of palmitoylation on the immune landscape, we employed three different immune infiltration algorithms and conducted a detailed analysis of the differences in immune infiltration between the high-risk and low-risk groups (Figure 4A). The results showed that in the evaluation via the ESTIMATE algorithm, the high-risk group presented higher immune scores and comprehensive ESTIMATE scores (Figures 4B–4D), which seemingly indicated that the high-risk group had a more significant immune infiltration signature. The results of the CIBERSORT algorithm revealed that plasma cells and activated memory CD4 T cells were significantly enriched in the low-risk group, whereas M0 macrophages were significantly enriched in the high-risk group (Figure 4E). The results of the EPIC algorithm indicate that B cells are expressed at a relatively high level in the low-risk group, whereas NK cells are expressed at a relatively high level in the high-risk group (Figure 4F). On the basis of the results of CIBERSORT, we subsequently analyzed the correlations between key genes and immune cells and generated a correlation heatmap (Figure 4G). This study provides a new perspective for understanding the mechanism of palmitoylation in ESCA and emphasizes the importance of further exploration of its specific pathways.

A nomogram that integrates the PGR score with clinical parameters aims to achieve precise prediction

During the process of exploring the potential clinical application value of the PGR risk score, we systematically evaluated the impact of various prognosis-related variables on the survival outcomes of patients with ESCA. Through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, all independent factors significantly related to prognosis were comprehensively screened and verified. The analysis results consistently indicated that the risk score constructed on the basis of PRGs was significantly associated with the OS of patients in different models and was not affected by other clinicopathological features. This confirmed that it is an independent and robust predictor of ESCA prognosis (Figures 5A and 5B). On the basis of the above results, we constructed a clinical prediction model by combining the independent prognostic factors identified through multivariate Cox regression analysis and constructed a nomogram (Figure 5C). To evaluate the predictive accuracy of the model, we plotted calibration curves. The results showed that the nomogram exhibited good consistency in the prediction of 1-year, 2-year and 3-year OS (Figure 5D), and the predicted probabilities were highly consistent with the actual observed results. Furthermore, through DCA, it was further verified that this line graph exhibited significant clinical net benefit within a wide range of threshold probabilities, indicating its excellent potential for application in clinical decision support (Figure 5E). The time-dependent AUC analysis further confirmed that the nomogram constructed in this study demonstrated superior and more effective predictive performance than did the use of risk scores alone or other conventional clinical measurement indicators (Figure 5F).

Drug sensitivity analysis

To systematically evaluate the potential value of the PGR risk score in predicting treatment response, we integrated multiple platforms in drug sensitivity databases and conducted drug sensitivity prediction and correlation analyses. First, we analyzed the drug response data from the CTRP and PRISM databases. The CTRP results indicated that the AUC values of ouabain, KU−0063794, and tipifarnib−P1 were significantly negatively correlated with the risk score (Figures 6A and 6B). The PRISM results indicated that the AUC values of compounds BIBR−1532, lidocaine, and romidepsin were significantly negatively correlated with the risk score (Figures 6C and 6D). These findings indicate that these drugs significantly enhance sensitivity in the high-risk group. To further elucidate the functional relationship between the PGR risk score and chemotherapy drugs, we used the GDSC database to calculate the IC50 values of common chemotherapy drugs. The results revealed that patients at high risk were associated with an increase in the IC50 of drugs, including AKT inhibitors. VIII, vinorelbine, methotrexate, and cyclopamine. The higher the IC50 value, the poorer the drug sensitivity (Figures 6E–6H). Finally, on the basis of the gene expression and drug sensitivity data from CCLE, we conducted a correlation analysis to identify the drug sensitivity patterns significantly associated with PGR expression and generated a scatterplot of the 18 genes most strongly correlated with drugs (Figure 6I). Due to the heterogeneity in drug composition, cell line sources, experimental procedures, and data standardization methods among the GDSC, CTRP, and PRISM platforms, the drug sensitivity results predicted based on the same genetic characteristics may not be completely consistent. These findings reveal a complex interregulatory relationship between PRGs and the sensitivity to cancer treatment drugs. This not only provides a new perspective for understanding the mechanism of tumor resistance but also offers an important theoretical basis and guidance for the formulation of individualized clinical treatment strategies and the development of therapeutic targets in the future.

The key gene PAH is highly expressed in ESCA and is associated with poor prognosis

When a comprehensive assessment of the 7 key genes (SLC6A7, PAH, MYPN, GPR82, CCDC88C, UGT8, and HMX2) in the PRS was conducted. On the basis of the TCGA data, we found that the palmitoylation score of the disease group was greater than that of the control group and that patients with high expression of the 7 key genes (SLC6A7, PAH, MYPN, GPR82, CCDC88C, UGT8, and HMX2) in the disease group had a higher palmitoylation score (Figures 7A and 7B; Figures S2A–S2F). Next, we noticed that in the multivariate Cox regression model, the hazard ratio (HR) of PAH was second only to SLC6A7 among the 7 genes (Figure 2C). We further analyzed the association of SLC6A7 with prognosis, metastasis, and palmitoylation score. We found that although SLC6A7 was associated with poor prognosis in patients, it was not related to tumor metastasis (Figures S2G and S2H). Moreover, the association of PAH with SLC6A7 and palmitoylation score was stronger (Figure S2A). This indicates that the expression level of PAH has a strong independent predictive ability for the poor prognosis of patients. Meanwhile, we systematically reviewed the research background of the 7 genes in pan-cancer. We noticed that SLC6A7, UGT8, CCDC88C, and so forth. have been extensively studied in neuroblastoma, cervical cancer, glioma, immune regulation, and other fields.22,23,24 The research on MYPN, GPR82, and HMX2 is relatively limited, but the functional clues are relatively clear (such as being related to sarcomere structure, GPCR signaling, and development).25,26,27,28 The situation of PAH is very unique. As a phenylalanine hydroxylase, its role in metabolic diseases is already very clear, but the functional research in solid tumors (especially ESCA) is almost completely blank. Considering the importance of metabolic reprogramming in the growth and development of tumors, PAH may become a potential target. On the basis of these findings, we decided to focus on the PAH gene for further analysis to fill this research gap and explore its potential role and value in ESCA. Moreover, high PAH expression was associated with the M stage of ESCA, indicating a potential correlation with the progression of ESCA (Figure 7C). Further analysis was conducted to investigate the relationship between PAH and the prognosis of patients with ECSA. A KM curve was plotted, revealing that high expression of PAH was associated with poor prognosis (Figure 7D). At the same time, in the GEO database (GSE53622), we found that the palmitoylation score of the disease group was higher than that of the control group, and in the disease group, patients with high expression of the key gene PAH had higher palmitoylation scores (Figures 7E and 7F). Next, to further verify the significant role of PAH in ESCA, we found that, compared with those in normal esophageal epithelial cells, the mRNA and protein expression levels of PAH in ESCA cell lines (ECA109, KYSE150, KYSE410, KYSE450, and KYSE510) were significantly increased (Figures 7G and 7H). We also collected tissue samples from patients with ESCA. Through qRT‒PCR and IHC, we found that the mRNA and protein levels of PAH in tumor tissues were greater than those in normal esophageal tissues (Figures 7I and 7J).

PAH promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of ESCA cells

Next, to further clarify the biological function of PAH in the progression and prognosis of ESCA, we conducted experiments using ECA109, KYSE150, and KYSE410 cells. We knocked down PAH in ECA109 and KYSE150 cells and evaluated the knockdown effect via qRT‒PCR and western blotting (Figures 8A‒8C). Similarly, we overexpressed PAH in KYSE410 cells and evaluated the effect of PAH overexpression through qRT‒PCR and western blotting (Figures 8D and 8E). Next, we conducted cell function experiments to investigate whether PAH induces malignant transformation in ESCA cells. We performed proliferation experiments, and the results revealed that PAH knockdown inhibited the proliferation of ECA109 and KYSE150 cells, whereas PAH overexpression promoted the proliferation of KYSE410 cells (Figures 8F–8H). We also conducted a colony formation assay to further confirm these results (Figures 8I–8L). Migration and invasion have been identified as the key molecular mechanisms driving tumor metastasis in most cancer cells.29 We conducted migration and invasion experiments. Compared with those of the corresponding control cells, the number of migrating and invading cells was significantly lower in the ECA109 and KYSE150 cells transfected with si-PAH#1 or si-PAH#2 (Figures 8M–8P), whereas the number of migrating and invading cells was significantly greater in the KYSE410 cells overexpressing PAH (Figures 8Q–8T). In conclusion, PAH can promote the proliferation, migration, and invasion of esophageal cancer cells, suggesting that PAH plays an important biological role in the malignant progression and prognosis of ESCA.

The carcinogenic effect of PAH is related to its palmitoylation modification

Next, to verify whether PAH is directly regulated by S-palmitoylation, we performed an acyl-biotin exchange (ABE) assay that allows for specific labeling of palmitoyl proteins, a commonly used tool for detecting S-palmitoylation. Our results show that PAH can be palmitoylated (Figure 9A). In order to determine whether the carcinogenic effect of PAH depends on its palmitoylation modification, we used the UniProt Web tool to analyze the alignment of PAH homolog protein sequences in different species.30 We found that the amino acid sequence of PAH has a highly conserved cysteine at position 217 in different species (Figure 8B). We mutated the cysteine at position 217 to serine and constructed a PAH C217S mutant with a FLAG tag. The palmitoylation ability of this mutant was confirmed by ABE experiments. Notably, PAH palmitoylation levels were significantly reduced compared to controls after mutating cysteine to serine at position 217(Figure 9C). Subsequently, PAH C217S was overexpressed in KYSE410 cells (Figure 9D). Functional experiments showed that this mutant could not promote the in vitro proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities of KYSE410 cells, as well as the tumor growth ability in vivo (Figures 9E–9L). In conclusion, the S-palmitoylation modification at the 217th cysteine site of PAH plays a crucial role in promoting the malignant progression of ESCA; without this modification, the carcinogenic function of PAH is also lost.

PAH promotes the malignant progression of ESCA by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and promotes the proliferation of ESCA cells in vivo

Next, we further investigated the molecular mechanism by which PAH promotes the progression of ESCA. Since PAH is a key enzyme in the phenylalanine metabolic pathway, its core function is to produce tyrosine, which is an important precursor of cellular signaling molecules and functional amino acids. It can activate PI3K, thereby activating the downstream molecule AKT and promoting tumor progression.31 To verify this hypothesis, we found that the knockdown of PAH reduced the phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT in ECA109 and KYSE150 cells (Figures 10A and 10B), whereas the overexpression of PAH increased the phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT in KYSE410 cells (Figure 10C). These results indicate that PAH participates in activating the PI3K‒AKT signaling pathway in ESCA cells. To further confirm this conclusion, we treated PAH-overexpressing KYSE410 cells with specific inhibitors targeting PI3K (LY294002) or AKT (MK2206) to investigate the PI3K‒AKT pathway. Our findings showed that LY294002 and MK2206 effectively blocked the proliferation, migration, and invasion promoted by PAH in KYSE410 cells (Figures 10D–10H). To determine the tumorigenic function of PAH in vivo, a xenograft mouse model was generated via the subcutaneous injection of ECA109 cells infected with sh-PAH#1, sh-PAH#2, or sh-NC. Consistent with the in vitro results, the tumor growth rate and average tumor weight in the PAH-knockdown group were significantly lower than those in the control group (Figures 10I–10K). After injecting KYSE410 cells that were infected with vector or OE-PAH subcutaneously, the tumor growth rate and average tumor weight in the PAH gene overexpression group were significantly greater than those in the control group (Figures 10L–10N). In conclusion, our experimental results preliminarily demonstrated that PAH promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of ESCA cells by activating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway within these cells and promotes the proliferation of ESCA cells in vivo.

Discussion

ESCA, a highly heterogeneous and highly aggressive malignant tumor, still faces significant challenges in clinical diagnosis and treatment. Reliable prognostic markers and therapeutic targets are urgently needed.8,32 This study systematically explored the role of PRGs in ESCA, constructed stable PRSs, and confirmed that the core gene PAH of PRS drives the progression of ESCA through the PI3K/AKT pathway, providing a new perspective for understanding the pathogenesis and personalized treatment of ESCA.

Tumor heterogeneity is the core reason for the poor prognosis and variable treatment response of patients with ESCA.33 The traditional TNM staging system and other prognostic systems are unable to reflect molecular-level variations.34 In recent years, prognostic models based on a single omics approach (such as mRNA and methylation) have been reported. However, these methods focus on a single biological process (such as metabolic reprogramming or immune checkpoint regulation) and have limited predictive value.35,36 For example, a model based on metabolic genes was able to predict the chemotherapy sensitivity of patients with ESCA, but it was unable to distinguish the population benefiting from immunotherapy37; another immune-related feature, although related to immune infiltration, had a lower predictive efficacy for prognosis (1-year AUC = 0.72) than the PRS of this study (1-year AUC = 0.772).38 The PRS constructed in this study includes 7 PRGs, namely, SLC6A7, PAH, MYPN, GPR82, CCDC88C, UGT8 and HMX2. It demonstrated stable prognostic stratification ability in the TCGA training set, test set, and external validation set (GSE130078). The AUC value within 1–3 years reached 0.805, indicating excellent prognostic value.

Notably, the PRS divided the patients into high-risk and low-risk groups, and these two groups presented significantly different biological characteristics. The high-risk group was characterized by the enrichment of granulocyte extracellular trap formation, NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and other immune-inflammatory pathways, with an increase in M0 macrophage infiltration, whereas the low-risk group was characterized by the activation of cell cycle-related pathways (such as the G2/M checkpoint). This finding is consistent with the conclusion from previous studies that “immune microenvironment remodeling and cell cycle disruption are the key driving forces for the progression of ESCA,”39,40 suggesting that PRS can capture molecular heterogeneity with functional significance. It is worth noting that in our results, the high-risk score was negatively correlated with the activity of the KRAS signaling pathway. At first glance, this seems to contradict the oncogenic function of KRAS. However, this finding has a reasonable biological explanation. Our pathway activity score reflects the overall level of downstream transcriptional output. The negative correlation indicates that the KRAS signaling network in the high-risk patient group is in a relatively inhibited state. Studies have reported that in some cancer subtypes, if KRAS mutant tumors are accompanied by the inactivation of certain tumor suppressor genes or abnormal activation of other pathways (such as TGF-β, YAP/TAZ), the classical MAPK signaling output downstream of KRAS may not be the strongest, but the malignancy of the tumor is higher.41,42,43 This result reveals the heterogeneity and adaptive reprogramming of the signaling pathways in ESCA, providing a new perspective for understanding the prognostic differences.

Furthermore, we chose the PRG score to analyze immune regulation rather than directly using enzymes from the ZDHHC family as a substitute, because the core of our research was not to generally measure the global palmitoylation activity, but to identify the key effect molecules that are functionally regulated by palmitoylation and are directly related to the prognosis of ESCA. It is precisely the expression and functional changes of these downstream effect molecules, rather than the total amount of palmitoylating enzymes, that can more directly drive tumor heterogeneity and clinical outcomes. Moreover, the ZDHHC enzyme family has numerous members (23) and has high substrate overlap.44,45 The knockout of a single enzyme often results in a weak phenotype due to compensation by other members.46,47 In ESCA, our previous expression analysis revealed that the expression of most ZDHHC enzymes was not strongly correlated with prognosis or had inconsistent correlations, making it difficult to construct a robust and simple prognostic model.

Furthermore, in terms of drug sensitivity, the compounds such as ouabain (from the CTRP database) and BIBR-1532 (from the PRISM database) identified in this study were more sensitive in the high-risk group. Previous studies have shown that ouabain affects tumor cell metabolism by inhibiting Na+/K+-ATPase,48 whereas BIBR-1532 is a telomerase inhibitor and is mainly used in breast cancer-related research.49,50 Neither of these compounds is associated with the palmitoylation characteristics of ESCA. This study is the first to combine them with PRS risk stratification, suggesting that high-risk patients may benefit from these drugs, providing new ideas for clinical trial design. Furthermore, GDSC indicates that the average IC50 values of the AKT inhibitor.VIII, vinorelbine, methotrexate, and cyclopamine in the high-risk group were significantly higher than those in the low-risk group. A higher IC50 value indicates poorer sensitivity. Furthermore, this study found that there was some heterogeneity in the analysis results among GDSC, CTRP, and PRISM. This inconsistency might be due to differences in the composition of drug libraries, cell line sources, experimental procedures, and data processing among the databases.51 Currently, our findings are generated through bioinformatics analysis and lead to verifiable scientific hypotheses. Further verification is needed through in vitro experiments or prospective clinical data in the future.

Among the 7 core PRGs, PAH is a gene that has not been reported in the field of ESCA. The exploration of its function represents a significant innovation. Previous studies on the biological function of PAH have focused mainly on phenylalanine metabolism, such as phenylalanine hydroxylase, whose mutation leads to phenylketonuria (a metabolic disorder).52 However, in the field of oncology, only studies have suggested that PAH affects chemotherapy resistance in bladder cancer by regulating tyrosine levels, but it is not involved in ESCA.53 This study revealed that PAH is highly expressed in ESCA tissues and cell lines and is significantly associated with M-stage progression and poor prognosis. In vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that PAH promoted the proliferation, migration and invasion of ESCA cells and accelerated tumor growth and lung metastasis in mice. This finding is consistent with its physiological function as a key enzyme in phenylalanine metabolism; the tyrosine generated by PAH is a precursor of signaling molecules and functional amino acids and participates in the regulation of tumor progression-related pathways.54,55 Further research has shown that PAH promotes metastasis by regulating EMT. The knockdown of the PAH gene can reduce the migration and invasion abilities of ECA109 cells and KYSE150 cells; in contrast, the overexpression of the PAH gene has the opposite effect. EMT is the core mechanism of ESCA metastasis.56 This study is the first to associate PAH with EMT, providing new clues for understanding the molecular trigger mechanism of ESCA metastasis. The PI3K/AKT pathway is often abnormally activated in ESCA and is involved in processes such as cell survival, proliferation, and metastasis.57,58 This study confirmed that PAH can activate this pathway and that the knockdown of PAH significantly reduces the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT, whereas the overexpression of PAH increases their phosphorylation. The use of a PI3K inhibitor (LY294002) or an AKT inhibitor (MK2206) could reverse the malignant phenotype mediated by PAH, clearly indicating that the PI3K/AKT pathway is the key downstream effector pathway of PAH. This mechanism is consistent with the known functions of palmitoylation. For example, in glioblastoma, ZDHHC11 activates the PI3K/AKT pathway through palmitoylation.19 This study suggested that PAH may regulate the PI3K/AKT pathway through direct (such as protein interactions) or indirect (such as tyrosine-mediated receptor tyrosine kinase activation) mechanisms, but the specific molecular mechanism (such as whether PAH is palmitoylated and whether it directly binds to the PI3K subunit) still needs to be further explored.

Limitations of the study

Although this multidimensional predictive ability of PRS, which includes prognosis, immune status, and drug sensitivity, is expected to become a practical tool for clinical decision-making, this study still has certain limitations. First, the clinical application of the PRS model needs to be verified in larger-scale, multicenter prospective studies. Furthermore, the findings of this study are mainly based on computational biology analysis. Although we have verified the reliability of the results through various bioinformatics methods and public data, the direct positive correlation between these genes and the palmitoylation activity of ESCA cells still needs to be further confirmed through subsequent experiments. Second, in addition to PAH, the functions of other core PRGs (such as CCDC88C and GPR82) in ESCA and their association with palmitoylation are not yet clear. Third, the specific molecular mechanism by which PAH activate the PI3K/AKT pathway (such as whether they directly bind to PI3K subunits or regulate their palmitoylation modification) is not yet clear and needs to be explored in depth through experiments such as coimmunoprecipitation and palmitoylation modification detection. Fourthly, although this study was validated through an external validation cohort (GSE130078) and obtained statistically significant results, we must clearly point out its limitations. The most significant point is that the external validation cohort (GSE130078) available for analysis has a relatively small sample size (n = 23). Although the PGR risk score still demonstrated significant prognostic discrimination ability in this cohort (p < 0.05), this result preliminarily supports the generalizability of the model. However, the small sample size inevitably affects the statistical power and limits our ability to conduct more in-depth subgroup analyses (such as interaction analyses based on clinical stage or treatment type) within this cohort. Therefore, the current external validation should be regarded as a preliminary piece of evidence. Finally, animal experiments using nude mouse models, which lack a complete immune system, may not fully reflect the interaction between PAH and the tumor microenvironment. The impact of PAH on immune escape in ESCA still needs to be verified in mouse models with normal immune function. Future research could focus on the following: First, the prognostic value of the PRS could be verified through multicenter clinical samples, and treatment plans could be optimized on the basis of treatment response data. Second, the roles of other PRGs in ESCA were analyzed, and the collaborative regulatory mechanism of the palmitoylation-related network was clarified. Third, the molecular interactions between PAH and the PI3K/AKT pathway were explored, and combined treatment strategies targeting PAH or the PI3K/AKT pathway were developed. Finally, it is necessary to conduct a robust validation of this prognostic model in a larger-scale, multi-center, and prospective design cohort of patients with esophageal cancer using a standardized detection platform. Only in this way can its independent clinical prognostic value be ultimately established, and its role in guiding individualized treatment decisions be evaluated. Furthermore, the model constructed in this study demonstrates potential in the stratification of ESCA prognosis and can serve as a supplementary tool for the existing clinical staging. In the future, it may guide individualized treatment. However, its clinical translation faces multiple challenges, such as the need to confirm its independent predictive value through large-scale prospective studies. These steps are crucial for realizing its transition from laboratory to routine clinical application.

Method details

Data collection and processing

The ESCA dataset was downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database59 (http://cancergenome.nih.gov). Among them, the TCGA-ESCA dataset includes 170 patient cases with complete prognostic information. Moreover, the somatic mutation data of ESCA patients were obtained from cBioPortal (www.cbioportal.org). The ESCA-related dataset GSE130078 (n = 23) and dataset GSE53622 (n = 119) was downloaded from the GEO database (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) via the GEOquery package.60,61 Next, we perform normalization on the data and then standardize it via the limma package for subsequent analysis.62 The ESCA single-cell data were obtained from the GEO database under the identifier GSE188900 (tumor n = 7, normal n = 1).63

Processing of single-cell transcriptome data

This study conducted a comprehensive analysis of single-cell sequencing data via the “Seurat” R package (version 4).64 First, the quality control steps, which include selecting cells that are expressed in at least three cells, ensure that each cell expresses 200 to 7,500 genes, and at the same time, the proportion of mitochondrial genes should not exceed 20%. After standardization, a total of 51,306 high-quality cells were identified. We subsequently carried out data integration, identification of highly variable genes (HVGs), and principal component analysis (PCA). On the basis of the PCA results, the “FindClusters” and “FindNeighbors” functions were used to cluster the cells into different clusters. To accurately annotate these cell clusters, we referred to the classic cell markers “CD3D” and “CD3E”, corresponding to T cells; “NKG7”, corresponding to NK cells; “BANK1” and “CD79A”, corresponding to B cells; “MZB1” and “IGJ”, corresponding to plasma cells; “S100A8” and “FCGR3B”, corresponding to neutrophils; “LYZ” and “MS4A6A”, corresponding to monocytic cells; “GATA2” and “KIT”, corresponding to mast cells; “VWF” and “ICAM1”, corresponding to endothelial cells; “LUM” and “THY1”, corresponding to fibroblasts; “KRT5” and “KRT6A”, “EPCAM”, and “KRT7”, corresponding to epithelial cells; and “ACTA2”, corresponding to smooth muscle cells.

Palmitoylation AUCell score

We obtained the gene set related to palmitoylation from the relevant literature, The genes related to palmitoylation are 23 in number (Table S1).65,66 AUCell is a single-cell RNA sequencing analysis algorithm used to evaluate the activity levels of specific gene sets in individual cells. Through this algorithm, we can estimate the palmitoylation activity of cells in the ESCA single-cell data, and thereby identify the genes related to palmitoylation in ESCA.67,68 Next, to assess the heterogeneity of palmitoylation in different cells and identify palmitoylation-related genes in ESCA, we used the “AUCell” R package to calculate the palmitoylation activity score for each individual cell based on the 23 palmitoylation-related genes.69 Next, we conducted a correlation analysis to identify and present the genes that were positively correlated and had a p value < 0.05. On the basis of the median AUCell score, we classified the cells into high-palmitoylation-activity groups and low-palmitoylation-activity groups. Furthermore, we also conducted differential expression analysis to identify the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the high palmitoylation activity group and the low palmitoylation activity group.

Construction and validation of the PGR prognostic model

To construct a prognostic risk model based on PRGs, we randomly divided the TCGA data into a training set and a test set at a 7:3 ratio. Univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted on 793 candidate palmitoylation genes identified from single-cell transcriptome data, and genes significantly associated with overall survival in ESCA patients (p value <0.05) were selected. Subsequently, by combining least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and multivariate Cox regression analysis,70 the candidate genes were dimensionally reduced, and their coefficients were estimated. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined through cross-validation, and the genes with nonzero regression coefficients were retained as the final risk gene set. Finally, the risk score formula is calculated on the basis of the expression of multivariate Cox genes and the estimated Cox regression coefficients:

Patients are classified into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the given risk score. The Kaplan‒Meier (KM) analysis and log rank test were conducted via the survival package to analyze the overall survival (OS) rate of the test set. Furthermore, we employed time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to evaluate survival predictions and used the timeROC R package to calculate the area under the ROC curve (AUC) value to measure the prognostic or predictive accuracy.71 To enhance the diagnostic capability of this prognostic model, an external validation was conducted in GSE130078. The validation results revealed that the KM survival curve also demonstrated significant prognostic ability, and the trend in the ROC-AUC was consistent with that of the training set. This further confirmed the robustness and generalization performance of the PGR risk signals.

Functional enrichment analysis

To identify the specific enriched biological pathways in the high-risk and low-risk groups, we implemented a comprehensive analysis strategy. Specifically, via the R package “clusterProfiler”, the selected core genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses to explore in detail the functional roles of these genes in cellular processes and the biochemical metabolic pathways in which they are involved.72 Furthermore, on the basis of the differential expression profiles between the high-risk and low-risk groups, we applied gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to identify the significantly enriched gene sets or pathways in the high-risk and low-risk groups, aiming to reveal the potential molecular mechanisms and differences in signal transduction pathways. Furthermore, the gene set variation analysis (GSVA) method was employed. The “GSVA” package was utilized to assess the changes in the activity of hallmark pathways in the high-risk and low-risk groups.73

Tumor mutational burden (TMB)

On the basis of the somatic mutation data of the TCGA cohort, we used the R package “maftools” to calculate the tumor mutational burden (TMB) of each sample.74 Through the waterfall plot generated by maftools, we systematically depicted the mutation panorama of genes related to the high- and low-risk groups and visually presented the mutation frequency, type, and heterogeneity among samples. On this basis, we further analyzed the mutation differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups and compared the mutations in different groups.

Immune infiltration analysis

To systematically evaluate the infiltration status of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) of ESCA patients, this study utilized the IOBR package to integrate three mainstream computational methods—CIBERSORT, EPIC, and ESTIMATE—for multidimensional inference of the immune landscape.75 Through multiple-algorithm cross-validation, the robustness and reliability of the immune infiltration assessment were enhanced. The obtained results were visually integrated via the R package “ComplexHeatmap” to present the heterogeneity of immune cell composition in different samples in an intuitive manner.76

Construction of the nomogram

To evaluate the independent prognostic value of the PGR risk score, we conducted univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses.51 The results showed that this score was an independent predictor of OS. Moreover, its correlation with the main clinical and pathological features was analyzed to verify its applicability. On the basis of the three independent prognostic factors of the PGR risk score, N stage and M stage, a nomogram for predicting 1-year, 2-year and 3-year outcomes was constructed. The good predictive consistency and clinical practicability were confirmed through calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA). ROC analysis further demonstrated that the AUC of the PGR risk score was superior to that of traditional clinical variables and that it exhibited better risk stratification capabilities.

Drug response prediction based on multisource drug sensitivity data and core gene‒drug association analysis

To systematically evaluate the potential value of the PGR risk score in predicting treatment response, we integrated multiple platform drug sensitivity databases and conducted drug sensitivity prediction and correlation analyses. Using the ridge regression model in the R package “pRRophetic”, the response of each sample to the drug was inferred on the basis of the gene expression profile. The drug sensitivity data are derived from two independent large-scale functional genomics resources: the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP; https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp) and PRISM (Profiling Relative Inhibition Simultaneously, in Mixtures; https://depmap.org/portal/prism). The intensity of the drug response was quantified by the area under the dose‒response curve (AUC). The lower the AUC value is, the more sensitive the cell line is to the drug. First, we compared the differences in drug responses between the high-risk and low-risk groups and identified the compounds that showed significant sensitization in the high-risk group. Spearman correlation analysis was subsequently used to evaluate the associations between palmitoylation scores and the AUC values of each drug. Special attention was given to the drugs that showed a significant negative correlation (Spearman’s r < −0.25) in both databases, suggesting that they may have greater therapeutic potential in high-risk patients. To further elucidate the functional relationship between core genes and chemotherapy drugs, we utilized the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC; https://www.cancerrxgene.org) database to calculate the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of commonly used chemotherapy drugs. Moreover, on the basis of gene expression and drug sensitivity data from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE; https://sites.broadinstitute.org/ccle), relevant analyses were conducted to identify drug sensitivity patterns significantly associated with the expression of related genes, and the most promising gene‒drug pairs were selected on the basis of statistical significance (p value).

Clinical sample collection

The EC samples (n = 6) utilized for immunohistochemistry and qRT‒PCR analyses were surgical samples obtained from patients who underwent thoracic surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Approval No. (2023)CDYFYYLK(01-074)). All the samples were collected with the patients' informed consent. The detailed specimen collection procedures were as follows: First, the specimen was examined to determine the location and extent of the tumor, with careful distinction between surrounding tissues and necrotic tissues. Within 10 min of being excised from the body, the specimens were sectioned. Both cancerous and adjacent tissues were cut into small pieces approximately 1 cm in diameter, placed into numbered sterile cryovials, and immediately stored in a liquid nitrogen tank.77

Transfection

In the transfection experiment, the plasmids used were purchased from Miaoling Biotechnology Company (Wuhan, China). First, the cells were seeded in the culture plates. When the cell density reached 50–60%, transfection began. Next, 2 μg of the plasmid (miaolingbio, China) and 4 μL of the transfection reagent (PUFEI BIOTECH, China) were separately mixed with 250 μL of Opti-MEM (Gibco, USA). The plasmid mixture and the transfection reagent mixture were mixed evenly, incubated at room temperature for 20 min, and then the mixture was added to the cells and cultured for 6 h. After the transfection was completed, the culture medium was replaced with a complete normal culture medium, and the cells were cultured for another 48 h.

Establishment of a stable cell line

First, we constructed lentiviral vectors, including those carrying sh-PAH#1, sh-PAH#2, and sh-Control, as well as OE-Vector, OE-PAH-Flag and OE-PAH C217S-Flag. Next, the target plasmids, along with pMD2. G and psPAX2, were cotransfected into 293T cells via SuperFectin II DNA transfection reagent (PUFEI BIOTECH, China) to generate the viral mixture. ECA109 and KEYS150 cells were cultured in 6-well plates. When the cell confluence reached 40–50%, each well was supplemented with 1 mL of PAH-knockdown lentiviral vector and 1 mL of complete medium, followed by incubation for 36 h. Thereafter, the cells were cultured in medium supplemented with puromycin (Servicebio, China) to establish stable PAH-knockdown cell lines. Similarly, when the KEYS410 cells reached 40–50% confluence, 1 mL of PAH-overexpressing or 1 mL of PAH C217S-Flag overexpression lentiviral vector and 1 mL of complete medium were added to each well, and the mixture was incubated for 36 h. These cells were then maintained in medium supplemented with puromycin (Servicebio, China) to establish stable PAH or PAH C217S-Flag overexpressing cell lines. Finally, the expression level of PAH or PAH C217S-Flag in the cells was detected by Western blotting.

Western blotting

The cells and freshly isolated tissue samples were immediately immersed in sample buffer consisting of Tris–HCl (pH 6.8), 4% SDS, 0.006% bromophenol blue, 10% glycerol, and 2% β-mercaptoethanol.78 Western blotting, an immunoassay method primarily utilized for qualitative and quantitative detection of target protein expression in cells or tissues, was subsequently performed. Following heat denaturation at 100 °C for 10 min, equal quantities of protein were separated via SDS‒PAGE. These proteins were then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes at 4 °C at a constant voltage of 110 V for 1 h. The membranes were cut into sections on the basis of the molecular weights of the target proteins and incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti-PAH (1:500; Proteintech, China), anti-GAPDH (1:10000; Proteintech, China), anti-AKT (1:1000; CST, USA), anti-p-AKT (1:1000; CST, USA), anti-PI3K (1:1000; CST, USA), anti-Flag (1:5000; Proteintech, China) and anti-p-PI3K (1:1000; CST, USA). After overnight incubation at 4 °C, the blots were rinsed three times, each for 10 min, with TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20. Next, the blots were incubated at room temperature for 1 h with secondary antibodies: a goat anti-rabbit polyclonal antibody at a dilution of 1:3000 was used for PAH, AKT, p-AKT, PI3K, and p-PI3K, while a goat anti-mouse polyclonal antibody at a dilution of 1:5000 was applied for GAPDH. The blots were then washed three times with the aforementioned buffer, each lasting 10 min, followed by a 1 min incubation in an enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagent (YEASEN, China). Finally, the blots were visualized via a Gel Doc XR + imaging system (Bio-Rad).

qRT‒PCR

RNA isolation from cells was conducted via TRIzol universal reagent (TIANGEN, China) following the standard protocol provided by the manufacturer. For the synthesis of complementary DNA (cDNA), 1 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed with the PrimeScript RT reagent kit supplemented with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan). The resulting cDNA product was subjected to a 10-fold dilution using RNase-free distilled water. Quantitative analysis was carried out with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Takara, Japan) on the ABI7500 platform (Thermo Fisher, USA). Each target gene in each sample was assayed in triplicate. GAPDH served as the internal reference gene for normalization. The specific sequences of primers used were as follows: PAH-F: GCTTGGGCAGGAAACTCTCT; PAH-R: ACTTTGGCCAATGCACCAAC; GAPDH-F: ACACCCACTCCTCCACCTTTG; GAPPDH-R: TCCACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAG.

Cell proliferation assay

A 24-well plate was used for cell seeding, with 4000 cells inoculated into each well, followed by the addition of 0.5 mL of culture medium supplemented with 10% FBS per well. After 8–12 h of incubation to allow cell adhesion, the 24-well plate was removed, and the corresponding data were recorded for the day 0 group. The data collected at 48 h, 96 h, and 144 h were designated the day 2, day 4, and day 6 groups, respectively. The culture medium was changed every other day. At room temperature, the cells were fixed with 500 μL of 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and then each well was stained with 200 μL of 1% crystal violet solution for 1 min at room temperature. The cells were gently rinsed with running water until the liquid in the wells became clear. At predetermined time points (days 2, 4, and 6) during the experiment, the 24-well plates were collected, and the cells in the plates were processed following the aforementioned experimental procedures. Once all 24-well plates were dried, 200 μL of 10% acetic acid solution was added to each well. The acetic acid solution containing dissolved crystal violet was transferred from the 24-well plate to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance at a wavelength of 595 nm was measured to assess cell proliferation.

Colony formation assay

The cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 10000 cells per well and maintained in culture for 7 consecutive days, with the medium replaced every 2 days. At the designated time points, the cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and stained with 0.05% crystal violet. Finally, the number of colonies was observed and analyzed.

Transwell assay

For the invasion assay, 80,000 cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper compartment of experimental plates precoated with Matrigel (Biozellen, USA). The lower chamber was filled with medium supplemented with 10% FBS. ECA109, KYSE150 and KYSE410 cells were incubated for 36 h. For the migration assay, the bottom chamber contained medium supplemented with 10% FBS, while the upper chamber contained 80,000 cells in medium supplemented with 1% FBS. ECA109, KYSE150 and KYSE410 cells were cultured for 36 h. After the respective incubation periods, the cells that had migrated to the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and stained with 1% crystal violet at the designated time points. The number of stained cells was quantified via ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Tumor samples from patients were processed into tissue sections, and IHC staining was performed using an anti-PAH antibody (Proteintech, China) to assess the expression levels of PAH in the tissues. The specific procedures were as follows: the tissue sections were dehydrated and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval. Subsequently, 6% hydrogen peroxide was applied to block endogenous peroxidase activity. After the sections were washed three times with PBS, they were incubated with anti-PAH antibody at 4°C overnight. The sections were then washed again with PBS and incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h. The cell nuclei were stained with hematoxylin solution, and images were captured for analysis.

Acyl-biotin exchange assay

The acyl-biotin exchange (ABE) assay was performed to detect protein palmitoylation as previously described by Guo H et al.79 The cells were lysed at 4°C for 2 h. The lysis buffer contained protease inhibitors (without EDTA, diluted with 100×DMSO) (Servicebio, China) and 50 mM N-ethylmaleimide (MCE, China). After centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 4°C, 10 min), the supernatant was collected and incubated with the corresponding antibodies and beads (MCE, China) at room temperature for 2 h to purify the target protein. The purified beads were resuspended in pH 7.2 lysis buffer and divided into two groups: one group was added with hydroxylamine (+HAM) (MCE, China), and the other group was not (−HAM). Both groups were rotated incubated at room temperature for 1 h, and then washed three times with pH 7.2 and pH 6.2 lysis buffers respectively. The beads were incubated with 2 μM Biotin-HPDP (MCE, China) at 4°C for 1 h, and then washed with pH 6.2 buffer. Finally, the beads were resuspended in 1×SDS-PAGE loading buffer and boiled at 95°Cfor 10 min, and the level of palmitoylation of the target protein was detected by streptavidin-HRP immunoblotting.

Establishment of a xenograft tumor model

After thirty 4-week-old BALB/c-nu mice arrived at the laboratory, they were randomly divided into 5 groups, with 6 mice in each group. KYSE410 cells (5 × 106) carrying an empty vector (OE-Vector), PAH overexpression construct (OE-PAH-Flag), or PAH-C217S-Flag overexpression construct (OE-PAH C217S-Flag) were injected subcutaneously into the axillary region of the mice. Similarly, 5 × 106 ECA109 cells harboring an empty vector (sh-Control) or PAH-knockdown construct (sh-PAH#1, sh-PAH#2) were also injected subcutaneously into the axillary region of the mice. The body weights and tumor volumes (V = 0.5 × length × width2) of the mice were measured every three days. After 28 days, the nude mice were sacrificed, and the tumor tissues were extracted and collected for subsequent studies. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Approval No. CDYFY-IACUC-202407QR033). All procedures strictly adhered to the regulations outlined in the “Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” established by the Institute of Laboratory Animals.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data processing and analysis were conducted via R software (version 4.2.3). For the comparison of two groups of continuous variables, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to analyze the differences between the variables. Survival analysis and survival curve generation were performed via the survival package in R, and the survminer package was used to find the optimal cutoff value for the log rank test (log rank test). Both univariate and multivariate Cox analyses were based on all the statistical p values from the survival R package. Differences between groups were calculated by ANOVA or Student’s t test where appropriate. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. All p values were two-tailed, and those with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
