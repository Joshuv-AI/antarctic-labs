I am excited to write this month’s editorial on multimodality cardiovascular imaging (MMI). This is a favorite topic of mine and something I have been involved with for over 2 decades (excuse the self-citations).1, 2, 3, 4, 5 I was fortunate to have received excellent cardiovascular imaging skills from my mentors ([echo] Masood Ahmad, David Weiner, Ioannis (John) Panidis, William C. Reeves, Navin C. Nanda, and more; [nuclear] Marvin Kronenberg, Chris Hansen, Assad Movahd, and more) and was comfortable starting my career in a clinical practice that incorporated both radiotracers (nuclear SPECT) and ultrasound (echocardiography). At that ancient, undisclosed time, I recall being an early career faculty (hint: Dr. Julius M. Gardin was the ASE president) who was told they should choose between either echocardiography or nuclear cardiology, but “don’t try to be an expert at both.” Looking back, I am certain that this advice was sound and based upon the risk of becoming a so-called ‘jack of all trades, master of none.’

However, instead of following that recommendation, I witnessed the evolution of magnets (magnetic resonance imaging; MRI) and x-rays (cardiac computed tomography; CCT) being used to expand our noninvasive understanding of cardiovascular anatomy and physiology and jumped on that early departing train. Since there were limited training programs in the US, I obtained an overseas sabbatical at the Royal Brompton and Harefield hospital in London, England under the mentorship of Professor Dudley Pennell, where I developed a deep appreciation for cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR). Later, I gained additional skills and expertise from my colleagues using CCT to perform noninvasive coronary angiography (initially using 16-slice scanners with practiced long breath holds). It was my recollection of that primitive approach to noninvasive coronary angiography that helped me select this month’s song lyrics by the J. Geils Band.

If you are active on any of the various educational social media outlets, you are certainly familiar with the concept of #EchoFirst.6 This has become a very popular hashtag that immediately conjures a vision of where echocardiography should be considered in a noninvasive, multimodality diagnostic algorithm of cardiovascular disease assessment: #EchoFirst. Other popular hashtags in this field include “#whyCMR”, “#yesCCT”, and #spect” which have their own subliminal messaging.

A detailed discussion on the value of MMI for the echocardiographer is not possible in this short editorial, but a few central concepts from my experience over the past few years may offer some insights for those taking the time to read this. A preeminent concept that I use nearly every day in my teaching institution is that (1) MMI makes you a better echocardiographer. There are many echo experts, but the very best have enhanced their interpretive abilities by participating in complementary imaging using MMI. I have expanded my diagnostic insights during echo reading simply by having an expanded 3D anatomic understanding of the heart. Another concept is that no matter how skilled you may become at echocardiography, there are limitations that cannot be overcome with ultrasound alone. For example, (2) echocardiography can not see everything. Even though we are expanding our ability to use echo to look at the air-filled lung parenchyma, incorporating a review of correlative x-rays to your developing lung ultrasound skills will greatly improve your confidence in acquisition of these non-cardiac findings.

Another somewhat humbling concept that took me a long time to accept is that (3) semi-quantitative assessment of valve regurgitation with Doppler echocardiography is complicated and at risk of both over- and under-estimation with minor measurement errors. Having alternative imaging technologies to compare to has been insightful and offers echocardiographers critical feedback on their assessment techniques. CMR findings matched to clinical outcomes are also driving us to reconsider certain thresholds of regurgitation severity. Finally, although it was always known to this echocardiographer that (4) there are some cardiovascular anatomic loopholes (e.g. we can’t see the entire aorta), I was fairly confident that I could see the entire myocardium. Then along comes CMR which demonstrated that (5) we were missing the LV apex at a surprising frequency.7 This knowledge resulted in a greater appreciation for the expanded use of the administration of an ultrasound enhancing agent and reiterates the initial point I wanted to make in this editorial: CMR makes us better at echo.

There are many more day-to-day examples of how MMI improves our echo reading skills. I am excited that this month’s issue of CASE is a Focus Issue on MULTIMODALITY IMAGING/ Complementary Role of Magnets, X-rays, and Isotopes. Be sure to read the entire June issue to advance your appreciation and understanding of the value of MMI to enhance your echocardiographic skills. You don’t have to be expert in CMR or CCT or nuclear SPECT or PET-CT to benefit from comparing your echo findings with those from other CV imaging modalities. You simply need to keep an open mind that echo has inherent limitations that benefit from alternative imaging and that your echo skills will improve if you dedicate time to review MMI.

In Multimodality Imaging of Two Unique Etiologies of Supravalvular Aortic Stenosis, these authors present supravalvular stenosis from congenital and iatrogenic etiologies and emphasize that they used echocardiography to assess severity, while they used CCT to provide diagnostic clarity.

In Multimodality Imaging of Acute Stanford Type B Dissection during Balloon Angioplasty for Native Aortic Coarctation, these authors used Doppler echocardiography to diagnose severe coarctation, used CCT to guide intervention, and periprocedural invasive angiography to detect an acute aortic dissection.

In A Rare Intracardiac Manifestation of an Aggressive Malignant Peripheral Nerve Sheath Tumor in a Young Adult, these authors used echocardiography and CMR to distinguish tumor from thrombus.

In Echocardiography Finds an Unusual Cause of Atrial Fibrillation, these authors beautifully demonstrate the complementary value of CCT to determine the origin and venous extension of an echocardiography-detected left atrial mass.

In Uncovering a Hidden Hilar Mass through Routine Pulmonary Venous Imaging on Transesophageal Echocardiogram, these authors also emphasized the complementary value of CT to Doppler echocardiography-detected abnormal pulmonary venous flow.

In The Hidden Constriction: Middle Aortic Syndrome Presenting as Acute Systolic Heart Failure in a 7-Month-Old Infant, these authors remind us that MMI is equally important in the pediatric population. In their report, they used echo to detect a severe cardiomyopathy in an infant with a recorded BP of 159/128mm Hg. Subsequently, CT angiography revealed middle aortic syndrome as the likely underlying pathology.

In Primary Mitral Valve Sarcoma Mimicking Infective Endocarditis, these authors reiterated that not all mitral valve masses are caused by endocarditis and included a discussion on how MMI can enhance tissue characterization and assess metabolic activity.

In Anatomically Corrected Malposition of the Great Arteries in an Adult with Left Juxtaposed Atrial Appendages and Anomalous Left Main Coronary Artery Origin, these authors highlight the central role of echocardiography in diagnosing complex congenital heart disease. They also stress the complementary importance of CCT to plan surgical intervention.

In Congenital Left Coronary Artery-to-Right Ventricle Fistula in a Dog, we are reminded that MMI does not only apply to human diagnostics. These veterinarian authors combined their findings on echo Doppler with CT to diagnose a rare coronary anomaly.

In summary, having a working knowledge of MMI is critical to becoming the best echocardiographer you can become. For this reason, the American Society of Echocardiography commonly includes MMI on many of our most important guideline documents (see Suggested Reading list below).

Thanks for reading this month’s editorial.
