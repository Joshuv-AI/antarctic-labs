Blood-based targeted sequencing of microbial cell-free DNA in severe pneumonia-associated sepsis
Authors: Hao Huang, Xiangrong Ye, Dejian Gu, Enpu Huang, Xuegao Yu, Lu Ai, Jiankai Deng, Penghao Guo, Hao Liu, Yili Chen, Ruizhi Wang, Yifeng Luo, Peisong Chen

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) improves pathogen detection in severe pneumonia-related sepsis, but sampling is invasive and prone to false-positive results. Blood is easier to obtain, and broad-spectrum targeted NGS (tNGS) of microbial cell-free DNA may offer a practical alternative to BALF-based testing. We evaluated the diagnostic and prognostic value of blood-based bstNGS.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort, 122 adults with suspected severe pneumonia-related sepsis and paired BALF and blood samples underwent BALF-mNGS, blood-bstNGS and blood-mNGS. Pathogens were adjudicated using a composite clinical reference. We assessed blood-BALF concordance, compared diagnostic performance across methods, and examined whether blood-bstNGS could down-weight likely false-positive BALF-only detections and stratify prognosis.ResultsBALF-mNGS identified 414 microorganisms; 51% were adjudicated as causative or possibly causative, corresponding to 85.24% of patients. Among these pathogenic microorganisms, blood-bstNGS detected 45.02%, significantly more than blood-mNGS (22.27%), and nearly all pathogens detected by blood-mNGS were also detected by blood-bstNGS. Against the clinical reference, blood-bstNGS showed higher sensitivity (63.46%) than blood-mNGS (35.58%), conventional microbiological tests (CMTs) (49.04%), and blood culture (9.62%). Organisms detected only in BALF but not in blood were less likely to be classified as causative.Patients with concordant blood-bstNGS and BALF-mNGS profiles had significantly lower 30-day and 90-day mortality.ConclusionsIn severe pneumonia-related sepsis, blood-bstNGS provides sensitive, non-invasive pathogen detection. It acts as a complementary tool rather than a replacement for BALF-mNGS, offering an important diagnostic alternative when BALF is unavailable and improving specificity and prognostic utility when used in combination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03786-0.

Background

Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome of dysregulated host response to infection and remains a leading cause of death worldwide [1–3]. Rapid identification of the infectious focus and the causative pathogen is central to effective management, enabling timely source control and optimization of antimicrobial therapy [1]. In patients with severe pneumonia - a major precipitant of sepsis - the lower respiratory tract is commonly interrogated by bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) sampling coupled with metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), which provides hypothesis-free detection across bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites [4–7]. Despite its breadth, BALF mNGS has practical and interpretive limitations. Bronchoscopy can be logistically constrained or contraindicated in unstable patients, and repeated sampling to monitor infection dynamics is seldom feasible [8]. In addition, the complex microbial milieu of the airways, together with specimen handling and library preparation artifacts, can yield spurious or clinically irrelevant detections that complicate antimicrobial decisions [9, 10].

In parallel, plasma-based pathogen detection has gained momentum as a minimally invasive alternative that can be performed serially. However, unbiased blood mNGS faces a high host-to-pathogen nucleic acid ratio and low microbial biomass, which together limit analytical sensitivity and inflate background noise [11–13]. Targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) approaches - typically using probe hybridization capture or multiplex PCR panels to enrich microbial DNA - can improve signal-to-noise by concentrating sequences from predefined, clinically relevant taxa while suppressing host reads [14–16]. Early studies suggest that targeted enrichment increases on-target microbial yield and may enhance diagnostic performance in respiratory infections [17–20]. Yet, rigorous evaluation of blood-based tNGS in patients with severe pneumonia-associated sepsis remains limited.

These knowledge gaps are clinically consequential. First, when BALF cannot be obtained in a timely fashion or when longitudinal pathogen surveillance is needed, clinicians require evidence on whether blood-based assays can substitute for, or complement, BALF mNGS without sacrificing etiologic accuracy. Second, because BALF mNGS can report numerous organisms of uncertain significance, a principled strategy to separate true pathogens from colonizers and contaminants is essential to avoid unnecessary or overly broad antimicrobial therapy [9, 10]. Orthogonal confirmation from a second anatomical compartment offers a biologically plausible filter: organisms causing severe pneumonia with systemic inflammatory responses exhibit increased vascular permeability, facilitating the shedding of detectable microbial cell-free DNA (mcfDNA) into the bloodstream, whereas airway-limited colonizers may not [21, 22]. Finally, within blood-based methods themselves, the relative performance of tNGS versus unbiased mNGS has not been clarified in this population, leaving uncertainty about the optimal assay when blood is chosen as the testing matrix.

To address these issues, we conducted a retrospective evaluation in patients with severe pneumonia-associated sepsis who had paired BALF and blood specimens.

Blood samples were analyzed by a broad-spectrum tNGS (bstNGS) assay targeting microbial cfDNA via probe capture, and an mNGS assay. BALF underwent mNGS according to institutional protocols. We compared (i) the concordance of blood bstNGS with BALF mNGS for pathogen detection; (ii) the diagnostic performance of blood bstNGS versus blood mNGS; and (iii) the utility of blood-based results to adjudicate likely false-positive organisms reported by BALF mNGS. If validated, these findings would support a pragmatic strategy in which blood bstNGS serves as a feasible substitute when BALF sampling is impractical and as a complementary check to enhance the interpretability of BALF mNGS, ultimately improving the accuracy and stewardship of pathogen-directed care in critically ill patients.

Methods

Sample enrollment and standard-of-care microbiology testing

We retrospectively included 183 patients with suspected severe pneumonia-associated sepsis treated in the intensive care unit (ICU) of The First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University, from February 2022 to February 2024.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for severe pneumonia; (2) concurrent diagnosis of sepsis defined by the Sepsis-3 criteria (suspected infection accompanied by an acute change in sequential organ failure assessment [SOFA] score ≥ 2); (3) aged 18 years or older; (4) having available plasma samples and paired BALF samples collected within 24 h; and (5) availability of clinical information.

Blood NGS was specifically indicated in this cohort for patients exhibiting signs of severe systemic inflammation where secondary bloodstream dissemination was highly suspected. The diagnosis of pneumonia was based on the Chinese guidelines for diagnosing and treating of pneumonia patients in adults [23, 24]. In this study, blood samples from the same batch were subjected to mNGS, bstNGS, and culture. BALF samples were subjected to mNGS. Although BALF mNGS is not routinely performed as a universal standard of care for all patients at our hospital, it was systematically conducted for all patients in this study cohort as part of their comprehensive microbiological workup and as a reference for antibiotic treatment, while the tNGS detection was performed on aliquots stored at -80 °C that have not been subjected to secondary freeze-thawing. Notably, the composite reference standard was established primarily based on BALF mNGS and CMT results, serving as the gold standard for evaluating the diagnostic performance of other assays. Additionally, conventional microbiological testing (CMT) is defined by the results of standard of care (SOC) tests and multiple cultures obtained within the last 48 h, including sputum, BALF, and blood culture results (Fig. 1). All CMT was performed at The First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. The Clinical Laboratory Department of the hospital has been rated as a National Key Clinical Specialty by the Ministry of Health of China.

Clinical diagnosis

In this study, the final clinical diagnosis was comprehensively assessed by two experienced ICU clinicians, each holding the title of associate chief physician or higher, and possessing over a decade of ICU experience. Disagreements were resolved by a third senior intensivist. Subsequently, the diagnoses underwent further review by specialists in respiratory and laboratory medicine; any persistent discrepancies were resolved through multidisciplinary consensus meetings. Referring to Fourgeaud et al. [25], the diagnosis primarily considered the patients’ clinical symptoms, infection markers, pathological information, imaging findings, results of SOC tests (including smear, culture, serology, and PCR-based tests [Daan Gene, Guangzhou, China; Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA]), NGS results, and other relevant factors (SFig 1). In cases of discordant results between conventional methods and NGS (e.g., culture-positive but NGS-negative), causality was adjudicated based on a holistic assessment of clinical symptoms, new infiltrates on chest imaging, and systemic inflammatory markers. Pathogens detected in BALF were judged as causative if their detection temporally aligned with these acute clinical deterioration, whereas typical oral flora or low-abundance detections without compatible clinical signs were deemed non-causative colonizers. Based on the clinical diagnosis, the diagnostic value of bstNGS and other methods was evaluated. Microorganisms were further subdivided into causative, possibly causative, or unlikely to be responsible for the symptoms.

bstNGS assay workflow and mNGS steps

The bstNGS testing workflow is consistent with that described in previous research [20, 26] and showed in Supplementary methods. In brief, mcfDNA were extracted from sterile body fluids using an extraction kit (VAMNE Magnetic Pathogen DNA/RNA kit, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), followed by the construction of a pre-library with a library construction kit (HieffNGS®C37P4 One Pot cDNA&gDNA Library Prep Kit, Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, the library was incubated with Geneplus probes (cover 1,872 microbial, as shown in STable 1) for 4 h to complete the library construction using the Gene+GIN96 (GenePlus-Suzhou, Suzhou, China) platform. Sequencing was performed on the Gene + Seq-100 platform with a sequencing depth of 5 M reads. The generated data were processed through a self-developed automated analysis solution (Gene + P-Seq-Tool Kits, GenePlus-Suzhou, Suzhou, China). Finally, reads aligned to individual organisms were normalized based on 1 million total sample reads to generate the reads per million (RPM) metric. Microorganisms with RPM exceeding a specified threshold were selected for further result interpretation. The threshold was set at RPM ≥ 6 for common pathogens (excluding mycobacteria) and ≥ 0.5 for fungi and mycobacteria.

In accordance with previous mNGS studies [27, 28], microbial nucleic acids were extracted from BALF and blood using dedicated extraction kits (TIANamp Micro DNA Kit, DP316, TIANGEN BIOTECH, Beijing, China). Individual sequencing libraries were constructed using library construction kits. Sequencing was then carried out on the Illumina NextSeq 550 platform (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA), with a sequencing depth of 20 M reads for each sample. The data were ultimately processed through a paired automated analysis solution. Human sequence data were removed using alignment to the human reference genome (hg38). Normalized microbial reads were analyzed, and any remaining human sequences were identified and excluded through alignment to hg38.

Statistics

Inter-group comparisons are made using the unpaired t-test, the Mann-Whitney U test or One-way ANOVA test. For comparisons between groups of categorical variables, the chi-square test is used. The McNemar test is utilized to compare the diagnostic performance between bstNGS and other methods.

Statistical comparisons were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). All figures were drawn using GraphPad Prism (version 9.2.0) and R 4.3.1 software (http://www.r-project.org, The R Foundation). Errors represent 95% confidence intervals or standard deviations where indicated. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Threshold levels of significance for correlation coefficients were adjusted for multiple comparisons by Bonferroni’s correction [29].

Results

Characteristics of the study cohort

A total of 122 patients with available paired BALF and blood samples were ultimately included, whereas 61 patients were excluded because of missing paired samples or insufficient clinical information (Fig. 1). All enrolled patients had severe pneumonia requiring ICU admission, representing a cohort of severe pneumonia-associated sepsis. As summarized in Table 1, the median age was 61 years, and 73.0% of patients were male. Systemic inflammation was pronounced, with a median white blood cell (WBC) count of 11.9 × 109/L, median C-reactive protein (CRP) of 117.8 mg/L, median procalcitonin (PCT) of 1.7 ng/mL, and an elevated baseline SOFA score reflecting the severity of sepsis. Hypertension (41.8%) and diabetes mellitus (24.6%) were the most common comorbidities, followed by active solid tumors (15.6%) and chronic heart disease (9.8%). The majority of patients (73.0%) were immunocompromised. The 28-day mortality was 26.2%, and the median length of ICU stay was 22 days, underscoring the severity of illness in this sepsis population. All BALF samples underwent mNGS, and all paired blood samples were tested by both bstNGS and mNGS according to the study protocol.

Pathogen landscape in BALF and baseline burden of non-causative detections

Comprehensive clinical diagnoses were established by integrating BALF-mNGS with CMTs. Across 122 BALF specimens, mNGS yielded 414 microorganisms, including 226 bacteria, 80 fungi, and 108 viruses (Fig. 2A). After multidisciplinary adjudication, 17% of these microorganisms were classified as causative pathogens and 34% as possibly causative, whereas 38% were judged non-causative and 11% were considered non-pathogenic. This distribution was comparable in bacteria and fungi. In contrast, among viruses only 35% were adjudicated as causative or possibly causative, highlighting the high background of viral colonization or reactivation in BALF. Specifically, viruses highly prone to latent reactivation in critically ill patients, such as Human betaherpesvirus 5, Human alphaherpesvirus 1, and Human gammaherpesvirus 4, were generally adjudicated as background or non-causative unless the patient exhibited specific clinical, laboratory, or radiological signs of viral pneumonitis.

At the patient level, BALF-mNGS detected causative or possibly causative pathogens in 85.24% of patients (39.34% causative and 45.90% possibly causative; Fig. 2B). Bacteria were the predominant etiologic category, with Klebsiella pneumoniae (n = 28) and Acinetobacter baumannii (n = 21) being the most frequently identified (SFig 2). Among fungal infections, Candida albicans (n = 14), Aspergillus fumigatus (n = 11), and Pneumocystis jirovecii (n = 9) were most common. For viral pathogens, Human betaherpesvirus 5 (n = 20) and Human alphaherpesvirus 1 (n = 11) predominated.

Overall, 44.26% of patients had bacterial infections, 13.93% had viral infections, and 11.48% had fungal infections (SFig 3 A). Co-infections were frequent (16.39%), mainly involving bacterial pathogens. The most common co-infection patterns were bacteria-bacteria (30%) and bacteria-fungi (30%), followed by bacteria-virus (20%).

Leading pathogens in co-infected patients included K. pneumoniae (n = 7), A. baumannii (n = 5), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (n = 3), C. albicans (n = 7), and Human betaherpesvirus 5 (n = 4) (SFig 3B). Taken together, BALF-mNGS provided a broad overview of pulmonary colonization and infection but also generated a high burden of non-causative detections, particularly among viruses and low-abundance organisms, underscoring the need for additional tools to refine etiologic attribution.

Concordance between BALF-mNGS and blood-based NGS for etiologic pathogen detection

We next evaluated the ability of blood-bstNGS and blood-mNGS to identify the same causative or possibly causative pathogens defined by BALF-mNGS and clinical adjudication. Among microorganisms adjudicated as causative or possibly causative (n = 211), 45.02% (95/211) were detected by blood-bstNGS, compared with 22.27% (47/211) by blood-mNGS (Fig. 2C, SFig 4). A total of 46 pathogens were concordantly detected by both blood-based NGS platforms. Among pathogens missed by both blood-based assays, most were gram-negative bacteria (51%), followed by fungi (31%) and gram-positive bacteria (15%) (Fig. 2D), suggesting that not all pulmonary pathogens in severe pneumonia-associated sepsis achieve sufficient bloodstream burden for NGS-based detection.

Importantly, blood-bstNGS showed substantially higher sensitivity than blood-mNGS for key gram-negative bacilli. Relative to blood-mNGS, blood-bstNGS more frequently detected K. pneumoniae (60.71% vs. 21.43%), A. baumannii (38.10% vs. 14.29%), P. aeruginosa (36.36% vs. 9.09%), and Human alphaherpesvirus 1 (100% vs. 54.55%) (Fig. 2E). Only one possibly causative case of A. fumigatus was identified by blood-mNGS but missed by blood-bstNGS. These findings indicate that, when blood is used as a surrogate matrix for infection monitoring in severe pneumonia-associated sepsis, targeted enrichment (blood-bstNGS) provides substantially better coverage of clinically adjudicated pulmonary pathogens than unbiased blood-mNGS.

Both blood-based NGS methods also identified pathogens not detected in BALF-mNGS. In total, 79 microorganisms - predominantly viruses (94%) - were concordantly identified by both blood-based NGS platforms but not by BALF-mNGS.

These were adjudicated as non-pathogenic or of non-pulmonary origin, reflecting systemic viral reactivation or remote foci rather than primary respiratory infection. In addition, blood-bstNGS alone identified 137 additional microorganisms, 78% of which were viral. While these blood-only detections did not contribute directly to the diagnosis of pneumonia, they delineate the systemic infectious milieu in sepsis and highlight the importance of clinical adjudication when interpreting highly sensitive blood-based assays.

Blood-based NGS as a tool to refine BALF-mNGS and mitigate putative false positives

To understand how blood-based testing might help filter the high number of putative false-positive detections from BALF-mNGS, we performed a nucleic acid-level comparative analysis using BALF-mNGS read counts (Fig. 2F-G, SFig 5).

Pathogens detected exclusively by BALF-mNGS exhibited significantly lower read counts than those detected by both BALF-mNGS and blood-bstNGS, with the largest differences observed among bacteria (p = 0.0008). This pattern suggests that low-abundance BALF organisms that fail to translocate into the bloodstream may in some cases reflect colonization or background microbiota, although this must be interpreted with caution as fastidious or atypical pathogens can present with low abundance yet drive severe disease.

Regarding microorganisms that remained undiagnosed after clinical adjudication, blood-bstNGS identified 216 such organisms. Of these, 18.98% (41/216) were also detected in BALF (SFig 5 A) and were classified as non-pathogenic (4.63%, 10/216) or non-causative (14.34%, 31/216), indicating that concordant detection alone is not sufficient to establish causality and must be interpreted in clinical context. Among the remaining 81.02% (175/216) blood-only undiagnosed microorganisms, herpesviruses predominated, and a substantial proportion of Human betaherpesvirus 5 (53.66%) and Human gammaherpesvirus 4 (42.86%) were concurrently identified by blood-mNGS and/or CMTs (SFig 5B), again underscoring the high background of latent or reactivated viral infection in this population.

At the nucleic acid level, we also compared read counts for pathogens concurrently detected by both blood-based NGS assays (SFig 5 C). Blood-bstNGS yielded significantly higher read counts than blood-mNGS overall (p < 0.0001), with pronounced differences for both bacterial (p < 0.0001) and viral pathogens (p < 0.0001). Furthermore, pathogens uniquely identified by blood-bstNGS had significantly lower read counts than those detected by both blood-bstNGS and blood-mNGS (p < 0.0001), particularly for viral pathogens (p = 0.0014) (SFig 5D).

These findings indicate that targeted enrichment in blood-bstNGS enhances detection of low-abundance pathogens, thereby extending the dynamic range of blood-based monitoring and enabling more stringent cross-compartment confirmation of high-priority BALF hits.

Taken together, these data suggest a pragmatic role for blood-bstNGS in refining BALF-mNGS results: high-abundance BALF organisms that are corroborated by blood-bstNGS represent high-priority etiologic candidates and may warrant targeted therapy, whereas low-read BALF detections without corresponding bloodstream signal are less likely to reflect invasive pulmonary infection and may reasonably be deprioritized, particularly for gram-negative bacteria.

Comparative diagnostic performance of blood-bstNGS as an alternative blood-based assay

To quantify the diagnostic utility of blood-bstNGS in severe pneumonia-associated sepsis, we compared its performance with BALF-mNGS, blood-mNGS, blood culture, and CMTs (Fig. 3A-C; Table 2). As expected, BALF-mNGS demonstrated the highest detection rate for causative or possibly causative pathogens (85.25%). Among blood-based modalities, blood-bstNGS (54.10%) outperformed blood-mNGS (30.33%) and blood culture (8.20%), and also surpassed CMTs (41.80%).

A similar pattern was observed for bacterial infections. BALF-mNGS detected bacterial pathogens in 70.49% of patients, followed by blood-bstNGS (31.97%), CMTs (29.51%), blood-mNGS (12.30%), and blood culture (8.20%) (Fig. 3A). For fungal infections, BALF-mNGS again had the highest detection rate (33.61%), compared with 12.30% for CMTs. Both blood-based NGS methods showed lower yields for fungal pneumonia (6.56% for blood-bstNGS; 4.92% for blood-mNGS; Fig. 3B), consistent with limited translocation of fungal DNA into the bloodstream.

Although blood-bstNGS detected viruses in 81.15% of patients, only 27.05% of patients had causative or possibly causative viral pathogens by this method, a proportion comparable to BALF-mNGS (29.51%) and higher than blood-mNGS (18.85%) and CMTs (11.48%) (Fig. 3C). For bacterial pathogens, blood-bstNGS and CMTs exhibited stronger concordance with BALF-mNGS than blood-mNGS or blood culture (Fig. 3D). CMTs showed the highest concordance with BALF-mNGS for fungal pathogens, whereas both blood-based NGS methods displayed high concordance with BALF-mNGS for viral pathogens, reflecting the systemic nature of viral replication and reactivation in this critically ill cohort.

When formally assessed against the final clinical diagnosis, blood-bstNGS achieved an overall diagnostic accuracy of 60.66%, significantly higher than blood-mNGS (39.34%, p < 0.0001), CMTs (54.92%, p = 0.0025), and blood culture (22.95%, p < 0.0001) (Table 2). Blood-bstNGS also demonstrated the highest sensitivity (63.46%), compared with 35.58% for blood-mNGS, 49.04% for CMTs, and 9.62% for blood culture. However, this gain in sensitivity was accompanied by a modest reduction in specificity (44.44% for blood-bstNGS vs. 61.11% for blood-mNGS, 88.89% for CMTs, and 100% for blood culture), reflecting the trade-off inherent in highly sensitive molecular assays.

In bacterial pneumonia, blood-bstNGS maintained significantly higher diagnostic performance than both blood-mNGS and blood culture (p < 0.01 for each). For gram-positive bacteria, the sensitivity and specificity of blood-bstNGS were 36.00% and 94.85%, respectively; for gram-negative bacteria, corresponding values were 46.58% and 81.63%. In fungal pneumonia, blood-bstNGS sensitivity was lower (21.95%) and did not differ significantly from CMTs (34.15%, p = 0.4042), consistent with the limited bloodstream dissemination of many fungal pathogens. Collectively, these findings suggest that, in sepsis secondary to severe bacterial pneumonia, blood-bstNGS can serve as a biologically plausible surrogate when BALF sampling is not feasible or must be minimized, particularly for gram-negative bacilli and invasive viral infections, whereas its value as a substitute for BALF is more limited in fungal disease.

Clinical predictors of cross-compartment concordance

To identify clinical scenarios in which blood-bstNGS is most informative as a complement or surrogate to BALF-mNGS, we performed multivariate logistic regression among patients with concordant pathogen detections between BALF-mNGS and blood-bstNGS. Older age (≥ 60 years) was independently associated with positive concordance (odds ratio [OR] 3.2, p = 0.0022), whereas sex and peripheral blood cell counts were not (p > 0.05 for all; SFig 6). Compared with patients with normal CRP and PCT, those with concordant BALF-mNGS and blood-bstNGS results tended to have higher inflammatory marker levels, corresponding to a 1.8-2.1-fold increased likelihood of CRP and PCT elevation, although these associations did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05).

Positive concordance between BALF-mNGS and blood-bstNGS was also significantly associated with prolonged ICU stay (≥ 28 days; OR 3.1, p = 0.0273), indicating that cross-compartment dissemination of pathogens reflects a more severe and protracted disease course. These findings delineate a subset of older, more severely ill patients with severe pneumonia-associated sepsis in whom blood-bstNGS is particularly likely to yield actionable concordant results and thus may be especially valuable when BALF sampling is not feasible or must be restricted.

Prognostic impact of concordant pathogen detection between BALF and blood

Finally, we evaluated whether concordant pathogen detection between BALF-mNGS and blood-based NGS had prognostic implications in severe pneumonia-associated sepsis. Complete survival data were available for all patients, with a median follow-up of 225 days (interquartile range, 26–225 days).

Patients with concordant pathogen detections between BALF-mNGS and blood-bstNGS had a significantly higher 30-day survival rate than those without concordance (p = 0.0052; Fig. 4A). Although 90-day and overall survival were also higher in this group, these differences did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05; Fig. 4B-C). Similarly, concordance between BALF-mNGS and blood-mNGS was associated with improved 30-day survival (p = 0.0007; Fig. 4D), whereas differences in 90-day and overall survival did not achieve statistical significance (p > 0.05; Fig. 4E-F).

When concordant detections were further stratified by positivity, patients with positive concordant results between blood-bstNGS and BALF-mNGS had significantly better 30-day survival (p = 0.0152) and prolonged overall survival (OR 4.3, p = 0.0156; Fig. 4G). A similar pattern was observed for blood-mNGS: positive concordance with BALF-mNGS was associated with improved 30-day survival (p = 0.0009) and extended overall survival (OR 13.2, p = 0.0001; Fig. 4H). These findings suggest that, in severe pneumonia-associated sepsis, timely identification of concordant pathogens across compartments - facilitated by blood-based NGS in addition to BALF-mNGS - may support more precise and effective antimicrobial decision-making and is associated with improved short-term outcomes.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of adults with severe pneumonia-associated sepsis, we show that blood-based targeted sequencing of microbial cell-free DNA using a broad-spectrum capture panel provides higher diagnostic yield than unbiased blood-mNGS, conventional microbiological tests, and blood culture, while achieving meaningful concordance with BALF-mNGS for clinically adjudicated pathogens. In parallel, cross-compartment concordance between blood-bstNGS and BALF-mNGS helps prioritize high-abundance BALF detections as likely causative and is associated with improved short-term survival. Together, these data support a pragmatic role for blood-bstNGS as both a non-invasive concession test and a complement to BALF-mNGS in severe pneumonia-associated sepsis.

Our findings extend prior work on mNGS-based diagnosis of severe pneumonia and sepsis, which has demonstrated the high analytical sensitivity of BALF-mNGS but also emphasized its high burden of non-causative or indeterminate organisms, particularly among viruses and low-abundance taxa [8–10]. Plasma mcfDNA assays and blood mNGS have emerged as minimally invasive options for sepsis diagnosis, yet their performance is limited by low microbial biomass and high host background [11–13]. Targeted NGS approaches using hybrid-capture or multiplex PCR can improve on-target yield and sensitivity [14–20], but data in severe pneumonia-associated sepsis with paired BALF sampling have been scarce. By analyzing paired BALF and blood specimens with multiple modalities and adjudicating results against a composite clinical reference, our study provides an integrated view of how blood-bstNGS and BALF-mNGS interact in real-world, critically ill patients.

A key clinical question is whether blood-based assays can substitute for BALF-based testing when bronchoscopy is unsafe, delayed, or cannot be repeated.

BALF-mNGS, as expected, had the highest overall detection rate for causative or possibly causative pathogens. However, blood-bstNGS detected nearly half of these adjudicated pathogens and clearly outperformed unbiased blood mNGS, CMTs, and blood culture. The advantage of blood-bstNGS was most evident for gram-negative bacilli such as Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which are central drivers of ICU pneumonia and sepsis [30, 31]. While conventional cultures often suffer from reduced sensitivity due to early empirical antibiotic use, blood-bstNGS demonstrated critical incremental yield for these gram-negative pathogens. We note that the higher sensitivity of blood-bstNGS is accompanied by lower specificity compared to conventional methods. This reflects its capacity to detect trace amounts of circulating microbial cell-free DNA (including DNA from resolving infections or non-viable organisms). Thus, blood-bstNGS is best positioned as a highly sensitive concession test or complement, rather than a standalone definitive tool. When evaluated against the clinical reference, blood-bstNGS achieved the highest sensitivity among blood-based tests, at the cost of some reduction in specificity - an expected trade-off for a broad, highly sensitive molecular assay. These observations suggest that, particularly for suspected severe bacterial or systemic viral pneumonia, blood-bstNGS can operate as a biologically plausible concession test when BALF sampling is not feasible, while acknowledging that BALF remains important in fungal disease and diagnostically challenging cases.

Our data also support a specific role for blood-bstNGS in refining BALF-mNGS and mitigating putative false positives. Organisms detected exclusively in BALF had significantly lower read counts than those concordantly detected in BALF and blood-bstNGS, especially for bacteria, suggesting that low-abundance BALF organisms without bloodstream signal are less likely to represent invasive infection.

This pattern aligns with the biological expectation that pathogens driving pneumonia with sepsis achieve sufficient pulmonary burden and vascular access to release detectable mcfDNA, whereas colonizers and contaminants do not [21, 22].

Operationally, this enables a tiered interpretive strategy: high-read BALF detections corroborated by blood-bstNGS can be prioritized as primary etiologic candidates, whereas low-read, BALF-only detections - particularly known colonizers or viruses commonly associated with subclinical reactivation - may be reasonably deprioritized or monitored rather than immediately targeted with broader or prolonged antimicrobial therapy.

At the same time, concordance cannot be equated with causality. Among undiagnosed microorganisms identified by blood-bstNGS, almost one-fifth were also present in BALF yet remained clinically classified as non-causative or non-pathogenic.

Blood-only undiagnosed detections were dominated by herpesviruses, consistent with the high background of latent or reactivated viral infection in immunocompromised, critically ill hosts [32, 33]. These findings underscore that both blood-bstNGS and BALF-mNGS must be interpreted within a rigorous clinical framework that incorporates imaging, host status, temporal trends, and response to therapy, and not solely on the presence or absence of cross-compartment concordance.

We identified older age and prolonged ICU stay as independent predictors of concordant pathogen detection between BALF-mNGS and blood-bstNGS, suggesting that cross-compartment dissemination is more likely in frail or more severely ill patients. Interestingly, patients with positive concordance between BALF-mNGS and blood-based NGS had better 30-day survival than those without concordance.

Although dissemination is often viewed as a marker of severity, several explanations are plausible. Concordance may identify patients in whom the causative pathogen is more readily detectable, enabling timely pathogen-directed therapy and de-escalation of empiric regimens. Conversely, non-concordant cases may include heterogeneous phenotypes - occult extra-pulmonary infections, non-infectious sepsis mimics, or low-burden infections under partial antimicrobial suppression - in which persistent diagnostic uncertainty hampers optimal management. Residual confounding and survivor bias cannot be excluded. Importantly, the survival advantages associated with concordant results likely reflect adjustments in the treatment approach, such as optimized targeted antimicrobial therapy, rather than the diagnostic finding itself. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, capturing the exact temporal sequence of dynamic antibiotic adjustments based on individual test results was constrained.

Prospective validation is therefore required to confirm these prognostic benefits.

Furthermore, while the lower specificity and high false-positive rate of highly sensitive blood-bstNGS could theoretically prompt unnecessary antimicrobial use, in our cohort, ultimate treatment decisions were guided by the multidisciplinary composite diagnosis, thereby safely mitigating direct adverse prognostic impacts from isolated false-positive signals.

Our study has notable strengths. We enrolled a relatively large, homogeneous ICU cohort with severe pneumonia-associated sepsis and standardized paired BALF and blood sampling. Pathogen attribution was adjudicated by experienced intensivists using a comprehensive clinical reference that included NGS, CMTs, and clinical course. Direct comparison of blood-bstNGS with unbiased blood-mNGS, BALF-mNGS, CMTs, and blood culture allowed a nuanced assessment of relative performance and complementary roles. The inclusion of nucleic acid-level analyses adds mechanistic plausibility to observed concordance patterns.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The study is retrospective and single-center, which may limit generalizability; local epidemiology, antimicrobial practices, and bronchoscopy thresholds may differ elsewhere. We evaluated a single bstNGS platform and analytic pipeline; performance may vary across panels and thresholds. The composite clinical reference is imperfect and susceptible to incorporation bias, particularly given the central role of BALF-mNGS in adjudication.

Sampling was constrained to a 24-hour window but not tightly synchronized with symptom onset or antimicrobial exposure, which may affect detectability. We did not assess serial samples or mcfDNA kinetics, and our exploratory prognostic analyses were not adjusted for all potential confounders. Finally, while demonstrating genomic homology between BALF and blood isolates would provide definitive proof of a causal relationship, blood-bstNGS specifically targets highly fragmented microbial cell-free DNA. The extremely short fragment length and discontinuous nature of mcfDNA captured by targeted probes make de novo assembly and reliable strain-level homology comparison bioinformatically unfeasible using current short-read sequencing technologies.

Future work should include prospective, multicenter trials integrating blood-bstNGS into predefined diagnostic algorithms for severe pneumonia-associated sepsis, with explicit criteria for when to replace or complement BALF-based testing and how to adjust antimicrobial therapy based on NGS results. Quantitative and longitudinal analyses of pathogen-specific mcfDNA, combined with host-response biomarkers, may further improve discrimination between colonization and invasive disease and refine risk stratification. Health economic evaluations will be essential to determine the cost-effectiveness of incorporating blood-bstNGS alongside or in place of BALF-mNGS in resource-constrained settings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, blood-bstNGS demonstrates enhanced diagnostic performance compared to unbiased blood mNGS and conventional blood-based methods in severe pneumonia-associated sepsis, showing clinically meaningful concordance with BALF-mNGS. Although blood-bstNGS is not intended to replace BALF-mNGS as an independent diagnostic standard, it serves as a highly valuable complementary tool.

When bronchoscopy is constrained or contraindicated, blood-bstNGS can function as an informative non-invasive diagnostic aid, particularly for identifying gram-negative bacterial and systemic viral infections. Furthermore, when BALF is available, concurrent blood-bstNGS improves the interpretability of BALF-mNGS by prioritizing cross-compartment, high-abundance pathogens and aiding in the deprioritization of low-abundance, likely non-causative detections. These findings provide a framework for the rational integration of blood-bstNGS as an adjunctive assay in diagnostic and antimicrobial stewardship strategies for critically ill patients.
