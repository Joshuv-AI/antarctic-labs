Absent internal carotid artery during neck exploration for penetrating trauma
Authors: Allison Perko, Andrew Gaugler, Bree Steinke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCongenital absence of the internal carotid artery (ICA) is an extremely rare vascular anomaly. Although typically asymptomatic, its presence can complicate the surgical management of penetrating neck injuries. We present the case of a 23-year-old man with active hemorrhage from a right-sided neck stab wound. Intraoperative neck exploration failed to identify the right ICA. Subsequent intraoperative angiography and postoperative computed tomography angiography confirmed a congenital absence of the right ICA and the corresponding carotid canal. Surgeons must be aware of rare vascular variants such as ICA agenesis, because they can mimic traumatic injury or vascular occlusion during emergent neck explorations.Keywords: Carotid artery, Penetrating trauma, Vascular anomaly

The internal carotid artery (ICA) typically provides the primary blood supply to the anterior cerebral circulation. Congenital absence, which encompasses agenesis, aplasia, and hypoplasia, is a rare finding with an estimated incidence of <0.01%.1 Most individuals remain asymptomatic throughout life due to robust collateral flow through the circle of Willis or collateral embryonic vessels.2 However, in the setting of penetrating trauma, this anomaly can lead to significant diagnostic confusion and technical challenges during emergent surgical exploration. The following case is presented to demonstrate the complexity in diagnosis congenital absence of the ICA in penetrating trauma. The patient has provided consent to publish their case details and images.

Case description

A 23-year-old man with no significant past medical history presented as a trauma alert following stab wounds to the right neck and back. Upon arrival, the patient exhibited active hemorrhage from a zone 2 neck wound, necessitating direct transfer to the operating room for emergent neck exploration.

During the neck dissection, the right common carotid artery (CCA) and external carotid artery (ECA) were clearly identified. However, despite thorough exploration, the right ICA could not be located in its expected anatomical position. To clarify the anatomy and rule out a traumatic laceration or occlusion, intraoperative selective angiography was performed via a transfemoral approach. The resulting images visualized the right CCA and ECA but confirmed the absence of the right ICA (Fig 1). The left-sided carotid system was found to be intact, with normal visualization of the CCA, ECA, and ICA (Fig 2). The right anterior cerebral circulation was supplied by the left ICA.

Right-sided angiography demonstrating visualization of the common carotid artery (CCA) and external carotid artery (ECA) as indicated by the arrows with a complete absence of the right internal carotid artery (ICA). Note the branches from the ECA without ICA flow.

Left-sided angiography demonstrating a normal common carotid artery (CCA) bifurcation into the internal carotid artery (ICA) and external carotid artery (ECA) as indicated by arrows. Note the perfusion to the right middle cerebral artery (MCA) via the left carotid circulation through the circle of Willis.

Cardiovascular surgery was consulted intraoperatively and identified a small, hypoplastic vessel thought to represent a vestigial right ICA. Further exploration of the wound identified the source of the hemorrhage as injuries to a venous branch and small arterial branches within the parotid gland. These were ligated, and hemostasis was achieved.

Postoperatively, a computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the head and neck was obtained for definitive diagnosis. The imaging confirmed the absence of the right ICA and demonstrated an absence of the right carotid canal (Fig 3), a pathognomonic finding that distinguishes congenital agenesis from acquired occlusion.

Axial computed tomography angiography (CTA) images of the skull base demonstrating a patent left carotid canal with the internal carotid artery (ICA) passing through, noted by the arrows, whereas the right carotid canal is absent confirming congenital agenesis.

Discussion

Congenital absence of the ICA is thought to result from developmental failure during the fourth to fifth weeks of embryogenesis.2 The hallmark for distinguishing this congenital anomaly from an acquired condition is the absence of the carotid canal in the petrous portion of the temporal bone, as seen in this patient.

To better conceptualize how these patients adapt to this congenital anomaly, Lie3 described a classification system based on the specific collateral pathways supplying the affected cerebral hemisphere. This has been expanded in the neurointerventional literature with the following collateral pathways described4:

•Type A: Unilateral absence of the ICA with collateral flow to the ipsilateral anterior cerebral artery (ACA) via a patent anterior communicating artery and ipsilateral middle cerebral artery (MCA) via a patent posterior communicating artery (PCOM).

•Type B: Unilateral absence of the ICA with collateral flow to the ipsilateral ACA and MCA via a patent anterior communicating artery.

•Type C: Bilateral absence of the ICAs with collateralization through hypertrophy of the PCOM.

•Type D: Unilateral absence of the ICA with collateral flow originating from the contralateral ICA via persistent embryonic intracarotid connections.

•Type E: Diminutive ACAs with flow via bilateral hypoplastic ICAs. Bilateral MCAs supplied by hypertrophy of the PCOMs.

•Type F: Collateral flow to the ipsilateral MCA via transcranial anastomosis from the ECA branches.

Based on our intraoperative selective angiography showing cross-filling to the right MCA from the left carotid system through the circle of Willis, this patient's anatomy most closely aligns with a Lie type A classification.3,4

In a trauma context, the inability to locate a major vessel like the ICA during an emergent neck exploration for active bleeding is highly stressful and may lead to unnecessary and potentially morbid maneuvers to find a “missing” vessel. This case highlights the importance of early transition to multimodal imaging (angiography and CTA) when operative findings do not align with expected anatomy. In addition, surgeons must be aware of congenital anomalies as they can mimic traumatic injury or vascular occlusion.

However, the presence of this anomaly shifts the standard trauma paradigms. In patients with an absent ICA, the brain is critically dependent on alternative collateral pathways. If this patient had a Lie type F variant, where cerebral perfusion relies on transcranial ECA collateralization, standard management of an ipsilateral ECA injury would change drastically as patency of this vessel is critical to cerebral vascularization. Although a typical penetrating ECA injury can often be safely ligated if necessary, doing so in a type F variant could cause a catastrophic ipsilateral stroke. Therefore, any suspected injury to the ipsilateral ECA branches in these patients requires aggressive temporary shunting and definitive primary vascular repair rather than ligation.

Conversely, the vulnerability of the intact, nonanomalous carotid artery (the left side in this patient's case) cannot be overstated. The left ICA was solely responsible for the perfusion of both cerebral hemispheres via the circle of Willis. Any major injury to the left carotid system would be instantly devastating with global anterior cerebral hypoperfusion. An injury to the normal carotid in this scenario would necessitate immediate intraoperative shunting with rapid surgical reconstruction or endovascular stenting to avoid profound bilateral ischemic stroke or brain death.

Conclusions

Although congenital absence of the ICA is an exceedingly rare vascular anomaly, it represents a critical diagnostic and therapeutic consideration during neck exploration for penetrating trauma. As demonstrated in this case, the unexpected absence of a major vessel can provoke significant intraoperative anxiety and mimic devastating traumatic occlusion or laceration. Encountering an unexpected “missing” vessel should prompt immediate transition to a multimodal imaging, such as intraoperative angiography and postoperative CTA, to confirm skull base anatomy and protect the patient from unnecessary, morbid surgical dissection.

Ultimately, recognizing this anomaly and establishing its specific collateral configuration via the Lie classification system fundamentally reshapes standard trauma paradigms. When the ICA system is absent, the cerebral circulation becomes entirely dependent on alternative pathways, such as the contralateral carotid system or transcranial ECA collaterals. Consequently, standard surgical mandates, such as the safe ligation of an injured ipsilateral ECA, may be strictly contraindicated in specific anatomical variants due to the risk of catastrophic stroke. Furthermore, the intact, contralateral carotid system must be recognized as the sole lifeline for bilateral cerebral perfusion, demanding aggressive protection, temporary shunting, and immediate reconstruction if injured. Incorporating a high index of suspicion for rare vascular variants allows trauma and vascular surgeons to optimize intraoperative decision-making, safeguard critical collateral pathways, and avoid ischemic neurological injury.
