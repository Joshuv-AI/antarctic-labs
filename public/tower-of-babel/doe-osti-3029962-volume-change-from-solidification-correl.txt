 1.0 INTRODUCTION
1.1 BACKGROUND

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), Hanford Field Office’s primary mission is to safely and
effectively treat Hanford’s tank waste and deliver environmental remediation. Mixed radioactive
waste is stored in the underground tanks at the Hanford Site. It was recently estimated that retrieval
of the waste in the 200 West Area underground tanks in the SY, S, SX, and U Tank Farms will
result in about 41 million gallons of mixed low-level waste (MLLW) (RPP-RPT-65147, Rev. 1).
The current plan is to retrieve at least 22 S, SX, and U Farm tanks and pretreat 1 to produce
pretreated tank waste (PTW) which will be further treated (including solidification
/immobilization) for Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) Land Disposal Restriction
(LDR) organics and inorganics before being transferred to an offsite out-of-state facility for
disposal (RPP-PLAN-66135, Rev. 2). In addition, solidified PTW from the 200 East Area may
also be transferred to an offsite out-of-state facility for disposal to ensure availability of critical
Double-Shell Tank space, meet retrieval obligations, and optimize 200 East Area operations.
Technology Maturation and Analysis staff within the Hanford Tank Waste Operations & Closure
LLC (H2C) Process Technology & Innovation organization (PT&I) have been mining historic data
on cementitious solidification material to develop a correlation between the volume change that
takes place when a liquid waste is solidified/immobilized and easily measured properties of the
grout or other solidification formulations such as mass of solids and volume of liquids added,
densities, and porosities of the final cured wasteform.

1.2 PURPOSE

The purpose for the development of a correlation for the volume change during
solidification/immobilization and water mass fraction of the formulation is to simplify
implementation of Hanford tank waste treatment by eliminating the need to individually measure
the volume and mass change for every formulation that may be used to solidify/immobilize the
PTW. Instead, only the mass fraction of water incorporated into the wasteform is needed to
estimate its final volume. This is important to Hanford tank waste treatment when implementing
the Sample-and-Send approach so that Hanford tank waste is only sampled once prior to
pretreatment to meet all applicable downstream waste acceptance requirements.
With the knowledge of other treatment process properties such as the retention factor, the Sample-
and-Send equation 2 shown below can be applied to estimate the maximum concentration of
organic or inorganic species within the PTW that can be treated and still ensure the final wasteform
complies with nonwastewater RCRA LDR treatment standards. Under the Sample-and-Send
approach, liquid waste can then be sampled to determine the concentration of relevant species.

 2.0 METHODS

2.1 INPUT DATA

The input data used for this computation includes waste simulant composition and density,
composition (including water content) and mass of solidification materials and other treatment
reagents/aggregates/fillers added to the liquid waste, total liquid volumes added, final cured
grout/wasteform density, and cured grout/wasteform porosity. Recent and historic reports on
cementitious materials used to solidify/immobilize or encapsulate a variety of Hanford wastes
were mined for the needed data. The data represent a broad range of dry wasteform formulations
(e.g., ultra-high-performance grouts (UHPG), geopolymers (GP), cements, slags, hydrated lime,
commercial vendor proprietary formulations, etc.) made with a broad range of liquids including
water, liquid treatment additives, and high and low ionic strength salt wastes (e.g., simulated
Hanford low activity waste (LAW), PTW, Effluent Treatment Facility (ETF) brine, Effluent
Management Facility (EMF) waste, etc.). All the reports used in the development of this
correlation are listed in the references section and include data from Pacific Northwest National
Laboratory (PNNL), Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL), and Vitreous State Laboratory
(VSL).

2.2 ASSUMPTIONS

The following assumptions were made for these computations:
 1. The data collected from the indicated source reports are correct and have been represented
 accurately in the spreadsheets used in this analysis.
 2. All grout-forming reagents, aggregates, fillers, and other reagents were assumed to be dry,
 therefore contributing no water to the total water content of the final grout wasteform
 unless otherwise noted in the report or additional information was provided on water
 content via email communications with the commercial vendors, original report authors,
 and/or H2C point of contact.
 3. No material is lost between simulant make-up, treatment, solidification, and curing steps
 (i.e. no residual transfer losses). All salts added to make up the initial liquid simulant and
 all solid and liquid reagents added to the formulation were assumed to go into and be
 present in the final solidified wasteform.
 4. When bulk dry density and apparent density was measured using ASTM Standard Test
 Method C642, the immersion and boiling in water step removes a negligible mass of salts
 or solid material from the cured grout. Only water was assumed to be lost from the pores
 and surfaces of the grout after immersion, boiling, and oven drying such that the final grout
 contains all non-water components (dissolved salts from the simulant and solids from the
 grout reagents) which were added.
 5. The measured apparent density using ASTM Standard Test Method C642 was used as the
 cured grout density ( , ) for all computations when available. For reports where ASTM

 7

 C642 was not used, engineering judgement was used to select an appropriate reported cured
 grout density which most closely approximates apparently density based on the
 measurement procedure (see details in Section 2.3).
 6. Actual measured values (e.g., from lab notebook records) for weights and volumes were
 used when available in the issued reports; otherwise, target values were used.
 7. For formulations where a water to dry mix (w/dm) ratio is given instead of individual
 masses of water and solidifying reagents (i.e., solid secondary waste formulations), the
 mass of water was assumed to be 1000 g as the basis for all other computations.

2.3 METHOD OF ANALYSIS

The total mass of water (mwater) in the solidified wasteform is calculated by adding the reported
masses of all water in the formulation, including:
 - Water added to make up the initial liquid waste simulant
 - Water (mass of waters of hydration only) contained in hydrated compounds (e.g.,
 CH3COONa 3H2O, Na2Cr2O7 2H2O, MgSO4 7H2O, NaH2PO4 H2O)
 - Water contained in liquid additives for pH adjustment and/or LDR treatment prior to
 solidification (e.g., mass of the water in 50 wt% NaOH, acids, etc.)
 - Moisture contained in fillers and aggregates added to the solidification reagents, if known
 and significant (e.g. moisture associated with sand, admixtures, etc.)
 - Water added to make up the fresh grout mixture (e.g., tap water, DI water, etc.).

The total mass of all species (mtotal) in the solidified wasteform is calculated by adding the reported
masses of all components in the formulation including:
 - Salts and water added to make up the initial liquid waste simulant (e.g., LAW, PTW, ETF
 brine, EMF bottoms)
 - All solid and/or liquid reagents used in the pre-treatment of the liquid waste for ammonia,
 LDR organic and inorganic species, and radioactive species (e.g., sorbents, zeolites,
 getters, MgSO4 7H2O, NaH2PO4 H2O, 50wt% NaOH, chemical oxidants and reductants)
 - All fine and coarse aggregates and other fillers (e.g., sand, admixtures, etc.)

 Portland cement (OPC), Portland limestone cement (PLC), fly ash (FA), natural pozzolans,

Note that the volume change from solidification term is distinct from the volume expansion factor
term which is discussed in other documents, e.g., for space utilization estimations. The volume
expansion factor term is outside of the scope of this report but is briefly discussed here for clarity
and differentiation from the volume change from solidification. The volume change from
solidification describes the volume increase from adding solidifying reagents such as grout dry
mix to liquid waste that may have undergone LDR treatment steps which may add or remove liquid
volume to or from the waste prior to solidification. Its purpose is to aid in the Hanford tank waste
treatment as part of the strategy to be able to predict the concentration of LDR species in the final
wasteform based on the initial concentration of the LDR species in the liquid waste and other
known properties of the LDR treatment and solidification steps. The volume expansion factor
describes the volume increase after solidification relative to the initial liquid waste volume prior
to pretreatment. Its purpose is to predict the amount of space the final solidified wasteform will
take up for any given volume of liquid waste to be treated for future planning and budgeting. The
volume expansion factor may be calculated by dividing the volume change from solidification by

3.2 DENSITY AND POROSITY RELATIONSHIP

The relationship between wasteform density and porosity as a function of water mass fraction in
the wasteform formulation were also plotted to determine if any correlations exist between those
variables. Figure 4 and Figure 5 below show the wasteform density and porosity respectively as a
function of water mass fraction, along with the fitted linear trendline equations and R 2 values. The
R2 value of 0.2266 for Figure 4 indicates a very weak correlation, suggesting no predictable
relationship between the mass fraction of water added to the wasteform and its final measured
cured density. This weak correlation also highlights the variability of density measurements
performed by different labs on different wasteform formulations, some of which may not produce
a uniform grout monolith. The poor density correlation is responsible for the higher scatter for the
volume change from solidification term shown in Figure 3 versus the / , term shown in

Figure 2. Meanwhile, the R value of 0.6709 for Figure 5 indicates a moderate correlation,
suggesting that while there is a noticeable relationship between the mass fraction of water added
to the wasteform and its final measured porosity, it is not particularly strong and should not be
used in a predictive manner. Though the correlations are not strong, these relationships may still
be useful to provide options and flexibility if future changes to the Sample-and-Send equation (and
thus, a correlation to variables other than volume change) are needed.

 4.0 CONCLUSIONS

A correlation based on historical grout and other solidified wasteform data has been developed
relating the volume change that takes place when a waste liquid is solidified/immobilized and the
water mass fraction of the formulation. This correlation is broadly applicable to various types of
liquid waste at various concentrations and solidification formulations. The Hanford tank waste
treatment strategy can therefore be simplified by eliminating the need to measure the volume and
mass change for every formulation that may be used to solidify/immobilize PTW. Instead, only
the mass fraction of water added to the wasteform is needed. With the knowledge of other
treatment process properties such as the retention factor and the destruction and removal
efficiency, the Sample-and-Send equation (Equation 1) can be applied to estimate the maximum
concentration of LDR organic or inorganic species that are regulated on a concentration-based
standard within liquid waste that can be treated and still ensure the final wasteform complies with
the nonwastewater RCRA LDR treatment standards. The Sample-and-Send approach can be
implemented so that Hanford tank waste is only sampled once prior to pretreatment to meet all
applicable downstream waste acceptance requirements. The Sample-and-Send approach
eliminates the need to sample the final solidified wasteform after treatment and wait for results
prior to disposal.
