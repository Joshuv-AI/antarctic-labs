MRI for patients with autism: evaluating radiologic technologists’ awareness and practice in Saudi healthcare settings
Authors: Njoud Aldusary, Sameeh Alharbi, Adnan A. S. Alahmadi, Ali H. Alghamdi, Arwa Baeshen, Jaber Alsalah, Ali S. Alyami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMRI can be challenging for patients with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) due to sensory sensitivities and radiologic technologists (RTs) play a key role in managing these challenges. Therefore, this study aims to assess Saudi RTs’ knowledge, practices, and experiences in managing individuals with ASD undergoing MRI, as well as identify barriers and facilitators of effective practice.MethodsA cross-sectional, mixed-methods study was conducted using an online questionnaire (n = 335) and semi-structured interviews (n = 8). Data collection occurred between April and May 2023, and the results were analyzed using SPSS v25 and thematic analysis.ResultsOnly 13% of RTs had formal training in ASD care. While knowledge scores were relatively high (61% high-level), practice and training scores were weak (51% and 50% low-level, respectively). In addition, a modest positive correlation was observed between training and practice domains (r = 0.121, p = 0.027). Moreover, thematic analysis highlighted three themes: RTs’ knowledge gaps, variability in practice, and the need for clear guidelines.ConclusionThese results indicate that there is a significant gap between RTs’ knowledge and practical readiness in handling individuals with ASD during MRI. Consequently, tailored training programs and standardized protocols are recommended.

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a multifaceted neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by enduring impairments in social communication and interaction, alongside restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities [1]. The global prevalence of ASD is steadily increasing, with the World Health Organization (WHO) estimating that one in every 100 children worldwide is affected by the condition [2]. In Saudi Arabia, the precise prevalence remains underreported, though estimates across the Gulf region suggest a wide range of 1.4 to 29 per 10,000 individuals [3].

Individuals with ASD have unique healthcare issues, especially in imaging settings like Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), where sensory sensitivities, communication barriers, and anxiety can impede the patient’s capacity to endure and complete procedures [4]. In modern diagnostic radiology, MRI is an essential tool for diagnosis, producing high-resolution soft tissue images without ionizing radiation. However, the procedure requires patients to remain still in a confined, noisy environment for extended periods, which can be distressing for individuals with ASD. Moreover, the MRI environment, characterized by its loud acoustic noise and confined space [5], can be highly distressing, which may contribute to reluctance and elevated anxiety about the procedure. This presents a particular challenge, as previous research indicates that the sensory sensitivity frequently exhibited by individuals with autism, including hypersensitivity to sound, touch, and visual stimuli, can be exacerbated during MRI procedures, resulting in incomplete scans, increased anxiety, or the necessity for sedation [6, 7].

Radiology technologists (RTs) play a critical role in facilitating MRI procedures and are often the primary healthcare professionals interacting directly with patients during imaging sessions. The effectiveness of MRI exams can be greatly impacted by their capacity for efficient communication, adaptive adjustments, and patient anxiety management, particularly among vulnerable populations such as those with ASD. Despite growing awareness of the needs of ASD, many RTs lack specialized training in ASD-sensitive care. This skills gap could make imaging services for individuals with ASD less reliable and make it harder for them to get fair diagnostic services [8].

Research over the past decade has increasingly focused on identifying barriers and facilitators in delivering healthcare to ASD. Numerous studies have underscored the distinctive challenges faced by individuals with ASD in imaging procedures specifically [4, 9, 10]. Stogiannos et al. conducted a UK-based study and reported that 70.8% of individuals with ASD undergoing MRI thought that getting real-time updates on how long the scan would take was very important for keeping their anxiety in check [4]. However, only 41.5% of technologists maintained consistent communication during the scan, and merely 17.1% made environmental adjustments to reduce sensory distress. The disparity between patient needs and provider practices highlights the necessity for customized communication strategies and environmental adjustments in imaging environments. Further studies have demonstrated that individuals with ASD report higher satisfaction and better outcomes when RTs adopt a person-centered approach, provide pre-visit information, reduce auditory and visual stimuli, and incorporate caregiver support [11, 12].

While the MRI environment poses significant challenges for individuals with ASD globally, the issue is particularly concerning in Saudi Arabia, where little is known about how RTs manage ASD-specific care. International evidence indicates that specialized training, environmental adaptation, and adherence to guidelines can significantly enhance the MRI experience for patients with ASD. Nonetheless, anecdotal evidence and limited local studies indicate that numerous RTs in Saudi Arabia lack formal training, access to ASD protocols, or institutional support for the implementation of adaptive strategies during MRI. In MRI settings, individuals with ASD may face multiple barriers, including sensory hypersensitivity to noise and confined spaces, communication difficulties, cognitive rigidity, and an increased likelihood of requiring sedation.

This gap between what we know and what we do could lead to less-than-ideal imaging results, more reliance on sedation, and worse experiences for patients. This study’s primary aim is to evaluate Saudi Arabian radiology technologists’ knowledge, practices and experiences around the treatment of individuals with ASD undergoing MRI. It seeks to pinpoint deficiencies in training, knowledge, and clinical protocols impacting the provision of MRI services to individuals with ASD, using both quantitative and qualitative assessments. The secondary aim is to examine RTs’ barriers and enablers to high-quality, ASD-sensitive imaging care. Despite increasing international attention to autism-friendly imaging practices, there is a clear lack of evidence describing how radiologic technologists in Saudi Arabia are prepared to manage patients with ASD. Existing regional literature primarily focuses on prevalence and clinical characteristics, with minimal attention to imaging-specific workflows, availability of ASD-adapted MRI protocols, or training exposure among technologists. Furthermore, no national data currently exist regarding sedation practices, environmental adaptations, or institutional support for ASD-sensitive imaging in Saudi Arabia. Therefore, this study addresses an important knowledge gap by providing the first national-level evaluation of MRI Technologists’ awareness, training, and clinical practices related to ASD in MRI units.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed a convergent parallel mixed-methods design, in which quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently, analyzed separately, and then integrated during interpretation. This approach enabled triangulation of findings, allowing quantitative trends to be complemented and explained by qualitative insights. Therefore, the study consisted of two components: [1] a quantitative descriptive study involving 335 radiologic technologists recruited using a convenience sampling approach, and [2] a qualitative study involving 8 MRI technologists who participated in in-depth semi-structured interviews to provide contextual insights. To facilitate meaningful integration of the mixed-methods data, a joint display approach was employed. In this approach, quantitative and qualitative findings were systematically compared and integrated during the interpretation phase. This process allowed for the identification of convergence, complementarity, and divergence across the datasets, thereby enhancing the overall rigor, validity, and depth of the study findings.

Study setting and participants

The study targeted RTs working in MRI departments across various healthcare sectors in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), including both government and private institutions. Participants were eligible if they had experience working with patients with special needs, particularly those with ASD. They were also required to be currently or previously involved in performing an MRI. Data collection occurred between April and May 2023. A varied national sample was pursued to enhance the generalizability of results and to guarantee representation from diverse regions and institutional settings.

Sampling and recruitment

A convenience snowball sampling technique was utilized. The survey was distributed electronically through professional networks, and participants were encouraged to share the survey with colleagues to increase reach. The survey link was initially sent to RTs employed at different private or government healthcare facilities in the KSA. Participants were asked to disseminate the survey to their colleagues, thereby increasing the sample size through peer referrals. The Raosoft program was used to perform a sample size calculation, which determined that a total of 323 participants is required for this study. Considering there are approximately 2,000 MRI technologists in the country, the minimum sample size needed was 323, with a 95% confidence interval and a 5% margin of error. We obtained a total of 335 valid responses, all of which were included in the analysis. Five initial responses were excluded, as they were completed during pilot testing. For the qualitative component, eight RTs were recruited from four hospitals using purposive sampling. The selection criteria included variation in institutional type, years of experience, and exposure to ASD cases to ensure a diversity of perspectives.

Data collection instruments

Survey questionnaire

A structured questionnaire was adapted from a previously validated tool [13]. Permission to use and modify the questionnaire was obtained from Elsevier*. To ensure relevance to the Saudi healthcare context, several modifications were made, including adapting institutional factors to the local setting, expanding practice-related questions, and refining training-related items. The questionnaire consisted of 23 questions designed to gather information in four main areas: demographic data, training on working with individuals with ASD, knowledge, experience, and understanding of ASD, and practices related to individuals with ASD during MRI procedures. The survey included a mix of multiple-choice questions, Likert-scale ratings, and matrix responses to comprehensively cover both factual knowledge and attitudinal aspects. The instrument was initially developed in English and underwent a thorough forward–backward translation process. It was first translated into Arabic by a bilingual healthcare professional. Then, a separate, independent bilingual academic, blinded to the original, performed a back-translation into English. Any discrepancies between the original and back-translated versions were carefully reviewed and resolved by an expert panel. This process ensured semantic and conceptual equivalence as well as cultural appropriateness of the final version. The questionnaire was distributed online via a survey platform to maximize accessibility and reach across Saudi Arabia.

An in-depth semi-structured interview was conducted in designated quiet areas within MRI units, featuring four open-ended questions:

Face-to-face in-depth interviews were conducted in private, quiet areas within MRI departments to ensure a comfortable and distraction-free setting. This approach facilitated the development of rapport between the interviewer and participants, enabled the observation of non-verbal cues, and allowed for immediate clarification of responses when necessary. Collectively, these factors contributed to enhancing the depth and credibility of the data collected. Microsoft Word was used to manually record and transcribe each session, which lasted roughly 20 min. All interviews were conducted in English, as participants were proficient in the language, which supported consistency in data collection and reduced the risk of errors during transcription and analysis. Participants were nevertheless allowed to request clarification or provide responses in Arabic when necessary. Such instances were carefully translated and verified during the transcription process to preserve accuracy. While this approach was appropriate for the present study, future research may benefit from conducting interviews in Arabic to further strengthen cultural and contextual relevance.

Pilot testing and validation

Both the questionnaire and semi-structured interview guide were reviewed by five expert MRI technologists, who were excluded from the final analysis. Feedback was used to improve content clarity and contextual relevance.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 was used to analyze the data from the online survey after exporting to Microsoft Excel. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and survey responses, including measures of central tendency and variability (means, standard deviations) as well as frequencies and percentages. Several inferential statistical tests were conducted to examine differences and correlations among groups. The chi-square test was used to examine categorical variables, the Pearson correlation coefficient was used to assess relationships among key areas such as training, knowledge, and practice, and the independent samples t-test and one-way ANOVA were used to compare group means. Cronbach’s alpha was computed for each primary domain to assess the internal consistency of the survey instrument. The reliability coefficients for the knowledge, training, and practice domains were α = 0.822, 0.774, and 0.881, respectively. These values confirmed the questionnaire’s reliability across all measured dimensions and indicated high internal consistency.

Domain scores were calculated by summing item responses within each domain and converting them into percentage scores. Based on prior literature and interpretability, scores were categorized as: Low: <50%, Moderate: 50–75%, and High: >75%. These thresholds were used to facilitate the interpretation of RT competency levels but have not been externally validated for this population, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Qualitative analysis

Interview transcripts were analyzed using thematic analysis, following Braun and Clarke’s six-step approach [14]. Initially, manual coding was used to identify key phrases and patterns. Codes were further categorized into broader classifications, and major themes were honed through iterative examination. Two academic researchers with expertise in qualitative analysis peer-reviewed thematic interpretations to enhance the credibility of the findings. Any discrepancies in coding were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

A total of 335 RTs from diverse healthcare institutions across Saudi Arabia participated in the survey. The majority were male (61.8%), and most participants were in the 31–35 age group (26.9%). Educationally, more than three-quarters held a bachelor’s degree 67.4%, while 15.8% reported having a master’s degree. A large proportion of respondents (88.1%) were employed in government hospitals. In terms of clinical experience, most participants (60.3%) had less than five years of MRI practice (Table 1).

Knowledge, experience, and understanding of ASD

When evaluating awareness of ASD-related characteristics, RTs demonstrated moderate-to-high familiarity with common behavioral and sensory traits (Table 4). For each characteristic, agreement was quantified by calculating the proportion of participants who indicated either “somewhat agree” or “strongly agree,” thereby representing the extent to which the feature was recognized and familiar to respondents. Most participants recognized the lack of eye contact (72.16%) and communication difficulties (72.54%) as key features of ASD. Other frequently identified characteristics included repetitive movements 68.58%, extreme anxiety 66.49%, and 60.82% heightened sensory sensitivities. These findings suggest a moderate-to-high level of familiarity with the behavioral profile of ASD among technologists, while also highlighting gaps in recognition of sensory-related and control-related issues. ASD patient-related practice of RTs

During the MRI scan, RTs employed several adaptive strategies to accommodate patients with ASD better. The most reported adjustments included using a modified volume and tone of voice (70.7%), avoiding unnecessary physical contact (51.6%), shortening the scan duration (62.4%), and minimizing acoustic noise (45.1%). Despite these efforts, a small subset of technologists (7.2%) reported making no specific adjustments for individuals with ASD (Table 7).

Several barriers to delivering ASD-friendly imaging services were identified, including a lack of relevant training (64.8%), the absence of Special Educational Needs (SEN) (64.8%), and the unavailability of institutional or departmental guidelines for managing individuals with ASD (51.9%). Conversely, technologists showed several facilitators that supported successful imaging outcomes for individuals with ASD. These included assistance from caregivers (66.6%), scheduling additional time (61.5%), and the use of empathetic communication strategies (51.0%), (Table 7).

Scoring for the “ASD Patient-Related Practice” domain showed a mean of 11.54 ± 4.76 (range: 3–23). Based on these results, 51.3% of participants demonstrated low practice levels, 35.8% were in the average range, and only 12.8% achieved a high level of ASD-related MRI practice performance (p < 0.001), (Table 8).

Correlation and demographic associations

Correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between training and practice scores (r = 0.121, p = 0.027), suggesting that improved training contributes to better clinical practice. No statistically significant association was found between knowledge and either training (p = 0.210) or practice (p = 0.480). Significant associations were found between demographic variables and domain scores. Training scores were higher among older and more experienced RTs (p = 0.026 and p < 0.001, respectively). Knowledge scores varied significantly with gender (p = 0.012) and qualification (p = 0.043), with higher scores among female participants and those with postgraduate degrees. Further indicating that demographic factors affect both awareness and the application of ASD-specific practices in the MRI setting. Practice scores were also influenced by age (p = 0.05) and qualification (p = 0.006).

Qualitative findings (Thematic analysis)

The thematic analysis of semi-structured interviews yielded three major themes: recognition of ASD patients, adaptive practices, and the need for institutional guidelines. In terms of recognition of ASD patients, participants reported primarily identifying ASD through observable behaviors such as lack of eye contact, repetitive movements, and communication difficulties. In certain instances, the presence of a caregiver or notes included in referral documentation functioned as significant indicators.

The second theme was the adaptive strategies that could be applied by technologists to manage individuals with ASD during MRI scans. These included allowing family presence during scans, adjusting tone of voice and body language to reduce anxiety, using sedation when necessary, and enabling patients to familiarize themselves with the scanning environment beforehand.

The strongest theme across the interviews was the absence of standardized guidelines. Participants consistently highlighted the lack of standardized ASD-specific protocols in their departments and strongly advocated for the creation and implementation of clear, evidence-based imaging recommendations. These guidelines were considered crucial for guaranteeing uniformity, security, and the best possible care for patients.

To enhance clarity in presenting the findings, a summary table was developed that outlines the identified themes alongside selected illustrative quotes (presented in italics) to reflect participants’ experiences and perspectives (Table 9). In addition, a joint display (Fig. 1) was constructed to integrate the quantitative and qualitative results, demonstrating areas of convergence and complementarity, and illustrating how qualitative insights help to explain and enrich the quantitative findings.

Discussion

This study explored the knowledge, perception, and clinical practices of RTs in Saudi Arabia when managing patients with ASD during an MRI procedure. Within the Gulf region, it represents one of the first national-level studies to examine this intersection, particularly from the perspective of the technologist. The findings provide important information about current gaps and possible directions for enhancing the provision of MRI services for individuals with ASD.

The analysis revealed a notable lack of formal training among RTs in dealing with ASD patients. While over 70% of participants reported being able to recognize autistic patients, only 13.4% had received formal training on the topic, and 50.4% demonstrated weak training scores. A statistically significant association was identified between training and clinical practice (r = 0.121, p = 0.027), suggesting that training positively influences real-world performance. However, the limitations of passive knowledge without applied training were highlighted by the fact that knowledge by itself did not significantly correlate with practice.

While knowledge scores were relatively high for 60.6% of respondents, this strength did not translate into adequate clinical readiness. The findings are consistent with earlier worldwide studies, particularly the work of Stiglianos et al. (2023), which similarly highlighted a gap between RTs’ theoretical knowledge and their practical implementation made for individuals with ASD during MRI procedures [4]. In the current study, merely 12.8% of RTs attained high practice scores, while more than half (51.3%) were categorized within the low range. This discrepancy suggests a substantial gap between knowledge and practice. Most participants understood that individuals with ASD are sensitive to sound, touch, and visual stimuli, yet fewer RTs reported using basic implementations such as noise reduction, lighting adjustments, or patient-preparation strategies like simulation or pre-visits. These findings align with the literature, indicating that despite increasing awareness, ASD-friendly procedures are still applied inconsistently [8, 11].

Thematic analysis and quantitative response both highlighted the absence of established guidelines. All interviewed RTs acknowledged the absence of department-specific protocols for imaging individuals with ASD and emphasized the need for clear, evidence-based guidelines. The potential for standardizing quality care for individuals with ASD is limited by this absence, which contributes to inconsistencies across institutions. Previous studies have emphasized the critical role that institutional support and policy development play in facilitating inclusive practices in diagnostic imaging [15, 16].

Different adaptive strategies have been suggested internationally to help people with ASD during MRI. These include environmental modifications, the use of visual storyboards, sedation protocols, and mock scanner training [6, 17]. However, such interventions appear to be underutilized in Saudi Arabia, as reflected by the low rate of environmental and procedural adjustments reported in this study.

In the UK, Stogiannos et al. found that 70.8% of individuals with ASD did not have a way to know how much time remained during the MRI scan, despite this being considered an important factor in reducing anxiety [4]. These findings emphasize the importance of effective communication and adaptive strategies in facilitating successful MRI examinations. Similar findings were observed in the present study, where only two-thirds of Saudi RTs reported adapting their communication style for patients with ASD, while only 36.7% facilitated pre-visit orientation sessions and 19.1% utilized simulation tools. This suggests a worldwide pattern in which RTs are eager yet inadequately qualified to assist individuals with ASD due to systemic limitations.

Furthermore, gender and academic qualification were significantly associated with higher knowledge scores in this study, with female RTs and those holding postgraduate degrees scoring better (p = 0.012 and p = 0.043, respectively). These associations are consistent with the findings of Mason et al. [18], who emphasized the improvement in healthcare professionals’ competence and confidence in providing care to people with ASD [18].

To achieve a more comprehensive interpretation of the findings, the quantitative and qualitative results were integrated. This combined approach offered a deeper understanding of radiologic technologists’ preparedness to manage patients with ASD during MRI procedures. While the quantitative data indicated moderate to high levels of knowledge and the use of adaptive strategies, the qualitative findings provided detailed insight into how these approaches are implemented in routine clinical practice. Notably, both strands of data consistently highlighted gaps in formal training and the lack of standardized guidelines. This alignment underscores the strength of the mixed-methods design, with qualitative insights adding context and depth to the quantitative results and revealing practical challenges and unmet needs that may not be fully captured through survey data alone.

Implications for practice and policy

The results of this study have several critical implications:

Urgent Need for Training Programs: The robust association between training and practice underscores the imperative of implementing specialized professional development programs. Education should emphasize person-centered care, communication strategies, and environmental adaptation.Interdisciplinary Collaboration: The study emphasizes how crucial it is for RTs to communicate with caregivers, pediatricians, anesthesiologists, and behavioral therapists in addition to their patients. Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study encompass its national scope, substantial sample size, and mixed-methods approach, facilitating the triangulation of results and providing contextual depth. It is among the few studies in the Middle East to look at how neurodevelopmental disorders and imaging practices interact from the perspective of a technologist. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The snowball sampling approach, while effective for reaching dispersed professionals, may introduce sampling bias and limit representativeness. Responses were self-reported, which introduces the potential for social desirability bias. Additionally, the qualitative component, though informative, involved a small sample, and the absence of ASD patients’ or caregivers’ perspectives limits the comprehensiveness of experiential insights. The study did not apply multivariable regression analysis to adjust for potential confounders, which may limit the interpretation of independent associations. Future research should investigate patient-centered outcomes like scan completion rates or patient satisfaction among populations with ASD, as well as incorporate direct observational data. Comparative international studies would also help benchmark Saudi practices against global standards.

Conclusions

This study uncovers a significant training and practice gap among RTs in Saudi Arabia when managing individuals with ASD during MRI procedures. Despite having moderate to high levels of knowledge, their practical application is inconsistent and insufficient, mainly due to a lack of formal training and clear guidelines. These findings highlight the urgent need for national ASD-specific MRI training programs and standardized departmental protocols. The findings recommend urgent regulatory, institutional, and educational adjustments to support inclusive, high-quality imaging services for neurodiverse communities. Implementing ASD-sensitive protocols is not only an ethical necessity but also a crucial step toward equitable radiologic care in the 21st century. The convergence of quantitative and qualitative findings confirms that while awareness exists, systemic barriers, including a lack of structured training and standardized protocols, limit effective implementation. This highlights the necessity of adopting a coordinated national strategy for ASD sensitive MRI practice.
