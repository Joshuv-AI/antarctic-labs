Advancing sensory metrology: establishing a standardized protocol for measuring the concentration-dependent sweetness potency of sweeteners utilizing gLMS
Authors: Runrou Wong, Seo-Jin Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to identify an optimal sensory evaluation protocol for accurately modeling concentration–response (C–R) curves of sweeteners using the generalized Labeled Magnitude Scale (gLMS). Four protocol configurations were compared, focusing on three variables: serving methodology (traditional [T-method] vs. sucrose–sweetener combined [SSC method]), sample presentation order (ascending vs. Williams Latin square), and scaling technique (15-point rating scale [15PRS] vs. gLMS). The gLMS proved highly robust for quantifying sweetness intensity and modeling C–R curves. Protocols employing the gLMS delivered more accurate sweetness potency measurements and exhibited significantly higher resistance to contextual bias compared to the 15PRS. The 15PRS consistently overestimated sweetness potency at low concentrations, especially for high-intensity sweeteners. Although the SSC method has previously mitigated contextual effects in category scales, it offered little significant improvement in accuracy when used with the gLMS. Additionally, serving order did not affect gLMS-derived potency measurements, showing the scale's stability. Overall, the gLMS is inherently less susceptible to contextual effects in sensory evaluation, making it a reliable alternative to traditional category scales for standardized sweetness metrology.

Introduction

Sweetness potency is defined as the intensity of a target sweetener relative to sucrose, the standard reference (Wee et al., 2018). This potency varies with concentration; higher concentrations typically result in lower sweetness potency, particularly for high-potency sweeteners (Ko et al., 2020). Understanding this concentration-dependence enables food manufacturers to formulate low-sugar products that maintain desirable sweetness without compromising taste.

Sweetness potency is most commonly derived by modeling the concentration–response (C–R) curves of a target sweetener and sucrose, based on measured sweetness intensities across a concentration range. Various sensory evaluation methods are used to obtain these intensities, including descriptive analysis (Choi and Chung, 2015), the two-alternative forced-choice (2-AFC) test (Gwak et al., 2012; Medel-Marabolí et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2019), and time–intensity measurements (Calviño et al., 2000; Ketelsen et al., 1993). Common scales for assessing sweetness include line scales (Lawless, 2013), interval rating scales, magnitude estimation (Stone and Oliver, 1969), and the generalized Labeled Magnitude Scale (gLMS) (Wee et al., 2018). The choice of method and scale is critical, because sweetness intensity ratings are susceptible to context effects. Broadly, a context effect is the influence that the surrounding context—other objects, events, or information—exerts on an organism's response to a stimulus, particularly in perception and cognition (Colman, 2009). In sensory intensity scaling, it refers more specifically to the systematic dependence of a sample's intensity rating on the composition of the evaluation context—such as the sample set, serving order, and rating scale—so that the same physical stimulus receives different ratings under different contexts rather than being judged independently on an absolute scale (Parducci and Wedell, 1986; Schifferstein, 1995, 1996; Lawless et al., 2000). The terms context effect and contextual bias are used interchangeably in the present study. Because C–R modeling treats each sweetness intensity rating as an absolute value, such context-dependent shifts propagate into the modeled C–R curve and bias the derived sweetness potency. For example, the 2-AFC test has been found to be more accurate than the gLMS owing to its reduction of response correlation and contextual bias through direct sample comparison, yet it is less suitable for sweeteners with multiple taste qualities (Lee et al., 2022). Likewise, when descriptive analysis is conducted with a 15-point rating scale (15PRS), contextual bias has been observed depending on the composition of the sample set within evaluation sessions (Choi and Chung, 2014).

Building on the framework established by Choi and Chung (2014) for optimizing C–R modeling with categorical scales, this study evaluates these protocols using the gLMS to refine sweetness metrology. The previous study compared two methods: the traditional method (T-method), which evaluates sweetener and sucrose samples separately across concentrations, and the sucrose–sweetener combined (SSC) method, which combines both in the same evaluation set. While the T-method can introduce contextual bias due to sample separation, the SSC method minimizes this bias by including sucrose standards in each evaluation set when using a 15PRS. However, the SSC method requires twice as many samples, as sucrose references must be included and recalibrated in every set to address potential bias.

The gLMS is a psychophysical rating method derived from the labeled magnitude scale (LMS) (Green et al., 1993), commonly used to quantify oral sensory intensities (Bartoshuk et al., 2003, 2004). It features semantic labels with quasi-logarithmic spacing, anchored from "no sensation" at the bottom to "strongest imaginable sensation of any kind" at the top. This design enables subjects to rate sensations relative to any sensory experience, not just the tested modality. The gLMS provides several advantages over categorical scales; its anchors are psychophysically validated and spaced to yield ratio-level data, enabling more precise cross-subject and cross-modality comparisons (Bartoshuk et al., 2004). However, the scale's complexity requires thorough training for consistent use, and data typically need logarithmic transformation for statistical analysis (Bartoshuk et al., 2004). Whether gLMS is more resistant to contextual biases than category scales, or still requires protocol-based remedies like the SSC method, remains unclear.

Randomization and counterbalancing are standard in sensory evaluation to minimize carryover effects and sensory fatigue. The Williams Latin square (WLS) design (Williams, 1949) is widely used to balance first-order carryover effects, but may amplify contrast effects when sample intensities vary widely—presenting extreme concentrations in sequence can distort perception. While pseudo-randomized sequences have been used to avoid such pairings (Fujimaru et al., 2012), the influence of presentation order on systematic sweetness potency measurements warrants further study.

This study aimed to identify the optimal sensory evaluation protocol for modeling C–R curves of sweetener potency using the gLMS. Different serving methods (T-method, SSC method), presentation orders (ascending [ASD], WLS), and evaluation scales (15PRS, gLMS) were compared to assess the impact of contextual bias on sweetness intensity measurements.

Materials and methods

Experimental overview

This study implemented four experimental protocols to accurately measure sweetener potency and assess context effects on sweetness potency measurements across various sweeteners. Protocol summaries are provided below and illustrated in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Experimental design overview. Four protocols were created by combining different serving sets, evaluation scales, and sample presentation orders.

Experimental design overview. Four protocols were created by combining different serving sets, evaluation scales, and sample presentation orders.

Protocol 1 (SSC-15PRS-WLS) served as the reference and used the SSC method for sample presentation, WLS for serving order, and a 15PRS for evaluation. Ten trained panelists performed generic descriptive analysis, with each sample evaluated in replicate.

Protocol 2 (SSC-gLMS-WLS) used the SSC method and WLS serving order, but sweetness intensity was evaluated by 51 participants using the gLMS.

Protocol 3 (T-gLMS-WLS) used the T-method for sample presentation, WLS serving order, and gLMS evaluation. Unlike protocol 2, the sweetness intensities of sucrose (reference) at various concentrations were evaluated in separate sessions from the target sweetener.

Protocol 4 (T-gLMS-ACD) was similar to protocol 3 but used an ACD for serving order. Protocols 2–4 did not include replicates.

Protocols 1–4 were compared to determine contextual effects. First, we hypothesized that the gLMS would yield more accurate and reliable C–R curves for sweetness potency than the 15PRS. To test whether the evaluation scale contributed to context effects, protocols 1 (SSC-15PRS-WLS) and 2 (SSC-gLMS-WLS) were compared.

Second, we hypothesized that the SSC method would reduce contextual bias in gLMS-based measurements compared to the T-method. Protocols 2 (SSC-gLMS-WLS) and 3 (T-gLMS-WLS) were compared to investigate serving method effects.

Third, we hypothesized that the WLS sample order would reduce order effects compared to the ACD order in T-method protocols using gLMS presentation. Thus, protocol 3 (T-gLMS-WLS) was compared to protocol 4 (T-gLMS-ASD).

Across all comparisons, the presence and magnitude of context effect was operationalized as the degree of inaccuracy in the sweetness potency generated by each protocol. Because sweetness potency was expressed relative to sucrose, an accurate protocol should yield a potency value that, when used to calculate the concentration of a sweetener matching a target sucrose level, produces a sweetness intensity equivalent to that of the corresponding sucrose solution. Accordingly, the closer the measured sweetness intensity of each sweetener's calculated concentration was to that of its corresponding sucrose solution, the lower the contextual bias attributable to that protocol. This deviation-from-sucrose criterion provided the basis for the validation procedure (Section 2.5) and for the quantitative comparison of contextual bias across protocols.

Sample and sample preparation

Three intense sweeteners—acesulfame-K (Vitasweet Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China), aspartame (Vitasweet Co., Ltd.), and saccharin (JMC Corp., Ulsan, Korea)—and two bulk sweeteners—erythritol and fructose (NOW Real Food®, IL, USA)—were selected. Sucrose served as the control. The selected sweeteners were chosen to represent commercially important bulk and high-potency sweeteners that are widely used in the food and beverage industry. Fructose and erythritol were selected as representative bulk sweeteners due to their extensive application in sugar reduction and sweetener formulations. According to Grand View ResearchInc., 2026a, Grand View ResearchInc., 2026b, Grand View ResearchInc., 2026c, Grand View ResearchInc., 2026d, the global fructose market was valued at USD 5.48 billion and is projected to reach USD 8.76 billion by 2033, while the global erythritol sweeteners market was valued at USD 284.8 million in 2024 and is projected to grow at a CAGR of 9.8% through 2033. Aspartame, acesulfame-K, and saccharin were selected as representative high-potency sweeteners because of their widespread use in sugar-free and reduced-sugar products. The saccharin market was valued at USD 425.0 million in 2024 and is projected to grow at a CAGR of 5.6% to 2030, while the aspartame market reached USD 768.5 million in 2024 and is expected to expand at a CAGR of 8.9% through 2033. In addition, acesulfame-K has been identified as one of the major high-intensity sweeteners driving growth in the global sweeteners market. In addition, these sweeteners span a broad range of sweetness potencies and physicochemical characteristics which allowed the evaluation of concentration–response relationships across diverse sweetener categories.

Sweetness intensities were determined at six sucrose-equivalent concentrations (SECs): 1% w/w, 2%, 3.5%, 5%, 7%, and 9%, herein referred to as levels 1–6, as described by Ko et al. (2020). Sweetener preparation was guided by manufacturer-recommended potency ranges, and initial simulations at 3% and 5% SEC were evaluated by seven researchers to verify appropriateness. All samples were prepared at varying concentrations using spring water (SamDaSoo, Jeju, South Korea) as the solvent. Dissolution was achieved with a magnetic stirrer (BA.37110-0238, Bel-Art Products, Pequannock, NJ, USA), and beakers were covered with plastic wrap to prevent evaporation. After dissolution, samples were refrigerated at 5 °C. Approximately 3 h before testing, 60 mL aliquots were dispensed into odorless disposable cups (7 cm diameter, 4 cm height; Samboopack Corp., Incheon, South Korea) and covered with lids. Samples were served at room temperature (23–28 °C). For protocol 1, reference samples were prepared as per Ko et al. (2020) and included four sucrose concentrations: 0.5% w/w (0.3 points), 3.5% (3 points), 7.5% (9 points), and 12% (14 points).

Subjects

A total of 166 female participants from Ewha Womans University, aged 19–38 years and without known sweetener allergies, were recruited via online announcement for sensory evaluation. As recruitment was conducted at a women's university, all participants were female; sex was therefore not a controlled or analyzed variable in the present study, and the implications of this for generalizing the results are addressed in Section 4 (Discussion). Protocol 1 is a form of generic descriptive analysis and included 10 trained sensory panel (19–36 years, mean ± SD: 24.2 ± 4.6 years), matching the panel size used in the study of (Choi and Chung, 2014), which served as the reference protocol for the present investigation. Protocols 2 and 3 each included 51 participants (20–37 years, mean ± SD: 24.6 ± 4.1 years); protocol 4 included 54 participants (20–38 years, mean ± SD: 24.6 ± 2.8 years). For Protocols 2–4, participant numbers (n = 51–54) were selected based on previous psychophysical studies employing gLMS methodology and concentration–response evaluation of sweeteners (Low et al., 2017; Wee et al., 2018), which commonly recruited approximately 40–60 participants. Therefore, the sample sizes used in the present study were comparable to those reported in previous sweetness perception and psychophysical scaling studies. Prior to testing, participants were instructed to abstain from food or beverages, especially those with strong flavors such as coffee—as well as smoking and the use of fragranced personal care products or cosmetics for at least 1 h before each session.

Sensory evaluation procedures

This study was approved by the Ewha Womans University Institutional Review Board (IRB; protocols Ewha-202206-0038 and Ewha-202008-0020) and conducted in accordance with the university's ethical guidelines.

All protocols included a training session and a main evaluation. Protocol 1 followed established methods (Choi and Chung, 2014; Ko et al., 2020), with a 5-day training program to enhance panelist accuracy in rating sweetness, bitterness, and astringency using sucrose, caffeine, and tannin solutions at various concentrations. These attributes were chosen for their impact on sweetener sensory profiles. Panelists were trained to rank the sweetness, bitterness, and astringency intensities on a 15-point scale, calibrated with four sucrose reference standards (0.5%, 3.5%, 7.5%, and 12%) corresponding to anchors at 0.3, 3, 9, and 14 points. Calibration for sweetness intensity evaluation was performed in each session prior to testing. In the main experiment, panelists rated the intensity of sensory attributes (taste, flavor, texture) for each sweetener sample at various concentrations using criteria established during training. Attributes were scored on a 15-point scale (0 = not detected, 7 = moderate, 14 = very strong). For protocol 1, each session included 12 samples (6 sucrose concentrations, 6 sweetener SECs), with serving order determined by WLS design. To minimize sensory fatigue, only 12 samples were evaluated per session, each completed in approximately 5 min, including breaks. All samples were evaluated in triplicate. Panelists rinsed their palates with warm spring water and ate unsalted crackers (Carr's Table Water, Carr's of Carlisle, Ltd., Carlisle, UK) between samples.

For protocols 2–4, training focused on proper use of the gLMS, following the Korean version of Hayes et al. (2013). Panelists practiced rating remembered/imagined sensations (e.g., warmth of lukewarm water, pain from biting the tongue, sweetness of banana, burning sensation of eating a whole hot pepper, weight of a feather on the hand) (Table 3), as described by Fujimaru et al. (2012) and Low et al. (2017), using the gLMS. Actual stimuli (sucrose solutions at 1%, 2%, and 5%; caffeine solutions at 0.01%, 0.02%, and 0.04%; mogroside V solution at 0.05%) were also included to improve accuracy in evaluating sweetener characteristics. In the main evaluation, panelists rated sweetness, bitterness, sweet aftertaste, bitter aftertaste, and astringency for each sample at different concentrations. Validation test of modeled C–R curve

To validate the accuracy of the modeled C–R curves and sweetener potencies from each protocol, sweetness intensities at interpolated concentrations were evaluated and compared with those of equivalent sucrose concentrations. Validation test was carried out to measure the level context effect present. The accuracy of sweetness potency of a sweetener indicates weaker context effect existing in the protocol applied. For protocol 1 (SSC-15PRS-WLS), validation was conducted at 6% SEC, representing the mid-range of the tested concentrations. This choice minimizes bias and contextual effects associated with extreme concentrations in the 15PRS. For protocols 2 and 3 (SSC-gLMS-WLS and T-gLMS-WLS), validation was performed at both low (1.5%) and high (8%) SECs to assess the reliability of the gLMS across a wide range. This approach accounts for non-linear perception and potential overestimation of sweetness potency at low concentrations in traditional scales. No validation was performed for protocol 4, as its results did not differ significantly from those of protocol 3.

Statistical analysis

Sweetness potency across concentrations was determined for all protocols using the calculation described by Ko et al. (2020). For protocols 2–4, gLMS responses were transformed using a common logarithm (log10) prior to analysis. This approach follows previous LMS/gLMS studies, which reported that magnitude estimation and LMS intensity ratings tend to approximate a log-normal distribution and are commonly analyzed after logarithmic transformation (Green et al., 1993, 1996). The logarithmic mean sweetness intensity at each concentration was then calculated and regression analysis was conducted for C–R curve modeling. For each sweetener, both linear and logarithmic (natural log) regression models were evaluated, and the model with the higher coefficient of determination (R2) was selected as the final model because it provided the best fit to the observed mean sweetness intensity data across concentrations. Logarithmic means were calculated in SPSS (version 26; IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA), and C–R curve modeling was performed using XLSTAT software. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess protocol effects on sweetness intensity for each sweetener, using a generalized linear model (GLM): [Sweetness intensity = Protocol + Concentration level + (Protocol × Concentration level)].

Discussion

The aim of this research was to find the most effective sensory evaluation protocol for representing the C-R curve of sweeteners' sweetness intensity using gLMS. The study involved a comparison of protocols involving different serving sets, sample serving order, and evaluation scales. It undertook a comprehensive exploration of the contextual bias effect when rating the sweetness intensities of various sweeteners with the gLMS scale.

When investigating the impact of evaluation scale on context effects in evaluating sweetness intensity of sweeteners by using SSC-15PRS-WLS and SSC-gLMS-WLS, the sweetness potency evaluated by 15-points category rating scales showed extremely high in low concentration compared to gLMS especially in high potency sweeteners. In the case of using gLMS to measure the sweetness potency, sweetness potency decreased from low concentration to high concentration gradually for high potency sweeteners such as aspartame, saccharin, and acesulfame-K. In the C-R curve validation test, using 15-points category rating method also showed that saccharin was significantly lower than the same concentration of sucrose. However, the C-R curves modeling from gLMS were validated almost similar to sucrose at low and high concentration. These results suggest that using gLMS for evaluating sweetness intensity of sweetener to model C-R curves might be more reliable than using the 15-point category scales. The reason could be the range bias of samples being evaluated by gLMS and 15-points category scales. The range bias and spacing bias, which are some examples of context effect, can influence how different sensory intensities of a sample are perceived based on the range of intensities presented during the testing session (Poulton, 1977, 1979). Range bias occurs when a fixed range is used in the response scale, regardless of the actual stimulus range. On the other hand, stimulus spacing bias happens when the range of the scale is adjusted differently, without considering the spacing of the stimuli. In the previous study, line scales was found less susceptible to range bias than the category rating procedure. Stillman (1993) found that when subjects used the line-length matching method such as magnitude estimation, their perception of sweetness was less influenced by the different sucrose concentrations presented to them compared to when they used the category rating method. Therefore, line scales such as gLMS might be a more reliable method to shift the contextual effect.

Using different sample serving sets (SSC method and T-method) to model the C-R curves of sweetener with 15 points category rating scales has shown a context effect in T-method but SSC method showed to remedy the context effect in previous study. However, when using the gLMS to evaluate the sweetness intensities of sweeteners, the contextual effect was minimally present, and both T-method and SSC produced similar C-R curve. In the validation of C-R curve test, all of the sweetness evaluated by SSC and T-method have no significant difference to sucrose at low concentration and high concentration.

The gLMS possesses unique psychophysical properties that are critical for accurate metrology. Unlike traditional category scales, which rely on discrete, equidistant intervals that may not capture the nuances between labels, the gLMS is designed to align with the actual magnitude of a subject's experience (Green et al., 1996). By providing a continuous, quasi-logarithmic range anchored by 'strongest imaginable sensation,' the gLMS allows for more relevant intensity ratings and valid across-group comparisons that discrete category scales cannot facilitate. Scale-induced biases, such as the ceiling effect, occur when a measurement system cannot accommodate high-level sensations, leading to a loss of resolution at the upper end of the scale. The gLMS has been found to reduce this effect by allowing for a greater breadth of responses at high intensities (González-Fernández et al., 2014). Furthermore, by utilizing scale extremes that reference universal sensations rather than specific food experiences, the gLMS minimizes the bias inherent in traditional 9-point or 15-point scales, where extreme labels often fail to capture the actual magnitude of a subject's sensory experience (Kalva et al., 2014).

Presentation order often dictates the perceived intensity of samples in sensory trials, yet our study found that T-gLMS-WLS and T-gLMS-ASD produced nearly identical C-R curves and sweetness potency values. This reinforces the idea that the gLMS effectively neutralizes the contextual bias often created by sample sequencing. While a minor discrepancy was noted for fructose—where potency decreased slightly in the ASD protocol but remained 'constant' in the WLS protocol—the overall difference was not statistically significant. Even though WLS is the established standard for yielding accurate, balanced results (Lee et al., 2000), our data indicates that the gLMS provides a robust framework that remains reliable across various serving designs.

Generally, distinct C–R curves characteristics were observed between bulk sweeteners and high-intensity sweeteners. Fructose and erythritol exhibited relatively stable sweetness potency values across the tested concentration range and generated C–R curves that were more similar to that of sucrose. In contrast, the high-intensity sweeteners, particularly acesulfame-K and saccharin, exhibited stronger concentration dependency, with sweetness potency decreasing as concentration increased. This behavior is consistent with previous reports that high-intensity sweeteners often display nonlinear psychophysical responses across a wide concentration range. Despite these differences in sweetness characteristics, gLMS-based protocols produced generally consistent sweetness potency estimates for both bulk and high-intensity sweeteners across serving methodologies and presentation orders. The applicability of gLMS is not limited to a specific sweetener category and that the scale can effectively characterize sweeteners with markedly different potency ranges and C-R curves patterns.

To clarify the innovative aspects of the present study relative to previous work, a comparison between the current study and that of Choi and Chung (2014) is summarized in Table 9. While both studies aimed to identify an optimal sensory evaluation protocol for generating accurate C–R curves of sweeteners, the previous study primarily focused on contextual bias associated with sample serving sets and demonstrated that the SSC method improved the accuracy of sweetness potency measurements when using a 15-point category rating scale. In contrast, the present study expanded the investigation to multiple sources of contextual variation, including evaluation scale, sample serving set, and sample presentation order. More importantly, the results demonstrated that sweetness potency estimates obtained using the gLMS remained generally consistent across protocols, regardless of serving methodology or presentation order. These findings suggest that the gLMS is inherently less susceptible to contextual bias than traditional category rating scales and may provide a more robust and standardized approach for sweetness potency measurement. The proposed protocol may therefore improve the reproducibility and comparability of sweetness potency data across future sensory studies and support the development of reduced-sugar food and beverage The primary limitation of this study is the reliance on a female-only pool for collecting the data, which may not fully represent the sensory responses of the general population. Some studies report that women perceive taste stimuli, including sweetness, as somewhat more intense than men (Barragán et al., 2018); however, this effect is inconsistent, as a large population study (n = 1119) found that gender did not significantly affect the rated sweetness intensity of sucrose, even though it did influence bitterness perception (Dinnella et al., 2018). More importantly, sweetness potency in the present study was expressed as the sweetness of a target sweetener relative to sucrose evaluated by the same panel. Because any general sex-related tendency in intensity scaling applies similarly to both the target sweetener and the sucrose reference, such a tendency is largely cancelled in the relative potency ratio. Sex differences specific to particular sweetener types—which would not cancel in this ratio—have not been commonly reported. The female-only sample is therefore unlikely to have substantially biased the sweetness potency estimates, although absolute intensity values should be extrapolated to male or mixed-sex populations with caution.

One plausible exception concerns sweeteners with pronounced bitter side-tastes, such as saccharin and acesulfame-K, whose off-taste is mediated by specific bitter taste receptors of the TAS2R family (Kuhn et al., 2004). Sensitivity to this receptor-specific bitterness varies among individuals owing to genetic polymorphisms (Allen et al., 2013), which could in principle introduce sweetener-specific variation in perceived intensity, and thus in potency, for these compounds. Whether such individual differences translate into systematic differences between male and female perceivers has not yet been established and warrants further investigation. Future studies using demographically inclusive—and ideally TAS2R-genotyped—samples are recommended to confirm that the rank order and concentration-dependence of sweetener potency, particularly for bitter-tinged high-intensity sweeteners, are not sex-dependent.

Conclusion

This study built on previous research (Choi and Chung, 2014) to identify the most effective protocol for modeling sweetener C–R curves using the gLMS. Four protocols were compared, varying serving set (T-method vs. SSC method), sample presentation order (ASD vs. WLS), and evaluation scale (15PRS vs. gLMS).

Three hypotheses were tested: 1) gLMS would produce more accurate and reliable C–R curves than the 15PRS—supported by more consistent and precise measurements with gLMS; 2) the SSC method, combined with gLMS, would further reduce contextual bias compared to the T-method—this was not supported, as no additional bias reduction was observed; 3) WLS presentation order would minimize contextual bias more than ASD order—this was also not supported, with no significant improvement in accuracy found for WLS over ASD.

Overall, the results suggest that the gLMS is less susceptible to contextual influences associated with serving set, sample presentation order, and evaluation scale than the traditional 15-point category rating scale. Although some protocol-dependent differences remained for specific sweeteners, sweetness potency estimates derived using the gLMS were generally more consistent across experimental conditions. These findings support the usefulness of the gLMS as a robust tool for C-R curve modeling and provide a practical framework for the standardization of sweetness potency measurements.
