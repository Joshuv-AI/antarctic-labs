Bivalve mollusks as sentinels: Molecular detection of Toxoplasma gondii on Maranhão Island in northeastern Brazil
Authors: Ellainy Maria Conceição Silva, Anna Leticia Pinto Silva, Luiz Ricardo Gonçalves, Gabriel Garcês Santos, Maria do Socorro Costa Oliveira, Rosângela Zacarias Machado, Nancyleni Pinto Chaves Bezerra, Alcina Vieira de Carvalho Neta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bivalve mollusks belong to a class of invertebrates that are cosmopolitan and globally traded. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the occurrence and genetic assessment of Toxoplasma gondii, based on the SAG1 gene in bivalve mollusks from natural growing areas on Maranhão Island in northeastern Brazil. Oysters (Crassostrea sp.), mussels (Mytella sp.) and clams (Anomalocardia sp.) were collected from natural mangrove áreas in São Luís, Paço do Lumiar, Raposa and São José de Ribamar on Maranhão Island during the period of January to December 2022 (rainy and dry seasons). The samples were organized into pools of gills from 3 animals, their DNA was extracted and subjected to detection of T. gondii (SAG-1 gene), and the positive samples subjected to additional nested PCR for the markers APICO, BTUB, SAG3, 3′ SAG2, 5’ SAG2, Alt.SAG2 and GRA6 for genetic characterization. Sequences obtained were analyzed for phylogenetic reconstruction using the MEGA X program. Three mussel samples tested positive (3/60; 1.8%), all collected in the rainy season from Raposa and São José de Ribamar. These samples were subjected to nested-PCR for other T. gondii markers; however, there was no amplification. BLASTn analysis confirmed 98 to 100% genetic similarity with T. gondii sequences. Although based on a single gene, which limits robust genotype inference, phylogenetic analysis of the SAG1 gene indicated clustering of the detected sequences with reference sequences related to classical genotypes. The current study provides an update on the molecular occurrence of the zoonotic protozoan T. gondii in an estuarine area of northeastern Brazil, and highlights the importance of research involving monitoring of shellfish harvesting areas, given their role in public health and food safety.

Introduction

The causative agent of toxoplasmosis, Toxoplasma gondii, is an obligate intracellular protozoan capable of infecting mammals and birds. This zoonotic protozoan has a cosmopolitan distribution, and its global prevalence among human populations is estimated to be 30% (Molan et al., 2019). Taxonomically, this protozoan is classified within the phylum Apicomplexa, subclass Coccidia, order Eucoccidiorida, and family Sarcocystidae, genus Toxoplasma, which includes only one known species, T. gondii (Levine, 1988).

Felids are the only definitive hosts of T. gondii and are responsible for shedding environmentally oocysts in their feces. Although oocyst shedding generally occurs for a relatively short period, millions of oocysts may be excreted and dispersed into the environment (Jones and Dubey, 2010; Fayer et al., 2004). Through rainfall, surface runoff, and sewage contamination, these oocysts can be transported from terrestrial environments into rivers, estuaries, and coastal waters, where they may be retained by filter-feeding aquatic organisms (VanWormer et al., 2016; Li et al., 2022).

The presence of T. gondii in aquatic environments has been widely reported, with bivalve mollusks recognized as important bioindicators of environmental contamination by this pathogen (Escobedo et al., 2010; Marín-García et al., 2022; Souza et al., 2013; Shapiro et al., 2019). In Brazil, species of the genera Crassostrea, Mytella, Perna and Anomalocardia are widely harvested from natural mangrove areas and aquaculture systems (Cova et al., 2015; Pinto and Boehs, 2008; Rondinelli, 2009). These mollusks are of considerable importance both for food security—due to their accessibility and high protein content—and for the economy, through job creation via their commercial exploitation (Valenti and New, 2000).

In the state of Maranhão, these animals are traditionally consumed raw or lightly cooked. This practice may represent a risk to public health, as experimental bioassays have confirmed that T. gondii oocysts, once filtered by bivalve mollusks, can remain infectious for up to 85 days post-exposure. This finding demonstrates not only the ability of mollusks to retain oocysts for long periods but also that these oocysts remain viable and capable of infecting other hosts (Lindsay et al., 2004).

Current Brazilian legislation provides guidelines on health standards, good practices, and biosecurity for cultivated mollusks. However, in many coastal regions, artisanal practices often involve the direct extraction of mollusks from natural banks, frequently without adherence to sanitary regulations or from unsuitable areas.

This situation is particularly evident in Brazilian states such as Maranhão, where poor sanitary conditions heighten the risks of infection by zoonotic protozoa. To date, few studies have been conducted to investigate T. gondii on the island of Maranhão (Silva et al., 2020), and this is the first study in this locality to generate sequence data for T. gondii detected in bivalve mollusks. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the occurrence of T. gondii in oysters, mussels, and clams from Maranhão island.

Material and methods

Study location

The study was conducted in the municipalities of São Luís, Paço do Lumiar, Raposa, and São José de Ribamar, which are located on Maranhão Island, at the geographic coordinates Latitude: −2.53073, Longitude: −44.3068, 2° 31′ 51″ South, 44° 18′ 24″ West (Fig. 1). These municipalities are characterized by a humid tropical climate, with high temperatures prevailing for most of the year. On average, the minimum is 23 °C in the mildest months, while the maximum is 32 °C in the hottest months. Furthermore, the region has two well-defined seasons throughout the year: a rainy season from January to June, and a dry season from July to December. Annual rainfall averages vary from 1800 mm to 2000 mm. Animals and sample collection

In the present study, oysters (Crassostrea sp.), mussels (Mytella sp.) and clams (Anomalocardia sp.) were analyzed, all collected from mangrove areas in the aforementioned municipalities. Samples were collected from January to May 2022 (rainy season) and from September to December 2022 (dry season), always from the same areas, selected based on convenience and availability of bivalves in natural stocks.

After collection, the mollusks were opened using a scalpel blade and examined for color, odor, and any macroscopic alterations. For T. gondii molecular detection, gill tissue was selected because it represents a primary interface for particle retention in filter-feeding bivalves, with comparable detection sensitivity to other tissues. The tissues were then stored in microtubes at −20 °C for molecular analyses.A total of 90 individuals of each bivalve species (oysters, mussels, and clams) were collected during each seasonal period (dry and rainy), totaling 270 animals per period and 540 in the entire study. The animals were then divided into pools consisting of gills from three individuals (Esmerini et al., 2010), from the same period and collection site, resulting in 30 pools per species in each period. Thus, 90 pools were formed for animals collected in the rainy season (30 oysters, 30 mussels, and 30 clams) and 90 pools in the dry season.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted and purified from pooled gill tissues using the phenol-chloroform method, as described by Cochenn ec et al. (2000). Tissue samples were digested overnight at 50 °C with proteinase K, however, it should be noted that this protocol has not been specifically validated for the disruption of intact T. gondii oocysts potentially present in the samples The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA samples were assessed using a spectrophotometer.

Molecular analysis of DNA extracted from bivalve mollusks

Detection of the universal endogenous 18S rRNA gene of eukaryotes

To verify the quality of the extraction, the integrity of the DNA obtained, and/or the presence of reaction inhibitors, all the samples were subjected to PCR for confirmation of the amplification of the endogenous 18S gene of eukaryotes, according to the protocol described by Fajardo et al. (2008), which amplifies a product of 140 base pairs (bp) (Supplementary Table S1).

Molecular detection of Toxoplasma gondii

To detect the presence of T. gondii DNA, samples were initially screened using nested PCR (nPCR) targeting the SAG1 gene. The primary PCR was performed using primers F1 and R1 and the secondary PCR with primers F2 and R2 (Table 1), according to the protocol described by Grigg et al. (2001). Each reaction had a final volume of 25 μL, consisting of 12.5 μL of PCR Master Mix Promega© (TaqPolymerase, dNTPs, MgCl2, buffer), 1 μL of each primer (2.5 pmol), 8.5 μL of nuclease-free water (Promega©) and 2 μL of extracted DNA at a concentration of 250 ng/μL. Positive controls consisted of T. gondii DNA from the ME49 strain, provided by the Protozoology Laboratory at Fiocruz. Ultrapure water was used as the negative control. Positive and negative controls were included in all PCR assays.

The samples positive for the SAG-1 gene were then subjected to additional amplifications for the markers APICO, BTUB, SAG3, 5′-SAG2, 3′-SAG2, alt.SAG2, and GRA6 (Table 1) to genetically characterize the positive samples. Sequencing and amplification of the primer nucleotide were performed according to the method proposed by Dubey et al. (2006) and Su et al. (2006).

Electrophoresis, purification and sequencing

All the PCR products were subjected to horizontal electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gel stained with SYBR Safe (Invitrogen®), using TBE running buffer at pH 8.0 to visualize the results.

Amplified products positive for the tested gene were purified using a Wizard® SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up System (Promega®) commercial kit, following the manufacturer's instructions. DNA was quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). Sanger sequencing was conducted following the dideoxynucleotide chain-termination method (Sanger et al., 1977), using an ABI PRISM 3700 Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Sequencing was carried out by ACTGene Molecular Analyses Ltda., Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil.

BLASTn and phylogenetic assessment

The nucleotide sequences obtained were analyzed for quality using FinchTV 1.4 software (Geospiza, Inc., Seattle, WA, USA). Consensus sequences were generated using the CAP3 Sequence Assembly Program (Huang and Madan, 1999). Using BLASTn (Altschul et al., 1990), the consensus sequences were identified by comparing them with others previously deposited in GenBank.

For phylogenetic analysis, the obtained sequences of the SAG1 gene were aligned with 38 homologous sequences retrieved from GenBank and ToxoDB (http://toxodb.org/toxo/), specific to T. gondii, using the Clustal/W tool via BioEdit 7.2 (Hall, 1999). Phylogenetic trees were constructed based on the Neighbor-Joining and Maximum likelihood methods, with 1000 bootstrap replications for validation. The Akaike information criterion (AIC) available in MEGA X (Tamura et al., 2021) was applied to identify the most suitable nucleotide substitution model.

The sequences generated in this search were deposited in GenBank and assigned accession numbers PV013025; PV013026 and PV013027.

Results

Detection of Toxoplasma gondii based on the SAG1 gene

Among the 180 tested pools (regardless of seasonal period and species) of DNA samples subjected to nPCR screening, 3 pools (3/180; 1.67%) were positive. All the positive samples were collected during the rainy season and belonged to pools of animals of the same genus (Mytella). Two of these pools came from the municipality of São José de Ribamar, and one from that of Raposa. These three pools were tested for the markers APICO, BTUB, SAG3, 5′-SAG2, 3′-SAG2, alt.SAG2, and GRA6, but amplification of these targets was not performed.

Analysis of sequenced products

All three positive pools were sequenced and presented quality of the assembled consensus sequences, which were successfully compared in BLASTn and subjected to subsequent genetic analyses. The samples presented genetic identity percentages ranging from 98 to 100% homology with T. gondii sequences deposited in the database for the SAG1 gene, from different locations and hosts, confirming the occurrence of the agent in mussels in the studied location. Query coverage ranged from 99 to 100% and all the samples showed an e-value of 0.0, confirming the quality of the sequenced products.

A single gene (SAG1) was successfully amplified, with no amplification observed for the other tested markers, which represented a limitation of the present study and restricted broader genetic analyses. However, as a comparative approach, the phylogenetic analysis was performed and demonstrated the formation of two clades, one of them composed of genotype I sequences and the other of genotype II and III sequences. The three sequences amplified in this study were grouped with sequences belonging to clades II and III (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Discussion

This study provides important data on the occurrence of T. gondii in bivalve mollusks from natural growing areas in mangroves on Maranhão Island. It also updates epidemiological information about the presence of zoonotic protozoa that may pose a risk to consumers, highlighting the sanitary conditions of these mollusks. Molecular tests for the detection of T. gondii revealed the positivity of three pools (1.8%) of Mytella sp., collected from two municipalities on the island, Raposa and São José de Ribamar.

Investigations into T. gondii in bivalves have been conducted in several countries and across different species of oysters, mussels, and other shellfish, which are important bioindicators of environmental contamination (Robertson, 2007). Globally, contamination rates vary significantly, with occurrences ranging from 0.007% to 46.3% in the United States (Marquis et al., 2015, 2019; Miller et al., 2008; Shapiro et al., 2019; Staggs et al., 2015) and from 2.8% to 2.68% in China (Cong et al., 2017, 2019, 2021).

In Brazil, T. gondii has been previously detected in bivalve mollusks in the states of São Paulo (Esmerini et al., 2010) and Bahia (Santos, 2014; Ribeiro et al., 2015), with detection rates of 3.3%, 7.8%, and 8.1%, respectively. In the state of Maranhão, a single previous study conducted by Silva et al. (2020) in the municipality of Primeira Cruz—located 104.91 km from Maranhão Island, the collection site of the current study—identified T. gondii DNA in 2.5% of oysters (Crassostrea sp.). These findings indicate that research into the occurrence of T. gondii in bivalve mollusks in Brazil remains limited, and that the positivity rates observed in the present study are consistent with those reported in other regions. Additionally, the detection of T. gondii in mussels highlights their potential as a valuable sample source for molecular surveys, given their widespread distribution in natural beds.

In the current study, all the positive pools were detected during the rainy season, whereas no positive samples were identified during the dry season. These findings suggest a possible relationship between rainfall and the presence of T. gondii in bivalve mollusks, as previously observed in other regions where the parasite's occurrence was higher during periods of intense precipitation, as reported by Coupe et al., in (2018) and Shapiro et al., in 2015. Increased rainfall may contribute to the mobilization of oocysts from contaminated soils to estuarine environments via runoff (Ureña López et al., 2022). However, due to the limited number of positive samples, further long-term studies are necessary to better understand the seasonal dynamics of T. gondii contamination in this region.

Although the three positive pools were subjected to multilocus PCR using additional markers no amplification was obtained for these targets. This finding highlights a limitation commonly associated with multilocus genotyping of T. gondii. Previous studies have demonstrated that complete amplification across all loci is not always achieved, often due to factors related to the quantity and quality of parasite DNA.

In congenital infections, multilocus nested PCR-RFLP applied to amniotic fluid samples resulted in amplification of only a subset of markers, with some samples failing to amplify any loci despite confirmed infection (Targa et al., 2022). Similarly, a study involving food matrices has shown that PCR-positive samples frequently yield incomplete genotyping profiles, with successful amplification restricted to one or a few loci (Sroka et al., 2019).

This limitation is particularly relevant for environmental samples, such as bivalve mollusks, in which parasite DNA may be present in low quantities and unevenly distributed within tissues. In this context, the absence of amplification of additional markers in the present study likely reflects these inherent constraints. Consequently, this limitation restricted a more detailed genetic characterization of the obtained sequences.

The molecular data obtained in this study provide preliminary insights into the genetic similarity of T. gondii detected in bivalve mollusks from Maranhão. BLASTn analysis of the SAG1 sequences confirmed high sequence identity, and phylogenetic analysis showed clustering with sequences related to classical lineages, including Types II and III. However, it is important to emphasize that these observations are based on a single genetic marker and, therefore, do not allow robust inference of genotype or population structure.

Studies conducted on bivalve mollusks in other regions have identified the presence of both classical and atypical genotypes. Cong et al. (2019, 2021) characterized the Chinese genotype 1 (ToxoDB#9) and the classical genotype II in bivalves from various locations in both China and the United States. Similarly, Shapiro et al. (2019) identified classical genotypes I and II/III, as well as an atypical genotype X associated with domestic felids, circulating in mussels. Although amplification of additional loci was unsuccessful in the present study, limiting a broader genetic assessment, our findings expand the available molecular data on T. gondii in bivalves from northeastern Brazil.

Given the widespread consumption of these mollusks, often raw or undercooked, continuous monitoring of harvesting areas is crucial, especially considering the role of bivalves as bioindicators of environmental contamination and potential vehicles for foodborne transmission of this protozoan when viable oocysts are present. However, it is important to note that the detection of T. gondii DNA alone does not necessarily indicate the presence of viable parasites or a direct risk of infection. In addition, since no specific protocol targeting oocyst wall disruption was employed during DNA extraction, the possibility that the detected signal originated from environmental DNA (eDNA) rather than intact oocysts cannot be excluded. Nevertheless these findings highlight the need for implementation of sanitary control measures in shellfish harvesting areas.

Conclusions

In this study, T. gondii DNA was detected in mussels collected from estuarine areas of Maranhão Island, northeastern Brazil, reinforcing the role of bivalve mollusks as indicators of environmental contamination by this protozoan. Sequence analysis of the SAG1 marker showed high similarity to reference sequences sequences from T. gondii, providing preliminary molecular information of the parasite circulating in the study area. However, because the analysis was based on a single genetic marker, broader inferences regarding genotype diversity or population structure are limited. These findings highlight the importance of continuous sanitary monitoring of shellfish harvesting areas and support the need for future studies addressing parasite viability and multilocus genetic characterization.
