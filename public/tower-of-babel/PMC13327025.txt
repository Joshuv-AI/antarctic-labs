Combine Short‐Segment Fixation and Leverage Reduction Technique to Correct Kyphotic Angle in Kümmell's Disease
Authors: Yunwei Long, Guangzi Chen, Delu Zeng, Xiangyu Tang, Chaoxu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveKümmell disease is a prevalent spinal condition characterized by vertebral fractures and subsequent spinal nerve injury, significantly impacting patients' quality of life and imposing a considerable economic burden on society. Current treatment modalities, including percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) and kyphoplasty (PKP), often fail to alleviate symptoms or restore vertebral height in advanced stages of the disease, highlighting the urgent need for improved surgical interventions. This study aims to evaluate the surgical outcomes of short‐segment fixation combined with leverage reduction technique in patients with Kümmell disease accompanied by kyphosis deformity.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 22 patients who were treated with short‐segment fixation and leverage reduction technique between 2020 and 2023. Clinical evaluation, including operation time, blood loss volume, and postoperative complications, were collected. The visual analog scale (VAS) score for pain and Frankel classification were used to evaluate the patients' functional outcome. The radiographic analysis included local kyphotic angle (LKA), regional kyphotic angle (RKA), anterior vertebral height (AVH), posterior vertebral height (PVH), and sagittal compression (SC). The student t‐test and the χ 2‐test (or the Fisher exact test) were used to compare the outcome measures between the preoperative group and postoperative group.ResultsThe results demonstrate that the surgical technique led to limited intro‐operative blood loss (average 85.7 ± 29.4 mL) and short surgery duration (average 100.1 ± 36.3 min), and significantly improved pain status postoperatively (p < 0.05). Average vertebral height restoration reached 83% ± 14% of predicted normal height, with mean kyphotic angle correction of 19.32° ± 6.27°. Notably, the proportion of patients classified as Frankel grade D and E improved significantly at the last follow‐up (p < 0.05), indicating favorable neurological recovery. Although there was an instance of cement leakage, no severe complications such as internal fixation failure or neurological damage occurred.ConclusionsThe results suggest that short‐segment fixation combined with leverage reduction technique is a promising surgical strategy for patients with Kümmell disease and kyphosis deformity, effectively alleviating pain, restoring vertebral height, and promoting neurological recovery.

Introduction

Delayed vertebral collapse after trauma was first reported by Hermann Kümmell in 1895 [1]. The characteristic of this disease is low back pain accompanied by kyphosis, which usually occurs several weeks or months after injury. With the continuous development of social aging, osteoporosis has become a common disease worldwide [2, 3]. One of the complications of this disease is brittle compression fractures, with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF) being the most common type [4, 5]. Research has shown that approximately 30% of OVCF cases cannot heal after conservative treatment [6, 7]. Subsequently, 13.5% of patients developed bone non‐union, and according to reports, 7%–13% of OVCF patients developed vertebral fissure sign [8, 9, 10]. In addition, it has been reported that the prognosis of OVCF with intravertebral vacuum cleft (IVC) is associated with Kümmell's disease (KD), which manifests as delayed vertebral collapse after trauma [6, 7, 8, 11, 12].

Kümmell's disease, characterized by vertebral body fractures and subsequent spinal cord injury, poses significant challenges to patient quality of life due to persistent pain and functional disability. This condition often leads to considerable socioeconomic burdens, necessitating effective management strategies. Current treatment modalities, such as percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) and kyphoplasty (PKP), although widely utilized, demonstrate limited efficacy in late‐stage Kümmell's disease, particularly in cases where the disease has progressed to stage three [13]. Thus, there is a pressing need for innovative surgical interventions that can yield better clinical outcomes for patients suffering from this debilitating condition.

Recent studies indicate that surgical approaches integrating long‐segment fixation (LSF) with cement‐augmented screws show promise in alleviating symptoms associated with Kümmell's disease, particularly in patients with concurrent spinal nerve injury [14]. However, LSF necessitates fusion of otherwise functional spinal segments, resulting in a 69.7%–80.3% reduction in lumbar range of motion according to biomechanical studies [15]. Additionally, LSF often achieves kyphosis correction through compensatory hyperlordosis of unfused segments rather than direct vertebral height restoration. This predisposes to proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) with an increased incidence of 17%–40% [16]. Leverage technique using monoaxial pedicle screw for reduction of thoracolumbar compression fractures was described in our previous study [17], which was considered a potential strategy to restore the height of vertebra in Kümmell's disease.

The present study undertakes a retrospective analysis of clinical data from patients diagnosed with Kümmell's disease who underwent surgery adopting short‐segment fixation with cement‐augmented screws and leverage reduction technique between 2020 and 2023. By analyzing existing clinical data, the study aims to provide a thorough assessment of treatment outcomes and complication rates. Specifically, the focus is on evaluating the impact of leverage technique using monoaxial pedicle screws on restoring and maintaining the height of vertebrae, with the goal of developing a more effective treatment paradigm that can be readily integrated into clinical practice.

Methods

Patients

With the approval of the hospital's ethics committee (TJ‐IRB20230917), we retrospectively analyzed patients with Kümmell's disease who underwent surgery in our hospital from 2020 to 2023. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) all patients were diagnosed with vertebral fractures by X‐ray or CT examination, and the “vacuum cleft sign” was shown on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). (2) The height loss of the vertebral body was no less than 20%. (3) All patients presented with or without symptoms of spinal nerve damage. (4) Preoperative imaging confirmed the integrity of the posterior ligamentous complex at the surgical segment. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of surgical contraindications; (2) history of surgery at the injured segment; and (3) presence of malignant tumors or infection. The indication of our surgical technique included: (1) Imaging findings demonstrate characteristic features of the fractured vertebra: preoperative radiographs reveal vertebral fracture, CT clearly shows an intravertebral vacuum cleft (IVC), and dynamic radiographs demonstrate instability‐related findings of the fractured vertebra; (2) the patient's bone mineral density meets the diagnostic criteria for osteoporosis, with bone density T‐score less than −2.5 SD; and (3) spinal stenosis with spinal cord and nerve compression with or without neurological symptoms. Contraindications included: (1) the patient has a known allergy to bone cement; (2) damage to the posterior wall of the affected vertebral body; and (3) absence of vertebral breathing sign on dynamic radiographs. According to the above criteria, 22 patients were enrolled in this study.

Surgical Procedure

The surgical technique was described in detail as below: (1) anesthesia and intraoperative positioning: After the patient was under general anesthesia, they were placed in the prone position. The surgical table was adjusted to maintain the spine in a neutral alignment, avoiding excessive flexion or extension to prevent further vertebral injury, followed by routine disinfection and draping. (2) Surgical approach and exposure: After a midline incision was made through the skin, subcutaneous layers, and thoracolumbar fascia, the paraspinal muscles were dissected along the intermuscular plane to expose the spinous processes, laminae, and facet joints of the injured vertebra and the adjacent upper and lower vertebrae, while carefully protecting the paraspinal muscles and neural elements throughout the surgery. (3) Pedicle screw insertion: Under C‐arm fluoroscopy guidance, four cannulated multiaxial pedicle screws were placed in the adjacent upper and lower vertebrae of the injured vertebra, and two cannulated monoaxial pedicle screws were placed in the injured vertebra, ensuring accurate screw placement without violating the anterior vertebral wall or the pedicle cortex. (4) Reinforcement of adjacent vertebral screws with bone cement: Confirmation of no cement leakage under fluoroscopy; 1–2 mL of bone cement was injected into each multiaxial screw to augment screw fixation stability (A and a). (5) Prebending and provisional placement of titanium rods: After the bone cement had completely solidified, two titanium rods were contoured according to the normal physiological curve of the fractured segment and then provisionally placed into the heads of the pedicle screws. (6) The leverage reduction technique described in previous literature was conducted [17]: First, the rods were placed, and the nuts of the caudal screws were tightened. Then, a rod gripper was locked on the rod, which closely touched the inferior edge of the monoaxial screws. The nuts of the monoaxial screws on both sides were tightened simultaneously. In the process of tightening the nuts of monoaxial screws with the rod holder as the fulcrum, the screw tail shifted caudally, and the tip rose toward the cranial side. The upper endplates of the injured vertebra were elevated and height was restored. Afterwards, the nuts in the upper screws were locked in situ without any distraction. The initial reduction was completed at this point (B and b). (7) Cement augmentation of fractured vertebral: Then, one titanium rod was maintained in place while the other was temporarily removed. Bone cement was injected through the screws of the injured vertebra, allowing it to diffuse around the screws from the tip and side holes to fill the fissures and strengthen the screws. The bone cement should diffuse across the midline of the vertebra to the anterior edge and upper endplate under the monitor of C‐arm fluoroscopy. The volume of cement injected was controlled within 3.0–6.0 mL per vertebra. (8) Reduction with secondary augmentation and contralateral cement injection: Before the bone cement solidified, the titanium rod was placed and leverage was repeated to restore the height of the injured vertebra. (C and c). The same method—temporary rod removal, cement injection through the screw, and rod reduction with fixation—was used to inject bone cement through the screws on the opposite side until the bone cement connected at the midline (D and d). After installing the titanium rod on the opposite side, a C‐arm fluoroscopy was used to confirm that the height of the injured vertebra was restored and the local kyphosis was corrected satisfactorily (E and e). (9) Neural decompression and bone graft fusion: If the patient had neurological symptoms, partial or total laminectomy was performed to thoroughly decompress the spinal cord and nerves. Poster‐lateral fusion was achieved by placing bone grafts between the transverse processes. (10) Drainage and closure: One or two drainage tubes were placed, the thoracolumbar fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin were closed in layers, and the procedure was then completed.

Intraoperative Technical Difficulties and Corresponding Managements

Inadequate Restoration or Deviation During Leverage Reduction

Cause: Severe compression of the fractured vertebra, presence of a large intravertebral vacuum cleft, or inaccurate positioning of the rod holder fulcrum may result in inadequate reduction torque and poor elevation of the upper endplate. Asynchronous leverage of the bilateral monoaxial screws can lead to asymmetric restoration of vertebral height.

Management: Confirm that the rod holder is in close contact with the inferior edge of the monoaxial screws before reduction to ensure precise fulcrum placement; adjust the sagittal curvature of the rod if necessary to increase reduction torque. And ensure that the U‐shaped heads of the caudal screws and the rod contour are symmetrical. The surgeon and assistant should simultaneously and evenly tighten the nuts of the bilateral monoaxial screws until the left and right vertebral heights are symmetric and restored to more than 80% of the normal vertebral body height.

Bone Cement Leakage

Cause: The communication between the intravertebral cleft and the spinal canal or paravertebral venous plexus, or excessively rapid injection with excessive cement volume, may result in cement leakage into the spinal canal or venous system, potentially causing neural compression or pulmonary embolism in severe cases. Improper placement of the screw in the fractured vertebra may also increase the risk of cement leakage.

Management: Under continuous real‐time C‐arm fluoroscopy, cement was injected following the principle of “low pressure, slow injection, and intermittent filling.” Each injection volume was controlled within 0.5 mL, with a brief pause after each injection to observe cement dispersion before proceeding. If cement dispersion toward the spinal canal or venous plexus was observed, the injection was immediately halted. The total cement volume in the fractured vertebra was strictly controlled to avoid overfilling. Screw insertion was performed to an adequate depth to ensure that the side holes were positioned within the anterior two‐thirds of the vertebral body, and penetration of the anterior vertebral wall was avoided to prevent cement extravasation anteriorly. In addition, cement injection was performed during the toothpaste phase to further reduce the risk of monomer embolization and leakage.

Loss of Injured Vertebra Restoration Height After Titanium Rod Removal

Cause: After temporary removal of one titanium rod, the reduction force provided by the screws on that side was lost. The upper endplate of the fractured vertebra may exhibit mild subsidence due to gravitational forces and osteoporotic bone quality, resulting in loss of the achieved height restoration.

Management: To prevent loss of height before cement solidification, the interval between rod removal and reinsertion was minimized, requiring the surgeon to complete rod repositioning and fixation within 1–2 min after cement injection. Immediately after rod reinsertion, the leverage reduction maneuver was repeated to secondarily elevate the fractured vertebra and restore the height to the initial reduction level.

Pedicle Screw Loosening in Osteoporotic Patients

Cause: Patients with Kümmell disease often have concomitant severe osteoporosis (bone mineral density T‐score < −2.5 SD), resulting in inadequate interfacial fixation strength. Also, during intraoperative reduction maneuvers, screw loosening or displacement may occur, compromising fixation stability.

Management: To enhance screw anchorage in osteoporotic bone, cement augmentation was first performed on the multiaxial screws in the adjacent vertebrae, and the reduction maneuver was initiated only after complete cement solidification. Additionally, for monoaxial screw placement in the fractured vertebra, appropriate screw diameter and length were selected to ensure sufficient intraosseous purchase depth and avoid shallow insertion. During reduction, nuts were tightened gradually rather than forcibly to minimize stress concentration at the screw–bone interface (Figure 1).

Data Collection

All clinical evaluations were performed preoperatively and postoperatively, with analysis of postoperative follow‐up data. All patients underwent routine preoperative and postoperative imaging: anteroposterior (AP) and lateral plain radiographs of the spine. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were analyzed when necessary.

Operative time, length of hospital stay, volume of blood loss, Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores, and surgery‐related complications were recorded retrospectively. The neurological status of each patient was documented according to the Frankel classification. Preoperative and postoperative radiological parameters were acquired and evaluated to identify the vertebra height restore, correction of kyphosis and nerve decompression as follows: Local kyphotic angle (LKA): Refers to the angle between lines drawn along the superior and inferior endplates of the fractured vertebra. Regional kyphotic angle (RKA): Refers to the angle between lines drawn along the superior endplate of the upper adjacent vertebra and the inferior endplate of the lower adjacent vertebra. Anterior vertebral height (AVH): The distance between the anterior side of the superior lower endplate and the inferior upper endplate of the compressed vertebrae. AVH ratio: The AVH of the fractured vertebra divided by the AVH of the inferior adjacent intact vertebra. Posterior vertebral height (PVH): The distance between the posterior aspects of the superior endplate of the upper adjacent vertebra and the inferior endplate of the fractured vertebra. PVH ratio: The PVH of the fractured vertebra divided by the PVH of the inferior adjacent intact vertebra. Sagittal compression ratio: The value obtained by dividing the narrowest sagittal width of the spinal canal at the fractured vertebra by the sagittal width of the normal vertebra below. Loss of correction: The difference between the parameter value at the final follow‐up stage and the value in the early postoperative period.

Statistical Analysis

The measurement data were tested by Shapiro–Wilk analysis. Normally distributed data were conveyed as mean ± SD; the Student's t‐test was applied for evaluation between the preoperative group and the postoperative group, whereas χ 2 tests were used for categorical variables.

If the theoretical frequency was too small, Fisher's exact test method was used. Analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 23.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General Results

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 22 patients were treated with short‐segment fixation and leverage reduction technique. There were eight males (36.4%) and 14 females (63.6%), aged 62–81 years, with an average age of (65.36 ± 7.82) years. The injured segments were T9 in 1 case, T10 in 3 cases, T11 in 2 cases, and T12 in 5 cases, L1 in 5 cases, L2 in 3 cases, L3 in 2 cases, and L4 in 1 case. The preoperative bone density T value was −5.1 to −2.9 SD, with an average of (−3.94 ± 0.56) SD. The preoperative neurological symptoms were classified as Frankel grade B in 2 cases, C in 7 cases, D in 15 cases. The course of nerve injury was 2–8 months, with an average of (4.1 ± 1.2) months. There were 15 cases with a history of trauma, including 7 cases of fall injury and 6 cases of traffic injury. Four cases had diabetes, and nine cases had hypertension. Radiographic Parameters Analysis

Based on the analysis of imaging results, significant differences were observed in LKA, RKA, AVH ratio, PVH ratio, and SC ratio between preoperative and postoperative measurements. The kyphotic angle was decreased from a preoperative value of 30.05° ± 7.21° to 11.55° ± 2.88° postoperatively (p < 0.01). AVH increased from 12.36 mm ± 3.82 mm preoperatively to 34.32 mm ± 3.12 mm postoperatively (p < 0.01). Only slight changes were detected at the final follow‐up examination (p > 0.05). MRI results further indicated an increase in thecal sac area from 167.44 mm ± 17.68 mm2 preoperatively to 190.32 mm ± 17.69 mm2 postoperatively (p < 0.05).

All patients demonstrated preserved neurological status without postoperative deterioration. According to the Frankel classification system: 15 cases showed improvement from preoperative grade D to postoperative grade E. Five cases progressed from preoperative grade C to postoperative grade D. Notably, two cases achieved significant functional recovery from preoperative grade C to postoperative grade E.

Complication was superficial skin infection (two cases) and cement leakage (one case). In two cases of superficial skin infection, the patients were treated with debridement. In the patient with bone cement leakage, no intervention was performed due to the absence of neurological symptoms. No obvious loosening of internal fixation or breakage occurred during follow‐up. The quality of poster‐lateral fusion was evaluated using postoperative CT scans and classified according to the system described by Singh et al. [18]. The rate of definite fusion and probably fusion was 86% and 14%, respectively, in the 22 patients.

Discussion

The significance of exploring Kümmell's disease lies in its profound impact on patients' quality of life and the limitations of traditional treatment methods. Our study introduces an advanced surgical technique aimed at improving treatment outcomes for this patient population. The results indicate that the short‐segment fixation combined with leverage technique significantly alleviates pain, restores vertebral height, and enhances neural function in patients suffering from Kümmell's disease accompanied by spinal cord injury. This technique fills a vital knowledge gap, demonstrating its efficacy compared to prior studies which primarily focused on smaller sample sizes and shorter follow‐up durations. For instance, previous research by Park et al. highlighted the potential of percutaneous fixation with bone cement in a limited cohort, whereas our study provides robust evidence of its effectiveness in a larger patient group, thereby reinforcing its clinical applicability for managing advanced stages of Kümmell's disease [19].

Kümmell's disease is characterized by vertebral body fractures with subsequent spinal cord injury, leading to significant declines in quality of life [20]. Existing treatment modalities such as percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) and kyphoplasty (PKP) often fail to achieve satisfactory outcomes in advanced stages of the disease. To address this, we developed a surgical technique combining prying reduction with short‐segment fixation, which achieves superior vertebral height restoration, kyphosis correction, and neurological improvement [21].

Kümmell's disease is frequently associated with an intravertebral vacuum cleft sign. Traditional vertebroplasty carries a high risk of cement leakage (occurring in 33.7%–80.0% of cases) during cement injection, potentially leading to serious complications such as neural compression or pulmonary embolism [22]. Additionally, this conventional technique fails to effectively correct progressive kyphotic deformity (mean residual Cobb angle > 15°) and provides limited restoration of vertebral height (postoperative anterior vertebral height improvement is only 1–14 mm) [23]. Approximately 12.16% of cases experience vertebral re‐collapse postoperatively, attributed to micromotion at the bone–cement interface and progression of osteoporosis, often necessitating revision surgery [24]. Moreover, sole cement augmentation cannot restore three‐column stability, requiring patients to wear braces long‐term, which negatively impacts quality of life.

Long‐segment fixation has also been used to treat kyphotic deformity caused by Kümmell's disease. However, long‐segment fixation (typically spanning 4–6 vertebrae) requires extensive dissection of the paraspinal muscles, leading to increased intraoperative blood loss (average > 800 mL) and a higher risk of postoperative muscular fibrosis [25]. Biomechanical studies reveal a significantly increase in intradiscal pressure at the adjacent level, indicating that instrumentation should be applied as short as reasonable [26]. Notably, patients with Kümmell's disease often have severe osteoporosis, making them prone to screw cut‐out loosening due to stress concentration around long‐segment screws (postoperative loosening rate: 25%) [27]. Additionally, the extended fixation range reduces lumbar flexion‐extension range of motion [28], resulting in persistent postoperative lumbar stiffness (average increase in VAS score: 1.8 points). Compared to short‐segment fixation, the long‐segment approach increases implant costs by 2–3 times (approximately USD 406.05) [29] and extends the hospital stay to 14–21 days.

Advantages and Clinical Outcomes of Using Leverage Reduction Technique Combined With Short‐Segment Fixation to Treat Kümmell's Disease

Leverage reduction technique was described in our previous study to treat thoracolumbar compression fractures [17]. In the present study, applying this technique to Kümmell's disease demonstrated significant advantages. This technique could supply enough torque to elevate the upper endplate and restore the anterior vertebra height, which ultimately achieved effective correction of kyphotic deformity (mean correction of 19.32° ± 6.27°) and favorable restoration of vertebral height (restored to 83% ± 14% of normal height). Moreover, it significantly alleviated patient pain (postoperative VAS score significantly reduced) and promoted neurological recovery, with the proportion of patients achieving Frankel grade D or E markedly increased at final follow‐up. Meanwhile, surgical trauma related indicators were favorable, with a mean intraoperative blood loss of only 85.7 ± 29.4 mL and a mean operative time of 100.1 ± 36.3 min. The incidence of serious complications such as implant failure and neurological injury was extremely low.

The Core Technical Highlights of Leverage Reduction Technique Combined With Short‐Segment Fixation

Rather than relying on compensatory mechanisms from other spinal segments, this technique innovatively applies monoaxial pedicle screw prying reduction for the treatment of Kümmell's disease, achieving vertebral height restoration through direct elevation of the affected vertebral endplate. During the procedure, the pedicle screws placed in the adjacent vertebra of the injured vertebra were augmented with the cement, avoiding the looseness of the screws prying the upper endplate. Notably, cement was injected into the injured vertebra subsequently with one rod maintaining the reduction. There was a crucial point in this procedure: once the rod on one side was removed, the fixed screw on that side could no longer effectively lever the upper endplate, causing it to slightly descend. Therefore, after injecting the bone cement, the rod should be replaced as soon as possible before the cement fully solidified, and the levering maneuver should be repeated to restore the vertebral height on that side. This staged cement injection combined with repeated prying reduction not only ensures adequate filling of the intravertebral cleft but stably maintains the reduction effect. Furthermore, the use of short‐segment fixation avoids excessive fusion of functional spinal segments, reduces paraspinal muscle injury, and maximizes preservation of lumbar range of motion.

Limitations and Potential Risks

However, it is essential to acknowledge the limitations inherent in our study. The relatively small sample size and the absence of a randomized controlled trial design may affect the generalizability of our findings. Moreover, our follow‐up period does not account for long‐term complications or the durability of the surgical benefits. Future research should aim to incorporate larger cohorts and longer follow‐up durations to validate our results. In terms of surgical procedure, it requires high standards for fluoroscopic guidance and standardized surgical techniques, and its application in patients with severe osteoporosis has not been fully established. And minor complications like superficial infection and cement leakage were observed in this study, while the incidence of serious complications remained low. Nevertheless, during removal of the unilateral rod for cement injection, slight subsidence of the affected vertebral endplate may occur. Failure to promptly reposition the rod and repeat the prying maneuver before cement solidification can compromise the reduction effect. Inadequate control of cement volume and distribution under fluoroscopy may lead to cement leakage. Moreover, long‐term follow‐up should account for the risk of screw loosening due to progression of osteoporosis. By addressing these limitations and potential risks, subsequent studies can further refine the understanding of optimal surgical interventions for Kümmell's disease and enhance patient care strategies moving forward.

Prospects of Clinical Application

The findings of this study hold crucial implications for clinical practice and policy formulation aimed at managing Kümmell's disease. The observed improvements in pain scores and functional outcomes suggest that short‐segment fixation with leverage technique could serve as a viable treatment alternative for patients who have not responded to conventional methods such as percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) or kyphoplasty (PKP) or for patients with Kümmell's disease presenting with kyphotic deformity, spinal cord compression, and intravertebral vacuum cleft (bone mineral density T‐score < −2.5 SD). The short operative time and low blood loss make it especially suitable for elderly patients with poor surgical tolerance. Moreover, the technique has well‐defined indications and contraindications, facilitating standardized clinical application. This surgical approach may not only enhance patient outcomes but also reduce the economic burden associated with prolonged pain and disability, as highlighted by Chen et al. in their assessment of surgical interventions for spinal disorders [30].

Future research should prioritize large‐sample, multicenter randomized controlled trials with traditional PVP/PKP and long‐segment fixation as control groups. Long‐term follow‐up (≥ 5 years) is needed to compare clinical outcomes, radiographic parameters, complication rates, and medical costs, thereby providing high‐level evidence for the superiority and indications of this technique.

Optimization of surgical techniques and the development of specialized auxiliary tools represent another important direction. Three‐dimensional printing technology can be utilized to create patient‐specific thoracolumbar models for preoperative simulation, facilitating precise design of screw placement and leverage fulcrum to enhance reduction accuracy. Development of specialized minimally invasive instruments, such as adjustable torque leverage rod grippers, precision cement injection syringes, and minimally invasive pedicle screw placement guides, may help reduce surgical trauma, shorten operative time, and lower the learning curve. Exploring the application of spinal navigation and robot‐assisted surgery could enable real‐time monitoring and precise control of the leverage reduction process, further improving surgical safety and correction accuracy.

Expanding the clinical application scope and formulating individualized treatment plans warrants further investigation. Clinical studies should evaluate the efficacy and safety of this technique in complex cases, including Kümmell's disease combined with multiple vertebral fractures, spinal dislocation, or severe spinal cord compression. A personalized treatment decision‐making system based on patient age, bone density T‐score, fracture severity, neurological status, and anatomical characteristics may help optimize patient selection and guide targeted surgical strategies, such as adjusting cement volume, optimizing leverage torque, or combining laminectomy decompression.

Compile a national industry‐level surgical operation manual and perioperative management protocol for this technique, including clear definitions of preoperative evaluation indicators, standardized surgical steps (screw placement, leverage reduction, cement injection), key operational points and risk control measures, and postoperative complication management. Formulate a staged postoperative rehabilitation training plan based on the patient's neurological recovery and spinal stability, including the timing, intensity, and methods of bed rest, functional exercise, and weight‐bearing activities, to promote the recovery of spinal motor function and reduce the risk of postoperative complications such as muscle atrophy and deep vein thrombosis.

Finally, evaluate the long‐term health economic benefits of this technique from the perspectives of direct medical costs, indirect economic costs, and long‐term medical resource consumption, and compare it with traditional PVP/PKP and LSF. Providing scientific evidence for healthcare policy formulation, medical insurance reimbursement, and rational allocation of medical resources, and promote the inclusion of this technique in clinical diagnosis and treatment guidelines and medical insurance reimbursement catalogs.

Experiences and Recommendations

Preoperative imaging evaluation should be thoroughly completed to identify the fracture level and planned cement distribution range, ensuring adequate surgical preparation. Intraoperatively, precise control of the rod‐holding instrument fulcrum is required, and the bilateral monoaxial screw nuts should be tightened simultaneously to ensure even force distribution on the affected vertebral endplate. When injecting cement, the titanium rod on one side should remain stable, and prompt repositioning of the rod with repeated prying reduction before cement solidification is essential to compensate for endplate subsidence. Cement distribution should be monitored throughout under fluoroscopy to prevent leakage beyond the anterior vertebral margin. For patients with neurological complications, thorough laminectomy decompression is necessary. Postoperative follow‐up should be conducted regularly to monitor implant stability and osteoporosis progression, and to ensure timely intervention to reduce the risk of vertebral re‐fracture.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this study indicate that the short‐segment fixation combined with leverage technique using monoaxial pedicle screw technique demonstrates significant efficacy in alleviating pain, improving functional outcomes, and facilitating neurological recovery in Kümmell disease patients with concomitant spinal nerve injury. The observed improvements in clinical parameters, alongside a favorable safety profile, underscore the potential of this surgical approach for enhancing patient quality of life. Continued research is essential to explore the long‐term outcomes and refine the selection criteria for optimal patient management.
