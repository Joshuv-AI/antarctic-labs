Optimization-Free Concentrated Matrix-Exponentials
Maria Laura Battagliola, Oscar Peralta

Abstract
Near-deterministic positive delays require highly concentrated distributions, but phase-type models are constrained by the Erlang variance limit. While matrix-exponential distributions can empirically bypass this barrier, prior low-variance constructions relied entirely on numerical optimization. We propose an explicit family of concentrated matrix-exponential densities for the unit delay, obtained by raising the trigonometric Fejér kernel to logarithmic power. With exact moments and closed-form parameters, this gives the first analytical proof of a matrix-exponential class that asymptotically surpasses the Erlang bound.



Keywords:
Matrix-exponential distributions, Fejér kernel, squared coefficient of variation, Erlangization

1
Introduction

Approximating nearly deterministic and strictly positive delays requires probability distributions with very small squared coefficient of variation
\mathrm{SCV}(X)=\Var(X)/\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}
, which equals
0
for a deterministic delay and is invariant under scaling. We therefore take the deterministic target to be the unit delay, without loss of generality. Within the phase-type (PH) class, the theorem of Aldous and Shepp states that, among all order-
n
laws
X\in\mathrm{PH}(n)
, the Erlang law is least variable, with
\mathrm{SCV}(X)=1/n

[
1
]
; approximating deterministic delays by Erlang laws is accordingly called Erlangization.
The matrix-exponential (ME) class is a broader rational-transform framework: ME laws have rational Laplace transforms and finite-dimensional moment formulas, yet can achieve substantially lower variability than PH laws of the same order. Numerical concentrated-ME methods
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
showed by high-dimensional optimization that ME distributions with complex-conjugate poles can far surpass the Erlang bound at many finite orders; this has also been exploited in numerical Laplace inversion
[
5
]
. Whether this superiority persists asymptotically remained open: each low-SCV construction required a finite-
n
numerical search.

This paper introduces the powered-Fejér concentrated matrix-exponential (PF-CME) family. The Fejér kernel
\Phi_{m}(\cdot)
is a nonnegative trigonometric polynomial of order
m
and provides a natural approximate identity inside the common-damping ME class. By raising this kernel to the logarithmically growing power
r_{m}=\lceil\log m\rceil
and choosing
h_{m}=2\log m+\log\log m
, we obtain an explicit ME family whose SCV decays, up to constant factors, as
\log m/m^{2}
in the construction index and as
\log^{3}n_{m}/n_{m}^{2}
in the corresponding ME dimension
n_{m}
. This is the first such concentration law for a common-damping ME family, confirming that the numerical superiority over Erlang in
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
is structural. The precise concentration statement is given in Theorem
6
.

Beyond this asymptotic result, the explicit parameters of the PF-CME family are directly applicable to numerical inverse Laplace transformation (NILT). Its density is nonnegative by construction, avoiding the Gibbs-type overshoots in some classical inversion formulas
[
5
]
. Practically, the family is asymptotically superior, but its SCV remains above the Erlang bound below order about
7260
. Thus PF-CME complements, rather than replaces, Erlang and optimized CME families at moderate orders.

Throughout we use standard asymptotic notation:
f(m)=O(g(m))
means

|f(m)|\leq Cg(m)
eventually for some
C>0
;

f(m)=\Theta(g(m))
, equivalently
f(m)\asymp g(m)
, means

cg(m)\leq f(m)\leq Cg(m)
eventually for some
c,C>0
;

f(m)=o(g(m))
means
f(m)/g(m)\to 0
; and
f(u)\propto g(u)

denotes proportionality up to a positive factor.

2
The common-damping framework

A phase-type law is the absorption time of a finite-state Markov jump process. If
X\sim\mathrm{PH}(n)
, its density takes the form
f(t)=\bm{\alpha}\,\mathrm{e}^{St}(-S{\bm{1}})
with sub-intensity matrix
S
and initial distribution
\bm{\alpha}
; positivity is automatic, and the Aldous–Shepp bound gives

\inf\bigl\{\mathrm{SCV}(X):X\in\mathrm{PH}(n)\bigr\}=\frac{1}{n}.

(1)

The class
\mathrm{ME}(n)
replaces
S
with a general real matrix
A
, requiring only a real, nonnegative density that integrates to
1

[
6
, Chapters 3–4]
. A density belongs to the ME class if and only if its Laplace transform is rational; its minimal algebraic order is the degree of the rational transform. In an order-
n
representation it can be written as
f(t)=\bm{\alpha}\,\mathrm{e}^{At}\bm{s}
with moments
\mu_{k}=k!\bm{\alpha}(-A)^{-(k+1)}\bm{s}
. Because
\mathrm{ME}(n)\supset\mathrm{PH}(n)
strictly, the bound (
1
) does not apply, and substantially lower SCV values are achievable. Matrix-exponential distributions numerically optimized to approach this deterministic limit are termed concentrated matrix-exponential (CME) laws.

If an ME density has only simple poles
-\beta
and
-\beta\pm\mathrm{i}\ell\omega
(
\ell=1,\dots,L
), all sharing the same real part, then

f(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta t}\Bigl(c_{0}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\bigl(c_{\ell}\cos(\ell\omega t)+d_{\ell}\sin(\ell\omega t)\bigr)\Bigr),

(2)

an exponentially damped trigonometric polynomial, with moment integrals reducing via
\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p}\mathrm{e}^{-(\beta-\mathrm{i}\ell\omega)t}\mathrm{d}t=p!(\beta-\mathrm{i}\ell\omega)^{-(p+1)}
.
Optimized CME laws often exhibit this common real part after normalization
[
3
,
7
]
, motivating the following analytically tractable subclass.

Assumption 1

(Common-damping framework)
.

Fix
L\in\mathbb{N}
. After scaling so that the common real part equals
-1
, consider densities of the form

f(t)=C\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,G(\omega t+\phi),\qquad t\geq 0,

(3)

where
\omega>0
,
\phi\in\mathbb{R}
,
C>0
, and
G
is a nonnegative trigonometric polynomial of degree
L
.

The class (
3
) has one real pole at
-1
and at most
L
conjugate pairs at
-1\pm\mathrm{i}\ell\omega
, so its algebraic order is at most
2L+1
, with equality if every frequency appears with nonzero coefficient.

3
The PF-CME family

For
m\geq 2
, the Fejér kernel of order
m
is

\Phi_{m}(\theta)=1+2\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\!\left(1-\frac{\ell}{m}\right)\cos(\ell\theta)=\frac{1}{m}\!\left(\frac{\sin(m\theta/2)}{\sin(\theta/2)}\right)^{\!2}.

(4)

It is nonnegative, has degree
m-1
, and attains its maximum of
m
at
\theta=0

[
8
, Vol. I, Chap. III, Sec. 3]
.

Throughout set

r_{m}:=\lceil\log m\rceil,\quad h_{m}:=2\log m+\log\log m,\quad\omega_{m}:=\frac{2\pi}{h_{m}}.

(5)

Definition 1

(PF-CME density)
.

The powered-Fejér concentrated matrix-exponential density of index
m\geq 2
is

f_{m}(t):=C_{m}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,W_{m}\!\bigl(\omega_{m}(t-1)\bigr),\qquad t\geq 0,

(6)

where
{W_{m}(\theta):=\bigl(\Phi_{m}(\theta)\bigr)^{r_{m}}}
and
C_{m}>0
.
We write
X_{m}\sim\mathrm{PF\mbox{-}CME}_{m}
for the random variable with density
f_{m}
.

The shift places the principal peak at
t=1
, the unit deterministic delay used here without loss of generality, while
\omega_{m}=2\pi/h_{m}
makes
[0,h_{m})
one full modulation period. Normalization need not impose
\mathbb{E}[X_{m}]=1
exactly, but our main result in Theorem
6
gives
\mathbb{E}[X_{m}]\to 1
. We now identify the ME order.

Proposition 1

(ME order)
.

For each
m\geq 2
,
X_{m}\sim\mathrm{PF\mbox{-}CME}_{m}
has minimal ME order
n_{m}:=2r_{m}(m-1)+1
.

Proof.

In complex form, the Fejér kernel satisfies

\Phi_{m}(\theta)=\frac{1}{m}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}j\theta}\right|^{2}=\sum_{|k|\leq m-1}\left(1-\frac{|k|}{m}\right)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\theta}=\sum_{{k\in\mathbb{Z}}}{\gamma_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\theta}.

Here
{\gamma_{k}}=1-|k|/m
for
|k|\leq m-1
and
{\gamma_{k}}=0
otherwise. Since

W_{m}=\Phi_{m}^{r_{m}}
, pointwise multiplication of trigonometric polynomials
corresponds to discrete convolution of their Fourier sequences. Hence

W_{m}
is a nonnegative trigonometric polynomial of degree

L_{m}:=r_{m}(m-1)
with cosine expansion

W_{m}(\theta)=B_{0,m}+2\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{m}}B_{\ell,m}\cos(\ell\theta),

(7)

where
B_{\ell,m}
is the
\ell
-th coefficient of the
r_{m}
-fold discrete
convolution of
{(\gamma_{k})}
.
Substituting
\theta=\omega_{m}(t-1)
and applying

\cos(a-b)=\cos a\cos b+\sin a\sin b
yields

f_{m}(t)=C_{m}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\Bigl[B_{0,m}+2\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{m}}B_{\ell,m}\bigl(\cos(\ell\omega_{m})\cos(\ell\omega_{m}t)+\sin(\ell\omega_{m})\sin(\ell\omega_{m}t)\bigr)\Bigr],

(8)

a linear combination of
\mathrm{e}^{-t}
,
\mathrm{e}^{-t}\cos(\ell\omega_{m}t)
, and

\mathrm{e}^{-t}\sin(\ell\omega_{m}t)
for
\ell=1,\dots,L_{m}
, whose Laplace transforms
share the denominator
(s+1)\prod_{\ell=1}^{L_{m}}((s+1)^{2}+\ell^{2}\omega_{m}^{2})

of degree
n_{m}
. Since convolution preserves strict positivity on gap-free finite supports, all
B_{\ell,m}>0
,
0\leq\ell\leq L_{m}
. After the shift, the complex coefficient at frequency
\ell
is
B_{\ell,m}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\ell\omega_{m}}\neq 0
, so every pole pair has nonzero residue and no cancellation occurs. Thus the Laplace transform has degree exactly
n_{m}
.
∎

The moments and SCV use the basis integrals

\displaystyle J_{0}(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\cos(at)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{1+a^{2}},

\displaystyle S_{0}(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\sin(at)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{a}{1+a^{2}},

(9)

\displaystyle J_{1}(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{-t}\cos(at)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1-a^{2}}{(1+a^{2})^{2}},

\displaystyle S_{1}(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{-t}\sin(at)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{2a}{(1+a^{2})^{2}},

(10)

\displaystyle J_{2}(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\cos(at)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{2(1-3a^{2})}{(1+a^{2})^{3}},

\displaystyle S_{2}(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\sin(at)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{2a(3-a^{2})}{(1+a^{2})^{3}}.

(11)

Theorem 2

(Exact moments)
.

For
k=0,1,2
, define

M_{k}(m):=B_{0,m}J_{k}(0)+2\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{m}}B_{\ell,m}\Bigl(\cos(\ell\omega_{m})J_{k}(\ell\omega_{m})+\sin(\ell\omega_{m})S_{k}(\ell\omega_{m})\Bigr).

Then
C_{m}=M_{0}(m)^{-1}
,
\mathbb{E}[X_{m}]=M_{1}(m)/M_{0}(m)
,
\mathbb{E}[X_{m}^{2}]=M_{2}(m)/M_{0}(m)
, and
\mathrm{SCV}(X_{m})=\tfrac{M_{2}(m)M_{0}(m)}{M_{1}(m)^{2}}-1
.

Proof.

Integrate (
8
) against
1
,
t
, and
t^{2}
term by term using (
9
)–(
11
).
∎

4
Asymptotics and comparisons

We first derive the law for
\mathrm{SCV}(X_{m})
in terms of
m
, then translate it using
r_{m}\asymp\log m
and
n_{m}\asymp m\log m
.

Lemma 3

(Geometric cell decomposition)
.

Set
q_{m}:=\mathrm{e}^{-h_{m}}=1/(m^{2}\log m)
from (
5
), let
K_{m}\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-q_{m})
on
\{0,1,2,\dots\}
, and let
Y_{m}
have the density
g_{m}(u)\propto\mathrm{e}^{-u}W_{m}(\omega_{m}(u-1))\mathbf{1}_{[0,h_{m})}(u)
.
Then
X_{m}\overset{d}{=}Y_{m}+K_{m}h_{m}
with
Y_{m}
and
K_{m}
independent, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{m}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y_{m}]+\frac{h_{m}q_{m}}{1-q_{m}},\quad\Var(X_{m})=\Var(Y_{m})+\frac{h_{m}^{2}q_{m}}{(1-q_{m})^{2}}.

(12)

Proof.

For
t=kh_{m}+u
with
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
u\in[0,h_{m})
, the periodicity
W_{m}(\omega_{m}(t-1))=W_{m}(\omega_{m}(u-1)+2\pi k)=W_{m}(\omega_{m}(u-1))
gives

f_{m}(t)=C_{m}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,W_{m}(\omega_{m}(t-1))=C_{m}\,\mathrm{e}^{-kh_{m}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-u}\,W_{m}(\omega_{m}(u-1))\,=\,C_{m}\,q_{m}^{k}\,\mathrm{e}^{-u}\,W_{m}(\omega_{m}(u-1)).

Summing over
k\geq 0
, the marginal density of
t\bmod h_{m}
is proportional to
\mathrm{e}^{-u}W_{m}(\omega_{m}(u-1))
on
[0,h_{m})
, which is exactly
g_{m}
. Integrating over the
k
-th cell
[kh_{m},(k+1)h_{m})
gives

\int_{kh_{m}}^{(k+1)h_{m}}f_{m}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=q_{m}^{k}\int_{0}^{h_{m}}C_{m}\mathrm{e}^{-u}W_{m}(\omega_{m}(u-1))\,\mathrm{d}u=(1-q_{m})q_{m}^{k},

Since
\int_{0}^{h_{m}}C_{m}\mathrm{e}^{-u}W_{m}(\omega_{m}(u-1))\,\mathrm{d}u=1-q_{m}
, this is the
\mathrm{Geom}(1-q_{m})
mass at
k
. The factorization yields
X_{m}=Y_{m}+K_{m}h_{m}
with
Y_{m}
and
K_{m}
independent, and (
12
) follows from geometric moments.
∎

Since
h_{m}\asymp\log m
and
q_{m}=1/(m^{2}\log m)
, the geometric variance term in (
12
) has order
\log m/m^{2}
. It remains to estimate the base-cell variance; we first need kernel bounds.

Lemma 4

(Scaled kernel bounds)
.

Let
\dist(x,2\pi\mathbb{Z}):=\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|x-2\pi k|
. There exist constants

0<a_{1}<a_{2}<\infty
and
m_{0}
such that for all
m\geq m_{0}
:

\displaystyle m\,\mathrm{e}^{-a_{2}m^{2}x^{2}}\leq\Phi_{m}(x)\leq m\,\mathrm{e}^{-a_{1}m^{2}x^{2}},

\displaystyle\qquad|x|\leq\tfrac{5}{m}\quad\text{(peak)},

(13)

\displaystyle\Phi_{m}(x)\leq\tfrac{6}{25}m,

\displaystyle\qquad\tfrac{5}{m}\leq|x|\leq\tfrac{20}{m}\quad\text{(transition)},

(14)

\displaystyle\Phi_{m}(x)\leq\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{400}m,

\displaystyle\qquad\tfrac{20}{m}\leq\dist(x,2\pi\mathbb{Z})\leq\pi\quad\text{(tail)}.

(15)

Proof.

By
2\pi
-periodicity, it suffices to work on
[-\pi,\pi]
and prove the tail bound for
20/m\leq|x|\leq\pi
. Write
\Phi_{m}(x)/m=\left(\sin(y/2)/(y/2)\right)^{2}\,\left((y/(2m))/\sin(y/(2m))\right)^{2}
,
y=mx.

On
|y|\leq 5
, the quotient
\sin(y/2)/(y/2)
is positive and has no zeros.

H(y):=-\log\left(\sin(y/2)/(y/2)\right)^{2}
,
H(0)=0
, is even and analytic on
(-2\pi,2\pi)
. Since
\left(\sin(y/2)/(y/2)\right)^{2}=1-y^{2}/12+O(y^{4})

and
-\log(1-z)=z+O(z^{2})
, we have
H(y)=y^{2}/12+O(y^{4})
near
0
.
By compactness,
H(y)\asymp y^{2}
on
|y|\leq 5
, so there are
b_{1},b_{2}>0
with

\mathrm{e}^{-b_{2}y^{2}}\leq\left(\sin(y/2)/(y/2)\right)^{2}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-b_{1}y^{2}}
,
|y|\leq 5.

As for the second factor,
\left((y/(2m))/\sin(y/(2m))\right)^{2}=1+O(y^{2}/m^{2})

uniformly on
|y|\leq 5
,
so, adjusting the constants and taking
m
large enough,

m\mathrm{e}^{-a_{2}m^{2}x^{2}}\leq\Phi_{m}(x)\leq m\mathrm{e}^{-a_{1}m^{2}x^{2}}
,
|x|\leq 5/m.

This proves (
13
).

The remaining two estimates follow from the same representation. First suppose

5/m\leq|x|\leq 20/m
, equivalently
5\leq|y|\leq 20
. Using the universal bound

|\sin(\cdot)|\leq 1
,

\left(\sin(y/2)/(y/2)\right)^{2}\leq 4/y^{2}\leq 4/25
.
Since
|y/(2m)|=o(1)
uniformly in this region, the second factor

\left((y/(2m))/\sin(y/(2m))\right)^{2}
is at most
3/2
for large
m
. Thus,
for
5/m\leq|x|\leq 20/m
,
\Phi_{m}(x)\leq(6/25)m
, proving (
14
).
Finally, for
20/m\leq|x|\leq\pi
, the inequality
\sin u\geq 2u/\pi
on

0\leq u\leq\pi/2
, with
u=|x|/2
, gives

|\sin(x/2)|\geq|x|/\pi\geq 20/(\pi m)
. Hence, for
20/m\leq|x|\leq\pi
,

\Phi_{m}(x)\leq\frac{1}{m\sin^{2}(x/2)}\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{m|x|^{2}}\leq\frac{\pi^{2}m}{400}
,
proving (
15
).
∎

We apply Lemma
4
on
{[-\omega_{m},2\pi-\omega_{m}]}
: the central peak is leading, while transition and tail parts are negligible.

Theorem 5

(Base-cell concentration)
.

As
m\to\infty
,
\Var(Y_{m})=\Theta(\log m/m^{2})
and
\mathbb{E}[Y_{m}]=1+O(\log m/m^{2})
.

Proof.

Write
r=r_{m}
,
\omega=\omega_{m}
, and
{W=W_{m}}
. With
x=\omega(u-1)
,

A_{m}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-1}}{\omega}\int_{-\omega}^{2\pi-\omega}\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}W(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad N_{2,m}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-1}}{\omega^{3}}\int_{-\omega}^{2\pi-\omega}x^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}W(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

so
\mathbb{E}[(Y_{m}-1)^{2}]=N_{2,m}/A_{m}
. Set

I_{0}=\{x\in[-\omega,2\pi-\omega]:|x|\leq 5/m\}
,
I_{1}=\{x\in[-\omega,2\pi-\omega]:5/m<|x|\leq 20/m\}

and
I_{2}=\{x\in[-\omega,2\pi-\omega]:|x|>20/m\}
. Since
\omega\asymp 1/\log m
, for large
m
we have
20/m<\omega
, so
I_{0}
and
I_{1}
are full symmetric intervals around the origin.

On
I_{0}
, Lemma
4
gives, after raising to the power
r
,

W(x)=\Theta(m^{r}\mathrm{e}^{-{\kappa}rm^{2}x^{2}})
in the two-sided Gaussian sense.
Since
|x|/\omega\leq 5/(m\omega)=o(1)
,
\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}
is uniformly
1+o(1)
and does not change the order. With the change of variables
y=m\sqrt{r}\,x
, we obtain

\int_{I_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}W(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\Theta\!\left({\frac{m^{r}}{m\sqrt{r}}\int_{-5\sqrt{r}}^{5\sqrt{r}}\mathrm{e}^{-{\kappa}y^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y}\right)=\Theta\!\left(\frac{m^{r}}{m\sqrt{r}}\right),

\int_{I_{0}}x^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}W(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\Theta\!\left({\frac{m^{r}}{m^{3}r^{3/2}}\int_{-5\sqrt{r}}^{5\sqrt{r}}y^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-{\kappa}y^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y}\right)=\Theta\!\left(\frac{m^{r}}{m^{3}r^{3/2}}\right).

I_{0}
supplies the leading mass and second moment about
1
.

On
I_{1}
, (
14
) gives
W(x)\leq(6m/25)^{r}
. Since
|I_{1}|=O(1/m)
and
x^{2}=O(m^{-2})
on this region,

\displaystyle\int_{I_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}W(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\,|I_{1}|\left(\frac{6m}{25}\right)^{r}=O\!\left(\frac{1}{m}\left(\frac{6m}{25}\right)^{r}\right)=O\!\left(m^{r-1}\left(\frac{6}{25}\right)^{r}\right),

\displaystyle\int_{I_{1}}x^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}W(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\,m^{-2}|I_{1}|\left(\frac{6m}{25}\right)^{r}=O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{3}}\left(\frac{6m}{25}\right)^{r}\right)=O\!\left(m^{r-3}\left(\frac{6}{25}\right)^{r}\right).

On
I_{2}
, points with
x\leq\pi
satisfy
\dist(x,2\pi\mathbb{Z})=|x|>20/m
, while for
x>\pi
we have
x\leq 2\pi-\omega
, hence
\dist(x,2\pi\mathbb{Z})=2\pi-x\geq\omega>20/m
by the choice of large
m
. Hence the tail bound in Lemma
4
applies throughout
I_{2}
, giving
W(x)\leq(\pi^{2}m/400)^{r}
. Also,
I_{2}
has bounded length,
x^{2}=O(1)
, and
\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}\leq\mathrm{e}
. Thus

\int_{I_{2}}(1+x^{2})\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}W(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\,|I_{2}|\left(\frac{\pi^{2}m}{400}\right)^{r}=O\!\left(m^{r}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{400}\right)^{r}\right).

Relative to the
I_{0}
scales, the largest transition and tail errors are bounded by
(6/25)^{r}r^{3/2}
and
(\pi^{2}/400)^{r}m^{3}r^{3/2}
for the second moment, and by
(6/25)^{r}\sqrt{r}
and
(\pi^{2}/400)^{r}m\sqrt{r}
for the mass. All vanish because

,
(\pi^{2}/400)^{r}m^{3}\leq m^{-\delta}
for some
\delta>0
, and
6/25<\mathrm{e}^{-1}
. Therefore

A_{m}=\Theta\!\left(\frac{m^{r}}{\omega m\sqrt{r}}\right),\qquad N_{2,m}=\Theta\!\left(\frac{m^{r}}{\omega^{3}m^{3}r^{3/2}}\right),\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[(Y_{m}-1)^{2}]=\Theta\!\left(\frac{1}{\omega^{2}m^{2}r}\right)=\Theta\!\left(\frac{\log m}{m^{2}}\right).

For the mean, the unperturbed first moment on
I_{0}
cancels because
W(\cdot)
is even:
\int_{I_{0}}xW(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0
. Hence

\int_{I_{0}}x\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}W(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{I_{0}}x(\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}-1)W(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using the Taylor bound
|\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}-1|\leq C|x|/\omega
on
I_{0}
and the second-moment estimate above gives

\int_{I_{0}}x\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}W(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=O\!\left(\frac{m^{r}}{\omega m^{3}r^{3/2}}\right).

The transition and tail first moments satisfy

\int_{I_{1}}|x|\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}W(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=O\!\left(m^{r-2}\left(\frac{6}{25}\right)^{r}\right),\qquad\int_{I_{2}}|x|\mathrm{e}^{-x/\omega}W(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=O\!\left(m^{r}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{400}\right)^{r}\right).

These are
o(m^{r}/(\omega m^{3}r^{3/2}))
: for
I_{1}
this uses
6/25<\mathrm{e}^{-1}
, and for
I_{2}
it uses
\pi^{2}/400<\mathrm{e}^{-3}
. Thus

.
