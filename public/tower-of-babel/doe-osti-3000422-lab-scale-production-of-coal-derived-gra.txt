1. Executive Summary:

The main goal of the project was to develop a lab-scale manufacturing process to fabricate filaments with

high carbon content for Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) three-dimensional (3D) printing use. Graphene

particles derived from domestic US coal waste was used as feedstock for filament development. The

specific objectives were to (1) develop a coal-enhanced filament production technology to fabricate

filament containing high-loading of coal-derived graphene, (2) develop debinding and sintering post-

processing to fabricate fully carbon structure, (3) develop a composite material based on fully carbon

preform structure and evaluate composite as a potential alternative to carbon fiber composite, (4) perform

a full techno-economic analysis to assess the coal-enhanced filament potential for the fast-growing and

high-value additive manufacturing and composite market. At the end of the project, we reached a

Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 5 for the proposed technology and we met each of the objectives.

Key expected outcomes of the project include:

In Phase I (Budget Period 1): Coal-derived graphene enhanced filament was successfully developed to

meet the following criteria: graphene loading up to 50 weight percent (wt. %) in filaments, graphene

alignment up to 50% in filament, filament 1.75 ± 0.05 millimeter (mm) of diameter, filament thermal

conductivity up to 12 Wm-1K-1 at 50 wt. % loading, 100 gram (g) filament.

In Phase II (Budget Period 2): Composite made from coal-derived graphene enhanced filament were

successfully fabricated based on commercial FDM 3D printers and post-processing to meet following

criteria: 3DBenchy torture-test sample with volume shrinkage less than 5%, composite density less than 2

grams per cubic centimeters (g/cm3), flexural strength/modulus 600 Megapascal/45 Gigapascal, tensile

strength/modulus 700 MPa /50 GPa, Compression strength/modulus 400 MPa/50 GPa, and interlaminar

shear strength 45 MPa. The filament production will be at the 1kilogram (kg) scale.

The project was be led by Prof. Kun Fu, an assistant professor of mechanical engineering at the University

of Delaware, who is an expert in the field of composite material and manufacturing. Prof. Feng Jiao, who

2. Summary of Technical Progress

This project successfully demonstrated a lab-scale process for manufacturing coal-derived graphene-

reinforced composite parts through a sequence of filament extrusion, additive manufacturing, carbonization,

and thermoset infiltration. The first stage focused on developing printable composite filaments

incorporating 20 wt.% to 50 wt.% coal-derived graphene into a PLA polymer matrix. A vertical extrusion

system with gravity-assisted drawing was designed and implemented to produce consistent filament

diameters (1.75 ± 0.05 mm) compatible with standard FDM printers. High graphene loadings required

optimization of extrusion parameters, including binder ratio and temperature control, to ensure mechanical

integrity and mitigate brittleness. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Thermogravimetric Analysis

(TGA) analyses confirmed uniform dispersion of carbon particles within the polymer matrix.

In the second stage, the filaments were evaluated for printability using commercially available FDM

printers. Due to the high melt viscosity of the 50 wt.% carbon-Polylactic Acid (PLA) filaments, commercial

printers were unable to extrude the material effectively. To overcome this limitation, a customized extrusion

head was developed to handle high-viscosity, particle-filled filaments. With this modified printer setup,

lattice structures were successfully printed with dimensional accuracy exceeding 95%. CAD models were

pre-compensated to account for shrinkage during the carbonization process. Slicing parameters were further

optimized to accommodate the high solid content, resulting in consistent bed adhesion and geometric

fidelity across all prints.

The printed parts were then subjected to pyrolysis under an inert argon atmosphere. The carbonization

process used a controlled thermal profile with a heating rate of 5 degree Celsius per minute (°C/min), a 2-

hour hold at 800°C, and continuous argon purging to prevent oxidation. This yielded carbon scaffolds

 4

with >95 wt.% carbon content and consistent linear shrinkage of approximately 18%. More than 90% of

printed structures retained their form and integrity without visible cracking or warping. Brunauer-Emmett-

Teller (BET) analysis showed surface areas ranging from 100 to 300 square meters per gram (m²/g),

indicating high porosity suitable for resin infiltration and functional applications.

In the final stage, the carbon scaffolds were infiltrated with a low-viscosity epoxy resin using vacuum-

assisted resin transfer molding (VARTM). Post-curing was carried out at 120°C to ensure complete

thermoset crosslinking. The resulting composite samples were machined into standardized test coupons and

subjected to mechanical evaluation. The final composites exhibited outstanding performance, achieving a

tensile strength of 1322 MPa, a tensile modulus of 115 GPa, and a flexural strength of 1100 MPa at a density

of 1.7 g/cm³. The baseline performance, corresponding to textile-grade carbon fiber composites, exhibits a

flexural strength of approximately 600 MPa with a modulus of 45 GPa, and a tensile strength of

approximately 700 MPa with a modulus of 50 GPa. SEM imaging revealed excellent fiber-matrix bonding

with minimal void formation. These results validate the technical feasibility, mechanical robustness, and

commercial promise of coal-derived graphene composites as high-performance alternatives to traditional

carbon fiber-reinforced polymers.

3. Key Achievements

 • High Graphene Loading Filament Demonstration: Developed particle-bonded filaments

 incorporating up to 50 wt.% coal-derived graphene with sufficient mechanical integrity and

 dimensional tolerance for use in commercial FDM printers. A customized extruder head was

 developed specifically for this application, which outperformed commercial extruders in

 processing high-viscosity, high-particle-content composite feedstocks. The custom design included

 optimized screw geometry and integrated filament cooling to ensure uniform extrusion and particle

 distribution.

 • Printability and Geometric Precision: The project demonstrated successful 3D printing of

 graphene-enhanced filaments into architected structures using standard commercial FDM printers.

 5

 Complex lattice geometries were printed with >95% dimensional accuracy and minimal print

 failure, enabling reliable downstream carbonization.

 • Carbon Scaffold Formation and Stability: The project optimized the carbonization process for

 printed parts, achieving carbon content >95 wt.%, and consistent linear shrinkage (~18%) with no

 structural delamination. Surface area and pore morphology were tuned to balance mechanical

 performance and resin infusion.

 • Composite Fabrication with High Mechanical Performance: Produced carbon-epoxy composite

 parts with tensile strength of 1322 MPa, a tensile modulus of 115 GPa, and a flexural strength of

 1100 MPa at a density of 1.7 g/cm³. These properties are on par with conventional carbon fiber

 reinforced polymer composites (CFRP), validating the structural utility of coal-derived graphene

 composites.

 • Process Integration Across Multiple Stages: Completed end-to-end process validation from

 filament production, 3D printing, carbonization, to composite part fabrication at the lab scale. The

 entire workflow was made reproducible and compatible with scalable manufacturing equipment.

 • TRL Advancement: Reached Technology Readiness Level 5 by demonstrating a complete lab-

 scale prototype system, including functional composite components with real-world mechanical

 performance metrics. The process is ready for transition to a pilot-scale system.

Graphene/PLA pellet production: Pellets are graphene/PLA mixture in the form of powders, and pellets

are used as feedstock to produce filament through melting, mixing, stretching, and cooling in an extrusion

process. PLA purchased from Filabot was used as polymer source. The pellet fabrication process includes

four steps: 1. Dissolution of PLA in Dichloromethane (DCM); 2. Dispersion of graphene in PLA/DCM

solution; 3. Evaporation of DCM. 4. Cutting and grinding. Finally, graphene/PLA powders can be produced.

The powder size was not controlled as the size did not influence the filament extrusion. The Recipient

produced graphene/PLA pellets with three graphene loading content by weight: 40%, 50%, and 60%. Figure

1 shows the photo image of 60 wt.% graphene/PLA pellets and the thermogravimetric (TGA) profiles of

PLA, graphene/PLA (40, 50, and 60%). The Recipient estimated that adding more graphene in PLA should

lead to earlier decomposition of PLA and should show an earlier weight loss. This occurs because graphene

as filler creates higher surface area and thermal conductance compared to neat PLA, and as the temperature

increases, graphene can be heated up fast than PLA, resulting in a weight loss at a lower temperature. In

the TGA profile, the 40 and 50 wt.% graphene/PLA approved the hypothesis. However, 60 wt.%

graphene/PLA sample showed a delayed weight loss. The Recipient will need to make more TGA

measurement to confirm the weight loss.

Thermal property measurment: The guarded hot plate technique determines the thermal conductivity of

a material by using the electrical power output of a hot plate with guided heat conduction. In this method,

the heat flow is generated by the electric heater with a constant heat rate and transferred from the hot plate,

through the specimen, to the cold plate, establishing an axial temperature gradient in the stack. The

temperature difference across the specimen is measured along with the output from the heat flow transducer

after thermal equilibrium is reached. Then the thermal conductivity is calculated based on these values and

specimen thickness. In this work, the samples were polished on both sides (for rough samples, thermal

interface pastes may also be used) and then held under a uniform compressive load of 30 pounds per square

inch (PSI) to ensure good contact with the heat and cold plate.

UD tested the thermal conductivity of different samples with the heat flow parallel or perpendicular to the

20 wt% graphene/PLA pellet was fabricated via the following procedure. First of all, a total 20g of

polylactic acid (PLA, from Filabot, Barre, Vermont, USA) was added to the 100 ml dichloromethane (DCM,

The laser flash method was used to evaluate the thermal conductivity of samples before and after

carbonization. As shown in Figure 20, the sample after the carbonization exhibits a lower thermal

conductivity (in-plane and out-of-plane) in comparison to that before the carbonization due to the removal

of polymer. The out-of-plane sample before canonization has a thermal conductivity of 0.6±0.01 W/m·K—

about two times that of the in-plane structure, indicating the anisotropic nature of the printed samples. This

anisotropic property in thermal conductivity is preserved after carbonization.

Four different samples with dimensions of 10 mm×10 mm×3 mm were measured: (I) carbonized, in-plane;

(II) carbonized, out-of-plane; (III) as printed, in plane; and (IV) as printed, out-of-plane. The thermal

conductivity of the materials (k) was determined by k=α×ρ×Cp with α being the thermal diffusivity, ρ being

the density of material, and Cp being the normalized specific heat capacity. The details of parameters are

summarized in Table 2. The thermal diffusivity α was determined using the laser flash method (Netzsch,

LFA467) at 21oC, where the average value was calculated from six measured results with standard deviation.

The density ρ was estimated based on the measured dimensions and weight. To minimize the error, each

 22

dimension was measured three times at different positions, and the density was calculated based on the

averaged dimensions. The specific heat capacity Cp was measured by DSC (TA Instruments, DSC 2500).

 • Plasticizer was added to improve the flexibility and printability of the filaments

The Recipient enhanced the printability and flexibility of the commercial graphene filament by

incorporating a plasticizer, polyethylene glycol (PEG; C₃H₈O₂–(C₂H₄O)ₙ–C₃H₈O₂, average molecular

weight Mn ≈ 500). Based on literature, when PEG, as a plasticizer, is added to a composite material, it can

effectively separate and lubricate the polymer chains. This reduces intermolecular forces between the

polymer chains and allows them to move more freely. As a result, the material becomes more flexible and

less likely to crack or break under stress. This also helps improving the additive dispersion in the mixtures

and reduces agglomeration of graphene and carbon nanotubes (CNT) that improves the attachment of

graphene to the polymer chains and improves the homogeneity of the mixture that makes the filament

printable. Literature reported that the optimal amount of plasticizer is 5 wt.% of PEG relative to the amount

 24

of PLA. Therefore, in this project, PEG at a concentration of 5 wt.% was incorporated with respect to the

PLA quantity. 6 wt.% of the PLA quantity was also tested, but as expected, continuous filament could not

be obtained since it had become too soft.

The Recipient first formulated a PEG -infused slurry (5 wt.% relative to the amount of PLA) that optimally

disperses commercial graphene flakes and facilitates its uniform distribution within the filament matrix.

Secondly, the filaments were manufactured with a diameter of 1.75 ± 0.05 mm at a temperature of 180°C

and the highest extrusion speed. Lower temperatures were tested, but because the PLA melting point is at

180°C, nothing came out of the extruder. Higher temperatures could decompose the PEG. So, 180°C was

the optimum temperature. Furthermore, CNTs act as a binding agent, facilitating the attachment of graphene

to the PLA chains. This interaction contributes to achieving a homogeneous mixture, which in turn has a

positive influence on the filament's printability.

Finally, the filament printability was evaluated with an FDM 3D printer. Due to the addition of plasticizer,

the filament was not brittle as previously seen. The printing process was meticulously monitored, with

special attention to filament feeding. The hot end temperature was set at 200ºC to make the melted filament

flow easier. The lower temperatures, owing to the high filament loading and the challenge in achieving

flow, proved to be ineffective.

The Recipient’s results indicated that adding PEG (5 wt.% relative to the amount of PLA) as plasticizer

could improve the flexibility and printability of the high loading (25 wt.% graphene and 25 wt.% CNT, 50

wt.% in total) filaments. Also, CNT addition (25 wt.%) improves the attachment of graphene to the PLA

chains that makes the mixture more homogeneous. This is why the Recipient added the same amount of

CNTs as graphene.

(002) peak at around 2θ = 26.5 degrees

(002) peak at around 2θ = 26.5 degrees

(004) peak at around 2θ = 54.7 degrees

Notably, these are the same peak positions as those found in graphite, and sometimes software cannot

intense, and sharper compared to the (004) peak. This is a key distinguishing feature, as in graphite, the

(004) peak is usually more prominent. Based on the intensity ratio, the results confirmed the existence of

graphene on the received powder.

The extruder and hot end are crucial components of FDM printers, fundamentally influencing the printer’s

functionality, the quality of the prints produced, and the range of materials that can be used. The extruder

is responsible for controlling the flow of the filament by pushing the filament with precise force and speed.

The high strength of CNTs/PLA composites requires the extruder to be durable and wear-resistant. All-

metal extruders generally have consistent performance over time compared with their plastic counterparts.

Hence, an extruder (Figure 27a) with dual drive gears and a 6.3:1 gear ratio was selected for filament. It

can be compatible with composite fiber filaments and is even used for steel, boron carbide, and tungsten

 31

materials. An all metal hot end with a heating block has a larger melting zone to meet the requirements of

high-speed and high-temperature printing. A hardened steel nozzle with a diameter of 0.4 mm was selected

to reduce the wear during printing composites.

Fig 27. All-metal extruder (a) and hot end (b).

Despite the different materials of the extruder, the mounting type of extruders also affects the functionality

and the quality of the prints produced. Bowden and direct drive setups are the main two types of extruders,

as shown in Figure 28.

 Figure 28. (a) direct-drive type, and (b) Bowden type extruding system.

Bowden extruders are designed to be mounted away from the moving mass of the Toolhead. A piece of

Bowden tube, which will vary in length depending on the size of the printer and the mounting position of

 32

the extruder, is then used to connect the extruder and the hot end. Bowden is widely used in FDM printers

due to its low cost and easy maintenance. However, the friction in the Bowden tube translates to a more

noticeable delay between the extruder and nozzle. In addition, some flexible and abrasive filaments can

easily bind or wear in Bowden tubes. Another issue with the Bowden design is that the brittle filament has

a larger bending radius, which can break in the tube. Direct drive setups have the extruder mounted

immediately before the hot end. They offer better control over the filament thanks to the distance between

the gears and the nozzle being much shorter. The overall design of the printer is shown in Fig. 29. Images

were taken during the printing process of a “Fortune Cat.”

 Figure 29. Direct drive setup of the extruder and hot end.

 • Composite fabrication and mechanical testing

Figure 36 illustrates the composite fabrication process, which involves three main steps. First, the green

part is created using a 3D printing process, where graphene is incorporated into the printed structure. This

is followed by a carbonization step, in which the polymer binder is removed, leaving behind a brown part

consisting primarily of a porous graphene matrix. Finally, the brown part undergoes resin impregnation,

where epoxy resin is infiltrated into the porous structure to reinforce and bind the graphene network,

resulting in a strong, lightweight composite material. This process allows for the precise creation of

graphene-based composites with tailored properties for various advanced applications.

carbonization to remove the polymer binder and obtain the brown part, and (c) resin impregnation to form

the final composite.

Conclusion

This project successfully demonstrated the feasibility of using coal-derived graphene for lightweight

composite manufacturing through additive manufacturing and thermal processing. As a result of the work

performed under this project, the technology reached Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 5 by the end of

relevant environment. the feasibility of using coal-derived graphene for lightweight composite

manufacturing through additive manufacturing and thermal processing. By completing the lab-scale

development, this work validated the structural integrity, cost-effectiveness, and commercial potential of

such materials.
