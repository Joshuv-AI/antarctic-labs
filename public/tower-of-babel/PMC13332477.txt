Association Between the Use of Gabapentinoids and Exacerbations of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Ranakishor Pelluri, Lalasa Sri Tulasi Royyuru, Suresh Varukolu, Paul Kitonga Muteti, Birungi Mariam Nalukenge, Sravani Thommandru, Buchi Naidu Nalluri, Prasada Chowdari Gurram, Bhima Sridevi, Blessy Chittelu, Vanitha Rani Nagasubramanian, Uday Venkat Mateti, Ananthesh L, Ramya Rachamanti, Nageswari Patnam, Kannan Gopal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence on gabapentinoids and its respiratory safety among the patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease(COPD) or asthma are remains uncertain.ObjectiveTo evaluate the risk of exacerbations with gabapentinoids.MethodsA thorough literature search up to January 2026 was done across different databases to find real-world studies examining gabapentinoid exposure and respiratory outcomes. The pooled hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence interval (CI) and incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were calculate using random-effects models. Subgroup and sensitivity analysis were also performed for included studies. Quality of studies was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.ResultsFour cohort studies (seven reports) included to this analysis. Gabapentinoids use did not associated with exacerbations of COPD or asthma (HR = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.93-1.34; p = 0.23; I2 = 90.7%). Further, analysis was stratified by gabapentinoid class and clinical indications. Subgroup analyses showed increased exacerbation risk with gabapentin (HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.02-1.58) and pregabalin (HR=1.37, 95% CI: 1.12-1.67), whereas mirogabalin showed no significant association. Elevated risk was consistent across clinical indications (HR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.35-1.48). Incidence rate analysis demonstrated higher exacerbation incidence among gabapentinoid users (IRR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.10-1.81; p=0.006).ConclusionsOverall, there was no significant association with gabapentinoids. However, gabapentin and pregabalin were linked to increased risk of exacerbations. Therefore, future long-term studies are required to establish this association with underlying biological mechanisms. [PROSPERO ID: CRD420251248968]Key words: Gabapentinoids, COPD exacerbations, Respiratory safety, Observational studies

Introduction

Gabapentinoids, such as gabapentin and pregabalin, are approved as anticonvulsant agents that were first discovered to treat epilepsy.1 They are widely prescribed in many countries to treat neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia, and anxiety disorders.2,3 Gabapentinoids have drawn interest for off-label indications such as chronic refractory cough because of its efficacy and tolerability profiles.4 They act by binding to the α2δ subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels and reducing central nervous system (CNS) neurotransmitter release, which raises safety concerns due to their CNS depressant effects.5, 6, 7 Evidence shows that gabapentin effectively controls refractory cough via modulating central cough hypersensitivity pathways.8 Gabapentinoids have a good tolerability profile, although they cause sedation, dizziness, and somnolence, and patients with asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) have chronic airway inflammation and reduced respiratory reserve, making them more vulnerable to adverse respiratory effects compared with the general population,9, 10, 11 despite their relatively predictable pharmacokinetic profile.12 In addition, the USFDA safety warnings are supported by safety reports.13 However, there is no convincing evidence establishing the respiratory tolerability of gabapentinoids in patients with asthma or COPD. The magnitude of potential harm remains uncertain, including comparative risks among individual gabapentinoids, monotherapy versus combination therapy, and respiratory-related outcomes. Furthermore, the FDA has issued safety warnings regarding serious breathing problems associated with gabapentinoids, particularly in vulnerable populations with impaired respiratory reserve. Given the inconsistent and fragmented existing evidence, clarifying the respiratory tolerability of gabapentinoids in COPD and asthma patients is of considerable clinical importance. Therefore, the present meta-analysis was undertaken to systematically evaluate the association between gabapentinoid use and the risk of respiratory issues in patients with asthma or COPD.

Materials and Methods

The present review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) guidelines, and it was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) Registration No. CRD420251248968.

Literature search

Three authors independently performed the exhaustive literature search using Web of Science, PubMed, and Scopus databases up to January 15, 2026, for observational cohort and/or case–control studies investigating an association between use of gabapentinoids and risk of respiratory issues. The search include: “Asthma” OR “pulmonary disease” OR “COPD” OR “Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary disease” OR “Gabapentinoids” OR “Gabapentin” OR “1 aminomethyl cyclohexaneacetic acid” OR “Neurontin” OR “Pregabalin” OR “(S)-3-(aminomethyl)-5-methylhexanoic acid” OR “Lyrica” OR “Mirogabalin” OR “(3S,5R)-5-(aminomethyl)-3-ethylepiperidin-2-one” OR “Tarlig” with limits; Human and English. We searched for additional studies in bibliographies and citation sections of retrieved articles. This study is reported in accordance with the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for meta-analysis of observational studies.14

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

In this review, we included all observational (case-control or cohort) studies that investigated exposure to gabapentinoids and the risk of worsening respiratory issues among COPD patients with different comorbid conditions. Any disagreements between authors concerning study inclusion were resolved by joint review and consensus. Reviewers are excluded the case reports, review articles, and letters to editorial. The most recent reports were included in the meta-analysis, and the remaining publications were excluded.

Quality assessment

Three reviewers (R.K.P., U.V.M., and L.T.R.) independently assessed the included studies’ methodological quality using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale.15 Studies scoring 9 stars were classified as high quality, whereas those scoring 7 or 8 stars were considered of moderate quality.

Data extraction

Three authors independently reviewed the primary studies to assess the appropriateness for inclusion in the present meta-analysis, and data were extracted. The following information was retrieved from each study: (a) authors’ name, year of publication, and country of the population studied; (b) study design; (c) number of patients; (d) estimates of hazard ratio (HR) and 95% CIs; (e) gabapentinoids exposure and exacerbations of respiratory issues; and (f) control for confounding factors by matching or adjustments, if applicable.

Statistical analysis

Time to respiratory exacerbations was analyzed using hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) as the primary measure of association. When multiple adjusted estimates were reported, the most fully adjusted HR was extracted. All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio (version 4.2, Posit Software, PBC, Boston, Massachusetts) and the meta package. Study-specific HRs were log-transformed, and standard errors were derived from reported CIs in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook.16 The pooled estimates were computed with a random-effects model (DerSimonian–Laird method with restricted maximum-likelihood estimation of between-study variance) and back-transformed for presentation. The source of heterogeneity was investigated using subgroup analyses based on the clinical indication (epilepsy, neuropathic pain, other chronic pain), individual gabapentinoids (pregabalin, gabapentin, and mirogabalin), and comparator groups (gabapentinoid users versus nonusers or alternative comparators, when available). For incidence outcomes, incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were calculated using study-specific event counts and corresponding person-time at risk. Pooled IRRs with 95% CIs were estimated using random-effects models, and incidence rates are standardized and reported per 100 person-years. Statistical heterogeneity was tested using the Cochran Q test and quantified with the I² statistic, with P < 0.10 and I² > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. Given the inclusion of fewer than 10 studies, publication bias was not formally assessed.16 Sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequential exclusion of individual studies. The primary outcome was the pooled HR for COPD exacerbations associated with gabapentinoid use.

A total of 465 records were identified, and after thorough screening, finally 4 studies were included in the review (Figure 1). Overall association between gabapentinoid use and exacerbation risk

The pooled random-effects meta-analysis showed no statistically significant association between gabapentinoid use and exacerbation risk in patients with COPD or asthma (HR = 1.12; 95% CI, 0.93–1.34; P = 0.23). However, substantial heterogeneity was noticed across the studies (I² = 90.7%), and the prediction interval (0.61–2.05) showed a wide variability in effect estimates, suggesting potential differences in population characteristics, exposure definitions, or outcome ascertainment (Figure 2).

Exacerbation risk in COPD or asthma patients treated with gabapentinoids (p= 0.2365). COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Subgroup analysis by gabapentinoid type

The findings of subgroup analyses showed significant differences between gabapentinoids. Both pregabalin and gabapentin reported a statistically significant increase in risk of exacerbations ([HR = 1.37; 95% CI, 1.12–1.67; I² = 92.9%] and [HR = 1.27; 95% CI, 1.02–1.58; I² = 7.2%]). However, low heterogeneity was observed for gabapentin. In contrast, mirogabalin was not associated with an increased risk of exacerbations (HR = 1.07; 95% CI, 0.97–1.17; I² = 0%), with no observed heterogeneity. Overall, a significant difference was noticed between these subgroups (P = 0.047) (Figure 3).

Subgroup analysis of exacerbation risk in COPD/asthma: comparison of pregabalin, gabapentin, and mirogabalin. COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Subgroup analysis by indication

All clinical indications, including epilepsy, neuropathic pain, and other chronic pain conditions, were associated with an increased risk of exacerbations. No significant differences were observed between indication subgroups (P = 0.66), and pooled analyses showed a consistently higher risk (HR = 1.42; 95% CI, 1.35–1.48). This corresponds to a 42% relative increase in the risk of exacerbations among gabapentinoid users, indicating that the elevated risk was constant across indications (Figure 4).

Subgroup analysis of exacerbation risk in COPD/asthma patients by indication for gabapentinoid use. COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Subgroup analysis of gabapentinoids versus nonusers/other treatments

Figure 5 presents a subgroup analysis comparing gabapentinoid users with nonusers and alternative therapies. Gabapentinoids use was significantly associated with a higher risk of exacerbation compared with nonusers (HR = 1.41; 95% CI, 1.35–1.48), TCAs (HR = 1.21, 95% CI, 1.03–1.42), and SSRIs (HR = 1.18; 95% CI, 1.09–1.27), but a lower risk compared with benzodiazepine/Z-hypnotics (HR = 0.69; 95% CI, 0.56–0.86). However, no significant differences were found when compared with opioids (HR = 1.02; 95% CI, 0.82–1.26) or opioids combined with benzodiazepine/Z-hypnotics (HR = 0.96; 95% CI, 0.77–1.20). Significant heterogeneity and subgroup differences were observed (I² = 90.7%; P < 0.0001).

Subgroup analysis: Gabapentinoids versus nonusers/other treatments for exacerbation risk.

Incidence rate analysis

We noticed 2 studies that reported incidence rates. Incidence rate analysis showed a significantly higher exacerbation incidence among gabapentinoid users compared with controls (IRR = 1.41 per 100 person-years, 95% CI, 1.10–1.81; P = 0.0065), with no heterogeneity observed among the studies (I² = 0.0%), supporting the robustness and consistency of this finding across included studies (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Sensitivity analysis

The robustness of the pooled effect estimate was assessed using a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. Sequential exclusion of individual studies resulted in minimal changes to the pooled HR, which remained close to the primary estimate and consistently crossed unity (HR range, 1.07–1.22). High heterogeneity persisted across all iterations (I² ≈ 89% to 92%), with 1 study (Sood A 2023a) exerting a disproportionate influence on the overall results. These findings suggest that although a single study contributed substantially to the observed heterogeneity, the pooled effect estimate remained generally stable across sensitivity analyses, thereby supporting the robustness of meta-analyses findings (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Quality assessment

Three reviewers used the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale to independently evaluate the quality of included studies across all the domains. Study group selection (4 stars), compatibility of studies based on design (cohort/cross-sectional) or analysis (2 stars), outcome/exposure assessment, and adequate follow-up (3 stars), with a maximum score of 9. Risk of bias was assessed based on the scores and categorized as low, moderate, and high risk (≥7 stars, 5–6 stars, and <5 stars), respectively. Any differences in the obtained scores were resolved through discussions and consensus. Overall, the majority of studies reported a low risk of bias, with only 1 study as moderate, indicating good methodological quality across included studies (Supplemental Table 1).

Discussion

This review synthesized data from observational studies and contextualized the pooled estimates from the included literature to assess the association between gabapentinoid use and the risk of COPD or asthma exacerbations. Although the overall pooled HR did not demonstrate a statistically significant association between gabapentinoid exposure and exacerbation risk, important patterns emerged when individual agents, comparators, and outcome metrics were examined, underscoring clinically relevant heterogeneity. The overall pooled estimate (HR = 1.12) demonstrates partial concordance with prior population-based cohort studies but suggests a more modest effect compared with several large real-world investigations. For instance, Rahman et al19 reported a significantly increased risk of severe COPD exacerbations associated with gabapentinoid use compared with nonusers (HR = 1.39; 95% CI, 1.29–1.50); in addition, confounders were not accounted for in their study. Similarly, Olaoye et al20 found an increased risk of severe exacerbations across indications such as epilepsy, neuropathic, chronic, and other pain in a UK cohort using time-conditional propensity score matching (HR = 1.43; 95% CI, 1.35–1.52) compared with gabapentinoid nonusers, with adjustment for smoking status. The comparatively lower pooled estimate in our meta-analysis likely reflects methodological differences, including inclusion of active-comparator designs and variation in outcome definitions, as well as dilution effects arising from between-study heterogeneity. In addition to patients with COPD, 2 observational studies conducted among surgical patients from the general population reported an increased incidence of respiratory depression during the early postanesthesia recovery phase after preoperative gabapentin administration.21,22 Together, these results suggest that gabapentinoids may increase respiratory risk in a variety of therapeutic settings, but they also highlight the need for cautious interpretation due to variations in study populations and outcome definitions.

In addition, significant divergence by gabapentinoid type was found in our subgroup analysis. Although mirogabalin was not linked to a higher incidence of exacerbation, both pregabalin and gabapentin were. This pattern is consistent with findings from Kimura et al,17 who found an elevated risk of corticosteroid-treated COPD exacerbations with pregabalin but not with gabapentin or mirogabalin when compared with TCAs or SNRIs among the middle-aged and older adult population. However, due to a lack of data, this study was unable to account for important confounders such as BMI and smoking status. In addition, comparisons with nonusers may yield more skewed results than comparisons with active comparators.23 Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic variability among the gabapentinoids, such as the greater bioavailability and linear pharmacokinetics of pregabalin (first order) and gabapentin (zero order), may put susceptible patients at increased risk of respiratory depression, potentially accounting for these observed variations.24,25 These results are further contextualized through comparative analysis. Sood et al20 reported that gabapentinoids, when used as substitutes for benzodiazepines in multimodal regimens, were associated with lower respiratory event risk compared with benzodiazepine-based combinations, but they were not risk-free. A nested case-control study by Olopoenia et al27 conifer that additive respiratory depression risk was noticed when gabapentinoids are combined with CNS depressants. This supports our finding that gabapentinoids may confer an intermediate respiratory risk-lower than sedatives but higher than nonsedating alternatives, highlighting the importance of comparator selection when interpreting safety signals. In our study, the incidence rate analyses showed a significant increase in exacerbation episodes among gabapentinoid users compared with controls. This finding is consistent with real-world studies demonstrating that gabapentinoids may increase the frequency of respiratory events rather than merely accelerating time to first exacerbation.18 These differences between IRR and HR highlight the potential for understanding clinically significant harm when relying solely on one effect metric. Biological plausibility lends additional credence to the aforementioned conclusions. Through modulation of α2δ calcium channels, gabapentinoids exert depressive effects on the CNS. These effects may impair respiratory drive, suppress cough reflexes, and promote hypoventilation, which are particularly consequential in patients with limited pulmonary reserve. In vulnerable populations, the dose-related adverse respiratory outcomes observed among patients with comorbid conditions and older adults further support the possibility of a causal relationship.26 When considered collectively, our findings both support and extend the existing evidence. Subgroup and incidence analysis suggest the gabapentinoid-related respiratory risk is agent-specific and context-dependent, despite a nonsignificant overall association. These results emphasize the need for careful initiation, dose escalation, and cautious use of gabapentin and pregabalin in patients with COPD or asthma, particularly when alternative therapies are available. The regulatory warnings have highlighted potential respiratory safety concerns associated with gabapentinoids. Future research should focus on large prospective longitudinal studies in diverse patient populations to clarify long-term tolerability, identify high-risk populations, evaluate drug-specific effects, and improve evidence for safer clinical use.

Strengths and limitations

Our review is strengthened by a comprehensive multidatabase search, real-world observational studies with adjusted estimates, and the adoption of reliable methodological techniques. The clinical relevance is consistent across the analyses. All included studies achieved high methodological quality and minimal risk of bias. However, residual confounding cannot be ruled out because all of the included studies were observational. Variations in demographic characteristics, exposure criteria, and outcome evaluation are reflected in significant variation across analyses. Causal inference is limited by exposure misclassification, a lack of dose-response data, and a small number of trials. The prevalence of data from high-income countries limits generalizability.
