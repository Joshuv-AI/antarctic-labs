Determinants of contraceptive use among married women in Rwanda: evidence from women’s empowerment-related indicators in the 2019/2020 Demographic and Health Survey
Authors: Emile Twagirumukiza, Egide Munyaneza, Valens Bubanje, Revocathe Girimpundu, Briand Mvuyekure, Emile Sebera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding factors influencing contraceptive use among married women is crucial for improving reproductive health outcomes. This study investigated the contribution of women’s empowerment-related indicators and contraceptive utilisation in Rwanda.MethodsThis study analysed data from the 2019/2020 Rwanda Demographic and Health Survey focusing on 7401 married women aged 15–49 years who had given birth within the 5 years before the survey. Women’s empowerment-related indicators included employment in the past 12 months, highest educational attainment, decision-making power and attitudes towards wife-beating. Additionally, sociodemographic factors including age, number of living children, the educational attainment of the husband, region, type of residence and wealth index were used to control for confounding effects. The outcome variable was the current use of any contraceptive method. Logistic regression analyses were conducted using SPSS V.25.0 to determine the association between predictors and the outcome variable.ResultsThe proportion of contraceptive use was 64.1%. Bivariate analysis showed significant associations between all sociodemographic characteristics, participation in decision-making, and contraceptive use (trend p<0.001). In the second model of multivariate analysis, women with primary education had higher odds of using contraceptives (adjusted OR (aOR): 1.376, 95% CI 1.178 to 1.608, p<0.001) compared with other educational levels. Employed women showed an increased likelihood of contraceptive use (aOR: 1.272, 95% CI 1.105 to 1.465, p=0.001). High participation in decision-making was strongly associated with contraceptive use (aOR: 1.664, 95% CI 1.311 to 2.111, p<0.001). The number of living children emerged as the strongest predictor, with women having 2–3 children showing the highest odds of contraceptive use (aOR: 175.209, 95% CI 91.268 to 336.353, p<0.001). Age exhibited an inverse relationship, with women aged 45–49 having significantly lower odds (aOR: 0.155, 95% CI 0.118 to 0.204, p<0.001).ConclusionsWomen’s empowerment through education, employment, and active participation in decision-making significantly influences contraceptive use among women in Rwanda. These findings underscore the importance of integrating women’s empowerment initiatives into reproductive health programs, focusing on education access, economic opportunities, and women’s involvement in household and reproductive health decisions to enhance reproductive health outcomes in Rwanda.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) and various global health institutions consider empowerment a fundamental element for promoting health and achieving fair social outcomes for all people.1 Research indicates an association between increased empowerment and reduced mortality and morbidity among women, as well as reduced rates of unintended pregnancies and sexually transmitted diseases, as far as reproductive health is concerned.24

The term ‘empowerment’ has lost much of its original meaning through overuse in international development contexts. While it initially referred to bottom-up movements challenging systemic power inequalities, the word has been co-opted and diluted by a diverse alliance of actors, including businesses, international non-governmental organisations, financial institutions, wealthy philanthropists, and development agencies.5 Empowerment is the process of enhancing the capacity of individuals or groups to make choices and to transform those choices into desired actions and outcomes.5 6

Research has historically suggested that improving women’s empowerment leads to better health outcomes for both mothers and children in developing nations.7 However, while studies continue to show connections between gender equality and improvements in maternal health and child survival rates, researchers still struggle to develop reliable methods to measure and quantify women’s empowerment.8 The 1994 International Conference on Population and Development declared that ‘the empowerment of women and ensuring women’s ability to control their fertility are cornerstones of population and development-related programmes’.9 The declaration emphasised that the ability to decide freely the number, spacing and timing of one’s children is a basic human right. Family planning programmes are associated with lower fertility and reduced maternal mortality. Through these programmes, women gain access to contraceptives, increasing the likelihood that they can achieve their desired family size.10

Despite its crucial importance, family planning remains unmet for many women. Among the 1.9 billion women of reproductive age (15–49 years) worldwide in 2021, 1.1 billion needed family planning. Of these, 874 million are using modern contraceptive methods and 164 million have an unmet need for contraception.11 Reasons for this slow increase include limited choice of methods, limited access to services, particularly among young and poorer women, fear or experience of side effects, cultural or religious opposition, gender-based barriers to accessing services and many others.12 If family planning needs were fully met, there would be 67% fewer unintended pregnancies, 70% fewer maternal deaths, 44% fewer newborn deaths and 73% fewer unsafe abortions.13

The literature clearly states the link between women’s empowerment and the utilisation of maternal and child health services.1416 Furthermore, a study which looked at data from 32 African nations found that when women had more power and autonomy in various aspects of their lives, they were more likely to use contraception. This relationship held even when researchers accounted for differences in factors like age, education, and economic status.17

The Rwandan government is committed to promoting gender equality and women’s empowerment, and, as a signatory to the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, it has adopted several institutional and policy measures that support these goals. Additionally, the government has also shown an increasing commitment to supporting the social and economic empowerment of women and fostering gender equality through constant review of policies, reassessment of priorities, commitment of adequate financial resources, encouragement of women’s participation in politics and effective implementation of programmes such as the Economic Development and Poverty Reduction Strategy and Vision 2020.18 19

Despite the crucial importance of family planning in reducing fertility and maternal mortality by preventing high-risk births and reducing abortions while also increasing female life expectancy,2022 the study indicated that 29% of women in Rwanda discontinued the use of contraceptives in 2020.23 24 This study, therefore, examined the association between selected women’s empowerment-related indicators and contraceptive use among married women in Rwanda.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study analysed data from the Rwanda Demographic and Health Survey (RDHS 2019/2020). RDHS is a nationally representative cross-sectional study that employed a two-stage sampling design. The first stage involved selecting sample clusters consisting of enumeration areas delineated for the 2012 Rwanda Population and Housing Census. The second stage involved systematic sampling of households. A household listing operation was undertaken in all selected enumeration areas from June to August 2019, and households to be included in the survey were randomly selected from these lists. This study used the individual recode file, which included data from 14 634 interviewed women aged 15–49 years who had given birth within the 5 years before the survey. The approval for using the dataset was granted by the DHS Programme following an online application (https://dhsprogram.com).

Study sample and participants

A total of 14 634 women aged 15–49 years were interviewed in the 2019/2020 RDHS. Of these, women who were not currently married or not living in union were excluded. Further exclusions were made for women with missing observations on key exposure or outcome variables. The final weighted analytic sample included 7401 currently married women.

Outcome variable

Current contraceptive use was derived from DHS variable v312. Methods were classified according to DHS definitions into modern methods (eg, pills, Intra-uterine devices, injectables, implants, condoms, sterilisation) and traditional methods (eg, periodic abstinence, withdrawal). For this study, contraceptive use was recoded as a binary variable: ‘Yes’ (currently using any modern or traditional method) and ‘No’ (not using any method).

Explanatory variables

Measures of women’s empowerment

The DHS women’s questionnaire also collected data on a woman’s participation in household decision-making and her attitude toward wife-beating. Two separate indices of empowerment were developed based on the number of household decisions in which the respondent participates and her opinion on the number of reasons that justify wife-beating. These indices were studied in relation to health outcomes, including contraceptive use, as well as the receipt of healthcare services during pregnancy, at delivery or in the postnatal period.

In this study, the attitude towards wife-beating was investigated by questioning whether women approved of being beaten by their husband under five circumstances: (1) neglect of a child; (2) going out without permission; (3) arguing with their husband/partner; (4) burning of food and (5) refusal to have sexual intercourse with their husband/partner. Responses to the five justification scenarios were summed to create a score ranging from 0 to 5. Women scoring 0 were categorised as disapproving of wife-beating, while those scoring ≥1 were categorised as approving of wife-beating. A ‘yes’ response for each condition was scored 1, which indicates approval of wife-beating; otherwise, it was recorded as ‘no’ referring to disapproval of wife-beating attitudes.

On the other hand, the decision-making participation variable was constructed by measuring women’s participation in: (1) deciding their healthcare; (2) large household purchases; (3) visits to family or relatives and (4) the use of the husband’s income. A score of 1 was given to women who reported participating in each decision-making aspect and 0 otherwise, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 4. The scores were then classified as low (0), medium (1–3) and high (4), consistent with previous DHS-based studies.2527

We also included women’s highest education attainment (no education, primary, secondary, higher) and past 12-month employment status (not employed, employed) among the indicators of empowerment, as they have been used in the other studies.28

Other-predicting variables

Additional variables that, based on the literature, might affect the use of contraceptive methods were considered.29 Six variables were included: age (15–24, 25–34, 35–44 and 45–49), husband’s educational level (no education, primary, secondary, higher and don’t know), household wealth index, number of living children, type of place of residence (urban or rural), and region (Kigali, East, West, North and South).

Missing data

A complete-case analysis approach was used. Observations with missing data on the outcome or key explanatory variables were excluded from regression analyses. The proportion of missing data was minimal (<5%) and consistent with DHS analytical guidelines.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were conducted to reflect the proportion of women using contraceptive methods across the independent variables. χ² was employed for the initial inferential analysis, as the outcome variable was nominal with two categories (using or not using any contraceptive). Second, survey-weighted binary and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted using SPSS V.25.0, accounting for the complex sampling design of the RDHS (clustering, stratification and sampling weights). Model 1 of multivariate logistic regression assessed the effect of women’s employment indicators only, while model 2 controlled for the effects of other sociodemographic characteristics. Results were presented as adjusted ORs (aOR) along with the respective 95% CIs signifying precision, using SPSS. The sample weight (wt) was used to account for the complex survey (svy) design and generalisability of the findings.

Results

This study analysed data from 7401 married women in Rwanda. The sample was predominantly composed of women aged 25–44 (77.9%), with most having a primary education (65.3%). The majority resided in rural areas (82.6%), with the Eastern region having the highest representation (27.1%). Additionally, most women’s partners also had a primary education (66.8%).

Regarding family size, most women had 1–3 children (72.9%) and a high proportion (84.2%) were employed in the past 12 months. Women’s empowerment indicators showed that most women (58.4%) participated highly in decision-making, though attitudes towards intimate partner violence were split nearly equally between approval and disapproval. The proportion of married women currently using any contraceptive method (modern or traditional) was 64.1% (table 1).

The first multivariate model examined the adjusted associations between women’s education, employment status, decision-making participation and contraceptive use while controlling for these variables simultaneously. Women with primary education had 44% higher odds of contraceptive use compared with those with no education (aOR:1.440, 95% CI 1.249 to 1.660, p<0.001). Women with secondary education also showed significantly higher odds of contraceptive use (aOR: 1.197, 95% CI 1.007 to 1.424, p=0.042). However, higher education showed no significant association with contraceptive use (aOR: 1.064, 95% CI 0.830 to 1.363, p=0.626). Employment status remained a significant predictor, with employed women having 29.1% higher odds of using contraceptives compared with unemployed women (aOR: 1.291, 95% CI 1.135 to 1.469, p<0.001). Women with high participation in decision-making had 56.8% higher odds (aOR: 1.568, 95% CI 1.256 to 1.957, p<0.001), while women with low participation had 49.3% higher odds of contraceptive use (AOR: 1.493, 95% CI 1.190 to 1.872, p=0.001).

Model 2 presents a more comprehensive multivariate analysis controlling for the confounding effects of demographic and socioeconomic factors. In this expanded model, the associations with primary education (aOR: 1.376, 95% CI 1.178 to 1.608, p<0.001), secondary education (aOR: 1.349, 95% CI 1.081 to 1.683, p=0.008) and higher education (aOR: 1.704, 95% CI 1.199 to 2.421, p=0.003) were maintained. Employment status sustained its significant association, with employed women showing 27.2% higher odds of contraceptive use (aOR: 1.272, 95% CI 1.105 to 1.465, p=0.001). The relationship between decision-making participation and contraceptive use strengthened in this model, with both medium participation (aOR: 1.662, 95% CI 1.304 to 2.118, p<0.001) and high participation (aOR: 1.664, 95% CI 1.311 to 2.111, p<0.001) showing similar increased odds compared with low participation. Age showed a strong inverse relationship with contraceptive use. Compared with women aged 15–24, all older age groups had significantly lower odds of contraceptive use, with odds decreasing progressively: 25–34 years (aOR: 0.566, 95% CI 0.459 to 0.698, p<0.001), 35–44 years (aOR: 0.344, 95% CI 0.272 to 0.435, p<0.001) and 45–49 years (aOR: 0.155, 95% CI 0.118 to 0.204, p<0.001). Regarding geographic variations, only the Northern region showed significantly higher odds of contraceptive use compared with Kigali (aOR: 1.335, 95% CI 1.074 to 1.659, p=0.009).

The number of living children remained the strongest predictor of contraceptive use. compared with women with no children, the odds were substantially higher for those with 1–2 children (aOR: 90.933, 95% CI 47.865 to 172.753, p<0.001), 2–3 children (aOR: 175.209, 95% CI 91.268 to 336.353, p<0.001) and 3–4 children (aOR: 148.111, 95% CI 76.547 to 286.580, p<0.001). Notably, after controlling for other predictors, rural residence, husband’s education level and the wealth index showed no significant association with contraceptive use (table 3).

Discussions

This study examined the association between women’s empowerment-related indicators and contraceptive use using a fully adjusted model. The findings indicate that education, employment and household decision-making participation were independently associated with contraceptive use. The findings revealed that 64.1% of married women were using contraceptive methods, which is higher than the sub-Saharan African contraceptive proportion of 29%, reflecting Rwanda’s successful family planning initiatives.30 This proportion is notably higher than in neighbouring countries, highlighting Rwanda’s progress in promoting family planning.31

The high proportion of women with primary education and those employed suggests improving socioeconomic conditions for women in Rwanda. However, the persistent gap in higher education access (4.9%) indicates ongoing challenges in advancing women’s educational opportunities. The high level of participation in household decision-making reflecting high participation reflects positive trends in women’s empowerment, though the nearly equal split in attitudes toward wife-beating (49% approving) suggests persistent gender-related social norms that require attention. The first multivariate model confirmed the independent effects of education, employment and decision-making participation on contraceptive use. The significant positive association between employment and contraceptive use (aOR: 1.291) aligns with studies from Burundi, showing how economic empowerment enhances women’s autonomy in reproductive health decisions.32 The strong relationship between high decision-making participation and contraceptive use reinforces evidence from Mali, which indicated that the women who jointly participated in the decision about spending, earnings and large purchases had high odds of using contraceptive use.33 Consistent results were reported in the study conducted by Tesfa et al34 in 33 sub-Saharan African countries.34 Additional evidence also indicated that enhancing women’s empowerment can lead to increased contraceptive use and improved utilisation of maternal healthcare services.17 35 However, disapproval of the justification of wife-beating was not significantly related to contraceptive use in our study. This aligns with the results of the studies conducted in Asian countries.36

The strengthened association with higher education in the second model suggests that advanced education might give women better knowledge, resources and autonomy in family planning decisions. The inverse relationship with age indicates that younger women might be more receptive to modern contraceptive methods, possibly due to changing social norms and better access to information. The low rate of contraceptive method utilisation in advanced age may be linked to the decreased perceived necessity for contraception as women near the end of their reproductive years, along with cultural or health-related factors that may shape contraceptive practices among older women.37

The substantially higher odds of contraceptive use among women with children (adjusted OR ranging from 90.933 to 175.209 for different parities) suggest that family planning decisions are strongly influenced by achieved family size. This finding is in line with other studies in Rwanda, Uganda and Ethiopia.3840 The non-significant association with the wealth index after controlling for other factors suggests that Rwanda’s family planning programmes might have successfully reduced economic barriers to contraceptive access.41 These findings highlight the complex interplay between women’s empowerment and contraceptive use in Rwanda. The results suggest that interventions targeting women’s education, employment opportunities and decision-making capacity could effectively promote contraceptive use and reproductive health outcomes.42

Women’s decision-making power and women’s autonomy together represent women’s empowerment. Many processes influence the use of contraception, but women’s decision-making power and their autonomy within the household are perhaps the most important factors affecting contraceptive use.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, contraceptive use was self-reported and may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. Third, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Fourth, empowerment was measured using proxy indicators rather than a validated composite index. Finally, complete-case analysis may introduce selection bias if missingness was not completely random.

Conclusions

This study explored the relationship between women’s empowerment and contraceptive use among married women aged 15–49 in Rwanda, using 2019/2020 DHS data. The findings showed the high prevalence of contraceptive method use, significantly exceeding the sub-Saharan African average and reflecting Rwanda’s success in family planning initiatives. Key factors such as education, employment and participation in household decision-making were strongly associated with contraceptive use, underscoring the role of socioeconomic empowerment in reproductive health decisions. However, gaps in higher education access and persistent gender norms highlight areas requiring further intervention. These results emphasise the need for strategies that enhance women’s education, economic opportunities and decision-making capacity to sustain progress in contraceptive use and overall maternal and child health outcomes in Rwanda. From a policy perspective, strengthening female education beyond the primary level, expanding women’s economic participation and promoting joint household decision-making may enhance contraceptive uptake. Integrating empowerment-sensitive strategies within Rwanda’s national family planning programmes may further accelerate progress toward reproductive health targets.
