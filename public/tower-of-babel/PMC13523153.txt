BMIPP Imaging in Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest
Authors: Hirohiko Aikawa, Kota Murai, Shimpei Seiki, Hirotaka Sawano, Makoto Kobayashi, Keiji Hirooka, Yasuyuki Hayashi, Yusuke Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVasospastic angina (VSA) is a potential cause of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA); however, diagnosis can be challenging when clinical evidence of ischemia is limited, making the indication for invasive spasm provocation testing uncertain. Iodine-123 β-methyl-p-iodophenyl-pentadecanoic acid (BMIPP) single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) reflects postischemic “ischemic memory” and can noninvasively suggest myocardial ischemia.Case SummaryWe report a 51-year-old man with exercise-related chest pain followed by ventricular fibrillation and OHCA. Emergency coronary angiography after return of spontaneous circulation showed no significant stenosis, whereas BMIPP SPECT imaging demonstrated reduced uptake in the inferior, posterolateral, apical, and septal regions. Subsequent ergometrine provocation testing provoked subocclusion of the left anterior descending artery. Cardiac magnetic resonance revealed no findings of cardiomyopathy or left ventricular thrombus. Based on the exertional symptom pattern, the case was diagnosed as exercise-induced VSA.DiscussionBMIPP SPECT imaging may guide decision-making on invasive spasm provocation testing in OHCA survivors with limited ischemic evidence.Take-Home MessagesExercise-induced VSA should be recognized as a potential cause of ventricular fibrillation and OHCA. BMIPP SPECT imaging can guide decisions regarding the indication for invasive coronary spasm provocation testing in OHCA survivors with limited evidence of ischemia.

History of Presentation

A 51-year-old man experienced chest pain at around 8:00 am during a 15-km run, which was relieved after a temporary cessation of exercise. After resuming running, he suddenly collapsed and lost consciousness. The event was witnessed by a bystander, who activated emergency medical services and initiated cardiopulmonary resuscitation. The initial electrocardiographic rhythm recorded after application of an automated external defibrillator by emergency responders was ventricular fibrillation (VF) (Figure 1). On arrival at our hospital, a 12-lead electrocardiogram obtained after ROSC showed atrial fibrillation with 0.5-mm ST-segment depression in leads V5-V6 and leads II, III, and aVF (Figure 2). Emergent coronary angiography (CAG) revealed no significant coronary artery stenosis (Video 1A and 1B), and left ventriculography demonstrated no regional wall motion abnormalities. Troponin T was only mildly elevated at 0.016 ng/mL, a level that could be explained by changes following cardiopulmonary arrest. Past Medical History

The patient had no medical history and no history of smoking.

Differential Diagnosis

The possible differential diagnoses included vasospastic angina (VSA), coronary embolism, spontaneous coronary artery dissection, Brugada syndrome, and idiopathic ventricular fibrillation.

Investigations

Although no clear clinical evidence suggestive of ischemic heart disease was identified, iodine-123 β-methyl-p-iodophenyl-pentadecanoic acid (BMIPP) single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) was performed in the hope of detecting “ischemic memory.” Because troponin T elevation was minimal, necrosis-based imaging modalities were considered unlikely to yield meaningful diagnostic findings. In contrast, BMIPP SPECT detects postischemic abnormalities of fatty acid metabolism rather than necrosis, and was therefore considered relatively more likely to capture evidence of ischemia. Our initial strategy was to refrain from invasive functional coronary testing with spasm provocation if the BMIPP SPECT images were negative. Accordingly, BMIPP SPECT imaging demonstrated reduced fatty acid metabolism in the inferior, posterolateral, apical, and septal regions, corresponding to the territories of the right coronary artery (RCA), the well-developed left circumflex artery, and the septal branches (Figure 3A and 3B).Figure 3BMIPP SPECT Images(A) Representative short-axis (Left) and long-axis (Right) images of BMIPP SPECT images. Based on these findings, coronary angiography was repeated, followed by an ergometrine provocation test to evaluate for suspected VSA (Figure 4A, Video 2A). After intracoronary administration of 32 μg of ergometrine into the left coronary artery, a 99% focal stenosis was induced at the proximal segment of the left anterior descending artery, accompanied by typical chest pain and ST-segment depression in leads V5-V6. Collateral flow from the left circumflex artery to the left anterior descending artery suggested repeated episodes of vasospasm (Figure 4B, Video 2B). The stenosis was resolved promptly after intracoronary nitrate administration, confirming the diagnosis of VSA (Figure 4C, Video 2C).Figure 4Ergometrine Provocation Test for the Left Coronary Artery(A) Baseline coronary angiography showing no significant stenosis. In this case, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation without anticoagulation therapy was noted; however, the initial coronary angiography revealed no findings suggestive of coronary embolism. Furthermore, subsequent cardiac magnetic resonance imaging revealed no late gadolinium enhancement suggestive of cardiomyopathy, no evidence of left ventricular thrombus, and no ischemic changes. In addition, in-hospital continuous electrocardiographic monitoring, resting 12-lead electrocardiograms after neurological recovery, and a cibenzoline provocation test revealed no findings suggestive of primary electrical disease such as Brugada syndrome or long QT syndrome. Although idiopathic VF remains a diagnosis of exclusion and cannot be entirely ruled out, we considered exercise-induced VSA to be the most plausible diagnosis based on the reproducible ischemic substrate demonstrated by BMIPP SPECT and ergometrine provocation testing.

Management

Treatment was initiated with nifedipine at a dose of 40 mg/d, after which no recurrence of chest pain was observed. For paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, edoxaban was initiated at a dose of 60 mg/d. Given that the patient had been resuscitated from VF-induced cardiac arrest, a subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was placed for secondary prevention. The patient was subsequently discharged in stable condition.

Discussion

VSA is one of the important functional abnormalities of the coronary artery that can contribute to not only variant angina but also acute myocardial infarction, ventricular arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death.1 Typically, VSA presents with attacks at rest during nighttime; however, a small number of cases have been reported in which exercise or the postexercise recovery phase triggers episodes.2 In particular, exercise-induced VSA tends to occur predominantly in the morning hours, reflecting the diurnal variation in coronary vasomotor reactivity. In the present case, on subsequent detailed history-taking, the patient reported that he had experienced similar exertional chest pain on multiple occasions during morning runs before the index event, which supports the diagnosis of exercise-induced VSA.

VSA is definitively diagnosed by invasive acetylcholine or ergometrine provocation testing, which is considered the gold standard. However, for safety reasons, provocation testing is often deferred and performed later in patients who show no apparent coronary abnormalities on CAG immediately after ROSC. In addition, patients with poor neurological outcomes may not have the opportunity to undergo subsequent provocation testing if the initial CAG is normal, or if CAG itself is not performed after ROSC. As a result, the prevalence of VSA may be under-reported, and the potential involvement of coronary artery spasm should always be considered in survivors of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA). Indeed, studies including all survivors of cardiogenic OHCA have reported that VSA accounts for 3.3% to 7% of cases.3,4 In contrast, among patients who underwent CAG after resuscitation from cardiogenic OHCA, 11.0% to 13.8% of cases were attributed to VSA.5,6 These findings suggest that etiological investigations may be insufficient in real-world practice, even among survivors of cardiogenic OHCA.

BMIPP SPECT imaging assesses reduced fatty acid metabolism in postischemic myocardium. Because metabolic recovery lags behind perfusion recovery, BMIPP SPECT imaging can detect ischemia within approximately 1 week after onset, providing a minimally invasive tool to evaluate ischemic involvement in OHCA survivors. A previous study has shown that BMIPP SPECT imaging performs well for diagnosing VSA, with a sensitivity of 72.5% and a specificity of 82.7%.7 Notably, BMIPP SPECT can also detect transient ischemia that may not be captured by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging with late gadolinium enhancement.8 Despite these advantages, BMIPP SPECT imaging is not mentioned in European and U.S. guidelines for VSA. In the present case, despite the absence of severe coronary stenosis or regional wall motion abnormalities, BMIPP SPECT provided clear evidence of myocardial ischemia, supporting the decision to proceed with additional invasive spasm-provocation testing. Although the diagnostic value of BMIPP SPECT for ischemic memory is well established, its application as a noninvasive gatekeeper to select candidates for invasive spasm provocation testing among OHCA survivors without conventional ischemic evidence has not been emphasized in prior reports or current guidelines. In this clinical context, the present case demonstrates that BMIPP SPECT imaging may help identify OHCA survivors who may benefit from invasive spasm provocation testing.

A discrepancy was also noted between the multiterritory BMIPP abnormalities and the focal left anterior descending artery spasm provoked during testing. Precise localization of the culprit vessel can be challenging, and several factors may account for these discrepancies. First, provocation testing was not performed in the RCA; therefore, RCA spasm could not be assessed. Second, previous studies have shown that the distribution of inducible coronary spasm may vary over time, indicating that a single provocation test may not necessarily identify the exact site responsible for the spontaneous ischemic event.9 Third, the possible contribution of coronary microvascular spasm could not be evaluated in this case. These considerations warrant cautious interpretation of the BMIPP findings.

Moreover, as shown in this case, multivessel coronary involvement and OHCA presentation are both established risk factors for recurrent cardiovascular events in VSA.10 In the present case, these high-risk features, together with the residual possibility that idiopathic VF could not be entirely excluded, supported the decision to proceed with ICD implantation for secondary prevention. Therefore, appropriate medical therapy and consideration of ICD implantation are crucial for improving prognosis.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After discharge, the patient continued nifedipine therapy, with no recurrence of angina.

Conclusions

In this case, BMIPP SPECT imaging suggested the presence of myocardial ischemia and helped determine the indication for invasive coronary spasm provocation testing in an OHCA survivor with limited clinical evidence of ischemia. This approach enabled a definitive diagnosis of exercise-induced VSA and contributed to the selection of an appropriate secondary prevention strategy. BMIPP SPECT imaging potentially serves as a useful adjunctive diagnostic tool to support clinical decision-making regarding invasive coronary spasm provocation testing in OHCA survivors with an unclear etiology.
