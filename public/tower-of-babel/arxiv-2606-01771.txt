Abstract
. This paper investigates an isoperimetric-type problem posed by L. A. Santaló, concerning convex surfaces in hyperbolic 3-space that minimize total mean curvature among all convex surfaces with fixed surface area. This problem asks for a characterization of the minimizers and for the optimal form of a Minkowski-type inequality in hyperbolic 3-space. In this work, we propose a conjectural description of the minimizers under certain regularity assumptions. We also construct a new family of convex surfaces as as potential minimizer candidates and establish a property of the singular points of any minimizer.

Primary: 52A40, 53C20; Secondary: 52A38, 53A05.

1.
Introduction

This paper concerns an isoperimetric-type problem raised by L. A. Santaló. In 1963, Santaló conjectured
[
21
]
(see also
[
23
, p. 78]
) that, for any compact convex domain
\Omega
in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}(a)
with constant curvature
a\leq 0
and with
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
,

(1.1)

M(\Gamma)\geq\sqrt{16\pi S(\Gamma)-4aS(\Gamma)^{2}},

where
S(\Gamma)
denotes the surface area of
\Gamma
,
M(\Gamma):=\int_{\Gamma}Hd\mu
is the
total mean curvature
of
\Gamma
, and the
mean curvature
of
\Gamma
is the trace of the second fundamental form
H:=\textup{trace}(\mathrm{I\!I}_{\Gamma})
.
The lower bound of (
1.1
) would then correspond to the total mean curvature of a sphere with the same area as
\Gamma
. That is, if (
1.1
) holds, then geodesic balls minimize total mean curvature among convex surfaces with fixed area.

By scaling the metric, we may take
a=-1
without loss of generality, and thus the ambient space is the standard hyperbolic
3
-space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. The conjecture was disproved by Naveira-Solanes
[
23
, p. 815]
(see also
[
20
, p. 109]
or
[
10
, Note 1.3]
). Naveira-Solanes constructed examples indicating that (
1.1
) is false in general. They showed that a flat double disk, which is isometric to a geodesic sphere in a totally geodesic plane isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, embedded in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, with its two faces counted in the surface area and its edge counted in the singular total mean curvature, gives a counterexample to (
1.1
) when the surface area
S(\Gamma)
is large enough. In fact, if we denote
M_{1}(S)
to be the total mean curvature of the geodesic sphere in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with surface area
S
, and
M_{2}(S)
to be the total mean curvature of the flat double disk in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with surface area
S
, then there exists a constant
\hat{S}=\frac{8\pi(\pi^{2}-8)}{16-\pi^{2}}\approx 7.665
such that
M_{1}(S)>M_{2}(S)
whenever
S>\hat{S}
. We will explain that this family of convex bodies provides a counterexample to (
1.1
) in Section
2
. On the positive side, Ge-Wang-Wu
[
8
, Theorem 6.1]
, see also
[
5
]
, proved that among horo-convex surfaces with fixed surface area, the minimizer of total mean curvature is the geodesic sphere. That is, (
1.1
) holds for all compact horo-convex surfaces
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.

In hyperbolic spaces, the total mean curvature of a general convex surface (without any regularity assumption) is well-defined by approximation of outer parallel surfaces, see
[
10
, Section 3]
. By Blaschke selection theorem, a minimizer of total mean curvature among convex domains in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with a given surface area exists. One would like to characterize the geometry of the minimizers, as well as the optimal form of Minkowski-type inequality. We call this
Santaló’s problem
. This is a generalization of the isoperimetric problem in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
related to the total mean curvature and surface area.

Minimization of total mean curvature of convex hypersurfaces has long been studied. In 1901, Minkowski
[
19
]
proved that the following inequality holds for any non-empty bounded

C^{2}
convex surface
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

(1.2)

M(\Gamma)\geq\sqrt{16\pi S(\Gamma)},

and equality holds only when
\Gamma
is a sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

The original proof of Minkowski is based on the isoperimetric inequality together with Steiner-Minkowski formulas. Hence, inequality (
1.2
) remains true if
\Gamma
is only a
C^{1,1}
surface (or equivalently, if
\Gamma
has positive reach). If we do not assume any regularity, the same inequality holds if we use the mean width of the convex body enclosed by the surface as an alternative definition for total mean curvature.
Guan-Li
[
13
, Theorem 2]
proved that (
1.2
) holds provided that
\Gamma
is star-shaped and mean-convex. G. Huisken
[
13
, Theorem 6]
(see also
[
17
]
) showed that (
1.2
) holds for outward-minimizing surfaces. Dalphin-Henrot-Masnou-Takahashi
[
6
, Theorem 1.1]

proved (
1.2
) in the case where
\Gamma
is axially symmetric and such that
\Gamma\cap P
is connected for every affine plane
P
orthogonal to the axis of symmetry.

Inequality (
1.2
) is actually a consequence of a generalization due to Minkowski of the isoperimetric inequality. This generalization uses the notion of mixed volumes and quermassintegrals of convex bodies, and total mean curvature is in fact the first quermassintegral in Euclidean spaces. We refer to
[
25
]
,
[
26
]
,
[
22
]
for details, and
[
27
]
for quermassintegrals in hyperbolic spaces.
Generalizations of (
1.2
) to hyperbolic spaces have been a long-standing problem
[
21
]
,
[
7
,
20
]
. Santaló’s problem is one such problem. In recent years, using of curvature flows has led to progress, see
[
27
,
8
,
3
,
24
,
4
,
10
]
.

Brendle-Guan-Li
[
4
]
proved that for any compact convex body
\Omega
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
,

(1.3)

W_{1}(\Gamma)\geq\sqrt{S(\Gamma)}\sqrt{S(\Gamma)+4\pi}+4\pi\operatorname{arcsinh}\left(\sqrt{\frac{S(\Gamma)}{4\pi}}\right),

where
W_{1}(\Gamma)=M(\Gamma)-2\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega)
is the first quermassintegral of the convex body
\Omega
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
enclosed by
\Gamma
, and
mean convex
means the mean curvature
H
is non-negative on
\Gamma
. Equality holds only if
\Gamma
is a geodesic sphere in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. Although (
1.3
) is sharp in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, the volume of the enclosed domain appears in the inequality. Ghomi-Spruck
[
10
]
proved that for any compact convex surface
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}(a)
with constant curvature
a\leq 0
,

(1.4)

M(\Gamma)\geq\sqrt{16\pi S(\Gamma)-2aS(\Gamma)^{2}},

where equality holds only if
a=0
and the domain bounded by
\Gamma
is isometric to a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Hence (
1.4
) is not sharp when the ambient space is fixed to be
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.
Recently the author
[
15
]
strengthened (
1.4
) to a sharp inequality in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. For any compact convex body
\Omega
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
,

(1.5)

M(\Gamma)\geq\sqrt{16\pi S(\Gamma)+2S(\Gamma)^{2}+2S_{0}(\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega))^{2}},

where
S_{0}
denotes the isoperimetric profile function, that is,
S_{0}(x)
is the surface area of the (geodesic) sphere in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with volume
x
. Equality holds only if
\Gamma
is a geodesic sphere in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. (
1.5
) appears to be the sharpest Minkowski-type inequality in hyperbolic 3-space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
currently known.

In
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, Blaschke selection theorem guarantees the existence of a minimizer of total mean curvature among convex domains with fixed surface area (we include a brief proof in Section
2
). Yet, the shape of the general convex minimizer is not known. Therefore,
Santaló’s problem
, on finding the optimal convex surface with the minimum total mean curvature
M
among convex surfaces with fixed surface area
S
, is still open. We believe a description of the minimizer and the related geometric inequality can deepen our understanding of hyperbolic geometry, and have applications in mathematical physics and general relativity. For example, the total mean curvature in hyperbolic space is also used in the definition of Wang-Yau’s quasi-local mass in
[
28
, Theorem 1.3]
.

In the search for the minimizer to Santaló’s problem, the following was observed by Guan
[
14
]
,

Proposition 1.1
.

If
\Gamma
is a convex surface in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
that attains the minimum total mean curvature among surfaces with fixed area, that is, there exists a constant
S>0
such that

\displaystyle M(\Gamma)=\min\left\{M(\Sigma):\Sigma\text{ is a convex surface in }\mathbb{H}^{3},S(\Sigma)=S\right\},

then

(1)

If
\Gamma
is degenerate, that is, the convex hull of
\Gamma
has no interior point, then
\Gamma
is a flat double disk.

(2)

If
\Gamma
is
C^{2}
and strictly convex, then
\Gamma
is a geodesic sphere.

Therefore, if one can prove that every minimizer of Santaló’s problem falls into these two cases, then the minimizer is determined to be either the geodesic sphere or the flat double disk. As a result, Proposition
1.1
led Guan to speculate the optimal inequality for Santaló’s problem to be

(1.6)

\displaystyle M(\Gamma)\geq

\displaystyle\min\left\{M_{1}(S(\Gamma)),M_{2}(S(\Gamma))\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\left\{\sqrt{16\pi S(\Gamma)+4S(\Gamma)^{2}},\sqrt{2\pi^{3}S(\Gamma)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}S(\Gamma)^{2}}\right\},

However, it turns out that Santaló’s problem is more complicated. We construct a family of convex bodies in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
which shows (
1.6
) does not hold in general.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist positive constants
S_{1},S_{2}
with
S_{1}<S_{2}
such that for any
S
with
S_{1}<S<S_{2}
, there exists a convex surface
\Gamma^{S}
such that
\Gamma^{S}
has surface area
S
and

\displaystyle M(\Gamma^{S})<

\displaystyle\min\left\{M_{1}(S),M_{2}(S)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\left\{\sqrt{16\pi S+4S^{2}},\sqrt{2\pi^{3}S+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}S^{2}}\right\}.

We will show that

\Gamma^{S}=\Gamma(r_{S},\alpha_{S})
arises from a two-variable family of convex surfaces, and can be described as a geodesic sphere cut by two totally geodesic planes in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, forming a "drum" shape.
The existence of such surfaces suggests that the optimal convex surface is possibly non-smooth.

In the study of convex bodies without any regularity assumption on their boundaries, the theory of singular points and curvature measures provides useful tools. General convex surfaces may have singular points, where there are multiple unit outer normal vectors (or supporting planes). If the ambient space is
3
-dimensional, there are two types of singular points:
1
-singular points, with a
2
-dimensional normal cone, behaving like edges of a cube;
0
-singular points, with a
3
-dimensional normal cone, behaving like vertices of a cube. We will give a brief review for singular points and normal cones in Section
2
, Subsection
2.4
.
Regarding the geometry of singular points of minimizers of Santaló’s problem, we will prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1.3
.

If
\Gamma
is a convex surface in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
that attains the minimal total mean curvature with fixed surface area, then
\Gamma
has no
0
-singular point. That is, for any point
p
on
\Gamma
, the normal cone
N(p,\operatorname{conv(\Gamma)})
of the convex body
\operatorname{conv}(\Gamma)
at
p
is
1
-dimensional or
2
-dimensional;
p
is either a
1
-singular point or a regular point.

Theorem
1.3
shows that even if the minimizer of Santaló’s problem is non-smooth and singular, it cannot have points that behave like the vertex of a cone or the vertex of a triangular pyramid. This theorem can help to rule out some convex surfaces as minimizers of Santaló’s problem, such as polytopes or cones in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we list some notations and facts about the known examples, and review the background in convex geometry. In Section
3
, we will prove Proposition
1.1
. In Section
4
, we will construct the new family of convex bodies whose total mean curvature is strictly less than those of the geodesic sphere and the flat double disk with the same area, thereby forming a family of counterexamples to (
1.6
) and proving Theorem
1.2
. In Section
5
, we will prove Theorem
1.3
using a local argument based on estimating the total mean curvature of a new convex body truncated by a family of planes at the singular point. We will discuss open questions and further study directions related to Santaló’s problem at the end of this paper.

2.
Preliminaries

In this paper, any ambient manifold
N
is an
(n+1)
-dimensional space form. A
convex set

X
in
N
is a set whose convex hull
\operatorname{conv}(X)
is itself. A
convex hypersurface
is the boundary of a convex set. If the ambient manifold
N
has dimension
\dim(N)=3
, then we call the convex hypersurface a
convex surface
. A
convex body
is a bounded closed convex set.

When a hypersurface is
C^{2}
, we have the following equivalent definition for convexity: A
convex
hypersurface
\Gamma
of an ambient manifold
N
is a closed embedded submanifold of codimension one which, when properly oriented,
has positive semidefinite second fundamental form
\mathrm{I\!I}_{\Gamma}
. A
strictly convex
hypersurface
\Gamma
of
N
is a convex hypersurface with positive definite second fundamental form.

2.1.
Naveira-Solanes’ counterexample

Here we will list some facts about the geometry of hyperbolic 3-space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and examine Naveira-Solanes’ counterexample
[
20
]
mentioned above to show that the flat double disks form a counterexample to (
1.1
). See also
[
10
, Note 1]
.

For a geodesic sphere
\mathbb{S}(r)
with radius
r
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
,
it is well known that its enclosed volume
V
, surface area
S
and total mean curvature
M
are

\displaystyle V=V(B(r))

\displaystyle=2\pi\left(\sinh(r)\cosh(r)-r\right),

\displaystyle S=S(\mathbb{S}(r))

\displaystyle=4\pi\sinh(r)^{2},

\displaystyle M=M(\mathbb{S}(r))

\displaystyle=8\pi\sinh(r)\cosh(r),

and in particular, we have

M=\sqrt{16\pi S+4S^{2}}
,
and we may denote this function by
M_{1}
, that is,

(2.1)

\displaystyle M_{1}(S):=\sqrt{16\pi S+4S^{2}}.

The flat double disk in Naveira-Solanes’ counterexample
[
20
]
mentioned above is constructed by taking a
disc
D(r)
of radius
r
in a totally geodesic surface isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. Its total mean curvature can be computed by taking the limit of the total mean curvature of its outer parallel surface
\Gamma=\Gamma(r,\epsilon)
at distance
\epsilon
. Its surface area consists of two faces of the disk
D(r)
:

S=2S_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(D)=4\pi(\cosh(r)-1),

where the
S
on the left-hand side denotes the total surface area of the flat double disk
D
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and the
S_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(D)
denotes the area of the disk with radius
r
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

Note that
\Gamma
consists of a pair of topological disks parallel to
D
plus a half tube
T
about
\partial D
. The mean curvature of the disks vanishes as
\epsilon\to 0
. On the other hand,
S(T)\to L(\partial D)\pi\sinh(\epsilon)
up to first order (where
L(\partial D)
denotes the perimeter of
\partial D
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
), since the full tube about
\partial D
is foliated by (geodesic) circles of radius
\epsilon
. Therefore
M(\Gamma)\to\partial S(T)/\partial\epsilon=L(\partial D)\pi\cosh(\epsilon)
. Thus

M=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}M(\Gamma)=L(\partial D)\pi=2\pi^{2}\sinh(r).

Thus for the flat double disk, its total mean curvature
M=M(D(r))
and surface area
S=S(D(r))
satisfies

\displaystyle M=\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{8\pi S+S^{2}}=\sqrt{2\pi^{3}S+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}S^{2}}.

We denote this function by
M_{2}
, that is,

(2.2)

\displaystyle M_{2}(S):=\sqrt{2\pi^{3}S+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}S^{2}}.

Clearly when
S
is large enough,
M_{2}(S)<M_{1}(S)
. In fact,
M_{2}(S)<M_{1}(S)
for all
S>\hat{S}=\frac{8\pi(\pi^{2}-8)}{16-\pi^{2}}\approx 7.665
.

In this paper, we will use the Beltrami-Klein model for hyperbolic 3-space. Specifically, we view
\mathbb{H}^{3}
as
(B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1),g^{BK})
. For
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the Riemannian metric tensor on
T_{x}\mathbb{H}^{3}
is

g^{BK}(x)=\frac{1}{1-\sum x_{i}^{2}}\left(\sum dx_{i}\otimes dx_{i}\right)+\frac{1}{(1-\sum x_{i}^{2})^{2}}\left(\left(\sum x_{i}dx_{i}\right)\otimes\left(\sum x_{i}dx_{i}\right)\right),

where all sums are taken as
\sum_{i=1}^{3}
.

The following coordinate identity map gives a canonical homeomorphism from
\mathbb{H}^{3}
to
B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1)
.

(2.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Id}:(B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1),g^{BK})\rightarrow

\displaystyle(B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1),g^{\mathbb{R}^{3}})

\displaystyle(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\mapsto

\displaystyle(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}).

An advantage of the Beltrami-Klein model is that geodesics are preserved. That is, for a continuous curve
\gamma=\gamma(t)
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}=(B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1),g^{BK})
,
\gamma
is a geodesic in
(B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1),g^{BK})
if and only if
\operatorname{Id}(\gamma)
is a geodesic in
(B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1),g^{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
(but not necessarily of the same speed).

2.2.
Notations and Facts about Geometric Flows

Here we list some evolution equations for geometric flows and recall outer parallel surfaces.

A
geometric flow
of a
C^{2}
closed hypersurface
\Gamma
in a Riemannian
(n+1)
-manifold
N

[
2
,
11
,
16
]
is a one-parameter family of immersions
X\colon\Gamma\times[0,T)\to N
,
X_{t}(\cdot):=X(\cdot,t)
, given by

(2.4)

X_{t}^{\prime}(p)=-F_{t}(p)\nu_{t}(p),\quad\quad\quad X_{0}(p)=p,

where
(\cdot)^{\prime}:=\partial/\partial t(\cdot)
,

\nu_{t}
is a normal vector field along
\Gamma_{t}:=X_{t}(\Gamma)
, and the
speed function

F_{t}
is a smooth function on
\Gamma_{t}
. Let
\nu_{t}(p)
be the normal and
\kappa_{i}^{t}(p)
the principal curvatures of
\Gamma_{t}
at the point
X_{t}(p)
.
Let
d\mu_{t}
be the area element induced on
\Gamma
by
X_{t}
.
G_{t}:=\det(\mathrm{I\!I}_{t})
and
H_{t}:=\textup{trace}(\mathrm{I\!I}_{t})
are the
Gauss-Kronecker curvature
and
mean curvature
of
\Gamma_{t}
, respectively.

By
[
16
, Thm. 3.2(v)]
and
[
16
, Lem. 7.4]
, for any geometric flow,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(H_{t})

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}F_{t}+\left(|\mathrm{I\!I}_{t}|^{2}+\operatorname{Ric}(\nu_{t})\right)F_{t},

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(d\mu_{t})

\displaystyle=-F_{t}H_{t}d\mu_{t},

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}S(\Gamma_{t})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Gamma}F_{t}H_{t}d\mu_{t},

where
|\mathrm{I\!I}_{t}|:=\sqrt{\sum(\kappa_{i}^{t})^{2}}
,
\Delta_{t}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator induced on
\Gamma
by
X_{t}
, and
\operatorname{Ric}(\nu_{t})
is the Ricci curvature of
N
at the point
X_{t}(p)
in the direction of
\nu_{t}(p)
, i.e., the sum of sectional curvatures of
N
with respect to a pair of orthogonal planes which contain
\nu_{t}(p)
.

In particular, when the speed function
F_{t}\equiv 1
, the geometric flow exists for all
t>0
and is called
geodesic normal flow
. The hypersurface
\Gamma_{t}
obtained from
\Gamma
under geodesic normal flow is called the
outer parallel surface
of
\Gamma
at distance
t
. An equivalent definition of outer parallel surface is given by
\Gamma_{t}:=\widehat{d_{\Gamma}}^{-1}(t)
, where
\widehat{d_{\Gamma}}
is the distance function of the convex surface
\Gamma
, see for example
[
9
, Sec. 2 & 3]
. Hence the outer parallel surface is well-defined for any convex surface as an initial surface.

A fact which will be used frequently below is that for any convex surface
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, its outer parallel surface
\Gamma_{t}
is
C^{1,1}
and convex for any
t>0

[
9
, Sec. 2 & 3]
. In particular, for
t>0
,
\Gamma_{t}
is twice differentiable almost everywhere and so its total mean curvature
M(\Gamma_{t})
is well-defined and positive.
We will prove the following invariant along geodesic normal flow:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a convex surface in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, and
\Gamma_{t}
be its outer parallel surface at distance
t
. Then for any
t\geq 0
,

(2.6)

\displaystyle M(\Gamma_{t})^{2}-16\pi S(\Gamma_{t})-4S(\Gamma_{t})^{2}=M(\Gamma)^{2}-16\pi S(\Gamma)-4S(\Gamma)^{2}

Proof.

For any
t>0
, since
\Gamma_{t}
is twice differentiable almost everywhere, we may apply (
2.5
)
and plug in
F_{t}\equiv 1
to get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}M(\Gamma_{t})=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{d}{dt}(H_{t})d\mu_{t}+H_{t}\frac{d}{dt}(d\mu_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\Big(\Delta_{t}F_{t}+\big(|\mathrm{I\!I}_{t}|^{2}-(H_{t})^{2}\big)F_{t}+\operatorname{Ric}(\nu_{t})F_{t}\Big)d\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Gamma}(G_{t}+1)d\mu_{t}

(2.7)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}S(\Gamma_{t})=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}H_{t}d\mu_{t}=M(\Gamma_{t}).

By Gauss’ equation, for all
p\in\Gamma_{t}
,

G_{t}(p)=K_{\Gamma_{t}}(p)-K_{N}(T_{p}\Gamma_{t}),

where
K_{\Gamma_{t}}
is the sectional curvature of
\Gamma_{t}
, and
K_{N}(T_{p}\Gamma_{t})
is the sectional curvature of
N
with respect to the tangent plane
T_{p}\Gamma_{t}\subset T_{p}N
. Thus by Gauss-Bonnet theorem,

\int_{\Gamma}G_{t}d\mu_{t}=4\pi-\int_{p\in\Gamma_{t}}K_{N}(T_{p}\Gamma_{t})=4\pi+S(\Gamma_{t}).

Substituting this into (
2.7
), we have for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(M(\Gamma_{t})^{2}-16\pi S(\Gamma_{t})-4S(\Gamma_{t})^{2}\right)=0,

which implies that the quantity

M(\Gamma_{t})^{2}-16\pi S(\Gamma_{t})-4S(\Gamma_{t})^{2}
is a constant for all
t\geq 0
.
∎

2.3.
Quermassintegrals of convex bodies in hyperbolic spaces

Here we list definitions and properties of quermassintegrals in Euclidean spaces and hyperbolic spaces.
One may refer to
[
26
]
,
[
22
]
and
[
27
]
for more background.

For a (geodesically) convex domain
K
in a
(n+1)
-dimensional space form
N
of constant curvature
C\in\{-1,0,1\}
, an equivalent definition of
quermassintegrals
is the following:

(2.8)

\displaystyle W_{k-1}(K):=\frac{(n+1-k)\omega_{k-1}\cdots\omega_{0}}{\omega_{n-1}\cdots\omega_{n-k}}\int_{\mathcal{L}_{k}(N)}\chi(L_{k}\cap K)dL_{k}(N),

\displaystyle\quad k=1,\cdots,n,

where
\omega_{k}
denotes the Lebesgue measure of the
k
-dimensional unit
sphere
\mathbb{S}^{k}
. Here
\mathcal{L}_{k}(N)
is the space of
k
-dimensional totally geodesic subspaces
L_{k}
in
N
and
dL_{k}(N)
is the natural (invariant) measure on
\mathcal{L}_{k}(N)

2.4.
Convexity and Normal Cones

Here we review the definitions and facts about the normal cone and singular (regular) points, together with basics about curvature measures used in convex geometry. One may refer to
[
25
]
for definitions and facts in Euclidean spaces. The counterparts in space forms are similar.

A
supporting hyperplane

P
of a closed set
X
is a totally geodesic hypersurface in
N
such that
X
is entirely contained in one of the two closed half-spaces bounded by the hyperplane
P
, and
P\cap\partial X\neq\emptyset
. We say that
P
is a supporting hyperplane of
X
at
p
, if
P
is supporting hyperplane of
X
, and
p\in P\cap\partial X
. If the ambient manifold
N
has dimension
\dim(N)=3
, then we call the supporting hyperplane a
supporting plane
.

If
K
is a convex body, and
p\in\partial K
, then clearly there exists a supporting hyperplane
P
of
K
at
p
. The supporting hyperplane at a point
p
may not be unique. The set of all supporting hyperplanes at
p\in\partial K
with respect to the convex body
K
is denoted by
\operatorname{Supp}(p,K)
.

Conversely, if
K
is a closed set with non-empty interior such that every point
p
on the boundary has a supporting hyperplane
P
, then
K
is a convex body and
K
is the intersection of all its supporting closed half-spaces (namely the half-space bounded by
P=P(p)
that contains K).

X
is a strictly convex set if every supporting hyperplane of
X
intersects
X
at only a single point.

In the following context of this subsection, we take the ambient manifold
N
to be the hyperbolic 3-space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.

If
\operatorname{Supp}(p,K)
consists of only one element (one plane), we say
p
is a
regular
point of the convex surface
\partial K
. In this case, there exists a unique supporting plane of
p\in\partial K
with respect to
K
. Otherwise, we say
p
is a
singular
point of
\partial K
. See
[
25
, Chapter 2.2]
.
If
p
is singular, let
r=r(p)
be the maximum number of linearly independent supporting planes at
p
with respect to
K
. Since
\dim(T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{3})=3
, we know
r=2
or
r=3
. We call
p
a
(3-r)
-singular point
.

We denote the set of regular points on
\partial K
by
\operatorname{Reg}(\partial K)
. For
s=0,1
, we denote the set of
s
-singular points on
\partial K
by
\operatorname{Sing}^{s}(\partial K)
.

If
K
is a convex body in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, for any
p\in\partial K
and any supporting plane
P\in\operatorname{Supp}(p,K)
,
P
can be determined by its non-zero normal vector
\nu(p,H)\in T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{3}
, that is,
\nu(p,H)
is the unique unit vector up to orientation such that

\displaystyle P=\left\{\exp_{p}(v):v\in T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{3},\bar{g}\left(v,\nu(p,H)\right)=0\text{ in }T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{3}\right\},

where
\bar{g}
is the Riemannian metric of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.
We take such a vector
\nu
to be outward with respect to
K
, that is,
\exp_{p}(t\nu)
lies in
\mathbb{H}^{3}\backslash K
for any
t>0
.

We define the
normal cone

N(p,K)
of
p\in\partial K
with respect to the convex body
K
as the set of outer normal vectors (not necessarily unit vectors) of all supporting planes of
p
with respect to
K
. That is,

(2.17)

\displaystyle N(p,K)=\{O\}\cup\left\{\lambda\nu(p,H):\lambda>0,H\in\operatorname{Supp}(p,K)\right\}\subset T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{3}.

Note that the vectors in a normal cone are not necessarily unit vectors. We denote the set of unit normal vectors at a point
p\in\partial K
by
\mathcal{N}(p,K)
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(p,K):=\left\{\nu\in N(p,K):\|\nu\|=1\right\}.

The unit normal bundle
\mathcal{N}(K)
is defined by
\mathcal{N}(K):=\bigcup_{p\in\partial K}\mathcal{N}(p,K)
.
In this paper, we will also use the notation
\operatorname{relint}\mathcal{N}(p,K)
, which means the relative interior of
\mathcal{N}(p,K)
in its affine hull in
\{\nu\in T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{3}:\|\nu\|=1\}
, which is isometric to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Claim
.

N(p,K)
is a convex cone in
(T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{3},\bar{g})
, where
\bar{g}
denotes the Riemannian metric in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.

Proof of the Claim.

It suffices to prove that for any non-zero vectors
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in N(p,K)
, and any
\lambda\in(0,1)
,
\lambda\nu_{1}+(1-\lambda)\nu_{2}\in N(p,K)
. If
\lambda\nu_{1}+(1-\lambda)\nu_{2}=0
, the claim follows directly. Hence we assume
\lambda\nu_{1}+(1-\lambda)\nu_{2}\neq 0
in the following proof.

Clearly if
H
is a supporting plane of
K
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}=(B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1),g^{BK})
, then after being mapped into
B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
as in (
2.3
),
\operatorname{Id}(H)
is a supporting plane of
\operatorname{Id}(K)
in
(B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1),g^{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
, where we choose the Beltrami-Klein model so that
\operatorname{Id}(p)
is the origin in
(B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1),g^{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
. Moreover,
\operatorname{Id}(K)
is also a convex body in
(B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1),g^{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
.

By definition, there exist two totally geodesic planes
H_{1},H_{2}
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
such that
\nu_{1}=\nu(p,H_{1}),\nu_{2}=\nu(p,H_{2})
. Consider
\operatorname{Id}(H_{1})
and
\operatorname{Id}(H_{2})
as planes in
(B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1),g^{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
passing through the origin.
Consider
\overline{H}_{3}=\lambda\operatorname{Id}(H_{1})+(1-\lambda)\operatorname{Id}(H_{2})
. Since
\operatorname{Id}(K)
is convex, and both

\operatorname{Id}(H_{1})
and
\operatorname{Id}(H_{2})
support
\operatorname{Id}(K)
,
\overline{H}_{3}
is also a supporting plane of
\operatorname{Id}(K)
, hence
H_{3}:=\operatorname{Id}^{-1}(\overline{H_{3}})
is a supporting plane of
K
.

Since
\nu(p,H_{3})=\frac{1}{\|\lambda\nu_{1}+(1-\lambda)\nu_{2}\|}\left(\lambda\nu_{1}+(1-\lambda)\nu_{2}\right)
, we have
\lambda\nu_{1}+(1-\lambda)\nu_{2}\in N(p,K)
.
∎

If
p
is regular, then
N(p,K)
is
1
-dimensional. In this case, there exists a unique unit outer normal vector in
T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{3}
at
p
. Otherwise, if
p
is singular, then
N(p,K)
is at least
2
-dimensional.
Since
\dim(T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{3})=3
, we know if
p
is singular, then
\dim N(p,K)=2
or
\dim N(p,K)=3
. If
p
is a
0
-singular point, then
\dim N(p,K)=3
. If
p
is a
1
-singular point, then
\dim N(p,K)=2
.

3.
Proof of Proposition
1.1
: Minimizers under special conditions

In this section, we will first prove the existence of a minimizer in Santaló’s problem. Then we will prove Proposition
1.1
, that is, the classification of minimizers in two cases: degenerate case and
C^{2}
strictly convex case. The proofs of Proposition
1.1
follow from Guan
[
14
]
.

3.1.
Existence of a minimizer

For any fixed real number
S_{0}\in(0,\infty)
, consider the set of convex bodies defined by
\mathcal{K}^{3}(S_{0})=\left\{K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}(\mathbb{H}^{3}):\partial K\text{ has surface area }S_{0}\right\}
, where
\mathcal{K}^{3}(\mathbb{H}^{3})
denotes the set of all convex bodies in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.
By Ghomi-Spruck
[
10
]
, for any convex surface
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, we have

M(\Gamma)\geq\sqrt{16\pi S(\Gamma)+2S(\Gamma)^{2}},

hence we know for any
K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}(S_{0})
,

M(\partial K)\geq\sqrt{16\pi S_{0}+2S_{0}^{2}},

thus

\inf\left\{M(\partial K):K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}(S_{0})\right\}\geq\sqrt{16\pi S_{0}+2S_{0}^{2}}>0.

We denote
M_{0}:=\inf\left\{M(\partial K):K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}(S_{0})\right\}
.
Consider a sequence of convex bodies
K_{n}
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}M(\partial K_{n})=M_{0}
. We denote
\Gamma_{n}:=\partial K_{n}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
M(\Gamma_{n})\leq M_{0}+1
for all
n
.

By the inequality relating diameter and total mean curvature in hyperbolic space
[
30
]
, there exists a constant
C
such that for any convex closed connected embedded surface
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
,

\operatorname{diam}(\Gamma)\leq C\int_{\Gamma}Hd\mu,

where
\operatorname{diam}(\cdot)
denotes the diameter of a set in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.
Hence for all
n
, since
\partial K_{n}
are convex surfaces,

\operatorname{diam}(K_{n})=\operatorname{diam}(\Gamma_{n})\leq C\int_{\Gamma_{n}}Hd\mu=C\cdot M(\Gamma_{n})\leq C(M_{0}+1).

Therefore by applying isometric translations to these convex bodies appropriately, we may assume for all
n
,
K_{n}
is contained in the geodesic ball
B(C(M_{0}+1))
with radius
C(M_{0}+1)
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.

By Blaschke Selection Theorem in metric spaces, see for example
[
12
]
, any bounded sequence of convex bodies in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
contains a convergent subsequence with respect to the Hausdorff metric. Hence there exists a convex body
K_{\infty}
and a sequence
m_{n}\rightarrow\infty
, such that

K_{m_{n}}
tends to
K_{\infty}
with respect to Hausdorff metric.

Hence
S_{0}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}S(\partial K_{m_{n}})=S(\partial K_{\infty})
, and
M_{0}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}M(\partial K_{m_{n}})=M(\partial K_{\infty})
, that is,
K_{\infty}\in\mathcal{K}^{3}(S_{0})
, and

M(\partial K_{\infty})=\inf\left\{M(\partial K):K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}(S_{0})\right\}.

That is,
\partial K_{\infty}
is the minimizer of total mean curvature of convex surfaces with surface area
S_{0}
.

3.2.
Degenerate minimizer

Proof of Proposition
1.1
(1).

If
\Gamma
is degenerate, then by convexity,
\Gamma
is a subset of a totally geodesic submanifold isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, with its two faces counted into surface area, and its edge counted into singular mean curvature.

Denote this convex body in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
by
K_{1}
. Then we have

\displaystyle S(\Gamma)=2S(K_{1}),\quad M(\Gamma)=\pi L(\partial K_{1}),

where
S(K_{1})
and
L(\partial K_{1})
denote area of
K_{1}
and perimeter of
\partial K_{1}
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, respectively. Then Santaló’s problem in this case is equivalent to the isoperimetric problem in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. By
[
29
]
, the convex body in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
that minimizes perimeter with fixed area is the geodesic ball in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Hence
\Gamma
is the flat double disk in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.
∎

3.3.
C^{2}
strictly convex minimizer

Fix the surface area
S
. If a convex surface
\Gamma
is the minimizer of total mean curvature, and a piece of
\Gamma
is
C^{2}
and strictly convex, that is, both of its principal curvatures are positive in an open subset
U
of
\Gamma
, then we may apply the method of Lagrange multipliers to derive the Euler-Lagrange equation of the surface.
Given any normal variation of the surface
\Gamma
by a geometric flow, say
\partial_{t}\Gamma_{t}=f_{t}\nu_{t}
, with
\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma
and
f_{0}=f
, where
f
is a function on
\Gamma
compactly supported in
U
which is a relatively open subset of
\Gamma
, we have
at time
t=0
, the first variation of total mean curvature and area are given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M=\int_{U}2(G+1)fd\mu,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S=\int_{U}Hfd\mu.

One may refer to Section
3
, Subsection
2.2
for details.

Since both principal curvatures of
\Gamma
are positive in
U
, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that for any
t
with
-\epsilon<t<\epsilon
,
\Gamma_{t}
is still a
C^{2}
strictly convex surface in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.

Therefore, as a critical point of the functional
M
,
\Gamma
satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation

2(G+1)=\tilde{\lambda}H\text{ in }U,

for a fixed real number
\tilde{\lambda}
.
By denoting
\lambda=\tilde{\lambda}/2
, we have

G+1=\lambda H\text{ in }U.

By convexity,
\lambda
is positive.
If the whole surface of the minimizer
\Gamma
is
C^{2}
and strictly convex, the equation above holds globally on
\Gamma
.

Proof of Proposition
1.1
(2).

Since the minimizer
\Gamma
is
C^{2}
and strictly convex, we may derive the Euler-Lagrange equation for the entire surface from the arguments above, hence there exists a constant
\lambda>0
, such that

(3.1)

G+1=\lambda H\text{ in }\Gamma.

Integrating both sides of the equation on
\Gamma
, and applying the Gauss-Bonnet Theorem
\int_{\Gamma}Gd\mu=4\pi+S
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, we obtain

(3.2)

4\pi+2S=\lambda M.

Since
\Gamma
is the minimizer of total mean curvature, we know its total mean curvature is no more than that of a sphere with the same area, that is,

(3.3)

\displaystyle M\leq M_{1}(S):=\sqrt{16\pi S+4S^{2}},

therefore

\lambda=\frac{4\pi+2S}{M}\geq\frac{4\pi+2S}{\sqrt{16\pi S+4S^{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{16\pi^{2}+16\pi S+4S^{2}}{16\pi S+4S^{2}}}>1.

Hence we may write (
3.1
) as

\det(\kappa-\lambda I)=(\kappa_{1}-\lambda)(\kappa_{2}-\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-1>0.

Therefore,
(\kappa-\lambda I)
is either positive-definite everywhere or negative-definite everywhere.
Now we claim: Every point in
\Gamma
is umbilical. We will first prove
(\kappa-\lambda I)
is positive-definite everywhere.

Suppose
(\kappa-\lambda I)
is negative-definite everywhere, that is,
(\lambda I-\kappa)
is positive-definite everywhere. Then we have for every point in
\Gamma
,

2\lambda-H=\operatorname{tr}(\lambda I-\kappa)\geq 2\sqrt{\det(\lambda I-\kappa)}=2\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}.

Integrating over
\Gamma
, we have

2\lambda S-M\geq 2\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}S,

that is,

(3.4)

M\leq 2S(\lambda-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1})=2S\frac{1}{\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}}

From (
3.2
) we get
\lambda={(4\pi+2S)}/{M}
. Substituting this into (
3.4
), we have

\displaystyle M\leq 2S\frac{M}{4\pi+2S+\sqrt{(4\pi+2S)^{2}-M^{2}}}\leq 2S\frac{M}{4\pi+2S}.

Since
M>0
, we now get

1\leq\frac{2S}{4\pi+2S},

that is,
4\pi+2S\leq 2S
, which leads to a contradiction.
Therefore,
(\kappa-\lambda I)
cannot be negative-definite everywhere.
(\kappa-\lambda I)
is positive-definite everywhere. Now we prove
\Gamma
is umbilical everywhere.

Now we have

H-2\lambda=\operatorname{tr}(\kappa-\lambda I)\geq 2\sqrt{\det(\kappa-\lambda I)}=2\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}.

Integrate over
\Gamma
, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle M\geq 2S(\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}),

where the equality holds if and only if
\Gamma
is umbilical everywhere.
Again from (
3.2
) we get
\lambda={(4\pi+2S)}/{M}
. Substituting this into (
3.5
), we get

\displaystyle M\geq

\displaystyle 2S\left(\frac{4\pi+2S}{M}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{4\pi+2S}{M}\right)^{2}-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2S\left(\frac{4\pi+2S}{M}+\frac{1}{M}\sqrt{\left({4\pi+2S}\right)^{2}-M^{2}}\right)

Now using (
3.3
) again, we have

\displaystyle M\geq

\displaystyle 2S\left(\frac{4\pi+2S}{M}+\frac{1}{M}\sqrt{\left({4\pi+2S}\right)^{2}-(16\pi S+4S^{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2S\frac{1}{M}\left(4\pi+2S+4\pi\right),

that is,

\displaystyle M^{2}\geq 16\pi S+4S^{2},

that is,

(3.6)

\displaystyle M\geq M_{1}(S).

Combining (
3.6
) and (
3.3
), we obtain
M=M_{1}(S)
and the equality in (
3.5
) holds, hence
\Gamma
is umbilical everywhere. That is,
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=\lambda>1
everywhere. Thus
\Gamma
is a sphere in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.
∎

In this section, we will prove Theorem

One may refer to Section

We will use a local argument. The idea is that if there exists a

has strictly less total mean curvature than

\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\operatorname{Id}(O),\operatorname{Id}(q))=\tanh\left(\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{H}^{3}}(O,q))\right)
,
we have

(5.8)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}=\operatorname{Id}(\mathcal{L})=\left\{\operatorname{Plane}(\tau,\tanh(R)):\tau\in\mathbb{S}^{2},R>0,\text{ such that }\operatorname{Plane}(\tau,R)\in\mathcal{L}.\right\}.

By (
2.16
), we know

(5.9)

\displaystyle dL_{2}(\mathbb{H}^{3})=2(1+2\sinh^{2}(R))dR\wedge d\mu_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}.

Combining (
5.8
) and (
5.9
), since
\sinh(R)/R\to 1
and
\tanh(R)/R\to 1
as
R\to 0
, if
d
is small enough, there exist constants
c
and
c_{1}
such that

\displaystyle dL_{2}(\mathbb{H}^{3})(\mathcal{L})>c\cdot dL_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})(\operatorname{Id}(\mathcal{L}))>c_{1}d,

hence (
5.2
) follows, which proves (
5.1
).
∎

We now combine the two lemmas to finish the proof. By Lemma
5.1
and Lemma
5.2
, we choose any
\nu\in\mathcal{N}(p,\Omega_{1})\subset\mathcal{N}(p,\Omega)
. As
d\to 0^{+}
, there exists positive constants
c
and
C
, depending only on
\Omega,p,\nu
, such that

(5.10)

\displaystyle M(\Gamma)-M(\Gamma^{-}(\nu,d))>cd,

(5.11)

\displaystyle S(\Gamma)-S(\Gamma^{-}(\nu,d))\leq Cd^{2}.

We now apply geodesic normal flow to
\Gamma^{-}(\nu,d)
. From the proof of Proposition
2.1
, there exists a constant
T>0
such that the outer parallel surface
X_{T}(\Gamma^{-}(\nu,d))
at distance
T
has the same surface area as
\Gamma
. Denote it by
\Gamma^{\prime}
. Then
S(\Gamma^{\prime})=S(\Gamma)
.

By Proposition
2.1
we have

M(\Gamma^{\prime})^{2}-16\pi S(\Gamma^{\prime})-4S(\Gamma^{\prime})^{2}=M(\Gamma^{-}(\nu,d))^{2}-16\pi S(\Gamma^{-}(\nu,d))-4S(\Gamma^{-}(\nu,d))^{2},

Hence,

\displaystyle M(\Gamma^{\prime})^{2}-M(\Gamma)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M(\Gamma^{-}(\nu,d))^{2}-M(\Gamma)^{2}-16\pi\left[S(\Gamma^{-}(\nu,d))-S(\Gamma)\right]

\displaystyle-4\left[S(\Gamma^{-}(\nu,d))^{2}-S(\Gamma)^{2}\right].

Substituting (
5.10
) and (
5.11
), we obtain

M(\Gamma^{\prime})^{2}-M(\Gamma)^{2}<-\left[M(\Gamma^{-}(\nu,d))+M(\Gamma)\right]cd+Cd^{2}\leq-c_{0}d+Cd^{2}

for constants
c_{0},C
depending only on
\Omega
,
p
and
\nu
.
Therefore, for
d
small enough,
S(\Gamma^{\prime})=S(\Gamma)
but
M(\Gamma^{\prime})<M(\Gamma)
, so
\Gamma
is not a minimizer, which contradicts the assumption. Hence if
\Gamma
is a convex minimizer, then
\Gamma
has no
0
-singular point.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

Open Questions.

Some further study directions to Santaló’s problem will be listed here. Clearly, the question of greatest interest is:

Question 5.5
.

What is the shape of a minimizer to Santaló’s problem, that is, of a convex surface in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
that minimizes total mean curvature among convex surfaces with fixed area?

The explicit form of the minimizer may be difficult to find; however, we may ask the following related questions to understand its geometry:

Question 5.6
.

Let
\Gamma
be a minimizer to Santaló’s problem then

•

Is
\Gamma
strictly convex?

•

Is
\Gamma
regular? (Here, regular means that at every point the supporting plane is unique.)

•

If
\Gamma
is regular, is it
C^{2}
, or even
C^{\infty}
?

•

Is
\Gamma
axially symmetric?

By the arguments in Section
4
, one can prove that the minimizing surface
\Gamma
to Santaló’s problem with area
S
is contained in a geodesic ball
B(g(S))
for some monotonically increasing function
g
such that
\lim_{S\to 0^{+}}g(S)=0
. Hence when
S
is small enough,
\Gamma
also lies in a small neighborhood of a point
p
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, and, as
S\to 0^{+}
, its geometric quantities can be arbitrarily close to its Euclidean image in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, under the Beltrami-Klein identity map
\operatorname{Id}
defined in (
2.3
) (where we take the origin to be
p
). Thus we may speculate when the area
S
is small enough, the minimizer is close to a geodesic sphere, which is the minimizer to Minkowski Inequality (
1.2
) in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

When
S
is large enough, some mechanism we do not fully understand yet causes the flat double disks, whose mean curvature is concentrated entirely on the "edge", to have lower total mean curvature than spheres with the same area, and to become candidates for minimizers to Santaló’s problem. This phenomenon is possibly connected with the exponential growth of geometric quantities in hyperbolic spaces. As
r\to\infty
, for a geodesic sphere in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, its area and total mean curvature both grow at the rate of
e^{2r}
; in contrast, for a geodesic sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, its area grows at the rate of
r^{2}
, and total mean curvature at the rate of
r
. This difference between hyperbolic and Euclidean geometry makes Santaló’s problem more complicated. In view of the family of convex surfaces constructed in Section
4
, when the area
S
is large, one may speculate that the minimizer has singular points, is therefore non-smooth, and is close to a flat double disk.

Question 5.7
.

Let
\Gamma(S)
be a minimizer to Santaló’s problem with surface area
S
. Then

•

If
S\to 0
, will
\Gamma(S)
, after proper rescaling, converge to a sphere?

•

If
S\to\infty
, will
\Gamma(S)
, after proper rescaling,
converge to a flat double disk?

Appendix: Computation of
\Phi_{1}(\Omega(r,\alpha),\Gamma_{3}(r,\alpha))

In this appendix, we will recall the notion of curvature measures in hyperbolic spaces, and give a detailed computation of
\Phi_{1}(\Omega(r,\alpha),\Gamma_{3}(r,\alpha))
defined in Section
4
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n+1})
be the set of compact convex sets in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
with non-empty interior. For any
K\in\mathcal{K}_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{n+1})
, and

\epsilon>0
, we define the set

\displaystyle K_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}:\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{H}^{n+1}}(x,K)\leq\epsilon\}.

The map
f_{K}:\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\backslash K\rightarrow\partial K
is defined by

(5.12)

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{H}^{n+1}}(f_{K}(x),x)=\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{H}^{n+1}}(x,K),

and is well-defined because
K
is convex. We call
f_{K}
the
metric projection
onto
K
.
For
\beta\subset\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, we also define

\displaystyle M_{\epsilon}(K,\beta):=f_{K}^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap(K_{\epsilon}\backslash K).

Following Kohlmann
[
18
]
, Allendoerfer
[
1
]
, define a Radon measure
\mu_{\epsilon}
on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of hyperbolic space

\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{H}^{n+1})
by

\displaystyle\mu_{\epsilon}(K,\beta)=\operatorname{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}^{n+1}}(M_{\epsilon}(K,\beta)),

Set
l_{n+1-r}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\sinh^{n-r}(x)\cosh^{r}(x)dx,r=0,\cdots,n
. Then the following Steiner-type formula holds. See
[
1
]
,
[
18
]
.

(5.13)

\mu_{\epsilon}(K,\beta)=\sum_{r=0}^{n}l_{n+1-r}(\epsilon)\Phi_{r}(K,\beta),\qquad\forall\beta\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{H}^{n+1}).

When
\eta=\partial K\cap\beta
is a
C^{3}
surface,
\Phi_{r}(K,\cdot)
has the following simple expression:

\Phi_{r}(K,\beta)=\int_{\eta}\sigma_{n-r}^{K}(\kappa)d\mu_{g},

where
\mu_{g}
is the surface area measure on
\partial K
induced by
\operatorname{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}^{n+1}}
.
\sigma_{n-r}^{K}

is the
(n-r)
-th elementary symmetric function of the principal curvatures of
\partial K
.
Generally,
\Phi_{n-r}(K,\cdot)
is called the
r
-th curvature measure of the convex body
K
. In particular,
\Phi_{0}(K,\cdot)
is called
Gaussian curvature measure
, and
\Phi_{n-1}(K,\cdot)
is called
mean curvature measure
.
For any convex surface
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
with enclosed convex body
\Omega=\operatorname{conv}(\Gamma)
, the total mean curvature of
\Gamma
is given by
M(\Gamma)=\Phi_{n-1}(\Omega,\mathbb{H}^{n+1})=\Phi_{n-1}(\Omega,\Gamma)
.

For the convex surface
\Gamma(r,\alpha)\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
defined in Section
4
, we will now use (
5.13
) to compute
\Phi_{1}(\Omega(r,\alpha),\Gamma_{3}(r,\alpha))
. Since
\Gamma_{3}(r,\alpha)
consists of two identical "circular edges", we define

\Gamma_{3}^{+}(r,\alpha):=\left\{x\in\Gamma_{3}(r,\alpha):\operatorname{Id}(x)\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1)\cap\{x_{3}>0\}\right\},

where
\operatorname{Id}
is the Beltrami-Klein identity map defined in (
2.3
). Then it suffices to compute
\Phi_{1}(\Omega(r,\alpha),\Gamma_{3}^{+}(r,\alpha))
.

To simplify the computation, we will use polar coordinates to model
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. That is, we identify
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with
(U,\bar{g})
by the coordinate isometry
\operatorname{Pol}:\mathbb{H}^{3}\to(U,\bar{g})
, where

\displaystyle U:=\left\{(\rho,\theta,\phi):\rho\geq 0,0\leq\theta\leq\pi,0\leq\phi<2\pi\right\},

endowed with metric

\displaystyle\bar{g}=d\rho^{2}+\sinh^{2}(\rho)d\theta^{2}+\sinh^{2}(\rho)\sin^{2}(\theta)d\phi^{2}.

\bar{g}
is well-defined in the subset of
U
where
\rho>0
and
0<\theta<\pi
, but can be naturally extended to all of
U
. Note that we will choose the origin such that the origin in polar coordinates
\operatorname{Pol}^{-1}\{\rho=0\}=A=\operatorname{Id}^{-1}(0,0,\hat{r}\cos(\alpha))
.

Then we can place
\Gamma(r,\alpha)
such that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{3}^{+}(r,\alpha)=\operatorname{Pol}^{-1}\left\{(\rho,\theta,\phi)\in U:\rho=r_{1},\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\right\},

and

\displaystyle M_{\epsilon}(\Omega(r,\alpha),\Gamma_{3}^{+}(r,\alpha))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Pol}^{-1}\{

\displaystyle(\rho,\theta,\phi)\in U:\text{There exists }0\leq\gamma\leq\frac{\pi}{2}-\beta,0<t<\epsilon,\text{ such that }

\displaystyle\frac{\sinh(t)}{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta)}=\frac{\sinh(\rho)}{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}+\gamma)},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\cosh(\rho)=\cosh(r_{1})\cosh(t)-\sinh(r_{1})\sinh(t)\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}+\gamma)\}

by the hyperbolic law of cosines and sines.
Set

N_{\epsilon}(r,\alpha):=\left\{x\in M_{\epsilon}(\Omega(r,\alpha),\Gamma_{3}(r,\alpha)):\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{H}^{3}}(x,\Gamma_{3}^{+}(r,\alpha))=\epsilon\right\}.

Then for any
\epsilon>0
,
N_{\epsilon}(r,\alpha)
is a
2
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\epsilon}(\Omega(r,\alpha),\Gamma_{3}^{+}(r,\alpha))=\operatorname{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}^{3}}M_{\epsilon}(\Omega(r,\alpha),\Gamma_{3}^{+}(r,\alpha))=\int_{0}^{\epsilon}S(N_{t}(r,\alpha))dt.

Clearly, for any
t>0
,
N_{t}(r,\alpha)
is also given by

(5.14)

\displaystyle N_{t}(r,\alpha)=\operatorname{Pol}^{-1}\{

\displaystyle(\rho,\theta,\phi)\in U:\text{There exists }0\leq\gamma\leq\frac{\pi}{2}-\beta,\text{ such that }

\displaystyle\frac{\sinh(t)}{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta)}=\frac{\sinh(\rho)}{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}+\gamma)},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\cosh(\rho)=\cosh(r_{1})\cosh(t)-\sinh(r_{1})\sinh(t)\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}+\gamma)\}

We may express
\rho
and
\theta
implicitly as functions of
\gamma
, and use them to parametrize
N_{t}(r,\alpha)
, namely

(5.15)

\displaystyle N_{t}(r,\alpha)=\operatorname{Pol}^{-1}\{

\displaystyle(\rho_{t}(\gamma),\theta_{t}(\gamma),\phi):0\leq\gamma\leq\frac{\pi}{2}-\beta,0\leq\phi<2\pi\},

where the functions
\rho=\rho_{t}(\gamma)
and
\theta=\theta_{t}(\gamma)
are determined by the defining conditions in (
5.14
).
By (
5.15
), the area element of
N_{t}(r,\alpha)
is given by

(5.16)

\displaystyle d\mu_{N_{t}(r,\alpha)}=\sinh(\rho_{t}(\gamma))\sin(\theta_{t}(\gamma))\sqrt{(\rho_{t}^{\prime}(\gamma))^{2}+\sinh^{2}(\rho_{t}(\gamma))(\theta_{t}^{\prime}(\gamma))^{2}}d\gamma d\phi.

Differentiating the two equations in (
5.14
), we have

(5.17)

\displaystyle\sinh(\rho_{t}(\gamma))\rho_{t}^{\prime}(\gamma)=\sinh(r_{1})\sinh(t)\cos(\gamma)

and

(5.18)

\displaystyle\cosh(\rho_{t}(\gamma))\rho_{t}^{\prime}(\gamma)\cos(\theta_{t}(\gamma))-\sinh(\rho_{t}(\gamma))\sin(\theta_{t}(\gamma))\theta_{t}^{\prime}(\gamma)=\sinh(t)\sin(\gamma).

Substituting (
5.18
) and (
5.17
) into (
5.16
) and using (
5.14
), we have

\displaystyle d\mu_{N_{t}(r,\alpha)}=\sinh t\left(\sinh r_{1}\cosh t+\cosh r_{1}\sinh t\sin\gamma\right)d\gamma d\phi

and hence

\displaystyle S(N_{t}(r,\alpha))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}-\beta}d\gamma\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\sinh t\left(\sinh r_{1}\cosh t+\cosh r_{1}\sinh t\sin\gamma\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\sinh(r_{1})\sinh(t)\cosh(t)(\frac{\pi}{2}-\beta)+2\pi\cosh(r_{1})\sinh^{2}(t)(1-\sin(\beta)).

Hence

\displaystyle M_{\epsilon}(\Omega(r,\alpha),\Gamma_{3}(r,\alpha))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2M_{\epsilon}(\Omega(r,\alpha),\Gamma_{3}^{+}(r,\alpha))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\pi\sinh(r_{1})(\frac{\pi}{2}-\beta)\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\sinh(t)\cosh(t)dt

\displaystyle+4\pi\cosh(r_{1})(1-\sin(\beta))\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\sinh(t)^{2}dt.

Comparing with (
5.13
), we obtain
\Phi_{1}(\Omega(r,\alpha),\Gamma_{3}(r,\alpha))=4\pi\sinh(r_{1})(\frac{\pi}{2}-\beta)
.
