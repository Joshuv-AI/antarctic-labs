Abstract
We report a detailed analysis on the young stellar cluster Berkeley 59 using Fermi -LAT. Using up-to-date source catalog and background models, we found significant extended GeV emission around Berkeley 59, which can be modeled by a radial disk of 1.02 ∘ 1.02^{\circ} radius with a significance of the extension of 10.6 σ \sigma . We investigated the molecular, neutral and ionized gas content and the hadronic origin. The gamma-ray spectrum of Berkeley 59 has a photon index of 2.88 ± 0.06 2.88\pm 0.06 . The derived gas mass from H 2 and HII around Berkeley 59 is ∼ 2.89 × 10 2 ​ M ⊙ \sim 2.89\times 10^{2}M_{\odot} . We derived the relationship between cosmic ray acceleration efficiency and diffusion coefficient. Our results suggest that the extended gamma-ray emission originates from cosmic rays accelerated by cluster winds interacting with surrounding gas.

Introduction

The origin of galactic cosmic rays (CRs) remains unknown. For a long time, it has been believed that the acceleration sites of Galactic CRs are supernova remnants (SNRs)
(
12
;
24
;
49
;
17
)
. Diffusive shock acceleration is considered as principal CR production mechanism for SNRs
(
10
;
35
)
. However, growing evidence suggests that stellar clusters are also the excellent energy sources of CRs in the Galaxy
(
3
;
36
;
7
)
. Most massive stars form in associations and/or clusters, which serve as important accelerators of very-high-energy (VHE) and ultra-high-energy (UHE) CRs
(
21
;
28
)
. The accelerated CRs interacting with the surrounding ambient gas may produce the observed
\gamma
-ray emissions. Nevertheless, only a few stellar clusters have been identified as VHE or UHE
\gamma
-ray sources, such as Westerlund 1
(
2
)
, Westerlund 2
(
56
)
and NGC 3603
(
55
)
. Stellar clusters hosting massive stars may experience numerous supernova explosions that can act as VHE CR accelerators
(
46
;
45
;
9
)
. A substantial fraction of CRs should be accelerated in young massive stellar clusters containing lots of OB stars and the superbubbles created by these clusters
(
1
;
33
)
. Superbubbles possess sufficient energy originating from stellar winds and supernova explosions
(
34
;
48
)
. Recent advancements in multi-wavelength observations and simulations have revealed that stellar clusters, especially their stellar wind-driven superbubbles, may contribute significantly to the CR budget
(
61
;
22
)
. Observations of stellar clusters allow us to constrain the fraction of stellar wind energy transferred into
\gamma
-ray .

Stellar clusters serve as unique laboratories for understanding the properties of the interstellar medium (ISM) and the nature of interactions between young stars and the ISM
(
13
;
4
;
41
)
. As such, they provide an alternative testing ground for CR origin theories, potentially helping to resolve discrepancies in the SNR-based paradigm, particularly concerning the maximum particle energy. Studies of the Cygnus cocoon and Westerlund 1 highlight the challenges in distinguishing between leptonic and hadronic origins of high-energy emissions
(
51
;
7
)
. The commonly invoked 1/r radial profile, often derived from diffusion models that omit key physical processes such as CR advection, distributed acceleration sites, and radiative losses, may not accurately represent the true CR spatial distribution
(
56
;
58
)
. Alternative scenarios involving CR injection at wind termination shocks or supernova events have been shown to reproduce the 1/r profile equivalently. Despite these degeneracies, the increasing number of
\gamma
-ray detections coincident with stellar clusters and the emerging spectral and morphological characterizations of these sources provide circumstantial evidence supporting a significant contribution to the high-energy CR population, although this remains to be conclusively validated
(
19
;
20
;
42
)
.

CRs are believed to be important regulators of the star-formation process
(
50
;
32
)
. As they travel through the ISM, CRs interact with the ambient gas and radiation fields to produce
\gamma
-ray  which trace the CR population. Therefore,
\gamma
-ray emissions are among the best tools for studying CR properties in star-forming environments
(
29
)
. By analyzing
\gamma
-ray emission in these systems, we can measure the fraction of interacting high-energy particles as a function of the star formation rate. Efficient particle acceleration makes star-forming regions significant
\gamma
-ray sources. Star-forming regions, such as G25.0+0.0
(
26
)
, W40
(
47
)
, Masgomas-6a
(
53
)
, Carina Nebula Complex
(
19
)
, G305
(
31
)
and W3
(
54
)
, have been observed by
Fermi
-LAT to exhibit diffuse
\gamma
-ray emissions.

CRs may affect the structure of molecular clouds and star formation
(
15
;
59
)
. Massive stars undergo supernova explosions at the end of their lives, enriching the ISM with heavy elements. SNRs can ionize the surrounding medium and contribute a significant pressure component to the ISM which amplifies magnetic fields
(
37
;
44
)
. Consequently, they can either suppress or enhance star formation in molecular clouds and their host galaxies
(
38
)
. CRs escape from their acceleration sites, and low-energy CRs are especially dominant sources of ionization in the Galaxy. They penetrate deeply into the dense cores of molecular clouds
(
57
)
, where they initiate complex chemistry and influence the star formation process. Increasing evidence also suggests that CRs are dynamically important in galaxy formation and must be considered in simulations.

Berkeley 59 (R.A.=0.570
∘
, Dec.=67.441
∘
) is a young (
\sim
2 Myr) and nearby (
\sim
1 kpc) stellar cluster. It is located in the central region of the OB association Cepheus OB4 surrounded by an H II region
(
27
;
14
)
. The younger pre-main-sequence (PMS) stars tend to concentrate closer to the inner region than to the outer region of the cluster, which is linked to the time evolution of star-forming clouds

\sim

\sim

\sim 10^{3}M_{\odot}
, which is similar to the well-studied young massive stellar clusters (YMSCs) Westerlund 1 and Westerlund 2. Therefore, it serves as a typical yet unique laboratory for studying CR acceleration in young stellar clusters due to its proximity, youth, and multiwavelength detectability.

2.2
Spectral Analysis

The spectral shape aids us to perform a judgment on the nature of
\gamma
-ray sources. To validate the spectral model, we test for spectral curvature that reveals deviations from a power law (PL) spectrum for each source via likelihood ratio test. For the LogParabola (LP) model, it provides: TS
LP
=
-2\left(\mathrm{ln}\left(L_{\rm PL}\right)-\mathrm{ln}\left(L_{\rm LP}\right)\right)
. The PL model is given by:

\frac{dN}{dE}=N_{0}\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)^{-\gamma}

(2)

where
N_{0}
,
E_{0}
and
\gamma
are normalization, scale and photon index, respectively.

The LP model in
Fermi
-LAT data analysis is defined by:

\frac{dN}{dE}=N_{0}\left(\frac{E}{E_{b}}\right)^{-(\alpha+\beta\ln(E/E_{b}))}

(3)

where
N_{0}
is the normalization,
\alpha
is the photon index,
\beta
is the curvature index, and
E_{b}
is the reference energy. Similarly, for PLSuperExpCutoff (PLSC) model, the curvature check gives:
TS_{\rm PLSC}=-2\left(\mathrm{ln}\left(L_{\rm PL}\right)-\mathrm{ln}\left(L_{\rm PLSC}\right)\right)

Here, the PLSC model is defined by:

\frac{dN}{dE}=N_{0}\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)^{\gamma_{0}-\frac{d}{2}\ln\frac{E}{E_{0}}-\frac{db}{6}\ln^{2}\frac{E}{E_{0}}-\frac{db^{2}}{24}\ln^{3}\frac{E}{E_{0}}}

(4)

If either TS
LP

4
Cosmic-ray Injection

The hadronic origin has been discussed in for several YSMCs
(
56
;
47
;
30
;
19
;
31
)
. Such
\gamma
-ray emission may originate from the decay of neutral pions produced by the interactions between the accelerated hadrons and the surrounding gas. Therefore, we explore the scenario in which the
\gamma
-ray emission is produced via the pion-decay process from the interaction of the CRs with the ambient gas. As provided by Section
3
, the average number density of the target protons for this region is
99.41\ \rm cm^{-3}
.

If Berkeley 59 can accelerate protons to high energies, then these protons could illuminate the molecular clouds around them via proton–proton interaction. We assume an exponential cutoff power-law spectrum for the parent proton distribution:
f_{\rm p}(E)=A_{\rm p}(E/E_{0})^{-\alpha_{\rm p}}\exp(-(E/E_{\rm c})
. We use
NAIMA
package to fit the SED
(
60
)
. Considering the distance of about 1 kpc, the best-fit proton index
\alpha_{\rm p}=2.91\pm 0.05
and the total proton energy
W_{\rm p}=1.51\times 10^{49}\ \rm erg
above 1 GeV are obtained. The model with best-fit parameters are shown in Figure
4
.

We also tested the potential leptonic scenario of Berkeley 59. It reflects the
\gamma
-ray generated via the inverse Compton (IC) scattering of electrons off the seed photons around the source. We considered the CMB radiation field, optical to UV radiation field from the star light as photon field, and the dust infrared radiation field
25
. In addition, nonthermal bremsstrahlung radiation resulting from the interaction between relativistic particles and thermal particle population may contribute
\gamma
-ray emissions
(
6
)
. Accordingly, we calculated the IC and bremsstrahlung spectrum using
NAIMA
as we did for hadronic scenario. We assumed a exponential cutoff power-law distribution of the relativistic electrons:
f_{\rm e}(E)=A_{\rm e}(E/E_{0})^{-\alpha_{\rm e}}\exp(-(E/E_{\rm c})
. The best-fit electron index of electron distribution is
\alpha_{\rm e}=2.19\pm 0.04
. We obtain the total energy above 1 GeV:
W_{\rm e}=2.24\times 10^{49}\ \rm erg
. We emphasize that the leptonic origin of Berkeley 59 can not be ruled out.

The total kinetic energy injection rate from the wind of the whole stellar cluster is defined by:

L_{\rm w}=(1/2)\sum^{N_{\rm OB}}_{i}\dot{M}_{\rm w}v_{\rm w,~i}^{2}

(8)

where
N_{\rm OB}
is number of OB stars,
\dot{M}_{\rm w}
is the mass-loss rate, and
v_{\rm w,~i}
is the wind velocity of each OB star. We assume
{\dot{M}_{\rm w}}=10^{-4}\ M_{\odot}\ \rm yr^{-1}
and
v_{\rm w}=1000\ \rm km\ s^{-1}
. Then we can derive the kinetic energy injection rate
L_{\rm w}=9.62\times 10^{35}\ \rm erg\ s^{-1}
. Berkeley 59 has a total stellar mass of
1.0\times 10^{3}\ M_{\odot}

(
40
)
. The energy injection rate into CRs by stellar winds is given by

\dot{E}_{\rm CR}=\eta_{\rm CR}L_{\rm w}

(9)

where
\eta_{\rm CR}
reflects the fraction of the wind kinetic energy which goes into accelerating CR protons. Here, the
\eta_{\rm CR}
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\eta_{\rm CR}\,

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\,0.4\left(\frac{10^{3}\,{\rm cm^{-3}}}{n_{\rm eff}}\right)\left(\frac{7\,{\rm pc}}{R}\right)^{2}

(10)

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{D}{10^{28}\,{\rm cm^{2}\,\,s^{-1}}}\right)\left(\frac{3L_{\gamma}/L_{\rm w}}{0.01}\right)

where
n_{\rm eff}
is the effective number density,
D
is the diffusion coefficient. Then, the diffusion coefficient
D
can be written as
(
40
)
:

\displaystyle D\,

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\,2.5\times 10^{27}\,{\rm cm^{2}\,\,s^{-1}}\left(\frac{\eta_{\rm CR}}{0.1}\right)\left(\frac{n_{\rm eff}}{10^{3}\,{\rm cm^{-3}}}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{R}{7\,{\rm pc}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{0.01}{3L_{\gamma}/L_{\rm w}}\right)

Assuming a spherical volume with radius
R=17.5\ \rm pc
and total mass of
5.4\times 10^{4}M_{\odot}
, one can obtain estimate
n_{\rm eff}\approx 19\ \rm cm^{-3}
. We use the derived
L_{\gamma}=1.15\times 10^{33}\ \rm erg\ s^{-1}
and
L_{\rm w}=9.62\times 10^{35}\ \rm erg\ s^{-1}
. For
\eta_{\rm CR}
, we assume
\eta_{\rm CR}=0.1
for typical value of supernova shock and
\eta_{\rm CR}=0.4
for high acceleration efficiency, following
52
and
39
. Notice that such assumption has uncertainty, which need to be tested in future observations. The estimated
D
should be
8.3\times 10^{26}\ \rm cm^{2}\ s^{-1}
and
3.3\times 10^{27}\ \rm cm^{2}\ s^{-1}
, respectively. Thus, for the diffusion radius
R_{\rm diff}=\sqrt{D\tau}
, we got 148 pc <
R_{\rm diff}
< 257 pc.

The estimation of the CR injection efficiency and the diffusion coefficient in Berkeley 59 indicates that the stellar winds from the OB stars can provide the necessary energy for CR acceleration. The derived diffusion coefficient is on the order of
10^{27}\ \rm cm^{2}\ s^{-1}
, which is slower than the Galactic average, suggesting that the CRs are confined better in the cluster environment. This enhanced confinement could be due to the turbulent magnetic fields in the cluster, which facilitates the acceleration of CRs to high energies. However, the uncertainties in the effective gas density and the acceleration efficiency require further observations to refine.

Considering the kinetic energy injection rate
L_{\rm w}=9.62\times 10^{35}\ \rm erg\ s^{-1}
and the age should be
\tau\approx 2\ \rm Myr
for Berkeley 59, the total energy injected is obtained as
6.07\times 10^{49}\ \rm erg
. Thus, this YSMC is powerful enough to accelerate CRs to account for the
\gamma
-ray emissions detected by
Fermi
-LAT .

5
Discussion and Conclusions

The estimated CR acceleration efficiency of
\eta_{\rm CR}=0.1-0.4
and diffusion coefficient
D\sim 2.5\times 10^{27}\ \rm cm^{2}\ s^{-1}
place Berkeley 59 in the context of other
\gamma
-ray emitting stellar clusters. This diffusion coefficient is significantly lower than the Galactic average (
\sim 10^{28}\ \rm cm^{2}\ s^{-1}
), suggesting enhanced CR confinement within the cluster environment, possibly due to turbulent magnetic fields amplified by stellar winds and supernova activity. Such conditions are conducive to efficient particle acceleration and
\gamma
-ray production.

We report the extended
\gamma
-ray emissions around the stellar cluster Berkeley 59 using 16 yr of
Fermi
-LAT data. Insights into the role of stellar clusters in the acceleration and propagation of CRs within our Galaxy is provided. The
\gamma
-ray source 4FGL J0002.1
+
6721 is potentially associated with Berkeley 59. The extended
\gamma
-ray emissions has an angular extension of
1.02^{\circ}
which can be modeled by a radial disk profile.

The total mass of the molecular cloud and HII region was found to be approximately
2.89\times 10^{2}\ M_{\odot}
, with an average target proton density
99.41\ \rm cm^{-3}
. According to our
Fermi
-LAT analysis, the estimated mass of Berkeley 59 is
