Navigating the Complexity Landscape of Nominee Selection in Schulze Voting
Katarína Cechlárová, Jörg Rothe, Šimon Schierreich, Ildikó Schlotter

Abstract
We study the Possible President problem and the Necessary President problem for Schulze voting, a rule that, due to its many desirable axiomatic properties, is popular in practice. In both problems, we are given an election with the candidates partitioned into a set of parties , and we are interested in questions about a given distinguished party . In the Possible President problem, we ask whether it is possible for the parties to each nominate exactly one candidate such that the nominee of the distinguished party is a Schulze winner of the resulting election with only the nominees running. In the Necessary President problem, we ask whether the distinguished party’s nominee is a Schulze winner of the resulting election, irrespective of the nomination from the other parties. Rothe and Woitaschik [ 29 ] have shown that Possible President is NP-complete and Necessary President is coNP -complete for Schulze elections. We complement and improve their results by a more fine-grained analysis: we determine the parameterized complexity of both problems with respect to all possible parameterizations, where we consider each of three natural parameters—the number of voters, the maximum party size, and the number of parties—to be either a constant, a parameter, or unbounded. In particular, we obtain dichotomies regarding the number of voters for both problems.

Our Contribution

We obtain a dichotomy for both the
Possible
and the
Necessary President
problem regarding the number of voters: For two voters, both problems are solvable in polynomial (actually, even in linear) time, whereas for three or more voters, the
Possible President
problem is
NP-complete
and the
Necessary President
problem
coNP-complete
.
In fact, these results hold even if each party has at most two members, with the exception of
Necessary President
for an odd number of voters, where we were able to show
coNP-completeness
for parties with at most three candidates.
Our dichotomies reveal that both of our problems are
paraNP-hard
for the combination of two parameters—the number of voters and the maximum number of candidates in a party.
The third natural parameter we study is the number of parties, and we establish
W[1]-hardness
regarding this parameter for both problems, even for a constant number of voters.
We thus determine the parameterized complexity for all possible parameter combinations, that is, for every choice of viewing the number of voters, the maximum party size, and the number of parties as (a) a constant, (b) a parameter, or (c) unbounded, we have pinpointed the parameterized complexity of both the
Possible
and the
Necessary President
problem for the resulting parameterization.

Although restricting the number of voters to be a constant does not guarantee fixed-parameter tractability with respect to the number of parties, we show that for the special case of three voters,
Possible President
becomes fixed-parameter tractable when parameterized by the number of parties.

2.1
Schulze Voting

In social choice theory, an
election
is a pair
\mathcal{E}=(C,V)
, where
C
is a set of candidates and
V
a list of votes over
C
, often expressed as a linear order
\succ_{v}
over
C
for each
v\in V
.
When specifying a vote
v
’s preferences in an election, we usually omit the symbol
\succ_{v}
.
For example, if
C=\{a,b,c\}
is a candidate set and
b
is preferred to
a
and
a
to
c
in
v
, we write
v:b\ a\ c
instead of
v:b\succ_{v}a\succ_{v}c
.
For any subset
D\subseteq C
, when
D
occurs in some vote
v
, this means that the members of
D
occur in some arbitrary, fixed order in
v
; e.g., if
D=\{a,b\}
, then the vote
c\ D
is a shorthand for
c\ a\ b
.
Further, again assuming some fixed order of the candidates,
\overrightarrow{D}
gives the members of
D
in that order and
\overleftarrow{D}
orders them backwards; e.g.,
c\ \overrightarrow{D}
is a shorthand for
c\ a\ b
and
c\ \overleftarrow{D}
a shorthand for
c\ b\ a
.

We define the
(weighted) majority graph
G(\mathcal{E})=(C,H)
of
\mathcal{E}
as an (edge-weighted) directed graph (digraph) over
C
that contains an arc
(a,b)
if and only if more votes in
V
prefer
a
to
b
than
b
to
a
, that is,
|\{v\in V:a\succ_{v}b\}|>|\{v\in V:b\succ_{v}a\}|
, and the difference
|\{u\in V:a\succ_{v}b\}|-|\{v\in V:b\succ_{v}a\}|
is the weight
\weight(a,b)
of
(a,b)
in
G(\mathcal{E})
.
The subgraph of
G(\mathcal{E})
containing only arcs with weight exactly
k
will be denoted by
G_{k}(\mathcal{E})
.
The
strength of a path
P
in
G(\mathcal{E})
is the minimum weight of all arcs along
P
.
The
beatpath strength of
a
over
b
is the maximum strength of any path leading from
a
to
b
in
G(\mathcal{E})
, and is denoted by
\mathsf{str}(a,b)
;
if there is no path from
a
to
b
in
G(\mathcal{E})
, then we set
\mathsf{str}(a,b)=0
.
We say that a candidate
a
is a
Schulze winner of
(C,V)
if for every other candidate
b\in C\setminus\{a\}
, it holds that
\mathsf{str}(a,b)\geq\mathsf{str}(b,a)
.

We conclude with an easy observation about the weighted majority graph
G(\mathcal{E})
, which we use to simplify our proofs.

Observation 1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=(C,V)
be an election and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
be an election constructed from
\mathcal{E}
by adding two voters
v
and
\hat{v}
such that the ballot of
v
is
\overleftarrow{C}
and the ballot of
\hat{v}
is
\overrightarrow{C}
. Then
G(\mathcal{E})=G(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})
.

A consequence of the above observation is that whenever we show a hardness result for an instance with
c
candidates, the same hardness immediately generalize to
c+2i
candidates for any
i\in\mathbb{N}
. We will not stress this explicitly in the remainder of the paper and our proofs.

2.2
Possible and Necessary President

Faliszewski
et al.

[
14
]
introduced the model of
elections with nominations
, which has been studied by several researchers since then
[
25
,
7
,
31
,
30
,
17
,
29
]
.
In this model, we represent an
election with parties
by a triple
(C,V,\mathcal{P})
, where
(C,V)
is an election as defined above and
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}\}
is a partition of
C
into
k
parties, i.e.,
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}P_{i}=C
and
P_{i}\cap P_{j}=\emptyset
for all
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
, where
[n]
denotes
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

In an election with parties,
(C,V,\mathcal{P})
, each party nominates exactly one candidate among its members, i.e., a
(valid) nomination

N\subseteq C
requires that
|P_{i}\cap N|=1
for all
P_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
.
We then consider the corresponding
reduced election

(N,V)
, where all votes in
V
are restricted to contain only the nominees from
N
. Every party is represented by its nominee in
(N,V)
, i.e., the winning parties are those whose nominees win the reduced election. Moreover, the weighted majority graph of the reduced election
(N,V)
induced by
N
will be denoted by
G(\mathcal{E}(N))
, and the beatpath strength of
a
over
b
is in this graph will be denoted by
\mathsf{str}_{N}(a,b)
.

Like Faliszewski
et al.

[
14
]
, we here adopt the
nonunique-winner model
where for a nominee to win it is enough to be one winner, possibly among several others.
Note that Cechlárová
et al.

[
7
]
and Schlotter
et al.

[
31
]
adopted the
unique-winner model
that requires a unique winner of the reduced election.

We will study two
problems, defined specifically for Schulze voting: In
Schulze Possible President
, given an election
(C,V,\mathcal{P})
with parties and a distinguished party
P_{i}\in P
, we ask whether there exists a valid nomination
N\subseteq C
such that
P_{i}
’s nominee is a Schulze winner of the reduced election.

In
Schulze Necessary President
, we are given the same input, and the question now is whether
P_{i}
can nominate a candidate that is a Schulze winner of each reduced election resulting from any valid nomination of the other parties.

Depending on which problem we consider, the distinguished party’s nominee is called the
possible president
and the
necessary president
, respectively.

2.3
Directed Graphs

In this section we summarize the graph-theoretic notation we will use.

Let
G=(U,E)
be a digraph.
Given a vertex
u\in U
, we denote by
\delta^{-}_{G}(u)=\{v:(v,u)\in E\}
the set of its
in-neighbors
and by
\delta^{+}_{G}(u)=\{v:(u,v)\in E\}
the set of its
out-neighbors
.
We say that vertex
v
is

from vertex
u
in
G
if there is a directed path from
u
to
v
.
A digraph is
strongly connected
if every vertex is reachable from every other vertex. A
strongly connected component
(SCC) of
G
is a maximal strongly connected subgraph of
G
.

A digraph
G=(C,H)
is
acyclic
if it contains no directed cycle.
A digraph
G=(C,H)
is
transitive
if for any three vertices
u,v,z\in V
, we have the following: If
(u,v)\in E
and
(v,z)\in E
, then
(u,z)\in E
as well.
If a transitive digraph contains no cycles of length two (i.e., an arc
(u,v)
and its opposite
(v,u)
), then it is acyclic.

3
Two Voters

We study two problems:
We shall ask whether a candidate
p\in C
is a
winner of some reduced election (i.e., a possible president), or if
p
is a winner of all reduced elections where
p
has been nominated (i.e., a necessary president).
For simplicity, we assume that the party containing candidate
p
is a singleton.

If
\mathcal{E}
is an election with two voters, then it suffices to consider the unweighted version of the majority graph
G(\mathcal{E})
, as each of its arcs has weight equal to
2
.
Furthermore, we make the following observation.

Observation 2
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=(C,V)
be an election with
{|V|}=2
. Then
G(\mathcal{E})
is transitive.

Proof.

For two voters,
(u,v)
is an arc in
G(\mathcal{E})
if both voters prefer candidate
u
to candidate
v
.
Hence, if
(u,v)
and
(v,z)
are both present in
G(\mathcal{E})
, then both voters prefer
u
to
v
and, simultaneously, both of them prefer
v
to
z
.
Consequently, both voters prefer
u
to
z
, and the assertion follows.
∎

Next, we prove a necessary and satisfying condition for a candidate to be a Schulze winner in an election with two voters.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=(C,V)
be an election with
{|V|}=2
. Then
c\in C
is a Schulze winner of
\mathcal{E}
if and only if
\delta^{-}_{G(\mathcal{E})}(c)=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Note that
G(\mathcal{E})
contains neither loops nor directed
2
-cycles by definition.
Thus, if
G(\mathcal{E})
contains an arc
(u,c)
then, due to transitivity of
G(\mathcal{E})
, there exists no path from
c
to
u
.
Thus
\mathsf{str}(c,u)=0
while
\mathsf{str}(u,c)=2
, and so
c
is not a Schulze winner.

Conversely, assume that
G(\mathcal{E})
contains no arc
(u,c)
.
For
c
not to be a Schulze winner, there must exist some candidate
z
such that
\mathsf{str}(z,c)>\mathsf{str}(c,z)
.
Since the beatpath strength of any candidate over another is either
0
or
2
, it follows that
\mathsf{str}(z,c)=2
.
In particular, there must exist some path from
z
to
c
, contradicting our assumption.
∎

And with
2
and
Lemma

3
in hand, we can prove the main result of this section, which is a linear time algorithm for both problems we study in this work.

Theorem 4
.

Given an election
\mathcal{E}=(C,V)
with
{|V|}=2
,
both
Schulze Possible President
and
Schulze Necessary President
can be solved in linear time.

Proof.

To check whether
p
is a possible president in
\mathcal{E}
, we simply delete all candidates
u
such that
(u,p)
is an arc in
G(\mathcal{E})
, as by
Lemma

3
such candidates cannot be nominated in a reduced election where
p
is a Schulze winner.
If all parties remain nonempty after this deletion,
p
is a possible president, as all possible nominations
leave
p
without incoming arcs. By contrast,
p
is not a possible president in
\mathcal{E}
if some party becomes empty as a result of this deletion.

By
Lemma

3
, the
candidate
p
from the distinguished party is a necessary president in
\mathcal{E}
if and only if
\delta^{-}_{G(\mathcal{E})}(p)=\emptyset
:
First, if

\delta^{-}_{G(\mathcal{E})}(p)=\emptyset
, then

\delta^{-}_{G(\mathcal{E}(N))}(p)=\emptyset
is true for any set
N
of nominated candidates.
Conversely, if
(u,p)\in\delta^{-}_{G(\mathcal{E})}(p)
, then nominating
u
already guarantees that
p
is
not
a winner of the resulting reduced election (regardless of the remaining nominations), so
p
is not a necessary president in
\mathcal{E}
.
Hence, both problems can be solved in linear time.
∎

4.1.2
Parameterization by the Number of Parties

Now we turn to studying the parameterized complexity of

Observation 7

•

Proof.

We give a simple brute-force algorithm.
For each of

Therefore, there are at most

This algorithm runs in

∎

The above brute-force approach becomes quickly infeasible as

Our approach for three voters relies on the following observations. First, if the number

of

and

More specifically, for

In addition, we have a similar assertion as in

Observation 8

Let

be an election with

is transitive.

Theorem 9

Given an election

with
{|V|}=3
,

Schulze Possible President
is fixed-parameter tractable when parameterized by the number
k
of parties.

Proof.

Let

be our input instance with election

.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that

, as otherwise we can guess a candidate in

that is a possible president and delete all other candidates from

We start by constructing the weighted majority graph

If

contains an arc

with weight 3, then due to transitivity of

as established in

cannot be a winner in any reduced election whose candidate set contains

To proceed, we will rely on the following claim:

Claim 10

Suppose that no arc of weight

enters

resulting from a valid set

to every other nominee in

{claimproof}

Suppose first that

is a winner in a valid set

is a winner in

, every other nominee

is either defeated by

For the other direction it suffices to observe that if there is a path from

Let us assume that

from

We will say that a rooted directed tree

As our next step, we guess the party ideal

for

Conclusions and Future Research

Complementing and improving the results of Rothe and Woitaschik
[
29
]
on the
Possible
and the
Necessary President
problem, we have performed a more fine-grained parameterized complexity analysis for these two problems.
Our main results are dichotomies for them with respect to the number of voters: For two voters, they both are solvable in linear time, whereas we show the
Possible President
problem
NP-complete
for three or more voters and maximum party size two, and the
Necessary President
problem
coNP-complete
for three or more voters and maximum party size two or three, respectively, depending on whether the number of voters is even or odd.
In addition to these two natural parameters, we have also considered parameterization by the number of parties.
For any choice of viewing these three values as being a constant, a parameter, or unbounded, we have determined the parameterized complexity of the
Possible
and the
Necessary President
problem with respect to the corresponding parameterization.
