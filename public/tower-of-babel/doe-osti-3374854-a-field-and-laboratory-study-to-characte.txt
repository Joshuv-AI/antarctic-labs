 Executive Summary
 Introduction
 Faults in residential comfort systems can have detrimental effects on the performance
 of the system, reducing the energy efficiency, the ability to maintain a healthy and
 comfortable indoor environment, and the lifetime of the equipment. Many of the faults
 occur at installation. However, there has not previously been a systematic and
 nationally representative study to indicate the prevalence of the faults, meaning the
 distribution of faults by fault type and fault intensity. Understanding true fault prevalence
 is essential to be able to make good decisions about how to address the problem of
 faults—knowing the magnitude of the problem, knowing the causes of installation faults,
 knowing what types of faults occur most frequently, etc. Armed with this knowledge, it
 will be possible to design improved quality installation practices and programs
 strategically to address the problem, provide certainty to the value proposition of fault
 diagnostics and maintenance programs, and potentially adapt the manufacture of
 systems to be less fault prone or less fault sensitive.

 Project Goal
 This report describes a project whose goal was to determine the prevalence of
 residential comfort system faults. The focus is on air conditioners and heat pumps, and
 on key faults that can occur during installation and have significant impacts on
 performance: (1) incorrect refrigerant charge level (undercharge or overcharge); (2)
 indoor coil airflow rate; (3) liquid line restrictions; (4) non-condensable gas in the
 refrigerant; and (5) duct leakage. In addition to quantifying fault prevalence, we
 collected metadata that could be studied to determine whether there are correlations
 that suggest drivers of fault prevalence, such as regional variations in practice,
 economic factors, climatic impacts, and others.

 Field Study Plan
 To determine fault prevalence, we conducted a field study of a sample of houses in a
 representative set of locations around the U.S. In parallel, the U.S. Department of
 Energy contracted with the Florida Solar Energy Center to collect comparable data, so
 that our field study results could be combined for a larger sample size and wider
 geographical representation. Part of our work was to coordinate with the Florida Solar
 Energy Center to use a consistent approach, and to overlap with some of the houses
 they tested to ensure that our results were also consistent.

 Laboratory Testing
 Prior to the commencement of our field study, we needed to develop a field test method
 that meets the following criteria for the study: accurately quantifies fault level for each
 fault type; can be conducted non-invasively and quickly, so that homeowners are willing
 to participate; and is able to individually diagnose faults even if multiple faults are
 present. First, we examined all known existing methods to create a shortlist of
 potentially viable candidates. Then we installed a 4-ton high-SEER (seasonal energy

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                   vii

 efficiency ratio) heat pump equipped with a thermostatic expansion valve (TXV) in our
 laboratory and ran an extensive battery of tests, imposing faults at varying fault
 intensities, combined with varying operating conditions. Next, we tried combining faults,
 again with various levels of fault intensity. We combined up to four simultaneous faults;
 something that has never been done before. We applied the potential field fault
 diagnostic methods to the test unit in the laboratory to measure their success. For most
 of the faults, we found that we needed to develop new methods or at least to refine
 existing methods.

 Our field study was delayed by more than a year by the COVID pandemic, so we
 focused additional effort on the laboratory testing. In response to a recommendation
 from the technical advisory group, we tested a second air conditioner—a 3-ton minimum
 efficiency system equipped with a fixed orifice expansion device—and applied the field
 fault methods to see whether they work on a very different type of system. We also built
 a new test apparatus to study non-condensable gas, finding that the existing methods
 for detecting and diagnosing this fault are inaccurate. We developed a more accurate
 method for determining the quantity of non-condensable gas in the field.

 Monitor and Virtual Superheat Sensor
 Part of our intended approach was to leave a long-term monitor on a subset of the
 houses we visited, to track runtime of the system over a cooling season. With the
 opportunity to monitor the system, we wanted to also track refrigerant charge to see if
 slow leakage occurred. We developed a monitor based on an Arduino 1 and developed a
 new method by which we could virtually measure suction superheat (called the virtual
 superheat sensor) without invasive pressure measurements or measurements indoors.
 We have patented this method and are currently negotiating a licensing agreement with
 a fault monitoring company.

 Field Testing
 With our resulting field method, we conducted field testing in 341 houses, distributed
 around the U.S. as shown by the red markers on the map in Figure ES-1. Prior to each
 site visit, we provided a survey to the homeowner to gather additional data about the
 house and system.

 Metadata
 For each house that we visited, we gathered several additional types of data, which are
 included or summarized in this report:
 • House area
 • House price
 • House age
 • Thermostat type
 • Thermostat location
 • Thermostat setpoint
 • Air conditioner (AC) make and model
 • Filter size and shape
 • Installer name
 • Quality Installation program
 • Installation details
 These were analyzed to determine if there were any significant correlations with the
 fault prevalence results.

 Results
 There are many types of results presented in the report. Here in the executive summary
 we present a few laboratory test results, sample characterization data and results, and
 the key fault prevalence results.

 For the two split systems that were tested in the laboratory, combined faults were found
 to:

 • Reduce capacity up to 42% and efficiency up to 39% for the minimum efficiency
 cooling-only system

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                   ix

 • Reduce capacity up to 38% and efficiency up to 34% for the high-efficiency heat
 pump system
 • Cause impacts to performance and feature variables that follow superposition for
 some cases, are synergistic for some cases, and have a cancelling effect for
 some cases
 • Assuming superposition of fault impacts on efficiency results in errors up to 25%.

 In the full field sample of 341:
 • Average house size: 2,108 ft²
 • Average house construction date: 1977
 • Average house price: $530k (as of November 2022)
 • 80% of houses had one AC system; 17% had two; 3% more than two
 • 79% were cooling only; 21% heat pumps
 • Average SEER: 15.0
 • Average area weighted capacity: 19 Btu/ft²
 • 39% of homes reported having a smart thermostat
 • Average thermostat setpoint: 74°F.

 Evaporator airflow was found to be low in every city, based on a nominal value of 400
 cfm/ton. The average value is 16% low, but there is an unsatisfactory amount of
 variation present in the airflow rates. The histogram below (Figure ES-4) shows that
 -20% (i.e., around 360 cfm/ton) is the most common fault level for evaporator airflow.
 The cities with the coldest winter weather—in order: Fargo, Sioux Falls, and Omaha—
 had the highest average airflow rates. We speculate that this could be because the
 larger airflow rates needed to provide heating result in higher-capacity air distribution
 systems.

 New Knowledge Generation and Technology Transfer
 We have published 15 peer-reviewed research papers stemming from this project so
 far, mainly from the results of our extensive laboratory testing. We anticipate publishing
 several more. The project has also resulted in a patent and two doctoral dissertations.
 These outputs are all listed in Appendix E.

 Conclusions
 Some key takeaways about faults and fault prevalence that were generated from the
 research:

 • 23% of systems had a serious refrigerant charge fault (at least 20%
 undercharged or overcharged)
 • Evaporator airflow is 16% low on average, but varies widely, and tends to be
 lower for heat pumps than ACs
 • Serious liquid line restrictions are quite rare, at 1%, but 25% had minor
 restrictions
 • Combined faults sometimes have synergistic effects and sometimes have
 cancelling effects on performance and on indicator variables, reducing efficiency
 up to 39%.

 1 Introduction
 In the United States, residential and commercial buildings account for a substantial
 portion of total energy consumption, with residential buildings alone responsible for 20%
 of the national energy use (Koebrich et al., 2017). Space cooling in residential buildings
 consumed an estimated 236 billion kWh in 2020, representing about 6% of the nation's
 total electricity usage (Energy Information Administration, 2021). The energy demands
 associated with residential heating and cooling not only reflect the substantial impact of
 these systems on energy resources but also indicate the potential benefits of research
 aimed at reducing energy consumption through more efficient practices and
 technologies.

 Many residential buildings’ heating, ventilation, and air-conditioning (HVAC) systems
 use air-source heat pumps (ASHP), either as air conditioners (AC), providing only
 cooling, or as heat pumps that offer both cooling and heating. The systems’
 performance, longevity, and efficiency can be compromised by installation and
 operating faults.

 can have the greatest impact, thus enhancing the overall efficiency and sustainability of
 residential HVAC systems. Moreover, a comprehensive understanding of these faults
 enables technicians, manufacturers, and homeowners to make better decisions
 concerning the installations, design, and maintenance of HVAC systems and thus
 reduce costs for home residents.

 Numerous research efforts have been focused on understanding how faults impact the
 performance of ACs and heat pumps. Mehrabi and Yuill (2017, 2018) gathered
 extensive data from both researcher and published literature, and developed general
 relationships between fault intensity and impacts.

 Complementing this, field research, as highlighted by both the National Institute of

 Table 2 provides an overview of prior field studies on refrigerant charge faults. These

 identified undercharging as more common. The wide scatter of results for these studies
 suggests that there is wide level of uncertainty in the prevalence of charge faults.

 subcooling (SC)-based chart for TXV systems, based on guidelines provided to

 What is missing from previous work is a comprehensive, accurate, and systematic study
 of the prevalence of all kinds of faults in a representative sample of U.S. residential
 buildings. Moreover, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE 2018) states that there are

 faults would be beneficial, including development of more effective field FDD methods
 to detect them, and field tests to quantify their distributions.

 1.2 Project Objectives
 The current project was intended to fill these knowledge gaps, quantifying the
 prevalence of installation faults in residential AC systems across different geographic
 regions. It also seeks to assess the impact of these faults on system performance and
 efficiency, with a particular focus on less-studied faults such as liquid line restrictions
 and non-condensable gases. The specific objectives of this study are:

 1. To determine the prevalence of different types of faults in residential AC
 systems, and identify their common characteristics and distributions. This
 includes a detailed analysis of both widely recognized and under-researched
 faults, particularly non-condensable gas and liquid line restriction faults, which
 have not been adequately covered in previous studies.
 2. To understand if there are any drivers that correlate with higher fault
 prevalence, such as geographical, socio-economic factors, or other external
 influences. This analysis will explore how different variables may affect the
 likelihood of faults, thereby informing more targeted interventions.

 By addressing these questions, the research will provide critical insights into the
 areas that have been less explored in existing literature. The findings are expected
 to:

 • Provide needed information to estimate energy lost due to these specific
 faults.
 • Inform targeted efforts in improving installation practices.
 • Assess the value proposition of using fault detection and diagnosis tools
 tailored to these specific faults.

 2 Methodology
 2.1 Introduction
 This section describes the research methodology used for the field study to investigate
 prevalence of installation faults in residential AC systems within the United States. The
 study aims to provide a comprehensive but practical representation of national
 conditions by including cities from seven temperature zones and three humidity
 classifications.

 Prior to conducting field studies, we carried out rigorously controlled laboratory
 experiments to assess the efficacy of fault detection and diagnosis (FDD) methods that
 would be used in our field measurements for detecting incorrect refrigerant charge
 levels, inappropriate indoor coil airflow rates, the presence of non-condensable gases,
 and liquid line restrictions. These experiments allowed us to test and refine our selected
 FDD techniques, ensuring reliable fault identification in real-world settings. This
 research includes experimental investigation to evaluate the impacts of installation
 faults—and combinations of these faults that have never before been tested—on AC
 system performance in both cooling and heating modes. This comprehensive approach,
 combining experimental studies and field investigations, provides a thorough view that
 not only defines the theoretical and empirical effects of these faults but also assesses
 their real-world significance.

 The primary objective of this study is to conduct a thorough analysis of installation faults
 in newly installed residential AC systems to identify patterns, contributing factors, and to
 determine the prevalence and intensity of faults. The methodology's specific objectives,
 which shape the strategies and tools for this investigation, are as follows:

 I. Survey Design: To develop a comprehensive online survey that captures detailed
 information on household AC systems, homeowner behavior, and
 environmental factors.
 II. City Selection and Household Identification: To select representative cities based
 on climate zones and size.
 III. Household Selection: To identify households with new AC installations, and to
 randomly choose participants from the identified addresses, inviting them to
 complete the survey and participate in on-site measurements.
 IV. Measurement Protocol Development: To establish a precise measurement
 protocol for each type of installation fault, such as refrigerant charge, liquid
 line restriction, and evaporator airflow rate.
 V. Data Collection: To systematically collect data through the designed survey and
 detailed on-site measurements, capturing both quantitative and qualitative
 aspects of AC systems.

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                   7

 VI. FDD Method Selection and Fault Intensity Analysis: Select FDD methods from
 the literature for faults detection and diagnosis, then use them to quantify
 faults intensities using field data.
 VII. Data Analysis for Correlation Discovery: To analyze the compiled data, including
 survey responses, field measurements, and fault intensities, to uncover
 meaningful relationships and patterns.
 VIII. Experimental Methodologies: Conduct experiments to directly assess the
 impact of specific installation faults on heat pump system efficiency and
 performance.

 2.2 Research Design
 This study uses a combination of cross-sectional observational and experimental
 approaches. In our field investigation, we systematically surveyed newly installed AC
 systems within several climate zones, house types, and cities of differing sizes and
 socio-economic characteristics. Our primary objective was to assess the prevalence of
 installation faults nationwide. This method, effectively embodying a cross-sectional
 observational design, ensured our data collection did not interfere with the normal
 operation of the AC systems, capturing authentic conditions without any modifications.

 In the laboratory phase, we conducted detailed experiments on a high-efficiency heat
 pump in both cooling and heating operational modes, and a minimum efficiency cooling-
 only system. These were tested with variations in refrigerant charge level, indoor coil
 airflow rate, liquid line restriction, and the presence of non-condensable gas, and
 combinations of those faults, measuring impacts on efficiency and performance, as well
 as fault feature variables.

 An additional set of experiments was conducted to evaluate the performance of a
 theoretical method for detecting non-condensable gas faults in AC systems; the only
 known method for diagnosing non-condensable gas faults.

 A subsequent set of experiments was run specifically to validate the use of liquid line
 temperature drops (LLTD) as a diagnostic tool for detecting liquid line restrictions in AC
 systems, particularly under conditions where multiple common installation faults are
 present. We imposed controlled combinations of liquid line restrictions alongside other
 typical installation faults on a heat pumps and an AC. Our objective was to determine
 reliable LLTD thresholds that can effectively diagnose liquid line faults even when other
 faults coexist.

 In addition to varying operating conditions, the systems were studied with common
 installation faults (refrigerant charge, evaporator airflow, liquid line, and non-
 condensable gas) imposed, as noted above. The systems were each tested with these
 faults applied individually at varying intensities. In addition, combinations of the faults—
 double, triple, and quadruple—were imposed at varying intensities. This represents the
 first experimental study of most of these fault combinations. The methods for imposing
 the faults and their quantification are described below.

 The experimental configurations incorporated setups within both indoor and outdoor
 simulated environments to mimic operational conditions typical to residential settings.
 These setups provided the controlled introduction of faults, allowing for the assessment
 under a variety of operational stress tests.

 Typical laboratory instrumentation for AC testing was added to the system, including:
 temperature measurements throughout the cycle; refrigerant pressure measurements
 on the suction line, condenser outlet, and expansion device inlet; refrigerant mass flow
 in the liquid line, electrical power on the outdoor unit; airflow on indoor coil; air dry-bulb
 temperatures at the inlet and outlet of the indoor coil; air wet-bulb temperatures at the
 inlet and outlet of the indoor coil; air dry-bulb temperature entering the outdoor coil; and
 air pressure (absolute).

 The measurements allow calculation of variables of interest in understanding how faults
 may impact the characteristic features of a system, which will be used to determine the
 presence and intensity of faults in the field. Of secondary importance, but broad interest,
 is the impact of faults on the capacity and efficiency (characterized with coefficient of
 performance, or COP) of the system when faults are imposed. These impacts vary from
 one system to the next, so characterizing overall impact from a large sample of systems
 (such as the systems tested in the current field study or the nation as a whole) is more
 appropriately done with a statistical analysis of a larger sample of laboratory-tested
 systems. Mehrabi and Yuill (2017, 2018) provide such an analysis for over 30 systems.
 The results provided in the current report fit within the range of the generalized results
 from Mehrabi and Yuill (2017, 2018).

 The laboratory measurements allowed redundant calculations of capacity, using (a) the
 air side capacity; and (b) the refrigerant side capacity. These calculations have good
 agreement in most cases, but sometimes an extreme condition may cause one to
 become unreliable; for example, when the refrigerant entering the Coriolis mass flow
 sensor begins to have phase change, which degrades its accuracy. Since refrigerant-
 side capacity is defined as the mass flow of refrigerant multiplied by the enthalpy across

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                                       10

 the indoor coil, such conditions make the refrigerant side calculation unreliable. In these
 cases, we use air-side measurements to calculate capacity (using the air mass flow rate
 multiplied by the enthalpy change of air across the indoor coil). The methodology
 adopted in this study removes the indoor blower from the calculation, because (a) it is
 highly dependent on the specific installation, particularly the pressure rise; and (b)
 because it can be calculated easily and reliably for a given setup, so that laboratory
 measurement is unnecessary.

 COP is calculated by dividing capacity by power to the outdoor unit. As noted above,
 the indoor blower fan power is not included in this calculation, but typically will be very
 small and could be easily added for a known blower efficiency, flow rate, and fan head.

 Suction superheat and liquid line subcooling are calculated with reference to pressure
 measurement at the service ports, consistent with practical measurements in the field.

 Additional detail on the laboratory calculations and setup are given in Hu et al. (2021a).

 2.3.3 Evaluation of Non-Condensable Gas Detecting Methodology
 The theoretical method introduced by Li and Braun (2007a, 2007b) for detecting non-
 condensable gases utilizes Dalton’s Law and the Ideal Gas Law to assess system
 pressures and temperatures. Initially, the total system pressure (Pmeas), which includes
 both refrigerant and non-condensable gas contributions, is measured. Then, the
 expected pressure of the refrigerant alone (Pref,vapor) is calculated based on its properties
 and operating conditions. The non-condensable pressure (PNC) is estimated by
 subtracting this calculated refrigerant pressure from the total measured pressure. Lastly,
 the temperature residual (ΔT) is computed using the Clapeyron equation to find the
 difference between the expected saturation temperature at the measured pressure and
 the actual temperature. A significant temperature residual indicates the presence of
 non-condensable gases.

 Previous research has not explored the accuracy of existing methods for detecting and
 diagnosing non-condensable gas faults. Therefore, we focused particular effort on
 investigating this fault and its impacts on fault features. To expedite and evaluate the

 Within the cities selected for their climatic diversity, households with recent installation

 2.5 Fault Calculation Methodology
 2.5.1 Introduction
 The installation faults studied in this research include:

 • Refrigerant charge level, whether from improper installation or physical leaks,
 can alter the pressure and temperature dynamics within the system, leading to
 suboptimal performance or system failure.
 • Liquid line restrictions, which can occur due to kinks in the line or debris
 accumulation at the filter-drier or expansion valve, leading to reduced refrigerant
 flow that can severely affect system efficiency
 • Improper evaporator airflow rate, which may arise from installation errors such
 as incorrectly sized or improperly installed ductwork, or fan systems and/or
 settings that do not meet design specifications. Reduced airflow can also be
 caused by an excessively fouled air filter or indoor coil, or by home occupants
 closing too many vents.
 • Presence of non-condensable gas, is often due to improper evacuation,
 decrease system efficiency and reducing cooling capacity. This can lead to
 higher energy consumption and added strain on the compressor, potentially
 shortening its lifespan.

 In addition to impacts on system capacity and efficiency, each of these faults can impact
 the sensible heat ratio, which can lead to poor humidity control, which can negatively
 impact indoor air quality, comfort, and energy usage.

 In this section, we will discuss the field methods utilized in our research to detect and
 characterize the intensities of these faults. Some of the initially developed methods,
 including the virtual superheat sensor and non-condensable gas detection, were
 ultimately not used in the final study because of their limitations in meeting the
 objectives of the research.

 2.5.3 Liquid Line Restriction
 We adopted the Li and Braun (2007) methodology with adaptation described in Hu and
 Yuill (2023a) to diagnose liquid line restrictions. The approach employs a virtual sensor
 during the transient phase of the system's startup. This critical phase can extend up to
 four minutes where the refrigerant in the liquid line is in a temporary two-phase state.
 During this period, we monitored the subcooling exiting the condenser. When it
 approaches zero, it indicates a two-phase (saturated) state. When a restriction is
 present, the pressure drop through the restriction causes the saturated fluid to decrease
 in temperature to a much greater extent than a single-phase fluid would decrease.
 Therefore, by measuring temperature at two points in the liquid line, a drop in
 temperature indicates the presence of a restriction (the fluid is at approximately the
 outdoor temperature, so it can’t lose a significant amount of heat). The temperature
 decrease is correlated to pressure using Equation 5, which is used with the results of
 Hu and Yuill (2023a) and Hu et al. (2021) to quantify the intensity of the fault. The
 methodology is capable of diagnosing systems equipped with both TXV and fixed orifice
 devices.

 2.5.5 Virtual Superheat Sensor
 The virtual superheat sensor was developed for use in long-term fault monitors that we
 had planned to deploy over a cooling season in a subset of the systems in the study.
 We built and deployed monitors in several homes in three cities, and retrieved them
 after one or more cooling seasons. However, challenges with this fault monitor and the
 resulting field measurements led us to use a different approach.

 The intent of the virtual superheat sensor was to accurately determine suction
 superheat in vapor compression refrigeration systems using only temperature
 measurements, thereby avoiding the cost and potential leakage issues associated with
 pressure sensors on the long-term monitors. The virtual superheat sensor detailed in
 Yuill & Rooholghodos (2023, 2024) allows for effective refrigerant charge level
 estimation (and potentially superheat control) in split AC systems without requiring
 access to the indoor unit. This method enhances the practicality and reliability of
 refrigerant diagnostics and system performance monitoring, and is novel, so it was
 patented, since it can be used in a wider set of useful superheat sensing tasks (Yuill
 and Rooholghodos, 2023).

 The virtual superheat sensor uses three measurements and one parameter:
 compressor discharge temperature, Tdis, condensing temperature, Tcond, suction
 temperature, Tsuc, and the compressor’s isentropic efficiency, η. The efficiency, η, can
 be determined from the compressor manufacturer’s datasheet or through a one-time
 field measurement. The algorithm to calculate virtual superheat, TSH,virtual uses

 3. Calculate discharge entropy: sdis = s(Tdis, pdis)

 2.5.6 Non-Condensable Gas
 There is only one known field method for testing non-condensable gas: comparing
 measured pressure of the refrigerant with the saturation pressure of the refrigerant at
 the measured temperature, as noted in Section 2.3.2. Service technicians normally
 isolate the refrigerant into the outdoor unit and run the outdoor fan to get the refrigerant
 in thermal equilibrium with the outdoors so that it has a homogeneous temperature. Hu
 and Yuill (2023a) found that this method tends to overestimate the amount of non-
 condensable gas, particularly at higher temperatures. Therefore, in our field test
 methodology we arranged a second visit, during the coolest weather in the winter, to
 each site. This had several advantages:

 i. The cold weather meant that the non-condensable gas/refrigerant mixture was
 cold, leading to a higher accuracy.
 ii. During cold weather the AC is not currently in use, so the outdoor unit is already
 in thermal equilibrium with ambient air.
 iii. During cold weather, our field research team was not occupied with testing other
 faults, which generally need to be done in typical conditions.
 We conducted these tests in the 82 houses tested in Omaha and in the 45 houses
 tested in San Antonio. Unfortunately, when we analyzed the data we found that the
 amount of random error was so large that the non-condensable gas fault diagnoses
 were entirely unreliable. For example, quite a few of them show negative non-
 condensable gas levels—a physical impossibility. Therefore, we discarded these results
 and do not include them in the current report. We also ceased making return visits to
 measure non-condensable gas in the remaining geographical locations.

 The sources of random error could include solar radiation on the surfaces of the coil or
 lineset, conduction heat transfer through the lineset from indoors to outdoors, thermo-
 siphoning refrigerant, and most significantly, measurement error. The measurement
 error can come from imperfect thermal contact between the sensor and refrigerant line,
 and sensor error. The non-condensable gas result is known to be very sensitive to
 temperature.

 Although it is unfortunate that we were unable to collect non-condensable gas fault
 prevalence, and that we spent significant effort gathering data that could not be used,
 an important outcome from this exercise is discovery that the only existing method
 technicians and researchers have to diagnose the presence of non-condensable gas
 gives believable but incorrect results. We would not have been able to make this
 conclusion without the large field sample that we gathered.

 2.6.2 On-Site Data Collection
 2.6.2.1 Refrigerant Charge Level
 The on-site measurement protocol begins with the AC system turned off to facilitate the
 attachment of the probes. Although they can be attached with the system running, it is
 common that the access ports’ Schrader valves leak, so the higher pressure of a
 running system would allow more refrigerant leakage while we’re attaching the sensors.
 Upon system initiation and a minimum 20-minute stabilization period, temperatures and
 pressures are monitored to ascertain that the system has reached equilibrium. The data
 is captured for recording once a consistent pattern in the readings is observed.

 When Schrader valves had excessive leakage, we would commonly replace the
 Schrader valve prior to making measurements, if time permitted. In some cases, if a
 significant amount of refrigerant was lost prior to valve replacement (which was obvious
 by observation of the sound and plume, and also confirmed with measurement) we
 would add refrigerant to correctly charge the system, and exclude the charge result from
 our study results.

 2.8 Analysis and Interpretation
 2.8.1 Statistical and Analytical Techniques
 2.8.1.1 Data Preparation
 After data cleaning, in which we checked for anomalous values, and confirmed that the
 electronic data matched field notes and photos, we supplemented the data in several
 ways.

 The dataset was enhanced by integrating estimated house price, net area, and zip
 code, using the online real estate website Zillow. All data collection from Zillow was
 conducted during a brief window in November 2023, so that fluctuations in house prices
 would not impact any comparisons between regions.

 Photos of nameplates from the AC system were used to look up model information to
 determine the manufacturers’ TSC,Rated and TSH,Rated. For about 20 of the systems,
 manufacturer data were unavailable, so the rated values were replaced with the mean
 of the rated values from the other systems, (9.5°F for the rated subcooling). The rated
 values have a small range, so this is unlikely to cause a significant source of error.

 For outlier detection, we applied two primary statistical techniques tailored to continuous
 variables: the interquartile range method and the Z-score method. The interquartile
 range method, effective for datasets with non-normal distributions or extreme values,
 identifies outliers as data points significantly outside the middle 50%. The Z-score

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                                33

 method, conversely, suits data closer to a normal distribution, flagging observations
 more than 3 standard deviations from the mean as outliers. This approach ensures
 thorough anomaly identification. The method choice (Z-score or interquartile range)
 depends on the planned analysis type, with Z-score preferred for parametric tests and
 interquartile range for non-parametric tests, accommodating skewed distributions better.

 A fault intensity result would not be excluded if it were classified as an outlier. More
 commonly, we would check raw measurements for outliers, and use this to investigate
 whether a transcription error may have occurred, for example.

 Additionally, for categorical variables, for applicable analyses we identified rare
 categories (those constituting less than 5% of the dataset) as outliers. This data
 cleaning approach mitigates potential biases or skewed analyses caused by infrequent
 categories.

  2.8.1.2 Exploratory Data Analysis
 For exploratory data analysis, we display various plots to elucidate patterns in our
 findings. This includes histograms, which illustrate the distribution of continuous
 variables such as fault intensities, house prices, and sizes. Additionally, scatter plots are
 employed to explore relationships between different character-type variables, such as
 the efficiency of AC systems and essential house features. Box plots are also used to
 demonstrate variability and outliers in system capacities and user engagement metrics.

  2.8.1.3 Statistical Testing and Relationship Exploration
 Initially, to investigate the relationships between continuous variables, we used
 correlation analysis. A fundamental prerequisite for employing Pearson's correlation is
 the assessment of normal distribution among the variables involved. The Shapiro-Wilk
 test is used for this assessment. However, we found that, with the exception of the
 indoor and outdoor dry-bulb temperatures recorded during the site visit, none of the
 continuous variables follows a normal distribution. Consequently, we pivoted to the
 Spearman rank test as an alternative to Pearson's correlation, owing to its non-reliance
 on the normality assumption.

 In scenarios where the objective is to compare a continuous variable across two distinct
 levels of a categorical variable, the T-test, which is dependent on the normality
 condition, is not applicable. We use the Mann-Whitney U test, a non-parametric option,
 to avoid the limits imposed by non-normal distributions.

 When comparing continuous variables within categorical variables with more than two
 levels, we typically used the analysis of variance (ANOVA) test. This approach requires
 a preliminary verification of two essential assumptions: normality of the residuals and
 variance homogeneity (homoscedasticity). The Shapiro-Wilk test evaluates normality,
 which is less strict in larger samples, by focusing on the distribution of residuals for each
 group. The homogeneity of variances, which is required for equal variance across all
 groups, is assessed using Levene's Test. When these prerequisites are confirmed, the
 ANOVA test is used. Both the outcomes of the residual normality checks and Levene's

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                   34

 test for homogeneity of variances are compiled in Appendix B, for the sake of
 completeness.

 We selected combinations of variables for further visual analysis. Our approach was to
 generate an exhaustive set of plots to seek patterns, and then to use statistical tools
 and common sense to eliminate comparisons that were not deemed useful. For
 example, if outdoor temperature (continuous variable) correlated with geographical
 region (categorical variable), we would ignore this correlation because it is not useful.

 Following our initial tests of the data, we conducted additional analyses utilizing post-
 hoc testing, particularly Dunn's tests, which were important when we observed
 significant results from our Kruskal-Wallis tests.

 The choice of which comparisons to visualize was informed by the p-value criterion,
 prioritizing statistically significant results (p-value < 0.05) for both Mann-Whitney
 U-tests, ANOVA, and Kruskal-Wallis test. This approach allowed us to apply the most
 appropriate statistical tests based on the characteristics of our dataset and to focus our
 visual representations, such as box plots, on showcasing the most meaningful and
 reliable findings.

 3.2.1.1.3 Liquid Line Fault
 Figure 21 and Figure 22 present the effects of liquid line faults on cooling capacity and
 COP. The transition from a no-fault state to the first level of liquid line fault shows
 minimal impact on performance, with a more pronounced decline occurring at the

 TXV-equipped systems (Kim et al., 2006; Kim et al., 2009; Cho et al., 2014; Domanski
 et al., 2014).

 Liquid line faults decrease pressure in the liquid line, a function typically managed by
 the TXV, which tries to offset this by opening further to counteract the increased suction

 superheat. However, once the TXV is fully open, it cannot adjust further, leading to a

 open even before reaching the first level, indicating an earlier onset of significant
 impacts. At the highest fault level, reductions in COP ranged from 6% to 18%,

 Figure 31 shows that heating capacity exhibits a slight and fairly linear relationship with

 in airflow in a 3-ton split heat pump equipped with a TXV in heating mode. Further,
 Domanski et al. (2014) found that a 50% reduction in condenser airflow resulted in a
 37% COP decrease, and a 20% increase led to only a 2% COP decrease, underscoring
 that while reduced airflow consistently impairs system performance, increases in airflow
 above the design condition do not necessarily enhance it.

 Heating capacity increases with higher refrigerant charge levels, while COP shows a
 parabolic variation with charge level. The rate of increase in heating capacity plateaus
 as it approaches 100% of the rated charge level. For instance, capacity rises by 8%
 when the charge level increases from 70% to 80%, but only by 2% from 90% to 100%.
 As discussed in the previous sections, in cooling mode, the optimal COP is achieved at

 the rated charge level, whereas in heating mode, it peaks between 80% and 90%
 charge. This suggests that despite the heat pump having two accumulators and a
 compensator to manage charge variations in heating mode, it can still be overcharged.
 Domanski et al. (2014), Palmiter et al. (2011), and Yoon et al. (2011) have consistently
 observed capacity increases with rising refrigerant charge across all levels. These
 studies also noted a parabolic trend in COP, with the highest values occurring at rated
 charge conditions, suggesting that the optimal charge level for heating mode may differ
 from that for cooling mode in systems with larger inactive refrigerant volumes, such as
 the heat pump tested in the current project.

 3.2.3 Air Conditioner System
 This section discusses the impacts of installation faults on the AC system’s performance
 in cooling mode (Hu and Yuill, 2022a).

  3.2.3.1 Evaporator Airflow Fault
 As shown in Figure 38, both cooling capacity and COP increase almost linearly with
 evaporator airflow across the tested conditions. Ambient temperature has a smaller
 effect on capacity than on COP. For example, at the nominal airflow rate (EA100), when
 outdoor temperature increases from 35°C to 40.6°C, capacity decreases by 5.7%, while
 COP drops by 15.2%. Increasing the airflow beyond the nominal rate further improves
 capacity and COP, though it reduces moisture removal from the air, which could
 negatively impact comfort and potentially encourage mold growth. A reduction in airflow
 by 20% leads to an approximate 4%–5% drop in both capacity and COP, demonstrating
 the significant impact of airflow reduction on system performance.

 Figure 38. Impacts of evaporator airflow faults on cooling capacity and COP

  3.2.3.2 Refrigerant Charge Fault
 As shown in Figure 39, increasing the refrigerant charge level from 100% to 120% leads
 to an increase in cooling capacity, especially at lower ambient temperatures. However,

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                              54

 the impact on COP depends on the ambient temperature. At higher temperatures, COP
 decreases with an overcharge, while at lower temperatures, it increases. This behavior
 is due to the increase in both cooling capacity and power consumption with higher
 charge levels, making the trend in COP dependent on the relative increase of these
 parameters.

 For undercharge faults, both cooling capacity and COP decrease significantly. At the A
 operating condition, COP decreases by 25.6%, 15.5%, and 1.4% for CH70, CH80, and
 CH120, respectively. Since a fixed orifice system cannot compensate for refrigerant
 undercharge as a TXV would, the system's performance declines sharply with lower
 charge levels.

 average, the capacity for LL20 was reduced by 6.4%, while COP decreased by 7.0%.
 Compressor lift increases with liquid line faults, but the reduced refrigerant mass flow
 rate compensates for this, resulting in only a small increase in power consumption

  3.2.3.4 Non-Condensable Gas Fault
 As shown in Figure 41, non-condensable gas faults caused only minor reductions in

 3.3 Field Study
 The field study section of this report describes the prevalence and characteristics of
 installation faults in residential AC systems across several regions of the United States.
 The analysis in this section describes the distribution of the common faults—incorrect
 refrigerant charge levels, inadequate indoor coil airflow, and liquid line restrictions—as a
 function of intensity, and also investigates their distribution and intensity correlations for
 individual geographic regions and other potential driving factors, such as: house size,
 year built, thermostat types, and AC system efficiency. A goal in this analysis is to shed
 light on underlying patterns that may inform targeted improvements in installation
 practices and regulatory standards, ultimately enhancing HVAC system performance

  3.3.1.9 Analysis of Interrelated Faults in System Installations
 Earlier, we discussed the hypothesis that the likelihood of a fault increases if another
 fault is already present. This notion implies a pattern in installer errors, where one
 mistake may predispose additional errors during the same installation process. By
 examining the relationships between different types of faults, this study aims to
 statistically evaluate whether the presence of one fault indeed increases the likelihood
 of subsequent faults.

 In our analysis, we used Spearman 15 correlation to investigate the relationships among
 the fault intensities FICH, FLL, and FIEA. The results, detailed in Table 41, reveal that the
 correlation coefficients are very small. These findings indicate minimal statistical
 relationships between the different types of faults, suggesting that their occurrences
 may not be closely linked. This lack of strong correlation helps to initially delineate the
 independence of these fault occurrences, pointing toward unique factors influencing
 each type of fault.

 Building on the initial findings from the Spearman correlation analysis, which revealed
 few direct correlations between different fault types, we further investigated the potential
 influence of one fault on the presence of another using conditional probability analysis.

 To effectively assess these probabilities, fault intensities, originally continuous
 measurements, were categorized into two levels: faulty and non-faulty. 16 This
 categorization was based on predefined thresholds, where measurements exceeding
 these limits were labeled as “faulty,” thus converting a continuous variable into a

 4 Summary and Conclusions
 This study provides significant new knowledge about AC faults. The effects of these
 faults on system performance have been thoroughly analyzed through an extensive
 series of controlled laboratory tests.

 From the laboratory testing, methods for determining the presence of faults and
 quantifying their intensity have been tested and refined or developed when necessary.
 These field methods can be applied by technicians to detect and quantify faults.

 The previously existing methods of detecting the presence of non-condensable gas
 have been shown to overpredict the amount of non-condensable gas present. In
 addition, since non-condensable gas is shown in laboratory testing to have quite minor
 impacts on performance, even at fairly high levels, it may be that tolerating non-
 condensable gas faults is the most cost-effective approach if non-condensable gas
 faults are suspected in the field. (However, it is still very important to fully evacuate a
 system prior to adding charge, because any moisture in the system can have a serious
 impact on the compressor life.)

 A new method was developed to estimate the suction superheat, using only surface
 temperature sensors on the outdoor unit. This method can be used in diagnostics and
 control, such as control of electronic expansion valves that are beginning to replace
 TXVs. Importantly, because it does not require a pressure sensor, it eliminates a
 potential refrigerant leakage point.

 Many interesting and useful insights were gathered alongside the fault prevalence data.
 At the time of the study, within our sample, 21% of the systems were heat pumps. This
 number is increasing rapidly (RMI 2026). Thirty-nine percent (39%) of thermostats were
 smart thermostats. This is also increasing (IMARC Group 2026). The average installed
 capacity is about 20.2 Btu/ft²; close to the 20 Btu/ft² rule of thumb.

 Conclusions
 • There is significant variation in indoor airflow in the systems tested within
 this study. This suggests that there may be a systemic problem in the way
 systems are installed, possibly related to ductwork, possibly to blower selection,
 or some other factor. Not only is there a wide range of airflows, but the mean
 airflow rate is significantly below where it should be, which is resulting in wasted
 energy. The highest airflow rates (closest to target) were found in the coldest
 regions, which we speculate could be because these regions must have air
 distribution systems that accommodate larger heating airflows. Airflow rates were
 slightly lower for heat pumps than ACs.
 • The refrigerant charge level in the study sample shows a clear tendency
 toward undercharging, with an average charge level that is 6.5% below the
 nominal amount. Additionally, 23% of systems are seriously under- or over-
 charged, defined as having at least a 20% deviation from the correct charge
 level. While some systems exhibit overcharging, undercharging is notably more

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                   113

 prevalent in the dataset. Liquid line restrictions are not very common, particularly
 at high levels.
 • In most cases, the presence of a single fault does not increase the
 likelihood of a second fault being present. The exception is an apparent
 relationship between liquid line and refrigerant charge faults. However, although
 the analysis shows that they are correlated, they have some overlap of
 symptoms, so it can’t be definitively concluded that both faults are present in all
 correlated cases.
 • Many hypothesized correlations, such as the relationship between faults
 and geographical region or house price, were not borne out by the data.

 Research Opportunities
 Several research gaps are presented here in relation to the current project.

 Using the fault prevalence data gathered in this study, in combination with the
 performance impacts from faults as a function of their intensity, there is an opportunity
 to develop a better estimate of the national impact of faults than has previously been
 possible.

 There is significant variation in evaporator airflow, but overall the airflow rates are below
 where they should be, resulting in lower performance than where systems are rated.
 There is a research opportunity to investigate why the airflow rates are low and varied.

 One element of faults that is not well understood is how they behave over time. For
 example, how common are slow refrigerant leaks? Do they leak continuously or
 sporadically? Do other faults occur spontaneously? Does efficiency degrade over time,
 even in the absence of diagnosed faults? A field study designed for long-term fault
 monitoring may offer new insight on temporal fault behavior.

 The concept of monitoring performance over a season to see whether a smaller system
 could still provide sufficient cooling seems like a straightforward way to truly determine
 oversizing or undersizing, but the analysis carried out on data collected during this study
 showed that it is not at all straightforward, and that there is a lack of research literature
 on the topic. Oversizing data was omitted from this report for this reason. A study that
 monitors a set of systems for one or more seasons, and investigates anomalous
 behavior, could inform a new method for determining correct capacity. An example of
 practical application includes right-sizing replacement systems.

 Manual J (ACCA 2016) is a straightforward way for air-conditioner installers to select
 the appropriate capacity of system to install. However, a widely held belief is that most
 air conditioners are oversized. Currently, there is no established method to determine if
 an existing air-conditioner is actually oversized. A method could potentially examine
 thermostat droop (inability to meet setpoint) during periods when setback recovery is
 not occurring, but would need to account for anomalous behavior (e.g., windows left
 open). Since the energy penalty and reduction in equipment life from oversized systems
 are associated with short-cycling, it may make sense to also include some form of the

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                   114

 overcycling methods developed in the current study. In addition, overcycling is easier to
 measure using existing infrastructure, so a study that could map connections between
 overcycling and oversizing would be useful.
