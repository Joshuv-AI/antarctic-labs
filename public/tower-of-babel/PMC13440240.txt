Correlated evolution of core skin structures in freshwater fishes and challenges to epidermal club cells as the material basis of alarm responses
Authors: Lu Li, Songtao Li, Haoyu Wang, Huan Xiong, Hongyan Wang, Haowen Wang, Zhijian Wang, Haoran Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCorrelated evolution refers to the coordinated changes of multiple traits during species evolution. Fish skin exhibits diverse structures comprising the epidermis, dermis, scales, epidermal mucous cells (EMCs), and epidermal club cells (ECCs), yet the correlated evolution among these components remains unexplored. ECCs are classically hypothesized to be the source of chemical alarm cues in ostariophysan fishes, which are passively released during predator-inflicted skin damage to alert nearby conspecifics of active predation threats, this process known as the alarm response. However, we identified two critical exceptions from 53 freshwater fishes: naked carp (Gymnocypris, ostariophysans) have lost ECCs, while mandarin fish (Siniperca, non-ostariophysans) have acquired them. The established link between ECCs and alarm responses in ostariophysans may be confounded by phylogeny. Their consistent co-occurrence does not establish a causal relationship. The two evolutionary exceptions serve as ideal models to test this relationship.ResultsWe investigated correlated evolution across seven core skin structures, including epidermal thickness, dermal thickness, scale thickness, EMCs size/number, ECCs size/number in 53 freshwater fishes. Analysis revealed significant correlated evolution among skin structures. Epidermal thickness showed positive correlations with all other metrics, except for the relationship between scale thickness and EMCs number. Scale thickness exhibited antagonistic correlations with both epidermal and dermal thickness. Notably, scale thickness positively correlated with EMCs number, reflecting functional adaptation to reduce inter-scale friction. Critically, behavioral experiments demonstrated that naked carp, despite lacking ECCs, exhibited robust alarm responses. Furthermore, we tested six close relatives of the naked carp, all of which possessed ECCs and exhibited robust alarm responses. Conversely, mandarin fish, despite possessing ECCs, showed no alarm responses. This evidence does not support ECCs as the necessary source of chemical alarm cues.ConclusionsIn conclusion, our study demonstrates significant correlated evolution among core skin structures in freshwater fishes. Crucially, we challenge the prevailing paradigm by showing that chemical alarm cues can originate from integumentary components other than ECCs, evidenced by the robust alarm response in ECCs-lacking naked carp. Secondly, ECCs may play a multifunctional role evidenced by the absence of alarm response in ECCs-possessing mandarin fish. These insights advance our understanding of fish skin functional morphology and ecology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12983-026-00618-1.

Introduction

Correlated evolution refers to the phenomenon in which two or more traits exhibit statistically significant coordinated changes during the species’ evolutionary history [1, 2]. For example, in the lizard genus Anolis, habitat-specific ecomorphs show correlated body length, leg length, and tail length evolution [3–5]; in the cichlid fishes (Cichlidae), the body shape, caudal fin, dorsal fin, and anal fin shapes all exhibited strong correlated evolution consistent with locomotor ecomorphs [6]; the oral jaw and pharyngeal jaw exhibited correlated evolution consistent with trophic levels [7]. Fish skin serves as a protective barrier for underlying tissues against the external environment. Typically, it comprises three distinct layers: the epidermis, scales and dermis. The fish epidermis comprises metabolically active epithelial cells with proliferative capacity, participating in barrier formation and wound repair, alongside epidermal mucous cells (EMCs) that secrete mucus for antimicrobial defense and skin lubrication, and epidermal club cells (ECCs) potentially executing additional secretory roles [8]. Beneath it, the dermis’s dense stratum compactum consists of fibrous connective tissue providing structural support, anchoring scales, and housing vascular/neural networks. Scales are interposed between the epidermis and dermal compact layer, serving dual functions: offering armor-like physical protection [8] and enhancing hydrodynamic efficiency by reducing hydrodynamic drag [9]. This stratified architecture synergistically facilitates defense, osmoregulation, and locomotory adaptability [8, 10]. Fish have evolved diverse skin modifications in response to varying environmental pressures, but the correlated evolution of these core structural components remains systematically unexplored.

ECCs have been extensively investigated in predator-prey ecology due to their hypothesized role as the source of chemical alarm cues released during predatory attacks [11, 12]. Initial documentation of conspecific-derived antipredator responses dates back to Von Frisch, who observed minnows reacting behaviorally to water-soluble compounds emanating from injured conspecifics’ damaged tissues [11, 12]; crucially, only epidermal tissue injury elicited these responses [13]. These findings spurred broader taxonomic surveys for analogous behaviors. Pfeiffer’s subsequent review [14], consolidating significant contributions from his own research, demonstrated that alarm responses are widespread among fish species within the superorder ostariophysans but absent in tested non-ostariophysans. He further noted that ECCs are exclusive to ostariophysans and proposed them as the primary candidate for the alarm cue source. Designating these structures “alarm substance cells”, Pfeiffer reasoned that their superficial location, thin-walled morphology, and lack of ducts facilitating content release render ECCs highly susceptible to rupture during predatory strikes. Consequently, the discharge of ECC contents would signal the presence of an actively foraging predator. This established the prevailing view that ECCs contain a chemical alarm cue, or pheromone, alerting conspecifics to imminent danger. Nevertheless, this rupture-triggered alarm mechanism remains largely hypothetical and lacks conclusive experimental verification [15].

Nearly half a century since Pfeiffer’s review [14], the diversity of fishes tested for alarm responses to conspecific skin has been broadened significantly. Subsequent research has consistently revealed flaws in this prevailing view, mainly from the following aspects. (1) Alarm responses to conspecific skin have been documented in non-ostariophysan fish lacking ECCs, such as tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) [16] and medaka (Oryzias latipes) [17], undermining the requirement for specialized structures to produce alarm signals. Tilapia can produce blood-borne alarm cues that function independently of ECCs [18]. (2) Some ostariophysan species possessing well-developed ECCs exhibit no alarm responses. For instance, both surface-ecomorphs and cave-ecomorphs of the Mexican tetra (Astyanax mexicanus) possess ECCs, yet only the surface-ecomorphs display pronounced alarm responses [19]. This suggests ECCs likely serve alternative functions. (3) Alarm responses persist during the larval stage prior to ECCs development. Larval fathead minnows (Pimephales promelas), for example, possess internal chemical alarm cues or exhibit antipredator responses before the formation of ECCs [20]. (4) The efficacy of the alarm responses was not compromised when ECCs were suppressed by UV radiation or Cd exposure [21, 22]. These discrepancies prompted us to re-examine the relationship between ECCs and alarm responses through a comprehensive comparative analysis of skin structure and behavioral assays across diverse freshwater fish species.

In a comparative study of over 50 freshwater fish species, we demonstrated that the degree of skin development in fish skin is strongly associated with water column position, with benthic species exhibiting the most developed integument [23]. In another study, we assessed the skin histological changes of 22 freshwater fish species after UVR exposure and quantified their damage levels. After UVR exposure, the size and number of EMCs and ECCs in the experimental fish were generally reduced, but the changes in epidermal and dermal thickness varied among species [24]. These two studies have yielded comprehensive histological datasets on freshwater fish integument, but they have not been fully explored, especially in terms of the correlated evolution of these core structural components. In addition, we found two previously overlooked exceptions to our knowledge: among the forty-three examined ostariophysans (Fig. 1), the naked carp (Gymnocypris eckloni) was the sole exception lacking ECCs (Fig. 2). Conversely, within the ten surveyed non-ostariophysans, ECCs were anomalously present in the mandarin fish (Siniperca chuatsi) (Fig. 3).

A major limitation in Pfeiffer’s conclusion is the inability to discern whether alarm responses are directly caused by ECCs or confounded by phylogenetic constraints, as both traits co-occur in ostariophysans [14]. Notably, naked carp lacks ECCs, while all its close congeners possess them, indicating a loss of ECCs during evolution—the first documented case of ECCs loss within Cypriniformes to our knowledge [15]. Conversely, ECCs are anomalously present in the mandarin fish, but are absent in other non-ostariophysans, suggesting these cells were acquired convergently. Thus, these two exceptional cases constitute valuable models for disentangling the relationship between ECCs and alarm responses.

Building upon these questions, this study investigated correlated evolution across seven core skin structures (Fig. 4), including epidermal thickness, dermal thickness, scale thickness, EMCs size/number, ECCs size/number, in 53 freshwater fishes. We experimentally tested the alarm responses in two naked carp species (G. eckloni and G. przewalskii) and their close relatives, as well as in mandarin fish (Fig. 5 and Additional movies). This integrated investigation aims to yield novel insights into freshwater fish skin structure evolution and evaluate the functional relationship between ECCs and alarm responses.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Southwest University, Chongqing, China (Protocol No. [2019] 5), and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Fish used for dissection were humanely euthanized with MS-222 (250 mg/L; Tricaine methanesulfonate).

Quantification of core skin structure

Considering species availability, taxonomic representativeness, scale size, and water layer distribution, a total of 55 species were compared in the previous two studies [23, 24]. In this study, we excluded data from two species with incomplete epidermal tissues and finally screened 53 species with complete core skin structural data (body lateral skin region) for subsequent analyses. These 53 species consisted of 43 ostariophysian fishes and 10 non-ostariophysian fishes. Among the ostariophysian fishes, 32 species belonged to the family Cyprinidae, due to their extraordinarily high diversity in China.

More specific histological and quantitative methods can refer to these two studies [23, 24], with key steps briefly summarized: Three individuals per species were euthanized with MS-222 overdose; lateral skin tissue (above the lateral line, anterior to the dorsal fin) was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, stored in 70% ethanol, and processed into 6 μm paraffin sections stained with AB-PAS (no decalcification for underdeveloped juvenile scales). Conspecific alarm substance (CAS) preparation

Fish from the same cohort were randomly selected for CAS preparation. Following euthanasia with MS-222, the specimens were fully exsanguinated to eliminate residual blood inside tissues. Afterwards, the body surface was rinsed with ddH₂O. Skin samples were then collected from the lateral body surface. The epidermal area was measured. The skin was homogenized in distilled water and filtered through polyester filter material to remove debris. With reference to previous studies, the skin homogenate was diluted to a final concentration of 0.1 cm²/mL and stored frozen at − 20 °C until use [25–27]. Notably, although we adopted exsanguination treatment to minimize blood contamination, the potential influence of trace blood-derived alarm substances in skin homogenates cannot be completely ruled out as reported in prior research [18].

Alarm responses test

Initially, we aimed to quantify the behavioral changes of experimental fish before and after adding ddH2O or CAS using Animal Tracker software from recorded videos. Fish were acclimated for four weeks in a common indoor recirculating system maintained at 20 °C, fed twice daily, and fasted for 24 h prior to experiments. Following this, individuals were acclimated in test tanks (30 cm × 30 cm × 20 cm) for 1 h within a sound-attenuated room. CAS or ddH₂O control stimuli were delivered remotely, and behavioral responses were quantified from video recordings across 40 replicates per species. However, preliminary trials applying this protocol to G. eckloni revealed near-complete immobility both before and after stimulus presentation, precluding meaningful quantification of CAS responses. These pronounced stress responses under the initial CAS testing conditions rendered the protocol ineffective for this species (see Additional material 1 for details).

To minimize stress responses, alarm response trials were conducted directly within the fish feeding tanks (60 cm × 50 cm × 30 cm), as in the experimental tank of Von Frisch [11, 13]. This study quantified alarm responses in nine species: Two species of naked carp (G. eckloni and G. przewalskii), six phylogenetically related congeners (Percocypris pingi, Schizothorax davidi, Spinibarbus sinensis, Acrossocheilus fasciatus, Luciobarbus capito, and Sinibrama taeniatus), and the mandarin fish. Two species of naked carp underwent four independent trials each, whereas the remaining seven species were tested in two trials each. This unequal replication was designed based on three considerations: first, two species of naked carp (G. eckloni and G. przewalskii) are the core research species, so we increased their replication to 4 trials to ensure data reliability; second, the number of available feeding tanks (used as test tanks to minimize stress) was limited, which restricted the replication number for the other seven species to 2 trials each; third, the six phylogenetically related congeners of the two naked carps served as the control group, and their total replication reached 12 trials (2 trials per species × 6 species), which was sufficient to support the comparative analysis with the naked carps. Each species was acclimatized in the feeding tanks of recirculating system (20 ℃) for a minimum of two weeks prior to experimentation, with trial initiation dependent on observed acclimation progress. All experimental fish were juveniles or subadults under one year of age, and the body length of the remaining species ranged from 70 to 120 mm except for mandarin fish (130 to 180 mm). Body size and ontogenetic stage can influence the intensity of antipredator responses [27, 28]. Accordingly, interspecific differences in response magnitude should be interpreted cautiously, and the presence or absence of alarm responses serves as the primary basis for interspecific comparison. Stocking density was appropriately adjusted according to individual fish body size.

Before starting the experiment, the circulating water system was turned off. Two peristaltic pumps were deployed: one for adding 10 ml of ddH2O control and the other for adding 10 ml of CAS solution (Fig. 5a). The entire experimental process was recorded at timed intervals using a mobile camera (Huawei Mate 30). For each test species, we selected representative videos to demonstrate their distribution and behavioral changes before and after adding CAS/ddH2O (see Additional movies).

Each trial lasted 18 min. ddH₂O was added at 5–6 min, followed by a CAS solution addition at 15–16 min. For behavioral analysis, video footage was extracted from 2 min before to 2 min after the addition of each stimulus (CAS or ddH₂O) (Fig. 5a). In feeding tanks, it was difficult to obtain high-quality videos suitable for data analysis using Animal Tracker software due to high fish density and obstructions (outflow pipe). Alarm responses were therefore assessed using standardized visual surveys. Prior to analysis, all video files were assigned randomized alphanumeric codes, and the observer remained blinded to treatment group identities during all data extraction steps to eliminate observer bias. These surveys quantified fish distribution across predefined tank zones and documented qualitative behavioral changes, including dashing, increased shoaling, area avoidance and sinking [29–31]. The tank was divided into four zones: Left (Zone 1, the left one-third region of the water tank), Center (Zone 2, the center one-third region of the water tank), Right (Zone 3, the right one-third region of the water tank), and Behind Outflow Pipe (Zone 4, a refuge area utilized post-stimulus) (Fig. 5a). During each 2-minute analysis segment, the number of fish within each zone was recorded at 10-second intervals, yielding 12 observations per segment (the numbers behind outflow pipe were calculated by subtracting the other three zones from the total number). Consequently, spatial distribution data across the four tank zones were obtained for experimental fish before and after exposure to CAS or ddH₂O control stimuli. Construction of phylogenetic tree

Complete mitochondrial genome sequences for all species included in this study were downloaded from GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/). Corresponding accession numbers are listed in Table S3. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using the MAFFT Online Service [32]. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was subsequently constructed using IQ-TREE [33] with its default settings and model selection. Node support was assessed via ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot) tests to evaluate phylogenetic node stability. The resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized and refined using FigTree software (version 1.3.1).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 21.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated. Group differences were assessed using T-test, and associations between variables were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation analysis. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, and high significance as P < 0.01. To account for potential phylogenetic influences on the relationships among the seven core skin structure indicators, Phylogenetic Independent Contrasts (PIC) [34] were implemented using the ape package [35] in R software. Additionally, Phylogenetic ANOVA [36] was performed using the phylANOVA function within the phytools package [37] in R to control for phylogenetic effects when comparing group differences. All figures were generated using SRplot (a freely accessible online data visualization platform) [38] and Microsoft Excel.

Results

Interspecific differences and correlation analysis of core skin structures

We constructed a phylogenetic tree comprising 53 fish species, annotating each species with relative mean values for seven quantitative indicators (Fig. 1). The 53 species comprised two major groups: 43 ostariophysans and 10 non-ostariophysans. Among ostariophysans, all species possessed ECCs except for naked carp (Fig. 2) [23, 24]. In addition, supplementary histological screening of lateral, dorsolateral, ventral, and caudal peduncle regions in G. eckloni and G. przewalskii consistently demonstrated the absence of ECCs across all sampled skin tissue sections (see Additional material 2 for details). Conversely, among non-ostariophysans, only mandarin fish possessed ECCs, while the remainder lacked them (Fig. 3) [23]. This pattern suggested that naked carp lost ECCs during the species’ evolutionary history, whereas mandarin fish acquired ECCs.

To assess the influence of phylogeny on the relationships among the seven core skin indicators, we calculated PIC to identify significant correlations independent of phylogenetic effects. Based on PIC values of seven traits, we constructed correlation heatmaps (Fig. 4a). We found that RET showed significantly (P < 0.01) positive correlations with SMC (r = 0.48), SCC (r = 0.57), and NCC (r = 0.38). RET showed significantly (P < 0.01) positive correlations with RDT (r = 0.57). RST exhibited significantly (P < 0.01) negative correlations with RET (r = -0.46) and RDT (r = -0.78). SMC showed a significantly (P < 0.01) positive correlation with SCC (r = 0.47), and SCC was significantly (P < 0.05) positively correlated with NCC (r = 0.35). Most notably, RST demonstrated a significantly (P < 0.01) positive correlation with NMC (r = 0.40).

Common carp (Cyprinus carpio) and its scaleless variant (C. carpio scaleless var.) serve as valuable models for investigating the effects of scale loss on skin structure. Scaleless carp exhibited significantly thicker epidermis and dermis compared to common carp (P < 0.01), while no significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed in SMC, SCC, and NCC. Notably, scale loss was associated with a significant reduction in NMC (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4b). On the other hand, thick-scaled fishes displayed overlapping scale regions, where the epidermis frequently contained more EMCs. Consequently, EMCs in thick-scaled fish can originate from multiple epidermal layers (Fig. 4c). In contrast, thin-scaled or scaleless fishes possessed a single epidermal layer, with EMCs confined to the outer layer (Fig. 4c). These findings further supported the positive correlation between RST and NMC in skin structure of freshwater fish.

Interspecific differences of alarm responses

To comprehensively determine alarm response capability in each species, we assessed four characteristic alarm behaviors and quantified experimental fish spatial distribution changes before and after stimulus exposure (ddH₂O or CAS). The addition of ddH₂O elicited transient alarm responses (e.g., sinking and increased shoaling) in some groups of both naked carps, likely attributable to stress responses or mechanical disturbance. However, these behaviors were transient relative to CAS-induced alarm responses, resulting in minimal distributional changes following ddH₂O addition (Fig. 5b). Conversely, CAS exposure induced intense and persistent alarm behaviors in nearly all groups of both naked carps, resulting in large distributional changes (Fig. 5b). Other ostariophysans exhibited minimal alarm responses to ddH₂O addition, with negligible distributional changes. In stark contrast, these species demonstrated robust and sustained alarm behaviors after CAS treatment, culminating in pronounced distributional changes (Fig. 5b). Both naked carps and other ostariophysans exhibited significantly greater changes (P < 0.05) in distribution following CAS stimulation compared to ddH₂O controls (Figs. 5c-e). Furthermore, no significant difference (P = 0.772) was observed in C/T ratios between two naked carps and other ostariophysans (Fig. 5f). These results demonstrate that both naked carps exhibit functional alarm responses despite lacking ECCs. On the other hand, the non-ostariophysan mandarin fish, despite possessing ECCs, exhibited neither the four typical alarm behaviors nor significant spatial redistribution following exposure to CAS. This indicates the absence of functional alarm responses in mandarin fish.

Discussion

Correlated evolution of core skin structures in freshwater fishes

Conventional wisdom posits an inverse relationship between scale thickness and skin thickness, as thin scale (or scaleless) loach (Cobitidae species) and catfish (Siluriformes species) typically exhibit thicker skin [10, 23]. Our findings further support this view, demonstrating an antagonistic relationship between scale thickness and the thickness of both the dermis and epidermis (Fig. 4a). Similarly, scaleless carp also possessed thicker epidermis and dermis compared to common carp (Figs. 4b-c). Given that the epidermis serves as the substrate for EMCs and ECCs, RET is theoretically expected to correlate positively with the size and abundance of these cells. This study confirms this relationship, except for the absence of a significant correlation between RET and NMC (Fig. 4a). Intriguingly, a significant positive correlation was observed between NMC and RST (Fig. 4a). In addition, scaleless carp exhibited a notable reduction in NMC relative to common carp (Fig. 4b). This finding contrasts with traditional observations, as scaleless or thin-scaled fish typically display well-developed EMCs [10, 23]. Further comparative analysis of skin structure among large-scaled, thin-scaled, and scaleless fish revealed that the epidermis underlying overlapping scale regions in large-scaled fish contain abundant mucous cells (Fig. 4c). This suggests a potential role in reducing inter-scale friction [39], likely explaining why fish with more developed scales possess greater NMC. However, this comprehensive result, incorporating phylogenetic constraints, indicates not that NMC is lower in loach or catfish, but rather that other species, particularly the Cyprinidae species observed near the top of the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1), play a dominant role in establishing the correlation between RST and NMC.

This study revealed a significant positive correlation between RET and RDT, indicating structural and functional co-adaptation between these layers. This correlation was most pronounced in benthic fish species, such as the loach (Cobitidae) and catfish (Siluriformes), which concurrently exhibit both thicker dermis and epidermis. This suggests that the coordinated thickening represents overall integument reinforcement, likely an adaptation to the high-friction benthic environment [23]. Similarly, this study identified a significant positive correlation between NCC and NMC, indicative of functional co-adaptation between these cell types. Recent studies further support synergistic roles of ECCs and EMCs in immune functions [40]. Notably, ECCs were also observed to exhibit mucin-secreting capability [10], particularly pronounced in mucus-rich benthic species (e.g., catfish and loach), which possess both large and abundant ECCs (Fig. 1A). These ECCs likely collaborate with EMCs to enhance epidermal resilience in high-friction benthic environments.

Challenging the one-to-one relationship between ECCs and alarm responses

Our findings provide additional evidence challenging epidermal club cells as the material basis of alarm responses. To date, naked carp is the only reported Cypriniform species exhibiting a loss of ECCs to our knowledge, making them ideal models to test the ECC-alarm response association. Critically, our results demonstrate obvious alarm responses in both naked carps, providing no support for an obligatory link between ECCs presence and alarm function. In addition, mandarin fish convergently evolved ECCs, yet it displayed no alarm responses—further undermining the hypothesized causal relationship between ECCs and alarm function. These findings further challenge the necessity of ECCs for alarm responses, indicating they may be a contributor, rather than the exclusive source, of alarm cues, and further imply that ECCs serve additional functions beyond alarm signaling.

Indeed, recent studies indicate that ECCs may serve as immunological roles, with evidence (1) As the organ with the most extensive contact with the external environment, the skin harbors abundant innate immune cells, forming the first line of defense against pathogens. Club cells are located within the middle layer of the epidermis [41]. (2) Pathogen exposure in fish increases club cell numbers, while exposure to alarm substances elevates cortisol levels, which suppress the immune system and subsequently reduces club cell abundance. This suggests a potential role for club cells in innate immunity [42–44]. (3) Numerous immune modulators, including chondroitin sulfate, leukocytes, and mucus, have been observed within club cells. Fluorescence tracing revealed that bacteria and mucus can reach the interior of club cells via transcytosis, potentially related to antigen presentation. This process may bridge innate and adaptive immunity in fish [40]. (4) Immunohistochemical analyses demonstrate positive immunoreactivity for antimicrobial peptides, neurotransmitters, and molecules associated with cellular damage within club cells, further substantiating their immune functions [45, 46].

In conclusion, ECCs represent a multifunctional cell type [15], and alarm responses—which can be induced by various factors including other skin components—lack a strict one-to-one correspondence with ECCs.

The loss of ECCs in both Gymnocypris species may be associated with UV radiation adaptation at Qinghai-Tibet Plateau

The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, with an average elevation exceeding 4500 m, is known as the “Roof of the World” and the “Third Pole” [47]. Its uplift has generated extreme environmental conditions, including severe cold, hypobaric hypoxia, intense ultraviolet radiation, and aridity [48–51]. Fish of the genus Gymnocypris are among the predominant freshwater fish groups on the plateau, distributed at elevations exceeding 3000 m. These species are sympatric with plateau loaches of the genus Triplophysa—small-bodied species of the family Cobitidae. Initially, we hypothesized that the loss of ECCs in the naked carps was an adaptation to the low-predation environment of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. Specifically, we proposed that under low predation pressure, the alarm function conferred by ECCs would be selected against, leading to reduced investment in ECCs in these species. However, our results do not support this hypothesis, as both species exhibited pronounced alarm responses. Although lacking aquatic predators, their alarm responses can be induced by CAS release from physical injuries inflicted during waterbird predation, particularly during their migration [52]. This indicates that alternative explanations exist for the loss of ECCs in naked carp.

In previous research [24], we examined histological changes in the skin of 22 fish species exposed to UV radiation, calibrated to plateau-level intensities (treatment for 1 week, with 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness). Naked carp exhibited the highest UV tolerance, with no significant damage observed in dorsal skin tissues. In contrast, other species possessing ECCs showed pronounced skin lesions. A key phenotypic alteration was the reduction in both the number and size of ECCs—a finding consistent with other studies [21, 22, 53]. These results indicate that ECCs exhibit high sensitivity to UV radiation. This suggests that the degeneration of ECCs in naked carp may represent a long-term evolutionary adaptation to intense plateau UV exposure. Although plateau loaches possess ECCs [24, 54], their benthic lifestyle likely provides refuge from UV exposure. Conversely, naked carp primarily inhabit surface layer [55] and migratory spawning through shallow streams during breeding seasons [56], substantially increasing their UV exposure risk.

Conclusion

This study investigated the correlated evolution of seven core skin structures (epidermal/dermal/scale thickness, epidermal mucous/club cell size/number) across 53 freshwater fish species. PIC analyses revealed significant correlations: epidermal thickness positively correlated with dermal thickness and club cell metrics, while scale thickness exhibited antagonism with epidermal/dermal thickness but a positive link with mucous cell number—representing a potential adaptation to reduce inter-scale friction. Crucially, we challenge the paradigm that ECCs are the sole source of alarm substances. We document two evolutionary exceptions: (1) Naked carp lack ECCs yet retain robust alarm responses; (2) Mandarin fish possess ECCs convergently but show no alarm response. Behavioral assays confirmed functional alarm responses in ECC-deficient naked carps and their absence in ECCs-possessing mandarin fish. Furthermore, ECCs loss in naked carps may represent an adaptation to intense UV radiation on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, given ECCs sensitivity to UV. These findings demonstrate that alarm cues can originate from non-ECCs integumentary components and underscore ECCs multifunctionality.
