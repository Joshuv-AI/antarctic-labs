Mechanical and physical properties investigations of carbon fiber prepreg composites fabricated by the out-of-autoclave method with rapid curing for prosthetic foot applications
Authors: Rifky Ismail, Deni Fajar Fitriyana, Akmal Putra Fardinansyah, Aldias Bahatmaka, Zuhriyan Ash Shiddieqy Bahlawan, Januar Parlaungan Siregar, Yusuf Subagyo, Boram Yun, Christian Chandra, Achmad Yanuar Maulana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The use of carbon fiber prepreg composites offers a superior material solution, the efficiency of the production process using conventional methods is often constrained by very long curing times. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the effect of heating rate variations in the Rapid Curing (RC) method on the mechanical and physical properties of carbon fiber prepreg composites for prosthetic foot applications. The fabrication process was carried out using the Out-of-Autoclave (OoA) method with the Vacuum Bag Only (VBO) technique at heating rate variations of 1.5 °C/min, 3 °C/min, and 5 °C/min. The fabricated specimens were evaluated through a series of mechanical tests, including tensile, bending, and impact tests, as well as physical tests consisting of density and porosity measurements. A heating rate of 3 °C/min produced the best composite performance, evidenced by the lowest porosity, highest density, and improved tensile, flexural, and impact strengths compared to specimens prepared at other heating rates.

Introduction

Indonesia has a comparatively large population of people with disabilities. Accidents, illnesses, and congenital problems can all result in disabilities1. Recent estimates indicate that the population of individuals with disabilities in Indonesia has reached roughly 22.97 million, or about 8.5% of the overall population. This escalation has significant ramifications for the state’s obligation to ensure the delivery of accessible and inclusive education, health care, and employment opportunities for people with disabilities2. The prevalence of individuals with impairments is increasing, propelled by the elevated occurrence of accidents and natural disasters. This condition highlights the significance of medical aids in facilitating the recovery of compromised bodily functions and mitigating the physical restrictions faced by people with disabilities3.

Syazwani et al.4 define a prosthesis as an artificial apparatus intended to substitute a missing limb or part of the body, hence facilitating patients in regaining mobility and executing standard functional tasks. Nonetheless, foreign goods continue to dominate the domestic prosthesis Indonesia market5,6. The substantial value of orthopedic equipment imports in Indonesia evidences this condition. In 2024, Indonesia imported orthopedic equipment valued at USD 75.6 million, ranking Indonesia 64th among the world’s orthopedic importers7. However, traditional production techniques, small-scale manufacturing, material supply limitations, and a lack of public awareness about the usage of prosthetic devices continue to impede the growth of the local prosthetic sector8. To provide optimal performance, biomechanical factors and usability must be taken into account during the design and manufacturing stages of prosthetic feet9,10. Tensile strength, density, corrosion resistance, shear strength, flexibility, durability, and cost effectiveness are among the requirements that prosthetic feet must fulfill. These factors rely on the characteristics of the material, the design of the prosthetic foot, and the manufacturing techniques used11.

Carbon fiber prepreg (pre-impregnated) composite is one of the best materials for creating prosthetic feet6,12. A process or method known as prepreg involves pre-impregnating the reinforcing fibers of composite materials with a resin or polymer matrix in a preset ratio13. This approach facilitates the production of prostheses that are lightweight, robust, and of superior quality. Furthermore, the use of prepreg materials, which are comparatively easy to treat, enhances production efficiency by reducing costs and energy consumption while simultaneously improving visibility into fiber content during manufacture6,14. Additionally, carbon fiber prepreg has exceptional stiffness, tensile strength, and fatigue resistance, all of which are critical for creating stronger and safer prosthetic devices. Additionally, prepreg materials have strong corrosion resistance, which is crucial for avoiding possible health hazards for patients utilizing prosthetic devices4.

Notwithstanding its numerous benefits, the efficacy of prepreg materials generally diminishes with prolonged processing times in traditional manufacturing techniques. The comparatively low processing pressure in out-of-autoclave (OoA) procedures requires extended consolidation cycles to achieve excellent material quality15,16. To give the resin enough time to penetrate porous areas before gelation takes place, dwelling at the proper temperature is required. On the other hand, overly quick curing processes in resins that are not autoclaved can shorten the resin flow time and cause voids or pores to form17. Because of these factors, OoA composites usually take seven to seventeen hours to fabricate15. According to Dong et al., high-quality laminates with superior mechanical qualities were produced by optimizing the rapid curing cycle for prepreg utilizing a heating rate of 5 °C/min, dwelling at 120 °C for one hour, and then dwelling at 180 °C for two hours. Additionally, this technique cut production time by as much as 48%15.

Mujahid et al.18,19. indicated that the vacuum-bag-only (VBO) methodology for prepreg processing is efficient. This method uses air pressure differences to cure prepreg ingredients, which are then treated in a standard oven. To reduce void formation while preserving the mechanical properties of the laminate, critical VBO parameters-such as bagging technique, curing cycle, and composite laminate material composition and arrangement-must be meticulously controlled. The VBO method is considered efficient because it is capable of producing high-quality laminates without the use of a high-pressure autoclave. The VBO process was reported to improve thickness uniformity by up to 110.81% compared to the conventional SVB method. In addition, the resulting laminates exhibited excellent conformity to the mold geometry, with R2 values ranging from 0.9824 to 0.9946. Takagaki et al.20 demonstrate that curing techniques using the extended manufacturer-recommended cure cycle (EMRCC) and the direct cure cycle (DC) effectively reduce residual stresses and improve tensile strength while maintaining consistent void content. Compared to the conventional curing cycle, the modified curing cycles were able to reduce surface porosity and through-thickness voids by 48.33% and 23.7%, respectively, while the tensile and flexural properties increased by 2%-3% and 17%-20%, respectively. Nevertheless, the EMRCC approach typically requires a longer curing period than the manufacturer’s recommended cure cycle (MRCC)18,19. An increase in curing duration in the EMRCC method directly leads to higher production costs21.

The carbon fiber-reinforced polymer (CFRP) composite, employing RGC200FR-43 carbon fiber prepreg (pre-impregnated), has established itself as the benchmark for advancing current prosthetic feet, especially the Energy Storage and Return (ESAR) and blade runner varieties, owing to its superior strength-to-weight ratio and favorable biocompatibility6,22. Prior research used the MRCC and EMRCC methods to produce blade runner and ESAR prosthetic feet, with processing times of 7 h 15 min22 and 9 h6, respectively. Long curing times pose substantial challenges for local producers in Indonesia in fulfilling consumer demands rapidly and economically. Nonetheless, the availability of high-quality prosthetic feet at reasonable rates is an essential requirement in underdeveloped nations, underscoring the pressing need to optimize the production process. Therefore, this study aims to investigate a rapid-curing technique with heating rates of 1.5 °C/min, 3 °C/min, and 5 °C/min to determine a more efficient production process that reduces manufacturing time and minimizes costs in the development of prosthetic foot replacements using carbon fiber prepreg composites. Rapid-curing prepregs, which allow processing via hot compression molding in less than 30 min, have been developed to overcome these limitations23. This method can reduce manufacturing time by up to 48% while preserving superior mechanical properties, equivalent to or exceeding those of traditional out-of-autoclave materials15. This technology offers a feasible alternative for the mass manufacture of structural components, maintaining mechanical performance while enhancing production efficiency15,23,24.

The laminate system was enclosed using a bagging film sealed to the mold with sealant tape to maintain an airtight condition during the vacuum process27. The bagging film functions as the outermost layer of the system, providing a vacuum enclosure and having a temperature resistance of up to 150 °C. The sealant tape ensures a tight bond between the mold and the bagging film so that the vacuum pressure is maintained during the curing process27. In addition, breather/bleeder cloth with heat resistance up to 200 °C was used to facilitate air flow and optimize the air evacuation process, thereby improving laminate consolidation28.

Specimen fabrication and testing

Carbon fiber prepreg composite specimens were fabricated using the out-of-autoclave (OoA) method with a vacuum bag only (VBO) approach. The mold was made of a flat steel plate coated with PVA release agent and allowed to dry for around 30 min at ambient temperature. The carbon fiber prepreg sheets were cut to the specimen dimensions specified by the applicable ASTM standards for porosity, density, tensile, flexural, and impact testing. Cutting was performed with a cutter to ensure dimensional conformance of each ply before lay-up. The lay-up technique consisted of successively applying each prepreg layer on the mold surface29. The present study employed a hand roller as an additional instrument to uniformly distribute the resin, exert pressure on the fiber layers, and eliminate air entrapment between the fibers and the matrix. This stage is crucial, as the composite’s quality heavily relies on the resin’s ability to uniformly wet the fibers while reducing the occurrence of voids or air cavities within the material. The procedure was repeated until the specified number of layers had been reached. The amount of carbon prepreg layers in each specimen was modified in accordance with the thickness specifications given in the pertinent ASTM standards for each test. The bending test specimens were constructed with 13 carbon prepreg layers, whilst the tensile test specimens were each composed of 20 layers. The specimens for impact, density and porosity tests were fabricated using 51 plies of carbon prepreg. The prepreg material used had a woven fiber orientation of 0/90° arranged uniformly throughout all layers. The laminate stack was subsequently covered with breather/bleeder cloth and sealed using bagging film and sealant tape. A vacuum hose was then connected to the system, and the vacuum process was applied for 30 min to ensure adequate consolidation and to check for potential leaks prior to the curing process. This study utilized a DZF-6020 laboratory vacuum drying oven from Shanghai Yiheng Manufacturer (Shanghai, People’s Republic of China) for the curing process. The oven has an internal chamber measuring 300 mm × 295 mm × 275 mm. The maximum operational temperature is 200 °C, with the temperature control precision of ± 1 °C. In the present study, the heating rate was adjusted to 1.5 °C/min, 3 °C/min, and 5 °C/min during the curing procedure. To facilitate systematic specimen identification, these variations were designated CR_1.5, CR_3, and CR_5, corresponding to heating rates of 1.5 °C/min, 3 °C/min, and 5 °C/min, respectively. The curing cycle consisted of an initial dwell at 120 °C for 1 h, after which the temperature was increased to 180 °C according to the predetermined heating rate and held again for 2 h15. The vacuum pressure applied during the VBO process was maintained at approximately 0.8 bar throughout the curing process to ensure adequate laminate consolidation. After the curing cycle was complete, the annealing process proceeded by switching off the oven and allowing the specimens to remain inside until they reached room temperature. A progressive-cooling approach is employed to reduce thermal stress in the specimens. The final stage included demolding the laminate, edge trimming, and surface finishing using a grinder to shape the specimen geometry from its initial sheet form prior to mechanical testing. The complete curing cycle for specimens CR_1.5, CR_3, and CR_5 was 4 h 40 min, 3 h 50 min, and 3 h 40 min, respectively. The overall fabrication process is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Results and discussion

Figure 5 depicts the porosity values of the specimens obtained in this study. The CR_1.5, CR_3, and CR_5 specimens have porosity values of 2.26%, 1.04%, and 1.63%, respectively. The results demonstrate that a curing profile for rapid curing at a heating rate of 3 °C/min yields the lowest porosity, whereas a heating rate of 1.5 °C/min yields the highest porosity. This study indicates that increasing the heating rate to 3 °C/min reduces the specimen’s porosity. Nonetheless, when the heating rate exceeds 3 °C/min, the porosity of the resultant specimens increases further. Porosity is a crucial characteristic that dictates the interior performance of composite materials33. The study’s results demonstrate that a heating rate of 1.5 °C/min yields the highest void content due to insufficient resin infiltration34.

Fig. 5Effect of heating rate on specimen porosity (%).

Effect of heating rate on specimen porosity (%).

This situation leads to regions inside the laminated structure that are inadequately saturated with resin. Conversely, at elevated heating rates, the material’s consolidation during rapid curing is enhanced, as indicated by the decrease in specimen porosity35. Elevating the heating rate to 3 °C/min in the CR_3 specimens results in a reduced minimum resin viscosity. This condition is thought to improve the resin’s capacity to uniformly wet the fibers, consequently diminishing the porosity in the specimens. The reduction in porosity indicates that the impregnation procedure is more efficient during curing. Thus, this situation may enhance adhesion within the matrix and fibers and strengthen the material’s resistance to delamination. Dong et al.15 assert that the formation of voids in the finished laminate results from inadequate air evacuation during curing rather than from volatile chemicals. The results indicate that a higher heating rate can improve the effectiveness of the curing process as well as resin impregnation through viscosity reduction. This condition facilitates resin flow and enhances fiber wetting, thereby potentially resulting in better laminate quality.

Ekuase et al.16 assert that porosity arises from insufficient resin infiltration into the reinforcement’s dry regions. This condition is typically induced by an elevation in resin viscosity resulting from environmental exposure and the application of lower temperatures curing cycles, leading to insufficient resin flow. In accordance with Dong et al.15, a heating rate of 5 °C/min yields a lower minimum resin viscosity of 6.9 Pa·s, compared with 10.5 Pa·s at 1.5 °C/min. The decrease in minimum viscosity indicates that the resin exhibits greater flowability, enabling it to penetrate the reinforcement’s dry regions and reducing the likelihood of porosity development.

The study’s findings demonstrate that increasing the heating rate from 3 °C/min to 5 °C/min reduces curing time. Nonetheless, the increase in heating rate is concomitant with an increase in porosity, which remains around 2%. The findings align with the research by Herring and Fox24, which indicated that G83C epoxy carbon fiber composite laminates cured at higher heating rates exhibit greater surface porosity than those cured at lower heating rates. This phenomenon refers to the elevated cross-linking activity of the resin during rapid heating, leading to a diminished pre-gelation window and obstructing resin flow before the completion of the curing process. Consequently, the development of pores on the laminated surface becomes more evident. Agius et al.36 have clearly recognized the influence of heating rate on void formation during the out-of-autoclave curing process of HexPly 8552 composites. The observations reveal that, before the initial isothermal dwell period, the void content at moderate and fast heating rates of 1.5 °C/min and 10 °C/min is comparable. An increase in void content is observed during the second heating phase, with an almost 50% rise, which corresponds to alterations in the resin’s thermal properties, specifically the degree of curing and viscosity. Void size research indicates that, at this juncture, the quantity of small voids diminishes, whilst the quantity of large spaces escalates, signifying the growth or coalescence of voids. This trend is more pronounced at a heating rate of 10 °C/min, owing to the elevated gelation temperature and the lower minimum resin viscosity. Nonetheless, increasing the heating rate can reduce curing time by up to 32% while maintaining a total void content below 2%.

In contrast to the findings of Park et al.37, the current study indicates that the CR_3 specimen attains a reduced porosity level with a comparatively shorter curing time of 180 min. Park et al. investigated the impact of a conventional autoclave and non-autoclave curing approach on the properties of carbon fiber composites. The non-autoclave curing approach used vacuum bag-only (VBO) prepreg followed by the manufacturer-recommended cure cycle (MRCC). Their processing research indicated that both the autoclave cycle and the VBO process, conducted at a dwell temperature of 65 °C, necessitated an equivalent total operation duration of 150 min. Simultaneously, the VBO process at a dwell temperature of 130 °C required an extended processing time of 210 min. The specimens manufactured via the autoclave method, VBO at 65 °C, and VBO at 130 °C had void volume percentages of 1.1%, 2.2%, and 1.3%, respectively. The research by Mujahid et al.19 assessed various bagging configurations, including single vacuum bag (SVB) and double vacuum bag (DVB), under both the manufacturer-recommended cure cycle (MRCC) and modified curing cycles, such as direct cure (DC) and extended manufacturer-recommended cure cycle (EMRCC). The integration of the DVB approach with the DC cycle effectively reduced the void fraction to an average of 1.57%. Achieving a low void content is crucial, as it indicates the quality and stability of laminated composite specimens. Nonetheless, although effective at minimizing vacuum formation, the approach required a significantly longer processing time, ranging from 500 to 600 min.

The CR_1.5, CR_3, and CR_5 specimens have densities of 1.30 g/cm³, 1.36 g/cm³, and 1.31 g/cm³, respectively, as illustrated in Fig. 6. The findings of this investigation suggest that raising the heating rate to 3 °C/min generally enhances the specimen density. This condition refers to a reduction in porosity, resulting in a denser composite structure. Conversely, once the heating rate exceeds 3 °C/min, density decreases as the porosity percentage in the specimens increases. This study’s findings indicate that the CR_3 specimen exhibited the maximum density at 1.36 g/cm³, whilst the CR_1.5 specimen had the lowest density at 1.30 g/cm³.

Fig. 6Effect of heating rate on specimen density (g/cm3).

Effect of heating rate on specimen density (g/cm3).

The decreased density in the CR_1.5 specimen, in contrast to that in the CR_3 specimen, is attributable to the resin viscosity profile throughout the heating process. A reduced heating rate accelerates the resin’s attainment of the vitrification stage, hence limiting its flow and impeding efficient infiltration through the fibers. This condition results in the development of arid zones or regions inadequately saturated with resin, thereby increasing porosity and reducing the total density of the composites38. The efficacy of air evacuation in the Out-of-Autoclave (OoA) method using a vacuum system is highly dependent on the resin’s low viscosity. A greater heating rate (3 °C/min) causes the resin to become more fluid, allowing the vacuum to displace air and fill the gaps with resin, resulting in a denser composite with increased density39.

Decreased porosity correlates with increased material density as a result of fewer voids within the structure30,34,40. This study’s findings align with those of Prasad et al.41, demonstrating that an increase in rice husk content is associated with elevated porosity in aluminum hybrid composites. The increase in porosity is likely due to multiple factors, including gas trapping during mixing, hydrogen evolution, shrinkage during compaction, and the inclusion of air bubbles in the slurry, either as distinct bubbles or as air films enveloping the reinforcing particles. As a result, the composite density diminishes. Afsana et al.42 reported a negative correlation between porosity and density, consistent with this observation. Enhanced porosity may affect interelement bonding and modify interatomic distance, thereby increasing the unit cell volume and decreasing the material density. Moreover, Shejkar et al.43 indicated that an increased Walnut Shell Powder (WSP) concentration reduces composite density while augmenting void content and water absorption. Composites reinforced with larger WSP particles demonstrated reduced density, increased void content, and enhanced water absorption compared to composites reinforced with smaller WSP particles.

Compared with the research by Bayuseno et al.22, the current results underscore the potential of the CR_3 specimen to enhance processing efficiency while preserving the composite’s density. In their study, a carbon fiber composite was fabricated from RGC200FR-43 carbon fiber prepreg via an out-of-autoclave (OOA) process in accordance with the Manufacturer’s Recommended Curing Cycle (MRCC). Their curing cycle required a total processing time of 7 h and 15 min (435 min) and yielded composites with final densities of 1.22, 1.23, and 1.41 g/cm³ for specimens with 25, 30, and 40 layers, respectively.

The ultimate tensile strengths recorded for CR_1.5, CR_3, and CR_5 were 379.09 MPa, 448.73 MPa, and 416.07 MPa, respectively (Fig. 7). According to Dong et al., the tensile strength of the material is primarily determined by fiber strength, as the mechanical properties of the fibers significantly influence the composite’s overall tensile performance15. The tensile test results indicated that the rapid-curing process at a heating rate of 1.5 °C/min yielded the lowest tensile strength. This phenomenon is likely due to inadequate resin viscosity at a low heating rate, which restricts effective resin infiltration into the dry fiber areas. Consequently, interlaminar adhesion within the composite is inadequate, facilitating the formation of voids and surface porosity15,44,45. The existence of these imperfections later leads to a decrease in both the density and tensile strength of the specimen46,47.

An extremely low heating rate may also cause vitrification to occur before gelation, preventing the curing reaction from fully progressing and resulting in an incomplete network structure, decreasing resin characteristics48,49. The deterioration of resin characteristics causes the composite strength to rely heavily on fiber strength. During the thermosetting process, chemical reactions transform a material from a fluid to a solid, resulting in gelation and vitrification49,50. Gelation causes a significant increase in viscosity and loss of flow ability. Following gelation, vitrification often happens as a result of increased molecular weight and further cross-linking processes, which restrict the degree of molecular mobility within the polymer network structure51.

The findings indicate that elevating the heating rate to 3 °C/min improves specimen density and enhances ultimate tensile strength. Conversely, heating rates exceeding 3 °C/min decrease density, thereby reducing ultimate tensile strength. This pattern signifies a positive correlation between density and tensile strength, as denser specimens typically exhibit enhanced ultimate tensile strength. The increase in density is likely attributable to stronger interlayer bonding and better structural consolidation, which improve the composite’s overall mechanical properties30,52. This assertion is corroborated by Irawan et al.53, who indicated that enhanced interfacial adhesion within composites leads to increased density and tensile strength.

The CR_3 specimen in this study demonstrated a tensile strength of 448.73 MPa, indicating strong mechanical performance and great appropriateness for prosthetic foot applications. This outcome exceeds the tensile strength of most prosthetic foot composites created in previous studies. Research by Kadhim et al.54 revealed that prosthetic feet made from carbon fiber, glass fiber, and hybrid composites exhibited tensile strengths of 448 MPa, 160 MPa, and 560 MPa, respectively. Conversely, the ramie-PLA composite prosthetic foot created by Sopiyan et al.54 attained a notably reduced tensile strength of 48.36 ± 0.95 MPa. Additionally, Faheedd et al.55 examined a running prosthesis foot made from polyester resin reinforced using diverse combinations of carbon fabrics, glass fiber, Perlon, Jute, and linen by vacuum molding. Their findings revealed a maximum tensile strength of 432 MPa, specifically in a reinforcement configuration of 2 layers of Perlon, 3 layers of Jute, 1 layer of Glass, 1 layer of Carbon, and 2 layers of Perlon. Ismail et al.56 assessed running prostheses foot using several matrices, including polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA)-orthocryl, epoxy chlorohydrin bisphenol, epoxy resin, and unsaturated polyester resin, all bonded to carbon fiber via vacuum infusion. Their findings indicated tensile strengths of 236.54 MPa, 483.94 MPa, 306.34 MPa, and 453.61 MPa, respectively. The CR_3 specimen exhibited a tensile strength analogous to that documented by Sehar et al.57. Their research showed that carbon fiber composite prosthetic feet with two, six, and ten reinforcement layers attained tensile strengths of 254.51, 341.54, and 576.07 N/mm², respectively. This comparison indicates that the tensile performance of CR_3 is comparable to previously developed multilayer carbon fiber composite materials for prosthetic foot applications.

The flexural strengths recorded for CR_1.5, CR_3, and CR_5 were 446.85 MPa, 460.90 MPa, and 457.61 MPa, respectively (Fig. 8). The results indicate that elevating the heating rate to 3 °C/min enhances flexural strength. However, when the heating rate exceeds 3 °C/min, the flexural strength decreases concurrently with a reduction in specimen density. These findings support a positive relationship between flexural strength and density, showing that specimens with higher densities typically perform better flexurally and vice versa. This trend is consistent with a study by Manalu et al.58, which found that the BP_10 specimen, with the highest density (1.881 g/cm³), exhibited the highest flexural strength of 35.84 MPa. In contrast, the BP_30 specimen, exhibiting the lowest density (1.869 g/cm³), demonstrated the lowest flexural strength (25.82 MPa). Irawan et al.59 observed that specimens with increased density generally exhibit superior flexural strength, with specimen BP_6 achieving the maximum value of 57.27 MPa at the highest density of 1.23 g/cm³.

Flexural strength is a critical mechanical parameter that indicates a material’s capacity to withstand bending deformation60. The enhancement in the flexural strength of prepreg carbon composites with increased density can be attributed to more effective load transfer between the carbon fibers and the epoxy matrix in the dense structure. This enhancement is directly associated with better interfacial properties, which enable more efficient stress transfer from the matrix to the reinforcement, hence enhancing mechanical performance61–63. Conversely, insufficient interfacial adhesion between carbon fibers and the polymer matrix remains a significant constraint in CFRPs. Weak bonding can lead to early failure during bending loads, mainly due to the inactive surface and lower surface energy of carbon fibers, which limit substantial interaction with the matrix64.

The results of this study indicate that the highest flexural strength was obtained under the rapid curing cycle at a heating rate of 3 °C/min. This condition is likely associated with a reduction in resin viscosity at higher heating rates, which allows the resin to penetrate the matrix more effectively65–67. It is assumed that the initial cure at 120 °C with a 1-hour dwell and a heating rate of 3 °C/min promotes better resin flow due to the lower viscosity caused by heating. Low resin viscosity during the early cure is required to allow the resin to penetrate dry fiber areas, maintaining proper interlaminar bonding and reducing voids and surface porosity15. High-quality, defect-free lamination improves the mechanical and physical properties of the composite68. However, at higher temperatures, resin gelation happens more quickly15. Consequently, void formation and delamination may happen from the resin reaching gelation before it completely wets all dry fiber regions66. Flexural strength was found to be lower in the 5 °C/min (rapid heating rate) variant than in the 3 °C/min variation. The cross-linked network of epoxy resin may potentially deteriorate due to extremely high temperatures. To avoid over-curing, it is therefore essential to carefully regulate the curing temperature and duration69.

At a rate of heating of 1.5 °C/min, the comparatively elevated viscosity of the resin inhibits the normal development of interlaminar bonding in the composite. Consequently, voids and surface porosity are more prone to formation, which subsequently diminishes interlaminar strength and induces defects such as delamination and debonding70,71. Delamination occurs when fibers separate from the matrix due to inadequate resin infiltration, leading to uneven load distribution within the composite and, subsequently, a decline in its mechanical and physical properties66,72,73. The experimental results corroborate this observation, indicating that the rapid curing procedure at a heating rate of 1.5 °C/min yielded the lowest flexural strength across all specimens.

The CR_3 specimen in this investigation had a flexural strength of 460.9 MPa, signifying robust mechanical performance and considerable potential for prosthetic foot applications. Compared with prior investigations, this value exceeds the majority of documented mechanical strength values for composite-based prosthetic feet. Sopiyan et al.54 created a ramie-PLA composite prosthetic foot exhibiting a significantly lowered flexural strength of 62.06 ± 3.43 MPa. In a similar vein, Faheedd et al.55 examined a running prosthetic foot constructed from polyester resin and reinforced with various combinations of carbon fabric, glass fiber, Perlon, jute, and linen via vacuum molding. Their findings indicated a peak flexural strength of 232 MPa in a reinforcement arrangement of 2 layers of Perlon, 3 layers of jute, 1 layer of glass fiber, 1 layer of carbon fabric, and 2 supplementary layers of Perlon. Additionally, Ismail et al.56 assessed running prosthetic feet constructed from several matrix systems, including polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA)-orthocryl, epoxy chlorohydrin bisphenol, epoxy resin, and unsaturated polyester resin, all reinforced with carbon fiber using the vacuum infusion method. The flexural strengths recorded were 115.53, 494.17, 446.94, and 294.99 MPa, respectively.

The impact test results for the rapid-curing specimens are shown in Fig. 9. The impact strengths of CR_1.5, CR_3, and CR_5 were 231 kJ/m², 304 kJ/m², and 263 kJ/m², respectively. The impact strength obtained in this study exceeded the values reported in previous investigations. Ismail et al.56 investigated running prosthetic feet constructed from several matrix systems, specifically polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA)-orthocryl, epoxy chlorohydrin bisphenol, epoxy resin, and unsaturated polyester resin, all bonded with carbon fiber by the vacuum infusion technique. The relevant impact strength results were 113, 117, 152, and 129 kJ/m².

The specimen heated at 3 °C/min showed the highest impact strength, while the lowest value was observed for the specimen heated at 1.5 °C/min. The reduced impact strength at 1.5 °C/min is likely due to inadequate resin infiltration, leading to the formation of voids and structural imperfections within the composite34. These flaws compromise the material’s integrity and diminish its ability to absorb the impact energy. Conversely, elevating the heating rate to 3 °C/min enhances resin flow, facilitating more uniform fiber wetting before curing. This action markedly improves material toughness, as evidenced by increased ductility and impact strength74–76. When the heating rate exceeds 3 °C/min, the resin may not have sufficient time for uniform infiltration into the material, potentially compromising the final composite’s quality and resulting in diminished strength and durability24,36.

This study’s findings reveal a positive relationship between density and impact strength, with specimens of higher density demonstrating greater resistance to sudden impacts77. The findings of this study demonstrate a positive correlation between density and impact strength, with higher-density specimens showing greater resistance to abrupt impact loading. This outcome aligns with the findings of Sonawane et al.78, who indicated that an increase in composite density from 1.12 to 1.134 g/cm³ corresponded with an enhancement in impact strength from 2100 to 2370 J/m². This increase indicates the material’s enhanced ability to withstand sudden high-energy impacts without fracturing. Megahed et al.79 documented a similar pattern, where density rose from 1.474 g/cm³ in the unreinforced composite to 1.512, 1.59, and 1.705 g/cm³ in the reinforced composites, accompanied by a matching enhancement in impact strength.

The fast heating rate of 3 °C/min produces the lowest porosity and relatively high density, according to the CR variations. Faster heating rates are thought to improve resin infiltration by lowering the viscosity of the resin. However, resin gelation happens quickly at a higher heating rate of 5 °C/min, causing the resin to cure before it can completely flow into all dry areas66,67. On the other hand, a slower heating rate of 1.5 °C/min causes the viscosity of the resin to be too high for adequate penetration, which can cause voids to form in the composite fiber layers because of sections that are not fully wetted48,49. According to the test results, the heating rate variation of 1.5 °C/min has the largest porosity, which negatively impacts its mechanical properties.

Impact testing was performed to assess the capacity of carbon fiber composite specimens to absorb energy during dynamic loading. The results indicated that the impact strength of CR_3 was the highest at 304 kJ/m², followed by CR_5 at 263 kJ/m², and CR_1.5 at 231 kJ/m². These discrepancies indicate differences in each specimen’s capacity to absorb internal energy under impact stress80. This discovery aligns with the macroscopic fracture observations depicted in Fig. 10. In general, composite materials’ failure modes can be categorized into four primary classifications, namely complete break, hinge break, partial break, and non-break81.

In the present study, all specimens exhibited the same hinge break. Despite specimen CR_3 exhibiting significantly more impact toughness than specimen CR_1.5, the woven carbon fiber construction in all specimens remained capable of enduring the pendulum impact. As a result, the specimens did not achieve complete separation into two segments, nor did they exhibit complete fracture failure81–85. Macro-fractographic analyses indicated that the damage processes aligned with the sequence of impact strength. In CR_3, the fracture surface exhibited a rough topography characterized by significant fiber–matrix debonding and substantial fiber pull-out, resulting in a brush-like morphology. The process enhanced impact energy absorption and resulted in a thicker and more robust fiber “hinge” structure. The current situation indicates that the woven carbon fiber structure remained crucial in supporting the remaining load after the attainment of maximum deformation. CR_5 exhibited moderate fiber pull-out, accompanied by reduced damage intensity. Conversely, CR_1.5 displayed a comparatively smooth and pristine fracture surface, termed a clean/flat fracture, characterized by a thin fiber “hinge” structure. These properties indicate the prevalence of brittle failure in the matrix, facilitating rapid crack initiation and propagation due to insufficient interfacial resistance. Similar failure behavior has been shown in impact-tested epoxy composite specimens reinforced with various synthetic and natural fibers81–85.

Conclusion

The present study illustrates that changes in heating rate during rapid curing markedly affect the physical and mechanical properties of carbon fiber prepreg composites produced via the out-of-autoclave technique. The research indicated that elevating the heating rate from 1.5 °C/min to 3 °C/min diminished specimen porosity. Heating rates above 3 °C/min led to an increase in porosity. Among the samples evaluated, the specimen processed at 3 °C/min (CR_3) showed the best performance, with the lowest porosity. Decreased porosity correlated with enhancements in density, tensile strength, flexural strength, and impact strength, consequently improving the overall mechanical properties of the material. The observed porosity, density, tensile strength, flexural strength, and impact strength for the CR_3 specimen were 1.04%, 1.36 g/cm³, 448.73 MPa, 460.90 MPa, and 301 kJ/m², respectively. A rapid curing rate of 3 °C/min is regarded as the ideal manufacturing parameter for prosthetic foot use, providing a balance of material performance, mechanical performance, and manufacturing efficiency.

Although this study successfully identified the most effective curing procedure formulation for prepreg carbon, the key limitation is the lack of manufacturing and assessment of a complete prosthetic foot prototype. Consequently, subsequent research should develop a prosthetic foot prototype using the CR_3 formulation to assess its mechanical and functional efficacy in actual working conditions. Furthermore, it is strongly recommended to conduct experimental testing in accordance with ISO 10,328 to ensure the structural integrity of the prototype. The below-knee prosthesis evaluation must encompass variations in loading angles and support points, namely during the heel-strike phase with a load of 824 N and a heel support angle of 15°, and during the toe-off phase with a load of 824 N and a toe support angle of 20°. These testing conditions are essential for replicating the gait cycle in a manner that accurately reflects daily use.
