Risk-Based Survival Outcomes in Early-Stage Endometrial Cancer: Impact of Radiation Therapy
Authors: Kyung Hee Han, Min Kyung Kim, Dong Hoon Suh, Kidong Kim, Yong Beom Kim, Jae Hong No
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough a new paradigm incorporating molecular and histological classifications has emerged, molecular classification is not routinely performed for all patients in real-world practice. Therefore, evaluating the impact of adjuvant radiation in early-stage endometrial cancer according to risk grouping without molecular classification remains important. This study aims to assess the prognostic impact of adjuvant radiation in intermediate-risk (IR) and high-intermediate-risk (HIR) groups, as defined by the 2021 European Society of Gynaecological Oncology (ESGO) guidelines.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 131 patients with stage I or II endometrial cancer classified as IR or HIR. All patients underwent staging surgery between July 2004 and April 2020 and were followed until December 2023. The prognostic impact of adjuvant radiation on survival outcomes was evaluated using multivariable analysis with a Cox proportional hazards model.ResultsThe median age of patients was 59 years, and the median follow-up duration was 73.4 months. Of the 131 patients, 72 (55.0%) were classified as IR and 59 (45.0%) as HIR. Adjuvant radiation was administered to 98 patients, including 45 (62.5%) in the IR group and 53 (89.8%) in the HIR group. Tumor recurrence occurred in 12.5% of IR patients and 8.5% of HIR patients (P = 0.574). In the HIR group, adjuvant radiation was associated with significantly improved progression-free survival (PFS) (P = 0.015). Multivariable analysis further identified older age (> 60 years) and receipt of adjuvant radiation as independent prognostic factors for PFS in the HIR group (P = 0.046 and P = 0.023, respectively).ConclusionAccording to the 2021 ESGO risk classification without molecular profiling, adjuvant radiation was associated with a significant improvement in PFS in the HIR group, but not in the IR group.

INTRODUCTION

Endometrial cancer is the most common gynecologic malignancy worldwide. In Korea, its incidence has been rapidly increasing, with an annual percent change of 8.74% between 2014 and 2017.1 Approximately 80% of cases are diagnosed at an early stage and are associated with a favorable prognosis. However, recurrent disease is associated with poor clinical outcomes. Consequently, novel therapeutic approaches, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, are being employed to improve survival.2

Surgery remains the standard treatment for early-stage endometrial cancer. However, the prognostic impact of adjuvant radiation following surgery remains controversial. The Gynecologic Oncology Group (GOG) 99 trial demonstrated that adjuvant radiation reduced tumor recurrence in patients with intermediate-risk (IR) endometrial cancer,3 whereas other studies have reported that adjuvant radiation does not improve survival in this population.4 In high-intermediate-risk (HIR) endometrial cancer, the Post Operative Radiation Therapy in Endometrial Carcinoma (PORTEC)-1 and PORTEC-2 trials showed that postoperative radiation provided a survival benefit.56 By contrast, the GOG 249 trial revealed that the combination of vaginal brachytherapy and chemotherapy was associated with increased toxicity compared with pelvic radiation in patients with HIR endometrial cancer.7

Molecular and histological classifications have introduced a new staging system for endometrial cancer in 2023.8 Ongoing trials are investigating the feasibility of using POLE mutations as a marker for the de-escalation of adjuvant therapy.9 In addition, the 2025 European Society of Gynaecological Oncology (ESGO) guideline provide recommendations for the management of endometrial cancer with known molecular classification.10 However, in real-world clinical practice, not all patients undergo molecular classification owing to financial constraints and reporting delays. Therefore, the 2021 ESGO risk stratification system still includes cases without molecular classification.11 Nevertheless, the optimal adjuvant treatment has yet to be established.12

Thus, we analyzed the efficacy of postoperative radiation in early-stage endometrial cancer without molecular classification to better reflect real-world practice. The survival benefit was assessed in the IR and HIR groups, based on the 2021 ESGO guidelines that incorporate the updated histological classification.

Methods

Study population

A total of 226 patients were diagnosed with endometrial cancer at 2009 the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage I or II and underwent primary staging operation with total hysterectomy between July 2004 and April 2020 at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital. Staging operations included total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and pelvic or para-aortic lymphadenectomies.

From the total study population, 131 patients from the IR and HIR groups were enrolled. The IR and HIR groups were categorized according to the 2021 ESGO risk stratification system without the inclusion of molecular classification, which was based on the previous 2009 FIGO staging system. The IR group included patients with stage IB, endometrioid, low-grade tumors with or without focal lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI); stage IA, endometrioid, high-grade, and negative or focal LVSI; and stage IA, non-endometrioid tumours without myometrial invasion. The HIR group comprised patients with stage I endometrioid tumors with substantial LVSI; stage IB, endometrioid, high-grade tumours; and stage II disease.11 The distribution of patients in our study is represented in Supplementary Table 1. The enrolled patients were followed up until December 2023.

Variables

After surgery, the surgical specimens underwent pathological review to assess tumor size, histologic type, pathological grade, depth of myometrial invasion, LVSI, and cervical stromal invasion. Postoperative adjuvant therapy was determined at the discretion of the treating physician. Radiation modalities included external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) or brachytherapy, with dose and duration individualized according to the judgment of the radiation oncologist.

Patient data were retrospectively reviewed. The primary outcome was the prognostic impact of radiation on progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). PFS was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to the date of first disease recurrence. OS was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to death from any cause. Tumor recurrence sites were classified into two categories: locoregional and distant. Locoregional recurrence included tumor recurrence in the vaginal vault, pelvic sidewall, or pelvic lymph nodes. Distant recurrence included extrapelvic lymph node involvement or distant organ metastases. Adverse events were classified as Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 6.0.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 25.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics of the study population. Chi-square tests were used for categorical variables, and the t-test or Fisher’s exact test was performed for continuous variables.

Kaplan-Meier survival analyses with log-rank tests were performed to evaluate PFS and OS based on the administration of radiation therapy within the classified risk groups. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for potential risk factors were analyzed with multivariate Cox-proportional hazard model. Because risk groups were classified based on established risk factors (e.g., FIGO stage, endometrioid histology, depth of myometrial invasion, pathologic tumor grade, LVSI, and cervical stromal invasion), these factors were excluded from the survival analyses. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (number: B-2402-882-103). The requirement for written informed consent from the enrolled patients was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study and the minimal risk involved.

RESULTS

Patients’ characteristics

The median age was 59 years (range, 39–87), and the median follow-up period was 73.4 months (range, 3.7–232.9). Among the 131 patients in the study population, adjuvant radiation therapy was administered to 33 patients (25.2%), while 98 patients (74.8%) did not receive radiation. Patient characteristics according to the receipt of adjuvant radiation therapy are summarized in Table 1. Significant differences between the groups were observed in the 2009 and 2023 FIGO stages, LVSI, cervical stromal invasion, and risk groups (P = 0.002, P = 0.012, P = 0.014, P < 0.001, and P < 0.001, respectively). Among patients who received radiation therapy, 30 (30.9%) underwent brachytherapy and 67 (69.1%) received EBRT. However, recurrence and mortality were not significantly associated with receipt of radiation therapy.

The comparison between patients with IR and HIR is presented in Supplementary Table 2. The two groups differed significantly with respect to established risk factors, including 2009 and 2023 FIGO stage, depth of myometrial invasion, LVSI, and cervical stromal invasion (P = 0.001, P < 0.001, P = 0.023, P < 0.001, and P < 0.001, respectively). Adjuvant radiation was administered to 98 of 131 patients (74.8%), including 45 of 72 patients in the IR group (62.5%) and 53 of 59 patients in the HIR group (89.8%) (P < 0.001). The method of radiation was not significantly associated with risk group (P = 0.665).

Three cases (2.3%) of CTCAE grade 2 radiation colitis occurred as adverse events related to radiation therapy. The type, dose, and duration of radiation therapy were not significantly associated with radiation colitis (P = 0.288, P = 0.134, and P = 0.234, respectively).

Tumor recurrence and survival analysis

Tumor recurrence occurred in 14 patients (10.7%) in the study population, including 9 of 72 patients in the IR group (12.5%) and 5 of 59 in the HIR group (8.5%) (P = 0.574). Clinocopathological profiles of patients with tumor recurrence of IR and HIR were represented in Supplementary Table 3. In the IR group, no significant association was observed between tumour recurrence and traditional risk factors such as age, stage, endometrioid histology, pathological high grade, over half of the myometrial invasion, and substantial LVSI. However, tumour recurrence was significantly associated with age > 60 years and the 2009 FIGO stage in the HIR group (P = 0.049 and P = 0.043, respectively).

Among the patients who received adjuvant radiation (45 in the IR group and 53 in the HIR group), distant recurrence occurred in 5 patients (11.1%) in the IR group and 3 patients (5.7%) in the HIR group. Recurrence sites were not significantly associated with risk groups when stratified by receipt of adjuvant radiation therapy (IR group, P = 1.000; HIR group, P = 0.400; Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the efficacy of adjuvant radiation therapy in the IR and HIR groups of patients with early-stage endometrial cancer based on the 2021 ESGO risk grouping without incorporating molecular classification. Age ≥ 60 years and adjuvant radiation therapy were identified as prognostic factors for PFS in the HIR group (P = 0.046 and P = 0.023, respectively) but not in the IR group. In the HIR group, adjuvant radiation therapy significantly prolonged PFS, as demonstrated by the Kaplan-Meier method with a log-rank test (P = 0.015). Among the 131 patients in the study population, radiation colitis occurred as an adverse event in three cases.

Adjuvant radiation therapy in the IR group of endometrial cancer remains controversial. One study reported that adjuvant therapy improved survival compared with no adjuvant therapy in early-stage disease.13 However, a more recent analysis demonstrated that 5-year survival in IR endometrial cancer was not affected by radiotherapy.14 Consistent with these findings, our study showed that adjuvant radiation therapy did not improve survival and had no prognostic relevance for patients with IR endometrial cancer.

In HIR group, postoperative radiation therapy significantly increased PFS in HIR group in the multivariate analysis. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies. In the GOG-99 trial, pelvic radiation improved local pelvic control, resulting in a 2-year cumulative recurrence incidence of 6%, compared with 26% in cases without adjuvant therapy. A subgroup analysis of the GOG 99 trial demonstrated the significance of adjuvant radiation therapy in reducing recurrence risk.3 In a recent report analyzing the 15-year follow-up results of the PORTEC-1 study, adjuvant pelvic radiation significantly reduced local recurrence compared to normal treatment in patients with HIR (6% vs. 15.5%, P < 0.001).15

Many previous studies have evaluated the association between risk groups and prognosis in early-stage endometrial cancer. However, the definitions and classification criteria varied across studies, leading to heterogeneity in clinical management. With recent advances in molecular and pathological classification, new concepts for risk stratification and treatment of endometrial cancer have been introduced. Nevertheless, in real-world practice, many patients are still classified solely based on clinicopathologic features, despite the growing adoption of molecular testing. Notably, the categorization of intermediate-risk endometrial cancer remains unchanged even when molecular classification is available, if the tumor demonstrates mismatch repair deficiency or lacks a specific molecular profile. Accordingly, our results remain clinically meaningful, as they reflect treatment decisions in both settings, with or without molecular data. Even when molecular classification is unavailable, selecting the appropriate adjuvant treatment, particularly radiation therapy, remains essential for optimal patient management.

Our study has some limitations, including the small sample size, retrospective design, and a limited number of cases with serous histology. The number of tumor recurrences and overall deaths was low, resulting in wide CIs and limiting the statistical power for survival analysis. In addition, because of the retrospective design, the distribution of patients who received radiation therapy versus those who did not was uneven.

However, this was a well-designed study aimed at evaluating the effects of adjuvant radiation in the IR and HIR groups of patients with early-stage endometrial cancer. More than half of the patients received adjuvant therapy, and sufficient numbers of recurrences and deaths were recorded to permit meaningful survival analysis. The limited number of serous histology cases was due to the exclusion of patients who underwent chemotherapy. The types of radiation used in our study varied and included both vaginal brachytherapy and pelvic EBRT. Following the PORTEC-2 trial, which demonstrated no significant survival difference between vaginal brachytherapy and EBRT,6 recent trends have shown a decline in EBRT use among HIR patients, despite evidence that brachytherapy combined with chemotherapy is not superior to EBRT.7 , 16

In conclusion, adjuvant radiation therapy demonstrated prognostic relevance in HIR patients with early-stage endometrial cancer, whereas no significant survival benefit was observed in the IR group. These findings support the continued role of radiation therapy in selected patients and underscore the importance of refining risk stratification systems in the absence of molecular classification. Future studies should also evaluate the cost-effectiveness of adjuvant treatment for early-stage endometrial cancer, with or without molecular data, to better guide the selection of optimal adjuvant strategies.
