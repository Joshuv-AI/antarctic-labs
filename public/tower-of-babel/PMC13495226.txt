From knowledge to everyday food choices: a qualitative study of nutrition literacy and eating behaviors among primary school students in Sabzevar, Iran
Authors: Ali Mehri, Malihe Seyedabadi, Shirin Shahbazi Seighaldeh, Maryam Bazargan, Mostafa Rad, Reza Shahrabadi, Hamid Jovini, Masoumeh Hashemian, Bahar Hassantabar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNutrition literacy refers to the ability to access, understand, evaluate, and apply nutrition information to make informed dietary choices. Evidence from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) on nutrition literacy and related behaviors among schoolchildren remains limited, particularly regarding how school, home, and community environments shape these dynamics. This study explored how primary school students in Sabzevar, Iran, understand nutrition literacy and apply it in their daily eating practices.MethodsA descriptive exploratory qualitative study was conducted in Sabzevar, Iran, between October 2021 and May 2022. Thirty-two students aged 10–12 years from three schools in different socioeconomic areas were recruited using purposive sampling, with limited snowball sampling to ensure variation in gender, BMI group, and socioeconomic status. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews guided by seven thematic sections and approximately 30 open-ended questions. Interviews lasted 45–60 min, were conducted in private school settings, and were audio-recorded with parental consent and child assent. Data saturation was reached after 28 interviews and confirmed with four additional interviews. Data were analyzed using Graneheim and Lundman’s content analysis, and trustworthiness was ensured using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria.ResultsParticipants included 18 girls and 14 boys (mean age: 11.06 years). Students demonstrated mainly functional nutrition literacy, identifying unhealthy foods and recognizing some health risks, particularly related to processed foods and food safety. However, knowledge rarely translated into healthy eating behaviors due to dependence on parents, simplified food classifications, taste preferences, peer norms, convenience, and irregular meal routines. Breakfast skipping and frequent consumption of processed snacks were common. Interactive and critical nutrition literacy skills were limited.ConclusionNutrition literacy among Iranian primary school students is largely functional, with a clear gap between knowledge and behavior. Bridging this gap requires multi-level interventions involving schools, families, and supportive food environments to strengthen interactive and critical literacy and promote lasting healthy eating habits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01370-9.

Introduction

Childhood and adolescent nutrition patterns are undergoing a marked global shift. According to UNICEF, for the first time in history, the prevalence of childhood obesity worldwide has surpassed that of underweight [1]. This change reflects a broader nutrition transition, increasingly observed in developing countries, characterized by higher consumption of processed foods, reduced physical activity, and more globalized food systems [2]. Although this transition affects all age groups, excess weight in childhood and adolescence is particularly concerning because it increases the risk of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), psychosocial difficulties, and long-term economic burdens [3]. Improving early-life nutrition is therefore both a public health priority and a strategic investment in sustainable development, requiring efforts that extend beyond combating stunting to promoting healthy weight throughout childhood [4].

Poor nutrition during the primary school years can impair growth, cognition, academic performance, immune function, and increase the risk of NCDs later in life [5–7]. These challenges are particularly acute in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where NCDs account for 74% of deaths and, in 2017, poor diets were responsible for 11 million deaths and 255 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), including 3.9 million deaths attributable to inadequate fruit and vegetable intake alone [8]. Expanding access to nutritious foods during childhood is therefore critical, especially in LMICs where constrained food environments heighten health vulnerability [9].

Iran exemplifies the dual burden of malnutrition, with undernutrition persisting alongside rapidly rising childhood obesity. The prevalence of obesity among Iranian children more than doubled between 2000 and 2016 and now exceeds the global average [10]. National surveys continue to report underweight, stunting, and wasting among young children, while overweight and obesity vary substantially across provinces due to cultural, socioeconomic, and environmental disparities [11, 12]. Overall, diet quality among Iranian children and adolescents remains suboptimal, with pronounced inequalities associated with household income, parental education, and access to nutritious foods within local communities [13, 14].

Despite the growing burden of malnutrition among Iranian children, including the coexistence of undernutrition and rising obesity [10–14], important gaps remain in the literature. Although nutrition literacy is recognized as foundational to healthy eating [15], and children’s dietary behaviors are shaped by family, peer, and school environments [16–20], there is no widely agreed-upon conceptual framework or validated tools for assessing childhood food and nutrition literacy in Iran [21]. Moreover, limited evidence exists on whether nutrition knowledge translates into healthier eating under real-world socioeconomic and environmental constraints [22–24], and little is known about how family, school, and community contexts interact to influence children’s food choices in LMICs, where research has largely focused on individual or sociodemographic factors [25, 26]. Accordingly, this study draws on Nutbeam’s model of health literacy to examine children’s nutrition literacy across functional, interactive, and critical levels [27], and on the Health Belief Model (HBM) to explain the gap between knowledge and behavior by accounting for beliefs, perceived benefits and barriers, and motivations that shape food choices [28]. These complementary frameworks provide a more comprehensive understanding of how nutrition knowledge is interpreted and enacted in children’s everyday lives.

This study examined how fourth- to sixth-grade students in Sabzevar, (a city in Razavi Khorasan Province in northeastern Iran) conceptualize nutrition literacy and experience the translation of knowledge into eating behaviors. Sabzevar, with its mix of public and private schools, provides a relevant context for exploring conditions common among Iranian students. The objectives were to: (1) describe how students define nutrition literacy in their own terms; (2) describe eating behaviors and routines and explore factors influencing the application of nutrition knowledge in daily choices, particularly within family and school contexts; and (3) derive practical implications for integrating nutrition education with supportive school and community environments. The findings aim to inform strategies to improve children’s diets in Sabzevar and similar settings across Iran, with potential relevance to LMICs experiencing comparable nutritional and environmental challenges.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This qualitative, descriptive–exploratory, cross-sectional study was conducted in Sabzevar, a mid-sized city in Razavi Khorasan Province in northeastern Iran, between October 2021 and May 2022. Sabzevar was selected because, as a rapidly growing secondary city undergoing food system transitions and demographic shifts, it provides a relevant context for assessing nutrition literacy among schoolchildren. The setting is also significant because nutrition and child health have become key public health priorities in the region, particularly in response to changing dietary patterns and the rising burden of nutrition-related health concerns among children [29] (Fig. 1).

Philosophical perspective

This study was guided by an interpretivist qualitative perspective, which recognizes that children’s nutrition literacy and eating behaviors are shaped by their family, school, social, and cultural contexts [30, 32]. A descriptive–exploratory qualitative design was appropriate because the study aimed to understand how students described, interpreted, and applied nutrition knowledge in everyday life, rather than measuring nutrition literacy as a fixed or objective construct [46]. Inductive qualitative content analysis was consistent with this approach, allowing categories to emerge from students’ accounts before interpretation using nutrition literacy concepts and the Health Belief Model [27, 28, 31].

Participant recruitment and sampling

A two-phase sampling strategy was employed. Purposive sampling was the primary approach, supplemented by limited snowball sampling when needed. In the first phase, three primary schools in Sabzevar were selected through convenience sampling, with approval from the Department of Education, to ensure feasibility and access (two public schools and one private school). In the second phase, students were purposively recruited to achieve variation in gender and BMI status. BMI-for-age percentiles from school health records were used solely to guide selection and ensure representation across categories; oversampling of children with overweight and obesity was intentional to capture experiences related to barriers in translating nutrition knowledge into behavior.

Individual BMI status was not disclosed during recruitment to avoid stigmatization. Eligible participants were students aged 10–12 years, enrolled in grades 4–6, who provided written parental consent and personal assent. This developmental stage was selected because children begin to exercise greater autonomy in food choices while remaining influenced by family and peer environments. School health staff assisted in identifying eligible students, and the research team contacted families directly. Snowball sampling was used sparingly to broaden perspectives, resulting in six additional participants. In total, 32 students were interviewed.

Data collection

Semi-structured individual interviews were conducted in private school settings using spare rooms to ensure students’ comfort, as the school environment was familiar and convenient. This approach allowed children to describe their experiences while enabling interviewers to probe emerging ideas. Before each interview, the study purpose was explained, and parental consent and student assent were reconfirmed.

Two trained researchers conducted the interviews: one experienced in qualitative interviewing and the other in health education, ensuring that children’s perspectives were explored clearly and sensitively. A flexible interview guide was used to explore nutrition literacy, food knowledge, eating experiences, and family influences. Interviews lasted 45–60 min, which was appropriate for children’s attention spans. With permission from students and parents, all interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Data collection occurred concurrently with preliminary analysis and continued until no new ideas or insights emerged, which occurred after 28 interviews. Four additional interviews were conducted to confirm data saturation, resulting in a final sample of 32 participants.

Data collection tool

Data were collected using a semi-structured interview guide (Appendix A) developed by the research team to explore students’ nutrition literacy and eating behaviors. The guide was informed by Nutbeam’s health literacy model and the Health Belief Model (HBM), and structured according to the Social Ecological Model [30] to capture influences across individual, interpersonal, institutional, and community levels.

The guide consisted of seven thematic sections and approximately 30 core open-ended questions, with additional probing prompts used as needed. The sections covered: (1) participant demographic information; (2) individual nutrition literacy and understanding of food and health; (3) daily eating behaviors; (4) family influences; (5) peer influences; (6) school food environments; and (7) community and media influences, including perceived benefits and barriers to healthy eating.

Interviews lasted 45–60 min in total. On average, 5–10 min were allocated to each thematic section; the demographic section required approximately 2–3 min, while sections on nutrition literacy and eating behaviors required slightly longer (8–10 min each) to allow participants to elaborate. Questions were open-ended and child-friendly, encouraging responses through prompts such as “what,” “how,” “where,” “when,” and “why,” with flexible probing (e.g., “Can you give an example?”) used to deepen discussion. The full interview guide is provided in Appendix A.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using Graneheim and Lundman’s qualitative content analysis approach [31]. Analysis was primarily inductive and conducted independently by two researchers to enhance analytic rigor. Transcripts were read repeatedly to achieve immersion, and meaning units relevant to the research aims were identified, condensed, and coded line-by-line to generate initial codes.

The two analysts compared coding, discussed differences, and reached consensus; a senior researcher reviewed unresolved discrepancies as a third check. Codes were then grouped into subcategories and categories based on similarities and differences, and an iterative codebook (including code definitions and exemplar quotations) was developed and refined throughout the process. Analytic memos were used to document key decisions and emerging interpretations. Data collection and analysis proceeded concurrently until no new codes or categories emerged.

To strengthen interpretive depth, Nutbeam’s health literacy model [27] and the Health Belief Model (HBM) [28] were applied after inductive coding to guide interpretation rather than constrain category development. Once categories were established, findings were iteratively mapped to Nutbeam’s functional, interactive, and critical literacy domains and to key HBM constructs (perceived benefits, perceived barriers, perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, and self-efficacy). Categories closely aligned with theoretical constructs were refined for clarity, while context-specific categories were retained to preserve novel insights. Through analytic discussion and consensus, the research team examined relationships among categories and refined a data-derived conceptual model showing how students’ nutrition literacy capabilities and constraints interacted with contextual and psychosocial influences to produce a knowledge–practice gap in everyday food choices. The resulting model represents an integrative synthesis of the inductive findings, theoretically informed by established health literacy and health behavior frameworks, and is presented in Fig. 2.

Rigor, ethics, and reflexivity

Trustworthiness was ensured using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria [32]. Credibility was supported through eight months of engagement, monthly in-person member-checking sessions, and analyst triangulation. Dependability was maintained through consistent analytic procedures and re-coding of selected transcripts, while confirmability was strengthened through external expert review and reflexive notes. Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of the study setting, participants, and methods.

Reflexivity was integrated throughout the research process. The research team included university-based researchers from health-related disciplines. Interviewers maintained reflexive notes and critically considered how their professional backgrounds in health and child education might influence data collection and interpretation. Interviews were conducted using open-ended, non-judgmental questions, and researchers avoided presenting particular foods or behaviors as right or wrong. Reflexive notes documented researchers’ impressions, nonverbal cues, and contextual observations during interviews, and were reviewed during analysis to consider alternative interpretations and to distinguish students’ individual choices from broader family, school, peer, and socioeconomic influences.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sabzevar University of Medical Sciences (IR.MEDSAB.REC.1400.077) and the Department of Education. Written parental consent and student assent were obtained prior to participation. The research followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [33] and respected local cultural values. To minimize potential stigmatization, interviews were conducted privately, and BMI categories were used solely for sampling purposes.

The manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Comprehensive Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (CCQR), a reporting guideline for qualitative global health research [47].

Results

Participant characteristics

Thirty-two students participated in the study. Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the participants (N = 32). The sample included 18 girls (56.3%) and 14 boys (43.7%), aged 10–12 years (mean = 11.06 ± 0.80). Students were relatively evenly distributed across grades 4–6. Fathers were most often self-employed (43.8%), while the majority of mothers were housewives (59.4%). Associate degrees were the most common education level among mothers (50.0%), whereas fathers most frequently held diplomas (37.5%). Participants were recruited from two public schools and one private school, with 22 students (68.8%) attending public schools and 10 (31.3%) attending a private school, reflecting the sampling distribution.

Nutrition literacy and dietary practices

Overall, students recognized unhealthy foods but framed nutrition in simplistic “good versus bad” terms, influenced by parents, peers, and body image concerns. Despite this awareness, dietary behaviors were often shaped by convenience, taste preferences, social norms, and family routines rather than knowledge. Nutrition literacy constraints

Most students (n = 28, 87.5%) showed limited and simplified understandings of nutrition, often relying on rigid binary thinking that categorized foods as either “good” or “bad.” As one student explained, “Good nutrition means not eating harmful things, like foods that have preservatives.” Dieting was commonly perceived in punitive terms; another remarked, “diet means eating foods you don’t like and not eating the ones you do like.” or some participants perceived it as food restriction with the primary goal of weight loss: “Diet means not to eat certain foods or to eat less in order to lose weight.”

Additionally, some students described healthy foods as unappealing and unhealthy foods as appealing: “Unhealthy foods are foods that are fatty and can make us ill.”; “Healthy foods are foods that we don't like and turn our stomach.”; “Healthy foods are substances that do not harm our body and are only useful.”

In addition, 75% of students (n = 24) viewed nutrition labeling as the responsibility of parents rather than themselves. One student stated, “I don’t know anything about the colored nutrition labels. I don’t pay attention to them, but my mom and dad do.”

Nutrition literacy capabilities

Despite these limitations, students demonstrated notable capabilities in identifying the health consequences of poor dietary choices, frequently mentioning risks such as obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. Much of this knowledge was transmitted through authority figures, as reflected in one student’s explanation: “Our teacher always tells us that cokes have high sugar and acid and are very bad for our teeth.”

All participants (n = 32, 100%) recognized the harmful properties of processed foods, describing them as “things that are made quickly and have lots of oil, fat, salt, and spices” and noting that “fast foods are fried in very hot oil, and that oil is used several times.” According to the findings, the participants considered fast foods to be harmful due to their high oil, fat and salt content, as well as the number of times they are fried in oil: “Fast foods are foods that are prepared quickly and contain a lot of oil, fat, salt and spices.”

50% of students (n = 16) also demonstrated basic food safety awareness by checking expiration dates. One student explained, “I check the expiration date because it shouldn’t be expired, otherwise we could get poisoned.” These findings suggest that students could link dietary choices with direct health outcomes.

Nutrition choices and behavior

Students’ eating behaviors revealed a persistent gap between knowledge and practice, as awareness of health benefits rarely translated into healthier choices. Immediate gratification, driven by taste, convenience, family routines, and peer influence often outweighed long-term considerations.

A total of 68.8% of students (n = 22) reported requently skipping breakfast or other meals, particularly among children of employed mothers (n = 11 of 13, 84.6%). One student explained, “Since my mom goes to work, sometimes we don’t have breakfast or lunch. Either I skip breakfast or eat cake or biscuits, and for lunch we usually buy food from outside.”

Skipped meals were often replaced with processed snacks. Another student noted, “I don’t eat breakfast; sometimes I just bring chips or biscuits to school,” while family routines normalized these practices: “In our family, we love snacks chips, cheese puffs, anything tasty, we eat.” Taste preferences further reinforced choices, as students reported, “I like fried foods more because they taste better,” and avoided vegetables even when available: “Vegetables have vitamins, but I don’t like them at all.”

Peer norms reinforced these behaviors. As one student stated, “All of us (in the classroom) have a drink with our meals, most of time we drink soda” indicating how sugary beverages became routine. Irregular schedules shaped by parental work further contributed to unstructured eating patterns.

Cross-cutting patterns and influencers

Across participants’ accounts, several cross-cutting themes were identified in relation to students’ nutrition literacy and eating behaviors. These themes included family influence, food availability and access, peer norms and school food culture, perceived barriers to healthy eating, body image concerns, and physical activity. When mapped to the Health Belief Model, these themes reflected perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action, perceived susceptibility and severity, and self-efficacy.

Family influence: cues to action, perceived benefits, and self-efficacy. Family practices were frequently described as shaping students’ food-related knowledge and eating behaviors. Parents acted as cues to action through guidance, role modelling, food preparation, and the foods they provided at home. Some students described parental practices that introduced them to foods perceived as healthier. For example, one student explained that “my dad is always looking for bakeries that have whole meal bread,” adding that this helped them know “whole meal bread is good for our health.” Another student described food preparation as a shared family practice, noting that “most of the cakes and ice creams we make at home together with my mom.” These accounts reflected perceived benefits when students linked family practices with health or food quality. At the same time, students’ self-efficacy appeared closely tied to family routines, as their ability to make food choices often depended on what parents prepared or provided. Some accounts also showed that parental provision could reinforce processed snack consumption, as one student reported that “whenever my dad comes home from work, he always brings home chips, biscuits, and all kinds of snacks.”

Food availability and access: perceived barriers and self-efficacy.Food availability and access were described as influencing students’ eating practices. Participants referred to foods available at home, foods provided by parents, and foods accessible at school when describing their daily choices. Some students mentioned homemade foods, while others described regular access to snacks, biscuits, chips, and sugary drinks. At school, several students reported buying biscuits or snacks from the buffet rather than bringing food from home. In terms of the Health Belief Model, limited availability of healthier options and easy access to processed foods reflected perceived barriers. Students’ self-efficacy was also linked to availability, as their ability to act on nutrition knowledge depended partly on the food options present in home and school settings.

Peer norms and school food culture: cues to action and perceived barriers.Peer and school routines were commonly described in relation to students’ food choices. Students presented some eating behaviors as shared classroom or peer practices. Sugary drinks, for instance, were described as part of regular meal routines among classmates, with one student stating that “all of us in the classroom are used to having a drink with our meals.” School food choices were also linked to peer behavior and the availability of food at school, as one student explained: “I don’t bring lunch to school because my friends and I buy biscuits from the buffet.” These accounts mapped onto cues to action, as peers and school routines prompted particular food choices. They also reflected perceived barriers when shared peer practices or school food options made healthier choices less common in students’ accounts.

Perceived barriers to healthy eating: taste, convenience, and limited control. Students described several barriers that affected their eating behaviors despite awareness of healthy and unhealthy foods. Taste preferences, convenience, food availability, and limited control over food choices were repeatedly mentioned. Fried foods, sweet snacks, and processed foods were often described as preferred or easier to access. One student expressed tension between body-related goals and food preferences, stating, “I like to have a fit body, but I can’t give up fatty foods and fast foods.” These accounts directly reflected the HBM construct of perceived barriers, especially when students described difficulty changing eating practices because of preference, access, or dependence on family and school food environments.

Body image concerns: perceived benefits, perceived susceptibility, and perceived severity. Body image and appearance-related concerns were mentioned by some students when describing food choices. Some participants associated healthy eating with appearance-related benefits. For example, one student said they ate fruit and vegetables because “they help me have good skin and look beautiful.” Other students described a contrast between body-related goals and food preferences, including the statement, “I like to have a fit body, but I can’t give up fatty foods and fast foods.” Weight-related perceptions were also expressed in simplified terms, such as the view that “overweight is different from fat” and that “when someone is fat it means they are sick.” These accounts reflected perceived benefits when students linked food choices with appearance or fitness, and perceived susceptibility and severity when they associated weight status with illness or health risk.

Physical activity: perceived benefits, cues to action, and self-efficacy. Physical activity appeared as a related theme in some interviews. Students described exercise in relation to health, enjoyment, and disease prevention. One student linked activity with both pleasure and health, stating, “I ride a bike in the yard, both for my pleasure and my health.” Another connected healthy eating and exercise with parental role modelling, explaining that “my mom and dad care about eating healthy foods and exercising.” These accounts reflected perceived benefits of physical activity, including health and disease prevention. Family encouragement also appeared as a cue to action, while students’ descriptions of activities such as cycling suggested that self-efficacy was connected to available spaces, family support, and opportunities for activity.

Discussion

This study examined how primary school students in Sabzevar, Iran, understand nutrition literacy and how this understanding translates into daily eating practices. Although students demonstrated basic knowledge of healthy and unhealthy foods, this knowledge rarely translated into healthier dietary behaviors. Instead, food choices were shaped by a combination of perceived barriers, family routines, peer norms, school environments, and media influences. These findings reinforce that knowledge alone is insufficient to drive behavior change and highlight the importance of contextual and structural influences on children’s eating practices.

Within Nutbeam’s framework, students’ nutrition literacy was predominantly functional. Most participants were able to identify unhealthy foods and recognize associated health risks, particularly in relation to processed foods and food safety. However, this understanding was often reduced to simplified “good versus bad” categorizations, and many students relied on parents rather than independently interpreting nutrition information. In this context, opportunities to apply knowledge in social situations were limited, and students demonstrated little engagement with more advanced skills such as evaluating food information or questioning external influences. These patterns are consistent with previous research indicating that children often possess basic nutrition knowledge but lack the higher-order skills required for independent decision-making and the effective translation of knowledge into practice [17, 21–24].

The persistence of the gap between knowledge and behavior can be understood in relation to how children weigh competing influences in their daily lives. Although students were aware of the health risks associated with unhealthy foods, their choices were more strongly shaped by immediate and practical considerations, including taste preferences, convenience, family routines, and peer expectations. In many cases, these factors outweighed the perceived benefits of healthy eating, particularly in situations where children had limited control over food availability or preparation. This pattern supports existing evidence that dietary behaviors are shaped not only by individual awareness but also by broader social and environmental conditions that influence how knowledge is applied in practice [17, 30, 34].

Family and social environments emerged as central influences on both nutrition literacy and dietary practices. Parents played a key role in shaping children’s understanding of food through guidance, role modeling, and control over food availability. However, this influence varied depending on household circumstances, including work schedules and time constraints. While some families supported healthier behaviors through home cooking and active engagement, others relied more heavily on convenience foods, contributing to irregular meal patterns and frequent snack consumption. At the same time, peer dynamics reinforced these behaviors, particularly in school settings where shared consumption of sugary drinks and processed snacks functioned as a form of social bonding. Taken together, these findings highlight the interconnected nature of family and peer influences and align with socioecological perspectives that emphasize the role of multiple interacting contexts in shaping children’s eating behaviors [30, 35–37].

Psychological and media-related influences further contributed to the observed gap between knowledge and behavior. For some students, body image concerns shaped how healthy eating was understood, with food choices linked more closely to appearance than to long-term health. At the same time, exposure to media and digital environments influenced both preferences and perceived norms. While advertising and social media can promote energy-dense foods and appearance-based comparisons, they can also reinforce positive behaviors when healthy eating is socially valued and supported within peer networks. These findings suggest that nutrition literacy should be understood as a socially mediated process, shaped not only by interpersonal relationships but also by broader media environments [34, 36–38].

At a broader level, children’s eating behaviors were embedded within food environments that extend beyond individual choice. In Sabzevar, as in many settings undergoing nutritional transition, economic pressures, urbanization, and changing family structures contribute to increased reliance on affordable, highly processed foods [26, 29, 39–41]. Although policy efforts such as food labeling and school-based initiatives have been introduced, their impact may be limited by low label comprehension and the continued availability of unhealthy options [42, 43]. Importantly, these challenges are not unique to Iran but reflect wider food-system dynamics across low- and middle-income countries, where rapid urbanization, economic constraints, and the widespread availability of inexpensive processed foods similarly shape children’s dietary choices and limit the application of nutrition knowledge in everyday contexts [44]. This underscores the need to situate children’s nutrition literacy within real-world contexts rather than viewing dietary behavior solely as an individual responsibility.

These findings have important implications for intervention design. Approaches to improving children’s diets should move beyond information-based strategies and instead support the development of practical and higher-order skills that enable children to apply their knowledge in everyday contexts. At the same time, interventions must address the environmental and social barriers identified in this study by improving access to healthier food options, reshaping social norms, and strengthening children’s confidence in making food choices. School-based strategies—including improving meal quality, regulating access to energy-dense snacks, and engaging students in hands-on activities such as cooking or gardening, may help bridge the gap between knowledge and practice [19, 43, 45]. Aligning these efforts with parental engagement and supportive home environments is essential to reinforce consistent behaviors. Ultimately, sustained improvements in children’s diets require coordinated, multi-level approaches that connect individual knowledge with supportive social and environmental conditions.

Policy implications

The findings point to the need for multi-level policy approaches to improve children’s nutrition literacy and dietary behaviors. School policies should promote healthier food environments and incorporate practical nutrition education. Family-focused initiatives can support consistent meal routines and informed food choices. At the broader level, policies should strengthen food labeling, regulate the marketing of unhealthy foods to children, and improve access to healthier options. Aligning these efforts can help children translate nutrition knowledge into healthier eating practices.

Limitations and future directions

This study provides in-depth, context-specific insights into children’s nutrition literacy and eating behaviors; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted in a single city with a relatively small sample, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other regions or populations. Second, the cross-sectional design captures participants’ experiences at one point in time and does not allow for examination of how nutrition literacy and dietary behaviors evolve over time.

Future research should adopt longitudinal and multi-site designs to better understand developmental changes in nutrition literacy and variations across socioeconomic and geographic contexts. There is also a need for intervention-based studies that evaluate integrated, multi-level strategies combining individual education with family engagement, school and institutional support, and broader food-environment improvements. Given the growing influence of digital media, further research is warranted to examine how online content, social media, and food marketing shape children’s food perceptions, preferences, and nutrition literacy.

Conclusion

Nutrition literacy among primary school students in this study was predominantly functional, with limited development of interactive and critical competencies. Although children were aware of the health risks associated with unhealthy foods, this awareness did not consistently translate into healthier eating behaviors. Their food choices were shaped by perceived barriers, family routines, peer dynamics, school environments, and broader food system conditions. These findings indicate that improving children’s diets requires more than increasing nutrition knowledge. Effective approaches should include multi-level interventions that strengthen children’s practical and critical skills while creating supportive family, school, and community environments that enable healthy eating to be both achievable and maintainable over time.
