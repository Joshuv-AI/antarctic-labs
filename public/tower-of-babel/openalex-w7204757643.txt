To develop inorganic antiviral materials that do not rely on rare-earth elements, iodate compounds composed of
relatively earth-abundant elements were synthesized and evaluated. In addition, coating textile ﬁbers using their
released ionic species was investigated. Powder samples of Al(IO3)3, Cu(IO3)2, Mn(IO3)2, and Y(IO3)3 (prepared
for comparison) were all conﬁrmed to be single-phase and exhibited a certain solubility in water. Antiviral
activity was evaluated against two types of viruses with distinct characteristics (non-enveloped and enveloped),
following ISO-based protocols. All samples showed high antiviral activity against enveloped viruses, whereas
Y(IO3)3 and Cu(IO3)2 also exhibited high activity against non-enveloped viruses. These results are attributed not
only to the oxidative capability of IO3¹ ions but also to synergistic eﬀects of coexisting cations, including charge
neutralization at the viral surface, protein denaturation, and structural distortion. Furthermore, all materials
were conﬁrmed to exhibit low cytotoxicity. In coating experiments on cotton ﬁbers, aqueous solutions containing
the released ions together with a commercially available PVA-based textile sizing agent were used. Antiviral
performance comparable to that of the corresponding powders was obtained for all samples except Al(IO3)3.
Notably, cotton ﬁbers treated with the coating solution derived from Al(IO3)3 exhibited high antiviral activity
even against non-enveloped viruses, likely attributable to the solution pH. These ﬁndings indicate that iodatebased materials composed of earth-abundant elements—particularly Cu(IO3)2—are promising candidates for
practical antiviral applications, and highlight the importance of ion-mediated mechanisms in coating systems.
Keywords: virus, PVA, Cu, Al, adsorption

1. Introduction
Since the pandemic of coronavirus disease 2019
(COVID-19), the importance of technologies for infection
prevention and mitigation has signiﬁcantly increased.
Against this backdrop, antiviral materials have attracted
considerable attention as a non-vaccine-based approach to
controlling infectious diseases. In particular, inorganic
antiviral materials have recently gained increasing interest
and have been actively studied, owing to their advantage
over organic counterparts in that they are less likely to
induce the emergence of resistant viruses.1,2)
Conventional inorganic antiviral materials include
metal-based systems utilizing nanoparticles or ions of
metals such as silver and copper,3–7) photocatalytic materials typiﬁed by TiO2,8,9) and metal oxides such as ZnO

CaO can cause alkalization of soil and groundwater, thereby limiting their applicability depending on the usage

demand for the development of novel inorganic antiviral

Against this background, we have focused on various
complex oxides and evaluated their antiviral activities,

La2Mo2O913) and £-Ce2Mo3O1314) exhibit high antiviral
activity even under dark conditions. This activity is thought

Ce,16) and strong aﬃnity for electron-donor sites, leading to
adsorption and the induction of structural distortion.17)

On the other hand, iodate ions (IO3¹) contained in iodate
compounds are known to possess a certain degree of oxidative capability.18) Recently, we reported that various rareearth iodates exhibit high antiviral activity both under dark
conditions and under UV irradiation.19,20) It has been clariﬁed that the antiviral activity of these materials is expressed
in the vicinity of the powder surface and is likely associated
with the contact of viruses with a high local concentration
of rare-earth ions and IO3¹ ions released from the powder.
Since IO3¹ can be reduced to iodine by reducing agents
such as glutathione in biological systems,21) it is considered
to exhibit relatively low toxicity to the human body. In fact,
the various rare-earth iodates we previously investigated
were also conﬁrmed to have low cytotoxicity.19)
We have also focused on the high solubility of rare-earth
iodate compounds in water and reported that polymer coatings exhibiting high antiviral activity under dark conditions can be obtained by applying a coating solution prepared by dissolving a water-soluble polymer, poly(vinyl
alcohol) (PVA), in an aqueous solution containing ions
released from these compounds.22) In this coating system,
dissolved species become concentrated at the surface during the drying process, leading to the precipitation of ﬁne
rare-earth iodate particles, thereby enabling the eﬀective
placement of antiviral-active substances on the surface of
the coated substrate.
However, rare-earth elements are more costly than ubiquitous elements and are unevenly distributed globally,
posing risks in terms of supply stability. Therefore, the
development of iodate compounds that do not rely on rareearth elements yet exhibit antiviral activity comparable to
that of rare-earth iodates is highly desirable from the perspective of sustainable materials development.
Among potential applications of antiviral coatings, textiles are particularly important from a public health perspective. Viruses adhering to textile surfaces can remain
viable for a certain period,23,24) and contaminated textiles
can directly transfer viruses to uncontaminated ones upon
contact.25) Therefore, imparting antiviral functionality to
commonly used textiles is an eﬀective approach to reducing viral survival on surfaces and preventing indirect
transmission.
Based on the above considerations, the present study
aimed to synthesize iodate compounds composed of earthabundant elements, rather than rare-earth elements, and to
investigate their antiviral activity. In addition, aqueous
solutions containing ions released from these iodate compounds were applied to textiles to evaluate their eﬀectiveness. In our previous study, coating solutions for glass substrates were prepared by directly adding PVA to aqueous
solutions containing the leached ions. In contrast, in this
study, with a greater emphasis on practical applicability to
textiles, a commercially available PVA-based sizing agent
for textiles was employed. The eﬀectiveness was evaluated by combining the aqueous solutions containing ions
released from the prepared samples with the PVA-based
textile sizing agent and applying the resulting formulation
to cotton fabrics under standard conditions.

In this study, Al, Cu, and Mn were selected as target
elements. Aluminum is inexpensive and forms trivalent
cations in solution, similar to rare-earth ions. Copper is
well known to exhibit antiviral activity in its metallic,
oxide, and ionic forms.5–7,26,27) Manganese is known to
preferentially coordinate with acidic amino acid residues
such as aspartic acid and glutamic acid in proteins,28) suggesting that similar interactions may occur with viral surface proteins. For comparison, iodate compounds of Y, a
rare-earth element that has not been previously investigated in this context, were also examined.

2. Experimental
2.1

Preparation of powder samples

Yttrium iodate [Y(IO3)3], aluminum iodate [Al(IO3)3],
copper(II) iodate [Cu(IO3)2], and manganese(II) iodate
[Mn(IO3)2] were synthesized as described below.
Yttrium iodate was synthesized by adding a solution of
6 mmol iodic acid (HIO3) in 15 mL of distilled water to
a solution of 2 mmol yttrium(III) nitrate hexahydrate
[Y(NO3)3·6H2O, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp.,
Osaka, Japan] in 5 mL of distilled water. The mixture was
stirred at room temperature for 1 h. The resulting precipitate was collected by vacuum ﬁltration, washed, and dried
at 80 °C overnight. The dried product was ground, calcined at 400 °C for 2 h, and re-ground.
Aluminum iodate was prepared by adding a solution of
24 mmol iodic acid in 22.5 mL of distilled water to a solution of 8 mmol aluminum nitrate nonahydrate [Al(NO3)3·
9H2O, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka,
Japan] in 7.5 mL of distilled water. The mixture was stirred
at room temperature for 1 h, then transferred to a Teﬂonlined 50 mL autoclave vessel and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 200 °C for 5 h. The resulting precipitate
was collected by vacuum ﬁltration, washed, dried at 80 °C
overnight, and ground.
Copper(II) iodate was synthesized by adding a solution
of 4 mmol iodic acid in 10 mL of distilled water to a solution of 2 mmol copper(II) nitrate trihydrate [Cu(NO3)2·
3H2O, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka,
Japan] in 5 mL of distilled water. The mixture was stirred
at room temperature for 1 h. The resulting precipitate was
collected by vacuum ﬁltration, washed, and dried at 80 °C
overnight. The dried product was ground, calcined at
300 °C for 2 h, and re-ground.
Manganese(II) iodate was synthesized by diluting a
manganese(II) nitrate solution [Mn(NO3)2, 48–52 wt %,
Sigma-Aldrich Co. LLC, St. Louis, MO, USA] to obtain a
solution containing 8 mmol Mn2+ in 15 mL, which was
then added to a solution of 16 mmol iodic acid in 30 mL of
distilled water. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 1 h. The resulting precipitate was collected by
vacuum ﬁltration, washed, dried at 80 °C overnight, and
ground.
The microstructures of the obtained powders were observed using a ﬁeld-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; JSM-7500F, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

2.3

Antiviral activity tests

Antiviral activity of the powder samples was evaluated
by a ﬁlm adhesion method with reference to ISO 18061.
Viruses are classiﬁed into two types according to their
structural characteristics: non-enveloped viruses, whose
surfaces consist of a thick protein shell called a capsid, and
enveloped viruses, which possess a lipid bilayer membrane
(envelope) surrounding a thinner capsid. In this study,
bacteriophage Q¢ (NBRC 20012; hereafter referred to as
Q¢) was used as a model non-enveloped virus, while bacteriophage ¯6 (NBRC 105899; hereafter referred to as
¯6) was used as a model enveloped virus. Escherichia coli
(NBRC 106373) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (NBRC
14084) were used as host bacteria for Q¢ and ¯6, respectively. The experimental procedures and conditions followed those reported previously.13,19) Antiviral activity
was quantiﬁed as ¹log(N/N0), where N0 is the number of
viral plaques on a control glass substrate without the
sample, and N is the number of plaques on the substrate
with the sample after 6 h of incubation. All measurements
were performed in triplicate, and mean values and standard deviations were calculated from these measurements.
In addition, catalase inactivation tests30) and cytotoxicity
assays31) were conducted for the powder samples according to previously reported methods.
Antiviral activity of the PVA-based sizing agent-coated
cotton fabrics was evaluated with reference to ISO 18184.
The coated cotton fabrics were cut into pieces of approximately 1 cm © 1 cm, and 0.4 g of samples was placed in a
50 mL centrifuge tube. A 200 ¯L aliquot of virus suspension (2 © 107 pfu/mL) was then dropped onto the samples
to ensure full penetration of the fabric. The tubes were
sealed and kept at room temperature in the dark for 6 h.
After incubation, 20 mL of SCDLP broth (E-MC72; Eiken
Chemical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was added to the tube,
and the virus was eluted from the fabric by manual shaking
(20 strokes), yielding a recovered solution. Subsequently,
plaques were formed on LB agar plates following the ﬁlm
adhesion method, and the number of surviving viruses was
calculated from plaque counts and dilution factors. Prior to
testing, all fabric samples were sterilized by UV irradiation
on both sides for 15 min. As a control, fabrics coated only
with the PVA-based sizing agent were used.

Figure 4 presents the results of the antiviral activity test
evaluated using the ﬁlm adhesion method. The vertical
axis is deﬁned as ¹log(N/N0), where an increase of 1 unit
corresponds to a tenfold reduction in viral plaques. Thus,
higher values indicate stronger antiviral activity. The use
of logarithmic values for plaque counts is a standard and
widely accepted approach in antiviral studies. In particular,
when evaluations are conducted in accordance with ISO
methods, as in this study, logarithmic representation is required. Even if the speciﬁc surface area (i.e., the reaction
ﬁeld) diﬀers by one order of magnitude (a factor of 10), the
resulting change in ¹log(N/N0) is only 1, provided that the
intrinsic surface activity is identical. Against Q¢, Y(IO3)3
and Cu(IO3)2 exhibited high antiviral activity, inactivating
the virus to below the detection limit. In contrast, Al(IO3)3
and Mn(IO3)2 exhibited lower activity, with less than a
one-log reduction in viral plaques. For ¯6, all powder
samples inactivated the virus to the detection limit or to
near-detection-limit levels.
Non-enveloped viruses such as Q¢ have genomes enclosed within a thick protein shell known as a capsid. In
contrast, enveloped viruses such as ¯6 possess a thin capsid surrounded by a ﬂexible lipid bilayer envelope containing phosphate groups, with functional spike proteins
protruding from its surface. Disruption or damage of this
envelope readily leads to viral inactivation.36,37) In general,
non-enveloped viruses are more resistant to environmental
changes and chemical stimuli than enveloped viruses.
Because the iodate compounds prepared in this study
exhibited relatively high solubility, their antiviral activity
is likely governed by dissolved ionic species. The ion668

leached solutions of all samples exhibited acidic pH.
Although Q¢ and ¯6 are known to show decreased survival under acidic conditions,38,39) the eﬀects of solutions
in the pH range of 4.0–5.0 on their inactivation have not
been systematically investigated. Therefore, to evaluate
the eﬀect of low pH, solutions in this pH range were prepared using an acetic acid/sodium acetate buﬀer, and antiviral tests were conducted using the dissolved ion contact
method (see Supporting Information for details).13,19,31)

the pH range of 4.0–5.0. This suggests that the high antiviral activity of Y(IO3)3 and Cu(IO3)2 against Q¢ is not
primarily due to pH, but rather to the intrinsic properties
of the dissolved ions. In contrast, for ¯6, ¹log(N/N0) was

to the antiviral activity of Y(IO3)3 against ¯6 [pH: 5.04,

The results of the catalase inactivation test are shown in
Fig. SI-3. Catalase is an enzyme that possesses an active
center composed of an Fe–porphyrin, where nearby residues such as histidine regulate the reaction through coordination and proton transfer.40) In enzymes such as catalase,
even slight structural perturbations near the active center
generally lead to a decrease in activity.
In the samples prepared in this study, the catalase inactivation activity was highest for Cu(IO3)2, followed by
Y(IO3)3, then Al(IO3)3, and lowest for Mn(IO3)2; thus, the
order is Cu(IO3)2 > Y(IO3)3 > Al(IO3)3 > Mn(IO3)2. On
the other hand, the order of the amount of iodate ions
eluted into the 1/500 NB aqueous solution in this study
was Y(IO3)3 > Mn(IO3)2 > Cu(IO3)2 > Al(IO3)3, while
the concentration of metal ions followed the order
Mn(IO3)2 > Cu(IO3)2 > Y(IO3)3 > Al(IO3)3. The pH was
generally in the range of 4–5, with the order Y(IO3)3 >
Al(IO3)3 > Cu(IO3)2 > Mn(IO3)2.
The catalase inactivation tests were conducted using a
commercial kit in a low-concentration (³0.01 %) NaCl
aqueous solution. Although there are diﬀerences in solvent composition, all conditions correspond to low ionic
strength, and the order of elution behavior is essentially
governed by the properties of the solids; therefore, it is
unlikely to change signiﬁcantly. In the samples prepared in
this study, the order of catalase inactivation activity does
not correlate with the order of eluted ion amounts or pH,
suggesting that these factors are unlikely to be dominant.
Instead, the diﬀerences in binding strength and persistence
of metal ions toward catalase are considered to be responsible for the diﬀerences in inactivation eﬃciency, which
has been demonstrated in various studies.41)
Copper(II) ions can coordinate relatively strongly to
histidine and cysteine residues and can further interact
with aspartic acid and glutamic acid residues, thereby inﬂuencing the structure and coordination environment near
the active center.42–44) In contrast, Mn2+ can also coordinate to these residues, but its interactions are generally

weaker and less selective. This diﬀerence arises because
Cu2+, with its d9 electronic conﬁguration, beneﬁts from
ligand ﬁeld stabilization and Jahn–Teller distortion, enabling the formation of strong, directional covalent interactions. On the other hand, Mn2+, in a high-spin d5 conﬁguration, has essentially zero ligand ﬁeld stabilization
energy, resulting in weaker bonding and faster ligand
exchange.
Because Y3+ and Al3+ have no electrons in their d orbitals, ligand ﬁeld stabilization energy does not arise. Within
the framework of HSAB theory,45) they are classiﬁed as
hard acids, and their interactions with catalase are primarily electrostatic in nature. Although both ions are trivalent,
Al3+ has a smaller ionic radius and therefore a higher
charge density, which stabilizes its hydration shell and
makes coordination by protein ligands more diﬃcult. In
contrast, Y3+ has a larger ionic radius, resulting in weaker
hydration, allowing protein ligands to approach more
easily and enabling a higher coordination number. Based
on these considerations, the order of binding strength and
persistence of metal ions toward catalase is inferred to be
Cu2+ > Y3+ > Al3+ > Mn2+, which accounts for the results obtained in this study. It should also be noted that
catalase contains few disulﬁde bonds, and such bonds are
not present at sites functioning as the active center.
Next, taking these points into account, the mechanism
of virus inactivation by released ions is discussed from
perspectives other than pH. A non-enveloped RNA virus,
Q¢ has a capsid consisting of a structure formed by the
cooperative assembly of numerous protein subunits.46) For
such a multi-subunit structure, Cu2+ can bind in a multivalent manner to surface residues such as histidine, cysteine, aspartic acid, and glutamic acid, thereby inducing
local structural constraints and distortions over a wide
area. In particular, with respect to cysteine residues, Cu2+
can contribute to the disruption of disulﬁde bonds through
interactions with thiol groups and redox reactions. As
these eﬀects accumulate, the cooperative interactions
among subunits collapse, and once a certain threshold is
exceeded, the overall stability of the capsid is rapidly
lost.47) Subsequent dissociation of subunits and exposure
of the internal genome lead to rapid inactivation.
Manganese(II) ions are hard acids and interact weakly
with sulfur, which is a soft base; therefore, their interaction
with disulﬁde bonds is limited. In addition, its coordination bonds are generally weak and ligand exchange is fast,
preventing the formation of multivalent and persistent
structural constraints. As a result, its impact on the capsid
structure is limited, making it less likely to induce cooperative collapse. The diﬀerence between Y3+ and Al3+ is
attributed to diﬀerences in their eﬀective accessibility to
viral components. Aluminum(III) ions form strongly hydrated and kinetically inert aqua complexes in water,
which limit direct interactions with viral proteins and
RNA. In contrast, Y3+ exhibits more labile coordination
behavior, enabling binding to the viral surface. In addition,
due to its larger ionic radius, Y3+ can achieve higher coordination numbers with oxygen (electron) donor groups

such as carboxylates, peptide oxygens, and phosphates,
facilitating multidentate bridging interactions and thereby
enhancing its eﬀect. This is considered to account for the
high activity of Y(IO3)3. The relatively low activity of
Al(IO3)3 may also be inﬂuenced by its slower ion release
rate (Fig. 3).
In addition, the surface of Q¢ is negatively charged near
neutral pH.48,49) Cations released from iodate compounds
are considered to adsorb onto the capsid via electrostatic
interactions. In fact, rare earth ions such as Y3+ have been
reported to adsorb onto the surface of non-enveloped
viruses and neutralize the negative charge of the capsid.16)
Furthermore, Al3+ has been shown to induce aggregation
of non-enveloped viruses at lower concentrations than
divalent cations under acidic conditions.50) Moreover, as
described above, Cu2+ and Mn2+ also have the ability to
form coordination bonds with acidic amino acids present
in the Q¢ capsid. Through these eﬀects, the negative
charge of Q¢ is neutralized, allowing the anionic species
IO3¹ to more easily approach the capsid. The structural
stability of the capsid is maintained by disulﬁde bonds
between cysteine residues,43,51) and IO3¹ may oxidatively
modify sulfur-containing amino acid residues, thereby impairing the stability of disulﬁde bonds.52,53) Under acidic
conditions, IO3¹ exhibits oxidizing power as represented
by the following half-reaction:18)

Taken together, the diﬀerences in coordination properties among ions appear as linear diﬀerences in activity for
catalase, which has a single active center, whereas for Q¢,

on a logarithmic scale.
Although the inactivation of ¯6 is largely attributed to
pH eﬀects, contributions from coexisting ions may also
play a role. The envelope surrounding ¯6 contains phosphate groups,56) and its surface is negatively charged under
neutral to mildly acidic conditions.57) Cations such as Y3+,
Al3+, and Cu2+ have a strong aﬃnity for phosphate species58–62) and are therefore expected to adsorb preferentially onto phosphate groups on the envelope surface.
Similar to Q¢, adsorption of these cations may neutralize
the negative surface charge of the virus, facilitating the
approach of iodate ions (IO3¹) to the envelope. In addition, these cations have been reported to induce structural
changes in lipid membranes,63–65) suggesting that their direct interaction with the membrane may also contribute to
viral inactivation. Once in close proximity to the viral
surface, IO3¹ may oxidize the lipids constituting the envelope, leading to structural destabilization of the membrane
and subsequent inactivation of ¯6.66) Furthermore, spike

The diﬀerence in cation release behavior between fabrics treated with Cu(IO3)2 and those treated with Y(IO3)3
can be attributed to diﬀerences in the interaction strength
between cellulose, the main component of cotton fabrics,
and the respective metal cations. According to Pearson’s

borderline acid and therefore exhibits relatively weak
interactions with hard bases, which likely promotes its
release from the ﬁbers. On the other hand, Y3+ is classiﬁed as a hard acid and therefore has a strong aﬃnity for
hydroxyl groups in cellulose. As a result, for Y(IO3)3, ion
release is slower, which likely leads to reduced antiviral
activity. Based on these results, Cu(IO3)2 can be considered a promising inorganic antiviral material among iodate
compounds composed of earth-abundant elements.
Cotton fabrics treated with the coating solution prepared
from Al(IO3)3 exhibited very high antiviral activity against
Q¢, reaching the quantiﬁcation limit [¹log(N/N0) = 5.5].
Although the origin of this behavior remains unclear, one
possible explanation is the role of hydrated aluminum species under acidic conditions. In aqueous solutions, aluminum exists as various hydrolyzed species, including mononuclear ions such as AlOH2+, Al(OH)2+, and Al(OH)4¹,
as well as polynuclear species such as Al2(OH)24+,
Al3(OH)45+, and Al13O4(OH)247+.72,73) While Al(OH)4¹
predominates under alkaline conditions, multiple species—including both mono- and polynuclear cations—
coexist in acidic to weakly acidic solutions. In particular,
at around pH μ 3, corresponding to the present sizing
solution, these species are expected to coexist to a signiﬁcant extent.72) Among these, the polynuclear cations
[e.g., Al2(OH)24+, Al3(OH)45+, and Al13O4(OH)247+] are
highly charged oxo/hydroxo clusters that behave as strong
hard acids. These species can electrostatically attract the
hard base IO3¹ and potentially form outer-sphere ion-pair
assemblies, in which iodate ions are arranged around the
polynuclear cation cores. Such assemblies may locally
concentrate IO3¹ near the virus surface, thereby enhancing
multivalent oxidative interactions with capsid proteins. As

veriﬁcation of this proposed mechanism is highly challenging, and further investigations, including computational approaches, are required.
The PVA-based sizing agent used in this study is originally intended for use in starching garments such as dress
shirts and lab coats after washing. Typically, it is applied
by immersing the fabric in a solution of the sizing agent
after a standard wash, followed by drying. Since the product is based on PVA, it is expected to be largely removed
by washing with water (i.e., it has little to no washing
durability). Therefore, it is assumed that the same sizing
treatment would be reapplied after each wash, which is
also consistent with the intended use of this product.
Regarding resistance to friction and long-term use, this
is expected to depend on the magnitude and duration of the
applied stress; however, as long as the surface coating
remains, a certain level of antiviral activity should be
maintained. On the other hand, since ion elution contributes to the eﬀect, factors such as perspiration and humidity
are also expected to have an inﬂuence.

4. Conclusions
This study was conducted to develop inorganic antiviral
materials that do not rely on resource-limited rare-earth

elements. Iodate compounds composed of earth-abundant
elements were synthesized and evaluated, and their applicability to coating technologies using eluted ions was
examined. All synthesized powder samples—Y(IO3)3,
Al(IO3)3, Cu(IO3)2, and Mn(IO3)2—exhibited high antiviral activity against the enveloped virus ¯6. For the nonenveloped virus Q¢, Y(IO3)3 and Cu(IO3)2 showed high
antiviral performance. These results suggest that, in addition to the oxidative eﬀect of iodate ions, synergistic contributions from coexisting cations, such as charge neutralization at the virus surface and protein denaturation, play
important roles in viral inactivation. All materials exhibited low cytotoxicity toward living cells. In coating systems using aqueous solutions containing eluted ions in
combination with a commercial PVA-based textile sizing
agent, the aﬃnity between cellulose (the substrate) and
metal cations was found to aﬀect antiviral activity. The
virus inactivation behavior generally followed trends similar to those of the powder samples, except for Al(IO3)3.
Among the tested materials, Cu(IO3)2 emerged as a promising candidate for inorganic antiviral applications. Cotton fabrics treated with coating solutions derived from
Al(IO3)3 exhibited high antiviral activity against Q¢, and
this behavior is likely inﬂuenced by the pH of the coating
solution.
