Hydrogen peroxide on Mars and icy moons: Implications for the search for extraterrestrial signatures of prebiotic chemistry
Authors: Mang Lin, Ruiqin Yi, Albert C. Fahrenbach, Henderson James Cleaves, Yi-Gang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is increasingly recognized as an important molecule in prebiotic chemistry that potentially had a large impact on life’s early evolution. We highlight recent examples of H2O2 in prebiotic syntheses that model potential protometabolic reactions. Recent discoveries have revealed multiple abiotic production mechanisms for H2O2, suggesting its prevalence in prebiotic and extraterrestrial environments, an idea that is supported by the detection of H2O2 in the Martian atmosphere and on the surfaces of icy moons. Given that Earth’s active geological processes have erased records of the earliest prebiotic chemical processes, we hypothesize that extraterrestrial environments that produce H2O2 such as Mars and icy moons may still have similar types of chemistry occurring, and the discovery of such reactions by advanced space missions would offer invaluable insights into prebiotic chemistry. To test our hypothesis, experimental and theoretical studies aimed at modeling prebiotic chemistry in these H2O2-producing environments are required. We propose the general principle of “Where There is Water, There is H2O2” as an astrobiology guideline that supplements and refines the traditional “Follow the Water” approach used to plan space missions.

Background: H2O2 and life’s early evolution

Humanity has long wondered about the origins of life on Earth and whether life exists somewhere beyond. It is widely accepted that life on Earth likely emerged under anoxic conditions between 4.4 and 3.8 billion years ago [1]. The subsequent evolution of oxygenic photosynthesis largely led to the accumulation of atmospheric O2, paving the way for the rise of more complex aerobic life [2]. The strong oxidizing power of O2 presents a paradox: early life would have needed to develop biochemical mechanisms to cope with, or even harness, the damaging effects of O2 before or during the emergence of oxygenic photosynthesis. The evolution of antioxidant systems likely preceded the advent of oxygen production, ensuring that early oxygen-producing organisms, such as cyanobacteria, were not completely stymied by their own metabolic products.

One molecule that could have provided a selection pressure for the evolution of these antioxidant systems is hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). In modern aerobic organisms, H2O2 is a natural byproduct of cellular aerobic metabolism, and its relatively weak O–O bond makes it prone to homolytic cleavage, yielding highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (·OH). To prevent oxidative damage, enzymes such as catalases (CATs) and peroxiredoxins (PXs) convert H2O2 into harmless products, with CATs producing water (H2O) and O2 and PXs producing H2O, all without generating ·OH. CATs and PXs occur in organisms across all three domains of life (Archaea, Bacteria, and Eukarya), indicating that antioxidant defenses are not confined to aerobes but also function broadly in anaerobes. Indeed, recent surveys reveal that approximately 93% of anaerobic organisms encode at least one of these antioxidant enzymes, suggesting that a primitive antioxidant toolkit was likely present in the last universal common ancestor (LUCA) [3]. Beyond its reputation as a cell-damaging oxidant, H2O2 is now recognized as a biologically important molecule, serving as a key redox signaling agent in eukaryotes and even as a respiratory substrate in certain prokaryotes via cytochrome c peroxidase [4,5]. Therefore, H2O2 likely played a significant role in early biochemical evolution due to its dual nature as both a toxin and a metabolically useful molecule, motivating studies aimed at understanding its potential abiotic production pathways on the early Earth. Early modeling studies of H2O photolysis and radiolysis investigated the potential for global accumulation of H2O2 on the early Earth [[6], [7], [8]]. Recent experimental works show that additional pathways, such as H2O splitting driven by mineral surface radicals and reactions within microdroplets, could have produced substantial H2O2 in localized environments [[9], [10], [11]].

Given H2O2’s involvement in ancient biochemical pathways and its availability in early Earth environments, it has been suggested that abiotically produced H2O2 had a major influence on the evolution of early life, particularly regarding the origin of oxygenic photosynthesis [8,9,12]. In oxygenic photosynthesis, H2O is oxidized to O2. This reaction is thermodynamically unfavorable due to the high midpoint reduction potential (Em) of the H2O/O2 couple (0.82 V at pH = 7 and 25 °C, relative to the normal hydrogen electrode) [12]. In the anoxic environment of early Earth, more thermodynamically favorable electron donors (e.g., Fe2+, S2−) were abundant, raising the question why H2O, a poor electron donor, was ultimately employed by early organisms. Calculations suggest that atmospheric H2O2 production and deposition contributed to Fe2+ oxidation in Eoarchean seawater, although this flux alone was insufficient to account for complete Fe2+ removal [13]. If more prebiotic processes consistently produced H2O2 and other reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as ·OH over time, these oxidizing molecules may have depleted these other available electron donors and pressured early life to develop mechanisms to utilize H2O as an electron donor, at least in localized settings [9,10]. Nevertheless, this hypothesis remains speculative and requires further testing. Moreover, H2O2 itself can serve as an electron donor. Its oxidation to O2 (Em = 0.27 V at pH = 7 and 25 °C, relative to the normal hydrogen electrode) is within the oxidative capabilities of reaction centers from extant anoxygenic photosynthetic bacteria [12]. It has been proposed that a primitive system combining an anoxygenic photosynthetic reaction center with a Mn-based catalase could have evolved to produce O2 using H2O2 as the source of electrons, with the di-manganese center of the catalase subsequently evolving into the tetra-manganese cluster capable of oxidizing water [12]. However, direct experimental and observational evidence remains lacking.

Hypothesis: H2O2-driven prebiotic chemistry

Building on the discussion of H2O2’s significance in early biological evolution, we now take this reasoning a step further by hypothesizing that H2O2 also played a critical role in the origin of life itself. The presence of H2O2-scavenging enzymes in anaerobes, and potentially in LUCA, along with its utilization in ancient respiratory pathways, suggests that H2O2 was an important environmental oxidant on early Earth. This likelihood motivates the hypothesis that H2O2 may also have contributed to prebiotic chemical networks preceding cellular life.

In a series of papers [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]], Ball and Brindley proposed the “H2O2 crucible hypothesis”, suggesting that life may have originated in a localized early Earth environment continuously supplied with abiotic H2O2. This setting, they argue, could sustain far-from-equilibrium thermal and pH oscillations driven by the production and decomposition of H2O2. Herein, we propose that H2O2 also played a broader role as a significant oxidant in prebiotic chemistry, particularly in the context of redox reactions associated with prebiotic or protometabolic pathways, where it may have facilitated chemical transformations in the absence of enzymes or molecular oxygen.

Under prebiotically plausible conditions (Fig. 1, Protometabolism), H2O2 has been shown to function as a chemical oxidant for glyoxylate [19], facilitating key steps in a nonenzymatic analogue of the citric acid cycle, a crucial part of core metabolism widely suggested to have prebiotic origins. Additionally, H2O2 is capable of generating hydroxide ions (OH−) in reactions catalyzed by membrane-bound, iron-bearing peptides, offering a plausible mechanism for the formation of pH gradients across protocell membranes [20]. ROS associated with H2O2 (e.g., ·OH) can also afford oxidative transformations in a variety of prebiotic biomolecule syntheses. For example, ROS generated by gamma-ray irradiation have been shown to drive the abiotic synthesis of amino acids (Fig. 1, Amino acids), offering a plausible pathway for their formation in meteorite parent bodies [21]. In another example (Fig. 1, Sugars), the non-selective oxidation of gluconate via the Fenton reaction, where ferrous iron reacts with H2O2 to generate ·OH, has been shown to yield pentose sugars under a mechanism resembling a nonenzymatic pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), suggesting a plausible abiotic route for sugar formation relevant to early carbohydrate metabolism [22].Fig. 1Graphical illustration showing planetary bodies (not to scale) where H2O2has been detected, the major production pathways of H2O2, and its role in a few examples of prebiotic chemical networks.Fig 1 dummy alt text

Beyond radiation-induced ROS, growing attention is being directed toward the formation of ROS and H2O2 in the surface layer of microdroplets and its implications for prebiotic chemistry. A recent study demonstrates the spontaneous prebiotic synthesis of urea from carbon dioxide (CO2) and ammonia in microdroplets [23]. Other prebiotically relevant reactions facilitated by microdroplet chemistry include CO2 fixation [24], sugar phosphorylation [25], and peptide bond formation [26], highlighting the role of these microenvironments in promoting key reduction and dehydration processes. However, ROS- or H2O2-induced oxidative transformations relevant to prebiotic chemistry within microdroplets remain largely underexplored and warrant further investigations. Recent findings have suggested that ROS in microdroplets can oxidize molecular nitrogen into reactive nitrogen oxides, offering a plausible abiotic pathway for nitrogen fixation [27].

In a chemically complex system, ROS and H2O2 may further engage in secondary reactions with anions or organic compounds, generating additional radicals and enabling further chemical transformations. For example, ·OH can oxidize chloride (Cl−) to produce chlorine radicals (Cl·) that react with HCN, a possible pathway for the radiolytic production of cyanogen chloride (ClCN), which is a proposed key intermediate in the synthesis of nucleotides and their activated forms [28,29]. Taken together, these experimental results underscore the multifaceted roles that H2O2 and its associated reactive species may have played in shaping early prebiotic pathways. The potential contributions of H2O2 to prebiotic chemical evolution deserve continued, rigorous scientific evaluation.

H2O2 on Mars and icy moons: opportunities for tracing prebiotic signatures

Observational evidence is essential to test the hypothesis of H2O2-driven prebiotic chemistry, which has thus far been supported primarily by experimental studies. Terrestrial samples are unlikely to preserve detailed records of the prebiotic chemistry that occurred >4 billion years ago, as Earth’s active geological processes have largely erased these ancient chemical signatures. However, if similar chemistry is occurring now in extraterrestrial environments rich in H2O2, these bodies could serve as chemical analogues of past Earth geochemistry, potentially offering us a unique window into the processes that paved the way for life. We propose that Mars and the icy moons are promising targets for such investigations, given the widespread presence of H2O2 on these planetary bodies (Fig. 1).

H2O2 in the Martian atmosphere has been detected remotely from Earth in the sub-millimeter wavelength range using the James Clerk Maxwell Telescope (JCMT) at Mauna Kea, Hawaii [30]. Its spatial distribution and seasonal cycle have been further mapped using ground-based imaging spectroscopy in the thermal infrared range with the Texas Echelon Cross Echelle Spectrograph (TEXES) on the Infrared Telescope Facility (IRTF), also located at Mauna Kea [31,32]. These observations reveal that H2O2 mixing ratios in the Martian atmosphere are highly variable, ranging from ∼0 to ∼45 ppbv, thereby providing important observational constraints to refine our understanding of Martian atmospheric chemistry and improving modeling capabilities.

H2O2 has also been detected on icy moons. On Europa, H2O2 was first detected by the Near-Infrared Mapping Spectrometer (NIMS) onboard the Galileo spacecraft [33]. The absorption feature at 3.5 μm observed by NIMS was attributed to H2O2, with an estimated concentration of 0.13% ± 0.07% relative to H2O in the anti-Jovian region of the leading hemisphere. This detection was later corroborated by the Near-InfraRed SPECtrograph (NIRSPEC) on the Keck telescope, which also identified the 3.5 μm feature [34]. Although Cassini’s Visual and Infrared Mapping Spectrometer (VIMS) arguably detected H2O2 on the icy moon Enceladus, the observation remains uncertain due to potential interference from CO2 on the weak 3.5 μm band [35,36]. More recently, the NIRSPEC Integral Field Unit on the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has afforded a clear detection of the H2O2 3.5 μm band on Ganymede, a moon of Jupiter, and Charon, a moon of Pluto [37,38], whereas JWST observations have not yet detected H2O2 on another Jovian moon Callisto [39], despite earlier indications of its possible presence [40].

The presence of H2O2 on these extraterrestrial bodies is closely linked to the processing of water/ice by high-energy particles, such as Jovian charged particles, solar ultraviolet radiation, interplanetary Lyman-α photons, solar wind, and galactic cosmic rays [10,[33], [34], [35], [36], [37]]. Experiments show that adding CO2 to simulations of radiolytic H2O2 production on icy moons enhances H2O2 yields and organic synthesis [41]. CO2, known as a major component in atmospheres of early Earth and Mars, may have similarly promoted H2O2 production and organic synthesis. The potential connection between newly proposed mechanisms, such as H2O splitting driven by mineral surface radicals and microdroplet chemistry [10,11], and H2O2 production in extraterrestrial environments remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Planetary processes such as marsquakes, icy moonquakes, and tidal strains have been suggested to fracture planetary crusts, creating permeable subsurface environments that may be habitable [42]. It is plausible that H2O splitting via mineral surface radicals could occur when these fractured subsurface environments become hydrated, as may have happened on early Mars [10].

Although extraterrestrial observations of H2O2 remain relatively scarce, its potential role in life’s early evolution on Earth, coupled with the growing evidence for the coexistence of H2O and H2O2 in potentially habitable environments on Mars and icy moons, underscore promising opportunities to trace prebiotic signatures beyond Earth. For example, an H2O2-driven sugar synthesis pathway has been proposed [22], potentially linked to the emergence of early carbohydrate metabolism. Consistent with this, structurally related compounds such as gluconate and other sugar acids have been identified in carbonaceous meteorites [43]. Whether these biologically relevant classes of compounds are also present on Mars or icy moons remains to be seen, but future missions could be designed to specifically search for key molecules related to H2O2-driven prebiotic chemistry (see discussion in Section 2). Given that the physicochemical conditions on Mars and icy moons share certain features with early Earth [e.g., the presence of liquid water (at least transiently), active water-rock interactions, and redox gradients], similar prebiotic processes could plausibly have operated in these extraterrestrial settings. Nevertheless, those environments also differ in temperature regimes, radiation fluxes, and geochemical context. Therefore, further experimental simulations under planetary-relevant conditions are required to assess whether H2O2-driven pathways could proceed with comparable efficiency and chemical selectivity. Such efforts may help identify the most promising target molecules for future missions and ultimately clarify which chemical routes may have occurred in extraterrestrial environments or early Earth and contributed to prebiotic chemistry.

Challenges and outlook

Despite the growing evidence that H2O2 may have contributed to protometabolism and is present across Mars and icy moons, fundamental questions remain unresolved. One key uncertainty concerns the large disparity between trace abundances of H2O2 observed in planetary surface environments (e.g., ppbv levels in the Martian atmosphere) [31,32] and the much higher concentrations commonly employed in laboratory studies to drive prebiotic reactions at measurable rates (e.g., wt% levels) [19,22]. Resolving this discrepancy will require improved observational constraints on planetary processes capable of generating transient or persistent H2O2-rich microenvironments and the concentrations achievable within them, together with experimental and numerical investigations that quantify prebiotic reaction kinetics under realistic planetary conditions.

Another challenge is the “double-edged sword” nature of H2O2, which can both facilitate protometabolic reactions and degrade key organic molecules within prebiotic reaction networks. In this respect, the role of H2O2 resembles the long-standing “water paradox” in prebiotic chemistry–water is essential for life, yet it also promotes the hydrolysis of many prebiotically relevant compounds [44]. One possible solution is that prebiotic reactions occurred within confined or nanostructured microenvironments, where chemical behavior may differ substantially from that in bulk aqueous solutions [45]. Furthermore, H2O2 may degrade potential signatures of prebiotic chemistry. In open planetary systems, however, the sites of organic synthesis and preservation need not coincide. Organic compounds generated in H2O2-rich environments may be transported to more favorable settings for accumulation and preservation. It is also possible that some macromolecular products are intrinsically more resistant to oxidative degradation and therefore represent more promising targets for detection [46]. We acknowledge, however, that physically plausible scenarios reconciling H2O2-driven synthesis with long-term organic preservation remain largely unexplored, warranting further theoretical, experimental, and observational investigation.

To fully understand the role of H2O2 in astrobiology, we first encourage planetary scientists to pay particular attention to environments where production, degradation, transport, and preservation processes may become decoupled in space and time. Second, future missions must develop robust capabilities to detect H2O2 in such environments through remote observations and direct measurements from flyby, lander and rover missions. These efforts help constrain the distribution of H2O2 and identify processes that generate transient or persistent H2O2-rich microenvironments. In parallel, geochemists should design laboratory experiments and numerical simulations to elucidate the underlying planetary chemistry of H2O2, including source and sink reactions. Last but not the least, prebiotic chemists should further investigate H2O2 concentration thresholds required to support substantial prebiotic organic synthesis and accumulation under geochemically and physically realistic planetary conditions, not just those convenient to laboratory timescales. Together, these efforts across multiple disciplines are crucial to understanding how our evolving knowledge of H2O2 chemistry can guide astrobiological exploration, given that H2O2 likely had a significant influence on prebiotic chemistry and life’s early evolution. In summary, we propose a heuristic principle of “Where There is Water, There is H2O2”, as a guiding idea that supplements and may ultimately refine the traditional “Follow the Water” strategy in astrobiological exploration.
