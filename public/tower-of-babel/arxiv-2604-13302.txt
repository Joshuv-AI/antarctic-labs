A simple approach to the Løkka-Zervos dichotomy for absolutely continuous dividend strategies
Tommy Mastromonaco, Nacer Fendri, Jean-François Renaud, Clarence Simard

Abstract
. We revisit the optimization problem solved in [ 10 ] , i.e., the maximization of dividends, in a Brownian risk model, with the possibility (not the obligation) of making capital injections. Following the approach introduced in [ 1 , 15 , 14 ] , we consider instead absolutely continuous (AC) dividend strategies with an affine bound on the payment rates, while singular capital injections are still allowed. In addition, we incorporate a parameter for the cost of ruin or, said differently, a penalty at ruin in the performance function. We show that the solution is a so-called Løkka-Zervos dichotomy: the surplus is never ruined by making bail-out payments, or no capital is injected and bankruptcy can occur; in either case, dividends are paid at full rate when the surplus is above a threshold. Our framework allows us to provide explicit conditions to express the dichotomy, either using the cost of capital injections or the cost of ruin as a criterion, which also exposes the underlying structure of the solution. In particular, for some values of the parameters, we show that it is optimal to liquidate . Moreover, we perform a numerical analysis highlighting the range of values generated under this AC affine-bound structure.



1.
Introduction

We revisit the optimization problem solved in
[
10
]
, i.e., the maximization of dividends, in a Brownian risk model, with the possibility (not the obligation) of making capital injections. As the main focus is on the maximization of dividends, we follow the approach introduced in
[
1
,
15
]
and we consider absolutely continuous dividend strategies with an affine bound on the payment rates, while singular capital injections are allowed. To further highlight the trade-off between the maximization of dividend payments and the minimization of both the cost of declaring ruin and/or making capital injections, we add a penalty-at-ruin component to our performance function, in the spirit of
[
18
]
. It is well known that the solution to the joint optimization of capital injections and dividend payments is somehow dictated by the solutions of two auxiliary/sub-problems: the pure maximization of dividends, without the possibility of injection capital, and the maximization of dividends with mandatory injections to avoid ruin.

The first optimization problem often bears the name of Bruno de Finetti
[
5
]
, but there is wealth of publications on the subject; see, e.g.,
[
7
,
2
,
16
,
4
]
. The second optimization problem has been considered for example by
[
18
]
and
[
17
]
who have introduced forced capital injections in the optimal dividends problem to avoid ruin.

However, our paper is more in the footsteps of Løkka & Zervos
[
10
]
who considered the maximization of dividends (in a Brownian model) with the possibility, but not the obligation, of injecting capital. Their solution takes the form of a dichotomy: either capital is always injected, whenever the firm faces bankruptcy, or no capital is ever injected. Note that it does not seem to be optimal to inject earlier than when facing bankruptcy nor is it optimal to inject for some time and then give up and let ruin occur.

A recent trend in dividend optimization considers absolutely continuous dividend payments with payment rates bounded by a function of the surplus. This has been partly motivated by the desire of obtaining optimal strategies with the property of yielding a stable flow of dividends; this motivation comes mainly from
[
3
]
. In
[
15
]
, the bound is a linear function of the surplus while in
[
9
]
a more general non-decreasing concave function is considered. In this framework, along with the possibility of injecting capitals, maximizing dividends was solved by
[
14
]
where they showed that the optimal solution is a dichotomy analogous to the one in
[
10
]
. They obtained their solution by combining a probabilistic approach with viscosity solutions theory.

In this paper, we adopt the mathematical framework established in
[
14
]
, we fix the bound on dividend rates to be an affine function of the surplus and incorporate a penalty for bankruptcy. This specific bound provides the flexibility to derive semi-explicit expressions for the value function and a allows for a simpler formulation of the dichotomy. For instance, we will find that, for some set of parameters, capital injections are sub-optimal regardless of the cost of capital injections. Moreover, we use our semi-explicit expressions to gain a better understanding of the dichotomous nature of the solution by performing numerical experiments. Finally, adding a penalty at ruin in the optimization problem creates an intriguing duality between the injection and penalty costs, as the two corresponding solutions of the dichotomy have a unified representation that mirrors both cost parameters, indicating that they may in fact represent the two sides of the same coin. This relationship yields interesting financial interpretations and promising considerations for future problems.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
, we formulate the main stochastic optimization problem studied in this article. Its dichotomous solution emerges from two related optimization sub-problems, for which we briefly present the solutions (see Section
3
). Then, in Section
4
, we use the solutions of those sub-problems to construct a solution to the main optimization problem. Finally, we perform the aforementioned numerical analyses in Section
5
.

2.
Formulation of the main optimization problem

This section introduces the underlying probability space, the relevant stochastic processes, and the class of admissible controls. We then formulate the stochastic optimization problem.

Let us consider a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0},\mathbb{P})
where
\mathbb{F}
supports a standard Brownian motion
B=(B_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
and satisfies the usual conditions. We suppose that the (uncontrolled) insurance surplus process evolves according to an arithmetic Brownian motion
X=(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
given by

(1)

X_{t}=X_{0}+\mu t+\sigma B_{t},\quad t\geqslant 0,

where
\mu,\sigma>0
are the model parameters. It is well known that, for a twice continuously differentiable function
f
, the infinitesimal generator of
X
is given by

(2)

\mathcal{A}f(x)=\mu f^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x).

As dividends can be deducted and capital injections can be made, a strategy is given by a pair of adapted processes
\pi=(\ell^{\pi},G^{\pi})
, where
\ell^{\pi}=(\ell^{\pi}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is the dividend rate process, which is such that the amount of dividend payments made up to time
t
is given by
L^{\pi}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\ell^{\pi}_{s}\mathrm{d}s
, and where
G^{\pi}=(G^{\pi}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is the capital injection process such that
G^{\pi}_{t}
is the amount of capital injections made up to time
t
. The corresponding surplus process is then given by

(3)

\mathrm{d}X^{\pi}_{t}=(\mu-\ell^{\pi}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathrm{d}G^{\pi}_{t},\quad t\geqslant 0,

and the corresponding ruin time by

(4)

\tau^{\pi}=\inf\{t\geqslant 0\mid X^{\pi}_{t}<0\}.

Note that in our problem, for a given
\pi
, the ruin time
\tau^{\pi}
can be infinite with probability one or it can be finite with a positive probability.

Finally, as alluded to above, we will consider the following set of admissible strategies: for fixed
K,S\geqslant 0
,

(5)

\displaystyle\Pi=\{\pi=(\ell^{\pi},G^{\pi})

\displaystyle\mid 0\leqslant\ell^{\pi}_{t}\leqslant KX^{\pi}_{t}+S\text{ for all $t\geqslant 0$}

(6)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{and $G^{\pi}$ is c\`{a}dl\`{a}g nondecreasing with $G^{\pi}_{0-}=0$}\}.

On this set, we seek to maximize the following performance function: for
\pi\in\Pi
, we define

(7)

J(x;\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\left(\ell^{\pi}_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\beta\mathrm{d}G^{\pi}_{t}\right)-P\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau^{\pi}}\right],\quad x\geqslant 0,

where
q>0
is the discount rate,
\beta>1
is the cost of capital injections, and
P\geqslant 0
is the penalty at ruin. We will refer to
K,S,q,\beta,P
as the problem parameters. Then, the goal is to compute the value function

(8)

V(x)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}J(x;\pi),\quad x\geqslant 0,

and find an optimal strategy (if it exists), i.e., a strategy
\pi^{*}\in\Pi
such that
V(x)=J(x;\pi^{*})
for all
x\geqslant 0
.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that, in the above performance function,
\mathbb{E}_{x}
stands for the expectation with respect to the probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{x}
associated with the initial surplus
X^{\pi}_{0-}=x
.

As it will be shown in more detail in Section
4.3
, the solution to this optimization problem takes a dichotomous form that depends on how
favourable
the (model and problem) parameters are on the underlying surplus process. In some sense, we could say that, if the parameters are not favourable, then this business is not viable. Later, we will provide a precise criterion to determine whether the parameters are
favourable
or not.

Here is, in words, the solution to the above optimization problem:

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume the parameters are favourable. In this case:

•

if the cost of injections is large, then an optimal dividend-injection strategy is to pay dividends at the maximal rate when the surplus exceeds a threshold and to never inject capital so that ruin occurs if and when the process reaches zero;

•

if the cost of injections is small, then an optimal dividend-injection strategy is to pay dividends at the maximal rate when the surplus exceeds another threshold and to make bail-out injections so that ruin never occurs.

Otherwise, the optimal dividend-injection strategy is to liquidate, i.e., to pay dividends constantly at the maximum rate until the process is ruined (it reaches zero) and never inject any capital.

A rigorous and mathematical proof of this last theorem is provided by Theorem
4.9
.

The dichotomous nature of this solution suggests to study, as in
[
10
]
, two optimization sub-problems. This is the purpose of the next section.

3.
Two optimization (sub-)problems

As in
[
10
]
, we now present the solutions to two optimization sub-problems which appear in the dichotomous solution of our main optimization problem. The first sub-problem corresponds to the classical optimal dividends problem with absolutely continuous dividend payment strategies and a penalty at ruin, while the second one is an optimal dividends problem with forced capital injections which is closely related to the so-called
bail-out dividend problem
(
[
4
,
12
]
). See Theorem
3.5
and Theorem
3.6
below; they are collections of known results already available in the literature.

Remark 3.1
.

In the next section, we will reformulate Theorem
3.5
and Theorem
3.6
with a unified representation that will eventually allow us to derive the solution to our main optimization problem.

Now, let us consider the following subsets of strategies:

(9)

\Pi_{d}=\{\pi\in\Pi\mid G^{\pi}\equiv 0\}\quad\text{and}\quad\Pi_{c}=\{\pi\in\Pi\mid X^{\pi}_{t}\geqslant 0\text{ for all }t\geqslant 0\}.

The first subset contains strategies in which no capital injections are made, while the second subset contains those in which capital injections must be made to keep the surplus process from getting ruined. Let us define the corresponding value functions by

(10)

V_{d}(x)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{d}}J(x;\pi)\quad\text{and}\quad V_{c}(x)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{c}}J(x;\pi),

in which, for each
\pi\in\Pi_{d}
, the performance function in (
7
) becomes

(11)

J(x;\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\ell^{\pi}_{t}\mathrm{d}t-P\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau^{\pi}}\right],

while, for each
\pi\in\Pi_{c}
, it becomes

(12)

J(x;\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\left(\ell^{\pi}_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\beta\mathrm{d}G^{\pi}_{t}\right)\right],

since for such strategies we have
\tau^{\pi}=\infty
.

Note that, because
\Pi_{d}\subset\Pi
and
\Pi_{c}\subset\Pi
, then we have

(13)

V(x)\geqslant\max\{V_{d}(x),V_{c}(x)\},\quad x\geqslant 0.

Remark 3.2
.

The set
\Pi_{c}
contains more injection strategies than simple bail-out strategies for which injections are made when the process reaches zero. For example, one could choose to inject before reaching zero, i.e., at a higher level, either with a singular control (a reflection of the process), an absolutely continuous one or even an impulse control.

Remark 3.3
.

A well-established approach to solving these two sub-problems is to consider subsets of strategies that we believe contain an optimal one, for which the performance functions can be computed. One can then identify an optimal strategy within this subset and use a verification theorem to prove its optimality among all admissible strategies, not just within the subset.

Before presenting the solutions to these two sub-problems, we need to introduce auxiliary functions. First, define

(14)

W^{(q)}(x)=\frac{2}{\Delta}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mu}{\sigma^{2}}x}\sinh\left(\frac{\Delta}{\sigma^{2}}x\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

where
\Delta=\sqrt{\mu^{2}+2\sigma^{2}q}
. Second, define

(15)

H^{(q)}_{K,S}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{K}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(x-\frac{\mu-S}{K}\right)^{2}}\mathrm{D}_{-\frac{q}{K}}\left(\left(x-\frac{\mu-S}{K}\right)\frac{\sqrt{2K}}{\sigma}\right),\quad x\geqslant 0,

where

(16)

\mathrm{D}_{-\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\lambda)}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/4}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\lambda-1}\mathrm{e}^{-xt-t^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}t

is the parabolic cylinder function.

Finally, let us define, for
b\geqslant 0
,

(17)

\eta(b)=\frac{S}{q}+\frac{K}{q+K}\left(b+\frac{\mu-S}{q}\right),

(18)

F(b)=\frac{q}{q+K}\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)}{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(b)}+\eta(b)-\frac{W^{(q)}(b)}{W^{(q)\prime}(b)}\left(1+P\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)}{W^{(q)}(-b)}\right)

and

(19)

G(b)=\frac{q}{q+K}\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)}{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(b)}+\eta(b)-\frac{W^{(q)}(b)}{W^{(q)\prime}(b)}\left(1-g(b)\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)}{W^{(q)}(-b)}\right),

where

(20)

g(b):=\frac{\beta\left(\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(b)}{W^{(q)}(b)}-\frac{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(b)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)}\right)-\left(\frac{K}{q+K}-\eta(b)\frac{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(b)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)}\right)\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)}{W^{(q)}(b)}}{\frac{W^{(q)}(-b)}{W^{(q)\prime}(-b)}\left(\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(b)}{W^{(q)}(b)}-\frac{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(b)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)}\right)-\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)^{2}}{W^{(q)}(b)W^{(q)}(-b)}}.

Remark 3.4
.

All these functions have probabilistic interpretations that we omit for now. In what follows, for simplicity we choose to keep the notation
W^{(q)}
and
H^{(q)}_{K,S}
, even though some values are known; for example,
W^{(q)\prime}(0)=\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}.

Here is the solution to the first sub-problem:

Theorem 3.5
.

[
[
15
,
13
,
6
]
]
If

(21)

\frac{q}{q+K}\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(0)}{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(0)}+\eta(0)+P>0,

then there exists a unique
b_{d}>0
, given by
F(b_{d})=0
, such that

(22)

V_{d}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(KX^{b_{d}}_{t}+S)\mathds{1}_{X^{b_{d}}_{t}\geqslant b_{d}}\mathrm{d}t-P\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{d}}\right]

with
\tau_{d}=\inf\{t\geqslant 0\mid X^{b_{d}}_{t}<0\}
, where
X^{b_{d}}
is the solution to

(23)

\mathrm{d}X^{b_{d}}_{t}=\left(\mu-(KX^{b_{d}}_{t}+S)\mathds{1}_{X^{b_{d}}_{t}\geqslant b_{d}}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t}.

Furthermore, the value function is such that

(24)

V_{d}(x)=\begin{cases}-P\frac{W^{(q)}(x-b_{d})}{W^{(q)}(-b_{d})}+\left(1+P\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)}{W^{(q)}(-b_{d})}\right)\frac{W^{(q)}(x)}{W^{(q)\prime}(b_{d})},&0\leqslant x\leqslant b_{d},\\
\hfil\eta(x)+\frac{q}{q+K}\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(x)}{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(b_{d})},&x>b_{d}.\end{cases}

Otherwise, if the condition in Equation (
21
) is not satisfied, then we have

(25)

V_{d}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(KX_{t}+S)\mathrm{d}t-P\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{d}}\right]

with
\tau_{d}=\inf\{t\geqslant 0\mid X_{t}<0\}
, where
X
is the solution to

(26)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\left(\mu-(KX_{t}+S)\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t}.

In this case, the value function is such that

(27)

V_{d}(x)=\eta(x)-\left(P+\eta(0)\right)\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(x)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(0)},\quad x\geqslant 0.

It is known that the value function
V_{d}\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a concave function and that it is a solution to the following HJB equation:

(28)

(\mathcal{A}-q)f(x)+\sup_{0\leqslant\lambda\leqslant Kx+S}\left[\lambda(1-f^{\prime}(x))\right]=0,\quad x>0,

with boundary condition

(29)

f(0)=-P.

Here is the solution to the second sub-problem:

Theorem 3.6
.

[
[
14
,
11
]
]
There exists a unique
b_{c}>0
, given by
G(b_{c})=0
, such that

(30)

V_{c}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\left((KY^{b_{c}}_{t}+S)\mathds{1}_{X^{b_{c}}_{t}\geqslant b_{c}}\mathrm{d}t-\beta\mathrm{d}G^{b_{c}}_{t}\right)\right],

where
(Y^{b_{c}},G^{b_{c}})
is the solution to the Skorohod problem

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y^{b_{c}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\mu-(KY^{b_{c}}_{t}+S)\mathds{1}_{Y^{b_{c}}_{t}\geqslant b_{c}}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathrm{d}G^{b_{c}}_{t},

\displaystyle G^{b_{c}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{Y^{b_{c}}_{s}=0}\mathrm{d}G^{b_{c}}_{s}.

Furthermore, the value function is such that

(31)

V_{c}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{W^{(q)}(x)\left(W^{(q)\prime}(-b_{c})-\beta W^{(q)\prime}(0)\right)+W^{(q)}(x-b_{c})\left(\beta W^{(q)\prime}(b_{c})-W^{(q)\prime}(0)\right)}{W^{(q)\prime}(-b_{c})W^{(q)\prime}(b_{c})-W^{(q)\prime}(0)^{2}},&0\leqslant x\leqslant b_{c},\\
\hfil\eta(x)+\frac{q}{q+K}\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(x)}{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(b_{c})},&x>b_{c}.\end{cases}

It is known that the value function
V_{c}\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a concave function and that it is a solution to the following HJB equation:

(32)

(\mathcal{A}-q)f(x)+\sup_{0\leqslant\lambda\leqslant Kx+S}\left[\lambda(1-f^{\prime}(x))\right]=0,\quad x>0,

with boundary condition

(33)

f^{\prime}(0)=\beta.

Remark 3.7
.

It is known that
b_{c}\in(0,\gamma)
, where
\gamma
is the unique positive solution of

(34)

\left(\Delta-\mu\right)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\Delta+\mu}{\sigma^{2}}\gamma}+\left(\Delta+\mu\right)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\Delta-\mu}{\sigma^{2}}\gamma}=2\beta\Delta,

which is equivalent to

(35)

-\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(\gamma)W^{(q)}(-\gamma)}{W^{(q)}(\gamma)W^{(q)\prime}(0)}=\beta.

The value of
\gamma
is the optimal barrier level for dividend payments in the (fully) singular problem studied by Løkka & Zervos
[
10
]
.

These two sub-problems represent each part of the dichotomy and will correspond to the optimal solution whenever the parameters satisfy some conditions. For illustrative purpose, there exists a set of parameters
\mathcal{A}
such that the optimal solution satisfies
V=V_{c}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{A}}+V_{d}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{A}^{c}}
.

4.
Proof of the main result

In this section, we present a full solution to our main optimization problem. First, in Section
4.1
, we unify the analytical representations of the two performance functions related to the sub-problems studied previously, which is instrumental in our approach. Then, in Section
4.2
, we derive some analytical properties of these two functions that will be useful in the final Section
4.3
.

4.1.
Two performance functions with a unified representation

To derive unified representations of the solutions to the sub-problems of Section
3
, we use similar
Markovian decompositions
.

In this direction, for a given
b\geqslant 0
, let us consider the process
X^{b}
defined by

(36)

\mathrm{d}X^{b}_{t}=\left(\mu-(KX^{b}_{t}+S)\mathds{1}_{X^{b}_{t}\geqslant b}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad t\geqslant 0,

and the pair of processes
(Y^{b},G^{b})
which is a solution to the following Skorohod problem (see, e.g., Lemma 3.6.14 in
[
8
]
):

(37)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y^{b}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\mu-(KY^{b}_{t}+S)\mathds{1}_{Y^{b}_{t}\geqslant b}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathrm{d}G^{b}_{t},\quad t\geqslant 0,

(38)

\displaystyle G^{b}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{Y^{b}_{s}=0}\mathrm{d}G^{b}_{s}.

Note that
Y^{b}_{t}\geqslant 0
for all
t\geqslant 0
.

Define

(39)

\ell^{b}_{t}=(KX^{b}_{t}+S)\mathds{1}_{X^{b}_{t}\geqslant b}\quad\text{and}\quad\underline{\ell}^{b}_{t}=(KY^{b}_{t}+S)\mathds{1}_{Y^{b}_{t}\geqslant b}.

In what follows, the (dividend-injection) strategies
\pi^{b}=(\ell^{b},0)\in\Pi_{d}
and
\underline{\pi}^{b}=(\underline{\ell}^{b},G^{b})\in\Pi_{c}
will be referred to as the
pure-dividend mean-reverting strategy at level
b
and the
bailout-dividend mean-reverting strategy at level
b
, respectively.

Remark 4.1
.

Note that the optimal strategy in Theorem
3.5
is the pure-dividend mean-reverting strategy
\pi^{b_{d}}=(\ell^{b_{d}},0)
while the optimal strategy in Theorem
3.6
is the bailout-dividend mean-reverting strategy
\underline{\pi}^{b_{c}}=(\underline{\ell}^{b_{c}},G^{b_{c}})
. Recall that these strategies also appear in Theorem
2.2
.

Remark 4.2
.

In
[
14
]
, a strategy
\underline{\pi}^{b}
is called a reflected mean-reverting dividend strategy. We have decided to use instead the word bailout because it is more frequently used in the literature to designate capital injections made when the process reaches zero.

Next, we modify slightly the notation of the performance function to emphasize the role played by the threshold when considering mean-reverting strategies. As mentioned in Section
3
, for each
\pi^{b}=(\ell^{b},0)
we have

(40)

J(x;\pi^{b})=J_{d}(x;b):=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau^{b}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\ell^{b}_{t}\mathrm{d}t-P\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau^{b}}\right],

where
\tau^{b}:=\tau^{\pi^{b}}
, and for each
\underline{\pi}^{b}=(\underline{\ell}^{b},G^{b})
we have

(41)

J(x;\underline{\pi}^{b})=J_{c}(x;b):=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\left(\underline{\ell}^{b}_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\beta\mathrm{d}G^{b}_{t}\right)\right].

Now, let us push further the analysis of the performance functions
J_{d}(\cdot;b)
and
J_{c}(\cdot;b)
. In this direction, let us define the expectations

(42)

\Phi(x;b)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau^{X^{b}}_{0}}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad R(x;b)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau^{b}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(KX^{b}_{t}+S)\mathds{1}_{X^{b}_{t}\geqslant b}\mathrm{d}t\right],\quad x\geqslant 0.

It is known (see
[
11
,
14
]
) that

(43)

\Phi(x;b)=\begin{cases}\frac{W^{(q)}(x-b)}{W^{(q)}(-b)}+\Phi(b;b)\frac{W^{(q)}(x)}{W^{(q)}(b)},&0\leqslant x\leqslant b,\\
\hfil\Phi(b;b)\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(x)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)},&x>b,\end{cases}

where

(44)

\Phi(b;b)=-\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)/W^{(q)}(-b)}{\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(b)}{W^{(q)}(b)}-\frac{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(b)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)}},

and it is known (see
[
15
,
13
]
) that

(45)

R(x;b)=\begin{cases}\hfil R(b;b)\frac{W^{(q)}(x)}{W^{(q)}(b)},&0\leqslant x\leqslant b,\\
\eta(x)+\left(R(b;b)-\eta(b)\right)\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(x)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)},&x>b,\end{cases}

where

(46)

R(b;b)=\frac{\frac{K}{q+K}-\eta(b)\frac{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(b)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)}}{\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(b)}{W^{(q)}(b)}-\frac{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(b)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)}}.

Since
X^{b}
and
(Y^{b},G^{b})
are Markovian processes, one can use the strong Markov property to obtain the following compact and unified expressions for
J_{d}(\cdot;b)
and
J_{c}(\cdot;b)
: for
x\geqslant 0
,

(47)

J_{d}(x;b)=R(x;b)-P\Phi(x;b)

and

(48)

J_{c}(x;b)=R(x;b)+J_{c}(0;b)\Phi(x;b).

Remark 4.3
.

In both (
47
) and (
48
), the first term is the present value of dividends until the surplus hits zero, while the second term is the residual value at ruin. In (
47
), this second term is the discounted value of the penalty at ruin, while in (
48
) it is the discounted value (function) when the surplus restarts at zero. However, note that, in the latter case, capital injections keep the surplus afloat.

Using the expressions for
R
and
\Phi
, we can further write

(49)

J_{d}(x;b)=\begin{cases}\hfil-P\frac{W^{(q)}(x-b)}{W^{(q)}(-b)}+J_{d}(b;b)\frac{W^{(q)}(x)}{W^{(q)}(b)},&0\leqslant x\leqslant b,\\
\eta(x)+\left(J_{d}(b;b)-\eta(b)\right)\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(x)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)},&x>b,\end{cases}

where

(50)

J_{d}(b;b)=\frac{\frac{K}{q+K}-\eta(b)\frac{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(b)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)}+P\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)}{W^{(q)}(-b)}}{\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(b)}{W^{(q)}(b)}-\frac{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(b)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)}}.

Similarly,

(51)

J_{c}(x;b)=\begin{cases}\hfil J_{c}(0;b)\frac{W^{(q)}(x-b)}{W^{(q)}(-b)}+J_{c}(b;b)\frac{W^{(q)}(x)}{W^{(q)}(b)},&0\leqslant x\leqslant b,\\
\eta(x)+\left(J_{c}(b;b)-\eta(b)\right)\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(x)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(b)},&x>b,\end{cases}

where

(52)

J_{c}(0;b)=\frac{\beta-\partial_{x}R(0;b)}{\partial_{x}\Phi(0;b)},\qquad J_{c}(b;b)=\frac{\beta\Phi(b;b)+R(b;b)\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(-b)}{W^{(q)}(-b)}}{\partial_{x}\Phi(0;b)}.

4.2.
Analytical properties of the performance functions

It is easy to see that both
J_{d}(\cdot;b)
and
J_{c}(\cdot;b)
are continuously differentiable on
(0,\infty)
, and twice continuously differentiable on
(0,\infty)\setminus\{b\}
. From Theorem
3.5
and Theorem
3.6
, we know there exist unique thresholds
b_{d}\geqslant 0
and
b_{c}>0
such that, for all
x\geqslant 0
,

V_{d}(x)=J_{d}(x;b_{d})\quad\text{and}\quad V_{c}(x)=J_{c}(x;b_{c}).

The optimal thresholds
b_{d}
and
b_{c}
are characterized as the levels for which the performance functions
J_{d}(\cdot;b_{d})
and
J_{c}(\cdot;b_{c})
are twice continuously differentiable on
(0,\infty)
.

Here is a new result in the analysis of the optimal thresholds:

Proposition 4.4
.

The optimal threshold
b_{d}
is increasing in
P
and the optimal threshold
b_{c}
is increasing in
\beta
.

Proof.

First, let us write
b_{d}(P)
and
F(b;P)
(for the function
F
defined in (
18
)) to emphasize their dependence on
P
. Note that
P\mapsto F(b;P)
is strictly increasing.

Take
P^{\prime}>P
. First, if
P
is such that (
21
) is not verified, then
b_{d}(P^{\prime})\geqslant 0=b_{d}(P)
, which proves the desired result. Second, if
P
is such that (
21
) is verified, then
b_{d}(P)>0
and
F(b_{d}(P);P^{\prime})>F(b_{d}(P);P)=0
, where the equality comes from the definition of
b_{d}(P)
. Because
b_{d}(P^{\prime})
is unique, then
F(b;P^{\prime})
crosses zero once at
b=b_{d}(P^{\prime})
. Thus,
F(b;P^{\prime})>0
if and only if
b\in(0,b_{d}(P^{\prime}))
since
F(0+)>0
due to the fact that (
21
) is satisfied. In consequence,
b_{d}(P)<b_{d}(P^{\prime})
.

Next, let us write
b_{c}(\beta)
and
G(b;\beta)
(for the function
G
defined in (
19
)) to emphasize their dependence on
\beta
. Note that the value of
G
decreases when the value of
J_{c}(0;b)
decreases, and the latter is strictly decreasing in
\beta
. Hence,
\beta\mapsto G(b;\beta)
is strictly increasing.

If
\beta^{\prime}>\beta
, then
G(b_{c}(\beta);\beta^{\prime})>G(b_{c}(\beta);\beta)=0
, where the equality comes from the definition of
b_{c}(\beta)
. Because
b_{c}(\beta^{\prime})
is unique, then
G(b;\beta^{\prime})
crosses zero once at
b=b_{c}(\beta^{\prime})
. Thus,
G(b;\beta^{\prime})>0
if and only if
b\in(0,b_{c}(\beta^{\prime}))
since
G(0+)>0
(c.f.
[
11
]
). Consequently, we have
b_{c}(\beta)<b_{c}(\beta^{\prime})
.
∎

Finally, let us consider the following technical but important lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

We have that:

(1)

\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{d})\geqslant\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b)
, for all
b\geqslant 0
;

(2)

for
b\geqslant 0
,
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b)=\beta
if and only if
J_{c}(0;b)=-P
;

(3)

if
J_{c}(0;b_{c})<-P
(resp.
>
or
=
), then
b_{d}<b_{c}
(resp.
>
or
=
).

Proof.

Since
V_{d}=J_{d}(\cdot;b_{d})
and since, for any
b\geqslant 0
,
J_{d}(\cdot;b)
is the performance function of a strategy
\pi_{b}\in\Pi_{d}
, then we have
J_{d}(x;b_{d})\geqslant J_{d}(x;b)
for all
x\geqslant 0
and any
b\geqslant 0
. As
J_{d}(0;b)=-P
for any
b\geqslant 0
, then we have
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{d})\geqslant\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b)
. This proves the first assertion.

Now, using the unified analytical representations of
J_{d}
and
J_{c}
(see (
47
) and (
48
)), and using the fact that
\partial_{x}J_{c}(0;b)=\beta
for any
b\geqslant 0
, we have

(53)

\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b)=\partial_{x}R(0;b)-P\partial_{x}\Phi(0;b)

and

(54)

\partial_{x}J_{c}(0;b)=\partial_{x}R(0;b)+J_{c}(0;b)\partial_{x}\Phi(0;b)=\beta,

from which the second result follows.

For the last assertion, let us prove first the case corresponding to an equality, i.e., if
J_{c}(0;b_{c})=-P
, then
b_{c}=b_{d}
. Suppose that
J_{c}(0;b_{c})=-P
, then by the second assertion, we have
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{c})=\beta
. Note from Theorem
3.5
and Theorem
3.6
that
V^{\prime}_{d}(b_{d})=1=V^{\prime}_{c}(b_{c})
, so that
b_{d}
and
b_{c}
uniquely satisfy
\partial_{x}J_{d}(b_{d};b_{d})=1=\partial_{x}J_{c}(b_{c};b_{c})
. Thus,

(55)

\partial_{x}R(b_{d};b_{d})-P\partial_{x}\Phi(b_{d};b_{d})=\partial_{x}R(b_{c};b_{c})-P\partial_{x}\Phi(b_{c};b_{c}),

where we used the assumption that
J_{c}(0;b_{c})=-P
. Clearly,
b_{c}=b_{d}
solve this equation.

Suppose now that
J_{c}(0;b_{c})<-P
and set
P^{\prime}:=-J_{c}(0;b_{c})>P
. Then, by the result shown in the previous paragraph, we have
b_{d}(P^{\prime})=b_{c}
. Recalling that
b_{d}
is increasing in
P
(by Proposition
4.4
), we get that
b_{d}(P^{\prime})>b_{d}(P)
since
P^{\prime}>P
. Consequently,
b_{c}>b_{d}(P)
, that is,
b_{d}<b_{c}
. The same arguments hold with reverse inequalities in the case
J_{c}(0;b_{c})>-P
.
∎

4.3.
Main result

Now, we have all the elements to complete the proof of our main result, i.e., Theorem
4.9
below.

As both
V_{d}
and
V_{c}
are solutions of the same HJB equation (see (
28
) and (
32
)), to prove Theorem
4.9
we will need to be able to compare the
initial conditions
of (
29
) and (
33
). This is the purpose of the following proposition.

Proposition 4.6
.

We have
V_{d}^{\prime}(0)>\beta
if and only if
V_{c}(0)+P>0
.

Proof.

Since
V_{d}=J_{d}(\cdot;b_{d})
and
V_{c}=J_{c}(\cdot;b_{c})
, then the equivalence becomes:
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{d})>\beta
if and only if
J_{c}(0;b_{c})+P>0
. Recall that the inequality
J_{c}(0;b_{c})+P>0
is the same as
\partial_{x}R(0;b_{c})-P\partial_{x}\Phi(0;b_{c})>\beta
. Consequently, by (
47
), the equivalence now becomes:
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{d})>\beta
if and only if
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{c})>\beta
.

To prove the equivalence, we assume first that
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{c})>\beta
. Taking
b
equal to
b_{c}
in statement (1) of Lemma
4.5
yields
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{d})>\beta
. Next, assume that
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{d})>\beta
and let us prove by contradiction that
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{c})>\beta
. Suppose that
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{c})\leqslant\beta
. If
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{c})=\beta
, or equivalently if
J_{c}(0;b_{c})=-P
(by statement (2) of Lemma
4.5
), then
b_{c}=b_{d}
by statement (3) of Lemma
4.5
. Hence,
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{d})=\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{c})=\beta
, which contradicts the assumption that
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{d})>\beta
.
Finally, if
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{c})<\beta
, then
b_{c}<b_{d}
by statement (3) of Lemma
4.5
. Because by assumption,
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{c})<\beta<\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{d})
, and
b\mapsto\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b)
is continuous, then there exists
b^{*}\in(b_{c},b_{d})
such that
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b^{*})=\beta
by the intermediate value theorem. Thus, by statement (2) of Lemma
4.5
, we have
J_{c}(0;b^{*})=-P
. Using the same arguments as in the beginning of the proof, we see that
\partial_{x}J_{d}(0;b_{c})<\beta
and
J_{c}(0;b_{c})<-P
are equivalent. However, we have
-P=J_{c}(0;b^{*})\leqslant J_{c}(0;b_{c})=V_{c}(0)
, which is a contradiction.
∎

The inequalities in Proposition
4.6
can be written more explicitly using the representations of
V_{d}
and
V_{c}
given in (
24
) and in (
31
), respectively. Written differently, Proposition
4.6
yields that

(56)

\beta<-P\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(-b_{d})}{W^{(q)}(-b_{d})}+\left(1+P\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)}{W^{(q)}(-b_{d})}\right)\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)}{W^{(q)\prime}(b_{d})}

if and only if

(57)

P>\frac{-W^{(q)}(-b_{c})\left(\beta W^{(q)\prime}(b_{c})-W^{(q)\prime}(0)\right)}{W^{(q)\prime}(-b_{c})W^{(q)\prime}(b_{c})-W^{(q)\prime}(0)^{2}},

assuming that (
21
) is verified, i.e., assuming that the parameters are
favourable
. This equivalence will allow us to formulate the optimal solution of our problem using either
\beta
or
P
as a criterion for the dichotomy; see Theorem
4.9
and Corollary
4.10
) below.

Remark 4.7
.

Of course, the criterion for optimality depends jointly on the parameters
\beta
and
P
, but these two equivalent forms of the criterion provide interesting insight on the effect of each parameter on the optimal dichotomy. Moreover, the statement of Corollary
4.10
has never appeared before in the literature.

As a last intermediate step, here is the verification lemma of the main optimization problem.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be a concave solution to the HJB equation

(58)

(\mathcal{A}-q)f(x)+\sup_{0\leqslant\lambda\leqslant Kx+S}\left[\lambda(1-f^{\prime}(x))\right]=0,\quad x>0,

with boundary condition

(59)

\max\{-(f(0)+P),f^{\prime}(0)-\beta\}=0,

then
f(x)\geqslant V(x)
for all
x\geqslant 0
.

The proof of this verification lemma is standard (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
), so it is left to the reader.

Now, using Lemma
4.8
with the properties of
V_{d}
and
V_{c}
, we can easily prove the main theorem.

Theorem 4.9
.

If
\frac{q}{q+K}\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(0)}{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(0)}+\eta(0)+P>0
, then
b_{d}>0
and the solution of the main optimization problem is given by:

•

if
\beta>-P\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(-b_{d})}{W^{(q)}(-b_{d})}+\left(1+P\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)}{W^{(q)}(-b_{d})}\right)\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)}{W^{(q)\prime}(b_{d})}
, then an optimal dividend-injection strategy is given by
(\ell^{b_{d}},0)
, and thus
V=V_{d}
with
b_{d}<b_{c}
;

•

if
\beta<-P\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(-b_{d})}{W^{(q)}(-b_{d})}+\left(1+P\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)}{W^{(q)}(-b_{d})}\right)\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)}{W^{(q)\prime}(b_{d})}
, then an optimal dividend-injection strategy is given by
(\underline{\ell}^{b_{c}},G^{b_{c}})
, and thus
V=V_{c}
with
b_{c}<b_{d}
;

•

if
\beta=-P\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(-b_{d})}{W^{(q)}(-b_{d})}+\left(1+P\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)}{W^{(q)}(-b_{d})}\right)\frac{W^{(q)\prime}(0)}{W^{(q)\prime}(b_{d})}
, then both aforementioned strategies are optimal, and thus
V=V_{c}=V_{d}
with
b_{c}=b_{d}
.

Otherwise,
b_{d}=0
and an optimal strategy is given by the liquidation strategy
(\ell^{0},0)
, and thus
V=V_{d}
.

Proof.

First, recall that

(60)

V(x)\geqslant\max\{V_{d}(x),V_{c}(x)\},\quad x\geqslant 0.

Assume that
\frac{q}{q+K}\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(0)}{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(0)}+\eta(0)+P>0
.

•

In the first case, the condition on
\beta
is equivalent to
V^{\prime}_{d}(0)<\beta
. We know from Section
4.2
that
V_{d}
is a concave solution of (
58
) with boundary condition
V_{d}(0)=-P
. But since
V^{\prime}_{d}(0)<\beta
, it follows that
V_{d}
also satisfies boundary condition (
59
). Thus, by Lemma
4.8
,
V_{d}\geqslant V
. By (
60
),
V\geqslant V_{d}
, thus
V=V_{d}
. Finally, since
V^{\prime}_{d}(0)<\beta
is equivalent to
V_{c}(0)<-P
by Proposition
4.6
, then
b_{d}<b_{c}
by statement (3) of Lemma
4.5
.

•

The condition on
\beta
in the second case is equivalent to
V_{c}(0)>-P
. We know from Section
4.2
that
V_{c}
is a concave solution of (
58
) with boundary condition
V^{\prime}_{c}(0)=\beta
. Coupled with
V_{c}(0)>-P
, it follows that boundary condition (
59
) is also satisfied by
V_{c}
. Hence,
V_{c}\geqslant V
by Lemma
4.8
, and
V\geqslant V_{c}
by (
60
). In conclusion,
V=V_{c}
. Finally,
V_{c}(0)>-P
implies that
b_{c}<b_{d}
by statement (3) of Lemma
4.5
.

•

In the co-optimal case, the condition on
\beta
is equivalent to
V^{\prime}_{d}(0)=\beta
and
V_{c}(0)=-P
. Thus, both
V_{d}
and
V_{c}
satisfy boundary condition (
59
), and as we know, they both are concave and satisfy (
58
). Hence,
V_{d}\geqslant V
and
V_{c}\geqslant V
. Additionally, we have
V\geqslant V_{d}
and
V\geqslant V_{c}
from (
60
), so we conclude that
V=V_{d}=V_{c}
. Finally,
b_{d}=b_{c}
from statement (3) of Lemma
4.5
since
V_{c}(0)=-P
.

Now, assume that
\frac{q}{q+K}\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(0)}{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(0)}+\eta(0)+P\leqslant 0
, in which case we have
b_{d}=0
with
V_{d}
given by (
27
) and such that

(61)

V^{\prime}_{d}(0)=\frac{K}{q+K}-\left(\eta(0)+P\right)\frac{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(0)}{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(0)}\leqslant 1.

Hence,
V^{\prime}_{d}(0)<\beta
. The rest of the proof is as above, so we conclude that
V=V_{d}
with
b_{d}=0
.
∎

As mentioned, the solution can also be stated from the point of view of parameter
P
.

Corollary 4.10
.

If
\frac{q}{q+K}\frac{H^{(q)}_{K,S}(0)}{H^{(q)\prime}_{K,S}(0)}+\eta(0)+P>0
, then
b_{d}>0
and the solution of the main optimization problem is given by:

•

if
P<\frac{-W^{(q)}(-b_{c})\left(\beta W^{(q)\prime}(b_{c})-W^{(q)\prime}(0)\right)}{W^{(q)\prime}(-b_{c})W^{(q)\prime}(b_{c})-W^{(q)\prime}(0)^{2}}
, then an optimal strategy is given by
(\ell^{b_{d}},0)
, and thus
V=V_{d}
with
b_{d}<b_{c}
;

•

if
P>\frac{-W^{(q)}(-b_{c})\left(\beta W^{(q)\prime}(b_{c})-W^{(q)\prime}(0)\right)}{W^{(q)\prime}(-b_{c})W^{(q)\prime}(b_{c})-W^{(q)\prime}(0)^{2}}
, then an optimal strategy is given by
(\underline{\ell}^{b_{c}},G^{b_{c}})
, and thus
V=V_{c}
with
b_{c}<b_{d}
;

•

if
P=\frac{-W^{(q)}(-b_{c})\left(\beta W^{(q)\prime}(b_{c})-W^{(q)\prime}(0)\right)}{W^{(q)\prime}(-b_{c})W^{(q)\prime}(b_{c})-W^{(q)\prime}(0)^{2}}
, then both aforementioned strategies are optimal, and thus
V=V_{c}=V_{d}
with
b_{c}=b_{d}
.

Otherwise,
b_{d}=0
and an optimal strategy is given by
(\ell^{0},0)
, and thus

.

Conclusion

Following the steps of
[
10
]
and
[
14
]
, we have considered the problem faced by an insurance company (or the managers of a cash fund) aiming at jointly optimizing capital injections and dividend payments. While allowing for general capital injections, we have restricted dividend payments to be absolutely continuous with payment rates bounded by an affine function of the controlled process. This restriction is motivated by the desire of obtaining optimal strategies with the property of yielding a stable flow of dividends. On the other hand, we have added a
cost for ruin
in the performance function, in order to have a handle on the role played by bankruptcy in the optimal trade-off between avoiding ruin and paying as much dividends as possible.

Using elementary techniques, we have shown that the so-called Løkka–Zervos dichotomy is valid: if the cost of capital injections is low (or if the penalty at ruin is high), it is optimal to pay dividends at the maximum rate and inject capital as a last resort so that ruin never occurs; conversely, if the cost of capital injections is high (or if the penalty at ruin is low), it is optimal to pay dividends at the maximum rate and never inject capital so that ruin occurs at the first passage below zero. Recall that, in
[
10
]
and
[
14
]
, as there is no penalty at ruin, the dichotomy is stated only in terms of the cost of capital injections. With this extra component, we were able to provide an additional perspective on this optimal behaviour.

Finally, our numerical analyses are showing that an affine bound on the dividend rate is sufficient to
fill in the gap
between absolutely continuous dividend payments and singular dividend payments.
