Mixing on k k Columns of the Transvection Walk
Natesh Pillai, Aaron Smith

Abstract
. In [ 12 ] , Diaconis and Saloff-Coste introduced the simple “transvection” walk on GL n ​ ( 𝔽 2 ) \mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2}) : at each step, choose two distinct rows and add one to the other. In [ 2 ] , Ben-Hamou recently proved that this walk has mixing time O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n^{2}\log n) . Inspired by applications in cryptography [ 29 ] , the paper [ 1 ] conjectured that the first k k columns of this walk mixed in O ⁡ ( n ​ k ​ log ⁡ ( n ) ) O(nk\log(n)) steps. Our main result is a proof of this conjecture uniformly in n n and k . k. Our proof is based on a local-to-global entropy estimate, in the spirit of block factorization results such as [ 4 , 5 ] . In our setting, the kernels that correspond roughly to the block kernels of [ 5 ] do not have uniformly large log-Sobolev constants, and so naively applying these techniques does not improve over [ 2 ] . We avoid these bad blocks by combining our entropy estimates with a burn-in argument similar to classical drift-and-minorization arguments [ 27 ] . This method may be of broader interest, and so we illustrate it by proving an analogous result for a family of product-replacement algorithms on the Heisenberg group.



1.
Introduction

The
transvection walk
on
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
, introduced in

[
12
]
, is the Markov chain obtained from the following row
operation. Given the current matrix
\widehat{Z}_{t}
, sample distinct “donor” and “recipient” indices

a_{t},b_{t}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
uniformly at random and set

\widehat{Z}_{t+1}[\ell,*]=\begin{cases}\widehat{Z}_{t}[b_{t},*]+\widehat{Z}_{t}[a_{t},*],&\ell=b_{t},\\[4.0pt]
\widehat{Z}_{t}[\ell,*],&\ell\neq b_{t}.\end{cases}

The upper bound

O(n^{2}\log n)
for the mixing time of this walk is proved in
[
2
, Theorem 1]
. This is conjectured to be optimal. A simple diameter-based lower bound gives
\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}=\Omega\left(\frac{n^{2}}{\log(n)}\right)
, so Theorem 1 of
[
2
]
is sharp up to
\text{polylog}(n)
factors.

In several applications, including the trapdoored-matrix authentication proposal of
[
29
]
, it is natural to only care about the mixing of a comparatively
small collection of columns. This motivates the present paper, where we study the projection of the transvection walk onto its first
k

columns. Writing
V=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, an element of the state space is an
n
-tuple of row vectors whose
span is all of
V
:

\mathrm{St}(n,k)=\{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in V^{n}:\operatorname{span}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=V\}.

The associated transition kernel
P_{n,k}
chooses an ordered donor-recipient
pair
(a,b)
, with
a\neq b
, and replaces
z_{b}
by
z_{b}+z_{a}
. Its
stationary distribution is uniform on
\mathrm{St}(n,k)
. To avoid periodicity
issues, we state the main result for the half-lazy kernel

Q_{n,k}=\frac{1}{2}(I+P_{n,k}).

Our main result settles a conjecture of
[
1
]
:

Theorem 1.1
.

There is an absolute constant
C<\infty
such that, for all sufficiently large

n
and every
1\leq k\leq n
, the half-lazy
k
-column transvection walk on

\mathrm{St}(n,k)
satisfies

\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}\leq Cnk\log n.

At the end of Section
3
, we prove a lower bound that matches up to
polylog(n)
factors:

Theorem 1.2
.

There is an absolute constant
c>0
such that, for all sufficiently large

n
and every
1\leq k\leq n
, the half-lazy
k
-column transvection walk on

\mathrm{St}(n,k)
satisfies

\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}\geq c\max\left(n\log n,\frac{nk}{\log(en)}\right).

The proof of Theorem
1.1
begins by comparing the transvection walk to a walk on a slightly larger state space, as in the previous work
[
2
]
. It also directly uses the main result of
[
2
]
to deal with the case that
k\approx n
is large. Our arguments are otherwise essentially unrelated to those of
[
2
]
.

The main idea behind our proof is to compare the transvection walk to a simpler collection of “frozen” walks obtained by conditioning on both the recipient coordinate and all non-recipient rows. The proof requires three main steps. First, we show that these frozen walks mix rapidly for a set of “typical” collections of donor vectors. Second, we show that the Markov chain rapidly enters this set of typical donor vectors and remains in this set for a long time. Finally, we show that it is possible to combine local estimates from steps 1 and 2 into a global bound. We think that this third step might be of independent interest, as it is often very natural to try to bound the mixing time of a complicated Markov chain in terms of simpler frozen or “censored” chains and these comparisons are known to be somewhat difficult in general
[
26
,
15
]
.

In order to illustrate the method, in Section
4
we introduce and analyze a random walk on generators of the Heisenberg group that is similar to (but more complicated than) the transvection walk.

1.1.
Notation and Detailed Proof sketch

1.1.1.
Notation for Fibres and Good Sets

We set some notation related to product spaces, fibres, and “good” sets that will be used throughout the proof sketch and the rest of the paper. Since our results will apply to walks other than the transvection walk, we use more generic notation.

For a product space
X=S^{N}
,

x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in X
,
i\in[N]
and
u\in S
, write

x^{i,u}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},u,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{N}).

For a fixed index
i
and configuration
x\in S^{N}
, define

x_{-i}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{N})\in S^{N-1}

and then define the corresponding
i
-fibre

\mathcal{F}_{i}(x_{-i})=\{x^{i,u}:u\in S\}\subseteq X.

For
G\subseteq X
, we call this fibre
G
-good if

\mathcal{F}_{i}(x_{-i})\cap G\neq\varnothing.

When the set
G
is clear from the context, we simply call the fibre
good.

Remark 1.3

(Relationship to Theorem
1.1
)
.

In the
k
-column transvection walk, we set
S=V=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
,
N=n
,
and
x^{i,u}
is the row tuple obtained from
x
by replacing row
i

by
u
. Notice that these fibres are fibres in the ambient product space; they are generally not contained in the subset
\mathrm{St}(n,k)
.

1.1.2.
Detailed Proof Sketch for the Transvection Walk

We give a proof sketch for the walk on
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
; the basic approach for the walk on the Heisenberg group will be similar. We use notation from this section when analyzing the transvection walk in Section
3
.

Let
V=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
and let
\mu
be the uniform distribution on
V^{n}
. For
\xi\in V^{*}
, define the associated function
\chi_{\xi}\,:\,V\to\{-1,1\}
by the formula

\chi_{\xi}(u)=(-1)^{\xi(u)},\qquad u\in V.

We extend this to a function
S_{\xi}\,:\,V^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}
by the formula

S_{\xi}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{\xi}(z_{i}).

The first important step is to show that the restriction of the walk to functions on the
following set of “good” or “well-balanced” elements of
\mathrm{St}(n,k)
:

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}=\left\{z\in\mathrm{St}(n,k):\max_{0\neq\xi\in V^{*}}|S_{\xi}(z)|\leq\frac{n}{4}\right\}

(1.1)

satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\Omega(\frac{1}{nk})
. See Inequality (
3.7
).

We now describe the proof of Theorem
3.5
. Fix a recipient row
i
and condition on the values
\{z_{j}\}_{j\neq i}
of all
other rows. Conditioning one step of the walk on row
i
being the recipient and then projecting to the fibre gives the following kernel on the possible value
u=z_{i}\in V
:

K_{i}\varphi(u)=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\varphi(u+z_{j}).

The functions
\{\chi_{\xi}\}_{\xi\in V^{*}}
diagonalize
K_{i}
, and the eigenvalue associated with index
\xi\neq 0
is

\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}(-1)^{\xi(z_{j})}.

By Proposition
3.4
, the condition that the fibre
\mathcal{F}_{i}(z_{-i})
be
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}
-good is equivalent, up to a contribution that is negligible for large
n
, to a uniform bound on these nontrivial eigenvalues.

In particular, the operators associated with good fibres have spectral gaps uniformly bounded away from 0; see Proposition
3.4
. The tensorization property of entropy (see Inequality (
2.2
)), combined with a standard bound on log-Sobolev inequalities in terms of spectral gaps, then gives a local estimate through Proposition
2.2

\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu}(F^{2})\leq Cnk\,\mathcal{E}_{P_{n,k}}^{\mu}(F,F)

(1.2)

for functions
F
supported on
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}
.

We will see that, in order to use these local log-Sobolev inequalities to obtain a global bound, it is enough to show that the chain enters
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}
quickly and remains there for a long time; see Lemma
3.3
and Theorem
2.7
. The required burn-in estimates can be obtained by careful use of existing strong results on the mixing time of the 1-column walk
[
1
, Theorem 1]
. More precisely, for each fixed
\xi\neq 0
, the projection

(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\mapsto(\xi(z_{1}),\ldots,\xi(z_{n}))

is simply a copy of the one-column transvection walk on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.
The one-column estimates
[
1
, Theorem 1]
and

[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
, combined with a union bound over the
2^{k}-1
nonzero
functionals, imply that the
k
-column walk enters
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}

after
O(nk\log n)
steps and then remains there for polynomially-in-
n
many steps
with high probability; see Lemma
3.3
.

Putting these ideas together: we show that the chain enters a good set quickly, prove a log-Sobolev inequality like (
1.2
) on this good set, and finally show that mixing on the good set implies fast mixing on the full state space by Theorem
2.7
.

Although this proof sketch is stated for the transvection walk, we will see in Section
4
that the same three-step structure of burn-in, local fibre estimates, and patching is used for the Heisenberg walk. The main differences come from the analogues of the functions
S_{\xi}
: for the Heisenberg walk these are replaced by the irreducible representations described in Proposition
4.11
and used in Proposition
4.14
.

1.2.
Related work

There is a long history of studying the mixing times of random walks on groups; see, for example,
[
14
,
28
,
20
]
. The product-replacement algorithm, introduced in

[
6
]
, is one of the most computationally-relevant such walks, as it is an efficient algorithm for generating random group elements; see
[
24
]
for
background. Closely-related walks on generating sets of abelian groups were studied in

[
12
]
, and their Theorem 4.1 gives a mixing time bound for the transvection walk studied in this paper.

The transvection walk is one of the better-studied special cases of the product-replacement algorithm. The spectral gap was computed as a consequence of Kassabov’s Kazhdan-constant estimates
[
19
]
. Since then, substantial progress has been made. The one-column walk is closely related to the stratified hypercube walk studied in
[
9
]
and was analyzed sharply in
[
1
]
. The paper
[
25
]
showed cutoff for product replacement algorithms on fixed finite groups, and in particular showed cutoff for the
k
-column walk for all fixed
k
as
n
grows. For the full transvection walk, the correct mixing time was found in
[
2
, Theorem 1]
.

Several closely-related random transvection walks on
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
were studied by Hildebrand
[
17
]
. The Heisenberg walk example in this paper is not as well-studied. We introduce it in this paper as a test case for our method and to show how one might deal with a problem that occurs in most walks but not in the transvection walk: the fibre kernel
K_{i}
can no longer be diagonalized in an extremely simple form.

At a high level, the ingredients in our paper are standard:
burn-in estimates, local Poincaré/log-Sobolev inequalities and a tensorization inequality, and a patching argument. The main contribution is making them work together for these walks.

The use of local log-Sobolev inequalities and the tensorization inequality (
2.2
) is closest in spirit to entropy
factorization methods as in

[
7
,
21
,
4
,
5
,
3
]
. These methods are also somewhat related to decomposition methods for Markov chains

[
22
,
18
]
. The local log-Sobolev inequalities are obtained for what we call “frozen” kernels, obtained by conditioning on or “freezing” certain parts of the state space. This is similar in spirit to previous work on censorship inequalities

[
26
,
15
]
, though we do not have any similar monotonicity result.

Finally, we find that the frozen kernels do not all have large spectral gaps. We avoid the kernels with small spectral gaps by using a burn-in argument. This is similar in spirit to the classical drift-and-minorization argument, which proves rapid mixing on a good set with a simple coupling bound and uses a burn-in argument to deal with the remainder of the state space
[
27
]
. The same basic approach of combining local estimates with a burn-in estimate has been used with other functional inequalities in many other contexts, as in
e.g.

[
8
]
. The details of the burn-in argument are idiosyncratic to these particular walks.

states and proves a generic mixing time upper bound, Theorem

2.
Generic Bounds for “Coordinate Replacement” Walks

This section is written independently of the two main examples.

2.1.
Generic notation

For a finite set
S
, we write
u_{S}
for the uniform distribution on
S
.

For a probability measure
\rho
on a finite set
E
, write

\operatorname{Ent}_{\rho}(f^{2})=\rho(f^{2}\log f^{2})-\rho(f^{2})\log\rho(f^{2}).

If
K
is a reversible Markov kernel with stationary measure
\rho
, define the Dirichlet form

\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\rho}(f,f)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in E}\rho(x)K(x,y)(f(x)-f(y))^{2},

with
\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\rho}(f,g)
the usual bilinear extension. When
\rho
is clear from context, we suppress it from the notation.

We often switch between continuous and discrete time for convenience. When bounds apply in both situations without important change, we sometimes replace a time
t
by
\lceil t\rceil
without comment.

A substochastic kernel is a nonnegative kernel whose row sums are at most one. If

K
is a reversible substochastic kernel on
(E,\rho)
, we use the
equivalent form

\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\rho}(f,f)=\langle f,(I-K)f\rangle_{\rho}.

For a reversible Markov kernel
K
with stationary law
\rho
, we define the
Poincaré constant
C_{P}
to be the smallest constant
C
satisfying

\operatorname{Var}_{\rho}(f)\leq C\,\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\rho}(f,f)

for all real
f
. The spectral gap is the reciprocal of this constant.

If
K
is a kernel on a finite set
E
and
A\subseteq E
is invariant under
K
, then the restriction of
K
to
A
is the Markov kernel
K_{A}(x,y)=K(x,y)
,
x,y\in A
.

For a nonnegative function
u
on
E
, define the entropy of the
subprobability density
u
by

H_{\rho}(u)=\rho\left[u\log\frac{u}{\rho(u)}\right],

with the usual convention at
u=0
.

We recall the following result for obtaining log-Sobolev inequalities from Poincaré inequalities (see
e.g.

[
11
]
):

Lemma 2.1

(Poincaré to log-Sobolev)
.

There is a universal constant
C<\infty
such that the following holds.
Let
K
be reversible on a finite set
E
, with stationary law
\rho
and
Poincaré constant at most
C_{P}
. Then for every real
f
,

\operatorname{Ent}_{\rho}(f^{2})\leq CC_{P}\log(1/\rho_{*})\,\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\rho}(f,f),

where
\rho_{*}=\min_{x\in E}\rho(x)
.

2.2.
Coordinate-replacement kernels

Let
S
be a finite set, let
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{X}=S^{N}
, and let
\mu
be the uniform measure on
\mathcal{X}
. We use the fibre notation introduced in Section
1.1.1
.

We consider kernels
P
on
\mathcal{X}
with the following structure. For
each coordinate
i
, and each

x_{-i}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{N})
, there is a Markov kernel

K_{i}(x_{-i})
on
S
, reversible with respect to uniform measure on
S
,
such that

Pf(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{u\in S}K_{i}(x_{-i};x_{i},u)f(x^{i,u}),

(2.1)

where
x^{i,u}
is obtained from
x
by replacing coordinate
i
by

u
. We call such kernels
coordinate replacement
kernels. The walks studied in this paper do not have this form, but the walks obtained by conditioning on the recipient coordinate do have this form; their fibre kernels are displayed in (
3.2
) and (
4.6
).

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be a set with
\mu(\Omega)>0
that is invariant under
P
, and define the restricted measure

\pi=\mu(\cdot\mid\Omega).

We also assume that
P
is reversible with respect to
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
; hence the restriction of
P
to
\Omega
is reversible with respect to
\pi
.

We shall repeatedly use the tensorization inequality

\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu}(F^{2})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu\left[\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu}\bigl(F^{2}\mid(X_{\ell})_{\ell\neq i}\bigr)\right],

(2.2)

where
\mu
is a product measure and
X\sim\mu
.

We apply Lemma
2.1
to our coordinate-replacement kernels:

Proposition 2.2

(Fibre-gap to local-LSI )
.

Let
P
be a coordinate-replacement kernel of the form
(
2.1
), and let
G\subseteq\Omega
. Suppose
there is
\gamma>0
such that, for every coordinate
i
and every
configuration
x_{-i}
whose
i
-fibre

\{x^{i,u}:u\in S\}

intersects
G
, the fibre kernel
K_{i}(x_{-i})
has spectral gap at least

\gamma
with respect to uniform measure on
S
. Then every nonnegative

F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\operatorname{supp}(F)\subseteq G
satisfies

\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu}(F^{2})\leq C\frac{N\log|S|}{\gamma}\,\mathcal{E}_{P}^{\mu}(F,F),

(2.3)

where
C<\infty
is universal.

Proof.

We begin with the tensorization inequality (
2.2
). Fix
i
and condition
on
X_{-i}=x_{-i}
. If the fibre does not intersect
G
, then
F

vanishes identically on that fibre and the conditional entropy is zero. If the
fibre intersects
G
, apply Lemma
2.1
to the kernel

K_{i}(x_{-i})
, whose stationary law is uniform on
S
, to get

\operatorname{Ent}_{u\sim\operatorname{Unif}(S)}(F(x^{i,u})^{2})\leq C\frac{\log|S|}{\gamma}\mathcal{E}_{K_{i}(x_{-i})}(u\mapsto F(x^{i,u}),u\mapsto F(x^{i,u})).

Averaging over
x_{-i}
and summing over
i
, while using
(
2.1
), gives

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu\bigl[\mathcal{E}_{K_{i}(X_{-i})}(F,F)\bigr]=N\mathcal{E}_{P}^{\mu}(F,F),

which proves (
2.3
).
∎

2.3.
Entropy decay for killed chains

We will be proving log-Sobolev inequalities only on “good” sets such as that in Equation (
1.1
). In order to apply these to the full state space, we will first apply the log-Sobolev inequalities to a sub-stochastic chain that is “killed” when it leaves the good set, then show that paths of the original chain are close (in an appropriate sense) to those in the killed chain. This section collects the estimates required to apply log-Sobolev inequalities to sub-stochastic chains, and mimics standard estimates such as those in
[
11
]
quite closely.

Let
G\subseteq\Omega
with
\pi(G)>0
, and let
K_{G}
be
P
killed on
exiting
G
:

K_{G}(x,y)=P(x,y)\mathbf{1}_{\{x,y\in G\}},\qquad x,y\in G.

(2.4)

Let
\pi_{G}=\pi(\cdot\mid G)
. The kernel
K_{G}
is reversible and
substochastic on
(G,\pi_{G})
. Its average killing rate is

\delta_{G}=\pi_{G}(1-K_{G}1).

By reversibility of
P
,

\delta_{G}\leq\frac{\pi(G^{c})}{\pi(G)}.

(2.5)

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume that, for every nonnegative
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with

\operatorname{supp}(F)\subseteq G
,

\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu}(F^{2})\leq A\mathcal{E}_{P}^{\mu}(F,F).

(2.6)

Then every nonnegative
f:G\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi_{G}}(f^{2})\leq A\mathcal{E}_{K_{G}}^{\pi_{G}}(f,f).

(2.7)

Proof.

Extend
f
by zero to
\mathcal{X}
:

\widetilde{f}(x)=f(x)\mathbf{1}_{G}(x).

Since
G\subseteq\Omega
and
\pi=\mu(\cdot\mid\Omega)
, we have

\mu(\cdot\mid G)=\pi_{G}
. Put
q=\mu(G)
. Then

\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu}(\widetilde{f}^{2})=q\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi_{G}}(f^{2})+q\pi_{G}(f^{2})\log\frac{1}{q}\geq q\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi_{G}}(f^{2}).

Also

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{P}^{\mu}(\widetilde{f},\widetilde{f})

\displaystyle=\langle\widetilde{f},(I-P)\widetilde{f}\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=q\langle f,(I-K_{G})f\rangle_{\pi_{G}}=q\mathcal{E}_{K_{G}}^{\pi_{G}}(f,f).

Applying (
2.6
) to
\widetilde{f}
and dividing by
q

proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 2.4

(Entropy decay for a substochastic chain)
.

Let
K
be a reversible substochastic kernel on a finite probability space

(E,\rho)
, and let

\delta=\rho(1-K1).

Assume that, for all nonnegative
f
,

\operatorname{Ent}_{\rho}(f^{2})\leq A\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\rho}(f,f).

(2.8)

Let
T_{t}=e^{t(K-I)}
and set
u_{t}=T_{t}u_{0}
for any
u_{0}\geq 0
. Then

H_{\rho}(u_{t})\leq e^{-t/A}H_{\rho}(u_{0})+A\delta\,\rho(u_{0})(1-e^{-t/A}).

(2.9)

Proof.

It is enough to consider only strictly positive
u_{t}
. Let

m_{t}=\rho(u_{t})
and
h_{t}=\sqrt{u_{t}}
. Differentiating gives

\frac{d}{dt}H_{\rho}(u_{t})=-\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\rho}\left(u_{t},\log\frac{u_{t}}{m_{t}}\right).

Write

w_{xy}=\rho(x)K(x,y),\qquad\kappa_{x}=\rho(x)(1-K1(x)).

Then, for real
f,g
,

\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\rho}(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y}w_{xy}(f(x)-f(y))(g(x)-g(y))+\sum_{x}\kappa_{x}f(x)g(x).

(2.10)

We claim that

\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\rho}\left(u_{t},\log\frac{u_{t}}{m_{t}}\right)\geq\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\rho}(h_{t},h_{t})-m_{t}\delta.

(2.11)

Write
u_{x}=u_{t}(x)
,
h_{x}=h_{t}(x)=\sqrt{u_{x}}
, and
m=m_{t}
. For the
first term in (
2.10
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y}w_{xy}(u_{x}-u_{y})\left(\log\frac{u_{x}}{m}-\log\frac{u_{y}}{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y}w_{xy}(u_{x}-u_{y})(\log u_{x}-\log u_{y})

\displaystyle\geq 2\sum_{x,y}w_{xy}(h_{x}-h_{y})^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y}w_{xy}(h_{x}-h_{y})^{2},

where we used

(a-b)(\log a-\log b)\geq 4(\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b})^{2}.

For the second term in (
2.10
), applying

s\log s\geq s-1
with
s=u_{x}/m
gives

u_{x}\log\frac{u_{x}}{m}=m\left(\frac{u_{x}}{m}\log\frac{u_{x}}{m}\right)\geq u_{x}-m.

Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\kappa_{x}u_{x}\log\frac{u_{x}}{m}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{x}\kappa_{x}(u_{x}-m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\kappa_{x}h_{x}^{2}-m\sum_{x}\kappa_{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\kappa_{x}h_{x}^{2}-m\delta,

since

\sum_{x}\kappa_{x}=\sum_{x}\rho(x)(1-K1(x))=\rho(1-K1)=\delta.

Combining the last two estimates in (
2.10
)
gives

\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\rho}\left(u_{t},\log\frac{u_{t}}{m_{t}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y}w_{xy}(h_{t}(x)-h_{t}(y))^{2}+\sum_{x}\kappa_{x}h_{t}(x)^{2}-m_{t}\delta=\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\rho}(h_{t},h_{t})-m_{t}\delta,

proving Inequality (
2.11
).

Continuing, by (
2.8
),

H_{\rho}(u_{t})=\operatorname{Ent}_{\rho}(h_{t}^{2})\leq A\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\rho}(h_{t},h_{t})
. Therefore

\frac{d}{dt}H_{\rho}(u_{t})\leq-\frac{1}{A}H_{\rho}(u_{t})+m_{t}\delta.

Since
m_{t}
is nonincreasing,
m_{t}\leq m_{0}=\rho(u_{0})
. Gronwall’s
inequality proves (
2.9
).
∎

Finally, we include the following standard estimate for future reference:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\lambda=u\rho
be a subprobability on
E
, and set

m=\lambda(E)=\rho(u)
. Then

\|\lambda-\rho\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq(1-m)+\sqrt{\frac{H_{\rho}(u)}{2}}.

Proof.

If
m=0
, the claim is trivial. Otherwise write

\widehat{\lambda}=\lambda/m
. Then

\|\lambda-\rho\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq(1-m)+m\|\widehat{\lambda}-\rho\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

Pinsker’s inequality gives

\|\widehat{\lambda}-\rho\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\sqrt{D(\widehat{\lambda}\|\rho)/2},

and
H_{\rho}(u)=mD(\widehat{\lambda}\|\rho)
. Since
m\leq 1
, the result
follows.
∎

2.4.
Mixing condition

We keep notation from the previous subsection. This subsection compares the original chain after burn-in with the chain killed on leaving the good set
G
. The comparison lets entropy decay for the killed chain be transferred back to the original chain, provided paths rarely leave
G
.

For
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\Omega
, define

\alpha_{s}^{x}=\delta_{x}P^{s},\qquad\alpha_{s,G}^{x}(A)=\alpha_{s}^{x}(A\cap G).

Let

\widetilde{\alpha}_{s,t}^{x}=\alpha_{s}^{x}e^{t(P-I)},\qquad\lambda_{s,t}^{x}=\alpha_{s,G}^{x}e^{t(K_{G}-I)}.

Thus
\widetilde{\alpha}_{s,t}^{x}
is the original continuous-time evolution
after a discrete burn-in, while
\lambda_{s,t}^{x}
is the killed evolution
started from the restriction of
\alpha_{s}^{x}
to
G
. In the following,
\operatorname{Poi}(t)
denotes the Poisson distribution with rate
t
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For every integer
s\geq 0
, integer
L\geq 1
, and
t\geq 0
,

\|\widetilde{\alpha}_{s,t}^{x}-\lambda_{s,t}^{x}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\mathbb{P}_{x}(\exists\,u\in\{0,1,\ldots,L\}:X_{s+u}\notin G)+\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Poi}(t)>L).

(2.12)

The same right-hand side also bounds
1-\lambda_{s,t}^{x}(G)
.

Proof.

This is a simple coupling argument. Note that the number of jumps made in any time interval by the original chain is a Poisson process. Conditional on making
r
jumps
after time
s
, the original and killed chains agree on every trajectory that
stays in
G
during the discrete interval
s,s+1,\ldots,s+r
. For

r\leq L
, disagreement is contained in the event displayed in
(
2.12
); for
r>L
, use the trivial bound
1
.
Averaging over
r\sim\operatorname{Poi}(t)
gives the total variation bound,
and the same coupling gives the bound on the lost mass of the killed chain.
∎

We now have the main generic result. We restate some of the basic assumptions in the theorem statement for ease of reference:

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
P
be a coordinate-replacement kernel as in
(
2.1
) on
\mathcal{X}=S^{N}
, with invariant
set
\Omega
, product law
\mu
, stationary law
\pi=\mu(\cdot\mid\Omega)
,
and good set
G\subseteq\Omega
. Assume
\pi(G)>0
. Suppose that the
following hold.

(i)

There is a constant
A<\infty
such that, for every nonnegative

F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\operatorname{supp}(F)\subseteq G
,

\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu}(F^{2})\leq A\mathcal{E}_{P}^{\mu}(F,F).

(ii)

There are
t_{*}\geq 0
, an integer
L\geq 1
, and
\eta\geq 0
such
that, uniformly in
x\in\Omega
and all integers
s\geq t_{*}
,

\mathbb{P}_{x}(\exists\,u\in\{0,1,\ldots,L\}:X_{s+u}\notin G)\leq\eta.

Set

t_{\mathrm{conf}}=2A\log(e+\log|\Omega|),

(2.13)

and define

\zeta=\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Poi}(t_{\mathrm{conf}})>L),\qquad R=e^{-t_{\mathrm{conf}}/A}\log|\Omega|+A\frac{\pi(G^{c})}{\pi(G)}.

(2.14)

Then, for every
x\in\Omega
and every integer
s\geq t_{*}
,

\left\|\delta_{x}P^{s}e^{t_{\mathrm{conf}}(P-I)}-\pi\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq 2(\eta+\zeta)+\sqrt{R/2}+\pi(G^{c}).

(2.15)

Consequently, if

2(\eta+\zeta)+\sqrt{R/2}+\pi(G^{c})+\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Poi}(2t_{*})<t_{*})\leq\frac{1}{4},

(2.16)

then the continuous-time chain satisfies

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\mathrm{cont}}(1/4)\leq 2t_{*}+t_{\mathrm{conf}}.

Proof.

Write
\pi_{G}=\pi(\cdot\mid G)
. By
Proposition
2.3
, applied to
P
and
G
,
the killed kernel satisfies

\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi_{G}}(f^{2})\leq A\mathcal{E}_{K_{G}}^{\pi_{G}}(f,f).

Let
\lambda_{0}
be a subprobability on
G
, let

u_{0}=d\lambda_{0}/d\pi_{G}
, and let
u_{t}=e^{t(K_{G}-I)}u_{0}
. If

m=\pi_{G}(u_{0})>0
, then

H_{\pi_{G}}(u_{0})=mD(\lambda_{0}/m\|\pi_{G})\leq\log|G|\leq\log|\Omega|.

If
m=0
, the entropy is zero. Lemma
2.4
and
(
2.5
) give

H_{\pi_{G}}(u_{t})\leq e^{-t/A}\log|\Omega|+A\frac{\pi(G^{c})}{\pi(G)}.

At
t=t_{\mathrm{conf}}
, the right-hand side is
R
.

Now take
\lambda_{0}=\alpha_{s,G}^{x}
. Lemma
2.6
,
applied with
t=t_{\mathrm{conf}}
, gives

\|\widetilde{\alpha}_{s,t_{\mathrm{conf}}}^{x}-\lambda_{s,t_{\mathrm{conf}}}^{x}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\eta+\zeta,\qquad 1-\lambda_{s,t_{\mathrm{conf}}}^{x}(G)\leq\eta+\zeta.

Combining the entropy bound with Lemma
2.5
gives

\|\lambda_{s,t_{\mathrm{conf}}}^{x}-\pi_{G}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\eta+\zeta+\sqrt{R/2}.

Finally,
\|\pi_{G}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\pi(G^{c})
. The triangle inequality proves
(
2.15
).

For continuous time, let
J\sim\operatorname{Poi}(2t_{*})
. Poissonization gives

\delta_{x}e^{(2t_{*}+t_{\mathrm{conf}})(P-I)}=\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{x}P^{J}e^{t_{\mathrm{conf}}(P-I)}\right].

On the event
\{J\geq t_{*}\}
, use (
2.15
); on the complement,
use the trivial bound. Condition (
2.16
)
proves the continuous-time bound.
∎

3.
The
k
-column transvection walk

3.1.
Basic notation and facts about the transvection chain

We recall some notation from the introduction, then introduce notation that will be used in the proofs. Fix
1\leq k\leq n
and set

V=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k},\qquad\mathrm{St}(n,k)=\{z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in V^{n}:\operatorname{span}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=V\}.

Let
\pi=\pi_{n,k}
be uniform on
\mathrm{St}(n,k)
, and let
\mu
be
uniform on
V^{n}
. For ordered distinct indices
a,b\in[n]
, define

z^{a\to b}_{\ell}=\begin{cases}z_{b}+z_{a},&\ell=b,\\
z_{\ell},&\ell\neq b.\end{cases}

We call
a
the donor and
b
the recipient. We define the kernel for the transvection walk

P=P_{n,k},\qquad Pf(z)=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{a\neq b}f(z^{a\to b}).

(3.1)

The maps
z\mapsto z^{a\to b}
are
\mu
-preserving involutions; hence
P

is reversible with respect to
\mu
on
V^{n}
and with respect to
\pi
on

\mathrm{St}(n,k)
. In particular, row addition preserves the span of the rows, so it preserves
\mathrm{St}(n,k)
.

We also claim the chain is irreducible on
\mathrm{St}(n,k)
. To see this, note that over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
you can write the row-swap elementary row operation
(r_{i},r_{j})\to(r_{j},r_{i})
as a sequence of the three additions
(r_{i},r_{j})\to(r_{i}+r_{j},r_{j})\to(r_{i}+r_{j},r_{i})\to(r_{j},r_{i})
; this means that all elementary row operations can be written in terms of the moves of this Markov chain.

In the notation of Section
2
, the
coordinate space is
S=V
, the number of coordinates is
N=n
, and the
recipient-coordinate fibre kernel is obtained by conditioning on the recipient coordinate and projecting the update to that fibre:

K_{i}\varphi(u)=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\varphi(u+z_{j}).

(3.2)

3.2.
Description of the good set

Let
V^{*}
be the dual of the vector space
V
. For
\xi\in V^{*}
, define

\chi_{\xi}(u)=(-1)^{\xi(u)},\qquad S_{\xi}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{\xi}(z_{i}).

Define the good set

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}=\left\{z\in\mathrm{St}(n,k):\max_{\xi\in V^{*}\setminus\{0\}}|S_{\xi}(z)|\leq\frac{n}{4}\right\}.

(3.3)

Lemma 3.1

(Stationary size of the transvection good set)
.

There are absolute constants
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
c_{0}>0
such that, if

k\leq\varepsilon_{0}n
, then

\pi(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}^{c})\leq e^{-c_{0}n}

and

\mu(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}^{c})\leq e^{-c_{0}n}.

Proof.

Fix
\xi\neq 0
. We claim that under
\pi
, the projection

z\mapsto(\xi(z_{1}),\ldots,\xi(z_{n}))

is uniform on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. To see this, choose coordinates so that

\xi
is projection onto the first coordinate. For any two nonzero vectors

q,q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, choose
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
with
Aq=q^{\prime}
. Left
multiplication by
A
bijects the full-rank
n\times k
matrices whose first
column is
q
with those whose first column is
q^{\prime}
. Hence the number of
rank-
k
completions is independent of
q
, and the pushforward of
\pi

is uniform on
\mathrm{St}(n,1)=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.

Consequently,
S_{\xi}
has the law of a sum of
n
independent Rademacher
variables, conditioned on the vector not being identically zero. The
conditioning event has probability at least
1/2
, so Hoeffding’s inequality
gives

\pi(|S_{\xi}|>n/4)\leq Ce^{-cn}.

A union bound over the
2^{k}-1
choices of
\xi\in V^{\ast}\backslash\{0\}
gives

\pi(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}^{c})\leq C2^{k}e^{-cn}.

Choose
0<\varepsilon_{0}<1
small enough that

\varepsilon_{0}\log 2<c/2
, and then choose
c_{0}<c/2
. If

k\leq\varepsilon_{0}n
, the last display is at most
e^{-c_{0}n}
for all large

n
, after decreasing
c_{0}
if necessary to absorb the leading constant.

The second bound is very similar. Let
Z\sim\mu
and observe that

\left\{z\in V^{n}:\max_{\xi\in V^{*}\setminus\{0\}}|S_{\xi}(z)|\leq\frac{n}{4}\right\}\subseteq\mathrm{St}(n,k).

Indeed, if
z\notin\mathrm{St}(n,k)
, then

\operatorname{span}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\neq V
, so there is some nonzero

\xi\in V^{*}
such that
\xi(z_{i})=0
for every
i
. For this
\xi
,

S_{\xi}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{\xi(z_{i})}=n,

which is larger than
n/4
. Hence, as subsets of the ambient space
V^{n}
,

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}=\left\{z\in V^{n}:\max_{\xi\in V^{*}\setminus\{0\}}|S_{\xi}(z)|\leq\frac{n}{4}\right\}.

Now fix
0\neq\xi\in V^{*}
. Under
\mu
, the random variables

\chi_{\xi}(Z_{1}),\ldots,\chi_{\xi}(Z_{n})
are independent Rademacher variables.
Thus Hoeffding’s inequality gives

\mu\left(|S_{\xi}(Z)|>\frac{n}{4}\right)\leq Ce^{-cn}.

Taking a union bound over the
2^{k}-1
nonzero functionals gives

\mu(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}^{c})\leq\sum_{\xi\in V^{*}\setminus\{0\}}\mu\left(|S_{\xi}(Z)|>\frac{n}{4}\right)\leq C2^{k}e^{-cn}.

After decreasing
\varepsilon_{0}
and
c_{0}
, if necessary, this is at most

e^{-c_{0}n}
whenever
k\leq\varepsilon_{0}n
, for all sufficiently large

n
.
∎

3.3.
Burn-in

We will prove our burn-in result by finding many copies of the
1
-column walk inside of the
k
-column walk. We repeatedly use the following mixing estimate from
[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
(see also
[
1
, Theorem 1]
):

Lemma 3.2
.

For every fixed
B<\infty
there is a constant
c_{B}<\infty
such that the
one-column transvection walk on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, started from any nonzero
vector, is within
n^{-B}
of stationarity after
c_{B}n\log n
steps.

Our main burn-in estimate is:

Lemma 3.3

(Burn-in and occupancy of the good set)
.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}
be as in Lemma
3.1
. For every
fixed
M<\infty
there is a constant
C_{M}<\infty
such that the following
holds. Assume
1\leq k\leq\varepsilon_{0}n
and set

t_{*}=C_{M}nk\log n.

(3.4)

Then, uniformly in
x\in\mathrm{St}(n,k)
and all integer times

t\geq t_{*}
,

\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\notin\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}})\leq n^{-M}.

(3.5)

Consequently, if
L\leq n^{3}
and
M=10
, then uniformly in
x
and

s\geq t_{*}
,

\mathbb{P}_{x}(\exists\,u\in\{0,1,\ldots,L\}:X_{s+u}\notin\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}})=o(1).

(3.6)

Proof.

For each nonzero
\xi\in V^{*}
, the projected process

\Pi_{\xi}(X_{t})=(\xi(X_{t}[1]),\ldots,\xi(X_{t}[n]))

is a nonzero vector, since
X_{t}\in\mathrm{St}(n,k)
and the rows span
V
. If
row
a
is added to row
b
, then

\xi(X_{t+1}[b])=\xi(X_{t}[b])+\xi(X_{t}[a]),

so
\Pi_{\xi}(X_{t})
is exactly the one-column transvection walk on

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.

Let
P_{1}
be the one-column kernel and let
\pi_{1}
be its stationary distribution. Set

d(t)=\sup_{x}\|P_{1}^{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi_{1}\|_{\mathrm{TV}},\qquad\bar{d}(t)=\sup_{x,y}\|P_{1}^{t}(x,\cdot)-P_{1}^{t}(y,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

By Lemma
3.2
, for every fixed
B<\infty
there is

c_{B}<\infty
such that
d(c_{B}n\log n)\leq n^{-B}
. Since

\bar{d}(s+t)\leq\bar{d}(s)\bar{d}(t)
and
\bar{d}(t)\leq 2d(t)
, after

\ell c_{B}n\log n
steps the distance is at most
(2n^{-B})^{\ell}
. Taking

B=M+2
and
\ell=k
, and then choosing
C_{M}
large enough in
(
3.4
), ensures that, for every fixed
\xi\neq 0
, the law of

\Pi_{\xi}(X_{t})
is within
2^{-k}n^{-M-1}
of stationarity for all integer
times
t\geq t_{*}
and all sufficiently large
n
.
Therefore, using the stationary bound from the proof of Lemma
3.1
,

\mathbb{P}_{x}(|S_{\xi}(X_{t})|>n/4)\leq Ce^{-cn}+2^{-k}n^{-M-1}.

Taking a union bound over
\xi\neq 0
gives

\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\notin\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}})\leq C2^{k}e^{-cn}+n^{-M-1}\leq n^{-M}

for all large
n
. This proves (
3.5
). Finally,
(
3.6
) follows by applying (
3.5
) at all
times
s,s+1,\ldots,s+L
and using
L\leq n^{3}
with
M=10
.
∎

3.4.
Spectral gap of the one-row convolution kernel

Proposition 3.4

(Transvection fibre spectral gap)
.

For all sufficiently large
n
, the following holds. Fix a recipient
coordinate
i
and the non-recipient rows
(z_{j})_{j\neq i}
. If the corresponding

i
-fibre intersects
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}
, then the fibre kernel
(
3.2
) on
V=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
has spectral gap at least
1/2
.

Proof.

Choose a point in the fibre,

z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},u_{0},z_{i+1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}.

The kernel
K_{i}
is reversible with respect to uniform measure on
V
, since

V
has exponent two. Its eigenfunctions are the characters
\chi_{\xi}
,
with eigenvalues

\widehat{\nu}_{i}(\xi)=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\chi_{\xi}(z_{j}).

For
\xi\neq 0
, since
z\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tr}}
,

\left|\sum_{j\neq i}\chi_{\xi}(z_{j})\right|=|S_{\xi}(z)-\chi_{\xi}(u_{0})|\leq\frac{n}{4}+1.

Thus, for all sufficiently large
n
,

|\widehat{\nu}_{i}(\xi)|\leq\frac{n/4+1}{n-1}\leq\frac{1}{2},\qquad\xi\neq 0.

Every nonconstant eigenvalue is therefore at most
1/2
in absolute value,
so the spectral gap is at least
1/2
.
∎

3.5.
Application of Theorem
2.7

We prove our main result for the transvection walk:

Theorem 3.5
.

There exist absolute constants
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
C_{\mathrm{lin}}<\infty

such that, for all sufficiently large

and all
1\leq k\leq\varepsilon_{0}n

We check that we can satisfy the assumptions of Theorem

, with the choices

By Propositions

and

, and using the fact that

Thus we may take

.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}
be the constant from Theorem

. If

, the result is exactly Theorem

.

If

, we note that the

. Passing to the half-lazy version changes the bound by at most a universal constant. Thus

For completeness, we give the lower bounds as well, though both are essentially well-known from

,

,

]
:

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We prove the two lower bounds separately.

To prove the first, consider the map

from

to
\mathrm{St}(n,1)
given by simply taking the first column. If

. The latter is

by

]

To prove the second, we use a counting bound. The state space has

|\mathrm{St}(n,k)|=\prod_{q=0}^{k-1}(2^{n}-2^{q})=2^{nk}\prod_{q=0}^{k-1}(1-2^{q-n}).

Since

\prod_{q=0}^{k-1}(1-2^{q-n})\geq\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}(1-2^{-j})=:c_{0}>0,

(3.8)

with

is uniform,

This proves

4.
Power-averaged product replacement algorithm on Heisenberg groups

We describe a small modification of the usual product-replacement algorithm (PRA; see
[
6
,
24
]
) on the Heisenberg group, which we call the
power-averaged
PRA (PA-PRA).

Remark 4.1

(Why Change the PRA?)
.

The PA-PRA algorithm defined in Equation (
4.4
) is not more challenging to implement or run than the usual PRA algorithm. We also expect it to be slightly more efficient; it is similar in spirit to lifted or momentum-based Markov chains such as
[
10
]
. In these senses, the PA-PRA is a natural generalization of the usual PRA.

However, we introduced this walk primarily for technical reasons. Taking random powers substantially simplifies several steps in the proof, for essentially the same reason that Fourier analysis of random walks on groups is much easier for conjugacy-invariant walks. See e.g.
[
14
,
13
]
for explanations of technical problems in applying Fourier analysis to random walks on groups without these sorts of symmetries.

4.1.
Notation for Heisenberg Group

We give basic notation and facts about the Heisenberg group. None of the group-theoretic results in this paper are new, and we closely follow the notation and development of
[
23
]
.

Throughout Section
4
, fix an odd prime
p
. Let
W
be a vector space
of dimension
h=2m
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, equipped with a nondegenerate alternating
form

\omega:W\times W\to\mathbb{F}_{p}.

We denote the set associated with the Heisenberg group by

\mathsf{H}_{m}=\mathsf{H}(W)=W\times\mathbb{F}_{p}.

We often omit the subscript
m
when the discussion applies to all
m
. For an element
(v,z)\in\mathsf{H}
, we call
v
the
horizontal
coordinate and
z
the
central
coordinate. For an element
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})\in\mathsf{H}^{r}
, we often write

g_{i}=(v_{i},z_{i})
, with
v_{i}\in W
and
z_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Multiplication in
\mathsf{H}
is given by

(v,z)(w,t)=\left(v+w,\,z+t+\frac{1}{2}\omega(v,w)\right).

(4.1)

The identity is
(0,0)
, the inverse of
(v,z)
is
(-v,-z)
, and the commutator is

[(v,z),(w,t)]=(0,\omega(v,w)).

We collect some basic facts about
\mathsf{H}
and related objects. Since
\omega(v,v)=0
,

(v,z)^{a}=(av,az),\qquad a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}.

(4.2)

Thus
\mathsf{H}
has exponent
p
. We claim that the center of
H
is

Z(H)=\{0\}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}.

To see this, not that if
v\neq 0
, then by nondegeneracy of
\omega
there is

w\in W
with
\omega(v,w)\neq 0
, so
(v,z)
does not commute with

(w,0)
. The quotient map

H\to H/Z(H)\simeq W,\qquad(v,z)\mapsto v,

keeps only the horizontal coordinate. Also, since

[(v,z),(w,t)]=(0,\omega(v,w))

and
\omega
is nondegenerate, the commutators generate all of

\{0\}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Thus the abelianization is naturally identified with

W
.

For
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\in\mathsf{H}
, denote by
\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\rangle
the subgroup of
\mathsf{H}
that they generate. Deliberately mimicking the notation for the transvection walk, set

\Omega=V_{r}(\mathsf{H})=\{g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})\in\mathsf{H}^{r}:\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\rangle=\mathsf{H}\}.

We have:

Lemma 4.2
.

A tuple
((v_{1},z_{1}),\ldots,(v_{r},z_{r}))
generates
\mathsf{H}

if and only if

\operatorname{span}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})=W.

(4.3)

Proof.

The condition (
4.3
) is necessary because the
quotient map
\mathsf{H}\to\mathsf{H}/(\{0\}\times\mathbb{F}_{p})\cong W
is given by
(v,z)\mapsto v
.

Conversely, assume that the horizontal vectors
v_{i}
span
W
.
The subgroup
H_{0}=\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\rangle
, where

g_{i}=(v_{i},z_{i})
, maps onto
W
under the quotient map

\mathsf{H}\to W
. Since
m\geq 1
and
\omega
is nondegenerate, there
exist
v,w\in W
with
\omega(v,w)=1
. By surjectivity, choose

x,y\in H_{0}
with horizontal coordinates
v
and
w
, respectively.
Writing
x=(v,z)
and
y=(w,t)
, the commutator formula gives

[x,y]=(0,\omega(v,w))=(0,1).

Thus
H_{0}
contains
\{0\}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Since
H_{0}
also maps
onto
W
, it follows that
H_{0}=\mathsf{H}
.
∎

A
symplectic basis
associated with the alternating form
\omega
is a basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{h}
such that

\omega(b_{2q-1},b_{2q})=1
,

\omega(b_{2q},b_{2q-1})=-1
, and all other pairings vanish. Such a basis
exists for every nondegenerate alternating form.

4.2.
Notation for PA-PRA on the Heisenberg group

Let
\pi
be uniform on
\Omega
, and let
\mu
be uniform on

\mathsf{H}^{r}
. By (
4.3
), conditioning
\mu
on

\Omega
gives
\pi
.

For distinct
i,j\in[r]
and
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, define the right and left maps
T^{R}_{j\to i,a},T^{L}_{j\to i,a}
by the formulas:

T^{R}_{j\to i,a}g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i-1},g_{i}g_{j}^{a},g_{i+1},\ldots,g_{r}),

and

T^{L}_{j\to i,a}g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i-1},g_{j}^{a}g_{i},g_{i+1},\ldots,g_{r}).

In both cases, we call the index
j
the
donor
and the index
i
the
recipient
.

We claim that, for any
i,j,a
, the subgroup generated by the collections
T^{R}_{j\to i,a}g
and
T^{L}_{j\to i,a}g
is the same as the subgroup generated by the original collection
g
. To see this, note that the changed element
g_{i}
is a simple function of the new
coordinate
g_{j}^{a}g_{i}
or
g_{i}g_{j}^{a}
and the unchanged donor
g_{j}
, and hence can be recovered when generating the subgroup. In particular, the maps both preserve
\Omega
.

We define the non-lazy kernel for PA-PRA by:

Pf(g)=\frac{1}{2pr(r-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\left[f(T^{R}_{j\to i,a}g)+f(T^{L}_{j\to i,a}g)\right],

(4.4)

and
Q=\frac{1}{2}(I+P)
the usual
\frac{1}{2}
-lazy version.

Equation (
4.4
) also defines a kernel on all of
\mathsf{H}^{r}
, not just
\Omega
. Each move is a
bijection and its inverse is the corresponding move with exponent
-a
and
the same side. Hence
P
is reversible with respect to
\mu
on

\mathsf{H}^{r}
and with respect to
\pi
on
\Omega
.

We note a close connection between this walk and the transvection walk. If
g_{i}=(v_{i},z_{i})
and
g_{j}=(v_{j},z_{j})
, then both left and right moves send

v_{i}\leftarrow v_{i}+av_{j}.

(4.5)

Thus the horizontal projection is exactly the
p
-ary transvection walk on spanning

r
-tuples in
W
.

The main result of this section is the following upper bound. In reading this, we think of
r
as roughly corresponding to
n
in the transvection walk and
h
as roughly corresponding to
k
; the requirement
h\leq\varepsilon r
comes from the same calculation as the requirement that
k\leq\varepsilon n
in the transvection walk.

Theorem 4.3
.

Fix an odd prime
p
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. There is a constant

C_{p,\varepsilon}<\infty
such that the following holds. Let

h=2m\geq 2
and suppose
h\leq\varepsilon r
. For all sufficiently large

r
, the half-lazy PA-PRA walk on

V_{r}(\mathsf{H})
satisfies

\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}\leq C_{p,\varepsilon}r(h+\log r)\log r.

In particular, if
h\geq\log r
, then

\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}\leq C_{p,\varepsilon}rh\log r.

At the end of the section, we give two lower bounds that are close to matching the upper bound when combined.

Theorem 4.4

(Heisenberg lower bounds)
.

Fix an odd prime
p
. There are constants
c_{p}>0
and
r_{0}(p)<\infty

such that, for every
h=2m\geq 2
and every
r\geq\max\{h+1,r_{0}(p)\}
,
the half-lazy PA-PRA walk on
V_{r}(\mathsf{H})
satisfies

\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}\geq c_{p}\max\left\{r\log r,\,\frac{r(h+1)}{\log(er)}\right\}.

4.3.
Basic Facts about the PA-PRA kernel

We collect some basic facts.

Lemma 4.5

(Connectivity)
.

Assume
m\geq 1
and
r\geq h+1
. The PA-PRA
chain is irreducible on
\Omega
.

Proof.

Since the walk is reversible, it is enough to find a path from every
g\in\Omega
to one fixed element
g^{\circ}
. Fix a symplectic basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{h}
of

W
, with
\omega(b_{1},b_{2})=1
, and set the destination point

g^{\circ}=((b_{1},0),\ldots,(b_{h},0),(0,1),(0,0),\ldots,(0,0)).

Now fix a generic point
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})\in\Omega
, write
g_{i}=(v_{i},z_{i})
, and set

B=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{h},0,\ldots,0)\in W^{r}.

We first write a sequence of moves that start at
g
and end at a point whose horizontal tuple is
B
. Since

(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})
has rank
h
, it is possible to choose
M_{0}\in\mathrm{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
with

M_{0}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})=B
. Put
d=\det M_{0}
. Since
r>h
, the diagonal
matrix

D=\operatorname{diag}(1,\ldots,1,d^{-1},1,\ldots,1),

with
d^{-1}
in position
h+1
, fixes
B
. Thus

M=DM_{0}\in\mathrm{SL}_{r}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
also satisfies
M(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})=B
. Since

\mathrm{SL}_{r}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is generated by elementary transvections, there exists a sequence of right moves (that is, moves of the form
T^{R}_{j\to i,a}
) that act on the horizontal parts of
g
just like the matrix
M
. In particular, they transform the
horizontal tuple to
B
.

After reducing to the case that the vector of horizontal elements is in
B
, keep the notation
g
for the current state.
Its horizontal tuple is
B
, so
g_{h+1}=(0,z_{h+1})
. We next change
this coordinate to
(0,1)
. For any
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, four right moves on the
recipient coordinate
h+1
, with successive donors
1,2,1,2
and exponents

a,1,-a,-1
, replace
g_{h+1}
by

g_{h+1}g_{1}^{a}g_{2}g_{1}^{-a}g_{2}^{-1}=g_{h+1}[g_{1}^{a},g_{2}]=g_{h+1}(0,a).

Taking
a=1-z_{h+1}
makes
g_{h+1}=(0,1)
, without changing any horizontal coordinate.

We can now use the element
g_{h+1}=(0,1)
as a donor to standardize all other rows. For
1\leq i\leq h
, write

g_{i}=(b_{i},z_{i})
and replace
g_{i}
by
g_{i}g_{h+1}^{-z_{i}}
. For

i>h+1
, write
g_{i}=(0,z_{i})
and replace
g_{i}
by

g_{i}g_{h+1}^{-z_{i}}
. These moves send
g
to
g^{\circ}
. Reversing the
sequence connects
g^{\circ}
to every state, so the chain is irreducible on

\Omega
.
∎

For a fixed recipient coordinate
i
and frozen donors
(g_{j})_{j\neq i}
,
conditioning on
i
being the recipient and projecting to the fibre gives the one-coordinate fibre kernel

K_{i}\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2(r-1)}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\left[\varphi(xg_{j}^{a})+\varphi(g_{j}^{a}x)\right],\qquad x\in\mathsf{H}.

(4.6)

Equivalently, define the operators
R_{\nu},L_{\nu}
by the formulas

R_{\nu}f(x)=\sum_{g\in\mathsf{H}}\nu(g)f(xg),\qquad L_{\nu}f(x)=\sum_{g\in\mathsf{H}}\nu(g)f(gx).

(4.7)

Then

K_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(R_{\nu_{i}}+L_{\nu_{i}}),\qquad\nu_{i}=\frac{1}{r-1}\sum_{j\neq i}u_{\langle g_{j}\rangle},

where
u_{\langle g_{j}\rangle}
is the uniform measure on the cyclic subgroup

\langle g_{j}\rangle
.

4.4.
The horizontal good set and stationary size

For
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})\in\mathsf{H}^{r}
, write
g_{i}=(v_{i},z_{i})
. For

\xi\in W^{*}
, define

N_{\xi}(g)=|\{i\in[r]:\xi(v_{i})=0\}|

and the good set

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{H}}=\left\{g\in\Omega:N_{\xi}(g)\leq\beta_{0}r\text{ for every }0\neq\xi\in W^{*}\right\}.

(4.8)

We now fix various special constants that will be used as scales for the burn-in argument and the remainder of the proof. Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Define the function

I_{p}(\beta)=\beta\log(\beta p)+(1-\beta)\log\left(\frac{(1-\beta)p}{p-1}\right),\qquad\frac{1}{p}<\beta<1,

which is the large-deviation rate for the upper tail of a

\operatorname{Bin}(r,1/p)
random variable. Since

I_{p}(\beta)\uparrow\log p
as
\beta\uparrow 1
, it is possible to choose

\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(p,\varepsilon)
so that

\frac{1}{p}<\beta_{0}<1,\qquad\varepsilon\log p<I_{p}(\beta_{0}).

(4.9)

Set

\beta_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(1+\beta_{0})

(4.10)

and

\alpha_{0}=1-\beta_{0},\qquad\alpha_{*}=\frac{p-1}{p}.

Define

J_{p}(a,b)=\int_{a}^{b}\log\left(\frac{(p-1)(1-u)}{u}\right)\,du,\qquad 0<a<b<\alpha_{*}.

(4.11)

A direct calculation gives

J_{p}(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{*})=I_{p}(\beta_{0}).

(4.12)

By (
4.9
), it is possible to choose
\alpha_{1}\in(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{*})
and

\eta_{0}>0
such that

J_{p}(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})\geq\varepsilon\log p+3\eta_{0}.

(4.13)

Lemma 4.6

(Stationary size of the good set)
.

Fix
p,\varepsilon
as above. There is a constant
c_{p,\varepsilon}>0
such that, for all
h\leq\varepsilon r
,

\pi(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{H}}^{c})\leq e^{-c_{p,\varepsilon}r}.

Similarly, if
\mu
is uniform on
\mathsf{H}^{r}
, then

\mu(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{H}})\geq 1-o(1).

Proof.

Under
\pi
, the horizontal tuple
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})
is uniform on

\operatorname{St}_{\mathsf{H}}(r,h)=\{(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})\in W^{r}:\operatorname{span}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})=W\},

and, conditionally on this tuple, the central coordinates are independent and
uniform in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Fix
0\neq\xi\in W^{*}
. We claim that, under the uniform law on
\operatorname{St}_{\mathsf{H}}(r,h)
,
the projection

(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})\mapsto(\xi(v_{1}),\ldots,\xi(v_{r}))

is uniform on
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r}\setminus\{0\}
. To see this, note that after choosing coordinates so that

\xi
is the first coordinate functional, the number of ways to complete a
fixed nonzero first coordinate column to a full-rank
r\times h
matrix is the
same for every nonzero column (this follows from the fact that
\mathrm{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
acts transitively on
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r}\setminus\{0\}
).

Consequently, if
g\sim\pi
, then
N_{\xi}=N_{\xi}(g)
has the law of the number of zero coordinates in an iid
uniform vector
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r}
, conditioned on the event
\mathcal{E}=\{a\not\equiv 0\}
that the vector
a
is not identically
zero. Since
\mathcal{E}
has probability
1-p^{-r}
, Chernoff’s inequality and (
4.9
) give, for every sufficiently small

\eta_{p,\varepsilon}>0
,

\pi(N_{\xi}>\beta_{0}r)\leq C_{p}\exp\{-(I_{p}(\beta_{0})-\eta_{p,\varepsilon})r\}.

Choose
\eta_{p,\varepsilon}>0
sufficiently small that this bound applies and also

\varepsilon\log p<I_{p}(\beta_{0})-2\eta_{p,\varepsilon}
. A union bound over the

p^{h}-1
nonzero linear functionals
\xi\in W^{\ast}
gives

\pi(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{H}}^{c})\leq C_{p}\exp\{h\log p-(I_{p}(\beta_{0})-\eta_{p,\varepsilon})r\}\leq e^{-c_{p,\varepsilon}r}

for some
c_{p,\varepsilon}>0
, whenever
h\leq\varepsilon r
and
r
is
large.

For
X\sim\mu
, the same Chernoff bound and union bound give

\mu\left(\exists\,0\neq\xi\in W^{*}:N_{\xi}(X)>\beta_{0}r\right)\leq p^{h}\exp\{-(I_{p}(\beta_{0})-\eta_{p,\varepsilon})r\}.

Also,

\displaystyle\mu(\mathsf{H}^{r}\setminus\Omega)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{span}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})\neq W)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{0\neq\xi\in W^{*}}\mathbb{P}(\xi(v_{1})=\cdots=\xi(v_{r})=0)

\displaystyle\leq p^{h}p^{-r}.

Therefore

\mu(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{H}}^{c})\leq p^{h}\exp\{-(I_{p}(\beta_{0})-\eta_{p,\varepsilon})r\}+p^{h-r}.

Because
h\leq\varepsilon r
with
\varepsilon<1
, the last display is

o(1)
. Hence
\mu(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{H}})=1-o(1)
.
∎

4.5.
Burn-in

We prove that the PA-PRA spends most of its time in the good set (
4.8
) after a suitable burn-in period. We will again take advantage of copies of the one-column transvection walk.

Let
Y_{t}
be the following one-column
p
-ary transvection walk on

\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r}\setminus\{0\}
: at each step choose distinct
i,j\in[r]
and

a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
uniformly, and replace
Y_{i}
by
Y_{i}+aY_{j}
. Let

S_{t}=|\{i:Y_{t}(i)\neq 0\}|.

Note that
(S_{t})
is a birth-death chain on
\{1,\ldots,r\}
. From state
s
,
its birth and death probabilities are

B_{s}=\frac{p-1}{p}\frac{s(r-s)}{r(r-1)},\qquad D_{s}=\frac{1}{p}\frac{s(s-1)}{r(r-1)}.

(4.14)

A birth occurs when the donor is nonzero, the recipient is zero, and
a\neq 0
;
a death occurs when donor and recipient are both nonzero and the unique value
of
a
making the recipient zero is chosen.

Lemma 4.7

(Support growth)
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{*})
. There is a constant
C_{p,\alpha}<\infty
such
that, for all sufficiently large
r
, the hitting time

\tau_{\alpha}=\inf\{t:S_{t}\geq\alpha r\}

satisfies

\sup_{1\leq s\leq r}\mathbb{E}_{s}\tau_{\alpha}\leq C_{p,\alpha}r\log r.

(4.15)

Consequently, for a suitable
B_{p,\alpha}<\infty
,

\sup_{1\leq s\leq r}\mathbb{P}_{s}(\tau_{\alpha}>B_{p,\alpha}r\log r)\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(4.16)

Proof.

Set
A=\lceil\alpha r\rceil
. If
s\geq A
, then
\tau_{\alpha}=0
, so
we consider
1\leq s<A
. We first control the birth-death ratios below

A
. For
1\leq s<A
, set

\rho_{s}=\frac{D_{s}}{B_{s}}=\frac{s-1}{(p-1)(r-s)}.

(4.17)

Since
\alpha<\alpha_{*}=(p-1)/p
, there is
q=q(p,\alpha)<1
such that

\rho_{s}\leq q
for all
s<A
and all large
r
. Also

B_{s}\geq c_{p,\alpha}\frac{s}{r},\qquad 1\leq s<A.

Let
\tau_{A}=\inf\{t:S_{t}=A\}
. Since the chain moves by at most one at
each step and
A=\lceil\alpha r\rceil
, this agrees with
\tau_{\alpha}
on
the event
S_{0}<A
. For
1\leq s\leq A
, write

e_{s}=\mathbb{E}_{s}\tau_{A},\qquad e_{A}=0,

and put
d_{s}=e_{s}-e_{s+1}
for
1\leq s\leq A-1
. We have the following recurrence for
1\leq s\leq A-1
,

B_{s}(e_{s}-e_{s+1})+D_{s}(e_{s}-e_{s-1})=1,

(4.18)

with the convention that the term involving
e_{0}
is absent when
s=1
.
Equivalently, since
D_{1}=0
,

d_{s}=\frac{1}{B_{s}}+\rho_{s}d_{s-1},\qquad\rho_{s}=\frac{D_{s}}{B_{s}},\qquad d_{0}=0.

Iterating this recursion yields

d_{k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{1}{B_{\ell}}\prod_{m=\ell+1}^{k}\rho_{m},\qquad 1\leq k\leq A-1,

(4.19)

where the empty product is interpreted as
1
. Since

e_{s}=\sum_{k=s}^{A-1}d_{k}
, (
4.19
) gives

\displaystyle e_{s}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{A-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{1}{B_{\ell}}\prod_{m=\ell+1}^{k}\rho_{m}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p,\alpha}r\sum_{k=1}^{A-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{q^{k-\ell}}{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p,\alpha}r\sum_{\ell=1}^{A-1}\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{k=\ell}^{A-1}q^{k-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p,\alpha}r\sum_{\ell=1}^{A-1}\frac{1}{\ell}\leq C_{p,\alpha}r\log r.

Here we used
\rho_{m}\leq q<1
and

B_{\ell}\geq c_{p,\alpha}\ell/r
for
\ell<A
. This proves
(
4.15
). Choosing
B_{p,\alpha}
sufficiently
large, Markov’s inequality gives

\sup_{1\leq s\leq r}\mathbb{P}_{s}(\tau_{\alpha}>B_{p,\alpha}r\log r)\leq\frac{1}{2},

which is (
4.16
).
∎

Lemma 4.8

(Downward crossing)
.

Let
0<\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{*}
, and define
J_{p}
by
(
4.11
). For every
T\leq r^{3}
,

\sup_{s\geq\alpha_{1}r}\mathbb{P}_{s}\left(\inf_{0\leq t\leq T}S_{t}<\alpha_{0}r\right)\leq T\exp\{-(J_{p}(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})+o(1))r\},

where the implicit constant in the
o(1)
term depends only on
p,\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}
and, when those are fixed, tends to zero as

r\to\infty
.

Proof.

It is enough to consider integer
T
; otherwise replace
T
by

\lfloor T\rfloor
. Set

A_{0}=\lfloor\alpha_{0}r\rfloor,\qquad A_{1}=\lceil\alpha_{1}r\rceil.

Let
\widehat{S}_{n}
be the embedded jump chain obtained from
S_{t}
. If
S_{t}
reaches
\{1,\ldots,A_{0}\}
within
T
steps, then
\widehat{S}_{n}
does as well, so it is enough to bound

\sup_{s\geq A_{1}}\mathbb{P}_{s}\left(\inf_{0\leq n\leq T}\widehat{S}_{n}\leq A_{0}\right).

For
1\leq m\leq r-1
, set

\rho_{m}=\frac{D_{m}}{B_{m}}=\frac{m-1}{(p-1)(r-m)}.

Let
\widehat{\tau}_{a}=\inf\{n\geq 0:\widehat{S}_{n}=a\}
. The standard
birth-death hitting-probability formula gives, for
A_{0}\leq s\leq A_{1}
,

\mathbb{P}_{s}(\widehat{\tau}_{A_{0}}<\widehat{\tau}_{A_{1}})=\frac{\sum_{k=s}^{A_{1}-1}\prod_{m=A_{0}+1}^{k}\rho_{m}}{\sum_{k=A_{0}}^{A_{1}-1}\prod_{m=A_{0}+1}^{k}\rho_{m}},

with the empty product interpreted as
1
. In particular,

\mathbb{P}_{A_{1}-1}(\widehat{\tau}_{A_{0}}<\widehat{\tau}_{A_{1}})\leq\prod_{m=A_{0}+1}^{A_{1}-1}\rho_{m}.

Every path from
[A_{1},\infty)
to
\{1,\ldots,A_{0}\}
has an attempt
beginning with a jump from
A_{1}
to
A_{1}-1
. There are at most
T

such attempts among the first
T
steps of
\widehat{S}
. By the strong Markov property and a union bound,

\mathbb{P}_{s}\left(\inf_{0\leq n\leq T}\widehat{S}_{n}\leq A_{0}\right)\leq T\prod_{m=A_{0}+1}^{A_{1}-1}\rho_{m}.

(4.20)

Finally, recalling that
\alpha_{0}>0
and
\alpha_{1}<1
,

\displaystyle\log\left(\prod_{m=A_{0}+1}^{A_{1}-1}\rho_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m=A_{0}+1}^{A_{1}-1}\log\left(\frac{(p-1)(r-m)}{m-1}\right)

\displaystyle=-r\int_{\alpha_{0}}^{\alpha_{1}}\log\left(\frac{(p-1)(1-u)}{u}\right)\,du+o(r)

\displaystyle=-(J_{p}(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})+o(1))r.

Combining with Inequality (
4.20
) completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

For every fixed
M<\infty
there is a constant

C_{p,\varepsilon,M}<\infty
such that the following holds. Let
Y_{t}
be
the one-column
p
-ary transvection walk on
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r}\setminus\{0\}
, and let

Z_{t}=|\{i:Y_{t}(i)=0\}|
. If

t_{*}=C_{p,\varepsilon,M}r\log r\,(h+M\log r),\qquad h\leq\varepsilon r,

then, uniformly in the initial state and in all
t\geq t_{*}
,

\mathbb{P}(Z_{t}>\beta_{0}r)\leq p^{-h}r^{-M-1}+\exp\{-(J_{p}(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})+o(1))r\}.

(4.21)

Proof.

It is enough to prove the estimate at
t=t_{*}
, because the bound is uniform
in the starting state and later times can be handled by conditioning on the
state at time
t-t_{*}
. Let
S_{t}=r-Z_{t}
, and set

\tau_{1}=\inf\{t:S_{t}\geq\alpha_{1}r\}.

By Lemma
4.7
, with
\alpha=\alpha_{1}
, there is a
block length
B=B_{p,\alpha_{1}}r\log r
such that the probability of not
hitting
\alpha_{1}r
during one block is at most
1/2
, uniformly in the
starting state. Iterating over

L=\left\lfloor\frac{t_{*}}{B}\right\rfloor

blocks gives
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}>t_{*})\leq 2^{-L}
. Choosing

C_{p,\varepsilon,M}
large enough gives

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}>t_{*})\leq p^{-h}r^{-M-1}.

(4.22)

We now consider the event
\{\tau_{1}\leq t_{*}\}
. On this event, we have
\{Z_{t_{*}}>\beta_{0}r\}=\{S_{t_{*}}<\alpha_{0}r\}
. Since
h\leq\varepsilon r
,

t_{*}=O_{p,\varepsilon,M}(r^{2}\log r)\leq r^{3}

for all large
r
. By the strong Markov property at
\tau_{1}
and
Lemma
4.8
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{t_{*}}<\alpha_{0}r,\ \tau_{1}\leq t_{*})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{s\geq\alpha_{1}r}\mathbb{P}_{s}\left(\inf_{0\leq u\leq t_{*}}S_{u}<\alpha_{0}r\right)

\displaystyle\leq t_{*}\exp\{-(J_{p}(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})+o(1))r\}.

Since
t_{*}\leq r^{3}
, the factor
t_{*}
is absorbed into the
o(1)r
term
in the exponent. This gives the second term in (
4.21
); combining with Inequality (
4.22
) completes the proof.
∎

We conclude with the burn-in estimate:

Lemma 4.10
.

For every fixed
M<\infty
there is a constant

C_{p,\varepsilon,M}<\infty
such that the following holds. If

h\leq\varepsilon r
and

t_{*}=C_{p,\varepsilon,M}r\log r\,(h+M\log r),

(4.23)

then, uniformly in
x\in\Omega
and all
t\geq t_{*}
,

\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\notin\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{H}})\leq r^{-M}.

(4.24)

Consequently, if
L\leq r^{3}
and
M=10
, then uniformly in
x
and

s\geq t_{*}
,

\mathbb{P}_{x}(\exists\,u\in\{0,1,\ldots,L\}:X_{s+u}\notin\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{H}})=o(1).

(4.25)

Proof.

For each nonzero
\xi\in W^{*}
, the projected process

\Pi_{\xi}(X_{t})=(\xi(v_{1}(t)),\ldots,\xi(v_{r}(t)))

is exactly the one-column
p
-ary transvection walk on

\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r}\setminus\{0\}
, because the horizontal update is

v_{i}\leftarrow v_{i}+av_{j}
. Since
X_{t}\in\Omega
, the vector

\Pi_{\xi}(X_{t})
is never identically zero.

By Lemma
4.9
, for every fixed
\xi\neq 0
,

\mathbb{P}_{x}(N_{\xi}(X_{t})>\beta_{0}r)\leq p^{-h}r^{-M-1}+\exp\{-(J_{p}(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})+o(1))r\},\qquad t\geq t_{*}.

Taking a union bound over at most
p^{h}
nonzero functionals
\xi
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\notin\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{H}})

\displaystyle\leq r^{-M-1}+p^{h}\exp\{-(J_{p}(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})+o(1))r\}

\displaystyle\leq r^{-M-1}+\exp\{-2\eta_{0}r+o(r)\}\leq r^{-M},

where we used
h\leq\varepsilon r
and (
4.13
). This
proves (
4.24
). The sojourn estimate in Inequality (
4.25
) follows by
applying (
4.24
) at all times

s,s+1,\ldots,s+L
and using
L\leq r^{3}
with
M=10
.
∎

4.6.
The Heisenberg fibre spectral estimate

In this section, we prove the analogue of Proposition
3.4
. The main difference between the PA-PRA and the transvection walk is that we are no longer dealing with only one-dimensional representations. We begin by collecting the notation and necessary facts from the already-understood representation theory of the Heisenberg group, following
[
23
]
very closely. None of the representation-theoretic results in this section are new.

Define the additive character

\psi(t)=e^{2\pi it/p},\qquad t\in\mathbb{F}_{p},

where we identify
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with
\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
in the
exponent.

Proposition 4.11

(Heisenberg representation facts)
.

Let
\mathsf{H}=W\times\mathbb{F}_{p}
be the group in (
4.1
), with

\dim W=2m
. For
\xi\in W^{*}
, define

\chi_{\xi}(v,z)=\psi(\xi(v)).

The following facts hold.

(i)

The functions
\chi_{\xi}
,
\xi\in W^{*}
, are exactly the
irreducible complex representations of
\mathsf{H}
on which the center

\{0\}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}
acts trivially.

(ii)

For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
, there is an irreducible unitary
representation
\rho_{\lambda}
of
\mathsf{H}
, of dimension
p^{m}
, such
that

\rho_{\lambda}(0,z)=\psi(\lambda z)I,\qquad z\in\mathbb{F}_{p}.

(iii)

If
\sigma
is any irreducible complex representation of
\mathsf{H}

on which the center does not act trivially, then there is a unique

\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
such that

\sigma(0,z)=\psi(\lambda z)I,\qquad z\in\mathbb{F}_{p}.

For this
\lambda
, there is a unitary map
U
from the representation
space of
\sigma
to that of
\rho_{\lambda}
such that

U\sigma(g)U^{-1}=\rho_{\lambda}(g),\qquad g\in\mathsf{H}.

Moreover, if
g=(v,z)
,
g^{\prime}=(w,t)
, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
, then

\rho_{\lambda}(g)\rho_{\lambda}(g^{\prime})=\psi(\lambda\omega(v,w))\rho_{\lambda}(g^{\prime})\rho_{\lambda}(g).

(4.26)

Proof.

We use the representation theory of the finite Heisenberg group from

[
23
]
. Misaghian works over a finite field
F
of order

q
, with
\dim_{F}W=2n
. Here
F=\mathbb{F}_{p}
,
q=p
, and
n=m
. His
multiplication rule is written using a symplectic form

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
; in our normalization this corresponds to

\langle v,w\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\omega(v,w).

Since
p
is odd, this is again a nondegenerate alternating form, so his
results apply to the group law (
4.1
).

We first discuss the representations on which the center acts trivially. Any
irreducible representation with trivial action of
Z(\mathsf{H})=\{0\}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}

factors through

\mathsf{H}/Z(\mathsf{H})\cong W.

This quotient is abelian, so all of its irreducible complex representations are
one-dimensional characters. The characters of the additive group
W
are
exactly

v\mapsto\psi(\xi(v)),\qquad\xi\in W^{*},

and pulling these characters back along
(v,z)\mapsto v
gives precisely the
functions
\chi_{\xi}
in the statement. This proves (i); this is also
recorded in
[
23
, Lemmas 5–6, Theorem 2, and Corollary 5,
pp. 164–166]
.

We now explain how
[
23
, Theorem 3]
gives the representations
in (ii). Choose subspaces
A,B\leq W
such that

W=A\oplus B,\qquad\dim A=\dim B=m,

and such that
\omega
vanishes on
A\times A
and on
B\times B
. Such a
choice exists: after choosing a symplectic basis

b_{1},\ldots,b_{2m}
as above, one may take

A=\operatorname{span}(b_{1},b_{3},\ldots,b_{2m-1}),\qquad B=\operatorname{span}(b_{2},b_{4},\ldots,b_{2m}).

Set

K=A\times\mathbb{F}_{p}\leq\mathsf{H}.

The condition that
\omega
vanishes on
A\times A
implies that
K
is a
subgroup of
\mathsf{H}
. Fix
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
and define a character of
K
by

\eta_{\lambda}(a,z)=\psi(\lambda z),\qquad(a,z)\in K.

Misaghian’s space
C(H,K)
is, in this notation,

E_{\lambda}=\{f:\mathsf{H}\to\mathbb{C}:f(kh)=\eta_{\lambda}(k)f(h)\text{ for all }k\in K,\ h\in\mathsf{H}\}.

Thus the functions
f
in Misaghian’s formula are vectors in the representation
space. For each fixed group element
g\in\mathsf{H}
, Misaghian defines the linear
operator

(\rho_{\lambda}(g)f)(h)=f(hg),\qquad f\in E_{\lambda},\ h\in\mathsf{H}.

Theorem 3 of
[
23
]
says that this gives an irreducible
representation of degree
q^{n}=p^{m}
.

We make the dimension and the associated matrices explicit. For each
b\in B
,
write

s_{b}=(b,0)\in\mathsf{H}.

If
x=(w,z)\in\mathsf{H}
and
w=a+b
with
a\in A
and
b\in B
, then

x=\left(a,z-\frac{1}{2}\omega(a,b)\right)s_{b}.

This decomposition is unique, so the elements
s_{b}
,
b\in B
, are a set of
representatives for the left cosets
K\backslash\mathsf{H}
. Since
|B|=p^{m}
, there
are
p^{m}
such cosets. Define functions
e_{b}\in E_{\lambda}
, indexed by

b\in B
, by

e_{b}(ks_{b^{\prime}})=\eta_{\lambda}(k)\mathbf{1}_{\{b=b^{\prime}\}},\qquad k\in K,\ b^{\prime}\in B.

The uniqueness of the decomposition shows that these functions are well-defined,
and they form a basis of
E_{\lambda}
: every
f\in E_{\lambda}
is determined by the

p^{m}
values
f(s_{b})
, and

f=\sum_{b\in B}f(s_{b})e_{b}.

Relative to this basis, the operator
\rho_{\lambda}(g)
is a
p^{m}\times p^{m}

matrix. Its entries are obtained by applying the operator to the basis vectors:

M_{b,b^{\prime}}(g)=(\rho_{\lambda}(g)e_{b^{\prime}})(s_{b})=e_{b^{\prime}}(s_{b}g),\qquad b,b^{\prime}\in B.

Thus Misaghian’s formula, although written as a formula for every vector
f
,
is exactly a formula for a linear operator, and a choice of the basis above turns
that operator into a matrix.

Now take a central element
c=(0,z)\in Z(\mathsf{H})
. Since
c\in K
and
c
is
central, for every
f\in E_{\lambda}
and every
h\in\mathsf{H}
we have

(\rho_{\lambda}(c)f)(h)=f(hc)=f(ch)=\eta_{\lambda}(c)f(h)=\psi(\lambda z)f(h).

Therefore
\rho_{\lambda}(0,z)
sends every vector of
E_{\lambda}
to

\psi(\lambda z)
times itself. In the basis
\{e_{b}:b\in B\}
, and hence in any
basis, this linear operator is

\rho_{\lambda}(0,z)=\psi(\lambda z)I.

This is the central-character computation recorded in

[
23
, Corollary 8, p. 169]
. Since
\mathsf{H}
is finite, any
finite-dimensional complex representation of
\mathsf{H}
can be made unitary by
averaging an arbitrary inner product over the group. Hence the representations
constructed above may be taken to be unitary, proving (ii).

It remains to explain the uniqueness statement in (iii). If
\sigma
is
irreducible, then Schur’s lemma implies that every central element
(0,z)
acts
by a scalar. These scalars form an additive character of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. If the center
does not act trivially, this character is nontrivial, and the nontrivial additive
characters of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
are exactly

z\mapsto\psi(\lambda z),\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times},

with
\lambda
uniquely determined. Misaghian’s Stone–von Neumann theorem,

[
23
, Theorem 1, p. 162]
, says that for each such nontrivial
central character there is only one irreducible representation up to equivalence.
Thus
\sigma
is equivalent to the representation
\rho_{\lambda}
constructed
above. Choosing invariant inner products on the two representation spaces, the
intertwining map can be chosen unitary. Finally,

[
23
, Corollary 10, p. 170]
says that the representations in
(i) and (ii) exhaust all irreducible representations of
\mathsf{H}
.

It remains only to check (
4.26
). By
(
4.1
),

gg^{\prime}=(v+w,z+t+\tfrac{1}{2}\omega(v,w)),\qquad g^{\prime}g=(v+w,z+t-\tfrac{1}{2}\omega(v,w)).

Hence

gg^{\prime}=(0,\omega(v,w))g^{\prime}g.

Applying
\rho_{\lambda}
, and using

\rho_{\lambda}(0,s)=\psi(\lambda s)I
, gives

\rho_{\lambda}(g)\rho_{\lambda}(g^{\prime})=\psi(\lambda\omega(v,w))\rho_{\lambda}(g^{\prime})\rho_{\lambda}(g),

as claimed.
∎

Having described the representations, we now give a series of bounds that will help us show the power-averaging of PA-PRA helps.

For a unitary matrix
U
with
U^{p}=I
, define

P_{U}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}U^{a}.

This is the orthogonal projection onto the fixed space of
U
(see Lemma 2.2.2 of
[
30
]
). The following lemma borrows calculations from
[
16
]
:

Lemma 4.12

(Two projections)
.

Let
U,V
be unitary operators on a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space,
with
U^{p}=V^{p}=I
. Suppose

UV=\zeta VU

for some nontrivial
p
th root of unity
\zeta\neq 1
. Then

\|P_{U}P_{V}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq p^{-1/2}.

Proof.

For
b\neq 0
, the relation
UV^{b}=\zeta^{b}V^{b}U
implies that
V^{b}
maps the

U
-fixed space into a nontrivial eigenspace of
U
. Therefore

P_{U}V^{b}P_{U}=0,\qquad b\neq 0.

It follows that

P_{U}P_{V}P_{U}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{b\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}P_{U}V^{b}P_{U}=\frac{1}{p}P_{U}.

Since
P_{U}
and
P_{V}
are orthogonal projections,

(P_{U}P_{V})^{*}=P_{V}P_{U}
. Hence

\|P_{U}P_{V}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}=\|(P_{U}P_{V})(P_{U}P_{V})^{*}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\|P_{U}P_{V}P_{U}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=p^{-1}\|P_{U}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq p^{-1}.

Taking square roots proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 4.13

(Average of projections)
.

Let
P_{1},\ldots,P_{N}
be orthogonal projections on a finite-dimensional
complex Hilbert space. Suppose that for at least

\delta{N\choose 2}
of the ordered pairs
(j,\ell)\in[N]^{2}
one has

\|P_{j}P_{\ell}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\alpha.

Then

\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}P_{j}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1-\frac{\delta}{2}(1-\alpha).

Proof.

We first prove an estimate with only two projections. Let
P
and
Q

be orthogonal projections on a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space, and
put
s=\|PQ\|_{\operatorname{op}}
. We claim that

\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{P+Q}{2}\right)\leq\frac{1+s}{2}.

(4.27)

To see this, let
f
be a unit eigenvector of
P+Q
with eigenvalue
\lambda
. If

\lambda\leq 1
, then (
4.27
) is immediate.
Assume
\lambda>1
. Set
u=Pf
and
v=Qf
. Applying
P
and
Q
to

(P+Q)f=\lambda f
gives

Pv=(\lambda-1)u,\qquad Qu=(\lambda-1)v.

Therefore

(\lambda-1)\|u\|=\|Pv\|\leq s\|v\|,\qquad(\lambda-1)\|v\|=\|Qu\|\leq s\|u\|,

where the second inequality uses
\|QP\|_{\operatorname{op}}=\|(PQ)^{*}\|_{\operatorname{op}}=s
. Since
\lambda>1
, neither
u
nor

v
is zero. Multiplying the two inequalities gives
\lambda-1\leq s
,
and hence (
4.27
).

We now consider the general-
N
case. Fix a unit vector
f
. Let
J,L
be independent uniform indices in

[N]
, and let
E
be the event

\|P_{J}P_{L}\|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq\alpha
. Since
\mathbb{P}(E)\geq\delta
,
(
4.27
) and the trivial bound

\lambda_{\max}((P_{J}+P_{L})/2)\leq 1
give

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}P_{j}f,f\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\langle P_{J}f,f\rangle+\langle P_{L}f,f\rangle}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(E^{c})+\mathbb{P}(E)\frac{1+\alpha}{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{\delta}{2}(1-\alpha).

Taking the supremum over unit vectors
f
proves the claim.

∎

We put these together to bound the spectral gaps of the fibre kernels:

Proposition 4.14
.

Fix
\beta\in(1/p,1)
. Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\in\mathsf{H}
, where

a_{j}=(v_{j},z_{j})
, and let
\mu_{W}=N^{-1}\sum_{j}\delta_{v_{j}}
. Suppose that
\mu_{W}
satisfies

\mu_{W}(L)\leq\beta\qquad\text{for every hyperplane }L\leq W.

(4.28)

Define

\nu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{\langle a_{j}\rangle}.

Recall the definition (
4.7
). Then the kernel
K=\frac{1}{2}(R_{\nu}+L_{\nu})
has spectral gap at least

\gamma_{p,\beta}=\min\left\{1-\beta,\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{2}(1-p^{-1/2})\right\}>0.

(4.29)

Proof.

We prove three estimates and then combine them. Define the functional
\widehat{\nu}
by the formula:

\widehat{\nu}(\rho)=\sum_{g\in\mathsf{H}}\nu(g)\rho(g).

We use the notation for representations of
\mathsf{H}
from the statement of Proposition
4.11
throughout the argument.

First, we control the one-dimensional representations. Fix

0\neq\xi\in W^{*}
. Recall the following fact: if
x
is a
p
th root of unity, then either
\sum_{j=1}^{p}x^{j}=p
(if
x=1
) or
\sum_{j=1}^{p}x^{j}=0
(otherwise). Using (
4.2
) and then this fact,

\displaystyle\widehat{\nu}(\chi_{\xi})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\chi_{\xi}(a_{j}^{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\psi(s\xi(v_{j}))

\displaystyle=\mu_{W}(\ker\xi).

Since
\ker\xi
is a hyperplane, the assumption in inequality (
4.28
)
gives

|\widehat{\nu}(\chi_{\xi})|\leq\beta.

(4.30)

Second, we control the irreducible representations with nontrivial central
character. Fix
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
, and set

U_{j}=\rho_{\lambda}(a_{j}),\qquad P_{j}^{(\lambda)}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}U_{j}^{a},\qquad A_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}P_{j}^{(\lambda)}.

By (
4.2
),
U_{j}^{p}=I
. By this identity and the fact that
U
is unitary, we have that
P_{j}^{(\lambda)}
is the
orthogonal projection onto the space fixed by
U_{j}
(see Lemma 2.2.2 of
[
30
]
). Furthermore,

\widehat{\nu}(\rho_{\lambda})=A_{\lambda}.

We now consider a pair
1\leq j<\ell\leq N
. If
\omega(v_{j},v_{\ell})\neq 0
, then (
4.26
)
gives

U_{j}U_{\ell}=\psi(\lambda\omega(v_{j},v_{\ell}))U_{\ell}U_{j},

and the scalar
\psi(\lambda\omega(v_{j},v_{\ell}))
is a nontrivial
p
th root
of unity. In this case, Lemma
4.12
gives

\|P_{j}^{(\lambda)}P_{\ell}^{(\lambda)}\|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq p^{-1/2}.

We now show that this estimate applies to many ordered pairs. Let

V,V^{\prime}\sim\mu_{W}
be independent, and define

q=\mu_{W}(\{0\})
. Since
\{0\}
is contained in every hyperplane,

q\leq\beta
. If
v\neq 0
, then the functional
w\mapsto\omega(v,w)
is nonzero by
nondegeneracy of
\omega
. Hence

v^{\perp}=\{w\in W:\omega(v,w)=0\}

is a hyperplane of
W
, and (
4.28
)
gives
\mu_{W}(v^{\perp})\leq\beta
. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\omega(V,V^{\prime})=0)

\displaystyle=q+\sum_{v\neq 0}\mu_{W}(v)\mu_{W}(v^{\perp})

\displaystyle\leq q+(1-q)\beta\leq\beta+(1-\beta)\beta=2\beta-\beta^{2}.

Thus at least a proportion
(1-\beta)^{2}
of ordered pairs satisfy

\omega(v_{j},v_{\ell})\neq 0
. Applying
Lemma
4.13
with

\delta=(1-\beta)^{2}
and
\alpha=p^{-1/2}
, we get

\|A_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq 1-\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{2}(1-p^{-1/2}).

(4.31)

Third, we pass from the representation bounds (
4.30
),
(
4.31
) to bounds on the Markov chain. Let

\operatorname{Irr}(\mathsf{H})
denote the following fixed choice of representatives:

\operatorname{Irr}(\mathsf{H})=\{\chi_{\xi}:\xi\in W^{*}\}\cup\{\rho_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}\},

where the representations are those in Proposition
4.11
.
Note that, by Proposition
4.11
,
\operatorname{Irr}(\mathsf{H})
contains one representative from each unitary
change-of-basis class of irreducible representations of
\mathsf{H}
.

For each representation
\rho\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathsf{H})
, let
V_{\rho}
be its representation space and
write

B_{\rho}=\widehat{\nu}(\rho)=\sum_{g\in\mathsf{H}}\nu(g)\rho(g).

The measure
\nu
is symmetric because each
u_{\langle a_{j}\rangle}
is
uniform on a subgroup, so
B_{\rho}
is self-adjoint. Define

\mathcal{H}_{\rho}=\operatorname{span}\{x\mapsto\langle\rho(x)u,v\rangle:u,v\in V_{\rho}\}\subseteq L^{2}(\mathsf{H}).

By Schur orthogonality
for irreducible representation coefficients (see
e.g.
the discussion of the Peter-Weyl theorem in
[
14
, Chapter 2]
), we have the orthogonal decomposition

L^{2}(\mathsf{H})=\bigoplus_{\rho\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathsf{H})}\mathcal{H}_{\rho}.

The
trivial block, corresponding to
\chi_{0}
, is the space of constant
functions.

For
u,v\in V_{\rho}
, write

f_{u,v}^{\rho}(x)=\langle\rho(x)u,v\rangle.

Using the definitions of
R_{\nu},L_{\nu}
in (
4.7
) and of

B_{\rho}
, we have

\displaystyle R_{\nu}f_{u,v}^{\rho}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in\mathsf{H}}\nu(g)\langle\rho(xg)u,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in\mathsf{H}}\nu(g)\langle\rho(x)\rho(g)u,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\rho(x)B_{\rho}u,v\rangle=f_{B_{\rho}u,v}^{\rho}(x),

and similarly

L_{\nu}f_{u,v}^{\rho}(x)=f_{u,B_{\rho}^{*}v}^{\rho}(x).

Thus
R_{\nu}
and
L_{\nu}
preserve
\mathcal{H}_{\rho}
. Under the
Peter-Weyl identification of
\mathcal{H}_{\rho}
with the corresponding
coefficient space, the preceding identities say that
R_{\nu}
acts by

B_{\rho}
on the first coordinate and
L_{\nu}
acts by
B_{\rho}^{*}
on the
second coordinate. Hence

\|R_{\nu}|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}}\|_{2\to 2}=\|L_{\nu}|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}}\|_{2\to 2}=\|B_{\rho}\|_{\operatorname{op}}.

Taking averages, this implies

\left\|K|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}}\right\|_{2\to 2}\leq\|B_{\rho}\|_{\operatorname{op}}.

For every nontrivial
\rho
, the Inequalities (
4.30
) and (
4.31
) give

\|B_{\rho}\|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq 1-\gamma_{p,\beta},

where
\gamma_{p,\beta}
is defined in (
4.29
). Since

K
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\mathsf{H})
, this bound on the orthogonal
complement of the constants implies that the spectral gap of

K
is at least
\gamma_{p,\beta}
.

∎

Lemma 4.15
.

For all sufficiently large
r
, the following holds. Fix
i
and freeze the
coordinates
(g_{j})_{j\neq i}
. If the corresponding
i
-fibre intersects

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{H}}
, then
K_{i}
in (
4.6
) has spectral
gap at least
\gamma_{p,\beta_{1}}
, where
\beta_{1}
is defined in
(
4.10
).

Proof.

Choose a point
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{H}}
in the fibre. For every nonzero

\xi\in W^{*}
,

|\{j\neq i:\xi(v_{j})=0\}|\leq|\{j\in[r]:\xi(v_{j})=0\}|\leq\beta_{0}r.

Thus, for all sufficiently large
r
,

\frac{1}{r-1}|\{j\neq i:\xi(v_{j})=0\}|\leq\beta_{1}.

This is precisely the condition
(
4.28
) with
\beta=\beta_{1}
. Applying Proposition

4.14
with this bound proves the claim.
∎

4.7.
Completing the proof of the upper bound

Theorem 4.16

(Continuous-time Heisenberg mixing)
.

Fix an odd prime
p
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. There is a constant

C_{p,\varepsilon}<\infty
such that, for all sufficiently large
r
and all

h=2m\geq 2
satisfying
h\leq\varepsilon r
,

\max_{x\in\Omega}\left\|\delta_{x}e^{C_{p,\varepsilon}r(h+\log r)\log r(P-I)}-\pi\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{1}{4}.

Proof.

We check the conditions of Theorem
2.7
with the choices

S=\mathsf{H},\qquad N=r,\qquad\Omega=V_{r}(\mathsf{H}),\qquad G=\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{H}}.

For all sufficiently large
r
, the assumption
h\leq\varepsilon r
implies

r\geq h+1
, so Lemma
4.5
gives irreducibility on

\Omega
and
\pi
is the unique stationary law. By
Lemma
4.15
and Proposition
2.2
,
for every nonnegative
F:\mathsf{H}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}
supported on
G
,

\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu}(F^{2})\leq C_{p,\varepsilon}r(h+1)\mathcal{E}_{P}^{\mu}(F,F),

because
|\mathsf{H}|=p^{h+1}
and
\gamma_{p,\beta_{1}}>0
depends only on

p
and
\varepsilon
. Thus
A=C_{p,\varepsilon}r(h+1)
.

Lemma
4.6
gives

\pi(G^{c})=o(1),\qquad A\frac{\pi(G^{c})}{\pi(G)}\leq C_{p,\varepsilon}r(h+1)e^{-c_{p,\varepsilon}r}=o(1),

using
h\leq\varepsilon r
. Take
L=r^{3}
. With
M=10
, Lemma
4.10
gives

t_{*}=O_{p,\varepsilon}(r\log r\,(h+\log r))

and the sojourn estimate (
4.25
); in the notation of
Theorem
2.7
, this means
\eta=o(1)
. The confinement
time satisfies

t_{\mathrm{conf}}\leq C_{p,\varepsilon}r(h+1)\log(e+\log|\Omega|)\leq C_{p,\varepsilon}r(h+1)\log(r(h+2)).

Since
h\leq\varepsilon r
, this is

O_{p,\varepsilon}(r(h+\log r)\log r)

and is
o(r^{3})
. Therefore

\zeta=\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Poi}(t_{\mathrm{conf}})>r^{3})=o(1)
. The right-hand
side of (
2.15
) is
o(1)
, and the Poisson lower tail
in (
2.16
) is also
o(1)
. Theorem
2.7

gives the stated continuous-time bound.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Theorem
4.16
gives the continuous-time bound for

e^{t(P-I)}
. The standard comparison between continuous time and the
half-lazy kernel gives the same bound up to a universal multiplicative constant

[
20
, Theorem 20.3(ii)]
.
∎

4.8.
Lower bounds

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

We prove the two lower bounds separately.

First, we prove the
r\log r
support-spreading bound. Fix a symplectic
basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{h}
of
W
with
\omega(b_{1},b_{2})=1
, and start the
chain from the canonical tuple appearing in Lemma
4.5
:

x_{0}=((b_{1},0),\ldots,(b_{h},0),(0,1),(0,0),\ldots,(0,0)).

Choose a nonzero
\xi\in W^{*}
with
\xi(b_{1})=1
and

\xi(b_{i})=0
for
2\leq i\leq h
. For the horizontal projection

Y_{t}=(\xi(v_{1}(t)),\ldots,\xi(v_{r}(t)))\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r}\setminus\{0\},

the initial support size is one. Let

S_{t}=|\{i:Y_{t}(i)\neq 0\}|.

For the non-lazy chain, using the birth probability in
(
4.14
),

\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}\mid S_{t}=s]\leq s+B_{s}\leq s+\frac{s}{r}.

Hence
\mathbb{E}S_{t}\leq(1+1/r)^{t}\leq e^{t/r}
. The half-lazy chain only slows this
support growth, so the same upper bound holds for it. Let

\alpha=\frac{1}{2}(p-1)/p
. If
t=cr\log r
with
c<1
, then

\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(S_{t}\geq\alpha r)\leq\frac{e^{t/r}}{\alpha r}=\frac{r^{c}}{\alpha r}=o(1).

Thus
\{S_{t}<\alpha r\}
has probability
1-o(1)
from this start.

Under stationarity, the projection
Y=(\xi(v_{1}),\ldots,\xi(v_{r}))
is uniform on

\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r}\setminus\{0\}
, as in the proof of Lemma
4.6
.
Therefore
S
has the law of a
\operatorname{Bin}(r,(p-1)/p)
variable
conditioned not to be zero. Hoeffding’s inequality gives

\pi(S<\alpha r)=e^{-c^{\prime}_{p}r+o(r)}=o(1).

The total variation distance at time
cr\log r
is therefore
1-o(1)
, for
any fixed
c<1
small enough. This proves

\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}\geq c_{p}r\log r
, after decreasing
c_{p}
if necessary.

Second, we prove the state-space counting lower bound. The state space has

|\Omega|=p^{r}\prod_{q=0}^{h-1}(p^{r}-p^{q}).

Indeed, the central coordinates are arbitrary, and the horizontal coordinates
are spanning
r
-tuples in an
h
-dimensional vector space. Since

r\geq h+1
,

\prod_{q=0}^{h-1}(1-p^{q-r})\geq\prod_{j=2}^{\infty}(1-p^{-j})=:c_{p}^{(0)}>0.

Consequently

|\Omega|\geq c_{p}^{(0)}p^{r(h+1)}.

(4.32)

From any fixed state, one step of the half-lazy chain has at most

M_{r}=1+2pr(r-1)

possible outcomes. Therefore, after
t
steps from a fixed starting point,
the law is supported on a set
A_{t}\subseteq\Omega
with

|A_{t}|\leq M_{r}^{t}.

Since
\pi
is uniform on
\Omega
,

\pi(A_{t})\leq\frac{M_{r}^{t}}{|\Omega|}.

If

t\leq c^{\prime}_{p}\frac{r(h+1)}{\log(er)}

with
c^{\prime}_{p}>0
small enough, then (
4.32
) and

\log M_{r}=O_{p}(\log(er))
imply
\pi(A_{t})\leq 1/2
for all sufficiently large

r
. Since the chain is supported on
A_{t}
at time
t
,

\|\delta_{x}Q^{t}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\geq 1-\pi(A_{t})\geq\frac{1}{2}.

This proves

\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}\geq c^{\prime}_{p}\frac{r(h+1)}{\log(er)}.

Combining the two estimates and decreasing the constant gives the asserted
maximum lower bound.
∎
