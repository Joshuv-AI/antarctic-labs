Can We Change the Stroke Size for Easier Diffusion?
Yunwei Bai, Ying Kiat Tan, Yao Shu, Tsuhan Chen

Abstract
Diffusion models can be challenged in the low signal-to-noise regime, where they have to make pixel-level predictions despite the presence of high noise. The geometric intuition is akin to using the finest stroke for oil painting throughout, which may be ineffective. We therefore study stroke-size control as a controlled intervention that changes the effective roughness of the supervised target, predictions and perturbations across timesteps, in an attempt to ease the low signal-to-noise challenge. We analyze the advantages and trade-offs of the intervention both theoretically and empirically. Code will be released.



1
Introduction

Oil painting artists usually change brush size: coarse strokes first to lay out global structure, then finer strokes to add detail. Diffusion image generation, in contrast, is trained as a per-pixel regression problem with noise, where the input has extremely low signal-to-noise ratio at high time steps. This creates a hard family of high-frequency regression targets with large stochastic-gradient variance, slows optimization, and causes reverse-chain error accumulation that often appears as high-frequency artifacts
(
6
)
.

We study a
mechanistic control knob
(named “MultiStroke” for easy reference) for reverse-chain roughness, focusing on class-conditional DDPM-style image diffusion, and
we do not claim universality across architectures or modalities.

Experiments are
compute-matched and diagnostic-driven
. Intuitively speaking, we ask a simple yet under-explored question:
can we change the effective stroke size across timesteps to simplify diffusion?

In this work, we formalize a stroke-size operator family that attenuates within-block detail in both the supervised regression targets, predictions and perturbation used during training and sampling. Our theory is operator-theoretic and is anchored to the practical pooling-plus-upsampling stroke operator: it is an orthogonal projection onto a block-coarse subspace and induces an exact coarse/detail decomposition. Under this decomposition, MultiStroke preserves coarse components while shrinking detail components, yielding explicit reductions in multiresolution target complexity, and contracted propagated/injected detail energy along the reverse chain under an affine surrogate dynamics.

We also implement the
intervention
that instantiates the operator which coarsens the supervision targets, predictions and sampling perturbations via nearest-neighbor downsampling and upsampling.
Our contributions include:

1.

Mechanism identification.
We formalize stroke-size control as a single operator
S_{k}
applied consistently to (i) the training prediction and objective and (ii) the sampling-time state and injected noise, yielding an explicit knob over reverse-chain roughness.

2.

Operator-theoretic framework for the practical stroke map.
For the implemented pooling-plus-upsampling operator, we prove
S_{k}
is an orthogonal projector, derive its Fourier attenuation envelope, and obtain an exact coarse/detail decomposition with closed-form projector algebra. Under an affine surrogate reverse dynamics, we derive a detail-energy recursion showing contraction of propagated detail and reduced injected detail noise.

3.

Mechanism validation.
Under compute-matched DDPM training and limited-step sampling on real datasets, we observe improved compute-limited quality and fewer high-frequency artifacts, together with a bias-stability trade-off with relatively aggressive stroke size control.

Not just blurring.

A natural concern is that MultiStroke simply “blurs” the learning problem.
Proposition
5.1
rules out this interpretation at the population level:
under our standing condition
w_{\max}<1
(so
A_{t}
has an identity component), the population minimizer of the stroke-space objective coincides with the standard DDPM
(
9
)
conditional mean.
The intervention therefore changes the optimization path and reverse-chain dynamics, not the population solution.

3
Spectral Difficulty at Low SNR

Diffusion training couples a family of regression problems across noise levels. For DDPM-style objectives, a network predicts Gaussian noise from a corrupted input

\displaystyle x_{t}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,x_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\varepsilon,

(1)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\quad t\in\{1,\dots,T\}.

The signal-to-noise ratio decreases with
t
as

\mathrm{SNR}(t)=\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}},

(2)

so at high timesteps the input is dominated by noise. Nevertheless, the supervision remains a pixel-scale noise field. This mismatch is the source of two coupled difficulties.

A high-frequency regression family.

Viewed in a Fourier basis, the noise target allocates substantial energy to high spatial frequencies. At low-SNR, the conditional prediction function
x_{t}\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid x_{t},t,y]
must infer these fine-scale components from an input that contains little information about
x_{0}
. In practice, this turns the high-timestep portion of training into a difficult, high-frequency regression family.

Large gradient noise and reverse-chain fragility.

For squared-error regression, stochastic-gradient variance is driven by the second moment of the residual target. When the target retains substantial high-frequency energy, the variance of gradients along high-frequency directions can dominate, slowing optimization and making the learned score more sensitive to noise. During sampling, per-step errors produced at high timesteps frequently manifest as high-frequency artifacts.

These observations motivate our stroke-size control design, a linear operator that attenuates high spatial frequencies early (when SNR is low), together with a schedule that returns to the standard objective in the final steps. The remainder of the paper analyzes its effect on training target complexity, and the reverse-chain inference.

4
Stroke-Size Control as a Controlled Intervention

4.1
Preliminaries

We work with a conditional DDPM
(
9
)
over images

x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
and labels

y\in\{1,\dots,K\}
.
Given a variance schedule
\{\beta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
with

\alpha_{t}=1-\beta_{t}
and

\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{s}
(with the convention
\bar{\alpha}_{0}:=1
), the forward process is

x_{t}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,x_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\varepsilon,\;\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\;t=1,\dots,T.

(3)

A network
\varepsilon_{\theta}(x_{t},t,y)
is trained to predict

\varepsilon
by minimizing

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DDPM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\varepsilon_{\theta}(x_{t},t,y)-\varepsilon\|_{2}^{2}\bigr].

(4)

A standard score estimator induced by the noise prediction parameterization is

\hat{s}_{t}(x_{t},t,y):=-\frac{\varepsilon_{\theta}(x_{t},t,y)}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}.

(5)

DDPM reverse mean and variance.

For completeness, the standard DDPM ancestral sampler uses

\mu_{t}(x,\hat{\varepsilon}_{t})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\Bigl(x-\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\,\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}\Bigr),\;\sigma_{t}^{2}=1-\alpha_{t}.

(6)

Variance convention.
Our implementation uses the “fixedlarge” choice
\sigma_{t}^{2}=1-\alpha_{t}
. The standard posterior (“fixedsmall”) variance is
\tilde{\beta}_{t}=\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}(1-\alpha_{t})
; all results hold after substituting
\sigma_{t}^{2}
with the variance used by the sampler.

Stroke-size control keeps the network architecture and base DDPM parameterization
fixed
.

4.2
Stroke Operator and Annealed Schedule

Let
S_{k_{\max}}:\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}\to\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
be a stroke operator defined
as average pooling with kernel and stride
k_{\max}
followed by
nearest-neighbor upsampling back to
(H,W)
.
We define a timestep-dependent weight
w_{t}\in[0,w_{\max}]
that interpolates between fine and coarse strokes,

t_{0}:=\big\lfloor(1-f_{\mathrm{rough}})T\big\rfloor,\;w_{t}=\begin{cases}0,&t\leq t_{0},\\[2.0pt]
w_{\max}\cdot\dfrac{t-t_{0}}{T-t_{0}},&t>t_{0}.\end{cases}

(7)

The schedule sets
w_{t}=0
for the final portion of the chain, so the last steps follow the standard DDPM objective and sampler. This avoids adding additional stroke mixing in the suffix, although any bias induced earlier is carried through the handoff distribution. We take
w_{\max}<1
, so all mixtures keep an identity component.

4.3
Stroke-Space Training Objective

MultiStroke does not modify the forward noising process in (
3
). Instead, it applies a timestep-dependent
stroke map
to (i) the state presented to the prediction network and (ii) the residual that defines the supervised objective, downweighting high-frequency components early in the chain.

Define the timestep-dependent stroke map

A_{t}(z):=(1-w_{t})z+w_{t}S_{k_{\max}}(z).

(8)

For a standard forward sample
x_{t}
and noise
\varepsilon
in (
3
), define the stroke-mixed network input and stroke-space target

x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}:=A_{t}(x_{t}).

(9)

\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}:=A_{t}(\varepsilon).

(10)

We train by comparing the transformed prediction to the transformed target:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MS}}(\theta)&=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|A_{t}(\varepsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y))-\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|A_{t}(\varepsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y)-\varepsilon)\|_{2}^{2}\bigr].\end{split}

(11)

When
w_{t}\equiv 0
,
A_{t}
is the identity,
x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}=x_{t}
, and (
11
) reduces to the standard DDPM objective (
4
). When
w_{t}>0
, errors are evaluated in a coarser stroke space, downweighting high-frequency components.

4.4
Sampling with Stroke-Controlled State and Perturbations

At sampling time, stroke control is applied to both (i) the state presented to the model and (ii) the stochastic perturbation injected by the reverse update. This keeps the reverse chain in the same roughness space as the training objective during early, low-SNR steps, and it becomes standard DDPM when
w_{t}=0
.

Concretely, draw
x_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and initialize

x_{T}\leftarrow x_{T}^{\mathrm{ms}}:=A_{T}(x_{T}).

(12)

For
t=T,\dots,1
, form a stroke-mixed version of the current state

x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}:=A_{t}(x_{t}),

(13)

predict noise
\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}=\varepsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y)
, and use the standard DDPM mean
\mu_{t}(\cdot,\cdot)
and variance
\sigma_{t}^{2}
.
Draw
\eta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
(with
\eta_{1}=0
) and apply stroke mixing aligned to the
destination
step:

\tilde{\eta}_{t}=(1-w_{t-1})\eta_{t}+w_{t-1}S_{k_{\max}}(\eta_{t}),\qquad w_{0}:=0.

(14)

The reverse update is

x_{t-1}=\mu_{t}(x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},\hat{\varepsilon}_{t})+\sigma_{t}\,\tilde{\eta}_{t}.

(15)

Subsampled (jump) schedules.
If sampling uses a decreasing schedule
\tau_{0}>\tau_{1}>\cdots>\tau_{N}=0
with jump steps
t\to s
(where
s<t
), replace the contiguous mean/variance
(\mu_{t},\sigma_{t}^{2})
by the jumped DDPM quantities
(\mu_{t:s},\sigma_{t:s}^{2})
from Eq. (
18
), and apply destination-indexed perturbation mixing with weight
w_{s}
(reducing to
w_{t-1}
when
s=t-1
).

Our analysis in Section
5
studies how the stroke-space loss (
11
) and the stroke-controlled sampling updates change training target complexity and inference reverse-chain stability. Stroke control trades stability for bias: large rough portions can cause background under-detail that later fine-step refinement may only partially conceal.

5
Theoretical Analysis

5.1
Problem Statement

For each timestep
t
in standard DDPM predictor, the optimal predictor is the conditional mean

f_{t}^{\star}(x_{t},y)\;=\;\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid x_{t},t,y],

(16)

so learning amounts to approximating the family of regression functions

\{f_{t}^{\star}\}_{t=1}^{T}
with a single network

\varepsilon_{\theta}
. When
t
is small,
x_{t}
retains most of the structure of
x_{0}
and
the conditional target
f_{t}^{\star}
primarily captures relatively smooth,
low-frequency residuals.
In contrast, for large
t
the signal-to-noise ratio of
x_{t}

decays and
x_{t}
becomes dominated by Gaussian noise.
In this high-noise regime, the network must reconstruct a
highly oscillatory, pixel-level noise field
\varepsilon
from an input
that contains very little information about
x_{0}
.
Consequently,
f_{t}^{\star}
is hard to approximate
and noisy as a regression target, which leads to optimization difficulties and artifact accumulation during sampling.

Given stroke-size control, our theoretical analysis asks how this operator
affects (i) the complexity of the regression targets
\{f_{t}^{\star}\}
,
and (ii) the regularity
and artifact level of the sampled images.
In the remainder of this section we (i) formalize the MultiStroke
training objective as regression under a linear-Gaussian corruption
family, and (ii) characterize how the resulting smoothing operations
reduce effective target complexity, and act as a frequency-aware regularizer at
sampling time.

5.2
Validity of the MultiStroke Objective

MultiStroke keeps the standard DDPM forward process (Section
4
, Eq. (
3
)). It changes the supervised signal by evaluating prediction errors in a stroke space through the linear map
A_{t}
defined in Eq. (
8
).
Given a standard forward sample
x_{t}
and noise
\varepsilon
, define the stroke-mixed input
x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}:=A_{t}(x_{t})
and the stroke-space target
\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}:=A_{t}(\varepsilon)
(Eqs. (
9
)-(
10
)) and consider the stroke-space loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MS}}(\theta)
in Eq. (
11
),
where
x_{t}
is sampled from the standard DDPM forward process (
3
).
Before analyzing complexity, we clarify what this objective implies at the population level.

Extension to subsampled DDPM schedules.

Our sampling-side analysis is written for the contiguous ancestral chain
t\to t-1
only for notational simplicity.
In practice, sampling often uses a decreasing schedule
\tau_{0}>\tau_{1}>\cdots>\tau_{N}=0
(subsampling), which induces “jump” steps
t\to s
with
s<t
.
For any such step, define the effective one-step coefficient

\alpha_{t:s}\;:=\;\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\bar{\alpha}_{s}}\in(0,1),

(17)

and the corresponding DDPM ancestral mean/variance

\mu_{t:s}(x,\hat{\varepsilon})\;:=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t:s}}}\Bigl(x-\frac{1-\alpha_{t:s}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\,\hat{\varepsilon}\Bigr),\;\sigma_{t:s}^{2}\;:=\;(1-\alpha_{t:s}).

(18)

Remark (jump variance).
We use the “fixedlarge” convention
\sigma_{t:s}^{2}=1-\alpha_{t:s}
. The posterior (“fixedsmall”) jump variance is
\tilde{\beta}_{t:s}=\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{s}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}(1-\alpha_{t:s})
; substituting
\sigma_{t:s}^{2}
with
\tilde{\beta}_{t:s}
leaves the deterministic stroke-attenuation arguments unchanged and only tightens the injected-noise term.

Under the destination-indexed convention, injected-noise mixing uses the destination weight
w_{s}
(recovering
w_{t-1}
when
s=t-1
).
All subsequent per-step statements extend to subsampled DDPM schedules by interpreting each occurrence of
t-1
as the destination index
s
and substituting
(\mu_{t},\sigma_{t}^{2})
with
(\mu_{t:s},\sigma_{t:s}^{2})
; we keep the contiguous notation in the proofs.

Population minimizer.

For the nearest-neighbor pooling-plus-upsampling stroke operator, Lemma
5.2
shows that
S_{k}
is an orthogonal projection onto the block-constant subspace, hence
\mathrm{spec}(S_{k})\subset\{0,1\}
.
For any
w_{t}<w_{\max}<1
, the map
A_{t}=(1-w_{t})I+w_{t}S_{k}
therefore has eigenvalues in
\{1,1-w_{t}\}
, so
A_{t}^{\top}A_{t}\succ 0
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MS}}
is a positive-definite weighted least-squares regression.

Proposition 5.1

(Optimal predictor for MultiStroke)
.

For the nearest-neighbor pooling-plus-upsampling stroke operator
S_{k}
(block-average pooling with stride
k
followed by nearest-neighbor upsampling; in our method
k=k_{\max}
) and any roughness schedule

\{w_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
with
w_{t}<w_{\max}<1
, the population minimizer of

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MS}}(\theta)
satisfies

\varepsilon_{\theta}^{\star}(x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varepsilon\mid x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y\bigr],\qquad t=1,\dots,T.

(19)

Moreover, under
w_{t}<w_{\max}<1
and Lemma
5.2
,
A_{t}
is invertible, so conditioning on
x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}=A_{t}(x_{t})
is equivalent to conditioning on
x_{t}
; hence the minimizer coincides with the standard DDPM conditional mean in
\varepsilon
space.

Proof.

Fix a timestep
t
and condition on
(x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y)
.
Since
A_{t}
is linear and depends only on
t
, the conditional risk of a predictor
f(x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y)
is

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|A_{t}(f(x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y)-\varepsilon)\|_{2}^{2}\mid x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y\bigr]=\\
&\mathbb{E}\bigl[(f(x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y)-\varepsilon)^{\top}Q_{t}(f(x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y)-\varepsilon)\mid x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y\bigr],\end{split}

(20)

where
Q_{t}:=A_{t}^{\top}A_{t}
.
By Lemma
5.2
, the nearest-neighbor
S_{k}
is an orthogonal projection, so for any
w_{t}<w_{\max}<1
the map

A_{t}=(1-w_{t})I+w_{t}S_{k}
has eigenvalues in
\{1,1-w_{t}\}
and thus
Q_{t}\succ 0
.
(Equivalently, it suffices that
S_{k}
is self-adjoint positive semidefinite with spectrum in
[0,1]
, in which case
\mathrm{spec}(A_{t})\subset[1-w_{t},1]
and again
Q_{t}\succ 0
.)
Expanding the quadratic and dropping constants independent of
f
gives

f^{\top}Q_{t}f-2f^{\top}Q_{t}\,\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y].

(21)

Because
Q_{t}\succ 0
, the unique minimizer satisfies
Q_{t}f=Q_{t}\,\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y]
, hence

f=\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y]
.
Since the overall objective averages this conditional risk over the sampling of
t
, the population minimizer satisfies (
19
) for each
t
.
∎

What the loss constrains.

Although the minimizer in the original
\varepsilon_{\theta}
coordinates is unchanged, the loss directly constrains the transformed output
A_{t}(\varepsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y))
. Since
A_{t}
is linear and independent of
(x_{t},y)
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\mid x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[A_{t}(\varepsilon)\mid x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y\bigr]\\
=A_{t}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varepsilon\mid x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y\bigr]=A_{t}f_{t}^{\star}(x_{t},y).\end{split}

(22)

This clarifies the mechanism studied in the remainder of this section. MultiStroke keeps the same Bayes predictor in
\varepsilon
space, but it reweights which components of the residual are emphasized during learning through the map
A_{t}
. This is the basis for the target-complexity and gradient-noise reductions we analyze next.

5.3
Target Complexity Reduction

Proposition
5.1
shows that MultiStroke is a weighted least-squares score-matching regression: it shares the same population minimizer in
\varepsilon
space, and the loss directly constrains the transformed target
\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=A_{t}(\varepsilon)
.
We now formalize how this blur reduces the effective
complexity of the regression problem, especially in high-noise
timesteps.

Effective target functions.

During training, a timestep index
J
is sampled (typically uniformly) from
\{1,\dots,T\}
; we write expectations conditional on the event
J=t
when focusing on a fixed step.
For standard DDPM
(
9
)
, the optimal target at timestep
t
is

f_{t}^{\star}(x_{t},y)=\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid X_{t}=x_{t},J=t,Y=y].

(23)

Under MultiStroke, the relevant supervised object in stroke space is

\begin{split}&h_{t}^{\star}(x_{t},y):=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\mid X_{t}=x_{t},J=t,Y=y]=\\
&A_{t}\,\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid X_{t}=x_{t},J=t,Y=y]=A_{t}f_{t}^{\star}(x_{t},y).\end{split}

(24)

The two families
\{f_{t}^{\star}\}
and
\{h_{t}^{\star}\}
share
the same domain
(x_{t},y)
but differ in how much high-frequency
structure they must express. We quantify this difference in an
idealized but illustrative setting.

A decomposition induced by the practical stroke operator.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
be the space of vectorized images with the standard inner product
\langle u,v\rangle=u^{\top}v
.
Rather than assuming an idealized frequency cutoff, we work with the
implemented
stroke operator
S_{k}
(block-average pooling with stride
k
followed by nearest-neighbor upsampling back to the original resolution; in our method
k=k_{\max}
).
Lemma
5.2
shows that this
S_{k}
is an orthogonal projection onto the block-constant subspace and gives an explicit Fourier-mode attenuation envelope.

Lemma 5.2

(A conservative bridge for pooling plus upsampling)
.

Let
S_{k}
be the practical stroke operator implemented as
k\times k
average pooling
(stride
k
) followed by nearest-neighbor upsampling back to the original resolution. In our method,
k=k_{\max}
. Assume
H
and
W
are divisible by
k
so that pooling partitions the grid into disjoint
k\times k
blocks.

The operator is linear, self-adjoint, and idempotent, because it maps each disjoint
k\times k
block to its average and then repeats that average across the block. In particular, it is the orthogonal projection onto the subspace of block-constant signals, so it never amplifies energy:
\|S_{k}x\|_{2}\leq\|x\|_{2}
for all
x
.

For a 2D complex Fourier mode aligned with the grid (interpreted on a periodic lattice with blocks aligned to the origin),

u[n_{1},n_{2}]=\exp(i(\omega_{1}n_{1}+\omega_{2}n_{2}))
,
define the amplitude attenuation ratio

\rho_{k}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):=\|S_{k}u\|_{2}/\|u\|_{2}
.
Then

\displaystyle\rho_{k}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{k}(\omega_{1})\,\gamma_{k}(\omega_{2}),

(25)

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}(\omega)

\displaystyle:=\left|\frac{\sin(k\omega/2)}{k\sin(\omega/2)}\right|,\qquad\gamma_{k}(0):=1.

In particular,
\gamma_{k}(\omega)
is close to
1
only for coarse frequencies; outside a neighborhood of
0
it is bounded away from
1
in magnitude and exhibits zeros at multiples of
2\pi/k
. This provides an explicit envelope for how pooling plus upsampling suppresses high-frequency content. A proof is given in Appendix
A.2
.

By Lemma
5.2
,
S_{k}
is an orthogonal projection. This induces the canonical orthogonal decomposition

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{c}}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{d}},\;\mathcal{H}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{c}}:=\mathrm{range}(S_{k}),\;\mathcal{H}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{d}}:=\ker(S_{k}),\\
Q_{\mathrm{c}}:=S_{k},\;Q_{\mathrm{d}}:=I-S_{k}.\end{split}

(26)

We refer to
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{c}}
as the
block-coarse
subspace (block averages) and
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{d}}
as the
detail
subspace (within-block variations).
Lemma
5.2
justifies this terminology by showing that aligned high-frequency Fourier modes have small retention ratio
\rho_{k}
under
S_{k}
.

For any timestep
t
, the stroke mixing map
A_{t}=(1-w_{t})I+w_{t}S_{k}
admits the exact projector form

A_{t}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}},\qquad A_{t}^{\top}A_{t}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}+(1-w_{t})^{2}Q_{\mathrm{d}}.

(27)

Hence the transformed noise decomposes
orthogonally
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=A_{t}(\varepsilon)=(1-w_{t})\varepsilon+w_{t}S_{k}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathrm{c}}\varepsilon+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon.

(28)

Thus MultiStroke leaves the block-coarse component unchanged while shrinking the detail component by a factor
(1-w_{t})
.

Proposition 5.3

(Detail-subspace variance reduction)
.

Assume the practical stroke operator
S_{k}
and define
Q_{\mathrm{c}},Q_{\mathrm{d}}
as in (
26
). If
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, then for any timestep
t
with
w_{t}\in[0,w_{\max}]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=(1-w_{t})^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon\|_{2}^{2}\bigr],

(29)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}\varepsilon\|_{2}^{2}\bigr].

(30)

Proof.

By (
28
) and
Q_{\mathrm{d}}Q_{\mathrm{c}}=0
,

Q_{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon
.
Hence

\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}=(1-w_{t})^{2}\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon\|_{2}^{2}
and taking expectations yields (
29
).
Similarly,
Q_{\mathrm{c}}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}\varepsilon
gives (
30
).
∎

Mode-wise energy envelope.

Equation (
27
) implies the exact identity

\|A_{t}x\|_{2}^{2}=\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}x\|_{2}^{2}+(1-w_{t})^{2}\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}x\|_{2}^{2}
.
For an aligned Fourier mode
u
in Lemma
5.2
, the retention ratio satisfies
\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}u\|_{2}/\|u\|_{2}=\rho_{k}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
and orthogonality gives
\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}u\|_{2}^{2}/\|u\|_{2}^{2}=1-\rho_{k}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})^{2}
.
Therefore MultiStroke attenuates the energy of that mode by

\frac{\|A_{t}u\|_{2}^{2}}{\|u\|_{2}^{2}}=\rho_{k}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})^{2}+(1-w_{t})^{2}\bigl(1-\rho_{k}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})^{2}\bigr).

(31)

When
\rho_{k}\approx 1
(coarse frequencies), the ratio is near
1
; when
\rho_{k}\approx 0
(frequencies strongly suppressed by pooling), the ratio approaches
(1-w_{t})^{2}
.

A multiresolution complexity proxy (one-scale Besov/Sobolev surrogate).

The decomposition (
26
) is a one-scale multiresolution split:
Q_{\mathrm{c}}
keeps block averages (coarse coefficients) and
Q_{\mathrm{d}}
keeps within-block variations (detail coefficients).
This construction is closely related to one-scale Besov/Sobolev smoothness surrogates that appear in nonparametric theory for diffusion models (e.g.,
24
).
To quantify “roughness” in a way that matches this operator, define for any
s\geq 0
the weighted quadratic functional

\mathcal{C}_{k,s}(x):=\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}x\|_{2}^{2}+k^{2s}\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}x\|_{2}^{2}.

(32)

Upweighting
Q_{\mathrm{d}}
by
k^{2s}
penalizes fine-scale detail more strongly; this functional can be viewed as a one-scale
B^{s}_{2,2}
(Haar) proxy for Sobolev-type roughness.
Using (
27
), for any
x
,

\mathcal{C}_{k,s}(A_{t}x)=\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}x\|_{2}^{2}+k^{2s}(1-w_{t})^{2}\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}x\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathcal{C}_{k,s}(x),

(33)

with strict inequality whenever
w_{t}>0
and
Q_{\mathrm{d}}x\neq 0
.

Implications for target complexity.

The stroke-space target function
h_{t}^{\star}
in (
24
) inherits the same projector structure because
h_{t}^{\star}=A_{t}f_{t}^{\star}
.
Specifically,

\displaystyle h_{t}^{\star}(x_{t},y)

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathrm{c}}f_{t}^{\star}(x_{t},y)+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}f_{t}^{\star}(x_{t},y).

(34)

Thus the “detail” component of the regression function is rescaled by
(1-w_{t})
, while the coarse component is preserved.
Consequently, any complexity measure that upweights fine-scale variation, for instance the multiresolution functional
\mathcal{C}_{k,s}
in (
32
), spectral RKHS norms, or Lipschitz/gradient seminorms along
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{d}}
, shrinks by at least a factor
(1-w_{t})^{2}
on the high component.
This makes the supervised object in the rough regime (large
w_{t}
) genuinely lower-complexity, without changing the Bayes predictor in
\varepsilon
space (Proposition
5.1
).

Finally, the split (
26
) is also aligned with the multiscale structure of U-Nets: in a Haar basis at scale
k
,
Q_{\mathrm{c}}
corresponds to scaling coefficients and
Q_{\mathrm{d}}
to detail coefficients.
MultiStroke therefore downweights the portion of the target that corresponds to fine-scale details, precisely the part that becomes most oscillatory and difficult to infer in the high-noise regime.
A more detailed derivation (including the multiresolution proxy and the Fourier-mode envelope) is provided in Appendix
A.3
.

5.4
Sampling-Time Regularization and Artifact Suppression

We now turn
to the reverse process and show how the MultiStroke modifications at
sampling time act as an explicit regularizer on the trajectory

\{X_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
, suppressing high-frequency artifacts in the generated
samples.

Recall from Section
4
that at each reverse step the
MultiStroke sampler first forms a stroke-mixed state
x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}

and a stroke-mixed perturbation
\tilde{\eta}_{t}

(Eqs. (
13
)-(
14
)),
predicts
\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}=\varepsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,y)
, and then
updates using the DDPM mean (Eq. (
6
)) via
Eq. (
15
).
For timesteps with
w_{t}=0
, this reduces to the standard DDPM update.

Coarse/detail decomposition induced by the stroke operator.

We reuse the projector decomposition from Section
5.3
.
Let
S_{k}
be the implemented pooling-plus-upsampling stroke operator and define the induced coarse/detail projections

Q_{\mathrm{c}}:=S_{k}
and
Q_{\mathrm{d}}:=I-S_{k}
(Eq. (
26
)).
Since
S_{k}
is an orthogonal projector (Lemma
5.2
), the stroke mixing map satisfies the exact identity

A_{t}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}
(Eq. (
27
)).
Therefore the sampling-time smoothing steps in
Eqs. (
13
)-(
14
)
admit the explicit component form

\displaystyle x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathrm{c}}x_{t}+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}x_{t},

(35)

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}_{t}

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathrm{c}}\eta_{t}+(1-w_{t-1})Q_{\mathrm{d}}\eta_{t}.

(36)

Thus MultiStroke preserves the block-coarse component of the state and noise, while shrinking the within-block detail component of the state by
(1-w_{t})
and the injected noise by
(1-w_{t-1})
(for jump steps
t\to s
, the destination-indexed convention uses
w_{s}
).

Affine surrogate reverse dynamics.

To keep the derivation fully explicit, we adopt an idealized local surrogate model in which the reverse mean is affine in the current (smoothed) state:

\mu_{t}(x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},\hat{\varepsilon}_{t})=M_{t}x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}+b_{t},

(37)

for some linear operator
M_{t}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and bias

b_{t}\in\mathcal{H}
depending on
t
and
y
.
Under this surrogate model, substituting (
37
) into the update
(
15
) yields the linear Gaussian recursion

x_{t-1}=M_{t}x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}+b_{t}+\sigma_{t}\tilde{\eta}_{t}.

(38)

For a general linear operator
M_{t}
, define the coarse-to-detail and detail-to-detail blocks

M_{t,\mathrm{dc}}:=Q_{\mathrm{d}}M_{t}Q_{\mathrm{c}}
and

M_{t,\mathrm{dd}}:=Q_{\mathrm{d}}M_{t}Q_{\mathrm{d}}
.
Substituting (
35
)-(
36
) into (
38
) yields the exact detail-component update:

\displaystyle Q_{\mathrm{d}}x_{t-1}

\displaystyle=M_{t,\mathrm{dc}}(Q_{\mathrm{c}}x_{t})+(1-w_{t})M_{t,\mathrm{dd}}(Q_{\mathrm{d}}x_{t})+Q_{\mathrm{d}}b_{t}

(39)

\displaystyle+\sigma_{t}(1-w_{t-1})Q_{\mathrm{d}}\eta_{t}.

The first term is a
signal-driven coarse-to-detail forcing
(details synthesized from coarse structure). This is the mechanism by which real U-Nets can (and should) add detail from coarse semantics.
The latter two stochastic terms are the ones that can accumulate into spurious high-frequency energy; MultiStroke contracts them by
(1-w_{t})
and
(1-w_{t-1})
.

Proposition 5.4

(Detail-energy control with coarse-to-detail forcing)
.

Under the affine surrogate reverse model (
37
)-(
38
), let

D_{t}:=Q_{\mathrm{d}}x_{t}
and
C_{t}:=Q_{\mathrm{c}}x_{t}
.
Define the operator norms

\rho_{t}:=\|M_{t,\mathrm{dd}}\|
and
\kappa_{t}:=\|M_{t,\mathrm{dc}}\|
.
Assume the injected noise
\eta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
is independent of
x_{t}
(conditional on
y
) and has zero mean.
Let

E_{t}:=\mathbb{E}\|D_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
,

C_{t}^{(2)}:=\mathbb{E}\|C_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
,

N_{t}:=\mathbb{E}\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}\eta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
,
and
B_{t}:=\mathbb{E}\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}b_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
.
Then

E_{t-1}\;\leq\;3\kappa_{t}^{2}\,C_{t}^{(2)}\;+3\rho_{t}^{2}(1-w_{t})^{2}E_{t}\;+3B_{t}\;+\sigma_{t}^{2}(1-w_{t-1})^{2}N_{t}.

(40)

In particular, the propagated detail energy and injected detail noise are explicitly scaled by
(1-w_{t})^{2}
and
(1-w_{t-1})^{2}
regardless of coarse-detail coupling.

Remark (clean contraction special case).

If coarse-to-detail coupling is negligible (
\kappa_{t}\approx 0
) and the detail bias is small (
B_{t}\approx 0
), (
40
) reduces to the clean recursion

E_{t-1}\lesssim\rho_{t}^{2}(1-w_{t})^{2}E_{t}+\sigma_{t}^{2}(1-w_{t-1})^{2}N_{t}
.

Proof.
See Appendix
A.4
.

Sampling-time regularization.

Proposition
5.4
formalizes the role of
MultiStroke as a sampling-time regularizer: in the high-noise regime
where the roughness weights
w_{t}
are largest, the reverse chain is
explicitly biased toward trajectories with small detail energy.
The block-coarse component, by contrast, evolves under the usual reverse
dynamics, as
Q_{\mathrm{c}}x_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}x_{t}

and
Q_{\mathrm{c}}\tilde{\eta}_{t}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}\eta_{t}
by
(
35
)-(
36
).
Intuitively, the early reverse steps operate on a block-coarse, smoothed
canvas, and only the later timesteps (with
w_{t}=0
) are allowed to
add fine-scale details.

7
Conclusion

We study stroke-size control as a
controlled intervention
for diffusion: a single stroke operator for coarsening supervision, prediction and perturbation.
Our analysis is anchored to a pooling-plus-upsampling stroke operator, which is an orthogonal projector and induces an exact coarse/detail decomposition.
Under this decomposition and assumptions, MultiStroke preserves coarse components while shrinking within-block detail components by
(1-w_{t})
, yielding explicit reductions in target complexity and suppressed detail-subspace gradient noise during training.
At sampling time, MultiStroke contracts propagated detail energy and attenuates injected detail noise, though there could be a bias-stability trade-off, introducing background bias when the application is aggressive. Future work could mitigate the issue through methods like background post-processing.

Broader Impact

MultiStroke is a lightweight control mechanism for diffusion training and sampling that potentially lowers energy and hardware costs for the generated content.
At the same time, it may lower the barrier to producing synthetic content, which can be misused for misinformation or deceptive purpose. Thus responsible practices are encouraged.

Proof of Proposition

We work on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
on
which the random variables

(X_{0},Y,J,\varepsilon)
are defined, with

(X_{0},Y)\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}
,
J
uniform on
\{1,\dots,T\}
,
and
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
independent of
(X_{0},Y,J)
.
For each timestep
t

A_{t}
denote the stroke-space map defined in Eq. (
8

We also define the stroke-mixed network input at step
t
by

X_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}:=A_{t}(X_{t}),

(41)

and set

\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}:=A_{t}(\varepsilon),

(42)

and we sample the forward noised input using the standard DDPM forward process

X_{t}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,X_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\varepsilon.

(43)

The MultiStroke loss (
11
) can be written as

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MS}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|A_{J}(\varepsilon_{\theta}(X_{J}^{\mathrm{ms}},J,Y))-\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(J)}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|A_{J}(\varepsilon_{\theta}(X_{J}^{\mathrm{ms}},J,Y)-\varepsilon)\|_{2}^{2}\bigr],

(44)

where
J
denotes the sampled training timestep,
X_{J}
is the standard forward sample (
43
) at that timestep,
X_{J}^{\mathrm{ms}}=A_{J}(X_{J})

8

\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=A_{t}(\varepsilon)
.

Lemma A.1

(Conditional expectation as
L^{2}
projection)
.

Let
Z
be a square-integrable random variable taking values in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
a sub-
\sigma
-algebra.
Then the function
g^{\star}=\mathbb{E}[Z\mid\mathcal{G}]
is the
unique element of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})
that minimizes

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|g-Z\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]\quad\text{over all }g\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P}).

Proof.

This is the standard Hilbert space projection property of conditional
expectation.
Equip
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
with the inner product

\langle U,V\rangle=\mathbb{E}[U^{\top}V]
.
Then
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})
is a closed subspace, and

\mathbb{E}[Z\mid\mathcal{G}]
is characterized as the unique element
of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})
such that

\mathbb{E}[(Z-\mathbb{E}[Z\mid\mathcal{G}])^{\top}G]=0

for all
G\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})
.
A short calculation then yields the Pythagorean identity

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|g-Z\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|g-\mathbb{E}[Z\mid\mathcal{G}]\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|Z-\mathbb{E}[Z\mid\mathcal{G}]\|_{2}^{2}\bigr],

which is minimized uniquely when
g=\mathbb{E}[Z\mid\mathcal{G}]
.
∎

We now apply Lemma
A.1
to the MultiStroke setting.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Let
J
denote the sampled timestep index in training. Fix
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
and condition on the event
\{J=t\}
.
For a measurable predictor
g(X_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,Y)
, define its stroke-space output

h(X_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,Y):=A_{t}\bigl(g(X_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,Y)\bigr).

Using (
44
) and
\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=A_{t}(\varepsilon)
, the conditional risk at timestep
t
is

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\|h(X_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},t,Y)-\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\,\Big|\,J=t\Bigr].

Let
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}=\sigma(X_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},Y,J=t)
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
(X_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}},Y)
under this conditioning.
By Lemma
A.1
, the unique minimizer in stroke space is

h^{\star}(x_{t},t,y)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\mid X_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}=x_{t},J=t,Y=y\bigr]=A_{t}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varepsilon\mid X_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}=x_{t},J=t,Y=y\bigr],

where the last identity uses the linearity of
A_{t}
and the fact that
A_{t}
is deterministic given
t
.
By Lemma
5.2
, the nearest-neighbor pooling+upsampling stroke operator is an orthogonal projection, so its eigenvalues are in
\{0,1\}
. Therefore, by Eq. (
8
), the map
A_{t}
has eigenvalues in
\{1,1-w_{t}\}
and is invertible whenever
w_{t}<w_{\max}<1
, so the corresponding minimizer in the original prediction space is

g^{\star}(x_{t},t,y)=A_{t}^{-1}h^{\star}(x_{t},t,y)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varepsilon\mid X_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}=x_{t},J=t,Y=y\bigr].

Because the training loss averages uniformly over
t
, the global population minimizer satisfies (
19
) for every timestep.
∎

A.2
Proof of Lemma
5.2

We give a derivation for the practical stroke operator
implemented as
k\times k
average pooling (stride
k
) followed by
nearest-neighbor upsampling back to the original resolution.
Assume
H
and
W
are divisible by
k
, so that the grid decomposes into disjoint blocks.

Step 1: Explicit block form.

Index the image as
x\in\mathbb{C}^{H\times W}
with entries
x[i,j]
for

i\in\{0,\dots,H-1\}
and
j\in\{0,\dots,W-1\}
.
For each block index
(p,q)
with
p\in\{0,\dots,H/k-1\}
and
q\in\{0,\dots,W/k-1\}
, define the
k\times k
block

B_{p,q}:=\{(pk+r_{1},\ qk+r_{2}):r_{1},r_{2}\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}\}.

Define the block average

\mathrm{avg}_{p,q}(x):=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in B_{p,q}}x[i,j].

Average pooling with stride
k
produces the coarse grid
(\mathrm{avg}_{p,q}(x))_{p,q}
, and nearest-neighbor upsampling repeats that coarse value across the entire block. Therefore the composed operator
S_{k}
satisfies, for every
(i,j)\in B_{p,q}
,

(S_{k}x)[i,j]\;=\;\mathrm{avg}_{p,q}(x).

(45)

Linearity is immediate from the fact that
\mathrm{avg}_{p,q}(\cdot)
is linear.

Step 2: Idempotence and the block-constant subspace.

Let
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{H\times W}
denote the subspace of block-constant signals,

\mathcal{V}:=\{v:\ \exists\ (c_{p,q})\text{ such that }v[i,j]=c_{p,q}\ \text{for all }(i,j)\in B_{p,q}\}.

From (
45
),
S_{k}x\in\mathcal{V}
for every
x
. Moreover, if
v\in\mathcal{V}
, then
\mathrm{avg}_{p,q}(v)=c_{p,q}
and hence
(S_{k}v)[i,j]=v[i,j]
on every block.
Thus
S_{k}
acts as the identity on
\mathcal{V}
, which implies
S_{k}^{2}=S_{k}
(idempotence).

Step 3: Orthogonality and self-adjointness.

Equip
\mathbb{C}^{H\times W}
with the standard inner product

\langle x,y\rangle:=\sum_{i,j}x[i,j]\overline{y[i,j]}
and norm
\|x\|_{2}^{2}=\langle x,x\rangle
.
Fix any
x
and any
v\in\mathcal{V}
with block constants
(c_{p,q})
.
Using (
45
),

\displaystyle\langle x-S_{k}x,\ v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q}\ \sum_{(i,j)\in B_{p,q}}\bigl(x[i,j]-\mathrm{avg}_{p,q}(x)\bigr)\ \overline{c_{p,q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q}\overline{c_{p,q}}\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in B_{p,q}}x[i,j]-\sum_{(i,j)\in B_{p,q}}\mathrm{avg}_{p,q}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q}\overline{c_{p,q}}\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in B_{p,q}}x[i,j]-k^{2}\,\mathrm{avg}_{p,q}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q}\overline{c_{p,q}}\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in B_{p,q}}x[i,j]-\sum_{(i,j)\in B_{p,q}}x[i,j]\right)=0.

Hence
x-S_{k}x
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{V}
, while
S_{k}x\in\mathcal{V}
.
This is exactly the characterization of an orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{V}
, so
S_{k}
is self-adjoint and idempotent.
In particular, by the Pythagorean theorem,

\|x\|_{2}^{2}\;=\;\|S_{k}x\|_{2}^{2}+\|x-S_{k}x\|_{2}^{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\|S_{k}x\|_{2}\leq\|x\|_{2}.

(46)

Step 4: Fourier attenuation ratio.

Assume a periodic lattice and consider the 2D complex Fourier mode aligned with the grid,

u[n_{1},n_{2}]\;=\;\exp\!\bigl(i(\omega_{1}n_{1}+\omega_{2}n_{2})\bigr),\qquad(n_{1},n_{2})\in\{0,\dots,H-1\}\times\{0,\dots,W-1\}.

Fix a block
B_{p,q}
and write
(n_{1},n_{2})=(pk+r_{1},\ qk+r_{2})
with
r_{1},r_{2}\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}
.
By (
45
),
(S_{k}u)
is constant on
B_{p,q}
with value equal to the block average:

\displaystyle(S_{k}u)[pk+r_{1},\ qk+r_{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{a=0}^{k-1}\sum_{b=0}^{k-1}\exp\!\bigl(i(\omega_{1}(pk+a)+\omega_{2}(qk+b))\bigr)

\displaystyle=\exp\!\bigl(i(\omega_{1}pk+\omega_{2}qk)\bigr)\,\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{a=0}^{k-1}e^{i\omega_{1}a}\right)\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{b=0}^{k-1}e^{i\omega_{2}b}\right).

Define the 1D averaging response

a_{k}(\omega):=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}e^{i\omega r}.

Since
S_{k}u
is block-constant with magnitude
|a_{k}(\omega_{1})a_{k}(\omega_{2})|
, we have

\|S_{k}u\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{p,q}\sum_{(i,j)\in B_{p,q}}|a_{k}(\omega_{1})a_{k}(\omega_{2})|^{2}=\frac{HW}{k^{2}}\cdot k^{2}\cdot|a_{k}(\omega_{1})a_{k}(\omega_{2})|^{2}=HW\,|a_{k}(\omega_{1})a_{k}(\omega_{2})|^{2}.

Also
\|u\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}}|u[n_{1},n_{2}]|^{2}=HW
.
Therefore

\rho_{k}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):=\frac{\|S_{k}u\|_{2}}{\|u\|_{2}}=|a_{k}(\omega_{1})|\,|a_{k}(\omega_{2})|.

It remains to compute
|a_{k}(\omega)|
.
For
\omega\not\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi)
, the geometric-series identity gives

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}e^{i\omega r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-e^{ik\omega}}{1-e^{i\omega}}=\frac{e^{ik\omega/2}\bigl(e^{-ik\omega/2}-e^{ik\omega/2}\bigr)}{e^{i\omega/2}\bigl(e^{-i\omega/2}-e^{i\omega/2}\bigr)}=e^{i\omega(k-1)/2}\,\frac{\sin(k\omega/2)}{\sin(\omega/2)}.

Hence

|a_{k}(\omega)|=\left|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}e^{i\omega r}\right|=\left|\frac{\sin(k\omega/2)}{k\sin(\omega/2)}\right|.

Define
\gamma_{k}(\omega):=|a_{k}(\omega)|
for
\omega\neq 0
and
\gamma_{k}(0):=\lim_{\omega\to 0}\gamma_{k}(\omega)=1
,
which yields exactly (
25
) and the factorization

\rho_{k}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\gamma_{k}(\omega_{1})\gamma_{k}(\omega_{2})
.

Finally,
\gamma_{k}(\omega)
is close to
1
only when
\omega
is close to
0
, and it has zeros at

\omega=2\pi\ell/k
for nonzero integers
\ell
, because
\sin(k\omega/2)=0
there.
This completes the proof.

A.3
Target Complexity Reduction

In this appendix we expand Section
5.3
using the
practical
stroke operator
S_{k}
(average pooling with stride
k
followed by nearest-neighbor upsampling back to the original resolution; in our method
k=k_{\max}
).
The key structural fact is that
S_{k}
is an
orthogonal projection
onto the block-constant subspace (Lemma
5.2
; proof in Appendix
A.2
).
This induces an exact coarse/detail decomposition and yields concrete, architecture-aligned complexity reductions for the training target.

Setup: induced coarse/detail subspaces.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
with inner product
\langle u,v\rangle=u^{\top}v
.
By Lemma
5.2
,
S_{k}
is self-adjoint and idempotent, hence an orthogonal projection.
Define the induced orthogonal decomposition

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{c}}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{d}},\qquad\mathcal{H}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{c}}:=\mathrm{range}(S_{k}),\;\mathcal{H}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{d}}:=\ker(S_{k}),\qquad Q_{\mathrm{c}}:=S_{k},\;Q_{\mathrm{d}}:=I-S_{k}.

(47)

We refer to
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{c}}
as the
block-coarse
subspace (block averages) and
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{d}}
as the
detail
subspace (within-block variations).

Exact projector algebra for
A_{t}
and for the transformed noise.

Fix a timestep
t
with roughness weight
w_{t}\in[0,w_{\max}]
.
Recall
A_{t}=(1-w_{t})I+w_{t}S_{k}
.
Using
I=Q_{\mathrm{c}}+Q_{\mathrm{d}}
and
S_{k}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}
, we obtain an exact projector form:

\displaystyle A_{t}

\displaystyle=(1-w_{t})(Q_{\mathrm{c}}+Q_{\mathrm{d}})+w_{t}Q_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathrm{c}}+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}.

(48)

Since
Q_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}
,
Q_{\mathrm{d}}^{2}=Q_{\mathrm{d}}
, and
Q_{\mathrm{c}}Q_{\mathrm{d}}=Q_{\mathrm{d}}Q_{\mathrm{c}}=0
, squaring gives

\displaystyle A_{t}^{\top}A_{t}

\displaystyle=\bigl(Q_{\mathrm{c}}+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}+(1-w_{t})^{2}Q_{\mathrm{d}}^{2}+(1-w_{t})(Q_{\mathrm{c}}Q_{\mathrm{d}}+Q_{\mathrm{d}}Q_{\mathrm{c}})

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathrm{c}}+(1-w_{t})^{2}Q_{\mathrm{d}}.

(49)

Consequently, for any
x\in\mathcal{H}
,

\|A_{t}x\|_{2}^{2}=x^{\top}A_{t}^{\top}A_{t}x=\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}x\|_{2}^{2}+(1-w_{t})^{2}\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}x\|_{2}^{2}.

(50)

Now let
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
be the diffusion noise.
The MultiStroke noise target is
\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}:=A_{t}(\varepsilon)
, which decomposes orthogonally as

\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}\varepsilon+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon.

(51)

Variance reduction in the detail subspace.

We restate Proposition
5.3
and give a proof.

Proposition A.2

(Detail-subspace variance reduction)
.

Assume the practical stroke operator
S_{k}
and define
Q_{\mathrm{c}},Q_{\mathrm{d}}
as in (
47
). If
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, then for any timestep
t
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=(1-w_{t})^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon\|_{2}^{2}\bigr],

(52)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}\varepsilon\|_{2}^{2}\bigr].

(53)

Proof.

From (
51
), applying
Q_{\mathrm{d}}
gives

Q_{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=Q_{\mathrm{d}}\bigl(Q_{\mathrm{c}}\varepsilon+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon\bigr)=Q_{\mathrm{d}}Q_{\mathrm{c}}\varepsilon+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}^{2}\varepsilon.

Because
Q_{\mathrm{d}}Q_{\mathrm{c}}=0
and
Q_{\mathrm{d}}^{2}=Q_{\mathrm{d}}
, this simplifies to

Q_{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon.

Taking squared norms yields

\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}=(1-w_{t})^{2}\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon\|_{2}^{2},

and taking expectations proves (
52
).
Similarly, applying
Q_{\mathrm{c}}
to (
51
) yields

Q_{\mathrm{c}}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}\bigl(Q_{\mathrm{c}}\varepsilon+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon\bigr)=Q_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\varepsilon+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{c}}Q_{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon=Q_{\mathrm{c}}\varepsilon,

using
Q_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}
and
Q_{\mathrm{c}}Q_{\mathrm{d}}=0
.
Taking expectations gives (
53
).
∎

Mode-wise envelope via the Fourier attenuation ratio.

Lemma
5.2
defines, for an aligned Fourier mode
u[n_{1},n_{2}]=\exp(i(\omega_{1}n_{1}+\omega_{2}n_{2}))
, the retention ratio

\rho_{k}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\|S_{k}u\|_{2}/\|u\|_{2}=\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}u\|_{2}/\|u\|_{2}
.
Because
Q_{\mathrm{c}}
is an orthogonal projection, the Pythagorean identity gives

\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}u\|_{2}^{2}=\|u\|_{2}^{2}-\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}u\|_{2}^{2}=\bigl(1-\rho_{k}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})^{2}\bigr)\|u\|_{2}^{2}.

Applying the energy identity (
50
) to
x=u
yields the exact energy attenuation

\frac{\|A_{t}u\|_{2}^{2}}{\|u\|_{2}^{2}}=\rho_{k}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})^{2}+(1-w_{t})^{2}\bigl(1-\rho_{k}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})^{2}\bigr),

(54)

which matches (
31
) in the main text.
When
\rho_{k}\approx 1
(low frequencies) the ratio is near
1
, and when
\rho_{k}\approx 0
(frequencies suppressed by pooling) the ratio approaches
(1-w_{t})^{2}
.

A multiresolution complexity functional.

The decomposition (
47
) yields a natural one-scale multiresolution proxy for roughness.
For
s\geq 0
, define

\mathcal{C}_{k,s}(x):=\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}x\|_{2}^{2}+k^{2s}\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}x\|_{2}^{2}.

(55)

Upweighting the detail subspace by
k^{2s}
penalizes fine-scale variation more strongly; this is a one-scale
B^{s}_{2,2}
(Haar) surrogate for Sobolev-type roughness.
Using (
48
), for any
x
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k,s}(A_{t}x)

\displaystyle=\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}A_{t}x\|_{2}^{2}+k^{2s}\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}A_{t}x\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}x\|_{2}^{2}+k^{2s}(1-w_{t})^{2}\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}x\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathcal{C}_{k,s}(x),

(56)

where we used
Q_{\mathrm{c}}A_{t}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}
and
Q_{\mathrm{d}}A_{t}=(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}
(both follow immediately from (
48
)).

Implications for the regression targets.

For a fixed timestep
t
, recall the standard DDPM regression target

f_{t}^{\star}(x_{t},y)=\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid X_{t}=x_{t},J=t,Y=y].

The objective compares
A_{t}(\varepsilon_{\theta})
to
A_{t}(\varepsilon)
, and the corresponding conditional-mean target is

h_{t}^{\star}(x_{t},y):=\mathbb{E}[A_{t}(\varepsilon)\mid X_{t}=x_{t},J=t,Y=y]=A_{t}f_{t}^{\star}(x_{t},y)

by linearity of conditional expectation.
Decomposing with (
48
) gives the exact split

h_{t}^{\star}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}f_{t}^{\star}+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}f_{t}^{\star}.

(57)

In particular, the detail component is rescaled by
(1-w_{t})
.
Applying (
55
)-(
56
) to
x=f_{t}^{\star}
yields

\mathcal{C}_{k,s}(h_{t}^{\star})=\|Q_{\mathrm{c}}f_{t}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}+k^{2s}(1-w_{t})^{2}\|Q_{\mathrm{d}}f_{t}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathcal{C}_{k,s}(f_{t}^{\star}),

(58)

with strict inequality whenever
w_{t}>0
and
Q_{\mathrm{d}}f_{t}^{\star}\neq 0
.

A.4
Sampling-Time Regularization

In this appendix we elaborate on the analysis in
Section
5.4
, providing a more explicit
derivation of the
detail-subspace
contraction bound for the reverse
trajectory under MultiStroke.

Setup and surrogate assumptions.

We reuse the coarse/detail projector decomposition induced by the
implemented stroke operator
S_{k}
(Section
5.3
):

Q_{\mathrm{c}}:=S_{k}
and
Q_{\mathrm{d}}:=I-S_{k}
.
Since
S_{k}
is an orthogonal projector (Lemma
5.2
),
the MultiStroke mixing map satisfies
A_{t}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}

(Eq. (
27
)).
Therefore, for the random reverse-chain state
X_{t}
and injected noise

\eta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, the stroke-mixed quantities satisfy the
exact identities

Q_{\mathrm{d}}X_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}=(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}X_{t},\qquad Q_{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\eta}_{t}=(1-w_{t-1})Q_{\mathrm{d}}\eta_{t}\quad\text{(contiguous chain)},

and under the destination-indexed convention for a jump
t\to s
the
factor
(1-w_{t-1})
is replaced by
(1-w_{s})
.

As in the main text, we adopt the affine surrogate reverse model
(
37
) together with the corresponding recursion
(
38
) (applied to the random variables
X_{t}

and
\eta_{t}
). No block-diagonal assumption is needed: we treat a
general

linear operator
M_{t}
and work with its coarse-to-detail and detail-to-detail
blocks. The main-text Proposition
5.4

is stated for a
general

M_{t}
and keeps coarse-to-detail forcing explicit;
the derivation below is purely algebraic once we expand the detail projection of
the surrogate update.

General coupling and coarse-to-detail forcing.

We record the exact detail recursion for a general linear operator
M_{t}
and
show that the MultiStroke scaling still acts
selectively
on the
propagated-detail and injected-noise terms, while leaving signal-driven
coarse-to-detail synthesis unaffected.

Define the coarse/detail components

C_{t}:=Q_{\mathrm{c}}X_{t}
and
D_{t}:=Q_{\mathrm{d}}X_{t}
and the detail-noise component
Z_{t}:=Q_{\mathrm{d}}\eta_{t}
.
For a general linear operator
M_{t}
, define the block operators

M_{t,\mathrm{dc}}:=Q_{\mathrm{d}}M_{t}Q_{\mathrm{c}}
and
M_{t,\mathrm{dd}}:=Q_{\mathrm{d}}M_{t}Q_{\mathrm{d}}
.
Substituting
X_{t}^{\mathrm{ms}}=Q_{\mathrm{c}}X_{t}+(1-w_{t})Q_{\mathrm{d}}X_{t}
and

Q_{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\eta}_{t}=(1-w_{t-1})Q_{\mathrm{d}}\eta_{t}
into the surrogate update (
38
) yields

D_{t-1}=M_{t,\mathrm{dc}}C_{t}+(1-w_{t})M_{t,\mathrm{dd}}D_{t}+(1-w_{t-1})\sigma_{t}Z_{t}+b_{t}^{\mathrm{d}},

(59)

where
b_{t}^{\mathrm{d}}:=Q_{\mathrm{d}}b_{t}
. This is the appendix form of the main-text decomposition in Eq. (
39
).
The term
M_{t,\mathrm{dc}}C_{t}+b_{t}^{\mathrm{d}}
can be viewed as
signal-driven detail synthesis
from coarse structure, while the propagated-detail and injected-noise terms are explicitly scaled by
(1-w_{t})
and
(1-w_{t-1})
.

A conservative second-moment bound follows by combining independence of the injected noise with a triangle-inequality bound on the deterministic part.

Lemma A.3

(Detail-energy bound with coarse-to-detail forcing)
.

Assume
\eta_{t}
is independent of
X_{t}
(conditional on
Y
) and has zero mean.
Let
\rho_{t}:=\|M_{t,\mathrm{dd}}\|
and
\kappa_{t}:=\|M_{t,\mathrm{dc}}\|
.
Define

E_{t}:=\mathbb{E}\|D_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
,

C_{t}^{(2)}:=\mathbb{E}\|C_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
,

N_{t}:=\mathbb{E}\|Z_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
,
and
B_{t}:=\mathbb{E}\|b_{t}^{\mathrm{d}}\|_{2}^{2}
.
Then

E_{t-1}\;\leq\;3\kappa_{t}^{2}\,C_{t}^{(2)}+3\rho_{t}^{2}(1-w_{t})^{2}E_{t}+3B_{t}+\sigma_{t}^{2}(1-w_{t-1})^{2}N_{t}.

(60)

In particular, when the coarse-to-detail coupling vanishes (
\kappa_{t}=0
) and
b_{t}^{\mathrm{d}}\equiv 0
, this reduces to the clean recursion (
62
).

Proof.

Starting from (
59
), write
D_{t-1}=u+v
where

u:=M_{t,\mathrm{dc}}C_{t}+(1-w_{t})M_{t,\mathrm{dd}}D_{t}+b_{t}^{\mathrm{d}},\qquad v:=(1-w_{t-1})\sigma_{t}Z_{t}.

By assumption,
v
is independent of
u
and has zero mean, hence
\mathbb{E}\langle u,v\rangle=0
and

E_{t-1}=\mathbb{E}\|u+v\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{2}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|v\|_{2}^{2}.

The noise term is
\mathbb{E}\|v\|_{2}^{2}=\sigma_{t}^{2}(1-w_{t-1})^{2}N_{t}
.
For the deterministic term, apply the inequality
\|a+b+c\|_{2}^{2}\leq 3(\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\|b\|_{2}^{2}+\|c\|_{2}^{2})
with

a:=M_{t,\mathrm{dc}}C_{t}
,
b:=(1-w_{t})M_{t,\mathrm{dd}}D_{t}
, and
c:=b_{t}^{\mathrm{d}}
:

\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{2}^{2}\leq 3\mathbb{E}\|M_{t,\mathrm{dc}}C_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+3(1-w_{t})^{2}\mathbb{E}\|M_{t,\mathrm{dd}}D_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+3\mathbb{E}\|b_{t}^{\mathrm{d}}\|_{2}^{2}.

Using
\|M_{t,\mathrm{dc}}C_{t}\|_{2}\leq\kappa_{t}\|C_{t}\|_{2}
and

\|M_{t,\mathrm{dd}}D_{t}\|_{2}\leq\rho_{t}\|D_{t}\|_{2}
gives

\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{2}^{2}\leq 3\kappa_{t}^{2}C_{t}^{(2)}+3\rho_{t}^{2}(1-w_{t})^{2}E_{t}+3B_{t}
.
Combining the two pieces yields (
60
).
∎

Special case: negligible coarse-to-detail forcing.

Lemma
A.3
(and the main-text Proposition
5.4
) are written for a
general
reverse-step linearization
M_{t}
, and therefore keep the coarse-to-detail forcing term
M_{t,\mathrm{dc}}C_{t}
explicit.
For intuition, it is also useful to record the clean special case in which coarse-to-detail coupling and detail bias are negligible.

Assume
M_{t,\mathrm{dc}}=0
(no coarse-to-detail forcing under the surrogate) and
b_{t}^{\mathrm{d}}\equiv 0
.
Then (
59
) reduces to the compact detail recursion

D_{t-1}=(1-w_{t})M_{t,\mathrm{dd}}D_{t}+(1-w_{t-1})\sigma_{t}Z_{t},

(61)

where
D_{t}:=Q_{\mathrm{d}}X_{t}
and
Z_{t}:=Q_{\mathrm{d}}\eta_{t}
.

Taking expectations and using independence of
Z_{t}
gives the clean second-moment recursion

E_{t-1}\;\leq\;\rho_{t}^{2}(1-w_{t})^{2}\,E_{t}+\sigma_{t}^{2}(1-w_{t-1})^{2}\,N_{t},

(62)

which is the
\kappa_{t}=0
,
B_{t}=0
specialization of (
60
).

Iterated contraction over multiple timesteps.

Consider a block of timesteps

t\in\mathcal{T}\subset\{1,\dots,T\}
in the rough regime where

w_{t}\geq w_{\min}:=\min_{t\in\mathcal{T}}w_{t}>0
and
\|M_{t,\mathrm{dd}}\|\leq\rho<1
for
all
t\in\mathcal{T}
.
Define the uniform contraction factor

q:=\rho^{2}(1-w_{\min})^{2}\in(0,1).

When
w_{\min}=0
, this reduces to the baseline contraction factor
q=\rho^{2}
; when
w_{\min}>0
, MultiStroke contributes an additional factor
(1-w_{\min})^{2}<1
.
Iterating (
62
) over the block and bounding the per-step
noise term by its maximum over
\mathcal{T}
gives

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|D_{t_{\mathrm{out}}}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]\;\leq\;q^{|\mathcal{T}|}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|D_{t_{\mathrm{in}}}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]+\frac{1-q^{|\mathcal{T}|}}{1-q}\,\max_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\Bigl(\sigma_{t}^{2}(1-w_{t-1})^{2}\,N_{t}\Bigr),

(63)

where
t_{\mathrm{in}}
and
t_{\mathrm{out}}
are the endpoints of

\mathcal{T}
.
Thus, in the rough regime, MultiStroke induces a geometric decay of
detail energy along the reverse trajectory, up to a reduced
detail noise floor.

This supports the interpretation of MultiStroke as a sampling-time
regularizer that explicitly biases the reverse chain toward smoother
trajectories: detail components are repeatedly contracted and
re-injected with reduced variance, while the block-coarse component
evolves under the usual reverse dynamics.

CelebA-HQ/FFHQ Diffusion Models

Dataset and preprocessing.

We use the CelebA-HQ dataset provided through HuggingFace (mattymchen/celeba-hq) in streaming mode. Each image is converted to RGB, resized to
256
using nearest-neighbour interpolation, center-cropped to
256
, and normalized to
[-1,1]
per channel.

Model initialization and diffusion setup.

We initialize from a pretrained
256\times 256
DDPM checkpoint (google/ddpm-ema-celebahq-256) using the diffusers UNet2DModel backbone. The forward process uses
T=1000
timesteps and we load the scheduler configuration from the checkpoint so that the variance type and prediction type match the pretrained model.

Optimization.

We train for
10{,}000
optimization steps with batch size
4
and AdamW with learning rate
5\times 10^{-5}
, betas
(0.9,0.999)
, epsilon
10^{-8}
, and weight decay
0
. We use
500
warmup steps, gradient clipping at
1.0
(global norm), and maintain an EMA of parameters with decay
0.9999
. Mixed precision is enabled in training. We fix the random seed to
0
for dataset shuffling and sampling.

MultiStroke configuration.

We use
k_{\max}=2
, rough fraction
3/4
, and
w_{\max}=0.5
. The stroke operator is
2\times 2
average pooling with stride
2
followed by nearest-neighbour upsampling back to
256\times 256
. The mixing weight
w_{t}
is zero on the first quarter of timesteps and increases linearly over the remaining timesteps.
In the fine-tuning implementation, the MultiStroke training target is timestep-aligned: the stroke-mixed noise target at step
t
uses the roughness weight of the adjacent next state in the reverse chain.

Sampling.

We sample with ancestral DDPM using
10/20
inference steps by uniformly sub-sampling the
1000
training timesteps. For MultiStroke, we apply the stroke operator to (i) the initial noise, (ii) the current state presented to the network at each reverse step, and (iii) the injected Gaussian noise at each step, using the timestep-dependent weight associated with the next timestep in the reverse chain. Stroke control stabilizes the early, low-SNR portion of the reverse chain, but it also introduces an early rough-portion bias. If the rough fraction or the maximum mixing weight is too high, fine background details can be washed out and remain under-specified. Later fine-detail timesteps can partially reintroduce high-frequency structure and can visually conceal some early bias, but the concealment is timestep dependent and the background bias can persist under aggressive stroke control. We therefore view stroke control as trading stability with bias.

For the FFHQ results, we fine-tune and evaluate the aforementioned CelebA pretrained model on the
256\times 256
FFHQ dataset
(
14
)
under the same hyperparameters as those used in CelebA
(
20
)
.

B.3
One-Class Classifier and Calibrated Score

We use a pretrained shared encoder with per-class centers to compute a calibrated typicality score for generated images.

Encoder and per-class centers.

The encoder is a small convolutional network with channels
32,64,128,128
and strided downsampling to
4\times 4
, followed by adaptive average pooling and a linear layer that outputs a
128
-dimensional feature. In addition, the model contains one learnable center vector per class. Given features
f(x)
and a class label
y
, we compute the squared distance to the class center as
\|f(x)-c_{y}\|_{2}^{2}
.

Training objective and hyperparameters.

We train the shared encoder and centers on CIFAR-10 using a prototype-classification objective: for class
j
, the logit is
-\|f_{\phi}(x)-c_{j}\|_{2}^{2}/\tau
, optimized with cross-entropy. We use
\tau=1.0
and train for
50
epochs with Adam (learning rate
10^{-3}
, weight decay
10^{-4}
) and batch size
128
. We split the CIFAR-10 training set into train/eval partitions with fraction
0.8/0.2
using a fixed seed (
0
) and reuse this split for calibration.

Label inference and scoring protocol.

For generated samples we infer the intended class from the filename convention used by our sampling scripts: if the basename ends with a
5
-digit image id
n
, we set
y=\lfloor n/8\rfloor\bmod 10
. Each generated image is resized to
32\times 32
with nearest-neighbour interpolation, embedded by the encoder, and scored via
d^{2}=\|f_{\phi}(x)-c_{y}\|_{2}^{2}
and
s(x,y)=1-F_{y}(d^{2})
. In our evaluation script we score up to
10,000
generated images per run.

Calibration split.

We reconstruct CIFAR-10 from the original batch files (data_batch_1 to data_batch_5) and split it into train and evaluation partitions using a fixed random seed. The train fraction is stored in the encoder checkpoint configuration (default
0.8
). For each evaluation image
(x,y)
we compute the squared distance
\|f(x)-c_{y}\|_{2}^{2}
and store the sorted values per class, defining an empirical CDF
F_{y}
.

Calibrated score.

For a generated image
x
with class label
y
, we compute
d^{2}=\|f(x)-c_{y}\|_{2}^{2}
and define the calibrated score

s(x,y)=1-F_{y}(d^{2})\in[0,1].

For convenience, we report
100\,s(x,y)
on a
[0,100]
scale. In the evaluation script we average this score over up to
10,000
generated images.

B.4
Frequency-Domain SNR Metric

We quantify whether a method preserves high-frequency content by comparing power spectra in low and high spatial-frequency bands.

Band definition.

We compute a 2D FFT on grayscale images and use radial masks on a centered frequency grid. Let
r_{\mathrm{norm}}\in[0,1]
denote the radius normalized by the maximum radius on this grid. The low-frequency band is defined by
r_{\mathrm{norm}}\leq 0.3
, and the high-frequency band is defined by
r_{\mathrm{norm}}\geq 0.6
.

Signal and noise spectra.

For CIFAR-10, we compute a per-class mean image from the CIFAR-10 test batch and treat its power spectrum as the signal. For a generated image
x
of class
c
, we form the deviation
\delta=x-\mu_{c}
and treat its power spectrum as the noise. For CelebA-HQ and FFHQ, we compute a global mean image from real samples at the same resolution and use it analogously.

SNR computation.

For each band
B
, we sum signal power and noise power over
B
and define

\mathrm{SNR}_{B}=10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_{\omega\in B}|\widehat{\mu}(\omega)|^{2}}{\sum_{\omega\in B}|\widehat{\delta}(\omega)|^{2}+\varepsilon},

with a small constant
\varepsilon
for numerical stability. We report the mean SNR in dB over generated samples.

Interpretation.

This SNR is defined relative to the mean image (signal) and therefore measures spectral
deviation-from-mean
. As a result, oversmoothing or reduced diversity can also increase SNR (by decreasing the deviation spectrum). We treat it as a diagnostic for spectral artifacts and interpret it alongside FID/OCS and qualitative samples.

B.5
Hyperparameter Tuning

For the ablation experiments we modify the MultiStroke configuration
while keeping the architecture, optimizer, and data unchanged.
We vary:

•

The fraction of timesteps affected by MultiStroke,

f_{\mathrm{rough}}\in\{0.5,0.75\}
, corresponding to applying
stroke control to the top
50\%
or
75\%
of timesteps.

•

The maximum roughness weight
w_{\max}\in\{0.5,0.99\}
,
with the same linear schedule in
t
.

•

Train-only vs. inference-only variants, where MultiStroke is
applied in training but not sampling, or vice versa, to probe the
relative importance of the stroke-space training objective and sampling-time
regularization.

All ablation runs are trained with the same number of epochs and
evaluated with the same sampling and metric protocols as the main
experiments.

B.6
Compute and Hardware

All reported experiments were run on a machine equipped with eight NVIDIA H200 GPUs.
We find that the per-step wall-clock cost is approximately the same for MultiStroke and DDPM. In the CelebA-HQ
256\times 256
fine-tuning runs (10,000 optimization steps, identical settings (batch size, optimizer, dataloader, mixed precision, and logging; only the MultiStroke toggle differs)), MultiStroke took 8,576 seconds while DDPM took 8,688 seconds.

Appendix D
Extended Related Work

D.1
Efficiency, Fast Sampling, and Latent Diffusion

Sampling speed has been a central concern for diffusion models.
Denoising diffusion implicit models (DDIMs)
(
32
)

introduced non-Markovian samplers that share the DDPM training
objective but enable deterministic or accelerated sampling.

5
demonstrated that diffusion models
can surpass GANs on image synthesis benchmarks and popularized
classifier guidance as a way to trade off diversity and fidelity.
Classifier-free guidance
(
11
)
later showed that
training joint conditional and unconditional models suffices to obtain
similar controllability without an external classifier.

A number of works design samplers that require fewer function evaluations.
DPM-Solver
(
21
)
and DEIS

(
37
)
derive high-order ODE solvers and exponential
integrators tailored to diffusion trajectories, achieving high-quality
samples in tens of steps.
Progressive distillation
(
30
)

iteratively halves the number of sampling steps by distilling a
slow teacher into faster students.
Consistency models
(
33
)
and related
consistency-based distillation methods further improve one- and few-step
sampling while remaining compatible with standard diffusion backbones.

Latent diffusion models
(
25
)
move the diffusion
process into the latent space of a pretrained autoencoder, dramatically
reducing both training and sampling cost while enabling high-resolution
generation and flexible conditioning.
Score-based generative modeling in latent space

(
36
)
similarly exploits latent representations to
improve likelihood and sampling efficiency.
These works modify the representation space or sampler; MultiStroke is
orthogonal in that it operates at the level of the noise targets and
reverse updates, and in principle can be combined with accelerated
solvers, distillation, or latent diffusion.

D.2
Multi-Scale Representations and Coarse-to-Fine Generation

Multi-scale structure is a key ingredient in modern generative models.
The U-Net architecture
(
26
)
provides an
encoder-decoder backbone with skip connections across scales and is
now standard in diffusion models.
Hierarchical and latent models such as LSGM
(
36
)

and latent diffusion
(
25
)
exploit coarse-to-fine
representations to trade off compute and detail, e.g., by running
diffusion in a lower-dimensional latent space and decoding to pixels
at the end.

Several diffusion systems explicitly adopt coarse-to-fine or cascaded
generation.
Cascaded diffusion models
(
10
)
chain a base model at
low resolution with super-resolution diffusion models.
SR3
(
27
)
performs conditional super-resolution via
iterative refinement and can serve as a component in cascaded pipelines.
Coarse-to-fine latent diffusion has also been explored in specialized
settings such as pose-guided person image synthesis

(
22
)
, where low-resolution or low-frequency structure is
generated first and refined with higher-frequency details.

These approaches primarily control the representation space or sampling
pipeline (e.g., resolution, latent dimensionality, or cascaded stages),
rather than the spatial scale of the noise supervision itself.
In standard diffusion training, the model predicts pixel-level noise at
the current resolution for all timesteps.

D.3
Noise Schedules, Regularization, and Task Simplification

A large body of work studies how noise schedules and parameterizations
affect optimization and sample quality.

23
showed that cosine schedules yield better
trade-offs between likelihood and sample quality than linear schedules.

16
and
12
analyzed how
parameterization, loss weighting, and noise levels control the effective
signal-to-noise ratio throughout training, leading to Min-SNR-style
reweighting schemes that de-emphasize the noisiest timesteps.
More recent work shows that the diffusion process itself can be learned:
MuLAN
(
28
)
introduces multivariate, data-dependent
noise that improves likelihood by adapting per-pixel noise rates rather
than relying on a fixed scalar schedule.

Beyond schedules, several methods aim to simplify denoising tasks at
difficult timesteps.
Immiscible Diffusion
(
18
)
reduces trajectory mixing
by assigning each image to a subset of the noise space, and a follow-up

(
19
)
generalizes this idea to broader miscibility
reduction mechanisms, yielding faster training and improved fidelity.
Denoising-task curricula
(
15
)
explicitly
measure timestep difficulty and train in an easy-to-hard order across
timestep clusters, improving convergence while remaining orthogonal to
architectural choices.
These methods change how data-noise pairs are assigned or ordered,
but still ask the model to predict full-resolution noise at every
timestep.

Our work is closely related in spirit to curriculum and target-smoothing
ideas, but operates along a different axis: instead of reordering or
reweighting timesteps, we coarsen the regression predictions, targets and
reverse updates at high noise levels.
This connects to analyses of spectral artifacts in deep generative
models, which show that high-frequency content is often poorly captured

(
6
)
, motivating frequency-aware regularization and
residual modeling.
To the best of our knowledge, prior work has not treated the
stroke size or spatial granularity of noise prediction as a
primary design axis.
MultiStroke fills this gap by explicitly modeling the spatial scale of
noise prediction across timesteps.

Appendix E
Visualization

To help interpret the frequency-band diagnostics in the main text, Figure
4
visualizes the
spatial-frequency content
of real images and generated samples.
For each condition, we compute a 2D discrete Fourier transform (DFT) on
grayscale
images (with the zero-frequency component shifted to the center), take the log-magnitude, and average the result over 1,000 images.
In these plots, the
center
corresponds to low spatial frequencies (global shape, large structures), while energy farther from the center corresponds to higher spatial frequencies (fine textures and pixel-scale artifacts).
Anisotropic streaks (e.g., a bright axis-aligned “cross”) indicate directional or grid-like artifacts, whereas excessive brightness near the corners indicates elevated high-frequency content.
MultiStroke aims to reduce spurious high-frequency energy in the reverse chain at early timesteps; thus, spectra whose
periphery
more closely matches real data are consistent with improved high-band SNR and fewer high-frequency artifacts.
Because our SNR is defined relative to the mean image (Appendix
B.4
), reduced diversity or oversmoothing can also reduce peripheral energy; we therefore treat these spectra as a diagnostic and interpret them alongside FID/OCS and qualitative samples.
