Community gardening as a conduit for bolstering wellbeing, employment, education and volunteering in vulnerable communities: a feasibility study
Authors: Danielle Tracey, Tonia Gray, Fiona Pigott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGrowing evidence promotes community gardening as an effective community-based initiative to enhance the wellbeing of community members, including those considered vulnerable. Whilst this expanding evidence-base is encouraging, little is known about the feasibility of community gardening thus limiting its scalability.MethodsThe current mixed-method study assesses the feasibility, acceptability and implementation of the Community Greening program delivered in vulnerable communities in New South Wales, Australia. Data was gathered from 33 survey participants, 39 interview participants, and case notes from seven sites.ResultsFindings demonstrate that participants have particular needs in wellbeing and employment outcomes, especially for those with reported disability. Attendance is variable with identified enablers and barriers. Satisfaction with the program is evident with suggestions for improvements. Finally, fidelity of implementation is strong, with an opportunity to increase community-led workshops, and enhance future implementation informed by the reported enablers and barriers.ConclusionsWith the positive impact of community gardening now established, findings guide how we effectively resource and sustainably implement programs with the fidelity required to realise their potential.Trial RegistrationN/A.

Background

Emerging evidence supports the salutogenic effect of community gardening particularly within vulnerable communities. Research suggests that engagement in community gardening activities results in physical, psychological, and social advantages [1–3]. Areas of impact encompass access to fresh produce and increased food security, opportunities for physical exertion, stress and anxiety mitigation, strengthened interpersonal connections and community resilience, and a bolstered sense of belonging and personal agency [4–7]. Systematic reviews indicate that community gardening can be a successful social or nature prescription activity across diverse settings [8–11]. Projects targeting youth, elderly, rural, urban, immigrant, hospital, social housing and low-social economic communities report positive impacts [12–16]. Whilst recent studies indicate the benefit of community garden programs for vulnerable communities, the nascent nature of this field warrants further empirical research to delineate optimal strategies for implementation, transference and sustainability across vulnerable communities to ensure potential impact is realised through fidelity of implementation.

Feasibility studies determine whether wellbeing interventions, like community gardening, are relevant, viable, transferable and scalable in other contexts [17]. They enable researchers, funding agencies and program providers to identify alternatives, explore new opportunities and determine factors which support or hinder implementation. Feasibility studies provide insights into the acceptability of interventions to their target populations which can inform refined design and optimisation of future programs. Identifying the ideal conditions for successful implementation and acceptability also contributes to the design of the larger-scale efficacy studies needed in this area [8].

A review of the research literature identifies several recent feasibility studies on community gardens as interventions for vulnerable populations. Prior to reviewing such studies, it is important to consider the concepts of community gardening and vulnerability. The term community gardening has been used in the literature to denote a range of gardening activities that share common features including the act of growing food or flowers in a public setting in a collaborative, communal and cooperative way for the benefit of all involved [18–20]. The term vulnerable remains a contested concept with a sociological approach proposing that vulnerability is socially constructed rather than indicating an inherent deficit within an individual [21]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines vulnerable groups as those that ‘due to factors usually considered outside of their control, do not have the same opportunities as other, more fortunate groups in society’ [22]. These factors may be related to living in remote or rural areas, low socioeconomic status, being a migrant, refugee or other ethnic minority, or having a disability [22–24].

The feasibility research studies include groups such as new arrivals, people living with physical and mental health conditions, people living in under-resourced communities, and older people. The interventions often involve short-term (7 week to 6 month) programs, small participant groups, and excluded people with mobility issues, cognitive disability and/or limited English-language skills. There are few studies that utilise mixed research methodology, and most call for further research with randomised control groups.

A feasibility study evaluated an urban garden-based nutrition and cooking program designed to reduce food insecurity and improve HIV therapy adherence in a low-resource Dominican Republic community [25]. Using qualitative content analysis of semi-structured interviews with 32 patients experiencing moderate to severe food insecurity, the study found the program feasible and acceptable. Completion rates were high, with 91% attending all nutrition sessions, 84% participating in the gardening workshop, and 71% applying gardening skills at home. Most participants rated the program as ‘helpful’ or ‘very helpful’ (93–98%) in improving dietary practices and emotional wellbeing. Reported implementation challenges included limited space and time, pest management issues, restricted plant varieties, infrequent sessions, and resource constraints such as transport and materials.

An Australian study explored the feasibility of community gardens within inner-city social housing for newly arrived populations [26]. Using phenomenological enquiry through focus groups (n = 19) and photo-elicitation, the study found high demand for community gardens among English-speaking residents, but notable acceptability and implementation barriers for Mandarin-speaking participants. English-speaking participants valued opportunities for social connection, physical activity, mental wellbeing, access to fresh produce, and engagement with nature. Factors enhancing participation included proximity, accessibility, and garden security. In contrast, language barriers, mobility limitations, and neighbourhood safety concerns hindered Mandarin-speaking residents’ involvement. The authors recommended applying community development principles, particularly participatory design, to better engage socially isolated groups in future garden initiatives.

In Pennsylvania, USA, a hospital-based mixed-methods study evaluated the feasibility of a community garden program aimed at promoting physical activity and nutrition education among patients (n = 15) at risk of cardiovascular disease [27]. Baseline and follow-up surveys were administered 12 weeks apart, complemented by semi-structured interviews at follow-up. The program was highly acceptable, with 93% of participants endorsing the intervention and 73% indicating they were likely to transfer gardening skills to their home environments. Attendance ranged from 73 to 100% across five sessions. Participants recommended additional opportunities for discussion, online or in-person, to enhance peer connection, and requested diverse learning resources, such as videos or online forums. Implementation was facilitated by emailed written materials, though participants sought more in-depth, practical content. The authors suggested future research incorporate longer interventions, larger randomised samples, and objective biomarker measures to assess health impacts.

In London, United Kingdom, an intergenerational, group and garden-based fall-prevention program was delivered to 16 older adults over three months [28]. Pre- and post-intervention questionnaires and mobility tests were complemented by post-program focus groups. Although statistically significant improvements in physical activity and social connectedness were limited, the program demonstrated strong feasibility through 100% retention, full attendance, and high enjoyment rates. Demand for continuation was substantial. Qualitative themes supporting feasibility included positive social interactions, a sense of achievement, purposeful physical activity, psychological safety, thoughtful site design, and aesthetically engaging natural experiences. Participants suggested longer sessions, raised garden beds to reduce kneeling, and more visible plant growth. Sustainability was found to depend on adequate preparation time, materials and tools, skilled facilitators, and high staff-to-participant ratios. The authors recommended further randomised controlled trials with larger cohorts to validate outcomes.

This current study responds and contributes to the research on the feasibility of community gardens as a health and wellbeing intervention in vulnerable populations, with a focus on the Botanic Gardens of Sydney’s Community Greening program. A strength of the study is that it reports on feasibility from the perspective of participant, partner organisation and delivery team. This mixed-method community gardening feasibility study will contribute to the evolving discourse on the feasibility of community gardening as a wellbeing intervention and identifies avenues for future inquiry and practical application.

Botanic gardens of sydney’s community greening program

Commencing in 2000, the Botanic Gardens of Sydney’s Community Greening Program has served approximately 212,700 participants across 1146 community gardens delivered in partnership with various not-for-profit social housing providers. The program aims to improve client outcomes and liveability; facilitate opportunities, build capacity, and strengthen communities; and help break the cycle of disadvantage in social housing estates in the longer term. More specifically, the program logic establishes immediate, intermediate, and long-term outcomes around education and skills, safety, home, physical and mental health, social and community and empowerment.

Communities can express their interest in joining the program either directly or through their housing provider. The creation of gardens is a collaborative effort with the community actively determining the type of garden, be it a nursery, flower, vegetable, or bushtucker garden. They also participate in designing the garden and, if physically able, contribute to its practical construction. Various groups, including youth, volunteers, and entities like NSW Youth Justice, corporate teams, or schools, are invited to assist the community in building these gardens. Scheduled workshops are conducted regularly over a minimum of 12 months, lasting 1–2 h each, and are facilitated by horticulturists from the Botanic Gardens of Sydney in partnership with staff from social housing partner organisations. During the initial setup phase, workshops occur fortnightly for 3–6 months and then transition to monthly or quarterly frequency, adapting to the specific needs of the groups involved. Workshop activities are tailored to each community’s needs and interests; for instance, one site may focus on constructing a picnic area while another establishes raised beds. Beyond practical gardening skills, activities encompass nature-based and cultural crafts, local garden tours, discussions on food security, and food preparation. The staff provides targeted training based on community requests, gradually enhancing participants’ learning capacities. This approach creates a consistent educational pathway, offering opportunities for volunteering with groups like Landcare or Bushcare and engaging in formal learning experiences such as horticulture studies at tertiary colleges.

To date, there is an emerging evidence-based demonstrating the positive impact of Community Greening on participants (see [1, 2, 10, 16]), however, no feasibility study has been conducted. With large scale implementation already reaching over 200,000 participants, understanding feasibility of the program is essential for further scale-up and increased geographical reach as well as setting a foundation for enhancing further efficacy evidence.

The current study

The current study aimed to explore the feasibility of a community gardening program in vulnerable communities. Using Bowen and colleagues’ [17] recommendations for conducting a feasibility study, the present research reports on three focus areas, including demand, acceptability, implementation. Usage demand relates to how extensively participants will utilise a program, while acceptability indicates whether the program meets participants’ satisfaction criteria. Implementation, on the other hand, pertains to the extent to which a program is executed as originally intended.

Methods

Research design

This study formed part of a larger piece of research which aimed to understand the implementation and impact of a community gardening program- Botanic Gardens of Sydney’s Community Greening Program- for vulnerable communities. Seven sites were involved in this study with all sites located in metropolitan suburbs of New South Wales, Australia. The socio-economic profiles of the suburbs varied from highly disadvantaged to more advantaged [29], although all communities were based in social housing. Characteristics of participants below highlight relative high proportions of migrants, Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islanders, and people with disability and medical conditions.

Whilst the limited-effectiveness testing [17] findings for the program have been reported elsewhere [12], this paper presents the findings regarding the feasibility of the Community Greening program. This study adopted a mixed-method approach to meet this objective including survey, interview and case note methodology.

Participants

This mixed-method study comprised three distinct participant groups: survey participants, interview participants and case note participants. Firstly, a total of 33 Community Greening participants completed a survey upon commencement1 into the program. Of the 33 participants, 21 (64%) were females, and 12 (36%) were males with a mean age of 55.85 (± 10.8) years. Fifteen participants (45%) were born in Australia and 18 (55%) were born overseas, whilst five participants (15%) reported being Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander. Twenty-two (67%) participants reported having a disability or long-term impairment. Thirteen (46%) participants reported having a medical condition and only five (18%) had carer responsibility (see Table 1 for further demographic characteristics).

Secondly, a total of 39 individuals from seven sites were interviewed including gardener participants (n = 27), partner agency staff (n = 9) and Botanic Gardens of Sydney staff (n = 3) who facilitated the program. Thirdly, four Botanic Gardens of Sydney staff (2 male, 2 female) completed the Case Note template for seven of the Community Greening programs under their responsibility.

Measures and procedures

Following ethical approval from the Western Sydney University Human Research Ethics Committee (approval H13481), new participants of the Community Greening program were invited to complete a survey. Participation was voluntary and participants could still engage in the program and opt out of the study. Information about the study including information sheets and consent forms were available to potential participants whilst they visited the Community Greening program. Informed written consent to participate was obtained from all participants of the study. The survey gathered demographics characteristics as well as measuring outcomes stated in the program logic and took approximately 15 min to complete. The outcomes were measured by psychometrically sound tools available in the literature and when these were not available, newly created tools. The following measure tools were utilised.

The Personal Wellbeing Index [30] gathered participants’ rating of their satisfaction with their health, safety, and connection with community. Ratings were made on a 11-point Likert scale which was transformed to a score of 0-100 with higher scores indicating higher satisfaction.

The Short Warwick Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale [31] measured reported mental health via 7 items on a 5-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate more positive mental wellbeing.

The General Self-Efficacy Scale [32] measured one’s belief in their ability to perform well in a variety of situations via 10 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale to generate a total score between 10 and 40 where higher scores indicate higher self-efficacy.

The researchers devised the following question to measure employment, education and volunteer participation: “Think about the activities you completed last week. How many hours did you spend on the following: a) working in paid employment, b) participating in an education or training course, c) volunteering.

At the commencement of the Community Greening program at the seven sites, the Botanic Gardens of Sydney staff were provided with a tailored case note template developed by the researchers to record the fidelity of implementation of the program, namely the ‘build and design’ and ‘workshop and tours’ components outlined in the program logic. Dates, duration, attendance, focus, resources required, and associate costs were recorded.

Approximately six months following the establishment of the Community Greening program, participant gardeners, Botanic Gardens of Sydney staff, and representatives from the partner implementation organisations participated in either focus groups or individual interviews (duration of 18 min to 44 min). All Botanic Gardens of Sydney staff and partner representatives were individually interviewed, whilst 12 gardeners were individually interviewed and 15 nominated to partake in focus groups. Semi-structured interview questions were designed to understand the perceptions and insights from stakeholders with a diverse range of experiences with the Community Greening program.

Participant gardeners were asked about their attendance patterns; what they liked most and least about the program; the barriers or enablers for their involvement; and how the program could be improved. The Botanic Gardens of Sydney and partner agency staff were asked to share their perspective given their involvement with the program. More specifically, the underlying factors that determine whether a person will experience positive outcomes from involvement; attendance rates; their ability to deliver the program as planned; enablers and barriers to implementing the program; and finally, the advantages or disadvantages of the gardening program over other programs.

Data analysis

Means and standard deviation of survey responses were calculated to examine the self-reported needs of the gardeners upon commencement into the Community Greening program. To understand the relative need compared to the general population, normative data available for the published measurement tools were used as a point of comparison. Group baseline characteristics were compared using either an independent sample t-test (for dichotomous demographic variables) and/or Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) (for demographic variables with > 2 categories) to check for differences in study outcomes across demography.

Semi-structured interviews were transcribed verbatim and analysed in NVivo software. Associative themes were identified in the data [33] using content analysis, a flexible primary data analysis method widely applied in community health and wellbeing qualitative research studies [34]. The research question was developed through the project logic and, along with existing research, guided the initial categorisation scheme and subsequent deductive coding approach [34]. Emergent themes and inductive codes were added to the organisational categories, ensuring participant priorities and agendas were represented in the results [35]. A peer-reviewed pilot analysis of two coded transcripts was conducted to check for reliability before data categorisation and coding was completed by one researcher [36]. Collaborative practices strengthened the data analysis with three researchers jointly interpreting the data and discussing emergent themes [37]. This practice is particularly necessary when reviewing small sample sizes from vulnerable communities [38]. Analytical reliability was increased, and bias was minimised by utilising diverse researcher perspectives when attending to the analysis quality of interviews [38].

Finally, frequencies and means were calculated from the data entered in the case note templates by the Botanic Gardens of Sydney staff across the seven sites to assess how well reported implementation aligned with the core components stipulated in the program logic.

Results

The results are presented to answer the three main objectives of the study: demand, acceptability, and implementation of the Community Greening program.

Demand for Community Greening

Attendance is cast as an indicator of demand, or more specifically, how extensively participants will use the program. Attendance rates demonstrated variability across sites and among individuals with some attending sporadically and others consistently throughout the life of the community garden. Flexibility to attend was valued by participants, while consistency in attendance was seen as a prerequisite to participants’ ownership of the garden, as explained by a partner agency staff: “I think it … really benefited the ones that have been coming because they keep coming. They take a bit of ownership and like to see it changing and growing.”

Factors that boosted attendance (see Table 2, presented by frequency of mentions) centred on relational, personal, and practical enablers. Feeling welcome, building friendships, and contributing to a shared purpose encouraged participation. As one staff member observed, “As soon as we have one or two community members keen, it becomes infectious.” Participants valued learning new gardening skills, access to fresh produce, and the enjoyment of being outdoors. Practical supports, such as raised garden beds, assistance for those with mobility challenges, and proximity of gardens to participants’ homes made attendance easier and more appealing.

Conversely, barriers to attendance (see Table 3, presented by frequency of mentions) reflected practical, personal, and relational constraints. Limited time, poor transport, vandalism, and restricted space reduced engagement, while ill health and perceptions of gardening as strenuous further deterred participation. Communication issues, including irregular scheduling and lack of promotion, also affected consistency. Relational barriers, such as cultural or language differences and low sense of ownership, compounded these challenges.

Acceptability of Community Greening

Acceptability indicates whether the program meets participants’ satisfaction criteria. Factors supporting program improvements (see Table 7, presented by frequency of mentions) were described across strategic, structural, content, logistical, and engagement domains. Strategically, participants and partner agencies emphasised embedding the garden within broader organisational plans, partnerships, and community governance to ensure continuity. Structurally, participants recommended more frequent sessions and embedding accredited or tailored training opportunities to increase skills and motivation. Content-related suggestions included providing written or digital fact sheets as “it’s hard when you’re just listening” and experiential learning through site visits to deepen knowledge and confidence. Logistical improvements, including secure storage for tools, “a small shed with a lock, otherwise it will get stolen”, and participant-led scheduling, were considered essential for engagement. Finally, participants and staff highlighted the importance of fostering belonging and visibility through shared uniforms, broader staff participation, and intergenerational or family sessions.

To strengthen fidelity of implementation, an analysis of interviews identified both enablers and barriers to implementation. Results indicate that factors supporting program implementation centred on collaboration, staff expertise, contextual fit, and intentional design (see Table 9, presented by frequency of mentions). Flexible, co-created sessions allowed BGS staff and partner agencies to respond to emerging community needs while valuing participants’ existing knowledge. Skilled, relationally attuned staff provided both technical guidance and encouragement, fostering trust and sustained engagement. One agency staff applauded the Botanic Gardens of Sydney staff as they “know the tenants like I do, … and so thoughtful, and also caring like above and beyond.” Contextual relevance was critical to success, for example, working in communities with pre-existing interest, suitable land, and modest resource requirements. Practical design features, including access to tools and ongoing post-program support, promoted independence and sustainability.

Discussion

Throughout this paper we have argued the benefits of community gardening programs for vulnerable communities are gaining credibility in scholarly circles [1, 2, 10, 12, 16]. Extant evidence has clearly established that community gardening works, but a myriad of more pressing questions remain. Firstly, how do we implement quality community gardening in vulnerable communities? Secondly, what are the key components needed for community gardening programs to thrive?

This study shifts attention from demonstrating program value to identifying the enablers and barriers that shape feasibility, acceptability and implementation fidelity: the practitioner-level knowledge required to translate evidence into sustained practice. Against this backdrop, the heightened need for this area of research cannot be underestimated. As Chari and Ngcamu [42] observe, more than half of the world’s population now lives in cities, with this proportion projected to reach 70% by 2050. Urban densification contributes to sedentary lifestyles and diminished access to fresh local produce [43]. Society increasingly favours supermarket chains offering unblemished products [44], a trend that cultivates ‘food deserts’ where vulnerable populations have limited access to healthy and affordable food [45]. Modern society’s disconnect from agricultural landscapes is a byproduct of escalating urbanisation [46], and community gardens represent an integral tool for addressing these challenges through social inclusion, food security, and community resilience.

Our findings identify several enablers that undergird effective implementation. At the outset, attendance level and relational factors proved paramount. Participants engaged consistently when they felt welcomed, could build meaningful friendships, and experience a shared community belonging and purpose. This aligns with Booth et al. [5], who found that participation levels correlate with strengthened social functions. Wood et al. [3] similarly demonstrated that therapeutic community gardening reduces loneliness, reinforcing the centrality of relational benefits to sustained engagement.

Perceived benefits from personal involvement in community gardening also enabled attendance. Participants valued learning practical skills, accessing fresh produce during food insecurity, and having meaningful outdoor activity. These findings echo Owens and Bunce [6], who highlighted the protective mental health effects of active nature-based engagement, and Bikomeye et al. [4], whose systematic review confirmed psychological wellbeing benefits from nature-based interventions. Practical supports, including accessibility modifications such as raised garden beds and proximity of gardens to homes, further reduced barriers to regular attendance. Correspondingly, Wang et al. [7] found that program acceptability is shaped by accessibility, garden size, location relative to users, training provision, and community involvement.

At the implementation level, collaboration amongst Botanic Gardens of Sydney staff, partner agency staff and gardener participants emerged as the cornerstone of success. Staff who co-created sessions responsive to community needs, acknowledged participants’ existing knowledge, and partnered genuinely with host agencies, undoubtedly achieved stronger engagement. Harrison et al. [13], reviewing nature-based interventions in socio-economically deprived communities, similarly emphasised tailoring programs to local contexts. The relational and technical skills that the staff infused into the delivery, mattered considerably to the participants. They valued facilitators who combined horticultural expertise with relationship-building and social capital. Our results correlate with other feasibility studies: Celeste-Villalvir et al. [25] and Veldheer et al. [27] both found that interagency delivery and sustainable human resourcing were integral to a program’s success. Mmako et al. [26] reinforced that community development principles, particularly participatory approaches, are essential for engaging socially isolated populations.

Contextual relevance functioned as an additional enabler. Programs building on existing community interest and providing locally applicable knowledge achieved greater engagement. Paquet et al. [15] found comparable results, noting that social prescription interventions were most effective when connecting participants with locally relevant resources. A scaffolded approach to program exit, with ongoing support post-program, contributed to sustained outcomes, consistent with Moore and Thew’s [14] emphasis on sustained rather than time-limited engagement with vulnerable populations.

A central finding, however, concerns barriers to implementation. Across all data sources, a common theme emerged: lack of long-term sustainable funding and resources weakens implementation regardless of other enabling factors. Limited host organisation resources, concerns about program continuity, and inadequate funding for essential logistics (including interpreter services for culturally diverse communities) constrained program reach and fidelity. Staff visits were often too infrequent and too brief, with sessions curtailed before meaningful engagement could occur. Briggs et al. [8] noted that program dosage and duration affect outcomes, suggesting under-resourced programs may fail to achieve their optimal potential.

Practical barriers reinforced these resource constraints. Participants faced competing time demands, transport difficulties, and frustration when vandalism or inadequate storage compromised their efforts. Celeste-Villalvir et al. [25] reported comparable challenges including limited space, infrequent sessions, and material constraints. Communication barriers, such as irregular scheduling and inadequate promotion, affected attendance consistency. Hsueh et al. [9] identified similar accessibility and reach barriers in their systematic review of place-based interventions.

Cultural and relational barriers presented additional challenges. Some participants reported feeling unwelcome due to community dynamics, while language differences hindered integration. Mmako et al. [26] found comparable barriers for non-English speaking residents, where language and safety concerns limited involvement despite high demand. At the implementation level, insufficient participatory governance structures were a major contributing factor to the reduced community ownership and long-term sustainability. Similarly, while implementation records demonstrated high fidelity, low rates of community led workshops had the potential to restrict authentic community involvement and leadership.

Finally, findings draw attention to the needs of vulnerable communities engaging in community gardening, with participants reporting lower relative wellbeing in personal health, feeling safe, community connectedness, mental wellbeing, and hours of employment. Feasibility is enhanced, as is program effectiveness, when the needs of participants are well understood and directs the pedagogy and curriculum of the experience. This is particularly heightened for participants with disability. This new knowledge advances how future programs might work with vulnerable communities as well as providing a model of a way of working which privileges understanding participants’ needs acutely from the outset.

Whilst this study advances our knowledge on implementing community gardening with vulnerable communities, the findings do not include the viewpoints of community members who either elected not to engage in the community gardens at all or who stopped attending. Additionally, the study analysis examines the seven sites as homogenous without giving due consideration to understanding the complexity and uniqueness of each local context, which is increasingly important for the successful implementation of public health interventions.

The findings offer recommendations for future research and practice. With attendance varying across sites, more research is needed to understand the local drivers of this variability to inform practice. Fidelity of implementation was high, which provides confidence in the program’s ability to meet the proposed outcomes for participants, apart from the portion of workshops that were led by the community. Research should strive to understand why this community leadership has not been realised and trial supports to elevate participants’ leadership to achieve authentic collaboration. Finally, a strength of this study was the assessment of participants’ needs upon commencement through the administration of a survey. Such processes could be embedded in practice to identify local needs and direct inputs to gain the positive outcomes postulated in the program logic.

Conclusions

Collectively, the findings from this current study offer clear implications at the organisational and structural levels, which are more malleable to change than the individual and community levels. Firstly, relational infrastructure is foundational, and organisations must invest in inclusive spaces where social connection flourishes alongside horticultural learning. Secondly, organisations must prioritise skilled facilitation which combines technical expertise with cultural responsiveness to promote feasibility. Thirdly, practical accessibility must be designed into programs by organisations from the outset. Finally, and most critically, at a structural level, sustainable funding for these programs is not peripheral, but central to implementation success. Short-term or episodic funding undermines the conditions (consistency, trust, community ownership) upon which effective community gardening depends. The question is no longer whether community gardening benefits vulnerable populations—but rather—how we effectively resource and sustainably implement programs with the fidelity required to realise their potential.
