1.0 Instrument Description
 The U.S. Department of Energy Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) user facility’s portable
 optical particle spectrometer (POPS) network (Figure 1) provides an aerosol size distribution spectrum
 from 150 nm to 5 μm in 16 logarithmically spaced bins. The data emanating from this network of up to
 four matching POPS in weatherproof enclosures comprises one-minute averages of the aerosol size and
 number spectrum and the co-located temperature and relative humidity. The total particle concentration
 and sample flow rate are also provided. The sample stream is not dried before its measurement. Two size
 spectra are provided: the default size distribution corresponds to the calibration using polystyrene latex
 spheres (PSL), while the second size distribution corresponds to the calibration using size-selected dry
 ammonium sulfate aerosol.

 Figure 1. One of three ARM POPS instruments installed across the Baltimore region during the
 Coast-Urban-Rural Atmospheric Gradient Experiment (CoURAGE). November 11, 1014.

 The principal scientific application of the data provided by the ARM POPS is to determine the aerosol
 size distribution across a region in three disparate locations – the size distribution is broadly relevant to
 studies of pollution and of conditions in the boundary layer. A high particle concentration can be an
 indicator of unhealthy levels of pollution; the dispersion of particles in the atmosphere is a predictor of
 long-range visibility; and particles serve as collectors of condensing gases including water vapor, and thus
 are a factor in the formation and properties of fogs and clouds, and in the initiation of precipitation.

1.1 Technical Specifications
This section summarizes specifications including the vendor, modifications, accuracy, sensitivity,
uncertainty, power consumption, diameter resolution, and flow rate.

1.1.1     Vendor

The modified POPS package has as its core the POPS developed by the National Oceanic and
Atmospheric Administration (NOAA; Gao et al. 2013, 2016, Watts 2017) and licensed and distributed by
Handix Scientific (model 1120). The Gao et al. POPS has undergone modifications in the past that
allowed it to be deployed in drone flights above the Arctic (Telg et al. 2017) and the Southern Great
Plains (Asher et al. 2021). The ARM instrumentation includes an airborne POPS described by Mei et al.
(Mei and Pekour 2026, Mei et al. 2020). Distinct from the airborne POPS of Mei et al., this handbook
describes a network of POPS nodes first deployed in 2024.

1.1.2 Modifications

Figure 2 depicts the schematic of the modified POPS instrument described in this handbook. The
modified POPS was developed at the University of California, Riverside (UCR) and is based on the work
of Kasparoglu et al. (2022). Modifications include the housing and onboard computer and sensors. Each
unit sits in a weatherproof enclosure mounted on a tripod. Inside the enclosure are the POPS, an onboard
data acquisition computer, a digital-to-analog signal converter (LabJack), and a temperature (T) and
relative humidity (RH) sensor (Rotronic Hygroclip HC2-S).

 Figure 2. Instrument schematic.

The elements within the enclosure are mounted to its steel backplate. Within the POPS case (tan area in
bottom half of Figure 2), the POPS circuit board (blue) is mounted on a separate backplate. The lid of the
POPS case has been removed and air therefore circulates freely; the fan in the side of the POPS case
assists. A larger pair of fans mounted into opposite sides of the enclosure bring in outdoor air and force
out internal air (Figure 2). These are sheltered externally by an attached rain shield (not pictured). The
1/16” steel inlet protrudes about 2 cm from the base of the enclosure and is unencumbered by netting or
rain covers, hovering about 1 m above the grass. An external vacuum pump provides flow through the
inline T and RH sensor and can be added into the POPS circuit as a backup (not pictured). This has a
higher power draw and has the potential to heat the instrument substantially; it is therefore housed at the
base of the tripod in a separate enclosure.

1.1.3 Accuracy

The accuracy of the POPS size distributions is within ±(3–5)% (Gao et al. 2016, Mei et al. 2020,
Kasparoglu et al. 2022). Mei et al. estimate an uncertainty of 5% based on the Cai et al. (2008)
comparison to a scanning mobility particle sizer (SMPS) measurement (Mei and Pekour 2026,
Mei et al. 2020). Gao et al. (2016) estimate an uncertainty of 3% for 500-nm PSL calibration standard.
Kasparoglu et al. (2022) characterize the POPS integration angle, response time, and coincidence rate.
Two additional comparisons between the UCR POPS and other instruments are introduced in Figure 3
and Figure 4.

Figure 3 shows the POPS (gray) size distribution alongside those measured by other instruments during
ARM’s 2023 Tracking Aerosol Convection Interactions Experiment (TRACER) near Houston, Texas,
which ran from October 2021 through September 2022. Solid lines show the mean spectral density for a
single day (July 1, 2022). The shaded area corresponds to the one-day mean multiplied by the geometric
standard deviation and the mean divided by the geometric standard deviation (n = 128), evaluated based
on 10-min time resolution data.

The attached temperature sensor is accurate to 0.1°C and the RH is accurate to 1% near saturation and
0.5% near 40% RH (Suda and Petters 2013). When the outdoor RH reaches 100%, the reported value
within the enclosure remains lower by about 20% but becomes constant with time. This is because the
interior is warmer than the exterior. To correct the data, apply an exterior temperature of around 2.25°C
lower than the interior and adjust your guess until the calculated RH is 100% at the point of saturation.

1.1.4 Sensitivity

The estimated upper limit of linearity is about 2000 cm-3 particles in total concentration, after which the
instrument begins to under-count. The upper limit of quantification is 4000 cm-3 , and the instrument
becomes saturated between 4000 and 5000 cm-3 .

The upper limitation is due to the rate at which the photomultiplier tube signal is digitized. The POPS
number concentration is obtained through an internal digitizer (Figure 1, BeagleBone) and preprocessed
into a size distribution using an edge computer that is part of the instrument (Figure 1, Computer). This
preprocessed size distribution is streamed to the network via a serial port. The digitizer can be too slow
when number concentrations exceed a certain threshold, leading to a bias in the reported concentration.
This is illustrated in Figure 4, which shows a comparison between the concentration reported by the serial
stream (black) and the concentration reported by an added multi-channel analyzer (MCA; red) card
compared to the total concentration measured by a condensation particle counter (CPC; x-axis). In these
experiments, the concentration was varied using a dilution valve and size-selected particles > 250 nm,
corresponding to sizes at which the MCA concentration and digitizer concentration agreed. The figure
shows that the POPS concentration begins to deviate for concentrations exceeding 2000 cm −3 and the
signal saturates below 5000 cm−3. The estimated upper limit of linearity is about 2000 cm-3 particles in
total concentration.

Figure 4. [source: Kasparoglu et al. 2022, Figure 6, licensed under CC BY 4.0] Relationship between
 CPC number concentration and POPS number concentration derived from the serial and
 MCA pulse height (PH) distributions. The solid black line corresponds to the 1:1 relationship.

The lower size cutoff of the POPS instrument was characterized by Mei et al. (2020) for 22 instruments
manufactured by Handix. After fitting a sigmoid to the detection efficiency of the POPS relative to the
total particle count detected by a CPC, they found that the diameter of 50% detection efficiency (the D50 )

 4

was 135 nm for size-selected ammonium sulfate particles. The prior assessment by Handix for 22 of their
instruments, conducted in 2020, resulted in a D50 of 129 ± 6 nm for PSL particles (Mei et al. 2020).

1.1.5 Power Consumption

Power consumption is ~ 15-20 W: 5 (POPS) + 6.5-10.5 (Computer) + 0.25 LabJack + 3.5 (power supply
original specification). If the box were closed, it would heat up proportionally to the temperature and air
movement outside the box and the radiative heating of the box by the sun. Since the box has two fans, its
interior temperature will be higher than the outside temperature and lower than the theoretical maximum
interior temperature based on the wattage and heat buildup in the 20 x 16 x 8'' enclosure of 1.5-mm steel.

1.1.6 Diameter Resolution

The diameter range and 17 bin boundaries are established by calibration with Mie scattering theory
(vendor calibration and Mei et al. 2020) and for the refractive index of polystyrene latex, these bounds are
115, 125, 135, 150, 165, 185, 210, 250, 350, 475, 575, 855, 1220, 1530, 1990, 2585, and 3370 nm for 16
size bins. The second calibration procedure based on ammonium sulfate (following Kasparoglu et al.
2022), performed for the ARM POPS units prior to their installment, gives the diameter bin bounds as
156, 172, 190, 210, 232, 269, 357, 469, 540, 690, 1068, 1534, 1910, 2538, 3288, 4331, and 5415 nm.

1.1.7 Flow Rate

The nominal POPS flow rate is 0.18 L/min (3 cm3 /s). The flow rate is measured via a laminar flow
element and actively controlled by regulating a miniature rotary vane pump controlled by a
proportional-integral-derivative (PID) circuit. Deviation from the nominal flow rate does not necessarily
correspond to corrupt data because the flow measurement and the flow control are separate circuits and
the instrument can operate at lower or higher flow rates. However, changes in flow rate may indicate
failing components associated with the PID circuit or with the pump itself. Data with deviation from the
nominal flow rate should be carefully investigated for integrity.

A backup pump is included in the setup along with a critical orifice designed to keep the flow at
approximately 3 cm3 /s. Choked flow through this orifice varies with pressure and temperature. Switching
to the backup pump results in an oscillating flow rate that does not precisely match the PID-controlled
flow rate with the default pump and circuit.

1.2 Instrument/Measurement Theory
The POPS is an optical particle spectrometer. The spectrometer records the passage of particles through a
continuous-flow detector and assigns a size to each particle, building a size distribution with high time
resolution. Particle size and number concentration are both assessed optically; the instrument does not
require a high-voltage power supply and it does not scan across diameters.

The basic geometry is as follows: particle-laden air passes through a laser beam and, when each particle
passes through the laser, the flash of scattered light is reflected by a cupped mirror below into a
photodetector above. The laser is a blue-violet 405-nm, 100-mW diode laser, the beam of which crosses
the length of the optical chamber and terminates in a beam dump. A concave mirror sits below the

 5

 intersection of the beam and the aerosol flow path, exactly 90° relative to the beam, and captures light
 over 38° to 142°, directing it into the photomultiplier tube above.

 The photomultiplier tube (PMT) converts incident photons into electrons via the photoelectric effect and
 the observed current is proportional to the brightness of the scattered light. The PMT uses a quartz
 window and a super bialkali antimonide photocathode with high gain. The peak sensitivity is near 400 nm
 and the typical dark current is 1 nA (max 10 nA); the rise time is 0.57 ns, and the signal produced by the
 particles is in the μA range.

 Particles scatter light as they traverse the laser beam. The amplitude of the collected light is not a linear
 function of particle size. The relationship between the particle size and the intensity of collected light
 depends on a number of factors including the particle’s refractive index, its diameter relative to the
 405-nm wavelength of the light, and the angle and breadth of the light collection, and additional
 instrument-specific factors that can raise or lower the entire response function. The calibration is
 performed by matching the instrument response to size-selected particles of known refractive index to the
 response predicted by Mie theory. The calibration was performed following Kasparoglu et al. (2022) with
 size-selected ammonium sulfate particles and additional calibrations were performed using PSL. The
 result of each calibration is a mapping of the instrument output to customized diameter bin boundaries.

2.0 Data

 2.1 Data Description
 The data are distributed as NetCDF files containing self-described variables of time, T, RH, flow, count,
 and particles per bin, among other variables. The particle spectral density has been normalized by the
 width of the bins.

 2.2 Data Quality and Uncertainty
 Under certain circumstances, the POPS data become suspect, or even unusable. The incidents and
 associated time periods are flagged by ARM and the mentors and archived in the form of Data Quality
 Reports (DQRs). Some examples of issues the POPS may encounter include:

 Laser power drops below threshold: This incapacitates the instrument and requires a replacement laser,
 laser realignment, and instrument recalibration. The signs of the laser failure are subtle and become more
 apparent when a steady and widening gap between the other POPS and the instrument in question opens
 up, with the failing instrument becoming slowly silent.

 Pump control failure: This happens when the onboard pump is not being controlled correctly by the
 software, and could indicate that the board is failing or that the pump itself is failing – the former will
 produce a more erratic signal, the latter produces a loud screeching that the onsite technicians may note.
 This does not render the data useless: it just means that the instrument is not controlling flow very well.
 Barring catastrophic failure of the pump, the measurement of flow, particle spectra, and particle number
 concentrations should be accurate.

 6

Instrument needs cleaning: This happens because the internal surfaces of the instrument’s optical
chamber (cavity containing the laser path, aerosol flow path, mirror, and beam dump) gradually acquire a
tiny layer of grime. This becomes apparent if the instrument does not report zero particles when sampling
through a filter (another reason would be if the instrument leaks). The POPS chamber, which is airtight
and operates below ambient pressure, must be dismantled to clean the optics.

Data flags: The data are flagged when the concentration is outside the range 0 to 4000 cm-3 or when the
flow deviates beyond a set range of acceptable values. Thus far the data flags have been lagging
indicators of issues that have arisen.

Temperature and relative humidity (RH): Temperature and RH are reported by a very robust sensor
that has not yet failed. If either holds the same value for excessively long, however, this could indicate a
problem. If the sensor becomes wet, it will reach 100% RH and remain at that value for a protracted
period before fully recovering (Suda and Petters 2013).

Counting uncertainty: Uncertainty in the total number concentration of particles reported by the POPS
can be attributed to uncertainty in the lower cutoff of particle detection and uncertainty in the measured
flow rate (Gao et al. 2016). The flow rate through the instrument is measured using the calibrated
relationship between the pressure drop across a laminar flow element and the flow rate as measured
externally. The margin of error was determined to be < 5% irrespective of the absolute pressure
(Gao et al. 2016). The diameter below which particles pass undetected is determined by the laser strength,
the photomultiplier tube’s low end of detection, and the background scattered light (cleanliness of optics).

Size uncertainty: Uncertainty in the diameter of particles reported by the POPS can be attributed to
uncertainties in particle refraction index and to Mie scattering resonances. Index of refraction varies with
both composition and water uptake, and can shift the calibration curve upwards or downwards. The
ambient population of particles may be internally or externally mixed, creating additional uncertainty in
the index of refraction.

Two calibrations are provided. These were measured separately using PSL and using size-selected
ammonium sulfate. The uncertainty due to Mie resonances is because the scattered light is not a
monotonic function of particle size in the Mie scattering regime. This starts above about 600 nm for the
405 nm wavelength laser used in the POPS. The calibration curve uses a smoothing algorithm to assign a
diameter based on scattered light; however, there are regions of ambiguity in the unsmoothed model
response. Gao et al. (2016) describe the uncertainty in more detail.

4.0 Maintenance Plan
 The POPS instrument requires service and sometimes repair every few months. At the time of service the
 instruments should be recalibrated and the optics cleaned.

5.0 Calibration Plan
 The POPS instrument can be calibrated in the field with PSL using a portable atomizer. The POPS does
 not need special preparation but the exact start/stop times will be important. Calibration can be
 coordinated with the mentor in near-real time. Note that the desiccant will need to be fresh in the atomizer
 setup.

 The atomizer outlet should not pressurize the POPS – an outlet vent with a HEPA filter should be
 attached to the atomizer to ensure that any overpressure is released. Sometimes a dilution bridge is inline
 and this is intended to help in case the particle concentration from the atomizer is too large for the
 instrument. The POPS has its own vacuum and should pull the atomized particles through at the
 appropriate rate. The link between the atomizer and POPS in the field is simple – hook the atomizer
 setup’s outlet onto the POPS inlet and try to make it airtight. The POPS inlet is very narrow and does not
 match the standard 1/4” tubing. Tape may be used unless 1/8” conductive tubing and an adapter are
 available.

 The solution of suspended PSL will vary in concentration of particles per mL of liquid because the
 solution is sold in a standard mass per volume rather than counts per volume. A typical solution consists
 of 1 droplet (50 uL) of PSL stock solution in 1 L of deionized water. This works for 100-nm PSL but does
 not work as well for larger PSL, which has exponentially fewer particles per volume of solution as
 diameter is increased. To calibrate the POPS, reduce the amount of pure water liquid and use one droplet
 of 200-nm PSL stock solution in 125 mL of distilled water; two droplets of 500-nm PSL stock solution in
 16 mL of distilled water; and four droplets of 700-nm PSL stock solution in 12 mL of distilled water.

 The diameters of PSL to atomize should span from about 200 to about 700 nm or higher. Testing 200-,
 500-, and 700-nm PSL will be appropriate. Gently rinse the atomizer by atomizing pure water between
 samples; run the POPS for a few seconds with nothing attached to ensure a blank between measurements.
 The Mie resonances kick in above about 700 nm.

 The duration of each PSL size check should be two to five minutes with a minute of clean air in between.
 Coordinating with the instrument mentor in real time to ensure that the PSL particles are detected is
 recommended. The PSL concentration recommendations are based on an atomizer liquid feed rate of
 0.4 mL/min and an airflow of about 3 L/min total at the point of atomization. The atomizer in the field
 may consume the solution at a faster rate.

6.0 User Notes and Known Issues
 The uncertainty of the smallest size bin is higher due to the uncertainty in the lower cutoff of detection. In
 the smallest bin, the data will not align as well with co-located SMPS instruments.
