Clinical Characteristics Associated With Response and Remission From Depression Using Measurement‐Based Care
Authors: Andres R Schneeberger, Joelle Robinett, Katia M Harlé, Eve Lasswell, Kristen L Duarte, Ashley Clark, Savita G Bhakta, Jessica Thackaberry, Kelly C Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMeasurement‐based care (MBC) is a vital tool in outpatient psychiatric care, enhancing treatment outcomes through systematic symptom monitoring. This study aimed to identify factors associated with remission and response in depression treatment using MBC, focusing on longitudinal changes in Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9) scores.MethodsElectronic health records of 4778 patients aged 12+ from an academic outpatient psychiatry clinic were analyzed. Inclusion criteria required a baseline PHQ‐9 score > 9 and at least one follow‐up PHQ‐9 measurement. Factors associated with treatment response and remission, using different definitions, were evaluated using Cox regression models, adjusting for demographic and clinical covariates.ResultsResponse and remission rates were 46.3% and 28.2%, respectively. For response, significant associated factors were combined treatment (HR = 1.189, p = 0.014), follow‐up PHQ‐9 within 4–9 months (HR = 1.303, p < 0.001), and absence of post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (HR = 0.817, p = 0.018). Key factors associated with faster remission (HR > 1) included combined psychiatry and psychotherapy treatment (HR = 1.189, p = 0.014), timely follow‐up PHQ‐9 administration within 4–9 months (HR = 1.255, p = 0.002), lower baseline PHQ‐9 scores (HR = 0.931, p < 0.001), and absence of PTSD (HR = 0.751, p = 0.010). Alternative remission definitions (PHQ‐9 < 12 or < 9) yielded similar findings.ConclusionIntegrated psychiatry‐psychotherapy approaches, regular symptom monitoring, and addressing comorbid conditions like PTSD are critical for improving depression outcomes. The findings shed light on how MBC can be used to evaluate treatment effectiveness, inform clinical practice, and guide optimized treatment strategies.Relevance to Clinical PracticeIntegrating MBC into a combined psychiatry‐psychotherapy framework and systematically addressing comorbidities provides clinicians with powerful strategies to significantly improve depression remission rates.

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a highly prevalent and debilitating condition, affecting an estimated 21 million adults in the United States annually and imposing substantial economic costs through healthcare expenditures and lost productivity. In 2019, the incremental societal economic burden of MDD was estimated at $333.7 billion, or $16,854 per adult with MDD (1). Depressive disorders are among the most prevalent and disabling conditions treated in outpatient behavioral health settings. The central challenge in its management lies in improving treatment response and achieving remission. While clinical response is important, remission is a critical goal as it is associated with superior long‐term outcomes, including a significantly lower risk of relapse and recurrence. However, a substantial proportion of patients do not achieve remission with initial treatment, underscoring the need for more effective strategies.

Measurement‐based care (MBC) is a systematic approach that utilizes validated tools to quantitatively monitor symptoms and guide treatment decisions. It is increasingly recognized as a critical component for improving the quality and outcomes of depression treatment. The systematic use of validated rating scales and questionnaires to quantitatively evaluate patients' symptoms, functioning, and progress serves as a cornerstone for collaborative treatment planning. MBC, supported by an increasing body of evidence, offers a pathway toward reduced symptom severity, enhanced treatment engagement, and optimized resource utilization (2, 3). In MDD, evidence demonstrates that MBC improves detection of residual symptoms, enhances treatment adjustment, and leads to better remission rates compared to treatment as usual (4).

A cornerstone instrument for MBC in depression is the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9). This is a brief, well‐validated self‐report scale that assesses presence and severity of the nine Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders 5 criteria for major depressive episodes (5, 6). When administered regularly (e.g., at each visit), longitudinal PHQ‐9 scores provide objective symptom monitoring to track treatment response trajectories and identify early non‐remitters who may benefit from therapeutic adjustments (7). Critically, specific residual depressive symptoms such as persistent sleep disturbance, fatigue, or anhedonia can signal risk for relapse, even in patients who have shown improvement, and highlights the importance of detailed, measurement‐based tracking (8). As such, expert treatment guidelines and consensus statements from organizations like the American Psychiatric Association and the American Psychological Association now promote routine use of the PHQ‐9 and broader MBC as standards of quality care for depression management (9, 10).

Central to the challenge of depression management is improving treatment response and achieving remission. Compared to clinical response, clinical remission may be more important because it is associated with lower risk of relapse (11, 12, 13). Most patients with depression do not achieve remission, making the identification of factors associated with remission crucial. Research has identified several factors associated with likelihood and timing of treatment response and remission in MDD including treatment type, dose, duration, baseline severity, treatment resistance, comorbid psychiatric, personality or medical conditions, environmental factors, illness course (e.g., chronic vs. acute), and genetic vulnerability (14, 15). Despite this, significant literature gaps remain, particularly in real‐world clinical settings following MBC implementation (16). There is a need for more studies that examine longitudinal symptom trajectories and contemporaneous factors associated with remission in routine practice. To address these gaps, this study evaluated longitudinal PHQ‐9 score changes and factors associated with depression treatment response and remission in an academic health system's outpatient psychiatry clinics following the adoption of MBC. The findings offer insights into MBC's utility in evaluating treatment effectiveness, informing clinical practice, and guiding optimized treatment strategies to improve patient care.

METHOD

Research Setting and Subjects

This study analyzed medical records of patients aged 12 years and older seen at outpatient psychiatry clinics between January 1, 2020 and December 31, 2023. Data were extracted from 26,641 patient electronic health records (EHR) using the research institution's data extraction concierge service and subsequently accessed through a secure research cloud environment.Inclusion criteria required patients to have a baseline PHQ‐9 score > 9 and at least one follow‐up PHQ‐9 measurement during the study period. Outpatient encounters included in‐person and telehealth visits. Demographic data included age, sex, gender, ethnicity, and race (17, 18).

Construct Definitions

Baseline PHQ‐9 scores were categorized as low (10–14) or high (15+). To ensure monitoring from baseline, PHQ‐9 follow‐up assessments within 4–9 months of baseline were analyzed. The presence or absence of psychiatric conditions of focus within our study period were determined according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM): major depressive disorder (MDD; F32.0–F32.4, F32.5, F32.9, F33.0–F33.3, F33.40–F33.42, F33.9), anxiety disorder (F41.x), bipolar disorder (F31.x), schizophrenia (F20.x), substance use disorder (SUD; F10.x), personality disorder (F6.x), and post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD; F43.10–F43.12). Severe mental illness (SMI) status was defined by ICD‐10 codes F20–F22, F24, F25, F28–F31, F32.3, F33.3 (19). Patient treatment complexity was based on the number of psychiatric conditions of focus present within the study observation period as a proxy and classified into three groups: no comorbidity (0–1), low comorbidity (2), and high comorbidity (3 or more). Treatment type was determined by analyzing outpatient psychiatry Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes recorded after a baseline PHQ‐9 score greater than 9. Patients were classified into one of two categories: 1. Medication Management Only: Included standard medication management visits (CPTs 99202–99205, 99212–99215) and medication management with a therapy add‐on (99202–99205/99212–99215 + 90833, 90836, or 90838). 2. Combined Treatment: Included medication management and psychotherapy with a therapist (CPTs 99202–99205, 99212–99215, or 99202–99205/99212–99215 + any of 90832, 90834, 90837, 90839, 90840, 90846, 90847, 90853). Treatment dosage was determined as the total number of encounters in the study period of treatment type. Aggregate CPTs indicative of receipt of psychotherapy treatment with a therapist alone or other forms of treatment (e.g., interventional psychiatry) were excluded from treatment type. Separate time‐to‐event variables were created for response and remission to indicate time in months from baseline PHQ‐9 until a patient either experienced an event or was censored (event of interest not observed before study end). Event status variables were also created for remission and response where 1 = event observed and 0 = censored.

Using the National Committee for Quality Assurance (NCQA) Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS) guidelines, response was defined as a follow‐up PHQ‐9 score that is at least 50% lower than the initial elevated PHQ‐9 score (where PHQ‐9 > 9) and remission was defined as a follow‐up PHQ‐9 score less than five after the initial elevated PHQ‐9 score (where PHQ‐9 > 9) (20). Alternative classifications of remission were also explored within an initial evaluation of meaningful change at 4–5 months post‐elevated baseline PHQ‐9 and subsequently within the study observation period overall. Alternative cutoffs for clinically significant remission were determined using definitions set forth by Jacobson and Truax (21) and normative data for the PHQ‐9 (22). Remission classifications at 4–5 months included a follow‐up PHQ‐9 score less than five (23), as well as < 9 or < 12 based on two standard formulations of reliable change (RC) score (21). Different studies defined the cut‐off scores accordingly, Kroenke et al. (6) recommended 9 as remission cut‐off score, while Kendrick et al. (24) suggested using 12 as the cut‐off score.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2.0. (25). Missing data were not included in the analyses. Categorical variables (i.e., age, gender, baseline elevated PHQ‐9 total score category, etc.) were analyzed using the chi‐square test. Index dates for variables were distinct for each patient and respective to their initial encounter.

Cox regression model analyses were conducted to assess the role of baseline clinical and demographic variables of interest on depression remission and response status. Selection of covariates for inclusion in our Cox regression models were guided by clinician background knowledge and expertise to concurrently assess the effects of predictors of depression remission and response on survival time. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs). Proportional hazards assumptions were met, and no multicollinearity was detected (VIFs < 2) (26, 27, 28). Cox models for response and remission were covariate‐adjusted for initial age, baseline elevated PHQ‐9 total score, gender, race, ethnicity, MDD, anxiety disorder, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, SUD, personality disorder, PTSD, treatment type, whether a patient had a follow‐up PHQ‐9 within 4–9 months of baseline elevated measure, and treatment dosage such that all VIFs were less than two. Additionally, propensity scores were calculated and included in our models by covariate adjustment to draw more robust interferences about the effect of treatment type on response and remission, as it helps reduce bias of coefficient estimates due to baseline differences in the absence of randomization (29, 30). Calculation of a propensity score for each patient entailed the use of a binary logistic regression model in which the exposure variable was treatment type, and the following covariates were included as independent variables: age, baseline PHQ‐9, gender, race, ethnicity, MDD, anxiety disorder, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, SUD, personality disorder, and PTSD.

RESULTS

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics for Remission and Response

A total of 4778 patients met the inclusion criteria (Figure 1). By the end of the study, 46.3% (2214/4778) achieved response (≥50% reduction in PHQ‐9 score) and 28.2% (1349/4778) achieved remission (PHQ‐9 < 5). Patients who achieved a response had a mean age of 44.5 years (SD 19.3) and a mean baseline PHQ‐9 score of 15.8 (SD 4.45). Patients who achieved remission had a mean age of 46.6 years (SD 19.8) and a mean baseline PHQ‐9 score of 14.5 (SD 4.12). The study population was predominantly White (65.8%), non‐Hispanic/Latino(a) (82.2%), and female (64.8%) (Table 1).

Among responders and remitters, the most frequently observed diagnoses were anxiety disorder (81.3% and 80.7%, respectively), MDD (64.7% and 64.1%, respectively), and SUD (23.4% and 23.0%, respectively) (Table 2). A greater proportion of patients who achieved response or remission received combined treatment (both psychiatry visits and psychotherapy sessions during treatment) and had a follow‐up PHQ‐9 within 4–9 months of baseline. Patients with low baseline PHQ‐9 scores were more likely to achieve remission. The main treatment type for patients who exhibited response or remission was concurrent engagement with a psychiatry provider, with most patients having low numbers of comorbid conditions (43.4%) and no SMI designation (81.6%). Women showed an improved response as compared to men (67.9% vs. 29.0%, p < 0.001). White race as compared to non‐white race also showed more improvement (67.5% vs. 32.5%, p = 0.019) (Table 2).

Cox Regression Analysis for Remission and Response

Among 2571 patients with complete data, median times to response and remission were 12.0 months (95% CI = [10.8, 13.2]) and 29.0 months (95% CI = [26.0, 32.0]), respectively. Multivariate cox regression revealed that combined treatment type (HR = 1.189, 95% CI = [1.035, 1.366]; p = 0.014), absence of PTSD (HR = 0.817, 95% CI = [0.692, 0.966]; p = 0.018), a follow up PHQ‐9 within 4–9 months of initial elevated measure (HR = [1.303, 95% CI 1.162, 1.460; p < 0.001), and lower treatment dosage (i.e., number of encounters received; HR = 0.994, 95% CI = [0.990, 0.998]; p = 0.005) predicted faster response (Table 3). Baseline lower PHQ‐9 (HR = 0.931, 95% CI = [0.908, 0.954]; p < 0.001), absence of PTSD (HR = 0.751, 95% CI = [0.604, 0.932]; p = 0.010), a follow‐up PHQ‐9 within 4–9 months of initial elevated measure (HR = 1.255, 95% CI = [1.087, 1.450]; p = 0.002), and lower treatment dosage (i.e., number of encounters received; HR = 0.993, 95% CI = [0.988, 0.998; p = 0.009) predicted faster remission times (using PHQ‐9 < 5 definition) (Table 4).

Apart from time‐to‐event considerations, alternative classifications of remission were explored to assess RC at 4–5 months post elevated baseline PHQ‐9. Reliable change is looking at how much change has occurred that is greater than expected from solely measurement error. Patients with reliable improvement have scores that change in the positive direction with a magnitude of change that exceeds the RC threshold (RC >1.96). Patients with indeterminate change have scores that change, but the magnitude of change is smaller than the RC threshold (RC ≤ 1.96). Patients with deterioration have scores that change in the negative direction, and the magnitude of the negative change is smaller than the RC threshold (RC ≤ −1.96). As such, reliable remission was defined as an instance in which a follow up PHQ‐9 score was indicative of remission and demonstrative of reliable improvement.

A total of 1012 patients had a follow up PHQ‐9 within 4–5 months of their baseline elevated PHQ‐9 with 54.5% demonstrating reliable improvement, 34.4% with indeterminate change, and 11.1% with reliable deterioration. When differentiating by reliable remission: 15.6% had a follow up PHQ‐9 less than five, 41.7% had a follow up PHQ‐9 < 12, and 31.5% had a follow up PHQ‐9 < 9. These findings prompted further investigation as to how alternative clinically significant cutoffs for remission could influence effect size. Notably, the effect size of treatment type became significant for classifications of remission as a follow‐up PHQ‐9 < 12 (cutoff b) and < 9 (cutoff c). When remission was defined as a follow‐up PHQ‐9 score < 12 after the initial elevated PHQ‐9 score, baseline lower PHQ‐9 (HR = 0.925, 95% CI = [0.910, 0.940]; p < 0.001), combined treatment type (HR = 1.168, 95% CI = [1.040, 1.312]; p = 0.009), absence of personality disorder (HR = 0.789, 95% CI = [0.661, 0.942]; p = 0.009), absence of PTSD (HR = 0.861, 95% CI = [0.748, 0.991]; p = 0.038), a follow‐up PHQ‐9 within 4–9 months of initial elevated measure (HR = 1.401, 95% CI = [1.274, 1.542]; p < 0.001), and lower treatment dosage (HR = 0.992, 95% CI = [0.989, 0.996]; p < 0.001) were predictive of faster remission (Table 4). When remission was defined as a follow‐up PHQ‐9 score less than nine after the initial elevated PHQ‐9 score, lower baseline PHQ‐9 (HR = 0.916, 95% CI = [0.898, 0.934]; p < 0.001), combined treatment type (HR = 1.228, 95% CI = [1.077, 1.400]; p = 0.002), absence of PTSD (HR = 0.810, 95% CI = [0.688, 0.955]; p = 0.012), a follow‐up PHQ‐9 within 4–9 months of initial elevated measure (HR = 1.275, 95% CI = [1.144, 1.420]; p < 0.001), and lower treatment dosage (HR = 0.994, 95% CI = [0.990, 0.998]; p = 0.002) predicted greater speed of remission (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective, longitudinal study of patients in an outpatient psychiatry clinic, the overall depression response and remission rates were 46.3% and 28.2%, respectively. Key predictors of positive outcomes included female sex, White race (for response only), late adulthood (age 65+), combined treatment (psychiatric medication management plus psychotherapy), and timely follow‐up PHQ‐9 administration within 4–9 months. Conversely, the presence of a personality disorder, a diagnosis of PTSD, and higher baseline PHQ‐9 scores were significant negative predictors of both response and remission. Furthermore, the study demonstrated that the choice of remission cutoff (e.g., PHQ‐9 < 12 vs. standard cutoffs) significantly influenced outcome rates, with less stringent cutoffs capturing broader treatment effects in complex or severe cases.

Our findings are largely consistent with the Sequenced Treatment Alternatives to Relieve Depression (STAR*D) trial, which reported response and remission rates of 47% and 27%, respectively, despite differences in symptom measurement tools. Similar to STAR*D, female sex was associated with higher likelihood of response and remission. However, our finding that White patients were more likely to achieve response (but not remission) contrasts with STAR*D, where race was not a significant predictor of remission (15). Notably, late adulthood (65+ years) showed markedly different rates compared to STAR*D (response: 21.5% vs. 12.8%; remission: 26.0% vs. 13.2%). While community‐based antidepressant trials in late‐life depression report remission rates of 55%–81% (31), our lower rates likely reflect the real‐world complexity of our sample, including multiple prior antidepressant trials (32, 33, 34), high comorbidity burdens (35, 36), and adverse psychosocial factors (37). Unlike STAR*D, age was not a predictor of remission in our multivariable models.

Our finding that combined psychiatric and psychotherapeutic treatment outperformed medication‐only approaches aligns with prior meta‐analyses and reviews (38, 39). The association between timely follow‐up (4–9 months) and better outcomes likely reflects improved treatment adherence, a finding that extends to racially and ethnically minoritized low‐income individuals (40, 41). The negative impact of personality disorder on depression outcomes is consistent with the broader literature showing that depressive symptoms in these patients are less likely to remit over time without longer‐term, personality‐focused treatment (42). Similarly, the finding that comorbid PTSD worsens depression outcomes aligns with evidence that PTSD and mood disorders mutually exacerbate each other (43), and that patients with less complex presentations respond more quickly (44). Finally, the inverse relationship between baseline PHQ‐9 severity and remission mirrors STAR*D findings (15, 45), and the significant impact of remission cutoff choice is well‐documented in the literature (39, 46, 47, 48).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the observational design using EHR data limits causal inference and control for confounding. To handle missing demographic and clinical values, we excluded incomplete records. While this can introduce selection bias, we applied propensity score adjustment to mitigate its impact. We also did not have any information about the completion rates. Second, treatment heterogeneity was not fully controlled; we did not account for specific modalities, dosages, or therapeutic approaches beyond broad categories (psychiatry only vs. combined treatment). Third, the definition of follow‐up PHQ‐9 within 4–9 months may not capture the full trajectory of treatment response, as patients with different assessment intervals may have varying outcomes. Fourth, while psychiatric comorbidities were assessed, we did not fully explore the impact of medical comorbidities or psychosocial factors (e.g., socioeconomic status, social support), all of which are known to influence depression management and outcomes.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this real‐world study found that MBC outcomes for major depression in an outpatient psychiatry setting were comparable to those from landmark trials like STAR*D. Combination treatment, older age, and timely follow‐up were positive predictors, while personality disorder, PTSD, and higher baseline severity were negative predictors. The observational design and limitations highlight critical gaps for future research. Prospective, randomized controlled trials are needed to establish causality and compare specific treatment protocols. Future studies should also incorporate broader patient populations, including those with medical comorbidities and diverse psychosocial backgrounds, and utilize more comprehensive outcome measures beyond PHQ‐9 alone. Additionally, research should examine whether longer‐term or specialized treatments (e.g., trauma‐informed or personality‐disorder‐focused therapies) can improve outcomes for the higher‐risk subgroups identified in this study.
