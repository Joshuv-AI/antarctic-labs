Crosstalk In: crosstalk-aware inference of tumor microenvironment cell infiltration
Authors: Qianbei Yi, Jiaqi Yuan, Zheng Ye, Peng Xu, Wenbin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe tumor microenvironment (TME) is a complex ecosystem whose cellular composition and interactions shape tumor progression, therapeutic response, and patient prognosis. However, most bulk deconvolution methods infer cell-type composition while treating cell types independently.MethodsIn this paper, we develop CrosstalkIn, a crosstalk-aware bulk deconvolution framework that constructs patient-specific cell-cell networks by integrating Gene Ontology (GO)-based functional similarity and protein-protein interaction (PPI)-based molecular relationships. A modified random walk with restart algorithm is then applied to calculate cell Infiltration Scores (InScores).ResultsBenchmark results on two flow-cytometry-validated cohorts demonstrate that CrosstalkIn achieves superior deconvolution performance, with the largest or second-largest Spearman correlations for most evaluated cell types. In lower-grade glioma, CrosstalkIn-derived InScores identify survival-associated cell types, generate accurate prognostic risk scores, and stratify patients into distinct survival groups. Across multiple adenocarcinoma cohorts, the risk score shows consistent prognostic value, particularly in advanced-stage patients. In melanoma, CrosstalkIn improves immunotherapy response prediction and identifies biologically interpretable cell-type biomarkers.ConclusionCrosstalkIn provides a robust framework for crosstalk-aware inference of TME cell infiltration from bulk transcriptomic data and supports cancer prognosis and immunotherapy response prediction.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08478-3.

Introduction

The occurrence and development of tumors are within a complex microenvironment comprising various components and cell types including immune cells, stromal cells, and stem cells, in addition to tumor cells [1, 2]. Recent studies further highlight that stromal elements and immune regulatory processes actively shape tumor progression, therapy resistance, and immune regulation [3, 4]. A comprehensive characterization of the tumor microenvironment (TME), especially, the relative abundances of various immune cells and their intercellular crosstalk is essential for understanding tumor biology and developing effective therapies [5–7]. Although accurate, traditional quantification methods (IHC, immunofluorescence, flow cytometry, and CyTOF mass spectrometry) are laborious and low throughput which limit their application in large numbers of samples [8–10]. During the last decade, computational approaches have been developed to quantitatively estimate cell-type population by the large amount of bulk RNA sequencing data.

Reference-based methods model bulk expression profiles as a linear combination of predefined cell-type-specific scRNA-seq expressions. CIBERSORT employed support vector regression (SVR) to determine the weight of each cell type [11]. To prevent invalid negative proportions, EPIC minimized expression discrepancy using a constrained least-squares regression strategy [12]. Based on the same idea, quanTIseq further mitigated the interference caused by overlapping signatures among different cell types [13]. TIMER corrected tumor purity and platform bias by assessing the partial gene-immune correlation [14]. MuSiC applied a tree-guided deconvolution procedure to iteratively impose more weight on influential genes by incorporating both cross-subject and cross-cell consistency [15]. BayesPrism accounted for the uncertainty in cell type contributions by a Bayesian hierarchical model [16]. Scaden was a deep network to directly deconvolute cell-type proportions from bulk expression [17]. However, these methods rely on large, well-annotated scRNA-seq references.

Marker-based methods quantify the relative fractions of cell types based on the expression of cell type–specific signatures. ssGSEA quantified the cell-type abundance based on the ranking of their marker genes within a single sample’s overall expression profile [18]. xCell further reduced the cross–cell-type interference using a spillover compensation matrix, which was constructed based on the quantified enrichment of each cell-type marker gene set in purified cell populations [19]. MCP-counter estimated the absolute abundance of specific cell types based on the average expression levels of highly specific transcriptomic markers, bypassing the need for complex full-mixture deconvolution [20]. ImmuCellAI refined enrichment scores by leveraging predefined similarity relationships among T-cell subsets [21]. Because of the low abundance of some cell populations and the lack of specificity of marker genes, CITMIC integrated bulk expression data with Gene-Ontology (GO) knowledge. Specifically, it inferred cell-type compositions on a weighted cell-cell network derived from GO similarity by the random-walk algorithm. Obviously, the key of CITMIC relies on the construction of the cell-cell interaction networks.

Given the markers of cell types, their interactions can be reflected in two perspectives: gene level and function level. The former captures shared function similarity among marker genes, whereas the latter captures their underlying interactions. To comprehensively characterize cell-cell crosstalk, we first construct a hybrid network that integrates GO similarity and PPI relations. Subsequently, we apply a modified random walk with restart (RWR) to this network to quantify the relative abundances of cell types. Quantitative evaluation on the SDY311 and SDY420 cohorts demonstrates that CrosstalkIn outperforms current methods, achieving the first or second highest correlation in cell types, while existing methods frequently yield near-zero or negative estimates. In lower-grade gliomas (LGGs), the InScores effectively identify survival-associated cell types, construct a risk score with high prognostic accuracy for survival prediction, and uncover the immune and non-immune pathways underlying glioma progression via functional enrichment analysis. This prognostic capacity is highly generalizable across multiple adenocarcinoma types (BRCA, LUAD, READ, STAD), with the risk score showing higher prediction accuracy for advanced patients and strong correlations with key clinical, molecular and immune-related features of breast cancer, serving as a comprehensive prognostic indicator. Additionally, in melanoma, CrosstalkIn leverages InScores to identify prognostic cell type biomarkers, and builds a prediction model that achieves superior accuracy for immunotherapy response. Collectively, these results confirm that CrosstalkIn not only refines cell infiltration estimation, but also provides a biologically meaningful basis for cancer prognosis stratification and immunotherapy response prediction, with broad application value across different cancer types and clinical scenarios.

We use Spearman correlation between estimated InScores and FACS-derived cell fractions to assess concordance in cell-abundance trends across samples, rather than direct equivalence to absolute cell proportions.

To quantify the added value of the hybrid network, we perform a network ablation analysis by comparing GO-only, PPI-only, and combined networks. For each network variant, we first calculate Spearman correlations between predicted InScores and FACS-derived cell fractions for each evaluated cell type, and then average these values across all evaluated cell types to obtain the mean Spearman correlation. These results suggest that CrosstalkIn is relatively stable across intermediate β values and that integrating GO-based functional similarity with PPI-derived molecular association information provides superior performance.

Survival prediction in lower-grade gliomas (LGGs)

We investigate the application of InScores in survival analysis on slow-growing primary brain tumors, lower-grade gliomas (LGGs), which exhibit considerable heterogeneity shaped by the cellular composition of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [22]. We download the bulk RNA-seq data and corresponding clinical information (including overall survival time and vital status) from 527 TCGA-LGG patients [23, 24]. To evaluate the predictive performance of the InScore-based risk score, we perform all prognostic analyses using a 5-fold cross-validation strategy. To further uncover the biological and clinical relevance of the risk score, we focus on BRCA as a representative adenocarcinoma and examine its associations with established clinical, molecular, and immune-related features. These features primarily include intrinsic molecular subtypes (Basal, Luminal A/B, HER2-enriched, and Normal-like), hormone receptor status (ER and PR), lymph node involvement, histological subtype (IDC, ILC, or mixed), and key genomic alterations (TP53 and FAT3 mutation status) [36]. In addition, we incorporate global tumor-state metrics, including stemness score, immune score, stromal score, and ESTIMATE score, to capture broader features of tumor differentiation and microenvironmental composition [37]. Fig. 5E shows the distribution of molecular subtypes and other clinical, molecular, and immune-related features along the ordered risk score. Specifically, Fig. 5F shows a moderate negative correlation between the risk score and Immune Score (R = −0.51), consistent with Thorsson et al.’s pan-cancer analysis that immune-hot tumors have distinct immune signatures linked to better outcomes [38]. Consistently, Fig. 5G shows subtype-specific differences in risk score, with lower risk scores in Basal-like tumors and higher scores in Normal-like/Luminal A tumors. This pattern is consistent with previous reports that basal-like/triple-negative breast cancers often exhibit higher immune infiltration [39]. Although Basal-like tumors are generally more immune-infiltrated, their poor clinical outcome is also influenced by intrinsic tumor aggressiveness and subtype-specific molecular features. Therefore, the risk score reflects a TME-related prognostic axis that complements, rather than replaces, molecular subtype information. Fig. 5H–I reveals higher risk scores in ER-positive and PR-positive patients. This aligns with McKenzie et al.’s finding that these tumors have a good initial prognosis but carry persistent long-term recurrence risk caused by endocrine therapy resistance [40]. Fig. 5J shows higher risk scores in TP53-mutant than wild-type patients, consistent with Zhang et al.’s finding that TP53 mutation was associated with poorer prognosis in breast cancer [41]. Fig. 5K shows lower risk scores in invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) than in invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), consistent with Chen et al.’s finding that ILC was associated with more favorable early overall survival compared to IDC [42]. These observations suggest that the risk score captures TME-related and clinically relevant features in BRCA and may complement established molecular and pathological classifications for prognosis assessment.

Immunotherapy response prediction in melanoma

Immunotherapy response refers to the extent of clinical benefit that a patient derives from immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) [43, 44]. Melanoma has served as the paradigmatic model for immunotherapy development due to its high mutational burden and frequent infiltration by immune cells [45, 46]. We obtain bulk RNA-seq data from the TCGA-SKCM cohort and matched immunotherapy response data from the melanoma anti-PD-1 cohort (phs000452). We calculate the InScores and perform univariate Cox regression to identify prognostic cell types (biomarkers), and subsequently use these significant features (p-value < 0.05) in a Lasso logistic regression model (randomly split into 70% training and 30% validation sets) to estimate the probability of immunotherapy response.

Figure 6A shows the ROC curves of immunotherapy response prediction by CrosstalkIn, ssGSEA, and CITMIC. CrosstalkIn achieves the highest predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.735), outperforming ssGSEA and CITMIC. Pairwise DeLong tests show that CrosstalkIn significantly outperforms CITMIC (p = 0.039) and shows a trend toward improved performance compared with ssGSEA (p = 0.054). Figure 6B shows the probability density function (PDF) distributions of prediction scores for responders versus non-responders. CrosstalkIn achieves a distinct separation between responders and non-responders. CrosstalkIn identifies far more cell type features with both positive and negative biological correlations, whereas CITMIC only detects cell types with negative correlations, and ssGSEA yields fewer cell type features. This difference in feature selection contributes to the lower predictive accuracy of CITMIC and ssGSEA. Notably, CrosstalkIn identifies several cell types with established biological significance as predictive features, such as astrocytes, aDCs, and exhausted T cells. Our analysis reveals that astrocytes exhibit a positive coefficient and higher abundance in non-responders, which aligns with the finding by Priego et al. that reactive astrocytes create an immunosuppressive niche facilitating brain metastasis [47]. We find that aDCs are enriched and positively associated with response in our cohort, which aligns with the finding by Spranger et al. that functional dendritic cell activation is essential for effective antigen presentation and T‑cell priming during immune‑checkpoint blockade [48]. We identify exhausted T cells as a relevant predictive feature, which aligns with the finding by Sade‑Feldman et al. that specific exhausted T cell states retain proliferative potential and are preferentially reinvigorated by immunotherapy in melanoma [49]. In conclusion, CrosstalkIn achieves superior accuracy and biological interpretability in predicting immunotherapy response in melanoma, highlighting its potential for both clinical prognostication and mechanistic insight into the tumor immune microenvironment. We performed the same analysis in glioblastoma (GBM), renal cell carcinoma (RCC), and stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), the detailed results are provided in Supplementary Figure S7.

Discussion

In this study, we propose CrosstalkIn, a deconvolution framework that incorporates cell–cell crosstalk to improve the estimation of tumor microenvironment (TME) composition from bulk transcriptomic data. By integrating protein–protein interactions (PPI) and gene ontology (GO) functional similarity information into a unified cell–cell crosstalk network, CrosstalkIn enables precise assessment of the degree of cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment. This network-driven perspective yields a more physiologically coherent representation of the TME, enhancing the accuracy of composition estimates and enabling biologically meaningful prognosis survival analysis and immunotherapy response prediction.

Compared with CITMIC, CrosstalkIn achieves a more comprehensive fusion of data-driven and knowledge-driven deconvolution. CITMIC achieves cell infiltration estimation by building cell–cell crosstalk networks using GO-based functional similarity, which captures shared biological processes and pathway-level co-annotation among marker genes. In contrast, the PPI-based network used in CrosstalkIn provides protein-level molecular association and network-topological information from STRING-derived evidence, with edges supported by multiple evidence sources, including experimental data, co-expression, text mining. Therefore, GO-based and PPI-based networks provide complementary views of cell-type relationships, and their integration enables a more comprehensive representation of potential intercellular crosstalk. This complementarity is further supported by ablation analysis (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Figures S1 and S2).

This cell-cell crosstalk design also distinguishes CrosstalkIn from traditional Reference-based and Marker-based deconvolution approaches. Reference-based methods, including recent single-cell reference-based approaches such as BayesPrism and RCTD, can provide high-resolution cell-type estimates when well-matched single-cell references are available. However, their performance may depend on reference quality, platform compatibility, batch effects, annotation accuracy, and the coverage of tumor-specific cell states. Marker-based approaches rely heavily on the choice of marker genes, whose limited specificity and context dependency often hinder accurate separation of transcriptional signals across cell types. Moreover, both strategies are sensitive to bulk transcriptomic data quality and vulnerable to technical noise and sample heterogeneity, while treating cell types as isolated entities and ignoring the regulatory impact of TME crosstalk. Instead, we introduce prior biological knowledge (PPI and GO) to construct patient-specific cell-cell crosstalk networks, which are incorporated into the deconvolution procedure through a modified random walk with restart algorithm. In this way, InScores are determined not only by gene expression levels, but also by the intrinsic interactions among cell types, thereby reducing excessive dependence on data quality alone and enabling a more biologically relevant characterization of TME cellular composition at both molecular and functional levels.

CrosstalkIn achieves superior performance on the SDY311 and SDY420 cohorts, consistently outperforming existing methods. In contrast, CITMIC yields lower correlation values and even negative coefficients for some cell types. In lower-grade glioma (LGG) survival analysis, CrosstalkIn identified many significant survival-associated cell types, with a number of these cell types unrecognized or only weakly associated with survival in CITMIC. Our risk score yields higher survival prediction AUCs than CITMIC and stratify patients into high- and low-risk groups with significant survival divergence. Besides, our risk score demonstrates strong generalizability across multiple adenocarcinomas, with particularly high predictive accuracy for advanced-stage patients. In BRCA, it correlates closely with key clinical, molecular, and immune features, reflecting its comprehensive capture of TME characteristics. In melanoma immunotherapy response prediction, CrosstalkIn achieves the highest predictive accuracy by identifying multiple treatment-responsive cell types. In contrast, CITMIC detects only a few negatively correlated cell types, resulting in poor discrimination between responders and non-responders. These results suggest that integrating GO-based functional similarity with PPI-based molecular association information provides a more informative representation of cell-type relationships than using a single network source alone. The β sensitivity analysis further provides insight into how GO-based functional similarity and PPI-derived molecular association contribute to CrosstalkIn. In this framework, β controls the balance between the two network sources. A balanced intermediate β value indicates that cell infiltration inference benefits from both shared biological functions and molecular network relationships among marker genes, rather than relying on either source alone. More shifted β values may suggest that one type of prior information contributes more strongly in a specific cohort, potentially reflecting differences in marker-gene relationships, cell-type composition, or the discriminative power of functional versus molecular network signals.

In conclusion, we present CrosstalkIn, a crosstalk-aware deconvolution method that captures both functional similarities and molecular association information within the TME. Through comprehensive benchmarking across diverse cohorts, we demonstrate its superior performance in cell infiltration estimation, survival analysis, and immunotherapy response prediction. Despite its promising performance, CrosstalkIn has several limitations. First, CrosstalkIn relies on predefined cell-type marker genes, and therefore marker quality and specificity may affect network construction and InScore estimation. This limitation may be more evident for rare, poorly characterized, context-dependent, or highly similar cell types, whose signals are weak or overlapping in bulk transcriptomic data. Second, although prior biological knowledge improves interpretability, CrosstalkIn still relies on bulk expression profiles and may therefore be affected by platform differences, batch effects, tumor purity, normalization strategies, and sample heterogeneity. Third, patient-specific network construction and random-walk-based calculation may increase computational cost and memory usage for large cohorts or dense molecular networks. Another limitation of this study is the lack of prospective wet-lab validation using independent patient samples. Although we used flow-cytometry-validated cohorts and multiple cancer datasets to evaluate CrosstalkIn, these analyses cannot fully replace direct experimental validation in tumor tissues. Future studies should further validate CrosstalkIn using independent tumor cohorts with matched bulk transcriptomic profiles and orthogonal measurements, such as flow cytometry, spatial transcriptomics, or single-cell-derived annotations. We will also explore additional biological priors, such as ligand–receptor interactions and signaling pathways, to improve the biological resolution of CrosstalkIn.

Materials and methods

Data description

To construct patient-specific GO-based functional similarity networks, we retrieved Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO-BP) terms from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) [50]. GO terms containing fewer than 5 genes were excluded, resulting in a curated set of 10,454 biological processes. GO-based functional relationships enhance the resolution and interpretability of TME deconvolution.

To construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network capturing structural relationships among genes, we retrieved interaction data from the STRING database, using both the protein links file and the protein annotation file [51]. Protein identifiers were mapped to gene symbols, and interactions were filtered based on a confidence score threshold, which was adjusted between 400 and 700 depending on the dataset characteristics. After filtering, we obtained a high-confidence PPI network consisting of genes and pairwise interactions. PPI-based structural relationships further improve the biological specificity and robustness of TME deconvolution.

We obtained gene expression and matched flow cytometry data from ImmPort [52], comprising two cohorts: SDY311 (73 samples) and SDY420 (104 samples). We obtained gene expression and clinical data (survival time and event status) from the Genomic Data Commons [53], comprising five cancer cohorts: SKCM (skin cutaneous melanoma, 417 samples), BRCA (breast cancer, 1069 samples), STAD (stomach adenocarcinoma, 347 samples), LUAD (lung adenocarcinoma, 496 samples), and READ (rectum adenocarcinoma, 153 samples). We obtained gene expression and immunotherapy response data from the TIGER database [54], comprising three cohorts: Melanoma-phs000452 (153 samples), RCC-Braun_2020 (311 samples), and STAD-PRJEB25780 (78 samples), which were used to evaluate model performance in immunotherapy-associated contexts.

We also model cell functional similarity networks. Specifically, the edge weight between cell types i and j was defined as: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{i,j}^{GO} = \mathop \sum \limits_{K = 1}^{{N_G}} \left( {{J_{k,i}} \times {S_{k,i}} + {J_{k,j}} \times {S_{k,j}}} \right)$$\end{document}

where JK,i, JK,j represents the Jaccard coefficient between cell types (i,j) and GO (K) gene sets, and S is the weighted expression of overlapping genes, calculated as: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S_{G,C}} = \mathop \sum \limits_{g \in G\mathop \cap \nolimits^ C} \left( {{{{w_g}} \over {\mathop \sum \nolimits_{g^{\prime} \in G\mathop \cap \nolimits^ C} {w_{g^{\prime}}}}} \times {E_g}} \right)$$\end{document}

where wg is the PageRank-derived importance of gene g in the PPI. Biologically, the PageRank-derived weight wg reflects the topological importance of gene g in the PPI-derived molecular association network. Genes with higher PageRank values tend to occupy more central positions in the molecular network and may have greater potential to participate in coordinated biological processes or transmit molecular signals. Eg represents the sample-specific transcriptional activity of gene g. Therefore, this weighting strategy emphasizes transcriptionally active and topologically influential marker genes, enabling S to capture network-aware functional activity beyond simple gene overlap or expression magnitude.

Integrated cell–cell crosstalk network

To ensure comparability between the functional similarity network \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{i,j}^{PPI}$$\end{document}and the dynamic interaction network \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{i,j}^{GO}$$\end{document}on the same scale, we normalize their edge weights. Subsequently, we construct a fused network by integrating two networks for each sample: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C_{i,j}} = \beta \times D_{i,j}^{PPI} + \left( {1 - \beta } \right) \times D_{i,j}^{GO}$$\end{document}

Where β ∈ [0,1] is a tunable parameter that controls the relative contribution of the GO-based network (β) and the PPI-based network (1-β) and to the cell–cell crosstalk network. In the flow-cytometry benchmark cohorts, β is searched from 0 to 1 with a step size of 0.1 and selected by maximizing the mean Spearman correlation between predicted InScores and FACS-derived cell fractions across evaluated cell types. For downstream cancer analyses, the benchmark-supported intermediate β setting (0.4–0.6) is used to avoid outcome-driven parameter tuning, unless otherwise specified.

InScore calculation

In the patient-specific cell–cell crosstalk network, edge weights represent interaction strength between cell types. Assuming that a cell’s activity is modulated by the functional influence of its neighbors, we quantify per-patient infiltration by applying a modified random walk with restart (RWR) to the weighted crosstalk network [56, 57]. The modified RWR algorithm is implemented with a restart probability (r) of 0.15 to balance network information propagation and initial signal retention, avoiding excessive signal dilution and insufficient capture of cell–cell interactions. The algorithm converges to the stationary distribution of node visit probabilities under uniform initialization and weighted transitions. This limiting probability reflecting the node’s centrality in the network is taken as the cell’s InScore. InScore should be interpreted as a network-informed infiltration score that reflects abundance-related signals modulated by cell–cell crosstalk, rather than an absolute cell fraction. Therefore, comparisons with FACS-derived fractions assess concordance with measured abundance trends, not numerical equivalence to cell proportions. In addition, to mitigate the influence of extreme values and ensure comparability across samples, we apply a logarithmic transformation (log₁₀(InScore)) and min–max normalization to normalize scores to the [0,1] range: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm{InScore}} = {{{\rm{InScore}} - {\rm{Min}}\left( {{\rm{InScore}}} \right)} \over {Max\left( {{\rm{InScore}}} \right) - {\rm{Min}}\left( {{\rm{InScore}}} \right)}}$$\end{document}
