Collective responsibility and ecological thinking in eating disorders: moving beyond individualistic models of illness and recovery
Authors: James Downs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eating disorder treatment and recovery are often framed as matters of individual responsibility, with change positioned as dependent on the person’s motivation, insight, and willingness to recover. While agency remains important, this paper argues that such framings are too narrow and can obscure the wider conditions that shape both illness and the possibility of recovery. Drawing on ecological systems theory, ecosocial approaches to health, evolutionary psychiatry, and lived experience, this paper develops an account of eating disorders as phenomena that arise within interacting systems of biological vulnerability, interpersonal relationships, sociocultural pressures, institutional arrangements, and political economy. It argues that individualistic models of illness and recovery may contribute to shame, stigma, epistemic injustice, and what is described here as hyper-responsibilisation, particularly where care is fragmented, delayed, or unequally distributed. In contrast, an ecological perspective allows eating disorders to be understood as embodied and socially patterned forms of suffering that cannot be adequately addressed through individual behaviour change alone. The paper concludes that if eating disorders are ecological in their production and maintenance, then responsibility for responding to them must also be ecological. Recommendations are made to support more collective, relational, and structurally informed approaches to prevention, treatment, and recovery.

Introduction

Eating disorder treatment is often shaped by the assumption that recovery depends, above all, on the individual’s readiness to change. The clinical literature’s longstanding interest in motivation, readiness, and engagement reflects a legitimate concern with ambivalence and treatment participation, and has generated tools and interventions that may be useful in some contexts [1–5]. Even so, this emphasis also reveals something broader about how recovery is imagined. Across treatment, research, and public discourse, recovery is often represented as something that must originate within the person who is unwell, driven by sufficient insight, determination, and self-discipline. The wider conditions in which eating disorders develop, and in which recovery becomes more or less possible, may risk appearing secondary, rather than constitutive [6, 7].

Over the course of my own twenty-year experience of living with an eating disorder, I have repeatedly encountered this logic in explicit forms. I have often been told that “recovery has to come from you” and that I “have to want to get better.” These statements were offered as encouragement, yet they also communicated a deeper assumption: that the central work of recovery lay with me, and that care depended on the expectation that I would demonstrate sufficient motivation and capacity to enact change within the context of my life. Of course, these factors mattered. Indeed, fluctuations in motivation are a natural part of recovery and should be compassionately handled in the therapeutic process, and many people with lived experience describe moments of personal commitment that have been important in changing the trajectory of their eating disorder. However, when recovery was presented to me chiefly as a matter of will, the relational, institutional, and material conditions that shaped both my illness and the possibility of recovery were obscured from view.

This paper proceeds from the perspective that lived experiences such as my own are not merely illustrative but epistemically important. The concept of epistemic injustice is especially relevant in the field of eating disorders, where people may be expected to demonstrate insight into their illness while simultaneously being disbelieved, pathologised, or treated as unreliable interpreters of their own experience [8–11]. This contradiction can take the form of testimonial injustice, when a person’s words are granted diminished credibility because they are mentally ill, [12] and hermeneutic injustice, when shared interpretive resources are inadequate for making sense of subjective experiences or communicating them intelligibly [13, 14]. Mladenov (2023) extends this analysis by linking testimonial and interpretive exclusion to broader structural inequalities, which has particular salience in the context of eating disorders, where illness is shaped and recognised within social worlds structured by class, race, gender, sexuality, neurodiversity, poverty, disability, body size, and unequal provision of care [15]. These factors (amongst others) affect who is able to access treatment, whose distress is identified early, whose illness is judged serious or legitimate, and whose account of illness or recovery is treated as credible.

Recent scholarship has increasingly recognised the value of lived experience within eating disorder research and care, but it has also highlighted the limitations of tokenistic involvement, where people are included symbolically but denied meaningful epistemic influence [9, 16–22]. In this paper, lived experience is not positioned as an illustrative supplement, rather, personal reflection is part of the analytic work itself, allowing me to speak on my own terms about the social, institutional, and interpretive conditions through which illness and recovery are lived and understood.

The aim of this paper is to develop a conceptual and practical account of eating disorders within their broader ecological contexts, drawing on theory, research, and lived experience. Although eating disorders are widely understood to arise through complex interactions among biological, psychological, social, and environmental factors, this recognition is not always reflected in accounts of treatment and recovery, where responsibility may remain disproportionately located within the individual. The paper therefore examines how biological vulnerability, interpersonal experience, sociocultural pressures, and institutional responses interact in shaping both illness and the conditions under which recovery becomes possible. It then considers how this ecological account might support a more collective and equitable distribution of responsibility, making recommendations for research, clinical care, and policy.

Ecosocial, biological, and embodied accounts of eating disorders

Rather than locating causes solely within the person, ecological models attend to causal interactions across biological, psychological, interpersonal, organisational, community, and policy levels [23–25]. Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory was foundational in conceptualising human development as unfolding within nested systems, from immediate relational environments to wider cultural, economic, and political structures [26]. From this perspective, eating disorders are best understood not as self-contained intrapsychic pathologies, but as phenomena lived in bodies, mediated through relationships, and shaped by institutions, social norms, and material conditions. Evidence from a recent rapid review of risk factors for eating disorders supports this broader view, concluding that eating disorders arise through interacting biopsychosocial risks rather than singular causes and reinforcing the need for multilevel explanatory models [27].

At the interpersonal level, eating disorders develop and persist within emotional and relational worlds. Cognitive-interpersonal models of anorexia nervosa, for example, have drawn attention to the ways in which interpersonal processes, emotional regulation, and patterns of accommodation may become implicated in the maintenance of illness, while family dynamics may also affect the course and experience of eating disorders more broadly [28–30]. However, qualitative research led by people with lived experience and caregivers suggests that many difficulties within caregiving relationships arise in response to escalating illness, unmet treatment needs, and inadequate support for caregivers themselves [31]. Viewing relational distress in isolation from the conditions in which it develops may therefore lead to the logical error of reverse causation: treating family difficulties observed after the onset or escalation of illness as evidence that they preceded and caused it, when they may have been produced or intensified by illness and inadequate care.

Sociocultural pressures include the internalisation of appearance ideals, habitual social comparison, and body surveillance, all of which are associated with eating pathology [32–35]. Within cultures organised around scrutiny, self-discipline, and bodily optimisation, eating disorders may become intelligible and, in some settings, even reinforced. Such dynamics are particularly visible, though not exclusive to, Western diet culture, [9] and can help explain why treatment can feel existentially fraught: recovery may require not simply changing behaviour, but relinquishing ways of being that may have become entangled with safety, identity, predictability, or social survival [36–39].

Institutional environments are equally important. Access to recognition and treatment is not evenly distributed, and recent work suggests that service design and responsiveness shape illness trajectories in significant ways. Research on the FREED model (First Episode Rapid Early Intervention for Eating Disorders) suggests that service organisation can improve clinical outcomes as well as administrative performance [40, 41]. How quickly someone is seen, how clearly and consistently they are able to enter treatment, and how far services deliver the model as intended all affect what kinds of care become possible, particularly early in the course of illness [40–42]. For example, people in rural and remote areas who would benefit from more intensive outpatient treatments (especially following discharge from inpatient treatment) may not be able to access them because of the costs and distance of travel, thus increasing the likelihood of insufficient follow-up and relapse.

Viljoen et al. (2024) document substantial shortfalls in adult community eating disorder services in England and Scotland before COVID-19, including waiting lists, rationed access, and limited capacity to provide continuity of care [43]. Inequitable access to treatment may be particularly pronounced for certain groups, such as those with low incomes and racial-ethnic minorities, leading to increased risk of insufficient treatment and poor outcomes [44, 45]. This suggests that illness and recovery need to be understood as processes mediated by systems of referral, eligibility, affordability, and recognition, rather than by individual motivation alone. The contexts of care are therefore not neutral backdrops to treatment, but part of the conditions under which someone can be recognised, believed, and supported in ways that make adverse outcomes preventable and recovery more viable [46].

An ecological account should not, however, be mistaken for a purely sociological one. One of its strengths is that it allows biological vulnerability to be taken seriously without treating biology as destiny. Family and twin studies indicate substantial heritability for eating disorders, even if heritability explains neither mechanism nor outcome in isolation [47, 48]. Neurobiological findings likewise complicate any simple model of choice or willpower, with differences in cognitive control and reward processing seen across eating disorder diagnoses [49, 50]. These findings do not replace social explanation, but extend it, suggesting that eating disorders involve patterned alterations in salience, reinforcement, and regulation that are lived through bodies as well as meanings and relationships.

Evolutionary psychiatry adds a further dimension by asking why human beings may be vulnerable to these forms of dysregulation at all. As Nesse (2023) argues, evolutionary psychiatry broadens explanation beyond proximate mechanisms to include the vulnerabilities built into human regulatory systems [51]. Humans evolved under conditions of intermittent food availability, high physical activity, and recurrent uncertainty. Contemporary environments, by contrast, are shaped by the conditions, actions, and omissions of commercial actors, with the World Health Organisation now treating commercial determinants as a key influence on health [52]. For example, ultra-processed foods are widely formulated, marketed, and distributed in ways that increase convenience, palatability, and repeated consumption, while broader food marketing environments intensify cue exposure and normalise frequent eating of products high in fat, sugar, and salt. Whilst it would be too simple to claim that eating disorders are merely products of this evolutionary mismatch, it is not difficult to see how the contemporary food environment may amplify reward-driven eating, dysregulation, and distress, especially when paired with simultaneous pressures towards dietary restraint, thinness, and bodily control.

Krieger’s ecosocial theory helps bring these strands together. Its key concept of embodiment directs attention to the ways people literally and biologically incorporate the worlds in which they live [53, 54]. From this perspective, eating disorders can be understood not simply as psychological disorders that give rise to behaviours enacted upon the body, but as socially patterned forms of embodied suffering, lived through physiological, metabolic, sensory, and affective processes that are shaped by wider environments. As I have argued elsewhere, one of the recurring failures in eating disorder care is the fragmentation and uneven appreciation of these dimensions [55–58].

Lived experience reflection

Over time, I have come to see my experiences of living with severe anorexia and bulimia for more than two decades as inseparable from the interactions between my physiology, sensory experience, neurodivergence, disability, and the material organisation of my everyday life. Understanding eating disorders in this way has helped me understand the forms my symptoms have taken, the environments in which they’ve intensified, and what recovery practically requires of me.

A specific example of this is how periods of extreme binge eating were bound up with the availability and sensory intensity of ultra-processed foods, and a sense that my symptoms were being driven by forces far less amenable to voluntary control than standard behavioural framings often allow. This pattern was shaped by my neurodivergence: sensation-seeking, autistic overwhelm, preference for sameness, repetition, and the use of food to modulate distress all influenced how, when, and why I would binge eat.

Physical comorbidity further complicated the picture. I live with Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (a heritable connective tissue disorder), which affects my body in a variety of ways, including allowing for an extreme degree of gastric distension during binge episodes, with consequences for both their scale and aftermath. Similarly, gastroparesis (delayed stomach emptying) - which predated the onset of my eating disorder, but was later exacerbated by it - has created substantial difficulties in tolerating food and obtaining nutrition. The late diagnosis of these conditions fundamentally improved my understanding of what recovery would require for me. This included recognition that certain foods, bodily states, and environments are predictably high-risk for me, and that many of my symptoms are responses to embodied processes rather than expressions of psychological phenomena typically characterised as driving eating disorders, such as the pursuit of purity, weight loss, body image ideals, or control.

Reducing or eliminating ultra-processed foods made a material difference to my symptoms, although I was forcefully discouraged from doing so by clinicians who characterised my dietary exclusion as pathological. Similarly, at a crucial point in the trajectory of my illness, the only way I was able to begin nutritional rehabilitation and eventually return to eating solid food was through adopting an entirely liquid diet. This step was central to my recovery from refractory and life-threatening profound hypokalaemia (low potassium). Yet I was also discouraged from this approach. In both cases, what I understood as pragmatic and pro-recovery responses to the realities of my own body were reinterpreted through pre-existing assumptions about my psychopathology. I felt as though my account of what was happening in my body, and of what was helping, was not treated as credible or trustworthy, which - over time - undermined my trust in myself. It also prolonged my illness.

Another pivotal shift in my recovery came when I tried to remove myself from environments of constant food availability, even though doing so required relatively extreme measures in the short term. This included staying in remote places with minimal access to transportation or shops. Whilst this was not a sustainable solution in itself, the improvements I experienced in such environments made plain how profoundly my symptoms were shaped by context. Some professionals questioned whether this action was evidence of me avoiding responsibility. From my perspective, it was taking responsibility. In fact, giving up on trying to become more self-sustaining within unhelpful environments has been one of the most important factors in my eventual progress towards a sustained recovery.

Whilst I do not present these experiences as models of recovery that will work for others, they illustrate the extent to which recovery may depend on recognising the specific embodied and environmental conditions through which symptoms intensify, and the equally specific conditions under which illness may become more interruptible and recovery more possible.

Hyper-responsibilisation, stigma, and stratified recognition

The emphasis on personal responsibility in eating disorder recovery can be situated within wider discourses of health. Writing in 1980, Crawford’s concept of “healthism” described a cultural shift in which health becomes not only desirable but moralised, with responsibility increasingly located in personal conduct rather than social conditions [59]. Subsequent scholarship has described how prevailing Western health cultures have placed increasing emphasis on individual responsibility for managing risk, monitoring the body, and pursuing self-improvement [60, 61]. Within these cultures, recovery may be read as evidence of discipline and virtue, while ongoing illness can more readily become legible as personal failure.

The moralisation of health and illness has particular salience in relation to eating disorders, as many symptoms initially resemble socially sanctioned forms of restraint, self-control, and bodily discipline [9, 62]. Restriction, intensive exercise, and visible effort may be admired within value systems organised around productivity and optimisation, despite their potential to become clinically problematic as symptoms. This may be particularly the case for some groups such as males who may experience gendered expectations of their physicality [62–64]. A paradox therefore arises whereby the same neoliberal culture that rewards self-denial may later judge it in pathological form. The line between behaviours being praised as virtues or recognised as disordered is often drawn late, unevenly, and selectively.

Moralistic views of health, illness, and recovery extend from prevailing culture to the clinical domain. Constructs such as motivation to change and readiness for recovery may be useful in some circumstances, [1, 4] but when they dominate how eating disorders are approached, they can potentially narrow the focus of attention onto the individual patient and away from appreciating the relational, institutional, and structural conditions in which they are situated [7, 65]. I have previously described this phenomenon as “hyper-responsibilisation”: a process in which responsibility for recovery becomes concentrated at the level of the person who is already compromised by illness, while the wider conditions shaping illness and care are rendered less visible [66, 67]. In practice, this may mean that the patient is expected to simultaneously account for symptoms, prove suffering, display insight, manage risk, engage consistently with treatment, and sustain hope, even in contexts where services are delayed, fragmented, or difficult to access.

These dynamics also implicate stigma [9]. Link and Phelan conceptualise stigma not as isolated prejudice but as a process involving labelling, stereotyping, separation, status loss, and discrimination within relations of power [68]. Hatzenbuehler and Link extend this by drawing attention to structural stigma, locating stigma not only in attitudes but in institutional policies, cultural norms, and social arrangements [69]. In the context of eating disorders, there are specific exclusionary stereotypes, disempowering narratives, and forms of discrimination which have changed over time but remain entrenched and uneven across diagnoses and populations [9, 62, 70–72].

Weight stigma provides one clear example which is well documented across social life, including within healthcare cultures [73, 74]. Such is the extent of weight stigma that eating disorder treatment provision itself may sometimes reproduce rather than challenge it, [75, 76] for example by warning people with a history of being underweight against recovering into larger bodies [77]. Quantitative research on social stigma has found that whilst eating disorders are highly stigmatised relative to depression, binge-eating disorder is judged especially negatively [78]. These attitudes may then play a role in the experiences of people in larger bodies presenting to healthcare with severe restriction, purging, bingeing, or compulsive exercise and have their illness minimised, sometimes being redirected away from eating disorder treatment and towards weight loss services [79–81].

Recognition is stratified in other ways too. Eating disorders have long been culturally coded as illnesses of thin, white, affluent girls and women, despite evidence that they occur across socioeconomic groups and affect diverse racial groups and those with minoritised gender and sexual identities [62, 82–85]. Mittertreiner et al. (2024) highlight the continuing effects of racism, classism, gender stereotypes, and fatphobia on equitable assessment and treatment [86]. These differential stigmas, which often intersect, [83] form part of the ecology in which illness is recognised, responded to, and lived. Not everyone is granted the same conditions under which engagement with treatment and recovery can become imaginable. Once this is acknowledged, recovery may be seen less as a straightforward test of individual motivation and more as a process shaped by whether recognition, credibility, and care are distributed justly.

Lived experience reflection

My ongoing struggles with eating disorders have frequently been interpreted through the language of insufficiency: not trying hard enough, not wanting recovery enough, not showing enough insight or consistency. Yet for much of this time my experiences were also shaped by financial insecurity, traumatic events, social isolation, and inconsistent or absent care. Under such conditions, personal responsibility was often not something I was materially capable of stepping into. The responsibility that I was taking was often not directed towards recovery - it was exhausted by managing high-risk medical symptoms and intense psychological suffering without support.

During such times, the notion of personal responsibility did not feel empowering to me. It felt like a moral burden to be able to singularly overcome my difficulties. This relied on a form of decontextualisation that may have involved a fundamental attribution error, whereby others interpreted my difficulties primarily in terms of my personal traits and motivation, while giving insufficient weight to the circumstances shaping them. My distress may well have been understandable within the context of my unique physiology and lifeworld, but my ambivalence was frequently misread as unwillingness, and the fact that I found treatment difficult was often seen as evidence of me being difficult, and so on. These misreadings were likely exacerbated by my identity and symptoms not aligning with dominant assumptions about who gets eating disorders and how they present.

One specific example is how I experienced recurrent episodes following binge eating and vomiting where I felt clammy, dizzy, and disorientated, and at times experienced palpitations, difficulty speaking, difficulty standing, and near loss of consciousness. I reported these symptoms repeatedly in different healthcare settings for more than a decade. I was usually told that they were a result of low potassium, even when my potassium remained normal and my symptoms persisted. Only much later, by chance, was blood glucose suggested to me by a friend as a possible factor. I ordered a testing device which showed clinically severe hypoglycaemia during symptoms [55].

These episodes were not captured on routine blood tests as I was unable to attend phlebotomy when symptomatic. The same episodes also affected my ability to participate in treatment. I was often experiencing what I now know were hypoglycaemic episodes during therapy sessions, which made it harder to think clearly, regulate emotion, speak coherently, remain physically present, and engage in the reflective work that therapy demands.

This overlooked medical detail provides an example of how clinically important information may be assimilated into pre-existing frameworks and assumptions rather than prompting a reconsideration of the whole picture. It also demonstrates a measurement gap, whereby self-report was ineffective and significant symptoms were unable to be recorded in the context in which they happened (i.e. if I was well enough to physically get to blood tests, I was not experiencing severe hypoglycaemia). Coming to understand the role of severe blood sugar dysregulation helped me make better sense of my symptoms, respond to them more effectively, and reduce some of the fear and confusion that had surrounded them.

Blood glucose monitoring when symptomatic was also key to ending over a decade of experiencing these symptoms. However, I was strongly discouraged from this by some healthcare professionals, who suggested it would become “another number to control”. This again shows the extent to which my own accounts of my symptoms (and of what was helping me) were treated with suspicion. More broadly, this example shows how hyper-responsibilisation and decontextualisation may obscure the physiological realities of illness, making it harder to recognise forms of self-understanding that may ultimately support recovery.

Redistributing responsibility: towards collective care

If eating disorders are ecological in their production and maintenance, then responses to them must be ecological too - not dismissing agency, but redistributing responsibility more honestly. This requires a renewed focus on how illness, treatment, and recovery from eating disorders are shaped by relationships, institutions, material conditions, bodily processes, and political priorities, with implications for treatment, research, and policy:

Clinical implications

At the level of treatment content, there is a need for assessment and intervention that can address medical, nutritional, psychological, sensory, and social dimensions together rather than treating them as separate or hierarchically ordered domains [55–58]. This may be particularly relevant where eating disorders coexist with neurodivergence, chronic illness, gastrointestinal symptoms, trauma, or recurrent physiological instability [87].

Evidence for more integrated approaches is beginning to emerge. In anorexia nervosa requiring inpatient treatment, Integrated Enhanced Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Eating Disorders (I-CBTE) has been associated with better outcomes than treatment as usual or crisis-management admissions, particularly where inpatient, day patient, and outpatient treatment are coordinated as part of a coherent pathway rather than delivered as disconnected episodes of care [88]. Models such as Multi-diagnostic Eating Disorder Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (MED-DBT) have also been developed in an effort to address eating disorder symptoms alongside severe emotion dysregulation and co-occurring diagnoses, rather than assuming that standard protocols will be sufficient for all patients [89, 90]. In relation to neurodivergence, the Pathway for Eating disorders and Autism developed from Clinical Experience (PEACE) provides a concrete example of service-level adaptation, incorporating changes to sensory environments, communication, menu planning, formulation, and staff training to improve patient engagement, clinician confidence, and treatment outcomes [91–93].

The organisation of treatment is also clinically consequential. Shortfalls in adult specialist adult services, including delays, rationing, and poor continuity, are associated with deterioration while waiting, interrupted treatment, repeated relapse, and avoidable harm, including preventable deaths from eating disorders [4]. Evaluation of the First Episode Rapid Early Intervention for Eating Disorders (FREED) model demonstrates that earlier access, clearer routes into care, and more consistent delivery are associated with better outcomes, particularly early in illness [40, 41]. Similarly, the I-CBTE approach suggests that patients with severe anorexia benefit when inpatient, day patient, and outpatient treatment programmes are coordinated as part of a coherent pathway rather than delivered as disconnected episodes of care [88].

Together, these findings suggest that clinical effectiveness depends not only on the treatment model itself, but on whether care is accessible, continuous, and organised in ways that allow different aspects of a person’s difficulties to be addressed in relation to one another.

Research implications

As seen in my own experiences, reliance on retrospective self-reports and static symptom measures may miss clinically important fluctuations. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) may therefore be useful in allowing affective states, contextual stressors, and specific eating disorder symptoms to be recorded as they unfold in daily life. For example, EMA has been used to study emotional food cravings across diagnostic groups and family mealtime dynamics in adolescents with anorexia nervosa - processes that are often difficult to capture retrospectively [94–96]. Similarly, where clinically appropriate and ethically justified, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) may help illuminate forms of physiological instability that are otherwise missed by occasional testing, with initial studies suggesting that CGM can identify glycaemic variability and hypoglycaemia across eating disorder presentations and may also have potential in characterising naturalistic binge eating and vomiting in binge-spectrum disorders [97, 98].

A further implication concerns the composition of the evidence base itself. Eating disorder research continues to be shaped by narrow assumptions about who gets eating disorders, with studies disproportionately focused on thin, white, affluent girls and women. In Halbeisen et al.‘s (2022) analysis of more than 300 studies summarised in German treatment guidelines, women comprised around 90% of binge-eating disorder samples, 95% of anorexia nervosa samples, and more than 99% of bulimia nervosa samples [85]. Evidence bases built on narrow samples risk mischaracterising symptom presentation, overlooking clinically relevant forms of complexity, and producing interventions that fit some groups better than others. Greater attention is therefore needed to forms of complexity that remain marginal in eating disorder research, including neurodivergence, chronic illness, trauma, hypermobility, and intersecting forms of social and economic disadvantage [9, 84–86].

Research designs that privilege what is easily measurable, verbally reportable, and already legible within established clinical frameworks may risk reproducing some of the same forms of interpretive narrowness and epistemic injustice discussed earlier in this paper [8–12]. Co-production and other forms of lived experience involvement may therefore widen the interpretive resources available within eating disorder research and reduce reliance on pre-existing clinical assumptions as a singularly authoritative source of knowledge [9]. A growing body of literature demonstrates how including people with lived experience as active and equal contributors in eating disorder research has the potential to widen the questions asked and increase the relevance and impact of findings [16–20]. However, this requires lived experience involvement to be well-resourced, ethically enacted, and epistemically meaningful rather than merely symbolic [11].

A more epistemically plural research agenda also requires a greater diversity of methodological approaches. Qualitative, participatory, and creative methods can be used throughout the research cycle to better integrate the perspectives and priorities of people with lived experience that may otherwise be overlooked [11]. These experiences may include diverse interpretations of embodied states, sensory distress, shame, ambivalence, exclusion, comorbidity, trauma, and social disadvantage. One example of such a method is storytelling, [99] which has been used in eating disorder recovery research to enable participants to represent their personal experiences without relying on conventional clinical narratives, thus challenging and broadening the prevailing discourses of recovery rather than merely reproducing them [100, 101].

Policy and public health implications: prevention and the wider environment

A more ecological account of eating disorders shifts attention upstream, towards primordial prevention (i.e. action on the social and environmental conditions that generate risk of illness in the first place) [31]. In relation to eating disorders, this includes policy responses that address factors such as weight stigma, narrow body ideals, normalised dieting, discrimination and marginalisation, poverty, food insecurity, and the wider food environment, rather than leaving these drivers to individual resilience or education alone. Primary prevention (i.e. the prevention of illness onset) is also needed, with meta-analytic evidence suggesting that interventions are more likely to reduce later eating disorder onset when they are interactive, targeted, and theory-driven rather than limited to generic psychoeducation [102]. Interventions such as the Body Project have been associated with reductions in thin-ideal internalisation, body dissatisfaction, eating disorder symptoms, and subsequent eating disorder onset [103–105]. Building on this requires developing and evaluating prevention measures that extend beyond the relatively narrow groups on which current evidence largely rests [85, 106, 107].

Commercial determinants of health also require more direct attention within eating disorder policy. Recent adolescent research has linked the use of platforms such as Instagram and TikTok with higher eating disorder pathology, greater appearance pressure, and stronger endorsement of appearance ideals, particularly among girls [108]. Commercial food environments warrant similar scrutiny. Ayton and Ibrahim (2020) have argued that the changing Western diet had been relatively neglected in eating disorder research, [109] and a subsequent study found that binge eating episodes in a clinical sample comprised of entirely ultra-processed foods [110]. More recent epidemiological evidence has also linked ultra-processed food consumption with eating disorder symptoms, food addiction, and body image concerns [111]. These findings do not establish a simple causal pathway, but they do indicate that prevention cannot be confined to messages about self-control or healthy choices while leaving the surrounding media and food environments unchanged.

As the discussion of clinical care has already suggested, action is needed at the level of service planning and commissioning, where funding and organisational decisions shape continuity, safety, and access to specialist eating disorder care [7, 43, 46]. The longstanding absence of strategic planning has contributed to fragmentation, inconsistency, and uneven provision across services, meaning urgent action is needed to ensure sufficient funding is allocated to public eating disorder services to correct longstanding inequities in access to high quality care. In the UK context, calls have therefore been made for a comprehensive and co-produced eating disorder strategy with coordinated service guidance, improved data, and greater consistency of provision across settings [112].

Conclusion

This paper has argued that eating disorders cannot be adequately understood and appropriately resourced through frameworks that place responsibility for illness and recovery primarily within the individual. While agency matters, individualised models obscure the wider ecological conditions in which eating disorders emerge, persist, and are responded to. They also risk intensifying shame, misrecognition, and blame by treating recovery as a matter of sufficient insight, motivation, or will.

Bringing ecological systems theory, ecosocial thinking, evolutionary perspectives, and lived experience into dialogue allows a broader account to emerge. Eating disorders appear not as isolated disorders of individual psychology, but as forms of intense or extreme suffering shaped by the interaction of biological vulnerability, relational life, institutional response, and structural inequality. From that perspective, recovery is more than a personal task - it is a relational and social process, shaped by what kinds of care, recognition, material support, and environments are made available.

The central implication of this paper is straightforward. If eating disorders are ecological, then responsibility for responding to them must also be ecological. This means moving beyond narrow models of individual behaviour change towards forms of care, policy, and prevention that take seriously the social conditions of recovery. It also means recognising lived experience not as supplementary to scholarship and care, but as essential to understanding what recovery requires. To respond adequately to eating disorders, we need accounts of illness and recovery that are as complex, relational, and materially grounded as the lives in which these disorders unfold.
