Association of AI-assisted quantitative coronary plaque burden and CT-derived fractional flow reserve with major adverse cardiovascular events
Authors: Xinwei Zhang, Mengyuan Bao, Yongshun Wu, Haicheng Qi, Yan Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rationale and objectiveThis single-center retrospective study evaluated the associations of AI-quantified coronary plaque parameters and CT-derived fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR) with major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in patients with coronary artery disease, and derived optimal risk cutoff values for plaque burden.MethodsA total of 381 patients who underwent CCTA were consecutively enrolled. MACEs were defined as a composite of all-cause death, myocardial infarction (fatal and nonfatal), heart failure death, malignant arrhythmia, coronary revascularization, and rehospitalization for angina exacerbation. Maximum follow-up was 18 months. Risk cutoff values were derived from receiver operating characteristic analysis. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression, Kaplan–Meier analysis, and five predictive models (plaque model, CT-FFR model, combined model, LASSO-Cox, and Cox survival neural network) were constructed.ResultsAmong 381 patients, 67 (17.6%) developed MACEs. All six total plaque parameters showed significant associations with MACEs. In multivariate Cox regression, total noncalcified percent atheroma volume (NCPAV) > 4.68% emerged as the strongest predictor (HR 5.073, 95% CI 2.930–8.786, P < 0.001). Analyzed continuously, each 1-SD increase in total-NCPAV conferred an HR of 1.82 (95% CI 1.54–2.14, P < 0.001). The combined model C-index was 0.750 (95% CI 0.696–0.804; optimism-corrected 0.708), comparable to the plaque model alone (0.744, 95% CI 0.686–0.801; corrected 0.705). The LASSO-Cox and Cox survival neural network models achieved C-indices of 0.747 (95% CI 0.674–0.816) and 0.730 (95% CI 0.628–0.833), respectively. In landmark sensitivity analyses excluding early events, the combined model C-index rose to 0.792, with the likelihood ratio test P value narrowing from 0.117 to 0.061, suggesting a trend toward incremental value for CT-FFR after accounting for potential incorporation bias.ConclusionsAI-quantified total noncalcified plaque burden was the strongest predictor of MACEs. The addition of CT-FFR to plaque parameters did not provide a clinically meaningful or statistically significant improvement in overall model performance, including discrimination, model fit, reclassification, or discrimination slope. Although landmark analyses suggested a possible trend toward incremental value after exclusion of early revascularization-driven events, this finding should be considered exploratory and requires further validation. Vessel-specific analyses identified RCA plaque burden as having the greatest prognostic weight among the target vessels; however, this exploratory finding also warrants confirmation in independent cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02534-6.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains a leading contributor to global morbidity and mortality, and its incidence continues to rise [1, 2]. For patients at intermediate and low risk, coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) has become the first-line imaging modality [3, 4]. Plaque burden—the ratio of total plaque volume to vessel volume—has been consistently associated with major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) across numerous studies [5–7]. Coronary atherosclerotic plaques comprise lipid, fibrous, and calcified components. Among these, low-attenuation plaques, characterized by necrotic lipid cores, may be complicated by intraplaque hemorrhage [8]. These unstable plaque constituents can trigger plaque rupture and subsequent MACEs [9]. Expansion of total plaque volume may also produce coronary lumen stenosis, which causes myocardial ischemia and, ultimately, MACEs [10]. Identifying patients at heightened MACE risk before events occur therefore carries direct clinical importance [11].

Currently, plaque burden quantification depends largely on postprocessing software, with some strategies requiring image transfer to dedicated workstations [12]. Manual delineation of the plaque-corresponding region of interest (ROI) constitutes semiautomatic segmentation—an approach that likewise demands dedicated postprocessing resources and specialized expertise. In the present study, we employed AI-powered software for fully automated coronary plaque segmentation [13]. This allowed clinicians and radiologists to promptly assess patient status after CCTA acquisition, bypassing the delays inherent to workstation-based workflows. The platform delivers one-stop, fully automated quantification of morphological features—both calcified and noncalcified plaque—alongside key hemodynamic parameters such as CT-FFR, thereby supporting more precise, individualized treatment. Plaque morphology and CT-FFR capture complementary dimensions of coronary pathophysiology: anatomic plaque burden and functional hemodynamic significance, respectively. Their integration may thus yield incremental prognostic value beyond either modality alone. While prior work has focused extensively on total plaque burden across the three major coronary branches [14, 15], the vessel-specific predictive value of plaque burden in the left anterior descending (LAD), left circumflex (LCX), and right coronary (RCA) arteries for MACEs remains poorly characterized, and the relative prognostic weight of each vessel has yet to be established.

The present study builds on these converging advances—in analytical tools, research perspectives, and methodology—to address the semiautomated constraints of conventional plaque burden measurement. Using AI-based fully automated segmentation, we achieved integrated, efficient quantification of both plaque morphology (calcified and noncalcified components) and CT-FFR functional data in a synchronous workflow. This fills a gap left by prior CAD studies, which concentrated mainly on aggregate whole-heart plaque burden. We hypothesized that vessel-specific plaque quantification would improve MACE risk discrimination relative to whole-heart measures, and we further examined whether CT-FFR, integrated with plaque morphology, might offer complementary prognostic information. To manage the high dimensionality of vessel-specific parameters and capture potential nonlinear predictor–outcome relationships, we additionally employed LASSO-Cox regression for variable selection and a Cox survival neural network model.

Materials and methods

Patient population

The present study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (approval number: 20210226-134), and informed consent from the participants was waived. A total of 381 inpatients who underwent CCTA and were diagnosed with CAD at our institution from January 2022 to May 2022 were consecutively enrolled. CAD was defined as coronary stenosis > 50% on CCTA, or mild stenosis accompanied by a strong clinical suspicion, based on comprehensive clinical judgment. After discharge, information regarding the occurrence of MACEs in these patients was obtained by reviewing outpatient and inpatient medical records, as well as conducting telephone follow-ups, with a maximum follow-up duration of 18 months. MACEs were defined as a composite of all-cause death, myocardial infarction (fatal and nonfatal), heart failure death, malignant arrhythmia, coronary revascularization (including percutaneous coronary intervention with stenting, balloon dilatation, and coronary artery bypass grafting), and rehospitalization for angina exacerbation. In the present study, all-cause death was incorporated into the definition of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), primarily because this endpoint is objective and clinically meaningful. In retrospective follow-up, the exact cause of death is often difficult to ascertain accurately, and restricting the endpoint to cardiovascular death alone may introduce misclassification bias. Therefore, including all-cause death facilitates a more conservative and reproducible assessment of adverse clinical prognosis. Hard endpoints were defined as myocardial infarction (fatal and nonfatal), and heart failure death. The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients diagnosed with CAD via comprehensive clinical evaluation; the availability of complete clinically relevant data; no history of known acute coronary events, stent implantation, or coronary artery bypass grafting prior to CCTA examination; clear CCTA images with adequate contrast medium filling; assessable quality on both subjective and objective evaluations; and the absence of significant artifacts. The exclusion criteria were as follows: prior history of confirmed acute myocardial infarction, severe cardiac insufficiency with New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class III–IV, or comorbidity with nonischemic myocardial diseases, such as dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy; severe arrhythmias, such as severe atrial fibrillation and severe atrioventricular block; implantation of temporary or permanent cardiac pacemakers; heart valve replacement; postvalvuloplasty status; presence of malignant tumors, hematological diseases, or autoimmune diseases; presence of renal dysfunction or underlying renal diseases; contrast medium allergy; and loss to follow-up. Although 398 patients were initially enrolled, 12 patients with unclear images and incomplete clinical data were excluded, and 5 patients lost to follow-up due to unavailable telephone contact were also excluded. Thus, 381 eligible patients were enrolled in the final cohort. Figure 1 presents the flowchart of the study.

CCTA acquisition

CCTA examinations were performed on a Canon Aquilion ONE Genesis CT scanner (Canon Medical Systems, Japan) using the following parameters: detector configuration, 320 × 0.5 mm; gantry rotation time, 0.275 s; tube voltage, 120 kV; tube current, 300 mAs; collimation, 128 × 0.5 mm; reconstruction slice thickness, 0.5 mm; and image matrix, 512 × 512. The scan range spanned from 1.0 cm below the tracheal carina to 1.5 cm below the inferior border of the heart. Prior to scanning, patients with a resting heart rate above 65 bpm received oral metoprolol (25–100 mg) approximately 1 h beforehand, targeting a rate below 65 bpm; unless contraindicated, sublingual nitroglycerin (0.4 mg) was given immediately before acquisition to achieve coronary vasodilation. A nonionic iodinated contrast agent was injected via an antecubital vein using a dual-syringe high-pressure injector according to a weight-adjusted protocol (1.0–1.5 mL/kg) at 4.5–5.5 mL/s, followed by a saline chaser, with scan timing determined by bolus tracking in the ascending aorta. Images were reconstructed at the best systolic phase (40% of the R-R interval) and the best diastolic phase (70% of the R-R interval), and the dataset yielding superior image quality was retained for analysis; radiation dose metrics (CTDIvol and DLP) were recorded for all examinations. All reconstructed images underwent both subjective and objective quality evaluation, and only those satisfying the predefined criteria proceeded to subsequent analysis. Objective image quality was assessed by intravascular CT attenuation within the coronary lumen—graded as excellent (351–450 HU), good (300–350 HU), moderate (200–299 HU), or poor (< 200 HU)—and by image noise, measured as the SD of CT attenuation in the aortic root and graded as excellent (< 20 HU), good (20–30 HU), fair (31–40 HU), or non-diagnostic (> 40 HU), with a noise level below 30 HU deemed acceptable.

Imaging analysis

After CCTA acquisition, images were uploaded to a commercially available AI platform (CoronaryDoc, version 1.0.8; Shukun Technology, Beijing, China) for automated analysis. The software employs a 3D U-Net architecture trained on over 1,000 manually annotated cases for each plaque subtype (calcified and noncalcified), achieving per-case sensitivity, specificity, and precision > 92% (calcified) and > 89% (noncalcified), with per-lesion sensitivity > 90% and > 86%, respectively, and demonstrated high reproducibility on repeated segmentations. No cases were excluded due to segmentation failure; all automated outputs were visually reviewed by an experienced cardiovascular imaging specialist. Although the software permits manual correction, no case required it in this study, as all datasets met image quality criteria for reliable automated segmentation. The software segmented the coronary artery tree and delineated lumen and outer vessel wall boundaries (external elastic membrane equivalent) for all major epicardial vessels with a reference diameter ≥ 2 mm. Total plaque volume (TPV, mm³) was computed as vessel volume minus lumen volume, integrated along each vessel segment. To account for variation in vessel size, plaque volume was normalized to vessel volume as (plaque volume / vessel volume) × 100%, termed percent atheroma volume (PAV). Plaque subtypes were classified by CT attenuation: noncalcified plaque (NCP, − 30 to + 350 HU) and calcified plaque (CP, > 350 HU). Side branches were excluded from main-vessel analysis at their ostia, and lesion boundaries were defined by the transition from diseased to normal-appearing vessel wall. CT-FFR was measured using a deep learning–based module (FFRDoc, version 1.0.8; Shukun Technology) in each of the three major coronary arteries; the lowest recorded value was used, with values < 0.76 considered abnormal. The analysis workflow is shown in Fig. 2. Six total whole-heart parameters were collected: total-TPV, total-TPAV, total-CPV, total-CPAV, total-NCPV, and total-NCPAV. Additionally, 18 vessel-specific parameters were recorded for the LAD, LCX, and RCA.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using R and Python. Categorical data are expressed as counts (%) and compared using chi-square tests. Normally distributed continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared via t tests. Non-normally distributed continuous data are expressed as medians (P25, P75) and compared using Mann–Whitney U tests. A time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve identified the optimal risk cutoff for each plaque parameter in predicting MACEs. Based on these cutoffs, plaque parameters were treated as categorical variables for univariate and multivariate Cox regression survival analyses; results are expressed as hazard ratios (HRs). Owing to strong collinearity (TPV = CPV + NCPV; TPAV = CPAV + NCPAV; see Table S1), multivariate Cox analysis was performed using individual plaque parameters alongside clinical indicators. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted. The discriminative performance of the plaque model, CT-FFR model, and combined plaque + CT-FFR model was assessed using the C-index. Bootstrap internal validation was performed for all three models (500 iterations). Calibration, Brier score calculation, and decision curve analysis were also conducted. Landmark sensitivity analysis re-evaluated the C-index after sequential exclusion of early MACE events. Finally, a LASSO-Cox model was built in R, and a Cox survival neural network was constructed in Python for MACE prediction. A two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05 was used throughout; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The LASSO-Cox model predicts MACEs through L1 regularization, which achieves variable sparsification and selection, eliminates redundant predictors (e.g., branch-level parameters with weak predictive power), and simplifies model structure while preserving the survival analysis framework of the traditional Cox model, thereby maintaining high interpretability.

The Cox survival neural network predicts MACEs using an architecture of 8 input features → 32 (hidden layer 1) → 16 (hidden layer 2) → 1 (risk score output), with ReLU activation and dropout of 0.2 to mitigate overfitting. Training was performed on a CPU for 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer (learning rate 1e-3, L2 regularization coefficient 1e-5); the loss function employed Cox partial likelihood loss, suited to event-time association learning in survival data.

Results

Clinical characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the clinical characteristics of the study population. Of 381 patients, 67 developed MACEs during follow-up. The MACE subgroup comprised 45 percutaneous coronary interventions with stent implantation, 5 coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) procedures, 1 percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty, 2 rehospitalizations for aggravated stable angina, 2 rehospitalizations for aggravated unstable angina, 1 nonfatal myocardial infarction, 1 malignant arrhythmia, 5 deaths from myocardial infarction, 1 death from heart failure, and 4 deaths from other causes. Hard endpoints totaled 7 events: 1 nonfatal myocardial infarction, 5 deaths from myocardial infarction, and 1 death from heart failure. The remaining 314 patients comprised the non-MACE group. No statistically significant differences in clinical indicators were observed between groups, whereas CPV, NCPV, TPV, CPAV, NCPAV, and TPAV all differed significantly (P < 0.01).

Cox analysis of total plaque burden based on risk cutoff values

Risk cutoff values for total-CPV, total-NCPV, and total-TPV were 68.78 mm³, 108.81 mm³, and 206.78 mm³, respectively; corresponding cutoffs for total-CPAV, total-NCPAV, and total-TPAV were 4.54%, 4.68%, and 11.11%. Compared with the low-risk group, the high-risk group showed an HR of 3.121 for total-CPV (P < 0.001). HRs for the high-risk versus low-risk groups were 4.528 (P < 0.001) for total-NCPV, 4.402 (P < 0.001) for total-TPV, 3.175 (P < 0.001) for total-CPAV, 4.859 (P < 0.001) for total-NCPAV, and 4.003 (P < 0.001) for total-TPAV. Detailed data are presented in Table 2; Kaplan–Meier survival curves are shown in Fig. 3. After adjusting for sex, age, diabetes, TC, TG, LDL-C, and statin use history, multivariate Cox regression identified total-NCPAV as the strongest MACE predictor (HR 5.07, P < 0.001). Table S3 provides patient counts per risk group, along with AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and Youden index for each ROC-derived cutoff. Among 7 hard endpoints (myocardial infarction [fatal and nonfatal], heart failure death), only univariate Cox regression was performed (Table S4). Total-NCPAV > 4.68% was the strongest predictor of hard endpoints among all parameters.

Predicting MACEs with plaque parameters as continuous variables

For each 1 mm³ increase in total-CPV, total-NCPV, and total-TPV, the HRs for MACEs were 1.002, 1.005, and 1.002, respectively. For each 1% increase in total-CPAV, total-NCPAV, and total-TPAV, the HRs were 1.049, 1.137, and 1.049, respectively. Per 1-SD increase, HRs for total-CPV, total-NCPV, and total-TPV were 1.39, 1.56, and 1.52; for total-CPAV, total-NCPAV, and total-TPAV, per-SD HRs were 1.48, 1.82, and 1.69. Total-NCPAV per 1-SD showed the strongest association with MACEs among all parameters. Detailed data appear in Table 3. Hard-endpoint prediction was limited to univariate Cox analysis (Table S5); total-NCPAV again yielded the highest HR.

Predictive performance of the combined model of plaque parameters and CT-FFR for MACEs

To distinguish the predictive contributions of total versus vessel-specific plaque parameters and to lay the groundwork for the LASSO-Cox and neural network models, Model 1 incorporated six vessel-specific percent atheroma volume parameters: LAD-CPAV, LAD-NCPAV, LCX-CPAV, LCX-NCPAV, RCA-CPAV, and RCA-NCPAV (Table 4). The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied (global Schoenfeld test, P > 0.05; Table S2), and multicollinearity among branch-level parameters was acceptable (all VIF < 3; Table S1); however, LAD-NCPAV showed evidence of non-proportional hazards. Therefore, LAD-NCPAV-related estimates and vessel-specific findings were interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory. After optimism correction (Table S6), C-indices were 0.705 (plaque model), 0.565 (CT-FFR model), and 0.708 (combined model), confirming acceptable discrimination for the plaque and combined models; CT-FFR alone, by contrast, showed poor discriminative performance. Calibration at 18 months (200 bootstrap resamples) demonstrated reasonable agreement with the ideal diagonal at lower predicted probabilities, though modest underestimation was observed at intermediate-to-high risk ranges for the plaque and combined models (Figure S1). Brier scores at 6, 12, and 18 months were closely comparable between the combined model (0.101, 0.119, 0.099) and the plaque model (0.101, 0.119, 0.100) (Table S7). Decision curve analysis at 18 months (Figure S2) showed nearly overlapping net benefit curves for the combined and plaque models across the clinically relevant threshold probability range of 5%–30%; both outperformed the CT-FFR model and the treat-all and treat-none strategies.

Formal incremental value testing, using the plaque model as reference, confirmed that adding CT-FFR did not significantly improve any of four complementary performance metrics: likelihood ratio test, change in C-index, continuous net reclassification improvement, and integrated discrimination improvement (Table S8). Collectively, these findings indicate that CT-FFR conferred limited incremental prognostic value beyond AI-quantified vessel-specific plaque morphology.

Landmark sensitivity analysis excluding early events

We performed landmark sensitivity analyses to test whether CCTA-driven early revascularization inflated model performance. Of 67 MACE events, 53.7% occurred within 90 days of the index CCTA and 68.7% within 180 days (Table S9). After sequentially excluding patients with events at ≤ 30, ≤60, ≤ 90, and ≤ 180 days, the C-index of the 6-parameter plaque model rose from 0.733 (full cohort) to 0.770, 0.758, 0.750, and 0.771, respectively; the combined model increased from 0.741 to 0.767, 0.760, 0.771, and 0.792 (Table 5). The combined model did not significantly outperform the plaque model at any landmark; however, the likelihood ratio test P value fell from 0.117 (full cohort) to 0.061 at 90 days and 0.081 at 180 days, hinting at incremental value for CT-FFR once early revascularization-driven events are removed—a signal requiring confirmation in larger cohorts. RCA-CPAV remained a significant predictor throughout (HR 1.045–1.067, P ranging from < 0.001 to 0.031), whereas LAD-NCPAV reached significance only in the full cohort (HR 1.050, P = 0.001) and at the 30-day landmark (HR 1.038, P = 0.046). Period-stratified Cox regression at 90 days (Table S10) revealed a larger effect of the plaque risk score in the late period—the opposite of what incorporation bias would predict. Removing early events did not weaken the association between plaque burden and MACE risk; if anything, it strengthened.

LASSO-Cox model

As shown in Table 2, total-NCPAV was the strongest MACE predictor. Before constructing the LASSO-Cox model, we performed separate univariate Cox regressions for TPV, TPAV, CPV, CPAV, NCPV, and NCPAV in the LAD, LCX, and RCA territories. RCA-CPAV and RCA-NCPAV each yielded HRs exceeding 3. A total of 10 indicators were therefore entered into the LASSO-Cox model: sex and age (demographics); total-CPV and total-NCPAV (whole-heart plaque burden); LAD-NCPAV, RCA-CPAV, and RCA-NCPAV (vessel-specific plaque burden); and RCA-CPAV > 2.8%, RCA-NCPAV > 3.49%, and CT-FFR < 0.76 (ROC-validated thresholds). L1 regularization eliminated redundant variables, retaining six predictors with independent contributions to MACE risk (all coefficients positive, indicating positive correlations with outcome). The LASSO-Cox model achieved a C-index of 0.747 (Table 6). Variables ranked by absolute coefficient magnitude (Table 7) identified RCA-CPAV > 2.8%, RCA-NCPAV, and RCA-NCPAV > 3.49% as the strongest predictors.

Cox survival neural network model

During development of the Cox survival neural network model, right coronary artery (RCA) indicators emerged as the strongest predictors of MACEs (Table 7). Total-NCPAV—a measure of global plaque burden—ranked third by contribution and was retained in the final model. To capture the joint effect of RCA plaque morphology and functional ischemia, an interaction term was constructed: RCA-NCPAV × CT-FFR < 0.76. This continuous × binary indicator works as follows: when RCA-NCPAV is 0 and CT-FFR is 1 (i.e., no ischemic risk), the interaction value is 0; when RCA-NCPAV is 0 and CT-FFR is 0 (ischemia positive), the interaction value is also 0. The metric becomes active only when both components are positive, at which point it assumes the value of RCA-NCPAV. In essence, this is an intensity measure under dual-positive conditions. It directly captures the synergistic risk of high morphological burden (abundant noncalcified plaque in the RCA) in combination with functional ischemia (low CT-FFR) and strengthens the clinical interpretability of the model.

Baseline demographic variables included sex and age. Global plaque burden was represented by total-NCPAV. Vessel-specific plaque measures comprised RCA-CPAV and RCA-NCPAV; the ROC-validated thresholds were RCA-NCPAV > 3.49% and CT-FFR < 0.76. The interaction-derived indicator was defined as RCA-NCPAV × CT-FFR < 0.76.

The dataset was split into training and test sets at a 7:3 ratio. The Cox survival neural network achieved C-index values of 0.751 in the training set (P < 0.001) and 0.730 in the test set (P < 0.001), reflecting strong predictive performance for MACEs (Table 8). Training curves showed steady improvement in MACE prediction with increasing epochs, indicating effective generalization to unseen data. The model assigned specific weights to eight indicators (Table 9); RCA plaque burden was the dominant contributor. Based on these feature weights, the test set was stratified into low-, medium-, and high-risk groups (Fig. 4), affording more granular risk discrimination. Representative risk predictions for individual test samples appear in Table 10.

Discussion

In this study, AI-quantified coronary plaque burden parameters were independently associated with MACEs in CAD patients, and total noncalcified plaque burden (total-NCPAV) stood out as the predominant predictor. Each 1% increase in total-NCPAV conferred a 13.7% higher MACE hazard; used as a categorical variable, total-NCPAV > 4.68% yielded an HR of 5.07. The plaque model showed acceptable discriminative performance (apparent C-index 0.744; optimism-corrected 0.705, bootstrap = 500 iterations), confirming reasonable internal validity, while the LASSO-Cox (C-index 0.747) and Cox survival neural network (test-set C-index 0.730) models demonstrated broadly comparable predictive performance. Adding CT-FFR to vessel-specific plaque parameters yielded only marginal improvement in discrimination (ΔC-index 0.010). Formal incremental value testing confirmed that CT-FFR provided very limited additional prognostic benefit: calibration, Brier scores, and decision curve analyses were near-identical between plaque-only and combined models. Notably, landmark sensitivity analyses excluding early revascularization events revealed a trend toward incremental value for CT-FFR, suggesting that CCTA-driven early revascularization may have partially obscured the functional contribution of CT-FFR in the full-cohort analysis. Across all models, RCA plaque burden was consistently the dominant vessel-specific predictor—a finding that remains exploratory and requires independent confirmation.

The relationship between coronary plaque composition and adverse cardiovascular events is well established [16, 17]. Prior studies in this area, however, have relied on semiautomated segmentation requiring manual correction—an approach that limits scalability in routine practice [12]. With the advent of AI-QCT [18], physicians can now bypass dedicated workstations and obtain quantitative plaque burden measurements directly on standard computers. This shift facilitates broader clinical implementation without the need for specialized technologist expertise. After adjustment for clinical risk factors, total-NCPAV emerged as the strongest MACE predictor—a finding consistent across categorical (risk-cutoff), continuous (per-unit and per-SD), and hard-endpoint analyses. Noncalcified plaques are rich in fibrous and lipid components critical to plaque rupture. Abdelwahed et al. [19] showed that lipid is present in 100% of plaques in fibrous-capped ruptured ACS patients, and the coexistence of lipid and calcified components within a single lesion alters plaque stress distributions, predisposing to rupture. These observations align with the present findings: noncalcified plaque components are powerful predictors of adverse events. The influence of calcification is more complex [20–22]. Punctate calcification ranks among the hallmark features of high-risk plaques [23], yet as overall calcified burden increases, plaque stress distributions tend toward greater stability, potentially reducing MACE risk. This biological duality—whereby microcalcification confers vulnerability while macroscopic calcification reflects plaque quiescence—may explain why total-CPV and total-CPAV produced the lowest hazard ratios among the plaque parameters we examined.

Although statins promote plaque phenotypic transformation—reducing lipid content, favoring calcification or fibrosis, and thereby lowering MACE risk [17, 24, 25]—coronary anatomical stenosis may persist, and patients may continue to exhibit hemodynamic abnormalities despite therapy. Early lipid-lowering therapy is therefore essential to prevent plaque progression. Identifying risk threshold values for plaque burden becomes clinically relevant at this stage. We derived optimal cutoff values using time-dependent ROC analysis; it is important to note, however, that these were obtained within a single-center cohort and tested in the same dataset. Prior work by Bär [26] has explored AI-based plaque burden safe thresholds for long-term ACS risk; these conceptually similar safe thresholds parallel the optimal cutoff values we derived here. Without external validation in independent populations, these thresholds should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than as established clinical decision boundaries. They may serve as preliminary references for prioritizing early pharmacological intervention in higher-risk patients, though prospective validation is required before such thresholds can inform decisions on deferring invasive procedures.

Prior investigations of plaque composition and MACEs have been constrained by incomplete vessel-specific characterization. Gall et al. [7] used the CAD-RADS score to examine plaque composition effects on MACEs in patients with obstructive CAD but did not provide detailed quantitative plaque burden parameters. Multicenter AI-QCT studies examining sex differences in plaque burden have concentrated on total coronary plaque burden without specific comparisons across the three major branches [27]. Tummala et al. [28] examined proximal plaque in the three major branches but did not quantify the differential vessel-specific influence on MACE outcomes. We sought to address these gaps by constructing a C-index model incorporating six vessel-specific percent atheroma volume parameters (LAD-CPAV, LAD-NCPAV, LCX-CPAV, LCX-NCPAV, RCA-CPAV, and RCA-NCPAV), with acceptable multicollinearity (all VIF < 3) and satisfaction of the proportional hazards assumption. The global Schoenfeld test was nonsignificant, although LAD-NCPAV showed a variable-level violation; therefore LAD-NCPAV-related and vessel-specific estimates were considered exploratory. We used percent plaque volume, rather than absolute volume, to ensure generalizability across vessels of differing sizes. After bootstrap internal validation (500 iterations), optimism-corrected C-indices were 0.705 (plaque), 0.565 (CT-FFR-only), and 0.708 (combined).

These results carry several implications. First, the CT-FFR-only model showed limited discriminatory performance (corrected C-index 0.565), likely because anatomic plaque burden captures multiple dimensions of atherosclerotic disease activity—total disease extent, plaque composition, and lesion vulnerability—that are not fully reflected by a single hemodynamic parameter. Sole reliance on a functional indicator may therefore substantially underestimate cardiovascular risk. Second, although the combined morphological–functional model aligns conceptually with comprehensive coronary assessment integrating anatomy and physiology, formal incremental value testing showed no statistically significant improvement in discrimination, reclassification, or model fit when CT-FFR was added. Calibration at 18 months and Brier scores at 6, 12, and 18 months were nearly identical between the plaque-only and combined models; decision curve analysis showed overlapping net benefit curves across the clinically relevant 5%–30% threshold probability range. Collectively, these results indicate that AI-quantified vessel-specific plaque morphology alone provides the predominant prognostic information, with CT-FFR conferring very limited incremental value in this cohort. It is worth considering that, in our patients, anatomical coronary stenosis may have played a more decisive role than functional stenosis in predicting the composite MACE endpoint.

Landmark sensitivity analyses yielded particularly noteworthy findings. Of 67 MACE events, 53.7% occurred within 90 days and 68.7% within 180 days of the index CCTA, with revascularization procedures constituting the majority of early events. This temporal clustering is suggestive of incorporation bias: CCTA findings of high plaque burden and/or abnormal CT-FFR likely triggered downstream revascularization, which was subsequently counted as MACE events. After sequentially excluding patients with events at ≤ 30, ≤60, ≤ 90, and ≤ 180 days, the combined model C-index rose to 0.767, 0.760, 0.771, and 0.792. The likelihood ratio test P value for CT-FFR’s incremental contribution narrowed from 0.117 (full cohort) to 0.061 (≤ 90 days) and 0.081 (≤ 180 days). Period-stratified Cox regression at the 90-day landmark showed a larger effect of the plaque risk score in the late period (HR 3.30 per SD) than in the early period (HR 2.12)—the opposite of what incorporation bias would predict—indicating that the plaque–MACE association was not artifactually inflated by early, CCTA-driven events. Taken together, these observations hint at incremental value for CT-FFR after accounting for incorporation bias. The trend did not reach statistical significance, however, and will require confirmation in larger, prospectively designed cohorts with blinded endpoint adjudication.

The LASSO-Cox model retained RCA-CPAV > 2.8%, RCA-NCPAV > 3.49%, and total-NCPAV as selected predictors (coefficients 0.55, 0.25, and 0.07), with a C-index of 0.747. Although not statistically superior to conventional approaches, this model served as an effective variable-selection tool—reducing 10 candidate predictors to 6 retained variables—and identified vessel-level contributions that would not have been readily discernible from traditional Cox models. The Cox survival neural network (8 inputs → 32 → 16 → 1 risk score, ReLU, dropout 0.2, 7:3 split) achieved a test-set C-index of 0.730; the feature importance ranking was broadly consistent with LASSO-Cox coefficients, with total-NCPAV and RCA-NCPAV among the top contributors. Key limitations of the neural network analysis include modest sample size (381 patients, 67 events; events-per-variable ratio ~ 8:1) and absence of external validation for the three-tier risk stratification. These results should therefore be interpreted as exploratory proof-of-concept rather than a validated clinical prediction tool. It is worth emphasizing that the core finding—predominant prognostic weight of RCA plaque burden—remained consistent across all three modeling frameworks.

The identification of RCA, rather than LAD, plaque burden as the dominant vessel-specific predictor departs from several prior reports. Bax et al. [29] first documented that the LAD carries the highest coronary plaque burden and subsequently reported that the LAD has the highest risk of progression to obstructive CAD [30]. Giesen et al. [31] found that noncalcified plaque burden in the LAD is most closely associated with the fat attenuation index (FAI) and that noncalcified plaque correlates most strongly with MACEs. Against this LAD-centric literature, our finding of RCA predominance represents a clear departure. Interestingly, a meta-analysis of 17 studies [32] suggested that elevated FAI around the RCA is most strongly associated with MACEs (risk ratio 1.22, P = 0.001), with no significant association for the LAD and only a borderline association for the LCX—findings consistent with our results. The weaker prognostic impact of the LCX thus appears relatively uncontroversial, whereas the relative importance of LAD versus RCA plaque burden remains unresolved. Our observation that RCA plaque burden was the strongest MACE predictor should be regarded as exploratory and requires confirmation in prospective studies. On a practical level, AI-based plaque quantification offers clear advantages—rapid, fully automated analysis deployable on standard clinical workstations with minimal post-processing—that may facilitate broader adoption of quantitative plaque assessment in routine cardiovascular care, provided its clinical utility is confirmed in prospective studies with hard clinical endpoints.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the single-center design inherently constrains generalizability—the derived cutoff values and model weights require validation in multicenter cohorts with differing demographic profiles, disease prevalence, and clinical practice patterns. Second, while the CT-FFR values used here have been validated against invasive FFR with good agreement [33], the AI-based plaque quantification employed has not been independently validated against intravascular imaging reference standards such as OCT or IVUS. Third, the follow-up duration (maximum 18 months) is relatively short for a prognostic study of stable CAD, and the number of hard endpoints (myocardial infarction, heart failure death; n = 7) was insufficient to support robust multivariable modeling; hard-endpoint analyses were therefore confined to univariate Cox regression and should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Fourth, the composite MACE endpoint was dominated by revascularization events, which are subject to treatment-decision bias as discussed above. Although landmark analyses and period-stratified Cox regression partially mitigated this concern and showed that the plaque–MACE association persisted—and if anything, strengthened—after removal of early events, optimal validation would require cohorts with independently adjudicated, purely spontaneous cardiovascular events. Fifth, treating physicians necessarily made management decisions based on patients’ CCTA data, precluding blinding to imaging results. Should future ethics committees approve protocols that safeguard patient safety under more rigorous conditions, blinded study designs could be considered. These limitations collectively underscore the need for prospective, multicenter studies with blinded endpoint adjudication, longer follow-up, and larger numbers of hard clinical events.

Conclusions

AI-quantified total-NCPAV was the strongest predictor of MACEs in 381 CAD patients. CT-FFR added limited incremental value beyond plaque morphology, though landmark analyses suggested a potential signal warranting prospective investigation. RCA plaque burden was the dominant vessel-specific predictor—an exploratory finding requiring independent validation. Prospective studies with hard endpoints are needed to establish clinical utility.
