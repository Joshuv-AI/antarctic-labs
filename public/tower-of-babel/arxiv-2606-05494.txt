MASF: A Multi-Model Adaptive Selection Framework for Abstractive Text summarization PubID: pubid: 979-8-3315-8488-7/26/$31.00 ©2026 IEEE
Ahmed Alansary, Ali Hamdi

Abstract
Automatic text summarization has become increasingly important due to the rapid growth of digital textual information. This paper presents a Multi-Model Adaptive Summarization Framework designed to improve the robustness and quality of abstractive text summarization. Relying on a single model often leads to inconsistent summarization quality across articles with varying structures and topics. To address this limitation, the proposed framework integrates multiple fine-tuned transformer-based summarization models and introduces an adaptive selection mechanism. In this framework, each model independently generates a candidate summary for the same input article. The generated summaries are then evaluated using automatic evaluation metrics that capture both lexical similarity and semantic relevance. Based on these scores, the framework selects the highest-quality summary as the final output. The models are fine-tuned and evaluated on the widely used CNN/DailyMail news summarization dataset. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed framework achieves the highest BERTScore among all compared methods with a score of 88.63%. It also outperforms several LLMs such as GPT3-D2, Falcon-7b, and Mpt-7b, highlighting its effectiveness and robustness. These findings highlight the effectiveness of leveraging multiple transformer-based models within an adaptive selection strategy to improve the quality and robustness of automatic text summarization systems.



I
Introduction

The exponential expansion of digital information has produced an enormous surge in
textual content across news platforms, social media, and online repositories.
Consequently, the ability to efficiently process and comprehend large volumes of
text has become an increasingly pressing challenge. Text summarization has
established itself as a vital Natural Language Processing (NLP) task, concerned
with producing compact summaries that retain the essential meaning of source
content. News summarization, in particular, serves a significant role in enabling
readers to quickly extract key insights from lengthy
articles
[
25
,
13
]
.

Conventional approaches relied predominantly on extractive techniques that identify
and select the most informative sentences from the source text. Such methods
generally follow a sequential pipeline encompassing text preprocessing, feature
extraction, sentence scoring, base model application, sentence selection, and
summary generation. A thorough review of extractive summarization approaches
reflects the broad spectrum of techniques employed in this area, spanning
statistical, rule-based, fuzzy logic, optimization, graph-based, clustering-based,
machine learning, and deep learning methods
[
25
]
. While certain extractive
techniques have made meaningful contributions to summarization system development,
many surveys note that current methods still contend with persistent challenges,
including limited robustness across varied article structures and topics,
inconsistent summary quality when depending on a single model architecture, and
overreliance on individual model outputs that may not consistently capture both
lexical and semantic aspects of quality
[
25
,
12
,
3
]
.

Transformer architectures have delivered outstanding results on benchmarks such as
CNN/DailyMail, owing to their self-attention mechanisms and parallel sequence
processing capabilities, which facilitate stronger modeling of long-range textual
dependencies
[
16
,
18
]
. Similarly, hybrid frameworks have been proposed
that integrate Optical Character Recognition (OCR) with deep learning summarization
models — including LSTM, Bi-LSTM, BERT, and T5 — to process text extracted from
images
[
13
]
. Notwithstanding these advances, deep learning models continue
to face notable constraints, such as substantial computational demands, sensitivity
to training data quality, and dependence on evaluation metrics like ROUGE that may
not fully reflect summary quality from a semantic standpoint
[
16
]
.

The rise of Large Language Models (LLMs) has further expanded the possibilities for
text summarization. Recent work has examined the deployment of multiple LLMs —
including architectures such as MPT-7B, Falcon-7B, and ChatGPT-based models — for
generating abstractive summaries
[
3
]
. While these efforts demonstrate the
versatility of contemporary summarization systems, they also expose a notable
constraint: most existing systems rely on a single model at inference time, which
does not always yield the most informative output.

Driven by these shortcomings, this study introduces a
Multi-Model Adaptive
Summarization Framework
for the CNN/DailyMail dataset. In contrast to conventional
single-model approaches, the proposed method employs multiple transformer-based
models to generate candidate summaries for each input article. Specifically, three
distinct summarization models produce alternative summaries, each of which is
assessed using automatic evaluation metrics measuring lexical overlap, n-gram
similarity, and semantic correspondence. The system subsequently selects the most
informative summary based on a composite evaluation score. By combining multiple
models with an adaptive selection mechanism, the framework aims to enhance
summarization quality and robustness beyond what single-model systems can achieve.
This approach allows the system to capitalize on the individual strengths of
different models while compensating for their respective weaknesses, ultimately
delivering more accurate and coherent summaries for news articles.

Related Work

Automatic Text Summarization (ATS) has been extensively studied as a core Natural
Language Processing (NLP) task concerned with producing condensed representations
of lengthy documents while retaining their key content

8
]
. The
proliferation of digital text across domains including news, academic literature,
and social media has driven growing demand for effective summarization
systems
[
5
,
24
]
. Early work in this area concentrated largely on
extractive approaches, in which relevant sentences are directly selected from the
source document to compose the summary. Broad surveys have traced the development
of summarization methodologies, examining core pipeline components such as
preprocessing, feature extraction, sentence scoring, and summary
generation
[
25
,
17
,
6

. These works also draw attention to the
growing complexity introduced by multi-document, multilingual, and multimodal
settings, which continue to challenge existing systems
[
25
]
. Moreover,
several surveys have tracked progress in abstractive summarization, covering
model developments, dataset construction, and evaluation strategies, while
identifying established benchmarks like CNN/DailyMail and widely used metrics
such as ROUGE
[
19
,
2
]
. Collectively, these contributions offer a
thorough picture of the current state of summarization research and outline
several open problems.

Conventional extractive summarization methods have drawn on statistical,
graph-based, and feature-engineered approaches to identify salient sentences.
Graph-based models have received considerable attention, where sentence relevance
is derived from inter-sentence similarity relationships. For instance, sentence
centrality and semantic similarity have been used to build graphs encoding
relationships among textual units, supporting more informed sentence
selection
[
9
]
. Likewise, ranking-based methods have been proposed to
integrate multiple sentence-level signals — including topical content, semantic
representations, keywords, and positional cues — to assess sentence importance
within a document
[
10
]
. Other research has improved upon classical
algorithms like TextRank by incorporating word embeddings and weighting schemes to
refine sentence representation and output quality
[
14
]
. Unsupervised
approaches have further explored clustering and topic modeling to reduce topical
bias and produce summaries that cover document subtopics more
evenly
[
20
]
. Despite their efficiency and interpretability, extractive
methods tend to produce redundant outputs and are inherently limited in their
capacity to generate coherent, paraphrased summaries comparable to human writing.

To overcome these shortcomings, research attention has progressively moved toward
abstractive summarization using neural architectures. Sequence-to-sequence models
built on Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
networks have been broadly adopted to generate summaries that rephrase source
content while preserving its meaning
[
11
]
. These architectures typically
follow an encoder–decoder design augmented with attention mechanisms to model
contextual dependencies across words and sentences. Performance has been further
improved through bidirectional encoders, deeper stacked architectures, and refined
attention mechanisms that strengthen sequence modeling
capabilities
[
11
,
1
]
. Discourse-aware neural models have also been
introduced to capture long-range dependencies and structural relations among
discourse units, enhancing extractive summarization by incorporating document-level
structural information
[
23
]
.

More recently, Transformer-based architectures and pre-trained language models have
brought substantial gains to summarization performance. Transformer models leverage
self-attention to capture long-range contextual relationships more effectively than
prior neural approaches
[
16
]
. Pre-trained models such as PEGASUS-xsum,
BART, and T5 have achieved strong results on standard benchmarks through large-scale
pre-training followed by task-specific fine-tuning
[
12
,
15
]
. However,
fine-tuning such large models frequently raises concerns around overfitting and
computational overhead, motivating work on optimization and adaptation
strategies
[
12
]
. Alternative problem formulations have also been explored,
including casting extractive summarization as a semantic matching task between source
documents and candidate summaries, yielding competitive results on
CNN/DailyMail
[
26
]
. Multi-modal summarization frameworks combining
Optical Character Recognition (OCR) with deep learning have further broadened the
scope of summarization to cover text extracted from images
[
13
]
.

Beyond general-domain applications, LLMs have been applied to specialized areas
such as clinical text summarization, where adapted models have matched or surpassed
human expert performance on certain tasks
[
21
]
. Despite these advances,
deep learning and LLM-based summarization systems continue to face challenges
including factual consistency, semantic accuracy, and reliable evaluation of
generated outputs
[

. These limitations motivate investigation into
hybrid frameworks that combine the complementary strengths of multiple summarization
models to achieve improved overall performance.

III
Dataset

This study employs the CNN/DailyMail news summarization dataset, a well-established
benchmark widely adopted for supervised summarization research. The dataset comprises
news articles sourced from the CNN and Daily Mail websites, each paired with
human-authored highlight sentences that function as reference summaries. In the
summarization framework, these highlights are joined together to construct the target
summary for each corresponding article.
[
22
]

The dataset encompasses over 300,000 article–summary pairs produced by professional
journalists. Individual articles generally range from 500 to 800 words, while their
corresponding summaries typically span 3–5 sentences covering the essential information
of the news piece. Each entry contains two primary fields:
Article
, holding
the complete news article text, and
Highlights
, containing the author-written
summary. Table
I
illustrates representative samples from the
dataset, demonstrating the structure of article–summary pairs used during training
and evaluation.

Following the conventional data split, the dataset is partitioned into training,
validation, and test subsets. The training partition comprises 287,113 samples,
the validation partition contains 13,368 samples, and the test partition includes
11,490 samples.

Originally developed for machine reading comprehension and question answering, the
CNN/DailyMail dataset was subsequently adapted for abstractive summarization by
repurposing article highlights as reference summaries. Its large scale and
journalistic quality have established it as one of the most prevalent benchmarks
for assessing the performance of neural summarization systems.

IV
Methodology

This study proposes a multi-model adaptive summarization framework designed to improve the quality of abstractive summaries by leveraging multiple transformer-based language models and automatically selecting the most informative output. Unlike traditional approaches that rely on a single summarization model, the proposed framework employs multiple fine-tuned models to generate candidate summaries and then selects the most suitable summary using an automatic evaluation mechanism.

The proposed architecture consists of five main stages: dataset preparation, model fine-tuning, input preprocessing, multi-model summarization, and adaptive evaluation and selection.

IV-A

Model Fine-Tuning

To improve domain adaptation and summarization performance, the pretrained models are fine-tuned on the training dataset. Three transformer-based models are used in this framework:

\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}\}

(1)

where
M_{1}
represents T5-small,
M_{2}
represents PEGASUS-xsum, and
M_{3}
represents LED-base.

The T5-small and PEGASUS-xsum models are fine-tuned using a standard sequence-to-sequence training objective. During training, the article text is used as input and the reference summary is used as the target output. Tokenization is applied using the corresponding tokenizer for each model, and the models learn to generate summaries that align with the reference highlights.

For the LED-base model, parameter-efficient fine-tuning is applied using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). Instead of updating all model parameters, LoRA introduces trainable low-rank matrices into specific attention layers of the transformer architecture. Let the adapted model be denoted as

M_{3}^{\prime}=\text{LoRA}(M_{3})

(2)

where only the injected LoRA parameters are trained while the base model parameters remain frozen. This approach reduces the number of trainable parameters while maintaining effective summarization performance.

IV-B

Input Preprocessing

Given an input article
A
, the text is first tokenized and truncated to ensure compatibility with the maximum input length supported by the models. In the case of T5-small and LED-base, task-specific prompts are appended to guide the summarization process.

Let the preprocessing function be denoted as

A^{\prime}=f(A)

(3)

where
A^{\prime}
represents the processed input sequence that is fed into the summarization models.

IV-C

Multi-Model Summarization

After fine-tuning, the processed article
A^{\prime}
is passed to the three summarization models to generate candidate summaries as illustrated in Figure
1
. Each model independently produces a summary for the same input article.

Formally, the summary generated by model
M_{i}
is defined as

S_{i}=M_{i}(A^{\prime})

(4)

where
S_{i}
denotes the candidate summary produced by model
M_{i}
. This process results in a set of candidate summaries:

\mathcal{S}=\{S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}\}

(5)

Each summary is generated using a maximum generation length of 128 tokens.

IV-D

Automatic Evaluation

To assess the quality of the generated summaries, three complementary evaluation metrics are used: ROUGE-L, BLEU and BERTScore. ROUGE-L measures lexical overlap between the generated summary and the article, BLEU measures the precision of n-gram overlap between the generated summary and the article, while BERTScore measures semantic similarity using contextual embeddings.

Let
R(S_{i})
denote the ROUGE-L score of summary
S_{i}
,
BL(S_{i})
denote the BLEU score, and
BS(S_{i})
denote the BERTScore F1 value. A combined evaluation score is computed as

Score(S_{i})=\frac{R(S_{i})+BL(S_{i})+BS(S_{i})}{3}

(6)

This combined score captures both surface-level textual overlap and deeper semantic similarity between the generated summaries and articles.

IV-E

Adaptive Selection

The final stage of the framework selects the most informative summary among the candidate outputs. Let
S^{*}
denote the final selected summary. The adaptive selection mechanism chooses the summary with the highest evaluation score:

S^{*}=\arg\max_{S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}}Score(S_{i})

(7)

By selecting the best-performing summary from multiple fine-tuned models, the framework leverages the complementary strengths of different transformer architectures. This adaptive strategy improves robustness and increases the likelihood of producing high-quality summaries across diverse input articles.

VI
Conclusion

This study presented a multi-model adaptive summarization framework designed to improve the quality and robustness of news text summarization on the CNN/DailyMail dataset. Unlike traditional approaches that rely on a single summarization model, the proposed framework integrates multiple transformer-based models to generate several candidate summaries for each input article. An automatic evaluation mechanism based on ROUGE-L, BLEU, and BERTScore is then used to enable the system to select the most informative summary through an adaptive selection strategy.

Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed framework consistently outperforms the individual base-line and fine-tuned summarization models in terms of overall summarization quality. By leveraging the complementary strengths of different transformer architectures, the framework produces more reliable and coherent summaries across diverse news articles. The adaptive selection mechanism enables the system to dynamically identify the most suitable candidate summary for each input article, thereby improving the robustness and consistency of the generated outputs compared with single-model approaches. Furthermore, when compared with recent related works, the proposed framework achieves superior performance over several competitive large models, including GPT3-D2 and T0, and remains highly competitive with strong approaches such as PEGASUS-xsum and BRIO. In particular, it achieves the highest BERTScore among all compared methods, highlighting its strong semantic alignment and contextual fidelity in generated summaries.

Future work may explore the integration of more advanced evaluation metrics, including reference-free and human-aligned evaluation methods, to improve the reliability of the selection mechanism. Furthermore, incorporating additional large language models and exploring lightweight model adaptation techniques could further enhance the scalability and effectiveness of the proposed framework for large-scale summarization tasks.
