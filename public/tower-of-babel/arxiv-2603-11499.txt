Nonlinear spin-motive force driven by mixed-space quantum geometry Preprint: APS/123-QED
Tomonari Meguro 1, Hiroaki Ishizuka 2, Kentaro Nomura 1, 3

Abstract
Spin-motive force, i.e., the electric current induced by magnetization dynamics, is theoretically studied beyond the Thouless-pump paradigm. In contrast to the linear-response regime, where the induced current is purely AC, we show that spin-motive force acquires both a DC component and a second-harmonic component at nonlinear order in magnetization dynamics. We further clarify that both contributions originate from the geometric properties of electronic bands — quantum geometry defined in the mixed parameter space ( 𝒌 , 𝒎 ) ({\bm{k}},{\bm{m}}) spanned by electron’s momentum 𝒌 {\bm{k}} and magnetization 𝒎 {\bm{m}} . By applying the theory to a Luttinger model, we demonstrate that our mechanism yields a finite nonlinear current even in the insulating regime, and the resulting electrical signal is measurable in a conventional current-measurement setup. Our findings offer a new operating principle of AC-to-DC conversion with magnetic materials, highlighting the pivotal role of the ( 𝒌 , 𝒎 ) ({\bm{k}},{\bm{m}}) -mixed space quantum geometry in magnetization-dynamics-induced electric currents.

Our findings suggest that quantum geometry in the mixed space plays a pivotal role in the field of nonlinear spintronics.

II
Wave-packet theory

II.1
Wave-packet

To study the effect of magnetization dynamics on electron dynamics, we consider a Hamiltonian that consists of a static term and time-dependent terms which are treated perturbatively. The Hamiltonian reads,

\displaystyle{\hat{H}}(t)={\hat{H}}^{(0)}+\sum_{\beta}\delta m_{\beta}(t){\hat{H}}^{\prime}_{\beta}.

(1)

where
\beta=x,y,z

In the time-dependent parts,

m_{0}=|\bm{m}_{0}|

The time-evolution of the wave packet is determined by a variational method. To this end, we consider a situation such that the slowly-varying potential in

\displaystyle\sigma^{\rm surf}_{i;\alpha\beta}(0;\omega,-\omega)

III.4.3
Interpretations of results

In previous studies, currents arising from the Berry curvature have been investigated primarily in the linear regime with respect to
\delta{\bm{m}}(t)
in the adiabatic regime
8
;
38
;
23
.
It arises from using the unperturbed Berry curvature
{\Omega}^{\bm{k}\bm{m}(0)}_{n}
as
\tilde{\Omega}^{\bm{k}\bm{m}}_{n}
in Eq. (
21
).
It is nothing but the displacement current for
\delta\bm{m}(t)
which is periodic in time.
In our formalism, the contribution from
\delta\bm{m}(t)
in the perturbation theory tunes the Bloch wavefunction,
and hence the expression of the Berry curvature. This leads to Eqs. (
34
), (
38
) and can be interpreted as the extension of the formula in the adiabatic regime.

The direct current induced by the Berry curvature contribution,
\sigma^{\mathrm{BC}}(0;\omega,-\omega)
, is proportional to
m_{\alpha}\dot{m}_{\beta}-m_{\beta}\dot{m}_{\alpha}
, as
m_{\alpha}\dot{m}_{\beta}+m_{\beta}\dot{m}_{\alpha}
term vanishes after taking the time integral.
As a consequence, the induced current is proportional to the area swept by
\bm{m}(t)
.
To see this, we note that the time dependence of this contribution arises from terms proportional to the product
m_{\alpha}\dot{m}_{\beta}
, whose antisymmetric component,
\bm{m}\times\dot{\bm{m}}
, corresponds to
\sigma^{\mathrm{BC}}(0;\omega,-\omega)
.
In a precessional motion, the magnetization traces a closed trajectory in
\bm{m}
-space, and
\bm{m}\times\dot{\bm{m}}
corresponds to the area enclosed by this curve.
Thus, when the magnetization dynamics forms such a closed loop, the
\sigma^{\mathrm{BC}}(0;\omega,-\omega)
contribution is proportional to the area.

By contrast, the symmetric component,
m_{\alpha}\dot{m}_{\beta}+m_{\beta}\dot{m}_{\alpha}
, yields terms proportional to
\cos 2\omega t
and
\sin 2\omega t
, i.e., it contributes to the SHG through
\sigma^{\mathrm{BC}}(2\omega;\omega,\omega)
term.
Mathematically, this type of product can be written as a total time derivative,
\frac{d}{dt}(m_{\alpha}m_{\beta})
.
Upon integrating the current over time, one obtains a contribution of the form
m_{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{end}})m_{\beta}(t_{\mathrm{end}})-m_{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{initial}})m_{\beta}(t_{\mathrm{initial}})
, where
t_{\mathrm{initial/end}}
is beginning or end of the time integral.
This term vanishes when the magnetization trajectory is closed as discussed above.
On the other hand, it yields a finite value for an open trajectory, corresponding to the oscillatory components at frequency
2\omega
.

In addition to the Berry curvature contribution,
we find contributions arising from a non-adiabatic effect (Eqs. (
III.4.1
) and (
III.4.2
)). These contributions are related to the normalized Berry connection polarizability. The dynamical correction proportional to
\delta\dot{\bm{m}}(t)
in perturbation theory is responsible for the virtual inter-band mixing for the Bloch wavefunction, which is presented in Eqs. (
III.3
) and (
III.3
).
This leads to the correction beyond the adiabatic regime
\sim{\mathcal{O}}(\omega)
, which can be confirmed by the factor
\sim{\mathcal{O}}(\omega^{2})
in Eqs. (
III.4.1
) and (
III.4.2
).
The above discussion is analogous to the one for the intrinsic nonlinear Hall effect
10
;
35
.
In that situation, the electric field, the temporal dynamics of the vector potential, mixes the Bloch wavefunctions of different bands, which leads to the appearance of the the Berry connection polarizability in the
\bm{k}
-space.
In our situation, the Berry connection polarizability in the
(\bm{k},\bm{m})
-mixed space arises from the temporal dynamics of the magnetization.

The current arising from the Berry connection polarizability appears through the product
\dot{m}_{\alpha}\dot{m}_{\beta}
.
For a precessing magnetization, this product explicitly takes the form
(1+\cos 2\omega t)
for
\alpha=\beta
, or
\sin 2\omega t
for
\alpha\neq\beta
.
The constant factor 1 in the former case, which corresponds to the time-averaged magnitude of the magnetization dynamics, gives rise to
\sigma^{\mathrm{BCP}}(0;\omega,-\omega)
, while the time-dependent deviation from it represents
\sigma^{\mathrm{BCP}}(2\omega;\omega,\omega)
.
The time dependence of
\sigma^{\mathrm{BCP}}
is governed not so much by a geometric interpretation in terms of whether the magnetization traces a closed loop, but rather by the temporal dynamics of the magnetization itself.

V
Discussion and Conclusion

In this work, we theoretically proposed the intrinsic nonlinear SMF which arises from the quantum geometry in the
({\bm{k}},{\bm{m}})
-mixed space.
We formulated the electron dynamics in the ferromagnets by the semiclassical wave-packet formalism up to the second order of the amplitude of the time-varying magnetization.
As a result, we found that the nonlinear SMF has two contributions; the DC term and the SHG term.
Both of them can be written in terms of the Berry curvature and the quantum metric in the
({\bm{k}},{\bm{m}})
-mixed.
To illustrate the significance of the nonlinear SMF,
we performed numerical calculations for a model of two-dimensional magnetic semiconductors.
As a result, we found that, even in the insulating regime, the nonlinear SMF shows a finite value, and that value can be detected by the usual current ammeter.

The nonlinear SMF we propose is expected to exist broadly in various materials, regardless of whether they are metallic, insulating, or semiconducting.
As strategies for observing or enhancing the nonlinear SMF, we first suggest exploring materials or junction systems in which the
({\bm{k}},{\bm{m}})
-space quantum geometry is already known to exhibit a nontrivial structure, as inferred from studies on current-induced spin-orbit torques
19
;
7
;
13
;
42
.
Second, one may focus on materials with topologically nontrivial band structures or with band degeneracy points. In this sense, magnetic Weyl semimetals provide a promising platform
24
and constitute the subject of our future studies.
More recently, MnBi
2
Te
4
has also been proposed as an intriguing candidate system
38
;
28
.

In our calculations, we assume that we can fix the gauge of the Berry connection in the
\bm{m}
-space to zero globally.
Under this gauge condition, linear-response current, Thouless pump, does not arise.
Since the main focus of this study is on nonlinear response, we intentionally restrict ourselves to situations where the Thouless pump is absent, and hence the nonlinear contribution becomes dominant.
On the other hand, for materials with nontrivial band structures where
a gauge cannot sets the
\bm{m}
-space Berry connection to zero globally,
it becomes necessary to evaluate the first-order correction to the Berry connection without relying on the above orthogonality condition.
The results for such cases will be reported elsewhere
4
4

4

T. Meguro, H. Ishizuka, and K. Nomura, in preparation.

.

Finally, we emphasize the crucial role of quantum geometry in the electric response driven by magnetization dynamics.
The usefulness of the
({\bm{k}},{\bm{m}})
-mixed space quantum geometry has been discussed primarily in the context of current-induced spin-orbit torques which is the charge-to-spin conversion

7
;
44
;
42
.
Although preceding studies have shown that the reciprocal process, SMF, is written by the Berry curvature in mixed space, it is limited to the linear response regime and hence AC.
It was also unknown how the quantum metric affects the conversion process from the magnetic dynamics to electric current.
Our study demonstrates that the SMF in the nonlinear regime can convert injected frequency into DC rectification and SHG, and their geometric origins are distinguished by their frequency scaling.
This suggests that
our mechanism serves as a new operating principle for an AC-to-DC converter, governed solely by the electron’s geometric property in magnetic materials.
Our finding not only forms the basis for exploring rich intrinsic nonlinear electric responses in dynamical magnetic systems, but also serves as a building block for the emerging field of nonlinear spintronics.

(\bm{x}_{c},\bm{k}_{c},t)
-dependence of the coefficients for notational simplicity.
The wave-packet follows the time-dependent Schrödinger equation.

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}\ket{\Psi_{{\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{k}}_{c}}}=\Bigl({\hat{H}}^{(0)}+{\hat{V}}(t)\Bigr)\ket{\Psi_{{\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{k}}_{c}}},

(45)

where
{\hat{V}}(t)=\sum_{\beta}\delta m_{\beta}(t){\hat{H}}^{\prime}_{\beta}
.
Substituting the wave-packet wavefunction, Eq. (
3
), into Eq. (
45
),
and multiplying the Bloch eigenstate
\bra{\psi^{(0)}_{m\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{m}_{0})}
on both sides of the equation,
we get the following differential equation,

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}C_{n\bm{k}}\delta_{mn}+i\hbar\sum_{l\neq n}\partial_{t}C_{l\bm{k}}\delta_{ml}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{(0)}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{x}_{c},\bm{m}_{0})C_{n\bm{k}}\delta_{mn}+\sum_{l(\neq n)}\varepsilon^{(0)}_{l\bm{k}}(\bm{x}_{c},\bm{m}_{0})C_{l\bm{k}}\delta_{ml}+({\hat{V}}(t))_{ln}C_{n\bm{k}}+\sum_{l(\neq n)}({\hat{V}}(t))_{ml}C_{l\bm{k}},

(46)

Here, we use the orthogonal relation,

\braket{u^{(0)}_{m\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{m}_{0})|u^{(0)}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{m}_{0})}=\delta(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\delta_{mn}
.
One can separate Eq.(
46
) into the intra-band contribution and inter-band contribution (
m\neq n
),

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}C_{n\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{(0)}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{x}_{c},\bm{m}_{0})C_{n\bm{k}}+({\hat{V}}(t))_{nn}C_{n\bm{k}}+\sum_{l(\neq n)}({\hat{V}}(t))_{nl}C_{l\bm{k}},

(47)

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}C_{m\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{(0)}_{m\bm{k}}(\bm{x}_{c},\bm{m}_{0})C_{m\bm{k}}+({\hat{V}}(t))_{mn}C_{n\bm{k}}+\sum_{l(\neq n)}({\hat{V}}(t))_{ml}C_{l\bm{k}}.

(48)

We need to solve the second differential equation, Eq. (
48
).
To proceed, we assume that
C_{l{\bm{k}}}
for
l\neq n
differs from
C_{n{\bm{k}}}
only by an overall factor
M_{ln,\bm{k}}
,

\displaystyle C_{l{\bm{k}}}

\displaystyle=M_{ln,\bm{k}}~C_{n\bm{k}}.

(49)

Then, substituting Eq.(
49
) into Eq.(
48
), and after straightforward algebra, we arrive at

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}M_{ln,\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{(0)}_{ln,\bm{k}}(\bm{x}_{c},\bm{m}_{0})M_{ln,\bm{k}}+({\hat{V}}(t))_{ln}-({\hat{V}}(t))_{nn}M_{ln,\bm{k}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m(\neq n)}({\hat{V}}(t))_{lm}M_{mn,\bm{k}}-\sum_{m(\neq n)}M_{ln,\bm{k}}({\hat{V}}(t))_{nm}M_{mn,\bm{k}},

(50)

The coefficient
M_{ln,\bm{k}}
is expanded in powers of the precession amplitude as
M_{ln,\bm{k}}=\sum_{N\geq 1}M^{(N)}_{ln,\bm{k}}
, and
M^{(N)}_{ln,\bm{k}}\propto\mathcal{O}(\delta m^{N})
.
Noting that
{\hat{H}}^{\prime}_{\beta}
is already
{\mathcal{O}}(\delta m^{1})
, the differential equation for
M^{(1)}_{ln,\bm{k}}
reads,

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}M^{(1)}_{ln,\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{(0)}_{ln,\bm{k}}(\bm{x}_{c},\bm{m}_{0})M^{(1)}_{ln,\bm{k}}+({\hat{V}}(t))_{ln}.

(51)

Employing the Fourier transformations for the time-varying magnetization and
M^{(1)}_{ln,\bm{k}}
,

\displaystyle\delta\bm{m}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Omega}\delta\bm{m}_{\Omega}e^{-i\Omega t},

(52)

\displaystyle M^{(1)}_{ln,\bm{k}}(\bm{x}_{c},\bm{k}_{c},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Omega}M^{(1)}_{ln,\bm{k}}(\bm{x}_{c},\bm{k}_{c},\Omega)e^{-i\Omega t}

(53)

we find that the solution of Eq.(
51
) reads,

\displaystyle M^{(1)}_{ln,\bm{k}}(\bm{x}_{c},\bm{k}_{c},t)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\Omega}\frac{\delta m_{\Omega,\beta}~e^{-i\Omega t}}{\varepsilon^{(0)}_{ln,\bm{k}}(\bm{x}_{c},\bm{m}_{0})-\hbar\Omega}({\hat{H}}^{\prime}_{\beta})_{ln}.

(54)

Appendix D
Definition of normalized quantum geometric tensor

Since the normalized quantum geometric quantities are used both in the main text and in this Appendix,
we summarize them here.
To this end, we consider the situation as the same as Sec.
III
in the main text, where
\bm{x}_{c}
-dependence no longer exists.
The conventional Quantum geometric tensor is defined as

{\mathcal{Q}}^{\bm{\xi}\bm{\xi}^{\prime},nl}_{\mu\nu}={\mathcal{A}}^{\bm{\xi},nl}_{\mu}{\mathcal{A}}^{\bm{\xi}^{\prime},ln}_{\nu}
, where
{\mathcal{A}}^{\bm{\xi},nl}_{\mu}=-i\bra{u^{(0)}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{m}_{0})}\partial_{\xi_{\mu}}\ket{u^{(0)}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{m}_{0})}
.
On the other hand, the “normalized” quantum geometric tensor is defined as

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{Q}}}^{\bm{\xi}\bm{\xi}^{\prime},nl}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\varepsilon^{(0)}_{nl,\bm{k}}(\bm{m}_{0}))^{2}}{(\varepsilon^{(0)}_{nl,\bm{k}}(\bm{m}_{0}))^{2}-\hbar^{2}\omega^{2}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\bm{\xi},nl}_{\mu}{\mathcal{A}}^{\bm{\xi}^{\prime},ln}_{\nu}.

(73)

As same as the conventional quantum geometric tensor, the real (imaginary) part of
{\overline{\mathcal{Q}}}^{\bm{\xi}\bm{\xi}^{\prime},nl}_{\mu\nu}
corresponds to the quantum metric (Berry curvature),

\displaystyle{\overline{g}}^{\bm{\xi}\bm{\xi}^{\prime}}_{n,\mu\nu}

\displaystyle={\rm Re}\left[\sum_{l(\neq n)}{\overline{\mathcal{Q}}}^{\bm{\xi},nl}_{\mu\nu}\right]

\displaystyle={\rm Re}\left[\sum_{l(\neq n)}\frac{(\varepsilon^{(0)}_{nl,\bm{k}}(\bm{m}_{0}))^{2}{\mathcal{A}}^{{\bm{\xi}},nl}_{\mu}{\mathcal{A}}^{{\bm{\xi}^{\prime}},ln}_{\nu}}{(\varepsilon^{(0)}_{nl,\bm{k}}(\bm{m}_{0}))^{2}-\hbar^{2}\omega^{2}}\right],

(74)

\displaystyle{\overline{\Omega}}^{\bm{\xi}\bm{\xi}^{\prime}}_{n,\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-2{\rm Im}\left[\sum_{l(\neq n)}{\overline{\mathcal{Q}}}^{\bm{\xi}\bm{\xi}^{\prime},nl}_{\mu\nu}\right]

\displaystyle=-2{\rm Im}\left[\sum_{l(\neq n)}\frac{(\varepsilon^{(0)}_{nl,\bm{k}}(\bm{m}_{0}))^{2}{\mathcal{A}}^{{\bm{\xi}},nl}_{\mu}{\mathcal{A}}^{{\bm{\xi}^{\prime}},ln}_{\nu}}{(\varepsilon^{(0)}_{nl,\bm{k}}(\bm{m}_{0}))^{2}-\hbar^{2}\omega^{2}}.\right]

(75)

The Berry connection polarizability is defined as

\displaystyle{\overline{G}}^{\bm{\xi}\bm{\xi}^{\prime}}_{n,\mu\nu}

\displaystyle={\rm Re}\left[\sum_{l(\neq n)}\frac{\varepsilon^{(0)}_{nl}(\bm{m}_{0}){\mathcal{A}}^{{\bm{\xi}},nl}_{\mu}{\mathcal{A}}^{{\bm{\xi}^{\prime}},ln}_{\nu}}{(\varepsilon^{(0)}_{nl,\bm{k}}(\bm{m}_{0}))^{2}-\hbar^{2}\omega^{2}}\right].

(76)
