Implicit Style Conditioning: A Structured Style-Rewrite Framework for Low-Resource Character Modeling
Chanhui Zhu

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive capabilities in role-playing (RP); however, small Language Models (SLMs) with highly stylized personas remains a challenge due to data scarcity and the complexity of style disentanglement. Standard Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) often captures surface-level semantics while failing to reproduce the intricate syntactic and pragmatic nuances of a character, leading to "Out-Of-Character" (OOC) generation. To address this, we propose a Structured Style-Rewrite Framework that explicitly disentangles style into three interpretable dimensions: lexical signatures (via PMI), syntactic patterns (grounded in PCFG rules), and pragmatic style. Furthermore, we introduce an implicit style conditioning strategy via Chain-of-Thought (CoT) distillation. By leveraging explicit reasoning traces during training as a strong inductive bias, our approach aligns the model’s latent representations with structured style features, enabling high-fidelity stylized generation without requiring explicit reasoning tokens during inference. Extensive experiments on a specific high-stylization domain (anime characters) demonstrate that our method enables a Qwen-1.7B model to outperform significantly larger baselines (e.g., 4B Vanilla SFT) in style consistency and semantic fidelity. Our approach offers a data-efficient paradigm for democratizing inference and deployment on consumer hardware.



1
Introduction

Controllable dialogue generation
is a central problem in conversational AI,
particularly for character-driven applications such as animation, games, and fictional narratives.
Despite recent advances, existing dialogue models still struggle to reproduce

consistent and distinctive character styles
,
especially under low-resource conditions.

This difficulty stems from two fundamental challenges.
First,
character style is inherently high-dimensional
,
involving lexical preferences, syntactic patterns, pragmatic tendencies,
and idiosyncratic speech habits.
Second,
most fictional characters exist in extremely low-resource settings
,
where only a small number of utterances are available.
These constraints make it difficult to learn reliable style representations
or to train robust style-conditioned generation models.

Prior work on text style transfer and persona-based dialogue generation
has demonstrated promising results by controlling attributes such as sentiment,
register, or predefined persona traits

Gao et al. 2019
;
Hu et al. 2023
;
Smith et al. 2020
.
However, most existing approaches rely on

holistic latent embeddings
or large amounts of annotated data,
which limits their interpretability and effectiveness
in low-resource, fine-grained character modeling.
Moreover, prompt-based role-playing with large language models
often suffers from stylistic instability,
leading to high output variance and frequent

out-of-character (OOC)
behaviors.

To address these limitations, we propose a unified framework
that combines
structured style modeling

with a
rewrite-based data augmentation
strategy.
Instead of representing style as a single latent vector,
we decompose character style into a

composable multi-dimensional representation
,
covering lexical, syntactic, and pragmatic aspects.
To obtain reliable supervision under low-resource conditions,
we further introduce a
Context-Aware Style Refiner

that corrects noisy pseudo style labels by leveraging
both clustering-based style prototypes and contextual embeddings.
Conditioned on the resulting structured style vector,
a scalable rewrite pipeline transforms neutral utterances
into character-consistent dialogue text.

Contributions.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

1.

Structured Multi-Dimensional Style Representation (
S
).

We decompose character style into interpretable lexical, syntactic,
and pragmatic components and unify them into a compositional style vector,
enabling fine-grained control and interpretability
in low-resource character scenarios.

2.

Context-Aware Style Refinement under Few-Shot Conditions.

We propose a lightweight style refiner that corrects noisy pseudo labels
by integrating clustering-based style priors with contextual embeddings,
providing reliable multi-label style supervision.

3.

Rewrite-Based Data Augmentation for Controllable Generation.

We construct a scalable rewrite pipeline that converts neutral utterances
into character-stylized dialogue conditioned on the structured style vector,
enabling the creation of large, consistent synthetic datasets.

4.

Empirical Validation of Controllability and Generalization.

Extensive experiments demonstrate that our approach
outperforms retrieval-based systems, few-shot prompting,
and holistic embedding baselines
in terms of style consistency and semantic fidelity.

3
Method

3.1
Task Formulation

Given a neutral sentence
x
and a target style vector
S
, the character style transfer task aims to generate a stylized sentence:

y=f(x,S)

(1)

The generated sentence
y
is required to satisfy the following criteria:

•

Content Consistency
: the core semantic proposition is preserved with respect to
x
;

•

Style Consistency
: lexical, syntactic, pragmatic, and idiolectal characteristics conform to the style specified by
S
;

•

Fluency
: the output adheres to the grammatical and distributional properties of natural language.

This task corresponds to a
rewrite-based
variant of text style transfer
Gao et al. 2019
;
Hu et al. 2023
;
Smith et al. 2020
, where the objective is to rewrite the input sentence into a target character style while preserving its original content.

Our training data are constructed as synthetic parallel
(Neutral, Stylized)
pairs with a strict
1\!:\!1
ratio between neutral inputs and stylized targets; per-character pair counts and oversampling rates are reported in Appendix
F
, Section
F.2
, Table
14
.

In the proposed framework, the style input
S
is defined as a structured vector, formally represented as:

S=[\text{Lexical},\text{Syntactic},\text{Pragmatic}]

(2)

Each component of the structured style vector
S
can be further decomposed into multiple interpretable sub-dimensions. A brief definition of the style vector is shown in Table

.

3.2
Style Representation and Extraction

Style Lexicon Construction.

Inspired by Roy et al.
Roy et al. 2023
, to capture character-specific lexical preferences, we extract style-indicative keywords using a TF-weighted PMI scheme.
Compared to conventional PMI, the proposed variant incorporates term frequency to better distinguish idiolectal habits and high-frequency stylistic markers.
All style corpora are balanced to form a global reference distribution.

The resulting TF–PMI scores are used to construct the lexical component of the structured style vector
S
.
Formal definitions, vocabulary statistics, and the Top-25 TF-PMI words for each style characters are provided in Appendix
C
.

Syntactic Style Features.

Syntactic preferences are modeled using PCFG-based statistics
extracted from constituency parses.
To avoid sparse rule-level representations,
significant productions are aggregated into
a compact 13-dimensional syntactic style vector,
following principles of multidimensional grammatical analysis.
This representation captures dominant structural tendencies
while preserving interpretability.

Formal definitions of PCFG statistics,
log-likelihood ratios,
and the mapping procedure are provided in Appendix
D
.

Pragmatic Style Modeling.

For stylistically salient characters, pragmatic preferences are often pronounced. Instead of sequentially predicting pragmatic styles—which may propagate errors—a
style profile
is constructed as a soft conditioning signal.
This profile encodes the global distribution of pragmatic tendencies inferred from the character corpus, enabling large language models to dynamically select appropriate pragmatic strategies.

To ensure the reliability of these profiles, we trained a Context-Aware Style Refiner (details in Appendix
E
) to correct noisy pseudo-labels via clustering-based prototypes.
The refiner is applied to MuICE, Hutao in ChatHaruhi
Li et al. 2023
, and counselors from PsyDTCorpus
Xie et al. 2025
, yielding distinct pragmatic distributions (Appendix
E
, Section
E.6
, Table
13
).
These results demonstrate that the proposed approach effectively differentiates highly stylized characters from neutral professional roles, providing a reliable foundation for downstream generation experiments.

3.3
Conditional Style Transfer

3.3.1
Conditional LoRA with Style Prefix Injection

We adopt Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) as the basis for parameter-efficient fine-tuning.
Instead of directly modifying the backbone model parameters,
we inject structured style information through a learnable
style prefix
.

Style Encoder.

We introduce a lightweight neural encoder, denoted as
StyleEncoder
,
which maps a structured syntactic style vector into the hidden representation space
of the backbone language model.
Given a syntactic style vector
s_{\text{syn}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
the encoder produces a style embedding

\mathbf{e}_{\text{style}}=\text{StyleEncoder}(s_{\text{syn}}),

where
\mathbf{e}_{\text{style}}
matches the model hidden dimension.

Prefix Injection.

During the forward pass, the style embedding is prepended to the token embeddings
of the input sequence, forming a soft prompt that conditions generation.
This prefix is masked from the language modeling loss,
ensuring that it serves solely as a conditioning signal rather than a generation target.

By injecting style information as a prefix rather than as textual tokens,
the model is encouraged to internalize structured stylistic constraints
within its latent representations.

3.3.2
Instruction-Based Input Formulation

We formulate the task using an explicit instructional prompt containing character identity,
stylistic profiles, and the neutral input (see Appendix
K
for prompt templates).

This formulation reframes style transfer as an explicit transformation task
rather than a conversational response,
thereby reducing semantic leakage and stabilizing training.

3.3.3
Chain-of-Thought Supervision

To enable the model to handle styles that involve semantic inversion or implicit reasoning,
we incorporate
chain-of-thought (CoT) supervision
during training.
Each training target consists of a reasoning trace followed by the final stylized output.

This design encourages the model to explicitly reason about
how stylistic constraints interact with semantic content,
rather than relying on shallow pattern matching.
The language modeling objective is applied jointly to both the reasoning trace
and the final response.

3.3.4
Multi-Task Auxiliary Objectives

A known failure mode of conditional generation models
is the tendency to ignore non-textual conditioning signals
when sufficient stylistic information is present in the prompt.
To mitigate this issue, we introduce auxiliary learning objectives
that explicitly supervise the usage of the injected style prefix.

The overall training objective is defined as:

L_{\text{total}}=L_{\text{lm}}+\lambda_{\text{recon}}L_{\text{recon}}+\lambda_{\text{style}}L_{\text{style}},

(3)

where:

Language Modeling Loss (
L_{\text{lm}}
).

The standard autoregressive loss ensures fluency and content preservation
for both the reasoning trace and the stylized output.

Syntactic Reconstruction Loss (
L_{\text{recon}}
).

To enforce that the style prefix encodes structured syntactic information,
we reconstruct the original syntactic style vector from the hidden representation
of the prefix token.
This loss regularizes the model to preserve syntactic signals
within the latent style embedding.

Pragmatic Style Classification Loss (
L_{\text{style}}
).

To encourage the encoding of semantic-pragmatic style information,
we apply a multi-label classification loss over pragmatic style categories,
using predictions derived from the same prefix representation.

Together, these auxiliary objectives prevent the model from bypassing
the injected style prefix and promote faithful utilization
of structured style representations.

3.3.5
Discussion

By combining structured style injection, instruction-based task formulation,
and multi-task supervision, our framework enables controllable style transfer
under limited data conditions.
Crucially, the proposed design allows the model to generalize
from non-parallel style corpora while maintaining explicit control
over both syntactic and pragmatic style dimensions.

4.3
Analysis

This section provides an in-depth analysis of the experimental results, focusing on the trade-off between semantic fidelity and stylistic expressiveness, robustness under semantic constraints, and the conditioning of implicit style.

4.3.1
Style–Semantic Trade-off

The results reveal a fundamental trade-off faced by existing style transfer approaches.

Retrieval-based methods and semantic hallucination.

Baseline A (RAG) achieves a high raw Style Score (
0.88
), yet its Semantic Score drops to
0.50
, which falls below an acceptable threshold. This observation indicates that retrieval-based methods tend to reproduce retrieved utterances rather than performing genuine style-preserving rewriting. Consequently, the extremely low Valid Style Score (
0.10
) suggests that stylistic gains are largely obtained at the cost of semantic collapse.

Semantic drift in vanilla fine-tuning.

Baseline C (Vanilla SFT) exhibits moderate semantic preservation (approximately
0.73
). Without explicit logical constraints, the model frequently distorts the original intent when attempting to imitate character styles, resulting in semantic drift.

Prompt-based methods under strong constraints.

Even with carefully engineered prompts, Baseline D demonstrates a persistent trade-off between semantic fidelity and stylistic richness. Although its Semantic Score improves to
0.77
, it remains substantially lower than that of Model v2 (
>0.83
). Moreover, maintaining semantic fidelity forces the model to adopt conservative stylistic strategies, leading to a decrease in raw Style Score compared with looser prompting variants.

Semantic anchoring in the proposed framework.

In contrast, Model v1 and Model v2 consistently exhibit strong semantic stability (
>0.83
). Although their raw Style Scores appear lower, this is attributed to the generation of novel utterances rather than the repetition of memorized expressions. Under the Valid Style Score metric, Model v2 achieves
0.4825
, representing a relative improvement of
33\%
over Baseline C. This result demonstrates that the proposed framework performs style transfer under the prerequisite of accurate intent preservation.

The “creative deviation” of the strong prompt baseline.

It is worth noting that in human evaluation (Table
4
), Baseline D achieved the highest Style and Overall scores. Although models with larger parameters possess inherent advantages in understanding character styles and imitating examples, we attribute this to a ’creativity bias’: annotators tend to favor the expansive and dramatic expressions typical of large models, even when they introduce subtle semantic hallucinations (see Appendix
O
and
P
). However, for tasks requiring strict semantic adherence (e.g., faithful rewriting), Model v2 offers a superior Pareto-optimal solution, significantly outperforming Baseline C while maintaining higher semantic fidelity than Baseline D.

4.3.2
Robustness under Semantic Constraints.

To examine whether the observed improvements depend on a specific semantic threshold, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by varying the penalty threshold
\tau\in\{0.70,0.75,0.80\}
.

Detailed scores for all models under each threshold are reported in Appendix
F.5
, Table
15
.

The results indicate that Model v2 (Inference-only) maintains a clear advantage across all threshold settings. Notably, when the constraint becomes stricter (
\tau=0.80
), the Valid Style Score decreases by only
12\%
, suggesting that semantic violations are rare in most generated outputs.

In contrast, Baseline C and Baseline B exhibit severe degradation under strict semantic constraints, with performance drops approaching
50\%
. This behavior exposes a latent weakness of conventional SFT approaches: stylistic imitation is often achieved by sacrificing semantic details, which fails under high-fidelity evaluation criteria.

4.3.3
Implicit Style Conditioning.

A notable observation is that Model v2 (Inference-only) achieves the highest Valid Style Score (
0.5687
), even without explicit reasoning outputs during inference.

This result suggests that chain-of-thought supervision functions not merely as a test-time reasoning aid, but as an effective training signal that enables the model to demonstrate that CoT supervision acts as a strong inductive bias, aligning the model’s latent representations with structured style features (Representation Alignment). Through parameter updates, the model absorbs the multi-step reasoning process into its internal representations, allowing complex style transfer decisions to be executed implicitly.

Compared with Baseline C, Model v2 (Inference-only) achieves a
56\%
improvement in effective style transfer while maintaining a Semantic Score of
0.878
. This finding provides empirical support for the reasoning compression hypothesis proposed by
Deng et al. 2024
, where explicit reasoning traces are compressed into latent representations during training.

While it remains an open question whether the model explicitly executes internalized reasoning steps, the empirical results suggest that training with explicit CoT constraints makes the style features more linearly accessible in the latent space, leading to better style-semantic alignment during direct generation.

4.3.4

To validate zero-shot generalization on out-of-distribution characters, we conducted a case study on Frieren with an extreme cold-start setting (

As detailed in Appendix
G.1
, our pipeline successfully extracted signature lexical markers (e.g., restrictive particles) and structural habits (high modifier density) without overfitting.
Crucially, Model v2 accurately injected these abstract features into rewritten outputs while strictly preserving semantic fidelity and avoiding background hallucination (see Appendix

This confirms that our framework enables explicit and safe stylistic steering rather than memorizing character knowledge.

The style auto-completion effect in syntax.

Rather than suggesting that syntactic modeling is redundant, we attribute this to two underlying phenomena.

Weight imprinting via CoT distillation.

During the multi-task fine-tuning stage, the small language model (SLM) develops a strong associative mapping between the target character’s identity and its underlying linguistic patterns.
Because the model was heavily penalized for structural deviations during training via the auxiliary reconstruction loss, it has effectively internalized these syntactic habits into its parameter space.
Consequently, during inference the model exhibits a “style auto-completion” capability, autonomously reconstructing missing structural tendencies from the contextual and lexical cues provided.

Classifier saliency bias.

The marginal drop also highlights a known limitation of current automated style evaluators.
Discriminator-based metrics naturally exhibit a saliency bias, heavily weighting high-frequency lexical tokens (e.g., catchphrases or modal particles) and pragmatic tone, while remaining relatively insensitive to deep structural variations (e.g., PCFG dependencies).

These findings confirm that, while explicit syntactic prompting can be relaxed during inference for efficiency, decomposing and enforcing these structural dimensions during training is fundamental to shaping the model’s robust stylistic latent space.

5
Conclusion

We present a unified framework for low-resource character style transfer that decomposes character style into interpretable lexical,
syntactic, and pragmatic dimensions, combined with a rewrite-based data augmentation pipeline and implicit Chain-of-Thought (CoT) supervision.
Experiments demonstrate that our approach achieves a Pareto-optimal balance between semantic fidelity and stylistic expressiveness,
significantly improving Valid Style Score over both retrieval-based and vanilla SFT baselines while maintaining high semantic consistency.
The structured style vector further enables zero-shot generalization to unseen characters under extreme cold-start conditions (N=25),
confirming that the model captures abstract stylistic tendencies rather than memorizing surface expressions.
Finally, CoT distillation allows complex style reasoning to be absorbed into model parameters, eliminating explicit reasoning tokens at inference time and reducing deployment overhead.
These findings suggest that combining explicit structural constraints with implicit reasoning internalization is a promising paradigm for controllable,
low-hallucination style generation on consumer hardware.

Limitations

Contextual limitations.

This work primarily focuses on sentence-level or short-turn style rewriting. In real-world role-playing scenarios, character consistency often depends on long-term memory and cross-turn style evolution. The current structured style vector
S
is statically extracted and does not yet incorporate dynamic adjustments based on dialogue history. This limitation mainly affects long-form multi-turn interactions, whereas the present study emphasizes controlled sentence-level rewriting.

Completeness of style features.

Although the proposed lexical, syntactic, and pragmatic dimensions cover the major aspects of character style, certain subtle phenomena—such as sarcasm, wordplay, or culturally specific metaphors—may not be fully captured by the current statistical features. This limitation may lead to partial style loss for characters whose personas rely heavily on implicit or subtextual cues.

Limitations of PCFG rule dimensionality.

The choice of 13 PCFG production-rule dimensions is based on linguistic intuition and achieves approximately 80% coverage across the evaluated style corpora. However, this mapping has not been systematically validated on characters with substantially different stylistic distributions. Future work should explore adaptive or data-driven adjustments of this hyperparameter.

Lack of truly neutral source sentences.

Most publicly available Chinese dialogue corpora (e.g., LCCC) are collected from social media and exhibit issues such as temporal bias, context fragmentation, and strong Internet slang tendencies. As a result, constructing fully neutral and style-free source sentences (
x
) is extremely challenging. Despite extensive cleaning and LLM-based neutral rewriting, the training data inevitably retains a weak implicit “netizen style,” which may slightly interfere with stylistic purity.

Scarcity of style corpora.

High-quality role-playing corpora are significantly scarcer than general-domain data. This study primarily relies on ChatHaruhi-Expand-118K
(
Li et al. 2023
)
, whose coverage is largely concentrated in the ACG domain. Such distributional bias may limit generalization to non-fictional characters (e.g., historical figures or professionals). At the same time, this scarcity highlights the value of our rewriting-based data augmentation pipeline, which enables the construction of high-quality synthetic style corpora from minimal seeds.

Bias in evaluation metrics.

As observed in the LLM-as-a-Judge experiments, existing automatic metrics and LLM-based evaluators still exhibit biases when balancing fluency and faithfulness. Developing a more objective evaluation framework that can reliably detect
style hallucination
remains an open challenge for the community.

Subjectivity in Human Evaluation

Although we employed multiple annotators, the evaluation of ’role-playing quality’ is inherently subjective, as different users may have varying interpretations of a character’s persona, reflected in the fair agreement rates. How to establish a style standard that can be defined and quantified by humans remains a subject requiring in-depth research.

Limitations of implicit reasoning

Despite its advantages, implicit reasoning does not universally replace explicit chain-of-thought. In scenarios requiring extreme pragmatic inversion (e.g., strong
tsundere
expressions), explicit reasoning remains beneficial. In most practical deployments, however, inference-only decoding offers a more efficient and robust trade-off between performance and cost.

Appendix B
Stability Analysis of Structured Style Vectors

A natural question for our low-resource framework is whether a small corpus (e.g.,
N=25
utterances) can statistically represent the full persona style profile.
To answer this, we conduct an N-shot stability analysis on the structured style vector.
Because exact-match set metrics (e.g., Jaccard) can over-penalize synonymous style markers under small-sample variance,
we adopt an embedding-based composite similarity.

Specifically, we encode extracted discrete features from the
Lexical
and
Pragmatic
modules using BGE-large-zh-v1.5,
then average them into high-dimensional centroids for soft semantic alignment.
For the
Syntactic
module, we retain the original 13-dimensional continuous PCFG probability vector and measure cosine similarity directly.
We then evaluate module-wise similarities and the composite similarity
\cos_{S}
against the full-corpus reference vector.

Appendix C
TF–PMI for Lexical Style Feature Extraction

Inspired by Roy et al.
Roy et al. 2023
, a Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI)-based method is adopted to extract style-indicative lexical features. Specifically, proxy utterances are first collected from each style-specific corpus and tokenized using the HanLP
He and Choi 2021
Python library. During preprocessing, punctuation marks and a predefined list of Chinese stop words are removed.

However, the conventional PMI formulation exhibits limited discriminative power when handling
style-exclusive words
(i.e., tokens that appear only in a single style corpus). Such words often receive identical high PMI scores, resulting in a loss of ranking granularity. Moreover, standard PMI fails to adequately reflect term frequency (TF) within a style, which is a crucial indicator for capturing idiolectal habits and high-frequency stylistic expressions.

To address these limitations, a modified
TF-PMI scoring scheme
is introduced by incorporating logarithmically smoothed term frequency. The score is computed as:

\text{Score}(w,t)=\left(1+\log_{e}(\text{Count}_{t}(w))\right)\times I(w,t),

(4)

where
\text{Count}_{t}(w)
denotes the occurrence count of word
w
in style
t
, and
I(w,t)
represents the PMI between the word and the style:

I(w,t)=\log_{2}\frac{P(w\mid t)}{P(w)}.

(5)

Here,
P(w\mid t)
is the probability of observing
w
in style
t
, and
P(w)
denotes its probability in the global corpus.

To establish a stable reference that distinguishes
different character styles
rather than merely separating “anime” from “non-anime” language, all style corpora (MuICE, Ayaka, Zhongli, Hutao, PsyDTCorpus, etc.) are combined after balanced down-sampling to form the global corpus. In addition, filtering thresholds are applied to remove overly common words (e.g.,
P(w)>10\%
) and rare noisy words (e.g.,
P(w\mid t)<0.01\%
).

TF-PMI scores are computed across five distinct style corpora, and the most representative style-indicative words are selected. Vocabulary statistics and the Top-25 TF-PMI words for each style are shown in Table
7
.

As shown in Table
7
, the enhanced TF-PMI method effectively captures distinctive stylistic characteristics across domains. For example,
t_{\text{MuICE}}
exhibits emotionally charged idiolectal markers (e.g., “喵”, “沐沐”), while
t_{\text{Ayaka}}
reflects a more formal register aligned with its narrative background. Although
t_{\text{Zhongli}}
and
t_{\text{Hutao}}
both belong to the “璃月” register, TF-PMI clearly differentiates their styles, contrasting Hutao’s playful expressions (e.g., “嘿嘿”) with Zhongli’s solemn lexical choices (e.g., “契约”, “帝君”). In contrast,
t_{\text{PsyDTCorpus}}
highlights empathetic and rational language characteristic of psychological counseling contexts.

Overall, the TF–PMI formulation preserves the interpretability of PMI-based methods while improving discriminability through frequency weighting.
This lexical representation serves as a transparent and controllable component of the structured style vector.

Syntactic Pattern Mapping.

To capture abstract syntactic style while avoiding sparse rule-level representations, a feature construction strategy combining
statistical significance filtering
and
linguistic aggregation
is proposed. High-dimensional PCFG rules are compressed into a compact
N=13
-dimensional syntactic style vector.

Following the tradition of multidimensional grammatical analysis
Biber 1988
, stylistic variation is modeled via distributions over interpretable structural features rather than individual rules. The mapping from PCFG rules to syntactic dimensions is manually derived based on dependency grammar principles
Tesnière 1959
and Penn Treebank annotation guidelines.

For a target corpus
C
, the
i
-th dimension of its syntactic vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{13}
is computed as:

v_{i}=\frac{\sum_{r\in\mathcal{D}_{i}}\text{Count}(r)}{\sum_{j=1}^{13}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\text{Count}(r)},

(9)

where
\mathcal{D}_{i}
denotes the set of rules mapped to the
i
-th dimension.

The choice of
N=13
balances interpretability and coverage, capturing
79.5\%
of observed rules (Table
11
). Unmapped rules are treated as
general syntax
and absorbed implicitly by the base language model, prioritizing stability over exhaustive coverage.

Dataset Construction

To construct reliable supervision for style refinement,
we curate a gold-standard dataset consisting of
731

manually annotated utterances.
Among them,
444
samples are collected from existing
anime-style dialogue corpora,
including MuICE, ChatHaruhi
(

)
, PsyDTCorpus
(
Xie et al. 2025
)
,
and other fragmented materials collected from the internet.
Due to limited character and style coverage,
the remaining
287
samples are generated using large language models
and subsequently filtered and re-annotated by human annotators
to reduce annotation bias.

To alleviate severe label imbalance,
we apply oversampling to style labels
with fewer than
23
instances,
resulting in a balanced training set
of
1{,}024
samples.

E.3
Model Architecture and Training

The Context-Aware Style Refiner is implemented
as a lightweight multi-layer perceptron (MLP)
with a single hidden layer.
The model takes as input the concatenation of
utterance embeddings,
context embeddings,
and cluster-based style prototype representations.

Training is performed using the Adam optimizer.
Early stopping is applied based on validation loss
to prevent overfitting.
To address label imbalance in the multi-label setting,
we employ a weighted binary cross-entropy loss,
where higher weights are assigned to rare style labels.

E.4
Evaluation Metrics

Given the multi-label and imbalanced nature of the task,
we adopt the
macro-averaged F1 score

as the primary evaluation metric.
This metric equally weights all labels
and is therefore sensitive to performance on rare styles.

In addition, we apply
dynamic per-label threshold optimization
,
where decision thresholds are independently tuned
on the validation set
to maximize the F1 score for each style label.

E.5
Extended Results and Ablation Studies

Under a fixed threshold setting,
the proposed refiner achieves a macro-F1 score of
0.715
.
With dynamic per-label threshold optimization,
performance further improves to
0.79
,
indicating that contextual refinement
substantially enhances label reliability
in low-resource settings.

To assess the contribution of each design choice,
we compare the proposed model against two intuitive baselines.

Centroid-Only Classification.

This baseline assigns style labels
based solely on cosine similarity
between response embeddings and style centroids,
without considering conversational context.
The model yields a macro-F1 score of
0.10
,
demonstrating that static style prototypes
are insufficient for context-dependent style inference.

End-to-End Input–Output Classification.

This variant directly predicts style labels
from concatenated context and response embeddings,
bypassing centroid-based style priors.
Despite its higher representational capacity,
this model underperforms the proposed approach,
suggesting that explicitly separating raw style signals
from contextual representations
leads to more stable learning
under limited data.

Appendix H
Data Sources, Licensing, and Ethical Considerations

This study exclusively uses publicly available datasets or fully synthetic data curated by the authors. No proprietary, private, or personally identifiable data were collected or used.

Data Sources.

We draw upon the following data sources:

•

Fictional Character Dialogue Dataset.

An internally curated corpus of fictional character dialogues released for research purposes. The dataset consists of transformed or AI-generated dialogue samples designed to avoid direct reproduction of copyrighted scripts. It is primarily used for training and evaluation of style representation and transfer.

•

PsyDTCorpus
(Xie et al., ACL 2025).
A publicly released dataset under the ACL Anthology license, used solely as a reference for syntactic and pragmatic pattern analysis. No samples from this dataset are used to train the style generation model.

•

ChatHaruhi-Expand-118K
(Li et al., 2023).
A publicly available persona-oriented dialogue dataset released for research use. Only the textual content is used, and no proprietary scripts or restricted materials are included.

Synthetic Data.

In addition to the above datasets, we construct a small set of
fully synthetic samples
(287 instances) generated by Qwen-series models
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
. These samples are manually reviewed and rewritten to ensure factual consistency, copyright safety, and stylistic correctness. The synthetic data are used exclusively for supervising style label calibration and are not derived from any real user data.

Licensing and Copyright Considerations.

All datasets used in this study are either publicly released for research purposes or fully synthetic. We do not include, reproduce, or train on copyrighted anime scripts, commercial dialogue corpora, or unpublished materials. The use of all datasets complies with their respective licenses and with ACL data usage policies.

Ethical Considerations.

This work focuses exclusively on
fictional characters
. The proposed framework models linguistic style patterns rather than real individuals, and it is not intended for impersonation of real persons. No user data are collected or processed. All data are anonymized, synthetic, or fictional in nature. As the study does not involve human subjects, it does not require institutional review board (IRB) approval.

Appendix I
Data Processing and Safety

All datasets used in this study undergo a unified data processing and safety pipeline before being used for analysis or model training. The goal of this pipeline is to ensure content safety, privacy preservation, and stylistic consistency under low-resource conditions.

Text Normalization.

All dialogue samples are normalized using Unicode standardization and token normalization. Residual markup artifacts are removed. Emojis and non-verbal symbols are preserved when they function as stylistic signals rather than semantic content.

Content Filtering.

We apply multiple filtering stages to remove unsafe or inappropriate content, including:

•

Explicit sexual content;

•

Personally identifiable information (PII);

•

Violent, hateful, or otherwise harmful expressions.

Only samples passing all filters are retained for subsequent processing.

Style Annotation and Refinement.

Initial pseudo-labels are generated using a combination of clustering-based heuristics and rule-based linguistic patterns. These labels are then refined by the proposed
Context-Aware Style Refiner
, which adjusts style annotations based on contextual embeddings to reduce noise and annotation bias.

Synthetic Data Safety.

The fully synthetic samples generated by Qwen-series models
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
are subject to additional manual inspection. Each sample is checked to ensure:

•

Absence of copyrighted phrases or direct quotations;

•

No hallucinated factual or narrative content beyond the input;

•

Alignment with predefined style dimensions;

•

No references to real individuals or private entities.

All processed data used in training and evaluation are therefore anonymized, fictional, or fully synthetic, and comply with ACL data safety and privacy requirements.

Appendix J
Reproducibility

To ensure reproducibility, we provide an anonymous repository containing all training and inference scripts, data preprocessing pipelines, and model checkpoints used in our experiments.

All datasets used in this work are either publicly available or fully synthetic. The structured style vectors can be reconstructed using the released feature extraction scripts.

Our code and data are publicly available at
https://anonymous.4open.science/r/OtakuLab-2E65/
.

Appendix K
Prompt Templates

This section documents the core prompt templates used for data construction, model training, baseline comparison, and automatic evaluation. All prompts are presented in a condensed and anonymized form to support reproducibility while avoiding unnecessary verbosity.

K.1
Neutralization Prompt for Data Construction

To construct parallel
(Neutral, Stylized)
sentence pairs, we employ a large language model to rewrite stylized character utterances into neutral sentences while preserving semantic content.

System Prompt.

You are a linguistic style analysis expert. Given a sentence with a specific character style, rewrite it into a semantically equivalent but stylistically neutral version.
Do not use emotional markers, discourse particles, rhetorical devices, or character-specific expressions.
Use plain written or spoken language without revealing speaker identity or personality.

This prompt is used with a small number of demonstration examples to guide neutralization. The resulting neutral sentences serve as semantic anchors for subsequent style-controlled rewriting.

K.2
Instructional Prompt for Style-Conditioned Generation

During training of Model v1 and Model v2, the structured style vector is converted into an instructional text format.

Input Format.

Target Character
: {character_id}

Pragmatic Styles
: {pragmatic_labels}

Lexical Keywords
: {lexical_keywords}

Neutral Content
: {neutral_sentence}

The model is instructed to generate a stylized sentence that preserves the original meaning while conforming to the specified style constraints.
For Model v1, intermediate reasoning steps are included during training; Model v2 omits explicit reasoning during inference.

K.3
Strong Prompting Baseline (Baseline D)

Baseline D uses
GLM-4.7

Team et al. 2025
as the backbone with 2-shot in-context examples.
Unlike purely instructed baselines, this baseline is supplied with
two components of the structured style vector
—the top-25 TF–PMI lexical keywords and the corpus-level pragmatic labels—as explicit textual constraints in the system prompt.
This design allows a fair comparison: the strong LLM has access to the same stylistic signals as our method, but lacks the syntactic vector and the implicit style conditioning mechanism.
Inference is conducted with temperature
=0.2
and top-
p=0.95
.

System Prompt Template.

You are a style transfer expert.
Your task is to rewrite the following neutral sentence into the style of {character},
based on the content of a neutral sentence.

Constraints:

1.

Keep the original meaning unchanged. Do
NOT
reply to it.

2.

Use keywords: {keywords}

3.

Adopt personality: {pragmatics}

where
{keywords}
is filled with the top-25 TF–PMI lexical keywords of the target character,
and
{pragmatics}
is filled with the corpus-level top-5 pragmatic style labels.

User Prompt Template.

Neutral Content: {neutral_sentence}

Rewritten Sentence:

In-Context Examples.

Two character-specific (neutral, stylized) pairs are prepended as few-shot demonstrations,
using the same style reference examples constructed for Baseline A (RAG+FS).
The assistant turns contain only the stylized target sentence without reasoning traces.

K.4
LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation Prompt

Automatic evaluation is conducted using an external large language model as a judge. The judge compares the neutral input and the generated stylized sentence under a given character profile.

The judge assigns scores (1–5) along three dimensions:

•

Semantic Faithfulness
: preservation of core meaning without hallucination;

•

Style Consistency
: alignment with the target character style;

•

Linguistic Naturalness
: fluency and appropriateness of expressions.

The judge is explicitly instructed to penalize hallucinated content and out-of-character behavior.

Output Format.

The evaluation output follows a structured JSON schema containing a brief justification and per-dimension scores.

K.5
Generation of CoT Traces

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning traces are generated automatically via few-shot prompting of a Teacher LLM.
We use
Qwen3-30B-A3B

(
Yang et al. 2025
)
(
qwen3-30b-a3b-instruct-2507
), a Mixture-of-Experts model with 30B total parameters and 3B active parameters, as the teacher.
Each trace is wrapped in a
<think>...</think>
block and is required to be no longer than 100 Chinese characters, concisely explaining the character’s emotion, motivation, and how they influence sentence structure, tone, and vocabulary.
Inference is conducted with temperature
=0.4
and top-
p=0.9
.

System Prompt.

You are a character style analyzer.
Given the neutral sentence and the stylized sentence below,
generate a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) for the character,
explaining step by step how the character transforms the neutral sentence
according to their dialogue style.
Output format:
<think>...</think>
.
Requirement: no more than 100 characters.
Clearly explain the character’s emotions and motivation,
and how these influence sentence structure, tone, and word choice.

User Prompt Template.

Each user-turn prompt is constructed from the following fields:

Character
: {character}

Neutral Sentence
: {neutral_sentence}

Stylized Sentence
: {stylized_sentence}

Keywords
: {lexical_keywords}

Character Style
: {pragmatic_labels}

where
lexical_keywords
are the top-25 TF–PMI keywords for that character,
and
pragmatic_labels
are the predicted pragmatic style tags for the specific stylized sentence.

Appendix P
Additional Error Analysis

P.1
Observed Failure Modes

While the proposed model performs robustly in most settings, we identify several recurring failure modes in qualitative inspection.

Ultra-short inputs
(e.g., single-clause utterances) may trigger mild over-generation, where the model introduces stylistic fillers that slightly exceed the minimal semantic requirement.

Modern or technical terms
occasionally lead to stylistic inconsistency when rewriting for characters with archaic or domain-specific language preferences. In such cases, the model tends to preserve the original term to avoid hallucination, at the cost of stylistic immersion.

P.2
Reasoning–Execution Mismatch

In rare cases, we observe a mismatch between the model’s internal style planning and the final surface realization. Specifically, the model may correctly identify stylistic strategies (e.g., sentence brevity or emotional restraint) but fail to consistently execute them in the generated output.

This phenomenon suggests that while the structured style representation improves controllability, further alignment between style planning and decoding remains an open challenge for future work.
