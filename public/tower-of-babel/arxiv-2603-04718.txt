Abstract
. In oral arguments, judges probe attorneys with questions about the factual record, legal claims, and the strength of their arguments. To prepare for this questioning, both law schools and practicing attorneys rely on moot courts: practice simulations of appellate hearings. Leveraging a dataset of U.S. Supreme Court oral argument transcripts, we examine whether AI models can effectively simulate justice-specific questioning for moot court-style training. Evaluating oral argument simulation is challenging because there is no single correct question for any given turn. Instead, effective questioning should reflect a combination of desirable qualities, such as anticipating substantive legal issues, detecting logical weaknesses, and maintaining an appropriately adversarial tone. We introduce a two-layer evaluation framework that assesses both the realism and pedagogical usefulness of simulated questions using complementary proxy metrics. We construct and evaluate both prompt-based and agentic oral argument simulators. We find that simulated questions are often perceived as realistic by human annotators and achieve high recall of ground truth substantive legal issues. However, models still face substantial shortcomings, including low diversity in question types and sycophancy. Importantly, these shortcomings would remain undetected under naive evaluation approaches.

Introduction

In
The Fight
, a documentary about the ACLU’s efforts to protect civil rights, Dale Ho (then a lawyer, now a judge) prepares for oral arguments by arguing his case out loud in front of a mirror, flipping over question-filled index cards at random to simulate justice interruptions.
Many attorneys with limited resources prepare for oral arguments in this way, with small, hand-crafted oral argument simulations.
On the other hand, well-resourced litigants can invest in more realistic moot courts, often by hiring former judges to conduct them
(
42
)
. Empirical work suggests that oral arguments can sometimes sway justices’ views
(
30
;
33
)
—summed up by the anecdote:
you might not be able to win a case via oral argument, but you surely can lose it

(
50
)
.

We envision a world where artificial intelligence helps level the playing field between well-resourced and underfunded attorneys by improving access to high-quality oral argument simulations. In qualitative interviews with public defenders,
10
report that preparing oral argument and cross-examination questions is viewed as a particularly promising application of AI in courtroom representation.
Taking inspiration from this work, we investigate whether AI models can effectively simulate justice-specific questioning for moot court-style training.
As a starting point, we focus on U.S. Supreme Court oral arguments.

Simulating Supreme Court oral arguments is challenging for many reasons. First, it requires reasoning over long, complicated legal documents: justices might ask questions from any filed brief or draw from previous court cases. Second, a simulator must model each individual justice’s preferences while also handling complex conversational dynamics. Third, the legal issues presented in this setting are among the most difficult to reason about.
47

which means that the legal arguments that get presented in oral arguments are among the most nuanced.

Finally, evaluation is especially difficult. For any given turn, there may be multiple reasonable questions a justice could ask. This makes one-to-one comparisons against the ground truth questions insufficient. Simple token-level evaluation metrics, such as n-gram overlap and perplexity, or even semantic similarity, are inadequate for capturing what makes a model-generated question “good.” Rather, effective judicial questioning is characterized by multiple valuable traits—identifying the most important legal issues in a case, detecting logically inconsistent arguments, surfacing creative counterarguments and hypotheticals, and responding appropriately even when advocates are uncooperative or evasive. Therefore, evaluation approaches that focus narrowly on any single metric may miss critical aspects of question quality.

In this work, we address these challenges by constructing simulators that generate justice questions and introducing an evaluation framework designed to assess their quality holistically along all the aforementioned dimensions.
This allows us to examine what current models can and cannot accomplish in this novel setting for human-AI collaboration and meaningfully guide further research.

Our contributions are threefold:

(1)

We introduce oral argument simulation as a testbed for frontier models, motivated by moot court-style training and preparation settings. This setting is distinct from standard legal question answering tasks due to its adversarial, dialogue-driven and pedagogical structure.

(2)

We propose a two-layered evaluation framework that jointly measures
realism
—via adversarial tests for sycophancy and human preference judgments—and
pedagogical usefulness
—via coverage of substantive legal issues, diversity of question types, detection of logical flaws and competitiveness of tone. We show that these metrics capture complementary aspects of performance and that no single metric suffices on its own.

(3)

We construct and evaluate a wide range of oral argument simulators, including both prompt-based and agentic variants. Using these, we show that while frontier models exhibit initial promise along certain dimensions, such as perceived realism, they also display significant shortcomings, including reduced question diversity and sycophantic behavior. Importantly, these limitations would be difficult to identify under more naive evaluation approaches.

Method

Our methodology consists of three components: the task design, the construction of oral argument simulators, and a two-layered evaluation framework.

3.2.
Oral Argument Simulators

We construct and evaluate two different kinds of oral argument simulators: prompt-based and agentic.

Prompt-based simulators

To construct the prompt-based simulators, we use five open- and closed-sourced large language models: Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Qwen3-32B, Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-4o and gpt-oss-120b. For each model, we apply three manually curated prompting strategies:

•

SCOTUS_DEFAULT
: Situates the model in a Supreme Court oral argument setting and instructs it to adopt a given justice persona.

•

SCOTUS_PROFILE
: Adds to the
SCOTUS_DEFAULT
prompt a hand-crafted description of the given justice’s “profile”, including their known judicial philosophy and political leaning.

•

MOOT_COURT
: Situates the persona in
SCOTUS_PROFILE
as a judge of the National Moot Court Competition with explicit instructions to nitpick logical errors.

Agentic simulators

For the agentic simulators, we use three base reasoning models: GPT-4o, gpt-oss-120b, and Gemini-2.5-Pro. Each agent can take one of 4 actions at each step:

•

THINK
: Allows the agent to reason about its history and plan next steps.

•

CLOSED_WORLD_SEARCH
: Allows the agent to search over case docket files.

•

JUSTICE_PROFILE
: Allows the agent to look up past voting patterns and political affiliations of particular SCOTUS justices.

•

PROVIDE_FINAL_RESPONSE
: Allows the agent to finalize and output a simulated justice response for the given task sample.

For each sample generation, we allow the agentic simulator a maximum of 10 steps to explore the tools and provide its final response.

3.3.
Evaluation Framework

Figure 2
.
Overview of our Evaluation Framework. We evaluate oral argument simulators using two complementary layers.

Evaluating oral argument simulators is challenging because there is no single “correct” question an advocate should expect to hear in practice. Rather than optimizing for a single metric, we organize our evaluation into two layers: 1)
Realism
and 2)
Pedagogical Usefulness
.
Each layer is designed to assess different kinds of potential weaknesses in the simulator, which we explain below.

3.3.1.
Realism

We treat realism as a set of necessary, but not sufficient, conditions for pedagogical effectiveness. Simulations that violate basic norms of oral argument—such as courtroom decorum or known judicial political leanings—are unlikely to be useful for training regardless of their substantive content. The realism layer is therefore intended to filter out simulations that fail to sustain a plausible courtroom interaction.
Within this layer, we use two approaches: (1) adversarial testing for unrealistic behaviors and (2) human evaluation via preference judgments.

Adversarial testing.

We construct a semi-synthetic adversarial benchmark where the advocate’s response at
t_{n-1}
, the last turn of the context given to the simulator, is purposefully set to stress test the simulator.
Advocates either violate courtroom decorum (
Decorum
), introduce political talking points that conflict with the given justice’s known views (
Rage-Bait
), or concede their own argument and argue on behalf of the opposing counsel (
Switching-Sides
).
In realistic settings, justices are expected to call out or push back against such egregious behavior. However, we expect sycophantic models to fail to do so. Each benchmark sample is manually reviewed by human annotators.
Appendix
B.1
provides examples and details on benchmark construction.

Human Evaluation.

In addition to adversarial testing, we assess realism using
Win-Rate
, computed using pairwise human preference judgments.
Annotators are shown a task sample, which includes the facts of the case, legal question, and conversation context, and presented pairs of potential justice responses (simulated or real).
For each pair, annotators indicate which response is more realistic, mark a tie, or indicate that both responses are bad.
These judgments are aggregated across annotators to compute win rates and model rankings.
Details of the annotation interface and win rate analysis are provided in Appendix
B.2
.

3.3.2.
Pedagogical Usefulness

The pedagogical usefulness layer is meant to evaluate whether simulators meaningfully pressure an advocate’s reasoning in ways that prepare them for real-world arguments. In particular, we test the simulator for 1) legal issue coverage, 2) question type diversity, (3) logical fallacy detection, and 4) valence.
5
5

5

We emphasize that these metrics should not be interpreted as a replacement for comprehensive pedagogical evaluation with real moot court participants. Instead, our framework offers an initial scalable approach to testing and iterating on simulator designs prior to real-world use.

Legal Issue Coverage

We assess whether simulators surface the substantive legal issues that advocates should expect justices to raise in real-world oral arguments. To do so, we use two metrics:
Issue-Narrow
, which requires
all
subcomponents of an expected legal issue to be covered by the simulated questions, and
Issue-Broad
, which requires that
some
aspect of the legal issue is covered by the simulated questions.
We test issue coverage on a random sample of 30 transcript sections, each beginning with an advocate’s opening statement.
To construct the set of expected legal issues for each section, we prompt GPT-4o to extract all legal issues raised within the transcript for that section.
For each simulator, we generate questions in response to the opening statements of each section, repeating with three different seeds and each of the nine justices. Finally, we prompt gpt-oss-120b to identify which legal issues are covered in the simulated questions and compute recall relative to the expected issues. See the below example and Appendix
B.3
for implementation details.

Issue:
Are Appeals Office determinations formal, binding decisions on the IRS, or informal actions lacking future legal effect?

Question:
Counsel, your argument hinges on characterizing the challenge to the underlying liability as merely a subsidiary issue. But the text of Section 6330(c)(2)(B) says the taxpayer “may raise” a challenge to the “existence or amount of the underlying tax liability.” When the IRS Office of Appeals makes a “determination” that addresses that challenge, why is that specific part of the determination not, itself, an appealable determination over which the Tax Court retains jurisdiction, regardless of whether the IRS later gets the money through some other means?

Broad:

Yes.
This question asks why that part of the determination is not appealable, i.e., about whether the Appeals Office determination is appealable, thus about whether it’s a binding decision. That matches the issue.

Narrow:

No.
The issue has two aspects: formal/binding vs informal lacking effect. The judge’s question… is about jurisdiction, not directly about formal vs informal. It does not fully address the issue of formal/binding status. So it does not address all points.

We evaluate question-type diversity using three metrics:

For each classification scheme, we compute the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the distributions of simulated turns
\hat{t_{n}}
and the real transcript turns,
t_{n}
. Smaller divergence indicates closer alignment to actual oral argument questioning patterns.

Fallacy Detection

We evaluate the simulator’s ability to detect logical fallacies using a semi-synthetic benchmark covering ten flaw types:
factual-general
,
factual-legal
,
comparison
,

correlation-vs-causation
,
exclusivity
,
numbers
,
sampling
,
sufficient-vs-necessary
,
ignoring-justice
, and
misstating-justice
. In pedagogically useful moot court settings, justices are expected to identify and challenge such flaws in the advocate’s argument. Each benchmark sample is constructed such that the advocate’s response at
t_{n-1}
contains one of the ten flaw types. We then evaluate whether the simulated questions challenge each type of flaw as expected. See Appendix
B.5
for details on each flaw type and benchmark construction.

Tone of questioning

We evaluate the tone of justice questioning using
VALENCE
, which checks whether a question is adversarial or cooperative.
Effective preparation for oral arguments requires sufficiently competitive questioning.
For each test sample, we prompt Qwen3-32B to classify both the ground truth turn
t_{n}
and the simulated turn
\hat{t_{n}}
into one of five fine-grained valence categories, which are then bucketed into {Competitive, Neutral, Supportive}. We compare the valence distributions of simulated turns and the real transcript turns. Here, the transcript valence distribution is not a strict target, since real oral arguments include many neutral inquiries that are less relevant for moot court–style training. Instead, we use valence distributions to detect overly cooperative and, by extension, sycophantic behavior. Simulators with valence distributions that are markedly more supportive than real transcripts indicate potential sycophancy.

While this result may appear surprising at first, it should not be interpreted as models outperforming real justices.
Models are explicitly instructed to probe the advocate’s argument, but not every justice comment in real proceedings tests the litigant’s argument directly.

4.3.
Qualitative Insights

During human evaluation for realism, in addition to submitting preference votes, the annotators could optionally provide open-ended feedback on the response pairs. Analyzing these comments, we found several recurring patterns not entirely captured by the other metrics:

Over-cooperativeness/lack of adversarial tone

The Gemini-2.5-Pro prompt-based simulator was frequently described as overly cooperative toward the advocate. This indicates that sycophancy remains a concern even for Gemini variants, despite their strong performance on several other metrics (Figure
1
). In contrast, Llama-3.3-70B responses were often perceived as more competitive and realistic in tone.

Misidentifying sides

The gpt-oss-120b model frequently misidentified which side the advocate represented, resulting in logically inconsistent questioning. This issue appeared across both prompt-based and agentic variants but was not observed in other models.

Depth and specificity of legal reasoning

Both Gemini-2.5-Pro prompt-based and agentic variants were noted for the depth and specificity of their questioning, while other models tended to ask broader, more generic questions.

Maintaining continuity of conversation in longer contexts

The Gemini-2.5-Pro agentic variant was particularly effective at maintaining the continuity of conversation across longer contexts, which was a challenge for most other simulators. Llama-3.3-70B, in particular, frequently asked questions from several turns earlier in the conversation.

Different simulators exhibit distinct strengths, but no single simulator achieves the full balance of realism and pedagogical usefulness.
These qualitative insights reinforce what our quantitative metrics suggest: effective evaluation of oral argument simulators requires assessing multiple complementary dimensions of performance.
No single metric alone can sufficiently capture the range of strengths and weaknesses we observe.

5.
Limitations and Future Directions

Limitations.

This work has several limitations. First, our evaluation focuses on U.S. Supreme Court oral arguments due to the availability of high quality data. However, the Supreme Court differs from other appellate courts and from moot courts in important ways, including norms of questioning and the competitiveness of justice interactions. As noted throughout the paper, we therefore treat transcript behavior as a reference point for evaluation rather than a reproducible gold standard.

Second, while our layered evaluation framework captures multiple dimensions of simulator quality, it relies on proxy metrics and limited human preference judgments. These metrics do not directly measure learning outcomes or skill improvement for real advocates. Comprehensive pedagogical evaluation with real moot court participants remains an important direction for future work.

Third, several components of our evaluation pipeline rely on language models as classifiers, including issue extraction, issue coverage, question type classification, adversarial response judgments, and valence classification. While this choice enables scalable analysis, it inevitably introduces inaccuracies and potential model-specific biases. We mitigate this risk through careful prompt design and human validation checks, but the use of LLM-as-judge remains a limitation.
10
10

10

We validate LLM-as-judge performance in Appendix
D
.

Fourth, we make simplifying assumptions that abstract away commonly observed characteristics of oral arguments. We focus on justice-advocate exchanges but do not model justice-justice interactions or interruptions. Similarly, while real oral arguments include a mix of questions and plain statements, our simulators mostly generate questions.
11
11

11

Though overrepresented questions may reduce realism, it is arguably more appropriate in educational settings
(
59
)
. We also use static justice profile prompts, while actual justices are known to dynamically vary their approach slightly depending on the case and topic
(
24
)
.

While some of the authors and annotators have legal backgrounds, none have participated in oral arguments in front of appellate courts. Seasoned Supreme Court attorneys might prioritize different lines of questioning. Future work should consider participatory design and evaluation methodologies involving real-world practitioners during actual oral argument preparation.

Future Directions.

This work opens up several directions for future research. Our evaluation framework can be extended to include more dimensions of simulator quality. Real-world deployment and testing with actual moot court participants remains an essential next step. Future work should consider incorporating richer evaluation signals, including feedback from advocates, learning outcomes of law students, and assessments from moot court instructors.

More broadly, oral argument simulation creates a novel setting for human-AI collaboration that is inherently multi-dimensional and cannot be reduced to a single notion of correctness. Our layered evaluation approach could extend to other pedagogical domains requiring adversarial or critical engagement, such as negotiation training or public speaking skill development. We view this work as an initial step towards principled design and evaluation of systems designed to support the development of human reasoning.

6.
Conclusion

We introduce oral argument simulation as a challenging testbed for human-AI collaboration, motivated by moot court-style training settings. Rather than treating simulation quality as a single metric, we demonstrate that effective evaluation requires assessing multiple, complementary dimensions of quality. We propose a two-layer evaluation framework that jointly assesses
realism
and
pedagogical usefulness
through multiple proxy metrics. We apply this framework to a range of prompt-based and agentic simulators.

Our findings reveal both initial promise and significant limitations of current models in this setting. Models often surface substantive legal issues successfully, and some are perceived as highly realistic by human evaluators. However, they also exhibit notable shortcomings, including low diversity in question types and persistent sycophancy that prevents realistic responses to adversarial or provocative advocate behavior. Importantly, no single model performs well across all evaluation dimensions. Instead, different models exhibit strengths and weaknesses across different metrics. This pattern highlights the importance of a nuanced and holistic approach to evaluation for oral argument simulation.

Beyond oral argument simulation, our results point to broader challenges in evaluating human-AI collaborative systems intended to support human learning. In pedagogical contexts, effective assistance requires systems that can meaningfully challenge users and adapt to interactions unfolding over long, multi-turn contexts. Our work suggests that progress in such settings requires careful work on both modeling and evaluation design. We hope our work encourages greater attention to evaluation methodology and system design that prioritizes the use of AI to support the cultivation of good reasoning and critical thinking among users rather than cognitive displacement.

A.1.
SCOTUS Oral Argument Transcripts

We gather Oyez transcripts from
6
and filter for only transcripts from 2024-2025 session that had been held by July 16, 2025. We discarded any case whose transcript was unavailable and restructured each existing transcript as follows:

(1)

We add speaker sides (“petitioner," “respondent") from the case briefs to the transcript data.

(2)

We remove the Chief Justice’s case introduction turn, the petitioner’s rebuttal argument turns, and other miscellaneous turns so that each section of a cleaned transcript starts with an advocate opening statement and ends at the end of questioning.

(3)

We remove traffic phrase turns (“thank you," “no, please", “go ahead, etc."), simple interjection turns (“yeah," “right," “mm-hmm," etc.), and false start turns (turns with duration less than 2 seconds that end in an interruption). Then, we consolidate turns with the same speaker into the same text block.

After cleaning, we 168 distinct sections from 62 distinct cases, with each section having an average of 89 turns.

A.2.
Data for the
CLOSED_SEARCH
tool

To construct the
CLOSED_SEARCH
tool for agentic simulators, we create a dense index over (a) case docket files, and (b) metadocuments associated with the case. Below we explain the data sources used to create these indexes. Section

provides the exact prompt used to explain how to query each of these indexes.

Scraping docket files.

We scrape all case docket files available on
https://www.supremecourt.gov
for U.S. Supreme Court cases from 2017-2024.

Scraping metadocuments.

We scrape all metadocuments associated with a specific case by using an automated pipeline. We prompt Gemini 2.5 Pro to write search queries to find sources that provide information about how domain experts analyze the jurisprudential philosophy of the court and the legal issues relevant to the case. Then, the pipeline uses SerpAPI to retrieve Google Search results for the search queries and scrape the top results to collect metadocuments for each case.

Building search indexes.

For both the docket files and metadocuments, we build searchable dense indexes. We embed all the documents using the Dewey long context embedding model
(
67
)
. The embeddings are stored in per-example Qdrant vector indices. At inference time, the agent has access to both document pools as separate search indexes. The top 3 results are returned for each query based on cosine similarity.

Vote Aggregation and Win Rates.

For samples annotated by more than one annotator, we aggregate the final label for each model pair using the following heuristic:

(1)

If all annotators agree, assign the agreed-upon label.

(2)

If any annotator marks the pair as
bad
, assign the label
bad
.

(3)

If the annotations include
tie
and a single model preference (either
A
or
B
), assign the preferred model.

(4)

If annotators select opposing model preferences (
A
and
B
), assign the label
disagree
.

We compute two variants of win rates. The
strict win rate
counts only outright wins:

\texttt{win\_rate\_strict}\;=\;\frac{\texttt{wins}}{\texttt{total\_matches}}.

The
weighted win rate
assigns partial credit to ties:

\texttt{win\_rate\_weighted}\;=\;\frac{\texttt{wins}+0.5\times\texttt{ties}}{\texttt{total\_matches}}.

Here,
ties
denotes the effective number of ties and is defined as

\texttt{ties}=\texttt{ties\_raw}+\texttt{disagree}.

That is, although we initially record
disagree
as a separate annotation category when annotators express opposing preferences, we ultimately treat annotator disagreement as a tie when computing the weighted win rate.

.

B.4.1.
LegalBench Classification

The LegalBench collaborative benchmark includes a task called
oral_argument_question_purpose
, which requires a model to determine the purpose behind a justice’s question during an oral argument
(
22
)
. Specifically, it categorizes each question into one of 7 purposes, listed below with the exact descriptions from
22
:

•

Background
: A question seeking factual or procedural information that is missing or not clear in the briefing.

•

Clarification
: A question seeking to get an advocate to clarify her position or the scope of the rule being advocated.

•

Implications
: A question about the limits of a rule or its implications for future cases.

•

Support
: A question offering support for the advocate’s position.

•

Criticism
: A question criticizing an advocate’s position.

•

Communicate
: A question designed primarily to communicate with one or more other judges on the court.

•

Humor
: A question designed to interject humor into the argument and relieve tension.

These categories are based on the framework developed by
66
. For our evaluation, we prompt our LLM-as-Judge to classify each predicted statement by each of our simulators into one of these categories for the
Legalbench
metric. Figure
15
shows the distribution of generated turns for all simulators across the
Legalbench
categories.

B.4.2.
MetaCog Classification

While the LegalBench categories build on the taxonomy proposed by
66
, the authors acknowledge that there is no universally accepted set of categories for the purposes of Supreme Court justices’ questions.
16
suggests some alternative approaches. We sought to derive categories empirically from recent historical data. Specifically, we employed an LLM-based approach inspired by
17
to analyze questions asked during Supreme Court oral arguments from 2021–2023 to arrive at novel question purpose categories.

Initial Clustering.

We experimented with traditional clustering techniques like k-means and DBSCAN but encountered challenges. For example, clusters often grouped questions by case topics (e.g., tax law), which lacked the granularity needed to differentiate between question purposes within specific cases.

LLM-Based Semantic Clustering.

To address these limitations, we employed an LLM-based approach inspired by
17
. First, we filtered out "incoherent" questions - i.e. those requiring additional context beyond the opening statement to be meaningful. Then, we prompted Llama 3.1-70B to label each question with a descriptive "purpose" in the context of the opening statement. Similar to
17
, we found that this approach resulted in too many fine-grained labels (
\sim 4000
). Subsequently, we prompted Llama 3.1-70B to perform semantic clustering, grouping similar purpose labels into broader, coarse-grained categories with brief descriptions. This reduced the set to
\sim 250
intermediate labels. A second round of semantic clustering further consolidated these into a final set of 7 purpose categories. Table
4
lists the final seven categories and their descriptions derived through this process. This process is visualized in Figure
14
. Since this automated purpose discovery and semantic clustering methodology was inspired by
17
, we refer to this question type diversity metric as
Metacog
in our study. Figure
17
shows the distribution of generated turns for all simulators across the
Metacog
categories.

B.5.
Fallacy Detection

We expect justices to push back against advocates whose statements contain common logical fallacies. To test this, we construct a semi-synthetic benchmark with GPT4o.

Dataset Construction.

We take an opening statement and generate a justice question to it, sampling one of the nine justices at random for each generation. Then, we go through a list of common logical fallacies found on the LSAT and generate an
advocate
response, with each response incorporating a logical fallacy. All synthetically generated samples were manually reviewed for quality.

The test set consists of samples across 10 different fallacy types:

(1)

factual–general
: The argument relies on a factual claim that is inaccurate or directly contradicted by the known facts.

(2)

factual–legal
: The argument misstates, misapplies, or inaccurately cites a legal rule, precedent, or holding.

(3)

correlation-vs-causation
: The argument assumes that because two events occur together or in sequence, one caused the other.

(4)

sufficient-vs-necessary
: The argument confuses necessary conditions with sufficient conditions, treating one as if it were the other.

(5)

comparison
: The argument treats two things as equivalent based on superficial similarities while ignoring relevant differences (false analogy).

(6)

sampling
: The argument draws a general conclusion from an unrepresentative, biased, or insufficient sample.

(7)

exclusivity
: The argument presents only two options as if they are exhaustive, ignoring additional plausible alternatives (false dichotomy).

(8)

numbers
: The argument improperly compares quantities by conflating absolute numbers with percentages or proportions.

(9)

ignoring-justice
: The response fails to address the substance of the question posed, instead shifting to an irrelevant or tangential point.

(10)

misstating-justice
: The response reframes or alters the question being asked and then answers that different question, rather than addressing the question as posed.

Experimental Setup

Like in the adversarial testing for realism setting, we evaluate simulators on this test set by generating a justice response to the purposefully set advocate turn.
We construct a human-reviewed explanation of the exact error made by the advocate in each of the logical fallacy samples.
Finally, we use Qwen3-32B as the judge model to classify whether the generated justice response successfully caught the error made in the advocate’s last turn.

EXAMPLE: SUFFICIENT VS. NECESSARY

Justice Alito (GPT-4o):
Counsel, I understand your argument against the so-called ‘background circumstances’ rule. But I want to focus on the practical side: How would a court evaluating a discrimination claim by a majority-group plaintiff determine whether the employer’s action was actually motivated by impermissible bias, without some contextual or background evidence suggesting that the employer treats majority-group employees differently? Are you suggesting that the same minimal prima facie framework used for minority plaintiffs should apply to majority plaintiffs without any adjustment at all?

Advocate (GPT-4o):
Justice Alito, the fact that Ms. Ames belongs to a majority group clearly means she will always face discrimination when applying for promotions at the Department. Therefore, the background circumstances rule must be eliminated because it prevents all majority-group employees from obtaining relief. Xiao Wang: Justice Alito, while Ms. Ames’s status as a majority-group employee does not by itself prove discrimination, the background circumstances rule imposes an extra evidentiary burden on plaintiffs like her that is not required by Title VII. Therefore, we argue that majority-group plaintiffs should be evaluated under the same prima facie framework as everyone else, without needing additional presumptions.

Counsel, that’s a rather breathtaking statement. ’She will *always* face discrimination?’ Is that your position? That every employment decision at this department, from this point forward, will be discriminatory against heterosexual individuals? Let’s be more precise. Are you arguing that simply being a member of a majority group and being replaced by a member of a minority group is, in itself, sufficient to establish a prima facie case under Title VII, regardless of any other circumstances?

B.5.1.
Fallacy Detection Ablations for Agentic Simulators

We test the performance of the agentic simulators on the logical fallacy adversarial benchmark with several different ablations. Across the ablations, we vary the amount of search tool access available to the agent.

•

V1
: No Search Tool access

•

V2
: Access to CLOSED_SEARCH only. This includes access to docket files associated with the case and metadocuments related to the case we scraped, but no direct access to open web search.

•

V3
: Access to CLOSED_SEARCH and OPEN_WEB_SEARCH. The agent has access to both the closed search tool and the open web search tool. The latter uses the SERPAPI to retrieve Google Search results for the agent’s search queries and return the top results.

We expect that greater access to search tools would improve performance on the logical fallacy adversarial set, particularly the
factual-general
and
factual-legal
fallacy types.

Figure 19
.
Ablations across agentic simulators for adversarial fallacy detection tests.

We observe mixed results across our ablations for both agents, shown in Figure
19
. Overall, Gemini-2.5-Pro performs substantially better than the gpt4o agent at detecting logical fallacies across all types. Focusing specifically on the two types of factual inaccuracies (
factual-general
and
factual-legal
), we find that counterintuitively, providing open search tool access does not improve the detection of these errors. However, the closed search access significantly improves Gemini’s performance on the
factual-legal
inaccuracies in particular.

To understand these mixed outcomes, we examined the agents’ reasoning logs and individual examples. We noticed a few interesting qualitative insights. The No-Search baseline already performs well on some of the Factual inaccuracies due to model memorization. Often, Gemini-2.5-Pro detects a factual inaccuracy simply by using the THINK action. The downside of this behavior is that, even when search tools are available, the agent does not always use them effectively. In some cases, the agent claims to have “looked at the text” of a particular statute even when the retrieved search results do not contain the specific information required to support its claim in the justice response.

Our analysis has several limitations. First, the logical fallacy set is fairly small (176 samples) and should be expanded to improve the statistical significance of our findings. Second, while we conducted human validation of the LLM-Judge on a small subset of examples, its accuracy may vary based on the difficulty and ambiguity of the individual examples.

B.6.
Tone of Questioning

We use the
Valence
metric to assess tone of questioning. Specifically, we prompt Qwen3-32B to classify each question as one of {Competitive, Slightly Competitive, Neutral, Slightly Supportive and Supportive}. We then bucket the first two and the last two labels to get three possible labels for each response: {Competitive, Neutral, Supportive}. This classification provides insight into whether a specific justice is likely to respond negatively or favorably to a lawyer’s position. Such information is particularly valuable for lawyers preparing oral arguments, as it helps anticipate and strategize for potential judicial reactions (
(
52
;
36
)
).
