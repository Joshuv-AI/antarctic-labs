Analysis of the training pathway in sleep medicine: Training capacity versus demand in the United States
Authors: Jason Silvestre, Robert J. Ferdon, Andrea M. Rinn, Robert A. Ravinsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previous studies have described an emerging deficiency of sleep medicine physicians in the United States (US). This study defines the training pipeline for sleep medicine physicians, including the annual number of applicants, training positions, and unfilled training positions. This was a cross-sectional analysis of all applicants for sleep medicine fellowship training in the US from 2012 to 2025. Annual match outcomes were calculated and trends analyzed with linear regression. From 2012 to 2025, growth in the annual number of applicants (103–249, 142% increase, P < 0.001) exceeded growth in the number of training positions (99–218, 120% increase, P < 0.001) for sleep medicine fellowship training. Accordingly, the annual applicant-to-training position ratio increased (1.04–1.14, P = 0.003) and the annual rate of unfilled training positions decreased (12.1% to 5.0%, P = 0.004) over the study period. The annual representation of US allopathic medical school graduates increased among incoming sleep medicine fellows (31.0% to 41.1%) while the annual representation of non-US allopathic medical school graduates decreased (69.0% to 58.9%), although this trend was not significant (P = 0.056). Match rates for US allopathic medical school graduates exceeded those for non-US allopathic medical school graduates (93.1% vs. 85.8%, P < 0.001). Growth in applicants for sleep medicine fellowship training has exceeded growth in training capacity, resulting in an increasing annual applicant-to-training position ratio. While interest appears to be increasing, surveillance of future match outcomes is warranted given anticipated deficiencies in the sleep medicine physician workforce.

Background

Previous studies have highlighted emerging deficiencies in the number of sleep medicine physicians in the United States (US).[1234] Professional societies in sleep medicine, including the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), have advocated for efforts to physician workforce adequacy in sleep medicine.[56] Adequate access to sleep medicine physicians is important to deliver clinical care to an increasing population of patients with sleep medicine disorders in the US.[789101112] Aging patient populations, rising rates of sleep disorders, and retiring physicians will challenge the adequacy of the sleep medicine physician workforce.[13141516]

There are several challenges to recruiting the next generation of sleep medicine physicians in the US.[17] The investment of an additional year of fellowship training for similar financial and lifestyle advantages afforded by internal medicine training may prove challenging to prospective internal medicine residents.[18] Medical school training is also often devoid of sleep medicine training.[19] Previous studies have highlighted these challenges with potential deleterious consequences for the adequacy of the emerging sleep medicine physician workforce in the US.[20]

Currently, there is a paucity of literature on the pipeline for US sleep medicine fellowship training. Accrediting bodies and professional societies in sleep medicine, including the AASM, must monitor the balance between training capacity and interested applicants for sleep medicine fellowship training. Eroding demand for available training positions may trigger deleterious consequences for the adequacy of the sleep medicine physician workforce to meet increasing population health needs in the US.[1720]

Thus, the objective of the present study was to analyze the training pipeline for the emerging sleep medicine physician workforce in the US. We analyzed recent trends in the annual number of applicants, training positions, and unfilled training positions for sleep medicine fellowship training. We also analyzed the medical training backgrounds of incoming sleep medicine fellows and analyzed where applicants matched on their rank lists for sleep medicine fellowship training.

Materials and Methods

This was a cross-sectional analysis of all applicants for sleep medicine fellowship training in the US. The National Resident Matching Program (NRMP) provided de-identified data for all applicants and programs participating in the Sleep Medicine Match from 2012 to 2025. NRMP match data included the annual number of applicants, training positions, unfilled training positions, medical school training backgrounds of incoming fellows, and outcomes from submitted rank lists on where applicants ultimately matched. This study adhered to observational reporting standards from the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology. Due to the lack of identifiable information, this study was deemed eligible for review exemption status based on the policies of the institutional review board.

Primary outcomes of interest included the annual number of applicants, training positions, and unfilled training positions. Secondary outcomes of interest included annual applicant-to-training position ratios, match rates, number of submitted ranks, medical school training backgrounds, and where applicants ultimately matched on their submitted rank lists. In this study, primary outcomes of interest were provided by the NRMP, and secondary outcomes of interest were calculated. Medical school training backgrounds were defined by the NRMP and included US allopathic medical school graduates and non-US allopathic medical school graduates. Annual applicant-to-training position ratios were calculated by dividing the annual number of sleep medicine fellowship applicants by the annual number of sleep medicine fellowship training positions. Annual match rates for sleep medicine fellowship training were calculated by dividing the annual number of matched applicants by the total number of applicants. The percentage of applicants that matched at their first-, second-, third-, and ≥ fourth-choice sleep medicine fellowship programs was also calculated.

Temporal trends from 2012 to 2025 were analyzed with linear regression. Chi-squared tests were used to compare match rates between US allopathic medical school graduates and non-US allopathic medical school graduates. All statistical tests were two-tailed and calculated with GraphPad Prism (San Diego, CA). P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

From 2012 to 2025, growth in the annual number of applicants (103–249, 142% increase, β = 12.1, R2 = 0.950, P < 0.001) exceeded growth in the annual number of training positions for sleep medicine fellowship training (99–218, 120% increase, β = 8.27, R2 = 0.956, P < 0.001) [Figure 1]. As such, the annual applicant-to-training position ratio for sleep medicine fellowship training increased over the study period (1.04–1.14, β = 0.022, R2 = 0.532, P = 0.003). The nadir in the annual applicant-to-training position ratio occurred in 2015 at 0.78 (102 applicants/130 training positions), while the zenith occurred in 2025 at 1.1 (249 applicants/218 training positions).

Number of applicants and training positions for sleep medicine fellowship training.

There was a significant decrease in the annual percentage of unfilled fellowship training positions in sleep medicine over the study period (12.1% to 5.0%, β = −1.28, R2 = 0.514, P = 0.004) [Figure 2]. There also was a significant decrease in the number of sleep medicine fellowship programs that had an unfilled training position (20.8% to 9.8%, β = −1.84, R2 = 0.583, P = 0.002). The zenith in the annual rate of unfilled training positions occurred in 2015 (36/130, 27.7%) where 34.3% of sleep medicine fellowship programs had at least one unfilled training position (24/70). The rate of sleep medicine fellowship training positions that went unfilled over the study period was 15.1% (353/2,333).

Annual rate of unfilled training positions for sleep medicine fellowship training.

The representation of US allopathic medical school graduates increased over the study period (31.0% to 41.1%) while the representation of non-US allopathic medical school graduates decreased (69.0% to 58.9%), although this did not reach statistical significance (β = −0.468, R2 = 0.271, P = 0.056) [Figure 3]. There was a greater representation of non-US allopathic medical school graduates (2945/4695, 63.7%) relative to US allopathic medical school graduates (1199/1980, 60.6%) over the study period (P < 0.001).

Annual representation of medical school training backgrounds among incoming sleep medicine fellows.

The overall match rate was 85.8% over the study period (1980 matches/2307 applicants). Match rates for US allopathic medical school graduates exceeded those for non-US allopathic medical school graduates [93.1% vs. 81.7%, P < 0.001, Figure 4]. Match rates for US allopathic medical school graduates remained stable over the study period (87.1% to 93.4%, β = −0.005, R2 = 0, P = 0.984), but decreased for non-US allopathic medical school graduates (83.3% to 77.2%, β = −0.976, R2 = 0.444, P = 0.009).

Comparison of match rates for sleep medicine fellowship training by medical school training background.

There was a significant increase in the annual mean number of submitted ranks per applicant over the study period (4.8–7.9, β = 0.314, R2 = 0.791, P < 0.001). The percentage of applicants matching at their first-choice fellowship decreased over the study period (57.3% to 47.2%, β = −1.56, R2 = 0.604, P = 0.001) [Table 1]. There was no change in the percentage of applicants matching at their second-choice (17.5% to 16.5%, β = 2.10, R2 = 0.075, P = 0.344) or third-choice (4.9% to 7.4%, β = 0.036, R2 = 0.010, P = 0.736) fellowship. The percentage of applicants matching at their ≥fourth-choice (4.9% to 12.5%, β = 0.765, R2 = 0.775, P < 0.001) fellowships increased over the study period. The percentage of applicants who went unmatched increased over the study period (15.5% to 16.5%, β =0.552, R2 = 0.306, P = 0.040).

Discussion

Over the past 14 years, growth in the number of applicants for sleep medicine fellowship training has exceeded growth in the number of training positions. This has resulted in a decrease in the number of unfilled training positions and an increase in the annual applicant-to-training position ratio. In 2015, the rate of unfilled training positions reached its zenith, and the annual applicant-to-training position ratio reached its nadir. Match rates for US allopathic medical school graduates exceed those for non-US allopathic medical school graduates. While US allopathic medical school graduates constitute the minority of incoming sleep medicine fellows, their representation has been increasing. While recent match outcomes are encouraging relative to match outcomes observed in 2015, ongoing surveillance is warranted given anticipated shortages of sleep medicine physicians in the US.[1234] Professional societies in sleep medicine should continue to promote the attractiveness of the specialty through fellow recruitment initiatives and physician advocacy efforts. While future outcomes in sleep medicine fellowship training remain difficult to predict, previous trends may be instructive to key stakeholders in the specialty. Insights generated from this study may assist accrediting bodies, professional societies, fellowship faculty, and prospective applicants on the current and potential future state of sleep medicine training in the US.

There are several challenges to recruiting and training an adequate number of sleep medicine physicians.[1720] Financial rates of return associated with sleep medicine training appear limited relative to the additional year of fellowship training.[2122] Internal medicine residents often have access to career opportunities in hospital medicine that are commensurate with or exceed prospective earnings from a career in sleep medicine.[23] For example, one recent analysis of AASM members demonstrated that annual compensation in sleep medicine ranged between $259,439 to $321,407 for women and men, respectively.[24] Thus, the opportunity costs associated with an additional year of fellowship training may disincentivize future internal medicine resident applicants. Yet, potential deficiencies in the supply of sleep medicine physicians may be associated with potentially deleterious public health consequences for increasing patient populations with sleep disorders.[78] Accelerated efforts may be required to recruit qualified internal medicine applicants for sleep medicine fellowship training to meet increasing population health needs in the US.[1213141516] Professional societies in sleep medicine, like the AASM, are instrumental in advocating for adequate reimbursement from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services for the delivery of sleep-related health services. Specifically, efforts are needed to ensure adequate reimbursement for nonprocedural work in sleep medicine, like sleep study interpretation and treatment plan adjustments. In addition, ongoing advocacy is needed to ensure policy adoption for the continued expansion and reimbursement of telehealth services across state lines.[25] Encouragingly, ongoing shifts in the treatment of sleep disorders, including precision medicine, advanced technologies, and other therapies, may prove useful in generating interest for the next generation of sleep medicine physicians.[2627]

The pipeline of future applicants for sleep medicine fellowship training begins in medical school. Yet, few medical students are exposed to sleep medicine during medical school training.[19] Moving forward, there remains a strong need for sleep medicine physicians to stimulate early interest at their affiliated medical schools, given the strong effect of early exposure and mentorship on guiding future career interests in medicine.[20] For example, the 4th year of medical school training offers several unique opportunities for selective rotations in sleep medicine, which can stimulate early interest in the specialty.[28] Professional societies in sleep medicine, including the AASM, can partner with sleep medicine faculty at local medical schools to create electives in sleep medicine with standardized curricula. In addition, research grants and other scholarship opportunities in sleep medicine can connect potential faculty mentors with junior medical students. Often, medical students have the summer between 1st and 2nd year of medical school to pursue these extracurricular research opportunities. As such, professional societies in sleep medicine can create research opportunities for prospective medical students as an additional avenue to stimulate early interest in sleep medicine. In addition, there remains a strong need to stimulate interest among prospective internal medicine residents during their first 2 years of training.[18] Increasingly, qualified applicants for sleep medicine fellowship training may lose interest in favor of competing career interests and training opportunities.[23] Longitudinal mentorship of internal medicine residents by sleep medicine faculty is necessary to stimulate and preserve interest in the specialty. Despite the numerous challenges facing the emerging sleep medicine physician workforce, the latest match results have been more optimistic, highlighting the potentially positive impact of ongoing recruitment initiatives from the AASM.[6]

Importantly, training capacity for the sleep medicine physician workforce, including the number of fellowship training positions and programs, is not tied to US workforce needs.[29] Future studies are needed to determine the desired future steady state in the sleep medicine training capacity to meet anticipated population health needs in the US. While outside the specific remit of this manuscript, there may be potential for integrated residency training in sleep medicine, given similar trends observed in adjacent subspecialties. Potential benefits include greater attractiveness and earlier recruitment of future applicants from US allopathic medical schools. Medical students may be incentivized to pursue sleep medicine training if given the option for reduced time in training associated with integrated residency training. Future adoption and implementation of alternative training pathways in sleep medicine will require alignment between regulatory bodies in US postgraduate medical education, including the American Board of Internal Medicine, the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education, and the AASM. In our study, allopathic medical school graduates constituted a growing minority of incoming sleep medicine fellows. Future studies are needed to analyze the impact of additional applicant characteristics on the sleep medicine match. Previous studies have highlighted the benefits of enhancing diversity and inclusion in the sleep medicine physician workforce.[3031] International medical graduates may represent an important source of trainees to enhance racial and ethnic diversity among future sleep medicine physicians.[32] Due to data limitations in the NRMP dataset, the impact of race and ethnicity on match outcomes in sleep medicine could not be assessed. In addition, future studies are needed to determine which sleep medicine fellowship program characteristics are most desirable to prospective applicants. The preference of sleep medicine fellowship programs in the match is complex and likely varies by several factors, including geographical location, access to key faculty, research opportunities, and perceived academic prestige.

Recent literature has highlighted increasing efforts to expand postgraduate medical training opportunities in sleep medicine across the globe.[3334] These efforts are driven by a growing awareness of the essential role of sleep in overall health and the rising prevalence of sleep-related disorders worldwide.[1213141516] Many countries have worked to standardize curricula in sleep medicine by integrating core competencies across sleep apnea, insomnia, and circadian rhythm disorders into recommendations for fellowship program accreditation, fellow-faculty ratios, and program evaluation guidelines.[35] International organizations like the World Sleep Society and various regional sleep societies have created evidence-based guidelines, certification pathways, and continuing professional development opportunities to harmonize training pathways in sleep medicine across borders.[36] Such collaborative initiatives, including global webinars, shared online education platforms, and multinational research networks, have expanded access to sleep medicine expertise and reduced disparities between high- and low-resourced regions.[33343536] Yet despite these advances, challenges remain, including inconsistent curriculum adoption and the persistent shortage of formally trained sleep medicine specialists worldwide.[333435] Collectively, there is an emerging consensus that strengthening postgraduate medical education in sleep medicine remains essential to improve patient outcomes and advance public health standards globally.

This study had several strengths and limitations. While all applicants for sleep medicine fellowship training were included in this study over 14 years, the NRMP data were de-identified and thus had limited attributes. Future studies are needed to determine the impact of additional applicant characteristics like gender, race, and ethnicity on sleep medicine match outcomes. A multi-institutional review of sleep medicine fellowship applications may afford these insights. Furthermore, the retrospective and cross-sectional nature of this study also afforded limited insights into etiologies for the trends observed in the emerging sleep medicine physician workforce. For example, the number of unfilled training positions decreased over the study period, which may have reflected reticence in match participation during early years and relative maturation of the sleep medicine match over time. Linear regression was used to understand the likely evolution of sleep medicine fellowship training, including potential trends in the number of applicants and training positions. However, previous match outcomes are not guaranteed to predict future outcomes. This study also focused on sleep medicine match outcomes and could not determine the fate of unfilled training positions, including those filled outside of the match. Similarly, we could not determine whether successfully matched fellows ultimately completed their sleep medicine fellowship training. As such, future studies are needed to understand the rates and reasons for attrition from sleep medicine training. Finally, insights from this study were limited to the US, and additional studies are needed to analyze the training pipelines for sleep medicine physicians in other countries. Previous studies have highlighted the lack of formalized recognition of sleep medicine as an independent medical specialty in other countries[34] and the lack of formalized training pathways in sleep medicine.[35] Future studies are needed to assess recruitment patterns for sleep medicine training across global settings.

Conclusion

In summary, growth in the annual number of interested applicants for sleep medicine fellowship training has exceeded growth in the annual training capacity. This has resulted in a significant increase in the annual applicant-to-training position ratio for sleep medicine fellowship training that reached its zenith in 2025. The rate of unfilled training positions has decreased, and the percentage of US allopathic medical school graduates entering sleep medicine fellowship training has increased. Professional societies in sleep medicine should continue to promote recruitment into the specialty through trainee recruitment initiatives and physician advocacy efforts. Future surveillance of match outcomes in sleep medicine is warranted, given projected deficiencies in the emerging sleep medicine physician workforce.
