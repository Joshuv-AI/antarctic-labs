This Final Summary Technical RPPR1 Report covers October 1, 2021, to August 31, 2025. The report
summarized major contributions and activities under this DOE SETO project funding. This includes
the methodologies used and the prediction of best-of-class PV suitable and stable compounds and
alloys. Reports of novel results in this report relate to the discovery of new chemical and physical
phenomena that govern optoelectronic properties of perovskites and perovskite alloys, and the
prediction of new, yet-to-be-synthesized perovskite materials for PV applications from computer
simulations utilizing both (1) models based on simulations of interatomic potentials such as DFT and
(2) machine learning approximations of material properties.
Project Description
The project aims to: (i) accelerate the discovery of “Missing HP alloys” by combining quantum
mechanics and artificial intelligence machine learning approaches, and (ii) analyze the stabilities
of candidate alloys, including those that do not pass selection filters (and are thus expected to
degrade over time) to decipher the nature of the instabilities to guide the development of
durable solar cell materials. Successful candidates will be subjected to validation experiments at
NREL's state-of-the-art facilities. The discoveries this effort will provide will be directly testable
and implementable and will greatly impact U.S. progress in HP PV as they will provide clear
direction and motivation for experimental studies including specific material synthetic targets,
device optimization, and device stability protocols. A key advantage of this effort is the feedback
and guidance provided by the Industry Collaborative Work Group that we established to
coordinate academic and national lab research with industry needs. The proposed work will
provide a basis for and direct the development of robust and reliable HP PV. It will also provide a
timely, valuable and extensive roadmap to the experimental HP PV community to enable it to
focus its efforts on improving and fine-tuning promising HP compositions that this effort predicts
will likely be the best performers rather than wandering in the vast chemical space for decades
spending enormous resources mostly evaluating unpromising candidate materials.

Background info and Distinguishing Strategy

The distinguishing features of the Halide Perovskite field from traditional PV semiconductor
require a different research strategy:

First, only a tiny fraction of the possible HPs has been studied thus far by the community, often
“rounding up the usual suspects”. Have we missed important cases looking at rather small set of
compounds?
Second, mutually conflicting material selection conditions abound: In single perovskite halides
the structural phase that has the PV-compatible band gap (the cubic Pm-3m phase) is generally
not the thermodynamically stable state. This often encountered “good gap; bad stability”
syndrome requires more complex methodology of material search.
Third, Traditional PV was able to utilize simple ordered compounds with digital (Daltonian)
stoichiometry – Silicon, GaAs or CdTe. Yet, Halide Perovskite based PV generally did not find such
digital composition, single ordered perovskites to be sufficiently flexible as solar absorbers. Have
we missed the ‘Si of PV “within HPs?
Fourth, Single cubic perovskites ABX3 in the nominal Pm-3m structure were often found to exhibit
“negative phonon energies” at some wavevectors, signaling intrinsic dynamic instability of this
phase. This was traced back to the fact that the nominal Pm-3m structure with a single repeated
octahedral motif (‘monomorphous’) should not be used for calculating properties. Instead, an
expanded cubic cell that has a distribution of local motifs (“polymorphous”) restores dynamically
stable phonons and is thus more realistic.
Fifth, Recent literature notes properties of ordered double perovskites [AA’]B2X6; A2[BB’]X6;
A2B2[XX’]3 as well as Ordered Vacancy compounds (“216”) such as Cs2TiX6 or Cs2SnX6 that require
further checking of PV relevance. Some examples:
 • Anti-site defects do form deleterious deep recombination centers: Cs2[InAg]Cl6 and
 Cs2[BiAg]Cl6 as well as Cs2SnI6 with B= Sn4+, B’ = vacancy.
 • Phase separation into components with different local coordination numbers creates.
 • A new shade of instability, as in Cs2AgInBr6. Also, Cs2[InCu]Br6 is found to decompose into
 Cs3Cu2Br5 where Cu is four-fold coordinated by Br, whereas InBr3 with six-fold coordinated
 In, and CsBr.
 • Some double perovskites have indirect bandgap: e.g., Cs2[SbAg]Cl6, Cs2BiAgCl6, Cs2BiAgBr6
 –not very useful. Even if the gap is direct, it can be orbitally forbidden, example:
       Cs2InAgCl6; Cs2SnCl6, Cs2SnBr6; and A2B+B3+X6 (B+=Na, K, Rb, Cs; B3+=Sc, Y, Al, Ga, In).

 3

Sixth, it is becoming apparent that ordered, ‘digital composition’ building-block halide
perovskites will be hard pressed to provide a coexistence of target band gaps as well as
acceptable stability. Indeed, the field of halide perovskite PV seem to have shifted to utilize solid
solution disordered HP alloys having continuous, non-digital composition, rather than single
ordered HP.

The conclusions from some of the observations noted briefly above is that one must exercise ‘PV
Realism’ in predictive calculations on HP, exercising care in validating the results considering
some of the literature observations noted.

To mitigate such risks, we opted for a two-pronged strategy:

(a) Search for novel, digital composition ordered HP compounds, that have appropriate band
gaps and stability. This is done by combining QM with ML, being mindful of the possible pitfalls
noted above. The “basic script” here is to use high quality DFT set of building blocks, then produce
in ML many more digital compositions compounds to be tested for the potential pitfalls noted
above. Diversity is created in this approach by considering many individual compounds.
(b) Generate novel PV properties by considering a single, internally complex alloy of multiple
HP components with continuous composition. Here one uses direct DFT calculations of the
properties of complex, multi-component, disordered non-digital halide perovskite alloys. This
finds all properties (electronic, thermodynamic) from a single QM calculation.

We inspect the DFT orbital densities after atomic relaxation to see if these holes are
conventionally delocalized (i.e., hydrogenic shallow acceptor) or prefer to lower the energy by
localizing the holes in specific locations. An example is the iodine Frenkel pair made of iodine
interstitial and iodine vacancy close to each other, indicated in the schematic Figure 1a. Figure
1b shows the formation energy of an associated FP (associated means that halogen vacancy is
closer to the halogen interstitial), in blue line, is favorable (negative energy) for intermediate Br
compositions, i.e., two holes at valence band maximum (VBM) localize and create an iodine
Frenkel pair. For the same range of Br compositions, the dissociated iodine FP (dissociated means
that the halogen vacancy is treated without interaction with halogen interstitial) formation
energy red line in (Figure 1b) is lower in energy (more stable) than the associated FP, indicating
that, in case the iodine Frenkel pair forms, it tends to separate into iodine interstitial and iodine
vacancy. When the Br concentration is outside the shaded green area (Figure 1b), the formation
energy, the formation energy of the FP becomes positive, meaning the hole at the VB does not
tend to spontaneously localize in forming a FP. At this point, the segregation process stops.

We propose a mechanism for phase segregation in alloys of CsPb(I1-xBrx)3 as summarized in Figure
2. Note that our explanation does not involve thermodynamic properties, but only analysis of
defects. Starting from the alloy (Figure 2a), the holes can be generated in the HP by light or
electrochemically, making the HP in a hole rich condition. In principle, the holes are delocalized
at the VBM, as shown by Figure 2b. However, when the Fermi level of the alloy moves from the
middle of the band gap towards the valence band, the formation energy of Frenkel pair (VI+Ii)2+
becomes negative for alloys with Br concentration between 22% and 66% and the Frenkel-pair
defect tends to form spontaneously. This is the driving force that determine the threshold of Br
concentration in the alloy. The Frenkel pair is shown in Figure 2c. However, in the same range of
alloy composition, the Frenkel pair tends to dissociate i.e., the Ii1+ and VI1+ move in the opposite
direction (Figure 2d,e). The dissociation process is caused by the energy gain in the system. In
intermediate concentrations of Br, we can say that the associated FP is a metastable
configuration (Figure 2b) despite being more stable than 2 holes at the valence band. Therefore,
under a hole rich condition, the FP tends to form spontaneously by localization of 2 holes in the
valence band. The I that composes the octahedra around the VI (not the I that composes the
Frenkel pair), moves towards the vacancy. This process creates a region rich in I (Figure 2f), and
another region with I depletion (large concentration of vacancies and Br) (Figure 2g). The creation
of Frenkel pair and the dissociation will be suppressed only when the local chemical environment
becomes similar to the average alloy concentration with x < 0.22 and x > 0.66. In both conditions,
the holes will not localize anymore in the Frenkel pair and the segregation will stop.

 • Traditional III-V and II-VI semiconductor phase-segregate when the components are size
 mismatched. HP alloys show good miscibility in the dark but can phase-segregate under
 light.
 • Quantum mechanical calculations described here show the interaction between a
 halogen vacancy VX with halogen interstitial Xi forming together a Frenkel-pair (FP) defect

 • At a threshold bromine composition in the Br-I alloys, the photogenerated holes in the
 valence band localize, creating thereby a doubly charged iodine Frenkel-pair (VI + Ii)2+.
 • The faster migration of iodine over bromine interstitial into the vacant iodine VI site leads

Before applying our recently developed machine learning interatomic potential to screen a
chemical space of 222,960 AA’[BB’]X6 compounds as described in Highlight III below, we used a
structure prediction code based on the GII to create a system-specific structure for each of the
222,960 compounds. The optimal lattice parameters for the unit cell of the structure were
obtained by minimizing the GII for each composition. GII quantifies the deviation from ideal bond
valences and serves as an indicator of structural stability. The same GII minimization scheme was
used to generate the primitive structures for 500 single perovskites with the formula ABX3. The
theoretical studies using the structure prediction code based on GII is described in the publication
listed above.

The structure prediction code based on GII was published in Github (URL:
https://github.com/zaba1157/bvm_cubic_perovskite) which is publicly available. The code
utilizes the bond valance method to provide an estimate of the ordered primitive cubic structure
for ABX3 and AA'BB'X6 perovskites by predicting the cubic lattice constant which is obtained by
minimizing the GII with a default distance cutoff (6 Å). The hierarchical computational-design
framework (Fig. 4) was applied to efficiently and reliably screen a significant number of
hypothetical HPs for optoelectronic applications.

 • The bond valance method is efficient for providing an estimate of the ordered structure
 of the halide perovskites.
 • Based on the bond valence method, the global instability index can predict the relative
 energy of different structures of halide perovskites.

We take the monomorphous phase as a reference and show the variation in total energy (Fig.
5a). In almost all the cases the total energy variation is negative, which indicates that the
polymorphous phase is more stable than the monomorphous phase. There are some cases where
the variation in the total energy is neglectable, indicating a degenerated state between
monomorphous and polymorphous configurations. In other words, during the atomic relaxation
of the polymorphous phase, all the atoms return to the ideal Wyckoff position of cubic Pm-3m,
recovering the monomorphous phase. Therefore, the polymorphous configuration is more
stable, or at least with the same total energy per formula unit, than the monomorphous phase.
To check the effects of symmetry breaking in the band, we plot the band gap for the
polymorphous phase as a function of the monomorphous phase gap (Fig. 5b). The dashed line
indicates the equality condition in the band gap for the two atomic configurations. An upper
deviation from the dashed line means that the band gap of the polymorphous configuration is
opening, while a lower deviation indicates that the band gap is closing. From these results, we
can separate the band gap of the polymorphous phase into three categories: (i) invariant band
gaps (i.e., monomorphous and polymorphous gaps are equal), (ii) compounds with band-gap
opening, and (iii) compounds with bandgap closing.

We investigate triple-mixed halide perovskites with various chemical compositions and in
different site substitutions and analyze the effects of different chemical composition in the
electronic structure and the stability (Figs. 6 and 7). For halogen mixing, atomic radii differences
play a crucial role in both band gap and mixing enthalpy. Intermediate mixing of Cl and I introduce
significant strain, increasing instability in the halide perovskite (HP) structure. Alkali atoms at the
A-site do not alter the band edge orbital characters but affect unit cell volume. In these two cases

can be estimated by a linear interpolation of the parent compounds. For group IV mixing at the
B-site (Ge, Sn and Pb), the chemical composition significantly impacts the band edge orbital
character, thus affecting band gap and mixing enthalpy.

Highlight V: “How can the complex predicted landscape of alloy mixing enthalpy
or alloy band gap vs. composition be explained in a simple geometric manner?”

“Local atomic motifs in halide perovskite alloys reflect the landscape of enthalpies and band
gaps”, Fernando P. Sabino, Jia-Xin Xiong, Xiuwen Zhang, Gustavo M. Dalpian, and Alex Zunger,
Under Review in Phys. Rev. B.

In this highlight, we demonstrate that the intricate landscape of density functional theory (DFT)-
calculated properties in relaxed, disordered multicomponent ABX3 halide perovskite (HP) alloys
can, to a surprising degree, be understood through simple local geometric motifs. Unlike binary
non-perovskite alloys, a single ABX3 perovskite can display polymorphous behavior, where
different local motifs emerge from symmetry breaking of the ideal cubic Pm-3m phase. Such
distortions manifest through octahedral rotations, atomic displacements from a single Wyckoff
position, and bond disproportionation. In halide perovskite alloys, this polymorphous character
is preserved and enhanced by distinct chemical composition, as schematically illustrated in
middle of Fig. 8 where each local motifs are distinct and represented by different colors.

The above noted polymorphous character can be seen from the histogram distributions of
cation–anion (B–X) bond lengths for the pure halide perovskite compounds CsPbCl3, CsPbBr3,
CsPbI3 and their alloy configurations CsPb(Cl0.5I0.5)3 and CsPb(Br0.5I0.5)3 (Fig. 9). We see that two
distinct peaks are observed in the B–X bond length distributions in the alloys. The positions of
these peaks—though not their intensities—are closer to those of the pure polymorphous phases,
as indicated by the dashed arrows. Furthermore, the distributions are broader, spanning a wider
range of B–X bond lengths compared to the pure compounds. This suggests that, while the local
chemical environment in the alloy tends to resemble that of the parent compounds, certain local
regions are significantly affected by the presence of mixed halogens.

The second structural metric used in our analysis is the B–X–Bʹ bond angle, illustrated in Fig. 8,
which reflects the degree of octahedral rotation in the perovskite lattice. Fig. 10 presents the
histogram distributions of the B–X–Bʹ angles for both pure compounds (CsPbCl3, CsPbBr3, and
CsPbI3) and alloyed systems [CsPb(Cl0.5Br0.5)3 and CsPb(Br0.5I0.5)3], with the corresponding values

 20

for the monomorphous high-symmetry cubic Pm-3m structure indicated by a red vertical line. In
the case of pure compounds, such as CsPbCl3, the histogram is narrow and centered closer to
180°, indicating a small octahedral rotation due to the stronger Pb–Cl bonding. In the low-mixing
enthalpy alloy, CsPb(Br0.5I0.5)3 (Fig. 10b, middle panel), the B–X–Bʹ angle distribution resembles a
superposition of the angle distributions of the parent compounds. The resulting histogram is
broader than those of the parent compounds, however, remains within their angular limits. This
behavior suggests that the standard deviation (SD) of the B–X–Bʹ angle is relatively low. In
contrast, for the high-mixing enthalpy alloy CsPb(Cl0.5I0.5)3 (Fig. 10a, middle panel), the B–X–Bʹ
angle distribution is significantly wider and spread, extending beyond the angular range of the
parent phases. This large structural discrepancy, caused by the large halogen radii mismatch,
results in a higher SD for the B–X–Bʹ angle, reflecting a higher degree of local strain and
frustration in the octahedral rotations.

the local chemical environment of the octahedra. The excess B–X–B’ angle variation captures

Figure 12. Correlation between band gap and B-X bond length.

The second electronic property we correlate with a geometric descriptor of alloys of halide
perovskite is the band gap. Figure 12 illustrates the correlation between the band gap and the
average B–X bond length—both plotted on the same compositional scale as the triangular alloy
diagrams—for (g) halogen mixing, CsPb(IxBryCl1-x-y)3; (h) alkali mixing, (ClxRbyK1-x-y)PbBr3; and (i)
group-IV mixing, Cs(PbxSnyGe1-x-y)Br3. A clear linear relationship emerges in all three cases. Across
different alloying strategies in halide perovskites, the average B–X bond length serves as a
unifying structural descriptor that correlates with band gap variations. In halogen mixing, a
negative correlation is observed: larger bond lengths, associated with I-rich compositions, lead
to smaller band gaps due to enhanced overlap between the B-site ns² lone pair and the more
delocalized halogen p-orbitals. This arises from the increased size and polarizability of heavier

 24

halogens, which elevate the valence band energy. In contrast, in alkali and group-IV mixing, a
positive correlation emerges—larger B-X bond lengths coincide with increased band gaps. In
alkali mixing, this is driven by enhanced octahedral tilting caused by smaller A-site cations, which
reduces orbital overlap. For group-IV mixing, the dominant factor is also octahedral tilting, with
Ge-rich alloys exhibiting reduced tilting (larger B–X–Bʹ angles) and smaller band gaps, while Pb-
rich alloys show stronger tilting and wider gaps. In all cases, the B–X bond length captures the
interplay between chemical composition, volume, and local geometry, offering a transferable
metric for predicting band edge behavior.

“Upward band gap bowing and negative mixing enthalpy in multi-component cubic halide
perovskite alloys”, Xiuwen Zhang, Fernando P. Sabino, Jia-Xin Xiong, and Alex Zunger, Accepted
for publication in Phys. Rev. Mater. (Paper 7 in Sec. II)

Main Result: Interesting trends for band gap bowing and material stability.

The paper was designated by the Phys. Rev. Highlight. The associated highlight text that
explains it succinctly was:

Semiconductor components are often alloyed to obtain target physical properties—such as band
gaps or effective masses—that are absent in the individual unmixed components yet are needed
for specific applications. Conventional tetrahedral semiconductors generally have alloy band
gaps that are lower than the composition average gap of the constituent (“downward bowing”).
Here we design theoretically via Density Functional Theory multi component halide perovskite
alloys that have upward bowing, where the alloy has a significantly larger gap than the
composition average of its components. It is remarkable to find that the alloy Cs4[GeSnPbCd]I12
has a DFT gap (1.96 eV) that is much larger than all its components (1.01 eV to 1.53 eV).
Furthermore, such multi component alloys can have a rather low mixing enthalpy, suggesting
stability towards phase separation.

The idea is to mix perovskite ABX3 that has low lying s-orbitals in the valence band, (bearing an
IVB-group atom Ge, Sn, Pb) with a compound that has IB atom (e.g., Cd) that has s-orbitals in the
conduction band. The ensuing s-s repulsion opens the alloy gap with respect to the constituents’
gap (Fig. 16a). Such upward bowing is absent when Cd is absent, being replaced by, say Ca (Fig.
16b).

At the same time, the calculated incoherent formation enthalpy (w.r.t. the relaxed components)
of the halide perovskite alloys A4[BB’B’’B’’’]X12 is small positive, yet the incoherent enthalpy
(when the alloy and its components are structurally coherent; Fig. 14b) are negative, signaling
significant alloy stability. This reflects the ease of creating inter penetrating networks in the
perovskite framework. The specific design principles for low enthalpy combined with upward
bowing open the door for attaining new alloy functionalities.

In this highlight, we focus on the band gap bowing effect in halide perovskite alloys which is
important for the design of optoelectronic materials. Fig. 13 shows our previously calculated
results on two-component halide perovskite alloys. For downward bowing w.r.t. linear average—
alloy has smaller gap than components—positive bowing coefficient (DFT prediction: CsSnBr3-
CsPbBr3). For upward bowing w.r.t. linear average—alloy has a larger gap than components—
negative bowing coefficient (DFT prediction: CsCdBr3-CsPbBr3). Both CsSnBr3-CsPbBr3 and
CsCdBr3-CsPbBr3 belong to the three-component alloy CsSnBr3-CsPbBr3-CsCdBr3 studied in
Highlight IV (see Figs. 6 and 7). In this highlight, we mainly focus on the band gap bowing effect

 27

in four-component halide perovskite alloys as well as its correlation with the coherent mixing
enthalpy (see Fig. 14).

We find that many of the four-component cubic halide perovskite alloys A4[BB’B’’B’’’]X12 (see Fig.
15) have negative mixing enthalpies relative to the polymorphous structures of cubic single
perovskites (calculated using the same type of supercell structure for the polymorphous alloy
structures) (coherent mixing enthalpy ∆𝐻$%&. ). This is due to the ease of creating inter
penetrating networks in the perovskite framework.

Coherent mixing enthalpies and band gap bowing effects are often thought to be in different
realms. However, we find from Fig. 15 that there is a trend that four-component halide perovskite
alloys A4[BB’B’’B’’’]X12 with negative (positive) band gap bowing tends to have negative (positive)
coherent mixing enthalpies. The Cd-containing alloys if with enough amount of group IVB
elements tends to have upward band gap bowing and negative coherent mixing enthalpies (or
nearly negative coherent mixing enthalpies) simultaneously. It is dramatical to find that the
halide perovskite alloy Cs4[GeSnPbCd]I12 has a band gap (1.96 eV) that is much larger than all its
ABX3 components (1.01 eV to 1.53 eV) (Fig. 16). From Fig. 16a, we can see that the upward band
gap bowing of Cs4[GeSnPbCd]I12 relative to its components is very strong, as compared to the
weak downward band gap bowing of Cs4[GeSnPbCa]I12 in Fig. 16b.

To understand the above noted clear trends of upward band gap bowing in the A4[BB’B’’B’’’]X12
halide perovskite alloys, we analyze the interaction of atomic orbitals in the alloys. In the IVB-
IVB-IVB-IIB and IVB-IVB-IIA-IIB groups of A4[BB’B’’B’’’]X12 alloys that can host upward band gap
bowing, B-site atoms with s2p2 configuration (e.g. Sn and Pb) will have the s2 orbital occupied in
the valence band, and B-site atoms with s2p0 configuration (e.g. Cd and Sr) will have the s2 orbital
in the conduction band. Especially, when the atoms with s2p0 shell are from group IIB in the
periodic table, here Cd, the s states of Cd are found to be a significant component at the CBM,
which could offer the opportunity for the s states of IVB group (Ge, Sn, Pb) in the valence bands
to repel the CBM especially the Cd-s states up as the s states are rather delocalized. This type of
cross-band-gap s-s band repulsion can lead to the upward band gap bowing in alloys.

For the above proposed cross-band-gap s-s band repulsion mechanism of upward band gap

and the pure compound CsCdI3. As the on-site U increases, both the band gaps of the alloy
Cs4[SnPbSrCd]I12 and compound CsCdI3 goes up. The on-site U term pushes the unoccupied Cd-s
orbital up, resulting in the transition from upward bowing to downward bowing at approximately
U = 4 eV, as shown in Fig. 17a. We show two snapshots as for a direct visualization: upward
bowing at U = 0 eV vs downward bowing at U = 15 eV (Fig. 17b,c).

APS March Meeting 2022 Volume 67, Number 3 Session A69: “Doping and Additives in Metal
Halide Perovskites” Abstract: A69.00009: “Failure to dope halide perovskites: insufficient
optimization or a physically mandated bottleneck?”

Intentional chemical doping of halide perovskites (HP) produces very low carriers concentration,
not exceeding 1014cm-3. One wonders if this represents a temporary setback due to insufficient

 34

optimization of the synthesis process, or an intrinsic, physically mandated bottleneck. To
elucidate this, we use 3 Design Principles (DP), needed to be satisfied for ideal doping that are
also supported by density functional theory calculations. These are DP(i): The impurities or
structural defect should have shallow thermodynamic transition levels, lying close to the
conduction band (CB) for n-type, or valence band (VB) for p-type; DP(ii): The Fermi level (EF)
pinning energy representing the maximum and minimum position of EF, should be closed to VBM
for p-type or CBM for n-type; and DP(iii): The shift in the doping-induced equilibrium Fermi
energy should be sufficiently large to have, after doping, EF close to VBM for p-type or CBM for
n-type. For inorganic HP, we find that there are numerous shallow level dopants that satisfy DP(i).
In contrast DP(ii) is satisfied only for holes; and DP(iii) fail for both holes and electrons, being the
ultimate bottleneck for the n-type and p-type doping in systems based on I, Br and Cl.

 APS March Meeting 2023; Volume 68, Number 3; Las Vegas, Nevada (March 5-10) Virtual (March
2022); Session S41: “Spin and Chiral Properties of Halide Perovskites” Abstract: S41.00008:
“Correlation between band gap shifts due to symmetry breaking and spin-orbital coupling in
halide perovskites”

The calculation of band gaps of Halide perovskites (HP) differs from analogous calculations of
more conventional semiconductors in that two additional factors need to be considered: (i)
creation of a structural polymorphous network (a distribution of octahedral titling for different
octahedra in cubic structures) – which increase the band gaps and (ii) spin-orbital coupling (SOC)
– generally reducing the band gaps for compounds with high Z atoms. This raises the question to
what extent effects (i) and (ii) compensate each other or if they are correlated in some way. We
addressed this question by a series of DFT band structure calculations on cubic ABX3 compounds
where effects (i) and (ii) are both included or both excluded or including one at the time. For the
most studied inorganic halide perovskites, the SOC - induced band gap reduction has a low
correlation with the band gap increase due to polymorphous network formation. The accuracy
of treating the gap shifts due to (i) and (ii) as independent corrections for nominal cubic Pm-3m
structures will be presented and discussed.

2024 APS March Meeting; March 4–8, 2024; Minneapolis & Virtual Session F01: Theory and
Simulation of Halide Perovskites Abstract: F01.00006: Photoinduced Frenkel Pair Formation Drive
Phase-Segregation in Halide Perovskite Alloys

Alloys of ABX3 halide perovskite (HP) display unique phase behavior distinct from traditional III-
V and II-VI semiconductor alloys. While the latter alloys phase-segregate based on size mismatch,
alloys of HP with different halogens exhibit good miscibility in the dark but segregate under light.
Our DFT alloy calculations1 reveal that the key factors are (i) light-induced defect formation and

 35

(ii) atomic migration of dissociated members. Specifically, Halogen vacancies (VX) and interstitials
(Xi) form Frenkel-pair defects, which drive phase segregation in HP alloys. At a threshold bromine
composition, photogenerated holes localize, creating a doubly charged iodine Frenkel-pair
(VI+Ii)2+. Faster iodine migration over bromine interstitials leads to iodine-rich and iodine-
depleted regions, which results in phase segregation. Dark thermal annealing removes mobile
defects, reversing the segregation. This understanding enables control of phase segregation by
selecting elements (e.g., replacing Pb with Sn) that cannot form stable Frenkel defects on the B
site, offering potential in solar technology. [1] F.P. Sabino et al. Adv. Energy Mater. 2023,
2301539.

V. Path forward: vision of what the next steps beyond this work might
be

Summary of the broader picture:

Background: This project focused on developing and applying a theoretical approach for
identifying new halide perovskites, with an experimental exploratory component (NREL,
Highlight VI), interacting with an Industry working group (Highlight VIII). The work involved two
categories of Halide perovskites (A) Single halide perovskite ordered compounds (‘digital
composition, results described in Sec II and III), and (B) Disordered alloys of multiple halide
perovskites (include fractional compositions; could phase separate; results described in
Highlights I, IV, V, VI and VII). The foundational realization in this field has been that the science
and technology developed historically for elemental semiconductors (Si, Ge) as well as III-V
compound semiconductors is very different than the underlying science required for
understanding and device development for halide perovskites systems. This called for a method
development effort, based on a series of developments of a theoretical methodology. Rather
than do all calculations by a single level of theory (impractical for an initial ensemble of 105
potential candidates), we have created a hierarchy of theory filters, starting with empirical
phenomenological methods, and successively moving to first principles method applied to
progressively smaller sets of single compounds (A above) that passed the simpler filters.
Applications to multi component alloys (B above) were based exclusively on the higher rung of
the Hierarchical Approach (first principles DFT, using the Special Quasirandom Structure
approach, developed earlier for alloys). Journal publications describing the resulting science are
given in Refs. [1]-[7].

Perspective on the Path Forward:

1. Applications to single ordered compounds (A above) predicted a number of previously
unforeseen halide perovskite systems. This offers now an opportunity for a future collaboration
with synthesis groups to examine such predictions. We believe that an effective collaboration
would involve iterative cycles between theory and experiment rather than using one of the two

disciplines. A successful example of laboratory synthesis of theoretically predicted compounds is

2. Applications to disordered alloys of multiple halide perovskites (B above) revealed a number
 of newly discovered alloy phenomena that call in our opinion for both experimental and
 theoretical follow up. Examples include:

 • Traditional III-V and II-VI semiconductor phase-segregate when the components are size
 mismatched. Halide perovskites alloys show good miscibility in the dark but can phase-
 segregate under light. Research indicated that removal of the mobile defects (the agent
 of segregation) by dark thermal annealing, supplies the opposing force, leading to reversal
 of phase-segregation (Ref. [1]).
 • Research showed that the complex predicted dependence of alloy mixing enthalpy and
 alloy band gap on composition can be explained by simple geometric manner. Specifically,
 the local atomic motifs in halide perovskite alloys reflect the landscape of enthalpies and
 band gaps. This opens a new outlook of future short cuts in navigating the space of mixing
 enthalpy and band gaps of complex multi component alloys by considering local structural
       motifs (Ref. [6]).
 • Conventional tetrahedral semiconductors generally have alloy band gaps that are lower
 than the composition average gap of the constituent (“downward bowing”). This project
 designed theoretically multi component halide perovskite alloys that have upward
 bowing, where the alloy has a significantly larger gap than the composition average of its
 components. Furthermore, such multi component alloys have a rather low mixing
 enthalpy, suggesting stability towards phase separation. The idea is to mix perovskite
 ABX3 that has low lying s-orbitals in the valence band, (bearing an IVB-group atom Ge, Sn,
 Pb) with a compound that has IB atom (e.g., Cd) that has s-orbitals in the conduction band.
 This opens a novel alloy design strategy for obtaining alloys that have wider gaps than any
 of their components. (Ref. [7]).

3. The novel addition to combine experimental and theoretical research with an “Industry
 working group” led by L.L. Kazmerski is an excellent way to accelerate progress from theoretical
 research to the lab, and hopefully to production.

 We combine graph neural networks (GNN) with an inexpensive and reliable structure generation
 approach based on the bond-valence method (BVM) to train accurate machine learning models for

 these two ML models, 48 compounds were identified out of 222,960 candidates as both stable and
 that have an HSE band gap that is relevant for photovoltaic applications. For this subset, only 8
 have been reported to date, indicating that 40 compounds remain unexplored to the best of our
