Limiting spectral distributions of large consistent rank correlation matrices
Zhaorui Dong, Fang Han, and Jianfeng Yao

Abstract
We study random matrices whose entries are obtained by applying consistent rank correlations, such as Hoeffding’s D D , pairwise to a high-dimensional random vector with mutually independent components. Prior work has shown that, in the proportional high-dimensional regime, the empirical spectral distributions of large Kendall’s tau and Spearman’s rho matrices converge weakly almost surely to the Marchenko–Pastur law. By contrast, we prove that for consistent rank correlations such as Hoeffding’s D D , the limiting spectral distribution is given by the semicircle law. Our result thus generalizes a recent work of Dong, Han, and Yao (2025), who considered the special case of Chatterjee’s rank correlation and established the first semicircle law for a large correlation matrix in the proportional regime.



Keywords
: consistent rank correlation, empirical spectral distribution, semicircle law, Hoeffding’s
D
.

Introduction

For each
n=1,2,\ldots
, let
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{n}
be independent and identically distributed
p
-dimensional random vectors, where

\bm{X}_{i}=(X_{i,1},\ldots,X_{i,p})^{\intercal},\qquad i\in[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}.

We work under the assumption that

\displaystyle X_{1,1},\ldots,X_{1,p}\text{ are mutually independent and continuous}.

(1.1)

Our interest lies in the proportional high-dimensional regime, in which the distribution of
\bm{X}_{1}
may depend on
n
and

\displaystyle p=p(n),\qquad n\rightarrow\infty,\qquad\frac{p}{n}\to\gamma\in(0,\infty).

(1.2)

1.1
Consistent rank correlations

Our focus is on correlation matrices whose entries are rank-based U-statistics capable of detecting nonlinear and non-monotone dependence between pairs
(X_{1,j},X_{1,k})
. More specifically, we study
p\times p
matrices

\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}=[\widehat{R}_{jk}]_{j,k\in[p]},

which we call
consistent rank correlation matrices
, with entries given by

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{jk}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }j=k,\\[3.00003pt]
\binom{n}{m}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leq n}h\Big(\binom{X_{i_{1},j}}{X_{i_{1},k}},\ldots,\binom{X_{i_{m},j}}{X_{i_{m},k}}\Big),&\text{if }j\neq k.\end{cases}

(1.3)

Here
h:(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a fixed and
symmetric
kernel of order
m
satisfying the following two critical properties:

(i)

Rank-based.
The kernel
h
depends on the inputs only through the rankings of the data; see Definition
2.2
for a precise definition.

(ii)

Consistent.
If
\bm{Z}=(Z_{1},Z_{2})
is Lebesgue absolutely continuous and
\bm{Z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{m}
are
m
independent copies of
\bm{Z}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[h(\bm{Z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{m})\big]=0\quad\text{if and only if}\quad Z_{1}\text{ is independent of }Z_{2}.

We call statistics of the form (
1.3
)
consistent rank correlations
, since they give rise to rank-based tests of independence that are consistent against general alternatives
(
23
)
. In particular,
12
established a number of results on their statistical properties, including the limiting distribution of

\max_{j\neq k\in[p]}|\widehat{R}_{jk}|

under (
1.1
) and (
1.2
). See also
23
for the power of independence tests using these consistent rank correlations, as well as
15
for some related results on Spearman and Kendall correlation matrices.

Prominent examples of consistent rank correlations of the form (
1.3
) include Hoeffding’s
D

(
17
)
, Blum–Kiefer–Rosenblatt’s
R

(
6
)
, and Bergsma–Dassios–Yanagimoto’s
\tau^{*}

(
5
;
25
)
. Specifically, for arbitrary
\bm{z}_{j}=(z_{j,1},z_{j,2})^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
j=1,2,\cdots
, these statistics correspond to (
1.3
) with kernels given by

\displaystyle\text{\bf(Hoeffding's $D$)}\qquad h_{D}(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{5})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{16}\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{5})\in\mathcal{P}_{5}}\Big[\big\{\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{1},1}\leq z_{i_{5},1})-\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{2},1}\leq z_{i_{5},1})\big\}\big\{\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{3},1}\leq z_{i_{5},1})-\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{4},1}\leq z_{i_{5},1})\big\}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\times\Big[\big\{\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{1},2}\leq z_{i_{5},2})-\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{2},2}\leq z_{i_{5},2})\big\}\big\{\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{3},2}\leq z_{i_{5},2})-\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{4},2}\leq z_{i_{5},2})\big\}\Big],

(1.4)

\displaystyle\text{\bf(Blum--Kiefer--Rosenblatt's $R$)}\qquad h_{R}(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{6})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{32}\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{6})\in\mathcal{P}_{6}}\big[\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{1},1}\leq z_{i_{5},1})-\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{2},1}\leq z_{i_{5},1})\big]\big[\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{3},1}\leq z_{i_{5},1})-\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{4},1}\leq z_{i_{5},1})\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\times\big[\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{1},2}\leq z_{i_{6},2})-\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{2},2}\leq z_{i_{6},2})\big]\big[\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{3},2}\leq z_{i_{6},2})-\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{4},2}\leq z_{i_{6},2})\big],

(1.5)

and

\displaystyle\text{\bf(Bergsma--Dassios--Yanagimoto's $\tau^{*}$)}\qquad h_{\tau^{*}}(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{4})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{16}\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{4})\in\mathcal{P}_{4}}\Big\{\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{1},1},z_{i_{3},1}<z_{i_{2},1},z_{i_{4},1})+\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{2},1},z_{i_{4},1}<z_{i_{1},1},z_{i_{3},1})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{1},1},z_{i_{4},1}<z_{i_{2},1},z_{i_{3},1})-\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{2},1},z_{i_{3},1}<z_{i_{1},1},z_{i_{4},1})\Big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\Big\{\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{1},2},z_{i_{3},2}<z_{i_{2},2},z_{i_{4},2})+\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{2},2},z_{i_{4},2}<z_{i_{1},2},z_{i_{3},2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{1},2},z_{i_{4},2}<z_{i_{2},2},z_{i_{3},2})-\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(z_{i_{2},2},z_{i_{3},2}<z_{i_{1},2},z_{i_{4},2})\Big\}.

(1.6)

Here, for each positive integer

\mathcal{P}_{m}

\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(\cdot)

In contrast to

) and (

), the spectrum of the standardized correlation matrix

\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}=\sqrt{n}\,(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}-\mathbf{I}_{p}),

1.2
Motivation

Our work contributes to a growing body of literature on the spectral analysis of large random matrices arising in statistics and machine learning, with particular emphasis on those whose limiting spectral distributions (LSDs) deviate in an essential way from the Marchenko-Pastur (MP) law.

To place our results in context, we briefly review the literature on LSD theory for large random matrices. Under the proportional regime (
1.2
), the seminal works of
24
and
21
established the two fundamental limiting laws in random matrix theory, namely, the semicircle law and the MP law. More precisely, the empirical spectral distributions (ESDs) of Wigner matrices and sample covariance matrices converge to these two limits, respectively. It was later discovered that the LSDs of a variety of correlation matrices of statistical interest, including large Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall correlation matrices (the latest up to an appropriate rescaling), are likewise governed by the MP law
(
18
;
3
;
4
)
. These results were further sharpened by
9
and
11
, who derived necessary and sufficient conditions for the MP law to hold for Pearson correlation matrices.

These developments naturally raise the following question: under (
1.2
), are there statistically meaningful classes of large random matrices whose LSDs are not given by the MP law? A partial answer was provided by
16
, who studied large Pearson correlation matrices in the heavy-tailed setting and showed that their LSD is given by a convolution of the MP law with a heavy-tailed component. More recently,
8
discovered that the LSD of a Chatterjee rank correlation matrix, whose entries are given by pairwise Chatterjee rank correlations
(
7
;
19
)
, is in fact the semicircle law.

It is also worth noting that a recent and active line of work in random matrix theory investigates limiting spectral behavior beyond the proportional regime (
1.2
). In particular, when
p/n\to 0
,
1
and
10
showed that the LSDs of Pearson’s covariance matrix and the correlation matrices of Spearman and Kendall (the latest again up to suitable rescaling), much like the phenomenon discovered in
8
, are in fact governed by the semicircle law. In a related direction,
14
,
20
, and
13
, among many others, studied the LSDs of random inner-product kernel matrices, which may be viewed as generalized covariance matrices, under polynomial scaling regimes. Their results show that the limiting law in this case is given by a free additive convolution of the MP law and the semicircle law.

The present paper contributes to this growing literature on non-MP limits. Our main motivation comes from
8
, which, to the best of our knowledge, provides by far the
only
non-MP limit theorem for a large correlation matrix in the proportional regime (
1.2
). More specifically, we extend the result therein from Chatterjee’s rank correlation to a broader class of consistent rank correlation coefficients, and in doing so provide a unified perspective that also interestingly connects with the works of
1
,
10
,
14
,
20
, and
13
.

1.3
Our results

Consider a
p\times p
(possibly random) matrix
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}_{p}
with real eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})\geq\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{A}).

The ESD of

F^{\mathbf{A}}:=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A})},

Next, let

denote Wigner’s semicircle law with center

\rho(x;r)=\frac{2}{\pi r^{2}}\sqrt{(r^{2}-x^{2})_{+}},

where
(x)_{+}:=x\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(x\geq 0)
denotes the positive part of
x
.

Lastly, denote

\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{D},~~\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{R},~~{\rm and}~~\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{\tau^{*}}

to be the sample correlation matrices based on Hoeffding’s
D
, Blum–Kiefer–Rosenblatt’s
R
, and Bergsma–Dassios–Yanagimoto’s
\tau^{*}
, respectively, obtained by choosing the kernel
h(\cdot)
in Section
1.1
to be
h_{D}
,
h_{R}
, and
h_{\tau^{*}}
introduced in (
1.4
), (
1.5
), and (
1.6
), respectively.

Our main theorem, presented in Section
2
, yields the following
distribution-free
semicircle laws for these matrices.

Corollary 1.1
.

Under (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), we have almost surely,

\displaystyle F^{\sqrt{n}\Big(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{D}-\mathbf{I}_{p}\Big)}\Rightarrow\operatorname{W}(2\sqrt{2\gamma}/3),

\displaystyle F^{\sqrt{n}\Big(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{R}-\mathbf{I}_{p}\Big)}\Rightarrow\operatorname{W}(2\sqrt{2\gamma}),

and

\displaystyle F^{\sqrt{n}\Big(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{\tau^{*}}-\mathbf{I}_{p}\Big)}\Rightarrow\operatorname{W}(6\sqrt{2\gamma}/5).

2
Main results

This section presents our main general results, of which Corollary
1.1
is a special case. Following
12
, we formulate these results within a general U-statistic framework. Specifically, for each
j\neq k\in[p]
, define

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{jk}=\binom{n}{m}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{m}\leq n}h\Big(\binom{X_{i_{1},j}}{X_{i_{1},k}},\ldots,\binom{X_{i_{m},j}}{X_{i_{m},k}}\Big),

(2.1)

so that the correlation matrix in (
1.3
) may equivalently be written as

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{jk}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }j=k,\\[3.00003pt]
\widehat{U}_{jk},&\text{if }j\neq k.\end{cases}

(2.2)

To state our general results, we first introduce several basic notions concerning U-statistics.

Definition 2.1

(Symmetric kernel)
.

A kernel
h:(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
symmetric
if

h(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{m})=h(\bm{z}_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,\bm{z}_{\sigma(m)})

for every permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
and every
\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Definition 2.2

(Rank-based kernel)
.

A kernel
h:(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
rank-based
if, for any vectors
\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have

h(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{m})=h(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{m}),

where
\bm{r}_{i}=(r_{i,1},r_{i,2})
records the ranks of
z_{i,1}
and
z_{i,2}
among
z_{1,1},\ldots,z_{m,1}
and
z_{1,2},\ldots,z_{m,2}
, respectively.

Definition 2.3

(
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{Z}}
-degenerate kernel)
.

Let
\bm{Z},\bm{Z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be independent random vectors with common distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{Z}}
. A symmetric kernel
h:(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{Z}}
-degenerate
, or simply
degenerate
, if

\operatorname{Var}\big(h_{1}(\bm{Z};\mathbb{P}_{\bm{Z}})\big)=0.

Here, for each
\ell\in[m]
, we define

h_{\ell}(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{\ell};\mathbb{P}_{\bm{Z}}):=\mathbb{E}\Big[h(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{\ell},\bm{Z}_{\ell+1},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{m})\Big].

With these notions in place, we are ready to state the main assumptions on the U-statistics under consideration. These assumptions are collected in the following condition. Throughout, let
\mathbb{P}_{0}
denote the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
, and let “
\otimes
” denote the product of measures. Then
\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{0}
denotes the uniform distribution on
[0,1]^{2}
.

Assumption 2.1
.

Let
\bm{Z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be independent random vectors sampled from
\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{0}
. The kernel
h(\cdot)
is assumed to satisfy the following conditions.

(i)

The kernel
h
is symmetric, rank-based, and bounded. Moreover, it is mean-zero and degenerate under independent continuous margins, in the sense that

\mathbb{E}\big[h(\bm{Z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{m})\big]=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}\Big[h_{1}\big(\bm{Z}_{1};\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{0}\big)^{2}\Big]=0.

(ii)

There exists a function
g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
such that, with
\bm{Z}_{1}=(Z_{1,1},Z_{1,2})^{\intercal}
and
\bm{Z}_{2}=(Z_{2,1},Z_{2,2})^{\intercal}
,

h_{2}(\bm{Z}_{1},\bm{Z}_{2};\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{0})=g(Z_{1,1},Z_{2,1})\,g(Z_{1,2},Z_{2,2})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

(iii)

Furthermore,
g(Z_{1,1},Z_{2,1})
admits the expansion

g(Z_{1,1},Z_{2,1})=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{r}\psi_{r}(Z_{1,1})\psi_{r}(Z_{2,1}),

where
\{\lambda_{r}\}
and
\{\psi_{r}\}
are the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions associated with the integral equation

\mathbb{E}\big[g(z_{1,1},Z_{1,2})\psi(Z_{1,2})\big]=\lambda\,\psi(z_{1,1}),\qquad\text{for all }z_{1,1}\in\mathbb{R},

and satisfy

\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{r}|\in(0,\infty),\qquad\sup_{r}\|\psi_{r}\|_{\infty}<\infty.

Remark 2.1
.

Assumption
2.1
(i) is exactly Assumption 2.1(i)–(ii) in
12
. Assumption
2.1
(ii)–(iii) is stronger than Assumption 2.1(iii) in
12
. Indeed, under Assumption
2.1
(ii)–(iii), the kernel
h_{2}(\bm{Z}_{1},\bm{Z}_{2};\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{0})
admits the expansion

h_{2}(\bm{Z}_{1},\bm{Z}_{2};\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{0})=\sum_{r,s=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{\lambda}_{r,s}\varphi_{r,s}(\bm{Z}_{1})\varphi_{r,s}(\bm{Z}_{2}),

with eigenvalues
\{\widetilde{\lambda}_{r,s}\}
and eigenfunctions
\{\varphi_{r,s}\}
given by

\widetilde{\lambda}_{r,s}=\lambda_{r}\lambda_{s},\qquad\varphi_{r,s}(\bm{Z}_{1})=\psi_{r}(Z_{1,1})\psi_{s}(Z_{1,2}),

and satisfying

\sum_{r,s=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{\lambda}_{r,s}=\Big(\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{r}\Big)^{2}<\infty.

Therefore, Assumption
2.1
(ii)–(iii) is a special case of
12
.

Based on the work of
12

1.1
all satisfy Assumption
2.1
.

Proposition 2.1
.

The following statements hold.

(i)

Hoeffding’s
D
.
The kernel

, with the corresponding function

(ii)

Blum–Kiefer–Rosenblatt’s
R
.
The kernel
h_{R}
in (
1.5
) satisfies Assumption
2.1
, with

h_{R,2}=2h_{D,2}.

(iii)

Bergsma–Dassios–Yanagimoto’s
\tau^{*}

The kernel

in (

) satisfies Assumption

, with

Moreover, the three consistent rank correlations discussed in Section

are all generated by kernels that are consistent for testing independence.

Proposition 2.2

(Proposition 3 in

)
.

Let
\bm{Z}=(Z_{1},Z_{2})^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

Assume (

), and Assumption

3
Proof of main results

We first introduce some additional notation. Write

\mathbb{R}^{+}:=\{u\in\mathbb{R}:u>0\},\qquad\mathbb{C}^{+}:=\{u+\mathrm{i}v:u,v\in\mathbb{R},\ v>0\},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\},

where
\mathrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}
is the imaginary unit. For any complex number
z
, let
\Im z
denote its imaginary part. For two random variables
X
and
Y
, we write
X\perp Y
if
X
and
Y
are independent. For two sequences
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty},\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we write
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
if
a_{n}\leq Cb_{n}
for some constant
C
and all sufficiently large
n
,
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
if
a_{n}=b_{n}(1+o(1))
, and
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
if both
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
and
b_{n}\lesssim a_{n}
hold.

For a matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
, let
\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{X})
denote its trace and
\|\mathbf{X}\|_{S_{p}}
denote its Schatten
p
-norm defined by

\|\mathbf{X}\|_{S_{p}}:=\Bigl(\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}(\mathbf{X})^{p}\Bigr)^{1/p},\qquad p\geq 1,

where
\{\sigma_{j}(\mathbf{X})\}_{j}
are the singular values of
\mathbf{X}
. In particular,

\|\mathbf{X}\|_{\mathrm{F}}:=\|\mathbf{X}\|_{S_{2}}~~~{\rm and}~~~\|\mathbf{X}\|_{\mathrm{op}}:=\|\mathbf{X}\|_{S_{\infty}}

correspond to the Frobenius and spectral norms, respectively. For a square matrix
\mathbf{A}
, write

D_{0}(\mathbf{A}):=\mathbf{A}-\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{A}).

Here
\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{A})
denotes the diagonal matrix made with the diagonal entries of
\mathbf{A}
.
For a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
, its Stieltjes transform is defined by

s(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mu(dx)}{x-z},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

In particular, the Stieltjes transform of Wigner’s semicircle distribution
\mathrm{W}(r)
is

m_{r}(z)=-\frac{2}{r^{2}}\Bigl(z-\sqrt{z^{2}-r^{2}}\Bigr),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

(3.1)

For a symmetric matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, we write
s_{\mathbf{A}}(z)
for the Stieltjes transform of

.

3.1
A proof outline

In our proof, without loss of generality, we assume that the entries
\{X_{ij}:i\in[n],\,j\in[p]\}
are independently distributed according to
\mathbb{P}_{0}
. This does not affect the distribution of
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
under Assumption
1.1
, since each
\widehat{R}_{jk}
is rank-based.

We first reduce the analysis of
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
to that of its leading term. Since the U-statistic
\widehat{R}_{jk}
is degenerate under Assumption
2.1
, the first nonvanishing term in its Hoeffding decomposition is a second-order U-statistic, given by

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{jk}^{(1)}:=\frac{m(m-1)}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n}h_{2}\left(\binom{X_{i_{1},j}}{X_{i_{1},k}},\binom{X_{i_{2},j}}{X_{i_{2},k}}\right).

By Assumption
2.1
,

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{jk}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{m(m-1)}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n}g(X_{i_{1},j},X_{i_{2},j})g(X_{i_{1},k},X_{i_{2},k})

\displaystyle=\frac{m(m-1)}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n}\sum_{r,s=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{r}\lambda_{s}\psi_{r}(X_{i_{1},j})\psi_{s}(X_{i_{1},k})\psi_{r}(X_{i_{2},j})\psi_{s}(X_{i_{2},k}).

(3.2)

Setting
\widehat{R}_{jj}^{(1)}:=1
, we define the leading term of
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
by

\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{(1)}:=[\widehat{R}_{jk}^{(1)}]_{j,k\in[p]}.

Later we will show that, at the LSD scale, the difference between
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{(1)}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
is negligible. We may therefore restrict attention to the LSD of
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{(1)}
.

Next, following the analysis in
23
, we apply a truncation argument to handle the infinite sum over
r
and
s
in (
3.2
). More precisely, for
T\in\mathbb{N}
, define the truncated coefficient

\widehat{R}_{jk;T}^{(1)}:=\frac{m(m-1)}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n}\sum_{r,s=1}^{T}\lambda_{r}\lambda_{s}\psi_{r}(X_{i_{1},j})\psi_{s}(X_{i_{1},k})\psi_{r}(X_{i_{2},j})\psi_{s}(X_{i_{2},k})

for
j\neq k\in[p]
, and set

\widehat{R}_{jj;T}^{(1)}:=1

for
j\in[p]
. We then introduce the truncated leading matrix

\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;T}^{(1)}:=\bigl(\widehat{R}_{jk;T}^{(1)}\bigr)_{j,k\in[p]}.

Later we will show that, at the LSD scale, for all sufficiently large
T
, the difference between
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;T}^{(1)}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{(1)}
is negligible. It therefore remains to analyze the LSD of
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;T}^{(1)}
.

Next, we rewrite
\widehat{R}_{jk;T}^{(1)}
as

\widehat{R}_{jk;T}^{(1)}=\frac{m(m-1)}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n}\Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^{T}\lambda_{r}\psi_{r}(X_{i_{1},j})\psi_{r}(X_{i_{2},j})\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{s=1}^{T}\lambda_{s}\psi_{s}(X_{i_{1},k})\psi_{s}(X_{i_{2},k})\Bigr).

This representation shows that the off-diagonal part of
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;T}^{(1)}
admits a high-dimensional Gram matrix representation. To formalize this, define

\mathcal{M}_{2}:=\{(i_{1},i_{2}):1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n\},\qquad M:=|\mathcal{M}_{2}|=\frac{n(n-1)}{2},

and set

A_{\bm{i},k}:=\sum_{r=1}^{T}\lambda_{r}\psi_{r}(X_{i_{1},k})\psi_{r}(X_{i_{2},k}),\qquad\text{for all }\bm{i}=(i_{1},i_{2})\in\mathcal{M}_{2}\text{ and }k\in[p].

Then, for
j\neq k\in[p]
, we have

\widehat{R}_{jk;T}^{(1)}=\frac{m(m-1)}{n(n-1)}\,\bm{A}_{\cdot,k}^{\intercal}\bm{A}_{\cdot,j},

where
\bm{A}_{\cdot,k}:=(A_{\bm{i},k})_{\bm{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Introducing

\mathbf{A}:=(A_{\bm{i},k})_{\bm{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{2},\,k\in[p]},

and recalling that
\widehat{R}_{jj;T}^{(1)}:=1
, the preceding identity yields the off-diagonal Gram representation

D_{0}\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;T}^{(1)}\bigr)=\frac{m(m-1)}{n(n-1)}\,D_{0}\bigl(\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}\bigr).

Equivalently,
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;T}^{(1)}
is obtained from

\frac{m(m-1)}{n(n-1)}\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}

by replacing its diagonal entries with ones. Here the matrix
\mathbf{A}
depends on both
n
and
T
. Thus, the off-diagonal part of
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;T}^{(1)}
admits an explicit Gram matrix representation. Accordingly, the associated rescaled matrix can be written as the following diagonal-removed Gram matrix:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{n}\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;T}^{(1)}-\mathbf{I}_{p}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{m(m-1)}{\sqrt{n}(n-1)}\,D_{0}\bigl(\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{m(m-1)}{\sqrt{n}(n-1)}\bigl(\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}-\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A})\bigr).

As will be shown later, the LSD of
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}
is well approximated by that of
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}
. Moreover, the matrix
\mathbf{A}
appearing in
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

The columns of
\mathbf{A}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), and each column has covariance matrix
\sigma_{T}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{M}
for some constant
\sigma_{T}>0
that possibly depends on
T
.

(ii)

Under (
1.2
), the dimension of
\mathbf{A}
is of order
O(n^{2})\times n
.

These observations strongly suggest that
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}
, and hence also
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}
, should satisfy the semicircle law, in view of the seminal result of
1
on the LSD of Gram matrices in the ultra-high-dimensional regime.

Unfortunately, several technical obstacles remain. In particular, the results of
1
are not directly applicable here, since the entries of
\mathbf{A}
are correlated. Establishing the semicircle law therefore requires additional control of the concentration of certain random quadratic forms associated with the columns of
\mathbf{A}
. This issue will be addressed in Section
4.1.3
, Lemma
4.2
.

To summarize, our proof proceeds through two approximation steps for the original sample correlation matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
. First, we approximate
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
entrywise by a second-order U-statistic, thereby obtaining
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{(1)}
. Second, we truncate the infinite sum in (
3.2
) to obtain
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;T}^{(1)}
. After these two approximations, the off-diagonal part of
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;T}^{(1)}
admits the useful Gram matrix representation

D_{0}\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;T}^{(1)}\bigr)=\frac{m(m-1)}{n(n-1)}D_{0}\bigl(\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}\bigr),

although the entries of
\mathbf{A}
remain correlated.

In Section
3.2
, we establish the semicircle limit of

\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}=\sqrt{n}\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;T}^{(1)}-\mathbf{I}_{p}\bigr)
,
which serves as a good approximation to the target matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}
, by following the classical Stieltjes transform method of
2
. The main technical challenge is to control the dependence among the entries of
\mathbf{A}
. We then prove the LSD of
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}
in Section
3.3
by carefully quantifying the errors introduced in these two approximation steps.

3.2
Technical preparation: Semicircle law of
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}

We retain the notation and definitions introduced in Section
3.1
. In addition, for
\bm{i},\bm{j}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
, with

\bm{i}=(i_{1},i_{2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bm{j}=(j_{1},j_{2}),

we
define

\delta_{\mu\nu}:=\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(\mu=\nu).

We further write

\bm{i}\cap\bm{j}\neq\emptyset\qquad\text{if}\qquad\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\cap\{j_{1},j_{2}\}\neq\emptyset.

Since the dependence among the entries of
\mathbf{A}
is the central issue in the proof, we begin by identifying the correlation structure of
\mathbf{A}
. Assumption
2.1
implies that for all
i\in[n]
and
k\in[p]
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\psi_{r}(X_{ik})\big]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}\big[\psi_{r}(X_{ik})\psi_{s}(X_{ik})\big]=\delta_{rs}.

(3.3)

Consequently, the entrywise variance of
\mathbf{A}
is given by

\mathbb{E}\big[A_{\bm{i},k}^{2}\big]=\sum_{r=1}^{T}\lambda_{r}^{2}.

Accordingly, we normalize
\mathbf{A}
by introducing

\sigma_{T}:=\Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^{T}\lambda_{r}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}:=\sigma_{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A},

so that each entry of
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
has unit variance. Note that

\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}=(\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i},k})_{\bm{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{2},\ k\in[p]}
, where

\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i},k}=\sigma_{T}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{T}\lambda_{r}\psi_{r}(X_{i_{1},k})\psi_{r}(X_{i_{2},k}),\qquad\bm{i}=(i_{1},i_{2})\in\mathcal{M}_{2}.

The following five properties of
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
are then immediate.

(i)

The column vectors
\{\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{\cdot,k}:k\in[p]\}
are i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
.

(ii)

By Assumption
2.1
, the entries of
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
are uniformly bounded:

\sup_{\bm{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{2},\ k\in[p]}|\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i},k}|\leq\sigma_{T}^{-1}\Bigl(\sup_{r}\|\psi_{r}\|_{\infty}\Bigr)^{2}\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{r}|<\infty.

(iii)

The random vector
\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{\cdot,1}
is isotropic in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
, since

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i},1}]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i},1}^{2}]=1,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i},1}\widetilde{A}_{\bm{j},1}]=\delta_{\bm{i}\bm{j}}.

(iv)

If
\bm{i}\cap\bm{j}=\emptyset
, then the random variables
\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i},1}
and
\widetilde{A}_{\bm{j},1}
are independent.

(v)

The collection
\{\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i},1}:\bm{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}\}
is not jointly independent. For example, let
\bm{i}_{1}=(1,2)
,
\bm{i}_{2}=(2,3)
, and
\bm{i}_{3}=(1,3)
. Using (
3.3
) once again,

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i}_{1},1}\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i}_{2},1}\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i}_{3},1}]=\sigma_{T}^{-3}\sum_{r=1}^{T}\lambda_{r}^{3}\neq\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i}_{1},1}]\,\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i}_{2},1}]\,\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i}_{3},1}].

Nevertheless, the dependence within
(\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i},k})_{\bm{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}}
is relatively weak. The following lemma captures this weak dependence by characterizing the vanishing of certain cross moments, and it will play a key role in the proof.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider arbitrary
\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{Q}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
, where
\bm{i}_{q}=(i_{1}^{q},i_{2}^{q})
for each
q\in[Q]
. If, among the
2Q
indices

i_{1}^{1},i_{2}^{1},\ldots,i_{1}^{Q},i_{2}^{Q},

there exists an index that appears exactly once, then

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\prod_{q=1}^{Q}\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i}_{q},1}\Bigr]=0.

We are now in a position to formulate the semicircle law for
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}
. Define

\mathbf{G}_{n;T}:=\sqrt{\frac{M}{p}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{M}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}-\mathrm{Diag}\Bigl(\frac{1}{M}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\Bigr)\Bigr).

Then

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}=\frac{m(m-1)\sqrt{pM}\sigma_{T}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}(n-1)}\,\mathbf{G}_{n;T},

(3.4)

where the matrix
\mathbf{G}_{n;T}
has a structure analogous to that of the ultra-high-dimensional Gram matrices appearing in
1
, which exhibit semicircle limits. Lemma
3.1
shows that the dependence within
(\widetilde{A}_{\bm{i},k})_{\bm{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}}
is sufficiently weak for
\mathbf{G}_{n;T}
to retain a semicircle limit. This, in turn, yields the semicircle limit of
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}
up to a deterministic scaling factor.

The semicircle law for
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}
is stated in the following propositions.

Proposition 3.1
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
,
z=u+\mathrm{i}v\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, and
T\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigl|s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}}(z)-\mathbb{E}\big[s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}}(z)\big]\bigr|>\varepsilon\Bigr)\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{4}v^{4}p^{2}}.

Proposition 3.2
.

Under (
1.2
), for any fixed
T\in\mathbb{N}
and
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big[s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}}(z)\Big]=m_{r_{T}}(z),

where
\vartheta=\lim_{T\to\infty}r_{T}
with

r_{T}:=m(m-1)\sqrt{2\gamma}\sum_{r=1}^{T}\lambda_{r}^{2},

and
m_{r_{T}}(z)
denotes the Stieltjes transform of
\operatorname{W}(r_{T})
given in (
3.1
).

3.3
Proof of Theorem
2.1

Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and for
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, write
v=\Im z>0
. Let
r_{T}
be as defined in Proposition
3.2
. Since
\lim_{T\to\infty}r_{T}=\vartheta
, there exists a constant
T_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\big|m_{r_{T}}(z)-m_{\vartheta}(z)\big|<\frac{\varepsilon}{5},\qquad\text{for all }T>T_{\varepsilon}.

(3.5)

Applying the same argument as in the proof of Proposition
3.1
to

\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{(1)}:=\sqrt{n}\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{(1)}-\mathbf{I}_{p}\bigr),

we obtain that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\big|s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{(1)}}(z)-\mathbb{E}\big[s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{(1)}}(z)\big]\big|>\frac{\varepsilon}{5}\Bigr)\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{4}v^{4}p^{2}}.

(3.6)

By the variance formula for U-statistics
(
22
, p. 189)
, as
n\to\infty
,

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\big|\widehat{R}_{12}^{(1)}-\widehat{R}_{12;T}^{(1)}\big|^{2}\Bigr]=\frac{\xi_{T}}{n^{2}}+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\Bigr)

for some positive constant
\xi_{T}
depending on
T
such that
\xi_{T}\to 0
as
T\to\infty
.
We then choose
\widetilde{T}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\widetilde{T}_{\varepsilon}>T_{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\xi_{\widetilde{T}_{\varepsilon}}<\frac{\varepsilon^{2}v^{4}}{100\gamma}.

By exchangeability of the samples and coordinates, all off-diagonal entries of

\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{(1)}-\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;\widetilde{T}_{\varepsilon}}^{(1)}

have the same distribution, and likewise all off-diagonal entries of

\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}-\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{(1)}

have the same distribution. We shall use this symmetry in the Frobenius norm bounds below.

Then, by Lemma
5.1
below, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}\big[s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{(1)}}(z)\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;\widetilde{T}_{\varepsilon}}^{(1)}}(z)\big]\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{(1)}}(z)-s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;\widetilde{T}_{\varepsilon}}^{(1)}}(z)\big|^{2}\Big]}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{pv^{4}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{(1)}-\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{n;\widetilde{T}_{\varepsilon}}^{(1)}\big\|_{\operatorname{F}}^{2}\Big]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{pv^{4}}\cdot p(p-1)\cdot\frac{2\xi_{\widetilde{T}_{\varepsilon}}}{n^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{5}.

By Proposition
3.2
and (
3.4
),

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;\widetilde{T}_{\varepsilon}}^{(1)}}(z)\big]=m_{r_{\widetilde{T}_{\varepsilon}}}(z).

Hence, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}\big[s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;\widetilde{T}_{\varepsilon}}^{(1)}}(z)\big]-m_{r_{\widetilde{T}_{\varepsilon}}}(z)\big|<\frac{\varepsilon}{5}.

(3.8)

In addition, by
22
, there exists a constant
D>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\widehat{R}_{12}-\widehat{R}_{12}^{(1)}\big|^{4}\Big]\leq\frac{D}{n^{6}}.

Then, by Lemma
5.1
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}}(z)-s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{(1)}}(z)\big|>\frac{\varepsilon}{5}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\varepsilon^{4}p^{2}v^{8}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{(1)}\big\|_{\operatorname{F}}^{4}\Big]

(3.9)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{\prime}n^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}p^{2}v^{8}}\sum_{j\neq k=1}^{p}\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq k^{\prime}=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\widehat{R}_{jk}-\widehat{R}_{jk}^{(1)}\big|^{2}\big|\widehat{R}_{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}-\widehat{R}_{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}^{(1)}\big|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{\prime}p^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}n^{4}v^{8}}.

Combining (
3.5
)–(
3.9
), we obtain

\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}}(z)-m_{\vartheta}(z)\big|>\varepsilon\Big)\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{4}v^{4}p^{2}}+\frac{C^{\prime}p^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}n^{4}v^{8}}.

By the Borel–Cantelli lemma and the assumption
p\sim n
, it follows that for any fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,

s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n}}(z)-m_{\vartheta}(z)\to 0,\qquad\text{almost surely.}

The proof is therefore completed by a standard argument based on Vitali’s convergence theorem
(
2
, Lemma 2.14)
and the continuity theorem for Stieltjes transforms
(
2
, Theorem B.9)
.

.

4.1.1
Proof of Proposition
2.1

We first focus on Part (i). To this end, by Example 2.1 of
12
, we have

h_{2}\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}z_{1,1}\\
z_{1,2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}z_{2,1}\\
z_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\frac{6}{\pi^{4}i^{2}j^{2}}\cos(\pi iz_{1,1})\cos(\pi iz_{2,1})\cos(\pi jz_{1,2})\cos(\pi jz_{2,2}).

Hence
h_{2}
can be written as

h_{2}\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}z_{1,1}\\
z_{1,2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}z_{2,1}\\
z_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\sum_{r,s=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{r}\lambda_{s}\psi_{r}(z_{1,1})\psi_{r}(z_{2,1})\psi_{s}(z_{1,2})\psi_{s}(z_{2,2}),

where

\lambda_{r}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi^{2}r^{2}},\qquad\psi_{r}(x)=\sqrt{2}\cos(\pi rx).

Equivalently,

h_{2}\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}z_{1,1}\\
z_{1,2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}z_{2,1}\\
z_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\left(\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{r}\psi_{r}(z_{1,1})\psi_{r}(z_{2,1})\right)\left(\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{s}\psi_{s}(z_{1,2})\psi_{s}(z_{2,2})\right).

Define

g(x,y)=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{r}\psi_{r}(x)\psi_{r}(y),\qquad x,y\in[0,1].

(4.1)

Then

h_{2}\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}z_{1,1}\\
z_{1,2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}z_{2,1}\\
z_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}\right)=g(z_{1,1},z_{2,1})\,g(z_{1,2},z_{2,2}).

We next show that
g(x,y)
is given by the expression stated in Proposition
2.1
. By (
4.1
),

\displaystyle g(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi^{2}r^{2}}\cdot 2\cos(\pi rx)\cos(\pi ry)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\pi^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(\pi r(x+y))}{r^{2}}+\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(\pi r(x-y))}{r^{2}}\right).

We use the identity

\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(r\theta)}{r^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\frac{\pi|\theta|}{2}+\frac{\theta^{2}}{4},\qquad\text{for all }\theta\in[-2\pi,2\pi].

Substituting
\theta=\pi(x+y)
and
\theta=\pi(x-y)
yields

\displaystyle g(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}\left(2+3x^{2}+3y^{2}-3x-3y-3|x-y|\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}\left(3x^{2}+3y^{2}-6\max(x,y)+2\right).

This proves Part (i). Parts (ii) and (iii) follow from Examples 2.2 and 2.3 of
12
, respectively.

4.1.2
Proof of Proposition
3.1

Recall that

\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}=b_{n}D_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}),\qquad{\rm with}~~b_{n}:=\frac{m(m-1)}{\sqrt{n}(n-1)}.

Define
\mathscr{A}_{0}:=\{\emptyset\}
and, for
1\leq k\leq p
,

\mathscr{A}_{k}:=\sigma\Bigl(\{\bm{A}_{\cdot,j}:1\leq j\leq k\}\Bigr),\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{A}_{k}}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid\mathscr{A}_{k}].

Let
\mathbf{A}_{-k}
denote the
M\times(p-1)
matrix obtained by removing the
k
th column
\bm{A}_{\cdot,k}
from
\mathbf{A}
. Then

s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}}(z)-\mathbb{E}\big[s_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{n;T}^{(1)}}(z)\big]=\sum_{k=1}^{p}Q_{k},

where

\displaystyle Q_{k}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{A}_{k}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{A}_{k-1}})\Bigl[\frac{1}{p}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(b_{n}D_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A})-z\mathbf{I}\bigr)^{-1}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{A}_{k}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{A}_{k-1}})\Bigl[\frac{1}{p}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(b_{n}D_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A})-z\mathbf{I}\bigr)^{-1}-\frac{1}{p}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(b_{n}D_{0}(\mathbf{A}_{-k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}_{-k})-z\mathbf{I}\bigr)^{-1}\Bigr].

By Lemma
5.2
and denoting
v=\Im z
, we have

\Bigl|\operatorname{tr}\bigl(b_{n}D_{0}(\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A})-z\mathbf{I}\bigr)^{-1}-\operatorname{tr}\bigl(b_{n}D_{0}(\mathbf{A}_{-k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}_{-k})-z\mathbf{I}\bigr)^{-1}\Bigr|\leq\frac{1}{v}.

Therefore,

|Q_{k}|\leq\frac{2}{pv}.

Applying Lemma

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{\cdot,k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}_{k}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{\cdot,k}-\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{B}_{k}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{\mu\neq\nu}b_{\mu\nu}\widetilde{A}_{\mu,k}\widetilde{A}_{\nu,k}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]+2\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{2}}b_{\mu\mu}(\widetilde{A}_{\mu,k}^{2}-1)\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr].

Since
\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{\cdot,k}\perp\mathbf{B}_{k}
, Lemma
3.1
and the uniform boundedness of
(\widetilde{A}_{\mu,k})
imply that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{\mu\neq\nu}b_{\mu\nu}\widetilde{A}_{\mu,k}\widetilde{A}_{\nu,k}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mu\neq\nu,\ \kappa\neq\tau}\mathbb{E}[|b_{\mu\nu}b_{\kappa\tau}|]\cdot\Bigl|\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{A}_{\mu,k}\widetilde{A}_{\nu,k}\widetilde{A}_{\kappa,k}\widetilde{A}_{\tau,k}\big]\Bigr|

\displaystyle\lesssim F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3},

where

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu,\nu,\kappa,\tau}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[|b_{\mu\nu}|^{2}\cdot\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(\mu=\kappa,\ \nu=\tau,\ \mu\neq\nu)\Bigr],

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu,\nu,\kappa,\tau}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[|b_{\mu\nu}b_{\kappa\tau}|\cdot\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(\tau=\nu,\ \mu\cap\kappa\neq\emptyset,\ \mu\cap\nu\neq\emptyset,\ \nu\cap\tau\neq\emptyset,\ \mu\neq\nu\neq\kappa)\Bigr],

\displaystyle F_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu,\nu,\kappa,\tau}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[|b_{\mu\nu}b_{\kappa\tau}|\cdot\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(\mu\cap\nu\neq\emptyset,\ \mu\cap\kappa\neq\emptyset,\ \nu\cap\tau\neq\emptyset,\ \tau\cap\kappa\neq\emptyset,\ \mu\neq\nu\neq\kappa\neq\tau)\Bigr].

By (
4.8
) and (
4.9
),

F_{1}=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\mathbb{E}[|b_{\mu\nu}|^{2}\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(\mu\neq\nu)]\lesssim pM^{2},

F_{2}\lesssim p\cdot\sum_{\mu\neq\nu\neq\kappa}\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(\mu\cap\kappa\neq\emptyset,\ \mu\cap\nu\neq\emptyset,\ \nu\cap\kappa\neq\emptyset)\lesssim p^{4},

F_{3}\lesssim p\cdot\sum_{\mu\neq\nu\neq\kappa\neq\tau}\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(\mu\cap\nu\neq\emptyset,\ \mu\cap\kappa\neq\emptyset,\ \nu\cap\tau\neq\emptyset,\ \tau\cap\kappa\neq\emptyset)\lesssim p^{5}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{\mu\neq\nu}b_{\mu\nu}\widetilde{A}_{\mu,k}\widetilde{A}_{\nu,k}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]\lesssim p^{5}.

(4.11)

Similarly, using the fact that
\widetilde{A}_{\mu,k}\perp\widetilde{A}_{\nu,k}
whenever
\mu\cap\nu=\emptyset
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{\mu}b_{\mu\mu}(\widetilde{A}_{\mu,k}^{2}-1)\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mu,\nu}\mathbb{E}[|b_{\mu\mu}b_{\nu\nu}|]\cdot\Bigl|\mathbb{E}\big[(\widetilde{A}_{\mu,k}^{2}-1)(\widetilde{A}_{\nu,k}^{2}-1)\big]\Bigr|

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\mathbb{E}[|b_{\mu\mu}b_{\nu\nu}|]\cdot\Bigl|\mathbb{E}\big[(\widetilde{A}_{\mu,k}^{2}-1)(\widetilde{A}_{\nu,k}^{2}-1)\big]\Bigr|\cdot\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(\mu\cap\nu\neq\emptyset)

\displaystyle\lesssim G_{1}+G_{2},

where

G_{1}=\sum_{\mu}\mathbb{E}[|b_{\mu\mu}|^{2}]~~~{\rm and}~~~G_{2}=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[|b_{\mu\mu}b_{\nu\nu}|\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(\mu\cap\nu\neq\emptyset,\ \mu\neq\nu)\Bigr].

Then, by (
4.10
) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

G_{1}\lesssim p^{2}M~~~{\rm and}~~~G_{2}\lesssim p^{2}\sum_{\mu\neq\nu}\text{\large 1\hskip-3.40738ptI}(\mu\cap\nu\neq\emptyset)\lesssim p^{5}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{2}}b_{\mu\mu}(\widetilde{A}_{\mu,k}^{2}-1)\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]\lesssim p^{5}.

(4.13)

Combining (
4.11
) and (
4.13
), we conclude that

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{\cdot,k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}_{k}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{\cdot,k}-\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{B}_{k}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]\lesssim p^{5},

and hence (
4.7
) follows.
