Matchings in permutations
Eduard Inozemtsev, Dmitrii Kolupaev, Andrey Kupavskii

Abstract
We say that two permutations [ n ] → [ n ] [n]\to[n] intersect if they map some element x x to the same element y y . A matching in a family of permutations is a collection of pairwise disjoint permutations. In this paper, we study families of permutations with no matchings of size s s . In particular, we obtain a characterization of the largest s s -matching-free families and a Hilton–Milner type result. We also obtain results for the families of derangements.

Introduction

We denote by
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
the standard
n
-element set and by

{[n]\choose k}:=\{F\subset[n]:|F|=k\}
the set of its
k
-element subsets.
A
family
is a collection of sets. For a family
\mathcal{F}
, let
\nu(\mathcal{F})

denote its
matching number
, that is, the maximum number of pairwise
disjoint sets in
\mathcal{F}
. If
\nu(\mathcal{F})=1
, then any two sets in
\mathcal{F}
intersect;
in this case we say that
\mathcal{F}
is
intersecting
.

One of the classical results in extremal combinatorics is the
Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem
[
8
]
, which states that, for
n\geq 2k
, the
largest intersecting family in
{[n]\choose k}
has size at most

{n-1\choose k-1}
. For
n>2k
, equality holds only if
\mathcal{F}
is of the form

\mathcal{S}_{x}:=\{F\in{[n]\choose k}:x\in F\}
for some
x\in[n]
. Such
families are called
stars
or
trivial
, and
x
is called their

center
. If an intersecting family is not pierced by a single element,
then we call it
non-trivial
.

The
covering number

\tau(\mathcal{F})
of
\mathcal{F}
is the size of the smallest set

X
such that
X\cap F\neq\emptyset
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
. The family

\mathcal{S}_{x}
has covering number
1
. Hilton and Milner
[
15
]
gave a
tight upper bound on the size of an intersecting family in
{[n]\choose k}

with covering number at least
2
: for
n>2k
,

|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-1\choose k-1}-{n-k-1\choose k-1}+1
. Note that the condition

\tau(\mathcal{F})\geq 2
is equivalent to
\mathcal{F}
being non-trivial. Results
of this type are called
stability
results. For a given extremal problem, they
provide a trade-off between the distance from the extremal examples and the
largest possible size of the family.

In 1968, Erdős
[
4
]
proposed a conjecture that generalizes the
Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem. It determines the largest size of a family

\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
with
\nu(\mathcal{F})<s
. There are two natural candidate
extremal families:

\mathcal{A}_{1}:=\{F\in{[n]\choose k}:[s-1]\cap F\neq\emptyset\},\qquad\mathcal{A}_{k}:={[sk-1]\choose k}.

The conjecture of Erdős states the following.

Conjecture 1

(Erdős Matching Conjecture
[
7
]
)
.

If
n\geq sk
and a family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
satisfies

\nu(\mathcal{F})<s
, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\max\{|\mathcal{A}_{1}|,|\mathcal{A}_{k}|\}
.

Erdős proved the conjecture for
n>n_{0}(s,k)
. By now, the conjecture has
been confirmed in several important ranges, both when
n
is very close to

sk
, where the second family is extremal, and when
n>Csk
, where the first
family is extremal. See
[
13
,
19
]
for the most recent results.

We call the range of parameters in which
\mathcal{A}_{1}
is extremal the

trivial regime
. In analogy with the Hilton–Milner theorem, one may
expect that a Hilton–Milner type stability result should hold for the
Erdős Matching Conjecture in the trivial regime. This is indeed the case,
as was shown by Frankl and Kupavskii
[
12
]
. Let us first define the
relevant family:

\mathcal{H}=\{H\in{[n]\choose k}:H\cap[s-2]\neq\emptyset\}\cup\{[s,s+k-1]\}\cup\{H\in{[s-1,n]\choose k}:s-1\in H,\ H\cap[s,s+k-1]\neq\emptyset\}.

The family
\mathcal{H}
is the union of
s-2
stars with centers in
[s-2]
and
the Hilton–Milner family on
[s-1,n]
. Clearly,
\nu(\mathcal{H})<s
and

\tau(\mathcal{H})=s
for
n\geq sk
.

Theorem 1

(Frankl and Kupavskii
[
12
]
)
.

Let
n\geq 2sk(1+o(1))
, where

is taken with respect to

s\to\infty
. Assume that
\mathcal{G}\subset{[n]\choose k}
satisfies

\nu(\mathcal{G})<s
and
\tau(\mathcal{G})\geq s
. Then

|\mathcal{G}|\leq|\mathcal{H}|=|\mathcal{A}_{1}|-{n-k-s+1\choose k-1}+1
.

This paper is devoted to EMC-type results for permutations. We denote by

Deza and Frankl

Proposition 2
.

If
\mathcal{F}\subset\Sigma_{n}

\nu(\mathcal{F})<s

|\mathcal{F}|\leq(s-1)(n-1)!
.

Proof.

Let
\pi
be an

be the cyclic group generated by

\pi

\Sigma_{n}
is partitioned into

left cosets

\sigma G

Hence each coset contains at most

permutations from
\mathcal{F}
, and the
result follows.
∎

and by Cameron and Ku

In this paper, we prove uniqueness and stability results for the analogue of

uniqueness theorem for all permutations.

Theorem 3
.

Let
n,s
be integers with

and

. Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\Sigma_{n}
be a family of

|\mathcal{F}|\leq(s-1)(n-1)!

must be

\bigcup_{i=1}^{s-1}\Sigma_{n}[(x,y_{i})]
or

\bigcup_{i=1}^{s-1}\Sigma_{n}[(x_{i},y)]
, where the
y_{i}
’s, respectively the

x_{i}
’s, are distinct.

In fact, our approach applies to more general classes of permutations. In
particular, we obtain the following analogous result for derangements.

Theorem 4

\bigcup_{i=1}^{s-1}\mathcal{D}_{n}[(x_{i},y)]

Spread approximations

We start with the notion of spreadness, the spread lemma, and some basic
properties of spread families. We say that a family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}

is
r
-spread, for some
r\geq 1
, if

\frac{|\mathcal{F}(X)|}{|\mathcal{F}|}\leq r^{-|X|}

for every
X\subset[n]
. The following statement is a variant due to
Stoeckl
[
26
]
of the breakthrough result of Alweiss, Lovett, Wu and
Zhang
[
1
]
.

Theorem 6

(Spread Lemma,
[
1
,
26
]
)
.

If, for some
n,k,r\geq 1
, a family

\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose\leq k}
is
r
-spread and
W
is a

\beta\delta
-random subset of
[n]
, then

\Pr[\exists F\in\mathcal{F}:F\subset W]\geq 1-\left(\frac{2}{\log_{2}(r\delta)}\right)^{\beta}k.

The next lemma states that any large subfamily of a spread family is also
spread.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an
r
-spread family, and let
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{F}
satisfy

|\mathcal{H}|\geq c|\mathcal{F}|
for some
c\in(0,1)
. Then
\mathcal{H}
is
cr
-spread.

Proof.

Indeed, for any set
X
we have

\frac{|\mathcal{H}(X)|}{|\mathcal{H}|}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{F}(X)|}{c|\mathcal{F}|}\leq\frac{r^{-|X|}}{c}\leq(cr)^{-|X|}.

∎

Lemma 8
.

Fix
r\geq 1
and a family
\mathcal{F}
. Let
X
be an inclusion-maximal set
satisfying

|\mathcal{F}(X)|\geq r^{-|X|}|\mathcal{F}|.

Then
\mathcal{F}(X)
is
r
-spread.

Proof.

Let
Y
be disjoint from
X
. If
Y=\emptyset
, there is nothing to prove.
Otherwise, by the maximality of
X
,

|\mathcal{F}(X\cup Y)|<r^{-|X|-|Y|}|\mathcal{F}|\leq r^{-|Y|}|\mathcal{F}(X)|.

Hence
\mathcal{F}(X)
is
r
-spread.
∎

Lemma 9
.

If
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose\leq k}
and
|\mathcal{F}|>r^{k}
, then
\mathcal{F}(X)
is

r
-spread for some
X
such that
|\mathcal{F}(X)|>1
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{F}
is
r
-spread, then we may take
X=\emptyset
. Otherwise, take
X
as in Lemma
8
. Then
\mathcal{F}(X)
is
r
-spread. Moreover,
|\mathcal{F}(X)|\geq 2
, since otherwise

|\mathcal{F}(X)|=1<r^{-k}|\mathcal{F}|\leq r^{-|X|}|\mathcal{F}|.

∎

Lemma 10

(

Let

.
Assume that, for some

\Pr[W\in\mathcal{H}_{i}]\geq 3sp

Next, we discuss the spreadness of several families of permutations, viewed as
families of sets.

We say that
S\subset[n]^{2}
is a
partial permutation
if
S\subset\sigma

for some permutation
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
. For every partial permutation
S
,
we have
|\Sigma_{n}(S)|=(n-|S|)!.

If
S
is not a partial permutation, then
|\Sigma_{n}(S)|=0
. We say that a
family
\mathcal{F}
is
(r,q)
-spread if, for every set
A
of size at most
q
, the
family
\mathcal{F}(A)
is
r
-spread. We shall need the following three lemmas.

Lemma 11
.

The family
\Sigma_{n}
is
\left(\frac{n}{4},\frac{n}{4}\right)
-spread.

Let
S
be a partial permutation with
|S|\leq n/4
. It is enough to show
that
\Sigma_{n}(S)
is
\frac{n}{4}
-spread. Let
X\supset S
.

If
X
is not a partial permutation, then
\Sigma_{n}(X)=\emptyset
and there
is nothing to prove. Otherwise,

\frac{|\Sigma_{n}(S)|}{|\Sigma_{n}(X)|}=\frac{(n-|S|)!}{(n-|X|)!}.

Put
m=n-|S|
and
t=|X|-|S|
. Then

\frac{(n-|S|)!}{(n-|X|)!}=m(m-1)\cdots(m-t+1)\geq(m!)^{t/m}.

Since
m!\geq(m/e)^{m}
, we get

\frac{|\Sigma_{n}(S)|}{|\Sigma_{n}(X)|}\geq\left(\frac{n-|S|}{e}\right)^{|X|-|S|}>\left(\frac{n}{4}\right)^{|X|-|S|},

because
|S|\leq n/4
.
∎

Next, we shall bound the spreadness of the family of derangements. Let us first discuss some simple properties of that family.

Using inclusion-exclusion principle, it is easy to see that
|\mathcal{D}_{n}|=n!\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{i+1}}{i!}
. It is a well-known fact that
|\mathcal{D}_{n}|=\lceil\frac{n!}{e}\rfloor
, i.e.
|\mathcal{D}_{n}|
is the nearest integer to
\frac{n!}{e}
. Let us show that

d_{n,1}=d_{n-1}+d_{n-2}.

Indeed, we can write
\mathcal{D}{[(x,y)]}=\mathcal{F}_{1}\cup\mathcal{F}_{2}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\{\pi\in\mathcal{D}[(x,y)]\mid\pi(2)=1\}
,
\mathcal{F}_{2}=\{\pi\in\mathcal{D}[(x,y)]\mid\pi(2)\neq 1\}
. It is easy to see that
|\mathcal{F}_{1}|=d_{n-2}
and
|\mathcal{F}_{2}|=d_{n-1}
.

Lemma 12
.

The family
\mathcal{D}_{n}
is
\left(\frac{n}{12},\frac{n}{4}\right)
-spread.

Let
S
be a partial permutation such that
|S|\leq n/4
and

\mathcal{D}_{n}(S)\neq\emptyset
. Then

|\mathcal{D}_{n}(S)|\geq d_{n-|S|}\geq\frac{(n-|S|)!}{e}-1>\frac{(n-|S|)!}{3}=\frac{|\Sigma_{n}(S)|}{3}.

Since
\mathcal{D}_{n}(S)\subset\Sigma_{n}(S)
and
\Sigma_{n}(S)
is
\frac{n}{4}
-spread
by Lemma
11
, Lemma
7
implies that

\mathcal{D}_{n}(S)
is
\frac{n}{12}
-spread.
∎

In order to lower bound the spreadness of the family of double derangements, we
need the following consequence of the Egorychev–Falikman theorem
[
4
,
9
]
.

Lemma 13
.

Let
A
be an
N\times N

(0,1)
-matrix such that every row and every
column contains at least
N-2
ones. Then, for all
N\geq 400
,

\operatorname{perm}(A)>\frac{1}{7.5}N!.

Proof.

Let
H
be the bipartite graph, with both parts of size
N
, whose edges
correspond to the zeros of
A
. Thus every vertex of
H
has degree at most

2
.

We shall add edges to
H
, or equivalently change some additional ones of

A
to zeros, while keeping all degrees in
H
at most
2
. This can only
decrease the permanent. Let
H^{\prime}
be a maximal graph obtained in this way,
and let
A^{\prime}
be the corresponding matrix. It is enough to prove the desired
lower bound for
\operatorname{perm}(A^{\prime})
.

We first record the structure of
H^{\prime}
. Let
L_{<2}
and
R_{<2}
be the sets
of vertices of degree less than
2
in the two parts. By maximality, every

u\in L_{<2}
is adjacent in
H^{\prime}
to every
v\in R_{<2}
; otherwise, we
could add the edge
uv
. Hence

|L_{<2}|,|R_{<2}|\leq 1
. Moreover, the total deficiency from degree
2
is the
same on the two sides. Therefore, either
H^{\prime}
is
2
-regular, or there is
exactly one vertex
u
on the left and exactly one vertex
v
on the right
of degree
1
, and they are adjacent.

In the first case, every row and every column of
A^{\prime}
contains exactly

N-2
ones. By the Egorychev–Falikman theorem
[
4
,
9
]
, applied to

A^{\prime}/(N-2)
, we have

\operatorname{perm}(A^{\prime})\geq(N-2)^{N}\frac{N!}{N^{N}}=\left(1-\frac{2}{N}\right)^{N}N!>\frac{1}{7.5}N!,

where the last inequality holds, for instance, for all
N\geq 200
.

It remains to consider the second case. Let
u
and
v
be the unique
vertices of degree
1
in the two parts. Then
uv
is an edge of
H^{\prime}
, so
the entry
a^{\prime}_{uv}
is zero, while all other entries in row
u
are equal to

1
. Let
A^{\prime}_{uj}
denote the matrix obtained from
A^{\prime}
by deleting row
u

and column
j
.

Since column
v
has no zeros except in row
u
, for every
j\neq v
the
matrix
A^{\prime}_{uj}
is obtained from
A^{\prime}_{uv}
, up to a relabelling of columns,
by replacing one column with an all-one column. Hence

\operatorname{perm}(A^{\prime}_{uj})\geq\operatorname{perm}(A^{\prime}_{uv})\qquad\text{for every }j\neq v.

Expanding the permanent along row
u
, we obtain

\operatorname{perm}(A^{\prime})=\sum_{j\neq v}\operatorname{perm}(A^{\prime}_{uj})\geq(N-1)\operatorname{perm}(A^{\prime}_{uv}).

The matrix
A^{\prime}_{uv}
has size
(N-1)\times(N-1)
, and every row and every
column contains exactly
N-3
ones. Applying the Egorychev–Falikman theorem to
A^{\prime}_{uv}
and combining with the last displayed inequality gives

\operatorname{perm}(A^{\prime})\geq(N-1)(N-3)^{N-1}\frac{(N-1)!}{(N-1)^{N-1}}=\frac{N-1}{N}\left(1-\frac{2}{N-1}\right)^{N-1}N!>\frac{1}{7.5}N!,

where the last inequality holds, for instance, for all
N\geq 400
.

Since
\operatorname{perm}(A)\geq\operatorname{perm}(A^{\prime})
, the lemma
follows.
∎

Lemma 14
.

The family
\mathcal{D}_{n,\overline{\sigma}}

\left(\frac{n}{30},\frac{n}{4}\right)

Proof.

By Lemma

The following theorem is a spread approximation result adapted for families
with no matchings. It says that a family without an
s
-matching can be
approximated by a family of much lower uniformity, which also has no

s
-matching.

Theorem 15
.

Let
n,k,s,q
be positive integers. Let
\mathcal{A}\subset{[n]\choose k}
and
let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{A}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is
r
-spread for

r>\max\{4(s-1)q,\;2^{9}s\log_{2}(2k)\}

and that
\nu(\mathcal{F})<s
. Then there exist a family

\mathcal{S}\subset{[n]\choose\leq q}
with
\nu(\mathcal{S})<s
, and a family

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{F}
, such that the following hold:

(i)

\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{A}[\mathcal{S}]
;

(ii)

for every
B\in\mathcal{S}
, there is a family
\mathcal{F}_{B}\subset\mathcal{F}
such
that
\mathcal{F}_{B}(B)
is
\frac{r}{2}
-spread;

(iii)

|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|\leq 2^{-q-1}|\mathcal{A}|
.

Proof.

We construct
\mathcal{S}
by the following greedy procedure. Start with

\mathcal{F}^{1}:=\mathcal{F}
. At step
i
, if
\mathcal{F}^{i}=\emptyset
, stop. Otherwise, choose an
inclusion-maximal set
S_{i}
such that

|\mathcal{F}^{i}(S_{i})|\geq(r/2)^{-|S_{i}|}|\mathcal{F}^{i}|.

If
|S_{i}|>q
, stop. Otherwise, put

\mathcal{F}^{i+1}:=\mathcal{F}^{i}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{i}[S_{i}].

By Lemma
8
, the family
\mathcal{F}^{i}(S_{i})
is

\frac{r}{2}
-spread. Let
m
be the step at which the procedure stops, and put

\mathcal{S}:=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{m-1}\}.

Clearly,
|S_{i}|\leq q
for each
i\in[m-1]
. For
B=S_{i}
, define

\mathcal{F}_{B}:=\mathcal{F}^{i}[S_{i}]
. Then
\mathcal{F}_{B}(B)=\mathcal{F}^{i}(S_{i})
, and so (ii) holds.

We now define
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. If the procedure stops because
\mathcal{F}^{m}=\emptyset
, put

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}:=\emptyset
. Otherwise, it stops because
|S_{m}|>q
, and we put

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{F}^{m}
. In this case,

|\mathcal{F}^{m}|\leq(r/2)^{|S_{m}|}|\mathcal{F}^{m}(S_{m})|\leq(r/2)^{|S_{m}|}|\mathcal{A}(S_{m})|<(r/2)^{|S_{m}|}r^{-|S_{m}|}|\mathcal{A}|=2^{-|S_{m}|}|\mathcal{A}|.

Since
|S_{m}|>q
, this gives

|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|\leq 2^{-q-1}|\mathcal{A}|.

Moreover, by construction,

\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{A}[\mathcal{S}].

Thus (i) and (iii) hold.

It remains to verify that
\nu(\mathcal{S})<s
. Suppose, to the contrary, that
there are pairwise disjoint sets
T_{1},\ldots,T_{s}\in\mathcal{S}
. Put

Z:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}T_{j},\qquad Z_{i}:=Z\setminus T_{i}.

Note that
|Z_{i}|\leq(s-1)q
. Recall that each family

\mathcal{F}_{T_{i}}(T_{i})
is
\frac{r}{2}
-spread.

Define
\mathcal{G}_{i}:=\{A\in\mathcal{F}_{T_{i}}(T_{i}):A\cap Z_{i}=\emptyset\}
. Then

|\mathcal{G}_{i}|\geq|\mathcal{F}_{T_{i}}(T_{i})|-\sum_{x\in Z_{i}}|\mathcal{F}_{T_{i}}(T_{i}\cup\{x\})|.

Using the
\frac{r}{2}
-spreadness of
\mathcal{F}_{T_{i}}(T_{i})
, we get

|\mathcal{G}_{i}|\geq\left(1-\frac{|Z_{i}|}{r/2}\right)|\mathcal{F}_{T_{i}}(T_{i})|\geq\frac{1}{2}|\mathcal{F}_{T_{i}}(T_{i})|,

because
r/2\geq 2(s-1)q\geq 2|Z_{i}|
. Therefore, by
Lemma
7
, each
\mathcal{G}_{i}
is
\frac{r}{4}
-spread.

We now apply Theorem
6
. Put

\beta:=\log_{2}(2k),\qquad\delta:=\frac{1}{8s\log_{2}(2k)}.

Then
\beta\delta=\frac{1}{8s}
. Moreover,

\frac{r}{4}\delta\geq 2^{4},

by our assumption
r\geq 2^{9}s\log_{2}(2k)
. Hence Theorem
6

implies that a
\frac{1}{8s}
-random subset
W
of
[n]\setminus Z

contains a member of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
with probability at least

1-\left(\frac{2}{\log_{2}2^{4}}\right)^{\log_{2}(2k)}k=1-2^{-\log_{2}(2k)}k=\frac{1}{2}.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{i}
be the upward closure of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
in
2^{[n]\setminus Z}
.
Then the above implies that

\Pr[W\in\mathcal{H}_{i}]\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Since
p:=1/(8s)
satisfies
3sp=3/8<1/2
, we may apply
Lemma
10
. We obtain pairwise disjoint sets

A_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{1},\ldots,A_{s}\in\mathcal{H}_{s}
. Hence there are pairwise disjoint sets

B_{1}\in\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,B_{s}\in\mathcal{G}_{s}
.
But then

T_{1}\cup B_{1},\ldots,T_{s}\cup B_{s}

are pairwise disjoint members of
\mathcal{F}
, contradicting
\nu(\mathcal{F})<s
.
Therefore
\nu(\mathcal{S})<s
, as required.
∎

The next theorem and corollary can be understood as follows: if a family has a
non-trivial approximation
\mathcal{S}
, then
\mathcal{A}[\mathcal{S}]
is significantly smaller
than a trivial union of stars.

Here, we say that
\mathcal{S}
is

if it consists of singletons only. A family
\mathcal{S}
with
\nu(\mathcal{S})<s
is

if for any
A\in\mathcal{S}
and
B\subsetneq A
, the family
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{S}\setminus\{A\}\cup\{B\}
satisfies
\nu(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})\geq s
.

Theorem 16

(
[
14
]
, Theorem 16)
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and let
n,r,q\geq 1
,
s\geq 2
, be integers
such that

\varepsilon r\geq 8e(s-1)q.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset 2^{[n]}
be
(r,1)
-spread, and let

\mathcal{S}\subset{[n]\choose\leq q}
be a maximal non-trivial family with

\nu(\mathcal{S})<s
. Suppose that
\mathcal{S}
contains exactly
l
singleton members

x_{1},\ldots,x_{l}\in[n]
. Then there exists an element

such that

|\mathcal{A}[\mathcal{S}]|\leq\left|\bigcup_{j=1}^{l}\mathcal{A}[x_{j}]\right|+\varepsilon(s-1-l)|\mathcal{A}[x]|.

Corollary 1
.

Let

be such that
|\mathcal{A}[x]|
is maximal. In the notation of
Theorem
16
, if
\mathcal{S}
is non-trivial and

, then

|\mathcal{A}[\mathcal{S}]|\leq(s-2+\varepsilon)|\mathcal{A}[x]|.

Since
\mathcal{S}

Therefore, in Theorem

we have

. Taking

so that

|\mathcal{A}[\mathcal{S}]|\leq l|\mathcal{A}[x]|+\varepsilon(s-1-l)|\mathcal{A}[x]|\leq(s-2+\varepsilon)|\mathcal{A}[x]|.

Proof of Theorem

To prove Theorem

and

, we need the following lemma. We state it in a rather general form, but with some prescribed functions for spreadness and the remainder. We note that those could be adjusted depending on the application. We will apply the lemma for the ambient families being derangements and double derangements.

Lemma 17
.

Let
n,s
be integers satisfying

s\leq\frac{n}{2^{17}\log n}.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\Sigma_{n}
be a family of permutations that is
(n/100,1)
-spread. Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{X}
be a family that satisfies

\nu(\mathcal{F})<s
. Let
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{X}
be the largest subfamily of all permutations containing one of
(s-1)
fixed elements. Then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq|\mathcal{Y}|+n^{-4}|\mathcal{X}|.

Note that this lemma itself gives an approximate variant of Theorems
3
and
4
.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
15
to
\mathcal{F}
with

q=\lfloor 4\log n\rfloor
. It is easy to see that the requirements on
r
from Theorem
15
are satisfied, notably
\frac{n}{100}>2^{9}s\log_{2}(2n)
. This gives a family

\mathcal{S}\subset{[n]^{2}\choose\leq q}
with
\nu(\mathcal{S})<s
and a subfamily

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{F}
such that

\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{X}[\mathcal{S}]\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|\leq n^{-4}|\mathcal{X}|.

It remains to bound
|\mathcal{X}[\mathcal{S}]|
.

Assume first that
\mathcal{S}
is trivial. Then
|\mathcal{X}[\mathcal{S}]|\leq|\mathcal{Y}|
by the definition of
\mathcal{Y}
. Assume
\mathcal{S}
is non-trivial and contains
l\leq s-2
singletons. We then apply
Theorem
16
with
\varepsilon=1/200
(again, the condition on
r
is readily checked), and get

|\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{S}]|\leq|\mathcal{Y}_{l}|+\frac{1}{200}(s-l-1)\max_{v}|\mathcal{X}[v]|,

where
\mathcal{Y}_{l}\subset X
is the largest family of permutations from
X
containing one of some fixed
l
singletons. Let us show that the right-hand side is at most
|\mathcal{Y}|
. First, we note that, by
n/100
-spreadness,
\max_{v}|\mathcal{X}[v]|\leq\frac{100}{n}|\mathcal{X}|,
and thus the second term in the summation is upper bounded by
\frac{1}{2n}(s-l-1)|\mathcal{X}|
. Next, let us lower bound
|\mathcal{Y}_{l+1}|-|\mathcal{Y}_{l}|
. By spreadness, we have

|\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{l}|\geq\Big(1-\frac{s}{n/100}\Big)|\mathcal{X}|\geq 0.99|\mathcal{X}|,

where the second inequality is in our assumption on
s
. Thus, via averaging, we can find a singleton
v
, such that
|(\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{l})(v)|\geq\frac{1}{n}|\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{l}|\geq\frac{0.99}{n}|\mathcal{X}|.
Adding all permutations containing

to
\mathcal{Y}_{l}
, we get that
|\mathcal{Y}_{l+1}|-|\mathcal{Y}_{l}|\geq\frac{0.99}{n}|\mathcal{X}|.
Consequently,
|\mathcal{Y}|-|\mathcal{Y}_{l}|\geq\frac{0.99}{n}(s-l-1)|\mathcal{X}|,
and we conclude that in the case when
\mathcal{S}
is non-trivial we have

|\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{S}]|<|\mathcal{Y}|.

Finally, in both cases

|\mathcal{F}|\leq|\mathcal{Y}|+|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|\leq|\mathcal{Y}|+n^{-4}|\mathcal{X}|.

.

\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{A}
be the subfamily of
\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
consisting of permutations
that do not intersect
\sigma
. Then
\nu(\mathcal{A})<s-1
and

\mathcal{A}\subset\Sigma_{n,\overline{\sigma}}
. Identifying

\Sigma_{n,\overline{\sigma}}
with
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, we apply
Lemma
18
with
t=s-1
to the families

\mathcal{F}_{i}:=\mathcal{A}\cap\Sigma_{n}[(x_{i},y_{i})]
.

If the second alternative of Lemma
18
holds, then

|\mathcal{A}|\leq(s-2.01)d_{n,1}
. Hence

|\mathcal{F}|\leq|\mathcal{A}|+\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^{s-1}\Sigma_{n,\sigma}[(x_{i},y_{i})]\right|+|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|\leq(s-1)(n-1)!-1.01d_{n,1}+\frac{n!}{n^{4}}<(s-1)(n-1)!-d_{n,1},

and the theorem follows. Therefore, we may assume that the first alternative
holds. Relabeling the pairs if necessary, we get

\mathcal{A}\subset\bigcup_{i=2}^{s-1}\Sigma_{n,\overline{\sigma}}[(x_{i},y_{i})]
.
Consequently,

\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subset\Sigma_{n,\sigma}[(x_{1},y_{1})]\cup\bigcup_{i=2}^{s-1}\Sigma_{n}[(x_{i},y_{i})].

We claim that
\sigma
is the only member of
\mathcal{F}
outside

\bigcup_{i=1}^{s-1}\Sigma_{n}[(x_{i},y_{i})]
. Suppose, to the contrary, that there
is another such permutation, say
\pi
. Repeating the previous argument with

\pi
in place of
\sigma
, we find some
j\in[s-1]
such that

\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subset\Sigma_{n,\pi}[(x_{j},y_{j})]\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{s-1}\Sigma_{n}[(x_{i},y_{i})].

Assume first that
j\neq 1
, say
j=s-1
. Combining the two inclusions
above, we get

|\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|\leq(s-1)(n-1)!-2d_{n,1}+(n-2)!.

Since
|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|\leq n!/n^{4}
, this gives

|\mathcal{F}|<(s-1)(n-1)!-d_{n,1}
, and the theorem follows. Thus we may
assume that
j=1
.
In this case,

\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subset\left(\Sigma_{n,\sigma}[(x_{1},y_{1})]\cap\Sigma_{n,\pi}[(x_{1},y_{1})]\right)\cup\bigcup_{i=2}^{s-1}\Sigma_{n}[(x_{i},y_{i})].

Equivalently, inside the first star we lose all permutations that are disjoint
from at least one of
\sigma
and
\pi
.

Since
\sigma\neq\pi
, there is
x^{\prime}\neq x_{1}
such that

\sigma(x^{\prime})\neq\pi(x^{\prime})
. Consider permutations
\rho
satisfying

\rho(x_{1})=y_{1}
,
\rho(x^{\prime})=\sigma(x^{\prime})
, and

\rho(z)\neq\pi(z)
for all
z\neq x_{1},x^{\prime}
. These permutations are disjoint
from
\pi
but intersect
\sigma
, and their number is at least
d_{n-2}
.
Therefore the first star loses at least
d_{n,1}+d_{n-2}
permutations, and so

|\mathcal{F}|\leq(s-1)(n-1)!-d_{n,1}-d_{n-2}+\frac{n!}{n^{4}}<(s-1)(n-1)!-d_{n,1}+1.

This contradiction proves that
\sigma
is indeed the only member outside the
s-1
stars.

Hence

\mathcal{F}\subset\{\sigma\}\cup\Sigma_{n,\sigma}[(x_{1},y_{1})]\cup\bigcup_{i=2}^{s-1}\Sigma_{n}[(x_{i},y_{i})],

where
\sigma(x_{i})\neq y_{i}
for all
i\in[s-1]
. It follows immediately that

|\mathcal{F}|\leq(s-1)(n-1)!-d_{n,1}+1
.

Finally, equality can hold only if the stars are pairwise disjoint:

\Sigma_{n,\sigma}[(x_{i},y_{i})]\cap\Sigma_{n,\sigma}[(x_{j},y_{j})]=\emptyset
for all distinct
i,j>1
, and

\Sigma_{n,\sigma}[(x_{1},y_{1})]\cap\Sigma_{n}[(x_{i},y_{i})]=\emptyset
for all

i>1
. This is possible only if either
x_{1}=\cdots=x_{s-1}
or

y_{1}=\cdots=y_{s-1}
. The theorem follows.
∎
