A deep learning framework for emotion recognition in music using multimodal data fusion
Authors: Runhua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper proposes a novel deep learning framework for music emotion recognition based on multimodal data fusion. To address limitations of existing approaches, such as weak cross genre generalization, insufficient modeling of long range temporal dependencies, and inadequate capture of hierarchical emotional structures, the study introduces two key components: the Harmonic Semantic Encoder (HSE) and the Contrastive Harmonic Alignment (CHA) strategy. The HSE adopts a dual pathway architecture that integrates convolutional neural networks for fine grained acoustic feature extraction with a Transformer based global encoder for modeling long range temporal and harmonic dependencies. In addition, a harmonic aware attention mechanism is designed to emphasize emotionally salient frequency bands, enabling the model to better capture melody lines, chord progressions, and other musically meaningful structures. To further enhance representation quality, the CHA strategy incorporates hierarchical contrastive objectives, including local invariant contrast, structural semantic contrast, and harmonic context alignment. These objectives encourage temporally consistent, semantically discriminative, and harmonically aligned embeddings. The framework also supports multimodal fusion of audio, lyrics, and metadata through a modality aware attention mechanism, with masking and placeholder embeddings to handle missing modalities robustly. Extensive experiments on the PMEmo and GlobalMood datasets demonstrate that the proposed method consistently outperforms strong baselines such as SVM, CNN, CRNN, ResNet-based models, and lightweight architectures. The framework achieves superior accuracy and Macro-F1 scores while maintaining a favorable balance between performance and computational complexity. Ablation studies further confirm the independent contributions of the global Transformer encoder, harmonic aware attention module, and CHA learning objective. The proposed framework provides a robust and scalable solution for multimodal music emotion recognition, advancing hierarchical modeling and harmonic aware representation learning in affective computing.

Introduction

Emotion recognition in music has become an increasingly vital area due to its wide applications in recommendation systems, music therapy, and human-computer interaction 1. Music, as a multimodal signal, conveys emotional content through various dimensions such as audio, lyrics, and metadata. Traditional single-modal analysis often fails to capture the complex emotional cues embedded within these diverse information sources 2. Therefore, fusing multiple modalities can provide a richer and more accurate understanding of emotional content 3. Not only does this approach enhance emotion detection accuracy, but it also enables more personalized and context-aware applications 4. Furthermore, the integration of deep learning techniques with multimodal data allows for automatic feature extraction and hierarchical representation learning, making emotion recognition systems more robust and adaptable to various musical contexts 5.

Early research in music emotion recognition primarily focused on establishing explicit mappings between musical features and emotional states using rule-driven frameworks combined with structured statistical modeling. These systems manually encoded relationships such as the influence of tempo, harmony, key, rhythm, and melodic contour on perceived emotions, often implemented through decision rules, finite state machines, or simple tree-based models 6. The underlying assumption was that certain musical attributes could be directly associated with universal or culturally agreed emotional states. For instance, fast tempos were commonly linked to excitement or happiness, while minor keys and slower rhythms were often correlated with sadness or melancholy. These frameworks provided a transparent reasoning process where the impact of each feature on emotional inference was explicitly traceable 7. This interpretability was valuable not only for system developers but also for researchers aiming to understand the cognitive mechanisms underlying human emotional responses to music. These models allowed for rule refinement based on domain knowledge, offering a certain degree of customization for specific use cases. However, despite their conceptual clarity, these systems were inherently limited in scalability and flexibility 8. Their performance heavily depended on the exhaustive and accurate design of feature sets, which was both labor-intensive and prone to subjective bias. This reliance on manually crafted rules made it difficult for the systems to adapt to the wide variability in musical forms, genres, and cultural expressions. Furthermore, such models often failed to capture subtle, context-dependent emotional cues that arise from complex interactions among multiple musical dimensions 9. They also struggled with dynamic changes over time, such as modulation, tempo shifts, or evolving lyrical narratives, thereby limiting their effectiveness in handling real-world, diverse musical content. Consequently, while foundational, these early approaches paved the way for the development of more flexible, data-driven, and adaptive models in subsequent research 10.

As research progressed, more adaptive computational models were developed to overcome the limitations of rigid rule-based paradigms. These models integrated statistical learning techniques with automated feature extraction pipelines, allowing for a more scalable and data-driven approach to capturing emotional patterns in music. Unlike earlier rule-dependent systems, these methods could infer relationships between musical features and emotional states directly from labeled datasets, reducing the dependency on manually curated rule sets 11. Audio descriptors such as Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), spectral contrast, zero-crossing rate, and rhythmic patterns were commonly extracted to represent acoustic properties, while natural language processing techniques were applied to lyrical content to derive sentiment cues, topic distributions, or semantic embeddings. Metadata like genre labels, artist profiles, and listener engagement statistics were often incorporated to enrich the feature space 12. This multimodal input enabled the models to capture diverse aspects of musical emotion, ranging from timbral textures to narrative semantics. Classification algorithms gained popularity for their capability to manage non-linear decision boundaries and effectively generalize across moderately sized datasets. Despite their advancements, these models exhibited several persistent challenges. The reliance on handcrafted features meant that the feature design process remained sensitive to the choice of preprocessing techniques, feature selection criteria, and domain-specific assumptions 13. This often led to suboptimal performance when encountering music with unconventional structures, genre-bending compositions, or culturally nuanced emotional expressions. Moreover, these systems struggled to model the hierarchical and temporal dependencies present in music, such as recurring motifs, dynamic tempo shifts, or evolving harmonic progressions 14. Their inability to effectively process sequential data limited their capacity to understand how emotions unfold over time within a musical piece. As a result, while offering a significant step forward from rigid rule-based systems, these models highlighted the need for architectures capable of end-to-end learning and richer temporal representation, ultimately motivating the transition toward deep learning-based approaches 15.

To further enhance model robustness and expressiveness, recent advancements have introduced deep representation learning frameworks that seamlessly unify feature extraction and emotion classification into end-to-end architectures. These models leverage convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to capture fine-grained local acoustic patterns from spectrograms and raw waveforms, effectively identifying timbral textures, rhythmic cues, and harmonic structures 16. In parallel, recurrent neural networks (RNNs), gated recurrent units (GRUs), or more recently, transformer-based architectures are employed to model the long-range temporal dependencies and sequential structures inherent in both audio and lyrical content. This combination enables the system to simultaneously process short-term acoustic fluctuations and broader narrative developments within a musical piece. Multimodal fusion strategies are typically implemented at either the feature level, where embeddings from different modalities are concatenated and jointly processed, or at the decision level, where separate models contribute to final predictions through weighted averaging or attention mechanisms 17. These fusion methods allow the model to integrate complementary information from audio, lyrics, and metadata, enhancing its ability to recognize complex emotional states. The emergence of large-scale pre-trained models, like BERT for lyrical analysis and music-specific embedding models trained on vast audio corpora, has further advanced the field by enabling effective transfer learning. This reduces the dependency on large labeled datasets and improves the generalization of models across diverse musical genres and languages 18. Despite these significant advancements, several challenges remain unresolved. One of the primary difficulties lies in dynamically balancing contributions from heterogeneous modalities, particularly when one modality may be noisy or less informative for a specific piece. These models often encounter domain shift issues when applied to music from different cultures, genres, or production styles not well represented in the training data 19. Furthermore, capturing subtle emotional nuances, especially those influenced by personal listening experiences or sociocultural context, remains a complex task. These limitations highlight the ongoing need for more sophisticated alignment strategies, adaptive fusion mechanisms, and culturally aware modeling techniques within the domain of music emotion recognition 20.

Recent literature has highlighted several limitations in existing music emotion recognition (MER) systems that further motivate the need for architectural innovation. For example, convolutional recurrent neural networks (CRNNs) have been commonly adopted for music classification tasks and offer strong performance on acoustic feature extraction. However, they still struggle with capturing global temporal structure and high-level semantic dependencies, particularly across longer musical phrases 21. Moreover, prior reviews and comparative studies have noted that unimodal approaches often underperform in real-world scenarios due to their inability to resolve emotional ambiguity from either audio or lyrics alone 22,23. These findings suggest that while modality-specific models offer technical advantages, they tend to be brittle when faced with expressive diversity and cultural variation in music. Recent multimodal methods attempt to address these shortcomings by combining high-level lyrical features with acoustic embeddings 24, but challenges remain in designing fusion strategies that are both adaptive and interpretable. For example, many existing systems rely on early or late fusion techniques without dynamically weighting modality relevance. Furthermore, benchmark datasets such as MERGE 25 reveal persistent issues in handling non-linear emotion transitions and label sparsity, which call for more semantically grounded and context-aware models.

In this work, we introduce a novel framework that explicitly addresses these issues through two key innovations. First, our HSE leverages a dual-pathway architecture combining convolutional and transformer-based modules to model both short-term acoustic textures and long-range harmonic progressions. Second, our Contrastive Harmonic Alignment (CHA) strategy formulates a multi-level objective that enforces semantic alignment across temporally segmented audio and ensures consistent emotional representation across musical transitions. Unlike conventional fusion techniques, our model dynamically adjusts the importance of each modality using attention-based mechanisms, making it more robust to content-specific variations. Related work

Multimodal emotion recognition models

Multimodal emotion recognition has emerged as a prominent research domain in affective computing, wherein multiple sources of information, such as audio, lyrics, visual cues, and physiological data, are fused to improve the accuracy and robustness of emotion classification systems. Conventional unimodal approaches often fail to capture the complex nature of human emotions, especially in music, where emotions are conveyed not only through acoustic signals but also through the semantic and contextual elements embedded in lyrics and accompanying visuals 26. In recent years, deep learning models have significantly advanced the state of the art in this field by enabling hierarchical feature extraction and complex nonlinear mapping across modalities. More recent works have also integrated attention mechanisms to dynamically weigh the contributions of different modalities, enhancing the interpretability and performance of the models. Moreover, multimodal fusion strategies can be broadly categorized into early fusion, late fusion, and hybrid fusion. Early fusion combines raw features from different modalities before feeding them into the model, allowing for joint representation learning. Late fusion, on the other hand, integrates decisions or high-level features from unimodal classifiers, thus preserving modality-specific characteristics 27. Hybrid fusion attempts to leverage the advantages of both methods, often through sophisticated architectures like Deep Canonical Correlation Analysis (DCCA), Multi-view Autoencoders, or Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). Challenges in this domain include modality alignment, missing data handling, and semantic gap bridging between modalities, which are being actively researched. Datasets like DEAM, EMO-Music, and MuSe have enabled large-scale evaluations of such multimodal systems, often annotated with emotion labels based on dimensional models (valence-arousal) or categorical models (happy, sad, angry, etc.). The shift towards multimodal deep learning frameworks represents a paradigm change in emotion recognition from music, promising systems that are more context-aware, emotionally intelligent, and applicable to real-world multimedia retrieval, recommendation systems, and interactive entertainment platforms 28.

Audio feature learning for emotion

Deep learning has revolutionized the way audio features are learned for emotion recognition tasks, particularly in the musical domain where traditional handcrafted features like MFCCs, chroma vectors, and spectral contrast, although informative, often fail to capture the higher-order dependencies and nuanced expressive characteristics inherent in music. Recent advances leverage the capacity of deep neural networks to automatically learn abstract and discriminative representations from raw audio waveforms or spectrograms, effectively replacing feature engineering with data-driven approaches. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have demonstrated strong performance in extracting spatial and frequency-based features from spectrogram representations of audio, capturing both local and global patterns relevant to emotional content 29. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), and in particular Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, have been employed to model temporal dependencies and dynamic changes in musical compositions that are crucial for tracking the evolution of emotion over time. Attention mechanisms, when incorporated into these architectures, allow the model to focus on emotionally salient parts of a track, thus enhancing recognition accuracy. Moreover, architectures such as CRNNs (Convolutional Recurrent Neural Networks) combine spatial and temporal modeling capabilities, offering a holistic view of audio emotion patterns 30. Pre-trained audio models like OpenL3, YAMNet, and VGGish have been repurposed via transfer learning to initialize deep models with generalized audio embeddings, which can then be fine-tuned for emotion classification. These approaches not only reduce the data requirements but also boost generalizability across datasets. Another emerging trend is the use of self-supervised learning frameworks such as wav2vec and BYOL-A, which learn rich audio representations without the need for manual annotations. These techniques are particularly valuable given the scarcity and subjectivity of emotion labels in music datasets. Challenges remain in dealing with polyphonic music, overlapping instruments, and background noise, which can obscure emotional cues 31. However, ongoing research is focusing on robust feature learning and disentanglement techniques that aim to isolate emotional information from confounding factors. Ultimately, deep learning-based audio feature learning stands as a cornerstone of music emotion recognition systems, underpinning their capacity to understand and respond to the affective dimensions of sound.

Lyrics and semantic emotion cues

Lyrics serve as a powerful semantic channel for conveying emotional meaning in music, often reflecting the songwriter’s intent, mood, and thematic content. In the context of emotion recognition, analyzing lyrics involves natural language processing (NLP) techniques to extract affective features that complement acoustic signals. Deep learning has significantly enhanced this area through the application of pre-trained language models such as BERT, GPT, and RoBERTa, which encode rich contextual information and semantic nuances from text 32. These models, when fine-tuned on emotion classification tasks, can capture subtle affective expressions, figurative language, and emotional subtext that are often present in musical lyrics. Sentiment analysis and emotion lexicons such as NRC and EmoLex have been used in conjunction with these models to provide explicit affective labels, but they are increasingly being supplanted by end-to-end systems capable of learning emotion representations directly from lyrics data. The hierarchical structure of songs, comprising verses, choruses, and bridges, poses unique challenges for NLP models, which need to maintain coherence and context across segments. Attention-based architectures, such as hierarchical attention networks, have been employed to address this, allowing models to selectively attend to emotionally informative parts of the lyrics 33. Multilingual and cross-cultural variations in lyrical content introduce additional complexity, as emotional expression can vary significantly with language and cultural context. Techniques such as cross-lingual transfer learning and multilingual embeddings are being explored to mitigate these challenges. Integration of lyric-based emotion cues with audio features has shown synergistic effects, with multimodal fusion often outperforming unimodal approaches. This is because lyrics can provide clarity and disambiguation in cases where acoustic signals are ambiguous or context-dependent 34. Furthermore, recent studies have investigated the alignment between lyrical themes and musical modes, demonstrating that congruent audio-text emotional patterns are associated with stronger and more consistent emotion perceptions. As datasets with aligned lyrics and audio annotations become more available, the potential of lyric-based emotion recognition continues to expand, offering new avenues for understanding the semantic and emotional landscape of music through the lens of computational models 35.

Recent advances in self-supervised learning and transformer-based architectures have significantly reshaped the landscape of audio and music representation learning, offering promising directions for music emotion recognition. Spijkervet and Burgoyne proposed a contrastive learning framework that leverages augmented views of musical audio to learn semantically rich representations without requiring labeled data 36. Their work demonstrated that unsupervised representations can rival supervised baselines on downstream music classification tasks, highlighting the potential of contrastive paradigms in domains where annotated emotional labels are scarce or ambiguous. Building on similar foundations, Gong et al. introduced the Self-Supervised Audio Spectrogram Transformer (SSAST), which adopts a transformer encoder architecture trained on masked spectrogram patches 37. This model achieved state-of-the-art results across multiple sound classification benchmarks and demonstrated strong generalization from unlabeled audio. Its architectural design supports global attention across time-frequency representations, making it particularly well-suited for tasks like music emotion analysis, where long-range dependencies and harmonic structures play a critical role. More recently, Li et al. proposed MERT, a large-scale acoustic model trained with self-supervised objectives over massive music corpora. Their framework unifies multiple audio understanding tasks, including emotion recognition, and outperforms prior models by exploiting a deeper contextual understanding of audio signals 38. These works collectively emphasize the importance of both data-efficient learning strategies and attention-based architectures, which directly motivate the use of the HSE and CHA strategies proposed in our work. By aligning with these advances, our method leverages the strengths of transformer-based temporal modeling and contrastive representation learning while tailoring them to the hierarchical and multimodal nature of musical emotion. This integration addresses previously underexplored challenges in modality alignment and emotional granularity within music understanding systems.

Visual cues and motion-based emotion recognition

To audio and text modalities, visual and motion cues play a significant role in conveying emotion, particularly in performative domains such as dance. Recent research has explored the use of structured motion descriptors like Laban Movement Analysis to extract expressive movement features for emotion classification 39. Such approaches capture subtle body dynamics, effort, and spatial flow that are often linked to affective states. Deep learning techniques have also been applied to this context, combining interpretable motion representations with neural models to improve recognition performance 40. In the domain of classical Indian dance, Mohanty and Sahay proposed a deep learning framework to recognize emotions by modeling fine-grained pose transitions and gestural semantics, demonstrating the potential of vision-based models to understand culturally grounded emotional expressions 41. These studies collectively highlight the importance of visual nuance and motion semantics in affective computing, which aligns with the goal of our work to capture emotionally salient features beyond conventional audio-text modalities.

Methods

Overview

In this section, we present the methodological framework designed to explore and model music from a computational and machine learning perspective. Our objective is to harness the intrinsic structure and semantic richness of music to build representations that are not only robust but also semantically interpretable. The subsequent parts of this section delineate our formalization of the problem, the model architecture, and the strategic innovations introduced to address domain-specific challenges.

We begin with “Preliminaries”, where we define the foundational problem in formal terms, introducing the key notations, assumptions, and mathematical abstractions that underlie our analysis of music signals. This includes modeling music as temporally ordered, high-dimensional signals and formalizing tasks such as correspondence learning, representation disentanglement, and multi-modal semantic alignment. Particular attention is given to the structural properties of musical signals, such as tonality, rhythm, and harmonic progression, which motivate the algorithm priors encoded in our formulation. Next, in “Harmonic semantic encoder”, we introduce a novel architecture, designed for learning music representations in an unsupervised manner. This model is tailored to exploit the temporal coherence and semantic redundancy inherent in audio signals. Inspired by audio-visual self-supervision paradigms, the model is trained to determine whether a given audio segment aligns semantically with other modalities or context features, without requiring explicit labels. Its design integrates convolutional and recurrent modules with spectral and harmonic priors to effectively capture both local timbre characteristics and long-range structural dependencies. In “Contrastive harmonic alignment”, we elaborate on a domain-specific learning strategy that enables the model to capture fine-grained musical attributes while remaining robust to variations in instrumentation, dynamics, and recording conditions. This strategy leverages the co-occurrence of visual and auditory cues in video datasets, unsupervised clustering of audio motifs, and synthetic augmentation of musical phrases. We detail how this learning approach encourages the emergence of musically meaningful latent structures, leading to representations that correlate with high-level concepts such as genre, mood, and instrument identity. Together, these sections form a coherent methodology that systematically addresses the unique challenges posed by musical data. By grounding our innovations in rigorous problem formulation, novel architectural design, and tailored training strategies, we provide a principled pathway to understanding and generating music through machine learning.

To provide a clearer view of the end-to-end processing pipeline, we illustrate the overall model architecture in Fig. 1. A cross-modal attention fusion mechanism then integrates the available modalities, guided by modality-specific masks and learned placeholder vectors for handling missing inputs. The resulting fused representation is projected through a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) and finally passed to a classifier for emotion recognition tasks. This architecture is designed to learn hierarchical, temporally aligned, and semantically rich representations of music in a self-supervised setting. Harmonic-Aware Attention

To incorporate domain-specific priors, we introduce a harmonic attention module \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {H}: \mathbb {R}^{T \times F} \rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{T \times F}$$\end{document} that reweights frequency bands according to harmonic salience. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k$$\end{document} denote harmonic templates centered at fundamental frequencies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_k$$\end{document}, where each template represents a harmonic series associated with a particular pitch. These harmonic templates are designed to capture the periodic and repetitive nature of harmonic content in music, such as melody lines and chord progressions.

The harmonic attention mechanism adjusts the frequency bands in the spectrogram based on their relevance to the harmonic structure of the music. The harmonic saliency map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {H}(X)$$\end{document} is defined.10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {H}(X)_t = X_t \odot \sum _{k=1}^{K} \alpha _k H_k, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_t$$\end{document} is the spectrogram of the audio signal at time t, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _k$$\end{document} are learned attention weights associated with each harmonic template \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k$$\end{document}. The attention weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _k$$\end{document} are computed by a softmax operation, which normalizes the weights to ensure that they sum to one across all frequency bands, allowing the model to focus more on harmonically significant components and less on irrelevant ones.

To model harmonic progression and further refine the attention mechanism, we introduce a hierarchical weighting scheme for different harmonic levels. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{k,m}$$\end{document} represent the m-th harmonic level of the template associated with the fundamental frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_k$$\end{document}, then the harmonic attention map can be written.11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {H}_m(X)_t = X_t \odot \sum _{k=1}^{K} \alpha _{k,m} H_{k,m}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _{k,m}$$\end{document} are the learned attention weights for the m-th harmonic level. This hierarchical approach allows the model to selectively emphasize different harmonic layers depending on their relevance to the musical content at each time step.

To further refine the harmonic attention mechanism, we introduce a multi-scale temporal attention process. This process uses the harmonic saliency map over different temporal windows, allowing the model to focus on both immediate and extended harmonic structures. The multi-scale attention at each time step t is computed.12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {H}_\text {multi-scale}(X)_t = \sum _{m=1}^{M} \beta _m \cdot \mathscr {H}_m(X)_t, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _m$$\end{document} represents the importance of each temporal scale and M is the number of temporal scales considered. This mechanism enables the model to capture both detailed harmonic nuances and broader harmonic progressions, which are crucial for understanding musical themes and emotional expressions.

The final harmonic attention map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {H}(X)_t$$\end{document} is passed to the encoder, guiding it to focus on harmonic structures that are characteristic of melody, chordal progressions, and other relevant musical features. This attention-based weighting ensures that the model emphasizes musically important components and ignores irrelevant noise, ultimately leading to more accurate and meaningful feature representations.

To further strengthen the model’s ability to learn harmonic relationships, we introduce a harmonic consistency loss that penalizes large deviations in harmonic attention across different time steps. This loss encourages the model to maintain a consistent focus on harmonic structures, enhancing its robustness to temporal variations in musical content. The harmonic consistency loss is defined.13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_\text {harmonic} = \sum _{t=1}^{T} \left\| \mathscr {H}(X)_t - \mathscr {H}(X)_{t-1} \right\| _2^2. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This term ensures that the learned attention maps remain temporally smooth, capturing the evolving harmonic structures throughout the musical piece.

Contrastive Correspondence Learning

The output Z from the global encoder is mean-pooled across the temporal dimension to obtain a fixed-size representation for each sequence. This operation consolidates information from all time steps to create a concise and informative feature vector that encapsulates the overall content of the sequence.14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \bar{z} = \frac{1}{L} \sum _{l=1}^L Z^{(l)} \in \mathbb {R}^{d}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where L is the number of temporal segments, and d is the dimensionality of the latent space. The mean pooling operation ensures that all segments contribute equally to the final representation, while retaining the key features learned by the model.

To perform the contrastive learning task, we compute the similarity score between two inputs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_a$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_b$$\end{document} using their respective latent representations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{z}_a$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{z}_b$$\end{document}. This is done by passing the concatenation of these two embeddings through a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) that computes a scalar similarity score.15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} s(X_a, X_b) = \sigma \left( \text {MLP}([\bar{z}_a \parallel \bar{z}_b]) \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[\cdot \parallel \cdot ]$$\end{document} denotes the concatenation of the two feature vectors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{z}_a$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{z}_b$$\end{document}, and σ is the sigmoid activation function that squashes the output to a value between 0 and 1. The MLP learns to project the concatenated feature vectors into a scalar similarity score that indicates how similar the two inputs are.

The model is trained using a binary cross-entropy loss function, which compares the predicted similarity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s(X_a, X_b)$$\end{document} with the ground truth label \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y \in \{0, 1\}$$\end{document}, where y = 1 denotes that the inputs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_a$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_b$$\end{document} are similar (i.e., belong to the same class or are temporally aligned), and y = 0 denotes that they are dissimilar.16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_\text {corr} = -y \log s(X_a, X_b) - (1 - y) \log (1 - s(X_a, X_b)). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This loss function drives the model to minimize the error in similarity prediction by adjusting the parameters of the encoder network and the MLP. By doing so, the model learns to better align the feature embeddings of similar samples while separating those of dissimilar samples.

To further improve the quality of the learned representations, we also introduce a contrastive term that encourages embeddings of similar samples to be closer in the latent space and those of dissimilar samples to be farther apart. This term is defined.17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_\text {contrast} = \frac{1}{B} \sum _{i=1}^B \left[ \max (0, \delta - \Vert \bar{z}_i - \bar{z}_j \Vert _2) \right] , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where B is the batch size, and δ is a margin hyperparameter that defines the minimum distance required between dissimilar samples. This contrastive loss penalizes pairs that are close in the embedding space but are expected to be dissimilar, enforcing a better separation between different classes.

We introduce a triplet loss-based constraint to further refine the embeddings. A triplet consists of an anchor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_a$$\end{document}, a positive sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_a^+$$\end{document} (which is semantically similar to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_a$$\end{document}), and a negative sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_a^-$$\end{document} (which is dissimilar to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_a$$\end{document}). The triplet loss is computed.18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_\text {triplet} = \max (0, \Vert \bar{z}_a - \bar{z}_a^+ \Vert _2^2 - \Vert \bar{z}_a - \bar{z}_a^- \Vert _2^2 + \alpha ), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where α is a margin that ensures a larger distance between the anchor and the negative sample than between the anchor and the positive sample. This loss further encourages the model to learn discriminative representations that separate positive and negative samples in the embedding space.

To ensure that the learned representations are robust to noise and perturbations, we introduce a regularization term that penalizes large deviations in the embeddings of similar samples across different batches.19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_\text {reg} = \lambda \sum _{i,j} \Vert \bar{z}_i - \bar{z}_j \Vert _2^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where λ is a regularization weight that controls the strength of the penalty. This regularization term encourages the embeddings to be stable and consistent across batches, further enhancing the quality of the learned representations.

Thus, the total objective function used for training the model is a combination of the contrastive loss, triplet loss, and regularization term.20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_\text {total} = \mathscr {L}_\text {corr} + \alpha \mathscr {L}_\text {contrast} + \beta \mathscr {L}_\text {triplet} + \gamma \mathscr {L}_\text {reg}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}To address the challenge of modality heterogeneity and incomplete data across music samples, we adopted several mechanisms during both data preprocessing and model training. First, during batch construction, we ensured that all modalities, audio, lyrics, and metadata, were consistently represented whenever available. We implemented a modality-aware sampler that dynamically adjusts batch composition to maintain a balanced representation of each modality across batches. For instance, when sampling mini-batches, we enforced proportional inclusion of samples containing all three modalities, and used oversampling or upsampling to avoid biasing the model toward the dominant modality (typically audio). In real-world music datasets, it is common to encounter missing modalities, instrumental tracks without lyrics or tracks lacking rich metadata. In such cases, our framework gracefully handles missing data using a modality dropout strategy. When a modality is missing, the corresponding embedding vector is replaced with a learned placeholder token (a trainable “missing-modality” vector), and a binary mask is passed into the fusion module to indicate the presence or absence of each modality. This allows the model to learn how to rely on the most informative available modalities without being disrupted by missing inputs. During training, we also applied random modality masking (akin to data augmentation) to improve robustness under partial modality conditions. This encourages the model to generalize better and prevents overfitting to any single modality. Our multimodal fusion block integrates inputs using cross-modal attention, which is conditioned on these modality masks to selectively attend to available features. These strategies ensure that the model remains functional even when certain modalities are absent, and that training remains balanced without over-reliance on dominant or more complete modalities.

Experiments

Task definition

This section presents the experimental protocol for music emotion classification under a supervised learning framework. The experiments strictly follow the predefined research design, including dataset selection, evaluation metrics, statistical testing procedure, and baseline configuration. All models are trained and evaluated on the PMEmo and GlobalMood datasets, both of which provide curated music audio samples with discrete emotion annotations. The primary evaluation metrics are Accuracy, Macro-F1, Precision, and Recall. Efficiency is assessed using the number of parameters and floating-point operations (FLOPs). Each experiment is repeated five times with different random seeds, and the results are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance is evaluated using a two-sided paired t-test with a threshold of p < 0.05. Baseline comparisons include SVM, CNN, CRNN, ResNet-based model, Lightweight CNN, and MobileNetV2 under identical training conditions.

The task is discrete music emotion classification. The input variable X is a fixed-length audio segment represented as a Log-Mel spectrogram constructed from the raw waveform s(t) using short-time Fourier transform followed by logarithmic scaling. After temporal segmentation, each sample is encoded through a local convolutional encoder and a global Transformer encoder to obtain a unified representation, which is further processed by harmonic-aware multi-scale modeling and multimodal fusion. The output variable Y is a discrete emotion category label predicted through a softmax classifier. The learning paradigm is supervised multi-class classification, where the model parameters are optimized using labeled emotion annotations. The supervision signals are human-annotated emotion labels provided in the PMEmo and GlobalMood datasets, which contain curated and validated discrete emotion categories assigned to each music sample.

Dataset and data preprocessing

Datasets

The experiments are conducted on two publicly available music emotion recognition benchmarks: PMEmo and GlobalMood. PMEmo is an open-access dataset containing 794 music clips with emotion annotations. Each sample is a professionally curated audio excerpt with fixed duration, and each clip is assigned a discrete emotion category label derived from aggregated human ratings. The annotations are collected through controlled listening experiments in which multiple participants independently evaluated the emotional content of each clip according to a predefined labeling protocol. Only samples with consistent majority agreement are retained. Audio clips shorter than the predefined segment length are removed to ensure uniform temporal resolution. The dataset is randomly split into training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 8:1:1 at the sample level, ensuring that no clip appears in more than one subset.

GlobalMood is a large-scale public dataset comprising 1,180 songs collected from 59 countries with cross-cultural emotion annotations. Each track is associated with a discrete emotion label obtained through structured annotation procedures involving multiple raters following standardized labeling guidelines. Songs with incomplete emotion labels or corrupted audio files are excluded during preprocessing. To ensure stable training, extremely short excerpts are discarded and all retained audio is resampled to a unified sampling rate before Log-Mel spectrogram extraction. The dataset is randomly divided into training, validation, and test subsets using a 8:1:1 ratio, with strict sample-level separation across splits.

The experiments are conducted on two publicly available music emotion recognition datasets: PMEmo and GlobalMood.

The PMEmo dataset is introduced by Zhang et al. (2018) and is available at: https://github.com/HuiZhangDB/PMEmo. The dataset is described in the original publication (DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3206025.3206037). It is distributed under the MIT License and we use the updated version (2019) with multimodal extensions including lyrics and metadata. The dataset contains 794 music clips with emotion annotations derived from aggregated human ratings.

The GlobalMood dataset is publicly available at Zenodo: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17706316. It is released under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 (CC BY 4.0) license. We use version v1 (2025 release), which includes 1,180 songs with cross-cultural emotion annotations collected from multiple countries.

Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing follows a unified pipeline for both PMEmo42 and GlobalMood43 datasets. First, data cleaning is conducted to ensure consistency and reliability. Duplicate audio files are identified using filename matching and waveform hash comparison and are removed. Samples with missing emotion labels or corrupted audio signals that fail waveform decoding are excluded. Audio clips shorter than 30 seconds are discarded to guarantee sufficient temporal coverage for segmentation. Abnormal samples with zero-energy segments longer than 5 consecutive seconds are removed to avoid silent bias. All retained audio signals are resampled to 22,050 Hz and converted to mono format for identity alignment across recording conditions. Log-Mel spectrograms are extracted using short-time Fourier transform with a 1024-point FFT, 512-point hop length, and 128 Mel filter banks. The resulting spectrograms are transformed using logarithmic scaling and normalized by global mean and variance computed on the training set. For temporal modeling, each spectrogram is segmented into fixed-length windows of 4 seconds with a stride of 2 seconds, following the temporal segmentation strategy defined in the method table. Each window is treated as one training instance. During training, additive Gaussian noise with zero mean and variance 0.005 is applied to the waveform to improve robustness. No external pretrained feature extractor is used, and all representations are learned directly from the Log-Mel spectrogram through the local convolutional encoder and global Transformer encoder.

Evaluation metrics and baseline

Metrics definition

The primary evaluation metrics follow standard practice in music emotion classification and include Accuracy, Macro-F1, Precision, and Recall. Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly predicted emotion labels over all test samples. Macro-F1 is computed by first calculating the F1-score for each emotion category and then averaging across categories, ensuring balanced evaluation under potential class imbalance. Precision is defined as the ratio of true positive predictions to all predicted positives, and Recall is defined as the ratio of true positives to all actual positives. In addition to these four performance metrics, two efficiency indicators are reported: the total number of trainable parameters (Params) and the number of floating-point operations (FLOPs). These resource metrics quantify model complexity and computational cost, providing a comprehensive assessment of both predictive performance and efficiency.

Evaluation protocol

The evaluation is conducted under an offline supervised multi-class classification setting. For each test sample, the model outputs a probability distribution over discrete emotion categories, and Top-1 accuracy is adopted as the primary ranking criterion. The predicted label corresponds to the category with the highest softmax probability. All metrics are computed on the held-out test set defined by the predefined 8:1:1 random split at the sample level. No sample appears in more than one subset, ensuring strict separation between training, validation, and testing data. The same preprocessing, segmentation, and feature extraction procedures are applied across all models for fairness. Model selection is performed on the validation set, and the final reported results are obtained by evaluating the best checkpoint on the test set without further tuning.

Statistical settings

To ensure robust and reliable evaluation, each model is trained and evaluated independently five times using different random initialization seeds. For every metric, the mean and standard deviation across the five runs are reported in the format of mean ± standard deviation. This repeated trial design reduces the influence of random fluctuations in optimization and data shuffling. Statistical significance between the proposed model and each baseline is assessed using a two-sided paired t-test conducted on the per-run results. A difference is considered statistically significant when the p-value is less than 0.05. All statistical tests are performed on the test set results under identical experimental conditions to guarantee fairness and comparability.

Baseline

Six representative baselines are selected to cover traditional machine learning methods, mainstream deep learning architectures, state-of-the-art convolutional networks, and lightweight models. SVM serves as the traditional machine learning baseline, trained on handcrafted acoustic features derived from Log-Mel representations. CNN represents a standard convolutional neural network for spectrogram-based classification. CRNN integrates convolutional layers with recurrent units to capture temporal dependencies. A ResNet-based model is included as a strong deep residual architecture widely adopted in audio classification tasks. Lightweight CNN is designed with reduced channel width and depth to evaluate compact architectures. MobileNetV2 is adopted as a parameter-efficient model using inverted residual blocks and depthwise separable convolutions. All baselines are trained under the same data preprocessing and evaluation protocol for fair comparison.

Implementation details

All experiments are conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel Xeon Silver 4210 CPU, a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU with 24GB memory, and 128GB RAM, running Ubuntu 20.04 LTS. The framework is implemented in PyTorch 1.13.1 with CUDA 11.7 and cuDNN 8.5. Core libraries include NumPy 1.23, SciPy 1.9, and librosa 0.9.2 for audio processing. Models are trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 32. The optimizer is Adam with an initial learning rate of 1e-4 and weight decay of 1e-5. A cosine annealing learning rate scheduler is applied over the full training schedule. The random seed is fixed to 42 for Python, NumPy, and PyTorch to ensure reproducibility. Early stopping is applied based on validation Macro-F1 with a patience of 15 epochs, and the checkpoint with the best validation performance is selected for final testing. The input to the model is a Log-Mel spectrogram with 128 Mel bins and a temporal resolution determined by a 1024-point FFT and 512-point hop length. Each segmented window has a duration of 4 seconds. The local encoder consists of three Conv1D layers with channel sizes of 64, 128, and 256, each followed by batch normalization and ReLU activation, and includes residual connections. The output embedding dimension of the local encoder is 256. The global encoder is a Transformer with 4 layers, model dimension 256, 8 attention heads, and a feed-forward dimension of 1024. Multi-scale aggregation applies max pooling across three temporal scales. Harmonic-aware attention uses learned harmonic templates with three hierarchical levels. The final fused representation has dimension 256 and is passed to a two-layer classification head with hidden dimension 128 and GELU activation. All baseline methods are trained and evaluated under the same data preprocessing pipeline, dataset splits, hardware environment, and optimization settings. Hyperparameters for each baseline are tuned exclusively on the validation set, and the best validation checkpoint is used for reporting test results to ensure fair and consistent comparison.

Each component is removed individually while keeping all other settings unchanged. Performance is evaluated using Accuracy, Macro-F1, Precision, and Recall on both PMEmo and GlobalMood. All ablation models follow the same training schedule, data preprocessing pipeline, and statistical protocol with five repeated runs and paired t-test evaluation to ensure fairness and reliability.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a novel deep learning framework for music emotion recognition through multimodal data fusion. By combining a HSE and a Contrastive Harmonic Alignment strategy, our model effectively captures both low-level acoustic cues and high-level semantic patterns while maintaining temporal and harmonic consistency. This dual-pathway design allows the system to better model the hierarchical and context-sensitive nature of emotional expression in music. Extensive experiments across multiple music-related datasets demonstrate that our method significantly outperforms a range of strong baselines in terms of accuracy, robustness, and cross-modal generalization.

Beyond raw performance, our approach contributes a flexible framework that can be extended to support additional modalities or domain-specific cues, including visual content in music videos or user-generated feedback. Despite the promising results, there remain several important directions for future research. First, while our current framework is trained on relatively well-annotated and balanced datasets, adapting the model to low-resource scenarios, such as rare emotion categories, underrepresented musical genres, or limited labeled data, would improve its robustness and inclusiveness. Techniques such as domain adaptation, few-shot learning, or semi-supervised multimodal training could be explored in this context. Second, the current model design prioritizes performance over latency. To enable real-time deployment in user-facing applications such as streaming music platforms or emotion-aware recommendation systems, future work could explore lightweight variants of our architecture using model pruning, quantization, or knowledge distillation. Third, emotional perception in music is often shaped by cultural and linguistic context. Extending our model’s evaluation to datasets across different cultures, languages, or regional genres could offer valuable insights into its generalization capabilities and help mitigate potential cultural biases in affective computing systems. These future directions will not only enhance the practical applicability of our approach but also support the broader goal of building culturally aware and resource-adaptive music understanding systems.
