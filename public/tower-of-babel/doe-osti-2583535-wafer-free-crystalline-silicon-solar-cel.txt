Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

This CRADA project, based on the DOE Solar Energy Technologies Office (SETO) Solar Prize
Voucher program, helped Leap Photovoltaics to develop methodologies to immobilize Si
particles by permanently attaching them to an Al-coated substrate and thereby forming carrier-
selective electrical contacts to the Si particles. The bigger goal was to help Leap Photovoltaics
develop these immobilized and contacted particle arrays into relatively efficient, inexpensive,
and industrially relevant solar cells.

 1

By using Si particles instead of wafers in a solar cell absorber layer, one can avoid costs
associated with growing monocrystalline Si ingots, then diamond-sawing them into wafers, then
processing wafers into cells – a mainstream practice in today’s high-efficiency Si cell and
module technology. Monocrystalline or polycrystalline Si particles can be obtained in various
ways: for example, Si kerf from wafer sawing is monocrystalline; recycled Si cell wafers can be
ball-milled into particles; particles can be grown using various gas-phase techniques (mostly
from SiH4). These Si particles can be assembled onto a substrate and serve as an absorber layer
for the solar cell, absorbing photons to generate photocarriers.

The challenge with this technique is to collect photocarriers from individual Si particles, with
separation of photogenerated electrons to the negative cell’s electrode and positive
photogenerated holes to the positive electrode. Therefore, each particle must have two isolated,
carrier-selective contacts: one for electrons and one for holes. Plus, particles need to be
immobilized onto a solid substrate. The goal of this work was focused on the immobilization of
Si particles and creating hole-selective contact to them at the same time, using industrially
relevant Si photovoltaic (PV) cell technology: screen printing of Al back-surface field electrodes.
This is used in the mainstream Propane Education & Research Council (PERC) technology for
hole-collecting contacts at the back of the cell. The work performed at NREL consisted of screen
printing of Al metal paste on substrates, spreading Si particles onto it, and thermally processing
the structures to form hole-collecting contacts. The final structures were investigated by scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) after focused ion beam (FIB) cross-sectioning and polishing. The
work was done jointly by NREL staff and Leap Photovoltaics (Leap PV) employees stationed at
NREL. The samples were then taken to Leap PV for further processing. Training the Leap PV
employee on various NREL techniques (laser cutting, screen printing, thermal processing,
characterization) was part of the scope.

1. Background:

Silicon wafers constitute about ½ of the cost of a finished Si solar cell. Producing Si wafers
starting from raw materials (quartz sand and charcoal) is an energy and CO2 and intensive
process [1] that consists of 1) making metallurgical grade (MG) silicon; 2) converting MG Si to
pure SiH4 or SiHCl3 gases; 3) cracking these gases to make solid, pure poly-Si feedstock; 4)
growing monocrystalline Cz-Si ingots from the feedstock; 5) sawing ingots into wafers. Various
ways have been sought to simplify this process; however, the solutions result in either
significantly lower performance (such as in multi-crystalline or “ribbon” c-Si) or high cost/lower
throughput (e.g., epitaxially grown Si on a porous Si template). In this work, NREL helped the
company Leap PV to develop approaches to eliminate the Si wafer from solar cell production by
replacing it with a densely packed array of individual Si particles, ~ 100-micron size each. In this
approach, each individual particle needs a hole-collecting contact and an electron-collecting
contact, and the particles need to be immobilized onto a substrate in a densely packed array. This
work focused on ways of forming immobilized Si particles with Al-based hole-collecting
contacts using industry-adapted screen print metallization technology.

2. Project Objectives:

The hole collecting contact was based on Al metal, which forms a so-called Al back surface field
(Al-BSF) at elevated temperatures > 575 ˚C, where a Si-Al eutectic alloy can be formed. The Al-
BSF consists of a heavily p-type doped region a few microns deep, which forms after the molten
Si-Al eutectic region re-crystallizes back into Al-doped Si by liquid phase epitaxy. Al
metallization paste is widely used to form local p+ Al-BSF contacts in p-PERC cells. We are
using the same principle here for the Si particles. First, a substrate (Si wafer, glass, etc.) is coated
with Al-BSF paste used in the Si PV industry. The particles are then immobilized onto a
substrate covered with still “wet” Al metallization paste by mechanical contact. These structures
are then fired in an RTP furnace, following temperature-time profiles commonly used for these
Al-BSF metallization pastes (typical peak temperature around 700 – 800 ˚C, time duration ~ 20
sec). The main goal is to demonstrate Al-BSF formation to immobilize Si particles on Al-paste-
coated substrates. To achieve this main objective, the project had 3 tasks: 1) Coating substrates
with Al-BSF-forming paste uniformly (Advanced Coatings Application); 2) Formation of
carrier-selective microcontacts to the particles; 3) Structural, optical, and electrical
characterization.

3. Project Results and Discussion:

First, Al-BSF pastes were selected for the project. Since these pastes are designed to work with
flat Si wafers, we tried several pastes to optimize the coating uniformity, particle immobilization
on wet paste, etc. The following pastes were used: 1) Al-BSF/ZQRE01 to screen print full-area
Al-BSF cells; 2) more advanced, recently sourced Al paste TOYO Al-TLCO-07A33. The Si
particles ~ 100-micron size were provided by the partner. These were produced by ball-milling
Si by an external company and were of irregular shape. In some cases, the particles were KOH-
etched to reduce their size and make them more rounded (Fig. 1).

First, the Al-BSF paste was screen-printed onto a Si wafer. Then, the Si particles were
mechanically distributed onto wet printed paste to ensure good adhesion. Then, the structures
were fitted in our RTP furnace for Al-BSF formation. Fig. 2 shows the result when the firing
temperature was still too low (650°C) to form Al-BSF contact. This illustrates that the Task 1
objectives were achieved: good Al-paste coating conformity ~ 10 microns thick by screen
printing and the Task 2 objective of immobilizing the Si particles onto Al paste. To achieve Task
2 further objectives, the structures were fired in an RTP furnace to temperatures designed to form
Al-BSF: 700°C and 750°C. The cross-sectional SEM results after FIB-cutting and polishing are
shown in Figs. 3 and 4 for 700°C firing: the Al-BSF regions start to form (seen as lighter
interfacial spots in Si particles and the wafer) but are still sparse. Finally, firing at higher
temperatures of 800°C and 850°C leads to the formation of more extended Al-BSF regions that
(especially for 850°C firing) spread out almost along the whole Si particle interface with Al paste
(Figs. 5 and 6).

Thus, the main objectives of Task1 and 2 were achieved. Task 3 objectives of characterization by
SEM were achieved as well. Achieving these objectives required making a total of 341 screen
prints on almost that many p-type multi-crystalline Silicon wafers, firing the RTP-600S furnace a
total of 393 times, and preparing numerous samples with FIB and SEM imaging them.

Finally, the results of detailed electrical characterization were mixed, due to hard-to-realize top
contacts to the Si particles to close the electrical contact for the resistivity measurements. We have
identified and realized several strategies to measure total resistance for our silicon particle samples:

 1. Using copper tape on top of the immobilized particles to measure resistivity. Issues with
 this include poor adhesion of the copper tape to the top of the particles, as well as the
 copper tape possibly being in contact with the aluminum, resulting in a much lower
 measured resistance. The collected data from multiple spots for a single sample show a
 wide window of variability. When we measure a low resistance, it may be due to the
 copper tape in contact with the aluminum on the wafer. In contrast, for high resistance,
 the copper may not be in good contact with the particles. We did not have a good
 measure of how many particles are in contact with the copper tape and what the contact
 area is to calculate contact resistance.
 2. Using liquid In-Ga eutectic on the end of a pogo probe to collect electrical measurements.
 We have run into similar issues with this as the copper tape, since it is hard to avoid
 liquid In-Ga metal contacting the Al paste back contact behind the Si particles. However,
 we were able to get some data that (using a conservative estimate of contact area)
 provided an upper bound on contact resistance. These values were short of targets for
 high-efficiency devices but showed promise.

 Fig. 7. Upper bound on series resistance (which should be minimized) as measured using liquid
 In-Ga eutectic on the end of a pogo probe compared with targets for high-efficiency solar cells.
 Mean value met target for 20% efficiency. Best measurements met target for 23% efficiency.

 7

 3. Co-firing a "sandwich structure": the bottom wafer (Si wafer with Al paste and Si
 particles) with the top wafer (Si wafer with only Al paste). Despite these high
 temperatures, the two wafers did not adhere during the firing process. To solve this, we
 tried rewetting the Al-covered wafer (previously dried at 200), placing the rewetted paste
 on top of the Si particles, and drying the sample together at 200°C to try to improve
 adhesion prior to co-firing. However, after drying, we haven’t been able to get the sample
 into the RTP without the top wafer sliding off before the co-fire. In summary, rewetting
 doesn't seem to improve adhesion or help the co-firing process.
Given the issues above, we decided to shift towards the process of placing an insulator material
down on top of the Si particles, etching back (either wet or dry) to electrically expose only the
tops of immobilized Si particles. Then, either sputtering an opaque metal layer on top to be able
to get a contact resistance measurement or making full solar cells. In the case of a simple metal
layer, the metal was chosen to form a Schottky junction with the silicon particles to distinguish
series and shunt resistance. This approach was pursued separately by Leap PV at their facilities
and provided measurements of total contact resistance in line with targets for high-efficiency
devices.

4. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:

The main significant accomplishment for this project was developing a process to immobilize Si
particles onto screen-printed Al paste and form Al-BSF regions into the particles for hole-
collecting contacts. This was demonstrated on numerous samples and verified by extensive
cross-sectional SEM studies, thus achieving project objectives.

5. Path Forward:

Commercialization plans for a Si particle-based PV lie solely with Leap PV. This project has
developed part of the needed technology, a way to immobilize the particles onto a substrate and
form p-type contacts to them at the same time. The next step is to passivate the rest of the
particle’s surface and create an n-type transparent contact on top. These tasks appear to be more
challenging, yet achievable.
