Learning missing physics from legacy simulators with alternating neural integrators
Authors: Hao Wang, Qinghe Wang, Caiyou Yuan, Kailiang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A recurring challenge in science and engineering is the model–reality gap, where trusted legacy simulators lose fidelity due to unresolved physics or structural incompleteness. This challenge has motivated remedies ranging from imperfect mechanistic models to fully data-driven surrogates. Here, we address this gap with Alternating Neural Integrators (ANI), a non-intrusive reuse-and-correct framework for upgrading executable legacy simulators without requiring access to or modification of their internal implementation. Guided by operator-splitting principles, ANI alternates the evolution of a fixed prior simulator with a learned neural correction that targets structured discrepancy between the prior and the supervisory data. We show that ANI can recover missing coupling in chaotic systems and act as an effective subgrid correction in turbulence, improving dynamical fidelity where prior models drift. In selected mechanistically structured settings, post hoc symbolic distillation yields compact hypotheses and, in controlled benchmarks, supports closed-loop refinement of the prior. By combining data-driven flexibility with reusable scientific simulators in a theoretically grounded framework, this work provides a practical gray-box route for systematically upgrading existing computational infrastructure when a callable prior and supervisory data are available.

Introduction

From forecasting climate change to designing next-generation aircraft and discovering new materials, computational models are indispensable across science and engineering. Yet even trusted simulators can lose predictive fidelity when unresolved physics, reduced-order closures, coarse discretizations, or simplifying assumptions become consequential, creating a systematic model–reality gap between mechanistic predictions and the dynamics ultimately observed.

In many scientific settings, the challenge is therefore not the absence of a model, but the presence of one that is informative, executable, and yet structurally incomplete. When experimental observations or high-fidelity simulations reveal this discrepancy, a practical tension often arises: whether to continue relying on an imperfect mechanistic model or to replace it with a fully data-driven surrogate. The central question, however, is how an existing, trusted simulator can be upgraded non-intrusively, without rewriting it from the ground up. In this work, we use the term missing physics in an operational sense to denote the structured discrepancy between a reusable prior and the reference dynamics, rather than to imply that every learned correction corresponds to a new fundamental law.

Scientific machine learning has developed a broad repertoire of hybrid strategies to address this gap, including discrepancy or gray-box modeling1, semigroup-aware and operator-splitting-inspired learning of evolution operators2,3, closure modeling traditions in large-eddy simulation and turbulence correction4–6, physics-informed learning7,8, operator learning9–11, and pretraining/fine-tuning strategies for surrogate models12. While learning residual or discrepancy structure is not new, a practical limitation remains in many settings: many existing frameworks require access to an explicit, differentiable formulation of the prior. The specific contribution of this work is to retain a callable prior step map inside each corrected update through alternating composition. This distinction is important for legacy simulators, compiled solvers, reduced models, and frozen neural priors, where intrusive reformulation or automatic differentiation may be impractical.

Here we introduce alternating neural integrators (ANI), a non-intrusive prior–corrector framework for upgrading reusable but incomplete simulators by alternating a trusted prior step with a learned neural correction that targets structured discrepancy between the prior and the supervisory data. This operator-splitting construction supports even-order extensions, admits mathematically grounded error analysis through a modified residual flow in the time-discrete setting, and applies across a broad range of executable priors. Across ten benchmark ordinary and partial differential equation systems, together with two scoped real-data case studies (single-cell battery aging and single-basin rainfall–runoff forecasting), ANI improves long-horizon accuracy and dynamical fidelity relative to the prior alone and to matched learned baselines; in representative ODE/PDE settings, it also outperforms matched additive gray-box baselines. In selected mechanistically structured cases, post hoc symbolic distillation yields compact hypotheses and, in controlled benchmarks, supports closed-loop refinement of the prior.

Taken together, this work makes three contributions. First, it formulates a non-intrusive alternating-composition framework that wraps callable prior step maps rather than requiring explicit differentiable vector fields. Second, it provides mathematically grounded analysis under standard regularity conditions for the resulting modified residual flow in the time-discrete setting. Third, it evaluates the framework across benchmark systems, scoped real-data case studies, and selected post hoc write-back examples. Figure 1 provides a schematic overview of the ANI prior–corrector framework.

In practical terms, ANI makes established scientific codebases reusable components within modern machine-learning workflows without requiring refactoring of their internal implementation. Its theoretically grounded design, supported by a modified residual-flow analysis, provides error and stability results under standard regularity assumptions (smooth local flows on compact reachable sets with bounded derivatives up to the order required by the expansion) and helps explain the observed long-horizon robustness. ANI is therefore aimed at the operating regime in which reusable prior and supervisory data coexist, changing what can be learned from that combination.

Results

We evaluate ANI on ten benchmark ordinary and partial differential equation problems together with a single-cell battery-aging study and a single-basin rainfall–runoff study. These studies span chaotic systems, oscillators, excitable media, compressible flow with shock-containing profiles, and turbulence, as well as battery aging13 and observational rainfall–runoff forecasting on CAMELS-US14. The priors cover empirical laws, coarse or low-order solvers, reduced-order models, incomplete physical models, established numerical schemes, coarse-grid approximations, and learned surrogates, such as Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy)15,16, Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KAN)17,18, and frozen neural propagators. Across all tasks, the comparison protocol is matched as closely as the problem class permits. ANI, the frozen prior, and the reported learned baselines are trained and evaluated on identical downstream splits and target trajectories. When a purely learned baseline is shown, it is capacity-matched to the ANI corrector backbone or, in PDE settings, uses the same operator family as the frozen prior where appropriate. The additive gray-box baselines reuse the same prior and residual ingredients and differ only in the coupling rule. For the real-data studies, mechanistic priors are calibrated on the training portion and then frozen before downstream comparison. Exact preprocessing, architecture, and split details are reported in the Supplementary Information.

We highlight four benchmark case studies in the Results and then turn to two real-data case studies that probe applicability in experimental and observational regimes. The benchmark cases probe complementary capabilities: restoring missing coupling in a chaotic system, learning an effective subgrid correction in turbulence, broadening a frozen neural prior under parameter shift, and recovering a known dissipative correction in a controlled damped mechanical example. Extended visualizations, additional gray-box comparisons, cost/accuracy sweeps, pretraining controls, and symbolic-distillation details are reported in the Supplementary Information. Matched additive gray-box comparisons are included for both the Lorenz–Stenflo and FitzHugh–Nagumo benchmarks. A compact summary of representative quantitative outcomes for these case studies is provided in Supplementary Table 7, while the key comparisons supporting the main claims are reported in the corresponding subsections below. Even when the priors are structurally flawed, they retain inductive bias that ANI can exploit by learning only the discrepancy relative to the reference evolution.

Recovering missing coupling in a chaotic system

We study the Lorenz–Stenflo equations 19 as a controlled chaotic benchmark in which the prior model is structurally incomplete. The reference dynamics are four-dimensional, whereas the prior evolves the Lorenz subsystem in (x, y, z) while carrying w as a frozen auxiliary coordinate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\dot{w}=0)$$\end{document}(w°=0), thereby omitting the coupling through w. In this setting, the task is not to make a small parametric adjustment to an otherwise correct model, but to learn an effective correction that compensates for a missing coupling mechanism and restores the correct long-horizon dynamics.

This benchmark is challenging for single-stage learned residual baselines. An additive gray-box RK4 baseline, built from the same prior and a learned residual, provides only limited short-horizon accuracy but does not recover the correct attractor geometry or tangent-space instability statistics. ANI-4, by contrast, improves all three reported chaotic diagnostics relative to the baseline. Supplementary Table 11 reports mean ± SEM over four trajectories on fixed 500-step evaluation windows, and Supplementary Fig. 16 shows the corresponding diagnostic-window robustness. Across longer windows, the baseline remains systematically low in approximate entropy and correlation dimension, whereas ANI-4 tracks the reference more closely. ANI-2 already improves correlation dimension and largest Lyapunov exponent relative to the baseline, while ANI-4 gives the closest overall agreement across all three diagnostics. In particular, the largest Lyapunov-exponent error drops from 20.9% for the baseline to 2.1% for ANI-4.

The baseline’s poor short-term accuracy (Fig. 2a) and the prior’s collapse to a fixed point (Fig. 2c) underscore the magnitude of the challenge. In contrast, the ANI-4 prediction (Fig. 2e) closely matches the reference attractor (Fig. 2b). In this controlled benchmark, ANI yields a corrected hybrid model from a structurally deficient prior and, through post hoc distillation, a compact correction hypothesis for the omitted coupling mechanism. Supplementary Section 9.2 further reinserts this distilled correction into the prior equations and shows that the resulting symbolic refined model reduces the mean 200-step relative L2 error from 4.6 × 10−1 for the frozen prior to 5.7 × 10−3 while retaining a much sparser support than the additive discrepancy alternative. Learning effective subgrid correction in turbulence

We next consider coarse-grid large-eddy simulation (LES)4,6 of Kolmogorov flow with forcing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\bf{f}}}}(x,y)=(0.1\,\sin (8\pi y),0)$$\end{document}f(x,y)=(0.1sin(8πy),0) and viscosity ν = 10−4. The prior is a 128 × 128 LES, which inherently filters out small-scale features relative to the 1024 × 1024 reference. In this setting, the model-reality gap corresponds to unresolved subgrid dynamics. Rather than replacing the turbulence solver, ANI learns an effective, data-driven subgrid correction layered on top of the executable LES before recovering these missing multiscale effects. To probe temporal extrapolation, training samples are randomly drawn from the first half of the time horizon, whereas validation and test rollouts are selected exclusively from the second half to ensure strict temporal separation.

The learned residual functions as an effective subgrid correction that improves long-horizon fidelity relative to the LES prior without disrupting the resolved cascade structure. As shown by the kinetic energy spectrum (Fig. 3b), both the LES prior and the ANI-enhanced models remain broadly consistent with the expected k−5/3 inertial-range and k−3 enstrophy-cascade scalings over the resolved range, with the sharp high-wavenumber drop in the LES curve reflecting the spectral dealiasing cutoff of the coarse solver. The critical distinction is revealed in the long-horizon trajectory error: ANI-2 and ANI-4 achieve a consistent reduction in relative vorticity error compared to the LES prior alone (Fig. 3a). This improved fidelity comes with an expected cost–accuracy trade-off: as reported in Supplementary Section 10.1, a direct FNO baseline remains the fastest inference-time option because it avoids repeated prior calls. Complementary evidence from a related Navier–Stokes crude-prior benchmark further shows that the benefit of prior-corrected learning persists under limited high-fidelity supervision, including against a pretrain–fine-tune FNO baseline (Supplementary Section 10.3).

Figure 3c adds a representative vorticity snapshot at t = 1.0 s: the coarse LES field attenuates some fine-scale structures, whereas ANI restores sharper reference-consistent resolved vortical features. Correcting a neural prior under parameter shift

We next consider a setting in which the prior is itself a frozen pretrained neural surrogate for the FitzHugh–Nagumo system. This prior is accurate only in a fixed parameter setting and degrades under shifts in the system parameters (ε, a, b), providing a useful test of whether ANI can correct not only mechanistic solvers but also callable learned propagators.

Importantly, ANI does not require an explicit continuous-time formula for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{A}}}}(u)$$\end{document}A(u). It requires only an executable prior step map. In this experiment, the role of the prior is played by a frozen one-step neural propagator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\mathcal{P}}}}}_{h}^{\eta }$$\end{document}Phη, and ANI composes this map with a separate learned corrector while keeping the prior parameters η fixed. The learned residual should therefore be interpreted here as an effective correction to the frozen prior map at step size h, rather than as a correction to a closed-form continuous-time vector field. Concretely, the frozen prior is a hybrid FNO–ETDRK4 surrogate inspired by the Exponential Time-Differencing fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (ETDRK4)20. The prior employs 8 Fourier modes in each direction, width 40, and 4 layers, while the ANI corrector is a lightweight parameter-conditioned CNN residual module (Supplementary Section 8.3).

We evaluate predictive performance across all tasks and examine post hoc distillation in selected cases where compact corrections are plausible. Discussion

ANI provides a non-intrusive pathway for upgrading reusable prior models by learning structured discrepancies. Its core contribution lies not in discrepancy learning itself—which is already well established in gray-box scientific machine learning—but in a specific alternating-composition framework that retains callable prior step maps at the target and fractional substep sizes used by the selected composition inside each corrected update. In this design, the prior contributes trusted structure at each step, while the learned component focuses on the discrepancy relative to the reference dynamics and supervisory data. Across the benchmark suite, this coupling improves long-horizon prediction and dynamical diagnostics relative to both the frozen prior and matched learned baselines. In representative ODE/PDE settings, matched additive gray-box controls on Lorenz–Stenflo and FitzHugh–Nagumo further suggest that these gains do not arise from residual learning alone but also reflect the alternating coupling rule. The battery and hydrology studies extend ANI to measured-data regimes and complement the controlled benchmarks.

Two features help explain these gains. First, the prior supplies inductive bias: even when incomplete, a reusable solver often preserves useful geometry, scales, invariants, or numerical structure that helps constrain the learning problem. Second, alternating composition places the learned correction inside the evolution operator itself rather than attaching it as an outer additive patch. This yields a cleaner separation between the trusted prior contribution and the learned discrepancy, and it is precisely this regime that the modified residual-flow analysis addresses. In the time-discrete setting, that analysis provides assumption-qualified error and stability statements under standard regularity assumptions, consistent with the improved long-horizon robustness observed empirically.

ANI is designed for settings in which a usable prior and paired supervisory data are available. Because the learned corrector can depend on discretization, boundary conditions, numerical stabilization, and operating regime, substantial shifts in these factors may require retraining or fine-tuning. The method trades inference speed for stronger prior anchoring and, in the regimes studied here, improved long-horizon fidelity and data efficiency. When inference latency or query throughput is the primary deployment constraint, especially in some PDE applications (with varying initial/boundary conditions or parameters) requiring many surrogate evaluations, ANI-4 may not be the preferred low-latency choice; a direct neural operator surrogate, such as FNO, can be preferable when its accuracy is sufficient for the target application. Consistent with this scope, the measured-data results are shown for a single measured cell and a fixed basin. While algorithmically non-intrusive, ANI also supports practical integration with compiled solvers by minimizing the need for internal code refactoring.

The potential of ANI to support hypothesis generation is illustrated by its ability, in selected mechanistically structured settings, to recover interpretable structures from model error. Across the tasks studied here, ANI learns a discrepancy operator that improves prediction. In selected low-dimensional or mechanistically structured settings, this learned operator can be distilled post hoc into compact symbolic hypotheses and, in controlled benchmarks, support a closed-loop path from prior model to learned correction to mechanistic refinement. In more complex systems, however, the neural residual itself may already constitute a scientifically meaningful outcome, even if it does not collapse to a simple closed form. ANI is therefore best understood as a pragmatic gray-box framework for upgrading reusable legacy simulators when callable prior step maps for the required substep sizes and paired supervisory data are available. Under those conditions, structured discrepancy is not only something to be reduced; it can also become an informative object of analysis in its own right.

This design supports CFL-controlled step sizes (e.g., shock tubes) and improves robustness under multi-resolution rollouts.

Runtime comparisons. Direct neural operators can be faster at inference because they do not call the prior solver; corresponding FNO timing comparisons for PDE rollouts are reported in Supplementary Section 10.1. ANI trades some of this speed for stronger inductive bias and improved long-horizon fidelity when a reusable prior is available. Empirical runtime measurements for representative FitzHugh–Nagumo and Kolmogorov-flow rollouts are summarized in Supplementary Table 16. We report empirical runtime cost as the total wall-clock time required to roll out all test trajectories in a batch, rather than timing very short single-trajectory runs. Wall-clock times are always measured on identical test sets and under identical hardware conditions. All experiments were run on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU.

For the FitzHugh–Nagumo benchmark, the reported timing uses four test trajectories rolled out for 200 steps. The corresponding accuracy curves visualize the first 100 steps, i.e., up to t = 1 s. The FNO baseline directly advances the solution with a learned one-step map and therefore has the lowest inference cost. In contrast, ANI-k decomposes each target step into alternating prior and neural correction stages. All ANI variants use the same target step size, but their runtime increases with the number of prior-plus-neural stages, which explains the higher cost of ANI-4 compared with ANI-2.

For the Kolmogorov-flow benchmark, the reported timing uses twenty test trajectories rolled out for 400 steps with target step size h = 0.005 s, corresponding to T = 2 s. The large-eddy simulation prior cannot advance stably with such a large internal time step and therefore integrates each target step using smaller substeps no larger than 5 × 10−4 s. Each ANI-k rollout is then constructed by alternating this prior evolution with neural correction stages over the target step h. As the number of stages increases, more prior evaluations are required, leading to a higher runtime of ANI-4 compared with the prior and ANI-2. These results quantify the expected speed–accuracy trade-off: direct FNO inference is fastest, whereas higher-order ANI compositions incur additional computational cost in exchange for substantially improved rollout fidelity.

Evaluation metrics and baselines

We quantify trajectory and statistical fidelity via relative Lp errors, Lyapunov exponents, correlation dimension, approximate entropy, and turbulence spectra, with precise definitions collected in Supplementary Section 3. High-fidelity numerical or exact solutions serve as references for the benchmark problems; training data are generated from these references while priors are kept fixed. For the real-data studies, observed trajectories are paired with frozen mechanistic priors. Unless otherwise noted, we compare second- and fourth-order ANI variants against the prior-only solver and capacity-matched learned baselines, including pure learned integrators and, where appropriate, additive gray-box alternatives. Task-specific data generation, architectures, and hyperparameters appear in Supplementary Section 8.

Post hoc symbolic distillation of learned discrepancy

In selected low-dimensional or mechanistically structured settings, the learned ANI correction can be interrogated after training to obtain a simpler hypothesis for the discrepancy. Our procedure is deliberately post hoc: ANI is first trained only for predictive correction, after which we sample state–correction pairs from the learned model, convert the discrete correction into derivative-level targets when needed, and apply sparse regression (SINDy) on a problem-specific library of candidate terms.

Not every discrepancy is expected to admit a compact symbolic form; when such a form is plausible, this workflow provides a route to refinement. In this work, the procedure is demonstrated on the Lorenz–Stenflo, FitzHugh–Nagumo, and battery-aging examples; the resulting expressions are reported in Supplementary Section 9. In more complex settings, the learned neural correction itself remains a valid effective scientific object even without a closed-form distillation.
