Nilpotency and Frattini theory for transposed Poisson algebras
Jiarou Jin, Yanyong Hong (corresponding author)

Abstract
. We develop the theory of nilpotency and the Frattini theory for transposed Poisson algebras. The lower central series is shown to admit a simplified form, and an analogue of Engel’s theorem is established: a finite-dimensional transposed Poisson algebra is nilpotent precisely when the left multiplication operators in both the associative and the Lie structures are nilpotent. Constructions of nilpotent and solvable algebras via tensor products and derivations are given. For a finite-dimensional Lie-nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra, we prove that the derived Lie subalgebra is a nilpotent ideal, which implies that the nilpotent radical coincides with the associative radical. In the framework of Frattini theory, we show that the Frattini subalgebra is always contained in the derived algebra and the Frattini ideal is associative nilpotent. When the algebra is nilpotent, all maximal subalgebras are ideals and the Frattini subalgebra equals the derived algebra. Conversely, for a Lie-nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra, if all maximal subalgebras are ideals, the algebra either is nilpotent or decomposes as a direct sum of a one-dimensional algebra generated by an idempotent and the nilpotent radical; if the Frattini subalgebra equals the derived algebra, the algebra is necessarily nilpotent. We also prove that the zero socle coincides with the nilpotent radical, and when the Frattini ideal is zero, the algebra splits into a subalgebra and its zero socle; in the Lie-nilpotent case this subalgebra is abelian as a Lie algebra.

1.
Introduction

Poisson algebras, which originated in the 1970s from the study of Poisson geometry
[
22
,
34
]
, have become fundamental algebraic structures with deep applications in symplectic geometry, quantization theory, integrable systems, and quantum groups. A Poisson algebra is equipped with two compatible operations: a commutative associative multiplication and a Lie bracket, related by a Leibniz rule. Recently, a dual notion, termed a transposed Poisson algebra, was introduced in
[
2
]
by exchanging the roles of the two operations in this Leibniz rule. Explicitly, a transposed Poisson algebra is a triple
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
where
(P,\cdot)
is a commutative associative algebra,
(P,[\cdot,\cdot])
is a Lie algebra, and the compatibility condition

(1)

\displaystyle 2z\cdot[x,y]=[z\cdot x,y]+[x,z\cdot y]\quad\text{for all}\>x,y,z\in P.

holds. For a transposed Poisson algebra
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
, if
(P,\cdot)
or
(P,[\cdot,\cdot])
is trivial, then we say that
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
is
trivial
. Transposed Novikov-Poisson algebras share many key properties with Poisson algebras, such as closure under tensor products and Koszul self-duality as an operad, and are closely connected to other algebraic structures including Novikov-Poisson algebras,
3
-Lie algebras, Gel¡¯fand-Dorfman algebras and algebras of Jordan brackets
[
2
,
28
,
10
]
. A significant link between
\frac{1}{2}
-derivations of Lie algebras and transposed Poisson algebras, established in
[
15
]
, has led to the classification of transposed Poisson algebra structures on many important Lie algebras, such as simple, Witt, Virasoro, and various other infinite-dimensional Lie algebras
[
35
,
19
,
20
,
36
,
21
]
. Simple transposed Poisson algebras have been studied in
[
10
]
and a bialgebra theory of such algebras have been presented in
[
23
]
.

A key feature of finite-dimensional Poisson algebras is the absence of non-trivial simple structures
[
18
]
, which naturally directs attention to the study of nilpotency and solvability. Moreover, the work
[
16
]
presented Poisson algebras in terms of non-associative algebras via polarization and depolarization, making it natural to involve both the associative and Lie products when defining nilpotency and solvability. Accordingly, the study of nilpotent and solvable Poisson algebras has progressed considerably in recent years. In
[
11
]
, Fernández Ouaridi and Omirov established Engel’s and Lie’s theorems for Poisson algebras, and further investigated the construction of nilpotent and solvable Poisson algebras via tensor products and generalized Jacobians, as well as study Poisson algebras on maximal solvable extensions of nilpotent Lie algebras. In addition, solvability and constructions of generalized Poisson algebras were investigated in
[
9
]
. These developments make it very natural to investigate solvability and nilpotency for transposed Poisson algebras.

The investigation of nilpotency and solvability in algebraic structures is deeply intertwined with Frattini theory. Originating in group theory, the Frattini subgroup captures the notion of non-generators and provides a powerful nilpotency criterion: a group is nilpotent if and only if its Frattini subgroup contains its derived subgroup
[
13
]
. This theory has been successfully extended to numerous algebraic settings, including Lie algebras
[
27
,
3
,
4
]
,
n
-Lie algebras
[
6
]
, associative algebras
[
29
]
, general non-associative algebras
[
33
,
30
]
, Leibniz algebras, bicommutative algebras, assosymmetric algebras, Novikov algebras
[
32
]
, restricted Lie algebras
[
26
]
, restricted Lie Superalgebras
[
7
]
, evolution algebras
[
25
]
, and most recently by Towers extended to Poisson algebras
[
31
]
. Therefore, it is natural to consider to develop a Frattini theory for transposed Poisson algebras.

In this paper, we address these questions and initiate the systematic study of the nilpotency structure and Frattini theory of transposed Poisson algebras. Our main contributions are as follows. We first show that the lower central series of a transposed Poisson algebra admits a particularly convenient form, and we identify several natural ideals arising from the compatibility condition. We establish an analogue of Engel¡¯s theorem, proving that a finite-dimensional transposed Poisson algebra is nilpotent if and only if the left multiplication operators in both the associative and Lie structures are nilpotent. We further provide constructions of nilpotent and solvable transposed Poisson algebras via tensor products and from commutative associative algebras with derivations. A key structural result is that for a finite-dimensional Lie-nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra, its derived Lie subalgebra
[P,P]
is a nilpotent ideal, which forces its nilpotent radical to coincide with the associative nilpotent radical.

Turning to Frattini theory, we prove that the Frattini subalgebra is always contained in the derived algebra, and the Frattini ideal is always associative nilpotent. We also give some bounds for them(see Proposition
4.4
and Corollary
4.17
). For nilpotent algebras, we obtain a precise analogue of the group-theoretic and Lie-algebraic results: all maximal subalgebras are ideals, and the Frattini subalgebra equals the derived algebra. We then explore the converse direction for Lie-nilpotent algebras, showing that if all maximal subalgebras are ideals, the algebra decomposes as a direct sum of a one-dimensional algebra generated by an idempotent and the nilpotent radical; if the Frattini subalgebra equals the derived algebra, the algebra must be nilpotent. Finally, we relate these concepts to the socle, proving that the zero socle coincides with the nilpotent radical, and we analyze the structure of transposed Poisson algebras with a vanishing Frattini ideal, showing they decompose into a subalgebra and its zero socle, with the subalgebra being abelian as a Lie algebra in the Lie-nilpotent case.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we first introduce the definitions and fundamental properties of nilpotent and solvable transposed Poisson algebras, along with several associated ideals and subalgebras. Moreover, we explore several properties of idempotents in transposed Poisson algebras.
Section 3 is devoted to an analogue of Engel’s theorem for transposed Poisson algebras, along with some constructions of nilpotent and solvable algebras. We then prove that in a finite-dimensional Lie-nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra, the derived Lie subalgebra is always a nilpotent ideal, which allows us to describe the nilpotent radical in detail. In Section 4, we develop the Frattini theory for transposed Poisson algebras. We introduce the Frattini subalgebra and Frattini ideal, establish their fundamental properties, and study the interplay among nilpotency, the condition that all maximal subalgebras are ideals, and the equality between the Frattini subalgebra and the derived subalgebra. Finally, we analyze complements of the zero socle when the Frattini ideal vanishes.

Proposition 2.3

The lower central series of transposed Poisson algebras can be given in a more concise form.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let

be a transposed Poisson algebra and

2.2.
Idempotents in transposed Poisson algebras

Idempotents in transposed Poisson algebras have many properties. They are related to multiplicative Hom-Lie algebra structures and play an important role in studying the Frattini theory.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a transposed Poisson algebra with an idempotent
e
of
P_{A}
. Then we have
e\cdot[x,y]=[e\cdot x,e\cdot y]
for all
x,y\in P
.

Proof.

Since
4e\cdot[x,y]=4e^{2}\cdot[x,y]
, by Eq. (
1
), we have

\displaystyle 2[e\cdot x,y]+2[x,e\cdot y]=2e\cdot\big([e\cdot x,y]+[x,e\cdot y]\big)=[e\cdot x,y]+[x,e\cdot y]+2[e\cdot x,e\cdot y].

Hence, we have

\displaystyle 2[e\cdot x,e\cdot y]=[e\cdot x,y]+[x,e\cdot y]=2e\cdot[x,y].

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

Proposition

is not only a

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a transposed Poisson algebra with an idempotent

Corollary 2.15

Let

3.

3.1.
Engel’s theorem and some constructions of nilpotent transposed Poisson algebras

In this subsection, we prove Engel’s theorem for transposed Poisson algebras. The proof is similar to that in
[
11

.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a transposed Poisson algebra. Then
P
is nilpotent if and only if
P_{A}
and
P_{L}
are nilpotent.

Proof.

If
P
is nilpotent, it is obvious that
P_{A}
and
P_{L}
are nilpotent.

Assume that
P_{A}
and
P_{L}
are nilpotent. Then there exist non-negative integers
r
and
s
such that
P_{L}^{s}=P_{A}^{r}=0
. By the definition of the lower central series, it suffices to show that there exists a positive integer
k
such that any product
T_{1}T_{2}\cdots T_{k}=0
, where each
T_{i}
is either
P_{x_{i}}
or
Q_{x_{i}}
for some
x_{i}\in P
.

Observe that Eq. (
1
) is equivalent to the relation

Q_{x}P_{y}=2P_{y}Q_{x}-Q_{x\cdot y}\qquad\text{for all}\>x,y\in P.

Thus, for any
T=T_{1}T_{2}\cdots T_{k}
that contains a factor
Q_{x_{i}}P_{x_{i+1}}
, we may rewrite it as

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{1}T_{2}\cdots T_{i-1}\,Q_{x_{i}}P_{x_{i+1}}\,T_{i+2}\cdots T_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,T_{1}T_{2}\cdots T_{i-1}\,P_{x_{i+1}}Q_{x_{i}}\,T_{i+2}\cdots T_{k}-T_{1}T_{2}\cdots T_{i-1}\,Q_{x_{i}\cdot x_{i+1}}\,T_{i+2}\cdots T_{k}.

This step does not change the number of
Q
in the product. Repeating it, we can give it a regular form

\displaystyle T=a\,P_{j_{1}}\cdots P_{j_{t}}Q_{j_{t+1}}\cdots Q_{j_{k}}+W,

where
a\in{\bf k}
and
W
is a linear combination of products of length strictly less than
k
. Notice that every term in
T
contains
k-t
times of
Q
. Consequently, if
k-t\geq s
, then we have
T=0
directly. If
k-t<s
, we choose
k\geq rs
. In such a long product there must appear a factor of the form
P_{x_{i}}P_{x_{i+1}}\cdots P_{x_{i+r-1}}
. Therefore we have
T=0
as well. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a finite-dimensional transposed Poisson algebra. Then
P
is nilpotent if and only if
P_{x}
and
Q_{x}
are nilpotent for all
x\in P
.

Proof.

The “only if ” part follows directly from Theorem
3.1
. Assume that
P_{x}
and
Q_{x}
are nilpotent for all
x\in P
. By Engel’s theorem for Lie algebras and the well-known analogous result for finite-dimensional commutative associative algebras, we conclude that both
P_{A}
and
P_{L}
are nilpotent. Hence,
P
is nilpotent by Theorem
3.1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Corollary

is an analogue of Engel’s theorem for Lie algebras. Corollary

for a finite-dimensional transposed Poisson algebra can also be proved by induction on

using Proposition

and Lemma

, similar to
[

.

Every finite-dimensional dialgebra possesses a unique maximal solvable ideal

]
. The same conclusion holds for nilpotent ideals.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a transposed Poisson algebra. If
I
and

are nilpotent ideals of

, then
I+J
is also a nilpotent ideal. In particular, there exists a unique maximal nilpotent ideal in a finite-dimensional transposed Poisson algebra.

Proof.

It is straightforward by Theorem

.
∎

Next, we present some constructions of nilpotent and solvable transposed Poisson algebras.

Recall that transposed Poisson algebras are closed under taking tensor products
[
2
]
. Let
(P_{1},\cdot_{1},[\cdot,\cdot]_{1})
and
(P_{2},\cdot_{2},[\cdot,\cdot]_{2})
be two transposed Poisson algebras. Define two binary operations
\cdot
and
[\cdot,\cdot]
on
P_{1}\otimes P_{2}
by

(3)

\displaystyle(x_{1}\otimes x_{2})\cdot(y_{1}\otimes y_{2})=x_{1}\cdot_{1}y_{1}\otimes x_{2}\cdot_{2}y_{2},

(4)

\displaystyle[x_{1}\otimes x_{2},y_{1}\otimes y_{2}]=[x_{1},y_{1}]_{1}\otimes x_{2}\cdot_{2}y_{2}+x_{1}\cdot_{1}y_{1}\otimes[x_{2},y_{2}]_{2},

for all
x_{1},y_{1}\in P_{1}
,
x_{2},y_{2}\in P_{2}
. Then
(P_{1}\otimes P_{2},\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
is a transposed Poisson algebra.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
(P_{1},\cdot_{1},[\cdot,\cdot]_{1})
and
(P_{2},\cdot_{2},[\cdot,\cdot]_{2})
be two transposed Poisson algebras. If
P_{1}
or
P_{2}
is nilpotent (resp. solvable), then
P_{1}\otimes P_{2}
is nilpotent (resp. solvable).

Proof.

For brevity, the subscripts
1
and
2
in the binary operations
\cdot
and
[\cdot,\cdot]
will be suppressed. Without loss of generality, assume that
P_{1}
is nilpotent. We claim that
(P_{1}\otimes P_{2})^{k}\subseteq P_{1}^{k}\otimes P_{2}
for all
k\geq 0
. It is obvious when
k=0
. Suppose by induction that
(P_{1}\otimes P_{2})^{i}\subseteq P_{1}^{i}\otimes P_{2}
holds for all
i\leq k
. Now consider
i=k+1
. Using Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
), we obtain

\displaystyle(P_{1}\otimes P_{2})^{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[(P_{1}\otimes P_{2})^{k},P_{1}\otimes P_{2}]+(P_{1}\otimes P_{2})^{k}\cdot(P_{1}\otimes P_{2})

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle(P_{1}\cdot P_{1}^{k})\otimes P_{2}+[P_{1},P_{1}^{k}]\otimes P_{2}+(P_{1}\cdot P_{1}^{k})\otimes P_{2}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle P_{1}^{k+1}\otimes P_{2}.

Thus,
P_{1}\otimes P_{2}
is nilpotent. The solvable case can be proved similarly.
∎

Recall
[
2
]
that there is a natural construction of transposed Poisson algebras from commutative associative algebras with a derivation. Let
(P,\cdot)
be a commutative associative algebra and
D
be a derivation of
(P,\cdot)
.
Define the binary operation

(5)

\displaystyle[x,y]:=x\cdot D(y)-D(x)\cdot y\qquad\text{for all}~~x,~y\in P.

Then
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
is a transposed Poisson algebra.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
(P,\cdot)
be a finite-dimensional commutative associative algebra and
D
be a derivation of
(P,\cdot)
. Then the transposed Poisson algebra
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
defined by Eq. (
5
) is nilpotent (resp. solvable) if and only if
P_{A}
is nilpotent (resp. solvable).

Proof.

We only need to prove the necessity. First, we claim that
D(P_{A}^{k})\subseteq P_{A}^{k}
for all
k
. When
k=0
, the claim is obvious. Assume inductively that
D(P_{A}^{i})\subseteq P_{A}^{i}
holds for all
0\leq i\leq k
. For
i=k+1
, we have

\displaystyle D(P_{A}^{k+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(P\cdot P_{A}^{k})

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle P\cdot P_{A}^{k}+D(P)\cdot P_{A}^{k}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle P_{A}^{k+1}.

This completes the first claim.

Next, we claim that
P_{L}^{k}\subseteq P_{A}^{k}
for all
k
. It is clear if
k=0
. Suppose that
P_{L}^{i}\subseteq P_{A}^{i}
for all
0\leq i\leq k
. Then for
i=k+1
, using Eq. (
5
), we obtain

\displaystyle P_{L}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[P,P_{L}^{k}]\subseteq[P,P_{A}^{k}]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle P\cdot D(P_{A}^{k})+D(P)\cdot P_{A}^{k}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle P_{A}^{k+1}.

This completes the second claim. Since
P_{A}
is nilpotent, the inclusions
P_{L}^{k}\subseteq P_{A}^{k}
for all
k
imply that
P_{L}
is also nilpotent. Consequently,
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
is nilpotent by Theorem
3.1
.

The solvable case is clear since
P_{A}
is nilpotent if and only if it is solvable.
∎

3.2.
Nilpotent radicals of transposed Poisson algebras

For a finite-dimensional transposed Poisson algebra
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
, we denote by
Nil(P)
the
nilpotent radical
of
P
, that is, the maximal nilpotent ideal of
P
. Similarly, let
Nil_{A}(P)
be the nilpotent radical of
P_{A}
, and
Nil_{L}(P)
be the nilpotent radical of
P_{L}
. In this subsection, we will study their properties and relations.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
A
be a minimal ideal and
N
a nilpotent ideal of a transposed Poisson algebra
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
. If
A\subseteq N
, then
A\subseteq\operatorname{Ann}_{P}(N)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.6
,
[A,N]
is an ideal of
P
. Since
[A,N]\subseteq A
, the minimality of
A
implies that either
[A,N]=A
or
[A,N]=0
. Because
N
is Lie nilpotent, we must have
[A,N]=0
.

Moreover, since
(A\cdot N)\cdot P=A\cdot(N\cdot P)=A\cdot N
and

[A\cdot N,P]\subseteq A\cdot[N,P]+[N,A\cdot P]\subseteq A\cdot N+[N,A]\subseteq A\cdot N,

it follows that
A\cdot N
is also an ideal of
P
contained in
A
. Thus, by the minimality of
A
, either
A\cdot N=0
or
A\cdot N=A
. Since
N
is associative nilpotent, we conclude that
A\cdot N=0
. Hence,
A\subseteq\operatorname{Ann}_{P}(N)
.
∎

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a finite-dimensional Lie nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra. Then
[P,P]
is a nilpotent ideal.

Proof.

We prove it by induction on
\operatorname{dim}(P)
. If
\operatorname{dim}(P)=1
, then
[P,P]=0
and the conclusion holds trivially. Assume that the conclusion holds for all Lie nilpotent transposed Poisson algebras of dimensions at most
k
. Suppose that
\operatorname{dim}(P)=k+1
.

If
[P,P]=0
, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, by Proposition
2.6
, there exists a minimal ideal
B
of
P
contained in
[P,P]
. Consider the quotient
[P,P]/B
. By the induction hypothesis,
[P,P]/B
is nilpotent. Hence there exists a non-negative integer
m
such that
[P,P]_{A}^{m}\subseteq B
. By the proof of Lemma
3.7
, we have
B\subseteq\operatorname{Ann}_{L}(P)
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle[P,P]_{A}^{m+1}=[P,P]\cdot[P,P]_{A}^{m}\subseteq[P,P]\cdot B\subseteq[B\cdot P,P]\subseteq[B,P]=0.

Thus
[P,P]
is associative nilpotent. Hence it is nilpotent by Theorem
3.1
. This completes the induction and the proof.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

For a transposed Poisson algebra
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
, Eq. (
1
) and Proposition
2.10
lead to the inclusion
\operatorname{Ann}_{L}(P)\subseteq\operatorname{Ann}_{A}([P,P])
directly. Thus, if
\operatorname{Ann}_{L}(P)\neq 0
, then
\operatorname{Ann}_{A}([P,P])\neq 0
. In fact, Theorem
3.8
gives a stronger statement: for a finite-dimensional Lie nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
(
\operatorname{Ann}_{L}(P)\neq 0
),

[P,P]
is a nilpotent ideal, which means that
\operatorname{Ann}_{A}([P,P])\neq 0
.

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a finite-dimensional Lie nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra. Then
Nil_{A}(P)
is a nilpotent ideal. Therefore,
Nil(P)=Nil_{A}(P)
.

Proof.

Obviously, we have
Nil(P)\subseteq Nil_{A}(P)
.
By Theorem
3.8
, we obtain

\displaystyle[P,Nil_{A}(P)]\subseteq[P,P]\subseteq Nil(P)\subseteq Nil_{A}(P).

Thus
Nil_{A}(P)
is a nilpotent ideal of
P
. Hence
Nil_{A}(P)\subseteq Nil(P)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Example 3.11
.

By
[

,
(P=\mathbb{C}e_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}e_{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}e_{3},\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])

4.
Frattini theory of transposed Poisson algebras

In this section, we introduce the Frattini theory of transposed Poisson algebras.

4.1.
Frattini subalgebras and Frattini ideals of transposed Poisson algebras

We first recall the definitions of Frattini subalgebras and Frattini ideals of a dialgebra.

Definition 4.1
.

[
31
]

For a dialgebra
P
, the
Frattini subalgebra

F(P)
is defined as the intersection of all maximal subalgebras of
P
, and the
Frattini ideal

\phi(P)
is the largest ideal of
P
contained in
F(P)
.

Remark 4.2
.

For a transposed Poisson algebra
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
, if
P_{A}
is nilpotent, we can construct a one-dimensional subalgebra of
P
by choosing an element
x\in\operatorname{Ann}_{A}(P)
. If
P_{A}
is not nilpotent, then we can construct a one-dimensional subalgebra of
P
by taking an idempotent element in
P_{A}
. Consequently, for any transposed Poisson algebra of dimension greater than one, a non-trivial maximal subalgebra must exist.

We first establish basic properties of Frattini subalgebras and Frattini ideals in dialgebras, which correspond to those in Lie algebras
[
27
,
3
,
4
]
. The proofs follow arguments from group theory.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
A
be a subalgebra of the dialgebra
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
. Then we have the following conclusions.

(a)

F(P),\phi(P)\subseteq P^{1}
.

(b)

J(P)\subseteq P^{1}
, where
J(P)
is the intersection of all maximal ideals of
P
.

(c)

If
A+F(P)=P
, then we have
A=P
.

(d)

If
A
is an ideal of
P
, then
A\not\subseteq F(P)
if and only if there exists a proper subalgebra
B
of
P
such that
P=A+B
.

Proof.

They are straightforward. Here we only show the proof of (
a
). If
F(P)\not\subseteq P^{1}
, then there exists
x\in F(P)
but
x\notin P^{1}
. We can construct a subspace
V
of
P
of codimension 1 that does not contain
x
. But
V
is a maximal subalgebra of
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
since
P^{1}\subseteq V
, which contradicts with
x\in F(P)
.
∎

Proposition
4.3
provides an upper bound for
F(P)
and
\phi(P)
with respect to the inclusion. Next, we give a lower bound for
F(P)
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a transposed Poisson algebra. Then we have
Ann_{P}(P)\cap P^{1}\subseteq F(P)
.

Proof.

When
Ann_{P}(P)\cap P^{1}=0
, the conclusion holds obviously.

Now, we suppose that
Ann_{P}(P)\cap P^{1}\neq 0
. For any non-zero element
x\in Ann_{P}(P)\cap P^{1}
, it can be written in the form
x=\sum_{i\in I}[r_{i},s_{i}]+\sum_{j\in J}r^{\prime}_{j}\cdot s^{\prime}_{j}
or
x=\sum_{i\in I}[r_{i},s_{i}]
or
x=\sum_{j\in J}r^{\prime}_{j}\cdot s^{\prime}_{j}
, where
r_{i},s_{i},r^{\prime}_{j},s^{\prime}_{j}\in P\setminus Ann_{P}(P)
. Thus
r_{i},s_{i},r^{\prime}_{j},s^{\prime}_{j}\notin{\bf k}x
for each
i\in I,j\in J
. Moreover, we can also assume that
\pi(r_{i})=0
,
\pi(s_{i})=0
,
\pi(r^{\prime}_{j})=0
and
\pi(s^{\prime}_{j})=0
, where
\pi:P\rightarrow Ann_{P}(P)
is the linear projection.

Here we only consider the first form. If
x\notin F(P)
, we can choose a maximal subalgebra
M
of
P
which does not contain
x
. Therefore, not all
r_{i},s_{i},r^{\prime}_{j},s^{\prime}_{j}
are in
M
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
r_{k}\notin M
. Now we consider the subalgebra
\langle M,x\rangle
, which is generated by
M
and
x
. Obviously,
P=\langle M,x\rangle=M\oplus{\bf k}x
since
M
is a maximal subalgebra and
x\in Ann_{P}(P)
, which contradicts the choice of
r_{k}
. Hence
x\in F(P)
. The other two forms are similar. Thus
Ann_{P}(P)\cap P^{1}\subseteq F(P)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Example 4.5
.

By
[
5
]
,
(P=\mathbb{C}e_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}e_{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}e_{3},\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
is a transposed Poisson algebra with the non-zero commutative associative product and the non-zero Lie bracket given by

\displaystyle e_{2}\cdot e_{2}=e_{3},\quad[e_{1},e_{2}]=e_{3}.

It is easy to see that

Ann_{P}(P)\cap P^{1}=F(P)=\phi(P)=P^{1}=\mathbb{C}e_{3}
.
Thus, this example demonstrates that the upper bound and the lower bound for
F(P)
may coincide.

It was shown in
[
31
, Corollary 4.2]
that the Frattini ideal of a Poisson algebra is nilpotent. Next, we obtain a similar result for transposed Poisson algebras.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a finite-dimensional transposed Poisson algebra. Suppose that
A
is a subideal of
P
and
B
is an ideal of
A
contained in
F(P)
. If the quotient
A/B
is associative nilpotent, then
A
is also associative nilpotent.

Proof.

Since
A
is a subideal of
P
, there exists a chain of subalgebras of
P
as follows:

\displaystyle 0=A_{0}\subset A_{1}\subset...\subset A_{r}=P,

where
A_{i}
is an ideal of
A_{i+1}
. Because
A/B
is associative nilpotent, there exists a non-negative integer
s
such that for every
a\in A
,

\displaystyle P_{a}^{r+s}(P)\subseteq P_{a}^{s}(A)\subseteq B\subseteq F(P).

Consider the Fitting decomposition
P=P_{a}^{r+s}(P)+E_{P}^{A}(a)
. So we have
E_{P}^{A}(a)=P
by Propositions
2.10
and
4.3
. Hence,
A
is associative nilpotent.
∎

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a finite-dimensional transposed Poisson algebra. Then
\phi(P)
is associative nilpotent.

Proof.

It follows by taking
A=B=\varphi(P)
in Lemma
4.6
directly.
∎

4.2.
Frattini subalgebras of nilpotent transposed Poisson algebras

Note that by
[
12
]
, all maximal subalgebras are ideals in a nilpotent Poisson algebra. We obtain an analogous result for nilpotent transposed Poisson algebras by using the normalizer.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a transposed Poisson algebra and let
A
be a subalgebra of
P
. Then the normalizer
N(A)
is a subalgebra of
P
.

Proof.

For all
x,y\in N(A)
, by Eq. (
1
), we have

\displaystyle[x,y]\cdot A+\big[[x,y],A\big]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle[x\cdot A,y]+[x,y\cdot A]+\big[[x,A],y\big]+\big[[y,A],x\big]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle[A,y]+[x,A]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle A

and

\displaystyle(x\cdot y)\cdot A+[x\cdot y,A]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle x\cdot(y\cdot A)+x\cdot[y,A]+[y,x\cdot A]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle x\cdot A+[y,A]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle A.

Hence,
x\cdot y,[x,y]\in N(A)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra. Then all maximal subalgebras of
P
are ideals and
F(P)=\phi(P)=J(P)
.

Proof.

Since the normalizer of a proper subalgebra in a nilpotent dialgebra properly contains the subalgebra itself
[
12
, Proposition 2.1]
, it follows that for any maximal subalgebra
A
of
P
, we have
N(A)=A
by Lemma
4.8
, that is,
A
is an ideal of
P
. Thus, we obtain that
F(P)=\phi(P)=J(P)
.
∎

Note that Example
4.5
is an example of Theorem
4.9
. But the converse of Theorem
4.9
is not true.

Example 4.10
.

By
[
2
]
,
(P=\mathbb{C}e_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}e_{2},\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
is a transposed Poisson algebra with the non-zero commutative associative product and the non-zero Lie bracket given by

\displaystyle e_{1}\cdot e_{1}=e_{2},\quad[e_{1},e_{2}]=e_{2}.

It is easy to see that the only maximal subalgebra of
P
is
\mathbb{C}e_{2}
, which is clearly an ideal. However,
P
itself is not nilpotent.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a finite-dimensional transposed Poisson algebra. If all maximal subalgebras of
P
are ideals, then
P_{A}
is nilpotent or
P={\bf k}e\oplus(1-e)\cdot P
, where
e
is an idempotent of
P_{A}
and
(1-e)\cdot P=\{x-x\cdot e|x\in P\}
.

Proof.

If
P_{A}
is not nilpotent, then there exists an idempotent
e
in
P_{A}
. We first claim that
(1-e)\cdot P
and
A:=\mathbf{k}e+(1-e)\cdot P\neq 0
are subalgebras of
P
. By Proposition
2.12
, for all
x,y\in P
, we have

\displaystyle[x-e\cdot x,y-e\cdot y]

\displaystyle=[x,y]-[e\cdot x,y]-[x,e\cdot y]+[e\cdot x,e\cdot y]

\displaystyle=[x,y]-2e\cdot[x,y]+e\cdot[x,y]

\displaystyle=[x,y]-e\cdot[x,y]\in(1-e)\cdot P.

Moreover, by Corollary
2.15
, we have
[{\bf k}e,y-e\cdot y]={\bf k}[e,y]-{\bf k}[e,e\cdot y]=0
. Also,
(x-e\cdot x)\cdot(y-e\cdot y)=x\cdot y-e\cdot x\cdot y\in(1-e)\cdot P
and
(x-e\cdot x)\cdot e=0
, for all
x,y\in P
. This proves the claim. Moreover,
\mathbf{k}e+(1-e)\cdot P
holds as a direct sum not only of vector spaces, but also of algebras.

If
A\neq P
, then there exists a maximal subalgebra
M
of
P
containing
A
. By assumption,
M
is an ideal of
P
. Consequently, for any
x\in P
, we have
x=e\cdot x+(x-e\cdot x)\in M
for every
x\in P
, which implies
M=P
. This contradicts with that
M
is a proper subalgebra. Therefore, we must have
A=\mathbf{k}e\oplus(1-e)\cdot P=P
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.12
.

Under the assumption that every maximal subalgebra of
P
is an ideal,
(1-e)\cdot P
must be an ideal of
P
.

Recall that an idempotent
e
is
principal
if there is no idempotent
u
orthogonal to
e
(i.e.,
ue=eu=0
with
u^{2}=u\neq 0
). If
(P,\cdot)
is not a nilpotent algebra, it has a principal idempotent element. In Theorem
4.11
, if we take
e
as a principal idempotent, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 4.13
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a finite-dimensional Lie nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra. Then all maximal subalgebras of
P
are ideals if and only if
P
is nilpotent or
P={\bf k}e\oplus Nil(P)
, where
e
is a principal idempotent.

Proof.

We first prove the necessity. If the associative algebra
P_{A}
is nilpotent, then
P
is nilpotent by Theorem
3.1
.
If
P_{A}
is not nilpotent, we can choose a principal idempotent
e
of
P_{A}
. By Theorem
4.11
, we obtain that
P={\bf k}e\oplus(1-e)\cdot P
, where
(1-e)\cdot P
is an ideal of
P
by Remark
4.12
. We claim that
(1-e)\cdot P
is associative nilpotent. If not, there exists an idempotent
e_{1}\in(1-e)\cdot P
, i.e.,
e_{1}=(1-e)\cdot x
for some
x\in P
. But then
e_{1}\cdot e=(1-e)\cdot x\cdot e=0
,
which contradicts with the fact that
e
is a principal idempotent. Hence,
(1-e)\cdot P
is associative nilpotent. By Theorem
3.1
, we obtain that
(1-e)\cdot P
is nilpotent, so
(1-e)\cdot P=Nil(P)
.

Next, we prove the sufficiency. If
P_{A}
is nilpotent, the conclusion follows by Theorem
4.9
. Now suppose
P={\bf k}e\oplus Nil(P)
. Then every maximal subalgebra of
P
has the form
Nil(P)
or
{\bf k}e\oplus M
, where
M
is a maximal subalgebra of
Nil(P)
. Since
Nil(P)
is nilpotent,
M
is in fact an ideal of
Nil(P)
by Theorem
4.9
. Hence, they are ideals of
P
by the condition
e\cdot Nil(P)=[e,Nil(P)]=0
.
∎

Note that Example
4.10
is an example of Corollary
4.13
where
P_{A}
is nilpotent. Next, we give an example of Corollary
4.13
where
P_{A}
is not nilpotent.

Example 4.14
.

By
[
2
]
,
(P=\mathbb{C}e_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}e_{2},\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
is a transposed Poisson algebra with the trivial Lie bracket and the non-zero commutative associative product given by

\displaystyle e_{1}\cdot e_{1}=e_{1}.

It is easy to see that the maximal subalgebras of
P
are
\mathbb{C}e_{1}
and
\mathbb{C}e_{2}
, which are clearly ideals. Moreover, we have
P=\mathbb{C}e\oplus(1-e)\cdot P
, where the idempotent
e
is
e_{1}
and
(1-e)\cdot P=\mathbb{C}e_{2}=Nil(P)
.

By Proposition
4.3
, we have
F(P)\subseteq P^{1}
. Under the stronger assumption that
P
is nilpotent, the reverse inclusion also holds. The proof is similar to that in
[
12
, Theorem 4.7]
.

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra. Then
F(P)=P^{1}
.

Proof.

For any maximal subalgebra
M
of
P
, since
P
is nilpotent, there exists a non-negative integer
n
such that
P^{n}\not\subseteq M
but
P^{n+1}\subseteq M
. By Proposition
2.5
and the maximality of
M
, we obtain
P=M+P^{n}
. Moreover,
M
is also an ideal of
P
by Theorem
4.9
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle P^{1}=P\cdot P+[P,P]=(M+P^{n})\cdot(M+P^{n})+[M+P^{n},M+P^{n}]\subseteq M+P^{n+1}\subseteq M.

Thus
P^{1}\subseteq F(P)
and it follows that
F(P)=P^{1}
by Proposition
4.3
.
∎

Note that Example
4.5
provides an example of Theorem
4.15
.

Lemma 4.16
.

[
31
, Lemma 3.1]

If
C
is a subalgebra of the dialgebra
A
, and
B
is an ideal of
A
contained in
F(C)
, then
B
is contained in
F(A)
.

Corollary 4.17
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a transposed Poisson algebra. Write
N=Nil(P)
for brevity. Then we have
N^{1}\subseteq N\cap F(P)\subseteq\phi(P)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.5
, Lemma
4.16
(let
B=N^{1},C=N,A=P
) and Theorem
4.15
, we have
F(N)=N^{1}\subseteq F(P)
. Therefore, we obtain
N^{1}\subseteq F(P)\cap N
. We claim that
K\coloneqq F(P)\cap N
is an ideal of
P
. Since
K\cdot P+[K,P]\subseteq N\cdot P+[N,P]\subseteq N
, we only need to prove that
K\cdot P+[K,P]\subseteq F(P)
. If not, there is a maximal subalgebra
M
of
P
such that
K\cdot P+[K,P]\not\subseteq M
, which also implies that
N\not\subseteq M
. So
P=N+M
by the maximality of
M
. Since
K\subseteq N
and
K\subseteq F(P)\subseteq M
, we obtain

\displaystyle K\cdot P+[K,P]=K\cdot(M+N)+[K,M+N]\subseteq N^{1}+M\subseteq K+M\subseteq F(P)+M\subseteq M,

which leads to a contradiction. Hence,
K
is an ideal of
P
and
K\subseteq\phi(P)
.
∎

Note that Example
4.5
provides an example of Corollary
4.17
.

Definition 4.18
.

The
Frattini series
of a transposed Poisson algebra
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
is the sequence of subalgebras
\{F_{i}\}
defined by
F_{0}=P
and
F_{i}
is the Frattini subalgebra of
F_{i-1}
, for
i>1
. The Frattini index of
P
is the smallest integer
f
for which
F_{f}=0
.

For a finite-dimensional transposed Poisson algebra, since each
F_{i+1}
is a proper subalgebra of
F_{i}
, the Frattini index exists and is at most the dimension of
P
. For a nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra, we obtain a stronger bound.

Corollary 4.19
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra. Then
F_{i}\subseteq P^{(i)}
holds for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, the Frattini index
f
of
P
satisfies
f\leq d
, where
d
is the solvable index, that is, the smallest integer
m
such that
P^{(m)}=0
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
i
. For
i=0
, the inclusion is clear. Now assume that
F_{i}\subseteq P^{(i)}
holds for some
i\geq 1
. By Theorem
4.15
, we have

\displaystyle F_{i+1}=F_{i}^{1}=F_{i}\cdot F_{i}+[F_{i},F_{i}]\subseteq\bigl(P^{(i)}\bigr)^{1}=P^{(i+1)}.

This completes the inductive step. We obtain the conclusion directly.

∎

Example
4.10
shows that the converse of Theorem
4.15
is not true. However, we can prove that
F(P)=P^{1}
implies associative nilpotency.

Theorem 4.20
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a finite-dimensional Lie nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra. If
F(P)=P^{1}
, then
P
is nilpotent.

Proof.

Since
F(P)=P^{1}
, every maximal subalgebra
M
of
P
satisfies
P\cdot P+[P,P]\subseteq M
. Therefore, all maximal subalgebras of
P
are ideals. If
P_{A}
is not nilpotent, then we have
P={\bf k}e\oplus Nil(P)
by Theorem
4.11
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle P^{1}=[{\bf k}e+Nil(P),{\bf k}e+Nil(P)]+\big({\bf k}e+Nil(P)\big)\cdot\big({\bf k}e+Nil(P)\big)=Nil(P)^{1}+{\bf k}e

and the maximal subalgebras of
P
are of the form
Nil(P)
or
{\bf k}e\oplus M^{\prime}
, where
M^{\prime}
is a maximal subalgebra of
Nil(P)
. Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle F(P)\subseteq\bigl(F(Nil(P))+\mathbf{k}e\bigr)\cap Nil(P)=F\big(Nil(P)\big).

Since
Nil(P)
is nilpotent, Theorem
4.15
implies
F\big(Nil(P)\big)=Nil(P)^{1}
. Thus we have

\displaystyle F(P)\subseteq Nil(P)^{1}\subsetneqq Nil(P)^{1}+{\bf k}e=P^{1},

which contradicts with
F(P)=P^{1}
. Hence,
P
is nilpotent by Theorem
3.1
.
∎

4.3.
Socles and zero socles of transposed Poisson algebras

The socle and zero socle of a Poisson algebra were introduced in
[
31
]
. In this subsection, we consider the socle and zero socle of a transposed Poisson algebra.

Definition 4.21
.

[
31
]

For a transposed Poisson algebra
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
, the sum of its minimal ideals is called the
socle
, denoted
\operatorname{Soc}(P)
. The sum of its minimal abelian ideals is called the
zero socle
, denoted
\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)
.

With a similar proof in
[
31
, Theorem 4.4]
, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.22
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a finite-dimensional transposed Poisson algebra. Then
\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)\subseteq Nil(P)\subseteq\operatorname{Ann}_{P}\big(\text{Soc}(P)\big)
. If
\phi(P)=0
, then we have
\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)=Nil(P)=\operatorname{Ann}_{P}\big(\text{Soc}(P)\big)
.

Note that Example
4.14
provides an example of Theorem
4.22
with
F(P)=\phi(P)=0
and
\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)=Nil(P)=\operatorname{Ann}_{P}\big(\text{Soc}(P)\big)=\mathbb{C}e_{2}
.

Lemma 4.23
.

[
31
, Lemma 3.6]

Let
B
be an abelian ideal of a dialgebra
A
such that
B\cap\phi(A)=0
. Then there is a subalgebra
C
of
A
such that
A=B\dot{+}C
.

In the next theorem and corollary, we consider the complement of
\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)
in a Lie nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra with
\phi(P)=0
.

Theorem 4.24
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a finite-dimensional Lie nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra with
\phi(P)=0
and
M
be a minimal abelian ideal of
P
. Then there exists a subalgebra
B
such that
P=M\dot{+}B
with
\phi(B)=0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.23
, there exists a subalgebra
B
such that
P=M\dot{+}B
. We only need to prove that
\phi(B)=0
. First consider the subalgebra
K:=M+\phi(B)
of
P
. By Theorem
4.7
,
\phi(B)
is associative nilpotent. Moreover, since
M
is abelian, we have

\displaystyle K_{A}^{k+1}\subseteq M\cdot\big(\phi(B)\big)_{A}^{k}+\big(\phi(B)\big)_{A}^{k+1}.

Therefore,
K
is also associative nilpotent. By Theorem
3.1
, we obtain that
K
is nilpotent.

Next, we claim that
K_{1}:=M\cdot\phi(B)+[M,\phi(B)]\subseteq M
is an abelian ideal. Indeed, we obtain

\displaystyle K_{1}\cdot P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(M\cdot\phi(B)+[M,\phi(B)]\big)\cdot P

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle M\cdot\phi(B)+[M\cdot P,\phi(B)]+[M,\phi(B)\cdot P]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle K_{1}+[M,\phi(B)\cdot(B+M)]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle K_{1},

and

\displaystyle[K_{1},P]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[M\cdot\phi(B),P]+\big[[M,\phi(B)],P\big]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\phi(B)\cdot[M,P]+[M,\phi(B)\cdot P]+\big[[M,P],\phi(B)\big]+\big[M,[\phi(B),P]\big]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle K_{1}+[M,\phi(B)\cdot(B+M)]+\big[M,[\phi(B),B+M]\big]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle K_{1}.

Thus
K_{1}
is an ideal of
P
. Hence, we obtain the claim. By the minimality of
M
, we must have
K_{1}=0
or
K_{1}=M
. If
K_{1}=M
, we obtain
M=K_{1}=M\cdot K+[M,K]\subseteq K^{1}
, since
M
is abelian. Repeating this step, we obtain that
M\subseteq K^{i}
for every positive integer
i
, which contradicts with the nilpotency of
K
. Therefore, we have
K_{1}=0
. Consequently,
\phi(B)
is not only an ideal of
B
but also of
P
.

By Lemma
4.16
(with
A=P
,
B=\phi(B)
,
C=B
), we obtain that
\phi(B)\subseteq\phi(P)=0
. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.25
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a finite-dimensional Lie nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra with
\phi(P)=0
. Then there exists a subalgebra
A
such that
P=\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)\dot{+}A
with
\phi(A)=0
.

Proof.

If
\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)=0
, then we may take
A=P
. Then the conclusion holds trivially.

If
\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)\neq 0
, choose a minimal ideal
A_{1}
of
P
contained in
\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)
. Since
\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)
is abelian,
A_{1}
is a minimal abelian ideal of
P
. By Theorem
4.24
, there exists a subalgebra
B_{1}
of
P
such that
P=A_{1}\dot{+}B_{1}
and
\phi(B_{1})=0=\phi(P/A_{1})
. If
A_{1}=\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)
, the conclusion follows directly. Otherwise, we can choose an ideal
A_{2}
of
P
that is contained in
\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)
and contains
A_{1}
such that
A_{2}/A_{1}
is a minimal ideal of
P/A_{1}
. Then
\phi\big((P/A_{1})/(A_{2}/A_{1})\big)=\phi(P/A_{2})=0
. Repeating this process, we obtain a sequence
A_{1}\subsetneqq A_{2}\subsetneqq\dots\subsetneqq\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)
with
\phi(P/A_{i})=0
at each step, until
A_{i}=\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)
and
\phi(P/A_{i})=0
. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.26
.

Let
(P,\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a finite-dimensional Lie nilpotent transposed Poisson algebra with
\phi(P)=0
. Then there exists a subalgebra
U
such that
P=Nil_{A}(P)\dot{+}U
with
[U,U]=0
.

Proof.

By Theorems
3.8
,
4.22
and Corollaries
3.10
,
4.25
, there exists a subalgebra
U
such that
P=Nil_{A}(P)\dot{+}U
. Moreover, we have
[P,P]\subseteq Nil(P)=Nil_{A}(P)
. Therefore, we obtain
[U,U]\subseteq U\cap Nil_{A}(P)=U\cap\operatorname{Zsoc}(P)=0
. This completes the proof.
∎
