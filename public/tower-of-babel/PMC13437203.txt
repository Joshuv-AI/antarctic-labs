Response to “Precision Medicine in Pediatric Thyroid Care: Moving Beyond Adult Frameworks Towards Age-Stratified Risk Assessment”
Authors: Pyeong Hwa Kim
Published: 2026

I thank Drs. Li and Zhang for their thoughtful commentary [1] on my review [2] and welcome the opportunity to address discussion points regarding age-stratified criteria, active surveillance, and the integration of artificial intelligence with molecular profiling.

First, I fully agree that the biological heterogeneity within the pediatric population represents a critical challenge [2]. As thyroid cancer in young children exhibits fundamentally distinct biological characteristics, with more aggressive tumor behavior and a greater prevalence of RET and NTRK oncogenic fusions than that in older pediatric patients and adults [345], age-stratified Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (TIRADS) criteria may enable more tailored management of pediatric thyroid nodules based on their distinct biological characteristics. However, translating this understanding into validated criteria faces the fundamental constraint of the extreme rarity of thyroid cancer in children under 10 years of age. Indeed, among 152 malignant nodules in our institutional retrospective cohort, only five (3.3%) were identified in children under 10 years of age [67]. Establishing such criteria will require large, multi-institutional cohorts to accumulate sufficient evidence and a broader global consensus on the development of further age-stratified pediatric TIRADS.

Second, the importance of overdiagnosis, as highlighted by Li and Zhang [1], warrants careful consideration in pediatric populations. Given that pediatric thyroid cancers paradoxically exhibit aggressive clinical behavior yet retain low mortality [8], the consequences of overdiagnosis should not be overlooked. Nonetheless, I urge caution against the direct extrapolation of active surveillance, validated exclusively in adults with low-risk papillary thyroid microcarcinoma [9], to the pediatric setting for several reasons. Eligibility criteria for active surveillance are rarely met in the pediatric population. Regional lymph node metastases are present at diagnosis in approximately 60%–80% of pediatric patients, and distant metastases occur in 5%–25% of cases [8]. Moreover, current pediatric-specific guidelines consistently recommend surgery as the primary treatment approach [101112], which fundamentally precludes prospective active surveillance studies and limits the accumulation of evidence. Furthermore, younger age (<30 years) has been independently associated with tumor progression during active surveillance [13], rendering children particularly poor candidates for this strategy. Rather than applying an adult-derived surveillance paradigm to children without prospective pediatric validation, the priority should be to refine risk-stratified surgical decision-making through improved preoperative characterization. Instead, future efforts should be directed toward establishing pediatric-specific eligibility criteria for active surveillance that account for the distinct biological and clinical characteristics of thyroid cancer in children, or toward prospectively evaluating minimally invasive locoregional therapies, such as radiofrequency ablation, which has demonstrated technical feasibility and safety for benign thyroid nodules in children [1415], as potential alternatives in carefully selected pediatric patients.

Third, I share the correspondents’ caution regarding the premature clinical translation of AI-based approaches for pediatric thyroid nodule assessment. The direct application of AI models trained predominantly on adult data to the pediatric population warrants considerable concern given the unique sonographic characteristics of pediatric thyroid nodules. Therefore, additional prospective validation and, where necessary, population-specific fine-tuning of multicenter pediatric datasets are prerequisites before integration into clinical practice. Looking ahead, I agree that AI models capable of predicting molecular profiles represent a particularly promising direction, although achieving this will require systematic accumulation of paired sonographic and molecular data in pediatric cohorts.

In conclusion, I appreciate the correspondents’ insightful engagement with the complexities of pediatric thyroid nodule management. The challenges of age-stratified risk stratification, the limitations of adult-derived surveillance paradigms, and the promise and current immaturity of AI-based tools collectively underscore the need for dedicated, prospectively validated frameworks based on the unique biological, clinical, and US characteristics of pediatric thyroid nodules. I hope that the present exchange will contribute to stimulating large-scale, multicenter collaborative efforts needed to advance this field.
