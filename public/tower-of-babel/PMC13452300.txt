Donor but Not Recipient Diabetes Is Associated With Kidney Graft Failure
Authors: Hatem Ali, Tibor Fülöp, Samar Abdelhafeez, Elham Asgari
Published: 2026

Introduction

Cohen et al.1 first reported that kidney graft survival was poorest when both donor and recipient were diabetic, concluding that diabetes in either party exerted comparable and additive detrimental effects on transplant outcomes. This observation, based on national data from 1994 to 2014, has profoundly influenced clinical decision-making—many centers remain reluctant to use kidneys from diabetic donors for diabetic recipients, fearing compounded risk.

However, those data predate the Kidney Allocation System (KAS) and the contemporary era of intensive glycemic, cardiovascular, and immunosuppressive management.2, 3, 4 Whether donor and recipient diabetes still confer equivalent and additive risk in the current clinical practice remains unclear. We therefore re-examined national United Network for Organ Sharing data from 2015 to 2025 using Fine-Gray competing-risk regression5, 6, 7 to account for death as a competing event, testing whether the “double-diabetic” combination continues to predict inferior graft survival in the post-KAS era. We hypothesized that recipient diabetes may no longer significantly enhance the negative impact of donor diabetes on graft survival in contemporary practices after the implementation of KAS.

Detailed methods are provided in the Supplementary Methods section.

Results

A total of 162,124 adult deceased-donor kidney transplants performed between 2015 and 2025 were included. Of these, 92,876 (57.3%) were nondiabetic donor–nondiabetic recipient pairs, 55,590 (34.3%) nondiabetic donor–diabetic recipient pairs, 7239 (4.5%) diabetic donor–nondiabetic recipient pairs, and 6419 (4.0%) double-diabetic donor–recipient combinations. Baseline characteristics are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2

In multivariable Fine-Gray competing-risk regression (Table 1), donor diabetes was strongly and independently associated with increased risk of graft failure. Other covariates independently associated with graft failure included older donor age (sHR: 1.013 per year, P < 0.001), delayed graft function (sHR: 1.70, P < 0.001), longer cold ischemia time (sHR: 1.006 per hour, P < 0.001), higher calculated panel reactive antibody (sHR: 1.001 per percent, P < 0.001), and donor history of hypertension (sHR: 1.19, P < 0.001). Recipient age was inversely associated with graft failure (sHR: 0.97 per year, P < 0.001). Recipient cytomegalovirus seropositivity was associated with a lower risk of graft failure (sHR: 0.93, P < 0.001), whereas donor cytomegalovirus status was not significantly associated with outcome.

In a prespecified sensitivity analysis excluding deceased donors with diabetes duration less than 5 years, the association between donor diabetes and graft failure became substantially stronger. Compared with the reference group, kidneys from diabetic donors were associated with a markedly higher risk of graft failure both in nondiabetic recipients (sHR: 1.66, 95% CI: 1.50–1.84; P < 0.001) and in diabetic recipients (sHR: 1.73, 95% CI: 1.55–1.93; P < 0.001). Recipient diabetes in the absence of donor diabetes again conferred no independent impact on graft failure (sHR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.96–1.05; P = 0.76).

The kidney donor profile index was intentionally excluded from the primary multivariable model because it incorporates several donor characteristics already included individually in the model, including donor diabetes, donor age, and donor hypertension, raising concerns regarding collinearity and overadjustment. In sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for kidney donor profile index, the association between donor diabetes and graft failure remained significant, although attenuated, both in nondiabetic recipients (sHR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.10–1.31; P < 0.001) and diabetic recipients (sHR: 1.23, 95% CI: 1.12–1.35; P < 0.001).

Sensitivity analyses restricted to 5-year follow-up demonstrated materially unchanged findings, with persistent associations for diabetic donor–nondiabetic recipient pairs (sHR: 1.54, 95% CI: 1.38–1.71; P < 0.001) and diabetic donor–diabetic recipient pairs (sHR: 1.51, 95% CI: 1.35–1.69; P < 0.001), whereas recipient diabetes alone remained nonsignificant (sHR: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.94–1.06; P = 0.88).

Discussion

In this large national cohort after implementation of KAS, we observed that donor diabetes—but not recipient diabetes was independently associated with reduced graft survival. This finding contrasts with earlier pre-KAS data by Cohen et al.,1 which suggested that diabetes in either party exerted comparable and additive adverse effects on transplant outcomes. The attenuation of the recipient effect may partially reflect a major transformation in diabetes care over the past decade. With the widespread introduction of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1receptor agonists, and continuous glucose monitoring, outcomes among transplant recipients with diabetes may have improved substantially in recent years, although medication-level data were not available within the registry.

Several additional factors may contribute to the attenuation of the recipient diabetes impact observed in this analysis. Over the past decade, selection practices for both diabetic donors and diabetic transplant candidates have evolved substantially. Kidneys from diabetic donors accepted for transplantation likely represent a highly selected subgroup, particularly in the post-KAS era, with exclusion of organs exhibiting advanced diabetic nephropathy, poor procurement biopsies, or unfavorable functional parameters. Increasing selectivity in organ acceptance practices, including preferential discard of higher-risk diabetic donor kidneys, may therefore partially attenuate the observed donor diabetes effect.

Similarly, contemporary diabetic transplant recipients undergo more intensive cardiovascular screening and stricter selection processes, potentially resulting in a healthier transplanted diabetic population than in previous years.8 These dynamics introduce the possibility of survivorship and selection bias, whereby both donors and recipients with advanced diabetic complications are systematically excluded before transplantation. Consequently, the present findings should be interpreted as applying primarily to accepted donor-recipient pairs rather than the broader pool of diabetic donors or transplant candidates.

However, the adverse influence of donor diabetes may persist because it reflects irreversible structural injury established before procurement. Even when metabolically well controlled, kidneys from diabetic donors frequently harbor chronic microvascular and tubulointerstitial damage that cannot be reversed after transplantation, rendering the allograft more susceptible to ischemia-reperfusion injury, progressive nephron loss, and chronic dysfunction. In this way, donor diabetes may capture the nonmodifiable legacy of previous glycemic exposure, whereas recipient diabetes has increasingly become a modifiable post-transplant risk in current clinical practice.

Improvements in organ allocation and perioperative management may have further mitigated the apparent impact of recipient diabetes. The introduction of the KAS, broader use of hypothermic machine perfusion, standardized peri-transplant glycemic protocols, and current diabetes therapies may collectively have diminished the incremental risk historically attributed to diabetic recipients. Nonetheless, in the context of organs and recipients deemed suitable for transplantation currently, our findings suggest that donor diabetes remains the dominant diabetes-related determinant of graft survival.

These findings have important clinical implications. The long-standing reluctance to transplant kidneys from diabetic donors into diabetic recipients may no longer be justified in carefully selected recipients. Decisions regarding organ acceptance should focus primarily on objective indicators of intrinsic donor organ quality—including donor age, histopathologic findings when available, and kidney donor profile index—rather than diabetes concordance alone. Incorporating these observations into organ acceptance practices could help reduce the unnecessary discard of diabetic donor kidneys while maintaining acceptable graft outcomes.

Residual confounding inherent to observational registry analyses cannot be fully excluded despite multivariable adjustment. Although our study is limited by the registry’s lack of detailed recipient diabetes duration, longitudinal metabolic control measures, and donor histopathologic data, it highlights a fundamental shift in risk attribution—in current practice, the dominant diabetes-related association with graft failure is attributable to donor rather than recipient diabetes. Recognizing this distinction should encourage more balanced risk-benefit assessments, guiding clinicians toward judicious but broader utilization of kidneys from diabetic donors and optimizing outcomes for patients awaiting transplantation.
