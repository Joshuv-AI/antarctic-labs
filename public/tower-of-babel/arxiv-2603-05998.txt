Abstract
. We study outer length billiards; our main results are as follows. We prove 3- and 4-periodic versions of the Ivrii conjecture. We show that, for every period n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , there exists a functional space of billiard tables that possess invariant curves consisting of n n -periodic points. For n = 4 n=4 , we explicitly parameterize such centrally symmetric billiard tables by functions of one variable and describe how to construct these tables geometrically, similarly to the known construction of Radon curves.



1.
Introduction

This paper continues the study of the outer length billiards, started in
[
1
,
3
,
5
]
. This discrete-time dynamical system is associated with a plane oval (a closed strictly convex smooth curve) and acts on its exterior. The precise definition of this billiard is given below, in Section
2
; here we just say that its
n
-periodic orbit is an
n
-gon, circumscribed about the oval and having an extremal perimeter.

This system is colloquially called “the fourth billiard”, the reason being that there are three other, better known, billiard models associated with plane ovals. These are the inner length, a.k.a. Birkhoff, billiards (the periodic orbits are the inscribed polygons of extremal perimeter), the outer billiards (the periodic orbits are the circumscribed polygons of extremal area), and the symplectic billiards (the periodic orbits are the inscribed polygons of extremal area); see
[
3
,
6
]
for unifying points of view.

In the present paper we consider two billiard-related problems: the Ivrii conjecture, and the existence and description of invariant curves entirely consisting of periodic points. Both problems have been investigated for the above mentioned other three billiard models.

The outer length billiard transformation is an area preserving twist map, the invariant area form depending on the billiard table (see Section
2
). The relevant version of the Ivrii conjecture states that the set of periodic points is a null set (a slightly weaker formulation is that this set has the empty interior).

We show that the second iteration of the outer length billiard map is also a twist map and, using this result, in Theorem
1
we prove the Ivrii conjecture for periods
3
and
4
. In Section
5
we provide two other proofs of the 3-periodic case, using different approaches. See
[
2
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
for similar results concerning the other three billiards.

A billiard is completely integrable if its phase cylinder, or at least a part of it, is foliated by invariant curves. For all four billiard models, this is the case when the billiard table is an ellipse and, conjecturally, this is also the “only if” statement, known as Birkhoff’s conjecture, see
[
10
]
. In particular, an integrable billiard has invariant curves consisting of periodic points with all periods
n\geq 3
. The second problem is to construct billiard tables that possess invariant curves consisting of periodic points with a given period.

In Section
3
, we show that, for every
n\geq 3
, there is a functional space of outer length billiard tables having invariant curves comprising
n
-periodic point. We use the sub-Riemannian geometric approach that was put forward in
[
7
,
15
]
in the case of Birkhoff billiards and applied to outer and symplectic billiards in
[
2
,
12
]
. We also use this approach in one of the proofs of the 3-periodic Ivrii conjecture in Section
5
.

Section
4
concerns centrally symmetric outer length billiard tables with invariant curves consisting of 4-periodic points. We show that these periodic quadrilaterals are parallelograms and, in Theorem
3
, we explicitly parameterize the respective billiard tables by functions of one variable. We also describe a construction of such ovals that resembles the construction of Radon curves (see, e.g.,
[
16
]
). Radon curves solve an analogous problem for outer and symplectic billiards
[
2
,
8
]
.

Invariant area form, twist condition

The outer length billiard map, denoted by
T
, acts in the exterior of the plane oval
{\gamma}

according to the following rule (see Figure
1
).

Figure 1.
Outer length billiard rule.

Given a point
M_{1}
in the exterior of
{\gamma}(x)
, consider the two tangent lines from
M_{1}
to
\gamma
and the unique circle tangent to
\gamma
at point
\gamma(x_{2})
and to the tangent line at
\gamma(x_{1})
. Then the point

M_{2}=T(M_{1})
is defined as the intersection point of the tangent line at
\gamma(x_{2})
and the unique line which is tangent to the circle and
\gamma
. We will denote the lengths of the tangent segments as follows:

l_{1}:=|M_{1}-\gamma(x_{1})|,\quad l_{2}:=|M_{1}-\gamma(x_{2})|.

One can prove
[
3
]
that
T
is a positive twist map, and that
S=l_{1}+l_{2}-\widearc{{\gamma}(x_{1}){\gamma}(x_{2})}
is a generating function for the outer length billiard.

We will use the envelope coordinates of
{\gamma}
to compute
the generating function
S=l_{1}+l_{2}-\widearc{{\gamma}(x_{1}){\gamma}(x_{2})}
.

Figure 2.
Computing
l_{1},l_{2}
in envelope coordinates.

Denote by
p
the support function of
{\gamma}
(see Figure
2
).
In particular:

(1)

\gamma(\alpha)=p(\alpha)\,(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)+p^{\prime}(\alpha)\,(-\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha).\\

We have the following formulae for
l_{1},l_{2}
, and
S
(see Lemma 4.4 of
[
6
]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

One has

\displaystyle l_{1}=-p^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})+\frac{p(\alpha_{2})}{\sin(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}-p(\alpha_{1})\cot(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}),

\displaystyle l_{2}=p^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})+\frac{p(\alpha_{1})}{\sin(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}-p(\alpha_{2})\cot(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}),

\displaystyle S(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=l_{1}+l_{2}-\widearc{{\gamma}(x_{1}){\gamma}(x_{2})}=\bigg(p(\alpha_{2})+p(\alpha_{1})\bigg)\tan\left(\frac{\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}}{2}\right)-\int_{\alpha_{1}}^{\alpha_{2}}pd\alpha.

Proof.

Since
p^{\prime\prime}+p
is the curvature radius of
{\gamma}
,
the arc length is given by

\widearc{{\gamma}(x_{1}){\gamma}(x_{2})}=\int_{\alpha_{1}}^{\alpha_{2}}(p^{\prime\prime}+p)d\alpha=p^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})-p^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})+\int_{\alpha_{1}}^{\alpha_{2}}pd\alpha.

Using this fact and the first two formulas of the lemma, we obtain the expression for
S
.

Now we compute
l_{1},l_{2}
.
The coordinates of the points
\gamma(\alpha_{1})
and
\gamma(\alpha_{2})
can be found using formula (
1
), yielding

\displaystyle\gamma(\alpha_{1})

\displaystyle=p(\alpha_{1})\,(\cos\alpha_{1},\sin\alpha_{1})+p^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})\,(-\sin\alpha_{1},\cos\alpha_{1}),

\displaystyle\gamma(\alpha_{2})

\displaystyle=p(\alpha_{2})\,(\cos\alpha_{2},\sin\alpha_{2})+p^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})\,(-\sin\alpha_{2},\cos\alpha_{2}).

Next, one needs to find the coordinates of the point
M
as the intersection of the tangent lines. Thus
(x_{M},y_{M})
, the coordinates of
M
, satisfy the system:

\begin{cases}\cos\alpha_{1}x+\sin\alpha_{1}y=p(\alpha_{1})\\
\cos\alpha_{2}x+\sin\alpha_{2}y=p(\alpha_{2}).\end{cases}

Solving this system, we get:

(x_{M},y_{M})=\frac{1}{\sin(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}\bigg(p(\alpha_{1})\sin\alpha_{2}-p(\alpha_{2})\sin\alpha_{1},\ p(\alpha_{2})\cos\alpha_{1}-p(\alpha_{1})\cos\alpha_{2}\bigg).

Hence

\displaystyle l_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{x_{M}-x_{\gamma(\alpha_{1})}}{\cos(\alpha_{1}+\pi/2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{p(\alpha_{1})\sin\alpha_{2}-p(\alpha_{2})\sin\alpha_{1}-\sin(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})(p(\alpha_{1})\cos\alpha_{1}-p^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})\sin\alpha_{1})}{\sin\alpha_{1}{\sin(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}}.

Substituting into the numerator of this formula the identity

\sin\alpha_{2}=\sin\alpha_{1}\cos(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})+\cos\alpha_{1}\sin(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}),

we obtain the first claim of Lemma
2.1
.
The second formula is proved similalrly.

\Box

Now we are in position to compute the partial derivatives of the function
S
, see Figure
3
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The partial derivatives of
S(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
with respect to the first and the second arguments are

\displaystyle S_{1}=\frac{1}{2\cos^{2}\omega/2}(p(\alpha_{1})\cos\omega-p(\alpha_{2})+p^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})\sin\omega)=-l_{1}\tan\frac{\omega}{2}=-R_{1},

\displaystyle S_{2}=\frac{1}{2\cos^{2}\omega/2}(-p(\alpha_{2})\cos\omega+p(\alpha_{1})+p^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})\sin\omega)=l_{2}\tan\frac{\omega}{2}=R_{2},

where
\omega:=\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}
, and
R_{1},R_{2}
are the radii of the auxiliary circles.

Corollary 2.3
.

One has

dS=R_{2}d\alpha_{2}-R_{1}d\alpha_{1}
, so
dR\wedge d\alpha
is an invariant symplectic form.

Figure 3.
Invariant symplectic form
dR\wedge d\alpha
.

Next we need to compute second partial derivatives of
S
.

Lemma 2.4
.

The second partial derivatives of
S
are as follows:

\displaystyle S_{11}=\tan\frac{\omega}{2}(R_{1}+\rho(\alpha_{1})),

\displaystyle S_{22}=\tan\frac{\omega}{2}(R_{2}+\rho(\alpha_{2})),

\displaystyle S_{12}=-\frac{1}{\sin\omega}(R_{1}+R_{2}),

where
\omega=\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}
and
\rho
is the radius of curvature of
{\gamma}
.
In particular
S_{12}<0
and
S_{11},S_{22}>0
.

Corollary 2.5
.

The maps
T
and
T^{2}
are positive twist maps of the phase cylinder with respect to the coordinates
R,\alpha
.

Proof.

The differential of
T:(R,\alpha)\mapsto(R^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
can be written in the form:

DT=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{S_{22}}{S_{12}}&\frac{S_{12}^{2}-S_{11}S_{22}}{S_{12}}\\
-\frac{1}{S_{12}}&-\frac{S_{11}}{S_{12}}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus
\frac{\partial\alpha^{\prime}}{\partial R}=-\frac{1}{S_{12}}>0
.
Moreover, since
S_{11}
and
S_{22}
are strictly positive, we see that the product of two matrices
DT(R,\alpha)\cdot DT(R^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
also has a positive entry
\frac{\partial\alpha^{\prime\prime}}{\partial R}>0
. Hence
T^{2}
is a positive twist map as well.

\Box

As an application of the twist map property for
T,T^{2}
, we have

Theorem 1
.

The sets of 3-periodic orbits and 4-periodic points of the outer length billiard have empty interior.

Proof.

We shall give a proof for the set of 4-periodic orbits.
The other case is analogous (and we provide two other proofs in the 3-periodic case later).

Arguing toward contradiction, assume that there is a disc of 4-periodic points. Choose a
point
P_{0}
in the disc and let
v_{0}=\frac{\partial}{\partial R}
be the vertical tangent vector. Define
v_{i}:=DT^{i}(v_{0}),i=1,2,3.
By 4-periodicity, we have

DT^{4}(v_{0})=v_{0}.

Let
z_{i},i=0,\ldots,3
, be the
\alpha
-coordinates of the vectors
v_{i}
. Then
z_{0}=0
, and
since
T,T^{2}
are positive twist maps, we have that
z_{1},z_{2}
are strictly positive. On the other hand,
the maps
T^{-1},T^{-2}
are negative twist maps (as the inverses of positive twists), and hence

z_{3}
and
z_{2}
are strictly negative, since
DT^{-1}v_{0}=v_{3},DT^{-2}v_{0}=v_{2}
. This is a contradiction.

\Box

Remark 2.6
.

1. Analogously one can prove that if
T,T^{2},..,T^{k},k\geq 1
are positive twist maps, then the sets of periodic orbits of periods
2,3,\ldots,2k
have empty interior.

2. If all positive powers of
T

are positive twist maps, then
T
is totally integrable. This situation is proved to be very rigid for many billiard models, see
[
10
]
.

3.
Periodic invariant curves and a distribution on the space of polygons

Our goal in this section is to show that, for every
n\geq 3
, there exists a functional space of outer length billiard curves that possess invariant curves consisting of
n
-periodic points. Our approach is via sub-Riemannian geometry, following
[
7
,
15
,
12
]
.

Let
{\gamma}
be an oval, the outer length billiard table, and assume that there exists an invariant curve consisting of
n
-periodic points.

Figure 4.
ABC
is a 3-periodic orbit of the outer length billiard.

For example, consider Figure
4
. If the 3-periodic orbit
ABC
belongs to a 1-parameter family of such orbits then the sides of these triangles envelop the oval
{\gamma}
. That is, the motion of each side is an infinitesimal rotation about the tangency point of the respective circle with this side.

This motivates the following definitions.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}
be the space of convex
n
-gons in the plane, a
2n
-dimensional manifold.
We think of a polygon as a cyclically ordered set of its sides
L_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,n
, cooriented by the external normals.
For the vertices of the polygon, we use the notation
V_{i+\frac{1}{2}}:=L_{i}\cap L_{i+1}
.
The vertices and the sides are cyclically ordered counterclockwise.

Choose an origin, and let
(\alpha_{i},p_{i})
be the support coordinates of
L_{i}
, that is,
\alpha_{i}
is the direction of the coorienting normal and
p_{i}
is the signed distance from
L_{i}
to the origin (if the origin is inside the polygon, all distances are positive).
Then the unit coorienting vector is
(\cos\alpha_{i},\sin\alpha_{i})
, and the equation of the line in Cartesian coordinates is
x\cos\alpha_{i}+y\sin\alpha_{i}=p_{i}
.

Define an
n

\mathcal{D}_{n}
on
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}

L_{i-1},L_{i},L_{i+1}

L_{i-1}
and
L_{i+1}
and on the positive side of the line
L_{i}
. Let
C_{i}
be the tangency point of this circle with the line
L_{i}
, see Figure

\xi_{i}
be the infinitesimal rotation of the line
L_{i}
about point
C_{i}
. The distribution
\mathcal{D}_{n}
is spanned by the vector fields
\xi_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,n
.

\mathcal{D}_{n}

The distribution

In view of the above lemma, we restrict attention to polygons with a fixed perimeter, say, unit perimeter. Let
\mathcal{C}_{n}

Here is the main result of this section.

Theorem 2
.

For every
n\geq 3
, there exists a functional space of outer length billiard curves, sufficiently close to a circular one and possessing invariant curves consisting of
n
-periodic orbits.

Here is a description, in broad strokes, of the proof. We construct outer length billiard curves comprising
n
-periodic orbits by perturbing a circle (which obviously has the said property).
The circular
n
-periodic invariant curve of the outer length billiard is a closed horizontal curve
{\gamma}_{0}
in the space
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. One has the action of the group
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
that cyclically permutes the sides of
n
-gons and that preserves the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, and
{\gamma}_{0}
is invariant under this cyclic permutation. We show that
{\gamma}_{0}
admits a functional space of perturbations in the class of horizontal closed
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
-invariant curves in
\mathcal{C}_{n}

[

]

One has

The equation of the lines through point

Proposition 3.5

In a sufficiently small neighborhood of the curve

\mathcal{D}
. It follows that, along
{\gamma}_{0}
, the matrix
\theta_{j}([\xi_{i},\xi_{i+1}]),j=1,\ldots,n-1,i=1,\ldots,n
, has rank
n-1
. Therefore this rank equals
n-1
is a sufficiently small neighborhood of
{\gamma}_{0}
as well, that is,
T\mathcal{C}_{n}=\mathcal{D}+[\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}]
, as claimed.

\Box

Remark 3.6
.

We believe that
\mathcal{D}_{n}
has the growth type
(n,2n-1)
everywhere in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, and we checked it for
n=3
, but, at the moment, do not have a proof of this conjecture.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

A circular outer length billiard yields a 1-parameter family of periodic
n
-gons
L(t)=(L_{1}(t),\ldots,L_{n}(t)),\ t\in[0,1]
, circumscribed about the circular table (we continue to think of a polygon as a tuple of its sides). Let
\sigma
be the cyclic perturbation of the sides; then
L(1)=\sigma(L(0))
. This family of
n
-gons is the initial horizontal curve
\gamma_{0}:[0,1]\to\mathcal{C}_{n}
, given, after rescaling, by

\alpha_{i}(t)=\frac{2\pi}{n}(t+(i-1)),\ p_{i}(t)=1,\ i=1,\ldots,n.

Denote by
H^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}
the space of horizontal curves
{\gamma}:[0,1]\to\mathcal{C}_{n}
whose first two derivatives are square integrable, and let
H^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(c)
denote the subspace of paths that start at
c\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
. Then
H^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(c)
is a Hilbert manifold whose local coordinate charts are as follows.

We have the horizontal vector fields
\xi_{i},i=1,\ldots,n
, that span
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. If
{\gamma}(t)
is a horizontal curve then
\gamma^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(t)\xi_{i}({\gamma}(t))
, where
f_{i}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
are square integrable functions. These functions are the coordinates of the curve
{\gamma}(t)
. Thus the functional dimension of
H^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(c)
is
n
, and the space of non-parameterized oriented horizontal curves with a fixed starting point has functional dimension
n-1
.

Denote by
H^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(c_{0},c_{1})\subset H^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}
the space of the horizontal curves with the starting point
c_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
and the terminal point
c_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
. The circular outer length billiard table yields the above mentioned horizontal curve
{\gamma}_{0}\in H^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(L(0),L(1))
. We want to perturb this curve within the subspace
H^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(L(0),L(1))
.

We show that
H^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(L(0),L(1))\subset H^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(L(0))
is a Hilbert submanifold of codimension
2n-1
. To this end, let
\pi:H^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(L(0))\to\mathcal{C}_{n}
be the projection that assigns to a path
{\gamma}
its endpoint
{\gamma}(1)
. If this smooth map is a submersion, then the preimage
\pi^{-1}(L(1))
is a Hilbert submanifold of codimension
2n-1
.

However
\pi
may fail to be a submersion; the curves for which this happens are called singular. An example of a singular curve is a segment of the
x
-axis in 3-space with the Martinet distribution given by
dz-y^{2}dx=0
: the perturbations of this curve as a horizontal curve with fixed endpoints are just its reparameterizations.

Thus we need to check that
{\gamma}_{0}
is not singular, and we use a criterion due to Hsu
[
14
]
, in the form presented in Section 4 of
[
18
]
. We formulate this criterion in general form and then apply it in our special case.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold with a distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and let
\eta_{i}
be a collection of differential 1-forms that constitute a basis of the conormal bundle
\mathcal{D}^{\perp}
. Then every covector
\mathcal{D}^{\perp}
can be written as
\sum\lambda_{i}\eta_{i}
. Consider the differential 2-form
\omega(\lambda):=\sum\lambda_{i}d\eta_{i}
. A horizontal curve
{\gamma}
is not singular if its tangent vector
\dot{\gamma}
never lies in the kernel of the restriction of
\omega(\lambda)
to
\mathcal{D}
.

In our situation, the 1-forms
\theta_{i},i=1,\ldots,n
, generate
\mathcal{D}^{\perp}
, but these forms are not independent: they satisfy one linear relation. Along the curve
{\gamma}_{0}
, one has
\theta_{i}=dp_{i}
. It follows from the formula for the perimeter in Remark
3.4
that, along
{\gamma}_{0}
,

0=dF=2\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n}dp_{i}.

Therefore
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}=0
along
{\gamma}_{0}
, and to factor this relation out, we assume that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=0
.

Along
{\gamma}_{0}
, one has

\frac{\partial\Phi_{i}}{\partial p_{i+1}}=-\frac{\partial\Phi_{i}}{\partial p_{i-1}}=\frac{1}{2\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{n}\right)},\ \frac{\partial\Phi_{i}}{\partial p_{i}}=0,\ \frac{\partial\Phi_{i}}{\partial\alpha_{i-1}}=\frac{\partial\Phi_{i}}{\partial\alpha_{i+1}}=\frac{1}{2\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right)},

hence

d\theta_{i}=\frac{1}{2\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{n}\right)}d\alpha_{i}\wedge(dp_{i+1}-dp_{i-1})+\frac{1}{2\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right)}(d\alpha_{i}\wedge d\alpha_{i+1}-d\alpha_{i-1}\wedge d\alpha_{i}).

One also has

\dot{\gamma}_{0}=\frac{2\pi}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial\alpha_{i},

therefore

\dot{\gamma}_{0}\,\lrcorner\,d\theta_{i}=\frac{\pi}{2n\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right)}(d\alpha_{i-1}-2d\alpha_{i}+d\alpha_{i+1}).

Finally, assume that
\dot{\gamma}_{0}\,\lrcorner\,\omega(\lambda)=0
on
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, that is,
\omega(\lambda)(\dot{\gamma}_{0},\xi_{i})=0
for
i=1,\dots,n
. Then
\lambda_{i-1}-2\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{i+1}=0
for all
i
,
hence
\lambda_{1}=\ldots=\lambda_{n}
. But
\sum_{i+1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=0
, therefore
\omega(\lambda)=0
.

We conclude that the curve
{\gamma}_{0}
is nonsingular, completing the proof.

\Box

4.
Period four, the centrally symmetric case

In this section, we provide an explicit parameterization of centrally symmetric outer length billiard curves
{\gamma}
that possess invariant curves consisting of 4-periodic points.

Lemma 4.1
.

If a centrally symmetric curve
{\gamma}
possesses an invariant curve
\delta
consisting of periodic points, then
\delta
is centrally symmetric.

Proof.

Let
S
be the reflection in the origin. Since
\gamma
is centrally-symmetric,

S
and
T
commute. Hence
S(\delta)
is an invariant curve of
T
with the same rotation number.

We shall use the following consequence of the Aubry-Mather theory on the structure of minimal orbits with rational rotation number (see, for example, the survey paper
[
4
]
, Theorem 5.8):

If
\delta
is a rotational invariant curve of a twist map of the cylinder with rational rotation number which consists solely of periodic orbits, then any other rotational invariant curve with the same rotation number must coincide with
\delta
.

Indeed, it follows from this theory that all orbits lying on a rotational invariant curve are minimizing. Moreover, if the curve has a rational rotation number
\rho
and all the orbits on this curve are periodic, then the set of these orbits coincides with the set of the minimizers with the rotation number
\rho
.

Applying this fact to the invariant curves
\delta
and
S(\delta)
we conclude that

\delta=S(\delta),

as claimed.

\Box

By the above lemma, the periodic quadrilateral must be parallelograms.
Let us specialize the discussion of the preceding section to this particular situation.

The space
{\mathcal{C}}
of origin-centered parallelograms of a fixed perimeter is 3-dimensional. Such a parallelogram is characterized by four parameters
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},p_{1},p_{2})
, see Figure
7
. The radii of the circles are equal to
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, and the perimeter of the parallelogram equals
\frac{4(p_{1}+p_{2})}{\sin(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}
. Assume that the perimeter is 4, that is,
p_{1}+p_{2}=\sin(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})
.

Figure 7.
Parameters of an origin centered parallelogram.

We apply Lemma
3.3
in this situation. The angles involved are
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1}+\pi,\alpha_{2}+\pi
, and the respective support numbers are
p_{1},p_{2},p_{1},p_{2}
. A calculation yields

Lemma 4.2
.

The 2-dimensional distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{C}
is spanned by the vector fields

\displaystyle\xi_{1}=\partial\alpha_{1}-\cot(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})(p_{1}+p_{2})\partial p_{1}=\partial\alpha_{1}-\cos(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})\partial p_{1},

\displaystyle\xi_{2}=\partial\alpha_{2}+\cot(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})(p_{1}+p_{2})\partial p_{2}=\partial\alpha_{2}+\cos(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})\partial p_{2},

and one has
[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]=\sin(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})(\partial p_{1}-\partial p_{2})\neq 0
.

We see that
\mathcal{D}
is a completely non-integrable, i.e., it is a contact structure. As a contact form, one may take

\lambda=\cos(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})(d\alpha_{1}+d\alpha_{2})+dp_{1}-dp_{2},

whereas the differential of the perimeter, a 1-form that vanishes on
\mathcal{C}
, is

dF=\cos(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})(d\alpha_{1}-d\alpha_{2})-dp_{1}-dp_{2}.

Corollary 4.3
.

There are no open sets consisting of 4-periodic orbits.

Proof.

Such a set would be a surface tangent to the distribution
\mathcal{D}
; this would contradict its complete non-integrability.

\Box

Now we shall parameterize the centrally symmetric smooth strictly convex outer length billiard curves
{\gamma}
that possess invariant curves consisting of 4-periodic points. Let us change coordinates:

x=\frac{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}{2},\ y=2\cos(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}),\ z=p_{2}-p_{1}.

Then
x\in[0,2\pi)
is an angle, while
y
and
z
are reals. In these coordinates, the contact 1-form has the standard form
\lambda=ydx-dz
. We are interested in horizontal, Legendrian, curves, parameterized by the variable
x
; they are given by

where

is a differentiable function satisfying
|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq 2
for all

.

In addition, we have an action of the cyclic group
{\mathbb{Z}}_{4}
given by the action of its generator:

(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},p_{1},p_{2})\mapsto(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1}+\pi,p_{2},p_{1}),

see Figure
7

f(x+\frac{\pi}{2})=-f(x)
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
f(0)=0
.

Expressing the old coordinates in terms of the new ones yields

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=x-\frac{1}{2}\arccos\left(\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{2}\right),\ \alpha_{2}=x+\frac{1}{2}\arccos\left(\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{2}\right),

\displaystyle p_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{4-f^{\prime}(x)^{2}}-f(x)}{4},\ p_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{4-f^{\prime}(x)^{2}}+f(x)}{4}.

We arrive at the next result.

Theorem 3
.

Let
f(x)
be a

for all

{\gamma}(x)=p(x)(\cos\alpha(x),\sin\alpha(x))+\frac{p^{\prime}(x)}{\alpha^{\prime}(x)}(-\sin\alpha(x),\cos\alpha(x)),

where

p(x)=\frac{\sqrt{4-f^{\prime}(x)^{2}}-f(x)}{4},\alpha(x)=x-\frac{1}{2}\arccos\left(\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{2}\right),

possesses an invariant curve consisting of 4-periodic points, and all such outer length billiard curves are given by the above formula with a suitable function
f(x)
.

For example, if
{\gamma}
is a circle, then
\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}=\frac{\pi}{2}
, and
z=y=0
, that is,
f(x)=0
identically. Let
f(x)
be a differentiable function satisfying

(i.e., its Fourier expansion contains only the

(2+4k)

Example 4.4
.

Let us consider the case when

5

We use the information about 4-periodic orbits from

We have

Since

Following the maxim that it’s better to have different proofs of the same theorem than the same proof of different theorems, we present two other proofs of the 3-periodic version of Ivrii’s conjecture for outer length billiard. The first is purely geometric; it is inspired by and similar to that in

The set of 3-periodic points of an outer length billiard has empty interior.

First proof.

Let
{\gamma}
be an oval that possesses an open set of 3-periodic orbits. We work toward a contradiction.

Let
ABC

and

A_{1}
and

and

and

Second proof.
We specialize the formulas of Section

\displaystyle W_{1}=\frac{\tan u}{\sin 2v},W_{2}=\frac{\tan v}{\sin 2w},W_{3}=\frac{\tan w}{\sin 2u},

We continue the proof of the 3-periodic Ivrii conjecture for outer length billiards.
Assume that there exists an open disc consisting of 3-periodic points. Then one has a horizontal disc

\xi_{i}

a_{1}\xi_{1}+a_{2}\xi_{2}+a_{3}\xi_{3}\ \ {\rm and}\ \ b_{1}\xi_{1}+b_{2}\xi_{2}+b_{3}\xi_{3}.

Without loss of generality, assume that
a_{1}\neq 0,b_{2}\neq 0
and, taking linear combinations, replace these fields by
\xi_{1}+a\xi_{3}
and
\xi_{2}+b\xi_{3}
. Then

[\xi_{1}+a\xi_{3},\xi_{2}+b\xi_{3}]=f(\xi_{1}+a\xi_{3})+g(\xi_{2}+b\xi_{3}).

The bracket on the left equals

[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]-a[\xi_{2},\xi_{3}]-b[\xi_{3},\xi_{1}]+\{\xi_{1}(b)+a\xi_{3}(b)-\xi_{2}(a)-b\xi_{3}(a)\}\,\xi_{3},

and this expression does not contain the terms with
\partial\alpha_{1}
and
\partial\alpha_{2}
. Hence
f=g=0
, and we
conclude that

(6)

[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]-a[\xi_{2},\xi_{3}]-b[\xi_{3},\xi_{1}]=0,\ \xi_{1}(b)+a\xi_{3}(b)-\xi_{2}(a)-b\xi_{3}(a)=0.

The first equation reads

W_{2}\partial p_{2}-U_{2}\partial p_{1}-a(W_{3}\partial p_{3}-U_{2}\partial p_{2})-b(W_{1}\partial p_{1}-U_{3}\partial p_{3})=0,

and solving it yields

a=-\frac{W_{2}}{U_{2}}=-\frac{\tan v}{\tan u},\ b=-\frac{U_{1}}{W_{1}}=-\frac{\tan w}{\tan u}.

Recall the equalities (
4
) and that
\xi_{i}=\partial\alpha_{i}+\Phi_{i}\partial p_{i}
.
We are in a position to compute (twice) the left hand side of the second equality in (
6
):

\displaystyle-(\partial w-\partial u)\left(\frac{\tan w}{\tan u}\right)+\frac{\tan v}{\tan u}(\partial v-\partial w)\left(\frac{\tan w}{\tan u}\right)

\displaystyle+(\partial u-\partial v)\left(\frac{\tan v}{\tan u}\right)-\frac{\tan w}{\tan u}(\partial v-\partial w)\left(\frac{\tan v}{\tan u}\right)=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\cos^{2}w\tan u}-\frac{\tan w}{\sin^{2}u}-\frac{\tan v}{\cos^{2}w\tan^{2}u}-\frac{\tan v}{\sin^{2}u}-\frac{1}{\cos^{2}v\tan u}-\frac{\tan w}{\cos^{2}v\tan^{2}u}.

Every term of this expression is negative, so it is not equal to zero. This contradiction completes the proof.

\Box

Remark 5.1
.

1. A completely non-integrable 3-dimensional distribution in 5-dimensional manifold may admit a horizontal surface.
Consider 5-dimensional space with coordinates
x,y,z,u,v
and a 3-dimensional distribution
\mathcal{D}
spanned by the vector fields
\partial x,\partial y,\partial z+x\partial u+y\partial v
. Then
\mathcal{D}
is non-integrable with the bracket growth type
(3,5)
, but
\mathcal{D}
admits a horizontal 2-dimensional disc spanned by the commuting vector fields
\partial x
and
\partial y
.

2. One can construct four germs of properly oriented convex curves so that the composition of the respective four outer length billiard reflections is the identity map. It suffices to take a pair of arcs on each of two concentric circles. The respective outer length billiard maps are rotations about the common center of the circles, and such rotations commute, see
Figure
14
. A similar consideration applies to other billiard models, including Birkhoff billiards (where one could also use confocal ellipses instead of concentric circles).
