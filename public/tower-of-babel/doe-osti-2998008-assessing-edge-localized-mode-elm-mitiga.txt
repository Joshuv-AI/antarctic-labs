 ABSTRACT

Simulations of pellet edge-localized mode (ELM) triggering with the M3D-C1 code confirmed
experimentally observed pellet mass thresholds in low-collisionality DIII-D discharges and
predicted extremely large thresholds in ITER [A. Wingen et al, Nucl. Fusion 64, 066015
(2024)]. The linear (static) and nonlinear (dynamic) workflows developed in this previous
work are now applied to the SPARC tokamak. Multiple synthetic equilibria for the high-
performance H-mode scenario are tested for MHD stability without pellets using the linear
workflow and ELITE simulations; a stable one is identified. Pellets of various mass are
injected along an inboard, near-midplane trajectory in the M3D-C1 simulation. No pellet
mass threshold for ELM triggering can be found for realistic pellet masses, concluding that
fueling from this injection location would not trigger ELMs. A poloidal scan of possible
injection locations shows very high thresholds on the high-field side and reasonable thresholds
on the low-field-side (LFS). Based on results of this work the location for a LFS injection
was finalized in the SPARC design. For this LFS injection the pellet mass threshold is
determined using the linear workflow, aligning well with the poloidal scan.

 1. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

The overall objective of this work in collaboration with the team at Commonwealth Fusion
Systems (CFS) is to answer the question if pellet ELM triggering is feasible in SPARC
and which poloidal injection location is the most promising in SPARC. This information
will then be used to inform the SPARC design and operational planning, in particular the
ELM mitigation strategy. If pellet ELM triggering is shown to be feasible, the results can
be used to set the scaling and design parameters for the ELM pellet triggering system on
SPARC. This can include modifying the injection location, setting minimum pellet size,
and finding synergies between the pellet fueling and ELM pacing pellet injector designs.
Depending on the required pellet throughput, these results may lead to a limit on operating
modes due to capacity limits in the tritium processing plant, or drive expansions to the
size of the tritium exhaust processing loop. If shown to be infeasible, it may lead to the
development of additional operating scenarios that increase the likelihood of pellet ELM
triggering feasibility and increase the time and resources spent developing alternative ELM
free scenarios and resonant magnetic perturbation ELM suppression systems. The results
either way will inform the plasma scenario development work and be used to reassess the
inherent risk of operating H-mode plasmas.
 The following detailed tasks were laid out in the INFUSE scope of work:
  • Determine pellet mass threshold for SPARC full-field, full-current D-T scenario using
 linear simulations with the M3D-C1 code [1].
  • Complete ELITE simulations of each scenario and determine the operational point with
 respect to the peeling-ballooning stability boundary.

 1

  • Determine pellet mass thresholds for all considered SPARC scenarios using linear and
 nonlinear M3D-C1 simulations.
  • Vary poloidal injection location and determine the one with the lowest mass threshold
 for a single scenario.
 Four possible operational scenarios were identified as possible candidates for the tasks
in the scope of work, leading with the full-field, full-current D-T scenario; the latter is in the
following referred to as high-performance discharge. The original pellet injection design in
SPARC considered the high-field-side (HFS) injection location for fueling, while a low-field-
side (LFS) injection tube was anticipated, but it’s location was yet undetermined.

 2. BENEFITS TO THE FUNDING DOE OFFICE’S MISSION

In a wider view, the results of this work will also inform on the e!ectiveness of pellet ELM
triggering in other, future burning plasma devices like ITER or FPP, since they will likely
operate under similarly low-collisionality conditions.

3. TECHNICAL DISCUSSION OF WORK PERFORMED BY ALL PARTIES

3.1. ESTABLISHING A SUITABLE EQUILIBRIUM
Establishing a suitable equilibrium is a crucial first step and the basis for all further work
done in this project. It took the majority of the project’s time to iterate with the team at
CFS to establish such an equilibrium for pellet ELM triggering simulations with the M3D-
C1 code [1]. The latter is a finite element code, designed for performing three-dimensional
nonlinear magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) calculations of a magnetized tokamak plasma. A
full time line of this process can be found in Appendix A. In previous applications of the
simulation workflows for the DIII-D tokamak [2] and the ITER tokamak [3], a suitable
equilibrium could be established right away. Hence, it was unexpected that this step took
up as much time as it did. The reason this initial step was slower than anticipated is that
we needed not only to design the equilibria but also the kinetic profiles and boostrap current
in a self consistent manner without reference from experimental data.
 The equilibrium, an axisymmetric solution to the Grad-Shafranov equation, also known
as EFIT equilibrium [4], in combination with 1D radial electron density and electron
temperature profiles, must fulfill the following criteria to be suitable for M3D-C1 simulations
for pellet ELM triggering.
  • All EFIT profiles are initially only defined up to the separatrix. For use with M3D-C1
 the density, temperature and pressure profiles must be extended into the scrape-o! layer
 (SOL) in a decaying way without ever becoming negative. Hence, these profile must
 have finite, non-zero values at the separatrix.

 2

  • The EFIT pressure profile is typically self-consistent with p = ne Te + ni Ti + pimp + pbeam
 for electrons e, main species ions i like Deuterium, all impurities imp, and other
 pressure sources like neutral beams beam. However, M3D-C1 assumes ne = ni for
 quasi-neutrality and then derives a ”e!ective” Ti from given inputs: pressure, ne and
 Te , while neglecting impurities and other sources. So it is possible that the e!ective
 Ti becomes negative in the edge, resulting in negative pressures in the simulation and
 nonphysical conditions leading to a failure of the simulation. So, the given input profiles
 must satisfy Ti = p/ne → Te > 0 everywhere.
  • The extension of the 1D profiles of pressure, ne and Te into the SOL must decay rapidly
 enough so that remaining gradients in the profiles are close to zero at the vessel wall.
 The code assumes a zero gradient beyond the vessel wall and therefore large gradients
 just inside the wall can lead to large oscillations when interpolated onto the 2D mesh,
 causing the 2D distributions to become negative in some areas, if the profile’s asymptotic
 value is small.
  • Finally, if all quantities are positive and the simulation is numerically stable, the
 equilibrium must be MHD stable. This means that linear growth rates to small
 perturbations are negative for all considered toroidal modes.
All provided equilibria before the one used here violated at least one of the criteria described
above, resulting in frequent iterations between the CFS team providing a new version and
the ORNL team testing it. A total number of 15 di!erent equilibria were tested between
September 2024 and July 2025.
 A numerically and MHD stable equilibrium was finally identified and is labeled ”2327”.
This equilibrium, at a machine time slice of 15 s, is used for all further simulations. It is
an up-down symmetric, 12 T field, 8.7 MA plasma current H-mode discharge with about
5 keV pedestal electron temperature and about 2.5 1020 m→3 pedestal electron density. This
represents a high-performance discharge scenario for SPARC. Figure 1 shows the poloidal
cross section on the right and the plasma profiles on the left. The blue and red lines in the
cross section mark the pellet injection trajectories planned in SPARC for the HFS injection
and the LFS injection, respectively.

3.1.1. M3D-C1 linear stability
 Now that a suitable equilibrium is established, M3D-C1 simulations can begin. A uniform
mesh with about 150k nodes and an adapted mesh with about 130k nodes, which has a higher
mesh resolution near the plasma edge than in the plasma core or SOL, are tested, and the
adapted mesh is used for all further simulations. The next step is to simulate the linear
stability of the equilibrium without injecting a pellet. Note that this is the final criterium
listed in the previous section. This provide the baseline for determining the pellet mass
threshold.

linear growth rate to a small ( 10→8 ) perturbation for a given toroidal mode number. Testing

3.1.2. ELITE stability
  In order to verify that the equilibrium is indeed stable, we perform a simulation with the
ELITE code [5]. ELITE calculates the pedestal stability diagram, as shown in Fig 3.

 Figure 3. ELITE stability diagram for equilibrium 2327.

 The x-axis represents the normalized pressure gradient, the y-axis the normalized edge
current density. The color contour where the diamagnetic stabilization is equal to one, given
by the solid black line in the top left corner, denotes where the ideal peeling-ballooning
instability overcomes diamagnetic stabilization and would result in an ELM crash. The blue
region represents MHD stable conditions. The black marker shows the operating point of
the discharge deep within the stable region.

3.2. HIGH-FIELD-SIDE INJECTION
In this section we inject pellets along the HFS injection trajectory, given by the blue line in
Fig. 1. Pellets travel ballistically at v = 200 m/s in the simulations. Note that the actual
HFS SPARC injection tube is limited to v < 100 m/s due to the bending of the guide tube.
However, as shown in Sec. 3.5 and observed in DIII-D pellet injection experiments [6], pellet
mass thresholds only depend weakly on pellet velocity.

3.2.1. Pellet ablation profiles
  A 2D linear M3D-C1 pellet simulation requires 4 inputs to initiate the pellet as a Gaussian
density source in the continuity equation, the pellet position R, Z, the amplitude of the
Gaussian density peak and it’s width. Note that in linear simulation the Gaussian density
peak is kept fixed, which means that the pellet does not move or ablate. We use the xPellet
code to obtain the density deposition profile for a given pellet size and velocity. Such profiles
are shown in Fig 4. Note that the pellet size is given as radius in mm for a spherical pellet.
 The xPellet code uses the temperature and density profiles of the equilibrium to ablate
the pellet along it’s trajectory and returns the densety deposited in the plasma by the pellet.
 5

 Figure 4. Pellet ablation profiles along the HFS injection trajectory using the xPellet
 code for multiple pellet sizes and pellet velocity 200 m/s. The x-axis is the normalized
 flux as radial coordinate and the y-axis is the change in electron density compared to the
 equilibrium without pellet.

By fitting each peak in the figure with a Gaussian, we obtain all 4 inputs. The pellet mass
increases with the third power of the radius. Hence, the deposited density goes up strongly
with increasing pellet size. Also, the peak of the pellet density moves radially inwards for
larger pellets. The pellet sizes used here represent typical numbers in present day and future
tokamaks. 0.72 mm pellets are used to trigger ELMs as well as fuel the plasma in DIII-D,
1.72 mm is the planned size for ITER ELM pacing pellets. 3 mm is the planned size for
ITER fueling pellets, and 4.5 mm represents an upper limit on pellet mass throughput. A
pellet of such size would terminate a plasma in a radiative collapse, similar to a disruption
mitigation system.

3.2.2. M3D-C1 linear stability
 To determine a pellet mass threshold for ELM triggering we simulate the linear stability
of the equilibrium with each of the five di!erent pellet sizes for a range of toroidal mode
numbers, as previously discussed in Sec. 3.1.1. The result is shown in Fig. 5.
 As can be seen, only the largest pellet with radius of 4.5 mm can cause a very
small increases in the linear stability growth rates around the n = 7 mode. Because
linear simulations keep the density and temperature profiles of the equilibrium fixed, these
simulations cannot model a radiative collapse. The conclusion is that no instability is
triggered by the injected pellets and a theoretical, yet nonphysical pellet mass threshold
would be larger than 4.5 mm in pellet radius, which corresponds to a pellet mass of 75 mg.
Pellets injected from the HFS injection location do not trigger ELMs. Therefore this location
is suitable for pellet fueling.

3.4. POLOIDAL INJECTION LOCATION SCAN
From the two designated injection locations we found that the HFS injection trajectory will
not trigger an instability in the simulations, while the pellet mass threshold found for the
LFS injection location is between planned ITER pacing and fueling pellet masses. Here we
asses any other poloidal location and inject pellets along radial trajectories from multiple
geometric poloidal angles. Note that we position, in flux ε, and scale, in amplitude and
width, the pellets the same as used for the HFS injection, see Fig. 4. The respective R, Z
locations are then determined by the flux coordinate ε and the respective poloidal angle.
The linear workflow is then used for all locations and pellet sizes to find possible thresholds.

 Figure 9. Pellet mass thresholds for various poloidal injection locations. The green and
 red circles show typical pellet masses for ITER pacing and fueling pellets respectively. The
 black markers show the mass thresholds for HFS and LFS injection.

 Figure 9 shows the result of the poloidal scan. On the HFS, between angles of 90↑ and
270↑ no threshold is found for the tested pellet masses, similar to the designated HFS injection
location. Therefore, we conclude that all of the HFS is suitable for pellet fueling without
triggering ELMs. On the LFS thresholds are found and all found thresholds are similira
in magnitude, in between the planned ITER fueling (red circle) and pacing (green circle)
pellet masses. While the threshold increases slightly towards the upper and lower poloidal

 9

directions, the lowest thresholds are found around the outboard midplane. This result is
in line with previous results found for the DIII-D tokamak [2] and the ITER tokamak [3].
Here we also observe, that the simulated thresholds are quite up-down-symmetric. As the
underlying equilibrium is up-down symmetric, this is not unexpected, but a good sanity
check.
 The black markers show the thresholds for the designated injection trajectories from
the previous sections; the HFS at 227↑ and the LFS at 292↑ . We can see that these are well
aligned with the results of the poloidal scan.

3.5. PELLET VELOCITY SCAN
As briefly mentioned earlier, the pellet mass threshold only depends quite weakly on the
pellet velocity. This has been observed in DIII-D experiments, where no correlation was
observed between the mass threshold and pellet velocities in a range between 50 m/s and
400 m/s. Here we use the LFS injection trajectory and change the pellet velocity between
50 m/s and 200 m/s. Note that in the linear M3D-C1 simulations the pellet is stationary.
So the pellet velocity is indirectly included in the simulation by changing the pellet velocity
in the xPellet simulations, see Fig. 6. For each velocity we repeat the xPellet run, refit the
resulting density ablation profiles with Gaussian peaks and use new location, amplitude and
width in the respective M3D-C1 simulation. Using the full linear workflow then results in a
pellet mass threshold for each velocity.

 Figure 10. Dependence of the pellet mass threshold on pellet velocity. This uses the LFS
 injection trajectory.

 In Fig. 10 we see that the change of the pellet mass threshold is quite small with
the increase in velocity. At 200 m/s the threshold drops about 16% compared to 50 m/s.
However, we note that the faster the pellet moves, the lower a pellet mass threshold becomes,
within the range of velocities considered here. Hence, with the velocity restriction on the
HFS injection guide tube, we conclude that the slower pellet injection there benefits the
pellet fueling without triggering ELMs strategy.

 7. CONCLUSIONS

The overall objective of this project was to asses if pellet ELM triggering in SPARC is
feasible and under which conditions. The simulations found that pellet ELM triggering is
indeed possible in the given scenario. A pellet mass threshold of 16.75 mg was found for the
LFS injection trajectory. Before this project only the HFS injection trajectory was finalized
in the SPARC design. The LFS trajectory was anticipated but it’s location was finalized
in the design during the course of this project as a result of the HFS injection simulations,
which found no possible threshold for ELM triggering at the HFS. However, the lack of a
threshold on the HFS is beneficial for pellet fueling without triggering ELMs.
 A full poloidal scan of pellet injection locations using radial trajectories revealed that
the entire HFS is suitable for fueling and does not trigger instabilities in the simulations.
The found pellet mass thresholds on the LFS are all similar in magnitude between planned
ITER pacing and fueling pellet masses. The previous results for HFS and LFS align well
with the poloidal scan.
 The following detailed tasks were laid out in the INFUSE scope of work and all were
successfully addressed:
  • We determined the pellet mass threshold for SPARC full-field, full-current D-T scenario
 using linear simulations with the M3D-C1 code. The HFS injection has a theoretical
 threshold larger than 75 mg, while the LFS injection has a threshold of 16.75 mg.
  • The ELITE simulation of the given scenario found the operational point with respect to
 the peeling-ballooning stability boundary well inside the stable region, which is in line
 with M3D-C1 simulations without pellet.
  • We determined pellet mass thresholds for one SPARC scenario using linear M3D-C1
 simulations. Other scenarios mentioned in the scope of work were not addressed due to
 11

 the significant issue of finding a suitable stable equilibrium. 2D nonlinear simulations
 were started, but will be continued and evaluated in the future collaboration.
  • We varied poloidal injection location and found the lowest mass threshold for a single
 scenario. It is on the LFS around the outboard midplane, which is consistend with
 previous published results.
 On top of these tasks, we found that the pellet mass threshold depends only weakly on
the pellet velocity and that the threshold is larger for slower pellets. This is beneficial for
pellet fueling using the HFS injection trajectory, as the HFS guide tube is limited to pellet
velocities below 100 m/s.
