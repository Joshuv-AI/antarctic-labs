3.
The case

The assumption that
p\equiv 1\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)
, in particular the assumption that
p\equiv 1\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)
, implies that either
m
or
n
must be
0\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)
. As our arguments (previous and following) are symmetric in
m
and
n
, no generality is lost in assuming that
n\equiv 0\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)
. With this assumption, in this section we set

N:=\frac{ns}{3},

and we recall that
3m\mu=1+ps
. The goal of this section is proof of the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Let
p\equiv 1\allowbreak\mkern 6.0mu({\operator@font mod}\,\,12)
and
p=m^{2}+n^{2}
, with
3|n
, and let
b>0
.

(1)

If
m\equiv 1\allowbreak\mkern 6.0mu({\operator@font mod}\,\,3)
, then

(3.1)

Q(q^{bm},q^{p})Q(q^{bn},q^{p})=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}q^{\gamma_{k}}Q\mspace{-1.5mu}\big({q^{(\alpha_{k}-p^{2})/3},q^{p^{2}}}\big)Q\mspace{-1.5mu}\big({q^{(2p^{2}-\beta_{k})/3},q^{p^{2}}}\big).

(2)

If
m\equiv 2\allowbreak\mkern 6.0mu({\operator@font mod}\,\,3)
, then

(3.2)

Q(q^{bm},q^{p})Q(q^{bn},q^{p})=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}q^{\gamma_{k}}Q\mspace{-1.5mu}\big({q^{(2p^{2}-\alpha_{k})/3},q^{p^{2}}}\big)Q\mspace{-1.5mu}\big({q^{(\beta_{k}-p^{2})/3},q^{p^{2}}}\big).

Proof.

In short, we aim to show that all
r
-components (for
0\leq r\leq p-1
) of both sides of (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) are equal. Thus, fix
r
with
0\leq r\leq p-1
, let
\kappa
satisfy

3b\kappa m\equiv r\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,p),\qquad 0\leq\kappa\leq p-1,

and for this
\kappa
let

\alpha=\alpha_{\kappa},\quad\beta=\beta_{\kappa},\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma=\gamma_{\kappa}.

By Lemma
3
, the
r
-component of
Q(q^{bm},q^{p})Q(q^{bn},q^{p})
is

(3.3)

\displaystyle q^{\gamma}\langle-q^{\alpha},-q^{\beta};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}-q^{\gamma+\delta}\langle-q^{\alpha-nsp^{2}},-q^{\beta+msp^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}

\displaystyle-q^{\gamma+\xi}\langle-q^{\alpha-msp^{2}},-q^{\beta-nsp^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}

\displaystyle+q^{\gamma+\delta+\xi}\langle-q^{\alpha-msp^{2}-nsp^{2}},-q^{\beta+msp^{2}-nsp^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty},

where

\delta=-bnsp+\tfrac{1}{6}(1+ps)p^{2}s\qquad\text{and}\qquad\xi=-bmps-\kappa p^{2}{s}+\tfrac{1}{6}(1+ps)p^{2}s.

Under our assumptions that
3|n
and
N=\tfrac{ns}{3}
,
we may further reduce (
3.3
). In particular, as
nsp^{2}=Ny
and
\sigma_{-N}=Ny+\sigma_{N}
, we use (
2.3a
) to transform (
3.3
) into

\displaystyle q^{\gamma}\langle-q^{\alpha},-q^{\beta};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}-q^{\gamma+\delta+[N(\alpha-y)-\sigma_{N}]}\langle-q^{\alpha},-q^{\beta+msp^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}

\displaystyle-q^{\gamma+\xi+[N(\beta-y)-\sigma_{N}]}\langle-q^{\alpha-msp^{2}},-q^{\beta};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}

\displaystyle+q^{\gamma+\delta+\xi+\left[N(\alpha-msp^{2}-y)-\sigma_{N}\right]+\left[N(\beta+msp^{2}-y)-\sigma_{N}\right]}\langle-q^{\alpha-msp^{2}},-q^{\beta+msp^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty},

which we see can be factored as

(3.4)

\displaystyle q^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\left(\langle-q^{\alpha};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}-q^{\xi+[N(\beta-y)-\sigma_{N}]}\langle-q^{\alpha-msp^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\langle-q^{\beta};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}-q^{\delta+[N(\alpha-y)-\sigma_{N}]}\langle-q^{\beta+msp^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}\right).

Pausing to consider the right-hand sides of (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we recall that

Q(z,q)=\langle-z^{3}q;q^{3}\rangle_{\infty}-z\langle-z^{3}q^{2};q^{3}\rangle_{\infty},

so that, for general integers
A
and
B
, one has

(3.5)

\begin{aligned} Q(q^{\frac{A}{3}},q^{p^{2}})Q(q^{\frac{B}{3}},q^{p^{2}})&=\left(\langle-q^{A+p^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}-q^{\frac{A}{3}}\langle-q^{A+2p^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}\right)\\
&\qquad\times\left(\langle-q^{B+p^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}-q^{\frac{B}{3}}\langle-q^{B+2p^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}\right)\end{aligned}\qquad(y=3p^{2}).

Comparing (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) (ignoring the extra
q^{\gamma}
in the former), we want to select
A
and
B
to both equate the expressions, and to have
A/3
and
B/3
be integral. To equate (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) then, evidently we want

A+p^{2}=\alpha\qquad\text{or}\qquad A+p^{2}=3p^{2}-\alpha,

and

B+p^{2}=\beta\qquad\text{or}\qquad B+p^{2}=3p^{2}-\beta.

For our integrality condition, dividing these potential
A
and
B
by
3
, we see that

\frac{\alpha-p^{2}}{3}=bp+\kappa mp+\frac{(p-m-n)p}{6}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{2p^{2}-\alpha}{3}=-bp-\kappa mp+\frac{(p+m+n)p}{6},

and

\frac{\beta-p^{2}}{3}=\kappa np+\frac{(p+m-n)p}{6}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{2p^{2}-\beta}{3}=-\kappa np+\frac{(p-m+n)p}{6}.

Thus, our choices for
A
and
B
must ensure that at least one of the numerators in each pair is
0\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,6)
. Already we know that
p\pm m\pm n\equiv 0\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,2)
, so it remains only to consider
p\pm m\pm n\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)
. We have assumed that
p\equiv 1\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)
and
n\equiv 0\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)
, so our choices for
A
and
B
depend only on
m\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)
.

Focusing on the case
m\equiv 1\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)
, we see at once that

\frac{\alpha-p^{2}}{3}=bp+\kappa mp+\frac{(p-m-n)p}{6}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{2p^{2}-\beta}{3}=-\kappa n+\frac{(p-m+n)p}{6}

are integral. Thus, in this case we let

A=\alpha-p^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad B=2p^{2}-\beta,

and, since
s\equiv 2\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)
, let

ms=2+3M\qquad(\text{cf.~$ns=3N$}),

and directly verify that (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) are equal. The computations are tedious and cumbersome: as an example we note that

\langle-q^{\alpha-msp^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}=\langle-q^{(\alpha+p^{2})-(M+1)y};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}=q^{\left[(M+1)(\alpha+p^{2}-y)-\sigma_{M+1}\right]}\langle-q^{\alpha+p^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty},

so that in (
3.4
) we have

q^{\xi+\left[N(\beta-y)-\sigma_{N}\right]}\langle-q^{\alpha-msp^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}=q^{\xi+\left[N(\beta-y)-\sigma_{N}\right]+\left[(M+1)(\alpha+p^{2}-y)-\sigma_{M+1}\right]}\langle-q^{A+2p^{2}};q^{y}\rangle_{\infty}.

One may then verify—using a symbolic calculator if desired—that indeed

(3.6)

\frac{A}{3}=\xi+\left[N(\beta-y)-\sigma_{N}\right]+\left[(M+1)(\alpha+p^{2}-y)-\sigma_{M+1}\right],

as desired, and then make an analogous verification for
B/3
. Thus, with the equality of (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) we deduce that

\sum_{t=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{pt+r}q^{pt+r}=q^{\gamma}Q\mspace{-1.5mu}\big(q^{(\alpha-p^{2})/3},q^{p^{2}}\big)Q\mspace{-1.5mu}\big(q^{(2p^{2}-\beta)/3},q^{p^{2}}\big),

and, repeating this for
0\leq r\leq p-1
, we establish part
(1)
of the theorem.

For the case
m\equiv 2\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)
, we find that

\frac{2p^{2}-\alpha}{3}=-bp-kmp+\frac{(p+m+n)p}{6}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\beta-p^{2}}{3}=knp+\frac{(p+m-n)p}{6}

are integral, so that letting

A=2p^{2}-\alpha\qquad\text{and}\qquad B=\beta-p^{2},

one may similarly directly verify that (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) are equal, and thereby establish part
(2)
of the theorem.
∎

6.
Concluding remarks

This paper was motivated by the experimental discovery of vanishing coefficients in products
Q(q^{r},q^{p})Q(q^{s},Q^{p})
with
r,s>0
and
p
a prime equivalent to 1 modulo 4. Further experiment suggests that some products
Q(q^{r},q^{p})Q(q^{s},Q^{p})Q(q^{t},q^{p})
also exhibit vanishing coefficients in progressions modulo
p
; akin to the example in the introduction, for the sequence
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
defined via

Q(q,q^{13})Q(q^{3},q^{13})Q(q^{4},q^{13})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}q^{k},

one has

a_{13k+2}=a_{13k+4}=a_{13k+10}=0\qquad\text{for all $k$}.

Furthermore, it appears that in the case of products
Q(q^{i},q^{p})Q(q^{j},q^{p})Q(q^{k},q^{p})
, this phenomenon of vanishing coefficients is not restricted only those
p\equiv 1\allowbreak\mkern 6.0mu({\operator@font mod}\,\,4)
; indeed, experiment also suggests that if

Q(q^{2},q^{19})Q(q^{3},q^{19})Q(q^{5},q^{19})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}q^{k},

then

b_{19k+4}=b_{19k+5}=b_{19k+16}=0\qquad\text{for all $k$}.

We leave it to the reader to spot a possibly required condition, suggested by the two examples, on the
i
,
j
and
k
in
Q(q^{i},q^{p})Q(q^{j},q^{p})Q(q^{k},q^{p})
for vanishing to occur.
