Probable respiratory sarcopenia trajectories and the risk of incident chronic diseases: findings from the china health and retirement longitudinal study
Authors: Jianhua Liang, Mengqing Yang, Siyun Tan, Ronggeng Li, Lijing Xu, Zexian Shi, Youwei Zhou, Junyang Liao, Jieyun Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the acceleration of population aging, the coexistence of multiple chronic diseases has become a major public health concern among older adults. Respiratory sarcopenia (RS) reflects dual impairments in systemic and respiratory muscle function; however, longitudinal evidence regarding its association with multisystem chronic diseases remains limited. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the prospective associations between longitudinal trajectories of an epidemiologic surrogate phenotype for probable respiratory sarcopenia (probable RS) and the risk of multiple incident chronic diseases.MethodsThis study was based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study cohort and included 4,784 middle-aged and older participants with follow-up data. Probable RS was defined using peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) and appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM). Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was used to identify joint longitudinal trajectories of PEFR and ASM. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were subsequently applied to evaluate the associations between these trajectories and incident chronic diseases. The Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method was used for multiple-comparison correction.ResultsThe GBTM identified three stable longitudinal trajectory groups: the dual high/normal trajectory group (29.8%), the single impaired trajectory group (47.6%), and the dual low trajectory group (22.6%). After multivariable Cox regression analysis with FDR correction, compared with the dual high/normal trajectory group, participants in the dual low trajectory group with persistently low PEFR and low ASM had significantly increased risks of incident arthritis (HR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.54–2.47), asthma (HR = 4.31, 95% CI: 2.11–8.81), chronic lung disease (HR = 2.27, 95% CI: 1.63–3.17), and hypertension (HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.21–2.00) during follow-up (all FDR-P < 0.05). Sensitivity analyses further confirmed a significant synergistic amplification effect of concurrent low PEFR and low ASM on disease risk.ConclusionsDual longitudinal declines in skeletal muscle and respiratory function were prospectively associated with increased risks of specific chronic diseases among middle-aged and older adults. These trajectories of this surrogate phenotype may serve as potential phenotypic markers for multimorbidity risk stratification. Future community-based prospective intervention trials may be warranted to determine whether targeted improvements in respiratory and skeletal muscle function can effectively prevent chronic diseases.Keywords: Probable respiratory sarcopenia, Longitudinal trajectories, Chronic disease, CHARLS cohort

Introduction

With the acceleration of global population aging, the prevalence of chronic diseases among older adults continues to increase and has become a major public health concern. A systematic review reported that nearly half of older adults worldwide suffer from at least two chronic diseases, and this proportion increases significantly with advancing age [1]. The coexistence of multiple chronic diseases not only substantially increases healthcare utilization and economic burden, but is also associated with adverse outcomes such as functional decline, increased disability risk, and reduced quality of life, particularly among older adults [2]. Therefore, identifying early risk factors that can predict the occurrence and progression of chronic diseases is of considerable clinical and public health importance for the prevention, management, and optimization of healthcare services in aging populations.

Sarcopenia is an age-related degenerative musculoskeletal disorder characterized by progressive loss of muscle mass, reduced muscle strength, and impaired physical function [3]. The prognosis of sarcopenia is associated with loss of independence resulting from physical dysfunction and mobility impairment [[4], [5], [6]]. In recent years, respiratory sarcopenia (RS), as a specific manifestation of sarcopenia involving the respiratory system, has gradually attracted increasing attention. RS was first proposed by Kera et al. [7]. Subsequently, the Japanese Working Group on Respiratory Sarcopenia (JWGRS) further established a diagnostic framework for RS, emphasizing that the diagnosis should be based on systemic sarcopenia combined with reduced respiratory muscle mass, decreased respiratory muscle strength, and/or impaired respiratory function [8]. However, in large population-based cohort studies, direct measurements of respiratory muscle mass and respiratory muscle strength, such as maximal inspiratory pressure and maximal expiratory pressure, are often unavailable. Therefore, some epidemiological studies have adopted peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) combined with low muscle mass as an alternative operational definition of Probable respiratory sarcopenia (probable RS) to identify individuals with reduced respiratory function and low muscle mass [9,10]. Because neither PEFR nor anthropometrically estimated appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) directly assesses respiratory muscle strength or respiratory muscle mass, the phenotype examined in this study should be explicitly framed as an epidemiologic surrogate construct rather than a guideline-defined clinical diagnosis of RS. It should be noted that PEFR is influenced not only by respiratory muscle function, but also by airway obstruction, lung compliance, and underlying respiratory diseases; therefore, PEFR cannot be considered entirely equivalent to respiratory muscle function itself [11]. In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that reduced respiratory function, low muscle mass, inflammatory status, malnutrition, and low physical activity commonly coexist in older adults and may share overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms with multiple chronic diseases [12].

Nevertheless, current research on probable RS remains relatively limited. Existing evidence has primarily focused on mortality and specific disease outcomes [9,13], whereas its association with the risk of multiple incident chronic diseases has not been systematically evaluated. Moreover, most previous studies assessed probable RS status at a single time point, and few studies have investigated the relationship between longitudinal changes in probable RS and the risk of incident chronic diseases. Considering that probable RS status defined using PEFR and muscle mass may change over time, and that such changes may reflect both genuine physiological alterations and fluctuations in health status or measurement error, further investigation into the associations between different trajectory patterns of probable RS and chronic disease risk is warranted. Therefore, based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) cohort, this study used an operational definition based on PEFR and low appendicular skeletal muscle mass to investigate the associations between longitudinal trajectories of probable RS and the risk of multiple incident chronic diseases.

Methods

Study design and population

The data used in this study were derived from CHARLS. CHARLS is a nationally representative cohort study conducted by Peking University that covers 28 provinces across China. Using a multistage stratified sampling design, CHARLS systematically collects information on demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, health conditions, healthcare utilization, lifestyle factors, and selected physical examination and laboratory indicators [14], providing both national representativeness and international comparability. The flowchart illustrates the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the present study. A total of 17,708 participants from the 2011–2012 CHARLS survey were initially included. Participants were sequentially excluded if they had missing probable RS component indicators in 2011, 2013, or 2015, missing covariate information, age <45 years, or extreme BMI values. Extreme BMI values were defined as the upper and lower 1% of the distribution (BMI <16.29 or >33.41 kg/m2) to minimize the potential influence of severe cachexia or extreme obesity on muscle mass estimation formulas. Ultimately, 4,784 participants with continuous probable RS measurements were included for analysis of the associations between probable RS trajectories and the risk of multiple incident chronic diseases (Fig. 1). The CHARLS project was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and the study design complied with the ethical principles of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after a full explanation of the study procedures.

Flowchart of the sample selection process.

Assessment of probable RS

This study referenced the conceptual framework proposed by JWGRS and previous epidemiological studies based on CHARLS [8], and defined probable RS as reduced respiratory function combined with low ASM. Respiratory muscle function was assessed using PEFR. In CHARLS, PEFR was measured three times, and the highest value among the three recordings was selected as the final PEFR value. Standardized assessment was performed using the percentage of predicted PEFR (% predicted), calculated as (measured PEFR / predicted PEFR) × 100%. A threshold of <80% was defined as an indicative marker for low respiratory muscle strength. The predictive equations were as follows: for men, PEFR (L/s) = 21.566 + 0.078 × H - 6.192 × ln(A); for women, PEFR (L/s) = −22.741 + 6.901 × ln(H) − 0.090 × A, where A represents age (years) and H represents height (cm) [15]. These equations have been validated in Chinese populations and are highly compatible with our study cohort [16]. Because muscle mass was not directly measured in CHARLS, ASM was estimated using an anthropometric regression equation that has been validated in multiple Chinese population studies [10,17]. This method has shown good agreement with DXA. The specific formula was as follows: ASM = 0.193 × weight (kg) + 0.107 × height (cm) − 4.157 × sex − 0.037 × age (years) − 2.631, where men were coded as 1 and women as 2. To reduce the influence of individual body size differences on muscle mass assessment, the muscle mass index corrected for height squared (ASM, kg/m2) was further calculated. According to the 2019 AWGS diagnostic criteria, low ASM was defined as <7.0 kg/m2 for men and <5.4 kg/m2 for women.

Covariates

This study considered multiple demographic, lifestyle, and clinical variables. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, educational level (middle school or below, high school or above), and marital status (married/separated or unmarried/divorced/widowed). Lifestyle factors included retirement status, smoking status (current smoking, never smoking, smoking cessation), smoking quantity (cigarettes/day), alcohol consumption status, and nighttime sleep duration. Physical measurements included BMI (continuous variable) and waist circumference. Laboratory indicators included LDL, CRP, and serum creatinine.

Assessment of chronic diseases

Chronic diseases were identified using the self-reported question: “Have you ever been diagnosed with [chronic disease] by a doctor?” Disease status for each participant included 11 noncommunicable chronic diseases: hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cancer (excluding minor skin cancer), chronic lung disease, liver disease (excluding fatty liver, tumors, and cancer), heart disease (including heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems), digestive diseases (excluding tumors or cancer), memory-related disorders, arthritis or rheumatism, and asthma.

Statistical analysis

To characterize longitudinal patterns of decline in respiratory function and skeletal muscle mass over time, GBTM was performed using the lcmm package in R. Survey years (2011, 2013, and 2015) were used as the time scale. Independent trajectory models for percentage predicted PEFR and ASM during follow-up were first constructed separately. The optimal number of latent classes and trajectory shapes were determined through a rigorous stepwise approach. Models containing 1–5 classes were iteratively fitted, and the final model selection comprehensively considered statistical goodness-of-fit indices and clinical interpretability according to the following predefined criteria: (1) lower BIC and AIC values; (2) APP > 0.70 for each class; (3) each trajectory class accounting for at least 5% of the total sample; and (4) OCC > 5. After determining the optimal univariable trajectory models for PEFR and ASM, participants were cross-classified according to the maximum posterior probability of assignment to each trajectory category. This consolidation strategy was justified by similarities in epidemiologic patterns of probable RS, empirical risk gradients, biological plausibility, and clinical interpretability. It should be emphasized that the trajectory categories reflected long-term change patterns rather than clinical diagnoses themselves. Considering the baseline sex-related differences in ASM, ASM was standardized using sex-specific Z-scores to eliminate the influence of sex on raw values. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. For between-group comparisons, continuous variables were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test, and categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test. For missing covariate data, complete-case analysis was performed by excluding records with missing values and retaining only participants with complete information for modeling. Cox regression models were first used to evaluate the associations between trajectories and incident chronic diseases, and HRs with 95% CIs were calculated. To control for inflation of type I error caused by multiple comparisons, all Cox regression models were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method. Using the healthy trajectory group as the reference, three models were sequentially constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, and BMI based on Model 1; and Model 3 was further adjusted for smoking quantity, smoking status, alcohol consumption, educational level, retirement status, marital status, LDL, CRP, sleep duration, creatinine, and waist circumference based on Model 2. Subgroup analyses and forest plots were subsequently performed to assess consistency across different populations. Considering the potential pathophysiological heterogeneity within the single impaired trajectory group, namely that isolated respiratory dysfunction and isolated muscle mass reduction may involve different underlying mechanisms, sensitivity analyses were conducted. Specifically, the single impaired trajectory group was further divided into an isolated low PEFR trajectory group and an isolated low ASM trajectory group, thereby constructing a new exposure variable consisting of four categories: dual high/normal trajectory group, isolated low PEFR trajectory group, isolated low ASM trajectory group, and dual low trajectory group. Based on this classification, fully adjusted multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models (Model 3) were rerun for diseases that showed significant positive associations in the primary analyses to validate the rationality of the grouping strategy and explore the independent effects of single-system impairment. In addition, considering that COVID-19 may have affected respiratory function and skeletal muscle systems in middle-aged and older adults, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed by advancing the follow-up endpoint to 2018 and evaluating incident cases occurring between 2015 and 2018. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2, and two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 4,784 participants were included in this study, including 373 participants in the probable RS group and 4,411 participants in the non-probable RS group (Table S1). Regarding sociodemographic characteristics, compared with the non-RS group, participants in the probable RS group were significantly older and had significantly higher proportions of individuals with a middle school education or below and those who were divorced, widowed, or unmarried (all P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in sex distribution or retirement status between the two groups. In terms of anthropometric measurements and lifestyle factors, the probable RS group had significantly lower BMI and waist circumference than the non-RS group (P < 0.001), and a higher proportion of current smokers (P = 0.030). No statistically significant differences were observed in nighttime sleep duration, alcohol consumption status, or daily cigarette consumption between the two groups. Regarding clinical indicators and comorbidity distributions, the probable RS group showed significantly lower PEFR and LDL levels (P < 0.001). In addition, the prevalence rates of chronic lung disease, asthma, and memory-related disorders were significantly higher in the probable RS group than in the non-RS group (P < 0.05), whereas the prevalence rates of hypertension and dyslipidemia were relatively lower (P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in the prevalence of other comorbidities, including diabetes, heart disease, and arthritis, between the two groups.

Dual-trajectory characteristics of ASM and PEFR

First, GBTM was used to identify the longitudinal trajectory patterns of PEFR and ASM from 2011 to 2015 (Fig. 2, Fig. S1). The PEFR trajectory model fitting results are shown in Table S2. The results indicated that the two-class model achieved the optimal balance between statistical performance (BIC = 38544.2) and model stability (all AvePP >0.86 and OCC > 6.5). These two trajectories were defined as follows: the high PEFR trajectory group (58.9%), characterized by relatively high baseline levels that remained stable over time, and the low PEFR trajectory group (41.1%), characterized by relatively low baseline levels with an evident declining trend. For ASM trajectories, the three-class model was identified as the optimal fitting model (BIC = 23992.4). This model clearly identified three trajectories with different baseline levels, all showing gradual declines over time: the high ASM trajectory group (30.1%), the middle ASM trajectory group (47.9%), and the low ASM trajectory group (22.0%). All trajectory classes demonstrated AvePP >0.81, sample proportions >5%, and OCC > 5.0, satisfying the predefined rigorous selection criteria. By cross-classifying the PEFR trajectories (2 classes) and ASM trajectories (3 classes), six potential combinations were initially generated. To construct a classification system with greater clinical applicability and statistical power, these six combinations were merged into the following three subgroups based on the actual prevalence distribution of probable RS and the risk characteristics of each indicator (Table S3). The Dual High/Normal reference group (n = 1424) combined the “high ASM + high PEFR” and “middle ASM + high PEFR” categories. The data showed that as long as participants maintained relatively preserved respiratory function (high PEFR), the prevalence of probable RS remained extremely low regardless of whether muscle mass was at a high or moderate level (prevalence only 0.1%–0.2%). These two groups demonstrated substantial homogeneity in health outcomes and were therefore merged as the reference group. The Single Impairment group (n = 2278) included the remaining four intermediate combinations, such as isolated low PEFR or isolated extremely low ASM. This population showed a modest increase in the prevalence of probable RS (approximately 5.0 %–7.0 %), reflecting a pathological transitional stage from a healthy state toward typical respiratory sarcopenia. The Dual Low group (n = 1082) consisted of participants with both low ASM and low PEFR. This group represented the core high-risk population of primary interest in the present study, characterized by persistently low levels of both ASM and PEFR below clinical threshold values. The data confirmed that the prevalence of probable RS in this group was markedly elevated, reaching 58.7%–60.8%. The independent classification of this subgroup may represent a high-risk longitudinal phenotype characterized by synergistic declines in respiratory and muscular function. Ultimately, the three final subgroups demonstrated a clear risk gradient. The Dual High/Normal group represented a healthy reference state with preserved respiratory and muscular function; the Single Impairment group reflected early physiological impairment; and the Dual Low group exhibited pronounced susceptibility to respiratory sarcopenia.

Baseline characteristics of the dual trajectories of ASM and PEFR

A total of 4,784 participants were included in this study and were classified according to the trajectory model into the Dual High/Normal group (n = 1424, 29.8%), the Single Impairment group (n = 2278, 47.6%), and the Dual Low group (n = 1082, 22.6%) (Table 1). Compared with the Dual High/Normal group, participants in the Dual Low group were older (62.39 vs. 57.33 years), had a higher proportion of individuals with a middle school education or below (95.5% vs. 85.7%), and were more likely to be unmarried, divorced, or widowed (14.8% vs. 6.4%) (all P < 0.001). Regarding lifestyle factors, the Dual Low group exhibited the greatest smoking exposure, with the highest proportion of current smokers (39.0%) and the highest daily cigarette consumption among the three groups (P = 0.002). In terms of body composition, the Dual Low group had significantly lower BMI (20.06 vs. 25.27 kg/m²) and waist circumference (76.21 vs. 88.48 cm) than the Dual High/Normal group (both P < 0.001). Regarding baseline comorbidities, the Dual High/Normal group had a greater burden of cardiometabolic diseases, including hypertension (27.5%), dyslipidemia (13.2%), and diabetes (7.4%). In contrast, the Dual Low group had the lowest prevalence of metabolic diseases but showed higher prevalence rates of respiratory and frailty-related conditions, including chronic lung disease (13.0% vs. 3.9%), asthma (6.9% vs. 1.6%), digestive diseases (27.4% vs. 21.4%), and arthritis (36.2% vs. 31.3%) (all P < 0.05).

Cox regression analysis of dual trajectories of ASM and PEFR with multiple chronic diseases

In the unadjusted model (Model 1), compared with the Dual High/Normal reference group, the Single Impairment group showed significantly increased risks of arthritis (HR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.17–1.86, P < 0.001), asthma (HR = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.56–3.85, P < 0.001), and chronic lung disease (HR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.21–1.92, P < 0.001). The risks were further increased in the Dual Low group (arthritis: HR = 1.66; asthma: HR = 3.55; chronic lung disease: HR = 1.95; all P < 0.001). No significant associations were observed for the remaining outcomes. In Model 2, after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI, the above associations remained largely stable. Both the Single Impairment group and the Dual Low group continued to show elevated risks of arthritis, asthma, and chronic lung disease (all P < 0.001), and the risk of hypertension in the Dual Low group also reached statistical significance (HR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.24–2.04, P < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), the results remained highly consistent. The Single Impairment group continued to show increased risks of arthritis (HR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.22–1.96), asthma (HR = 2.58, 95% CI: 1.63–4.09), and chronic lung disease (HR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.30–2.09) (all P < 0.001). The risks were further elevated in the Dual Low group (arthritis: HR = 1.95; asthma: HR = 4.31; chronic lung disease: HR = 2.27; all P < 0.001), and the association with hypertension remained significant (HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.21–2.00, P < 0.001), with all FDR-P < 0.05. No stable statistically significant associations were observed for the remaining diseases (Fig. 3A–C).

Subgroup analysis

Overall, the increased risks of disease incidence associated with the Dual Low and Single Impairment trajectory groups showed high consistency across most demographic and lifestyle subgroups. Notably, except for a few stratified analyses, no significant interactions were observed between subgroup variables and trajectory classifications (most P for interaction >0.05), suggesting that the adverse effects of respiratory sarcopenia on these four diseases were largely independent of traditional risk factors (Fig. S2A–D).

Sensitivity analysis

To minimize the potential confounding effect of the COVID-19 pandemic, sensitivity analyses were performed by advancing the follow-up endpoint to 2018 (Fig. S3). The results demonstrated that the primary findings remained robust and highly significant after FDR correction during the shortened 3-year natural follow-up period. Compared with the Dual High/Normal trajectory group, the Dual Low trajectory group remained significantly associated with increased risks of asthma (HR = 3.45, 95% CI: 1.56–7.62), chronic lung disease (HR = 2.42, 95% CI: 1.66–3.52), arthritis (HR = 1.90, 95% CI: 1.26–2.85), and hypertension (HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.15–2.11) (all FDR-P < 0.05). In addition, the Single Impairment group continued to show significantly elevated risks of arthritis, asthma, and chronic lung disease.

To further explore the potential heterogeneity within the Single Impairment group, sensitivity analyses were performed by separating this group into isolated low PEFR and isolated low ASM trajectory groups (Table S4). The results showed that although the effects of single-system impairment differed across diseases, with respiratory disease risk mainly driven by isolated low PEFR and the isolated low ASM group even showing a relatively lower risk of hypertension, the Dual Low trajectory group consistently exhibited the highest disease risk. When low pulmonary function and low muscle mass coexisted, the risks of all aforementioned diseases demonstrated substantial synergistic amplification or reversal increases (arthritis: HR = 2.03; asthma: HR = 3.52; chronic lung disease: HR = 2.12; hypertension: HR = 1.34; all P < 0.05).

Discussion

In the present study, we combined GBTM with prospective Cox regression analyses to systematically evaluate the associations between longitudinal trajectory patterns of the epidemiologic surrogate phenotype for probable RS and the risk of multiple incident chronic diseases. The results showed that compared with the Dual High/Normal trajectory group, which maintained relatively preserved ASM and PEFR levels, participants in the Dual Low trajectory group with persistently low ASM and low PEFR exhibited significantly increased risks of asthma, chronic lung disease, arthritis, and hypertension during follow-up, and these associations remained robust after FDR correction. Meanwhile, the Single Impairment trajectory group also demonstrated modestly elevated risks. These findings suggest that persistent synergistic declines in respiratory function and skeletal muscle function may be associated with an increased risk of multiple chronic diseases.

Notably, this study observed strong associations between probable RS trajectories and respiratory diseases. Because reduced PEFR reflects not only impaired respiratory function but may also be influenced by underlying airflow limitation and undiagnosed airway diseases, this association may partially reflect subclinical respiratory abnormalities already present at baseline. Nevertheless, further sensitivity analyses demonstrated that compared with the isolated low PEFR trajectory group, the Dual Low trajectory group with concomitant low ASM exhibited substantially higher risks of asthma and chronic lung disease. These findings suggest that the long-term coexistence of low pulmonary function and low muscle mass may not only represent localized respiratory dysfunction, but may also reflect a systemic vulnerability phenotype characterized by generalized muscle loss, chronic inflammation, malnutrition, and reduced physiological reserve [12,18,19]. In other words, the Dual Low trajectory identified in this study may represent not merely a surrogate marker of airway dysfunction, but rather an integrated risk signal reflecting the concurrent progression of respiratory decline and systemic frailty [20].

The long-term synergistic decline in respiratory and skeletal muscle function may further contribute to the development of multiple diseases associated with metabolic dysregulation, chronic inflammation, and reduced physiological reserve. In addition to respiratory diseases, this study also observed associations between the Dual Low trajectory and increased risks of hypertension and arthritis. These findings suggest that the long-term respiratory-muscular functional decline reflected by probable RS may not be limited to localized organ dysfunction, but may also involve broader systemic metabolic and inflammatory disturbances. Regarding hypertension, skeletal muscle serves as a major peripheral tissue for glucose uptake and metabolism, and reduced muscle mass has been closely associated with insulin resistance [21]. Previous studies have suggested that insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinemia accompanying sarcopenia may further promote renal sodium retention, activate the renin-angiotensin system (RAS), and induce vascular endothelial dysfunction, thereby contributing to the development and progression of hypertension [[22], [23], [24]]. Meanwhile, chronic low-grade inflammation, persistent elevation of proinflammatory cytokines, insulin resistance, and impaired muscle protein synthesis may also simultaneously participate in the development of sarcopenia and degenerative joint changes [[25], [26], [27]]. In addition, further subgroup analyses demonstrated that the associations between the Dual Low trajectory and increased risks of arthritis, hypertension, and respiratory diseases remained significant even among participants with BMI > 22 kg/m2. These findings suggest that the relationship between concurrent declines in respiratory and skeletal muscle function and chronic disease risk may not be entirely dependent on low body weight or cachectic status, but may instead reflect a systemic vulnerability state characterized by generalized inflammatory activation, metabolic abnormalities, and reduced physiological reserve. Notably, this study did not observe stable associations between probable RS trajectories and incident dyslipidemia. Meanwhile, the Dual Low trajectory group exhibited lower LDL levels and a lower prevalence of dyslipidemia at baseline. Previous studies have proposed that in frailty, chronic inflammatory states, or muscle wasting conditions, lower lipid levels may sometimes reflect malnutrition, disease-related lipid depletion, or reduced overall health reserve rather than traditional metabolic health, a phenomenon referred to as the “lipid paradox” [28]. Therefore, the absence of a significant increase in dyslipidemia risk observed in this study may be related to these complex metabolic backgrounds.

RS may not simply reflect impaired pulmonary function, but may instead represent a systemic degenerative phenotype characterized by concurrent declines in global skeletal muscle mass and respiratory muscle function. Basic and clinical studies have demonstrated that sarcopenia is closely associated with age-related chronic low-grade inflammation, increased oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inadequate nutritional intake, and reduced physical activity, all of which are also widely involved in the development and progression of multiple chronic diseases [29]. Persistent inflammatory responses and oxidative stress may accelerate the decline in skeletal muscle mass and strength, including respiratory muscles, by activating proteolytic pathways, suppressing muscle anabolic signaling, and impairing mitochondrial energy metabolism. This process has been validated in multiple studies investigating muscle wasting associated with chronic diseases [30,31]. Meanwhile, sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity are closely associated with insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and endocrine-metabolic dysregulation, thereby potentially contributing to the development and progression of various chronic diseases [32]. Therefore, a bidirectional relationship may exist between RS and chronic diseases: chronic diseases may promote muscle wasting through inflammatory and metabolic disturbances, whereas the insufficient respiratory and systemic muscle reserve reflected by RS may reduce physiological reserve and resilience to disease, thereby increasing the risk of multimorbidity [33]. In addition, our findings should be interpreted in the context of the recently updated AWGS 2025 consensus [34]. The AWGS 2025 framework further emphasizes early identification, prevention, and lifelong muscle health, while simplifying diagnostic pathways for sarcopenia assessment by extending attention to middle-aged populations. Although the phenotype examined in the present study should be regarded as an epidemiologic surrogate construct rather than a guideline-defined clinical diagnosis of RS, its focus on the coexistence of impaired respiratory function, as reflected by PEFR, and low muscle mass, as reflected by estimated ASM, is conceptually aligned with the evolving AWGS 2025 paradigm, which encourages earlier recognition of concurrent functional and structural decline before overt disability develops.

Given the observational nature of this study, the findings primarily provide epidemiological evidence for understanding the potential associations between probable RS and multiple chronic diseases, while also offering references for future mechanistic and prospective interventional studies. The longitudinal trajectory patterns of probable RS constructed using PEFR and muscle mass may reflect an underlying state of physiological vulnerability. Previous studies have suggested that RS and its associated respiratory dysfunction may not represent irreversible conditions. Respiratory muscle training, including inspiratory and expiratory muscle training, has been shown to improve dyspnea severity, 6-minute walk distance, and maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures, with beneficial effects observed in both chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and older populations [[35], [36], [37]]. In addition, lower-limb resistance training and whole-body resistance training can effectively increase muscle mass and strength, improve exercise performance, and in some studies, improve respiratory functional parameters such as peak expiratory flow [38,39]. Combined aerobic and resistance exercise has also been demonstrated to improve exercise tolerance and muscle structure, with potential benefits in patients with chronic respiratory diseases and lung transplant candidates [40]. Regarding nutritional interventions, studies have shown that nutritional supplementation may improve lean body mass, exercise capacity, and respiratory muscle strength, and may exert synergistic effects in comprehensive rehabilitation programs [41,42]. However, most current exercise and nutritional intervention studies have been conducted in highly controlled clinical settings and specific study populations, and their applicability to middle-aged and older adults in rural Chinese communities remains uncertain. Therefore, before incorporating probable RS into routine public health screening or intervention strategies, prospective intervention studies targeting this population are still needed to further clarify its long-term health implications and determine whether improvement in probable RS trajectories is associated with reduced chronic disease risk.

This study has several strengths. First, based on CHARLS, a nationally representative large-scale cohort, this study included a relatively large sample size with comprehensive follow-up information, thereby enhancing the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Second, by combining GBTM with prospective Cox regression analyses, this study characterized dynamic longitudinal changes in respiratory and muscular function, which may reduce random errors associated with single measurements and more reasonably establish the temporal sequence between exposures and outcomes. In addition, strict FDR correction, subgroup analyses, and multiple sensitivity analyses were further conducted, thereby enhancing the robustness of the findings.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, due to limitations of the CHARLS database, this study lacked direct measurements of respiratory muscle strength, such as maximal inspiratory pressure and maximal expiratory pressure, as well as objective muscle mass assessment methods such as DXA or bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). Therefore, only an operational definition of probable RS could be adopted, which is more appropriately framed as an epidemiologic surrogate construct rather than a definitive clinical diagnosis. At the same time, anthropometric estimation of ASM has inherent structural limitations. Because body weight is mathematically embedded in the ASM estimation equation, individuals with lower BMI are structurally more likely to be classified as having low muscle mass. Although baseline BMI was adjusted for in the statistical models, potential structural bias arising from shared anthropometric components cannot be fully eliminated. Because the ASM estimation equation incorporates body weight, while BMI was also included as a covariate in the models, the exposure definition and covariate structure are intrinsically intertwined. Therefore, we emphasize that residual structural confounding cannot be completely excluded, and the corresponding estimates of independent effects should be interpreted cautiously in light of this methodological limitation. In addition, although GBTM provided a robust longitudinal framework, the final trajectory grouping strategy was partly investigator-guided, with latent classes consolidated according to prespecified epidemiologic considerations and clinical interpretability. Although this approach was intended to improve statistical stability and facilitate meaningful interpretation, a certain degree of subjectivity was unavoidable. Therefore, the reproducibility of this classification strategy and its generalizability to other populations warrant further external validation. Second, chronic disease outcomes were mainly based on self-reported physician diagnoses, which may still be subject to misclassification and recall bias. Third, although multiple covariates were included and smoking-related variables were further refined, including daily smoking quantity and smoking cessation history, residual confounding could not be completely excluded. For example, the lack of quantitative assessment of passive tobacco exposure may have partially influenced the interpretation of respiratory disease-related findings. In addition, this study lacked information on several important potential confounders, including statin-related muscle adverse effects, nutritional intake status such as protein intake, socioeconomic deprivation, and healthcare accessibility, all of which may influence metabolic and chronic disease outcomes. Finally, although longitudinal analyses partially strengthened temporal inference, the observational nature of this study precludes definitive causal conclusions, and the potential influence of subclinical disease and reverse causality cannot be completely excluded.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that among middle-aged and older adults, the longitudinal Dual Low trajectory of the surrogate phenotype for probable RS, characterized by persistently low PEFR combined with low ASM, was prospectively associated with increased risks of multiple chronic diseases, including asthma, chronic lung disease, arthritis, and hypertension. These findings suggest that the longitudinal trajectory patterns of this epidemiologic surrogate construct may reflect a systemic physiologically vulnerable state accompanied by persistent declines in respiratory and skeletal muscle function, and may serve as a potential functional phenotype for identifying individuals at high risk of multimorbidity. Given the observational nature of this study, and the potential influence of residual confounding and reverse causality, the current findings primarily provide epidemiological evidence for mechanistic exploration and hypothesis generation. Future well-designed prospective interventional studies in community populations are still needed to further determine whether improving respiratory and skeletal muscle function is associated with reduced risks of chronic diseases.
