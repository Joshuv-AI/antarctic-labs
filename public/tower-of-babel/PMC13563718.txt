Cultural adaptation and psychometric evaluation of the Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Questionnaire- Revised 2 (PRAQ-R2) in the Sri Lankan setting
Authors: Ahamad Salman Amathullah, Mohamed Rishard, Kanthi Hettigoda, Millawage Supun Dilara Wijesinghe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnancy-Related Anxiety (PRA) is a significant yet often overlooked mental health issue. The absence of validated tools for assessing PRA in multilingual contexts hinders the provision of equitable care. This study aimed to fill this gap by culturally adapting and validating the Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Questionnaire-Revised 2 (PRAQ-R2) for Sinhala and Tamil populations.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study involving 200 pregnant women, with 100 participants in each language group. These women attended antenatal clinics at two tertiary care hospitals in the Western Province of Sri Lanka between 2024 and 2025. Participants were selected using convenience sampling, excluding those with diagnosed psychiatric disorders. The PRAQ-R2 was translated following WHO guidelines, including forward and back-translation, expert panel review by clinical psychologists, obstetricians, and midwives, and cognitive pretesting. We used exploratory factor analysis to validate the tool. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha.ResultsThe exploratory factor analysis of both translated versions confirmed the original three-factor structure of the PRAQ-R2, comprising fear of giving birth, worries about bearing a child with a disability, and concerns about physical appearance. All items showed acceptable factor loadings. Internal consistency was good for both versions. The Sinhala version demonstrated subscale Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.813, 0.922, and 0.733, with an overall alpha of 0.86, while the Tamil version showed corresponding values of 0.705, 0.879, and 0.733, with an overall alpha of 0.81.ConclusionSinhala and Tamil PRAQ-R2 are valid and reliable tools for assessing PRA in Sri Lanka.Keywords: Anxiety, Pregnancy, Psychometrics, Surveys and Questionnaires, Sri Lanka

Background

Pregnancy is a transformative period characterised by profound physical and psychological changes that heighten vulnerability to mental health challenges [1–3]. Pregnancy-Related Anxiety (PRA), a distinct condition characterised by fear of childbirth, foetal abnormalities, and maternal incompetence, has emerged as a critical concern [4]. Unlike generalised anxiety, PRA is uniquely linked to adverse maternal outcomes, including breastfeeding difficulties, postpartum depression, and impaired maternal-child attachment [5], as well as neonatal risks, such as preterm birth, low birth weight, and developmental disorders [6]. The urgency of addressing PRA is emphasised by its pervasive yet underrecognised impact, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where sociocultural factors, such as stigma and familial expectations, may amplify distress [7, 8]. Despite its clinical significance, targeted screening is hindered by the lack of culturally adapted tools, which limits intervention efficacy in diverse settings [9].

Advances in the understanding of PRA have driven the development of specialised assessment instruments. Systematic reviews have highlighted that self-reported measures, such as the Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Questionnaire (PRAQ) and its revisions (PRAQ-R and PRAQ-R2), outperform diagnostic interviews in capturing perinatal anxiety [10, 11]. The PRAQ-R2, validated in European and Middle Eastern populations [12–14], assesses three core domains: fear of childbirth, foetal health concerns, and anxiety regarding physical changes [4]. Although its psychometric robustness has been established in high-resource settings, critical gaps persist in LMICs, where linguistic diversity and cultural norms necessitate contextual adaptation [15, 16]. For instance, in Sri Lanka, a multilingual nation with Sinhala- and Tamil-speaking populations, no validated tool exists to screen for PRA in native languages, despite evidence that culturally incongruent instruments risk misclassification and impede care [9, 17].

This gap highlights the need to adapt and validate PRA measures in LMIC contexts. Prior studies have emphasised that anxiety manifestations are deeply influenced by cultural perceptions of motherhood, family dynamics, and healthcare access [8, 16]. However, existing tools rarely account for these nuances. In Sri Lanka, where maternal mental health services are expanding, linguistically accessible PRAQ-R2 could enhance screening accuracy, inform policies, and facilitate timely interventions. For this purpose, researchers chose PRAQ-R2, which was published by Huizink et al. [15]. Therefore, this study aimed to translate PRAQ-R2 into Sinhala and Tamil and validate its psychometric properties in pregnant women in Sri Lanka. By addressing this methodological void, our findings will advance equitable mental health support and provide a framework for cross-cultural adaptation to similar settings in the future.

Methods

Study design and setting

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted between 2024 and 2025 at the antenatal clinics of the De Soysa Hospital for Women (DSHW) and Castle Street Hospital for Women (CSHW) in Sri Lanka’s Western Province. The DSHW and CSHW are two large tertiary referral centres for specialised obstetric and gynaecological care in Sri Lanka. These hospitals provide care for patients from the city of Colombo and its suburbs, referrals from secondary centres, and patients who choose one of these two hospitals as their referral centre. They serve ethnically and socioeconomically diverse populations and provide a representative sample of pregnant women from Sinhalese, Tamil, and English-speaking communities. Pregnant women attending DSHW and CSHW clinics who were able to read and understand the Sinhala or Tamil language sufficiently were eligible. The exclusion criteria were a diagnosed psychological or psychiatric disorder (self-reported or documented in medical records) and an inability to read Sinhala and Tamil.

Sample size and sampling strategy

A patient-to-variable ratio of 10:1 [18] was used to determine the sample size for the exploratory factor analyses. For the 10-item PRAQ-R2, a minimum of 100 participants per language (Sinhala and Tamil) was required. Owing to feasibility constraints, convenience sampling was employed, and recruitment continued until the target sample size of 100 participants per language was reached.

Data collection procedure

Pregnant women attending the antenatal clinics of DSHW and CSHW were approached by the principal investigator during routine clinic visits. Eligible participants were informed about the objectives and procedures of the study and that participation was voluntary. No eligible participants declined to participate. Those who met the inclusion criteria and were invited agreed to participate, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. The PRAQ-R2 was self-administered. Questionnaire completion required approximately 5–10 min. All responses were collected anonymously and handled confidentially.

Study instrument

The PRAQ-R2 comprises 10 Likert-scale items (1 = “absolutely not relevant” to 5 = “very relevant”) that assess fear related to childbirth, foetal health, and physical changes [15]. The original scale demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α > 0.80) and acceptable confirmatory factor analysis fit indices in diverse populations [15].

Translation and cultural adaptation

The PRAQ-R2 was translated into Sinhala and Tamil according to the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines [19]. Forward Translation: Two bilingual experts independently translated the English PRAQ-R2 into Sinhala and Tamil. A panel (two obstetricians, two clinical psychologists, and two midwives) reviewed the translations for cultural relevance and clinical appropriateness. Back Translation: Two bilingual translators, who were blinded to the original version, converted the Sinhala/Tamil versions back into English. The panel resolved discrepancies to ensure semantic equivalence with the original scale.

Both translated versions were pilot tested with 20 pregnant women (a convenience sample) at a separate Western Province hospital. Cognitive interviews were conducted to assess clarity, language appropriateness, and response consistency. An expert panel (an obstetrician, a clinical psychologist, and a midwife) confirmed the content, concept, and semantic validity of the final translation.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation) were used to summarise the demographic data. Psychometric validation included exploratory factor analysis (EFA), which was conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 25 using principal axis factoring with Promax rotation to assess the underlying factor structure [15]. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha (threshold > 0.70) for reliability. Routine EFA cutoffs were used to validate the psychological instruments [20].

Internal consistency analyses revealed robust reliability for both translations. The Sinhala version showed Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.813 for fear of childbirth, 0.922 for worries about a handicapped child, and 0.733 for appearance concerns, with an overall α of 0.86. Similarly, the Tamil version demonstrated α values of 0.705, 0.879, and 0.733 for the respective subscales, and an overall α of 0.81. These results align with the psychometric properties of the original PRAQ-R2 [15], supporting the linguistic and structural validity of the translated tools.

Discussion

This study provides initial evidence that PRAQ-R2 can be used in both Sinhala- and Tamil-speaking pregnant women in Sri Lanka. The main finding was the replication of the original three-factor structure in both translated versions, with factors corresponding to fear of childbirth, worries about bearing a handicapped child, and concerns about one’s own appearance, and with all items loading above the accepted minimum threshold. This pattern is consistent with the original PRAQ-R2 work and later validation studies from Italy, Turkey, and Poland, which also retained the multidimensional structure of pregnancy-related anxiety across different linguistic and cultural settings [12–15]. Our findings therefore support the view that pregnancy-related anxiety is a distinct construct with stable core domains, while still requiring careful local adaptation before clinical use [4, 11, 15].

The reliability findings further strengthen the case for the use of Sinhala and Tamil versions. Overall internal consistency was good in both language groups, and the subscale coefficients were acceptable to high. These values are broadly comparable with the psychometric performance reported in the Turkish, Italian, and Polish validations, as well as the original adaptation of the PRAQ-R2 [12–15]. At the same time, the pattern of coefficients in our study suggests that some domains may be more culturally stable than others. In both translations, worries about bearing a child with a disability showed the strongest internal consistency, whereas the fear of childbirth subscale was comparatively lower in the Tamil version. This may indicate that concerns about foetal well-being are especially coherent in this setting, while labor-related fears may be shaped more strongly by prior birth experience, communication with health staff, and local social beliefs. Similar domain-level variations have been noted in prior validations, which suggests that cross-cultural consistency should not be interpreted as complete uniformity in how pregnancy-related anxiety is experienced or expressed [12–15].

The clinical importance of these findings becomes clearer when they are placed in the Sri Lankan context. Systematic reviews have shown that antenatal anxiety is influenced by financial strain, poor social support, stressful life events, family conflict, and uncertainty about pregnancy outcomes [9, 10]. Local evidence from Sri Lanka also indicates that antenatal anxiety is associated with a wide range of social and familial stressors, including health problems in the woman or close family members, spouse-related substance use, loss of support, financial hardship, and recent bereavement [21]. Against this background, a validated pregnancy-specific tool has clear value because generic anxiety or depression measures may miss fears that are directly tied to childbirth, fetal health, and maternal role concerns [4, 11, 15]. The present study therefore adds practical measurement value, in addition to psychometric evidence.

These findings also have implications for service delivery and policy. Sri Lanka has a strong maternal and child health platform, with structured antenatal follow-up through field services and a national maternal care program; however, gaps remain in the routine detection of perinatal mental health problems, particularly during pregnancy [22, 23]. The routine postpartum use of the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale in MOH clinics shows that mental health screening can be incorporated into existing care pathways; however, pregnancy-related anxiety requires a more specific instrument than a postnatal depression screen alone [23]. In this context, the Sinhala and Tamil PRAQ-R2 may complement existing maternal services by helping identify women whose main distress is pregnancy-specific rather than depressive. Earlier recognition during antenatal care may support timely counselling, referral, and closer follow-up within the public health system [7, 8, 22, 23].

In summary, the present results suggest that the Sinhala and Tamil versions of the PRAQ-R2 are suitable for research use and have promise for clinical screening in Sri Lanka. However, this should be viewed as an important first step rather than definitive psychometric completion. The scale requires further testing across broader populations and care settings before routine national use can be recommended with confidence.

Strengths and Limitations

A major strength of this study is the structured translation and cultural adaptation process based on established guidance, followed by psychometric testing in both Sinhala and Tamil-speaking groups [19, 20]. The inclusion of both primiparous and multiparous women also improves the practical relevance of the findings, given that the PRAQ-R2 was specifically developed for use across parity groups [15]. However, several limitations should be considered. First, participants were recruited through convenience sampling from two tertiary hospitals in the Western Province, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to rural settings and to women receiving care in lower-level facilities. Second, women with diagnosed psychiatric disorders and those who were unable to read Sinhala or Tamil were excluded, which was appropriate for this initial validation but narrowed the applicability of the tool to more vulnerable groups. Third, this study assessed factor structure using exploratory factor analysis and reliability using internal consistency but did not evaluate confirmatory factor analysis, test-retest reliability, convergent validity, or criterion validity. These additional steps are important in scale development and have been included in some other validation studies of the PRAQ-R2 [12, 13]. Future multicentre studies should therefore examine the performance of the Sinhala and Tamil versions in more diverse populations and assess longitudinal stability and other forms of validity before wider implementation.

Conclusion

The Sinhala and Tamil versions of the PRAQ-R2 are valid and reliable tools for assessing pregnancy-related anxiety in Sri Lanka. Their cross-cultural consistency with international studies emphasises the universality of PRA constructs while highlighting the necessity for linguistic and contextual adaptation. This tool can enhance clinical screening and inform interventions to improve maternal mental health outcomes, as advocated by global maternal health frameworks. Future studies should explore the longitudinal associations between PRA and pregnancy outcomes and validate the tool in rural settings to advance equitable mental health care across diverse populations.
