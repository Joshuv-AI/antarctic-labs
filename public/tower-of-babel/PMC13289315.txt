8 years of strengthening AMR response capacity in the WHO African region (2017–2024): progress in antimicrobial consumption monitoring, surveillance and laboratory capacity
Authors: Laetitia Gahimbare, Chinwe Iwu-Jaja, Yidnekachew Degefaw Mazengiya, Sheick O Coulibaly, Lubaju R Z Lugor, Ambele Judith Mwamelo, Olushayo Oluseun Olu, Ali Ahmed Yahaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major global health threat, and African countries are disproportionately affected due to limited diagnostic capacity, weak surveillance and inadequate monitoring of antimicrobial consumption (AMC). This study analysed regional trends using the Tracking Antimicrobial Resistance Country Self-Assessment Survey (TrACSS), a standardised tool for monitoring national progress in AMR response.MethodsWe conducted an analysis of the TrACSS database covering 47 African countries (2017–2024). Indicators included national AMC monitoring systems, national AMR surveillance systems and laboratory capacity focusing on public and private bacteriology coverage and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) for WHO-priority bacteria and critical fungi. Descriptive and trend analyses were performed, and the association between AMC and AMR system maturity was examined using logistic regression and correlation tests.ResultsBetween 2017 and 2024, the proportion of countries with advanced AMC monitoring more than doubled (13%–30%), while advanced AMR surveillance quadrupled (13%–57%). From 2022 to 2024, public-sector bacteriology coverage rose from 80%–94% and private sector coverage from 60%–75%. Nearly all countries (95.7% in 2024) reported at least one reference laboratory performing AST for priority bacteria, though pathogen-specific reporting remained limited, with the lowest capacity for Campylobacter spp. (reported by a maximum of 12.2% of countries annually). Dual Candida and Aspergillus testing increased from 23% to 30%, but nearly half (46.8%) of countries still lacked a national mycology reference laboratory. Countries with advanced AMC systems were significantly more likely to also have advanced AMR surveillance (OR 8.1; p<0.001).ConclusionThe WHO African Region has made measurable progress in AMR response over the past 8 years, though gains remain uneven. Strengthening mycology services, expanding bacteriology coverage and integrating AMC and AMR data systems are critical next steps towards achieving resilient, evidence-driven surveillance networks that can better guide stewardship and investment decisions.Keywords: Public Health; Disease Transmission, Infectious; Epidemiology

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) remains a significant threat to global public health, undermining decades of progress in the treatment of infectious diseases,1 2 with particular challenges in resource-limited settings.2 By 2050, AMR could result in up to 10 million deaths annually and a global economic loss of US$100 trillion if no effective response is implemented.3 In particular, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) in Africa face a disproportionate burden due to limited access to diagnostics, underdeveloped surveillance systems and inadequate implementation of antimicrobial stewardship interventions.4 5 Effective mitigation of AMR requires robust, integrated systems that support surveillance, rational use of antimicrobials, adequate laboratory capacity and coordinated policy decisions across multiple sectors.6 7 Similarly, effective surveillance and response systems require robust clinical bacteriology laboratory capacity to accurately detect resistant pathogens, guide appropriate treatment decisions and inform public health interventions.8 9 These are systematically assessed through the TrACSS framework to evaluate national capacities and track progress in AMR containment. In the African Region (AFRO), where the burden of infectious diseases remains high, strengthening national laboratory infrastructure is critical for containing the spread of AMR.4 10 11

To combat AMR, the WHO developed the Global Action Plan using the ‘One Health’ approach, which was adopted by the 68th World Health Assembly in May 2015 to reduce the widespread rise of AMR.7 This plan is comprised of five objectives: (1) to improve awareness and understanding of AMR through effective communication, education and training; (2) strengthen the knowledge and evidence base through surveillance and research; (3) reduce the incidence of infection through effective sanitation, hygiene and infection prevention measures; (4) optimise the use of antimicrobial medicines in human and animal health; (5) develop the economic case for sustainable investment that takes account of the needs of all countries and to increase investment in new medicines, diagnostic tools, vaccines and other interventions.7

Furthermore, to support countries in monitoring progress on their national action plans against AMR, the WHO, in collaboration with the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH), launched the Tripartite Antimicrobial Resistance Country Self-Assessment Survey (TrACSS) in 2016. Since 2022, the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) has joined as the fourth Quadripartite partner. The survey provides a standardised platform for countries to report annual progress across key AMR domains, including surveillance, laboratory capacity, antimicrobial consumption (AMC), infection prevention and control and awareness activities.12

The WHO AFRO comprises countries with diverse levels of capacity and varying health system challenges.4 11 However, a comprehensive assessment of progress in AMR control, including laboratory services, antimicrobial use monitoring and surveillance systems has not been systematically documented. This study addresses this gap by analysing TrACSS data from 47 African countries between 2017 and 2024. The analysis is essential to highlight gaps and regional trends, showcase areas of progress and good practice, inform policy direction, investment and technical support and strengthen regional coordination and peer learning.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional analysis of data from the TrACSS database, a standardised assessment tool that collects country-level self-reported information on AMR capacities and the implementation of the Global National Action Plan on AMR on an annual basis.

Description of the TrACSS database

While TrACSS encompasses all sectors—human, animal, plant and environment—this analysis focuses exclusively on the human health component. Specifically, it draws on questionnaire sections addressing (1) the national monitoring system for AMC and rational use in human health, (2) the national surveillance system for AMR in humans and (3) capacities related to clinical bacteriology and mycology laboratory services for patient management. Administratively, the TrACSS questionnaire is coordinated by the Ministry of Health national focal point, who engages a multisectoral group including representatives from the FAO, the UNEP and the WOAH to assess national progress. These multisectoral inputs are then consolidated, validated and submitted as a single, officially agreed-upon national response by the AMR focal point or coordination group.12

Data extraction and collection

Data were extracted from the publicly available TrACSS database, which provided the dataset used in this study12 (CI-J), cleaned (CI-J) and validated for completeness and consistency (LG). The analysis included all 47 countries in the WHO AFRO. Countries were categorised according to their responses to each indicator, and we analysed the number of countries under each category.

Data analysis

Data were visualised using various graphical formats, including bar charts, heat maps and scatter plots to facilitate interpretation. For categorical data, stacked bar charts were created to show the distribution of countries across response categories for each year. Heat maps were used to display country-specific capacities.

We examined the association between national AMC monitoring systems and AMR surveillance systems in African countries over the period 2017–2024. Each country’s capacity was rated annually on a five-level scale (A–E). For analysis, ratings were grouped into two categories: Limited (A–B) and advanced (C–E).

We conducted logistic regression with AMR capacity (Limited vs Advanced) as the dependent variable, AMC capacity (Limited vs Advanced) as the main predictor and year (2017–2024) as a covariate. An interaction term was tested to assess whether the AMC–AMR association changed over time. In addition, year-specific contingency tables were generated, and χ2 tests, Phi coefficients and Cohen’s Kappa statistics were calculated to provide descriptive trends of association and agreement between AMC and AMR systems.

Key indicators analysed for laboratory capacity included (1) the coverage of bacteriology laboratory services in both public and private sectors, (2) the capacity of national reference laboratories to conduct antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) for 11 WHO-priority bacterial pathogens—Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacteriaceae, Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Campylobacter spp., Salmonella spp., Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae and Shigella spp. and (3) the capacity to perform AST for critically important fungi, specifically the presence of one or more national mycology reference laboratories designated to test Candida and Aspergillus species.

Data cleaning, analyses and visualisation were done using the R software and Excel (LG and CI-J).

Results

National monitoring system for consumption and rational use of antimicrobials in human health

Between 2017 and 2024, there was a gradual but notable improvement in the implementation of national systems for monitoring AMC and rational use in human health. The number of WHO AFRO countries responding to the TrACSS question on national monitoring systems for antimicrobial consumption and rational use increased from 23 (49%) in 2017 to 47 (100%) in 2024. The highest level of non-reporting was recorded in 2020, when 28 countries did not submit data; however, this number declined to zero in subsequent years.

Figure 1 summarises the trends in national monitoring systems for AMC and rational use, with categories A and B classified as limited AMC monitoring capacity and categories C to E as advanced capacity. Detailed ratings for each country are provided in Annex 1 (AMC monitoring heatmap). The detailed definitions for each capacity category (A–E) are provided in table 1. In 2017, only 13.0% of responding countries (3 of 23) reported advanced AMC monitoring capacity. This proportion grew steadily to reach its highest peak in 2021. Following a drop in 2022, the proportion has not yet fully recovered, standing at 29.8% in 2024. In 2024, 14 countries had achieved advanced AMC monitoring (categories C, D or E), including Namibia and Zimbabwe in category C (n=2); Algeria, Benin, Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya and Uganda in category D (n=8) and Eswatini, Gabon, Malawi and the United Republic of Tanzania in category E (n=4). Conversely, this highlights that a significant majority—70.2% (33 of 47 countries)—still operate with limited AMC monitoring capacity (categories A and B) as of 2024, including 20 with no national plan or system at all (category A).

National surveillance system for AMR in humans

Between 2017 and 2024, there was a gradual and notable improvement in the implementation of national AMR surveillance systems in human health. The number of WHO AFRO countries responding to the TrACSS question on national AMR surveillance increased from 24 (51%) in 2017 to 47 (100%) in 2024. The highest level of non-reporting was recorded in 2020, when 29 countries did not provide data; however, this number declined steadily thereafter, reaching full reporting in 2024.

Figure 2 summarises the status of national AMR surveillance systems, with categories A and B classified as limited AMR surveillance capacity and categories C to E as advanced capacity. Detailed ratings for each country are provided in online supplemental figure 2. The specific definitions for these AMR surveillance categories are also outlined in table 1. In 2017, only 12.5% of responding countries (3 of 24) reported advanced AMR surveillance capacity. This proportion more than quadrupled over the study period. Notably, the proportion of countries with advanced AMR surveillance in 2024 successfully exceeded its previous peak observed in 2021, reaching 57.4% (27 of 47 countries) in 2024. These 27 countries had achieved advanced AMR surveillance (categories C and D), including Liberia, Madagascar, Namibia, Togo and Zimbabwe in category C (n=5); Algeria, Burkina Faso, Burundi, Cameroon, Chad, Côte d’Ivoire, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Eswatini, Ethiopia, Ghana, Guinea, Kenya, Malawi, Mali, Mauritius, Niger, Nigeria, Rwanda, South Africa, Uganda, United Republic of Tanzania and Zambia in category D (n=22) and no country in category E (n=0). Despite this remarkable progress, a significant gap remains: 42.6% of responding countries (20 of 47) still operate with limited AMR surveillance capacity (categories A and B) as of 2024, including five in category A with no capacity for generating data and reporting on antibiotic resistance (AST and accompanying clinical and epidemiological data collection).

From 2023 to 2024, four countries—Algeria, Guinea, Namibia and Zambia—newly joined the advanced group while no country dropped out, and 43 countries remained stable. Overall, across 2017–2024, 35 countries reached categories C–E at least once, reflecting a substantial but still uneven adoption of advanced AMR surveillance capacity. Despite year-to-year fluctuations, the regional trajectory shows more than a fourfold increase in the proportion of countries with advanced AMR surveillance since 2017, with recent years marked by a broadening distribution across categories C and D.

Association between AMC monitoring and AMR surveillance

The regression analysis showed that countries with advanced AMC monitoring were about eight times more likely to also have advanced AMR surveillance (OR 8.1, p<0.001). Independent of AMC, there was a positive time trend (OR 1.18 per year, p=0.008). The interaction term between AMC and year was not significant, indicating that the effect of AMC on AMR remained stable over time. This was consistent with the year-by-year analysis (online supplemental table 1): χ2 tests showed significant associations in 2017 and again from 2022 to 2024 (p<0.05), while the middle years (2018–2021) were not significant. Effect sizes (Phi and Kappa) varied accordingly, ranging from weak–fair in 2018–2019 to strong–substantial in 2017, but without a clear long-term trend.

Capacity to perform AST for critically important bacteria

Between 2022 and 2024, the number of countries reporting the presence of one or more reference laboratories performing AST for at least some of the 11 WHO priority bacteria increased from 39 of 41 respondents (95.1%) in 2022 to 45 of 47 respondents (95.7%) in 2024. However, only a minority provided details on the specific pathogens tested. In 2022, 13 of 41 countries (31.7%) reported pathogen-level data; this proportion rose modestly to 17 of 46 (37.0%) in 2023, before declining sharply to 8 of 47 (17%) in 2024 (online supplemental figure 7).

All countries that reported pathogen-level data indicated having the capacity to perform AST for most of the priority pathogens, except Mozambique, which reported no capacity for any of the priority pathogens in both 2022 and 2023. In 2023, four countries (Benin, Burkina Faso, Cameroon and Ethiopia) reported the capacity to test all 11 priority bacteria, compared with two countries in 2024 (Namibia and Sierra Leone). The lowest reported capacity throughout the period was for Campylobacter spp., with only 2–5 countries acknowledging the ability to test for this pathogen each year. Detailed information on years 2022–2024 is presented in online supplemental figures 3–5, respectively.

Capacity to perform AST for critically important fungi

Between 2022 and 2024, the number of countries reporting the presence of one or more National Mycology Reference Laboratories designated by the Ministry of Health to perform identification and/or susceptibility testing of Candida spp. and Aspergillus spp. remained broadly stable, with modest improvements observed in dual testing capacity.

In 2022, among 40 respondents, 20 (50.0%) reported no capacity, 9 (22.5%) reported capacity for both Candida and Aspergillus, 10 (25.0%) for Candida only and 1 (2.5%) for Aspergillus only. Seven countries (14.9%) did not provide data.

In 2023, among 44 respondents, 23 (52.3%) reported no capacity, 9 (20.5%) reported capacity for both pathogens, 11 (25.0%) for Candida only and 3 (6.8%) reported their status as unknown. Three countries (6.4%) did not provide data.

By 2024, among 47 respondents, 22 (46.8%) reported no capacity, 14 (29.8%) reported capacity for both Candida and Aspergillus, 10 (21.3%) for Candida only, and 1 (2.1%) reported their status as unknown. No countries failed to provide data (online supplemental figure 8).

Discussion

Our analysis of TrACSS submissions from 47 WHO AFRO countries shows measurable, though uneven, progress across AMC monitoring, AMR surveillance, and clinical bacteriology laboratory capacity between 2017 and 2024. Taken together, the trends suggest that countries are gradually institutionalising the core building blocks of AMR control efforts, monitoring of use, surveillance of resistance and reliable, quality-assured laboratory services, while persistent gaps in guideline adoption, external quality assessment, supply chain resilience and fungal diagnostics continue to limit impact at the point of care. These findings align with the WHO’s Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS), which links national AMC/AMR surveillance as integral pillars of health system strengthening.13 However, it is important to note that these TrACSS indicators primarily reflect the establishment of central policy frameworks and national surveillance architecture. Advancing to a higher capacity category indicates national-level coordination but does not necessarily equate to equitable subnational coverage or guarantee that these services are accessible to all local communities.

AMC monitoring and AMR surveillance: converging trajectories, reinforcing functions

Across the Region, the share of countries reporting advanced AMR surveillance capacity more than quadrupled from 2017 to 2024, while limited capacity declined but still involved ~43% of countries in 2024. These improvements coincided with increased response completeness and wider national coordination of surveillance activities. In parallel, AMC monitoring systems expanded, with a growing subset of countries reporting higher maturity levels. While both systems demonstrated overall long-term growth, a temporary dip in advanced capacity was observed in 2022 for both AMC and AMR. This decline may likely reflect methodological changes rather than a sudden loss of infrastructure; specifically, the TrACSS definitions and indicators for advanced categories (C, D and E) were revised between 2022 and 2024. This change in survey criteria likely resulted in the temporary downward reclassification of several countries as they adjusted to the new reporting frameworks. Although the pace of progress in AFRO is accelerating, the Region continues to lag behind areas such as Europe and the Western Pacific, where participation in platforms and regional networks/databases for surveillance are better established.14

From a policy perspective, these dual gains are encouraging in a Region that bears one of the world’s highest AMR burdens. Global estimates attribute around 1.27 million deaths to bacterial AMR in 2019, with the highest mortality rates in sub-Saharan Africa (Murray et al., 2022). Sustained investment to consolidate standardised national AMR surveillance should therefore be paired with expansion of AMC systems that can generate actionable prescribing and consumption metrics. Credible drivers of AFRO’s upward trend include targeted external investments such as the Fleming Fund programmes.15

Furthermore, findings confirm that countries with advanced systems for monitoring AMC are significantly more likely to also have advanced AMR surveillance systems. This association was consistent across 2017–2024, with both systems showing overall strengthening over time. The dip in association observed between 2018 and 2021 may reflect uneven investment or reporting, but the resurgence in 2022–2024 suggests renewed alignment. This association is consistent with GLASS guidance, which positions AMC and AMR surveillance as mutually reinforcing: AMC data inform stewardship targets (eg, AWaRe benchmarks), while AMR trends guide optimisation of antimicrobial use.13 16 These results underscore the importance of coordinated investment: strengthening AMC monitoring is closely tied to strengthening AMR surveillance. For policy makers, this implies that advancing one system is likely to accelerate progress in the other, reinforcing the value of integrated national strategies.

Coverage of bacteriology services: public sector nearing universality; private sector rising

Public sector bacteriology capacity to perform culture, identification and AST rose among countries from ~80% in 2022 to ~94% in 2024, while private sector availability increased from ~60% to ~75% over the same period. Strengthening bacteriology capacity in both sectors is crucial in AFRO settings where mixed health markets are common. National surveillance networks benefit through more comprehensive and representative AMR data when private laboratories are integrated into standardised reporting, quality systems and referral pathways, as emphasised in the WHO AFRO Regional Strategy for laboratory strengthening.17

Bacterial AST capacity: breadth at reference level, but pathogen-specific gaps

Most countries reported that at least one reference laboratory is capable of AST for WHO priority bacteria, yet pathogen-level reporting remains sparse and capacity for certain pathogens such as Campylobacter spp. is limited. Similar gaps have been documented in other low- and middle-income countries participating in WHO’s GLASS, where implementation of pathogen–antibiotic combinations remains incomplete due to shortages of reagents, technical expertise and validated methods for fastidious organisms.14 18 19 Addressing these gaps requires targeted procurement, validation of methods, proficiency testing for fastidious organisms and investments in data systems consistent with GLASS pathogen panels.13

Mycology capacity: modest improvement, large unmet need

Between 2022 and 2024, the number of countries reporting dual capacity for Candida and Aspergillus testing increased, yet nearly half still reported no national mycology reference capacity in 2024. This is particularly concerning considering WHO’s Fungal Priority Pathogens List (FPPL), which designates Aspergillus fumigatus and Candida species among the most critical fungal threats, calling for enhanced diagnostics and stewardship.20 This shortfall likely reflects a combination of factors: chronic underinvestment in fungal diagnostics, limited availability of trained mycologists and the high cost and supply constraints of fungal culture media and antifungal susceptibility reagents.21 Country laboratory strategies should translate these global priorities into investments in culture, species-level identification, including matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation–time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF) where feasible, antifungal susceptibility testing, and referral pathways.

Implications for regional policy and investment

The WHO AFRO Regional Strategy on Diagnostic and Laboratory Services and Systems 2023–2032 calls for coordinated, quality-assured diagnostic networks integrated across health system tiers.22 Our results, therefore, highlight key policy priorities. First, the consolidation of national surveillance architecture, which entails supporting more countries to reach standardised national surveillance (TrACSS category D), integrating private sector data and linking AMC with AMR to inform stewardship targets.13 Strengthening national capacity for bacteriology across both public and private sectors will improve data representativeness and ensure that AMR surveillance findings are more reliable for policy and planning. Additionally, the analysis underscores the growing importance of mycology within AMR control efforts. Nearly half of the countries in the Region still lack a national mycology reference laboratory, underscoring the need to expand fungal diagnostic capacity in line with WHO’s FPPL.20 23 These actions should be pursued within a One Health framework that links human, animal and environmental health surveillance to address AMR comprehensively. Prioritising countries with limited surveillance capacity will help reduce regional inequalities, while sustained investment in infrastructure, training, and intersectoral data integration will ensure that the progress achieved between 2017 and 2024 translates into durable regional capacity for AMR prevention and control.

Strengths and limitations

This study leverages an 8-year dataset covering all AFRO Member States and triangulates trends across AMC, AMR and laboratory capacity. Findings should, however, be interpreted in light of self-reported TrACSS data, indicator revisions over time and missing responses for some parameters, limitations common to global monitoring systems. Our study has several limitations. First, TrACSS data are self-reported by countries and not independently verified, which may introduce reporting biases (eg, over-reporting of achievements to align with global targets). Second, changes in questionnaire structure and indicators over time limited the comparability of some parameters across the full study period. Third, non-reporting by some countries and indicators created gaps in the regional assessment. Also, the binary or categorical nature of many indicators may not fully capture the quality or functionality of existing laboratory services. Finally, TrACSS does not appear to capture financing levels, workforce capacity or subnational inequalities, which are critical determinants of laboratory functionality in the AFRO.

Conclusion

This analysis of TrACSS data from 47 African countries reveals measurable, though uneven, progress in strengthening AMR response systems between 2017 and 2024. The proportion of countries with advanced AMR surveillance more than quadrupled, and those with advanced AMC monitoring more than doubled, reflecting a gradual consolidation of national monitoring frameworks. Laboratory capacity also improved, with widespread expansion of public and private bacteriology services and modest gains in fungal diagnostics, although nearly half of the countries still lack a national mycology reference laboratory.

The strong association between AMC and AMR system maturity highlights the interdependence of monitoring antimicrobial use and tracking resistance trends. Sustaining these gains requires continued investment to integrate AMC and AMR systems, expand laboratory and workforce capacity and strengthen coordination under a One Health approach. These efforts are essential to generate reliable evidence, guide stewardship actions and reduce the burden of AMR in the WHO AFRO.
