Causal Effects with Unobserved Unit Types in Interacting Human–AI Systems
William Overman, Sadegh Shirani, Mohsen Bayati

Abstract
We study experiments on interacting populations of humans and AI agents, where both unit types and the interaction network remain unobserved. Although causal effects propagate throughout the system, the goal is to estimate effects on humans. Examples include online platforms where human users interact alongside AI-driven accounts. We assume a human–AI prior that gives each unit a probability of being human. While humans cannot be distinguished at the unit level, the prior allows us to compute the average human composition within large subpopulations. We then model outcome dynamics through a causal message passing (CMP) framework and analyze sample-mean outcomes across subpopulations. We show that by constructing subpopulations that vary in expected human composition and treatment exposure, one can consistently recover human-specific causal effects. Our results characterize when distributional knowledge of population composition (without observing unit types or the interaction network) is sufficient for identification. We validate the approach on a simulated human–AI platform driven by behaviorally differentiated LLM agents. Together, these results provide a theoretical and practical framework for experimentation in emerging human–AI systems.

Experimentation in network environments is fundamental in scientific disciplines. When outcomes depend on interactions among individuals, classical methods no longer isolate treatment effects, and careful modeling of spillovers and dynamics becomes essential
[

,

,

.

At the same time, human–AI systems are emerging as a new paradigm in digital platforms. Recent advances in modern AI have enabled autonomous agents to closely mimic human behavior
[
22
,
34
]
and interact with human users at scale. As a result, online environments such as social media platforms may include mixed populations of humans and AI-driven agents whose interactions jointly shape aggregate outcomes.

In this context, experimentation with mixed populations of humans and AI agents poses challenges that go beyond classical network interference problems. In standard settings, experimental units are typically homogeneous, and the interaction network may be partially observed. In contrast, in human–AI platforms, the identities of units are often unobserved, and the interaction structure is difficult to characterize directly: while humans interact through multiple online and offline channels, AI agents are confined to online, platform-mediated interactions.

When unit types are latent, individual-level observations provide only limited information about whether a user is human or AI—at best, one can construct classifiers that assign each unit a probability of being human. Outcomes therefore evolve through dynamic interactions among heterogeneous units with opaque connectivity, yet the estimand of interest is type-specific (for example, the causal effect of an intervention on human users only). This uncertainty in unit types, and consequently in the underlying interaction structure, makes the estimation of human-specific effects intrinsically challenging.

For experimentation under unobserved network structures,
44
propose the CMP framework. In settings with homogeneous unit types, CMP avoids explicit network knowledge by analyzing the evolution of aggregate outcomes over time
[
45
]
. By shifting the analysis to population-level states, CMP provides a natural entry point for human–AI systems: in large populations, distributional knowledge of unit types is sufficient to characterize aggregate outcome evolutions.

In this work, we adapt the CMP framework to settings with uncertain unit types. Figure
1
provides an outline of our approach. Specifically, we assume that each user is associated with a prior probability of being human and incorporate this information into a type-dependent outcome model. The model allows treatment responses and interaction effects to vary by latent type, capturing heterogeneous dynamics without observing user identities. We then derive state evolution (SE) equations that characterize the low-dimensional dynamics of sample mean outcomes. In particular, we show that the evolution of aggregate outcomes within suitably constructed subpopulations depends only on the average of their human–AI priors. This leads to a practical estimation procedure that recovers the causal effect of an intervention on humans,
as if
unit types were observed.

To evaluate our framework, we develop a simulator of a mixed human–AI online platform. Both humans and AI agents are instantiated using large language models (LLMs), but with distinct prompting and temperature configurations to induce systematically different behavioral patterns. User types are unobserved by the platform, and their engagement level as outcomes evolve through dynamic interactions. The treatment corresponds to exposing users to platform-curated “success stories,” designed to counteract bot-generated activities. Using data generated from this environment, we show that our method accurately estimates human-specific treatment effects from aggregate observations, despite latent unit types and unobserved interaction structure.

In summary, we introduce a framework for estimating human-specific causal effects in mixed human–AI systems with hidden unit types and unobserved interactions. We derive low-dimensional state evolution equations that enable identification from aggregate data using only distributional knowledge of population composition. Finally, we provide an LLM-based simulation and show that our estimator accurately recovers ground-truth human treatment effects. Together, these results establish a practical methodology for experimentation in emerging human–AI environments.

Problem Outline

We consider an interacting population of size
N
, indexed by
i\in[N]:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
, observed over a finite time horizon
t=0,1,\ldots,T
. For each unit
i
and time
t
, we observe a binary treatment assignment
W^{i}_{t}\in\{0,1\}
and a scalar outcome
Y^{i}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
. We assume that no treatments are assigned prior to
t=1
, so that
W^{i}_{0}=0
for all
i
. We collect treatment assignments at time
t
in the vector
\vec{W}_{t}=[W^{i}_{t}]_{i\in[N]}
, and denote by
\vec{W}_{1:t}:=(\vec{W}_{1},\ldots,\vec{W}_{t})
the history of treatment assignments up to time
t
. Following
[
25
]
, potential outcomes are collected in the vector
\vec{Y}_{t}(\vec{W}_{1:t})=\big[Y^{i}_{t}(\vec{W}_{1:t})\big]_{i\in[N]}
.
So, each outcome may depend on the population-wide history of treatments.

Each unit
i\in[N]
is associated with an unobserved type variable
U^{i}\in{0,1}
, where
U^{i}=1
denotes a human and
U^{i}=0
an AI agent. The realizations of
U^{i}
are not observed. Instead, we assume
U^{i}\overset{\text{i.i.d}}{\sim}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(Q^{i})
, where the observed parameters
Q^{1},\ldots,Q^{N}\in[0,1]
are independently drawn from a prior
distribution
p_{u}
supported on
[0,1]
. These
Q^{i}
’s represent a prior over the human–AI composition of the population and are assumed known or estimable from external data (e.g., as outputs of a classifier mapping historical data to type probabilities). Types are fixed over time and unaffected by treatment assignments or outcomes.

3.1
Total Treatment Effect on Humans

For the realized treatment allocation
\vec{w}_{1:T}:=(\vec{w}_{1},\ldots,\vec{w}_{T})
, let
Y^{i}_{t}(\vec{w}_{1:t})
denote the observed outcome of unit
i
at time
t
under the treatment path
\vec{w}_{1:t}
applied to the experimental population. Our goal is to quantify the total effect of treatment on human units. Specifically, we contrast the average outcomes of humans under the counterfactual scenario in which all units are treated,
\vec{W}_{1:T}=\mathbf{1}
, with the scenario in which all units are assigned control,
\vec{W}_{1:T}=\mathbf{0}
. Formally, we define the total treatment effect on humans at time
t
as

\displaystyle\text{H-TTE}_{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\!\Bigg[\frac{Y^{i}_{t}\!(\vec{W}_{1:t}=\mathbf{1})-\,Y^{i}_{t}\!(\vec{W}_{1:t}=\mathbf{0})}{N_{H}}\Big|U^{i}=1\Bigg].

(1)

Here,
N_{H}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\{U^{i}=1\}
denotes the total number of humans in the population, which is unknown. Additionally, the expectation is with respect to the outcome distribution induced by the treatment path,
holding the population composition fixed
. Then, the estimand
\text{H-TTE}_{t}
captures a
system-level
causal effect: it measures how the average outcome of human users would change if the entire population were treated versus not treated, allowing effects to propagate through interactions among humans and AI agents.

3.2
Outcome Dynamics

To define the outcome model, we denote by
\vec{Y}_{0}
the vector of pre-treatment outcomes at time
t=0
. For notational convenience, when there is no ambiguity, we suppress the explicit dependence of outcomes on the treatment history. We posit the following outcome model for
t=1,\dots,T
,

\displaystyle Y^{i}_{t}:=\,\delta_{H}U^{i}+\delta_{A}(1-U^{i})+\big(\tau_{H}U^{i}+\tau_{A}(1-U^{i})\big)W^{i}_{t}

(2)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{N}({\text{A}}^{ij}+{\text{B}}^{ij}_{t})\,\big(\alpha W^{j}_{t}+\beta Y^{j}_{t-1}+\gamma W^{j}_{t}Y^{j}_{t-1}\big)+\epsilon^{i}_{t}.

Here,
(\delta_{H},\delta_{A})
capture baseline outcome heterogeneity across unit types in the absence of treatment. The parameters
(\tau_{H},\tau_{A})
govern type-specific direct treatment effects. The coefficients
{\text{A}}^{ij}
represent fixed (time-invariant) interaction effects from unit
j
to unit
i
, while
{\text{B}}^{ij}_{t}
allow for time-varying interaction strengths. Through these terms, current treatments, lagged outcomes, and their interaction propagate across units, accommodating both immediate spillovers and dynamic effects. Finally, the noise terms
\{\epsilon^{i}_{t}\}
are assumed to be mean-zero and capture idiosyncratic shocks not explained by the structured dynamics.

The outcome model in Eq. (
2
) admits several natural generalizations. For example, one may incorporate unit-specific covariates or allow the baseline terms
(\delta_{H},\delta_{A})
and direct treatment effects
(\tau_{H},\tau_{A})
to vary across users. The analysis in this work continues to hold under such extensions; see
44
,
47
for details. We next introduce the assumptions underlying our model, which enable us to reduce the analysis of the high-dimensional dynamics in Eq. (
2
) to a one-dimensional state evolution for the sample mean of outcomes.

4
Main Theory

We proceed by analyzing the outcome model (
2
). In addition to the standard challenges of network interference (notably correlated observed outcomes), the presence of unobserved unit types
U^{i}
further complicates the analysis. Our goal is to characterize the sample-mean dynamics of outcomes for a generic subpopulation. We therefore start by introducing the required assumptions.

We assume that the interference weights
{\text{A}}^{ij}
and
{\text{B}}^{ij}_{t}
are Gaussian, with parameters that depend on the type of unit that receives the impact.

Assumption 1
(Type-dependent Gaussian Interference)

For all
i,j\in[N]
and
t=1,\ldots,T
, conditional on the value of
\,U^{i}
, the interference weights
{\text{A}}^{ij}
and
{\text{B}}^{ij}_{t}
are Gaussian random variables independent of each other and everything else in the model:

\displaystyle{\text{A}}^{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{U^{i}\mu^{ij}_{H}+(1-U^{i})\mu^{ij}_{A}}{N},\,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N}\right),

\displaystyle{\text{B}}^{ij}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{U^{i}\mu^{ij}_{H,t}+(1-U^{i})\mu^{ij}_{A,t}}{N},\,\frac{\sigma^{2}_{t}}{N}\right),

Above,
(\mu^{ij}_{H},\mu^{ij}_{A})
define type-specific mean interaction strengths for the fixed component, while
(\mu^{ij}_{H,t},\mu^{ij}_{A,t})
allow these means to vary over time. The
1/N
scaling keeps aggregate interference
O(1)
, and the variances
\sigma^{2}/N
and
\sigma_{t}^{2}/N
capture unstructured heterogeneity around these means.

The next assumption sets moment and independence conditions that ensure concentration and a stable analysis of the outcome model (
2
) as the population size
N\to\infty
.

Assumption 2
(Regularity and Concentration)

Fix the time horizon
T
. Considering the outcome dynamics in (
2
), we impose the following regularity conditions:

(i)

Initialization.

The initial outcomes
\{Y^{i}_{0}\}_{i=1}^{N}
follow a distribution
p_{y}
with finite second moment.

(ii)

Noise moments.

For each
t=1,\ldots,T
and
i\in[N]
, the noise terms
\epsilon^{i}_{t}
are independent draws from a distribution
p_{e_{t}}
with finite second moment.

(iii)

Interference-mean.

For each
i,j\in[N]
, the terms

\mu^{ij}_{H}+\mu^{ij}_{H,t}

and

\mu^{ij}_{A}+\mu^{ij}_{A,t}

are drawn (independent from other randomnesses) from distributions
p_{H}
and
p_{A}
, respectively, each with finite second moment.

We now state the main theoretical result of our framework.

Theorem 1
(Experimental State Evolution – ESE)

Consider the outcome dynamics (
2
) and suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2

Assume a Bernoulli randomized design: for each
i
and
t

W^{i}_{t}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi_{t}),

t

Assume that, for each

q^{\mathsf{S}}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\left|{\mathsf{S}}\right|}\sum_{i\in\mathsf{S}}Q^{i},\qquad\pi^{\mathsf{S}}_{t}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\left|{\mathsf{S}}\right|}\sum_{i\in\mathsf{S}}W^{i}_{t}.

(3)

Then, for each fixed

\displaystyle\nu^{\mathsf{S}}_{t}=

\displaystyle\delta_{H}q^{\mathsf{S}}+\delta_{A}(1-q^{\mathsf{S}})

(4)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\big(\tau_{H}q^{\mathsf{S}}+\tau_{A}(1-q^{\mathsf{S}})\big)\pi^{\mathsf{S}}_{t}

\displaystyle\nu_{t}=

Here,

First, it computes average outcomes and treatment rates within each subpopulation, yielding multiple aggregate trajectories with systematic differences in exposure and composition. 5.1
Construction of Subpopulations

Algorithm
1
takes as input a collection of subpopulations
\mathsf{S}^{1},\ldots,\mathsf{S}^{K}
. These subpopulations are
not
assumed to be random samples of the population but are instead constructed systematically to induce identifying variation in aggregate statistics. We next provide heuristics for creating such subpopulations in practice, while leaving the development of optimal subpopulation design strategies to future work.
The objective is to construct subpopulations with heterogeneous human–AI composition
q^{(k)}:=q^{\mathsf{S}^{k}}
and distinct treatment trajectories
\{\hat{\pi}_{t}^{(k)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, where variation in
q^{(k)}
identifies type-specific baseline and direct treatment effects, and variation in
\hat{\pi}_{t}^{(k)}
identifies the dynamic response of aggregate outcomes to treatment exposure.

Step 1: Stratify to control composition.

Choose a small number of composition strata by binning units according to their priors
Q^{i}
. We treat each stratum as a
candidate pool
so that any subpopulation
\mathsf{S}^{k}
drawn from that pool has a predictable and systematically different composition
q^{(k)}
.

Step 2: Create exposure-diverse batches in each stratum.

For each candidate pool, compute each unit’s treatment duration
d_{i}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}w^{i}_{t}
, sort units by
d_{i}
, and pick evenly spaced anchor positions along this sorted list. Then, for each anchor form a candidate set by merging (i) a
systematic
contiguous block of units around the anchor (to sweep across durations and hence induce different
\{\hat{\pi}_{t}^{(k)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
) with (ii) a
random
block sampled uniformly from the pool (to inject additional variation), then sample from the merged candidate set (removing duplicates) to obtain
\mathsf{S}^{k}
with the desired size on average.

Remark 1

(i) Outcome data
Y^{i}_{t}
are never used in forming
\mathsf{S}^{k}
, ensuring that subpopulation construction is outcome-free and avoids selection bias.
(ii) Subpopulations may overlap without affecting consistency, since estimation relies only on aggregate moments rather than unit-level independence.
(iii) In practice, enforcing minimum batch sizes and pruning subpopulations with nearly identical treatment trajectories
\{\hat{\pi}_{t}^{(k)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
yields a small set of well-separated subpopulations that is sufficient for stable estimation.

5.2
Insight: why subpopulations help

In our proposed framework, each subpopulation
\mathsf{S}^{k}
serves as a coarse-grained “experimental unit”: we summarize it by the empirical treatment path
\{\hat{\pi}_{t}^{(k)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and its aggregate composition
q^{(k)}
, and then fit the same state-evolution recursion across
k=1,\ldots,K
. The key point is that while unit-level types are unobserved, the ESE (Eq. (
4
)) guarantees that the sample-mean evolution within a sufficiently large batch
\mathsf{S}^{k}
depends on types only through the aggregate composition parameter. Therefore,
q^{(k)}
can be used as an effective observed regressor that captures how humans and AIs contribute to treatment responses at the population level. Indeed, varying
(q^{(k)},\{\hat{\pi}_{t}^{(k)}\})
across subpopulations yields multiple aggregate realizations of the same state evolution, which provides the sample needed for Step II of Algorithm
1
to identify
\theta
from sample-mean dynamics.

Consistency

We establish that Algorithm

(i)

Among the limiting subpopulation summaries

.

(ii)

Let
\{\nu_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

\theta^{*}
. The
T\times 3

6
Experiments

We evaluate Algorithm
1
on a simulated online social platform where human users and AI bots interact through threaded discussions. This setting captures the core challenge of our framework: estimating treatment effects on humans when user types are unobserved and both populations respond to interventions through dynamic social feedback.

6.1
Simulation Environment

Platform and users.

We simulate a discussion-based dating platform with
N=200
users, of which 50% are humans and 50% are AI bots masquerading as genuine users. Each user
i
is assigned a demographic persona—name, gender, age (drawn uniformly from 20–40), occupation (from 16 categories), and four interests (sampled from a pool of 24)—producing demographically varied profiles following
11
and
47
. User types
U^{i}\in\{0,1\}
, for human or AI, are fixed at initialization and unobserved by the platform. AI bots receive the same demographic initialization as humans, as they are taken to be bots adopting such personas.

All user responses are generated by the same underlying language model (Claude Sonnet 4.5), with human–AI differentiation arising from two mechanisms:

Temperature
, where human responses use temperature
1.0
(more stochastic) and AI responses use temperature
0.2
(more deterministic); and

Prompt framing
, where humans receive a personality description stating they are “generally optimistic” and that “when something inspires you (like a success story), you feel more motivated to participate and connect,” while AI users are described as “jaded and cynical,” such that “negative/venting posts resonate with you” and “positive posts feel naive to you.”

Content and feed.

At initialization, each user generates one seed post that becomes a discussion thread. AI users produce cynical, venting-style posts about dating frustrations (generated at temperature
0.2
), while human users produce positive or neutral posts about dating and social life (generated at temperature
1.0
). This yields a thread pool of 200 seed threads—100 negative (AI-authored) and 100 positive (human-authored)—that grows organically as users reply throughout the simulation.

Each round, every user’s feed consists of 4 threads sampled without replacement from the pool with probability proportional to
\log(\text{reply\_count}+1)
. This popularity-weighted sampling creates a network feedback loop: threads that attract more replies become more visible, which attracts further replies. Each thread is displayed as the original post followed by the 5 most recent replies from prior rounds and a total reply count. Full prompts are in Appendix
B
.

Engagement.

After viewing the feed, each user outputs structured JSON specifying an action for each thread:
reply
(with short text appended to the thread pool),
like
, or
skip
. Our primary outcome
Y^{i}_{t}
is engagement, defined as the number of non-skip actions (replies
+
likes) across four threads, yielding
Y^{i}_{t}\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}
.

Treatment.

The treatment
W^{i}_{t}=1
replaces one of the four feed threads with a platform-curated “Success Story of the Day”—a sponsored post in which a couple describes meeting through the platform and celebrating their six-month anniversary. This in-feed intervention is designed to counteract the negativity from bot-authored threads. Treated users see 3 organic threads plus 1 treatment thread; untreated users see 4 organic threads. Treatment is assigned independently across users each round with probability
p_{t}
determined by the experimental phase.

6.2
Experimental Design

Each simulation run consists of 4 warmup rounds followed by 12 main rounds, for a total of 16 rounds. The warmup phase uses no treatment to establish baseline dynamics and allow the thread pool to develop an initial reply distribution. The 12 main rounds are divided into three treatment phases of 4 rounds each, with escalating treatment probabilities
p_{t}\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}
. This escalating design provides temporal variation in treatment intensity, which, combined with compositional variation across subpopulations, enables identification of the state-evolution parameters.

From a shared warmup state, we run three parallel scenarios:

Control
(
p_{t}=0
for all 12 main rounds, no treatment);

Treatment
(
p_{t}=1
for all 12 main rounds, full treatment); and

Experiment
(
p_{t}\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}
across the three phases, mixed treatment).

The control and treatment scenarios provide the ground-truth human treatment effect

\text{H-TTE}_{t}{}=\bar{Y}_{t}^{\text{treat, human}}-\bar{Y}_{t}^{\text{ctrl, human}}

by comparing average human outcomes across the two worlds. The experiment scenario provides the panel data

\{(Y^{i}_{t},w^{i}_{t})\}

from which the estimator must recover
\text{H-TTE}_{t}{}
without access to type labels or the parallel worlds. We run 10 independent seeds, each generating a fresh set of personas, seed threads, and treatment assignments.

6.3
Priors and Subpopulation Construction

Algorithm
1
requires per-user priors
Q^{i}
representing the belief that user
i
is human. In our simulation the true population human fraction is
q=0.5
, so the population-level prior is always correctly specified. What varies is the quality of per-user classification—how well individual
Q^{i}
values separate true humans from true AIs.

We parameterize classifier quality by an accuracy parameter
a\in[0.5,1]
and a noise level
\sigma\geq 0
. For each user

with true type indicator

:

Q^{i}=\mathrm{clip}\!\Big(U^{i}\cdot a+(1-U^{i})\cdot(1-a)+\varepsilon_{i},\;0,\;1\Big),

with
\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. As
a
decreases toward
0.5

and the prior becomes uninformative. The noise
\sigma
spreads the
Q^{i}
distribution, creating compositional variation across subpopulations even when
a
is moderate. We evaluate three configurations, all with
\sigma=0.15
:

a=0.7
(
{\sim}8\%
misclassification rate),

a=0.8
(
{\sim}2\%
misclassification), and

a=0.9
(
{\sim}1\%
misclassification).

Following Section
5.1
, we construct subpopulations in two steps. First, we partition all
N
users into 3 strata based on quantiles of
Q^{i}
, yielding low-
q
(AI-heavy), medium-
q
(mixed), and high-
q
(human-heavy) pools. Second, within each stratum we sort users by realized treatment duration
d_{i}=\sum_{t}w^{i}_{t}
and form batches at evenly spaced positions, yielding
K\approx 9
subpopulations varying in both composition
q^{(k)}
and treatment trajectory.

7
Conclusion

We introduced a framework for estimating causal effects on humans in interacting populations where unit types and the network structure are unobserved. By embedding type structure into a dynamic interference model, our approach addresses two challenges simultaneously: network interference methods ignore type-dependent responses, while heterogeneous treatment effect methods ignore spillovers. Our theory shows that distributional knowledge of population composition suffices for identification, and our simulations confirm that the estimator accurately recovers ground-truth human treatment effects, degrading gracefully as classifier quality decreases. As AI agents become increasingly prevalent on online platforms, such tools will be essential for credible experimentation.
