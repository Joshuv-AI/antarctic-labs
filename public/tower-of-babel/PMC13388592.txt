Comparative efficacy and safety of aspirin, low-molecular-weight heparin, and rivaroxaban for deep venous thrombosis prophylaxis after hip arthroplasty for femoral neck fracture: a single-center randomized controlled trial
Authors: Shaogang Miao, Qiaoning Yue, Xiguang Zhang, Jinghua Lu, Yi Zhang, Ning Lu, Liangsheng Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of aspirin, low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), and rivaroxaban for deep vein thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis after total hip arthroplasty (THA) or bipolar hemiarthroplasty (BHA) for femoral neck fracture, with subgroup analysis stratified by surgical type.MethodsThis single-center randomized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted between June 2024 and June 2025. A total of 217 consecutive patients with femoral neck fracture undergoing hip arthroplasty were initially screened, and 161 eligible patients were finally enrolled and randomly assigned to the aspirin group, LMWH group, or rivaroxaban group at a 1:1:1 ratio. All patients received standardized intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) and graduated compression stockings for mechanical DVT prophylaxis, as well as a unified postoperative rehabilitation protocol. The primary outcome was the incidence of lower extremity DVT within 12 weeks postoperatively, confirmed by color Doppler ultrasound. The secondary outcome was the incidence of bleeding-related complications graded by the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) criteria. All patients were followed up at 6, 8, and 12 weeks postoperatively, with routine bilateral lower extremity ultrasound performed at 12 weeks regardless of symptoms. Subgroup analysis was performed on the basis of surgical type (THA versus BHA).ResultsAmong the 50 patients in the aspirin group, 5 (10.00%) developed DVT and 2 (4.00%) experienced bleeding complications. In the LMWH group (n = 58), 3 (5.17%) developed DVT and 3 (5.17%) had bleeding complications. In the rivaroxaban group (n = 53), 4 (7.55%) developed DVT and 12 (22.64%) had bleeding complications. No statistically significant difference in DVT incidence was observed among the three groups in either the overall cohort or THA/BHA subgroups (all P > 0.05). However, the incidence of bleeding complications in the rivaroxaban group was significantly higher than that in the aspirin and LMWH groups in both the overall cohort and surgical subgroups (all adjusted P < 0.0167). The baseline characteristics, surgical type distribution, operative duration, intraoperative complications, and postoperative ambulation time were well balanced among the three groups (all P > 0.05).ConclusionsAspirin, LMWH, and rivaroxaban have comparable efficacy in reducing DVT incidence after both THA and BHA for femoral neck fracture. LMWH and aspirin exhibit more favorable safety profiles with significantly lower bleeding risk, while rivaroxaban is associated with a markedly higher incidence of bleeding complications and should be used with caution in elderly patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10195-026-00941-7.

Introduction

Femoral neck fracture is a prevalent osteoporotic fracture in the elderly, accounting for more than 50% of hip fractures globally [1]. Hip arthroplasty, including total hip arthroplasty (THA) and bipolar hemiarthroplasty (BHA, artificial femoral head replacement), is widely recognized as the gold standard for restoring mobility and alleviating pain in these patients [2]. THA is preferred for younger, active patients with longer life expectancy and better bone quality, while BHA is widely used in elderly, frail patients with limited activity to reduce surgical trauma and shorten operative duration [3]. However, both surgical procedures carry a substantial risk of deep venous thrombosis (DVT) owing to perioperative venous stasis, hypercoagulability, and endothelial injury—the core components of Virchow’s triad [4].

In the absence of thromboprophylaxis, lower limb DVT occurs in 40–84% of patients undergoing THA and 30–65% of patients undergoing BHA, with rates of proximal DVT, symptomatic pulmonary embolism (PE), and fatal PE ranging from 8–24%, 1.8–7%, and 0.2–2%, respectively [1, 5]. This not only increases morbidity and mortality but also prolongs hospital stays and elevates healthcare costs. Conversely, the administration of antithrombotic agents is associated with an increased risk of bleeding and other complications, such as hematoma formation, surgical site infection, reoperation, and even death [6]. Therefore, the selection of antithrombotic agents with both favorable efficacy and safety profiles, as well as the evaluation of their performance in different surgical types, is of paramount clinical importance.

To mitigate DVT risk, three agents are widely utilized in clinical practice: low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), a guideline-recommended first-line thromboprophylactic agent [1]; aspirin, a cost-effective oral option suitable for resource-limited settings or low-risk patients [7]; and rivaroxaban, a convenient direct oral anticoagulant that does not require routine coagulation monitoring [8]. However, the optimal choice among these agents remains controversial. Their safety profiles, particularly with respect to bleeding risk, differ substantially, and direct head-to-head comparisons in homogeneous populations—such as elderly patients with femoral neck fracture undergoing THA or BHA—are limited, especially in single-center real-world clinical settings.

Elderly patients with femoral neck fracture often present with multiple comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and atrial fibrillation) and require prolonged bed rest, which further increases their risk of both thrombosis and bleeding [9, 10]. Previous studies have either focused on mixed orthopedic populations [11, 12] or lacked direct head-to-head comparisons of these three agents [7, 13], while multicenter trials may be confounded by variations in surgical techniques and perioperative care protocols across centers. By contrast, single-center studies with standardized surgical and care protocols can provide complementary homogeneous data with minimized confounding, addressing the existing evidence gap.

Accordingly, this single-center RCT was designed to compare the efficacy and safety of aspirin, LMWH, and rivaroxaban for DVT prophylaxis following hip arthroplasty for femoral neck fracture, with prespecified subgroup analysis based on THA and BHA. Standardized surgical procedures (performed by the same senior surgeon), perioperative care, mechanical prophylaxis, and rehabilitation protocols were implemented to minimize confounding variables, with the aim of guiding personalized clinical decision-making on the basis of patient risk profiles and surgical type.

Materials and methods

Study design and Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) compliance

This single-center randomized controlled trial was designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the (CONSORT) 2010 statement. The methodological quality and reporting standards were further informed by the recent recommendations of Migliorini et al. [14], who provide comprehensive guidance on raising standards in clinical research and evidence synthesis. A completed CONSORT 2010 checklist is provided as a Supplementary file. The flow diagram of participant recruitment and follow-up, in accordance with CONSORT 2010, is presented in Fig. 1 (see Results section). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee and Institutional Review Board of our hospital (approval no.:2025kmykdx6f273) prior to patient enrollment. All enrolled patients (or their legal representatives) provided written informed consent after fully understanding the study purpose, methods, and potential risks, and the study was conducted in strict adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision).Fig. 1CONSORT 2010 flow diagram of a single-center randomized controlled trial comparing aspirin, low-molecular-weight heparin, and rivaroxaban for deep venous thrombosis prophylaxis after hip arthroplasty for femoral neck fracture

Study population

This single-center RCT was conducted at our institution between June 2024 and June 2025. We initially screened a total of 217 consecutive patients who underwent primary hip arthroplasty for acute femoral neck fracture during the study period.

Inclusion criteria: (1) patients aged ≥ > 50 years with acute femoral neck fracture (Garden III or IV type) confirmed by imaging; (2) undergoing primary unilateral THA or BHA; (3) no prior history of venous thromboembolism (VTE); (4) able to complete the 12-week follow-up as required. No restrictions were imposed on gender or body mass index (BMI).

Exclusion criteria: (1) prior history of VTE or ongoing long-term antithrombotic/antiplatelet treatment (including aspirin, clopidogrel, or oral anticoagulants) within 4 weeks before surgery; (2) history of bleeding disorders (e.g., intracranial hemorrhage or recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding within 6 months); (3) severe liver dysfunction (Child–Pugh class C); (4) severe renal dysfunction (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2); (5) coagulopathy or other absolute contraindications to anticoagulation; (6) pathological fracture or multiple trauma; (7) refusal to sign informed consent.

A total of 56 patients were excluded according to the above criteria: 32 patients for long-term preoperative antithrombotic/antiplatelet treatment, 11 for prior VTE history, 1 for history of bleeding disorders, 9 for severe liver/renal dysfunction, and 3 for coagulopathy, pathological fracture, or refusal to participate. Finally, 161 eligible patients with no preoperative antithrombotic exposure were enrolled and randomized in this study, including 112 patients undergoing THA and 49 patients undergoing BHA.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1 statistical software. Based on previous literature [7, 8], the expected DVT incidence without thromboprophylaxis was set at 10%. Assuming a noninferiority margin of 5% between groups, a two-tailed α = 0.05, and a β = 0.2 (statistical power = 80%), the estimated required sample size was 147 patients. To account for a 10% potential dropout rate, a total of 161 patients were enrolled. Post hoc power analysis was performed, indicating that the study had 62% power to detect a 5% difference in DVT incidence between groups, which was below the ideal 80% threshold owing to the lower-than-expected overall DVT incidence (7.45%).

Surgical technique and perioperative care

All surgical procedures were performed by the same senior orthopedic surgeon with more than 15 years of experience in joint surgery via a standard posterior approach. Eligible patients underwent either THA or BHA according to the patient’s age, activity level, bone quality, general condition, and patient/family preference. Prosthesis fixation type (cemented or uncemented) was selected on the basis of the patient’s bone quality: cemented prostheses were used for patients with severe osteoporosis, and uncemented prostheses were used for patients with good bone quality. Operative duration was defined as the time from skin incision to skin closure. All intraoperative complications (including periprosthetic fracture, neurovascular injury, and massive blood loss > 1000 mL), intraoperative blood loss, and infusion volume were prospectively recorded in the patient’s medical record.

Standard perioperative care included intravenous antibiotic prophylaxis (second-generation cephalosporin, administered within 30 min before incision) and multimodal analgesia (oral acetaminophen combined with tramadol, with patient-controlled analgesia for the first 24 h postoperatively).

Standardized mechanical DVT prophylaxis: all patients received bilateral intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) twice daily (2 h per session) from postoperative day 1 until full ambulation, as well as graduated compression stockings during hospitalization. No additional mechanical prophylaxis was used after discharge.

Unified postoperative rehabilitation protocol: immediately after regaining consciousness from anesthesia, all patients underwent supervised active and passive functional training (including ankle plantar flexion/dorsiflexion, isometric quadriceps contraction, and knee extension). Out-of-bed ambulation with a walker was initiated as soon as the patient’s general condition and intraoperative prosthesis stability allowed. The time to first ambulation and full weight bearing was recorded for each patient.

Randomization and intervention

Eligible patients were randomly allocated to three groups at a 1:1:1 ratio using a computer-generated random number table with block randomization (block size = 6) to maintain group balance. The distribution of THA and BHA was balanced among the three groups. Allocation concealment was implemented using sealed, opaque envelopes, and the randomization sequence was not accessible to the operating surgeon and nursing staff. The intervention regimens were as follows:

Aspirin group (n = 50): 100 mg oral aspirin administered twice daily, initiated 12–24 h postoperatively after confirming no active bleeding, for a consecutive 6-week course. This regimen was selected in accordance with Chinese guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of elderly femoral neck fractures [2], which recommend 100 mg aspirin twice daily for extended thromboprophylaxis in high-risk elderly patients to ensure adequate and sustained antiplatelet effect.

LMWH group (n = 58): 4000 IU enoxaparin sodium administered subcutaneously once daily, initiated 12–24 h postoperatively, for 14 consecutive days (ACCP guideline-recommended standard course for hip arthroplasty [1]).

Rivaroxaban group (n = 53): 10 mg oral rivaroxaban administered once daily, initiated 12–24 h postoperatively, for 14 consecutive days (standard labeled dose and course for thromboprophylaxis after hip arthroplasty).

Outcome measures and follow-up

Primary and secondary outcomes were predefined for this study. The primary outcome was the incidence of lower extremity deep vein thrombosis (DVT) during the 12-week follow-up period, wherein DVT was defined as the presence of intraluminal filling defect on color Doppler ultrasound and further classified into distal (muscular calf vein) DVT and proximal (popliteal vein and above) DVT. All patients underwent routine bilateral lower extremity color Doppler ultrasound at the 12-week follow-up regardless of clinical symptoms, and patients with suspected DVT at the 6-week and 8-week follow-ups, as identified by clinical symptoms including lower limb swelling, pain, or warmth, received prompt ultrasound confirmation. All ultrasound examinations were performed and interpreted by two independent radiologists who were blinded to the group allocation.

The secondary outcome was the incidence of bleeding-related complications within 12 weeks postoperatively, which were graded in accordance with the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) bleeding criteria: major bleeding was defined as fatal bleeding, bleeding in a critical organ, bleeding requiring transfusion of > 2 units of red blood cells, or bleeding requiring surgical reintervention; minor bleeding was defined as all other bleeding events that did not meet the criteria for major bleeding, including incision ecchymosis, noninterventional superficial hematoma, and microscopic hematuria.

All patients completed follow-up at 6, 8, and 12 weeks postoperatively via outpatient visits, and bleeding complications were documented and graded by the attending orthopedic surgeon at each follow-up visit.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{\mathrm{x} }$$\end{document}x¯ ± s), with intergroup comparisons performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Qualitative data are expressed as frequencies and percentages, with comparisons conducted using the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test (when the expected frequency was < 5).

Prespecified subgroup analysis based on surgical type (THA versus BHA) was performed for both primary and secondary outcomes. Continuous variables between the two surgical subgroups were compared using independent samples t-test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test when the expected frequency was < 5.

For pairwise comparisons of bleeding complications among the three groups, Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons, with the adjusted significance level set to α = 0.0167 (0.05/3 pairwise comparisons). A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for overall comparisons. Post hoc power analysis was conducted to assess the statistical power of the primary outcome.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 161 patients were enrolled and randomized into three groups: aspirin group (n = 50), LMWH group (n = 58), and rivaroxaban group (n = 53). No patients were lost to follow-up or withdrew from the study during the 12-week follow-up period. Discussion

This single-center RCT compared the efficacy and safety of three first-line antithrombotic agents—aspirin, LMWH, and rivaroxaban—for DVT prophylaxis after hip arthroplasty for femoral neck fracture, with prespecified subgroup analysis based on THA and BHA. Standardized surgical procedures, perioperative care, mechanical prophylaxis, and rehabilitation protocols were implemented to minimize confounding variables. This study demonstrates that all three agents effectively reduced the incidence of DVT to an overall rate of 7.45%, which is considerably lower than that in untreated patients, with no significant intergroup differences in efficacy observed in either the overall cohort or THA/BHA subgroups; meanwhile, rivaroxaban was associated with a significantly higher incidence of bleeding complications than aspirin and LMWH across both surgical procedures, whereas aspirin and LMWH exhibited comparable and favorable safety profiles in both THA and BHA patients, thereby providing valuable clinical implications for thromboprophylaxis in this high-risk elderly population.

Efficacy of antithrombotic agents in different surgical types

The overall DVT incidence of 7.45% in our study is substantially lower than the 40–84% reported in untreated patients [1], confirming the critical role of combined pharmacological and mechanical thromboprophylaxis, plus early standardized mobilization, in reducing DVT risk after hip arthroplasty. Notably, there was no significant difference in DVT incidence between the three groups in both THA and BHA subgroups, which is consistent with previous large-scale clinical studies.

Jameson et al. [7] conducted a study of 108,584 THA cases and found similar DVT rates between aspirin and LMWH (0.99% versus 0.94%, P = 0.23), while multiple phase III trials have shown that rivaroxaban is noninferior to LMWH in DVT prevention after THA [8, 12]. For patients undergoing BHA , previous meta-analyses also confirmed that aspirin, LMWH, and rivaroxaban have equivalent thromboprophylaxis efficacy [5], which is consistent with our BHA subgroup results. Although THA was associated with significantly greater surgical trauma and longer operative duration than BHA in our study, there was no significant difference in DVT incidence between the two surgical types, which may be attributed to the standardized combined prophylaxis protocol and early mobilization that effectively offset the increased thrombotic risk associated with more invasive surgery.

Notably, the aspirin group received a 6-week extended course of prophylaxis, while the LMWH and rivaroxaban groups received a standard 14-day course. Despite the longer treatment course, the aspirin group did not show an increased bleeding risk, and its DVT prevention efficacy was comparable to the other two agents, which further supports the safety of aspirin for extended thromboprophylaxis after hip arthroplasty in elderly patients. The difference in treatment course may be a potential confounding factor, and the noninferiority of short-course LMWH/rivaroxaban to long-course aspirin needs to be verified in future head-to-head trials.

These findings collectively suggest that aspirin, LMWH, and rivaroxaban have equivalent efficacy in reducing thrombotic risk in both THA and BHA patients with femoral neck fracture, and the choice of agent should be guided primarily by safety profile, patient characteristics, and clinical context. The equivalent efficacy of aspirin to LMWH and rivaroxaban is particularly clinically meaningful. Aspirin is a low-cost, oral antiplatelet agent with wide availability and good long-term adherence, making it an attractive option for both THA and BHA patients, especially for elderly, frail patients undergoing BHA who cannot tolerate injectable medications or require extended prophylaxis after discharge. However, it should be noted that aspirin’s efficacy may be influenced by patient adherence (twice-daily dosing) and individual response, and it should be used with caution in patients with a history of gastrointestinal ulcers or bleeding disorders.

Safety profile: bleeding risk in different surgical types

The present study identified a significantly higher bleeding risk with rivaroxaban in both THA and BHA subgroups. In THA patients, the bleeding rate of rivaroxaban (24.32%) was nearly fivefold higher than that of aspirin (5.88%) and LMWH (4.88%), and a similar trend was observed in the less traumatic BHA subgroup (18.75% versus 0.00% and 5.88%). These findings are consistent with previous real-world studies demonstrating higher wound complications and hidden blood loss with rivaroxaban compared with LMWH or enoxaparin. Such discrepancies from phase III trials may be explained by the younger and healthier populations enrolled in controlled studies, whereas our cohort consisted of elderly, comorbid patients at elevated bleeding risk; moreover, the 12-week follow-up enabled identification of late bleeding events that might be overlooked in shorter investigations. Accordingly, rivaroxaban should be used cautiously in elderly patients with femoral neck fractures regardless of surgical procedure [15, 16].

The increased bleeding tendency of rivaroxaban is mainly attributed to its potent and predictable factor Xa inhibition, which may raise the risk of minor bleeding in elderly individuals with impaired coagulation reserve, especially given the limited availability of its specific reversal agent during the study period. Notably, extended 6-week aspirin prophylaxis exhibited a favorable safety profile comparable to 14-day LMWH without increased bleeding risk. Furthermore, despite greater surgical trauma in THA, overall bleeding rates were similar between THA and BHA subgroups, likely owing to standardized hemostasis, controlled hypotensive anesthesia, and uniform postoperative anticoagulation timing, which effectively reduced procedure-related bleeding risk [8, 12].

Clinical implications and recommendations

Based on the results of this study, combined with current international guidelines and surgical type differences, we propose the following clinical recommendations for DVT prophylaxis after hip arthroplasty for femoral neck fracture:

LMWH is recommended as the first-line agent for both THA and BHA patients, owing to its well-balanced efficacy and safety profiles. It is recommended by multiple guidelines such as Antithrombotic Therapy and Prevention of Thrombosis, ninth edition (ACCP-9) [1], with a proven track record of reducing DVT risk with a low bleeding rate in both surgical types. Its once-daily subcutaneous dosing ensures good adherence during hospitalization, and it can be easily switched to oral agents for long-term prophylaxis after discharge.

Aspirin is a suitable alternative for low DVT risk patients (e.g., young, healthy THA patients with uncomplicated surgery and early mobilization, or elderly BHA patients with limited activity) or those with contraindications to LMWH or rivaroxaban (e.g., heparin-induced thrombocytopenia or severe renal dysfunction). It is also a cost-effective option for extended long-term prophylaxis after discharge for both surgical types.

Rivaroxaban should be used with extreme caution in both THA and BHA elderly patients with femoral neck fracture. It may be considered only in patients who cannot tolerate LMWH or aspirin, with close monitoring for bleeding complications throughout the treatment period. Its convenient oral dosing must be carefully weighed against its significantly higher bleeding risk in this vulnerable population.

We also propose a hybrid prophylaxis strategy for high-risk THA patients: LMWH during hospitalization (to ensure rapid onset of action and close monitoring) followed by long-term oral aspirin after discharge (to maintain prophylaxis with low bleeding risk). This strategy may optimize the benefit–risk balance, and future large-scale RCTs are needed to validate its efficacy and safety in different surgical types.

Study strengths and limitations

This randomized controlled trial (RCT) presents clear methodological strengths and direct clinical relevance across four core dimensions. First, its single-center design, with standardized surgical procedures (completed by a single senior surgeon), perioperative care, mechanical prophylaxis, and rehabilitation protocols, minimized confounding and ensured high internal validity. Second, it enrolled a homogeneous cohort of elderly patients with femoral neck fracture, a group at dual high risk of thrombosis and bleeding, lending direct clinical applicability to the findings. Third, it conducted a head-to-head comparison of three first-line antithrombotic agents, with prespecified subgroup analyses stratified by total hip arthroplasty (THA) and bipolar hemiarthroplasty (BHA), filling a critical evidence gap in this specific population. Finally, standardized blinded deep vein thrombosis (DVT) ultrasound assessment and International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) bleeding grading criteria were implemented, ensuring objective and accurate outcome measurement.

Nevertheless, several non-negligible limitations must be considered when interpreting the results. First, the single-center design and modest sample size (n = 161), particularly the underpowered BHA subgroup (n = 49), limited the statistical robustness of subgroup analyses; multicenter large-scale trials are therefore required to validate these findings. Furthermore, the 12-week follow-up may fail to capture late-onset DVT or bleeding events beyond 3 months postoperatively, so extended follow-up (6 months to 1 year) is recommended for future research. In addition, excluding patients on long-term preoperative antiplatelet/antithrombotic therapy may restrict the generalizability of the results to this common clinical subgroup. Meanwhile, patient-reported functional outcomes and cost-effectiveness—core factors for clinical decision-making—were not evaluated. Additionally, discrepancies in intervention duration across the three groups may introduce confounding bias. Of note, the exploratory surgery-based subgroup analysis was unadjusted for multiple comparisons, carrying an inherent type I error risk. Finally, routine DVT ultrasound was only performed at the 12-week postoperative endpoint, potentially missing transient asymptomatic DVT events that resolved spontaneously between 6 and 8 weeks prior to final follow-up. Additionally, this trial was not prospectively registered in a public clinical trials registry, which is a limitation.
