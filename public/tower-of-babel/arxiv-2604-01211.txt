Making Every Bit Count for A A -Optimal State Estimation
Cameron Khanpour, Daniel Turizo, Samuel Talkington

Abstract
We study the problem of controlling how a limited communication bandwidth budget is allocated across heterogeneously quantized sensor measurements. The performance criterion is the trace of the error covariance matrix of the linear minimum mean square error (LMMSE) state estimator, i.e., an A A -optimal design criterion. Minimizing this criterion with a bit budget constraint yields a nonconvex optimization problem. We derive a formula that reduces each evaluation of the gradient to a single Cholesky factorization. This enables efficient optimization by both a projection-free Frank–Wolfe method (with a computable convergence certificate) and an interior point method with L-BFGS Hessian approximation over the problem’s continuous relaxation. A largest remainder rounding procedure recovers integer bit allocations with a bound on the quality of the rounded solution. Numerical experiments in IEEE power grid test cases with up to 300 buses compare both solvers and demonstrate that the analytic gradient is the key computational enabler for both methods. Additionally, the heterogeneous bit allocation is compared to standard uniform bit allocation on the 500 bus IEEE power grid test case.



I
Introduction

Analog-to-digital
quantization
methods improve computational and communication efficiency by mapping continuous measurements to discrete intervals
[
12
]
. While coarse quantization accelerates computations
[
11
]
, it introduces
nonlinear
, typically
non-Gaussian
, measurement noise
[
23
,
28
]
, which contrasts with the additive Gaussian noise assumptions that are frequently used for analyzing state estimation algorithms.

At the same time, sensing technologies are increasingly widespread in modern engineering practice. For example, advanced metering infrastructure (AMI) has become widespread in electric power distribution infrastructure, where
variable precision
sensing has emerged as a promising operational paradigm for communication-constrained sensing. To address these challenges, we cast heterogeneous quantizer bit allocation under a global bandwidth budget as an
A
-optimal design problem for linear minimum mean square error (LMMSE) estimation—i.e., minimizing the trace of the covariance matrix of the error of the LMMSE estimator. The resource allocation acts on measurement precisions and induces a nonconvex optimization problem that falls outside the scope of existing sensor selection methods.

Another component of this work is the incorporation of the effects of quantization and communication bandwidth in state estimation. Quantization has a significant effect on the accuracy of state estimation in scenarios limited by bandwidth. In communication-constrained settings—such as remote monitoring over low-bandwidth links or large-scale sensor networks with shared communication channels—the number of bits allocated to each measurement directly determines the quantization noise variance and, consequently, the estimation quality. Contributions

In this paper, we study optimal bit allocation for LMMSE state estimation under a global communication budget. We formulate the problem as a nonconvex
A
-optimal design problem with the following contributions:

1.

We present two algorithms: i.) A first-order Frank–Wolfe algorithm with a closed-form linear minimization oracle (Proposition
3
), and show that the minimum Frank–Wolfe gap over iterates converges to zero at rate
O(1/\sqrt{T})
(Theorem
1
), providing a computable convergence certificate. ii.) A second-order interior point algorithm. We derive a closed-form gradient for both FW and the interior point method to reduce each evaluation to a single Cholesky factorization of the information matrix. Further, the interior point method is accelerated with a L-BFGS Hessian approximation and converges in very few iterations.

2.

We introduce a solver-agnostic largest remainder rounding procedure (Algorithm
2
) that maps continuous relaxations to integer allocations that are guaranteed to be feasible and have bounded solution quality gap.

3.

We evaluate the speed and solution quality of the algorithms in the practical setting of experimental design for power network state estimation within different regimes (“sensor rich” and limited bandwidth). We also validate the problem formulation of heterogeneous bit allocation to a standard uniform bit allocation, achieving up to
53\%
improvement in the limited bandwidth regime.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
II

IV

V
reports numerical experiments on IEEE power system test cases, comparing solver performance and evaluating the benefits of heterogeneous allocation relative to uniform allocation. Finally, Section
VI

II-A

Measurement Model and Estimation

Given a fixed sensing matrix
\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
whose rows are
\{\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, where each
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, corresponding to
m
linear projections of an unknown state vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we consider heterogeneously quantized measurements with quantization bin widths
\{\Delta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
. We assume throughout that
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
for all
i
, i.e., every sensor observes a nontrivial linear combination of the state.

Each quantized measurement
i
is generated from a uniformly dithered quantization function
\mathcal{Q}_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
(see
[
12
,
28
]
) with bin width
\Delta_{i}>0
such that

\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\left\langle\boldsymbol{h}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle)=\Delta_{i}\cdot\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\left\langle\boldsymbol{h}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle+\tau_{i}}{\Delta_{i}}\right\rfloor+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(1)

where
\tau_{i}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(-\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2},\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2})
, and
\operatorname{E}_{\tau_{i}}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\left\langle\boldsymbol{h}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle)\,|\,\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\right]=\left\langle\boldsymbol{h}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle
, i.e.,
\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\left\langle\boldsymbol{h}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle)
is conditionally unbiased. The independent dither
\tau_{i}
is noise purposely applied prior to quantization; see
[
12
,
13
]
.
The quantization error is well approximated by the additive model

\boldsymbol{y}:=\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z},

(2)

where
\operatorname{E}\left[\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\right]=\frac{1}{12}\operatorname{diag}(\Delta_{1}^{2},\ldots,\Delta_{m}^{2})
. Set
d_{i}:=\Delta_{i}^{2}/12
and
\boldsymbol{D}:=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{m})
. Suppose
\boldsymbol{x}
is zero-mean with prior covariance
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\succ 0
. The LMMSE state estimator is

\boldsymbol{\hat{x}}=\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{D}\right)^{-1}\!\boldsymbol{y},

(3)

with error covariance

\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}=\left(\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\boldsymbol{H}+\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\!.

(4)

Quantization error is generally signal-dependent, so an additive noise model can be inaccurate, especially at coarse resolutions
[
12
]
. Our use of uniformly dithered quantizers in (
1
) is intended to mitigate this dependence and justify the covariance model
[
13
]
. Accordingly, the optimization problem below should be interpreted as the
A
-optimal design problem induced by this dithered additive noise LMMSE model.

II-B

Bandwidth Allocation Problem

We allocate a limited number of quantization bits across
m
channels to minimize the MSE. For a
b_{i}
-bit uniform quantizer with dynamic range
R_{i}
,

\Delta_{i}=\frac{R_{i}}{2^{b_{i}}},\quad d_{i}=\frac{R_{i}^{2}}{12\cdot 4^{b_{i}}},\quad\rho_{i}:=d_{i}^{-1}=\kappa_{i}\,4^{b_{i}},

(5)

where
\kappa_{i}:=12/R_{i}^{2}
. Allocating more bits to channel
i
increases its precision exponentially. The integer program is

\min_{\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}}\quad\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{b})\bigr)\quad\operatorname{\sf s.t.}\quad\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}\leq B.

(6)

The standard continuous relaxation replaces
\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}
by
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
. Define
\mathcal{B}:=\{\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}:\ \boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}\leq B\}
and write

\boldsymbol{M}(\boldsymbol{b}):=\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{b}))\boldsymbol{H},\quad\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{b}):=\boldsymbol{M}(\boldsymbol{b})^{-1}.

(7)

The relaxed problem is

\min_{\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\quad F(\boldsymbol{b}):=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{b})\bigr).

(8)

The feasible set
\mathcal{B}
is compact and convex, but
F
is generally nonconvex in the bit variables due to the exponential reparameterization
\rho_{i}=\kappa_{i}4^{b_{i}}
. Introducing the auxiliary function
f(\boldsymbol{\rho}):=\operatorname{tr}((\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\boldsymbol{H})^{-1})
in precision variables, we have
F(\boldsymbol{b})=f(\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{b}))
. While
f
is convex in
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, the chain rule yields

\begin{split}\nabla^{2}F(\boldsymbol{b})=(\ln 4)^{2}&\Bigl[\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{\rho})\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\\
&+\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\rho}\odot\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\rho})\bigr)\Bigr],\end{split}

(9)

where the second term is negative semidefinite (since
\nabla f\leq\boldsymbol{0}
componentwise), so convexity does not transfer from
\boldsymbol{\rho}
to
\boldsymbol{b}
.

Remark

(Convexity in precision space)
.

Although
F(\boldsymbol{b})
is nonconvex in the bit variables, the function

f(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\operatorname{tr}\bigl((\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\boldsymbol{H})^{-1}\bigr)

is convex in the precision variables
\boldsymbol{\rho}
.
However, the budget constraint
\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}\leq B
becomes

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log_{4}(\rho_{i}/\kappa_{i})\leq B
in
\boldsymbol{\rho}
-space, which is

not
convex (it is the sublevel set of a concave function).
Thus, the
\boldsymbol{b}
-space formulation trades a nonconvex objective for a convex
(polyhedral) feasible set, which is precisely what enables the Frank–Wolfe
method with a closed-form linear minimization oracle.
This tradeoff is complementary to the dual formulation
of
[
6
]
, which achieves a convex objective
at the cost of a more complex constraint geometry.

Lemma 1

(Gradient with respect to bits)
.

For each measurement
i=1,\dots,m
,

\frac{\partial F}{\partial b_{i}}(\boldsymbol{b})=-(\ln 4)\,\rho_{i}(\boldsymbol{b})\,\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{b})^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}.

(10)

Equivalently,

\nabla F(\boldsymbol{b})=-(\ln 4)\,\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{b})\odot\left[\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{b})^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\right]_{i=1}^{m}.

(11)

In particular,
\partial F/\partial b_{i}<0
whenever
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
.
Each gradient evaluation reduces to a single Cholesky factorization of
\boldsymbol{M}(\boldsymbol{b})
: the diagonal entries
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}
are extracted column-by-column from
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}
without forming the full
m\times m
product, at cost
O(d^{2}m)
dominated by the
O(d^{3})
factorization when
m=O(d)
.

Proof:

Define

\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\rho}):=\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\boldsymbol{H},\quad\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\rho})^{-1}.

Since

\partial\boldsymbol{A}/\partial\rho_{i}=\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}=\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}
,
the derivative identity for matrix inverses gives

\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}{\partial\rho_{i}}=-\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{A}}{\partial\rho_{i}}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}=-\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}.

Taking traces,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho_{i}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}{\partial\rho_{i}}\right)=-\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\bigr)=-\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}.

Since
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\succ 0
, the quadratic form
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}
is nonnegative. Now
F(\boldsymbol{b})=f(\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{b}))
with
\rho_{i}(\boldsymbol{b})=\kappa_{i}4^{b_{i}}
, so by chain rule

\frac{\partial F}{\partial b_{i}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho_{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial\rho_{i}}{\partial b_{i}}=\left(-\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{b})^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\right)\cdot(\ln 4)\rho_{i}(\boldsymbol{b}),

which yields (
10
). The vector form (
11
) follows by stacking. If
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, then
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{b})^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}>0
because
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{b})\succ 0
, so the derivative is strictly negative.
∎

Proposition 1

(Budget saturation)
.

If
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
for all
i
and
\boldsymbol{b}^{\star}
minimizes (
8
), then
\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}^{\star}=B
.

Proof:

Consider any feasible
\boldsymbol{b}
such that
\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}<B
. Then
\boldsymbol{b}+t\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
for some index
i
and all sufficiently small
t>0
. By Lemma
1
,

\partial F/\partial b_{i}(\boldsymbol{b})<0
,
so increasing
b_{i}
decreases the objective, which means that
\boldsymbol{b}
is not a minimum. Conversely, if
\boldsymbol{b}^{\star}
minimizes (
8
), we must have that
\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}^{\star}=B
.
∎

Proposition 2

(Lipschitz continuity of the gradient)
.

The function
F
is
C^{\infty}
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\nabla F
is
L
-Lipschitz on
\mathcal{B}
with

L=(\ln 4)^{2}\,\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}\,(2m+1).

(12)

Proof:

Recall that

F(\boldsymbol{b})=f(\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{b}))
and

\rho_{i}(\boldsymbol{b})=\kappa_{i}4^{b_{i}},

where

f(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\operatorname{tr}\Bigl(\bigl(\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\boldsymbol{H}\bigr)^{-1}\Bigr).

Since each coordinate map
\boldsymbol{b}\mapsto\rho_{i}(\boldsymbol{b})=\kappa_{i}4^{b_{i}}
is smooth and strictly positive on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and matrix inversion is smooth on the positive definite cone,
F
is
C^{\infty}
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Since
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{b})\succ 0
for all
\boldsymbol{b}
, and

\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{b})\preceq\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

implies

\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{b})\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}.

From Lemma
1
,

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=-\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}.

Differentiating once more with respect to
\rho_{j}
gives

\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\rho_{j}\partial\rho_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=2\,\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{j}\boldsymbol{h}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\bigr).

(13)

Fix
i
, and write

\boldsymbol{A}_{i}:=\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{-1}+\sum_{k\neq i}\rho_{k}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\top}\succ 0.

By the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury identity,

\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}=\boldsymbol{A}_{i}^{-1}-\frac{\rho_{i}\,\boldsymbol{A}_{i}^{-1}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}_{i}^{-1}}{1+\rho_{i}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}_{i}^{-1}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}}.

Hence, with

\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}_{i}^{-1}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\geq 0,

we obtain

\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}=\frac{\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}_{i}^{-1}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}}{1+\rho_{i}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}_{i}^{-1}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{\rho_{i}}.

Therefore

\rho_{i}\,\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\leq 1.

(14)

Since
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\succeq 0
and
\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}
, we have

\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}\preceq\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\|_{2}\,\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\preceq\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}.

Thus

\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\leq\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}\,\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}.

Using (
14
),

\rho_{i}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho_{i}}\right|=\rho_{i}\,\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\leq\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}\,\rho_{i}\,\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\leq\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}.

Therefore

\rho_{i}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho_{i}}\right|\leq\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}.

(15)

From (
13
),

\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\rho_{j}\partial\rho_{i}}=2\,(\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{j})(\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{j}).

Since
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\succeq 0
, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in the
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
-inner product yields

|\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{j}|\leq\bigl(\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\bigr)^{1/2}\bigl(\boldsymbol{h}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{j}\bigr)^{1/2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho_{i}\rho_{j}}},

where the last step uses (
14
). Since
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}\preceq\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
,

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\bigr)^{1/2}\bigl(\boldsymbol{h}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}\boldsymbol{h}_{j}\bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}\bigl(\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\bigr)^{1/2}\bigl(\boldsymbol{h}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{j}\bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{\rho_{i}\rho_{j}}}.

Combining the two bounds,

\left|\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\rho_{j}\partial\rho_{i}}\right|\leq\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}}{\rho_{i}\rho_{j}},

and therefore

\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\rho_{j}\partial\rho_{i}}\right|\leq 2\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}.

(16)

From the chain rule with
\delta_{ij}
denoting the Kronecker delta,

\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial b_{j}\partial b_{i}}=(\ln 4)^{2}\left[\delta_{ij}\,\rho_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho_{i}}+\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\rho_{j}\partial\rho_{i}}\right].

(17)

Using (
15
) and (
16
),

\left|\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial b_{j}\partial b_{i}}\right|\leq(\ln 4)^{2}\bigl[\delta_{ij}\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}+2\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}\bigr].

Hence, for each fixed
i
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial b_{j}\partial b_{i}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq(\ln 4)^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl[\delta_{ij}\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}+2\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=(\ln 4)^{2}\,\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}\,(2m+1)=:L.

Thus

\|\nabla^{2}F(\boldsymbol{b})\|_{2}\leq\|\nabla^{2}F(\boldsymbol{b})\|_{\infty}\leq(\ln 4)^{2}\,\|\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}\,(2m+1)

for all
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathcal{B}
. Since the Hessian is uniformly bounded on the convex set
\mathcal{B}
, the mean value theorem implies that
\nabla F
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{B}
.
∎

III
Bit Allocation Procedure

We solve the relaxed problem (
8
) using the Frank–Wolfe (FW) method
[
10
]
; see
[
14
,
24
]
for introductions. Unlike the convex setting of
[
1
]
, our objective is nonconvex in
\boldsymbol{b}
, so we apply the nonconvex FW analysis of
[
18
]
. FW is well suited because the feasible set is compact and convex, the objective is smooth, and the linear minimization oracle (LMO) admits a closed-form solution.

III-A

Gradient and Linear Minimization Oracle

Given a feasible iterate
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathcal{B}
, the FW linear minimization oracle solves

\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{b})\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{B}}\left\langle\nabla F(\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{s}\right\rangle.

(18)

The FW direction is
\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{b}):=\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{b})-\boldsymbol{b}
, and the FW gap is

g_{\mathrm{FW}}(\boldsymbol{b}):=\max_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{B}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{s},\nabla F(\boldsymbol{b})\right\rangle=-\left\langle\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{b}),\nabla F(\boldsymbol{b})\right\rangle.

(19)

As usual,
g_{\mathrm{FW}}(\boldsymbol{b})\geq 0
, with equality if and only if
\boldsymbol{b}
is a first-order stationary point.

Proposition 3

(Closed-form LMO)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{g}:=\nabla F(\boldsymbol{b})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then an optimal solution of (
18
) is

\boldsymbol{s}^{\star}=\begin{cases}B\,\boldsymbol{e}_{i^{\star}},&\text{if }\min_{i}g_{i}<0\text{ and }i^{\star}\in\argmin_{i}g_{i},\\
\boldsymbol{0},&\text{if }g_{i}\geq 0\text{ for all }i.\end{cases}

(20)

Under the standing assumption
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
for all
i
(so
\boldsymbol{g}<\boldsymbol{0}
by Lemma
1
), this simplifies to

\boldsymbol{s}^{\star}=B\,\boldsymbol{e}_{i^{\star}},\quad i^{\star}\in\argmin_{1\leq i\leq m}[\nabla F(\boldsymbol{b})]_{i}.

(21)

Proof:

The feasible set
\mathcal{B}
is a polytope with extreme points
\boldsymbol{0},B\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\dots,B\boldsymbol{e}_{m}
. Since
\boldsymbol{s}\mapsto\left\langle\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{s}\right\rangle

Corollary 3.1

(Explicit Frank–Wolfe gap)
.

Under the assumption
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
for all

g_{\mathrm{FW}}(\boldsymbol{b})=\left\langle\boldsymbol{b},\nabla F(\boldsymbol{b})\right\rangle-B\min_{1\leq i\leq m}[\nabla F(\boldsymbol{b})]_{i}.

Proof:

By definition of FW gap in (

.

III-C

Interior Point Method with Analytic Gradient

The gradient formula from Lemma
1
can also be supplied to general purpose nonlinear programming solvers. We use the interior point solver Ipopt
[
29
]
via JuMP
[
8
]
with the option
hessian_approximation = ‘‘limited-memory’’
, which activates an L-BFGS Hessian approximation. Ipopt replaces the constrained problem (
8
) with a sequence of log-barrier subproblems

\min_{\boldsymbol{b}}\;F(\boldsymbol{b})-\mu\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ln b_{i}-\mu\ln\!\bigl(B-\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}\bigr),

where
\mu>0
is driven to zero. Each subproblem is solved by a damped Newton method on the primal-dual KKT system. Since the constraint structure is simple (
m
bound constraints plus one linear budget constraint), the KKT system is inexpensive to solve once the gradient is available.

The Hessian
\nabla^{2}F(\boldsymbol{b})
is costly to compute exactly. Instead, we use the limited-memory BFGS (L-BFGS) approximation, which maintains a low-rank estimate from recent gradient differences. With L-BFGS, each iteration requires only the gradient
\nabla F(\boldsymbol{b})
from Lemma
1
—the same
O(d^{3})
Cholesky factorization used by Frank–Wolfe—plus
O(m)
work for the L-BFGS update and KKT solve.

Remark

(Comparison of per-iteration costs)
.

Both Frank–Wolfe and the interior point method are bottlenecked by the same
O(d^{3})
Cholesky factorization for the gradient. FW adds an
O(m)
LMO step, whereas Ipopt adds an
O(m)
KKT solve. The key difference is iteration count; while FW and interior point methods both globally converge sublinearly at rate
O(1/\sqrt{T})
, IPMs also exhibit local superlinear convergence, typically requiring 20–50 iterations. FW provides a computable convergence certificate (the FW gap), while Ipopt reports KKT residuals.

The relaxed problem (

\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}=B

,

Similarly to the Quota Method for apportionment
[

to round up the

and round the rest down. Equivalently,

\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\argmax_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{m}\\
\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}=R_{\mathrm{rem}}\end{subarray}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rangle,\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}:=\lfloor\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\rfloor+\boldsymbol{\xi}.

Proposition 4

(Feasibility and nearest point property of largest remainder rounding
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{B}
satisfy
\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}=B
, and define

\boldsymbol{r}=\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}-\lfloor\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\rfloor
and
R_{\mathrm{rem}}=\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{r}
.
Then
\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}
defined by (
24
)
belongs to
\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}
, satisfies
\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}=B
, and solves

\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m},\\
\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}=\lfloor\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\rfloor+\boldsymbol{\xi}\end{subarray}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\|_{2}^{2}\ \text{\rm s.t. }\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{m},\ \boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}=R_{\mathrm{rem}}.

(25)

Moreover,

\|\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}(1-r_{i}).

(26)

Proof:

By construction,
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
and

\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}=R_{\mathrm{rem}}
, so

\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}=\lfloor\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\rfloor+\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}

and

\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}=\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\lfloor\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\rfloor+\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}=(B-R_{\mathrm{rem}})+R_{\mathrm{rem}}=B.

Now let
\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}=\lfloor\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\rfloor+\boldsymbol{\xi}
with

\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
and
\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}=R_{\mathrm{rem}}
. Since

\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}=\lfloor\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\rfloor+\boldsymbol{r}
, we obtain

\|\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{r}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{r}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}-2\left\langle\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rangle.

Because
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
with
\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}=R_{\mathrm{rem}}
, we have

\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}=R_{\mathrm{rem}}
. Hence

\|\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{r}\|_{2}^{2}+R_{\mathrm{rem}}-2\left\langle\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rangle.

Therefore minimizing
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\|_{2}^{2}
over all feasible

\boldsymbol{\xi}
is equivalent to maximizing
\left\langle\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rangle
, which proves (
25
).

For (
26
), note that

\boldsymbol{r}\in[0,1]^{m}
and
\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{r}=R_{\mathrm{rem}}
, so
\boldsymbol{r}
is feasible for the
continuous relaxation of the maximization problem in (
24
). Hence

\left\langle\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}\right\rangle=\|\boldsymbol{r}\|_{2}^{2}.

Substituting into the norm identity above gives

\displaystyle\|\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{r}\|_{2}^{2}+R_{\mathrm{rem}}-2\left\langle\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq R_{\mathrm{rem}}-\|\boldsymbol{r}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}(1-r_{i}),

as claimed.
∎

Theorem 2

(Rounding gap for largest remainder rounding)
.

Assume
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
for all
i
, and let
\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{B}

satisfy
\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}=B
. Suppose
\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}
is a KKT point of the relaxed problem (
8
). Then the largest remainder rounding

\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}
defined by (
24
) satisfies

F(\hat{\boldsymbol{b}})\leq F(\bar{\boldsymbol{b}})+\frac{L}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}(1-r_{i}).

(27)

In particular,

F(\hat{\boldsymbol{b}})\leq F(\bar{\boldsymbol{b}})+\frac{L}{2}\min\left\{R_{\mathrm{rem}},m/4\right\}.

(28)

Proof:

Since
\bar{\boldsymbol{b}},\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}
is convex,

L
-smoothness of
F
on
\mathcal{B}
gives the following quadratic upper bound

F(\hat{\boldsymbol{b}})\leq F(\bar{\boldsymbol{b}})+\left\langle\nabla F(\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}),\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(29)

Let

\boldsymbol{\delta}:=\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}=\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{r}.

Since
\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{r}=R_{\mathrm{rem}}
, we have

\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}=0
.
Let
(\lambda,\boldsymbol{\mu})
be KKT multipliers for the constraints

\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}\leq B
and
\boldsymbol{b}\geq\boldsymbol{0}
, so

\nabla F(\bar{\boldsymbol{b}})+\lambda\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\lambda\geq 0,\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}\geq\boldsymbol{0},\quad\mu_{i}\bar{b}_{i}=0\quad\forall i.

If
r_{i}>0
, then
\bar{b}_{i}>0
, so complementary slackness gives
\mu_{i}=0
.
If
r_{i}=0
, then
\delta_{i}=\xi_{i}-r_{i}=0
because largest remainder
rounding only rounds up coordinates with positive fractional part. Therefore

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla F(\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}),\boldsymbol{\delta}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(-\lambda+\mu_{i}\right)\delta_{i}

\displaystyle=-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{i}=-\lambda\,\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}=0.

Substituting into (
29
) and applying
Proposition
4
yields

F(\hat{\boldsymbol{b}})\leq F(\bar{\boldsymbol{b}})+\frac{L}{2}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq F(\bar{\boldsymbol{b}})+\frac{L}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}(1-r_{i}),

which proves (
27
). The simplified bound
follows from
r_{i}(1-r_{i})\leq r_{i}
and
r_{i}(1-r_{i})\leq 1/4
for each
i
.
∎

Remark
.

The bound in Theorem
2
requires KKT stationarity of
the relaxed solution, which is satisfied by interior point solutions.
Frank–Wolfe only guarantees a small FW gap, so the bound applies approximately. In practice, the bound depending on the global Lipschitz constant can be loose; some future work would be to tighten this bound using local Lipschitz constant or specific structural properties of a given problem’s sensing matrix such as sparsity.

VI
Conclusions

We proposed an optimal bit allocation method for state estimation with variable precision measurements. The method reduces the MSE of a state estimator by controlling how a limited number of bits are allocated to sensors.

Discussion

Two algorithms were proposed: a first-order and second-order method to solve the corresponding nonconvex optimization problem. The algorithms demonstrated the ability to heterogeneously allocate a limited bit budget across quantized measurements, significantly reducing the
A
-optimal design criterion for the state estimator. We derived a closed-form gradient formula that reduces computing the gradient to a single Cholesky factorization. We presented two methods that exploit this gradient: a Frank–Wolfe method with a closed-form LMO and guaranteed
O(1/\sqrt{T})
convergence rate guarantee in terms of the FW gap, and an interior point method with L-BFGS Hessian approximation. Numerical experiments on IEEE power grid test cases show that the interior point method converges in fewer iterations, as expected for a second-order method. Additionally, the Frank–Wolfe method, being a first order method compared to a second order interior point method, is more memory efficient, allowing it to scale to large problems where storing the L-BFGS Hessian itself becomes infeasible.

Future work

This work opens a wide array of future directions. This includes adding stochastic trace estimation for scaling to larger problem sizes, such as the classical Hutchinson estimator or more modern approaches
[
20
]
. This paper has thus far considered only an offline state estimation problem. Future work should extend these methods to dynamic bit allocation in time-varying systems. Another promising direction is the consideration of a broader class of objectives that measure communication cost and performance criteria complementary to the
A
-optimal design criterion. Future work will consider general classes of convex communication cost functionals, and applying decision-focused learning (DFL) principles to optimize over the dynamic ranges of the channels.
