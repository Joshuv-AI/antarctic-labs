Immune-related gene signature for predicting biochemical recurrence after RP in prostate cancer subtypes
Authors: Yiwei Lin#, Jiatong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognostic role of molecular subtypes of prostate cancer (PCa) patients is still unclear. And we speculated whether the change of immunologic and hallmark gene sets of adjacent notumor tissues could promote the BCR of PCa patients.MethodsWe intended to identify PCa subtypes and find significant gene sets based on the activity changes of immunologic and hallmark gene sets in tumor and nontumor tissues to predict biochemical recurrence (BCR)-free survival. In addition, cell-based assays were utilized to validate the function of the gene in PCa.ResultsWe calculated and analyzed these gene sets related to BCR in tumor and notumor tissues through gene set variation analysis (GSVA) and then found three clinically relevant subtypes of PCa by nonnegative matrix factorization method (NMF) in immunologic and hallmark gene sets. Patients with subtype 2 had better BCR-free survival than other subtypes. Using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator method (LASSO), two prognostic gene sets in tumor but not in notumor tissue were identified frequently. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that genes from these two significant gene sets closely correlated with cell cycle in tumor tissue. Then, we applied random forest (RF) and artificial neural network (ANN), to identify gene In review biomarkers in predicting BCR and we found 36 genes related to the high risk of BCR. CENPE is the only common gene and can be used as a prognostic gene of BCR. Cell-based assays validated that CENPF promoted tumor proliferation in PCa.ConclusionThree clinically relevant subtypes of PCa were identified. Two significant genes sets were related to the cell cycle and ATPase activity. And CENPF promotes tumor proliferation as an oncogene.Keywords: GSVA, ANN, Prostate cancer, BCR, Subtype

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most common malignant tumor in men. There are nearly 1.3 million new cases globally, and 41,600 patients die due to this tumor every year [1]. With the widespread popularity of early screening based on prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing, many early PCa patients have been found and received complementary treatments. For the treatment of localized PCa, the current standard treatment is radical prostatectomy (RP) or radical radiotherapy(RT) [2]. Unfortunately, nearly 20% of patients experience biochemical recurrence (BCR) after RP, defined as two consecutive rising PSA values > 0.2 ng/ml following RP [3]. BCR is an early evaluation indicator of the progression of PCa and an important predictor of poor prognosis in PCa patients [4]. For patients with locally advanced PCa or positive lymph nodes, adjuvant radiotherapy may be chosen after RP to reduce the risk of tumor recurrence. However, studies had shown that nearly 50% of these patients without undergoing adjuvant radiotherapy did not have BCR after follow-up five years [5]. Therefore, it is high time to find a significant indicator that can identify the high-risk population of BCR early after RP.

Due to the constant maturity and development of current high-throughput sequencing technologies, significant transcriptome profiling data of tumor tissues are used as prognostic factors in the clinic to predict the prognosis of different tumors. At present, there have been many studies linking the prognosis of PCa patients with gene signatures by using public database data and constructing related predictive models to identify PCa patients with a high risk of BCR after RP [6–8]. Although most PCa were adenocarcinomas in terms of clinical pathology, certain histological subtypes of PCa identified clinically do not belong to the adenocarcinoma category. These include ductal carcinoma, neuroendocrine carcinoma, and others. Patients with different histological subtypes had different prognoses [9–10]. Due to significant differences in histological subtypes, at the microscopic level, studies had found that within the immune microenvironment of prostate tumors, immune cells or certain stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment can alter the gene expression of tumor cells, thereby affecting their biological behavior [11–12]. However, there are few studies on identifying PCa subtype or prognostic gene sets based on activity changes of immunologic and hallmark gene sets in tumor and adjacent nontumor tissues. Most studies may focus on activity changes in gene levels in tumor tissues but ignore the correlation between genetic changes in adjacent non-tumor tissues and the prognosis of PCa patients.

In this study, we explored the changes in the activity of immunologic and hallmark gene sets in tumor tissues and adjacent nontumor tissues, and worked to find the significant gene sets closely related to BCR, and analyzed important pathways based on these gene sets, and we obtained some hub gens through Cytoscape. In addition, we combined random forest (RF) and artificial neural network (ANN) analysis to determine meaningful key genes related to BCR from the GEO database. Later, we found on common gene CENPF as molecular markers for predicting the high risk of BCR for PCa.

Methodology

Data collection

Transcriptome data from TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) were downloaded by GDCRNA tools (https://cancergenome.nih.gov). Gene expression profiling data and clinical data of tumor tissues (GSE25136,GSE26242, GSE46602, GSE70768 and GSE89317) was downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (http://www.NCBI.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database. Qualified gene expression profiling data in GEO have been selected according to the following criteria: the sample size of PCa tissues was ≥ 30 with full BCR-free survival time and survival status. A total of 4922 immunologic and hallmark genes sets were downloaded from Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp). Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) can estimate the relative enrichment of a gene set of interest over a sample population, which is used to observe the variation in the activity of a set of genes (e.g., a pathway) corresponding to a particular biological condition [13]. Gene ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis were carried out using the Gene Ontology website (http://www.geneontology.org/) and KEGG PATHWAY ( http://www.genome.jp/kegg) with a p-value < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted with R software (version 4.1.0). A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Heatmaps and classification of PCa samples were analyzed by the ComplexHeatmap package and CancerSubtypes package [14], respectively. Nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF) is a useful dimension reduction method, which could distinguish molecular patterns for high dimensional genomic data and make a powerful method for class discovery [14]. NMF package was applied to perform the NMF for the cancer genomics dataset. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression method was used to determine the potential predictive gene sets. Random Forest (RF) was used to classify the hub genes with the R package random forest [15]. According to the Gini coefficient method, the important genes were selected as candidate-specific genes. Artificial Neural Network (ANN) was used to calculate the weight of significant genes and then construct the neural network model [16]. The R package neuralnet was used for neural network analysis [16]. The area under Curve (AUC) was used to assess the sensitivity and specificity of the neural network model using R package pROC.

Cell culture and reagents

Prostate cancer cell lines DU145 and PC-3 were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Hyclone, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

RNA extraction and PCR assays

Total RNA of cell lines was extracted by RNAiso Plus Reagent (Takara) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed to generate cDNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) designed for quantitative PCR. Then, the quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech). β-actin was used as internal control for relative quantification using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. All data were analyzed using the Bio-Rad CFX96 Detection System(Hercules, CA, USA). All primers were obtained from Tsingke Bio (Beijing, China).

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK8) cell proliferation assay

Cell viability was evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay kit (Dojindo, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well. At scheduled time points defined by the experimental design, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. Following a 1-hour incubation in the dark, absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

EdU cell proliferation assay

The proliferation of cells was detected using EdU cell proliferation assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. About 4 × 103 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and maintained for 24 h before the assay. A total of EdU reagent (Beyotime, C0071S) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h to label the cells. After three washes with PBS, cells were fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for another 15 min, and then incubated with the click-reaction reagent for 30 min at room temperature in the dark environment. In all, 1× Hoechst33342 reagent was used to counterstain the nucleus. The result of staining was observed with a STELLARIS 5 Scanning Confocal Microscope.

Colony formation assays

For the colony formation assay, PC-3 and DU145 cells were seeded in 6-wells plates at a density of 600 cells per well and cultured for 2 weeks. The cell colonies were fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Servicebio) for another 20 min at room temperature.

Small interfering RNA

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) for knockdown were obtained from Tsingke Bio (Beijing, China). Following protocols, siRNA and vectors were transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000.

Results

The activity changes of immunologic and hallmark gene sets in PCa and adjacent nontumor samples in TCGA database

GSVA can be used to detect subtle pathway activity changes within an entire gene expression set. To comprehensively reveal the variation in the activity of immunologic and hallmark gene sets in PCa and adjacent nontumor samples, we first downloaded 4922 immunologic and hallmark genes sets from GSEA (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp) and gene expression profiling data from TCGA (N = 51, T = 490). Immunologic gene sets are defined by microarray gene expression data from immunologic studies, and hallmark gene sets are coherently expressed signatures derived from aggregating many MSigDB (Molecular Signatures Database) gene sets representing well-defined biological states or processes. Based on expression data of TCGA, the enrichment score (ES) of 4922 gene sets was calculated by GSVA (Fig. 1). Both in PCa and adjacent nontumor tissues, several gene sets appeared to classify samples into several categories. Based on the ESs of immunologic and hallmark gene sets in PCa and adjacent nontumor samples, we attempted to classify PCa patients into different subtypes. The Cox regression model was used to perform feature selection through the CancerSubtypes package and remained for further analysis. The optimal number of clusters (K) was generated (K = 3, Fig. 2A and D) using factoextra package. By NMF method, which is an effective dimension reduction method for cancer subtype identification, PCa patients were clustered into three distinct subtypes (Fig. 2E). Silhouette width value was 0.96 in silhouette width plots (Fig. 2C), suggesting a fine match between a PCa sample and its identified subtype compared with other subtypes. Interestingly, PCa patients with subtype 2 displayed better BCR-free survival, whereas subtype 1 and subtype 3 had poor prognoses (Fig. 2B).

Correlation between clinical characteristics and PCa subtypes

We further explored the correlation between clinical characteristics and PCa subtypes. PCa patients with subtype 2 tended to have lower BCR rates than patients with subtypes 1 and 3 (Fig. 3A; Table 1). To identify representative gene sets for each subtype, we calculated the differential ES of gene sets between each of the two subgroups and intersected them (|logFC|≥0.02 and adj. p-value < 0.05). In Fig. 3B, subtype 1, subtype 2 and subtype 3 had common 30 distinct gene sets. The correlation between representative gene sets and clinical characteristics is shown in Fig. 4: subtype 1 had lower ESs of one gene set in nontumor samples than that in other subtypes, such as N_HALLMARK_MYOGENESIS. Interestingly, subtype 1 had higher ESs of 29 gene sets in tumor samples than subtype 2 and subtype 3, such as T_GSE15750_DAY6_VS_DAY10_TRAF6KO_EFF_CD8_TCELL_UP. To identify our classification, we had to select another gene expression profiling data (GSE46602, N = 14, T = 36) with a different array platform. Interestingly, these representative gene sets also yielded similar subgroups. Cox regression model was used to perform feature selection through CancerSubtypes package. The optimal number of clusters (K) was generated (K = 3, Fig. 5A and D) using factoextra package. By NMF method, which is an effective dimension reduction method for cancer subtype identification, PCa patients were clustered into three distinct subtypes (Fig. 5E). Silhouette width value was 0.84 in silhouette width plots (Fig. 5C), suggesting a fine match between a PCa sample and its identified subtype compared with other subtypes. Interestingly, PCa patients with subtype 2 also displayed better BCR-free survival, whereas subtype 1 and subtype 3 had poor prognoses (Fig. 5B).

Function and pathway enrichment analyses

To figure out the mechanism of the two prognostic gene sets in prognosis, we extracted the genes contained in each gene set, and GO terms including biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF), as well as KEGG pathways, were enriched utilizing R package clusterProfiler. With p-value < 0.05 as the threshold, significantly enriched GO terms and pathways of the identified genes in tumor tissue were present in Fig. 7. In the GO-associated category, the most significantly enriched GO terms in BP were nuclear division (GO:0000280) with p-value = 2.63E-36, chromosome segregation (GO:0007059) with p-value = 1.76E-34, organelle fission (GO:0048285) with p-value = 3.46E-33, mitotic nuclear division (GO:0140014) with p-value = 7.19E-33, sister chromatid segregation (GO:0000819) with p-value = 2.88E-31 and in CC, the most significantly enriched GO terms were chromosomal region (GO:0098687) with p-value = 4.74E-27, spindle (GO:0005819) with p-value = 2.55E-23, condensed chromosome (GO:0000793) with p-value = 5.61E-18, chromosome, centromeric region (GO:0000775) with p-value = 2.90E-16, nuclear chromosome (GO:0000228) with p-value = 1.59E-14. In MF, catalytic activity, acting on DNA (GO:0140097) with p-value = 6.18E-09, tubulin binding (GO:0015631) with p-value = 1.17E-06, microtubule-binding (GO:0008017) with p-value = 1.24E-06, ATPase activity (GO:0016887) with p-value = 6.16E-06, transcription coregulator activity (GO:0003712) with p-value = 0.0161. Moreover, the identified DEGs were mainly enriched in Cell cycle (KEGG: hsa04110) with p-value = 9.25E-09, Progesterone-mediated monocyte maturation (KEGG: hsa04914) with p-value = 0.00151, Oocyte meiosis (KEGG: hsa04114) with p-value = 0.0021, Neutrophil extracellular trap formation (KEGG: hsa04613) with p-value = 0.00289, Alcoholism (KEGG: hsa05034) with p-value = 0.0077. Identifying the results of GSVA analysis using ANN test

We included five Gene expression data about tumor tissue in 115 BCR patients and 206 no-BCR patients (GSE25136, GSE26242, GSE46602, GSE70768 and GSE89317) from the GEO database. After merging all microarray data and adjusting batch effect, a total of 36 gene symbols were identified after annotation (P < 0.05, |logFC| >0.1) and a heat map of the screened 36 different expression genes (DEG) was shown in Fig. 10A. And we also conducted function and pathway enrichment analyses to identify important GO terms and KEGG pathways. GOChord and KEGGChord were performed. They indicated that 16 DEGs were enriched into 8 GO terms, including epithelial cell development and morphogenesis of an epithelial sheet, and 12 DEGs were enriched into 8 KEGG pathways, including Endocrine resistance and MicroRNAs in cancer (Fig. 10B and C). To get more specific genes, we inputted the above 36 genes into the RF classifier. The lowest error rate occurred when the number of variables was 2 (Fig. 10D). Interestingly, we finally chose 36 genes in the results of MeanDecreaseGini were shown in Fig. 10E. The RF classifier identified the key genes, which optimally differentiated between the BCR and no BCR groups. ANN analysis was performed to calculate the weight of 36 genes. ANN topology indicated 36 input layers, 5 hidden layers, and 2 output layers (Fig. 10G). To determine the validation of this model, the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) was performed in Fig. 10F. The results showed that ANN model had a high-level classification performance with an AUC of 0.994.

CENPF promoted proliferation of PCa cells

To further investigate the function of CENPF in PCa, We first interrogated the Cellular Transcriptomics of Prostate Cancer (CTPC) database (https://pcatools.shinyapps.io/CTPC_V2/) [17] to examine CENPF expression across different prostate cancer cell lines. The analysis revealed that CENPF expression was relatively higher in PC3 and DU145 cells compared to other cell lines in Fig 12. Therefore, these two cell lines were selected for subsequent experiments. we reduced the expression of CENPF in prostate cancer cells PC3 and DU145 using small interfering RNA, as shown in Fig. 13A. CCK8 assays indicated that cells with reduced CENPF expression also exhibited significantly decreased proliferation ability, as shown in Fig. 13B. Subsequent colony formation assays and EdU experiments further demonstrated that reduced CENPF expression levels significantly inhibited cell proliferation in Fig. 13C and D.

Discussion

Most prostate cancer patients with BCR would experience clinical recurrence and distant metastasis of PCa. The occurrence of BCR is an important indicator of clinical interventions. However, not all patients would have BCR during long-term follow-up. Some patients with indolent tumors often did not need adjuvant therapy after RP to reduce the risk of tumor recurrence [18]. At present, many studies have reported several important genetic markers to predict the high-risk population of BCR for PCa [19–21].

In our study, we had researched the activity changes of immunologic and hallmark gene sets in PCa and adjacent nontumor tissues and use adjacent non-tumor important gene sets as risk factors to predict the BCR of PCa patients. Based on the ES of immunologic and hallmark gene sets in PCa and adjacent nontumor tissues, PCa patients were divided into three different subtypes. Interestingly, PCa patients with subtype 2 had better BCR-free survival, whereas subtype 1 and subtype 3 had worse BCR-free survival. After LASSO regression analysis, we obtained two prognostic gene sets in tumor tissues. We had not found that changes in the immune-related gene sets in adjacent non-tumor tissues could independently predict the BRC in PCa patients. This result might indicate that the BCR of PCa patients was mainly caused by changes in the gene sets of the tumor itself. For tumor tissue, genes from T gene sets are closely correlated with the development of the tumor itself.

Moreover, the hub genes were mainly related to cell cycle, ATPase activity, transcription coregulator activity, and microtubule binding for T gene sets. Subsequently, we calculated 5 main clusters from the PPI network through Cytoscape and found the three hub genes with the highest correlation in each cluster. Our research divided PCa patients into three subtypes, and we found that patients with subtype 2 had a lower risk of BCR than other types. Arora et al. conducted a review and classified PCa patients into different significant molecular subtypes based on clinical status of the tumor [22]. Sun et al. conducted research based on TCGA and GEO. They found Six BCR-free survival-related gene signatures (NOX4, F12, TPX2, PHYHD1, AURKA, and YIPF1) [7], and also Shi et al. performed another significant predictive model of gene related to BCR based on the expression of ALDH1A2, ASNS, SSTR1, FAM171B, FREM2, RSPO2, SRD5A2, TRIM14, and VPS4A [6]. However, we found that the key genes related to BCR selected by different studies were quite different, and there were no common representative specific genes that could be used to predict the BCR of PCa. Therefore, to obtain more accurate and specific genes, we obtained 5 transcriptome profile data from the GEO database and used RF and ANN analysis to identify 36 key genes between BCR patients and no BCR patients. These key genes were mainly related to endocrine resistance and MicroRNAs in cancer and epithelial cell development. We compared these 36 genes with the highest-scoring hub gene cluster we found before and found a key specific gene centromere protein F (CENPF). Shahid et al. reported that CENPF might play a significant role in regulating PCa metabolism and promoting cell proliferation [23]. Aytes et al. carried out a Cross-species analysis for human and mouse prostate cancer. Their results demonstrated that CENPF was a robust prognostic predictor of poor survival and metastasis [24]. Their research further identified that the analytical methods we used had high accuracy and credibility. Meanwhile, we validated the role of the CENPF gene, and consistent with their findings, CENPF can act as an oncogene in tumors to promote proliferation. In the future, CENPF may serve as a potential therapeutic target for prostate cancer treatment.

There were some limitations in our research. Firstly, the data we used was obtained from TCGA and GEO, and no real-world data were obtained. Secondly, due to the small number of selected samples from GEO, clinical data was relatively limited. Third, the function of the key gene we got had not been verified in our research, and further research should be continued.

Conclusion

In general, we found three clinically relevant subtypes of PCa based on tumor and nontumor data. Finally, we got two significant genes sets in tumor tissues. These gene sets were closely related to the cell cycle and ATPase activity. After identifying these genes sets through RF and ANN analysis, CENPF was common gen that could be used as a characteristic gene to predict the risk of BCR after RP.
