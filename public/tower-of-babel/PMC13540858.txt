Grief experiences among LGBTQ+ populations: a scoping review
Authors: Tamara Rodríguez Pérez, Cristo Manuel Marrero González, Héctor Vera Ortega, Alfonso Miguel García Hernández
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextLGBTQ+ individuals face grieving processes complicated by minority stress, structural discrimination, and fears related to disclosure of their sexual orientation or gender identity, which leads to disenfranchised grief within healthcare systems that remain heteronormative. Recent literature on palliative care highlights inequities in access, a lack of recognition of chosen families, and gaps in professionals’ LGBTQ+ cultural competence.ObjectiveTo explore research published between 2021 and 2026 on grief and bereavement-related experiences among LGBTQ+ adults in healthcare and palliative care settings, identifying the main topics addressed and knowledge gaps relevant to clinical practice.MethodsA scoping review was conducted following the framework of Arksey and O’Malley and the recommendations of the Joanna Briggs Institute and reported according to PRISMA-ScR. Original articles were searched for in 6 databases (February 2026). Independent double screening (k = 0.87) with removal of duplicates. Primary empirical studies on grief and bereavement-related experiences in LGBTQ+ adults in healthcare or palliative care settings were included, including studies on end-of-life communication, advance care planning, disclosure processes, and bereavement-related support when they provided identifiable primary data. Data were extracted using structured templates and analyzed through inductive thematic analysis.ResultsSeven primary empirical studies were included, with qualitative designs predominating. The evidence was mainly produced in English-speaking countries. Four main themes were identified: minority stress, complexities of disclosure, disenfranchised grief, and gaps in professional training. Critical gaps included underrepresentation of transgender people, a scarcity of quantitative data, an absence of intersectional perspectives, and concentration in Western contexts.ConclusionsGrief and bereavement-related experiences of LGBTQ+ adults in healthcare and palliative care settings appear to be profoundly shaped by structural dynamics of stigma, the invisibility of the chosen family, and training deficits in LGBTQ+ cultural competence. These findings suggest the need to develop institutional protocols sensitive to disclosure preferences, formally recognize the chosen family in end-of-life decisions, and incorporate mandatory training in LGBTQ+ competencies into curricula and continuing education programs. Future research should prioritize longitudinal and interventionist designs, intersectional approaches, and studies in non-Western contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-026-02202-0.Keywords: Bereavement, LGBTQ+, Minority stress, Palliative care, Chosen family, Disclosure

Background

Grief is a universal process that affects 100% of the adult population at some point in life, constituting a natural adaptive response to the loss of a loved one [1]. Globally, the prevalence of prolonged grief disorder (PGD) is estimated at 7–10%, rising to 49% in cases of unnatural deaths such as suicides or violent homicides [2], while in Spain the crude mortality rate reached 888.3 deaths per 100,000 inhabitants in 2024, overburdening healthcare systems and requiring comprehensive interventions in primary care and palliative care [3].

In LGBTQ+ populations, this process is dramatically complicated by chronic minority stress—stemming from structural discrimination, sociocultural stigma, and symbolic violence—which leads to disenfranchised grief [4, 5], social hypervigilance limiting access to support, and an 88% higher risk of post-loss mortality compared to non-stigmatized grief [6]. Systematic reviews confirm that this accumulated stress intensifies anxiety and depressive symptoms in palliative care settings [7].

Affirmative nursing interventions represent key advances: active listening without assumptions about gender or sexual orientation, sensitive assessment of disclosure preferences, and protocols that explicitly recognize the chosen family (found family) to mitigate isolation and foster resilience [8]. However, structural gaps persist in professional training and heteronormative clinical settings that perpetuate stigma in end-of-life decisions [9, 10].

Recent scoping reviews document persistent inequities in access to palliative care services [11], with institutional exclusions that prioritize biological ties over chosen emotional bonds and fears of forced outing that exacerbate suffering [12, 13]. Intersectional frameworks in nursing research underscore the urgency of LGBTQ+ cultural competencies [14], while digital technologies emerge as promising tools for inclusive care and the implementation of clinical guidelines addresses documented limitations [15–17].

The specific experiences of queer populations demand adapted protocols [18], and end-of-life care policies emphasize equity to mitigate disenfranchised grief among LGBTQ+ individuals [19, 20].

This scoping review aimed to identify, map, and synthesize empirical evidence on grief and bereavement-related experiences among LGBTQ+ adults in healthcare and palliative care settings, including end-of-life communication, advance care planning, disclosure processes, and bereavement-related support, and to characterize the main thematic patterns, methodological features, and gaps with implications for clinical practice.

Methods

This study was designed as a scoping review following the original five-step methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [21], subsequently updated and refined by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [22]. Arksey and O’Malley are notable for having established the conceptual foundations of this type of review, positioning them as an ideal tool for exploring the available literature in emerging or under-researched areas, without the rigidity of a quantitative synthesis [21].

Rationale for the scoping review approach

As Fernández et al. [23] argue, scoping reviews are particularly valuable for emerging phenomena such as bereavement experiences in LGBTQ+ populations, where heterogeneous qualitative research predominates. Among their main advantages are the breadth of the subject matter, which allows for an exhaustive exploration of the research output; the clear identification of knowledge gaps, useful for guiding future research agendas; and the flexibility to include diverse perspectives without requiring an exhaustive critical assessment of biases, unlike systematic reviews. However, they have disadvantages such as the absence of quantitative synthesis (e.g., meta-analysis), the lack of formal evaluation of the methodological quality of the included studies, and potentially less depth in the critical appraisal, which makes them complementary—but not substitutes—for more analytical reviews [21–23].

Search strategy

The systematic search was conducted in six major databases (PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Web of Science, and Embase), covering the period from January 1, 2021, to February 24, 2026 [21, 22].

The search strategy combined controlled MeSH terms and free-text terms in English and Spanish, including synonyms and morphological variations: (`"LGBTQ+” OR “LGBTQ*” OR “LGBT” OR “sexual minorit” OR “gender minorit"`) AND (`"grief” OR “bereavement” OR “duelo” OR “mourning” OR “loss"`) AND (`"palliative” OR “end-of-life” OR “EOL” OR “healthcare” OR “health servic*"`). Filters were applied to peer-reviewed articles in English or Spanish involving adult populations.

Selection criteria

Only original peer-reviewed articles published between 2021 and 2026 that explicitly addressed grief and bereavement-related experiences among LGBTQ+ adults in healthcare or palliative care settings, with clear and contemporary primary empirical data, were included [21, 22]. This scope ensures direct empirical results relevant to clinical practice.

The following were deliberately excluded: systematic, scoping, and narrative reviews; clinical guidelines; gray literature; pediatric populations; studies without primary empirical data; and publications prior to 2021. The excluded reviews and guidelines were utilized narratively to enrich the introduction, thereby strengthening the interpretation without contaminating the primary results [21, 22].

In this review, LGBTQ + was used as an umbrella term following the terminology of the included studies; no studies specifically focused on intersex populations were identified.

Selection process

The selection process was conducted independently by two reviewers using the Rayyan® collaborative platform, achieving an inter-observer agreement of k = 0.87 [23]. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. Duplicates were systematically removed using EndNote® X9 (n = 64). Subsequently, structured data extraction was performed in Excel® spreadsheets, which included: author/year, country of origin, methodological design, population characteristics, and main findings related to grief in the LGBTQ+ community [24].

Analysis

Finally, data were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis supported by NVivo® 14, in accordance with JBI recommendations for scoping reviews [23]. Two reviewers independently read the extracted findings from the included studies to familiarize themselves with the data and identify recurrent concepts related to grief, bereavement, disclosure, minority stress, chosen family, end-of-life communication, and professional support. Initial codes were generated inductively from the extracted data and subsequently organized in NVivo into preliminary categories. The coding framework and emerging thematic structure were then compared and refined through iterative discussions among the research team until consensus was reached. Discrepancies in coding or interpretation were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. NVivo was used as an organizational tool to facilitate coding transparency, traceability, and data management, rather than as an automated analytic procedure. Analytic rigor was strengthened through investigator triangulation, iterative review of codes and categories, and maintenance of an audit trail linking extracted findings, codes, preliminary categories, and final themes.

The review protocol was previously registrad on OSF.IO https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/3R54G, and the completed PRISMA-ScR checklist is available as Supplementary File 1.

Result

Study selection

The systematic search initially yielded 312 records. After removal of 64 duplicates using EndNote® X9, 248 titles and abstracts remained for screening. Of these, 59 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 7 original empirical studies were ultimately included because they met all pre-established criteria, as shown in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Fig. 1). At the full-text stage, 52 reports were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria, mainly because they were review articles, clinical guidelines, gray literature, pediatric studies, studies published before 2021, or papers without primary empirical data [25].

This decision to focus on primary empirical studies—deliberately excluding the 11 identified systematic and scoping reviews—ensured clear and contemporary results directly relevant to clinical practice, while these reviews were utilized narratively to contextualize the introduction and discussion.

Characteristics of included studies

Among the 7 included primary empirical studies, qualitative designs predominated. The evidence was mainly produced in English-speaking countries. The studies covered diverse settings, including palliative care, community care, hospice, oncology, and end-of-life communication.

Across the included studies, authors used different umbrella labels to designate the study populations, most frequently “LGBTQ+”, “LGBT”, and “sexual and gender minorities (SGM)”, while there was a marked underrepresentation of studies focusing specifically on transgender individuals. Overall, sample sizes ranged from small qualitative interview studies to large survey datasets, providing a substantial empirical basis for the subsequent thematic analysis.

The included evidence was methodologically heterogeneous; therefore, findings were synthesized narratively and thematically rather than weighted as equivalent forms of evidence. This approach allowed the review to integrate studies that differed substantially in design, population, setting, and proximity to bereavement outcomes while preserving the specific contribution of each article to the broader thematic interpretation.

Key themes identified

Through inductive thematic analysis conducted in NVivo® 14, four central themes emerged in the bereavement experiences of LGBTQ+ adults in healthcare and palliative care settings: minority stress, the complexities of disclosure and fear of outing, disenfranchised grief due to the non-recognition of the found family, and persistent gaps in LGBTQ+ cultural training for healthcare professionals. Together, these themes capture the main patterns identified across the included studies and provide a structured account of the specific complexities of LGBTQ+ grief in care settings.

First, minority stress stood out as a pervasive factor in the included studies, where it was described as a chronic stress stemming from structural discrimination, stigmatization, and symbolic violence that may exacerbate the intensity of grief [26–32].

Second, disclosure and fear of outing appeared as a recurring and highly influential theme across the included studies. Participants’ fears related to forced disclosure affected whether they revealed the sexual orientation or gender identity of the deceased, or their own identity as bereaved persons to healthcare professionals, with consequences for access to support and quality of interactions [26, 28, 29, 32].

Third, the included studies reflected a pattern of disenfranchised grief linked to the limited recognition of found or chosen family within healthcare and palliative care settings, particularly when same-sex partners and close friends were excluded from decision-making or bereavement support [28–32].

Finally, persistent gaps in LGBTQ+ cultural training for healthcare professionals emerged as a major theme in the included studies, where providers reported limited competence and discomfort addressing sexual orientation and gender identity in end-of-life and bereavement contexts [30–32].

Discussion

The findings of this scoping review suggest that the bereavement experiences of LGBTQ+ adults are shaped by four interrelated dynamics: minority stress as a chronic amplifier of bereavement-related suffering [33, 34], the strategic complexities of disclosure and fear of outing [35], disenfranchised grief linked to the limited recognition of chosen family within care settings, and persistent training gaps in LGBTQ+ cultural competence among healthcare professionals [36, 37]. This emerging narrative, built upon 7 primary empirical studies analyzed inductively, is consistent with the discussion in the introduction regarding how structural stigma can permeate the care continuum and shape bereavement experiences among LGBTQ+ adults, although this review does not collect primary data but maps existing published evidence [38, 39].

Minority stress: expanded confirmation of Timmins et al

The centrality of minority stress, observed across most of the included studies, directly aligns with the findings of Timmins et al. [27] mentioned in the introduction, who identified social positioning as a key determinant of grief outcomes in LGBTQ+ couples. Our synthesis is consistent with their finding that stress accumulated due to structural discrimination may intensify post-loss depressive symptoms, but our synthesis extends this conclusion beyond couples to include parental and found family losses, suggesting that social hypervigilance operates as a cross-cutting mechanism regardless of the relational configuration [27, 40]. This broadening suggests that Timmins’ model may have broader applicability than initially posited.

Disclosure: critical dialogue with Bristowe’s ADM model

The complexities of disclosure documented in several studies align directly with the Acceptance-Disclosure Model (ADM) proposed by Bristowe et al. [26], whose centrality we noted in the introduction. However, while the ADM emphasizes disclosure as a binary risk-benefit decision in couple bereavement, our findings reveal a much more dynamic and multifaceted process that includes decisions regarding the disclosure of the deceased’s identity (outing) and the bereaved’s own identity to healthcare professionals. We differ here from Lucas et al. [41], who attribute these tensions primarily to pandemic disruptions, as our data demonstrate their structural nature and pre-existence to COVID-19, thus suggesting that disclosure emerged across several studies as a recurring structural and relational concern.

Disenfranchised grief: an expansion of Doka’s classic framework

Disenfranchised grief due to the non-recognition of found family, identified in several studies, faithfully reproduces the classic pattern described by Doka [5] in the introduction, but significantly enriches its application to sexual/gender minorities. We agree with De Jong et al. [9] regarding the systematic exclusion of same-sex couples from end-of-life decisions, but our synthesis documents an even broader exclusion that includes chosen friends as primary support networks, expanding the traditional definition of unrecognized grief to include non-conventional yet central affective configurations for LGBTQ+ individuals [13, 29]. This finding extends previous literature by showing that disenfranchisement operates not only through the prioritization of biological ties, but also through care practices that may fail to recognize chosen families within institutional settings.

Educational gaps: beyond technical skills

Gaps in LGBTQ+ cultural training, present in several studies, confirm the warnings of Stein et al. [42] regarding heteronormative curricula, but reveal a deeper problem that transcends specific competencies. While Stein proposes specific training, the data reveal structural institutional resistance: The included studies suggest that current palliative care frameworks may insufficiently recognize LGBTQ+ relationships, chosen families, and bereavement needs. These findings suggest that training should not only address technical knowledge, but also help professionals recognize LGBTQ+ relationships, chosen families, and bereavement needs within care settings. This nuance suggests the need to move beyond competency-based approaches toward a broader institutional and cultural transformation.

Dialogue with the broader contextual literature

Beyond the seven primary empirical studies synthesized in this review, several contextual contributions help situate the broader structural mechanisms shaping LGBTQ+ bereavement. A scoping review in oncology has highlighted persistent gaps in LGBTQ+ cancer care and structural barriers that limit both access to services and participation in research, providing additional support for our interpretation of institutional exclusion and minority stress [43].

Rapid review with SGM cancer survivors has similarly described disenfranchised grief in the post-treatment phase, where the lack of recognition of same-sex relationships and chosen families is consistent with experiences of unacknowledged losses and unmet bereavement needs, offering additional context for the visibility of found families within care systems [44].

Educational literature on LGBTQIA+-inclusive nursing and palliative care training has highlighted the importance of structured preparation for healthcare professionals. In addition, work on advance care planning conversations with LGBTQ+ older adults, cultural humility at the end of life, and the care of transgender and gender-nonconforming patients underscores that technical competence alone is insufficient without explicit attention to communication, bias, identity, and inclusive care practices [39, 45, 46].

From an epistemic perspective, qualitative evidence syntheses and rapid evaluations have also documented how heteronormative policies and paywalls shape what is known, published, and accessible about LGBTQ+ grief and loss. For example, Lucas et al. describe how cis-heteronormative responses to the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted LGBTQ+ kinship networks and intensified experiences of loss [41], while Cartwright et al. show that paywalls and restricted access to LGBTQ+ research can limit the dissemination of evidence and hinder the integration of best practices into clinical guidelines [47]. Together, these contextual studies provide additional context for the structural barriers and knowledge gaps surrounding the seven primary empirical articles synthesized here.

Strengths, limitations, and clinical relevance

This scoping review presents significant methodological strengths: a rigorous JBI design with double-blind independent screening (kappa = 0.87), post-pandemic temporal coverage that captures current care dynamics, and in-depth inductive thematic analysis in NVivo® 14 that allowed for authentic emergences from the data. As limitations inherent to this design, we did not conduct a formal critical assessment of methodological quality or a quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis), and the deliberate exclusion of gray literature may have omitted valuable community experiences.

The heterogeneity of the included studies, including differences in design, population, setting, and proximity to bereavement outcomes, limits the extent to which findings can be interpreted as directly comparable. In addition, the review included both studies directly focused on bereavement and studies addressing related phenomena such as disclosure, end-of-life communication, advance care planning, and provider experiences, which broadens interpretive scope but also increases conceptual heterogeneity.

The geographical concentration in high-income, English-speaking countries limits global transferability, while the predominantly qualitative nature of the included studies limits the quantification of population-level patterns [22, 23].

From the perspective of clinical nursing practice, these findings support the consideration of affirmative interventions: institutional protocols sensitive to disclosure preferences, formal recognition of chosen families in end-of-life and bereavement-related decision-making in critical decisions, and mandatory LGBTQ+ curriculum training. Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies, randomized controlled trials, intersectional perspectives, and contexts in the Global South.

Conclusions

This scoping review suggests that grief and bereavement-related experiences among LGBTQ+ adults in healthcare and palliative care settings are shaped by interrelated factors, including minority stress, disclosure-related concerns, limited recognition of chosen families, and gaps in professionals’ LGBTQ+ cultural competence. Across the seven included empirical studies, these themes emerged as recurrent patterns that may influence access to support, communication with healthcare professionals, and the quality of bereavement-related care.

Given the small and methodologically heterogeneous evidence base, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Rather than establishing definitive conclusions, this review maps an emerging field and highlights the need for greater attention to how LGBTQ+ identities, relationships, and support networks are recognized within palliative and bereavement care contexts.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal, mixed-methods, and intervention-focused studies, with stronger intersectional approaches and greater representation of transgender, non-Western, and culturally diverse populations. From a clinical perspective, the findings point to the importance of developing more inclusive care practices, including sensitive approaches to disclosure, formal recognition of chosen families, and structured LGBTQ+ training for healthcare professionals.
