Predictive modeling of peripheral blood EGFR positivity in patients with advanced primary non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Ying-Bo Xie, Yi-Ke Wang, Xuan Yao, Kelan Ke, Pei Ma, Feng-Nan Wang, Qin Liu, Xiao-Hong Xie, Ming Liu, Xin-Qing Lin, Cheng-Zhi Zhou, Zhan-Hong Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for approximately 85% of cases. Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations are the most prevalent driver alterations in NSCLC, and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have markedly improved survival outcomes. Accurate molecular testing is essential for guiding targeted therapy. However, tissue biopsy, the current gold standard, is often limited by insufficient samples, invasive procedure risks, or tumor heterogeneity, some patients experiencing sampling failure. Liquid biopsy based on circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) offers a minimally invasive alternative and can shorten the detection cycle by 2 to 3 weeks, but its sensitivity depends on ctDNA shedding, and patient selection for blood-based testing remains challenging in clinical practice. This study aimed to develop a peripheral blood EGFR positivity prediction model and a simple scoring system to identify high probability candidates for liquid biopsy and prioritize blood-based EGFR testing when tissue samples are inadequate.MethodsA retrospective case-control study design was employed. Data were collected from 384 patients with NSCLC who were hospitalized at The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University between June 2018 and September 2024 and met the study’s inclusion criteria. The patients were categorized into two groups: the peripheral blood EGFR-positive group (191 cases) and peripheral blood EGFR-negative group (193 cases). Univariate and binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to establish a predictive model for EGFR in peripheral blood, which was subsequently converted into a scoring system.ResultsThe results of multi-factor analysis showed that gender, smoking status, pulmonary lymphangitic carcinomatosis, non-vertebral bone metastasis, brain metastasis, and liver metastasis were independent factors for the prediction of EGFR positivity in peripheral blood (P<0.05). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis of predicted peripheral blood EGFR positivity showed that the area under the curve (AUC) was 0.884. A score of ≥3 was considered a high-probability population with positive EGFR in peripheral blood. The sensitivity for diagnosing positive EGFR in peripheral blood was 84.8%, and the specificity was 73.1%. Taking the next-generation sequencing (NGS) detection results as the reference standard, the sensitivity of amplification refractory mutation system polymerase chain reaction (ARMS-PCR) detection was 94.96%, the specificity was 100%, and the overall compliance rate was 96.2% [Kappa (Cohen’s kappa coefficient) =0.903, P<0.001].ConclusionsThe Gspnbl score demonstrates good predictive performance in identifying patients with a high probability of peripheral blood EGFR positivity. This easy‑to‑apply tool helps clinicians rapidly select appropriate candidates for liquid‑biopsy EGFR testing. ARMS‑PCR and NGS have high consistency in detecting common EGFR‑sensitive mutations. ARMS‑PCR can be adopted for rapid initial screening in critically‑ill patients with high suspicion of EGFR mutations.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), primary treatment, peripheral blood EGFR, logistic regression, points-scoring system

Highlight box

Key findings

• A novel 6-variable clinical scoring system (Gspnbl score) was developed to predict peripheral blood epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) positivity in treatment-naïve advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).

• Female gender, never-smoking status, pulmonary lymphangitis, non-vertebral bone metastasis, brain metastasis, and liver metastasis were identified as independent predictors.

• The Gspnbl score demonstrated robust discriminative ability [area under the curve (AUC) =0.863], with a sensitivity of 84.8% and a specificity of 73.1% at a cut-off of ≥3.

• Amplification refractory mutation system polymerase chain reaction (ARMS-PCR) showed high concordance with next-generation sequencing (NGS) (96.2%) for common EGFR mutations but failed to detect rare alterations such as exon 20 insertions.

What is known and what is new?

• Liquid biopsy can shorten the detection cycle compared with tissue biopsy, but patient selection remains challenging in clinical practice.

• This study introduces the first clinical-metastatic scoring system (Gspnbl) that integrates demographic features and multi-organ metastatic patterns to identify high-probability candidates for blood-based EGFR testing without relying on radiomics or artificial intelligence.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Patients with a Gspnbl score ≥3 should be prioritized for rapid ARMS-PCR or simultaneous NGS to avoid treatment delays in critically ill patients.

• For patients with negative ARMS-PCR but strong clinical suspicion, NGS re-evaluation is recommended to prevent missed diagnoses of rare mutations and guide personalized treatment decisions.

Introduction

According to the 2022 GLOBOCAN study, there were 2.48 million new cases of lung cancer and 1.81 million deaths worldwide, establishing it as one of the most prevalent cancers globally and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality (1). Pathologically, lung cancer is categorized into small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Approximately 85% of lung cancers are classified as NSCLC, which includes subtypes such as lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma, with lung adenocarcinoma being the most common subtype within NSCLC (2,3). Most lung cancer patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, often presenting with distant metastatic lesions. This clinical characteristic significantly contributes to the high mortality rate and poor prognosis associated with lung cancer. The occurrence and progression of lung cancer are closely linked to genomic alterations. Various driver mutations have been identified in NSCLC, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), v-raf murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B (BRAF), Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS), mesenchymal to epithelial transition factor (MET), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and ROS proto-oncogene 1 (ROS1) (4,5). Among these, EGFR is the most prevalent driver mutation in NSCLC patients.

EGFR belongs to the Erb B family of transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs). Located on the cell surface, it can bind to ligands such as epidermal growth factor and transforming growth factor. Upon ligand binding and subsequent activation, EGFR forms a dimer. The intracellular tyrosine kinase domain binds adenosine triphosphate (ATP), resulting in phosphorylation that activates the MAPK, MTOR/AKT, and STAT signaling pathways (6,7). These processes ultimately lead to enhanced DNA replication, increased cell division, and the inhibition of angiogenesis and apoptosis. The rapid advancement of molecular targeted therapy has markedly enhanced the survival prognosis for lung cancer patients. The IPASS study, published in 2009, demonstrated that the objective response rate for patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations treated with first-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) was significantly superior to that of conventional chemotherapy (8). This marked the onset of precision treatment for lung cancer. The FLAURA study (9) revealed that the median progression-free survival (PFS) for patients receiving the third-generation osimertinib was 18.9 months. Compared to first-generation EGFR-TKIs, the third-generation agents significantly extend PFS and overall survival (OS) while also enhancing the duration of response. Furthermore, they exhibit favorable safety and tolerability profiles. With their precise therapeutic targets and relatively low toxicity and side effects, TKIs have substantially improved the prognosis for NSCLC and have now become the first-line treatment option for advanced NSCLC (10,11). The T790M mutation serves as the primary mechanism of resistance to first- and second-generation EGFR TKIs. Third-generation EGFR TKIs, exemplified by osimertinib, can effectively counteract the secondary resistance induced by the T790M mutation. This is achieved primarily through the covalent modification of the cysteine-797 residue at the ATP binding site, resulting in irreversible binding to the EGFR kinase (12).

In patients with NSCLC, particularly those diagnosed with lung adenocarcinoma, the detection rate of high-frequency driver gene alterations, including EGFR, ALK, and ROS1, is notably elevated. This underscores the pivotal role of targeted therapy in the personalized treatment of advanced NSCLC (13). The effective implementation of targeted therapy relies heavily on precise molecular typing detection. While tissue biopsy is considered the gold standard for genetic testing, some patients may present insufficient tissue samples for detection and analysis. Additionally, the tumor’s specific anatomical location may render safe sample acquisition impossible, or patients may suffer from heart and lung insufficiencies or coagulation disorders that preclude them from tolerating invasive procedures. Furthermore, repeated biopsies can elevate the risk of complications and limit their utility in dynamic monitoring. Due to the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of tumors, local biopsies may fail to capture the complete mutation profile. Moreover, repeated punctures not only impose an economic burden on patients but also cause them increased discomfort. Liquid biopsy, a minimally invasive detection technique, has garnered significant attention in oncology in recent years. Its primary advantage is the ability to achieve dynamic monitoring through repeatable sampling, which provides multi-dimensional biological information for tracking tumor progression and evaluating treatment response (14). During the proliferation of tumor cells, they release circulating tumor cells (CTC), circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), non-coding RNA (ncRNA), and microRNA (miRNA) into the peripheral circulatory system. By capturing and analyzing these tumor-derived components, liquid biopsy can dynamically assess the genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic characteristics of tumors, thereby revealing critical biological information such as tumor heterogeneity, pathological classification, clinical stage, and driver gene mutations (15). Blood is the most frequently utilized sample type in clinical applications. Other sample types include urine, sputum, cerebrospinal fluid, pleural effusion, and ascites (16). Relevant studies have established that the detection cycle for liquid biopsy is 2 to 3 weeks shorter than that for tissue biopsy (17-19). Liquid biopsy can complement genetic testing of tissue specimens or, to a certain extent, serve as a substitute when tissue samples are inadequate, thereby revealing the genetic information of tumors. Numerous studies have demonstrated that liquid biopsy based on ctDNA exhibits a high degree of concordance with genetic testing of tumor tissue (20-25). In the clinical management of lung cancer, liquid biopsy has shown considerable value. Its high sensitivity and specificity not only facilitate early screening but also aid in guiding targeted therapy decisions, evaluating therapeutic effects in real time, providing early warnings of recurrence risks, and managing prognosis stratification (26). The detection of ctDNA is the preferred approach for liquid biopsy in lung cancer. Its sensitivity primarily depends on the concentration of ctDNA in peripheral blood, which is influenced by factors such as tumor stage, histological type, and metastatic characteristics. Research indicates that in patients with advanced stages, particularly stage IV, or those with a high tumor burden, the sensitivity of detection significantly increases due to enhanced ctDNA release (27). Furthermore, patients with distant metastases (bone metastases or liver metastases) exhibit a significant increase in ctDNA release (28,29). Conversely, early-stage lung cancer or localized lesions, such as small nodular formations, are often associated with false-negative results, as ctDNA release may fall below the detection threshold of current technologies. Identifying patients who would benefit from peripheral blood EGFR testing is crucial for optimizing testing timing, minimizing ineffective assessments, and providing a precise treatment basis for patients whose tumors are inaccessible through tissue sampling. This challenge has become an urgent issue in clinical practice.

Significant advancements have recently been achieved in the integration of radiomics, clinical features, and artificial intelligence technology for predicting EGFR mutations in NSCLC patients. Prediction models utilizing multi-parameter imaging data from computed tomography (CT) and positron emission tomography (PET) have become the predominant research approach. Wang et al. (30) introduced an automated artificial intelligence system called FAIS, which analyzes whole lung CT images and incorporates clinical factors (e.g., smoking status, gender, histological subtype) to predict peripheral blood EGFR positivity in lung cancer patients. In a multicenter, multi-cohort study of 18,232 patients, the FAIS system exhibited remarkable performance, with AUC values ranging from 0.755 to 0.813 on independent validation sets, surpassing conventional tumor-based deep learning models [area under the curve (AUC) values from 0.637 to 0.758]. Wang et al. (31) developed and validated a deep learning predictor based on CT imaging using transfer learning to forecast EGFR status, achieving an AUC of 0.81. Additionally, Wu et al. (32) reported in a systematic review and meta-analysis that artificial intelligence models incorporating PET/CT, CT, and immunohistochemical full section images (including radiomics and deep learning) excel in predicting EGFR mutations. Among these models, the PET/CT model demonstrated the highest predictive efficacy for EGFR mutations (AUC =0.85), significantly outperforming the traditional CT model (AUC =0.83). However, the current methods still face significant limitations. Firstly, the reliance on high-resolution imaging tools like PET/CT and the complexity of the radiomics process, involving lesion segmentation algorithms and high-order texture feature extraction, hinder the model’s clinical implementation in primary medical facilities. While a CT radiomics model in the literature achieved an AUC of 0.85, its implementation heavily relies on specialized techniques, thus complicating its clinical adoption (33). Secondly, existing prediction systems lack the systematic integration of multi-omics data, often limited to correlating imaging features with clinical parameters and overlooking liquid biopsy biomarkers like ctDNA, potentially leading to the omission of crucial molecular information (34). Thirdly, although some artificial intelligence models exhibit outstanding predictive capabilities, their decision-making lacks visual interpretation tools, impeding the technology’s translation into clinical settings (35). These limitations underscore the limited uptake of current EGFR mutation prediction models among clinicians due to issues with technical applicability, multi-omics integration, and clinical interpretability.

For patients with newly diagnosed advanced NSCLC, particularly those with compromised physical condition or significant comorbidities, the execution of invasive tissue biopsy procedures may pose challenges due to respiratory insufficiency, heightened bleeding risk, or deterioration of overall health. These patients frequently encounter rapid symptom progression resulting from a substantial tumor burden, which may manifest as malignant pleural effusion, lung cancer lymphangitis, or superior vena cava syndrome. Consequently, there is an urgent need to promptly initiate effective treatment to alleviate symptoms and enhance quality of life. However, traditional tissue biopsy typically requires about 1 week, with approximately 15% to 30% of patients facing the risk of sampling failure or insufficient samples, which further delays treatment decisions. In this clinical context, there is a pressing need for a non-invasive, accurate, and repeatable method to assess peripheral blood EGFR positivity, providing a rapid testing alternative for critically ill patients. Such an approach would enable clinicians to identify indications for targeted therapy within 24 to 28 hours, thereby avoiding the necessity of opting for palliative chemotherapy while awaiting test results. This would secure earlier treatment opportunities and enhance survival benefits for patients. This study innovatively integrates peripheral blood biomarkers and clinical features of NSCLC patients to develop a straightforward EGFR gene mutation prediction model. This model screens populations with a high probability of EGFR mutations through multi-dimensional data, facilitating the early and rapid identification of patients carrying these mutations. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0625/rc).

Methods

Study population and inclusion criteria

This study was a single-center, retrospective case-control investigation. The subjects were patients with NSCLC who underwent detection of peripheral blood EGFR positivity at The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University from June 2018 to September 2024. Inclusion criteria included: (I) age ≥18 years; (II) patients diagnosed with NSCLC via pathological or cytological examination; (III) tumor stage classified as stage IIIB–IV according to the ninth edition of the IASLC lung cancer TNM staging system, with a positive result for EGFR mutation in the blood gene test. Exclusion criteria were: (I) absence of pathological or cytological diagnosis; (II) genetic evidence of ALK fusion mutation, KRAS mutation, MET mutation, BRAF mutation, HER2 mutation, RET fusion mutation, or ROS1 fusion mutation. Patients carrying these concurrent actionable driver alterations were excluded to eliminate confounding effects of alternative oncogenic pathways on ctDNA release and EGFR pathway activity, though this design may introduce selection bias and restrict the generalizable population of our predictive scoring system. (III) Presence of other malignant tumors. (IV) Prior surgical intervention or other forms of anti-tumor treatment. (V) Incomplete clinical data. Following the application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, data from a total of 384 eligible research subjects were retrospectively collected for this study. Based on the results of peripheral blood next-generation sequencing (NGS) detection, the subjects were categorized into two groups: the peripheral blood EGFR positive group (191 cases) and the peripheral blood EGFR-negative group (193 cases). The selection process for research subjects is shown in Figure 1. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (No. 164[2022]). Individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Clinical data collection

In this study, the mutation status of EGFR in peripheral blood was utilized as the clinical outcome. By integrating current research advancements both domestically and internationally, along with the characteristics of NSCLC patients at our institution, we identified the following risk factors associated with peripheral blood EGFR positivity: (I) general information: age and gender; (II) admission medical history: smoking status, alcohol consumption, and the presence of a history of hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; (III) laboratory test data: lung tumor markers including neuron-specific enolase (NSE), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125), carbohydrate antigen 153 (CA153), cytokeratin 19 fragment antigen 21-1 (CYFRA21-1), and squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCCA); (IV) imaging examination data: CT scans of the chest, upper abdomen, and lower abdomen, along with PET/CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the head, as well as emission computed tomography (ECT) of the bone, are utilized to assess the size of the primary tumor. These imaging modalities help determine the presence of multiple metastases in both lungs, bronchial invasion, vascular invasion, esophageal invasion, pleural involvement, pleural metastasis, pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, lung cancer lymphangitis, and the status of lymph nodes, including ipsilateral hilar lymph nodes, ipsilateral mediastinal lymph nodes, contralateral hilar lymph nodes, contralateral mediastinal lymph nodes, and supraclavicular lymph nodes. Additionally, they evaluate for distant lymph node metastasis, non-vertebral bone metastases, vertebral metastases, brain metastases, liver metastases, adrenal metastases, renal metastases, splenic metastases, pancreatic metastases, skin metastases, and muscle metastases. (V) Pathological examination data: this includes the histological type (e.g., adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma) and the degree of tumor differentiation.

Statistical analysis

In this study, data were entered using Excel, and statistical descriptions and analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0. The imaging and clinical characteristics of patients in the peripheral blood EGFR-positive group were compared to those in the negative group using the chi-square test, t-test, and non-parametric Wilcoxon rank sum test. A bilateral P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Univariate logistic regression analysis was employed to identify factors that may influence the peripheral blood EGFR positivity. Collinearity tests were conducted on factors that demonstrated statistical significance in the univariate analysis. After excluding independent variables with high correlation, continuous variables with significant univariate associations were retained as continuous in the primary multivariate model to preserve statistical power. Categorization using receiver operating characteristic (ROC)-derived cut-offs was performed only for the scoring system to facilitate clinical usability, acknowledging the associated loss of information. Multivariate analysis was performed using the binary logistic stepwise regression method to develop a predictive model for EGFR in the peripheral blood of patients with advanced and newly diagnosed NSCLC. The model’s discrimination was assessed through the AUC, which predicts the probability of positive EGFR in peripheral blood, while the model’s calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Scores were assigned to each risk factor based on the regression coefficient β value, calculated using the formula: score of each variable = β value of each variable / minimum β value of all variables. The results were rounded to the nearest whole number to create a scoring system. Scores for each research subject were calculated, and risk stratification was conducted based on the scoring results and the proportion of peripheral blood EGFR-positive individuals corresponding to different scores. The predictive ability of the scoring system for positive EGFR in peripheral blood was evaluated using the ROC and the AUC. With 191 events and 14 candidate predictors, the EPV was approximately 13.6, exceeding the minimum recommended threshold of 10 events per variable for multivariate logistic regression, which reduces the risk of model overfitting from extensive variable screening. Continuous variables with significant univariate associations were retained as continuous in the primary multivariate model to preserve statistical power. Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 384 patients with NSCLC participated in this study, comprising 261 males and 123 females. Among these patients, 191 were classified in the peripheral blood EGFR positive group, while 193 were in the peripheral blood EGFR-negative group (Table 1). Compared to the peripheral blood EGFR-negative group, the peripheral blood EGFR positive group exhibited a lower smoking rate (18.3% vs. 66.8%) and a higher proportion of female patients (50.8% vs. 13.5%), with these differences reaching statistical significance (P<0.05). However, there was no significant difference in age, with a median of 63 years (P=0.42). In the peripheral blood EGFR positive group, levels of NSE {19.12 [interquartile range (IQR), 15.4–24.6675] vs. 17.255 (IQR, 14.6–21.65) ng/mL}, CEA [29.51 (IQR, 7.38–126) vs. 12.64 (IQR, 14.6–21.65) ng/mL], CA153 [30.83 (IQR, 15.875–65.81) vs. 23.745 (IQR, 13.7–48.025) U/mL], and CYFRA21-1 [7.62 (IQR, 4.465–14.4075) vs. 15.215 (IQR, 3.495–10.7975) ng/mL] were higher. In contrast, the SCCA level was elevated in the peripheral blood EGFR-negative group [0.7 (IQR, 0.5–1.0) vs. 0.96 (IQR, 0.6–1.42) ng/mL], with this difference also being statistically significant (P<0.05). No significant statistical difference in CA125 levels was observed between the two groups (P>0.05). In terms of pathology, no significant statistical difference was observed between the two groups (P>0.05). However, a significant difference in total stage distribution was noted between the two patient groups (P<0.001), with a markedly higher proportion of patients in the IVB stage within the peripheral blood EGFR-positive group compared to the peripheral blood EGFR-negative group. In imaging assessments, patients in the peripheral blood EGFR positive group exhibited a smaller tumor diameter compared to the peripheral blood EGFR-negative group (3.75 vs. 4.4 cm, P=0.033). Furthermore, the incidence of multiple metastases in both lungs was significantly higher in the positive group than in the negative group (45.5% vs. 20.2%, P<0.001). Additionally, the positive group demonstrated elevated rates of pleural metastasis (41.4% vs. 31.6%, P=0.047), lung cancer-related lymphangitis (29.3% vs. 13.5%, P<0.001), ipsilateral mediastinal lymph node metastasis (87.4% vs. 78.8%, P=0.02), non-vertebral bone metastasis (45.0% vs. 17.1%, P<0.001), vertebral metastasis (29.8% vs. 15.5%, P=0.002), brain metastasis (45.0% vs. 15.5%, P<0.001), and liver metastasis (19.9% vs. 4.7%, P<0.001) compared to the negative group. However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding bronchial invasion, vascular invasion, esophageal invasion, pleural involvement, pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, contralateral hilar lymph node metastasis, contralateral mediastinal lymph node metastasis, supraclavicular lymph node metastasis, distant lymph node metastasis, renal metastasis, splenic metastasis, pancreatic metastasis, skin metastasis, and muscle metastasis (P>0.05).

Univariate logistic regression analysis

Univariate regression analysis indicated that gender [odds ratio (OR) =6.628, 95% confidence interval (CI): 4.015–10.941], smoking status (OR =8.984, 95% CI: 5.595–14.424), and NSE (OR =1.021, 95% CI: 1.003–1.038) were associated with positive EGFR in peripheral blood. Additionally, CA153 (OR =1.005, 95% CI: 1.001–1.009), Pleural involvement (OR =1.527, 95% CI: 1.005–2.320), Pulmonary lymphangitis (OR =2.667, 95% CI: 1.587–4.464), Contralateral lung multipl metastasis (OR =3.303, 95% CI: 2.102–5.192), ipsilateral mediastinal lymph node metastasis (OR =1.876, 95% CI: 1.083–3.247), non-vertebral bone metastases (OR =3.968, 95% CI: 2.481–6.369), vertebral metastasis (OR =2.310, 95% CI: 1.404–3.802), brain metastasis (OR =4.444, 95% CI: 2.747–7.194), liver metastasis (OR =5.076, 95% CI: 2.381–10.870), and adrenal metastasis (OR =2.770, 95% CI: 1.432–5.348) may also serve as predictive factors (P<0.05). Conversely, no statistically significant differences were observed regarding age, CEA, CA125, CYFRA21-1, tumor diameter of SCCA, pathology, bronchial invasion, vascular invasion, esophageal invasion, involvement of pleura, pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, ipsilateral hilar lymph nodes, contralateral hilar lymph nodes, contralateral mediastinal lymph nodes, supraclavicular lymph nodes, distant lymph nodes, renal metastasis, splenic metastasis, pancreatic metastasis, skin metastasis, and muscle metastasis (P>0.05, Table 2).

Evaluation of consistency between NGS and PCR methods

This study included 191 patients whose peripheral blood samples tested positive via NGS. Of these, 158 also underwent ARMS-PCR testing. Among these 158 patients, 119 were NGS-positive with evaluable ARMS-PCR results: 113 exhibited positive PCR results, while 6 had negative results. The remaining 39 patients were NGS-negative, and all were ARMS-PCR-negative. Using the NGS test results as the reference standard, the sensitivity of ARMS-PCR was calculated as 94.96% (113/119), the specificity as 100% (39/39), and the overall agreement rate as 96.2% [(113+39)/158; Kappa value =0.903, P<0.001]. Six patients who tested negative by PCR but positive by NGS were analyzed. Among these, five exhibited common EGFR mutations, specifically a deletion mutation in exon 19 and a missense mutation (L858R) in exon 21, with NGS gene abundances in their peripheral blood measuring 0.12%, 0.20%, 0.19%, 1.79%, and 0.17%, respectively. Discussion

Lung cancer has emerged as the malignant tumor with the highest incidence and mortality rates in China, with both metrics continuing to rise annually. Early detection and treatment of lung cancer can substantially enhance the cure rate and survival duration of patients (36,37). Nevertheless, a majority of lung cancer patients receive diagnoses at advanced stages, thereby missing the opportunity for radical surgical intervention. Consequently, they are limited to managing their condition through chemotherapy or radiotherapy, which serve primarily to prolong life. However, these treatments are associated with considerable toxicity and side effects, adversely affecting patients’ quality of life. Additionally, some patients may be unable to tolerate these therapies due to poor physical condition and elevated performance status (PS) scores, thereby restricting their clinical applicability. The advent of targeted therapy has fundamentally altered the treatment landscape and prognosis for specific NSCLC patients harboring particular driver gene alterations (38,39). Therefore, the precise identification of driver genes holds substantial clinical significance for the individualized treatment and prognosis assessment of advanced NSCLC. Currently, the primary driver genes associated with lung cancer include EGFR mutations, ALK rearrangements, ROS1 rearrangements, KRAS mutations, and BRAF mutations (40). Molecular detection based on tumor tissue biopsy or surgical specimens remains the clinical gold standard for identifying EGFR mutations. However, this approach presents significant clinical limitations, which include sampling bias resulting from tumor heterogeneity, the risk of complications linked to invasive procedures, delays in clinical decision-making due to prolonged testing cycles, high testing costs, and the potential for false negatives arising from inadequate sample size or quality defects (41).

EGFR gene mutations in NSCLC are closely associated with female gender, non-smoking status, and the histological characteristics of adenocarcinoma. This relationship has been substantiated by multiple large-scale studies. Epidemiological data indicate that the overall incidence of EGFR mutations among non-smoking patients is approximately 40%; however, significant regional variations exist. The mutation rate among non-smokers in Asia reaches as high as 68%, whereas in the United States, it is only 28% (42,43). Evidence from multi-center studies in China demonstrates a significantly elevated EGFR mutation rate in non-smoking patients with lung adenocarcinoma. Notably, the L858R point mutation in exon 21 is particularly enriched in the non-smoking population, suggesting that tobacco exposure may influence the mutation spectrum through epigenetic regulation (44). Zhou et al. (45) and the Cao team (46) jointly confirmed through logistic regression and genome-wide association analysis that non-smoking status and the pathological type of adenocarcinoma are independent predictors of EGFR mutations. Their predictive efficacy is significantly superior to that of 18F-FDG PET-CT metabolic parameters (47,48). Numerous studies have demonstrated that the EGFR mutation rate in female patients is significantly higher than that in male patients, with an OR of 2.33 (95% CI: 1.97–2.75) for females as an independent risk factor (49). This interaction between gender and genetic factors may be linked to hormone metabolic pathways; however, the precise molecular basis remains to be elucidated. Notably, the prediction model developed by Aye et al. (50) incorporates three primary characteristics: female gender, Asian ethnicity, and non-smoking status. Its effectiveness has been corroborated in the studies conducted by Zhang (51), Midha (52), and Ortiz (53). Additionally, geographical and racial factors significantly influence these findings. Lv et al. (48) analyzed 849 patients with NSCLC in China and discovered that the EGFR mutation rate among non-smoking female patients exhibited a dual advantage effect. This ethnic specificity may be attributed to the unique genetic susceptibility of the Asian population, yet the underlying molecular mechanisms require further investigation through genome-wide studies.

Numerous studies have investigated the relationship between serum tumor markers and EGFR mutation status, yielding heterogeneous conclusions. Jin et al. (54) reported that elevated serum CEA levels in non-smoking Chinese patients with lung adenocarcinoma were significantly associated with a higher rate of EGFR mutations. In a study involving 1,089 patients with NSCLC, Wang et al. (55) found that the levels of SCCA and CA125 in the EGFR mutation-positive group were significantly lower than those in the wild-type group. Conversely, Cho et al. (56) reported no statistically significant differences in the levels of serum CEA, CYFRA 21-1, and SCC between patients with EGFR mutations and those with wild-type status. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified adenocarcinoma, smoking history, CA125, and SCCA levels as independent predictors of EGFR mutations. In contrast, Wen et al. (57) demonstrated through univariate logistic regression analysis that SCCA is a risk factor for positive EGFR gene mutations in NSCLC patients; however, this association did not achieve statistical significance in multivariate analysis (P>0.05). The data from this study indicate that serum levels of NSE, CEA, CA153, and CYFRA 21-1 in patients with EGFR mutations were significantly elevated compared to those in the wild-type group (all P<0.05). In contrast, SCCA exhibited higher expression in the wild-type group (P<0.05), while the levels of CA125 did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference between the two groups. Univariate logistic regression analysis suggested that NSE (OR =1.021, 95% CI: 1.003–1.038) and CA153 (OR =1.005, 95% CI: 1.001–1.009) could be potential risk factors for EGFR mutations. However, multivariate analysis revealed that neither marker exhibited independent predictive value (P>0.05). This finding is partially consistent with the conclusions of Cho et al. (56), which suggest that NSE and CA153 may not be directly linked to EGFR mutations. It is important to note that discrepancies in results across different studies may stem from variations in sample size, inclusion criteria, and population characteristics. Future research should aim to further validate the clinical applicability of these markers by increasing the cohort size.

Lung cancer lymphangitis represents a distinct manifestation of pulmonary metastatic cancer. Its pathological characteristics primarily involve the metastasis of cancer cells to the interstitial lymphatic vessels within the lungs, resulting in blockages that obstruct lymphatic return and cause lumen dilation. This condition subsequently disseminates via lymphatic and vascular pathways, ultimately leading to the formation of cancerous nodules along the lymphatic vessels. Clinical observations in this study indicate a significant association between the occurrence of lung cancer-related lymphangitis and the positive status of EGFR in peripheral blood, suggesting its potential as a biological marker for EGFR mutations. Multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that, after adjusting for confounding factors such as age and smoking status, cancerous lymphangitis remained an independent risk factor for effective EGFR mutations. This association may partly be explained by the hypothesis that EGFR mutations could enhance tumor invasive potential via activating PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascades to facilitate lymphatic infiltration; however, this causal biological link lacks direct experimental evidence and requires further verification in subsequent mechanistic studies (58). Nonetheless, the current research is limited by a relatively small sample size, and further validation through multi-center, large-sample cohort studies is necessary to determine whether it should be utilized as an independent observational indicator.

In the clinical management of patients with NSCLC, the incidence of brain metastases ranges from 30% to 50%, while bone metastases occur in 30% to 40% of cases (59-62). A potential correlation exists between the clinical characteristics of these metastases and the status of EGFR mutations, offering a novel perspective for predicting EGFR mutation status. Yao et al. (63) performed a retrospective analysis involving 75 patients with lung adenocarcinoma and concomitant bone metastasis. Their findings indicated that bone metastasis and a non-smoking status serve as independent predictors of EGFR mutations. This observation implies that metastatic lesions may harbor biological information pertinent to driver gene mutations, thereby paving the way for future research endeavors. In recent years, advancements in radiomics technology have elucidated the intrinsic relationship between the imaging features of metastatic foci and EGFR mutations (64-68). Radiomic studies focusing on brain and bone metastases have demonstrated that models integrating features from both the primary and metastatic lesions offer significant advantages. Specifically, research on brain metastases indicates that a combined model incorporating features from both the primary lesion and brain metastasis achieves an AUC of 0.936 in the training set, markedly surpassing the AUC of 0.786 for models relying solely on the primary lesion. Similarly, the AUC for the combined model of bone metastasis features is 0.929, representing a substantial improvement over the single metastasis model, which has an AUC of 0.843. Furthermore, the multi-organ combined model exhibits stable predictive performance in external validation, with AUC values ranging from 0.865 to 0.882 (69). These findings affirm that the radiomic features of metastatic lesions can effectively augment the molecular information derived from the primary tumor, thereby offering novel insights for the precise diagnosis and treatment of metastatic lung cancer (70,71). Additional research suggests that the heterogeneous characteristics of metastatic foci may reflect the EGFR mutation status through quantitative imaging analysis.

This study further confirmed through multivariate logistic regression analysis that non-vertebral bone metastasis (OR =4.085, 95% CI: 1.853–9.005), brain metastasis (OR =5.107, 95% CI: 2.654–9.828), and liver metastasis (OR =4.409, 95% CI: 1.594–12.191) serve as independent predictors of peripheral blood EGFR positivity (P<0.01). These findings corroborate the conclusion that radiomic features from different metastatic sites convey specific information regarding peripheral blood EGFR positivity. The current research primarily investigates the correlation between bone metastasis and peripheral blood EGFR positivity, without performing an anatomical subdivision of the bone metastasis sites. This study identifies non-vertebral bone metastasis as an independent predictor of peripheral blood EGFR positivity; however, vertebral metastasis does not exhibit a significant association with peripheral blood EGFR positivity (P>0.05). We hypothesize that the systemic hematogenous spread pattern and unique local microenvironment of non-vertebral bone lesions may lead to higher ctDNA shedding, which could partially account for its independent predictive value for blood EGFR positivity. In contrast, vertebral metastases feature different hemodynamic and microenvironmental conditions, which may reduce ctDNA release efficiency. These interpretations remain purely speculative and cannot be confirmed with the current clinical imaging and genetic data; targeted translational research is required to validate these differential microenvironment effects. These potential biological mechanisms require further investigation for clarification. EGFR signal transduction plays a crucial role in the proliferation of osteoclast precursors. Specifically, EGFR signaling promotes osteoclast formation by inhibiting the expression of OPG and enhancing the expression of monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP1), which induces osteoclast fusion and activity, as well as macrophage colony-stimulating factor (MCSF) and RANKL (72,73). While this mechanism offers a plausible theoretical framework linking EGFR mutations to bone metastasis biology, it is important to emphasize that our clinical cohort data do not provide direct molecular or histological evidence to substantiate this causal pathway. The integration of these preclinical findings with our clinical observations is speculative and intended primarily to generate hypotheses for future translational research rather than to serve as definitive biological evidence. EGFR-driven osteoclast activation may modify the metabolism and structure of the bone microenvironment, thereby influencing the quantitative features of CT/MRI images, such as bone density heterogeneity and osteolytic lesion morphology. This provides theoretical support for the biological interpretability of radiomics models.

Currently, ARMS-PCR is extensively utilized in domestic hospitals, supplanting traditional Sanger sequencing for the detection of EGFR gene mutation types through quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) (74). This method offers several advantages, including its established reliability, simplicity, and ease of experimental execution. Additionally, it features a brief detection cycle, minimizes the risk of toxic procedures, and enables the early identification of genetic variations, thereby aligning more closely with contemporary clinical requirements (75). However, ARMS-PCR is limited to detecting known mutations. Screening for unknown mutation regions using ARMS-PCR requires consuming a large number of DNA samples and designing multiple sets of primers, leading to a detection cost similar to that of NGS. Several studies have validated the high specificity (>98%) of ARMS-PCR in identifying common sensitive mutations of EGFR, such as exon 19 deletion and L858R, showing relatively strong concordance with NGS results (76,77). Prior research (78,79) has indicated that co-mutations of EGFR with tumor suppressor genes like TP53, RB1, and NF1 can notably elevate the tumor mutation burden (TMB), diminishing the response to EGFR-TKI treatment by enhancing tumor heterogeneity and activating drug resistance pathways, consequently significantly shortening patients’ PFS and OS.

For critically ill patients with a strong suspicion of EGFR positivity, such as those experiencing rapid disease progression or respiratory failure, it is advisable to prioritize ARMS PCR for rapid initial screening. This approach facilitates the timely initiation of targeted therapy, maximizes the opportunity for survival benefits, and mitigates treatment delays associated with the 7–10-day turnaround for NGS testing. ARMS-PCR is limited to detecting known mutations, with each reaction system targeting specific mutation types. Detection of rare mutations, like exon 20 insertions, may require supplementary verification with NGS due to the restricted sensitivity of ARMS-PCR determined by preset primer coverage (80). A case study highlighted these limitations: despite a high mutation abundance of 18.33% detected by peripheral blood NGS in a patient with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutation, ARMS-PCR failed to detect the mutation because the probe was not pre-set. This case illustrates that even with relatively high ctDNA abundance (>15%), ARMS-PCR may yield false negatives due to target design constraints, whereas NGS can effectively identify rare variations through comprehensive sequencing. Therefore, for patients with negative routine test results but strong clinical suspicion, NGS re-evaluation should be considered to prevent missed diagnoses and guide personalized treatment decisions.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, this is a single-center retrospective study that may be subject to selection bias. Although we performed internal bootstrap validation (optimism-corrected AUC 0.866), the model’s generalizability must be verified through external validation in prospective, multi-center, multi-ethnic cohorts before widespread clinical implementation. Second, the biological mechanisms linking organ-specific metastatic patterns to EGFR gene positivity remain insufficiently characterized; future research should incorporate quantitative imaging parameters to clarify the dynamic relationship between metastatic burden and molecular subtypes. Third, and most importantly from a translational perspective, our Gspnbl score is specifically designed to predict the detectability of EGFR mutations in peripheral blood ctDNA—reflecting ctDNA shedding capacity and systemic tumor dissemination—rather than to predict the intrinsic EGFR mutation status of the tumor tissue itself. Therefore, the score should be interpreted as a tool for identifying candidates most suitable for liquid biopsy, not as a definitive surrogate for tissue genotyping. This distinction is critical to avoid overinterpretation of the model’s clinical utility. Furthermore, our exclusion of patients with other actionable driver mutations (e.g., ALK, ROS1, KRAS, MET, BRAF, HER2, RET) may introduce selection bias, as these patients represent distinct molecular subgroups with potentially different clinical and metastatic characteristics; therefore, the applicability of our Gspnbl score to the broader NSCLC population warrants further investigation in unselected cohorts.

Conclusions

This study introduces, for the first time, a Gspnbl scoring system that incorporates clinical characteristics, such as gender and smoking status, along with patterns of multi-organ metastasis, including brain metastasis, non-vertebral bone metastasis, liver metastasis, and lung cancer-related lymphangitis. This system facilitates precise stratification of EGFR mutation risk in peripheral blood. Based on the score, a staged detection strategy is recommended. For patients with a high probability of peripheral blood EGFR positivity (Gspnbl score ≥3), blood ARMS-PCR detection or simultaneous NGS detection is preferred; if the result is negative, tumor tissue detection is subsequently performed. Conversely, patients with a score ≤2 may proceed directly to tissue multi-gene NGS to avoid unnecessary costs and potential false-negative liquid biopsy results. Importantly, this score is designed to screen candidates most likely to yield positive liquid biopsy results, rather than to predict tissue EGFR mutation status. This approach reduces the time required for molecular pathology diagnosis and mitigates the treatment delays associated with NGS testing, which can elevate the risk of patient mortality.
