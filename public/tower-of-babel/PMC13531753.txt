Ceramide: a pivotal lipid mediator shaping female reproductive homeostasis and pathophysiology
Authors: Wujia Wang, FangYi Zhang, Lixuan Huang, Jie Qiao, Xinyu Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCeramide, a central hub in sphingolipid metabolism, serves as a critical bioactive signalling mediator that integrates lipid metabolism with multiple signalling pathways, primarily promoting autophagy and apoptosis. It influences female reproductive homeostasis and pathophysiology through multiple dimensions: altered ceramide levels have been observed in various reproductive disorders—such as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) and endometriosis (EM)—as well as across the female lifespan. These changes impact key processes including gonadotropin release, follicular development, and uterine microenvironment homeostasis. This review systematically summarizes the effects of ceramide on female fertility, highlighting its role as a pivotal entry point for understanding reproductive endocrine disorders in women. Modulating ceramide levels may represent a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance fertility and promote female reproductive health.Keywords: Ceramides, Female fertility, Reproductive health, Ovarian diseases, Follicular development, Uterine diseases, Female reproductive health across the lifespan

Introduction

In recent years, the growing prominence of population ageing and the rising incidence of reproductive disorders in China have drawn increasing attention to female fertility issues. Sphingolipid metabolism regulates follicle fate, oocyte quality, and embryo implantation. Disruptions in this metabolism are observed in various female fertility disorders. Ceramide (Cer) serves as the central hub of sphingolipid metabolism. It functions as both a membrane structural component and a second messenger, and is involved in membrane function, cytoskeletal regulation, signal transduction, autophagy, apoptosis, oxidative stress, and extracellular vesicle formation. Thus, Cer holds potential as a biomarker for female fertility diseases. In general, Cer promotes apoptosis, whereas its downstream metabolite Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) promotes cell survival. The Cer/S1P balance is critical for female fertility. However, Cer is not a single entity but a heterogeneous class of molecules. Due to differences in chain length and subcellular localization, different Cer species may exert opposite biological effects. A detailed understanding of Cer heterogeneity will improve our knowledge of how sphingolipids affect female fertility and help identify more precise therapeutic targets. This review focuses on changes in Cer levels and its role in ovarian and uterine diseases, as well as across the female reproductive lifespan. We highlight the potential of Cer as a biomarker and drug target, and summarize the impact of the Cer/S1P rheostat and Cer heterogeneity on pathological processes.

Biosynthesis of ceramides

Ceramides consist of a sphingosine backbone linked to a variable-length fatty acyl chain. Fatty acyl chains are typically saturated or monounsaturated, with long-chain (C16–C20) and very long-chain (C22–C24) ceramides being the most abundant in mammalian cells.

Ceramides are synthesized through three major pathways: de novo synthesis, the salvage pathway, and sphingomyelin hydrolysis (Fig. 1). The de novo pathway occurs mainly on the outer leaflet of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), although mitochondria-associated synthesis has also been reported. Sphinganine reacts with fatty acyl-CoAs of various chain lengths in a reaction catalysed by (dihydro)ceramide synthases (CerSs) to produce dihydroceramide. Six CerS isoforms (CerS1–6) have been identified, each of which displays distinct substrate selectivity towards specific fatty acyl-CoA chain lengths. Among these, CerS6 serves as the principal enzyme that catalyses the synthesis of C16-dihydroceramide. In the salvage pathway, complex sphingolipids are hydrolysed within lysosomes to produce ceramide. Sphingomyelin hydrolysis serves as the primary cellular source of ceramide, a process that can be activated by stimuli such as cytokines (e.g., tumour necrosis factor-α,TNF-α; interleukin-1β,IL-1β), thermal stress, and radiation.In this process, sphingomyelinase (SMase) cleaves Sphingomyelin (SM) to generate ceramide. Mammalian cells express three types of SMases, which are classified based on their optimal pH: acidic, alkaline, or neutral.

Ceramide serves as the precursor for various sphingolipids. Newly synthesized ceramide in the ER can be transported to the Golgi apparatus via ceramide transfer protein (CERT), where it undergoes modifications, including glucosylation, phosphorylation, and deacylation, to form complex sphingolipids.

Alternatively, ceramide can be hydrolysed by ceramidase (CDase) to yield sphingosine, which is then phosphorylated by sphingosine kinase (SphK) to form sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P). SphK exists as two major isoenzymes: SphK1 and SphK2. SphK1 is primarily localized in the cytoplasm and at the plasma membrane, whereas SphK2 is predominantly found in the endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, and nucleus. Interestingly, SphK1 overexpression is associated with cell survival, while SphK2 overexpression promotes apoptosis, which may be attributed to the unique pro-apoptotic BH3 domain of SphK2 [2]. The ceramide/S1P rheostat maintains the pivotal balance between proapoptotic and prosurvival signalling in cells. Alterations in the ceramide/S1P ratio underlie the development of various metabolic disorders.

Subcellular localization of ceramide

Owing to their high hydrophobicity, ceramides cannot exist in a soluble state within intracellular or extracellular fluids. Consequently, unless transported by carrier proteins or vesicles, ceramides remain embedded in the lipid membranes where they are synthesized. They form relatively isolated compartments that function as localized centres of sphingolipid metabolism—a phenomenon termed “sphingolipid compartmentalization”.

Ceramide in specific membrane pools mediates relatively independent signaling pathways. For example, mitochondrial ceramide induces membrane channel formation, leading to cytochrome c release and interaction with the pro-apoptotic protein Bax to mediate apoptosis [3, 4]. Endoplasmic reticulum ceramide accumulation triggers ER stress, promotes ceramide transport to mitochondria, and induces apoptosis [5]. Plasma membrane ceramide forms lipid rafts to recruit membrane receptors and activate downstream signaling [6], or directly interacts with protein targets to trigger signaling events [7–9].

Cer/S1P rheostat

The Cer/S1P rheostat was first proposed by Cuvillier et al. in 1996 [10]. Upon stimulation with tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, Fas ligand (members of the TNF superfamily), iCAM, CD28, CD5, as well as UV and heat stress, aSMase translocates to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane and hydrolyzes SM to generate Cer [11]. Cer can form lipid rafts to recruit receptor molecules such as CD95 and CD40 [12], or directly bind and activate protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A) and certain PKC isoforms (e.g., protein kinase C-ζ, PKCζ) [13], thereby inhibiting survival signals. A typical signaling pathway involved is the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) pathway. PI3K/Akt pathway mediates sex hormone signalling (e.g., progesterone, P4, and oestrogen) and plays a critical role in regulating early follicular development. Dysregulation of this pathway is implicated in various diseases, including IR, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), and ovarian cancer (OC).

PI3K is a member of the intracellular lipid kinase family and is composed of regulatory subunits (p55 and p85) and a catalytic subunit (p110). PI3Ks are classified into three classes based on their subunit composition and substrate specificity, with class I PI3Ks being the most prominent. Upon stimulation with upstream signals (e.g., G protein-coupled receptors, GPCRs and receptor tyrosine kinases, RTKs), PI3K catalyses the conversion of the membrane lipid phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) into phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP3), which exposes binding sites for proteins containing a pleckstrin homology domain (PH).

Akt is a serine/threonine kinase. PI3K participates in Akt activation, and activated Akt targets multiple downstream substrates, including Bcl-2, FOXO1, Bax, and mTORC. Overall, the PI3K/Akt pathway promotes cell survival and suppresses apoptosis.

Ceramide accumulation activates PP2A, leading to the dephosphorylation of Akt at Thr308, thereby suppressing its activation. Ceramide can also activate PKCζ, which phosphorylates Ser34 in the PH domain of Akt, preventing its interaction with PIP3. The dominant inhibitory mechanism in vivo, however, remains unclear [13].

Cer also activates inflammatory pathways. TNF-α activates aSMase, which promotes Cer generation [14]. On one hand, Cer promotes the transcription of inflammation-related genes by activating the transcription factor NF-κB; on the other hand, it influences the activity of CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein (C/EBP) family transcription factors, thereby inducing the expression of various inflammatory protein genes such as TNF, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-1β [15]. Furthermore, Cer-enhanced MAPK signaling activates c-Jun, which in turn induces COX-2 gene expression via the cAMP response element in the COX-2 promoter [16].

S1P is a critical signalling lipid with distinct intra- and extracellular functions. In the cytoplasm, TRAF2 activates proteins such as tumour necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2) and peroxisome proliferation-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) to regulate inflammation and angiogenesis. Following export, extracellular S1P binds to one of five GPCRs (S1PRs), activating pathways such as phospholipase C (PLC), PI3K, and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) to promote cell survival and proliferation [17, 18]. The ceramide/S1P rheostat serves as a key determinant of cellular fate. Its dysregulation has been observed in various female reproductive disorders, suggesting that restoring this lipid balance has therapeutic potential.

Ceramide alterations in obesity/metabolic diseases

Obesity and dysregulated lipid metabolism can increase ceramide levels through multiple mechanisms, which in turn exacerbate these conditions, leading to complex metabolic syndromes. In obese individuals, saturated long-chain fatty acids(FAs)are less efficiently oxidized than unsaturated FAs are, driving ceramide synthesis. Obesity is often associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, characterized by the secretion of various inflammatory mediators—including TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, and C-reactive protein—from both hypertrophied adipocytes and macrophages infiltrating adipose tissue. TNF-α activates key enzymes involved in ceramide synthesis, including aSMase, nSMase and SPT, thereby increasing ceramide levels. FAs can also activate Toll-like receptors (TLRs), promoting the secretion of TNF-α and IL-6. Additionally, the dysregulation of glucocorticoids, the gut microbiota, and bile acid homeostasis is linked to ceramide accumulation [19].

IR is a common feature of many female reproductive disorders. Insulin acts on its receptors in adipose and muscle tissues, upregulating glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) to increase cellular glucose uptake. Concurrently, it activates glycogen synthase and suppresses glycogenolysis, thereby converting glucose into glycogen. Insulin primarily exerts its effects via the PI3K/Akt pathway. As mentioned earlier, ceramide inhibits Akt activity, impairs GLUT4 translocation to the membrane, and disrupts glucose utilization, leading to IR [20]. Indeed, the antilipolytic action of insulin can further promote ceramide accumulation, exacerbating chronic inflammation.

The relationship between ceramides and the development of the female reproductive system is highly complex. Ceramide-mediated sphingolipid signalling regulates gonadal cell development, apoptosis, and pregnancy processes in both foetal and postnatal females and is associated with changes in gonadal function. Elevated ceramide levels have been detected in individuals with one of several female reproductive disorders, suggesting that ceramides may facilitate disease progression by promoting inflammation and amplifying OS.

Ceramide and ovarian diseases

Ceramides and disorders of follicular development

The ovarian follicle is the fundamental functional unit of the ovary and consists of an oocyte surrounded by granulosa cells and theca cells. According to the developmental stage, follicles can be classified as primordial, primary, secondary, preantral, early antral, antral, or mature. Following successful ovulation, the remaining follicular cells form the corpus luteum (CL) under the influence of luteinizing hormone (LH), which secretes P4. In mammalian ovaries, only a small fraction of follicles reach maturity and ovulate, whereas the vast majority undergo apoptosis and atresia. Follicular growth is achieved through granulosa cell proliferation and antrum formation, whereas follicular development involves coordinated processes of cell proliferation, apoptosis, and angiogenesis.

aSMase serves as a key mediator of stress-induced and developmentally programmed oocyte apoptosis, with normal mice exhibiting an 80% loss of oocytes prior to birth. aSMase-deficient mice present a twofold increase in the oocyte number at birth. Radiation exposure triggers aSMase activation in oocytes, leading to apoptotic cell death. Genetic ablation of aSMase confers protection against oocyte apoptosis [21]. Incubation of Xenopus oocytes with bSMase increased ceramide levels and induced germinal vesicle breakdown without cytotoxicity. In contrast, microinjection of bSMase led to ROS production, cytochrome c release from mitochondria, and caspase-3 activation, ultimately resulting in oocyte apoptosis. These findings indicate that the effects of ceramide are determined by its localization. Intracellular ceramide induces apoptosis via the mitochondrial pathway in an oxidative stress-dependent manner [22].

Steroid hormones coordinate follicular development through a spatiotemporal regulatory network. Steroid hormone synthesis utilizes cholesterol as a substrate and is initiated by the binding of trophic peptide hormones—adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), LH, and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)—to GPCRs in target tissues, a process mediated through the protein kinase A/cAMP-dependent pathway. FSH and LH are the primary hormones that promote follicular proliferation and survival. Deficiencies in FSH and LH signalling lead to follicular atresia. Ceramide can function as a second messenger in cytokine and growth factor signalling pathways, regulating the biosynthetic pathways of steroid hormones such as P4 and testosterone (T) at multiple levels. In ovarian granulosa cells, IL-1β inhibits P4 production in a ceramide-dependent manner by activating SMase [23], whereas IL-1 reduces prostaglandin expression through ceramide-mediated postinhibitory effects, thereby suppressing ovulation [24]. In rat Leydig cells and luteal cells, ceramide inhibits human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG)-stimulated T and P4 production [25, 26].

Ceramide is involved in the signalling of steroid hormones (Fig. 2). Oocytes remain arrested at prophase I of meiosis for an extended period and resume meiosis upon hormonal stimulation. P4 serves as the maturation-inducing hormone for oocytes. Within minutes after P4 exposure, the methylation of glycerophospholipids, as well as changes in other plasma membrane lipids, including sphingomyelin, are observed in frog oocytes [27]. P4 can activate Mg²⁺-dependent nSMase, resulting in increased ceramide production [28]. These findings suggest that lipids function as secondary messengers during the initial signalling events in oocyte maturation.

Through membrane receptor (mPR)-mediated signalling, P4 suppresses cAMP and activates maturation-promoting factor (MPF), thereby initiating Xenopus oocyte maturation. Under physiological conditions, ceramide is produced via sphingomyelin hydrolysis. The inhibition of glucosylceramide (GlcCer) synthase or SMase results in the accumulation of ceramide on the inner membrane of transport vesicles, which can induce oocyte maturation in a cAMP downregulation-dependent manner [29].

Ceramide is an essential second messenger in oocyte meiosis. Blocking the conversion of sphingosine to ceramide with fumonisin B1 completely inhibits sphingosine-induced germinal vesicle breakdown in oocytes [28]. However, the effects of exogenous ceramide treatment on oocytes have been inconsistent in previous studies. For example, Véronique De Smedt et al. [29] reported that neither an incubation with nor microinjection of ceramide could initiate meiosis in unstimulated Xenopus oocytes. During the breeding season, exogenous ceramide or SMase treatment of defolliculated frog oocytes can induce meiosis, whereas no such effect is observed outside the reproductive period [30]. Thus, elevated ceramide levels do not appear to directly initiate oocyte meiosis, but rather depend on the initial state of the oocyte and are associated with P4-dependent downstream signaling pathways. The downstream metabolite of ceramide, S1P, can be transported extracellularly and activate various signalling pathways—from phospholipase to cAMP—via S1P receptors, thereby influencing oocyte maturation [31].

During folliculogenesis, mitochondrial metabolism is highly active. Unlike most somatic cells, oocytes exhibit nearly complete glycolytic shutdown and rely exclusively on mitochondria for ATP production. With advancing age, mitochondrial dysfunction impairs oogenesis and embryonic development [32]. Decreased CERT activity disrupts ceramide trafficking, leading to its accumulation in the ER. This process may indirectly alter mitochondrial ceramide levels and contribute to the functional decline of oocytes [33].

The balance of signalling pathways is also critical for follicular development. The PI3K/Akt pathway accelerates primordial follicle recruitment, maintains the follicular reserve, and promotes the transition from primary to secondary follicles [34]. Loss of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN, a negative regulator of PI3K) results in excessive primordial follicle activation, rapid follicular depletion, and premature ovarian failure [35]. Transgenic mice with PTEN deletion develop granulosa cell tumours and ovarian serous adenocarcinoma [36]. Conversely, deficiency of the protein kinase CK2β downregulates PI3K/Akt signalling, reduces phosphorylated Akt levels, and leads to massive oocyte loss [37]. Autophagy plays a critical role in follicular development, while the PI3K/Akt/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signalling pathway suppresses autophagic activity to prevent excessive autophagy-induced apoptosis in both oocytes and granulosa cells [38]. Ceramide may promote follicular atresia by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt pathway through PP2A and PKCζ. However, its specific role in ovulation disorders remains poorly understood.

In summary, ovulation is a cyclical mild inflammatory process in which ceramide—acting as both a proinflammatory substance and a second messenger—plays a complex dual role. While intense inflammatory signals often trigger oocyte apoptosis, moderate inflammatory stimuli promote oocyte maturation and expulsion. This outcome is not contradictory; rather, it reflects the precise subcellular localization, localized concentration, and chain-length specificity of distinct ceramide species.

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)

PCOS is a leading cause of infertility among women of reproductive age, with a steadily increasing global incidence. This condition is characterized by core endocrine dysfunction, which disrupts oocyte maturation and promotes the development of multiple ovarian follicular cysts. Ovulatory dysfunction results in irregular or absent menstrual cycles. PCOS hinders fertilization and conception, and even if embryo implantation is successful, the risks of miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm birth, and eclampsia are significantly increased [39]. The key diagnostic criteria include oligo-ovulation or anovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and a polycystic ovarian morphology. Additionally, the majority of patients present with metabolic disturbances such as obesity and IR.

Characteristic metabolic alterations in PCOS

PCOS is a complex endocrine disorder associated with various metabolic and inflammation-related biomarkers, among which sex hormones and lipid metabolites are key indicators.

Patients with PCOS present elevated levels of androgens, oestradiol (E2), and T but reduced P4 levels [40].Multiple studies have investigated lipid metabolism changes in PCOS, but conclusions vary due to common comorbidities such as IR and obesity. Elevated Cer in serum and follicular fluid of PCOS patients has been reported [41–43], particularly in those with obesity and IR, where levels of S1P, Cer, and SM are significantly increased [44]. After BMI adjustment, plasma sphingolipid levels were slightly downregulated and Cer decreased in PCOS, possibly due to reduced de novo synthesis [45], while follicular fluid Cer showed no significant change [42]. Moran et al. found no differences in lipid profiles between PCOS and controls, and free androgen index was positively correlated with Cer independent of PCOS [46].

PCOS itself has different subtypes. An unsupervised hierarchical cluster analysis suggested two subtypes: (i) a reproductive group, characterized by higher LH and SHBG with lower BMI and insulin; (ii) a metabolic group, characterized by higher BMI, glucose, and insulin with lower LH and SHBG [47]. These findings suggest that lipidomic profiles differ between PCOS subtypes, and Cer elevation is more closely associated with obesity than with a pathological process unique to PCOS. Nevertheless, Cer may serve as an indicator for distinguishing subtypes and predicting response to metabolic therapies such as metformin [48].

Key pathogenic mechanisms of ceramide in PCOS

In PCOS patients with metabolic disturbances, elevated ceramides disrupt follicle maturation and dominant follicle formation via an IR-HI-HA-neuroendocrine vicious cycle.

Regardless of BMI in PCOS patients, insulin-mediated glucose uptake is reduced [49]. Ceramides are both downstream products and upstream contributors to IR. Insulin primarily mediates glucose uptake through the PI3K/Akt pathway, and ceramides can inhibit this process by attenuating Akt activation, thereby suppressing the insulin-stimulated translocation of GLUT4.

After metformin treatment, patients with PCOS exhibit improved glucose effectiveness and weight loss and reduced levels of total ceramides, total Cer(d16:0), and total Cer(d18:2). Baseline Cer(d16:0) levels may predict the therapeutic efficacy of metformin, but since ceramides are associated with BMI, the predictive effect becomes nonsignificant after adjusting for BMI subgroups [48]. In PCOS women with normal BMI but impaired insulin signaling, skeletal muscle ceramide levels are significantly elevated, yet no significant difference in Akt phosphorylation within the insulin pathway is observed, suggesting that ceramides may not be the primary inhibitors of insulin signaling in skeletal muscle. The skeletal muscle resistance in normal-BMI PCOS patients is more likely associated with decreased plasma adiponectin levels, reduced AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activity, and downregulated peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα) transcription. This further demonstrates the heterogeneity of PCOS patients [50].

The levels of female sex hormones are regulated by the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒ovarian (HPO) axis. Pulsatile secretion of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from hypothalamic GnRH neurons stimulates the anterior pituitary to secrete FSH and LH, which regulate follicular growth, ovulation, and CL formation.

In patients with PCOS, the GnRH pulse frequency is increased, leading to increased LH secretion. This excess LH stimulates theca cells to produce excess androgens. Moreover, FSH levels remain unchanged or decrease, impairing follicular maturation and resulting in polycystic ovarian changes. HI can further stimulate GnRH release, promoting LH secretion and contributing to the PCOS phenotype [51]. Moreover, insulin upregulates the transcription factor kruppel-like factor 15 (KLF15), thereby promoting T synthesis [52]. Both HI and hyperandrogenaemia (HA) can suppress the hepatic synthesis of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), thereby increasing the serum levels of free sex hormones [53]. The initiating factors underlying abnormal GnRH secretion in patients with PCOS remain unclear. Early-onset obesity may increase the expression of SPTLC1, a key enzyme involved in ceramide synthesis in the paraventricular nucleus, which enhances ovarian sympathetic nerve activity. This process drives premature ovarian maturation through a nongonadotropin-dependent pathway, suggesting that the central ceramide/sympathetic nervous system may be associated with PCOS [54].

In patients with PCOS, ovarian granulosa cells commonly exhibit OS, mitochondrial dysfunction, OS-induced apoptosis, and dysregulation of the PI3K/Akt pathway. Activation of the PI3K/Akt signalling pathway reduces downstream signal molecule-induced apoptosis, protects granulosa cells from OS, and ameliorates both IR and autophagy in PCOS [55, 56].

Currently, limited literature is available on the association between S1P and PCOS. Li et al. reported elevated S1P levels in PCOS patients with obesity and insulin resistance (IR), which correlated with IR status [44]. Increased SphK1 mRNA expression has been observed in adipose tissue of obese mice and patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus [57], suggesting that elevated S1P may result from obesity and metabolic stress rather than being a pathological feature unique to PCOS. Endogenous S1P promotes obesity and type 2 diabetes via S1P2, whereas exogenous S1P counteracts these effects through S1P1. Moreover, high S1P levels can exacerbate lipotoxicity by promoting lipolysis and releasing free fatty acids (FFAs) [58, 59]. Therefore, elevated S1P levels are likely to amplify the inherent metabolic disturbances in PCOS, although further studies are needed to clarify its specific impact.

In summary, ceramide levels reflect the IR–HI–HA imbalance in women with PCOS. Impaired follicular maturation in patients with PCOS may result primarily from steroid hormone dysregulation, whereas ceramide accumulation exacerbates PCOS symptoms by promoting OS and apoptosis.

Translational potential of ceramide in PCOS

Sphingolipid metabolism and its associated cellular senescence are intervention targets in PCOS. Genes such as LYN, PLCG2, STAT5B, MMP9, and IL6R have the potential as biomarkers for PCOS-related anovulation, and the COX inhibitor aspirin can ameliorate cellular senescence and sphingolipid dysregulation in PCOS [60]. Treatment with metformin reduces the levels of three ceramide species, suggesting that the effects of metformin on androgen metabolism and the improvement of insulin resistance may be partially mediated by the regulation of sphingolipid metabolism [61]. Biliopancreatic diversion with duodenal switch surgery improves PCOS-related insulin resistance and reproductive disorders by elevating bile acids and reducing ceramide levels, indicating that targeting the bile acid-ceramide axis may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for metabolic dysfunction in PCOS [62].

Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)

POI refers to the irreversible decline in ovarian function before the age of 40 and is characterized by primary or secondary amenorrhea. Elevated FSH levels (> 25 IU/L) constitute one of the diagnostic criteria for POI. Common aetiologies include genetic disorders, chromosomal abnormalities, autoimmune diseases, and cancer treatments [63]. Studies suggest that POI patients have an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases, which is likely due to a decrease in ovarian function and endogenous oestrogen deficiency [64]. Additionally, POI is associated with impaired short-term memory, reduced cognitive function, a higher incidence of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and an elevated risk of type 2 diabetes [65].

Patients with POI exhibit abnormal serum lipid profiles. Compared with those in the control groups, those in the POI groups presented significantly higher serum levels of cholesterol, triglycerides, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) [66]. Excess FAs impair oocyte developmental quality, leading to a decline in ovarian function and potentially playing a role in the pathogenesis of POI [67]. aSMase is highly expressed in ovarian tissue. Its downregulation may lead to sphingomyelin accumulation. SM subtypes d40:2 and d42:2 promote inflammatory responses by suppressing IL-10 expression, resulting in abnormal steroid synthesis, increased granulosa apoptosis, and ovarian dysfunction [68]. Ceramide also participates in intercellular communication within the aging ovary. Cumulus cells facilitate the transfer of ceramide to adjacent oocytes through gap junctions and lipid raft-mediated transport, thereby inducing apoptosis in the neighboring oocytes [69]. In ovarian tissue treated with cyclophosphate (a chemotherapeutic agent), aSMase expression is elevated and the number of atretic follicles increases [70]. An untargeted lipidomic study revealed increased levels of various ceramides in patients with POI. Ceramide treatment of KGN cells resulted in reduced E2 secretion and mirrored the elevated serum FSH levels observed clinically. A BCAA deficiency-induced increase in ceramide concentrations is among the pathogenic factors of POI and is associated with increased ROS levels triggered by ceramides [71]. Quercetin (an antioxidant) can activate the PI3K/Akt/forkhead box O3 (FOXO3) pathway, restore ovarian function in POI rats, and inhibit OS [72]. In vitro treatment of ovarian tissue from POI patients with an Akt activator promotes follicular growth and maturation [73]. In adult female mice, radiation-induced oocyte loss—a key event leading to premature ovarian failure and infertility in female cancer patients—is completely prevented by in vivo S1P treatment [74]. Dietary supplementation with serine promotes S1P synthesis, which in turn inhibits Nrf2 nuclear translocation and subsequent HO-1 expression, thereby suppressing granulosa cell ferroptosis [75].

Although studies on the relationship between POI and sphingolipids remain limited, it is plausible that regulating the Cer/S1P balance holds potential value for maintaining ovarian function.

Ovarian cancer (OC)

OC is the gynaecological malignancy with the highest mortality rate, with a five-year survival rate of only 46%. The absence of definitive screening methods and nonspecific early symptoms often leads to a diagnosis at an advanced stage.

Characteristic metabolic alterations in OC

Dysregulation of the sphingolipid pathway is prevalent in OC, and lipid metabolism disorders contribute significantly to this disease. OC cells rely on lipid metabolism to meet their energy demands, with phospholipids and sphingolipids being the primary altered lipids in these cells [76]. Tzelepi et al. reviewed 22 targeted and untargeted lipidomic studies on OC and revealed that ceramides containing 18:0, 20:0, and 24:1 FA) chains were upregulated in OC patients, whereas those containing 23:0 and 24:0 FA chains were downregulated. With respect to disease progression, the concentrations of C18-d18:1 Cer and C18-d18:0 Cer increased in early-stage OC and increased further with disease progression [77]. C18-Cer levels exhibited a positive linear trend from benign ovarian tumours to early-stage (I–II) and advanced-stage (III–IV) OC, while other lipid species declined [78, 79]. Salminen et al. [80] proposed that a dual-lipid signature of C18:0-Cer/PC (O-38:4) could predict the prognosis and recurrence rates of patients with OC.

In summary, alterations in ceramide levels in OC patients depend on the FA chain composition. Specifically, ceramide species containing 16:0, 18:0, 20:0, and 24:1 FA chains increase, whereas certain very-long-chain ceramides decrease.

Key pathogenic mechanisms of ceramide in OC

Ceramide exerts a central role in OC by promoting apoptosis, yet it can also activate autophagy as a compensatory survival mechanism. The PI3K/Akt pathway is activated in approximately 70% of OC cases, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target [81].

FOXO3, a member of the FOXO transcription factor subfamily, is highly expressed in ovarian tissue and is involved in key cellular processes, including inflammation, aerobic glycolysis, autophagy, apoptosis, OS, cell cycle arrest, and DNA damage repair. It also plays a role in suppressing tumour cell proliferation and invasion, thereby interfering with disease progression in various contexts [82]. When phosphorylated by activated Akt, FOXO3 translocates to the cytoplasm, where it reduces the transcription of target genes. Additionally, FOXO3 can be phosphorylated by AMPK [83].

Ceramide treatment of OC cells inhibits Akt phosphorylation and concurrently increases AMPK phosphorylation. This process leads to the significant upregulation of the expression of FOXO3 target genes associated with autophagy and cell death, ultimately inducing both autophagy and apoptosis in OC cells [84].

Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), the rate-limiting enzyme in prostaglandin synthesis, is linked to tumour cell growth and metastasis, increased cell adhesion, and inhibition of apoptosis. In human OVCAR-3 °C cells, ceramide activates the ERK1/2 and p38 kinase-dependent apoptotic pathways while upregulating COX-2 expression and promoting p53 phosphorylation. Notably, inhibiting COX-2 reduces ceramide-induced p53 phosphorylation and markedly attenuates apoptosis [85].

Sphingolipid dysbalance drives ovarian cancer (OC) progression and drug resistance. Upregulation of Cer/S1P-regulating enzymes (e.g., SphK, CERT, GCS) correlates with chemotherapy resistance [86–88]. Ceramide levels are lower in ovarian tumor cells than in normal tissues and further reduced in paclitaxel-resistant cells, shifting the Cer/S1P balance toward pro-survival S1P [89]. Thus, OC generally exhibits hyperactive S1P signaling alongside deficient ceramide signaling.

Translational potential of ceramide in OC

Ceramide is an important target for ovarian cancer (OC) drug development. The natural compound Sparstolonin B (SsnB) downregulates proliferation-related proteins such as PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR in OVCAR-3 cells, reduces the pro-tumorigenic sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) level, and significantly elevates long-chain C16–C24 ceramides (Cer). This disruption of sphingolipid homeostasis inhibits cancer cell growth and promotes apoptosis [90]. Glycerol di(palmitoleate) (C2), a potent cannabinoid receptor type 1 (CB1) agonist, triples CB1 expression in CB1-expressing ovarian cancer PA1 cells and induces acute ceramide accumulation (7-fold increase in C16-Cer and 10-fold in C18-Cer), leading to apoptosis [91]. In cisplatin-resistant breast cancer cells, CerK inhibition (via gene knockdown or the drug NVP-231) suppresses resistant cell growth, induces apoptosis, and enhances cisplatin efficacy [92]. Furthermore, paclitaxel promotes exosome production to induce drug resistance, whereas inhibiting ceramide synthesis with GW4869 blocks exosome formation and significantly improves the anti-tumor effect of paclitaxel [93].

Ceramides and uterine diseases

Endometriosis (EM)

EM is an oestrogen-dependent chronic gynaecological disorder that affects approximately 10% of reproductive-aged women and adolescents globally. It is characterized by the ectopic growth of endometrial-like tissue outside the uterine cavity and typically involves the ovaries, fallopian tubes, and pelvic peritoneum. It triggers chronic inflammatory responses, leading to chronic pelvic pain (CPP), including severe dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia and rectal pain. Notably, approximately 50% of EM patients suffer from EM-associated infertility (EAI), which significantly impairs their physical and psychological well-being.

Currently, the exact pathogenesis of EM remains inconclusive. Although studies have confirmed that EM affects critical reproductive processes, such as folliculogenesis, ovulation, oocyte quality and embryo implantation, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear [94]. Treatment primarily focuses on alleviating symptoms and complications while preventing recurrence. However, the clinical manifestations of EM are often nonspecific, hindering early diagnosis. Therefore, elucidating the pathogenic mechanisms to facilitate the development of targeted therapies, along with establishing reliable strategies for early detection, represent critical challenges in the field.

The pathophysiology of EM involves multiple factors, including hormonal, immune, and genetic influences. However, in recent years, increasing evidence has suggested that lipid metabolism plays a crucial role in the onset and progression of EM. Ceramide has attracted increasing attention because of its altered lipid metabolic profile in patients with EM. These changes may contribute to future research on EM (Fig. 3).

Pathophysiological roles and clinical translational potential of ceramides in EM and EC. Ceramide and its metabolic pathways contribute significantly to the pathophysiology of two major uterine disorders: EM and EC. Their specific alterations in various biofluids make them promising diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Ceramide levels exhibit distinct changes in EM tissue extracts, positioning them as potential noninvasive diagnostic tools. Furthermore, ceramide signalling is involved in regulating CPP through multiple pathways and is associated with EAI via ROS-dependent mechanisms; it is also implicated in RPL. In EC, specific ceramide species, ceramide combinations, or the S1P/ceramide ratio have strong diagnostic value. Differential ceramide metabolic profiles in endometrial biopsies may also aid in distinguishing the tumour grade. Therapeutically, targeting ceramide metabolism offers broad prospects: ceramide itself can exert direct antitumour effects; it may increase sensitivity to chemotherapy and hormonal therapy; its physicochemical properties allow its use in CNL; and several drugs targeting ceramide metabolism have already entered clinical trials

Characteristic metabolic alterations in EM

Ceramide concentrations in various body fluids of patients with EM have been reported to exhibit alterations, indicating their potential relevance in disease characterization rather than definitive diagnostic utility. A study reported that endometrial fluid from patients with ovarian EM showed relatively lower levels of glycosphingolipid monoglycosylceramide (CMH) and ceramide compared with controls [95].

In contrast, increased ceramide levels have been observed in the peritoneal fluid of patients with EAI in a clinical cohort, suggesting a possible association with infertility-related phenotypes [96]. Additionally, a pilot lipidomics study indicated that menstrual blood from EM patients exhibited elevated ceramide levels along with enrichment of ceramide-related metabolic pathways [97].

Taken together, these findings suggest that ceramide alterations may reflect disease-associated metabolic changes. However, given the variability in sample sources and study scale, their utility as reliable biomarkers for early diagnosis requires further validation in larger and standardized cohorts.

Key pathogenic mechanisms of ceramide in EM

Ceramides play an essential role in the pathophysiology of EM, particularly in the development of CPP, a hallmark symptom of the disease. CPP has substantial cognitive, behavioral, and emotional consequences, impairing the quality of life of affected women [98, 99]. Ceramides are critical lipid mediators in the modulation of pain sensitivity and have been implicated in various pain-related disorders. Experimental evidence from murine neuropathic pain models indicates that increased expression of CerS in spinal cord tissues is associated with enhanced pain sensitivity, and pharmacological inhibition of CerS can attenuate mechanical hypersensitivity [100]. However, these findings are derived from nerve injury models and may not fully recapitulate EM-associated pain.

In EM-related CPP, ceramides contribute to pain persistence through multiple signaling pathways. Inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α have been shown to activate sphingomyelinase-dependent ceramide production in neuronal systems [101]. Clinical studies have reported elevated TNF-α levels in peritoneal and follicular fluids of EM patients [101], suggesting a potential, but not yet directly established, link between inflammation and ceramide-mediated pain pathways in EM. Similarly, IL-1β has been reported to induce nerve growth factor (NGF) expression and promote neurogenesis in EM lesions, contributing to pain symptoms [101].

Ceramide has also been reported to modulate inflammatory signaling pathways, including NF-κB and p38 MAPK, which regulate COX-2 and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) expression [102]. Elevated PGE2 levels are associated with inflammation and pain severity in EM. But the extent to which ceramide directly drives this process in EM-specific contexts is not fully established. In addition, experimental studies suggest that ceramide may activate inflammasome pathways, such as NLRP2, leading to increased IL-1β production and inflammatory pain responses [103]. However, these findings are primarily based on neuronal or inflammatory models and should be interpreted with caution when extrapolated to EM.

Overall, ceramides are recognized as bioactive lipids involved in inflammatory regulation [104], but their specific mechanistic role in EM-associated CPP remains to be clarified through disease-specific studies.

Translational potential of ceramide in EM

Ceramide’s role extends beyond pain regulation, impacting reproductive health in EM patients, especially in infertility. Ceramide has been reported to exert cytotoxic effects on oocytes and embryos in experimental systems, including in vitro and animal models [105]. However, these findings are largely derived from genetic or mechanistic studies and may not directly reflect physiological conditions in EM patients. Disruption of aSMase or administration of S1P has been shown to reduce oocyte apoptosis in murine models [106], suggesting that sphingolipid balance may influence oocyte survival. while ceramide has been shown to induce apoptosis, where aSMase becomes activated under stress and generates ceramide at the plasma membrane as a second messenger, thereby triggering apoptosis in specific cells [107].

A clinical study reported increased levels of specific ceramide species, including C22:0-Cer, C24:0-Cer, and C24:1-Cer, in the peritoneal fluid of infertile EM patients compared with controls [96]. Notably, C24:1-Cer was positively associated with infertility risk, whereas C22:0-Cer and C24:0-Cer showed an inverse association. These findings suggest that ceramide species with different chain lengths may exhibit distinct biological associations; however, causality cannot be inferred from this observational study.

Mechanistic studies have suggested that long-chain ceramides may influence mitochondrial function by increasing ROS production and disrupting mitochondrial homeostasis [108]. The balance between ROS and antioxidants is crucial for oocyte maturation, and ceramides may impair this balance, leading to compromised oocyte quality and reduced fertility in EM patients. These findings underscore the importance of ceramide metabolism in reproductive pathophysiology and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target in EM-related infertility.

Emerging metabolomics data indicate that ceramide dysregulation may also be associated with recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) [109]. Increased levels of certain ceramide species, such as C24:1-Cer, have been linked to altered metabolic profiles in decidual tissues. Increased ceramide levels, particularly C24:1-Cer, have been associated with poor oocyte quality and compromised pregnancy outcomes. However, clinical studies examining ceramide’s role in RPL are still limited by small sample sizes, and further research is needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which ceramides contribute to RPL and other reproductive disorders in EM patients.

In conclusion, ceramides play multifaceted roles in the pathogenesis of EM, ranging from the regulation of pain sensitivity to their involvement in infertility and pregnancy complications. Understanding the complex mechanisms by which ceramides affect EM will provide novel insights into potential biomarkers for early diagnosis and targeted therapies. The emerging role of ceramide in EM-related diseases offers promising avenues for the development of precision medicine approaches in the management of EM.

Endometrial cancer (EC)

EC, the most common gynaecologic malignancy in developed countries, arises from the endometrial epithelium. The incidence of this disease has increased because of increasing obesity rates, tamoxifen use in breast cancer treatment, and an extended life expectancy [110]. While approximately 70% of cases are diagnosed at an early stage and are associated with excellent 5-year survival rates (95%), 10–20% of patients presenting with metastatic disease face dramatically poorer outcomes (< 20% survival), stressing the urgent need for effective early detection methods [111, 112]. EC predominantly affects perimenopausal and postmenopausal women (with a peak incidence of 50–54 years). However, 4% of cases occur in women under 40 years of age who often require fertility-sparing approaches, necessitating precise molecular classification to guide personalized treatment decisions [110]. Current diagnostic tools, including ultrasound, CT and biopsy, are useful for initial detection, but they have limitations in accurately assessing the tumour grade and stage. The standard treatment paradigm combines primary surgery with adjuvant therapies (radiation, chemotherapy, and emerging hormonal/immune-targeted options), complemented by the revolutionary The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) molecular classification system established in 2013 [113]. Most recently, metabolomic profiling has revealed distinctive alterations in the biological samples of EC patients. These lipids are emerging as particularly noteworthy metabolic markers that may serve not only as diagnostic or prognostic indicators but also as potential therapeutic targets, creating new methods for the management of EC through metabolic intervention strategies.

Characteristic metabolic alterations in EC

Alterations in ceramide metabolism have been reported in both circulating and tissue samples from patients with EC. A targeted metabolomic study analyzing serum samples from a relatively small cohort (15 EC patients and 21 controls) identified ceramides as discriminative metabolites, with a multi-marker panel achieving high diagnostic performance (AUC 0.925) [114]. Individual ceramide species, such as C16-Cer and C22-Cer, also demonstrated moderate predictive value. While these findings suggest a potential role for ceramides in EC detection, the limited sample size and lack of external validation warrant cautious interpretation.

At the tissue level, studies have reported dysregulation of sphingolipid metabolism in EC. For example, increased total sphingolipid levels and reduced S1P/ceramide ratios have been observed in tumor tissues compared with non-malignant controls [115]. These alterations may reflect changes in the balance between pro-survival and pro-apoptotic lipid signaling pathways. However, as these findings are derived from tissue-based analyses, their direct applicability to non-invasive diagnostic strategies remains to be established.

Overall, current evidence suggests that ceramide-related metabolic changes are associated with EC; however, their robustness and specificity as diagnostic biomarkers require further validation in larger, well-controlled clinical studies.

Key pathogenic mechanisms of ceramide in EC

Ceramides have been implicated in various cellular processes relevant to tumor biology, including differentiation, proliferation, and stress responses. However, evidence specifically linking ceramide metabolism to EC pathogenesis remains limited and is largely based on observational or indirect studies.

Histopathological analyses have demonstrated that ceramide-related lipid profiles differ between well-differentiated and poorly differentiated EC tumors. In particular, enhanced expression of hydroxylated ceramide species has been observed in well-differentiated endometrial adenocarcinomas [116]. Additionally, variations in fatty acid chain composition, including differences in chain length and hydroxylation status, have been associated with tumor differentiation states. These findings suggest that ceramide composition may correlate with tumor phenotype; however, whether these lipid alterations actively contribute to tumor differentiation or represent secondary metabolic changes remains unclear.

It is also important to distinguish evidence derived from normal physiological conditions. For instance, studies examining glycosphingolipid expression across the menstrual cycle have shown that certain ceramide derivatives are regulated during the secretory phase in normal endometrium [117]. While these findings highlight the hormonal sensitivity of sphingolipid metabolism, their direct relevance to EC development has not been established.

Taken together, available data indicate that ceramide metabolism may be associated with EC tumor characteristics, but mechanistic insights into causality and functional roles in tumor progression remain insufficient.

Translational potential of ceramide in EC

Ceramide metabolism has been explored as a potential therapeutic target in cancer due to its involvement in cell death and stress signaling pathways. Experimental studies have shown that pharmacological modulation of ceramide levels can induce endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and activate the unfolded protein response (UPR), leading to autophagy-associated cell death. For example, the small molecule ABTL0812 has been reported to increase dihydroceramide levels and activate the ATF4–DDIT3–TRIB3 signaling axis in cancer cell models [118]. However, these findings are primarily based on in vitro systems and are not specific to EC.

Evidence from other malignancies further supports a role for ceramides in regulating cancer cell fate. For instance, short-chain ceramide analogs (e.g., C2-Cer) have been shown to induce senescence and apoptosis in breast cancer cell lines [119]. While such studies provide insight into potential mechanisms, extrapolation to EC should be made with caution due to tumor-specific differences in metabolism and signaling pathways.

Ceramide metabolism may also influence therapeutic responses. Previous studies have suggested that modulation of ceramide synthase activity can affect sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents [120]. In the context of EC, resistance to progestin therapy remains a clinical challenge, particularly in fertility-preserving treatment strategies. Experimental evidence indicates that CerS2 (LASS2) may be involved in progestin resistance through regulation of oxidative stress pathways [121]. Although these findings provide a potential mechanistic link, they are primarily derived from cellular models and require clinical validation.

The development of ceramide-based therapeutic delivery systems has also been explored. Due to the hydrophobic nature and short half-life of ceramide, nanoliposome formulations have been designed to improve its bioavailability and reduce toxicity. Preclinical studies in other gynecologic malignancies, such as ovarian cancer, have demonstrated that ceramide nanoliposomes can enhance antitumor efficacy by promoting apoptosis and inhibiting tumor growth [122]. However, evidence supporting their efficacy in EC specifically is currently lacking.

In summary, although ceramide-targeting strategies represent a promising area of research, most evidence to date is derived from preclinical or non-EC-specific studies. Ceramide and women’s health during the lifespan

In the previous sections, we delved into the role of ceramide in ovarian, endometrial, and other female reproductive system-related diseases, particularly focusing on its potential mechanisms and clinical applications in conditions such as cancer, infertility, and endometriosis. However, women’s health extends far beyond the reproductive system. From adolescence and pubertal development to the health challenges faced during the perimenopausal and postmenopausal years, women undergo a variety of unique physiological and pathological changes that are intrinsically linked to their gender. With a more comprehensive understanding of ceramide’s role in reproductive health, it is essential to broaden our focus from gynecological and reproductive diseases to encompass overall female health across the entire lifespan. This includes the developmental processes in adolescence, the physiological changes during perimenopause and menopause, as well as the gender differences in common chronic diseases in older age. Moving forward, we will explore women’s health through the lens of the entire life course, addressing the health needs and challenges at different stages of life, and further uncovering the broad impact of biological mechanisms on women’s health across the lifespan (Fig. 4).

The association between plasma ceramide levels and health conditions across the female lifespan. This schematic illustrates the potential correlations between circulating ceramide levels and the risk or occurrence of specific health disorders at various life stages, from infancy to old age. Conditions such as acneiform dermatitis, asthma, and autism spectrum disorder may be associated with ceramide dysregulation earlier in life. During adolescence and adulthood, elevated ceramide levels are increasingly linked to metabolic and reproductive health issues, including insulin resistance, precocious puberty, ovarian and uterine diseases, and pregnancy complications such as gestational diabetes mellitus and preeclampsia. In later life stages (perimenopausal and old age), sustained high ceramide levels are strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of age-related chronic diseases, notably cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and Alzheimer's disease

Adolescent females

Ceramide in precocious puberty (PP)

Puberty represents a critical developmental stage in the female lifespan, during which individuals acquire secondary sexual characteristics and full reproductive ability. The initiation of puberty depends on the activation of the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒gonadal (HPG) axis, where increased pulsatile secretion of GnRH from the hypothalamus stimulates the production of LH and FSH, subsequently triggering the release of gonadal steroids and growth hormones. While the precise timing of normal pubertal onset varies due to genetic, nutritional, environmental and socioeconomic factors, it typically occurs between the ages of 8 and 13 years.

PP is clinically defined as the development of secondary sexual characteristics before 8 years of age or the occurrence of menarche prior to 10 years of age in girls. This condition is pathologically classified into two main subtypes: central precocious puberty, which results from premature activation of the HPG axis, and peripheral precocious puberty (PPP), which is caused by excessive peripheral production of sex steroids independent of central regulation. PP poses significant multisystem health risks for affected adolescents, including compromised linear growth due to premature epiphyseal closure, psychological distress and social adjustment difficulties stemming from physical maturation discordant with chronological age, as well as long-term metabolic consequences such as increased lifetime risks of breast cancer and metabolic syndrome. These adverse outcomes reflect complex interactions between endocrine disruption during critical developmental windows and subsequent pathophysiological alterations in multiple organ systems [123].

Central precocious puberty, the most prevalent form of precocious sexual development, is more common in females than in males and typically results from premature activation of the HPG axis. Experimental studies have suggested a potential role for ceramide metabolism in the regulation of pubertal timing. In particular, findings from obese mouse models indicate that increased ceramide synthesis in the hypothalamus may influence neuroendocrine pathways involved in reproductive maturation, although these observations are model-specific and their relevance to human physiology remains to be established [54]. In human studies, lipidomic analyses have identified differences in circulating sphingolipids between subtypes of PP. For example, specific ceramide-related metabolites, such as ceramide-1-phosphate species, have been reported to distinguish central precocious puberty from other conditions in exploratory, cross-sectional cohorts, suggesting potential biomarker utility; however, these findings require validation in larger and independent populations [124].

In summary, an in-depth investigation into the relationship between ceramide metabolism and PP has significant implications for elucidating its underlying molecular mechanisms and developing potential therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, the exploration of ceramide-related biomarkers offers new avenues for advancing targeted treatment approaches in this clinical context.

Ceramide and adolescent metabolic disorders

The increasing global prevalence of metabolic disorders (including obesity, IR, and diabetes) among adolescents represents a critical public health challenge [125]. These conditions are closely linked to long-term health risks, such as cardiovascular disease, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, and metabolic syndrome. Moreover, they may profoundly affect female reproductive health through mechanisms involving chronic inflammation and dysregulated lipid metabolism. Consequently, enhanced prevention and management of adolescent metabolic disorders have significant long-term implications for population health.

Ceramides have also been associated with metabolic disturbances, including obesity and insulin resistance, based on evidence from clinical and epidemiological studies, as well as mechanistic investigations in experimental systems [126, 127]. However, much of this evidence derives from adult populations or non-specific cohorts, and extrapolation to adolescents should be made cautiously. In younger populations, limited studies have examined ceramide metabolism in relation to metabolic disease risk. For instance, increased glycosphingolipid levels have been observed in adolescents at elevated risk for type 1 diabetes, although these findings are based on specific at-risk cohorts rather than established disease populations [128]. Additional lipidomic studies in adolescents have reported distinct metabolic profiles associated with obesity and diabetes, but these results are primarily descriptive and do not establish mechanistic relationships [129]. Nutritional interventions have been explored as potential modulators of ceramide metabolism. Some studies have reported changes in circulating ceramide levels following dietary interventions in adolescents; however, these effects are typically observed within multi-metabolite profiles, and the specific contribution of ceramide modulation to clinical outcomes remains unclear [130]. Additionally, research by Heras et al. suggested that central nervous system ceramides may participate in regulating glucose homeostasis and IR during pubertal transition [54].

Perimenopausal/menopausal females

Menopause refers to the permanent cessation of menstruation in women, representing a failure of ovarian function and the end of the reproductive capacity. Physiological menopause typically occurs between 45 and 55 years of age. The perimenopausal period constitutes a transitional phase from reproductive age to postmenopause, during which women experience gradual decreases in ovarian function and decreasing oestrogen levels. These hormonal changes induce significant metabolic alterations that substantially increase the risk of developing diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, visceral obesity, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).

Many studies have described significant changes in ceramide concentrations within the plasma lipidome of perimenopausal and postmenopausal women. Evidence from animal models, such as ovariectomized mice, has shown that estrogen deficiency may be accompanied by increases in specific ceramide species; however, these findings are derived from experimental systems and may not fully reflect human physiology [131]. In human studies, small cohort analyses have reported elevated circulating ceramide levels in postmenopausal women compared with premenopausal controls, suggesting a potential association between hormonal status and sphingolipid metabolism [132]. Additionally, cross-sectional studies across a wide age range have identified positive correlations between age and ceramide concentrations, although these findings are observational and do not establish causality [133].

A decrease in oestrogen levels and impaired hormone receptor function during menopause increase the cardiovascular disease risk in women. Meta-analyses and clinical studies have identified associations between specific ceramide species and cardiovascular outcomes; however, these analyses are typically conducted in mixed populations, and their applicability to menopausal women specifically remains to be further clarified [134].

Several interventional studies have suggested that circulating ceramide levels can be modulated through pharmacological, exercise and dietary interventions. Some intervention studies have reported reductions in circulating ceramides following exercise or pharmacological treatment, although these findings are often based on small sample sizes and short-term interventions, and their long-term clinical significance remains uncertain [135, 136]. These findings align with those of Meng et al.’s animal study. Compared with intact controls, ovariectomized mice fed high-fat diets presented significantly elevated plasma lipid levels and altered gut microbiota diversity and composition. However, this evidence is derived from animal studies, and the relevance of these interactions to human menopausal metabolism requires further investigation [137].

Notably, compared with age-matched males, perimenopausal and postmenopausal women present a higher incidence of hypertension and poorer blood pressure control [138]. The ceramide/S1P rheostat, which represents the dynamic balance between vasoconstrictive long- or very long-chain ceramides and vasodilatory S1P, plays an important role in vascular physiology [139, 140]. In the study by Li et al., ovariectomized mice presented an imbalance in the aortic ceramide/S1P rheostat and subsequent hypertension, both of which were ameliorated by oestrogen supplementation, although these findings have not yet been confirmed in human populations [141]. These findings underscore the intricate relationships among menopausal hormonal changes, dysregulation of sphingolipid metabolism and cardiovascular pathophysiology.

Although the pathophysiological mechanisms of ceramides in cardiovascular diseases and other inflammatory metabolic disorders have been extensively studied, the significance of plasma ceramide concentrations in patients with T2DM remains to be fully elucidated. Mantovani et al. conducted a series of investigations examining the associations between specific plasma ceramide concentrations and other metabolic markers in postmenopausal women with T2DM. Their analysis focused on plasma samples from postmenopausal Caucasian women with T2DM who underwent a 3-month outpatient glycaemic management program and measured six previously identified cardiovascular risk-associated ceramides (C16:0-Cer, C18:0-Cer, C20:0-Cer, C22:0-Cer, C24:0-Cer, and C24:1-Cer). After adjusting for confounding factors, elevated levels of leucine-rich α-2-glycoprotein 1 (LRG1) and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), along with the C/T or T/T genotype of the KLF6 gene rs3750861, were significantly associated with increased concentrations of these ceramides [142, 143]. However, these findings remain correlational and do not imply a direct mechanistic role.

Ageing women

Accumulating evidence suggests that ceramide levels undergo age-associated changes with distinct sexual dimorphism. The Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging revealed that women exhibit significantly higher plasma concentrations of both ceramides and dihydroceramides than men, with more pronounced age-dependent increases that correlate with the waist-to-hip ratio, cholesterol level, triglyceride level and diabetes status [144]. These associations suggest a potential link between ceramide metabolism and age-related metabolic changes, although causality cannot be established from observational data alone. Complementary serum sphingolipid profiling across female age groups revealed markedly elevated ceramide levels in elderly (75–77 years) and centenarian (105–107 years) individuals. This shift was characterized by a pronounced accumulation of hexosylceramides (HexCers) and a relative decrease in the levels of dihydroceramide species. Mechanistic investigations suggest that these age-related ceramide changes in women may stem from upregulated de novo synthesis, it also synergistically interacts with enhanced sphingomyelin degradation pathways, as evidenced by increased sphingomyelinase mRNA expression in centenarians [145]. However, these findings are primarily based on associative and omics-level analyses without direct functional validation. Furthermore, the significant increase in C24:1-Cer levels in extracellular vesicles from elderly females may influence cellular processes related to senescence in in vitro systems, although the extent to which these findings translate to human aging remains unclear [146]. These findings highlight that sex-specific and age-dependent dysregulation of ceramide metabolism likely constitutes a critical molecular basis for the heightened susceptibility to metabolic disorders observed in ageing women.

Elevated ceramide levels have also been associated with insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes in clinical and epidemiological studies. An analysis of the Mayo Clinic Aging Cohort revealed that increased ratios of C18:0/16:0 ceramide and C18:0-Cer were associated with an increased T2DM incidence and risk, whereas C16:0-Cer levels showed an inverse relationship with the diabetes risk [147]. Furthermore, increased C18:0/24:0 ratios indicated greater susceptibility to T2DM. Notably, racial differences exist in ceramide–T2DM associations: Western populations primarily exhibit correlations with saturated ceramides, whereas Chinese populations demonstrate positive associations between the T2DM risk and unsaturated ceramide species such as C20:1-Cer [148]. Additionally, elevated levels of Cer(d16:1/20:0) and Cer(d18:1/18:0) positively correlate with hepatic fat accumulation, whereas the level of LacCer(d18:1/16:0) is negatively correlated. These ceramide species have been consistently linked to impaired glucose tolerance and diabetes progression in both animal and human models [149]. However, whether ageing women exhibit distinct changes in the ceramide profile related to T2DM remains unclear, as the current findings are primarily derived from male-dominant studies investigating insulin sensitivity alterations induced by reduced physical activity [150]. This gap in knowledge suggests potential sex-specific mechanisms underlying the influence of ceramide on T2DM pathogenesis.

Research on the association between ceramide levels and the cardiovascular disease risk in elderly women is limited. Controlled human studies examining short-term physical inactivity (e.g., bed rest) have demonstrated increases in circulating ceramide levels accompanied by adverse cardiometabolic changes, suggesting a potential link between ceramide accumulation and cardiovascular risk under these conditions [151]. Furthermore, elevated levels of specific ceramide subspecies (including C18:0, C20:0, C24:1 ceramides and C20:0 dihydroceramide) correlate with decreased cardiopulmonary function in elderly individuals [152]. Notably, while individual ceramide species show variable reliability as cardiovascular disease biomarkers because of influences from sex and age factors, the C24:0/C16:0 ratio maintains relatively stable associations with cardiopulmonary fitness [153]. However, existing studies predominantly focus on the general elderly population and lack a specific analysis of elderly women; thus, sex differences in ceramide metabolism and their mechanistic effects on the cardiovascular disease risk remain unclear.

In the context of neurodegeneration, altered ceramide metabolism has been associated with AD in both human and experimental studies. While some reports suggest possible sex-related differences, the evidence remains limited and requires further validation. An increased ratio of very-long-chain (C24:0, C22:0) to long-chain (C16:0) ceramides is inversely correlated with dementia and AD risks [154]. Significant sex differences influence ceramide distribution patterns in animal models. Compared with male mice, female mice have lower ceramide levels in the hippocampus but higher ceramide concentrations in the cortex, with these regional and plasma variations influenced by the acyl chain length [155]. Notably, while elevated ceramide levels are significantly associated with an increased AD risk in males, no similar correlation has been observed in females [156]. These findings suggest that ceramides may influence AD pathogenesis through sex-specific mechanisms, although the roles of sex hormones and the precise differences in ceramide profiles between male and female AD patients require further investigation.

Maternal and child health

Ceramide metabolism plays a crucial role in maternal and infant health. Emerging evidence suggests that dynamic changes in ceramide profiles can serve as important biomarkers for metabolic disorders during pregnancy, particularly gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), preeclampsia (PE), foetal developmental abnormalities and adverse pregnancy outcomes. The regulatory mechanisms of various ceramide molecular subspecies and their metabolic enzymes during the perinatal period have been progressively elucidated. Research has established associations between ceramide dysregulation and multiple neonatal disorders, including asthma, dermatitis and autism spectrum disorder.

Disturbances in ceramide metabolism are particularly significantly associated with pregnancy complications. Elevated levels of C18:0 and C18:1 ceramide combined with reduced C24:0-Cer during early pregnancy substantially increase the risk of GDM, with trimethylamine (TMA) accumulation synergistically amplifying this effect [157, 158]. Concurrently, increased serum HYAL1 levels may further promote GDM development by increasing the ratio of high C18:0-Cer levels to low C24:0-Cer levels while also serving as a potential biomarker [159]. However, these findings are based on correlational analyses and do not establish a causal relationship. Given that pregnancy represents a state of physiological IR and considering the role of ceramide in glucose metabolic dysregulation during gestation, a metabolic lipid profiling study of Hutterite women across different reproductive states revealed characteristic lipid signatures distinguishing pregnant women from lactating women and their counterparts [160]. But the generalizability of these findings to broader populations may be limited.

PE patients not only exhibit specific alterations in plasma ceramide profiles but also exhibit excessive placental ceramide-mediated cell death, where the activation of necroptosis pathways is correlated with the protective effect of exogenous hydrogen sulfide through the suppression of ceramide toxicity mediated by the p38 MAPK pathway [161]. While high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-associated S1P and ceramide contribute to vascular protection in healthy pregnancies, this mechanism appears to be impaired in PE patients [162].

Furthermore, studies investigating therapeutic interventions during pregnancy have incorporated ceramide monitoring. Interventions that modify metabolic status, such as bariatric surgery, have been associated with changes in circulating ceramide levels and improvements in pregnancy outcomes. However, ceramides are typically part of broader metabolic profiles, and their independent contribution to clinical effects is not fully defined [163]. In patients with pregnancy-associated breast cancer (PABC) receiving chemotherapy, placental upregulation of both proapoptotic and antiapoptotic sphingolipid metabolism genes, including acid ceramidase (ASAH1), sphingosine-1-phosphate lyase 1 (SGPL1), SphK1 and CerS1-3, 5, and 6, may represent a compensatory protective mechanism [164].

Studies have shown that elevated maternal ceramide levels are also associated with poor infant development. In pregnant women with PCOS, increased blood HexCer (d18:2/24:0) levels are correlated with low infant birth weight [165]. Experimental studies in animal models have indicated that altered ceramide metabolism may affect placental function and fetal development. Placental adiponectin receptor 2 (Adipor2) overexpression can activate PPARα signalling to increase ceramide synthesis and inhibit the mTORC1 pathway, leading to foetal growth restriction [166]. Ceramides are also involved in the effects of abnormal maternal stimuli on the foetus during pregnancy. Prenatal ethanol exposure increases ceramide levels in the foetal brain, triggering apoptotic neurodegeneration in areas such as the hippocampus and cerebellum, which is associated with the pathogenesis of foetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASDs) [167]. But translation of these findings to human pregnancy should be approached with caution. Prenatal exposure to perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS) may even upregulate plasma LacCer levels in offspring, which affects the BMI of female children in a sex-specific manner [168]. Additionally, ceramides are associated with various infant and childhood conditions, including acneiform dermatitis, bronchial asthma, bronchitis, autism spectrum disorder and Down syndrome. These observational studies have reported associations between maternal ceramide levels and neonatal outcomes, but the relationships are influenced by multiple confounding factors, and further research is needed to clarify their clinical significance.

The currently available evidence has demonstrated that the ceramide metabolic network plays a special role in maintaining maternal–foetal health, where the balance and spatiotemporal regulation of its subtypes directly influence pregnancy outcomes. The interdisciplinary integration of lipidomics and clinical medicine holds promising potential for providing precise solutions for high-risk pregnancy management.

Proposed role of ceramide across the female lifespan

From a female lifespan perspective, ceramide, as a central molecule in sphingolipid metabolism, may act as an important metabolic integrator linking physiological and pathological processes across different stages of life. Through its involvement in insulin resistance, inflammation, oxidative stress, and cell fate regulation (e.g., apoptosis and autophagy), ceramide has the potential to influence both systemic metabolic states and tissue-specific microenvironments. Based on current mechanistic insights, it is plausible that metabolic disturbances at one life stage may shape disease susceptibility at subsequent stages, although such connections remain largely inferential.

During adolescence, metabolic conditions such as obesity, insulin resistance, and high-fat dietary exposure may contribute to elevated ceramide levels both systemically and in the central nervous system. These alterations could potentially affect the HPO axis, for example via the paraventricular nucleus (PVN)–sympathetic ovarian pathway, thereby influencing pubertal timing and ovarian neurodevelopment [54, 169]. At the same time, ceramide-associated metabolic imbalance and low-grade inflammation may create a permissive environment for later reproductive disorders, although direct causal links remain to be established [170].

The reproductive stage represents the longest and most functionally stable period in the female lifespan, during which reproductive capacity is fully established. The role of ceramide in common reproductive disorders affecting the ovary and endometrium has been discussed in the preceding sections. However, upon entering pregnancy, women undergo substantial physiological changes that distinguish this state from the non-pregnant condition. These include elevated estrogen and progesterone levels, increased placental hormone secretion, progressive insulin resistance to support fetal growth, enhanced lipid accumulation, and shifts in immune and inflammatory balance [171].

Against this background, ceramide metabolism may also be dynamically altered. For instance, changes in ceramide profiles have been linked to impaired insulin signaling in GDM, while in PE, placental ceramide accumulation may contribute to oxidative stress, autophagy, and trophoblast dysfunction [172]. It is therefore possible that both pre-existing metabolic disturbances and pregnancy-induced changes interact through ceramide-related pathways to influence maternal and placental outcomes [173], although such relationships remain to be fully elucidated.

As women transition into the perimenopausal and menopausal stages, declining estrogen levels are accompanied by significant metabolic shifts. Ceramide and sphingolipid dysregulation may represent a common pathway linking prior reproductive disorders (e.g., PCOS, POI, or pregnancy complications) with increased risks of metabolic diseases [174]. It is conceivable that pre-existing metabolic and ceramide-related alterations accumulated during earlier life stages could interact with hormonal changes to exacerbate insulin resistance, adipose redistribution, and systemic inflammation [175]. However, these interactions are primarily based on mechanistic extrapolation rather than direct longitudinal evidence.

In ageing women, particularly after menopause, ceramide accumulation may further contribute to the development of chronic diseases such as T2DM, CVD, and possibly neurodegenerative disorders. As a key mediator of lipotoxicity, ceramide could link earlier ovarian dysfunction and long-term metabolic burden to organ-specific pathologies in the heart, vasculature, and brain. The concept of a “two-hit” model has been proposed, in which early-life metabolic or reproductive disturbances initiate ceramide-related cellular stress, while postmenopausal hormonal decline amplifies these effects [176, 177]. Nonetheless, this framework remains hypothetical and requires further validation.

Overall, ceramide and sphingolipid metabolism abnormalities may represent a potential unifying axis connecting metabolic syndrome, pregnancy complications, and reproductive diseases or tumors across the female lifespan. While existing studies allow for the construction of plausible mechanistic links between diseases at different life stages, current evidence is largely derived from cross-sectional observations or disease-specific models. There is a notable lack of direct longitudinal or integrative studies specifically addressing whether and how ceramide mediates interactions between diseases across different stages of life. Therefore, although a life-course framework centered on ceramide is conceptually compelling, it should be interpreted with caution and regarded as a hypothesis-generating perspective that warrants further investigation.

Differential roles of ceramide subspecies in female reproductive diseases

As summarized in the preceding sections, accumulating evidence suggests that different ceramide subspecies—distinguished by acyl chain length and subcellular localization—may exert heterogeneous and sometimes even opposing biological effects. These observations support a general concept that ceramide is not a single functional entity, but rather a structurally and functionally diverse lipid family. At a macroscopic level, certain preliminary patterns can be proposed: chain length, metabolic flux, and intracellular compartmentalization collectively determine whether ceramide signaling is biased toward cell death, survival, or metabolic adaptation.

Ceramide subspecies in female reproductive tumors: a “Double-Edged Sword”

In female reproductive malignancies, ceramide exhibits a characteristic “double-edged sword” role. As a central hub of sphingolipid metabolism, ceramide can function as a tumor suppressor by inducing apoptosis and inhibiting proliferation, while simultaneously serving as a precursor for multiple pro-survival sphingolipids that promote tumor progression. This duality appears to be tightly regulated by enzymatic networks, including ceramide synthases (CerS), sphingomyelinases, ceramidases, and glycosyltransferases, as well as by the metabolic and inflammatory state of the tumor microenvironment [178].

On the one hand, ceramide accumulation is widely associated with tumor-suppressive effects. It can activate apoptotic signaling pathways, inhibit pro-survival signaling cascades, and suppress cellular metabolism, thereby limiting tumor growth. Indeed, many conventional anticancer therapies—including chemotherapy and radiotherapy—partly exert their cytotoxic effects by increasing intracellular ceramide levels via sphingomyelin hydrolysis or de novo synthesis. In ovarian cancer, for example, the CerS2–C24:1-ceramide axis has been suggested to inhibit lamellipodia formation, thereby reducing cell migration and metastatic potential. Correspondingly, downregulation of CerS2 or reduction of very-long-chain ceramides (C22–C24) may facilitate tumor dissemination, highlighting a potential anti-metastatic role of these species [179].

On the other hand, the pro-apoptotic role of ceramide is highly context-dependent and may be counterbalanced by its metabolic conversion. A key determinant lies in chain-length specificity: long-chain ceramides such as C14–C16 (often generated by CerS5/6) have, in some tumor contexts, been associated with enhanced proliferation or survival, whereas very-long-chain ceramides (C22–C24, primarily generated by CerS2) are more frequently linked to tumor-suppressive or structural roles [179]. Ceramide subspecies in female reproductive endocrine disorders

In female reproductive endocrine and metabolic-related disorders, the functional heterogeneity of ceramide subspecies appears to be more closely associated with systemic metabolism, hormonal regulation, and the local reproductive microenvironment. Although direct mechanistic studies in classical female reproductive endocrine diseases remain relatively limited, evidence from metabolic disorders, hormone-dependent cancers, and neuroendocrine regulation allows for cautious inference of several emerging patterns.

One key dimension is chain-length specificity. Long-chain ceramides (LC; e.g., C16:0, C18:0, C20:0) are consistently associated with insulin resistance, obesity, T2DM, and cardiovascular risk. For example, C16:0-ceramide is markedly elevated in adipose tissue in obesity and is strongly linked to impaired insulin signaling, reduced glucose uptake, and lipotoxicity [184]. These features closely resemble the metabolic phenotype observed in PCOS, suggesting that long-chain ceramides may contribute to its pathophysiology. Indeed, in PCOS patients, circulating ceramide levels are elevated, and specific species such as Cer(OH_N16:0/N18:0) and Cer(N22:0) have demonstrated potential diagnostic value (AUC ≈ 0.89) [48]. In addition, baseline levels of C16:0-related ceramides appear to be associated with metabolic responsiveness to metformin treatment, further supporting their relevance to insulin resistance–related phenotypes [185].

In contrast, very-long-chain ceramides (VLC; C22–C24 and above) may exhibit more complex and context-dependent roles. In systemic metabolism, these species are often considered neutral or even protective, with ratios such as C16/C24 or C18/C24 being used to predict cardiometabolic risk [183]. However, in reproductive microenvironments, excessive accumulation of certain VLC ceramides may have detrimental effects. For example, in PCOS, elevated levels of specific ceramide species in follicular fluid (e.g., Cer 36:1;2, 38:1;2, 38:2;2, 40:2;2) have been negatively associated with oocyte quality and embryo development [41]. Similarly, in endometriosis-associated infertility, increased levels of VLC ceramides in peritoneal fluid have been linked to impaired oocyte maturation, potentially through mechanisms involving mitochondrial oxidative stress and superoxide overproduction [186].

Subcellular localization further adds another layer of functional specificity. Ceramide accumulation in organelles such as the endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, Golgi apparatus, and lysosomes may trigger distinct cellular outcomes, including apoptosis, autophagy, or senescence [186]. In endocrine-related tumor models, endoplasmic reticulum–localized ceramide has been shown to induce strong ER stress and selectively eliminate therapy-resistant cells, suggesting that compartment-specific ceramide pools may influence treatment response [187]. In ovarian physiology, similar mechanisms may plausibly affect granulosa cell survival and follicular development, although direct evidence remains limited.

In addition, central (hypothalamic) ceramide has emerged as a potential integrator of metabolic and hormonal signals. Experimental studies indicate that estradiol can regulate hypothalamic ceramide levels, while ovarian insufficiency is associated with ceramide accumulation in this region, accompanied by increased food intake, reduced energy expenditure, and disrupted glucose metabolism [188, 189]. These findings suggest that ceramide may link estrogen signaling with energy homeostasis and reproductive axis regulation, thereby providing a mechanistic bridge between metabolic disorders, puberty timing, and conditions such as PCOS or obesity-associated menstrual dysfunction.

Overall, current evidence indicates that ceramide subspecies exhibit substantial heterogeneity in female reproductive endocrine disorders, with chain length, concentration, tissue distribution, and hormonal context all influencing their biological effects. While certain trends—such as the association of long-chain ceramides with metabolic dysfunction and the context-dependent roles of very-long-chain species—can be identified, these relationships are not uniform. Importantly, most findings are based on associative studies or extrapolated mechanisms, and direct, disease-specific investigations into ceramide subspecies in female reproductive endocrine conditions remain insufficient.

Conclusions

Taken together, current evidence suggests that ceramide and its subspecies may represent a potentially unifying molecular axis linking female reproductive diseases with broader metabolic and systemic health across the female life course. Rather than acting as a single bioactive molecule, ceramide appears to function as a dynamic and heterogeneous lipid network, whose biological effects are shaped by acyl chain composition, metabolic flux, tissue specificity, and subcellular localization. Within female reproductive tumors, ceramide demonstrates a context-dependent “double-edged sword” role, balancing pro-apoptotic and pro-survival signaling through its own accumulation and its conversion into downstream sphingolipids. In endocrine and metabolic-related reproductive disorders, ceramide is more consistently associated with insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and disruption of the reproductive microenvironment, although species-specific effects remain incompletely defined.

Importantly, when viewed through the lens of female life course health, ceramide may plausibly act as a mechanistic bridge connecting disease processes across different physiological stages—from adolescence and reproductive years to pregnancy, menopause, and ageing. Early-life metabolic disturbances, reproductive disorders (e.g., PCOS, POI, endometriosis, and pregnancy complications), and menopausal hormonal shifts may all contribute to a shared metabolic milieu characterized, in part, by altered sphingolipid metabolism. This raises the possibility of a cumulative or “two-hit” model, in which reproductive-stage abnormalities initiate ceramide-associated lipotoxicity and cellular stress, while menopausal estrogen decline further amplifies these perturbations, ultimately contributing to increased risks of cardiometabolic diseases and possibly neurodegeneration in later life.

However, it is critical to emphasize that most of these connections remain inferential and are largely based on mechanistic associations, cross-sectional observations, or extrapolation from non-reproductive systems. Direct longitudinal and causal evidence demonstrating that ceramide mediates disease progression across different stages of the female lifespan is still lacking. In particular, there is a notable gap in studies that simultaneously track ceramide dynamics across tissues, life stages, and disease states, or that experimentally validate its role as a driver—rather than a byproduct—of disease transitions.

Future research should therefore move beyond single-disease frameworks toward integrative, life course–oriented approaches. Key priorities include: (i) systematic characterization of ceramide subspecies profiles across different female physiological stages and disease contexts; (ii) clarification of tissue- and organelle-specific functions of ceramide in reproductive versus systemic metabolism; (iii) longitudinal cohort studies to evaluate whether ceramide signatures can predict disease trajectories; and (iv) exploration of targeted interventions modulating sphingolipid metabolism as potential preventive or therapeutic strategies.
