Annexin A1 as a diagnostic biomarker for cyclic Cushing disease
Authors: Zichen Rao, Luming Wu, Lei Jiang, Tingwei Su, Yanan Cao, Shaojian Lin, Xiadi He, Youqiong Ye, Guang Ning, Weiqing Wang, Cui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCyclic Cushing syndrome, a rare endocrine disorder marked by periodic fluctuations of cortisol secretion and clinical manifestations, is diagnostically challenging. This study integrates clinical and multi-omics data to advance early diagnosis. We analyzed four patients with pituitary cyclic Cushing syndrome and fifty-six patients with conventional Cushing disease. Proteomics profiling identified differentially expressed proteins; Annexin A1 expression was validated via immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Single-cell RNA sequencing mapped its cellular origin. Annexin A1 expression was significantly elevated in pituitary tumors and serum of patients with cyclic Cushing syndrome. Serum Annexin A1 measured during the remission phase in patients with cyclic Cushing syndrome demonstrated promising diagnostic ability, and a related diagnostic approach was proposed. Single-cell transcriptomics revealed a distinct Annexin A1-enriched tumor subpopulation, predominantly in patients with cyclic Cushing syndrome. Multi-omics and clinical integration identify Annexin A1 as a diagnostic biomarker for cyclic Cushing disease.Keywords: cyclic Cushing syndrome, Cushing disease, Annexin A1, proteomics, single-cell transcriptomics, diagnosis

Introduction

Cyclic Cushing syndrome (CCS) is an uncommon variant of Cushing syndrome identified in the 1950s,1,2 marked by periodic excessive cortisol production (peaks) followed by spontaneous remission. It is mainly caused by pituitary adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)-producing tumors (67%), ectopic ACTH-producing tumors (17%), and adrenocortical tumors (11%).3 Hypercortisolism and remission episodes can vary regularly or irregularly and range from hours to years. Fluctuations in cortisol levels result in variable clinical appearance and discrepant biochemical findings, making the diagnosis of CCS challenging.4,5 Prolonged uncontrolled hypercortisolemia can severely harm cardiovascular, metabolic, skeletal, and nervous systems,6,7 while periodic cortisol peaks can lead to complications like hypertension, hypokalemia, and diabetes mellitus.6,8,9 Missed diagnosis and misdiagnosis frequently occur, significantly impairing patients' quality of life and mental health.10 Moreover, the unclear pathogenesis of CCS hinders the development of targeted diagnostics. Current hypotheses for the spontaneous remission include spontaneous hemorrhage of ACTH-producing tumors, inhibition of the glucocorticoid positive feedback loop, and dopaminergic, serotoninergic, and other hypothalamic influences,11,12,13,14,15 though direct evidence is limited. Given its rarity, large prospective cohorts are infeasible; deep molecular profiling of small, well-characterized cohorts thus offers a valuable alternative for discovery.

Therefore, we integrated multi-omics approaches with clinical measurements to analyze four pituitary CCS cases and investigate their clinical and molecular characteristics, aiming to facilitate early diagnosis and establish efficient diagnostic strategies.

Results

Clinical manifestations of four cases of CCS

Four CCS cases (three females and one male) all exhibited typical cushingoid features. Cases 1, 2, and 4 presented with hypertension and/or hypokalemia, whereas case 3 had progressive weight-gain. Case 1 developed symptoms at 20 weeks of gestation, worsening postpartum, but diagnosis was delayed until 1 year after delivery.

All cases experienced marked fluctuations in serum cortisol and plasma ACTH levels (Figure 1), with cycles ranging from 16 h to 8 days. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) detected pituitary microadenomas in cases 1, 2, and 3, while case 4 had negative MRI and DOTATATE positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) results. Adrenal CT showed adrenal hyperplasia in all four cases (bilateral in cases 1, 3, 4, and predominantly unilateral in case 2). These morphological changes are consistent with the chronic ACTH stimulation characteristic of CCS (Figure 1). Bilateral inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS) during peak phases confirmed pituitary ACTH source (central/peripheral gradient >2). Cases 1 and 3 underwent trans-sphenoidal surgery, and serum cortisol and plasma ACTH levels decreased in both cases’ post-surgery. Both ACTH adenomas were diagnosed with proopiomelanocortin (POMC)-positive and USP8 wild type. Case 1 remained recurrence-free at 7 year follow-up. Case 2 is scheduled for surgery; no intervention was given during this period, and the patient awaited surgery under natural disease progression. Based on the patient’s opinion, case 4 was managed medically with metyrapone and ketoconazole and is currently under follow-up.

Comparison of clinical features between CCS and CD

No differences in age, sex, body mass index, disease duration, glucose and lipid metabolism, or plasma potassium were observed between CCS and non-cyclic Cushing disease (CD). Osteoporosis was more common in non-cyclic CD than in CCS; no patient had received any medications known to affect bone metabolism prior to bone density assessment. Interestingly, patients with CCS had significantly higher peak cortisol levels and urinary free cortisol (UFC) than patients with non-cyclic CD, with cortisol often exceeding detection limits and UFC over 158 times the normal upper limit, and experienced spontaneous remission, unlike patients with CD. Despite cortisol variability potentially compromising IPSS accuracy, all four patients with CCS underwent IPSS successfully during confirmed cortisol peaks, with no prior unsuccessful attempts, ensuring reliable localization (Table 1).

ANXA1 was identified differentially expressed between CCS and CD in proteomics

Mass spectrometry analysis of serum from patients with CCS and CD identified 413 upregulated and 53 downregulated differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) during peak cortisol phases, and 385 upregulated and 56 downregulated DEPs during trough cortisol phases (Figure 2A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed significant enrichment in extracellular matrix (ECM)-related pathways (Figure 2B). IGKV2D-28 was the only DEP showing periodic changes (Figure 2C). Tumor proteomics revealed 192 upregulated and 256 downregulated DEPs in CCS versus CD (Figure 2D). In CCS compared to CD, 315 DEPs were significantly upregulated and 42 downregulated (Figure 2E). Eight DEPs were revealed upregulated in both serum and tumor samples, with ANXA1 showing the highest fold change and was selected for further study due to its link to the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.16

ANXA1 was validated upregulated in CCS and exhibited diagnostic power

Immunohistochemistry confirmed strong ANXA1 expression in CCS pituitary tumors but minimal in CD controls, with POMC highly expressed in both (Figure 3A). Notably, ANXA1 and POMC co-localized in the pituitary tumors of patients with CCS (Figure 3B). Serum ANXA1 levels were significantly higher in CCS than in CD during both peak and trough phases (p < 0.0001; Figure 4A). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve evaluation of ANXA1’s discriminative capacity between patients with CCS during cortisol troughs and those with CD yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.886 (95% confidence interval, CI: 0.831–0.942; Figure 4B). The optimal cutoff (3.629) provided sensitivity of 66.2% and specificity of 91.1% in this dataset, corresponding to positive and negative predictive values of 89.6% and 69.9%. When sensitivity was set at 99%, the cutoff was 1.256 ng/mL; when specificity was set at 99%, the cutoff ANXA1 concentration was 4.875 ng/mL (Document S1, Table S1). Dynamic ANXA1 changes varied across CCS patients (Figure 4C). While these preliminary findings suggest that ANXA1 may hold potential as a biomarker to aid in CCS diagnosis, it should be emphasized that these cutoff values are derived from a small cohort and require further validation in larger independent studies before clinical application.

Based on our findings, we proposed a potential diagnostic approach for ACTH-dependent CCS (Figure 5). In patients with ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome and spontaneous remission, measure serum ANXA1 during remission. If ANXA1 is <1.256 ng/mL, it is unlikely CCS. If ANXA1 is between 1.256 and 4.875 ng/mL, the patient requires continuous monitoring of cortisol until the pattern of three cortisol peaks and two cortisol troughs is confirmed. If ANXA1 is >4.875 ng/mL, a diagnosis of CCS could be made directly. For confirmed CCS, bilateral IPSS is then recommended to be performed during the next cortisol peak to localize the source of ACTH secretion. However, this framework is exploratory and requires prospective validation.

Single-nucleus analysis revealed a distinct ANXA1+ tumor cell subpopulation

Unsupervized clustering of 26,663 cells from CCS and CD pituitary tumors identified 7 major populations, including corticotroph tumor cells, SYN3+ tumor cells, ANXA1+ tumor cells, immune cells, mesenchymal cells, ISG15+ epithelial cells, and DIO2+ epithelial cells (Figure 6A) (complete marker gene list for each cluster is provided in Data S1, and detailed quality control metrics are available in Data S2). A unique tumor cell subpopulation that enriched for ANXA1 was identified significantly more abundant in CCS than in CD (Figure 6C). Functional analysis showed enrichment in ECM-related pathways, consistent with the proteomic signature (Figure 6B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed activation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-driving pathways, including TGF-beta, Wnt, and Hippo signaling,17,18,19 and high expression of EMT markers, including CD44, HMGA2, ITGB4, and MYOF (Figures 6B and 6D).20,21,22,23 Co-expression of ANXA1 and POMC was found in ANXA1+ tumor cells, corroborated the immunofluorescence findings (Figure 6E).

Discussion

We explored the clinical characteristics between CCS and CD, finding that patients with CCS had higher peak serum cortisol levels, indicating more dynamic hormone secretion and fluctuating symptoms. This hormonal pattern may explain the reduced osteoporosis rates in CCS, possibly aided by intermittent eucortisolemia allowing bone recovery. Recognizing these cyclic patterns is crucial for avoiding unnecessary treatments and improving management. Our findings highlight a substantial diagnostic delay in CCS. By integrating multi-omics with clinical phenotyping in four pituitary CCS cases, we identified significantly elevated ANXA1 in serum and tumor tissues compared to CD. Traditionally, diagnosing CCS relies on identifying three cortisol peaks and two cortisol troughs, after which clinicians must predict the next subsequent peak to perform bilateral IPSS.24 Bilateral IPSS is reliable only when performed during a cortisol peak (positive predictive value (PPV) 96%, negative predictive value (NPV) 67%); performing it during a trough can cause false positives.5,24 This diagnostic approach requires at least four distinct cortisol peaks, leading to a protracted diagnostic timeline. Our study revealed ANXA1 as a potential diagnostic biomarker and enabled a novel approach incorporating serum ANXA1 measurement during spontaneous remission to screen CCS and shorten time to definitive CCS diagnosis. Therefore, we propose a more efficient approach by detecting one cortisol peak combined with serum ANXA1 measurement during spontaneous remission. Our preliminary findings indicate that during CCS remission, ANXA1 levels >1.256 ng/mL predict CCS with >99% sensitivity, while levels >4.875 ng/mL offer >99% specificity, suggesting ANXA1 as a promising biomarker for early differential diagnosis. These results require further validation to establish reliable cutoff values.

Single-cell analysis revealed that the ANXA1-enriched tumor subpopulation was more abundant in CCS than CD, providing the cellular source of the elevated serum ANXA1. This subpopulation’s functional profile aligned with the CCS proteomic signature and showed enrichment of ECM and EMT pathways, implicating the tumor microenvironment’s role in ANXA1 release. ANXA1 is known to mediate early glucocorticoid negative feedback by inhibiting ACTH secretion via an formyl peptide receptor (FPR)-like receptor on corticotrophs.25 Consistent with the paracrine mechanism, our data showed ANXA1 expression absent in corticotroph cells but in a distinct tumor subpopulation. Notably, canonical FPR1, FPR2, and FPR3 were lowly expressed in corticotrophs, which corroborates prior evidence that an FPR-like receptor, rather than the classical FPR family,25 mediates this effect. The precise receptor identity warrants further investigation. Meanwhile, a SYN3+ tumor subpopulation, marked by Syntaxin 3, a protein known to promote neuronal-like differentiation, and expressing neuron-related genes such as DSCAM, SORCS1, and RGS6,26,27,28,29 was also found more abundant in CCS. This aligns with the observation that serum cortisol and ANXA1 both fluctuate substantially over time but are not synchronized (Figure 4C), indicating that ANXA1 alone does not trigger individual cycles, and the precise regulatory mechanisms, which are likely multifactorial, warrant further investigation.

Among eight proteins upregulated in both serum and tumor tissue, ANXA1 showed the highest fold change and a direct link to the HPA axis,16 justifying its prioritization. Yet the remaining proteins deserve attention: EFEMP1 and FN1 are ECM components, resonating with the ECM pathway enrichment seen in the ANXA1+ subpopulation30,31; EHD1, S100A6, SLPI, CSRP1, and LGALS1 currently lack known pituitary or cortisol-regulating functions,32,33,34,35,36 but their roles warrant future exploration given the likely multifactorial nature of CCS. In contrast, IGKV2D-28 varied with cortisol but was absent from tumor tissue, indicating a systemic reactive response rather than a tumor-derived marker.

In summary, by integrating multi-omics profiling with clinical measurements, we identify ANXA1 as a promising diagnostic biomarker for CCS, with elevated expression in serum and pituitary tumors compared to CD. We proposed a potential diagnostic approach using serum ANXA1 measured during the spontaneous remission. Single-cell transcriptomics reveal an ANXA1+ tumor subpopulation, suggesting its involvement in CCS pathogenesis. Collectively, these findings position ANXA1 as a candidate biomarker to expedite the diagnosis of CCS and provide a paradigm for integrating multi-omics with clinical approaches to study rare endocrine disorders.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these preliminary results. First, the small sample size, an inherent challenge in studying rare diseases, may reduce the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Second, the single-center design could introduce selection bias and limit the diversity of the study population. Therefore, our findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and require further validation. In terms of the proposed diagnostic approach, false negatives could lead to delayed diagnosis and treatment, while false positives might prompt unnecessary invasive procedures such as bilateral IPSS. Additional validation steps, such as establishing reliable ANXA1 cutoff values through prospective multi-center cohorts with comprehensive clinical phenotyping, would mitigate clinical risks.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study enrolled 4 patients with cyclic Cushing’s disease (CCS) (3 females, 1 male; mean age 39 ± 8 years) diagnosed at Ruijin Hospital between 2017 and 2024, and 56 patients with conventional Cushing’s disease (CD) (44 females, 12 males; mean age 42 ± 13 years) diagnosed between 2021 and 2024. Cushing’s syndrome was diagnosed based on typical symptoms, elevated late-night serum cortisol, increased 24-h urinary free cortisol, and unsuppressed low-dose dexamethasone suppression test.41 Cushing’s disease was confirmed by high-dose dexamethasone suppression test, pituitary MRI (2 mm), and bilateral inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS).42 CCS was identified by ≥ 3 peaks (cortisol above upper normal limit) and ≥2 troughs (cortisol within or below normal range)3; for these patients, IPSS was conducted during the peak cortisol phase. Hypertension, impaired fasting glucose, diabetes, and dyslipidemia were defined per established criteria.43,44,45 The study was approved by the Ruijin Hospital Ethics Committee, Shanghai Jiao-Tong University School of Medicine (No. MR-31-25-005731), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. No statistically significant difference in sex distribution was observed between groups; however, sex-stratified analyses were not performed, and potential sex-related influences on the results cannot be excluded, representing a limitation of this study.

Method details

Laboratory assays

Tests were conducted in a College of American Pathologists accredited lab (No.7217913). Blood samples for serum cortisol and plasma ACTH were collected at 0800 h from patients with pituitary CCS and CD, and at 1600 h and 2400 h from patients with CCS continuously. Serum cortisol and Urinary Free Cortisol (UFC) levels were measured as detailed in the Document S1. All blood samples for ANXA1 measurement were paired with cortisol measurements from the same blood draws, collected during the diagnostic evaluation period and prior to any cortisol-lowering medical therapy. Serum ANXA1 concentrations were measured using the Human Annexin A1 ELISA Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), following the manufacturer’s protocol. 50 μL of serum was incubated per well for 2h at room temperature, followed by sequential 1h biotinylated antibody, 30min streptavidin-peroxidase, and 30 min chromogen substrate incubations. Absorbance was read at 450 nm (570 nm reference) using a BIOTEK XS2 reader.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF)

IHC used anti-Annexin A1 (ANXA1) (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), anti-proopiomelanocortin (POMC) (Huabio, Hangzhou, China) and anti-ANXA1 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) antibodies at 1:50, 1:1000, and 1:1600 dilutions. IF used anti-POMC (Huabio, Hangzhou, China) and anti-ANXA1 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) antibodies at 1:1000 and 1:200 dilutions.

Data independent acquisition (DIA) quantification proteomics analysis

Proteins from tissue samples were extracted, quantified, alkylated, and digested as described previously.46 The resulting peptides were then analyzed by nano-liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (nano-LC-MS/MS) in DIA mode. Data were processed using a direct library-free procedure with MSFragger-DIA and DIA-NN (v1.8.1) for protein identification and quantification.

Label-free quantification proteomic analysis

For label-free quantitative proteomics, protein samples were extracted, reduced, alkylated, and digested as described previously.47 Peptides were desalted and analyzed by nano-LC-MS/MS in data-dependent acquisition mode. Proteins were identified using Proteome Discoverer 2.4 with the SwissProt human database. Peptide and protein false discovery rates were controlled at ≤ 1% using a target-decoy strategy (Strict false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01, Relaxed FDR <0.05). Quantification relied on precursor ion intensity, normalizing protein abundances across samples. Proteins with |fold change| ≥ 4.0 and at least 2 unique peptides were considered differentially expressed.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

SnRNA-seq was performed on a CCS pituitary tumor sample using a mortar-based method for nuclei isolation and the 10X Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell.3′ Kit v3.1 for library construction. Sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus targeted 20,000 reads per nucleus. Publicly available scRNA-seq datasets (GSA-human: HRA003483, HRA005096) were also included in the analysis.48,49 Cell Ranger (v5.0.1) processed the raw data, with quality control filtering retained cells expressing 300–4,500 genes, removed doublets, and excluded cells with high mitochondrial gene content. The snRNA-seq and scRNA-seq datasets were integrated, normalized, and batch-corrected using canonical correlation analysis (CCA) in Seurat (v5.3.0). Dimensionality reduction, clustering, and UMAP visualization were performed for downstream analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 22.0 and R 4.4.1. Group comparisons employed t-tests and ANOVA, and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. ANXA1 differences between CCS and CD were evaluated with a mixed effects model. Diagnostic performance was measured via ROC curve analysis, with the best cutoff identified by Youden’s index. Significance was set at p < 0.05.
