Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic Health: Insights from Wearables and Blood Biomarkers
Zeinab Esmaeilpour, A. Ali Heydari, Daniel McDuff, Anthony Z. Faranesh, Conor Heneghan, Shwetak Patel, Mark Malhotra, Cathy Speed, Javier L. Prieto, Ahmed A. Metwally

Abstract
Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome represents a growing public health crisis, yet the subclinical heterogeneity of its component systems remains underexplored. Early detection of physiological deviation is critical for preventing irreversible organ damage and mortality. Here, we characterize the prevalence and interplay of CKM impairment in a US cohort (N=841) by integrating continuous wearable data with clinical biomarkers. We assessed cardiovascular, kidney via clinical biomarkers, namely Chol/HDL, eGFR, as well as metabolic health risk through Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR). We show that while metabolic and cardiovascular disruptions are significantly associated ( r = 0.26 , p < 0.001 r=0.26,p<0.001 ), early-stage kidney impairment manifests independently. Utilizing a normalized deviance score, we identified significant health impairments in 29.0% of the cohort. Cardiovascular deviation was the most prevalent singular phenotype (13.3%), followed by metabolic (9.1%) and renal (6.25%) deviations, with dual metabolic-cardiovascular impairment occurring in only 2.2% of participants. These findings suggest that high system-specific deviance may serve as an indicator for accelerated physiological aging within the respective organ system. Furthermore, feature ablation analysis revealed that step count, Active Zone Minutes, and resting heart rate are the most potent wearable-derived predictors of cardiovascular and metabolic decline. These findings underscore the necessity of a multi-system subtyping approach, demonstrating that wearable-derived phenotypes can facilitate the early, targeted interventions required to manage the complex landscape of CKM syndrome.

Introduction

Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome represents a critical, interconnected health challenge. It is a progressive condition where dysfunction in any of the three systems: cardiovascular, kidney, or metabolic rapidly cascades, leading to multi-organ damage, premature morbidity, and mortality
(
36
)
. This syndrome has high prevalence in the population with recent estimates of approximately 25% of individuals having at least one condition
(
37
;
15
)
. Early identification and intervention, particularly focusing on lifestyle modifications and recognizing early markers, are paramount to disrupting this devastating cycle.

The American Heart Association’s staging of CKM syndrome highlights its progression, starting from Stage 0 (no risk factors) to Stage 4 (established cardiovascular disease with or without kidney failure)
(
37
)
. Crucially, this progression often begins early in life with biological, social, and lifestyle factors leading to dysfunctional adipose tissue. This early metabolic dysfunction, often underestimated by Body mass index (BMI) alone, is a key instigator, preceding and contributing to both cardiovascular and kidney complications
(
29
;
52
)
. The metabolic abnormalities often precede and contribute to the development of both cardiovascular and kidney complications, making them important early warning signs in the CKM continuum. While CKM syndrome is classified by its metabolic roots, it’s crucial to recognize the inherent interdependence of these dysfunctions. Problems in one system inevitably worsen the others. Kidney dysfunction, in particular, acts as a central link between metabolic risk factors and cardiovascular disease. It can initiate, worsen, or even trigger cardiac dysfunction, and the reverse is also true. Specifically, chronic kidney disease impacts heart function through altered blood flow, and by retaining salt and water, which leads to venous congestion and, consequently, heart failure
(
10
)
. The systemic nature of CKM syndrome necessitates comprehensive screening that addresses all components rather than siloed approaches to individual conditions. By identifying early markers of dysfunction clinicians can intervene at stages when preventive measures may have the greatest impact. Thus, identifying early markers for each component of CKM syndrome is crucial for timely intervention before irreversible damage occurs. Each component of CKM syndrome has specific indicators that can signal early dysfunction and increased risk.
The evolution of consumer wearable technology presents a transformative opportunity to realize this goal of proactive CKM risk management. Wearable devices, such as smartwatches and rings, are now capable of continuously and passively collecting high-fidelity physiological signals, including heart rate variability (HRV), resting heart rate (RHR), sleep metrics, and activity levels that serve as digital biomarkers of systemic health
(
42
;
47
;
51
)
. This capacity for long-term, real-world data collection, or “digital phenotyping,” overcomes the limitations of episodic clinical assessments by providing a dense, personalized health timeline
(
59
)
. Several studies have demonstrated the utility of these data streams for disease surveillance and prediction
(
34
;
14
;
43
;
30
)
. For instance, wearable vital signs have been successfully utilized for early prediction of conditions like respiratory illness and changes in inflammation
(
14
)
. In a highly relevant exploration of this utility, Dunn et al. showed that vital sign data derived from smartwatches could predict components of routine clinical laboratory panels, specifically finding utility in estimating hematocrit (HCT) and hemoglobin (HGB)
(
13
)
. Given the inherent heterogeneity of CKM pathology, which can manifest in diverse organ-specific patterns, the critical next step is to leverage these high-resolution wearable signals to precisely characterize distinct subtypes of early CKM disruption. This allows for the generation of valuable, actionable lifestyle signals.
The present work addresses this gap by undertaking an early-stage analysis of CKM pathology using a novel approach to quantify physiological deviance. We leveraged comprehensive, concurrent data from wearables and blood biomarkers in a large US cohort (N=824) to assess early markers of cardiovascular health (Chol/HDL), metabolic health (HOMA-IR), and renal function (eGFR). Our methodology introduced a deviance score to objectively establish an impairment threshold, enabling a detailed subtyping of CKM presentation in a population not yet defined by advanced clinical disease. We present a method for predicting markers of cardiovascular health (Chol/HDL), metabolic health (HOMA-IR), and renal function (eGFR) using (a) signals derived from a consumer smartwatch (e.g., activity level, sleep duration and sleep stages, resting heart rate and heart rate variability), (b) demographics (i.e., Age, Gender and BMI), and (c) commonly measured blood biomarkers (e.g, lipid panel, liver panel, metabolic panel). To quantify the predictive contribution of these diverse factors, a rigorous regression analysis was executed. This modeling approach provides actionable insights by identifying and ranking the most important demographic and lifestyle features influencing these health markers.

2.1
Study Design and cohort characteristics

We implemented a prospective observational investigation, recruiting adult participants from the United States to take part in a consented research study
(
34
)
. This study received approval from Advarra, serving as the institutional review board (IRB) of record (protocol number Pro00074093), and was executed remotely utilizing the Google Health Studies (GHS) application. GHS functions as a consumer-centric, secure platform for conducting digital studies, facilitating participant enrollment, eligibility verification, and the provision of informed consent (Methods). In this particular instance, the platform was configured to enable the acquisition of wearable data from Fitbit and Google Pixel Watch devices (referred to collectively as “wearables” herein), the completion of questionnaires, and the arrangement of blood tests with Quest Diagnostics. Participants were requested to: (1) establish a link between their Fitbit/Google account and the GHS application to facilitate data sharing, (2) complete questionnaires pertaining to demographic details, health history, and general health information, (3) schedule and undergo a blood draw at a Quest Patient Service Center within 65 days of their enrollment.

We consented 4,416 participants, of which 841 (19% completion rate) had complete data (at least 14 days of wearable data (activity levels, AZM, sleep duration and sleep stages, RHR, HRV, respiration rate), blood biomarkers, demographics, and medical conditions), and passed preprocessing, and were included in our analysis
(Methods)
. Inclusion criteria included U.S. residents between the ages of 21 and 80 who wear a Fitbit wearable device or a Pixel Watch with heart rate sensing capabilities and are willing to go to a Quest Diagnostics location for blood draws. Participants were asked to do blood draws early in the morning after fasting for at least 8 hours to minimize the effect of the solar diurnal cycle.

The recruited cohort participants exhibited a wide range of daily physical activity, as measured by average daily step counts. Overall, 22.45% (185 individuals) of participants met the criteria for active status, defined as an average daily step count exceeding 10,000 daily steps. The remaining participants were distributed across three lower activity categories: 27.76 % (228 individuals) of participants were categorized as somewhat active (i.e., average daily step count between 7,500 and 10,000), 28.76% (237 individuals) were categorized as low active (i.e., average daily step count between 5,000 to 7,500), and 21.12% (174 individuals) were classified as sedentary (i.e., average daily step count of below 5,000). These distributions highlight a notable heterogeneity in habitual activity patterns across the study population.

For cardiovascular health, the ratio of total cholesterol to HDL cholesterol (Chol/HDL) was chosen since it is widely recognized as a valuable metric for assessing cardiovascular health and predicting the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD). Estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR) is a cornerstone in the assessment of kidney health, widely used by clinicians to detect kidney disease, track its progression, and guide treatment decisions. This calculated measure provides an estimate of how well the kidneys are filtering waste products from the blood 19. In this study we used eGFR as a metric to evaluate kidney health status. In the study population, the median eGFR was 96.5 mL/min/1.73m2. A clinical threshold of 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 was adopted to delineate elevated risk, with higher values indicating a more favorable kidney health profile 20,21. In this study we used HOMA-IR as a metric to evaluate metabolic health status. In the study population, the median HOMA-IR was 1.81. A clinical threshold of 2.9 was adopted to delineate elevated risk, with lower values indicating a more favorable metabolic health

2.2
Individual Heterogeneity in CKM Health as a Reflectance of System-Specific Accelerated Aging

To quantify the departure from a healthy state, we used the concept of deviance. Here, for each biomarker, deviance was determined by subtracting the normal threshold from the observed value and then normalizing this difference by the population standard deviation (STD). In this framework, a positive deviance value corresponds to a healthy condition in the respective subsystem, whereas a negative value indicates a decline in health (
Figure 2
). The median values for each CKM biomarker (i.e., Chol/HDL , eGFR, and HOMA-IR) in our dataset closely align with established normal ranges, indicating that our data accurately represents the general population (
Figure 2
).

Figure 3A
visually represents the calculated deviance from normal for each CKM subsystem across participants. Each cell in the heatmap is color-coded, with the intensity and hue directly corresponding to the deviance value; where positive deviance values indicate healthier status and negative deviance values indicate less healthy status. This visualization allows for a rapid assessment of an individual’s overall health profile across the targeted CKM subsystems and facilitates the identification of patterns, such as individuals exhibiting consistently healthy or less healthy states across multiple subsystems, or those with specific subsystem impairments.

A deviance value below
-1
was subsequently used as the threshold for identifying departure from the healthy status within a specific subsystem (i.e., cardiovascular, kidney or metabolic). This threshold is statistically robust because it utilizes standardized units (
Z-scores
) to ensure comparability across different biological subsystems, while the
-1
cut-off provides the necessary sensitivity to detect subclinical shifts before they reach extreme outlier status. Applying this threshold to the cohort allowed for the identification of the prevalence of singular and concurrent subsystem issues. The highest singular prevalence was observed in the cardiovascular subsystem, with 13.35% of the population exhibiting a Chol/HDL deviance below
-1
. This high prevalence aligns with global epidemiological data underscoring the ubiquity of dyslipidemia as a leading cardiovascular risk factor, often preceding overt disease for many years 24. Metabolic health impairment (HOMA-IR deviance below
-1
) was observed in 9.1% of the population, followed by kidney health impairment (eGFR deviance below
-1
) at 6.25%. The combined assessment revealed that 29% of the participants exhibited at least one of the CKM conditions, emphasizing the significant burden of interconnected subclinical risk within the population.
Dual-system issues were most common between the metabolic and cardiovascular subsystems, affecting 2.18% of the population. This link is biologically plausible given that insulin resistance and dyslipidemia often coexist and drive the atherosclerotic process 24,25. The prevalence of dual issues between the cardiovascular and kidney subsystems (cardiorenal axis) was 0.9%, while the metabolic and kidney pairing (reno-metabolic axis) was the least frequent, affecting 0.6% of the population. Although less frequent, the existence of these cardio-kidney and kidney-metabolic axes is consistent with the established physiological cross-talk where chronic dysfunction in one system imposes a mechanical or humoral burden on the others, often leading to a vicious cycle of progressive organ damage.

Importantly, no individuals were identified with significant deviance (below
-1
) across all three health conditions simultaneously. Early identification of a single-system deviance thus presents a critical window for intervention to prevent the cascading failure into multi-system disease.

2.4
Characterization of Modifiable CKM Health Determinants Independent of Age and Gender

To mitigate the strong confounding effects of non modifiable demographic attributes, we used partial Pearson correlation analysis to adjust for confounding effects. Specifically, the correlation analysis described in this section systematically controlled age and gender. The correlation with age and gender are presented in
supplementary figure S4
. This methodology was crucial for stripping away the variance explained by demographics, thereby revealing the underlying and modifiable influences of CKM metrics, wearables data, and blood biomarkers, which represent critical targets for future health interventions. Following the partial correlation calculations, we applied a multiple comparisons correction to the resulting p-values to mitigate the risk of Type I errors (false positives). The Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) procedure was used for this correction, with a false discovery rate (FDR) controlled at a significance level of
\alpha=0.05
. Only features with a partial correlation coefficient with a corrected p-value below the significance level were considered statistically significant and included in the final analysis.

Similarly, the renal and endocrine panels were excluded from the feature sets for predicting eGFR and HOMA-IR, respectively.

For the prediction of the Cholesterol/High-Density Lipoprotein (Chol/HDL) ratio, a sequential or cumulative addition of feature categories generally resulted in a progressive enhancement of model fit, demonstrating the utility of a multi-domain data approach. Specifically, the inclusion of demographic information, wearable data, the Complete Blood Count (CBC) panel, and the Liver panel collectively formed the optimal predictive model. This combination achieved a maximal
R2
of 0.17.

Prediction of estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR) exhibited a distinct pattern of feature importance. The regression model utilizing the demographic feature set alone showed a significantly robust predictive capacity, achieving an R2 of 0.30. This finding underscores the established importance of age, sex, and race, key components of standard eGFR calculation formulas (e.g., CKD-EPI equation) as the dominant determinants of renal function prediction.

The optimal model for eGFR prediction was subsequently achieved by an expansive combination, integrating demographic information, wearable data, the CBC panel, Liver panel, Endocrine panel, Metabolic panel, and Lipid panel. This comprehensive model yielded a marginal but statistically significant increase in performance, reaching an R2 of 0.31. The limited incremental gain beyond the demographic features suggests that while multi-omic or clinical data (including wearables) may contribute minor predictive signals related to subclinical renal stress or inflammation, the fundamental predictive influence remains heavily weighted toward the demographic components.

The regression model developed for predicting the Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) demonstrated the strongest overall predictive performance across all target biomarkers. A consistent, gradual increase in model performance was observed as feature sets were incrementally added, indicating that the multi-domain data provided increasingly valuable and non-redundant information for estimating insulin sensitivity. The optimal predictive model for HOMA-IR was constructed by combining demographic information, wearable data, the CBC panel, the Basic Metabolic panel, and the Lipid panel features. This maximal feature set yielded a high R2 of 0.46. This relatively high predictive value highlights the polygenic and multi-systemic nature of insulin resistance, where factors related to body composition (demographics/wearables), inflammation (CBC), basic metabolic function, and dyslipidemia (Lipid panel) collectively account for nearly half of the variance in HOMA-IR in the study cohort.

3
Discussion

This study demonstrated that CKM syndrome is a heterogeneous condition with physiological systems exhibiting varying degrees and patterns of disruptions using a large cohort of 841 participants. We developed regression models using wearables features capturing lifestyle factors, demographics information and blood lab biomarkers. The model showed significant correlations between specific wearable-derived data and demographic factors with cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic health biomarkers.

Our findings demonstrate that features derived from consumer-grade wearable devices, specifically average daily RHR, daily step count, active zone minutes, and various sleep parameters are among the most significant correlates for cardiovascular health outcomes (Chol/HDL ratio). The integration of these continuous, objective measures aligns with a growing body of literature advocating for the use of digital phenotyping in enhancing traditional cardiovascular risk stratification models
(
23
)
. Wearable devices capture physiological and behavioral data with a granularity impossible through sporadic clinical visits, offering a comprehensive view of an individual’s health trajectory and homeostatic resilience.
The heterogeneity observed in CKM health, quantified by system-specific deviance scores, suggests a model of non-uniform or accelerated physiological aging. This challenges the traditional view of aging as a synchronous decline, highlighting instead that individual organ systems can undergo independent deterioration
(
prattichizzo2024organ
)
. For example, the cardiovascular system exhibited the highest prevalence of deviance at 13.3%. Identifying these system-specific impairments early provides a critical window for targeted, preventative interventions to mitigate organ-specific decline. Such lifestyle interventions could be personalized using objective data captured by wearable devices.

The observed positive correlation between RHR and the Chol/HDL ratio is a critical finding that is well-supported in the literature. A high RHR is a recognized, independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and is frequently associated with an adverse metabolic profile, including dyslipidemia
(
23
;
56
)
. Specifically, studies have reported that elevated RHR is associated with an increased prevalence of metabolic syndrome and its components, such as high blood glucose and elevated triglycerides (TG). Accelerometer-based metrics, such as daily step count and measures of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (i.e., active zone minutes), are strongly and inversely associated with adverse cardiometabolic markers. Higher daily step counts are consistently linked to a more favorable lipid profile, including lower levels of total cholesterol and higher levels of HDL-C
(
23
;
56
;
5
)
. The American Heart Association (AHA) and other major health organizations emphasize that increased physical activity significantly improves cardiovascular health outcomes by improving metabolic fitness and reducing insulin resistance, which in turn positively regulates lipid metabolism
(
3
)
. Our finding of a negative correlation between sleep features and the Chol/HDL ratio supports the critical role of sleep health in cardiometabolic regulation. Poor sleep quality and inadequate or irregular sleep duration are recognized as independent risk factors for CVD. Large-scale studies have demonstrated that the combination of insufficient sleep regularity (or duration) and low daily physical activity is associated with the least favorable composite cardiometabolic health scores, including elevated total cholesterol and lower HDL levels
5
;
3
. These data highlight that the protective association of adequate sleep with a favorable lipid profile is likely mediated through improved metabolic pathways and stable circadian rhythm, further underscoring the joint association of sleep and physical activity
5
;
3

.

Key demographic factors including age, gender, and BMI were found to be significantly correlated with markers of cardiovascular health. The observed positive correlation between BMI and the Cholesterol/HDL ratio is strongly supported by decades of epidemiological research. BMI serves as a proxy for adiposity, particularly visceral fat accumulation, which is mechanistically linked to unfavorable changes in lipid metabolism. Our finding that males exhibit a higher mean Chol/HDL ratio compared to females is consistent with known sex-based disparities in cardiovascular risk prior to menopause. In premenopausal women, circulating estrogens exert a protective effect on the lipid profile. The negative correlation observed between age and the Chol/HDL ratio warrants careful contextualization, as general epidemiological studies often show increasing total cardiovascular risk with age. Our analysis identified key blood laboratory biomarkers red blood cell count, Hematocrit (Hct), Hemoglobin (Hb), and Insulin as highly correlated with cardiovascular health. The relationships for the hematological markers are particularly complex, often demonstrating a U or J-shaped association with outcomes, where both low and high concentrations carry increased risk, while mid-range levels are protective.

In our study cohort, 13.3% of participants were below
-1
deviance from ideal cardiovascular health status. The subtype of individuals with both cardiovascular and metabolic health below

Crucially, the analysis revealed that no participant was found to be in the subtype where all three measured health systems were simultaneously below one standard deviation from ideal status. This finding suggests that, while compound risk exists for a small group, the profound simultaneous deficit across all evaluated health systems is extremely rare or non-existent in this cohort. This may be due to survival bias, cohort characteristics.

The assessment of kidney health status, measured by estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR), revealed initial significant associations with a comprehensive set of wearable-derived features. The metrics most strongly correlated with eGFR included average daily deep sleep minutes, Heart Rate Variability (HRV), respiration rate, and REM and light sleep minutes. The observation that various sleep phases and autonomic metrics initially correlate with eGFR is relevant, as sleep disturbance (including sleep apnea and poor sleep quality) is well-documented in the literature as a risk factor for both the development and progression of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)
(
32
;
55
)
. A higher HRV generally reflects greater parasympathetic tone and better autonomic balance, which is broadly associated with cardiovascular and metabolic health, and potentially indirectly protective of kidney function
(
6
;
50
)
. Higher duration of Deep Sleep is a hallmark of restorative sleep, where both heart rate and respiration rate are at their most stable and slowest
(

. These parameters often decline with age and disease, suggesting that better sleep architecture is linked to preserved eGFR. Crucially, multivariate analysis incorporating age and gender removed the statistical significance of all tested wearable features. This finding indicates that the observed correlations between sleep/autonomic metrics and eGFR are primarily mediated by or confounded by these foundational demographic variables. Following this rigorous adjustment, only the fundamental blood biomarkers of calcium and hematocrit remained as independent, significant predictors of eGFR. This outcome underscores the primacy of direct physiological and metabolic markers in kidney risk assessment.
Kidney impairment, especially in advanced stages, severely disrupts the homeostasis of mineral and bone metabolism. As eGFR declines, the kidneys lose the ability to regulate calcium and phosphate, often leading to secondary hyperparathyroidism and abnormal serum calcium levels. The strong correlation between Ca2+ and eGFR reflects this intricate, clinically established relationship, where calcium balance is a critical measure of nephrology health. The kidney is the primary source of erythropoietin (EPO), the hormone essential for red blood cell production. Declining eGFR leads to EPO deficiency, resulting in renal anemia (low Hct/Hb levels). This robust, inverse relationship confirms Hct as a core physiological marker directly linked to the consequences of diminishing renal function, independent of demographic factors

Given the asymptomatic and silent nature of CKD in its early stages, a proactive approach to prevention and surveillance is essential. Our findings confirm the established physiological reality that eGFR exhibits an age-dependent decline in the general population, even in the absence of overt kidney disease

Our modeling approach identified multiple continuous measures derived from wearable technology as highly correlated predictors of metabolic health, quantified by the Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR). The most influential features included average daily step counts, average daily sleep duration, average daily entropy, and average daily RHR. These findings highlight the significant and objective contribution of lifestyle and autonomic function to insulin sensitivity, a cornerstone of metabolic health.

The observed negative correlation between both average daily step counts and average daily sleep duration with HOMA-IR confirms a fundamental principle in endocrinology: greater levels of physical activity and adequate sleep are robustly associated with improved metabolic status. The finding that RHR is a highly correlated feature with HOMA-IR, is consistent with the hypothesis that higher RHR is a marker of sympathetic nervous system overactivity and/or reduced cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF)

The observed correlations between multiple blood biomarkers and HOMA-IR demonstrate that metabolic dysfunction is a complex, multi-system pathology involving glucose regulation, lipid transport, inflammation, and liver function. Insulin resistance is tightly linked to atherogenic dyslipidemia, characterized by hypertriglyceridemia (high Triglycerides) and reduced HDL cholesterol (low HDL). The resulting elevated Triglycerides and Chol/HDL ratio (a surrogate for atherogenic risk) are therefore highly correlated with HOMA-IR and serve as critical clinical components of the Metabolic Syndrome definition

The significant correlation of C-Reactive Protein (CRP) and White Blood Cell (WBC) counts with HOMA-IR confirms that low-grade systemic inflammation is an integral component of insulin resistance. IR and obesity stimulate this inflammatory milieu, creating a vicious cycle where inflammation further impairs insulin signaling. Many studies show that CRP concentrations are elevated predominantly in individuals who are insulin resistant, supporting its role as a biomarker that links excess adiposity to metabolic dysfunction

The correlations observed with Albumin/Globulin (A/G) ratio and Total Bilirubin (TB) provide insight into nutritional, inflammatory, and hepatic health. The correlation between TB and HOMA-IR is typically inverse (higher TB is protective). Bilirubin is a potent, endogenous antioxidant and cytoprotective. Low serum bilirubin levels are associated with increased oxidative stress, which is a key mechanism in the development and progression of IR

The relationship of serum proteins with IR is complex. Albumin is a general marker of liver synthetic function and nutritional status, and it can be positively associated with IR in some cohorts

Our analysis identified BMI as the sole demographic feature maintaining a statistically significant correlation with metabolic health status, as assessed by the Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR). This singularity underscores the dominant role of adiposity, rather than non-modifiable factors like age or gender alone, in driving insulin sensitivity within the cohort. Consistent with our results, numerous epidemiological and clinical studies have unequivocally demonstrated a strong, positive, and independent correlation between BMI and HOMA-IR across diverse populations. Research frequently identifies a dose-response relationship, where increasing BMI categories correlate with progressively higher mean HOMA-IR values, indicating a continuous deterioration of insulin sensitivity as body weight increases

To rigorously quantify the departure from a healthy physiological state across the three major interacting subsystems, cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic, we introduced the concept of a deviance. This score was calculated by subtracting the observed value of a health marker from its established healthy mean and normalizing the result by the marker’s standard deviation (SD). Specifically, a negative deviance value indicates a decline in the health status of that respective subsystem. A deviance score below -1 was subsequently established as the threshold for identifying departure from the healthy status within a specific subsystem.
Applying this threshold to the cohort allowed for the identification of the prevalence of singular and concurrent subsystem issues. The highest singular prevalence was observed in the cardiovascular subsystem, with 13.35% of the population exhibiting a Chol/HDL deviance below
-1
. Metabolic health impairment (HOMA-IR deviance below -1) was observed in 9.1% of the population, followed by renal health impairment (eGFR deviance below -1) at 6.25%. 29% of the participants exhibited at least one of the CKM conditions.
Dual-system issues were most common between the metabolic and cardiovascular subsystems, affecting 2.18% of the population. This link is biologically plausible given that insulin resistance and dyslipidemia often coexist and drive the atherosclerotic process
(
57
;
41
;
11
)
. The prevalence of dual issues between the cardiovascular and kidney subsystems was 0.9%, while the metabolic and kidney pairing was the least frequent, affecting 0.6% of the population. Importantly, no individuals were identified with significant deviance (below
-1
) across all three health conditions simultaneously. These findings underscore the need for integrated screening.
Recent estimates suggest that over 25% of US adults have at least one CKM condition, and approximately 8% to 10% have two or more co-occurring conditions, a prevalence that increases sharply with age
(
40
;

. The co-occurrence dyads observed in the general population shows the combination of metabolic dysfunction (Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus) and kidney impairment (Chronic Kidney Disease) often represents the most common dyad in national surveys, followed closely by the Cardiorenal (CVD and CKD) and Cardiometabolic (CVD and Type 2 Diabetes) pairings. In our study we observe a higher rate of cardio metabolic dyad followed by Cardio-kidney pairing and kidney-metabolic dyad. This could be due to the fact that our cohort may have overrepresented those with cardiometabolic disease.
This work underscores the complex and multi-system nature of Cardio-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome. Rather than representing distinct, isolated diseases, our findings demonstrate that poor cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic health coexist within a tightly interwoven pathophysiological continuum. The strength of this study lies in its multi-domain data approach, where non-invasive, continuous wearable metrics are shown to capture signals indicative of early metabolic distress, complementing the prognostic power of traditional blood panels. Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies using integrated, multi-domain datasets (wearable, biochemical, and genetic) to fully elucidate the causal pathways and determine if interventions guided by early digital biomarkers can successfully mitigate the progression of clustered CKM risk.
We enrolled a diverse group of participants spanning various ages and genders. We are aware that our final cohort may have overrepresented those with cardiometabolic disease due to the study name (i.e. Wearables for Metabolic Health Study) or proactive in health management due to the requirement for blood lab tests and use of wearable devices. However, many basic wearable fitness trackers with the necessary measurement capabilities are becoming increasingly affordable and widely available, and we expect this trend to continue. In our models, we incorporated features commonly found on lower-cost devices, such as resting heart rate, daily steps, sleep stages, and heart rate variability. Additionally, recognizing the limitations of affordable wearables in providing high-resolution data, we utilized aggregate measures of these metrics rather than time-series data, ensuring the applicability of our models to a wider range of devices while maintaining their relevance and practical utility. We acknowledge that consumer smartwatch brands, sensors, and firmware may provide different accuracy, precision, and temporal resolution for measuring digital biomarkers (heart rate, steps, sleep) compared to research-grade or clinical-grade devices. In our study, we utilized data from seven different smartwatches and four trackers, all from the same manufacturer, Google/Fitbit, and employing similar algorithms. We implemented extensive quality control measures to ensure the data used for the model were of high fidelity and accuracy, and that the model received sufficient wearable data input before generating a prediction. While we expect the results to generalize to other brands, further investigation and more specific quality control steps to mitigate known accuracy issues are warranted.

Method

4.1
Participant recruitment via Google Health studies (GHS)

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board at Advarra (Columbia, MD, Protocol Pro00074093). All participants provided informed consent. In addition to the consent, participants were also asked to sign the Quest HIPAA authorization as part of the consent flow within the GHS app. Participants were recruited using the “Google Health Studies” (GHS) Application and Fitbit App. GHS is a platform to run digital studies that allows participants to enroll into studies, check eligibility and provide informed consent. GHS enables the collection of wearable features using Fitbit devices or Pixel watches and allows participants to complete questionnaires and order blood tests with Quest Diagnostics. This prospective, observational study resulted in consent of 4418 participants in the United States, where a subset of those (N=841) had complete data and thus were included in our analysis. The study was conducted remotely with one visit to a Quest Patient Service Center for a blood draw. Participants were asked to continuously wear their wrist-worn wearable devices, schedule and complete a blood draw, and answer questionnaires. This study used an electronic informed consent process managed by the GHS App using electronic signatures.

Inclusion criteria included U.S. residents between the ages of 21 - 80, Fitbit users in Android that wear a Fitbit wearable device or a Pixel Watch with heart rate sensing capabilities, users who have at least 3 months of existing data where they have used the device for at least 75% of the days to track their activity and sleep, participants willing to update their Fitbit Android App, participants willing to install or update their Google Health Studies (GHS) app on their Android phone, participants willing to link (or create) their Quest Diagnostics account to the GHS app, participants able to speak and read English and provide informed consent and HIPAA authorization to participate in study, participants who have access to and be willing to go to a Quest Diagnostics location for blood draws. Wearable devices were limited to any model of a Fitbit device or Pixel watch with heart rate (HR) sensing capabilities (e.g., Pixel Watch, Charge HR, Charge models 2 - 5, Sense, Sense 2, Versa, Versa 2, 3 & 4, Versa Lite, Inspire HR, Inspire 2, and Luxe).

Exclusion criteria included participants living in Alaska, Arizona, Hawaii and the US territories since Quest Diagnostics cannot provide blood tests in those states, participants with uncontrolled disease (for example, a recent change in treatment in the past 6 weeks, awaiting review to trigger a change of treatment, a treating physician has indicated the condition is not yet controlled, or where symptoms of the condition are not responding to treatment), or participants with conditions that might make collection of blood samples through venipuncture impractical.

4.2
Study Design

As part of this study, participants were asked to link their Fitbit account with the GHS app. They were also asked to grant GHS permission to collect Fitbit data throughout the study, including data for up to 3 months before study enrollment. Once participants were enrolled in the study they were asked to perform the following: (1) wear their Fitbit device or Pixel watch during the day and while they sleep (at least 3 out of every 4 days) for the duration of the study; (2) complete four questionnaires which will request demographic information, health history and health information such as sleep and exercise habits, participant perception of health, and blood test interpretation (see below for details); (3) schedule an appointment to complete the lab test orders that were placed for them and go to a Quest Patient Service Center for a blood draw within 65 days of enrollment; (4) complete a blood draw at the Quest Patient Service Center; (5) review blood test results in the GHS App when available.

4.3
Metadata collection

Demographics (e.g., age, gender, ethnicity, weight, height), optional measures, such as medical conditions (e.g. diabetes, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, hypertension, etc.), blood pressure, waist circumference, medications, self-reported health management and habits were collected through a baseline questionnaire that participants completed immediately after enrollment through the GHS app.

5
Blood biomarkers via Quest diagnostics

Eligible participants were asked to schedule and complete a visit at a Quest Diagnostics Patient Service Center of their choice in their local area. This visit included a standard blood draw to measure the following: Complete Blood Count, Comprehensive Metabolic Panel, insulin, Lipid panel, HbA1c, hs-CRP, GGT and Total Testosterone. Participants were asked to have their blood drawn while fasting for at least 8 hours, early morning, 7am-10am local time, to minimize the effect of the solar diurnal cycle. This study also had clinical oversight by a physician network partner. Lab results from the blood draw were returned to participants and made available for participant review in the GHS app for the duration of the study; however, these were pulled directly from Quest Diagnostics each time it is requested by the participant and not stored in the GHS app. Participants provided consent and HIPAA authorization to grant GHS permission to collect the corresponding results from Quest Diagnostics. Data transferred from Quest Diagnostics were retrieved securely using encrypted protocols. Once the study was completed, lab results remained in the participant’s Quest Account in accordance with Quest’s standard practices.

Wearables data via Fitbit devices and Pixel Watches

Participants were asked to wear their own Fitbit device or Pixel Watch that may include sensors such as PPG optical heart rate tracker, multipurpose electrical sensors, gyroscope, altimeter, accelerometer, on-wrist skin temperature, blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), and ambient light sensor. Participants consented to share the following data from these devices: (1) heart rate metrics: heart rate (HR), resting heart rate captured daily, interbeat interval (IBI, also known as RR interval) calculated from the PPG sensor, and HRV metrics (such as RMSSD, SDNN,Entropy, SDRR, pNN50, etc.) (2) physical activity metrics: Measures of physical activity, including steps, floors, and active zone minutes (AZMs), (3) sleep metrics: bedtime, wake time, sleep duration, sleep stages, sleep quality, sleep coefficients, and sleep logs, (4) respiration and skin temperature: Respiration rate values during the day/night and skin temperature (if the sensor is available on the wearable), (5) SpO2: Blood oxygen saturation values during the day/night (if available on the wearable), (6) weight: Measure of weight that may be logged in the Fitbit account, (7) exercise and activity data: Daily total exercise sessions completed and logs of activities that have been classified. 6.2
Categorization of Clinical and Physiological Features

)
. To assess the relationship between various wearable and demographic features and the outcome variable for each CKM subsystem while controlling for confounding effects of age and gender, we utilized partial correlation analysis. This method determines the correlation between two variables after the influence of age and gender variables has been removed. The analysis was performed using Python, leveraging the statsmodels and scipy libraries.
The procedure for calculating the partial correlation coefficient between a feature and the outcome variable, adjusting for age and gender, was as follows:

•

A separate linear regression model was constructed for both the target outcome variable and each individual feature. In each model, the outcome variable was regressed on the two controlling variables, age and gender.

•

The residuals were extracted from each of these regression models. The residuals represent the part of each variable (the target and the feature) that cannot be explained by age and gender.

•

A standard Pearson correlation coefficient was then computed between the residuals of the target outcome and the residuals of each feature. This correlation between the residuals is the partial correlation coefficient, representing the unique association between the feature and the target after removing the shared linear effect of age and gender.

Following the partial correlation calculations, we applied a multiple comparisons correction to the resulting p-values to mitigate the risk of Type I errors (false positives). The Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) procedure was used for this correction, with a false discovery rate (FDR) controlled at a significance level of
\alpha=0.05
. Only features with a partial correlation coefficient above a predefined threshold and a corrected p-value below the significance level were considered statistically significant and included in the final analysis.

7
Modeling and Computational Pipeline

As shown in Figure 1A, our method consists of three stages: (i) Data preprocessing, (ii) modeling and training, (iii) Prediction and classification. We describe each of these components below.

7.1
Data preprocessing

Age and BMI (Demographics): From the user self-reported data on recruiting surveys, we extract a user’s age and compute BMI from height and weight. As a quality control process, Users were excluded if their BMI was greater than 65 or less than 12, resulting in the exclusion of 326 users from the analysis.

Digital Markers Derived from Wearables (Wearables Features): Using the estimated digital markers from Fitbit algorithms, we aggregated users’ digital markers using mean, standard deviation for the past 14 days prior to blood test collection.

Biomarkers from Blood Biochemistry (Blood Tests): As a first filter, we removed any participant who was not fasting as reported in a survey at the time of blood test collection. Additionally, for each experiment, we exclude participants with missing values from the input feature set. Lastly, to remove outliers, we use the true HOMA-IR value (
HOMA-IR=[FastingInsulin(µU/ml)×FastingGlucose(mg/dL)]/405
) and exclude any participant whose HOMA-IR value is greater than or equal to 20. This exclusion criteria removed 1343 users from the study.

Data Normalization: The data used for modeling is a concatenation of demographics, aggregated digital markers and blood biomarkers. In order to create a consistent modeling data that is agnostic to the learning model, we standardized input features to have zero mean and unit variance. For each training fold, our “normalizer” object was fit to the data in the training subset (not including samples in the testing subset). The fitted object was then used to transform the samples in both the training and testing subsets. The standardized data was used for all modeling tasks and evaluations.

7.2
Modeling

In order to predict biomarkers of CKM, our goal was to predict the value of Chol/HDL, eGFR and HOMA-IR and then use the existing thresholds for normal values in each of the aforementioned subsystems to classify individuals. This approach allows for a better interpretation of our results.

7.3
Direct Regression

Our regression analysis for each CKM subsystem utilized Gradient Boosting Machines, specifically XGBoost
31
;
9
. This approach was ideal given its proven ability to manage complex datasets and capture non-linear relationships.

Our regression analysis employed a nested 5-fold cross-validation approach to robustly tune hyperparameters and evaluate the performance of an XGBoost Regressor model. This methodology ensures a more reliable assessment of the model’s generalization capabilities by mitigating the risk of data leakage during hyperparameter optimization.
The nested cross-validation was implemented with two loops: an outer loop for model evaluation and an inner loop for hyperparameter tuning.
Outer Loop (Model Evaluation): A 5-fold shuffled cross-validation (k=5) was implemented in the outer loop. To enhance the robustness of our results, five distinct random seeds (10, 55, 67, 51, 29) were utilized, ensuring varied data partitions across iterations. For each fold in the outer loop, the following steps were taken:

•

The dataset was partitioned into training and testing sets.

•

Feature standardization was performed. The scaler was exclusively fitted on the training data, and then applied to transform both the training and testing sets, thereby preventing any information leakage from the test set.

Inner Loop (
HyperparameterTuning
): Within the training set of each outer loop fold, a second 5-fold shuffled cross-validation (k=5) was executed. A consistent random seed (10) was used for the inner cross-validation to maintain reproducibility. A Grid Search was conducted to systematically explore a predefined hyperparameter space. The goal was to identify the optimal combination of parameters that minimized the negative mean squared error. The hyperparameter grid included:

•

n\_estimators
: [50, 100, 200]

•

learning\_rate
: [0.01, 0.1, 0.2]

•

reg\_alpha
(
L1
regularization term): [0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4]

•

reg\_lambda
(
L2
regularization term): [0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4]

•

booster
: [
gblinear
,
gbtree
]

Upon completion of the nested cross-validation for each outer fold, the identified final_model (the best_model from the inner loop, evaluated on the outer test set) was utilized to generate predictions on the corresponding held-out test set. Model performance was quantitatively assessed using Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and R-squared (
R2
).

7.4
Machine Learning Model and Evaluation

To predict the target variable, a supervised machine learning approach was employed using an XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting) regressor. Given the importance of robust model evaluation and hyperparameter selection, a nested K-fold cross-validation strategy was implemented. This method provides a more reliable estimate of a model’s performance on unseen data by separating the hyperparameter tuning process from the final model evaluation.

7.5
Nested Cross-Validation Procedure

The analysis was performed using a 5-fold nested cross-validation with five different outer loop seeds to ensure a comprehensive evaluation. This procedure consisted of two main loops:

•

Outer Loop: The dataset was split into five distinct training and testing sets. This loop’s primary purpose was to evaluate the model’s final performance. For each iteration, the model was trained on 80% of the data and evaluated on the remaining 20% test set. This process was repeated five times, with each fold serving as the test set once.

•

Inner Loop: For each training set from the outer loop, a separate 5-fold cross-validation was conducted to perform hyperparameter tuning. A grid search was used to systematically explore a predefined set of hyperparameters to identify the optimal combination. The hyperparameters and their respective ranges included:

–

n\_estimators
: [50, 100, 200]

–

learning\_rate
: [0.01, 0.1, 0.2]

–

reg\_alpha
(
L1
regularization): [0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4]

–

reg\_lambda
(
L2
regularization): [0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4]

–

booster
: [
gblinear
,
gbtree
]

The inner loop selected the best model based on the lowest Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) from its cross-validation results.

7.6
Model Training and Evaluation

Prior to training, all features were standardized using
StandardScaler
to ensure they had a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. This step is crucial for models that are sensitive to feature scales, such as tree-based models with linear boosters. The scaler was fitted exclusively on the training data to prevent data leakage from the test set.
The best-performing model from the inner loop was then used to make predictions on the held-out test set of the outer loop. The performance on this test set was evaluated using two key metrics: Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and the coefficient of determination (
R2
).
This nested cross-validation process was repeated for each of the five outer folds. The final performance was reported as the average of the RMSE and R2 scores across all outer loop iterations, providing a robust and unbiased estimate of the model’s predictive capability.
