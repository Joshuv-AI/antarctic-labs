Norepinephrine versus colloid for preventing spinal anesthesia-induced hypotension and its impact on intracranial pressure in preeclamptic parturients: a randomized double-blind controlled trial
Authors: Wei Chen, ChuChu Du, Yulong Wang, Weidong Yao, Rui Wang, Bin Wang, Feifei Zhu, Yongquan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aims to evaluate the effects of two strategies for preventing hypotension after spinal anesthesia (prophylactic fluid loading and norepinephrine infusion) on intracranial pressure (ICP) in preeclamptic women by measuring the optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) using ultrasound.MethodsIn this prospective, randomized, controlled trial, 60 healthy parturients and 60 preeclamptic parturients undergoing cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia were enrolled. Normal parturients were randomly assigned to the infusion group (NA group, n = 30) and NE (norepinephrine) group (NB group, n = 30), while preeclamptic parturients were randomly assigned to the infusion group (PA group, n = 30) and NE group (PB group, n = 30). The primary outcome was bilateral ONSD values at baseline, pretreatment, 5 min after spinal anesthesia (Post-SA), and 5 min after fetal delivery (Post-birth). The secondary outcomes included maternal general characteristics, renal function indices, postoperative headache, fetal umbilical cord arterial blood gas indices, and Apgar scores.ResultsPretreatment ONSD was significantly higher than baseline in both infusion groups, and baseline ONSD was higher in preeclamptic versus normal parturients (all P < 0.01). Among preeclamptic parturients, pretreatment ONSD was higher in the PA group than in the PB group (P < 0.01), with a similar difference (PA > PB) confirmed in normal parturients (P < 0.01). In addition, compared to the PA group, the PB group showed no significant differences in systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), or mean arterial pressure (MAP) at any time point in preeclamptic parturients (P > 0.05). Conversely, in normal parturients, post-birth SBP, DBP, and MAP were significantly higher in the NB than in the NA group (P < 0.01). Baseline hemodynamic values were higher in preeclamptic versus normal parturients (P < 0.01), and post-birth values decreased significantly from baseline in the PA and PB groups (P < 0.001). No significant intergroup differences were observed in maternal heart rate (HR), renal function indices, umbilical artery blood gas indices, Apgar scores, or the incidence of postpartum headache (all P > 0.05).ConclusionFor preeclamptic parturients, the risks induced by ICP increase should be noted. Compared to colloids, prophylactic infusion of NE was more effective and safer and did not increase ICP.Trial registrationThis randomized controlled trial was registered on Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400092317; http://www.chictr.org.cn/) with the Clinical Trial Registry ({1})Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09440-3.

Introduction

Preeclampsia is diagnosed by the new onset of hypertension together with other maternal organ dysfunction after 20 weeks of gestation [1]. It is a pregnancy-specific obstetric disease and remains a leading cause of maternal and perinatal deaths in developing countries [2]. The incidence of severe preeclampsia has been rising every year [3]. Currently, there are no reliable treatments to prevent preeclampsia, with the most effective strategy being early detection and treatment. In practice, clinicians have observed that the most severe complication in preeclamptic parturients is acute cerebral complications [4], which initially manifests as elevated ICP, often without remarkable clinical signs. Elevated blood pressure in preeclamptic parturients could result in increased blood-brain barrier permeability, with 93% having reversible vasogenic edema that may progress to cytotoxic edema and infarction in up to a fourth of women [5]. Cerebral edema and hemorrhage due to ICP increase account for at least 75% of deaths in these patients [6]. Hence, real-time ICP monitoring in preeclamptic parturients is essential.

The clinical application of invasive ICP monitoring is limited by risks including intracranial infection, hemorrhage, and brain tissue injury. Non-invasive imaging techniques such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT), although not associated with these complications, are costly, less readily accessible, and offer limited utility for dynamic monitoring in acute settings, thereby restricting their use in parturients. Ultrasonographic ONSD has been suggested as a potential non-invasive ICP estimator [7], and can effectively help clinicians monitor changes in ICP in an early, rapid, real-time, and dynamic manner. Anatomically, the optic nerve sheath is a direct extension of the intracranial meningeal structure [8]. ICP increase could lead to ONSD enlargement. A meta-analysis showed that ONSD > 5 mm as a critical value for the diagnosis of elevated ICP in adult patients yields a specificity of 92.3% and a sensitivity of 95.6% [9].

Spinal anesthesia can improve the blood flow in the placental villi, reduce the levels of catecholamines in maternal plasma, and lower the incidence of adverse maternal outcomes. Thus, it is the preferred approach for preeclamptic parturients who must undergo a cesarean section [10]. During pregnancy, the enlarged uterus compresses the inferior vena cava, resulting in aortocaval compression and creating a compensatory dependence on sympathetic tone to maintain hemodynamic stability in the supine position. The sympathetic blockade induced by spinal anesthesia disrupts this compensatory response, thereby increasing the incidence of hypotension. This may be life-threatening for mothers and their fetuses. Effective clinical strategies to prevent maternal hypotension after spinal anesthesia include fluid administration and the use of vasopressors. Studies have increasingly demonstrated [11–13] that these two prophylactic measures can effectively prevent hypotension after spinal anesthesia.

Clinically, colloid fluid resuscitation and norepinephrine are the two most commonly adopted prophylactic regimens for post-spinal hypotension. However, clinical decision-making regarding regimen selection mainly relies on individual experience rather than high-quality clinical evidence. Although ONSD has been widely validated for noninvasive ICP assessment, head-to-head comparative studies investigating the effects of colloid and norepinephrine on ICP (indirectly evaluated via ONSD) in preeclamptic parturients remain scarce. Given the impaired cerebral autoregulation and vulnerable intracranial status of preeclamptic patients, it is unknown whether the two interventions exert distinct impacts on intracranial homeostasis. Furthermore, it also remains unclear whether their overall effects differ between preeclamptic parturients and healthy parturients with normal hemodynamics.

To address this critical knowledge gap between existing research and clinical practice, this study aimed to evaluate, using ultrasonographic ONSD measurement, the impact of these two preventive strategies on ICP in preeclamptic parturients, while also observing whether the same interventions yield different effects in healthy versus preeclamptic parturients. The findings are expected to provide novel evidence for refined hemodynamic management peripartum period in this special population and to offer supportive data for future clinical management and monitoring of ICP.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, randomized, controlled trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical College and registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400092317; http://www.chictr.org.cn/) with the Clinical Trial Registry ({1}). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians before the study.

This study enrolled normal parturients and parturients with preeclampsia scheduled for elective cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia between November 2024 and February 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: singleton pregnancy, age 18–48 years, body mass index (BMI) 20–40 kg/m2, and gestational age 34–40 weeks.The diagnostic criteria for pre-eclampsia were as follows: SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg (at least 4 hours apart for two random measurements); 24-h urinary protein ≥ 300 mg after 20 weeks of gestation, or urine test paper 1+. Exclusion criteria were as follows: refusal to sign the informed consent; history of ophthalmic diseases; coagulation disorders; total placenta previa, hyperthyroidism, chronic diseases of the heart, liver, lungs, or central nervous system; brain lesions causing changes in ICP or being unable to cooperate.

Randomization and blinding

In this study, randomization was conducted using a random number table. A total of 120 parturients were enrolled, including 60 normal parturients and 60 parturients with preeclampsia, all scheduled for elective cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia. Normal parturients were randomly assigned to either the fluid infusion group (Group NA, n = 30) or the norepinephrine group (Group NB, n = 30). Parturients with preeclampsia were randomly assigned to either the fluid infusion group (Group PA, n = 30) or the norepinephrine group (Group PB, n = 30). The group allocation was blinded to the pregnant women, nursing nurses, sonographers, anesthesiologists performing the procedure, and researchers responsible for data collection.

Interventions and anesthesia protocol

Upon entering the anesthesia preparation room, the sonographer performed bilateral optic nerve sheath diameter measurements on the parturient to record the baseline (taken at 30 minutes before anesthesia induction), which together reflect the steady-state physiological condition prior to intervention. Concurrently, the anesthesia nurse continuously monitored and recorded the parturient's MAP, SBP, DBP, HR, oxygen saturation (SpO₂), and electrocardiogram (ECG).

The anesthesia nurse administered norepinephrine at a rate of 0.05 μg/kg/min to the NE group according to the grouping protocol [14, 15]. For the fluid group, 300 mL hydroxyethyl starch (HES) was infused over 30 minutes. To maintain identical infusion appearance and achieve double-blinding, normal saline was then continuously pumped at a rate of 0.05 μg/kg/min in this group, matching the infusion mode of the NE group [16]. After pretreatment, ONSD was re-measured to assess changes following interventions, and hemodynamic parameters were recorded simultaneously. After entering the operating room,the parturient was placed in the left lateral position, and the L3–L4 intervertebral space was selected as the puncture site. According to the parturient's height, 1.0–1.2 mL of 0.5% hyperbaric bupivacaine combined with 2.5 μg sufentanil was administered. After clear cerebrospinal fluid outflow was observed, the cerebrospinal fluid was aspirated back to the 2 mL mark on the syringe (to confirm proper needle placement and to dilute the local anesthetic), and the injection was completed within 15–20 seconds. The same experienced anesthesiologist performed all procedures. Immediately after anesthesia, the parturient was body positioned to the left by 15° to prevent supine hypotensive syndrome. SBP, DBP, and HR were measured every 2 min, and the average value was recorded after 3 consecutive measurements. ONSD was measured via ultrasonography at 5 minutes after spinal anesthesia. This post-spinal (Post-SA) time point was selected because sensory and sympathetic blockade typically peak at this stage, resulting in the most pronounced decrease in blood pressure. It therefore represents the most critical window for assessing the immediate efficacy of preventive interventions.The level was controlled at T8-T6, not lower than T10, and not higher than T4. ONSD and hemodynamic parameters were further recorded at 5 minutes after fetal delivery (Post-birth). This postpartum time point captures the moment of abrupt reduction in intra‑abdominal pressure and the most dramatic physiological shift in venous return, thereby serving to evaluate the impact of interventions during the post‑delivery phase. All parturients received an intravenous injection of 3U oxytocin after omphalotomy.

If the maternal MAP decreased more than 30% of the baseline value or SBP was less than 80% of the baseline value, an intravenous injection of NE 4 μg was administered. If there was no improvement, the treatment was repeated. After the operation, another anesthesiologist followed up the occurrence of postoperative headache.

Ultrasonic ONSD Measurement

Ultrasonic ONSD measurement was performed by a sonographer. The parturient was placed in the supine position, and a sterile dressing was placed over the eyes. Afterward, a 12-MHz high-frequency ultrasound probe filled with couplant was placed on both eyes with gentle movement, avoiding compressing the eyeball. The probe position was adjusted to provide an appropriate angle to display the optic nerve entering the augen. ONSD was measured 3 mm behind the globe in a two-dimensional mode with electronic calipers, on an axis perpendicular to the optic nerve. Three measurements were performed for each optic nerve in the transverse and sagittal planes, and the average (i.e., the transverse and sagittal planes of both eyes) of the six measurements were obtained, accurate to 0.01mm; the measurement duration was 3 min, as shown in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Ultrasonographic measurement of optic nerve sheath diameter. 1 indicates the measuring site 0.30 cm posterior to the eyeball; 2 shows the measured optic nerve sheath diameter of 0.39 cm.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of the study was the ultrasonographic bilateral eyeball ONSD values at Baseline, Pretreatment, Post-SA, and Post-birth.

The secondary outcomes included maternal hemodynamic parameters (MAP, SBP, DBP, HR); demographic and obstetric characteristics (age, gravidity, parity, gestational age, height, weight, ASA classification); perioperative variables, including renal function indices (blood urea nitrogen [BUN], creatinine [Cre], and cystatin C [Cys-C]), sensory block level, and surgery time; anthropometric and fetal ultrasound measurements (fundal height, abdominal circumference, fetal biparietal diameter [BPD] and femur length [FL]); and maternal and neonatal outcomes (postpartum headache, umbilical cord arterial blood gas indices, and neonatal Apgar scores).

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using PASS 15 software (NCSS, LLC., Kaysville, UT, USA). Based on a pilot study of 20 parturients (data not included in the final analysis), the mean ONSD after pretreatment was 5.42 ± 0.25 mm in the preeclampsia infusion group and 5.21 ± 0.23 mm in the preeclampsia norepinephrine group. To detect this difference with a two‑sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, 21 participants per group were required. Accounting for a 20% dropout rate, the sample size was increased to 26 per group. To facilitate balanced randomization using a block design, we rounded this to 30 participants per group, resulting in a planned total of 120.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). The null hypothesis was that there was no difference in the primary outcomes between the norepinephrine group and the colloid group, while the alternative hypothesis assumed a between-group difference. All statistical tests adopted a two-tailed (bi-directional) design. The normality of continuous data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical data are presented as frequency (percentage) and were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher,s exact test, as appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant

Results

Patient characteristics and obstetric data

A total of 120 parturients were recruited, comprising 60 healthy parturients and 60 parturients with preeclampsia. During the study, two cases were excluded due to spinal anesthesia levels above T4, three cases were excluded because of conversion to general anesthesia (i.e., spinal anesthesia levels below T10), and one case was excluded for exceeding 3 hours in duration. Consequently, the final cohort included 30 participants in the NA group, 29 in the PA group, 28 in the NB group, and 27 in the PB group. Thus, a total of 114 patients were included in the final data analysis (Fig 2). No significant differences in maternal age, gravidity, parity, BMI, gestational age, fundal height, abdominal circumference, BPD, FL, surgery time, and block levels among the four groups (P > 0.05) were observed (Table 1).

Discussion

Hypotension induced by spinal anesthesia during cesarean delivery is a significant clinical concern, particularly for preeclamptic parturients, whose blood-brain barrier permeability may vary with blood pressure changes, thereby affecting intracranial pressure. In this study, we assessed maternal intracranial pressure changes indirectly by measuring ONSD. We found that baseline ONSD values were significantly higher in preeclamptic parturients than in normal parturients, alerting clinicians to the inherent risk of elevated baseline ICP among preeclamptic women. Furthermore, we observed similar trends in ONSD changes between preeclamptic and normal parturients under different preventive strategies. More importantly, for preeclamptic parturients, prophylactic norepinephrine infusion, compared with colloid infusion, generally did not increase intracranial pressure and provided more stable hemodynamic parameters. These findings underscore the potential cerebroprotective role of prophylactic norepinephrine infusion in preeclamptic women undergoing cesarean delivery and offer a robust supportive strategy for guiding peri-partum hemodynamic management in this special population.

The superior efficacy of norepinephrine can be attributed to its targeted physiological action. Preeclampsia is characterized by systemic endothelial dysfunction and impaired baroreflex sensitivity, resulting in unstable hemodynamic status. This condition is accompanied by hypovolemia proportional to disease severity [17], which can be further exacerbated by preoperative fasting and spinal anesthesia-induced vasodilation. While colloids increase plasma oncotic pressure and expand intravascular volume, they may fail to reduce or could even elevate cerebral water content and intracranial pressure [18]. As a potent α1-adrenergic agonist, norepinephrine directly increases systemic vascular resistance, thereby sustaining mean arterial pressure and cerebral perfusion pressure more reliably than volume expansion alone [18, 19]. This may effectively prevent abrupt decreases in cerebral blood flow that could trigger intracranial compensatory mechanisms. Moreover, norepinephrine exhibits moderate β-adrenergic activity [18, 20] and has a limited effect on venous capacitance. By maintaining perfusion pressure within a more stable range, it may help preserve cerebral autoregulation and avoid the increase in cerebral blood volume associated with aggressive fluid loading, thereby preventing the rise in intracranial pressure observed with colloid administration.

This study observed a downward trend in post-spinal and post-delivery ONSD across all four groups. Previous research has established a linear correlation between subarachnoid pressure and ICP [21]. Spinal anesthesia blocks sympathetic preganglionic fibers, leading to peripheral vasodilation and a consequent decrease in systemic blood pressure. This reduction in blood pressure lowers cerebral perfusion pressure a key determinant of cerebral blood volume and thus ICP. Furthermore, in full-term parturients, increased intra-abdominal pressure and compression of the inferior vena cava by the gravid uterus elevate epidural space pressure compared to non-pregnant women [22]. After delivery, when mechanical compression on the spine and abdominal cavity is relieved, the epidural space pressure decreases. This reduction in extravascular pressure facilitates central venous drainage, which in turn promotes a decrease in ICP [23]. The combination of sympathectomy-induced hypotension and the relief of aortocaval compression after delivery provides a coherent physiological explanation for the reduction in ONSD observed in our study. Notably, however, the ONSD in the NE group decreased significantly from baseline after fetal delivery, suggesting a faster normalization of ICP, which is crucial for cerebral protection. In contrast, the infusion group showed no such change, likely due to the ONSD enlargement induced by pre-anesthetic colloid administration. This further indicates that continuous NE infusion may more effectively facilitate the return of ICP to normal levels in preeclamptic parturients compared with colloid infusion.

International guidelines recommend a restrictive fluid strategy in this population [24, 25], which informed our protocol of limiting pre-spinal hydroxyethyl starch to 300 mL [16]. Concurrently, prophylactic NE infusion at 0.05 μg/kg/min has been shown to effectively prevent hypotension without inducing hypertension in both healthy and preeclamptic parturients [14, 15]. Our findings align with this evidence: in preeclamptic parturients, NE achieved hemodynamic stability comparable to that with colloid, without significantly elevating blood pressure (intergroup comparisons of SBP, DBP, and MAP showed P > 0.05). In normal parturients, NE provided superior post-delivery hemodynamic support. Notably, NE had minimal effect on heart rate across all groups, consistent with previous reports [26].

Furthermore, while current evidence indicates that colloid preload is more effective than crystalloid in reducing the incidence of hypotension [27] and may be physiologically better suited to the lowered plasma osmotic pressure in preeclampsia [28, 29], concerns regarding its renal impact persist. In line with this, our study also monitored maternal renal function, and consistent with previous reports, no short-term adverse effects were observed [30]. This study noted a higher incidence of postpartum headache in the PA group (20.7%) than in the PB group (11.1%) among preeclamptic parturients. This difference may be related to an acute reduction in intracranial pressure after delivery, potentially affecting cerebrospinal fluid dynamics or dural tension and thereby contributing to headache onset. However, as intracranial pressure was not directly measured in this study, the mechanism remains speculative. Future investigations incorporating synchronous invasive intracranial pressure monitoring are warranted to establish a direct causal relationship between ONSD changes and postpartum headache. Additionally, no significant differences were found in neonatal blood gas indices or Apgar scores across the four groups, indicating that both prophylactic strategies were safe for the newborn.

These findings challenge the cautious approach toward vasopressor use in hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and suggest that prophylactic norepinephrine does not impose an additional intracranial risk. This supports a paradigm shift toward goal-directed hemodynamic management, which prioritizes the maintenance of cerebral perfusion pressure over aggressive fluid administration in this vulnerable population.

This study has several limitations. ONSD measurements were confined to the peri-anesthetic period without postpartum follow-up. Only non-severe (mild-to-moderate) preeclampsia cases were included, limiting generalizability to severe forms of the disease. Fixed fluid and NE protocols were employed; individualized titration might yield different effects. Most importantly, ICP was assessed indirectly via ONSD, and the precise cerebrovascular effects of NE warrant direct investigation in future studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, for preeclamptic parturients, the risks associated with elevated ICP should be noted. Compared with colloid preloading, prophylactic norepinephrine was found to be more effective and safer, without further increasing ICP. This regimen is worthy of clinical promotion, and multicenter, large-sample prospective trials are required to further validate our conclusions.
