A chromosome-level genome assembly of the scaly osman Diptychus maculatus
Authors: Jiangong Niu, Yibang Wang, Tao Zhang, Jiangwei Hu, Bowen Yang, Renming Zhang, Hui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diptychus maculatus, a rare cold-water cyprinid endemic to the high-altitude river systems of Xinjiang, China, has experienced range contraction and increasing conservation concern. However, the lack of a chromosome-level reference genome has limited downstream studies in conservation genomics and comparative genomics for this non-model species. Here, we present a chromosome-level genome assembly of D. maculatus generated using PacBio HiFi, Illumina short-read, and Hi-C data, with RNA-seq supporting gene annotation. The assembled genome totals 1.79 Gb with a scaffold N50 of 33.13 Mb, anchoring 90.68% (1.63 Gb) of the assembly onto 49 pseudo-chromosomes. BUSCO analysis indicates high completeness with a score of 99.30%, including 15.10% single-copy and 84.20% duplicated BUSCOs. The genome contains 44.55% (802.16 Mb) repetitive elements and encodes 60,481 predicted protein-coding genes, of which 85.66% are functionally annotated in at least one public database. Chromosome-scale synteny analysis reveals extensive collinearity between D. maculatus and Aspiorhynchus laticeps, supporting the utility of this assembly for comparative analyses within Cypriniformes. This genome assembly and associated annotations provide a valuable resource for future studies of genome evolution and conservation genomics in high-altitude freshwater fishes.

Background & Summary

The scaly osman (Diptychus maculatus) is a rare, cold-water cyprinid fish endemic to high-altitude freshwater ecosystems in Xinjiang, China1. Historically, it was distributed across the Tarim and Ili river systems, whereas recent records indicate that its distribution has become more restricted, with occurrences reported from the Tekes River (upper Ili) and the Yarkant River (upper Tarim)2. Although D. maculatus inhabits harsh high-altitude environments, it shows less pronounced extreme morphological and physiological specializations than those reported for some other high-altitude schizothoracine fishes. Notable morphological divergence has been observed between river-system populations, such as a distinct silver longitudinal stripe in the Tarim population3. The species’ slow reproductive rate, low fecundity, and delayed sexual maturation increase its vulnerability to anthropogenic pressures, including overfishing, which has contributed to population declines4. Habitat alteration driven by climate change and human activities has further exacerbated these threats. Rising water temperatures and altered hydrological regimes in high-altitude streams, for instance, may reduce the availability of suitable spawning and feeding habitats for this cold-adapted species. Consequently, D. maculatus was listed as a Class II protected species in China in 20211,2.

To date, research on D. maculatus has largely focused on morphology5, population distribution6, and genetic diversity based on limited markers7. Mitochondrial genomes have been reported to clarify its evolutionary placement within schizothoracine lineages8–10, but a high-quality reference genome and comprehensive annotations have remained unavailable, constraining conservation genomics and comparative evolutionary analyses. Generating chromosome-level genomic resources can help address this gap by providing a foundation for investigating genome structure, genetic diversity, and candidate molecular pathways associated with environmental adaptation, thereby supporting conservation planning and management for species with fragmented habitats and limited genomic data11,12.

Here, we report the first chromosome-level genome assembly for D. maculatus, generated using a multi-platform sequencing strategy combining Illumina short reads, PacBio HiFi reads, Hi-C, and RNA-seq data (Fig. 1). The final assembly is 1.79 Gb in length, with a scaffold N50 of 33.13 Mb, and 90.68% of sequences anchored to 49 pseudo-chromosomes. Genome completeness assessed with BUSCO reached 99.30% (Actinopterygii_odb10). We also provide repeat annotations, non-coding RNA annotations, and protein-coding gene models to facilitate downstream reuse. Together, these resources establish a genomic reference for future studies on high-altitude freshwater fishes and enable comparative analyses relevant to conservation and evolutionary research.Fig. 1The pipelines overview of D. maculatus chromosome-level genome assembly.

Methods

Sample collection and sequencing

A wild adult female Diptychus maculatus was collected from the Ili River, Xinjiang, China (43°54′N, 82°28′E) under official sampling permission (Fig. 2). Muscle tissue was dissected immediately, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for genomic DNA extraction. In addition, total RNA was extracted from multiple tissues for RNA-seq library construction. Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified CTAB method11, purified with phenol–chloroform, and quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA integrity was assessed by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis.Fig. 2Photographs of D. maculatus used in the genome sequencing and assembly (Sex: Female; standard length: 215 mm; standard weight: 320 g).

Photographs of D. maculatus used in the genome sequencing and assembly (Sex: Female; standard length: 215 mm; standard weight: 320 g).

For Illumina short-read sequencing, a 350-bp insert paired-end library (PE150) was prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. For long-read sequencing, a ~15-kb SMRTbell library was constructed following the manufacturer’s protocol (PacBio) and sequenced on the PacBio Sequel IIe platform to generate subreads; circular consensus sequencing was then used to generate HiFi reads for de novo assembly. Hi-C libraries were prepared using the Arima-HiC kit (A510008, Arima Genomics) and sequenced on an Illumina platform (PE150) to capture chromatin contact information for chromosome-scale scaffolding. RNA-seq libraries were constructed from total RNA of multiple tissues and sequenced to support gene model prediction and functional annotation.

In total, 1,407.26 Gb of raw sequencing data were generated, including 184.69 Gb of Illumina short reads, 928.98 Gb of PacBio subreads, 44.77 Gb of PacBio HiFi reads, 170.24 Gb of Hi-C data, and 78.58 Gb of RNA-seq data (Table 1). Raw reads were quality-checked using FastQC v0.11.913. Genome size estimation and assembly

Genome size and heterozygosity were estimated from the k-mer frequency-depth distribution of quality-filtered Illumina reads. K-mers (k = 19) were counted using Jellyfish v2.3.015, and the distribution was modeled with GenomeScope v2.016. To construct chromosome-scale scaffolds, Hi-C reads were aligned to the polished assembly using BWA-MEM v0.7.1720, and valid interaction pairs were obtained using HiC-Pro v3.1.021 and formatted as required for downstream scaffolding. Chromosome clustering, ordering, and orientation were performed using the 3D-DNA pipeline (v180922), followed by manual refinement in Juicebox v1.11.0822 and final scaffolding with YaHS v1.2a.123. The final assembly spanned 1,800.81 Mb across 481 scaffolds, with a scaffold N50 of 33.13 Mb (Table 2). Genome annotation

Repeat sequences were annotated using a combination of de novo and homology-based approaches. A species-specific repeat library was first constructed with RepeatModeler v2.0.424, and the genome was masked using RepeatMasker v4.1.525. In total, 802.16 Mb of repetitive sequences were identified, accounting for 44.55% of the assembled genome (Table 4). DNA transposons represented the largest component (427.69 Mb; 23.75%), followed by retroelements (245.11 Mb; 13.61%), including LTR elements (108.52 Mb; 6.03%), LINEs (101.86 Mb; 5.66%), and SINEs (34.73 Mb; 1.93%) (Table 3). Protein-coding gene models were predicted by integrating ab initio, homology-based, and transcriptome-assisted evidence. RNA-seq reads were aligned to the repeat-masked genome using HISAT2 v2.2.128 and assembled into transcript models with StringTie v2.3.329. For homology-based prediction, protein sequences from closely related teleosts (Danio rerio, Cyprinus carpio, Sinocyclocheilus grahami, and Aspiorhynchus laticeps) were aligned to the genome using Exonerate v2.4.0 with thresholds of E ≤ 1 × 10−⁵ and coverage ≥50%30. Ab initio prediction was performed using Augustus v3.4.031 and GeneMark-ES v4.7132, with model training supported by the D. maculatus transcriptome. Evidence from the three strategies was integrated within the MAKER v3.01.03 framework33, and the final consensus gene set was generated using EvidenceModeler v1.1.134. Ultimately, 60,481 protein-coding genes were predicted (Table 6). The mean gene length was 13,857 bp, with a mean CDS length per gene of 1,459.4 bp; each gene contained an average of 8.4 exons (mean exon length 172.7 bp; mean intron length 1,664.4 bp) (Table 5). Protein-coding regions spanned 838.09 Mb, accounting for 46.54% of the genome (Table 6). The predicted number of protein-coding genes in D. maculatus was higher than that reported for many diploid teleost genomes. For comparison, 48,537 protein-coding genes were predicted in the chromosome-level genome of Aspiorhynchus laticeps11, whereas 52,610 protein-coding genes were reported in the domesticated common carp (Cyprinus carpio)35 and 80,062 protein-coding genes in the goldfish (Carassius auratus)36 genome assembly. The relatively high number of predicted protein-coding genes in D. maculatus may primarily reflect its distinctive genomic background, particularly its high chromosome number and the retention of homologous or duplicated genomic regions. Chromosomal collinearity analysis

Chromosome-scale synteny among D. maculatus, A. laticeps, and Carassius auratus was examined using the jcvi toolkit39. Orthologous gene pairs used as anchors were inferred with OrthoFinder v2.2.740 and converted into the required jcvi formats for pairwise comparisons. Anchor searches were performed with the jcvi.compara.catalog pipeline (BLAST+, –noself). Syntenic blocks were identified using the MCScan module in jcvi, retaining only blocks containing ≥5 anchors, and visualized using jcvi plotting utilities. Comparative analysis revealed extensive conservation of chromosomal organization between D. maculatus and A. laticeps across large genomic segments (Fig. 6). In contrast, syntenic links between D. maculatus and C. auratus were more fragmented, showing multiple inter-chromosomal correspondences. This fragmentation is consistent with the complex genome history of C. auratus36, which is an allotetraploid that has undergone an independent round of whole-genome duplication and extensive chromosomal rearrangements after its divergence from D. maculatus.Fig. 6Chromosomal collinearity relationships among D. maculatus, Aspiorhynchus laticeps, and Carassius auratus.

Technical Validation

Genome assembly completeness was assessed using BUSCO v6.0.041 in genome mode against the Actinopterygii_odb10 dataset. BUSCO recovered 3,615 complete BUSCOs (99.3%), of which 550 (15.1%) were single-copy and 3,065 (84.2%) were duplicated, with 5 fragmented and 20 missing (Table 8). To determine whether the elevated duplication rate reflects genuine ohnolog retention or unresolved haplotypic redundancy, we performed a genome-wide self-synteny analysis using MCScanX v1.0.042. This analysis identified large inter-chromosomal collinear blocks encompassing 74.5% of all predicted genes (Fig. 7), with the majority of duplicated BUSCOs located within these syntenic regions.
