UV-C irradiation enhances the dual-use potential of Desmodesmus quadricauda and Scenedesmus dimorphus for food and biodiesel production
Authors: Jiraporn Sirison, Suneerat Ruangsomboon, Pattanasak Chiwpreecha, Buppha Jongput, Duenrut Chonudomkul, Kankanit Khwanpruk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
UV-C irradiation was evaluated as a strategy to enhance the dual-use potential of green microalgae Desmodesmus quadricauda and Scenedesmus dimorphus for functional food and biodiesel applications. Cultures were exposed to UV-C for 0–3 h and cultivated for 14 days. Biomass production increased significantly, reaching maxima at 3 h in D. quadricauda (1.63 ± 0.10 g L−1) and at 1 h in S. dimorphus (1.27 ± 0.05 g L−1). UV-C irradiation markedly enhanced carotenoid content and increased protein levels by 1.2–1.3-fold, suggesting improved nutritional value. Lipid content and productivity peaked at 1 h in both species, with 1.4–1.9-fold increases relative to controls. Fatty acid composition was modified under UV-C treatment, including increased oleic acid levels and the detection of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), supporting potential functional food applications. Biodiesel properties, calculated from fatty acid methyl ester profiles, showed cetane numbers exceeding EN 14,214 minimum requirements, however, the cold-flow properties (CP and CFPP) of both species failed to meet the standard specifications. Overall, UV-C irradiation modulated biomass accumulation and metabolite composition, enhancing lipid productivity and altering fatty acid profiles, although further optimization is required to fully meet biodiesel fuel standards.

Introduction

Microalgae have emerged as one of the most versatile and sustainable biological resources for addressing global challenges in food security and renewable energy production. Their rapid growth rates, high photosynthetic efficiency, and ability to utilize CO2 directly from the atmosphere or industrial emissions position them as promising candidates within the bioeconomy. Compared to conventional terrestrial crops, microalgae require less land, and yield substantially higher biomass productivity, underscoring their scalability for both food and energy applications1,2. Their biochemical richness-including essential amino acids, omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids, pigments, polysaccharides, vitamins, and antioxidants-supports diverse applications in human nutrition, animal feed, functional foods, and nutraceuticals.

From an energy perspective, the transition toward low-carbon technologies and the depletion of fossil fuel reserves have intensified the search for sustainable alternatives capable of reducing greenhouse gas emissions. Biodiesel derived from microalgal lipids is regarded as one of the most promising renewable fuels due to its biodegradability, carbon neutrality, and high compatibility with existing diesel infrastructure. Importantly, microalgae can generate lipid yields far exceeding those of oilseed crops, while their fatty acid profiles can be tailored to improve key fuel properties such as cetane number, oxidative stability, and cold flow characteristics3–5.

Within this context, members of the green microalgal genera Desmodesmus and Scenedesmus are of particular interest. These species are characterized by their resilience to environmental fluctuations, high biomass productivity, and broad biochemical potential. Their lipid fractions often contain a balanced mixture of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids suitable for efficient biodiesel conversion, while their high protein content, pigments (chlorophylls and carotenoids), and antioxidant compounds add value for food and nutraceutical applications6–8. These attributes make them well suited for integrated biorefinery systems aimed at maximizing both nutritional and energy outputs.

Enhancing the economic feasibility of microalgal cultivation requires strategies that increase both biomass and the accumulation of desirable metabolites. One effective approach involves the application of controlled stressors-such as nutrient limitation, salinity shifts, high-light exposure, or ultraviolet (UV) radiation-which can redirect cellular metabolism toward the production of lipids, pigments, and other valuable compounds9–12. Among these stressors, UV-C irradiation has gained notable attention due to its dual role as both a mutagenic agent and a metabolic enhancer. UV-C exposure induces oxidative stress and DNA damage, triggering cellular responses that may stimulate lipid biosynthesis, modify fatty acid composition, and elevate the accumulation of pigments and antioxidants13–18. Several studies have reported increased levels of carotenoids, chlorophylls, carbohydrates, and proteins after controlled UV-C exposure, as demonstrated in Scenedesmus vacuolatus and other species19. Furthermore, UV-C treatment has been shown to increase beneficial omega-3 fatty acids such as alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) in Desmodesmus sp.20, highlighting its potential for nutritional enhancement.

Beyond nutritional implications, UV-C induced modifications in lipid content and fatty acid distribution can substantially influence biodiesel quality. Increases in saturated fatty acids can enhance cetane number and oxidative stability, whereas higher unsaturation can improve cold-flow behavior, allowing the resulting biodiesel to meet or exceed international specifications21. UV-C based mutagenesis has also been demonstrated to improve productivity and hydrocarbon yields in biofuel-producing species such as Botryococcus braunii12, reinforcing the utility of this approach for bioenergy applications.

In Thailand, Desmodesmus quadricauda and Scenedesmus dimorphus are among the most commonly found freshwater microalgae. Their capacity to grow rapidly under high temperatures and outdoor conditions makes them especially suitable for large-scale cultivation systems. Although their lipid content is moderate compared to highly oleaginous species, their cultivation robustness, resistance to contamination, and rich nutritional profiles-including essential fatty acids, proteins, and antioxidants-present significant advantages for dual-purpose applications in food and energy22,23.

Despite the growing interest in UV-C mediated enhancement strategies, most studies have focused narrowly on biomass, lipid accumulation, or biodiesel yield. Comprehensive evaluations that simultaneously assess the impacts of UV-C exposure on growth performance, biochemical composition across multiple metabolite classes (pigments, proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, fatty acids), and biodiesel properties remain scarce. This represents a critical knowledge gap, particularly for microalgae intended for integrated food-energy biorefinery systems.

Accordingly, the present study investigates the effects of UV-C irradiation on the biomass production, biochemical profiles, and biodiesel quality of D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus. By examining both nutritional components and energy-related traits, this work aims to establish optimized UV-C pretreatment strategies that enhance the dual functionality of these microalgae as sustainable feedstocks for high-value food products and renewable biodiesel production. The findings are expected to support the development of integrated cultivation frameworks that simultaneously improve nutritional benefits and energy yields, contributing to the advancement of sustainable food-energy systems.

Materials and methods

Preparation of algal cultures

The green microalgae, Desmodesmus quadricauda (Turpin) Hegewald and Scenedesmus dimorphus (Turpin) Kützing were isolated from a freshwater pond at the School of Agricultural Technology, King Mongkut’s Institute of Technology Ladkrabang. Single cells were aseptically cultured in Bold’s Basal Medium (BBM), a medium commonly used for green microalgae cultivation, at 25 ± 1 °C in 250-mL flasks on a rotary shaker under a 24-h light cycle (60 µmol photons m−2 s−1, fluorescent lamps). Cultures were scaled up in 1-L flasks with continuous aeration and used as stock inocula for subsequent experiments.

Experimental design and sampling

UV-C pretreatment and cultivation conditions

Microalgal cultures in the exponential phase were exposed to UV-C radiation using a low-pressure mercury lamp (Philips TUV 30 W G30 T8, Philips, The Netherlands) emitting at 254 nm. This wavelength was selected because it closely matches the maximal nucleic acid absorption peak (~ 260 nm) and efficiently induces direct DNA photolesions, particularly cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) and 6 − 4 photoproducts, which are established indicators of UV-induced genotoxic stress. Algal suspensions were transferred to sterile Petri dishes to form a uniform 0.2 cm liquid layer and positioned 20 cm beneath the lamp (top-down exposure, without lids), resulting in an incident irradiance of 7.0 W m−2 at the culture surface. Exposure times of 0, 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 h corresponded to cumulative doses of 0, 12.6, 25.2, 50.4, and 75.6 kJ m−2, respectively. Irradiation was performed under static conditions at 25 °C. Following exposure, cultures were incubated in complete darkness overnight to suppress light-dependent photoreactivation by photolyase enzymes, thereby preventing rapid reversal of CPDs and enabling assessment of UV-induced physiological responses independent of immediate light-driven DNA repair24.

Subsequently, the pretreated biomass was inoculated at 10% (v/v) into fresh medium in 1-L Erlenmeyer flasks. Cultivation was maintained at 25 °C under a light intensity of 60 µmol photons m−2 s−1 with a 10-h light/14-h dark cycle. To ensure uniform cell exposure and prevent sedimentation, the cultures were continuously aerated with filtered ambient air at a constant flow rate of 0.4 vvm.

Cultivation was continued for 14 days, with samples collected every 2 days. This duration was selected based on a preliminary test showing that control cultures reached the stationary phase after 14 days. At each sampling, biomass, chlorophyll-a, carotenoid, carbohydrate, and protein contents were determined after 2 h of illumination, while lipid and fatty acid contents were measured on day 14.

Analytical methods

Biomass and biochemical analyses

Biomass concentration was determined by the dry weight method as described by Ruangsomboon25. Chlorophyll-a and carotenoid contents were determined following the procedure of Becker26. Carbohydrate content was measured using the phenol-sulfuric acid method (DuBois et al.27), and protein content was quantified by the Lowry method (Lowry et al.28).

Lipid extraction and quantification

Algal biomass was dried at 40 °C and ground into powder with a mortar and pestle. Lipids were extracted using a chloroform-methanol mixture (1:2, v/v) following Bligh and Dyer29. The suspension was sonicated with a Transonic 460/H unit (Elma, Singen, Germany) at 70 Hz and room temperature for 90 min. The crude extract was concentrated with a rotary evaporator, and lipid content was expressed as a percentage of sample dry weight.

Fatty acid analysis

Fatty acid composition was determined following direct transmethylation of crude lipid extracts to generate fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs). The resulting FAMEs were analyzed on an Agilent Technologies 6890 N gas chromatograph equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID). Separation was achieved using a Supelco fused-silica capillary column (100 m × 0.25 mm internal diameter, 0.2 μm film thickness). Helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.1 mL min−1. Samples (1 µL) were introduced in split mode (100:1) with the injector maintained at 250 °C. The oven program began at 140 °C with a 5 min hold, followed by a linear increase of 3 °C min−1 to 250 °C and a final hold of 17 min. The FID was operated at 250 °C with air and hydrogen flow rates of 450 and 40 mL min−1, respectively.

Identification of individual fatty acids, including docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and trans isomers such as linoelaidic acid, was established by matching retention times against certified external FAME standards (Supelco FAME Mix, C4-C24, Merck). Peak identification relied solely on agreement with these authenticated standards.

Calculation of biodiesel fuel properties

Biodiesel quality parameters, including saponification value (SV), iodine value (IV), cetane number (CN), degree of unsaturation (DU), long-chain saturated factor (LCSF), cold filter plugging point (CFPP), cloud point (CP), pour point (PP), oxidation stability (OS), kinematic viscosity (ν), specific gravity (ρ), and density (D) were calculated from the fatty acid composition using established equations1,30–33.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from four biological replicates (n = 4). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 30.0.

Data were analyzed via one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test to identify specific differences between treatment groups. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Effect of UV-C radiation on biomass production

Exposure to UV-C radiation influenced the growth of both D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus. In D. quadricauda, UV-C exposure for 0–3 h produced similar growth patterns, with most cultures reaching the stationary phase on day 14, while the control reached it on day 10. The highest biomass (1.63 ± 0.10 g L−1) was observed at 3 h of UV-C, though this was not significantly different from 0.5 to 2 h treatments. All UV-C-exposed groups showed significantly higher biomass than the control (1.40 ± 0.06 g L−1), representing a 1.1-1.2-fold increase (Fig. 1A).

For S. dimorphus, the maximum biomass (1.27 ± 0.05 g L−1) occurred at 1 h of UV-C, statistically similar to the 2 h treatment but higher than the control, 0.5, and 3 h exposures (Fig. 1B). Biomass in UV-C exposed cultures was 1.1-1.2-fold greater than the control (1.04 ± 0.03 g L−1). Growth increased with UV-C exposure up to 1 h, whereas longer irradiation reduced biomass, though values remained above the control.

In this study, D. quadricauda exposed to 3 h of UV-C exhibited the highest biomass exceeding previously reported values for the same species. For example, cultures grown without stress produced only 0.399 ± 0.074 g L−122, while those cultivated under varying nitrogen levels and aeration rates reached 0.230–0.382 g L−134. These results align with earlier findings showing that Desmodesmus species exposed to the longest UV-C duration achieved the greatest biomass, with a 1.4-fold increase relative to non-irradiated controls20, and that UV-C induced mutants of Desmodesmus armatus displayed enhanced biomass productivity compared with wild-type strains17.

For S. dimorphus, biomass peaked at 1 h UV-C exposure. This value is higher than that reported for cultures grown with a fourfold increase in nitrogen concentration (1.04 ± 0.03 g L−1), but lower than biomass achieved under elevated phosphorus (1.35 ± 0.01 g L−1) or iron supplementation (1.75 ± 0.01 g L−1)8. Comparisons with other studies further highlight the influence of environmental stress: S. dimorphus grown under salinity stress reached only 0.38–0.51 g L−1 and entered the stationary phase earlier than the control23, while cultures grown in landfill leachate had biomass of 0.696 ± 0.026 g L−135. Biomass values of 0.74 ± 0.02 to 1.16 ± 0.06 g L−1 were reported under 0.03-2.0% CO2, and 0.39 ± 0.00 to 1.03 ± 0.00 g L−1 under different nitrogen sources36. Consistent with these trends, Scenedesmus sp. have also shown higher biomass-1.3-fold relative to controls-following UV-C exposure18.

Overall, the present findings demonstrate that UV-C exposure can enhance biomass production in both D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus, in line with previously reported stress-induced improvements in microalgal growth. The stimulation of growth by moderate UV-C exposure may be related to enhanced nutrient uptake, while prolonged exposure likely induced cellular stress, diverting energy to maintain homeostasis. Comparing the two species, D. quadricauda achieved higher final biomass than S. dimorphus, suggesting greater resilience and adaptability to UV-C stress. In contrast, S. dimorphus showed a narrow optimal exposure window (1 h), beyond which growth was not effectively promoted.

Effect of UV-C radiation on algal pigments

At the end of the study, D. quadricauda exhibited the highest chlorophyll-a content in cultures exposed to 1 h of UV-C (1.52 ± 0.04 mg g−1) and the lowest in the 2 h treatment, while the control showed minimal variation (Fig. 2A). Following UV-C exposure, chlorophyll-a declined across all treatments during the first 6 days, consistent with transient chloroplast impairment, then recovered by day 8 and remained relatively stable thereafter. By the end of the cultivation period, chlorophyll-a levels no longer differed significantly among treatments. Carotenoid content was highest in the 3 h UV-C treatment (0.45 ± 0.03 mg g−1), significantly exceeding that of the 0 and 0.5 h treatments (Fig. 2B). Carotenoids accumulated rapidly during the first 2 days after exposure, with greater accumulation observed in cultures receiving longer irradiation, and gradually decreased by day 12 as stress diminished.

In S. dimorphus, chlorophyll-a content peaked in the 2 h UV-C treatment (1.71 ± 0.05 mg g−1), significantly higher than in cultures exposed for 0–1 h (Fig. 2C). Similar to D. quadricauda, chlorophyll-a declined during the initial 8 days following UV-C exposure and subsequently recovered from day 10 onward, indicating successful acclimation. Carotenoid content was highest in the 3 h UV-C treatment (0.87 ± 0.06 mg g−1), with rapid accumulation occurring within the first 2 days and increasing proportionally with exposure duration, reflecting the activation of photoprotective mechanisms (Fig. 2D).

Overall, UV-C exposure stimulated pigment synthesis in both species, although the responses of chlorophyll-a and carotenoids differed in magnitude and timing. Relative to their respective controls, chlorophyll-a in S. dimorphus increased 1.4-fold after 2 h of exposure, whereas carotenoids reached maximal levels at 3 h, increasing 1.3-fold in D. quadricauda and 1.7-fold in S. dimorphus (Fig. 2B and D). The greater carotenoid accumulation at prolonged UV-C exposure times likely reflects a photoprotective response to UV-induced oxidative stress. Under elevated UV radiation, microalgae activate stress-response mechanisms that enhance carotenoid biosynthesis to mitigate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and protect cellular components. Such responses are widely documented in green microalgae and are consistent with the established role of carotenoids as non-enzymatic antioxidants capable of scavenging ROS and quenching singlet oxygen generated under UV stress37–39. Although ROS levels and antioxidant enzyme activities were not directly quantified in the present study, the enhanced carotenoid content may reflect a protective acclimation response aimed at preserving photosynthetic integrity and cellular redox balance.

In contrast, chlorophyll-a may transiently decline during early exposure due to pigment degradation or the downregulation of photosynthetic activity under stress conditions. However, no significant differences in chlorophyll-a were observed among UV-C treatments at later cultivation stages, suggesting physiological acclimation over time. Notably, chlorophyll-a was expressed as mg g−1 (intracellular pigment concentration per unit biomass), whereas biomass was measured as g L−1 (total culture density). Therefore, chlorophyll-a concentration does not directly reflect overall culture productivity. Although the highest chlorophyll-a content was detected under 1 h UV-C exposure, biomass productivity was significantly enhanced across all UV-C treatments (0.5–3 h), with the greatest biomass recorded at 3 h.

This apparent decoupling between pigment concentration and biomass accumulation indicates stress-induced metabolic reprogramming rather than a simple stimulation of photosynthetic pigment synthesis. Under UV-C stress, carbon flux and metabolic energy are likely redirected from growth toward the synthesis of protective and storage compounds that support cellular repair and redox balance40. Consistent with this interpretation, lipid accumulation peaked at 1 h of UV-C exposure, and protein content was significantly elevated across 0.5–3 h treatments compared with the control. The enhanced synthesis of storage and structural macromolecules directly contributes to dry weight accumulation and may explain the higher biomass observed at 3 h despite comparable chlorophyll-a levels. Collectively, these findings suggest that biomass enhancement under UV-C irradiation arises from the combined effects of sustained cell proliferation and coordinated metabolic adjustments, rather than from increased chlorophyll concentration per unit biomass alone.

Species-specific patterns were also apparent. S. dimorphus showed a more pronounced carotenoid response than D. quadricauda, despite maintaining lower biomass. This observation aligns with reports that the magnitude of pigment induction under acute stress varies among species. Examples include enhanced pigment synthesis in D. quadricauda under moderate sulfacetamide stress22, maximal carotenoid accumulation in B. braunii following prolonged UV-C irradiation12, and elevated carotenoid levels in S. dimorphus under increasing salinity with limited effects on chlorophyll36.

In this study, UV-C treated cultures exhibited higher chlorophyll-a and carotenoid contents than the control, yet biomass increases remained modest. Treatments with the highest carotenoid levels did not show significantly greater biomass, supporting the interpretation that pigment enhancement represents a stress-response mechanism triggered by initial UV-C exposure rather than a stimulus for growth.

Effect of UV-C radiation on carbohydrate and protein contents

In D. quadricauda, the highest carbohydrate content at the end of the experiment (33.25 ± 0.19%) was recorded in the 1 h UV-C treatment, although differences among treatments were not statistically significant (Fig. 3A). Carbohydrate content increased during the first four days of cultivation and then gradually declined until day 14. Protein content, in contrast, responded strongly to UV-C exposure: cultures irradiated for 0.5–3 h accumulated significantly higher protein levels (24.24 ± 1.36–24.74 ± 1.39%) than the non-irradiated control (19.65 ± 1.78%) (Fig. 3B). In UV-C treated cultures, protein content rose from day 2 onward and remained stable, whereas the control showed only a slight increase (1.73%) by day 2 and remained nearly unchanged thereafter.

In S. dimorphus, carbohydrate content was unaffected by UV-C exposure, with final values ranging from 33.35 ± 0.05% to 34.44 ± 0.20% across all treatments (Fig. 3C). Protein content, however, increased in response to UV-C. The highest protein level (28.68 ± 1.34%) occurred in the 3 h treatment and was significantly greater than the 0 and 0.5 h treatments, though similar to the 1–2 h groups (Fig. 3D). Protein levels in irradiated cultures increased steadily over time, while those in the control remained relatively stable between 19.47% and 22.89%.

Overall, UV-C radiation did not alter carbohydrate accumulation in either species but clearly stimulated protein synthesis. By day 14, UV-C treated cultures of D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus contained 1.2-1.3-fold more protein than their respective controls. In D. quadricauda, protein levels did not differ significantly among the 0.5–3 h treatments, indicating that 0.5 h may be the most efficient exposure duration. For S. dimorphus, 1 h of UV-C yielded significantly higher protein content than 0.5 h and was comparable to 2–3 h, suggesting that 1 h is the optimal treatment.

Across both species, UV-C exposure increased protein content by 1.2-1.3-fold relative to the control (Fig. 3B and D), whereas biomass increased only 1.1–1.2-fold (Fig. 1). This disproportionate enhancement suggests that protein accumulation may be associated with stress-related metabolic adjustments rather than solely growth-driven biosynthesis37. UV exposure has been associated with the induction of stress-responsive and repair-related proteins involved in cellular stabilization and damage mitigation in microalgae41. In this context, the elevated total protein content observed here may reflect, at least in part, the accumulation of such stress-associated proteins rather than solely growth-related biosynthesis.

While this enhancement indicates a promising potential for functional food applications, it is important to note that the amino acid profile and protein digestibility were not evaluated in this study. Therefore, further nutritional characterization is required to fully validate its suitability for human or animal consumption.

Previous studies also report species-specific metabolic responses to UV-C. In B. braunii, UV-C exposure increased carbohydrate content but reduced protein levels compared with the control12. Conversely, S. vacuolatus exposed to UV-C showed marked increases in both carbohydrates (4.6-fold) and proteins (1.4-fold)19. Collectively, these results demonstrate that microalgal responses to UV-C radiation vary widely among species, reflecting differences in their intrinsic physiological and stress-adaptation capacities.

Effect of UV-C radiation on lipid productivity

In D. quadricauda, the highest lipid content (39.00 ± 0.34%), lipid yield (0.63 ± 0.01 g L−1), and lipid productivity (3,699 ± 104 mg L−1 d−1) were recorded in cultures exposed to 1 h of UV-C radiation. These values were significantly higher than those obtained at 0 and 0.5 h (Table 1). Compared with the non-irradiated control, lipid content, yield, and productivity increased by 1.5, 1.8, and 1.9-fold, respectively. Overall, all UV-C treatments (0.5–3 h) resulted in significantly higher biomass productivity, lipid content, lipid yield, and lipid productivity compared with the control.

In S. dimorphus, the maximum lipid content (39.31 ± 0.13%), lipid yield (0.50 ± 0.02 g L−1), and lipid productivity (2,695 ± 61 mg L−1 d−1) were also observed at 1 h of UV-C exposure, significantly exceeding those in the 0, 0.5, and 3 h treatments (Table 1). Relative to the control, these values represented 1.4, 1.8, and 1.9-fold increases, respectively. As with D. quadricauda, all UV-C treatments enhanced biomass productivity, lipid content, lipid yield, and lipid productivity compared with the control.

A comparison between species revealed that in D. quadricauda, lipid content did not differ significantly among the 1–3 h treatments, although all were higher than at 0.5 h. In contrast, S. dimorphus showed an optimal response at 1 h, with lipid content, yield, and productivity decreasing at longer exposures. Thus, 1 h of UV-C irradiation appears to be the most effective condition for maximizing lipid production in S. dimorphus.

When both species were compared under 1 h of UV-C exposure, D. quadricauda exhibited a stronger lipid-inducing response, with lipid content 1.5-fold greater than the control, compared with a 1.4-fold increase in S. dimorphus. In addition, lipid yield was substantially higher in D. quadricauda. These findings suggest that D. quadricauda is more suitable than S. dimorphus for lipid-oriented applications.

This study showed that UV-C irradiation enhanced lipid accumulation in both D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus, consistent with previous reports of UV-C induced lipid enhancement in microalgae. For instance, lipid content increased by 37% in Desmodesmus sp. exposed to UV-C20, while UV-C mutagenized D. armatus and Scenedesmus sp. exhibited markedly higher lipid yields than their wild types17,18. The lipid contents observed in this study were also comparable to those reported for Desmodesmus sp. RUC-2 (26.5–39%)6.

UV-C treated D. quadricauda showed higher lipid yield than Scenedesmus quadricauda reported previously (0.026–0.044 g L−1)34. Likewise, UV-C treated S. dimorphus exceeded earlier records for this species, including the 17.83–19.6%36 and 14.3–24.7% ranges8. The lipid contents also surpassed those of Desmodesmus sp., Scenedesmus sp., Oocystis pusilla, Chlorococcus infusionum, Chlorella vulgaris, Scenedesmus obliquus, and S. quadricauda reported in various studies4,33,42–44.

Effect of UV-C radiation on beneficial fatty acid profiles

The fatty acid composition of both microalgal species was substantially altered by UV-C exposure (Tables 2 and 3). Beneficial fatty acids, including oleic acid (C18:1n9c) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, C22:6n3), were detected in the irradiated cultures of both species. DHA is essential for neural development, cognitive function, and cardiovascular protection, whereas C18:1n9c supports cholesterol regulation and anti-inflammatory responses. In D. quadricauda, C18:1n9c accounted for 21.07 ± 0.20% to 23.59 ± 0.08% of total fatty acids under UV-C exposure, representing a 2.2-2.5-fold increase over the control. DHA also increased by approximately 2.0-2.8-fold (1.74 ± 0.10% to 2.50 ± 0.03%), with a progressive rise at longer irradiation times, indicating that UV-C stimulates the biosynthesis of high-value fatty acids in this species.

Scenedesmus dimorphus showed a similar response in C18:1n9c, which increased to 25.84 ± 0.29–29.79 ± 0.33% compared with 14.64 ± 0.41% in the control (1.8-2.1-fold increase). DHA, which was not detected in untreated cultures, was observed only in UV-C treated groups (0.5–2 h). The highest DHA level (7.44 ± 0.28%) occurred after 1 h of exposure, whereas longer exposure (2–3 h) markedly reduced or eliminated DHA. These findings indicate that moderate UV-C exposure is associated with enhanced DHA accumulation in S. dimorphus under the tested conditions.

Besides beneficial fatty acids, D. quadricauda also contained linoelaidic acid (C18:2n6t), a trans fatty acid linked to adverse health effects such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and obesity. The control cultures contained 2.83 ± 0.16% C18:2n6t, whereas UV-C exposure reduced it drastically to 0.47 ± 0.01–0.76 ± 0.05%. This indicates that UV-C treatment can improve the lipid quality of D. quadricauda by lowering harmful trans fats. While trans fats can extend product stability due to their resistance to oxidation, their negative health impacts outweigh any technological advantages. Notably, C18:2n6t was not detected in S. dimorphus.

Saturated fatty acids (SFAs) decreased in both species following irradiation. In D. quadricauda, SFAs declined from 81.50 ± 0.27% to 61.97 ± 0.31–65.76 ± 0.36%, and in S. dimorphus from 70.87 ± 0.08% to 56.24 ± 0.25–58.76 ± 0.28%. Although SFAs contribute to membrane structure and serve as energy reserves, excessive levels are nutritionally undesirable because they raise LDL cholesterol; thus, UV-C mediated reductions represent a beneficial shift.

With respect to unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs), D. quadricauda showed a marked increase in monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs; 31.28 ± 0.32–35.43 ± 0.29%) compared with the control (14.79 ± 0.15%), while polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) decreased slightly (3.72 ± 0.13% to 2.38 ± 0.03–2.97 ± 0.04%). In S. dimorphus, both MUFAs (36.32 ± 0.17–41.35 ± 0.28%) and PUFAs (1.92 ± 0.78–7.44 ± 0.29%) increased; PUFAs were detected only in UV-C treated cultures (0.5–2 h) and were absent in controls. MUFAs and PUFAs are widely associated with cardiovascular and metabolic health, although their high susceptibility to oxidation may influence product stability.

Overall, UV-C irradiation enhanced desirable fatty acids-particularly oleic acid and DHA-while reducing harmful or less desirable components such as certain SFAs and trans fats. These alterations enhance the nutritional quality of both microalgae for potential food or nutraceutical applications.

Comparisons with previous studies highlight the uniqueness of these responses. Earlier reports indicate that S. dimorphus typically lacks DHA8,35,36. The exclusive detection of DHA in UV-C-treated cultures strongly indicates a stress-mediated shift in lipid metabolic regulation in S. dimorphus. One plausible explanation is that UV-C exposure modulates the expression or catalytic activity of key enzymes involved in long-chain omega-3 PUFA biosynthesis, thereby enhancing metabolic flux from C18 precursors toward DHA. In microalgae, the sequential actions of front-end desaturases (Δ6 and Δ5) and elongases are central to the conversion of linoleic and α-linolenic acid derivatives into long-chain PUFAs, including DHA, as comprehensively described in omega-3 biosynthetic pathway analyses45. Alternatively, the observed DHA accumulation may reflect stress-induced membrane lipid remodeling. Environmental stressors are known to trigger adjustments in fatty acid composition to preserve membrane integrity, optimize fluidity, and counteract oxidative damage associated with ROS46,47. Under such conditions, enrichment of highly unsaturated fatty acids such as DHA can contribute to maintaining membrane functionality while simultaneously participating in cellular redox homeostasis.

However, the transient pattern observed-where DHA levels peak under moderate UV-C exposure and decline with prolonged stress-suggests a dose-dependent stress response rather than stable metabolic reprogramming. Because transcriptomic regulation and lipid class-specific partitioning of fatty acids (e.g., between neutral storage lipids and membrane lipids) were not evaluated in this study, it remains uncertain whether DHA induction reflects sustained activation of the omega-3 biosynthetic pathway or a transient stress-associated remodeling process. Future studies integrating transcriptomic profiling with comprehensive lipidomic analyses will be necessary to resolve these regulatory mechanisms.

Biodiesel properties of algae exposed to UV-C radiation

Biodiesel properties are influenced significantly by the fatty acid comprising its composition. In D. quadricauda, the control cultures were dominated by palmitic acid (C16:0, 42.35 ± 0.18%) and myristic acid (C14:0, 11.23 ± 0.25%). Following UV-C exposure, C16:0 remained the predominant fatty acid (34.13 ± 0.37–38.77 ± 0.28%), but C18:1n9c increased markedly compared with the control (Table 2). Several long-chain fatty acids, including C16:1, C18:0, C20:0, C22:0, C22:6n3, and C24:0, were also elevated in UV-C treated cultures, indicating that irradiation promoted elongation toward longer-chain fatty acids (C16:1-C24:0), while reducing the proportion of short- to medium-chain fatty acids (C6:0-C16:0). In S. dimorphus, C16:0 (33.56 ± 0.33–38.80 ± 0.47%) was consistently the most abundant fatty acid across all treatments, followed by C18:1n9c. UV-C exposure slightly reduced C16:0, arachidic acid (C20:0), and eicosenoic acid (C20:1), but significantly increased stearic acid (C18:0) and C18:1n9c compared with the control (Table 3).

In this study, C16:0 was the predominant fatty acid in both D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus following UV-C exposure, consistent with previous reports identifying C16:0 as the major component in S. dimorphus8,36, Desmodesmus sp. and S. obliquus44, and UV-C irradiated B. braunii12. However, some strains exhibit alternative dominant profiles, such as C18:4 in S. dimorphus35 or C18:1 in UV-C treated D. armatus and Scenedesmus strains17,18. Other species within these genera frequently show mixed dominance of C18:3n3 and C18:140 or combinations of C16:0, C18:1, and C18:36, with recent studies reporting either C18:13 or C16:015,48 as principal fatty acids.

UV-C exposure substantially improved the biodiesel-relevant fatty acid profiles of both microalgae. In D. quadricauda, C16-C18 fatty acids increased to 78.89 ± 0.30-83.89 ± 0.21%, compared with 69.39 ± 0.08% in the control, while S. dimorphus rose from 58.89 ± 0.47% to 68.73 ± 0.36–70.92 ± 0.33%. UV-C irradiation also enriched C10:0-C18:1 fatty acids-important contributors to higher cetane values and compliance with EN 14,21449 and ASTM D675150 standards-in S. dimorphus, yielding 79.35 ± 0.16–86.87 ± 0.25% relative to 73.99 ± 0.23% in the untreated cultures, although these components remained unchanged in D. quadricauda.

According to the EN 14,214 biodiesel standard, linolenic acid (C18:3) must not exceed 12%49,51, a criterion fully met in this study as C18:3 was undetectable in both D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus across all treatments. In contrast, the standard also limits PUFAs with four or more double bonds to below 1%, a requirement not satisfied by the UV-C irradiated cultures due to elevated levels of DHA; C22:6n3. Although the presence of DHA can negatively affect biodiesel stability, its accumulation is noteworthy given its high nutritional and commercial value. Current bioprocessing approaches allow efficient recovery of omega-3 fatty acids from biodiesel production streams, enabling their use in dietary supplements and other nutraceutical applications, which yield substantially higher economic returns than biodiesel52.

Across all treatments, SFAs remained more abundant than UFAs; however, UV-C irradiation consistently reduced SFA content relative to non-irradiated controls (Tables 2 and 3). Because high SFA levels are associated with poor cold-flow performance due to crystallization at low temperatures53, the observed reduction indicates that UV-C treatment may enhance biodiesel cold-flow properties. These results align with previous findings in B. braunii KMITL2, where UV-C exposure similarly lowered SFA levels12.

The estimated biodiesel properties of D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus exposed to UV-C radiation are presented in Table 4. The saponification value (SV), which reflects the average molecular weight of fatty acids, generally decreases as fatty acid chain length increases. According to ASTM specifications50, the SV of biodiesel should not exceed 202 mg KOH g−1. In this study, D. quadricauda exposed to 0.5–1 h of UV-C radiation and S. dimorphus exposed to 0.5–2 h of UV-C radiation complied with this standard. The SV values of UV-C treated D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus ranged from 201.40 ± 0.23 to 203.62 ± 0.52 mg KOH g−1 and 197.74 ± 0.45 to 205.04 ± 0.05 mg KOH g−1, respectively, both of which were significantly lower than those of non-irradiated cultures. These findings indicate that UV-C exposure can effectively reduce SV, suggesting a potential improvement in biodiesel quality through the production of longer-chain fatty acids. The reductions in SV observed in this study align with previous findings for B. braunii, where extended UV-C exposure similarly decreased SV values12, while Scenedesmus sp. exhibited no significant SV change relative to non-irradiated controls18. The SV values measured for UV-C treated D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus were comparable to those reported for B. braunii and Scenedesmus sp. in earlier studies, but remained lower than the values documented for Anabaena sphaerica1 and Scenedesmus sp.15 (Table 4).

The iodine values (IV) of both D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus increased significantly following UV-C exposure compared with non-irradiated cultures. IV reflects the overall degree of unsaturation in biodiesel and is directly related to its susceptibility to oxidative degradation. Biodiesel with lower IV values generally exhibits better oxidative stability. Although UV-C radiation led to higher IV values in this study, all treatments remained within the European specification49, which limits IV to a maximum of 120 g I2 100 g−1. The IV values obtained for UV-C exposed D. quadricauda (37.57 ± 1.22–41.17 ± 0.54 g I2 100 g−1) and S. dimorphus (34.47 ± 0.25–63.47 ± 1.21 g I2 100 g−1) were lower than those reported for Scenedesmus sp.15,18, and Pseudochlorella pringsheimii54.

The cetane number (CN), a key indicator of ignition quality and combustion performance of biodiesel, also varied with UV-C treatment. A minimum CN of 47–51 is required by international standards49,50. UV-C exposed D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus exhibited CN values ranging from 62.89 ± 0.14 to 63.82 ± 0.31 and 57.55 ± 0.30 to 64.13 ± 0.06, respectively, all of which exceeded the required threshold. Despite this, CN values were significantly reduced in UV-C treated samples relative to untreated cultures. The CN values observed in this study were higher than those previously reported for Scenedesmus sp.15,18,55, S. obliquus3, C. vulgaris56, P. pringsheimii54, and O. pusilla33.

Degree of unsaturation (DU) is another parameter of importance because it influences both oxidative stability and cold flow behavior. Higher DU typically enhances cold flow properties but compromises long-term storage stability. UV-C exposure increased the DU of both species, with D. quadricauda ranging from 37.13 ± 1.18 to 40.63 ± 0.36% and S. dimorphus from 41.24 ± 0.28 to 51.21 ± 0.51%, significantly higher than non-exposed cultures (22.22 ± 0.40% and 29.13 ± 0.09%, respectively). Although UV-C treatment reduce long-term stability of biodiesel, the DU values remained lower than those documented for Scenedesmus sp. (wild type and UV-C treated)18, Scenedesmus sp. and Nannochloropsis sp.15, and P. pringsheimii54.

Low-temperature biodiesel performance, commonly assessed by long-chain saturated factor (LCSF) and cold-filter plugging point (CFPP)57, responded differently to UV-C irradiation in the two algal species examined. In D. quadricauda, UV-C exposure increased LCSF from 11.26 ± 0.22% in the control to 17.38 ± 0.35–17.96 ± 0.09%, and raised CFPP from 18.89 ± 0.69 °C to 38.12 ± 1.09–39.95 ± 0.29 °C. These elevated values correspond with higher proportions of long-chain saturated fatty acids (C18:0, C20:0, C22:0, C24:0) (Table 2). In contrast, S. dimorphus displayed reduced LCSF (16.12 ± 0.23% to 7.82 ± 0.10-11.93 ± 0.33%) and lower CFPP (34.15 ± 0.73 °C to 8.10 ± 0.31–20.99 ± 1.04 °C) following UV-C treatment, consistent with decreased C20:0 and the absence of C24:0. Although all LCSF and CFPP values measured in this study were lower than those previously reported for P. pringsheimii54, the CFPP values of all treatments, which exceeded 8 °C, exceed the EN 14,214 recommended range (≤ 5 to ≥-20 °C), implying a high risk of filter plugging under cold conditions. Consequently, while UV-C exposure may improve the cold-flow properties of biodiesel derived from S. dimorphus, its application in both algae will require further optimization, potentially involving modification of fatty acid profiles or other established CFPP-improvement strategies58.

Cloud point (CP), the temperature at which wax crystals first become visible, also influences biodiesel performance under low-temperature conditions. In this study, the CP values of UV-C treated D. quadricauda (12.96 ± 0.20–15.40 ± 0.15 °C) and S. dimorphus (12.66 ± 0.31–13.56 ± 0.25 °C) were significantly lower than those of the untreated cultures. Although the CP values of all treatments exceeded the ASTM specification for biodiesel (− 15 to 10 °C), the data indicate that UV-C exposure, particularly at 2 h, effectively reduced CP in both species. The pour point (PP), defined as the lowest temperature at which biodiesel remains flowable, also improved following UV-C treatment. All PP values fell within the ASTM requirement (− 3 to 12 °C), with UV-C treated D. quadricauda (7.25 ± 0.21–9.90 ± 0.16 °C) and S. dimorphus (6.92 ± 0.33–7.90 ± 0.27 °C) exhibiting significantly lower PP than the corresponding controls, demonstrating that UV-C irradiation enhanced both CP and PP properties.

Oxidation stability (OS) reflects the resistance of biodiesel to oxidative degradation during storage. In this study, untreated D. quadricauda (6.92 ± 0.02 h) and S. dimorphus (6.65 ± 0.00 h) exhibited significantly higher OS than the UV-C treated samples, indicating that UV-C exposure reduced oxidative stability. Nevertheless, most treatments still met the EN 14,214 requirement of ≥ 6 h, with the exception of S. dimorphus exposed for 0.5 h (5.88 ± 0.04 h) and 1 h (5.80 ± 0.02 h). Kinematic viscosity (ν), which influences fuel atomization and combustion efficiency, also responded differently to UV-C irradiation in the two species. In D. quadricauda, viscosity increased significantly following UV-C treatment (5.10 ± 0.02–5.19 ± 0.00 mm² s−1), whereas S. dimorphus showed the opposite trend (5.10 ± 0.01–5.06 ± 0.01 mm² s−1). Despite these variations, all viscosity values remained within the ASTM specification of 1.9–6.0 mm² s−1.

Specific gravity (ρ), which relates to fuel composition and energy content, ranged from 0.87 ± 0.01 to 0.88 ± 0.01 g cm−3 for D. quadricauda and 0.88 ± 0.00 to 0.89 ± 0.00 g cm−3 for S. dimorphus. These values meet the EN 14,214 recommended range (0.86–0.90 g cm−3). Biodiesel density (D), directly related to volumetric energy content, ranged from 873 ± 0.24 to 878 ± 0.37 kg m−3 in D. quadricauda and 884 ± 0.36 to 889 ± 0.26 kg m−3 in S. dimorphus. All values complied with the ASTM biodiesel standard of 870–900 kg m−3.

When comparing the biodiesel characteristics of D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus with commonly used vegetable oils such as palm, olive, peanut, rapeseed, soybean, sunflower, grape, high-oleic sunflower, almond, and corn oils, notable differences were observed. The DU values of both algal species were lower than those reported for these vegetable oils (64.2–157.8), whereas their CFPP values were comparatively higher (− 12 to 10 °C for vegetable oils). In contrast, the cetane number (CN) of UV-C treated cultures, ranging from 57.55 ± 0.30 to 66.73 ± 0.03, generally exceeded the CN values typical of vegetable oils (48–57)31.

Overall, UV-C irradiation substantially altered the fatty acid composition and biodiesel properties of D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus, yielding both beneficial and adverse outcomes. The treatment enhanced the proportion of C16-C18 and C10:0-C18:1 fatty acids, reduced saponification values, and improved cold-flow behavior-particularly in S. dimorphus. However, increases in polyunsaturated long-chain fatty acids, reduced oxidative stability, and elevated CFPP and LCSF in D. quadricauda highlight important limitations. Although both species consistently met key ASTM and EN 14,214 specifications, the contrasting responses underscore the need to optimize UV-C exposure conditions to balance improved fuel quality with minimized negative effects. These findings demonstrate that UV-C treatment can be a useful tool for modulating algal biodiesel characteristics, while also revealing opportunities for co-production of high-value fatty acids such as DHA.

From a practical deployment perspective, the trade-off between improved cold-flow properties and reduced oxidative stability observed after UV-C treatment can be mitigated through several downstream strategies. Blending the resulting biodiesel with conventional petroleum diesel or biodiesels richer in saturated and monounsaturated fatty acid methyl esters may dilute highly unsaturated FAMEs and enhance oxidative stability while maintaining acceptable low-temperature performance59. The incorporation of antioxidants has also been widely reported to improve storage stability60. Alternatively, fractionation techniques may be employed to tailor the FAMEs composition and achieve improved oxidative stability or optimized cold-flow behavior61.

Conclusions

Controlled UV-C irradiation modulated nutritional and energy-related traits in D. quadricauda and S. dimorphus. A 1 h exposure enhanced biomass productivity, protein content, lipid accumulation, and altered fatty acid composition, including increased oleic acid and DHA, supporting potential functional food applications. Changes in lipid productivity and fatty acid profiles indicate biodiesel relevance; however, fuel properties were species-dependent, and cold-flow limitations requiring further optimization to meet EN 14,214 standards. Among the two species, D. quadricauda exhibited stronger growth and lipid responses, suggesting species-specific biotechnological potential. Although UV-C irradiation acts as an effective non-chemical physiological modulator under laboratory conditions, further optimization and techno-economic evaluation are required to determine scalability and practical feasibility.
