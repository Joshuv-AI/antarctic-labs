Data-Informed Intuition in Hepatology: Integrating Evidence, Context, and Clinical Reasoning
Authors: Jin Dong Kim, Mi Na Kim, Do Young Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence and data-driven models are changing hepatology, but expert clinical judgment remains essential. Liver diseases are complex and evolve over time, requiring reasoning that links numerical data with clinical context. We propose data-informed intuition (DII), a conceptual framework bridging evidence-based medicine with clinical experience and reflective reasoning. Instead of presenting a validated prediction model, it proposes DII as an integrative scaffold for clinical reasoning that can be examined and revised. DII is viewed as a trainable skill that improves with data feedback, not mere guesswork. Clinicians continually calibrate their judgment as patient information accumulates. In hepatology, DII helps interpret disease trajectories, adapt predictive scores to context, and handle uncertainty in liver transplantation, hepatocellular carcinoma, acute liver failure, and related conditions. The DII framework describes eight axes (time, rate, magnitude, pattern, context, causality, integration, and uncertainty) that make visible how data and intuition interact in clinical reasoning. Integrating DII into fellowship training and artificial intelligence-assisted systems could nurture clinicians who combine algorithmic precision with human insight. This perspective summarizes the conceptual basis, clinical uses, and educational value of DII, and outlines both a research agenda for its empirical testing and a path toward data-informed professionalism in hepatology.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Data-informed intuition, Hepatology, Clinical reasoning, Education

INTRODUCTION

Evidence-based medicine has reshaped hepatology, with prognostic tools such as the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) informing liver transplantation (LT) allocation and staging systems such as Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer guiding hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) care.1,2 Yet daily decisions still involve uncertainty, especially when trajectories, frailty, and social or system constraints are not captured by scores. Experienced hepatologists often rely on intuitive synthesis, but that skill remains hard to teach or evaluate consistently.3,4

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning can identify complex patterns, but clinical adoption remains limited and often constrained by interpretability, workflow integration, governance, and clinician literacy.5 Data and algorithms can also miss patient-specific priorities or context, which raises the risk of over-reliance.6,7

We propose data-informed intuition (DII), an explicit calibration loop in which clinicians repeatedly update their intuitive impressions using structured data, then re-check data interpretations against context and mechanism. DII is an integrative scaffold rather than a validated prediction tool. This perspective outlines the framework, illustrates how it maps to common hepatology decisions and summarizes preliminary expert feedback.

Two features distinguish DII from generic appeals to unstructured intuition. First, DII requires explicit comparison between a clinician’s expectation and the data that arrive over time, including when the expectation was wrong. Second, it encourages deliberate reflection and team feedback, so that intuitive judgments become calibratable rather than private. This focus makes DII usable as a training target and as a lens for responsible use of AI outputs.

CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK OF DII

DII describes how clinicians link analytical reasoning with experiential judgment by cycling between data and context.4,8,9 It treats intuition as a trainable skill, not a mysterious gift or a license to ignore evidence. Intuitive impressions are generated from prior cases and pattern recognition, then tested and revised using structured information and feedback.10-12

In practice, clinicians form an initial impression, compare it with evolving laboratory, imaging, and bedside findings, then recalibrate.3,4 Recalibration can mean updating the initial risk estimate, reinterpreting an outlier result, or revising the causal story that links findings together. When intuition and data conflict, DII encourages making assumptions explicit, checking whether the data are being interpreted in the right clinical frame, and adjusting reasoning transparently rather than defaulting to either gut feeling or a single score.6,7

This aligns with dual-process accounts of expert reasoning, where fast pattern recognition is monitored and refined by slower, deliberate analysis.13,14 DII does not claim a new cognitive system. It makes the calibration between rapid impressions and data-based analysis explicit and teachable.8,9

THE DII FRAMEWORK: EIGHT AXES OF CALIBRATED REASONING

The DII framework operationalizes this calibration using eight axes that can be grouped into a data-facing set (Time, Rate, Magnitude, Pattern) and a context-and-action set (Context, Causality, Integration, Uncertainty). We derived the eight axes for bedside calibration through conceptual synthesis of the clinical-reasoning and uncertainty literature and iterative author discussion to preserve bedside usability, non-redundancy, and coverage across common hepatology decisions.15 The axes are conceptually interrelated and may interact during calibration. At this stage, the framework does not propose weights or a formal structure.

POSITIONING THE DII FRAMEWORK AMONG EXISTING PARADIGMS

DII is not proposed as a new theory of cognition or as a model for AI system design. Rather, it is a clinician-facing scaffold for making calibration explicit at the bedside, that is, for showing how clinicians translate population evidence, evolving data, and contextual constraints into patient-specific judgments. In this sense, DII complements evidence-based medicine, data-driven medicine, human-in-the-loop AI, and explainable AI by focusing on the clinician’s internal reasoning loop rather than on the design or oversight of external systems.16-19 It also offers a common language for multidisciplinary discussion by making the basis of judgment more explicit and open to challenge. Unlike descriptive accounts of expertise development, such as knowledge encapsulation and illness scripts, DII does not primarily explain how expertise forms.20 Instead, it offers a compact vocabulary for articulating, challenging, and revising judgment in real time, rather than leaving it as unstructured gut feeling.

In practice, DII helps translate population evidence and algorithmic output into patient-specific decisions. This is especially useful in multidisciplinary settings, where disagreement often reflects different weighting of context, mechanism, and uncertainty rather than the raw numbers alone. DII also clarifies how outputs from explainable AI should be interpreted. Explanations may identify which variables influenced a model, but they do not determine whether those variables reflect reversible physiology, unmeasured confounding, or downstream severity.19 Using the Causality and Uncertainty axes, clinicians can ask what mechanism would make the explanation plausible, what alternatives remain, and how much confidence is justified before integrating model output. This framing encourages cautious integration of model output, rather than treating interpretability as proof of correctness.

CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS OF DII IN HEPATOLOGY

DII is most visible in gray-zone decisions where standard tools provide options, but context determines timing, sequencing, and priorities. The examples below are intended to show how the axes highlight what experienced hepatologists often notice implicitly, and how that reasoning can be made explicit for trainees.

1. LT candidate selection

Prognostic scores such as MELD stratify population risk, but they can under-represent vulnerability in an individual course. In a patient with a stable MELD but progressive sarcopenia, recurrent infections, and rising creatinine, DII emphasizes trajectory rather than a single score. The Time and Rate axes capture gradual decline, Pattern captures repeated decompensation signals, and Context captures frailty and limited support. Integration then links these to the MELD estimate, supporting earlier transplant evaluation or closer monitoring while the patient is still clinically stable.

2. Intermediate-stage HCC treatment

Intermediate-stage HCC often has multiple guideline-supported strategies. In a patient with borderline hepatic reserve and recurrent ascites, a guideline-only approach may default to repeated transarterial chemoembolization. DII makes competing risks explicit: Time and Pattern track prior decompensation and response to therapy, Magnitude tracks tumor burden and liver reserve, and Context captures feasibility of repeated visits and tolerance of complications. A DII-informed plan can still stay within guideline boundaries while favoring earlier reassessment and earlier transition to systemic therapy when reserve appears to be eroding.

3. Acute liver failure prognostication

In acute liver failure (ALF), small changes can signal large prognostic shifts. When selected markers improve but mental status, lactate, or ammonia trends worsen, DII prioritizes dynamic trends and coherence across markers (Time, Rate, Pattern), mechanism and etiology (Causality), and explicit uncertainty about possible trajectories. This can support early transplant activation despite borderline score thresholds, especially when the overall pattern suggests impending multi-organ deterioration.

Across scenarios, DII frames expertise as repeated recalibration of judgment with data feedback, consistent with cognitive accounts of expertise and pattern-based reasoning.21

FACE VALIDITY SURVEY RESULTS ON THE DII FRAMEWORK

We surveyed 20 hepatologists in Korea with at least 10 years of subspecialty experience (median 14.5 years; interquartile range, 11.8 to 17.3 years). Participants reviewed four short vignettes spanning LT prioritization, intermediate-stage HCC treatment, ALF prognostication, and portal hypertension management, then rated the eight axes on Likert scales. All axes received generally high importance ratings, with Pattern and Integration highest (Table 2). Half of respondents reported that the framework aligned well with their real-world reasoning (scores 4–5), and half supported its use in fellowship training, with the remainder neutral. Free-text feedback was limited. One respondent noted that applying all axes routinely could be time-consuming in busy clinical work, which suggests a need for pragmatic use as a prompt rather than a checklist.

EDUCATIONAL AND PROFESSIONAL IMPLICATIONS OF DII

DII is relevant to training because it makes reasoning discussable. When decisions are explained only as experience, learners cannot identify what to practice or how to improve. DII offers labels for the specific information that triggered recalibration, which can support feedback and shared language in teams.

1. Making reasoning visible and feedback-driven

Structured debriefing and cognitive apprenticeship can make the axes explicit during rounds, transplant selection meetings, and tumor boards.22,23 For example, supervisors can state which axis changed their mind (Time, Rate, Magnitude, Pattern, Context) and invite trainees to articulate their own assumptions before revealing outcomes.

2. Building data literacy and reflective awareness

Basic data literacy supports appropriate trust and appropriate skepticism in quantitative tools.10 This includes understanding what a score was designed to predict, how case mix can shift performance, and how to reason with probabilities rather than binary conclusions.21

3. Creating supportive professional culture

Professional culture influences whether recalibration is safe to perform openly. Team norms that allow uncertainty and revision to be discussed without blame can reduce hindsight bias and support learning from near-misses.24

4. Addressing potential pitfalls and cognitive bias

Because intuition is vulnerable to cognitive bias, DII treats calibration as an explicit safeguard rather than a license for subjective judgment.24 It requires clinicians to state a working hypothesis, identify what finding would prompt revision, cross-check against validated tools when available, and invite challenge through team review or second opinions. These steps can be applied during rounds, transplant meetings, tumor boards, or structured debriefings. Unlike gut feeling, DII does not leave judgment implicit: it makes assumptions visible, revisable, and accountable to outcomes. The distinction between unstructured gut feeling and DII lies not in whether clinicians use intuition, but in whether judgment is made explicit, challengeable, and revisable; these contrasts are summarized in Table 3.

LIMITATIONS OF THIS PERSPECTIVE

This perspective proposes a conceptual scaffold rather than an empirically derived model. The axes have not been tested for reliability, independence, or weighting. The expert feedback was small, self-selected, and descriptive. We did not test whether DII changes clinical outcomes or decision quality, so the framework should be viewed as a proposal and research agenda.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Next steps include mapping the axes onto structured case debriefings and testing whether clinicians can apply them with acceptable agreement, which may also indicate whether simplification is needed. Small feasibility studies could examine uptake, time burden, and perceived usefulness in routine meetings.

Vignette-based or simulation studies could compare DII-scaffolded teaching with usual instruction using outcomes such as explicitness of assumptions, handling of uncertainty, and calibration between confidence and accuracy. Longer-term work could explore whether DII changes decisions in ways that align with patient-centered goals, without claiming causal effects prematurely.

Finally, prototypes that log when clinicians follow or override algorithmic outputs, and which DII axes they cite, could support hypothesis-generating evaluation of safety and equity across settings. Such efforts will inform the human-centered design of explainable systems, a necessity further underscored by the rapid advancement of AI in hepatology, especially in the field of HCC.19,25 While these sophisticated algorithms can process vast datasets beyond human capacity, their effective clinical integration still requires an approach that addresses contextual nuances and explainability.25 The DII framework complements these technological leaps by providing clinicians with a cognitive scaffold to critically appraise and synthesize AI-generated insights within the nuanced reality of clinical practice.

CONCLUSIONS

DII frames intuition as a data-calibrated, revisable skill that links quantitative evidence with context and mechanism. In hepatology, where disease trajectories and uncertainty are central, a shared vocabulary for calibration may support both training and responsible use of AI-assisted tools. The framework is provisional, but it offers a practical way to make expert reasoning visible and open to improvement.
