 1. S UMMARY OF RESEARCH OBJECTIVES

The project supported the development of new methodologies for performing matrix com-
putations that form key building blocks in modern scientific computing, such as low rank
approximation of matrices, and efficient representations of global operators that arise in
simulations of physical phenomena.
Specifically, the proposal identified three sets of objectives:
 (1) Low rank approximation of matrices: The task of computing a low rank approx-
 imation to a matrix is omnipresent in scientific computing and in data analysis.
 The project drew on a set of recently developed randomized algorithms that are
 highly communication efficient, and often achieve superior speed on modern archi-
 tectures. A key innovation of the project was the development of a new “hybridized”
 approach to randomized dimension reduction that combines the best properties of
 several existing methodologies.
 (2) Compression of rank structured matrices: The project supported the development
 of methods for computing data sparse representations of “rank structured” matrices
 through black box randomized algorithms. Rank structured matrices arise in a broad
 range of applications, and are of central importance to the recent emergence of
 powerful scalable direct solvers for elliptic PDEs and fast algorithms for kernel
 methods in data science. The project led to the development of several new classes
 of randomized compression algorithms, including a break-through that enabled the
 first known method that is both fully black box, and has strictly linear execution
 time.
 (3) A posteriori error estimation and certificates of accuracy: A key obstacle to the
 adaptation of randomized algorithms has been user concerns about the accuracy
 and the reliability of the output. The problem becomes particularly acute for algo-
 rithms that compute approximations to matrices in environments where the residual
 cannot readily be evaluated. To address these concerns, the project supported the
 development of new “a posteriori” error estimators. These estimators rely on su-
 perbly dependable randomized techniques for estimating matrix norms that can of-
 ten provide bounds on the error that hold to probability 10−10 or better. These new
 methods are exceptionally effective, and hardly increase the computational time at
 all.
To attain these objectives, we exploited recently developed techniques that use random-
ized projections to reduce the effective dimensionality of intermediate problems, while still

maintaining high accuracy in the final answer. The algorithms resulting from the project
are more communication efficient than previously existing techniques and have proven to
execute particularly well in communication constrained environments such as GPUs and for
data stored out-of-core.
The project also advanced our theoretical understanding of a large class of algorithms that
rely on properties of random matrices.

 2. ACCOMPLISHMENTS AND RESULTS

As summarized in Section 1, the project involved three areas of investigation. In this section,
we summarize the key accomplishments in each area:

Area 1 (low rank approximation). We have developed new algorithms for computing ap-
proximate matrix factorizations such as the “CUR” and “interpolatory” factorizations. Such
factorizations rely on a “natural” basis for the row/column space where a subset of the
rows/columns of the original matrix forms a spanning set is identified. These algorithms
were inspired by the previously known method “DEIM-CUR” by Sorensen and Embree.
The novelty is that we discovered that a middle step could be cut out which greatly boosts
performance. We also incorporated a faster technique for executing a randomized embed-
ding. A remarkable finding in this part of the project is that when randomization is used, it
is possible to compute rank-revealing factorizations using LU with partial pivoting (LUPP)
[15, 5]. This is a powerful discovery since LUPP is much more communication efficient,
and therefore faster, than traditional rank-revealing factorizations such as column pivoted
QR, or methods based on Krylov subspaces.
In a related effort, we developed a new method [4] for selecting batches of pivots in rank
revealing factorizations. The new method combines the best properties of “sampling based”
techniques and “sketching+pivoting” based techniques. This method was inspired by the
“randomized Cholesky” method of [2], but introduced pivoting to reject unhelpful pivots.
We have developed an evolution of the “randomized SVD” that is suitable for implementa-
tion in optimized software packages. The new algorithm is adaptive (so that it determines
the numerical rank), relies on blocked linear algebraic primitives that exploit the very high
practical efficiency of the matrix-matrix multiplication, and is economical about how over-
sampling and power iteration is implemented. The new algorithm has the same theoretical
complexity as previously known methods, but executes much faster in practice. To be pre-
cise, these methods incrementally build a “UTV factorization”
 A=UT V∗
of a given matrix A. Here, U and U are unitary, and T is triangular (or trapezoidal). We
demonstrated that using randomized methods, it is possible to efficiently build a UTV fac-
torization that is very rank revealing, in the sense that the matrix T has almost all its mass
on the diagonal. This means that the UTV factorization is for practical purposes just as nu-
merically stable for solving ill-conditioned and inconsistent linear system problems as the
state-of-the-art SVD. Early versions of these methods were developed in collaboration with
PI Martinsson’s doctoral student Nathan Heavner. As part of the work supported by the
present award, the PI further refined and developed these methods in collaboration with a
team at Universidad Jaume I de Castelón in Spain. Four papers have appeared [7, 8, 9, 13],
and a fifth is in the pipeline [3].

Area 2 (compression of “rank structured” matrices). The project produced major advances
in this field. Most notably, it supported the development of an algorithm [10] that constructs
highly accurate representations of rank-structured matrices using only input-output pairs
from vectors sampled from specific probability distributions. This approach represents the
first known method that is genuinely black box while maintaining true linear complexity.
The technique has demonstrated strong performance on practical problems and is already
having a significant impact.

A second noteworthy finding involved the development of a randomized algorithm [17] that
simultaneously compresses and factorizes a category of rank-structured matrices. This al-
gorithm appears to be the first computationally efficient method for an important class of
rank-structured matrices capable of addressing realistic three-dimensional problems. The
approach integrates compression and factorization processes, which streamlines the algo-
rithm and substantially reduces memory requirements.

To be slightly more precise it relies on the hierarchical matrix (H2 -matrix) formalism that
provides a way to reinterpret the Fast Multipole Method and related fast summation schemes
in linear algebraic terms. The idea is to tessellate a matrix into blocks in such as way that
each block is either small or of numerically low rank; this enables the storage of the ma-
trix and the application of it to a vector in linear or close to linear complexity. A key
motivation for the reformulation is to extend the range of dense matrices that can be repre-
sented. Additionally, H2 -matrices in principle also extend the range of operations that can
be executed to include matrix inversion and factorization. While such algorithms can be
highly efficient for certain specialized formats (such as HBS/HSS matrices based on “weak
admissibility”), inversion algorithms for general H2 -matrices tend to be based on nested
recursions and recompressions, making them challenging to implement efficiently. An ex-
ception is the “strong recursive skeletonization (SRS)” algorithm by Minden, Ho, Damle,
and Ying, which involves a simpler algorithmic flow. However, SRS greatly increases the
number of blocks of the matrix that need to be stored explicitly, leading to high memory
requirements. Our new algorithm “randomized strong recursive skeletonization (RSRS)” is
a reformulation of SRS that incorporates the randomized SVD (RSVD) to simultaneously
compress and factorize an H2 -matrix. RSRS is a “black box” algorithm that interacts with
the matrix to be compressed only via its action on vectors; this extends the range of the SRS
algorithm (which relied on the ”proxy source” compression technique) to include dense
matrices that arise in sparse direct solvers. Moreover, RSRS leads to dramatically simpler
data structures, faster execution time, and easier parallelization. RSRS is particularly ef-
fective when applied to geometries that have lower dimensionality than the ambient space
(as when discretizing a boundary integral equation on a surface in R3 ), as it enables off-
diagonal blocks to be compressed using their actual numerical ranks, rather than the rank
imputed by a proxy surface.

Beside the two major discoveries described, significant progress was also made in other
areas of matrix compression [11], as well as on methods for inverting and manipulating the
matrices once compressed [12, 1].

Although it was not part of the intended objectives, the project also led to the development
of a highly simplified version of the Fast Multipole Method [16]. The new method exploits
randomization in order to greatly reduce the complexity of the underlying data structures.

Tentative results indicate that this leads to dramatic simplification and acceleration. We ex-
pect this new technique to be particularly well suited for parallelization on large distributed
memory systems.

Area 3 (a posteriori error estimators). Randomized methods in linear algebra have proven
capable of attaining exceptional execution speed, and have rendered many previous inacces-
sible problems tractable. However, any method of this nature must necessarily come with a
failure probability. These probabilities are typically tiny, but they are nonzero, and could in
principle get larger than expected if the data to be processed exhibits anomalous behavior,
if “fast” Johnson-Lindenstrauss projections are used, or if aggressive randomized sampling
is deployed. To regain complete reliability in such situations, the project supported the de-
velopment of highly trustworthy “a posteriori error estimators” that assess the accuracy of
a particular instantiation of a randomized algorithm.
The most significant result in this area was the development of a very computationally
efficient method to compute a “certificates of accuracy” that certifies the accuracy of any
algorithm for computing an approximation to a matrix. The idea of such certificates was
described in [14, Sec. 4], and is in its basic form very simple: Suppose that A is some
given matrix, that we have by some means computed an approximation Aapprox , that we
can rapidly apply both A and Aapprox to vectors, and that we seek to estimate the error
e = ∥A − Aapprox ∥. Then draw a tall thin Gaussian random matrix Ω, with say p columns.
The quantity eest = √1p ∥ A − Aapprox Ω∥ will then be a probabilistic estimate of e in
the sense that E[e2est ] = e2 , when the Frobenius norm is used. The estimate e is itself of
course a random variable. But the kicker is that the likelihood of a large deviation decreases
very rapidly as the number of samples p increases. For instance, if we set p = 10, then
a fairly tight upper bound on the error can be proven to hold with probability no less than
1 − 10−10 . As a part of the project, Yuji  Nakatsukasa of Oxford University and the PI
showed that the quantity ∥ A − Aapprox Ω∥ can be estimated accurately and efficiently
through a “sketched” least squares problem. This reduced the cost to the point where the
computation of the certificate of accuracy becomes essentially free, compared to the other
parts of the algorithm.
A second result concerns the development of new methods to estimate the accuracy of the
singular vectors that are computed by the randomized SVD (RSVD). We recall that the
RSVD is a by now well established algorithm for computing a rank-k approximation A′ to
a given m × n input matrix A. The approximation takes the form A′ = U D V ∗ , where U
and V are matrices with k columns each that form approximate orthonormal bases of the
row and column spaces of A, and D is a k × k diagonal matrix that holds approximations
to the k leading singular values of A. The existing literature provides detailed and sharp
bounds on the approximation error ∥A − A′ ∥. Our new analysis [6] clarifies how well the
columns of U and V approximate the corresponding left and right singular vectors of A
(or, to be precise, how close the subspaces spanned by the respective sets of vectors are).
We built upon earlier work by Arvind Saibaba, and derived sharper bounds by exploiting
information about A that is revealed by the algorithm.

 3. T RAINING

The project has provided direct and indirect support for training multiple exceptional early-
career researchers, with five obtaining tenure-track appointments at research universities.

3.1. Postdoctoral researchers.

 (1) Chao Chen participated in the project between 2021 and 2023. CC works on ran-
 domized algorithms for compressing rank structured matrices, and on GPU acceler-
 ation of randomized algorithms for computing full factorizations of dense matrices.
 CC currently holds a tenure track position at North Carolina State University.
 (2) Ke Chen participated in the project between 2021 and 2022. He started working
 on constructing fast solvers for linear systems whose coefficient matrix is a graph
 Laplacian. The idea was to look for accelerations to techniques developed by Dan
 Spielman and Rasmus Kyng. This effort was not successful. KC then transitioned
 to work on high order solvers for parabolic equations related to fluids modeling,
 and to the development of a novel solver for the radiative transfer equation. For
 both projects, randomized methods for low rank approximation are essential for
 attaining high practical speed in the solvers. KC has also worked on developing fast
 algorithms for solving parabolic equations, and has started to apply these solvers
 to various fluid flow problems. KC is currently a researcher at the University of
 Maryland.
 (3) Kate Pearce joined the project in 2022. She works on randomized algorithms for
 compressing rank structured matrices with strong admissibility, and randomized
 algorithms for computing CUR and interpolatory decompositions. She earned an
 NSF Ascend postdoctoral fellowship in 2024, and will continue to work on further-
 ing the project objectives over the next two years.
 (4) Heather Wilber participated in the project between 2021 and 2023. She worked
 on randomized algorithms for compressing matrices in the “butterfly” format, for
 extending certain rank structured algorithms to the task of solving least squares
 problems, and on efficient algorithms for solving time domain scattering problems.
 HW currently holds a tenure track position at the University of Washington.
 (5) Bowei Wu participated in the project between 2021 and 2022. He has developed
 a new class of quadrature rules for discretizing boundary integral operators from
 potential theory, with applications to acoustic and electromagnetic scattering, fluid
 flows, etc. He is currently working on developing fast direct solvers for large scale
 3D problems, drawing on randomized algorithms for compressing rank structured
 matrices. Current efforts focus on solvers for toroidal geometries with applications
 to tokamaks, and to a “rough surface scattering” problem where we investigate in-
 teractions between a number of obstacles on a flat reflecting surface. BW currently
 holds a tenure track position at the University of Massachussetts at Lowell.

3.2. Doctoral students.

 (1) Yunhui Cai is a doctoral student in the mathematics program at UT-Austin. Her
 research concerns numerical methods for integral equations modeling acoustic and
 electromagnetic scattering, exploring how the methods developed in the current
 project perform in realistic problem settings. She is expected to graduate in 2026.
 (2) Yijun Dong graduated from the Science, Engineering, and Mathematic program at
 UT-Austin in August 2023. She is working on analyzing randomized algorithms for
 computing so called CUR factorizations of matrices. She has also in recent months
 started working on fast algorithms for compressing rank structured matrices. YD is
 currently a Courant Instructor at NYU.

 (3) Joseph Kump is a doctoral student in the CSEM program at the University of Texas
 at Austin. He works on incorporating randomized algorithms into direct solvers for
 elliptic PDEs in three dimensions. He is expected to graduate in 2026.
 (4) James Levitt graduated from the Science, Engineering, and Mathematic program at
 UT-Austin in 2022. He developed two new algorithms for computing data sparse
 representations of rank structured matrices. JL currently runs a company developing
 algorithms for commodities trading.
 (5) Anna Yesypenko was a student in the Computational Science, Engineering, and
 Mathematics program at UT-Austin who graduated in 2024. She is working on
 high order accurate direct solver for acoustic scattering problems in two and three
 dimensions. These solvers rely on randomized methods for compressing low-rank
 structured in discretized integral operators. AY has secured a position as a tenure-
 track Assistant Professor in Mathematics at Ohio State University.
