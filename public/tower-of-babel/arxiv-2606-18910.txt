REVES: REvision and VErification–Augmented Training for Test-Time Scaling
Yuanxin Liu Ruida Zhou Xinyan Zhao Amr Sharaf Hongzhou Lin, Arijit Biswas Mohammad Ghavamzadeh Zhaoran Wang Mingyi Hong, [5pt] Northwestern University Amazon AGI Qualcomm AI Research University of Minnesota

Abstract
Test-time scaling via sequential revision has emerged as a powerful paradigm for enhancing Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning. However, standard post-training methods primarily optimize single-shot objectives, creating a fundamental misalignment with multi-step inference dynamics. While recent work treats this as multi-turn reinforcement learning (RL), conventional approaches optimize over the multi-step trajectories directly, failing to further exploit the high-quality mistakes in intermediate steps that model can learn from correcting them. We propose a two-stage iterative framework that alternates between online data/prompt augmentation and policy optimization. By converting the intermediate steps (“near-miss” answers) in the successful recovery trajectories into decoupled revision and verification prompts, our approach concentrates training on both effective answer transformation and error identification. This approach enables efficient off-policy data generation and reduces the computational overhead of long-horizon sampling compared to standard multi-turn RL. On LiveCodeBench, using publicly available test cases as feedback, we observe gains of +6.5 points over the RL baseline and +4.0 points over standard multi-turn training. Beyond coding, our approach matches the previously reported SOTA result on circle packing while using the smallest base model (4B) and far fewer rollouts than the much larger evolutionary search systems. Math results under ground-truth verification further confirm improved correction ability. It also generalizes to out-of-distribution constraint-satisfaction puzzles such as n_queens and mini_sudoku, where correctness is defined entirely by problem constraints. Code is available at https://github.com/yxliu02/REVES.git .

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) deployed in challenging settings rarely produce a correct answer on the first attempt; real-world workflows routinely loop back with feedback. A growing family of test-time scaling (TTS) algorithms formalizes this pattern, including sequential revision
(
Madaan et al. 2023
;
Shinn et al. 2023
)
, tree search
(
Inoue et al. 2025
)
, evolutionary refinement
(
Lee et al. 2025b
)
, and best-of-
N
with a verifier
(
Chow et al. 2025
)
. They all share a single primitive: given a problem, a previous attempt, and feedback, produce an improved response.

This raises a basic question:
can we design a post-training algorithm that explicitly improves a model’s test-time sequential revision capability?
We focus on sequential revision (SR) because gains on SR transfer to the rest of the family: every revision-using TTS algorithm eventually calls the policy on revise-from-prior-attempt inputs. SR is also a strong TTS algorithm in its own right, matching or surpassing tree search and evolutionary refinement on planning tasks (Figure
2
). Theorem
3.1
shows that increasing the policy’s one-step revision value on SR-induced inputs also raises
J_{\phi}
for every revision-using TTS algorithm that covers SR’s revision inputs, up to small drift terms.

How, then, do we post-train an LLM to optimize the SR objective
J_{{\phi_{\mathrm{SR}}}}
? Standard post-training, RLHF
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
, RLVR, and GRPO
(
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
)
, optimizes a single-shot expected reward, which is fundamentally misaligned with the multi-step nature of test-time deployment. The natural fix is multi-turn RL on revision rollouts, but trajectory-level credit broadcasts assign path-dependent credit. Take a wrong, wrong, correct rollout: every per-turn log-probability gradient receives the same positive credit, including the wrong intermediates. Per rollout this credit is biased, and only cancels in expectation across many rollouts.

We propose REVES, a two-stage iterative framework that targets the SR objective
J_{{\phi_{\mathrm{SR}}}}
directly through its per-state structure. We show that
J_{{\phi_{\mathrm{SR}}}}
decomposes exactly into a weighted sum of per-state one-step recovery probabilities along visited rollouts (Lemma
4.1
), exposing single-state, single-turn gradients with no horizon credit assignment. REVES is an offline realization of this per-state training signal: each epoch, it runs SR rollouts under the current policy, retains those that succeed within budget, converts the intermediate (“near-miss”) answers into decoupled
revision
and
verification
prompts, and trains with standard single-turn RL on the augmented prompt set; the next epoch refreshes the augmentation. This concentrates training on per-state recovery and avoids both the bias of trajectory broadcasts and the cost of long-horizon online sampling.

Empirically, on LiveCodeBench with public test cases as feedback, REVES achieves
+6.5
points over the single-shot RL baseline and
+4.0
points over standard multi-turn training. On the circle packing benchmark, a Qwen3-4B base trained with REVES matches the previously reported best results from much larger evolutionary search systems built on Gemini-2.0 Pro/Flash and Qwen3-8B. Math benchmarks (MATH500, AIME24/25) show consistent improvements under both ground-truth and self-confidence stopping, and the trained policies generalize to out-of-distribution constraint-satisfaction puzzles (n_queens, mini_sudoku). Beyond standalone SR, REVES improves every revision-using TTS algorithm we tested (MCTS, AB-MCTS variants, Mind Evolution), in line with the conditional bound in Theorem
3.1
.

Our contributions are threefold:

1.

Conceptual.
We formulate test-time scaling as a meta-RL problem and show that improving sequential revision also improves any revision-using TTS algorithm that covers SR’s revision inputs (Theorem
3.1
).

2.

Methodological.
We exactly decompose
J_{{\phi_{\mathrm{SR}}}}
into per-state one-step recovery probabilities (Lemma
4.1
) and propose REVES, a two-stage framework that recasts intermediate near-miss answers as decoupled revision and verification prompts trained with single-turn RL, avoiding the path-dependent credit assignment of multi-turn RL.

3.

Empirical.
REVES delivers consistent test-time gains across math, coding, out-of-distribution puzzles, and circle packing, and improves every revision-using TTS algorithm we evaluated.

Related Work

Test-time-aware post-training.

A growing line of work shows the importance of aligning the post-training objective with the intended test-time inference strategy. For parallel-sampling families, this includes optimizing pass@

revision-using
algorithms whose calls take as input a previous attempt together with its feedback. Aligning training with this inference family therefore requires a different objective.

Training sequential revision capability.

Closer to our setting, several prior methods explicitly train the policy to revise, including supervised and preference-based approaches
(
Qu et al. 2024
;
Xiong et al. 2025
)
, RL-based approaches
(
Kumar et al. 2024
;

;

;

;
Li et al. 2025a
;
Zhang et al. 2026
)
. Our work shares the broader goal of strengthening the model’s revision capability, but differs in framing: we primarily focus on how to align training with the test-time objective of a revision-using inference strategy.

Weakness-driven data synthesis and guided exploration.

SwS
(
Liang et al. 2025
)
synthesizes new problems targeting the model’s weaknesses using strong external teacher models, and POPE
(

This raises an important question:
does there exist a training target

Optimizing

is not optimizing single-shot pass@1.

A natural question is whether standard single-shot RLVR training already addresses

Theorem 3.2

(Objective mismatch)
.

For any revision length

The implication is direct:

Takeaway: we adopt the sequential-revision objective
J_{{\phi_{\mathrm{SR}}}}
as our training target.

Improving test-time performance is not the same as optimizing single-shot pass@1 (Theorem
3.2
). Among test-time scaling algorithms, sequential revision is both empirically strong and the one whose gains transfer to every other revision-using algorithm (Theorem
3.1
), which makes it the right objective to optimize.

REVES: From Path-Level Credit to Recovery-Level Supervision

Section
3
established that improving
J_{{\phi_{\mathrm{SR}}}}
is our focus. This section turns that target into an algorithm. We start by writing
J_{{\phi_{\mathrm{SR}}}}
exactly as a sum of per-state one-step recovery probabilities along visited trajectories; this hazard decomposition makes the natural training signal explicit and reads off the algorithm directly from the figure.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\zeta=(z_{1},y_{1},\ldots,z_{\tau},y_{\tau})\sim(\pi_{\theta},{\phi_{\mathrm{SR}}})
be a stopped SR trajectory under
\pi_{\theta}
: at each step
t
the model samples
y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid z_{t})
and stops at the first verified correct response or at budget
K
, with
z_{t}=(x,y_{t-1},f_{t-1})
the revision state before generating
y_{t}
. Let

V_{\pi}(z_{t}):=\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid z_{t})}\!\big(r^{\star}(x,y^{\prime})=1\big)

denote the one-step recovery probability at
z_{t}
, where
y^{\prime}
is a fresh sample (not the trajectory’s own
y_{t}
), and let

\rho_{\theta}(z):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X},\,\zeta\sim(\pi_{\theta},{\phi_{\mathrm{SR}}})}\!\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=z\}\Big]

denote the expected number of times state
z
is visited along a SR rollout. Then

\displaystyle J_{{\phi_{\mathrm{SR}}}}(\theta)=\sum_{t=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{x,\zeta}\!\big[\mathbf{1}\{\tau\geq t\}\,V_{\pi}(z_{t})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X},\,\zeta\sim(\pi_{\theta},{\phi_{\mathrm{SR}}})}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}V_{\pi}(z_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{z}\rho_{\theta}(z)\,\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid z)}\!\big[r^{\star}(x,y^{\prime})\big].

(1)

is a nonnegative-weighted sum of these factors, raising

Comparison with multi-turn RL.

Multi-turn RL provides an unbiased policy-gradient estimator for the
trajectory-level objective. However, its per-turn learning signal is
highly indirect. Consider a wrong–wrong–correct rollout: the gradients
for all three turns are weighted by the same positive terminal advantage,
including the two turns that produced incorrect intermediate responses.
This is valid for optimizing terminal success of the whole trajectory,
but it yields a high-variance and coarse signal for improving the local
revision behavior at each intermediate state.

Our method instead reuses such incorrect intermediate responses as
revision states. Given a state

z=(x,y_{\mathrm{prev}},f_{\mathrm{prev}}),

we draw fresh samples from
\pi(\cdot\mid z)
and estimate the local
one-step recovery probability

V_{\pi}(z)=\Pr_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid z)}\bigl[r^{\star}(x,y)=1\bigr].

Thus, the intermediate wrong response is not treated as an action to
imitate; it is treated as a state from which the model should learn to
recover. This converts a successful long-horizon trajectory into several
short-horizon revision problems, providing a sharper training signal for
local recovery while relying on the current policy to discover useful
intermediate states.

From recovery target to algorithm.

An ideal on-policy implementation of Lemma
4.1
draws
z\sim\rho_{\theta}
by running SR and runs single-turn RL at each
z
. Three design choices turn this into the algorithm we use.

(1) Verification prompts alongside revision data.
At test time the verifier is typically unavailable. We therefore include both
revision prompts
and
verification prompts
(asking the policy to judge whether an intermediate response is correct) in the augmented data, so the trained policy can self-stop at deployment. The split of gains across the two prompt types is in Table
5
.

(2) Offline generation.
An on-policy estimator of
\nabla J_{{\phi_{\mathrm{SR}}}}
would need fresh SR rollouts every gradient step, which is intrinsically serial and prohibitively slow. We instead generate visited states once per epoch in Stage I, reuse them through Stage II, and refresh at the next epoch.

(3) Successful-trajectory filtering.
For a far-from-optimal base policy, online samples at states from failed rollouts rarely produce a verifier-accepted recovery and waste tokens. We restrict Stage I to states on trajectories that succeed within budget
K
, where at least one recovery target exists by construction; the filter loosens as
\pi_{\theta}
improves.

Stage I (Data augmentation).
For each input

Efficiency.

Our algorithm is also more efficient than standard multi-turn training. First, augmented samples are generated in an
off-policy
manner. With a well-designed infrastructure, data augmentation can run in parallel with RL training, so the model continues training on the original RL prompts while new augmented data are generated asynchronously, avoiding any interruption to the main training loop. Second, training itself is single-turn. Each gradient step processes one (prompt, response) pair as in ordinary RLVR, with no per-step sequential rollout in the inner loop, which is the dominant cost of multi-turn RL with budget
K
. Appendix
F.2
reports the wall-clock comparison.

Test-time stopping rule.

At test time the oracle stopping time
\tau
is almost always unavailable, so we adopt a surrogate stopping time
\hat{\tau}
that replaces
r^{\star}
with a domain-specific signal.

For mathematical reasoning tasks, we leverage the
Tail Confidence
metric of
Fu et al. 2025
as the surrogate signal. At each revision step
t
, the token-level confidence at position
i
of
y_{t}
is
C_{i}=-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log P_{i}(j)
, where
P_{i}(j)
is the probability of the
j
-th most likely token (a larger
C_{i}
indicates a more concentrated, more confident distribution). The response-level score
c_{t}
averages
C_{i}
over the tail positions
i\in T_{\text{tail}}
of
y_{t}
. The stopping time is

\hat{\tau}:=K\land\min\!\Big\{t\geq 3:{c_{t}}/{\sum_{j=1}^{t}c_{j}}>c\Big\}

for a predefined threshold
c
. If
\hat{\tau}=K
(budget exhausted without trigger), we output
\hat{y}=y_{t^{\ast}}
with
t^{\ast}=\arg\max_{1\leq t\leq K}c_{t}
, breaking ties uniformly at random when several responses share the same confidence. Selecting the most confident response across all
K
revisions instead of just
y_{K}
improves robustness under small budgets by mitigating noise in confidence estimates at the stopping step.

For coding tasks, we use publicly available test cases as a surrogate reward
\tilde{r}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\{0,1\}
, with
\tilde{r}(x,y)=\mathbf{1}(y\text{ passes all public test cases})
. The stopping time is

\hat{\tau}\;:=\;K\land\min\{t:\tilde{r}(x,y_{t})=1\},

and we output
\hat{y}=y_{K}
when
\hat{\tau}=K
.

5.2
Ablation Study

The role of revision and verification prompts.

As shown in Table
5
, we investigate the roles of revision and verification prompts in sequential revision training.
Qwen-2.5-7B-RevisionOnly augments RL-generated data using only revision prompts, while
Qwen-2.5-7B-VerificationOnly augments data using only verification prompts.
Qwen-2.5-7B-REVES combines both prompts during data augmentation.
Results under Oracle-4 indicate that revision prompts primarily improve
sequential revision capability
, whereas verification prompts do not directly enhance revision but instead train the model to assess solution validity.

To isolate the effect of verification prompts, we evaluate confidence calibration using Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC) on AIME25.
For each problem, we sample multiple model responses, label them as correct or incorrect based on the final answer, and compute the probability that a randomly sampled correct response receives a higher
TailConfidence
score than a randomly sampled incorrect one.
The final AUROC is obtained by averaging over problems with sufficient correct samples.
Under this evaluation, Qwen-2.5-7B-REVES achieves a higher AUROC (74.1%) than RevisionOnly (72.1%), demonstrating that verification prompts improve confidence calibration.
Thus, combining revision and verification prompts yields consistent gains by jointly enhancing revision capability and confidence estimation.

Necessity of continual data augmentation.

As shown in Figure

This is because once the model stops making earlier mistakes, stale augmented data no longer provides informative learning signals, whereas regenerating data each epoch keeps the supervision aligned with the model’s current failure modes.

6
Conclusion

We show that standard single-shot RL objectives are misaligned with test-time sequential revision, and propose
REVES
, which pairs RL with continual, mistake-driven data augmentation so the model learns both to revise incorrect solutions and to identify errors. Augmentation must be continual, since static data becomes uninformative once mistakes disappear. Across math, coding, and out-of-distribution tasks, REVES improves test-time sequential-revision performance over the single-shot RL and multi-turn baselines under fixed budgets, and, as predicted by our transfer analysis, these gains further carry over to other revision-using test-time algorithms. On the circle packing benchmark, a Qwen3-4B trained with REVES matches the previously reported state-of-the-art achieved by much larger evolutionary search systems, using the smallest base model and far fewer rollouts. d

Appendix B
Limitation and Future Direction

Limitation.

REVES requires a ground-truth verifier during training. Math, coding, puzzle, and circle-packing benchmarks all admit deterministic verifiers, so REVES applies directly. For tasks without such verifiers (free-form QA, creative writing, RLHF-style preference learning), REVES does not directly extend; the verification prompts trained alongside revision data can be repurposed for self-verification at test time, but their reliability depends on the policy’s own calibration.

Future direction.

The verifier requirement points to a natural class of problems where REVES is especially well suited: scientific tasks whose objectives admit a clear, automated reward. Examples include solving partial differential equations (PDEs), where residual norms or analytic checks serve as verifiers; symbolic regression, where fit and complexity supply a numeric score; theorem proving, where a proof checker provides binary feedback; and discovery-style optimization tasks like circle packing (Appendix
H
), where the verifier is the geometric solver itself. In all of these settings, the cost of producing the verifier signal is offset by an essentially unlimited supply of training problems and a precise notion of correctness, both of which line up well with REVES’s reliance on per-state one-step recovery as the gradient signal. Scaling REVES to such scientific-discovery domains is a natural next step.

REVES (Ours).

Our method builds on top of the same training framework as the baselines.
The key difference lies in the data augmentation procedure.
Before training, we first perform an offline augmentation pass to initialize the augmented dataset.

When generating augmented data, we allow a maximum revision budget of

Appendix F
Method Details: Multi-Turn Comparison, Efficiency, and Test-Time Sensitivity

F.1
Comparison with Naive Multi-Turn RL

A natural alternative to our offline scheme is online sequential revision
with standard multi-turn credit assignment. Two such schemes appear in the
recent literature. The first,
trajectory-level broadcast
, samples
G

trajectories of
M
turns per prompt, scores each trajectory by its final
reward, computes a trajectory-level advantage, and broadcasts that single
advantage uniformly to every turn in the trajectory. The second,
pooled
per-response
, pools all
G\times M
responses across the
G
trajectories,
treats each response as an independent sample, and computes advantages within
the pool.

Neither scheme suits our setting. Sequential revision is structurally
different from a vanilla multi-turn task: each turn is a fresh attempt at the

same
problem
x
, not a step in a longer-horizon decision process with
intermediate sub-rewards. Trajectory-level broadcast assigns identical credit
to every turn and therefore ignores that some intermediate responses are much
closer to correctness than others. Pooled per-response also fails to capture
this structure: the wrong intermediate responses are treated identically,
even though the near-miss failures are exactly the ones we most want the
policy to learn from. Our offline selection-then-replay scheme bypasses both
pathologies. We identify near-miss failures explicitly through the
success-trajectory filter and replay them as single-turn revision prompts,
where standard single-turn credit assignment applies cleanly.

F.2
Wall-Clock Efficiency

Beyond avoiding credit-assignment pitfalls, our offline scheme is
substantially faster than online multi-turn training. Multi-turn RL pays a
length-
M
serial sequential-revision cost per prompt at every gradient step,
multiplied by the
G
rollouts needed to estimate the advantage. Our method
pays the length-
K
serial cost
only once
per prompt at the offline
stage, and during the online RL stage performs only single-turn sampling on
the extracted prompts, which can be parallelized across
N
Best-of-
N

samples. Single-turn parallel sampling is much cheaper per gradient step than

M
-turn serial generation across
G
rollouts, since serial generation does
not benefit from the parallelism that modern inference engines provide for
single-turn sampling.

Empirically, on
8\times
NVIDIA H200 GPUs, three full iterations of our
method completed in roughly 30 hours, while the equivalent online multi-turn
baseline required over 48 hours on identical hardware
(Table

). With a more carefully optimized
infrastructure, Stage I data generation and Stage II RL training could run
concurrently (Stage II first trains on the original RL data while Stage I
generates augmented data asynchronously), yielding further gains.

Appendix H
Circle Packing Implementation Details

This section gives the implementation details behind the circle packing results in Table
2
. Circle packing maximizes the sum of radii of
n
non-overlapping circles inscribed in the unit square, with verifier feedback given by a non-overlap and inscription check together with the realized objective value. We instantiate REVES on this benchmark by directly optimizing
J_{{\phi_{\mathrm{SR}}}}
in the recovery form of Lemma
4.1
: each visited state
z_{t}
corresponds to a partially-improved candidate configuration, the policy proposes a refined configuration
y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid z_{t})
, and the verifier returns
r^{\star}(x,y_{t})=1
iff
y_{t}
improves on the running best while remaining feasible.

Reward shaping.

The raw objective of a candidate
y
is its sum of radii
g(y):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}(y)
(a continuous quantity), which clusters in a narrow band near the optimum (
g\approx 2.63
for the
n=26
instance we evaluate) and differs across candidates only at the third or fourth decimal. Used directly, this signal is too compressed to drive learning. We therefore shape it in two steps. Let
v(y)\geq 0
denote the total constraint violation (circle overlap plus boundary excess), so that
y
is feasible iff
v(y)=0
. First, define the raw score
s(y)
as

s(y)=\begin{cases}g(y),&v(y)=0,\\
g(y)-v(y),&v(y)>0\ \text{and}\ g(y)\leq 2.64,\\
-1,&v(y)>0\ \text{and}\ g(y)>2.64,\end{cases}

i.e., minor violations are absorbed as a soft penalty, while implausibly high sums coupled with infeasibility are flagged as cheating and assigned
s=-1
. Second, we amplify by squaring the offset from
2.5
:
R(s):=(s-2.5)^{2}
. Since feasible raw scores live above
2.5
, this magnifies small absolute differences, e.g., a
\Delta s=10^{-3}
gain near
s=2.6
produces a
\Delta R\approx 2\cdot 10^{-4}
, which is no longer dwarfed by gradient noise. The reward used for a revision
y
from parent state
x
is the resulting improvement,

U(y\mid x):=R(s(y))-R(s(x)),

so the policy is rewarded for any move that improves on the parent and penalized for any move that worsens it.

Stage I: search rollouts and prompt-set construction.

We run
4
sequential-revision rollouts under the current
\pi_{\theta}
with budget
K=100
, where each step proposes a refinement of the previous candidate configuration and the verifier returns the shaped reward
U(y\mid x)
defined above. From the visited intermediate states across these rollouts, we filter to retain those whose subsequent revisions produce verified improvements, and assemble the resulting revision prompts into the augmented prompt set.

Stage II: RL training.

We train Qwen3-4B with GRPO
(
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
)
on the prompt set from Stage I. Each gradient step samples
8
prompts; for each prompt we draw
64
rollouts to form the group used for the GRPO advantage estimate.

10
visualizes one of the configurations REVES discovers, and Figure
11
reproduces the Python program emitted by the policy that generates it: an initial grid placement followed by an SLSQP refinement against the non-overlap and inscription constraints.
