Modelling Network Resilience: The Complexity of Some Graph Division Games
Grzegorz Gutowski, Konstanty Junosza-Szaniawski, Antonio Lauerbach, Alexander Wolff

Abstract
Motivated by the controller placement problems in software-defined networks and the fair division principles of classical “cake cutting”, we investigate the following two-player zero-sum game. In our model, a defender places a limited number of controllers on graph vertices, while an attacker deletes a limited number of vertices. The defender score is the total number of surviving vertices reachable from any remaining controller. We formalize the computational problems associated with various game dynamics (defender plays first; attacker plays first; players play simultaneously; pure or mixed strategies). We show that these natural problems are 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -complete or Σ 2 𝖯 \Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2} -complete, depending on the specific variant. These hardness results provide limitations for optimal controller placement algorithms under different notions of quality of a solution. Finally, we present structural insights that yield efficient algorithms for restricted graph classes (namely interval graphs and graphs of bounded treewidth).

Introduction

Cake cutting is a classical problem in fair division
[
52
,
23
,
13
,
42
,
40
]
. Its goal is to divide a heterogeneous divisible resource, called a cake, among several agents with different preferences. In the standard model, the cake is represented by the interval
[0,1]
, and each agent has a valuation function over pieces of the cake. For two agents, a well-known procedure is the
I cut, you choose
rule: one agent cuts the cake into two pieces, and the other chooses one of them. For three or more agents, however, the problem becomes significantly more difficult. In particular, Stromquist
[
54
]
showed that no finite protocol can guarantee an envy-free division when each agent is required to receive a single connected piece of the interval.

A natural extension of the model is to replace the interval by a graph. In this setting, the resource is distributed over the graph, and each agent is typically required to receive a connected piece. This captures situations where the resource has an underlying network structure and disconnected allocations are not allowed. Unlike the interval case, the graph setting introduces additional difficulties because the topology of the graph limits the possible allocations. Therefore, fairness and computational complexity both become closely tied to the structure of the graph and the agents’ valuations
[
11
,
3
]
.

This perspective becomes even more natural when one views division as
an interactive process rather than a static outcome. Already in
classical cake cutting, procedures such as
I cut, you choose

involve sequential decisions made by the agents.
On graphs, similar ideas lead to strategic settings in which players
with conflicting objectives can influence the resulting division. The
problem then is no longer only to determine whether a fair or good
division exists, but also to understand which player can enforce a
desirable outcome.

Once division on graphs is viewed as a strategic process, it becomes natural to study its algorithmic complexity from a game-theoretic perspective. The main question is whether one of the players can guarantee a desired outcome against all possible responses of the opponent. Such problems are often computationally hard, because evaluating a move requires reasoning about the entire game tree. Classical examples include the
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-completeness of
Generalized Geography

[
49
]
and
Hex

[
41
]
, and the
\mathsf{EXPTIME}
-completeness of generalized
Chess
and
Checkers

[
25
,
43
]
.

In this paper, we study a game introduced by Fortz, Mycek, Pióro, and Tomaszewski
[
57
,
24
]
, motivated by the problem of placing controllers in a telecommunication network so as to improve its resilience to attacks. A network operator places controllers at selected vertices of the network, while an attacker chooses a set of vertices to remove and disconnect the network. A vertex is said to survive if it belongs to a connected component containing at least one controller. The outcome of the game is the number of surviving vertices. This game can be viewed as a variant of the classical
I cut, you choose
procedure, with the crucial difference that the chooser must act before knowing where the cuts will occur.

From a game-theoretic perspective, a
pure strategy
of the
defender is a choice of controller locations, while a pure strategy of
the attacker is a choice of attacked vertices. The payoff is
determined by the number of surviving vertices for the pair of
strategies chosen by the players. A
mixed strategy
is a
probability distribution over pure strategies, meaning that a player
randomizes between several possible choices. Thus, if the complete
strategy sets of both players are given explicitly as part of the
input, then the game can be represented by a payoff matrix and its
value can be computed in polynomial time. In general, however, the
number of possible strategies of each player may be exponential in the
size of the graph. To cope with this difficulty, one may apply the
double-oracle method. Starting from restricted sets of strategies for
both players, one repeatedly computes a best response to the current
strategy set of the opponent and adds this best response to the
corresponding set. The process continues until no player can improve
by adding a new strategy. In practice, best-response problems are
often solved using mixed-integer linear programming, and the overall
approach is closely related to the column-generation
method
[
38
]
. The game was originally studied as a
min–max game with pure strategies, while Junosza-Szaniawski and
Nogalski
[
32
]
also considered mixed strategies for
restricted strategy sets. This approach was extended by applying the
double-oracle method to the mixed-strategy version of the
game
[
39
]
.

The double-oracle method is frequently used in robust optimization, where one seeks solutions that perform well under uncertainty in the input data
[
4
,
5
]
. In this sense, the game studied here can be interpreted as an optimal placement problem under uncertainty regarding the set of vertices attacked by the adversary.

Our contribution

The main contribution of this paper is a complexity classification of
several natural variants of the controller-placement game on graphs.
We show that the defender’s optimization problem against a fixed
attack is solvable in linear time (
\cref
obs:optdef), whereas the
attacker’s optimization problem against a fixed defense is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete
(
\cref
thm:pureattfixdef). For the sequential game, we prove that
deciding whether the defender has a strategy that guarantees a
prescribed value is
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
-complete (
\cref
thm:puredefpureatt), whereas
the analogous problem for the attacker is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete
(
\cref
thm:pureattpuredef). We further study mixed-strategy settings
arising from double-oracle approaches and show that the associated
best-response problems are
\mathsf{NP}
-complete; see
\cref
sec:mixed. Finally,
we provide structural results that yield efficient
algorithms on interval graphs and graphs of bounded treewidth; see

\cref
sec:restricted.

Many two-player graph games can be expressed as existential–universal

Several related graph problems have

[

showed that

and several of its variants are

\Sigma_{2}^{P}
-complete. Cardinal and Joret
[

-complete for every

[

More recently, Grüne and Wulf
[

-completeness for many interdiction problems, including

Our game can also be viewed through an existential–universal lens, but it is strongly motivated by controller placement problems in software-defined networks. This literature considers a broad range of objectives, including communication delay
[

,

]
, controller capacity
[

, dynamic traffic and topology changes

A similar existential–universal structure appears in robust optimization: one asks whether there exists a solution that remains good for all realizations of the uncertain data from a given uncertainty set. Robust optimization is a central framework for optimization under uncertainty
[

Preliminaries

For a graph
G
, let
V(G)
denote the set of vertices of
G
and let

E(G)\subseteq{V(G)\choose 2}
denote the set of edges of
G
.
In a graph, a
(
k
-)defense
is a set of (at most
k
) vertices,
and an
(
\ell
-)attack
is a set of (at most
\ell
) vertices.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{k}(G)=\{D\subseteq V(G):|D|=k\}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(G)=\{A\subseteq V(G):|A|=\ell\}
denote the sets of
k
-defenses and
\ell
-attacks. Given a graph
G
with a defense
D
and an attack
A
, we say that a
vertex
v\in V(G)\setminus A

survives
if the graph
G-A

contains a path from
v
to some vertex in
D
.
If a vertex does not survive under defense
D
and attack
A
, we say
that it gets
disabled
.
We define the payoff function
\payoff(G,D,A)
for
the defender to be the number of vertices that survive under
defense
D
and attack
A
.
Correspondingly, we define the payoff
function
\payoffatt(G,D,A)
for the attacker to be the number of
vertices that get disabled.
Clearly, we have
\payoff(G,D,A)+\payoffatt(G,D,A)={\left|V(G)\right|}
,
so the game is
zero-sum
. The defender seeks to
maximize
\payoff
, while the attacker seeks to maximize
\payoffatt

so to minimize
\payoff
. Moreover, every vertex in
A
is disabled
by the attack, hence
\payoffatt(G,D,A)\geqslant{\left|A\right|}
.

A
mixed
k
-defense

\mathcal{D}
is a set

\left\{(D,p_{D}):D\in\mathcal{D}_{k}(G)\right\}
, where each
p_{D}
is a
non-negative rational and
\sum_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{k}(G)}p_{D}=1
.
A
mixed
\ell
-attack

\mathcal{A}
is a

\left\{(A,q_{A}):A\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(G)\right\}
, where each
q_{A}
is a
non-negative rational and
\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(G)}q_{A}=1
.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\textrm{mix}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}^{\textrm{mix}}
denote the set of all mixed
k
-defenses and all
\ell
-attacks, respectively.
For
\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\textrm{mix}}
and
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}^{\textrm{mix}}
, we define the expected payoff

\payoff(G,\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A})=\sum_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}}p_{D}\cdot q_{A}\cdot\payoff(G,D,A).

The value of the game is defined by

\operatorname{val}(G,k,\ell)=\max_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\mathrm{mix}}}\min_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{mix}}}\payoff(G,\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A})=\min_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{mix}}(G)}\max_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\mathrm{mix}}(G)}\payoff(G,\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}).

The second equality follows from the minimax theorem for finite two-player zero-sum games
[
59
]
.
A pair
(\mathcal{D}^{*},\mathcal{A}^{*})\in\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\mathrm{mix}}\times\mathcal{A}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{mix}}

is a
Nash equilibrium
if

\forall\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\mathrm{mix}}:\quad\forall\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{mix}}:\quad\payoff(G,\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}^{*})\leqslant\payoff(G,\mathcal{D}^{*},\mathcal{A}^{*})\leqslant\payoff(G,\mathcal{D}^{*},\mathcal{A})\text{.}

In other words, neither player can improve by a unilateral change of the strategy.

Complexity

Our objective is to understand the computational complexity of computing optimal strategies for the defender and the attacker under different game dynamics.
The complexity classes
\mathsf{NP}
and
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
are the most relevant for our purposes.
In order to show hardness results, we use polynomial-time many-to-one reductions to show
\mathsf{NP}
-completeness and
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
-completeness of natural decision problems associated with the optimization problems of interest.
For the class
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
, we give a very brief introduction and refer the reader to the textbook by Arora and Barak
[
1
, Chapter 5]
.
Schaefer and
Umans
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
give an extensive
list of complete problems for different classes in the polynomial
hierarchy. For a very brief introduction,
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
is defined as

\mathsf{NP}^{\mathsf{NP}}
– a class of languages decidable in polynomial time by
nondeterministic Turing machines with access to an
\mathsf{NP}
-oracle, which
can decide any language in
\mathsf{NP}
in a single step of execution. The
canonical complete problem for
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
is the following.

Problem:

Existential-
2
-Level-SAT

Input:

Boolean formula
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{a},y_{1},\ldots,y_{b})
with variables in two disjoint sets
\left\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{a}\right\}
and
\left\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{b}\right\}

Question:

Is the following Boolean formula true?

\exists{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{a}}:\quad\forall{y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{b}}:\quad\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{a},y_{1},\ldots,y_{b})

Stockmeyer
[
53
]
and Wrathall
[
62
]

independently proved that the class
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
is exactly the class of
languages reducible to
Existential-
2
-Level-SAT
via polynomial-time many-to-one
reductions.

As our problems are naturally expressed as two-round games and ask if there exists a move for the first player such that, for every move of the second player, a certain payoff is guaranteed,
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
is a natural complexity class for some of our problems.

Problem statements

We first consider division games in which the players play one after another, and the second player can observe the move of the first player before making their own move.
In this case, we can distinguish two variants: one in which the defender plays first and one in which the attacker plays first.
We are interested in computing an optimal response of the second player to a given move of the first player.

Problem:

CP(Fixed Attack, Pure Defense)

Input:

A graph
G
, an attack
A
, positive integers
k
and
\delta
.

Question:

Is there a
k
-defense
D
such that
\payoff(G,D,A)\geqslant\delta
?

It is easy to see that
CP(Fixed Attack, Pure Defense)
can be solved efficiently.

Observation 1
.

CP(Fixed Attack, Pure Defense)
can be solved in time linear in the size of
G
.

Proof.

Compute
G^{\prime}=G-A
by removing the vertices in
A
from
G
.
Traverse
G^{\prime}
(e.g., by breadth-first search) to compute a list (that
is, a multiset)
C
of the sizes of the connected components
of
G^{\prime}
.
If
|C|\leqslant k
, let
C^{\prime}=C
. If
|C|>k
, find (in

O(|C|)
time) the
k
-th largest number
c^{\star}
in
C
. In this
case, let
C^{\prime}
be the sublist of
\{c\in C,c\geqslant c^{\star}\}
that
contains only as many copies of
c^{\star}
as needed so that

|C^{\prime}|=k
. Clearly,
\payoff(G,D,A)=\sum_{c\in C^{\prime}}c
.
It is easy to see that the algorithm can be implement to run in time
linear in the size of
G
.
∎

Symmetrically, we are interested in computing an optimal response to a

Problem:

CP(Fixed Defense, Pure Attack)

A graph

Question:

It is an interesting phenomenon that this problem is computationally harder than

We are also interested in deciding whether there exists a

strategy
for the first player that guarantees a certain payoff
against
any
response of the opponent. As a consequence of the
above disparity, we can also expect that the complexities of deciding

3
Pure Strategies

In this section, we settle the complexity of the deterministic
problems that we defined in
\cref
sec:preliminaries. We start with a
given defense: Can an
\ell
-attack secure a payoff of
\alpha
?

Theorem 2
.

CP(Fixed Defense, Pure Attack)
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

Proof.

Membership in
\mathsf{NP}
is obvious as we can compute
\payoffatt(G,D,A)
in polynomial time.
To show
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness, we reduce from
Clique
, which is defined as
follows.

Problem:

Clique

Input:

A graph
G
and a positive integer
t
.

Question:

Does
G
contain a
t
-clique, that is, a subgraph that is isomorphic to
K_{t}
?

Given an instance
\langle G,t\rangle
of
Clique
, we set

n=|V(G)|
,
m=|E(G)|
,
\alpha=t+{t\choose 2}
,
\ell=t
, and
construct a graph
H
and a defense
D\subseteq V(H)
such that there
exists an
\ell
-attack
A
with
\payoffatt(H,D,A)\geqslant\alpha
if and
only if
G
contains a
t
-clique.

Let
H
be the graph that has a
node vertex

v^{\prime}
for every
node
v
of
G
and an
edge vertex

e^{\prime}
for every edge
e

of
G
. In
H
, every two node vertices are adjacent, and every edge
vertex is adjacent to the two corresponding node vertices (that is,

e^{\prime}
is adjacent to
u^{\prime}
and
v^{\prime}
if
e=\{u,v\}
is an edge of
G
).
We let
D=\left\{v^{\prime}:v\in V(G)\right\}
be the set of node vertices of
H
.

We say that a
t
-attack
A
is
successful
if
\payoffatt(H,D,A)\geqslant t+{t\choose 2}
.
For
A
to be successful, it has to disable at least

{t\choose 2}
vertices apart from those in
A
.
It remains to show that there exists a successful
t
-attack if and
only if
G
contains a
t
-clique.

If
G
contains a
t
-clique
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{t}
, then the attack

A=\left\{v^{\prime}_{1},v^{\prime}_{2},\dots,v^{\prime}_{t}\right\}
disables, apart from the
t
vertices
in
A
, the
{t\choose 2}
vertices in the set

\left\{e^{\prime}:e=\left\{v_{i},v_{j}\right\},1\leqslant i<j\leqslant t\right\}
. Hence,
A
is
successful.

For the other direction, let

A=\left\{v^{\prime}_{1},v^{\prime}_{2},\dots,v^{\prime}_{p},e^{\prime}_{1},e^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{r}\right\}
be a
t
-attack
with
t=p+r
. Then
A
disables the vertices in
A
and every edge
vertex
e^{\prime}
such that both endpoints of
e
are in

\left\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{p}\right\}
. Hence,

\payoffatt(H,D,A)\leqslant t+{p\choose 2}
. If
A
is successful, then

\payoffatt(H,D,A)=t+{t\choose 2}
. Hence, in this case, it holds
that
p=t
and
r=0
. Moreover, each of the
{t\choose 2}
pairs of
vertices in
\left\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{t}\right\}
must be connected by an edge
in
G
(so that
A
disables the corresponding edge vertex). This
means that these vertices induce a
t
-clique in
G
.
∎

Next, we ask: Is there a
k
-defense with a payoff of
\delta
against
every possible
\ell
-attack?

Theorem 3
.

CP(Pure Defense, Any Pure Attack)
is
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
-complete.

Proof.

Membership in
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
is clear: Given a graph
G
, a
k
-defense
D
,
and integers
\ell
and
\delta
, we can use an
\mathsf{NP}
-oracle to test
whether, for every
\ell
-attack
A
, we have

\payoff(G,D,A)\geqslant\delta
.

To show hardness, we reduce from
CliqueNodeDeletion
, which is

\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
-hard
[
44

A graph

, and let

. W.l.o.g., we can assume

First, observe that if any of the node vertices in

Given that

As this can trivially be achieved by placing all defenses on node vertices, we can assume w.l.o.g. that regardless of the attack, at least one node vertex, and therefore all node vertices that are not attacked directly, survive.
Therefore, since a node vertex can only be disabled if attacked directly, attacking an edge vertex or a copy vertex yields only one disabled vertex.
As attacking a node vertex disables at least one vertex,

By construction, the graph

4
Mixed Strategies

In this section, we consider mixed strategies for both players.
First, consider for a moment a variant, where we are given a set of pure strategies for the defender and a set of pure strategies for the attacker, and we are asked to compute the value of the game when players are restricted to use only these strategies.
The assignment of weights to the strategies in a Nash equilibrium for such a variant can be solved in polynomial time by linear programming.
As there are exponentially many pure strategies for both players, this gives us an
\mathsf{EXPTIME}
algorithm for
CP(Mixed Defense, Mixed Attack)
.

A natural heuristic to solve
CP(Mixed Defense, Mixed Attack)
is to start with a small set of pure strategies for both players, compute a Nash equilibrium for this restricted game, and then iteratively add pure strategies that are best responses to the current mixed strategy of the opponent.
This heuristic is known as
column generation
and is a common approach to solve large linear programs.
It is not guaranteed to terminate in polynomial time, but it can be efficient in practice when there are only a few pure strategies that are relevant for the optimal mixed strategy.
In our case, even for the simple example of a clique
K_{2n}
and

k=\ell=n
, it is easy to see that the optimal mixed strategies for
both players must use all possible
n
-element subsets of the
2n

vertices.
Thus column generation would require an exponential number of
iterations to find the optimal mixed strategies.

We establish another difficulty within the column generation approach, by showing that given a mixed strategy for one player, it is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to compute a best response for the other player.
We formalize these problems below, starting with the mixed defense.

Problem:

CP(Fixed Mixed Defense, Pure Attack)

Input:

A graph
G
, a mixed defense
\mathcal{D}
, positive integers
\ell
and
\alpha
.

Question:

Is there an
\ell
-attack
A
such that
\payoffatt(G,\mathcal{D},A)\geqslant\alpha
?

By
\cref
thm:pureattfixdef, we get that
CP(Fixed Mixed Defense, Pure Attack)
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete even in the special case where the defense contains only one strategy with probability
1
and thus, the following corollary.

Corollary 5
.

CP(Fixed Mixed Defense, Pure Attack)
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

To get a similar result for column generation for the defender, we need to be more careful.

Problem:

CP(Fixed Mixed Attack, Pure Defense)

Restricted Graph Classes

We know from
\cref
thm:pureattfixdef that
CP(Fixed Defense, Pure Attack)
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard in general. In the following, we show that it is polynomial-time solvable on interval graphs as well as on graphs of bounded treewidth. This means that
CP(Pure Defense, Any Pure Attack)
is in
\mathsf{NP}
for these graph classes. We start with interval graphs.

For
CP(Fixed Defense, Pure Attack)
, it suffices to consider connected graphs.

Lemma 7
.

Given a graph
G
, a defense
D
, an integer
\ell
, and for each
connected component
C
of
G
and
0\leqslant\ell^{\prime}\leqslant\ell
an optimal

\ell^{\prime}
-attack on
C
under defense
D
, we can find an optimal

\ell
-attack on
G
under defense
D
in
O{\left(c\cdot\ell^{2}\right)}
time,
where
c
is the number of connected components.

Proof.

Let
\left\{C_{1},\dots,C_{c}\right\}
be the connected components of
G
and,
for
i\in[c]
and
0\leqslant\ell^{\prime}\leqslant\ell
, let
A_{i,\ell^{\prime}}
be an
optimal
\ell^{\prime}
-attack on component
C_{i}
.
Let
P_{i,\ell^{\prime}}=\payoffatt(G,D,A_{i,\ell^{\prime}})
.
Let
G_{i}=G[C_{1}\cup\dots\cup C_{i}]
.
For
i\in[c]
and
0\leqslant\ell^{\prime}\leqslant\ell
, let
T[i,\ell^{\prime}]
be the payoff of the optimal
\ell^{\prime}
-attack on
G_{i}
.
Clearly, for every
i\in[c]
, a 0-attack on
G_{i}
yields
T[i,0]=0
.
Furthermore,
T[1,\ell^{\prime}]=P_{1,\ell^{\prime}}
.
For
i>1
and
\ell^{\prime}>0
, it holds that
T[i,\ell^{\prime}]=\max\left\{T[i-1,\ell^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime\prime}]+P_{i,\ell^{\prime\prime}}\mid 0\leqslant\ell^{\prime\prime}\leqslant\ell^{\prime}\right\}
.
The optimal payoff is given by
T[c,\ell]
. Using dynamic
programming, each entry of

can be computed in
O{\left(\ell\right)}
time.
Since
T
has
O{\left(c\cdot\ell\right)}
entries, the dynamic program runs

O{\left(c\cdot\ell^{2}\right)}

∎

.
If
y>v.\mathrm{right}
, then it is not specified whether
v
survives or not, and we can therefore choose whether to attack it. If
v
is a controller under
D
, it holds that
T[x,y,z,j]=\max\left\{T[x^{\prime},y,v.\mathrm{left},j],T[x^{\prime},y,z,j-1]+1\right\}
. If
v
is not a controller, it holds that
T[x,y,z,j]=\max\left\{T[x^{\prime},y,z,j],T[x^{\prime},y,z,j-1]+1\right\}
.
That leaves the case that
y=v.\mathrm{right}
, meaning that
v
must survive. We have to consider all possible entries
y^{\prime}
for the previous endpoint
x^{\prime}
, which can be any right endpoint of an interval in
\mathcal{I}_{x^{\prime}}
that ends before
v.\mathrm{right}
or
\mathrm{none}
. Furthermore, we have to determine the entry
z^{\prime}
for the earliest surviving interval outside
G_{x^{\prime}}
. If
v
is a controller under
D
or if
z<v.\mathrm{right}
, then
v
is kept alive without
G_{x^{\prime}}
, and therefore
z^{\prime}=v.\mathrm{left}
. Otherwise, if
v
is not a controller and,
z>v.\mathrm{right}
or
z=\mathrm{none}
,
v
cannot be kept alive without
G_{x^{\prime}}
, and therefore
z^{\prime}=z
. Thus, it holds that
T[x,v.\mathrm{right},z,j]=\max_{y^{\prime}\in\left\{w.\mathrm{right}<v.\mathrm{right}\mid w\in\mathcal{I}_{x^{\prime}}\right\}\cup\{\mathrm{none}\}}T[x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime},j]
where
z^{\prime}=v.\mathrm{left}
if
v
is a controller under
D
or
z<v.\mathrm{right}
, and
z^{\prime}=z
otherwise.

Next, we consider the case that the current eventpoint
x
is the right endpoint of an interval
v
, i.e.,
x=v.\mathrm{right}
.
Let again
x^{\prime}
be the previous eventpoint.
Recall that we defined our intervals to be open on the right, meaning that
v\notin\mathcal{I}_{x}
.
Therefore, if
y\neq\mathrm{none}
, it holds that
T[x,y,z,j]=T[x^{\prime},y,z,j]
as this does not restrict the survival of
v
.
However, if
y=\mathrm{none}
, we have to consider whether
v
survives or not.
Thus, it holds that
T[x,\mathrm{none},z,j]=\max(T[x^{\prime},v.\mathrm{right},z,j],T[x^{\prime},\mathrm{none},z,j])
.

It remains to show that this algorithm runs in time polynomial in
n=|V(G)|
.
There are
O{\left(n\right)}
eventpoints, and for each eventpoint there are
O{\left(n\right)}
choices for
y
,
O{\left(n\right)}
choices for
z
, and
O{\left(n\right)}
options for
j
, leading to a total of
O{\left(n^{4}\right)}
entries in the status.
Each entry can be computed in linear time.
This leads to a total runtime of
O{\left(n^{5}\right)}
for a connected component.
Using
\cref
lem:component-attack-knapsack, we can handle disconnected graphs in polynomial time as well.
∎

Now we turn to graphs of bounded treewidth. Treewidth is a
fundamental graph parameter and expresses the tree-similarity of a
graph. For a formal definition, see, for example

[
18
]
.

Theorem 9
.

Given a graph
G
, a tree decomposition
(T,\mathcal{B})
of
G
, a
defense, and an attack size
\ell
,
CP(Fixed Defense, Pure Attack)
can be solved
in
O{\left(9^{\mathsf{tw}}\cdot\mathsf{tw}^{2}\cdot\ell^{2}\cdot n\right)}
time, where
n
is the
number of vertices of
G
and
\mathsf{tw}
is the width of
(T,\mathcal{B})
.

Proof.

We can assume w.l.o.g.
[
18
, Lemma
7.4]
that the given tree
decomposition
(T,\mathcal{B})
of
G
is
nice
, that is,
T

is a rooted tree with the following node types:
For each
leaf node

t
of
T
, the corresponding
bag
B_{t}
in
\mathcal{B}
is empty, i.e.,
B_{t}=\emptyset
. A
node
t
of
T
is an
introduce node
if it has exactly one
child
t^{\prime}
and it holds that
B_{t}=B_{t^{\prime}}\cup\left\{v\right\}
for some
vertex
v
of
G
. We say that
v
is
introduced
at
t
. A
node
t
of
T
is a
forget node
if it has exactly one
child
t^{\prime}
and it holds that
B_{t}=B_{t^{\prime}}\setminus\left\{v\right\}
for some
vertex
v
of
G
. We say that
v
is
forgotten
at
t
. A
node
t
of
T
is a
join node
if it has exactly two
children
t^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime\prime}
and it holds that
B_{t}=B_{t^{\prime}}=B_{t^{\prime\prime}}
.
The root
r
of
T
has
B_{r}=\emptyset
.

We now present a dynamic program for finding an optimal

\ell
-attack
A
against a defense
D
.

For each node
t
of the tree decomposition, let
V_{t}
be the union
of the bags corresponding to the nodes in the subtree of
T
rooted
at
t
. Let
G_{t}=G[V_{t}]
. We maintain a
table
P_{t}[\ell^{\prime},S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}},C]
,
where
S_{\mathrm{a}}\,\dot{\cup}\,S_{\mathrm{s}}\,\dot{\cup}\,S_{\mathrm{d}}=B_{t}
and
C\subseteq S_{\mathrm{s}}
. The value
of
P_{t}[\ell^{\prime},S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}},C]
is the
maximum number of vertices in
G_{t}
that can be disabled by an

\ell^{\prime}
-attack on
G_{t}
under defense
D\cap V_{t}
such that the
following conditions hold:

•

All vertices in
S_{\mathrm{a}}
are attacked.

•

All vertices in
S_{\mathrm{d}}
are disabled.

•

All vertices in
S_{\mathrm{s}}
survive.

The vertices in
C
represent vertices having a live connection from
outside
G_{t}
, and thus act as controllers with regard to attacks
on
G_{t}
.

The base cases correspond to the leaf nodes of
T
. For each leaf
node
t
,
B_{t}=\emptyset
. Hence, for
each
\ell^{\prime}\in\{0,\dots,\ell\}
, there is only one
entry
P_{t}[\ell^{\prime},\emptyset,\emptyset,\emptyset,\emptyset]
, which we
set to
0
. If an entry is infeasible, we set it to
-\infty
. For
convenience, we implicitly add infeasible entries for
\ell^{\prime}=-1
.

Introduce

Let
t
be an introduce node with child
t^{\prime}
, and let
v
be the vertex introduced at
t
. We compute the entries of
P_{t}
as follows:
If
v
is attacked, i.e.,
v\in S_{\mathrm{a}}
, it holds that
P_{t}[\ell^{\prime},S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}},C]=P_{t^{\prime}}[\ell^{\prime}-1,S_{\mathrm{a}}\setminus\left\{v\right\},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}},C]+1
.
If
v
is disabled, i.e.,
v\in S_{\mathrm{d}}
, it must hold that no neighbor of
v
is surviving.
In other words,
N(v)\cap S_{\mathrm{s}}=\emptyset
and
v
is not a controller nor has a connection to the outside, i.e.,
v\not\in D
and
v\not\in C
.
In this case,
P_{t}[\ell,S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}},C]=P_{t^{\prime}}[\ell,S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}}\setminus\left\{v\right\},C]+1
.
Otherwise, the entry is infeasible.

If
v
survives, i.e.,
v\in S_{\mathrm{s}}
, we distinguish two cases.
First, if
v
is a controller, i.e.,
v\in D
, or if
v\in C
(i.e.,
v
has a live connection from outside
G_{t}
), then
v
provides a live connection to the set of its neighbors in
B_{t}
that are not attacked.
Call this set
N^{\prime}
and note that
N^{\prime}=N(v)\cap B_{t}\setminus S_{\mathrm{a}}
.
For the entry to be feasible, it must therefore hold that
N^{\prime}\subset S_{\mathrm{s}}
.
In this case, it holds that
P_{t}[\ell,S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}},C]=P_{t^{\prime}}[\ell,S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}}\setminus\left\{v\right\},S_{\mathrm{d}},(C\setminus\left\{v\right\})\cup N^{\prime}]
.
Otherwise, if
v
is not a controller and has no live connection from the outside,
v
must have a live connection inside
G_{t}
.
Thus at least one neighbor of
v
in
B_{t}
must survive, otherwise the entry is infeasible.
If the entry is feasible, we have
P_{t}[\ell,S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}},C]=P_{t^{\prime}}[\ell,S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}}\setminus\left\{v\right\},S_{\mathrm{d}},C]
.

Forget

Let
t
be a forget node with child
t^{\prime}
, and let
v
be the vertex forgotten at
t
. We compute the entries of
P_{t}
as follows:
Since we have forgotten
v
, it can be in any of the three states (attacked/disabled/survived). Further, it cannot be in
C
, as it has no neighbors outside of
G_{t^{\prime}}
. Thus, it holds that

\displaystyle P_{t}[\ell,S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}},C]=\max\big\{

\displaystyle P_{t^{\prime}}[\ell,S_{\mathrm{a}}\cup\left\{v\right\},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}},C],~P_{t^{\prime}}[\ell,S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}}\cup\left\{v\right\},C],

\displaystyle P_{t^{\prime}}[\ell,S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}}\cup\left\{v\right\},S_{\mathrm{d}},C]\big\}.

Join

Let
t
be a join node with children
t^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime\prime}
. As we join
the two subgraphs
G_{t^{\prime}}
and
G_{t^{\prime\prime}}
, the number of combined
disabled vertices is the sum of the disabled vertices in both
subgraphs, minus the vertices in
S_{\mathrm{a}}
, which are attacked
in both subgraphs and thus counted twice. Further, we have to
consider that some vertices in
S_{\mathrm{s}}
in
G_{t^{\prime}}
may
have a live connection that goes through
G_{t^{\prime\prime}}
, and vice versa.
We account for these possibilities by maximizing over all
C^{\prime}

and
C^{\prime\prime}
such that
C\subseteq C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}\subseteq S_{\mathrm{s}}

and
(C^{\prime}\setminus C)\cap(C^{\prime\prime}\setminus C)=\emptyset
. Therefore,
it holds that

\displaystyle\hskip-8.61108ptP_{t}[\ell,S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}},C]=\max\big\{

\displaystyle P_{t^{\prime}}[\ell^{\prime},S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}},C^{\prime}]+P_{t^{\prime\prime}}[\ell^{\prime\prime},S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}},C^{\prime\prime}]-{\left|S_{\mathrm{a}}\right|}:

\displaystyle\ell^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime\prime}=\ell+{\left|S_{\mathrm{a}}\right|},C\subseteq C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}\subseteq S_{\mathrm{s}},(C^{\prime}\setminus C)\cap(C^{\prime\prime}\setminus C)=\emptyset\big\}.

Runtime

There are
O{\left(3^{\mathsf{tw}}\cdot 2^{\mathsf{tw}}\cdot\ell\right)}
entries
(as
S_{\mathrm{a}},S_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\mathrm{d}}
partition
B_{t}
)
and
C\subseteq S_{\mathrm{s}}\subseteq B_{t}
. Computing an entry of a
introduce or forget node takes
O{\left(\mathsf{tw}\right)}
time, whereas computing an
entry of a join node takes
O{\left(3^{\mathsf{tw}}\cdot\ell\right)}
time. Since
there are
O{\left(\mathsf{tw}\cdot n\right)}
nodes in the nice tree decomposition,
we obtain the desired runtime.
∎

6
Open Problems

From our last two theorems, it follows that
CP(Pure Defense, Any Pure Attack)
is in
\mathsf{NP}
for interval graphs and graphs of bounded treewidth.
While it is tempting to use similar dynamic programs to solve the more general case of
CP(Pure Defense, Any Pure Attack)
in polynomial time, by additionally deciding for each vertex whether it is defended, there is a fundamental issue with this approach: In
CP(Pure Defense, Any Pure Attack)
, the defender has to determine its whole defense before the attacker attacks. However, a dynamic program that iteratively proceeds along a graph allows the defender to adapt its defense to the attack decisions made in the previous steps, something not possible in the actual problem.
Still, we conjecture that
CP(Pure Defense, Any Pure Attack)
is in
\mathsf{P}
for interval graphs and graphs of bounded treewidth.

Another natural question is whether any of the problems that are hard
on general graphs admit polynomial-time solutions on other graph
classes, e.g., planar graphs.
