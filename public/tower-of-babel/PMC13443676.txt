Hierarchical interplay between H3K27ac and H3K4me3 in transcriptional regulation
Authors: Chenwei Zhou, Chanjuan Dong, Weiye Zhao, Fu-Sen Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
H3K27ac and H3K4me3 are enriched at transcriptional start sites and have been implicated in transcription. However, how these marks concertedly regulate transcription is not fully understood. Here, we develop a dual chemically inducible CRISPR/dCas9-based epigenome editing system that enables independent, temporal and transcription stage-specific modulation of H3K27ac and H3K4me3 at a specific gene locus. Stage-specific removal of H3K4me3 impairs RNA polymerase II recruitment, increases promoter-proximal pausing, reduces productive elongation, and accelerates mRNA decay via increased m⁶A deposition. Losing both H3K27ac and H3K4me3 rapidly abolishes transcriptional activity, while preserving H3K4me3 without H3K27ac can partially sustain transcription. These findings reveal a functional hierarchy and interdependence between H3K27ac and H3K4me3 in different transcription stages at the tested gene loci. This versatile tool will contribute to the functional dissection of the temporal dynamics of chromatin modifications in gene regulation.

Introduction

Regulation of gene expression is fundamental to cellular function and responses to environmental cues1,2. A key underlying mechanism is histone modification-mediated remodeling of chromatin that shapes DNA accessibility and transcriptional activity3–5. Among these modifications, histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) and histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) are highly enriched at active promoters and frequently colocalize, suggesting a functional synergy in transcription6–10. H3K27ac is known to remodel chromatin structure and facilitate the recruitment of transcriptional machinery, including RNA polymerase II (pol-II), thereby promoting transcription initiation11–14. Moreover, H3K27ac-associated bromodomain proteins such as bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4), recruit positive transcription elongation factor b (p-TEFb), which phosphorylates RNA pol-II and promotes its pause-release15–17. H3K4me3 has been recognized as a hallmark of transcription initiation through the recruitment of plant homeodomain (PHD)-containing proteins such as TBP-associated factor 3 (TAF3)18–20. More recently, H3K4me3 has been shown to recruit integrator complex subunit 11 (INTS11), which evicts paused RNA pol-II and promotes productive elongation19,21. H3K4me3 has also been reported to regulate transcription in a context-dependent manner22. Ectopic H3K4me3 writing studies have yielded conflicting results regarding whether H3K4me3 is sufficient to induce transcription22–24. However, the relationship between H3K27ac and H3K4me3 during transcription remains not completely understood.

CRISPR-based epigenome editing technologies have enabled programmable, locus-specific manipulation of chromatin states. Nuclease-dead Cas9 (dCas9) has been fused to chromatin-modifying effectors to achieve sequence-specific editing of histone modifications25–28. To interrogate the functional and temporal interplay between H3K27ac and H3K4me3 during transcription, in this study, we developed a dual chemically inducible dCas9-based epigenome editing system that integrates orthogonal chemically-induced proximity (CIP) modules within a dCas9 targeting framework29–32, which enables rapid, reversible, and stage-specific independent modulation of H3K27ac and H3K4me3 at defined genomic loci with a minute-scale temporal resolution. We found that, at the studied gene loci, H3K27ac acts as the primary upstream trigger of transcriptional activation, which is required for transcription initiation, promoter-proximal pausing, pause-release, and productive elongation. H3K4me3, deposited downstream of H3K27ac, plays critical supporting roles in facilitating RNA pol-II recruitment and stabilizing pre-initiation complex assembly through TAF3 recruitment at initiation, limits promoter-proximal pausing, and promotes efficient pause-release and productive elongation. Moreover, H3K4me3 can partially preserve the transcriptional state established by H3K27ac, it cannot substitute H3K27ac in driving full scale transcription. Beyond its transcriptional roles, H3K4me3 also enhances mRNA stability by restricting co-transcriptional m⁶A deposition through the recruitment of INTS11, which antagonizes METTL3 chromatin occupancy.

Results

H3K4me3 is required for optimal transcription initiated by H3K27ac

To determine the contribution of H3K4me3 to H3K27ac-driven gene expression, we used a reported dCas9-P300(CD) acetyltransferase fusion protein to deposit H3K27ac (Supplementary Fig. 1a) at the promoter region of IL1RN gene, which is transcriptionally silent and lacks significant endogenous enrichment of H3K27ac and H3K4me3 (Supplementary Fig. 1b)33,34. We cloned a fusion protein of dCas9 with the catalytic domain of H3K4me3/me2 demethylase35–38 KDM5B to erase H3K4me3 (Supplementary Fig. 1a)39–42. We transfected HEK293T cells with either dCas9-P300(CD) (DF1), dCas9-PYL (DF3, a negative control24,43), or both dCas9-P300(CD) and dCas9-KDM5B(CD) (DF1 + DF2), and four single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting the IL1RN promoter (Supplementary Fig. 1c)34. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assays were used to quantify the enrichment of H3K27ac and H3K4me3 near the transcription start site (TSS). We observed that dCas9-P300(CD) alone increased both H3K27ac and H3K4me3 levels at the targeted IL1RN promoter24, while co-expression of dCas9-P300(CD) and dCas9-KDM5B(CD) resulted in complete depletion of H3K4me3 without affecting H3K27ac (Supplementary Fig. 1d). We quantified IL1RN mRNA levels by reverse transcription qPCR (RT-qPCR) and observed that while H3K27ac alone led to robust expression of IL1RN, the simultaneous removal of H3K4me3 caused a significant reduction in IL1RN expression, although not completely abolishing it (Supplementary Fig. 1e). These results indicate that H3K4me3 plays a key role in supporting transcription.

Establishing a dual inducible editing system for independent H3K27ac and H3K4me3 editing

We suspect that the abundant endogenous FKBP proteins may compete with FKBP-fusion proteins for HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD) binding39,40. As a result, we cloned HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD) driven by stronger promoters, including pCAG (V2-ID7) and pCMV (V3-ID7) (Supplementary Fig. 3a)44. Combining V2-ID4 (dCas9-PYL-3×FKBP) with V1-ID6 (Flag-ABI-P300(CD)), and V1/2/3-ID7 (HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD) with different promoters), we observed that HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD) driven by the high-expression promoters (V2-ID7 and V3-ID7) significantly reduced gene expression upon ABA+Rap treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3b), indicating successful H3K4me3 removal. ChIP-qPCR analyses confirmed that adding ABA alone recruited Flag-ABI-P300(CD) and increased H3K27ac and H3K4me3 enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Rap alone recruited HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD) with no effects on H3K27ac or H3K4me3 (Supplementary Fig. 3c). ABA+Rap enriched Flag-ABI-P300(CD), HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD) and H3K27ac, and depleted H3K4me3 (Supplementary Fig. 3c). However, both Flag-ABI-P300(CD) and H3K27ac levels were significantly reduced compared to ABA alone, with no decrease in HA–FRB–KDM5B(CD) recruitment (Supplementary Fig. 3c), indicating a disruption of Flag-ABI-P300(CD) recruitment and H3K27ac editing, potentially due to the spatial interference from the co-recruited HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD).

To address this issue, we decreased the number of FKBP from 3 copies to one (V2-ID8, Fig. 1b) to reduce the number of HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD) being recruited. We then tested the H3K27ac and H3K4me3 co-editing using V2-ID8 combined with V1-ID6 and V2-ID7 and found that when both ABA and Rap were applied, both Flag-ABI-P300(CD) and HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD) were efficiently recruited, with a robust H3K27ac enrichment and complete depletion of H3K4me3 (Fig. 1c), which also reduced IL1RN expression (Fig. 1d).

To confirm that the induced changes in H3K27ac and H3K4me3 by our method were comparable to physiological chromatin states, we compared the enrichment of both marks at the IL1RN promoter to those at other endogenous loci. We observed that the induced enrichments of both histone marks were comparable to those at the promoter of the moderately active AKT1 gene, significantly higher than those at the transcriptionally silent POU5F1 gene locus, but lower than the levels at the highly expressed GAPDH promoter (Supplementary Fig. 4). These results indicate that the induced H3K27ac and H3K4me3 were within the physiological range.

To confirm that the observed changes in gene expression were specifically due to H3K4me3 depletion rather than non-catalytic scaffolding effects of the recruited KDM5B domain, we generated a catalytically inactive FRB-KDM5B(H499A/E501A) mutant41. Despite efficient recruitment of the inactive mutant to the IL1RN locus by Rap, no changes in H3K27ac, H3K4me3 or IL1RN expression were observed (Supplementary Fig. 5a and b). These results confirm that the observed reduction in gene expression was due to the removal of H3K4me3, instead of the scaffolding effects of the recruited KDM5B domain.

To further confirm that the observed H3K27ac and H3K4me3 changes were not caused by the overexpression of the effector domains alone, we compared cells co-transfected with the dual editing modules plus IL1RN-targeting sgRNAs or non-targeting (nt) sgRNAs (or non-transfected as a negative control). Changes in H3K27ac, H3K4me3, and IL1RN mRNA levels were observed exclusively in cells expressing IL1RN-targeting sgRNAs, but not in nt-sgRNAs samples (Supplementary Fig. 6a and b). These results demonstrate that the chromatin and transcriptional effects of the dual editing system are strictly dependent on locus-specific dCas9 targeting.

Loss of H3K4me3 reduces the rate of transcription and the stability of mRNA

To monitor H3K27ac-induced gene expression time course in the presence or absence of H3K4me3, we transfected cells with dual-editing modules (V1-ID6, V2-ID8 and V2-ID7) and IL1RN-targeting sgRNAs for 24 h, then treated cells with ABA, ABA+Rap, or DMSO, and harvested at the indicated time points within 48 h post-treatment (Fig. 1e), followed by RT-qPCR analyses. Compared to cells treated with ABA alone (H3K27ac+/H3K4me3+), cells treated with both ABA and Rap (H3K27ac+/H3K4me3-) showed a significant reduction in steady-state mRNA level (Fig. 1f).

Steady-state mRNA abundance reflects the balance between mRNA synthesis and degradation42. To assess if H3K4me3 influences mRNA degradation, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with dual-editing modules and IL1RN targeting sgRNAs for 24 h, followed by treating with ABA alone, ABA+Rap, or DMSO for another 24 h. Actinomycin D, an inhibitor for transcription42, was then added to block new RNA synthesis, and cells were harvested at multiple time points after treatment (Fig. 1g) and subjected to RT-qPCR assays to measure the mRNA levels and calculate its half-life under each condition. We observed that the half-life of IL1RN mRNA was 3.97 h in ABA-treated cells and 2.92 h in cells treated with ABA+Rap (Fig. 1h), indicating a faster decay rate upon H3K4me3 depletion. We further calculated the mRNA production rate under each condition using the relationship that at steady state, mRNA abundance equals the production rate divided by the degradation rate45, by combining the steady-state mRNA levels derived from the ABA or ABA+Rap induced time-course assay (Fig. 1e, f) with the degradation rates derived from the actinomycin D time-course assay (Fig. 1g, h). This analysis revealed that H3K4me3 depletion reduced the mRNA production rate (Fig. 1i), indicating that H3K4me3 contributes to maintaining the full rate of mRNA synthesis.

To examine whether these findings were observed at other gene loci and cell types, we performed similar experiments at GRM2 and MYOD1 gene loci in HEK293T cells, and at the IL1RN locus in HeLa cells. At the GRM2 and MYOD1 loci, we confirmed that H3K27ac and H3K4me3 can be successfully edited (Supplementary Fig. 7b and g), and H3K4me3 depletion reduced the steady-state mRNA level for both genes (Supplementary Fig. 7c and h). Actinomycin D-treated experiments revealed that H3K4me3 depletion accelerated mRNA decay at both loci (Supplementary Fig. 7d and i). Calculation of the mRNA production rates also revealed slower mRNA synthesis upon H3K4me3 depletion at both loci (Supplementary Fig. 7e and j). In HeLa cells, H3K4me3 depletion similarly reduced the steady-state IL1RN mRNA level, accelerated mRNA decay, and decreased the mRNA production rate (Supplementary Fig. 8). Overall, these results indicate that H3K4me3 is required not only to maintain the full rate of mRNA synthesis but also to stabilize transcribed mRNAs among the tested gene loci and cell types.

H3K27ac triggers transcription initiation and sequential recruitment of transcriptional machineries

Transcription proceeds rapidly through distinct stages. Initiation occurs within minutes of activation by recruiting RNA pol-II and its phosphorylation at Ser5 of the C-terminal domain (CTD) (RNA pol-II Ser5Ph)46–48, followed by promoter-proximal pausing within minutes stabilized by Negative Elongation Factor (NELF)49–51, where RNA pol-II remains stalled for several minutes before being released into productive elongation by positive transcription elongation factor b (p-TEFb), which phosphorylates NELF, and RNA pol-II CTD at Ser2 (RNA pol-II Ser2Ph)52,53. Elongation follows and completes transcription depending on gene length47,54. H3K4me3 has been shown to recruit transcription regulators to facilitate transcription initiation and RNA pol II promoter proximal pause-release18,21.

To investigate whether H3K4me3 affect any of these stages, we planned to deplete H3K4me3 at specific transcription stages after gene activation induced by H3K27ac. The CIP-integrated epigenome editing methods have been shown to be fast and able to remodel chromatin within minutes55,56. To confirm the fast kinetics of our editing system, we characterized the kinetics of ABA-induced recruitment of Flag-ABI-P300(CD), the installation of H3K27ac, and the resulting increase of H3K4me3 (Fig. 2a). HEK293T cells were co-transfected with Flag-ABI-P300(CD), PYL-dCas9-FKBP-V5, and IL1RN-targeting sgRNAs for 48 h, then treated with ABA and harvested over a short time course (5−60 min after ABA addition, Supplementary Fig. 9a). ChIP-qPCR analyses revealed the rapid recruitment of Flag-ABI-P300(CD) to the gene locus within 5 min (Supplementary Fig. 9b), coinciding with immediate H3K27ac deposition within 5 min at the locus (Supplementary Fig. 9b). We next mapped the timing and order of deposition of H3K27ac, H3K4me3 and the recruitment of stage-specific transcriptional regulators along the transcription time course. According to established models, initiation and promoter-proximal pause factors such as RNA pol-II Ser5P and NELF localized near the TSS and shifted downstream as elongation-associated factors including p-TEFb and RNA pol-II Ser2Ph progressively accumulated, reflecting the transition from initiation to productive elongation57–59. To capture this spatial progression, we selected primer sites 100 bp upstream of the TSS and 100, 200, and 300 bp downstream to monitor initiation, pausing, pause-release, and elongation, respectively. We transfected cells with Flag-ABI-P300(CD), PYL-dCas9-FKBP-V5, and IL1RN-targeting sgRNAs for 48 h, then treated cells with ABA over the indicated time course before analyzed by ChIP-qPCR for the enrichment of H3K27ac, H3K4me3 and stage-specific regulator around TSS along the time course (Fig. 2a, b). We defined a significant increase at each time point and at each locus when the p-value (calculated from the two-way ANOVA statistical analyses) for the enrichment fold change under each condition was <0.05 when compared to no ABA treatment. We found that H3K27ac was significantly enriched within 5 min following ABA treatment at both upstream ( − 100 bp) and downstream ( + 100 to 300 bp) of the TSS (Fig. 2c). H3K4me3 deposition reached significance at 10 min at the −100 bp position but not at other sites at this time point. By 15 min, all positions displayed significant enrichment (Fig. 2c). To define the onset of each transcription stage, we identified it based on the earliest time point showing significant enrichment of the corresponding stage-specific transcription regulators. The initiation marker RNA pol-II Ser5Ph was significantly enriched by 10 min at −100 bp and +100 bp, while the enrichment at downstream sites was not detected at this time (Fig. 2c). We probed the promoter-proximal pause marker NELF complex (by its subunit TH1L), which was enriched at +100 bp by 15 min but not at other sites at 15 min (Fig. 2c). The pause-release and elongation marker p-TEFb complex (by its subunit Cyclin-T1) was first enriched at +100 bp at 20 min and subsequently at +200 bp and +300 bp by 30 min (Fig. 2c). Consistent with this, the elongation marker RNA pol-II Ser2Ph was detected at +100 bp at 20 min and shifted downstream to +200 bp and +300 bp by 30 min (Fig. 2c). The temporal lag in p-TEFb and RNA pol II Ser2Ph enrichment between +100 bp and +200 bp is consistent with the time required for pol II to traverse the initial nucleosome barrier57–59. We further applied nonlinear regression analyses using ChIP-qPCR time-course data to estimate the time required to reach 50% maximal enrichment (t50%) for each modification and regulator (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 9c). H3K27ac was the first to accumulate (t50% = 9.33 min at −100 bp), followed by RNA pol-II Ser5Ph (t50% = 12.47 min at −100 bp). H3K4me3 followed initiation (t50% = 16.04 min at −100 bp and 19.54 min at +100 bp) and was closely coincident with NELF recruitment (t50% = 18.81 min at +100 bp). Pause-release and elongation followed with p-TEFb and RNA pol-II Ser2Ph reaching 50% enrichment at +100 bp around 28 min and delayed accumulation downstream (t50% = 42.19 and 47.73 min at +200/ + 300 bp). Overall, the observed timeline of H3K27ac and H3K4me3 deposition as well as the recruitment sequence of transcription stage markers closely mirror the canonical transcription progress, providing a precise temporal map of ABA-induced transcriptional events at the IL1RN locus.

The effects of H3K4me3 on initiation, pausing, pause-release and elongation during transcription

We next employed our dual editing system to investigate the stage-specific function of H3K4me3 at the IL1RN locus. To confirm that H3K4me3 can be erased rapidly, we determined the kinetics of Rap-induced recruitment of HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD) and H3K4me3 depletion in H3K27ac-induced gene activation. Cells were co-transfected with Flag-ABI-P300(CD), HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD), PYL-dCas9-FKBP-V5 and IL1RN sgRNA for 48 h, followed by ABA treatment to induce H3K27ac/H3K4me3 deposition and transcription. 60 min after ABA addition, Rap was added to remove H3K4me3, and cells were harvested across a short time course (5−60 min after Rap addition, Supplementary Fig. 10a). ChIP-qPCR analyses revealed the rapid recruitment of HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD) and reduction of H3K4me3 within 5 min after Rap addition (Supplementary Fig. 10b), which supported the feasibility of using this tool within the expected transcription timeframe.

To examine how the H3K27ac-driven transcription process proceeds in the absence of H3K4me3, we defined the precise time course of transcription stages following ABA addition, including initiation (10 min), promoter-proximal pausing (20 min), pause-release (30 min), and elongation (45 min). These time points represent the approximate midpoint of each stage based on the determined t50% value (Fig. 2d), ensuring that the chosen time points capture periods when each process is actively engaged. We used our dual editing tool to remove H3K4me3 from the very beginning during H3K27ac-driven transcription and observed the resulting impacts on each transcription stage. Cells were transfected with the dual editing modules and treated with either ABA (H3K27ac+/H3K4me3+) or ABA+Rap (H3K27ac+/H3K4me3-) for 10, 20, 30 and 45 min before harvesting and ChIP-qPCR analyses (Fig. 3a). In cells treated with ABA+Rap, H3K4me3 was efficiently depleted while H3K27ac remained intact (Fig. 3b). At the transcription initiation stage (10 min), RNA pol-II Ser5Ph was enriched at the TSS −100 bp site in cells treated with ABA alone (with intact H3K4me3), while in ABA+Rap treated cells (without H3K4me3), RNA pol-II Ser5Ph was not enriched at the same site until 20 min post-treatment (Fig. 3b). At the promoter-proximal pausing stage, the timing of NELF recruitment at +100 bp was observed to be around 20 min in ABA+Rap treated cells, with no detectable delay compared to the ABA alone condition (Fig. 3b). However, the relative enrichment level of NELF in ABA+Rap treated cells was increased at this position compared to cells with ABA alone, suggesting enhanced stabilization of promoter-proximal pausing in the absence of H3K4me3 (Fig. 3b). To precisely dissect the impact of H3K4me3 at each specific transcription stage, we performed stage-specific H3K4me3 removal by adding Rap 10 min prior to each defined stage time point. Cells were transfected as described above and treated first with ABA to induce the transcription, then with Rap at 10 min prior to indicated time points corresponding to different transcription stages before harvesting for ChIP-qPCR assays (treated at 10, 20, and 35 min and harvested at 20, 30, and 45 min) (Fig. 3c). Under these conditions, H3K27ac remained unaffected while H3K4me3 was efficiently erased at each stage (Fig. 3d, f). Acute, stage-specific depletion of H3K4me3 led to a significant increase in NELF recruitment at the TSS + 100 bp site when compared to cell treated with ABA alone the entire period (Fig. 3d), indicating an enhanced stabilization of promoter-proximal pausing when losing H3K4me3. For pause-release and early elongation stages, we observed approximately significant reductions in the recruitment of p-TEFb and RNA pol-II Ser2Ph, respectively, at the TSS + 100 bp position (Fig. 3e). Productive elongation was also impaired when losing H3K4me3, with RNA pol-II Ser2Ph enrichment reduced significantly at both +200 and +300 bp downstream of TSS (Fig. 3f). Collectively, these results reveal critical and direct roles of H3K4me3 in pausing, pause-release, and elongation stages of transcription.

To validate these findings at a different gene, we performed similar experiments at the GRM2 gene locus. We first mapped the temporal sequence of transcription regulator recruitment following ABA-induced H3K27ac deposition across the GRM2 locus. ChIP-qPCR time-course analyses revealed that the sequential recruitment of stage-specific transcription regulators, with t50% values of 14.63 min for RNA pol-II Ser5Ph (TSS − 150 bp), 23.64 min for NELF (TSS + 150 bp), 30.41 min for p-TEFb (TSS + 150 bp), and 44.12 min for RNA pol-II Ser2Ph (TSS + 300 bp) (Supplementary Fig. 11a and 11b), consistent with the temporal progression observed at the IL1RN locus. Using these defined time points, we performed stage-specific H3K4me3 depletion experiments at GRM2. Consistent with our observations at IL1RN, H3K4me3 depletion at the initiation stage reduced RNA pol-II Ser5Ph recruitment at the GRM2 TSS − 150 bp position. (Supplementary Fig. 11c). At the pausing stage, H3K4me3 depletion led to a significant increase in NELF recruitment at TSS + 150 bp (Supplementary Fig. 11d). At the pause-release stage, reduced p-TEFb recruitment was observed upon H3K4me3 depletion at TSS + 150 bp (Supplementary Fig. 11e), and at the elongation stage, H3K4me3 depletion caused a significant reduction in RNA pol-II Ser2Ph enrichment at TSS + 300 bp (Supplementary Fig. 11f). These results demonstrate the consistent regulatory roles of H3K4me3 in different transcription stage as observed at the IL1RN locus.

To further investigate the role of H3K4me3 in transcription initiation, we examined the recruitment of TAF3, a PHD finger-containing component of the TFIID complex that directly binds H3K4me3 and serves as a marker for pre-initiation complex (PIC) assembly18. Using our dual inducible system (Fig. 1a), we observed that ABA-induced H3K27ac and H3K4me3 deposition led to significant TAF3 recruitment at the IL1RN promoter, while acute depletion of H3K4me3 by Rap addition significantly reduced TAF3 enrichment compared to ABA alone treatment (Supplementary Fig. 12). These results demonstrate that H3K4me3 contributes to the stabilization of the TFIID complex at the promoter and participates in initiation in the presence of H3K27ac.

Establishment of the H3K27ac and H3K4me3 co-writing platform

After investigating the direct effects of H3K4me3 on specific transcriptional stages, we investigated whether H3K27ac contributes to later stages in transcription beyond initiation. A key advantage of the ABA-inducible system is its reversibility31,43, which allows us to rapidly remove H3K27ac at specific transcription stage and study the resulting impacts. To confirm that the depletion of H3K27ac can occur in a timely fashion after ABA removal, we examined the kinetics regarding the release of Flag-ABI-P300(CD) from the induced IL1RN locus following ABA washout, along with the subsequent changes of H3K27ac and H3K4me3 (Supplementary Fig. 13a). We co-transfected cells with Flag-ABI-P300(CD), PYL-dCas9-FKBP-V5, and IL1RN sgRNAs for 48 h, then treated cells with ABA for 1 h to induce H3K27ac and H3K4me3 writing. ABA was then removed by washing cells with fresh medium, and cells were incubated for an additional 5, 15, 30 or 60 min without ABA before being harvested and analyzed by ChIP–qPCR assays (Supplementary Fig. 13b). We observed that the disassociation of Flag-ABI-P300(CD) and the reduction of H3K27ac and H3K4me3 at the IL1RN locus all occurred within 5 min after ABA removal (Supplementary Fig. 13c), which demonstrated the highly dynamic nature of both modifications and the fast kinetics of our editing system.

We next sought to remove H3K27ac at each defined transcription stage to investigate its roles beyond initiation. In addition, as opposed to our studies above to dissect the role of H3K4me3 in transcription with the presence of H3K27ac, we want to examine the impact of H3K4me3 in transcription in the absence of H3K27ac. Since H3K4me3 quickly declined upon H3K27ac depletion (Supplementary Fig. 13c), to maintain H3K4me3 without H3K27ac, we engineered another dual editing system in which the Rap-inducible FRB–KDM5B(CD) eraser used in the studies above was replaced with a fusion of FRB to the H3K4me3-specific histone methyltransferase SET1A catalytic domain (SET(CD)) that allowed us to write H3K4me3 when H3K27ac disappeared (Supplementary Fig. 14a and b)60. To validate this system, cells were co-transfected with Flag-ABI-P300(CD), HA-FRB-SET(CD), PYL-dCas9-FKBP-V5 and IL1RN sgRNA for 24 h and then treated with ABA, Rap, ABA+Rap, or DMSO for another 24 h, before analyzing by ChIP-qPCR. We confirmed that ABA treatment efficiently recruited Flag-ABI-P300(CD), resulting in increased H3K27ac, H3K4me3, and IL1RN expression (Supplementary Fig. 14c and d). Rap alone recruited HA-FRB-SET(CD), leading to elevated H3K4me3 but not H3K27ac, and failed to activate IL1RN expression. Co-treatment with ABA and Rap efficiently recruited both Flag-ABI-P300(CD) and HA-FRB-SET(CD), leading to H3K27ac and H3K4me3 writing and IL1RN expression (Supplementary Fig. 14c and d). We further validated the kinetics of HA-FRB-SET(CD) recruitment and H3K4me3 writing. We transfected cells were with HA-FRB-SET(CD), PYL-dCas9-FKBP-V5 and IL1RN-targeting sgRNAs and treated cells with Rap for 5, 15, 30 and 60 min before harvesting cells for ChIP-qPCR analyses (Supplementary Fig. 15a). We observed that both FRB-SET(CD) recruitment and H3K4me3 writing rapidly occurred within 5 min (Supplementary Fig. 15b), confirming the fast kinetics of the H3K4me3 writing platform.

We observed that the Rap-induced H3K4me3 enrichment achieved by the inducible FRB-SET(CD) system was relatively modest ( < 3 folds). To confirm that the deposited H3K4me3 level was within the physiologically relevant range, we compared H3K4me3 enrichment at the IL1RN promoter to other endogenous gene loci. We observed that the induced H3K4me3 enrichment was comparable to the H3K4me3 level at the promoter of the moderately expressed AKT1 gene, significantly higher than that at the transcriptionally silent POU5F1 locus, but lower than the level observed at the highly expressed GAPDH promoter (Supplementary Fig. 16). Moreover, we have previously shown that using a constitutive dCas9-SET(CD) system, even with a higher induced H3K4me3 enrichment ( > 10 folds), H3K4me3 deposition alone was insufficient to activate the tested genes, including IL1RN and GRM224. To determine if H3K4me3 deposition alone was sufficient to induce the assembly of PIC, we determined the H3K4me3-dependent enrichment of TAF3 at the IL1RN locus using the constitutive dCas9-SET(CD) system. We observed that even with high H3K4me3 enrichment, H3K4me3 deposition alone was insufficient to recruit TAF3 at the IL1RN locus (Supplementary Fig. 17). These results indicate that while H3K4me3 can stabilize PIC assembly during active transcription, it cannot independently initiate this process without H3K27ac.

It is known that m⁶A modification can lead to diverse functional consequences besides affecting RNA stability, depending on the transcript and cellular context63. To test whether the observed m⁶A increase on IL1RN mRNA led to its destabilization, we employed an orthogonal dCas13b-based m⁶A editing system that we were able to increase m6A independent of H3K4me3 changes (Fig. 5c)64. We transfected cells with dCas9-P300(CD) and sgRNAs targeting IL1RN promoter to induce IL1RN expression in HEK293T cells. We also quantify the changes in IL1RN mRNA levels under each condition and observed a reduction in IL1RN mRNA level when m⁶A was increased (Fig. 5d). These results indicate that m⁶A on IL1RN facilitates mRNA decay, which phenocopies the effect observed upon H3K4me3 depletion.

To further investigate the potential mechanism linking H3K4me3 depletion to increased m6A and METTL3 enrichments, we examined the involvement of the integrator complex subunit INTS11. It has been shown that METTL3-dependent m6A deposition antagonizes the chromatin localization of INTS11 through m6A binders, suggesting potential spatial exclusion between INTS11 on METTL3 on chromatin66. Moreover, H3K4me3 has been shown to be required for recruiting INTS1121, raising the possibility that H3K4me3 depletion reduces INTS11 occupancy at the IL1RN locus, thereby enabling increased METTL3 localization at the site. To examine this possibility, we transfected cells with Flag-ABI-P300(CD), HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD), PYL-dCas9-FKBP-V5 and IL1RN-targeting sgRNAs for 24 h, followed by treating cells with ABA, ABA+Rap, or DMSO for 24 h before harvesting cells for ChIP-qPCR experiments to determine INTS11 enrichment. We observed that H3K4me3 depletion reduced INTS11 enrichment around IL1RN TSS (Fig. 5e), suggesting a possible connection between H3K4me3 depletion and enhanced METTL3 recruitment through reduced INTS11 occupancy.

To directly test whether INTS11 influences METTL3 recruitment and mRNA stability, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of INTS11 in HEK293T cells. We tested two INTS11 siRNAs for INTS11 knockdown and identified the most effective siRNA (siRNA-2) by western blot analysis (Supplementary Fig. 19b). To confirm the METTL3 expression was not affected after INTS11 knockdown, we also examined the METTL3 protein levels by western blot analysis and observed no changes (Supplementary Fig. 19c). We then transfected cells with INTS11 siRNA-2 (or scrambled siRNA as a negative control), PYL-dCas9-FKBP, ABI-P300(CD) and IL1RN-targeting sgRNAs for 48 h. ABA was then added to induce IL1RN expression for 24 h before harvesting cells for METTL3 ChIP-qPCR analyses. The changes in IL1RN mRNA stability under each condition were also determined using actinomycin D treatment (Fig. 5f). We observed that INTS11 knockdown led to a significant increase in METTL3 enrichment at the IL1RN locus when compared to cells transfected with scrambled siRNA (Fig. 5g). Furthermore, we observed an accelerated IL1RN mRNA decay when INTS11 was knocked down (Fig. 5h). These results suggest a mechanistic connection between H3K4me3 and m6A through the interplay of INTS11 and METTL3 that determine the stability of nascent transcripts.

Discussion

Deciphering how chromatin modifications cooperatively regulate transcription dynamics is central to understanding gene expression mechanisms. Previous studies have implicated H3K27ac in establishing a permissive chromatin environment that facilitates transcription initiation11,13,14, while whether H3K4me3 can initiate transcription is highly context dependent22–24. H3K27ac deposition has been shown to precede and promote H3K4me3 deposition24. However, a recent study showed that H3K4me3 writing increases H3K27ac22. H3K4me3 has been shown to contribute to multiple phases of transcription, including initiation, promoter-proximal pause-release, and elongation, which can independently or coordinately regulate transcription progression and speed18,20,21,67. Most studies focus on the individual roles of H3K4me3 or H3K27ac in regulating transcription rather than their interplay8,18,21. Methods used to study specific transcription stages after initiation often rely on transcription stage-specific inhibitors (e.g., triptolide for initiation, DRB or flavopiridol for pause-release), which globally disrupt the transcription machinery and influence many genes simultaneously21,68–70. Furthermore, most studies examining the roles of individual epigenetic marks have been conducted under steady-state conditions, when robust gene expression has already been established, potentially overlooking regulatory events that occur during the process of establishing transcription21,68. Overall, the mechanistic hierarchy, interdependence, temporal dynamics, and transcription stage-specific roles of H3K4me3 and H3K27ac during the process of transcriptional activation have not been fully understood.

In this study, we developed a dual, chemically inducible dCas9-based epigenome editing system that enables rapid, reversible, and independent editing of H3K4me3 and H3K27ac at specific gene loci and at levels comparable to other endogenous gene loci. This system provides unique opportunities to temporally and precisely tune chromatin states at chosen gene loci, offering insight into how specific histone modifications cooperatively shape transcriptional outcomes. Our findings reveal that the depletion of H3K4me3 during H3K27ac-driven transcription significantly reduces steady-state mRNA abundance at multiple gene loci in both HEK293T and HeLa cells, which arises from a combination of reduced mRNA production rate and accelerated mRNA decay. These results demonstrate that H3K4me3 regulates gene activity both transcriptionally and post-transcriptionally. Furthermore, using this dual epigenome editing system, we had the unique opportunity to initiate transcription of a specific gene at a defined time point by ectopically writing H3K27ac and follow transcriptional progression in the presence or absence of another epigenetic modification, H3K4me3, at distinct transcription stages during the first hour of gene activation, well before steady-state is reached. This approach provides unique insight into the roles of H3K27ac and H3K4me3 during the early activation period of a specific gene. Following the temporal accumulation of transcription stage-specific markers, we defined the timing and sequence of each transcription stage within the first hour of gene activation at IL1RN and GRM2 loci. Coupling this temporal framework with the rapid inducibility of our dual editing tool, we achieved temporal and stage-specific removal of H3K4me3 at defined gene loci and found that the loss of H3K4me3 impairs RNA pol-II recruitment and initiation, enhances NELF-mediated pausing and impedes pause-release and productive elongation at both IL1RN and GRM2 loci. These findings support a model where H3K4me3 acts as a key regulator of transcriptional progression by promoting initiation, pause-release, and elongation18,21. It has been suggested that H3K4me3 regulate the transcription in a dosage dependent manner22. We found that even when using a constitutive dCas9-SET(CD) system to deposit a high level of H3K4me3, without H3K27ac, TAF3 recruitment (an indicator of PIC assembly) was not detected, confirming that H3K4me3 alone cannot independently initiate transcription without H3K27ac. Furthermore, the acute depletion of H3K4me3 during active transcription significantly reduced TAF3 enrichment, demonstrating that H3K4me3 contributes to the stabilization of the TFIID complex and PIC maintenance once transcription has been activated.

Leveraging the rapid and reversible nature of our method, we acutely removed H3K27ac while maintaining H3K4me3 at distinct transcription stages at IL1RN and GRM2 loci. We found that removing H3K27ac impairs the progression through transcription stages, confirming that H3K27ac is not only essential for transcription initiation but also plays key roles in promoter-proximal pausing, pause-release, and productive elongation. These results align with previous studies showing that H3K27ac recruits bromodomain-containing proteins such as BRD4, which engage mediators and p-TEFb to sustain transcriptional activities16,71. Although H3K4me3 alone could not sustain full transcriptional output after H3K27ac removal, it preserves the transcriptional activity established by H3K27ac at both the pause-release and elongation stages, suggesting that H3K4me3 may stabilize a poised or partially active transcriptional state. These findings are consistent with recent studies showing that H3K4me3 reinforces RNA pol-II engagement and helps protect partially assembled transcription complexes from premature dissociation21.

We observed that H3K4me3 depletion did not fully abolish transcription, suggesting that H3K27ac alone is sufficient to maintain basal gene expression through alternative regulatory pathways. One such mechanism may involve enhancer-promoter communication mediated by bromodomain-containing proteins such as BRD2 and BRD4, which recognize H3K27ac and facilitate enhancer-promoter looping14,72–75. Previous studies have demonstrated that the deposition of H3K27ac at enhancers is sufficient to drive gene activation34,76–79. This may offer a potential alternative transcriptional regulation pathway not involving H3K4me3, which is not enriched at enhancer regions9. However, the precise mechanism of this compensatory pathway remain to be defined.

To contextualize the chromatin environment at the tested loci with the observed regulatory roles of H3K27ac and H3K4me3, we examined endogenous enrichment of H3K27ac, H3K4me3, H3K27me3, and H3K9me3, together with CpG island annotation, at the IL1RN, GRM2, MYOD1 and other gene loci in HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 1b and 20). We observed that IL1RN exhibits minimal H3K27me3 enrichment, whereas GRM2 and MYOD1 display moderate levels. All three loci show comparable, moderate enrichment of H3K9me3, suggesting a shared baseline of heterochromatic features. Given prior evidence that the function of H3K4me3 can be influenced by DNA methylation status23,80, we examined the DNA methylation and CpG island configurations across these loci and found that IL1RN promoter is highly methylated and lacks a CpG island, MYOD1 contains a CpG island spanning the gene body with minimal DNA methylation, and GRM2 displays an intermediate state, with a promoter-associated CpG island and moderate DNA methylation. Taken together, these observations indicate that the targeted loci reside in divergent epigenetic contexts, suggesting that the observed effects of H3K4me3 and H3K27ac in our studies are not dictated by specific chromatin features.

Modifications on mRNAs, including m⁶A, have emerged as a key regulator to control mRNA stability. m⁶A can be recognized by human YTH domain family 2 (YTHDF2) protein to regulate mRNA degradation, thereby fine-tuning transcript abundance61. Increasing evidence indicates that chromatin states can influence m⁶A deposition, highlighting a direct connection between epigenome and epitranscriptome81. For example, H3K36me3 has been shown to guide METTL14/METTL3-mediated m⁶A deposition on nascent transcripts82. While prior studies have suggested that m⁶A on the RNA transcripts can feed back to affect H3K4me3 on chromatin81,83–86, whether H3K4me3 can influence m⁶A is unknown. Our results demonstrate that H3K4me3 depletion led to increased m⁶A deposition on IL1RN, GRM2, and MYOD1 mRNA transcripts. Further investigation at the IL1RN locus revealed that METTL3 was enriched on chromatin upon H3K4me3 removal. Using another CRISPR-based tool with dCas13b-METTL3 to install m⁶A within the 5’UTR on IL1RN transcripts, we observed the accelerated mRNA decay that phenocopied the effect of H3K4me3 depletion. We found that H3K4me3 depletion simultaneously reduced INTS11 enrichment and increased METTL3 occupancy around IL1RN TSS. siRNA-mediated INTS11 knockdown recapitulated the effects of H3K4me3 depletion, leading to increased METTL3 enrichment and accelerated mRNA decay at the IL1RN locus. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that METTL3-mediated m6A deposition impedes INTS11 from binding to the same chromatin sites66, and reveal the mechanistic interplay between H3K4me3 and mRNA stability, in which H3K4me3-dependent recruitment of INTS11 restricts METTL3 chromatin occupancy and limits co-transcriptional m⁶A deposition on nascent transcripts. These results demonstrate that H3K4me3 plays versatile roles in regulating gene expression beyond transcription, extending its functional impact to post-transcriptional gene regulation.

Some limitations of our study should be noted. First, the spatial resolution of our ChIP-qPCR analyses is constrained by the chromatin fragment size, which precludes nucleotide-level mapping of H3K4me3 dynamics. Methods such as ChIP-exo or CUT&Tag achieving higher-resolution could potentially address this limitation87,88. Second, although tracking RNA pol-II phosphorylation at promoter regions provides useful insights into transcription, the actual elongation rate can be more accurately determined by monitoring RNA pol-II progression across gene bodies using approaches such as ChIP-seq or PRO-seq89,90. Third, while the occupancy of transcription regulators informs transcription states, complementary approaches that monitor nascent RNA, such as PRO-seq and mNET-seq, will provide a more direct readout of transcriptional output91,92. Finally, this study was performed at selected gene loci and cell lines. Expanded studies in a larger set of genes with diverse transcription activities or genome-wide studies in multiple cell types will reveal how broadly these findings here can be applied.

In conclusion, our studies reveal the temporal hierarchy and functional interplay between H3K27ac and H3K4me3 across distinct transcription stages. H3K27ac acts as the primary upstream trigger of transcriptional activation, while H3K4me3 plays essential supporting roles in transcriptional initiation, pause-release, elongation, and co-transcriptionally determines mRNA stability. The dual-inducible epigenome editing system developed here provides a powerful and modular platform that can be adapted to study the functional relationships between other histone modifications during gene expression. Future studies incorporating additional epigenetic and transcriptional regulators will further illuminate how chromatin modifications orchestrate dynamic gene regulation in cellular processes and diseases.

Methods

Plasmid construction

All DNA fragments were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) from intermediate constructs using CloneAmp HiFi PCR Premix (Takara #639298) on an S1000 thermal cycler (Bio-Rad). Primer sequences used to generate individual constructs for dCas9, effector domains, and sgRNA nucleotide sequences are provided in the Supplementary Table 1 and 2.

Human codon-optimized Streptococcus pyogenes dCas9 containing three C-terminal SV40 nuclear localization signals (NLSs) was derived from a previously reported dCas9-PYL-HA construct43 and further engineered to incorporate a synthesized 3×FKBP sequence. For transcriptional effector fusions, Flag-ABI-P300(CD) (amino acids 1048–1664) was cloned and codon-optimized based on a previously reported Flag-ABI-P300(CD)43, with a flexible XTEN linker inserted between ABI and P300(CD). For Rap-inducible recruitment, the H3K4me3/2-specific demethylase KDM5B catalytic core domain (amino acids 3–846) was cloned from Addgene plasmid #143615 (a gift from Feng Zhang) to incorporate a synthesized FRB sequence. A catalytically inactive mutant, HA-FRB-KDM5B*(H499A/E501A), was generated by site-directed mutagenesis of the wild-type HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD) construct. The SET catalytic domain of SET1A (amino acids 1414–1707) was cloned from a previously reported dCas9-SET(CD) fusion24 to incorporate a synthesized FRB sequence.

The IL1RN, MYOD1 and GRM2 sgRNA have been described previously43. In brief, the backbone plasmid pU6-sgGAL4-1 (Addgene plasmid #46915) was digested with BstXI and XhoI to remove the sgGAL4-1 sequence, and the sgRNA-containing insert targeting IL1RN, MYOD1, or GRM2 was ligated in its place. sgRNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco #11995073) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biotechne|R&D systems #S11550), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco #15140122), and 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco #35050061) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Cells were seeded at a density of 4 × 10⁵ cells per ml in either 24-well plates or 10-cm dishes one day before transfection.

For transfection with the dual editing modules, 10-cm dishes were transfected with 4 µg PYL-dCas9-FKBP, 6 µg Flag-ABI-P300(CD), 6 µg HA-FRB-KDM5B(CD) or HA-FRB-SET(CD), and a total of 1 µg sgRNAs (250 ng each). Plasmid DNA was mixed with 36 µL P3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher #L3000015) in 500 µL Opti-MEM (Gibco #31985062), and in parallel, 36 µL Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher #L3000015) was diluted in 500 µL Opti-MEM. After 5 min, the two mixtures were combined and incubated for 15 min at room temperature before being added to the cells. Transfected cells were cultured for an additional 24–48 h prior to downstream assays. For 24-well plate transfections, 50 µL of the prepared transfection mixture was added per well.

For transfection of direct dCas9 fusion constructs (dCas9-P300(CD), dCas9-SET(CD), or dCas9-PYL), 10 cm dishes were transfected with 8 µg of the dCas9 plasmid and a total of 2 µg sgRNA plasmid (500 ng each). Plasmid DNA was mixed with 20 µL P3000 reagent in 500 µL Opti-MEM, and in parallel, 20 µL Lipofectamine 3000 was diluted in 500 µL Opti-MEM. After 5 min, the two mixtures were combined and incubated for 15 min at room temperature before being added to the cells. Transfected cells were cultured for an additional 48 h prior to downstream assays.

For co-transfection of dCas9-P300(CD) with dCas13b-METTL3, 10-cm dishes were transfected with 6 µg dCas9-P300(CD), 1.5 µg dCas9-specific sgRNA, 6 µg dCas13b-METTL3, and 1.5 µg dCas13b-coupled sgRNA targeting the IL1RN 5’UTR. Plasmid DNA was mixed with 30 µL P3000 reagent in 500 µL Opti-MEM, and in parallel, 30 µL Lipofectamine 3000 was diluted in 500 µL Opti-MEM. After 5 min, the two mixtures were combined and incubated for 15 min at room temperature before being added to the cells. Transfected cells were cultured for an additional 48 h prior to downstream assays.

For co-transfection of the dual inducible editing modules with siRNA, 10-cm dishes were transfected with 4 µg PYL-dCas9-FKBP, 6 µg Flag-ABI-P300(CD), 1 µg total sgRNA, and 300 pmol siRNA (either INTS11 siRNA or scramble siRNA as negative control). Plasmid DNA and siRNA were mixed with 40 µL P3000 reagent in 500 µL Opti-MEM, and in parallel, 40 µL Lipofectamine 3000 was diluted in 500 µL Opti-MEM. After 5 min, the two mixtures were combined and incubated for 15 min at room temperature before being added to the cells. Transfected cells were cultured for an additional 48 h prior to downstream assays to ensure efficient INTS11 knockdown prior to gene activation.

Western blot analysis

To confirm siRNA-mediated knockdown efficiency. HEK293T cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 10⁵ cells per mL 24 h before transfection. Cells were transfected with 300 pmol INTS11 siRNA or scramble siRNA using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions..

At 48 h post-transfection, cells were washed twice with 2 mL ice-cold PBS and lysed on ice with 100 µL RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher #89901) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher #89901). Lysates were gently vortexed and incubated on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce 660 nm Protein Assay (Thermo Fisher #22660) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Equal amounts of protein (40 µg per sample) were separated by SDS–PAGE at 120 V for 90 min and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Biorad #1620177) at 10 mA for 16 h at 4 °C. Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature in 5% non-fat milk in TBST, washed three times with TBST, and incubated for 2 h at room temperature with primary antibodies against INTS11 (abcam #ab75276), METTL3 (CST# 86132S), or GAPDH (#5174S). After three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with the HRP-conjugated anti-Rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (CST #7074) for 1 h, washed again, and visualized using the ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). GAPDH served as a loading control.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

HEK293T cells were seeded in 10 cm or 5 cm dishes at a density of 4 × 10⁵ cells per mL in culture medium 24 h prior to transfection. Cells were transfected with 4 µg PYL-dCas9-FKBP, 6 µg Flga-ABI-P300(CD), 6 µg HA-FRB–X [KDM5B(CD) or SET(CD)], and 2 µg sgRNA plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

At 24 or 48 h post-transfection, cells were treated with 100 µM abscisic acid (ABA; Glod Biotech #A-050-5), 20 nM rapamycin (Rap; SantaCruz#sc-3504), or an equal volume of DMSO as control, and incubated for 24 h or for the indicated shorter durations depending on the experimental design. At the designated time point, cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde (Sigma Aldrich#F8775) at room temperature for 10 min. Crosslinking was quenched by adding 1 mL of 1 M glycine (Fisher Scientific#AAJ16440736) to each 10 mL of culture medium and incubating for 5 min at room temperature. Media were removed, and cells were washed twice with 10 mL of ice-cold PBS. The small-molecule chemical probes Abscisic Acid (ABA) and Rapamycin used in this study were purchased from commercial sources with high purity as guaranteed by the manufacturers. Both molecules are well-characterized, universally accepted reference probes that meet established community requirements for chemical biology applications.

Cells were scraped in 1 mL ice-cold PBS supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (PIC; ThermoFisher#78438) and pelleted by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The pellets were resuspended in cell lysis buffer, incubated on ice for 10 min, and centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C; this step was repeated twice. Nuclei were lysed in nuclear lysis buffer for 10 min on ice, and chromatin was fragmented by sonication at 4 °C using a Bioruptor Pico (Diagenode) for 10 cycles of 30 s on/30 s off.

For each ChIP reaction, 100 µL of sheared chromatin was combined with 410 µL 1× ChIP buffer (CST#7008), the indicated ChIP-grade antibody (anti-H3K27ac CST#8173S; anti-H3K4me3 CST#9751S; Anti-Flag CST#14793S; Anti-HA CST#3724S; Anti-RNA pol II CTD Ser 5Ph CST#13499S; Anti-TH1L CST#12265S; Anti-CyclinT1 CST#81464S; Anti-RNA pol II CTD Ser2Ph CST#13523S; Anti-METTL3 CST#86132S; Anti-INTS11 abcam #ab75276), and PIC. 2% of each sample was saved as input control. Reactions were rotated overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with 30 µL ChIP-grade Protein G magnetic beads (CST#9006S) for 2 h at 4 °C. Beads were sequentially washed three times with 1 mL low-salt wash buffer (1× ChIP buffer) and once with 1 mL high-salt wash buffer (1× ChIP buffer supplemented with 70 µL of 5 M NaCl). Bound chromatin was eluted with 150 µL ChIP Elution Buffer at 65 °C for 30 min. Crosslinks were reversed by incubation at 65 °C for ≥2 h in the presence of proteinase K (Thermo Scientific #EO0491) and 6 µL of 5 M NaCl. DNA was purified using the ChIP DNA Purification Kit (CST#14209) and analyzed by qPCR.

Total RNA extraction and purification

Total RNA was extracted from HEK293T cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher#15596026) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with chloroform (Fisher Scientific#AAJ67241), isopropanol (Fisher Scientific#AC317270010), and 75% ethanol for phase separation and precipitation. Briefly, HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates or 10-cm dishes at a density of 4 × 10⁵ cells/mL 24 h prior to transfection. At 24 h post-transfection, cells were treated with 100 µM abscisic acid ABA (Glod Biotechnology #A-050-5), 20 nM Rap (Santa Cruz #sc-3504), or an equivalent volume of DMSO (negative control) and incubated for an additional 24 h. For large-scale preparations, cells cultured in 10-cm dishes were lysed directly with 2 mL TRIzol, collected by pipetting, and incubated on ice for 3–5 min. Chloroform (0.2 mL per 1 mL TRIzol) was added, samples were shaken vigorously for 2 min, incubated on ice for 10–15 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The aqueous phase was transferred to a fresh tube, and RNA was precipitated with isopropanol (0.5 mL per 1 mL TRIzol), incubated on ice for 10–30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Pellets were washed once with 75% ethanol, air dried, and resuspended in 50–100 μL nuclease-free water. Purified RNA was either immediately subjected to reverse transcription or stored at −80 °C.

RNA immunoprecipitation

Total RNA was extracted from cells cultured in 6-well plates using TRIzol reagent. For each reaction, 10 µg of total RNA was incubated with 3 µL of N6-methyladenosine (m6A) antibody (CST, #56593) in 250 µL of Reaction Buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 0.1% NP-40 in nuclease-free H2O) supplemented with 3 µL of RNasin® Plus inhibitor (Promega, #N2611) for 1 h at 4 °C with orbital rotation. Protein G magnetic beads (25 µL per reaction; NEB, #S1430) were prewashed twice with 200 µL of reaction buffer and subsequently added to the RNA–antibody mixture, followed by incubation for 2 h at 4 °C with rotation. The beads were sequentially washed twice with 200 µL of Reaction Buffer, twice with 200 µL of Low-Salt Buffer (50 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 0.1% NP-40), and twice with 200 µL of High-Salt Buffer (500 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 0.1% NP-40). Bound RNA was eluted with 30 µL of Buffer RLT (Qiagen, #79216) for 1 min at room temperature.

To purify the RNA, 20 µL of Dynabeads MyOne Silane (Life Technologies, #37002D) prewashed with 100 µL of Buffer RLT and resuspended in 30 µL of Buffer RLT were added to the eluate. Subsequently, 60 µL of absolute ethanol was mixed with the RNA–Dynabeads suspension and incubated for 1 min at room temperature. The beads were washed twice with 200 µL of 70% ethanol and air-dried for 5 min at room temperature. Purified RNA was eluted with 20 µL of RNase-free water (Gibco) by incubation for 1 min at room temperature. The recovered RNA, together with 1 µg of input total RNA, was used for cDNA synthesis and RT-qPCR analysis.

Reverse transcription

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 200 ng of total RNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad#1708890) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each 20 μL reaction contained 4 μL 5× reaction mix, 1 μL reverse transcriptase, nuclease-free water, and RNA template. Reverse transcription was performed in a thermal cycler (Bio-Rad) under the following program: 25 °C for 5 min (priming), 46 °C for 25 min (reverse transcription), and 95 °C for 1 min (enzyme inactivation). The resulting cDNA was either used immediately for qPCR analysis or stored at −20 °C.

Quantitative q-PCR

For both ChIP-derived DNA and reverse-transcribed cDNA, 1 μL of DNA template was used per reaction. qPCR was performed using SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad#1725274) in a 10 μL total volume containing 5 μL 2× SYBR Green mix, 2 μL primer mix (0.25 μM each forward and reverse), nuclease-free water, and template DNA, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reactions were conducted in a 384-well format on a 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems), with all assays performed in at least technical triplicate. Raw data were collected and analyzed using SDS 2.4 software under default parameters.

For gene expression analysis, GAPDH served as the internal control. For ChIP–qPCR, enrichment was normalized to 2% input DNA. Thermal cycling conditions were as follows:

Polymerase activation/initial denaturation: 95 °C for 30 s (cDNA) or 95 °C for 3 min (ChIP DNA)

Denaturation: 95 °C (cDNA) or 98 °C (ChIP DNA) for 15 s

Annealing/extension: 60 °C for 1 min with plate read, repeated for 40 cycles

Dissociation curve: 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, 95 °C for 15 s

(See also Supplementary Table 3 for q-PCR primer sequence)

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 with default settings. Differences between experimental and control groups in ChIP-qPCR and gene expression assays were evaluated by one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, or unpaired t-test, as appropriate. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant and marked as *; less than 0.01 as **; less than 0.001 as ***; and less than 0.0001 as ****. Detailed p-values and R² values from nonlinear regression analyses are provided in Source Data File.

For mRNA decay analyses, mRNA stability was modeled using a one-phase exponential decay equation:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y(t)\,=\,{Yst}\,\times \,{e}^{-\alpha t}$$\end{document}Y(t)=Yst×e−αtwhich is derived from the condition β = 0 after transcription inhibition by actinomycin D, giving dY/dt = −αY. The mRNA half-life was determined as t₁/₂ = ln(2)/α. To formally test whether mRNA decay rates differed between experimental conditions, an Extra sum-of-squares F-test was applied to compare the one-phase decay models fitted to each condition. This test evaluates whether a single shared decay rate or two separate decay rates better describe the data, providing a formal statistical comparison of the fitted curves.

For ChIP-qPCR base histone marks and transcription factors enrichment time-course analyses, enrichment dynamics were modeled using a first-order association equation:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y(t)=Y0+(Y\max -Y0)(1-{e}^{\wedge }(-{kt}))$$\end{document}Y(t)=Y0+(Ymax−Y0)(1−e∧(−kt))where Y0 is the baseline signal, Ymax is the maximal enrichment, and k is the rate constant. The time required to reach 50% maximum enrichment or expression was calculated as t50% = ln(2)/k. All curve fitting was performed using individual replicate data points rather than their means, using nonlinear least squares regression in GraphPad Prism 10 with default settings.

For mRNA production rate calculations, mRNA dynamics were modeled using the ordinary differential equation:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${dY}/{dt}\,=\,\beta \,-\,\alpha Y$$\end{document}dY/dt=β−αYwhere β denotes the mRNA production rate and α represents the degradation rate. At steady state (dY/dt = 0), the steady-state mRNA abundance is given by Yst = β/α. The mRNA production rate was therefore inferred as β = Yst × α, consistent with standard gene expression kinetics45.
