Retrospective Analysis of 5-Year Outcomes with the Hydrus Microstent in Combination with Cataract Surgery
Authors: Gagan K Sawhney, Brittany Bowman, Brad Hall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the efficacy of the Hydrus microstent in conjunction with cataract surgery over a period of 5 years.MethodsThis was a retrospective, single site study. Eligible charts were from adults that had a diagnosis of mild to severe primary open angle glaucoma and underwent combined cataract surgery with Hydrus Microstent with a minimum of twelve months of follow-up data available. Outcome measures included mean number of ocular hypotensive medication prescribed, intraocular pressure, number of glaucoma secondary surgical interventions, corrected distance visual acuity, and visual field mean deviation.ResultsA total of 280 eyes were identified that met the inclusion and exclusion criteria above. Mean medications improved from 2.3 ± 1.1 at baseline to 1.6 ± 1.3, 1.5 ± 1.3, 1.6 ± 1.3, 1.7 ± 1.4, and 2.5 ± 1.1 at 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months postoperatively, respectively. Intraocular pressure improved from 15.2 ± 4.1 mmHg at baseline to 12.8 ± 2.9 mmHg, 12.9 ± 2.8 mmHg, 13.0 ± 2.4 mmHg, 12.8 ± 3.1 mmHg, and 12.4 ± 2.5 mmHg at 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months postoperatively, respectively. Mean postoperative mean deviation was −3.8 ± 5.1, −3.9 ± 4.2, −2.9 ± 3.5, and −2.5 ± 3.6 dB at 12, 24, 36, and 48 months, respectively, compared to −5.4 ± 6.0 dB at baseline.ConclusionThe results of this study suggest that after combination cataract and Hydrus Microstent surgery, the number of glaucoma medications and intraocular pressure are reduced, and visual fields are stable through 4 years postoperatively. Additional data collected at 5 years postoperatively suggest persistent medication and intraocular pressure reduction, though cautious interpretation is warranted given substantial patient attrition.

Introduction

Glaucoma is the leading cause of irreversible blindness, with an estimated 60.5 million people worldwide and 5.7 million people in the United States impacted by this disease.1 Intraocular pressure (IOP) has been implicated as the main modifiable risk factor for the progression of glaucoma.2,3 First line treatment for glaucoma is typically IOP lowering eye drops or laser therapy before considering traditional glaucoma surgery, as the latter carries more risk of complications.4 Microinvasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) uses microscopic‑sized equipment and small incisions to mitigate some of the complications associated with traditional surgery.5 There are currently several MIGS devices available to lower IOP by improving subconjunctival outflow,6 improving uveoscleral/suprachoroidal/supraciliary outflow,7 or circumventing trabecular outflow.8

The Hydrus Microstent (Alcon Vision LLC; Fort Worth, TX, USA) is designed to bypass the trabecular meshwork, with the inlet in the anterior chamber and the rest of the device inserted into Schlemm’s canal.9 It was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2018 for cases of mild to moderate primary open angle glaucoma (POAG) in conjunction with cataract surgery. In the HORIZON trial, outcomes of cataract surgery alone to cataract surgery combined with Hydrus Microstent were compared over a 5-year period.10 Results of this trial suggest a greater reduction in the number of glaucoma medications in the combined group versus the cataract surgery group. However, randomized controlled trials like HORIZON often exclude complex patient profiles and require strict medication washouts that do not reflect daily clinical practice. Furthermore, there is a paucity of long-term data regarding the device’s performance in diverse racial demographics and advanced disease stages. The purpose of this study was to bridge this gap by evaluating the efficacy of the Hydrus Microstent in conjunction with cataract surgery over a period of 5 years in a clinical setting.

Methods

This was a retrospective chart review of five-year outcomes with Hydrus in combination with cataract surgery. The study was reviewed and approved by an institutional review board (Salus IRB; approval GS-23-01), as this study was conducted in private practice. As the study was retrospective, Salus IRB granted a waiver of informed consent. The study followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and International Harmonization (ICH) guidelines, and good clinical practice.

Charts were reviewed consecutively for patients that had surgery between March 2019 and October 2022. Confidentiality was preserved for all data. Inclusion criteria were: adults that had a diagnosis of mild to severe primary open angle glaucoma and underwent combined cataract surgery with Hydrus Microstent with a minimum of twelve months of follow-up data available; successful implantation of Hydrus and uncomplicated cataract surgery; on 1–4 topical ocular hypotensive medications at baseline; visible glaucomatous optic neuropathy; Shaffer grade III to IV angle width in all 4 quadrants; medicated IOP of 31 mmHg or less. Exclusion criteria were: any cataract-related surgical complications; any active proliferative diabetic retinopathy; secondary glaucoma; angle closure; neovascular glaucoma; any prior penetrating keratoplasty, trabeculectomy or tube shunts; any oral ocular hypotensive medications.

The same surgeon performed all surgeries. The ophthalmic viscosurgical device used to fill the anterior chamber and maintain stability was Healon (Johnson and Johnson), and a gonioprism was placed on the cornea. The Hydrus injector was used to pierce the trabecular meshwork, just anterior to the scleral spur. The Hydrus microstent was then progressively deployed into Schlemm’s canal using the injector. Finally, the injector tip was used to place the inlet further into Schlemm’s canal. The postoperative regimen was prednisolone acetate 1%, moxifloxacin, and ketorolac.

De-identified data were collected preoperatively and at 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months postoperatively. Data were collected for number of glaucoma medications, IOP (measured by Goldmann applanation tonometry), secondary surgical interventions, corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), and visual field mean deviation (measured with the Humphrey Field Analyzer (Carl Zeiss Meditec) using SITA Fast 24–2.

The primary outcome measure of interest was the change in number of glaucoma medications preoperatively to postoperatively. Other outcomes measure were IOP and number of secondary surgical interventions, CDVA, and visual field mean deviation.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). As this was a retrospective study, not all data points were available for all patients. Therefore, where possible the analysis focused on the mean difference from the same eyes preoperative to postoperative rather than summarizing the means of all data collected at each timepoint or summarizing the difference in means.

Discussion

We evaluated the efficacy of the Hydrus Microstent in conjunction with cataract surgery over a period of 5 years using real-world clinical data. Real-world data has the advantage of informing other clinicians of the results they might expect, since the inclusion and exclusion criteria are generally more reflective of how surgeons are using the device as compared to patients in the clinical trials, such as the HORIZON trial.10 Real-world data may also more accurately reflect patient demographics. For example, 47% of the subjects in our study identified as Black, compared to 12% in the HORIZON trial.11

The primary outcome measure of interest in our study was the change in number of medication compared to baseline. The HORIZON trial reported a medication reduction at 24 months of 1.4 compared to 0.8 in our study,11 and 1.1 at 60 months compared to 0.4 in our study.10 However, our patient population could have more advanced disease as there was a greater mean number of medications at baseline (2.3 compared to 1.7) and larger visual deficit (mean deviation of −5.4 dB compared to −3.6 dB).11 There have also been reports from several retrospective studies that have reported outcomes up to 36 months with the Hydrus Microstent in conjunction with cataract surgery. Holmes et al12 observed a mean reduction in the number of medications of 0.8 at 24 months (mean 2.1 at baseline) in real-world data, similar to our study. Lee et al13 reported a mean reduction in the number of medications of 12 at 24 months (mean 2.6 at baseline), also from real-world data. Other retrospective studies have reported similar results.14,15 The results of our study and others suggest successful medication reduction up to 60 months postoperatively with combined phacoemulsification and Hydrus Microstent implantation.

Coinciding with a reduction in the number of medications, we observed a reduction in IOP postoperatively up to 60 months. The HORIZON trial reported a mean IOP reduction at 24 months of 7.6 compared to 2.5 mmHg in our study,11 and 8.0 mmHg at 60 months compared to 2.2 mmHg in our study.10,16 However, the results in the HORIZON trial at 24 and 60 months are after washout or inclusive of only medication-free eyes, respectively, whereas the data in our study are collected without washout. Our results are similar to those observed by Holmes et al,12 who noted a mean reduction in IOP of 3.0 mmHg at 24 months (mean 18.1 mmHg at baseline). Lee et al13 reported a mean reduction in IOP of 4.2 mmHg at 24 months (mean 16.8 mmHg at baseline). Salimi et al15 observed a 5.0 mmHg mean reduction from baseline (18.9 mmHg) at 36 months. Finally, Fea et al14 reported a 3.7 mmHg mean reduction from baseline (19.4 mmHg) at 24 months. Differences in our study may be explained by the lower mean IOP at baseline (15.2 mmHg) and different patient population. The results of our study and other studies suggest successful IOP reduction up to 60 months postoperatively with combined phacoemulsification and Hydrus Microstent implantation.

We observed that a total of 10/280 (3.6%) eyes that required secondary surgical interventions (SSI). This is similar to, but higher than, the overall 60 month SSI rate reported in the HORIZON trial (8/369, 2.1%).10 Holmes et al12 observed an SSI rate of 8/120 (6.7%) after 24 months, higher than our study. The results of our study and others suggest long term durability of the Hydrus Microstent combined with phacoemulsification.

The primary limitation of this study is selection bias, whereby patient characteristics influenced the approach of combined phacoemulsification and Hydrus Microstent implantation. Another significant limitation was subject attrition over time from the retrospective study design. We analyzed data from patients that had surgery between March 2019 and October 2022. Patients who had surgery at the end of this range may only have data from 2 to 3 years, which partially explains the attrition. In addition, as this is real-world data, patients with either poor or stable outcomes may not have returned for followup appointments, which can create bias in our outcomes. Also, the collection of real-world data led to unexpected consequences such as mean deviation not being collected for all patients at all visits. A further limitation was that this study did not use washout, which reduces comparability to washout-based pivotal trials. Further, a lack of washout makes it more difficult to attribute the IOP lowering effects to Hydrus alone. Finally, patient medication compliance could confound our results.

Conclusion

The results of this study suggest that after combination cataract and Hydrus Microstent surgery, the number of glaucoma medications and intraocular pressure are reduced, and visual fields are stable through 4 years postoperatively. Additional data collected at 5 years postoperatively suggest persistent medication and intraocular pressure reduction, though cautious interpretation is warranted given substantial patient attrition.
