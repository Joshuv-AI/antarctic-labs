Expressive Power of Floating-Point Neural Networks with Arbitrary Reduction Orders and Inexact Activation Implementations
Yeachan Park, Geonho Hwang, Wonyeol Lee, Sejun Park

Abstract
Most existing expressivity theories for neural networks assume exact real arithmetic, whereas practical neural networks are executed under finite-precision floating-point arithmetic with implementation-dependent execution semantics. Recent works have begun studying the expressive power of floating-point neural networks, but existing results are limited to highly restricted activation functions and idealized assumptions such as fixed left-to-right reduction orders and correctly rounded activation implementations. In this work, we study the expressive power of floating-point neural networks under generalized floating-point execution semantics, including arbitrary reduction orders and inexact activation implementations with bounded ulp errors. We investigate when floating-point neural networks can represent arbitrary functions between floating-point domains exactly. To this end, we introduce a general distinguishability framework and show that the ability to distinguish every pair of distinct inputs in the first layer is necessary for universal representability. This characterization yields broad classes of activation implementations that are not universal representators, extending previous isolated counterexamples such as the correctly rounded cosine activation. We further prove that a suitable form of distinguishability is also sufficient for universal representability under mild conditions on the activation implementation. Using this framework, we establish universal representability results for a broad class of practical activation functions, including implementations of Sigmoid \mathrm{Sigmoid} , tanh \tanh , ReLU \mathrm{ReLU} , ELU \mathrm{ELU} , SeLU \mathrm{SeLU} , GELU \mathrm{GELU} , Swish \mathrm{Swish} , Mish \mathrm{Mish} , and sin \sin , under significantly more realistic floating-point execution models than previously known.



I
Introduction

Deep neural networks have achieved remarkable success across a wide range of scientific and engineering applications
[
15
]
.
A central theoretical foundation behind this success is the universal approximation theorem, which states that neural networks can approximate broad classes of target functions.
Classical results show that shallow neural networks with non-polynomial activation functions can approximate arbitrary continuous functions on compact domains
[
3
,
9
,
22
,
16
]
.
These results have since been extended to width-bounded architectures and modern neural network models
[
17
,
13
,
20
,
28
,
25
,
27
,
24
,
26
]
.

Most existing expressivity theories, however, analyze neural networks under exact real arithmetic.
In practice, neural networks are executed using finite-precision machine arithmetic, where parameters belong only to a finite set of machine-representable numbers and arithmetic operations incur rounding errors.
This discrepancy between theoretical assumptions and practical computation becomes particularly significant in low-precision settings such as float16, bfloat16, and float8, where both the number of representable values and the precision of arithmetic operations are severely limited.

Motivated by this gap between theory and practice, several recent works have begun studying neural networks under finite-precision computation.
For example,
[
4
]
and
[
6
]
studied neural networks with quantized parameters under exact arithmetic.
More recent works investigated neural networks under inexact machine arithmetic.

[
11
]
studied neural networks under fixed-point arithmetic and established universal approximation results for several practical activation functions.
Likewise,
[
21
]
analyzed floating-point neural networks under floating-point arithmetic and showed that networks using
\mathrm{ReLU}
and
\mathrm{Step}
activations can represent arbitrary floating-point functions over a bounded domain.

Despite these advances, existing analyses of floating-point neural networks remain limited in several important aspects.
First, previous floating-point representability results only cover highly restricted activation functions such as
\mathrm{ReLU}
and
\mathrm{Step}
.
In particular, the analysis of
[
21
]
relies heavily on the piecewise-linearity of activation functions, making it unclear whether the theory extends to smooth and widely used activations such as
\mathrm{Sigmoid}
,
\tanh
,
\mathrm{ReLU}
,
\mathrm{ELU}
,
\mathrm{SeLU}
,
\mathrm{GELU}
,
\mathrm{Swish}
,
\mathrm{Mish}
, and
\sin
.
Second, previous works typically assume idealized execution semantics, including fixed left-to-right reduction orders and correctly rounded activation implementations.

However, these assumptions do not fully capture how neural networks are evaluated in practical floating-point environments.
Floating-point addition is non-associative, so the output of a network may depend on the order in which reductions are performed.
This phenomenon becomes particularly relevant in massively parallel systems such as GPUs, where reductions are often implemented through hardware-dependent evaluation orders rather than a fixed left-to-right order.
Furthermore, practical implementations of activation functions such as
\exp
,
\tanh
,
\sin
, and
\mathrm{GELU}
are typically not correctly rounded, and may differ from the correctly rounded values by several ulps.
Consequently, it remains unclear whether floating-point neural networks retain their expressive power under realistic execution semantics and general practical activation functions.

Extending representability results to such realistic floating-point execution models is highly nontrivial.
Since floating-point addition is non-associative, changing reduction orders can alter the outputs of affine transformations and intermediate network computations.
Furthermore, prior analyses often rely on exact behaviors induced by correctly rounded activation functions.
As a result, even small implementation-dependent perturbations in activation outputs may fundamentally affect the representability of floating-point neural networks.
It therefore remains unclear whether the expressive power established under idealized execution semantics persists under general practical activation functions, arbitrary reduction orders, and inexact activation implementations.
Addressing this question requires new techniques that do not rely on piecewise-linearity, fixed summation orders, or exact activation behaviors induced by correct rounding.

I-A

Contribution

In this work, we study when floating-point neural networks are
universal representators
, i.e., whether they can represent every function between prescribed floating-point domains exactly, under generalized floating-point execution semantics.
We consider floating-point neural networks with arbitrary reduction orders and inexact activation implementations with bounded ulp errors.
Our framework substantially generalizes previous floating-point representability results by extending them beyond piecewise-linear activation functions, fixed left-to-right reduction orders, and correctly rounded activation implementations.
In particular, we establish universal representability results for a broad class of practical activation functions under significantly more realistic floating-point execution models.

Our first contribution is a general representability framework for floating-point neural networks using general activation functions.
In particular, we show that to represent all functions from a floating-point domain
\mathcal{X}
to floating-point outputs, the network must be able to distinguish every pair of distinct inputs in the first layer.
That is, for any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
with
x\neq x^{\prime}
, there should exist a floating-point affine transformation
\phi
such that
\sigma(\phi(x))\neq\sigma(\phi(x^{\prime}))
, where
\sigma
denotes the implemented activation function.
This gives a necessary condition for universal representability and provides a general framework for analyzing when floating-point neural networks fail to represent arbitrary floating-point functions.
As an application, we show that the correctly rounded cosine activation is not a universal representator under various floating-point formats.

Our second contribution is to prove that a suitable form of distinguishability is also sufficient for universal representability.
Under mild output conditions on the activation implementation, we construct floating-point neural networks that represent arbitrary functions on the prescribed floating-point domain.
The construction works under arbitrary reduction orders, rather than relying on a fixed left-to-right summation convention.
This shows that the universal representation phenomenon is not an artifact of a particular reduction order.
Based on this, we also provide an easily verifiable sufficient condition for correctly rounded real activation functions.

Our third contribution is to extend the theory beyond correctly rounded activation functions.
We present easily verifiable conditions under which inexact activation implementations with bounded ulp (unit in the last place) errors remain distinguishable.
As a consequence, our results apply to practical activation functions whose implementations may deviate from the correctly rounded values, including implementations of activations such as
\mathrm{Sigmoid}
,
\tanh
,
\mathrm{ReLU}
,
\mathrm{ELU}
,
\mathrm{SeLU}
,
\mathrm{GELU}
,
\mathrm{Swish}
,
\mathrm{Mish}
, and
\sin
.
Overall, our results establish expressive power guarantees for floating-point neural networks under significantly more realistic floating-point execution semantics than previously known.

This manuscript is an extended version of our previous conference paper
[
10
]
.
The conference version studied the expressive power of floating-point neural networks under fixed left-to-right floating-point summation orders and correctly rounded activation implementations.
The present manuscript substantially generalizes this setting to a more realistic scenario.
In particular, we allow arbitrary reduction orders in floating-point summations and activation implementations with bounded ulp errors, rather than assuming fixed reduction orders and correct rounding.
The journal version also strengthens the negative side of the theory by establishing more general necessary conditions and counterexamples for universal representability, where the previously known cosine-based counterexample appears as a special case.
Importantly, these generalized execution semantics fundamentally change the behavior of floating-point neural networks.
As a result, all representability results and proofs from the conference version are substantially rewritten and re-established under the new assumptions.

Floating-Point Arithmetic

Floating-point numbers.

For
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
,
we define
\mathbb{F}_{p,q}
as the set of

floating-point numbers:

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p,q}\coloneq\big\{

\displaystyle s\times(1.m_{1}\cdots m_{p})\times 2^{e}:s\in\{-1,1\},

\displaystyle m_{1},\dots,m_{p}\in\{0,1\},e\in[\mathfrak{e}_{\min},\mathfrak{e}_{\max}]_{\mathbb{Z}}\big\}

\displaystyle{}\cup\big\{

\displaystyle s\times(0.m_{1}\cdots m_{p})\times 2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\min}}:

\displaystyle s\in\{-1,1\},m_{1},\dots,m_{p}\in\{0,1\}\big\},

(1)

where
\mathfrak{e}_{\min}
and
\mathfrak{e}_{\max}
are defined as
\mathfrak{e}_{\min}\coloneq-2^{q-1}+2
and
\mathfrak{e}_{\max}\coloneq 2^{q-1}-1
.
Here,
s
,
m_{1}\dots m_{p}
, and

are called the

,

, and

of a floating-point number, respectively. Note that
p+q+1
bits suffice to represent all numbers in
\mathbb{F}_{p,q}
:
p
bits and
q
bits for representing the mantissa and exponent, respectively, and one additional bit for the sign.
For simplicity, we omit the subscript and write
\mathbb{F}
for
\mathbb{F}_{p,q}
when
p
and
q
are clear from the context.

We use
\infty
and
-\infty
to denote positive and negative
infinities
, and assume the usual order:
-\infty<x<\infty
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
We use
NaN
to denote

,
which can be produced, e.g., when
\infty
is added to
-\infty
under floating-point arithmetic.
We assume any operation including
NaN
produces
NaN
; this does hold for floating-point addition, subtraction, and multiplication.
We use
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
to denote the set of

floating-point numbers (or floats)
\overline{\mathbb{F}}\coloneq\mathbb{F}\cup\{-\infty,\infty,\text{NaN}\}
. We note that
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p,q}
can also be represented using
p+q+1
bits since we are not using the whole
2^{q}
representations for the exponent of floats in
\mathbb{F}_{p,q}
. For
x\in\mathbb{F}
,
x^{+}
and
x^{-}
denote the smallest and largest floats
greater than and less than
x
, respectively.
For
x\in\mathbb{F}
, we use
\mathfrak{e}_{x}\coloneq\max\{\mathfrak{e}_{\min},\lfloor\log_{2}|x|\rfloor\}
.
The smallest and largest finite positive floats are denoted by
\omega\coloneq 2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\min}-p}
and
\Omega\coloneq\left({2-2^{-p}}\right)\times 2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}
, respectively.

The IEEE-754 standard
[
12
]
defines
(p,q)
for widely used floating-point formats:
e.g.,

(10,5)
for the 16-bit half precision (float16),

(23,8)
for the 32-bit single precision (float32), and
(52,11)
for the 64-bit double precision (float64).
In this paper, we assume that
(p,q)
satisfies

2\leq p\leq 2^{q-1}-3.

Other popular floating-point formats also satisfy this condition, e.g., bfloat16 with
(7,8)

[

,
1
]
and 8-bit E5M2 and E4M3 with

and

]

Floating-point operations.

The rounding function
\left\lceil{\cdot}\right\rfloor_{\mathbb{F}}:\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,\infty,\text{NaN}\}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{F}}
is defined as

\left\lceil{x}\right\rfloor_{\mathbb{F}}\coloneq\begin{cases}\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{F}}|x-y|&\text{ if }|x|<\Omega+2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}-p-1}\!,\\
\infty&\text{ if }x\geq\Omega+2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}-p-1}\!,\\
-\infty&\text{ if }x\leq-\Omega-2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}-p-1}\!,\\
\text{NaN}&\text{ if }x=\text{NaN}.\end{cases}

There can be two floats equidistant from a real number. In such a case, we break the tie using the tie-to-even rule:
\left\lceil{x}\right\rfloor_{\mathbb{F}}
is defined by the (unique) float whose last mantissa bit
m_{p}
(see
Equation

1
) is zero.
If
\mathbb{F}
is clear from the text, we omit the subscript
\mathbb{F}
in
\left\lceil{\cdot}\right\rfloor_{\mathbb{F}}
.

For
\rho:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
we define the
correctly rounded
function
\left\lceil{\rho}\right\rfloor:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\rightarrow{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
of
\rho
as follows:

\left\lceil{\rho}\right\rfloor(x)\coloneq\begin{cases}\left\lceil{\rho(x)}\right\rfloor&\text{ if }x\in\mathbb{F},\\
\left\lceil{l}\right\rfloor&\text{ if }x=-\infty\land\exists\lim_{x\rightarrow-\infty}{\rho(x)},\\
\left\lceil{r}\right\rfloor&\text{ if }x=\infty\land\exists\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}{\rho(x)},\\
\text{NaN}&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

where

and

.
Here, the existence of

. We define

.

For

, we define the floating-point operations

, and

For

II-C

Reduction Order in Floating-Point Summations

For
n\geq 2
, let
\mathcal{O}_{n}
denote the set of all possible reduction orders for summing
n
floating-point numbers.
For
n=2
, there is only one possible reduction order:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{2}=\{(1,2)\}.

For
n\geq 3
, each element

o=((i_{1},j_{1}),\dots,(i_{n-1},j_{n-1}))\in\mathcal{O}_{n}

specifies a sequence of pairwise floating-point reductions.
At the first step, the
i_{1}
-th and
j_{1}
-th entries are reduced to their floating-point sum.
The remaining pairs

o^{\prime}=((i_{2},j_{2}),\dots,(i_{n-1},j_{n-1}))

then form an element of
\mathcal{O}_{n-1}
for the resulting
(n-1)
-tuple.

More precisely, for
1\leq i<j\leq n
and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, define

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}=(x_{i}\oplus x_{j},x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{n}),

where the reduced value
x_{i}\oplus x_{j}
is placed at the first coordinate.
Then, for

o=((i_{1},j_{1}),\dots,(i_{n-1},j_{n-1}))\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
,
we recursively define

\displaystyle\Sigma(\mathbf{x};o)\coloneq\Sigma(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{i_{1},j_{1}};o^{\prime}),

where

o^{\prime}=((i_{2},j_{2}),\dots,(i_{n-1},j_{n-1}))\in\mathcal{O}_{n-1}
,
with the base case

\displaystyle\Sigma((x_{1},x_{2});(1,2))=x_{1}\oplus x_{2}.

For notational convenience, we also define
\mathcal{O}_{1}\coloneq\{(1)\}
and
\Sigma(x;(1))\coloneq x
.

For example,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{3}=\{((1,2),(1,2)),((1,3),(1,2)),((2,3),(1,2))\},

and the corresponding summations are

\displaystyle\Sigma(\mathbf{x}_{3};((1,2),(1,2)))

\displaystyle=(x_{1}\oplus x_{2})\oplus x_{3},

\displaystyle\Sigma(\mathbf{x}_{3};((1,3),(1,2)))

\displaystyle=(x_{1}\oplus x_{3})\oplus x_{2},

\displaystyle\Sigma(\mathbf{x}_{3};((2,3),(1,2)))

\displaystyle=(x_{2}\oplus x_{3})\oplus x_{1}.

Likewise, for
\mathbf{x}_{4}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
,

\displaystyle\Sigma(\mathbf{x}_{4};((1,2),(1,2),(1,2)))

\displaystyle=((x_{1}\oplus x_{2})\oplus x_{3})\oplus x_{4},

\displaystyle\Sigma(\mathbf{x}_{4};((1,2),(2,3),(1,2)))

\displaystyle=(x_{1}\oplus x_{2})\oplus(x_{3}\oplus x_{4}).

A
reduction order
is a set

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\{o_{1},o_{2},o_{3},\dots\},

where
o_{n}\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The sequence
\mathcal{S}
specifies how sums of floating-point numbers are reduced in the system.
For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
,
\mathcal{S}=\{o_{1},o_{2},o_{3},\dots\}
, we define
\Sigma(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathcal{S})
as

\displaystyle\Sigma(\mathbf{x},\mathcal{S})\coloneq\Sigma(\mathbf{x},o_{n}),\quad o_{n}\in\mathcal{S}

We note that left-to-right addition, i.e., sequentially adding numbers from left to right, can be represented using the reduction order.
Let

\displaystyle\bar{o}_{n}=\underbrace{\left((1,2),(1,2),\dots,(1,2)\right)}_{(n-1)\text{ times}}.

Then, the left-to-right addition of
n
floating-point numbers,

(((x_{1}\oplus x_{2})\oplus x_{3})\oplus\cdots\oplus x_{n-1})\oplus x_{n},

can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Sigma(\mathbf{x}_{n};\bar{o}_{n})=(((x_{1}\oplus x_{2})\oplus x_{3})\oplus\cdots\oplus x_{n-1})\oplus x_{n}.

II-D

Floating-Point Neural Network under Reduction Order

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{o_{1},o_{2},o_{3},\dots\}
be a reduction order.
For
d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}}\in\mathbb{N}
, let

w_{i}=(w_{i,1},\dots,w_{i,d_{\mathrm{in}}})\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}

and
b_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
for all
i\in[d_{\mathrm{out}}]
.
Given

\displaystyle I=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d_{\mathrm{out}}},b_{1},\dots,b_{d_{\mathrm{out}}}),

we define the
floating-point affine transformation under
\mathcal{S}
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{aff}_{(I,\mathcal{S})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d_{\mathrm{in}}})_{i}

\displaystyle=\Sigma((w_{i,1}\otimes x_{1},\dots,w_{i,d_{\mathrm{in}}}\otimes x_{d_{\mathrm{in}}});o_{d_{\mathrm{in}}})\oplus b_{i}

for every
i\in[d_{\mathrm{out}}]
, where the bias parameter
b_{i}
is added at last.
Namely, each coordinate is computed by floating-point multiplications followed by a floating-point reduction according to
o_{d_{\mathrm{in}}}
and a floating-point bias addition.

For a floating-point activation function
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
, we slightly abuse notation and let
\sigma
also denote the corresponding coordinate-wise operation on vectors:

\displaystyle\sigma(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=(\sigma(x_{1}),\dots,\sigma(x_{d})).

We now define floating-point neural networks under the reduction order
\mathcal{S}
.
Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
and let

\displaystyle\mathrm{aff}_{(I_{\ell},\mathcal{S})}:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}^{d_{\ell-1}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}^{d_{\ell}}

be floating-point affine transformations under
\mathcal{S}
for all
\ell\in[L]
.
Given
\mathcal{I}=(I_{1},\dots,I_{L})
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{S})}=\mathrm{aff}_{(I_{L},\mathcal{S})}\circ\sigma\circ\dots\circ\mathrm{aff}_{(I_{2},\mathcal{S})}\circ\sigma\circ\mathrm{aff}_{(I_{1},\mathcal{S})}.

(2)

We call
\mathcal{N}_{(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{S})}
a (floating-point)
\sigma
network under
\mathcal{S}
.
The number of layers of the network is defined as the number of affine transformations; thus
\mathcal{N}_{(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{S})}
has
L
layers.

For technical purposes, we also consider neural networks ending with activation functions.
For
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=(I_{1},\dots,I_{L-1})
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{(\mathcal{I}^{\prime},\mathcal{S})}=\sigma\circ\mathrm{aff}_{(I_{L-1},\mathcal{S})}\circ\dots\circ\sigma\circ\mathrm{aff}_{(I_{1},\mathcal{S})}.

That is,
\mathcal{M}_{(\mathcal{I}^{\prime},\mathcal{S})}
omits the final affine transformation and ends with an activation function.
The network
\mathcal{M}_{(\mathcal{I}^{\prime},\mathcal{S})}
contains
L-1
affine transformations and therefore has
L-1
layers.

III-A

Necessary Condition on Activation Functions

Given a reduction order
\mathcal{S}
, a floating-point activation function
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
, and a domain
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
, we are interested in identifying whether
\sigma
networks under
\mathcal{S}
can represent all functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{F}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
.
A natural observation is that such universal representation is
impossible
if there exist
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\sigma(\phi(x))=\sigma(\phi(x^{\prime}))
for all floating-point affine transformations
\phi
under
\mathcal{S}
. That is, for any
\sigma
network, the outputs of the first layer at
x
and
x^{\prime}
are identical.
By the definition of neural networks (
Equation

2
), this implies that the final outputs of the network at
x
and
x^{\prime}
must also be identical for all
\sigma
networks; and thus,
\sigma
networks cannot represent any function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{F}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
such that
f(x)\neq f(x^{\prime})
.

To formally describe the above idea, we define the
distinguishability
of an input domain.

Definition 1

(Distinguishability)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a reduction order,
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\rightarrow{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
, and
\mathcal{Y}\subset{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
. We say that “
\mathcal{X}
is
\sigma
-distinguishable with range
\mathcal{Y}
under
\mathcal{S}
” if for any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
with
x\neq x^{\prime}
, there exists a floating-point affine transformation
\phi
under
\mathcal{S}
such that

\sigma(\phi(x))\neq\sigma(\phi(x^{\prime}))~~\text{and}~~\sigma(\phi(\mathcal{X}))\subset\mathcal{Y}.

(3)

To represent all functions from
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
to
\mathbb{F}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}

III-B

Sufficient Condition on Activation Functions

Although the distinguishability of
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
with range
\mathbb{F}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
under
\mathcal{S}
is necessary to represent all floating-point functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{F}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
, the distinguishability is also sufficient under mild assumptions on its range and the activation function (
Theorem

5
).

We first introduce the following condition to describe our sufficient condition.

Condition 1
.

For an activation function
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
,
there exist
C_{0},C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathbb{F}
such that
|C_{i}|,|C_{i}-C_{j}|\leq 2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}
for all
0\leq i,j\leq 2
, and

\displaystyle\sigma(C_{0})=0,

\displaystyle\!\!2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\min}}\leq|\sigma(C_{1})|<\frac{5}{4},

\displaystyle\!\!|\sigma(C_{2})|>(2^{-p-2})^{+}.

1
requires the existence of three points
C_{0},C_{1},C_{2}
for an activation function
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
.
This condition can easily be satisfied for rounded versions of popular activation functions.
For example,
\sigma(C_{0})=0
can be satisfied for
C_{0}=0
(e.g.,
\mathrm{ReLU}
,
\mathrm{GELU}
,
\sin
,
\tanh
) or for
C_{0}
of large magnitude such as
-2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}
(e.g.,
\mathrm{Sigmoid}
).
Furthermore, the conditions on
C_{1},C_{2}
can easily be satisfied by choosing some
C_{1}=C_{2}
so that
\sigma(C_{1}=C_{2})\in((2^{-p-2})^{+},5/4)
.

Under
1
, we show that the distinguishability of
\mathcal{X}
with range
[-2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}},2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}]
suffices for representing all functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
(\mathbb{F}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\})^{d_{\text{\rm out}}}
.
The proof of
Theorem

5
is in
Section

VII-A
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a reduction order,
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
,
d_{\text{\rm in}},d_{\text{\rm out}}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{F}^{d_{\text{\rm in}}}
, and
f:\mathcal{X}\to\left({\mathbb{F}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}}\right)^{d_{\text{\rm out}}}
.
Suppose that
\sigma
satisfies
1
and
\mathcal{X}
is
\sigma
-distinguishable with range
\left[-2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}},2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}\right]_{\mathbb{F}}
under
\mathcal{S}
.
Then, there exists a four-layer
\sigma
network
g
under
\mathcal{S}
such that
f=g
on
\mathcal{X}
.

There are two notable differences between our necessary condition (
Lemma

2
) and sufficient condition (
Theorem

5
):
Theorem

5
requires
1
, and considers a smaller range
\left[-2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}},2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}\right]_{\mathbb{F}}
for distinguishability.
First, we use
1
for networks to generate all possible values in
\mathbb{F}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
(see
Lemma

36
). If an activation function
\sigma
can only output too large values (e.g.,
[2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}},\infty]
) or too small values (e.g., in
[-\omega,\omega]
), then a
\sigma
network may not be able to generate some values in
\mathbb{F}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
.
Second, the smaller range is due to technical reasons in our proof, which is used to avoid overflow during the evaluation of networks. If a network can generate values with large absolute values (e.g., close to
\Omega
) while distinguishing inputs, then multiplying/adding constants to those values may incur overflow and the network may output NaN.
However, if
\sigma
has a well-bounded range (i.e.,
\sigma(\mathbb{F}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\})\subset[2^{-\mathfrak{e}_{\max}},\allowbreak 2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}]

and
\tanh

To describe our conditions, we first define the

Definition 6

(Separating Point)
.

For
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
, we call
\eta\in\mathbb{F}
a “separating point of
\sigma
” if

\displaystyle\sigma(\eta^{-})\notin\{\sigma(\eta),\sigma(\eta^{+})\}\quad\text{or}\quad\sigma(\eta^{+})\notin\{\sigma(\eta),\sigma(\eta^{-})\}.

Here,
\eta^{-}
(or
\eta^{+}
) denotes the largest (or smallest) float that is smaller (or larger) than
\eta
(see
Section

II-B
).

We design our sufficient conditions using separating points. Specifically, for each distinct pair
(x,x^{\prime})
of inputs in a domain
\mathcal{X}
, we aim to find a floating-point affine transformation
\phi_{x,x^{\prime}}
and a separating point
\eta_{x,x^{\prime}}
of
\sigma

such that
\phi_{x,x^{\prime}}(x)=\eta_{x,x^{\prime}}^{-}
(or
\eta_{x,x^{\prime}}^{+}
) and
\phi_{x,x^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})\in\{\eta_{x,x^{\prime}},\eta_{x,x^{\prime}}^{+}\}
(or
\{\eta_{x,x^{\prime}}^{-},\eta_{x,x^{\prime}}\})
.
If we can find such an affine transformation and a separating point for all distinct pairs in the domain, then the domain is distinguishable
since
\sigma(\phi_{x,x^{\prime}}(x))\neq\sigma(\phi_{x,x^{\prime}}(x^{\prime}))
(see
Equation

3
).

Based on the above idea, we propose our sufficient condition for distinguishability in the following lemma. The proof of

is in

Let
\mathcal{S}

III-D

Sufficient Conditions for Distinguishability using Activations with Bounded Ulp Errors

In practice, implementations of activation functions are often not correctly rounded.
While correctly rounded implementations return the nearest floating-point value of the target real function, practical implementations often allow small approximation errors to reduce computational cost and implementation complexity.
For example, elementary functions such as
\exp
,
\tanh
,
\sin
, and
\cos
are commonly implemented using polynomial approximations, table-based methods, or hardware-specific instructions, which may introduce small floating-point errors.
As a result, practical floating-point implementations are often analyzed using ulp (
unit in the last place
) error bounds rather than exact correct-rounding guarantees.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\left\lceil{x}\right\rfloor\in\mathbb{F}
and
2^{e}\leq x<2^{e+1}
for some
e\in\mathbb{Z}
(
e=-\infty
if
x=0
), we define

\displaystyle\mathrm{ulp}(x)\coloneq 2^{\max\{e,\mathfrak{e}_{\min}\}-p}.

Intuitively,
\mathrm{ulp}(x)
represents the spacing between nearby floating-point numbers around
x
.
Under the round-to-nearest mode, a correctly rounded implementation has at most
0.5
ulp error.

Given
\rho:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
, and
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{F}
, we say that
\sigma
approximates
\rho
with
K
ulp error on
\mathcal{Z}
if
\left\lceil{\rho}\right\rfloor(\mathcal{Z})\subset\mathbb{F}
and

\displaystyle|\sigma(x)-\rho(x)|\leq K\times\mathrm{ulp}(\rho(x))

for all
x\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Practical floating-point implementations of elementary functions often provide ulp-error guarantees or empirical ulp-error bounds rather than exact correct rounding.
For single precision,
[
5
]
reports exhaustive accuracy measurements over all float32 inputs for univariate functions.
Their results show that, for most elementary functions relevant to activation implementations, such as
\exp
,
\log
,
\sin
,
\cos
, and
\tanh
, the observed ulp errors are below
1
for several widely used mathematical libraries.
Motivated by these observations, we study the expressive power of floating-point neural networks with activation functions allowing bounded ulp errors.

Here, we mainly focus on high-precision floating-point formats such as float16, bfloat16, float32, and float64.
In contrast, for very low-precision formats such as float8, correctly rounded activation functions can often be implemented efficiently using lookup tables because the number of representable values is small.

To describe our results, we present our assumption on a real activation function and its implementation.

Condition 2
.

Consider a real number
0\leq K\leq 10
.
For a real activation function
\rho:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and its floating-point implementation
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
,
\sigma
approximates
\rho
in
K
ulp errors on
[0,8]_{\mathbb{F}}
,
|\rho([0,8])|\leq 2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}
,
|\sigma(x)|\leq 2|\rho(x)|
for
|x|\geq 1
and
\sigma(C_{0})=0
for some
C_{0}\in\mathbb{F}
.

The assumptions in
2
are motivated by practical implementations.
Referring to
[
5
]
, several mathematical libraries such as GNU libc and LLVM libc report ulp errors below
1
for many elementary functions in single precision.
Although widely used activations such as
\mathrm{GELU}
and
\mathrm{Swish}
are not themselves elementary functions, they are compositions of elementary functions, and their implementations are expected to exhibit moderate ulp errors.
Accordingly,
2
assumes bounded ulp error on
[0,8]
.
We also report the maximum ulp errors of several activations on
[0,8]
in half and single precision in
Table

I
.

The condition
\sigma(C_{0})=0
for some
C_{0}\in\mathbb{F}
is also natural in practice.
For example, although the real sigmoid function never attains zero, its floating-point implementation can underflow to zero for sufficiently negative inputs.
Under
2
, implementations with bounded ulp error still admit separating points, which allows us to establish universal representability results for
\mathrm{Sigmoid}
and
\tanh
networks.
The proof of
Corollary

13
is in
Section

X-A
.

Corollary 13
.

Let
p\geq 7
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and

Unlike the correctly rounded setting, implementations with bounded ulp error require an additional assumption such as

The main reason is to guarantee the existence of separating points.

IV
Additional notations and definitions for proofs

We denote the left-to-right addition

\bar{o}_{n}=\underbrace{\left((1,2),(1,2),\dots,(1,2)\right)}_{(n-1)\;times},

which is summed as

\displaystyle\Sigma(\mathbf{x}_{n};\bar{o}_{n})=(\dots(x_{1}\oplus x_{2})\oplus x_{3})\oplus\dots\oplus x_{n-1})\oplus x_{n}.

We also define

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{S}}=\{\bar{o}_{i}:i=1,2,3,\dots\}=\{\bar{o}_{1},\bar{o}_{2},\bar{o}_{3},\dots\}

for the set of the left-to-right additions, where
\bar{o}_{1}=(1)
.

Definition 17

(Embedding)
.

Let
m>n
.
We define an embedding

\iota:\mathbb{F}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}^{m}

such that, for any
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
,

\iota(\mathbf{x})=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m}),

and there exists an injective map
I:[n]\to[m]
satisfying

x_{i}=y_{I(i)}

for all
i\in[n]
.

Definition 18

(Transferability)
.

Define
\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}\coloneq\{w\otimes\sigma(c):w,c\in\mathbb{F}~\text{with}~w\otimes\sigma(c)\in\mathbb{F}\}.
Let
\mathcal{S}
be a reduction order,
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
,
(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
. We say “
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
is transferable to
(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
with
(\sigma,\mathbf{z},j,\mathcal{S})
” or write

\displaystyle(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\xRightarrow{(\sigma,\mathbf{z},j,\mathcal{S})}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m}),

if there exist

\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\dots z_{n})\in\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}^{n}
and embedding
\iota_{\mathbf{z},j}:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}^{n+1}
for

such that

\displaystyle\iota_{\mathbf{z},j}(x)=(z_{1},\dots,z_{j},x,z_{j+1},\dots,z_{n}),\;\Sigma(\iota_{\mathbf{z},j}(x_{i}),\mathcal{S})=y_{i},

for all
i\in[m]
.
For simplicity, we often omit
\mathbf{z},j
and simply write

\displaystyle(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\xRightarrow{(\sigma,\mathcal{S})}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m}).

For left-to-right addition
\overline{\mathcal{S}}

(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\xRightarrow{(\sigma,\overline{\mathcal{S}})}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})

\displaystyle(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\xRightarrow{(\sigma,\mathbf{z},0,\overline{\mathcal{S}})}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m}).

We often drop
\sigma

to denote

Lemma 19
.

Let
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}

\sigma

.
Use mathematical induction on
|x_{1}|
.
If
x_{1}=0

|x^{\prime}|<x_{1}

y^{\prime}

28

If

28
, there exists
\gamma\in\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}

.

by the induction hypothesis.
This completes the proof.

We present several technical lemmas on reduction order.

Lemma 29
.

For every
n\geq 1
and every
o\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
, there exists a full binary tree

T_{o}
with exactly
n
leaves, whose leaves are labeled by

x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, such that
\Sigma(\mathbf{x};o)
is obtained by evaluating

T_{o}
with the binary operation
\oplus
at every internal node.
In particular, every reduction order
o\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
determines a full binary
addition tree for
n
inputs.

Proof.

We prove the claim by induction on
n
.

For
n=1
, the reduction order is
(1)
, and
\Sigma(x_{1};(1))=x_{1}
.
The corresponding tree consists of a single leaf labeled by
x_{1}
.

For
n=2
, the only reduction order is
(1,2)
, and

\Sigma((x_{1},x_{2});(1,2))=x_{1}\oplus x_{2}.

Thus the corresponding tree has one internal node with two leaves labeled

x_{1}
and
x_{2}
.

Assume the claim holds for
n-1
. Let

o=((i_{1},j_{1}),\dots,(i_{n-1},j_{n-1}))\in\mathcal{O}_{n}.

By definition, the first reduction replaces
x_{i_{1}}
and
x_{j_{1}}
by

x_{i_{1}}\oplus x_{j_{1}}
and produces the
(n-1)
-tuple

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{i_{1},j_{1}}
. Let

o^{\prime}=((i_{2},j_{2}),\dots,(i_{n-1},j_{n-1}))\in\mathcal{O}_{n-1}.

By the induction hypothesis,
o^{\prime}
determines a full binary tree for

\Sigma(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{i_{1},j_{1}};o^{\prime})
. Replacing the leaf corresponding to
the entry
x_{i_{1}}\oplus x_{j_{1}}
by an internal node with two children
labeled
x_{i_{1}}
and
x_{j_{1}}
gives a full binary tree with
n
leaves.
Evaluating this tree gives exactly

\Sigma(\mathbf{x};o)=\Sigma(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{i_{1},j_{1}};o^{\prime}).

Therefore
o
determines a full binary addition tree. This completes the
induction.
∎

Definition 30

(Depth of a reduction order)
.

Let
o\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
, and let
T_{o}
be the full binary tree corresponding to
the reduction order
o
as in Lemma
29
.

For a node
v
of
T_{o}
, define its depth as the number of edges on the
unique path from the root to
v
.
The depth of the tree
T_{o}
is

0pt(T_{o})\coloneq\max_{v\in\mathrm{Leaf}(T_{o})}0pt(v).

We define the depth of the reduction order
o
by

0pt(o)\coloneq 0pt(T_{o}).

Note that

0pt\bigl(((1,2),(1,2),\dots,(1,2))\bigr)=n-1,

and

0pt\bigl(((1,2),(2,3),(1,2))\bigr)=2.

Lemma 31
.

Let
o\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
. Then

\displaystyle\lceil\log_{2}(n)\rceil\leq\mathrm{depth}(o)\leq n-1.

Proof.

Let
T_{o}
be the full binary tree corresponding to the reduction order
o
.
By definition,

0pt(o)=0pt(T_{o}).

Since
T_{o}
has exactly
n
leaves and every internal node has exactly two
children, a binary tree of depth
d
can have at most
2^{d}
leaves.
Therefore

n\leq 2^{0pt(T_{o})}.

Taking
\log_{2}(\cdot)
gives

\log_{2}n\leq 0pt(T_{o}).

Since
0pt(T_{o})
is an integer, we obtain

\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil\leq 0pt(T_{o})=0pt(o).

It remains to prove the upper bound. Any full binary tree with
n
leaves
has exactly
n-1
internal nodes. Every path from the root to a leaf contains
at most all internal nodes of the tree. Hence the number of edges on such a
path is at most
n-1
. Therefore

0pt(T_{o})\leq n-1.

Thus

\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil\leq 0pt(o)\leq n-1.

∎
