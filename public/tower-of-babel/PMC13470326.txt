Hedgehog/GLI1 regulates EMT and cancer stem cell properties via the GLI1-Bmi1 axis in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
Authors: Huifang Xiao, Chuntuan Li, Yan Han, Jingjing Gao, Wenqian Xu, Pengliang Xin, Xiongpeng Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
R-CHOP-treated diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) shows heterogeneous outcomes. High-risk relapsed/refractory (R/R) patients, especially activated B-cell (ABC) and double-/triple-hit subtypes, have poor prognosis, highlighting an urgent need to uncover the underlying aggressive molecular mechanisms. Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and cancer stem cell (CSC) properties drive its invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance. This study investigated the regulatory role and mechanism of Hedgehog (Hh) pathway key factor GLI1 in DLBCL. Immunohistochemistry detected GLI1, Bmi1, and SOX2 expression in DLBCL tissues. DLBCL cell lines (HBL-1, SUDHL-2) were treated with pan-GLI inhibitor GANT61, or transfected with GLI1 overexpression/silencing plasmids combined with Bmi1 intervention. CCK-8, flow cytometry, RT-qPCR, Western blot, Co-IP, tumor sphere, and immunofluorescence assays were performed. In vivo xenograft experiments were conducted to verify GANT61’s anti-tumor effect. GLI1 was aberrantly activated in DLBCL tissues and positively correlated with Bmi1/SOX2. GANT61 inhibited DLBCL cell proliferation, induced G0/G1 arrest and apoptosis, reversed EMT, and reduced CSC-related molecules. Co-IP confirmed GLI1-Bmi1 interaction. GLI1 overexpression enhanced proliferation, EMT, and CSC properties, while GLI1 silencing exerted opposite effects. Bmi1 inhibitor reversed GLI1’s pro-tumor effects, and Bmi1 agonist partially restored GLI1 silencing-induced inhibition. In vivo, GANT61 significantly reduced tumor volume/weight in HBL-1 xenografts with good tolerability. GLI1 modulates EMT-like plasticity and stem cell traits in DLBCL via direct protein interaction with Bmi1, confirming a functional regulatory connection between these two molecules that forms the core Hh–GLI1–Bmi1 regulatory pathway. Targeting this axis provides a novel therapeutic strategy for DLBCL. Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, accounting for approximately 30–40% of all cases1. Despite advances in immunochemotherapy and targeted therapies, one-third of patients develop relapsed or refractory (R/R) disease, which is associated with a poor prognosis2. In these patients, the limited therapeutic efficacy is closely associated with the invasive growth, distant metastasis, and chemoresistance of DLBCL cells3. Therefore, exploring the underlying molecular mechanisms driving these malignant phenotypes is crucial for developing novel, effective therapies.

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a biological process in which epithelial cells lose their polarity and cell–cell adhesion, acquiring mesenchymal characteristics such as enhanced motility and invasiveness4. Lymphocytes are non-epithelial cells and do not undergo classical epithelial-mesenchymal transition, in hematological malignancies such as DLBCL, the EMT-like process refers to the changes in cytoskeleton remodeling, enhanced cell movement, invasion ability and stem cell characteristics accompanied by the abnormal expression of classic EMT marker molecules (E-cadherin, Vimentin), which is a typical tumor cell phenotypic plasticity in lymphoma5, and EMT-like processes contribute to tumor cell dissemination and tissue infiltration6. Cancer stem cells (CSCs), a small subpopulation of tumor cells with self-renewal and differentiation capabilities, are considered the root cause of tumor recurrence, metastasis, and drug resistance7. Accumulating evidence indicates that EMT and CSC properties are closely interconnected, forming a regulatory network that promotes tumor progression8.

The Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway is an evolutionarily conserved pathway that plays essential roles in embryonic development, tissue homeostasis, and stem cell maintenance9. Abnormal activation of the Hh pathway has been reported in various solid tumors and hematological malignancies, including DLBCL10,11. GLI1, a key transcription factor in the Hh pathway, mediates the downstream signaling cascade by regulating the expression of target genes involved in cell proliferation, survival, and stem cell maintenance12. Previous studies have shown that GLI1 contributes to tumor progression by promoting EMT and CSC properties in solid tumors such as head and neck squamous cell carcinoma and colorectal cancer13,14. However, the role and molecular mechanism of GLI1 in regulating EMT and CSC properties in DLBCL remain unclear.

Bmi1, a member of the Polycomb group (PcG) family of transcriptional repressors, is a critical regulator of stem cell self-renewal and tumorigenesis15. It has been shown to interact with various signaling pathways to promote EMT and maintain CSC properties16. Recent studies have suggested a potential crosstalk between the Hh pathway and Bmi1 in tumor progression17, but whether GLI1 directly interacts with Bmi1 to regulate EMT and CSC properties in DLBCL requires further investigation.

In this study, we aimed to explore the expression pattern of GLI1 in DLBCL tissues and its regulatory role in DLBCL cell proliferation, apoptosis, EMT, and CSC properties. We further investigated the molecular mechanism underlying these effects, focusing on the interaction between GLI1 and Bmi1. Our findings may provide new insights into the development of targeted therapies for DLBCL.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

DLBCL tissue samples and corresponding normal lymphoid tissue samples were collected from patients who underwent surgical resection or biopsy at our hospital. All samples were confirmed by pathological diagnosis. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Quanzhou First Hospital, and informed consent was obtained from all patients (Ethics Approval Code: 2023K068).

Reagents

The reagents used in this study were obtained from the following commercial sources: RIPA cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B), BCA protein quantitative kit (Cwbio, CW0014S), total RNA extraction kit (Bkm-bio, BKM-RTQ-K50), FastKing RT Kit (Tiangen, KR116), FastReal qPCR PreMix (Tiangen, FR217), CD133 antibody (Invitrogen, 53–1331-80), CD44 antibody (Invitrogen, 14–0441-82), CCK-8 kit (Bkm-bio, BKM-CK-5 ml), rabbit IgG two-step immunohistochemistry kit (BOSTER, SV0002), HE staining kit (Solarbio, G1120), cell cycle detection kit (Keygen, KGA9101-50), Annexin V-PE/ 7AAD Kit (Solarbio, CA1030), OE-GLI1 and sh-GLI1 plasmids were purchased from Obiosh, GANT61 (MedChemExpress, HY-13901), Bmi1 inhibitor (MedChemExpress, HY-103696), Bmi1 agonist (MedChemExpress, HY-P701224), primary antibodies against GLI1 (Abmart, P08151), Bmi1 (Proteintech, 10,832–1-AP), SOX2 (Proteintech, 20,118–1-AP), Smo (Abcam, ab235183), Sufu (Cell Signaling Technology, 2520), Cyclin D1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 2978), Vimentin (Proteintech, 10,366–1-AP), E-cadherin (Proteintech, 20,874–1-AP), β-catenin (Cell Signaling Technology, 8480), and Actin (Proteintech, 83,115–1-RR).

Cell culture

DLBCL cell lines HBL-1 and SUDHL-2 were purchased from the Fudancell. HBL-1 cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S). SUDHL-2 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. All cell lines were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination using mycoplasma detection kits, and the results confirmed that all cell lines were free from mycoplasma contamination throughout the experimental process.

Tumor stem cell culture

DLBCL cells (HBL-1 and SUDHL-2) were seeded in 24-well ultra-low attachment plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL in 500 μL per well. Cells were cultured for 10–14 days in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with B27 and N2 supplements, 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF), and 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor. The medium was refreshed every 3–4 days. The formed tumor spheroids were observed under a microscope and collected for subsequent experiments.

Cell transfection

GLI1 overexpression plasmid (OE-GLI1), GLI1 silencing plasmid (sh-GLI1), and corresponding empty vectors (negative control, NC) were purchased from Obiosh. Transfection was performed using KeygenMax 3000 transfection reagent (Keygen, KGA9705) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were resuspended in antibiotic‑free complete medium at a density of 1.6 × 106 cells/mL. A 500 μL aliquot of the cell suspension was added to each well of 24‑well plates and incubated overnight. This seeding density ensured a cell number of approximately 8 × 105 cells per well at transfection, corresponding to 70–80% of the optimal cell density for transfection. For each well, transfection complexes were prepared by gently mixing 1 μL of KeygenMAX™ 3000 reagent with 25 μL of serum-free and antibiotic-free medium, followed by incubation at room temperature for 5 min. Separately, 0.5 μg of plasmid DNA was diluted in 25 μL of serum-free and antibiotic-free medium, and 1 μL of enhancer was added. The DNA/enhancer mixture was then combined with the diluted transfection reagent, gently vortexed, and incubated at room temperature for 10–15 min to allow complex formation. The resulting 50 μL transfection mixture was added dropwise to the corresponding well. After incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere for 48–72 h, the transfection efficiency was validated by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis.

Drug treatment

GANT61 was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a stock solution. HBL-1 and SUDHL-2 cells were treated with different concentrations of GANT61 (0–160 μM) for 24 h, and the optimal concentration was determined by CCK-8 assay. For subsequent experiments, HBL-1 cells were treated with 40 μM GANT61, and SUDHL-2 cells were treated with 50 μM GANT61. For combination treatment, cells were transfected with GLI1 overexpression or silencing plasmids, followed by treatment with Bmi1 inhibitor (100 nM) or Bmi1 agonist (20 μg/mL, recombinant human Bmi1 protein, when adding purified recombinant Bmi1 protein to the culture medium, we used a commercial cell-penetrating peptide (CPP) as the delivery carrier to assist the recombinant protein to cross the cell membrane) for 24 h.

CCK-8 assay for cell viability

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 104 cells/well in 200 μL of medium and cultured for 12 h. After drug treatment, 20 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for another 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using an automatic microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated as follows: Viability (%) = (A2—A0)/(A1—A0) × 100%, where A0 is the absorbance of the blank group, A1 is the absorbance of the control group, and A2 is the absorbance of the drug-treated group. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Flow cytometry for cell cycle and apoptosis

For cell cycle analysis: Cells were collected by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 3 min, washed once with PBS, and fixed with 70% pre-cooled ethanol at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with RNase A and PI staining working solution (1:9 volume ratio) at room temperature in the dark for 30–60 min, and then analyzed by flow cytometry.

For cell apoptosis analysis: Cells were collected by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in 500 μL of Binding Buffer. 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL of PI were added, mixed well, and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 5–10 min. Apoptosis was detected by flow cytometry within 1 h.

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells using a total RNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a UV spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized using the FastKing RT Kit. RT-qPCR was performed using the FastReal qPCR PreMix (SYBR Green) on a Real-Time PCR System. The reaction conditions were as follows: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 15 s. GAPDH was used as an internal reference gene. The relative expression level of genes was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Western blot analysis

Cells were collected by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 5 min, washed with PBS, and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer containing phosphatase inhibitor, protease inhibitor, and 1 × PMSF on ice for 30 min. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein quantitative kit. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk powder in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing with TBST three times, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1.5 h. Protein bands were visualized using ultra-sensitive luminescent solution and detected by an automatic chemiluminescence imaging analysis system. The relative expression level of proteins was quantified using ImageJ software, with Actin as an internal reference.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. 500 μg of total protein was incubated with 2 μg of anti-GLI1 antibody or anti-Bmi1 antibody at 4 °C overnight, followed by the addition of 20 μL of Protein A/G agarose beads and incubation for another 4 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed five times with IP wash buffer, and the immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS loading buffer for 10 min. The eluted proteins were analyzed by Western blot using anti-Bmi1 antibody or anti-GLI1 antibody. IgG was used as a negative control.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were baked at 65 °C for 90 min, dewaxed in xylene, and hydrated through a gradient of ethanol. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2 at 37 °C for 8 min. Antigen retrieval was performed by boiling the sections in citrate antigen retrieval solution. Sections were blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 30 min, then incubated with primary antibodies against GLI1, SOX2, Bmi1 and Ki67 at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with secondary antibody at 37 °C for 30 min. DAB chromogenic reagent was used for color development, and hematoxylin was used for counterstaining. Sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral resin. Images were captured using a light microscope, and the positive expression was evaluated by ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence staining for CD133 and CD44

Tumor stem cells (HBL-1 and SUDHL-2) cultured for 10–14 days were divided into blank, OE-GLI1, and sh-GLI1 groups. Cells were washed twice with pre-cooled PBS, resuspended in 500 μL PBS, and incubated with 0.5 μg CD44 antibody at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark. After discarding the supernatant, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and then incubated with 0.5 μg CD133 antibody at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. Following two additional PBS washes, cells were resuspended in 500 μL PBS and observed under a fluorescence microscope. Images were captured to analyze the expression of CD133 and CD44.

In vivo xenograft tumor assay

Female NOD-scid IL2Rg mice (6–8 weeks old) were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment (20–26 °C, 40–70% humidity) and adaptively raised for 5 days. A total of 12 mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6 per group): Control group: Subcutaneous injection of HBL-1 cells + intraperitoneal injection of DMSO; GANT61 group: Subcutaneous injection of HBL-1 cells + intraperitoneal injection of GANT61 (50 mg/kg/d, formulated in DMSO at 5 mg/mL). HBL-1 cells (1 × 107 cells/100 μL PBS) were injected subcutaneously into the right axilla of each mouse. When tumors reached ~ 100 mm3, GANT61 or DMSO was administered 3 times/week. Tumor volume was calculated as V = 0.5 × length × width2 using vernier calipers every other day. On day 21 post-injection, mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium, and tumor tissues were dissected, photographed, and weighed for subsequent analysis. All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Quanzhou First Hospital (Ethics Approval Code: 2023K068). All experimental methods carried out in this study were performed in full compliance with relevant institutional guidelines and regulations.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 19.0 software. Comparison between two groups was performed using t-test, and comparison among multiple groups was performed using one-way ANOVA. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphs were plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software.

Abnormal high co-expression of GLI1, Bmi1 in DLBCL tissues

IHC results revealed that GLI1 was significantly highly expressed in DLBCL tissues compared with normal lymphoid tissues, with stronger staining intensity and wider distribution of positive cells. Bmi1 and SOX2, which are associated with cancer stem cell (CSC) properties, also exhibited abnormally high expression consistent with GLI1; all three proteins were presented with high expression in the DLBCL tissues simultaneously (Fig. 1A, B). Discussion

DLBCL is characterized by marked clinical and biological heterogeneity. While the majority of patients achieve durable remission with R-CHOP therapy, the 30–40% who develop relapsed/refractory disease face a dismal prognosis due to aggressive tumor behavior and therapy resistance. The malignant progression of DLBCL is a complex process involving multiple signaling pathways and biological processes18. EMT and CSC properties are key factors contributing to tumor invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance19. EMT/MET and related factors are key focuses in studies on the metastatic mechanisms of DLBCL and represent potential therapeutic targets20. The Hh signaling pathway is aberrantly activated in various tumors and plays an important role in regulating cell proliferation, survival, EMT, and CSC properties21,22. In this study, we demonstrated that GLI1, a key transcription factor in the Hh pathway, regulates EMT and CSC properties in DLBCL through direct interaction with Bmi1, providing a novel therapeutic target for DLBCL.

First, we found that GLI1 is abnormally highly expressed in DLBCL tissues, and its expression is positively correlated with Bmi1 and SOX2. This is consistent with previous studies showing that the Hh pathway is activated in DLBCL23, suggesting that GLI1 may play an oncogenic role in DLBCL. However, the small clinical sample size imposes inherent limitations on the reliability of our statistical analyses. Larger patient cohorts must be enrolled in follow-up studies to corroborate the synchronous high expression of GLI1, Bmi1, and SOX2 in DLBCL tissues. Nevertheless, we acknowledge a key limitation of the current immunohistochemical analysis: separate single-marker IHC staining merely demonstrates spatially concurrent high expression of GLI1, Bmi1, and SOX2 across tumor regions and cannot confirm their co-expression within individual malignant cells. Future investigations will address this gap by performing dual/triple immunofluorescence staining paired with quantitative co-localization analysis to rigorously validate single-cell co-localization of these three proteins.

We further confirmed the inhibitory effect of GANT61, a pan-GLI inhibitor, on DLBCL cells. GANT61 significantly inhibited the proliferation of HBL-1 and SUDHL-2 cells in a dose- and time-dependent manner, induced G0/G1 phase arrest and apoptosis, which is consistent with the effect of GANT61 in other tumors24. A series of studies on related B-cell malignancies have confirmed that GLI1 is the main effector molecule downstream of the Hh pathway, while GLI2 plays a secondary regulatory role in DLBCL cell biological functions25,26. These results indicate that targeting GLI1 may be an effective strategy for inhibiting DLBCL cell growth. Since GANT61 also acts on GLI2, we cannot completely exclude the partial involvement of GLI2. Subsequent studies will construct GLI2 knockdown cell lines to further distinguish the respective functions of GLI1 and GLI2 in DLBCL.

EMT is a key process in tumor metastasis, and the switch between E-cadherin and Vimentin is a hallmark of EMT27. Unlike solid epithelial tumors, lymphomas and other hematological malignancies lack true epithelial identity; accordingly, concurrent quantification of E-cadherin and Vimentin is an approach to assess EMT-like phenotypic plasticity within these blood-borne neoplasms28. Our results showed that GANT61 downregulated the expression of Vimentin and upregulated the expression of E-cadherin in DLBCL cells, indicating that GLI1 inhibition can reverse EMT. Conversely, GLI1 overexpression promoted EMT, while GLI1 silencing inhibited EMT. These results suggest that GLI1 plays a crucial role in regulating EMT in DLBCL. Nevertheless, we acknowledge a notable limitation of the present study: only a small panel of classical EMT markers was interrogated at both mRNA and protein levels. High-throughput transcriptome sequencing combined with EMT gene set enrichment analysis will be performed in future research to systematically verify the regulatory effect of GLI1 on the overall EMT transcriptional program.

CSCs are considered the root cause of tumor recurrence and drug resistance, and Bmi1 and SOX2 are important regulators of CSC self-renewal and maintenance29. We found that GANT61 downregulated the expression of Bmi1 and SOX2, and inhibited tumor sphere formation, a key characteristic of CSCs. GLI1 overexpression enhanced tumor sphere formation and upregulated the expression of Bmi1 and SOX2, while GLI1 silencing had the opposite effect. These results indicate that GLI1 is involved in maintaining CSC properties in DLBCL.

The molecular mechanism by which GLI1 regulates EMT and CSC properties in DLBCL remains unclear. Previous studies have shown that GLI1 can interact with other proteins to regulate downstream signaling pathways30. Co-IP assay in our study confirmed a direct interaction between GLI1 and Bmi1 in DLBCL cells. Bmi1 is a key regulator of stem cell self-renewal and EMT31. As a canonical transcription factor, GLI1 may modulate BMI1 expression via two potential regulatory layers. On one hand, GLI1 could directly occupy the promoter region of BMI1 to drive its transcription at the transcriptional level32. On the other hand, physical binding between GLI1 and Bmi1 protein may block the ubiquitin–proteasome-mediated degradation of Bmi1, consequently elevating Bmi1 protein stability at the post-translational level. Consistent with our Co-IP findings, GLI1 and Bmi1 assemble into a direct protein complex in DLBCL cells independent of intermediate adaptor proteins. Nevertheless, this study bears a notable limitation: we have only validated the physical interaction and functional correlation between these two molecules, without dissecting their precise upstream regulatory hierarchy. Future investigations will integrate ChIP-qPCR, protein half-life assays and ubiquitination profiling experiments to fully delineate the exact molecular mechanism underlying GLI1-mediated control of Bmi1.

We further found that the Bmi1 inhibitor reversed the pro-tumor effects of GLI1 overexpression, including promoting proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, promoting EMT, and enhancing CSC properties. Conversely, the Bmi1 agonist partially restored the inhibitory effects caused by GLI1 silencing. These results suggest that GLI1 regulates EMT and CSC properties in DLBCL through interaction with Bmi1, forming the Hh-GLI1-Bmi1 regulatory axis. Notably, HBL-1 and SUDHL-2 exhibited divergent molecular responses to Bmi1 inhibitor treatment, a discrepancy we attribute to their distinct molecular subtypes: HBL-1 represents ABC-type DLBCL, while SUDHL-2 belongs to the GCB subtype18. These two subtypes display intrinsic disparities in endogenous Bmi1 protein stability and post-translational modification profiles. Importantly, the Bmi1 inhibitor utilized in this study primarily targets the functional enzymatic activity of Bmi1 rather than triggering its proteasomal degradation. In HBL-1 cells, Bmi1 protein possesses high intrinsic stability, explaining why total Bmi1 protein abundance remains largely unaltered following inhibitor exposure. Apart from the pharmacological intervention above, we also noticed limitations associated with the use of recombinant Bmi1 protein as an agonist, particularly its low cellular delivery efficiency. For this reason, we will employ a gene rescue assay with Bmi1 overexpression plasmids to conduct further validation in follow-up investigations.

The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is closely associated with EMT and CSC properties33. Our results showed that GANT61 downregulated the expression of β-catenin, while GLI1 overexpression upregulated β-catenin. This suggests that the Hh-GLI1-Bmi1 axis may crosstalk with the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to regulate EMT and CSC properties in DLBCL. However, the specific crosstalk mechanism requires further investigation.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that GLI1 is abnormally activated in DLBCL and regulates EMT and CSC properties through direct interaction with Bmi1. Targeting the Hh-GLI1-Bmi1 axis may provide a novel therapeutic strategy for DLBCL. However, this study has some limitations. First, it was conducted in vitro, and in vivo experiments are needed to verify the findings. Second, the clinical significance of the Hh-GLI1-Bmi1 axis needs to be further confirmed in a larger cohort of DLBCL patients. Third, the specific crosstalk between the Hh-GLI1-Bmi1 axis and other signaling pathways requires further investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, our study reveals that GLI1 plays an oncogenic role in DLBCL by regulating EMT and CSC properties through direct interaction with Bmi1, forming the Hh-GLI1-Bmi1 regulatory axis. Targeting this axis may provide a novel therapeutic approach for DLBCL patients, especially those with relapsed or refractory disease. Further in vivo and clinical studies are needed to validate the therapeutic potential of targeting the Hh-GLI1-Bmi1 axis in DLBCL.
