 CSP Update

• China is building two 350-megawatt (MW) CSP projects with 14 hours of storage, each with three
  towers, three solar fields, and one central power block. These “Gen-3” projects have an investment
  cost of $7B each and will sell power for $0.07/kWh.
• Swiss company Synhelion, which uses high-temperature process heat (partially made from
  heliostats and a solar receiver) to convert bio-waste into synthetic fuel, has delivered its first barrel
  of aviation fuel to SWISS airlines and ran its first car on its “solar gasoline.”
 • The company is still in its piloting stage.
• In 2024, Brazil grew its market 11%, moving past Turkey to be the second-largest solar thermal heat
  market, after China.
 • In 2024, San Paolo passed a law requiring solar water heaters in a variety of buildings.
 • Half of Brazil’s solar thermal market is made up of products related to heating swimming
 pools.

 How HR 1: The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBB)
 Affects Solar
• Clean Production Tax Credit (PTC) and ITC (48E, 45Y) begins ramping down for projects starting construction after 2032 (e.g., storage), but solar
  and wind must be placed in service before 2028, EXCEPT for any PV/wind projects that begin construction before by July 4, 2026 (12 months after
  enactment).
• Projects starting construction after 2025 cannot get 48E, 45Y, or Advanced Manufacturing Production Tax Credit (MPTC; 45X) if (1) a certain
  percentage of components (which grows over time) comes from a “prohibited foreign entity” or (2) the project is owned or controlled by a
  “prohibited foreign entity.”
 – For 45Y/48E qualifying facilities, the threshold percentage of direct costs of products and components starts at 40% in 2026 and scales up to
 60% in 2029. For storage, it starts at 55% and goes to 75%.
 – For 45X, solar starts at 50% in 2026 and scales up to 85% in 2029.

 IRS Updates the Definition of
 “Commence Construction”
 • On August 15, 2025, the IRS released updated guidance (Notice 2025-42) on how companies seeking the 48E and 45Y Clean Electricity
 Tax Credits can establish that they have started construction on a solar or wind project.
 ◦ This guidance comes in response to a July 7 executive order from the president to issue “new and revised guidance” to make it
 more difficult for a project to be considered under construction unless a “substantial portion” has been built.
 ◦ Per OBBB, solar and wind projects have until July 4, 2026, to start construction if they want to claim the ITC or PTC for projects
 placed in service after 2027.

 • Previously issued IRS guidance on how to establish that a project had started construction created two tests of which a project must
 satisfy at least one:
 ◦ Five percent of the final project’s cost must be incurred on property “integral” to generating electricity (Five Percent Safe Harbor).
 – Developer preferred test, as it is easier to establish.

 ◦ “Physical work of significant nature” is commenced on the project site or on project equipment at the factory (Physical Work
 Test). Work must be “integral” and does not include site clearing, planning, securing financing, or permitting.
 – Typically the less-preferred method by developers because there is more subjectivity and thus less certainty if there is an audit.

 ◦ Projects qualifying under either test must also make continuous progress toward completion once under construction (which can
 be satisfied by finishing within 4 years).

• On July 7, 2025, the president issued an executive order titled “Ending Market Distorting Subsidies for Unreliable, Foreign-Controlled Energy Sources,” directing the
 U.S. Department of the Interior (DOI), with 45 days, to review whether they provide preferential treatment to solar and wind facilities over “dispatchable energy
 sources,” and if so, to end those practices. In response, DOI has taken the following steps:

• On July 15, the U.S. Secretary of the Interior issued a department memo requiring that all “decisions, actions, consultations, and undertakings” related to wind and

 ◦ The memo included 69 activities falling under this procedure, including National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) review, rights of way, and notices to proceed.
 ◦ Legal experts report that the memo shifts decisions historically made at the staff level to the secretary level.
 ◦ In response, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) stopped allowing solar and wind projects to use their Information for Planning and Consultation website,
 which facilitates environmental review and permitting for projects.
 – Information for Planning and Consultation helps determine how certain activities may impact species protected under the Endangered Species Act and
 associated natural resources, and provides suggestions to address these impacts. There are some states and counties that require a record of consultation
 with USFWS as part of their permitting process, but they can’t get that now.

• On July 29, the secretary issued Order 3437, which directs DOI to (1) within 30 days, to identify and make recommendations for ending preferential treatment toward
 wind and solar facilities; (2) within 45 days, prepare a report regarding the economic cost of “intermittent” electricity and the environmental and “military readiness”
 impact of wind facilities; and (3) within 45 days, review any pending litigation challenging the approval of solar and wind facilities.

• On August 1, the secretary issued Order 3438, which directs the Bureau of Land Management to consider reasonable alternatives when conducting NEPA reviews for
 project applications on the basis of capacity density, identifying solar and wind as having the lowest capacity densities. The order questions whether any solar or wind
 projects on federal land are “consistent with the law.”

 Above Average Residential
 Electricity Rates in California
• Although California electricity rates have consistently exceeded the national average, recent double-digit increases in rates,
  causing higher bills, have alarmed consumers, with some regulators, utilities and advocates blaming the rapid deployment of
  solar within the state—particularly distributed solar—as the reason for the increase in costs.
• NREL has not performed its own analysis; however, reported reasons for historically above average electricity rates in
  California (that predate recent events, such as wildfires) include:
 – Relatively low electricity consumption, attributable to a milder climate than other parts of the country, as well as energy
 efficiency investments cause fixed costs (transmission and distribution) to be spread over fewer kWh.
 – Some have argued that California’s size and geography increase pricing due to the large distances that energy travels and
 the diverse topography (e.g., coastlines, high mountains), which can increase costs and rates.
 • Utilities in other states, such as Nevada and Arizona, also have large territories, but they do not experience the same increased rates.
 • California’s three largest investor-owned utilities (IOUs), which sell 90% of the power in the state, have much bigger service territories than
 other electricity providers in the state, which may explain some of their higher costs. The IOUs also have additional requirements that incur
 additional costs, including net billing and low-income assistance.

 – Environmental programs, including renewable portfolio standards, energy efficiency, and wildlife protection, as well as
 encouraging zero-emission vehicle adoption, result in surcharges on electricity bills. About 4% of average IOU rates are
      dedicated to climate-related programs.                                                                              NLR | 59

 Reports State Reasons for
 Increased California Rates
• NREL has not performed its own analysis; however, reports point to several potential reasons why the historically higher-than-
  average rates in California have been exacerbated in recent years. There is disagreement as to the magnitude of the impacts of
  many of the reported causes.
 – UC Berkeley and UCLA law schools estimate that wildfire prevention (tree-trimming, vegetation management, rebuilding
 infrastructure damaged by wildfires, and building underground power lines) and increased fire insurance costs now represent
 7%–13% of ratepayers’ bills and are the primary driver of rising electricity costs in California.
 • In 2024, 24% of Pacific Gas and Electric’s (PG&E’s) revenue requirement1 (and 11% and 12% of Southern California Edison’s
 [SCE’s] and San Diego Gas and Electric’s [SDG&E’s], respectively) were wildfire related.
 • A report from Charles Rivers Associates stated that wildfires increase utilities’ costs of capital because they are viewed as
 riskier investments, which further exacerbates the additional costs of preventing and recovering from wildfires.
 – Despite record profits in 2024, PG&E and SCE net margins were still below the national average (both in 2024 and over
 the past 10 years). From 2013–2024, the cumulative net profit of PG&E (which declared bankruptcy in 2020) was
 negative.
 – SDG&E, which began prioritizing wildfire prevention in 2007 after three major fires, has had far fewer fires than SCE
 and PG&E, and its net margins are also much higher.
         • Nine of the top 20 largest wildfires, by area, have occurred since 2019.                                                             NLR | 60
 1 Revenue requirement is the total amount of money a regulated utility is authorized to collect from its customers over a specific period.

 Reports State Reasons for
 Increased California Rates
 (Continued)
– EIA and various journal articles have stated that wildfires and increased renewable energy result in more
  expenditures on grid infrastructure, including battery energy storage.
 • EIA stated that capital investment from utilities doubled from 2003 to 2023 as aging infrastructure was replaced to be more fire- and storm-resistant. It
 also states that new transmission and distribution lines were built to connect to renewable resources.
 • From 2015–2024, California installed 11.7 GWac (40.1 GWh) of grid-scale batteries at a price of more than $10 billion.1

– EIA reported that there have been increases in California natural gas pricing, which analysts stated was due in part to
  reduced storage (Aliso Canyon reduced capacity after a major leak in 2015) and closed pipelines from leaks and
  explosions. CAISO reported that it is common for natural gas to set wholesale electricity pricing.
– The California Legislative Analyst’s Office wrote that the rapid increase in distributed solar has exacerbated
  California’s already low electricity consumption, increasing rates as the grid electric load reduction from solar spreads
  fixed costs over fewer kWh.
– The LA Times reported that California cannot fully use all the solar energy generated within the state with the
  increased solar production (in part because of a lack of storage capacity), which has caused a significant amount of
  electricity to be exported to neighboring states during peak solar generation hours, often below market rates, which
  the LA Times characterized as effectively subsidized by California consumers.

 Reports State That Variable Rates in
 California Exacerbate Costs for Some
 Customers
• Some customers have experienced even higher rates due to variability in California’s electricity rates.
 – The California Public Advocates Office reported that in 2024, $8.5 billion in costs were shifted from customers in legacy solar
 net energy metering programs to customers without solar.
 • Solar advocates disputed this analysis.
 – Columbia Law also reports that higher-usage customers pay more, due to California’s tiered pricing, where rates increases
 with usage (to encourage energy efficiency).
 • Tiered pricing is designed to encourage energy efficiency; however, under some circumstances, high-income residents who take advantage of
 energy efficiency and solar incentives may pay less than the cost of the delivered energy.
 – As is the case with most states, California electricity rates are reduced for low-income customers. CPUC reports a 30%–35%
 discount in pricing.
 – The California Legislative Analyst’s Office also reports higher rates from the three leading IOUs in the state compared to all
 other IOUs and public-owned utilities in the state. The three leading IOUs are subject to more regulations, which require
 additional spending not mandated to other electric utilities in the state.
• Proponents have argued for different billing and compensation structures to alleviate some of these cost shifts. These measures
  include net billing, time-of-use rates, non-bypassable charges, and fixed charges. However, cost shifting is still likely to continue
  to some extent due to mismatches between rate structure and cost to serve (e.g., differences in cost to serve customers using
  distribution lines with fewer customers).

 Various Groups Detail Strategies To
 Lower California’s Electricity Costs

• The governor’s office, analysts, and industry experts have called for proactive wildfire mitigation strategies. These would
  still represent large initial costs that would be reflected in current rates.
• California legislators have proposed reducing bill compensation for distributed PV.
 – California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) reformed net metering (NEM 3.0) in an effort to better align the value of
 exported solar electricity with compensation, but systems installed under previous policies still receive full retail rate
 net metering.
 • PV systems under previous NEM programs will eventually switch over after 20 years of system operation; however, some California legislators are
 attempting to have the switch occur earlier.

• Efforts are also underway to change the rate structure in California beyond NEM 3.0. Starting in late 2025, a larger
  monthly fixed charge, regardless of use, will be implemented.
 – The change aims to redistribute the costs of infrastructure maintenance. The large utilities and the CPUC president
 argue that the change will also help make the bills more equitable.
 – The fixed charge will be $24.15 for most and $12 for low-income customers.

 Additional Reported Proposals To
 Lower California Electricity Costs
• In October 2024, the California governor signed an executive order aimed at curbing increases in electricity bills. The
  order:
 – Directs more funds from the Cap-and-Trade Program as credits to electricity bills. In March 2025, the governor
 announced that customers would get, on average, a $137 credit in April, and receive another credit in October.
 – Directs the CPUC and California Energy Commission to make recommendations for lowering electricity bills, including
 evaluating whether utilities were taking cost-effective wildlife mitigation measures.
• Several bills have been introduced that intend to cut electricity costs or prevent cost-shifting.
 – The original text of House Bill 942 would end net metering after 10 years (instead of 20) if a customer sold their home
 (though this was later removed from the bill).
 – Senate Bill 254, which incorporated previous years’ bills, would require CPUC to consider the cost-effectiveness of
 wildfire prevention; limit the profits utilities can receive on certain spending, including wildfire expenditures; publicly
 finance transmission and storage build-out; and benchmark rate increases against inflation.
