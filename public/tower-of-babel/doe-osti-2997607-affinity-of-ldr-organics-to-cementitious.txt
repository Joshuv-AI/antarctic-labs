 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The Hanford site stores approximately 56 million gallons of radioactive and hazardous waste in
underground storage tanks. The current low-activity waste (LAW) vitrification facility does not have the
capacity to immobilize all of the LAW. Therefore, cementitious waste forms are being evaluated as a cost-
effective supplemental waste treatment, focusing on the solidification/immobilization of Land Disposal
Restricted (LDR) organics, which is currently not recognized by the Environmental Protection Agency
(EPA) as a standard treatment method (40 CFR 268.42). Previous efforts by the EPA and waste
management agencies have highlighted the potential for cementitious materials to retain certain organic
species through physical (encapsulation) and chemical (sorption) interactions.

This study investigates the interactions of LDR organics with cementitious materials used for the
solidification and immobilization of LAW from the Hanford site. This study addresses the limitations and
improvements identified in prior proof-of-concept testing and expands upon these by incorporating
improved experimental methods, a broader selection of compounds, and affinity testing of compound
mixtures to explore potential co-sorption or competition effects.

A diverse range of 15 compounds was selected for sorption testing, chosen to represent various chemical
interactions with cementitious materials. These include neutral and negatively charged compounds at a high
pH, common in LAW and cementitious porewater/leachate environments. Results indicate that specific
organics exhibit a strong affinity for cementitious materials. The use of activated carbon in Cast Stone
significantly improved the retention of organic compounds. 4,6-dinitro-o-cresol and pentachlorophenol
demonstrated a preference for binding via van der Waals interactions and hydrophobic driving forces to the
selected solids.

These findings support the feasibility of using a cementitious waste form with added activated carbon for
the treatment and immobilization of a majority of LDR organics in LAW. Future work should focus on
testing additional representative compounds to fill data gaps and to cover all relevant organics.

1.0 Introduction
The Hanford site has approximately 56 million gallons of radioactive and hazardous waste stored in
underground storage tanks. Alternate treatment of Low Activity Wate (LAW) by
solidification/immobilization in a cementitious waste form is being investigated as a potential lower-cost
supplement to baseline vitrification. Application of an ambient temperature process rather than vitrification
creates the need to assess potential treatment options for Land Disposal Restriction (LDR) organic
chemicals in Hanford LAW since thermal destruction by the vitrification process will no longer be part of
the treatment train.

Currently, the solidification/immobilization of organic molecules such as LDR organics is not a recognized
treatment standard by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) as defined by/in 40 CFR 268.42.1
However, in 2001, the EPA provided notice of information concerning stabilization/immobilization of
organics and a draft memorandum for comment. † The key paragraph in the draft memorandum is found in
Section 2, paragraph (b) which states:

 New adsorptive stabilization reagents such as modified clays, zeolites and specialized
 activated carbon have been specifically developed to entrap or immobilize certain types of
 hazardous organic constituents, particularly some of those found in contaminated soils.
 For the most part, the adsorbed organics cannot be easily desorbed. There are, however,
 certain conditions under which the adsorbed organics could desorb. For instance, co-
 disposal with other organic wastes could cause the adsorbed organics to desorb, and
 changes in the disposal conditions, such as fluctuations in pH, may have similar impacts.
 We recommend that stabilization reagents be examined on a site-specific basis to
 determine that they will be effective in the pH range expected in the leachate and that their
 adsorptive capacity would not be exceeded as the waste ages over time. These factors
 should be considered by the implementing regulatory agency. The use of these stabilization
 reagents to control the mobility of the hazardous organic constituents can thus be
 considered to provide substantial treatment through immobilization rather than being a
 type of impermissible dilution.

While this memo was never finalized, it demonstrates that there has been historic recognition that there are
physical and chemical interactions within cementitious matrices that can retain certain organic species.
For this reason, Hanford Tank Waste Operations and Closure (H2C) is working to develop a treatment
variance specific for Hanford LAW to establish solidification/immobilization in cementitious waste forms
that can provide acceptable treatment to that specified in 40 CFR 268.42 for some organic LDR constituents.

There are 132 LDR organics that are reasonably expected to be present in the Hanford LAW.2 Establishing
that some or all of these 132 LDR organics interact or are immobilized by cementitious materials through
encapsulation, chemisorption, and other interactions (i.e. evaporation during setting) would support a basis
for an LDR treatment variance petition. However, previous studies of organic stabilization using
cementitious materials (Portland cement, fly ash, slag) are largely waste/process specific, giving rise to
mixed efficacy in treatment.3-7 Therefore, process and compound (or groups of compounds) specific testing
is required to support any proposed variance.

† See 66 F.R. 52918 (October 18, 2001), and the Draft Interpretative Memorandum on the Stabilization of Organic-Bearing

Hazardous Wastes dated September 2001 identified in the Federal Register notice. Although EPA requested comments on the draft
memorandum, there is no record of EPA publishing the comments received or finalizing the memorandum so that the draft
memorandum is the basis for this study.

 1

During solidification/immobilization, cementitious material treats organic and inorganic species through
both encapsulation and chemisorption. Encapsulation effects are the physical isolation of species wherein
organic compounds may be held in the solid matrix, absorbed, slowing their diffusion and advection of the
waste form. Chemisorption is the chemical interaction of the organic species with the mineral surface of
the solids and is the result of chemical bonding between species and solid matrix. For chemisorption,
organic compounds interact with solids through both absorption (into the solids) and adsorption (onto the
solids). Cementitious materials have a variety of pore structures, so it is difficult to differentiate between
absorption and adsorption. These will be referred to as “sorption” to encompass both processes.

Sorption of organics to cementitious materials during both the set/curing process and any potential gradual
remobilization are defined by three underlying mechanisms:
 • ionic interactions with charged or partially charged sites on the surface8, (this can include ion
 exchange and complexation type processes)
 • hydrophobic forces driving the molecule out of the aqueous phase towards or into a phase with
 more similar solubility parameters or into the porous microstructure of cementitious materials, and
 • van der Waals interactions between polar areas on the compound and solid surfaces.9

Proof-of-concept testing performed in FY24 was successful in demonstrating the affinity of some of the
organic compounds to cementitious materials with and without activated carbon.10 Both sorption and
leaching tests were used in this study to help isolate sorption and encapsulation effects during
solidification/immobilization. For charged interactions, the high pH (>13) of both the LAW and the
cementitious porewater/leachate results in either neutral or negatively charged organic molecules for all
functional groups (e.g., carboxyl and hydroxyl) and a net negative charge on the material surface for both
cementitious material and activated carbons. Therefore, minimal direct charge interactions are expected
directly between the compound and the surface. However, cation bridging, likely through divalent calcium,
was evident as two phthalic acid isomers, 1,4-benzendicarboxylic acid and 1,2-benzendicarboxylic acid,
were removed to below detection limits during sorption tests and not detected in the leachate tests. These
compounds both have two negatively charged carboxyl groups which facilitate cation bridging. Hydroxyl
groups did not facilitate these interactions, though 4,6-trichlorophenol and pentachlorophenol have a high
an affinity for Cast Stone with added activated carbon. These compounds had high octanol-water partition
coefficients (logKow) compared to the others tested, indicating a compound preference to be out of the
solution and into the organic phase, likely through a combination of van der Waals interactions and
hydrophobic driving forces. The affinity of 4-chloroaniline for unmodified Cast Stone and the affinity of
o-cresol for Cast Stone with added activated carbon highlights the overall impact electronegative functional
groups interacting with the surface through van der Waals interactions. Several volatile compounds were
tested. However, these high vapor pressure molecules very likely escaped the system, and the interactions
were not quantified. As such, it was concluded that these compounds would likely behave similarly in a
scaled grouting operation and further testing was not performed.

Though this proof-of-concept testing was successful, several improvements were identified. The present
study sought to address these improvements and expand testing by:
 1) Using improved experiment methods: The background solution of the positive controls was
 adjusted to a higher pH to address changes in compound solubility between cement (Cast Stone)
 supernate and pure water,
 2) Adding several different compounds to represent more of the 132 LDR organics, and
 3) Performing affinity tests compounds in a mixture to establish if compound-to-compound
 interactions either in solution (cosolvent effects) or on the surface (co-sorption or competition
 effects) impact the affinity of each compound.

 2

Overall, results produced through this expanded testing strengthens the argument that the LDR organics are
indeed treated through solidification via cementitious materials when a binding material (e.g. activated
carbon) is included.

Cast Stone was made with 8% Type IL Portland cement from Lafarge, 47% blast furnace slag (Lafarge
NUCEM Slag), and 45% fly ash from Centralia. The inclusion of activated carbon as an additive was also

mesh) to the Cast Stone.

For a clean Cast Stone grout (without organics), 43.9 g of cement, 257.9 g of slag, and 246.9 g of fly ash
were added to a plastic bag and blended by hand until homogeneous. For samples containing activated
carbon, 5.5 g (~1 wt%) of activated carbon was also added and blended. A water to premix ratio of 0.5

Once visually homogeneous, the slurry was poured into three 2” by 4” cylindrical molds and allowed to
cure for 28 days.

Organic-containing Cast Stone was formed by adding 90 mg of each organic compound to 353.9 g salt
simulant. By adding 90 mg of each organic, the target concentration of the cured grout was ~100 mg/kg of
each organic compound per mass of final material. The high concentration of organic compounds formed
micelles/discrete organic phases, preventing complete dissolution in the salt solution as shown in Figure
2-1. The organic-containing solution was mixed in a 500 mL volumetric flask for 24 hours to saturate the
salt solution with organics. Before mixing the wet and dry materials together, the organic solution was
transferred to a large pitcher and stirred with a Caframo overhead mixer with an impeller attachment for 3
minutes in a fume hood. Next, 548.9 g of dry mix materials were slowly poured into solution while the
impeller spun at about 200 rpm. The grout was mixed for approximately 5 minutes with frequent stops to
break up clumps and remove material from the walls of the container using a large disposable laboratory
spatula. Once the grout looked homogenous with no clumps, it was poured into three cylindrical borosilicate
glass jars with polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) screw top lids to cure for 28 days. Glass jars were used to
minimize the potential volatilization of the organic compounds.

2.3 Batch Sorption Tests
Sorption tests were performed for organic compounds on the clean (without organics) Cast Stone grout.
After curing for at least 4 weeks, the monoliths were removed by cutting the plastic mold with a cast saw.
The monolith was then size-reduced/crushed with a hammer. The crushed material was size-selected
between 1.2 mm (16 mesh) and 9.5 mm (3/8 in). This size range was selected to remove fines (< 16 mesh)
from the standard <3/8” used for Toxic Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP).13

The size-selected material (17 g) was added to 30 ml borosilicate sample vials with PTFE screw top lids
and spiked with 17 mL of various organic-containing Cast Stone supernate solutions. Cast Stone supernate
solution was made by soaking a clean Cast Stone Monolith in 1.75 L of DI water for a week. The supernate
was filtered through a 0.45 micron vacuum filter before use. The supernate solution was assumed to be
close to that of pure water (1 g/mL).

Individual organic-containing Cast Stone supernate solutions were spiked with 100 mg/L of each organic.
The organic solutions were mixed in volumetric flasks with Cast Stone supernate solution. For naphthalene,
diphenylamine, and 2,4-dinitrophenol, which have solubilities below 100 mg/L, the stock solutions
contained 10, 24, and 24 mg/L respectively. Also, after preparing the first pentachlorophenol sample, an
undissolved portion of the compound was discovered. Therefore, the experiment was repeated. 17 mL of
the stock solutions were spiked into the 30 ml borosilicate sample vials and placed on a rotary mixer at
ambient temperature for 7 days. At the end of testing, samples were pipetted into 4 ml borosilicate sample
vials with PTFE screw top lids while minimizing headspace. Samples were not filtered to ensure
undissolved organics within the aqueous phase remained in solution, which is especially important for

 7

samples containing multiple organic species. Once taken, samples were refrigerated before analysis to
minimize loss of volatile compounds. The pH of each sorption sample was measured.

Sorption testing was also performed with two groups of organic solutions. The first group (Group 1) of
sorption tests used a stock solution containing terephthalic acid, 4-chloroaniline, 2,4,6-trichlorophenol,
pentachlorophenol, and o-cresol each with a target concentration of 100 mg/L. The other group (Group 2)
of sorption tests used a stock solution containing all organics listed in Table 2-1, except for N-
nitrosomethylamine.

2.4 Leachate Tests
The TCLP was followed for the three batches of organic-containing grouts to study organics leaching of
the grout. For accurate results, the extraction fluid must be 20 times the weight of the solid phase, per TCLP
Method 1311.13 Borosilicate glass containers with PTFE screw top lids were used. Each organic-containing
grout sample was analyzed for organic volatiles extraction and for non-volatile organic extraction. The
same extraction fluid and procedure were used to prepare both tests, but the organic volatiles extraction
requires zero headspace to prevent sample loss due to volatilization.13

To find the correct extraction fluid, the procedure in Section 7.1.4 of the TCLP 1311 method was followed.13
A clean grout sample containing activated carbon was used to determine the extraction fluid. The grout was
initially sized with a hammer to a diameter of ≤1 mm. Then, 5 g of grout was transferred to a 500 mL

2.5 Extractions
Aliquots were prepared for volatile organics analysis (VOA) by sub-sampling 1 mL of the submitted parent
sample, followed by 1:5 dilution using deionized water. The sample aliquot was spiked with internal
standards consisting of deuterated halogenated aromatics and deuterated toluene. Samples were sealed in
VOA headspace vials.

Samples for HPLC analysis were processed by pH adjustment and organic extraction. A 1 mL sub-aliquot
of the parent sample was diluted into 2.5 mL of phosphate buffer (H2PO4- and HPO42-) for pH adjustment.
Further adjustment with phosphoric acid or sodium hydroxide was performed to attain an ideal pH range
of 5.5 – 7.5. Organic extraction was performed on the buffered sample by the addition of 6.5 mL of HPLC-
grade acetonitrile. To some samples, phase separation was further induced by the addition of milligrams of
sodium chloride. The organic supernate was removed for analysis.

Samples of 100-mesh ground solids were prepared for HPLC by immersion into 6.5 mL of acetonitrile for
1 hour. Following immersion, phosphate buffer and (if necessary) sodium chloride was added. The organic
supernate was removed and reserved for analysis.

2.6 Analysis
The organic species in Table 2-1 were quantified by VOA and/or HPLC. N-Nitrosomethylamine samples
were processed via gas chromatography – mass spectrometry, with sample introduction performed by purge
and trap sparging. Chromatography was performed on an Agilent 8890 multimode inlet gas chromatograph
(Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), coupled with an Agilent 5977B extractor-source, single
quadrupole mass selective detector. Purge and trap sparging was performed at 35 ºC using an OI-Corp 4760
sample concentrator (Xylem Inc., Washington, D.C.) with a Tenax TA (2,6-diphenylene oxide) sorptive
trap, coupled with an OI-Corp 4100 automatic water and soil sample processor. Data processing was
performed using Agilent MSD Chemstation quantitative software.

For HPLC samples, analysis was performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II with a DyChrom Chemcosorb
5-ODS-UH (3.2 mm x 250 mm) column using a gradient method utilizing water and acetonitrile for the
mobile phase. The analytes were measured by a UV-Vis detector over a broad UV wavelength range of 190
– 400 nm, with a 4 nm bandwidth and a reference wavelength of 360 nm with a 20 nm reference bandwidth.

UV/Vis analysis was performed on 2 mL of solution using a PerkinElmer Lambda 850 UV/Vis
spectrometer. Spectra were collected from 800 nm to 250 nm using tungsten-halogen and deuterium lamps
at a scan speed of 266.75 nm/min.

3.0 Results and Discussion

3.1 Individual Compound Sorption Tests
Most compounds demonstrated affinity for the solid during the sorption tests for individual compounds.
Individual sorption tests were completed for 11 different compounds. Of the 11 compounds, data was
available for all but naphthalene. Naphthalene was not detected in the positive control or the batch sorption
test samples to Cast Stone with or without activated carbon. Naphthalene appears to leave the system even
when stored within a Teflon-capped, glass sample vial with minimal headspace. Naphthalene likely adhered
to the vial’s walls due to its limited solubility and the strong hydrophobic driving force from the aqueous
phase. Naphthalene was detected and did demonstrate a high affinity to the solids within the test containing
a mixture of compounds (see discussion below). The average pH for the positive control, Cast Stone, and
Cast Stone with activated carbon were 12.1 ± 0.1, 13.0 ± 0.1, and 13.0 ± 0.1, respectively.

Most compounds demonstrated an increased affinity for Cast Stone with activated carbon compared to Cast
Stone alone (Figure 3-1). Pentachlorophenol when in contact with Cast Stone containing activated carbon,
resulting in over 90% of the compound being sorbed to the solid. Pentachlorophenol has a large
hydrophobic driving force from the solution. These results correlate well with the results from proof-of-
concept testing. Future tests should include isotherms to evaluate the capacity of both Cast Stone and
activated carbon for the LDR organics. 4-nitroaniline and 2,4-dinitrophenol showed similar affinities for
the solid phase with an increase in affinity for Cast Stone with activated carbon. These two compounds

 11

contain polar nitro-groups and do not have high Kow values; as such, van der Waals interactions are expected
to be the dominant mechanism. 4-Nitroaniline had similar sorption as 4-chloroaniline, a comparable
compound, during proof-of-concept testing.

Benzoic acid, p-nitrophenol, p-chloro-m-cresol, and N-nitrosomethylamine demonstrated the lowest
affinity for Cast Stone. Benzoic acid was selected for testing to understand whether the high affinity of
phthalic acid could be attributed to a single negative carboxyl group facilitating cation bridging. The low
affinity of benzoic acid to both Cast Stone with and without activated carbon suggests that two carboxyl
groups are needed to facilitate this type of interaction. p-Nitrophenol showed no measurable sorption onto
the solid phase, while p-chloro-m-cresol and N-nitrosomethylamine had only ~30% and ~40% of the
organics onto the solid phase, respectively. Each demonstrated a significant increase in sorption from the
solution when activated carbon was present, indicating a preference for the carbon.

Pyridine, 4,6-dinitro-o-cresol, and diphenylamine each demonstrated slightly decreased sorption when in
contact with Cast Stone with activated carbon compared to Cast Stone alone. 4,6-Dinitro-o-cresol showed
a high percent sorbed, and the difference between Cast Stone and Cast Stone with activated carbon is likely
insignificant. Pyridine and diphenylamine both contain amine groups which would be neutral at high pH
with otherwise non-polar structures. Each may not facilitate the van der Waals interactions that appear to
drive sorption for the compounds tested.

Overall, at least 50% of all LDR compounds of concern sorbed to the solids for Cast Stone with activated
carbon, indicating a significant presence of the compound in the solid phase. Therefore, treatment through
retention on the solids can be inferred when solidifying LDR organics within low-activity waste. The
removals did not simply follow the Kow, indicating that van der Waals interactions are important for overall
affinity to the surface.

3.2 Five Compound Mixture Sorption Tests (Group 1)
Terephthalic acid, 4-chloroaniline, 2,4,6-trichlorophenol, pentachlorophenol, and o-cresol were selected for
the first group of compound sorption tests, as each had shown varied affinity for both Cast Stone with and
without activated carbon in the proof-of-concept testing (Figure 3-2). The pH for the positive control, Cast
Stone, and Cast Stone with activated carbon were 12.0, 13.0, and 13.0, respectively. The use of Cast Stone
supernate in this study, as opposed to the deionized water used in the proof-of-concept testing, sought to
address the pH difference in the positive control and the sample solutions. This was seemingly successful
within the individual compound sorption tests; however, within the group tested, a number of organic
compounds had higher concentrations in Cast Stone-only samples than for the positive control, as
exemplified by negative calculated percent sorbed (Figure 3-2). This was especially evident for 2,4,6-
trichlorophenol and pentachlorophenol, for which sorption or percent sorbed values for Cast Stone could
not be calculated in the proof-of-concept testing. All compounds affected by pH (2,4,6-trichlorophenol,
pentachlorophenol, and o-cresol) were those containing phenolic groups that would be ionized at high pH.
The pKa of all compounds is at least 2 log units below the positive control pH, meaning over 95% of each
compound would be ionized. Therefore, this pH effect is unexpected, and further exploration of the higher
concentration in the positive control is warranted. For the two compounds that were seemingly unaffected
by the change in pH, terephthalic acid and 4-chloroaniline showed diminished sorption from the compound
mixture to Cast Stone. The mixture of compounds may create a more favorable environment for organic
compounds within the aqueous phase, especially with the limited number of sorption sites present on the
Cast Stone.

investigated.

The Cast Stone-only sample prepared with organics had visible yellowing of both the solid samples and the
leachate samples (Figure 2-2, Figure 2-3). This coloration was not evident in the Cast Stone containing
activated carbon. This qualitative assessment is consistent with the high retention of organics within the
activated carbon samples. The concentration of organics in the solution prior to solidification within Cast
Stone varied. This was likely due to the presence of discrete organic phases causing heterogeneities across
the subsamples. Similar phenomena likely led to the variabilities within the concentration extracted from
the solid phase. These extracted concentrations were rather variable and high extractions efficiency are
difficult to achieve as such data only provide a qualitative glimpse of the organic within the solids.

The expected concentration due to the ~100 mg/kg in the solids and the 20:1 dilution prescribed in TCLP
leads to a maximum concentration of 5 mg/L in the leachate. The detection limit for each compound was 1
mg/L, therefore if leachate concentrations were below the limits of detection RFs would be at least 80%,
which was indeed the case (Table 3-2, Table 3-3, Table 3-4). UV/Vis analysis confirmed that the filtering
of the TCLP leachate did not remove the compounds. No substantial difference was seen between the
minimal head space (MHS) and traditional samples. Concentrations would be expected to be higher in the
MHS samples as less of the organic would be expected to leave the solution for the gaseous phase. Five
compounds were detected in the TCLP leachate samples. Three of the detected compounds
(pentachlorophenol, terephthalic acid, benzoic acid) were also detected in the TCLP fluid prior to contact,
suggests some interference or contamination from the solution. 2,4,6-trichlorophenol was detected in one
of the leachates from the Cast Stone samples with concentrations well above the 5 mg/L maximum
concentration expected. Such a high concentration suggests a discrete phase may have been present in the
solid prior to leaching. Activated carbon in the sorption tests is speculated to have reduced the possibility
of discrete phases but this should be confirmed with further testing. Pyridine was detected in the Cast Stone
Batch 1 and the Activated Carbon Cast Stone leachates. The concentrations are above the expected 5 mg/L
maximum concentration expected which again suggest discrete phases. The presentence in the leachate
contradicts the strong sorption seen in the batch testing and further investigation is warranted. Between the
identified yellowing of the leachate and the reduction of possible discrete phases, activated carbon appears
to be important in binding the LDR organics within cementitious materials.

4.0 Representative Compounds
A total of 19 compounds (17 LDR organics, phthalic acid, and benzoic acid) have been tested for
interactions with cementitious materials between the work described here and the prior proof-of-concept
testing. Each was selected to investigate the effect of variation in compound structure on the various
interactions possible with the surface. These compounds would represent an additional 76 compounds based
on compound structure (Table 4-1). In previous testing, several compounds (i.e. N-nitrosodimethylamine,
2-butanone, 1,2-dichlorobenzene and toluene) seemingly escaped the system due to volatility. These
compounds and those they represent are labeled as “expected to volatilize”. Although these compounds
seemingly escaped and most are highly volatile, a mass balance was not performed.

With the 76 compounds already represented, an additional 49 are not expected to be able to be tested as
they are either volatile (halogenated solvents, alcohols), will be insoluble, (chlorinated compounds, e.g.,
PCBs), or undergo alkaline hydrolysis (nitrile-based compounds) (Table 4-2). The final seven compounds
of the original 132 LDR organics reasonably expected to be present in the feed tank waste compounds
include three nitrobenzene compounds (1,4-dinitrobenzene, 2,4-dinitrotoluene, nitrobenzene) and four
compounds that were uncategorized (bis(2-chloroethoxy) methane, cyclohexanone, acetophenone, 1,4-

5.0 Conclusions
This study demonstrated that certain LDR organics exhibit affinity towards cementitious materials used in
the potential solidification of LAW from the Hanford site. Through a series of sorption and leaching tests,
it was found that materials such as Cast Stone and activated carbon can effectively immobilize various
organic compounds. Specifically, the incorporation of activated carbon into Cast Stone significantly
increased the retention of organic contaminants compared to Cast Stone alone.

Both van der Waals interactions and hydrophobic driving forces were important for the immobilization
process. For instance, compounds like 2,4,6-trichlorophenol and pentachlorophenol, which have a high
logKow, showed a strong preference for being drawn out of solution and into the activated carbon facilitated
by these interactions. Activated carbon seemingly enhanced the retention as illustrated by the absence of
yellowing in the activated carbon samples compared to Cast Stone alone.

Therefore, the results of this study support the feasibility of using a cementitious waste form with activated
carbon as a viable method for the treatment and immobilization of LDR organics in LAW.

6.0 Path Forward
Efforts should be extended to include the other representative LDR organic compounds identified and other
waste formulations. These materials include a baseline solid matrix and the baseline material with 1-10
wt% activated carbon added to the formulation. Other leachate tests, including EPA 1313 and longer
leaching times, should be explored.

In addition, future testing should increase organic concentration in the solids and isolating targeted or single
compound during testing so that retention factors (RF) for organics in the final solidified/immobilized waste
forms can be further quantified. This approach was attempted in the study however, most concentrations
were below limits of detection. Such can be accomplished by
 1) testing for total concentration of an individual organic (Ci,total in mg/kg) by exposing a
 representative sample of the waste form to an aggressive extractant as performed here.
 2) measuring the mass of the organic per unit sample mass that is released (Ci,released in mg/kg)
 during EPA Method 1311 leach testing13.
 3) Coeluting compounds should be selectively removed so that only one from coeluting set is added
 at a time to ensure meaningful results.

Finally, a model to predict sorption/affinity for cementitious materials should be developed to support the
proposed treatment variance for the immobilization of LDR organics within cementitious materials. A
thermodynamic model would help establish the underlying mechanisms, increasing the scientific evidence
within the variance and extending the theory beyond the exact conditions experimentally tested.
