ITM2A promotes thyroid cancer differentiation through metabolic reprogramming and enhances PD-L1-dependent T-cell responses
Authors: Wuhui Sun, Cheng Xiang, Yaqun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The differentiation status of thyroid cancer (THCA) is closely associated with prognosis; however, the underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to identify hub genes associated with THCA differentiation based on progression-free interval (PFI)-related genes and to explore the underlying mechanisms. The common database datasets were integrated to identify differentially expressed genes, and univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to determine PFI-related genes. Molecular subtypes were constructed using consensus clustering based on PFI-related genes, and the differentiation status of different subtypes was evaluated. Hub genes were subsequently identified using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) regression and multivariate Cox regression analyses. In vitro experiments were conducted to validate the role of the hub gene in regulating dedifferentiation. Two PFI-related molecular subtypes were identified in this study. Cluster 2 exhibited a higher thyroid differentiation score (TDS), more favorable PFI outcomes, and metabolic features predominantly characterized by oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and fatty acid oxidation (FAO). ITM2A was identified as a hub gene. High expression of ITM2A promoted differentiation of THCA cells, suppressed malignant characteristics, and drove a metabolic shift from glycolysis toward FAO. In addition, ITM2A counteracted TGF-β–induced dedifferentiation and epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and enhanced antigen presentation as well as PD-L1–dependent T-cell responses. ITM2A maintained the differentiated state of THCA through metabolic reprogramming and shaped an immunologically favorable microenvironment, suggesting that it may serve as a potential biomarker for prognosis prediction and a therapeutic target for optimizing immunotherapy strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10238-026-02229-w.

Introduction

Thyroid cancer (THCA) is the most common malignancy of the endocrine system, and its incidence has been steadily increasing worldwide [1]. Among its histological subtypes, Papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) accounts for the majority of cases and is generally associated with a favorable prognosis, with a 10-year survival rate exceeding 90% [2, 3]. However, with the progressive accumulation of tumor mutational burden and epigenetic alterations, a subset of differentiated THCA may undergo a continuous evolutionary trajectory from well-differentiated to poorly differentiated and ultimately undifferentiated states, giving rise to poorly differentiated thyroid cancer (PDTC) and eventually transforming into anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC) [4]. Although patients with PTC generally have excellent long-term survival, PDTC and ATC represent highly aggressive subtypes characterized by rapid progression, with median survival times of approximately 3.2 years and 0.5 years, respectively [5]. Tumor differentiation status profoundly influences therapeutic responsiveness. Patients with PDTC and ATC often exhibit resistance to conventional treatments, including surgery and radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy, resulting in increased mortality [6]. These findings underscore dedifferentiation as a critical biological process driving clinical deterioration and poor outcomes in THCA.

During THCA progression, loss of differentiation is commonly accompanied by disruption of thyroid-specific transcriptional programs. Persistent activation of oncogenic signaling pathways, such as MAPK, suppresses the expression and transcriptional activity of thyroid lineage–defining transcription factors, thereby dismantling differentiation-associated gene networks. This process promotes dedifferentiation and enhances tumor invasiveness, metastatic potential, and therapeutic resistance [7–9]. Notably, the majority of PTCs harbor activating BRAF mutations, particularly BRAFV600E, which constitutively stimulate the MAPK pathway [10]. These mutations not only drive dedifferentiation and aggressive phenotypes but also influence metabolic processes, including fatty acid and energy metabolism, thereby linking genetic alterations to tumor metabolic reprogramming [11–13]. Therefore, systematically elucidating the molecular drivers and regulatory networks underlying THCA dedifferentiation is essential for overcoming current therapeutic limitations, restoring differentiation phenotypes, and improving treatment sensitivity. Emerging evidence indicates that metabolic reprogramming is closely linked to tumor differentiation status. Well-differentiated thyroid cells rely on specialized metabolic programs to sustain hormone synthesis, whereas dedifferentiated tumor cells undergo metabolic remodeling to accommodate rapid proliferation and microenvironmental stress [14]. Moreover, metabolic alterations can modulate epigenetic modifications and reshape the immune microenvironment, thereby influencing differentiation maintenance and immune evasion [15]. However, the key regulatory factors mediating the interplay between differentiation loss and metabolic dysregulation in THCA remain to be fully defined.

In this study, we integrated multi-cohort transcriptomic datasets to identify candidate genes significantly associated with progression-free interval (PFI), constructed molecular subtypes of THCA, and systematically characterized their clinical features and metabolic phenotypes. Subsequently, hub genes were identified using Lasso and Cox regression analyses, followed by in vitro functional validation to elucidate their roles in regulating THCA differentiation status, metabolic reprogramming, and immune responses. This study aims to identify core regulators that maintain differentiation phenotypes and metabolic homeostasis in THCA, clarify their contributions to disease progression, and provide a theoretical and experimental foundation for differentiation-restoration strategies and the development of novel targeted therapies.

Methods

Data source and preprocessing

RNA-sequencing data and corresponding clinical information for THCA were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA-THCA cohort, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Patients with complete PFI information were included. Gene expression data GSE29265 (including 9 ATC samples and 20 PTC samples) and GSE33630 (including 11 ATC samples and 49 PTC samples) were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database. The two datasets were merged into a combined cohort, and batch effects between datasets were corrected using the ComBat algorithm implemented in the “sva” R package to ensure comparability across samples. PTC and ATC samples from the GEO cohort were included for differential expression analysis.

Screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

In the TCGA cohort, differential expression analysis between tumor tissues and normal thyroid tissues was performed using the “limma” R package. The screening criteria were set as |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1 and adj. p < 0.05. In the GEO cohort, DEGs between PTC and ATC samples were compared using the same criteria. The “VennDiagram” R package was utilized to identify the intersection of DEGs from the TCGA and GEO groups, yielding overlapping candidate genes for subsequent analysis.

Screening of PFI-related genes

In the TCGA cohort, univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted on the overlapping candidate genes to assess their association with PFI. The “survival” R package was used to calculate the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Genes with p-value < 0.05 were defined as significantly associated with PFI.

To explore the potential biological functions of the PFI-related genes, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the “clusterProfiler” R package. An adjusted p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Consensus clustering for subtyping

Unsupervised consensus clustering analysis was performed based on the expression profiles of the PFI-related genes using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package. The clustering algorithm was set to k-means with 1,000 resamplings to enhance stability. The optimal number of clusters (k-value) was determined by comprehensively evaluating the consensus matrix, cumulative distribution function (CDF) curve, and delta area curve. Patients were subsequently classified into distinct molecular subtypes accordingly.

Analysis of clinical characteristics and survival

The Chi-square test or Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare differences in clinicopathological features between the molecular subtypes. Differences in PFI between subtypes were compared using the restricted mean survival time (RMST) method, calculated with the “survRM2” R package. The thyroid differentiation score (TDS) was calculated as previously described [16], and differences in the expression of differentiation-related genes between subtypes were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Screening of hub genes

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) regression analysis was applied to the PFI-related genes using the “glmnet” R package. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined via 10-fold cross-validation. Candidate genes selected by Lasso were then incorporated into a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The “pROC” R package was used to plot receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculate the area under curve (AUC) to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of ITM2A in distinguishing tumor from normal tissue and its performance across different differentiation statuses. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted using the “rmda” R package to assess the clinical net benefit of the model under different threshold probabilities. Additionally, Patients were divided into high- and low-groups based on the median ITM2A expression. The combined effects of BRAFV600E mutation status and ITM2A expression on differentiation-related markers were analyzed.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA was performed using the GSEA software. The reference gene set used in the analysis was obtained from the KEGG gene sets in the Molecular Signatures Database. The normalized enrichment score was calculated. An adj. p < 0.05 served as the criterion for identifying significantly enriched pathways.

Immune infiltration and metabolic correlation analysis

The CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to estimate the relative proportions of 22 types of immune cells in each tumor sample. Differences in immune cell infiltration levels between molecular subtypes were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Expression data for key metabolic enzymes related to the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), fatty acid oxidation (FAO), and glycolysis were extracted from the TCGA expression matrix. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the correlation between ITM2A and these key metabolic enzymes, calculating the correlation coefficient (r) and corresponding p-value.

Cell culture and transfection

The human normal thyroid cell line Nthy-ori 3 − 1 (Cat. JY068, Jinyuan Biology, Shanghai, China) and human THCA cell lines TPC-1 (Cat. JY259, Jinyuan Biology), BCPAP (Cat. JY586, Jinyuan Biology), CAL-62 (Cat. JY264, Jinyuan Biology), and 8505 C (Cat. MZ-1384, Mingzhou Biology, Ningbo, China) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco). Cells were maintained in a constant-temperature incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. When cell confluence reached 80%-90%, cells were passaged using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution.

The ITM2A overexpression vector was constructed using the pcDNA3.1(-) backbone. The ITM2A coding sequence (CDS, 798 bp) was cloned into the vector via the XbaI and HindIII restriction sites, generating the recombinant plasmid pcDNA3.1(-)-ITM2A, with a total length of 6154 bp. The ITM2A knockdown vector was constructed using the pLKO.1 backbone. The sh-ITM2A plasmid was generated via the AgeI and EcoRI restriction sites, with the target sequence 5’-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTTT-3’. A negative control plasmid, pLKO.1-shNC, was also constructed. Cell transfection was performed using the jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus). One day before transfection, cells were seeded into 6-well plates to achieve 60%-70% confluence at the time of transfection. For transfection, 2 µg of plasmid DNA was mixed with 200 µL of jetPRIME buffer, followed by the addition of 4 µL of jetPRIME reagent. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The transfection complex was then added dropwise to cells containing fresh medium. Cells were harvested 24–48 h post-transfection.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Cat. 74104, QIAGEN, Shanghai, China). RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. For reverse transcription, 1 µg of total RNA was used to synthesize cDNA with the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Cat. 205311, QIAGEN). PCR was performed using the QuantiNova SYBR Green PCR Kit (Cat. 208054, QIAGEN) on a real-time PCR system. The 20 µL reaction mixture contained 10 µL of 2× SYBR Green PCR Master Mix, 0.5 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 2 µL of cDNA template, and 7 µL of nuclease-free water. The reaction protocol was set as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene. The relative expression level of the target gene was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors on ice for 30 min. After lysis, the lysate was centrifuged at 4 °C, 12,000 ×g for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The concentration of each sample was adjusted to be equal using the lysis buffer. An equal amount of protein sample (30 µg) was mixed with 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer and denatured at 95 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE using a 10% separation gel. Electrophoresis was performed at 80 V constant voltage through the stacking gel, and then switched to 120 V constant voltage through the separation gel until the bromophenol blue dye front reached the bottom. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane at 300 mA constant current for 90 min, with the transfer apparatus maintained in an ice bath. The membrane was then blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h. After blocking, the membrane was incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C on a shaker. Primary antibodies used in this study included: ITM2A (1:1000; Cat. HA500598, Huabio, Hangzhou, China), Vimentin (1:1000; Cat. 10366-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), E-cadherin (1:1000; Cat. 20874-1-AP, Proteintech), with GAPDH (1:50000; Cat. ET1601-4, Huabio) as the loading control. After primary antibody incubation, the membrane was washed three times with TBST. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:50000; Cat. HA1001, Huabio) at room temperature for 1 h. After three times of TBST washes, protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit, and images were captured with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Band intensity was semi-quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Sterile coverslips were pre-placed in 24-well plates and coated with poly-lysine to enhance cell adhesion. Cells were seeded onto the coverslips at an appropriate density. When cell confluence reached 60%-70%, the experiment was conducted. The medium was discarded, and cells were gently washed twice with pre-cooled PBS. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min to preserve cell morphology and antigen localization. After three PBS washes, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 10 min to allow antibody penetration. Following PBS washes, cells were blocked with 5% BSA (dissolved in PBS) at room temperature for 1 h to block non-specific binding sites. After blocking, cells were incubated with corresponding primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. Primary antibodies used for detecting thyroid differentiation markers included: anti-TG antibody (1:200; Cat. 21714-1-AP, Proteintech) and anti-TSHR antibody (1:200; Cat. 14450-1-AP, Proteintech). The next day, after three 5-minute PBS washes, cells were incubated with corresponding fluorescently-labeled secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 or 594 conjugated, 1:500 dilution) at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. After PBS washes, nuclei were stained with 1 µg/mL DAPI working solution at room temperature in the dark for 5 min. Coverslips were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium. Images were observed and captured under a fluorescence microscope.

Cell counting kit 8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2,000 cells/well. The plates were placed in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator, and cell proliferation was assessed at 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. At each time point, the original medium was removed, and 100 µL of fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 solution (Cat. CK04, Dojindo) was added to each well. The plates were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 2 h. After incubation, the absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Colony formation assay

Treated cells were digested into single-cell suspensions and seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells/well. The plates were placed in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator and cultured for approximately 2 weeks, with the medium being replaced with fresh medium every 3 days. When visible colonies formed, the medium was discarded, and cells were gently washed twice with PBS to remove residual medium and dead cells. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. After removing the fixative, cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution at room temperature for 20 min. Excess stain was removed by gentle rinsing with running tap water. The plates were air-dried at room temperature and then photographed. The number of colonies was counted using ImageJ software.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

For the migration assay, transfected cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin, washed twice with serum-free medium, and resuspended. Then, 200 µL of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (8 μm pore size), and 600 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. The Transwell plate was placed in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h. After incubation, non-migratory cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. After washing with PBS to remove excess stain, five randomly selected non-overlapping fields were photographed under an inverted microscope, and the number of migrated cells was counted using ImageJ software.

For the invasion assay, diluted Matrigel matrix (Cat. 356234, BD Biosciences) was uniformly coated onto the surface of the Transwell membrane before the assay and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to allow polymerization, forming an artificial basement membrane. The subsequent steps were similar to the migration assay.

Flow cytometry analysis of apoptosis

Apoptosis was detected using the Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining method. Cells were washed twice with pre-cooled PBS, collected by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and the cell pellet was obtained. Staining was performed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Cat. 556547, BD Biosciences). Cells were resuspended in 100 µL of 1× binding buffer at a density of approximately 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Then, 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of PI staining solution were added. The mixture was gently mixed and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. After incubation, 400 µL of 1× Binding Buffer was added to stop the reaction. Samples were analyzed within 1 h using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCalibur), and at least 10,000 cell events were collected per sample. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Seahorse cellular energy metabolism analysis

Cellular mitochondrial OXPHOS and glycolysis levels were measured in real-time using a Seahorse XFe96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent). One day before the assay, 200 µL/well of XF Calibrant was added to the sensor cartridge of the Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer, which was placed in a 37 °C, non-CO₂ incubator to hydrate overnight. Simultaneously, transfected cells were seeded in a Seahorse 96-well cell culture microplate at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well, with medium added to the edge wells for background correction. On the day of the assay, the medium was removed, and cells were gently washed twice with pre-warmed Seahorse XF Assay Medium. Then, 180 µL of assay medium (XF Base Medium supplemented with 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 10 mM glucose, with pH adjusted to 7.4 using 1 M NaOH, and filter-sterilized through a 0.22 μm filter) was added to each well. The cell plate was placed in a 37 °C, non-CO₂ incubator for 1 h for equilibration. Drug working solutions were prepared and loaded into the corresponding injection ports of the sensor cartridge. For oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measurement, the Mito Stress Test Kit was used, sequentially injecting 1.5 µM oligomycin, 1 µM FCCP, and a mixture of 0.5 µM rotenone/antimycin A. For extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) measurement, the glycolysis stress test kit was used, sequentially injecting 10 mM glucose, 1 µM oligomycin, and 50 mM 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG). After the assay, data were analyzed and processed using Wave software.

Detection of lactate and ATP levels

The lactate assay kit (Cat. A019-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) was used to detect the extracellular lactate content according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, samples and standards were added to a 96-well plate, followed by the sequential addition of enzyme working solution and chromogenic agent. After incubation at 37 °C for 10 min, the absorbance at 530 nm was measured using a microplate reader. The lactate concentration was calculated based on the standard curve.

The intracellular ATP content was detected using an enhanced ATP assay kit (Cat. S0027, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Briefly, the ATP detection working solution was added to a white opaque 96-well assay plate and allowed to stand at room temperature for 3–5 min to deplete background ATP. Samples or standards were then added, mixed rapidly, and the chemiluminescence signal was measured within 2 s using a multifunctional microplate reader. The ATP concentration in the samples was calculated based on the ATP standard curve.

Flow cytometric analysis of PD-L1+ cell proportion

TPC-1 cells were treated with either PBS or recombinant human IFN-γ (final concentration 10 ng/mL) for 24 h. After treatment, cells were collected and prepared as a single-cell suspension. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded. Cells were resuspended in PBS containing 5% BSA and blocked at room temperature for 20 min. After blocking, the solution was removed by centrifugation, and a fluorochrome-conjugated anti-human PD-L1 antibody was added. The cells were incubated with the antibody at 4 °C in the dark for 1 h. Subsequently, cells were washed twice with PBS, pelleted by centrifugation, and finally resuspended in an appropriate volume of PBS for immediate flow cytometric analysis. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software, with the negative threshold set using an isotype control antibody.

Establishment of the Jurkat and TPC-1 cell co-culture system

To evaluate the effect of ITM2A on T cell function, a co-culture system of Jurkat T cells and TPC-1 cells was established. TPC-1 Vector or oe-ITM2A cells were seeded in 6-well plates. After cell adherence, Jurkat T cells were added at an effector-to-target (E: T) ratio of 5:1. An IgG isotype control group and a PD-L1 neutralizing antibody treatment group (anti-PD-L1, 10 µg/mL) were also set up simultaneously. The co-culture duration was 24 h.

Flow cytometric analysis of CD69+ cell proportion

The expression level of CD69 on the surface of Jurkat T cells in each treatment group was detected by flow cytometry. After co-culture, Jurkat T cells in suspension were collected, washed with PBS, and resuspended in PBS buffer containing 1% BSA. A fluorochrome-conjugated anti-human CD69 monoclonal antibody (PE-labeled) was added, and the cells were incubated at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in an appropriate volume of PBS for flow cytometric detection. Data were acquired on a flow cytometer, with CD69 signals detected in the PE-H channel. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software. Based on the negative control, the positive threshold was set, and the proportion of CD69+ cells was calculated.

Tumor cell killing assay

The lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay was performed to evaluate T cell-mediated tumor cell killing. After co-culture, the culture supernatant was collected, and the assay was conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions of the LDH cytotoxicity detection kit. Absorbance at 490 nm wavelength was measured to calculate the cytotoxicity percentage. The cytotoxicity was calculated using the following formula: Cytotoxicity (%) = [(LDH release in experimental group - Spontaneous release) / (Maximum release - Spontaneous release)] × 100%.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

A sandwich ELISA method was employed to detect the secretion levels of relevant cytokines in the cell culture supernatant. After co-culture, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min to remove debris. Standards and samples (100 µL) were added to a 96-well plate pre-coated with capture antibody and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After washing, 100 µL of biotin-labeled detection antibody was added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The plate was washed again, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated streptavidin at 37 °C for 30 min. After further washing, TMB substrate was added for color development in the dark for 15–20 min, and the reaction was stopped with stop solution. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and cytokine concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were performed using R (v4.0.3) or GraphPad Prism (v10.1.3) software. All experiments were independently repeated at least 3 times. Experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Before data analysis, normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test) were assessed. For data conforming to a normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent samples t-test (Student’s t-test), and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons using Tukey’s HSD test or Dunnett’s test. For data that did not conform to a normal distribution or had unequal variances, non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test) were used. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of PFI-related molecular subtypes of THCA and their clinical and biological characteristics

To identify prognostically relevant molecular subtypes of THCA, candidate genes were first screened across multiple cohorts. In the TCGA cohort, 900 DEGs were identified between tumor tissues and normal thyroid tissues. In the GEO cohort, 1,385 DEGs were obtained by comparing PTC with ATC. The intersection of these two DEG sets yielded 209 overlapping genes (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed on these 209 genes in the TCGA cohort to evaluate their association with PFI. A total of 26 genes were found to be significantly associated with PFI (p < 0.05; Fig. 1B). Among them, most genes exhibited hazard ratios (HRs) less than 1, suggesting that higher expression levels were associated with better prognosis. To further explore the biological functions of these 26 PFI-related genes, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed. GO analysis showed that these genes were mainly enriched in cellular components such as the extracellular region, extracellular vesicles, and exosomes, and were significantly involved in hormone metabolism and multiple metabolic processes (Figure S1A). KEGG pathway analysis further indicated significant enrichment in thyroid hormone synthesis, autoimmune thyroid disease, prolactin signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, and several metabolism-related pathways, suggesting that these genes might play important roles in thyroid function maintenance, immune regulation, and tumor biology (Figure S1B). Based on the expression profiles of the 26 PFI-related genes, consensus clustering analysis was subsequently performed to classify THCA patients. By comprehensively evaluating the consensus matrix, CDF curves, and delta area curves, k = 2 was determined to be the optimal number of clusters (Fig. 1C). Accordingly, patients were divided into Cluster 1 (n = 315) and Cluster 2 (n = 194). The two subtypes exhibited distinct expression patterns of PFI-related genes (Figure S1C). Clinical characteristics between the two molecular subtypes were further compared (Table S1). The proportion of female patients was significantly higher in Cluster 1 (p = 0.017). However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two subtypes in terms of pathological N stage, tumor status, race, histological subtype, tumor stage, age, tumor size-related indicators, or the number of positive lymph nodes. To evaluate the prognostic differences between the two subtypes, RMST analysis was performed. The results showed that the RMST of Cluster 2 was 12.48 years, which was longer than that of Cluster 1 (11.77 years), indicating a more favorable PFI outcome in Cluster 2 (Fig. 1D).

Given that the degree of thyroid tumor differentiation is closely associated with prognosis, the TDS and the expression levels of key differentiation markers were further compared between the two subtypes. The results showed that the TDS in Cluster 2 was significantly higher than that in Cluster 1 (p < 0.001; Fig. 1E). Meanwhile, the classical thyroid differentiation markers TG, TSHR, PAX8, and NKX2-1 were significantly upregulated in Cluster 2, indicating a higher differentiation status in this subtype (p < 0.01; Fig. 1E). Subsequently, the tumor immune microenvironment of the two molecular subtypes was evaluated using the CIBERSORT algorithm. Significant differences in the infiltration levels of multiple immune cell types were observed between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2, including several T-cell subsets, M1 macrophages, dendritic cells, and mast cells (p < 0.05; Fig. 1F).

Given that functional enrichment analysis indicated significant enrichment of metabolic pathways, the expression differences of key metabolic enzymes between the two subtypes were further compared (Table S2). Compared with Cluster 1, multiple metabolic enzymes associated with the TCA cycle, OXPHOS, and FAO were significantly upregulated in Cluster 2, including IDH3A, OGDH, SDHA, NDUFA9, SDHB, COX5A, CPT1A, ACADM, and HADHA. In contrast, key glycolytic enzymes PKM and LDHA were significantly downregulated in Cluster 2 (all p < 0.01). These findings suggested that Cluster 2, which exhibited a better prognosis, was characterized by a metabolic phenotype dominated by mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and FAO, with relatively suppressed glycolysis.

ITM2A is identified as the hub gene and shows metabolism-related characteristics

To further identify key genes that may play central roles in THCA progression, the 26 PFI-related genes were subjected to Lasso regression analysis. As the penalty parameter λ increased, the number of variables in the model was gradually reduced, and four candidate genes (GJA4, SDC2, TK1, and ITM2A) were ultimately selected at the optimal λ value (Fig. 2A). These four genes were subsequently included in a multivariate Cox regression model to evaluate their independent predictive value. The results showed that only ITM2A remained significantly associated with PFI in the multivariate analysis (HR < 1, p = 0.02; Fig. 2B). This finding suggested that ITM2A might represent a key hub gene influencing the prognosis of THCA; therefore, subsequent analyses focused primarily on ITM2A.

Based on the TCGA-THCA dataset, the clinical application potential of ITM2A was further evaluated. First, in the diagnostic analysis distinguishing tumor tissues from normal thyroid tissues, ITM2A demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.884 (Fig. 2C). In addition, DCA showed that the model based on ITM2A achieved a higher net benefit across a relatively wide range of risk thresholds (0.7–1.0). After patients were stratified into high-differentiation and low-differentiation groups according to the median of TDS, ITM2A also effectively distinguished different differentiation states, with an AUC of 0.810 (Fig. 2D), further supporting the close association between ITM2A and tumor differentiation status. Considering the strong association between BRAFV600E mutation and dedifferentiation, we further analyzed dedifferentiation-related markers in patients with high or low ITM2A expression within BRAFV600E-mutant and wild-type groups. Regardless of mutational status, patients with high ITM2A expression exhibited significantly higher TDS scores and elevated expression of TG, TSHR, and PAX8 compared with low-expression patients (p < 0.05, Figure S2A). Stratification by both BRAFV600E mutation status and ITM2A expression revealed that patients with BRAFV600E mutation and low ITM2A expression had the lowest TDS scores, whereas wild-type patients with high ITM2A expression had the highest scores (p < 0.001, Figure S2B).

Additionally, GSEA results showed that the ITM2A high-expression phenotype was significantly enriched in multiple metabolism-related pathways, including fatty acid metabolism, valine/leucine/isoleucine degradation, glycine/serine/threonine metabolism, peroxisome, the mTOR signaling pathway, and glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchor biosynthesis (Fig. 2E), suggesting that ITM2A might participate in the biological progression of THCA through regulation of tumor metabolic reprogramming. Furthermore, correlations between ITM2A and key metabolic enzymes were analyzed (Table 1). ITM2A was found to be significantly positively correlated with the FAO-related enzymes CPT1A (r = 0.603) and ACADM (r = 0.433), whereas significant negative correlations were observed with the glycolysis-related enzymes PKM (r = − 0.430) and LDHA (r = − 0.348) (all p < 0.001). Scatter plots of the four metabolic enzymes with the highest correlation coefficients further illustrated these relationships (Fig. 2F). Metabolic reprogramming may also be influenced by mutational status; therefore, we analyzed the correlations between ITM2A and these four metabolic enzymes in both BRAFV600E-mutant and wild-type groups. The results showed that ITM2A remained positively correlated with CPT1A and ACADM and negatively correlated with PKM and LDHA regardless of mutation status (Figure S2C), further suggesting that ITM2A may exert a stable regulatory role in tumor metabolic control.

Association of ITM2A expression with differentiation status and metabolic phenotypes of THCA cells

To validate the association between ITM2A expression and the differentiation status and metabolic characteristics of THCA at the cellular level, thyroid-derived cell lines with different differentiation states were selected for baseline expression analysis. The results showed that, compared with normal thyroid cells, ITM2A expression was significantly reduced in differentiated PTC cell lines (TPC-1 and BCPAP) (p < 0.01) and was further markedly downregulated in undifferentiated ATC cell lines (8505 C and CAL-62) (p < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Consistent with the mRNA results, ITM2A protein expression exhibited a progressive decrease with declining differentiation levels (p < 0.05; Fig. 3B). Based on these observations, the expression of thyroid differentiation–related markers in different cell lines was further examined. The results showed that the TDS was highest in normal thyroid cells, significantly reduced in differentiated PTC cell lines, and lowest in undifferentiated ATC cell lines (p < 0.001; Fig. 3C). Similarly, key thyroid differentiation regulators, including PAX8 and NKX2-1, as well as thyroid function–related markers such as TSHR, TG, and NIS, displayed a consistent pattern, with expression progressively decreasing as the degree of cellular dedifferentiation increased (p < 0.05; Fig. 3C). Given the close association between ITM2A and metabolic pathways observed in previous analyses, metabolic gene expression profiles under different differentiation states were further evaluated. Glycolysis-related genes, including HK2, PFKP, PKM, and LDHA, were significantly upregulated in undifferentiated ATC cell lines compared with normal thyroid cells and differentiated PTC cells (p < 0.05; Fig. 3D). In contrast, the mRNA expression levels of genes involved in the TCA cycle (IDH3A, OGDH, and SDHA), fatty acid β-oxidation (ACADM, CPT1A, and HADHA), and OXPHOS (COX5A and NDUFA9) gradually decreased with the loss of differentiation (p < 0.05; Fig. 3D).

ITM2A suppresses malignant biological behaviors of THCA cells

To further clarify the functional role of ITM2A in THCA progression, an ITM2A overexpression model was established in TPC-1 cells, while an ITM2A knockdown model was constructed in 8505 C cells. The efficiency of transfection was confirmed by qPCR and western blotting (p < 0.001; Fig. 4A-B). CCK-8 assays showed that ITM2A overexpression significantly reduced cell viability in TPC-1 cells compared with the control group, whereas ITM2A knockdown markedly enhanced the growth capacity of 8505 C cells (p < 0.001; Fig. 4C). Colony formation assays further confirmed that ITM2A overexpression significantly suppressed colony-forming ability, while ITM2A knockdown significantly increased the number of colonies (p < 0.001; Fig. 4D). Transwell migration and invasion assays demonstrated that ITM2A overexpression markedly inhibited the migratory and invasive capacities of TPC-1 cells, whereas ITM2A knockdown significantly enhanced migration and invasion in 8505 C cells (p < 0.001; Fig. 4E-F). In addition, flow cytometry analysis revealed that ITM2A overexpression significantly increased the proportion of apoptotic cells, whereas ITM2A knockdown significantly reduced apoptosis levels (p < 0.001; Fig. 4G).

Furthermore, ITM2A was additionally overexpressed in 8505 C cells and knocked down in TPC-1 cells. qPCR and western blotting confirmed efficient modulation of ITM2A expression (p < 0.05; Figure S3A-B). The results of CCK-8, Transwell, and flow cytometry analyses were consistent with those observed in the primary models, confirming that ITM2A suppresses THCA cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and promotes apoptosis across different cell lines (Figure S3C-E).

ITM2A promotes THCA cell differentiation and drives metabolic reprogramming

Previous findings suggested that ITM2A was associated with THCA differentiation and metabolic regulation. Therefore, the role of ITM2A in regulating cellular differentiation and metabolic balance in THCA cells was further investigated. As shown in Fig. 5A, ITM2A overexpression in TPC-1 cells significantly increased the TDS and upregulated the expression of PAX8, NKX2-1, TSHR, TG, and NIS (p < 0.001). Conversely, knockdown of ITM2A in 8505 C cells significantly reduced the TDS and the expression of these differentiation markers (p < 0.01; Fig. 5A). Further immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that ITM2A overexpression markedly enhanced the fluorescence intensity of TG and TSHR in TPC-1 cells, whereas ITM2A knockdown significantly reduced the protein expression of these differentiation markers in 8505 C cells (p < 0.001; Fig. 5B). These findings suggested that ITM2A promoted THCA cell differentiation.

Seahorse XFe96 metabolic analysis showed that ITM2A overexpression significantly increased both basal and maximal OCR while simultaneously reducing the ECAR in TPC-1 cells (p < 0.001; Fig. 5C). In contrast, ITM2A knockdown in 8505 C cells resulted in a significant decrease in OCR and a marked increase in ECAR (p < 0.001; Fig. 5C). Metabolite measurements further showed that ITM2A overexpression significantly decreased lactate production and increased intracellular ATP levels, whereas ITM2A knockdown led to increased lactate accumulation and reduced ATP levels (p < 0.001; Fig. 5D). These results indicated that ITM2A promoted a metabolic shift in THCA cells from a glycolysis-dominant state toward an OXPHOS-dominant metabolic phenotype.

ITM2A maintains THCA differentiation and suppresses cell proliferation through metabolic regulation

To further clarify the impact of ITM2A-mediated metabolic remodeling on THCA progression, TPC-1 cells overexpressing ITM2A were treated with either a FAO inhibitor (FAOi) or the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG. Seahorse XFe96 energy metabolism analysis showed that FAOi treatment significantly attenuated the increase in OCR induced by ITM2A overexpression and partially restored ECAR levels, whereas 2-DG primarily reduced ECAR with relatively limited effects on OCR (p < 0.05; Fig. 6A). Metabolite analysis further showed that FAOi treatment increased lactate levels and reduced ATP production, whereas 2-DG exerted the opposite effects (p < 0.001; Fig. 6B). These findings further confirmed that ITM2A improved cellular energy metabolism primarily through enhancing FAO. Analysis of differentiation marker levels revealed that FAOi treatment significantly attenuated the ITM2A-induced increase in TDS and the expression of PAX8, NKX2-1, TSHR, TG, and NIS (p < 0.05); in contrast, treatment with 2-DG further enhanced the expression of these differentiation markers (p < 0.05; Fig. 6C). Immunofluorescence analysis also demonstrated that FAOi markedly reduced the protein expression levels of TG and TSHR, whereas relatively strong fluorescence signals were maintained in the 2-DG treatment group (p < 0.01; Fig. 6D). Furthermore, under the background of ITM2A overexpression, FAOi treatment significantly restored cell viability and promoted cell proliferation (p < 0.001), whereas 2-DG treatment further suppressed cell growth and proliferation (p < 0.001; Fig. 6E-F). Collectively, these findings indicated that ITM2A promoted the expression of thyroid differentiation markers and suppressed cell proliferation by remodeling cellular energy metabolism.

ITM2A counteracts TGF-β–induced dedifferentiation

Given that tumor microenvironmental signals such as TGF-β are known to drive dedifferentiation of THCA cells, it was further investigated whether ITM2A could maintain the differentiated phenotype under pro-dedifferentiation stress conditions. To further verify the functional role of ITM2A in a dedifferentiation microenvironment, a THCA dedifferentiation model was established by continuous TGF-β treatment for 14 days. As shown in Fig. 7A, TGF-β treatment significantly reduced the TDS and the expression of PAX8, NKX2-1, TSHR, TG, and NIS (p < 0.001), indicating that a dedifferentiated cellular state was successfully induced by TGF-β. Under TGF-β induction conditions, ITM2A overexpression partially restored the TDS and the expression of these differentiation markers (p < 0.01). The results of immunofluorescence staining were consistent with the mRNA expression patterns. TGF-β treatment markedly reduced the protein expression intensity of TG and TSHR, whereas the dedifferentiation effect induced by TGF-β was significantly reversed by ITM2A overexpression (p < 0.01; Fig. 7B). These results indicated that ITM2A may inhibit the THCA cell dedifferentiation. Considering that the dedifferentiation process is frequently accompanied by epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), the expression of EMT-related molecules was further examined. The results showed that the expression levels of EMT transcription factors SNAIL and ZEB1 were significantly increased following TGF-β treatment (p < 0.01). Under TGF-β induction conditions, the expression of SNAIL and ZEB1 was significantly suppressed by ITM2A overexpression (p < 0.01; Fig. 7C). Western blotting analysis further confirmed that, at the protein level, TGF-β treatment significantly decreased the expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin and increased the expression of the mesenchymal marker vimentin (p < 0.001). These effects were reversed by ITM2A overexpression (p < 0.001; Fig. 7D). Collectively, these findings indicated that THCA dedifferentiation could be counteracted by ITM2A and that EMT progression under dedifferentiation stimuli could also be suppressed.

ITM2A maintains a differentiation-associated immunologically favorable phenotype and enhanced PD-L1-dependent T-cell responses

The aforementioned findings demonstrated that the differentiated state of THCA cells was maintained by ITM2A through metabolic reprogramming and that EMT progression was suppressed. Given that tumor differentiation status and metabolic state are closely associated with immunogenicity, it was further investigated whether immune-related phenotypes could be shaped by ITM2A through its differentiation–metabolism regulatory axis. First, the expression changes of antigen-presentation and chemotaxis-related molecules were examined. The results showed that the expression levels of HLA-A, HLA-B, B2M, and TAP1 were significantly increased following ITM2A overexpression, whereas these genes were significantly downregulated after ITM2A knockdown (p < 0.01; Fig. 8A), suggesting that MHC-I antigen-presentation capacity might be enhanced by ITM2A. Meanwhile, the expression levels of T-cell-recruiting chemokines, including CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, and CCL5, were markedly upregulated by ITM2A overexpression, whereas their expression was significantly suppressed after ITM2A knockdown (p < 0.05; Fig. 8A). These results indicated that an immune microenvironment favorable for T-cell recruitment might be promoted by ITM2A. Given that IFN-γ is a key inflammatory cytokine regulating PD-L1 expression, Vector and oe-ITM2A cells were treated with PBS or IFN-γ, respectively, and the proportion of PD-L1+ cells was subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry. The results showed that PD-L1 expression was significantly increased after IFN-γ treatment, and the proportion of PD-L1+ cells in the oe-ITM2A + IFN-γ group was significantly higher than that in the Vector + IFN-γ group (p < 0.001; Fig. 8B), suggesting that the responsiveness of tumor cells to IFN-γ stimulation could be enhanced by ITM2A. To further evaluate the influence of ITM2A on T-cell function, a co-culture system of Jurkat cells and TPC-1 cells was established. Flow cytometry analysis showed that the proportion of CD69+ T cells was significantly increased in the oe-ITM2A + IgG group compared with the Vector + IgG group. After the addition of a PD-L1 neutralizing antibody, the proportion of CD69⁺, CD25+, and Granzyme B+ T cells was further significantly increased (p < 0.001; Fig. 8C-E). These findings indicated that T-cell activation was promoted by ITM2A, and that this effect could be further enhanced after PD-L1 blockade. ELISA results showed that the secretion levels of IFN-γ and IL-2 in the co-culture system were significantly increased in the oe-ITM2A + IgG group compared with the Vector + IgG group. Under PD-L1 blockade conditions, the secretion of these cytokines was further enhanced (p < 0.001; Fig. 8F). In addition, LDH release assays showed that tumor cell killing was significantly increased in the oe-ITM2A group, and the cytotoxic effect was further enhanced after anti-PD-L1 treatment (p < 0.001; Fig. 8D). These results suggested that the antigen-presentation capacity and chemokine expression of THCA cells were enhanced by ITM2A, thereby promoting PD-L1-dependent T-cell recruitment and activation. Moreover, the responsiveness of tumor cells to IFN-γ was increased, indicating that an immunologically favorable tumor microenvironment might be established by ITM2A.

Discussion

THCA generally has a favorable prognosis; however, a subset of patients experience tumor dedifferentiation during disease progression, leading to failure of RAI therapy and significantly reduced survival [17]. In the present study, focusing on the critical issue of dedifferentiation in THCA, we integrated bioinformatics analyses with in vitro functional experiments to gradually establish a regulatory framework of “ITM2A–FAO–maintenance of differentiation–immune remodeling.” Overall, our results indicate that ITM2A is not only an independent protective factor significantly associated with PFI but also plays a crucial role in maintaining thyroid differentiation phenotypes, reshaping metabolic patterns, and regulating the tumor immune microenvironment.

ITM2A is a member of the ITM2 family and is a highly conserved transmembrane protein [18]. Previous studies have shown that ITM2A participates in various biological processes, including regulation of autophagic flux, chondrogenic differentiation, and tissue lipid metabolism [19–21]. In recent years, the potential role of ITM2A in cancer has gradually attracted attention. Several studies have reported that ITM2A is downregulated in multiple tumors, including breast cancer, bladder cancer, cervical cancer, and head and neck cancer, and that low ITM2A expression is generally associated with tumor progression and poor prognosis, suggesting a potential tumor-suppressive role [22–25]. However, studies investigating ITM2A in THCA remain very limited. Recently, a study by Wan et al. suggested that ITM2A may serve as a potential diagnostic biomarker for THCA; based on analyses of GEO datasets, the authors reported that ITM2A expression was significantly downregulated in THCA tumor tissues [26]. Consistent with their findings, our multi-cohort integrated analysis also demonstrated that ITM2A is significantly downregulated in THCA. Furthermore, we found that ITM2A expression was significantly associated with PFI in patients with THCA and was highly expressed in the molecular subtype with a better prognosis. Lasso regression and multivariate Cox regression analyses further confirmed that ITM2A is an independent protective factor for THCA, suggesting that it may exert tumor-suppressive effects during tumor progression. Functional experiments further supported this hypothesis. In vitro experiments showed that overexpression of ITM2A significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of TPC-1 cells while promoting apoptosis. In contrast, knockdown of ITM2A in K1 cells enhanced malignant phenotypes, including increased proliferation, migration, and invasion. Collectively, these findings indicate that ITM2A may play an important tumor-suppressive role in THCA.

Previous studies have demonstrated that ITM2A is involved in various tissue differentiation processes, such as osteogenesis and chondrogenic differentiation [27, 28], but its role in regulating tumor differentiation has rarely been investigated. In this study, we found that ITM2A was significantly upregulated in THCA subtypes with a higher differentiation status and exhibited good diagnostic performance in distinguishing THCA samples with different differentiation states. Further cellular experiments showed that ITM2A expression displayed a pattern consistent with the differentiation status of THCA: compared with normal thyroid cells, ITM2A expression was markedly reduced in PTC cell lines and further decreased in ATC cell lines with lower differentiation. This progressively decreasing expression pattern along with tumor dedifferentiation suggests that ITM2A may be an important regulatory molecule involved in maintaining the thyroid-specific differentiation network. Functional experiments further supported this hypothesis. Overexpression of ITM2A significantly upregulated multiple thyroid differentiation-related markers, including PAX8, NKX2-1, TSHR, TG, and NIS, whereas knockdown of ITM2A markedly reduced their expression. PAX8 and NKX2-1 (also known as TTF-1) are core transcription factors that maintain thyroid development and differentiation. They cooperatively regulate the transcription of thyroid-specific genes and play critical roles in maintaining the functional phenotype and differentiation status of thyroid follicular cells [29, 30]. TSHR is a key membrane receptor required for maintaining thyroid cell differentiation and functional activity; its signaling pathway promotes the expression of thyroid-specific genes and sustains the differentiated state of thyroid cells [31, 32]. TSH–TSHR signaling further stimulates the expression of TG and NIS, thereby increasing iodide uptake in normal or malignant thyroid cells and ultimately determining the sensitivity of THCA to RAI therapy [32, 33]. The coordinated expression of these differentiation markers is considered the key molecular basis for maintaining the differentiated phenotype and functional integrity of thyroid cells. Our findings indicate that ITM2A maintains the differentiation status of THCA cells by regulating key thyroid differentiation transcriptional networks.

In addition, we observed that as the differentiation status of THCA cells decreased, the levels of glycolysis-related metabolites gradually increased, whereas metabolites associated with FAO and OXPHOS progressively decreased. The classical Warburg effect suggests that tumor cells shift their energy metabolism from mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis to adapt to rapid proliferation and hypoxic microenvironments, thereby obtaining a faster energy supply [34]. In THCA, previous studies have similarly shown that as differentiated THCA progresses toward a dedifferentiated state, tumor cells exhibit a markedly increased dependence on glucose and meet the energetic and biosynthetic demands required for rapid growth and survival by enhancing glycolytic flux [35]. In the present study, we found that ITM2A expression was negatively correlated with glycolysis-related metabolic products but showed a significant positive correlation with FAO-associated metabolic features, suggesting that ITM2A may play an important role in regulating metabolic reprogramming in THCA. A recent study also proposed that ITM2A may influence disease progression in patients with triple-negative breast cancer through the regulation of cellular metabolism [22], providing preliminary evidence for the involvement of ITM2A in tumor metabolic regulation. Our results showed that overexpression of ITM2A significantly increased the OCR and decreased the ECAR, reduced lactate accumulation, and enhanced ATP production, indicating that ITM2A promotes a metabolic shift from glycolysis-dominant energy metabolism toward mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. Pharmacological intervention experiments further demonstrated that inhibition of FAO attenuated the ITM2A-induced upregulation of differentiation markers and its antiproliferative effects, whereas inhibition of glycolysis further enhanced the expression of differentiation markers. Collectively, these findings suggest that ITM2A stabilizes the thyroid-specific differentiation transcriptional network by coordinating the metabolic balance between glycolysis and FAO. Notably, in a dedifferentiation model established by sustained TGF-β stimulation, ITM2A was still able to partially restore the expression of differentiation markers and suppress the upregulation of EMT-related molecules. TGF-β signaling is widely recognized as a key driver of dedifferentiation and EMT, potentially accelerating the loss of differentiation by promoting glycolysis and impairing mitochondrial function [36–38]. These findings suggest that ITM2A not only maintains differentiation under homeostatic conditions but also counteracts dedifferentiation under microenvironmental stress.

Metabolic reprogramming may also be influenced by oncogenic mutation status. Previous studies have shown that driver mutations can induce metabolic reprogramming in THCA [39]. For example, the BRAFV600E/p-ERK/p-DRP1 (Ser616) signaling pathway promotes cell proliferation by upregulating hexokinase 2 expression, thereby enhancing aerobic glycolysis [13]. In addition, BRAFV600E-mutant PTC is often accompanied by a marked reduction in the expression of thyroid differentiation markers [12]. Previous research has shown that knockdown of ITM2A is sufficient to rescue the differentiation-inhibitory effects of the lamin AWT and R482W mutants in 3T3-L1 cells [21]. In the present study, the associations between ITM2A and differentiation markers or metabolism-related enzymes remained stable regardless of BRAFV600E mutational status. Future studies using larger-scale multi-omics datasets could further validate the potential relationship between ITM2A and mutation status and clarify its role in maintaining thyroid cell differentiation and metabolic homeostasis.

Changes in differentiation and metabolic states can also profoundly influence the immune microenvironment [40–42]. In this study, we found that the ITM2A-high subtype exhibited a more active T-cell infiltration profile. In vitro experiments further demonstrated that ITM2A upregulated the expression of antigen-presenting molecules and chemokines, enhanced the IFN-γ response, and promoted PD-L1–dependent T-cell activation. Well-differentiated tumor cells with predominantly oxidative metabolism are more likely to maintain intact antigen-presenting functions [43]. In contrast, tumor dedifferentiation and the accompanying glycolytic metabolic state are often closely associated with immune evasion [15, 44]. Our results further showed that in the presence of PD-L1–blocking antibodies, tumors with high ITM2A expression may be more sensitive to immune checkpoint therapy. Therefore, ITM2A may function not only as a differentiation-maintaining factor but also as a potential predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response.

From a clinical perspective, ITM2A may have multiple potential applications. First, its expression level may serve as a potential biomarker for stratifying the risk of disease progression in THCA. Second, ITM2A may represent a novel metabolic therapeutic target for maintaining tumor differentiation. In addition, the immune-activated phenotype associated with high ITM2A expression suggests that it may be linked to improved responses to immune checkpoint therapy. Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. For example, the precise molecular mechanisms by which ITM2A suppresses tumor dedifferentiation and regulates metabolic reprogramming remain to be validated in animal models and further functional studies.

Conclusion

Overall, this study demonstrates that ITM2A plays an important regulatory role in the progression of THCA. By coordinating the metabolic balance between glycolysis and FAO, ITM2A helps maintain the differentiated state of THCA, suppresses EMT-associated phenotypes and malignant progression, and shapes a more immunologically active tumor microenvironment. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying THCA dedifferentiation but also provide a new theoretical basis for future therapeutic strategies that combine metabolic regulation with immunomodulation.
