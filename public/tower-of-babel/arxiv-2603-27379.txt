Multidimensional Gradient-MUSIC: A Global Nonconvex Optimization Framework for Optimal Resolution
Albert Fannjiang, Weilin Li

Abstract
We develop a multidimensional version of Gradient-MUSIC for estimating the frequencies of a nonharmonic signal from noisy samples. The guiding principle is that frequency recovery should be based only on the signal subspace determined by the data. From this viewpoint, the MUSIC functional is an economical nonconvex objective encoding the relevant information, and the problem becomes one of understanding the geometry of its perturbed landscape. Our main contribution is a general structural theory showing that, under explicit conditions on the measurement kernel and the perturbation of the signal subspace, the perturbed MUSIC function is an admissible optimization landscape: suitable initial points can be found efficiently by coarse thresholding, gradient descent converges to the relevant local minima, and these minima obey quantitative error bounds. Thus the theory is not merely existential; it provides a constructive global optimization framework for multidimensional optimal resolution. We verify the abstract conditions in detail for two canonical sampling geometries: discrete samples on a cube and continuous samples on a ball. In both cases we obtain uniform, nonasymptotic recovery guarantees under deterministic as well as stochastic noise. In particular, for lattice samples in a cube of side length 4 ​ m 4m , if the true frequencies are separated by at least β d / m \beta_{d}/m and the noise has ℓ ∞ \ell^{\infty} norm at most ε \varepsilon , then Gradient-MUSIC recovers the frequencies with error at most C d ​ ε m , C_{d}\frac{\varepsilon}{m}, where C d , β d > 0 C_{d},\beta_{d}>0 depend only on the dimension. This scaling is minimax optimal in m m and ε \varepsilon . Under stationary Gaussian noise, the error improves to C d ​ σ ​ log ⁡ ( m ) m 1 + d / 2 . C_{d}\frac{\sigma\sqrt{\log(m)}}{m^{1+d/2}}. This is the noisy super-resolution scaling : rather than referring to resolution below a classical diffraction threshold, it quantifies the enhanced decay of localization error with sampling diameter in the presence of random noise.

Introduction

Spectral estimation is the inverse problem of recovering latent frequencies from high-dimensional, and typically noisy, observations of superposed non-harmonic Fourier modes. Although the forward model is linear in the amplitudes, the recovery of the frequencies themselves is intrinsically nonlinear. In this sense, spectral estimation belongs to the broader class of nonlinear inverse problems in which the physically meaningful parameters are not directly accessible through Fourier inversion, but must instead be inferred from the geometry of the measurement data.

More specifically, let the noiseless measurement be described by the function
y\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
,

y(x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}a_{\ell}e^{2\pi i\theta_{\ell}\cdot x},\qquad\text{for some }\vartheta:=\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}\subseteq\Omega\quad\text{and}\quad a:=\{a_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\},

(1.1)

where
d\geq 1
is the ambient dimension and
\Omega
is either the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}=(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{d}
or a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The unknown parameters are the number of sources
s
, their locations
\vartheta
, and their amplitudes
a
.

In practice, one does not observe
y
on all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but only on a prescribed sampling set
X_{\star}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus the noiseless data are given by the restriction
y|_{X_{\star}}
. In addition, the measurements are corrupted by noise. Writing
\eta\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
for the noise and
\widetilde{y}=y+\eta
for the corrupted signal, the observed data take the form

\widetilde{y}|_{X_{\star}}.

(1.2)

The multidimensional spectral estimation problem is to recover, or approximate, the unknown parameters
(s,\vartheta,a)
from the data (
1.2
). Since only the relative size of signal and noise matters, we may normalize and assume without loss of generality that

\min_{\ell}|a_{\ell}|=1.

Models of the form (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) arise throughout signal processing, imaging, inverse scattering and, more recently, quantum computing
[
18
,
28
,
40
,
43
]
. When
\Omega
parametrizes frequency, the
\theta_{\ell}
represent latent spectral components, as in multivariate spectral analysis. When
\Omega
represents physical space or angular variables, the
\theta_{\ell}
may encode source locations or directions of arrival, as in radar, array imaging, and inverse scattering. In all such settings, the parameters are generally
off-grid
: the set
\vartheta
is an arbitrary discrete subset of
\Omega
, not assumed a priori to lie on a prescribed lattice. This point is essential. Grid-based sparse methods replace the continuum by a finite dictionary, but this introduces basis mismatch whenever the true parameters do not coincide with grid points
[
13
]
. The resulting discretization error is not merely numerical; it changes the inverse problem itself.

The starting point of the present work is the observation that, although the map (
1.1
) is nonlinear in the parameters
(s,\vartheta,a)
, the information relevant for recovering
\vartheta
is carried by a much simpler object: the signal subspace. Indeed, if one defines

\phi_{\omega}(x)=e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot x},

then in the noiseless setting the measurement model is generated by the span of the steering vectors
\{\phi_{\theta_{\ell}}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
. Let

U:={\rm span}\{\phi_{\theta_{1}},\dots,\phi_{\theta_{s}}\},

and let
P_{U}
denote the orthogonal projection onto
U
. A natural surrogate for parameter recovery is then provided by the functional

q(\omega)={\left\|(I-P_{U})\phi_{\omega}\right\|}^{2}.

(1.3)

We refer to any function of the form (
1.3
) as a MUSIC function, after the classical MUltiple SIgnal Classification method of Schmidt
[
39
]
. In the noiseless case,
q(\theta_{\ell})=0
for each
\ell
, so the ground truth
\vartheta
is encoded by the set of global minima of the MUSIC landscape.

In practice, however, the subspace
U
is not known exactly and must be estimated from the noisy data. If
\widetilde{U}
denotes a suitable estimator of
U
, then one is led to the perturbed MUSIC functional

\widetilde{q}(\omega)={\left\|(I-P_{\widetilde{U}})\phi_{\omega}\right\|}^{2},

(1.4)

whose smallest
s
minima
\widetilde{\vartheta}:=\{\widetilde{\theta}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
are taken as estimates of the unknown parameters. This formulation achieves a striking reduction in complexity. Rather than optimizing over the full parameter space
(\vartheta,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{sd}\times\mathbb{C}^{s}
, one optimizes a scalar function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, independently of the number of sources. The reduction is made possible by separating the reconstruction into two steps: first estimate the signal subspace, then recover the parameters by probing that subspace with the steering family
\phi_{\omega}
.

This dimensional reduction is one of the chief attractions of subspace methods, but it also creates the central difficulty addressed in this paper. The function
\widetilde{q}
is typically nonconvex, and one seeks not one minimizer but the smallest
s
local minima. The classical MUSIC algorithm bypasses this difficulty by evaluating
\widetilde{q}
on a fine grid in
\Omega
and identifying approximate minima by exhaustive search
[
39
,
41
,
26
]
. Such a procedure is computationally tied to the desired accuracy and becomes rapidly prohibitive in dimension
d\geq 2
: a grid of mesh width
\delta
contains
O(\delta^{-d})
points. This curse of dimensionality has long limited the use of MUSIC in multidimensional problems and motivates the need for a genuinely optimization-based treatment.

At first sight, however, this prescription is far from self-evident. Why should
q_{\widetilde{U}}
even possess at least
s
relevant local minima? Why should these minima lie near the true configuration
\vartheta
? And, from a computational point of view, how can they be found efficiently, given that
q_{\widetilde{U}}
is nonconvex? Classical MUSIC addresses this difficulty through an expensive brute-force search over a fine grid, precisely because it lacks a principled global initialization strategy.

The present paper develops such a treatment. Our goal is to formulate a global optimization framework for MUSIC that is both computationally efficient and mathematically transparent. The guiding principle is that one should work with the minimal information structurally required for reconstruction, namely, the signal subspace. From this perspective, the MUSIC function is not merely a classical heuristic; it is an economical nonconvex functional encoding the geometric information relevant to the inverse problem. The question is then whether this landscape possesses sufficient global regularity to permit reliable optimization.

This question should be viewed against the general background of ill-posed inverse problems. In the sense of Hadamard, a problem is well-posed if a solution exists, is unique, and depends continuously on the data. Spectral estimation typically violates these properties in several ways. The forward map may fail to be injective under insufficient sampling; noise may move the data away from the range of the ideal model; and even when the parameters remain identifiable, reconstruction may be unstable. From an optimization viewpoint, one faces an additional obstacle: a nonconvex objective may have many local minima unrelated to the ground truth. For MUSIC, this issue is especially acute because one must locate multiple minima globally and distinguish the relevant ones from spurious critical points.

To make this difficulty concrete, one may recall that closeness of objective functions does not by itself imply closeness of their minimizers. For example, the two functions

f(t)=\varepsilon^{2}|t|,\qquad g(t)=\varepsilon^{2}|t-\varepsilon^{-1}|,

satisfy
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)-g(t)|\leq\varepsilon
, yet their minimizers are separated by distance
\varepsilon^{-1}
. Thus even a small perturbation of the objective may drastically alter the location of the minimum.
The source of this instability is the flatness of the landscape near the minimum which results in sensitivity to perturbations. This example shows that pointwise or uniform control of the objective is insufficient for stable recovery: one also needs geometric information about the basin of attraction, such as curvature or a quantitative rate of increase away from the minimum.
In inverse problems, stability of the reconstruction therefore requires much more than pointwise closeness of data or functionals; it requires structural control of the optimization landscape itself.

A large body of literature approaches spectral estimation through other optimization principles. Classical methods such as nonlinear least squares and maximum likelihood estimation, when the noise model is known, optimize objective functions on the full high-dimensional parameter space
(\vartheta,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{sd}\times\mathbb{C}^{s}
; see, for example,
[
42
,
44
]
. These formulations are likewise nonconvex and usually require carefully chosen initializations in a space whose dimension grows with the number of sources. At the other extreme, continuous compressed sensing and atomic norm methods embed the problem into an infinite-dimensional convex program over purely atomic measures
[
6
]
. In higher dimensions, however, numerical implementation typically requires either discretization in the primal domain or semidefinite representations in the dual domain
[
9
,
33
,
32
]
, both of which are computationally intensive.

The MUSIC framework occupies a different position. Once the signal subspace has been estimated, the recovery problem is reduced to a function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, regardless of the number of sources. In return, one must understand the geometry of a nonconvex landscape and devise a principled global search strategy. A basic requirement for constructing the signal subspace is that there exist sets
X,X^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
X+X^{\prime}\subseteq X_{\star}
, where
X+X^{\prime}
denotes the Minkowski sum. Under such a condition, one can assemble structured data matrices whose range encodes the desired subspace. The fundamental challenge is then no longer dimensionality, but landscape geometry: why should one expect the MUSIC function to be globally navigable by efficient local methods?

1.1
Informal sketch of our contributions

Our answer, building on our earlier one-dimensional work
[
14
]
, is that the MUSIC landscape possesses a global structure that is both analyzable and exploitable. The main theorem (
Theorem

3.2
) of the paper identifies a class of
admissible landscapes
for which simultaneous multiple initialization followed by gradient descent recovers the correct configuration.
The geometry of an admissible landscape is highly structured, see for example,
Fig.

1
. Regions where the MUSIC function is large are precisely those most susceptible to perturbation by noise, whereas regions where it is small—most importantly, the basins of attraction surrounding the relevant minima—remain comparatively stable. In particular, the size of these favorable regions does not collapse as the noise level increases within the admissible regime. This separation between unstable and stable parts of the landscape is the mechanism that makes global initialization by coarse thresholding, followed by local descent, both reliable and computationally effective. In this way, the theorem connects three issues that are often studied separately: the perturbation of subspace information by noise, the geometry of the resulting nonconvex objective, and the design of a computationally efficient recovery algorithm.

:
Contour plot of a perturbed MUSIC function
\widetilde{q}
. The red dots indicate the true parameters
\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{16}
, assumed separated by at least
1/8
, while the amplitudes
\{a_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{16}
are chosen independently from
\{\pm 1\}
. Samples are collected on the discrete square
Q_{10}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and corrupted by i.i.d. Gaussian noise with mean zero and variance one. The smallest local minima of the MUSIC function closely track the true parameter configuration.

A second main theme of the paper is optimal noise stability. Even if one can compute the relevant minima of
\widetilde{q}
, one must still ask whether these minima provide good approximations of the true parameters. This leads naturally to a minimax viewpoint. Given an estimator
\widehat{\vartheta}=\{\widehat{\theta}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
for
\vartheta=\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
, we measure the reconstruction error by

\min_{\psi\text{ permutation}}\max_{\ell=1,\dots,s}|\theta_{\ell}-\widehat{\theta}_{\psi(\ell)}|.

For notational convenience, one may re-index
\widehat{\vartheta}
so that the optimal permutation is the identity. The natural class of configurations is determined by a lower bound

\Delta(\vartheta)\gtrsim_{d}\big(\hbox{Sampling Scale}\big)^{-1}.

(1.5)

where

\Delta:=\Delta(\vartheta):=\min_{j\neq k}|\theta_{j}-\theta_{k}|

(1.6)

is the minimum separation among the point objects.
Traditional criteria such as the half-width or FWHM rule
[
5
]
provide an ad hoc notion of distinguishability, but they do not explicitly quantify stability under measurement perturbation. Donoho’s formulation of super-resolution
[
12
]
shifts the emphasis to inequalities of the form

\hbox{Error}\leq\hbox{Constant}\times\hbox{Noise Level},

thereby making the scaling of the stability constant itself part of the problem. The results of the present paper should be understood in precisely this spirit.
A principal objective of this paper is to determine how the optimal reconstruction error scales with the sampling diameter, the separation
\Delta
, and the noise level, under both adversarial and stochastic noise models.

To state our results concisely, we use the notation
x\lesssim y
to mean that
x\leq Cy
for an absolute constant
C
, and similarly for
x\gtrsim y
. We write
x\asymp y
when both inequalities hold. If the implicit constant depends only on the dimension
d
, we write
x\lesssim_{d}y
,
x\gtrsim_{d}y
, and
x\asymp_{d}y
.

The conclusions reached in this work may be summarized informally as follows. First, the MUSIC landscape admits a structural description in terms of subspace perturbation, and this description yields a constructive global optimization procedure (
Theorem

3.2
). Second, for natural multidimensional sampling geometries such as discrete cubes (
Theorem

4.1
) and continuous balls (
Theorem

5.1
), Gradient-MUSIC achieves the worst-case error scaling

\hbox{(Location) Error}\lesssim\big(\hbox{Sampling {Scale}}\big)^{-1}\times\hbox{Noise Level}

under adversarial noise, and the improved scaling

\hbox{(Location) Error}\lesssim\big(\hbox{Sampling {Scale}}\big)^{-1-d/2}\times\hbox{Noise Level}

up to logarithmic factors under Gaussian stationary noise.
This is the multidimensional analogue of the
noisy super-resolution scaling
, identified for one-dimensional ESPRIT in
[
11
]
. Up to logarithmic factors, this matches the optimal error scaling by the Cramér–Rao lower bound in
[
41
]
when
d=1
, and strongly suggests Gradient-MUSIC is optimal for Gaussian stationary noise for
d\geq 2
. The improvement reflects an averaging effect unavailable in the adversarial setting, and shows that the familiar reciprocal-aperture scaling is only the deterministic part of a richer statistical phenomenon.

This notion of noisy super-resolution scaling concerns not distinguishability itself, but
stability in accuracy under noise
and is different from the conventional meaning of super-resolution as breaking the diffraction limit.
The classical diffraction limit is a deterministic geometric concept: it describes the smallest scale at which nearby objects remain distinguishable, and typically leads to a resolution scale proportional to the reciprocal of the aperture or sampling diameter. In that sense, super-resolution means the ability to recover features below this classical resolution scale. In other words, conventional diffraction-limited super-resolution is about surpassing a geometric
resolution threshold, whereas noisy super-resolution scaling is about surpassing the noise-limited
accuracy one would predict from the deterministic aperture law alone. The former is a statement
about what scales are identifiable; the latter is a statement about how the estimation error decays
with aperture in a stochastic environment.

Third, these results are uniform and nonasymptotic, holding whenever the minimum separation is at least a sufficiently large constant multiple of the reciprocal sampling diameter. In the two examples and for deterministic noise, Gradient-MUSIC attains the optimal minimax scaling law in multiple dimensions, extending the one-dimensional optimality theory established in
[
14
]
. Definition of minimax optimal and lower bounds are deferred to
Section

6
. For the stochastic case, in view of Cramér-Rao lower bounds for
d=1

[
41
]
, our results strongly suggest that Gradient-MUSIC is also statistically optimal for Gaussian noise in higher dimensions.

The broader message is that multidimensional super-resolution can be understood geometrically through the signal subspace. Once this geometry is encoded by the MUSIC function, one obtains not only a practical reconstruction principle, but also a unified framework in which stability theory, nonconvex optimization, and resolution analysis become different aspects of the same underlying structure.

1.2
Related work in the literature

We conclude this section by situating the present work within the broader literature on spectral estimation, with particular emphasis on the multidimensional setting. The most relevant lines of work for comparison are classical MUSIC and related subspace methods, optimization-based approaches, and projection-based methods. A recurring theme is that the multidimensional problem is not merely a straightforward extension of the one-dimensional theory: both the geometry of the parameter space and the structure of the sampling set introduce phenomena that do not appear in the single-variable setting.

MUSIC and subspace methods.

Classical asymptotic analyses of MUSIC, such as
[
41
]
, control the discrepancy between
\vartheta
and the perturbed configuration
\widetilde{\vartheta}
in terms of complicated expressions that depend implicitly on both the model parameters and the realization of the noise. By contrast, our results provide explicit error bounds in terms of the fundamental quantities of the problem, such as the sampling diameter, the separation scale, and the noise level. Just as importantly, the classical theory does not address whether
\widetilde{\vartheta}
can in fact be computed numerically: it does not prove that the elements of
\widetilde{\vartheta}
are precisely the smallest
s
local minima of the perturbed MUSIC function, nor does it provide a constructive procedure for finding them.

Several works, including
[
26
,
27
,
24
]
, study perturbation and stability properties of the MUSIC function itself. Such results are certainly informative, but by themselves they do not imply stability of the underlying parameter set
\vartheta
. Controlling the perturbation of a function is not the same as controlling the perturbation of its relevant minima, and this is precisely why a more geometric analysis of the landscape is needed. In dimension
d=1
, these issues were addressed in our recent work
[
14
]
; the present paper extends that viewpoint to the multidimensional setting.

The multidimensional literature is considerably more limited. In particular,
[
27
]
derives pointwise perturbation bounds for the MUSIC function under the additional assumption that the projections of
\vartheta
onto the coordinate axes are well separated. Such an assumption is not intrinsic to the problem, and is not needed in our analysis:
Theorems

4.1
and
5.1
require only that
\vartheta
itself be separated in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. More fundamentally, as noted above, stability of the MUSIC function alone does not yield stability of the recovered configuration without further geometric arguments.

Optimization-based methods.

A substantial body of work approaches spectral estimation through optimization. On the convex side, several multidimensional formulations based on continuous compressed sensing or semidefinite relaxations are developed in
[
9
,
33
,
32
]
. These methods are powerful and mathematically elegant, but they may produce false positives, in the sense of outputting a point
\varphi
not close to any true parameter
\theta_{\ell}
, or may place multiple recovered points near the same
\theta_{\ell}
. Even setting aside these issues, the available quantitative guarantees do not appear to reach the optimal minimax scaling in the noisy multidimensional regime. For example, the theory in
[
32
]
implies that for samples on
X_{\star}=\{-m,\dots,m\}^{d}
and sufficiently small deterministic noise in
\ell^{2}
, the recovery accuracy is of order

C_{s}\frac{\sqrt{\|\eta\|_{\ell^{2}(X_{\star})}}}{m},

whereas the optimal minimax rate is of order

C\frac{\|\eta\|_{\ell^{2}(X_{\star})}}{m^{3/2}}

in the one-dimensional benchmark case.

A more classical nonconvex route is to estimate the full parameter vector
(\vartheta,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{sd}\times\mathbb{C}^{s}
via nonlinear least squares or maximum likelihood estimation; see, for example,
[
42
,
44
]
. These are optimization problems in very high dimension, especially when the number of sources
s
is large. From this viewpoint, the main obstacle is the curse of dimensionality: even if the global minimum is statistically optimal, its basin of attraction is expected to occupy only a tiny fraction of parameter space. This has been quantified in the one-dimensional setting under random Fourier measurements
[
44
]
, where the basin radius is on the order of the minimum separation
\Delta
, so that the basin volume decays exponentially in
s
. One of the main conceptual differences of the present work is that MUSIC reduces the optimization from the full parameter space to a function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, at the price of having to understand a nonconvex landscape with multiple relevant minima.

Projection-based methods.

A natural strategy in dimensions
d\geq 2
is to reduce the problem to a family of lower-dimensional ones by projection. For example, in dimension
d=2
, if
u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a unit vector, then

y(tu)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}a_{\ell}e^{2\pi i(\theta_{\ell}\cdot u)t},\qquad|t|\leq m,

is a one-dimensional signal of the same general form as (
1.1
), with projected frequencies
\{\theta_{\ell}\cdot u\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
. One may then attempt to recover the projected frequencies along sufficiently many directions
u
and reconstruct
\theta_{\ell}
from these projected data.

Projection methods are often attractive from the standpoint of sample complexity and dimensional scaling, since they reduce a multidimensional problem to a collection of one-dimensional ones. Their main weakness, however, is instability. Even when the original configuration
\vartheta
is well separated in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, its projection onto a given direction may have arbitrarily small separation, thereby producing an ill-conditioned one-dimensional inverse problem regardless of the reconstruction method used
[
24
,
4
]
. Some works, such as
[
8
,
29
]
, avoid this issue by assuming that suitable projection directions exist and are known in advance.

Recent papers
[
7
,
20
]
do not assume access to such good directions. A comparison between these two approaches is given in
[
20
]
. The algorithm in
[
7
]
, formulated for
d=2
, assumes a separation condition
\Delta\geq c\,m^{-1}
for an explicit constant
c
, but requires time and sample complexity at least

C\left(\frac{s\sigma}{\Delta}\right)^{s^{2}},

and also assumes that
\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{m})}
is sufficiently small compared with an inverse polynomial in
s
. By contrast,
Theorem

4.1
uses
O_{d}(m^{d})
samples and permits a much weaker noise condition; see (
4.4
).

The randomized algorithm of
[
20
]
shows that, when

m\Delta\gtrsim_{d}\log(s/\delta),

the frequencies can be estimated with accuracy

C_{d}m^{-1}\sqrt{m^{-d}\|\eta\|_{L^{2}([0,m]^{d})}^{2}+\delta|a|_{1}}

with probability
1-o(s)
. In particular, this yields the rate

C_{d}\,m^{-d/2-1}\|\eta\|_{L^{2}([0,m]^{d})}

under the stronger separation requirement

m\Delta\gtrsim_{d}\log\!\Bigl(|a|_{1}sm^{d}\|\eta\|_{L^{2}([0,m]^{d})}^{-2}\Bigr).

Gradient-MUSIC attains the same scaling; see
Theorem

5.1
(with the corresponding stochastic-noise estimate), but under the milder condition

m\Delta\gtrsim_{d}1.

The trade-off is that the randomized projection-based algorithm is faster.

ESPRIT is another widely used subspace method. In one dimension
[
36
]
, it exploits the fact that translation becomes modulation under the Fourier transform, thereby recasting spectral estimation as an eigenvalue problem. Multidimensional extensions construct a family of matrices corresponding to several shift directions spanning
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In the noiseless case these matrices are simultaneously diagonalizable; in the noisy case, however, poor choice of shift directions can again lead to instability. Various strategies have been proposed to mitigate this issue; see
[
1
,
16
,
37
,
3
]
. Nevertheless, many fundamental questions about the stability and optimality of multidimensional ESPRIT remain open.

Other methods.

Prony’s method in one dimension
[
34
,
22
]
recovers the parameters by locating the roots of an associated trigonometric polynomial. In its classical form, it uses only the minimal number of samples and therefore cannot benefit from oversampling when
m\gg s
. Two multivariate extensions may be found in
[
23
,
38
]
, although we are not aware of corresponding sharp stability results under noise. In one dimension, root-MUSIC can also be formulated through the roots of a univariate polynomial
[
35
]
; we are not aware of a satisfactory multidimensional analogue. There are also recent approaches based on rational approximation and interpolation; see
[
49
,
10
]
.

In summary, the distinctive feature of the present work is not merely that it studies MUSIC in multiple dimensions, but that it gives a constructive and quantitatively sharp theory linking subspace perturbation, nonconvex landscape geometry, and computational recovery. This combination appears to be largely absent from the existing multidimensional literature.

Notation

We collect here the notation used throughout the paper.

Asymptotic notation.

Absolute constants are denoted by

Assumption 1

3
Main structural theorem and algorithm

In general, greedy local methods such as gradient descent need not converge to the minima one wishes to recover. They may become trapped at stationary points, or converge to undesirable local minima, unless the objective function enjoys additional structure or the initialization is already sufficiently accurate. In many inverse problems, especially in high dimension, the real difficulty is therefore not the local convergence mechanism itself, but rather the construction of reliable initialization.

Since the MUSIC function is generally nonconvex and one seeks multiple minima rather than a single global minimizer, the practical success of the method depends on whether one can locate suitable initial points efficiently. Gradient-MUSIC addresses this by thresholding the MUSIC function on a coarse grid and then using local descent only after the relevant regions of the landscape have been identified. The theoretical question is therefore not merely whether gradient descent converges locally, but whether the landscape admits a global organization that makes this two-stage strategy reliable. One of the central results of the paper is that, under natural assumptions, it does.

The following definition isolates a class of landscapes for which this difficulty can be overcome. Informally, an admissible landscape is one in which each true parameter is accompanied by a nearby local minimum with a basin of attraction of controlled size, while all points sufficiently far from the true configuration lie above a definite threshold. Such a structure makes it possible to combine coarse global search with local descent in a provably effective way.

For a given local optimization scheme, we say that a set
S\subseteq\Omega
is a
basin of attraction
of
\omega\in\Omega
if every trajectory of the scheme initialized at a point
\omega_{0}\in S
converges to
\omega
.

Definition 3.1

(Admissible optimization landscape)
.

Let
f\colon\Omega\to[0,\infty)
, let
\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}\subseteq\Omega
, and let
\varepsilon,\rho,\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}
satisfy

0<\varepsilon<\rho,\qquad 0\leq\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}.

We say that
f
is an
admissible optimization landscape
for the given configuration
\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
, with parameters
(\varepsilon,\rho,\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})
, if the following hold:

1.

For each
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
, the function
f
has a local minimum
\widetilde{\theta}_{\ell}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\ell})
, and the ball
B_{\rho}(\theta_{\ell})
is contained in the basin of attraction of
\widetilde{\theta}_{\ell}
.

2.

The relevant minima are uniformly shallow in value and all points outside the attraction regions are uniformly higher:

\max_{\ell=1,\dots,s}f(\widetilde{\theta}_{\ell})\leq\alpha_{0},\qquad f(\omega)>\alpha_{1}\quad\text{for all }\omega\notin\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{s}B_{\rho}(\theta_{\ell}).

Let us briefly explain why this is the right notion. The first requirement says that each
\widetilde{\theta}_{\ell}
is an
\varepsilon
-accurate approximation of
\theta_{\ell}
, and that it can be found by a local method initialized anywhere in the larger ball
B_{\rho}(\theta_{\ell})
. Thus
\rho
measures the effective width of the attraction region and may be much larger than the target accuracy
\varepsilon
. The second requirement separates the desirable wells from the rest of the landscape by a definite gap
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{0}
. This gap is precisely what makes coarse thresholding possible.

The next lemma formalizes this observation.

Lemma 3.1

(Thresholding yields valid initialization)
.

Suppose that
f\colon\Omega\to[0,\infty)
is an admissible optimization landscape for
\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
with parameters
(\varepsilon,\rho,\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})
. Let
G\subseteq\Omega
be a finite set satisfying

{\rm mesh}(G)\leq\min\left\{\rho,\,\frac{\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{0}}{\|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}}\right\}.

Then for every
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
,

B_{\rho}(\theta_{\ell})\cap\{\omega\in G:f(\omega)\leq\alpha_{1}\}\neq\emptyset.

Proof.

Fix
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
. By the definition of mesh norm, there exists
\omega_{\ell}\in G
such that

|\omega_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell}|\leq{\rm mesh}(G)\leq\rho.

Hence
\omega_{\ell}\in B_{\rho}(\theta_{\ell})
. By the mean value theorem,

|f(\omega_{\ell})-f(\theta_{\ell})|\leq\|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,|\omega_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell}|\leq\|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,{\rm mesh}(G).

Since
f
is admissible,
f(\theta_{\ell})\leq\alpha_{0}
, and therefore

f(\omega_{\ell})\leq\alpha_{0}+\|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,{\rm mesh}(G)\leq\alpha_{1}.

Thus
\omega_{\ell}\in B_{\rho}(\theta_{\ell})\cap\{\omega\in G:f(\omega)\leq\alpha_{1}\}
, as claimed.
∎

The significance of
Lemma

3.1
is that the mesh norm of the initialization set
G
need not depend on the final accuracy parameter
\varepsilon
. This is crucial: in the small-noise regime one expects
\varepsilon
to be very small, but the lemma shows that one may still initialize from a much coarser set, provided the basin width
\rho
and threshold gap
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{0}
are sufficiently large. Consequently, the thresholded set

\{\omega\in G:f(\omega)\leq\alpha_{1}\}

contains, near each true parameter, at least one point belonging to the appropriate basin of attraction. In practice these points appear in well-separated clusters, and one may simply choose one representative per cluster as an initialization for local descent.

Of course, for our purposes it is not enough merely to know that an admissible landscape exists. The actual values of
(\varepsilon,\rho,\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})
matter. One wants
\varepsilon
to be small, since it controls the final reconstruction error, and ideally it should decrease as the sampling aperture grows or the noise decreases. At the same time, one wants both
\rho
and the separation gap
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{0}
to be as large as possible, since these determine how coarse the initialization grid may be and hence how efficient the thresholding step becomes.

The following theorem is the main abstract result of the paper. It shows that, under explicit conditions on the measurement kernel and the subspace perturbation, the perturbed MUSIC function is an admissible landscape for gradient descent. The concrete theorems for the cube and ball settings,
Theorems

4.1
and
5.1
, are obtained by verifying these abstract conditions in the corresponding model geometries.

It is worth emphasizing that

In summary,

is the structural bridge between subspace perturbation and computational recovery. It shows that once the measurement kernel has the appropriate local and global properties, and once the estimated subspace is sufficiently accurate, the perturbed MUSIC function automatically acquires the geometry needed for a practical global optimization method: coarse thresholding supplies reliable initialization, and local descent completes the recovery.

3.1
The algorithm

The computational philosophy of Gradient-MUSIC is simple. Rather than attempting to solve the full spectral estimation problem directly in the high-dimensional parameter space
(\vartheta,a)
, one first extracts the signal subspace from the data and then recovers the parameters by optimizing the associated MUSIC landscape in the physical parameter domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This separates the problem into two conceptually distinct tasks: subspace estimation and landscape navigation. The first task compresses the data into the geometrically relevant information; the second exploits the fact that, under suitable conditions, the smallest minima of the MUSIC function remain both identifiable and computationally accessible. The role of the present section is to describe this algorithmic framework and to indicate, through canonical examples, how the general theory applies to concrete sampling geometries.

Algorithm 1

Gradient-MUSIC – coarse thresholding followed by local descent

Projection operator
P_{\widetilde{U}}
onto an
s
-dimensional subspace
\widetilde{U}
.

Identify the connected clusters of

.

The input is a projection operator

3.2
Subspace estimation

The groundwork for applying the structural theorem and algorithm is the approximation of the projection operator onto the signal subspace
U={\rm span}\{e^{2\pi ix\cdot\theta_{\ell}}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}.

This step is both natural and unavoidable: if the parameters
\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
were known, then the associated subspace would be known as well. In this sense, subspace estimation is the minimal linear-algebraic task underlying parameter recovery.

A standard way to estimate this subspace is to lift the measurement data
\widetilde{y}
on
X_{\star}
to a structured Hankel matrix or operator.
Under this assumption we define the Hankel-type operator

H(\widetilde{y})\colon L^{2}_{\nu^{\prime}}(X^{\prime})\to L^{2}_{\nu}(X)

formally by

H(\widetilde{y})f(x)=\int_{X^{\prime}}\widetilde{y}(x+x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})\,d\nu^{\prime}(x^{\prime}).

(3.9)

Since
x\in X
and
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
imply
x+x^{\prime}\in X_{\star}
, the operator
H(\widetilde{y})
depends only on the observed data
\widetilde{y}|_{X_{\star}}
. When
\widetilde{y}(x+x^{\prime})\in L^{2}_{\nu\times\nu^{\prime}}(X\times X^{\prime})
(i.e., we interpret
\widetilde{y}
as a function on
X\times X^{\prime}
), the operator
H(\widetilde{y})
is Hilbert–Schmidt and consequently has point spectrum with zero as the only accumulation point.
To well define

\widetilde{U}\subseteq L^{2}_{\nu}(X)

as the leading
s
-dimensional left singular space of
H(\widetilde{y})
we need a singular value gap,

\sigma_{s}(H(\widetilde{y}))>\sigma_{s+1}(H(\widetilde{y})),

(3.10)

see
Lemma

3.3
below.

When
U
and
\widetilde{U}
are finite-dimensional,
\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\|

is exactly the sine of the largest canonical angle between the two subspaces. Thus the problem of subspace estimation is naturally quantified in terms of principal-angle perturbation.

This perspective is foundational in modern perturbation theory. In the case where
H(\widetilde{y})
can be identified with a matrix (i.e., both
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
are purely atomic with finitely many dirac masses), a central tool is the Wedin sine-theta theorem
[
47
]
. We will, however, adopt the approach of a general theory for perturbation of linear operators in Kato
[
21
, Theorem 3.18 on page 214]
.

To this end, define the operators

\displaystyle T_{\vartheta}\colon\mathbb{C}^{s}\to L^{2}_{\nu}(X),

\displaystyle\quad\text{where}\quad T_{\vartheta}v(x):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}v_{\ell}e^{2\pi i\theta_{\ell}\cdot x}\in L^{2}_{\nu}(X),

(3.11)

\displaystyle T_{\vartheta}^{\prime}\colon\mathbb{C}^{s}\to L^{2}_{\nu^{\prime}}(X^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\quad\text{where}\quad(T_{\vartheta}^{\prime})v(x):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}v_{\ell}e^{2\pi i\theta_{\ell}\cdot x}\in L^{2}_{\nu^{\prime}}(X^{\prime}).

(3.12)

The operator
T_{\vartheta}
has the adjoint

T_{\vartheta}^{*}\colon L^{2}_{\nu}(X)\to\mathbb{C}^{s},\quad\text{where}\quad(T_{\vartheta}^{*}f)_{\ell}=\int_{X}f(x)e^{-2\pi i\theta_{\ell}\cdot x}\,d\nu(x).

The kernel matrix
K_{\vartheta}
in (
2.10
) and
T_{\vartheta}
are related by the formula

K_{\vartheta}=\frac{1}{\nu(X)}T_{\vartheta}^{*}T_{\vartheta}.

(3.13)

In the language of harmonic analysis, in particular frame theory, both
T_{\vartheta}
and
T_{\vartheta}^{\prime}
can be interpreted as “synthesis” operators. We prove the following lemma in
Appendix

A
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
y
defined in (
1.1
), the operator
H(y)
has rank
s
and

\sigma_{s}(H(y))\geq\sigma_{s}(T_{\vartheta})\sigma_{s}(T_{\vartheta}^{\prime}).

Suppose

4\|H(\eta)\|\leq\sigma_{s}(H(y))\quad\hbox{and}\quad\widetilde{y}(x+x^{\prime})\in L^{2}_{\nu\times\nu^{\prime}}(X\times X^{\prime}).

(3.14)

Then

\sigma_{s}(H(\widetilde{y}))>\sigma_{s+1}(H(\widetilde{y})),

the projection
P_{\widetilde{U}}
onto the
s
-dimensional leading left singular space of
H(\widetilde{y})
is well-defined, and

{\left\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\right\|}\leq\frac{64}{\pi}\frac{\sigma_{1}(H(y))}{\sigma_{s}^{2}(H(y))}\,\|H(\eta)\|\leq\frac{64|a|_{\infty}}{\pi}\frac{\sigma_{1}(T_{\vartheta})\sigma_{1}(T_{\vartheta}^{\prime})}{\sigma_{s}^{2}(T_{\vartheta})\sigma_{s}^{2}(T_{\vartheta}^{\prime})}\,\|H(\eta)\|.

If additionally
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
are purely atomic with a finite number of atoms (i.e.,
H(y)
and
H(\widetilde{y})
are matrices), then the previous inequality can be improved to

{\left\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\right\|}\leq\frac{4}{3}\frac{1}{\sigma_{s}(H(y))}\|H(\eta)\|\leq\frac{4}{3}\frac{1}{\sigma_{s}(T_{\vartheta})\sigma_{s}(T_{\vartheta}^{\prime})}\|H(\eta)\|.

In addition to initiating the Gradient-MUSIC algorithm,
Lemma

3.3
is an important tool for showing that Gradient-MUSIC achieves the minimax optimality under adversarial noise and noisy super-resolution scaling under random noise by deriving useful estimates of
\sigma_{s}(H(y))
and
\|H(\eta)\|
.

Theorem 4.1

(Gradient-MUSIC for discrete samples in a cube)
.

Suppose we are given noisy samples of
\widetilde{y}=y+\eta

X_{\star}=\{-2m,\dots,2m\}^{d},

with

Choose a grid

Then the following hold.

(a)

Adversarial noise.

Suppose that for some

(4.4)

Then

is well-defined as the leading

Let

(4.5)

(4.6)

(b)

Random noise.

Suppose
\eta=\sigma g
, where the values of

are i.i.d. standard Normal random variables

, and

\|\sigma\|_{\ell^{2}(X_{\star})}\lesssim_{d}\frac{m^{d}}{|a|_{\infty}\sqrt{\log m}}.

(4.7)

Then
\widetilde{U}
is well-defined as the leading
s
-dimensional left singular space of
H(\widetilde{y})
.

Let
\{\theta_{\ell}^{\sharp}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
denote the
n
-th iterate of Gradient-MUSIC, where

n\asymp\log\!\left(\frac{m^{d}}{\|\sigma\|_{\ell^{2}(X_{\star})}\sqrt{\log m}}\right).

(4.8)

Then, with probability at least

,

(4.9)

Part (a) of

4.1
concerns deterministic, or adversarial, perturbations. The size condition (

) is essentially necessary, and estimate (

and

. The conclusion admits an interpretation that is both important and somewhat surprising. For instance, when

, the quantity

need not decrease with

Thus the pointwise noise level may remain uniformly comparable to the signal strength as the sampling cube grows. Nevertheless, the reconstruction error in (

) still decays like

(or faster for smaller

). In other words, the location error tends to zero even when the local signal-to-noise ratio does not improve. This quantifies a basic benefit of oversampling: increasing the sampling set yields more information about the configuration even when individual measurements do not become more accurate.

Part (b) concerns stochastic noise and exhibits a genuinely stronger scaling law. If the noise values on

are i.i.d.

, so that

. The hypothesis (

Thus even when the noise variance increases with distance from the origin, and eventually dominates the signal magnitude in the sense that

remains bounded while

grows, the reconstruction error may still converge to zero as

\Delta(\vartheta)\geq\beta d/m
,
we have

(2m+1)^{d/2}\sqrt{1-(e^{1/(2\beta)}-1)}\leq\sigma_{\min}(T_{\vartheta})\leq\sigma_{\max}(T_{\vartheta})\leq(2m+1)^{d/2}\sqrt{1+(e^{1/(2\beta)}-1)}.

This proposition is stated using the

norm on the separation of
\vartheta
rather than the

distance. The following lemma is a reformulation of

for future ease of reference.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
d\geq 2
,
\vartheta\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
, and
\beta\geq\pi d^{3/2}/\log(2)
such that
\Delta(\vartheta)\geq\beta/m
, we have

1-\frac{d^{3/2}}{\beta}\leq\lambda_{\min}(K_{\vartheta})\leq\lambda_{\max}(K_{\vartheta})\leq 1+\frac{d^{3/2}}{\beta}.

Proof.

Notice that the kernel matrix (
2.10
) is related to
T_{\vartheta}
by the formula

K_{\vartheta}=\frac{1}{(2m+1)^{d}}\,T_{\vartheta}^{*}T_{\vartheta}.

(4.14)

We have

\Delta_{\infty}(\vartheta)=\min_{j\neq k}|\theta_{j}-\theta_{k}|_{\infty}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\min_{j\neq k}|\theta_{j}-\theta_{k}|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\Delta(\vartheta)\geq\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{d}m}.

We use
Proposition

4.4
(where
\beta/d^{3/2}
plays the role of
\beta
) and use (
4.14
) to see that

1-\left(e^{d^{3/2}/(2\beta)}-1\right)\leq\lambda_{\min}(K_{\vartheta})\leq\lambda_{\max}(K_{\vartheta})\leq 1+\left(e^{d^{3/2}/(2\beta)}-1\right).

To finish the proof, we use the inequality
e^{t}-1\leq 2t
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
∎

Next we estimate the behavior of
E_{0}(\vartheta)
and
E_{1}(\vartheta)
. The main technical obstruction is that
D_{m}
decays slowly along the coordinate axes. Indeed,

|d_{m}(t)|\lesssim\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{m|t|}\right\},

so for any
\xi
on the

-coordinate axis,

regardless of dimension
d
. This decay is too weak to control
E_{0}(\vartheta)
and
E_{1}(\vartheta)
through a naive argument. Instead, we must resort to a more intricate strategy. The following lemma requires additional preparatory lemmas, which are stated and proved in
Section

B.2
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
d\geq 2
,
\vartheta\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
, and
\beta\geq 4\pi\sqrt{d}
such that
\Delta:=\Delta(\vartheta)\geq\beta/m
, we have

E_{0}(\vartheta)=\frac{16\pi^{2}d^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad E_{1}(\vartheta)=\frac{64\pi^{2}d^{3}m^{2}}{\beta^{2}}.

Proof.

Fix any
\vartheta
satisfying the lemma’s assumptions and
\omega\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Consider the set

\Gamma:=\{\omega-\theta_{\ell}\colon|\omega-\theta_{\ell}|\geq\Delta/2\}.

Since
\Gamma
is a subset of
\vartheta
shifted by
\omega
which does not change its minimum separation, we have
\Delta(\Gamma)\geq\Delta(\vartheta)\geq\beta/m
and consequently,
\Delta_{\infty}(\Gamma)\geq\beta/(\sqrt{d}m)
. By construction,
\emptyset=\Gamma\cap B_{\Delta/2}\supseteq\Gamma\cap B_{\beta/(2m)}\supseteq\Gamma\cap Q_{\beta/(2\sqrt{d}m)}
. Then we apply
Lemma

B.1
for

to get

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\colon|\omega-\theta_{\ell}|\geq\Delta/2}|D_{m}(\omega-\theta_{\ell})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16\pi^{2}d^{2}}{\beta^{2}},

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\colon|\omega-\theta_{\ell}|\geq\Delta/2}|\nabla D_{m}(\omega-\theta_{\ell})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{64\pi^{4}d^{3}m^{2}}{\beta^{2}}.

Both inequalities hold uniformly over
\omega\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
, which completes the proof.
∎

At this point, we have obtained inequalities for the quantities that appear in
Theorem

3.2
which are uniform in
\vartheta
, depending only on
\beta
, the constant that appears in the separation condition

Model case 2: Continuous samples in a ball

We next consider the continuous sampling geometry

the ball of radius
2m

This represents a continuous sampling setup, where
\widetilde{y}(x)
is known at each

and
we define the effective sampling set

X:=B_{m}\quad\text{and}\quad\nu:=|\cdot|\quad\text{is Lebesgue measure restricted to $B_{m}$}.

The symmetry and moment conditions in
1
clearly hold.

For this case, we have
\phi_{\omega}\colon B_{m}\to\mathbb{C}
, where

\phi_{\omega}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|B_{m}|}}e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot x}.

To distinguish between the general notation and this special setting, we use
W_{m}
to denote the measurement kernel
K
. Letting
J_{\alpha}
be the Bessel function of the first kind with parameter
\alpha
, where we follow the convention in
[
48

, we have the formula,

W_{m}(\xi):=\frac{1}{|B_{m}|}\int_{B_{m}}e^{2\pi i\xi\cdot x}\,dx=\frac{1}{|B_{1}|}\frac{J_{d/2}(2\pi m|\xi|)}{m^{d/2}|\xi|^{d/2}}.

(5.1)

A calculation shows that

\Psi:=-\nabla^{2}W_{m}(0)=\frac{1}{d+2}4\pi^{2}m^{2}I.

(5.2)

For the perturbation
\eta
, we allow either deterministic noise or a class of Gaussian random fields with mild regularity, described below.

Definition 5.1

(Gaussian random field with spectral representation)
.

Let
W
be a standard Brownian field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
be bounded and compactly supported, normalized so that

\|\gamma\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1.

We say that
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
is a
Gaussian random field with spectral representation

\gamma
if

g(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma(t)e^{2\pi it\cdot x}\,dW(t).

Then
g
is stationary, mean zero, and has unit variance:

\mathbb{E}|g(x)|^{2}=\|\gamma\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=1.

The following theorem gives the corresponding guarantees for Gradient-MUSIC.

Theorem 5.1

(Gradient-MUSIC for continuous samples in a ball)
.

Suppose we are given noisy samples of
\widetilde{y}=y+\eta
on

X_{\star}=B_{2m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},

with
d\geq 1
and
m>0
. Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded, and suppose

\vartheta=\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}\subseteq\Omega,\qquad s\geq 1,

satisfies the separation condition

\Delta(\vartheta)\gtrsim_{d}m^{-1}.

Choose a grid
G\subseteq\Omega
satisfying

{\rm mesh}(G)\asymp_{d}m^{-1},\qquad\alpha_{1}\asymp_{d}1,\qquad h\asymp m^{-2}.

Then the following hold.

(a)

Adversarial noise.

Suppose that for some
p\in[1,\infty]
,

\|\eta\|_{L^{p}(B_{2m})}\lesssim_{d}\frac{m^{d/p}}{|a|_{\infty}}.

(5.3)

Then
\widetilde{U}
is well-defined as the leading
s
-dimensional left singular space of
H(\widetilde{y})
.

Let
\{\theta_{\ell}^{\sharp}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
denote the
n
-th iterate of Gradient-MUSIC, where

n\asymp\log\!\left(\frac{m^{d/p}}{|a|_{\infty}\|\eta\|_{L^{p}(B_{2m})}}\right).

(5.4)

Then

\max_{\ell=1,\dots,s}\bigl|\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell}^{\sharp}\bigr|\lesssim_{d}\frac{|a|_{\infty}\|\eta\|_{L^{p}(B_{2m})}}{m^{1+d/p}}.

(5.5)

(b)

Random noise.

Suppose
\eta=\sigma g
, where
g
is a Gaussian random field with spectral representation
\gamma
. Let
\chi_{m}
be any function satisfying
\chi_{m}=1
on
B_{2m}
, and let
\sigma\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be nonnegative and satisfy

\displaystyle\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}(B_{2m})}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d}\frac{m^{d}}{|a|_{\infty}\sqrt{\log m}},

(5.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl|\widehat{\sigma\chi_{m}}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}\log(1+|\xi|)\,d\xi

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d}\log(m)\,\|\sigma\chi_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

(5.7)

Then
\widetilde{U}
is well-defined as the leading
s
-dimensional left singular space of
H(\widetilde{y})
.

Let
\{\theta_{\ell}^{\sharp}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
denote the
n
-th iterate of Gradient-MUSIC, where

n\asymp\log\!\left(\frac{m^{d}}{|a|_{\infty}\|\sigma\chi_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sqrt{\log m}}\right).

(5.8)

Then, with probability at least
1-m^{-d}

\max_{\ell=1,\dots,s}\bigl|\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell}^{\sharp}\bigr|\lesssim_{d,\gamma}\frac{|a|_{\infty}\|\sigma\chi_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sqrt{\log m}}{m^{d+1}}.

(5.9)

Part (a) of
Theorem

5.1
concerns deterministic, or adversarial, perturbations. The size condition (

Part (b) concerns Gaussian random fields with spectral representation. The size condition (

The second condition (

. By Parseval’s identity, the same inequality without the logarithmic weight would hold automatically, so (

This framework also captures more realistic imaging models. In tomography, for instance, one often samples along hyperplanes through the origin whose orientations are uniformly distributed, with measurements corrupted by stationary noise. Because this sampling scheme oversamples regions near the origin, the effective signal-to-noise ratio is spatially inhomogeneous. After suitable preprocessing and re-weighting as discussed in
Section

6
Minimax error bounds

We show in this section that the error bounds in
Theorems

4.1
and
5.1
are minimax optimal in the sense defined below. Throughout we fix an integer
s\geq 1
and let
m\geq s
. We define the
parameter space
,

\mathcal{P}:=\big\{(\vartheta,a)\colon|\vartheta|=|a|=s,\,\min_{\ell}|a_{\ell}|=1\big\}\subseteq(\Omega\times\mathbb{C})^{s}.

(6.1)

Let
\mathcal{N}
denote a set of functions on
X_{\star}
, called the
noise class
, which becomes a
\ell^{p}
or
L^{p}
ball of prescribed radius for Model case 1 or 2, respectively. Let the
signal class

\mathcal{S}
be the set of parameters such that
m\Delta\geq\beta
for an appropriate
\beta
.

With the notation

\widetilde{y}:=\widetilde{y}(\vartheta,a,\eta)=(y+\eta)|_{X_{\star}}

(6.2)

we refer to

\mathcal{Y}:=\{\widetilde{y}(\vartheta,a,\eta)\colon(\vartheta,a)\in\mathcal{S},\,\eta\in\mathcal{N}\},

as the
data space
.

Any measurable function
\psi:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{P}
defines a
method
. Let

(\widehat{\vartheta},\widehat{a})=\psi(\widetilde{y}(\vartheta,a,\eta))

denote the output of
\psi
. From here onward, we simply write
(\widehat{\vartheta},\widehat{a})
instead of
\psi
. Just like the other parts of this paper, we have implicitly assumed that
\vartheta
and
\widehat{\vartheta}
have been indexed to best match each other.

For any method
\psi
, with its output
(\widehat{\vartheta},\widehat{a})
, the worst case errors are given by

\displaystyle\Theta(\psi,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{(\vartheta,a)\in\mathcal{S}}\,\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{N}}\,\max_{j=1,\dots,s}|\theta_{\ell}-\widehat{\theta}_{\ell}|,

\displaystyle A(\psi,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{(\vartheta,a)\in\mathcal{S}}\,\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{N}}\,\max_{j=1,\dots,s}|a_{\ell}-\widehat{a}_{\ell}|.

For given
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{N}
, the
minimax errors
among all methods are given by

\displaystyle\Theta_{\star}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N}):=\inf_{\psi}\Theta(\psi,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N}),

\displaystyle A_{\star}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N}):=\inf_{\psi}A(\psi,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N}).

Since we do not preclude the use of intractable algorithms or even non-computable functions, it is not apriori clear the minimizers exist and, when they do, if they are computable or tractable.

We are interested in obtaining an explicit lower bounds for these quantities. There is a standard abstract argument (see
[
4
,
24
,
14
]
) which reduces the problem down to providing an explicit example.

Lemma 6.1
.

Suppose there are distinct pairs
(\vartheta,a),(\vartheta^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}
, and
\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
such that

\widetilde{y}(\vartheta,a,\eta)=\widetilde{y}(\vartheta^{\prime},a^{\prime},\eta^{\prime}).

(6.3)

Then

\displaystyle\Theta_{\star}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})\geq\frac{1}{2}\max_{\ell=1,\dots,s}|\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell}^{\prime}|,\quad\text{and}\quad A_{\star}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})\geq\frac{1}{2}\max_{\ell=1,\dots,s}|a_{\ell}-a_{\ell}^{\prime}|.

Proof.

By assumption (
6.3
), there are two sets of parameters
(\vartheta,a)
and
(\vartheta^{\prime},a^{\prime})
that generate the same noisy data
\widetilde{y}
. Let
\psi
be an arbitrary method with the output
(\widehat{\vartheta},\widehat{a})
. Given data (
6.3
), by definition of
\Theta(\psi,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})
, we have

\displaystyle\Theta(\psi,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})\geq\max_{\ell=1,\dots,s}|\widehat{\theta}_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell}|\quad\text{and}\quad\Theta(\psi,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})\geq\max_{\ell=1,\dots,s}|\widehat{\theta}_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell}^{\prime}|.

(Here,
\widehat{\vartheta}
is sorted to best match
\vartheta
in the first expression, while it is sorted by a possibly different permutation to best match
\vartheta^{\prime}
in the second expression.) By triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\Theta(\psi,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})\geq\max\left\{\max_{\ell=1,\dots,s}|\widehat{\theta}_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell}|,\,\max_{\ell=1,\dots,s}|\widehat{\theta}_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell}^{\prime}|\right\}\geq\frac{1}{2}\max_{\ell=1,\dots,s}|\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell}^{\prime}|.

Since
\psi
is arbitrary, taking the sup over all
\psi
proves the first claim inequality for
\Theta_{\star}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})
. Repeating the same argument for the amplitudes instead completes the proof.
∎

For the two model cases in
Sections

4
and
5
, let
\mathcal{S}
have a
\beta
and
\mathcal{N}
a
\ell^{p}
or
L^{p}
ball as specified in
Theorem

4.1
or
Theorem

5.1
, respectively.

Theorem 6.2

(Minimax lower bound for

Let

(\vartheta^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}
and the current ordering of

\eta:=y(\vartheta,a)-y(\vartheta^{\prime},a^{\prime}),

so that trivially,

\widetilde{y}(\vartheta,a,0)=\widetilde{y}(\vartheta^{\prime},a^{\prime},\eta).

We proceed to control the
\ell^{p}
norm of
\eta
. Since
\vartheta
and
\vartheta^{\prime}
are identical except for
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{1}^{\prime}
(and analogously for

We proceed to estimate the
\ell^{p}
norm of
\eta
. Using that
|1-e^{2\pi it}|\leq 2\pi t
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
and that
|x_{1}|\leq 2m
for
x\in X_{\star}
, we get

\displaystyle|\eta(x)|

\displaystyle=|1-a_{1}^{\prime}e^{2\pi i\delta x_{1}}|

\displaystyle\leq|1-a_{1}^{\prime}|+|a_{1}^{\prime}||1-e^{2\pi i\delta x_{1}}|

\displaystyle\leq m\delta+2\pi(1+\delta)\delta|x_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq m\delta(1+4\pi+4\pi\delta).

By choice of
\delta
and by making

sufficiently small, we have

which proves that

. By

6.1
, we have

\displaystyle\Theta_{\star}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\max_{\ell=1,\dots,s}|\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell}^{\prime}|=\frac{1}{2}|\theta_{1}-\theta_{1}^{\prime}|=\frac{1}{2}\delta=\frac{c_{d}\varepsilon}{2m^{1+d/p}},

\displaystyle A_{\star}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\max_{\ell=1,\dots,s}|a_{\ell}-a_{\ell}^{\prime}|=\frac{1}{2}|a_{1}-a_{1}^{\prime}|=\frac{1}{2}m\delta=\frac{c_{d}\varepsilon}{2m^{d/p}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Comparing
Theorem

4.1
and
Theorem

6.2
, we see that Gradient-MUSIC is minimax optimal in
\|\eta\|_{\ell^{p}(X_{\star})}
and
m
for all
p\in[1,\infty]
.

(Minimax lower bound for

.

Let

,

,

, and

7
Geometric analysis: Proof of structural theorem

The approach proposed in this paper hinges on a geometric analysis of the MUSIC function
q:=q_{U}
associated with the true subspace
U:=U_{\vartheta}
and its perturbed counterpart
\widetilde{q}:=q_{\widetilde{U}}
. The geometric analysis will show how
q
and
\widetilde{q}
are influenced by properties of
K
, which itself depends on the sampling set
X_{\star}
.

The outline of our analysis is as follows. First we establish some basic properties, formulas, and inequalities in
Section

7.1
. Afterwards, we start with the simpler task of analyzing the noiseless MUSIC function
q
. We will derive various quantitative estimates for
q
.
Lemma

7.1
identifies an important parameter
\tau
such that such that

\nabla^{2}q(\omega)\approx-2\nabla^{2}K(0)=2\Psi\quad\text{for all}\quad\omega\in B_{\tau}(\theta_{\ell}).

Hence,
q
is strictly convex in
B_{\tau}(\theta_{\ell})
if
\Psi
is strictly positive definite and so
\tau
can be thought of as the proper scale on which properties of
q
can be deduced from that of
K
.

For
\omega
far away from all the
\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
, we use a different argument. The proof of
Lemma

7.2
provides a global approximation result such that

1-q(\omega)\approx\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}|K(\omega-\theta_{\ell})|^{2}\quad\text{for all}\quad\omega\in\Omega.

Under our main assumptions, this is enough to deduce that

q(\omega)\geq C_{\vartheta}\quad\text{for all}\quad\omega\not\in\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{s}B_{\tau}(\theta_{\ell})

for a
C_{\vartheta}>0
which can be further estimated.

We are of course more interested in a perturbed MUSIC function
\widetilde{q}
under an assumption that
{\left\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\right\|}
is small enough. Several properties of
q
carry over to
\widetilde{q}
through
Lemma

7.3
. We call these Bernstein-type inequalities since they control

\|\partial^{\alpha}q-\partial^{\alpha}\widetilde{q}\|_{L^{2}_{\nu}}

in terms of
K
and
\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\|
.

The main technical difficultly is showing that
\widetilde{q}
has
s
local minima
\widetilde{\vartheta}
that are within
\varepsilon
to
\vartheta
, where
\varepsilon
is potentially much closer than both
\tau
and
{\left\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\right\|}
. This step is carried out in an abstract result,
Lemma

7.4
.

Combining these ingredients yields
Theorem

3.2
and is done in
Section

7.4
.

7.1
Some formulas and inequalities

Throughout the expository portions, we assume that
1
holds. It is straightforward to verify that
\phi_{\omega}
is infinitely differentiable in
\omega
. Due to the fourth moment condition in
1
, we see that

\partial^{\alpha}_{\omega}\phi_{\omega}\in L^{2}_{\nu},\quad\text{for all}\quad|\alpha|\leq 2.

For all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
\max\{|\alpha|,|\beta|\}\leq 2
, we have

\begin{split}{\left\langle\partial_{\omega^{\prime}}^{\alpha}\phi_{\omega^{\prime}},\partial_{\omega}^{\beta}\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}&=(-1)^{|\alpha|}i^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}\partial^{\alpha+\beta}K(\omega-\omega^{\prime}).\end{split}

(7.1)

Let us move onto basic properties of
q
. For any
|\alpha|\leq 2
and bounded linear operator
B\colon L^{2}_{\nu}\to L^{2}_{\nu}
, since
\partial_{\omega}^{\alpha}\phi_{\omega}\in L^{2}_{\nu}
, we have

\partial_{\omega}^{\alpha}(B\phi_{\omega})=B(\partial_{\omega}^{\alpha}\phi_{\omega}).

Likewise, for any bounded linear functional
\ell\colon L^{2}_{\nu}\to\mathbb{C}
, we have

\partial_{\omega}^{\alpha}(\ell(\phi_{\omega}))=\ell(\partial_{\omega}^{\alpha}\phi_{\omega}).

This allows us to switch the order of derivatives in
\omega\in\Omega
and bounded operators on
L^{2}_{\nu}
, which we will use frequently without explicit mention, typically in the case where
B=P_{U}
,
B=P_{\widetilde{U}}
, or
\ell(\cdot)={\left\langle f,\cdot\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}
for some
f\in L^{2}_{\nu}
. In particular, this observation implies that for any closed subspace
W\subseteq L^{2}_{\nu}
, the MUSIC function
q_{W}
is at least twice differentiable. Some calculations yield

\displaystyle q_{W}(\omega)

\displaystyle=1-{\left\langle\phi_{\omega},P_{W}\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}},

(7.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{j}q_{W}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-2{\rm Re}{\left\langle\partial_{\omega_{j}}\phi_{\omega},P_{W}\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}},

(7.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{j}\partial_{k}q_{W}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-2{\rm Re}{\left\langle\partial_{\omega_{j}}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega},P_{W}\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}-2{\rm Re}{\left\langle\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega},P_{W}\partial_{\omega_{j}}\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}.

(7.4)

7.2
Geometric analysis of
q

The first step is to control the eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}q(\omega)
for
\omega
near
\vartheta
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose
1
holds. For any
\vartheta:=\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
,
\tau\leq\Delta(\vartheta)/2
, and
\omega\in\Omega
such that
{\rm dist}(\omega,\vartheta)\leq\tau
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}q(\omega)-2\Psi\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{2\tau\|\nabla K\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\tau})}\Delta^{2}K(0)}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(K_{\vartheta})}\left(\|\nabla K\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\tau})}^{2}+E_{1}(\vartheta)\right).

Proof.

Recall the shorthand notation
\Psi=-\nabla^{2}K(0)
, which is a real symmetric matrix. Starting with formula (
7.4
) and doing some further manipulations,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}q(\omega)&=-{\rm Re}{\left\langle\partial_{\omega_{j}}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega},\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}+{\rm Re}{\left\langle\partial_{\omega_{j}}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega},(I-P_{U})\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}-{\rm Re}{\left\langle\partial_{\omega_{j}}\phi_{\omega},P_{U}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}\\
&=\Psi_{j,k}+{\rm Re}{\left\langle\partial_{\omega_{j}}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega},(I-P_{U})\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}-{\rm Re}{\left\langle\partial_{\omega_{j}}\phi_{\omega},P_{U}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}.\end{split}

The first term is dominant, while the second and third terms are error quantities. Define the matrices
A(\omega)
and
B(\omega)
such that

\displaystyle A_{j,k}(\omega)

\displaystyle={\rm Re}{\left\langle\partial_{\omega_{j}}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega},(I-P_{U})\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}},

\displaystyle B_{j,k}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-{\rm Re}{\left\langle\partial_{\omega_{j}}\phi_{\omega},P_{U}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}.

Hence, we have derived the formula

\nabla^{2}q(\omega)=2\Psi+2A(\omega)+2B(\omega).

(7.5)

From here onward, fix any
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
and assume that
\omega\in B_{\tau}(\theta_{\ell})
.

For the first matrix
A(\omega)
, notice that
(I-P_{U})\phi_{\theta_{\ell}}=0
because
P_{U}\phi_{\theta_{\ell}}=\phi_{\theta_{\ell}}
. For each
j,k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
,

\displaystyle|A_{j,k}(\omega)|

\displaystyle=\left|{\rm Re}{\left\langle\partial_{\omega_{j}}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega},(I-P_{U})(\phi_{\omega}-\phi_{\theta_{\ell}})\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq{\left\|\partial_{\omega_{j}}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega}\right\|}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}{\left\|\phi_{\omega}-\phi_{\theta_{\ell}}\right\|}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}.

A calculation together with the mean value theorem and that
|\omega-\theta_{\ell}|\leq\tau
shows that

\displaystyle{\left\|\phi_{\omega}-\phi_{\theta_{\ell}}\right\|}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}^{2}

\displaystyle=2K(0)-2K(\omega-\theta_{\ell})\leq 2\tau\|\nabla K\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\tau})},

\displaystyle{\left\|\partial_{\omega_{j}}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega}\right\|}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\partial_{j}^{2}\partial_{k}^{2}K(0).

Using the above inequalities, we can conclude that

\|A(\omega)\|_{F}^{2}\leq 2\tau\|\nabla K\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\tau})}\sum_{j,k}\partial_{j}^{2}\partial_{k}^{2}K(0)=2\tau\|\nabla K\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\tau})}\Delta^{2}K(0).

(7.6)

For the second matrix
B(\omega)
, recall the synthesis operator
T_{\vartheta}
defined in (
3.11
). For convenience, let
T=T_{\vartheta}/\sqrt{\nu(X)}
be a normalized version and so

P_{U}=T(T^{*}T)^{-1}T^{*}
. A calculation yields

\displaystyle|B_{j,k}(\omega)|

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\left\langle\partial_{\omega_{j}}\phi_{\omega},T(T^{*}T)^{-1}T^{*}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\left\langle T^{*}\partial_{\omega_{j}}\phi_{\omega},(T^{*}T)^{-1}(T^{*}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega})\right\rangle}_{\mathbb{C}^{s}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq{\left\|(T^{*}T)^{-1}\right\|}|T^{*}\partial_{\omega_{j}}\phi_{\omega}||T^{*}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega}|.

Note that
T^{*}T=K_{\vartheta}
and
T^{*}\partial_{\omega_{j}}\phi_{\omega}=\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{j}K(\omega-\theta_{\ell})\end{bmatrix}_{\ell=1,\dots,s}
. Recall that
|\omega-\omega_{\ell}|\leq\tau
and the definition of
E_{1}(\vartheta)
defined in (
2.12
). Then,

\begin{split}\|B(\omega)\|_{F}&\leq{\left\|(T^{*}T)^{-1}\right\|}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|T^{*}\partial_{\omega_{j}}\phi_{\omega}|^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}|T^{*}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(K_{\vartheta})}\sum_{j=1}^{s}|\nabla K(\omega-\theta_{j})|^{2}.\end{split}

(7.7)

We next control the summation in (
7.7
) by breaking it into two terms. For all
j\neq\ell
, recalling that
|\omega-\theta_{\ell}|\leq\tau
, we have

|\omega-\theta_{j}|\geq|\theta_{j}-\theta_{\ell}|-|\omega-\theta_{\ell}|\geq\Delta-\tau\geq\Delta/2.

By definition of
E_{1}(\vartheta,\tau)
, this now implies

\sum_{j=1}^{s}|\nabla K(\omega-\theta_{j})|^{2}=|\nabla K(\omega-\theta_{\ell})|^{2}+\sum_{j\neq\ell}|\nabla K(\omega-\theta_{j})|^{2}\leq\|\nabla K\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\tau})}^{2}+E_{1}(\vartheta).

(7.8)

Combining (
7.5
), (
7.6
), (
7.7
), and (
7.8
) completes the proof.
∎

While the previous lemma controlled the behavior of
q
near
\vartheta
, we need to control
q
on the complement set.

Lemma 7.2
.

Suppose
1
holds. For any
\vartheta=\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}
,
\tau\leq\Delta(\vartheta)/2
, and
\omega\in\Omega
such that
{\rm dist}(\omega,\vartheta)\geq\tau
, we have

q(\omega)\geq 1-\|K\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\tau}^{c})}^{2}-E_{0}(\vartheta)-\lambda_{\max}(K_{\vartheta})\max\left\{\Big|\frac{1}{\lambda_{\max}(K_{\vartheta})}-1\Big|,\,\Big|\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(K_{\vartheta})}-1\Big|\right\}.

Proof.

Recall the synthesis operator
T_{\vartheta}
defined in (
3.11
), and for convenience, let
T=T_{\vartheta}/\sqrt{\nu(X)}
be a normalized version. Note that
P_{U}=T(T^{*}T)^{-1}T^{*}
and
T^{*}\phi_{\omega}=\begin{bmatrix}K(\omega-\theta_{\ell})\end{bmatrix}_{\ell=1,\dots,s}
. A calculation together with the definition of
E_{0}(\vartheta)
in (
2.11
) shows that

\begin{split}q(\omega)&=1-{\left\langle\phi_{\omega},P_{U}\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}\\
&=1-{\left\langle\phi_{\omega},TT^{*}\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}-{\left\langle\phi_{\omega},(P_{U}-TT^{*})\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}\\
&=1-{\left\langle T^{*}\phi_{\omega},T^{*}\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{\mathbb{C}^{s}}-{\left\langle T^{*}\phi_{\omega},\left((T^{*}T)^{-1}-I\right)T^{*}\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{\mathbb{C}^{s}}\\
&\geq 1-\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}|K(\omega-\theta_{\ell})|^{2}-{\left\|I-(T^{*}T)^{-1}\right\|}|T^{*}\phi_{\omega}|^{2}.\end{split}

It remains to control the right hand side term.

For the second term, note that
T^{*}T=K_{\vartheta}
. Then

\displaystyle|T^{*}\phi_{\omega}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\left\|T^{*}\right\|}^{2}{\left\|\phi_{\omega}\right\|}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}^{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}(K_{\vartheta}),

\displaystyle{\left\|I-(T^{*}T)^{-1}\right\|}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\Big|\frac{1}{\lambda_{\max}(K_{\vartheta})}-1\Big|,\,\Big|\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(K_{\vartheta})}-1\Big|\right\}.

For the summation, we first note that either there is a
\theta_{j}
such that
|\omega-\theta_{j}|<\Delta/2
or no such
\theta_{j}
exists. Suppose there is a
\theta_{j}
. Then it is unique because for all
\ell\neq j
, we have
|\omega-\theta_{\ell}|\geq|\theta_{j}-\theta_{\ell}|-|\omega-\theta_{j}|\geq\Delta/2
. Using the definition of
E_{0}(\vartheta)
and the assumption that
|\omega-\theta_{j}|\geq\tau
, we get

\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}|K(\omega-\theta_{\ell})|^{2}=|K(\omega-\theta_{j})|^{2}+\sum_{\ell\neq j}|K(\omega-\theta_{\ell})|^{2}\leq\|K\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\tau}^{c})}^{2}+E_{0}(\vartheta).

This settles the first case. In the second case where no
\theta_{j}
exists, then
{\rm dist}(\omega,\vartheta)\geq\Delta/2
and we immediately get

\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}|K(\omega-\theta_{\ell})|^{2}\leq E_{0}(\vartheta).

The upper bound for the first case is larger. Combining the above completes the proof.
∎

7.3
Geometric analysis of
\widetilde{q}

In the following, we develop Bernstein-type inequalities for the difference between two MUSIC functions and their derivatives.

Lemma 7.3
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds. For any finite dimensional subspaces
U,\widetilde{U}\subseteq L^{2}_{\nu}(X)
of equal dimension, their associated MUSIC functions
q=q_{U}
and
\widetilde{q}=q_{\widetilde{U}}
satisfy the following inequalities. For all
\omega\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle|q(\omega)-\widetilde{q}(\omega)|

\displaystyle\leq{\left\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\right\|},

(7.9)

\displaystyle|\nabla q(\omega)-\nabla\widetilde{q}(\omega)|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{{\rm tr}(\Psi)}\,\|P_{\widetilde{U}}-P_{U}\|,

(7.10)

\displaystyle{\left\|\nabla^{2}q(\omega)-\nabla^{2}\widetilde{q}(\omega)\right\|}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\Delta^{2}K(0)+(\Delta K(0))^{2}}{\left\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\right\|}.

(7.11)

Proof.

The first inequality of this lemma follows immediately from using (
7.2
) and

\displaystyle|q(\omega)-\widetilde{q}(\omega)|=\left|{\left\langle\phi_{\omega},\left(P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\right)\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}\right|\leq{\left\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\right\|}.

For the second inequality of this lemma, we use formulas (
7.3
) and (
7.1
) to get

\displaystyle|\nabla q(\omega)-\nabla\widetilde{q}(\omega)|

\displaystyle=\left|{\left\langle\nabla\phi_{\omega},(P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}})\phi_{\omega}\right\rangle}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}\right|\leq\|\nabla\phi_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}_{\nu}}\,{\left\|P_{\widetilde{U}}-P_{U}\right\|}=\sqrt{{\rm tr}(\Psi)}\,{\left\|P_{\widetilde{U}}-P_{U}\right\|}.

For the final inequality, we use formulas (
7.4
) and (
7.1
) to see that

\displaystyle|\partial_{j}\partial_{k}q(\omega)-\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\widetilde{q}(\omega)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4{\left\|\partial_{\omega_{j}}\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega}\right\|}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}^{2}{\left\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\right\|}^{2}+4{\left\|\partial_{\omega_{j}}\phi_{\omega}\right\|}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}^{2}{\left\|\partial_{\omega_{k}}\phi_{\omega}\right\|}_{L^{2}_{\nu}}^{2}{\left\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\right\|}^{2}

\displaystyle=4\left(\partial_{j}^{2}\partial_{k}^{2}K(0)+\,\partial_{j}^{2}K(0)\partial_{k}^{2}K(0)\right){\left\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\right\|}^{2}.

This now yields

\displaystyle{\left\|\nabla^{2}q(\omega)-\nabla^{2}\widetilde{q}(\omega)\right\|}_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\left(\Delta^{2}K(0)+(\Delta K(0))^{2}\right){\left\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\right\|}^{2}.

This completes the lemma.
∎

The following is an abstract lemma which shows that under an assumption on the Hessian of
q
near
\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}

\widetilde{q}
has critical points nearby the true ones.

Lemma 7.4
.

Suppose
1
holds and let
\vartheta=\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}\subseteq\Omega
. Assume that there are
\widetilde{U}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

\inf_{\omega\colon{\rm dist}(\omega,\vartheta)\leq\varepsilon}\,\lambda_{d}\left(\nabla^{2}q(\omega)\right)>\max\left\{\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\,\sqrt{{\rm tr}(\Psi)},\,2\sqrt{\Delta^{2}K(0)+(\Delta K(0))^{2}}\right\}{\left\|P_{U}-P_{\widetilde{U}}\right\|}.

(7.12)

Then there exist
\{\widetilde{\theta}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{s}\subseteq\Omega
such that
\widetilde{\theta}_{\ell}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\ell})
, it is a local minimum of
\widetilde{q}:=q_{\widetilde{U}}
, and it is the only critical point of
\widetilde{q}
in
B_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\ell})
.

Proof.

Let
q:=q_{U}
be the MUSIC function corresponding to the true subspace associated with
\vartheta
. Fix
\ell
, consider the ball
B_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\ell})
where
\varepsilon>0
will be chosen later, and let
\omega\in\partial B(\theta_{\ell},\varepsilon)
. By the Taylor remainder formula, there is a
\zeta_{\ell}\in B(\theta_{\ell},\varepsilon)
such that

\displaystyle 0=q(\theta_{\ell})=q(\omega)+\nabla q(\omega)\cdot(\theta_{\ell}-\omega)+\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{\ell}-\omega)\cdot\nabla^{2}q(\zeta_{\ell})\,(\theta_{\ell}-\omega).

Using this formula, we see that

\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{q}(\omega)\cdot(\omega-\theta_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\nabla q(\omega)\cdot(\omega-\theta_{\ell})+(\nabla\widetilde{q}(\omega)-\nabla q(\omega))\cdot(\omega-\theta_{\ell})

\displaystyle=q(\omega)+\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{\ell}-\omega)\cdot\nabla^{2}q(\zeta_{\ell})\,(\theta_{\ell}-\omega)+(\nabla\widetilde{q}(\omega)-\nabla q(\omega))\cdot(\omega-\theta_{\ell}).

We proceed to show that the right hand side is nonnegative for all
\omega\in\partial B_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\ell})
. To do this, we use that
q
is nonnegative and (
7.10
) to get

\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{q}(\omega)\cdot(\omega-\theta_{\ell})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{\ell}-\omega)\cdot\nabla^{2}q(\zeta_{\ell})\,(\theta_{\ell}-\omega)+(\nabla\widetilde{q}(\omega)-\nabla q(\omega))\cdot(\omega-\theta_{\ell})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\,\lambda_{d}\left(\nabla^{2}q(\zeta_{\ell})\right)|\theta_{\ell}-\omega|^{2}-|\nabla\widetilde{q}(\omega)-\nabla q(\omega)||\omega-\theta_{\ell}|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\,\lambda_{d}\left(\nabla^{2}q(\zeta_{\ell})\right)\,\varepsilon^{2}-\sqrt{{\rm tr}(\Psi)}\,\|P_{\widetilde{U}}-P_{U}\|\,\varepsilon.

Since
\zeta_{\ell}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\ell})
, we use assumption (
7.12
) and the previous displayed inequality to see that

\nabla\widetilde{q}(\omega)\cdot(\omega-\theta_{\ell})\geq 0.

This inequality holds for all
\omega\in\partial B_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\ell})
, so by
[
30
, Theorem 1]
, there is a
\widetilde{\theta}_{\ell}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\ell})
such that
\nabla\widetilde{q}(\widetilde{\theta}_{\ell})=0
. This proves the existence of

8.1
Computational complexity

Concrete parameter choices for Gradient-MUSIC are given in
Theorems

4.1
and
5.1
. Among these, the most significant from a computational point of view are the mesh norm
{\rm mesh}(G)
of the initialization grid and the number of gradient iterations
n
.

Suppose first that
G
is a uniform grid satisfying

{\rm mesh}(G)\asymp_{d}m^{-1}.

Then
G
has cardinality
O_{d}(m^{d})
. Since the MUSIC function
\widetilde{q}
can be evaluated on such a grid using the fast Fourier transform (FFT), the cost of the initialization step is

O_{d}\bigl(s\,m^{d}\log m\bigr).

Once suitable representatives have been selected from the thresholded clusters, the local refinement stage is comparatively inexpensive. Indeed, gradient descent requires only
O(\log(1/\varepsilon))
iterations per cluster in order to achieve numerical accuracy
\varepsilon/m
. Moreover, these
s
local descents are independent and can therefore be performed in parallel. The resulting cost of the refinement stage is

O_{d}\bigl(s\,m^{d}\log(1/\varepsilon)\bigr).

Thus the overall complexity of Gradient-MUSIC scales essentially like

O_{d}\bigl(s\,m^{d}(\log m+\log(1/\varepsilon))\bigr),

up to constants depending only on the dimension.

It is instructive to compare this with the complexity of classical MUSIC. Since classical MUSIC proceeds by brute-force search over the parameter domain, achieving numerical accuracy
\varepsilon/m
requires evaluating
\widetilde{q}
on a grid
G
with

{\rm mesh}(G)\leq\varepsilon/m.

If one again uses a uniform grid, then
G
has cardinality

O_{d}(m^{d}\varepsilon^{-d}),

and the corresponding FFT-based evaluation cost is

O_{d}\bigl(m^{d}\varepsilon^{-d}\log(m/\varepsilon)\bigr).

This is substantially worse than the cost of Gradient-MUSIC, especially in high dimension. The key point is that, for Gradient-MUSIC, the coarse initialization grid is tied only to the aperture scale
m^{-1}
and not to the final numerical accuracy. The refinement to accuracy
\varepsilon/m
is then achieved by local descent rather than by global grid refinement. Consequently, the computational gap between Gradient-MUSIC and classical MUSIC widens rapidly as the ambient dimension
d
increases.

9
Conclusion and discussion

The present work has been concerned with a basic question in super-resolution: how much of the measurement data is truly essential for stable and computable parameter recovery, and how should one formulate an optimization principle that uses precisely this information and no more. Our answer is that the decisive object is the signal subspace. In the noiseless setting, the data lie in the span of the steering vectors associated with the true configuration; in the noisy setting, this subspace is perturbed, but it remains the central geometric carrier of information. The MUSIC functional is therefore not introduced here merely as a convenient algorithmic device. Rather, it appears as the most economical nonconvex functional that directly encodes the subspace structure relevant to the inverse problem.

From this point of view, the main theorem,
Theorem

3.2
, should be understood as a structural result about the geometry of reconstruction. It identifies, in an abstract and model-independent way, how perturbation of the signal subspace by deterministic, including adversarial, noise translates into deformation of the MUSIC landscape. At the same time, the theorem is constructive in a strong sense. Through the notion of an
admissible landscape
, it yields a global geometric criterion under which a simple computational procedure—multiple initialization followed by gradient descent—provably succeeds. Thus the theorem does not merely assert that the desired parameters remain identifiable under noise; it explains why they remain computationally accessible.

In this respect, the present analysis sits at the intersection of several traditions. Classical resolution theory, beginning with optical and spectral considerations, is primarily concerned with the distinguishability of nearby objects and often formulates this question through aperture heuristics or peak-width criteria. The inverse problems literature emphasizes stability under perturbation, while the modern optimization literature focuses on the tractability of nonconvex objectives. One of the purposes of this paper has been to show that, in the super-resolution setting, these three viewpoints are not independent. The geometry of the subspace determines the geometry of the optimization landscape, and this geometry in turn governs both stability and algorithmic recovery.

A traditional heuristic expresses the resolution scale
\rho
in the form

\rho:=\hbox{Resolution Scale}\sim\big(\hbox{Aperture Size}\big)^{-1}

(9.1)

where the aperture size is determined by the geometry of the sampling set and is, roughly speaking, proportional to its diameter.
This principle is certainly not without content, and indeed part of our analysis recovers it. In the framework of
Theorem

3.2
, the relevant local scale is governed by the parameter
\tau
, and the reciprocal-aperture law corresponds essentially to the behavior of the local quantity
\|\nabla K\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\tau})}
, which typically grows in proportion to the diameter of the sampling set. In that sense, the classical heuristic captures the first-order local mechanism by which increased sampling extent improves resolution.

What it does not capture, however, is that super-resolution is not determined solely by local geometry. The admissibility conditions involve, in addition, the quantities
E_{0}(\vartheta)
and
E_{1}(\vartheta)
associated with the kernel matrix
K_{\vartheta}
, and these depend on the global arrangement of the object configuration. They measure, in effect, how the configuration interacts with itself through the sampling geometry, and hence they encode collective interference phenomena that are invisible to any purely local aperture principle. This distinction is particularly transparent in the case of sampling on a circle of radius
m
in two dimensions, where

K(\xi)=J_{0}(2\pi m|\xi|)

and

\nabla K(\xi)=-2\pi mJ_{1}(2\pi m|\xi|)\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\sim m\qquad\hbox{for sufficiently small }|\xi|.

Here the local scaling agrees with the reciprocal-diameter picture. But since

J_{0}(z),\,J_{1}(z)\sim z^{-1/2},\qquad z\gg 1,

neither function is square integrable on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and consequently the global quantities
E_{0}(\vartheta)
and
E_{1}(\vartheta)
remain sensitive to the overall spatial spread of the configuration. Widely separated objects continue to interact through the slow decay of the kernel. Thus sparse sampling on a curve exhibits a genuinely global interference effect that is absent from the standard heuristic. By contrast, when the sampling set is filled in, as for the full disk, these effects become controllable; see Example 2 and
Theorem

5.1
. In this way, the present theory may be viewed as a refinement of the classical aperture principle: local resolution and global interference are distinct mechanisms, and both must be accounted for in a satisfactory theory.

The resulting picture is by now quite clear. For sampling sets given by a discrete cube or a continuous ball,
Theorem

4.1
and
Theorem

5.1
show informally that Gradient-MUSIC obeys the bound

\hbox{(Location) Error}\lesssim\hbox{Resolution Scale}\times\hbox{Noise Level}

(9.2)

under adversarial noise, where the noise level is measured in the sup norm over the sampling set. This is the natural worst-case law. Under Gaussian stationary noise, however, the estimate improves to

\hbox{(Location) Error}\lesssim\big(\hbox{Resolution Scale}\big)^{1+d/2}\times\hbox{Noise Level},

(9.3)

up to a logarithmic factor.

Equally important is the fact that these results are uniform and nonasymptotic. They do not rely on a perturbative regime tied to a special configuration, nor do they require asymptotic separation assumptions depending on the particular arrangement of objects. Rather, they apply whenever the separation is at least a sufficiently large constant multiple of resolution scale, independently of the detailed geometry of the configuration. This uniformity is one of the main reasons to regard the theory as a candidate for a genuine minimax description of super-resolution. Indeed, the results strongly suggest that Gradient-MUSIC attains the optimal minimax scaling law in multiple dimensions, and this has recently been proved in the one-dimensional case
[
14
]
.

It is worth emphasizing that the present paper has not pursued quantitative sharpness of constants. This is a deliberate choice. In our earlier one-dimensional work
[
14
]
, a substantially more delicate geometric analysis yielded explicit constants, most notably the constant
4
in the separation condition
\Delta(\vartheta)\geq 4/m
. Such refinements are important when one seeks near-optimal thresholds, but they require technical estimates whose role is primarily quantitative rather than structural. Here we have preferred to isolate the general mechanism: the passage from subspace perturbation to global landscape geometry, and from there to stability and computability. To pursue sharp constants in the full multidimensional setting would be an interesting problem in its own right, but it would belong to a different level of analysis.

There are several directions in which the present framework should develop further. One is to extend the class of admissible sampling geometries beyond the cube and the ball, and to understand more systematically which geometric features of the sampling domain suppress or amplify long-range interference. Another is to sharpen the connection with minimax lower bounds in higher dimensions, thereby completing the optimality theory suggested here. A third is to bring the present structural viewpoint into closer contact with related subspace methods, such as ESPRIT and matrix-pencil type algorithms, for which one may hope to formulate analogous landscape principles. More broadly, it would be of interest to understand whether the notion of admissibility has a counterpart in other inverse problems where the physically meaningful information is carried by a low-dimensional subspace but the natural reconstruction principles are nonconvex.

Lemma B.2

Proof of

The proof hinges on a connection between the extreme singular values of
T_{\vartheta}
and special functions. Suppose for some
\beta>0
and all
m>0
, there exist functions
\varphi
and
\psi
that are continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, zero outside

for almost all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The functions
\widehat{\psi}
and
\widehat{\varphi}
are called minorants and majorants of

, respectively.

The existence of appropriate minorants and majorants were established, such as
[
46
]
for

and
[
19

for

(with parameter choices

,

in the reference’s notation). There is an absolute constant
C>0
and function
\varepsilon_{0}
such that
\varepsilon_{0}(\beta)=O(1/\beta)

and if
\beta\geq Cd
, then there are radial
\psi
and
\varphi
such that
\widehat{\psi}(\omega)\leq\mathbbm{1}_{B_{m}}(\omega)\leq\widehat{\varphi}(\omega)
for almost all

While

]
provides a complicated explicit formula for

, it may be more convenient to use the asymptotic bound

. Reference
[

shows that if

such that

and

, then

To complete the proof, we use that

C.3
Preparatory lemma used in proof of
Lemma

Lemma C.1
.

For any
m>0
,
\gamma\geq 1
and
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\Delta(\Gamma)\geq\beta/m
and
\Gamma\cap(B_{\beta/m})^{\circ}=\emptyset
, we have

\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}|W_{m}(\gamma)|^{2}\leq\frac{d\,2^{d+1}}{\pi^{2}|B_{1}|^{2}\beta^{d+1}}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}|\nabla W_{m}(\gamma)|^{2}\leq\frac{d\,2^{d+1}4\pi^{2}m^{2}}{|B_{1}|^{2}\beta^{d+1}}.

Proof.

We first concentrate on the estimate for
|W_{m}(\gamma)|^{2}
. Recall the pointwise inequality,
|J_{\alpha}(t)|\leq\sqrt{2/(\pi t)}
for all
t>0
and
\alpha>0
. Together with the explicit formula for
W_{m}
in (
5.1
), we get the pointwise bound

|W_{m}(\xi)|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\pi^{2}|B_{1}|^{2}}\frac{1}{(m|\xi|)^{d+1}}.

For convenience, let
C_{d}:=1/(\pi^{2}|B_{1}|^{2})
.
A calculation shows that for any
\xi\neq 0
,

\Delta_{\xi}\left(\frac{1}{|\xi|^{d+1}}\right)=\frac{(d+1)(d+2)}{|\xi|^{d+3}}-\frac{2}{|\xi|}\frac{d+1}{|\xi|^{d+2}}=\frac{d(d+1)}{|\xi|^{d+3}}>0.

This proves that
\xi\mapsto 1/|\xi|^{d+1}
is sub-harmonic on any ball that does not intersect the origin. In view of the assumptions that
\Delta(\Gamma)\geq\beta/m
and
\Gamma\cap(B_{\beta/m})^{\circ}=\emptyset
, for each
\gamma\in\Gamma
, the ball
B_{\beta/(2m)}(\gamma)
does not contain the origin. This, along with recalling the mean value property of sub-harmonic functions, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}|W_{m}(\gamma)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{d}}{m^{d+1}}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\frac{1}{|\gamma|^{d+1}}\leq\frac{C_{d}}{m^{d+1}}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\frac{1}{|B_{\beta/(2m)}|}\int_{B_{\beta/(2m)}(\gamma)}\frac{1}{|\xi|^{d+1}}\,d\xi.

Since
\Delta(\Gamma)\geq\beta/m
, we see that
\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}B_{\beta/(2m)}(\gamma)
is a union of balls that overlap on a set of measure zero and is contained in the closure of
(B_{\beta/(2m)})^{c}
. Then continuing from the prior calculation,

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}|W_{m}(\gamma)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{d}}{m^{d+1}}\frac{1}{|B_{\beta/(2m)}|}\int_{(B_{\beta/(2m)})^{c}}\frac{1}{|\xi|^{d+1}}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{m^{d+1}}\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|}{|B_{\beta/(2m)}|}\int_{\beta/(2m)}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\,dr=\frac{C_{d}d}{m^{d+1}}\left(\frac{2m}{\beta}\right)^{d+1}=\frac{d\,2^{d+1}}{\pi^{2}|B_{1}|^{2}\beta^{d+1}}.

This completes the first part of the proof.

For the second estimate for
|\nabla W_{m}|^{2}
, recall the explicit formula for
\nabla W_{m}
in (
C.11
). Using the pointwise inequality,
|J_{\alpha}(t)|\leq\sqrt{2/(\pi t)}
, we obtain the upper bound

|\nabla W_{m}(\xi)|^{2}\leq\frac{4m^{2}}{|B_{1}|^{2}}\frac{1}{(m|\xi|)^{d+1}}.

Since these upper bounds for
|W_{m}|^{2}
and

are identical up a constant factor, repeating the same argument completes the proof. ∎

C.4
Proof of
Lemma

5.2

Proof.

For convenience, define
\eta_{m}:=\mathbbm{1}_{B_{2m}}\eta\in L^{2}(Q_{2m})
, which we treat as a periodic function. For any
f\in L^{2}(B_{m})
, we let
f
also denote its zero extension to
Q_{2m}
and then periodically extended to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Recall the reflection map
R
. Letting
*
be the convolution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}/Q_{2m}
, for each

,

(H(\eta)f)(x)=\int_{B_{m}}\eta(x+u)f(u)\,du=(\eta_{m}*Rf)(x).

By various algebraic properties of the Fourier transform, Plancherel’s, and Hölder’s,

\|H(\eta)f\|_{L^{2}(B_{m})}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|Q_{2m}|}}\,\big\|\widehat{\eta_{m}}\,\widehat{Rf}\,\big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\leq\|\widehat{\eta_{m}}\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\|f\|_{L^{2}(B_{m})}.

(C.12)

Thus, it suffices to bound
\|\widehat{\eta_{m}}\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}
.

To accomplish this, we will control the variance of
\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k)
and use a union bound. For convenience, define
\sigma_{m}:=\sigma\chi_{m}
, where recall that
\chi_{m}
is any function such that
\chi_{m}=1
on
B_{2m}
. Recall
Definition

5.1
and a calculation yields

\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta_{m}(x)e^{-2\pi ik\cdot x/(4m)}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma(t)\,\widehat{\sigma_{m}}\left(\tfrac{k}{4m}-t\right)\,dW(t).

The switch of integrals is justified since
\gamma\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and its support is contained in a compact set implies
\gamma\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, while
\sigma\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
implies
\sigma_{m}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This shows that
\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k)
for any

is a mean zero normal random variable and its variance is, by Ito isometry,

{\rm var}(\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k))=\mathbb{E}|\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k)|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\gamma(t)\,\widehat{\sigma_{m}}\left(\tfrac{k}{4m}-t\right)\right|^{2}\,dt.

(C.13)

Pick a sufficiently large constant
C_{d}
that depends only on dimension such that
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}C_{d}(1+|k|)^{-(d+1)}\leq 1
. Let

be arbitrary and define

v_{k}^{2}:={\rm var}(\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k))\log\left(\frac{(1+|k|)^{d+1}}{C_{d}\,\delta}\right).

By the union bound, definition of

, and tail bound for Gaussian random variables, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\text{ s.t. }|\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k)|\geq v_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\left(|\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k)|\geq v_{k}\right)\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\exp\left(-\frac{v_{k}^{2}}{{\rm var}(\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k))}\right)\leq\delta.

This implies with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
|\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k)|\leq v_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and so

\big\|\widehat{\eta_{m}}\big\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}^{2}\leq\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}v_{k}^{2}\lesssim_{d}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{\rm var}(\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k))\big(\log(1/\delta)+\log(1+|k|)\big).

In view of this inequality, to finish the proof of the first statement of this lemma, it suffices to prove that

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{\rm var}(\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k))\log(1+|k|)\lesssim_{d,\gamma}\|\sigma_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\log(m).

(C.14)

Since
\gamma
is assumed to be compactly supported, let
R\geq 1
be big enough so that the support of
\gamma
is contained in
B_{R}
. If
|k|\leq CRm
for any absolute
C
, then using (
C.13
), we have

{\rm var}(\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k))\log(1+|k|)\leq\|\gamma\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\|\widehat{\sigma_{m}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\log(1+CRm)\lesssim_{\gamma}\|\sigma_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\log(m).

From here onward, assume that
|k|\geq CRm
. Then for all

, we have
m|\frac{k}{4m}-t|\geq\frac{1}{4}|k|-mR\gtrsim_{R}|k|
. Using that the support of
\gamma

C.13
) and assumption (

\begin{split}&{\rm var}(\widehat{\eta_{m}}(k))\log(1+|k|)\\
&\quad\leq\|\gamma\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\widehat{\sigma_{m}}\left(\tfrac{k}{4m}-t\right)\right|^{2}\log(1+m|\tfrac{k}{4m}-t|)\,dt\\
&\quad\lesssim_{R}\|\gamma\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\widehat{\sigma_{m}}{\left(\tfrac{k}{4m}-t\right)}\right|^{2}\log(m)\,dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\widehat{\sigma_{m}}\left(\tfrac{k}{4m}-t\right)\right|^{2}\log{\left(1+\left|\tfrac{k}{4m}-t\right|\right)}\,dt\\

&\quad\lesssim_{d}\|\gamma\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\|\sigma_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\log(m).\end{split}

(C.15)

This proves (

For the second part of this lemma, we verify that the constant function
\sigma(x)=\sigma
satisfies condition (

to be the indicator function of
