Carbothermal regeneration of LiFePO4/C cathode material by recycling the precursors from out-of-line LiFePO4 cells
Authors: A.R. Abbasi, S.M. Masoudpanah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn this work, the LiFePO4/C (LFP/C) cathode material was regenerated by carbothermal processing using Li2CO3, Li3PO4, and FePO4 precursors recycled from out-of-line (substandard) LFP cells. The separated black mass was converted into the various metal precursors, Li2CO3, Li3PO4, and FePO4. The LFP/C powders were then regenerated by the carbothermal reaction between the metal precursors in various molar ratios of lithium precursor to FePO4 (1:1, 1.05:1, and 1.10:1) and sucrose as a carbon source. The LFP/C powders regenerated from Li2CO3 and FePO4 precursors at a molar ratio of 1:1 exhibited long-term cycling performance, with 90% capacity retention after 2,000 charge/discharge cycles at a current density of 20C.

Introduction

The demand for lithium-ion technology in electric vehicles (EVs) and energy storage systems has increased significantly over the past decade due to the high specific energy density and long cycling life.1,2 An essential component of lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) is the cathode electrode, which is based on various materials, such as LiCoO2, LiNi0.6Co0.2Mn0.2O2, and LiFePO4 (LFP).3,4 The olivine-structured LFP material is the most common cathode for EVs, due to its low cost, relatively low toxicity, reliability, and long cycling life.5,6,7

There is also rapid growth in spent LIBs, which must be recycled due to their valuable materials, such as lithium, nickel, cobalt, manganese, aluminum, copper, and graphite, and to address environmental issues.8,9,10,11,12 Sorting, stabilization, dismantling, separation, and recovery are the five key steps in recovering valuable materials from LIBs.13,14 The recovery process is traditionally based on pyrometallurgical and hydrometallurgical treatments.15,16,17,18 Furthermore, a new approach to direct recycling is proposed in which the cathode material is regenerated from the recovered precursors without separating the individual elements.19,20 The simplicity, flexibility, low cost, and high time- and energy efficiency are the advantages of the direct regeneration approach.21 For example, Huang et al.22 used a direct regeneration approach to repair spent LFP materials using residual conductive carbon and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) as reductive agents. The regenerated LFP/C cathode materials showed a comparable specific capacity of 113.3 mAh g−1 at a current rate of 0.1C. Wu et al.23 selectively extracted lithium from spent LFP cells and regenerated LFP powders from FePO4 and Li2CO3 using a mechanochemical method.

Among various synthesis methods, the carbothermal method has attracted significant attention for the regeneration of LFP powders. The carbothermal reaction is a high-temperature process in which a carbon source is used for reducing the metallic oxides.24,25 The carbothermal treatment for synthesizing the LFP/C material is finely tuned not only to reduce iron but also to simultaneously create a highly desirable carbon coating on the LFP particles, all in one high-temperature step. The carbothermal reaction for preparation the LFP/C using an inexpensive and chemically stable Fe2O3 precursor is as follows26:(Equation 1)Li2CO3+Fe2O3+2NH4H2PO4+0.5C→2LiFePO4+2NH3+3H2O+1.5CO2.

The carbon sources, such as glucose, sucrose, citric acid, and phenolic resins are oxidized into CO or CO2, reducing Fe3+ into Fe2+ during high-temperature calcination which facilitates the crystallization of the LFP phase.27 The application of the low-cost Fe3+ precursors and deposition of a conductive carbon network on the LFP particles are major advantages of the carbothermal synthesis method.28

In the carbothermal regeneration, the separated black mass is first converted to lithium and iron salts by acid leaching.29 Then the stoichiometric ratios of the proper precursors are mixed with sucrose or glucose as a carbon source. The mixture is calcined at high temperatures under an inert atmosphere, such as N2 or Ar, to crystallize the LFP/C particles.30,31,32 The type and amount of metal precursors play a significant role in determining the electrochemical properties of the carbothermally regenerated LFP/C powders, as they control the crystallinity and microstructure. Herein, the different precursors, such as Li2CO3, Li3PO4, and FePO4, were obtained from the separated black mass via a chemical process. The well-characterized precursors were used in the carbothermal method to regenerate the LFP/C particles. The procedure was previously reported for recycling the spent LiFePO4 cells, but there was no comparison of the type of lithium precursors and ratio of metal precursors on the electrochemical properties of the LFP/C powders. In other words, our work showed a practical strategy for recycling the spent LiFePO4 cells to regenerate the LFP/C cathode material.

Carbothermal regeneration

Figure 3 shows the XRD patterns of the regenerated LFP/C powders from the various amounts of the Li3PO4 and Li2CO3 precursors. The reflections are well matched to the olivine crystal structure of LFP phase, with a space group of Pnma (PDF#96-110-1112), for the lithium phosphate precursor at all molar ratios. However, the Li2CO3 precursor leads to the impurity Fe(0) phase (PDF2#00-006-0696) at a 1:1 ratio (1C) (Table 2). The LFP phase was crystallized by solid-state reaction between the Li2CO3 and Li3PO4 precursors and FePO4 as follows22,29:(Equation 5)Li3PO4+3FePO4→3LiFePO4+PO4,(Equation 6)Li2CO3+2FePO4+2LiFePO4+CO2+0.5O2.In fact, the Fe3+ species in FePO4 were first reduced to Fe2+ species during the carbothermal treatment using sucrose as a carbon source under Ar/H2 atmosphere.34 Simultaneously, the Li precursors are thermally decomposed and react with the Fe2+ and PO43− species to crystallize the LFP phase. The formation of the impure Fe(0) phase in the 1C sample can be attributed to the high reductive atmosphere generated during the carbothermal treatment. However, the further liberation of CO2 decreased the reduction capability of atmosphere at the higher ratio of Li2CO3 precursor. The absence of Fe(0) in the P samples is related to the role of metal precursor on the decomposition rate of carbon source.35,36 The weak interaction of carbonate precursor is a responsible for the development of the reductive atmosphere.35 Furthermore, the molar ratio of lithium precursors plays a crucial role in determining the crystal arrangement by controlling the diffusion pathway for Li+ ions during crystallization of the olivine-structured LFP phase.37 The crystal quality of the LFP phase was usually evaluated by the ratio of various diffraction peaks, (101)/(311), (111)/(311), and (020)/(311), as a criterion of the crystal defects.38 The well-crystalline LFP material shows a higher (111)/(311) ratio because the crystal defects, such as antisite, interstitial, and vacancy defects, increase the intensity of (020) peaks.39 The 1.1P and 1.1C samples exhibit a higher ratio of (111)/(311) peaks, indicating higher crystallinity of the LFP phase due to its facile crystallization at higher Li precursor concentrations. Raman spectroscopy was performed to evaluate the degree of graphitization of the carbon layer on the LFP particles. The Raman spectra can be deconvoluted into four peaks using the Lorentzian function. The D1 and G bands are associated with the disordered (A1g symmetry) and ordered (E2g symmetry) graphite carbon layers, respectively. The amorphous carbon moieties such as organic molecules and functional groups and stretching vibrations of C=C/C–C result in the D3 and D4 bands, respectively. There are G peaks at 1,579 and 1,577 cm−1 and the D1 peaks at 1,329 and 1,324 cm−1 for the 1.1P and 1.1C samples, respectively (Figure 6). The calculated IG/ID1 ratios are 1.31 and 1.25 for the 1.1C and 1.1P samples, respectively, indicating a higher degree of structural order in the carbon coating of the 1.1C sample. The carbon layer was deposited on the LFP particles via the thermal decomposition of sucrose during calcination. The lower agglomeration of LFP particles in the carbonate-assisted samples promotes uniform coatings, as the particle surface acts as a catalyst for carbon deposition.42 The enhanced degree of graphitization suggests improved electrical conductivity, positively influencing the material’s electrochemical performance. It was worth noting that the 1.1C and 1.1P samples have similar carbon contents of 7.1 and 8.5 wt. %, respectively, according to the carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, and sulfur (CHNS) analysis.Figure 6Raman spectra(A) 1.1P and (B) 1.1C samples.

Figures 7A and 7B show the rate capability and cycling performance of the powders prepared using the Li2CO3 and Li3PO4 precursors. The specific capacity increased with the increase in the amount of Li precursor, irrespective of the precursor type. Furthermore, the carbonate-assisted LFP/C powders exhibit a higher specific capacity than the Li3PO4 precursor at all current rates. The higher specific capacity of the LFP/C powders prepared using the Li2CO3 precursor can be attributed to their higher crystallinity, which facilitates the Li+ de/intercalation process. The specific capacity of the 1.1P sample decreases from 93 to 18 mAh g−1 with an increase in current rate from 0.1C to 20C, corresponding to a rate capability of 22%. However, the 1.1C sample shows specific capacities of 135, 127, 119, 93, 73, 58, 49, and 39 mAh g−1 for 0.1C, 0.2C, 0.5C, 1C, 2C, 5C, 10C, and 20C, respectively, indicating a rate capability of 28%. The higher rate capability of the 1.1C sample can be attributed to its LFP/C nanoparticles and higher degree of graphitization of the carbon layer. Furthermore, the specific capacity was reversibly returned to 135 mAh g−1 by decreasing the current rate to 0.1C. The galvanostatic charge and discharge (GCD) curves of the 1.1C sample at various current rates are shown in Figure 7C. The specific capacity of LFP/C material is strongly related to the many factors, such as crystallinity, particle size and morphology, carbon coating, electronic conductivity, ion diffusivity, electrode formulation, and electrolyte compatibility.28,43,44 The smaller particles with uniform size distribution shorten lithium-ion diffusion paths and avoided the inactive dead zone, improving rate capability and accessible capacity.45 Poor electronic conductivity limits active material utilization, leading to the lower specific capacity.28 The crystal defects, such as oxygen vacancies, antisite defects (Fe on Li sites), and Fe3+ impurity can block the fast diffusion paths of <010> directions, leading to the capacity fade.45 Excess carbon reduces gravimetric capacity by adding inactive mass.46 The difference in the electrochemical properties of our synthesized samples can be attributed to their crystallinity, particle size, and quality of carbon layer, as characterized by XRD, SEM, and Raman spectroscopy results.Figure 7Rate capability of LFP/C powders prepared by(A) Li3PO4 and (B) Li2CO3, and (C) charge/discharge curves of the 1.1C sample at various current rates, and cycling performance of LFP/C powders prepared by (D) Li3PO4 and (E) Li2CO3 at 20C, and (F) charge/discharge curves of the 1.1C sample at various cycles at 20C.

Charge/discharge plateaus are observed at approximately 3.4 V vs. Li/Li+, with shrinking the plateau as the current rate increases, which is attributed to electrode polarization. The voltage plateaus are due to the Li de/insertion from the olivine crystal structure of the LFP material, accompanied by the following phase transition47,48:(Equation 7)LiFePO4↔FePO4+Li++e−.

Figures 7D and 7E show the long-term cycling performance of the 1.1P and 1.1C samples at a current rate of 20C for 2,000 charge/discharge cycles. The phosphate-assisted LFP/C powders exhibit a relatively similar capacity retention of ∼72%, irrespective of the amount of Li3PO4 precursor. However, the values of capacity retention are 90%, 82%, and 55% for the 1C, 1.05C, and 1.1C samples, respectively. The improved cycling performance of the 1C and 1.05C samples can be attributed to their smaller particle size and higher crystallinity, which provide a greater number of electroactive sites. Figure 7F presents the charge/discharge curves of the 1.1C sample at various cycles. The clear plateaus at high current rates confirm the high stability of structural and microstructural features of the regenerated LFP/C powders. Furthermore, the coulombic efficiency remains stable at 95.7% for various cycles, indicating the highly reversible redox reaction for storage Li ions.

The reversibility of the electrochemical oxidation and reduction reactions is evaluated using a cyclic voltammetry (CV) test. Figures 8A and 8B compare the CV curves of the P and C samples at a scan rate of 0.2 mV s−1. The anodic peak at ∼3.54 V vs. Li/Li+ and cathodic peak at ∼3.33 V vs. Li/Li+ are due to the Li+ ion deintercalation and intercalation, respectively.49,50 With the increase in lithium contents, the redox peaks became sharp and intense, indicating the presence of a crystal structure with high crystallinity. Furthermore, the potential differences between the anodic and cathodic peaks are 208 and 218 mV at a scan rate of 0.2 mV s−1 for the 1.1C and 1.1P samples, respectively (Figure 8C), indicating lower electrode polarization due to the higher quality of the carbon layer on the LFP particles. Also, the 1.1C electrode shows a higher current response at scan rates of 0.2 and 0.5 mV s−1, confirming its higher specific capacity. The CV curves show a stable shape at high scan rates, suggesting high reversibility (Figure 8D).Figure 8Electrochemical propertiesCV curves of (A) P and (B) C samples at 0.2 mV s−1 and (C) comparison of CV curves of the 1.1C and 1.1P samples at 0.2 mV s−1 and (D) the CV curves of the 1.1C samples at scan rates of 0.2 and 0.5 mV s−1.

To compare electrode kinetics, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed. Figure 9 shows the Nyquist plots and the equivalent circuit. The Nyquist plots are modeled using an equivalent circuit comprising the ohmic resistance (Rs), charge transfer resistance (Rct), constant phase element (CPE), and Warburg impedance (Zw) elements. The charge transfer resistance results in a semicircle in the mid-frequency range. The Warburg impedance, arising from Li+ ion diffusion within the LFP structure, appears as an inclined line in the low-frequency region.51,52 The fitted resistance values are presented in Table 4. The carbonate-assisted LFP/C samples exhibit lower Rct values than those of the Li3PO4 precursor due to their higher crystallinity, which induces higher electrical conductivity. Furthermore, the Rct values decrease with increasing the amount of lithium precursor. The carbon layer on the LFP particles in the 1.1C sample exhibits greater graphitization, thereby promoting electrical conductivity. The diffusion coefficient (D) of Li+ ion can be calculated by the Warburg factor (σ) as the linear slope of Z′ vs. ω−1/2 plot as follows53,54:(Equation 8)D=2R2T2A2n4F4C2σ2,which R and F are constant parameters, T is the temperature, A is the effective area of the electrode, Li+ concentration is shown by C, and n is the number of electrons exchanged. The C electrodes show a lower σ value than the P sample at all lithium ratios. Furthermore, the σ value decreases when a further lithium precursor is used. Accordingly, the C samples have the higher diffusion coefficients than those of the P samples (Table 5). Electrochemical performance of the regenerated LFP/C powders via the carbothermal method

The electrochemical performance of the regenerated LFP cathode materials, obtained via the carbothermal method, is compared with other works in Table 5. The carbothermal-regenerated LFP/C powders prepared from the Li2CO3 precursor exhibited comparable electrochemical properties owing to their high crystallinity. Furthermore, their smaller particle size benefits the quality of the carbon layer on the LFP particles. Higher amounts of lithium precursors improve electrochemical performance by controlling the crystal structure, microstructure, and quality of the carbon layer.

Finally, the FePO4, Li2CO3, and Li3PO4 precursors were recovered from the black mass separated from the out-of-line (substandard) LiFePO4 cells. The precursors were mechanically mixed at various molar ratios and were calcined at high temperature to regenerate the LFP/C powders. With increasing lithium precursor concentration, the structural, microstructural, and electrochemical properties improved by promoting crystallinity, reducing particle size, and enhancing the quality of the carbon layer. The different morphologies of LFP/C powders, as compared to their precursors, indicated thermal decomposition of metal precursors during carbothermal treatment. A combination of Li2CO3 and FePO4 precursors at a molar ratio of 1:1 yielded high-performance LFP/C powders with a high capacity retention of 90% after 2,000 charge and discharge cycles at 20C.

Limitations of the study

This study has two main limitations. First, access to high-resolution transmission electron microscop (TEM) imaging was limited; such data could directly visualize LiFePO4 and carbon interfaces and atomic-scale defects, further validating the proposed mechanisms. Second, there was limited access to experimental facilities for post-cycling characterizations.

Method details

The out-of-line cylindrical LFP 33140 cells were sourced from uninterruptible power supply (UPS) systems. The LFP 33140 cells were substandard cells, provided by the Saba battery company. These cells failed during quality control after 10 cycles of charging/discharging at a current rate of 1C. The Na3PO4, Na2CO3, NaCl, H2O2, and H2SO4 were purchased from Merck.

Recycling of precursor compounds

The pre-treatment, recovery, and regeneration procedures of the LFP/C powders are shown in Scheme 1. The cells were discharged with a 10% NaCl solution, manually disassembled with a saw, and their constituent parts were separated. The black mass was obtained from the aluminum current collector by burning the PVDF binder at 500 °C for 90 min with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 under an Ar/10 Vol. %H2 atmosphere with a flowing rate of 2 cc min-1.60 The black mass was leached using 0.3 M H2SO4 and H2O2 as oxidants at 70 °C for 2 h.33 The lithium was selectively leached, and iron was precipitated as FePO4 by adjusting pH to 3.7 using NaOH agent. Subsequently, lithium was precipitated as either Li3PO4 or Li2CO3 by adding a suitable precipitating Na3PO4 or Na2CO3 agent. Both filtered precipitates were dried at 80 °C for 24 h in a vacuum oven.

Carbothermal regeneration

To regenerate the LFP/C material, FePO4 was subjected to ball milling with Li3PO4 and Li2CO3 separately, using various FePO4 to Li precursor molar ratios of 1:1, 1:1.05, and 1:1.1. The milling process was conducted for 5 h at a speed of 400 rpm with a ball-to-powder mass ratio of 10:1. The balls were high chromium steel. After milling, sucrose, as a carbon source, was added to the ball-milled powder at a weight ratio of 30:70. The mixture was calcined at 750 °C for 6 h at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 under an Ar-10 Vol. % H2 atmosphere with a flow rate of 2 cc min−1. After naturally cooling in the furnace, the calcined powders were manually crushed in a mortar. For easy presentation, the regenerated LFP/C powders were coded as shown in Table 1.

Materials characterization

A DRON-8 X-ray diffractometer (Bourevestnik, Russia) was used to analyze the phase and structure of the powder samples. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed using CuKα radiation (λ = 1.540 Å) with the following parameters: 2θ = 10°–80°, a step size of 0.04°, and a scan rate of 0.5 s per step. The particle size and morphology were analyzed using a Vega II scanning electron microscope (SEM) (TESCAN, Czech Republic) at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. Raman spectra were recorded on a DL-G100 Raman Microscope (TEKSAN, IRAN) using a 532 nm laser excitation to evaluate the quality of the carbon layer. The amount of carbon was quantified by a Vario EL (Germany) element analyzer. The ICP-OES measurements were conducted using the ARCOS (SPECTRO Analytical Instruments GmbH, Germany) with an axial plasma viewing configuration.

Electrochemical measurements

To evaluate the electrochemical performance of the regenerated LFP/C powders as a cathode, a button cell (CR 2016) was assembled in a glove box, using lithium metal as anode and polypropylene film (Celgard 2400) as separator. The working electrode was prepared by pasting a slurry of regenerated LFP/C, carbon black, and polyvinylidene fluoride (in a 70:15:15 mass ratio) in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone onto an aluminum foil for 5 h. The coated Al foil was dried at 80 °C for 8 h. The mass loading was about 2 mg cm−2. The electrolyte was a 1 M solution of LiPF6 in a 1:1 (V: V) mixture of ethylene carbonate and dimethyl carbonate. The electrochemical charge/discharge tests were performed within a voltage range of 2–4 V vs. Li/Li+ using a BTS-5V10mA battery tester (NEWARE, China). The cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests were recorded on a 1A Radstat workstation (Kianshardanesh, Iran). The CV curves were obtained at the various scanning rates of 0.2 and 0.5 mV s−1 in the potential range of 2.0–4.1 V vs. Li/Li+. The AC amplitude of EIS was set to 10 mV over the frequency range of 0.01–50,000 Hz.
