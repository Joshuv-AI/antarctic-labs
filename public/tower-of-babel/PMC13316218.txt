Atrial fibrillation and cardiovascular comorbidity burden as predictors of in-hospital mortality in acute pancreatitis: A National Inpatient Sample analysis of 1.9 million hospitalizations
Authors: Brett David Cohen, Nishanth Eluri, Samuel Levine, Luke Miller, Nicholas Roma, Michael Durkin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) and cardiovascular (CV) comorbidities are prevalent in acute pancreatitis (AP) and may compromise hemodynamic tolerance of systemic inflammation. Their independent contributions to in-hospital mortality in AP have not been quantified nationally.MethodsWe analyzed discharge data from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS), Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP), Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), 2016–2022. Adult AP hospitalizations were identified by primary ICD-10-CM diagnosis. Twelve CV risk factors were examined. Survey-weighted logistic regression generated unadjusted, adjusted, and fully adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for in-hospital mortality. A cumulative risk factor count was constructed.ResultsAmong 1,919,159 weighted AP hospitalizations, overall mortality was 0.59%. On full multivariable adjustment, cerebrovascular disease (OR 2.83, 95% CI 2.33–3.44), AF (2.10, 1.85–2.37), CKD (1.89, 1.68–2.12), MI (1.86, 1.56–2.23), and heart failure (1.79, 1.56–2.05) were the strongest independent CV-comorbidity predictors of in-hospital death. Weighted mortality rose progressively from 0.21% at zero to 3.03% at seven cumulative risk factors; per additional risk factor the crude OR was 1.47 (1.45–1.50, p < 0.001). The dose-response gradient persisted after full adjustment, with integer-level adjusted ORs rising from 1.25 at one risk factor to 5.05 at seven, and a binary cutoff at three or more versus two or fewer risk factors carrying an adjusted OR of 2.01 (1.80–2.25, p < 0.001).ConclusionsCerebrovascular disease, AF, CKD, MI, and heart failure independently predict in-hospital mortality in AP, with a robust adjusted dose-response by cumulative CV burden. These associative findings are hypothesis-generating; prospective studies are needed to determine whether structured cardiovascular risk assessment improves outcomes in this population.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Acute pancreatitis, Cardiovascular comorbidity, Mortality, National inpatient sample

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease remains a leading cause of mortality and morbidity in the United States [1]. Acute pancreatitis (AP) is among the most common gastrointestinal causes of hospitalization, with U.S. age-adjusted incidence rising from 65 to 82 cases per 100,000 adults annually between 2001 and 2014 [2] in parallel with rising global rates [3], and represents a high-acuity systemic inflammatory illness in which baseline cardiovascular reserve may be critically tested.

Systemic injury in AP manifests as organ failure in approximately 20% of cases and is the principal determinant of in-hospital outcome [4]. Pre-existing cardiovascular and metabolic comorbidities are associated with progression to multiorgan dysfunction and worse outcomes in AP [5,6], which itself produces frequent acute cardiovascular manifestations including arrhythmias and hemodynamic instability [7].

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent sustained cardiac arrhythmia and represents a particularly important comorbidity. AF frequently coexists with advanced age, hypertension, and structural heart disease, all common among hospitalized AP patients. Furthermore, the proinflammatory and prothrombotic milieu of AP can precipitate new-onset or paroxysmal AF, and pre-existing AF predicts subsequent AP risk in population data, creating a bidirectional cardiovascular-pancreatic relationship that compounds mortality risk [[8], [9], [10], [11]].

Despite the clinical plausibility of a cardiovascular-pancreatic mortality relationship, prior characterizations have come predominantly from European national cohorts (German and Spanish) and from single-center or single-comorbidity U.S. analyses; the cumulative cardiovascular comorbidity burden and its dose-response relationship with AP mortality have not been characterized in a contemporary U.S. national sample. Understanding which cardiovascular risk factors independently predict mortality, and how their accumulation confers graded risk, is essential for refining risk stratification at AP admission.

Using a nationally representative sample spanning 2016–2022, this study aimed to: (1) determine the independent association of AF, heart failure, and ten additional cardiovascular risk factors with in-hospital mortality among AP hospitalizations; (2) characterize the dose-response relationship between cumulative cardiovascular risk burden and mortality; and (3) define the cardiovascular comorbidity profile that distinguishes non-surviving from surviving AP hospitalizations, to inform cardiovascular risk stratification at the time of admission.

Methods

Data source

This study used discharge data from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS), Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP), Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) [12]. The NIS is the largest publicly available all-payer inpatient care database in the United States, designed to produce national and regional estimates. Beginning with data year 2012, the NIS is a 20% stratified sample of discharges from all HCUP-participating community hospitals, covering more than 97% of the U.S. population; rehabilitation and long-term acute care hospitals are excluded. Application of AHRQ-provided discharge weights (DISCWT) yields nationally representative estimates. The unit of analysis is the individual hospitalization, not the individual patient, as the NIS does not include patient identifiers. This study was exempt from institutional review board oversight as the NIS consists of de-identified, publicly available data (45 CFR 46.101(b)).

Study population

We identified adult hospitalizations (patient age ≥18 years) from 2016 to 2022 with AP as the principal discharge diagnosis using ICD-10-CM codes K85.0x–K85.3x, K85.8x, and K85.9x. Hospitalizations with AP coded in a non-primary position were excluded.

Cardiovascular risk factor variables

Twelve CV risk factors were defined from ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes recorded in any of the 39 secondary diagnosis positions (DX2–DX40); the principal diagnosis (DX1) was acute pancreatitis by cohort definition. The risk factors were: AF or flutter (I48.x), heart failure (I50.x), cerebrovascular disease (I60–I63, I65–I67, I69), hypertension (I10–I13, I15), diabetes mellitus (E10–E11, E13), peripheral artery disease (I70–I71, I73.1, I73.9, I74.8–I74.9), chronic kidney disease (N18.x), coronary artery disease (I25.x), myocardial infarction (I21–I22, I25.2, Z86.19), and history of CABG (Z95.1), plus age categories 65–75 years and >75 years. A composite CV risk factor count was generated along with a sensitivity binary indicator for ≥3 versus <3 risk factors.

Statistical analysis

All analyses used Stata (StataCorp, College Station, TX) with the HCUP-recommended survey design framework: DISCWT as the discharge weight, NIS_STRATUM for stratification, HOSP_NIS as the primary sampling unit, and the singleunit (scaled) option per HCUP Methods Series Report #2015-09 [13]. Subpopulation analyses used the svy, subpop command. Two logistic regression models were fit for each risk factor: unadjusted; and fully adjusted with all twelve CV risk factors simultaneously plus demographics (age, sex, race, ZIP income quartile), hospital characteristics (region, bed size, teaching status), payer, year fixed effects, and additional non-overlapping comorbidities (hyperlipidemia, OSA, COPD, HIV, cirrhosis, obesity). The Charlson comorbidity index was not included as a covariate because six of its component conditions (heart failure, myocardial infarction, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease) overlap with our individual CV exposures; including both produces over-adjustment that artificially attenuates per-CV-factor estimates. A continuous logistic model assessed mortality per additional risk factor, and a binary cutoff at three or more versus two or fewer risk factors was examined as an exploratory threshold; this cutoff was selected post hoc because the transition from two to three risk factors produced the largest single-step increase in adjusted log-odds across the integer-level dose-response curve, and is presented as hypothesis-generating given that confidence intervals for adjacent integer-level odds ratios overlap. Baseline characteristics between surviving and non-surviving hospitalizations were compared with survey-weighted chi-square and regression tests. Per HCUP data use requirements, cell estimates based on ≤10 unweighted observations were suppressed. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Two prespecified sensitivity analyses were added in revision. To assess whether the AF association reflected pre-existing rather than acute, reactive arrhythmia, AF was reclassified by ICD-10-CM subtype into chronic AF (persistent, I48.11 and I48.19; chronic or permanent, I48.20 and I48.21), paroxysmal AF (I48.0), and unspecified AF (I48.91), with atrial flutter (I48.3, I48.4, I48.92) considered separately, and the fully adjusted model was refit with these categories in place of the composite AF term. To assess the robustness of the AF association to the conditions exhibiting paradoxical protective associations, the fully adjusted model was refit excluding hospitalizations with coronary artery disease, hypertension, or prior CABG. Results appear in Supplementary Table S1 and S2.

Results

Study cohort and overall mortality

From 2016 to 2022, 383,832 unweighted hospitalizations represented a weighted total of 1,919,159 AP hospitalizations nationally. Overall in-hospital mortality was 0.59%. Annual rates ranged from 0.53% (2018) to 0.68% (2022) without a consistent trend; year fixed effects in adjusted models were not statistically significant. Non-surviving hospitalizations involved significantly older patients (mean 65.2 (SEM 0.35) vs. 50.7 (SEM 0.03) years, p < 0.001), were more often in males (59.4% vs. 53.7%), and had substantially higher Charlson burden (47.2% with index ≥3 vs. 15.8%). Medicare was the predominant payer among non-survivors (56.9% vs. 28.5%), and non-surviving hospitalizations more frequently occurred at large (53.5% vs. 44.3%) and urban teaching hospitals (72.8% vs. 65.6%). Cardiovascular risk factor prevalence and mortality

All twelve CV risk factors were significantly more prevalent in non-surviving hospitalizations (Table 1). The most striking differences were for AF (26.0% vs. 5.9%), heart failure (22.7% vs. 5.9%), CKD (26.9% vs. 8.7%), and age >75 years (30.5% vs. 9.0%).

On unadjusted analysis (Table 2), CVA and AF showed the highest crude ORs for in-hospital death (OR 5.62, 95% CI 4.72–6.70 and 5.62, 5.11–6.19, respectively), followed by heart failure (OR 4.71, 4.26–5.20) and age >75 years (OR 4.46, 4.08–4.89). In the fully adjusted model (all twelve CV factors simultaneously plus demographics, hospital characteristics, payer, year fixed effects, and additional non-overlapping comorbidities), CVA (OR 2.83, 95% CI 2.33–3.44), AF (2.10, 1.85–2.37), CKD (1.89, 1.68–2.12), MI (1.86, 1.56–2.23), heart failure (1.79, 1.56–2.05), age 65–75 (1.42, 1.20–1.69), age >75 (1.32, 1.05–1.66), and PAD (1.25, 1.05–1.49) were each independently associated with higher mortality. CAD (0.67, 0.57–0.78), history of CABG (0.78, 0.62–0.99), and hypertension (0.71, 0.64–0.80) retained apparent protective associations after full adjustment, while diabetes was not independently associated with mortality (0.94, 0.85–1.04).

Dose-response relationship

Mortality rose in a consistent stepwise fashion with cumulative CV risk factor count (Table 3). Weighted in-hospital mortality was 0.21% at zero risk factors, then 0.36%, 0.59%, 1.12%, 1.51%, 1.70%, 2.46%, and 3.03% at one through seven risk factors respectively. Mortality at eight, nine, and ten risk factors was 2.29%, 3.61%, and 5.88%, with progressively smaller unweighted cell sizes (786, 166, and 17, respectively) and wider confidence intervals; cells at eleven and twelve risk factors were suppressed per HCUP requirements (unweighted N ≤ 10). Crude odds ratios versus zero risk factors rose stepwise through seven: 1.69 (95% CI 1.44–1.98), 2.84 (2.43–3.31), 5.39 (4.61–6.30), 7.28 (6.17–8.59), 8.21 (6.79–9.91), 11.98 (9.74–14.73), and 14.86 (11.48–19.24); estimates at eight, nine, and ten were 11.14 (6.87–18.05), 17.82 (7.81–40.68), and 29.70 (3.92–224.88), reflecting both true effect size and small-cell imprecision. At the binary cutoff, hospitalizations with three or more risk factors had an unadjusted OR of 4.25 (3.91–4.62, p < 0.001) versus those with two or fewer. Per additional risk factor, the crude OR was 1.47 (1.45–1.50, p < 0.001). After full adjustment, the cumulative-burden gradient persisted with a graded rise: integer-level adjusted ORs were 1.25 (1.05–1.49), 1.61 (1.34–1.94), 2.42 (1.97–2.96), 2.96 (2.37–3.69), 3.12 (2.43–3.99), 4.29 (3.29–5.59), and 5.05 (3.70–6.89) at one through seven risk factors respectively, with progressively wider confidence intervals beyond seven (3.79, 5.79, and 11.00 at eight, nine, and ten). The binary cutoff at three or more versus two or fewer risk factors carried an adjusted OR of 2.01 (1.80–2.25, p < 0.001).

Healthcare utilization

Non-surviving hospitalizations had markedly longer stays (mean 10.85 (SEM 0.33) vs. 4.26 (SEM 0.01) days, p < 0.001) and higher charges ($186,114 (SEM $7211) vs. $42,428 (SEM $118), p < 0.001).

Sensitivity analyses

Restricting AF to chronic subtypes that by definition predate the index admission (persistent, chronic, or permanent AF), the association with mortality persisted on full adjustment (adjusted OR 1.66, 95% CI 1.33–2.06), although paroxysmal or unspecified AF carried a higher adjusted OR (2.12, 1.86–2.43), indicating that reactive arrhythmia contributes to but does not fully account for the association (Supplementary Table S1). In a model contrasting chronic AF with no AF, the association was maintained (1.39, 1.12–1.74). Excluding hospitalizations with coronary artery disease, hypertension, or prior CABG, the conditions exhibiting paradoxical protective associations, the AF association was unchanged or stronger (adjusted OR 2.65, 1.98–3.55; Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

In this nationally representative analysis of 1.9 million AP hospitalizations, cerebrovascular disease (fully adjusted OR 2.83), atrial fibrillation (2.10), chronic kidney disease (1.89), myocardial infarction (1.86), and heart failure (1.79) each emerged as strong independent cardiovascular predictors of in-hospital mortality. These findings, together with the persistent dose-response gradient that survives full multivariable adjustment, carry direct implications for how cardiologists, hospitalists, and intensivists approach cardiovascular risk in the AP setting.

Pre-existing AF signals underlying cardiovascular fragility that limits hemodynamic tolerance during the systemic inflammatory response of AP, while AP-precipitated new-onset AF may complicate management by creating competing demands for fluid resuscitation and anticoagulation. The persistence of the AF association through full multivariable adjustment alongside the other eleven CV risk factors confirms that AF captures mortality risk distinct from general comorbidity burden, although the independent contribution of new-onset AF beyond comorbid sepsis and other acute conditions in non-cardiac critical illness remains debated [14].

A complementary mechanistic explanation links our AF, heart failure, and CKD findings to the hemodynamic challenges of AP. Loss of organized atrial systole in AF reduces cardiac output and predisposes to hemodynamic compromise [15], an effect amplified by diastolic dysfunction or left atrial enlargement, and high ventricular rates further impair diastolic filling. Superimposed on the systemic inflammatory response and the substantial volume requirements of AP, these effects can progress rapidly to pulmonary edema, right ventricular strain, and forward failure, plausibly contributing to the disproportionate mortality observed among patients with AF and heart failure. New-onset AF in this setting may also serve as a marker of vulnerable atrial substrate unmasked by acute physiologic stress, capturing both a hemodynamic insult and a sicker underlying phenotype. The independent contribution of CKD may reflect reduced renal reserve to tolerate the volume shifts and acute kidney injury risk inherent to AP.

These hemodynamic considerations intersect with evolving fluid-resuscitation practice. Because the WATERFALL trial, which favored moderated over aggressive resuscitation in acute pancreatitis, excluded patients with NYHA class II or worse heart failure and chronic renal failure, its findings do not directly inform fluid management in the cardiovascular-comorbid subgroup our analysis identifies as highest-risk, a gap warranting dedicated prospective evaluation [16].

The apparent protective associations of hypertension, coronary artery disease, and history of CABG in the fully adjusted model are consistent with prior administrative-data analyses of acute pancreatitis: Hidalgo et al. (Spanish national cohort, n = 110,021) similarly identified hypertension as a protective factor in the AP setting [5]. Analogous protective signals from chronic comorbidities in critical illness have been documented for diabetes in sepsis [17] and for obesity in the intensive care unit (the obesity paradox) [18]. Two structural mechanisms likely contribute. First, restricting analysis to hospitalized patients enriches the no-comorbidity group for more severe acute disease (because admission thresholds differ by comorbidity status), which can manufacture an apparent protective association without any true biological effect [19]. Second, patients with well-managed chronic conditions may be more likely to be on chronic medications with independent benefit in critical illness or have better controlled comorbidities. Critically, secondary ICD-10-CM codes cannot distinguish pre-existing comorbidities from in-hospital complications, a limitation that may further contribute [20,21]. Diabetes was not independently associated with mortality in our cohort (fully adjusted OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.85–1.04), consistent with Hidalgo et al.‘s null finding.

Robustness of the atrial fibrillation association. Because administrative data cannot directly establish the timing of AF onset, we examined whether the association reflected reactive, acutely-precipitated arrhythmia rather than pre-existing disease. Restricting the exposure to chronic AF subtypes that by clinical definition predate admission (persistent, chronic, or permanent AF) preserved a significant independent association (adjusted OR 1.66; 1.39 when chronic AF was contrasted directly with no AF), indicating that the relationship is not solely attributable to new-onset arrhythmia provoked by systemic inflammation, hypovolemia, or fever. The larger adjusted OR for paroxysmal or unspecified AF (2.12) is consistent with an additional contribution from acute, reactive AF that may also mark illness severity. Separately, excluding patients with the conditions showing paradoxical protective associations did not attenuate the AF association, which was stronger (adjusted OR 2.65 versus 2.10 in the full cohort). Together these analyses indicate that pre-existing AF, itself a cardiovascular comorbidity, carries independent prognostic weight in AP, while the magnitude of the composite AF estimate partly reflects reactive arrhythmia.

The dose-response relationship (crude mortality rising from 0.21% at zero risk factors to 3.03% at seven) describes an admission-level risk gradient that may inform cardiovascular risk assessment. The gradient persists with a graded rise after full adjustment for age, demographics, and additional non-overlapping comorbidities: integer-level adjusted ORs are 1.25, 1.61, 2.42, 2.96, 3.12, 4.29, and 5.05 at one through seven risk factors. The largest single-step increase occurs at the transition from two to three risk factors (per-step adjusted OR ratio 1.50 versus 1.22–1.29 at adjacent transitions); because confidence intervals for adjacent integer-level estimates overlap, we present the three-or-more threshold (adjusted OR 2.01, 95% CI 1.80–2.25) as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating cut-point rather than a validated clinical rule. These figures translate into a substantial absolute risk increment given the high case volume of AP nationally. A simple risk factor count could complement existing AP severity scores (BISAP, Revised Atlanta Classification) [22,23] to guide triage intensity and early cardiology consultation.

Our findings extend and align with prior literature. Keller and colleagues, in a 797,364-case German nationwide analysis published in the International Journal of Cardiology, examined four cardiovascular conditions (CAD, heart failure, PAD, AF) jointly as a count and individually in multivariable models, reporting a per-cardiovascular-disease odds ratio of 1.48 (95% CI 1.45–1.50) for in-hospital mortality with adjustment for age, sex, obesity, cancer, hypertension, CKD stages 3–5, diabetes, and COPD [24]. Our broader 12-risk-factor count produced a comparable adjusted per-factor OR of 1.25 (1.21–1.29) under analogous full multivariable adjustment, supporting external validity across two large national datasets with different health systems and coding conventions. In Keller's separately adjusted individual-condition models, heart failure (OR 2.16), AF (1.61), and PAD (1.25) each independently predicted mortality, with the same direction and similar magnitudes as our adjusted estimates (heart failure 1.79; AF 2.10; PAD 1.25). The convergence of effect sizes despite different cohorts, periods, and covariate sets strengthens the inference that these specific cardiovascular comorbidities carry independent prognostic weight in AP. Jamal et al. (NIS, 2016–2017) reported a two-to threefold mortality increase among AP patients with concurrent atrial fibrillation, consistent with our fully adjusted AF odds ratio of 2.10; Ju et al. (3-center cohort, n = 3537) confirmed an independent association between AF and AP severity but did not detect a significant in-hospital mortality difference in their smaller sample [8,9]. The Taiwanese cohort of Lai et al. which followed AF patients forward for incident AP (HR 1.46), supports the bidirectional cardiovascular-pancreatic relationship referenced in our Introduction [10]. Our analysis adds three contributions: simultaneous adjustment for twelve cardiovascular and demographic factors that quantifies each independent contribution; characterization of a graded mortality response across cumulative cardiovascular burden; and recent (2016–2022) data from a U.S. national sample, enabling direct relevance to contemporary practice.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The NIS unit of analysis is the hospitalization, not the patient; patient-level tracking is not possible. ICD-10-CM coding accuracy is assumed, and clinical variables (AP severity, laboratory values, imaging) are unavailable. Secondary diagnosis codes cannot distinguish pre-existing comorbidities from in-hospital complications. Although AF classification from discharge codes cannot definitively establish onset timing, a subtype-based sensitivity analysis restricting the exposure to chronic AF that predates admission preserved the association (Supplementary Table S1), mitigating though not completely eliminating this concern. Cell estimates with ≤10 unweighted observations were suppressed per HCUP requirements. The composite risk factor score weights each condition equally. AP etiology (gallstone, alcoholic, hypertriglyceridemia, post-ERCP, idiopathic) was not used as an adjustment variable, and these subgroups carry differing cardiovascular comorbidity profiles and baseline mortality. Coronary artery disease (I25.x) and old myocardial infarction (I25.2) overlap by definition, which can broaden uncertainty around the CAD coefficient. The Charlson comorbidity index was intentionally omitted from the fully adjusted model because six of its weighted components overlap with our individual CV exposures and would induce over-adjustment; sensitivity analyses including Charlson reproduced the well-known artifactual attenuation of per-condition CV estimates and the associated paradoxical “protective” findings for chronic kidney disease and diabetes. Findings apply to in-hospital outcomes only. The study period (2016–2022) includes the early COVID-19 pandemic, which may have influenced both case-mix and inpatient mortality through indirect pathways, although year fixed effects in adjusted models were not statistically significant.

Conclusions

Cerebrovascular disease, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, myocardial infarction, and heart failure are the dominant independent cardiovascular predictors of in-hospital mortality in AP, with mortality risk escalating in a robust dose-response pattern that survives full multivariable adjustment as cardiovascular comorbidity accumulates. Although these findings are associative rather than causal, they identify cardiovascular comorbidity burden, and atrial fibrillation in particular, as a risk marker for in-hospital mortality in AP. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether structured cardiovascular risk assessment or targeted management, such as early cardiology involvement, can improve outcomes in this high-risk population.
