Rebalancing innovation, affordability, and access for orphan drugs in the European Union
Authors: Hilde Stevens, Georges Siotis, Micael Castanheira, Mathias Dewatripont, Sanae Akodad, Sam Daems, Alain Fischer, Michel Goldman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOrphan drug policy in the European Union faces a double price-and-innovation gap: a small fraction of rare diseases receive important resources while the overwhelming majority are under- or un-researched, leaving most rare disease patients facing high unmet medical needs. The European Commission's reform proposals, notably the Pharma Package and the European Biotech Act, seek to rebalance incentives by adjusting market exclusivity. We argue that, while these reforms move in the right direction, they are insufficient to foster meaningful innovation while safeguarding affordability, and that a broader, more structural approach is needed. We show how proposals from the Draghi Report could complement the reforms through an EU-level HTA Coordination Office, a US-style EU ARPA-H, and expanded regulatory sandboxing. We then propose two additional instruments: public-private Special Purpose Vehicles to de-risk high-need innovation, and EU-level joint procurement to strengthen affordability and create predictable demand. Ultimately, only a coherent, well-calibrated framework can align industrial policy with the EU's ambition of leaving no rare-disease patient behind.

We reviewed relevant legislative and policy documents from the European Commission, relevant publications from the European Medicines Agency, and relevant articles identified in the PubMed/MEDLINE database. Introduction

The emergence of the orphan drug industry is largely the result of targeted policy interventions, notably the US Orphan Drug Act (1983) and the Regulation (EC) No 141/2000 on Orphan Medicinal Products (OMPs) (2000).1, 2, 3 These frameworks introduced a combination of market exclusivity, financial incentives, and regulatory support mechanisms designed to reduce both the cost and risk of developing therapies for small patient populations. In addition to granting regulatory market exclusivity beyond standard patent protection under the TRIPS agreement, the policies provide tax credits, fee waivers, protocol assistance, and greater regulatory predictability, together reshaping the economic calculus of rare disease innovation.1, 2, 3, 4, 5

However, the policy's success came at the cost of sometimes very high prices, enabled by long periods of market exclusivity (7-to-9 years in the US; 10-to-12 in the EU). For instance, Novartis's Kymriah, a gene therapy for leukaemia, is priced at USD 475,000 per course. Biogen's Spinraza, a Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA) drug, is priced at USD 750,000 for the first year of treatment and USD 375,000 for subsequent years. Novartis' Zolgensma, a gene therapy for SMA, is priced at USD 2.1 million, and Lenmeldy, Orchard Therapeutic's gene-therapeutic one-off infusion to treat metachromatic leukodystrophy (MLD), is priced at USD 4.25 million. With such stratospheric prices, sales quickly4, 5, 6, 7 become substantial. Although a distinction can be made between orphan products that achieve blockbuster-like revenues in broader or growing markets, the so-called “nichebusters”, and therapies intended for extremely small patient populations, this distinction should not be read as implying that the prices of the latter are necessarily justified. Even in ultra-rare conditions, prices may remain excessive, especially where development risks have already been substantially reduced by prior academic research and early clinical demonstration of safety and efficacy. These high costs have contributed to a broader rethinking of OMP protection and incentives.

The challenge today is a double price-and-innovation gap. The market for rare diseases has indeed split in two parts: a small fraction of the rare diseases receive a lot of attention and huge resourcesg while the huge majority are under- or un-researched, leaving the largest part of the rare disease patients facing high unmet medical needs (HUMN).1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7

Since the number of rare diseases is uncertain, assessments vary, but it is commonly perceived that only between 5 and 10% of rare diseases currently benefit from a treatment. In spite of this, as detailed in the ‘Draghi Report,’ sales of orphan medicinal products (OMPs) increased 24-fold between 2012 and 2022, with the trend expected to accelerate.8 The OMPs' share of global pharmaceutical sales has doubled over the past decade and is expected to reach one-fifth of the projected USD 1.6 trillion global prescription drug market by 2030, a level that is financially unsustainable.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 To address this dual challenge, the European Commission launched an extensive impact assessment of the Regulation,9 which shaped the “EU Pharma Package”,14 detailed in the next section. While the proposed reforms go in the right direction, we argue that they will be insufficient to boost research and development where it has been lacking. Moreover, the Package does not fully address the problem of manifold price increases after obtaining market exclusivity. To jointly address these conflicting objectives, the EU needs to complement the existing proposed reforms with additional, targeted instruments.

The proposals included in the Draghi Report8 and the subsequent EU Biotech Act,15 discussed in the following section, already help improve the situation, in particular by defragmenting the EU market, thereby lowering innovation costs. We detail in particular how these proposals can be further developed to benefit rare-disease innovation in three dimensions, namely the creation of an EU Health Technology Assessment (HTA) Coordination Office, a US-style Advanced Research Projects Agency for Health (ARPA-H), and expanded regulatory sandboxing.

Finally, we propose two complementary instruments and argue that they would be most effective when combined in ways tailored to the problem at hand. The first one is the creation of Public-Private Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs), which are legal entities, to invest in high-social-value research. The public sector, together with patient organisations and non-profit research organisations, would provide a significant share of the seed funding needed for high-risk, high-social-value research. The private sector would be guaranteed a share of the profits to ensure that the endeavour is financially viable. The profits retained by the public sector would then either be used to finance the cost of rare disease treatments or for reinvestment in long-term research, in the spirit of the US’ ARPA-H. This positive feedback loop in R&D would reinforce Europe's long-run industrial strengths.

The second complementary instrument, joint procurement, either through coalitions of countries or at the EU level, as in the case of COVID vaccines, can both limit “significantly above-market” profits and boost the profitability of OMPs that currently generate “insufficient” returns. For nichebuster drugs, where there is little doubt that prices are abnormally high, joint procurement increases buyers' bargaining power. Conversely, committing in advance to purchase a predefined volume of OMPs for a large market can increase profitability and, hence, the attractiveness of investing in HUMN.

This Personal View does not seek to propose a formal implementation roadmap. Rather, it is structured around three categories of policy action: reforms already nearing adoption, policy initiatives already under discussion that could be better mobilised for orphan diseases, and two additional complementary instruments that could further improve the innovation-access trade-off.

The EU pharma package: a good but only partial solution

Following a landmark political agreement reached on December 11, 2025, the “EU Pharma Package” is set to replace the 2000 orphan-drug framework. The Package should come into force after a 2-year transition period following formal adoption, i.e., in 2028. In a nutshell, the reform of the Orphan Drug Regulation focuses on fine-tuning exclusivity.14

Under the new regime, the duration of exclusivity is more clearly tiered according to the product's impact. Instead of 10 years, the base exclusivity will be 9 years, with two extra years for “Breakthrough OMPs”, referring to conditions with no existing treatments (hence encompassing HUMN) and which demonstrate a clinically relevant reduction in morbidity or mortality in the target population. For “Well-Established Use” drugs, primarily consisting of repurposed drugs whose MA draws on existing scientific literature or bibliographic data, rather than new proprietary clinical trial data, exclusivity will be reduced to 5 years.14 This reduced exclusivity reflects the EU's aim to balance incentives for repurposing with timely generic competition where less investment has been made in generating new knowledge. Colchicine is the ‘ghost example’ behind the EU Reform proposal: the EC presumably wants to avoid the US experience with repurposed colchicine that was accompanied by price spikes on well-known medicines.16 The Package also introduces the concept of “Global (Orphan) Marketing Authorisation” (GOMA). Under the EU Pharma reform, the GOMA principle ensures that all uses of the same active substance are treated as a single lifecycle, preventing exclusivity resets and limiting exclusivity for well-established drugs. Under the previous 2000 Regulation, each time a given drug obtained a new orphan drug designation (ODD), it would also receive an additional 10-year period of market exclusivity for that indication. Under the new regime, a single clock will start ticking for the substance at the time the first MA is granted. This transforms the model from one based on indications to one based on the active ingredient. If the drug obtains an orphan-label extension (new indication), the marketing authorisation holder will be granted a 1-year extension, for a maximum of two extensions. In addition, new indications cannot be granted if the application is made within the last two years of the existing exclusivity period. Hence, combined with base exclusivity, the maximum period is either 11 years (9 + 1 + 1) or 13 years for a breakthrough OMP (11 + 1 + 1).14 These new rules should severely curtail “indication stacking” and “evergreening”.17,18

In addition, the reform strengthens the “Bolar Exemption”. This essentially allows generic and biosimilar manufacturers to prepare for market entry (including pricing and reimbursement filings) while the original drug is still protected. The reason is that, thanks to GOMA, the exclusivity period becomes predictable and fixed, allowing generic and biosimilar companies to launch health technology assessment (HTA) and price negotiations years earlier. This should ensure that, when the exclusivity period ends, competition could materialise across all indications simultaneously.14

Finally, the reform should curtail “salami tactics”, whereby common diseases are subdivided into increasingly narrow sub-populations based on biomarkers. In the future, sponsors will have to meet stricter criteria regarding prevalence and “significant benefit” to obtain an OMP marketing authorisation. More precisely, if a drug treats a subset of a common disease, the company will have to prove that it cannot be used for the broader population. Otherwise, the EMA may refuse the ODD for the sub-population.

These measures aim to contain costs, curb excesses and opportunism, and steer incentives towards drugs addressing HUMNs. While these are, per se, laudable improvements, these measures are unlikely to suffice. On the one hand, the current environment steers private investment towards incremental, lower-risk projects and away from radical innovations targeting HUMNs.17 Without complementary incentives, the biomedical innovation landscape for OMPs will remain structurally unbalanced. On the other hand, public actors already invest heavily in research through push incentives (e.g., grants, prizes, tax credits, and public-private partnerships or PPPs), but receive insufficient return or price reductions when projects are successful.

In the presence of multiple objectives, separate policy instruments ought to be available: this simple rule, known as the ‘Tinbergen rule’, was brought forward by the first economics Nobel recipient, Jan Tinbergen, who stated that “achieving the desired values of a certain number of targets requires the policy maker to control an equal number of instruments”.19 The Draghi Report echoes this principle, calling for a coordinated industrial strategy for EU industry in general that spans investment, taxation, access to finance, R&D policy, and regulation. The Commission acknowledges that “one-size-fits-all” policies are no longer adequate.9

In line with the Tinbergen Rule, both the Draghi Report8 and the European Biotech Act15 identify complementary instruments to reduce innovation costs by better integrating the EU market, through improved data use, stronger innovation hubs, non-profit manufacturing models (especially for advanced therapy medicinal products or ATMPs),20 strengthening of European Reference Networks (ERNs), regulatory streamlining (in particular through an EU HTA body or by including experimentation through extensive ‘regulatory sandboxing’), interoperable rare disease registries, and a robust EU ARPA-H-like structure. The following section identifies actions that we believe are both achievable in the current EU legal context and that usefully complement the Pharma Package, before turning to two additional instruments aimed at reconciling innovation and affordability for orphan drugs.

The Draghi report and the EU Biotech Act: additional promising avenues

The Draghi Report highlights the strategic importance of the pharmaceutical and biotechnology sectors for Europe's economic resilience and technological sovereignty. It warns that the EU is steadily losing ground to the US and emerging markets such as China and India in high-growth domains, notably biological medicines and ATMPs. Between 2012 and 2022, global ATMP sales grew by 150%, three times faster than the overall pharmaceutical market, while global orphan drug sales expanded twenty-fourfold.8 These figures underscore the dynamism of rare and advanced therapies, but they also reveal Europe's relative underperformance in such breakthrough science.

For rare disease therapeutics in particular, this competitiveness gap has profound implications. The field depends on highly specialised science, regulatory agility, integrated clinical trial networks, and scalable manufacturing capacity—areas in which fragmentation can significantly delay innovation and patient access. The Report identifies five structural drivers behind Europe's lagging performance: (i) fragmented public R&D investment; (ii) declining private R&D investment, including from venture capital and private equity; (iii) insufficient support for globally competitive innovation hubs; (iv) fragmented pricing and reimbursement systems; and (v) fragmentation of the European Health Data Space.8 Each of these barriers disproportionately affects rare disease development, where small patient populations and high development risk and complex regulatory requirements already pose significant challenges.

These structural weaknesses are compounded by regulatory complexity and Europe's slower integration of artificial intelligence and digital infrastructure in biomedical innovation. For rare disease developers, who depend on cross-border patient recruitment, interoperable data systems, adaptive regulatory pathways, and coordinated HTA, fragmentation is a crippling problem.21 Regulation (EU) 2021/2282 on Health Technology Assessment (HTAR) is a meaningful step in reinforcing coordination among national HTA bodies. Similarly, even if not designed for OMPs, the nascent European Biotech Act represents an important step in the right direction. By emphasizing regulatory sandboxing, streamlining clinical trial processes, strengthening biomanufacturing capacity, and improving coordination across Member States, the Act directly addresses key bottlenecks.15 However, as we detail below, it will need to be leveraged in specific ways to meaningfully support OMPs.

We broadly endorse the Draghi Report's conclusions on the need to strengthen the competitiveness of the European pharmaceutical sector and welcome the European Commission's subsequent proposals under the European Biotech Act to reduce fragmentation and enhance regulatory coherence. In the specific context of rare diseases, three priority reforms merit particular emphasis: (i) the establishment of a more centralized and coordinated EU-level HTA framework to reduce post-authorization market fragmentation; (ii) increased, strategically targeted public R&D investment at EU level to de-risk early-stage innovation and crowd in private capital, and (iii) the introduction of regulatory sandboxing mechanisms under the European Biotech Act.15

Together, these reforms could substantially improve Europe's ability not only to generate rare disease innovation but also to translate it efficiently into approved and accessible therapies. The Draghi Report and the European Biotech Act thus hold potential not merely as industrial competitiveness instruments, but they serve as a structural accelerator for rare disease innovation and patient access across the Union. We now discuss the main reforms affecting OMP innovation in turn.

Towards an EU-level HTA coordination office

Currently, once a medicinal product receives an EMA marketing authorisation, companies must navigate diverse national pricing and reimbursement procedures. This fragmentation creates delays, typically 12–24 months, between EU approval and actual patient access.21

Under the HTAR, which is effective since January 2025, Joint Clinical Assessments (JCAs) will initially apply to oncology medicines and ATMPs. The process expands to OMPs in 2028 and all medicines by 2030. Medical devices and in vitro diagnostic medical devices (IVDs) under the EU-MDR and EU-IVDR (Regulation (EU) 2017/746) will eventually be included, though transition timelines are still being defined. However, the JCA covers only clinical domains (effectiveness and safety).

To bridge the gap between clinical assessment and national pricing negotiations without infringing Art. 168 TFEU, an enhanced EU HTA coordination framework could offer methodological support for cost-effectiveness assessments. This unit could develop standardised health economic modelling frameworks that synthesise clinical data and outline budget impact methodologies. National HTA bodies could adapt these shared models to tailor them to their domestic environment. Early structured interactions between the EMA and this HTA network would ensure that both clinical and health-economic evidence generation, including real-world data, is optimised. This enhanced coordination could technically and logistically support voluntary, cross-border pricing coalitions among Member States, fostering gradual convergence and reducing access disparities while respecting national prerogatives enshrined in Art. 168 TFEU.

Towards an EU ARPA-H

As stressed by the Draghi Report, Europe could significantly reinforce its competitive position by concentrating public investment and adopting mission-driven R&D strategies.8 Indeed, the EU Innovative Medicine Initiative (IMI) and its successor, the Innovative Health Initiative (IHI), joint undertakings bringing together public and private investments, already delivered significant advances in healthcare,22,23 but their scope is limited to precompetitive research and their operations are often hindered by the bureaucratic rules imposed by the EC. Across the pond, the Operation Warp Speed (OWS), supported by the NIH and BARDA (now complemented by ARPA-H), which was instrumental in delivering COVID-19 vaccines, demonstrated that, to secure a decisive first-mover advantage, it is essential to lift bureaucratic restrictions.24 This allows to fully benefit from coordinated funding, regulatory flexibility, and cross-sector collaboration.

The EU's more complex structure, requiring consensus among Member States and navigating multiple funding sources (EU budget, European Investment Bank, national budgets), slows decision-making and limits agility. Strict eligibility rules restricting non-EU participation further impede scientific collaboration and technological leadership.

Europe could actually emulate the US by creating an EU ARPA-H equivalent: an agile, mission-oriented biomedical agency with authority to coordinate R&D, de-risk high-impact projects, and integrate regulatory and HTA considerations early in the development. Such an institution would strengthen Europe's position in fast-moving fields such as ATMPs, AI-driven drug discovery, and genomic medicine in general, and for orphan drugs in particular.

Regulatory sandboxing to keep up with the pace of innovation

Regulatory sandboxes are structured, time-limited, and supervised environments in which innovative products, processes, or regulatory approaches can be tested under controlled conditions. They operationalise the EU “experimentation clause” by permitting targeted and proportionate exemptions from specific regulatory requirements while maintaining high standards of safety and oversight as a baseline. By enabling iterative dialogue between regulators and innovators, sandboxes reduce uncertainty, accelerate institutional learning, and lower compliance costs.

The proposed European Biotech Act introduces a novel coordinated framework for regulatory sandboxing in biotechnology and biomanufacturing.15 It proposes harmonised criteria for participation, structured cooperation between national authorities and EU agencies, cross-border acknowledgement of sandbox outcomes, and enhanced regulatory support for small and medium-sized enterprises. It also foresees closer integration between sandbox activities, clinical trial frameworks, and data infrastructures, including improved use of real-world evidence and digital health data.

These provisions should be actively exploited to develop OMPs. With rare diseases, Phase III trials are hindered by narrow patient populations and, in many instances, the absence of any treatment results in particularly adverse outcomes. The opportunities provided by sandboxing can be used to proactively address these shortcomings: it can better align supervisory oversight with scientific and clinical realities, e.g., by allowing the use of advanced preclinical methods (including in silico modelling, organoids, organ-on-chips, and digital twins, instead of animal experimentation25, 26, 27, 28, 29). It can also accelerate procedures –and possibly facilitate temporary market authorisations–when the absence of treatment results in severely adverse outcomes.21

A concrete example is provided by the demonstration by Canaud et al. that BYL719, a PIK3CA inhibitor, produced substantial clinical benefit in patients with CLOVES syndrome, a condition affecting extremely small patient populations.27 Because a conventional Phase III trial was infeasible, the FDA granted accelerated approval for the therapy (as alpelisib), whereas the EMA lacked a comparable adaptive pathway. Despite the discovery originating in Europe, European patients have faced delayed access. A structured sandbox mechanism could have enabled evaluation under adaptive, closely monitored conditions, aligning regulatory oversight with clinical urgency.

Complementary measures to improve the innovation-access trade-off

Public-private investment in Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs) for new orphan drugs

Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs) are legally independent entities established to pool capital, distribute risk and allocate returns among multiple investors in accordance with predefined rules.30 Unlike purely contractual public-private partnerships, SPVs are standalone legal structures that can own assets, such as intellectual property, and enter into licensing agreements, thereby retaining direct control over future revenues. This legal separation enables ex ante specification of key contractual parameters, including pricing frameworks, profit-sharing mechanisms and return caps. By embedding these provisions in the SPV's governance and founding documents, the model reduces the scope for ex post renegotiations, such as excessive pricing by private partners or downward price renegotiations by public payers, that have historically undermined the stability and credibility of similar collaborative arrangements.31

Mission-oriented, e.g., in the form of a Benefit Corporation, public-private SPVs can address structural market failures in therapies for HUMN conditions by reallocating risk and reward across partners and, in doing so, reducing the effective cost of capital. Under conventional financial models, early-stage drug development relies primarily on venture capital or private equity, which typically requires high internal rates of return (often 20–30%) to compensate for scientific and regulatory risk. By incorporating public and philanthropic capital, whose investment objectives allow for substantially lower discount rates, the SPV can structurally lower the cost of capital for development programmes.

Reducing the financial hurdle rate through risk-sharing generates two main effects. First, it enables viable development pathways for economically marginal programmes, including ultra-rare HUMN indications, for which small end markets have historically rendered development unattractive. Second, by lowering financial costs and spreading risk across multiple stakeholders, the SPV model can reduce upward pressure on launch prices, thereby improving the affordability of new medicines in European health systems.

De-risking alone does not automatically translate into lower prices. However, the SPV structure enables the use of enforceable contractual mechanisms to ensure that public contributions are not captured as excess private profit. In particular, SPVs can implement a predefined distribution waterfall in which private investors receive capped or target returns, after which revenues are shared with public and philanthropic partners. Such arrangements can support a sustainable reinvestment cycle and allow non-profit or public investors to recover an agreed, proportionate share of returns once private research and development costs have been recouped.

The value creation potential of SPVs can be further enhanced through portfolio and platform-based strategies. By pursuing multiple development pathways in parallel, SPVs can diversify scientific risk, allowing returns from successful projects to generate a ‘reinvestment dividend’ that supports subsequent programmes.32 Such portfolio effects are particularly relevant when SPVs focus on platform technologies, such as gene therapy vectors, RNA-based modalities, or CAR-T platforms, whose applicability spans multiple rare diseases. Experience from mission-driven US institutions, such as DARPA, BARDA, and ARPA-H, illustrates how coordinated high-risk, high-reward funding models can accelerate innovation. A European entity with a similar mandate could act as a central funder and coordinator of SPVs operating in this space.

In addition, SPVs could allow for differentiated value distribution across jurisdictions. Participation in the SPV implies that contributing countries bear a share of development costs and risks ex ante, and it is therefore economically justified that they capture part of the resulting surplus ex post. Importantly, this need not rely on higher prices in non-participating countries. Instead, prices can remain aligned (with value-based or ability-to-pay principles) across jurisdictions, while participating countries receive a return on their investment through dividends, profit-sharing mechanisms, or equivalent financial flows that effectively reduce their net expenditure. In this way, part of the public return is realised through financial participation in the SPV rather than through geographically differentiated pricing, thereby avoiding both double payment by public investors and inequitable pricing across countries. However, such cross-subsidy mechanisms raise important governance, fiscal, and political economy questions that remain to be evaluated on a case-by-case basis.

These SPVs also raise the potential to address well-known bottlenecks in drug development. Public authorities could, for example, facilitate faster and more predictable administrative procedures, while structured involvement of patient organisations and academic institutions could improve patient identification and recruitment. These challenges are highly consequential in practice: Gomase et al. (2026) show that fragmented health technology assessment (HTA) processes and difficulties in patient recruitment generate substantial additional costs for OMPs.4 Multigovernmental participation in SPVs would create incentives to enhance cooperation on joint or aligned HTA processes, thereby reducing regulatory fragmentation. To support long-term positive dynamics, we propose that a significant share of any public return be earmarked for national health systems, with the remaining share reinvested in further research. SPVs specifically designed to address HUMN conditions could therefore align incentives on both sides: limited private investment indicates that expected financial returns alone are insufficient, necessitating risk-sharing mechanisms or complementary public guarantees, while the prioritisation of these conditions by governments and patient communities reflects substantial social value beyond purely financial returns.

From a public policy perspective, providing funding at an early stage can be economically efficient: deploying low-cost public capital upfront may reduce the need for governments to later absorb compounded risk premiums through high medicine prices. A common criticism of such approaches is that public actors are less effective than the private sector at selecting successful projects. In the SPV model, this concern is partly addressed by embedding private-sector expertise into governance and decision-making, including professional fund management, portfolio diversification, and rigorous, milestone-based stage–gate processes.

At the same time, the challenges should not be underestimated. Much will depend on the contractual design, governance arrangements, and implementation details, and any such initiative must ultimately be assessed against the current counterfactual, namely the lack of viable solutions for HUMN conditions. Even well-capitalised SPVs may fail to deliver expected outcomes. Nevertheless, careful design and cooperative governance could also help address structural weaknesses in the European life sciences ecosystem, including the chronic shortage of late-stage risk capital for EU-based biotechnology and pharmaceutical scale-ups.

EU joint procurement of orphan medicinal products

The Draghi Report calls for greater centralisation of public investment and improved coordination of clinical research, particularly in rare diseases.8 In parallel, pharmaceutical expenditure has risen sharply across Member States, intensifying tensions between innovation and affordability. In this context, joint procurement of OMPs merits serious consideration as a structural instrument to reduce fragmentation, strengthen bargaining power, and improve access.33

The EU's collective procurement of COVID-19 vaccines demonstrated that pooling purchasing power can accelerate access and secure more favourable pricing conditions.8,33 Although the pandemic context was exceptional, the underlying rationale—aggregation of demand to correct market imbalances—may be even more relevant in rare diseases. Unlike COVID-19, where urgency was paramount, rare disease procurement is mainly characterised by sustainability and equity concerns. Because orphan markets are intrinsically small, fragmented national negotiations both increase regulatory costs for the industry and weaken Member States' bargaining position.

A coordinated procurement mechanism would strengthen the buyer's position while providing innovators with more predictable volumes and clearer access timelines.34 Aggregating demand at the EU level could further reduce duplication in HTA and market access procedures. Regional alliances already illustrate the feasibility of such coordination: the BeNeLuxA Initiative, the Valletta Declaration Group, the Visegrad group, and the Nordic Pharmaceutical Forum have conducted joint assessments and joint negotiations for high-cost therapies, including advanced and orphan treatments.35,36 For instance, in 2024, BeNeLuxA successfully concluded joint pricing negotiations for Libmeldy, a treatment designed for Metachromatic Leukodystrophy (MLD).37 In 2021, the alliance had negotiated an agreement for Zolgensma, based on the success of the first joint clinical assessment of Spinraza.37 These experiences suggest that expanding cooperation, whether at full EU level or through ‘coalitions of the willing’, is institutionally feasible. However, they do not in themselves provide robust public evidence on the size of price reductions achieved, the long-term sustainability of such arrangements, or their generalisability across the EU. Public evaluation remains limited, in part because confidentiality around negotiated discounts and net prices constrains independent assessment.

A pan-EU procurement framework could be complemented with advance market commitments (AMCs).38,39 By guaranteeing a minimum purchase volume or revenue floor, AMCs support investment in high-risk therapeutic areas. In exchange, contracts could incorporate claw-back or profit-sharing mechanisms when realised revenues exceed projections, thereby improving the balance between incentives and affordability. The pneumococcal vaccine AMC (2009–2015), which mobilised USD 1.5 billion from donors to accelerate access in low-income countries, illustrates how such mechanisms can both stimulate supply and expand access. Joint procurement also addresses recurrent concerns expressed by both industry and Member States. For companies, it mitigates the inefficiencies associated with negotiating separately in 27 jurisdictions. For governments, it offers a pathway to greater price transparency and fairer distribution of costs. Such mechanisms could complement current reform proposals linking extended market exclusivity to EU-wide launch commitments by making coordinated access economically viable. Managed access agreements and other innovative pricing and reimbursement schemes may also complement joint procurement, particularly for HUMN conditions characterised by substantial evidentiary uncertainty at launch. By linking early access to stepwise generation of real-world evidence, such arrangements can allow provisional reimbursement with subsequent price reassessment or reset. However, this approach also entails substantial practical complexity, since the collection of sufficiently robust and decision-relevant data is difficult unless the evidence requirements are clearly specified from the outset. Also, their effectiveness is limited by the frequent lack of transparency surrounding confidential discounts and the actual net prices paid by health systems. This opacity can generate spillover effects in external reference pricing and subsequent negotiations, thereby contributing to cumulative upward pressure on prices. For this reason, such agreements could be further developed to complement broader structural reforms. Some initiatives borne by payers already provide valuable templates.40

We recognize that pan-EU joint procurement raises political and distributive challenges. Uniform pricing may reduce parallel trade and inequitable access but could increase budgetary pressure in lower-income Member States. These distributional effects could be mitigated through internal financial adjustments, for example allocating part of the savings generated through collective bargaining to solidarity transfers or using structural instruments to support equitable access. A detailed quantitative assessment of such a mechanism is warranted but lies beyond the scope of this commentary.

In sum, joint procurement of OMPs represents a pragmatic and potentially high-impact reform to address Europe's structural market fragmentation. Carefully designed, it could strengthen negotiating leverage, enhance predictability for innovators, and improve timely and equitable access to OMPs across the Union. For rare diseases without any available solution, such procurement procedures may be worth exploring on a case-by-case basis, particularly where they can strengthen negotiating capacity and improve predictability of access. Their benefits, however, should not be presumed, as feasibility and impact will depend on legal, institutional, and pricing conditions across participating countries.

Conclusion

The evolution of orphan drug legislation has undeniably catalysed innovation in rare disease therapeutics. However, it has also generated unintended consequences, including high launch prices, heterogeneous access across Member States, and persistent gaps in unmet medical needs. Although the EU regulatory framework initially succeeded in attracting investment into small and previously neglected markets, its current configuration requires recalibration to restore balance between incentives for innovation and long-term affordability.

The proposed EU Pharma Package represents a constructive step forward, but it does not yet amount to a comprehensive strategic reorientation. The Draghi Report and the EU Biotech Act identify important structural reforms to strengthen competitiveness and reduce fragmentation, yet additional measures are required to address the specific economic and regulatory features of rare disease markets.

A more coherent approach would combine regulatory simplification with targeted public investment, strengthened public-private partnerships, structured risk-sharing mechanisms (including special purpose vehicles), regulatory sandboxing for novel therapies, and coordinated procurement strategies. Such an integrated framework would better align industrial policy with public health objectives, support sustainable innovation, and reduce inequities in access across the Union.

Reform of the orphan drug landscape should therefore move beyond incremental adjustment towards a mission-oriented strategy that recognises rare diseases as both a biomedical and societal priority. With coordinated and forward-looking action, Europe can reinforce its leadership in rare disease research while ensuring that incentives for the development of orphan drugs are proportionate and patient access to these therapies is equitable.
