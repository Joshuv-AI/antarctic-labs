Age-related vulnerability and clinical outcomes in acute pancreatitis: a stratified cohort study of 1,034 patients
Authors: Bingjun Yu, Yunshan Li, Nianshuang Li, Lingyu Luo, Yupeng Lei, Xin Huang, Xiaoyu Yang, Cong He, Liang Xia, Wenhua He, Nonghua Lu, Yin Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute pancreatitis (AP) demonstrates substantial heterogeneity across adult age groups; however, the relative contributions of chronological age, baseline vulnerability, acute organ dysfunction, and local disease complexity to short-term outcomes remain incompletely understood.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 1,034 adults admitted with AP to the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University between January 2018 and April 2021. Patients were stratified into three age groups (18–40, 41–60, and 61–80 years). Clinical characteristics, organ dysfunction, local complications, interventions, and short-term outcomes were compared across age groups. Organ failure and local complications were defined according to the Revised Atlanta Classification. Multivariable regression analyses were performed to identify factors independently associated with adverse outcomes, ICU admission, and length of hospital stay (LOS).ResultsHypertriglyceridemia predominated in younger adults, whereas biliary pancreatitis was most common in older patients. Older adults demonstrated higher bilirubin, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, BISAP, and APACHE II scores. Adverse outcomes increased with age; however, after adjustment, chronological age showed only a modest independent association with adverse outcomes (adjusted OR 1.07 per 1-year increase, 95% CI 1.04–1.09). In contrast, acute respiratory failure remained the strongest predictor of adverse outcomes (adjusted OR 15.6, 95% CI 7.69–33.97). ICU admission and prolonged hospitalization were driven primarily by acute organ dysfunction and local disease complexity rather than chronological age alone.ConclusionAcute pancreatitis exhibits clear age-related differences in etiology and clinical presentation. However, short-term adverse outcomes are driven predominantly by acute organ dysfunction and local disease complexity, whereas chronological age may reflect increased baseline vulnerability rather than acting as the primary determinant of disease trajectory. Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is an acute inflammatory disorder of the pancreas that ranges from mild and self-limiting disease to severe forms characterized by persistent organ failure and systemic complications, resulting in substantial morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Although most patients experience a favorable clinical course, approximately 15–20% progress to severe acute pancreatitis (SAP), which is associated with prolonged hospitalization, intensive care utilization, and significantly increased risk of death [3–6]. The revised Atlanta classification provides a clinically relevant framework for stratifying disease severity based on organ failure and local complications [7, 8]. Meanwhile, epidemiological data indicate that the global incidence of AP continues to rise, imposing an increasing clinical and healthcare burden across diverse populations [9–11].

Age is a key modifier of disease presentation and outcomes in AP. Older patients are more likely to develop persistent systemic inflammation, organ dysfunction, and adverse outcomes compared with younger individuals [12, 13]. Mortality rises with advancing age, particularly once persistent organ failure occurs [14, 15]. The heightened susceptibility of elderly patients is thought to reflect reduced physiological reserve, impaired immunoregulatory capacity, and greater burden of comorbid conditions, which collectively limit tolerance to acute inflammatory stress and increase vulnerability to systemic decompensation [16, 17].

In addition to influencing disease severity, age is closely associated with the distribution of etiologies in AP. Population-based and multi-centre cohort studies consistently demonstrate that biliary pancreatitis becomes increasingly predominant with advancing age, whereas hypertriglyceridaemia-associated and alcohol-related pancreatitis occur more frequently in younger adults [18–22]. These age-dependent etiologic patterns carry direct clinical implications: biliary pancreatitis often requires timely biliary source control strategies, while hypertriglyceridaemia-associated pancreatitis may benefit from early lipid-lowering therapies and metabolic risk management [23–25]. This divergence suggests that AP across the adult lifespan encompasses distinct clinical phenotypes with differing underlying pathophysiology and therapeutic priorities.

Despite growing recognition that age meaningfully modifies the clinical presentation and trajectory of acute pancreatitis (AP), systematic evidence comparing multiple adult age strata remains limited—particularly in settings where metabolic and biliary risk profiles are evolving over time. Most existing studies have contrasted AP outcomes only between broadly defined “young” and “elderly” groups, rather than examining gradients across the adult lifespan. Moreover, prior work has often focused on mortality or disease severity in isolation, with less emphasis on parallel, age-stratified comparisons of etiologic profiles, systemic inflammatory responses, organ dysfunction indicators, and clinical management strategies. This gap is especially relevant in regions such as China, where both metabolic risk and biliary disease patterns have been changing rapidly, underscoring the need for updated age-stratified evidence.

Therefore, we aimed to compare the aetiology, biochemical features, disease severity, management strategies, and clinical outcomes of AP across three predefined age groups (18–40, 41–60, and 61–80 years). By clarifying the relative contributions of age, organ dysfunction, and local disease complexity to short-term outcomes, our findings may facilitate earlier risk stratification and support age-informed management strategies for AP.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Approval No. 2011001). Informed consent was obtained from all patients during hospitalization and documented in the electronic inpatient database.

Study population

We collected data retrospectively from January 2018 to April 2021 from the electronic inpatient database. A total of 2,523 patients with AP were screened, and 1,034 were included. The exclusion criteria are depicted in Fig. 1.

Study cohort flow diagram. Flowchart of patient selection from January 2018 to April 2021. From 2,523 records, adults aged 18–80 years were retained (n = 2,398); patients admitted > 3 days after onset were excluded (n = 1,024). Additional exclusions were chronic pancreatitis (n = 5), recurrent pancreatitis (n = 285), pregnancy-related pancreatitis (n = 39), and other pancreatic diseases (n = 11), yielding the final analytic cohort (n = 1,034). Abbreviations: AP, acute pancreatitis

Two or more of the following three characteristics were used as a basis to diagnose acute pancreatitis: (1) Acute onset of persistent epigastric pain, usually radiating backwards; (2) Amylase activity (or serum lipase activity) at least 3 times higher than the upper normal level; (3) Typical signs of pancreatitis on abdominal imaging [3]. Definitions of etiologies, severity of AP, pancreatic complications, and organ failure follow the Revised Atlanta Classification [3], detailed diagnostic criteria are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Data collection and variable definitions

Data included demographic characteristics, etiology of AP, history of diabetes and hypertension, smoking, drinking, type of organ failure, shock, operative treatment, total hospital stay and ICU stay, local complications, death and laboratory results. The Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score and severity of AP were calculated based on patient data.

Patients were categorized into three predefined age groups (18–40, 41–60, and 61–80 years), based on epidemiologic and clinical distinctions related to metabolic risk, biliary disease prevalence, and physiologic reserve. Clinical outcomes included development of local or systemic complications, ICU admission, need for organ support interventions, length of hospital stay, and in-hospital mortality. Information on prior outside-hospital treatment was systematically recorded and included in baseline comparisons. This variable reflects whether patients had received any medical treatment at another healthcare facility prior to admission, rather than formal inter-hospital transfer, and was included to account for potential differences in pre-admission management.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when approximately normally distributed and as median (interquartile range, IQR) otherwise. Categorical variables are summarized as number (percentage). Differences among the three age strata (18–40, 41–60, and 61–80 years) were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, and the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Adverse outcomes were defined as in-hospital death or critically ill discharge. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated to quantify baseline comorbidity burden, and CCI > 0 was included as an adjustment variable in multivariable analyses.

For outcome modeling, univariable regression analyses were first performed for candidate predictors. Variables with statistical significance in univariable analyses and/or considered clinically relevant a priori were considered for inclusion in multivariable models. To reduce model overfitting and avoid multicollinearity, conceptually overlapping variables reflecting similar clinical pathways were not simultaneously included in the same model. Variable selection was additionally guided by clinical interpretability and the limited number of adverse outcome events. For adverse outcomes modeling, chronological age was entered as a continuous variable to improve model stability and reduce overparameterization given the limited number of adverse outcome events.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors independently associated with adverse outcomes and ICU admission, while multivariable linear regression was used to evaluate factors associated with hospital length of stay (LOS). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and all included variables demonstrated acceptable collinearity (all VIFs ≤ 3.64). Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) or regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Etiologic distribution differed substantially by age (Table 1). Hypertriglyceridemia-associated AP predominated in younger adults (72.9% in 18–40) but declined sharply with age (3.6% in 61–80; P < 0.001). Conversely, biliary AP increased progressively and became the leading etiology in older adults (14.6% in 18–40 vs. 81.8% in 61–80; P < 0.001). Alcohol-related AP also decreased with age (15.0% vs. 5.5%; P = 0.001).

Comorbidity profiles also differed across age groups. Hypertension was more common in older adults (7.1% in 18–40 vs. 33.6% in 61–80; P < 0.001), whereas diabetes prevalence was similar across strata (P = 0.712). Smoking and drinking were more common in younger patients (smoking: 42.9% vs. 24.8%; drinking: 46.1% vs. 21.2%; both P < 0.001).

ICU admission rates were comparable across age groups (22.5%–25.7%; P = 0.647), whereas adverse outcomes increased with age. Severity categories defined by the revised Atlanta classification (MAP/MSAP/SAP) were similar among the three groups (P = 0.783), while APACHE II scores increased substantially with age (7.75 ± 4.07 vs. 11.70 ± 4.42; P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Laboratory indicators and severity scores

Laboratory profiles differed significantly across age groups (Table 2). TG levels were highest in patients aged 18–40 years (8.995 ± 9.996 mmol/L) and decreased markedly with age (1.595 ± 3.721 mmol/L in the 61–80 group; P < 0.001). In contrast, markers associated with cholestatic and renal stress increased with age, including TBIL (19.593 ± 14.819 vs. 34.706 ± 31.391 µmol/L; P < 0.001), BUN (5.016 ± 2.907 vs. 7.262 ± 5.102 mmol/L; P < 0.001), and creatinine (77.672 ± 50.114 vs. 92.878 ± 84.990 µmol/L; P = 0.038). Albumin levels decreased progressively with age (39.743 ± 5.635 vs. 36.112 ± 5.031 g/L; P < 0.001).

Predictors of adverse outcomes

Multivariable logistic regression analysis for adverse outcomes is presented in Table 4. Chronological age remained independently associated with adverse outcomes, with each 1-year increase in age associated with a higher risk of adverse outcomes (OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.04–1.09; P < 0.001). Acute respiratory failure was strongly associated with adverse outcomes (OR 15.6, 95% CI 7.69–33.97; P < 0.001), followed by PCD (OR 3.46, 95% CI 1.69–7.12; P < 0.001). In contrast, CCI > 0 was not independently associated with adverse outcomes after adjustment (P = 0.749).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort, we identified distinct age-related patterns in the clinical presentation, management, and outcomes of acute pancreatitis (AP). After adjustment, chronological age remained independently associated with adverse outcomes, whereas ICU admission and prolonged hospitalization (LOS) were driven primarily by acute organ dysfunction and local disease complexity.

In multivariable analyses, acute respiratory failure and PCD were strongly associated with adverse outcomes, whereas chronological age demonstrated a more modest but independent association. In contrast, age was not independently associated with ICU admission or prolonged hospitalization after adjustment for organ dysfunction and local complications. Together, these findings indicate that short-term clinical deterioration in AP is driven primarily by acute systemic and local disease burden, while aging may contribute more substantially to reduced resilience once severe complications occur.

Etiologic patterns across age groups

Consistent with prior epidemiologic reports [18–22, 26–28], hypertriglyceridemia (HTG) was more common in younger adults, whereas biliary pancreatitis increased with advancing age. The predominance of hypertriglyceridemia-associated pancreatitis among younger adults may, in part, reflect broader epidemiologic trends toward increased metabolic risk in younger populations. While regional characteristics could influence etiologic distribution, this pattern aligns with reports from other cohorts experiencing a rising burden of dyslipidemia and metabolic syndrome. The markedly elevated triglyceride levels in the youngest group support experimental and clinical evidence that excess triglycerides and free fatty acids can trigger endothelial injury, acinar dysfunction, and an exaggerated inflammatory response, increasing susceptibility to severe AP [19, 21, 27]. In contrast, the age-related rise in biliary pancreatitis likely reflects the increased prevalence of gallstone formation, age-dependent alterations in bile composition, and impaired gallbladder motility in older adults [6, 29–34]. In addition, delayed symptom recognition and prolonged biliary obstruction may occur in older patients due to reduced visceral pain sensitivity and a higher burden of comorbid conditions [35–39]. Together, these findings support age-adapted diagnostic priorities, emphasizing early metabolic stabilization in younger adults with suspected HTG-associated pancreatitis and timely biliary evaluation in older patients [23–25, 40–43]. Importantly, although etiologic patterns differed substantially across age groups, chronological age alone was not sufficient to explain differences in ICU admission or hospital length of stay after adjustment. This suggests that the impact of aging in AP may be mediated more through reduced physiological resilience and vulnerability to complications rather than through etiology itself.

Biochemical profiles and severity indicators

Laboratory parameters also differed across age strata. Younger patients exhibited higher triglyceride levels, while older adults demonstrated higher bilirubin, BUN, and creatinine, reflecting greater cholestatic and renal stress and reduced physiological reserve [44–47]. These findings are consistent with previous research identifying renal parameters as important early indicators of systemic involvement and severity in AP [45–47].

Severity scores showed a parallel gradient. APACHE II and BISAP scores increased progressively with age, whereas Marshall scores varied minimally. This pattern suggests that scoring systems incorporating age, renal function, or systemic inflammatory response may be more sensitive to age-related vulnerability than purely organ-based tools [48–50]. Prior studies have shown that older adults have diminished immune responsiveness and reduced capacity for homeostatic compensation [12, 13, 51–53], and the current findings are consistent with this framework. Notably, despite higher APACHE II and BISAP scores in older adults, chronological age was not independently associated with ICU admission or prolonged hospitalization after adjustment, suggesting that age-related vulnerability may influence outcomes through reduced resilience to complications rather than greater initial disease burden alone.

Treatment patterns and age-specific considerations

Differences in management across age groups largely mirrored underlying etiologies. ERCP use increased markedly in older adults, which is consistent with the predominance of biliary disease and guideline recommendations supporting early endoscopic intervention in the presence of cholangitis or suspected biliary obstruction [23–25, 54].

Mechanical ventilation duration was longer among older adults, potentially reflecting increased susceptibility to respiratory deterioration during systemic inflammation [55, 56]. In contrast, renal replacement therapy remained uncommon across all age groups, suggesting that the need for renal support may be driven more by the severity and trajectory of organ dysfunction than by chronological age alone [44, 45, 57, 58]. Overall, these findings support the importance of integrating etiologic information with early physiological assessment when planning individualized management strategies for patients with AP.

Organ failure and clinical outcomes

We found that acute organ dysfunction and local disease complexity were the primary determinants of adverse outcomes, ICU admission, and prolonged hospitalization. Notably, chronological age itself was not independently associated with ICU admission after adjustment, whereas acute respiratory failure, ANC, and PCD were strongly associated with ICU admission. This finding reinforces that escalation of care in acute pancreatitis is driven primarily by dynamic organ dysfunction rather than age alone. This agrees with previous evidence showing that persistent organ failure is a major determinant of fatal outcome in AP [59–61]. In our cohort, respiratory failure exhibited the strongest association with adverse outcomes, echoing national and international data showing that respiratory dysfunction in AP is closely linked to escalated critical-care resource use and adverse outcomes [56, 62–64].

The lungs are especially vulnerable during systemic inflammation, with cytokine-mediated endothelial injury and increased alveolar–capillary permeability contributing to early hypoxemia and ARDS [49, 50, 62, 63]. The strong association between respiratory failure and poor outcomes in our cohort supports close respiratory monitoring and, when appropriate, early use of non-invasive or protective ventilation strategies.

Local complications—including acute necrotic collection (ANC) and walled-off necrosis (WON)—were also associated with prolonged hospitalization, consistent with studies showing that complex necrotizing collections and the procedures required to manage them contribute substantially to healthcare burden [44, 59, 65–68]. Integrating these findings, our results indicate that while age influences baseline vulnerability, it is systemic inflammation, organ failure, and local disease complexity that ultimately determine the clinical trajectory.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the relatively large sample size, predefined age stratification, and comprehensive characterization of etiologies, laboratory features, severity indices, interventions, and short-term outcomes. The multivariable models further enabled comparison of the relative contributions of age, organ dysfunction, and local complications to adverse outcomes, ICU admission, and LOS.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center design may limit generalizability, and management decisions (e.g., timing of ERCP, ICU transfer, and initiation of organ support) may reflect local practice patterns. Second, although key variables included in the analyses were complete in this dataset, residual confounding remains possible due to unmeasured clinical dimensions, particularly frailty, sarcopenia, functional reserve, treatment limitations, granular comorbidity burden, and potential genetic susceptibility related to lipid metabolism, which may partially mediate the observed association between age and adverse outcomes. Genetic data were not available in this cohort, and future studies incorporating genetic information may help clarify predisposition to hypertriglyceridemia-associated pancreatitis, especially in younger adults. Third, variability in pre-admission management across referring institutions may also have introduced heterogeneity in early treatment pathways. Fourth, we did not assess long-term outcomes such as recurrence, exocrine/endocrine function, or quality of life. Finally, biomarkers reflecting immune response and nutritional status were not systematically collected, which may be relevant to age-related vulnerability.

Implications and future directions

Our findings provide an age-informed interpretation of risk in AP. After adjustment for organ dysfunction and local disease complexity, chronological age remained independently associated with adverse outcomes, whereas ICU admission and prolonged hospitalization were driven primarily by acute systemic and local complications. These findings suggest that aging in AP may reflect increased physiological vulnerability and reduced resilience to complications rather than greater initial disease severity alone. Clinically, this supports integrating etiologic information with early physiologic assessment to guide triage, monitoring, and individualized management across different age groups.

Future studies should validate these findings in multicenter prospective cohorts and further investigate mechanisms underlying age-related vulnerability in AP. In particular, integrating frailty assessment, dynamic organ-function trajectories, nutritional status, and molecular or genetic markers may improve risk stratification beyond chronological age alone. Age-adapted management pathways—such as early metabolic optimization in younger adults with HTG-associated AP and proactive biliary evaluation and respiratory surveillance in older adults—may also help improve short-term outcomes.

Conclusion

In this large retrospective cohort, acute pancreatitis revealed distinct age-related clinical phenotypes in etiology, biochemical profiles, and management patterns. Hypertriglyceridemia predominated in younger adults, whereas biliary pancreatitis was most common in older patients. Although categorical severity classifications were comparable across age groups, continuous severity indicators and markers of organ dysfunction increased progressively with age.

After multivariable adjustment, chronological age remained independently associated with adverse outcomes, whereas ICU admission and prolonged hospitalization were driven primarily by acute organ dysfunction and local disease complexity. These findings suggest that aging in AP may reflect increased physiological vulnerability rather than greater initial disease severity alone. Clinically, our results support age-informed assessment strategies while emphasizing the importance of early recognition and management of evolving organ dysfunction and local complications across the adult age spectrum.
