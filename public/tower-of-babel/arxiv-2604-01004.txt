Regularizations of point charges, the Liénard-Wiechert potential, and the electron self-energy
Günther Hörmann, Nathalie Tassotti

Abstract
. We apply Colombeau-type regularization to the electromagnetic field of a point-charge and show how the Liénard-Wiechert potential can be derived from a generalized function based on the geometry of Minkowski space. Furthermore, for a charged particle in its rest frame, we discuss the electric monopole, magnetic dipole, electron singularity, and self-energy.

Generalized functions and coherence properties

For the convenience of the reader, we review the very basics from the theory of Colombeau generalized functions. It can be considered an extension of the so-called
sequential approach to distributions
, where each distribution is represented by approximating regularizing weakly convergent sequences modulo null sequences.

Colombeau regularization methods model nonsmooth objects by approximating nets of smooth functions, regardless of convergence, but with conditions of
moderate
asymptotics, and identify regularizing nets whose differences compared to the moderateness scale are
negligible
. A comprehensive modern introduction to Colombeau theory is
[
4
]
, although we will also make use of constructions and notations from
[
3
]
, where generalized functions based on a locally convex topological vector space
E
are defined. Let the topology on
E
be given by the family of seminorms
\{p_{j}\}_{j\in J}
, then the elements of

\mathcal{M}_{E}:=\{(u_{\varepsilon})\in E^{(0,1]}:\,\forall j\in J\,\,\exists N\in\mathbb{N}\quad p_{j}(u_{\varepsilon})=O(\varepsilon^{-N})\,\text{as}\,\varepsilon\to 0\}

and

\mathcal{N}_{E}:=\{(u_{\varepsilon})\in E^{(0,1]}:\,\forall j\in J\,\,\forall q\in\mathbb{N}\quad p_{j}(u_{\varepsilon})=O(\varepsilon^{q})\,\text{as}\,\varepsilon\to 0\},

are called
E
-moderate
and
E
-negligible
, respectively. With respect to the componentwise operations, e.g.,
(u_{\varepsilon})+(v_{\varepsilon}):=(u_{\varepsilon}+v_{\varepsilon})
etc.,
\mathcal{N}_{E}
becomes a vector subspace of
\mathcal{M}_{E}
. The
Generalized functions based on
E
are defined as the factor space
\mathcal{G}_{E}:=\mathcal{M}_{E}/\mathcal{N}_{E}
. If
E
is a differential algebra, i.e., an associative commutative algebra possessing commuting linear maps
\partial_{j}\colon E\to E
(
j=1,\ldots,n
) that satisfy the Leibniz rule
\partial_{j}(f\cdot g)=(\partial_{j}f)\cdot g+f\cdot\partial_{j}g
, then
\mathcal{N}_{E}
is an ideal in
\mathcal{M}_{E}
and
\mathcal{G}_{E}
is a differential algebra as well.

Specific choices of the underlying space
E
yield the standard Colombeau algebras of generalized functions. For example, equipping
E=\mathbb{C}
with the absolute value gives the generalized complex numbers
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}=\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
. If
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is open, then
E=\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with the topology of compact uniform convergence of all derivatives provides the so-called special Colombeau algebra
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)}=\mathcal{G}(\Omega)
. Recall that
\Omega\mapsto\mathcal{G}(\Omega)
is a fine sheaf, thus, in particular, the restriction
u|_{B}
of
u\in\mathcal{G}(\Omega)
to an arbitrary open subset
B\subseteq\Omega
is well-defined and yields
u|_{B}\in\mathcal{G}(B)
. Moreover, we may embed
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
into
\mathcal{G}(\Omega)
by appropriate localization and convolution regularization.

If
E\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
, then certain generalized functions can be projected into the space of distributions by taking weak limits: We say that
u\in\mathcal{G}_{E}
is
associated
with
w\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
, if
u_{\varepsilon}\to w
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
holds for any (hence every) representative
(u_{\varepsilon})
of
u
. This fact is also denoted by
u\approx w
.

On an open strip of the form
\Omega_{T}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\,]0,T[\,\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
(with
T>0
arbitrary) we have the spaces
E=H^{\infty}({\Omega_{T}})=\{h\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega_{T}):\partial^{\alpha}h\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T})\;\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}\}
with the family of (semi-)norms

{\left\|h\right\|}_{H^{k}}=\Big(\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}{\left\|\partial^{\alpha}h\right\|}_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\quad(k\in\mathbb{N}),

or also

E=W^{\infty,\infty}({\Omega_{T}})=\{h\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega_{T}):\partial^{\alpha}h\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})\;\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}\}
with the family of (semi-)norms

{\left\|h\right\|}_{W^{k,\infty}}=\max_{|\alpha|\leq k}{\left\|\partial^{\alpha}h\right\|}_{L^{\infty}}\quad(k\in\mathbb{N}).

Clearly,
\Omega_{T}
satisfies the strong local Lipschitz property
[
1
, Chapter IV, 4.6, p. 66]
, hence every element of
H^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
and
W^{\infty,\infty}(\Omega_{T})
belongs to
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{T}})
by the Sobolev embedding theorem
[
1
, Chapter V, Theorem 5.4, Part II, p. 98]
.

We will occasionally use the notation

\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]):=\mathcal{G}_{H^{\infty}({\Omega_{T}})}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]):=\mathcal{G}_{W^{\infty,\infty}({\Omega_{T}})}
.
A generalized function
u\in\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])
is thus represented by a net
(u_{\varepsilon})
with
u_{\varepsilon}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\,]0,T[)
and the moderateness property

\forall k\,\exists m:\quad{\left\|u_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{H^{k}}=O(\varepsilon^{-m})\quad(\varepsilon\to 0).

If
(\widetilde{u_{\varepsilon}})
is another representative of
u
, then

\forall k\,\forall p:\quad{\left\|u_{\varepsilon}-\widetilde{u_{\varepsilon}}\right\|}_{H^{k}}=O(\varepsilon^{p})\quad(\varepsilon\to 0).

Similar constructions and notations are used in case of
E=H^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
E=W^{\infty,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Note that by Young’s inequality (
[
2
, Proposition 8.9.(a)]
) any standard convolution regularization with a scaled mollifier of Schwartz class provides embeddings
L^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{G}_{2}
and
L^{p}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
(
1\leq p\leq\infty
).

Colombeau-generalized functions from
\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])
have been employed successfully in the context of quantum physics, namely as solutions to the Cauchy problem for the Schrödinger equation. On the one hand, results on existence, uniqueness, and coherence—in the form of association with distributional solutions—have been established (cf.
[
16
]
). On the other hand, also limiting behavior of particular solutions, asymptotics of scattering, and spectral properties have been studied in this context (cf.
[
15
,
14
]
).

2.1.
Set-up and notation

The underlying spacetime is Minkowski space

With

For given

i.e.,

is a future-directed null four-vector for every

Notation:
For a quantity

Implicit differentiation involving the retarded proper time

Let

Differentiation of

(or

Lemma 2.3
.

It holds that

(7)

\partial_{\mu}\widetilde{E}=\widetilde{\underline{\partial}_{\mu}E}+\widetilde{K}^{\mu}\cdot\widetilde{\partial_{\tau}E},

which means in detail

(8)

\partial_{\mu}\Big(E(X,\tau_{r}(X))\Big)=\underline{\partial}_{\mu}E(X,\tau_{r}(X))+K^{\mu}(X,\tau_{r}(X))\cdot\partial_{\tau}E(X,\tau_{r}(X)).

Proof.

We have from the chain rule

\partial_{\mu}\Big(E(X,\tau_{r}(X))\Big)=\underline{\partial}_{\mu}E(X,\tau_{r}(X))+\partial_{\tau}E(X,\tau_{r}(X))\cdot\underline{\partial}_{\mu}\tau_{r}(X)

and it remains to show that
\underline{\partial}_{\mu}\tau_{r}=\widetilde{K}^{\mu}
.

Recalling

X_{\nu}-Z_{\nu}(\tau_{r}(X))=R_{\nu}(X,\tau_{r}(X))

and applying
\underline{\partial}_{\mu}
leads to

\delta_{\mu\nu}-\dot{Z}_{\nu}(\tau_{r}(X))\underline{\partial}_{\mu}\tau_{r}(X)=\underline{\partial}_{\mu}\Big(R_{\nu}(X,\tau_{r}(X))\Big).

Upon multiplication by
\widetilde{R}^{\nu}
and using
\widetilde{R}_{\nu}\widetilde{R}^{\nu}=0
(with summation over
\nu
) we obtain

\widetilde{R}^{\mu}-\underbrace{(\dot{Z}_{\nu}\circ\tau_{r})\cdot\widetilde{R}^{\nu}}_{\widetilde{\xi}}\cdot\underline{\partial}_{\mu}\tau_{r}=\frac{1}{2}\underline{\partial}_{\mu}(\underbrace{\widetilde{R}_{\nu}\widetilde{R}^{\nu}}_{=0}),

hence
\widetilde{R}^{\mu}-\widetilde{\xi}\cdot\underline{\partial}_{\mu}\tau_{r}=0
and therefore

\underline{\partial}_{\mu}\tau_{r}=\frac{\widetilde{R}^{\mu}}{\widetilde{\xi}}=\widetilde{K}^{\mu},

which implies (
7
).
∎

By analogous reasoning we also get

(
\text{\ref{3.5}}^{\prime}
)

\partial^{\mu}\widetilde{E}=\widetilde{\underline{\partial}^{\mu}E}+\widetilde{K}_{\mu}\cdot\widetilde{\partial_{\tau}E},

which we will apply immediately in the proof of (ii) in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

We have

(i)
\partial_{\tau}\xi=\xi\kappa-1
,

(ii)
\underline{\partial}^{\mu}\xi=\dot{Z}_{\mu}
and
\underline{\partial}_{\mu}\xi=\dot{Z}^{\mu}
,

(iii)
\nabla\widetilde{\xi}(X)\neq 0
for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

Proof.

(i): Recall
\xi=\dot{Z}_{\mu}R^{\mu}=\dot{Z}_{\mu}(X^{\mu}-Z^{\mu})
to obtain

\partial_{\tau}\xi=\ddot{Z}_{\mu}\underbrace{R^{\mu}}_{=\xi K^{\mu}}-\underbrace{\dot{Z}_{\mu}\dot{Z}^{\mu}}_{=1}=\xi\kappa-1.

(ii): Follows from
\underline{\partial}^{\mu}\xi=\partial_{X^{\mu}}(\dot{Z}_{\alpha}R^{\alpha})=\dot{Z}_{\alpha}\,\partial_{X^{\mu}}(X^{\alpha}-Z^{\alpha})=\dot{Z}_{\mu}
etc.

(iii): Applying (i) in the second step and (ii) in the third step, we obtain

\partial^{\mu}\widetilde{\xi}=\widetilde{\underline{\partial}^{\mu}\xi}+\widetilde{K}_{\mu}\widetilde{\partial_{\tau}\xi}=\widetilde{\underline{\partial}^{\mu}\xi}+\widetilde{K}_{\mu}(\widetilde{\xi}\widetilde{\kappa}-1)=\widetilde{\dot{Z}^{\mu}}+\frac{\widetilde{R}_{\mu}}{\widetilde{\xi}}(\widetilde{\xi}\widetilde{\kappa}-1)=\widetilde{\dot{Z}^{\mu}}+\widetilde{R}_{\mu}\widetilde{\kappa}-\frac{\widetilde{R}_{\mu}}{\widetilde{\xi}}.

Therefore, the equation
\partial^{\mu}\widetilde{\xi}=0
is equivalent to

\widetilde{\dot{Z}^{\mu}}=\left(\frac{1}{\widetilde{\xi}}-\widetilde{\kappa}\right)\widetilde{R}_{\mu},

which is impossible to obtain, because
\dot{Z}
is timelike while
\widetilde{R}
is null.
∎

3.
Regularization of the electromagnetic self-interaction energy of a point particle at rest

We consider now the situation of a charged particle in its rest frame. The static case of the Liénard-Wiechert potential yields the Coulomb potential (cf.
[

[
9
]
, we discuss a regularization approach in describing the electric monopole, magnetic dipole, electron singularity, and self-energy. Our analysis is more rigorous and for a class of regularizations not restricted to convolution techniques.

To begin with, we rewrite the potential
[
9
, Equation (6.1)]
as generalized function on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in the form

(11)

\phi(x)=e\frac{H(|x|)}{|x|},

where, as in (
4
),
H
denotes a regularization of the Heaviside function
\theta
. More precisely, we assume
H
to be represented by a family
(H_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
of smooth functions
H_{\varepsilon}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with the following properties:

(i)

H_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
and
H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\geq 0
,

(ii)

H_{\varepsilon}(r)=0
if
r\leq\varepsilon
,

(iii)

H_{\varepsilon}(r)=1
if
r\geq 2\varepsilon
,

(iv)

\varepsilon H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}
is bounded uniformly with respect to
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

Note that (ii) enables us to define the right-hand side in (
11
) via the smooth representatives
x\mapsto H_{\varepsilon}(|x|)/|x|
,
\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
. An example of such a regularizing family can be achieved in the form

H_{\varepsilon}(r):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\theta(r-s)\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\chi\left(\frac{s}{\varepsilon}\right)\,ds=\int_{0}^{r}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\chi\left(\frac{s}{\varepsilon}\right)\,ds=\int_{0}^{r/\varepsilon}\chi(t)\,dt

with a mollifier
\chi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi(s)\,ds=1
,
\chi\geq 0
, and
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\chi\subseteq[1,2]
.

We may further calculate in the usual way, either with representatives or Colombeau classes, and obtain the electric field (
[
9
, Equation (6.2)]
)

E(x)=-\nabla\phi(x)=e\left(\frac{H(|x|)}{|x|^{2}}-\frac{H^{\prime}(|x|)}{|x|}\right)\frac{x}{|x|}

as well as the charge density
\rho
(
[
9
, Equation (6.3)]
) following from

4\pi\rho(x)=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}E(x)=-e\frac{H^{\prime\prime}(|x|)}{|x|}.

Proposition 3.1
.

We have
\rho\approx e\delta_{0}
.

Proof.

The action of
\rho_{\varepsilon}
on a test function
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is given by

-\frac{4\pi}{e}\langle\rho_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(|x|)}{|x|}\varphi(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{S^{2}}\frac{H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(r)}{r}\varphi(r\omega)\,r^{2}\,d\omega\,dr=\int_{0}^{\infty}rH_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(r)\overbrace{\int_{S^{2}}\varphi(r\omega)\,d\omega}^{(M\varphi)(r)}\,dr\\
=\underbrace{\left.H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)r(M\varphi)(r)\right|_{0}^{\infty}}_{0}-\;\int_{0}^{\infty}H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)\big(r(M\varphi)(r)\big)^{\prime}\,dr\\
=\int_{0}^{\infty}H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)\big((M\varphi)(r)+r(M\varphi)^{\prime}(r)\big)\,dr.

Note that
M\varphi
can be extended to
r<0

) and that
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}H_{\varepsilon}\subseteq[\varepsilon,\infty[

-\frac{4\pi}{e}\langle\rho_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)\big((M\varphi)(r)+r(M\varphi)^{\prime}(r)\big)\,dr=-\langle H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime},M\varphi+r(M\varphi)^{\prime}\rangle.

Since

-\frac{4\pi}{e}\langle\rho_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle\to-(M\varphi)(0)=-\int_{S^{2}}\varphi(0)\,d\omega=-4\pi\varphi(0)=-4\pi\langle\delta_{0},\varphi\rangle.

∎

The electric self-energy of the point charge is defined by integration of

|E(x)|^{2}=\frac{e^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\left(\frac{H(|x|)}{|x|}-H^{\prime}(|x|)\right)^{2},

namely, as the generalized number

U_{\text{ele}}:=\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|E(x)|^{2}\,dx.

A typical representative of
U_{\text{ele}}
, using spherical coordinates, is given by

U_{\text{ele}}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{e^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{H_{\varepsilon}(r)}{r}-H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)\right)^{2}\,dr=\frac{e^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{H_{\varepsilon}(r)^{2}}{r^{2}}\,dr+\frac{e^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)^{2}\,dr-e^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{H_{\varepsilon}(r)H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)}{r}\,dr,

where integration by parts in the last term shows
\int_{0}^{\infty}H_{\varepsilon}(r)H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)/r\,dr=\int_{0}^{\infty}H_{\varepsilon}(r)^{2}/r^{2}\,dr/2
, hence

U_{\text{ele}}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{e^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)^{2}\,dr.

Theorem 3.2
.

The generalized number
U_{\text{ele}}
is infinite in the sense that

U_{\text{ele}}^{\varepsilon}\to\infty\quad(\varepsilon\to 0).

Proof.

By assumptions (ii) and (iii) on
H_{\varepsilon}
we have
H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)=0
if
r<\varepsilon
or
r>2\varepsilon
, hence

a_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{2}{e^{2}}U_{\text{ele}}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{\varepsilon}^{2\varepsilon}H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)^{2}\,dr\geq 0.

Let
c_{\varepsilon}:=\max\{H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)\mid r\in[\varepsilon,2\varepsilon]\}
, recall
H_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon)=0
,
H_{\varepsilon}(2\varepsilon)=1
, and observe

1=H_{\varepsilon}(2\varepsilon)-H_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon)=\int_{\varepsilon}^{2\varepsilon}H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)\,dr\leq c_{\varepsilon}(2\varepsilon-\varepsilon)=\varepsilon c_{\varepsilon},

i.e.,
c_{\varepsilon}\geq 1/\varepsilon
. Defining

I_{\varepsilon}:=\{r\in\mathbb{R}\mid\varepsilon\leq r\leq 2\varepsilon\text{ and }\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\leq H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)\leq c_{\varepsilon}\}=(H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{-1}\left(\Big[\frac{1}{2\varepsilon},c_{\varepsilon}\Big]\right)

we have

1=\int_{\varepsilon}^{2\varepsilon}H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)\,dr=\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)\,dr+\!\!\!\!\int_{[\varepsilon,2\varepsilon]\setminus I_{\varepsilon}}\!\!\!\!H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)\,dr\leq c_{\varepsilon}\,|I_{\varepsilon}|+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\,\Big|[\varepsilon,2\varepsilon]\setminus I_{\varepsilon}\Big|\\
=c_{\varepsilon}\,|I_{\varepsilon}|+\frac{\varepsilon-|I_{\varepsilon}|}{2\varepsilon}=|I_{\varepsilon}|c_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}-|I_{\varepsilon}|\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2}+|I_{\varepsilon}|\Big(c_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\Big),

which leads to

\frac{1}{2}\leq|I_{\varepsilon}|\Big(c_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\Big).

The estimate
c_{\varepsilon}\geq 1/\varepsilon
gives
2\varepsilon c_{\varepsilon}-1\geq 2-1=1
, hence we may write

|I_{\varepsilon}|\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\varepsilon c_{\varepsilon}-1}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\varepsilon c_{\varepsilon}}=\frac{1}{2c_{\varepsilon}}

and obtain

a_{\varepsilon}=\int_{\varepsilon}^{2\varepsilon}H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)^{2}\,dr\geq\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)^{2}\,dr\geq\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\,dr=\frac{|I_{\varepsilon}|}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{8\varepsilon^{2}c_{\varepsilon}}=\frac{1}{8\varepsilon c_{\varepsilon}}\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}.

Note that assumption (iv) on the regularization
H_{\varepsilon}
implies that
\varepsilon c_{\varepsilon}
is bounded, independently of
\varepsilon\in]0,1]
, thus there exists
c_{0}>0
such that

a_{\varepsilon}\geq\frac{c_{0}}{\varepsilon}.

∎

in the context of determining the function
\Upsilon(\xi)=\xi\varphi(\xi)
. We will address this specific issue at the end of this appendix. Now we will focus on the general structure behind (
12
) without the context from geometry and physics inherent in the notation
\xi
and
\varphi
. Therefore, we determine the solutions
u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
of the distributional differential equation

(13)

tu^{\prime}+u=\delta_{0},

where
tu^{\prime}
denotes the multiplication of the distribution
u^{\prime}
by the smooth identity function on
\mathbb{R}
.
On the subdomain with
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
the equation simplifies to
tu^{\prime}+u=0
.
Hence, the solutions restricted to the subdomains
\mathbb{R}^{+}=\,]0,\infty[
und
\mathbb{R}^{-}=\,]-\infty,0[
are the classical solutions (
[
11
, Corollary 3.1.5]
) of the form

(14)

u(t)=\frac{c_{\pm}}{t}\quad(\pm t>0)

with arbitrary constants
c_{\pm}\in\mathbb{C}
.

Global distributional solutions

Suppose
u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
solves (
13
), then its restrictions to
\mathbb{R}^{\pm}
have to be given as above by

(15)

u|_{\mathbb{R}^{\pm}}=\frac{c_{\pm}}{t}.

Two distributional identities

In the sequel we will employ the formulae

(16)

t\cdot t_{+}^{-2}=t_{+}^{-1}\ \text{in\ }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})

and

(17)

t\cdot t_{+}^{-1}=\theta\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}).

A proof of both identities can be based on
[
11
, Equation (3.2.5)]
as definition of
t_{+}^{-2}
and
t_{+}^{-1}
and direct calculation of the product with the smooth function
t\mapsto t
.

Case 1,
\bm{c_{+}=c_{-}=0}

We have
u|_{\mathbb{R}^{\pm}}=0
, thus
u
has support in
\{0\}
and is therefore of the form (
[
11
, Theorem 2.3.4]
)

u=\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}\delta_{0}^{(k)}}.

The left-hand side of Equation (
13
) then reads

(18)

tu^{\prime}+u=\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}(t\delta_{0}^{(k+1)}+\delta_{0}^{(k)})}.

Using
[
11
, Exercise 3.1.17]
, i.e.,
t\delta_{0}^{(k+1)}=-\frac{(k+1)!}{k!}\delta_{0}^{(k)}=-(k+1)\delta_{0}^{(k)}
, implies

(19)

tu^{\prime}+u=-\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}k\delta_{0}^{(k)}}.

Hence
tu^{\prime}+u=\delta_{0}
forces
-\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}k\delta_{0}^{(k)}}=\delta_{0}
. Linear independence yields
a_{k}=0
for all
k\geq 1
and
0=a_{0}\cdot 0\cdot\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}
, a contradiction. We conclude that there is no solution in this case.

Case 2,
\bm{c_{+}\neq 0}
,
\bm{c_{-}=0}

We have

u|_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}=0
and
u|_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}=\frac{c_{+}}{t}
, hence

u=c_{+}t_{+}^{-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}\delta_{0}^{(k)}}\quad\text{and}\quad u^{\prime}=c_{+}\cdot(t_{+}^{-1})^{\prime}+\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}\delta_{0}^{(k+1)}}.

By
[
11
, Equation (3.2.2)
′′
]
,
(t_{+}^{-1})^{\prime}=-t_{+}^{-2}-\delta_{0}^{\prime}
, therefore,

tu^{\prime}=-c_{+}\cdot\underbrace{t(t_{+}^{-1})^{\prime}}_{t_{+}^{-1}}-\underbrace{c_{+}t\delta_{0}^{\prime}}_{-c_{+}\delta_{0}}+\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}\underbrace{t\delta_{0}^{(k+1)}}_{-(k+1)\delta_{0}^{(k)}}}.

Equation (
13
) now reads

(20)

tu^{\prime}+u=-c_{+}t_{+}^{-1}+c_{+}\delta_{0}-\sum_{k=0}^{N}{-a_{k}(k+1)\delta_{0}^{(k)}}+c_{+}t_{+}^{-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}\delta_{0}^{(k)}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}{ka_{k}\delta_{0}^{(k)}}+c_{+}\delta_{0}

and
tu^{\prime}+u=\delta_{0}
implies
c_{+}\delta_{0}-\sum_{k=1}^{N}{a_{k}k\delta_{0}^{(k)}}=\delta_{0}
. Linear independence gives
c_{+}\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}
, i.e.,
c_{+}=1
, and
a_{k}=0
for all
k\geq 1
, with
a_{0}
undetermined.

Thus we have
u=t_{+}^{-1}+a_{0}\delta_{0}
,
u^{\prime}=-t_{+}^{-2}-\delta_{0}^{\prime}+a_{0}\delta_{0}^{\prime}
and
tu^{\prime}=-\underbrace{tt_{+}^{-2}}_{t_{+}^{-1}}-\underbrace{t\delta_{0}^{\prime}}_{-\delta_{0}}+a_{0}\underbrace{t\delta_{0}^{\prime}}_{-\delta_{0}}
, to summarize,

(21)

tu^{\prime}+u=-t_{+}^{-1}+\delta_{0}+t_{+}^{-1}+a_{0}\delta_{0}-a_{0}\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}.

We conclude that
u=t_{+}^{-1}+a_{0}\delta_{0}
solves (
13
) for every
a_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
.

Case 3,
\bm{c_{+}=0}
,
\bm{c_{-}\neq 0}

Analogous to Case 2 with
u=-t_{-}^{-1}+a_{0}\delta_{0}
and
a_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
arbitrary.

Case 4,
\bm{c_{+}\neq 0}
,
\bm{c_{-}\neq 0}

We have

u=c_{+}t_{+}^{-1}+c_{-}t_{-}^{-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}\delta_{0}^{(k)}},

u^{\prime}=c_{+}\cdot(-t_{+}^{-2}-\delta_{0}^{\prime})+c_{-}(-t_{-}^{-2}+\delta_{0}^{\prime})+\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}\delta_{0}^{(k+1)}},

and

tu^{\prime}=-c_{+}\cdot\underbrace{t(t_{+}^{-2})}_{t_{+}^{-1}}-\underbrace{c_{+}t\delta_{0}^{\prime}}_{-c_{+}\delta_{0}}-c_{-}\cdot\underbrace{t(t_{-}^{-2})}_{t_{-}^{-1}}+\underbrace{c_{-}t\delta_{0}^{\prime}}_{-c_{-}\delta_{0}}+\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}\underbrace{t\delta_{0}^{(k+1)}}_{-(k+1)\delta_{0}^{(k)}}}\\
=-c_{+}t_{+}^{-1}-c_{-}t_{-}^{-1}+(c_{+}-c_{-})\delta_{0}-\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}(k+1)\delta_{0}^{(k)}}.

Equation (
13
) then reads

tu^{\prime}+u=-c_{+}t_{+}^{-1}-c_{-}t_{-}^{-1}+(c_{+}-c_{-})\delta_{0}-\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}(k+1)\delta_{0}^{(k)}}+c_{+}t_{+}^{-1}+c_{-}t_{-}^{-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{N}{a_{k}\delta_{0}^{(k)}}\\
=(c_{+}-c_{-})\delta_{0}-\sum_{k=0}^{N}{ka_{k}\delta_{0}^{(k)}}=\delta_{0}.

Again by linear independence,
a_{k}=0
for all
k\geq 1
and

c_{+}-c_{-}=1

has to hold, with
a_{0}
undetermined. In conclusion, we obtain

(22)

u=c\cdot t_{+}^{-1}+(c-1)t_{-}^{-1}+a_{0}\delta_{0}\ \text{mit\ }c,a_{0}\in\mathbb{C},

which is also consistent with all previous cases.

Determination of
\bm{\Upsilon}

We apply (
22
) to obtain solutions of (
12
) in the form

\varphi(\xi)=c\cdot\xi_{+}^{-1}+(c-1)\xi_{-}^{-1}+a_{0}\delta_{0}(\xi)\quad\text{with }c,a_{0}\in\mathbb{C}.

The condition

\varphi(\xi)=\frac{1}{\xi}\Upsilon(\xi)

then yields

(23)

\Upsilon(\xi)=\xi\varphi(\xi)=c\cdot\xi\cdot\xi_{+}^{-1}+(c-1)\cdot\xi\cdot\xi_{-}^{-1}+\underbrace{a_{0}\,\xi\,\delta_{0}(\xi)}_{=0}.

Thanks to (
17
) we arrive at

\Upsilon(\xi)=c\theta(\xi)+(c-1)\theta(-\xi)=\theta(\xi)+c-1=\theta(\xi)+d\quad\text{with }d\in\mathbb{C}.
