Mistaken Identity: Superior Mesenteric Vein Syndrome Presenting as Superior Mesenteric Artery Syndrome
Authors: Parth Patel, Adam Vinall, Shree Patel, Saatchi Kuwelker, Venkata Katabathina, Juan Echavarria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSuperior mesenteric artery syndrome is a rare condition where the third portion of the duodenum becomes compressed between the superior mesenteric artery and the aorta. A 60-year-old woman presented with abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. Initial imaging was concerning for obstruction of the duodenum between the level of the aorta and the superior mesenteric vein. The patient was taken for esophagogastroduodenoscopy, which showed extrinsic compression of the duodenum with a visible pulsatile mass causing proximal dilation. She subsequently underwent robotic-assisted laparoscopic diagnostic laparoscopy, lysis of small bowel adhesions, and duodenojejunostomy proximal to the location of the obstruction.

INTRODUCTION

Superior mesenteric artery (SMA) syndrome is a rare condition in which the third segment of the duodenum is extrinsically compressed between the SMA and the aorta resulting in small bowel obstruction.1 Compression of the duodenum typically occurs when the aortomesenteric angle is decreased to less than 25 degrees. Decreased angles typically occur due to loss of fat pad between the SMA and the aorta, which can be congenital or following weight loss.2,3 The exact epidemiologic burden of this disease is unknown. However, it is estimated to be 0.1% to 0.3% and is typically seen in young adults with a 3:2 female-to-male predominance.2 SMA syndrome is difficult to diagnose and easily confused for other pathology. Patients present with vague symptoms ranging from nausea, vomiting, and postprandial abdominal pain to more concerning signs such as anorexia, weight loss, and early satiety.4 This condition can be mimicked by the rarer superior mesenteric vein (SMV) syndrome, where duodenal obstruction occurs via the SMV as opposed to the SMA. Little is known about the risk factors, pathogenesis, and epidemiology of SMV syndrome, as only 2 other cases have been described in the literature.5,6 In this report, we present a case of SMV syndrome in a 60-year-old female patient.

CASE REPORT

A 60-year-old woman presented with severe abdominal pain, food intolerance, and persistent nausea. Her surgical history was notable for remote laparoscopic hysterectomy, laparoscopic appendectomy, and exploratory laparotomy for small bowel obstruction secondary to volvulus with resulted resection and end-to-end anastomosis in the setting of bowel infarction. The patient reported chronic intermittent moderate-to-severe epigastric abdominal pain following bowel resection. However, over the 4 days before presentation, the abdominal pain had become unrelenting and severe. She reported associated nausea, oral intolerance, and only passing one small, hard stool in the 2 days before admission. Other pertinent review of systems, medications, and social and family history were noncontributory.

Her initial vitals were within normal limits, but she was noted to have a low body mass index of 16.8 kg/m2. She appeared nontoxic, but frail, with marked temporal wasting. Abdominal examination was significant for diffuse generalized tenderness to palpation without rebound or guarding. Her complete blood count was within normal limits and complete metabolic panel did not indicate any electrolyte abnormalities, liver disease, or worsening renal function. Her lactic acid was elevated to 2.2 mmol/L. Abdominal/pelvic computed tomography (CT) with intravenous contrast was concerning for duodenal obstruction in the third portion between the level of the aorta and the SMV. Both radiology and gastroenterology concurred that the SMV rather than the SMA was involved (Figures 1–3).

Subsequently, a nasogastric tube was placed for decompression, producing notable bilious output; however, symptoms persisted. The patient was then taken for small bowel enteroscopy, which showed extrinsic compression of the third portion of the duodenum by a visible pulsatile mass posteriorly compressing the lumen, likely representing the adjacent abdominal aorta (Figures 4 and 5).

The patient was subsequently taken to the operating room for robotic-assisted diagnostic laparoscopy, lysis of small bowel adhesions, and duodenojejunostomy proximal to the location of the obstruction. The patient tolerated the procedure well, was transitioned to a full liquid diet hours after the procedure, and was discharged 36 hours postoperatively. Six months after the surgical procedure, a repeat CT scan showed a well-appearing duodenojejunostomy and resolution of bowel obstruction.

DISCUSSION

SMV syndrome is an extremely rare disorder characterized by extrinsic compression of the third portion of the duodenum by the SMV. The SMV collects blood from the terminal ileum, cecum, and appendix before ascending adjacent to the SMA and joining the splenic vein to form the hepatic portal vein. The SMV carries blood from the small intestine, ascending colon, and proximal portion of the transverse colon to the liver.3 The SMA branches off the aorta, creating the aortomesenteric angle, as it supplies blood to distal parts of the small intestine, proximal colon, and pancreas. In standard anatomy, the third segment of the duodenum extends between the aorta and the origin of the SMA, where it can be pathologically compressed in the setting of a decreased aortomesenteric angle.1 Conversely, in SMV syndrome, the third portion of the bowel is extrinsically compressed between the SMV and the aorta.

To our knowledge, only 2 other cases of SMV syndrome have been described by Hecht et al and Ahmed et al. In the first case, the patient had a significant surgical history with a prior Billroth II gastrectomy and multiple exploratory laparotomies for recurrent small bowel ileus.5 In the second case, the third and fourth portions of the patient’s duodenum were compressed between the SMV and the right common iliac artery.6 Prior operative reports for this patient were not readily available;, however, intraoperative findings were notable for adhesions and clear involvement of the SMV and the aorta. Although the exact pathophysiology of SMV syndrome is not fully understood, these cases suggest that SMV syndrome may result from a combination of anatomic variation or previous surgical intervention.5,7,8

SMV syndrome provides similar clinical presentation to SMA syndrome with intermittent extrinsic obstruction of the duodenum causing postprandial discomfort, abdominal pain, and ultimately weight loss.4 Abdominal and pelvic CT with contrast is the diagnostic imaging study of choice as anatomy is evaluated in the portal venous phase following intravenous contrast administration. Opacification of both arterial and venous structures allows for appropriate assessment of vascular anatomy and associated complications.9 Alternately, abdominal and pelvic CT venogram can be beneficial for preoperative planning, evaluation of concurrent pathology, and postsurgical assessment.10 Surgical intervention via duodenojejunostomy allows for bypass of the obstruction when conservative management fails.6,11,12
