Investigation of the relationship between stress and temporomandibular joint dysfunction during preclinical and postclinical education among dentistry faculty students at Kütahya Health Sciences University
Authors: Begüm Özcan, Emine Mustafaoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo determine the relationship between the presence of joint dysfunction and stress levels during preclinical and postclinical education in dentistry students.MethodsThe study was conducted with a total of 250 students, 50 from each year, at Kütahya Health Sciences University’s Faculty of Dentistry. The students were divided into two main groups: the preclinical education and postclinical education groups. The research data were collected via a 28-item questionnaire conducted face to face with the students, consisting of sociodemographic questions, the Fonseca Anamnestic Index, and the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS).ResultsThe correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between the Fonseca Index score and the PSS score (r = 0.869, p < 0.05). Higher PSS scores were observed in the participants with higher temporomandibular disorder (TMD) severity. Moreover, significant differences were observed in the PSS scores of those who were in postclinical education, women, smokers, those who did not live with their families, those whose first choice was not this faculty, and those who did not exercise regularly, and they exhibited higher stress levels. There was no significant relationship between TMD and PSS scores in the second-year smokers, the first-year students whose first choice was not this faculty, and the fourth-year students who exercised regularly (p > 0.05). Binary logistic regression analysis showed that postclinical education and PSS scores were significantly associated with TMD.ConclusionsWithin the limitations of this cross-sectional study, a significant association was found between TMD severity and perceived stress among dental students. Postclinical education may be associated with higher stress levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08910-6.Keywords: Dentistry student, Temporomandibular joint, Temporomandibular disorders, Stress

Introduction

The etiology of masticatory system disorders is difficult to determine. No single etiology explains all symptoms.

Numerous factors may affect chewing function, but five etiological factors are mentioned in the literature, namely occlusal factors, trauma, emotional stress, deep pain sensation, and parafunctional habits [1, 2]. Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) are a group of conditions characterized by pain in the temporomandibular joint, masticatory muscles, and associated structures; joint sounds during function; and deviation or alteration in the direction of mandibular movement. Physical factors, together with psychological and social influences, may contribute to the development of TMDs [3, 4].

A significant relationship between emotional state and stress and signs and symptoms of TMD has been shown in several studies [5–9].

In previous studies, it was demonstrated that stress activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to an increase in muscle tone, promoting parafunctional habits such as bruxism, and stimulating the release of biochemical mediators associated with pain [10, 11]. These mechanisms suggest that stress may play a significant role in the pathophysiology of TMDs [1, 12].

Dentistry is a highly stressful profession, and dental education is considered a stressful learning environment, as theoretical knowledge, interpersonal skills, and clinical competency are required [13, 14].

The factors that cause stress in dental students include examinations, clinical dentistry practice, patient management, the need to meet academic and clinical requirements, clinical staff, emotional and financial issues, and relationships with friends and family [15, 16].

Dental education generally consists of two main phases: the preclinical period, which includes the first, second, and third academic years, and the postclinical period, which comprises the fourth and fifth years. During the preclinical phase, education primarily focuses on theoretical instruction, laboratory-based training, and the development of manual skills. In contrast, during the postclinical phase, students actively participate in patient care. Throughout postclinical education, students are exposed to numerous stressors, including patient management, the obligation to fulfill clinical requirements, interactions with clinical staff, increased workload, time management challenges, performance evaluations, professional responsibility, and concerns about their future careers [13–16]. As different stress-related factors may emerge during the transition from preclinical to postclinical education or across academic years, variations in stress levels may occur, which may contribute to the development or exacerbation of TMDs.

While the literature includes several studies evaluating the relationship between TMDs and stress, [5–7, 17–22] regional and socioeconomic differences related to the city of residence have been shown to influence stress levels [23].

Understanding how different stages and years of study of dental education influence stress levels and how these stress-related changes are associated with the severity of TMDs may contribute to the development of timely preventive strategies aimed at protecting the temporomandibular joint, the masticatory system, and the psychological well-being of dental students.

There is a lack of studies conducted in Turkey in which the association between TMD severity and perceived stress levels is evaluated simultaneously during the transition from preclinical to postclinical dental education. Furthermore, the potential influence of sociodemographic and lifestyle-related factors on this relationship remains insufficiently explored. Therefore, the aim in the present study was to address this gap by providing a comprehensive evaluation of stress–TMD interactions across different stages of dental education within a regional context.

The null hypothesis of the present study was that there would be no difference in stress levels between the preclinical and postclinical education periods among students at the Faculty of Dentistry at Kütahya Health Sciences University, and that stress levels would not affect the presence of TMDs.

The first hypothesis of the present study was that sociodemographic and lifestyle-related factors influence the relationship between perceived stress and the severity of TMDs.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was designed as a cross-sectional observational study conducted with students at the Faculty of Dentistry at Kütahya Health Sciences University in Kütahya, Turkey, in September and October 2024. The cross-sectional study design allowed evaluation of the relationship between education period and sociodemographic factors, and perceived stress level and TMD severity among dental students at a single point in time.

Participants

The participants comprised 250 dental students, 50 from each year, studying at Kütahya Health Sciences University’s Faculty of Dentistry.

Students in all academic years were included in the study in order to represent the entire dental education process and to ensure adequate representation of both educational periods within the sample.

The students were divided into two main groups: first-, second-, and third-year students, who had not yet started patient treatment in the preclinical education period, and fourth- and fifth-year students, who had started patient treatment in the postclinical education period.

The sample size of the study was calculated using the statistical software G*Power (ver. 3.1.9.7). When the test power was set at 0.80, the effect size at 0.25 (medium effect size for the F-test), and the type I error (α) at 0.05, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 40 participants per subgroup (class), resulting in a total of 200 participants. However, in order to ensure adequate statistical power, the sample size was increased, and a total of 250 participants, with 50 participants in each group, were included in the study. Based on this sample size, the post-hoc power analysis indicated a power of 86.6%.

Although the primary comparison of the study was conducted between the two educational periods, the inclusion of students from all academic years allowed for a more comprehensive evaluation of dental education by enabling the identification of period-related differences as well as class-specific variations in stress levels and TMD severity.

The inclusion and exclusion criteria for participation in the study were determined. Students at Kütahya Health Sciences University’s Faculty of Dentistry who met the inclusion criteria (no history of maxillofacial trauma, no acute antibiotic use, at least 20 teeth present, not pregnant or lactating, and not receiving regulatory medication) were included in the study, while individuals under 18 years of age, with a history of substance abuse, taking psychiatric or myorelaxant drugs, undergoing orthodontic treatment, having a history of TMD treatment, or who did not fully complete the questionnaires were excluded. Participation was voluntary and the identity of all participants was kept confidential.

Variables

The dependent variable was TMD severity and the independent variable was perceived stress level. Sociodemographic and lifestyle-related factors such as gender, academic year, smoking status, regular exercise, living with family, and the faculty being their first choice were evaluated as potential confounding variables.

Instruments

The research data were collected via a 28-question questionnaire completed face to face with the students, consisting of sociodemographic questions (8 questions) [See supplementary material 1], the Fonseca Anamnestic Index to determine TMD level (10 questions), and the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) to determine stress level (10 questions).

The Fonseca Anamnestic Index was used to determine the presence and severity of TMD [See supplementary material 2]. This 10-question index, developed by Fonseca et al., [24] is widely used in epidemiological studies because of its ease of application and low cost [25, 26].

Each question can be answered “Yes,” “No,” or “Sometimes,” and the points given for these answers are 10, 0, and 5, respectively. A total score between 0 and 15 is defined as indicating no TMD, between 20 and 40 as indicating mild TMD, between 45 and 65 as indicating moderate TMD, and between 70 and 100 as indicating severe TMD [25, 27].

The validity and reliability of the Turkish translation of the PSS were confirmed by Eskin et al. [28] and there are 4-, 10-, and 14-question versions of the test. The results obtained from that study indicated that all three versions had sufficient reliability. In the present study, the 10-question version was used [See supplementary material 3]. The test scores range from 0 to 40. An answer of “Never” receives 0 points, “Almost never” 1 point, “Sometimes” 2 points, “Quite often” 3 points, and “Very often” 4 points. Questions 4, 5, 7, and 8 consist of positive sentences and are reverse scored [28, 29].

In addition, the part of the survey concerning sociodemographic information included questions about the students’ year of study, gender, age, whether they lived with their families, whether the faculty of dentistry was their first choice, whether they were accepted to the faculty after their first attempt at taking the university entrance examination, whether they exercised regularly, and whether they smoked [See supplementary material 1].

Statistical analysis

The data were collected during the fall semester of the 2024–2025 academic year. SPSS (IBM SPSS for Windows, ver.26) was used for the analysis. In the data analysis, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (n > 50) and skewness and kurtosis tests were used to check whether the continuous measurements in the study were normally distributed. According to the results of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (n > 50), the PSS score was not normally distributed; however, since the skewness and kurtosis values of this score were within the normal distribution confidence limits (± 1.5), the data were accepted as normally distributed. Therefore, parametric tests were used. Descriptive statistics for the variables in the study are expressed as mean and standard deviation. The independent t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare measurements according to categorical factors. Binary multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify the independent effects of various sociodemographic and clinical factors (year of study, educational stage, gender, smoking status, living with family, faculty preference, and regular exercise) on TMD symptom severity. In addition, separate multivariate binary logistic regression was performed to evaluate the independent effects of year of study and gender on TMD severity. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to determine the relationship between continuous measurements. In the calculations, the statistical significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Kütahya Health Sciences University (Approval No: 2024/10-22, Date: August 12, 2024) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to administration of the questionnaire.

Results

A total of 250 students, 50 from each year of the 5-year program at Kütahya Health Sciences University’s Faculty of Dentistry, were included in the present survey study. The students were grouped according to the preclinical period (first, second, and third years) and the postclinical period (fourth and fifth years). The relationship between stress during preclinical and postclinical education and TMD and the factors that may affect stress such as which year the students were in, their age, their gender, whether they were living with their families, whether the faculty was their first choice, whether they were accepted to the faculty on their first attempt at taking the university entrance examination, and whether they exercised regularly exercise and smoked was examined.

The participants were dentistry students aged 18–23, 35.6% of whom were male (n=89) and 64.4% of whom were female (n=161). Students who did not live with their families (62%, n=155), whose first choice was this faculty (77.6%, n=194), who did not exercise regularly (79.6%, n=199), and who did not smoke (81.6%, n=204) made up the majority. Forty-four percent (n=110) of the students were accepted to the faculty after their first attempt at taking the university entrance examination. Comparison of the PSS scores across different year students revealed a significant difference in the PSS scores of the participants according to their year (p=0.001). Higher scores were obtained by the fourth- and fifth-year students. The scores were similar between these years. The scores were also similar between the first-, second-, and third-year students (Table 3).

PSS scores were also compared between the different years according to TMD severity. A significant difference was observed in the PSS scores of first-year students according to TMD severity (p=0.001), and they differed from each other at all TMD levels. Mean PSS scores were higher in the participants with higher TMD severity levels. Similarly, a significant difference was observed in the PSS scores of the second-, third-, fourth-, and fifth-year students according to TMD severity (p=0.001), and they were found to differ from each other at all TMD levels. PSS scores tended to increase as TMD severity increased (Table 4).

A significant difference was observed in the PSS scores of the participants according to gender (p=0.007), with higher scores being obtained by the women (Table 3).

A significant difference was also determined in PSS scores according to TMD severity in both men and women (p=0.001). PSS scores differed in men and women at all TMD levels, with a tendency to increase as TMD severity increased.

When the participants' PSS scores were compared according to whether they smoked, a significant difference was observed (p=0.019), with smokers having higher scores than nonsmokers (Table 3).

The PSS scores were also compared according to TMD severity, separately in different years and among smokers. A significant difference was observed in the PSS scores of the first-, third-, fourth-, and fifth-year smokers according to TMD severity (p=0.004, p=0.017, p=0.003, p=0.001, respectively). According to the results obtained, the PSS score tended to increase as TMD severity increased.

However, no significant difference was observed in the PSS scores of the second-year student smokers according to TMD severity (p>0.05).

A statistically significant difference was observed in the participants' PSS scores based on whether they lived with their families or not (p=0.009), with those who did not having higher scores (Table 3).

The PSS scores were also compared according to TMD severity in different years and different family living situations. The results showed a significant difference in the PSS scores of the participants who did not live with their families in all years according to TMD severity (p=0.001), with PSS scores tending to increase as TMD severity increased. Similarly, a significant difference was determined in the PSS scores of the participants living with their families in all years according to TMD severity (p=0.001, p=0.001, p=0.001, p=0.011, p=0.001, respectively).

When the participants' PSS scores were compared according to whether this faculty was their first choice, a significant difference was observed (p=0.004), with those for whom the faculty was not their first choice having higher scores (Table 3).

The PSS scores were compared separately according to TMD severity in different year students and whether the faculty was their first choice or not. A significant difference was observed in the PSS scores of the participants for whom the faculty was their first choice in all years, according to TMD severity (p=0.001). A significant difference was also seen in the PSS scores in the second-, third-, fourth-, and fifth-year students according to TMD severity in the participants for whom the faculty was not their first choice (p=0.002, p=0.002, p=0.003, p=0.001, respectively). However, there was no significant difference in the PSS scores of the first-year participants for whom the faculty was not their first choice, according to TMD severity (p>0.05).

There was no significant difference in the participants' PSS scores based on whether they were accepted to the faculty after their first attempt at the examination or not (p>0.05). PSS scores were similar in the two cases (Table 3).

A comparison of PSS scores was also made separately according to TMD severity in different year students and whether they were accepted after their first attempt at taking the university entrance examination. The results showed a significant difference in the PSS scores of the participants in all years and who were not accepted at their first attempt, according to TMD severity, and they were different from each other at all TMD levels. PSS scores tended to increase as TMD severity increased in all year students (p=0.001). Similarly, there was a significant difference in the PSS scores of students accepted at their first attempt in all years according to TMD severity (p=0.001).

The participants' PSS scores differed significantly based on whether they exercised regularly or not (p=0.002), with who did not exercise regularly having a higher score than those who did (Table 3).

Comparison of the PSS scores in different year students according to TMD severity and whether they exercised or not showed a significant difference in the scores of those who did not exercise regularly in all years (p=0.001), with PSS scores tending to increase as TMD severity increased. Similarly, there was a significant difference in the PSS scores of the first-, second-, third-, and fifth-year students who exercised regularly according to TMD severity (p=0.002, p=0.001, p=0.001, p=0.002, respectively). However, no significant difference was observed in the PSS scores of the fourth-year students who exercised regularly according to TMD severity (p>0.05).

Discussion

In the present study, a significant association was observed between perceived stress levels and the severity of TMDs among dental students, with higher stress levels also associated with the initiation of postclinical education. The findings of the multivariate regression analysis also supported the results, showing that postclinical education stage and perceived stress level were independent predictors of high TMD symptom severity. Based on these findings, the null hypothesis stating that there is no difference in stress levels between the preclinical and postclinical education periods among students of the Faculty of Dentistry at Kütahya Health Sciences University, and that stress levels are not associated with the presence of TMDs, was rejected.

Temporomandibular joint dysfunction is a condition that represents the leading cause of nondental pain in the orofacial region and encompasses pain, functional limitation, and the musculoskeletal relationships among the temporomandibular joint, masticatory muscles, and surrounding tissues [30–32]. TMDs have a multifactorial etiology and may be associated with several factors, including depression, stress, anxiety, poor posture, and bruxism [33, 34]. A potentially bidirectional relationship may exist between stress and bruxism; stress may be associated with the development of bruxism, while bruxism-related pain may also be associated with increased psychological distress, potentially contributing to the continuation of this cycle [35].

It is well known that stress, particularly with increasing muscle activity, can lead to pain in the temporomandibular joint region [10, 11]. Stress levels, which have wide-ranging effects on individuals’ lives, can be measured using various methods, one of which is the PSS developed by Cohen, Kamarck, and Mermelstein [28]. In the diagnosis of TMD, anamnesis, clinical examination, and radiological imaging methods are used [36]. However, since standardization cannot always be achieved in clinical evaluations, comparisons in studies are generally made based on specific criteria using standardized diagnostic forms [37]. Since the Fonseca Anamnestic Index is an method that is widely used due to its ease of application and low cost [25], it was utilized in the present study to determine the severity of TMD, while the PSS-10 was used to measure stress levels.

Different results have been reported in the literature regarding stress levels. Peker et al. [38] reported similar stress levels in fourth- and fifth-year students and lower levels than those observed in first-, second-, and third-year students, and evaluated these findings within the framework of the preclinical theoretical curriculum.

Çelik and Tuncer [39] reported higher stress levels among postclinical students compared with preclinical students and stated that this was due to stress experienced during clinical practice and concerns about the future. The present study revealed a statistically significant difference in stress levels between the preclinical and postclinical education periods, with higher stress levels observed among fourth- and fifth-year students in the postclinical education period. This situation may be associated with students taking an active role in patient treatment, the stress experienced during clinical practice, concerns about the future, and the inclusion of female participants in the study.

In the present study, the relationship between stress and several sociodemographic factors was also evaluated. Consistent with the literature, stress levels were found to be significantly higher in females [40–44], in those not living with their families, [45] in smokers [46], in students for whom the faculty was not their first choice [41], and in those who did not exercise regularly [40]. Although the PSS scores were higher among students who were not admitted to the faculty on their first attempt at the university entrance examination, this difference was not statistically significant. This factor demonstrated a limited association compared with other stress-related variables. The effect of living with family has shown variability among studies in the literature [41]. Cavallo et al. [40] reported higher stress levels among females living with their families, with these findings attributed to family expectations. In the present study, higher stress levels among students who did not live with their families were attributed to the absence of the comfort provided by the home environment and the supportive characteristics of a stable home environment [45].

An examination of the literature reveals various studies on the relationship between TMDs and stress [5–7, 17–22] Aranha et al. [47] reported no definitive association between workplace stress and TMDs in adults and evaluated these findings in relation to differences in diagnostic criteria and sample characteristics. In contrast, numerous studies in the literature have demonstrated a significant relationship between stress and the signs and symptoms of TMDs [6–9, 17–20, 22, 34, 42, 48]. Bayramoğlu and Gümrükçü [21] reported a positive relationship between TMDs and anxiety and depression. Wieckiewicz et al. [48] identified significant associations between TMDs, emotional factors, and parafunctional habits. In the present study, correlation analysis evaluating the relationship between TMDs and stress revealed a significant association between Fonseca Index and PSS scores (r=0.869, p<0.05). Accordingly, higher TMD severity was associated with higher stress levels. In addition, the binary multiple logistic regression demonstrated a significant association between PSS scores and TMD severity, with higher PSS scores corresponding to a 1.9-fold greater likelihood of high TMD symptom severity (p=0.001). These findings are supported by biophysiological explanations suggesting associations between emotional stress and muscle activity, prolonged muscle stimulation, lactic acid accumulation, and local hypoxic pain through the extrapyramidal gamma motor neuron system [17].

A significant positive correlation between TMD severity and stress was observed across both educational periods and all years of study. Furthermore, the binary multiple logistic regression examining academic year and gender in relation to TMD severity demonstrated significant associations among the fourth- and fifth-year students.

In addition to psychological stress, physical factors associated with the clinical training process may also be taken into account in relation to TMDs. During the postclinical education period, dental students may encounter prolonged static postures, repetitive movements, and ergonomically unfavorable positions while performing clinical procedures [33, 39, 49].

When sociodemographic factors were considered, significant positive correlations between TMD severity and stress were observed across most subgroups for gender, living with family, university admission on the first attempt, smoking status, faculty preference, and regular exercise, whereas no significant correlation was identified among second-year students who smoked, first-year students for whom the faculty was not their first choice, and fourth-year students who exercised regularly. The lack of a significant relationship between TMD severity and stress in some groups may be associated with the small sample size and the lower stress levels in first-year students due to their lighter workload.

Yang et al. [23] reported that regional and socioeconomic differences in the city of residence may be associated with stress. The regional context in which the study was conducted may be related to stress perception, as students studying in mid-sized cities may experience different socioeconomic conditions and levels of access to stress-coping resources compared with those in larger metropolitan areas.

In this context, the present study provides a novel and integrative contribution by elucidating the multidimensional relationship between perceived stress and TMD severity across different stages of dental education. By simultaneously incorporating psychosocial, educational, and lifestyle-related determinants and directly comparing preclinical and postclinical periods, the findings provide additional insight into the relationship between postclinical education and students’ psychological well-being and musculoskeletal health. The identification of the transition to clinical practice as a critical vulnerability phase underscores the necessity for early screening, structured psychological support, and ergonomically informed educational interventions, thereby yielding important implications for curriculum development and preventive strategies in dental education.

Our study has several limitations. One of the main limitations is that the sample size was relatively small and obtained from a single center. This may limit the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, the sample does not represent all dental students in Turkey. However, the study is important in terms of contributing to the development of future epidemiological studies at local, regional, national, and international levels in this research area. This limitation can be overcome by increasing the sample size and conducting multicenter studies. In addition, the cross-sectional design of the study did not allow evaluation of the temporal continuity of stress-related TMDs among dental students. The cross-sectional design of the study precludes making a causal inference between TMDs and stress. Longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate the long-term effects of stress on these disorders. Furthermore, it is recommended that various sociodemographic factors affecting stress be evaluated in future studies. In particular, factors that may cause stress during the postclinical education period and their relationship with TMDs should be examined in more detail. Another limitation is the use of a questionnaire-based method, which makes it difficult to establish a definitive diagnosis of temporomandibular joint disorders. The Fonseca Anamnestic Index is not a definitive clinical diagnostic method but rather a self-reported screening tool. The use of self-reported measures may increase the risk of reporting bias, as the data analyzed are based on participants’ self-reports. The reliability of the results could be improved by including clinical and radiological examinations in future studies. Another limitation is that variables that may be associated with TMDs, such as sleep quality, static physical load, and posture position, were not evaluated. In addition, the development of psychological support programs to promote temporomandibular joint health and mental health may be beneficial, and the effects of such programs on TMDs should be evaluated in future studies.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this cross-sectional study, it was found that the postclinical education period and the fourth and fifth academic years were associated with higher levels of stress.

In terms of sociodemographic factors, it is thought that stress may be associated with smoking, not living with their families, the faculty not being their first choice, not engaging in regular exercise, and being female. Additionally, with respect to sociodemographic factors, a generally positive correlation was observed between TMD severity and stress.

The postclinical education period and PSS scores were found to be significant factors associated with high TMD symptom severity.

However, due to the cross-sectional design of the study, causal relationships cannot be inferred from these findings.

Prospective and clinical studies are needed to better understand these relationships and to clarify their clinical importance.
