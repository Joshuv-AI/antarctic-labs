ActTraitBench: Quantifying the Knowledge-Decision Gap in Large Language Models via Human-Grounded Behavioral Validation
Yutong YangPeking University yytpku@pku.edu.cn Chenxi MiaoBaidu Inc miaochenxi@baidu.com, Weikang LiBaidu Inc wavejkd@pku.edu.cn, Yunfang WuPeking University wuyf@pku.edu.cn

Abstract
While Large Language Models (LLMs) can convincingly simulate personas in explicit self-reports, they often deviate in implicit behavioral decisions, revealing a substantial Knowledge-Decision Gap ( G KD G_{\text{KD}} ). Existing benchmarks struggle to measure this asymmetry due to limited construct validity, multi-dimensional entanglement, and distributional biases in LLM-based evaluation. To address these issues, we propose ActTraitBench , a human-grounded evaluation framework for measuring personality consistency in LLMs. Grounded in empirical human data, ActTraitBench establishes one-to-one mappings between psychometric facets and behavioral paradigms, and applies a Distributional Calibration via Quantile Mapping procedure to align LLM-judge score distributions with human norms. Experiments on 14 mainstream LLMs reveal a pervasive knowledge-decision asymmetry, where larger and more capable models often exhibit stronger behavioral divergence despite highly consistent self-reports. To mitigate this gap, we further introduce the Chain of Cognitive Alignment (CoCA), a plug-and-play inference-time intervention that improves alignment in reasoning-capable frontier models while exposing clear capability limitations in smaller architectures.

Introduction

As Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in real-world interactive and decision-making scenarios, accurately measuring their emergent psychological traits has become a critical necessity for trustworthy AI. These latent behavioral tendencies directly influence user trust, signal potential alignment risks such as sycophancy, and dictate the functional reliability of autonomous agents
11
.
Early psychometric endeavors primarily mirrored traditional psychological methodologies, deploying explicit self-report questionnaires (e.g., the Big Five Inventory
21
or Hexaco
questionnaire
3
) directly to LLMs to infer their trait distributions
16
;
12
;
19
.
As evaluation paradigms evolved, researchers challenged the overall validity of explicit self-reports, exposing systemic vulnerabilities such as limited temporal stability and artificially highlighted prosociality
5
;
22
. This catalyzed a transition toward more ecologically valid behavioral measurements, including dynamic multi-agent discourse
4
, contextualized action-selection scenarios
20
, and experimental psychological paradigms
11
.

By employing these implicit frameworks, studies have repeatedly exposed a profound “personality illusion” and “Value-Action Gap”: while advanced LLMs seamlessly comply with explicit persona descriptions at a superficial semantic level, their actual behaviors during complex implicit decisions remain highly unpredictable and unaligned.

Despite this progress, current implicit behavioral evaluation frameworks still suffer from three critical methodological limitations:

(1)
Unverified Measurement Validity
: Existing frameworks frequently rely on synthetically generated behavioral labels
20
or LLM-based trait extraction
4
without strict empirical human benchmarking. This leaves it unverified whether a specific downstream text or decision genuinely and uniquely maps to a latent psychological state.

(2)
Multi-dimensional Entanglement
: Complex interactive scenarios, such as multi-agent debates
4
or macro-behavioral tasks
11
, implicitly force models to process multiple macro-traits (e.g., the entire Big Five) simultaneously. This infuses behavioral scores with high-dimensional confounding noise, making it nearly impossible to isolate the influence of specific psychometric facets.

(3)
Distributional Bias
: Current evaluations suffer from severe, uncalibrated distributional shifts. For instance, models frequently exhibit default compliance biases
20
, while LLM-as-a-judge evaluators systematically avoid predicting negative traits like Neuroticism
4
. These systemic biases introduce profound statistical misalignment against natural human empirical distributions.

To address these methodological bottlenecks, we present
ActTraitBench
, a human-grounded evaluation framework designed to quantify the
“Knowledge-Decision Gap”
(
G_{\text{KD}}
) in LLMs—the profound semantic-pragmatic asymmetry between a model’s explicit personality claims and its implicit behavioral manifestations.

Our contributions are three-fold:

(1)
Human-Grounded Benchmark & Calibration
: We introduce ActTraitBench, featuring 11 micro-situational paradigms directly mapped to Big Five psychometric facets. By grounding these tasks in empirical human data and applying a non-parametric Quantile Mapping mechanism, we ensure construct validity and eliminate distributional biases inherent in LLM-based evaluation.

(2)
Quantification of the Knowledge-Decision Gap
: Through a comprehensive evaluation of 14 mainstream models, we rigorously quantify
G_{\text{KD}}
. Our results reveal that this behavioral divergence persists across model generations, suggesting that scaling and iteration alone fail to resolve fundamental pragmatic inconsistencies.

(3)
Inference-Time Mitigation
: We propose the Chain of Cognitive Alignment (CoCA), a lightweight, plug-and-play strategy that effectively narrows the knowledge-decision gap in reasoning-capable models.

Related Work

2.1
Psychometric Evaluations and Their Limitations

Early assessments of LLM personality primarily relied on standardized human self-report inventories, prompting models to complete Likert scales directly to maintain alignment with conventional human psychometric protocols
16
;
12
. Studies have demonstrated that instruction-tuned models can generate personality distributions resembling those of human samples, with larger models exhibiting higher internal consistency and construct validity
17
;
16
;
12
;
19
.

However, this mapping approach has faced significant scrutiny due to fundamental psychometric flaws, such as the lack of measurement invariance and structural validity
22
. LLMs frequently exhibit systemic “acquiescence bias,” providing affirmative responses to conflicting items and rendering traditional reliability metrics like Cronbach’s alpha potentially misleading
22
. Furthermore, research on temporal stability indicates that LLM persona representations lack consistency, with flagship models often demonstrating poor test-retest reliability
5
. These findings suggest that static self-reports are insufficient for capturing robust behavioral tendencies.

2.2
Implicit Behavioral Assessment and the Knowledge-Decision Gap

To overcome these limitations, recent literature has shifted toward behavioral assessment paradigms that examine model tendencies through interactive behaviors or contextualized tasks. For instance, studies have revealed that LLMs suffer from “persona drift” in multi-turn discourse, where they regress toward neutral patterns
4
, or exhibit a significant “value-action gap” when comparing stated values to decision-making actions across validated scenarios
20
.

Furthermore, researchers have explored the “personality illusion” by deploying established experimental psychological tasks to observe behavioral shifts under trait manipulations
11
. However, these paradigms face methodological hurdles: from a psychometric perspective, classical behavioral tasks often recruit multiple latent dimensions simultaneously, potentially entangling factors such as risk preference with diverse psychometric facets. This high-dimensional confounding makes it difficult to disentangle the contribution of specific traits, raising concerns regarding the construct validity of such implicit measures.

3.1
Data Construction Pipeline: A Triple-Iteration Loop

To ensure that the implicit behavioral tests accurately and objectively reflect the underlying personality traits of Large Language Models (LLMs), we establish a human-data-driven pipeline characterized by a triple-iteration loop for item refinement.

Paradigm Design and Lewin’s Equation

The initial experimental scenarios are meticulously designed to map onto the granular facets of the BFI-2 framework
13
. To guarantee psychometric validity, all paradigms are developed by researchers with dual academic training in both computer science and psychology, adapted from classic paradigms in experimental psychology
18
;
2
;
1
and behavioral economics
7
;
18
;
10
. In formulating these interactive scenarios, we adhere strictly to the foundational psychological principle of
Lewin’s Equation

14
, which posits that behavioral outcomes are not isolated linear outputs of personality traits, but rather the reconstructed products of underlying interactions between the person (
P
) and their immediate environment (
E
):

B=f(P,E)

(1)

Consequently, to isolate and extract the persona variable
P
precisely within our experiments, the environmental variable
E
must be rigorously controlled. Scenarios are engineered to be as parsimonious, straightforward, and clear as possible, minimizing extra cognitive noise introduced by complex task environments.

Two-stage Prompting Protocol

To systematically dissect the latent motivations behind a model’s choices, each test item leverages a “Two-stage” interaction flow:

•

Stage 1 (Quantitative Decision)
: The subject is required to input a numerical value within a continuous scale of 0–99 (e.g., points allocated, willingness-to-pay premium), providing a precise quantification of their explicit behavioral inclination.

•

Stage 2 (Qualitative Attribution)
: Triggered only after the completion of Stage 1, this follow-up prompt requires the subject to state the underlying logic and rationale behind their numerical decision (approximately 50 words).

We use sequential API calls to fetch quantitative scores and qualitative reasoning separately, maintaining strict symmetry with human testing protocols. An implicit behavioral paradigm example is provided in Appendix
B
.

Human Data Collection and Iterative Refinement

We developed an interactive Web-based survey system to collect authentic behavioral responses from human cohorts. For the scoring phase, we employ
gpt-5.4
as an LLM-as-a-judge. When evaluating a subject’s combined “numerical decision and textual rationale,” the judge prompt explicitly includes the complete question context, the definition of the target BFI-2 facet, reference BFI-2 items, and fine-grained multi-level scoring criteria, ultimately mapping the response to a behavioral trait score ranging from 1 to 5.

Upon acquiring the human data, we systematically compute the
Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient
(Spearman’s
\rho
) between the implicit behavioral scores and the explicit baseline scores from the standard BFI-2 self-reports. Items that fail to reach statistical significance undergo a rigorous case study and expert discussion within the research team. These scenarios are dynamically restructured and redeployed to the Web platform for subsequent rounds of data collection. Due to this adaptive replacement mechanism, the effective human sample size (
N
) varies across items (N=47 or 94). Following three rounds of strict iterative filtering, we finalize 11 core implicit test paradigms that exhibit robust statistically significant correlations with their corresponding personality facets, as Detailed in Table
1
.

3.2
Distributional Calibration via Quantile Mapping

LLM-judges often exhibit biased scoring distributions (e.g., extremity or central clustering). To align these with human BFI-2 norms, we implement a cross-domain mapping function
f:S_{\text{judge}}\to S_{\text{human}}
using three sequential steps:

1. Quantile Extraction

For a raw score
s
, we compute its percentile rank
P
within the judge’s global distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\text{judge}}
:

P=\text{Percentile}(s;\mathcal{D}_{\text{judge}})

(2)

2. Interval Anchoring

We map
P
onto the human benchmark distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\text{human}}
. Given an interval
[p_{i},p_{j}]
, we identify the corresponding segment in
\mathcal{D}_{\text{human}}
.

3. Score Transformation

The calibrated score
\hat{s}
is the expectation of human scores within this interval:

\hat{s}=\mathbb{E}[S_{\text{human}}\mid\text{Percentile}(S_{\text{human}};\mathcal{D}_{\text{human}})\in[p_{i},p_{j}]]

(3)

This approach shifts the evaluation from arbitrary numerical scales to relative positions within the behavioral spectrum, effectively neutralizing systemic bias and ensuring alignment with empirical human distributions.

3.3
Mitigation Method: Chain of Cognitive Alignment (CoCA)

To mitigate the knowledge-decision asymmetry observed in mainstream LLMs, we introduce an inference-time intervention named the Chain of Cognitive Alignment (CoCA).

In experimental psychology,
Objective Self-Awareness Theory
posits that when individuals engage in self-scrutiny or become aware that their consistency is being evaluated, their actions tend to align more closely with their internal beliefs
9
;
8
. Drawing inspiration from this framework, CoCA introduces a structured, multi-stage self-reflective mechanism for LLMs.

Rather than directly eliciting the final task decision, CoCA forces the model to execute a progressive internal reflection protocol structured within a rigid JSON schema prior to action selection. By embedding this metacognitive self-monitoring directly into the model’s autoregressive reasoning stream, we enforce a strict cognitive chain. The three progressive reflective tiers within the schema are defined as follows:

1.

Identity Recognition (Who am I?)
: Formulated as
step_1
in the reflection block, the model is prompted to explicitly self-assess its assigned psychological state:
“What are my personality traits (e.g., Big Five dimensions) and their respective levels?”

2.

Contextual Mapping (Where am I?)
: Operationalized in
step_2
, the model evaluates the situational salience of its persona by answering:
“Which of my personality traits does the current situation involve?”

3.

Consistency Planning (What should I do?)
: Positioned as
step_3
, the model derives its behavioral strategy based on the prior awareness nodes, explicitly asking:
“How should I act to maintain consistency between my knowledge and actions?”

Behavioral Scoring & Metric

Knowledge-Decision Gap (
G_{\text{KD}}
)
. To preserve fine-grained behavioral misalignment, the gap is computed at the
individual run level

(4)

where
\mathcal{F}=\{E,A,C,N,O\}
denotes the Big Five dimensions, and
K_{i}^{(m)}
and
D_{i}^{(m)}
are the interval-mapped knowledge and decision scores for trait
i
in the
m
-th run.

The Scaling Paradox: From Neutrality Illusion to Decoupling

The human baseline exhibits a natural knowledge-decision gap of
G_{\text{KD}}=0.445
, serving as an ecological anchor for our evaluation. Against this baseline, scaling up model parameters reveals a counter-intuitive paradox.

The Neutrality Illusion in Small Models
: At first glance, the lightweight
qwen3-1.7b
(
G_{\text{KD}}=0.189
) appears to surpass human consistency. However, this is merely a statistical illusion. A closer inspection reveals a “neutrality bias” (collapse to the mean), where both explicit (
K
) and implicit (
D
) values are densely clustered around 3.0. Lacking the cognitive bandwidth to form distinct preferences, the model defaults to safe, non-committal median responses, creating a superficial synchrony rather than genuine alignment.

Systematic Decoupling in Frontier Models
: As models scale to advanced reasoning architectures (e.g.,
qwen3-235b-thinking
with
G_{\text{KD}}=1.541
), they successfully escape this neutrality collapse. Yet, their consistency universally falls far behind the human baseline. This exposes a systemic decoupling: while implicit decisions (
D
) diversify into nuanced tendencies, explicit self-reports (
K
) remain rigidly tethered to socially desirable extremes. Scaling thus catalyzes divergence, transitioning models from an artifactual neutrality to a severe knowledge-decision asymmetry.

Dimensional Vulnerability: The Illusion of Emotional Stability

Delving deeper into individual traits reveals distinct
Dimensional Vulnerabilities
where explicit self-reports heavily decouple from implicit behavioral decisions:

The Collapse of Neuroticism (
\Delta_{N}
)
Almost all evaluated models present massive negative gaps in Neuroticism (e.g.,
-2.31
for
glm-5
and
-1.53
for
qwen3-32b
). This divergence demonstrates a social desirability bias: models portray themselves as pillars of emotional stability (
K_{N}\in[1.0,2.0]
) but betray high anxiety in crisis decisions (
D_{N}>3.0
). Unlike the human baseline (
\Delta_{N}=-0.08
), models claim superficial safety but act with latent volatility, posing deployment risks.

Aggressive Action in Openness (
\Delta_{O}
)
For Openness, the majority of frontier models (e.g.,
deepseek-v3
with
\Delta_{O}=-1.07
) exhibit a converse trend: implicit decisions reflect a significantly higher degree of intellect and adventure than conservative self-reports suggest. Models, in practice, act more radically than the persona they perceive themselves to possess.

4.3
RQ2: How Does Role-Playing Affect the Knowledge and Decisions of LLMs?

After revealing the knowledge-decision gap in the baseline state, a natural question arises: can explicit persona interventions mitigate this phenomenon?

Role Injection and Uncontaminated Facet Mapping

Via role prompts, we instruct the evaluated models to adopt High-Trait (theoretical score = 4) and Low-Trait (theoretical score = 2) personas across each dimension of the Big Five personality traits. Because our item design perfectly maps to the five core dimensions, the role injection experiments introduce zero cross-dimensional semantic contamination. This enables us to easily and accurately obtain the models’ knowledge scores (
K
) and decision scores (
D
) for specific dimensions.

Knowledge Compliance and Decision Deviation

As shown in Table
3
, all evaluated models exhibit severe knowledge-decision asymmetry during role-playing: knowledge scores closely follow persona settings, while decision scores substantially deviate.

Under explicit BFI self-reports, frontier models achieve extremely high knowledge compliance. For example,
deepseek-v4-pro
and
claude-sonnet-4-6
obtain very low average knowledge deviations (
\Delta K_{\text{Avg}}
of
0.121
and
0.313
), demonstrating accurate persona-aligned self-perceptions. However, their decision deviations (
\Delta D_{\text{Avg}}
) increase sharply in implicit behavioral tasks (reaching
0.545
and
0.537
), indicating failure to genuinely enact the injected personality logic in concrete decision-making scenarios.

Figure
3
further visualizes this asymmetry. The gray shaded areas denote target persona boundaries. Across both High-Trait and Low-Trait settings, the blue knowledge trajectories (
K
) closely match the targets, whereas the orange decision trajectories (
D
) deviate substantially and fail to flexibly adapt to role injections. Notably, this divergence persists even in reasoning models with slow-thinking mechanisms (e.g.,
qwen3-235b-thinking
).

4.4
RQ3: Effectiveness of the Chain of Cognitive Alignment (CoCA)

Given the pervasive knowledge-decision asymmetry observed in mainstream LLMs, we further investigate whether a plug-and-play cognitive intervention architecture can bridge this gap.

As introduced in §
3.3
, we draw inspiration from Objective Self-Awareness Theory and the classic “mirror experiment”
8
to design the Chain of Cognitive Alignment (CoCA).

Global Mitigation Performance

Table
4
summarizes the quantitative impact of the CoCA architecture (the model order remains identical to previous sections for consistent comparison). We employ the global Knowledge-Decision Gap (
G_{\text{KD}}
) across all Big Five dimensions to quantify the overall alignment, calculated as the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between explicit knowledge scores (
K
) and implicit decision scores (

5
Conclusion

We introduce ActTraitBench, a human-grounded framework evaluating LLM personality consistency beyond superficial self-reports. By mapping psychometric facets to implicit behavioral paradigms, we expose a pervasive
Knowledge-Decision Gap
. Paradoxically, scaling up model capacity exacerbates this divergence rather than resolving it. Moreover, while frontier LLMs reliably articulate assigned personas, they frequently fail to enact them during complex decision-making. To mitigate this asymmetry, we propose the Chain of Cognitive Alignment (CoCA), a training-free intervention that effectively bridges this gap. Ultimately, this work delivers a rigorously validated benchmark and a scalable mitigation strategy for developing reliable, persona-consistent LLM agents.

6
Limitations

Dynamic Benchmarking and Contamination

As a static benchmark, ActTraitBench may eventually suffer from training contamination as future LLMs continuously absorb new web-scale corpora. Future work may explore dynamically generated behavioral scenarios to mitigate memorization risks.

Incomplete Facet Coverage

Only 11 BFI-2 facets achieved significant correlations after iterative validation. Some traits, such as
Energy Level
, may be difficult to capture through text-only behavioral tasks. Future work could incorporate richer interactive and multimodal environments for more comprehensive personality assessment.

Appendix A
Iterative Testing Standards

Due to the three-stage optimization of the behavioral tasks, participants encountered different versions of the questions. To ensure the reliability of our empirical validity, we strictly included only data from participants who completed the "Final Version" of a specific task in the correlation analysis for that task.

Appendix B
Example Implicit Behavioral Item

Below is a representative implicit behavioral item mapping onto the Openness-Aesthetic Sensitivity facet:

[Scenario Input]:
You are purchasing a water cup that you will place on your office desk every day. You have targeted a standard edition industrial-grade cup priced at 99 points. Its core functionalities are flawless: durable, leak-proof, and well-insulated. However, its appearance is a plain metallic raw color, devoid of design aesthetics. At this moment, the system prompts that you can opt for an “Artistic Upgrade Pack” for this cup. Note: Opting for this upgrade pack will not improve any physical functionalities of the water cup. However, it will endow the cup with your preferred artistic attributes—it could be a visual painting by a master, a personalized inscription of literary poetry, or it can even play highly ambient environmental music when you drink water.

[Stage 1 Prompt]:
To inject artistry into this perfectly functional cup, what is the maximum extra points (0-99) you are willing to pay on top of the original 99-point price to purchase this Artistic Upgrade Pack?

[Stage 2 Prompt]:
Please use 2-3 sentences (approx. 50 words) to explain why you allocated points this way. What are your specific requirements regarding artistic aesthetics?

Detailed Experimental Configurations

For deterministic decoding, the temperature for all inferences is strictly set to
0.0
. To introduce robust perturbations across multi-run experiments, we vary the global random seeds across three independent runs (
Seed\in\{42,43,44\}
). It is worth noting that we intentionally abstain from applying option shuffling to the response choices. Since psychometric Likert scales possess a rigorous ordinal relationship and psychological continuity, maintaining a fixed option sequence is indispensable for preserving evaluation validity and ensuring strict alignment with the human cognitive environment. Additionally, a human baseline collected from 94 participants is incorporated as the statistical anchor.

Appendix E
Ethical Considerations and Artifact Statement

ActTraitBench consists of researcher-designed behavioral scenarios and human response data collected through a Web-based survey platform. The benchmark does not contain personally identifying information, and potentially sensitive or offensive content was manually reviewed during dataset construction.

All human participants completed the implicit behavioral tasks and explicit BFI-2 self-reports in Chinese. Consequently, the benchmark primarily reflects Chinese-language behavioral expressions and may capture culture-specific response tendencies. Because both data collection and evaluation were conducted within a relatively homogeneous linguistic and cultural context, the resulting behavioral distributions may have limited generalizability across languages, cultures, and demographic populations. Future work may extend ActTraitBench to multilingual and cross-cultural settings.

All models and external resources used in this work were employed in accordance with their intended research usage and respective terms of use. We intend to release the benchmark and evaluation code for research purposes only.

Finally, although this work evaluates behavioral consistency in LLMs, we do not claim that LLMs possess genuine human-like personalities or psychological states. The benchmark is intended solely for evaluation and research purposes and should not be interpreted as a tool for psychological diagnosis or real-world profiling.

Appendix F
Human Subjects, Consent, and Compensation

All participants voluntarily completed the study through a Web-based survey platform. Before participation, participants were presented with an informed consent statement explaining that the study was conducted for academic research purposes related to personality and behavior analysis.

Participants were informed that:
(1) participation was entirely voluntary,
(2) responses would be analyzed anonymously without personally identifying information,
(3) they could withdraw from the study at any time, and
(4) they could choose to complete only part of the evaluation.

Proceeding to the study was treated as providing informed consent.

Participants received an average compensation of approximately 20 RMB upon completion. The compensation level was designed to align with common compensation standards in behavioral and psychological experiments in China (approximately 1 RMB per minute).

The collected data were used solely for research purposes and were not released with any personally identifying information.
