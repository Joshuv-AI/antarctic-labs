Prevalence of fetal macrosomia and associated factors among delivered neonates in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Bekalu Getachew Gebreegziabher, Soressa Abebe Geneti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally, macrosomia affects 3 to 15% of all pregnancies; there is a global variation in the magnitude of macrosomia among countries. It is one of the public health problems in most developing countries and contributes to maternal and newborn complications. There are some inconsistent individual studies done in Ethiopia, but there is no current pooled national data on its prevalence and factors associated with it. So the study will be used to design and implement a well-comprehensive intervention targeting maternal and child health services.ObjectiveTo estimate the pooled prevalence and associated factors of fetal macrosomia in Ethiopia.MethodsA systematic review and Meta-analysis of published and grey literature was conducted. A Web of Science, EMBASE, PubMed, Scopus, African Journals Online and Elsevier Science direct were searched as electronic databases, while Google Scholar was utilized as a supplementary search engine. Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality appraisal tools were used for quality assessment and the study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist to maintain scientific robustness. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA software (version 14). For each identified associated factor, pooled effect sizes, 95% confidence intervals and measures of statistical heterogeneity were calculated. Heterogeneity among the included studies was quantified using I2 statistic where a value greater than 50% indicated substantial heterogeneity. Potential sources of heterogeneity were explored using meta-regression, sensitivity analysis, and subgroup analysis. Either random-effects or fixed-effect models were employed based on the observed level of heterogeneity. Potential publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test. Statistical significance was declared at a p-value less than 0.05 with a 95% CI.ResultA total of 4309 neonates were delivered in Ethiopian Health institutions, from which 3670 singleton births were included to estimate the pooled effect of fetal macrosomia. The pooled prevalence of fetal macrosomia among delivered neonates in Ethiopia was 10.84% (95% CI: 6.38%–15.31%). Regionally, the highest and lowest pooled estimates were reported from Tigray (13.24%) and Amhara region (7.41%) respectively. Significant heterogeneity was observed across the pooled effect (I2= 71.6%, P-value = 0.004) 95% CI. Subgroup analysis by year revealed that the pooled estimate of macrosomia before and after 2019 was 12.31% and 7.42%, respectively. Meta-regression indicated that this heterogeneity was not significantly driven by sample size (p = 0.08), geographic region (SNNP: p = 0.558, Tigray: p = 0.30, Amhara: p = 0.150), or publication year (p = 0.20). Sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate individual study effects; the pooled estimate fluctuated between 9.1% and 12.35%, indicating no single study disproportionately influenced the overall result. In the random-effects meta-analysis, five potential determinants were evaluated; however, none demonstrated a statistically significant pooled association due to marked statistical imprecision. The pooled adjusted odds ratios were as follows: Previous history of macrosomia.(AOR = 4.23, 95%CI, 0.334–53.668), being male(AOR = 2.32, 95%CI, 0.40-13.33), physical inactivity (AOR = 3.014, 95%CI, 0.229–39.614), weight gain during pregnancy((AOR = 4.88, 95%CI, 0.169–140.97), and higher gestational age (AOR = 4.71, 95%CI, 0.47–46.28).ConclusionFetal macrosomia represents a notable public health concern in Ethiopia, with a pooled prevalence exceeding multiple Sub-Saharan regional reports. Because pooled exposure analyses were highly underpowered and statistically non-significant, routine clinical screening during antenatal care should be strengthened, and larger prospective cohort studies are required to establish precise national risk pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09476-5.

Introduction

Fetal growth is a complex process that involves interactions among the mother, placenta and fetus [1] Fetal macrosomia can be defined in several different ways, including birth weight greater than 4000–4500 g, The most widely used measure of fetal macrosomia in developed countries is the weight above 4500 gm [2]. Macrosomia, negatively affect both maternal and neonatal outcomes [3]. Globally, macrosomia affects 3 to 15% of all pregnancies [4] There is a global variation in the magnitude of macrosomia ranging from 5 to 20% of all births [5]. It affects 12% of normal pregnancy and 15%-45% of mothers with gestational diabetes [6]. According to a report from births in the United States, in 2017, approximately 7.8% of infants had a birth weight > 4000 g, 1% had a birth weight greater than 4500 g, and 0.1% had a birth weight greater than 5000 g [7].

In Europe, the magnitude of macrosomia was 8.0% [8] According to a study conducted in Africa, the reported frequencies of fetal macrosomia were 2.7% in the Democratic Republic of Congo, 3.1% Angola, and 9.1% in Kenya [2]. In Ethiopia, the Ethiopia Demographic Health Survey (EDHS) of 2011 reported that the prevalence of fetal macrosomia among 5% of weighted infants at birth was 10.46% [9]. Recent studies have shown that macrosomia prevalence in Ethiopia ranges from 6.7% to 19.1% [10, 11] Different risk factors have been identified as the cause of macrosomia, these are maternal age; multiparity, gestational age (GA), previous macrosomia baby, ethnicity, and being male [8, 12] Fetal macrosomia can lead to life-threatening complications for both mothers and babies. Maternal complications can include a high rate of caesarean delivery, obstetrical maneuvers, tearing of the perineum, postpartum hemorrhage and the dreaded shoulder dystocia [13].

Fetal complications include birth asphyxia, clavicle fractures, and brachial plexus injuries in the fetus [14]. Brachial plexus injury is rare, with an incidence of fewer than 2 cases per 1000 vaginal deliveries. This risk is approximately 20 times higher when the birth weight is more than 4500 g [15] A systematic review performed in 2019 regarding neonatal and maternal complications associated with fetal macrosomia found that it was associated with serious neonatal and maternal issues [16]. Beyond the perinatal period, neonatal macrosomia is a determinant of diseases in adolescence and adulthood, notably obesity, hypertension and metabolic syndrome [17]. There are primary studies conducted in Ethiopia on the magnitude and risk factors of fetal macrosomia, and there is an individual variation among the primary studies in Ethiopia, so this systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to pool aggregate data or evidence on macrosomia for designing strategies that can potentially improve maternal and child health services.

Methods

Design

This study followed the systematic review and Meta-analysis recommended protocols of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [18].The procedures for the screening and selection of eligible studies was presented using the PRISMA flow diagram [18].

Protocol and registration

To ensure transparency and prevent duplicate efforts, the study protocol was retrospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD42023482946. Protocol registration was finalized following initial search deployments but prior to the commencement of formal statistical synthesis, meta-analysis pooling, and data charting. All eligible original studies were qualitatively described in terms of prevalence, study design, sample size, region and publication year.

Information source and Search strategy

A systematic, comprehensive search was systematically executed across five primary electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science, and African Journals OnLine (AJOL). The search scope was restricted to articles published between September 1, 2005, and December 31, 2023, limited to human studies and English-language publications.

The search architecture was strictly structured around a localized Population, Exposure, Comparison, and Outcome (PECO) framework: Population (P): Delivered neonates/newborns in Ethiopia; Exposure (E): Associated risk factors (e.g., maternal obesity, gestational diabetes, multiparity, advanced maternal age, male fetal sex); Comparison (C): Normal birth weight neonates (2500 gram to 3999 gram; and Outcome (O): Fetal macrosomia or large-for-gestational-age (LGA) status (≥ 4000gram).

International Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were paired with comprehensive Title/Abstract [tiab] free-text keywords using Boolean operators (AND, OR). To ensure complete reproducibility and eliminate retrieval bias, full, un-truncated database-specific search strings for all utilized electronic platforms—including explicit advanced search strings, syntax variations, exact execution dates (final search update run on January 13, 2024), and strict Google Scholar/Grey literature documentation parameters—are provided in Supplementary File 1. The final data extraction expansion was officially finalized on February 14, 2024. Reference lists of all eligible studies were manually screened via backward citation tracking to identify any additional non-indexed institutional reports.

Study selection

The articles gathered from different sources were exported to Endnote X7, and duplicates were identified and removed. The remaining articles were assessed with respect to the topic, study area, study participants, and language. The independent reviewers had screened the title and abstract of each of the studies. The agreement between the two reviewers was accepted [19]. After reaching in an agreement, a full-text review was performed (Fig. 1).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

This study included all observational study designs (cross-sectional, case-control, and cohort studies) published as full-text articles in the English language. To be eligible, studies must have reported on singleton deliveries and provided the primary outcome (fetal macrosomia) relative to total births. Only studies available electronically on or before December 31, 2023, were considered for inclusion.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded duplicate studies, anonymous reports, letters, editorials, commentaries, and articles published in languages other than English. Furthermore, we excluded qualitative studies, articles lacking accessible abstracts or full texts, and those that failed to report the primary outcomes. Finally, studies focusing exclusively on high-risk populations, such as diabetic pregnant women only, were also excluded.

Data extraction

After identification of Eligible studies, Data were extracted using the Microsoft Excel spreadsheet format, which was adopted from the JBI data extraction format [20] prepared on Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. It includes the primary investigator’s name, sample size, prevalence of macrosomia, associated factors, publication year, study setting/region, study design using a structured data abstraction format were extracted.

Data items

Outcome variables

Fetal macrosomia can be defined as a birth weight ≥ 4000 gram.

Participants

Studies reported on fetal macrosomia and associated factors among delivered neonates in Ethiopia.

Quality appraisal and risk of bias assessment

The quality of each article were assessed by two independent reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tool prepared for case–control, analytical, cross-sectional and descriptive cross-sectional studies each with 10, 8 and 9 item question respectively [21]. All of the tools have a ‘Yes’ and ‘No’ question types and scores was given 1 for a ‘Yes’ and 0 for a ‘No’ responses. Scores were summed up and transformed into a percentage. Only studies that scored 50% were considered for both systematic review and meta-analysis. The inter rater agreement score was 0.69. For any scoring disagreements occurred between the evaluators, the source of disagreement was investigated by detail revisions. The disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Summary measures

Effect measures were calculated for each study. Since, the data were dichotomous, effect sizes were expressed as prevalence rate and odds ratio (OR). To achieve scale symmetry for the analysis, the odds ratios underwent natural log transformations. The pooled log-transformed estimates were subsequently back-transformed to ratio values for intuitive interpretation.

Summary effects were evaluated using both fixed-effect and random-effects models. The fixed-effect model assumes that the true effect size is identical across all studies, meaning the summary effect represents our estimate of this single, common effect size. Conversely, the random-effects model assumes that the true effect size varies across studies, treating the included studies as a random sample drawn from a broader distribution of effect sizes.

Synthesis of results

The extracted data were exported to STATA/SE V.14 for comprehensive analysis., with data synthesis structured around presence or absence of fetal macrosomia. Heterogeneity among included studies was assessed by Forest plot, and I2 statistic. I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as representing low, moderate, and high levels of heterogeneity, respectively [22]. A pooled estimate of the overall prevalence was generated. To explore potential source of heterogeneity, meta-regression, sensitivity analysis and subgroup analyses were performed. The final results are presented through narratives, tables and forest plots. All statistical interpretations are based on p-values and 95% confidence intervals (CI), with statistical significance declared at a p-value < 0.05.

Risk of bias across studies

Publication bias was asseed subjectively by Funnel plot and objectively via Egger’s regression test. Visual asymmetry in the funnel plot or a statistically significant Egger’s test result (p < 0.05) is suggestive of potential publication bias.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis was conducted based on sample size, region and year of the study as a grouping variables and potential sources of variation.

Five studies were Cross-sectional [10, 11, 23–25], two studies were Case-control [26, 27] and one study was a comparative cross-sectional study [28]. A total 4309 neonates were delivered in Ethiopian health institutions. Six of the articles were from southern nations and nationalities, and Amhara regions. Two articles were from Tigray region (Table 1).

Meta-analysis on pooled prevalence of fetal macrosomia

Eight articles were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis, but six studies with 3670 singleton deliveries were used to pool the estimate. The pooled prevalence of fetal macrosomia among delivered neonates in Ethiopia was 10.84% [95%CI, 6.38%-15.31%]. The highest prevalence was reported from the study done in the Tigray region. Significant heterogeneity is reported from the pooled effect (I2= 71.6%, P-value = 0.004) 95% CI (Fig. 2).

Meta regression

Meta-regression was used to explore the sources of heterogeneity between the studies. A random effect meta-analysis was run by year of publication, region, and sample size. The analysis indicated that there was no heterogeneity by sample size (P = 0.08), publication year (P = 0.20), and Region (P-value (SNNP = 0.558, Tigray = 0.30, Amhara = 0.150).

Sensitivity analysis on pooled estimate of fetal macrosomia in Ethiopia

Sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the effect of studies, by excluding one study at a time and recalculating the prevalence rate to evaluate whether the summary prevalence was significantly influenced by any Individual Study. Based on the sensitivity analysis, no study had a notable influence on the overall estimate, the pooled prevalence varying between 9.1% [25] and 12.35% [22].

Publication Bias

The publication bias was assessed by using the Funnel plot for subjective assessment and objectively determined using Egger’s test at a 5% significant level. The funnel plots had asymmetrical distribution and Eggers’ regression test (P-value = 0.000) was significant. The Eggers’ test results showed that there is a significant publication bias (Fig. 3).

Factors associated with fetal macrosomia in Ethiopia

Five potential determinants were evaluated across the included primary studies: previous history of fetal macrosomia, neonatal sex, maternal physical inactivity, gestational weight gain, and advanced gestational age (gestational age ≥ 37weeks). In the random-effects meta-analysis, none of the evaluated factors demonstrated a statistically significant pooled association with fetal macrosomia, characterized by wide confidence interval and P-value > 0.05. Specifically, the pooled adjusted odds ratios indicated higher point estimates for mothers with a previous history of macrosomia (AOR = 4.23 (95%CI, 0.334–53.668, P-value = 0.265), gestational weight gain (AOR = 4.88(95%CI, 0.169–140.97,P-value = 0.355), advanced gestational age (AOR = 4.71(95%CI, 0.47–46.28,P-value = 0.184), maternal physical inactivity (AOR = 3.01( 95%CI, 0.229–39.614, P-value = 0.401), and male neonatal sex (AOR = 2.32(95%CI, 0.40-13.33, P-value = 0.845), These broad intervals and p-values indicate substantial statistical instability and a lack of precision across the pooled exposures.(Table 4).

Discussion

The current systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted to estimate the pooled prevalence of fetal macrosomia and associated factors among delivered neonates in Ethiopia. According to the current meta-analysis, the pooled prevalence of fetal macrosomia among delivered neonates in Ethiopia was 10.84% [95%CI, 6.38%-15.31%]. The current pooled estimate is comparable with a study done in Iran (9%) [29], EDHS data (10.4%) from Ethiopia [30] and a systematic review and meta-analysis (15.1%) done among diabetic women in Ethiopia [31]. Consistent findings were reported from a study done in Algeria (14.9%). The current finding was higher than a previous multicounty level analysis done in the United states(8%), Cambodia(2.3%) and Kenya (3.6%) [8]. It is also higher than a previously conducted study in Finland (3.4%) [32]. The possible variation could be due to differences in sample size (total number of births), socio economic status and geographical differences between the study populations. The higher prevalence of macrosomia might also be related to a higher prevalence of NCDs (diabetes and chronic hypertension) during pregnancy in Ethiopia.

The current pooled estimates were lower than a previously done comparative cross sectional study done in central India (31.4%) [33]. The variation could be the result of differences in the cut-point used to categorize macrosomia. According to the sub-group analysis by region, the Tigray region had the highest pooled estimate of fetal macrosomia (13.24%) and the lowest estimate was from Amhara region (7.41%). These regional variations point to potential underlying geographical, cultural, or local nutritional differences across the study populations, alongside potential differences in maternal access to antenatal care (ANC) screening. The sub-group analysis by publication year also showed that the pooled prevalence of fetal macrosomia was 12.3% before 2019 and 7.42% after 2019. This downward trend may be attributed to stricter implementation of clinical screening protocols, improved accuracy in maternal health recording systems, or evolving public health interventions targeting maternal metabolic risk profiles over the decade.

When evaluating potential determinants, our meta-analysis revealed substantial statistical imprecision and instability across several conventional risk factors, which warrants cautious epidemiological interpretation. Mothers with a previous history of macrosomia exhibited a high pooled point estimate (AOR = 4.23); however, this association did not reach statistical significance, displaying an excessively wide confidence interval and a non-significant p-value [95% CI: 0.334–53.668, p = 0.265]. This extreme interval width directly reflects data sparsity, high inter-study variance, and underpowered primary data configurations within the localized Ethiopian literature.

Similarly, while neonatal male sex demonstrated a point estimate in line with standard physiological paradigms (pooled OR = 2.32)—often attributed to genetic factors, uteroplacental growth dynamics [34], or increased fat-free mass in male fetuses [35] —other behavioral and anthropometric variables failed to demonstrate true statistical association. Maternal physical inactivity produced a point estimate of AOR = 3.014, but crossed the null line completely [95% CI: 0.229–39.614, p = 0.401]. Gestational weight gain (AOR = 4.88; 95% CI: 0.169–140.97, p = 0.355) and higher gestational age (AOR = 4.7; 95% CI: 0.47–46.28, p = 0.184) similarly demonstrated extreme statistical instability.

Although individual primary literature frequently highlights these variables as predictors due to maternal metabolic influences [36], glycemic intake patterns [37], or continuous hormonal fetal stimulation [38] our pooled meta-analytic outcomes cannot confirm them as statistically significant risk factors within this specific national pool. The exceptionally wide, unprecise 95% confidence intervals across these exposures underscore a critical systemic limitation in the primary Ethiopian literature: specifically, low sample counts within specific exposure groups, heterogeneous definitions of variables, and inadequate adjustment for confounding variables across primary researchers. Consequently, these findings highlight a pressing need for larger, highly standardized, prospectively tracked multicenter cohort studies across Ethiopia to generate stable, sufficiently powered estimations of these potential metabolic risk pathways.

Strength and limitation

A primary strength of this systematic review and meta-analysis is that it provides a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence regarding the pooled prevalence and potential determinants of fetal macrosomia across Ethiopia. however, Several critical limitations must be acknowledged. First, language bias may have been introduced as primary studies published in non-English languages were excluded from the protocol. Second, the primary studies meeting inclusion parameters were restricted to only three administrative regions of Ethiopia, which limits the geographical comprehensiveness and generalizability of the pooled prevalence to the entire diverse national population. Third, the pooled odds ratios for conventional risk factors exhibited severe statistical imprecision, characterized by excessively wide 95% confidence intervals and underpowered exposure groups, which restricts our ability to draw definitive causal conclusions. Lastly, although standardized critical appraisal checklists were employed, the risk of subjective inter-observer variation during quality assessment remains a potential limitation; however, this was mitigated by utilizing two independent reviewers and resolving discrepancies through consensus.

Conclusion and recommendation

Conclusion

Fetal macrosomia represents a prominent maternal and child health concern in Ethiopia, with a pooled national prevalence of 10.84%. Although behavioral and clinical factors trended toward higher point estimates, their pooled associations lacked statistical significance and displayed high imprecision, indicating that routine screening within antenatal care systems should be prioritized alongside more robust, adequately powered primary research.

Recommendations

Based on the empirical findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis, the following evidence-based recommendations are proposed:

❖ Healthcare facilities and concerned public health bodies in Ethiopia should prioritize and standardize fetal growth monitoring and metabolic screening within routine Antenatal Care services.❖Stakeholders and health ministries should integrate general maternal health education regarding balanced nutrition and safe physical activity into comprehensive ANC services as a supportive, low-risk component of care.❖Healthcare providers should continue to monitor maternal weight trajectories and gestational age benchmarks as part of standard obstetric safety protocols to mitigate general adverse perinatal outcomes.❖Stakeholders and health ministries should collaborate to improve the infrastructure of maternal and child health services, ensuring equitable access to high-quality diagnostic screening across all regions.❖Researchers in Ethiopia should prioritize large-scale, prospective longitudinal cohort studies with rigorous confounding adjustments to establish stable, definitive national risk pathways.
