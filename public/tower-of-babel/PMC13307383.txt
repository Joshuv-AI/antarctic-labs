From subtle symptoms to disclosure: Communication in violence detection in primary care
Authors: Orlane Pivert, Clémence Loubrieu, Julie Magret, Nicolas Naiditch, Stéphanie Mignot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdentifying victims of interpersonal violence remains a challenge for general practitioners, who must rely on subtle clinical cues and foster an environment conducive to disclosure.ObjectivesTo describe the symptoms, behaviours, and communication strategies that facilitate the recognition of interpersonal violence in primary care consultations.MethodsFor this qualitative study, general practice consultations by three physicians in the Poitou-Charentes department, France, were audio-recorded from June to September 2019. Adult patients (≥18 years, fluent in French) were included unless they rejected audio recording. Only the audio-recording of consultations during which interpersonal violence was disclosed were analysed following an inductive approach to identify categories and themes until saturation was reached.ResultsIn total 25/326 patients disclosed experiences of violence. Detection combined verbal cues (vague somatic complaints, ambiguous trauma references) by the patient and open-ended or direct questions by the general practitioners. Techniques included linking symptoms to violence, broad funnel questions, and most effectively, direct queries, such as ‘Did someone hurt you?. The three physicians adapted their approach, balancing respect for the patient readiness and clinical conviction, often revisiting the topic from different perspectives. Empathy and a trusted doctor-patient relationship proved crucial for violence disclosure.ConclusionOur findings highlight the value of the physicians’ tenacity and empathic attitude for identifying hidden situations of violence, and suggest that integrating these strategies in routine general practice can enhance detection. The findings underscore the need of training physicians in patient-centred communication and suggest future research avenues for refining detection protocols in primary care.

Background

Violence is defined as the intentional use of physical force or power, threatened or actual, against oneself, another person or a group or community that results in or has a high likelihood of resulting in injury, death, psychological harm, maldevelopment or deprivation [1]. It is commonly categorised into self-directed, interpersonal, and collective violence. Interpersonal violence is divided into two categories: family and intimate partner violence (IPV), and community violence. Violent acts can be of a physical, sexual and/or psychological (involving deprivation or neglect) nature [1].

Interpersonal violence represents a major global public health issue. The World Health Organisation (WHO) estimates that nearly one in three women worldwide experiences physical or sexual violence by an intimate partner or non-partner in her lifetime [2]. According to UNICEF, one in four children under five lives with a mother who is a IPV victim and 15 million adolescent girls (15–19 years of age) have experienced forced sex [3]. Besides the immediate trauma, the impact of violence extends across the lifespan and affects both mental and physical health. Survivors present an increased risk of depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, suicidal behaviours and substance use, as well as somatic conditions such as chronic pelvic pain, fibromyalgia, and gastrointestinal disorders [4–6].

Recognising violence victims in primary care is critical, but remains challenging. Validated screening tools exist, such as the Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST), the Hurts, Insult, Threaten and Scream (HITS) and WAST-short tests [7,8], yet their direct application in general practice is often unrealistic. In agreement, a 2021 systematic review on IPV screening programmes found high screening reach (median = 82%), but substantial variability in positivity and referral rates. This emphasises that screening efficacy depends also on institutional support, training and referral infrastructure and not on tools alone [9].

As patients rarely disclose violence explicitly, presenting instead with somatic or psychological complaints, it is important to explore how signs of interpersonal violence can emerge naturally during a routine medical consultation. Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify the symptoms, behaviours, and attitudes expressed by patients in general practice consultations that may suggest medium- or long-term consequences of interpersonal violence.

Methods

Study design and participants

This qualitative study was conducted in general practice settings in the Poitou-Charentes department (Nouvelle-Aquitaine region), France. Three female physicians participated: one established GP in an urban practice in Poitiers, and two locum doctors, at the beginning of their career, in semi-rural or urban group practices. The aim was to identify patients’ symptoms, signs and behaviours during medical consultations that could suggest medium- or long-term consequences of past interpersonal violence. Participants were adult (≥18-year-old) patients who consulted their GP. Inclusion criteria were: sufficient proficiency in French to understand the study and to give their oral consent for audio recording their routine consultation. Consultation audio-recording was carried out for half or one day on several days.

Data collection

Routine general practice consultations were audio recorded using a digital recorder placed in the physician’s office. Consultations were conducted as usual, without any modification in the approach or management. Patients were invited to speak freely, and the detection of potential interpersonal violence was based on the physician’s usual clinical judgement, in line with their professional practice.

At the end of each consultation, each physician completed a short questionnaire in which they reported the patient demographic information, socio-professional status, context of the consultation, and immediate impressions, including the patient’s attitudes and behaviours. Consultations without mention of interpersonal violence were deleted immediately after the post-consultation questionnaire and not transcribed. Sampling continued until thematic saturation was reached. This was defined as the absence of new violence-related symptoms, behavioural patterns, or general practitioners’ communication strategies across consecutive consultations. Two investigators (initials) independently reviewed the collected transcripts weekly using the RQDA software application (add a link or reference).

Data collection took place between June and September 2019 and analyses were completed in November 2019.

Data analysis

The audio-recordings of violence-disclosing consultations were anonymised and transcribed verbatim at the end of each week during the data collection period. Ethics

According to the French Jardé law on research involving human beings of 16 November 2016, a notification was first sent the Committee for the Protection of Persons on non-interventional type 3 research. Ethics approval was obtained on 7 June, 2019 (number 19.02.04.56426).

Results

Sample

A total of 326 patients accepted the audio recording of their consultation and seven refused: two felt that the topic was not relevant to them, one refused to be questioned on the subject, and four did not provide any reason.

During the consultation, violence was mentioned by 25/326 patients (19 women and 6 men; ∼7.7% of consultations). The age of these 25 patients ranged from 18 to 80 years and the mean consultation time was 33.23 min. Almost all socio-professional categories were represented, except skilled trades workers, small business owners and farmers (Table 1). All violence types were identified (Table 2). Women reported experiencing violence more frequently than men, particularly IPV, and were more likely to have experienced multiple forms of violence throughout their lives.

Symptoms

Somatic manifestations

Such symptoms were typically vague, chronic, treatment-resistant, prompting repeated consultations. Pain, chronic fatigue, cardiorespiratory discomfort, headache and digestive problems were commonly reported (Table 3). Such complaints were rarely explained by a clear biomedical cause and were perceived by physicians as possible bodily expressions of psychological distress.

Gynaecological and urological symptoms also emerged across narratives (Table 3). Sleep disturbances, appetite changes, and unexplained weight variations were described as part of an overall malaise rather than a single condition.

Psychological and emotional symptoms

Patients frequently reported emotional lability, anxiety, and inner tension, sometimes with episodes of irritability or anger (Table 3).

The three physicians identified several consultations where depressive symptoms or anxiety were evident, but remained undeclared by the patient. Sometimes, patients revealed intrusive memories, flashbacks, or avoidance behaviours consistent with post-traumatic stress disorder.

Behavioural and coping responses

Some patients reported resorting to drugs, alcohol, tobacco, or other substances as a means of coping with stress (Table 3). The three physicians also identified risk-taking behaviours, including treatment non-adherence and impulsive actions, interpreted as indirect expressions of suffering. Other patients described a strong sense of responsibility towards others, positioning themselves as caregivers or protectors, sometimes to the detriment of their own health (Table 3).

Relational and social difficulties

Patients described difficulties in trusting others, feeling misunderstood, or avoiding discussions about personal issues. Experiences of divorce, family conflicts, or professional burnout were common. Some linked their current suffering to unresolved tensions at work or in intimate relationships (Table 3).

Physicians’ communication approach

The three physicians employed a three-step communication approach: (1) creating a safe space through empathic posture, (2) progressive questioning, and (3) persevering despite the patient’s initial hesitations. Tables 4 and 5 present illustrative quotations and Table 6 summarises these techniques.

Having an empathic posture

Physicians began by listening in an empathic, non-directive manner, often inviting free speech that yielded extended narratives, reflecting deliberate space-making (Table 4). Such listening combined verbal cues and attentive non-verbal behaviour (eye contact, open posture, nodding). Respect for silence played a pivotal role. These pauses were recognised by patients as meaningful for enabling them to formulate their thoughts and feel truly heard (Table 4).

The three physicians reinforced this supportive environment with short phrases, ‘yes’ ‘I’m listening’ ‘I understand,’ and when appropriate, mirrored the patient’s words to convey understanding.

Concomitantly, they engaged in active observation: body posture, gaze, emotional expression, speech, and even changes in dress or personal hygiene.

Patient autonomy remained paramount. The three physicians reminded patients that they could choose what to share, ‘We don’t have to talk about it if you don’t want to…’ (M2P4) and refrained from probing when disclosure was difficult (Table 4).

Questioning strategies

A ‘call’ sentence was used to address the patients’ comments or to pursue a lead that warranted further exploration. For example, when patients were unclear or only hinted at difficult experiences, physicians prompted them with interpretive extensions: ‘Am I to understand that he was very demanding of sexual relations?’ (M1P10), rather than letting implications go unspoken (Table 5).

General, non-stigmatising questions were frequently employed (e.g. ‘Is he a difficult person?,’ ‘Have you been hurt?’), allowing the patient to interpret freely and avoiding direct focus on the aggressor. If clarification was needed, the three physicians provided concrete examples:

‘He’s hot tempered and distant angry… he sulks, it always has to be his way, it never goes well whatever you do, he always has something to say.’ (M2P1), which sometimes enabled patients to recognise that their situation was not normal and to make connections between their symptoms and home life (Table 5).

The three physicians also used questions focused on the perpetrator’s behaviour (jealousy, control, ambivalence, aggression, insults, or isolation) and sometimes introduced the theme by explaining the cycle of violence, or by naming specific manipulative tactics, such as threats and guilt induction.

Physical violence was addressed with simple, direct vocabulary. Questions became more targeted as the conversation developed, sometimes culminating in exploratory or interpretative statements: ‘Have people ever hurt you in your life?’ (M2P11). Funnel questions were used to gently explore well-being, relational history, and home life (Table 5).

Persevere on questioning

When they identified a violence type, the three physicians routinely screened for other types of violence that may have occurred, consistent with the complex, cumulative nature of the phenomenon. If a patient responded negatively but warning signs persisted, physicians reformulated their questions, sometimes several times if they suspected a history of violence: ‘And was there other violence? Verbal, I mean, and psychological, from what you’re telling me. And also physical or sexual?’ (M1P11).

Some patients described vague or ‘fuzzy’ memories. Therefore, physicians had to patiently explore associations until gradually, significant disclosures emerged. Sometimes, patients completed the narrative, eventually linking their symptoms or current fears to childhood or adult trauma.

Discussion

Main findings

This qualitative study found that violence detection in general practice involves three steps: empathic and safe attitude, facilitative communication strategies, and persistent adaptive questioning. In our study, disclosures often emerged after GPs created space for free speech, responded attentively to indirect references or ‘test disclosures,’ and noticed links between patients’ symptoms and their relational or domestic context. Direct, simple questions, such as ‘Has anyone hurt you?’ or ‘Is he a difficult person?’ proved both accessible and effective. Linking vague, chronic, or treatment-resistant symptoms to possible histories of violence appeared to be a particularly pragmatic strategy, as it often helped patients recognise connections they had not initially made. When suspicion persisted, GPs reformulated their questions, screened for other forms of violence, and continued the dialogue until patients were ready to disclose.

Comparison with existing literature

Similarly, a clinical review on IPV screening and physician interventions also recommends talking first about the subject, using a general statement about the prevalence of violence against women, followed by an open question ‘Because violence against women is so common in our society, I ask all my women patients about violence in their lives. Do you feel safe in your current relationship?’ [11]. In our study, this type of question was often used, allowing a very broad discussion. The other questions were ‘Is he harsh?,’ ‘Is he complicated?.’

The progressive, funnel-shaped diagnostic approach is recommended by the Belgian Scientific Society of General Medicine [12]. It starts with open questions, with various entry points, adapted to the context (e.g. home life), up to questions about true acts of violence (all types). Even if violence is denied, progressive questioning may bring it out during the consultation or later and opens a door to possible help. The WHO suggests using general remarks [1] and proposes five simple tasks for frontline support for victims of IPV and sexual assault [13]. It also recommends to use straightforward questions to identify threats, injuries, insults, brutality, control over money or dating, forced sex, as found in the present study and in a previous clinical review [11], for instance ‘Does your partner hit you, threaten you, try to control you?.’

Many standardised tools, such as the WAST, have been developed to support the identification of intimate partner violence in primary care. Although the French version has excellent sensitivity (97.7%) and specificity (97.1%) in primary care [7], our study was specifically designed to explore how violence emerges during routine consultations in French general practice, rather than to evaluate the implementation of a specific questionnaire. Typically, consultations with a GP are short, patients usually consult for other reasons, and the therapeutic relationship relies heavily on continuity and a face‑to‑face dialogue centred on the patient’s immediate concerns. Therefore, a formal screening instrument at each visit may be difficult to integrate into the daily workflow and could be perceived as distancing or intrusive by both patients and GPs, especially when the reason for the encounter is unrelated to violence. Our findings highlight how empathic listening, attention to somatic and psychological cues, and progressive questioning can be used during a consultation to facilitate disclosure.

Recent studies conducted after our fieldwork provide additional context for interpreting our findings. A trial in the United States of America (USA) assessed both the implementation and effectiveness of an IPV screening programme. It found that implementation facilitation strategies can triple screening rates and double population-level IPV detection in primary care, although disclosure among screened patients remains stable at 7-10% [14]. Another USA trial showed that electronic health record-based confidential screening significantly outperforms routine nurse-led oral screening (past-year IPV prevalence: 1.5% versus 0.1%), highlighting the critical role of privacy and non-interruptive clinician alerts [15]. Recent European primary care surveys confirmed the suboptimal gender-based violence detection despite protocols. In addition, a survey of healthcare workers in the United Kingdom found that despite the high rates of domestic abuse also among primary care staff, workplace support is often lacking [16]. Similarly, a study in Spain stressed that gender-based violence detection in primary care needs to improve [17].

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is the analysis of consultation audio-recordings, which is rarely used in qualitative research in medicine. This provided rich material for studying the behaviour and reactions of physicians and patients, as well as their interactions. The originality of this study lies in the fact that it focused on the detection of current and past violence, all violence types, not just domestic or sexual violence.

This study has some limitations. First, the small number of positive cases limits the scope and findings remain exploratory. Additional studies with larger samples are required to consolidate these observations. Second, the lack of transcription and analysis of non-disclosure consultations precluded a comparative analysis; however, resource constraints justified prioritising the thorough analysis only of disclosure cases. Third, this study was limited to three physicians and one region. The three physicians were purposely selected to reflect the diversity in experience, age, and practice settings typical of French general practice. Their medical training background is in line with the French national standards. The study was carried out in France’s largest region, Nouvelle-Aquitaine. This region’s extensive mixed urban-rural demographic (84,000 km2, ∼6 M inhabitants) and socioeconomic diversity is representative of the French national primary care contexts. Moreover, thematic saturation, coder triangulation, and patient/practice diversity support transferability beyond the specific context. Fourth, all participating physicians were women, and this could have influenced the patients’ responses or the sensitivity of disclosure. Fifth, the detection rates might have been influenced by a social desirability bias. Future research should include GPs of different genders, backgrounds, and clinical approaches to better evaluate the universality and applicability of these findings. Sixth, data were collected between June and September 2019, which represents a six-year interval between fieldwork and publication. Nevertheless, several factors support the ongoing validity and relevance of our results. Interpersonal violence remains a persistent structural phenomenon and global prevalence estimates have remained largely stable over the past decade [18,19]. In France, IPV continues to concern a significant proportion of women consulting in primary care, without evidence of substantial declines in incidence or health service utilisation patterns since 2019. Furthermore, the core features of French general practice (i.e. short multi-purpose consultations, emphasis on therapeutic continuity, and reliance on the GP-patient trust) have not undergone major organisational changes that would fundamentally alter the GP-patient interaction dynamics. Our focus on enduring relational and communicative processes (empathic listening, symptom interpretation, progressive questioning) rather than time-sensitive epidemiological indicators further enhances the transferability of these qualitative insights to current practice.

Recommendations for general practice

Recognising and facilitating disclosure of interpersonal violence

Empathy is central to violence recognition and management in medical consultations. It allows GPs to identify subtle verbal and non-verbal cues and to establish the therapeutic alliance necessary to explore sensitive issues [20–22]. In our study, disclosures often followed attentive responses to indirect references, ‘test disclosures,’ symptom–violence associations, or non-verbal expressions of distress.

From the patient’s perspective, empathy is associated with being heard, understood, and treated with humanity [22]. An empathic, non-judgmental posture fosters trust and creates a safe environment for disclosure. Emotional empathy may further support this relational dynamic [23,24]. Psychological violence is particularly difficult to detect, as patients may minimise, normalise, or fail to recognise abusive dynamics [25]. Awareness of behavioural patterns such as control, jealousy, ambivalence, and isolation is therefore essential.

Supporting patients’ awareness and insight

Many patients do not immediately recognise their experiences as violence and may attribute their symptoms to external circumstances or personal vulnerability [25,26]. Trauma-related mechanisms, including impaired insight, dissociation, or normalisation, may further hinder this recognition [27].

Helping patients to establish links between their symptoms and possible experiences of violence is a key component of care. This process should be gradual and patient-centred, avoiding premature labelling and allowing patients to develop their own understanding over time [28].

Communicating about violence: strategies and ethical considerations

Effective communication relies on simple, non-judgmental, and behaviour-focused questions. Concrete experiences such as jealousy, control, isolation, or humiliation can help patients reflect on their situation without feeling judged or pressured. These conversations often need to be revisited across multiple consultations.

Introducing the concept of violence may raise ethical considerations, as reframing experiences can imply a professional interpretation of the patient’s lived experience [29]. GPs should therefore adopt a cautious and collaborative approach, supporting insight while respecting the patient’s perspective, language, and readiness. Once violence is identified, appropriate referral and follow-up should be considered as part of ongoing care.

Conclusion

These qualitative findings suggest that the identification of interpersonal violence in primary care depends on specific communicative qualities rather than on screening alone. Important qualities include non-judgmental listening, attention to indirect cues, progressive questioning, and repeated opportunities for disclosure over time. Our findings suggest that this communication-based approach could complement structured screening strategies, particularly when used selectively in patients presenting with vague, chronic, or relationally suggestive symptoms. Future studies should assess which communicative behaviours are most effective, whether brief screening prompts can be integrated into the clinical interview, and how these approaches compare with standardised protocols in different primary care settings.
