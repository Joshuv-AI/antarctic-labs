Abstract
. We study the Falconer distance set problem in Euclidean space and obtain improved dimensional estimates under natural Fourier analytic assumptions cast in terms of the Fourier dimension and spectrum. Interestingly, under reasonably mild assumptions, we are able to beat the d / 2 d/2 dimension threshold in dimensions d ⩾ 5 d\geqslant 5 . For example, we show that (in any ambient spatial dimension d d ) a Borel set with Fourier dimension at least 2 2 has a distance set of full Hausdorff dimension. We also show that (in any ambient spatial dimension d d ) a Borel set with Fourier spectrum at least d / 4 + 1 d/4+1 at θ = 1 / 2 \theta=1/2 has a distance set of full Hausdorff dimension. In particular, this can hold for sets with Fourier dimension zero (provided d ⩾ 4 d\geqslant 4 ). We also consider pinned variants of these problems and construct examples that demonstrate the sharpness (or near sharpness) of our results. Mathematics Subject Classification 2020 . primary: 28A80, 42B10; secondary: 28A75, 28A78. Key words and phrases : distance set problem, Hausdorff dimension, Fourier dimension, Fourier spectrum.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The distance set problem and summary of our results

The
distance set problem
is a famous and important problem in geometric measure theory. It was introduced in an influential paper of Falconer from 1985
[
8
]
, but can be viewed as a continuous analogue of the famous distance problem of Erdős which asks how many distinct distances are guaranteed to be determined by a set of
N
points in the plane.

Given a Borel set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
distance set
is defined by

D(E)=\{|x-y|:x,y\in E\},

that is, it is the set of distances realised between points in
E
. The distance set problem is then to determine how large
D(E)
must be, given certain geometric constraints on
E
. Here we interpret ‘large’ in terms of Hausdorff dimension, and the geometric constraint on
E
might simply be that it is itself large in terms of dimension. For example, one of the most well-known versions of the problem is the conjecture that

(1.1)

\dim_{\textup{H}}E\geqslant d/2\ \Rightarrow\ \dim_{\textup{H}}D(E)=1.

This conjecture is open for all
d\geqslant 2
despite sustained interest over several decades and much recent progress, see
[
5
,
7
,
6
,
12
,
14
,
17
]
. It is known, and important to keep in mind for this paper, that the threshold
d/2
in (
1.1
) cannot be improved, see
[
8
]
. That is, there exist (compact)
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\dim_{\textup{H}}E
arbitrarily close to (but smaller than)
d/2
, but yet
\dim_{\textup{H}}D(E)<1
. The sets used to demonstrate the
d/2
threshold are very rigid and possess a lot of arithmetic resonance. These features mean that the Fourier analytic behaviour of measures supported on these examples must be very bad. This perhaps suggests that, under mild Fourier analytic assumptions, the threshold
d/2
might be lowered. This is our main objective in this paper. In the opposite direction from the rigid examples described above, it is natural to consider sets with optimal Fourier decay, namely, Salem sets (see the next section for a precise definition). Mattila proved in
[
15
]
that the distance set problem (
1.1
) can be resolved for Salem sets in the sense that if
E
is Salem, then the conjectured implication

\dim_{\textup{H}}E\geqslant d/2\Rightarrow\dim_{\textup{H}}D(E)=1

indeed holds. We show that this result can be dramatically improved in high dimensions by proving that if
E
is Salem, then

\dim_{\textup{H}}E\geqslant 2\Rightarrow\dim_{\textup{H}}D(E)=1,

see
Theorem
2.4
and
Corollaries
2.7
and
2.8
. In particular, our result beats the
d/2
threshold for
d\geqslant 5
. This result is a special case of more general results which require much weaker Fourier analytic assumptions. These assumptions are cast in terms of the Fourier spectrum and give concrete, checkable, and relatively mild conditions ensuring that the distance set of a given set is of full Hausdorff dimension or even has positive 1-dimensional Lebesgue measure, see
Corollaries
2.5
and
2.7
.

We also consider the more general problem where one studies the distances realised between two given sets, see our
main Theorem
2.1
as well as
Corollary
2.3
, and the problem where one fixes a ‘pin’ and considers only distances realised between the pin and other points. More precisely, given Borel sets
E,F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the
distance set

D(E,F)=\{|x-y|:x\in E,y\in F\}.

Then
D(E,E)=D(E)
and

D(\{x\},E)=:D_{x}(E)

is the
pinned
distance set for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
x\in E
, then
D_{x}(E)\subseteq D(E)
and
a priori
the pinned distance set should be much smaller than the full distance set. In particular, there is interest in proving the stronger conclusion that, for example, there exists
x\in E
such that
\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(E)=1
or that
\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(E)=1
for ‘many’ pins
x\in E
. Our results are of this type and we are often able to find a set of pins
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
of positive Hausdorff dimension where the desired conclusion holds.

1.2.
Hausdorff dimension, the Fourier spectrum and previous work

In this section we briefly recall and discuss our key notions of dimension, including the Hausdorff dimension, Fourier dimension, and the Fourier spectrum.

In order to define the Hausdorff dimension, first define the
s
-energy
of a finite Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

I_{s}(\mu)=\iint|x-y|^{-s}\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y).

The
Hausdorff dimension
of a Borel set
E
may then be defined by

\dim_{\textup{H}}E=\sup\{s\geqslant 0:\exists\mu\in\mathcal{M}(E):I_{s}(\mu)<\infty\}

where
\mathcal{M}(E)
denotes the set of finite, non-zero Borel measures with support contained in
E
. Deriving the Hausdorff dimension via this alternative definition is often referred to as the
potential theoretic method
; see
[
9
,
16
]
for more details. These energy integrals can be expressed in terms of the Fourier transform, and this connection opens up a rich interplay between fractal geometry and Fourier analysis. More precisely, the
s
-energy of
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for
s\in(0,d)
, satisfies

(1.2)

I_{s}(\mu)=C({d,s})\int\big|\widehat{\mu}(z)\big|^{2}|z|^{s-d}\,dz

where
C(d,s)
is a constant depending only on
s
and
d
. This relationship between the Hausdorff dimension of sets and the Fourier transform of measures they support motivates the definition of the
Fourier dimension
of a finite Borel measure. This captures the optimal decay rate of the Fourier transform and is defined by

\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu=\sup\big\{s\geqslant 0:\sup_{z}\big|\widehat{\mu}(z)\big|^{2}|z|^{s}<\infty\big\}.

The
Fourier dimension
of a Borel set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is then defined by

\dim_{\mathrm{F}}E=\sup\big\{\min\{\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu,d\}:\mu\in\mathcal{M}(E)\big\}.

By leveraging a uniform decay estimate for the Fourier transform in the Fourier analytic expression for energy, one can easily check that

0\leqslant\dim_{\mathrm{F}}E\leqslant\dim_{\textup{H}}E\leqslant d.

Moreover, these inequalities can be strict in any combination. Sets for which the Fourier and Hausdorff dimensions coincide are called
Salem sets
and such sets can be thought of as having ‘optimal Fourier analytic properties’.

The Fourier spectrum was introduced by Fraser in
[
11
]
to interpolate between the Fourier and Hausdorff dimensions. As observed above, the Fourier dimension concerns
L^{\infty}
decay of the Fourier transform, and the Hausdorff dimension concerns
L^{2}
decay. If the Fourier and Hausdorff dimensions are distinct, then it is natural to try to understand the decay in an appropriate
L^{p}
sense and this is what the Fourier spectrum does.

In order to define the Fourier spectrum, first define
(s,\theta)
-energies
of
\mu
by

\mathcal{J}_{s,\theta}(\mu)=\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\widehat{\mu}(z)\big|^{\frac{2}{\theta}}|z|^{\frac{s}{\theta}-d}\,dz\bigg)^{\theta},

for
\theta\in(0,1]

and, for
\theta=0
, by

\mathcal{J}_{s,0}(\mu)=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\widehat{\mu}(z)\big|^{2}|z|^{s}.

Then the
Fourier spectrum
of
\mu
at
\theta\in[0,1]
is defined by

\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\mu=\sup\{s\geqslant 0:\mathcal{J}_{s,\theta}(\mu)<\infty\},

and, for each
\theta\in[0,1]
,
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu\leqslant\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\mu\leqslant\dim_{\mathrm{S}}\mu\leqslant\dim_{\textup{H}}\mu
, with equality on the left if
\theta=0
and equality on the right if
\theta=1
. Here
\dim_{\mathrm{S}}\mu
denotes the Sobolev (or energy) dimension of
\mu
and
\dim_{\textup{H}}\mu
denotes the Hausdorff dimension of
\mu
defined by

\dim_{\textup{H}}\mu=\inf\{\dim_{\textup{H}}E:\mu(E)>0\}.

We write
\operatorname{supp}\mu
for the support of
\mu
and then

\dim_{\textup{H}}\mu\leqslant\dim_{\textup{H}}\operatorname{supp}\mu.

As a function of
\theta
,
\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\mu
is concave and continuous for
\theta\in(0,1]
by
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
and, in addition, continuous at
\theta=0
provided
\mu
is compactly supported by
[
11
, Theorem 1.3]
. For a Borel set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
Fourier spectrum
is defined by

\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}E=\sup\big\{\min\{\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\mu,d\}:\mu\in\mathcal{M}(E)\big\}.

Then, for all
\theta\in[0,1]
,
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}E\leqslant\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}E\leqslant\dim_{\textup{H}}E
, with equality on the left if
\theta=0
and equality on the right if
\theta=1
. Moreover,
\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}E
is continuous for all
\theta\in[0,1]
by
[
11
, Theorem 1.5]
. Unlike for measures,
\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}E
need not be concave.

The main purpose of the Fourier spectrum is to provide a more nuanced quantitative description of the Fourier analytic properties of sets and measures and to leverage this description to make progress on various problems where there is an interplay between Fourier analysis and fractal geometry. The Fourier spectrum has already found numerous applications of this type, often leading to stronger results than one can get by appealing to the Fourier dimension alone. These applications include new Hausdorff dimension estimates in the Falconer distance problem
[
11
, Section 7]
and the celebrated restriction problem in harmonic analysis
[
1
]
. In particular, the following was obtained in
[
11
]
. Here and throughout, we write
\mathcal{L}^{1}
for 1-dimensional Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
and note that
\mathcal{L}^{1}(E)>0
is a strictly stronger conclusion than
\dim_{\textup{H}}E=1
.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
11
]
, Thm. 7.3)
.

Suppose
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are finite Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta}(\mu_{1})<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{J}_{v,1-\theta}(\mu_{2})<\infty

for some
u,v\geqslant 0
and
\theta\in(0,1]
. If
u+v\geqslant d
, then

\mathcal{L}^{1}\!\big(D(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{1},\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\big)>0,

and, if
u+v<d
, then

\dim_{\textup{H}}D(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{1},\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\ \geqslant\ 1-d+u+v.

This extends Mattila’s result, which relies only on Fourier dimension. Indeed, a simple consequence of the above is that

(1.3)

\dim_{\textup{F}}^{1/2}E\geqslant d/2\Rightarrow\dim_{\textup{H}}D(E)=1

holds. In particular, this solves the distance set conjecture at the
d/2
threshold for a much larger class of sets than Salem sets. Indeed, a set
E
may have Fourier dimension 0, but still have
\dim_{\textup{F}}^{1/2}E=d/2
. In this paper we significantly improve the above theorem, for example, replacing the
d/2
threshold in (
1.3
) with
d/4+1
, see
Corollary
2.8
.

2.
Main results

In this section we state our main results. First we give a general result and then we state some corollaries which may be simpler to digest and to compare with the literature.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
, and let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
compact supports. Let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[0,1]
and write

u=\dim_{\textup{F}}^{\theta_{1}}\mu_{1}

and

a=\min\!\left\{\frac{d}{2},\,\dim_{\textup{F}}^{\theta_{2}}\mu_{2},\,\frac{\dim_{\textup{F}}^{\theta_{2}}\mu_{2}}{2\theta_{2}}\right\}.

Assume
u\leqslant d
and define

\beta(u):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{u+2\theta_{1}a-d\theta_{1}}{2},&0\leqslant u<d\theta_{1},\\[8.61108pt]
\dfrac{ua}{d},&d\theta_{1}\leqslant u\leqslant d.\end{cases}

If
\beta(u)>0
, then

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\geqslant\min\{1,\beta(u)\},

for
\mu_{1}
almost all
x
and, if
\beta(u)>1
, then

\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\bigr)>0

for
\mu_{1}
almost all
x
. In particular,

\dim_{\textup{H}}D(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{1},\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\geqslant\min\{1,\beta(u)\},

and if
\beta(u)>1
, then

\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(D(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{1},\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\bigr)>0.

Remark 2.2
.

By convention, for
\theta_{2}=0
we interpret

\frac{\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{\theta_{2}}\mu_{2}}{2\theta_{2}}=+\infty.

Hence, in this case

a=\min\left\{\frac{d}{2},\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{0}\mu_{2},\infty\right\}=\min\left\{\frac{d}{2},\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu_{2}\right\}.

We prove Theorem
2.1
in Section
5
. The assumption that
u\leqslant d
is for convenience and if
u>d
then we can take
\beta(u)=a
. The case
u>d
is not so interesting since by
[
16
, Theorem 5.4]
the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of
\operatorname{supp}\mu_{1}
is positive and then it is easy to show that (for example) the 1-dimensional Lebesgue measure of
D(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{1},\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})
is also positive.

The special case of the above when
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=1/2
is notable. Partly because it makes it easy to compare with Theorem
1.1
, but also because this is the regime in which our proof approach works most directly. We will also scrutinise the ‘near sharpness’ of this result in Section
3
. We assume
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{1/2}\mu_{1},\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{1/2}\mu_{2}\leqslant d/2
in the following since this is the case of interest and it also simplifies the exposition.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
, and let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
compact supports. Further, assume both
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{1/2}\mu_{1}\leqslant d/2
and
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{1/2}\mu_{2}\leqslant d/2
. Then

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\geqslant\min\left\{1,\,\frac{\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{1/2}\mu_{1}+\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{1/2}\mu_{2}}{2}-\frac{d}{4}\right\}

for
\mu_{1}
almost all
x
.

Note that the previous corollary gives a (trivial) lower bound of zero in the case
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{1/2}\mu_{1}+\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{1/2}\mu_{2}=d/2
. Perhaps surprisingly, this is sharp, see Proposition
3.1
where we construct measures with dimensions satisfying this equality for which there is only a single distance realised in
D(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{1},\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})
.

Theorem
2.1
works especially well at
\theta=1/2
and generally for larger
\theta
. In another direction, we obtain the following theorem which works especially well at
\theta=0
and generally for small
\theta
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
, and let
\mu
be a compactly supported probability
measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(1)

For
\mu
-almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\geqslant\min\left\{\frac{\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu}{2},1\right\}.

Moreover, if
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu>2
, then

\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\bigr)>0\qquad\text{for $\mu$-almost all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2)

Fix
\theta\in(0,1]
. Then

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\geqslant\min\left\{\frac{\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\mu-d\theta}{2},1\right\}\qquad\text{for $\mu$-almost all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

In particular, if

\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\mu>2+d\theta,

then

\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\bigr)>0\qquad\text{for $\mu$-almost all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We prove Theorem
2.4
in Section
6
. Next we state a corollary which combines the large
\theta
and small
\theta
estimates from Theorems
2.1
and
2.4
. The aim is to bound the Hausdorff dimension of the pinned distance set of a single set from below for a large set of pins. Again we start with a general result and then specialise to statements which are easier to digest.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and let
\mu
be a compactly supported probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Fix
\theta\in[0,1]
, and write

s=\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{\theta}\mu.

Define

\beta(s):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{s}{2},&\theta=0,\\[8.61108pt]
\max\!\left\{\dfrac{s-d\theta}{2},\,\min\left\{\dfrac{s(1+2\theta)-d\theta}{2},\dfrac{s}{2},\dfrac{s^{2}}{d}\right\}\right\},&0<\theta<\dfrac{1}{2},\\[12.91663pt]
\min\left\{s-\dfrac{d\theta}{2},\dfrac{s}{2}\right\},&\dfrac{1}{2}\leqslant\theta\leqslant 1.\end{cases}

If
\beta(s)>0
, then

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\geqslant\min\{1,\beta(s)\}\qquad\text{for $\mu$-almost all }x.

Moreover, if
\beta(s)>1
, then
\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\bigr)>0
for
\mu
-almost all
x
.

Remark 2.6
.

In the regime
0<\theta<\frac{1}{2}
, the quantity

\max\!\left\{\frac{s-d\theta}{2},\,\min\left\{\frac{s(1+2\theta)-d\theta}{2},\frac{s}{2},\frac{s^{2}}{d}\right\}\right\}

admits the equivalent piecewise form

\beta(s)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{s(1+2\theta)-d\theta}{2},&0\leqslant s\leqslant d\theta,\\[8.61108pt]
\max\!\left\{\dfrac{s-d\theta}{2},\dfrac{s^{2}}{d}\right\},&d\theta\leqslant s\leqslant\dfrac{d}{2},\\[12.91663pt]
\dfrac{s}{2},&\dfrac{d}{2}\leqslant s\leqslant d.\end{cases}

We now record certain special cases of the previous corollary corresponding to the values
\theta=1,1/2,0
. Recall that
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{1}\mu=\dim_{\mathrm{S}}\mu
and
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{0}\mu=\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu
.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
d\geqslant 4
and let
\mu
be a compactly supported probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(1)

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\geqslant\min\left\{\dim_{\mathrm{S}}\mu-\frac{d}{2},1\right\}
for
\mu
-almost all
x
.

(2)

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\geqslant\min\left\{\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{1/2}\mu-\frac{d}{4},1\right\}
for
\mu
-almost all
x
.

(3)

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\geqslant\min\left\{\frac{\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu}{2},1\right\}
for
\mu
-almost all
x
.

It is also of particular interest to determine conditions which guarantee that the distance set has full dimension. The next corollary gives simple, checkable, and relatively mild conditions depending on the Fourier spectrum.

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
d\geqslant 4
and let
\mu
be a compactly supported probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
\theta\in[0,1]
, define

T_{d}(\theta):=\begin{cases}2+d\theta,&0\leqslant\theta<\dfrac{\sqrt{d}-2}{d},\\[4.30554pt]
\sqrt{d},&\dfrac{\sqrt{d}-2}{d}\leqslant\theta<d^{-1/2},\\[4.30554pt]
\dfrac{2+d\theta}{1+2\theta},&d^{-1/2}\leqslant\theta<\dfrac{1}{2},\\[8.61108pt]
1+\dfrac{d\theta}{2},&\dfrac{1}{2}\leqslant\theta\leqslant 1.\end{cases}

If, for some
\theta\in[0,1]
,

\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{\theta}\mu\geqslant T_{d}(\theta),

then

\dim_{\textup{H}}D(\operatorname{supp}\mu)=1.

Moreover,

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)=1

for
\mu
-almost all
x
.

Remark 2.9
.

For simplicity of exposition, we chose to present the results in this section in terms of the Fourier dimension and spectrum of measures rather than sets. However, by appealing to the definition of the Fourier spectrum of sets, one may produce statements about the distance set
D(E)
of a given Borel set
E
based on the Fourier spectrum of
E
. We leave the details to the reader. Furthermore, our results on pinned distance sets are achieved for ‘almost all pins’, but these statements can be turned into statements about sets of pins of large Hausdorff dimension. For example, if
\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)=1
for
\mu
-almost all
x
, then
\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)=1
for a set of pins
x\in\operatorname{supp}\mu
of Hausdorff dimension at least
\dim_{\textup{H}}\mu
. Moreover,
\dim_{\textup{H}}\mu
can in turn be bounded from below by
\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\mu
for any
\theta\in[0,1]
of interest. Again, we leave the details to the reader.

3.
Sharpness examples

In this section we construct examples pertaining to the sharpness (or near sharpness) of our results from the previous section.

First we build an example of a pair of compact sets which together witness very few distances but yet both satisfy good Fourier analytic estimates.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
d\geqslant 4
, and set

k_{1}=\left\lceil\frac{d}{2}\right\rceil,\qquad k_{2}=\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor,\qquad k_{1}+k_{2}=d.

Then, there exist compact sets
E,F\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that:

(i)

Only a single distance is realised between
E
and
F
, that is,

D(E,F)=\{\sqrt{2}\},\qquad\text{and so}\qquad\dim_{\textup{H}}D(E,F)=0;

(ii)

The Fourier spectra satisfy

\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}E\geqslant\min\{\theta k_{1},k_{1}-1\}

and

\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}F\geqslant\min\{\theta k_{2},k_{2}-1\}.

In particular, when
\theta=1/2
,
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{1/2}E+\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{1/2}F=d/2
and so the bound from Corollary
2.3
is sharp.

Proof.

Write
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{k_{2}}
, and let

E=S^{k_{1}-1}\times\{0\},\qquad F=\{0\}\times S^{k_{2}-1}.

Let
\sigma_{k_{1}-1}
and
\sigma_{k_{2}-1}
denote the normalized surface measures on the corresponding spheres, and define

\mu=\sigma_{k_{1}-1}\otimes\delta_{0},\qquad\nu=\delta_{0}\otimes\sigma_{k_{2}-1}.

Then
\mu
and
\nu
are probability measures supported on
E
and
F
, respectively.

If
x=(x_{1},0)\in E
and
y=(0,y_{2})\in F
, then

|x-y|^{2}=|x_{1}|^{2}+|y_{2}|^{2}=1+1=2.

Therefore,
D(E,F)=\{\sqrt{2}\}
and so
\dim_{\textup{H}}D(E,F)=0
.

Since
\mu
and
\nu
are product measures, the desired estimates for the Fourier spectra follow from
[
10
, Theorem 2.1]
where we use the fact that the surface measure on the sphere is Salem (and so has Fourier spectrum constantly equal to the Hausdorff dimension). This completes the proof.
∎

In the next example, we allow larger Fourier spectra, while keeping the distance set with dimension strictly smaller than 1.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
k_{1},k_{2}\geqslant 2
, set

d=k_{1}+k_{2}+1

and fix
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Then there exist compact sets
E,F\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that:

(i)

The sets
E
and
F
realise a small set of distances, that is,

\dim_{\textup{H}}D(E,F)\leqslant 2\alpha<1;

(ii)

The Fourier spectra satisfy:

\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}E\geqslant\min\{(k_{1}+1)\theta,k_{1}\theta+\alpha,k_{1}-1+\alpha\}

and

\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}F\geqslant\min\{(k_{2}+1)\theta,k_{2}\theta+\alpha,k_{2}-1+\alpha\}.

Proof.

By
[
3
, Theorem 2]
, for the chosen
\alpha\in(0,1)
there exists a compact set
A\subset[0,1]
and a probability measure
\lambda
supported on
A
such that

\lambda([x-r,x+r])\approx r^{\alpha}\qquad(x\in A,\ 0<r<1),

and

|\widehat{\lambda}(\tau)|\lesssim|\tau|^{-\alpha/2}(\log|\tau|)^{1/2}\qquad(|\tau|\geqslant 2).

The lower regularity bound shows that
\operatorname{supp}\lambda=A
, and the two-sided interval estimate implies that
A
is
\alpha
-Ahlfors regular. The Fourier decay bound implies that
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\lambda\geqslant\alpha
and since it is supported on a set with Hausdorff dimension
\alpha
we get that
\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\lambda=\alpha
for all
\theta\in[0,1]
. Now set

A_{1}=1+A\subset[1,2],\qquad A_{2}=5+A\subset[5,6],

and let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
be the translates of
\lambda
to
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, and observe that translation preserves Fourier spectrum. Let
\sigma_{k_{1}-1}
be the normalised surface measure on
S^{k_{1}-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}}
, and define

\mu=\sigma_{k_{1}-1}\otimes\lambda_{1}\otimes\delta_{0},\qquad\nu=\delta_{0}\otimes\lambda_{2}\otimes\sigma_{k_{2}-1}

on
\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{k_{2}}=\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus

E=S^{k_{1}-1}\times A_{1}\times\{0\},\qquad F=\{0\}\times A_{2}\times S^{k_{2}-1}.

Take
x=(u,t_{1},0)\in E
and
y=(0,t_{2},v)\in F
. Then

|x-y|^{2}=|u|^{2}+|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{2}+|v|^{2}=2+|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{2}.

Therefore

D(E,F)=\left\{\sqrt{2+|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{2}}:t_{1}\in A_{1},\ t_{2}\in A_{2}\right\}.

Since
A_{2}-A_{1}\subset[3,5]
, the map

f(t)=\sqrt{2+t^{2}}

is bi-Lipschitz on
[3,5]
. Hence

\dim_{\textup{H}}D(E,F)=\dim_{\textup{H}}(A_{2}-A_{1}).

Since
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are
\alpha
-Ahlfors regular, we have
\dim_{\textup{H}}(A_{1}-A_{2})\leqslant\dim_{\textup{H}}(A_{1}\times A_{2})\leqslant 2\alpha
.

Since
\mu
and
\nu
are product measures, the desired lower bounds for the Fourier spectra of
\mu
and
\nu
, which then pass to
E
and
F
, once again follow from
[
10
, Theorem 2.1]
, although this time we need to apply the estimates twice. This completes the proof.
∎

The following examples are immediate from the sharpness examples for the Hausdorff dimension version of the problem. It is useful to record them and to keep them in mind here. In particular, for
\theta=1/2
we can never go lower than the threshold
d/4
and so our threshold
d/4+1
from Corollary
2.8
is near optimal.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and let
0<s<d/2
. Then, there exists
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\dim_{\textup{H}}D(E)<1
and
\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}E\geqslant s\theta
for all
\theta\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

It follows from
[
8
]
(see also
[
16
, Example 4.8]
) that there exists a compact set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\dim_{\textup{H}}E=s\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dim_{\textup{H}}D(E)<1.

But then concavity of the Fourier spectrum (for measures) implies
\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}E\geqslant s\theta
for all
\theta\in[0,1]
.
∎

Proposition
3.3
gives some simple but useful information regarding the optimal threshold for the Fourier spectrum to ensure a distance set with full Hausdorff dimension. Recall the threshold we obtain in Corollary
2.8
, and see the following section for a more detailed discussion. We conclude this section with another simple observation which improves on the lower bound from Proposition
3.3
for small
\theta
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and let
0<s<1/2
. Then there exists
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\dim_{\textup{H}}D(E)<1
and
\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}E\geqslant\dim_{\mathrm{F}}E\geqslant s
for all
\theta\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that
\overline{\dim}_{\textup{B}}E=\dim_{\textup{H}}E=\dim_{\mathrm{F}}E=s
. That is, we require
E
to be a Salem set of dimension
s
with the additional property that the upper box dimension is also given by
s
. Such sets are well-known to exist and can be constructed, for example, by taking the image of a self-similar set in
[0,1]
of (box and Hausdorff) dimension
s/2
under
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
B:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The image is almost surely a Salem set of dimension
s
by
[
13
, Theorem 1, Chapter 17]
and, moreover, the box dimension cannot exceed
s
since
d
-dimensional Brownian motion is almost surely
\alpha
-Holder for all
0<\alpha<1/2
. But then

D(E)
is a Lipschitz image of the product set
E\times E
under the map
(x,y)\mapsto|x-y|
and so

\dim_{\textup{H}}D(E)\leqslant\dim_{\textup{H}}(E\times E)\leqslant\dim_{\textup{H}}E+\overline{\dim}_{\textup{B}}E=2s<1,

as required. We needed information about the box dimension because the inequality
\dim_{\textup{H}}(E\times E)\leqslant\dim_{\textup{H}}E+\dim_{\textup{H}}E
does not hold in general.
∎

4.
Conjectures

In this section we consider Corollary
2.8
which gives thresholds for the Fourier spectrum which ensure the distance set has full dimension. By considering certain test points and heuristics, we formulate what we believe the sharp threshold should be.

Recall Corollary
2.8
shows that the distance set of the support of a measure
\mu
has full Hausdorff dimension follows whenever

\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\mu\geqslant T_{d}(\theta)

for some
\theta\in[0,1]
, where

T_{d}(\theta):=\begin{cases}2+d\theta,&0\leqslant\theta<\frac{\sqrt{d}-2}{d},\\[4.30554pt]
\sqrt{d},&\frac{\sqrt{d}-2}{d}\leqslant\theta<d^{-1/2},\\[4.30554pt]
\dfrac{2+d\theta}{1+2\theta},&d^{-1/2}\leqslant\theta\leqslant\dfrac{1}{2},\\[8.61108pt]
1+\dfrac{d\theta}{2},&\dfrac{1}{2}\leqslant\theta\leqslant 1.\end{cases}

This piecewise result arises naturally from our method of proof. Nevertheless, we do not expect it to be optimal in all regimes. In particular, the flat branch

T_{d}(\theta)=\sqrt{d}\qquad\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}-2}{d}\leqslant\theta<d^{-1/2}\right)

appears to be an analytic byproduct of the present argument, more specifically, of the loss of
\theta
-dependence in the passage from
L^{2}
- to
L^{4}
-type estimates, rather than a reflection of the underlying geometry. To formulate a more realistic conjectural picture, it is natural to identify the expected values of the threshold at two anchor points, namely
\theta=1
and
\theta=\frac{1}{2}
.

The first anchor point is the endpoint
\theta=1
. By definition,
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{1}\mu=\dim_{\mathrm{S}}\mu
is the Sobolev dimension of
\mu
, so the condition
\dim_{\mathrm{S}}\mu>s
is equivalent to the finiteness of the
s
-energy of
\mu
. Thus, the endpoint
\theta=1
corresponds to the classical energy formulation of the distance set problem, with no additional gain coming from intermediate Fourier spectrum information. From this perspective, it is natural to conjecture that the optimal endpoint should agree with the Falconer threshold, i.e.

T_{d}^{\mathrm{conj}}(1)=\frac{d}{2}.

Our current method yields instead the threshold

T_{d}(1)=1+\frac{d}{2},

which is not expected to be optimal.

The second anchor point is the midpoint
\theta=\frac{1}{2}
, which is naturally tied to the
L^{4}
-type mechanism appearing in our argument. Based on the sharp restriction phenomena observed in the finite field setting in
[
4
]
, we believe that the correct threshold at this point should be

T_{d}^{\mathrm{conj}}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{d}{4}+\frac{1}{2},

rather than the value
\frac{d}{4}+1
taken from Corollary
2.8
.

If one assumes these two anchor values, then the simplest curve joining them is the affine line

(4.1)

T_{d}^{\mathrm{conj}}(\theta)=1+\Bigl(\frac{d}{2}-1\Bigr)\theta,\qquad 0\leqslant\theta\leqslant 1.

This line has several interesting features. First, it recovers exactly the two anchor values:

T_{d}^{\mathrm{conj}}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{d}{4}+\frac{1}{2},\qquad T_{d}^{\mathrm{conj}}(1)=\frac{d}{2}.

Second, it is strictly increasing on
[0,1]
, and therefore removes the flat branch. In particular, at the transition point
\theta=d^{-1/2}
, it gives

(4.2)

T_{d}^{\mathrm{conj}}(d^{-1/2})=1+\Bigl(\frac{d}{2}-1\Bigr)\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}=1+\frac{\sqrt{d}}{2}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}},

which is substantially smaller than the currently proved value
\sqrt{d}
. Third, at the endpoint
\theta=0
, we get

T_{d}^{\mathrm{conj}}(0)=1.

This suggests the possibility that, for Salem-type sets with essentially optimal Fourier decay, the true threshold for full distance set dimension may be 1 (independent of the ambient dimension). Note that we obtain the threshold 2 above which is already independent of the ambient dimension. The threshold 1 is natural since the distance set is related to nonlinear projections and the Fourier dimension behaves very well with respect to linear orthogonal projections. More precisely, the pinned distance map
y\mapsto|x-y|
is a nonlinear projection and maps a set
E
into the pinned distance set
D_{x}(E)\subseteq D(E)
for every
x\in E
. Moreover, for
every
orthogonal projection
\pi
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
onto lines

\dim_{\textup{H}}\pi(E)\geqslant\min\{\dim_{\mathrm{F}}E,1\}.

Therefore, one might expect
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}E=1
to be enough to ensure that the distance set has full Hausdorff dimension. That said, one has to be cautious with this ‘linearisation heuristic’ because it is not true that all pinned distance maps send sets with Fourier dimension at least 1 to sets with Hausdorff dimension 1. For example, consider the sphere
S^{d-1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
which has Fourier dimension
d-1
. However, the pinned distance map
y\mapsto|y|
(that is, the pin is the point
x=0
) sends the sphere to a single point.

Given the above discussion, we are led to the following conjecture.

\mu

\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\mu\geqslant T_{d}^{\mathrm{conj}}(\theta)
for some
\theta\in[0,1]
, then
\dim_{\textup{H}}D(\operatorname{supp}\mu)=1.

5.
Proof of Theorem
2.1

In this section we prove our main result, Theorem
2.1
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in(0,1]
. We can do this because the desired bounds are continuous in
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
. Moreover, we assume without loss of generality that
0<u\leqslant d
. Indeed, if
u=0
then the bounds are trivial and there is nothing to prove. We start by introducing some notation which will be used throughout.

Notation

Although our main results pertain to standard distance sets, it will be convenient to work with squared distances.
For Borel sets
E,F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, write

D(E,F)=\{|x-y|:x\in E,\ y\in F\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad D^{2}(E,F)=\{|x-y|^{2}:x\in E,\ y\in F\}.

In particular,
D(E)=D(E,E)
and
D^{2}(E)=D^{2}(E,E)
. It is easy to see that

\dim_{\textup{H}}D^{2}(E,F)=\dim_{\textup{H}}D(E,F).

We write
X\lesssim_{\gamma}Y
to mean
X\leqslant C_{\gamma}Y
for a constant
C_{\gamma}

depending only on the indicated parameters
\gamma
(and
d
if not listed), and write
X\approx_{\gamma}Y

when both
X\lesssim_{\gamma}Y
and
Y\lesssim_{\gamma}X
hold. We set
\langle x\rangle=(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}
.

Fix compactly supported probability measures
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Choose
R\geqslant 1
such that

\operatorname{supp}\mu_{1}\cup\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2}\subset B(0,R).

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the
pinned squared-distance measure
by

\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}(B):=\mu_{2}\bigl(\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x-y|^{2}\in B\}\bigr),

for Borel sets
B\subset\mathbb{R}
. Thus,
\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}
is the pushforward of
\mu_{2}
under the map

y\mapsto|x-y|^{2}
, and

\operatorname{supp}\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}=D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})^{2}=D^{2}(\{x\},\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2}).

Define the map

(5.1)

F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d+1},\qquad\text{by}\qquad F(y)=(y,|y|^{2}),

and the lifted measure

(5.2)

\nu\coloneqq F_{\#}\mu_{2}\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{d+1}.

The following lemma expresses the Fourier transform of
\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}

in terms of
\widehat{\nu}
evaluated along the line through the origin in
the direction
(-2x,1)
, and then derives Hausdorff dimension and
positive Lebesgue measure criteria for the pinned distance set

D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for every
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5.3)

\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)=e^{-2\pi i\tau|x|^{2}}\,\widehat{\nu}\bigl(\tau(-2x,1)\bigr),

and, in particular,

(5.4)

\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}=\bigl|\widehat{\nu}\bigl(\tau(-2x,1)\bigr)\bigr|^{2}.

Consequently, if

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau<\infty

for some
0<\sigma<1
, then

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\geqslant\sigma.

Moreover, if

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}\,d\tau<\infty,

then

\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\bigr)>0.

Proof.

By definition,

\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\tau|x-y|^{2}}\,d\mu_{2}(y).

Expanding the square gives

\displaystyle\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\tau(|x|^{2}-2x\cdot y+|y|^{2})}\,d\mu_{2}(y)

\displaystyle=e^{-2\pi i\tau|x|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\tau(-2x\cdot y+|y|^{2})}\,d\mu_{2}(y)

\displaystyle=e^{-2\pi i\tau|x|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}e^{-2\pi i\tau(-2x,1)\cdot w}\,d\nu(w)=e^{-2\pi i\tau|x|^{2}}\,\widehat{\nu}\bigl(\tau(-2x,1)\bigr).

This proves (
5.3
), and (
5.4
) is immediate.

If

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau<\infty

for some
0<\sigma<1
, then by (
1.2
)

I_{\sigma}(\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2})<\infty.

Hence

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})=\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})^{2}=\dim_{\textup{H}}\operatorname{supp}\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}\geqslant\sigma.

If instead

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}\,d\tau<\infty,

then Plancherel implies that
\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}
is absolutely continuous with an
L^{2}
density. Since
\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}
has total mass
1
, that density is non-zero, and therefore

\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})^{2}\bigr)=\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(\operatorname{supp}\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}\bigr)>0.

Therefore

\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\bigr)>0.

This completes the proof.
∎

Define

(5.5)

A(\tau)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl|\widehat{\nu}\bigl(\tau(-2x,1)\bigr)\bigr|^{2}\,d\mu_{1}(x).

Fix
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\chi\equiv 1
on
B(0,R)
.
Set

G_{\tau}(x):=\bigl|\widehat{\nu}\bigl(\tau(-2x,1)\bigr)\bigr|^{2},\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{\tau}(x):=\chi(x)G_{\tau}(x).

Then
A(\tau)=\int f_{\tau}(x)\,d\mu_{1}(x)
.

The next lemma gives an explicit formula for
\widehat{f}_{\tau}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

The following identity holds

(5.6)

\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)=\iint e^{-2\pi i\tau(|y|^{2}-|z|^{2})}\,\widehat{\chi}\bigl(\xi-2\tau(y-z)\bigr)\,d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z).

Proof.

Fix
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
\nu=F_{\#}\mu_{2}
and
F(y)=(y,|y|^{2})
, we have for every

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\nu}\bigl(\tau(-2x,1)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}e^{-2\pi i\,\tau(-2x,1)\cdot w}\,d\nu(w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\,\tau(-2x,1)\cdot F(y)}\,d\mu_{2}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\,\tau(-2x\cdot y+|y|^{2})}\,d\mu_{2}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\tau(|y|^{2}-2x\cdot y)}\,d\mu_{2}(y).

Therefore,

\displaystyle G_{\tau}(x)

\displaystyle=\left|\widehat{\nu}\bigl(\tau(-2x,1)\bigr)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\tau(|y|^{2}-2x\cdot y)}\,d\mu_{2}(y)\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\,2\pi i\tau(|z|^{2}-2x\cdot z)}\,d\mu_{2}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\tau(|y|^{2}-|z|^{2})}\,e^{4\pi i\tau x\cdot(y-z)}\,d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z).

Since
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mu_{2}
is finite,

\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\chi(x)|\,dx\,d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z)=\|\chi\|_{L^{1}}\,\mu_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}<\infty.

Hence, Fubini’s theorem applies, and we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}\,\chi(x)G_{\tau}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\iiint e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}\chi(x)\,e^{-2\pi i\tau(|y|^{2}-|z|^{2})}\,e^{4\pi i\tau x\cdot(y-z)}\,dx\,d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=\iint e^{-2\pi i\tau(|y|^{2}-|z|^{2})}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\chi(x)e^{-2\pi ix\cdot(\xi-2\tau(y-z))}\,dx\right)\,d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z).

The inner integral is exactly

\widehat{\chi}\bigl(\xi-2\tau(y-z)\bigr),

so

\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)=\iint e^{-2\pi i\tau(|y|^{2}-|z|^{2})}\,\widehat{\chi}\bigl(\xi-2\tau(y-z)\bigr)\,d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z).

This proves (
5.6
).
∎

To bound
A(\tau)=\int f_{\tau}\,d\mu_{1}
, we will use the following duality estimate, which converts
\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta}(\mu_{1})
-control into a weighted Fourier norm bound for
f_{\tau}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
0<\theta\leqslant 1
and
0<u<d\theta
. Assume
\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta}(\mu)<\infty
, where

\mu
is a compactly supported probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for every
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta}}|\xi|^{\frac{\theta d-u}{2-\theta}}\,d\xi<\infty,

one has

(5.7)

\left|\int f\,d\mu\right|\leqslant\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta}(\mu)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta}}|\xi|^{\frac{\theta d-u}{2-\theta}}\,d\xi\right)^{\frac{2-\theta}{2}}.

Proof.

Set

p:=\frac{2}{\theta},\qquad\text{and}\qquad q:=\frac{2}{2-\theta},

so that
2\leqslant p<\infty
,
1<q\leqslant 2
, and

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

We first prove (
5.7
) for Schwartz functions. To this end, let
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Since
\widehat{f}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|\leqslant\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
\xi
, Fourier inversion and Fubini give

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\,d\mu(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\xi)\,\overline{\widehat{\mu}(\xi)}\,d\xi.

Hence

\displaystyle\left|\int f\,d\mu\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|\,|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl(|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{\theta}}|\xi|^{\frac{u}{\theta}-d}\Bigr)^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\Bigl(|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta}}|\xi|^{\frac{\theta d-u}{2-\theta}}\Bigr)^{\frac{2-\theta}{2}}\,d\xi.

Applying Hölder’s inequality with exponents
p=2/\theta
and
q=2/(2-\theta)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int f\,d\mu\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{\theta}}|\xi|^{\frac{u}{\theta}-d}\,d\xi\right)^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta}}|\xi|^{\frac{\theta d-u}{2-\theta}}\,d\xi\right)^{\frac{2-\theta}{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta}(\mu)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta}}|\xi|^{\frac{\theta d-u}{2-\theta}}\,d\xi\right)^{\frac{2-\theta}{2}}.

Thus, (
5.7
) holds for all
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We now pass to the case
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta}}|\xi|^{\frac{\theta d-u}{2-\theta}}\,d\xi<\infty.

Choose
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\widehat{\psi}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad 0\leqslant\widehat{\psi}\leqslant 1,\qquad\widehat{\psi}(\xi)=1\ \text{for }|\xi|\leqslant 1.

For
N\geqslant 1
, define

\psi_{N}(x):=N^{d}\psi(Nx).

Then, by the scaling property of the Fourier transform,

\widehat{\psi_{N}}(\xi)=\widehat{\psi}(\xi/N).

Now define
f_{N}
by

\widehat{f_{N}}(\xi):=\widehat{f}(\xi)\widehat{\psi_{N}}(\xi).

Since
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, its Fourier transform
\widehat{f}
is
C^{\infty}
, and since

\widehat{\psi_{N}}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it follows that

\widehat{f_{N}}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Hence
f_{N}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Therefore,
by the estimate already proved for Schwartz functions,

(5.8)

\left|\int f_{N}\,d\mu\right|\leqslant\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta}(\mu)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta}}|\widehat{\psi_{N}}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta}}|\xi|^{\frac{\theta d-u}{2-\theta}}\,d\xi\right)^{\frac{2-\theta}{2}}.

Since
0\leqslant\widehat{\psi_{N}}\leqslant 1
and

\widehat{\psi_{N}}(\xi)=\widehat{\psi}(\xi/N)\to 1

for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the dominated convergence theorem yields

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta}}|\widehat{\psi_{N}}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta}}|\xi|^{\frac{\theta d-u}{2-\theta}}\,d\xi\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta}}|\xi|^{\frac{\theta d-u}{2-\theta}}\,d\xi.

It remains to show that

\int f_{N}\,d\mu\to\int f\,d\mu.

Since
\widehat{f_{N}}=\widehat{f}\,\widehat{\psi_{N}},

we have

f_{N}=f*\psi_{N}.

We next show that
f_{N}\to f
uniformly on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. First,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi_{N}(x)\,dx=\widehat{\psi_{N}}(0)=\widehat{\psi}(0)=1.

Also, for every
\delta>0
,

\int_{|x|\geqslant\delta}|\psi_{N}(x)|\,dx=\int_{|y|\geqslant N\delta}|\psi(y)|\,dy\to 0\qquad(N\to\infty),

because
\psi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Thus,
(\psi_{N})
is an approximate identity in
L^{1}
. A direct computation shows that

\|f_{N}-f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0.

Since
\mu
is finite, it follows that

\left|\int(f_{N}-f)\,d\mu\right|\leqslant\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,\|f_{N}-f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0.

Thus

\int f_{N}\,d\mu\to\int f\,d\mu.

We may now pass to the limit in (
5.8
) and obtain

\left|\int f\,d\mu\right|\leqslant\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta}(\mu)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta}}|\xi|^{\frac{\theta d-u}{2-\theta}}\,d\xi\right)^{\frac{2-\theta}{2}}.

This is exactly (
5.7
).
∎

We next provide a weighted
L^{2}
estimate for
\widehat{f}_{\tau}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
0<m<d
and
0<\gamma<d/2
, and set

\eta_{2}:=\mu_{2}*\widetilde{\mu}_{2},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\mu}_{2}(E):=\mu_{2}(-E).

Then, for every
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5.9)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{d-m}\,d\xi\lesssim_{d,m,\gamma,R}(1+|\tau|)^{d-m-2\gamma}I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2}).

Proof.

Fix
N>2\gamma
. Since
\widehat{\chi}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, all integrals below are
absolutely convergent, and hence Fubini’s theorem applies. Using
(
5.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{d-m}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\leqslant\iiiint\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{d-m}\widehat{\chi}(\xi-2\tau u)\overline{\widehat{\chi}(\xi-2\tau u^{\prime})}\,d\xi\Bigr|\,d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z)\,d\mu_{2}(y^{\prime})\,d\mu_{2}(z^{\prime}),

where
u=y-z
and
u^{\prime}=y^{\prime}-z^{\prime}
. After the change of variables

\xi\mapsto\xi+2\tau u
, the inner integral becomes

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi+2\tau u|^{d-m}\widehat{\chi}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{\chi}(\xi+r)}\,d\xi,\qquad r:=2\tau(u-u^{\prime}).

Since
0<m<d
, we have
d-m>0
, and therefore

|\xi+2\tau u|^{d-m}\leqslant\langle\xi+2\tau u\rangle^{\,d-m}\lesssim\langle\tau u\rangle^{\,d-m}\langle\xi\rangle^{\,d-m}

by Peetre’s inequality. Hence

\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi+2\tau u|^{d-m}\widehat{\chi}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{\chi}(\xi+r)}\,d\xi\Bigr|\lesssim\langle\tau u\rangle^{\,d-m}\,\kappa(r),

where

\kappa(r):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\xi\rangle^{\,d-m}|\widehat{\chi}(\xi)|\,|\widehat{\chi}(\xi+r)|\,d\xi.

Since
\widehat{\chi}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for every
N>0
one has

\kappa(r)\lesssim_{N}\langle r\rangle^{-N}.

Also,
\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2}\subset B(0,R)
implies
|u|\leqslant 2R
, and therefore

\langle\tau u\rangle^{\,d-m}\lesssim_{R}(1+|\tau|)^{d-m}.

Consequently,

\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{d-m}\widehat{\chi}(\xi-2\tau u)\overline{\widehat{\chi}(\xi-2\tau u^{\prime})}\,d\xi\Bigr|\lesssim_{N,R}(1+|\tau|)^{d-m}(1+|\tau||u-u^{\prime}|)^{-N}.

It follows that

(5.10)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{d-m}\,d\xi\lesssim_{N,R}(1+|\tau|)^{d-m}\iiiint(1+|\tau||u-u^{\prime}|)^{-N}\,d\mu_{2}^{\otimes 4}.

Since
u=y-z
and
u^{\prime}=y^{\prime}-z^{\prime}
are distributed according to

\eta_{2}=\mu_{2}*\widetilde{\mu}_{2}
, (
5.10
) becomes

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{d-m}\,d\xi\lesssim_{N,R}(1+|\tau|)^{d-m}\iint(1+|\tau||u-u^{\prime}|)^{-N}\,d\eta_{2}(u)\,d\eta_{2}(u^{\prime}).

Set

J(\tau):=\iint(1+|\tau||u-u^{\prime}|)^{-N}\,d\eta_{2}(u)\,d\eta_{2}(u^{\prime}).

We claim that

(5.11)

J(\tau)\lesssim_{\gamma,N,R}(1+|\tau|)^{-2\gamma}I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2})\qquad(\tau\in\mathbb{R}).

If
|\tau|\geqslant 1
, then

(1+|\tau|r)^{-N}\lesssim\sum_{k\geqslant 0}2^{-kN}\mathbf{1}_{\{r\leqslant 2^{k+1}/|\tau|\}},

so

J(\tau)\lesssim\sum_{k\geqslant 0}2^{-kN}\,\eta_{2}\otimes\eta_{2}\Bigl(\{|u-u^{\prime}|\leqslant 2^{k+1}/|\tau|\}\Bigr).

For every
r>0
,

I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2})\geqslant\iint_{|u-u^{\prime}|\leqslant r}|u-u^{\prime}|^{-2\gamma}\,d\eta_{2}(u)\,d\eta_{2}(u^{\prime})\geqslant r^{-2\gamma}\,\eta_{2}\otimes\eta_{2}\Bigl(\{|u-u^{\prime}|\leqslant r\}\Bigr),

and hence

\eta_{2}\otimes\eta_{2}\Bigl(\{|u-u^{\prime}|\leqslant r\}\Bigr)\leqslant r^{2\gamma}I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2}).

With
r=2^{k+1}/|\tau|
, this gives

J(\tau)\lesssim|\tau|^{-2\gamma}I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2})\sum_{k\geqslant 0}2^{-kN}2^{2\gamma(k+1)}\lesssim_{\gamma,N}|\tau|^{-2\gamma}I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2}),

since
N>2\gamma
.

If
|\tau|\leqslant 1
, then
J(\tau)\leqslant\eta_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}
. Also

\operatorname{supp}\eta_{2}\subset B(0,2R)
, and hence
|u-u^{\prime}|\leqslant 4R
on

\operatorname{supp}\eta_{2}\times\operatorname{supp}\eta_{2}
. Therefore

I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2})=\iint|u-u^{\prime}|^{-2\gamma}\,d\eta_{2}(u)\,d\eta_{2}(u^{\prime})\geqslant(4R)^{-2\gamma}\eta_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}.

Thus,
\eta_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}\lesssim_{\gamma,R}I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2})
, and since

(1+|\tau|)^{-2\gamma}\approx 1
for
|\tau|\leqslant 1
, we obtain
(
5.11
) in this range as well.

In other words, we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{d-m}\,d\xi\lesssim_{d,m,\gamma,R}(1+|\tau|)^{d-m-2\gamma}I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2}),

which is exactly (
5.9
).
∎

In the next step, we control
I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2})
directly from
\mathcal{J}_{v,\theta_{2}}(\mu_{2})
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
0<\theta_{2}\leqslant 1
and
v>0
.
Set

a(v):=\min\!\left\{\frac{d}{2},\,v,\frac{v}{2\theta_{2}}\right\}.

If
\mathcal{J}_{v,\theta_{2}}(\mu_{2})<\infty
, then for every
0<\gamma<a(v)
,

(5.12)

I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2})^{1/2}\lesssim_{d,\gamma,\theta_{2},v,R}\mathcal{J}_{v,\theta_{2}}(\mu_{2})^{\rho(\theta_{2})},

where

\rho(\theta_{2}):=\begin{cases}1,&0<\theta_{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2},\\[4.30554pt]
\dfrac{1}{2\theta_{2}},&\frac{1}{2}\leqslant\theta_{2}\leqslant 1.\end{cases}

Proof.

Since
0<\gamma<d/2
, (
1.2
) gives

I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2})\approx_{d,\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\eta_{2}}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{2\gamma-d}\,d\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|^{4}|\xi|^{2\gamma-d}\,d\xi,

since
\widehat{\eta_{2}}(\xi)=|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|^{2}
.

Since
\mu_{2}
is a probability measure,
|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|\leqslant 1
for all
\xi
.
Therefore,

\int_{|\xi|\leqslant 1}|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|^{4}|\xi|^{2\gamma-d}\,d\xi\leqslant\int_{|\xi|\leqslant 1}|\xi|^{2\gamma-d}\,d\xi\lesssim_{d,\gamma}1.

It remains to estimate the contribution from
\{|\xi|>1\}
.

Case 1:
\frac{1}{2}\leqslant\theta_{2}\leqslant 1
.

Since
2/\theta_{2}\leqslant 4
and
0\leqslant|\widehat{\mu}_{2}|\leqslant 1
, we have

|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|^{4}\leqslant|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|^{2/\theta_{2}}.

Also, since
\gamma<a(v)\leqslant v/(2\theta_{2})
and
|\xi|>1
,

|\xi|^{2\gamma-d}\leqslant|\xi|^{v/\theta_{2}-d}.

Hence,

\int_{|\xi|>1}|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|^{4}|\xi|^{2\gamma-d}\,d\xi\leqslant\int_{|\xi|>1}|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|^{2/\theta_{2}}|\xi|^{v/\theta_{2}-d}\,d\xi\leqslant\mathcal{J}_{v,\theta_{2}}(\mu_{2})^{1/\theta_{2}}.

Case 2:
0<\theta_{2}<\frac{1}{2}
.

Set

g(\xi):=|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|^{2/\theta_{2}}|\xi|^{v/\theta_{2}-d}.

Then, for
|\xi|>1
,

|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|^{4}|\xi|^{2\gamma-d}=g(\xi)^{2\theta_{2}}\,|\xi|^{2\gamma-2v+d(2\theta_{2}-1)}.

Apply Hölder’s inequality with exponents

p=\frac{1}{2\theta_{2}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad p^{\prime}=\frac{1}{1-2\theta_{2}}.

Since
\gamma<a(v)\leqslant v
, we have
\gamma<v
, and therefore

\frac{2\gamma-2v+d(2\theta_{2}-1)}{1-2\theta_{2}}<-d.

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{|\xi|>1}|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|^{4}|\xi|^{2\gamma-d}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{|\xi|>1}g(\xi)^{2\theta_{2}}|\xi|^{2\gamma-2v+d(2\theta_{2}-1)}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\leqslant\Bigl(\int_{|\xi|>1}g(\xi)\,d\xi\Bigr)^{2\theta_{2}}\Bigl(\int_{|\xi|>1}|\xi|^{\frac{2\gamma-2v+d(2\theta_{2}-1)}{1-2\theta_{2}}}\,d\xi\Bigr)^{1-2\theta_{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,\gamma,\theta_{2},v}\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|^{2/\theta_{2}}|\xi|^{v/\theta_{2}-d}\,d\xi\Bigr)^{2\theta_{2}}=\mathcal{J}_{v,\theta_{2}}(\mu_{2})^{2}.

Combining the estimates together, we obtain

I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2})\lesssim_{d,\gamma,\theta_{2},v}1+\mathcal{J}_{v,\theta_{2}}(\mu_{2})^{2\rho(\theta_{2})}.

Since
\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2}\subset B(0,R)
and
\mu_{2}
is a probability measure,

|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)-1|=\Bigl|\int\bigl(e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}-1\bigr)\,d\mu_{2}(x)\Bigr|\leqslant 2\pi R|\xi|.

Thus, if
|\xi|\leqslant(4\pi R)^{-1}
, then
|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\xi)|^{2/\theta_{2}}|\xi|^{v/\theta_{2}-d}\,d\xi\geqslant 2^{-2/\theta_{2}}\int_{|\xi|\leqslant(4\pi R)^{-1}}|\xi|^{v/\theta_{2}-d}\,d\xi\gtrsim_{v,\theta_{2},d,R}1,

and hence

\mathcal{J}_{v,\theta_{2}}(\mu_{2})\gtrsim_{v,\theta_{2},d,R}1.

So the additive constant can be absorbed. Taking square roots proves
(
5.12
).
∎

We now derive the mixed decay estimate for
A(\tau)
.

Proposition 5.6
.

Assume

0<\theta_{1}\leqslant 1,\qquad 0<u<d\theta_{1},\qquad 0<\theta_{2}\leqslant 1,\qquad v>0,

and

\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta_{1}}(\mu_{1})<\infty,\qquad\mathcal{J}_{v,\theta_{2}}(\mu_{2})<\infty.

Set

a(v):=\min\!\left\{\frac{d}{2},\,v,\frac{v}{2\theta_{2}}\right\}.

Then, for every
0<\gamma<a(v)
,

(5.13)

A(\tau)\lesssim_{d,u,\theta_{1},\gamma,\theta_{2},v,R}\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta_{1}}(\mu_{1})^{1/2}\mathcal{J}_{v,\theta_{2}}(\mu_{2})^{\theta_{1}\rho(\theta_{2})}(1+|\tau|)^{-\beta_{\gamma}(u)},

where

\beta_{\gamma}(u):=\frac{u+2\theta_{1}\gamma-d\theta_{1}}{2}.

Proof.

Set

m:=\frac{u}{\theta_{1}}.

Since
0<u<d\theta_{1}
, we have
0<m<d
.

First, (
5.6
) and Fubini imply

\|\widehat{f}_{\tau}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant\iint\|\widehat{\chi}(\cdot-2\tau(y-z))\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z)=\|\widehat{\chi}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Also, Lemma
5.4
gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{d-m}\,d\xi\lesssim_{d,m,\gamma,R}(1+|\tau|)^{d-m-2\gamma}I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2}).

Hence, the weighted
L^{2/(2-\theta_{1})}
quantity in
Lemma
5.3
is finite, and the lemma applies to
f_{\tau}
.

Applying Lemma
5.3
with
\theta=\theta_{1}
and
f=f_{\tau}
,
we obtain

A(\tau)\leqslant\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta_{1}}(\mu_{1})^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta_{1}}}|\xi|^{\frac{\theta_{1}d-u}{2-\theta_{1}}}\,d\xi\right)^{\frac{2-\theta_{1}}{2}}.

If
0<\theta_{1}<1
, then Hölder’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta_{1}}}|\xi|^{\frac{\theta_{1}d-u}{2-\theta_{1}}}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)|^{\frac{2(1-\theta_{1})}{2-\theta_{1}}}\Bigl(|\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{d-u/\theta_{1}}\Bigr)^{\frac{\theta_{1}}{2-\theta_{1}}}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\widehat{f}_{\tau}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{\frac{2(1-\theta_{1})}{2-\theta_{1}}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{d-u/\theta_{1}}\,d\xi\right)^{\frac{\theta_{1}}{2-\theta_{1}}}.

Raising both sides to the power
(2-\theta_{1})/2
, we get

(5.14)

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)|^{\frac{2}{2-\theta_{1}}}|\xi|^{\frac{\theta_{1}d-u}{2-\theta_{1}}}\,d\xi\right)^{\frac{2-\theta_{1}}{2}}\leqslant\|\widehat{f}_{\tau}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{1-\theta_{1}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{\tau}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{d-u/\theta_{1}}\,d\xi\right)^{\theta_{1}/2}.

If
\theta_{1}=1
, then (
5.14
) is immediate.

Combining the inequalities, we obtain

A(\tau)\lesssim_{d,u,\theta_{1},\gamma,R}\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta_{1}}(\mu_{1})^{1/2}I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2})^{\theta_{1}/2}(1+|\tau|)^{\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}(d-u/\theta_{1}-2\gamma)}.

Since

\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\left(d-\frac{u}{\theta_{1}}-2\gamma\right)=-\frac{u+2\theta_{1}\gamma-d\theta_{1}}{2}=-\beta_{\gamma}(u),

and since Lemma
5.5
gives

I_{2\gamma}(\eta_{2})^{1/2}\lesssim_{d,\gamma,\theta_{2},v,R}\mathcal{J}_{v,\theta_{2}}(\mu_{2})^{\rho(\theta_{2})},

we arrive at (
5.13
).
∎

We will also need the following elementary monotonicity property, which allows us to pass from
\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta}
-control to
\mathcal{J}_{w,\eta}
-control for suitable ranges of parameters.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
0<\theta\leqslant 1
, let
\mu
be a compactly supported probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and assume

\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta}(\mu)<\infty

for some
u>0
.

(1)

If
0<\eta\leqslant\theta
, then

\mathcal{J}_{w,\eta}(\mu)<\infty\qquad\text{for every }0<w\leqslant\frac{\eta}{\theta}u.

(2)

If
\theta<\eta\leqslant 1
, then

\mathcal{J}_{w,\eta}(\mu)<\infty\qquad\text{for every }0<w<u.

Proof.

Write

g(\rho):=\dim_{\mathrm{F}}^{\rho}\mu=\sup\{s\geqslant 0:\mathcal{J}_{s,\rho}(\mu)<\infty\},\qquad 0\leqslant\rho\leqslant 1.

It follows from
[
11
, Theorems 1.1 and 1.3]
that
g
is non-decreasing and concave on
[0,1]
.

Since
\mu
is finite,
|\widehat{\mu}|\leqslant\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and therefore
g(0)\geqslant 0
.

Assume
\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta}(\mu)<\infty
. Then
g(\theta)\geqslant u
.

We first suppose
0<\eta\leqslant\theta
. Since
g
is concave on
[0,1]
,

g(\eta)\geqslant\frac{\eta}{\theta}g(\theta)+\left(1-\frac{\eta}{\theta}\right)g(0)\geqslant\frac{\eta}{\theta}u.

Hence

\mathcal{J}_{w,\eta}(\mu)<\infty\qquad\text{for every }0<w<\frac{\eta}{\theta}u.

It remains to treat the endpoint
w=\frac{\eta}{\theta}u
. Set
M:=\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then

\mathcal{J}_{\eta u/\theta,\eta}(\mu)^{1/\eta}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2/\eta}|\xi|^{u/\theta-d}\,d\xi.

We split the integral into
|\xi|\leqslant 1
and
|\xi|>1
.

On
\{|\xi|\leqslant 1\}
, using
|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|\leqslant M
,

\int_{|\xi|\leqslant 1}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2/\eta}|\xi|^{u/\theta-d}\,d\xi\leqslant M^{2/\eta}\int_{|\xi|\leqslant 1}|\xi|^{u/\theta-d}\,d\xi<\infty,

since
u/\theta>0
.

On
\{|\xi|>1\}
, since
\eta\leqslant\theta
, we have
2/\eta-2/\theta\geqslant 0
, and so

|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2/\eta}=|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2/\theta}\,|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2/\eta-2/\theta}\leqslant M^{2/\eta-2/\theta}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2/\theta}.

Therefore,

\int_{|\xi|>1}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2/\eta}|\xi|^{u/\theta-d}\,d\xi\leqslant M^{2/\eta-2/\theta}\int_{|\xi|>1}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2/\theta}|\xi|^{u/\theta-d}\,d\xi<\infty,

because
\mathcal{J}_{u,\theta}(\mu)<\infty
. Thus,
\mathcal{J}_{\eta u/\theta,\eta}(\mu)<\infty
, proving part (1).

We now move to the case
\theta<\eta\leqslant 1
. Since
g
is non-decreasing,

g(\eta)\geqslant g(\theta)\geqslant u.

Hence

\mathcal{J}_{w,\eta}(\mu)<\infty\qquad\text{for every }0<w<u.

This proves part (2).
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

For simplicity, let
s:=\dim_{\textup{F}}^{\theta_{2}}\mu_{2}
. For each
v\in(0,s)
, write

a(v):=\min\!\left\{\frac{d}{2},\,v,\,\frac{v}{2\theta_{2}}\right\}.

Then
a(v)\uparrow a
as
v\uparrow s
.

Case 1:
0<u\leqslant d\theta_{1}
.

Fix

0<\sigma<\min\{1,\beta(u)\}.

We may choose parameters

w<u,\qquad 0<v<s,\qquad 0<\gamma<a(v),

so that

(5.15)

\sigma<\frac{w+2\theta_{1}\gamma-d\theta_{1}}{2},

since

\frac{w+2\theta_{1}\gamma-d\theta_{1}}{2}\longrightarrow\frac{u+2\theta_{1}a-d\theta_{1}}{2}=\beta(u),

as
w\uparrow u
,
v\uparrow s
and
\gamma\uparrow a(v)
.

By the definition of
\dim_{\textup{F}}^{\theta_{1}}\mu_{1}
and
\dim_{\textup{F}}^{\theta_{2}}\mu_{2}
, we have

\mathcal{J}_{w,\theta_{1}}(\mu_{1})<\infty,

and

\mathcal{J}_{v,\theta_{2}}(\mu_{2})<\infty.

Hence, Proposition
5.6
implies

A(\tau)\lesssim(1+|\tau|)^{-\frac{w+2\theta_{1}\gamma-d\theta_{1}}{2}}.

By (
5.15
),

\int_{\mathbb{R}}A(\tau)|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau<\infty.

By (
5.4
) and the definition (
5.5
) of

, we have, for every
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5.16)

A(\tau)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}\,d\mu_{1}(x).

Thus, Tonelli’s theorem gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau\,d\mu_{1}(x)<\infty.

Therefore, for
\mu_{1}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau<\infty,

and Lemma
5.1

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\geqslant\sigma\qquad\text{for $\mu_{1}$-almost every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Since
\sigma<\min\{1,\beta(u)\}
was arbitrary, we conclude that

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\geqslant\min\{1,\beta(u)\}\qquad\text{for $\mu_{1}$-almost every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

If
\beta(u)>1
, choose
v\in(0,s)
and
\gamma\in(0,a(v))
so that

\frac{u+2\theta_{1}\gamma-d\theta_{1}}{2}>1.

Then, Proposition
5.6
gives
A\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, and (
5.16
) implies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}\,d\tau\,d\mu_{1}(x)<\infty.

Hence, for
\mu_{1}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}\,d\tau<\infty,

and Lemma
5.1
yields

\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\bigr)>0\qquad\text{for $\mu_{1}$-almost every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Case 2:
d\theta_{1}<u\leqslant d
.

Fix

0<\sigma<\min\left\{1,\frac{ua}{d}\right\}.

Choose
u_{0}\in(d\theta_{1},u)
and
v\in(0,s)
so close to
u
and
s
, respectively, that

(5.17)

\sigma<\frac{u_{0}a(v)}{d}.

Set

\eta:=\frac{u_{0}}{d}.

Then
\theta_{1}<\eta\leqslant 1
, and since
u_{0}<u=\dim_{\textup{F}}^{\theta_{1}}\mu_{1}
, we have

\mathcal{J}_{u_{0},\theta_{1}}(\mu_{1})<\infty.

Part (2) of Lemma
5.7
therefore implies

\mathcal{J}_{w,\eta}(\mu_{1})<\infty\qquad\text{for every }0<w<u_{0}.

Because
d\eta=u_{0}
, the exponent

\frac{w+2\eta\gamma-d\eta}{2}=\frac{w+2(u_{0}/d)\gamma-u_{0}}{2}

converges to
u_{0}a(v)/d
as
w\uparrow u_{0}
and
\gamma\uparrow a(v)
. In view of
(
5.17
), we may choose

0<w<u_{0},\qquad 0<\gamma<a(v),

so that

(5.18)

\sigma<\frac{w+2\eta\gamma-d\eta}{2}.

Applying Proposition
5.6
with
(w,\eta)
in place of
(u,\theta_{1})
and with the same
v
gives

A(\tau)\lesssim(1+|\tau|)^{-\frac{w+2\eta\gamma-d\eta}{2}}.

By (
5.18
),

\int_{\mathbb{R}}A(\tau)|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau<\infty.

Using (
5.16
) and Tonelli once again, we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau\,d\mu_{1}(x)<\infty.

Hence, for
\mu_{1}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau<\infty,

and Lemma
5.1
gives

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\geqslant\sigma\qquad\text{for $\mu_{1}$-almost every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Since
\sigma<\min\{1,ua/d\}
was arbitrary, we conclude that

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\geqslant\min\left\{1,\frac{ua}{d}\right\}\qquad\text{for $\mu_{1}$-almost every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

If
ua/d>1
, choose
u_{0}\in(d\theta_{1},u)
and
v\in(0,s)
such that

u_{0}a(v)/d>1
. Define
\eta=u_{0}/d
as above. Then choose
w<u_{0}
and

\gamma<a(v)
so that

\frac{w+2\eta\gamma-d\eta}{2}>1.

Proposition
5.6
again gives
A\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, and the same Tonelli argument as above shows that, for
\mu_{1}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}\,d\tau<\infty.

Lemma
5.1
then implies

\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\bigr)>0\qquad\text{for $\mu_{1}$-almost every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Proof of Theorem
2.4

To prove Theorem
2.4
, we make use of the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
, and let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be compactly supported probability
measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Assume that, for some
s>0
,

|\widehat{\mu_{1}}(\xi)|\lesssim\langle\xi\rangle^{-s/2}\qquad(\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Then, for every
0<\sigma<\min\{1,s/2\}
such that
I_{\sigma}(\mu_{2})<\infty
,

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\geqslant\sigma\qquad\text{for $\mu_{1}$-almost all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Moreover, if
s>2
and
I_{1}(\mu_{2})<\infty
, then

\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\bigr)>0\qquad\text{for $\mu_{1}$-almost all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Proof.

For
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, recall

A(\tau)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}\,d\mu_{1}(x).

By Lemma
5.1
, Fubini’s theorem, and the identity

|x-y|^{2}-|x-z|^{2}=|y|^{2}-|z|^{2}-2x\cdot(y-z),

we obtain

\displaystyle A(\tau)

\displaystyle=\iiint e^{-2\pi i\tau(|x-y|^{2}-|x-z|^{2})}\,d\mu_{1}(x)\,d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z)

(6.1)

\displaystyle=\iint e^{-2\pi i\tau(|y|^{2}-|z|^{2})}\widehat{\mu_{1}}\bigl(-2\tau(y-z)\bigr)\,d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z).

Hence

A(\tau)\lesssim\iint(1+2|\tau||y-z|)^{-s/2}\,d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z).

Fix
0<\sigma<\min\{1,s/2\}
, and assume
I_{\sigma}(\mu_{2})<\infty
.
Since the integrand is non-negative, Tonelli’s theorem gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}A(\tau)|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+2|\tau||y-z|)^{-s/2}|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau\right)d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z).

For
r=|y-z|>0
, the change of variables
u=2|\tau|r
yields

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+2|\tau|r)^{-s/2}|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau=c_{\sigma,s}\,r^{-\sigma},

where

c_{\sigma,s}=2^{1-\sigma}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+u)^{-s/2}u^{\sigma-1}\,du<\infty,

since
0<\sigma<s/2
. Therefore

\int_{\mathbb{R}}A(\tau)|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau\lesssim\iint|y-z|^{-\sigma}\,d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z)=I_{\sigma}(\mu_{2})<\infty.

It follows from Tonelli’s theorem again that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau\,d\mu_{1}(x)<\infty.

Hence, for
\mu_{1}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}|\tau|^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau<\infty.

Lemma
5.1
then implies

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\geqslant\sigma\qquad\text{for $\mu_{1}$-almost all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For the positive Lebesgue measure statement, assume
s>2
and
I_{1}(\mu_{2})<\infty
.
By the same argument, but without the weight
|\tau|^{\sigma-1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}A(\tau)\,d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+2|\tau||y-z|)^{-s/2}\,d\tau\right)d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=C_{s}\iint|y-z|^{-1}\,d\mu_{2}(y)\,d\mu_{2}(z)=C_{s}I_{1}(\mu_{2})<\infty,

where

C_{s}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+|u|)^{-s/2}\,du<\infty.

Therefore,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}\,d\tau\,d\mu_{1}(x)<\infty,

so, for
\mu_{1}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{\delta_{x,\mu_{2}}^{2}}(\tau)\bigr|^{2}\,d\tau<\infty.

Lemma
5.1
implies

\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2})\bigr)>0\qquad\text{for $\mu_{1}$-almost all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

We first prove (1). If
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu=0
, then the conclusion is trivial. So assume

\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu>0
.

Let
(\sigma_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be an increasing sequence in

\left(0,\min\left\{1,\frac{\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu}{2}\right\}\right)

such that

\sigma_{n}\uparrow\min\left\{\frac{\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu}{2},1\right\}.

For each
n
, choose
t_{n}
such that

2\sigma_{n}<t_{n}<\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu.

Since
t_{n}<\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu
, by the definition of Fourier dimension,

|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|\lesssim\langle\xi\rangle^{-t_{n}/2}\qquad(\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Moreover,

I_{\sigma_{n}}(\mu)\approx\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{\sigma_{n}-d}\,d\xi\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\xi\rangle^{-t_{n}}|\xi|^{\sigma_{n}-d}\,d\xi<\infty,

because
\sigma_{n}<t_{n}
.

Therefore, Lemma
6.1
, applied with

\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=\mu
and
s=t_{n}
, yields a set
G_{n}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}\mu
such that

\mu(G_{n})=1

and

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\geqslant\sigma_{n}\qquad\text{for all }x\in G_{n}.

Now let

G=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}G_{n}.

Then
\mu(G)=1
. Also, for every
x\in G
and every
n
,

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\geqslant\sigma_{n}.

Letting
n\to\infty
, we conclude that

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\geqslant\min\left\{\frac{\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu}{2},1\right\}\qquad\text{for all }x\in G.

This proves the first claim.

To prove the second claim in (1), assume that
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu>2
. Choose
t
such that

2<t<\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu.

Then, by the definition of Fourier dimension,

|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|\lesssim\langle\xi\rangle^{-t/2}\qquad(\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Since
1<t
, we have

I_{1}(\mu)\approx\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{1-d}\,d\xi\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\xi\rangle^{-t}|\xi|^{1-d}\,d\xi<\infty.

Applying Lemma
6.1
with
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=\mu
, we obtain

\mathcal{L}^{1}\bigl(D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\bigr)>0\qquad\text{for $\mu$-almost all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We now prove (2). Recalling that
\mu
is compactly supported, by
[
2
, Proposition 4.2]
,

\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\mu\leqslant\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu+d\theta

and therefore

\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu\geqslant\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\mu-d\theta.

Applying (1), we obtain

\dim_{\textup{H}}D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\geqslant\min\left\{\frac{\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu}{2},1\right\}\geqslant\min\left\{\frac{\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\mu-d\theta}{2},1\right\}\qquad\text{for $\mu$-almost all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

This proves the first claim in (2).

If
\dim^{\theta}_{\mathrm{F}}\mu>2+d\theta
, then
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}\mu>2
and applying (1)

\mathcal{L}^{1}\left(D_{x}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\right)>0\qquad\text{for $\mu$-almost all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

This completes the proof.
∎
