An analogue of irreducible cuspidal representations for the group PGL ⁡ ( 2 ) \operatorname{PGL}(2) over a two-dimensional local field
Alexander Braverman, David Kazhdan

Abstract
. Let F F be a local non-archimedian field of odd residue characteristic and let G = PGL ⁡ ( 2 ) G=\operatorname{PGL}(2) . In this paper we study an analog of irreducible cuspidal representations of the group G ⁡ ( F ) G(F) when F F is replaced by the field K = F ⁡ ( ( t ) ) K=F((t)) . The story turns out to be similar to the classical case, but also with some differences. We present a construction of such representations essentially (up to a small subtlety) starting from a quadratic extension L L of K K and a character θ : L ∗ / K ∗ → ℂ ∗ \theta:L^{*}/K^{*}\to\mathbb{C}^{*} which is not Galois invariant. We also show that the restriction of the representations we construct to the group P ⁡ ( K ) P(K) (here P P is a Borel subgroup of PGL ⁡ ( 2 ) \operatorname{PGL}(2) ) is irreducible. However, contrary to the classical case it turns out that these restrictions are not isomorphic to the ”standard” irreducible cuspidal representation of P ⁡ ( K ) P(K) . In the Appendix we propose a notion of cuspidality for smooth representations of the group H ⁡ ( K ) H(K) for an arbitrary split group H H .

1.6.
Remarks

Several remarks are in order here.
First, if we go back to
G(F)
instead of
G(K)
, then not only the restriction of a cuspidal irreducible representation of
G(F)
to
P(F)
is irreducible, but in addition all those irreducible representations of
P(F)
are isomorphic. In fact,
P(F)
has unique irreducible representation which is not of dimension 1 (and this representation is cuspidal).
The latter statement is wrong when we replace
F
by
K
. Namely, on the one hand, we can
explicitly construct certain irreducible cuspidal representation
\Upsilon
of
P(K)
which is a direct analog of the above (unique) irreducible cuspidal representation of
P(F)
(the construction is essentially a word-by-word repetition of a similar construction when
K
is replaced by
F
). On the other hand, we show that
\Upsilon
cannot be extended to
G(K)
. We expect that the first statement is also wrong in our setting (i.e. we expect that the restriction of
\Pi(\pi)
to
P(K)
actually depends on
\pi
; more precisely, similarly to the usual situation one can define the notion of depth of
\pi
and we expect that
\Pi(\pi)|_{P(K)}
and
\Pi(\pi^{\prime})|_{P(K)}
are not isomorphic if
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
have different depths). However, we show that all the representations
\Pi(\pi)
(for
\pi
being a special representation of either
G(O)
or
G^{\prime}(O)
) admit a certain
\mathbb{Z}
-filtration such that the corresponding associated graded still has a natural action of
P(K)
and it is isomorphic to
\Upsilon
. This is the main difference between representation theory of
G(K)
and that of
G(F)
.

Second, we expect that any special representation of
G(K)
is induced from a special representation of either
G(O)
or
G^{\prime}(O)

(i.e. it is of the form
\Pi(\pi)
in the above notations) but at the moment we don’t know how to prove this.

Third, an important feature of
[
7
]
and subsequent companion papers was the fact
G(K)
admits a natural central extension by
F^{*}
; thus one can study representations of this central extension of certain
level
which is a character of the central

F^{*}
. Usually, this character is taken to be unramified and thus it is given just by a complex number. It is observed in
[
7
]
that the category of representations of
G(K)
behaves quite differently depending on the level. However, the choice of level doesn’t seem to be important when we study cuspidal representation of
G(K)
. That is why in this paper we just restrict ourselves to level zero (i.e. we ignore the central extension).

1.7.
A remark about terminology

We write ”Claim” for results to which we do not provide a proof either in the case when
we provide a reference or for results which we consider almost obvious. The statements for which we do provide proof are called ”Theorem”, ”Proposition” etc.

Also abusing slightly the terminology we shall usually use the words ”algebraic

-points

for an algebraic variety

over

. In the case when

is a local field algebraic

-varieties carry the topology inherited from the topology on

The following statement is probably well-known, but we include a proof for completeness.

Proposition 3.4

Let

Proof.

In view of what is written above in order to prove Proposition

is a homeomorphism. Since it is obviously a continuous bijection, it is enough to show that this map is open, which is equivalent to showing that the natural map

is open. This is a special case of the following general statement.

Lemma 3.5

Let

be a toplogical group acting transitively on a topological space

and let

(1)

is locally compact and Hausdorff

(2)

The element

Clearly it is enough to show that for any open compact subgroup

(1)

(2)

(Passage to

Thus

(3)

(extension to Pro

\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ect}

By definition

a_{2}(\mathcal{X},V)\;=\;\varinjlim_{n}a_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{n},V)\quad\text{for }\mathcal{X}=\varprojlim_{n}\mathbf{X}_{n},

•Associativity for

The full action

\mathbb{X}\in\mathbb{S}et

(5)

Compatibility with
\widetilde{(\cdot)}
— the functors

\mathbb{X}\mapsto\widetilde{\mathbb{X}},\mathbb{V}\mapsto\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}

Recall (Definition

\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{S}et}(*,\mathbb{X}),\qquad\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}=\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ect}}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbb{V}).

\widetilde{a(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{V})}\;\cong\;a\big(\widetilde{\mathbb{X}},\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}\big),

or equivalently the diagram of functors

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{S}et\times\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ect}&\xrightarrow{\,a\,}&\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ect}\\
\downarrow{(\widetilde{\cdot},\widetilde{\cdot})}&&\downarrow{\widetilde{(\cdot)}}\\

Why? Reduce to the building blocks:
• For
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbf{S}et
and

\widetilde{a_{1}(\mathbf{X},V)}=\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ect}}({\mathbb{C}},a_{1}(\mathbf{X},V))\;\cong\;a_{0}\big(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{S}et}(*,\mathbf{X}),\,\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ect}}({\mathbb{C}},V)\big)

9.2.
The exactness of the functor
\mu

Proposition 9.4
.

The functor
\mu
is exact.

Proof.

The proof of Proposition
9.6
is based on the following result.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an abelian category,
F,G:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
be exact functors and
a:F\to G
a morphism such that the maps
a(X):F(X)\to G(X)
are surjective for all
X\in Ob(\mathcal{C})
. We define
R:=\ker(a)
.

Lemma 9.5
.

The functor
R
is exact.

Proof.

Let

0\to A^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\,i\,}A\xrightarrow{\,p\,}A^{\prime\prime}\to 0

be a short exact sequence in
\mathcal{C}
. Since the functors
F
and
G
are exact we get two short exact rows and a commutative diagram

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}0&\to&F(A^{\prime})&\xrightarrow{F(i)}&F(A)&\xrightarrow{F(p)}&F(A^{\prime\prime})&\to&0\\
&&\downarrow^{a_{A^{\prime}}}&&\downarrow^{a_{A}}&&\downarrow^{a_{A^{\prime\prime}}}&&\\
0&\to&G(A^{\prime})&\xrightarrow{G(i)}&G(A)&\xrightarrow{G(p)}&G(A^{\prime\prime})&\to&0.\end{array}

The snake lemma provides the exact sequence

0\to\ker(a_{A^{\prime}})\to\ker(a_{A})\to\ker(a_{A^{\prime\prime}})\to\operatorname{coker}(a_{A^{\prime}})\to\cdots

We have
\operatorname{coker}(a_{X})=0
since
, by the hypothesis, each
a_{X}:F(X)\to G(X)
is surjective. Therefore the snake lemma yields the exactness of the sequence

0\to\ker(a_{A^{\prime}})\to\ker(a_{A})\to\ker(a_{A^{\prime\prime}})\to 0
. Thus
R
is exact.
∎

Proposition
9.4
follows from Lemma
9.3
.

∎

10.1.
Topology on the affine Grassmanian

For any algebraic group

Remark 10.1
.

The topological space
\operatorname{Gr}_{H}
is usually not locally compact.

10.2.
The basic geometry of
\operatorname{Gr}_{G}

The following statement follows from the Iwasawa decomposition proved in

Claim 10.2
.

The group

acts transitively on

Remark 10.3

Although the map

is a quasi-affine variety (in particular, in this case

is isomorphic to its image in
\operatorname{Gr}_{G}
). However, in our case the quotient

is just the projective line

, which is not quasi-affine, so the above lemma is not applicable.

For an integer

, let
\operatorname{Gr}_{G}^{l}
denote the

-orbit of the image in
\operatorname{Gr}_{G}
of the diagonal matrix

(3)

\operatorname{Gr}_{G}=\bigsqcup\limits_{l=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Gr}_{G}^{l};\ \ \ \overline{\operatorname{Gr}}_{G}^{l}=\bigsqcup\limits_{0\leq i\leq l,\ \text{$i-l$ is even}}\operatorname{Gr}_{G}^{i};

In particular, we have closed imbeddings

10.3.
Semi-infinite orbits

The Iwasawa decomposition implies that

\operatorname{Gr}_{G}=\bigsqcup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}N(K)\cdot\hat{t}\,^{i},

where as before we do not distinguish notationally the matrix

Note also that

10.4.
The ind-schemes

Let us now consider the quotient

\operatorname{Gr}_{P,m}\to\operatorname{Gr}_{P}\underset{\operatorname{Gr}_{G}}{\times}\operatorname{Gr}_{G,m}

The twisted affine Grassmannian

11.1.
Restriction from
G(K)
to
P(K)

We would like to study representations of
G(K)
whose restriction to
P(K)
is irreducible. Recall that if we replace
K
by
F
, then this property is equivalent to irreducibility and cuspidality. Moreover, the group
P(F)
has unique (up to isomorphism) irreducible cuspidal representation which is naturally realized in the space
\mathcal{S}(F^{*})
. When
F
is replaced by
K
the uniqueness statement is no longer true (however, we have Proposition
9.16
which shows that some weaker version of this uniqueness does hold – i.e. all irreducible cuspidal representations of
P(K)
have the same associated graded with respect to the filtration
\mathcal{F}
). However, naively the most straightforward analog of the unique irreducible cuspidal representation of
P(F)
is the representation
\Upsilon
of
P(K)
defined above. So, it is natural to ask whether there exist irreducible representations of
G(K)
whose restriction to
P(K)
is actually isomorphic to
\Upsilon
. In this Section we show that the answer is always ”no”. However, we shall see later that any representation
\Pi
of
G(K)
which is induced from a special representation of either
G(O)
or

is cuspidal and satisfies
\bar{\Pi}|_{P(K)}\simeq\Upsilon
(which in view of Proposition
9.16
(2) will imply that
\Pi|_{P(K)}
is irreducible).

As before we identify
K
with
N(K)\subset P(K)
and therefore identify
O

with a subgroup of
G(K)
. We also denote by
G^{+}(K)
the subgroup of elements
\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(K)
such that the valuation of their determinant is even.

Lemma 11.1
.

Let
(\Pi,\mathbb{W})
a smooth representation of
G(K)
. Then the map
\mathbb{W}^{G^{+}(K)}\to\mathbb{W}^{O}
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

It is clear that the subobject
\mathbb{W}^{O}\subset\mathbb{W}
is invariant under
\tilde{b},b\in K
(see Definition
12
). Since any smooth representation of
G(K)
is a projective
limit of smooth representations of
G(O)
(see Claim
7.22
), it
follows from Lemma
4.8
that groups
\tilde{b}^{-1}G(O)\tilde{b}
for
b\in K
act trivially on the subobject
\mathbb{W}^{O}\subset\mathbb{W}
. But such groups generate
G^{+}(K)
.
∎

Corollary 11.2
.

There is no representation of
G(K)
whose restriction to
P(K)
is isomorphic to
\Upsilon
.

12.
Representations of

In this Section we study representations of

. We use freely notations of Section

12.1.
Induction from
H(O)
to
H(K)
for general algebraic group
H

We would like to discuss the functor of compact induction
\operatorname{ind}_{H(O)}^{H(K)}
for a general linear algebraic group
H
. This functor is well-defined but for general
H
it is not adjoint to the restriction functor on any side. However, it is actually right adjoint to the restriction functor if
H
is reductive. The definition of this functor, as well as the proof of the above statement can be found in Section 3 of
[
7
]
. For the reader’s convenience we reproduce the definition here.

Namely, let
(\pi,V)
a smooth representation of
G(O)
. Since such a
\pi
is always equal to the projective limit of representations for which
V
is a vector space (as opposed to a pro-vector space) and since the functor
\operatorname{ind}
will commute with such limits, it is enough to assume that
V
is a vector space. Such a
\pi
is equal to the inductive limit of representations trivial on
H(O)(m+1)
for some
m
, and since
\operatorname{ind}
will commute with inductive limits, we may assume that
\pi
is actually a smooth representation of
H(O)_{m}=H(O)/H(O)(m+1)
. As before consider
\operatorname{Gr}_{H,m}=H(K)/H(O)(m+1)
. It has an action of
H(K)
on the left and of
H(O)_{m}
on the right. We know that it can be written as an inductive limit of some
X_{i}
which are finite-dimensional
F
-varieties where the maps
X_{i}\to X_{i+1}
are closed embeddings. We may also assume that each
X_{i}
is
H(O)_{m}
-invariant.

Definition 12.1
.

We set
\mathcal{S}(X_{i},V)
to be the space functions
f:X_{i}\to V
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

f
is locally constant

(2)

f
is
H(O)_{m}
-equivariant, i.e.
f(xh)=\pi(h)f(x)
where
x\in X_{i}
and
h\in H(O)_{m}
.

(3)

The quotient of the support of
f
by
H(O)_{m}
is compact.

We have obvious (restriction) maps
\mathcal{S}(X_{i+1},V)\to S(X_{i},V)
.
Thus we can form he pro-vector space
\mathbb{S}(V)
equal to the projective limit of the
\mathcal{S}(X_{i},V)
.

The pro-vector space
\mathbb{S}(V)
has an obvious action of the group
H(K)
on the left (one has to check that this is actually a smooth representation of
H(K)
on the pro-vector space
\mathbb{S}(V)
– this is done in Section 3 of
[
7
]
; also one has to check that it is independent of the choice of the
X_{i}
which is easy and we shall not discuss it here).

The same definition makes sense for induction from any ”thick” subgroup of
H(O)
(cf.
[
7
]
): by definition a subgroup of
H(O)
is thick if it is equal to the preimage of a closed subgroup of
H(O)_{m}
for some
m\geq 0
.

Remark 12.2
.

The reader should be warned that this functor is denoted by
i\,_{H(O)}^{H(K)}
in
[
7
]
. In
loc.cit.
the authors also define another induction functor
\widetilde{i}\,_{H(O)}^{H(K)}
which is always right adjoint to restriction functor from
H(K)
to
H(O)
. This functor is endowed with a natural map of functors

i\,_{H(O)}^{H(K)}\to\widetilde{i}\,_{H(O)}^{H(K)}
. The definition of
\widetilde{i}\,_{H(O)}^{H(K)}
is similar to the above, except that we drop condition (3) above. In the case when
H
is reductive, the quotient
X_{i}/H(O)_{m}
is automatically compact, so this condition is satisfied automatically, therefore for reductive
G
the two functors coincide. This is the reason why in this case our functor
\operatorname{ind}_{H(O)}^{H(K)}
is actually right adjoint to the restriction functor.

12.2.
The representations

Let

of depth

12.3.
A contradiction and its resolution

At this point it might seem that we have a contradiction. Namely, since

but its image in

(which is equal to the semi-infinite orbit

Thus to prove both assertions of Theorem

Lemma 12.7

Since the corresponding terms of both filtrations are permuted by
