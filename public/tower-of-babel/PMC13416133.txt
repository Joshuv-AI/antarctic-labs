Simulating 135 years of post-hatchling sea turtle dispersal in a dynamic North Atlantic Ocean
Authors: Michael D Arendt, Nathan F Putman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAfter emerging from sandy beaches, sea turtle hatchlings enter oceans where surface circulation influences their distribution and survival. Studies report associations between sea turtle nesting and climate indices in the years and decades prior to nesting. Here we investigated the relationship between climate indicators and predicted dispersal of hatchling sea turtles to better understand the mechanisms by which climate may influence sea turtle population dynamics. We used the Simple Ocean Data Assimilation (SODA) model to simulate dispersal for 135 cohorts of loggerhead sea turtles (Caretta caretta) from nesting locations along the eastern U.S. coast. Simulations revealed 68% of 6.075 million particles traveled east of 60°W (NEA), with 78% dispersing to within 501–2000 km of a reference location in the Azores Islands, Portugal. Time series analysis revealed the lowest NEA dispersal during 1896–1900, but highest dispersal a century later. NEA particle dispersal significantly (and positively) correlated with eastward current, salinity, cyclonic activity, ocean temperature, solar irradiation, and (since 1958), atmospheric CO2. These findings, coupled with significant associations between climate indices and oceanographic conditions extracted from the SODA model, suggest valuable context can come from further examining lagged climate associations with population trends for sea turtles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-59726-x.Keywords: North Atlantic (NA) Ocean, particle simulation, SODA model, Climate, Sea turtlesSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Ocean sciences

Introduction

Global ocean teleconnection represents “a complicated, turbulent flow” that begins with heat loss and cool water sinking in the North Atlantic (NA) Ocean1. Due to the feedback loop nature of climate, the spatial distribution of global heat gain and sink regions remains relatively stable through time1. As such, latitudes 20°N and 40°N in the NA Ocean consistently represent the greatest meridional heat fluxes among global oceans1. In the NA Ocean, net heat gain for latitudes 20–40°N extends eastward to longitude 50°W and precedes heat loss and cool water sinking which occurs further north and east2.

Within this broad heat flux zone in the NA Ocean the Gulf Stream current plays a prominent thermodynamic role and influences marine life recruitment and dispersal dynamics via advection. Economically important finfish species that spawn near the edge of the continental shelf3,4 benefit from Gulf Stream eddies which enhance larval transport onto the continental shelf both south5 and north6,7 of Cape Hatteras, NC. The Gulf Stream current also bolsters long-range dispersal of highly migratory species like the European eel (Anguilla anguilla) and loggerhead sea turtle (Caretta caretta) to developmental habitats in the eastern Atlantic8,9. Considering the potential for oceanic transport of both eel leptocephali9,10 and sea turtle hatchlings11,12, simulations reveal adaptive value for their respective geographic origination areas. Notably, concentrated eel spawning in the Sargasso Sea increases the likelihood of larval transport into the Gulf Stream9,10, while shorter transit distances for hatchling sea turtles from nesting beaches correlate with greater loggerhead sea turtle nest density along the U.S. coastline11.

Reproducing at locations with favorable transport for offspring likely provides evolutionary advantages but shifts in hydrographic conditions can also occur which accentuate temporal variability among cohorts. In the NA Ocean, the trajectory of the Gulf Stream current reflects the intensity and positioning of the atmospheric Bermuda-Azores high2, which in turn influences the biological success of events that occur within entrainment proximity of this important current. Shifts in the Bermuda-Azores high align with positive and negative phases of the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO13;, one of three tele-connected indices of natural variability in the NA Ocean14. NAO phase alters trophic regimes15 and influences cyclone tracks16. As such, association between the NAO in March a year prior to loggerhead sea turtle nesting17 may reflect indirect foraging effects on the timing and frequency of reproductive activity18. In the Gulf of Mexico, virtual particle simulations reveal decreased hatchling dispersal with increased cyclone activity19, whereas in the eastern NA Ocean, increased storms may drive broader dispersal20. These findings collectively suggest that NAO phase could also contribute to long-term population dynamics that differ among regions.

Lagged associations between climatic conditions and sea turtle nesting data pique interest in natural influences on population dynamics, but to date researchers have not fully elucidated the hydrographic bases for climate associations reported in the literature21,22. To better elucidate potential oceanographic effects driving associations between climate indices and population trends, we simulated annual particle dispersal within an ocean circulation model for more than a century (i.e., 1871–2005 cohorts) from five locations spanning important loggerhead sea turtle nesting beaches in the southeast U.S. Prior simulations identified spatial aspects of post-hatchling loggerhead habitat23,24. Here we investigate long-term temporal variation in ocean circulation that influences post-hatchling dispersal of loggerhead sea turtles in the North Atlantic. Specifically, we quantify the potential for passive eastward transport, as this would be expected to reduce energetic costs to young turtles as they travel towards productive foraging grounds around the Azores Islands and could play a role in long-term fitness8,12. Our first objective was to characterize the distribution of particle fates relative to 60°W, further east than the net heat gain region in the NA Ocean2. Our second objective was to evaluate particle dispersal in the context of time series anomalies among particle fates. Our third objective was to examine associations between particle time series trends and 14 indices of hydrographic and meteorological conditions25.

Methods

Particle release location selection

The biological review team (BRT) for loggerhead sea turtles recognizes nine global populations, two of which occur in the NA Ocean26. The Northwest Atlantic (NWA) population features the largest global rookery for this species and occurs in the Peninsular Florida Recovery Unit (PRU) which comprises ~ 90% of annual nesting for this population27. The northern recovery unit (NRU) for NWA loggerheads spans Georgia through Virginia and comprises ~ 7% of annual nesting, with the remainder of nesting for this population in the Caribbean Sea and Gulf of Mexico27. We simulated the dispersal of post-hatchling loggerheads from five locations along the eastern coast of the U.S. which approximated these important nesting rookeries (Fig. 1) using output from the Simple Ocean Data Assimilation (SODA) model28,29, version 2.2.4; https://iridl.ldeo.columbia.edu/SOURCES/.CARTON-GIESE/.SODA/.v2p2p4/).

The blue star in the eastern NA Ocean denotes the Azores Islands, Portugal. At ocean basin scales, the SODA model promotes the study of ocean circulation30–32 as a monthly mean of ocean velocity and temperature at a spatial resolution of 0.5° grid spacing. Some models offer finer spatial resolution (e.g., the Hybrid Coordinate Ocean Model (HYCOM) with 0.08° grid spacing and sub-daily snapshots of velocity fields) but only provide coverage since the 1990s. In contrast, the coarser resolution of SODA covers a much longer temporal range (1871–2008) but which in turn requires coarser spatial resolution given increased computational burden. Transport predictions reflect ocean model resolution, and coarser products tend to “average out” aspects of circulation that play important roles in the movement of marine organisms33. While the SODA model does not adequately resolve the complex flow patterns in coastal areas, it can predict long-term trends in basin-scale transport. Notably, virtual particles released in the Florida Current for both SODA and HYCOM simulations demonstrated similar spatial and temporal trends in eastward transport over the same 14-year period32. Thus, broadscale transport variation in the context of long-term climate indices represents an appropriate use of the SODA model to simulate sea turtle cohort effects.

Particle releases

To account for the offshore swimming of newly hatched loggerheads in which yolk reserves fuel ~ 40 km of swimming34 and the limited ability of SODA to resolve fine scale features of ocean circulation in coastal waters, we chose particle release locations seaward of nesting beaches at a target distance of 45 km (actual seaward distance spanned 37 to 50 km). Narrow continental shelf width off southeast Florida (SE FL) placed this release location east of the continental shelf break (by 14 km) and in deeper water (507 m) than four other release locations which occurred 36–88 km west of the continental shelf and in water 21–29 m deep. Particle releases occurred 153 to 261 km apart off North Carolina (NC; 33.50°N, -77.75°W), South Carolina (SC; 32.65°N, -79.00°W), Georgia (GA; 31.25°N, -80.75°W), northeastern Florida (NE FL; 29.25°N, -80.50°W) and southeastern Florida (SE FL; 27.00°N, -79.70°W). Virtual particle releases in July, August, and September coincided with months when most loggerhead sea turtle hatchlings disperse from nesting beaches. Particle releases occurred at random coordinates within a 25 km radius of each of the five locations. As such, these particle release areas (area = 1964 km2) comprised 73% of the 0.5°-degree latitude x 0.5° longitude resolution (~ 2680 km2) of the model.

Using Ichthyop software (version 3; https://ichthyop.org/), we virtually released 3000 particles in July, August and September at each of the five ‘parent’ locations annually during 1871–2005 for a total 45,000 virtual particles released along the southeast U.S. coast each year. Particle transport reflected advection through the upper most SODA velocity field (5 m depth) using the Runge-Kutta 4th -order method each hour and saved daily35, with the location after 1 year of transport used for subsequent analyses.

We released the same number of particles from each release site as our aim was to account for the underlying variability in the SODA model to statistically characterize transport potential from each location12. Although large differences in hatchling production exist across the sites modeled11, we did not need to explicitly account for this to consider relative changes in transport probability among sites12. Weighting transport predictions for hatchling production at each site to produce distribution maps aids with assessing bycatch or other anthropogenic risks24,36, but our study emphasized transport variability over time and space.

Initially, we tracked particles for 3 years (e.g., 1871–1874 … 2005–2008), hence the final cohort modeled was for 2005 rather than 2007. Here, we present only transport predictions for the first year of transport, as this allows for a more direct comparison with annually derived climate indices for the release year (see below). Moreover, the distribution of sea turtles beyond this period remains strongly influenced by these initial trajectories37, even for loggerhead sea turtles with oceanic durations of 5 to 15 years38,39. Furthermore, swimming behavior (not modeled herein) becomes increasingly important with age for the net movement of turtles as they select preferred habitat40–43. We did not simulate swimming behavior (i) to avoid obfuscated and layered assumptions regarding turtle orientation21,23 and (ii) to emphasize quantifying variability and potential trends in the underlying ocean circulation processes that influence dispersal. Moreover, irrespective of assumptions about swimming behavior, tracking potential for eastward transport provides a useful metric as greater probability of passive transport would increase the chances and reduce the energy expenditure required for turtles to reach productive foraging areas of the eastern Atlantic such as the Azores Islands8,12,40.

Companion data sets

In addition to U (east – west) and V (north – south) current velocities, SODA model grids also featured two additional metrics for a water depth of 5 m below the water surface: vertical (W) current velocity (m/s) and salinity (Sal). At the water surface, SODA model grids also featured water temperature (SST, °C) and sea surface height (SSH, m). For analysis, we condensed gridded values for all six of these metrics to monthly means (in each year) spanning 21°N to 55°N latitude and 80°W to 7°W longitude. The northern latitude criteria reflected the occurrence of sea turtle strandings in the British Isles44, while the southern latitude criteria reflected distinction between nesting populations in the western vs. eastern NA Ocean26. Longitudinal criteria reflected the furthest west particle release locations in the western NA Ocean, and the geographic boundary with the Mediterranean Sea population in the eastern NA Ocean26.

We compiled (prior to 1 June 2023) monthly data for six broad regional climate indices from the following online sources: (a) unsmoothed, long-format Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation (AMO), https://psl.noaa.gov/data/correlation/amon.us.long.data); (b-c) Atlantic Meridional Mode, sea surface temperature (AMMs) and wind (AMMw) components, https://psl.noaa.gov/data/timeseries/monthly/AMM/); (d) North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO), www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/precip/CWlink/pna/nao.shtml); (e) Atlantic cyclones (NHC), https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/#hurdat); and (f) El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/ensostuff/ensoyears.shtml. NHC (1851–) and AMO (1856–) data origin greatly preceded AMM, NAO, and ENSO data sets which began between 1948 and 1950. Hurricane data consisted of a maximum of four daily observations with associated geographical position and maximum wind speed data. Remaining climate data sets comprised monthly or running tri-monthly (El Niño-Southern Oscillation) values.

We also included two broad-scale atmospheric indices, with data for both obtained online on 18 July and 22 August 2025, respectively: monthly (1882–) total solar irradiance (TSI, W/m2) data (version 2.1) from the NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information (https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/metadata/geoportal/rest/metadata/item/gov.noaa.ncdc:C01541/html) and monthly (1958–) mean carbon dioxide (CO2) concentration in parts per million (ppm) measured at an altitude of 3400 m at the Mauna Loa Observatory of the NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory (https://gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends/data.html).

Statistical analysis, objective 1 (particle dispersal)

We assigned particles to one of three “fates” based on location after a year at liberty. Irrespective of latitude, particles with longitudes ≤ 60°W, the approximate longitude of the Lesser Antilles islands through Nova Scotia, Canada, received the designation NEA. Particles that remained west of this longitude received the designation NWA, unless they drifted onto land, in which case we designated them Excluded. We next computed a distance metric for each particle, which for NEA particles represented linear distance to a central reference location in the Azores Islands [38.499°N, -28.556°W]45, but which reflected dispersal distance relative to the central release coordinate for NWA and Excluded particles.

Given 6.075 million particles released, we summarized distances traveled for each particle fate and particle release month (independent of fate) using a histogram with 500-km intervals which spanned ≤ 500 km through the maximum distance interval observed. Next, we tested for histogram distribution normality (Anderson-Darling) for each fate and release month prior to comparing fates and release month distributions using correlation analysis (Pearson r). We used Minitab 20™ (Minitab Inc., State College, PA) for all statistical significance (α = 0.05) testing.

Statistical analysis, objective 2 (time series analysis)

To minimize the impact of outlier events and better convey temporal trends, we ‘smoothed’ time series analysis by condensing time to 27, five-year intervals vs. 135 annual increments. To account for both (i) the abundance of particles and (ii) variability in particle distances traveled, we divided each five-year particle abundance by the weighted histogram distance bin value, herein referred to as the dispersal index. To compute the weighted bin value, we first multiplied each bin abundance by sequential bin order (1 to max), after which we divided the sum of multiplier components by the raw sum of particles in the histogram. Lastly, we scaled dispersal index series by reporting them as anomaly series (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) to facilitate standardized comparisons between particle release locations and fates. Conversion to anomaly series involved subtracting the grand mean of index values (per release location and fate) from the appropriate five-year interval index value, then dividing this adjusted value by the appropriate grand standard deviation. To gauge the ability to pool anomaly series by release location within each fate, we also computed an anomaly series for each fate for all release locations combined, then assessed statistical similarity using correlation analysis.

Statistical analysis, objective 3 (time series associations)

For all 14 associative data sets, we converted monthly observations to a singular 14-month time series for each observation year, which reflected the earliest release month (July) through August of the subsequent year to coincide with the 12th month after release the previous September. Consistent with Objectives 1 and 2, we condensed time series from annual to five-year intervals, which reflected the sum of NHC days but the mean for the remaining 13 associative data sets. Consistent with Objective 2, we then reported time series for each data set as an anomaly series. To increase statistical power for correlation analysis, we included the maximum number of five-year intervals available for each associative data set. To evaluate the potential effect of comparing anomalies computed from dissimilar time intervals, we also used correlation analysis to test for statistical alignment for all three particle fates and nine of 14 associative data sets which sufficient duration to compare anomaly series originating in 19th century vs. since 1948. Anomaly time series for the longest iterations for all 14 associative data sets appear in Supplement 1; however, to better visualize trajectory inflection points, we reported “Cusum” anomaly series for the most statistically important associated data sets in the Results. Due to high serial correlation46, though, we did not include Cusum series in correlation analyses.

Results

Objective 1, particle dispersal fates

Between 1871 and 2005, we released 6.075 million virtual particles to simulate loggerhead sea turtle hatchling dispersal from SE FL through NC for 135 years (Fig. 2). 68% of particles (n = 4,122,787) traveled east of longitude 60°W within a year (Table 1). Year-end locations for these particles occurred 3 to 3,268 km from a reference location in the Azores Islands and exhibited a normal distribution (P = 0.543) among 500-km distance bins from this location. 19% (n = 1,143,654) of simulated particles remained in the NA Ocean but occurred west of 60°W after a year at liberty and exhibited a normal distribution (P = 0.418) relative to 500-km distance bins from where released (Table 1). 13% (n = 808,559) of simulated particles drifted onto land and did not exhibit a normal distribution (P < 0.005), largely attributed to 92% of these particles drifting onto land < 500 km from where released (Table 1).

Objective 3, time series associations

For three particle fates and nine associative data sets, significant correlation (P < 0.001, r ≥ 0.94) existed from 1946–50 through 2001–05 when anomaly series began with true series origin from 1871 to 1881 (Supplement 1, panels A-C) vs. when anomaly series commenced in 1946. Consequently, anomaly series for five parameters with later onsets (Supplement 1, panel D) should reflect realistic trends for their respective anomaly series originating during 1948–1958.

Among 12 associative data series which originated no later than 1881, only SSH, W, V, and AMO did not significantly correlate (P > 0.05) with sequential five-year interval (Table 3). NEA particles significantly increased over time and positively aligned with five associative data sets (presented with decreasing r-value): U, Sal, NHC, SST and Solar (Table 3). Post-1946, only one (NHC) of these five associations remained significant, and only one (CO2) new associative data set exhibited significant correlation (Table 4). Cusum visualization revealed systematic decline in NEA dispersal from 1871 to 1925, which mostly remained plateaued through 1960, then systematically increased thereafter (Fig. 4a). Cusum depiction revealed similar trajectories for five long-term associations, but with the most rapid trajectory reversal for U current flow after 1920 and the most protracted plateau (1926–1960) for NHC (Fig. 4b).

Discussion

We initiated this study to evaluate the mechanics of hypotheses proposed in recent decades that suggest a greater climate role in sea turtle population dynamics than traditionally appreciated for a clade often associated with anthropogenic-caused decline47. In the NA Ocean, Van Houtan and Halley48 first proposed that first-year survival of loggerhead sea turtles shaped cohort abundance which resonated decades later as nest count variability. However, subsequent mathematical modeling17,49 did not support high importance of first-year survival relative to (i) other life history parameters but especially (ii) interaction effects among parameters. In our present study, greater association between climate indices and SODA model hydrography than between climate indices and particle fates (Table 4) further downplays the importance of acute events during the first year of loggerhead sea turtle dispersal. Greater first year associations between hydrography and climate indices favor inferences which manifest over numerous years to explain association between climate indices and nesting, such as variable age at neritic recruitment and/or protracted variability in population sex ratio50.

Particles that traveled east of 60°W (i.e., NEA) dispersed widely relative to longitude but with greater density south of 40°N (Fig. 2), the approximate latitudinal boundary for cold water sinking in the NA Ocean2. Alternatively, NEA particles also reached latitudes north of 50°N, consistent with increased sea turtle sightings at such latitudes over the past century44. Irrespective of simulation year, greatest particle density occurred between latitudes 35 to 40°N and longitudes 44 to 36°W (Fig. 2). Within a year of transport, relatively few particles settled out near the Azores Islands, a seasonal hotspot for young loggerhead sea turtles38,39 located at a similar latitude (37°N) but further east (26°W). Even fewer particles (Fig. 2) occurred near the Madeira Archipelago and Canary Islands, also seasonal hotspots for loggerhead sea turtles in the NEA51,52. Likewise, after a year at liberty, no NEA particles passively reached the Mediterranean Sea (Fig. 2), a waterbody infrequently occupied by loggerhead sea turtles of NWA origin53. While longer drift times would increase the potential for particles to travel further east54, loggerhead behavior not depicted in our simulations likely contributes to this discrepancy. Specifically, simulations investigating the magnetic navigation behavior of young turtles show that even relatively weak swimming behavior increases their chances of targeting foraging areas around the Azores, highlighting the likely importance of active swimming for loggerheads to reach seasonal hot spots40.

Correspondence between climate indices and hydrographic conditions featured within the SODA model support and encourage further examination of association between biological indices and climatic conditions at appropriate lagged intervals. In the longest series analyzed (1871–2005), simulated hatchling dispersal to the NEA best aligned with east-west current flow, which inversely aligned with upwelling current (Table 3). Likewise, east-west current flow positively aligned with two oceanographic metrics (salinity, sea surface temperature), solar irradiance, and cyclonic activity (Table 3). Like our findings, global analysis for a similar time series (i.e., 1880–2010) also found both (i) long-term increase and (ii) pronounced inflection in the latter half of the time series for radiative forcing and sea surface temperature, as well as atmospheric CO2 which we only analyzed since 195855. In our analysis that included additional indices with origination dates between 1948 and 1958, simulated hatchling transport to the NEA only aligned with atmospheric CO2 and cyclonic activity (Table 4). Curiously, despite analyzing a nearly identical data set (i.e., 1958–2005 vs. 1959–2004), we did not detect a previously reported association between ENSO and atmospheric CO256, but which perhaps may reflect analyzing data using multi-year (present-study) vs. monthly56 intervals.

Although post-WWII constitutes a ‘long’ data set by most biological data set standards, analysis of the full data set since 1871 revealed several associations not evident in the ‘shorter’ time series due to a longer periodicity over which crucial changes in these parameters occurred. During 1871–2005, U current and Sal had the strongest alignment with NEA dispersal (Table 3), with the long-term trajectory change in U current preceding Sal by a decade (Fig. 4b). Long-term trajectory shifts for SST and NHC cyclonic activity also coincided with the SAL inflection point (1926–30, Fig. 4b) but did not vary as abruptly over the ensuing decades, which resulted in reduced correlation strength with NEA dispersal (Table 3). Given significant association between NEA dispersal and SST but not with the AMO, we attribute this finding to the oscillatory nature of the AMO (Supplement 1), which also reduced correlation strength between the AMO and SST in full data set (Table 3). Alternatively, AMO and SST correlated strongly in the post-WWII data set when neither parameter correlated with NEA dispersal (Table 4).

We observed a similar finding for solar irradiance, which exhibited similar correlation strength with NEA dispersal and NHC cyclonic activity in the full data set, but due to a plateaued oscillation in solar irradiance in the final six decades (Supplement 1), only NHC remained significantly correlated with NEA dispersal for the post-WWII data set (Table 4). In recent decades, satellite data document an 11-year activity cycle57, with > 80% of solar irradiance variation attributed to sunspots or related phenomenon58. As the central source of energy reaching Earth, basic tenants of physics support the notion that variation in solar activity should in turn contribute to variability in NA Ocean circulation, the first of many steps along the global ocean heat exchange conveyer belt1,2.

Particles that remained west of 60°W, both in the NA Ocean and those excluded due to drift onto land, mostly exhibited inverse associations with parameters that influenced NEA dispersal; however, a few novel associations did emerge. In the full data set, V current and excluded particle dispersal positively aligned (Table 3). In the post-WWII data set, neither V nor U current significantly correlated with any of the parameters considered in the pairwise comparisons, but NAO did significantly correlate with excluded particles (Table 4). NAO phase reflects meridional shifts in barometric pressure, which in turn shifts the jet stream latitude in the upper atmosphere59. However, a feedback loop exists between jet stream latitude prior to NAO onset59 as well as feedback intensity during southern displacement eddy propagation and NAO phase duration60. As such, inverse NAO pairing with excluded particles may an provide important insight for managing coastal fisheries stocks in the NA Ocean (i.e., negative NAO phase may favor coastal entrainment and vice-versa). Notably, European fisheries managers give similar credence to examining climatic conditions preceding abundances61 as traditionally reserved for analyzing biomass changes relative to fishing effort62. Given the importance of subtle but compounded changes in vital rates over years of survival49,61, we hope that the present study provides further encouragement to equally consider the importance of natural variation and direct anthropogenic impacts to sea turtles when interpreting changes in population metrics over time.

Conclusion

Transport predictions for loggerhead sea turtle post-hatchlings from 1871 to 2005 indicate both interannual variability and longer-term trends in the potential for eastward transport into the NEA, an oceanic expanse east of longitude 60°W where loggerhead sea turtles develop for roughly a decade before emigrating to neritic waters38,45. Combining all release sites, transport to the NEA varied from a low of 5% of tracked particles in 1871 to a high of 97% of particles in 1979. This broad range in NEA dispersal variability also exhibited a normal distribution, a component of long-term system equilibrium, both with respect to particle abundance and distances traveled relative to the Azores Islands. Nonetheless, we detected a significant trend in eastward transport over the 135 year time series, suggesting environmental conditions becoming increasingly favorable for sea turtles to access distant foraging grounds. We encourage further investigations by physical scientists into possible climate-transport associations described here using finer scale data than available with the SODA model, and by ecologists and managers to consider background physical processes alongside human activities to help contextualize demographic data sets in sea turtles and other species.
