Designing entry-monotone risk-sharing pools
Christopher Blier-Wong, Jean-Gabriel Lauzier

Abstract
While risk pooling lowers the total cost of risk, efficiency alone does not make a pool viable. Participants need terms that ensure their participation, that are immune to subgroups breaking away, and that allow new members to join. Under cash-additive risk measures, the minimum cost of a coalition’s risk determines the value created by that coalition, and deterministic side payments redistribute that value among participants. Institutional risk sharing is thus a transferable-utility cooperative game. We prove that the game is totally balanced whenever the risk measures are convex (agents are risk averse), so every coalition has a nonempty core and stable allocations always exist. We then analyze entry monotonicity through Population-Monotonic Allocation Schemes ( 57 ) , a strong requirement that is notoriously difficult to construct and has received limited attention in risk sharing. We find several structural conditions that ensure that either the Arrow–Debreu pricing surplus allocation rule or the proportional-cost surplus allocation rule satisfies this entry-monotonicity property, the latter being a novel cooperative notion we propose. These verifiable structural conditions naturally arise in pooled (re)insurance and credit portfolios, providing pool designers with a practical toolkit for building risk pools that remain stable and attractive as they expand.

Introduction

Risk pooling is a central mechanism for managing large losses that are difficult for any single organization to absorb. Countries and regional authorities pool catastrophe and climate-related disaster risks
(
65
;
21
)
; firms use insurance and contractual arrangements to share supply-chain disruption risks
(
58
;
26
)
, and insurers form pools to manage losses whose scale or volatility exceeds the capacity of a single balance sheet
(
27
;
4
)
. In all of these settings, pooling can create value by reducing the total cost of risk
(
15
;
64
;
48
)
.

However, the value created by risk pooling may be unequally distributed among participants depending on the details of the risk-sharing arrangement. Risk pooling, therefore, involves two decisions. The first is the (pure) risk allocation decision: who bears which random losses after the pool is formed. The second is the implementation decision: which premiums, prices, or side payments among participants make that allocation acceptable. In a risk exchange economy, the two are jointly determined by equilibrium prices.
The resulting exchange is efficient in the sense of Pareto optimality, as no participant can be made better off without making someone else worse off. In equilibrium, the market also determines how the surplus is divided, and the outcome might not be “fair” in any intuitive sense. Can risk pools sometimes do “better” than market mechanisms? If not, should risk pools emulate risk markets and use standard pricing mechanisms?

This paper analyzes the implementation problem for institutional risk sharing from the perspective of cooperative game theory. The intended applications are financial institutions, insurers, reinsurers, firms, governments, regional pools, and other organizations that evaluate risk in monetary terms. We work with cash-additive monetary risk measures, with particular attention to distortion risk measures
(
5
;
62
;
30
;
2
)
. These evaluations are natural in institutional settings because they encode tail-sensitive capital charges, premium principles, and risk-management objectives in monetary units
(
31
)
. They are less intended as a model of household-level informal risk sharing, where expected utility, consumption smoothing, or behavioural models are more appropriate.

The pivotal insight of our approach is that when deterministic cash transfers between agents are feasible, and agents evaluate positions in a common monetary numeraire using cash-additive criteria, then the induced risk-sharing game is a transferable-utility (TU) game. Cash additivity gives the implementation problem an exact monetary structure: under the standard loss convention, adding a deterministic cost
c
to an agent shifts that agent’s evaluation by exactly
c
. Balanced deterministic transfers thus do not change either the aggregate random allocation or the planner’s aggregate objective, but they redistribute welfare gains across participants. Once an efficient random allocation has been found, the remaining question is how to redistribute the surplus generated by pooling using cash payments. This redistribution problem is the central question that we tackle.

While an allocation can be efficient in the sense of minimizing the aggregate evaluated cost of risk, it can nevertheless fail as a business or policy mechanism if one participant is made worse off, if a subgroup can object to the proposed terms, or if incumbents lose when a new participant is admitted. We show that when all agents use convex risk measures (so they are risk averse), the risk-sharing game is always totally balanced, and each subcoalition has a nonempty core. This result, paralleling the Shapley-Shubik Theorem
(
52
)
, sheds light on the cooperative nature of risk-sharing games: when agents are risk averse, one can always find allocations that are stable in the sense that no coalition can profitably block them.

Having established the general stability of risk pools when agents are risk averse, we then ask whether they are entry monotone, meaning that incumbents never lose when a new participant is admitted. We surmise that entry monotonicity is the most natural notion of fairness that one can desire besides coalitional stability. Answering this question amounts to asking whether risk-sharing games admit a
Population Monotonic Allocation Scheme
(PMAS)
(
57
)
. PMAS is a refinement of the notion of core allocation that is notoriously difficult to construct because it requires the underlying TU game to be totally balanced.

We focus on two surplus allocation rules. The first is a novel allocation rule called the proportional-cost rule, which assigns each agent a share of the surplus proportional to the risk they contribute to the pool. We interpret the proportional-cost rule as a purely cooperative solution, since it is not generally guaranteed to yield a surplus allocation consistent with equilibrium. The second is the welfare allocation generated by the Arrow–Debreu pricing measure in a fictitious economy consisting only of the agents of a subcoalition; clearly, the Arrow–Debreu pricing rule is consistent with equilibrium behaviour by construction.

Both rules have a key operational advantage compared to other PMAS: they have the property that they allow for ‘‘myopic extensions of the pool” in the sense that they do not require the knowledge of the full coalition composition beforehand. They can thus be written into a contract or the pool’s constitution at the time of formation, without requiring any knowledge of future potential participants. To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first to propose such a notion of PMAS that satisfies this notion of myopic extensions.
1
1

1

The term myopic extension explicitly evokes both myopic policies in one-step-ahead decision processes, like in dynamic programming, and the notion of extendability in probability theory.

We provide many structural conditions that make these surplus allocation rules PMAS. For example, we show that when normalized coalition risks are ordered by convex order, then the proportional-cost rule allocation is a PMAS. Similarly, the Arrow–Debreu allocation rule yields a PMAS when the expected cost of agents’ risk contributions decreases as the coalition extends.
These conditions cover many cases used in practice, such as a broad family of elliptical risks used in quantitative risk management
(
45
)
, and comonotonic risks, the worst-case dependence structure in a given Fréchet class.

A practical takeaway from our approach is that the surplus allocation rules we analyze need not be PMAS under the same circumstances. One can easily find examples in which the proportional-cost allocation rule is a PMAS, but the Arrow–Debreu allocation rule is not, and vice versa. That is, there are circumstances in which a purely cooperative approach is justified because the underlying coalition-wise pricing measure does not guarantee entry monotonicity, and other circumstances in which it does, allowing the pool designer to simply emulate a market for risk. We note with interest that this observation opens the possibility of studying assortative matching, risk, and optimal pool design in a market for risk-sharing pools.

1.1
Contributions and literature

Our first contribution is in the definition of risk-sharing games. Let
\zeta
denote idiosyncratic risks and
\rho
denote monetary risk measures, such as the Expected Shortfall in regulatory capital reserve
(
5
;
7
;
61
)
. For a coalition
A\subseteq[n]
of financial institutions, the infimal cost achievable by pooling and redistributing risks is

C(A):=\inf_{(X_{i})_{i\in A}}\left\{\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(X_{i})\text{ subject to }\sum_{i\in A}X_{i}=\sum_{i\in A}\zeta_{i}\right\}.

Finding solutions to the problem above is the central problem of the standard risk-sharing literature
(
6
;
1
;
37
;
29
;
27
;
41
)
, and we say that the risk allocation
(X_{i})_{i\in A}
is efficient if it solves the problem.

By construction, the aggregate cost
C(A)
of such a coalition is less than the sum of the individual costs. We thus define its welfare gain as

\nu(A):=\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-C(A).

By cash additivity, balanced side payments do not affect the value of a coalition and
\nu(A)
is uniquely determined. The function
\nu
is therefore the
(scalar) characteristic function of a TU game
. We show in Lemma
1
that
\nu
is a zero-normalized
(
57
)
monotone and bounded set function
(
43
)
.

Three well-known consequences immediately stem from the TU structure of risk-sharing games. First, the utility possibility set of individually rational Pareto optimal allocations is a convex polyhedron; in fact, it is a simplex in our case (Theorem
1
). Second, any such allocation can be implemented through budget-balanced side payments. The third consequence is operational: one can often separate the problem of efficiency (solving for an efficient
risk allocation
to obtain
C(A)
) and the problem of redistributing the welfare surplus (finding adequate side payments). Operationally, this means that a coalition of financial institutions can rely on their quantitative analyst division to efficiently allocate risk and leave the executive members to bargain over the price of participating in the pool.

2

Interesting exceptions arise when one uses a “mechanical” pricing rule like actuarial fairness, which is not based on game theoretical foundations. In this case, it is possible to have two different efficient allocations that differ in their welfare split, and thus in their coalitional stability; see Appendix
C.3
for a longer discussion.

The next natural question is how the core of risk-sharing games behaves, where the core is defined as the subset of welfare allocations that no coalition can block. We show in Theorem
3
that the risk-sharing game is totally balanced when risk measures are convex, so each coalition has a nonempty core. This intuitive result establishes that one can always find a stable, mutually beneficial risk-sharing arrangement when agents are risk averse, paralleling the Shapley-Shubik Theorem
(
52
)
in market games without uncertainty but with concave utility functions. Section
3.3
gives the general pricing measure construction using the usual dual approach, and Appendix
B
records its lower-envelope formulation when specializing to distortion risk measures.

The distinction between efficiently allocating risk and fairly allocating the surplus of risk pooling is especially important because pools are not static. Catastrophe pools admit new countries, financial syndicates add counterparties, reinsurance arrangements expand capacity, and institutional risk-sharing mechanisms might evolve as new exposures appear. For example, a catastrophe pool may lower the risk-adjusted total cost by admitting a new country, while still requiring premium terms that prevent incumbents from bearing an unattractive share of the entrant’s hurricane or earthquake exposure. The
Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Facility
’s staggered, and legally segmented expansion illustrates the operational complexity of admitting heterogeneous members under a common catastrophe-risk-pooling platform
(
65
;
17
)
. Similar issues arise in operations research models of supply-chain disruption, profit-sharing agreements, central counterparty risk allocation, and risk-pooling expansion
(
26
;
32
;
33
;
4
)
.

New participants can increase aggregate welfare through two channels: diversification and risk-bearing capacity. Diversification arises when the entrant’s loss is imperfectly correlated with the existing aggregate loss, so that pooling can reduce risk-adjusted cost, capital intensity, or variability relative to stand-alone coverage. Risk-bearing capacity arises when the entrant is willing to absorb some of the aggregate risk at a lower evaluated cost than the incumbents. An example of risk-bearing capacity is reinsurance, where an external risk bearer supplies capacity for a premium and absorbs part of the aggregate exposure without adding new underlying exposure. But aggregate value creation does not imply that every incumbent benefits. A transfer rule (i.e., a price or side payments) must therefore address not only implementability for a fixed pool but also guarantee that expansion is beneficial to everyone. Despite their practical importance, the cooperative-game properties of canonical risk-sharing transfer rules, particularly their behaviour under coalition expansion, have not been systematically studied in the existing literature.

This motivates our extensive analysis of stable expansion and PMAS
(
57
)
in Section
4
. A PMAS requires a coherent family of core allocations for all coalitions, chosen so that incumbents weakly benefit whenever a coalition expands, a stronger requirement than merely requiring the core property. Most risk/surplus allocation rules do not generally satisfy this property. This includes not only the proportional-cost and the coalition-wise pricing rule analyzed in the main text, but also the actuarially fair rule, the coalition-wise Shapley rule, and the coalition-wise nucleolus; see Appendix
C.6
for more details.

We identify conditions that generate PMAS surplus allocation rules. Convex-order consistency gives a PMAS for the proportional-cost rule: when each incumbent’s coalitional average improves in convex order as the coalition expands, convexity of the risk measures ensures that no incumbent is made worse off (Theorem
6
). For dual pricing, we find a cross-coalition compatibility criterion: prices selected by larger coalitions must support the larger-coalition allocation relative to the allocations available in smaller coalitions (Theorem
9
).

We collect some special cases that satisfy the conditions under which proportional-cost or dual pricing rules are PMAS, focusing on the most useful cases for institutional risk-sharing. For elliptical losses, Arrow–Debreu pricing yields a PMAS under a cross-monotonicity condition involving correlations with coalition aggregate risks (Corollary
4
), generalizing the results of
19
. For comonotonic endowments, the Arrow–Debreu pricing rule is also a PMAS if agents evaluate risk through distortion risk measures (Corollary
6
). More generally, we provide conditions under which convex ordering of endowments or conditional means supports PMAS constructions for independent risks, exchangeable pools, and some specific families of random vectors that are relevant for (re)insurance, including mean-parametrized convolution semigroups, Dirichlet–Liouville radial risks, and frailty-type portfolios (Appendix
D
).

Although cooperative allocation, fair pricing, and surplus division are well-developed themes in the operations research literature, only a small part of the risk-sharing literature explicitly analyzes risk-sharing through cooperative game theory. This literature provides only partial results, and no previous paper has fully leveraged the TU structure of risk-sharing games. See
(
23
;
35
;
19
;
4
)
for risk sharing, and
(
28
;
12
)
for the core property of equilibrium allocations. A complementary line of research approaches the pricing problem from an equilibrium perspective.
14
separate allocation and pricing in bilateral risk sharing under comonotonic additive preferences;
13
study competitive equilibria in comonotonic markets with dual-utility agents;
12
analyze Pareto-efficient contracts in a centralized insurance market, and
34
characterize efficiency and equilibrium support in pure-exchange economies with risk-averse monetary utilities. Our lower-envelope Arrow–Debreu pricing rule in Appendix
B
is closely related to this equilibrium logic, but our interpretation differs. We use the pricing measure to implement efficient multilateral risk-sharing cooperative arrangements through deterministic transfers and to prove the coalitional stability of the induced welfare split, but we do not necessarily interpret those as an equilibrium outcome in a market.

Risk-sharing is related to a large operations research literature on cooperative cost allocation and surplus division. Axiomatic shared-cost allocation and equitable cost-sharing prices study how common costs should be divided among participants
(
9
;
46
)
. Optimization-induced cooperative games also arise in stochastic inventory centralization and newsvendor settings, where duality, core stability, and entry monotonicity are used to construct stable allocation rules
(
20
;
18
)
. Related surplus-division questions appear in supply chains through cost-savings allocation, profit-sharing agreements, and Nash bargaining
(
40
;
47
;
32
)
. Institutional risk sharing fits this broader research line: pooling creates a joint surplus, and the central operational question is whether that surplus can be allocated through transfers that are individually rational, coalitionally stable, and robust to myopic extension.

This perspective is also consistent with operational research work in which cooperative games are generated by optimization models under uncertainty: stochastic-programming duality yields constructive core allocations in inventory centralization games
(
20
)
, while dynamic linear programming games have been extended to risk-averse players using coherent conditional risk measures
(
59
)
. Our total-balancedness condition is related to the result of
3
, who show that subadditivity together with degree-one homogeneity implies total balancedness for regular cooperative cost games.

Finally, a related paper on pool expansion is
4
, which studies entropic risk measures with jointly normal losses and introduces strong and weak consensus conditions for one-step expansion under actuarial and equilibrium pricing. While they are motivated by a notion similar to our “myopic extensions”, their framework focuses on entry analysis without characterizing full PMAS requirements over the entire coalition lattice. Our intended applications are institutional, including nations, reinsurers, and financial syndicates, which have the capacity to evaluate subcoalition deviations. For such participants, a transfer rule that fails PMAS is harder to defend against subgroup objections, and we surmise that the appropriate stability benchmark is consistency across the entire coalition lattice rather than one-step expansion alone.

Preliminaries

2.1
Risk measures

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be an atomless probability space. We denote by
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{R}
, namely the smallest
\sigma
-algebra containing the open subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. Unless otherwise stated, random variables are real-valued Borel-measurable functions on
\Omega
. Fix
p\in[1,\infty]
and set
\mathcal{X}:=L^{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Random variables represent losses, so positive values correspond to costs.

An extended-valued functional
\rho:\mathcal{X}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is
proper
if its effective domain
\dom\rho:=\{Y\in\mathcal{X}:\rho(Y)<\infty\}
is nonempty. Fix a positive integer
n
and let
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
. For each
i\in[n]
, agent
i
evaluates losses through a proper risk measure
\rho_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to(-\infty,\infty]
. Consider the following properties:

(A1)

Monotonicity
:
Y\leq Y^{\prime}
a.s. implies
\rho(Y)\leq\rho(Y^{\prime})
.

(A2)

Cash additivity
:
\rho(0)\in\mathbb{R}
and
\rho(Y+c)=\rho(Y)+c
for all
Y\in\mathcal{X}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

(A3)

Subadditivity
:
\rho(Y+Y^{\prime})\leq\rho(Y)+\rho(Y^{\prime})
for all
Y,Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.

(A4)

Positive homogeneity
:
\rho(\lambda Y)=\lambda\rho(Y)
for all
Y\in\mathcal{X}
and
\lambda\geq 0
.

(A5)

Convexity
:
\rho(\lambda Y+(1-\lambda)Y^{\prime})\leq\lambda\rho(Y)+(1-\lambda)\rho(Y^{\prime})
for all
Y,Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

(A6)

Lower semicontinuity
:
\rho
is lower semicontinuous on
\mathcal{X}
.

(A7)

Law invariance
:
\rho(Y)=\rho(Y^{\prime})
whenever
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
have the same distribution.

(A8)

Comonotonic additivity
:
\rho(Y+Y^{\prime})=\rho(Y)+\rho(Y^{\prime})
for all comonotonic
Y,Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Throughout the paper, each
\rho_{i}
,
i\in[n]
, is proper and satisfies (
A2
); the initial losses introduced below satisfy
\zeta_{i}\in\dom\rho_{i}
for every
i\in[n]
. A functional satisfying (
A1
) and (
A2
) is called a
monetary risk measure
. It is
convex
if it satisfies (
A5
), and
coherent
if it additionally satisfies (
A3
) and (
A4
); every coherent risk measure is convex. Convexity is imposed where total balancedness is proved, and monotonicity is used only when explicitly stated.

Convex risk measures admit a standard dual description in terms of supporting linear functionals. To state the version used below, fix a separating dual pair
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Z})
, so that
\mathcal{Z}
is the chosen dual partner of
\mathcal{X}
, with pairing
\langle Y,\pi\rangle:=\mathbb{E}[\pi Y]
whenever this expectation is well-defined. If a proper convex risk measure
\rho:\mathcal{X}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is lower semicontinuous for the weak topology
\sigma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Z})
induced by this dual pair, i.e. the topology generated by the maps
Y\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\pi Y]
for
\pi\in\mathcal{Z}
, then the Fenchel–Moreau theorem gives the dual representation

\rho(Y)=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{Z}}\big\{\mathbb{E}[\pi Y]-\alpha_{\rho}(\pi)\big\},\qquad Y\in\mathcal{X},

(1)

where the minimal penalty is the convex conjugate

\alpha_{\rho}(\pi):=\sup_{Y\in\mathcal{X}}\{\mathbb{E}[\pi Y]-\rho(Y)\},\qquad\pi\in\mathcal{Z}.

(2)

For the agent-specific risk measures, write
\alpha_{i}:=\alpha_{\rho_{i}}
for
i\in[n]
. On the effective domain of the penalty, cash additivity forces
\mathbb{E}[\pi]=1
, and monotonicity forces
\pi\geq 0
; the two together make
\pi
a probability density.

Distortion risk measures
are monetary risk measures satisfying (
A4
) and (
A8
).
Let

\mathcal{H}:=\{h:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}:\ h\text{ is non-decreasing with }h(0)=0\text{ and }h(1)=1\}.

A function
h\in\mathcal{H}
is called a probability distortion, the set function
h\circ\mathbb{P}
is called a capacity, and a distortion risk measure
\rho_{h}
is the Choquet integral defined as

\rho_{h}(Y)=\int Y\,\mathrm{d}h\circ\mathbb{P}=\int_{0}^{\infty}h(\mathbb{P}(Y>x))\,\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left[h(\mathbb{P}(Y>x))-h(1)\right]\,\,\mathrm{d}x,

(3)

where the first integral is in the sense of Choquet and the last two are in the sense of Lebesgue. Under the convention that
Y\in\mathcal{X}
represents a loss, if the distortion function
h
is concave, then the distortion risk measure
\rho_{h}
is subadditive, and thus coherent.

2.2
Optimal allocations

Each agent
i\in[n]
holds an initial loss
\zeta_{i}
. For a coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, set
X_{A}:=\sum_{i\in A}\zeta_{i}
; in particular,
X:=X_{[n]}
is the aggregate loss of the grand coalition. Here and elsewhere, when a coalition-indexed object has no coalition subscript, it refers to the grand coalition
[n]
. For a loss
Y\in\mathcal{X}
and
1\leq m\leq n
, write

\mathbb{A}_{m}(Y):=\left\{(Y_{i})_{i\in[m]}\in\mathcal{X}^{m}:\ \sum_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}=Y\right\}.

We interpret
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(X)
as a risk-sharing arrangement, with original no-sharing allocation
\boldsymbol{\zeta}:=(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n})\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(X)
.

Definition 1

(Comonotonicity)
.

Random variables
Y,Z\in\mathcal{X}
are
comonotonic
if

\bigl(Y(\omega)-Y(\omega^{\prime})\bigr)\bigl(Z(\omega)-Z(\omega^{\prime})\bigr)\geq 0\quad\text{for $(\mathbb{P}\otimes\mathbb{P})$-a.s.\ $(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\in\Omega\times\Omega$.}

A collection
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
is comonotonic if each pair
(Y_{i},Y_{j})
,
i,j\in[n]
, is comonotonic.

By Denneberg’s Lemma
(
24
, Proposition 4.5)
,
Y
and
Z
are comonotonic if and only if there exists a random variable
W
and nondecreasing functions
f,g
such that
Y=f(W)
and
Z=g(W)
almost surely. The same representation extends to
n
-tuples:
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
is comonotonic if and only if there exists
W
and nondecreasing
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
with
Y_{i}=f_{i}(W)
for all
i\in[n]
. In particular, one may take
W=\sum_{i\in[n]}Y_{i}
.

For a nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, let
\mathbb{A}_{|A|}^{+}(Y):=\{\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{A}_{|A|}(Y):\ \boldsymbol{Y}\text{ is comonotonic}\}
. The coalition inf-convolution is

\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(Y):=\inf_{\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{A}_{|A|}(Y)}\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(Y_{i}),\qquad Y\in\mathcal{X},

(4)

and the coalition cost is

C(A):=\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(X_{A}).

(5)

Set
X_{\varnothing}:=0
and
C(\varnothing):=0
. The associated comonotonic inf-convolution is

\mathop{\boxplus}\displaylimits_{i\in[n]}\rho_{i}(X):=\inf_{\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}^{+}(X)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i}).

(6)

For every nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
and loss
Y\in\mathcal{X}
, define

\mathrm{Opt}_{A}(Y):=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{A}_{|A|}(Y)}\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(Y_{i})\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{|A|}(Y).

In particular,
\mathrm{Opt}(X)
denotes the efficient allocations of the aggregate loss
X
for the grand coalition.

Unless explicitly stated otherwise, the following assumptions are in force throughout the paper.

Assumption 1
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be an atomless standard probability space,
p\in[1,\infty]
,
and
\mathcal{X}=L^{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. For every
i\in[n]
, assume

\rho_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to(-\infty,\infty]

is proper, cash additive, convex, lower semicontinuous, and law invariant, and

\zeta_{i}\in\operatorname{dom}\rho_{i}
.

The following proposition is the Comonotonic Improvement Theorem
(
38
;
42
)
. It states that any non-comonotonic allocation can be Pareto-improved by a comonotonic rearrangement when the evaluations are convex, so the infimum over all allocations coincides with the infimum over comonotonic ones.

Proposition 1

(Comonotonic Improvement Theorem)
.

Let
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n}
be convex risk measures. Then

\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{{i\in[n]}}\rho_{i}(X)=\mathop{\boxplus}\displaylimits_{i\in[n]}\rho_{i}(X)
for
X\in\mathcal{X}.

The following standard exactness result gives finite, attained coalition inf-convolutions. We use the loss-convention version of
29
.

Proposition 2
.

For every nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, the inf-convolution
\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}
is proper, cash additive, convex, lower semicontinuous, and law invariant. Moreover,
\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(X_{A})
is exact: for every
Y\in\mathcal{X}
there exists
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{A}_{|A|}(Y)
such that

\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(Y)=\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(Y_{i}).

In particular,
C(A)=\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(X_{A})
is finite,
\mathrm{Opt}_{A}(X_{A})
is nonempty, and
\mathrm{Opt}_{A}(X_{A})
contains a comonotonic allocation.

An allocation
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(X)
is
Pareto optimal
if there is no
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(X)
with
\rho_{i}(Y_{i})\leq\rho_{i}(X_{i})
for all
i\in[n]
and strict inequality for some
i\in[n]
.

Individual rationality
(IR) requires that agents weakly prefer participation to autarky:

\rho_{i}(X_{i})\leq\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i}),\qquad i\in[n].

(7)

Let
\mathbb{A}_{n,\mathrm{IR}}(X):=\{\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(X):\ \boldsymbol{X}\text{ satisfies \eqref{eq:IR}}\}
. An
individually rational Pareto optimal
allocation is any Pareto optimal allocation in
\mathbb{A}_{n,\mathrm{IR}}(X)
.

3
Risk-sharing games

We define the risk-sharing game generated by coalition-wise inf-convolution, i.e., efficient coalition-wise risk allocation.
Because of cash additivity, the problem of implementing risk sharing is a TU cooperative game. We analyze two basic properties that an allocation must satisfy: individual rationality and coalitional stability. Section
3.3
develops the general dual-pricing rule, while Appendix
B
records the lower-envelope distortion specialization.

3.1
Risk-sharing games as TU games

Consider a risk-sharing pool whose members may reallocate only their own aggregate endowment. The cost of this restricted pool is the coalition-wise inf-convolution of the members’ risk measures, and the difference between this cost and autarky is the welfare gain available for redistribution.

Definition 2

(Gain game)
.

For a coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, define its welfare gain by

\nu(A):=\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-C(A).

(8)

The gain game records the best welfare improvement that a coalition can obtain by reallocating only its own endowments. The next lemma shows that
\nu
is a (i) zero-normalized, (ii) nonnegative, (iii) monotone, and (iv) bounded set function.

Lemma 1
.

The gain game in (
8
) satisfies:

(i)

\nu(\{i\})=0
for every
i\in[n]
;

(ii)

\nu(A)\geq 0
for every
A\subseteq[n]
;

(iii)

if
A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
, then
\nu(A)\leq\nu(B)
;

(iv)

\nu([n])<\infty
.

Lemma
1
shows that (i) autarky yields zero gain, that (ii) no coalition loses from having the option to share internally, and that (iii) enlarging the coalition cannot reduce the attainable gain.

Since
\nu(A)
is a scalar welfare gain attached to each coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, the pair
([n],\nu)
is the characteristic-function form of the risk-sharing game. It is a transferable utility game because of cash additivity. Formally, a vector
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
on the balanced-budget hyperplane

H:=\left\{\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \sum_{i\in[n]}c_{i}=0\right\}

(9)

preserves feasibility,
\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{c}:=(X_{1}+c_{1},\dots,X_{n}+c_{n})\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(X)
, and shifts evaluations componentwise,
\rho_{i}(X_{i}+c_{i})=\rho_{i}(X_{i})+c_{i}
. We define a
transfer rule
as a deterministic mapping

((\rho_{1},\zeta_{1}),\dots,(\rho_{n},\zeta_{n}))\longmapsto(c_{1}(X_{1},\zeta_{1}),\dots,c_{n}(X_{n},\zeta_{n}))

attached to an optimal allocation
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
. Pareto optimality of
\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{c}
is equivalent to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i})=\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in[n]}\rho_{i}
, so the question of which transfer rule to apply is a question of selection within the set of efficient allocations.

Definition 3

(Transfer-equivalence classes)
.

For allocations
\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(X)
write
\boldsymbol{X}\sim\boldsymbol{Y}
if
\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{c}\in H
. The equivalence classes defined by
\sim
are called the transfer-equivalence classes, and we write
\mathrm{Opt}(X)_{/\sim}
for the quotient space defined by
\sim
.

Notice that a transfer-equivalence class always has a canonical pure risk allocation
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
, namely a representative whose components
X_{0,i}
consist only of the random reallocation of risk and carry no additive cash component. Other elements of the class are obtained by applying a balanced transfer
\boldsymbol{c}\in H
to
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}
. Notice that we also meaningfully refer to
\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)_{/\sim}
as different risk allocations when
\boldsymbol{X}\nsim\boldsymbol{Y}
so they do not belong to the same transfer-equivalence class. Finally, notice that
\boldsymbol{X}\nsim\boldsymbol{Y}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\nsim\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}
for
\boldsymbol{X}\sim\boldsymbol{X}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}\sim\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}
. Taken together, these observations imply that we can slightly abuse the nomenclature and refer to different
\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)_{/\sim}
as either risk allocations or pure risk allocations whenever this is unambiguous.

While we write deterministic transfers in the form
\boldsymbol{X}\mapsto\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{c}
with
\boldsymbol{c}\in H
from (
9
), it is generally more convenient to track transfers through evaluation vectors and welfare gains.

Definition 4

(Evaluation vector and welfare baselines)
.

For
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(X)
with finite component evaluations, define the evaluation vector

\boldsymbol{r}(\boldsymbol{X}):=(\rho_{1}(X_{1}),\dots,\rho_{n}(X_{n}))\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Define agentwise welfare baselines (risk reductions) relative to the initial endowment
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
by

b_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}):=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-\rho_{i}(X_{i}),\qquad i\in[n].

We reserve the symbol
w_{i}
for the post-transfer welfare share assigned to agent
i\in[n]
, defined by
w_{i}:=b_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})-c_{i}
when a balanced transfer
\boldsymbol{c}\in H
is applied to
\boldsymbol{X}
. Since transfers act componentwise on evaluations, meaning
\boldsymbol{r}(\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{c})=\boldsymbol{r}(\boldsymbol{X})+\boldsymbol{c}
for
\boldsymbol{c}\in H
, we have that
\{\boldsymbol{r}(\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{c}):\boldsymbol{c}\in H\}=\boldsymbol{r}(\boldsymbol{X})+H
is an affine hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The next theorem describes the geometry of individually rational allocations for an efficient risk allocation: the simplex below is the imputation simplex of the gain game, and the theorem recovers the well-known result that the utility possibility frontier of zero-normalized TU games is a simplex.

Theorem 1
.

Fix a pure risk allocation
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
, let
b_{i}:=b_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})
for
i\in[n]
be as in Definition
4
, and write
W:=\nu([n])
for the grand-coalition gain. Then:

(i)

The set of transfers that make
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}
individually rational is

H_{\mathrm{IR}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0}):=\left\{\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \sum_{i\in[n]}c_{i}=0,\ \ c_{i}\leq b_{i}\ \forall i\in[n]\right\},

(10)

and is nonempty. For
\boldsymbol{c}\in H
, the post-transfer welfare shares
w_{i}:=b_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0}+\boldsymbol{c})
satisfy
w_{i}=b_{i}-c_{i}
for
i\in[n]
, and
\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}=W
.

(ii)

H_{\mathrm{IR}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})
is in bijection with the imputation simplex
\Delta_{W}:=\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}:\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}=W\}
via
w_{i}=b_{i}-c_{i}
and
c_{i}=b_{i}-w_{i}
for
i\in[n]
. When
W>0
, the extreme points of
\Delta_{W}
are
\{W\boldsymbol{e}^{(k)}:k\in[n]\}
, where
\boldsymbol{e}^{(k)}
denotes the
k

Theorem
1
separates the risk-sharing problem into an efficiency part and an implementability part. Once a pure risk allocation is fixed, individual rationality reduces to a linear system in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the welfare simplex
\Delta_{W}
parameterizes admissible transfers. At the vertex
\boldsymbol{w}=W\boldsymbol{e}^{(k)}
, agent
k
captures the entire grand-coalition gain while every other agent’s participation constraint binds; when
W=0
, the simplex collapses to
\boldsymbol{0}
and the unique IR transfer leaves every agent at their reservation level.

The bijection
c_{i}=b_{i}-w_{i}
of Theorem
1
(
ii
) turns the welfare simplex into explicit transfer coordinates:
H_{\mathrm{IR}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})
is itself an
(n-1)
-dimensional simplex that can be written in closed form. The interior of the simplex corresponds to allocations in which every agent receives a strictly positive share.

Corollary 1
.

In the setting of Theorem
1
, suppose
W>0
. Then
H_{\mathrm{IR}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})
is an
(n-1)
-dimensional simplex with extreme points
\boldsymbol{c}^{(k)}:=\boldsymbol{b}-W\boldsymbol{e}^{(k)}
,
k=1,\dots,n
, and
H_{\mathrm{IR}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})=\operatorname{conv}\{\boldsymbol{c}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{c}^{(n)}\}
. The set of IR-efficient allocations in the deterministic-transfer class of
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}
is
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{0}+\boldsymbol{c}:\boldsymbol{c}\in H_{\mathrm{IR}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})\}=\operatorname{conv}\{\boldsymbol{X}_{0}+\boldsymbol{c}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{X}_{0}+\boldsymbol{c}^{(n)}\}
.

Once an efficient transfer class is fixed, the random part of every allocation in
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}+H
is determined by efficiency, and deterministic transfers choose a point in the welfare simplex.
The welfare simplex itself
\Delta_{W}
is uniquely determined from the characteristic function
\nu
, and so we refer to it as the IR-efficient set. However, when
\mathrm{Opt}(X)
contains multiple transfer classes, the set of possible transfers
\cup_{\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)_{\sim}}H_{\mathrm{IR}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})
is a union of simplices, one per transfer-equivalence class, and this union need not be convex. Because of this lack of convexity, the sequel often avoids this possible ambiguity by referring only to the IR-efficient set and constructing the transfers directly through the vertices of
\Delta_{W}
.

3.2
Core implementation and existence

The gain game
\nu
associates with each coalition its aggregate welfare gain, and we now inquire which splits of the grand-coalition gain
\nu([n])
are stable in the sense that no subgroup can improve by deviating. The classical answer is given by the core.

Definition 5

(Core)
.

The core of the TU game
([n],\nu)
is

\mathrm{Core}(\nu):=\left\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}=\nu([n]),\ \sum_{i\in A}w_{i}\geq\nu(A)\ \forall A\subseteq[n]\right\}.

If a proposed split lies outside the core, some coalition could “walk away” and do better on its own, so the arrangement is unstable. In risk sharing, core stability means that every subgroup receives at least as much benefit as it could achieve by pooling only among its own members, a strong yet natural participation constraint.

The next proposition records the basic correspondence: every core element is realized by a deterministic transfer on an efficient allocation, and the resulting allocation is IR.

Proposition 3
.

In the setting of Theorem
1
, the following hold:

(i)

every
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathrm{Core}(\nu)
satisfies
w_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in[n]
;

(ii)

for any
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathrm{Core}(\nu)
, the transfer

c_{i}:=b_{i}-w_{i},
for
i\in[n]
, belongs to
H_{\mathrm{IR}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})
, and the allocation
\boldsymbol{X}:=\boldsymbol{X}_{0}+\boldsymbol{c}
is feasible, efficient, and IR. Its welfare baselines equal
\boldsymbol{w}
, meaning
b_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})=w_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
.

By cash additivity, any core element
\boldsymbol{w}
translates into a transfer via

\boldsymbol{X}_{0}

We now turn to the conditions under which the core is nonempty. In general TU games, the Bondareva-Shapley Theorem states that the core is nonempty if and only if the game is balanced.

Definition 6
.

Let
([n],\nu)
be a TU game with
\nu(\varnothing)=0
.

(i)

The game
\nu
is

if for every
m\geq 1
, every
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\geq 0
, and every

\sum_{j:\,i\in A_{j}}\lambda_{j}=1\;\;\text{for all }i\in[n]\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}\nu(A_{j})\leq\nu([n]).

(ii)

The game
\nu
is

if for every nonempty coalition
B\subseteq[n]

\nu^{B}:2^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\nu^{B}(A):=\nu(A)
for subcoalitions
A\subseteq B
is balanced.

The first part of the next theorem is the well-known Bondareva-Shapley Theorem; see e.g.,
43

Theorem 2

(

.

Assume

\nu(\varnothing)=0

(i)

The core

(ii)

The core of the cost game in (

\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}C(A_{j})\geq C([n]).

(12)

The cost formulation (

Theorem 3
.

The cost game

The argument used in the proof of Theorem
3
adapts

Here, coalition costs are defined via inf-convolution of heterogeneous functionals, so the proof constructs a feasible grand-coalition allocation from optimal subcoalition allocations and controls its cost using only the convexity of each
\rho_{i}
,
i\in[n]
, without positive homogeneity.
3

3

In the capital-allocation setting, where a single convex risk measure evaluates the coalition aggregate directly,
60

Theorem
3
is important because it guarantees the existence of stable risk-sharing arrangements. When the core is empty, an efficient risk-sharing arrangement creates a positive aggregate surplus yet remains impossible to implement: some coalition could leave the grand coalition, reallocate internally, and obtain a larger welfare gain. In that case, the problem is not merely to choose a better price, as no deterministic transfer can make the proposed efficient allocation immune to all deviations across subgroups. Theorem
3
shows that the core is always nonempty for standard risk-averse preferences, and this obstruction disappears. This existence result also clarifies the role of the transfer rules studied in Section
3.3
and Appendix
C
. Once the core is known to be nonempty, different transfer rules can be compared as points in the welfare simplex, with the core distinguishing those that are robust to coalitional deviations. A rule that falls in the core implements efficient sharing in a way that is robust to coalitional deviations; a rule outside the core may still be individually rational and efficient, but it is vulnerable to subgroup objections. Total balancedness is stronger still: it guarantees the same nonemptiness property for every possible subcoalition, which is the prerequisite for the entry-monotonicity analysis in Section
4
.

3.3
Dual pricing allocation

It is now clear that the inf-convolution defining the cost
C
uniquely determines the welfare simplex
\Delta_{W}
and the core
\mathrm{Core}(\nu)
, and that the efficiency of a random allocation
\boldsymbol{X}\in\textrm{Opt}(X)
alone does not guarantee it is implementable. Although the risk-sharing literature has mostly focused on efficient random allocations, implementation also requires a well-specified and meaningful transfer rule. Appendix
C
collects several such rules; here we focus on the Arrow–Debreu state-price rule, which is central to the main results of the paper. This rule prices the original endowments with a common Arrow–Debreu state-price functional and uses the resulting cost shares as the target post-transfer evaluations. It is generated by a dual optimizer of the grand-coalition inf-convolution: the optimizer supports the representative-agent risk measure at the aggregate loss, and its linear part supplies the state prices used to charge each agent for its endowment.

This construction is more specific than an arbitrary supporting price, but more general than the lower-envelope distortion formula that motivates much of the distortion risk measure analysis in Appendix
B
. The aim is to isolate a pricing rule whose individual-rationality and core properties can be directly read off from dual feasibility and Fenchel inequalities, since the same object will later be selected, coalition by coalition, when we study stable expansion in Section
4
. When the individual risk measures are distortion risk measures, the intersection of the dual envelopes yields the envelope associated with lower-envelope distortion, and the construction specializes to the well-known lower-envelope Arrow–Debreu pricing rule, which we recall in Appendix
B
. For general convex risk measures, the same notation also keeps track of the penalty terms that replace zero-penalty coherent support.

The next proposition is the finite-coalition inf-convolution duality in
6
. Their statement is written for two convex risk measures on bounded positions; here, we formulate the result on
\mathcal{X}=L^{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and allow arbitrary finite coalitions. With the individual minimal penalties from (
2
), set

\alpha_{A}(\pi):=\sum_{i\in A}\alpha_{i}(\pi),\qquad\pi\in\mathcal{Z},

for every nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
; for the grand coalition, write
\alpha:=\alpha_{[n]}
.

Proposition 4
.

For every nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, the penalty function associated with
\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}
is
\alpha_{A}
, and

\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(Y)=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\mathbb{E}[\pi Y]-\alpha_{A}(\pi)\right\},\qquad Y\in\mathcal{X}.

The following assumption will sometimes be applied in addition to Assumption
1
.

Assumption 2
.

For every nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
and every
Y\in\mathcal{X}
, write
\Pi_{A}(Y):=\argmax_{\pi\in\mathcal{Z}}\{\mathbb{E}[\pi Y]-\alpha_{A}(\pi)\}
for the dual-optimizer set. Assume
\Pi_{A}(X_{A})\neq\varnothing
for every nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, and there exists a
\pi_{A}^{\star}\in\Pi_{A}(X_{A})
that is
\sigma(X_{A})

Theorem 4
.

Under Assumption
2
,
\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in[n]}\rho_{i}(Y)\geq\mathbb{E}[\pi^{\star}Y]-\alpha(\pi^{\star})
for every
Y\in\mathcal{X}
, with equality at
Y=X
. Moreover, for every
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
and every
i\in[n]

\rho_{i}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\pi^{\star}X_{i}]=-\alpha_{i}(\pi^{\star})=\inf_{Y\in\mathcal{X}}\{\rho_{i}(Y)-\mathbb{E}[\pi^{\star}Y]\}
, so that
X_{i}\in\argmin_{Y\in\mathcal{X}}\{\rho_{i}(Y)-\mathbb{E}[\pi^{\star}Y]\}
.

The condition
X_{i}\in\argmin_{Y\in\mathcal{X}}\{\rho_{i}(Y)-\mathbb{E}[\pi^{\star}Y]\}
states that the allocation solves the individual net-cost problem
\inf_{Y\in\mathcal{X}}\{\rho_{i}(Y)-\mathbb{E}[\pi^{\star}Y]\}
against this common price, which implies that Theorem
4
pins down the Arrow–Debreu pricing measure from a risk-trading equilibrium defined in the sense of
27
. However, one must be careful with this interpretation, as a given pure risk allocation
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
need not be affordable for some agent. Indeed, the set
\mathrm{Opt}(X)
can contain many pure risk allocations (from different transfer-equivalence classes in
\mathrm{Opt}(X)_{/\sim}
), and not every such allocation needs to be affordable for a given set of budget constraints. This problem cannot be tackled without explicitly modelling such constraints, and so one cannot claim that a particular tuple
(\boldsymbol{X},\pi^{\star})
necessarily constitutes an equilibrium.

Theorem
4
can still be understood as a full decentralization result, where the corresponding allocation would be obtained in an economy where the final positions can be afforded by all agents participating in the pool. More formally, there always exists an economy, potentially fictitious, such that
(\boldsymbol{X},\pi^{\star})
constitutes an equilibrium.

Theorem 5
.

Under Assumption
2

B\subseteq[n]

C_{i}^{\star}(B):=\mathbb{E}[\pi_{B}^{\star}\zeta_{i}]-\alpha_{i}(\pi_{B}^{\star})

(i)

\sum_{i\in A}C_{i}^{\star}(B)\leq C(A)
for every

(ii)

Equivalently,
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}(B)=(w_{i}^{\star}(B))_{i\in B}\in\mathrm{Core}(\nu^{B})
.

(iii)

For every
\boldsymbol{X}^{B}\in\mathrm{Opt}_{B}(X_{B})
, the deterministic transfers
c_{i}^{\star}(B):=C_{i}^{\star}(B)-\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{B})
,

c_{i}^{\star}(B)=\mathbb{E}[\pi_{B}^{\star}(\zeta_{i}-X_{i}^{B})]

Example 2

Consequently,

C_{i}^{\star}(A)=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}]+2\gamma_{A}\Cov(\zeta_{i},X_{A})-\frac{\gamma_{A}^{2}}{\gamma_{i}}\Var(X_{A}),\qquad i\in A.

Note that mean-variance is not monotone in general, meaning that
\pi_{A}^{\star}
can be signed. This implies that
\pi_{A}^{\star}
is not a probability measure but can still be used as a pricing measure; see also the discussion after Theorem
4
.

Example 3

(Entropic)
.

For each
i\in[n]
and loss
Y\in\mathcal{X}
with
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{Y/\iota}]<\infty
, let
\rho_{i}(Y)=\iota_{i}\log\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{Y/\iota_{i}}]
, where
\iota_{i}>0
. This functional is convex, cash additive, monotone, and law invariant. Its dual representation is

\rho_{i}(Y)=\sup_{\pi\geq 0,\ \mathbb{E}[\pi]=1}\left\{\mathbb{E}[\pi Y]-\iota_{i}\mathbb{E}[\pi\log\pi]\right\},\qquad i\in[n].

For a nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, the inf-convolution is again entropic:
\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(Y)=\iota_{A}\log\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{Y/\iota_{A}}]
, where
\iota_{A}:=\sum_{i\in A}\iota_{i}
. The aggregate penalty is
\alpha_{A}(\pi)=\iota_{A}\mathbb{E}[\pi\log\pi]
on
\{\pi\geq 0,\ \mathbb{E}[\pi]=1\}
, and the dual optimizer at
X_{A}
is
\pi_{A}^{\star}=\mathrm{e}^{X_{A}/\iota_{A}}/\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{X_{A}/\iota_{A}}]
. With
\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{A}^{\star}/\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}=\pi_{A}^{\star}
,

C_{i}^{\star}(A)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}_{A}^{\star}}[\zeta_{i}]-\frac{\iota_{i}}{\iota_{A}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}_{A}^{\star}}[X_{A}]+\iota_{i}\log\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{X_{A}/\iota_{A}}],\qquad i\in A.

4
Entry monotonicity

Stable expansion of risk-sharing pools requires transfer rules that guarantee adding new agents not only leads to a Pareto improvement but also yields a coalitionally stable allocation. This is the fundamental idea underlying
Population Monotonic Allocation Schemes
(PMAS), a refinement of the notion of core due to
57
.

Since each incumbent’s standalone cost
\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})
is fixed, entry monotonicity can be checked equivalently on evaluated costs: incumbents must be assigned weakly lower costs in larger coalitions. We therefore formulate the PMAS problem using coalition costs
C(A)=\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(X_{A})
and coalition-wise cost shares for
A\subseteq[n]
. For clarity, the first three subsections emphasize formalism, and we defer applications to the last subsection.

4.1
PMAS and cost-based entry monotonicity

For a coalition-wise rule
R
, write
C_{i}^{R}(A)
for the evaluated cost assigned to incumbent
i\in A
when coalition
A
forms, and write
w_{i}^{R}(A):=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-C_{i}^{R}(A)
for the corresponding welfare-gain share. For an efficient transfer rule, we have

\sum_{i\in A}C_{i}^{R}(A)=C(A),\qquad A\subseteq[n],

or equivalently
\sum_{i\in A}w_{i}^{R}(A)=\nu(A)
.

Definition 7

(Entry monotonicity)
.

A coalition-wise rule
R
is
entry-monotone
if
w_{i}^{R}(B)\geq w_{i}^{R}(A)
whenever
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
and
i\in A
. Equivalently, incumbent evaluated costs are antitone under inclusion:
C_{i}^{R}(B)\leq C_{i}^{R}(A)
whenever
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
and
i\in A
.

Definition 8

(PMAS)
.

A coalition-wise rule
R
is a
Population Monotonic Allocation Scheme
(PMAS) for the gain game
\nu
if, for every nonempty coalition
B\subseteq[n]
,

(i)

\boldsymbol{w}^{R}(B)\in\mathrm{Core}(\nu^{B})
, and

(ii)

w_{i}^{R}(B)\geq w_{i}^{R}(A)
whenever
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
and
i\in A
.

It is clear that the game needs to be totally balanced for a PMAS to exist.
57
originally provided the next two results. The first records the relation between entry monotonicity and core stability for efficient coalition-wise rules.

Proposition 5
.

Suppose a coalition-wise rule
R
is efficient on every coalition. If
R
is entry-monotone, then
\boldsymbol{w}^{R}(B)\in\mathrm{Core}(\nu^{B})
for every nonempty coalition
B\subseteq[n]
.

The author also shows that convex games always guarantee the existence of PMAS, where convexity (supermodularity) means that

\nu(A)+\nu(B)\leq\nu(A\cup B)+\nu(A\cap B),\qquad A,B\subseteq[n].

Proposition 6
.

If the gain game
\nu
is convex, then it admits a PMAS. In particular, the coalition-wise Shapley scheme is a PMAS.

In practice, risk-sharing games are rarely convex. We are therefore interested in providing alternative sufficient conditions for the existence of PMAS. The next proposition provides a cost criterion that will be used in the rest of the section.

Proposition 7
.

For each nonempty coalition
B\subseteq[n]
, let
\boldsymbol{C}^{R}(B)=(C_{i}^{R}(B))_{i\in B}
be a cost-core selector for the restricted cost game on
B
, so that

\sum_{i\in B}C_{i}^{R}(B)=C(B),\qquad\sum_{i\in A}C_{i}^{R}(B)\leq C(A),\quad A\subseteq B.

If
C_{i}^{R}(B)\leq C_{i}^{R}(A)
whenever
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
and
i\in A
, then the welfare gains
w_{i}^{R}(A)=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-C_{i}^{R}(A)
is a PMAS of
\nu
.

The rest of this section focuses on two surplus allocation rules that share the desirable property of being implementable at pool formation without full knowledge of potential participants from pool extensions. Generally, a PMAS is a selection on the coalition lattice, and such a selection typically requires full knowledge of the coalition lattice. However, if one can find “procedural” rules in the sense that they can be directly applied at entry without knowledge of the potential other pool configurations, then this procedure is essentially a myopic policy like one finds in one-step-ahead decision processes (dynamic programming).

The first allocation rule we analyze is the proportional-cost allocation rule. It requires fixing a set of weights to the agent, and attributing surplus in proportion to these weights. When these weights are chosen procedurally, then the allocation is myopic. Examples include uniform weights or the expected risk of Theorem
8
. The second surplus allocation rule we analyze is the Arrow–Debreu pricing; clearly, it is purely procedural and thus myopic.

4.2
Proportional-cost allocations

We now apply the PMAS criterion to concrete transfer rules. The first family splits each coalition’s cost in fixed proportions, providing a simple benchmark that is useful in practice.

Definition 9

(Proportional-cost allocation)
.

Fix weights
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
such that
\xi_{i}>0
for
i\in[n]
and set
\xi_{A}:=\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}
. The proportional-cost allocation assigns

C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A):=\xi_{i}\frac{C(A)}{\xi_{A}},\qquad i\in A.

(13)

This definition is purely cooperative and depends only on coalition costs and the chosen weights.

Theorem 6
.

The coalition-wise proportional-cost allocation (
13
) is a PMAS if and only if normalized coalition costs are antitone:

\frac{C(B)}{\xi_{B}}\leq\frac{C(A)}{\xi_{A}},\qquad\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n].

(14)

PMAS conditions are usually difficult to check directly, and only a small number of tractable constructions are known. Proposition
4
turns the proportional-cost condition (
14
) into a comparison of normalized coalition evaluators, which gives a sufficient condition that can be verified in concrete risk-sharing models. For each nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, define
R_{A}^{\xi}:\mathcal{X}\to(-\infty,\infty]
by

R_{A}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(Y):=\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(\xi_{A}Y)/\xi_{A}.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
4
, we have

R_{A}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(Y)=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\mathbb{E}[\pi Y]-\frac{\alpha_{A}(\pi)}{\xi_{A}}\right\},

and thus
C(A)/\xi_{A}=R_{A}^{\xi}(X_{A}/\xi_{A})
.

Because
R_{A}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is a positive multiple of
\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}
, which is convex, lower semicontinuous, and law invariant (Proposition
2
), it is automatically
convex-order consistent
:
Y\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}Z
implies
R_{A}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(Y)\leq R_{A}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(Z)
, since law-invariant convex functionals are consistent with the convex order
(
8
, Theorem 4.3)
. The next theorem uses this property to compare normalized coalition costs.

Theorem 7
.

Suppose that for every
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
, the normalized losses satisfy
X_{B}/\xi_{B}\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X_{A}/\xi_{A}
and
R_{B}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(X_{A}/\xi_{A})\leq R_{A}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(X_{A}/\xi_{A})
.
Then, normalized coalition costs are antitone, and the proportional-cost allocation (
13
) is a PMAS.

A simple sufficient condition for
R_{B}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\leq R_{A}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
,
A\subseteq B
, is the normalized penalty order

\frac{\alpha_{B}(\pi)}{\xi_{B}}\geq\frac{\alpha_{A}(\pi)}{\xi_{A}},\qquad\pi\in\mathcal{Z}.

This specializes cleanly in the three running examples.

•

Coherent risk measures
(Example
1
):
\alpha_{A}=\delta_{\mathcal{D}_{A}}
with
\mathcal{D}_{A}=\cap_{i\in A}\mathcal{D}_{i}
, so
\mathcal{D}_{B}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{A}
whenever
A\subseteq B
and the normalized penalty order holds automatically for any weights
\xi
.

•

Mean–variance
(Example
2
): the order reduces to
\xi_{B}\gamma_{B}\leq\xi_{A}\gamma_{A}
, which holds across the coalition lattice exactly when
\xi_{i}\gamma_{i}
is constant in
i\in[n]
, that is,
\gamma_{i}=c/\xi_{i}
for some
c>0
.

•

Entropic
(Example
3
): the order reduces to
\iota_{B}/\xi_{B}\geq\iota_{A}/\xi_{A}
, which holds across the coalition lattice exactly when
\iota_{i}/\xi_{i}
is constant in
i\in[n]
, that is,
\iota_{i}=c\xi_{i}
for some
c>0
.

This controls the order of evaluators, not the arguments being evaluated. The remaining primitive condition is then the convex-order comparison of the normalized aggregate risks,
X_{B}/\xi_{B}\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X_{A}/\xi_{A}
.

Convex-order comparisons require equal means, so the relevant proportional weights are effectively mean weights, up to a common scale. Indeed, for
B=A\cup\{i\}
with
i\notin A
, the equal-means condition
\mathbb{E}[X_{B}/\xi_{B}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{A}/\xi_{A}]=c
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
gives
\mu_{B}/\xi_{B}=\mu_{A}/\xi_{A}=c
, hence
\xi_{i}=\xi_{B}-\xi_{A}=\mu_{i}/c
, so
\xi_{i}
is proportional to
\mu_{i}
. Multiplying all weights by the same constant does not change the allocation, so that we may take
\xi_{i}=\mu_{i}:=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}]
for
i\in[n]
.

Assume now that
\mu_{i}>0
for every
i\in[n]
. Write
\mu_{A}:=\sum_{i\in A}\mu_{i}
,
\bar{X}_{A}:=X_{A}/\mu_{A}
, and
\bar{C}(A):=C(A)/\mu_{A}
.
The mean-proportional allocation is the proportional-cost allocation with
\xi_{i}=\mu_{i}
:

C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(A):=\mu_{i}\frac{C(A)}{\mu_{A}},\qquad i\in A.

(15)

Theorem 8
.

Suppose that, for every
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
,

\bar{X}_{B}\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}\bar{X}_{A},

(16)

and that
R_{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\bar{X}_{A})\leq R_{A}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\bar{X}_{A})
.
Then
\bar{C}(B)\leq\bar{C}(A)
for all
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
, and the mean-proportional allocation (
15
) is a PMAS.

Next, we turn to allocations spanned by a pricing measure.

4.3
Dual-pricing allocations and conditional-mean criteria

The proportional and mean-proportional allocations are cooperative cost-sharing rules. We now return to pricing. Under Assumption
2
, the associated dual-pricing cost share is

C_{i}^{\star}(A):=\mathbb{E}[\pi_{A}^{\star}\zeta_{i}]-\alpha_{i}(\pi_{A}^{\star}),\qquad i\in A.

(17)

Proposition 8
.

Under Assumption
2
,
\boldsymbol{C}^{\star}(A)
is a cost-core selector for every nonempty
A\subseteq[n]
. If, in addition,
C_{i}^{\star}(B)\leq C_{i}^{\star}(A)
whenever
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
and
i\in A
, then the welfare gains
w_{i}^{\star}(A)=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-C_{i}^{\star}(A)
,
i\in A
, is a PMAS.

In the coherent-risk-measure setting of Example
1
, (
17
) becomes
C_{i}^{\star}(A)=\mathbb{E}[\pi_{A}^{\star}\zeta_{i}]
for
i\in A
, with
\pi_{A}^{\star}\in\argmax_{\pi\in\cap_{j\in A}\mathcal{D}_{j}}\mathbb{E}[\pi X_{A}]
. For coherent distortion risk measures, Appendix
B
identifies this rule with lower-envelope pricing
\mathbb{Q}_{A}^{\star}
so that
C_{i}^{\star}(A)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}_{A}^{\star}}[\zeta_{i}]
.

The next results give sufficient conditions for this antitonicity condition. For every nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
and every
i\in A
, define the conditional-mean random variable

m_{i,A}(X_{A}):=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}\mid X_{A}]
. For a selected dual optimizer, define
\Gamma_{i,A}^{\star}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\Gamma_{i,A}^{\star}(Y):=\mathbb{E}[\pi_{A}^{\star}Y]-\alpha_{i}(\pi_{A}^{\star}).

The evaluator
\Gamma_{i,A}^{\star}
is affine in
Y
, but its intercept depends on the individual penalty of agent
i\in A
at the coalition optimizer
\pi_{A}^{\star}
.

Theorem 9
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds and that the selected dual prices are cross-coalition compatible in the sense that
\Gamma_{i,B}^{\star}(m_{i,B}(X_{B}))\leq\Gamma_{i,A}^{\star}(m_{i,A}(X_{A}))
for every
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
and every
i\in A
. Then the dual-pricing welfare gains
w_{i}^{\star}(A)=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-C_{i}^{\star}(A)
are a PMAS.

The compatibility condition is a price condition on the relevant conditional-mean variables. One sufficient way to verify it is

\mathbb{E}[\pi_{B}^{\star}m_{i,B}(X_{B})]\leq\mathbb{E}[\pi_{A}^{\star}m_{i,A}(X_{A})]\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{i}(\pi_{B}^{\star})\geq\alpha_{i}(\pi_{A}^{\star}).

In coherent models, the penalty inequality is automatic on the feasible dual envelopes.

The next identity gives a density-level way to inspect the conditional-mean components.

Proposition 9
.

Fix a nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
and
i\in A
. Assume
\zeta_{i}
is integrable and define the finite signed allocation measure

\eta_{i,A}(D):=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{A}\in D\}}],\qquad D\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}).

Suppose
X_{A}
has density
f_{A}>0
and
\eta_{i,A}
has density
\varsigma_{i,A}
. Then
m_{i,A}(s)=\varsigma_{i,A}(s)/f_{A}(s)
for
f_{A}
-a.e.
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Consequently,
m_{i,A}
has a nondecreasing version whenever
\varsigma_{i,A}/f_{A}
is nondecreasing.

Conditional-mean independence gives a primitive condition for convex-order contraction.

Proposition 10
.

Fix nonempty coalitions
A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
and
i\in A
. Write
Y:=X_{B\setminus A}
. If

\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}\mid X_{A},Y]=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}\mid X_{A}]
, then
m_{i,B}(X_{B})\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}m_{i,A}(X_{A})
.

For the dual-pricing rule, PMAS verification reduces to cross-coalition compatibility of the priced conditional-mean components. The next corollary records a useful convex-order contraction of conditional means.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose
\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n}
are independent and integrable. Then
m_{i,B}(X_{B})\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}m_{i,A}(X_{A})
for every
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
and every
i\in A
.

For general convex risk measures, proportional conditional means alone do not ensure that dual pricing yields mean-proportional cost sharing, because the individual penalty terms need not split in mean proportion. The missing condition is a proportional split of the selected dual penalty. The next theorem states the resulting identification.

Theorem 10
.

Assume that
\mu_{i}>0
for every
i\in[n]
, and that Assumption
2
holds. If, for every nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
and every
i\in A
,

\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}\mid X_{A}]=\frac{\mu_{i}}{\mu_{A}}X_{A}

(18)

and

\alpha_{i}(\pi_{A}^{\star})=(\mu_{i}/\mu_{A})\alpha_{A}(\pi_{A}^{\star}),

then the dual pricing and the mean-proportional allocation coincide and are a PMAS.

The penalty-splitting condition is automatic in coherent models.

Example 4

(Coherent specialization)
.

Suppose that, for each
i\in[n]
,
\rho_{i}
is coherent with dual envelope
\mathcal{D}_{i}
. For every nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, the individual penalties are indicators and vanish on
\cap_{j\in A}\mathcal{D}_{j}
, so the dual-pricing allocation in (
17
) is

C_{i}^{\star}(A)=\mathbb{E}[\pi_{A}^{\star}\zeta_{i}],\qquad\pi_{A}^{\star}\in\argmax_{\pi\in\cap_{j\in A}\mathcal{D}_{j}}\mathbb{E}[\pi X_{A}],\quad i\in A.

Theorem
10
identifies this allocation with the mean-proportional allocation. Appendix
B
gives the PMAS obtained from dual pricing to distortion risk measures.

Following
10
, the condition (
18
) can be verified from Laplace transforms rather than conditional densities. Fix a nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
and
i\in A
. Assume
\zeta_{i}\in L^{1}
,
X_{A}\in L^{1}
, and
\mu_{A}\neq 0
. Define

\mathcal{L}_{A}(t):=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{-tX_{A}}],\qquad\mathcal{L}_{i,A}^{\ast}(t):=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}\mathrm{e}^{-tX_{A}}],

and define the finite signed measures
\eta(D):=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{A}\in D\}}]
and
\beta(D):=\mathbb{E}[X_{A}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{A}\in D\}}]
for Borel sets
D\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
. Assume the Laplace–Stieltjes transforms of
\eta
and
\beta
are finite on an interval
t\in(0,\varepsilon)
for some
\varepsilon>0
, that these transforms uniquely determine the finite signed measures, and that differentiation under the expectation gives
-\mathcal{L}_{A}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbb{E}[X_{A}\mathrm{e}^{-tX_{A}}]
on this interval.

Proposition 11
.

If
\mathcal{L}_{i,A}^{\ast}(t)=(\mu_{i}/\mu_{A})(-\mathcal{L}_{A}^{\prime}(t))
for
t\in(0,\varepsilon)
for some
\varepsilon>0
, then (
18
) holds for this coalition
A
and agent
i
.

Theorem
10
explains why proportional-regression models are useful: the mean-proportional PMAS becomes a pricing PMAS. Outside these cases, mean-proportional allocations remain a cooperative solution rather than an equilibrium concept.

4.4
PMAS special cases

The preceding results give abstract conditions for PMAS. We now record special cases where those conditions can be verified, with an emphasis on dependence structures that arise in institutional risk-sharing pools. The first results use the dual-pricing allocation from Section
4.3
. In other words, these conditions allow for myopic extensions of the pool. If some original pool satisfies the conditions, any new agent that also satisfies them can join, but the original pool members need not know the exact information about the new members at the pool’s inception; they only need to agree on a surplus allocation rule.

We begin with the case of independent endowments. Independence describes many routine insurance and lending settings, including geographically diversified motor or property portfolios, multi-line insurance books, and loan portfolios whose cross-exposures have been hedged or sold down. In this case, Corollary
2
supplies a useful convex-order contraction of conditional means.

Corollary 3
.

Suppose the endowments
\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n}
are independent and integrable and that Assumption
2
holds. If the selected dual prices satisfy the cross-coalition compatibility condition in Theorem
9
, then the dual-pricing allocation is a PMAS.

The next case turns the same idea into a closed-form regression condition. Elliptical distributions extend the Gaussian framework to symmetric heavy tails and are widely used in quantitative risk management, including the multivariate-
t
models that underlie market-risk capital aggregation, credit risk modelling and when determining the solvency capital requirement in Solvency II
(
45
)
. In the elliptical family, the conditional mean
\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}\mid X_{A}]
is affine in
X_{A}
for every nonempty
A\subseteq[n]
and every
i\in A
, so the dual-pricing comparison reduces to a scalar comparison of priced aggregate deviations and individual penalties.

Corollary 4
.

Suppose Assumption
2
and that
\boldsymbol{\zeta}=(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n})
is elliptically distributed with mean vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, positive-definite covariance matrix
\Sigma
, and finite second moments. For every nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, set
\mu_{A}:=\mathbb{E}[X_{A}]
,
\sigma_{A}^{2}:=\Var(X_{A})
, and
\beta_{i,A}:=\Cov(\zeta_{i},X_{A})/\Var(X_{A})
for
i\in A
. Then

C_{i}^{\star}(A)=\mu_{i}+\beta_{i,A}\left(\mathbb{E}[\pi_{A}^{\star}X_{A}]-\mu_{A}\right)-\alpha_{i}(\pi_{A}^{\star}),\qquad i\in A.

If, for every
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
and every
i\in A
,

\beta_{i,B}\left(\mathbb{E}[\pi_{B}^{\star}X_{B}]-\mu_{B}\right)-\alpha_{i}(\pi_{B}^{\star})\leq\beta_{i,A}\left(\mathbb{E}[\pi_{A}^{\star}X_{A}]-\mu_{A}\right)-\alpha_{i}(\pi_{A}^{\star}),

then the dual-pricing allocation is a PMAS.

The next corollary is useful for risk sharing among homogeneous pools. When
(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n})
is an exchangeable random vector, the conditional means are equal for every participant, which means that the equal-weight proportional-cost share coincides with each participant’s dual-pricing share.

Corollary 5
.

Suppose Assumption
2
, that
(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n})
is exchangeable and integrable, and that
\alpha_{i}(\pi_{A}^{\star})=|A|^{-1}\alpha_{A}(\pi_{A}^{\star})
for every nonempty
A\subseteq[n]
and every
i\in A
. Then
\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}\mid X_{A}]=X_{A}/|A|
for every nonempty
A\subseteq[n]
and every
i\in A
, and the normalized convex-order condition (
16
) holds. The dual-pricing allocation coincides with the mean-proportional allocation, and it is a PMAS.

The penalty-split hypothesis is automatic in the two leading cases: when the risk measures are coherent, the individual penalties vanish at any feasible dual optimizer; and when all agents share the same convex risk measure, the individual penalties are equal, so each equals
|A|^{-1}\alpha_{A}(\pi_{A}^{\star})
.

Consider the situation in which
(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n})
is a vector of comonotonic risks, so there is no diversification benefit. This worst-case dependence scenario is relevant for catastrophe, pandemic, and other systemic-risk insurance pools, where individual losses move together, and stable expansion must rely on the entry of additional risk bearers, such as reinsurers, rather than on diversification. Coalition expansion may still lower the distortion used to evaluate shared losses, and this risk-capacity effect alone produces entry monotonicity. As the set of participants grows, the coalition’s willingness to bear each layer of risk increases.

Corollary 6
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds, that
(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n})
are comonotonic, and suppose
\rho_{i}(0)=0
for every
i\in[n]
. Assume that each coalition inf-convolution is comonotonic additive on the cone generated by the endowments, in the sense that, for every pair of nonempty coalitions
A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
,

\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{j\in B}\rho_{j}(X_{A})=\sum_{i\in A}\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{j\in B}\rho_{j}(\zeta_{i}).

(19)

For each nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
and every
i\in A
, define
w_{i}(A):=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{j\in A}\rho_{j}(\zeta_{i})
. Then
(\boldsymbol{w}(A))_{A\subseteq[n]}
is a PMAS.

Note that comonotonic additivity of the inf-convolution in (
19
) holds for distortion risk measures from Appendix
B
, which includes the most relevant risk measures in practice.

We collect further examples of conditions for PMAS in Appendix
D
. Among others, we provide PMAS conditions for risk-sharing with mean–variance and entropic risk measures; proportional conditional-mean examples based on convolution semigroups; Dirichlet–Liouville radial risks; conditional-convolution mixtures; and an example showing that a mean-proportional cost can be PMAS without coinciding with Arrow–Debreu pricing as in Theorem
10
.

5
Conclusion

We analyze how efficient risk sharing under cash-additive evaluations can be implemented in practice. Computing efficient allocations through inf-convolution is only half the problem: without a rule that determines deterministic transfers, the resulting allocation is not implementable, and the exercise has little practical content. Cash additivity makes the distributional step explicit and casts the problem as a transferable-utility game, allowing us to draw directly on the cooperative game literature. We immediately obtain (Theorem
1
) that individually rational transfers form a polyhedron in each efficient transfer class, which is homeomorphic to the welfare simplex.

We then show that the gain game is totally balanced whenever the agents are risk averse (Theorem
3
), so every coalition has a nonempty core and stable transfers always exist. Total balancedness is a necessary condition for the existence of PMAS, and the aforementioned result motivates our analysis of coalition expansion.

Since risk-sharing games are rarely convex, we aim to provide novel sufficient conditions to guarantee the existence of a PMAS. We focus on two allocation rules, the proportional-cost allocation rule and the (dual-)pricing allocation rule. Both rules have the advantage of being myopic, in that they do not require full knowledge of all potential future participants at inception. The guiding principle is that proportional-cost allocations are PMAS when normalized coalition risks decrease in the convex order (Theorem
7
), and that pricing allocations are PMAS under cross-coalition compatibility of the priced conditional means (Theorem
9
). These novel PMAS conditions are often satisfied in many real-world applications, such as independent risks (Corollary
3
), elliptical risks (Corollary
4
), and comonotonic risks (Corollary
6
).

Proposition 12
.

Let
A\subseteq[n]
be a nonempty coalition. For each
i\in A
, let

be a concave distortion, and let
h_{A}:=\min_{i\in A}h_{i}

\rho_{h}(Y)=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}_{h}}\mathbb{E}[\pi Y]
for every concave distortion

\mathcal{D}_{h_{A}}=\bigcap_{i\in A}\mathcal{D}_{h_{i}}

C(A)=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}_{h_{A}}}\mathbb{E}[\pi X_{A}]

Proof.

The first assertion is the dual representation of coherent distortion risk measures. For the envelope identity, the constraints
\mathbb{E}[\pi\mathbbm{1}_{E}]\leq h_{i}(\mathbb{P}(E))

for every

∎

Thus, the lower-envelope distortion determines both sides of the construction: it gives the primal efficient allocation in Theorem

3.3

Theorem 12
.

Let
\rho_{i}=\rho_{h_{i}}

(ii)

\sum_{i\in A}C_{i}^{\star}(A)=C(A),\qquad\sum_{i\in S}C_{i}^{\star}(A)\leq C(S),\quad\varnothing\neq S\subseteq A.

(iii)

For every

12
.

By Proposition

We note that

Appendix C
Other transfer rules

Section
3
defines the risk-sharing game and reduces implementation of efficient risk sharing to selecting a welfare-gain split (equivalently, a deterministic transfer) in the welfare simplex, subject to individual-rationality and core constraints. Each such selection defines a transfer rule, a function that maps agent characteristics to balanced-budget transfers on an optimal allocation. This appendix derives the implementation, individual-rationality, and core-stability properties of other canonical transfer rules in the literature, and records counterexamples showing that none of these rules is a PMAS in general.

C.1
Proportional-cost transfer rules

Section
4.2
defines the proportional-cost rule and gives a condition under which it is a PMAS. Here, we record its fixed-coalition implementation, IR criterion, and core criterion. If
\boldsymbol{X}^{A}=(X_{i})_{i\in A}
is an efficient allocation for the restricted coalition problem, the deterministic transfer that implements the target evaluated costs in (
13
) is

c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A):=C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)-\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{A}),\qquad i\in A.

(25)

The normalized object in (
13
) is the cost per unit of
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, not cost per capita unless
\xi_{i}\equiv 1
for each
i\in[n]
.

The proportional-cost rule equalizes cost per unit of the chosen weight: every member
i\in A
of a coalition
A\subseteq[n]
pays
C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)
and obtains welfare
w_{i}^{\xi}(A)=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)
, so costs split proportionally to
\xi
while welfare gains favour agents whose autarky cost per unit of weight,
\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})/\xi_{i}
, most exceeds the coalition rate
C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)/\xi_{i}
. Choosing
\xi_{i}\equiv 1
gives an egalitarian per-capita cost split,
\xi_{i}=\mu_{i}
allocates by expected loss,
\xi_{i}=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})
implements a pay-as-you-stand split under which welfare gains are proportional to autarky cost, and a contractual exposure measure for
\xi_{i}
allocates by that exposure.

Theorem 14
.

Fix a nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
and let
\boldsymbol{X}^{A}\in\mathrm{Opt}_{A}(X_{A})
. Define
C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)
and
c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)
by (
13
) and (
25
), for
i\in A
. Then:

(i)

\sum_{i\in A}c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)=0
, so the transfer is budget-balanced.

(ii)

The transferred allocation
\boldsymbol{X}^{A}+\boldsymbol{c}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)
is efficient and satisfies
\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{A}+c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A))=C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)
for every
i\in A
.

(iii)

(C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A))_{i\in A}
is the unique allocation in the deterministic-transfer class
\boldsymbol{X}^{A}+H_{A}
, where
H_{A}:=\{\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{A}:\sum_{i\in A}d_{i}=0\}
, whose evaluated costs are proportional to
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.

(iv)

It is individually rational for coalition
A
if and only if
C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)\leq\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})
for every
i\in A
.

Proof.

Since
\boldsymbol{X}^{A}
is efficient,
\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{A})=C(A)
. Hence

\sum_{i\in A}c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)=\sum_{i\in A}C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)-\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{A})=C(A)-C(A)=0.

Cash additivity gives
\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{A}+c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A))=\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{A})+c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)=C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)
. Deterministic balanced transfers preserve the aggregate loss and the objective value, so the transferred allocation remains efficient.

For uniqueness, suppose
\boldsymbol{d}\in H_{A}
and the evaluated costs of
\boldsymbol{X}^{A}+\boldsymbol{d}
are proportional to some constant
\xi>0
, say
\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{A}+d_{i})=a\xi
for all
i\in A
. Summing over
A
and using efficiency gives
a\xi=C(A)
, so
a=C(A)/\xi
and
d_{i}=C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)-\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{A})=c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A)
. Finally, IR is exactly
\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{A}+c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(A))\leq\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})
for every
i\in A
, which is the stated condition.
∎

The proportional-cost IR criterion depends only on
C([n])
and the weights
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, not on the representative efficient random allocation within a deterministic-transfer class. This contrasts with other pricing rules, such as actuarial fairness, where IR can depend on the risk premia of the chosen efficient allocation.

Theorem 15
.

Fix a nonempty coalition
B\subseteq[n]
. The proportional-cost welfare-gain vector
\boldsymbol{w}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(B)
belongs to
\mathrm{Core}(\nu^{B})
if and only if

\frac{C(B)}{\xi_{B}}\leq\frac{C(A)}{\xi_{A}},\qquad\forall\,\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B.

(26)

Equivalently,
\bar{C}_{\xi}(B)\leq\bar{C}_{\xi}(A)
for every nonempty
A\subseteq B
.

Proof.

Efficiency holds because
\sum_{i\in B}w_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(B)=\sum_{i\in B}\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-C(B)=\nu(B)
. For a nonempty
A\subseteq B
, the gain-core inequality is

\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-\xi_{A}\frac{C(B)}{\xi_{B}}\geq\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-C(A).

Cancelling the stand-alone terms gives
\xi_{A}C(B)/\xi_{B}\leq C(A)
, which is (
26
). This is the same as the cost-core inequality
\sum_{i\in A}C_{i}^{\xi}(B)\leq C(A)
. The singleton cases recover the IR conditions because
C(\{i\})=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})
.
∎

C.2
Expected-value pricing transfers

The simplest way to specify a transfer rule is to price each agent’s position under a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}
, where
\mathbb{P}
is the physical measure from Section
2.1
. Given any such
\mathbb{Q}
, the
\mathbb{Q}
-expected-value transfer rule maps agent characteristics to the balanced-budget transfer
c_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}:=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\zeta_{i}]-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{i}]
. Note that
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
is the Arrow–Debreu pricing measure in
12
, which is one possible
\mathbb{Q}
-pricing transfer rule. The next proposition shows that IR reduces to an agent-wise comparison of
\mathbb{Q}
-risk premia.

Proposition 13
.

Fix an efficient allocation
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
and let
\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}
be any probability measure such that
\zeta_{i},X_{i}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{Q})
for each
i\in[n]
. Define

c_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}:=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\zeta_{i}]-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{i}],\qquad i\in[n].

(27)

Then
\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathbb{Q}}\in H
, and
\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathbb{Q}}
is IR if and only if

\rho_{i}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{i}]\leq\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\zeta_{i}],\qquad\forall\,i\in[n].

(28)

Proof of Proposition
13
.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}=X
, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X]-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X]=0
. By cash additivity,

\rho_{i}(X_{i}+c_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}})=\rho_{i}(X_{i})+c_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}=\rho_{i}(X_{i})+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\zeta_{i}]-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{i}].

Thus
\rho_{i}(X_{i}+c_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}})\leq\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})
is equivalent to (
28
). ∎

The coalitional analogue reduces core membership to an inequality for each sub-coalition.

Proposition 14
.

Fix an efficient allocation
\boldsymbol{X}^{\star}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
and let
\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}
be such that
\zeta_{i},X_{i}^{\star}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{Q})
for each
i\in[n]
. Define

c_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}:=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\zeta_{i}]-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{i}^{\star}],\qquad w_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}:=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{\star}+c_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}),\qquad i\in[n],

and, for each coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, write
X_{A}:=\sum_{i\in A}\zeta_{i}
and
X_{A}^{\star}:=\sum_{i\in A}X_{i}^{\star}
.
Then the welfare-gain vector
\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathbb{Q}}
belongs to
\mathrm{Core}(\nu)
if and only if

C(A)-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{A}]\;\geq\;\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{\star})-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{A}^{\star}],\qquad\forall A\subseteq[n].

(29)

Equivalently,

\sum_{i\in A}\Big(\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{\star})-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{i}^{\star}]\Big)\;\leq\;C(A)-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{A}],\qquad\forall A\subseteq[n].

(30)

Proof of Proposition
14
.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}=0
and
\boldsymbol{X}^{\star}
is efficient,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}=\nu([n])
. Cash additivity gives
w_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{\star})-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\zeta_{i}]+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{i}^{\star}]
for
i\in[n]
. Summing over
i\in A
and substituting
\nu(A)=\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-C(A)
, the core condition
\sum_{i\in A}w_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}\geq\nu(A)
reduces to (
29
) after cancelling
\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})
. Linearity of
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}
gives (
30
).
∎

For any
\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}
, the
\mathbb{Q}
-risk premium
\Pi_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}(Y):=\rho_{i}(Y)-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[Y]
is cash-invariant for each
i\in[n]
. It follows that IR and core membership of a
\mathbb{Q}
-expected-value transfer rule are invariant within a fixed deterministic-transfer class, and Proposition
13
shows that the transfer vector is unique once
\mathbb{Q}
and the representative class
\boldsymbol{X}+H
are fixed. Nonuniqueness enters across transfer classes. Within the lower-envelope comonotonic family from Theorem
11
, ties in the lower envelope generate multiple transfer-equivalence classes: when
|M_{x}|>1
on a set of positive
\mu_{X}
-measure, the equal-split rule (
22
) is only one admissible assignment of marginal layers to minimizers. Any tie-consistent reassignment of these layers produces a different random allocation in
\mathbb{A}_{n}^{+}(X)
and hence a different element of
\mathrm{Opt}(X)_{/\sim}
, while each such class shares the same aggregate cost
\rho_{h_{\wedge}}(X)
. The lower-envelope Arrow–Debreu pricing measure
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
is still determined by the aggregate loss and the lower-envelope distortion, not by the tie-breaking allocation. What may differ across tied efficient classes is the induced expected-value transfer, since
c_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\zeta_{i}]-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{i}]
depends on the random allocation; for
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
, the welfare-gain vector remains class-independent because Theorem
12
(
iii
) gives
\Pi_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\star}}(X_{i})=0
for every
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
and every
i\in[n]
.

C.3
Actuarial fairness: transfers under the physical measure

Actuarial fairness is the special case when
\mathbb{Q}
is the physical measure
\mathbb{P}
, using a risk-neutral price. The actuarial-fairness rule makes each agent pay a premium equal to the expected value of their allocated loss, preserving expected losses agent by agent. While canonical under risk-neutral evaluation, this rule can conflict with IR when agents use nonlinear functionals. We provide a criterion for compatibility in terms of each agent’s risk premium.

Definition 10

(Actuarial fairness)
.

An allocation
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(X)
is actuarially fair relative to
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
if

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}],\qquad\forall i\in[n].

(31)

Definition 11

(Risk premium)
.

For each
i\in[n]
, define the risk premium functional
\Pi_{i}(Y):=\rho_{i}(Y)-\mathbb{E}[Y]
, for
Y\in\mathcal{X}
.
Cash additivity implies
\Pi_{i}(Y+c)=\Pi_{i}(Y)
for every
i\in[n]
,
Y\in\mathcal{X}
, and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Once an efficient allocation
\boldsymbol{X}
is fixed, actuarial fairness leads to a unique transfer vector within
\boldsymbol{X}+H
. Individual rationality then reduces entirely to comparing risk premia before and after reallocation.

Theorem 16
.

Fix an efficient allocation
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
. Define the actuarially fair transfer
c_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}:=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]
for
i\in[n],

and set
\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathrm{AF}}:=\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathrm{AF}}
.
Then:

(i)

\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathrm{AF}}\in H
and
\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathrm{AF}}
is the unique efficient, actuarially fair allocation in the deterministic-transfer class
\boldsymbol{X}+H
.

(ii)

\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathrm{AF}}
is individually rational if and only if

\Pi_{i}(X_{i})\leq\Pi_{i}(\zeta_{i}),\qquad\forall i\in[n].

(32)

Proof of Theorem
16
.

Part (
i
): since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}=X
, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}=0
, hence
\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathrm{AF}}\in H
. By cash additivity and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}=0
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i})=C([n])
, so
\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathrm{AF}}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
, and
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}]=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}]
by construction. Uniqueness: if
\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{X}^{\star}+\boldsymbol{c}
with
\boldsymbol{c}\in H
satisfies (
31
), then
c_{i}=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{\star}]=c_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}
for each
i\in[n]
. Part (
ii
) is Proposition
13
with
\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{P}
, noting that
\rho_{i}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\Pi_{i}(X_{i})
for
i\in[n]
.
∎

Actuarial fairness is IR if and only if each agent’s risk premium weakly decreases from autarky to an efficient allocation position.

Theorem
16
highlights a contrast with the common approach in the risk-sharing literature, which takes the aggregate risk
X
as given and characterizes optimal allocations through the set
\mathrm{Opt}(X)_{/\sim}
and the inf-convolution
\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in[n]}\rho_{i}(X)
. However, without modelling the endowments
\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n}
, one cannot assess whether efficient allocations can be implemented by simple rules such as actuarial fairness. Theorem
16
also characterizes when actuarial fairness satisfies individual rationality, but IR is the weakest stability requirement, requiring only that no single agent prefers autarky. A stronger requirement is coalitional stability, which requires that no subgroup of agents can collectively improve upon the proposed welfare-gain vector by pooling among themselves. While a general statement is currently unavailable, we can characterize exactly when actuarial fairness yields a welfare-gain vector in the core of the gain game for distortion risk measures.

Risk premia are constant on each transfer-equivalence class because they are cash-invariant. However,
\mathrm{Opt}(X)
can contain multiple transfer classes (i.e.,
\mathrm{Opt}(X)_{/\sim}
is not a singleton) corresponding to different random allocations, and the risk premia can depend on which class is chosen. The next theorem provides a criterion for the existence of allocations that are simultaneously efficient, actuarially fair, and IR.

Theorem 17
.

There exists an efficient allocation that is both actuarially fair and IR if and only if there exists
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
satisfying

\Pi_{i}(X_{i})\leq\Pi_{i}(\zeta_{i}),\qquad\forall i\in[n].

(33)

Proof of Theorem
17
.

Fix
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
. By Theorem
16
, the unique actuarially fair point in the class
\boldsymbol{X}+H
is IR if and only if (
32
) holds, which is (
33
). Existence of an efficient AF–IR allocation is therefore equivalent to the existence of at least one efficient transfer class passing this test.
∎

Notice that if
\mathrm{Opt}(X)
consists of a single deterministic-transfer class so that
\mathrm{Opt}(X)_{/\sim}
is a singleton, then AF–IR existence does not depend on the choice of an efficient allocation. In this case, either (
33
) is true, or AF can never satisfy IR.

Corollary 7
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
. Then the actuarially fair welfare-gain vector
\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathrm{AF}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
w_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}=\Pi_{i}(\zeta_{i})-\Pi_{i}(X_{i})
belongs to
\mathrm{Core}(\nu)
if and only if

\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(X_{A})-\mathbb{E}[X_{A}]\geq\sum_{i\in A}\left(\rho_{i}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]\right),\qquad\forall\,\varnothing\neq A\subseteq[n].

(34)

In particular, the singleton case
A=\{i\}
recovers the IR condition (
32
).

Proof of Corollary
7
.

Since
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i})=C([n])
. Hence
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\Pi_{i}(\zeta_{i})-\Pi_{i}(X_{i}))=\nu([n])
, using
\rho_{i}=\Pi_{i}+\mathbb{E}
and
\sum_{i}\zeta_{i}=\sum_{i}X_{i}=X
. Thus, only the coalitional inequalities need to be checked. For a nonempty
A\subseteq[n]
, the core inequality
\sum_{i\in A}w_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}\geq\nu(A)
is equivalent, after substituting
w_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}=\Pi_{i}(\zeta_{i})-\Pi_{i}(X_{i})
and
\nu(A)=\sum_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-C(A)
, to
C(A)-\mathbb{E}[X_{A}]\geq\sum_{i\in A}\Pi_{i}(X_{i})
. Since
C(A)=\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(X_{A})
and
\Pi_{i}(X_{i})=\rho_{i}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]
, this is exactly (
34
). For
A=\{i\}
,
\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(X_{A})=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})
, so (
34
) becomes
\Pi_{i}(\zeta_{i})\geq\Pi_{i}(X_{i})
.
∎

We note that Corollary
7
does not require coalitional stability of AF for all sub-allocations, as condition (
34
) is computed directly on the aggregate loss
X_{A}
using the lower envelope
h_{\wedge}^{A}
of the coalition
A\subseteq[n]
. The AF core condition is therefore coalition-specific and must be verified on a coalition-by-coalition basis.

To interpret the IR criterion (
32
), we record how the sign of the risk premium
\Pi_{h}=\rho_{h}-\mathbb{E}
depends on whether the distortion
h\in\mathcal{H}
lies above or below the identity.
The following result is well known in the theory of distortion risk measures, but we include a proof for completeness and to clarify its connection to actuarial fairness.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\rho_{h}
be the distortion risk measure defined in (
3
). Define
\Pi_{h}(Y):=\rho_{h}(Y)-\mathbb{E}[Y]
for bounded
Y\in\mathcal{X}
. Then:

(i)

if
h(u)\geq u
for all
u\in[0,1]
, then
\Pi_{h}(Y)\geq 0
for all bounded
Y\in\mathcal{X}
;

(ii)

if
h(u)\leq u
for all
u\in[0,1]
, then
\Pi_{h}(Y)\leq 0
for all bounded
Y\in\mathcal{X}
.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

Since
Y\in\mathcal{X}
is bounded, there exists
m\in\mathbb{R}
such that
Y+m\geq 0
almost surely. By cash additivity,
\Pi_{h}(Y)=\Pi_{h}(Y+m)
. Since
Y+m\geq 0
, the tail representations

\rho_{h}(Y+m)=\int_{0}^{\infty}h\big(\mathbb{P}(Y+m>t)\big)\,\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\mathbb{E}[Y+m]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(Y+m>t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t

yield

\Pi_{h}(Y)=\Pi_{h}(Y+m)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(h\big(\mathbb{P}(Y+m>t)\big)-\mathbb{P}(Y+m>t)\Big)\,\,\mathrm{d}t.

The integrand is pointwise
\geq 0
when
h(u)\geq u
for all
u\in[0,1]
, giving (i), and pointwise
\leq 0
when
h(u)\leq u
for all
u\in[0,1]
, giving (ii).
∎

Distortions above the identity overweight adverse quantiles and produce a nonnegative premium; those below the identity produce a nonpositive premium. This explains why actuarial fairness may systematically violate IR for agents with conservative distortions.

Example 6

(Actuarial fairness: diversification determines IR)
.

Let
n=2
and consider dual-power distortions
h_{i}(u)=1-(1-u)^{\theta_{i}}
for
i\in[2]
, where
\theta_{1}=2
and
\theta_{2}=3
, so that
h_{1}\leq h_{2}
pointwise and agent 1 is the less conservative. Both agents hold symmetric Bernoulli endowments
\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{i}=1)=1/2
for
i\in[2]
, giving
\rho_{1}(\zeta_{1})=3/4
,
\rho_{2}(\zeta_{2})=7/8
, and
\Pi_{i}(\zeta_{i})=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-1/2>0
for both agents. Since
h_{1}\leq h_{2}
, the inf-convolution equals
\rho_{h_{1}}(X)
and the efficient allocation is
\boldsymbol{X}=(X,0)
regardless of the dependence structure; only the law of
X=\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}
changes.

•

Take
\zeta_{1}=\zeta_{2}=Y
for a single
Y\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/2)
, so that
X=2Y
. The actuarially fair transfer is
c_{1}^{\mathrm{AF}}=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[2Y]=-1/2
and
c_{2}^{\mathrm{AF}}=1/2
. Agent 1 absorbs the full aggregate loss: positive homogeneity gives
\Pi_{1}(2Y)=2\Pi_{1}(Y)=1/2>1/4=\Pi_{1}(\zeta_{1})
, violating (
32
). Comonotonicity eliminates diversification, so the risk premium doubles, while actuarial fairness compensates only the additional expected cost. The actuarially fair welfare-gain vector is
(w_{1}^{\mathrm{AF}},w_{2}^{\mathrm{AF}})=(-1/4,\,3/8)
, which violates IR.

•

Take
\zeta_{1}=Y
and
\zeta_{2}=1-Y
, so that
X=1
almost surely. The efficient split from the inf-convolution is
\boldsymbol{X}=(1,0)
. The actuarially fair transfer is
c_{1}^{\mathrm{AF}}=-1/2
and
c_{2}^{\mathrm{AF}}=1/2
, and both post-transfer evaluations satisfy IR:
\rho_{1}(1)+c_{1}^{\mathrm{AF}}=1/2<3/4=\rho_{1}(\zeta_{1})
and
\rho_{2}(0)+c_{2}^{\mathrm{AF}}=1/2<7/8=\rho_{2}(\zeta_{2})
. Equivalently,
\Pi_{i}(X_{i})=0
for both agents since each receives a constant, so (
32
) holds with strict inequality. Counter-monotonicity creates a perfect hedge: the aggregate is riskless, and all risk premia vanish. The actuarially fair welfare-gain vector is
(w_{1}^{\mathrm{AF}},w_{2}^{\mathrm{AF}})=(1/4,\,3/8)
.

In both cases, agent 2 cedes all risk and receives the same welfare gain,
w_{2}^{\mathrm{AF}}=3/8
; the dependence structure affects only agent 1, who bears the aggregate cost of insufficient diversification.

In Example
6
, actuarial fairness satisfies IR if and only if the efficient allocation reduces each agent’s risk premium below its stand-alone level. When the endowments are comonotonic, agent 1 absorbs a scaled copy of her own loss and gains no diversification. The risk premium grows, while actuarial fairness compensates only for the additional expected cost, resulting in a negative welfare gain. When the endowments are countermonotonic, pooling eliminates all risk, and the risk premia vanish, so both agents gain. The extreme case is a risk-averse reinsurer entering the pool with no initial exposure, so that the reinsurer’s risk premium is zero. Since the efficient allocation assigns her a nonconstant share, her risk premium can only increase; actuarial fairness offers no compensation beyond expected value, so condition (
32
) necessarily fails. For this reason, actuarial fairness is viable only when pooling provides sufficient diversification to spread the welfare gain among all participants.

In practice, insurers and reinsurers often charge an actuarially fair premium plus a loading that covers operating costs and profit margins. Adding a loading makes IR more likely but does not guarantee it: if the risk premium shortfall exceeds the loading, the agent still loses from participation. A more systematic remedy is to replace the physical measure
\mathbb{P}
with a pricing measure that accounts for risk aversion, as discussed in Section
3.3
.

C.4
The Shapley value and the Weber set

The Shapley value
(
53
)
is a classical solution concept in cooperative game theory that assigns to each player a payoff reflecting their average marginal contribution across all possible orderings of the players. It is the unique division rule satisfying four axioms: efficiency, meaning the payoffs exhaust the total surplus
\nu([n])
; symmetry, meaning two players who make identical marginal contributions to every coalition receive the same payoff; the null-player property, meaning a player whose marginal contribution to every coalition is zero receives nothing; and additivity, meaning the value of a sum of two games equals the sum of the values.

Definition 12

(Shapley value)
.

For a TU game
([n],\nu)
, the Shapley value
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\nu)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

\phi_{i}(\nu):=\sum_{A\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}}\frac{|A|!\,(n-|A|-1)!}{n!}\,\big(\nu(A\cup\{i\})-\nu(A)\big),\qquad i\in[n].

(35)

Equivalently,
\phi_{i}(\nu)
is the expected marginal contribution of
i
under a uniformly random permutation of
[n]
.

The Shapley value depends only on the characteristic function
\nu
, not on the choice of efficient allocation
\boldsymbol{X}
; it is therefore uniquely determined on the quotient space
\mathrm{Opt}(X)_{/\sim}
and, a fortiori, invariant within any transfer class. Since it averages marginal contributions over all orderings of the agents, monotonicity of
\nu
implies that the Shapley payoffs are nonnegative and sum to
W
. The transfer construction from Theorem
1
then yields an implementable allocation on any efficient representative, with the induced final allocation invariant within each transfer-equivalence class.

Theorem 18
.

Define the Shapley gain split by
w_{i}^{\mathrm{Sh}}:=\phi_{i}(\nu)
for
i\in[n]
and
\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathrm{Sh}}:=(w_{i}^{\mathrm{Sh}})_{i\in[n]}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then:

(i)

\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathrm{Sh}}\in\Delta_{W}
: in particular,
w_{i}^{\mathrm{Sh}}\geq 0
for all
i\in[n]
and
\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}^{\mathrm{Sh}}=W
.

(ii)

The transfer
c^{\mathrm{Sh}}_{i}:=b_{i}-w_{i}^{\mathrm{Sh}},
for
i\in[n],
belongs to
H_{\mathrm{IR}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})
, and
\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathrm{Sh}}:=\boldsymbol{X}_{0}+\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathrm{Sh}}
is feasible, efficient, and IR. Its welfare baselines equal the Shapley gains, that is,
b_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathrm{Sh}})=w_{i}^{\mathrm{Sh}},
for
i\in[n].

(iii)

\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathrm{Sh}}
depends only on
\nu
and hence is the same for every
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
.

Proof of Theorem
18
.

To show part (
i
), recall that the Shapley value is the average of the marginal vectors induced by all orderings of the agents. Every marginal vector has nonnegative components by monotonicity of
\nu
(Lemma
1
) and sums to
\nu([n])=W
, so their average lies in
\Delta_{W}
.

The argument for part (
ii
) is identical to the proof of Proposition
3
(
ii
): one has
\sum_{i\in[n]}^{n}c_{i}^{\mathrm{Sh}}=\sum_{i\in[n]}^{n}b_{i}-\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}^{\mathrm{Sh}}=W-W=0
, and
c_{i}^{\mathrm{Sh}}\leq b_{i}
because
w_{i}^{\mathrm{Sh}}\geq 0
for every
i\in[n]
. Hence
\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathrm{Sh}}\in H_{\mathrm{IR}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})
, and cash additivity yields IR together with the welfare-gain identity.

For part (
iii
), observe that the Shapley value (
35
) is defined entirely in terms of
\nu
. Neither
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}
nor the representative within a transfer class enters the definition, so
\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathrm{Sh}}
is the same for every element of
\mathrm{Opt}(X)
.
∎

The only substantive requirement for the Shapley transfer above is that the gain game be well defined and monotone. Coalitional stability requires more. The standard object linking the Shapley value to the core is the Weber set, which also explains why convexity of the gain game is enough to put the Shapley value in the core.

The Weber set is a relaxation of the core obtained from marginal-contribution vectors along orderings of the agents. Before defining it, we recall the class of convex (supermodular) games, in which the Weber set and the core coincide. Games with increasing marginal value are said to be
supermodular
, or convex.

Definition 13

(Convex (supermodular) game)
.

A TU game
([n],\nu)
is
convex
if for all coalitions
A,B\subseteq[n]
,
\nu(A)+\nu(B)\;\leq\;\nu(A\cup B)+\nu(A\cap B).

Let
A\subseteq[n]
be a given coalition and let
i\in[n]\setminus A
be an individual agent. Convexity requires that
\nu(A\cup\{i\})\geq\nu(A)+\nu(\{i\}).
In our risk-sharing game,
\nu(\{i\})=0
, and this condition is always satisfied when adding one agent to the pool. However, a necessary condition for supermodularity is that for every pair of disjoint coalitions
A,B\subseteq[n]
(that is,
A\cap B=\emptyset
) we have
\nu(A\cup B)\geq\nu(A)+\nu(B),
so that merging any disjoint coalitions yields greater welfare gains, which is an increasing marginal value interpretation. Further, the convexity of
\nu
can be defined directly as having increasing marginal contributions: for every
i\in[n]
and all
A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}
, we have
\nu(A\cup\{i\})-\nu(A)\;\leq\;\nu(B\cup\{i\})-\nu(B).

The latter states that adding an agent to a larger coalition always produces a weakly larger marginal contribution than adding the same agent to a smaller subcoalition. For risk-sharing games, this condition holds when each agent’s marginal contribution to the welfare gain is greater in a larger coalition than in a smaller one. We do not pursue general primitive conditions for the convexity of the risk-sharing game, as the property typically fails to hold (especially for large pools), though particular setups may yield it.

Convex TU games have many desirable properties, including being balanced (i.e., having a nonempty core). The converse is not true. The Shapley value always belongs to the core of convex games. For risk-sharing games, the convexity of the risk measures is not necessary to obtain the convexity of the game. This opens up the possibility that some risk-sharing games with non-convex risk measures might admit coalitionally stable allocations. General results on the convexity of risk-sharing games remain scarce. Identifying primitive conditions under which a cost game is convex is an active line of research outside risk sharing. For instance,
33
study the allocation of over-the-counter derivative trades across central counterparty portfolios. They give two covariance-matrix conditions under which portfolio standard deviation is submodular on the trade subset lattice: nonpositive off-diagonals and a diagonal-dominance bound.

When the core is nonempty, coalitionally stable allocations exist. The Shapley value provides a unique fair split, but it may lie outside the core when the game is not convex. A natural question is whether there exists a universal outer bound that contains both the core and the Shapley value. The Weber set, introduced by
63
, is the object in question.

Definition 14

(Marginal vectors and Weber set)
.

Let
\mathfrak{P}([n])
be the set of all permutations of
[n]
. For
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}([n])
and
i\in[n]
, define the predecessor set
P_{\mathfrak{p}}(i):=\{j\in[n]:\mathfrak{p}(j)<\mathfrak{p}(i)\}
and the associated
marginal vector

\boldsymbol{m}_{\mathfrak{p}}(\nu)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

m_{\mathfrak{p},i}(\nu):=\nu\bigl(P_{\mathfrak{p}}(i)\cup\{i\}\bigr)-\nu\bigl(P_{\mathfrak{p}}(i)\bigr),\qquad i\in[n].

Note that
\sum_{i\in[n]}m_{\mathfrak{p},i}(\nu)=\nu([n])
for every
\mathfrak{p}
. The
Weber set
of
([n],\nu)
is the convex hull of all marginal vectors:

\mathcal{W}(\nu):=\operatorname{conv}\bigl\{\,\boldsymbol{m}_{\mathfrak{p}}(\nu):\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}([n])\,\bigr\}.

Equivalently,
\mathcal{W}(\nu)
is the set of random-order values:
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{W}(\nu)
if and only if
\boldsymbol{w}=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}([n])}q(\mathfrak{p})\,\boldsymbol{m}_{\mathfrak{p}}(\nu)
for some probability distribution
q
on
\mathfrak{P}([n])
. The Shapley value is the barycentre of the Weber set, that is, the random-order value under the uniform distribution,
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\nu)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}\boldsymbol{m}_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}([n])}(\nu)
, so
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\nu)\in\mathcal{W}(\nu)
always holds.

It is well known that the core, when nonempty, is contained in the Weber set. Welfare-gain vectors in the Weber set are sometimes called
admissible
; core stability is thus a refinement of admissibility. Finally, when the game is convex (supermodular), every marginal vector is itself a core element, so the entire Weber set collapses to the core. The next proposition collects these facts. The inclusion of the core in the Weber set is due to
63
, and the convex-game equivalence was established by
55
and
36
.

Proposition 15
.

For every TU game
([n],\nu)
,
\mathrm{Core}(\nu)\subseteq\mathcal{W}(\nu).

Moreover,
\nu
is convex
if and only if
\mathrm{Core}(\nu)=\mathcal{W}(\nu)
. In particular,
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\nu)\in\mathcal{W}(\nu)
always.

In this paper, payoffs
\boldsymbol{w}
are post-transfer welfare shares relative to autarky: for an efficient allocation
\boldsymbol{X}
and a balanced transfer
\boldsymbol{c}\in H
, agent
i
’s welfare share is
w_{i}=b_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})-c_{i}=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-\rho_{i}(X_{i}+c_{i})
for
i\in[n]
. On any fixed efficient transfer class, the set of individually rational Pareto-optimal allocations coincides with the welfare simplex
\Delta_{\nu([n])}=\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}:\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}=\nu([n])\}
from Theorem
1
. Since every marginal vector sums to
\nu([n])
and has nonnegative components by monotonicity of
\nu
(Lemma
1
), it follows that
\mathcal{W}(\nu)\subseteq\Delta_{\nu([n])}
. The admissible set in welfare-share coordinates is therefore
\mathcal{W}(\nu)\cap\Delta_{\nu([n])}=\mathcal{W}(\nu)
, and the corresponding evaluation vectors
\boldsymbol{r}=\bigl(\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})\bigr)_{i\in[n]}-\boldsymbol{w}
satisfy
r_{i}\leq\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})
for
i\in[n]
. The preceding results give the following chain of cooperative requirements on a welfare-gain vector
\boldsymbol{w}
with
\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}=\nu([n])
:

\text{efficiency}\;\supseteq\;\underbrace{\Delta_{\nu([n])}}_{\text{IR}}\;\supseteq\;\underbrace{\mathcal{W}(\nu)}_{\text{admissibility}}\;\supseteq\;\underbrace{\mathrm{Core}(\nu)}_{\text{core stability}}.

(36)

C.5
The nucleolus

The Shapley value selects a welfare-gain vector by averaging the marginal contributions across all orderings of the set of agents; it is the unique rule that satisfies efficiency, symmetry, and additivity. A complementary approach is to select the core element that is most robust to coalitional deviation. The nucleolus, introduced by
51
, is the unique imputation that lexicographically minimizes the maximum excess across all coalitions. It therefore identifies the welfare-gain vector under which the most dissatisfied coalition has the least incentive to leave the grand coalition.

Definition 15

(Excess)
.

For a TU game
([n],\nu)
with
\nu(\varnothing)=0
and a payoff vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=\nu([n])
, the
excess
of coalition
A\subseteq[n]
at
\boldsymbol{w}
is

e(A,\boldsymbol{w}):=\nu(A)-\sum_{i\in A}w_{i}.

(37)

Definition 16

(Nucleolus)
.

Let
([n],\nu)
be a TU game with
\nu(\varnothing)=0
and let
\mathcal{I}(\nu):=\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}=\nu([n]),\;w_{i}\geq\nu(\{i\})\;\forall i\in[n]\}
denote the set of
imputations
, that is, efficient payoff vectors that give each player at least their stand-alone value. For each
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{I}(\nu)
, define the
excess vector

\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}-2}
as the vector of excesses
e(A,\boldsymbol{w})
for all proper nonempty coalitions
A\subset[n]
, arranged in non-increasing order. The
nucleolus

\mathrm{Nuc}(\nu)
is the unique imputation that lexicographically minimises
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w})
over
\mathcal{I}(\nu)
:

\mathrm{Nuc}(\nu):=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{I}(\nu)}\;\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w}).

(38)

The nucleolus has several fundamental properties that make it a natural transfer rule. It always exists and is unique
(
51
)
; if the core is nonempty, the nucleolus lies in the core. In particular, since the gain game is totally balanced under convex risk measures (Theorem
3
), the nucleolus is well defined and lies in the core.

Like the Shapley value, the nucleolus depends only on
\nu
and is therefore uniquely determined on the quotient space
\mathrm{Opt}(X)_{/\sim}
. Since the nucleolus is an imputation, it lies in
\Delta_{W}
, and the transfer construction from Theorem
1
yields an implementable allocation on any efficient representative.

Theorem 19
.

Define the nucleolus gain split by
w_{i}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}:=\mathrm{Nuc}_{i}(\nu)
for
i\in[n]
and
\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}:=(w_{i}^{\mathrm{Nuc}})_{i\in[n]}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then:

(i)

\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}\in\Delta_{W}
: in particular,
w_{i}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}\geq 0
for all
i\in[n]
and
\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}=W
.

(ii)

The transfer
c^{\mathrm{Nuc}}_{i}:=b_{i}-w_{i}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}
for each
i\in[n]
belongs to
H_{\mathrm{IR}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})
, and
\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}:=\boldsymbol{X}_{0}+\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}
is feasible, efficient, and IR. Its welfare baselines equal the nucleolus gains,
b_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathrm{Nuc}})=w_{i}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}
for each
i\in[n]
.

(iii)

\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}
depends only on
\nu
and hence is the same for every
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathrm{Opt}(X)
.

Proof of Theorem
19
.

To show part (
i
), recall that the nucleolus is an imputation
(
51
)
, so
w_{i}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}\geq\nu(\{i\})=0
for every
i\in[n]
and
\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}=\nu([n])=W
, hence
\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}\in\Delta_{W}
. The argument for part (
ii
) is identical to that of Theorem
18
(
ii
). Finally, for part (
iii
), the nucleolus (
38
) is defined entirely in terms of
\nu
, so
\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathrm{Nuc}}
is the same for every element of
\mathrm{Opt}(X)
.
∎

Together with the lower-envelope
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
rule from Section
3.3
, these transfer rules divide the welfare gain according to different principles. The
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
-pricing rule compensates each agent at the risk-adjusted cost of their endowment: the agent who absorbs the aggregate loss bears more risk but is also the one willing to accept it at the lowest cost, so
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
assigns this agent a small share of the surplus. The Shapley value averages marginal contributions across all coalition orderings, yielding a more balanced split. The nucleolus pushes welfare toward the agent whose absence would cost the grand coalition the most, because equalizing coalitional excesses concentrates surplus on agents that no subgroup can easily replace. The examples of Appendix
E
confirm this ordering across several dependence structures.

C.6
Canonical transfer rules are not PMAS

The proportional-cost rule (for any fixed positive weights), the actuarially fair rule, the coalition-wise lower-envelope pricing rule, the coalition-wise Shapley rule, and the coalition-wise nucleolus are not entry-monotone in general. Applying a rule separately to every restricted game may destroy consistency across coalitions. None of the canonical rules considered here is, in general, a PMAS. The separation examples below exhibit instances where proportional-cost sharing satisfies PMAS while lower-envelope pricing fails, and instances where the reverse holds; neither rule dominates the other.

Theorem 20
.

The proportional-cost rule, for fixed positive weights, the actuarially fair rule, the coalition-wise lower-envelope pricing rule, the coalition-wise Shapley rule, and the coalition-wise nucleolus are not PMAS in general.

Proof.

For the proportional-cost rule, fix positive weights
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}
and choose
M>\xi_{3}/(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})
. Let
Y\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/2)
, set
\zeta_{1}=Y
,
\zeta_{2}=1-Y
, and
\zeta_{3}=MY
, and let all agents use
h(u)=\min\{2u,1\}
, so that
C(\{1,2\})=\rho_{h}(1)=1
and
C(\{1,2,3\})=\rho_{h}(1+MY)=1+M
. Hence incumbent 1’s proportional costs are
C_{1}^{\xi}(\{1,2\})=\xi_{1}/(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})
and
C_{1}^{\xi}(\{1,2,3\})=\xi_{1}(1+M)/(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}+\xi_{3})
. By the choice of
M
, the latter is larger, so
w_{1}^{\xi}(\{1,2,3\})<w_{1}^{\xi}(\{1,2\})
and entry monotonicity fails.

For the actuarially fair rule, take
n=2
, let
Y\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/2)
, set
\zeta_{1}=\zeta_{2}=Y
, and use dual-power distortions
h_{1}(u)=1-(1-u)^{2}
and
h_{2}(u)=1-(1-u)^{3}
.
Since
h_{1}\leq h_{2}
, the efficient allocation assigns the aggregate to agent 1:
X_{1}=2Y
and
X_{2}=0
. With
\rho_{h_{1}}(Y)=h_{1}(1/2)=3/4
, positive homogeneity gives
\Pi_{1}(\zeta_{1})=\rho_{h_{1}}(Y)-\mathbb{E}[Y]=1/4
and
\Pi_{1}(X_{1})=2\Pi_{1}(Y)=1/2
.
Thus
w_{1}^{\mathrm{AF}}(\{1,2\})=-1/4<0=w_{1}^{\mathrm{AF}}(\{1\})
, so entry monotonicity fails.

For coalition-wise lower-envelope pricing, it suffices to violate entry monotonicity, since the rule is core-selecting at every coalition. Let
Z_{1},Z_{2},Z_{3}
be independent
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/2)
variables, set
\zeta_{1}=Z_{1}
,
\zeta_{2}=2Z_{2}
, and
\zeta_{3}=2Z_{3}
, and let all agents use
h(u)=\min\{2u,1\}
. For
A=\{1,2\}
, the aggregate
X_{A}=Z_{1}+2Z_{2}
takes the four values
0,1,2,3
with equal probability, and the lower-envelope pricing measure prices the worst half of the states. Hence
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}_{A}^{\star}}[\zeta_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}\mid Z_{2}=1]=1/2
. For
B=\{1,2,3\}
, the worst half of
X_{B}=Z_{1}+2Z_{2}+2Z_{3}
is
\{X_{B}\geq 3\}
; among those four states, three have
Z_{1}=1
. Hence
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}_{B}^{\star}}[\zeta_{1}]=3/4
.
Since
\rho_{h}(\zeta_{1})=1
is coalition-independent, incumbent 1’s welfare falls from
1/2
to
1/4
when player 3 enters coalition
\{1,2\}
.

For the coalition-wise Shapley rule, we use a different setup. Let
U,V
be independent
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/2)
variables, set
\zeta_{1}=\zeta_{2}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{U=V=1\}}
and
\zeta_{3}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{U\neq V\}}
, and again let all agents use
h(u)=\min\{2u,1\}
. The induced gain game satisfies
\nu(\{i\})=\nu(\{1,2\})=0
,
\nu(\{1,3\})=\nu(\{2,3\})=1/2
, and
\nu([3])=1/2
.
For the subgame on
A=\{1,3\}
, the Shapley value splits
\nu(A)=1/2
equally, so
\phi_{1}(\nu^{A})=1/4
. In the grand coalition,

\phi_{1}(\nu)=\frac{1}{3}\nu(\{1\})+\frac{1}{6}(\nu(\{1,2\})-\nu(\{2\}))+\frac{1}{6}(\nu(\{1,3\})-\nu(\{3\}))+\frac{1}{3}(\nu([3])-\nu(\{2,3\}))=1/12
.
Thus entry of player 2 harms incumbent 1.

For the coalition-wise nucleolus, the same gain game gives a counterexample. The two-player subgame on
A=\{1,3\}
has nucleolus
(1/4,1/4)
, so
\mathrm{Nuc}_{1}(\nu^{A})=1/4
. In the grand coalition, the pair constraints
w_{1}+w_{3}\geq 1/2
and
w_{2}+w_{3}\geq 1/2
, together with efficiency and nonnegativity, force
\mathrm{Core}(\nu)=\{(0,0,1/2)\}
.
Hence
\mathrm{Nuc}(\nu)=(0,0,1/2)
and incumbent 1’s nucleolus payoff falls from
1/4
to
0
after player 2 enters. This failure is not only a nonconvex-game pathology;
56
gives analogous failures for convex games.
∎

The PMAS criteria for the mean-proportional rule and for lower-envelope pricing are logically independent. Return to the lower-envelope-pricing counterexample in the proof above: let
Z_{1},Z_{2},Z_{3}
be independent
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/2)
variables, set
\zeta_{1}=Z_{1}
,
\zeta_{2}=2Z_{2}
, and
\zeta_{3}=2Z_{3}
, and let all agents use
h(u)=\min\{2u,1\}
. With mean weights
\mu=(\tfrac{1}{2},1,1)
, the normalized costs
\bar{C}(A):=C(A)/\mu_{A}
are antitone under inclusion on
[3]
, so Theorem
6
makes the mean-proportional rule a PMAS; lower-envelope pricing, by contrast, charges incumbent 1
C_{1}^{\star}(\{1,2\})=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
C_{1}^{\star}([3])=\tfrac{3}{4}
, so its welfare falls when agent 3 enters
\{1,2\}
.

Conversely, the fully comonotonic Bernoulli scenario in Table
2
, with
\zeta_{i}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{U\leq p_{i}\}}
for
i\in[3]
,
(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})=(0.3,0.4,0.5)
, and
h_{1}\leq h_{2}\leq h_{3}
, gives the reverse separation: comonotonicity makes lower-envelope pricing a PMAS by Corollary
6
, whereas the mean-proportional rule with
\mu_{i}=p_{i}
for every
i\in[3]
violates the normalized-cost antitonicity of Theorem
6
already along
\{1,3\}\subseteq[3]
. Neither proportional-cost sharing nor lower-envelope Arrow–Debreu pricing dominates the other as a route to PMAS.

Example 7

(Elliptical dual-pricing formula)
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\zeta}=(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n})
is elliptically distributed with finite second moments, mean vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and positive-definite covariance matrix
\Sigma
. For every nonempty
A\subseteq[n]
, assume
\Var(X_{A})>0
and write
\beta_{i,A}:=\Cov(\zeta_{i},X_{A})/\Var(X_{A})
for
i\in A
. Elliptical regression from
16
gives
\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}\mid X_{A}]=\mu_{i}+\beta_{i,A}(X_{A}-\mu_{A})
. If a dual optimizer
\pi_{A}^{\star}
is
\sigma(X_{A})
-measurable, then the convex-penalty dual-pricing share is

C_{i}^{\star}(A)=\mu_{i}+\beta_{i,A}\left(\mathbb{E}[\pi_{A}^{\star}X_{A}]-\mu_{A}\right)-\alpha_{i}(\pi_{A}^{\star}),\qquad i\in A.

For coherent risk measures, this reduces to
C_{i}^{\star}(A)=\mu_{i}+\beta_{i,A}(C(A)-\mu_{A})
for
i\in A
. For distortion risk measures,
C_{i}^{\star}(A)=\mu_{i}+\sigma_{i}r_{i,A}\rho_{h_{A}}(Z)
, where
\sigma_{i}:=\sqrt{\Var(\zeta_{i})}
and
r_{i,A}:=\Cor(\zeta_{i},X_{A})
.

The Gaussian case is the specialization of Corollary
4
in which the elliptical regression coefficients are correlations. The dual condition is a comparison between the standardized aggregate deviations and the individual penalties.

Corollary 8
.

Suppose that Assumption
2
holds, and that
(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n})
is jointly Gaussian with mean vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and positive-definite covariance matrix
\Sigma
. For every nonempty
A\subseteq[n]
, define
\mu_{A}:=\mathbb{E}[X_{A}]
,
\sigma_{A}:=\sqrt{\Var(X_{A})}
, and
d_{A}^{\star}:=(\mathbb{E}[\pi_{A}^{\star}X_{A}]-\mu_{A})/\sigma_{A}
; for every
i\in[n]
, define
\sigma_{i}:=\sqrt{\Var(\zeta_{i})}
, and for every
i\in A
, define
r_{i,A}:=\Cor(\zeta_{i},X_{A})
. If, for every
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
and every
i\in A
,

\sigma_{i}r_{i,B}d_{B}^{\star}-\alpha_{i}(\pi_{B}^{\star})\leq\sigma_{i}r_{i,A}d_{A}^{\star}-\alpha_{i}(\pi_{A}^{\star}),

then the dual-pricing allocation is a PMAS.

Proof.

For Gaussian endowments, the elliptical formula in Corollary
4
gives

C_{i}^{\star}(A)=\mu_{i}+\sigma_{i}r_{i,A}d_{A}^{\star}-\alpha_{i}(\pi_{A}^{\star}),\qquad i\in A.

The displayed condition is exactly the cross-coalition antitonicity
C_{i}^{\star}(B)\leq C_{i}^{\star}(A)
for
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
and
i\in A
. Proposition
8
gives the PMAS conclusion.
∎

Mean–variance preferences provide a useful setup because the coalition inf-convolution is explicit, and the PMAS condition can be read directly from the normalized variance. In this case, the risk measure is not monotone, implying that the dual price need not be a probability measure, unless the supporting kernel is non-negative.

Example 8

(Mean–variance PMAS conditions)
.

For each
i\in[n]
and
Y\in L^{2}
, let
\rho_{i}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[Y]+\gamma_{i}\Var(Y)
with
\gamma_{i}>0
, and write
\vartheta_{i}:=1/\gamma_{i}
. For every nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, set
\vartheta_{A}:=\sum_{i\in A}\vartheta_{i}
,
\mu_{A}:=\mathbb{E}[X_{A}]
, and
\bar{X}_{A}:=X_{A}/\mu_{A}
when
\mu_{A}>0
. Then
\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i\in A}\rho_{i}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[Y]+\vartheta_{A}^{-1}\Var(Y)
and
C(A)=\mu_{A}+\vartheta_{A}^{-1}\Var(X_{A})
.
Assume
\mu_{i}>0
for every
i\in[n]
. The mean-proportional allocation (
15
) defines a PMAS if and only if

\frac{\Var(X_{B})}{\vartheta_{B}\mu_{B}}\leq\frac{\Var(X_{A})}{\vartheta_{A}\mu_{A}},\qquad\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n].

(39)

Equivalently,
(\mu_{B}/\vartheta_{B})\Var(\bar{X}_{B})\leq(\mu_{A}/\vartheta_{A})\Var(\bar{X}_{A})
for every
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
. A primitive sufficient condition for (
39
) is
\bar{X}_{B}\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}\bar{X}_{A}
and
\vartheta_{B}/\mu_{B}\geq\vartheta_{A}/\mu_{A}
for every
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
, since the first comparison gives
\Var(\bar{X}_{B})\leq\Var(\bar{X}_{A})
and the second comparison gives
\mu_{B}/\vartheta_{B}\leq\mu_{A}/\vartheta_{A}
.

For the dual-pricing allocation of Example
2
, the exact PMAS condition is the antitonicity of the displayed cost shares: for every
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
and every
i\in A
,

2\gamma_{B}\Cov(\zeta_{i},X_{B})-\frac{\gamma_{B}^{2}}{\gamma_{i}}\Var(X_{B})\leq 2\gamma_{A}\Cov(\zeta_{i},X_{A})-\frac{\gamma_{A}^{2}}{\gamma_{i}}\Var(X_{A}).

As noted in Example
2
, the optimizer
\pi_{A}^{\star}=1+2\gamma_{A}(X_{A}-\mathbb{E}[X_{A}])
is a probability density only under the additional nonnegativity condition
\pi_{A}^{\star}\geq 0
almost surely; otherwise it should be interpreted as a signed pricing kernel.

We now turn to entropic risk measures. This class is useful here for two reasons: the inf-convolution adds risk tolerances exactly, and the optimal sharing rule is linear in aggregate losses, which makes the connection with
4
transparent. The actuarially fair entropic selector is most naturally written by separating the physical mean from the centred risk premium. This is the exponential analogue of the mean-proportional logic above, but the scalar that must be antitone is now a normalized cumulant-generating function rather than a normalized variance.

Corollary 9
.

Assume risk measures are entropic as in Example
3
. For every nonempty coalition
A\subseteq[n]
, define
\mu_{A}:=\mathbb{E}[X_{A}]
and
\psi_{A}:=\log\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{(X_{A}-\mu_{A})/\iota_{A}}]
. For
i\in A
, define
C_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}(A):=\mu_{i}+\iota_{i}\psi_{A}
. If
\psi_{B}\leq\psi_{A}
whenever
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
, then the welfare gains
w_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}(A)=\rho_{i}(\zeta_{i})-C_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}(A)
define a PMAS. In particular, the condition holds if

\frac{X_{B}-\mu_{B}}{\iota_{B}}\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}\frac{X_{A}-\mu_{A}}{\iota_{A}},\qquad\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n].

(40)

Proof.

Efficiency follows from
\sum_{i\in A}C_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}(A)=\mu_{A}+\iota_{A}\psi_{A}=\iota_{A}\log\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{X_{A}/\iota_{A}}]=C(A)
. If
\psi_{B}\leq\psi_{A}
and
i\in A\subseteq B
, then
C_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}(B)\leq C_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}(A)
, so the rule is entry-monotone. Proposition
5
establishes core membership for every restricted coalition; hence, the rule is a PMAS. Finally, (
40
) implies
\psi_{B}\leq\psi_{A}
because
x\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{x}
is convex.
∎

The preceding condition has the expected homogeneous-pool specialization. Exchangeability yields the convex-order contraction, while common risk tolerance ensures compatibility of the normalization across coalitions.

Corollary 10
.

Assume the entropic setup of Example
3
. Suppose
(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n})
is exchangeable,
\mu_{i}=\mu
for every

\iota_{i}=\iota

For

(X_{B}-|B|\mu)/(|B|\iota)\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}(X_{A}-|A|\mu)/(|A|\iota)

\pi_{A}^{\star}
is
\sigma(X_{A})

\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}\mid X_{A}]=X_{A}/|A|

Comonotonicity has a different interpretation in the entropic model. It does not by itself produce entry monotonicity; one also needs the risk-tolerance scale to grow proportionally with mean exposure.

.

The jointly normal specialization recovers a condition identified in

Example 9

(Normal entropic expansion)
.

Assume the entropic setup of Example
3
, and suppose the coalition aggregate risks are normally distributed. If
X_{A}
has standard deviation
\sigma_{A}
, then
\psi_{A}=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{A}/\iota_{A})^{2}
. If each
\zeta_{i}
,
i\in[n]
, is normal with standard deviation
\sigma_{i}
, then the actuarially fair welfare gain in coalition

is
w_{i}^{\mathrm{AF}}(A)=(\iota_{i}/2)\{(\sigma_{i}/\iota_{i})^{2}-(\sigma_{A}/\iota_{A})^{2}\}
for
i\in A
. Thus, individual rationality under actuarial fairness is governed by the ratio
\sigma_{A}/\iota_{A}
. For a one-step expansion
B=A\cup\{j\}
, the actuarially fair incumbent costs weakly decrease when
\sigma_{B}/\iota_{B}\leq\sigma_{A}/\iota_{A}
, and the entrant is willing to join when
\sigma_{B}/\iota_{B}\leq\sigma_{j}/\iota_{j}
; these are exactly the strong-consensus inequalities in the terminology of
4
, while their weak-consensus condition requires the new aggregate ratio to be no larger than the incumbent individual ratios.

For jointly normal endowments, exponential tilting gives
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}_{A}^{\star}}[\zeta_{i}]=\mu_{i}+\Cov(\zeta_{i},X_{A})/\iota_{A}
and
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}_{A}^{\star}}[\log\pi_{A}^{\star}]=\Var(X_{A})/(2\iota_{A}^{2})
. Consequently,
C_{i}^{\star}(A)=\mu_{i}+\Cov(\zeta_{i},X_{A})/\iota_{A}-\iota_{i}\Var(X_{A})/(2\iota_{A}^{2})
for
i\in A
.
The dual-pricing allocation is a PMAS if and only if, for every
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
and every
i\in A
,

\frac{\Cov(\zeta_{i},X_{B})}{\iota_{B}}-\frac{\iota_{i}}{2\iota_{B}^{2}}\Var(X_{B})\leq\frac{\Cov(\zeta_{i},X_{A})}{\iota_{A}}-\frac{\iota_{i}}{2\iota_{A}^{2}}\Var(X_{A}).

Equivalently, since
w_{i}^{\star}(A)=(2\iota_{i})^{-1}\Var(\zeta_{i}-(\iota_{i}/\iota_{A})X_{A})
, entry monotonicity under dual pricing is equivalent to

\Var\left(\zeta_{i}-\frac{\iota_{i}}{\iota_{B}}X_{B}\right)\geq\Var\left(\zeta_{i}-\frac{\iota_{i}}{\iota_{A}}X_{A}\right),\qquad\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n],\ i\in A.

This identity explains why equilibrium pricing automatically gives fixed-coalition stability in the normal entropic model, and why strong consensus under entry is a sharper requirement than weak consensus.

The remaining applications are proportional conditional-mean risk-sharing models. In each case, the conditional expectation of an individual endowment, given the coalition aggregate, equals the deterministic mean share of that aggregate. Combined with the proportional penalty split
\alpha_{i}(\pi_{A}^{\star})=(\mu_{i}/\mu_{A})\alpha_{A}(\pi_{A}^{\star})
, which holds automatically for coherent risk measures, Theorem
10
identifies the dual-pricing allocation with the mean-proportional allocation and yields a PMAS.

The first such model is the convolution-semigroup case, in which each agent’s endowment has the law of a common nonnegative subordinator at a time parameter equal to the agent’s mean. This family covers several classical actuarial aggregate-loss models, including compound Poisson claim totals, gamma aggregate losses, and inverse-Gaussian aggregate-loss models, with the mean parameter
\mu_{i}
for
i\in[n]
scaling each agent’s exposure. If
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an integrable subordinator with Laplace exponent
\Psi
, then
\mathbb{E}[e^{-uZ_{t}}]=\exp\{-t\Psi(u)\}
for
u\geq 0
, the laws of
Z_{t}

Corollary 12
.

Under Assumption

Dirichlet–Liouville models reallocate a random aggregate loss across participants via Dirichlet-distributed shares that are independent of the loss. This fits settings such as catastrophe pools, in which aggregate event severity is random, and the participants’ losses fluctuate around a fixed exposure profile.

Corollary 13
.

Under Assumption
2
, fix
\tau>0
and
\mu_{i}>0

The preceding corollaries establish PMAS by showing that dual pricing coincides with the mean-proportional allocation. However, PMAS transfer rules need not coincide with the dual price. The mean-proportional allocation (
15
) charges each agent the same cost per unit of mean exposure, and the following example shows that it can support a PMAS even when the conditional-mean shares are not proportional, so this PMAS is not a dual price.

Corollary 15
.

Fix
\alpha>0
and
0<a<b
, let
G_{1},\dots,G_{n}
be independent and identically distributed
\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,1)
random variables, and set
\zeta_{1}=aG_{1}/\alpha
and
\zeta_{i}=bG_{i}/\alpha
for
i\in[n]\setminus\{1\}
, so that
\mu_{1}=a
and
\mu_{i}=b
for
i\in[n]\setminus\{1\}
. Suppose Assumption
2
holds. Suppose also that, for every
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
,
R_{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\bar{X}_{A})\leq R_{A}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\bar{X}_{A})
. Then the mean-proportional allocation
C_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(A)=\mu_{i}C(A)/\mu_{A}
,
i\in A
, is a PMAS. If, for some
j\in[n]\setminus\{1\}
and
A=\{1,j\}
, the selected coherent dual optimizer
\pi_{A}^{\star}
is a nonconstant nondecreasing function of
X_{A}
, then this PMAS does not coincide with the dual-pricing allocation.

Proof.

Set
Z_{i}:=G_{i}/\alpha
, so the
Z_{i}
are independent and identically distributed with mean one and
X_{A}/\mu_{A}=\sum_{i\in A}(\mu_{i}/\mu_{A})Z_{i}
. Fix
\emptyset\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
. Because the exposures
(\mu_{i})_{i\in[n]}
take only the two values
a
and
b
with
a<b
, comparing decreasing-order partial sums shows that
(\mu_{i}/\mu_{B})_{i\in B}\in\mathbb{R}^{B}
is a convex combination of permutations of the vector
p\in\mathbb{R}^{B}
with
p_{i}=\mu_{i}/\mu_{A}
for
i\in A
and
p_{i}=0
for
i\in B\setminus A
. Exchangeability of the
Z_{i}
then gives, via Jensen’s inequality,
X_{B}/\mu_{B}\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X_{A}/\mu_{A}
, which is the normalized convex-order condition (
16
). Theorem
8
delivers the PMAS conclusion.

For the second claim, fix
j\in[n]\setminus\{1\}
and let
A=\{1,j\}
. The pair
(aZ_{1},bZ_{j})
has independent
\mathrm{Gamma}
marginals with common shape
\alpha
but distinct rates
\alpha/a
and
\alpha/b
, so the conditional density of
aZ_{1}/X_{A}
given
X_{A}=s
is proportional to
t^{\alpha-1}(1-t)^{\alpha-1}\exp(-\alpha st(b-a)/(ab))
on
(0,1)
and depends on
s
when
a\neq b
. Consequently
s\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{j}\mid X_{A}=s]
is strictly increasing in
s
and not proportional to
s
, so the proportional conditional-mean identity (
18
) fails on
A
. For coherent risk measures, the dual-pricing cost-share difference is
C_{j}^{\star}(A)-C_{j}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(A)=\mathbb{E}[\pi_{A}^{\star}r(X_{A})]
, where
r(s):=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{j}\mid X_{A}=s]-bs/(a+b)
. The residual has mean zero but is not monotone; instead
r(s)/s=\mathbb{E}[1-aZ_{1}/X_{A}\mid X_{A}=s]-b/(a+b)
is nondecreasing in
s
, because the conditional law of
aZ_{1}/X_{A}
given
X_{A}=s
shifts toward smaller values as
s
increases. Thus
r
has a single sign change, say at
s_{0}
, and for
\pi_{A}^{\star}=g(X_{A})
nondecreasing,
\mathbb{E}[\pi_{A}^{\star}r(X_{A})]=\mathbb{E}[(g(X_{A})-g(s_{0}))r(X_{A})]>0
under the stated nonconstant selection. Hence dual pricing charges the high-exposure agent strictly more than the proportional-cost rule, and the two rules differ on
A
.
∎

5\to 1\to 3\to 2\to 4

\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
pricing, agent 5 always receives zero welfare gain: since
\zeta_{5}=0

w_{5}(A)=\rho_{5}(0)-\rho_{h_{\wedge}^{A}}(0)=0
for every coalition
A\subseteq[5]
containing agent 5. When agent 2 enters the coalition
\{1,3,5\}

1.5695
to
1.7772

\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
pricing allocates welfare through the change in the lower-envelope distortion: a new entrant gains welfare when its distortion becomes the cheapest on layers of its own endowment but leaves the incumbents’ valuations essentially unchanged, whereas incumbents gain as well when the entrant lowers the envelope on layers that are active in their own endowments. The final step, when agent 4 enters, has the latter form: the lower envelope shifts on layers that matter for several players, so agents 1, 2, and 3 all gain in addition to agent 4. 0.6862
to
1.1114

0.6862
to
0.3292
to
0.1947

0.2567

\mathbb{Q}^{\star}

6

A=\{5\}
and
A=\{1,5\}

\{1,5\}

\{1,3\}

These two tables display only two of the
5!=120
possible maximal chains. PMAS is stronger than monotonicity along maximal chains, since it must also handle inclusions where several agents enter at once; maximal chains alone cover only single-entrant steps. Exhaustively checking the coalition-wise Shapley rule across all
120
permutations shows that only four chains are entry-monotone for incumbents:
2\to 5\to 3\to 1\to 4
,
2\to 5\to 3\to 4\to 1
,
5\to 2\to 3\to 1\to 4
, and
5\to 2\to 3\to 4\to 1
. These four chains have the same ordering structure: among the agents with nonzero endowments, the monotone Shapley orders are
2\to 3\to(1,4)
, while the riskless entrant 5 may be inserted only in one of the two early positions, namely before
2
or between
2
and
3
. In particular, agent 5 cannot enter last in any entry-monotone Shapley chain: for instance, along
2\to 3\to 1\to 4\to 5
, the final entry of agent 5 lowers the Shapley payoffs of agents 2 and 3 from
0.4980
and
0.3841
to
0.3207
and
0.2948
. Nor can agent 5 enter after agent 3, since already along
2\to 3\to 5\to 1\to 4
the entry of 5 lowers agent 2’s payoff from
0.1372
to
0.1299
. This ordering is not dictated by a simple theorem that ranks distortions alone, nor by marginal risks alone; rather, it is a consequence of the full cooperative game generated by the chosen marginals and distortions through the Shapley average-marginal-contribution formula. In this example, entry monotonicity is preserved only when agents 2 and 3 enter before agents 1 and 4, with agent 5 inserted before 2 or between 2 and 3; other orderings reassign average marginal contributions away from incumbents and violate monotonicity. \{2,3,5\}
has formed, either agent 1 or agent 4 may enter next without reducing an incumbent’s payoff. Table

5\to 2\to 3\to 1\to 4

6
, the Shapley row is outside the core at
\{1,2,3,5\}
and at
[5]
. Hence, coalition-wise Shapley entry monotonicity holds only for these four permutations, and compatibility with the core along an entire chain never holds; the total number of permutations for which the coalition-wise Shapley allocations form a sequential PMAS is zero.
