Hybrid close-up model for active face liveness
Authors: Bruno Kamarowski, Raul Almeida, Bernardo Biesseck, Roger Granada, Luiz Coelho, David Menotti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFace Liveness Detection, also known as Face Anti-Spoofing (FAS), plays a critical role in facial authentication technology by detecting attempts from malicious users to impersonate others or hide their own identity. Within this field, active liveness detection focuses on analyzing both the input signal and user behavior during a designated task to verify the authenticity of the presented face. Although researchers have made substantial progress in FAS, active liveness detection still holds significant potential for improvement. The limited availability of public datasets suitable for active liveness tasks reflects this opportunity, as it often forces researchers to develop and evaluate new solutions using in-house data that cannot be shared due to its sensitive nature, which makes some obtained results irreproducible and significantly limits mutual contribution within the scientific community. In this paper, we introduce a new FAS dataset, UFPR-Close-Up, acquired while users performed close-up movements during active interactions. Instructions for obtaining it for research purposes only are available at https://web.inf.ufpr.br/vri/databases/ufpr-closeup/. The dataset includes genuine samples from volunteer subjects and spoof samples generated using selected face images from the datasets CelebA and CelebV, displayed through several presentation attack instruments. Additionally, we propose a new active spoof detector that combines distortion features with spatial embeddings, achieving an ACER of 4.33% under the full-data protocol and consistently outperforming all recently proposed active liveness models based on similar interaction across all four evaluation protocols of the dataset.Keywords: Face liveness, Active dataset, Close-up challengeSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Ensuring user authenticity is fundamental for various systems, including mobile applications, financial services, border control, and access to secure facilities. These systems typically rely on identity verification to prevent unauthorized access and protect sensitive information. Face Anti-Spoofing (FAS) methods work as a preliminary step in such a process by confirming the liveness of the presented face, that is, differentiating real faces (indicative of legitimate access) from fake ones (signifying potential impersonation attacks)1–5. This distinction is vital to prevent attackers from using presentation attack instruments (PAIs), such as photos or videos, to impersonate another person (victim) in a presentation attack (PA). FAS is also known in the literature as Facial Liveness Detection or Face Presentation Attack Detection (Face PAD)6.

FAS can be broadly categorized into two types: active and passive methods. Active methods require users to perform specific actions or gestures during authentication to confirm their real presence. In contrast, passive methods detect signs of vitality from facial images or videos without requiring any explicit user interaction. Active face liveness detection encompasses a variety of user interactions ranging from involuntary physiological signals to facial or head movements. Some methods may introduce external information, such as light patterns displayed on the user’s screen7–9, or inaudible sounds to observe the response and determine whether it interacts with a live face or a PAI10–13. A more intrusive approach prompts the user to perform voluntary actions, such as smiling, nodding, or blinking14,15. These techniques depend on the successful completion of challenges while extracting dynamic information, such as 3D facial properties, as the user’s face moves relative to the camera16.

In recent years, passive FAS methods have shifted from handcrafted features to learned feature maps, which require extensive data for training. Although many public datasets support the development and evaluation of passive methods, active liveness detection research often relies on proprietary, internal datasets. The lack of publicly available active datasets makes it difficult to achieve fair comparisons between approaches and makes the reproduction of experiments challenging. Furthermore, previous studies that address the described active interaction, such as Face Close-up17 and Camera Close-up16, have also been constrained by datasets with an insufficient number of subjects or controlled environments that fail to reflect real world conditions. To address these challenges, we constructed a rich active liveness dataset named UFPR-Close-Up exploring the close-up challenge, consisting of 714 live videos, from 382 volunteer subjects, and 1847 spoof videos from 661 subjects. Live videos were recorded by volunteers with their own smartphones under a wide range of variables, such as camera model, background, ethnicity, gender, and lighting conditions. Spoof videos were collected in indoor and outdoor settings, featuring 1043 unique identities and five different PAIs. See Data availability section for further details on how to obtain the UFPR-Close-Up datasets.

In the first phase of the close-up challenge, users must position their face within a small designated area on the screen, maintaining a specified distance from the device. After briefly holding this position, the second phase begins, prompting the user to move closer to the device and align their face within a larger area on the screen. Moreover, the few studies that explore the challenge-response approach based on close-ups do not directly incorporate raw image information into their models. Instead, they typically rely only on extracting handcrafted features from a few frames of the input media. In light of this opportunity, we propose a new active FAS method “Hybrid Close-Up” by combining the well-known distortion features with global embeddings extracted from sampled frames. The proposed method outperforms both baselines across all four evaluation protocols. On Protocol I (all PAIs and devices seen during training), it achieves an ACER of 4.33%, compared to 9.46% for Camera Close-Up and 12.28% for Face Close-Up. On the generalization protocols (II, III, and IV), the proposed method continues to achieve the lowest ACER in all scenarios, demonstrating superior robustness to unseen PAIs, unknown attack types, and cross-device variation.

Datasets for passive face liveness

To advance research on passive liveness detection, the community has created various datasets over the years, focusing not only on the number of individuals and attacks, but also on the variety of scenarios, lighting, camera quality, and diversity of biometrical features such as gender, age and ethnicity.

One of the first is the NUAA Imposter Database18, which contains images of print attacks from 15 subjects captured under controlled indoor conditions. Despite its limited variability in terms of lighting, resolution, and spoof types, it laid the foundation for early studies. The PRINT-ATTACK19 dataset introduced photo attack videos recorded in a lab setting using webcams, enabling reproducible evaluation of print-based spoofing. CASIA-FASD20 expanded the diversity of attack types, including print, cut photo, and replay attacks under varying imaging qualities, making it a widely used benchmark. Replay-Attack21 further enriched the evaluation landscape with high-resolution print and replay videos under different lighting setups, accompanied by standardized protocols. MSU-MFSD22 introduced mobile device scenarios, offering print and replay attacks captured using webcams and smartphones in realistic conditions. Similarly, MSU-USSA23 focused on smartphone-based authentication with a larger subject pool and device diversity. To address 3D mask attacks, the MLFP24 dataset provided multispectral videos of latex mask presentations. Oulu-NPU25 simulated mobile usage environments, capturing 4950 videos across varied lighting and background conditions with multiple attack types and four evaluation protocols.

The SiW26 dataset introduced more naturalistic variations in pose, expression, and illumination, while its successor SiW-M27 significantly increased attack diversity and supported generalized and zero-shot learning through a unified evaluation protocol. HQ-WMCA28 offered multimodal recordings including RGB, depth, and IR data, enabling the study of high-fidelity 3D and material based attacks. The DMAD29 dataset emphasized spatial and temporal spoofing cues by combining RGB and depth modalities across multiple spoof types. CelebA-Spoof30, a large-scale dataset derived from CelebA, contains over 625,000 annotated images and supports attribute-aware and data-rich FAS model training. Most recently, the Wild Face Anti-Spoofing (WFAS)31 dataset introduced over 1.3 million unconstrained images with diverse spoof types and conditions, providing a large-scale benchmark for evaluating generalization in real-world scenarios. Passive facial liveness methods

Over the years, FAS approaches have transitioned from detecting simple handcrafted features to automatically learning feature maps. Among the earlier strategies, researchers used Fisher vector encoding on features extracted from different color spaces for FAS, as well as texture features based on Local Binary Patterns and their variations for classification21,32. Such low-level features, describing pixel neighborhood relationships, are powerful and useful in other security-related tasks, such as image tamper detection and localization33, and also in medical image classification for breast cancer detection34.

Some recent methods combine handcrafted features with those extracted by deep neural networks35,36 in a hybrid manner. Traditional deep learning-based methods use end-to-end Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) to learn a direct mapping from face image to liveness label (live or spoof), relying on global37 or pixelwise38 supervision to train the model.

Generalized deep learning methods take a step further, aiming to be robust against domain changes (e.g., variations in the input sensor or attack types). These methods may focus on domain generalization, where a model is trained only once39,40; adaptation, where the model undergoes an adaptation algorithm that leverages test data41,42; or generalization to unseen attack types using zero / few-shot strategies, where none or only a small amount of data is available for training. Anomaly detection is also successfully employed27, in which the model learns an accurate representation of live samples instead of learning the characteristics of spoofs.

Active facial liveness methods

Unlike passive methods, active methods depend on user interaction for liveness detection. They can be classified into two main groups: those based on injected information and those based on voluntary interaction.

In methods based on injected data, additional information is introduced during media capture, and the signal response is recorded. In this strategy, live faces are expected to reflect different patterns than spoofs. Light pattern emissions have been used10 together with a CNN for facial depth map recovery and liveness classification, with a regression branch that performs light CAPTCHA checking to detect the injected pattern in the user’s face and eyes11. Another work focuses on emitting sound signals while the user performs a simple task. By analyzing the echo response, 3D facial geometry properties are extracted and fed to an SVM classifier for final prediction13.

In systems that rely on user cooperation, also known as challenge-response systems, individuals are instructed to perform simple actions. For instance, one might be required to follow a displayed pattern with their eyes or focus on a designated point on the screen43,44. Failure to complete the challenge or a performance with suspicious patterns, might result in classification as spoofed. Another approach involves pronouncing a randomized sequence of words, with liveness detection based on the consistency between mouth and facial movements and the corresponding audio sample45–47. Facial three-dimensionality can also be verified using projective invariants derived from a sequence of head movements48.

Regarding the close-up challenge, the Face Close-Up17 method selects a reference frame and a set of additional frames from an input video, based on the relative face size compared to the entire image. It computes facial landmarks for each selected frame and generates feature vectors from the distances between landmark pairs. Each feature vector is then normalized using a reference vector derived from the distances in the reference frame. The resulting vectors are stacked into a matrix and used as input to a CNN. Camera Close-Up16 builds upon this approach by introducing a bin-based frame selection strategy and modifying the CNN architecture, while retaining the matrix of landmark-based feature vectors. These adjustments are sufficient to outperform the original method on their own collected dataset. To the best of our knowledge, these are the most recently published techniques addressing the close-up challenge for active liveness classification.

The existing close-up methods share notable limitations. The Face Close-Up dataset contains only 71 subjects recorded in controlled environments, which may not reflect real-world variability. The Camera Close-Up dataset is even smaller, comprising 41 subjects. Crucially, neither work discloses its data publicly, preventing reproduction and fair cross-study comparison. Furthermore, both methods rely exclusively on handcrafted distortion features derived from facial landmarks, without incorporating raw image information. This limits their ability to exploit appearance-level cues that may be discriminative in more complex or less constrained scenarios.

UFPR close-up dataset

The UFPR-Close-Up dataset contains 714 live videos from 382 volunteer subjects and 1847 spoof videos from 661 subjects (1043 unique identities). It covers 5 types of presentation attacks (Photo, Display, Replay, Scaling, Mask) across 10 distinct Presentation Attack Instruments (PAIs). Spoof images were sourced from the publicly available CelebA dataset. The dataset was collected under ethics approval (CAAE 62899722.6.0000.0102, Plataforma Brasil) and all participants provided written informed consent. The dataset is available upon request for research purposes, and instructions are presented in section Data availability.

Due to the lack of public datasets for active liveness detection, we propose the UFPR-Close-Up Database, which contains short video recordings of both live and spoof attempts of subjects performing the close-up challenge. Our goal is to introduce the first database for active interaction, establish challenging protocols, and provide a common benchmark for evaluating the performance of active approaches.

To this end, we developed a dedicated mobile application (app), for Android and iOS, enabling the participants to collect their live samples using their smartphones (Project approved by the Ethics Committee Board from the Health Science Sector of the Federal University of Paraná, Brazil–Process CAAE 62899722.6.0000.0102, registered in the Plataforma Brazil system–https://plataformabrasil.saude.gov.br/). The same app was also used to build the spoof samples. We confirm that all methods were carried out following relevant guidelines and regulations by the Ethics Committee Board from the Health Science Sector of the Federal University of Paraná. Furthermore, we confirm that an informed consent form has been obtained from all subjects, and we do not store any data that could be used to identify the subject. We confirm that all images presented in this paper were extracted from the UFPR-Close-Up dataset and that we have permission to publish these images in an open-access journal.

Recordings were made with the front camera at 20 FPS. In the following, we describe the close-up challenge, the acquisition process for live and spoof samples, and the evaluation protocols.

Close-up challenge

The challenge consists of four sequential steps: (i) Distant Align, the user positions their face inside a small ellipse centered on the screen; (ii) Hold Distant, the user remains still for at least one second; (iii) Close Align, the user moves closer and fits their face within a larger ellipse; (iv) Hold Close, the user remains still for at least one second. The ellipses are defined relative to the screen width W: the distant ellipse has width 0.5W and height 1.3 × 0.5W; the close ellipse has width 0.95W and height 1.3 × 0.95W. Alignment is verified in real time via IoU between the detected face bounding box and the ellipse, with thresholds of ≥ 0.80 to confirm alignment and ≥ 0.65 to confirm hold. Collection of live samples

All live samples were recorded by volunteers themselves, who agreed to participate in our data collection, with their own personal device, thus constituting a wide range of camera models. To emulate a realistic scenario, no additional instructions were provided on how to perform the close-up challenge.

Subjects were asked to record at least two sessions, with a minimum interval of 12 hours between them, though additional sessions were allowed. This approach ensured that the dataset contains live samples from the same individual under varying conditions, primarily due to differences in lighting and background, as shown in Fig. 3.

Examples of live subjects in two different sessions.

Collection of spoof samples

Spoof samples were recorded using publicly available images from the CelebA dataset, which we call “spoof targets”. They were displayed on six Android devices (Xiaomi Redmi Note 13, Moto G54, Samsung Galaxy S22FE, Samsung Galaxy S23, Samsung Galaxy A54, Samsung Galaxy A34) and three iOS devices (iPhone 8, iPhone 12, iPhone 14). This way, some spoof samples may present some unusual characteristics for an authentication process, such as non-frontalized body posture and non-neutral facial expressions.

To avoid large discrepancies in body pose between live and spoof samples, which could introduce undesired bias and lead to inflated classification rates, spoof targets were selected based on the distribution of yaw and pitch face angles observed in the live samples. This was done by randomly selecting 10 frames from each live recorded video and computing their average yaw and pitch angles, which produced the distributions and . A relaxed interval for spoof targets was considered. Spoof target candidates outside the intervals [-max(), max()] and [-max(), max()] were discarded. The remaining images were randomly selected to maintain the same gender proportion (men and women) observed in the live samples. Protocols

The dataset is divided into training, validation, and testing subsets with an approximate ratio of 3:1:1. Each subset contains a proportional distribution of gender, different identities, and attack type. Additionally, each subject appears only once in our dataset to avoid biased results caused by memorization of identity facial cues.

To evaluate the generalization capabilities of active liveness algorithms in scenarios with slight domain shifts on the UFPR-Close-up database, four protocols are proposed:

Protocol I: evaluates the ability to learn and generalize features in a scenario with minimal domain shift. All types of PA and PAI are present in the training, validation, and testing sets. This protocol is designed to provide an overall result of the proposed PAD on the dataset and to serve as a baseline for comparison with the results of the following protocols.

Protocol I: evaluates the ability to learn and generalize features in a scenario with minimal domain shift. All types of PA and PAI are present in the training, validation, and testing sets. This protocol is designed to provide an overall result of the proposed PAD on the dataset and to serve as a baseline for comparison with the results of the following protocols.

Protocol II: evaluates the robustness of the proposed PAD against unseen PAI. Each PAI produces a specific noise pattern when recorded by a digital camera. The train set contains only attacks with flat paper, monitor, and hanger masks. The validation and test sets are composed by wrapped paper, TV, and cut-out.

Protocol III: similar to Protocol II, it evaluates the ability to generalize learned features to unseen attacks. The training set contains photo, display, and scaling PAs, while the validation and testing sets contain only replay and mask attacks.

Hybrid close-up method

In addition to the created dataset, we propose a new active liveness detection method named “Hybrid Close-Up”, which extracts features from faces performing the close-up movement to classify them as live or spoof. Our approach was inspired by the Camera Close-Up liveness detector16, sharing similarities in the creation of distortion feature vectors. It merges distortion feature embeddings, extracted from facial landmarks, based on the concept of projective invariants50, with global face embeddings in a fusion model.

Our Hybrid Close-Up method is composed of three modules: Frame Selection, Feature Extraction, and Classification. Figure 6 provides an overview of the method, and each module is described in the followings.

Frame selection

The first module is responsible for selecting some frames from input video, which will be used in the next step. This process is represented by the yellow box in Fig. 6. In this work, we employ the MMOD CNN method51 for face detection and an ensemble of regression trees52 for landmark detection. Both methods were used by the mentioned baselines for similar tasks. First, the face detector is used to discard frames without a face. Next, from each valid frame, the facial landmarks are extracted and the distortion features are computed by calculating the Euclidean distances between all landmark pairs, excluding the distances between landmarks in the mouth region. Feature extraction

This module is responsible for both processing the distortion feature vectors and also extracting texture features of the N selected frames, which are combined at the end. The distortion feature vectors are reorganized into an N × M matrix by stacking each vector on top of the previous one, which serves as input to a distortion feature encoder, defined in Table 4, to compute a global distortion embedding for the input video. Texture features are computed using a CNN encoder, which can automatically learns low, intermediate, and high-level discriminative features to produce an embedding of length E. Specifically, we employed the ResNet54 architecture of different sizes, which has been successfully applied to various computer vision tasks. It is important to clarify that neither encoder in the proposed method operates as an autoencoder. An autoencoder learns a latent representation by reconstructing its input in an unsupervised manner, which is not the case here. Instead, the distortion feature encoder is a 1D CNN that maps the matrix of pairwise landmark distances to a fixed-size embedding, and the texture encoder is a ResNet that maps each selected frame to a visual embedding. Both are trained end-to-end in a supervised binary classification setting (live vs. spoof), without any reconstruction objective. All the N texture embeddings are then merged using a bottleneck structure based on learned maps defined in Table 5, producing a global texture embedding of length M, equal to that global distortion embedding, for the input video.

Classification

The hybrid model integrates geometric cues (distortion features) and appearance cues (texture embeddings) through feature-level concatenation. No temporal modeling (e.g., recurrent networks or attention over time) is employed; instead, temporal diversity is addressed upstream at the frame selection stage, where the FPS algorithm selects the N most dissimilar frames. This design choice allows the classification module to operate on a fixed-size, order-invariant representation of the video.

Experiments

This section presents and discusses the experimental results obtained by evaluating our proposed method, Hybrid Close-Up, using various configurations and scenarios. All experiments were conducted on a single machine equipped with the following GPUs: NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 and NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090.

We followed the distortion feature extraction steps outlined in the Camera Close-Up method16 using the Python implementation of the Dlib library55 for face detection and extraction of 68 landmarks. We ignore the distances between the 10 mouth region landmarks, as for both live and spoof instances these landmarks were overlapping, resulting in zero distances and adding little to no discriminative information. Thus, the distortion feature vector has a length M = 2088.

Note that the Face Close-Up17 authors claim to use 66 landmarks but do not specify the landmark extractor employed. Therefore, their distortion feature vector extraction routine was adapted to use 68 landmarks. This adaptation was implemented by using all pairwise distances between the extracted landmarks.

We implemented the Camera Close-Up16 and Face Close-Up17 methods as baselines, based on the descriptions available in their respective papers, using the PyTorch library. All hyperparameters and other parameters from these published works (e.g., number of selected frames) were set according to their original papers. Our proposed method was trained using the ADAM optimizer, with a learning rate of 0.001, a batch size of 20, and early stopping with the patience value set to 30 epochs. Additionally, we employed the binary cross-entropy loss function. All experiments were performed over the protocols of the proposed dataset UFPR Close-Up Dataset.

We hypothesize that the improved performance of FPS-based frame selection stems from its sampling strategy, which prioritizes selecting the most dissimilar frames. This approach reduces redundancy and ensures that the active liveness detection model receives more diverse and informative data. As expected, the Bin-based approach achieved similar results. The underlying reason is comparable because it selects a fixed number of frames from each segment of a video. It promotes diversity across different portions of the input. However, it has a higher tendency to sample similar frames.

Furthermore, as discussed in Camera Close-Up work, selecting frames based on face size may not be a generalizable approach. This method relies on a uniform movement pattern across all samples to ensure that each video contains frames representing the desired face sizes. However, in less constrained scenarios, such uniformity is unlikely, limiting the effectiveness of this technique. In light of the presented results, we can observe that our proposed FPS-based fame selection approach is more efficient in sampling relevant data.

Conclusion

As mentioned previously, the scarcity of publicly available data suitable for developing and evaluating active liveness solutions, which are essential for ensuring reproducibility and fostering collaborative research, is one difficulties faced by active face liveness researchers. To address this issue, we focus on creating a new active FAS dataset named “UFPR Close-Up”, which contains subjects performing close-up interactions. We hope to contribute to the field by establishing a common dataset and a set of evaluation protocols.

The conducted experiments demonstrate that the collected dataset poses significant challenges to recent active close-up methods proposed in the literature. While our proposed FAS method “Hybrid Close-Up” outperforms existing approaches in the evaluated scenarios by merging distortion and texture features, it still struggles with generalization issues, as observed in Protocols II, III, and IV.

Future research may explore some directions for improvement. One is to refine the frame selection process by incorporating image quality metrics to ensure more informative samples. Another is to enhance the classification module by integrating strategies from passive PAD methods, such as auxiliary tasks like depth map estimation. Experiments may also be performed with our FAS method “Hybrid Close-Up” to increase the security of authentication face recognition systems using embedded low-resolution cameras, such as the one proposed by Venkata et al.56 with an ESP32-CAM.

Furthermore, future studies could also explore other active interactions. One example is the head movement challenge-response method16, where users rotate their heads toward prompted positions. Another is the flash challenge, which projects varying light patterns onto the user’s face to reveal live cues such as depth and shadow projections11.
