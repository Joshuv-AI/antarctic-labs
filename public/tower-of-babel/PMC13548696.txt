Development of a visualized calculation nomogram for comorbid depression and anxiety symptoms among nurses based on second victim experience and support-A cross-sectional study
Authors: Ke Zhang, ZhiHui Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis cross-sectional study aimed to explore factors associated with comorbid depression and anxiety symptoms (CADS) among nurses who had experienced adverse medical events, and to develop a visualized calculation nomogram for estimating the probability of CADS in individuals.MethodsA total of 2,457 nurses from the Northeastern Three Provinces China were enrolled based on Wenjuanxing from March to July 2024. Depression and anxiety were evaluated using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), and CADS was defined using prespecified clinical thresholds (PHQ-9 ≥ 10 and GAD-7 ≥ 7). Latent class analysis (LCA) was conducted to identify subgroups of second victim experience and support (SVES). Multivariate logistic regression was performed to screen independent associated factors, and a visualized calculation nomogram was constructed and internally validated using a 70/30 split sample and bootstrap resampling.Results40% of the nurses exhibited CADS. Four classes were identified: “Low SVE and Low Support” (21.6%), “Low SVE and High Support” (10.2%), “Moderate SVE and Moderate Support” (50.4%), and “High SVE and Low Support” (17.8%). Independent risk factors for CADS included Moderate/High SVE with corresponding Support, age 30 ~ 40 years and >40 years, ≥3 weekly coffee drinks, weekly working hours 45 ~ 50 and >50 hours, and ≥6 monthly night shifts. Protective factors included psychological detachment, psychological empowerment, weekly physical exercise, and monthly income ≥ 8001 CNY. Internal evaluation of the nomogram showed good discriminative ability (AUC = 0.734), calibration, and clinical applicability, and the validation cohort confirmed robust performance (AUC = 0.938) with excellent calibration and clinical value.ConclusionsCADS is prevalent among nurses. This study provides a user-friendly visualized calculation nomogram to help non-specialists quickly estimate individual probabilities of CADS in a cross-sectional sample.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Nomogram, Comorbid depression and anxiety symptoms, Second victim experience and support, Psychological empowerment, Latent class analysis, Nurses

Introduction

By the end of 2023, China had reached a total of 5.63 million registered nurses, with approximately 4 registered nurses per 1,000 population [1]. As frontline healthcare workers, nurses face heavy workloads, prolonged working hours, irregular sleep patterns, medical disputes, and potential exposure to traumatic events, patient deaths, and public health emergencies-all of which significantly increase their psychological burdens. Particularly under the intensified demand for healthcare services driven by population aging, nurses’ mental health challenges are further exacerbated [2]. Among various physical and mental health disorders, anxiety and depression stand out as the most common psychiatric conditions, often coexisting with other psychological or physical symptoms and disorders.

Comorbid Anxiety and Depressive Symptoms (CADS) refer to the simultaneous presence of both depressive and anxiety symptoms in an individual. Depression manifests as symptoms such as low mood, loss of interest, feelings of guilt and worthlessness, disruptions in sleep and appetite, and reduced energy and concentration. International data indicate that the prevalence of depression among nurses ranges from 3.8% to 60.5% [3–5]. In contrast, data from China suggest a generally higher prevalence of depression among nurses, ranging from 43.8% to 86.7% [6, 7]. Anxiety, often characterized by fear and restlessness accompanied by fatigue, irritability, and palpitations, has shown varying prevalence rates among nurses in international studies, spanning 28% to 85% depending on measurement methods [8]. A Chinese study revealed that 55.85% of nurses experience some degree of anxiety, with 58.82% and 62.08% of shift-working nurses suffering from depression and anxiety, respectively [9]. Previous studies across diverse populations highlight that depression and anxiety are two prevalent mental disorders that frequently coexist [10–12]. Compared to those with isolated symptoms, individuals with CADS exhibit more severe manifestations of both conditions, along with poorer quality of life, sleep disturbances, and impaired social functioning [13, 14]. However, the vast majority of existing research on nurses’ mental health treats depression and anxiety as independent, parallel emotional issues, with minimal focus on CADS among this population.

Nurses’ occupational mental health is crucial for healthcare system stability. Chronic exposure to high workloads not only compromises their well-being but may also increase clinical errors, impair teamwork, and ultimately jeopardize patient safety. Patient safety incidents not only impact patients’ physical and mental health but also harm the healthcare professionals involved, turning them into “second victims”. Approximately 1 in 10 patients is harmed during healthcare delivery, with over 3 million deaths annually attributed to unsafe care. In low- and middle-income countries, 4 out of every 100 individuals die due to unsafe medical practices [15]. When adverse medical events occur, nurses as frontline responders frequently experience the “second victim” phenomenon-enduring not only the immediate psychological trauma but also profound professional repercussions including guilt, anxiety, and identity crises. In 2022, the European Researchers’ Network Working on Second Victims expanded the scope of the term “second victim” beyond Scott’s [16] original definition (healthcare professionals suffering psychological or physical trauma after medical errors or patient harm), broadening it in three dimensions: affected personnel, scope of incidents, and consequences. They emphasized that second victims include any healthcare worker who were directly or indirectly involved in unintended adverse events, medical errors, or patient harm, and thus experienced negative psychological or professional impacts [17]. A survey revealed that 47.8% of nurses experience second victim syndrome during their careers [18]. Furthermore, studies indicate that women and nurses are disproportionately affected by second victimization and are more likely to endure intense negative emotions [19].

After experiencing patient safety incidents, healthcare providers may develop physical symptoms such as sleep disturbances, elevated heart rate, increased blood pressure, and rapid breathing, alongside psychological and social symptoms like anxiety, regret, depression, burnout, self-doubt, and compassion fatigue-collectively termed Second Victim Syndrome (SVS) [20]. A survey of 2,897 Chinese nurses regarding their experiences and support as second victims revealed that nurses predominantly face “psychological distress” [21]. The Global Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030 recommends ensuring sustained psychological and other support for patients, their families, and healthcare workers (second victims) following severe patient safety incidents [22]. However, most healthcare institutions worldwide lack structured support interventions [23]. A study of Spanish healthcare workers found that only 43.0% received emotional support after being involved in patient safety incidents [24]. When addressing adverse events, healthcare institutions often prioritize meeting patient needs and assigning accountability to those involved (second victims), frequently adopting dismissive, silent, or even punitive attitudes toward them. Due to insufficient organizational focus on supporting second victims, healthcare providers-especially nurses-are more likely to endure prolonged negative experiences without timely and effective institutional protection. Workplace psychological factors have been identified as significant contributors to nurses’ depression and anxiety. However, no studies have yet explored the relationship between nurses’ Second Victim Experiences and Support (SVES) and CADS, leaving a critical gap in understanding how institutional responses to adverse events shape mental health outcomes.

Clinical prediction models (CPMs) are statistical tools that evaluate the association between multidimensional health data and the likelihood of clinical outcomes [25, 26]. These models integrate individual characteristics, psychological factors, and social indicators to quantify the probability of current health conditions. Among various CPM approaches, nomograms have become widely used in psychological research due to their superior intuitiveness and practicability [27–29]. A nomogram is a graphical calculation tool that converts complex multivariable regression models (e.g., logistic regression) into an easy-to-read visual system using proportionally scaled axes. This design allows researchers and healthcare providers without specialized statistical expertise to quickly estimate individual-level probabilities by assigning points and summing scores, avoiding reliance on professional software or complex calculations [30–32].

Nomograms can simultaneously incorporate multidimensional factors, including physiological, psychological, and social indicators, and clearly display the relative contribution of each variable. Such features make them highly valuable for simplifying interpretation of multivariable analyses. In mental health research, nomograms have been increasingly applied to studies of depression and anxiety to visualize factor strengths and improve result interpretability [33–35].

However, most CPMs rely on sophisticated regression formulas that are difficult for non-statisticians to understand and apply. A nomogram overcomes this barrier by serving as a user-friendly visualized calculator, transforming abstract statistical results into a straightforward graphical tool. To date, few studies have developed a nomogram for CADS among nurses with second victim experience and support. Therefore, this study aimed to explore factors associated with CADS and to develop a visualized calculation nomogram that enables simple, intuitive estimation of individual probabilities for CADS.

Material and methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional, association-focused model-development study based on WenJuanXing (https://www.wjx.cn/) between March and July 2024, covering three northeastern provinces of China (Liaoning, Heilongjiang, and Jilin). Self-administered questionnaires were distributed to hospital nurses via an online link on their work platform. To minimize duplicate responses, we implemented IP address filtering and unique identifier checks to ensure only one submission per participant. The sampling frame included nurses from multiple hospitals within the three provinces; the denominator comprised all eligible nurses invited to participate, and the response rate is reported accordingly. This study was reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Prognosis (TRIPOD) checklist [36].

Generalizability statement: Participants were enrolled based on an inclusion criterion of prior adverse-event exposure within the past year. Given that adverse-event exposure is common among hospital nurses in clinical practice, the study population with prior adverse-event exposure represents a large and clinically relevant subgroup. Nevertheless, as enrollment was restricted to nurses with a history of recent adverse events, findings are generalizable mainly to this subgroup and may not be directly extended to nurses without such experience. Potential clustering by hospital or clinical unit was considered in study design and interpretation.

Sample size calculation

Sample size for multivariable model development was based on a minimum of 20 events per predictor variable (EPV) to ensure stable coefficient estimation, consistent with established guidelines for logistic regression [37]. Considering the number of candidate variables and expected outcome prevalence, a target sample size exceeding 1000 participants was determined to be adequate. A total of 2457 nurses were included. The sample was randomly split into a model-development cohort (70%, n = 1719) and a hold-out internal validation cohort (30%, n = 738). Bootstrap resampling was performed within the development cohort for further internal validation. Notably, both split-sample validation and bootstrap validation represent internal performance assessment only; no external validation was performed. Model performance estimates should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution regarding overfitting and generalizability.

Inclusion criteria: age > 18 years; voluntary participation; holding a valid nursing qualification certificate; experience of medical adverse events within the previous year (a defining inclusion criterion that restricts generalizability).

Exclusion criteria: pre-existing diagnosis of depression; other mental disorders or personality disorders; psychological treatment within the past year.

Measurement

Demographic and working characteristics

We used a self-filled questionnaire, including age, marital status (“single”, “married”), education level (“junior college”, “undergraduate or above”), weekly physical exercise, weekly coffee drinking, working years, title (“nurse”, “senior nurse”, “nurse-in-charge”, “vice professor or above”), department (“clinical”, “non clinical”), monthly income (1 CNY≈ 0.14 USD), employment method (“formal incorporation”, “contract nurses”, “labor dispatching”), weekly working hours (“≤44”, “45–50”, “>50”), and monthly night shifts.

Depression and anxiety

Depression was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), and anxiety was assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) [38, 39]. The PHQ‑9 comprises 9 items and the GAD‑7 comprises 7 items, each measuring symptom frequency over the past two weeks. Responses were rated on a 4‑point Likert scale (0 = not at all; 1 = several days; 2 = more than half the days; 3 = nearly every day). Total scores range from 0 to 27 for the PHQ‑9 and 0 to 21 for the GAD‑7. CADS were defined as the co‑occurrence of clinically significant depressive symptoms (PHQ‑9 ≥ 10) and clinically significant anxiety symptoms (GAD‑7 ≥ 7) [38, 39]. This prespecified, clinically interpretable threshold was used as the binary outcome variable in all analyses. In the present study, Cronbach’s α values were 0.954 for the PHQ‑9 and 0.970 for the GAD‑7, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Second victim experience and support

The Chinese version of the Second Victim Experience and Support Tool (CVEST), revised by Chen, was used to assess second victim experience and support (SVES) [40]. The scale includes two main sections: the “second victim experience” domain (covering psychological distress, physical distress, and professional difficulties) and the “support” domain (including colleague support, managerial support, and support from family and friends). The scale consists of 24 items rated on a 5‑point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 24 to 120. Higher scores indicate greater self‑reported distress or perceived support. Cronbach’s α for the scale was 0.852 in this study. Latent class analysis (LCA) was performed to identify distinct response patterns and subgroups of nurses with differing levels of SVES.

Moral courage

The Nurse Moral Courage Scale (NMCS), developed by Numminen et al., was used to evaluate moral courage [41]. The scale contains 21 items across four dimensions: moral resilience, commitment to good care, compassion and empathy, and moral responsibility. Each item was rated on a 5‑point scale (1 = completely disagree; 5 = completely agree), with total scores ranging from 21 to 105. Higher scores reflect higher levels of self‑reported moral courage. Cronbach’s α for this scale was 0.983.

Psychological empowerment

The Psychological Empowerment Instrument, developed by Spreitzer, was used to measure psychological empowerment [42]. The instrument includes 12 items covering four dimensions: meaning of work, self‑efficacy, autonomy, and job impact. Responses were rated on a 5‑point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 12 to 60. Higher scores indicate greater perceived psychological empowerment. Cronbach’s α for the scale was 0.966.

Psychological detachment

Psychological detachment was assessed using the Recovery Experience Questionnaire developed by Sonnentag and Fritz [43]. The scale includes 4 items rated on a 5‑point scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 4 to 20. Higher scores indicate a greater ability to mentally disengage from work during off‑job hours. Cronbach’s α for the scale was 0.959.

Work value

The Work Value Scale, developed by Hou et al., was used to assess work values [44]. The scale contains 20 items rated on a 5‑point Likert scale (1 = very unimportant; 5 = very important), with total scores ranging from 20 to 100. Higher scores indicate stronger endorsement of the measured work values. Cronbach’s α for the scale was 0.979.

Statistical methods

First, Mplus 8.3 software was used to conduct LCA. The evaluation indicators for optimal model fitting include Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC), with smaller values indicating better fitting of the model, and higher entropy values (>0.8) indicated better model fit [45]. Additionally the Lo Mendel Rubin (LMR) and Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) were used to compare solutions with class k and solutions with class k-1, and statistically significant p-values showed an improvement in fit due to the inclusion of additional classes [46]. Second, statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26, encompassing chi-square tests and binary logistic regression. Third, the association nomogram was developed and internally validated in R (v4.1.2) using packages rms, ROCit, ResourceSelection, and rmda. Model discrimination was assessed by AUC and bootstrap resampling; calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and calibration plots; clinical utility was examined by decision curve analysis. A 70/30 sample split was used for internal validation only. All tests were two-tailed, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Establishment of the nomogram model

The nomogram model for CADS was constructed based on the logistic regression results presented in Table 4. As shown in Fig. 2, this nomogram allows us to assign corresponding points to each predictor, sum these points to obtain a total score, and map this score to the predicted probability of moderate to severe CADS among nurses.

Nomogram model for predicting the risk of Moderate and sever CADS. Notes: SVE, second victim experience

Internal evaluation of the nomogram model

Figure 3(A) demonstrated an AUC of 0.734 (95% CI: 0.710–0.757), confirming robust discriminative capacity. The Youden Index was 0.353, with an optimal nomogram prediction probability cutoff of 0.375, yielding model sensitivity (0.605), specificity (0.750), and accuracy (0.663). In Fig. 3(B), the calibration curve revealed strong agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, further supported by Hosmer-Lemeshow test results (χ2 = 9.8263, p = 0.277), indicating no significant deviation from ideal calibration. In Fig. 3(C), DCA of the prediction model showed a positive net benefit over the treat-all and treat-none strategies across a wide range of threshold probabilities from 0.00 to 0.70, indicating good clinical applicability.

The stability and accuracy of the nomogram model in training set. (A) ROC diagram of the nomogram model; (B) Calibration diagram of the nomogram model; (C) DCA curve diagram of the nomogram model

Validation of the nomogram model

The established nomogram enabled calculation of moderate-to-severe depression-anxiety probabilities in the validation cohort. Subsequent ROC, calibration, and decision curve analyses were derived from these probabilities. As shown in Fig. 4(A), the ROC curve demonstrated an AUC of 0.938 (95% CI: 0.921–0.955), and the Youden Index was 0.741, with an optimal prediction probability cutoff at 0.538, yielding sensitivity (0.845), specificity (0.896), and accuracy (0.875) values, confirming robust discriminative performance. The calibration plot in Fig. 4(B) revealed excellent agreement between predicted and observed outcomes (Hosmer-Lemeshow test: χ2 = 8.859, p = 0.354). In Fig. 4(C), DCA of the prediction model showed a positive net benefit over the treat-all and treat-none strategies across a wide range of threshold probabilities from 0.00 to 1, indicating good clinical applicability.

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that there is a common CADS (40.6%) among nurses, which is consistent with previous research [47]. Nurses, as frontline healthcare workers, are at risk of experiencing chronic CADS, which may negatively impact their physical health, including disturbances in sleep [4] and diet [48]. CADS can also lead to psychological issues, such as occupational stress [49], burnout [50], and may even exacerbate turnover intention [51]. These cross-sectional associations underscore the high relevance of mental health symptoms in this nurse subgroup and support the development of an association-based stratification tool for CADS. However, all findings are observational in nature and do not imply causal direction or predictive capacity.

Second victim experience and support and CADS

After experiencing a patient safety incident, nurses will go through different psychological experiences and feel different levels of support. Our study indicates that the SVES of nurses can be classified into four categories: Low SVE and Low Support (21.6%), Low SVE and High Support (10.2%), Moderate SVE and Moderate Support (50.4%), and High SVE and Low Support (17.8%). The risk of CADS among nurses in the “Moderate SVE and Moderate Support” group and the “High SVE and Low Support” group is 3.711 times and 5.596 times higher than that of nurses in the “Low SVE and High support” group, respectively. This study reveals that if nursing administrators neglect nurses’ psychological experiences as secondary victims in adverse events, it will lead to divergent categories of secondary victim experiences and support needs. Advanced secondary victimization has causal links to occupational burnout, post-traumatic stress disorder, and other conditions, severely impacting nurses’ physical and mental health and triggering psychological and emotional distress such as tension, distress, and anger, while exacerbating symptoms of CADS [52, 53]. After experiencing adverse clinical events, nurses who receive multilevel organizational and societal support systems-particularly high-level institutional support-can mitigate the psychological and emotional impacts of secondary victimization following patient safety incidents [54, 55]. To address this, the following recommendations are proposed: Establish specialized training and management programs for second victims, enabling them to share critical information about patient safety incidents, incident resolution strategies, and external support mechanisms; Develop a standardized, systematic, and professional support system for second victims, prioritizing preventive measures, timely post-event interventions, and long-term follow-up and improvement initiatives, thereby providing healthcare workers with comprehensive support and protection. These observations may inform the design of supportive initiatives for nurses with prior adverse event exposure, but cannot confirm intervention efficacy or causal pathways.

Job-related cognitions and recovery processes and CADS

Psychological empowerment comprises four cognitive dimensions: meaning of work, self-efficacy, impact of work, and autonomy [56]. As a key intrinsic motivator, it reflects individuals’ perceptions of organizational authority, is shaped by work environment and personality traits, and drives subsequent work behaviors. Employees with low psychological empowerment often lack awareness of work value, underestimate their influence, doubt their competence, and have limited decision-making autonomy [57]. Research indicates that psychological empowerment can reduce work stress, enhance task confidence, and improve adaptability [58]. It is negatively associated with depression in transplant patients [59], as well as fatigue and depression among healthcare professionals [60]. Empowerment in life decisions also predicts lower anxiety and depression [61]. For nurses, psychological empowerment improves autonomy, decision-making control, and self-efficacy [62], strengthens stress resilience, and reduces anxiety and depression risks. Conversely, low psychological empowerment may predict higher CADS in nurses.Accordingly, hospital administrators should focus on enhancing nurses’ psychological empowerment through counseling and training to strengthen role confidence, as well as by increasing job autonomy to promote a stronger sense of control and work impact.

Psychological detachment is closely associated with depression and anxiety. Sufficient psychological detachment from work or stressors can effectively alleviate mental fatigue, reduce anxiety and depressive symptoms, and promote emotional recovery [63, 64]. In contrast, insufficient psychological detachment, emotional numbing, social alienation, or maladaptive detachment coping strategies significantly increase an individual’s levels of depression and anxiety, and this association has been confirmed in general employees, cancer caregivers, and community adults [63–65]. The above evidence suggests that psychological detachment may also be an important factor influencing the occurrence of depression and anxiety among nurses, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. Based on the above findings, targeted strategies are recommended to improve mental health among nurses. Hospitals and nursing managers should provide psychological training such as mindfulness and cognitive behavioral interventions to enhance psychological detachment and relieve occupational fatigue. Reasonable scheduling and workload control should be implemented to reduce long‑term work stress and create favorable conditions for emotional recovery.

General demographic and working characteristics and CADS

Nurses aged 40 and above are more likely to develop CADS. This may be attributed to their extensive clinical experience, long-term rotating shifts leading to chronic circadian rhythm disruption, and accumulated pressures such as limited career advancement and work–family conflict, especially the difficulty balancing caregiving and work, resulting in physical and mental exhaustion. Nurses who exercise three or more times per week have a lower risk of CADS. Regular physical activity promotes the release of neurotransmitters such as dopamine and serotonin, which helps regulate metabolism and relieve depression and anxiety [66, 67]. By contrast, nurses who consume coffee three or more times weekly face an elevated CADS risk. High-dose caffeine intake may trigger anxiety and mood disturbances [68], while additives such as sugar and creamers may further exacerbate depressive symptoms [69]. Nurses working more than 50 hours weekly or on multiple rotating shifts also exhibit a higher risk of CADS. Frequent shift changes, consecutive night shifts, and weekend duties, together with high-acuity patient care and heavy workloads, contribute to severe physical and mental fatigue, chronic stress, circadian disruption, and emotional instability [70]. Long-term overtime not only causes fatigue but also impairs work performance and increases the risk of depression and anxiety [71, 72].

Evaluation and analysis of the nomogram

The model showed favorable discriminative ability, predictive accuracy, and clinical relevance. AUC values of both ROC curves were above 0.7, indicating good performance of the nomogram in identifying nurses at risk of CADS [73] and effectively differentiating different risk levels. Calibration analysis confirmed favorable consistency between predicted and actual probabilities in both cohorts (p > 0.05), supporting the high predictive accuracy of the model. Decision curve analysis verified its clinical utility, with obvious net benefits across most threshold probabilities, suggesting that risk stratification using this model has practical value. Using the optimal cutoff value of 0.560 derived from ROC analysis, nurses were classified into high-and low-risk CADS subgroups, providing an operable basis for risk classification. For those at or above this threshold, targeted early interventions-such as improving psychological empowerment, reducing second-victim experiences, and strengthening social support-are recommended to alleviate CADS. However, this model was only validated internally, without external validation; thus, it is not yet suitable for clinical application, prospective screening, or intervention guidance. The cutoff was used only for descriptive stratification in this study sample, not for clinical risk classification or decision-making.

Limitations

Several limitations must be emphasized. First, this is a single‑time‑point cross‑sectional observational study; all variables were measured simultaneously via self‑report, creating substantial risk of common‑method bias and precluding causal or temporal inference. Residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Second, all model performance results are based on internal validation only (70/30 split and bootstrap), with no external validation or prospective testing; thus, generalizability to other populations or settings is unproven. Third, the study sample was restricted to nurses with prior adverse event exposure, limiting comparability to nurses without such exposure. Fourth, all outcomes are based on self‑reported symptoms rather than clinical diagnoses, which may introduce reporting bias. Finally, the model reflects cross‑sectional association strength only and provides no evidence of clinical utility, predictive validity, or intervention guidance.

Clinical implications

The model enhances risk stratification through three modifiable predictors-exercise frequency, night shift load, and SVES latent class membership. For instance, a 41 year old nurse engaging in exercise 1–2 times/week, working > 6 night shifts/month, and classified into the “High SVE and Low Support” SVES subgroup would receive individualized scores for each predictor via the nomogram. Aggregating these scores yields a total risk profile, which directly corresponds to the probability of CADS development. These findings may help guide the design of supportive programs for this nurse subgroup, but no clinical implementation, prospective screening, or intervention recommendations can be justified from these cross‑sectional data alone.

Conclusion

Concurrent anxiety and depression symptoms are highly prevalent among nurses with prior adverse event exposure in this cross-sectional sample. Second victim experience and support, psychological empowerment, and multiple work and lifestyle factors are significantly associated with CADS in this population. An internally derived stratification model shows acceptable performance for describing cross‑sectional symptom prevalence but has not been clinically validated or externally verified. All findings are limited to observational associations from a single-time-point self-report survey, with no causal, predictive, or clinical utility claims supported. Future longitudinal or interventional research is needed to clarify temporal relationships, test causal hypotheses, and validate any clinical application.
