InHabit: Leveraging Image Foundation Models for Scalable 3D Human Placement
Nikita Kister * Pradyumna YM * István Sárándi Jiayi Wang, Anna Khoreva Gerard Pons-Moll, [5pt]

Abstract
Training embodied agents to understand 3D scenes as humans do requires large-scale data of people meaningfully interacting with diverse environments, yet such data is scarce. Real-world motion capture is costly and limited to controlled settings, while existing synthetic datasets rely on simple geometric heuristics that ignore rich scene context. In contrast, 2D foundation models trained on internet-scale data have implicitly acquired commonsense knowledge of human–environment interactions. To transfer this knowledge into 3D, we introduce InHabit, a fully automatic and scalable data generator for populating 3D scenes with interacting humans. InHabit follows a render–generate–lift principle: given a rendered 3D scene, a vision–language model proposes contextually meaningful actions, an image-editing model inserts a human, and an optimization procedure lifts the edited result into physically plausible SMPL-X bodies aligned with the scene geometry. Applied to Habitat-Matterport3D, InHabit produces the first large-scale photorealistic 3D human–scene interaction dataset, containing 78K samples across ∼ \sim 800 building-scale scenes with complete 3D geometry, SMPL-X bodies, and RGB images. Augmenting standard training data with our samples improves RGB-based 3D human–scene reconstruction and contact estimation, and in a perceptual user study our data is preferred in 78% of cases over the state of the art.

. Beyond geometric reconstruction and semantic labeling, this requires perceiving how humans would naturally interact with a scene, whether that is cooking at a stove, sitting on a sofa, or pausing at a window to look outside. However, progress is bottlenecked by a data scarcity problem. While we do have large collections of static 3D scenes
[
8
,
29
]
, and separately large collections of 3D human motions captured in studios
[
25
]
, there are no large datasets of 3D humans meaningfully interacting with diverse 3D environments. Capturing such interactions with motion capture inside natural scenes is logistically prohibitive and scales poorly. The handful of existing captured datasets
[
16
,
18
,
15
]
cover fewer than 30 scenes each.

Synthetic generation offers an alternative, but existing approaches rely on geometric heuristics that optimize for collision avoidance and contacts without deeper consideration of what a person would be doing in a given scene
[
17
,
38
]
.
Meanwhile, 2D foundation models trained on internet-scale data have implicitly acquired rich commonsense knowledge of how humans interact with their environments
[

]
. Image-editing models
[

can synthesize what it looks like to cook at a stove or read on a couch, because they have seen millions of such images and vision–language models
[

12

We introduce InHabit, a fully automated data-generation engine that brings the 2D capabilities of foundation models into the 3D domain for synthesizing large-scale 3D human–scene interaction data, as follows.

To show scalability, we apply InHabit to the Habitat-Matterport 3D dataset

2.

2
Related Works

2.1
Human Placement in 3D Scenes

The dominant approach to placing humans in 3D scenes relies on optimization over geometric constraints such as contact and penetration
[
16
,
38
]
.
POSA
[
17
]
goes further by learning data-driven contact priors from captured interactions.
These methods are effective at producing physically plausible configurations, but not necessarily semantically plausible ones. A body can be placed standing at any flat surface, yet there is no reasoning about what exactly one should be doing, or where one’s attention should be directed.
Alternatively,
[
24
]
generates 3D human-scene interactions using natural language description but also requires the user to provide a coarse point location in the 3D scene where the interaction should occur.
Our method addresses this limitation by leveraging the implicit interaction priors of 2D foundation models to place humans in a scene-appropriate way, without human in the loop.

Leveraging 2D Image Generation for 3D Tasks.

A recent line of work uses 2D image or video generation models as a source of training signal for 3D tasks.
HumanWild
[
11
]
generates humans in diverse scenes using diffusion models and recovers 3D annotations from the generated images, showing that 2D generation can substitute for expensive real-world capture.
Beyond the Contact
[
21
]
uses 2D inpainting diffusion to synthesize images of humans interacting with objects, then lifts these to 3D affordance maps, showing that interaction knowledge latent in 2D models can be extracted and grounded in 3D.
DAViD
[
20
]
generates 2D human–object interaction videos using video diffusion and lifts them to 4D interaction samples.
The same paradigm of using 2D generative models to produce annotated 3D human data has been explored for pose estimation
[
22
]
.
These works establish the feasibility of using 2D generative models as a source of 3D human data, but they operate on isolated human–object pairs or single tasks without reasoning about the broader scene context, and none produce full building-scale HSI data.
InHabit extends this paradigm to complete indoor environments: a VLM reasons about what interactions a scene affords, an image-editing model visualizes them in context, and an optimization-based reconstructor grounds the result in the full 3D scene geometry.

Subsequent work

CIRCLE

InHabitants

3
Method

Our goal is to generate large-scale 3D human–scene interaction (HSI) data with natural, diverse, and context-aware behaviors. Existing 3D scene collections are relatively abundant, but they typically contain no people and therefore lack interaction supervision for embodied scene understanding. We formulate data generation as a
render–generate–lift loop
(
Fig.

2
): we render an existing 3D scene, use image foundation models to generate realistic humans in image space, and lift the results back into physically grounded 3D bodies aligned with the known scene geometry.

Given any static 3D indoor scene (e.g., large-scale scanned environments such as HM3D
[
29
]
), InHabit has four stages. (1) We automatically sample camera viewpoints, render RGB/depth views, and perform scene-aware human insertion in image space (
Section

3.1
), where foundation models generate interaction proposals and edited images. (2) We reconstruct and place inserted humans in 3D with geometric and physical constraints from the full scene mesh (
Section

3.2
). (3) We scale this process to large corpora through automated multi-view generation and batching (
Section

3.3
). (4) Because each stage can introduce artifacts, we apply post-reconstruction quality control to retain only physically plausible samples (
Section

3.4
). The final output is a dataset of RGB-D images paired with complete 3D scene geometry and aligned SMPL-X human meshes.

3.1
Generating Scene-aware Humans in 2D

The goal of this stage is to populate rendered scene views with humans performing diverse, contextually meaningful interactions.
This requires two complementary capabilities: understanding
what
interactions a scene affords, and knowing
how
those interactions look when realized visually.
We address both through the interplay of two foundation models—a vision–language model (VLM) and an image-editing model—whose combined knowledge of human behavior in indoor environments drives our insertion pipeline.

A natural starting point is to simply instruct the editing model with a generic prompt such as “Add a person to this image.”
In practice, however, this yields mostly generic standing poses that ignore the rich affordances visible in the scene—a kitchen with a stove affords cooking, a living room with a sofa facing a TV affords watching, yet none of this is captured by a generic instruction.
Hand-crafting scene-specific prompts (
e.g
.
, “Add a person sitting on the couch watching TV”) would require per-scene annotations or a human in the loop, neither of which scales.

Instead, we query the VLM to analyze each rendered view and propose plausible activities given the visible scene context.
The VLM’s world knowledge lets it reason jointly about objects, their functions, and typical human behaviors—recognizing, for instance, that a kitchen with a microwave affords operating it, or that a bedroom affords lying down.
We refer to this as
affordance-driven prompting
: it produces interactions that are semantically appropriate for each scene without any predefined text prompts or manual supervision.
However, affordance-driven proposals tend to converge on the most common activities and miss rarer but plausible ones.
We therefore complement it with an
action-driven
strategy: given a compact set of predefined actions (
e.g
.
, standing, reaching, leaning), we ask the VLM to contextualize each within the current scene.
For example, the generic action “standing” in a room with a ceiling light may become “standing on a chair to replace the light bulb.”
Starting from actions rather than affordances unlocks a substantially wider range of interactions from a small vocabulary.

Since InHabit inserts humans into a fixed 3D scene, interactions that alter the scene state (
e.g
.
, opening a door, moving furniture) or introduce new objects would break consistency with the known geometry.
We therefore explicitly constrain the VLM to propose only interactions that preserve the scene layout; residual violations are caught by our post-reconstruction filtering (
Section

3.4
).
The framework also naturally extends to multi-person scenarios—we prompt for interactions involving multiple people (
e.g
.
, two people conversing in a hallway, a group seated around a dining table), and the editing model inserts them with coherent spatial relationships and social context, something difficult to achieve with optimization-based placement methods
[
17
,
38
,
24
]
that operate per-person without modeling interpersonal coordination.

Given the VLM’s textual interaction proposals, the image-editing model serves as the visual realization engine.
Crucially, it does not simply paste a human template into the image—it generates body poses, proportions, and spatial placements that are adapted to the local scene context.
A person watching TV is posed seated and facing the screen; a person cooking is oriented toward the stove with arms extended (Fig. 1).
These fine-grained, scene-adapted pose variations emerge naturally from the editing model’s large-scale visual pretraining and would be difficult to reproduce with optimization-based methods that rely on restricted pose priors without visual scene understanding (
Fig.

5
).
The output of this stage is the rendered scene image with one or more photorealistic humans inserted, ready for 3D lifting (
Section

3.2
).
Full prompt templates and additional design details are provided in the supplementary material.

3.2
Lifting Inserted Humans to 3D

Our lifting framework is inspired by prior work on physically-grounded human-scene reconstruction, such as PhySIC
[
36
]
and PROX
[
16
]
. PhySIC directly estimates 3D scenes from RGB images by jointly optimizing predicted human meshes and partial depth maps, whereas PROX fits humans to RGB-D frames using physical constraints. In contrast, we leverage known, complete 3D scene geometry, which removes one degree of freedom from optimization and constrains the problem more tightly, resulting in more accurate human placement and alignment with scene geometry. At the same time, our method retains the flexibility of image-based approaches, as it does not rely on RGB-D inputs like PROX. This enables us to generate accurate 3D data at scale, leveraging image foundational models.

Crucially, access to the complete scene mesh means the scene SDF is globally well-defined, making penetration and contact terms stable and informative. It also improves occlusion handling, since body parts not visible in the RGB view can still be constrained by nearby scene surfaces. We provide a detailed ablation comparing full-scene versus partial-geometry lifting in the supplementary material.

Specifically, we estimate a monocular depth map of the inserted human using a depth estimator
[
32
]
and back-project it to 3D to obtain a partial human point cloud. This point cloud is then aligned with known 3D scene geometry to initialize the human localization.

Unlike PhySIC, which co-optimizes scene geometry and can implicitly absorb metric inconsistencies, we operate with fixed scene geometry and introduce an explicit scale parameter
s
applied to the human mesh in camera-relative space. This parameter controls the human’s metric size and apparent scale in the scene. Without
s
, the optimizer would need to adjust body shape
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and translation
\mathbf{t}
to match the scene scale; however, modifying
\boldsymbol{\beta}
alters the body silhouette and is penalized by the 2D reprojection loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{proj}}
, preventing reliable recovery of the correct scale. The explicit scale parameter decouples metric sizing from body shape, improving convergence and physical consistency. Additional analysis is provided in the supplementary material.

We optimize the human parameters — pose
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, shape
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, camera-relative translation
\mathbf{t}
, and scale
s
— with respect to the following objective:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\boldsymbol{\beta},\,\mathbf{t},\,s}

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{proj}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{proj}}+\lambda_{\text{depth}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{depth}}+\lambda_{\text{con}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{contact}}

(1)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\text{pen}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{penetration}}+\lambda_{\text{reg}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}.

Here,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{proj}}
enforces 2D reprojection consistency,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{depth}}
aligns the visible human surface with the estimated depth,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{contact}}
encourages plausible contact with scene surfaces, and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{penetration}}
penalizes intersections with the scene geometry. All losses are supervised by the complete 3D scene geometry, allowing physically grounded alignment without scene optimization. Full definitions of each loss term and their weighting coefficients are provided in the supplementary material.

3.3
Scaling up HSI Data Generation

Figure 3
:
Representative samples from
InHabitants
. Each pair shows the edited RGB image (left) and the corresponding lifted 3D human in the 3D scene (right). Our dataset contains diverse, context-aware interactions across varied indoor environments.

InHabit is agnostic to the source of 3D scenes and can operate on any textured mesh with known camera parameters.
In this work, we instantiate it on the Habitat-Matterport 3D dataset (HM3D)
[
29
]
, which offers approximately 800 building-scale scans spanning residential, commercial, and institutional interiors with multiple floors and rooms each.
This diversity exposes the generation pipeline to kitchens, bedrooms, offices, hallways, stairwells, and many other room types, enabling a broad distribution of human–scene interactions in the resulting dataset (
Fig.

3
).

Generating high-quality data at this scale requires careful viewpoint selection.
Not every camera pose yields a view that is suitable for human insertion: some look directly at a nearby wall or are obstructed by furniture, while others show only ceilings or narrow corridors where inserting a full-body human is infeasible.
We therefore filter the available viewpoints using a depth-based heuristic.
Specifically, we require that (i) the floor is visible in the lower portion of the image, so that inserted humans can be grounded rather than truncated, and (ii) the view extends sufficiently deep into the room, so that the scene context is rich enough for meaningful interactions.
Both criteria are evaluated by checking the rendered depth map against simple thresholds; views that fail either check are discarded.
Details of the heuristic are provided in the supplementary material.

Regardless of the underlying scene source, some rooms will inevitably be largely empty and offer limited affordances for human–scene interaction (
e.g
.
, bare corridors, unfurnished storage rooms).
For such views, the VLM’s affordance-driven prompting (
Section

3.1
) naturally produces only a narrow set of generic activities.
To maintain pose diversity even in such scenes, we augment the prompting strategy with textual pose descriptions sourced from the PoseScript dataset
[
9
]
, which provides fine-grained natural-language descriptions of human body poses.
By conditioning the editing model on these descriptions, we can inject varied body articulations that go beyond what affordance- or action-driven prompting alone would produce, ensuring that the dataset covers a broad pose distribution even in visually sparse environments.

To characterize the resulting dataset, we use a VLM
[
13
]
to classify each generated sample by its action and the primary object involved, in an open-ended manner.
Fig.

4
shows the distribution over the most frequent action and object categories, illustrating the breadth of interactions produced by InHabit.

3.4
Post-reconstruction Filtering

While image editing models can successfully insert humans into scene renders, these edits are not guaranteed to be physically consistent with the underlying 3D scene geometry. To address this, we apply three complementary post-reconstruction filters—a visibility filter, a depth-based boundary consistency filter, and a volume filter—to remove physically implausible or incomplete human insertions and ensure high-quality, consistent 3D reconstructions.

Visibility Filter.

The editing model can place humans in reflective surfaces (e.g., mirrors or glass) without a corresponding physical person in the scene, or produce severely truncated insertions (e.g., only a hand). To detect these cases, let
\mathcal{H}_{i}
denote the reconstructed human mesh for human
i
in an image. We uniformly sample a set of points
\{\mathbf{p}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
on
\mathcal{H}_{i}
and project them onto the image plane using the camera projection
\pi(\cdot)
. Given a human segmentation mask
M_{i}
in the image, we collect the
mask-consistent
points whose projections fall inside
M_{i}
:

V_{i}=\{\mathbf{p}_{j}:\pi(\mathbf{p}_{j})\in M_{i}\}.

We define the
visibility ratio
as

\mathrm{vis}(\mathcal{H}_{i})=\frac{|V_{i}|}{N}.

Humans with
\mathrm{vis}(\mathcal{H}_{i})<\tau_{\text{vis}}
are discarded. If no humans remain in an image after this step, the image is dropped entirely, indicating the editing model failed to produce a valid insertion.

Depth-Based Filter.

The editing model can also introduce unintended local geometry changes near the inserted human, such as hallucinated or displaced furniture. To detect these cases, let
D_{\text{edit}}
denote the monocular depth map estimated from the edited image, aligned to metric scale using the known scene geometry, and let
M=\bigcup_{i}M_{i}
be the combined binary mask of all humans. We define a thin boundary region around the humans as
M^{\text{b}}=\text{dilate}(M)-M
. We focus on this near-human band because edits are typically local to inserted people; distant regions can be preserved by compositing the near-human edited patch back into the original image. For each pixel
\mathbf{q}
in this boundary, we compare
D_{\text{edit}}
against the known scene depth
D_{\text{scene}}
and compute the fraction of pixels whose absolute depth discrepancy exceeds a per-pixel tolerance
\tau_{\text{depth}}
:

\Delta=\frac{|\{\mathbf{q}\in M^{\text{b}}:|D_{\text{edit}}(\mathbf{q})-D_{\text{scene}}(\mathbf{q})|>\tau_{\text{depth}}\}|}{|M^{\text{b}}|}.

If the violation ratio
\Delta
exceeds an image-level threshold
\tau_{\text{border}}
, the reconstruction is discarded. This operates as a joint, image-level filter, since geometry edits can extend beyond any single person’s boundary.

Volume Filter.

The image editing model can occasionally insert children, which fall outside the adult body model we employ (SMPL-X), and the human detector can misfire on depictions of people in portraits, posters, or sculptures. In both cases, fitting an adult SMPL-X mesh to these detections produces highly deformed body shapes: either unnaturally compressed to match a child’s proportions or distorted to conform to a non-human surface. We observe that these failure modes consistently yield meshes with anomalously low body volume. Motivated by this, we compute the volume
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{H}_{i})
of each reconstructed SMPL-X mesh
\mathcal{H}_{i}
and discard any instance with
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{H}_{i})<\tau_{\text{vol}}
. Despite its simplicity, this filter is highly effective: it removes the vast majority of problematic cases and, in practice, performs comparably to more expensive alternatives such as dedicated human detectors (e.g., Multi-HMR
[
3
]
) or pose-prior-based filtering (e.g., VPoser
[
27
]
).

Together, these three filters ensure that the final dataset contains only humans that are sufficiently visible, properly aligned, plausibly shaped, and do not introduce implausible scene changes.

4.1
Experimental Setup

We compare against the state of the art on the task of text-based HSI-Generation.

Baselines.

GenZI
[
24
]
is a zero-shot human–scene interaction method that combines Stable Diffusion inpainting with 2D-to-3D lifting. Given a text prompt and a target location, it renders the scene from multiple views, inpaints a human in each view, and recovers SMPL-X parameters via multi-view optimization. Generation is performed in two stages: four candidate poses are produced, and the best is refined in a second stage.

POSA

[
17
]
is an optimization-based method that predicts per-vertex contacts and semantic classes using a trained VAE, and then optimizes body pose and placement to align predicted contacts with scene geometry and semantics (limited to 40 object categories). As POSA does not support text-based generation, we extend it with text-driven pose retrieval to form POSA++. Specifically, we use PoseEmbroider
[
10
]
to retrieve poses from text via a joint embedding space. Since PoseEmbroider requires PoseScript-style descriptions
[
9
]
, we employ an LLM to convert our HSI prompts accordingly. We use AGORA poses
[
26
]
, on which POSA’s contact prediction performs best.

Data.

We use scenes from HM3D
[
28
]
to compare InHabit against the baselines.
As POSA requires semantic labels for the 3D scene, so we restrict ourself to the subset of 200 scenes from HM3D.
We sample 150 of our generated affordance-aware text prompts and corresponding scene locations.
We do furthest point sampling with respect to sentence embeddings to create a diverse set of interactions.

Metrics.

We adopt the CLIP score, as in
[
24
]
, to evaluate whether placements are semantically consistent with the input prompt. For each pose, we render ten views of the generated human mesh using a fixed human texture for realism, following GenZI. We compute the CLIP score for each view with respect to the input text and report the average.

To evaluate the physical plausibility and semantic alignment of generated contacts, we introduce a set of contact metrics focused on specific interaction using interaction-contact priors.
For a given text prompt we use a LLM to predict the body parts expected to be involved in the interaction (e.g. "feet", "hands" and "arms" for
leaning on a counter
)
We define a body surface vertex as being in contact if its distance to the nearest scene surface falls below a predefined threshold.
Using our LLM-derived labels, we calculate Recall to measure the proportion of expected contact vertices successfully recovered, and Precision to quantify the presence of false-positive contacts in unrelated body regions. These are summarized via the F1-score.
Additionally, we report the contact distance which is the average distance of the body vertices that should be in contact for the interaction.
This provides a more fine-grained measure of how closely the generated pose adheres to the interaction.

In contrast, InHabit reasons about the scene prior to lifting, producing physically consistent and semantically coherent interactions, such as touching the tiger statue, lying on the sofa, playing pool, or pointing at a map. We refer to the supplementary material for many more examples.

User Study.

We conduct a perceptual user study comparing interactive 3D interaction synthesis from GenZI, Text-POSA, and our method. In each question, participants are asked to choose the single best result based on three criteria: (i) realism of human–scene interaction (plausible contact/support without floating or severe intersections), (ii) physical feasibility (stable, collision-consistent placement), and (iii) pose naturalness (anatomically plausible posture).

To reduce sampling bias, each participant evaluates 20 randomly selected questions. For each question, the three methods are compared at the same scene location for the inserted human, and samples are drawn from a pool of 50 generations per method.

Across 726 total responses, our method is preferred 567 times, compared with 110 for Text-POSA and 49 for GenZI (Fig.
6
), corresponding to 78.1%, 15.2%, and 6.7%, respectively.

Contact Estimation on DAMON.

To scale contact supervision, we extract contact labels directly from our reconstructed humans by using spatial proximity between body vertices and scene surfaces. This yields large-scale pseudo-labels without additional manual annotation. In total, our dataset provides 97K humans, i.e., 97K image-to-contact pairs. More importantly, it provides substantially higher scene diversity: unlike video-based datasets with many redundant frames, our data spans roughly 800 building-scale scenes with significantly more distinct rooms and layouts, see Fig.

Conclusion

We have presented InHabit, a fully automatic data generator for large-scale 3D human–scene interactions by transferring the implicit interaction knowledge of 2D foundation models into 3D. Using a render–generate–lift principle, we populated approximately 800 building-scale scenes from Habitat-Matterport 3D with over 78k contextually meaningful SMPL-X humans, producing
InHabitants
, the first large-scale 3D HSI dataset with complete scene geometry, body meshes, and RGB images.
We showed that InHabit outperforms existing placement methods both quantitatively and in a perceptual user study, and that augmenting training data with
InHabitants
improves state-of-the-art contact estimation and feed-forward HSI reconstruction on established benchmarks.
Currently, InHabit generates static poses within static 3D scenes, but the principle can be extended to video generation and dynamic scenes in the future as the underlying generative models continue to evolve.
More broadly, our results show that internet-scale 2D foundation models encode enough knowledge of human–environment interactions to serve as a practical, scalable alternative to expensive 3D motion capture for training embodied AI methods.
