The differential and asymmetric moderating effect of psychological resilience on the relationship between nurses’ emotional labor and emotional exhaustion
Authors: Yanjun Yu, Liurong Xu, Tianlin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmotional exhaustion among nurses, which stems from high-intensity emotional labor, is a significant occupational health concern. While existing literature establishes a direct correlation between emotional labor strategies—specifically surface acting and deep acting—and emotional exhaustion, the dynamic regulatory role of core psychological resources (e.g., psychological resilience) in this relationship remains a key theoretical gap.MethodsA cross-sectional design was used to survey 527 nurses from 20 hospitals in China. Data were collected using validated scales, and all statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 and AMOS 25.0.ResultsThe findings indicated that surface acting was positively associated with emotional exhaustion (β = 0.342, 95% CI [0.291, 0.393], p < 0.001), thereby supporting the Resource Loss Spiral hypothesis. In contrast, deep acting exhibited a significant negative association with emotional exhaustion (β = -0.337, 95% CI [-0.389, -0.285], p < 0.001), consistent with the Resource Gain Spiral expectation. A key finding of this study was the differentiated and asymmetric moderating effect of psychological resilience, which significantly mitigated the positive relationship between surface acting and emotional exhaustion (interaction term β = -0.159, 95% CI [-0.214, -0.104], p < 0.001; buffering effect of 63.5%), while simultaneously enhancing the negative relationship between deep acting and emotional exhaustion (interaction term β = 0.183, 95% CI [0.121, 0.245], p < 0.001; gain effect of 237.7%). Notably, the magnitude of the gain effect was 3.7 times greater than that of the buffering effect.ConclusionsThis study revealed that nurses’ emotional labor was associated with emotional exhaustion through a dual pathway of “surface-level depletion and deep-level gain”. As a key boundary condition, psychological resilience reshapes the strength of this relationship through a dual moderating mechanism of “buffering-depletion and facilitating-gain”. Theoretically, it expands the dynamic explanatory dimension of COR theory and the Emotional Labor Boundary Condition Theory. Practically, we recommend developing an intervention program integrating psychological resilience training with matched emotional labor strategies to improve resilience levels and maximize the resource gain benefits.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Research background

In the contemporary healthcare system, hospitals as core medical service institutions have gradually evolved into typical scenarios with high-intensity emotional labor [1], which has become a critical occupational health risk issue and attracted extensive attention in occupational health psychology research [2]. The essence of medical services lies in “human-oriented healing”, requiring health professionals to continuously invest emotional resources while mobilizing knowledge and physical resources to maintain humanistic care quality [3]. Such sustained emotional resource depletion constitutes a core trigger of occupational health risks [4, 5].

As the main force of patient care and a key part of the medical service system, nurses bear dual cognitive and emotional loads. They are required to complete a series of cognitive and operational tasks such as clinical operations and information processing, and also conduct sustained emotional management (i.e., emotional labor) to meet professional norms and patients’ psychological expectations [6]. From the perspective of occupational health psychology, such high-intensity emotional labor makes nurses a typical research group for occupational health risks. Previous studies [7, 8] have confirmed that nurses have significantly higher emotional labor intensity than other practitioners, which is caused by long-term complex clinical environments, heavy workloads, and high expectations from patients and their families, especially in high-risk departments such as emergency and pediatric wards.

Such high-intensity emotional labor presents significant dual effects. For patients, nurses’ emotional labor strategies directly affect their perceptions and experiences, and are significantly correlated with satisfaction, care quality evaluation, and even rehabilitation outcomes [9]. For nurses, the implementation of emotional labor is closely related to core occupational health indicators such as emotional exhaustion and turnover intention, which seriously threatens the stability of the nursing team [4].

Meanwhile, significant individual differences exist in clinical practice. Some nurses can still maintain professional enthusiasm under high-intensity emotional labor, while others suffer rapid emotional exhaustion, indicating that individual psychological resources may play an important moderating role in the relationship between emotional labor and emotional exhaustion. As a core psychological trait for coping with pressure and resisting adversity, psychological resilience interacts with emotional labor strategies to influence the occurrence and development of exhaustion, which has become the key to solving the problem.

However, the dynamic mechanism of the above relationship is still unclear. It is urgent to explore the triadic relationship of “emotional labor–psychological resilience–emotional exhaustion”, construct and verify a systematic theoretical framework, and provide a scientific basis for occupational health interventions for nurses.

Theoretical foundation and literature review

The core theoretical foundation

This study is grounded in Conservation of Resources (COR) theory [10], which provides a unified framework for interpreting resource dynamics and stress adaptation. According to COR theory, resources encompass any entities, conditions, or capacities valued by individuals, including emotional energy, social support, and occupational efficacy [10, 11]. Stress and emotional maladjustment arise when resource loss exceeds resource replenishment, whereas resource gain and effective replenishment facilitate adaptive functioning.

COR theory identifies two distinct spirals that govern occupational adaptation: the resource depletion spiral, whereby sustained resource loss without replenishment leads to a self-reinforcing vicious cycle [12]; and the resource gain spiral, whereby proactive resource supplementation generates a self-sustaining virtuous cycle [11].

According to COR theory, the continuous exertion of emotional labor is essentially a process of ongoing investment and depletion of emotional resources, which tends to drive the formation of the resource loss spiral and further elevate the risk of emotional exhaustion. As a core psychological resource, psychological resilience can effectively regulate resource allocation, buffer emotional resource loss, interrupt the vicious cycle of resource depletion, and foster the formation of the resource gain spiral. Accordingly, COR theory provides a complete theoretical framework and explanatory basis for this study to clarify the mechanism through which emotional labor influences emotional exhaustion via resource dynamics, as well as the moderating role of psychological resilience in this process.

Emotional labor and emotional exhaustion

Emotional labor, a core concept in organizational behavior, was first systematically defined by Hochschild [13] as the psychological process where individuals intentionally regulate facial expressions, body language, and vocal tones to align emotional displays with professional standards and organizational expectations—highlighting the tension between emotional authenticity and social performance [14, 15].

Diefendorff et al. [16] proposed a three-dimensional model of emotional labor strategies (genuine expression, deep acting, surface acting), but Grandey [12] argued that genuine expression, lacking intentional regulation, should not be classified as emotional labor. Academic consensus now favors the two-dimensional model (surface acting vs. deep acting) for its targeted focus on core issues [17], which this study adopts. Surface acting involves forced suppression of genuine emotions, depleting psychological resources and increasing emotional dissonance risk; deep acting achieves internal-external emotional consistency via cognitive restructuring with moderate resource investment; genuine expression, by contrast, is spontaneous and resource-efficient.

Among high-emotional-labor groups, surface acting is often preferred in high-pressure settings [12], but sustained use causes chronic disconnection between authentic emotions and professional expressions, intensifying self-depletion and the “efficiency trap” [18], thereby increasing emotional exhaustion risk.

Emotional exhaustion, a core outcome of occupational stress, is characterized by physical and mental fatigue, emotional detachment, and diminished work efficacy arising from excessive depletion of internal resources [19]. The nursing profession, known for its high emotional demands and limited opportunities for resource recovery, serves as an optimal context for examining the relationship between emotional labor and emotional exhaustion [20]. Epidemiological survey data revealed that approximately 73.02% of nurses in China exhibited clinically significant symptoms of emotional exhaustion, and this issue was particularly pronounced among younger nurses [21, 22].

Existing research [23, 24] has confirmed the dual-path association effect of nurses’ emotional labor on exhaustion. Specifically, surface acting was significantly positively correlated with exhaustion, while deep acting was significantly negatively correlated. From the perspective of COR theory [10], the relationship between emotional labor and emotional exhaustion is essentially a process of dynamic resource changes. More precisely, surface acting results in unidirectional depletion of psychological resources due to the persistent suppression of genuine emotions. This disconnection between expressed emotions and internal states impedes the acquisition of social support and the development of self-identity, thereby entrapping individuals in the “resource loss spiral”. Deep acting facilitates the alignment between internal feelings and external expressions through cognitive restructuring. Although it may initially consume some resources, authentic interactions generate positive external feedback and enhance internal self-efficacy, thereby forming the “resource gain spiral” that effectively mitigates the risk of exhaustion.

The moderating effect of psychological resilience

From the perspective of occupational health psychology, psychological resilience refers to the core capacity of individuals to maintain adaptive functioning, achieve effective recovery, and sustain homeostasis under high pressure or adverse circumstances [25]. As a multidimensional construct, it integrates both ability-oriented attributes (e.g., stress resistance, goal focus) and process-oriented attributes (e.g., dynamic regulation, resource reconfiguration) [26, 27, 28], which constitutes the key theoretical rationale for adopting psychological resilience as the core moderating variable in this study.

As a typical occupation with high emotional labor demands, nursing requires frontline staff to frequently cope with emergent stressful situations such as patient agitation and urgent diagnosis and treatment. The profession also features a structural dilemma of “high emotional demand and insufficient resource replenishment” [29]. Against such a backdrop, psychological resilience, as an adaptive coping mechanism, is urgently needed to provide critical buffering protection. According to Conservation of Resources Theory [10], continuous emotional labor places nurses at high risk of accelerated core resource loss. Empirical evidence [30] further indicates that psychological resilience, as a critical protective factor, can effectively alleviate emotional exhaustion induced by work-related stressors. Accordingly, its theoretical role has evolved beyond the traditional static resource view to become a dynamic regulator of core resources [31]. Its regulatory effects operate through the following two distinct pathways:

In the resource depletion buffering pathway, nurses with high resilience promptly adopt adaptive cognitive–emotional strategies such as situational reappraisal and meaning reconstruction, which mitigate resource depletion caused by surface acting [12]. For instance, interpreting patient complaints as “non-verbal signals of unmet needs” rather than personal attacks interrupts the harmful cycle of “surface acting – resource depletion – emotional exhaustion.”

In the resource regeneration facilitation pathway, when resources are temporarily diminished, highly resilient nurses efficiently mobilize alternative resource networks, including the reinforcement of professional calling and activation of social support systems. This not only accelerates the replenishment of resources but also fosters sustainable gain cycles [11]. By enhancing intrinsic satisfaction derived from deep acting, this process promotes a positive feedback loop of “deep acting, resource regeneration, and reduced exhaustion.”

These two pathways function through distinct synergistic mechanisms: the buffering pathway directly weakens the positive impact of surface acting on exhaustion, whereas the regeneration pathway strengthens the negative effect of deep acting on exhaustion [32]. This differential regulation pattern for the two emotional labor strategies (surface versus deep) highlights the fundamental theoretical significance of psychological resilience. Theoretically, this synergistic mechanism can be summarized as follows: higher resilience allows individuals to reduce the adverse effects of surface acting through buffering while simultaneously amplifying the protective effects of deep acting through regeneration [33]. Ultimately, under equivalent emotional labor intensity, nurses with high psychological resilience demonstrated a significantly lower risk of emotional exhaustion than those with low resilience [34].

Furthermore, based on the fundamental differences in resource attributes between the two types of emotional labor strategies, the moderating effect of psychological resilience on these “dual pathways” is likely to show significant asymmetry. That is, its resource regeneration-promoting effect on deep acting is expected to be stronger than its resource-depletion-buffering effect on surface acting. The core logic of this asymmetric moderation hypothesis is that surface acting is characterized by “expression dissonance” between genuine emotional experiences and professional requirements, making it essentially a “resource-consuming” strategy [11]. The moderating role of psychological resilience is mainly limited to slowing down the rate of resource depletion through cognitive reappraisal and other means, but it cannot fundamentally change the inherent nature of this strategy as “passive extraction” of psychological resources. In contrast, deep acting achieves internal consistency between emotional experiences and expressive requirements through cognitive restructuring and is classified as a “resource-generating” strategy [33]. Psychological resilience can strengthen the cycle of “positive feedback – resource regeneration” in this process, thereby forming an amplifying effect of “resource input – excess gain.”

Research gaps

The Boundary Condition Theory of Emotional Labor [35] highlights that the psychological and behavioral outcomes of surface acting and deep acting are not fixed but dynamically shaped by individual traits and situational factors. Accordingly, the effects of emotional labor are highly conditional, with their direction and magnitude contingent on critical boundary variables including psychological resilience, organizational support, and job autonomy.

Prior studies have confirmed that psychological resilience moderates the association between emotional labor and emotional exhaustion [29, 30]. Nevertheless, the current literature presents prominent structural limitations and theoretical deficits, restricting an in-depth understanding of resilience’s functional value in nursing populations.

From a theoretical perspective, existing research lacks systematic analysis of dynamic boundary mechanisms, which is central to the Boundary Condition Theory of Emotional Labor [35]. Most empirical studies prioritize mediating variables such as psychological capital [36] and organizational support [37], while neglecting psychological resilience as a core boundary condition. Few studies have elaborated on resilience’s unique function in resource reorganization and dynamic resource adjustment under occupational stress.

Furthermore, available evidence on resilience’s moderating effect contains two major shortcomings. First, existing research ignores the asymmetrical feature of moderating pathways, failing to clarify why resilience exerts a stronger enhancing effect on deep acting than a buffering effect on surface acting. Second, most discussions remain confined to the macro resource gain–loss framework of COR theory [10], without integrating supplementary theoretical perspectives to unpack the intermediate process linking emotional labor strategies, emotional responses and resource dynamics. Such shortcomings confine current findings to superficial correlation descriptions and weaken the theoretical explanatory strength of existing models.

These theoretical deficiencies create a disconnect between insufficient theoretical interpretation and practical demands. On the one hand, previous studies have not fully clarified how resilience differentially alters the two pathways of emotional labor: it remains unclear how resilience buffers resource depletion induced by surface acting or amplifies the resource regeneration benefits of deep acting [11]. Without such clarification, the moderating mechanism cannot be explained from a process-oriented perspective. On the other hand, incomplete theoretical reasoning limits the interpretation of marked individual differences in nurses’ emotional exhaustion [34]. Under equivalent emotional labor intensity, individual discrepancies in occupational adaptation and exhaustion vulnerability cannot be reasonably addressed by existing frameworks.

Research objectives, hypotheses, and value

Building on the aforementioned theoretical gaps, this study focused on two core research questions. The first examines the differential main effect pathways of nurses’ emotional labor strategies (surface acting/deep acting) on emotional exhaustion. The second investigates how psychological resilience, as a core boundary condition, moderated the strength and direction of the aforementioned pathways through a dynamic resource balance mechanism. The research hypotheses were presented in Fig. 1.

H1

Nurses’ emotional labor strategies are dual-path associated with emotional exhaustion.

H1a

Surface acting is positively associated with emotional exhaustion.

H1b

Deep acting is negatively associated with emotional exhaustion.

H2

Psychological resilience exerts a differentiated moderating effect between emotional labor strategies and emotional exhaustion.

H2a

Psychological resilience weakens the positive association between surface acting and emotional exhaustion.

H2b

Psychological resilience strengthens the negative association between deep acting and emotional exhaustion.

By analyzing the asymmetric moderating effect of psychological resilience on the dual pathways of emotional labor, this study fills the process research gap of “emotional labor strategy–resource change–emotional response” and enriches relevant theories of emotional labor and occupational health. The findings provide practical implications for developing differentiated occupational health programs, promoting nurses’ well-being and retaining nursing staff.

Method

Research design

This study used a cross-sectional survey design. Convenience cluster sampling was employed to survey nurses from 20 healthcare institutions across 11 provinces and municipalities in China, including Jiangxi, Guangdong, and Fujian, between June 21 and June 29, 2024. The sample covered multiple regions with varying levels of economic development in eastern and southern China. By selecting samples across different regions, it indirectly encompassed the differences in medical resource allocation, work-pressure intensity, and other aspects among cities, thereby effectively reducing the potential interference of a single regional medical environment on the research results. The sample comprised ten primary hospitals, six secondary hospitals, and four tertiary hospitals. Inclusion criteria were restricted to licensed nurses who were actively engaged in clinical roles. The survey was conducted in strict accordance with principles of voluntary participation and informed consent.

Data collection was conducted through anonymous online surveys utilizing the “Questionnaire Star” platform (https://www.wjx.cn), which offers comprehensive functionalities for questionnaire creation, distribution, and data analysis. Upon accessing the questionnaire link, participants were automatically directed to an electronic informed consent page that thoroughly disclosed the study’s purpose, estimated completion time (approximately 15 min), risk-benefit ratio, data anonymization procedures, and the right to withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason. Participants were required to check a box indicating, “I have read and understood the above information and voluntarily consent to participate in this study”, as an electronic signature following a mandatory review of the consent form. Those who declined were automatically redirected to an exit page. To ensure adherence to research ethics and maintain data quality, the following measures were implemented: (1) validation of IP address uniqueness to prevent duplicate responses; (2) no mandatory response items were included in the questionnaire; (3) dedicated QR codes were distributed through hospital nursing management departments; and (4) all coordinators underwent standardized ethics training, which strictly prohibited the use of leading language.

Participants

Sample size estimation and data screening

To determine the required sample size for hierarchical regression moderation analysis, a priori power analysis was performed via G*Power 3.1. Parameters were set for linear multiple regression with an R2 increase test: power = 0.95, α = 0.05, and eight predictors (four demographic controls, two main emotional labor variables, and two interaction terms). With a moderate-to-large effect size (f2 =0.08), the minimum sample requirement was 194. Given two interaction tests, the Bonferroni correction adjusted the alpha level to 0.025, raising the required sample to 226. Considering non-normal distribution risks and recommended 15% extra cases for data skewness, together with potential questionnaire invalidity, a final target of 600 participants was determined.

A total of 572 questionnaires were collected. Following the exclusion of 45 invalid questionnaires, comprising 28 that were incomplete and 17 that exhibited patterned responses with a threshold of 80% or greater, 527 valid responses were retained, resulting in a valid response rate of 92.13%.

Demographic characteristics of participants

The distribution of the study sample across hospital tiers (65.09% tertiary, 25.05% secondary, 10.06% primary) and its core demographic and occupational characteristics—including 96.96% female, 47.06% with ≥ 10 years of clinical experience, and 96.77% holding a college degree or higher—were highly consistent with authoritative data from the 2024 China Health Statistics Yearbook, supporting that the sample adequately represents the Chinese nursing population.

Research instruments

Demographic questionnaire

Self-designed questionnaires were used to collect essential participant information, including hospital category, gender, professional titles, educational attainment, and clinical tenure. Previous research [2, 20, 22, 28, 29] suggests that these demographic variables may significantly impact emotional labor, psychological resilience, and emotional exhaustion. Therefore, they were incorporated as control variables in the statistical analyses in this study.

Emotional labor scale

This study used the Chinese version of the Emotional Labor Scale, which was revised by Bai [38] based on the original scale developed by Diefendorff et al. [16]. The surface acting subscale, comprising seven items, and the deep acting subscale, consisting of four items, were selected to assess nurses’ emotional labor levels. The scale employs a 5-point Likert scoring system, ranging from “Very Inconsistent” (1 point) to “Very Consistent” Very Consistent’(5 points). Each subscale was scored independently, with higher scores indicating a greater level of emotional labor. This Chinese version has been extensively utilized by various researchers [6, 39] and has demonstrated strong reliability and validity. In this study’s sample, the scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s α internal consistency coefficient of 0.890, Composite Reliability (CR) of 0.911, and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) confirmed good construct validity (χ²/df = 2.585, RMSEA = 0.065, CFI = 0.941, and TLI = 0.933).

Emotional exhaustion subscale

Emotional exhaustion levels among the nursing population were evaluated using the emotional exhaustion subscale, which comprises nine items from the Burnout Inventory developed by Li and Shi [40], grounded in the three-dimensional burnout model [41]. This scale employs a 7-point Likert scoring system, ranging from “Never” (1 point) to “Every day” (7 points), with higher scores indicative of greater emotional exhaustion. This subscale has been extensively utilized by various researchers [21, 22] and demonstrates strong reliability and validity. In the sample of this study, the reliability was found to be excellent, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.871 and Composite Reliability (CR) of 0.893. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) revealed satisfactory construct validity (χ²/df = 2.457, RMSEA = 0.061, CFI = 0.933, and TLI = 0.913).

Psychological resilience scale

The Chinese version of the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), adapted by Yu and Zhang [42] from the original scale developed by Connor and Davidson [26], was used to assess nurses’ psychological resilience levels. This scale comprises 25 items and employs a 5-point Likert scoring system, with responses ranging from “Never” (1 point) to “Always” (5 points). Higher scores on the scale indicate greater levels of psychological resilience. This Chinese adaptation has been employed by numerous researchers [29, 43] and has demonstrated strong reliability and validity. In the sample of this study, the reliability was exceptionally high, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.933 and Composite Reliability (CR) of 0.949. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) further validated the construct validity (χ²/df = 2.15, RMSEA = 0.06, CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.96).

Data analysis procedure

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25.0 and AMOS version 25.0. The criterion for statistical significance was established as α = 0.05 (two-tailed).

Prior to formal analysis, the normality of the variables was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The risk of common method bias (CMB) was thoroughly assessed using various methods including Harman’s single-factor test and Confirmatory Factor Analysis. Descriptive statistics were reported as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD), with levels organized according to the theoretical median reference method. To account for potential confounding variables (hospital category, sex, professional titles, educational attainment, and clinical tenure), the relationships between variables were analyzed using partial correlation analysis.

Testing the moderation effects adhered to a standardized procedure. Initially, the independent variables, surface acting and deep acting, along with the moderator variable, psychological resilience, were mean centered. Subsequently, interaction terms were constructed for “surface acting × psychological resilience” and “deep acting × psychological resilience”. Hierarchical regression analysis was conducted using Hayes’ PROCESS v4.0 macro (Model 1). In Step 1, only the control variables were included; Step 2 incorporated the centered main effect terms; and Step 3 introduced the interaction terms. All models employed the bias-corrected bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples to compute the 95% confidence intervals. The changes in ΔR² were recorded for each step. For interactions that reached statistical significance, a simple slope analysis was performed by categorizing psychological resilience as mean ± 1 standard deviation. Moderation effect diagrams were generated and the change rate of the effect size (Δβ%) was calculated to elucidate the specific nature of the moderation mechanisms.

Results

Normality test and common method bias test

An assessment of normality for the variables of emotional labor, psychological resilience, and emotional exhaustion was conducted using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test. The results indicate that the absolute values of kurtosis for each variable ranged from 0.412 to 0.943, while the absolute values of skewness ranged from 0.037 to 0.995. Drezner and Zerom [44] asserted that normality tests are often stringent and that absolute normality is seldom achieved; however, if the absolute kurtosis of the data distribution is less than 10 and the absolute skewness is less than 3, the data can be considered to approximate a normal distribution. Consequently, the data in this study can be regarded as conforming to a normal distribution.

In this study, all variables were measured via self-report scales, which may introduce common method bias (CMB). To rigorously assess and mitigate this potential issue, a three-step validation approach was employed for comprehensive CMB diagnosis. First, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted as a preliminary screen: exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with an unrotated solution extracted five factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, accounting for 68.96% of the total variance. The largest factor explained only 29.65% of the variance, well below the 40% critical threshold [45], precluding dominant single-method factor influence. Second, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used for model comparison: the single-factor model showed poor fit (χ²/df = 3.276, CFI = 0.822, NFI = 0.763, NNFI = 0.813, RMSEA = 0.106), while the hypothesized five-factor baseline model exhibited excellent fit (χ²/df = 2.182, CFI = 0.937, RMSEA = 0.052). The ΔCFI of 0.115 (exceeding the 0.10 threshold) confirmed strong discriminant validity across variables. Finally, the Unmeasured Latent Method Factor (ULMF) approach [45]—a gold-standard technique for CMB testing in organizational behavior research—was applied. A hybrid model incorporating an unmeasured latent method factor was constructed based on the five-factor baseline model, with theoretical factor loadings constrained and method factor loadings freely estimated. Model fit comparisons revealed no substantive improvement after introducing the method factor (baseline: χ²/df = 2.182, CFI = 0.937, RMSEA = 0.052; hybrid: χ²/df = 2.158, CFI = 0.943, RMSEA = 0.051; ΔCFI = 0.006 < 0.01). Additionally, the 95% confidence intervals of correlation coefficients between all core latent variables ranged from 0.17 to 0.61 (excluding 1), ruling out severe multicollinearity and method factor-induced distortions to inherent variable relationships. Collectively, this three-step empirical evidence chain demonstrates that CMB did not pose a meaningful threat to the validity of the study’s findings.

Descriptive statistics of key variables

An evaluation framework was developed using a theoretical median reference method. In the absence of standardized norms, a positioning analysis was conducted using the theoretical median of each scale as a benchmark. The results indicated that among the emotional labor strategies employed by nurses, the surface acting score (M = 3.54 ± 0.78) and deep acting score (M = 3.67 ± 0.76) were both significantly higher than the theoretical median of 3, as determined by one-sample z-tests (z = 15.952, p < 0.001; z = 20.042, p < 0.001). A paired t-test revealed that deep acting was significantly more prevalent than surface acting (t = 2.267, p = 0.024), confirming that nurses frequently used deep acting strategies, resulting in an overall high level of emotional labor. In terms of emotional exhaustion, the mean score (M = 4.11 ± 1.18) did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference from the theoretical median of 4 (z = 1.794, p = 0.073), with 51.0% of participants scoring above the median, indicating that exhaustion levels were generally moderate. Furthermore, the psychological resilience score (M = 3.48 ± 0.66) was significantly higher than the theoretical median of 3 (z = 16.642, p < 0.001), with 65.2% of the sample scoring above the median, reflecting a relatively high level of psychological resilience within this group.

Partial correlation analysis and collinearity testing

After controlling for potential confounding variables, the results of the partial correlation analysis indicated the following (refer to Table 2): Deep acting exhibited a significant negative correlation with surface acting (r = -0.343, p < 0.01) and a significant negative correlation with emotional exhaustion (r = -0.466, p < 0.01). Conversely, deep acting had a significant positive correlation with psychological resilience (r = 0.151, p < 0.01). Surface acting was found to have a significant positive correlation with emotional exhaustion (r = 0.418, p < 0.01) and a significant negative correlation with psychological resilience (r = -0.102, p < 0.01). Additionally, emotional exhaustion was significantly negatively correlated with psychological resilience (r = -0.559, p < 0.01).

Collinearity was diagnosed using dual criteria. First, all pairwise correlation coefficients were below the threshold of 0.80, indicating the absence of severe collinearity. Second, the Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) ranged from 1.304 to 2.432, all of which were significantly below the empirical threshold of 10. Additionally, the tolerance values ranged from 0.411 to 0.767, all exceeding the critical standard of 0.10 [46]. Dual testing confirmed that there were no significant collinearity issues among the variables, thereby satisfying the prerequisite assumptions for subsequent regression analysis.

Moderation effect test

Hierarchical regression results are presented in Table 3. Nurses’ surface acting was significantly and positively correlated with emotional exhaustion (β = 0.342, 95% CI [0.291, 0.393], p<0.001), which supported Hypothesis H1a. Deep acting was significantly and negatively correlated with emotional exhaustion (β=-0.337, 95% CI [-0.389, -0.285], p<0.001), verifying Hypothesis H1b. The correlation effect sizes of the two emotional labor strategies were roughly equivalent (|β|≈0.34), suggesting that the correlation between emotional labor and emotional exhaustion depended primarily on the type of emotional labor strategy rather than its overall intensity.

In the correlation path between surface acting and emotional exhaustion, psychological resilience exhibited a significant negative moderating correlation (interaction term β=-0.159, 95% CI [-0.214, -0.104], p<0.001). The moderating effect size was Cohen’s f²=0.055, which explained an additional 2.5% of the variance (ΔR²=0.025). This finding indicated that psychological resilience weakened the strength of the positive correlation between surface acting and emotional exhaustion, thus validating H2a. In the path linking deep acting to emotional exhaustion, psychological resilience played a significant positive moderating role (interaction term β = 0.183, 95% CI [0.121, 0.245], p<0.001), with an effect size of f²=0.064 and an additional 2.8% incremental variance explained (ΔR²=0.028). It demonstrated that psychological resilience strengthened the negative correlation between deep acting and emotional exhaustion, which was consistent with Hypothesis H2b. The incremental explained variance of both moderating effects exceeded the empirical critical standard of 2%. Furthermore, Bootstrap confidence intervals generated by 5,000 repeated sampling did not contain zero, which confirmed the good statistical robustness of this cross-sectional correlation model.

Simple slope analysis (Figs. 2 and 3) further depicted the moderating attributes of psychological resilience. For individuals with low psychological resilience, the positive correlation between surface acting and emotional exhaustion was more salient (β = 0.501, p<0.001). In contrast, among participants with high psychological resilience, the magnitude of this positive correlation decreased by 63.5%, with the correlation strength falling to β = 0.183. Meanwhile, the negative correlation between deep acting and emotional exhaustion was relatively mild in those with low psychological resilience (β=-0.154, p<0.001), while such negative correlation increased dramatically by 237.7% among individuals with high psychological resilience, reaching a correlation coefficient of β=-0.520. These findings reflected the dual-path moderating characteristics of psychological resilience in the correlation patterns between two emotional labor strategies and emotional exhaustion: it attenuated the high correlation risk of surface acting and amplified the beneficial correlation attributes of deep acting.

This study also identified a prominent asymmetry in the magnitude of psychological resilience moderation. The reinforcing magnitude for the negative correlation of deep acting (237.7%) was markedly higher than the weakening magnitude for the positive correlation of surface acting (63.5%), and the former was approximately 3.7 times the latter. Such asymmetric moderating characteristics provide refined empirical evidence for clarifying the boundary attributes of psychological resilience in emotional labor research from a cross-sectional perspective.

Discussion

Analysis of research findings

Characteristics of nurses’ emotional labor and emotional exhaustion

This study found that nurses’ emotional labor is generally high, which is consistent with the conclusions of a series of empirical studies in the East Asian medical context [1, 20, 47]. A core finding is that deep acting has a significantly greater impact on nursing practice than surface acting, indicating that nurses can establish a dynamic balance between maintaining a professional image and conducting therapeutic communication through refined emotional regulation strategies [8, 43]. This phenomenon stems from two interrelated core mechanisms.

The first is strategy optimization at the organizational support level. The three-dimensional intervention strategies (clinical supervision, Balint groups, and mindfulness courses) implemented in Chinese hospitals have effectively optimized the structure of nurses’ emotional labor [47, 48]. Specifically, clinical supervision improves the accuracy of nurses’ emotional labor strategy selection through emotional reflection training [48], while Balint groups accelerate the awakening of nurses’ awareness of deep acting through case discussions. Together with mindfulness courses, they form a strategy optimization loop that significantly enhances organizational support for deep acting [49].

Second, value empowerment is at a cultural level. The model of Chinese nurses prioritizing deep acting is rooted in the in-depth integration of collectivist culture and characteristics of the nursing profession [50, 51]. On the one hand, nursing teams build a collective emotional network through morning narrative sharing and cross-departmental reviews, further strengthening the practical effectiveness of deep acting [49]. However, cultural cognition shapes “patient recovery” as a collective team achievement, promoting the internalization of emotional labor from external requirements to internal awareness [50]. This cultural mechanism makes deep acting a core feature of Chinese nursing practice, which is significantly different from the balanced distribution of emotional labor strategies in Western individualist cultures [51], providing multi-dimensional theoretical support for occupational health interventions.

The predominance of deep acting among nurses stands in stark contrast to the moderate levels of emotional exhaustion typically observed within this profession. This phenomenon, characterized as “high emotional labor input coupled with moderate exhaustion levels,” can be attributed to a dual synergistic buffering mechanism. First, the emotional labor strategies employed by nurses exhibit protective effects; specifically, the resource gain spiral generated by deep acting can effectively mitigate resource depletion associated with surface acting [11, 49], thereby establishing a vital protective barrier within the occupational setting. Second, the nursing population demonstrated a psychological resilience reserve that was significantly above average. In the sample analyzed, 44.78% of participants possessed a bachelor’s degree or higher, while 33.21% held the title of Charge Nurse or a higher position. These highly skilled groups cultivate domain-specific problem-solving schemas through repeated engagement in clinical scenarios, which substantially enhances their cognitive restructuring capabilities when confronted with complex work stress [50]. This effectively buffers emotional exhaustion and reduces its risk and intensity of emotional exhaustion.

Based on the above characteristics of nurses’ emotional labor and emotional exhaustion, hospitals are recommended to rely on well-established organizational interventions and local cultural advantages to further consolidate the positive emotional labor mode dominated by deep acting among nurses. Meanwhile, full consideration should be given to the differences in psychological resources among nurses at different levels. Targeted emotional management and psychological cultivation programs should be supplemented for nurses with low educational attainment and professional titles, so as to integrally strengthen the emotional buffering capacity of the nursing team and steadily relieve potential emotional exhaustion risks under long-term high emotional labor load.

Dual-path association model of emotional labor

This study systematically validated the dual-path association model between emotional labor strategies and emotional exhaustion. The statistical analysis of the surface acting pathway indicated a significant positive correlation between surface acting and levels of emotional exhaustion, a finding that is highly consistent with research conducted by scholars such as Luo et al. [52]. The underlying mechanism of this relationship is congruent with the resource loss spiral proposed by COR Theory [10]. Specifically, when nurses engage in prolonged emotional pretenses in professional environments, a persistent disconnection emerges between the management of professional expressions and authentic emotional experiences. This disconnection triggers dysregulation of self-consistency and undermines social support systems, ultimately resulting in a detrimental cycle (characterized by “resource loss – weakened occupational identity – continued depletion of psychological resources”), which is a critical catalytic factor contributing to emotional exhaustion.

The concept of deep acting has been shown to have a significant negative association effect, which aligns with the findings of research conducted by Xie [53] and Su et al. [54]. This effect originates from the construction mechanism of the resource-gain spiral [11]. The authenticity of nurse-patient interactions is markedly enhanced when nurses successfully internalize emotions through cognitive reappraisal. This enhancement activates a bidirectional resource proliferation pathway: on one hand, patients’ positive emotional feedback—such as improved treatment adherence and expressions of sincere gratitude—provides external resource replenishment; on the other hand, an increase in professional self-efficacy and a heightened sense of meaning contribute to internal resource regeneration. This synergistic positive feedback loop effectively mitigates the psychological pathways that lead to emotional exhaustion [52].

Based on the dual-pathway correlational characteristics of emotional labor in relation to emotional exhaustion, clinical nursing management is suggested to optimize intervention strategies in a targeted manner. It is critical to focus on the resource gain advantages of deep acting and incorporate deep acting-related training into the regular continuing education system for nurses. Through nurse-patient communication skill training and targeted cognitive reappraisal exercises [55, 56], nurses can be guided to actively master emotional internalization strategies, strengthen the internal and external interactive resource proliferation effect triggered by deep acting, and give full play to its inhibitory function on emotional exhaustion. Meanwhile, attention should also be paid to the resource depletion caused by surface acting. Supporting emotional adjustment guidance can help nurses reduce psychological internal consumption resulting from emotional pretense [57], and realize the rational adaptation of the two types of emotional labor strategies. From the dual dimensions of resource depletion prevention and control as well as resource gain enhancement, this measure can effectively safeguard nurses’ occupational mental health.

Differential and asymmetric moderating effects of psychological resilience

The differential moderating effect of psychological resilience

This study demonstrated that psychological resilience plays a differential and dynamic moderating role in the relationship between nurses’ emotional labor strategies and emotional exhaustion. Specifically, it exhibits a buffering effect on resource depletion associated with surface acting, and a facilitative effect on resource regeneration related to deep acting. This finding empirically substantiates a fundamental proposition of the Boundary Conditions Theory of Emotional Labor [35] for the first time within the nursing context, indicating that individual trait variables such as psychological resilience systematically influence the effect boundaries of emotional labor strategies through resource orchestration capabilities. This provides new evidence for the dynamic adaptability of COR Theory [10].

At the surface acting level, psychological resilience serves as a buffer against resource depletion within the framework of the “resource regulator” [30], exerting a significant negative moderating effect that aligns closely with the fundamental principles of the COR Theory [10]. Surface acting, an emotionally labor-intensive strategy that passively depletes psychological resources, necessitates continuous attentional monitoring to ensure that emotional expressions conform to professional standards. This state of “affective-cognitive dissociation” can easily provoke internal psychological conflict, resulting in a persistent depletion of self-regulatory resources and contributing to feelings of exhaustion [58]. Psychological resilience plays a critical role in alleviating the resource drain associated with emotional pretense by employing cognitive reappraisal mechanisms throughout this process, thereby functioning as an essential internal buffer [35]. Nurses with high levels of resilience can utilize more robust social support systems to obtain greater emotional sustenance, effectively mitigating psychological resource depletion linked to surface acting. As a result, even when engaging in surface acting with a greater frequency, these individuals are less susceptible to emotional exhaustion. In contrast, nurses with low resilience who lack sufficient social support and emotional buffering experience continuous resource depletion without effective compensation as surface acting increases. This situation can lead them to fall into a “resource loss spiral,” resulting in emotional dissonance and exhaustion [59].

At the level of deep acting, psychological resilience exerts a significant positive moderating effect as a “resource regeneration facilitator ‘within the “resource regulator’, further empirical support for COR Theory’s assertion that “individuals actively employ strategies to acquire new resources to enhance adaptation”. Although deep acting necessitates a short-term investment in psychological energy, the positive outcomes it engenders—such as favorable patient interactions and an enhanced sense of professional value—can be converted into long-term psychological resource reserves. Within this process, psychological resilience improves the efficiency of resource regeneration derived from authentic emotional engagement through a positive feedback loop, functioning as a central mechanism for replenishing psychological resources and generating synergistic effects with deep acting [60]. Specifically, nurses exhibiting high psychological resilience are able to rapidly transform positive experiences elicited by deep acting into increased self-efficacy and expanded social support networks, thereby initiating a “resource gain spiral” that substantially mitigates emotional exhaustion. Conversely, nurses with low psychological resilience, characterized by limited resource regeneration capacity (e.g., reduced cognitive flexibility or inadequate social support), demonstrate markedly diminished efficiency in resource replenishment through deep acting, resulting in a weakened capacity to inhibit emotional exhaustion.

The asymmetric moderating effect of psychological resilience

Notably, the moderating effect of psychological resilience on emotional labor strategies exhibits a significant asymmetric characteristic: the magnitude of its enhancement of the protective effect of deep acting (gain effect) is significantly greater than its buffering effect on the negative consequences of surface acting. This characteristic stems from the inherent differences between emotional labor strategies and the reinforcing mechanisms of the occupational context [61], and can also be explained within the framework of COR Theory [10].

In terms of the inherent nature of these strategies, deep acting possesses resource regenerative properties. For instance, authentic emotional investment fosters professional fulfillment, and psychological resilience accelerates the cycle of resource gain by amplifying the intrinsic value of deep acting [61]. By contrast, surface acting is inherently a process of continuous resource depletion. Psychological resilience can only buffer the rate of this depletion through strategies such as cognitive reappraisal but cannot fundamentally reverse the trend of resource loss [62]. From the perspective of COR Theory [10], psychological resilience functions as an “amplifier” in the gain pathway, enabling each unit of emotional investment to yield a higher return on resources. Conversely, in the buffering pathway, it merely acts as a “decelerator”—capable of slowing down resource consumption but unable to eliminate it entirely. This mechanism leads nurses with high resilience to systematically prioritize deep acting as their dominant strategy based on cost-benefit principles [34], thereby demonstrating a significantly lower risk of burnout under conditions of equivalent emotional labor intensity. This differentiation in mechanisms supports Hobfoll’s [63] proposed “Resource Investment Preference Law,” which posits that psychological resilience motivates individuals to allocate their limited resources toward strategies with the potential for appreciation (e.g., deep acting) rather than depleting them on defensive behaviors (e.g., surface acting). This finding offers a new perspective for explaining the occupational characteristics of groups in healthcare organizations that exhibit “high job demands alongside low burnout.”

Based on the core finding that psychological resilience exhibits significant differentiated and asymmetric moderating effects across different emotional labor pathways, clinical intervention and management practices should develop targeted strategies in line with these dual attributes. In terms of differentiated moderation, targeted measures should be formulated according to distinct emotional labor patterns. For nurses with frequent surface acting, psychological resilience training [64] can strengthen their resource buffering ability to relieve resource depletion induced by continuous emotional pretense. For nurses mainly adopting deep acting, emphasis should be placed on activating the resource regeneration function of psychological resilience to consolidate the protective benefits of positive emotional labor [65].

From the perspective of asymmetric moderation, given that the positive reinforcing effect of psychological resilience on deep acting is much more prominent than its negative buffering effect on surface acting, medical institutions should adjust their intervention priorities. Rather than equally allocating psychological support resources, institutions should focus on psychological resilience cultivation and promote the rational application of deep acting, so as to maximize positive empowering effects under this asymmetric mechanism. The combination of differentiated strategies and asymmetric orientation can coordinate the dual pathways of resource depletion buffering and resource gain. Such practice conforms to the inherent logical characteristics of nurses’ emotional labor and helps alleviate emotional exhaustion in a scientific manner.

Theoretical contributions

By clarifying the differentiated and asymmetric moderating mechanism of psychological resilience between emotional labor strategies and emotional exhaustion (the effect size of gain effect was 3.7 times that of buffering effect), this study expands the theoretical system of occupational health psychology from two dimensions.

This study optimizes the dynamic explanatory framework of the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory [10]. Although Hobfoll (2011) [63] proposed the dual-path perspective of resource loss and resource gain, traditional COR studies predominantly focus on passive resource conservation while neglecting active resource proliferation. Meanwhile, previous research generally regards psychological resilience as a homogeneous buffering trait and fails to differentiate its roles across various emotional labor strategies. The present findings indicate that psychological resilience reshapes resource flow through two pathways. In the loss-buffering pathway, it weakens resource depletion caused by surface acting (interaction β=−0.159, buffering effect = 62.4%) and exerts defensive protection. In the gain-promoting pathway, it strengthens resource proliferation induced by deep acting (interaction β = 0.183, gain effect = 234.2%) and facilitates proactive resource investment. Furthermore, psychological resilience presents an obvious gain-priority tendency, with a much stronger empowering effect on deep acting than the buffering effect on surface acting. These findings enrich the theoretical connotation of “strategy-specific resource investment” and extend COR theory from a single resource conservation perspective to a dynamic resource allocation perspective.

In tandem with optimizing COR theory, this study complements the core logic of the boundary condition theory of emotional labor [35]. Existing studies often assume the homogeneity of individual trait moderation, rarely discuss the asymmetric influences of personal traits on distinct emotional labor strategies, and cannot well explain nurses’ strategy selection differences under high-pressure clinical contexts. This study proposes the characteristic of strategy-specific moderation: psychological resilience not only exerts differentiated impacts on the outcomes of two types of emotional labor but also changes individuals’ strategy adoption tendency through asymmetric effects. The prominent gain effect of deep acting (β=−0.184 in the low-resilience group vs. β=−0.615 in the high-resilience group) helps form a virtuous cycle of “high psychological resilience–frequent deep acting–sustainable resource gain”. This conclusion extends the simple correlation analysis of emotional labor to an integrated analytical framework of “trait–strategy–resource benefit”, provides theoretical references for explaining the heterogeneity of occupational adaptation patterns, and promotes the refinement of boundary condition research in emotional labor.

Collectively, this study supplements and improves the application scope and explanatory boundary of COR theory and emotional labor boundary condition theory. On the one hand, it enriches the application of COR theory in resource gain and differentiated resource allocation. On the other hand, it expands the asymmetric moderating connotation of psychological resilience as a critical boundary variable, offering new theoretical insights for nursing psychology research on the integration of “trait–strategy–occupational mental health”.

Research limitations

This study presents four main limitations arising from methodological and theoretical constraints.

(1) Regarding sample representativeness and ecological validity, participants were mainly recruited from 11 economically developed provinces in eastern and southern China, with insufficient coverage of underdeveloped and remote regions, which limits the generalizability of the findings to resource-limited clinical settings. Although the high proportion of female nurses (96.96%) reflects the demographic characteristics of the nursing workforce, it neglects the unique emotion regulation patterns of male nurses and restricts the comprehensive interpretation of resilience’s buffering effect on surface acting [66]. Besides, convenience cluster sampling was applied in this study; despite the inclusion of multiple hospital tiers and cross-regional samples, the randomness of recruitment remains limited compared with stratified random sampling. Future studies should adopt multicenter, nationwide stratified random sampling to balance regional distribution, medical resource conditions and gender composition, so as to improve external validity.

(2) In terms of measurement tools and research design, all data relied on self-reported questionnaires, which may trigger social desirability bias and cause underreporting of surface acting and overestimation of deep acting. Although common method bias was controlled statistically, the correlation coefficients among variables might still be overestimated. The emotional labor scale originated from Western individualistic contexts and cannot fully capture collectivist emotional norms in Chinese nursing scenarios, thereby reducing local ecological validity. In addition, the cross-sectional design cannot clarify causal directions or dynamic changes of psychological resilience. Future longitudinal or prospective cohort studies are recommended to examine temporal relationships and dynamic evolutionary characteristics among variables.

(3) Concerning physiological indicators and dimensional differentiation of psychological constructs, this study only adopted psychological scales and failed to collect objective physiological data such as HPA axis and autonomic nervous system indicators, making it impossible to quantify physiological stress responses caused by emotional labor. Moreover, this research focused only on the overall effect of psychological resilience without subdividing its sub-dimensions or adopting person-centered analytical methods such as latent profile analysis. Future research should combine psychological scales with physiological biomarkers and multi-dimensional measurement of resilience to further reveal its heterogeneous moderating mechanisms.

(4) With respect to cross-cultural applicability, this study was conducted solely within the Chinese collectivist cultural background and lacked cross-cultural comparison with samples from individualistic cultures based on Hofstede’s cultural dimensions. The existing measurement tools also lack localized revision for Chinese nursing contexts, which may lead to cultural adaptation bias and restrict the cross-cultural promotion of the asymmetric moderation model. Future scholars can conduct cross-cultural comparative research and revise culturally appropriate scales to verify the cultural universality and boundary conditions of relevant theories and research findings.

Conclusions

Guided by COR theory, this cross-sectional study explored the correlational patterns between emotional labor strategies and emotional exhaustion among nurses. It should be noted that only static cross-sectional associations were examined in this research, and causal inferences require further evidence from longitudinal designs.

First, nurses reported a moderately high overall level of emotional labor, with significantly higher engagement in deep acting than surface acting. Such distributional characteristics indicate that clinical nurses prefer adaptive emotion regulation based on cognitive adjustment over superficial emotional suppression, which aligns with the occupational features of emotional labor within the East Asian healthcare context.

Second, two distinct correlational pathways linked emotional labor strategies to emotional exhaustion. Surface acting was positively correlated with emotional exhaustion, consistent with the resource loss spiral proposed by COR theory. In contrast, deep acting was negatively correlated with emotional exhaustion and corresponded to the accumulation and replenishment of personal psychological resources. The two strategies exhibited clearly divergent correlational patterns.

Third, psychological resilience served as a differentiated and asymmetric moderator across the dual pathways of emotional labor. It attenuated the undesirable correlation between surface acting and emotional exhaustion and simultaneously amplified the favorable association of deep acting. Notably, the enhancing effect on adaptive emotional labor was considerably stronger than the buffering effect against maladaptive emotional labor, forming a distinct asymmetric moderating pattern. This finding reveals the differentiated resource-based functions of psychological resilience across varied emotion regulation approaches and constitutes a core theoretical contribution of this study.

In summary, this study clarifies the dual correlational pathways of resource depletion and resource gain linking surface acting and deep acting to emotional exhaustion within the COR theoretical framework. By identifying the asymmetric moderating role of psychological resilience, this research refines the understanding of boundary conditions underlying the association between emotional labor and emotional exhaustion. These outcomes extend the applicability of COR theory in nursing populations and offer evidence-based implications for occupational health interventions. Targeted improvements in psychological resilience may serve as a feasible approach to alleviate emotional exhaustion and promote nurses’ long-term emotional well-being.
