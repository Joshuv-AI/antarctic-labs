IVCM-Insight: automated interactive interpretation of in vivo confocal microscopy
Authors: Zijun Zhang, Shangyang Jiang, Zhiqun Wang, Yang Zhang, Kexin Chen, Mingda Wei, Qingquan Shi, Yuan Wei, Jinding Pang, Xinxin Lu, Qingfeng Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) is a critical ophthalmic examination that provides in vivo cytological and neurological information essential for diagnosing corneal and certain systemic diseases, but its clinical utility is limited by time-consuming interpretation and the need for subspecialty expertise. We developed IVCM-Insight, an artificial intelligence (AI) system integrating image–text contrastive learning with large language models (LLMs) for automated report generation and interactive question answering (QA). Based on 30,368 IVCM images and 4155 paired clinical reports, the model was trained with contrastive alignment, image-conditioned language modeling, and multi-image consistency loss to produce structured diagnostic reports while a domain-adapted LLM supported patient-centered QA. Automated evaluation showed strong agreement with the reference reports: Bilingual Evaluation Understudy (BLEU)-1 to BLEU-4 scores were 0.69, 0.58, 0.47, and 0.41, Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation (ROUGE-L) was 0.67, Consensus-based Image Description Evaluation (CIDEr) was 1.85, and Metric for Evaluation of Translation with Explicit Ordering (METEOR) was 0.66. In addition, the multi-label classification achieved an accuracy of 0.96 and an F1 score of 0.80. Manual assessment by corneal specialists rated report accuracy (4.17), completeness (4.19), coherence (4.70), and diagnostic support (4.06), with excellent inter-rater reliability; QA outputs achieved high accuracy (4.33), relevance (4.54), and non-harmfulness (4.81). Representative cases, including cytomegalovirus, fungal, and Acanthamoeba keratitis, demonstrated accurate detection of key findings and clinically safe explanations. To our knowledge, IVCM-Insight is the first dedicated AI system for comprehensive IVCM interpretation, with potential to enhance diagnostic efficiency, strengthen physician–patient communication, and broaden access to advanced corneal imaging across care settings.

Introduction

In vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) is a non-invasive, high-resolution imaging technique that enables real-time visualization of living tissues at the cellular resolution without biopsy1–3. In ophthalmology, IVCM is widely used to assess corneal microstructural features, including corneal deposits, subbasal nerves, and inflammatory cell-related changes, etc4–7. In neuroscience, corneal nerve and immune-cell-associated readouts from IVCM have been investigated as minimally invasive biomarkers in systemic and neurologic diseases, including type 2 diabetes-related neuropathy and inflammation, Parkinson’s disease, and multiple sclerosis8–11. However, IVCM provides image-based data rather than direct neural information, making interpretation time-consuming and highly expertise-dependent12. In remote or densely populated settings, there is an urgent need for a reliable, generalizable AI-assisted system to support image interpretation. Moreover, the technical language of IVCM reports often limits patient understanding, underscoring the necessity of AI-driven tools to aid both interpretation and report generation.

Generative AI models create clinically relevant text and images by learning data patterns13. Their use in medicine has expanded rapidly, especially for image interpretation and automated reporting14,15. Among them, large language models (LLMs) generate human-like responses to complex queries16 and, in ophthalmology, support imaging explanations, structured summaries, and patient-friendly communication17–20. Generative AI also enables fundus analysis, optical coherence tomography (OCT) reporting, and retinal pathology characterization21–23, underscoring its potential to improve diagnosis, efficiency, and patient care.

To our knowledge, no AI model has been specifically developed for the comprehensive interpretation and automated report generation of IVCM images. Previous studies have primarily applied deep learning to narrowly defined tasks, such as disease detection or classification. For example, Ruiz-Lozano et al. analyzed subbasal nerve plexus features at the inferior whorl in neuropathic corneal pain and dry eye disease using deep learning24, while Shareef et al.25 and Lincke et al.26 developed AI-based systems for diagnosing Acanthamoeba keratitis from IVCM images. While these approaches have demonstrated clinical value by improving diagnostic accuracy, their scope remains limited, lacking the capacity to integrate diverse image features, provide narrative explanations, or generate structured clinical reports that support broader decision-making. Furthermore, they lack interactive capabilities, which prevents them from supporting communication between clinicians and patients.

To overcome these limitations, we developed a framework that integrates contrastive learning with LLMs to generate accurate IVCM reports and support diagnostic decisions, aiming to improve clinical efficiency and broaden access to corneal imaging technologies. Furthermore, by embedding a patient-centered QA component into this workflow, we build directly on the novel paradigm proposed by Badian et al.27 to bridge the gap between automated algorithm verification and contextual, person-centered clinical application.

Results

The training set (3324 reports and 24,339 images) and validation set (415 reports and 3017 images) were used for model development, while the test set (416 reports and 3012 images) was reserved for evaluation. The mean age of the cohort was 51.4 ± 8.3 years, 35.0% of patients were male, and the included examination was from the left eye (OS) in 2186 patients (52.6%). Images covered all major corneal layers, including the epithelium (32.1%), corneal subbasal nerve plexus (SBNP) (22.7%), stroma (22.6%), and endothelium (22.6%). Each patient had an accompanying clinical IVCM report, with a mean length of 117.8 ± 41.4 words. Functional modules of the integrated IVCM-Insight platform

To improve clinical efficiency and patient access, we developed IVCM-Insight, an integrated platform that combines automated IVCM report generation with interactive question answering (Fig. 2). The platform consists of three core modules: (A) a report generation module that automatically produces structured diagnostic reports from 6-9 uploaded IVCM images organized by scanning depth and extracts condition-related keywords to support clinical decision-making (Fig. 2A); (B) a question-answering module that generates concise, report-based responses to user queries, facilitating clinician–patient communication (Fig. 2B); and (C) an auxiliary module that supports JPEG images and text inputs, initiates new conversations, and provides access to prior reports through a historical function (Fig. 2C). Automatic evaluation of report generation and condition classification

The performance of IVCM-Insight in automated report generation was evaluated using standard NLG and MLC metrics, demonstrating strong agreement with reference reports (Table 3). In the test dataset, the automated report generation module of our deployed implementation took 2.68 ± 0.61 s/case, substantially less than manual reporting, 209.9 ± 41.5 s/case. The model achieved BLEU1-4 scores of 0.69, 0.58, 0.47, and 0.41; a ROUGE-L score of 0.67; a CIDEr score of 1.85; and a METEOR score of 0.66. These results indicate strong lexical and semantic concordance with the original reports. MLC metrics demonstrated that IVCM-Insight effectively identified key pathological conditions from IVCM reports, supporting image interpretation and automated report generation. The system achieved an average accuracy of 0.96 and an F1 score of 0.80. At the condition level, performance was high with F1 scores of 0.95 for epithelial defects, 0.88 for reduced subbasal nerve density, 0.90 for stromal cell activation, and 0.87 for keratic precipitates. Inflammatory cell infiltration, affecting multiple layers, achieved the highest performance (F1 = 0.99). Manual evaluation of report quality and QA performance

In the manual evaluation, three experienced corneal specialists independently reviewed the reports and QA outputs generated by IVCM-Insight. For report quality (Table 4), the system received consistently high ratings across all four dimensions, with mean scores of 4.17 for accuracy, 4.19 for completeness, 4.70 for logical coherence, and 4.06 for diagnostic support. Logical coherence achieved the highest score, indicating that the reports maintained a clear structure and clinically appropriate reasoning. Diagnostic support was rated slightly lower, indicating limited emphasis on differential diagnosis or treatment implications. The ICC values ranged from 0.78 to 0.92, demonstrating excellent inter-rater reliability. Following the manual evaluation of report quality, QA performance was assessed using 100 QA pairs derived from 20 representative reports with perfect quality scores. IVCM-Insight took 12.24 ± 10.81 s/question and demonstrated strong performance, with mean scores of 4.33 for accuracy, 4.21 for completeness, 4.54 for relevance, and 4.81 for non-harmfulness (Table 4). Non-harmfulness achieved the highest score, highlighting the system’s safety in avoiding misleading or potentially harmful content. The ICC values ranged from 0.71 to 0.83, indicating substantial inter-rater consistency. Clinical application of IVCM-Insight

To evaluate the clinical applicability of IVCM-Insight, the system was implemented in a series of selected cases alongside routine clinical reporting. Each case was independently diagnosed and documented through the standard clinical workflow, with IVCM-Insight applied exclusively as a supplementary tool to enable comparative analysis. Representative cases illustrating its performance and potential clinical utility are presented in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Representative real-world clinical cases analyzed with IVCM-Insight.A Cytomegalovirus keratitis; B fungal keratitis; and C Acanthamoeba keratitis.

Representative real-world clinical cases analyzed with IVCM-Insight.

A Cytomegalovirus keratitis; B fungal keratitis; and C Acanthamoeba keratitis.

Across different conditions, IVCM-Insight reproduced key findings consistent with clinician-generated reports. In cytomegalovirus keratitis (Fig. 4A), the system identified keratic precipitates and owl’s eye appearance cells, with the QA module explaining their association with chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. In fungal keratitis (Fig. 4B), epithelial swelling, inflammatory infiltration, and stromal hyphae extending to 132 μm were detected, with the QA module underscoring their diagnostic value for confirming infection and estimating disease severity. In Acanthamoeba keratitis (Fig. 4C), epithelial defects, cyst-like structures, and stromal activation were recognized, and the QA module clarified that cysts represent the resistant form of Acanthamoeba, highlighting their role in persistent infection and the consequent need for targeted therapeutic intervention. Taken together, these representative cases demonstrate the ability of IVCM-Insight to complement standard practice by generating structured reports and providing patient-accessible explanations. While exploratory in nature, these findings indicate potential clinical value and highlight the necessity of validation in larger, systematically assessed cohorts.

Discussion

IVCM is a critical ophthalmic examination that provides in vivo cytological and neurological information essential for diagnosing corneal and certain systemic diseases28. However, the interpretation of IVCM images relies heavily on a limited number of subspecialty experts, which significantly restricts its routine clinical application and widespread adoption12. In this study, we developed IVCM-Insight, an AI system that innovatively integrates contrastive learning with LLMs to facilitate automated IVCM report generation and interactive clinical question–answering. The system was validated using both automated metrics, including NLG and MLC, as well as manual evaluation by experienced ophthalmologists, demonstrating high reliability and clinical relevance. These results highlight the potential of advanced AI approaches to improve IVCM interpretation and communication, addressing a critical gap by providing a dedicated tool for IVCM report generation and interactive clinical explanation.

At present, most AI models based on IVCM remain focused on image-level deep learning. By constructing disease-specific datasets, these models have been applied to image classification to assist in corneal disease diagnosis6,29,30, to differentiate inflammatory cell types31, and other tasks. Essalat et al. employed multiple CNN architectures to distinguish Acanthamoeba keratitis, fungal keratitis, nonspecific keratitis, and normal corneas using IVCM images29. Lin et al. developed a model to detect fungal hyphae in IVCM images with an accuracy of 93.6%30. Koseoglu et al. trained an EfficientNet-B3 on a large multicenter dataset to identify microneuromas, enabling screening for neuropathic corneal pain6. Beyond classification, annotation of IVCM elements such as cell boundaries and nerves has enabled deep learning models to perform entity recognition tasks, supporting quantitative analysis of corneal nerve morphology32–35 and automated endothelial cell counting36–38. Additionally, Tang et al. proposed an unsupervised framework for anomaly detection in IVCM images, aiming to identify atypical cellular or structural features without relying on extensive labeled datasets39. Recently, further progress has been made in IVCM imaging itself. Downie et al. combined time-lapse imaging with computational methods to develop the FUN-IVCM software40–43, enabling repeated scanning of the same tissue region and, for the first time, in vivo characterization of corneal cell motility, thereby expanding the clinical capabilities of IVCM. Collectively, these studies underscore the progress in AI-driven image analysis for IVCM, while highlighting the need for systems that integrate higher-level reasoning, report generation, and interactive clinical interpretation. Despite these advances, existing approaches exhibit several common limitations. First, they are generally restricted to single-image analysis and do not integrate information across multiple corneal layers or regions, limiting their applicability to single-disease or lesion-specific tasks. Second, these models rely predominantly on image-level supervision and do not incorporate the rich semantic information embedded in clinical reports, limiting their ability to generate structured diagnostic outputs.

To address these gaps, we developed a structured report generation model within IVCM-Insight, based on an image–text contrastive learning framework. The model was trained on a comprehensive IVCM dataset encompassing all corneal layers, scanning depths, and lesion regions, enabling the integration of multi-layered spatial information. By aligning visual features with textual descriptions, the model generated coherent and clinically interpretable reports. Furthermore, through automated keyword extraction, IVCM-Insight accurately captured key pathological findings, demonstrating strong performance in detecting common abnormalities such as epithelial defects, reduced subbasal nerve density, and inflammatory cell infiltration. This framework facilitates a more holistic analysis of corneal pathology while substantially reducing report preparation time and dependence on physician expertise.

In clinical practice, effective physician–patient communication is essential44. However, the technical complexity of IVCM reports often impedes patient comprehension. To our knowledge, no LLM has been specifically designed to analyze and interpret IVCM reports. To address this gap, IVCM-Insight incorporates an LLM-driven interactive QA module. This module generates concise, accessible, and accurate responses to patient queries regarding report content and key findings, while ensuring clinical safety, thereby facilitating more effective communication. Notably, this capability is particularly valuable in primary healthcare settings, where limited clinical experience with IVCM may impede accurate interpretation, despite the availability of automated structured reports. By harnessing the interpretive and explanatory power of LLMs, clinicians can achieve a more comprehensive understanding of report content, better recognize the pathological features captured by IVCM, and more effectively apply its clinical information. In this way, IVCM-Insight has the potential to overcome the “last mile” barrier to IVCM adoption in community practice, ultimately contributing to improving standards of corneal disease diagnosis and management.

Contrastive learning is a self-supervised learning paradigm that acquires discriminative and generalizable feature representations by drawing semantically similar samples closer in the embedding space while pushing dissimilar samples farther apart. Its application in ophthalmology has recently attracted increasing attention. For example, Chen et al.18 developed a contrastive learning framework based on the BLIP model for fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) image–report pairs, which was further integrated with the LLaMA-2 model to enable automated report generation and interactive question–answering. Similarly, Shi et al.45 leveraged a CLIP-based framework incorporating eleven ophthalmic imaging modalities and text pairs, achieving strong performance in disease classification, visual question answering, and cross-modal retrieval tasks. Inspired by these advances, the contrastive learning module in this study was designed in a BLIP-like fashion, employing a dual-stream encoder and cross-modal decoder to achieve precise image–text alignment. Furthermore, joint optimization through contrastive learning, image-conditioned language modeling (ICLM), and multi-image consistency constraints were applied to enhance semantic coherence across multiple corneal layers within the same patient. This design constitutes a methodological advance over previous IVCM-based AI models, which were largely restricted to single-disease classification, by enabling the generation of structured, logically coherent, and clinically interpretable diagnostic reports.

To further enhance system interactivity, this study also incorporated an LLM. LLMs are deep neural networks trained on massive text corpora, capable of understanding and generating natural language, and demonstrating human-like reasoning and expressive abilities across a wide range of tasks. In ophthalmology, Wu et al.46 explored the application of LLMs in responding to questions related to diabetic retinopathy (DR), validating through expert evaluation that such models can provide educational and explanatory support for patients. Building on this, Haghighi et al.47 demonstrated that domain-specific fine-tuning with ophthalmology-focused corpora can markedly improve the performance of LLM-based question–answering systems. In our study, we constructed a domain-adapted corpus comprising 30 representative clinical question–answer pairs and applied lightweight fine-tuning to DeepSeek:70B, a bilingual LLM with strong ophthalmic text-generation capability. This strategy enabled the system to achieve a complete functional loop in the IVCM setting, bridging automated report generation with patient-centered communication.

In this study, IVCM-Insight established a generalizable and efficient paradigm for medical image analysis and interpretation. Beyond IVCM, this framework has the potential to be extended to other ophthalmic modalities, including slit-lamp photography, OCT, FFA, and even histopathological analysis. More importantly, the model demonstrates broad scalability and could be applied to systemic diseases, thereby supporting cross-disciplinary diagnostic workflows and enabling more effective communication between clinicians and patients.

This study has several limitations. First, the included conditions were not fully comprehensive or balanced, and the model demonstrated limited accuracy and sensitivity for certain rare pathologies. Second, although performance was evaluated using both automatic metrics and manual assessment, further validation in real-world clinical settings is still required. Third, the model currently relies solely on IVCM images, which are insufficient to establish definitive diagnoses; future work should focus on developing multimodal frameworks that integrate patient demographics, clinical history, and complementary examinations for more accurate diagnosis of corneal diseases. Fourth, age has been reported to influence sub-basal nerve morphology and density, particularly in the infero-central whorl region, which may confound nerve-density assessments. Future studies should incorporate age as a covariate for model adjustment or statistical correction to mitigate potential bias48. Finally, as this was a single-center study, external validation across multiple centers will be necessary to confirm generalizability.

In conclusion, we developed IVCM-Insight, an AI system that integrates image–text contrastive learning with LLMs to enable automated IVCM report generation and interactive clinical QA. Validated through both automated metrics and expert assessment, the system demonstrated consistent reliability in clinically relevant interpretation. These findings indicate that IVCM-Insight has the potential to streamline clinical workflows, support diagnostic decision-making, enhance patient understanding, and facilitate adoption in primary care, thereby addressing a critical gap in AI applications for IVCM.

Methods

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the study workflow comprised four components: (1) collection of IVCM reports and construction of the dataset; (2) development of IVCM-Insight, an AI model for automated IVCM report generation and question–answering; (3) evaluation of report performance using natural language generation (NLG) and multi-label classification (MLC) metrics; and (4) independent clinical assessment by three ophthalmologists, who rated AI-generated reports and QA outputs.

Dataset construction

This was a retrospective study conducted at the Department of Ophthalmology, Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, between June 2022 and August 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tongren Hospital (TREC2024-KY165) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, with informed consent waived due to its retrospective design.

All IVCM images were acquired using the Heidelberg Retina Tomograph III with Rostock Cornea Module (Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany). Examinations were performed using a standardized scanning and reporting protocol. Prior to acquisition, the operator established a zero reference (z = 0 µm) at the corneal epithelial interface after achieving stable contact and a clear superficial epithelial image using the coupling gel and contact cap. Then the cornea was scanned sequentially from the superficial to deep layers in the following order: epithelium, Bowman’s layer, stroma, Descemet’s membrane, and endothelium. For each layer, the operator acquired at least 3–5 well-focused, high-contrast images with minimal motion artifacts. Image sampling covered two predefined regions: (1) lesion-focused region, prioritizing the infiltration zone and/or the ulcer margin, and (2) control region, obtained from the peripheral clinically normal cornea. During clinical reporting, representative images were selected to ensure coverage of each scanned layer and any lesion regions, resulting in 6–9 representative images per report, and the corresponding text report provided a description of findings at each corneal layer and across regions. Images were captured at a resolution of 384 × 384 pixels and saved in JPEG format. Imaging depth was recorded as the device-reported axial (z) position in micrometers (µm), with the zero reference (z = 0 µm) defined at the corneal surface/epithelial interface. Images were subsequently obtained by advancing the focal plane along the z-axis, with each frame corresponding to a depth value relative to the epithelial surface.

For each patient, images were organized by eye laterality (OD/OS) and imaging depth, and filenames were assigned to encode metadata including patient ID, eye laterality, and image depth. For patients with bilateral involvement, one eye was randomly selected for inclusion to avoid within-subject dependence. Clinical reports were retrospectively retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical records using patient IDs. Each report was assigned a unique ID, and all corresponding images were organized under the folder named with the same ID. Then a JSON file was constructed to maintain this correspondence, with each entry representing a report-level sample and its associated image paths.

Patients who underwent IVCM examination for various corneal conditions during the study period were included. Images from the corneal epithelium, SBNP, stroma, and endothelium were analyzed for features including reduced sub-basal nerve density, stromal cell activation, and inflammatory cell infiltration, etc. To align with clinically informative IVCM readouts, we ensured that SBNP-related features were explicitly captured alongside epithelial and stromal findings. Descemet’s membrane, which is difficult to distinguish during scanning, was included with the endothelial layer. Notably, “reduced sub-basal nerve density” was treated as a qualitative descriptor extracted from clinician-written reports rather than a standardized quantitative metric with units.

A rigorous three-step quality control (QC) protocol was applied: (1) image QC: images with poor focus, low contrast, motion artifacts, or other acquisition errors were excluded. (2) report QC: two ophthalmologists reviewed clinical reports for spelling errors, incomplete descriptions, or inconsistencies; problematic reports were corrected or excluded. (3) Cross-checking: consistency of images and clinical reports was confirmed by a senior ophthalmologist.

Model development

The proposed AI system comprises two components: (1) a multimodal image–text framework using contrastive learning and matching objectives to generate structured clinical reports from IVCM images, and (2) an LLM-based module for interactive clinical question answering (QA). The multimodal image–text framework aligns IVCM images with corresponding clinical reports to generate structured diagnostic outputs. It employs a dual-stream encoder architecture consisting of a visual encoder, a text encoder, and a cross-modal decoder. Among them, a Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone serves as the visual encoder, extracting patch-level features from up to 9 IVCM images per patient. These features are aggregated by a multi-image fusion module into a unified visual embedding. The text encoder, a transformer-based language model, encodes clinical reports into contextualized embeddings. Finally, a cross-modal decoder integrates fused visual embeddings with textual context to generate structured diagnostic reports.

The proposed framework was implemented in PyTorch within a distributed data-parallel setting and trained using two NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs. The training pipeline started with the JSON file deserialized, and a sample list was created. For each sample, the image paths were read and transformed into a stacked tensor, resulting in an input of shape (9, 3, 384, 384). A range of augmentation strategies was applied to each image, including color jitter (brightness = 0.2, contrast = 0.2, hue = 0.05, saturation = 0.1), random horizontal flipping with a probability of 0.5, and random rotation within ±5°. The stacked multi-image tensor and the corresponding report text were then jointly fed to the model.

The training process was formulated as a multi-task optimization problem with three objectives: (1) image–text contrastive alignment loss (ITC), a symmetric contrastive loss that aligns image and report embeddings in a shared latent space; (2) ICLM, where the decoder generates report tokens conditioned on fused image embeddings and optimized via negative log-likelihood; and (3) joint multi-image consistency loss (MI), which enforces representational coherence across multiple images. Model optimization followed a three-phase progressive schedule for 30 epochs: the vision encoder was frozen for the first 5 epochs, then joint training was performed for 15 epochs with advanced fusion modules (spatial attention, temporal fusion, and adaptive pooling) initially frozen, and finally all modules were unfrozen for 10 epochs49. Optimization uses AdamW50 with weight decay 0.01 and gradient clipping at 1.0. Base learning rate is 1e-4 with parameter-group multipliers of 0.1 (vision), 1.0 (text), and 2.0 (fusion), and the optimizer is re-created at phase transitions to reflect updated trainable parameters. The learning rate follows a OneCycleLR schedule51 over the total number of training steps, with max_lr set to the base learning rate and pct_start = 0.1. Early stopping52 was employed on validation loss (patience 5, min_delta 0.001).

An auxiliary regularization term was introduced to exploit inter-image correlations so that semantic consistency among embeddings from the same patient visit could be enforced. An LLM-driven component was integrated into the system to facilitate clinical QA. A set of 30 representative questions was designed based on common patient concerns and potential corneal conditions observed in the dataset (Supplementary Table 4). Subsequently, a separate report-question-answer dataset was constructed in JSON format using the same report IDs. Each entry included: (1) the report index and text, (2) a question selected from our predefined question list, and (3) a clinician-validated answer. Following an evaluation of several widely adopted LLMs such as the GPT-series, LLaMA-family, and ChatGLM, DeepSeek: 70B was selected as the language backbone because it supports local deployment, which is better for patient privacy and data governance, and showed the best overall performance among the open-source candidates in our internal benchmarking on the test dataset (Supplementary Table 1). A lightweight supervised fine-tuning was performed with LoRA54 to enhance its reasoning abilities and strengthen contextual understanding of IVCM-specific findings.

Model automatic evaluation

Automatic evaluation of the AI-generated reports was conducted using two complementary approaches: natural language generation (NLG) metrics and condition-based MLC metrics. For the NLG assessment, AI-generated reports were compared with reference reports using four established indices. The Bilingual Evaluation Understudy (BLEU, 1–4) captured n-gram overlap to assess lexical accuracy, whereas the Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation (ROUGE-L) evaluated structural completeness by measuring the longest common subsequence. The Consensus-based Image Description Evaluation (CIDEr) emphasized agreement on clinically salient terms, while the Metric for Evaluation of Translation with Explicit ORdering (METEOR) provided precision and recall, incorporating synonyms and word variants.

For the MLC evaluation, we aimed to assess whether the AI-generated reports accurately captured key clinical findings compared with the reference clinical reports authored by ophthalmologists (Supplementary Fig. 1). We innovatively constructed a dictionary of 25 representative corneal conditions (Supplementary Table 5), with each entry linked to a set of regular expressions. The dictionary was applied to both AI-generated reports and original reports to extract condition labels, allowing each report to be assigned multiple labels. Negation detection rules (e.g., “no”, “not seen”, or “absent”) were incorporated to avoid false-positive matches. After automated label extraction, two ophthalmologists manually reviewed the assigned labels to verify extraction accuracy and to correct mismatches. By aligning the extracted labels from AI reports with those from original reports, we evaluated the model’s ability to preserve clinically relevant information. Performance was quantified using accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score.

Model manual evaluation

To complement automated evaluation, we conducted a structured manual assessment of both AI‑generated reports and QA outputs. For report assessment, 100 AI-generated reports with paired IVCM images and reference clinical reports were randomly sampled from the test set and independently rated by three ophthalmologists with more than five years of clinical experience and more than three years of IVCM image interpretation experience. A study‑specific 5‑point Semantic Differential Scale (SDS; Supplementary Table 6) was used with four dimensions: accuracy, completeness, logical coherence, and diagnostic support. Accuracy assessed whether clinical findings were faithfully represented; completeness examined whether essential information was sufficiently covered; logical coherence evaluated internal consistency of descriptions; diagnostic support measured whether the report provided meaningful assistance for clinical decision‑making.

To evaluate the QA component, we selected 20 reports that achieved top scores across all four dimensions, ensuring representation of diverse corneal conditions. For each report, five representative questions were crafted, yielding 100 QA pairs for evaluation. The same three ophthalmologists independently rated AI-generated answers using a modified 5-point SDS across four dimensions: accuracy, completeness, relevance, and non‑harmfulness. Accuracy reflected factual correctness; completeness assessed whether answers sufficiently addressed the question; relevance evaluated contextual appropriateness; and non-harmfulness captured clinical safety by ensuring that responses did not provide misleading or unsafe information.

Report and QA assessments employed a 5-point scale (1 = poor, 5 = excellent), with analyses using the average of three raters’ scores. For items scored ≤ 3, inter-rater reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, model 2,1), interpreted as <0.50 = poor, 0.50–0.75 = moderate, 0.75–0.90 = good, and >0.90 = excellent55.
