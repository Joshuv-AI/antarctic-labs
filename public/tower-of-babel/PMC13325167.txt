Chitosan-Gum Arabic Coated Nanostructured Lipid Carriers for Enhanced Gastrointestinal Stability of Curcuminoids
Authors: Liudmila Yarovaya, Sarunya Tuntiyasawasdikul, Peera Tabboon, Jintana Junlatat, Kusarin Khetklang, Panumart Phumart, Duangkamon Sakloetsakun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Curcuminoids possess potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties but suffer from poor aqueous solubility and rapid gastric degradation, limiting their therapeutic efficacy. This study developed turmeric curcuminoid-loaded nanostructured lipid carriers (TUR-NLCs) with chitosan-gum Arabic (CS-GA) polyelectrolyte complex coatings to enhance gastrointestinal stability and controlled oral delivery. TUR-NLCs were prepared via hot emulsification-microfluidization using optimized medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) and glycerol monostearate (GMS) ratios, followed by coating with a preformed CS-GA polyelectrolyte complex. The nanocarriers were comprehensively characterized for physicochemical properties, drug release kinetics, gastrointestinal stability, and biological activity. The optimized formulation (6MCT/3GMS) achieved a particle size of 53.20 ± 2.1 nm with 68.04 ± 0.98% encapsulation efficiency. Solid-state characterization indicated an amorphous or noncrystalline state of curcuminoids within the lipid matrix. The CS-GA coating significantly enhanced stability under simulated gastric conditions (pH 1.2), with controlled intestinal release following Fickian diffusion mechanisms (Korsmeyer–Peppas model, R 2 > 0.94, n = 0.304–0.336). The nanocarriers demonstrated good in vitro safety in RAW 264.7 macrophages (IC50 > 1000 μg/mL) while preserving anti-inflammatory activity (up to 25.91% NO inhibition at 100 μg/mL). This formulation development study demonstrates that CS-GA-coated NLCs provide enhanced gastrointestinal stability and controlled release characteristics, establishing proof of concept for the coating strategy. In vivo studies are warranted to validate therapeutic potential.

Introduction

Curcuminoids, the primary bioactive constituents of turmeric (Curcuma longa Linn), comprise curcumin, demethoxycurcumin, and bisdemethoxycurcumin. , These molecules exhibit potent antioxidant and anticancer effects, − yet their clinical translation is limited by their poor physicochemical properties. Curcumin, the principal curcuminoid, exhibits extremely low aqueous solubility (∼11–14 ng/mL at physiological pH), undergoes rapid hydrolytic degradation under acidic and alkaline conditions, and is extensively metabolized during first-pass hepatic elimination. Collectively, these factors result in oral bioavailability of less than 1%, representing a major barrier to therapeutic efficacy.

To overcome these biopharmaceutical challenges, numerous nanodelivery strategies have been investigated, including polymeric nanoparticles, liposomes, nanoemulsions, and lipid-based nanocarriers. Among these approaches, lipid nanoparticles have garnered considerable attention owing to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and capacity for sustained release of lipophilic compounds. First-generation solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), composed exclusively of solid lipids, offer advantages such as physical stability and scalable production using established pharmaceutical techniques. , However, SLNs exhibit inherent limitations, most notably restricted drug loading capacity and the tendency for drug expulsion during storage as the lipid matrix undergoes polymorphic transitions toward more thermodynamically stable crystalline forms. ,

Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) were subsequently developed as second-generation lipid nanoparticles to address these shortcomings. Unlike SLNs, NLCs incorporate a blend of solid and liquid lipids that are spatially incompatible, creating a disordered matrix with numerous imperfections. This less organized structure accommodates higher drug payloads and substantially reduces the crystallization-driven drug expulsion observed in conventional SLNs. The combination of medium-chain triglycerides (MCT) as the liquid lipid component and glycerol monostearate (GMS) as the solid lipid has proven particularly effective for encapsulating lipophilic bioactives, as MCT provides excellent solubilizing capacity while GMS contributes structural integrity. Given these advantages, we selected the NLC platform over traditional SLN systems for curcuminoid delivery in the present study.

Surface modification of lipid nanocarriers with biopolymers represents an additional strategy to enhance their functionality for oral delivery. Chitosan offers favorable attributes including biocompatibility, mucoadhesive properties, and the ability to transiently open epithelial tight junctions, thereby promoting paracellular absorption. , However, chitosan alone exhibits high solubility under acidic gastric conditions (pH < 6), which may result in premature coating dissolution before the formulation reaches the intestinal absorption site. Formation of polyelectrolyte complexes with anionic polysaccharides provides a means to enhance coating stability across the gastrointestinal pH gradient. , Gum Arabic, an anionic polysaccharide with excellent water solubility and resistance to digestive enzymes, serves as an ideal complexation partner for chitosan. , Previous work from our laboratory and others has demonstrated that chitosan-gum Arabic (CS-GA) films formed through electrostatic interaction exhibit improved mechanical properties and pH-responsive behavior suitable for gastric protection with subsequent intestinal release. ,

Despite the extensive body of literature on curcumin-loaded lipid nanocarriers and chitosan-coated delivery systems, several critical knowledge gaps remain unaddressed. First, while individually studied, the effects of chitosan-gum Arabic polyelectrolyte complexes as protective coatings for curcuminoid-loaded NLCs have not been systematically investigated. Second, comparative evaluation of different solid-to-liquid lipid ratios on the physicochemical properties, gastrointestinal stability, and biological activity of coated NLCs is lacking in the current literature. Third, most previous studies have focused on either formulation optimization or biological assessment in isolation, without establishing correlations between lipid matrix composition, release behavior, and preserved bioactivity. These limitations hinder the rational design of optimized NLC formulations for oral curcuminoid delivery.

Addressing this knowledge gap, the present study aimed to develop turmeric curcuminoid-loaded NLCs (TUR-NLCs) with CS-GA polyelectrolyte complex coatings optimized for enhanced gastric stability and controlled intestinal release. The specific objectives were to: (1) systematically evaluate the effects of MCT-to-GMS ratios on NLC physicochemical properties; (2) comprehensively characterize the solid-state structure using spectroscopic (FTIR) and thermal analytical (DSC, XRD) techniques; (3) investigate the protective effect of CS-GA coatings under simulated gastrointestinal conditions; (4) assess cytotoxicity and anti-inflammatory activity in RAW 264.7 macrophage models; (5) determine release kinetics and mechanisms; and (6) evaluate storage stability under refrigerated conditions.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic comparision of CS-GA polyelectrolyte complex coatings on NLCs with varying lipid compositions, establishing structure–function relationships between formulation variables and performance outcomes. Unlike previous studies that employed single-polymer coatings, the dual-polymer CS-GA system provides pH-responsive protectionremaining intact under acidic gastric conditions while allowing controlled release in the neutral intestinal environment. The present study was designed as a systematic formulation development and in vitro characterization investigation. The comprehensive physicochemical and biological characterization conducted provides essential foundational data for rational formulation design prior to in vivo evaluation.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Fresh turmeric rhizomes (C. longa Linn) were sourced from Mueang District, Maha Sarakham Province, and Phu Phan District, Sakon Nakhon Province, Thailand. Medium-chain triglycerides (Kollisolv MCT 70), glycerol monostearate (Kolliwax GMS II), poloxamer 188 (Kolliphor P188), and PEG-40 hydrogenated castor oil (Cremophor RH40) were provided by VITA Company (Bangkok, Thailand). Low molecular weight chitosan (150 kDa, 75% deacetylation), MTT, Griess reagent, and curcumin reference standard (≥98%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. RAW 264.7 cells were obtained from PromoCell GmbH. All solvents were HPLC grade.

Raw Material Preparation

Rhizomes were washed, sectioned (2–3 cm), and thermally treated at 80 °C for 4 h. The extract was freeze-dried (−50 °C, 0.1 mbar, 48 h) and stored at −20 °C. Quality assessment, including microbiological contamination, heavy metals, and pesticide residues, was conducted by accredited laboratories following pharmacopeial standards.

Curcuminoid Extraction and Quantification

Dried turmeric powder (12 kg) was extracted with 95% ethanol (1:3 w/v) by maceration for 48 h at 25 °C. The extract was concentrated by rotary evaporation at 45 °C. Curcuminoids were quantified by validated HPLC using a Hypersil ODS C18 column with an acetonitrile: 2% acetic acid (40:60 v/v) mobile phase at 1.0 mL/min and detection at 420 nm.

NLC Preparation

TUR-NLCs were prepared by hot homogenization-microfluidization. Four formulations containing MCT alone or combined with GMS were developed (Table ). The lipid phase (heated to 75 °C) containing turmeric extract (0.5% w/v curcuminoids) in PEG 400 was combined with the aqueous phase containing poloxamer 188 (2% w/v) and Cremophor RH40 (1% w/v) at 80 °C. The coarse emulsion was processed using a microfluidizer at 11,000 psi for 15 cycles and cooled under stirring.

Nanostructured Lipid Carrier Formulations

CS-GA Coating

NLC dispersions were coated with CS-GA polyelectrolyte complexes according to our previously established protocol. Chitosan (1% w/v) was dissolved in 1% (v/v) acetic acid overnight, and gum Arabic dispersion (5% w/v) was dispersed in deionized water for 2 h. The CS-GA complex was formed by adding GA dispersion to CS solution (0.5:1 w/w, GA/CS) under stirring for 2 h. The preformed CS-GA solution was then added to NLC dispersion (1:10 v/v) under stirring for 30 min. Final pH was adjusted to 5.5 ± 0.1. Coated dispersions were lyophilized (−50 °C, 0.1 mbar, 48 h) and stored at 4 °C until characterization.

Physicochemical Characterization

Particle Size and Polydispersity Index

Mean particle size (Z-average) and polydispersity index (PDI) of the NLC formulations were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and electrophoretic light scattering using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical, UK) at 25 ± 1 °C with detection at a 90° scattering angle. Lyophilized NLC samples (5 mg) were reconstituted in 10 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and briefly sonicated for 30 s to ensure complete dispersion. Measurements were performed in triplicate using 1 mL aliquots in disposable polystyrene cuvettes.

Encapsulation Efficiency

The encapsulation efficiency of curcuminoids was quantified using ultrafiltration separation, followed by HPLC analysis. TUR-NLC suspensions (500 μL) were transferred to Amicon Ultra-0.5 centrifugal filter units (10 kDa MWCO, Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) and centrifuged at 14,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C. The filtrate containing unencapsulated curcuminoids was analyzed by HPLC as previously described. 1 EE(%)=Tcur−ScurTcur×100 where T cur is the amount of curcumin in turmeric extract added to the nanostructured lipid carrier formulation, and S cur is the amount of curcumin in turmeric extract measured in the supernatant.

Production Yield

Production yield was calculated as the percentage ratio of the actual mass of dried NLC powder obtained after lyophilization to the theoretical mass of all solid components used in the formulation.

Viscosity Measurement

The viscosity of NLC dispersions was measured using a Brookfield DV-II+Pro viscometer (Brookfield Engineering, USA) equipped with a spindle SC4-18 at 25 ± 0.5 °C. Measurements were performed at a shear rate of 100 s–1, and values are reported in centipoise (cP).

Morphological Analysis

The morphology of NLC formulations was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (JSM-6400, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Lyophilized samples were mounted on aluminum stubs using double-sided carbon tape and sputter-coated with gold under vacuum using an ion sputter coater. Images were acquired at 20 kV accelerating voltage with magnifications ranging from 10,000× to 50,000×.

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

Molecular interactions and functional group identification were analyzed using FTIR spectroscopy (Spectrum One, PerkinElmer Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Lyophilized samples were thoroughly mixed with potassium bromide (1:100 w/w ratio) and compressed into transparent discs using a hydraulic press (8 tons of pressure, 10 min). Spectra were recorded from 4000 to 500 cm–1 with 4 cm–1 resolution and 32 scans per sample.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry

Thermal analysis was performed using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC822e, Mettler Toledo, Greifensee, Switzerland). Lyophilized samples (3–5 mg) were sealed in aluminum pans and heated from 40 to 300 °C at 10 °C/min. An empty aluminum pan served as a reference, and thermograms were analyzed for melting transitions and crystallization behavior.

X-ray Diffraction Analysis

The crystalline structure and polymorphic forms were characterized using powder X-ray diffraction (D8 ADVANCE, Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA). Samples were scanned over a 2θ range of 5–40° with a step size of 0.02° and a scan rate of 1°/min using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å, 45 kV, 40 mA). Diffraction patterns were processed using FITYK software v1.3.1 (Wojdyr, 2010), and the crystallinity index was calculated as 2 Crystallinitydegree(%)=[IcIa+Ic]×100 where I a and I c denote the amorphous and crystallized areas, respectively, in the XRD diffractogram.

Cell Studies

Cell Culture

Murine macrophage RAW 264.7 cells (PromoCell GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Cytotoxicity Assessment

Cell viability was evaluated using the MTT assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well) and treated with turmeric extract (5–156 μg/mL) and TUR-NLC formulations (156–2500 μg/mL) for 24 h. MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL, 100 μL) was added and incubated for 4 h. The formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (EZ Read 2000, Biochrom Ltd., Cambridge, UK). Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to untreated controls.

Nitric Oxide Inhibition

Anti-inflammatory activity was assessed by measuring nitric oxide (NO) production inhibition. RAW 264.7 cells (1.5 × 105 cells/mL) were pretreated with turmeric extract and test samples for 1 h, then stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (1 μg/mL) for 24 h. Nitrite accumulation in culture supernatants was quantified using Griess reagent, with absorbance measured at 540 nm. Results were expressed as IC50 values calculated from dose–response curves.

In Vitro Cumulative Release Kinetics

Curcuminoid release profiles were determined using the dialysis method under simulated physiological conditions. TUR-NLC suspensions (20 mL) were placed in cellulose dialysis bags (MWCO 12–14 kDa) and immersed in 100 mL release medium containing 50% ethanol. The ethanol fraction was added to maintain sink conditions, ensuring that the solubility of curcuminoids stayed above the maximum released amount. Three different media were evaluated: simulated salivary fluid (SSF, pH 6.8), simulated gastric fluid (SGF, pH 1.2), and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, pH 6.8). Experiments were conducted at 37 °C with continuous stirring (100 rpm). Samples (2 mL) were withdrawn at predetermined intervals and replaced with fresh medium. Curcuminoid concentrations were quantified by HPLC, and release kinetics were analyzed using DDSolver software by fitting data to zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, Korsmeyer–Peppas, and Hixson–Crowell models.

Gastrointestinal Stability Assessment

The stability of NLC formulations under simulated gastrointestinal conditions was evaluated by incubating samples in SSF (pH 6.8), SGF (pH 1.2), and SIF (pH 6.8) at 37 °C for 1 and 2 h, respectively. At predetermined intervals, aliquots were withdrawn and analyzed for particle size and PDI.

Accelerated Stability Study

Storage stability was assessed in accordance with ICH Q1A­(R2) guidelines. Formulations were stored under accelerated conditions (40 ± 2 °C, 75% ± 5% RH), ambient (30 ± 2 °C), and long-term conditions (4 ± 2 °C) for 8 wk. Curcuminoid content, particle size, and physical appearance were evaluated at 0, 2, 4, and 8 wk.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation of at least three independent experiments. Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test (SPSS v28.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Raw Material Characterization and Standardization

Quality assessment of turmeric rhizomes from two northeastern Thai provinces was conducted prior to extract preparation to ensure raw material standards. Both sources demonstrated compliance with Thai Herbal Pharmacopeia (2021) specifications and Ministry of Public Health regulations. All safety parameters, including microbial contamination (total viable count < 103 CFU/g, absence of Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp.), heavy metal content (Pb < 10 mg/kg, Cd < 0.3 mg/kg, Hg < 0.1 mg/kg, As < 3 mg/kg), and pesticide residues, fell within acceptable regulatory thresholds.

Quantitative HPLC analysis revealed geographical variation in curcuminoid composition. Sakon Nakhon Province material exhibited a superior phytochemical profile containing 32.86% total curcuminoids, comprising curcumin (CM, 20.45%), bisdemethoxycurcumin (BCM, 8.25%), and demethoxycurcumin (DCM, 4.16%), compared to Maha Sarakham Province samples. This compositional difference may be attributed to climatic and soil conditions in the Phu Phan region, which have been reported to influence curcuminoid biosynthesis and accumulation in turmeric rhizomes. Representative HPLC chromatograms demonstrating the baseline separation of curcuminoid standards and sample extracts are presented in Figure S1, confirming the analytical method’s selectivity and precision for quality control applications.

Formulation Development and Optimization

Systematic formulation screening was initiated with solvent selection for curcuminoid solubilization, as the lipophilic nature of these compounds presents significant dissolution challenges in conventional aqueous systems. Among the evaluated cosolvents, including propylene glycol, Transcutol(R) P, and various polyethylene glycols, PEG 400 demonstrated optimal performance with complete solubilization of all three curcuminoids at therapeutic concentrations while maintaining acceptable safety for oral administration.

The formulation matrix was systematically optimized by varying two critical parameters: lipid phase composition (medium-chain triglycerides and glycerol monostearate ratios) and lipid-to-aqueous phase volume ratios. These factors directly influence particle size distribution, encapsulation efficiency, and release characteristics in lipid nanoparticle systems. Medium-chain triglycerides were selected as the primary liquid lipid component due to their liquid state at physiological temperature, facilitating drug incorporation and subsequent controlled release. Glycerol monostearate served as the solid lipid component to provide structural stability and modulate crystallization behavior, creating a nanostructured lipid carrier (NLC) system rather than a conventional solid lipid nanoparticle formulation.

The aqueous phase contained fixed concentrations of complementary nonionic surfactants: poloxamer 188 (2% w/v) and PEG-40 hydrogenated castor oil (1% w/v), selected for their synergistic stabilization effects and biocompatibility profiles. Integration of chitosan-gum Arabic (CS-GA) polyelectrolyte complexes into the formulation was implemented to enhance mucoadhesive properties and provide pH-responsive release characteristics. Following physicochemical evaluation of six preliminary formulations, four candidates demonstrated favorable characteristics, including size distribution (PDI 0.50–0.98), satisfactory encapsulation efficiency (43.55–69.16%), and stable colloidal properties, warranting detailed characterization as presented in Table .

Rheological Properties

Viscosity measurements ranged from 28.6 to 164.2 cP (Table ). The lowest viscosity was achieved by 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC (28.6 ± 0.56 cP), while 12MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC showed the highest (164.2 ± 0.25 cP). The elevated viscosity of 12MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC can be attributed to the combined effects of GMS content and intermediate lipid concentration, creating enhanced particle interactions.

Encapsulation Efficiency (%)

Curcuminoid encapsulation efficiency varied across formulations, ranging from 43.55% to 69.16% (Table ). The moderate encapsulation efficiency of the optimized formulation may reflect the use of a multicomponent turmeric extract rather than purified curcumin, together with drug partitioning during emulsification and subsequent processing. The 12MCT-TUR-NLC formulation achieved the highest encapsulation efficiency (69.16 ± 1.32%), followed by 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC (68.04 ± 0.98%), while 18MCT-TUR-NLC exhibited the lowest efficiency (43.55 ± 1.46%). The superior performance of 12MCT-TUR-NLC can be attributed to its optimized MCT content and balanced lipid-to-aqueous phase ratio (25.5:74.5), which facilitates effective molecular dispersion of curcuminoids within the lipid matrix without oversaturation.

The high encapsulation efficiency of 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC highlights the significant role of GMS in enhancing drug accommodation within the lipid matrix. GMS creates a more structured crystalline network that provides enhanced molecular accommodation sites for curcuminoids through hydrophobic interactions. In contrast, the lower encapsulation efficiency of 18MCT-TUR-NLC (43.55%) can be explained by increased system viscosity reducing homogenization efficiency, and the predominantly liquid matrix providing insufficient structural integrity for effective drug entrapment. These encapsulation patterns suggest that curcuminoid encapsulation in NLC systems follows a biphasic relationship with lipid content, where intermediate concentrations provide maximum efficiency through balanced solubilization capacity and matrix stability.

To determine whether drug leakage occurred during the coating process, encapsulation efficiency was measured for both uncoated NLC and CS-GA-coated TUR-NLC formulations (Table ). All formulations exhibited minimal EE changes after coating, ranging from −1.70% to +0.85%. Interestingly, three out of four formulations (18MCT-TUR-NLC, 12MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC, and 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC) showed slight EE increases after coating (+0.25% to +0.85%), suggesting that the coating procedure may provide minimal protective effects during handling. Only 12MCT-TUR-NLC exhibited a minor decrease (−1.70%), which was not statistically significant (p = 0.149) and falls within the range of experimental variability considering the combined standard deviations of both measurements (±0.91% and ±1.32%).

The gentle nature of the coating procedure explains these minimal impacts on encapsulation efficiency. The CS-GA coating involves simple addition of a preformed polyelectrolyte complex to NLC dispersion at room temperature (25 °C) with mild stirring for 30 min, without high-energy homogenization or elevated temperatures that could disrupt the lipid matrix integrity. The coating deposits onto the particle surface through electrostatic interaction rather than penetrating or destabilizing the lipid core.

These results definitively establish that the moderate encapsulation efficiencies observed in this study (43–70%) are primarily determined by the lipid matrix composition and hot emulsification-microfluidization conditions during NLC preparation, rather than subsequent coating application. The low EE of 18MCT-TUR-NLC (43.15–43.55%) reflects the liquid nature of long-chain MCT, which provides a less structured matrix for curcuminoid accommodation compared to the semisolid GMS-containing formulations. Conversely, the higher EE values achieved with GMS-containing formulations (60–68%) demonstrate the benefit of solid lipid incorporation in enhancing drug encapsulation capacity while maintaining nanometric dimensions.

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy provided definitive evidence of successful turmeric extract incorporation within the NLC matrix through comparative analysis of blank and loaded formulations (Figures S2 and ). All formulations exhibited fundamental lipid matrix characteristics, including asymmetric and symmetric C–H stretching vibrations at 2923–2915 cm–1 and 2867–2852 cm–1, respectively, attributed to methylene groups in MCT fatty acid chains. The carbonyl stretching vibration (CO) at 1741–1742 cm–1 confirmed the preservation of ester linkages throughout all formulations, indicating thermal stability of the lipid matrix during processing.

FTIR spectra (4000–500 cm–1) of TUR-NLC formulations showing characteristic absorption bands of lipid matrix and encapsulated curcuminoids.

Comparative spectral analysis between blank and turmeric-loaded formulations revealed significant molecular evidence supporting successful drug incorporation and matrix–drug interactions. The appearance of broad O–H stretching bands at 3443–3416 cm–1 in TUR-NLC formulations, substantially intensified compared to the weaker corresponding bands (3451–3280 cm–1) in blank formulations, definitively indicating the presence of phenolic hydroxyl groups characteristic of curcuminoid compounds. The fingerprint region (600–1600 cm–1) exhibited dramatic spectral enhancement in loaded formulations, with multiple characteristic absorption bands at 1614–1513, 1461–1456, 1347–1345, 1284–1281, and 1247–1244 cm–1 representing aromatic CC stretching, C–H bending, C–O stretching, and aromatic C–H out-of-plane bending vibrations typical of curcuminoid molecular structures. These spectral features were either absent or minimally detectable in corresponding blank formulations, providing unambiguous confirmation of curcuminoid encapsulation.

Peak broadening observed in TUR-NLC spectra, particularly in the 1100–1200 cm–1 region, suggests favorable intermolecular interactions between curcuminoids and the lipid matrix through hydrogen bonding. The preservation of characteristic curcuminoid peaks without significant wavelength shifts indicates successful encapsulation without covalent modification or chemical degradation of the bioactive compounds. Formulations containing glycerol monostearate exhibited additional spectroscopic signatures at 1560–1561 cm–1 and enhanced intensity at 1029–1061 cm–1, attributed to the structured lipid component and confirming successful multicomponent lipid matrix formation. ,

Differential Scanning Calorimetry

DSC analysis provided comprehensive insights into crystalline behavior modifications resulting from turmeric extract incorporation, revealing fundamental changes in lipid matrix organization and drug–carrier interactions (Figure S3). Blank NLC formulations exhibited characteristic endothermic transitions typical of structured lipid systems, with melting events in the 250–300 °C range reflecting the thermal behavior of MCT and GMS components (Figure S3a). The 12MCT-NLC formulation demonstrated the most pronounced endothermic transition with sharp, high-intensity peaks, while formulations containing higher MCT content or GMS showed progressively broader transitions with reduced peak intensity, indicating compositional effects on crystalline organization consistent with lipid polymorphism behavior in nanostructured lipid carriers (Figure S3b).

Comparative thermal analysis between blank and turmeric-loaded formulations revealed significant modifications in crystalline behavior that confirm successful drug incorporation and matrix–drug interactions. All TUR-loaded formulations exhibited markedly altered thermal profiles characterized by peak broadening, intensity reduction, and slight temperature shifts compared to their blank counterparts, indicating disruption of lipid crystalline arrangements through molecular integration of curcuminoid compounds. The 12MCT-TUR-NLC formulation showed substantial endothermic peak intensity reduction with concurrent peak broadening, suggesting molecular dispersion of turmeric extract within the lipid matrix rather than phase separation.

The thermal analysis revealed decreased crystallinity in all turmeric-loaded formulations, evidenced by broader, less intense endothermic transitions compared to blank controls. This crystalline reduction represents a favorable modification for bioactive delivery applications, as decreased lipid crystalline perfection typically correlates with enhanced drug release kinetics and improved bioavailability through the creation of molecular-level defects that facilitate drug diffusion. The absence of distinct curcumin melting endotherms (typically observed at 183 °C for crystalline curcumin) confirmed that curcuminoids were present in an amorphous or noncrystalline state within the lipid matrix, indicating successful prevention of drug crystallization during processing.

X-ray Diffraction Analysis

XRD analysis provided quantitative crystallographic evidence for curcuminoid incorporation within the lipid matrix (Figure S4). All formulations exhibited characteristic lipid diffraction signatures at 2θ values of 19° and 23°, representing the β-polymorph crystalline arrangement typical of triglyceride systems.

Blank NLC formulations demonstrated crystallinity indices ranging from 27.73% to 33.98%, with 12MCT/3GMS-NLC achieving the highest crystalline order (33.98%) due to GMS hydrogen bonding networks. , Upon turmeric extract incorporation, all formulations exhibited crystallinity reductions consistent with drug-induced disruption of lipid crystalline arrangements. MCT-only formulations showed moderate decreases (12MCT-TUR-NLC: 29.34% to 24.07%; 18MCT-TUR-NLC: 27.73% to 27.05%), while GMS-containing formulations demonstrated more pronounced reductions (12MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC: 33.98% to 20.24%; 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC: 29.47% to 12.87%).

The substantial crystallinity reduction in GMS-containing formulations indicates effective curcuminoid intercalation within the glyceryl monostearate lattice, disrupting hydrogen bonding networks and creating molecular-level defects favorable for drug accommodation. The lowest crystallinity (12.87%) achieved by 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC correlates with its optimal particle size (53.20 nm) and encapsulation efficiency (68.04%).

The absence of characteristic curcumin diffraction peaks at 2θ = 8.9°, 14.4°, 17.2°, and 25.5° indicated that no detectable crystalline curcumin was present in the formulations. These findings corroborate DSC results, collectively confirming successful molecular-level curcuminoid dispersion.

In Vitro Cytotoxicity Evaluation

The cytotoxicity of turmeric extract and TUR-NLC formulations was evaluated in RAW 264.7 macrophages using the MTT colorimetric assay (Figure a). Due to its higher cytotoxicity, free turmeric extract was tested at a lower concentration range (5–156 μg/mL) compared to TUR-NLC formulations (156–2500 μg/mL). Cell viability remained above 90% at 5–10 μg/mL, followed by a decrease at higher concentrations, reaching 72.70% at 20 μg/mL and 50.99% and 50.35% at 39 μg/mL and 78 μg/mL, respectively. A significant reduction was observed at 156 μg/mL (17.11%). The calculated IC50 for turmeric extract was 79.71 μg/mL, indicating relatively high cytotoxicity of the free extract.

Biological evaluation of TUR-NLC formulations in RAW 264.7 macrophages: (a) cell viability determined by MTT assay; (b) inhibition of nitric oxide production in LPS-stimulated cells. Values represent mean ± SD (n = 3). Different letters indicate statistically significant differences (p < 0.05).

In contrast, all TUR-NLC formulations showed improved cell compatibility compared to free turmeric extract. After 24 h exposure at 156 μg/mL, cell viability remained between 88.08% and 95.57%, indicating minimal cytotoxic effects at initial screening concentrations. Increasing the concentration to 312 μg/mL maintained cell viability within the 85.14–90.59% range across all tested formulations, demonstrating acceptable safety margins consistent with recent curcumin nanocarrier studies. , Further concentration increases revealed formulation-dependent cytotoxicity patterns, with calculated IC50 values of 541.97 ± 30.67, 478.65 ± 18.37, 1442.74 ± 107.32, and 1080.49 ± 67.37 μg/mL for 12MCT-TUR-NLC, 18MCT-TUR-NLC, 12MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC, and 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC, respectively.

The difference between the IC50 value of turmeric extract and the TUR-NLC formulations confirms the protective effect of encapsulation in NLCs. Encapsulation of turmeric bioactive compounds within the lipid matrix likely controls their release and decreases direct cellular exposure to high local concentrations of curcuminoids, thereby reducing acute cytotoxic effects. In contrast, free turmeric extract delivers bioactive compounds directly to the cellular environment, which may lead to rapid cellular uptake and increased toxicity at lower concentrations. These findings indicate that lipid-based nanocarriers can enhance the cellular tolerance of turmeric bioactives while maintaining their biological activity.

The favorable safety profiles of GMS-containing formulations (12MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC and 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC) represent significant advantages for pharmaceutical applications, requiring approximately 2- to 3-fold higher concentrations to achieve comparable cytotoxic effects compared to MCT-only formulations. This enhanced biocompatibility can be attributed to the structured lipid matrix provided by glycerol monostearate, which may facilitate more controlled drug release kinetics and reduced cellular exposure to high local concentrations of curcuminoids. , At moderate concentrations (625–1250 μg/mL), these formulations maintained 39.40–76.81% cell viability, substantially higher than concentrations required for therapeutic efficacy. The convergence of all formulations to similar low viability levels (12.99–15.74%) at the maximum tested concentration (2500 μg/mL) indicates that the protective effects of structured lipid matrices have limitations at extremely high doses, consistent with concentration-dependent cytotoxicity patterns observed in other bioactive nanocarrier systems. Importantly, these cytotoxic concentrations substantially exceed therapeutic dose ranges, indicating an acceptable in vitro safety window under the tested conditions.

Anti-Inflammatory Activity

Anti-inflammatory efficacy was evaluated by nitric oxide (NO) production inhibition in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages (Figure b). Both turmeric extract and TUR-NLC formulations inhibited NO production compared to LPS-induced controls, indicating the anti-inflammatory activity in both free and nanoencapsulated forms. Free turmeric extract significantly inhibited NO production by 17.17% and 15.29% relative to the LPS control at 50 and 100 μg/mL, respectively, demonstrating moderate inhibitory activity within the tested concentration range.

Similarly, the TUR-NLC formulations demonstrated comparable inhibitory effects across the same concentration range, indicating that the anti-inflammatory activity of turmeric extract was retained after encapsulation. All TUR-NLC formulations achieved maximum inhibitory activity at a 100 μg/mL concentration, with inhibition rates ranging from 14.67% to 25.91%, comparable to previous studies reporting 20–30% NO inhibition for curcumin-loaded nanocarriers at similar concentrations.

Among the formulations, 18MCT-TUR-NLC exhibited the highest NO inhibition (25.91%) at 100 μg/mL (p < 0.05), while 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC showed greater potency at lower concentrations, with inhibition rates of 14.88% and 16.53% at 10 and 50 μg/mL, respectively, compared to other formulations and turmeric extract (p < 0.05). This enhanced activity of 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC at lower concentrations may be related to its smaller particle size (53.20 nm) and higher encapsulation efficiency (68.04%), supporting established principles that nanoparticles in the 50–100 nm range demonstrate optimal cellular uptake and intracellular delivery efficiency. , The smaller particle size facilitates enhanced membrane penetration and intracellular curcuminoid delivery, while the encapsulation efficiency ensures adequate bioactive payload per unit mass of nanocarrier.

The anti-inflammatory mechanisms underlying NO inhibition likely involve curcuminoid-mediated suppression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway modulation, consistent with established curcumin pharmacology. , The retained bioactivity following nanoencapsulation confirms successful protection of curcuminoids from degradation while maintaining their molecular target accessibility.

Integration of cytotoxicity and anti-inflammatory data establishes favorable therapeutic windows for 18MCT-TUR-NLC and 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC, with effective anti-inflammatory concentrations (10–100 μg/mL) positioned well below their respective cytotoxicity thresholds (IC50 478–1442 μg/mL). These concentrations should be interpreted only within the context of the present in vitro macrophage model and should not be taken as evidence that equivalent systemic levels can be achieved after oral administration. Further pharmacokinetic and in vivo efficacy studies are needed to determine whether the improved gastrointestinal protection of the coated NLCs translates into enhanced in vivo performance. Overall, these results indicate that although the anti-inflammatory activity of TUR-NLC formulations was comparable to that of free turmeric extract, certain formulations, particularly 18MCT-TUR-NLC and 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC, demonstrated improved effects at the highest tested concentration, suggesting that formulation characteristics influence the anti-inflammatory activity of turmeric extract.

In Vitro Release Kinetics

Curcuminoid release behavior from TUR-NLC formulations was evaluated across physiologically relevant media (simulated salivary fluid (SSF), simulated gastric fluid (SGF), and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF)) to assess controlled release capabilities and gastrointestinal stability (Figure ). Free turmeric extract exhibited rapid burst release kinetics, achieving 12–19% cumulative release within 30 min across all media with minimal subsequent release, indicating poor stability and rapid degradation in physiological conditions. In contrast, all TUR-NLC formulations demonstrated improved release control, with 8 h cumulative release ranging from 6% to 13% depending on formulation and medium, confirming successful sustained release characteristics consistent with lipid nanocarrier systems.

Values represent mean ± SD (n = 3).

Medium-specific release patterns revealed formulation stability variations across gastrointestinal conditions. In SSF, all formulations achieved comparable release profiles (10–13% at 8 h), with CM demonstrating slightly higher release than DCM and BCM in MCT-only formulations, attributed to its superior lipid matrix compatibility. SGF conditions produced similar release rates (7–11% at 8 h) with a consistent hierarchy of BCM > DCM > CM across all formulations, reflecting differential stability of curcuminoid analogs under acidic conditions. SIF demonstrated the lowest overall release (6–11% at 8 h), with 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC exhibiting the most controlled release profile (5.98–8.40%), indicating enhanced matrix stability in intestinal conditions favorable for targeted absorption.

The relatively low cumulative release (6–13% at 8 h) compared to uncoated curcumin-NLC systems reported in literature (typically 40–60% at 8 h) can be attributed to the CS-GA polyelectrolyte complex coating, which functions as a diffusion barrier. This coating was specifically designed to minimize premature drug release under gastric conditions, providing gastric protection for subsequent intestinal delivery. The intact CS-GA coating at acidic pH restricts both water ingress and curcuminoid egress, while the structured GMS matrix further retards molecular diffusion through increased tortuosity. This dual-barrier system ensures that the majority of encapsulated curcuminoids remain protected during gastric transit, enabling controlled release at the intestinal absorption site where the coating undergoes pH-triggered dissociation.

Mathematical modeling using DDSolver analysis revealed that all formulations followed the Korsmeyer–Peppas model (R 2 > 0.94), with release exponent values (n = 0.304–0.336) confirming Fickian diffusion-controlled mechanisms for spherical systems (n ≤ 0.43) (Table ). This indicated that the drug transport occurs predominantly via concentration-driven diffusion rather than polymer relaxation or matrix erosion mechanisms.

The observed diffusion behavior is consistent with the structural characteristics of the CS-GA polyelectrolyte complex coating. The electrostatic interaction between protonated amino groups of chitosan and carboxyl groups of gum Arabic forms a compact network that functions as a semipermeable barrier surrounding the lipid core. In this coated NLC system, curcuminoids are dispersed within the partially amorphous lipid matrix and are likely to diffuse through the hydrated polymeric layer before entering the external medium. Long-term thermal stability assessment of curcuminoids in TUR-NLC formulations revealed temperature-dependent degradation kinetics that informs optimal storage conditions for oral delivery applications (Figure ). Under refrigerated storage conditions, all curcuminoids maintained excellent stability with retention rates ranging from 79% to 118% across formulations, confirming the protective effect of lipid encapsulation against thermal degradation. The 18MCT-TUR-NLC formulation exhibited superior curcuminoid preservation, followed by 12MCT-TUR-NLC, 12MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC, and 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC, indicating that higher MCT content provides enhanced protection under cold storage conditions. Conversely, 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC exhibited reduced stability, particularly at elevated temperatures, correlating with its higher aqueous proportion (77.5%) that increases interfacial areas and promotes degradation reactions.

Accelerated stability testing revealed pronounced temperature sensitivity, with curcuminoid retention decreasing to 23–57% at 30 °C and 27–52% at 40 °C after 8 wk. Under these elevated temperature conditions, 12MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC demonstrated relatively better protection compared to MCT-only formulations, attributed to the structured lipid matrix formed by GMS. However, under recommended refrigerated storage conditions, 18MCT-TUR-NLC consistently provided superior curcuminoid preservation, consistent with the stability hierarchy (18MCT-TUR-NLC > 12MCT-TUR-NLC > 12MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC > 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC) that correlates inversely with aqueous phase proportion. At elevated temperatures, GMS-containing formulations demonstrated relatively better protection due to the structured lipid matrix that creates enhanced diffusion barriers.

Discussion

Physicochemical Characteristics of TUR-NLC Formulations

The present study successfully developed turmeric extract-loaded nanostructured lipid carriers (TUR-NLCs) with chitosan-gum Arabic polyelectrolyte complex coatings. Among the four formulations investigated, the 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC formulation exhibited the most favorable physicochemical properties, characterized by the smallest particle size (53.20 ± 2.1 nm), narrow size distribution (PDI = 0.50 ± 0.02), and satisfactory encapsulation efficiency (68.04 ± 0.98%). The nanometric dimensions achieved fall well within the optimal range for intestinal absorption, as particles below 100 nm demonstrate enhanced cellular uptake and lymphatic transport compared with larger counterparts. ,

The influence of lipid composition on particle characteristics warrants particular attention. Formulations containing only MCT as the lipid phase (12MCT-TUR-NLC and 18MCT-TUR-NLC) yielded larger particles with higher polydispersity indices, whereas the incorporation of GMS as a solid lipid component resulted in smaller, more uniform particles. This optimized composition aligns with the current understanding of nanostructured lipid carriers, where the balance between solid and liquid lipids is crucial for achieving desired particle characteristics, and the presence of solid lipid provides a structural scaffold that restricts particle growth during the emulsification process. The 18MCT-TUR-NLC formulation exhibited the largest particle size (418.20 ± 15.3 nm) and highest PDI (0.98 ± 0.05), suggesting that excessive liquid lipid content compromises the structural integrity of the nanocarrier matrix.

The encapsulation efficiency values obtained (43–69%) are consistent with those reported for curcumin-loaded NLC systems in the literature. Lacatusu et al. reported encapsulation efficiencies ranging from 55% to 75% for curcumin-loaded NLCs prepared with different lipid combinations, while Aditya et al. achieved approximately 70% encapsulation using a similar hot homogenization approach. The moderate encapsulation efficiency observed for the 18MCT-TUR-NLC formulation (43.55 ± 1.46%) likely reflects the limited capacity of the highly disordered liquid lipid matrix to retain curcuminoids, as the absence of solid lipid domains reduces the number of available accommodation sites within the nanocarrier structure.

The comprehensive evaluation of encapsulation efficiency before and after coating (Table ) provides critical insights into the formulation process and addresses important questions regarding the origins of moderate EE values in this system. The minimal impact of coating on EE (<2% absolute change, p > 0.05 for all formulations) can be attributed to the gentle nature of the coating procedure, which involves room temperature processing with mild stirring, without the high-energy homogenization or elevated temperatures that could disrupt the preformed lipid matrix. This finding confirms that the CS-GA polyelectrolyte complex deposits onto the NLC surface through electrostatic interaction rather than penetrating or destabilizing the lipid core structure.

This validation is particularly important when interpreting the moderate encapsulation efficiencies achieved in this study (43–70%) compared to pure curcumin-loaded NLCs reported in the literature, , which frequently demonstrate EE values exceeding 85–90%. Several formulation-specific factors contribute to our observed EE range. First, this system utilizes turmeric extract containing only 32.86% total curcuminoids, along with other phytochemical components (proteins, carbohydrates, minor polyphenols) that compete for accommodation sites within the lipid matrix and may interfere with curcuminoid solubilization. Pure curcumin systems, by contrast, achieve higher loading efficiency due to the absence of competing components and the well-characterized lipophilicity of isolated curcumin.

Second, the MCT/GMS ratios in this study were optimized primarily to achieve nanometric dimensions (50–150 nm) suitable for intestinal absorption and lymphatic transport, rather than to maximize drug loading capacity. The optimized 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC formulation achieved an excellent balance among multiple performance parameters: particle size of 53.20 nm, EE of 68.04%, production yield of 98.05%, and superior gastrointestinal stability. While increasing total lipid content or GMS proportion could theoretically improve encapsulation, such modifications would compromise the critical nanometric size range essential for effective oral delivery.

Third, the hot emulsification-microfluidization process employed in this study involves exposure to elevated temperatures (75–80 °C) and high shear forces (11,000 psi, 15 cycles) that may cause partial degradation of heat-sensitive curcuminoids, reducing the concentration available for encapsulation. This hypothesis is supported by the observation that GMS-containing formulations, which provide more structured lipid matrices offering enhanced thermal protection, consistently achieved higher EE values than the liquid MCT-only system. The moderately disordered NLC structure, as evidenced by crystallinity indices ranging from 12 to 30%, provides sufficient imperfections for drug accommodation while maintaining physical stability during storage.

Furthermore, it is important to recognize that encapsulation efficiency represents only one dimension of formulation performance. The ultimate therapeutic efficacy of oral curcuminoid delivery systems depends on multiple factors, including particle size (governing absorption rate), gastrointestinal stability (preventing premature drug release), and production scalability (enabling clinical translation). From this holistic perspective, the moderate EE of 68.04% achieved by the optimized 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC formulation is acceptable when considered alongside its nanometric size (53.20 nm), excellent production yield (98.05%), and demonstrated superiority in simulated gastrointestinal stability testing. These attributes collectively position this formulation as a promising candidate for oral curcuminoid delivery, with the moderate drug loading offset by enhanced delivery potential from the optimized physicochemical properties.

While the current study establishes that coating does not contribute to EE reduction, future optimization efforts could focus on the primary emulsification stage to enhance initial drug loading. Potential strategies include the following: (i) investigating milder emulsification conditions (lower temperature or pressure) that balance particle size reduction with curcuminoid preservation; (ii) exploring alternative solid lipids with higher curcuminoid solubility; (iii) incorporating penetration enhancers or solubilizers to improve drug-lipid miscibility; and (iv) investigating precomplexation of curcuminoids with cyclodextrins or phospholipids before NLC preparation. Such modifications could potentially increase EE toward 80–85% while maintaining the nanometric dimensions and stability advantages demonstrated in this study.

Solid-State Characterization

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, differential scanning calorimetry, and X-ray diffraction analyses provided complementary evidence for successful curcuminoid incorporation within the NLC matrix. The FTIR spectra of TUR-NLC formulations retained characteristic absorption bands of both the lipid components and the CS-GA coating polymers, with no appearance of new peaks indicative of chemical interactions or degradation. The preservation of the phenolic O–H stretching vibration (∼3400 cm–1) and carbonyl CO stretching (∼1630 cm–1) bands confirmed that the curcuminoid functional groups remained intact following the encapsulation process.

Differential scanning calorimetry thermograms revealed a reduction in the melting endotherm intensity of GMS in the TUR-NLC formulations compared with the physical mixture, indicating disruption of the crystalline lipid structure upon nanoparticle formation. This observation is characteristic of NLC systems, where the incorporation of liquid lipid creates lattice defects that reduce the overall crystallinity of the matrix. The absence of the characteristic melting peak of curcumin (∼183 °C) in the loaded formulations suggests that curcuminoids were present in an amorphous or noncrystalline state in the lipid matrix rather than crystalline encapsulation.

X-ray diffraction patterns corroborated the DSC findings, demonstrating a marked decrease in peak intensity and the disappearance of sharp diffraction peaks associated with crystalline curcumin in the TUR-NLC formulations. Quantitative analysis revealed that GMS-containing formulations exhibited the most pronounced crystallinity reduction (up to 56% decrease in 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC), indicating effective curcuminoid intercalation within the structured lipid matrix. The transformation from crystalline to amorphous state is therapeutically advantageous, as amorphous dispersions generally exhibit enhanced dissolution rates and improved bioavailability compared with their crystalline counterparts. The correlation between reduced crystallinity and optimal physicochemical properties in 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC supports the importance of matrix disorder for effective drug accommodation.

Release Kinetics and Mechanisms

The release behavior of curcuminoids from TUR-NLC formulations followed sustained release profiles under both simulated gastric (pH 1.2) and intestinal (pH 6.8) conditions. Mathematical modeling revealed that the Korsmeyer–Peppas equation provided the best fit to the experimental data (R 2 > 0.94), with release exponent (n) values ranging from 0.304 to 0.336. According to the Korsmeyer–Peppas model, n values below 0.43 for spherical particles indicate Fickian diffusion-controlled release, where drug transport occurs primarily through molecular diffusion rather than matrix erosion.

The Fickian diffusion mechanism observed is consistent with the structural characteristics of the NLC system. In this release model, curcuminoids diffuse through the lipid matrix driven by concentration gradient, with the rate of release determined by the diffusion coefficient of the drug within the carrier and the path length of diffusion. The CS-GA coating layer provides a diffusion barrier that modulates release kinetics, contributing to the sustained release profiles observed over the 24 h study period.

Notably, the release rate under gastric conditions (pH 1.2) was substantially lower than under intestinal conditions (pH 6.8) for all formulations. This pH-dependent release behavior can be attributed to the properties of the CS-GA polyelectrolyte complex coating. At acidic pH, the coating remains relatively intact due to protonation of chitosan amino groups and maintenance of electrostatic interactions with gum Arabic. As the pH increases toward neutrality, partial dissociation of the polyelectrolyte complex occurs, increasing coating permeability and facilitating curcuminoid release. This pH-responsive behavior is particularly advantageous for oral delivery applications, as it provides gastric protection while enabling controlled release at the intestinal absorption site.

The modest cumulative release observed in TUR-NLC formulations (6–13% at 8 h) warrants specific discussion, as this value is substantially lower than uncoated curcumin-NLC systems reported by other investigators. For instance, Sadati Behbahani et al. (2019) reported 41% curcumin release within 2 h from uncoated NLCs, while Ban et al. (2020) achieved approximately 50–70% release at 8 h. The markedly lower release from our CS-GA-coated formulations reflects the intentional design of a gastro-protective delivery system rather than incomplete drug release.

The CS-GA polyelectrolyte complex coating provides a diffusion barrier that substantially restricts drug release under conditions simulating a gastric environment. This behavior aligns with the coating’s intended function of protecting acid-labile curcuminoids during gastric transit while enabling controlled release in the neutral intestinal environment. Furthermore, the low crystallinity of GMS-containing formulations (12–20%) indicates deep molecular dispersion of curcuminoids within the lipid matrix, which may also contribute to slower diffusion rates compared to surface-adsorbed drug. The combination of structured lipid matrix and polyelectrolyte coating creates a dual-barrier system optimized for oral delivery applications requiring gastric protection with intestinal targeting.

Mechanistic Integration: Structure-Release Relationships

The release kinetics data can be comprehensively explained through integration with solid-state characterization findings. DSC analysis revealed decreased crystallinity in all TUR-NLC formulations, evidenced by broader and less intense endothermic transitions compared to blank controls. XRD patterns similarly showed reduced crystalline peaks, indicating partial amorphization of the lipid matrix. This structural modification is critical for the observed Fickian diffusion behavior (n = 0.304–0.336), as the partially amorphous matrix creates structural defects that serve as preferential diffusion pathways for curcuminoids. The absence of distinct curcumin melting endotherms (typically at 183 °C) in loaded formulations confirms curcuminoids exist in an amorphous or noncrystalline state within the lipid matrix, facilitating their gradual diffusion rather than requiring dissolution from crystalline drug domains.

The CS-GA coating exhibits pH-responsive behavior that explains the differential release patterns across simulated gastrointestinal conditions. At gastric pH 1.2, chitosan undergoes extensive protonation of amino groups, promoting strong electrostatic complexation with carboxyl groups of gum Arabic. This creates a compact, tightly bound polyelectrolyte complex that functions as an effective barrier limiting both water penetration and drug diffusion, explaining the minimal release observed in SGF (7–11% at 8 h). Conversely, at intestinal pH 6.8, partial deprotonation of chitosan weakens electrostatic interactions, leading to controlled coating swelling and increased hydration. This pH-triggered transition facilitates gradual drug diffusion while maintaining overall structural integrity, consistent with the sustained release profile observed in SIF.

Quantitative correlation exists between lipid composition and release kinetics parameters. The 6MCT/3GMS formulation exhibits the lowest release rate constant (k KP = 4.092) attributable to the structured crystalline network provided by glycerol monostearate, which increases diffusion pathway tortuosity and reduces drug mobility. In contrast, MCT-only formulations show higher rate constants (k KP = 5.408–5.626) due to their more liquid-like matrix structure with lower diffusion resistance. This demonstrates that lipid composition directly modulates release kinetics through control of matrix crystallinity and structural organization.

Drug release from CS-GA-coated TUR-NLCs occurs through a sequential three-step process: (i) drug dissolution or desorption from the partially amorphous lipid matrix, (ii) diffusion through the hydrated CS-GA coating layer, and (iii) partitioning into the external aqueous phase. The Fickian diffusion behavior (n < 0.43 for spherical systems) indicates that step (ii), diffusion through the coating, represents the rate-limiting process. This validates the intended function of the coating as a diffusion barrier rather than an erodible release matrix, enabling sustained release while maintaining formulation stability.

Gastrointestinal Stability

The CS-GA-coated TUR-NLC formulations demonstrated formulation-dependent particle size changes during exposure to simulated gastrointestinal fluids. Under simulated gastric fluid (SGF, pH 1.2) conditions, the coated formulations showed smaller size changes than under simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, pH 6.8), suggesting greater structural stability in an acidic medium, whereas larger increases in SIF indicated swelling, aggregation, or matrix destabilization under intestinal conditions. This behavior was consistent with the pH-responsive performance of the coated system observed in the release study.

The protective effect of the CS-GA coating under acidic conditions can be attributed to several factors. First, although chitosan is soluble under acidic gastric conditions, the electrostatic complexation with gum Arabic forms a compact polyelectrolyte network that restricts diffusion of both water into the particle core and curcuminoids out of the matrix. Second, the electrostatic interaction between chitosan and gum Arabic creates a cross-linked network that resists dissociation under acidic conditions. Third, gum Arabic itself demonstrates resistance to degradation by gastric enzymes, providing protection for the underlying lipid matrix.

These findings have important implications for oral curcuminoid delivery. The acidic environment of the stomach and the presence of digestive enzymes represent major barriers to effective oral administration of lipid-based formulations. By providing gastric protection through the CS-GA coating, the developed TUR-NLC system minimizes premature drug release and potential degradation in the stomach, thereby maximizing the amount of intact curcuminoid reaching the intestinal absorption site.

In Vitro Cytotoxicity and Anti-Inflammatory Activity

Cytotoxicity evaluation in RAW 264.7 murine macrophages demonstrated favorable biocompatibility profiles for all TUR-NLC formulations. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values ranged from 478.65 to 1442.74 μg/mL. The GMS-containing formulations (12MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC and 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC) showed lower cytotoxicity and a better in vitro safety profile than the MCT-only formulations. This enhanced safety profile of GMS-containing formulations indicates acceptable cytotoxicity thresholds at concentrations relevant for therapeutic applications. The favorable biocompatibility can be attributed to the use of well-established pharmaceutical excipients (MCT, GMS) and biocompatible coating polymers (chitosan, gum Arabic) with documented safety profiles in drug delivery applications. ,

Anti-inflammatory activity was assessed through inhibition of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced nitric oxide (NO) production in RAW 264.7 macrophages. The 18MCT-TUR-NLC formulation exhibited the highest NO inhibition (25.91%) at 100 μg/mL, demonstrating preserved anti-inflammatory activity of the encapsulated curcuminoids. The observed anti-inflammatory effect likely results from suppression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression and modulation of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathways, consistent with the established pharmacology of curcumin. ,

It should be acknowledged that the biological assessment in this study was limited to NO inhibition in a single cell line. A more comprehensive evaluation incorporating additional assays such as antioxidant activity (DPPH, ABTS), cytokine quantification (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), and cellular uptake studies would provide deeper mechanistic insight into the therapeutic potential of the developed formulations. Furthermore, comparison with free curcumin or unencapsulated turmeric extract at equivalent curcuminoid concentrations would enable quantification of the enhancement provided by NLC encapsulation.

Storage Stability

Thermal stability assessment under refrigerated conditions (4 °C) revealed satisfactory curcuminoid retention (79–118%) over an 8 week storage period. The retention values exceeding 100% observed in some formulations likely reflect measurement variability or minor evaporation of the dispersion medium during storage, concentrating the curcuminoid content. Importantly, no formulation exhibited curcuminoid retention below 79%, indicating adequate protection against degradation under the storage conditions evaluated.

The stability observed can be attributed to several factors inherent to the NLC system. First, encapsulation within the lipid matrix physically isolates curcuminoids from environmental degradation factors, including oxygen, light, and moisture. Second, the relatively low storage temperature reduces the rate of chemical degradation reactions. Third, the solid lipid component (GMS) provides structural stability to the nanoparticle matrix, preventing coalescence and drug expulsion that can occur in liquid lipid-only systems. The CS-GA coating may provide protection by creating a physical barrier between the lipid core and the external environment.

Study Limitations and Future Perspectives

While this study provides comprehensive in vitro characterization, establishing proof of concept for CS-GA-coated TUR-NLC systems, several important limitations should be acknowledged. The primary limitation is the absence of in vivo data demonstrating enhanced oral bioavailability, pharmacokinetic profiles, anti-inflammatory efficacy in disease models, and systemic toxicity. Animal studies are essential for complete therapeutic validation and clinical translation. The present study serves as systematic formulation development, providing critical preliminary data for subsequent in vivo investigation.

From a methodological perspective, stability assessment could be enhanced through additional analytical techniques. Direct visualization of particle morphology changes during simulated digestion using TEM or cryo-TEM would provide valuable insights into coating integrity and structural evolution under GI conditions. Quantitative assessment of coating erosion or dissolution rates through fluorescent-labeled polymer tracking would complement the functional stability evidence provided by release studies. Extended stability studies (3–6 months) under various storage conditions would strengthen the long-term stability profile.

Future investigations should include the following: (1) comprehensive in vivo pharmacokinetic studies in rats to assess oral bioavailability enhancement, (2) biodistribution analysis to evaluate tissue accumulation patterns, (3) anti-inflammatory efficacy evaluation in relevant disease models such as DSS-induced colitis or adjuvant-induced arthritis, (4) chronic toxicity assessment and safety profiling, and (5) advanced stability characterization including TEM visualization and spectroscopic monitoring of coating integrity. These studies will validate the therapeutic potential demonstrated through in vitro characterization and advance the formulation toward clinical application.

Conclusion

This systematic formulation development study demonstrates that chitosan-gum Arabic polyelectrolyte complex coating significantly enhances the gastrointestinal stability of turmeric curcuminoid-loaded nanostructured lipid carriers. Through comprehensive physicochemical characterization, solid-state analysis, and biological activity assessment, the following key findings were established: (1) The optimized 6MCT/3GMS-TUR-NLC formulation achieves favorable physicochemical properties (particle size 53.20 nm) with 68% encapsulation efficiency and a favorable in vitro safety profile (IC50 > 1000 μg/mL). (2) CS-GA coating provides pH-responsive protection, maintaining stability under simulated gastric conditions while enabling controlled release in the intestinal environment through Fickian diffusion mechanisms. (3) Nanoencapsulation preserves anti-inflammatory bioactivity (up to 25.91% NO inhibition) while reducing acute cytotoxicity compared to free turmeric extract, as demonstrated through direct comparative evaluation. (4) The coating strategy demonstrates proof of concept for enhanced gastrointestinal protection of bioactive curcuminoids. This formulation development study provides essential foundational data for the rational design of oral curcuminoid delivery systems. In vivo pharmacokinetic studies, bioavailability assessment, and anti-inflammatory efficacy evaluation in disease models (e.g., DSS-induced colitis and adjuvant-induced arthritis) are warranted as the next phase to validate the therapeutic potential demonstrated through comprehensive in vitro characterization.
