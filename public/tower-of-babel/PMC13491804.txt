Association between problematic smartphone use, loneliness, and risk behaviors among high school students in a conflict-affected setting: A cross-sectional study from Palestine
Authors: Muna Ahmead, Maisa Nabulsi, Raj’a Zyoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudents and young adults are known to use their mobile devices at significant rates, which may put them at risk for problematic use, especially in war-torn countries. However, there is limited research on problematic smartphone use in Palestine. This study aims to examine the relationship between sociodemographic variables, feelings of loneliness, smoking, consumption of energy drinks, alcohol use, and problematic smartphone use among 12th-grade students.MethodsThe study utilized a cross-sectional research design. We gathered data using a self-reported questionnaire that included the Smartphone Addiction Scale (SAS-SV), and the UCLA Loneliness Scale.ResultsA total of 1,083 participants were recruited, of whom 57.3% experienced problematic smartphone use. Also, 6.5% of the participants drank alcohol, 52.5% consumed energy drinks, 9% smoked cigarettes and 20.3% smoked water-pipe. The results of multivariate regression analysis revealed that participants who used smartphones for less than 2 h daily (AOR: 0.112, p < 0.001), those who used smartphones for 2 to 3 h daily (AOR: 0.354, p < 0.001) were at a high risk of problematic smartphone use compared to other participants. Also, participants who reported not feeling lonely were at a low risk of problematic smartphone use compared to those who felt lonely (AOR: 0.112, p < 0.001). Finally, participants who smoke cigarettes were at a high risk to experience problematic smartphone use than those who did not smoke (AOR: 1.630, p = 0.040). The sociodemographic variables, alcohol consumption, energy drink consumption, or water-pipe use were significant in the bivariate analysis but were not significant in the multivariate regression analysis.ConclusionProblematic smartphone use was prevalent among 12th-grade high school students in the current study. Feelings of loneliness, smoking cigarettes, and increased time spent on smartphones were associated with this issue. Programs that educate students, parents, and teachers on problematic smartphone use and risk factors can help prevent, detect, and manage this problem.Keywords: Problematic smartphone use, Loneliness, Substance use, Mobile devices, Palestine

Introduction

Communication technologies significantly influence adolescents in educational settings [1]. They spend a considerable amount of time on mobile devices, which have become their main focus. This extensive use may affect their social interactions and academic performance [2, 3], potentially resulting in diminished face-to-face communication skills and lower academic achievement due to distractions from mobile devices. By the end of 2022, the global number of smartphone subscriptions reached 6.6 billion and is projected to more than double, rising from 3.6 billion in 2016 to 7.5 billion by 2026 [4]. In the United States, 95% of adolescents use mobile devices for leisure, interpersonal communication, and educational activities, often opting to avoid in-person interactions [5]. In Palestine, 93% of adolescents own mobile devices [6], and 83.3% of Palestinian students primarily use them [7].

Mobile devices are increasingly favored by young people and teenagers over laptops and desktop computers due to their wide range of integrated features. These features include completing academic assignments, watching sporting events, playing online games, conducting financial transactions, sending emails, communicating, organizing schedules, making online purchases, and engaging with social media platforms [8, 9]. Additionally, mobile devices provide accessible applications and built-in functions for individuals with various impairments [10]. For instance, they incorporate electronic information and communication technology, which creates new opportunities for addressing many challenges faced by people with visual impairments [10]. However, individuals who excessively and unrestrictedly use mobile devices for extended periods may develop problematic smartphone use. This behavior is characterized by obsessive usage that negatively affects daily functioning, including productivity, social interactions, physical health, and emotional well-being [11].

Billieux (2012) provided one of the first and most widely accepted definitions of problematic mobile phone usage, describing it as the inability to control one’s phone use, which can ultimately lead to negative outcomes in daily life [12]. Elhai et al. (2017) defined problematic smartphone use (PSU) as an excessive and uncontrolled pattern of smartphone use that is associated with psychosocial dysfunction. This dysfunction may manifest through poor interpersonal relationships, reduced academic and occupational performance, and negative mental health outcomes such as anxiety and depression [13]. Although these terms are often used interchangeably in the literature, PSU is distinct from smartphone addiction. The term “smartphone addiction” suggests that excessive use meets the clinical criteria for an addictive disorder, while PSU is intentionally broad, encompassing various dysregulated usage patterns that lead to functional problems without necessarily fulfilling the full criteria for addiction [14, 15]. Panova and Carbonell (2018) caution that labeling smartphone use as an addiction may contribute to pathologizing normal behavior and may obscure the different motivations behind excessive use [16]. This study will utilize the term “problematic smartphone use,” reserving the term “smartphone addiction” for studies that specifically employ that terminology.

Problematic smartphone use among adolescents has been linked to various negative effects, including anxiety, stress, depression, low self-esteem, low self-confidence, bipolar disorder, dependent personality disorder, obsessive-compulsive personality disorder, and somatization [17–19]. This problematic use may adversely affect academic performance, social interactions, and coping strategies [20–23]. Dong et al. (2020) reported that 33.37% of teenagers exhibited problematic smartphone use [24]. Sohn et al. (2019) found that 25% of children and adolescents displayed symptoms of problematic smartphone use [25]. Also, one systematic review revealed that the rates of problematic smartphone use in Iran, Lebanon, Kuwait, Egypt, and Saudi Arabia ranged from 17% to 71.96%, with Egypt showing the highest prevalence at 95.8% [26]. A cross-cultural study of four MENA nations revealed varying prevalence rates: 59.8% in Jordan, 27.2% in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), 17.3% in Sudan, and 8.6% in Yemen [27].

There are numerous research studies that have examined the potential causes of problematic smartphone use, particularly the rise in feelings of loneliness stemming from inadequate social support [28–31]. The term “loneliness” describes the unpleasant feeling that occurs when an individual’s social network is insufficient [32]. It is also defined as the subjective negative emotions that arise from perceived gaps between expected and actual social relationships [33]. Research indicates that adolescents lacking social support may experience loneliness and develop an excessive reliance on these devices to fulfill their emotional needs [34]. Compensatory Internet Use Theory suggests that when individuals face psychosocial challenges in their lives, they may turn to their mobile devices or the internet for support [35]. Moreover, this theory asserts that individuals often use smartphones as a means to cope with negative emotions or psychological difficulties. Those experiencing social isolation or loneliness are more likely to develop distorted perceptions of their self-image and surroundings. This distortion can lead to significant disengagement from the physical world and an aversion to it, as illustrated by Davis’s (2001) cognitive-behavioral model of problematic Internet use [36, 37]. Consequently, individuals may depend on smartphones for virtual interactions, which can provide temporary relief from feelings of loneliness. However, this reliance may also heighten their risk of developing problematic smartphone use [37].

In addition, there is a lack of research investigating the potential association between problematic smartphone use and various risk behaviors including substance use, excessive alcohol consumption, energy drink consumption, and cigarette smoking [38–41]. A study conducted on Palestinian 10th-grade students revealed that tobacco was the most commonly used substance (40.6%), followed by alcohol (3.2%) and illicit narcotics (2.0%). Approximately 59.7% of students reported having used tobacco at least once in their lifetime, while 7.9% reported similar usage for alcohol. The average age at which students began smoking was 12, whereas the average age for initiating alcohol consumption was 14 [42]. In substance use disorders such as those involving alcohol and nicotine, the brain’s impulse control mechanisms become more sensitive, and the reward system becomes overactive. This may lead to smartphone addiction and impaired self-control [43, 44].

Digital devices may influence young adults’ inclinations, purchases, and intentions to consume unhealthy beverages, such as energy drinks [45]. Energy drinks are non-alcoholic beverages that typically contain ingredients like caffeine, guarana, taurine, botanicals, and vitamins [46]. Their prevalence has notably increased, especially among adolescents and young adults. For instance, a study found that 52.5% of Palestinian adolescents reported consuming energy beverages [47]. Additionally, research indicates that the likelihood of exceeding the World Health Organization’s recommended daily limits for sugar and caffeine rises by 14% for each extra hour spent talking on a mobile phone [48]. Adolescents who consume soda, sweetened beverages, and highly caffeinated drinks, which can be considered addictive, are more likely to use mobile devices [49].

Palestine’s unique socioeconomic context, shaped by political and economic challenges, may worsen the rising prevalence of risk behaviors, including consumption of alcohol, cigarettes, and energy drinks. As a coping mechanism for the harsh conditions they face—such as political violence, home demolitions, arrests, restrictions on movement, and land encroachments—certain studies suggest that Palestinian youth may turn to these substances or behaviors [50–53]. Additionally, academic stress significantly contributes to the psychological distress, anxiety, and decreased well-being of Palestinian students [54, 55]. Evidence also shows that 70.6% of nurses and nursing students in the West Bank have experienced violence related to checkpoints, with a poor quality of life being highly prevalent in both environmental and psychological domains [56]. Together, these findings present a compelling, contextually relevant argument supporting the argument that the overlap of academic and conflict-related stressors may lead to maladaptive coping behaviors, including problematic smartphone use [54, 55].

To the best of our knowledge, there has been no prior study examining the relationship between problematic smartphone use and risk behaviors including energy drink consumption, smoking, and alcohol intake among Palestinian adolescents. Also, there has been limited focus on problematic smartphone use among adolescents, particularly those in the 12th grade. This study aimed to examine the relationship between problematic smartphone use and mental health problems such as smoking, energy drink consumption, alcohol, and loneliness among 12th graders. The study further examined the factors that predict problematic smartphone use in these students.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study was conducted from February 21 to May 1, 2025, utilizing a descriptive cross-sectional survey. It included Palestinian students in the twelfth grade, those aged 18, and living in Jerusalem and the West Bank. Also, the study included both male and female students. The sample size was determined based on a smartphone addiction prevalence rate of 37.9% [57], with a 95% confidence level and a 3% margin of error, resulting in a minimum required sample of 996 students. After accounting for a projected non-response rate of 5%, the final target sample size was established at 1,048 students. Finally, 1,083 students completed the distributed questionnaire. Participants were selected through convenience sampling, a non-probability method that involves selecting individuals from the target population based on their availability. Data were collected via an anonymous online survey, which participants completed independently. The researcher distributed an electronic version of the questionnaire through Google Forms, along with an official invitation to participate. The questionnaire was shared through email, WhatsApp, Facebook student groups, and various other social media platforms.

Measures

The participants in this study completed a self-reported questionnaire that consisted of four sections. The first section included a socio-demographic sheet that requested information on the participants’ age, gender, grade point average (GPA), location of residence, education levels of both parents, and employment status. Additionally, it contained a question regarding the duration of mobile device use. Participants were also questioned about their substance use, with questions adapted from previous studies [58–61]. These questions addressed their alcohol consumption, cigarette smoking, use of water pipes, and intake of energy drinks. Furthermore, participants were asked about the frequency of their usage. The second section of the study included the UCLA Loneliness Scale (UCLA-LS-20) [62, 63], a 20-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess loneliness. Respondents rated their feelings on a scale from “not at all” (1) to “often” (4). Higher scores indicate a greater level of loneliness, with overall scores ranging from 20 to 80. For the UCLA-LS-20, a score above 43 is commonly used as the cut-off point [62]. The internal consistency of the scale, measured by Cronbach’s α, was found to be 0.94.

The study’s third section had the Smartphone Addiction Scale short version (SAS-SV). which was developed by Kwon et al. [64] and had high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.91). It is composed of 10 items that assess smartphone addiction and is rated on a six-point Likert scale that goes from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree.” The scale’s total scores range from 10 to 60, and the current study used a threshold of 32 as the cut-off point to differentiate between problematic and non-problematic users [65, 66]. Cronbach’s alpha for the SAS-SV was 0.90.

A team of five mental health experts evaluated the scales’ content for cultural appropriateness, and no modifications were implemented. The scales were initially translated into Arabic by the study team and thereafter back-translated into English by a certified translator. To assess language comprehensibility, the instrument was evaluated on 25 students during the pilot phase. The original English questionnaire and its back-translated version were analyzed to confirm the accuracy of the translation, and the Arabic version was utilized in the current study.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive analyses of all research variables were presented in terms of frequencies and percentages, alongside the results of a chi-square test. A multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted, and the findings are reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of a statistically significant association. In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, we included variables that we anticipated would have an association with the study outcome, regardless of their significance in the univariate analysis. The dependent variable was dichotomized for the logistic regression (PSU: ≥32 vs. <32 on SAS-SV). We also assessed multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (VIF) in our logistic regression model, finding all VIF values to be less than 5, which indicated no concerns regarding multicollinearity. As a result, we did not identify any multicollinearity among our study variables. To evaluate the fit of the multivariate logistic regression model, we performed the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The result yielded a p-value of 0.468, which is well above the 0.05 significance threshold. This finding confirmed that the logistic regression model fits the data adequately and can be considered a good fit for the study sample.

Discussion

This study examined the factors associated with problematic smartphone use among 12th-grade students. The results indicated that 57.3% of respondents reported experiencing problematic smartphone use, which is higher than findings from previous research [8, 25, 61, 67–70]. For example, 37.9% of Palestinian medical and health sciences university students reported experiencing problematic smartphone use [61]. Palestinian secondary students utilized mobile devices and other electronic devices primarily for communication (77.5%), entertainment (76.7%), and education (67.5%) [71]. According to Scott et al. (2016), problematic use of technology, including mobile devices, was associated with decreased social skills, emotional intelligence, and empathy, as well as increased social conflict [72]. Furthermore, Zhou et al. (2025) demonstrated that excessive use and dependence on mobile phones were linked to social anxiety and loneliness, suggesting that interpersonal dysfunction may arise from mobile device misuse [73]. Additionally, Hawi and Samaha (2016) found a negative correlation between academic performance and mobile device use [74]. In regions where infrastructure and educational resources are limited due to conflict and emergencies, such as war-torn countries like Palestine, the executive functions of students can be assessed and potentially improved through mobile devices. These devices can provide access to educational resources and cognitive training applications, which may enhance learning outcomes despite challenging circumstances [75].

The data indicate that the mental health of Palestinian 12th-grade students is concerning. This highlights the need of addressing their problematic smartphone use and implementing effective interventions and support networks to reduce its prevalence. It is essential for teachers, education policymakers, and family members to raise awareness among these students about the adverse effects of problematic smartphone use on both their academic performance and mental health. Additionally, incorporating behavioral therapies, such as cognitive behavioral therapy and mindfulness techniques, within educational settings is crucial for providing adequate support to these adolescents.

This study identified various factors associated with a high prevalence of problematic smartphone use among 12th-grade students, including the duration of mobile device use, addiction-like behaviors, and feelings of loneliness.

Our findings indicated that participants using smartphones for less than 2 h per day, as well as those using smartphones for 2–3 h daily, were at a low risk of experiencing problematic smartphone use compared to those using smartphones for 4 or more hours per day. Other research supports this finding [76]. Çağan and Koca (2020) found that students’ problematic smartphone use increased with rising daily screen time, noting that daily mobile device use of 5 h or more heightens the likelihood of problematic smartphone use [68]. Moreover, Mora-Monteros et al. (2023) revealed that screen usage among young people has surged, with teens spending over 4 h on screens in 2020 compared to 2012 [77]. Lin et al. (2015) reported a correlation between increased mobile device use and two primary signs of addiction: compulsion and tolerance [78]. However, Sohn et al. (2021) [25] noted that, although many individuals use their mobile devices excessively, this behavior does not necessarily indicate addiction [25]. Excessive mobile device use can manifest as both subjective suffering and functional impairment. Some individuals may develop problematic use of their phones after just a few uses, while others might use them beneficially for longer periods without negative consequences, enabling them to maintain healthy behaviors, such as connecting with loved ones or getting adequate sleep [79]. To reduce screen time, students should prioritize physical education and leisure activities while promoting real-life interactions. Efforts should focus on limiting screen time and enhancing physical activity by creating new activities or class assignments.

The present study found that individuals who reported no feelings of loneliness were less likely to engage in problematic smartphone use compared to those who felt lonely. These findings support previous research that has identified a link between loneliness and problematic smartphone use [36, 80–82]. Loneliness, defined as a sense of loss of belonging, is regarded as a fundamental human motivation [79]. Individuals who experience loneliness are more likely to engage in inappropriate, excessive, or continuous use of mobile devices, which can result in negative smartphone behaviors [83]. According to the compensatory internet-use theory, people often turn to the internet as a means to cope with negative emotions or to evade real-life problems. Additionally, Katz and Blumler’s (1974) theory of uses and gratifications suggests that psychological needs drive the use of mobile devices [84]. Farajalla et al. (2025) found that, despite significant declines in psychological and environmental quality of life during the October 7 war, the social ties among Palestinian students remained largely intact [85]. This suggests that social interaction may serve as an important protective factor in conflict situations, supporting the hypothesis that a decrease in perceived support among students may lead to increased compensatory smartphone use.

Therefore, loneliness—whether viewed as an unpleasant experience or a challenging living condition—may drive individuals to develop problematic smartphone use [69, 83]. In response to COVID-19 and the ongoing conflict in Palestine following October 7, 2023, the Palestinian Ministry of Education and Higher Education has launched an online secondary program. Due to city and school closures, this initiative offers live courses through YouTube, radio, and television, creating a comprehensive daily class schedule for 12th-grade students. Students and educators engage via applications such as Zoom and Google Hangouts [86]. The transition to remote learning heightens students’ dependence on mobile devices for studying and managing stress, which may worsen feelings of loneliness and contribute to problematic smartphone use. Consequently, our findings may underscore the significance of social relationships and networks among 12th-grade students, including connections with their families, teachers, and classmates, especially considering the challenges of preparing for and passing the General Secondary Education Certificate exam.

Also, our findings showed that smokers were at a significantly higher risk of problematic smartphone use compared to nonsmokers. The relationship between smoking and smartphone addiction has been unclear in previous studies; some found an association [87–90], while others did not [91, 92]. Toda et al. reported that the association between smoking and smartphone use was found in males [93]. Piko et al. hypothesized that this relationship may be influenced by other factors, such as mental health problems (e.g., anxiety or depression) or the underlying motivations for these addictions. They also suggested that adolescents may use both smartphones and cigarettes to alleviate boredom, meet social obligations, and boost self-confidence. As a result, when these adolescents experience boredom or stress, they may spend more time on their smartphones, which could lead to an increase in their smoking behaviors. Thus, engaging in a single health-risk behavior may give rise to complex health problems, potentially contributing to the adoption of additional risky behaviors [41]. Further research is needed to strengthen our understanding of the association between smartphone addiction and smoking, as well as to develop effective prevention and management strategies to address this issue.

The current study did not identify any association between problematic smartphone use and alcohol or energy drink consumption in the multivariate regression analaysis, aligning with previous research [94]. However, other studies have found significant associations between these variables [40, 89, 90]. For instance, Jeong et al. (2016) suggested that individuals who frequently use social networks on their mobile devices develop addictive tendencies more rapidly [95]. Additionally. Jeong et al. (2025) reported that continuous mobile device usage is linked to a higher risk of substance addiction, alcohol consumption, and smoking in Korea [39]. Conversely, Grant et al. (2019) indicated that excessive mobile device use does not seem to directly elevate the risk of alcohol use disorder unless another factor is involved. Since mobile device usage typically begins at a younger age than the onset of alcohol-related issues, it is unlikely that alcohol consumption would lead to increased mobile device usage [38]. Therefore, a longitudinal study is necessary to explore the cause-and-effect relationship between substance use and problematic smartphone use among twelfth-grade students.

There are some limitations to the study. The data were collected cross-sectionally; however, a longitudinal approach is necessary to investigate the association between loneliness, smoking, alcohol intake, energy drink consumption and problematic smartphone use. We employed a convenience sampling method due to limited access to 12th-grade students in schools. The use of convenience sampling through social media platforms may introduce selection bias, leading to an overrepresentation of digitally active students and an underrepresentation of underprivileged or low-connectivity groups. Furthermore, the study may be affected by self-report bias regarding smartphone usage hours, which may not accurately reflect actual usage. Additionally, the online recruitment method may exclude students with limited digital access, which is particularly relevant in a conflict-affected region with unequal infrastructure.

Administering a self-administered online survey to assess substance use such as alcohol may present challenges due to the conservative sociocultural context in which these adolescents reside. This environment may result in hesitance to disclose their substance use. As a result, the study is at a significant risk of underreporting and social desirability bias. Additionally, recruiting participants online could systematically exclude students with limited access to smartphones or the internet, potentially distorting the prevalence estimates of problematic smartphone use. Future research could address these limitations by employing a longitudinal design to more effectively evaluate causal associations over time and explore additional factors, such as social support, PTSD, and traumatic events. Despite these limitations, this research serves as the preliminary nationwide study assessing problematic smartphone use and its association with substance use among adolescents in war conflict zones.

Implication for practice

The results of our study are crucial for informing future research and policy, highlighting the need for comprehensive strategies to prevent and address problematic smartphone use among adolescents. Our findings suggest an increased emphasis on monitoring mobile device usage among 12th-grade students. Moreover, the high prevalence of problematic smartphone use among high school students necessitates a preventative campaign aimed at raising awareness among students, families, teachers, and officials from the Palestinian Ministry of Education regarding the academic and health consequences of excessive mobile device usage. Students who smoke cigarettes or experience loneliness due to a lack of social support often resort to smartphones instead of engaging in face-to-face communication to cope with their emotions. Our research indicates that as students become more reliant on mobile devices, they tend to experience increased feelings of loneliness. This underscores the vital importance of social support from family, teachers, and peers for 12th-grade students, particularly in light of the significant pressures associated with preparing for and passing the General Secondary Education Certificate exam. We may need to prioritize physical learning and recreational activities for this demographic while also promoting daily routines that limit screen time. Additionally, developing programs or classroom activities designed to reduce screen time and encourage a healthier lifestyle is essential. Consequently, longitudinal research is necessary to investigate any causal relationships between energy drinks, substance use such as alcohol intake and other drugs and problematic smartphone use to safeguard the health of 12th-grade students.

Conclusion

Problematic smartphone use was prevalent among 12th-grade high school students in this study. It was associated with feelings of loneliness, cigarette smoking, and increased smartphone usage. There is a need to create programs that educate students, parents, and teachers about problematic smartphone use and its risk factors to help prevent, detect, and manage issues related to mobile device usage. Additionally, further qualitative and quantitative research is necessary to explore the factors associated with problematic smartphone use in conflict zones.
