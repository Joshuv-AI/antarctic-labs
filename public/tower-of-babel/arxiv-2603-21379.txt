A PRACTICAL MODE-PARALLEL IMPLEMENTATION OF THE (H-)TUCKER DECOMPOSITION VIA RANDOMIZATION
Martina Iannacito, Sascha Portaro, Davide Palitta, Claudio Arlandini, Domitilla Brandoni

Abstract
In the last decades, tensors have emerged as the right tool to represent multidimensional data in a compact yet informative manner. Moreover, it is well-known that by performing low-rank factorizations of such tensors one is often able to effectively unveil possible hidden structure in data, mainly due to unexpected dependencies among the different variables encoded in the given tensor. However, computing these factorizations is extremely energy-consuming and memory-demanding, especially for high-dimensional tensors, namely those with a large number of modes. In this paper we focus on two state-of-the-art tensor decompositions: the Tucker and H-Tucker decompositions. We propose novel numerical strategies able to perform these factorizations in a mode-parallel fashion, that is the operations required by the algorithm along all modes are performed in parallel. This is in contrast to what is achieved by many procedures available in the literature that parallelize some of the operations along each mode, e.g., tensor-times-matrix steps, while still visiting one mode at the time in a sequential manner. Our strategies make use of cutting-edge randomization techniques comprising fiber sampling and randomized range-finding steps. We provide upper bounds on the expected value of the error provided by our factorizations while a panel of numerical results showcases the potential of our approach in reducing both the running time and the storage demand of the whole procedure. Moreover, experiments carried out in HPC environments illustrate the good scaling of our mode-parallel approach.

Introduction

In the last years, tensors have become one of the most suitable mathematical objects for representing large datasets and their fundamental role is widely recognized.
Tensor techniques find application in a variety of different fields including deep neural networks
[

]
, image classification and recognition

The main feature of tensor techniques is the possibility of encoding data of very different nature in a single, compact tool that, on one hand, obeys to a strict mathematical formalism and, on the other hand, has the flexibility of being represented by means of one’s favorite tensor format. A non complete list of tensor formats available in the literature include the Tucker format
[

In this paper we focus on the Tucker and H-Tucker formats as these are among the most widely used tensor formats for compressing and revealing hidden patterns in data while preserving its multiway nature.

Given an order-
d
tensor
\mathbfcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
, representing it in the Tucker format means factorizing
\mathbfcal{X}
as the product of
d

{\mathbf{{U}}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times r_{i}}
and an order-
d
tensor of smaller size
\mathbfcal{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
,
r_{i}\leq n_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,d
, such that

\mathbfcal{X}=\mathbfcal{S}\times_{1}{\mathbf{{U}}}_{1}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}{\mathbf{{U}}}_{d},

(1)

where
\times_{i}
denotes the mode-
i

2.2

Each factor matrix
{\mathbf{{U}}}_{i}
tries to capture most of the information of the column space of the corresponding tensor matricization, whose column number grows exponentially with
d
. Classical Tucker decomposition algorithms form the
d

[
8
]

One of the main disadvantages of the Tucker format (
1
) is the allocation of the core tensor
\mathbfcal{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
whose number of entries grows exponentially with the number of modes
d
. To reduce this important storage cost, Hackbusch proposed the H-Tucker format
[
12
]
which is able to attain memory requirements that now scale linearly with
d
.
This is achieved by relying on a binary tree structure to factorize a given tensor, forming and decomposing a tensor matricization for each tree node; see
[
11
]
and also section
4
for further details.

The computation of both the Tucker and H-Tucker decompositions requires performing a significant number of SVDs of large dimensional objects oftentimes leading to excessive computational costs and memory requirements. The drawbacks of these deterministic algorithms have motivated the search for more efficient methods and randomization-based approaches are frequently employed to decrease these computational costs, boosting algorithmic performance; see, e.g.,
[
1
]
. Many of these techniques simply amount to applying state-of-the-art randomized techniques for matrix (low-rank) approximation to each matricization of the tensor of interest. While this approach often reduces the running time of the overall procedure, it does not cut down its storage demand due to the requirement of assembling the full matricizations.

In this paper we present a new Tucker decomposition technique combining tensor fiber sampling and randomized range-finding techniques. In particular, thanks to the fiber sampling step we overcome the computational costs and memory requirements of forming any tensor matricizations, while the range-finding step improves the approximation of the tensor matricization column space.
Avoiding the allocation of any matricization is key to attain a practical mode-parallel algorithm for Tucker decomposition. Indeed, while the original algorithm is embarrassingly parallelizable mode-wise, the need of assembling tensor matricizations, whose number of entries is equal to the one of the original tensor, makes it impractical. Now, thanks to our fiber sampling step, we are able to allocate only a sizable number of fibers on each computational node thus attaining an actual mode-parallel approach able to reduce inter-node communication costs and local memory requirements. Other contributions available in the literature proposed a parallel implementation of the Tucker decomposition; see, e.g.,
[
2
,
6
,
16
]
. However, their main focus is to increase the efficiency of Tensor-Times-Matrix (TTM) operations via parallelization. Our goal here is different. Our mode-wise approach fully unlocks the natural parallel nature of the Tucker decomposition by overcoming the need of allocating
d
copies of the entire original tensor. Clearly, along each mode, parallel TTMs can be then performed by taking inspiration from the aforementioned works to further improve the performance and scalability of the overall method. For the H-Tucker decomposition, instead, we have not been successful at finding any work considering the parallelization of this algorithm.

Starting from our randomized Tucker decomposition, we introduce the same modifications in the H-Tucker decomposition. Since, in this case, the number of nodes in the decomposition tree is usually larger than
d
, the computational benefits of our randomized H-Tucker variant are even more appreciable.

2.2
Tensor basics

In this section we revise some of the basic operations involving tensors by setting the notation and conventions that will be largely used in the remainder of this paper.
Several tensor decomposition algorithms involve reshaping tensors into matrices. To rearrange elements of tensors into other mathematical objects, we have to establish a map that associates to each multilinear index a linear index. Let
\mathbb{L}:[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{d}]\rightarrow[n_{1}\cdots n_{d}]
be the linear index map, defined as

\mathbb{L}(\mathbf{i})=i_{1}+\sum_{k=2}^{d}(i_{k}-1)g_{k}\qquad\text{where}\qquad g_{k}=\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}n_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad g_{1}=1.

(2)

The inverse of this function associates a multilinear index with a linear index. Let
\mathbb{T}:[n_{1}\cdots n_{d}]\rightarrow[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{d}]
be the tuple index map, such that
\mathbb{T}(i)=\mathbf{i}
where

\mathbf{i}(k)=1+\frac{1}{g_{k}}\bigl((i-1){\rm mod}(n_{k}g_{k})\bigr).

Note that
\mathbb{L}
and its inverse
\mathbb{T}
are just a conventional choice: as discussed in
[
3
]
other possible maps can be selected. Once the linear index map,
\mathbb{L}
, has been fixed, the ordering followed in operations like matricization and vectorization is fixed.

Definition 1
.

The matricization (or unfolding) along mode-
k

{\mathbf{{A}}}^{(k)}(i_{k},j)=\mathbfcal{A}(i_{1},\dots,i_{d}),

The unfolding operation can be extended to a set of modes. Let

along the modes in

produces a matrix,

{\mathbf{{A}}}^{({\mathcal{S}})}(i,j)=\mathbfcal{A}(i_{1},\dots,i_{d}),

j=\mathbb{L}(i_{\ell_{1}},\dots,i_{\ell_{h}})

\{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{h}\}=[d]-\mathcal{S}

The size of

and

.

Definition 2
.

The Tensor-Times-Matrix (TTM) product of

Finally, when we want to write the TTM product of

2.3
Randomized range-finder

The randomized range-finder algorithm
[
13
]
is a foundational tool in randomized numerical linear algebra. The main feature behind the success of this scheme relies on the fact that the column space of a given matrix
{\mathbf{{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
m\geq n
, can often be well approximated by taking a moderate number of linear combinations of its columns where the coefficients of such combinations are chosen randomly. More precisely, given
{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(k+p)}
where
k
is a target rank and
p
an oversampling parameter, we have
\text{Range}({\mathbf{{A\Omega}}})\approx\text{Range}({\mathbf{{A}}})
. The quality of such approximation is mainly driven by the singular value distribution of
{\mathbf{{A}}}
and the nature of
{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}
. For instance, for a Gaussian
{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}
, if
{\mathbf{{Q}}}
denotes the orthogonal factor of the skinny QR factorization of
{\mathbf{{A\Omega}}}
and
\sigma_{i}
is the
i
th singular value of
{\mathbf{{A}}}
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\mathbf{{A}}}-{\mathbf{{QQ}}}^{T}{\mathbf{{A}}}\|_{F}\right]\leq\left(1+\frac{k}{p-1}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}};

(3)

see, e.g.,
[
13
]
.
With the matrix
{\mathbf{{Q}}}
at hand, one can then compute different quantities of interest. The most prominent one is probably given by the SVD-like approximation of
{\mathbf{{A}}}
given by the so-called randomized SVD. This simply consists in computing the standard SVD of
{\mathbf{{Q}}}^{T}{\mathbf{{A}}}={\mathbf{{U\Sigma V}}}^{T}
so that
{\mathbf{{A}}}\approx({\mathbf{{QU}}}){\mathbf{{\Sigma V}}}^{T}
; see, e.g.,
[
13
]
.

In this paper we employ the randomized range-finder scheme, along with a preliminary fiber sampling step, and we provide error estimates on the same lines of (
3
).

3
The Tucker decomposition

The Tucker decomposition
[
29
]
can be seen as a compression technique for tensors which relies on the concept of multilinear rank. The multilinear rank,
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})^{T}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
of
\mathbfcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbf{n}}
of order
d
is such that

r_{k}={\rm rank}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}),\qquad\text{for }k=1,\dots,d.

The Tucker decomposition factorizes
\mathbfcal{X}
as the product of a smaller order-
d
tensor,
\mathbfcal{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbf{r}}
called
core tensor
, and
d

factor matrices
,
{\mathbf{{U}}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times r_{k}}
, that is

\mathbfcal{X}=({\mathbf{{U}}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{{U}}}_{d})\mathbfcal{S}.

(4)

Henceforth, by
low-rank
we will indicate tensors that can be factorized as in (
4
) in a non-trivial way, with multilinear rank smaller than the tensor size.

Among the several available algorithms for computing the Tucker decomposition of a tensor, two of the most widely used are the High-Order SVD (HOSVD)
[
8
]
and the Sequentially Truncated HOSVD (ST-HOSVD)
[
31
]
. The HOSVD finds the Tucker factor matrix
{\mathbf{{U}}}_{k}
of
\mathbfcal{X}
as the left orthogonal factor of the SVD of
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
, for every mode
k=1,\dots,d
. The core tensor is then computed as
\mathbfcal{S}=({\mathbf{{U}}}^{T}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{{U}}}^{T}_{d})\mathbfcal{X}
.

Algorithm 1

[
\mathbfcal{S}
,
\{{\mathbf{{U}}}_{k}\}
] = T-HOSVD(
\mathbfcal{X}
,
\mathbf{t}
)

\mathbfcal{X}
an order-
d

\mathbf{n}
, and a prescribed multilinear rank
\mathbf{t}

2:

output:

\mathbfcal{S}
the core order-
d
tensor of size
\mathbf{r}
, and the factor matrices
\{{\mathbf{{U}}}_{k}\}_{k}

3:

for

k=1,\ldots,d

do

4:

Form
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
, the mode-
k
unfolding of
\mathbfcal{X}

5:

Compute the SVD of
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
and collect the first

left singular vectors on the columns of
{\mathbf{{U}}}_{k}

6:

end

for

7:

Compute
\mathbfcal{S}
as
({\mathbf{{U}}}^{T}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{{U}}}^{T}_{d})\mathbfcal{X}

To further compress the information of a tensor, we can compute the Tucker approximation of
\mathbfcal{X}
at multilinear rank
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})^{T}
such that
t_{k}<r_{k}
for all
k=1,\ldots,d

If we look for an approximation with a given multilinear rank
\mathbf{t}
, the truncated SVD replaces the SVD, that is we keep only the first
t_{k}
left singular vectors of
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
. This variant is called Truncated HOSVD (T-HOSVD). The T-HOSVD is quasi-optimal, that is if
\mathbfcal{X}^{\star}
is the best approximation of
\mathbfcal{X}
at multilinear rank
\mathbf{t}
and
\widehat{\mathbfcal{X}}
is the approximation produced by the T-HOSVD, then

\|{\mathbfcal{X}-\widehat{\mathbfcal{X}}}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{d}\left|\left|\mathbfcal{X}-\mathbfcal{X}^{\star}\right|\right|_{F},

where
d
is the order of
\mathbfcal{X}
. In other words, the T-HOSVD approximation has at most distance
\sqrt{d}
from the best approximation at multilinear rank
\mathbf{t}
, as proven in
[
31
]
.

In Algorithm
1
we report a pseudo-algorithm for the T-HOSVD and it is evident that, from a numerical point of view, the
d
SVDs computed in line
5
are the most computationally expensive steps, dominating the algorithm complexity.
The final complexity of the (T-)HOSVD algorithm is
\mathcal{O}\bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{d}n_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\min\{n_{k},n_{\neq k}\}\bigr)
flops and assuming the
d
mode size of the input tensor to be all equal to

, the computational complexity of the (T-)HOSVD is
\mathcal{O}(dn^{d+1})
. Variants of the HOSVD have been developed to reduce these computational costs, either deterministic, e.g. the ST-HOSVD
[
31
]
or randomized
[
1
]
. The ST-HOSVD alternates the computation of each Tucker factor
{\mathbf{{U}}}_{k}
with the TTM product along the corresponding mode, resulting in a distribution of the TTM steps to form the core tensor. In Algorithm
1
, it means updating
\mathbfcal{X}
by
\mathbfcal{X}\times_{k}{\mathbf{{U}}}_{k}^{T}
after line
5

\mathbfcal{S}=\mathbfcal{X}
in line
7

\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{d}n_{k}r_{<k}n_{>k}\min\{n_{k},r_{<k}n_{>k}\}\bigr)
where
r_{<k}=\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}r_{j}
and
n_{>k}=\prod_{j=k+1}^{d}n_{j}
. This complexity reduces to
\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{d}n^{d-k+2}r^{k-1}\bigr)
assuming that all components of the size and the multilinear rank are equal, and
n=\min\{n,r^{k-1}n^{d-k}\}
. Finally, recalling that
r\leq n
, we can estimate the computational complexity of the SVDs in the ST-HOSVD to amoun to
\mathcal{O}(n^{d+1})
. On the other hand, ST-HOSVD has an intrinsic serial nature not suitable for parallelization.

A first randomized variant of the HOSVD (R-HOSVD) replaces the deterministic SVD of the tensor unfolding in line
5
with the randomized SVD for each mode. In
[
17
]
, the authors discuss possible choices for the random matrices

and they prove in

that if

is the approximation of
\mathbfcal{X}
at multilinear rank
\mathbf{r}
generated by the R-HOSVD with Gaussian matrices

and oversampling parameter

such that

, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbfcal{X}-\widehat{\mathbfcal{X}}\|_{F}\right]\leq\biggl(d+\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{d}r_{k}}{p-1}\biggr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|\left|\mathbfcal{X}-\mathbfcal{X}^{\star}\right|\right|_{F},

(5)

where
\mathbfcal{X}^{\star}
is the best approximation of
\mathbfcal{X}
at multilinear rank
\mathbf{r}
.
The computational cost of the

randomized SVDs reduces to
\mathcal{O}(\sum_{k=1}^{d}r_{k}\prod_{j=1}^{d}n_{j})
flops, that is
{\cal O}(drn^{d})
assuming that all the multilinear rank and the mode size components are equal to
r
and

, respectively. Therefore, the R-HOSVD is able to reduce the main computational cost of the overall procedure by one order of magnitude assuming
r\ll n
. On the other hand, the memory requirements of HOSVD and R-HOSVD remain the same due to the allocation of the matricizations
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
.

3.1
Subsampled randomized HOSVD

In this section we present one of the most important contribution of this paper. Our goal is to further reduce the complexity of computing the Tucker decomposition with a given multilinear rank
\mathbf{r}
in terms of both flops and storage demand.

Let assume, for the time being, that
r_{k}=\text{rank}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)})
and no truncation is performed. In this case, the
columns of the factor
{\mathbf{{U}}}_{k}
amount to an orthogonal basis for the column space of a very fat matrix,
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times n_{\neq k}}
. In general,
n_{k}\ll n_{\neq k}
, so that the column space of
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
has dimension at most
n_{k}
and, in most cases, we do not need all the
n_{\neq k}
columns of
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
to construct a basis for
\text{Range}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)})
. This is the reasoning behind the subsampling step we introduce in our procedure. In place of constructing an orthonormal basis for
\text{Range}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)})
, we construct a basis for
\text{Range}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}{\mathbf{{E}}})
where
{\mathbf{{E}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\neq k}\times s_{k}}
contains on its columns
s_{k}
randomly chosen vectors of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{\neq k}}
for a given
s_{k}\geq n_{k}
. Clearly, if among the
s_{k}
columns of
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}{\mathbf{{E}}}
,

\text{rank}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)})
of them turn out to be linearly independent, then
\text{Range}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)})=\text{Range}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}{\mathbf{{E}}})
.

The construction of
{\mathbf{{Y}}}_{k}:={\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}{\mathbf{{E}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times s_{k}}
faces a couple of challenges. The first one is computational. In particular, we want to avoid at all the construction of any matricization of
\mathbfcal{X}
. This means that we are not allowed to form
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
and then extract some of its columns to form
{\mathbf{{Y}}}_{k}
. A careful handling of index transformations does the job so that we can directly sample fibers of
\mathbfcal{X}
- that would correspond to columns of
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
- to construct
{\mathbf{{Y}}}_{k}
. This fiber sampling step remarkably impacts the performance of our algorithm. First, we avoid the extremely time-consuming operation of rearranging the entries of
\mathbfcal{X}
into the matrix
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
. Second, the explicit construction of
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
requires to copy all the
\prod_{k=1}^{d}n_{k}
entries of
\mathbfcal{X}
; a tremendous cost in terms of storage allocation. The construction of
{\mathbf{{Y}}}_{k}
, on the other hand, requires the storage of
s_{k}
fibers of
\mathbfcal{X}
and since
s_{k}
can often be chosen much smaller than
n_{\neq k}
, storing
{\mathbf{{Y}}}_{k}
turns out to be significantly cheaper than storing
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
. This fact is crucial for achieving a real mode-parallel implementation of the Tucker decomposition. Indeed, assuming we have
d
computational nodes at our disposal, it is very unlikely that each of them has the resources to store a full copy of
\mathbfcal{X}
, namely the matricization
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
. Thanks to our subsampling step, we no longer need to assemble
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
on the
k
-th computational node but rather
{\mathbf{{Y}}}_{k}
. As shown in our numerical results in section
3.3
, this operation is often affordable leading to the realistic possibility of running a mode-parallel implementation of the Tucker decomposition.

The second challenge we need to face when we perform a subsampling step is clearly how to choose the
right
columns of
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
to form
{\mathbf{{Y}}}_{k}={\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}{\mathbf{{E}}}
. Many selection strategies to sample sensible columns of matrices have been proposed in the literature; see, e.g.,
[
26
,
7
,
27
,
4
,
10
,
34
]
. However, computing the measures providing the best column indices is very expensive, with a cost that is often comparable to the one of computing the SVD of
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
. We thus refrain from employing any of these techniques and we simply pick
s_{k}
indices in
[n_{\neq k}]
uniformly at random. It is easy to construct counterexamples where this naive strategy fails, namely, in our context,
\text{Range}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}{\mathbf{{E}}})
does not contain any sensible information coming from
\text{Range}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)})
. On the other hand, in section
3.2
we picture the right theoretical frame where our sumbsampling step succeeds and in section
3.3.2
we report a series of real-world examples where the assumptions we draw are indeed satisfied.

Once we have
{\mathbf{{Y}}}_{k}={\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}{\mathbf{{E}}}
at hand, we compute an approximation to
\text{Range}({\mathbf{{Y}}}_{k})
by the range-finder algorithm recalled in section
2.3
. Indeed, our initial goal was to construct a sensible subspace of dimension

\text{Range}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)})
. The
s_{k}\geq n_{k}

{\mathbf{{Y}}}_{k}

for a given oversampling parameter

, and set as

the orthogonal factor of the skinny QR of
{\mathbf{{Y}}}_{k}{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}_{k}
. Notice that the construction of a much smaller Gaussian matrix in our setting –
{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}_{k}
is

instead of

as in R-HOSVD – is another important feature of our scheme. Indeed, even if it is linear in the dimension of
{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}_{k}
, the cost of drawing a Gaussian matrix should not get overlooked, especially in a tensor setting where
n_{\neq k}
grows exponentially with the number of modes

The reasoning behind our overall approach can thus be summarized as follows

\text{Range}({\mathbf{{U}}}_{k})\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
\text{Range Finder}\end{subarray}}{\approx}\text{Range}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}{\mathbf{{E}}})\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
\text{Subsampling}\end{subarray}}{\approx}\text{Range}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}),

and the related routine, named the Sub-R-HOSVD, is shown in Algorithm

Its computational cost is
\mathcal{O}(\sum_{k=1}^{d}n_{k}s_{k}r_{k}+n_{k}r_{k}^{2})
, which, assuming

, and

for all modes, simplifies to
{\cal O}(d(nsr+nr^{2}))
which leads to significant computational gains for
s\ll n^{d-1}
. The main memory usage of Algorithm
2
is given by the storage of the matrix
{\mathbf{{Y}}}_{k}
that amounts to
s_{k}
vectors of length
n_{k}
, again way less than
\prod_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}
for
s_{k}\ll n_{\neq k}
.

3.2
Error bounds

In this section we show that the Tucker decomposition computed by Algorithm
2
fulfills error bounds similar to the one in (
5
). To this end, we first work mode by mode, showing that the subsampling of the column of
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
followed by a range finding step leads to the construction of good approximations of
\text{Range}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)})
. Then, we put these error bounds together to estimate
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbfcal{X}-\mathbfcal{\widehat{X}}\|_{F}]
where
\mathbfcal{\widehat{X}}
is the approximation given by Algorithm
2
whereas
\mathbfcal{X}
is the original tensor.

By taking inspiration from the results in
[
21
]
, our analysis starts with studying the effects of the subsampling step on a fat matrix
{\mathbf{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
with
m\gg n
. This matrix plays the role of the matricizations
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
.
We first partition
{\mathbf{{X}}}
as follows

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&\displaystyle r\quad n-r&m\\
{\mathbf{{X}}}={\mathbf{{U}}}&{\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{{\Sigma}}}_{1}\\
&{\mathbf{{\Sigma}}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}}&{\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{{V}}}_{1}^{T}\\
{\mathbf{{V}}}_{2}^{T}\end{bmatrix}}&{\begin{matrix}r\\
n-r\end{matrix}}\end{array}

(6)

where
r
will be the
k
-th component
r_{k}
of the target multilinear rank
\mathbf{r}
in Algorithm
2
.

In the next lemma we present some criteria for choosing the subsampling parameter
s
such that
{\mathbf{{XE}}}
preserves some good properties, where
{\mathbf{{E}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times s}
denotes the sampling matrix. These criteria are given in terms of the
coherence
of the row space of
{\mathbf{{X}}}
. In general, given a subspace
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and an orthogonal projector
{\mathbf{{P}}}_{\mathcal{V}}={\mathbf{{VV}}}^{T}
onto
\mathcal{V}
, the coherence of
\mathcal{V}
is defined as

\mu(\mathcal{V}):=\max_{i=1,\ldots,m}\|{\mathbf{{P}}}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{e}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,m}\|{\mathbf{{V}}}^{T}\mathbf{e}_{i}\|_{2}^{2},

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denotes the
i
-th canonical basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Following the partition in (
6
), we are interested in the coherence of
\text{Range}({\mathbf{{V}}}_{1})
and
\text{Range}({\mathbf{{V}}}_{2})
that, for the sake of brevity and with abuse of notation, we will denote as
\mu({\mathbf{{V}}}_{1})
and
\mu({\mathbf{{V}}}_{2})
, respectively.

Lemma 3
.

Let
{\mathbf{{X}}}={\mathbf{{U\Sigma V}}}^{T}
with
{\mathbf{{V}}}=[{\mathbf{{V}}}_{1},{\mathbf{{V}}}_{2}]
,
{\mathbf{{V}}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
,
{\mathbf{{V}}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(n-r)}
as in (
6
). Define the quantities
M_{i}:=m\cdot\mu({\mathbf{{V}}}_{i})
,
i=1,2
. Select the sample size
s\geq\max\{\alpha_{1}M_{1}\log(r),\alpha_{2}M_{2}\log(n-r)\}
, with
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\geq 0
. Then, for
\delta\in[0,1)
and
\eta\in[0,m/s-1]
, it holds

\sigma_{r}({\mathbf{{V}}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}})\geq\sqrt{\frac{(1-\delta)s}{m}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{1}({\mathbf{{V}}}_{2}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}})\leq\sqrt{\frac{(1+\eta)s}{m}},

(7)

with failure probability at most
r\cdot\left(\frac{\mathtt{e}^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}}\right)^{\alpha_{1}\log(r)}+(n-r)\cdot\left(\frac{\mathtt{e}^{\eta}}{(1+\eta)^{1+\eta}}\right)^{\alpha_{2}\log(n-r)}
.

Proof.

The first part directly comes from applying
[
28
, Lemma 3.4]
twice: in the first case, we consider only the lower bound for the
k
-th singular value of
{\mathbf{{V}}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}}
, while, in the second case, we use only the upper bound for the first singular value of
{\mathbf{{V}}}_{2}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}}
. The overall failure probability of these two events follows from a simple union bound. Moreover, the upper bound on
\eta
, namely
\eta\leq m/s-1
comes from simply noticing that
\sigma_{1}({\mathbf{{V}}}_{2}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}})\leq 1
always holds true, regardless of
s
.

Lemma
3
shows that if the subsampling parameter
s
is sufficiently large, then, with high probability, the subsampled right singular matrix
{\mathbf{{V}}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}}
is full rank; this is crucial for showing the effectiveness of the range finding step, as shown next.
Moreover, it also provides the following bound

\|({\mathbf{{V}}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}})^{\dagger}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}({\mathbf{{V}}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}})^{2}}\leq\frac{r}{\sigma_{r}({\mathbf{{V}}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}})^{2}}\leq\frac{r\cdot m}{(1-\delta)s},

(8)

which holds with failure probability at most
r\cdot\left(\frac{\mathtt{e}^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}}\right)^{\alpha_{1}\log(r)}
, if
s\geq\alpha_{1}M_{1}\log(r)
.

The second result in (
7
) says that

\|{\mathbf{{V}}}_{2}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}}\|_{2}
decreases by increasing
s
. This second property turns out to be key to show that the accuracy of the overall procedure does improve by increasing
s
, as intuition may suggests.

Before continuing, let us discuss first the meaning of the subsampling parameter
s
being
sufficiently large
. As shown in Lemma
3
, this must be chosen in terms of coherence as it is customary in subsampling techniques; see, e.g.,
[
15
]
. We must mention that it is easy to construct examples where a subsampling step fails, namely
{\mathbf{{XE}}}
retains information of
{\mathbf{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
only for extremely large
s\approx m
. This is the case, e.g, when
\mu({\mathbf{{V}}})=1
. In these scenarios, the subsampling step does not lead to any computational gain. Our selection of
s
, however, is in terms of
\mu({\mathbf{{V}}}_{1})
and
\mu({\mathbf{{V}}}_{2})
which are in general smaller than
\mu({\mathbf{{V}}})
.
Moreover, in certain practical situations one could expect
\mu({\mathbf{{V}}})
to be small by, e.g., having prior knowledge about the application setting of interest. Indeed,
\mu({\mathbf{{V}}})
may have some proper physical meaning. For instance, when clustering objects from partial observations, the coherence is a function of the minimum cluster size
[
35
]
. Similarly, in recovering spectrally sparse signals, low coherence means that the supporting frequencies are spread out; a natural assumption in this framework
[
5
]
. We also want to stress that the concept of coherence is not strictly related to that of sparsity. In particular, a very sparse matrix does not need necessarily to have a large coherence. For instance, in
[
21
]
a panel of results shows that the sampling step does not lead to any accuracy deterioration even in case of extremely sparse matrices. Similarly,
in section
3.3
we show that for tensors collecting application-driven data, performing a subsampling step does not cause any loss of accuracy, also for modest subsampling parameters
s
. This suggests that we can indeed expect
many
real-world datasets to have low coherence.

We now proceed with our error analysis presenting the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Consider the same assumptions and notation of Lemma
3
. Moreover, let
{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times(r+p)}
be a Gaussian matrix.
Then,
{\mathbf{{V}}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}}{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}
has full rank with probability
1-r\cdot\left(\frac{\mathtt{e}^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}}\right)^{\alpha_{1}\log(r)}
.

Proof.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
3
,
{\mathbf{{V}}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times s}
is full rank with high probability so that if
{\mathbf{{W}}}{\mathbf{{S}}}{\mathbf{{Z}}}^{T}
denotes its SVD, then the matrix
{\mathbf{{Z}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times r}
has full rank with the same probability, i.e.
1-r\cdot\left(\frac{\mathtt{e}^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}}\right)^{\alpha_{1}\log(r)}
.
Since
{\mathbf{{Z}}}
has orthogonal columns,
{\mathbf{{Z}}}^{T}{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}
is Gaussian and, consequently, the matrix
{\mathbf{{V}}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}}{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}={\mathbf{{W}}}{\mathbf{{S}}}({\mathbf{{Z}}}^{T}{\mathbf{{\Omega}}})
is full rank with the same probability of
{\mathbf{{V}}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{{E}}}
being full rank.

With Lemma
3
and
4

{\mathbf{{X}}}

In the synthetic experiments, we consider low-rank tensors of mode dimensions and rank components all equal to

We then construct the Tucker factors as orthogonal matrices coming from the orthogonalization of randomly generated matrices with i.i.d. entries from the normal distribution for test

1
. Once the Tucker factors and the corresponding cores are generated, the dense tensors are constructed and then decomposed by the considered algorithms, to test the ability of the latter ones in computing accurate low-rank decompositions.

For each tensor order, boxplots summarize the distribution of errors and computational times, while logarithmic-scale plots show the median performance as a function of
d
, highlighting the good scaling behavior of the methods. As mentioned above, for these problems we run Algorithm
2
in a sequential manner, namely the loops are performed one after the other. Indeed, one of the goals of this section is to show how our computational improvements lead to dramatic speed ups of the tensor compression even when not working with proper parallel computing architectures.

The results are consistent across all the different tests and can be summarized as follows. In terms of running time, our Sub-R-HOSVD is always very competitive to the point that it becomes one order of magnitude faster than most of the other routines for a large number of modes
d\geq 5
. Only when compared to ST-HOSVD, this performance gap becomes narrower. Indeed, the sequential reductions of the tensor performed by ST-HOSVD allow for the computation of the SVDs of objects of decreasing size, with a consequent cost reduction. Nevertheless, Sub-R-HOSVD is still faster than ST-HOSVD, in addition to be extremely suitable for parallelization as we will show in section
3.3.3
.

The good performance of Sub-R-HOSVD is mainly due to our subsampling step that avoids handling any large matricization. Moreover, it is interesting to see how an extremely small number of fibers is indeed enough to get very accurate results for these examples, where the factorized tensors are low-rank in the Tucker sense by construction. In particular, when
d=8
and the subsampling parameter is set to 75, it means that, for each mode, we are employing only 75 columns of the current matricization instead of using all its
n^{7}=170\,859\,375
columns. R-Sub-HOSVD thus works with 0.00005% of the data without occurring in any loss of accuracy. We also highlight that it looks quite the opposite. In particular, the error achieved by R-Sub-HOSVD remains pretty much constant when increasing the number of modes
d
. This does not happen for HOSVD, ST-HOSVD, R-HOSVD, and SI-HOSVD whose error badly scales with
d
. We conjecture that this different trend is due to handling much smaller objects in our setting. This feature, that is shared also by the rHKron scheme, may alleviate the accumulation of numerical error in finite precision arithmetic. We believe this interesting phenomenon deserves further study that we plan to carry out in the near future.

For the randomized subsampling-based methods, the Sub-R-HOSVD sampling parameter reported here has a different meaning than before. 3.3.3
Parallel experiment

The goal of this section is to assess the parallel scalability of Sub-R-HOSVD.
The parallel implementation of our scheme consists of two main stages.
The first stage computes the orthogonal factor matrices
{\mathbf{{Q}}}_{k}
,
k=1,\dots,d
, in Algorithm
2
in parallel.
The second stage performs the successive TTM operations
(line
8
in Algorithm
2
) to
progressively reduce the tensor and compute the core tensor.
To this end, the input tensor is first partitioned into a number of slices equal to the number
of available processes along a suitable mode
\bar{k}
, chosen to ensure a balanced
workload. Each slice is assigned to a different process.
Each process then performs, in serial, all TTM operations along the modes except
for mode
\bar{k}
, producing a locally reduced tensor
\bar{\mathbfcal{S}}
. The master
process subsequently gathers all reduced tensors and performs the final TTM along
mode
\bar{k}
to obtain the core tensor
\mathbfcal{S}
. Consequently, the only serial
component of this stage is the last TTM executed after the collection of all
\bar{\mathbfcal{S}}
.

We test the parallel implementation of Sub-R-HOSVD on a synthetic low-rank tensor, constructed as in test 1 of section
3.3.1
, with order
d=8
and rank
5
along each mode, and mode dimension varying from
n=16
to
n=20
. For each mode
k=1,\ldots,d
, the subsampling parameter is set to
s\equiv s_{k}=10^{4}
. This means that the percentage of columns of the matricization
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(k)}
we employ varies from
0.004\%
(for
n=16
) to
0.0008\%
(for
n=20
) for each
k=1,\ldots,d
.

In Figure
5a
and Figure
5b

1

8

Notice the logarithmic scale in Figure

We conclude this section with a couple of comments. First, the reasoning behind splitting
[1,n^{d-1}]
into four parts is due to the computational resources we have available. Indeed, if we split this interval into

The second comment is about the selection of the integers for the fiber sampling. It is true that randomly picking

To the best of our knowledge, no contribution available in the current literature has ever proposed the use of RSVD within either the RtL-HT or the LtR-HT algorithm. While its implementation is trivial – just replace the SVD in line

Therefore, we do not have any error bound achieved by the subsampled randomized H-Tucker scheme we propose in the next section, as we did for Sub-R-HOSVD; cf., Theorem

4.1
Subsampled randomized RtL-HT

In this section, we present the second most important contribution of this paper. Our goal is to further reduce the complexity of decomposing an order-
d
tensor
\mathbfcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbf{n}}
by the RtL-HT algorithm for a given dimension tree
\mathcal{T}
and hierarchical rank
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{z}
in terms of both flops and storage demand.

Firstly, we assume that
r_{\mathcal{\footnotesize S}}=\text{rank}({\mathbf{{X}}}^{(\mathcal{\footnotesize S})})
for every node
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{T}
and no truncation is performed. We assume to take as input an oversampling parameter,
p\in\mathbb{N}
, and a sampling vector
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{N}^{z}
, such that
w_{k}
corresponds to the number of columns to sample from
{\mathbf{{X}}}^{(\mathcal{\footnotesize S})}
where
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{T}
is the
k

and

\mathcal{S}
and the leaf
\{k\}

\mathbf{w}_{\mathcal{\footnotesize L}}:=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})

In the deterministic RtL-HT the first step is computing the Tucker factors via the T-HOSVD of the input tensor at multilinear rank
\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{\tiny L}}

\mathcal{T}

\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{\tiny L}}

\mathbb{R}^{n_{\neq k}}

\text{Range}({\mathbf{{Y}}}_{k})

Once the leaf factors are computed, we visit all the internal nodes of
\mathcal{T}

4.2
Numerical experiments

In this section, we report the numerical performance of Sub-R-RtL-HT, RtL-HT, and LtR-HT on artificially constructed problems in a serial (section

Similarly to the standard Tucker case, we assess the performance of the method on synthetic tensors while varying the tensor order

4.2.1
Serial tests

The tensor considered in this example is constructed as follows.
After fixing a dimension tree (see, e.g., Figure
7
), we generate a low-rank tensor in the Hierarchical-sense, assuming as mode dimension
n=15
and hierarchical ranks
r=5
across all nodes, and order ranging from
d=4
to
d=8
. We randomly generate
d

matrices of size
(n\times r)

with i.i.d. entries uniformly distributed in
[0,1]
, and factorize them via skinny QR. Each Q factor is associated with a dimension tree leaf. Similarly, the transfer tensors are randomly generated as order-
3
tensors of size
(r\times r\times r)
with i.i.d. entries uniformly distributed in
[0,1]
.
We compare our method with the deterministic RtL-HT and LtR-HT methods.
The results are reported in Figure
8
.
For each tensor order, boxplots summarize the distribution of approximation errors (Figure
8c
) while logarithmic-scale plots report the median performance as a function of
d
in order to highlight the scaling behavior of the methods in terms of running time (Figure
8b
) and accuracy (Figure
8a
).

The results achieved by Sub-R-RtL-HT have been obtained by considering different sampling factors. In particular, the parameter reported in the in the legend of Figure
8
for Sub-R-RtL-HT denotes a scaling coefficient
\alpha
, which controls the number of fibers employed at each node of the dimension tree. More precisely, for every unfolding associated with a given node
\mathcal{S}
, the algorithm selects
\min\{\alpha\cdot n_{\mathcal{S}},\,n_{\mathrel{\scalebox{0.55}{$\notin$}}\mathcal{S}}\}
fibers, where
n_{\mathcal{S}}
is the number of rows of the corresponding unfolding and
n_{\mathrel{\scalebox{0.55}{$\notin$}}\mathcal{S}}
is the total number of available fibers. We stress, once again, that tensor unfoldings are never explicitly formed in the implementation; the description above is provided only for clarity and to give an intuitive interpretation of the sampling rule.

The observed trends are consistent with those obtained in the Tucker setting. Sub-R-RtL-HT achieves comparable approximation accuracy while attaining computational timings significantly smaller than those obtained by its deterministic counterpart, namely RtL-HT. This speedup is mainly due to the subsampling strategy, which avoids the construction of large intermediate objects and allows the algorithm to operate on substantially smaller representations. Despite using only a limited subset of fibers, the approximation error remains low as the tensor order increases. It is interesting to notice how Sub-R-RtL-HT is rather competitive with LtR-HT. The latter method is often the method of choice for computing H-Tucker decompositions. Indeed, thanks to the dimension reductions taking place every time the left singular vectors of the matricization at a given node are available, this method is rather fast. However, its serial nature is clearly not suited for parallelization. On the other hand, as we will show in the next section, Sub-R-RtL-HT is easily parallelizable while achieving satisfactory results also in a serial computing environment.

4.2.2
Parallel tests for Sub-R-RtL-HT

In this section we assess the parallel scalability of Sub-R-RtL-HT.
We test the parallel implementation on low-rank tensors in the hierarchical-sense of fixed order
d=8
and increasing mode dimension from
n=16
to
n=20
, with a fixed hierarchical rank
r=6
in all cases, which is also the truncation rank used in the decomposition.
All tensors are randomly generated in the same way as described in Section
4.2.1
, and at each node of the dimension tree we extract the same number of fibers, equal to
20n
for each tensor.

One of the main advantage of the RtL approach over LtR is the complete independence of the SVD computations associated with the nodes of the dimension tree. This means that these SVDs can all be performed in parallel, at the same time. However, as we will show in this section, it is important to keep in mind that these SVDs do not all have the same cost due to the different dimensions of the objects we handle. A naive distribution of the workflow may result in processes waiting long for others to complete their tasks, thus reducing the overall speed up of the algorithm. Maintaining a balanced workflow among all the available processes is vital, yet challenging, to make the most out of a parallel implementation. The main goal of this section is to show the potential of our randomized approach but, at the same time, to illustrate some of the limitations intrinsic to the H-Tucker decomposition.

For a tensor of order
d=8
and for the dimension tree shown in Figure
7

14

7

{\mathbf{{U}}}_{\mathcal{S}}

\mathbfcal{B}_{i}

We start by testing the scalability of our approach for
2

4

8

In principle, this step only requires the left singular vectors of the root children to be completed, but in the current implementation it is performed after all SVD computations have been completed, resulting in an additional serial section at the end of the parallel phase.
Nevertheless, Figure

To mitigate this effect, we parallelize the fiber extraction step, following the same strategy used for Sub-R-HOSVD.
In this setting, each process performs the extraction using

Although this introduces a mild oversubscription, since the total number of requested workers exceeds the number of available cores, the overall performance improves.
This is confirmed in Figure

To conclude, we would like to comment on some limitations of the parallelization of the RtL-HT algorithm. As we mentioned earlier, maintaining a balanced workflow is key to fully capitalize on the parallel computing architecture at hand.
To show the importance of this aspect we now test the parallel scalability using

We believe that these results show that, even though RtL is amenable for a full parallelization of the algorithm - with 14 processes we were able to compute all the 14 necessary SVDs in parallel – this does not necessarily lead to good performance and a more careful, less wasteful implementation can achieve similar results.

5
Conclusions

A novel mode-parallel implementation of the Tucker decomposition has been proposed. This has been achieved by combining a sampling step along with a range-finding strategy. In particular, the sampling step is crucial to prevent the expensive construction of any matricization of the tensor thus leading to dramatic savings in both the computational costs and memory requirements of our tensor compression scheme. It is thank to this significant reduction in storage allocation that we are actually able to run our scheme in a mode-parallel fashion. Indeed, we no longer need to store
d
copies of the full tensor as required by a naive mode-parallel implementation of the Tucker decomposition. In addition to the sampling step, the range-finder improves the quality of the computed approximation.
We also derived error bounds attained by our novel numerical scheme. Due to the randomized nature of the tools employed in our procedure (sampling and range-finder), these error bounds are in expected value. A panel of different numerical experiments, both in a serial and parallel computing setting, showcases the performance of our novel approach. In particular, our method turns out to be one order of magnitude faster than state-of-the-art randomization-based techniques while always achieving very similar accuracy records. Strong scaling results also illustrate the good scalability of our approach.
We generalized the scheme to the computation of the H-Tucker decomposition for which promising results have been reported.

We believe this work opens to a couple of very interesting research questions. The first one would be to get a better understanding of the phenomenon observed in, e.g., Figure
2a
, namely the error attained by our Sub-R-HOSVD scales better with respect to the number of modes
d
than what happens for the (deterministic) HOSVD. As already mentioned, we believe this is due to the handling of much smaller objects in our setting which may alleviate error accumulation in finite arithmetic. It would be thus interesting to explore a finite precision analysis of our scheme. Secondly, a thorough study of the H-Tucker decomposition equipped with RSVD is still lacking in the literature. The derivation of error bounds for this scheme could be seen as a first step towards getting bounds for our Sub-R-RtL-HT.

Appendix A
Technical results

Here we report a technical result, along with its proof, used to prove Theorem
5
.

Proposition 8
.

Fix two matrices
{\mathbf{{S}}},{\mathbf{{T}}}
and draw a standard Gaussian matrix
{\mathbf{{G}}}
. Then

\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\mathbf{{SGT}}}\|_{F}^{4}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\leq 3^{\frac{1}{4}}\|{\mathbf{{S}}}\|_{F}\|{\mathbf{{T}}}\|_{F}.

(18)

Proof.

By unitary invariance of both the Gaussian matrix and the Frobenius norm, we may assume

{\mathbf{{S}}}=\mathrm{diag}(s_{j})
and
{\mathbf{{T}}}=\mathrm{diag}(t_{k})
. Then

\|{\mathbf{{SGT}}}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j,k}s_{j}^{2}t_{k}^{2}g_{jk}^{2}=:X.

Let
a_{jk}=s_{j}^{2}t_{k}^{2}
. Using
\mathbb{E}[g_{jk}^{4}]=3
and
\mathbb{E}[g_{jk}^{2}g_{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}^{2}]=1
for

(j,k)\neq(j^{\prime},k^{\prime})
,

\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=3\sum_{j,k}{a_{jk}^{2}}+2\sum_{(j,k)<(j^{\prime},k^{\prime})}{a_{jk}a_{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}}=\Big(\sum_{j,k}{a_{jk}}\Big)^{2}+2\sum_{j,k}{a_{jk}^{2}}.

Since
a_{jk}=s_{j}^{2}t_{k}^{2}
,

\sum_{j,k}{a_{jk}}=\|{\mathbf{{S}}}\|_{F}^{2}\|{\mathbf{{T}}}\|_{F}^{2},\qquad\sum_{j,k}{a_{jk}^{2}}=\Big(\sum_{j}{s_{j}^{4}}\Big)\Big(\sum_{k}{t_{k}^{4}}\Big).

Thus

\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\mathbf{{SGT}}}\|_{F}^{4}\right]=\|{\mathbf{{S}}}\|_{F}^{4}\|{\mathbf{{T}}}\|_{F}^{4}+2\Big(\sum_{j}{s_{j}^{4}}\Big)\Big(\sum_{k}{t_{k}^{4}}\Big)\leq 3\|{\mathbf{{S}}}\|_{F}^{4}\|{\mathbf{{T}}}\|_{F}^{4}.
