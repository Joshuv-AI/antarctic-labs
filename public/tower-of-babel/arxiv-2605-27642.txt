Learning to Translate from Soft to Hard LLM Prompts
Pitipat Kongsomjit Suryansh Goyal Jacob Whitehill

Abstract
Soft prompt tuning is a parameter-efficient method for adapting LLMs to specific tasks, but suffers from a lack of interpretability. Building on recent work on interpreting soft prompts 23 , we explore how training a dedicated soft prompt to natural language translation model can yield higher translation quality. In particular, in both quantitative and qualitative comparisons on multiple Datasets of Datasets (DoDs), we demonstrate that our translator produces fluent, accurate verbalizations that outperforms existing training-free methods like InSPEcT. In addition to advancing interpretability, our work suggests a promising downstream application: soft prompts optimized on small, open-source models can be translated into portable text prompts that, when deployed on larger closed-API models, exceed the performance of the original soft prompt and, in some cases, even few-shot learning.



1
Introduction

As an alternative to manual prompt engineering, LLM researchers and practitioners sometimes employ
soft prompt tuning
, whereby a sequence of learnable embeddings is prepended to the input sequence and then fed to an LLM for fine-tuning on some downstream task. Soft prompt tuning matches, and in some cases, surpasses fine-tuning of LLMs while containing significantly fewer parameters
(
16
;
12
)
. Due to their ease of implementation and training, soft prompts are versatile and can be applied to a wide variety of tasks such as text classification
(
4
)
, relation extraction
(
20
)
, and even drug discovery
(
17
)
. Further, because soft prompts do not modify the model weights themselves, there is reduced risk of catastrophic forgetting
(
28
)
.

In contrast to LLM parameters or adapters, soft prompts share the same embedding space as natural language tokens, and it is thus tempting to hope that they might be more interpretable than other LLM fine-tuning methods. Unfortunately,
naive projections of soft prompts into discrete token space are often misleading and yield contradictory results
(
11
)
. Some works have found that soft prompts occupy regions in the embedding space that are distinct from those containing natural language
(
2
)
. Methods designed to optimize soft prompts for interpretability often incur reduced task performance
(
19
)
.

Until recently, it was widely thought that soft prompts, while useful as a light-weight LLM optimization method, were fundamentally not interpretable. However, the InSPEcT technique
23
showed that the same LLM that uses soft prompts for prediction can also be utilized to elicit textual verbalizations of soft prompts. The technique is training-free: it simply relies on the model’s in-context learning ability to continue generating tokens after being fed the soft prompt as input. Such verbalizations, it turns out, often provide a glimpse into how the soft prompt operates. Nonetheless, InSPEcT leaves much to be desired in terms of reliable, clear descriptions of what the learned soft prompt means and what behaviors it induces in the LLM using it.

In this paper, we explore whether there are stronger, more effective methods of interpreting soft prompts. In particular, instead of relying on in-context learning, we train a dedicated translation model, using the same architecture (but different model weights) as the LLM on which the soft prompts were trained. We find that our approach is promising and outperforms InSPEcT.

Contributions
: (1) We train a soft-to-hard prompt translator by constructing a Dataset of Datasets (DoD) from which many (
soft prompt
,
hard prompt
) pairs can be trained; we then show that the resulting translation model can verbalize soft prompts into natural language that is grammatically fluent and has substantial agreement with ground-truth task descriptions. As a baseline, we compare with InSPEcT and observe a clear improvement. (2) Beyond just greater interpretability, the translator’s verbalizations also show utility – they can be successfully used as prompts and given to larger LLMs. On a variety of tasks, verbalized prompts achieved superior performance to soft-prompting and even few-shot learning, indicating that the verbalizations are relevant and useful for the intended task. (3) We discuss how the existence of an accurate translator could open the door for novel LLM prompt tuning workflows whereby a small LLM is used to train a soft prompt, which is then translated to a hard prompt and given to a larger more powerful LLM for inference—potentially achieving superior inference performance than the small LLM.

2
Related Works

A number of works have attempted to interpret soft prompts into natural language via obtaining their nearest discrete token projections. However,
11
showed that these naive projections of soft prompts into discrete token space are often misleading, completely uninformative, and in some cases contradictory to the underlying task through a phenomenon they termed
prompt waywardness
. Other works support this finding, showing that soft prompt embeddings occupy regions in the embedding space that are distinct from those containing natural language
(
2
)
. A number of methods designed to optimize soft prompts for interpretability have found a trade-off between task performance and interpretability
(
19
)
,
(
5
)
.

Closer to the idea of translating a soft prompt into natural language,
23
recently proposed InSPEcT, a training-free method of eliciting textual descriptions of soft prompts. InSPEcT works by utilizing the Patchscopes
7
framework to patch soft prompt activations into a target prompt designed to verbalize descriptions of the task. While InSPEcT can successfully verbalize correct class labels for a given task, we found that verbalizations are often unreliable, sensitive to the specific source and target layers used for patching, and often contain unnatural language. Their experiments were also quite limited and assessed verbalization on only a handful of datasets.

To date, there is no method which can reliably, fluently, and robustly (without any performance-interpretability trade-off) translate soft prompts into natural language. We thus explore whether a more powerful translation model can help.

3
Translating from Soft to Hard Prompts

We explore a simple workflow (see Figure
1
) for translating soft prompts to natural language whereby a dedicated translation model is trained with supervised learning on a dataset of (
soft prompt, hard prompt
) pairs. Here, each soft prompt was itself optimized for a specific task – e.g., sentiment classification – on a fixed LLM (typically small and open-source, e.g., Llama-8B) using a dataset of (
input
,
output
) pairs.
The trained translation model, given some soft prompt trained on the same LLM, can produce a corresponding “hard prompt”, i.e., a natural language translation of the soft prompt. For a sentiment classification soft prompt, the hard prompt might be,
‘‘Examine the input sentence and classify it as positive or negative. A sentence is positive if...’’

Optionally, the generated hard prompt can then be fed to another LLM (typically larger and with a closed API, e.g., GPT-5.5). Given a high-quality verbalization and an arbitrary input example, the larger model possibly delivers higher task accuracy compared to the small LLM on which the soft prompt itself was trained.

4
Experiments

We conduct experiments to investigate the following research questions:
(1) Can a dedicated translation model produce more accurate soft prompt translations as compared to just in-context learning methods (such as InSPEcT)?
(2) What aspects of the translation model’s training set (size, composition, etc.) are important?
(3) Are the verbalizations simply plausible-sounding prompts, or are they actually relevant to the task and useful for inference?

4.1
Translator: Architecture & Training Strategy

To train the translator model, we model the task of soft prompt translation as a supervised, sequence-to-sequence language generation task, minimizing cross-entropy of next-token prediction. Conditioned on a sequence of input soft tokens, the translator is tasked with autoregressively generating its translation in natural language. This follows standard next-token decoding logic, where decoding ends upon seeing an end-of-sequence token. Since the LLM for which the soft prompt was trained clearly has the ability to decode and use it for inference, we decided to
construct our translator by fine-tuning the same pre-trained model. Specifically, we used
meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
.

For training data, we need a labeled dataset of (
soft prompt, hard prompt
) pairs, where the “hard prompt” corresponds to a ground-truth textual description of the task for which the soft prompt was trained. An example of (
soft prompt, hard prompt
) might be (
(0.12,-1.94,\ldots)
,
"Classify the news article as: Sports, Business, World, or Culture"
), where the array of numbers is the real-valued vector representing a soft prompt. Each unique soft prompt in the translator’s training set also needs to be trained on its own dataset of paired (
input, output
) sequences for some unique downstream task. For example, for a news classification task, the dataset might contain pairs:
("Oil and Economy Cloud...", "Business")
,
(Indians Beat Twins 7-1...", "Sports")
,
("Democratic Senator Urges Energy Reform...", "World")
, etc.

To train a translation model, we thus need a dataset of datasets (DoD). Prior work on deciphering soft prompts (e.g.,
23
) evaluated on just 5 datasets and is thus insufficient. Instead, we construct two different DoDs: one synthetic dataset for classification tasks, and another based on the Super-NaturalInstructions dataset
29
for natural language tasks such as Text Summarization, Question Generation, Machine Translation, etc.

Generalized (SuperNatural) DoD
: Next, we experimented with more general natural language tasks. We used the Super-NaturalInstructions dataset, which was originally compiled to train and benchmark instruction-following and reasoning ability of LLMs. It contains a very diverse set of 1616 tasks along with labeled datasets for each; it covers 76 distinct task types and 55 different languages (though we used only English-language data in our experiments). It thus seemed a useful basis on which to construct a DoD. We preprocessed the original dataset to create the General DoD containing 539 training and 96 validation tasks, each consisting of 500 (
input, output

mistralai/Mistral-Small-3.1-24B-Instruct-2503

4.3
Training Details

Soft Prompt Training
: For each of the two DoDs, we first trained soft prompts: For the Classification DoD, we used an 80/20 train/validation split per dataset and trained a soft prompt consisting of 20 virtual tokens using a learning rate of 1e-4 for 20 epochs (patience of 5 epochs based on the validation set loss), batch size of 32, and the AdamW optimizer. For the General DoD, we used a 90/10 split per dataset, learning rate of 1e-3, 20 epochs (patience of 3 epochs), and batch size of 8.

The trained soft prompts for Classification DoD achieved 73.73% average validation task accuracy (i.e., correct classification rate), while soft prompts for General DoD achieved average validation task ROUGE-L of ~0.17. We note that, although the low ROUGE-L score suggests weak soft prompt performance, the General DoD tasks are often very challenging. Moreover, it is possible that, while the LLM is unable to reliably generate the correct response due to its modest size (8B parameters), the soft prompt itself might nonetheless encode the correct task that the LLM is supposed to be doing. In fact, we show evidence supporting this in Section
5.2
and Appendix
A.9
. More details on soft prompt training can be found in Appendix
A.4
.

Translator Training
:
We train two translator models in total (one for Classification DoD, and one for General DoD). For each, we used a 90/10 train/test split on the (
soft prompt
,
hard prompt
) pairs in each DoD. We used LoRA to fine-tune the base LLM (
meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
) and the following hyperparameters: learning rate of 1e-4, 5 epochs, batch size of 16, LoRA rank as 4, LoRA dropout as 0.1, with AdamW optimizer with weight decay of 0.1. More details are in Appendix
A.5
.

For the Classification DoD, we define the target hard prompt for each dataset as a string consisting of a comma separated list of the 5 class labels, e.g.,
"nurse, physician, surgeon, pharmacist, radiologist"
. For the General DoD, target hard prompts were already provided by the Super-NaturalInstructions dataset itself.

4.4
Evaluating Soft-to-Hard Prompt Verbalization Quality

We evaluate both the translator and the InSPEcT method on multiple datasets of datasets: Classification DoD, General DoD, as well as the set of 5 datasets used in
23
, namely: SST-2
26
, SST-5
26
, AG-News
32
, Subj
18
, and TREC
27
; for the remainder of this paper, they will be referred to as InSPEcT datasets.

Metrics
:
For classification tasks (Classification DoD, and the 5 InSPEcT datasets), we compute the Recall of the ground-truth label set (this was called Class Rate in
23
). We also compute the F1-Score (harmonic mean of Recall and Precision rates) to account for “runaway generations”, as it penalizes the presence of irrelevant tokens. For general tasks (General DoD), we use ROUGE-L and cosine similarity of embedded verbalized soft prompts (computed using the sentence transformer
all-MiniLM-L6-v2
) relative to ground-truth hard prompts.

As a baseline, we also compare our translators against InSPEcT (see
5.1
). We note that while InSPEcT is training-free, it has an important hyperparameter: namely the pairs of layers used by the Patchscopes framework
7
as part of InSPEcT. We found that InSPEcT is highly sensitive to this hyperparameter, and we thus optimized it on the training set of each DoD for all comparisons. See Appendix
A.3
for details.

4.5
Evaluating Relevance and Utility of Verbalizations

Beyond measuring the similarity of the soft prompt’s verbalizations to ground-truth hard prompts (Section
4.4
), we also evaluated the task performance of verbalizations when used as a prompt by a larger, downstream LLM on new inputs. Specifically, we used the following methodology: (1) Train a soft prompt using a small, open-source LLM for a certain task; (2) Use the translator to verbalize the soft prompt into a hard prompt; (3) pass the verbalized hard prompt, along with an input example, to a large, closed-source LLM for inference on the same task (see Figure
1
for an illustration). If, using the verbalization as a prompt, the LLM performs at least as well as the original soft prompt, then that suggests the verbalizations are relevant to the task and useful for inference. It would also show that the verbalizations are portable hard prompts.

We apply this procedure to tasks within the testing set of the General DoD: Using the associated translator model, we verbalize a predicted hard prompt and pass it to a closed-source LLM (GPT-4o-mini) for inference using the prompt template:

{Verbalized prompt}
Input:
{input}

Output:

Control
:
To verify whether any observed improvement in task performance is due to the translated soft prompt being useful for the task, rather than just due to the larger LLM being inherently more proficient, we added a baseline condition that omits the verbalized prompt itself.

5.2
Relevance and Utility of Verbalizations

To assess whether verbalized soft prompts are useful for the task or merely plausible sounding prompts, we experiment with passing the verbalizations to a larger LLM for inference (as described in Section
4.5
) on a set of input examples. We compare this to (1) giving the large LLM the same inputs
without
the verbalization (“baseline”) as well as (2) giving the small LLM (SLM) the soft prompts themselves and with the input sentences.

Results (Figure
3

Comparison to Few-Shot Learning
:
Can translating soft prompts trained on a smaller model into human-readable prompts outperform in-context few-shot learning, whereby the LLM implicitly infers a pattern from the given examples to make a prediction? We investigate this by applying few-shot learning on the same General DoD tasks using the same average task ROUGE-L metric.

While for most of the tasks the few-shot learning approach was more successful in terms of task accuracy (ROUGE-L), we did find
a set of 10 tasks (out of 96) where the prompt that was discovered through soft prompt translation outperformed few-shot learning. Many of these tasks were often more obscure and difficult to infer just by looking at a few examples alone. We provide a qualitative example in Fig.

We speculate that these 10 tasks represents a class of problems where there are many hypotheses that could explain the input-output relationship between a sentence and its label, thus a large number of examples is needed to identify the true hypothesis. For soft prompt training, this is no problem (since it can be trained on the entire train set), but this is very inefficient for few-shot learning.

6
Discussion

Over all our experiments, we found that
the dedicated translation models consistently outperformed the training-free InSPEcT technique on the InSPEcT datasets as well as the Classification and General DoDs in terms of quantitative verbalization quality. The translator also showed significant ability to generalize out-of-distribution. Qualitatively, the translator’s verbalizations were mixed: some completely captured the goal of the task, whereas others missed the mark (often by a wide margin).

The performance of the translator model, especially its generalization capability, is surprising given the small training set size (539 samples). We speculate a possible explanation: the Rosetta Stone contained only a few thousand words in equivalent English translations, yet it was sufficient to establish a correspondence between ancient Greek and ancient Egyptian. Suppose that soft prompts constitute a language in their own right. It is possible that LLMs already innately understand this language, and obviously they can understand and express natural language. The main difficulty in translating from soft to hard prompts is thus in finding a
correspondence
between these two languages, and perhaps a small dataset of (
soft prompt
,
hard prompt
) pairs is sufficient. This contrasts with, say, the LLM being fine-tuned to translate between two entirely new languages it was never trained on.

Finally, the workflow we tested (far right side of Figure
1
), whereby verbalized soft prompts can be passed to a large, more powerful LLM for inference, showed promise. It has an important advantage over in-context few-shot learning that the soft prompt can be trained on a large dataset, allowing it to infer more nuanced patterns, whereas few-shot learning is generally limited to a small number of inputs due to token limits.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

Soft prompts are opaque and difficult to interpret. We show that by training a dedicated soft prompt translator model, it is possible to extract a human readable, natural language translation of these soft prompts that is more accurate than the prior training-free InSPEcT approach. Verbalizations obtained from the translator are useful and relevant to the task at hand, which also potentially opens up a new workflow for obtaining portable prompts that can be applied onto closed source models.

With a more powerful soft prompt translator trained on a larger dataset, it may be possible to train soft prompts on tasks with the specific goal of later translating them to learn new knowledge. It would be exciting if, after training a soft prompt for a complex task—like drug discovery
17
—insights into the mechanism of how the LLM makes its predictions (e.g., the molecular interactions) could be expressed in natural language.

8
Limitations

We did not explore in this work whether our soft prompt translator model can work with soft prompts trained on a different base model, though given that soft prompts are not normally transferrable between models, it is safe to assume this is not the case. This means unless the soft prompt is already trained using the same base model as ours (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), a new translator model would need to be trained.

While testing the soft prompt translation model out of distribution on real-world tasks, we noticed that while verbalizations were truthful and relevant to the task, it was often the case that they were also terse and lacked relevant details. We believe this is an issue with stylistic alignment—where the translator is erroneously choosing to omit relevant task details to produce more fluent verbalizations. See Appendix
A.12
for further details.

As is, soft prompt translation is a promising proof of concept. In order for it to become a practical tool, however, a larger dataset is needed along with dedicated stylistic alignment (i.e.: preference optimization or Supervised-Fine Tuning on real world datasets) to ensure verbose, informative verbalizations.

Since the original Super-NaturalInstructions Dataset is in a GitHub repository (

We performed some data analysis and decided to further filter the dataset for training soft prompts and translators on it. Based on our data analysis, we performed the following preprocessing:

1.

Filter out any instance sequences (input + output) longer than 400 tokens, as we found that there were certain instances where the sequence length was over 200k tokens, with P99 of 1312 and P90 of 337. Filter out any tasks with less than 500 instances, as we found that some tasks had a minimum of 29 instances only, which would not be sufficient for training soft prompts. Hence, we decided to use 500 instances as a minimum threshold for selecting tasks for future experiments.

Added a column for total tokens of input + output sequences, calculated using tokenizer of
meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct

Created instances of (input, output[

A.9
Correlation between Interpretability and Task Accuracy

While testing Classification DoD translator on the testing split of Classification DoD, we observed an interesting phenomenon: the accuracy of the soft prompts does not need to be high in order to get good verbalizations out of them. For example, most verbalizations of the soft prompts from the testing split of Classification DoD are commendable in terms of Recall and F1-score, despite having low task performance (< 50%). This is contrary to the idea proposed in the InSPEcT paper, where they claim a correlation between quality of verbalizations and the task accuracy of the soft prompts. Some verbalizations for soft prompts with low task accuracy can be found in table
10
.

We hypothesize that while the soft prompt applied on the LLM may achieve poor performance, this might not just be because they encode irrelevant task instructions; another valid possibility is that the soft prompt does encode relevant task instructions, but rather our small base model of only 8 billion parameters may be failing to execute them.

This hypothesis is consistent with our finding in the General DoD: we showed that extracting the instructions encoded within the soft prompt using the translator and passing these instructions to a larger, more capable LLM results in better performance—indicating that the soft prompts encode relevant task instructions but the smaller base model partially failed to execute them.
Taking these soft prompts and verbalizing them with the translator, we can pass their encoded instructions to a larger, more intelligent LLM for inference on the same task. If the soft prompts do encode correct instructions for a given task, we could then expect that a more intelligent LLM would be able to carry it out. Indeed, this was shown to be the case—in one instance (See: Fig.
4
), while the original soft prompt had a task ROUGE-L of only 0.012, verbalizing the soft prompt and passing the instructions to an LLM achieves a task ROUGE-L of 0.8, surpassing even the performance of few-shot learning on the same LLM which achieved a task ROUGE-L of 0.4.

A.10
Task Differentiability Analysis

We also evaluate verbalizations of the General DoD Translator using the Mean Percentile Rank (MPR) of cosine similarities.
Although ROUGE-L and cosine similarity capture absolute textual and semantic alignment, MPR provides a relative measure of task discriminability. To calculate MPR, each predicted verbalization’s cosine similarity is scored against all ground-truth prompts’ similarity (including its true paired hard prompt and all other unrelated hard prompts) in the evaluation set. We then compute its percentile rank and average this across all predicted hard prompts, which evaluates how well the translator distinguishes unique tasks based on verbalized soft prompts alone.

A.12
Stylistic Alignment

A soft prompt is a high dimensional object holding many details, some of which are trivial like expected formatting. In order to produce natural-language-like verbalizations, the translator must decide which details need to be omitted. In practice, we found that there were many cases where verbalizations were too concise than ideal (for instance in figure
2
). This is where style alignment can be applied to ensure that important details are not omitted.

As a rudimentary method of encouraging a more verbose generation style, we experiment with prefilling text. (i.e.: start autoregressive generation pre-conditioned on [soft tokens; prefill text]). Often, after applying a prefill text, we see additional factual details in verbalizations. We show an example of one such case below in Fig
5
.

Figure 5:
Example showing the effect of prefill conditioning ("First, I should") on verbalizations, in particular In this case a regression task involving quadratics

In this anecdotal example, the translator model omitted relevant task details in its original verbalization, which is only revealed once prefill is applied. What this suggests is that, in such instances, the translator correctly comprehends the semantics of the soft prompt, but incorrectly omitted crucial details as a stylistic choice. This is a clear example of a stylistic misalignment which dedicated preference optimization could potentially address.

To verify that this is not anecdotal, we also investigated the effects of prefill conditioning more rigorously on 550 classification datasets from Classification DoD. Across 550 classification datasets, the average class rate of verbalizations doubled after applying the prefill.

This was found by applying our general translator model out of distribution onto the Classification DoD. Without prefill conditioning, the general translator model had an average class rate of 1.16% across 550 classification tasks, which is quite poor. We then repeated the same experiment, applying a simple prefill ("Classify the given input into one of the following:"), in which our class rate went up to 2.62%. This is consistent with our speculation that the translator may omit certain details due to stylistic misalignment, suggesting potentially significant improvements through preference optimization.
