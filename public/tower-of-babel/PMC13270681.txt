The impact of telemedicine on the efficiency and accuracy of emergency department triage: a staff perspective
Authors: Muhammad Nauman Qureshi, Hamad Aldukhayl, Abdullah Alnatheer, Irteza Neyaz Ahmad, Amjad Alshehry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the field of healthcare, telemedicine has emerged as a crucial tool for enhancing the effectiveness and precision of emergency department (ED) triage. Tele-triage is a term used to describe a technology in which a patient’s health record is evaluated over the phone or via another means of communication and treatments or suitable referrals in the EDs are suggested. We assessed emergency department healthcare workers’ familiarity, perceptions, and attitudes toward the use of telemedicine for triage.MethodsThis cross-sectional study assessed 131 healthcare workers in the ED in one of the largest hospitals in Riyadh. The questionnaire included various measures to evaluate the perception of ED staff about the impact of telemedicine in the ED.ResultsPhysicians accounted for the highest proportion of the survey respondents (62.6%, n = 82), followed by nurses (32.8%, n = 43) and then paramedics (4.6%, n = 6). Physicians demonstrated greater familiarity with telemedicine (70.7%) than nonphysicians (38.8%) (p < 0.001). Majority of the participants agreed that telemedicine could be used as a tool for initial assessment. The most frequently used technology was AI-based triage systems, as perceived by ED staff (43.1%, n = 56). The challenges faced in telemedicine implementation for triage included resistance by staff (30.5%, n = 40), privacy concerns (11.5%, n = 15), and lack of training (8.4%, n = 11).ConclusionIn summary, the findings indicate an overall positive perception of ED staff towards using telemedicine in triage within ED, with notable concerns regarding staff resistance, privacy, and training. Despite these challenges, nearly half of the respondents expected an increase in its use, highlighting the need for specific training, alongside technological advancements and equity to enable its widespread adoption.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Emergency, Triage, Telemedicine, Telephone, Remote

Introduction

In recent years, the integration of telemedicine into emergency care has attracted considerable attention owing to its potential impact on triage effectiveness and accuracy. Telemedicine and telehealth are used interchangeably; however, they differ in meaning [1]. The Health Resources and Services Administration defines telehealth as a subset of e-health and refers to the use of telecommunications and technology in healthcare delivery, information, and education. Telemedicine is under the umbrella of telehealth but specifically refers to clinical services [1]. Meanwhile, the American Academy of Family Physicians defines telemedicine as the use of telecommunications technologies by physicians or other medical practitioners to deliver care. In contrast, telehealth encompasses a wider variety of remote healthcare services beyond clinical services, such as provider training, continuing medical education, public health education, and administrative meetings [2].

With the constant development and advancement of technology, telemedicine has become increasingly popular and more accessible. Consequently, it is being incorporated into several specialties, such as family medicine, ophthalmology, dermatology, and emergency medicine. Although the original concept of telehealth was to provide basic care to rural and underserved patients, it is currently being adopted and implemented as an efficient and cost-effective means to deliver and access quality healthcare services and outcomes for various patient groups. This study aimed to examine ED healthcare personnel’s perceptions, familiarity, and perceived advantages and challenges of using telemedicine for emergency department triage.

Across the different studies and reviews, only patient outcomes and patient satisfaction remained constant. Patients who had undergone remote triage reported outcomes consistent with those who had undergone onsite triage [3, 4]; furthermore, patient safety was comparable between the two groups [5]. In addition, patients who have undergone triage remotely expressed high levels of satisfaction with the service provided [5, 6]. One study reported reduced frequency of over-triage and unnecessary referrals [7]. Some studies observed cost reduction as another positive factor [8]. Meanwhile, the levels of ED visits and hospital admissions varied between the two groups from one study to the other. While some studies reported reduced patient visits/admissions in the remotely triaged group [9, 10], others did not find any remarkable difference [11], with some even recording an increase [7, 12]. Length of stay is another factor that differed among the studies. Moreover, concerns were raised regarding the difficulty of remotely conducting comprehensive physical exams [3]. Each study came with its own set of limitations. Generally, a considerable variation was observed in the analyzed variables, sample size, study design, and triage algorithms among the studies, and data on some of the outcomes were not reported by all the included studies [4, 11, 12].

Materials and methods

A survey was conducted in the King Faisal Specialist Hospital Emergency Department to assess the knowledge and perception of paramedics, nurses, and physicians regarding the use of remote triage in the ED. A survey of 10 questions aimed to assess their understanding, perception, if it is currently being used for triage, and how it can be improved in the future. The questionnaire was adapted from previously published telemedicine assessment surveys and reviewed by three emergency medicine experts to ensure content relevance. A pilot test was conducted with 10 staff to refine clarity. Although the instrument demonstrated face validity, no formal psychometric validation was performed.

Statistical considerations

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.0. Texas, USA Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the data. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the chi-squared test to evaluate the associations between categorical responses.

To investigate the association between the survey items, a pairwise Pearson correlation analysis was conducted. This method was selected to evaluate the strength and direction of linear associations between variables measured on Likert-type scales, which are commonly treated as interval-level data. The pairwise approach enabled the inclusion of all available data for each variable pair, and p-values were reported to determine statistical significance.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Center (KFSHRC). The study protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and KFSHRC guidelines.

Results

A total of 131 health professionals participated in the survey, of which the majority were physicians (62.6%, n = 82), followed by nurses (32.8%, n = 43) and then paramedics (4.6%, n = 6) (Table 1).

Overall cohort

As regards familiarity with telemedicine in ED triage, the participants had a wide range of opinions: 45.8% (n = 60) were somewhat familiar, 13.0% (n = 17) were very familiar, 24.4% (n = 32) showed neutrality, 15.3% (n = 20) were not very familiar, and 1.5% (n = 2) were not familiar at all (Fig. 1).

Profession distribution by familiarity with telemedicine in the emergency department

The most frequently used technology was AI-based triage systems (43.1%, n = 56), followed by remote monitoring devices (36.9%, n = 48) and then mobile apps (17.7%, n = 23). Video consultation was the least used (2.3%, n = 3).

As regards the perception that telemedicine in triage is cost-effective and reduces ED visits, 38.9% (n = 51) agreed, 13.0% (n = 17) strongly agreed, 20.6% (n = 27) were neutral, 16.0% (n = 21) disagreed, and 11.5% (n = 15) strongly disagreed.

As regards the perception that telemedicine improves patient satisfaction, 49.6% (n = 65) agreed, 9.2% (n = 12) strongly agreed, 32.1% (n = 42) were neutral, and 9.2% (n = 12) disagreed. None of the participants strongly disagreed.

As regards the perception that telemedicine is time-efficient for both patients and doctors, 48.1% (n = 63) agreed, 26.7% (n = 35) strongly agreed, 19.1% (n = 25) were neutral, 5.3% (n = 7) disagreed, and 0.8% (n = 1) strongly disagreed.

As regards the perception that telemedicine could be used as a tool for initial assessment, 59.5% (n = 78) agreed, 9.2% (n = 12) disagreed, and 31.3% (n = 41) were neutral.

The frequently mentioned barriers to the implementation of telemedicine for triage were resistance by staff (30.5%, n = 40), privacy concerns (11.5%, n = 15), lack of training (8.4%, n = 11), both resistance and privacy concerns (17.6%, n = 23), both resistance and lack of training (16.8%, n = 22), and all three barriers (6.9%, n = 9). Another barrier was the inability to conduct direct visual assessments (0.8%, n = 1).

When asked regarding the expected increase in the utilization of telemedicine in EDs, 48.1% (n = 63) answered yes, 24.4% (n = 32) were unsure, and 27.5% (n = 36) did not foresee an increase.

Of the 63 respondents who anticipated an increase, the suggested areas for improvement were enhanced staff training (50.0%, n = 31), improved technology (32.3%, n = 20), and better connectivity (17.7%, n = 11)

Chi-squared analysis was conducted to compare the responses among the groups.

Physicians demonstrated greater familiarity with telemedicine (70.7%) than nonphysicians (38.8%) (p < 0.001).

Agreement on the economic benefits of telemedicine was not markedly different across the groups (p = 0.513). Patient satisfaction, time-saving, and feasibility for the initial assessment were perceived differently but with no significant statistical differences. The main challenges in the implementation were consistent for all professional groups (p > 0.05) (Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the ED staff perception as to how telemedicine impacts the efficiency and accuracy of ED triage in comparison with traditional onsite triage. The key takeaway is that healthcare professionals generally view telemedicine as a beneficial adjunct for emergency triage, particularly in enhancing time efficiency and patient satisfaction.

Nearly 75% of the respondents agreed that telemedicine triage saves time, and 59.5% agreed that it can be effectively used for initial assessments. Although only 51.9% supported its contribution to the reduction of unnecessary visits, these results suggest strong support for the utility of telemedicine in the operational aspects of emergency care.

These results are consistent with those of the existing literature demonstrating that telemedicine enhances workflow and reduces patient wait times in acute care settings [6, 13]. Consistent with our study particpants perception, previous studies found high levels of patient satisfaction with tele-triage systems, particularly when standardized protocols were applied [3, 4].

However, our study cohort indicated that the belief in the ability of telemedicine to lower cost and reduce ED visits is not uniformly held, aligning with mixed outcomes from recent reviews [9, 11, 12]. These insights confirm the importance of the implementation context and the variability of outcomes in tele-triage studies.

An unexpected result was the stark difference in perception between partcipating physicians and non-physicians. Although physicians were significantly more familiar with telemedicine (70.7%), nonphysicians exhibited a stronger association between familiarity and belief in the diagnostic value (r = 0.5153).

This contrast may reflect varying degrees of reliance on structured protocols compared with direct clinical assessment, revealing a potentially overlooked dimension in user training. Moreover, the minimal use of video consultations (2.3%) was surprising given their availability, suggesting usability or workflow concerns in high-pressure environments.

This study has significant limitations. First, the data relies on self-reported perceptions rather than objective clinical metrics, such as diagnostic accuracy and treatment time. Second, the survey was conducted at a single center and among a physician-dominant sample (62.6%), potentially limiting its generalizability.

Third, no multivariable analysis was conducted to adjust for confounders, such as clinical experience or institutional familiarity with digital tools. In addition, the absence of patient-level outcome data limits our ability to evaluate the true clinical impact of tele-triage.

Future research should prioritize prospective or randomized studies comparing tele-triage with onsite triage in category 3–5 emergency cases using objective clinical outcomes, such as time to provider, diagnostic accuracy, and admission rates.

Studies investigating hybrid models—integrating AI-assisted triage with clinician oversight—may facilitate the identification of scalable and safe systems. Moreover, structured training programs and usability evaluations are warranted to enhance their adoption across diverse clinical roles.

In summary,the evidence from ED staff perceptions in our study indicates telemedicine can enhance emergency triage, particularly by enhancing efficiency and patient satisfaction. While its impact on cost savings and diagnostic accuracy remains unclear, the positive perception among healthcare providers indicates readiness for broader integration.

Future success depends on targeted investments in training, infrastructure, and clinical outcome validation. These findings support telemedicine not as a replacement but as a complementary strategy to enhance triage efficiency and resource utilization in EDs.

Conclusion

In conclusion, participants expressed generally positive perceptions regarding the potential role of telemedicine in ED triage; however, these perspectives do not provide direct evidence of clinical effectiveness. Future controlled studies are required to objectively evaluate triage accuracy, efficiency, and patient outcomes.The findings of our study indicate an overall positive perception toward telemedicine in triage within EDs, with notable concerns regarding staff resistance, privacy, and training. Despite these challenges, nearly half of the respondents anticipated an increase in its use, highlighting the need for specific training, along with technological advancements and equity to enable widespread adoption. However, further research is warranted to evaluate its effectiveness in high-acuity emergency settings and to address the technological and logistical challenges associated with its implementation.
