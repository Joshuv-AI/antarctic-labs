Light Deflection due to Spinoptic Effects in Parametrized and Spherically Symmetric Hairy Black Holes
Kelvin S. Alves ID, Rogerio T. Cavalcanti ID, Santiago E. Perez Bergliaffa ID

Abstract
In the standard geometric optics approximation, null rays propagating in a spherically symmetric black hole background follow planar geodesics. This picture changes, however, when the helicity-dependent effects of light are incorporated into the dynamics. Specifically, the interaction between the helicity of light and the spacetime curvature induces a significant angular deflection out of the geodesic plane. In this paper, we employ the spinoptics formalism to study light deflection due to the helicity-curvature interaction in two spherically symmetric geometries: the Rezzolla–Zhidenko (RZ) parametrized metric, and a hairy regular black hole solution obtained via gravitational decoupling. Our results reveal clear imprints of both the RZ parametrization coefficients and the hairy black hole parameter on the deflection angle. Furthermore, we assess the viability of using the RZ parametrization to mimic the regular hairy black hole, discussing the validity and limitations of such an approximation.

Introduction

One of the most effective ways to obtain information about astrophysical objects is through the detection of electromagnetic radiation emitted by them or by their surroundings
2
. This type of observation, fundamental to the development of modern astrophysics, has also gained relevance in the investigation of black hole physics. An example is the construction of images of the black holes
M87^{\star}
and Sagittarius
A^{\star}
, as reported by the
Event Horizon Telescope
collaboration
3
;
4

The electromagnetic waves travel long distances before reaching detectors. Hence, studying their propagation in curved spacetime proves to be essential not only from a theoretical standpoint but also for the interpretation and analysis of observational data.

From the perspective of the geometric optics approximation, the propagation of electromagnetic waves in curved spacetimes is well- understood. This approach aims to find high-frequency asymptotic solutions to Maxwell’s equations

and is parallely-transported along the geodesic;

3.

Let
a
be the scalar amplitude. The conservation equation
\nabla_{\mu}(a\ell^{\mu})=0
ensures that the number of photons in a beam remains constant along the propagation.

The geometric optics approximation, however, does not capture the gravitational spin-Hall effect, which stems from the helicity–curvature interaction: depending on helicity, light rays
follows slightly different paths

Whether through electromagnetic radiation or gravitational waves
1
, modern astrophysics has enabled tests of fundamental aspects of General Relativity and its extensions, reaching previously inaccessible regimes

22

additional parameter of the hairy black hole.

After establishing the effect of interest, namely, spinoptics effects on light-ray propagation, and specifying the spacetime metric modelled by the RZ parametrization and the hairy regular GD black hole, we investigate how the additional parameters of these black holes influence light-ray trajectories when the interaction between spacetime curvature and photon helicity is taken into account. II
Regular Black Hole from Gravitational Decoupling

This section briefly reproduces the derivation of a regular hairy black hole solution
51
, as obtained through the gravitational decoupling (GD) method
52
. We begin with the Einstein field equations

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}=-k^{2}\tilde{T}_{\mu\nu},

(1)

where
G_{\mu\nu}=R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}Rg_{\mu\nu}
is the Einstein’s tensor and
\tilde{T}_{\mu\nu}
denotes a generic energy-momentum tensor. The starting point is to assume that
\tilde{T}_{\mu\nu}
can be decomposed as the sum of two distinct energy-momentum tensor contributions, namely

\displaystyle\tilde{T}_{\mu\nu}=T_{\mu\nu}+\Theta_{\mu\nu}.

(2)

In this context,
T_{\mu\nu}
denotes an energy–momentum tensor associated with a solution of the Einstein field equations. The tensor
\Theta_{\mu\nu}
, on the other hand, is not determined a priori. It can originate from fundamental or effective fields, or from a gravitational sector beyond General Relativity. Given that the Einstein tensor satisfies the Bianchi identity, the total energy–momentum tensor must fulfill the conservation equation
\nabla_{\mu}\tilde{T}^{\mu\nu}=0
.

Assuming a spherically symmetric spacetime, the metric in spherical polar coordinates is given by

\displaystyle{\rm d}s^{2}=-e^{\nu(r)}{\rm d}t^{2}+e^{\lambda(r)}{\rm d}r^{2}+r^{2}{\rm d}\theta^{2}+r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta{\rm d}\phi^{2}.

(3)

The substitution of the metric (
3
) into Einstein’s equations yields

\displaystyle k^{2}\left(T_{0}^{\phantom{i}0}+\Theta_{0}^{\phantom{i}0}\right)=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}-\left(\lambda^{\prime}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{r},

\displaystyle k^{2}\left(T_{1}^{\phantom{i}1}+\Theta_{1}^{\phantom{i}1}\right)=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}+\left(\nu^{\prime}+\frac{1}{r}\right)\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{r},

\displaystyle k^{2}\left(T_{2}^{\phantom{i}2}+\Theta_{2}^{\phantom{i}2}\right)=\left[2r\nu^{\prime\prime}+r\left(\nu^{\prime}\right)^{2}-\left(r\lambda^{\prime}-2\right)\nu^{\prime}-2\lambda^{\prime}\right]\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{4r},

(4)

where the prime
(^{\prime})
denotes differentiation with respect to
r
, and the components satisfy
T_{2}^{\phantom{i}2}=T_{3}^{\phantom{i}3}
due to spherical symmetry. Assuming a perfect fluid model for
T_{\mu\nu}
and
\Theta_{\mu\nu}
, the effective density, radial pressure, and tangential pressure can be identified, respectively, as

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}\equiv T_{0}^{\phantom{i}0}+\Theta_{0}^{\phantom{i}0}

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{r}\equiv-T_{1}^{\phantom{i}1}-\Theta_{1}^{\phantom{i}1}

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{t}\equiv-T_{2}^{\phantom{i}2}-\Theta_{2}^{\phantom{i}2}.

(5)

In general,
\Pi\equiv\tilde{p}_{r}-\tilde{p}t\neq 0
, implying that the GD method is capable of generating anisotropic fluid solutions, where the anisotropy originates from the presence of the source
\Theta_{\mu\nu}
.

Now, assuming a vanishing additional source,
\Theta_{\mu\nu}=0
, and that the solution of Einstein’s equations for the energy-momentum tensor
T_{\mu\nu}
, referred to as the seed solution, is given by

\displaystyle{\rm d}s^{2}_{\rm seed}=-e^{\xi(r)}{\rm d}t^{2}+e^{\mu(r)}{\rm d}r^{2}+r^{2}{\rm d}\theta^{2}+r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta{\rm d}\phi^{2},

(6)

the Einstein equations become

\displaystyle k^{2}T_{0}^{\phantom{i}0}=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}-\left(\mu^{\prime}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\frac{e^{-\mu}}{r},

\displaystyle k^{2}T_{1}^{\phantom{i}1}=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}+\left(\xi^{\prime}+\frac{1}{r}\right)\frac{e^{-\mu}}{r},

\displaystyle k^{2}T_{2}^{\phantom{i}2}=\left[2r\xi^{\prime\prime}+r\left(\xi^{\prime}\right)^{2}-\left(r\mu^{\prime}-2\right)\xi^{\prime}-2\mu^{\prime}\right]\frac{e^{-\mu}}{4r}.

(7)

To take the source
\Theta_{\mu\nu}
back into account, it is assumed that it is encoded in the metric (
6
) via a geometric deformation of the radial and temporal components. This deformation, described by two new functions
f=f(r)
and
g=g(r)
, can be expressed as

\displaystyle\xi\rightarrow\nu=\xi+\alpha g,

\displaystyle e^{-\mu}\rightarrow e^{-\lambda}=e^{-\mu}+\alpha f.

(8)

The constant parameter
\alpha
indicates the deviation from the seed solution. The essence of the decoupling method lies in the fact that substituting Eq. (
8
) into the Einstein equations (
II
) yields two independent systems. The first corresponds to the Einstein equations for the source
T_{\mu\nu}
, given by Eqs. (
II
). The second comprises the field equations for the additional source
\Theta_{\mu\nu}
, which take the form

\displaystyle k^{2}\Theta_{0}^{\phantom{i}0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha}{r}\left(f^{\prime}+\frac{f}{r}\right),

(9)

\displaystyle k^{2}\Theta_{1}^{\phantom{i}1}+\alpha\mathcal{J}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\alpha f\left(\frac{\nu^{\prime}r+1}{r^{2}}\right),

(10)

\displaystyle k^{2}\Theta_{2}^{\phantom{i}2}+\alpha\mathcal{J}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha f}{4r}\left(2r\nu^{\prime\prime}+r\nu^{\prime 2}+2\nu^{\prime}\right)-\frac{\alpha f^{\prime}}{4r}\left(r\nu^{\prime}+2\right),

(11)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\mu}g^{\prime}}{r}

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\mu}}{4}\left(2g^{\prime\prime}+\alpha g^{\prime 2}+\frac{2g^{\prime}}{r}+2\xi^{\prime}g^{\prime}-\mu g^{\prime}\right).

(13)

This system of equations is also known as the quasi-Einstein equations. Note that, when
\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\Theta_{\mu\nu}=0

Assuming the Schwarzschild solution as the seed metric, i.e.,
e^{\xi(r)}=e^{-\mu(r)}=1-\frac{2M}{r}

\displaystyle{\rm d}s^{2}=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)h(r){\rm d}t^{2}+\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}\frac{{\rm d}r^{2}}{h(r)}+r^{2}{\rm d}\theta^{2}+r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta{\rm d}\phi^{2},

Due to the additional degrees of freedom arising from the deformation functions, the field equations do not completely determine the solution, and additional constraints must be imposed. The choice made in
51

\Theta_{\mu\nu}
satisfies the weak energy condition, given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}\geq 0,

(15)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}+\tilde{p}_{r}\geq 0,

(16)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}+\tilde{p}_{\Theta}\geq 0,

(17)

\displaystyle k\tilde{\rho}=\frac{\alpha}{\ell^{2}}e^{-r/\ell}.

(18)

The parameter

\displaystyle e^{\nu(r)}=e^{-\lambda(r)}=1+\frac{2M}{r}+\frac{e^{-\alpha r/M}}{rM}\left(2M^{2}+2M\alpha r+\alpha^{2}r^{2}\right).

\displaystyle{\rm d}s^{2}=-f(r,\beta){\rm d}t^{2}+f(r,\beta)^{-1}{\rm d}r^{2}+r^{2}{\rm d}\theta^{2}+r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta{\rm d}\phi^{2},

\displaystyle f(r,\beta)\equiv e^{\nu(r)}=e^{-\lambda(r)}=1-\frac{2}{r}+\frac{2e^{-\frac{r}{\beta}}}{r}\left(1+\frac{r}{\beta}+\frac{r^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\right).

\displaystyle K(r,\beta)=

\displaystyle\frac{48}{r^{6}}-8\,{\left(\frac{12}{r^{6}}+\frac{1}{r^{2}\beta^{4}}+\frac{2}{r^{3}\beta^{3}}+\frac{6}{r^{4}\beta^{2}}+\frac{12}{r^{5}\beta}\right)}e^{\left(-\frac{r}{\beta}\right)}

\displaystyle+{\left(\frac{48}{r^{6}}+\frac{r^{2}}{\beta^{8}}+\frac{8}{\beta^{6}}+\frac{16}{r\beta^{5}}+\frac{36}{r^{2}\beta^{4}}+\frac{64}{r^{3}\beta^{3}}+\frac{96}{r^{4}\beta^{2}}+\frac{96}{r^{5}\beta}\right)}e^{\left(-\frac{2\,r}{\beta}\right)}.

IV
Spinoptics Formalism

The spinoptics approach is employed to study the propagation of high-frequency electromagnetic waves by incorporating into the ray dynamics the effects arising from the interaction between helicity and spacetime curvature. These modified ray equations are known as the spinoptics equations. This helicity–curvature coupling becomes particularly significant in the presence of strong gravitational fields, as will be introduced here and further explored in Section
V

A_{\mu}
is a complex vector potential. Adopting a WKB ansatz for the vector potential,

\displaystyle A_{\mu}=aM_{\mu}e^{i\omega S},

(37)

where
a
and
S
are real scalar functions representing the amplitude and phase, respectively, and
M_{\mu}
is a complex null vector satisfying the normalization conditions

\displaystyle M^{\mu}M_{\mu}=\bar{M}^{\mu}\bar{M}_{\mu}=0,\qquad M^{\mu}\bar{M}_{\mu}=1,\qquad\bar{M}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}S=0.

(38)

Applying the ansatz in Eq. (
37
) to the electromagnetic action (
36
) and imposing the normalization conditions in eq. (
38
) yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\frac{\omega^{2}}{2}\int\left\{a^{2}\left[\frac{1}{2}(\nabla S)^{2}-\frac{1}{\omega}B^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}S\right]\right\}\sqrt{-g}\>\mathrm{d}^{4}x,

(39)

where
B_{\mu}=i\bar{M}^{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}M_{\nu}
is a real vector. The spinoptics effective action can be identified from Eq. (
39
) as

\displaystyle I=\int\left\{a^{2}\left[\frac{1}{2}(\nabla S)^{2}-\frac{1}{\omega}B^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}S\right]+\Lambda\right\}\sqrt{-g}\>\mathrm{d}^{4}x.

(40)

where
\mathcal{I}=\omega^{2}I/2
. The two actions thus differ by a constant factor, which does not affect the equations of motion. The term
\Lambda
encapsulates the constraints incorporated in deriving the spinoptics approximation and is given by

\displaystyle\Lambda=\frac{1}{2}\bar{\lambda}_{1}M_{\mu}M^{\mu}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{1}\bar{M}_{\mu}\bar{M}^{\mu}+\lambda_{2}(M_{\mu}\bar{M}^{\mu}-1)+\bar{\lambda}_{3}M^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}S+\lambda_{3}\bar{M}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}S,

(41)

where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{3}
are complex constants, and
\lambda_{2}
is a real constant, and act as Lagrange multipliers.

From the variation
of the effective action
with respect to
a
, a Hamilton–Jacobi-type condition is obtained:

\displaystyle H=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla S)^{2}-\frac{1}{\omega}B^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}S=0,

(42)

while variation with respect to
S
gives the conservation law

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}J^{\mu}=0,

(43)

where the current
J^{\mu}
is defined as

\displaystyle J^{\mu}=a^{2}\left(\partial^{\mu}S-\frac{1}{\omega}B^{\mu}\right)+\bar{\lambda}{3}M^{\mu}+\lambda{3}\bar{M}^{\mu}.

(44)

Finally, variation with respect to
M_{\mu}
, together with the invariance of
M^{\mu}
under the transformation
M^{\mu}\rightarrow e^{i\psi}M^{\mu}
, allows us to impose the condition
B^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}S=0
. This leads to the dynamical equation

\displaystyle i\frac{a^{2}}{\omega}\partial^{\nu}S\nabla_{\nu}M_{\mu}=\lambda_{3}\partial_{\mu}S.

(45)

Equation (
42
) can be identified as the Hamilton–Jacobi equation for a particle with four-momentum
p_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}S
and determines the trajectories of high-frequency electromagnetic waves

\displaystyle H(p,x)=\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}p_{\mu}p_{\nu}-\frac{1}{\omega}B^{\mu}p_{\mu}.

(46)

Note that in the geometric optics limit,
\omega\to\infty
, Eq. (
46
) reduces to
the Hamiltonian of geometric optics.

The Hamilton equations that follow from Eq. (
46
) are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{\mu}}

\displaystyle=p_{\mu}-\frac{1}{\omega}B_{\mu}=\frac{{\rm d}x^{\mu}}{{\rm d}\lambda},

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^{\mu}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm d}p_{\mu}}{{\rm d}x^{\mu}}.

(47)

The above equations can be expressed more conveniently in a null tetrad basis. Indeed, let
\{{\bf\it\ell},{\bf\it n},{\bf\it m},{\bf\it\bar{m}}\}
be a null basis of the spacetime
(\mathcal{M},{g})
, satisfying the normalization conditions
\ell^{\mu}n_{\mu}=-1
and
m^{\mu}\bar{m}_{\mu}=1
with all other scalar products vanishing, and where
{\bf\it\bar{m}}
denotes the complex conjugate of
{\bf\it m}
. Using these normalization conditions, one can show that

\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\ell_{\mu}n_{\nu}m_{\alpha}\bar{m}_{\beta}=i\sigma,

where
\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}
is the Levi-Civita tensor and

\displaystyle\sigma=\begin{cases}1,&\text{right-handed tetrad},\\
-1,&\text{left-handed tetrad}.\end{cases}

(48)

The null ray
{\bf\it\ell}
can be interpreted as the four-vector describing the trajectory of a light ray interacting with spacetime curvature. Using Eq. (
IV
), it can be expressed as
\ell^{\mu}=p^{\mu}-\frac{1}{\omega}B^{\mu}
. The second equation in (
IV
) yields

\displaystyle D\ell^{\mu}=\ell^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\ell^{\mu}=w^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\omega}K^{\mu}_{\phantom{i}\nu}\ell^{\nu},

(49)

where
K^{\mu}_{\phantom{i}\nu}=\nabla_{\mu}B_{\nu}-\nabla_{\nu}B_{\mu}
is an antisymmetric tensor. Moreover,
\ell^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}({\bf\it\ell}^{2})=0
, which implies that if
{\bf\it\ell}
vanishes at one point along the trajectory, it vanishes everywhere. It is worth noting that
{\bf\it\ell}
is not a geodesic vector, but becomes one in the limit
\omega\to\infty
.

The choice of the null complex tetrad is constrained by the requirement that the same limit be recovered when its vectors are propagated along the null ray
{\bf\it\ell}
. Accordingly, we have

\displaystyle D\ell^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega}(\bar{\kappa}m^{\mu}+\kappa\bar{m}^{\mu}),

(50)

\displaystyle Dn^{\mu}

\displaystyle=0,

(51)

\displaystyle Dm^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega}\kappa n^{\mu},

(52)

where
\kappa
are the complex coefficients of
w^{\mu}
when expressed in the tetrad basis. Using the set of vectors defined above, the spinoptics equations, corresponding to eqs. (
43
), (
45
), (
50
), (
51
), and (
52
), can be written as

\displaystyle D\ell^{\mu}

\displaystyle=w^{\mu},

(53)

\displaystyle Dn^{\mu}

\displaystyle=0,

(54)

\displaystyle Dm^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma}{\omega}\kappa n^{\mu},

(55)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}(a^{2}\ell^{\mu})

\displaystyle=0.

(56)

Here

\displaystyle w^{\mu}=\frac{\sigma}{\omega}\left(\bar{\kappa}m^{\mu}+\kappa\bar{m}^{\mu}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\kappa=iR_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}m^{\mu}\ell^{\nu}m^{\alpha}\bar{m}^{\beta}.

(57)

Equation (
56
) indicates that the number of photons in a beam is conserved.

V
Spinoptics for Parametrized and Hairy Black Holes

The spinoptics formalism has previously been applied to the Schwarzschild black hole
29
and the Kerr black hole
23
. In this section, we apply it to study light propagation in the vicinity of the parametrized black holes introduced in Section
III
and the hairy regular solution from Section
II
. To do so, we first construct a null tetrad basis that is parallel-propagated along the geodesic in the high-frequency limit
\omega\to\infty
, following the approach of Ref.
28
, which uses spacetime symmetries to obtain the null basis and then transforms it into a parallel-propagated one. In fact, we slightly generalize the null tetrad basis used in Frolov’s paper, which is valid for spherically symmetric spacetimes with a metric of the form
g=-f(r){\rm d}t^{2}+\dfrac{1}{f(r)}{\rm d}r^{2}+r^{2}{\rm d}\theta^{2}+r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta{\rm d}\phi^{2}
. However, for the Rezzolla–Zhidenko metric, as shown in eq. (
24
), when
B^{2}(r)\neq 1
the metric does not take the form assumed in
28
. The difference in the null tetrad construction arises because the equation
\nabla_{\lambda}h_{\mu\nu}=\xi_{\mu}g_{\nu\lambda}-\xi_{\nu}g_{\mu\lambda}
, whose solution is the conformal Killing–Yano two-form, imposes the condition
g_{tt}g_{rr}=c
(with
c
an integration constant). This condition does not hold for the RZ metric (
24
); consequently, no conformal Killing–Yano two-form exists. Nevertheless, the conformal Killing–Yano two-form and its dual (the Killing–Yano two-form) are used only to construct tensors that project vectors onto orthogonal directions. We thus use, instead, a two-form for projection that is not a conformal Killing–Yano tensor. The explicit construction of generalized null tetrad parallel-propagated along the geodesic is given in Appendix
A
.

V.1
Parametrized Black Holes

For practical reasons, we must first truncate the parametrized metric of Eq. (
24
). Following Refs.
41
;
65
;
67
, we assume that the geometry is determined by only three parametrization coefficients:
\epsilon
,
a_{1}
, and

\displaystyle B^{2}(r)=\left(1+\frac{r_{h}^{2}b_{1}}{r^{2}}\right)^{2}.

\displaystyle N^{2}\left(r\right)\equiv N^{2}=\left(1-\frac{2}{r}\right)\left(1-\frac{2\epsilon}{r}-\frac{4\epsilon}{r^{2}}+\frac{8a_{1}}{r^{3}}\right),

We now apply the formalism from the previous section, together with the null tetrad adapted to the parametrized spherically symmetric spacetime introduced in Appendix

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}iL^{3}{\left[3r^{5}-20r^{3}-4\,b_{1}\,r{\left(r^{2}+4\right)}\right]}\Phi\epsilon

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2}i\,L{\left[-8E^{2}{b_{1}}r^{6}+16L^{2}{b_{1}}r^{4}+3L^{2}r^{5}+64L^{2}{b_{1}}^{3}-16L^{2}{a_{1}}{b_{1}}r\right.}

\displaystyle{\left.+64L^{2}{a_{1}}{b_{1}}-4L^{2}\,{\left(5{a_{1}}+{b_{1}}\right)}r^{3}+48\,L^{2}{\left({b_{1}}^{2}+{a_{1}}\right)}r^{2}\right]}\Phi

\displaystyle+8\sqrt{2}iEb_{1}r^{4}\,\sqrt{{E^{2}r^{6}-L^{2}\left[r^{4}-2\,r^{3}+8\,{a_{1}}r-16\,{a_{1}}-2\,r{\left(r^{2}-4\,\right)}\epsilon\right]}}

\displaystyle\kappa_{Schw}=\frac{3\sqrt{2}i\,L^{2}\Phi}{2\,r^{5}}.

Here, the function

\displaystyle l^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\Phi=\frac{d\Phi}{d\lambda}=\frac{E}{LB(r)}.

In the discussion below, we adopt the rescaling of parameters in terms of the energy

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle\equiv E\lambda,\qquad L\equiv\dfrac{L}{E},\qquad L_{z}\equiv\dfrac{L_{z}}{E},\qquad{\bf\it l}\equiv\dfrac{{\bf\it l}}{E},\qquad{\bf\it w}\equiv\dfrac{{\bf\it w}}{E^{2}}

(66)

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle\equiv E\Phi,\qquad\mathcal{R}\equiv\dfrac{\mathcal{R}}{E}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{r^{2}-L^{2}N^{2}}{B^{2}}},\qquad\Theta\equiv\frac{\Theta}{E}=\pm\sqrt{L^{2}\sin^{2}\theta-L_{z}^{2}},

(67)

where

\displaystyle\psi=\frac{\mathcal{B}\sigma}{\,Lr^{4}{\left(r^{3}+4\,{b_{1}}\right)^{3}}\omega}.

In the above expression,

denotes the photon’s helicity,

is the frequency of the wave, and
\mathcal{B}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=

\displaystyle\left\{{L^{3}\left[3r^{5}-20r^{3}-4\,b_{1}r{\left(r^{2}+4\right)}\right]}\Phi\epsilon\right.

\displaystyle\psi_{Schw}=\frac{3L^{2}\sigma\Phi}{\omega r^{5}}.

(71)

Since we are considering a spherically symmetric spacetime, we may assume, without loss of generality, that the unperturbed null vector

lies in the equatorial plane. This allows us to set

\displaystyle L=L_{z},\qquad\Theta=0,\qquad{\bf\it l}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{\mathcal{R}}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{L}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi},\qquad{\bf\it e}_{\theta}\equiv\evaluated{\vb*{e}_{2}}_{\theta={\pi/2}}=\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}

so that the trajectory of the corresponding null ray is governed by the following differential equations

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}t}{{\rm d}\lambda}=\frac{1}{N^{2}},\qquad\frac{{\rm d}r}{{\rm d}\lambda}=\frac{\mathcal{R}}{r},\qquad\frac{{\rm d}\phi}{{\rm d}\lambda}=\frac{L}{r^{2}}.

(73)

Notice that, under the rescaling given in Eq. (

coincides with the impact parameter. Assuming

\displaystyle\left.\frac{{\rm d}r}{{\rm d}\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=0}=\frac{\mathcal{R}}{r_{m}}=0.

(74)

This condition implies

\displaystyle L=\frac{r_{m}}{\sqrt{N^{2}(r_{m})}}=\frac{{r_{m}}^{3}}{\sqrt{{r_{m}}^{4}-{r_{m}}^{3}+{a_{1}}{r_{m}}-{\left({r_{m}}^{3}-{r_{m}}\right)}\epsilon-{a_{1}}}}.

(75)

Here, the affine parameter

To understand how the helicity–curvature interaction affects the trajectory of photons propagating in spacetime, we assume that their four-velocity vector can be written as

\displaystyle\ell^{\mu}=l^{\mu}+k^{\mu}=\frac{{\rm d}x^{\mu}}{{\rm d}\lambda}+\frac{{\rm d}\left(\delta x^{\mu}\right)}{{\rm d}\lambda},

(76)

where

is the null geodesic vector describing the trajectory of the null ray in the absence of helicity-induced corrections. The vector

\mathcal{O}\left(1/\omega\right)
. According to the spinoptics formalism,
{\bf\it\ell}
must be a null vector. Thus, in our high-frequency approximation,
\ell^{\mu}\ell_{\mu}=\mathcal{O}(1/\omega^{2})\approx 0

{\bf\it k}
and
{\bf\it l}
are mutually orthogonal. We have already constructed two vectors,
{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}
and

(see Eq. (

{\bf\it k}
can be expressed as a linear combination of
{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}
and
{\bf\it e}_{2}

\displaystyle{\bf\it k}=k^{1}\left[\frac{{B(r)}\mathcal{R}(r)}{LN^{2}(r)}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{r}{L{B(r)}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right]\;+\;\frac{k^{2}}{r}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}.

\displaystyle{\bf\it k}=k^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+k^{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+k^{\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta},

\displaystyle k^{t}=k^{1}\frac{{B(r)}\mathcal{R}(r)}{LN^{2}(r)},\qquad k^{r}=k^{1}\frac{r}{L{B(r)}},\qquad k^{\theta}=\frac{k^{2}}{r}.

53

\displaystyle D\ell^{\mu}=\frac{{\rm d}\ell^{\mu}}{{\rm d}\lambda}+\Gamma^{\mu}_{\phantom{\mu}\alpha\beta}\ell^{\alpha}\ell^{\beta}=\left(\frac{{\rm d}l^{\mu}}{{\rm d}\lambda}+\Gamma^{\mu}_{\phantom{\mu}\alpha\beta}l^{\alpha}l^{\beta}\right)+\left(\frac{{\rm d}k^{\mu}}{{\rm d}\lambda}+2\Gamma^{\mu}_{\phantom{\mu}\alpha\beta}l^{\alpha}k^{\beta}\right)+\Gamma^{\mu}_{\phantom{\mu}\alpha\beta}k^{\alpha}k^{\beta}=\psi e_{\theta}^{\mu}.

).

In the following discussion, we again rescale the parameters in terms of the energy and constrain the null radius vector
{\bf\it l}
to the equatorial plane. Under these conditions, the vector
w^{\mu}
from Eq. (
53
) takes the form
w^{\mu}=\psi,e_{2}^{\mu}
, where
\psi
is given by

\displaystyle\psi=\frac{3L^{2}\Phi\sigma}{r^{5}\omega}-\frac{3L^{2}\sigma e^{-r/\beta}}{\omega}\left(\frac{1}{r^{5}}+\frac{1}{r^{4}\beta}+\frac{1}{2r^{3}\beta^{2}}+\frac{1}{6r^{2}\beta^{3}}\right)\Phi,

(90)

the
{\bf\it l}
components by

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}t}{{\rm d}\lambda}=\frac{1}{f(r,\beta)},\qquad\frac{{\rm d}r}{{\rm d}\lambda}=\frac{\mathcal{R}}{r},\qquad\frac{{\rm d}\phi}{{\rm d}\lambda}=\frac{L}{r^{2}},

(91)

and the vector
e^{\mu}_{2}
is adjusted according to the spacetime metric. Consequently, for the regular hairy black hole, the differential equation governing the particle’s deflection angle relative to the equatorial plane becomes,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}^{2}{\delta\tilde{\theta}}}{{\rm d}{\lambda}^{2}}+\frac{2{\mathcal{R}}}{{r}^{2}}\frac{{\rm d}\delta\tilde{\theta}}{{\rm d}{\lambda}}=\frac{L\lambda}{r^{6}}\left[1-\left(1+\frac{r}{\beta}+\frac{r^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}+\frac{r^{3}}{6\beta^{3}}\right)e^{-r/\beta}\right].

(92)

Adopting
\delta\tilde{\theta}=\delta\theta\,\omega/3\sigma
as before, and with Eq. (
65
) reducing to
\derivative{\Phi}{\lambda}=\frac{1}{L}
,
\Phi
simplifies to
\Phi=\lambda/L
, in agreement with Ref.
29
. Note that this deflection angle depends on the particle’s interaction with the spacetime curvature, which ultimately depends on the additional parameter
\beta
.

VI
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we investigated null ray dynamics in the presence of helicity-curvature interactions. To this end, we selected two models to describe the
background. The first is the Rezzolla–Zhidenko parametrized metric
56
;
17
;
40
, which describes a wide class of spherically symmetric spacetimes, both within and beyond General Relativity. The second model is
a
regular hairy black hole solution derived via the gravitational decoupling method
51
;
52
. Before applying the spinoptics formalism to these spacetimes, we tested the ability of the Rezzolla–Zhidenko metric, at the order suggested in Ref.
40
, to describe the regular hairy black hole. Both metrics could be used to describe low-energy corrections to general relativity solutions. However, to the best of our knowledge, they had not been compared as we have done here. In fact, the Rezzolla–Zhidenko model best approximates the hairy black hole as the hairy parameter
\beta
approaches zero, that is, the closer the hairy black hole is to the Schwarzschild solution. This assessment is first carried out by comparing the shadow of the hairy black hole with that of the Rezzolla–Zhidenko metric when the latter is used to describe the same hairy black hole, following
40
. The relative error reaches
\sim 6.7940\%
as
\beta
approaches
\beta_{\rm crit}\approx 0.3883945
.

We proceed by applying the spinoptics formalism to the spacetime models mentioned above. The main outcome of this formalism for our purpose is that, due to the helicity–curvature interaction, the propagation of light rays
in spherically symmetric spacetimes is not planar. To achieve this, the results of Ref.
29

were extended
to generalized spherically symmetric spacetimes where
g_{rr}g_{tt}\neq-1
. The off-equatorial-plane deflection angles
were then computed
for various configurations of the RZ metric, as well as for a range of the hairy parameter
\beta
for the hairy regular solution, and compared with the results for the Schwarzschild solution. Our results show a clear signature of the additional parameters in the deflection angle, which might lead to a reexamination of black hole shadow images, as suggested in Ref.
23
. Moreover, the RZ metric
was used here to reproduce the results of the hairy regular solution and to compare them with those obtained directly from the latter. This highlights the limitations of the RZ metric, at the order employed, in mimicking the hairy black hole. In particular, the relative error between them becomes unacceptable as
\beta
approaches
\beta_{\rm crit}
, reaching
\sim 500\%
for
\beta=0.33
. While extending the RZ metric could yield some improvement, Ref.
40
suggests that this improvement is limited, and would come at the cost of increased computational expense.

Appendix A
Parallel-propagated null tetrad

In this appendix, we derive the null tetrad that is parallel-propagated along null geodesics for a general spherically symmetric metric of the form given by the RZ metric:

\displaystyle{\rm d}s^{2}=-N^{2}(r){\rm d}t^{2}+\frac{B^{2}(r)}{N^{2}(r)}{\rm d}r^{2}+r^{2}{\rm d}\theta^{2}+r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta{\rm d}\phi^{2}.

(93)

We follow the approach of Refs.
28
;
24
, which in summary proceeds as follows: starting from the null tangent vector, it uses the orthogonal projection properties of the spherically symmetric conformal Killing–Yano and Killing–Yano forms to obtain two additional mutually orthogonal vectors. It then finds a fourth vector orthogonal to the plane formed by these two. From this set of four vectors, the tetrad basis parallel-propagated along null geodesics is constructed.

Let
\displaystyle{\bf\it\xi}=\partialderivative{t}
and
\displaystyle{\bf\it\zeta}=\partialderivative{\phi}
denote the Killing vectors associated with local energy conservation and angular momentum in the
z
-direction, respectively. The closed conformal Killing–Yano form
\mathbf{h}
is given as the solution to the equation

\displaystyle\nabla_{\lambda}h_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\xi_{\mu}g_{\nu\lambda}-\xi_{\nu}g_{\mu\lambda},

(94)

and is dual to the Killing–Yano form
\mathbf{k}=\star\,\mathbf{h}
. The difficulty here is that
\mathbf{h}=-r{\rm d}t\wedge{\rm d}r
is not a solution to the above equation, as in Ref.
28
. In fact, assuming a general antisymmetric two-form
\mathbf{h}(r,\theta)
and substituting it into the equation, we find that only the component
h_{01}(r)
is not identically zero. The only relevant equations in (
94
) are those corresponding to the components
t\theta\theta
and
trr
, whose solutions are respectively given by
h_{01}=rg_{tt}g_{rr}=-rB(r)^{2}
and
g_{tt}g_{rr}=c
,
where
c
is an integration constant. Together, they imply that
B(r)
should be constant and
h_{01}
proportional to
r
. Since this case is not of interest here, we disregard the
trr
component and retain a more general expression for
h_{01}
, namely
\widetilde{h}_{01}=-rF(g_{tt},g_{rr})
, where
F
is a general function of the metric components
g_{tt}
and
g_{rr}
. To determine
F
, we first note that the Killing–Yano form from Ref.
28
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}=r^{3}\sin\theta\,{\rm d}\theta\wedge{\rm d}\phi,

(95)

still satisfies
\nabla_{(\lambda}k_{\mu)\nu}=0
, and therefore remains a Killing–Yano form. Consequently,
K_{\mu\nu}=k_{\mu}^{\ \alpha}k_{\nu\alpha}

is a symmetric rank-2 Killing tensor. We then fix
F
by imposing that
\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}=-\star\mathbf{k}=-rB(r)\,{\rm d}t\wedge{\rm d}r
,
which implies

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{01}=-rF(g_{tt},g_{rr})=-r\sqrt{-g_{tt}g_{rr}}.

(96)

Since
\widetilde{h}^{\mu\alpha}k_{\alpha\nu}
vanishes, it follows that for any vector
{v}
, the three vectors
k^{\mu}{{}_{\nu}}v^{\nu}
,
\widetilde{h}^{\mu}{{}_{\nu}}v^{\nu}
, and
{v}
are mutually orthogonal. The construction of the parallel-propagated null tetrad below follows the same general procedure as in Ref.
28
. However, certain key details must be modified. Accordingly, we present it here for the sake of clarity and completeness.

Given a null geodesic
x^{\mu}(\lambda)
, its tangent vector can be expressed in terms of conserved quantities. This tangent vector also allows us to construct two independent vectors orthogonal to it by applying
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{\widetilde{h}}
as above. Considering metric (
93
), the tangent vector is

\displaystyle{\bf\it l}=\frac{{\rm d}x^{\mu}}{{\rm d}\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}=\frac{E}{N^{2}(r)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{\mathcal{R}(r)}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{\Theta(\theta)}{r^{2}\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}+\frac{L_{z}}{r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi},

(97)

where
-\xi_{\alpha}l^{\alpha}=E,\quad\zeta_{\alpha}l^{\alpha}=L_{z},\quad K_{\mu\nu}l^{\mu}l^{\nu}=L^{2}
correspond, respectively, to the energy, the azimuthal angular momentum, and the total angular momentum. The functions
\mathcal{R}(r)
and
\Theta(\theta)
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(r)=\pm\sqrt{\frac{E^{2}r^{2}-L^{2}N^{2}(r)}{B^{2}(r)}},\quad\Theta(\theta)=\pm\sqrt{L^{2}\sin^{2}\theta-L_{z}^{2}}.

(98)

The signs
\pm
in eq. (
98
) are independent. They are positive when the corresponding coordinate increases along the light ray’s propagation and negative otherwise. Sign changes occur at the turning points, where either
\mathcal{R}
or
\Theta
vanishes. From these, the two normalized vectors orthogonal to
{l}
are given by
{\widetilde{e}}_{1}^{\mu}=\dfrac{1}{L}\,\widetilde{h}^{\mu}{{}_{\nu}}\,l^{\nu}
and
{e}_{2}^{\mu}=\dfrac{1}{L}\,k^{\mu}{{}_{\nu}}\,l^{\nu}.
Explicitly, they read

\displaystyle{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}=\frac{{B(r)}\mathcal{R}(r)}{LN^{2}(r)}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{Er}{L{B(r)}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\;\quad\text{and}\quad\;\displaystyle{\bf\it e}_{2}=\frac{L_{z}}{Lr\sin\theta}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}-\frac{\Theta(\theta)}{Lr\sin^{2}\theta}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}.

(99)

The remaining independent vector,
{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{3}
, can be obtained by starting with an arbitrary vector
{\bf\it u}
and imposing orthogonality to the plane spanned by
{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}
and
{\bf\it e}_{2}
. This condition does not determine
{\bf\it u}
uniquely; it fixes only two of its components. Uniqueness is achieved by additionally requiring that
{\bf\it u}
be normalized and future-directed, satisfying
u^{\mu}l_{\mu}=-1
. The resulting vector is then denoted by
{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{3}
, and is explicitly given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{3}=\frac{Er^{2}}{2\,L^{2}N^{2}(r)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{rR(r)}{2\,L^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}-\frac{\Theta(\theta)}{2\,L^{2}\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}-\frac{L_{z}}{2\,L^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}.

(100)

The final step is to construct, from
{\bf\it l}
,
{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}
,
{\bf\it e}_{2}
, and
{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{3}
, a complex null tetrad that is parallel-propagated along the null geodesic. By definition,
{\bf\it l}
is parallel-propagated along the null geodesic. Explicit computation shows that
{\bf\it e}_{2}
is also parallel-propagated, i.e.,
\nabla_{{\bf\it l}}{\bf\it e}_{2}=0
. For
{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}
, notice that
\nabla_{{\bf\it l}}{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}=-\dfrac{\xi_{\alpha}l^{\alpha}}{LB(r)}{\bf\it l}=\dfrac{E}{LB(r)}{\bf\it l}
, which suggests that we may subtract an appropriate multiple of
{\bf\it l}
from
{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}
to obtain a vector that is parallel-propagated along the null geodesic. To this end, we introduce a scalar field
\Phi(r)
such that, along the geodesic,

\displaystyle\nabla_{l}(\Phi(r){\bf\it l})=l^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\Phi(r){\bf\it l}=-\dfrac{\xi_{\alpha}l^{\alpha}}{L{B}(r)}{\bf\it l}.

(101)

Consequently,
\nabla_{{\bf\it l}}({\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}-\Phi(r){\bf\it l})=\nabla_{{\bf\it l}}{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}-\nabla_{{\bf\it l}}(\Phi(r){\bf\it l})=0
. Hence, by construction, the vector
{\bf\it e}_{1}={\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}-\Phi(r){\bf\it l}
is parallel-propagated along the null geodesic. Observing that
\nabla_{{\bf\it l}}{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{3}=\dfrac{E}{LB(r)}{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}
, we apply the same strategy. Defining
n={\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{3}-\Phi(r){\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}+\dfrac{\Phi(r)^{2}}{2}{\bf\it l}
one finds that
{\bf\it n}
is likewise parallel-propagated by construction. The result is a set of parallel-propagated vectors from which we construct the following complex null tetrad

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\bf\it l}&={\bf\it l},\\
{\bf\it m}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}({\bf\it e}_{1}+i{\bf\it e}_{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}({\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}-\Phi(r){\bf\it l}+i{\bf\it e}_{2}),\\
{\bf\it\bar{m}}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}({\bf\it e}_{1}-i{\bf\it e}_{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}({\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}-\Phi(r){\bf\it l}-i{\bf\it e}_{2}),\\
{\bf\it n}&={\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{3}-\Phi{\bf\it\widetilde{e}}_{1}+\dfrac{\Phi^{2}}{2}{\bf\it l},\end{cases}

(102)

with
{l}^{\mu}n_{\mu}=m^{\mu}m_{\mu}=-1
. Or, expressed in the coordinate basis

\displaystyle{\bf\it l}

\displaystyle=\frac{E}{N^{2}(r)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{\mathcal{R}(r)}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{\Theta(\theta)}{r^{2}\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}+\frac{L_{z}}{r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}

(103)

\displaystyle{\bf\it m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\frac{EL\Phi(r)-B(r)R(r)}{LN^{2}(r)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{LB(r)R(r)\Phi(r)-Er^{2}}{LrB(r)}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{L\Theta(\theta)\Phi(r)-i\,L_{z}r}{Lr^{2}\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{LL_{z}\Phi(r)+i\,r\Theta(\theta)}{Lr^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\right]

(104)

\displaystyle{\bf\it\bar{m}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\frac{EL\Phi(r)-B(r)R(r)}{LN^{2}(r)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{LB(r)R(r)\Phi(r)-Er^{2}}{LrB(r)}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{L\Theta(\theta)\Phi(r)+i\,L_{z}r}{Lr^{2}\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{LL_{z}\Phi(r)-i\,r\Theta(\theta)}{Lr^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\right]

(105)

\displaystyle{\bf\it n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\,L^{2}}\left[\frac{(L^{2}\Phi(r)^{2}+r^{2})E-2\,LB(r)R(r)\Phi(r)}{N^{2}(r)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{(L^{2}\Phi(r)^{2}+r^{2})R(r)B(r)-2\,ELr^{2}\Phi(r)}{rB(r)}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left.\frac{{(L^{2}\Phi(r)^{2}-r^{2})}\Theta(\theta)}{r^{2}\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}+\frac{(L^{2}\Phi(r)^{2}-r^{2})L_{z}}{r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\right].

(106)

This completes the construction of a null tetrad that is parallel-propagated along the null ray in a general spherically symmetric spacetime.
