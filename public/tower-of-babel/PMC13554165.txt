A one-parameter poisson–emrem model for count data: Theory and applications to cytogenetic and COVID-19 studies
Authors: Tabassum Naz Sindhu, Anum Shafiq, Hany Mohamed Hamouda, Tahani A Abushal, Yasser M Ayid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper proposes the Poisson–Emrem (PEm) distribution, a new one-parameter discrete probability model obtained by compounding the Poisson and Emrem distributions. The model preserves the mathematical tractability of the Poisson law while offering greater flexibility for analyzing count data in health and biological sciences. A theoretical study establishes its principal properties, showing that increases in the parameter lead to systematic reductions in the mean, variance, and interquartile range, with a corresponding increase in skewness. Additional features such as moments, dispersion index, and the hazard rate function are derived, the latter characterized by a bathtub-shaped form that is particularly suitable for lifetime studies. Parameters are estimated using maximum likelihood, and Monte Carlo simulations confirm the accuracy and efficiency of the estimator. The applicability of the PEm distribution is demonstrated through two empirical datasets involving cytogenetic abnormalities in rabbit lymphoid cells under streptogramin treatment and daily COVID-19 mortality counts in South Korea. Comparative goodness-of-fit analyses show that the proposed model consistently provides superior performance over competing discrete distributions. Owing to its ability to effectively capture skewness, over-dispersion, and zero inflation, the PEm distribution offers a flexible and reliable framework for modeling complex count data arising in health and biological sciences. These findings highlight its potential usefulness in public health monitoring and related statistical applications.Keywords: Count data, PEm, Lifetime model, Over-dispersion, Compounding, Simulation studySubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The statistical analysis of count-valued data plays a central role in many contemporary research areas, including the biological sciences, econometrics, clinical studies, quantitative finance, and data-driven computational applications. The development of discrete probability models for count data has evolved around the Poisson and negative binomial distributions as foundational cases. Despite its widespread use, the Poisson distribution is constrained by the equidispersion assumption, which requires the mean and variance to be equal. In practice, this assumption is rarely satisfied, as real-world data often display variability that exceeds or falls below the mean. Overdispersion is particularly prevalent and can lead to misleading inference when the standard Poisson model is applied. Underdispersion, although less common, also poses challenges. These empirical realities have encouraged the design of more versatile statistical models effective in encompassing a broader spectrum of variability patterns. Like developments have considerably advanced both inferential reliability and interpretability in discrete data modeling1–3.

Although the Poisson distribution remains a fundamental tool for count data analysis, its restrictive variance structure limits its effectiveness in many applied settings characterized by heterogeneous variability. In response, substantial methodological progress has been made toward constructing flexible distributional alternatives that relax the equidispersion constraint. One of the most successful strategies in this regard is compounding, in which a parameter of the base distribution is treated as a random variable following an auxiliary mixing distribution. This mixing distribution may be discrete or continuous, and its incorporation yields a compound model that retains the discrete nature of count data while offering greater flexibility in capturing complex stochastic behavior.

Compound distributions are particularly well suited for modeling data arising from heterogeneous populations or processes with inherent variability. By appropriately selecting the mixing distribution, these models can better reflect the underlying data-generating mechanism, resulting in improved model fit and more accurate statistical inference, while still preserving mathematical tractability. Seminal contributions in this area date back to Greenwood and Yule4, who assumed a gamma distribution for the Poisson rate parameter and derived the negative binomial model. Extending this framework, recently, Sindhu et al.5 introduced the Poisson–Juchez distribution, obtained by compounding the Poisson and Juchez distributions, and demonstrated its effectiveness as a flexible and tractable model for over-dispersed count data. Seghier et al.6 proposed the Poisson–QGamma distribution, a compound discrete model that effectively captures over-dispersion, skewness, and excess kurtosis in count data while maintaining analytical tractability. Maya et al.7 constructed a new model, the Poisson–Mirra and studied parameter estimation under both maximum likelihood and Bayesian approaches. Their work was further extended to the construction of an integer-valued autoregressive process and a regression model based on this distribution, with applications to seismic and medical datasets. Similarly, Grine and Zeghdoudi8 proposed the Poisson Quasi-Lindley distribution to enhance modeling flexibility for count data. Shanker9 established the Poisson–Garima model and analyzed its key statistical characteristics, while Shukla and Shanker10 later developed the Poisson–Ishita distribution to address overdispersion, illustrating its applicability through biological data analysis.

Continuing along this line of research, Hassan et al.11 formulated the Poisson–Ailamujia model and investigated its statistical behavior using maximum likelihood estimation techniques. In recent years, a growing number of compound Poisson models have been introduced, highlighting the sustained interest and versatility of compounding methods in count data analysis. Examples include the Poisson–Quasi Shanker12, Poisson–Moment Exponential13, Poisson–Samade14, Poisson–XLindley15, Poisson–Loai16, Poisson–Extended Exponential17, Poisson–Entropy-Based Weighted Exponential18, Poisson–New XLindley19, Poisson–Quasi XLindley20, Poisson–Xgamma21, and Poisson–Rama22 distributions. Collectively, these models aim to overcome the limitations of traditional count distributions by offering enhanced flexibility for handling overdispersion, thereby improving model performance and expanding applicability across a wide range of empirical contexts.

As part of recent developments, Emmanuel W. Okereke and Onyebuchi R. Uwaeme23 introduced the Emrem distribution, a one-parameter lifetime model whose probability density and hazard rate functions display two turning points and a bathtub-shaped form, among other distinctive statistical features. Despite the growing literature on Poisson-compounded distributions, there remains a need for simpler yet more flexible count models capable of simultaneously accommodating skewness, overdispersion, and heterogeneous event structures commonly observed in biological and health-related datasets. Existing models may provide adequate performance in certain settings, but often lack sufficient adaptability when the observed counts exhibit complex distributional behavior. This limitation motivates the development of the proposed Poisson–Emrem distribution.

A new discrete probability model is introduced by combining the Poisson distribution with the Emrem mixing distribution, resulting in a new one-parameter distribution hereafter referred to as the Poisson–Emrem (PEm) distribution. In this theoretical setting, the Poisson mean parameter is assumed to be governed by an Emrem distribution. This modeling approach yields a flexible alternative to the traditional Poisson framework and is particularly effective in situations where empirical data exhibit features such as excess variability and asymmetry. The proposed distribution is effective for representing complex count processes found in different applied settings, like radiation measurements, financial risk modeling, actuarial science, biological and ecological systems, surveillance studies, environmental epidemiology, and reliability analysis. In such settings, classical Poisson-based models often prove inadequate due to their inability to capture intrinsic heterogeneity and distributional irregularities.

The present study is designed with three main aims. First, a discrete distribution governed by one parameter is developed as a competitive alternative to existing count models, followed by an in-depth investigation of its theoretical properties. These include the derivation of moments, the Lorenz curve, factorial moments, past lifetime functions, cumulants, mean deviation measures, mean residual life, probability generating functions, and several reliability-related characteristics. Second, classical inferential techniques are employed to estimate the model parameter, with particular focus on maximum likelihood estimation. The performance of the proposed estimator is evaluated in terms of bias, consistency, and stability through an extensive Monte Carlo simulation framework. Third, the empirical value of the proposed approach is established via applications to biological and public health data. Specifically, the distribution is fitted to two real datasets: cytogenetic abnormalities observed in rabbit lymphoid cells following streptogramin exposure, and daily counts of COVID-19-related fatalities recorded in South Korea between February 15 and December 12, 2020, as reported by Worldometers. The empirical results illustrate the adaptability and effectiveness of the PEm distribution for modeling complex biological count data and epidemiological outcomes.

The structure of the paper is arranged in the following manner. Section "Principal features of the PEm" is devoted to the analytical derivation of its fundamental statistical properties. Reliability-based measures are discussed in Section "Relevant reliability metrics". Next section holds the proposed framework for real datasets to evaluate its empirical effectiveness. The paper concludes in Section "Conclusions" with a summary of the principal outcomes and recommendations for subsequent investigation.

The results as a whole confirm the model’s solid theoretical foundation, numerical tractability, and wide applicability in practice. Unlike the standard Poisson framework, which is inherently restricted to equal mean and variance, the proposed formulation accommodates both pronounced overdispersion and scenarios close to equidispersion. Moreover, the presence of a single adjustable shape parameter enables the model to capture a wide spectrum of count data patterns, ranging from short-tailed and moderately concentrated forms to distributions with substantial skewness and heavy tails. Owing to this adaptability, the model is well suited for contexts in which count data display structural variability or violate Poisson-based assumptions, including but not limited to reliability engineering, survival analysis, epidemiological studies, queueing theory, and actuarial science.

Lorenz curve

To assess distributional variability, multiple indices are available. A common choice is the Lorenz-curve (LC), a graphical summary based on cumulative proportions of the population and the variable of interest. Originating in studies of income and wealth inequality, the LC now serves broadly to quantify heterogeneity in probabilistic models. Departures from the 45° line of equality capture inequality; their overall magnitude is summarized by the Lorenz modulus. Large departures indicate marked heterogeneity, while small departures indicate near homogeneity.

Figure 9 shows the characteristic nondecreasing shape of the LC surface with contour projections for two parameter ranges. In both panels, LC rises with i and with δ, approaching unity as mass becomes more concentrated. Steeper regions and denser contour bands mark parameter combinations where the curve is most sensitive, highlighting where small changes in i or δ produce noticeable shifts in inequality.

Although relative error intensifies for higher δ, the overall tendency toward stabilization remains evident, with values reaching satisfactory levels once the sample size exceeds approximately n = 50. Interval estimates further reflect this behavior, as uncertainty bands expand noticeably for large δ, particularly in small samples. For instance, when δ = 2.6,2.85, interval lengths surpass 8 at n = 10 before contracting progressively with increasing sample size. These observations underscore the heightened variability associated with large parameter values and reinforce the need for sufficient data to support reliable inference.

Application

In this section, the applicability of the PEm distribution is evaluated using two real datasets. The fitted models are compared through several information-based measures, including the log-likelihood, Akaike information criterion (AIC), corrected Akaike information criterion (CAIC), Hannan–Quinn information criterion (HQIC), and the nd the Chi-square goodness-of-fit statistic, p-value, and associated degrees of freedom (DFDM). These criteria provide a structured way of balancing model fit against parsimony, though they differ in how strongly they penalize model complexity. AIC is widely used for its capacity to detect intricate patterns, making it particularly useful for smaller datasets such as the cytogenetic anomalies, where capturing subtle biological variation is crucial. CAIC applies a stronger penalty in small-sample contexts, offering a more conservative alternative that guards against overfitting in laboratory-based studies. HQIC, with its emphasis on simplicity, is better suited for larger-scale applications such as the COVID-19 mortality dataset, where generalizability across populations is essential. Taken together, these criteria ensure that model choice is guided by both goodness-of-fit and reliability, making them relevant to applications in both biomedical and public health domains. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of statistical modeling and flexible probability distributions for analyzing COVID-19 mortality data and understanding the dynamics of the pandemic. Such investigations have provided valuable insights into disease progression, mortality patterns, and public health planning. Conclusions

This study introduced and investigated the PEm distribution, a novel one-parameter discrete model derived from compounding the Poisson and Emrem distributions. Analytical results established its key structural properties, demonstrating that increases in the model parameter correspond to systematic reductions in the mean, variance, and dispersion index, while simultaneously inducing greater skewness and heterogeneity. Such features highlight the model’s adaptability in capturing diverse distributional behaviors, ranging from symmetric peaked forms with heavy tails to more skewed and overdispersed profiles.

Estimation of the model parameter was carried out using MLE. A detailed Monte Carlo simulation study confirmed the robustness of MLE, with accuracy and precision improving under larger parameter values and sample sizes. Empirical coverage probabilities of 95% confidence intervals further validated the stability of the estimation procedure.

The practical relevance of the PEm distribution was illustrated through two applications: cytogenetic abnormalities in rabbit lymphoblasts subjected to streptogramin treatment, and daily COVID-19 mortality counts in South Korea. Both datasets displayed overdispersion, asymmetry, and heavy tails, features inadequately captured by the standard Poisson framework. In both cases, the PEm distribution provided superior fit relative to competing models, as evidenced by lower AIC and CAIC values, alongside higher chi-square goodness-of-fit p-values. These results underscore its ability to capture the complex empirical behavior of biological and epidemiological count data.

Taken together, the theoretical, simulation-based, and empirical analyses position the PEm distribution as a parsimonious yet flexible model for overdispersed and skewed count data. Its applicability to distinct domains ranging from cytogenetic dosimetry to pandemic mortality modeling demonstrates its broader potential as a versatile statistical tool in biosciences and public health research. Although the proposed PEm distribution demonstrates considerable flexibility and strong empirical performance, the present study has some limitations. The model is restricted to a one-parameter framework, and inference is conducted using maximum likelihood estimation only. Future research may focus on developing extended versions of the distribution with additional shape parameters, examining alternative estimation techniques, and applying the model to other classes of count data, including zero-inflated and regression-based settings.
