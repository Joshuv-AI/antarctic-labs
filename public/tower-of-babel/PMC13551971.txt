Association of Helicobacter pylori infection with elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and fasting glucose: a large-scale analysis of 83,054 participants in central China
Authors: Meng Xie, Jing Xia, Qin Zeng, Dai-jin Ren, Yun Gong, Yin-hui Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHelicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection is increasingly recognized for its extragastric metabolic effects. However, its specific association with lipid and glucose dysregulation remains controversial. This study aimed to quantify the independent association between H. pylori infection and key metabolic markers, particularly LDL-C and fasting glucose, in a large-scale Chinese cohort.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed 83,054 participants from a health management center in a central Chinese city (2021–2025). H. pylori status was determined by the 13C Urea Breath Test. Multivariable logistic regression was employed to identify independent clinical and metabolic correlates associated with H. pylori positive status, adjusting for age, gender, BMI, and a comprehensive biochemical profile.ResultsThe overall H. pylori prevalence was 26.55%, with an age-specific peak of 28.80% in the 50–54 cohort. After multivariable adjustment, elevated LDL-C emerged as the strongest independent correlate of H. pylori presence (aOR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.27–1.41, P< .001). Furthermore, fasting glucose was significantly associated with higher odds of being H. pylori positive (aOR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.03–1.05, P < .001). In unadjusted analyses, total cholesterol was also significantly higher in the H. pylori-positive group (P = .005); however, in the multivariable model its independent association was attenuated after adjustment for LDL-C (see Table 2).ConclusionH. pylori infection is independently associated with elevated LDL-C and fasting glucose in this large urban population. These findings underscore the role of H. pylori as a systemic metabolic stressor and suggest that H. pylori eradication may offer collateral benefits for cardiovascular and metabolic risk management.Keywords: Helicobacter pylori, Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, Fasting glucose, Metabolic syndrome, Epidemiology

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is a Gram-negative microaerophilic bacterium that colonizes the human gastric mucosa, affecting approximately half of the world’s population [1, 2]. It is well-established as a primary etiological factor for chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, and gastric adenocarcinoma [3, 4]. In China, despite significant improvements in sanitation and public health infrastructure over the past decades, the burden of H. pylori infection remains substantial, necessitating continued epidemiological surveillance and a deeper understanding of its systemic implications [5, 6].

In recent years, the scientific focus on H. pylori has expanded beyond the gastrointestinal tract [7]. Emerging evidence suggests that chronic H. pylori infection may act as a driver of systemic inflammation, contributing to various extragastric manifestations, particularly metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular diseases [8–10]. Chronic infection by H. pylori, especially the highly virulent Cag A-positive strains prevalent in East Asia, is thought to trigger a persistent low-grade inflammatory state [11, 12]. This systemic response, characterized by the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-alpha) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), potentially disrupts host lipid and glucose metabolism [13, 14].

Despite the growing number of studies exploring the link between H. pylori infection and metabolic markers, the results remain inconsistent and, at times, contradictory [15, 16]. Some clinical observations in Asian cohorts have suggested that H. pylori might sequester host cholesterol for its own cell wall synthesis, leading to lower circulating low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels [17, 18]. Conversely, other studies propose that the infection-induced inflammatory milieu inhibits lipoprotein lipase activity, thereby elevating serum triglycerides and LDL-C [19, 20]. Most existing studies are limited by relatively small sample sizes or a lack of comprehensive adjustment for confounding variables, leaving the true nature of the H. pylori-metabolic association a subject of ongoing debate.

Given the transitioning socioeconomic landscape of China, there is a critical need for large-scale, contemporary data to clarify these associations. The current study utilizes a massive cohort of 83,054 participants from a tertiary health management center in a central Chinese city. By employing the 13C Urea Breath Test—the gold standard for non-invasive H. pylori diagnosis—and a rigorous data cleaning protocol, we aimed to map the current prevalence of H. pylori across various age groups and investigate its independent association with a broad range of biochemical and metabolic markers. This study provides a robust statistical framework to evaluate whether clinical metabolic disturbances are independently correlated with H. pylori colonization status in a large urban Chinese population.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cross-sectional study utilized health examination records collected between January 2021 and December 2025. The data were sourced from the Health Management Center of Jiangxi Provincial People’s Hospital (the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang Medical College). The initial dataset comprised 117,677 individual records. The study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection and its association with metabolic and biochemical markers in a large adult cohort.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangxi Provincial People’s Hospital (the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang Medical College). The ethical approval number for this study is 2026(02). Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Data collection and standardization

Clinical and laboratory data were extracted from the electronic health record (EHR) system. Variables included demographic characteristics (age, gender), physical measurements (height, weight, systolic blood pressure [SBP], and diastolic blood pressure [DBP]), and biochemical indicators (total cholesterol [TC], triglycerides [TG], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], fasting blood glucose [GLU], total bilirubin [TBIL], and blood urea nitrogen [BUN]). Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2).

Data cleaning and quality control

A rigorous data cleaning pipeline was implemented to ensure the integrity of the multivariable analysis. The transition from the raw dataset (N = 117,677) to the final analytical cohort (N = 83,054) followed several sequential steps:

Outcome Standardization: The primary outcome was H. pylori status determined by the 13C Urea Breath Test (UBT) [21]. We implemented a robust text-parsing algorithm to standardize varied reporting formats across the study period. For records in 2025 that included explanatory parenthetical notes (e.g., defining the DOB ≥ 4.0 threshold), the algorithm was calibrated to isolate the primary diagnostic result and avoid false-positive classifications.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent Student’s t-test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (%) and compared using the 𝝌2 test.

To visualize the relationship between age and H. pylori prevalence, participants were categorized into 5-year intervals. Due to the limited sample size in the youngest cohort, participants aged 15–24 were merged into a single baseline category (< 25 years). Multivariable logistic regression was performed to calculate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for clinical and metabolic parameters associated with H. pylori positivity. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.10) utilizing the statsmodels and scipy libraries. A two-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant selection and characteristics

From an initial pool of 117,677 health examination records at the Health Management Center of Jiangxi Provincial People’s Hospital, a final cohort of 83,054 participants was included after rigorous data cleaning and quality control. The primary reasons for exclusion were missing 13C UBT results (n = 33,641), incomplete biochemical profiles (n = 1,367), and biological outliers (n = 38).

The overall prevalence of H. pylori (H. pylori) infection in this refined cohort was 26.55% (22,051/83,054). The baseline characteristics of the study population, stratified by H. pylori status, are summarized in Table 1. Participants in the H. pylori-positive group were significantly older (47.43 ± 15.20 vs. 47.01 ± 15.35 years, P < .001) and had a higher proportion of males (65.5% vs. 62.5%, P < .001) compared to the H. pylori-negative group. Additionally, the H. pylori-positive group exhibited significantly higher BMI, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) (all P < .001).

Age-specific prevalence trends

The relationship between age and H. pylori prevalence demonstrated a distinct non-linear pattern (Fig. 1). After merging younger participants into a baseline category (< 25 years), the infection rate showed a steady increase from 24.36% in the < 25 group to a prominent peak of 28.80% in the 50–54 age group. Following this peak, a gradual decline was observed through the 60s and 70s, with a notable late-life resurgence reaching 35.64% in participants aged 90 and above (𝝌2, P < .001).

Biochemical and metabolic associations

Significant differences were observed in the biochemical profiles between the two groups (Table 1). H. pylori-positive participants exhibited significantly higher levels of triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and fasting blood glucose (GLU) (all P < .001). Furthermore, total cholesterol (TC) was significantly elevated in the H. pylori-positive group in unadjusted analysis (P = .005), while high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) was significantly lower compared to the H. pylori-negative group (P < .001).

Discussion

The current study, involving a large-scale cohort of 83,054 participants from a tertiary health management center, reveals a Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) prevalence of 26.55%. Beyond the epidemiological mapping, our multivariable analysis identifies a robust and independent association between H. pylori infection and unfavorable metabolic profiles, most notably elevated LDL-C (aOR = 1.34) and fasting glucose. These findings provide significant evidence for the systemic impact of H. pylori infection in a contemporary Chinese population.

Global epidemiological context and shifts

The observed prevalence of 26.55% represents a significant departure from recent nationwide Chinese benchmarks (45–55% in mixed urban-rural adult populations) [5, 22]. While H. pylori remains a major public health burden, infection rates vary drastically by region, often exceeding 70% in parts of Africa and South America [1, 23], and dropping below 20% in many Northern European and North American populations. Our findings align with the lower end of this spectrum, suggesting that urban centers in China are approaching the prevalence levels observed in developed Western nations and other transitioning economies [22, 24]. This decline likely reflects the transition in public health landscapes in urban China, characterized by improved sanitation, the widespread adoption of communal dining hygiene (e.g., serving spoons), and increased access to eradication therapies. Furthermore, the data originates from a health management center, suggesting a “healthy user bias” where participants may possess higher health literacy and better socioeconomic status than the general public.

A distinct non-linear age distribution was identified, with infection rates peaking in the 50–54 age group (28.80%) [6, 25]. This “M-shaped” curve differs from the linear increase often reported in developing regions, where infection is typically acquired in early childhood and persists throughout life. The pattern identified here likely represents a birth-cohort effect; individuals in this age bracket were born during the 1970s, a period when hygiene infrastructure in China was still developing. The subsequent decline in prevalence among those in their 60s and 70s diverges from traditional models but mirrors trends seen in transitioning economies where older symptomatic patients have higher cumulative rates of clinical intervention and antibiotic exposure.

Metabolic and lipid implications in international comparison

The association between H. pylori infection and dyslipidemia remains a subject of international debate [15, 26]. Some smaller Asian cohorts previously proposed a “cholesterol-scavenging” hypothesis, suggesting that the bacteria consume host LDL-C for cell wall synthesis, thereby lowering serum levels [17, 27]. Conversely, several European studies have reported neutral or inconsistent associations between H. pylori status and lipid profiles. However, our multivariable model (N = 83,054) strongly contradicts these smaller observations, identifying elevated LDL-C as a highly significant independent correlate (aOR = 1.34) [28, 29].

This association is likely driven by the systemic inflammatory response, a mechanism that may be more pronounced in East Asian populations due to the dominance of CagA-positive H. pylori strains [11, 30]. These strains are known to trigger a more intense release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-alpha. These cytokines interfere with hepatic lipid metabolism by inhibiting lipoprotein lipase (LPL) activity and stimulating the synthesis of fatty acids, leading to elevated LDL-C and triglycerides [13, 31]. The strength of the association in our study highlights that the link between clinical metabolic profiles and H. pylori status warrants closer attention in clinical screening guidelines globally.

Association with glycemic dysregulation and insulin resistance

Beyond lipid disturbances, our study identifies a significant independent association between H. pylori infection and elevated fasting glucose (aOR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.03–1.05, P < .001). This finding is consistent with several large-scale studies in East Asia but contrasts with some Western cohorts where the link remains tenuous [32, 33]. The disparity between these findings may be rooted in the higher prevalence of the CagA virulence factor in Chinese H. pylori strains, which has been shown to exacerbate systemic insulin resistance.

Mechanistically, the chronic inflammatory state induced by H. pylori colonization leads to a persistent elevation of inflammatory mediators, such as TNF-alpha and IL-6, which interfere with the insulin signaling pathway [11, 34]. Specifically, these cytokines can promote the phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate (IRS) proteins, thereby impairing glucose uptake in peripheral tissues. Our results suggest that in the context of an aging and urbanizing Chinese population, H. pylori infection may serve as a non-negligible contributor to the rising burden of impaired fasting glucose and type 2 diabetes.

Implications for clinical practice and public health

The findings suggest that the clinical management of H. pylori should extend beyond gastric symptomatic relief. Given the strong correlation with LDL-C and glucose (aOR = 1.04), H. pylori eradication could be explored as a potential auxiliary measure in managing refractory dyslipidemia and metabolic syndrome. Public health initiatives should consider targeted screening for the 50–54 age cohort, where the infection burden is highest. Integrating H. pylori screening into routine metabolic health checks may offer a dual benefit: reducing the risk of gastric malignancies and mitigating systemic metabolic disturbances.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting our findings. First, due to the retrospective design utilizing an anonymized screening database, we lacked information on historical medical diagnoses (e.g., self-reported diabetes or hyperlipidemia durations) and baseline medication history, such as lipid-lowering therapies or oral hypoglycemic agents, which may shift metabolic profiles. Second, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) measurements were not systematically captured in this routine check-up registry, requiring reliance on fasting plasma glucose as the primary glycemic indicator. Third, although the 13C UBT exhibits excellent diagnostic accuracy, potential false-negative results due to undetected gastric atrophy or undisclosed recent antibiotic/PPI use could not be verified via histopathological assessment, as invasive biopsies are ethically unavailable for asymptomatic screening populations retrospectively. Lastly, our cohort selectively represents an urban, health-conscious screening population in a provincial capital city, which introduces a “healthy user bias” and explains the lower H. pylori prevalence (26.55%) compared to broader nationwide mixed-sampling benchmarks.

Conclusion

In conclusion, H. pylori infection remains a prevalent concern in a central Chinese city, with a distinct peak in middle-aged adults. The infection is independently associated with pro-atherogenic lipid shifts and elevated fasting glucose, emphasizing its role as a systemic metabolic stressor. Future longitudinal studies should prioritize investigating the impact of H. pylori eradication on cardiovascular outcomes and metabolic stability.
