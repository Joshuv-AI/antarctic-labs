Risk Factors for Radiation-Induced Oral Mucositis in Patients With Head and Neck Cancer Undergoing Intensity-Modulated Radiotherapy
Authors: Jiajie Tang, Runjuan Xu, Wenqiang Ji, Zhengchun Liu, Yan Peng, Guoyao Tang, Chenghui Lu, Xinxiang Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the incidence and risk factors of radiation-induced oral mucositis (RTOM) in patients with head and neck cancer (HNC) undergoing intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), and to provide evidence for early prediction and prevention.Subjects and methodsIn this prospective observational study, 134 HNC patients receiving IMRT were enrolled. The incidence, time to onset, and severity of RTOM were recorded. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed using SPSS 28.0 to assess the association between RTOM and 16 potential variables, including demographics, clinical parameters, oral hygiene index (OHI), tumour site, and mean cumulative oral radiation dose.ResultsThe overall incidence of RTOM was 79.9% (107/134). RTOM typically developed during the second week of RT, peaked in the third week, and most commonly presented as Grade 2 (45.5%). Univariate analysis indicated that tumour site, OHI, and mean cumulative oral radiation dose were significantly associated with RTOM (P < .05). However, multivariate analysis identified mean cumulative oral radiation dose as the sole independent risk factor (OR = 1.105, 95% CI: 1.016-1.203, P = .020), corresponding to a 10.5% increase in risk per Gy. Tumour site and OHI were not statistically significant in the multivariate model.ConclusionsPatients with HNC undergoing IMRT exhibit a high incidence (79.9%) of RTOM. Cumulative oral radiation dose is a critical independent risk factor. Although tumour site and oral hygiene were associated with RTOM in univariate analysis, they were not independent predictors in the multivariate model. Clinical management should prioritize rigorous dose monitoring and early intervention for patients who require relativley high oral doses.

Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) is the sixth most prevalent malignancy worldwide,1 encompassing nasopharyngeal carcinoma, oral cancer, hypopharyngeal carcinoma, laryngeal cancer, and others. Radiotherapy (RT) is a cornerstone in the management of HNC, playing a critical role in local tumour control and postoperative adjuvant therapy. By delivering high-energy radiation to directly destroy tumour cells or inhibit tumour proliferation, overall survival and quality of life in patients with HNC. However, due to the complex anatomy of the head and neck region and the proximity of tumours to critical organs, RT is frequently associated with a range of complications.

Among these, radiation-induced oral mucositis (RTOM) is one of the most debilitating acute toxicities. The incidence of RTOM in patients with HNC has been reported to range from 59.4% to 100%.2 Clinically, RTOM often manifests as erythema, hyperaemia, ulceration, erosion, and pseudomembrane formation. Early symptoms include xerostomia, taste disturbances, and painful mucosal erythema and oedema. As the condition progresses, patients may develop ulceration, erosion, bleeding, and dysphagia.3 Severe RTOM markedly impairs patients’ nutritional intake, oral function, and psychological well-being, thereby reducing their quality of life. It also decreases their tolerance and adherence to anticancer treatments, which can lead to treatment interruptions, dose reductions, or even termination of RT, ultimately affecting patient survival rates. Furthermore, RTOM significantly increases healthcare resource consumption due to prolonged hospitalization and supportive care costs.4

Currently, RTOM is considered to result from a series of interrelated and overlapping factors during tumour treatment. Clinical risk factors identified for RTOM in patients with HNC include poor oral hygiene, tumour site, and concurrent chemotherapy. However, existing studies have largely overlooked radiation dose distribution as the fundamental mechanistic driver. These studies were also limited by sample size, lack of quantitative radiation dose metrics, retrospective designs, and absence of intensity-modulated RT (IMRT)-specific dosimetric analysis, resulting in inconsistent findings regarding the true impact of risk factors, particularly in the contemporary IMRT era. To address this critical gap, we conducted a preliminary prospective study of 134 HNC patients undergoing IMRT to systematically characterize RTOM incidence and severity while rigorously integrating quantitative oral radiation dosimetry with clinical risk assessment. By clarifying these associations, we aim to provide data to support personalized risk stratification and preventative management.

Materials and methods

Patients

This prospective study consecutively enrolled 134 patients with HNC treated at the Department of Radiation Oncology, Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University, from January 2024 to December 2024, representing all eligible patients treated at our centre during the study period. The diagnosis of RTOM refers to the 2025 edition of the Expert consensus on the prevention and treatment of radiochemotherapy-induced oral mucositis.5 All patients underwent standard dental evaluation and treatment prior to RT, including oral hygiene education, panoramic radiographs, periodontal scaling, composite resin restorations for caries, root canal therapy, and removal of poorly restored restorations. Teeth with severe periodontal disease, periapical lesions, or residual roots and crowns were extracted at least 1 week before RT. At enrolment, all patients received standardized oral hygiene education emphasizing the use of soft-bristled toothbrushes and daily flossing; however, no additional intervention measures were administered prior to the oral hygiene index (OHI) recording at the fourth week of RT.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥18 years, excluding pregnant or lactating women; (2) pathologically confirmed diagnosis of HNC; (3) treated with IMRT; (4) provision of informed consent and willingness to participate in oral examinations and interviews; (5) presence of at least one functional tooth in each segment.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Multiple concurrent primary tumours or distant metastasis; (2) pregnancy or lactation; (3) recent use of antibiotics or prophylactic mucosal protectants; (4) cognitive impairment preventing cooperation; (5) pre-existing significant oral mucosal lesions prior to RT.

Data collection

Sixteen potential risk factors were investigated.

Demographic and clinical variables: Data regarding age, gender, pre-RT body mass index (pre-RT BMI), comorbidities, previous tumour treatment history, TNM stage, tumour site, chemotherapy status, and mean cumulative oral radiation dose (D mean) were extracted from electronic medical records. Lifestyle factors, including smoking status, alcohol consumption, occupation, education level, and previous oral mucosal disease history, were obtained via standardized interviews.

Oral health assessment: Baseline oral examinations were performed by a trained dental professional. All patients received appropriate oral treatment before RT, and this was documented. The OHI was assessed during the fourth week of RT (T4) using standardized criteria.

Classification standards

RTOM was graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. The toxicity grading of oral mucositis6 includes the patient’s pain symptoms, ability to eat, and need for intervention: Grade 0 = asymptomatic; Grade 1 = asymptomatic or mild symptoms requiring no intervention; Grade 2 = moderate pain or ulceration requiring a modified diet; Grade 3 = severe pain interfering with oral intake; Grade 4 = life-threatening, requiring urgent intervention; Grade 5 = death.

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation was performed based on the criterion proposed by Kendall M (1975) for multivariate analysis, suggesting a sample size of 5 to 10 times the number of independent variables. With 16 variables included in our study, the required sample size was estimated to be between 80 and 160. Considering a potential dropout rate of 10%, a minimum of 132 patients was targeted. Consequently, 134 eligible patients were consecutively recruited during the study period. The endpoint is the occurrence and grading of RTOM. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 28.0 software. Categorical variables were presented as case numbers (proportions) and were compared using Fisher’s exact test or the Chi-square test. Continuous variables underwent normality testing. Variables exhibiting a normal distribution were characterized by the mean ± standard deviation and were compared using independent samples t tests. Conversely, variables not adhering to a normal distribution were presented as the median (interquartile range) (M [Q1, Q3]) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Logistic regression models were applied to execute univariate analysis, with variables yielding P < .05 included in the multivariate logistic regression model. Statistical significance was set at P < .05 unless otherwise indicated.

Ethics

The study protocol conformed to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committees of Guilin Medical University Affiliated Hospital (2023YJSLL-18) and Affiliated Stomatology Hospital of Guilin Medical University (KQ-LW2023-013). Written informed consent was given by all participants. The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and to the STROBE guidelines.

Discussion

This prospective cohort study investigated the incidence and associated risk factors of RTOM in patients with HNC. Reported RTOM incidence rates in patients receiving IMRT vary widely (16.9%-81%).7,8 In the present study, the incidence was 79.9% (107/134), which is consistent with previous reports. Three patients in our study discontinued RT due to impaired oral function and reduced quality of life following RTOM, highlighting RTOM’s impact on both patient well-being and treatment adherence. The most common symptoms observed were dysphagia, xerostomia, discomfort, and taste disturbance. RTOM started at T2 and peaked at T3 (35.5%), with a cumulative incidence of 72.9% by the end of T4. The 107 patients with RTOM showed distinct grading patterns: Grade 2 was most prevalent (57.0%), followed by Grade 1 (33.6%), with no observed cases of Grade 4 or 5 (0%). This distribution may be explained by differences in clinical presentation and assessment methods. Grade 2, characterized by moderate pain or ulceration affecting oral intake, is more readily identified and reported, whereas Grade 1, often presenting as mild or asymptomatic erythema, requires clinical examination for confirmation to prevent missed diagnoses. The absence of severe grades (4/5) may be attributed to the time-dependent progression of RTOM, the use of IMRT techniques to optimizes dose distribution and reduces normal tissue injury,9 and rigorous RT planning and monitoring, which together help limit the severe toxicity.

Risk factors for RTOM are generally categorized into patient-related factors (eg, age, gender, pre-RT BMI, oral hygiene, and tumour site) and treatment-related factors (eg, radiation dose and technique). Previous studies have identified several controversial risk factors, including cumulative radiation doses, smoking, poor oral hygiene, diabetes, concurrent chemotherapy, and prolonged duration of RT.10, 11, 12, 13 However, existing RTOM studies remain predominantly retrospective, with limited systematic analysis of actual RT conditions. In this study, univariate analysis of 16 potential factors revealed significant associations between RTOM and tumour site, OHI, and mean cumulative oral radiation dose. Multivariate analysis confirmed mean cumulative oral radiation dose as an independent risk factor for RTOM (OR = 1.105). Notably, unlike historical NTCP literature based on 3D-CRT cohorts that often emphasized clinical factors as primary predictors, our findings in this IMRT cohort identify mean cumulative oral radiation dose as the dominant risk factor. This suggests that the technical precision of modern IMRT fundamentally alters the relative weighting of risk factors, where meticulous dose control to the oral cavity may overshadow the influence of traditional clinical variables that were significant in older, less conformal RT techniques. During RT, ionizing radiation (X-rays and γ-rays) induces lethal DNA damage in cancer cells via direct ionization and indirect oxidative damage mediated by reactive oxygen species. Reactive oxygen species generated via radiolysis of cellular water accounts for the majority of DNA strand breaks.14,15 Critically, this process is nonspecific and may also damage DNA and biomolecules in adjacent normal tissues, thereby contributing to treatment-related toxicity.16 The basal layer of oral mucosa maintains tissue homeostasis through continuous basal cell proliferation and differentiation, and its exceptional radiosensitivity renders it the primary site of radiation‑induced injury.14,17 When basal cell damage exceeds regenerative capacity, mucosal integrity is disrupted, leading to RTOM. Evidence indicates that irradiation at 15 to 25 Gy significantly reduces basal cell counts,18 with higher doses progressively exacerbating epithelial cell damage and apoptosis, thereby increasing RTOM incidence and severity.10 Notably, cumulative radiation doses exceeding 15 Gy trigger RTOM onset, with symptoms typically reaching full severity at around 30 Gy.19 Consistent with these findings, our results showed that each 1 Gy increase in radiation dose was associated with a 10.5% increase in the risk of RTOM (OR = 1.105). The incidence of RTOM also increased across dose ranges (15.5-32.2 Gy: 70.6%; 32.3-35.1 Gy: 71.4%; 35.4-39.8 Gy: 87.5%; 40.0-62.7 Gy: 90.9%). Given that increased radiation exposure to oral mucosa adjacent to the target volume is the primary risk factor,20 early preventive interventions are warranted in patients with HNC receiving high-dose RT to mitigate RTOM.

In univariate analysis, tumour site (P = .030) and OHI (P = .047) were significantly associated with RTOM. However, these associations were no longer statistically significant in the multivariate model after adjustment for mean cumulative oral radiation dose (P = .304 and P = .176, respectively), suggesting that their effects may be mediated through radiation dose distribution or confounded by dose factors. Anatomically, the oral mucosa is highly susceptible to radiation injury due to its rich vascularity and rapid cell turnover. Tumours located closer to the oral cavity inherently receive higher mucosal doses.21 In this study, RTOM incidence was highest in patients with oral or oropharyngeal cancer (100%), the lowest in those with laryngeal cancer (44.4%), and 71.4% in patients with hypopharyngeal cancer, consistent with the radiation sensitivity of the oral mucosa.21, 22, 23 Other studies have also reported greater severity of RTOM in oropharyngeal cancer compared with oral cancer.24 However, the incidence rate for nasopharyngeal cancer remains controversial,24,25 and uneven sample distribution may introduce bias. The OHI in RT is also associated with the occurrence of RTOM. Although RTOM is not directly caused by bacterial infection, poor oral hygiene may alter the oral microbiome, thereby promoting inflammation and secondary infection. Maintaining good oral hygiene may help reduce the risk of bacterial infection.26 This study utilized objective OHI measures rather than self-reports to assess oral hygiene, finding that higher OHI scores (indicating poorer oral hygiene) correlated with increased RTOM risk. This association may be related to microbial dysbiosis27,28 as well as to inflammation-mediated suppression of mucosal repair.29 At T4, patients in the RTOM group had higher OHI scores than those in the non-RTOM group (median: 1/2 vs 1/6). However, as OHI was assessed at T4, reverse causality cannot be excluded: severe mucositis pain may have prevented effective brushing, leading to higher OHI scores, rather than poor hygiene causing mucositis. Therefore, dynamic monitoring of oral hygiene status throughout the RT course is necessary to establish temporal relationships.

In this study, age, gender, pre-RT BMI, occupation, educational level, smoking status, alcohol consumption, comorbidities, previous oral mucosal disease history, chemotherapy status, and oral disease treatment before RT were not statistically significant, possibly due to limited sample size. Lower educational level is linked to higher levels of pain catastrophizing,30 however, sample imbalances in our study may have obscured this association. A history of oral mucosal diseases (eg, recurrent aphthous ulcers) could theoretically exacerbate RTOM by compromising the mucosal barrier.31,32 However, the limited number of affected cases and high disease heterogeneity require further investigation. Regarding smoking, although prior studies identify it as a significant risk factor for RTOM,33 our study failed to demonstrate a significant correlation. This discrepancy may be attributed to shorter cumulative smoking histories among patients or strict adherence to smoking cessation protocols during treatment – factors that illustrate the evolving nature of risk factor significance in the IMRT era, where precise dose control potentially diminishes the relative impact of traditional clinical risk factors.

Furthermore, while oral disease treatment before RT (eg, removal of root remnants, treatment of caries and periodontitis) did not reach statistical significance in our analysis, eliminating these infection sources is generally recognized to mitigate risk.34 Although guidelines recommend performing tooth extractions 10 to 21 days before RT,34 clinical practice often allows for a shorter interval (7-14 days). Notably, a previous study identified a higher number of pretreatment extractions as an independent risk factor for severe RTOM,35 suggesting that both the timing and extent of dental interventions require careful optimization. Chemotherapy was not significantly associated with RTOM in this study. However, previous studies indicate that concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) is an independent risk factor for RTOM,12 and is associated with a 2.69-fold increase in RTOM incidence, earlier onset,36 and a heightened risk of severe toxicity.37 However, the impact of combined targeted or immunotherapy was not assessed due to heterogeneity in administration schedules. Furthermore, while different chemotherapeutic agents (eg, cisplatin vs cetuximab) may exert distinct effects, a stratified analysis was not feasible owing to significant interpatient variability in drug tolerance and dosing protocols.

Limitations and future research

First, it was conducted at a single centre with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, oral hygiene was not assessed on a weekly basis during RT. Future studies should incorporate dynamic weekly evaluations to validate the impact of oral hygiene as a factor on RTOM. Finally, several potential confounding factors were not addressed in this study, which could influence RTOM development, such as differences in chemotherapy agents, tooth extraction time, nutrition intake, and whether the patients were hospitalized or not. Future studies are warranted to be conducted in collaboration with multiple centres to address these limitations.

Conclusions

Among HNC patients undergoing IMRT, the incidence of RTOM is relatively high at 79.9%, with Grade 2 RTOM being the most common and typically occurring at the T3 stage of RT. This prospective analysis identified mean cumulative oral radiation dose as an independent risk factor for RTOM. These findings suggest that precise radiation dose planning, particularly for tumours adjacent to the oral mucosa, combined with proactive oral hygiene management, may serve as effective strategies for RTOM prevention.
