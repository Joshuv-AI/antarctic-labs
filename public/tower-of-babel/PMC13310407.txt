Mechanistic Insights and Therapeutic Advances of Anti-Inflammatory Biologics in Immune-Mediated Glomerulonephritis: A Narrative Review
Authors: Mingjing Gao, Jianbo Qing, Ákos Géza Pethő, Xiaoling Xiong, Junnan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune-mediated glomerulonephritis represents a diverse group of kidney disorders caused by dysregulated immune responses and constitutes a major contributor to chronic kidney disease. Its pathogenesis can be conceptualized as a three-step cascade: aberrant activation of innate immunity through pattern-recognition receptors, inflammasome complexes, and complement pathways; adaptive immune dysregulation characterized by imbalanced B- and T-cell responses and the production of pathogenic autoantibodies; and terminal effector injury involving complement-mediated damage and progressive fibrosis. This narrative review summarizes current advances in anti-inflammatory biologics that intervene at these critical points across a broad spectrum of entities, including IgA nephropathy (IgAN), membranous nephropathy (MN), lupus nephritis (LN), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), minimal change nephropathy (MCN), anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) disease, ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV), and C3 glomerulopathy (C3G). Therapies including cytokine inhibitors targeting TNF-α or IL-6, B-cell–depleting antibodies such as anti-CD20, plasma cell–directed agents, and complement blockers against C5 or Factor B have demonstrated clinical benefits in IgAN, MN, and LN, where evidence from randomized controlled trials supports their efficacy in reducing proteinuria and preserving renal function. For other entities such as FSGS, MCN, anti-GBM disease, and C3G, the evidence remains preliminary, based largely on case reports, preclinical models, or early-phase trials. Despite therapeutic advances, challenges persist regarding efficacy variability, infection risks, and financial burden. Future research aims to achieve precision therapy through multi-omics–based stratification, next-generation biologics such as bispecific antibodies and gene therapies, and optimized drug delivery systems, steering immune-mediated glomerulonephritis management toward truly disease-modifying treatment.Keywords: immune-mediated glomerulonephritis, bispecific antibodies, immune responses, TLR4, targeted therapy

Introduction

Glomerulonephritis represents a group of glomerular inflammatory disorders induced by the interplay of immune-mediated mechanisms, host characteristics, and environmental factors such as infection. Among them, immune-mediated glomerulonephritis specifically refers to a group of diseases in which immune complex deposition, direct autoantibody attack, or aberrant activation of cellular immunity serves as the central pathogenic driver, leading to glomerular inflammation and injury.1,2 These disorders share a common pathological hallmark, disruption of the structure and function of the glomerular filtration barrier, involving key components such as endothelial cells, the basement membrane, and podocytes, resulting in clinical manifestations including proteinuria, hematuria, edema, and hypertension.3 As one of the major causes of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) worldwide, immune-mediated glomerulonephritis imposes a substantial disease burden.4 Persistent inflammation contributes to glomerulosclerosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis, ultimately progressing to ESKD requiring renal replacement therapy, thereby exerting a heavy toll on patients and healthcare systems.5,6

The etiology and pathogenesis of immune-mediated glomerulonephritis are multifactorial and complex,7 involving genetic susceptibility, infectious triggers, environmental factors, and immune dysregulation.8 Among these, immune mechanisms play a central role in the onset and progression of the majority of glomerulonephritis cases.3 Representative examples include IgA nephropathy (IgAN), the most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide, accounting for approximately 45% of primary glomerular diseases in East Asia and 20–30% in European and North American populations;9 lupus nephritis (LN), affecting approximately 50% of systemic lupus erythematosus patients globally; membranous nephropathy (MN); anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) disease, which constitutes 10–15% of rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis.10 ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV) with an annual incidence of 1.5–3.0 per million; minimal change nephropathy (MCN); focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), which accounts for 20% of adult nephrotic syndrome; and C3 glomerulopathy (C3G), a rare condition predominantly affecting young adults. All these entities are characterized by immune complex deposition, autoantibody-mediated injury, or complement dysregulation as shared pathogenic features (Table 1).11,12

The management of immune-mediated glomerulonephritis remains largely dependent on glucocorticoids and conventional immunosuppressants, such as cyclophosphamide pulse therapy or mycophenolate mofetil in LN, as recommended by the KDIGO 2021 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Glomerular Diseases.33 Although these agents can induce partial remission, their long-term use is associated with infection risk, secondary diabetes, osteoporosis, and limited efficacy in a subset of patients, highlighting the need for safer and more effective therapeutic strategies. In recent years, with the deepening understanding of the mechanisms underlying immune-mediated glomerulonephritis, targeted therapy has emerged as a new therapeutic direction. Studies on the molecular mechanisms of immune-mediated glomerulonephritis have laid the foundation for targeted interventions.34 First, in terms of innate immune activation, Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) recognizes endogenous damage-associated molecular patterns and triggers the MyD88/NF-κB signaling cascade, leading to inflammatory cytokine release and subsequent inflammation. Based on this mechanism, budesonide delayed-release capsules have been developed to selectively inhibit the TLR4/NF-κB pathway in intestinal mucosal B cells, thereby treating IgAN.35 Secondly, regarding complement system dysregulation, continuous activation of the alternative pathway characterizes C3G. Accordingly, factor B inhibitor Iptacopan (LNP023) and complement C3 inhibitor Pegcetacoplan (APL-2) are employed to treat C3G. Notably, although anti-MASP-2 therapy (narsoplimab) was initially explored for IgA nephropathy targeting the lectin complement pathway, the randomized controlled trial (OMS-721) failed to demonstrate efficacy and was prematurely terminated, underscoring the complexity of complement-targeted strategies in immune-mediated glomerulonephritis. Third, within the gut–kidney axis, intestinal dysbiosis promotes the overproduction of galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) via BAFF/APRIL signaling, leading to immune complex deposition and local inflammatory activation in IgAN. Based on this mechanism, Telitacicept, a fusion protein targeting both BAFF and APRIL, has been developed for IgAN treatment, while Blisibimod, a BAFF inhibitor currently under Phase III clinical evaluation, is being investigated for LN.36 Fourth, B cell–driven pathogenic mechanisms are central to diseases such as MN, in which autoantibodies directly attack podocytes, while imbalances in Th17 and Tfh cell differentiation exacerbate the inflammatory microenvironment. The classical CD20 monoclonal antibody Rituximab, which targets and depletes B cells, has become a first-line therapy for MN.37 However, approximately 30–40% of MN patients do not respond adequately to rituximab. Recently, obinutuzumab, a next-generation type II anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody with enhanced B-cell depletion capacity, has shown promising efficacy in rituximab-resistant MN, achieving remission in the majority of refractory cases.38,39 Collectively, these discoveries not only elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms of immune-mediated glomerulonephritis but also establish a foundation for individualized and precision-based therapeutic strategies.13

Therefore, this review aims to summarize the latest research advances in various kinds of immune-mediated glomerulonephritis, providing an in-depth analysis of disease mechanisms and treatment strategies to offer evidence-based insights for clinical management. By exploring the mechanisms of action and clinical applications of targeted biologics, we seek to promote a paradigm shift in immune-mediated glomerulonephritis therapy, from conventional immunosuppression toward precise immune modulation to deliver more effective treatment options for patients. Core Pathogenesis of Immune-Mediated Glomerulonephritis: From Common Pathways to Disease-Specific Mechanisms

Immune-mediated kidney injury exhibits both complexity and high conservation in its underlying mechanisms. Its central feature can be summarized as a “three-tier cascade”: dysregulated innate immune activation, adaptive immune imbalance, and terminal effector-mediated damage.40 This framework not only reflects the continuum from disease initiation to end-stage fibrosis in immune-mediated glomerulonephritis but also highlights common pathological nodes shared across different diseases. Innate immunity serves as the starting point,41 where uncontrolled activation of the TLR4 pathway, NLRP3 inflammasome, and complement system triggers inflammation and provides amplification signals for subsequent adaptive immune responses. B cell-mediated antibody production, immune complex deposition, and T cell subset polarization confer disease specificity and persistent damage.42 Finally, neutrophils, macrophages, and terminal complement attack complexes act in concert to drive the transition from acute injury to chronic fibrosis. Across diseases, conserved signaling pathways, such as JAK-STAT, BAFF/APRIL, and the complement alternative pathway, form a “core scaffold” of inflammation, showing consistent pathogenic roles in diverse immune-mediated glomerulonephritis contexts. At the same time, disease-specific mechanisms determine the precise therapeutic strategies: MN centers on the anti-PLA2R-B cell axis and responds to B cell depletion, C3G focuses on complement alternative pathway inhibition, and IgAN is primarily driven by the mucosa-kidney axis.14 Understanding these mechanisms provides a solid theoretical foundation for targeted therapies. This section will focus on how biologics targeting key inflammatory pathways and immune cells in immune-mediated glomerulonephritis can precisely intervene, overcoming limitations of traditional treatments and advancing translational and individualized therapy (Figure 2).

Common Mechanisms of Immune-Mediated Glomerulonephritis

Immune-mediated kidney injury can be conceptualized as a multi-layered, sequential activation and amplification of immune components. Its common mechanisms can be summarized as a three-tier cascade: initial recognition of danger signals by innate immunity, followed by adaptive immune activation that confers disease specificity and persistence, and culminating in terminal effectors that mediate structural destruction and functional loss. This framework helps explain why immune-mediated glomerulonephritis exhibits heterogeneous clinical phenotypes yet shares certain pathological features. Here, we examine the shared mechanisms across innate immunity, adaptive immunity, and terminal effectors to elucidate the common patterns of action in different immune-mediated glomerulonephritis.

Pivotal Role of the Innate Immune System

Innate immunity acts as the “frontline defense” and “bridging hub” in the development and progression of immune-mediated glomerulonephritis. Its primary function is not only to detect and eliminate danger signals via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that recognize damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), but also to amplify adaptive immune responses through the release of cytokines, chemokines, and the activation of the complement system.43 Recent studies have revealed the complexity of innate immunity, which encompasses both pathogenic and protective/regulatory roles. The classical mechanisms and therapeutic targets can be summarized in three major dimensions.

First, Toll-like receptors (TLRs), particularly TLR4, play a central role in innate immune sensing. Renal tubular epithelial cells release DAMPs such as HMGB1 and HSP60 following injury. Podocytes and mesangial cells recognize these signals through TLR4, activating MyD88-dependent NF-κB pathways and inducing pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-6.44 These mediators directly disrupt the glomerular filtration barrier and promote leukocyte infiltration. Animal models show that TLR4 knockout significantly reduces proteinuria and renal inflammation, highlighting its central role.45 Clinically, TLR4 or NF-κB inhibitors, including small molecules and siRNA therapies, are under investigation as potential modulators of the inflammatory cascade.46

Second, NLRP3 inflammasome activation represents another key innate mechanism. NLRP3 inflammasome activation requires two distinct signals: the priming signal, derived from pattern-recognition receptors such as TLRs, TNFRs, or IL-1R, activates NF-κB and transcriptionally upregulates NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β; the activation signal then triggers inflammasome assembly and caspase-1-dependent enzymatic cleavage, which is required for the maturation and secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 into the extracellular space.47,48 In IgAN, uric acid crystals promote NLRP3 assembly, while cholesterol crystals play a similar role in diabetic nephropathy.49 Activated caspase-1 mediates podocyte apoptosis and endothelial injury, thereby accelerating glomerulosclerosis. Urinary IL-1β correlates with pathological severity in IgAN patients.50,51 NLRP3 inhibitors and IL-1 receptor antagonists, such as MCC950 and anakinra, have demonstrated renal anti-inflammatory effects in clinical trials, offering a new avenue for precision therapy.52–54

Third, complement dysregulation is a shared pathway across immune-mediated glomerulonephritis, manifesting as sustained activation or regulatory imbalance. In the alternative pathway, C3 nephritic factor stabilizes C3 convertase (C3bBb), leading to persistent C3 consumption and characteristic features of C3G. In the lectin pathway, endothelial injury triggers MBL-MASP2 complex formation,55 initiating C4/C2 cleavage and complement cascade activation. Complement-targeted therapies have rapidly advanced, with C5 inhibitors (eculizumab, ravulizumab) and emerging C3 inhibitors (pegcetacoplan) demonstrating renal protective potential. However, MASP2 inhibition with narsoplimab has yielded disappointing results; the OMS-721 randomized controlled trial in IgA nephropathy failed to demonstrate efficacy and was prematurely stopped, highlighting that not all complement-targeted approaches translate successfully into clinical benefit.

Beyond classical pathways, innate immune cell immunometabolism, innate lymphoid cells, and macrophage polarization (M1/M2) also regulate kidney injury.56 M1 macrophages promote inflammation, whereas M2 macrophages facilitate repair, highlighting therapeutic opportunities for functional modulation.57 Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses have revealed the heterogeneity of innate signals within the renal microenvironment, providing a theoretical basis for precise, individualized interventions. Collectively, innate immunity acts as the first responder and critical hub connecting injury detection, inflammatory amplification, and terminal effector activation, with expanding translational potential for biologic therapeutics.

Pathogenic Axes of Adaptive Immunity

Adaptive immunity occupies a central role in immune-mediated glomerulonephritis, conferring disease specificity and shaping pathological manifestations. It translates non-specific inflammatory signals from innate activation into antigen-specific, sustained organ-targeted injury.58 The main effectors are B-cell-mediated humoral immunity and T cell-mediated cellular immunity, which synergistically constitute a “pathogenic axis” of antigen recognition, antibody production, and inflammation amplification.59

B cells contribute via circulating immune complex deposition and in situ immune complex formation. Circulating complexes, such as Gd-IgA1 in IgAN or dsDNA-anti-dsDNA in LN, deposit in glomeruli and activate complement, inducing neutrophil infiltration and GBM damage.60 In situ complexes involve antibodies targeting intrinsic renal antigens, exemplified by anti-GBM disease and MN (anti-PLA2R antibodies).61 Recent discoveries of THSD7A and NELL-1 further elucidate MN pathogenesis.62,63 Pathogenic antibody production arises from aberrant germinal center reactions, where APCs present self-antigens to B cells, promoting clonal expansion and affinity maturation. Therapies targeting B cells, such as anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies (rituximab, obinutuzumab), have shown efficacy in LN and MN.

T cells amplify adaptive responses via helper and regulatory functions. Th17 differentiation driven by TGF-β and IL-6 produces IL-17A, exacerbating tubular inflammation, while Tfh cells secrete IL-21 to promote plasma cell differentiation and antibody generation.64 Impaired immune tolerance, such as Treg dysfunction in LN, further perpetuates autoimmunity. Low-dose IL-2 therapy or IL-2 receptor agonists are being explored to restore Treg-mediated regulation.65,66 Overall, adaptive immunity not only determines disease-specific patterns but also serves as a foundation for precise, immune-targeted interventions.

Terminal Effectors as a Common Endpoint

Both innate and adaptive immunity ultimately converge on highly conserved terminal effector mechanisms, forming a shared endpoint across immune-mediated glomerulonephritis. In acute phases, neutrophils rapidly infiltrate, releasing ROS, MPO, and MMP-9, leading to GBM degradation and podocyte injury. Macrophages amplify inflammation through TREM-1 signaling, thereby sustaining the production of TNF-α and IL-1β.67 Complement-mediated damage is prominent in MN and anti-GBM disease, with C5b-9 MAC disrupting podocyte membranes, causing foot process effacement and filtration barrier loss.68 Complement inhibitors like eculizumab demonstrate efficacy in C3G and antibody-mediated injury, highlighting terminal complement as an actionable target.69

Chronic progression culminates in fibrosis, a shared endpoint across immune-mediated glomerulonephritis. TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling drives fibroblast-to-myofibroblast conversion, α-SMA upregulation, and ECM deposition.70 TIMPs inhibit matrix degradation, promoting interstitial scarring, while persistent renin-angiotensin system activation accelerates fibrogenesis, justifying long-term RAS inhibition in FSGS and MCN management. Emerging antifibrotic agents,71 including TGF-β neutralizing antibodies and Smad3 inhibitors, aim to interrupt the “inflammation-fibrosis” cycle. These terminal effectors represent both common pathological outcomes and universal therapeutic entry points, tightly regulated and amplified by upstream signaling networks to construct a cross-disease “inflammatory scaffold” essential for clinical intervention.

Cross-Disease Regulatory Role of Key Signaling Pathways

Although the initiating mechanisms differ among immune-mediated glomerulonephritis, their signaling pathways converge to form an “inflammatory scaffold” linking innate immunity, adaptive immunity, and terminal effectors. This convergence makes them ideal therapeutic targets.

JAK-STAT Pathway: IL-6 activation of gp130 on podocytes triggers JAK1/STAT3 signaling, suppressing nephrin transcription and inducing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT).72 In LN and FSGS, this amplifies inflammation and accelerates proteinuria. In diabetic nephropathy,73 high glucose via AGE-RAGE further activates JAK-STAT, forming a metabolic-immune coupling loop.74 JAK inhibitors like baricitinib have shown efficacy in reducing proteinuria and inflammatory markers across different etiologies.75

BAFF/APRIL Pathway: BAFF promotes transitional B cell survival, and APRIL enhances plasma cell IgA secretion. Their synergistic activation is prominent in IgAN, correlating with Gd-IgA1 levels.76,77 Atacicept blocks BAFF/APRIL, reducing Gd-IgA1+ plasma cell expansion and proteinuria, demonstrating cross-disease therapeutic potential.78

Complement Alternative Pathway Amplification Loop: C3b deposition forms C3bBb convertase, generating more C3b in a self-amplifying cycle. This mechanism is central in C3G and observed in anti-GBM and MN patients. Factor B inhibition (iptacopan) controls upstream amplification without complete terminal inhibition, reducing infection risk compared to C5 inhibitors.79 Notably, the alternative complement pathway has also emerged as a key therapeutic target in IgAN. Iptacopan received accelerated FDA approval in 2024, based on the Phase III APPLAUSE-IgAN trial, which demonstrated a significant reduction in proteinuria.80 Beyond small-molecule factor B inhibition, sefaxersen, an antisense oligonucleotide inhibitor of factor B mRNA, showed a 45% reduction with stable eGFR in a Phase 2 trial and is now advancing to the Phase 3 IMAGINATION trial.81 Ravulizumab, a long-acting C5 inhibitor, demonstrated clinically meaningful proteinuria reduction in the Phase 2 SANCTUARY study and is currently being evaluated in the Phase 3 I CAN trial. Additionally, other complement-targeted strategies under investigation include factor D inhibitors (eg., BCX10013), C3 inhibitors (pegcetacoplan, ARO-C3), and MASP-2 inhibitors targeting the lectin pathway.82,83 These diverse approaches highlight a rapidly expanding therapeutic landscape for IgAN, with multiple agents expected to gain regulatory approval in the coming years.84

Additional pathways include NF-κB, a core inflammatory hub, and TGF-β/Smad, directly driving fibrosis in refractory FSGS and MCN. Collectively, JAK-STAT, BAFF/APRIL, and complement alternative pathways constitute a cross-disease “inflammatory backbone”, explaining shared mechanisms among LN, IgAN, MN, and persistent inflammation in FSGS, acute nephritis, and MCN.

In MN, antibody production induced by PLA2R or THSD7A autoantigens is the core driver Antibody levels not only reflect disease activity but also guide the choice of immunosuppressive regimens. Rituximab, by depleting CD20⁺ B cells, can significantly reduce PLA2R antibody titers, and its role as first-line therapy has gradually surpassed traditional cyclophosphamide regimens.120 However, approximately 30–40% of MN patients do not achieve adequate response to rituximab. In these refractory cases, obinutuzumab, a next-generation type II anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody with enhanced antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity, has demonstrated promising efficacy in achieving immunological and clinical remission.38,39 Meanwhile, FcRn antagonists (nipocalimab) promote clearance of pathogenic IgG and have also shown potential in reducing proteinuria.121

The disease-specific mechanism of IgAN lies in excessive generation and glomerular deposition of Gd-IgA1, driving inflammation and fibrosis. Dual BAFF/APRIL blockade agents, such as atacicept, can suppress abnormal plasma cell activation at the source.122 Phase II studies have confirmed a significant reduction in proteinuria. Additionally, orally administered small-molecule mucosal-targeted therapies (eg., Nefecon), which regulate IgA secretion from gut-associated lymphoid tissue, offer individualized intervention strategies for IgAN.96

The pathological core of LN involves multi-site deposition of immune complexes and widespread T cell imbalance. Belimumab (anti-BAFF antibody) and anifrolumab (IFNAR blocker) have been approved for systemic lupus erythematosus and shown improvement in renal outcomes,100 demonstrating a dual approach of selectively blocking B cell overactivation and type I interferon signaling.106 In addition, therapies using low-dose IL-2 to restore Treg function and reshape the IL-2 pathway are under exploration, representing a new direction in T cell immune regulation.123

In anti-GBM disease, the specific pathogenic step involves antibodies directly targeting the NC1 domain of GBM type IV collagen α3/α5 chains.124 Standard treatment combines plasma exchange with immunosuppression, while emerging targeted therapies such as FcRn antagonists may rapidly reduce circulating pathogenic IgG, providing a new treatment window for critically ill patients.

In contrast, acute nephritis, including post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PIGN), is often triggered by exogenous antigens, leading to immune complex deposition and acute complement activation. Its self-limiting nature generally obviates the need for long-term immunosuppression, but short-term intervention at the complement activation stage has shown potential value in severe cases.

In FSGS and MCN, although the immune mechanisms are less typical than in MN and LN, adaptive immunity still contributes indirectly. Some FSGS cases involve autoantibodies against podocyte antigens such as nephrin or α-actinin-4, suggesting a combination of immune mechanisms and genetic susceptibility.125,126 Notably, Hengel et al reported that anti-nephrin autoantibodies were detectable in approximately 44% of adult MCN and 9% of primary FSGS patients, suggesting that a significant subset of these podocytopathies may represent antibody-mediated autoimmune diseases with potential implications for B-cell-targeted therapy.125 Corresponding targeted attempts include rituximab use in refractory FSGS, potentially stabilizing podocyte cytoskeleton or inhibiting autoantibody production.127,128 Additionally, complement activation has been implicated in a subset of FSGS patients with glomerular IgM and C3 deposition, and elevated plasma and urinary levels of C3a, C5a, and soluble C5b-9 have been observed compared to healthy controls Tissue-targeted complement inhibitors, such as C3d-Factor H fusion proteins, are currently entering early-phase clinical trials that include FSGS among their target indications, reflecting growing interest in complement modulation for this disease.129,130 The classical mechanism of MCN is thought to involve T cell secretion of soluble factors, causing podocyte dysfunction. However, the recent identification of anti-nephrin antibodies in a substantial proportion of MCN patients indicates that humoral autoimmunity may also play a role, complementing the traditional T-cell-centric paradigm.123,125 Emerging studies suggest IL-13 as a key molecule, and IL-13 inhibitors as well as JAK inhibitors are under investigation, indicating a trend toward shifting from traditional “steroid dependence” to molecular-targeted therapy.131

Targeted Anti-Inflammatory Biologics: Molecular Mechanisms and Clinical Translation

Building on the in-depth analysis of immune-mediated glomerulonephritis pathogenesis and key signaling pathways, the development of biologics targeting specific pathogenic nodes has become a central driver of clinical treatment paradigm shifts. This section systematically presents current and investigational biologics applied to immune-mediated glomerulonephritis, categorized according to their targets into three major groups: pro-inflammatory cytokine/receptor inhibitors, immune cell-targeted therapies, and complement system hierarchical interventions. These therapies interrupt the amplification cascade of immune-mediated inflammation at different levels: cytokine inhibitors directly neutralize tissue-damaging mediators; immune cell-targeted therapies deplete or modulate pathogenic B cells, T cells, and plasma cells, reducing autoantibody production at the source and restoring immune tolerance; complement inhibitors aim to control aberrant complement activation, a common terminal effector pathway.132 Clinical practice has gradually shifted from broad-spectrum immunosuppression toward precision interventions based on molecular disease subtypes. This chapter integrates the latest clinical trial evidence to detail the mechanisms, efficacy, target populations, and safety challenges of each class of biologics, and discusses emerging opportunities from gene editing and cell therapies for potential immune-mediated glomerulonephritis cures (Table 3).

Targeting Pro-Inflammatory Cytokines and Receptors

Dual Effects of TNF-α Inhibitors

TNF-α is considered a key upstream mediator in early inflammatory responses of immune-mediated glomerulonephritis By binding to TNFR1 on tubular epithelial cells, podocytes, and infiltrating immune cells, TNF-α activates NF-κB and MAPK signaling, triggers death domain assembly, recruits FADD and caspase-8 to form the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC), activates the caspase-8 cascade, cleaves Bid, induces mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening, and ultimately leads to tubular cell apoptosis.150 Animal studies suggest that elevated TNF-α levels correlate positively with the severity of tubulointerstitial injury and proteinuria. Clinically, TNF-α inhibitors such as infliximab and golimumab have been explored in subsets of patients with LN and IgAN. However, current evidence remains limited to animal models and individual case reports. For instance, the reported reductions in renal interstitial CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and IgAN-related proteinuria are derived from a single case report of IgAN secondary to Crohn’s disease (Wan et al, 2025) and cannot be generalized as representative clinical efficacy.133 Notably, the broader literature has also described infliximab as a potential trigger of IgAN or IgAN flares during IBD treatment, underscoring the need for caution151 Overall, robust clinical trial evidence for TNF-α inhibitors in immune-mediated glomerulonephritis is still lacking, and future studies should include controlled trials to establish their efficacy and safety in this context. TNF-α inhibitors carry notable risks, including reactivation of latent hepatitis B, necessitating pre-treatment viral screening, prophylaxis, and liver function monitoring.152 Injection reactions and autoimmune adverse events are also reported. Future directions focus on selective TNFR1 inhibition to minimize interference with TNFR2-mediated tissue repair, aiming for an optimal efficacy-safety balance.

IL-6/IL-6R Axis Inhibition

IL-6 signaling is upregulated across multiple immune-mediated glomerulonephritis types, particularly in LN and diabetic nephropathy, correlating with disease progression. IL-6 binds membrane-bound or soluble IL-6R to activate the JAK-STAT3 pathway, promoting podocyte injury, inflammatory cell recruitment, and glomerular matrix deposition, significantly reducing slit diaphragm protein expression. Tocilizumab, an IL-6R blocker, has shown ~38% reduction in urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) in diabetic nephropathy and trends toward anti-proteinuric and renal function benefits in small LN studies.136,137 Clinical application is limited by metabolism-related adverse effects, especially JAK2-STAT3-mediated dysregulation of lipid and glucose metabolism, potentially increasing LDL-C and inducing hyperlipidemia or insulin resistance.153 Combination strategies with PCSK9 inhibitors (evolocumab) or siRNA delivery are under investigation to mitigate metabolic burden.154 IL-6 blockade may also benefit refractory or relapsing cases by both suppressing inflammation and indirectly reducing autoantibody production, highlighting its role as a nodal intersection between inflammation and metabolism in stratified immune-mediated glomerulonephritis therapy.

BAFF/APRIL Dual Pathway Blockade: Precision Intervention in the Mucosa-Kidney Axis

BAFF and APRIL are essential for plasma cell survival and sustained antibody production and are upregulated in antibody-dependent immune-mediated glomerulonephritis, such as IgAN, MN, and LN155 Activation of NF-κB by this pathway promotes B cell differentiation and maintains the survival of memory B cells and long-lived plasma cells. Atacicept, a TACI-Ig fusion protein, blocks BAFF and APRIL binding to BCMA,141 inducing plasma cell apoptosis, significantly reducing serum Gd-IgA1 levels and mesangial C3 deposition in IgAN patients, indicating potential pathological reversal.156

Next-generation Telitacicept, with improved tissue distribution, enhances target engagement, reduces proteinuria, and improves renal function.91 Combined use with CD20 monoclonal antibodies further decreases relapse, demonstrating layered control of different B cell populations. Adverse effects include hypogammaglobulinemia and infection risk, particularly with long-term therapy, necessitating immunoglobulin monitoring. Future studies explore individualized therapy guided by circulating BAFF levels or B cell subset profiling (Figure 3).157

Immune Cell-Targeted Therapy: From B-Cell Depletion to T-Cell Modulation

B-Cell Targeted Therapy and Evolution

B cells contribute to antibody production, antigen presentation, and cytokine secretion in immune-mediated glomerulonephritis. Rituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, selectively depletes mature B cells while sparing precursors and plasma cells, effectively reducing circulating pathogenic antibodies. In MN, PLA2R antibody titers drop >70%, with partial or complete remission rates exceeding 60% at one year. Limitations include incomplete response, relapse due to long-lived plasma cells, hypogammaglobulinemia, and infection risk. Next-generation anti-CD20 agents (Obinutuzumab, Ofatumumab) with higher affinity and depletion efficiency are entering immune-mediated glomerulonephritis clinical trials. Combining B-cell depletion with plasma cell or BAFF/APRIL blockade may achieve more durable immunocontrol. In addition, felzartamab, an anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody, has recently shown encouraging results in the STARFALL study for membranous nephropathy, demonstrating the expanding role of B-cell and plasma cell–targeted approaches in glomerular diseases.

Precision Intervention in T-Cell Co-Stimulation Pathways

T cells support B cell antibody production and mediate direct cytotoxicity in immune-mediated glomerulonephritis. Activation relies on TCR and co-stimulatory signals, particularly CD28-CD80/86 and CD40-CD40L pathways. Abatacept, a CTLA4-Ig fusion, competitively inhibits CD28 binding, reducing T cell overactivation, improving proteinuria, and decreasing renal inflammation in refractory LN and some diabetic nephropathy patients.158 CD40-CD40L blockade (Iscalimab, CFZ533) shows early potential to reduce immune complex deposition and improve renal function in IgAN, LN and MN.143 Co-stimulatory blockade acts earlier than B cell depletion, potentially suppressing antibody generation and inflammation simultaneously, though efficacy varies and long-term immunosuppression risks remain. Biomarker-guided patient selection and combination with B-cell-targeted agents may enhance durable remission.

Plasma Cell-Targeted Therapeutics

Long-lived plasma cells sustain autoantibody production, contributing to relapse or refractory disease.159 Proteasome inhibitors (Bortezomib) induce plasma cell apoptosis, improving proteinuria in refractory LN, but are limited by systemic toxicity.160 Anti-CD38 antibodies (Daratumumab) and BCMA-targeted bispecific antibodies/ADCs, adapted from multiple myeloma therapy, effectively deplete pathogenic plasma cells and reduce autoantibodies.144 Emerging CAR-T therapy, particularly anti-CD19 CAR-T cells, represents a promising frontier for refractory immune-mediated glomerulonephritis. In individual case reports and early trials in refractory lupus nephritis, anti-CD19 CAR-T therapy has achieved deep B-cell and plasmablast depletion, with approximately 50% of patients attaining complete remission, anti-dsDNA antibody seroconversion, and stable eGFR over 24 months of follow-up.145 CAR-NK therapies are also under preclinical investigation. However, clinical experience remains limited to individual case reports and small case series, and long-term safety concerns, including the risk of graft-versus-host disease, hypogammaglobulinemia, and immune reconstitution failure, remain to be fully addressed.

Hierarchical Complement Intervention and Terminal Effector Targeting

Terminal Complement Inhibitors: Controlling End-Organ Damage

The complement system amplifies immune-mediated glomerulonephritis pathogenesis, with C5a/C5aR driving inflammatory infiltration and cell injury. C5 inhibitors (Eculizumab) successfully treat aHUS and antibody-mediated transplant rejection, and are applied in LN, C3G, and MN.146 Blocking C5 prevents C5a-mediated chemotaxis/neutrophil activation and C5b-9 MAC formation, protecting glomerular endothelium and epithelium. Long-acting C5 inhibitors (Ravulizumab) improve dosing and adherence.147 C5aR antagonists (Avacopan) show steroid-sparing efficacy in ANCA vasculitis.148,161 High costs and infection risks limit clinical use, but these agents remain critical for severe or refractory immune-mediated glomerulonephritis.

Advantages of Upstream Alternative Pathway Blockade

In some immune-mediated glomerulonephritis types, pathogenesis is driven by persistent C3/alternative pathway activation. Pegcetacoplan (C3 inhibitor) blocks cleavage, reducing C3a-mediated inflammation and the downstream cascade, showing proteinuria improvement in early trials.111 Alternative pathway inhibitors (Iptacopan, Danicopan) block C3 convertase formation while preserving classical/lectin function,79,110 effectively controlling inflammation.162 Iptacopan significantly reduces proteinuria and restores serum C3 in C3G and IgAN, representing a pathogenesis-based precision strategy.

Emerging Complement Targets and Gene Editing

Genetic and RNA-based interventions enable precise complement modulation. LNP-siRNA targeting Factor B/D or C5 reduces hepatic synthesis, decreasing complement activity; antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) target C3 or regulatory proteins.149 Combination strategies (B cell depletion + complement blockade, C5aR + Factor B inhibitors) show synergistic effects in models. More broadly, combination therapy is widely used and broadly recognized in the management of immune-mediated glomerulonephritis. As demonstrated in lupus nephritis, combining cyclophosphamide or belimumab with glucocorticoids can achieve superior clinical outcomes and higher remission rates compared with monotherapy, a principle also supported by the experience in kidney transplantation. By simultaneously targeting different immunological pathways, combination regimens may overcome the limitations of single-agent approaches, particularly in refractory or relapsing disease. Balancing maximal pathogenic pathway suppression with preserved basal immunity remains a core challenge (Figure 4).

Challenges and Future Directions

Despite their promise, anti-inflammatory biologics face three major clinical constraints: efficacy heterogeneity, safety concerns, and accessibility. Heterogeneity stems from the temporal-spatial variation of renal inflammation. Single-cell sequencing reveals NF-κB-high patients (TLR4/MYD88-upregulated) respond threefold better to TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242, reducing proteinuria by 37% versus 12% in placebo.163 Patients with dominant NLRP3 activation may benefit preferentially from IL-1R antagonists, while JAK-STAT-enriched patients have shown favorable eGFR responses to baricitinib. Gut microbiota also modulates responses: Gut microbiota composition may also modulate therapeutic responses, with emerging evidence suggesting differential efficacy based on microbial profiles.164

Safety remains a key barrier. TNF-α inhibitors carry a 3–5% TB reactivation risk; JAK inhibitors slightly increase lymphoma risk; anti-CD38 therapy can induce 9% acute uric acid nephropathy. Mechanism-based, proactive monitoring is essential.

Accessibility challenges include cost and adherence. Biosimilars (CT-P10) reduce annual rituximab cost by ~40%; oral JAK inhibitors (Upadacitinib) improve adherence to 92% versus 68% for injections.165 Notably, the high cost of novel therapies such as CAR-T cell therapy, which requires individualized cell manufacturing, lymphodepletion conditioning, and specialized monitoring, poses a significant barrier to widespread clinical application in immune-mediated glomerulonephritis. More broadly, the expanding arsenal of targeted biologics, including complement inhibitors, bispecific antibodies, and gene therapies, raises important concerns about long-term drug costs and equitable access, particularly in times of growing uncertainty about healthcare funding. Addressing this challenge will require coordinated efforts in biosimilar development, health economic evaluation, and policy-level support to ensure that therapeutic advances translate into accessible patient benefits. Sustained-release subcutaneous systems (IL-1Ra micro-pumps) allow up to 6-month dosing, minimizing clinical visits. Together, these strategies create a more affordable, manageable, and sustainable immunomodulation framework. It is also worth noting that targeted biologics for immune-mediated glomerulonephritis should be integrated with established renal-protective therapies. Agents such as sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), and non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) have demonstrated robust renal-protective effects across a broad range of kidney diseases and may serve as a complementary backbone to biologic therapy. Furthermore, sparsentan, a dual endothelin type A and angiotensin II type 1 receptor antagonist, has shown significant antiproteinuric efficacy in IgA nephropathy (PROTECT trial) and FSGS, representing a new class of disease-modifying agents that bridge hemodynamic and immune-mediated mechanisms. The integration of these renal-protective strategies with targeted biologics may further enhance overall renal outcomes.

Future breakthroughs rely on technical innovation and precision stratification. Bispecific antibodies that simultaneously target two pathogenic pathways represent a promising direction, though most candidates remain in early-stage development for nephrology indications. Of note, Izokibep is a mono-specific anti-IL-17A Affibody molecule currently in Phase 1/2 trials for psoriasis, and its potential application in kidney diseases remains to be explored. In vivo gene editing approaches (AAV-CRISPR targeting NF-κBp65) and smart delivery systems (CD44-targeted hyaluronic acid nanoparticles) have shown promising results in preclinical models, including enhanced renal drug concentration and reduced inflammatory mediators, but have not yet been tested in clinical trials. Similarly, engineered probiotics designed to induce gut immune tolerance and reduce Gd-IgA1 production represent an innovative preclinical concept for IgAN. These emerging technologies require further validation in clinical settings before their therapeutic potential can be fully assessed.

Future development requires: (1) multi-omics-driven stratified interventions; (2) mechanism-based safety management; (3) innovation in biosimilars, oral small molecules, and smart delivery. The ultimate goal is to shift immune-mediated glomerulonephritis therapy from symptom control to disease-modifying precision immunotherapy.

Conclusion: Toward a New Era of Precision Immunotherapy

This narrative review has systematically examined the pathogenesis and targeted biologic therapies across the spectrum of immune-mediated glomerulonephritis, organized around a three-tier cascade framework of innate immunity, adaptive immunity, and terminal effector injury. Based on the current evidence, the strength of clinical support for targeted biologics varies considerably across disease entities.

For IgA nephropathy, the strongest evidence supports BAFF/APRIL pathway inhibition (telitacicept, atacicept), targeted mucosal immunomodulation (budesonide enteric-release capsules, Nefecon), and complement alternative pathway blockade (iptacopan, approved based on the APPLAUSE-IgAN trial). In membranous nephropathy, rituximab is established as first-line therapy supported by the MENTOR trial, with obinutuzumab emerging as a promising option for rituximab-resistant cases. For lupus nephritis, belimumab and voclosporin have gained regulatory approval as add-on therapies, and anifrolumab is under active investigation. Complement-targeted therapies, particularly C5 inhibitors (eculizumab, ravulizumab), remain the cornerstone of treatment for C3 glomerulopathy and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome.

In contrast, evidence for targeted biologics in anti-GBM disease, ANCA-associated vasculitis, FSGS, and minimal change nephropathy remains more limited. For anti-GBM disease, rituximab is used in combination regimens, but no disease-specific biologic has been validated in controlled trials. In FSGS and MCN, the recent identification of anti-nephrin autoantibodies has opened new avenues for B-cell-targeted therapy, but clinical evidence is currently limited to case series and observational studies. For AAV, avacopan (C5aR inhibitor) has demonstrated steroid-sparing benefits, though broader biologic strategies remain under investigation.

Critical knowledge gaps persist in several areas: the long-term efficacy and safety of newer biologics (including CAR-T cell therapy), the optimal strategies for combination therapy, the development of reliable biomarkers for patient stratification, and the translation of promising preclinical approaches, such as in vivo gene editing and kidney-targeted delivery systems, into clinical application. Furthermore, the high cost of novel therapies, particularly cell-based and gene-based approaches, poses a significant barrier to equitable access.

Looking ahead, the integration of multi-omics data, including single-cell transcriptomics, spatial proteomics, and metagenomics, holds the potential to refine patient stratification and guide individualized treatment selection, although these approaches remain largely in the research phase. The ultimate goal is to transition immune-mediated glomerulonephritis management from broad immunosuppression and symptom control toward precision, disease-modifying immunotherapy.

It is important to acknowledge several limitations of this narrative review. Many of the discussed therapies are still under investigation, and their long-term efficacy and safety profiles remain to be fully established. For several disease entities, including anti-GBM disease, FSGS, and MCN, the evidence base is limited to case reports, preclinical models, or early-phase trials, and the quantitative claims derived from these sources should be interpreted with caution. Emerging strategies such as CAR-T cell therapy carry the risk of immune-related adverse events, including hypogammaglobulinemia and novel autoimmune phenomena, and require further clinical validation. Future updates will be necessary as more data from ongoing randomized controlled trials and real-world studies become available.
