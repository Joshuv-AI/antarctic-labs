Data integrity of the 223 randomized controlled clinical trials produced by multiple groups centered around a single author
Authors: Daan Langeveld, Buğra Sipahioğlu, Marlena Oebel, Steven Miletić, Roy de Kleijn, Marc Molendijk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe suspected unusual consistency in baseline variables and dropout rate in the randomized controlled trials (RCTs) produced by a team of researchers. We define this group of RCTs by the presence of an individual researcher in the author team, whom we call here the ‘key author’. We investigated the trustworthiness of these data and with that its scientific and clinical value.MethodsSystematic searches were performed in Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science for all the RCTs published by this author team. The final search date was 01/08/2024. Preregistered analyses tested for the presence of sampling variability in baseline characteristics of the RCTs and participant flow, operationalized as an even vs. odd dropout and an equal dropout, through the trials. Control data was gathered from RCTs that were performed by other groups but on similar topics as the investigated RCTs.ResultsWe found strong evidence that the expected sampling variability was not present in the 223 RCTs produced by this group relative to the expected uniform distribution (Bayes Factor [BF10] > 100000) and to what we observed in the 103 control RCTs (BF10 = 172). The proportion of even vs. odd dropout (e.g., 4 vs. 3) was .76 (95% CI = 0.70, 0.81) and different from the theoretical value of .50 (BF10 = 8.5×1011) and from what we observed in control RCTs (BF10 = 379). Equal dropout over arms of a trial was 3 to 4 times more likely in the tested trials, relative to what we observed in control trials (BF10 = 1.8×1016). The results were not explained by the method of randomization or sample size. Associations were mostly evident when the ‘key author’ had first or last authorship.ConclusionIt is unlikely that the RCTs by this team were generated through the process of randomization, and the flow of participants in these trials seems implausible. Hence, we question the scientific and clinical value of the RCTs that are produced by this team.Registration https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/8MTG6Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41073-026-00224-z.Keywords: Randomized controlled trials; RCT; Non-randomness; Data fabrication, data integrity

Introduction

A Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) is the most powerful method to generate data on treatment efficacy. This is due to the process of randomization, which leads to the random allocation of covariates over groups [1]. Nonetheless, sampling variability should be present following randomization. Absence of sufficient sampling variability after randomization can happen in the rare case of chance. Also, sampling variability could be absent for some variables in case the sample is drawn from a highly homogenous population (e.g., due to a restricted age range as an inclusion criterion) or when stratified randomization methods are used [2, 3].

Methods are available that can detect deviations from the expected uniform distribution of P-values representing between-group variability in baseline characteristics under proper randomization. Carlisle [2] showed unusual patterns in RCTs from a single author in his field of work, anesthesiology. His work indicated that it is unlikely that the investigated data were generated by a random process. Although his work did not prove error or fraud, further investigation did [2, 4]. Evidently, the applied methods aid in the appreciation of the value of RCTs. A systematic search (date 12/06/2025, in PubMed) learns that there have been some, but not many investigations using such an approach to value the data from RCTs [5–8].

A huge number of RCTs produced by a team of researchers surrounding a pharmacologist, whom we call the ‘key author’ from here on, made us suspicious of compromised data integrity. Together with the editor of Research Integrity and Peer Review, we decided not to report any names or affiliations but instead to present data, analyses, and arguments. Anonymity of data sources, however, cannot be offered or guaranteed. It is necessary to offer the reader transparency about the data and the sources that we used and hence the interested reader can seek contact with the corresponding author for details about the input data.

The reasons for us being suspicious were that this body of data looks too good to be true; groups seem extremely well balanced as are dropout rates and the results nearly always favor the treatment arm. Our suspicion was further fueled by our earlier investigations into the lack of placebo effects in trials performed by researchers related to this team [9] and by a retraction, initiated by one of us, of an article authored by researchers who are part of this team [10]. The RCTs that caught our attention mostly report efficacy of pharmaceuticals and nutraceuticals in the treatment of various types of psychiatric conditions (e.g., bupropion vs. methylphenidate in children with ADHD [11] or Crocus sativus L vs. fluoxetine for adults with major depressive disorder [12]). The output of the ‘key author’ we aim to investigate is very large (200+ RCTs) and consequential. The data is pooled in meta-analyses (e.g., in those on bupropion for ADHD [13] and saffron on major depressive disorder [14]), and these form the basis for clinical guidelines. These RCTs affect the lives of people.

Triggered by the size and impact of the output and inspired by the work of Carlisle [2, 4] and Mol [6, 15] we decided to investigate the RCTs produced by this particular ‘key author’ by means of frequentist and Bayesian analyses. We hypothesized that the baseline data in the RCTs of this group would not show the sampling variability that can be expected when the data is generated by simple randomization. We made another observation in the work of this group that struck us as peculiar: the dropout rate from their trials seems remarkably similar in the treatment and control arm and in a great deal of RCTs they seem to be an even number. This observation led us to investigate the flow of participants through the trial under the hypothesis that it does not follow expected distributions. In the end, we want to develop a better appreciation of the trustworthiness of these data and, with that, their value.

Methods

This review has been pre-registered (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/8MTG6, at the Open Science Framework, date 11/09/2024).

Data source, search, and inclusion and exclusion criteria

The objective of the search was to identify all published RCTs authored or co-authored by the ‘key author’. Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science were systematically searched for eligible studies. To increase our coverage, we additionally screened Google Scholar and the preprint servers BioRxiv and MedRxiv by making use of the name of the ‘key author’ as search term for potentially eligible RCTs not indexed in the databases. Full search strings and the rationale for their construction are provided in the online supplement. Two reviewers (DL and MM) independently conducted the literature search. After screening and initial selection based on title and abstract, full-text articles were evaluated against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. To be included, articles needed to: (1) be authored or coauthored by the key author, (2) report an RCT, and (3) be written in English, Dutch, Farsi (Persian), French, German, Spanish, or Turkish. Articles that were not based on original data were excluded.

Data extraction

We extracted baseline means and standard deviations on age and primary trial outcome measure(s) separately for the groups that were randomized. Proportions were extracted for gender distribution and level of education. To analyze the flow of participants, we extracted data on the number of participants who were screened, excluded after screening, randomized, and allocated per arm of the trial. Details on dropout after allocation were also extracted. In case RCTs displayed data on baseline variables only in a graph, values were estimated using Automeris software (https://automeris.io/). The data that were gathered by means of this procedure were validated against data from articles that reported data both in tables or text, as well as in figures. We found a correlation coefficient of 1.00 (df = 28) between the estimated and the recorded data. Hence, the estimated data were included. No risk of bias assessment was performed.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R [16]. Statistical significance was set at p <.05. One-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were used, against a uniform distribution, for the p-values on differences in baseline variables. Two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were used to compare empirical distributions. Besides being compared against the theoretical uniform distribution, the baseline data from the group of interest were compared to data from RCTs that we selected from meta-analyses on topics related to the RCTs that we aimed to investigate. These analyses served as a form of outcome-neutral test or control, as data from random RCTs should, in theory, not show significant deviations from what would be expected under randomization. This allowed us to demonstrate that the analysis performed in this review can reliably distinguish between data that deviates from what would be expected by chance and data that does not. For this purpose, the authors DL and MM independently extracted data from the RCTs included in meta-analyses on: (1) nutraceuticals and phytoceuticals in the treatment of schizophrenia (53 RCTs) [17], (2) antioxidants as supplements in the treatment of anxiety and depression (52 RCTs) [18], and (3) dropout rates in placebo controlled trials of antidepressants (68 RCTs) [19]. There were no RCTs that were authored or co-authored by the key author in the control dataset. Monte Carlo simulations were used to generate a p-value for differences in means of baseline variables. The Stouffer’s-Fisher method was then used to combine p-values for continuous and categorical variables. We looked at both p-values for individual variables as well as pooled variables. Dependence of variables within single RCTs was shown not to be problematic in simulations [20, 21]. The code used to compare the observed distributions of baseline data to those generated by Monte Carlo simulations is based largely on the simdistr package [22]. Subgroup analyses were performed with the data split up by: (1) randomization method, categorized as simple vs. block randomization (studies applying stratified randomization were a priori excluded for reasons of artificially high levels of between-group similarities at baseline), (2) year of publication, categorized as published in the periods 1998-2006, 2007-2014, or 2015-2025, (3) sample size, categorized as below the median sample size vs. the median or above this value, (4) evidence for the study hypothesis, yes vs. no, and (5) author position of the key author, categorized as first, last, and other author position. Details on the method used, the calculation of Bayes Factors (BFs), which was developed for the current purposes, are provided in the online supplement.

Analyses were conducted to investigate equal numbers of dropout over arms of a trial and whether dropout was an even (vs. an odd) number. Observed proportions on these variables were tested against the theoretical expected value of .50 for an even number of dropouts and against an empirical distribution that we gathered from the control RCTs. These analyses were repeated with studies that reported zero total dropout being excluded. By doing so, we wanted to exclude the potential overrepresentation of even-number dropout resulting from groups with zero dropout, which is related to sample size. In analyses with zero dropouts excluded, the theoretical value that served for comparison for frequencies of even dropout was set at .44 (which reflects 4/9, instead of 5/10 numbers that are even). Similar subgroup analyses were performed as the ones described above in the investigation of baseline variables.

Results

Search and selection

Our searches led to the detection of 223 (k) unique records that met our inclusion criteria. Details on the search, screening, and selection are provided in a flowchart (Figure S1) that is presented in the online supplement. The references to the included records can be requested through an email to the corresponding author. The records were published between 1998 and 2024. The ‘key author’ of the group that we investigated was first author on 35 RCTs (16%), last author on 163 RCTs (72%), and on the remaining 26 RCTs (12%) a contributing author, but not as first or last author. The RCTs were published in 65 different journals. The Journal of Pharmacy and Therapeutics (publisher Wiley, k = 23, 11%), Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology and Biological Psychiatry (publisher Elsevier, k = 12, 6%), and Clinical Neuropharmacology (publisher Lippincott, k = 11, 5%) were the most frequent outlets for their work. A quarter of their work (k = 56, 25%) was published in Iranian-based journals, albeit all were written in the English language.

The RCTS, the interventions, the patients

Among the included records were 2-arm trials (k = 218), 3-arm trials (k = 2), and 4-arm trials (k = 3), reporting on a total of 11072 patients. A total of 5460 patients were assigned to the intervention of interest and 5400 patients to an active or inert control condition. The remaining 172 patients were assigned to a third or fourth condition. The interventions were targeted at improvement in people (diagnosed) with: unipolar depression (k = 56, 25%), schizophrenia (k = 46, 21%), autism (k = 25, 11%), ADHD (k = 24, 11%), obsessive compulsive disorder (k = 18, 8%), bipolar disorder (k = 15, 7%), addiction (k = 10, 4%), sexual dysfunction (k = 10, 4%), Alzheimer’s disease (k = 5, 2%), and a mixed/other category (e.g., epilepsy, k = 14, 6%). Interventions were classified as constituting either pharmaceutical (k = 142, 64%), nutraceutical (k = 69, 31%), or pharmaceutical + nutraceutical (k = 12, 5%) treatment. Average age in years ranged between 5.82 and 77.73, with the youngest group children with autism who were subjected to risperidone (n = 13) vs. risperidone plus prednisolone (n = 13), and the oldest group people with Alzheimer’s disease who were subjected to saffron (n = 34) vs. memantine (n = 34). In almost all cases, patients benefited from the interventions, as only 4% (8) of the trials failed to reject the null hypothesis. In a set of 103 control RCTs of similar sample size from the meta-analyses by [17, 18], this percentage was 43% (44 RCTs) (Bayes Factor10 > 100000, p < 0.000000000000001). Here we report Bayes Factors (BF10) to indicate the level of evidence for the null hypothesis of no association (1-1/3 = anecdotal evidence, 1/3-1/10 = moderate evidence, 1/10-1/30 = strong evidence, < 1/30 = very strong evidence) or the alternative hypothesis (1–3 = anecdotal evidence, 3–10 = moderate evidence, 10–30 = strong evidence, > 30 = very strong evidence) [23].

Sampling variability between the different arms of a trial

The expected sampling variability was not evident in the RCTs produced by the team we investigated (BF10 > 100000, p < 0.000000000000001)(see Fig. 1). Baseline variability did not only deviate from the theoretically expected uniform distribution, but also from the control RCTs (BF10 = 172, p = 0.000005). Control data favored the null hypothesis of the presence of a uniform distribution (BF10 = 0.304)(see Fig. 1).

The cumulative distribution of p-values of the baseline variables of 223 RCTs published by S.A. and his team, as well as 96 control RCTs by other authors. Control RCTs come from meta-analyses reporting on interventions and patient populations similar as the ‘key author’ and their team report on. The diagonal (dotted line) represents the expected uniform distribution

We compared the distribution of pooled p-values for RCTs using simple randomization to those using block randomization, as far as we could infer the exact randomization method from the articles. Both distributions deviated from the expected uniform distribution, p values < 0.000001, but did not differ from each other (see Fig. 2, panel A). Publication year was associated with the distribution of p-values for the RCTs produced by the group of interest (see Fig. 2 panel B), with more recent publications showing smaller deviation from the expected distribution. We analyzed potential effects of sample size, with the data produced by the group of the key author categorized above and below the median sample size of n = 25 per arm, with ties being assigned 50/50 to both values above and below the median. These analyses showed that in both groups the distribution of p-values deviated from the expected uniform distribution without there being a difference between them (see Fig. 2 panel C). The distribution of p-values did not differ based on whether the study hypothesis was confirmed or not. It should be noted that there was little variance in this variable, making it unlikely to detect any association even if it truly existed in the data. The distribution of pooled p-values differed depending on the author position of the key author. For RCTs where the ‘key author’ held an authorship somewhere in the middle, the distribution of pooled p-values followed the expected uniform distribution. When the key author was either the first or last author, the distribution did not follow the expected uniform distribution, p-values < 0.000001 (see Fig. 2 panel D).

Flow of participants through the trial

Investigating the flow of participants through the RCTs, we found that the probability of an even versus odd number of total dropouts was .76 (95% CI = 0.70, 0.81). The data contain extremely strong evidence that this probability differs from the expected probability of .50 (BF10 > 10000, p < .0001) and from what we observed in control RCTs (.56, 95% CI = 0.47, 0.64, BF10 = 379)(see Figure S5). The observed effect remained evident when we excluded studies that reported even dropout based on zero dropout. Zero dropout was observed in 25 out of 215 RCT’s (12%) (data on dropout was not reported in 8 RCT’s). In the control data, zero dropout was observed in 10 of the 103 RCT’s (9%).

The proportion of trials that reported an equal number of dropouts over the intervention and control arm (e.g., 2 and 2) was also high in the RCTs produced by the team (.65, 95% CI = .59, .71) vs. the frequency of this event in the control RCTs (.19, 95% CI = .13, 0,24 pdiff <.0001, BF10 = 1.8×1016). Results remained similar after excluding studies with zero dropouts.

Randomization method, sample size, and whether the study hypothesis was confirmed or not were not associated with the dropout variables, but year of publication and author position were. Even and similar dropout were mostly evident for papers published in more recent years (Kendall’s tau = 0.15, p <.001, BF10 = 23.13 and Kendall’s tau = 0.17, p <.001, BF10 = 105.27 respectively) and when the ‘key author’ served as last author of the article (Chi-square = 25.87, p <.001, BF10 = 4489.07 and Chi-square = 33.07, p <.001, BF10 > 100000 respectively). Note that the variables author position and year of publication were related to each other, as over the years, there was a gradual shift for the ‘key author’ to have last versus first authorships on papers. Even dropout and similar dropout are obviously also related variables, as similar dropout over arms of a trial always results in an even number.

Discussion

We tested the hypothesis that in the RCTs from a team of researchers centered around a single author, trial figures would deviate from the expected distribution. We also investigated the flow of participants in their RCTs under the hypothesis that this is unusually consistent within and over trials. The data from all the RCTs published by this team, 223 in total, confirm our hypotheses. The expected distribution of sampling variability that should be present is absent in their RCTs, and the dropout of participants is so remarkably similar within and over trials that manipulation of it seems likely. Hence, we question the validity of the body of data we investigated and believe that theoretical advancement, clinical decision making, and policy cannot be built upon it.

Sampling variability and dropout in the investigated trials

The absence of sampling variability was observed relative to the expected ‘uniform’ distribution and control RCTs. It was observed regardless of potential effect moderators, such as randomization method. This suggests that the investigated data were not generated by the process of randomization, while they are presented as RCTs. The exception here are the minority of trials (~10%) in which the key author had an author position ‘in the middle’ and did not serve as first or last author, as in these RCTs, the expected sampling variability was present.

We find that in the RCTs published by this team; dropout was more often similar over intervention and control arms than in control RCTs. Over studies, we show that the proportion of trials with an even vs. an odd number of dropouts was much higher than what can be expected from a theoretical distribution and to what we observed in control RCTs. These deviations were more evident when the key author was last, and by extension, the senior author. In contrast to the observations relating to sampling variability at baseline, we observed a higher proportion of similar dropout over arms and an even number of dropouts in more recently published RCTs than older ones.

What could be the mechanisms underlying the observed associations?

Our findings show that the investigated RCT data are unlikely to be the result of randomization, and hence they are extremely unlikely to represent proper RCTs. Our findings also suggest that the dropout patterns reported on are manipulated. Before submitting this work, we informed the ‘key author’ about our findings and asked what could have been the mechanisms underlying the observed associations. The key author argued that the RCTs typically have a small sample size and that they apply narrow inclusion and exclusion criteria. As such, a homogenous group is selected from the population of interest, and when random allocation takes place from such a group, subgroups result that are homogenous too. This is not an explanation for the data at hand. There is variability in the data, as we know it from the Standard Deviations (SDs) describing the distributions of baseline variables. Importantly, the analyses that we report on account for variability by incorporating SD and sample size. Up till now, the ‘key author’ did not share with us a potential explanation for the consistency of dropout patterns in the investigated trials.

Our approach to investigating dropout patterns, which consisted of binomial tests against theoretical values and cross-tabulations, is straightforward. Its results are compelling. In our view, there are only a few possible explanations for them. One explanation would be that we report chance findings, which is unlikely given the extremely small p-values on these associations. Another explanation that we can think of is that the reported dropout rates are manipulated (or beautified in this case; people prefer even over odd numbers [24] and equal dropout is a desirable characteristic of an RCT [1]. We do know of a study that addresses a related issue, albeit using a different approach. Bolland et al. [25] found that the number of people who withdrew from the control and intervention conditions of a trial was much more similar over arms in RCTs with than without integrity concerns.

Most of the investigated RCTs that were produced by the ‘key author’ and their team reported results that were in line with the study hypothesis (96%). In a set of control RCTS of similar sample size, this percentage was much lower (43%). This observation also suggests manipulation or beautification of data, although the observation could also result from publication bias. If this is the case, the team only submitted RCTs with positive results to journals for consideration for publication, and they left a huge number of manuscripts on RCTs with null results in the file drawers at their institutes.

Our observations of a lack of variability in baseline variables and the highly consistent dropout rates, could be issues of research or researcher integrity. Research integrity refers to any conduct of a study that invalidates the study's findings. Researcher integrity refers to the invalidation of study findings due to deliberate misconduct by the researchers conducting the study (e.g., falsification/beautification [26, 27]. The fact that deviant patterns were mostly evident in case the ‘key author’ holds first or last authorship suggests a case of researcher integrity. First and last authors often have access to data; in most of the cases they perform the analyses and draft the article, including flowcharts and tables with baseline descriptive data. They stand out from authors who have other positions on a paper in that they can more easily manipulate aspects of a study. This could suggest a direct role of the key author in generating the observed deviant patterns, although we do not prove this.

The analyses and results on the lack of between-group variability at baseline are complex and need consideration. Mascha et al. [3] noted that earlier studies assessing between-condition variability assumed that simple randomization was used in the input studies, without verifying this. Obviously, in case studies use a randomization method that is developed to increase the likelihood of matched samples (e.g., stratification), there will be less variability between groups when compared to simple randomization. We took this into account and found equally strong evidence for both randomization methods. The Stouffer-Fisher method that we applied works under the assumption of independence between variables that come from the same input study. This assumption is often violated (e.g., height and weight). However, in a recent simulation study, Bolland et al. [28] showed that in case baseline variables correlate to the extent that they typically do in clinical studies, this does not influence the outcome. Another consideration is that comparisons against theoretical criteria are prone to false positive findings [3]. We could counter this by also creating a control data set that served as an empirical criterion.

Limitations

Our approach has some limitations. First, the comparisons with the control RCTs may have been compromised by the fact that they differed from the investigated data on more than just the author team, although we aimed to match for topic and sample size. Second, our conclusions apply to the body of data that we investigated and not to individual studies. This is also why we present data without having flagged individual articles to the editors of the journals where this work is published. Notwithstanding this, for us, the trustworthiness of each individual study produced by this group is at stake, whether we can prove this or not. Or, and paraphrasing Professor Ben Mol, pioneer in the assessment of trustworthiness of RCT data (23/10/2025); Imagine the Royal Dutch Airline Company would fabricate 30% of their airplane maintenance reports, all their planes would be grounded overnight.

Integrity assessments receive an increasing amount of attention. Nielsen et al. [15] published a comprehensive approach in the investigation of data integrity of single authors or groups of authors. And experts have recently published a tool (the INSPECT-SR tool) with which the trustworthiness of RCTs can be routinely checked [29]. We did not use this tool because we only got hold of it after drafting our preregistration. Notwithstanding this, our work aligns with the work of these authors, albeit our approach is less comprehensive. A comparison of the trustworthiness tool and our assessment may be worthwhile for the further cross-validation of both approaches. What we could add to the toolbox investigating post-publication data is R code for Bayesian assessment of variability in baseline variables and a straightforward approach for the assessment of the trustworthiness of dropout data. Finally, we believe we are the first to analyze baseline and dropout data relative to both theoretical and empirical criteria.

Relevance and future work

Results from RCTs translate into recommendations and treatment guidelines, and these carry forward in the lives of people. The investigated data is widely used to this end. Given that the RCTs almost equivocally report intervention efficacy, it can be imagined that the investigated work puts a large stamp on the conclusions derived from systematic reviews. Although the investigated group primarily reports on the clinical efficacy of pharmaceuticals, their footprint in the field of studies investigating the efficacy of nutraceuticals is notable simply because few research groups are interested in this. It is easy to find actual examples. The treatment guidelines by the World Federation of Societies of Biological Psychiatry [30], for instance, provisionally recommend Crocus sativus L. (saffron) in the treatment of unipolar depression. We estimate that 1/3 of the trials investigating saffron vs. placebo and 2/3 of the trials investigating saffron vs. an active comparison [14] come from collaborations with the ‘key author’ of the investigated data.

Papers produced by paper mills contaminate the literature when left undetected [31]. In addition, in the area where the data that we investigated are produced, there is a particular big and active market that sells theses and articles [32]. We do not know whether the investigated work is part of this business, but this is a working hypothesis. A growing concern is that low-quality, fraudulent, or even bogus manuscripts are disseminated in massive amounts by predatory journals [33] while no, or no solid peer-review took place [34]. The output by the investigated group in contrast typically does not end up in predatory journals, but rather in titles governed by publishers such as Elsevier and Wiley. For this reason, we find it striking that we are the first to flag this huge body of RCTs as being potentially untrustworthy.

What should or can be done with the investigated data from here? To prove that the data is fabricated or manipulated, further investigation is needed. The Committee of Publication Ethics (COPE) provides a way forward. Given that most of the investigated articles are published in Journals that adhere to the guidelines set forth by this committee, we follow their advice. Upon publication of this manuscript, we will seek contact with the editors of the journals in which the investigated work is published and inform them about our findings. Following the COPE guidelines, we expect that the editors will investigate individual cases in a prespecified order: (I) contact the authors with all details about the case, (II) an assessment of the author response, (III) when necessary, inform the research integrity office of the University or the institute, and (IV) the editor decides what to do with an individual record.

Conclusion

We show that it is unlikely that the investigated data were generated by the process of randomization. We also show that the flow of participants through the investigated RCTs does not follow a natural pattern. We have learned to be distrustful when signs like these are present, and for us and for now, data manipulation or even fabrication is a candidate explaining our observations. Reaching out to the ‘key author’ of the group that produced the investigated data led to no further insights into the nature of the process(es) that generated the data. Hence, we question the validity of the RCTs by the ‘key author’ and their team, and until further clarification, we caution against using their data.

In case people are interested in our data sources, they can seek contact with the corresponding author at molendijkml@fsw.leidenuniv.nl and a reference list of the investigated RCTs will be sent in return. The reason for us not sharing this material here with this publication is that, with the editor it was decided to emphasize the data and our arguments, and not the individual researchers and the institutes where they work.
