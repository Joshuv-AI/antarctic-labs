Abstract




Double-scaled bosonic and fermionic embedded ensembles, complex SYK, and the dual Hilbert space

Jarod Tall and Steven Tomsovic

Abstract

We derive the density of states along with the
2
- and
4
-point functions of embedded ensembles for both fermions and bosons in the double-scaled limit. It is shown the models are equivalent to the double-scaled Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev model, expanding the double-scaled universality class to include both fermionic and bosonic systems. The models can be solved by introducing the Wick product of non-commuting Gaussian random variables. We show that deriving the Wick product is sufficient for computing the density of states, and properties of the Wick product can be used to compute
n
-point functions directly in the energy basis. In this context, the Wick product is equivalent to normal ordering of
q
-oscillators, which leads to the duality between moments of double-scaled models and expectation values in the chord Hilbert space. By considering operator probes as a second set of oscillators, we extend the duality to compute
n
-point functions. Embedded ensembles are equivalent to complex SYK at fixed charge, and we show working directly with embedded ensembles streamlines the derivations.

Hamiltonians of quantum chaotic systems are often modeled by the Gaussian ensembles (GE) of random Hermitian matrices

Double-scaled fermionic and bosonic EGUE

q_{\pm}
. Contrary to prior derivations for other double-scaled models, our proof does not rely on the arguments of
[
30
]
. The derivation essentially follows from the moment method introduced in
[
7
]
and unitary group decompositions of
p
-body operators introduced in
[
16
,
9
]
. An immediate corollary of the result is that the moments of DS-EGUE, for both fermions and bosons, are exactly equivalent to DS-SYK with
q_{SYK}\to q_{\pm}
.

2.1
Fermions

Consider

N

D_{f}=\binom{N}{m}
and the relevant operators are
{}_{i}:\hskip 2.84544pti\in(1,\dots,N)

\{{}_{i},{}^{\dagger}_{j}\}=\delta_{i,j},\hskip 14.22636pt\{{}_{i},{}_{j}\}=\{{}^{\dagger}_{i},{}^{\dagger}_{j}\}=0.

(2.1)

It is useful to define a product of fermion operators as

{}^{\dagger}_{p,I}={}^{\dagger}_{i_{p}}\dots{}^{\dagger}_{i_{1}},\hskip 14.22636pt{}_{p,I}={}_{i_{1}}\dots{}_{i_{p}}

(2.2)

\ket{\mu}={}^{\dagger}_{\mu_{m}}\dots{}^{\dagger}_{\mu_{1}}\ket{0}={}^{\dagger}_{m,\mu}\ket{0}.

\bra{\nu}H\ket{\mu}|_{p=m}=J_{\nu,\mu}.

\displaystyle

To do this, note that a generic

[\chi_{i},\chi^{\dagger}_{j}]=\delta_{i,j},\hskip 14.22636pt[\chi_{i},\chi_{j}]=[\chi^{\dagger}_{i},\chi^{\dagger}_{j}]=0.

(2.22)

products of bosonic operators are defined as

\chi_{p,I}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\chi_{i}^{p_{i}}}{\sqrt{p_{i}!}},\hskip 14.22636pt\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}=p,

(2.23)

where the occupation number of the
i^{th}
site is defined as
p_{i}
, and
I
denotes the set of occupation numbers. The basis vectors of the Hilbert space are

\ket{\mu}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(\chi^{\dagger}_{i})^{m_{i}}}{\sqrt{m_{i}!}}\ket{0},\hskip 14.22636pt\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}=m.

(2.24)

The Hamiltonian can be defined as a constrained sum over occupation numbers,

H=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}+\dots+p_{N}=p\\
p^{\prime}_{1}+\dots+p^{\prime}_{N}=p\end{subarray}}J_{I,L}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(\chi_{i}^{\dagger})^{p_{i}}}{\sqrt{p_{i}!}}\frac{\chi_{i}^{p^{\prime}_{i}}}{\sqrt{p^{\prime}_{i}!}}=\sum_{I,L}J_{I,L}\chi^{\dagger}_{p,I}\chi_{p,L}

(2.25)

where
J_{I,L}
is a GUE random matrix with a dimensionality of
\binom{N+p-1}{p}
and second moment

\expectationvalue{J_{I,L}J_{I',L'}}_{J}=\binom{m}{p}^{-1}\binom{N+m-1}{p}^{-1}\delta_{I,L^{\prime}}\delta_{L,I^{\prime}}.

(2.26)

which is chosen such that the second moment of the Hamiltonian is one. The bosonic embedded ensemble also reduces to the GUE when
p=m
,

\bra{\nu}H\ket{\mu}|_{p=m}=J_{\nu,\mu}.

(2.27)

The following identities are necessary for computing the moments,

\displaystyle\sum_{I}\chi^{\dagger}_{p,I}\chi_{p,I}\ket{\mu}=\sum_{p_{1}+\dots+p_{N}=p}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\binom{m_{i}}{p_{i}}\ket{\mu}=\binom{m}{p}\ket{\mu}\hskip 2.84544pt\rightarrow\sum_{I}\chi^{\dagger}_{p,I}\chi_{p,I}=\binom{\hat{n}}{p}

(2.28)

\displaystyle\sum_{I}\chi_{p,I}\chi^{\dagger}_{p,I}\ket{\mu}=\sum_{p_{1}+\dots+p_{N}=p}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\binom{m_{i}+p_{i}}{p_{i}}\ket{\mu}=\binom{N+m+p-1}{p}\ket{\mu}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\sum_{I}\chi_{p,I}\chi^{\dagger}_{p,I}=\binom{N+\hat{n}+p-1}{p},

where the Vandermonde identity was used in both lines. The second moment can then be solved for:

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{H^2}

\displaystyle=\binom{m}{p}^{-1}\binom{N+m-1}{p}^{-1}\sum_{I,J}\expectationvalue{\chi^{\dagger}_{I,p}\chi_{J,p}\chi^{\dagger}_{J,p}\chi_{I,p}}_{m}

(2.29)

\displaystyle=\binom{m}{p}^{-1}\binom{N+m-1}{p}^{-1}\sum_{I}\chi^{\dagger}_{I,p}\expectationvalue{\chi^{\dagger}_{I,p}\binom{N+\hat{n}+p-1}{p}\chi_{I,p}}\chi_{I,p}_{m}

\displaystyle=\binom{m}{p}^{-1}\sum_{I}\expectationvalue{\chi^{\dagger}_{I,p}\chi_{I,p}}_{m}

\displaystyle=1.

To solve for higher order moments, the weight of intersections, i.e. the term
ABA
, needs to be computed. This follows exactly the same steps as in the fermion case, Eq. (
2.12
):

\displaystyle ABA=\sum^{p}_{s=0}\binom{m}{p}^{-1}\binom{N+m-1}{p}^{-1}\binom{N+m+s-1}{p}\binom{m-s}{p}B^{(s,p)}_{K,L}\chi^{\dagger}_{s,K}\chi_{s,L}.

(2.30)

Making the substitution
s\rightarrow p-s
and evaluating the binomial coefficients gives,

\displaystyle\binom{m}{p}^{-1}\binom{N+m-1}{p}^{-1}\binom{N+m+(p-s)-1}{p}\binom{m-(p-s)}{p}

(2.31)

\displaystyle\sim\exp\left\{-\frac{p^{2}}{m}\frac{1}{1+\frac{m}{N}}\right\}=q_{+},

so that in the double-scaled limit,

\displaystyle ABA=q_{+}B.

(2.32)

The same logic leads to a direct evaluation of
AB_{1}\dots B_{k}A
, as was done in Eq. (
2.17
) for fermions,

\displaystyle AB_{1}\dots B_{k}A=(q_{+})^{k}B_{1}\dots B_{k}.

(2.33)

Therefore, computing moments of the bosonic embedded ensembles reduces to counting intersections of chord diagrams,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{H^{2k}}=\sum_{\text{chord diagrams w/ $k$ chords}}\left(q_{+}\right)^{\text{\# of intersections}}.

(2.34)

Again this result is sufficient for demonstrating that the reduced moments of bosonic DS-EGUE are equivalent to the reduced moments of DS-SYK. To conclude, the moments of both fermionic and bosonic DS-EGUE are

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{H^{2k}}_{\pm}=\sum_{\text{chord diagrams w/ $k$ chords}}\left(q_{\pm}\right)^{\text{\# of intersections}},\hskip 14.22636ptq_{\pm}=\exp\left\{-\frac{p^{2}}{m}\frac{1}{1\pm\frac{m}{N}}\right\},

(2.35)

where the
-
sign is for fermions and the
+
sign is for bosons.

3
New derivation of the density of states

In this section we give a new derivation for the density of states of a double-scaled
p
-body model. We use
q\in(0,1)
to denote a generic transition parameter, which could be that of DS-SYK, DS-EGUE, or any other
p
-body model that is equivalent in the double-scaled limit. The essential technique stems from the relationship between the Wick product and orthogonal polynomials.

3.1
Wick product and its
q
-generalization

The Wick product is a way of defining a product of random variables such that the expectation value vanishes
[
51
]
.
8
8

8

The Wick product for Gaussian random variables is equivalent to normal ordering of creation and annihilation operators, see Section

Here, we will define the Wick product in a way suitable for the purposes of computing moments of non-commuting Gaussian random variables. The Wick product of
H^{n}
is a polynomial of order
n
in
H

\displaystyle\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n}}\vcentcolon\,\,=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}H^{j}

(3.1)

where
\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n}}\vcentcolon
denotes the Wick product of
H^{n}
. We define the Wick product for Gaussian random variables by the set of equations,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{H^m \normord{H^{n}}}=c_{n}\delta_{n,m},\hskip 14.22636ptm\leq n,

(3.2)

which are
n+1
equations that determine the
n+1
coefficients in Eq. (
3.1
). A recursive definition of the Wick product can be derived by considering
H\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n}}\vcentcolon
and noting that it is a polynomial of order
n+1
. We then assume it can be expanded in terms of Wick products,

\displaystyle H\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n}}\vcentcolon\,\,=\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}b_{j}\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{j}}\vcentcolon.

(3.3)

Applying Eq. (
3.2
) for

\Big\langle H^{m}H\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n}}\vcentcolon\Big\rangle=0\hskip 14.22636ptm<n-1

(3.4)

immediately shows

for
j<n-1
. To derive
b_{n-1}
, consider
m=n-1

\displaystyle\Big\langle H^{n-1}H\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n}}\vcentcolon\Big\rangle=c_{n}=b_{n-1}c_{n-1}\rightarrow b_{n-1}=\frac{c_{n}}{c_{n-1}}.

(3.5)

The case
m=n
is not interesting since odd moments vanish. Setting

gives

\Big\langle H^{n+1}H\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n}}\vcentcolon\Big\rangle=b_{n+1}c_{n+1}+b_{n-1}\Big\langle H^{n+1}\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n-1}}\vcentcolon\Big\rangle.

(3.6)

It is then necessary to evaluate
\expectationvalue{H^{n+2}\normord{H^n}}
, which can be done by choosing two
H

H^{n+2}
to be contracted. An important point is that the remaining
H^{n}
will have no more internal contractions since these will all give a zero contribution by the definition of the Wick product. For example, if the right most
H
is chosen, summing over all contractions will give,

\displaystyle H^{n}AA+H^{n-1}AHA+\dots+AH^{n}A=H^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}q^{j}=[n+1]_{q}H^{n},

(3.7)

where
[n]_{q}

9

[n]_{q}\equiv\frac{1-q^{n}}{1-q}.

(3.8)

Choosing the second

and so on, so that

\displaystyle\Big\langle H^{n+2}\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n}}\vcentcolon\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle H^{n}\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n}}\vcentcolon\Big\rangle\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\,[k]_{q}=c_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\,[k]_{q}

(3.9)

Rearranging Eq. (
3.6
) and using Eq. (
3.5

3.9

b_{n+1}=\frac{c_{n}}{c_{n+1}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\,[k]_{q}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,[k]_{q}\right)=\frac{c_{n}}{c_{n+1}}[n+1]_{q}.

(3.10)

The Wick product then has the recursive formula,

H\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n}}\vcentcolon\,\,=[n+1]_{q}\frac{c_{n}}{c_{n+1}}\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n+1}}\vcentcolon+\frac{c_{n}}{c_{n-1}}\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n-1}}\vcentcolon.

(3.11)

Setting the normalization to

\displaystyle c_{n}=\frac{(q)_{n}}{(1-q)^{n}}=[n]_{q}!,

(3.12)

where the

(a)_{n}\equiv(a;q)_{n}\equiv\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1-aq^{i}),

(3.13)

shows the Wick product is equivalent to
q
-Hermite polynomials,
\vcentcolon\mathrel{H^{n}}\vcentcolon\,\,=\mathbf{He}_{n}(H,q)
, which satisfy the recursion

\displaystyle\mathbf{He}_{n+1}(H|q)=(H)\mathbf{He}_{n}(H|q)-[n]_{q}\mathbf{He}_{n-1}(H|q).

(3.14)

When

\mathbf{He}_{n}(H|q=0)=\mathbf{U}_{n}(H/2)
.

3.2
Density of states

The density of states can now be derived using the properties of orthogonal polynomials.
The definition of the Wick product Eq. (
3.2
) can be written

\displaystyle\Big\langle H^{m}\,\mathbf{He}_{n}(H|q)\Big\rangle=\delta_{n,m}[n]_{q}!,\hskip 14.22636ptm\leq n.

(3.15)

so that

\displaystyle\Bigg\langle\mathbf{He}_{n}(H|q)\mathbf{He}_{m}(H|q)\Bigg\rangle=\delta_{n,m}\Bigg\langle H^{n}\,\mathbf{He}_{n}(H|q)\Bigg\rangle=[n]_{q}!\delta_{n,m}.

(3.16)

The result Eq. (
3.16
) uniquely determines the density of states, since the expectation value can be expanded in a continuous energy basis,

\displaystyle\Bigg\langle\mathbf{He}_{n}(H|q)\mathbf{He}_{m}(H|q)\Bigg\rangle=\int\,dE\ \rho(E|q)\,\,\mathbf{He}_{n}(E|q)\mathbf{He}_{m}(E|q)=[n]_{q}!\delta_{n,m},

(3.17)

and
\rho(E,q)
can be read off from the orthogonality properties of
q
-Hermite polynomials
[
52
]
,

\displaystyle\rho(E(\theta),q)=\absolutevalue{\left(e^{2i\theta}\right)_{\infty}}^{2}\left(q\right)_{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{1-q}}{4\pi\sin{\theta}},

(3.18)

where
E(\theta)=\frac{2\cos(\theta)}{\sqrt{1-q}}
with
E\in\left(-\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-q}},\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-q}}\right)
. The density of states when
q\to 1
can be read off from the orthogonality properties of Hermite polynomials
\mathbf{He}_{n}(E)
,

\lim_{q\to 1}\rho(E,q)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{E^{2}}{2}}.

(3.19)

and when
q=0
from the properites of Chebyshev polynomials
\mathbf{U}_{n}(\frac{E}{2})
,

\lim_{q\to 0}\rho(E,q)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-E^{2}}.

(3.20)

The reduced moments of double-scaled
p
-body models are then,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{H^k}=\int_{-\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-q}}}^{\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-q}}}\,dE\ \rho(E,q)E^{k}.

(3.21)

4
Strength density and the
4
-point function

In this section we show how properties of the Wick product and orthogonal polynomials can be used to compute
n
-point functions of DS-EGUE directly in the energy basis. We explicitly compute the
2
- and
4
-point functions and show they agree with the DS-SYK results in
[
25
]
.

The
2
-point function of the EGUE in the energy basis was originally studied in
[
9
]
by formulating a theory of strength distributions. The motivation was to understand transitions from an initial energy state
\ket{E_i}
, acted on by an operator
O
, to some final state
\ket{E_f}
. It was recognized in
[
9
,
10
]
, that to compute the transition strength or transition probability, i.e the
2
-point function,
\absolutevalue{\bra{E_f}O\ket{E_i}}^{2}
, it is necessary to evaluate
\expectationvalue{O^{\dagger}\delta(H-E_i)O\delta(H-E_f)}
, which is the strength density/distribution. This quantity can be directly evaluated by expanding the delta functions in terms of orthogonal polynomials and computing the moments
[
10
,
12
]
. By computing low order moments, the strength density was shown to be a bivariate Gaussian distribution when
q\to 1
in
[
10
,
11
]
, and more recently a bivariate
q
-normal distribution for generic
q
in
[
53
,
54
]
. We extend these results by developing a technique to compute the moments to all orders in the double-scaled limit for the
2
- and
4
-point density.

In the embedded ensemble literature, it is standard to take the operator
O
to be a
t
-body embedded ensemble, EGUE(t), that is statistically independent from the Hamiltonian which is represented by EGUE(p). The reasoning for the statistical independence is that any part of the operator
O
that is statistically correlated with
H
can separated as
O=\alpha H+R
, where
R
is statistically independent from
H

[
12
]
. The off diagonal matrix element of
O
in the energy basis are then completely determined by
R
. Therefore, as long as it is specified
E_{i}\neq E_{f}
in
\absolutevalue{\bra{E_f}O\ket{E_i}}^{2}
then
O
and
R
do not have to be distinguished.

From a different perspective, it was argued in
[
31
]
that taking
O
to be a random operator independent from
H
is the correct choice to understand black hole physics. The general argument being that the spectrum of a black hole near the horizon in nearly AdS
2
spacetimes can be described by
p
-body ensembles in the correct temperature and large
N
regimes. The relevant operators to probe the spectrum should be made up of the same basic constituents of the theory, such as fermions or bosons, but general enough that it leads to universal results. This leads to considering the operators of the same statistical class as the Hamiltonian, whether that is DS-SYK, DS-EGUE, or something else.

4.1
Strength density

We define
O
to be represented by the EGUE(t). For fermions this is

O=\sum_{I,L}J^{O}_{I,L}{}^{\dagger}_{t,I}{}_{t,L}

(4.1)

and
J^{O}_{I,L}
are complex gaussian random variables with variance

\expectationvalue{(J^{O \dagger})_{I',L'}\,J^{O}_{I,L}}_{J^{O}}=\binom{m}{t}^{-1}\binom{N-m+t}{t}^{-1}\delta_{I,L^{\prime}}\delta_{L,I^{\prime}}.

(4.2)

The bosonic case follows analogously. In the double-scaled limit the quantity
\frac{tp}{m}
is held fixed, and we define the correlation coefficient in the double-scaled limit as

\rho\equiv\expectationvalue{O^\dagger H O H}=\exp\left\{-\frac{tp}{m}\frac{1}{1\pm\frac{m}{N}}\right\},

(4.3)

where again the plus sign is for bosons and the minus sign is for fermions, and we are now averaging over both the
O
and
H
ensembles
10
10

10

This
\rho
is not to be confused with the density of states
\rho(E|q)
. The result, Eq. (
4.3
), follows from the exact same steps as was done for the fourth order moment in Sec.
2
. It is not necessary to consider
t
-body operators and more general operators consisting of
t_{1}
creation operators and
t_{2}
annihilation operators can be considered instead:

\displaystyle O=\sum_{I,L}J^{O}_{I,L}{}^{\dagger}_{t_{1},I}{}_{t_{2},L},

(4.4)

where

\expectationvalue{(J^{O \dagger})_{I',L'}\,J^{O}_{I,L}}_{J^{O}}=\binom{m}{t_{2}}^{-1}\binom{N-m+t_{2}}{t_{1}}^{-1}\delta_{I,L^{\prime}}\delta_{L,I^{\prime}}.

(4.5)

Then the correlation coefficient in the double-scaled limit would be changed to

\displaystyle\rho=\exp\left\{\frac{-t_{2}p}{m}\frac{1}{1\pm\frac{m}{N}}\right\}\exp\left\{\frac{-p(t_{2}-t_{1})}{(m\pm N)}\right\}.

(4.6)

In what follows we will not make specific reference to the form of
\rho

(0,1)
.

11

11

For the bosonic case this means restricting

\exp\left\{\frac{-p(t_{2}-t_{1})}{(m\pm N)}\right\}
would go to one.
The strength density is defined as

\expectationvalue{O^{\dagger}\delta(H-E_1)O\delta(H-E_2)}.

(4.7)

The Fourier transform of this object gives the time-dependent
2

\expectationvalue{O^{\dagger}e^{-it_1 H}Oe^{-it_2H}}=\int\,dE_{1}\,dE_{2}\ e^{-it_{1}E_{1}-it_{2}E_{2}}\expectationvalue{O^{\dagger}\delta(H-E_1)O\delta(H-E_2)}.

(4.8)

The focus here is on introducing general techniques that allow Eq. (

\delta(H-E_{1})=\sum_{n_{1}}\frac{1}{[n_{1}]_{q}!}\rho(E_{1}|q)\mathbf{He}_{n_{1}}(H|q)\mathbf{He}_{n_{1}}(E_{1}|q),

(4.9)

so the strength density can be written

\expectationvalue{O^{\dagger}\delta(H-E_1)O\delta(H-E_2)}=D\rho(E_{1}|q)\rho(E_{2}|q)O(E_{1},E_{2}|\rho),

(4.10)

where

is the dimensionality of the Hilbert space

12

12

The dimensionality factor is here because
\rho(E|q)
is the unit normalized density of states and
\expectationvalue{}
is the normalized
m

and we have defined the
2
-point function

O(E_{1},E_{2}|\rho)=\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}}\frac{\mathbf{He}_{n_{1}}(E_{1}|q)\mathbf{He}_{n_{2}}(E_{2}|q)}{[n_{1}]_{q}![n_{2}]_{q}!}\Big\langle O^{\dagger}\,\mathbf{He}_{n_{1}}(H|q)\,O\,\mathbf{He}_{n_{2}}(H|q)\Big\rangle.

(4.11)

Before evaluating the
2
- and
4
-point function it is necessary to discuss properties of the Wick product that allow expectation values of the form appearing in Eq. (

\mathbf{He}_{n_{1}}(H|q)
appears in an expectation value it can never have any internal contractions that contribute, thus it can only contract with
H
’s occurring in other polynomials. The reason for this is that internal contractions of higher order terms in the polynomial will always cancel with lower order terms in the polynomial. This property follows from the definition of the Wick product which we repeat here,

\displaystyle\Big\langle H^{m}\,\mathbf{He}_{n}(H|q)\Big\rangle=\delta_{n,m}[n]_{q}!,\hskip 14.22636ptm\leq n.

(4.12)

As a simple example consider,

\mathbf{He}_{3}(H|q)=H^{3}-(2+q)H.

(4.13)

There are three ways that the

AAH+AHA+HAA=(2+q)H
, which cancels with the second term in Eq. (
4.13

3.2
) when

. Extending this logic, only the highest order term in
\mathbf{He}_{n}(H|q)

H

n

\{H^{n}\}
. In expectation values it is then valid to make the replacement
\mathbf{He}_{n}(H|q)\to\{H^{n}\}
. From the orthogonality of

\displaystyle\Big\langle\mathbf{He}_{n}(H|q)\mathbf{He}_{m}(H|q)\Big\rangle=\Big\langle\{H^{n}\}\,\{H^{m}\}\Big\rangle=[n]_{q}!\delta_{n,m}.

(4.14)

It is also follows that in expectation values
O^{\dagger}\{H^{n}\}O=\rho^{n}\{H^{n}\}
.

4.11
) is simply

\displaystyle\Big\langle O^{\dagger}\,\mathbf{He}_{n_{1}}(H|q)\,O\,\mathbf{He}_{n_{2}}(H|q)\Big\rangle=\Big\langle O^{\dagger}\,\{H^{n_{1}}\}\,O\,\{H^{n_{2}}\}\Big\rangle=\rho^{n_{1}}\Big\langle\{H^{n_{1}}\}\,\{H^{n_{2}}\}\Big\rangle=\rho^{n_{1}}[n_{1}]_{q}!\delta_{n_{1},n_{2}},

(4.15)

and Eq. (

\displaystyle O(E_{1},E_{2}|\rho)=\sum_{n_{1}}\frac{\rho^{n_{1}}\mathbf{He}_{n_{1}}(E_{1}|q)\mathbf{He}_{n_{1}}(E_{2}|q)}{[n_{1}]!}.

(4.16)

The sum can be evaluated

\displaystyle O(E_{1}(\theta_{1}),E_{2}(\theta_{2})|\rho)=\frac{(\rho^{2})_{\infty}}{(\rho e^{i(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})},\rho e^{-i(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})},\rho e^{i(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})},\rho e^{-i(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})})_{\infty}},

(4.17)

with
E_{i}(\theta_{i})=\frac{2\cos(\theta_i)}{\sqrt{1-q}}
. The strength density,

\frac{1}{D}\expectationvalue{O^{\dagger}\delta(H-E_1)O\delta(H-E_2)}=\rho(E_{1}|q)\rho(E_{2}|q)O(E_{1},E_{2}|\rho)

(4.18)

is a bivariate
q
-normal distribution. The result agrees with the
2-

[

,
25
]
. For embedded ensembles, the bivariate

-normal distribution of the strength density was demonstrated by computing low order moments in
[

]
, whereas here we have given an all orders proof valid in the double-scaled limit.

The form of the sum in Eq. (
4.16
) makes it very easy to take the limiting cases, since the

-Hermite polynomials are just replaced with either Hermite polynomials or Chebychev polynomials. When

[
55
]
,

\lim_{q\to 1}\frac{1}{D}\expectationvalue{O^{\dagger}\delta(H-E_1)O\delta(H-E_2)}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{E_{1}^{2}+E_{2}^{2}-2\rho E_{1}E_{2}}{1-\rho^{2}}\right\},

(4.19)

which is a bivariate normal distribution that was first derived for embedded ensembles in
[
10
,
11
]
. When

[
55
]
,

\lim_{q\to 0}\frac{1}{D}\expectationvalue{O^{\dagger}\delta(H-E_1)O\delta(H-E_2)}=\frac{(1-\rho^{2})\sqrt{4-E_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{4-E_{2}^{2}}}{4\pi^{2}\left((1-\rho^{2})^{2}-\rho(1+\rho^{2})E_{1}E_{2}+\rho^{2}(E_{1}^{2}+E_{2}^{2})\right)}.

(4.20)

The strength density is of fundamental importance to understanding the off-diagonal matrix elements in the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH) in many-body quantum chaotic systems.
For example,
q\to 1
is the case relevant for physical quantum systems, which are typically governed by few-body interactions, and the strength density has exactly the form predicted by ETH. When

the Hamiltonian is a Wigner-Dyson ensemble and is not physical. However, the strength density is a surprisingly non-trivial function of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, which is contrary to the usual discussion of off-diagonal matrix elements when the eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian are Haar random unitaries
[
56
]
. A more thorough discussion of embedded ensembles and ETH will be left for a follow up paper.

4.2
4
-point function

In this section we show how to compute the

7
Conclusion

Embedded ensembles are very old models of many-body quantum chaos first studied analytically more than 50 years ago
[
7
]
. We have shown in the double-scaled limit embedded ensembles have the exact same properties as the SYK model. This broadens the double-scaled universality class to include both fermionic and bosonic systems. We worked specifically with the EGUE, but in the double-scaled limit the results are also valid for the time-reversal invariant case, i.e. the EGOE and EGSE.

Embedded ensembles are known to be solved, beyond the double-scaled limit, in terms of the Wigner Racah coefficients of
U(N)

[
12
]
. Developing an analogous formalism for other
p
-body systems, e.g. SYK, could provide a group theoretical understanding of the double-scaled universality class. In the double-scaled limit there are connections to the quantum group
SU_{q}(1,1)

[
25
,
33
]
. For example, the
4
-point function is related to Wigner
6j
-symbol of this group
[
25
,
33
]
. It is natural to ask if there are any connections between the
U(N)
solution valid at any
N
and the
SU_{q}(1,1)
solution valid in the double-scaled limit. This might be connected to the work of
[
59
]
.

Traditional random matrix models admit a pertubative genus expansion and there are efficient analytic techniques to compute correlation functions to all orders in this expansion
[
60
]
. The genus expansion is essential for understanding the connection to two-dimensional gravity, since the gravitational path integral has the same expansion
[
61
]
. For traditional matrix models the genus expansion is suppressed by powers of the dimensionality. Embedded ensembles become the GUE when
p=m
, and the genus expansion is suppressed by
\binom{N}{m}
. It was found in
[
8
]
that fluctuations, i.e. the two-trace correlator, for
m\gg p
are suppressed by powers of
\binom{m}{p}
for the EGOE. Assessing these regimes in
p
-body systems is difficult using the moment method since
1/N
corrections are neglected. Different approaches have been taken, such as the diagrammatic method based on chords developed in
[
62
]
, or the sigma model approach of
[
63
]
. The approach of this paper, based on normal mode expansions similar to
[
8
]
, could be useful in this regard. The previously mentioned group theoretical techniques based on the
U(N)
Wigner Racah algebra could also play an important role.

We have developed new techniques to compute the density of states and
n
-point functions, and these techniques allow the
n
-point functions to be computed directly in the energy basis. For the
2
-point function, or strength density, the formalism makes it very easy to take the limiting cases of
q\to 1
and
q\to 0
. It would be interesting to compute the same limits for the
4
-point function. The limit
q\to 1
is typically taken while keeping low temperature corrections
[
26
,
64
]
due to its importance for AdS
2
holography. However, at the center of the spectrum the results are still physically relevant for many-body chaos, and the results simplify dramatically. For example, the strength density at the center of the spectrum is just a bivariate Gaussian when
q\to 1
. Understanding the
n
-point strength density as
q\to 1
and
q\to 0
has important implications for the role of few-body interactions in generalized ETH.

Appendix B
Review of chords and the transfer matrix

In this section we review the chord diagram approach to deriving the density of states developed in
[
31
,
25
,
33
]
. Moments of double-scaled models can be written as a sum over chord diagrams,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{H^{2k}}=\sum_{\text{chord diagrams w/
$k$ chords}}\left(q\right)^{\text{\# of intersections}}.

(B.1)

We first introduce the chord Hilbert space which is defined by the space of open chords. For example, the state with
n
open chords is represented by the basis vector,

\displaystyle\ket{n}=\ket{\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{n-1},1,0,\dots}.

(B.2)

This is taken to be an infinite dimensional Hilbert space, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\Big\{\,\ket{n}\,|\,n\in\mdmathbb{Z_{+}}\,\Big\}.

(B.3)

An example of counting open chords is given for the
ABCACB
diagram of the sixth moment in fig.
2
.

Figure 2:
Example of counting open chords for the
ABCACB
diagram term in the 6th moment.

The key point is that for an arbitrary chord diagram, at a state of
n
open chords, there are only two options that can occur: At the next node a chord can open resulting in a state of
n+1
open chords, or anyone of the
n
open chords can close resulting in a state of
n-1
open chords. This is depicted in fig.
3
. The operator that evolves the state from one node to the next is refereed to as the transfer matrix
T
.

Figure 3:
Application of the transfer matrix on a state of
n
open chords.

We can then write the operation of the transfer matrix on a state
\ket{n}
as

\displaystyle T\ket{n}=\ket{n+1}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}q^{i}\ket{n-1}=\ket{n+1}+[n]_{q}\ket{n-1}.

(B.4)

Every diagram involved in the computation of
\expectationvalue{H^{2k}}
begins with
0
open chords, i.e. the ground state
\ket{0}
, and ends with
0
open chords. Each diagram has
2k
nodes and thus the transfer matrix needs to be applied
2k
times. The solution for the moments is then

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{H^{2k}}=\bra{0}T^{2k}\ket{0}.

(B.5)

The transfer matrix can be written in terms of creation and annihilation operators of
q
-oscillators. The operators are defined as follows:

\displaystyle a\ket{n}=[n]_{q}\ket{n-1},\hskip 2.84544pta^{\dagger}\ket{n}=\ket{n+1},

(B.6)

and they satisfy the
q
-commutator relation,

\displaystyle[a,a^{\dagger}]_{q}=aa^{\dagger}-qa^{\dagger}a=1.

(B.7)

The norm on the Hilbert space is defined so that
a
and
a^{\dagger}
are hermitian conjugates,

\displaystyle\bra{m}\ket{n}=[n]_{q}!\delta_{n,m}.

(B.8)

The transfer matrix can then be written as

\displaystyle T=a+a^{\dagger}.

(B.9)

The eigenstates and eigenvalues of
T
will be denoted as
T\ket{E}=E\ket{E}
. The wavefunctions,
{}_{n}(E)=\innerproduct{E}{n}
, can be found using Eq. (
B.4
),

\displaystyle\bra{E}T\ket{n}=E{}_{n}(E)={}_{n+1}(E)+[n]_{q}{}_{n-1}(E)

(B.10)

From the recursion, it is seen the wave functions are proportional to the
q
-Hermite polynomials, which satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbf{He}_{n+1}(E|q)=E\,\mathbf{He}_{n}(E|q)-[n]_{q}\mathbf{He}_{n-1}(E|q).

(B.11)

with
\mathbf{He}_{0}(E|q)=1
. This gives

\displaystyle{}_{n}(E)={}_{0}(E)\mathbf{He}_{n}\left(E|q\right).

(B.12)

To solve for
{}_{0}(E)
, we can insert a complete set of states,
\mdmathbb{I}=\int dE\ket{E}\bra{E}
, into the orthogonality condition Eq. (
3.16
),

\displaystyle\bra{m}\ket{n}=\int\,dE\ \absolutevalue{\psi_0(E)}^{2}\,\mathbf{He}_{m}(E|q)\mathbf{He}_{n}(E|q)=[n]_{q}!\delta_{n,m},

(B.13)

which show
|{}_{0}(E)|^{2}
must be the
q
-normal distribution defined in Eq. (
3.18
). The moments are thus

\displaystyle\bra{0}T^{2k}\ket{0}=\int\,dE\ \absolutevalue{\psi_0(E)}^{2}E^{2k}=\langle H^{2k}\rangle.

(B.14)

The chord diagram method can be extended to compute
n
-point functions and we refer the reader to
[
31
,
25
,
33
]
for details.

Appendix C
Normal ordering of the transfer matrix

Here we prove that
\vcentcolon\mathrel{T^{n}}\vcentcolon\,\,=\mathbf{He}_{n}(T|q)
. Start with
T\vcentcolon\mathrel{T^{n}}\vcentcolon
, which is a polynomial of degree
n+1
in
\vcentcolon\mathrel{T}\vcentcolon
,

\displaystyle T\vcentcolon\mathrel{T^{n}}\vcentcolon\,\,=\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}b_{j}\vcentcolon\mathrel{T^{j}}\vcentcolon.

(C.1)

The definition Eq. (
5.6
) for
m<n-1
shows
b_{j}=0
when
j<n-1
. Applying Eq. (
5.6
) for
m=n-1
shows
b_{n-1}=[n]_{q}
, and for
m=n+1
,

\displaystyle\bra{0}T^{n+2}\vcentcolon\mathrel{T^{n}}\vcentcolon\ket{0}=b_{n+1}[n+1]_{q}!+[n]_{q}\bra{0}T^{n+1}\vcentcolon\mathrel{T^{n-1}}\vcentcolon\ket{0}.

(C.2)

To solve
\bra{0}T^{n+2}\vcentcolon\mathrel{T^{n}}\vcentcolon\ket{0}
, note that only
(a_{1}^{\dagger})^{n}
in
\vcentcolon\mathrel{T^{n}}\vcentcolon
can contribute so that there has to be a corresponding
a_{1}^{n}
from
T^{n+2}
. We can choose a
T^{2}
from
T^{n+2}
to factor out, which will contribute

\displaystyle\bra{0}T^{n}T^{2}\to\bra{0}a^{n}(aa^{\dagger})=[n+1]_{q}\bra{n}

(C.3)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\bra{0}T^{n+2}\vcentcolon\mathrel{T^{n}}\vcentcolon\ket{0}=[n]_{q}!\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}[k]_{q},

(C.4)

and

\displaystyle b_{n+1}=\frac{[n]_{q}!}{[n+1]_{q}!}(\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}[k]_{q}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}[k]_{q})=1.

(C.5)

The normal ordered product
\vcentcolon\mathrel{T^{n}}\vcentcolon

\displaystyle\vcentcolon\mathrel{T^{n+1}}\vcentcolon\hskip 2.84544pt=T\vcentcolon\mathrel{T^{n}}\vcentcolon\hskip 2.84544pt-\hskip 2.84544pt[n]_{q}\vcentcolon\mathrel{T^{n-1}}\vcentcolon
