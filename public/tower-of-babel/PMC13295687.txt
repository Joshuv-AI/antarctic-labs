Green chitosan–starch–polyvinyl alcohol bio-composite hydrogel for zirconium capture from zircon raffinate
Authors: G. A. Dakroury, Sh. M. Abdo, E. A. A. El Shazly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A novel polymer-based sorbent comprising three blended components was synthesized via aqueous mixing and ultrasonication for the efficient removal of Zr(IV) from highly acidic solutions. Characterization by FT-IR, SEM, and surface area analysis confirmed active functional groups and a mesoporous structure (16.704 m2 g⁻1, 3 nm pores). Batch studies revealed that maximum uptake (102 mg g⁻1) occurred at pH 1.25 after 120 min at 20 °C, increasing to 188 mg g⁻1 with higher initial concentrations (50–750 mg L−1). Uptake decreased at elevated temperatures (293–323 K), indicating exothermic adsorption. Kinetic analysis was consistent with adsorption involving surface interactions and was best described by the pseudo-second-order and Elovich models, while equilibrium data were better fitted by the Freundlich model, suggesting a heterogeneous adsorption surface. Thermodynamic parameters (ΔG = –18.84 to –19.60 kJ mol−1; ΔH = − 11.35 kJ mol−1; ΔS = 25.57 J mol−1 K−1) confirmed a spontaneous and exothermic process. The sorbent remained stable in acidic media up to 2 M H2SO4 and was effectively regenerated, with H2SO4 achieving the highest desorption efficiency. These findings suggest that the prepared material shows potential as an effective sorbent for Zr(IV) recovery from acidic process streams.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01841-x.

Introduction

Zirconium is a transition metal derived mainly from the mineral zircon and is represented by the chemical symbol Zr with atomic number 40. Owing to its low thermal neutron capture cross-Section (0.185 barns) [1], high corrosion resistance, and excellent mechanical stability at elevated temperatures, zirconium plays a significant role in nuclear technology and nuclear chemistry [2]. It is widely used in nuclear reactors, particularly in fuel cladding, core structural components, and chemical reprocessing systems [3]. The growing global demand for nuclear energy has increased the need for efficient and sustainable methods for the extraction, purification, and recovery of zirconium from various industrial process streams [4, 5].

During the hydrometallurgical processing of zircon-bearing minerals, Zr(IV) is typically leached in acidic media and subsequently separated using solvent extraction systems. Although solvent extraction is widely applied on an industrial scale, it generates an aqueous waste stream known as zircon raffinate [5], which still contains considerable concentrations of zirconium along with other metal ions. This raffinate represents not only a loss of valuable zirconium but also an environmental concern due to its high acidity and metal content [6]. Therefore, recovering Zr(IV) from raffinate is essential for improving process efficiency, minimizing waste generation, and supporting circular economy strategies in nuclear materials management [7].

Despite its effectiveness, solvent extraction suffers from several limitations, including high organic solvent consumption, emulsion formation, poor selectivity in complex matrices, and environmental and safety concerns [8, 9]. These drawbacks have motivated researchers to explore alternative, cleaner, and more cost-effective separation technologies. Among these, adsorption using polymer-based sorbents has emerged as a promising approach owing to its operational simplicity, environmental compatibility, and reusability [5, 10].

Biopolymer-based adsorbents, particularly those incorporating chitosan and starch, have attracted considerable attention due to their abundance, biodegradability, and excellent metal-binding capabilities [11]. Chitosan (CS), derived from chitin, contains amino and hydroxyl functional groups that readily interact with metal ions through chelation and electrostatic attraction. Its high affinity for multivalent cations and ease of modification make it a promising material for the development of advanced sorbents [12]. Starch (St), another naturally abundant biopolymer, is cost-effective and contains numerous hydroxyl groups that enhance hydrophilicity and provide additional sorption sites when blended with other polymers. However, starch exhibits relatively poor mechanical properties, particularly low strength and flexibility, due to its semicrystalline structure [13]. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) is commonly incorporated into biopolymer matrices to improve film-forming ability, mechanical strength, and thermal stability, as well as to support the formation of three-dimensional polymeric networks [14] suitable for metal sorption under harsh conditions such as acidic leachates.

The combination of chitosan, starch, and PVA provides a synergistic composite material with improved chemical stability, high porosity, abundant functional groups, and good resistance to acidic conditions. These properties make the composite highly suitable for zirconium recovery from raffinate solutions.

However, despite the extensive use of biopolymers for heavy-metal recovery, studies focusing specifically on Zr(IV) sorption from zircon raffinate using chitosan–starch–PVA composites remain very limited. Moreover, the behavior of such composites in highly acidic and metal-rich environments typical of nuclear raffinate streams is still insufficiently understood.

A quantitative comparison of the maximum Zr(IV) adsorption capacity of the present chitosan–starch–polyvinyl alcohol (CSP) hydrogel with previously reported sorbents is presented in Table S1. Although some reported materials exhibit higher adsorption capacities (e.g., MCCh-N, 180 mg g⁻1), the CSP hydrogel demonstrates a favorable combination of acid stability (up to 2 M H2SO4), applicability to real zircon raffinate solutions, and relatively high adsorption capacity (102–188 mg g⁻1) under strongly acidic conditions that were not evaluated for many previously reported sorbents.

In this work, a novel chitosan–starch–polyvinyl alcohol (CSP) composite hydrogel was prepared and evaluated for the adsorption of Zr(IV) from zircon raffinate. The effects of sorption parameters, including pH, contact time, volume-to-mass ratio, and initial concentration, were systematically investigated to optimize the recovery efficiency. The adsorption mechanism was further evaluated using kinetic, isotherm, and thermodynamic models. The findings provide insight into the potential of CSP biopolymer composites as environmentally friendly, cost-effective, and efficient materials for zirconium recovery from acidic process streams, thereby contributing to the sustainable management of nuclear process residues and the development of advanced separation technologies.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Most chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade and used as received without further purification. Low-molecular-weight chitosan (75–85% degree of deacetylation) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Acetic acid (CH3COOH) was supplied by ADWIC (Egypt). Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) with an average molecular weight of 88.000 g mol−1 was purchased from Acros Organics. The zirconium raffinate solution was provided by the Nuclear Materials Authority; it was a waste stream generated after the solvent extraction of zirconium using trioctylamine (TOA). The elemental composition of the zircon raffinate was analyzed by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). The major elements detected (in mg L⁻1) were Zr (999.8), Ti (52.8), Hf (22.6), P (4.85), K (2.67), Ce (1.66), and Al (0.97). The free acidity of the raffinate was determined to be 1.8 M H2SO4. Hydrochloric acid (HCl) and Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), obtained from El-Nasr Co. (ADWIC Egypt) were used for pH adjustment. Double-distilled water was employed for the preparation of all solutions throughout the experimental work.

Instrument

DAIHAN Scientific ultrasonic cleaner (model WUC-AOIH, 60 Hz, 230 V; Korea) was employed to ensure thorough homogenization of the hydrogel mixture. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were obtained using the KBr pellet technique on a PerkinElmer 1600 spectrophotometer over the range of 400–4000 cm⁻1. The surface morphology and elemental composition of the prepared composite were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) on an FEI Quanta FEG-250 instrument. The nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements were carried out using a Quantachrome NOVA BET surface area analyzer (Nova Win software, version 11.04). Prior to analysis, the samples were degassed under vacuum to remove adsorbed moisture and gases. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was applied to determine the specific surface area, while the pore size distribution was obtained from the desorption branch using the BJH model. Zirconium concentration in solution were quantified spectrophotometrically using the alizarin red method.

These models help identify the controlling steps and evaluate the chemisorption or diffusion-dominated behavior of the process.

Thermal analysis

The thermal behavior of the CSP bio-composite hydrogel was investigated in Fig. 2d using simultaneous DTA–TGA analysis over the temperature range up to ~ 600 oC. The TGA curve exhibits multi-step weight loss, while the DTA profile shows corresponding exothermic and endothermic thermal peaks, confirming the composite nature and thermal stability of the hydrogel. For the 1st stage (30−150°C), a slight weight loss (11.988%) is observed in the TGA curve, accompanied by a weak exothermic DTA peak due to physical relaxation and restructuring as water desorbs, which stabilizes local hydrogen bonds before the onset of chemical degradation.

Although moisture loss is typically associated with endothermic dehydration, in polysaccharide-based composites the removal of loosely bound and structurally entrapped water can be coupled with rearrangement of hydrogen-bonded networks and relaxation of polymer chains, yielding a net exothermic signal. Similar exothermic low-temperature events have been reported in chitosan-based systems where early structural reorganization occurs during moisture release prior to the major degradation steps [17].

The 2nd stage (200−350°C), a pronounced weight loss (71.81%) occurs in this region, accompanied with strong endothermic DTA peak centered around ~ 250–300 °C. This stage is mainly associated with the thermal degradation of the polymer backbone; decomposition of starch glycosidic bonds, deacetylated chitosan chain scission, and partial dehydration and degradation of PVA chains [18]. The 3rd stage (400–500°C) shows a further gradual weight loss (13.72%) is detected, accompanied by a clear endothermic DTA peak around ~ 480–520°C. This endothermic behavior is attributed to further decomposition of thermally stable polymer fragments and carbonaceous residue after the main degradation. EDX analysis

The elemental composition of the CSP bio-composite hydrogel before and after Zr(IV) loading was investigated in Fig. 3a, b respectively using EDX analysis to confirm metal uptake and elucidate the sorption mechanism. Before Zr(IV) sorption, the EDX spectrum of CSP hydrogel shows the presence of C, O, and N as the dominant elements, with weight percentages of 38.5 wt.% C, 51.3 wt.% O, and 10.2 wt.% N. Sodium may originate from residual neutralization steps or ionic exchange processes, while sulfur and chloride are attributed to sulfate and chloride counter-ions associated with the Zr(IV) precursor solution, further supporting the ion-exchange and complexation mechanism. Sorption studies

pH effect

Figure 4a shows that the sorption of Zr(IV) onto CSP bio-composite hydrogel is strongly influenced by the pH change of the solution. As the pH of the solution increases, the sorbed amount of Zr(IV) increases. At low pH, the affinity of the active sites toward Zr(IV) ions is reduced due to the high concentration of H+ which competes Zr(IV) sorption and the presence of [Zr (H2O)8] complex species at strongly acidic conditions and electric repulsion available protonated active sites [20]. Increasing the pH decreases the degree of protonation, consequently, reduces repulsion and enhances the accessibility of binding sites and Zr(IV) uptake. The precipitation curve (Fig. 4c) confirms that at pH 1.25, less than 1% of Zr(IV) is lost due to hydrolysis or colloid formation in the absence of sorbent, ensuring that the measured removal is entirely attributable to adsorption onto the CSP hydrogel Fig. 4b, the species distribution diagram determined via Hydra/Medusa chemical equilibrium software [21], indicates the formation of hydrolysis polymeric species as Zr (OH)3+ and Zr (OH)22+ which has stronger affinity towards oxygenated active sites and an increase in sorption efficiency. After pH 1.25, the sorption gradually approaches a plateau due to saturation of the available active sites, and the sorption process reached equilibrium. Moreover, Zr(IV) tends to form neutral or colloidal hydroxide species at higher pH which impedes the sorption process. Therefore, maintaining the pH within optimal range is essential to ensure the dominant mechanism remains surface complex rather than precipitation. The sorbed amount at this pH reached 102 mg g−1. Figure 4d depicts the chemical stability of the prepared hydrogel in hydrochloric acid solutions within a molar range 0.1–3 M H2SO4. The weight loss (or solubility percentage) remains essentially zero till 2 M H2SO4 indicates that the composite network structure remains stable. A slight increase in solubility was observed at 2.5 M H2SO4, consistent with the early stages of acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of glycosidic and related acetal/ether linkages in the polysaccharide backbone. Under sufficiently strong acidic conditions, protonation and cleavage of glycosidic bonds begin to occur, leading to partial swelling and minor polymer dissolution without catastrophic breakdown of the composite matrix [22]. A more noticeable solubilization appears at 3 M H2SO4 due to strong protonation and disruption of hydrogen bonded domains, hydrolysis of carbohydrates linkages, and increased polymer chain mobility. Effect of volume/mass ratio

The impact of the volume/mass ratio (V/m) is a key operational parameter affecting the sorption process and the availability of active functional groups on the sorbent. As shown in Fig. 6b, the sorbed amount increases as the V/m ratio increases. This is because the increasing in the V/m ratio means that a given volume of solution contains more Zr(IV) ions relative to the limited number of available active sites [30]. This enhances the driving force for mass transfer and allows a larger fraction of the sorption sites to become occupied, thereby increasing the adsorption capacity. The observed increase in q with higher V/m ratios suggests that the adsorbent possesses a sufficiently high number of accessible active sites capable of binding additional Zr(IV) ions when more ions are available in the solution.

Desorption and regeneration

The desorption results presented in Fig. 7a were used to evaluate the most effective desorbing agent for the recovery of sorbed Zr(IV). Among the tested eluents, sulfuric acid (H2SO4) exhibited the highest desorption efficiency, indicating its effectiveness as a stripping agent for Zr(IV) recovery from the loaded adsorbent [5]. The higher stability constant of the Zr(IV)–sulfate complex suggests stronger interactions between Zr(IV) and SO42− ions compared with Cl⁻ ions [31]. In contrast, HNO3 exhibited moderate desorption efficiency, while deionized water showed negligible desorption performance, confirming its poor ability to disrupt the interactions between Zr(IV) ions and the sorbent surface [32]. The desorbed amount increased markedly with increasing H2SO4 concentration. At low acid concentrations, desorption efficiency remained limited because the proton concentration was insufficient to effectively disrupt the strong interactions between Zr(IV) ions and the functional groups on the sorbent surface. Zr(IV) ions are believed to interact strongly with oxygen-containing functional groups through electrostatic attraction and surface complexation; therefore, stronger acidic conditions are required to weaken these interactions. As the H2SO4 concentration increased, the availability of H⁺ ions also increased, promoting competition between protons and Zr(IV) ions for the active adsorption sites. This facilitated the release of Zr(IV) ions from the sorbent surface into the solution, resulting in a significant increase in desorption efficiency. Conclusion

The polymer-based material prepared in this study demonstrated high efficiency for the removal of Zr(IV) from strongly acidic process solutions. Characterization confirmed that the material contains active functional groups and a mesoporous structure that supported metal uptake. Sorption studies demonstrated that Zr(IV)) removal is strongly affected by pH, contact time, initial concentration, temperature, and liquid-to-solid ratio. The best performance was obtained at pH 1.25, where an uptake of 102 mg g−1 was reached within 120 min. The decrease in adsorption capacity at higher temperatures, together with the calculated thermodynamic parameters, confirmed that the process is spontaneous and exothermic. Kinetic results indicated that chemisorption is the controlling mechanism, while equilibrium data were best described by the Freundlich model, confirming a heterogeneous surface with multilayer interaction. The material also showed excellent stability in strong acids and could be regenerated effectively, especially using sulfate solutions, which achieved the highest desorption efficiency. Overall, the prepared sorbent is a promising, low-cost option for recovering Zr(IV) from harsh acidic streams and can contribute to more sustainable metal recovery processes.
