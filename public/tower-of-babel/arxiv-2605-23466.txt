Arithmetic Definability of Henselian Valuations: Definability via the tilting correspondence
Gessica Alecci, Ihsane Hadeg, Franziska Jahnke, Margarete Ketelsen, Isabella Negrini

Abstract
. We show that arithmetic definability of henselian valuations is preserved by the tilting correspondence. Moreover, we show that if a perfectoid valuation is arithmetically definable, then no parameters are needed. We also investigate whether these definitions can be chosen uniformly, and discuss the required quantifier complexity.



1.
Introduction

The tilting equivalence is a fundamental tool in arithmetic geometry which allows
one to “transfer arithmetic” between certain fields of mixed characteristic
and their positive characteristic analoga.
More precisely, tilting
transfers arithmetical data between a perfectoid field of mixed
characteristic and its tilt, an associated perfectoid field of positive characteristic, and vice versa.
Instances of this transfer principle are the Fontaine-Wintenberger Theorem which asserts
that the absolute Galois group of
a perfectoid field and its tilt are isomorphic as profinite groups (
[
11
]
), Scholze’s extension of the
tilting equivalence to perfectoid spaces which led to his proof of
(many cases of) the
weight-monodromy conjecture
in mixed characteristic by reduction to positive characteristic (
[
33
]
),
as well as André’s proof of the direct summand conjecture which also crucially
uses
perfectoid spaces (
[
1
]
).

These results give rise to the model-theoretic question how much of
the first-order structure of perfectoid fields (in the language of rings or in the language of valued fields, see
Section

2.2
for definitions) is preserved by the tilting equivalence. We now give a rough overview of what is known
in this respect so far:
Kartas shows in
[
20
]
that as long as

K
is a computable untilt of
K^{\flat}
(in an appropriate sense defined in
[
20
]
),
then decidability of
\textrm{Th}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}}(K^{\flat},v^{\flat})
implies
decidability of
\textrm{Th}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}}(K,v)
.
Jahnke and Kartas show in
[
17
]
that tilting preserves
elementary equivalence, as well as a version for untilting
(which requires working over a base field). Moreover, they also obtain a converse to Kartas’ results above, showing that decidability of
\textrm{Th}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}}(K,v)

implies
decidability of
\textrm{Th}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}}(K^{\flat},v^{\flat})
.
A key result of
[
17
]
is that – up to elementary equivalence –
any nonpricipal ultrapower
(K^{\mathcal{U}},v^{\mathcal{U}})

of a perfectoid field
(K,v)
admits
a coarsening
w
of
v^{\mathcal{U}}
such that
(K^{\mathcal{U}},w)
is tame
and such that

(K^{\flat},v^{\flat})
embeds elementarily into the residue field of
(K^{\mathcal{U}},w)
.
This implies immediately that being
C_{i}
in the sense
of Lang (which is an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-elementary property)
tilts. The converse of this was investigated
by Kartas in
[
21
]
, but is still
open in full generality.
In
[
13
]
,
Gambardella and Kartas show that having the strong
C_{1}^{0}
-property untilts.
When perfectoid fields are considered in continuous logic (rather than in
discrete first-order logic),
Rideau-Kikuchi, Scanlon and Simon (
[
32
]
) show that the tilting equivalence can be seen
as a bi-interpretation in continuous logic.

The present work adds to the picture described above.
By definition, perfectoid fields come equipped with a rank-1 valuation with
respect to which they are complete. In particular, the valuation is henselian.
Nontrivial henselian valuations are often so closely related to the arithmetic of the underlying field that they are encoded in it, i.e., that their valuation ring is first-order definable in the language of rings.
A classical example which goes back to J. Robinson is that, for
p\neq 2
, the formula

\varphi_{p}(x)\equiv\exists y(y^{2}=1+px^{2})

defines the
p
-adic valuation on the
p
-adic numbers, i.e.,
it is satisfied exactly by those
p
-adic numbers which are
p
-adic integers.
In this note, we investigate when a
perfectoid valuation is
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-definable (with or without parameters; we refer to the latter as being
\emptyset
-definable), and obtain a complete classification as our main
result:

Theorem 0
(see Theorem
3.15
)
.

Let
(K,v)
be perfectoid. Then
v
is definable if and only if

v
is
\emptyset
-definable if and only if at least one of the following holds

(1)

(K,v)
is not defectless,

(2)

vK
is not divisible, or

(3)

Kv
is not t-henselian of divisible-tame type.

The definitions and notations are explained in
Section

2.1
and
3.2
.
Since the characterizing conditions in

3.15
are all preserved under tilting, we conclude:

Corollary 0
(see
3.16
)
.

Let
(K,v)
be perfectoid.
Then, the following are equivalent:

•

v
is definable,

•

v
is
\emptyset
-definable,

•

v^{\flat}
is definable,

•

v^{\flat}
is
\emptyset
- definable.

We then comment on how uniformity of definitions can be produced or dispelled arbitrarily,
see
Section

4
. In
Section

5
, we focus on quantifier complexity of
definitions (cf.
Section

2.2
for definitions and notations). Building on work of Anscombe and Fehm (
[
2
]
), we show that
\forall
-
\emptyset
-definability and

\exists
-
\emptyset
-definability untilt (
5.3
), but that

\forall
-
\emptyset
-definability does not tilt (
5.4
). Whether

\exists
-
\emptyset
-definability tilts remains an open question. Finally, in
Section

6
, we discuss the classical case of perfectoid fields
(K,v)
for which
the residue field is an algebraic extension of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. The results in this section
are also straightforward applications of those proven in

[
2
]
. We show that if
K
is not algebraically closed, then
v
is
\emptyset
-definable, and that if the residue field
is not algebraically closed,
v
is
\exists
-
\emptyset
-definable.

2.1.
Algebraic background

In this subsection, we give an introduction to the notions and tools from

we are using throughout. For a more thorough introduction to valued fields,
see
[
9
]

[

]
.

Notation

Given a valued field
(K,v)
, we denote its value group by
vK
, its valuation ring by
\mathcal{O}_{v}
, with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{v}
, and its residue field by
Kv
. We denote the residue map
\mathcal{O}_{v}\to Kv=\mathcal{O}_{v}/\mathfrak{m}_{v}

\res_{v}

2.1.1.
Coarsenings and induced valuations

In the following we briefly recall the concepts of
coarsenings and valuation (de)composition. For more details, see
[
9
, Section 2.3]
.

Given a field
K
and valuations
v
and
w
on
K
, we say that
w
is a
coarsening
of
v
if
\mathcal{O}_{w}\supseteq\mathcal{O}_{v}
.
In this case, we also say that
w
is coarser than
v
,
v
is finer than
w
, or
v
is a refinement of
w
.

Given a valued field
(K,v)
and a coarsening
w
of
v
, we find the
induced valuation

\overline{v}
on the residue field
Kw
:

\overline{v}\colon(Kw)^{\times}\to\Delta,\;\overline{v}(\res_{w}(x))=v(x),\;x\in\mathcal{O}_{w}^{\times}

where
\Delta\coloneqq\overline{v}((Kw)^{\times})=v(\mathcal{O}_{w}^{\times})\subseteq vK
is a convex subgroup of
vK
.
Note that
(Kw)\overline{v}=Kv
.

In this case, we can also write
v=\overline{v}\circ w
; this is called
valuation decomposition
.
When representing the valuations by the corresponding places, we obtain the following diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Kw}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Kv.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces w}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces v}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{v}}

Conversely, given a valued field
(K,w)
and a valuation
u
on the residue field

Kw
, one can also form a
valuation composition

v=u\circ w
: this is a valuation with valuation
ring
\mathcal{O}_{v}=\res_{w}^{-1}(\mathcal{O}_{u})
and in particular a refinement of
w
.

2.1.2.
Defectless valuations

Let
(K,v)
be a valued field and let
K\subseteq L
be a finite field
extension. Then
v
extends to finitely many valuations
w_{1},\dotsc,w_{r}
on
L

(with
r\leq[L:K]
, see
[
9
, Theorem 3.2.9]
).
Recall that any extension of valued fields
(K,v)\subseteq(L,w)
induces embeddings of the value groups
vK\subseteq Lw
and of the residue fields
Kv\subseteq Lw
.
We have the following fact:

Fact 2.1
(Fundamental inequality, see
[
9
, Theorem 3.3.4]
)
.

For a valued field
(K,v)
and
L/K
finite, let
w_{1},\dotsc,w_{r}
denote the
prolongations of
v
to
L
. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{r}(w_{i}L:vK)[Lw_{i}:Kv]\leq[L:K].

We say that
(K,v)
is
defectless
if equality holds for all finite field
extensions
L
in the fundamental inequality.
As henselian valuations extend uniquely to any finite extension, a henselian valuation

(K,v)
is hence defectless if for all
L/K
finite

(wL:vK)[Lw:Kv]=[L:K].

holds, where
w
denotes the unique prolongation of
v
to
L
. If

(K,v)
is henselian and the fundamental equality fails for

(L,w)/(K,v)
, we say the extension
(L,w)/(K,v)
has
defect
.

2.1.3.
Tame fields

Tame fields were been introduced and studied by Franz-Viktor Kuhlmann in
[
27
]
. See
[
28
]
for a survey on recent developments.

Definition 2.2.

A henselian valued field
(K,v)
is
tame
if

(1)

(K,v)
is defectless,

(2)

Kv
is perfect, and

(3)

vK
is
p
-divisible, if
\chara(Kv)=p>0
.

In the following, we will often encounter tame valued fields with
divisible value group
, i.e., for each
\gamma\in vK
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is
\delta\in vK
such that
n\delta=\gamma
.

Example 2.3.

Let
k
be a perfect field of positive characteristic and let
\Gamma
be a
p
-divisible ordered abelian group.
Then, the
Hahn series
field
(k(\!(\Gamma)\!),v_{\Gamma})
is a tame valued field.
The Hahn field construction provides a canonical way to obtain maximally valued fields of equal characteristic with a given value group and residue field.
For more details see
[
8
, Section 2.8]
.

2.1.4.
Perfectoid fields and tilting

We now give the definitions for the key objects in this paper: perfectoid
fields and their tilts. For more details, see

[
33
]
and
[
29
]
.

Definition 2.4.

A valued field
(K,v)
of residue characteristic
\chara(Kv)=p>0
is
perfectoid
if all of the following hold:

(1)

vK
has rank 1, i.e.
vK\leq\mathbb{R}
, and
(K,v)
is complete,

(2)

\mathcal{O}_{v}/p\mathcal{O}_{v}
is
semi-perfect
, that is the Frobenius map

\mathcal{O}_{v}/p\mathcal{O}_{v}\to\mathcal{O}_{v}/p\mathcal{O}_{v},x\mapsto x^{p}
is surjective, and

(3)

(K,v)
is not discretely valued, that is
vK\ncong\mathbb{Z}
.

Examples of perfectoid fields include Hahn series fields
(k(\!(\Gamma)\!),v_{\Gamma})

where
k
is a perfect field and
\Gamma\leq\mathbb{R}
is
p
-divisible,
as well as more generally any rank-
1
complete tame field (
[
26
, see the remarks on p. 2695 and Theorem 1.2]
). More generally however,
perfectoid fields are necessarily henselian but need not be defectless, e.g., the completions of

(\mathbb{F}_{p}(\!(t)\!)^{\mathrm{perf}},v_{t})
and
(\mathbb{Q}_{p}(p^{1/p^{\infty}}),v_{p})

are perfectoid but not defectless.

Crucially, to any perfectoid field
(K,v)
one can associate its tilt
(K^{\flat},v^{\flat})
, a perfectoid field of positive characteristic.

Definition 2.5.

Let
(K,v)
be perfectoid.
There is an inverse system of rings

\ldots\overset{x\mapsto x^{p}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{O}_{v}/p\mathcal{O}_{v}\overset{x\mapsto x^{p}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{O}_{v}/p\mathcal{O}_{v}.

We define

\mathcal{O}_{v}^{\flat}\coloneqq\varprojlim_{x\mapsto x^{p}}\mathcal{O}_{v}/p\mathcal{O}_{v}.

Now, set
K^{\flat}\coloneqq\operatorname{Frac}(\mathcal{O}_{v}^{\flat})
, and let
v^{\flat}
be the valuation on
K^{\flat}
with valuation ring
\mathcal{O}_{v}^{\flat}
and value group
vK
.
The valued field
(K^{\flat},v^{\flat})
is called the
tilt
of
(K,v)
.

As mentioned in the introduction, the
arithmetic of
(K,v)
and
(K^{\flat},v^{\flat})
are ‘similar’. We only recount
the few basic instances of this similarity that we need for our purposes:

Fact 2.6.

Let
(K,v)
be a perfectoid field.

(1)

If
\chara(K)=p>0
, then
(K^{\flat},v^{\flat})\cong(K,v)
.

(2)

If
\chara(K)=0
, then
(K,v)
is a perfectoid field of positive characteristic and we have

(a)

vK\cong v^{\flat}K^{\flat}
,

(b)

\mathcal{O}_{v}/p\cong\mathcal{O}_{v^{\flat}}/\varpi
for some element
\varpi\in\mathcal{O}_{v^{\flat}}
, called
pseudouniformizer
,

(c)

Kv\cong K^{\flat}v^{\flat}
, and

(3)

(K,v)
is defectless if and only if
(K^{\flat},v^{\flat})
is defectless.

Proof.

(1) and (2) are part of
[
33
, Lemma 3.4]
. (3)
follows from
[
34
, Corollaire 3.3.4]
– see also

[
17
, Remark 6.2.5]
for the precise statement.

2.2.
Model-theoretic background

In this section, we give a very brief introduction into the model-theoretic
concepts which appear throughout the paper. For more details, we refer the reader to

[
14
]
and
[
15
]
.
Throughout this section, let
\mathcal{L}
be a first-order language and
M
and
N
and
\mathcal{L}
-structures.
We write
M\equiv_{\mathcal{L}}N
if
M
and
N
are elementarily equivalent as
\mathcal{L}
-structures,
i.e., the same
\mathcal{L}
-sentences hold in
M
and
N
. If
M
is an
\mathcal{L}
-substructure of

N
, we
write
M\preceq_{\mathcal{L}}N
in case the given inclusion of
M
into
N
is
elementary, that is for all
\mathcal{L}
-formula
\varphi(\overline{x})
, where
\overline{x}
is
finite tuple of variables of length
|\overline{x}|
and all
\overline{m}\in M^{|\overline{x}|}
,
we have

M\models\varphi(\overline{m})\Longleftrightarrow N\models\varphi(\overline{m}).

We tend to drop the subscript
\mathcal{L}
if the language is clear from the context.
For an
\mathcal{L}
-theory
T
, i.e., whenever
T
is a set of
\mathcal{L}
-sentences, we write

M\models T
to indicate that any
\psi\in T
holds true in
M
.

2.2.1.
Definability

We say that a subset
A\subseteq M^{n}
is
definable
if there is an

\mathcal{L}
-formula
\varphi(\overline{x},\overline{y})
, where
\overline{x}
and
\overline{y}
are finite tuples of variables of length
|\overline{x}|=n
and
|\overline{y}|

respectively, and some
\overline{b}\in M^{|\overline{y}|}
, such that for any

\overline{a}\in M^{n}
, we have

\overline{a}\in A\Longleftrightarrow M\models\varphi(\overline{a},\overline{b}),

holds,
in which case we also say that
\varphi(\overline{x},\overline{b})
defines
A
. We write

\varphi(M^{n},\overline{b})
for the set
of elements of
M^{n}
which satisfy
\varphi(\overline{x},\overline{b})
.

For a subset
B
of
M
we call
\varphi(\overline{x},\overline{b})
a
B
-formula if

\overline{b}\in B^{|\overline{y}|}
and call
\overline{b}
the parameters needed to define the
set

\varphi(M^{|\overline{x}|},\overline{b})
.
Moreover, we call
\varphi(\overline{x},\overline{y})
an
\exists
-formula (resp.
\forall
-formula etc.) if
it is equivalent to a formula in prenex normal form with precisely these quantifiers.
We then say that
A
is
B
-definable,
\exists
-
B
-definable etc. if there exists a corresponding formula defining
A
. If
B=\{b\}
is a singleton, we use
B
-definability and
b
-definability synonymously. A crucial case for us is when

B=\emptyset
, in which case we say that the corresponding set is defined without parameters.

2.2.2.
Ultrapowers, Łoś and Keisler–Shelah

The ultrapower construction is a very common and powerful tool.
We refer the reader to
[
15
, Section 8.5]
for an introduction to filters, ultrafilters, ultrapowers and ultraproducts.
Here, we just state two very useful theorems: Łoś’s theorem, and the Keisler–Shelah isomorphism theorem.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be an ultrafilter on some index set.
We denote the
ultrapower
of
M
by
M^{\mathcal{U}}
:

The underlying set is
M^{\mathcal{U}}={\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\prod_{i\in I}M}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.83437pt}{$\displaystyle{\sim_{\mathcal{U}}}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\prod_{i\in I}M}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.83437pt}{$\textstyle{\sim_{\mathcal{U}}}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\prod_{i\in I}M}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.34427pt}{$\scriptstyle{\sim_{\mathcal{U}}}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\prod_{i\in I}M}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-0.99101pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\sim_{\mathcal{U}}}$}}}
, where
\sim_{\mathcal{U}}
is the equivalence relation given by

(a_{i})_{i\in I}\sim_{\mathcal{U}}(b_{i})_{i\in I}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\{i\in I:a_{i}=b_{i}\}\in\mathcal{U}.

The
\mathcal{L}
-structure on
M
induces a natural
\mathcal{L}
-structure on
M^{\mathcal{U}}
, see

[
15
, Section 8.5]
for details.
The
diagonal embedding
of
M
into
M^{\mathcal{U}}
is given by

\displaystyle\iota_{M}\colon M

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow M^{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\mapsto\left[(a)_{i\in I}\right].

The diagonal embedding is an embedding of
\mathcal{L}
-structures, and it is even an elementary
embedding:

Theorem 2.7
(Łoś’s theorem, see
[
15
, Corollary 8.5.4]
)
.

Let
M
be a structure in some language
\mathcal{L}
and let
\mathcal{U}
be an ultrafilter on some index set

.
The diagonal embedding
\iota_{M}\colon M\hookrightarrow M^{\mathcal{U}}

M\equiv_{\mathcal{L}}M^{\mathcal{U}}
.

The second key theorem we need about ultraproducts is the Keisler–Shelah Theorem:

Theorem 2.8
(Keisler–Shelah isomorphism theorem, see
[
15
, Theorem 8.5.10]
)
.

Let
M
and
N
be structures in some language
\mathcal{L}
, and assume
M\equiv_{\mathcal{L}}N
.
Then there is an ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
on some index set
I
such that

M^{\mathcal{U}}\cong N^{\mathcal{U}}.

2.2.3.
Some model theory of valued fields

In this paper, we consider (valued) fields in the language

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}=\{0,1,+,-,\cdot\}
of rings and in the language

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}\cup\{\mathcal{O}\}
of valued rings,
where
\mathcal{O}
is a unary relation symbol interpreted
as the valuation ring. Given fields
K
and
L
, we often write
K\equiv L
as
a shorthand for
K\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}}L
, and when
(K,v)
and
(L,w)
are valued fields
we similarly write
(K,v)\equiv(L,w)
for
(K,v)\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}}(L,w)
.

We say that a valuation
v
on
K
is
definable
if
\mathcal{O}_{v}
is a
definable subset of
K
in the language
\mathcal{L}_{\rm ring}
of rings, i.e.,
there is an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-formula
\varphi(x,\overline{y})
and a tuple
\overline{c}\in K^{|\overline{y}|}

such that
\mathcal{O}_{v}=\varphi(K,\overline{c})

in which case we also say that
\varphi(x,\overline{c})
defines
v
.
If a valuation is
\emptyset
-definable, then the same formula defines a valuation
w

in any
L\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}}K
, and for any such
L
and
w
we have

(K,v)\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}}(L,w)
. In particular, properties like henselianity carry over
from
(K,v)
to
(L,w)
. See
[
10
]
for a survey on definable henselian valuations.

3.
(Un)titling Definability

In this section, we show our main result (
3.15
): if
(K,v)
is a perfectoid field of
mixed characteristic
(0,p)
and its tilt is
(K^{\flat},v^{\flat})
, then
\mathcal{O}_{v}
is an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-definable subset of

K
if and only if
\mathcal{O}_{v^{\flat}}
is an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-definable subset of

K^{\flat}
. We also show that if
v
(or equivalently
v^{\flat}
) is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-definable, then no parameters are needed, i.e., it is
\emptyset
-definable.
Roughly speaking, a henselian valuation can be definable due to its value group,
its residue field or sometimes even using an extension with defect.
Since the tilting equivalence
preserves value group, residue field and the existence of defect extensions,
we prove that in each of these cases definability is preserved by (un)tilting. Moreover,
we show that for perfectoid fields, definability of a valuation necessarily
stems from one of the aforementioned cases.

Definability via non-divisible value groups

Let
(K,v)
be a perfectoid field.
We start by asserting that
if the value group
vK
is not divisible, then
v
is definable.
This follows from a Theorem of Hong, see
[
16
]
(which in turn generalizes earlier work of Koenigsmann,
see
[
24
, Lemma 3.6]
), which we state below.
While the statement we give is more
precise than Hong’s (who makes no statements about uniformity or quantifier complexity),
his proof yields what is stated below. We thus sketch the proof for the convenience
of the reader.

Theorem 3.1
(see

Definability via residue fields

Akin for the non-divisibility of the value group in the previous section, we now
discuss properties of the residue field which give rise to the definability of a
henselian valuation. The key concept here is t-henselianity, first introduced by Prestel
and Ziegler in

Definition 3.2.

A field

The definition of being t-henselian of divisible tame-type stems from

Example 3.3.

Every algebraically closed field is t-henselian of divisible-tame type.
Indeed, if

, and the Hahn series valuation on

Remark 3.4.

Let

on some index set

such that the ultrapower

admits a nontrivial tame valuation with divisible value group.

Indeed, let

such that
L
admits a nontrivial tame valuation

By the Keisler-Shelah isomorphism theorem, see

, there is an ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
on some index set
I
such that

. Since tameness is an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}
-aximatizable property (

, Lemma 4.4]

if we consider the ultrapower

of the valued field

Let

Definability via defect

Defining valuation rings via properties of the residue field and value group is
a standard technique, initiated in the work of
Julia Robinson and extensively studied ever since.
This reflects the Ax–Kochen/Ershov philosophy: in sufficiently nice settings
(e.g., in equicharacteristic
0
), the first-order theory of a valued field is completely
determined by those of its residue field and value group. However, building on recent
work of Kuhlmann and Rzepka, Ketelsen, Ramello and Szewczyk were able to use Galois
extensions of independent defect-type to define henselian valuations (see
[
22
]
for more details). In this section,
we refine their result to show that in the rank-1 case, these
definitions can be done without parameters.
In
[
22

, the authors show that in any henselian valued field
(K,v)
with
\chara{Kv}=p>0
such that
(K,v)
is not defectless, and such that either

(i)

K
is perfect, if
\chara{K}=p>0
, or

(ii)

\mathcal{O}_{v}/p
is semi-perfect, if
\chara{K}=0

holds, some nontrivial coarsening of

is definable, possibly with parameters.
Since the only defect occuring in
perfectoid fields is of independent-defect type by
[
26
, Theorem 1.10]

this gives rise to definability of non-defectless perfectoid valuations:

Corollary 3.8.

Let
(K,v)
be a perfectoid field which is not defectless.
Then
v
is definable (possibly with parameters).

Proof.

By
[
22
, Corollary 4.14]
,

has a nontrivial coarsening

which is definable.
Since
(K,v)
is perfectoid,
v
is a rank-1-valuation.
Thus, there are no proper nontrivial coarsenings of
v
, so
w=v
.
∎

Non-definability

We now show that if a perfectoid valuation is not already definable due to
3.1
,
3.7
, or
3.10
, it is in fact
not

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-definable. In order to prove this, we
need another statement from
[
22
]
.
This fact in turn relies on quantifier elimination in divisible ordered abelian groups and stable embeddedness of the value group in tame valued fields.

Fact 3.11
(see
[
22
, Lemma 3.4]
)
.

Let
(K,v)
be tame with divisible value group.
No proper coarsening of
v
can be
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}
-definable in
(K,v)
.
In particular, no proper coarsening of
v
can be
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-definable in
K
.

As a consequence, we obtain the following non-definability result:

Proposition 3.12.

Let
(K,v)
be tame with divisible value group such that
Kv
is t-henselian of divisible-tame type.
Then,
v
is not definable (even with parameters).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be an ultrafilter on some index set
I
such that
(Kv)^{\mathcal{U}}
admits a nontrivial tame valuation
u
with divisible value group.
We consider the ultrapower
(K^{\mathcal{U}},v^{\mathcal{U}})\coloneqq(K,v)^{\mathcal{U}}
of the valued field
(K,v)
.
Then, the composition
w\coloneqq u\circ v^{\mathcal{U}}
is tame with divisible value group as
u
and
v^{\mathcal{U}}
are both tame with divisible value group.

Now assume for a contradiction that
v
on
K
is definable, say
\mathcal{O}_{v}=\varphi(K,\overline{c})
for some
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-formula
\varphi
and some parameter tuple
\overline{c}\in K^{n}
.
Then, by Łoś’s theorem,
\mathcal{O}_{v^{\mathcal{U}}}=\varphi(K^{\mathcal{U}},\overline{c})
, so
v^{\mathcal{U}}
is definable in
K^{\mathcal{U}}
.
This contradicts
3.11
, as
v^{\mathcal{U}}
is a proper coarsening of the tame valuation
w
with divisible value group.
∎

We now give examples of perfectoid fields
(K,v)
, both in mixed and in positive characteristic, where the valuation
v
is not
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-definable, applying Proposition

3.12
.

Example 3.13.

Let
k
be a non-algebraically closed field of positive characteristic
such that
k\equiv k(\!(\mathbb{Q})\!)
. More precisely, we can start with any perfect field

l
, e.g.,
l=\mathbb{F}_{p}
, and consider the field
k=l(\!(\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q})\!)
.
We claim that
k\equiv k(\!(\mathbb{Q})\!)
.
Let
\nu
denote the Hahn series valuation on
k
with residue field

l
and value group
\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
, and let

w
be the composition of the
Hahn series valuation
v
on
k(\!(\mathbb{Q})\!)
and the valuation
\nu
on
k
.
Then, both
(k,\nu)
and
(k(\!(\mathbb{Q})\!),w)
are tame valued fields of characteristic
p

with divisible value group and the same perfect residue field
l
.
Thus, the Ax-Kochen/Ershov Theorem for tame fields
[
27
, Theorem 7.1]
implies

(k,\nu)\equiv(k(\!(\mathbb{Q})\!),w)

as valued fields.
In particular, we conclude that
k\equiv k(\!(\mathbb{Q})\!)
holds in the language of rings.

Then
k
is
t
-henselian of divisible tame type, and the valued field

(k(\!(\mathbb{Q})\!),v)
is perfectoid. By Proposition
3.12
,
v
is not definable.
Moreover, if
(K,u)
is any untilt of
(k(\!(\mathbb{Q})\!),v)
, then
u
is also
not definable by Proposition
3.12
.

While the example above provided an explicit example of a perfectoid field of positive
characteristic where the valuation was not
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-definable (even with parameters), it was less explicit in
mixed characteristic. Thus, we now also give an explicit construction in mixed characteristic.

Example 3.14.

Take once again a non-algebraically closed
field
k
of positive characteristic with
k\equiv k(\!(\mathbb{Q})\!)
.
An explicit example of a tame field
of mixed characteristic with divisible value group and residue field
k
can be constructed
as follows: start with the ring of Witt vectors
W[k(\!(\mathbb{Q})\!)]
, take its
fraction field
K_{0}=W[k(\!(\mathbb{Q})\!)][1/p]
,
and let
v_{0}

be the Witt vector valuation on
K_{0}
. Let
(K_{1},v_{1})
be a maximal totally ramified algebraic extension of
(K_{0},v_{0})
,
and let
v^{\mathrm{alg}}
be the unique extension of
v_{1}
to a fixed algebraic closure
K^{\mathrm{alg}}
.
Let
R
denote the ramification subgroup corresponding to
v^{\mathrm{alg}}/v_{1}
of
the absolute Galois group
\mathrm{Gal}(K_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}/K_{1})

(see
[
9
, Theorem 5.2.7]
).
Let
C
be any group theoretic complement of
R
in
\mathrm{Gal}(K_{1}^{\mathrm{alg}}/K_{1})
(such exist by
[
25
, Theorem 2.2]
), and let
K
be a fixed field of
C
.
Writing
v
for the
restriction of
v^{\mathrm{alg}}
to
K
, we get that
(K,v)
is a tame field of mixed characteristic
with value group
\mathbb{Q}

and residue field
k

[
25
, Proposition 4.5]
. Its completion

\widehat{(K,v)}
is a perfectoid field of mixed characteristic such that
v

is not
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-definable.

Main Theorem

We are now in a position to prove our main theorem:

Theorem 3.15.

Let
(K,v)
be perfectoid. Then
v
is definable if and only if

v
is
\emptyset
-definable if and only if at least one of the following holds

(1)

(K,v)
is not defectless,

(2)

vK
is not divisible, or

(3)

Kv
is not t-henselian of divisible-tame type.

Proof.

If
(K,v)
is not defectless, then
v
is
\emptyset
-definable by
3.8
.
If
vK
is not divisible, then
v
is
\emptyset
-definable by
3.1
.
If
Kv
is not t-henselian of divisible-tame type, then
v
is

\emptyset
-definable by
3.7
.

Conversely, assume that
\neg(1)
and
\neg(2)
and
\neg(3)
hold.
Since
(K,v)
is perfectoid, we know that it is henselian and
Kv
is perfect.
Together with
\neg(1)
,
(K,v)
is defectless, and
\neg(2)
,
vK
is divisible, we get that
(K,v)
is tame with divisible value group.
Now, by
\neg(3)
,
Kv
is t-henselian of divisible-tame type.
Thus, by
3.12
,
v
cannot be definable (even with parameters).
∎

Recall that, by
2.6
, a perfectoid field
(K,v)
and its tilt
(K^{\flat},v^{\flat})
have the same residue field and value group, and one of them is defectless if and only if the other is.
Thus we get the following corollary.

Corollary 3.16.

Let
(K,v)
be a perfectoid field of mixed characteristic.
Then,
v
is
(\emptyset
-
)
definable if and only if
v^{\flat}
is
(\emptyset
-
)
definable.

On closer inspection one can see that none of

3.1
,
3.7
,
3.9
or
3.12
required the
field
(K,v)
to be complete.
In each case, the assumptions applied to any rank-1 henselian valued field
(K,v)
of
residue characteristic
p>0
such that
K
is perfect (if
\chara{K}=p)
or
\mathcal{O}_{v}/p
is
semi-perfect (otherwise). However, by
[
17
, Theorem 5.1.4]
, any
such field
(K,v)
is an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}
-elementary substructure of its completion

\widehat{({K},{v})}=(\widehat{K},\widehat{v})
, and the latter is a perfectoid field. Thus, the
\emptyset
-definability
(respectively definability) of
v
and
\widehat{v}
are in fact equivalent.

4.
Producing and avoiding uniformity

One might hope to ask whether one can use the same formula when defining
v
and
v^{\flat}
.
In the explicit definition we have encountered when defining a valuation using a non-divisibility in the value group (see
3.1
), in a natural way the same
formulae defined
v
and
v^{\flat}
. However, due to the fact that characteristic
p
and characteristic
0

can be differentiated by an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-formula, we can always artificially
create or dispel uniformity, as illustrated in the next proposition:

Proposition 4.1.

Let
(K,v)
be perfectoid of mixed characteristic.
Assume that
v
is definable.
Then,

(1)

there is an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-formula
\varphi(x,\overline{y})
such that
\mathcal{O}_{v}=\varphi(K,\overline{c})
for some parameter tuple
\overline{c}\in K^{n}
but for any choice of
\overline{c}^{\prime}\in(K^{\flat})^{n}
we have
\mathcal{O}_{v^{\flat}}\neq\varphi(K^{\flat},\overline{c}^{\prime})
;

(2)

there is an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-formula
\varphi(x,\overline{y})
such that
\mathcal{O}_{v^{\flat}}=\varphi(K^{\flat},\overline{c}^{\prime})
for some parameter tuple
\overline{c}^{\prime}\in(K^{\flat})^{n}
but for any choice of
\overline{c}\in K^{n}
we have
\mathcal{O}_{v}\neq\varphi(K,\overline{c})
;

(3)

there is an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-formula
\varphi(x,\overline{y})
such that there are parameter tuples
\overline{c}\in K^{n}
and
\overline{c}\in(K^{\flat})^{n}
with
\mathcal{O}_{v}=\varphi(K,\overline{c})
and
\mathcal{O}_{v^{\flat}}=\varphi(K^{\flat},\overline{c}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Note that by
3.16
, also
v^{\flat}
is definable. Let
p=\chara(Kv)=\chara(K^{\flat})
and let
\chi_{p}:\equiv\underbrace{1+1+\ldots+1}_{p\text{-many}}=0
.

(1)

Let
\psi(x,\overline{y})
be some
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-formula defining
v
in
K
, i.e., there is a parameter tuple
\overline{c}\in K^{n}
such that
\mathcal{O}_{v}=\psi(K,\overline{c})
.
Let
\varphi(x,\overline{y}):\equiv\neg\chi_{p}\wedge\psi(x,\overline{y})
.
Since
K
has characteristic zero,
\neg\chi_{p}
holds true in
K
, so
\mathcal{O}_{v}=\psi(K,\overline{c})=\varphi(K,\overline{c})
.

On the other hand, as
K^{\flat}
has characteristic
p
,
\neg\chi_{p}
is false in
K^{\flat}
.
Thus, for any parameter tuple
\overline{c}^{\prime}\in(K^{\flat})^{n}
, we get

\mathcal{O}_{v^{\flat}}\neq\emptyset=\varphi(K^{\flat},\overline{c}^{\prime})
.

(2)

Similar to (1), use
\chi_{p}
instead of
\neg\chi_{p}
.

(3)

Let
\psi_{1}(x,\overline{y})
be some
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-formula defining
v
in
K
, i.e., there is a parameter tuple
\overline{c}\in K^{n}
such that
\mathcal{O}_{v}=\psi_{1}(K,\overline{c})
.
Let
\psi_{2}(x,\overline{y})
be some
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-formula defining
v^{\flat}
in
K^{\flat}
, i.e., there is a parameter tuple
\overline{c}^{\prime}\in(K^{\flat})^{n}
such that
\mathcal{O}_{v^{\flat}}=\psi_{2}(K^{\flat},\overline{c}^{\prime})
.
We may assume that both parameter tuples have the same length as we can always extend it by unused parameters.
Then,

\varphi(x,\overline{y}):\equiv(\neg\chi_{p}\wedge\psi_{1}(x,\overline{y}))\vee(\chi_{p}\wedge\psi_{2}(x,\overline{y}))

defines both
v
in
K
and
v^{\flat}
in
K^{\flat}
. Indeed, as
\neg\chi_{p}
holds in
K
, we have
\mathcal{O}_{v}=\psi_{1}(K,\overline{c})=\varphi(K,\overline{c})
, and as
\chi_{p}
holds in
K^{\flat}
, we have
\mathcal{O}_{v^{\flat}}=\psi_{2}(K^{\flat},\overline{c}^{\prime})=\varphi(K^{\flat},\overline{c}^{\prime})
.∎

5.
Quantifier complexity

The previous section illustrates that asking for a uniform definition for a perfectoid
valuation and its tilt has no merit.
In this section, we focus on the quantifier
complexity of such definitions instead. We show that
\exists
-
\emptyset
-definability
and
\forall
-
\emptyset
-definability both untilt, and that

\forall
-
\emptyset
-definability does not tilt. The question whether

\exists
-
\emptyset
-definability tilts remains open.

Our approach relies on work by Anscombe and Fehm
[
2
]
, and
we begin by recalling their definitions. They phrase all their definitions and statements in terms of

C
-fields. Here, we only consider the special case where
C=\mathbb{Z}.

Definition 5.1
(see
[
2
, Definition 3.5 and Lemma 3.7]
)
.

Let
K
be a field.

(1)

We say that
K
has embedded residue if there is some

K^{\prime}\equiv K
(in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
) and a nontrivial valuation
v
on
K^{\prime}
such that there is an embedding of
Kv
into
K^{\prime}
.

(2)

We say that
K
is
\mathbb{Z}
-large if there is some
K^{\prime}\equiv K
(in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
),
a subfield
E\subseteq K^{\prime}
and a nontrivial henselian valuation
v
on
E

such that
Ev
is isomorphic to
K^{\prime}
.

In fact, by
[
5
, Lemma 3.3]
, a field
K
is
\mathbb{Z}
-large if and only if it has the same
existential
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-theory as some field admitting a nontrivial henselian
valuation. Any large field in the sense of Pop is
\mathbb{Z}
-large.
Anscombe and Fehm show that not having embedded residue (respectively not being
\mathbb{Z}
-large) can be used to characterize which henselian valuations are

\exists
-
\emptyset
-definable (respectively
\forall
-
\emptyset
-definable):

Fact 5.2
(
[
2
, Corollary 5.3]
)
.

For any field
F
, the following are equivalent.

(0^{\exists})

The valuation ring is
\exists
-
\emptyset
-definable in
some
nontrivially henselian valued
field
(K,v)
with
\chara{K}=\chara{Kv}
and
Kv\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}}F
.

(1^{\exists})

There is a
\exists
-
\emptyset
-
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-formula

\psi_{F}(x)
that defines
the valuation ring in any henselian valued field
(K,v)
with
Kv\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}}F
.

(2^{\exists})

F
does not have embedded residue.

Also, the following are equivalent.

(0^{\forall})

The valuation ring is
\forall
-
\emptyset
-definable in

some
nontrivially henselian valued
field
(K,v)
with
\chara{K}=\chara{Kv}
and
Kv\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}}F
.

(1^{\forall})

There is a
\forall
-
\emptyset
-
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
-formula

\psi_{F}(x)
that defines
the valuation ring in any henselian valued field
(K,v)
with
Kv\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}}F
.

(2^{\forall})

F
is not
\mathbb{Z}
-large.

As an immediate consequence, we get that
\exists
-
\emptyset
-definability and

\forall
-
\emptyset
-definability untilt:

Corollary 5.3.

Let
(K,v)
be perfectoid with tilt
(K^{\flat},v^{\flat})
. If
v^{\flat}
is
definable by an
\exists
-
\emptyset
-formula (respectively

\forall
-
\emptyset
-formula) in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ring}}
, then so is
v
.

Proof.

As
Kv=K^{\flat}v^{\flat}
, this follows from
5.2

(0^{\exists})\implies(1^{\exists})

(respectively
(0^{\forall})\implies(1^{\forall})
).
∎

For
\forall
-
\emptyset
-definability, we can use
3.1
to show that it does
not tilt.

Example 5.4.

Let
(K,v)=\widehat{(\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\mu_{\infty}),v_{p})}
. Its residue field is the algebraically closed field
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathrm{alg}}
. Let
q\neq p
be a prime.
As
q\nmid v_{p}(p)
, the valuation
v_{p}
is

\forall
-
p
-definable by
3.1
, and – as
p
can be replaced by
{1+\dots+1}
(
p
times) – even
\forall
-
\emptyset
-definable.

However,
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathrm{alg}}
is
\mathbb{Z}
-large as it is algebraically closed
(
[
2
, Lemma 3.8]
).
Thus, by
5.2

(0^{\forall})\implies(2^{\forall})
,
in
(K^{\flat},v^{\flat})
the valuation
v^{\flat}
is not
\forall
-
\emptyset
-definable. We conclude that
\forall
-
\emptyset
-definability is not preserved under tilting.

If we could show that rank-1 non-divisible value groups also give rise
to
\exists
-
t
-definability of the valuation akin to
3.1
, we could use a variant of the same argument to see that
\exists
-
\emptyset
-definability
does not tilt. However, the existential analogue of Hong’s Theorem no longer holds.

Finding an example that illustrates that
\exists
-
\emptyset
-definability does not tilt
could also give a negative answer to

[
2
, Question 6.25]
.

6.
The classical case

We now take a closer look at the
classical case
, i.e., when

(K,v)
is perfectoid and
Kv
is an algebraic extension of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
For example, this happens if
(K,v)
is
obtained as
the completion of an algebraic extension
of one of
(\mathbb{Q}_{p},v_{p})
,
(\mathbb{Q},v_{p})
,
(\mathbb{F}_{p}(\!(t)\!),v_{t})
or

(\mathbb{F}_{p}(t),v_{t})
.

We prove that in this setting,
the perfectoid valuation is
\emptyset
-definable unless
K
is algebraically closed.
Moreover, in this case, we always get an

\exists
-
\emptyset
-definition, unless
Kv
is
algebraically closed.

We start with a simple lemma:

Lemma 6.1.

Let
F
be an algebraic extension of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

F
is t-henselian of divisible tame type.

(2)

F
is t-henselian.

(3)

F=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathrm{alg}}
.

Proof.

Clearly, (1) implies (2).

To prove that (2) implies (3), assume
F/\mathbb{F}_{p}
is algebraic. Then
F
is either finite or pseudo-algebraically closed (PAC)

[
12
, Corollary 11.2.4]
. If
F
is finite, so is any
L\equiv F
.
Since finite fields admit no nontrivial valuations,
L
cannot admit a nontrivial henselian valuation, so
F
is not
t
-henselian.
On the other hand, if
F
is PAC, so is any
L\equiv F

[
12
, Proposition 11.3.2]
.
By
[
12
, Corollary 11.5.5]
, any field which is both PAC and henselian must be separably closed.
Thus
L
, and by elementary equivalence also
F
, is separably closed, so
F=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathrm{alg}}
.

For (3) implies (1), see
3.3
.
∎

Proposition 6.2.

Let
(K,v)
be perfectoid and assume that
Kv
is an algebraic extension of

\mathbb{F}_{p}
. If
K
is not algebraically closed, then
v
is
\emptyset
-definable.

Proof.

Recall that
a defectless henselian valued field with algebraically closed residue field
and divisible value group is algebraically closed. Thus, assuming
K
is not algebraically
closed,
either
(K,v)
has defect, or
vK
is no divisible, or
Kv
is not algebraically
closed. In the first two cases,
v
is
\emptyset
-definable by

3.8
and

3.1
respectively. In the third case, we conclude that
v
is
\emptyset
-definable by combining
3.7
with
6.1
.
∎

Finally, we not that we get
\exists
-
\emptyset
-definability in the classical
case, unless
Kv
is algebraically closed. This is shown in
[
2
, Corollary 6.2]
:

Fact 6.3.

Let
(K,v)
be henselian and assume that
Kv\neq Kv^{\mathrm{alg}}

is an algebraic extension of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Then
v
is
\exists
-
\emptyset
-definable.

We cannot hope to obtain the same for
\forall
-
\emptyset
-definability, as the
following example shows.
This also gives us another example that
\forall
-
\emptyset
-definability does not tilt.

Example 6.4.

Let
F\neq F^{\mathrm{alg}}
be an infinite algebraic extension of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Consider
the perfectoid field
(F(\!(\mathbb{Z}[1/p])\!),v)
. Then
F
is large, and so

v
is not
\forall
-
\emptyset
-definable by

[
2
, Corollary 6.21]
. However, in every untilt
(K,u)
of
(F(\!(\mathbb{Z}[1/p])\!),v)
, the valuation
u
is
\forall
-
p
-definable: in each such untilt, we have

q\nmid u(p)
for some prime
q\neq p
(note that no non-zero element of
\mathbb{Z}[1/p]
is divisible by all primes
q
)
and so we can apply
3.1
. Since
p
is a
\emptyset
-definable
constant,
u
is in fact
\forall
-
\emptyset
-definable.
