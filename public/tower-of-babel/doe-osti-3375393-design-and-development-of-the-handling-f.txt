 1. Abstract
 The Handling Frame Assembly Table is a custom designed piece of equipment developed
to support the NIF Sustainment Project. The goal of the NIF Sustainment Project is to refurbish
the National Ignition Facility (NIF), the world’s largest and most energetic laser, so it can
continue advancing research in fusion energy, national security, and high energy density physics.
A key aspect of this effort involves replacing all 1,728 blast shields on the NIF, which requires
specialized equipment such as the Handling Frame Assembly Table to support the production of
new blast shields. This project follows a systems engineering approach that guides the design
sequence. This report documents the design process from initial concept through final design,
including stakeholder analysis, requirements development, conceptual design, final design, and
design validation. Future work will focus on building and commissioning the system.

 2. Background and Motivation
 The National Ignition Facility (NIF) (Figure 2.1), is the world’s largest and most
energetic laser, designed to create nuclear fusion by compressing and heating small fuel targets
with powerful lasers. It is roughly the size of three football fields. NIF has made significant
contributions to fusion energy research, national security, and high energy density physics,
including being the first facility in the world to achieve controlled fusion ignition in December
2022. Fusion ignition is defined as the point where fusion energy output exceeds laser energy
input. After over 15 years of operation, the team at NIF has become increasingly concerned over
decline in key components such as its amplifier systems (Figure 2.2). These systems are essential
for boosting the laser’s energy to its maximum of 2.2 MJ. One major issue within the amplifier
systems is the degradation of the sealant on the amplifier blast shields, which deposits onto the
amplifier slabs, increasing light scatter and the risk of laser-induced damage. This reduces the
optics’ lifetime and performance, making replacement critical for NIF’s continued mission.

 Figure 2.1: Diagram of the National Ignition Facility (NIF) [4]

 Figure 2.2: NIF Amplifier Frame Assembly Unit (FAU) [2]
 To maintain NIF’s high energy output, the NIF Sustainment Project aims to refurbish
many laser components, including all 1,728 blast shields and 772 laser glass slabs. The blast
shields are unique optics that require multiple processing steps, including cleaning, coating,
assembly into an LRU (Line Replaceable Unit), and testing, prior to installation onto NIF. The
original production loop for these shields was created and ran over 20 years ago, but much of the
technology and documentation has been lost, necessitating a complete redesign and rebuilding of
the process. While lessons can be drawn from other optic production loops supporting the NIF,
each system within this blast shield production loop must be custom designed due to differences
in size and function of the optic.
 A critical component of the blast shield production loop is the Handling Frame Assembly
Table. Its purpose is to assist operators in assembling blast shield glass into a specialized
handling frame (Figure 3.1), which is necessary for safe transport and processing of the optic
through the production loop. The system also stores the components required for assembly of the

handling frame. Key design challenges include ensuring operator safety, meeting stakeholder
requirements, maintaining cleanliness, and managing risk.
 This capstone project centers on the design development of the Handling Frame
Assembly Table, applying a systems engineering approach, which is a structured,
multidisciplinary method for developing complex systems from concept to operation. The
systems engineering process follows the V diagram model, illustrated in Figure 2.1. This project
addresses the left side of the V diagram, including stakeholder analysis, requirements
development, conceptual design, final design, and design validation. Additionally, lessons
learned and future work are discussed.

 As the responsible engineer (RE), I oversaw all aspects of the work from initial
stakeholder engagement and requirements definition through design development, analysis, and
validation, while leading and collaborating with a designer who developed the detailed CAD
model of the system under my direction.

 3. Objectives and Stakeholder Analysis
 Following a systems engineering approach, the first step in any project is to determine its
scope. For this project, my primary responsibility was to design a system that would assist
operators with assembling the blast shield optic into a handling frame (Figure 3.1), while also
providing storage for the handling frame components. The assembled blast shield handing frame
is 6.4 ft x 2.6 ft x 2 inches (L x W x H). Once I understood the overall scope, I identified all
relevant entities (individuals or groups) with a vested interest in the project’s outcome. Examples
of stakeholders included representatives from process engineering, production, cleanliness,
interfacing systems, management, safety and health, and maintenance. Within each stakeholder
area, I interviewed specific individuals to gather their unique needs.

 6

 Figure 3.1: Blast Shield Glass Installed into Handling Frame
 For example, the cleanliness stakeholder group emphasized that the system must maintain
both the cleanliness of the room and the optic. The production team outlined requirements for
throughput and specified the number of operators who would be able to support using the system
during production. Management set constraints on the project timeline and budget, while safety
and health highlighted compliance with laboratory policies. These examples highlight only a
subset of the stakeholders needs. While interviewing stakeholders, a high-level concept of
operations (ConOps) was created to understand how the system would be used. This is necessary
in order to help develop the requirements described in the next section. Stakeholders were
interviewed multiple times to ensure their needs were accurately captured. The needs identified
during these interviews directly informed the system requirements, which are described in the
following section.

 4. Design Requirements
 After conducting interviews with stakeholders and identifying their needs, the next step
was to translate this information into specific requirements. Requirements are criteria that guide
the design of the system, ensuring that, if met, the stakeholders’ needs will be fulfilled. These
requirements are documented and subsequently reviewed with both stakeholders and the project
team. They function as a formal agreement between the engineer and the stakeholders, outlining
the expectations for the system’s design. Stakeholders provide feedback during this process to
confirm that the requirements accurately reflect their needs.

 For organizational clarity, the requirements for this project were divided into the
following categories: Functional & Performance, Safety, Interface, Operational, and Standards &
Regulations. Each requirement includes a rationale that connects it directly to the underlying
stakeholder need. It also includes a method to which the requirement will be verified that it is
met during system commissioning. The information presented in this section highlights the key
aspects of the requirements development process; additional details and the full requirements list
can be found in [5]. A list of the key driving requirements from the system requirements review
are shown in Figure 4.1. The requirements are not fully finalized at this stage and were
developed further along with the design of the system given feedback from stakeholders at the
review. After reviewing the requirements with stakeholders and the project team, the committee
approved the project to proceed to the next phase.

 5. Concept Development
 After the requirements for the system have been agreed upon with stakeholders, the next
part of the systems engineering process is to create a high-level concept for a system that would
meet all requirements. While there are many requirements for the system, only a few of the
requirements drive the high-level conceptual design of the system. The overall conceptual design
is shown in Figure 5.1.

 8

 Figure 5.1: Conceptual Design Presented at CDR [6]
 The conceptual design measures 96 in x 53 in x 43 in (L x W x H) and has a total weight
of approximately 1200 lbs when fully loaded with handling frames and an optic. One of the high-
level requirements for the system was to provide physical support for operators for assembling
the optic into the handling frame, which drove the overall system to be a table-like structure.
Another key requirement was storage of the L-frame components of the handling frame. The
weight of the system was driven by the requirement to store the handling frame components
(~300 lbs); while also needing to withstand expected seismic loading (requirement 3.2). The L-
frames are the two largest components that make up the handling frame (Figure 3.1) that
surround the optic. Multiple methods were considered such as adding slots below the table to slot
the L-frames into, creating a whole separate rack to store them, and a drawer under the table
(Figure 5.2). Ultimately a drawer concept was pursued as it maximizes operators ergonomics,
saves space within the cleanroom, and minimizes risk of damaging the frames.

 Figure 5.2: L-Frame Storage Concepts Presented at CDR [6]

 9

 Additional key requirements included the system’s ability to withstand normal and
seismic loading (requirement 3.2). This was addressed in part by adding angled brackets to the
table legs, allowing the system to be securely bolted to the floor. Bolting the system to the floor
is necessary to prevent it from sliding or tipping during a seismic event, which could pose a
safety risk to operators. Pins on the tabletop were incorporated to directly support the optic,
while surrounding blocks were designed to hold the handling frame components during
assembly. The surrounding blocks were attached to clamps that allowed the blocks to be pushed
in and out. This enables operators to set the glass on the pins without needing fine alignment,
which would be difficult given the glass’s heavy weight (70 lbs). Ultimately the combination of
the features was believed necessary to meet requirement 2.1. Delrin was selected for these
support features to minimize the risk of scratching the optics, which is essential for maintaining
optic integrity (requirement 2.8). The overall system is constructed from aluminum, stainless
steel, and Delrin to meet stringent cleanliness requirements (requirement 2.6 and 2.7).
 Another requirement we had to meet was an interface requirement with a lift used to
unload the handling frame after assembly (requirement 4.1). This requirement specifies that the
design must comply with the criteria outlined in the Interface Control Document (ICD), ensuring
that the equipment does not interfere geometrically. To verify compliance, the responsible
individual (RI) for the lift provided a CAD model of the lift’s geometrical design space, which
was incorporated into my system model for comparison and validation. To assist operators with
alignment when lifting the handling frame, an alignment guide was installed beneath the table.
This guide captures the lift’s legs and positions the lift correctly for connection to the handling
frame.
 Another important element of the conceptual design is the concept of operations, which
outlines how the system will be used in practice. This helps demonstrate the system’s
functionality in the field. The high-level concept of operations (ConOps) for the system is
illustrated in Figure 5.3. The concept of operations was reviewed with the production team to
ensure that it meets their expectations.

Environment, Safety, and Health (ES&H) team reviewed the planned activities and their

 6. Final Design
 After the conceptual design for the system had been completed, the next step was to take
the system to its final design. This involved finalizing the CAD of the system and releasing all
part/assembly engineering drawings. Figure 6.1 shows the final design presented at the final
design review.

 11

 Figure 6.1: Handling Frame Assembly Table Design Presented at FDR [7]
 The system measures 96 in x 53 in x 43 in (L x W x H) and has a total weight of
approximately 1200 lbs when fully loaded with handling frames and an optic. It is divided into
three main subsystems: the drawer, the table structure, and the tabletop fixturing.

 Figure 6.2: Drawer Subsystem Isometric View [7]
 The drawer subsystem (Figure 6.2) measures 86 in x 44 in x 11 in (L x W x H). The
subsystem uses commercial off the shelf (COTS) drawer slides that are bolted to the tabletop
structure to pull the drawer in and out. The drawer itself is constructed from a 6061-T6
aluminum frame using a combination of welded and bolted joints. 6061-T6 aluminum was
selected for its high strength to weight ratio and low cost.
 A key feature of the drawer is the set of Delrin guides used to hold and store the handling
frames. Delrin was chosen to minimize scratching or damage to the frames during loading and
unloading. The edge-mounted Delrin guides also help prevent the handling frames from shifting
while the operator opens and closes the drawer.

 12

 Figure 6.3: Table Structure Subsystem Isometric View [7]
 The tabletop structure (Figure 6.3) consists of an aluminum tabletop bolted to solid
aluminum legs. Each leg is equipped with a leveling foot to allow precise leveling of the system,
which is required for alignment during assembly of the handling frames. The legs are also bolted
to L brackets that attach to the floor where the system is installed. These connections act as
seismic restraints to prevent tipping or sliding during a seismic event.
 The tabletop is made from a large 5052-H32 aluminum sheet that is bent and reinforced
with welded stiffener bars. The structure includes storage troughs on each side for handling
frame assembly components (pins and center bars), cleaning supplies for wiping down the
frames, and a torque wrench for tightening fasteners.
 Another key aspect of the table structure is the side and rear bracing. This bracing
provides additional structural support so the system can withstand seismic loading. The brace
bars are bolted to the legs at approximately mid height.

 13

 Figure 6.4: Tabletop Fixturing Isometric View [7]
 The table fixturing subsystem was the part of the final design that changed the most from
the conceptual design, as the clamps were removed. This was done based on reviewer feedback
at the CDR, as stakeholders believed there was risk with sliding the glass on the pins when it is
pushed into place. The new subsystem consists of only three primary components and is
designed to support operators during assembly and to protect the blast shield optic from damage.
The fixturing includes pins, a handling frame support block, and a center bar alignment block.
 The pins support the glass during assembly and have a rounded profile to minimize the
risk of damage. All glass contact surfaces are made from Delrin to further reduce the chance of
scratching or chipping the optic.
 The handling frame support block incorporates a dual height feature. The lower surface
supports the frames before they are connected to the glass, which reduces the risk of damaging
the optic while the frames are being positioned. Guides around the glass act as stops to prevent
the optic from sliding during installation and under seismic loading. The rear blocks are set at a
higher elevation so the optic can be banked during installation, making it easier for operators to
place the glass onto the system.
 The center bar alignment fixture is used to position the center bar prior to installing it
onto the frame. Because operator visibility of the pin holes is limited by the optic located above
the bar, this fixture helps align the holes and reduces both installation time and difficulty. The
bar is not installed above the optic in order to eliminate the risk of dropping it onto the glass.

 7. Design Validation
 As part of the Failure Mode and Effects Analysis, one identified failure mode was
operator injury resulting from collapse of the system during a seismic event. The selected
mitigation was to perform a structural seismic analysis of the system to ensure that it would not
collapse under the expected seismic loading. The system is assumed to be fully loaded with
handling frames along with a blast shield optic on top of the table as it is the most conservative
scenario. This analysis was conducted and documented within a safety note [8]. The following
section presents a high-level description of the analysis that was performed, along with a
summary of the calculated safety factors for each component.
 The analysis began by modeling the system as a single rigid body, applying seismic
loads at the center of gravity, and calculating the reactions at the connection between the table
and the floor (see Figure 7.1). After determining these reactions, the table was divided into
multiple rigid bodies, and the forces and moments at each interface were computed using the
reaction forces solved for in the first single rigid body model. Once all forces and moments
acting on each component were established, the stress within each component along the load
path was evaluated to assess potential failure. To validate the calculations, the resulting forces at
the tabletop center of gravity from the multiple rigid body model were compared to the seismic
forces initially applied to the table to show they are equivalent.
 The key assumptions of the system are listed below to solve the reactions and forces
acting on the system during a seismic event.
 1. The front legs connection to the floor are modeled as rollers under seismic force in the
 X direction, since there is no front bracing plate and stiffness is lower compared to the
 rear legs, allowing movement in that direction. The front legs under seismic force in
 the remaining directions and the rear legs under load in all directions are modeled as
 pinned.
 2. Connections between bracing plates (Item 5 & 6) and legs are translationally fixed in
 all directions. The back bracing plate to leg connections is considered rotationally
 fixed about the Y and the side bracing plate to leg connections is considered
 rotationally fixed about the X. These assumptions are made because the bracing plates
 stiffness prevents rotation about specific axes.
 3. Connections between legs and tabletop are assumed to be pinned, as the stiffness of the
 tabletop to leg connection is assumed to be less than the bracing plates below and
 allows rotation.

 15

 4. Axial forces in the bracing plates are assumed to be zero, as bending stresses
 dominate.
 5. Axial forces in the bolts connecting the bracing plates to the legs are assumed to be
 zero, since the bracing plate in the parallel direction to axial loading is expected to
 carry the load due to its higher stiffness than the perpendicular bracing plate.

 Figure 7.1 – Overall Seismic Free Body Diagram (One Rigid Body) [8]
 Since the direction of seismic forces can vary, each component within the system may
experience different combinations of forces and, consequently, a different worst-case SF on an
individual component basis. For the purposes of this note, only a single case is analyzed with
respect to the directions of the applied forces. However, all possible combinations of seismic
force directions were evaluated, and each component achieved a rare event safety factor greater
than 1. The scenario analyzed in this note is SDZ and SDY as positive, while SDX is negative.
This is the worst-case scenario as it results in the lowest safety factor for any individual
component. The square root of the sum of the squares (SRSS) method, discussed in the DSS, is
used for combining forces acting in the same direction resulting from seismic loading in
different directions.
 The loading of the blast shield handling frame assembly on the Delrin fixturing and its
bolted connection to the table surface during a seismic event is considered acceptable based on
engineering judgment and is therefore not included in the load path discussion below. In the
worst case, during a seismic event, the handling frame assembly (weight approximately 100 lb)
is expected to apply a total shear load of about 200 lb to the Delrin guides. This load is shared by
two guides, each with a cross section of 1/4 in by 3 in. Each Delrin guide is attached to the table
with two M6 bolts, so each bolt is expected to carry approximately 50 lb of shear. Given the low
magnitude of the applied loads relative to the size and capacity of the Delrin guides and bolts,
failure of these components is not worth analyzing in a detailed calculation.

 8. Lessons Learned
 Throughout the system design cycle, I have gained a wide range of insights, but
several key lessons stand out.
 One significant challenge was designing a tabletop that simultaneously satisfied
stakeholder aesthetic expectations and provided sufficient structural capacity for the anticipated
loading. Addressing this required considerable effort to develop an appropriate interface between
the tabletop and the supporting legs. This helped increase my skills in integrating aesthetic
requirements with structural connection design. I also developed a deeper appreciation for
cleanliness requirements. Because the table is intended for use in a class 100 cleanroom, material
selection becomes critical, and through this project I learned which materials are suitable for
such controlled environments.
 Another major lesson was the importance of well-defined requirements in the product
development process. Requirements effectively function as a contract between the stakeholders
and the engineer, clearly stating what the system will and will not do. I learned that stakeholders
do not always initially articulate their true needs, and that it is essential to ask detailed, probing
questions to uncover those needs. I also observed how seemingly minor requirements can
significantly increase both cost and development time, which underscored the need to ensure that
each requirement exists to satisfy a genuine stakeholder need and not to add unnecessary scope.
 In addition, I learned how to perform structural analysis to demonstrate that the system is
safe under both operational and seismic loading conditions. This work helped me better
understand how analysis supports design decisions and risk reduction. I also gained experience in
applying rigor selectively, learning to identify which aspects of the system warrant more detailed
analysis and effort. Since both budget and engineering time are finite, it is necessary to prioritize
higher risk items rather than distributing effort uniformly across all elements of the design.

 19

 Finally, I learned how influential decisions made during the conceptual design phase can
be on the overall difficulty and trajectory of a project. Early design choices can either simplify
downstream work or introduce avoidable complexity, so investing sufficient time and thought in
the conceptual design phase is critical to the success of the overall system.

 9. Conclusion & Future Work
 The primary objective of this project was to develop and deliver a validated final design
for the handling frame assembly table system that meets the needs of all identified stakeholders.
To achieve this, the work encompassed stakeholder analysis, requirements development,
conceptual design, final design refinement, and engineering analysis to validate the system
performance.
 Through the execution of this project, I strengthened my proficiency in several key areas,
including stakeholder analysis, requirements development, risk mitigation, engineering design,
material selection, and structural analysis. The final design and its supporting engineering
analyses have been reviewed by the relevant stakeholders, and consensus has been reached that
the design is suitable to move forward. The immediate next step was the procurement of all
components required for the system, which began is January 2026.
 The subsequent phase of the project will involve system assembly, during which the
handling frame assembly table system will be fully constructed. Following assembly, the system
will be installed at the OPFX facility and commissioned to verify that it satisfies all specified
requirements. Current plans anticipate that the system will enter operation in late 2026 or early
2027.
 In terms of future work, several activities will be critical. Following system assembly and
installation at the OPFX facility, an equipment commissioning test and a process engineering
commissioning test will be carried out to confirm that the as-built system aligns with the design
intent and stakeholder requirements. Once operational, ongoing monitoring and evaluation
should be implemented to track system performance, identify any emerging issues, and support
continuous improvement. These activities will be governed by an operational procedure and a
maintenance procedure that will be developed in the coming months. Finally, after successful
commissioning and validation, the system will be formally handed over to operations, where it
will be used to support the production of new blast shields for the National Ignition Facility
(NIF).
