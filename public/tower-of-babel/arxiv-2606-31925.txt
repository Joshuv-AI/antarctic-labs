Fate of "Space-like singularities" in c = 1 c=1 Matrix Model
Sumit R. Das, Shaun D. Hampton, Sinong Liu, Gautam Mandal

Abstract
A class of time dependent backgrounds in two dimensional String Theory leads to superluminal Liouville walls on the worldsheet. In the dual double scaled c = 1 c=1 matrix model these backgrounds involve eigenvalues leaking out to infinity, and the collective field fluctuations become strongly coupled along space-like regions, resembling singularities. We realize these backgrounds as results of quantum quenches in the matrix model, retaining non-linear terms in the matrix potential, thus departing from a double scaling limit. Working in the fermion picture in a Thomas-Fermi approximation, we show that while the early time behavior of the phase space density near the maximum of the potential agrees with that obtained in the double scaled theory, at times of the order ( log ⁡ N ) (\log~N) the effect of the IR wall becomes significant. At later times, with a characteristic winding time of order ( log ⁡ N ) 2 (\log N)^{2} , folds on the fermi surface proliferate and eventually cover the allowed region in phase space densely. Using action-angle variables, we show that the phase space density oscillates around a time independent and angle independent value rapidly at late times. A coarse-grained density in the angle space relaxes to a time independent equilibrium value as a power law with a universal exponent largely independent of the details of the initial state. Thus, the appearance of a space-like singularity is an artifact of the strict double scaling limit. We comment on the interpretation of the final state in String Theory.

In this sense the late time fate of the space-like singularity is robust.

5
The late time fate

The results of
section 4
show that away from the double scaling limit an initial fermi surface (
22
) develops folds which proliferate. In fact folds are almost inevitable in a theory of non-relativistic fermions since fermions with higher momentum move faster. It was shown in
fold1
;
fold2
that such folds are signatures of the presence of large quantum fluactations of the density operator.
This means that the
classical bosonization formulae
(
14
) do not work any more, even though a bosonization at the quantum level is possible. It is, however, still possible to describe the state in terms of a classical (Thomas-Fermi) phase space density.
Appendix D
contains a review of this argument.

At late times, the phase space density develops thin filaments which uniformly fill the region between the minimum and maximum energy orbits, pretty much like the systems discussed in
mandalkulkarni
. Significantly, this outcome holds for both
r<1
and
r>1
.

It is useful to discuss this final state in terms of action-angle variables in phase space. For our case these are given by

\displaystyle J(x,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint dx~p(x)=-\frac{1}{3\pi}\omega(x,p)\left[\frac{2}{g}{\bf{E}}(k(x,p))-\frac{4}{g}\left(1-\omega(x,p)^{2}\right){\bf{K}}(k(x,p))\right]~,

\displaystyle\theta(x,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{{\bf{K}}(k(x,p))}{\bf{F}}(\phi(x,p),k(x,p))~,&p\cdot\operatorname{sgn}(x)>0\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{{\bf{K}}(k(x,p))}\left[2{\bf{K}}(k(x,p))-{\bf{F}}(\phi(x,p),k(x,p))\right]~,&p\cdot\operatorname{sgn}(x)<0\end{cases}~.

(33)

Here
{\bf{K}}(k),{\bf{E}}(k)
are the complete elliptic integrals of the first and second kinds, respectively and
{\bf{F}}(\phi,k)
is the incomplete elliptic function of the first kind with

\sin^{2}\phi(x,p)=\frac{1-\frac{g}{2}\frac{x^{2}}{\omega^{2}(x,p)}}{k^{2}}~.

(34)

The functions
\omega(x,p),k(x,p)
are defined in (
24
).
More details can be found in
Appendix C
.
Since this is a canonical transformation the phase space density is

u(\theta,J,t)=u(x,p,t)

(35)

With our convention that the physical orbit energy is
-E
, the trajectory of a point in phase space
(\theta,J)
now obeys

\partial_{t}\theta=\Omega(J)=\frac{\partial(-E)}{\partial J}~,\qquad\partial_{t}J=0~,

(36)

thus mapping the problem to that of free fermions
without any external potential
on a circle. A trajectory in phase space is then characterized by a constant value of
J
and

\theta(t)=\Omega(J)t+\theta_{0}~.

(37)

The Liouville equation is

[\partial_{t}+\Omega(J)\partial_{\theta}]u(\theta,J,t)=0~.

(38)

In our case we have for a given point in phase space

\Omega(J)=-\frac{\pi\omega}{{\bf{K}}(k)}=-|\Omega(J)|~,

(39)

where
k
is the modulus defined in (
24
).

The equations (
37
) and (
38
) then imply that given some initial phase space distribution
u_{0}(\theta,J)
at time
t=0
, the phase space density at any given time is

u(\theta,J,t)=u_{0}(\theta(-t),J)=\theta[{\tilde{F}}(\theta,J)-1]~,

(40)

where
{\tilde{F}}(\theta,J)=F(x,p)
as defined in section (
3
), where we have taken the initial time
T=0
. Due to the quadratic nature of the initial profile (
20
) and the expression for the energy (
28
), a constant
J
(which is also constant
E
) line intersects the initial fermi surface at most twice, say at
\theta_{0,\pm}(J)
. Then we can write, using (
37
),

u(\theta,J,t)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\Theta\left[(\theta_{0+}(J)-\left|\Omega(J)\right|t)-(\theta+2\pi m)\right]\Theta\left[(\theta+2\pi m)-(\theta_{0-}(J)-\left|\Omega(J)\right|t)\right]~,

(41)

where the sum over the integer
m
is required to ensure periodicity of
u(\theta,J,t)
under
\theta\sim\theta+2\pi
. Using Poisson resummation

\sum_{m}f(2\pi m)=\sum_{k}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dz~f(z)~e^{-ikz}

(42)

and performing the
z
integral using the explicit form of
u(\theta,J,t)
we get, after separating the
k=0
term,

\displaystyle u(\theta,J,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\theta_{0+}(J)-\theta_{0-}(J)\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{k\neq 0}\frac{1}{2\pi ik}\left[{\rm{exp}}\left(ik(\theta_{0+}(J)-|\Omega(J)|t-\theta)\right)-{\rm{exp}}\left(ik(\theta_{0-}(J)-|\Omega(J)|t-\theta)\right)\right]~.

The first time independent term is the total number of fermions with a given
J
, or given energy. Since the fermions are free, this is a conserved quantity.

The time dependent terms are oscillatory in time. At late times one expects that they vanish. To determine the way this relaxation occurs it is useful to define a coarse grained phase space density by averaging over
J
with some kernel whose width is determined below.

It is easier to peform this averaging by integrating over
\Omega
, with a kernel
K_{\sigma}(\Omega)

with a central value
\bar{\Omega}
and a width
\sigma
:

u_{cg,\sigma}(\bar{J},\theta,t)=\int d\Omega~K_{\sigma}(\Omega)u(J(\Omega),\theta,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\beta~\tilde{K}_{\sigma}(\beta)\int_{\Omega_{1}}^{\Omega_{2}}d\Omega~e^{-i\beta\Omega}u(J(\Omega,\theta,t))~,

(44)

where
\bar{J}
is the value of
J
at
\bar{\Omega}
.

\tilde{K}_{\sigma}(\beta)
is the Fourier transform of
K_{\sigma}(\Omega)

and
\Omega_{1}=|\Omega(J(E_{\min}))|,~\Omega_{2}=|\Omega(J(E_{\max}))|
are the smallest and largest value of
\Omega
on the initial Fermi surface, repectively. Clearly
\theta_{0,+}(\Omega_{1,2})-\theta_{0,-}(\Omega_{1,2})=0\pmod{2\pi}
.
In (
44
) all quantities in the integrand should be expressed as functions of
\Omega
.

We are interested in evaluating (
44
) at late times, much larger than the width
\frac{1}{\sigma}
of the kernel
\tilde{K}_{\sigma}(\beta)
,

t\gg\frac{1}{\sigma}~.

(45)

The integral over
\beta
is then effectively over small values of
\beta
. Now, the integral over
\Omega
can now be performed along the lines of
mandalkulkarni
. Consider the contribution from a term with
k>0
.
We need to evaluate

\int_{\Omega_{1}}^{\Omega_{2}}\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi ik}\left[e^{[ik(\theta_{0,+}-\Omega t-\theta)-i\beta\Omega]}-e^{[ik(\theta_{0,-}-\Omega t-\theta)-i\beta\Omega]}\right]~.

(46)

Now deform the contour of integration to the contour in the lower half plane:
[\Omega_{1},\Omega_{1}-i\Lambda,\Omega_{2}-i\Lambda,\Omega_{2}]
with
\Lambda\gg 1
,
so that we get for the first term in (
46
)

\left[\int_{\Omega_{1}}^{\Omega_{1}-i\Lambda}-\int_{\Omega_{2}}^{\Omega_{2}-i\Lambda}+\int_{\Omega_{1}-i\Lambda}^{\Omega_{2}-i\Lambda}\right]\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi ik}~e^{[ik(\theta_{0,+}-\Omega t-\theta)-i\beta\Omega]}~.

(47)

The third term in (
47
) decays exponentially for large
t
, provided
kt+\beta>0
. In the first and second terms, the dominant contributions come from the region near the real axis, where
\Omega=\Omega_{1}-i\eta
in the first term and
\Omega=\Omega_{2}-i\zeta
and taking the range of
(\eta,\zeta)\in(0,\infty)
. Therefore the leading effect can be estimated by looking at the expansion of
\theta_{0,+}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\theta_{0,+}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(\Omega_{1})+\gamma_{1}^{+}(\Omega-\Omega_{1})^{\alpha_{1}^{+}}+\cdots~,\qquad\Omega\sim\Omega_{1}~,

\displaystyle\theta_{0,+}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(\Omega_{2})+\gamma_{2}^{+}(\Omega_{2}-\Omega)^{\alpha_{2}^{+}}+\cdots~,\qquad\Omega\sim\Omega_{2}~.

(48)

Expanding the integrand in small
\eta,\zeta
and performing the integrals of
\eta,\zeta
one gets for large
t
,

\int_{\Omega_{1}}^{\Omega_{2}}\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi ik}e^{[ik(\theta_{0,+}-\Omega t-\theta)-i\beta\Omega]}\sim O\left(\frac{1}{(\beta+kt)^{\alpha_{1}^{+}+1}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{(\beta+kt)^{\alpha_{2}^{+}+1}}\right)

(49)

upto terms which oscillate as a function of time. For details of the integrals, see a completely analogous calculation in
mandalkulkarni
.
The estimation of the second term in (
46
) is entirely similar. Expanding

\displaystyle\theta_{0,-}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(\Omega_{1})+\gamma_{1}^{-}(\Omega-\Omega_{1})^{\alpha_{1}^{-}}+\cdots~,\qquad\Omega\sim\Omega_{1}

\displaystyle\theta_{0,-}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(\Omega_{2})+\gamma_{2}^{-}(\Omega_{2}-\Omega)^{\alpha_{2}^{-}}+\cdots~,\qquad\Omega\sim\Omega_{2}

(50)

and one gets estimates like (
49
) with
\alpha_{1,2}^{+}\rightarrow\alpha_{1,2}^{-}
. Therefore the time dependent parts of
u_{cg}
behave as

\int d\beta~\tilde{K}_{\sigma}(\beta)\frac{1}{(\beta+kt)^{\alpha+1}}~,\qquad\alpha={\rm min}(\alpha_{1,2}^{\pm})~.

(51)

For late times (
45
) we need to consider only values of
\beta
such that
t\gg\beta
. Therefore
we conclude that the coarse grained density approaches a time independent steady state value with a power law decay
t^{-(\alpha+1)}
. For a coarse graining scale
\sigma
which is small compared to the scale of the variation of
\theta_{0,\pm}(\Omega)
, the steady state value is given by

u_{cg,\sigma}(\bar{J},\theta)\sim\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\theta_{0+}(\bar{\Omega})-\theta_{0-}(\bar{\Omega})\right)~.

(52)

This is of course independent of
\theta
, a signature that the system asymptotes to a Generalized Gibbs Ensemble characterized by the conserved number of fermions at each value of the energy.

It is significant that the exponent
\alpha
of the relaxation to this steady state value does not depend on the details of the initial profile – this only depends on how the
\theta_{0,\pm}
behave near their extrema.

6
Conclusion

Our results show that the space-like singularities found in geometries emerging from time dependent solutions of the
c=1
matrix quantum mechanics are artifacts of the double scaling limit. In the fermion phase space these solutions correspond to fermions draining off at infinity, and a proper understanding of the late time state cannot be possible without an IR completion. In this paper we used a quartic potential as the IR completion and showed that at time scales of
O(\log N)
the fermion system approaches a Generalized Gibbs Ensemble. The details of this approach of course depend on the particular IR completion and are not universal. However the fact that such a steady state is approached is clear.

It is important to note that the phase space density keeps oscillating in time around some background function at arbitrarily late times. However in terms of a coarse grained density obtained by averaging over an energy window the system relaxes to a steady state in a power law fashion with universal exponents.

The main question is: what is the String Theory interpretation of this state ? Note that we are working in a regime where the unscaled fermi level
\mu_{F}
is not tuned to its critical value. Consequently, if we think of the ’t Hooft Feynman diagrams generated by the matrix model as discretized world sheets, we are away from the limit where the worldsheet is continuous. Intriguingly, it has been recently suggested that matrix integrals can have a string interpretation even away from the double scaling limit
gopa
;
mazenc
– it would be interesting if there is a similar correspondence in our case. We hope to return to this important issue in the near future.

Appendix A
Double Scaling Limit

The action of this model is given by

S=\beta\int dt~{\rm Tr}\left[(D_{t}M)^{2}-U(M)\right]~,\qquad\beta=\frac{N}{\kappa}~.

(53)

Here
M(t)
is a
N\times N
Hermitian matrix,
\beta\sim N
is a coupling and
D_{t}=\partial_{t}-iA_{t}
is a covariant derivative.
U(M)
is a potential which has a quadratic maximum at
M=0
, e.g.

U(M)=-\frac{1}{2}M^{2}+\frac{1}{4}M^{4}+\cdots

(54)

As is well known, in the
A_{t}=0
gauge the Gauss law constraint restricts us to the singlet sector. The theory then reduces to the theory of eigenvalues
\lambda_{i},i=1\cdots N
. After a standard redefinition of the wavefunction, these eigenvalues become free fermions. The Hamiltonian of the second quantized fermion field
\Psi(\lambda,t)

H_{F}=\int d\lambda\left[\frac{1}{2\beta}(\partial_{\lambda}\Psi^{\dagger})(\partial_{\lambda}\Psi)+\beta U(\lambda)\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi\right]~,\qquad\int d\lambda~\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi=N~.

(55)

The double scaling limit is
N\rightarrow\infty
and the fermi energy
-\epsilon_{F}\rightarrow 0
with

g_{s}=-\frac{1}{2\beta\mu_{F}}

(56)

and
\kappa
held fixed. The limit
\mu_{F}\rightarrow 0
renders the worldsheet formed by Feynman-’t Hooft diagrams continuous. Then
g_{s}
appears as the string coupling providing weights of worldsheets with different topologies.
Rescaling coordinates and fields

\lambda=(\beta g_{s})^{-1/2}x~,\qquad\Psi=(\beta g_{s})^{1/4}\psi~,

(57)

one immediately sees that only the universal quadratic part of the potential survives in the double scaling limit. The higher order terms in
U(\lambda)
are suppressed by powers of
1/(\beta g_{s})
. The double scaled fermion hamiltonian becomes

H_{ds}=\frac{1}{g_{s}}\int dx\left[\frac{g_{s}^{2}}{2}(\partial_{x}\psi^{\dagger})(\partial_{x}\psi)+\frac{1}{2}U(x)\psi^{\dagger}\psi\right]~,\qquad\int dx~\psi^{\dagger}\psi=N~.

(58)

The collective field and its conjugate momentum are given by the operator relations

\rho(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\beta}\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi=\frac{1}{\beta}\delta(\lambda\cdot I-M)~,\qquad\Pi_{\rho}=-i\frac{\delta}{\delta\rho}~.

(59)

We now rescale

\rho=(\beta g_{s})^{-1/2}\partial_{x}\varphi~,\qquad\partial_{\lambda}\Pi_{\rho}=-(\beta g_{s})^{3/2}\Pi_{\varphi}

(60)

to pass to the double scaling limit. At leading order, the Hamiltonian for the collective field is given by

H=\int dx\left[\frac{g_{s}^{2}}{2}\Pi_{\varphi}(\partial_{x}\varphi)\Pi_{\varphi}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6g_{s}^{2}}(\partial_{x}\varphi)^{3}-\frac{x^{2}}{2g_{s}^{2}}(\partial_{x}\varphi)+\frac{1}{2g_{s}^{2}}(\partial_{x}\varphi)\right]~.

(61)

The last term comes from a Lagrange multiplier which imposes the condition
\int dx\psi^{\dagger}\psi=N
.

Appendix B
Calculation of
E_{\max}
,
E_{\min}
, and the corresponding winding time

B.1
Period of individual fermions in the double-well potential

To obtain the period, it is useful to rewrite the Jacobi elliptic functions in terms of the Jacobi amplitude,

\operatorname{sn}(u,k)=\sin[\operatorname{am}(u,k)]~,\qquad\operatorname{cn}(u,k)=\cos[\operatorname{am}(u,k)]~,\qquad\operatorname{dn}(u,k)=\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}[\operatorname{am}(u,k)]}~.

(62)

Since

\operatorname{am}(u+2K(k),k)=\operatorname{am}(u,k)+\pi~,

(63)

the orbit returns to itself after
u\to u+2K
. Therefore,

T(-E)=2K(k)\omega^{-1}~.

(
30
)

The same result can also be obtained from the standard period integral representation, as shown in (
29
)-(
30
).

The logarithmic divergence of the period follows from the standard asymptotic expansion of
K(k)
in the limit
E\rightarrow 0+
. Expanding at small
gE
gives

T(-E)=\log(64)-\log(4gE)+\frac{1}{16}(4gE)\left[-3\log(4gE)-10+18\log 2\right]+\mathcal{O}\!\left((4gE)^{2}\right)~.

(64)

This energy dependence will be used below to estimate the period difference and, from it, the time scale for the deformed Fermi surface to wind once.

B.2
E_{\max}
and
E_{\min}
on the deformed Fermi surface

Consider the deformed Fermi surface

x^{2}-p^{2}-\frac{1}{2}gx^{4}-|x-p|^{r}=2\mu~.

(65)

The extremal-energy trajectories,

x^{2}-p^{2}-\frac{1}{2}gx^{4}=2E~,\qquad E>0~,

(66)

are tangent to the deformed Fermi surface. Therefore, at the tangent point, the two curves have the same energy and the gradients of their defining functions must be parallel. This gives

p_{0}=x_{0}-gx_{0}^{3}~.

(67)

Substituting (
67
) into (
65
) gives

\frac{3}{2}gx_{0}^{4}-g^{2}x_{0}^{6}-g^{r}|x_{0}|^{3r}=2\mu~.

(68)

It is convenient to introduce

4gE=4g\mu+2y~.

(69)

Using

2y=2g|x_{0}-p_{0}|^{r}=2g^{1+r}|x_{0}|^{3r}~,

(70)

(
68
) becomes

H(y)=\frac{3}{2}g^{-1/3}\left(g^{-1}y\right)^{\frac{4}{3r}}-\left(g^{-1}y\right)^{\frac{2}{r}}-g^{-1}y-2\mu=0~.

(71)

Although the roots cannot be obtained analytically, their small-
g
scaling can be estimated from the leading terms of
H(y)
. This gives the smaller root

y_{\min}\sim\left(\frac{4\mu}{3}\right)^{\frac{3r}{4}}g^{1+\frac{r}{4}}~,

(72)

and the larger root

y_{\max}\sim\begin{cases}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{\frac{3r}{2}}g^{\frac{2-r}{2}}~,&r\leq 2~,\\[6.0pt]
\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{\frac{3r}{3r-4}}g^{\frac{2r-4}{3r-4}}~,&r>2~.\end{cases}

(73)

According to the definition of
y
in (
69
), the extremal values are

4gE_{\min}=4g\mu+2y_{\min}~,\qquad 4gE_{\max}=4g\mu+2y_{\max}~.

(74)

For small
g
,
y_{\min}\ll g\mu
while
y_{\max}\gg g\mu
in the regime of interest. Hence

4gE_{\min}\sim 4g\mu~,\qquad 4gE_{\max}\sim 2y_{\max}~.

(75)

B.3
Time scale for winding once

We first estimate the period difference between the extremal trajectories,

\Delta T=T(-E_{\min})-T(-E_{\max})~.

(76)

Substituting the extremal energies into (
64
) yields for
E\to 0+
and small
g

\Delta T\sim\log\frac{4gE_{\max}}{4gE_{\min}}\sim-\log(2\mu)+\frac{r}{\max\{3r-4,\,2\}}\log\left(\frac{27}{8g}\right)~.

(77)

We introduce a typical time scale, the winding time, as the time needed for the fastest orbit, namely the orbit with energy
-E_{\max}
, to get one period ahead of the slowest orbit, with energy
-E_{\min}
. This gives

T_{\rm winding}=\frac{2\pi}{\frac{2\pi}{T(-E_{\max})}-\frac{2\pi}{T(-E_{\min})}}=\frac{T(-E_{\min})T(-E_{\max})}{\Delta T}~.

(78)

Using the leading logarithmic behaviour of (
64
), one finds

T_{\rm winding}\sim\left[\log(4g\mu)\right]^{2}~.

(79)

Hence the detailed energy range depends on the deformation exponent
r
, whereas the leading winding time is universal and grows as
(\log g)^{2}
in the weak-coupling limit.

In this appendix we summarize the action-angle variables for the one-particle motion in the double-well potential.

Consider the trajectory of individual fermions with energy

given in (

and

Since the transformed Hamiltonian depends only on
