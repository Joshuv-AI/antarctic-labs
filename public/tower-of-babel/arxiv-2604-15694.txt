Neural Continuous-Time Markov Chain: Discrete Diffusion via Decoupled Jump Timing and Direction
Jingyuan Li, Xiaoyi Jiang, Fukang Wen, Wei LiuRenqian Luo, Yi Zhu, Zuoqiang Shi, Pipi Hu, [0.5ex] Tsinghua University

Abstract
Discrete diffusion models based on continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs) have shown strong performance on language and discrete data generation, yet existing approaches typically parameterize the reverse rate matrix as a single object—via concrete scores, clean-data predictions ( x 0 x_{0} -parameterization), or denoising distributions—rather than aligning the parameterization with the intrinsic CTMC decomposition into jump timing and jump direction. Since a CTMC is fundamentally a Poisson process fully determined by these two quantities, decomposing along this structure is closer to first principles and naturally leads to our formulation. We propose Neural CTMC , which separately parameterizes the reverse process through an exit rate (when to jump) and a jump distribution (where to jump) using two dedicated network heads. We show that the evidence lower bound (ELBO) differs from a path-space KL divergence between the true and learned reverse processes by a θ \theta -independent constant, so that the training objective is fully governed by the exit rate and jump distribution we parameterize. Moreover, this KL factorizes into a Poisson KL for timing and a categorical KL for direction. We further show that the tractable conditional surrogate preserves the gradients and minimizers of the corresponding marginal reverse-process objective under standard regularity assumptions. Our theoretical framework also covers masked and GIDD-style noise schedules. Empirically, while the uniform forward process has been explored in prior work, our model, to our best of the knowledge, is the first pure-uniform method to outperform mask-based methods on the OpenWebText dataset.To facilitate reproducibility, we release our pretrained weights at https://huggingface.co/Jiangxy1117/Neural-CTMC .

Moreover, the two mainstream forward processes in discrete diffusion are masked (absorbing) and uniform. The masked forward process is dominant in current state-of-the-art methods

We propose

This decomposition is not introduced ad hoc at the architecture level; rather, it is induced by the CTMC path-space objective itself, which suggests learning timing and direction with separate heads. Notably, when specialized to the masked (absorbing) forward process, our objective recovers the MDLM loss exactly, showing that the decomposed formulation strictly generalizes existing approaches.
Second, equipped with this decomposed parameterization, Neural CTMC with a pure uniform forward process surpasses several state-of-the-art masked-based methods on the benchmarks we study.

Path measure.

Càdlàg (right-continuous with left limits) paths provide a general framework for stochastic processes with jumps, encompassing Lévy processes, semimartingales, and others. A CTMC corresponds to piecewise constant càdlàg paths and is characterized by a path measure
\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}

W_{0},W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}

W_{k}

Definition 2.3

A path space measure

assigns probabilities to a drawn trajectory
W

to
T

\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}(W\in\mathrm{d}\omega):=\mathbb{P}\!\left(W_{0}\in\mathrm{d}\omega_{0},\,(T_{1},W_{T_{1}})\in\mathrm{d}(t_{1},\omega_{1}),\,\ldots,\,(T_{n},W_{T_{n}})\in\mathrm{d}(t_{n},\omega_{n}),\,T_{n+1}\geq T\right),

where
\mathrm{d}\omega_{k}

and

Writing

Let

(3)

2.2
Discrete Diffusion and Existing Parameterizations

A CTMC-based discrete diffusion model consists of a
forward
process and a
reverse
(generative) process. The forward process corrupts data
X_{0}\sim p_{\text{data}}
via a CTMC with rate matrix
R_{t}
, yielding conditionals
q_{t|0}(x\mid x_{0}):=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=x\mid X_{0}=x_{0})
and marginals
q_{t}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p_{\text{data}}}[q_{t|0}(x\mid x_{0})]
, with
q_{t}\to p_{\text{prior}}
as
t\to T
. A key structural property of the rate matrix is the following decomposition.

Exit rate and jump distribution.

Any rate matrix admits the unique decomposition
R_{t}(i,j)=\lambda_{t}(i)\cdot r_{t}(j\mid i)
, where

\lambda_{t}(i):=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R_{t}(i,j)\;\;\text{(exit rate)},\qquad r_{t}(j\mid i):=\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{\lambda_{t}(i)}\;\;\text{(jump distribution)}.

(4)

\lambda_{t}(i)
governs the holding time and
r_{t}(\cdot\mid i)
is a categorical distribution over jump destinations. This factorization separates
when
to jump from
where
to jump—a structural property of any CTMC.

Reverse process.

The time-reversal of the forward process is a CTMC with rate matrix
(
5
)
:

\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)=R_{t}(j,i)\frac{q_{t}(j)}{q_{t}(i)},\quad i\neq j,

(5)

A neural network parameterizes a reverse CTMC with rate matrix
R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)
starting from
X_{T}\sim p_{\text{prior}}
, with the goal of minimizing an upper bound on the negative log-likelihood
-\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log p_{\theta}(x_{0})]
.

Existing parameterizations.

Prior work parameterizes
R^{\theta}_{t}
through different proxy quantities, each implicitly determining the full rate matrix:

•

Concrete score

(
17
)
: parameterize
s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)_{y}\approx\frac{q_{t}(y)}{q_{t}(x_{t})}
, recover
R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)=R_{t}(j,i)\cdot s_{\theta}(i,t)_{j}
. Trained via score entropy.

•

Denoising distribution
(
x_{0}
-param.)
(
5
)
: predict
p_{\theta}(x_{0}\mid x_{t})
, construct
R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)=\sum\limits_{x_{0}}p_{\theta}(x_{0}\mid i)\,R_{t}(j,i)\,\frac{q_{t|0}(j\mid x_{0})}{q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})}
.

•

Clean data prediction

(
3
;
24
;
31
)
: the model distribution
p_{\theta}(z_{s}\mid z_{t})=q_{t|s}(z_{t}\mid z_{s})\,\frac{q_{s}(z_{s}\mid x_{\theta})}{q_{t}(z_{t}\mid x_{\theta})}
, where
x_{\theta}(z_{t},t)
is a neural network that predicts the clean data; subsumes the
x_{0}
-parameterization as a special case.

All three strategies determine
R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)
without decomposing it into exit rate and jump distribution—which, as we show, correspond to independent learning objectives.

x_{0}

\hat{R}_{t}(i,j\mid x_{0}):=R_{t}(j,i)\,\frac{q_{t|0}(j\mid x_{0})}{q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})},\quad i\neq j,

(19)

and the Poisson KL density
f(r,c):=r\log\frac{r}{c}-r+c
. The
conditional KL training loss

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta):=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p_{\text{data}},\;x_{t}\sim q_{t|0}(\cdot\mid x_{0})}\!\left[\sum\limits_{j\neq x_{t}}f\!\left(\hat{R}_{t}(x_{t},j\mid x_{0}),\;R^{\theta}_{t}(x_{t},j)\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t

(20)

is a tractable upper bound on
\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{Q}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta})
:

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[-\log p_{\theta}(x_{0})]\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta)+C_{\mathrm{total}},

(21)

where
C_{\mathrm{total}}
is independent of
\theta
. Moreover,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta)
equals the loss in (
18
) up to a
\theta
-independent constant.

Theorem
4.7
establishes a tractable objective value upper bound. The next proposition strengthens this by showing that, under standard regularity assumptions, the conditional surrogate differs from the marginal reverse-process KL only by a
\theta
-independent gap.

Proposition 4.8
.

Assume: (i) forward quantities (
q_{t}
,
q_{t|0}
,
p(x_{0}\mid x_{t})
) are independent of
\theta
; (ii)
R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)>0
and differentiable in
\theta
for all
i\neq j
; and (iii) differentiation can be exchanged with expectation/integration. Then there exists a
\theta
-independent constant
C_{\mathrm{gap}}\geq 0
such that

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta)=\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{Q}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta})+C_{\mathrm{gap}},

hence

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta)=\nabla_{\theta}\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{Q}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta}).

In particular,

\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta)=\arg\min_{\theta}\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{Q}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta}),

and every stationary point of one objective is a stationary point of the other.

Further discussion of the upper-bound interpretation and the information-theoretic form of
C_{\mathrm{gap}}
is deferred to Appendix
C
.

Corollary 4.9
.

Defining
K(a):=a(\log a-1)
, the losses (
18
) and (
20
) are both equivalent to:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cond}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,\,x_{0},\,i}\!\Bigl[

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)r^{\theta}_{t}(j\mid i)-\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R_{t}(j,i)\frac{q_{t|0}(j\mid x_{0})}{q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})}\log\frac{\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)\,r^{\theta}_{t}(j\mid i)}{R_{t}(j,i)}

(22)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R_{t}(j,i)\,K\!\!\left(\frac{q_{t|0}(j\mid x_{0})}{q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})}\right)\Bigr].

The first two terms form the per-pair Poisson KL density
f\!\left(\hat{R}_{t}(i,j\mid x_{0}),\,R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)\right)\geq 0
; the last term is
\theta
-independent and can be precomputed. This avoids large numerical cancellations between the
-\hat{\lambda}_{t|0}\log\lambda^{\theta}_{t}
and
+\lambda^{\theta}_{t}
terms in (
18

.

Through the above derivations, we obtain two equivalent training losses:
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta)
(

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cond}}(\theta)

\theta

Corollary 4.10

(Equivalence to MDLM)
.

If the forward process is the masked (absorbing) process on

6
Conclusion

We presented Neural CTMC, a discrete diffusion framework that parameterizes the reverse CTMC through separate exit-rate and jump-distribution heads, aligning the model with the intrinsic timing/direction structure of CTMC dynamics. We showed that the
\theta
-dependent part of the ELBO differs from a reverse-process path-space KL only by a constant, that this KL factorizes into a Poisson KL for timing and a categorical KL for direction, and that the tractable conditional surrogate preserves the gradients and minimizers of the corresponding marginal objective under standard regularity assumptions.

Empirically, this decomposition makes the pure uniform forward process competitive on the language modeling benchmarks we study. On TinyStories, Neural CTMC achieves generative perplexity
\leq 16.36
, outperforming the baselines evaluated under the same protocol. On OpenWebText, it achieves the best equal-budget generative perplexity among the methods we evaluate across 16 to 128 sampling steps and is, to the best of our knowledge, the first pure-uniform-forward-process method to outperform mask-based methods on this benchmark; it also remains competitive with SEDD despite using
2.6\times
fewer training tokens.

Appendix A
Mathematical Preliminaries

We collect here the full definitions of continuous-time Markov chains and point processes used throughout the paper.

A.1
Continuous-Time Markov Chains

Definition A.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\ldots,S\}
be a finite state space and
T>0
a time horizon. A
continuous-time Markov chain
(CTMC) on
\mathcal{S}
over
[0,T]
is a stochastic process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
with the Markov property:

\mathbb{P}(X_{t+s}=j\mid X_{t}=i,\{X_{u}\}_{u<t})=\mathbb{P}(X_{t+s}=j\mid X_{t}=i).

(24)

Definition A.2
.

A CTMC is characterized by a
rate matrix

R_{t}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying:
(i)
R_{t}(i,j)\geq 0
for all
i\neq j
, and
(ii)
\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{S}}R_{t}(i,j)=0
for all
i\in\mathcal{S}
.
The off-diagonal entry
R_{t}(i,j)
for
i\neq j
represents the
transition rate
from state
i
to state
j
at time
t
.

Definition A.3
.

For state
i\in\mathcal{S}
at time
t
:

\lambda_{t}(i):=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R_{t}(i,j)\quad\text{(exit rate)},\qquad r_{t}(j\mid i):=\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{\lambda_{t}(i)}\quad\text{for }j\neq i\quad\text{(jump distribution)}.

(25)

The exit rate
\lambda_{t}(i)
controls the holding time in state
i
(exponentially distributed), and
r_{t}(\cdot\mid i)
is a probability distribution over the
S-1
possible jump destinations.

Theorem A.4
.

Let
q_{t}(i):=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=i)
be the marginal distribution at time
t
. Then
q_{t}
satisfies the master equation:

\frac{\mathrm{d}q_{t}(i)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(j)R_{t}(j,i)=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}q_{t}(j)R_{t}(j,i)-q_{t}(i)\lambda_{t}(i).

(26)

Definition A.5
.

Given a CTMC with rate matrix
R_{t}
and marginal distribution
q_{t}(i)
at time
t
, the
time-reversed
CTMC has rate matrix
\hat{R}_{t}
given by:

\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)=R_{t}(j,i)\frac{q_{t}(j)}{q_{t}(i)},\quad i\neq j.

(27)

A.2
Point Process

Definition A.6
.

A
point process
on
[0,T]
is a random collection of points (jump times)
\{t_{1},t_{2},\ldots\}
in
[0,T]
. For a CTMC, the jump times form a point process whose intensity at time
t
is
\lambda_{t}(X_{t})
.

Theorem A.7
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}
be the path measure of a CTMC starting from
x_{0}
with rate matrix
R_{t}
. For any measurable function
f:[0,T]\times\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
, the expected sum over all jumps satisfies:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}f(t_{k},X_{t_{k}^{-}},X_{t_{k}})\right]=\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R_{t}(i,j)f(t,i,j)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(28)

where
N
is the (random) number of jumps,
X_{t_{k}^{-}}
is the state immediately before jump
k
,
X_{t_{k}}
is the state after jump
k
, and
q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0}):=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}(X_{t}=i)
is the marginal distribution.

Appendix B
Formal Problem Setup

We establish here the formal mathematical framework for CTMC-based discrete diffusion models, providing explicit definitions for the objects introduced informally in the main text.

B.1
Forward Process

Definition B.1
.

The
forward
or
noising
process is a time-dependent CTMC on
\mathcal{S}
with:

•

Initial distribution
:
X_{0}\sim p_{\text{data}}
, the data distribution.

•

Rate matrix
:
R_{t}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
.

•

Transition rate
:
R_{t}(i,j)\geq 0
for
i\neq j
.

•

Exit rate
:
\lambda^{\text{fwd}}_{t}(i):=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R_{t}(i,j)
.

•

Jump distribution
:
r_{t}(j\mid i):=\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{\lambda^{\text{fwd}}_{t}(i)}
for
j\neq i
.

Definition B.2
.

For the forward process:

\displaystyle q_{t|0}(x\mid x_{0})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=x\mid X_{0}=x_{0}),\quad\text{(conditional distribution)}

(29)

\displaystyle q_{t}(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p_{\text{data}}}[q_{t|0}(x\mid x_{0})],\quad\text{(marginal distribution)}.

B.2
True Reverse Process

Definition B.3
.

The
true reverse
process is the time-reversal of the forward process, characterized by the rate matrix:

\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)=R_{t}(j,i)\frac{q_{t}(j)}{q_{t}(i)},\quad i\neq j.

(30)

This is a CTMC evolving from time
0
to
T
with:

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)

\displaystyle:=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)\quad\text{(true reverse exit rate)},

(31)

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{t}(j\mid i)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)}{\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)}\quad\text{(true reverse jump distribution)}.

B.3
Neural Reverse Process

Definition B.4
.

The
neural reverse process
or
generative model
is a parameterized CTMC with:

•

Initial distribution
:
X_{T}\sim p_{\text{prior}}
(reference distribution).

•

Rate matrix
:
R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)
parameterized by
\theta\in\Theta
.

•

Neural exit rate
:
\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i):=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)
.

•

Neural jump distribution
:
r^{\theta}_{t}(j\mid i):=\frac{R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)}{\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)}
.

Definition B.5
.

Typically, a neural network
\Phi_{\theta}:\mathcal{S}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\Delta^{S-1}
outputs:

\Phi_{\theta}(x_{t},t)=(\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(x_{t}),r^{\theta}_{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t})),

(32)

where
\Delta^{S-1}
is the
(S-1)
-dimensional probability simplex (excluding self-transitions).

B.4
Training Objective

Definition B.6
.

The goal is to maximize the expected log-likelihood over the data distribution:

\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log p_{\theta}(x_{0})].

(33)

Equivalently, minimize the negative log-likelihood:

\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p_{\text{data}}}[-\log p_{\theta}(x_{0})].

(34)

Remark B.7

(Intractability)
.

Direct evaluation of

requires marginalizing over all possible paths in continuous time, which is computationally intractable. This motivates the ELBO approach developed in Appendix

.

Appendix C
Proofs

Lemma C.1
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}
denote the path measure induced by a CTMC with rate matrix
R_{t}
starting from initial state
x_{0}
. For a path
\omega
with jumps at times
0\leq t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}<T
and states
x_{0}\to x_{1}\to\cdots\to x_{n}
:

\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}(\omega)=\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n}R_{t_{k}}(x_{k-1},x_{k})\right]\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T}\lambda_{t}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

(35)

Proof.

We decompose the path probability into a product of conditional distributions following the CTMC dynamics. A trajectory starting from
x_{0}
is fully described by jump times
0\leq t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}<T
and states
x_{0}\to x_{1}\to\cdots\to x_{n}
, where at jump time
t_{k}
the chain transitions from
x_{k-1}
to
x_{k}
, and
X_{t}=x_{k}
for
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
(with
t_{0}:=0
and
t_{n+1}:=T
).

Step 1: Holding time distribution.

When the CTMC is in state
x_{k-1}
starting from time
t_{k-1}
, the waiting time until the next jump is governed by a time-inhomogeneous exponential distribution. The cumulative distribution function for the next jump time
t_{k}
is:

\mathbb{P}(T_{k}<t\mid T_{k-1}=t_{k-1})=1-\exp\left(-\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t}\lambda_{s}(x_{k-1})\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

(36)

Differentiating, the density of the
k
-th jump time is:

p(t_{k}\mid T_{k-1}=t_{k-1})=\lambda_{t_{k}}(x_{k-1})\exp\left(-\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\lambda_{s}(x_{k-1})\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

(37)

Step 2: Jump destination distribution.

Conditional on a jump occurring at time
t_{k}
from state
x_{k-1}
, the destination
x_{k}
is chosen according to:

\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{k}}=x_{k}\mid X_{t_{k}^{-}}=x_{k-1})=r_{t_{k}}(x_{k}\mid x_{k-1})=\frac{R_{t_{k}}(x_{k-1},x_{k})}{\lambda_{t_{k}}(x_{k-1})}.

(38)

Step 3: Combining the contributions.

The joint contribution of the
k
-th jump (both timing and destination) is:

\displaystyle p(t_{k}\mid T_{k-1}=t_{k-1})\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{k}}=x_{k}\mid X_{t_{k}^{-}}=x_{k-1})

(39)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{t_{k}}(x_{k-1})\exp\left(-\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\lambda_{s}(x_{k-1})\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\cdot\frac{R_{t_{k}}(x_{k-1},x_{k})}{\lambda_{t_{k}}(x_{k-1})}

\displaystyle=R_{t_{k}}(x_{k-1},x_{k})\exp\left(-\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\lambda_{s}(x_{k-1})\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

The survival probability after the last jump (no further jump in
[t_{n},T]
) is:

\mathbb{P}(T_{n+1}\geq T\mid T_{n}=t_{n})=\exp\left(-\int_{t_{n}}^{T}\lambda_{s}(x_{n})\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

(40)

Step 4: Full path density.

Taking the product over all
n
jumps and the final survival term:

\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}(\omega)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}R_{t_{k}}(x_{k-1},x_{k})\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\lambda_{s}(x_{k-1})\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\cdot\exp\left(-\int_{t_{n}}^{T}\lambda_{s}(x_{n})\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

(41)

Since
X_{t}=x_{k-1}
for
t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k})
, the exponential terms combine into a single integral over the entire time horizon:

\prod_{k=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\lambda_{s}(x_{k-1})\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\cdot\exp\left(-\int_{t_{n}}^{T}\lambda_{s}(x_{n})\,\mathrm{d}s\right)=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T}\lambda_{t}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right),

(42)

which yields the desired result.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}
be the forward path measure starting from
x_{0}
, and
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}
be the neural reverse path measure. For a path
\omega
with jumps at times
0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}<T
and visited states
x_{0}\to x_{1}\to\cdots\to x_{n}
, the log Radon-Nikodym derivative is:

\begin{split}\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}(\omega)&=\log p_{\text{prior}}(x_{n})+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\log\frac{R^{\theta}_{t_{k}}(x_{k},x_{k-1})}{R_{t_{k}}(x_{k-1},x_{k})}\\
&\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\left[\lambda^{\text{fwd}}_{t}(X_{t}(\omega))-\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(X_{t}(\omega))\right]\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(43)

Proof.

From Lemma
2.4
, the forward path density (conditional on
X_{0}=x_{0}
) is:

\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}(\omega)=\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n}R_{t_{k}}(x_{k-1},x_{k})\right]\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T}\lambda^{\text{fwd}}_{t}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

(44)

The neural reverse path density for the
same path
(note the index reversal: jumps are
x_{k}\to x_{k-1}
in reverse time) is:

\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\omega)=p_{\text{prior}}(x_{n})\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n}R^{\theta}_{t_{k}}(x_{k},x_{k-1})\right]\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T}\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

(45)

Taking the ratio:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}(\omega)=p_{\text{prior}}(x_{n})\cdot\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{n}R^{\theta}_{t_{k}}(x_{k},x_{k-1})}{\prod_{k=1}^{n}R_{t_{k}}(x_{k-1},x_{k})}\cdot\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left[\lambda^{\text{fwd}}_{t}(X_{t})-\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(X_{t})\right]\mathrm{d}t\right).

(46)

Taking logarithms yields (
43
).
∎

Theorem C.3
.

For any data point
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
, the negative log-likelihood satisfies:

-\log p_{\theta}(x_{0})\leq-\mathbb{E}_{q_{T|0}}[\log p_{\text{prior}}(X_{T})]+\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})\,\ell_{t}(i)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(47)

where the integrand
\ell_{t}(i)
is defined as:

\ell_{t}(i):=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\left[R_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}-R_{t}(i,j)+R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)\right].

(48)

Proof.

Step 1: Importance sampling.

Since the forward path measure
\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}
has
X_{0}=x_{0}
with probability
1
, we can write:

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{1}[X_{0}(\omega)=x_{0}]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\omega)

(49)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{1}[X_{0}(\omega)=x_{0}]\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}(\omega)\quad\text{(Radon-Nikodym)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}(\omega)\quad\text{(since $\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}$ starts from $x_{0}$)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\right].

Step 2: Jensen’s inequality.

Since
\log
is concave, we have:

\log p_{\theta}(x_{0})=\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\left[\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\right].

(50)

Negating both sides:

-\log p_{\theta}(x_{0})\leq-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\left[\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\right].

(51)

Step 3: Expand using Lemma 3.1.

Substituting (
43
) into (
51
):

\displaystyle-\log p_{\theta}(x_{0})

\displaystyle\leq-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}[\log p_{\text{prior}}(X_{T})]

(52)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\log\frac{R^{\theta}_{t_{k}}(X_{t_{k}},X_{t_{k}^{-}})}{R_{t_{k}}(X_{t_{k}^{-}},X_{t_{k}})}\right]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}(\lambda^{\text{fwd}}_{t}(X_{t})-\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(X_{t}))\,\mathrm{d}t\right],

where
N
is the random number of jumps.

Step 4: Apply Campbell-Mecke formula (Theorem
A.7
).

For the jump term, set
f(t,i,j)=\log\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}
:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\log\frac{R_{t_{k}}(X_{t_{k}^{-}},X_{t_{k}})}{R^{\theta}_{t_{k}}(X_{t_{k}},X_{t_{k}^{-}})}\right]=\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(53)

For the survival term, by Fubini’s theorem:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}(\lambda^{\text{fwd}}_{t}(X_{t})-\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(X_{t}))\,\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}[\lambda^{\text{fwd}}_{t}(X_{t})-\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(X_{t})]\,\mathrm{d}t

(54)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})\left[\lambda^{\text{fwd}}_{t}(i)-\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\left[R_{t}(i,j)-R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)\right]\mathrm{d}t.

Step 5: Combine jump and survival terms.

From Steps 3 and 4, we need to compute:

-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\left[\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}\right]=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{x_{0}}}[\log p_{\text{prior}}(X_{T})]+I_{\text{jump}}-I_{\text{surv}},

(55)

where the jump and survival integrals are (from Step 4):

\displaystyle I_{\text{jump}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}\,\mathrm{d}t,

(56)

\displaystyle I_{\text{surv}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\left[R_{t}(i,j)-R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(57)

Combining (39) and the survival term:

\displaystyle I_{\text{jump}}-I_{\text{surv}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\left[R_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}-\left(R_{t}(i,j)-R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t

(58)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\left[R_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}-R_{t}(i,j)+R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})\,\ell_{t}(i)\,\mathrm{d}t,

where we define:

\ell_{t}(i):=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\left[R_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}-R_{t}(i,j)+R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)\right],

(59)

which exactly matches (
48
). This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem C.4
.

The expected negative log-likelihood over the data distribution satisfies:

\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p_{\text{data}}}[-\log p_{\theta}(x_{0})]\leq\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{Q}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta})+C_{\text{total}},

(60)

where
\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
is the path measure of the true reverse process,
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}
is the path measure of the neural reverse process, and
C_{\text{total}}
is a constant independent of
\theta
. Explicitly:

\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{Q}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta})=\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\left[\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)}-\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)+R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(61)

Proof.

Step 1: Average over data.

Taking expectation of both sides of (
47
) over
x_{0}\sim p_{\text{data}}
:

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[-\log p_{\theta}(x_{0})]\leq\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[-\mathbb{E}_{q_{T|0}}[\log p_{\text{prior}}(X_{T})]]+\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})\,\ell_{t}(i)\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

(62)

Step 2: Define marginal distribution.

Define the (unconditional) marginal distribution at time
t
:

q_{t}(i):=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p_{\text{data}}}[q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})]=\int_{\mathcal{S}}p_{\text{data}}(x_{0})\,q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0}).

(63)

By Fubini’s theorem:

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})\,\ell_{t}(i)\,\mathrm{d}t\right]=\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\,\ell_{t}(i)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(64)

Step 3: Transform jump term.

Expand
\ell_{t}(i)
from (
48
):

q_{t}(i)\,\ell_{t}(i)=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}q_{t}(i)\left[R_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}-R_{t}(i,j)+R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)\right].

(65)

For the
jump term
, since
\hat{R}_{t}(j,i)=R_{t}(i,j)\frac{q_{t}(i)}{q_{t}(j)}
, we have
q_{t}(i)R_{t}(i,j)=q_{t}(j)\hat{R}_{t}(j,i)
. Substitute:

q_{t}(i)R_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}=q_{t}(j)\hat{R}_{t}(j,i)\log\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}.

(66)

Since
R_{t}(i,j)=\hat{R}_{t}(j,i)\frac{q_{t}(j)}{q_{t}(i)}
:

\displaystyle q_{t}(j)\hat{R}_{t}(j,i)\log\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}

\displaystyle=q_{t}(j)\hat{R}_{t}(j,i)\log\frac{\hat{R}_{t}(j,i)\,q_{t}(j)/q_{t}(i)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}

(67)

\displaystyle=q_{t}(j)\hat{R}_{t}(j,i)\left[\log\frac{\hat{R}_{t}(j,i)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}+\log\frac{q_{t}(j)}{q_{t}(i)}\right].

Summing over
i
and
j
(relabel
i\leftrightarrow j
):

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}q_{t}(i)R_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{R_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(j,i)}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}q_{t}(i)\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)}

(68)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}q_{t}(i)\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{q_{t}(i)}{q_{t}(j)}}_{C_{t}^{(1)}\text{ (independent of }\theta)}.

Step 4: Transform survival term.

For the
survival term

\sum\limits_{j\neq i}[-R_{t}(i,j)+R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)]
, since
q_{t}(i)R_{t}(i,j)=q_{t}(j)\hat{R}_{t}(j,i)
:

\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\lambda^{\text{fwd}}_{t}(i)=\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R_{t}(i,j)=\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}q_{t}(j)\hat{R}_{t}(j,i)=\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i).

(69)

Therefore:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\sum\limits_{j\neq i}[-R_{t}(i,j)+R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)]

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\lambda^{\text{fwd}}_{t}(i)+\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)

(70)

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)+\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\sum\limits_{j\neq i}[R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)-\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)].

Step 5: Combine jump and survival terms.

Putting everything together from Steps 3 and 4:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\,\ell_{t}(i)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\left[\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)}+R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)-\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)\right]\mathrm{d}t

(71)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}C_{t}^{(1)}\,\mathrm{d}t.

The first term can be rewritten as:

\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\left[\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)}-\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)+R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)\right]\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{Q}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta}),

(72)

and the second term (plus the prior term) is
C_{\text{total}}
, independent of
\theta
.
∎

Corollary C.5
.

Define the true reverse quantities:

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)

\displaystyle:=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)\quad\text{(exit rate)},

(73)

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{t}(j\mid i)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)}{\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)}\quad\text{for }j\neq i\quad\text{(jump distribution)},

and similarly for the neural reverse:

\displaystyle\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)

\displaystyle:=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j),

(74)

\displaystyle r^{\theta}_{t}(j\mid i)

\displaystyle:=\frac{R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)}{\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)}.

Then:

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[-\log p_{\theta}(x_{0})]\leq\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\left[\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Poi}}(\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\|\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i))+\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\cdot\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Cat}}(\hat{r}_{t}(\cdot\mid i)\|r^{\theta}_{t}(\cdot\mid i))\right]\mathrm{d}t+C,

(75)

where:

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Poi}}(\lambda\|\lambda^{\theta})

\displaystyle:=\lambda\log\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{\theta}}-\lambda+\lambda^{\theta},

(76)

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Cat}}(\hat{r}\|r^{\theta})

\displaystyle:=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\hat{r}(j\mid i)\log\frac{\hat{r}(j\mid i)}{r^{\theta}(j\mid i)}.

Proof.

Substitute
\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)=\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\hat{r}_{t}(j\mid i)
and
R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)=\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)r^{\theta}_{t}(j\mid i)
into (
61
):

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\left[\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)\log\frac{\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)}{R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)}-\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)+R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)\right]

(77)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\hat{r}_{t}(j\mid i)\log\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\hat{r}_{t}(j\mid i)}{\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)r^{\theta}_{t}(j\mid i)}-\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)+\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)

\displaystyle=\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\log\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)}{\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)}+\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\hat{r}_{t}(j\mid i)\log\frac{\hat{r}_{t}(j\mid i)}{r^{\theta}_{t}(j\mid i)}-\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)+\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Poi}}(\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\|\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i))+\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\cdot\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Cat}}(\hat{r}_{t}(\cdot\mid i)\|r^{\theta}_{t}(\cdot\mid i)).

∎

Corollary C.6
.

The training objective (
14
) can be estimated via Monte Carlo sampling:

\mathcal{L}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,x_{t}\sim q_{t}}\left[\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Poi}}(\hat{\lambda}_{t}(x_{t})\|\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(x_{t}))+\hat{\lambda}_{t}(x_{t})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{j\sim\hat{r}_{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t})}\left[-\log r^{\theta}_{t}(j\mid x_{t})\right]-\hat{\lambda}_{t}(x_{t})H(\hat{r}_{t})\right],

(78)

where
H(\hat{r}_{t}):=-\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\hat{r}_{t}(j\mid i)\log\hat{r}_{t}(j\mid i)
is the entropy of the true reverse jump distribution. Equivalently, using the cross-entropy (CE) identity
\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Cat}}=CE-H
:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,x_{t}}\left[\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Poi}}(\hat{\lambda}_{t}(x_{t})\|\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(x_{t}))+\hat{\lambda}_{t}(x_{t})\cdot CE(\hat{r}_{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t}),r^{\theta}_{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t}))\right]+\text{const}.

(79)

Proof.

Direct application of Monte Carlo integration to (
14
):

\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\cdot(\cdots)\,\mathrm{d}t=T\cdot\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)}\left[\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\cdot(\cdots)\right]=T\cdot\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,x_{t}\sim q_{t}}[(\cdots)].

(80)

The categorical KL can be written as:

\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Cat}}(\hat{r}_{t}\|r^{\theta}_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{j\sim\hat{r}_{t}}[-\log r^{\theta}_{t}(j\mid x_{t})]-H(\hat{r}_{t}),

(81)

where the first term is the cross-entropy and the second is the entropy (independent of
\theta
).
∎

Lemma C.7
.

Let
p_{0}
be a distribution over
\mathcal{S}
, and let
p(\cdot\mid x_{0})
be conditional distributions such that
p(x)=\sum\limits_{x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}}p_{0}(x_{0})p(x\mid x_{0})
is the marginal distribution. For any measurable function
f:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have:

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\left[\sum\limits_{y\neq x}f(x,y)\frac{p(y)}{p(x)}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p_{0},\,x\sim p(\cdot\mid x_{0})}\left[\sum\limits_{y\neq x}f(x,y)\frac{p(y\mid x_{0})}{p(x\mid x_{0})}\right].

(82)

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\left[\sum\limits_{y\neq x}f(x,y)\frac{p(y)}{p(x)}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{S}}p(x)\sum\limits_{y\neq x}f(x,y)\frac{p(y)}{p(x)}

(83)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{S}}\sum\limits_{y\neq x}f(x,y)p(y)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{S}}\sum\limits_{y\neq x}f(x,y)\sum\limits_{x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}}p_{0}(x_{0})p(y\mid x_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}}p_{0}(x_{0})\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{S}}\sum\limits_{y\neq x}f(x,y)p(y\mid x_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}}p_{0}(x_{0})\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{S}}p(x\mid x_{0})\sum\limits_{y\neq x}f(x,y)\frac{p(y\mid x_{0})}{p(x\mid x_{0})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p_{0},\,x\sim p(\cdot\mid x_{0})}\left[\sum\limits_{y\neq x}f(x,y)\frac{p(y\mid x_{0})}{p(x\mid x_{0})}\right].

∎

Proposition C.8
.

For a fixed data point
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
, define the conditional reverse rates:

\hat{R}_{t|0}(j,i\mid x_{0}):=R_{t}(i,j)\frac{q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})}{q_{t|0}(j\mid x_{0})},\quad i\neq j,

(84)

\hat{\lambda}_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0}):=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\hat{R}_{t|0}(i,j\mid x_{0})=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R_{t}(j,i)\frac{q_{t|0}(j\mid x_{0})}{q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})},

(85)

\hat{r}_{t|0}(j\mid i,x_{0}):=\frac{\hat{R}_{t|0}(i,j\mid x_{0})}{\hat{\lambda}_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})}=\frac{R_{t}(j,i)\frac{q_{t|0}(j\mid x_{0})}{q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})}}{\hat{\lambda}_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})},\quad j\neq i.

(86)

Then the training loss (
79
) can be equivalently written as:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,x_{0}\sim p_{\text{data}},\,i\sim q_{t|0}(\cdot\mid x_{0})}\left[-\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R_{t}(j,i)\frac{q_{t|0}(j\mid x_{0})}{q_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})}\log\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)r^{\theta}_{t}(j\mid i)-\hat{\lambda}_{t|0}(i\mid x_{0})+\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)\right]+\text{const}.

(87)

Proof.

We expand the training loss (
79
) and apply Lemma
C.7
to transform marginal distributions to conditional distributions.

Starting from (
79
), expand the KL divergence and cross-entropy:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,i\sim q_{t}}\left[\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Poi}}(\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\|\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i))+\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\cdot CE(\hat{r}_{t}(\cdot\mid i),r^{\theta}_{t}(\cdot\mid i))\right]+\text{const}

(88)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,i\sim q_{t}}\left[\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\log\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)}{\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)}-\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)+\lambda^{\theta}_{t}(i)-\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\hat{r}_{t}(j\mid i)\log r^{\theta}_{t}(j\mid i)\right]+\text{const}.

Using
\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}R_{t}(j,i)\frac{q_{t}(j)}{q_{t}(i)}
and
\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\hat{r}_{t}(j\mid i)=\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)=R_{t}(j,i)\frac{q_{t}(j)}{q_{t}(i)}
, we can write:

The first term:

Proposition C.9
.

Assume: (i) forward quantities (
q_{t}
,
q_{t|0}
,
p(x_{0}\mid x_{t})
) are independent of
\theta
; (ii)
R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)>0
and differentiable in
\theta
for all
i\neq j
; and (iii) differentiation can be exchanged with expectation/integration. Then there exists a
\theta
-independent constant
C_{\mathrm{gap}}\geq 0
such that

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta)=\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{Q}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta})+C_{\mathrm{gap}},

hence

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta)=\nabla_{\theta}\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{Q}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta}).

In particular,

\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta)=\arg\min_{\theta}\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{Q}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta}),

and every stationary point of one objective is a stationary point of the other.

Proof.

For fixed
(t,i,j)
, let
R:=\hat{R}_{t}(i,j\mid X_{0})
with
X_{0}\sim p(x_{0}\mid x_{t}=i)
and let
c:=R^{\theta}_{t}(i,j)
. Since
\mathbb{E}[R]=\hat{R}_{t}(i,j)
, expanding
f(r,c)=r\log\frac{r}{c}-r+c
gives

\mathbb{E}[f(R,c)]-f(\mathbb{E}[R],c)=\mathbb{E}[R\log R]-\mathbb{E}[R]\log\mathbb{E}[R]=:\Delta_{t,i,j}\geq 0,

where
\Delta_{t,i,j}
is independent of
c
and thus independent of
\theta
. Multiplying by
q_{t}(i)
, summing over
i\in\mathcal{S}
and
j\neq i
, and integrating over
t
yields

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta)=\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{Q}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta})+\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\Delta_{t,i,j}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Setting
C_{\mathrm{gap}}:=\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}}q_{t}(i)\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\Delta_{t,i,j}\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 0
, under assumptions (i)–(iii), differentiation can be passed through the expectation and integral, so
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta)=\nabla_{\theta}\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{Q}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta})
. Adding a
\theta
-independent constant does not change the minimizers, and identical gradients imply identical stationary points.

Proposition C.12

(Pointwise optimality, restated)
.

For fixed
t
and
i
, define
g_{t,i}(\lambda,r):=\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Poi}}\!\bigl(\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\|\lambda\bigr)+\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)\,\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Cat}}\!\bigl(\hat{r}_{t}(\cdot\mid i)\|r\bigr)
.
If
\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)>0
, then
g_{t,i}(\lambda,r)\geq 0
with equality if and only if
\lambda=\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)
and
r=\hat{r}_{t}(\cdot\mid i)
. If
\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)=0
, the unique minimizer in
\lambda
is
\lambda=0
and the choice of
r
is irrelevant.

Proof.

Since both terms are non-negative,
g_{t,i}(\lambda,r)\geq 0
. For the Poisson KL,
\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Poi}}(\hat{\lambda}\|\lambda)=\hat{\lambda}\log\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{\lambda}-\hat{\lambda}+\lambda
. Taking the derivative with respect to
\lambda
and setting it to zero:
-\hat{\lambda}/\lambda+1=0
, giving
\lambda^{*}=\hat{\lambda}
. The second derivative
\hat{\lambda}/\lambda^{2}>0
confirms this is a strict minimum, and
\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Poi}}(\hat{\lambda}\|\hat{\lambda})=0
. For the categorical KL,
\mathrm{KL}^{\text{Cat}}(\hat{r}\|r)\geq 0
by Gibbs’ inequality, with equality if and only if
r=\hat{r}
. When
\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)=0
, the Poisson KL reduces to
\lambda\geq 0
with minimum at
\lambda=0
, and the categorical term vanishes since it is weighted by
\hat{\lambda}_{t}(i)=0
.
∎

Appendix D
Additional Experimental Details

Datasets.

We evaluate on two datasets: (1) TinyStories
[
7
]
, a corpus of simple short stories for language modeling; (2) OpenWebText (OWT)
[
23
]
, a large-scale web text corpus. Both are tokenized with the GPT-2 BPE tokenizer (vocabulary size 50257) and truncated or padded to a maximum sequence length of 512.

Architecture.

Neural CTMC uses a 12-layer DiT-style Transformer with hidden dimension 768, 12 attention heads, and a time-conditioning dimension of 128. The vocabulary embedding is rounded up to 50304 (the nearest multiple of 128) for hardware efficiency. The total parameter count is approximately 163M.

Baselines.

For TinyStories, we train MDLM and GIDD from their official source code using the same architecture and training budget as Neural CTMC. For OpenWebText, we use the official released checkpoints of SEDD (682B training tokens), MDLM (262B), and GIDD (262B). GIDD is evaluated with its best interpolation parameter
p_{\text{unif}}=0.1
. Tokenization and maximum sequence length are matched across methods; for OpenWebText, we regard the resulting comparison as best-effort because the released checkpoints are not trained under a completely identical pipeline.

Evaluation.

All methods are evaluated using the same protocol: we generate 1024 unconditional samples per configuration and score them with a pretrained Gemma-2 model to obtain generative perplexity (PPL). This measures sample quality under a common external language model, rather than the ELBO-based perplexity reported in some prior work. Because the estimate is computed from a finite sample set, we report the resulting values using
\leq
notation in the tables and discussion. For TinyStories, we report PPL over training epochs with 50 sampling steps. For OpenWebText, we sweep the number of sampling steps over

at the final checkpoint.
