A Multiepitope Intranasal Adenoviral Vaccine Induces Robust Mucosal Immunity and Protection against SARS‑CoV‑2
Authors: Ahmed Elkashif, Muralimanohara S T Murala, Carolyn M Lee, Raksha Suresh, Marwa Alhashimi, Wen-Chien Wang, Vivek Gairola, Andrea Pires dos Santos, Patricia A Boley, Jennifer Schrock, Scott Kenney, Ekramy E Sayedahmed, Gourapura J Renukaradhya, Suresh K Mittal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVaccination has been central to mitigating the COVID-19 pandemic; however, the continual emergence of SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern (VOCs) has reduced the effectiveness of current intramuscular vaccines that primarily target the Spike (S) protein. Although updated formulations are periodically introduced, there remains a critical need for next-generation vaccine platforms capable of inducing broad, variant-independent protection. Here we evaluate a heterologous intranasal (i.n.) prime-boost vaccination strategy using bovine adenoviral (BAd) and chimpanzee adenoviral (ChAd) vectors expressing the S1 subunit in combination with either full-length membrane (M) and nucleocapsid (N) proteins (Ad-S1 + N + M) or multiepitope constructs derived from M and N (Ad-S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M). The constructs were incorporated with the autophagy-inducing peptide C5 (AIP-C5) to enhance antigen-specific T-cell responses.ResultsIn BALB/c mice, Ad-S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M vaccination induced robust S1-specific immunity while simultaneously inducing strong N- and M-specific humoral and cellular responses that were comparable to or greater than those induced by Ad-S1 + N + M. All S1-containing formulations generated high neutralizing antibody titers (~ 3.8 log₁₀) against Omicron B.1.1.529 and BA.2.86 variants, although titers against the ancestral Wuhan strain were approximately one log₁₀ lower. In K18-hACE2 mice, i.n. immunization with S1-expressing vectors provided near-complete protection against BA.2.86 challenge, with undetectable lung viral titers and viral genome copies.ConclusionAn i.n. multiepitope adenoviral vaccine incorporating conserved SARS-CoV-2 antigens induces robust mucosal, humoral, and cellular immune responses and confers significant protection following SARS-CoV-2 challenge.Graphical abstract Keywords: Bovine adenoviral vector, chimpanzee adenoviral vector, adenoviral vector; Vaccine platform; COVID-19, SARS-CoV-2; Spike protein; Nucleocapsid protein; Membrane protein, epitopes; Multi-epitope vaccine

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has had an unprecedented impact on global public health and society and has now transitioned into an endemic state [1]. Despite extensive global vaccination efforts and the deployment of first- and second-generation vaccines, SARS-CoV-2 continues to pose a major public health challenge due to its high transmissibility and rapid evolutionary capacity. The recurrent emergence of variants of concern (VOCs) with enhanced infectivity and immune escape capabilities has progressively reduced vaccine effectiveness and contributed to breakthrough infections and persistent disease burden [2, 3].

Current vaccination strategies remain largely Spike-centric and therefore vulnerable to ongoing viral antigenic drift. On the viral side, the accumulation of mutations particularly within the Spike (S) glycoprotein enhances transmissibility and reduces susceptibility to neutralizing antibodies (NAbs). On the host side, vaccine formulations are repeatedly updated to antigenically match circulating strains, most often by modifying the S antigen [4, 5]. While this approach can temporarily restore vaccine effectiveness, it also increases selective pressure on the virus, accelerating the emergence of immune-evasive variants. As a result, vaccine-induced protection is often short-lived, necessitating frequent booster immunizations [6–8].

Most licensed COVID-19 vaccines rely predominantly on S-specific immunity, in which anti-S antibodies mediate virus neutralization and S-specific CD8⁺ T cells contribute to viral control [9, 10]. However, the heavy dependence on S-focused immunity has facilitated the emergence of VOCs with extensive immune escape, particularly among Omicron sub-lineages, which have dominated global circulation since late 2022 and continue to diversify into novel immune-evasive variants [11, 12]. These observations underscore the need to expand vaccine antigenic targets beyond Spike.

Among alternative SARS-CoV-2 structural proteins, the nucleocapsid (N) and membrane (M) proteins have emerged as attractive vaccine targets [13, 14]. Both proteins are highly conserved across SARS-CoV-2 lineages and VOCs and have been shown to elicit robust humoral and cell-mediated immune (CMI) responses in convalescent individuals and vaccinated animal models [13–18]. Although antibodies directed against N and M lack virus-neutralizing activity, immunization with these antigens has conferred partial protection against multiple SARS-CoV-2 strains, suggesting a dominant role for T-cell–mediated mechanisms in viral control [13–19]. Incorporating conserved viral antigens into vaccine formulations therefore offers a promising strategy for inducing cross-variant immunity and reducing the need for frequent antigenic updates [20].

Another key limitation of current COVID-19 vaccines is their reliance on intramuscular (i.m.) administration. While i.m. vaccination effectively induces systemic humoral and cellular immune responses, it is suboptimal for stimulating mucosal immunity in the respiratory tract, the primary site of SARS-CoV-2 infection [21]. In contrast, intranasal (i.n.) vaccination can elicit both systemic and mucosal immune responses, including secretory IgA and tissue-resident memory T cells, which are critical for controlling viral replication at the site of entry and limiting transmission. To support mucosal vaccine delivery, several nonhuman adenoviral (Ad) vectors have been developed, including bovine Ad (BAd) type 3 and chimpanzee Ad (ChAd) type 7 vectors [20, 22]. These vectors circumvent the limitations imposed by preexisting immunity to common human Ads (HAds) and exhibit robust transgene expression, broad tissue tropism, and minimal cross-reactivity with HAd–specific neutralizing antibodies [23–26].

To further enhance vaccine-induced cellular immunity, we incorporated the autophagy-inducing peptide C5 (AIP-C5), a 21-amino-acid motif derived from the CFP10 protein of Mycobacterium tuberculosis. AIP-C5 has previously been shown to potentiate antigen-specific T-cell responses when delivered via Ad vectors, thereby promoting viral clearance through enhanced CMI [27]. In addition, we included the Exin21 cis-regulatory element, a 21-nucleotide sequence encoding a heptapeptide (QPRFAAA) known to improve mRNA stability, translational efficiency, and antigen expression [28]. Together, these elements were incorporated to optimize antigen presentation and immune activation.

In our previous work, we demonstrated that a multivalent Ad vaccination strategy expressing full-length S, N, and M proteins elicited enhanced antigen-specific immune responses and provided complete protection against SARS-CoV-2 challenge in K18-hACE2 mice [13]. Notably, immunization with full-length N + M alone induced IL-4- and IL-10-associated immune suppression, an effect that was reversed upon inclusion of S, resulting in restored protective immunity. These findings highlighted both the immunological benefits and potential limitations of full-length non-S antigens and suggested that epitope-based designs could offer a refined alternative by retaining immunogenicity while minimizing immunosuppressive effects.

Building on these observations, we hypothesized that a multiepitope vaccine incorporating conserved CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell and B-cell epitopes from N and M proteins, in combination with the S1 subunit of S, could induce balanced humoral and cellular immunity while providing broad cross-variant protection. The method for epitope selection is based on library selection from mouse T-cell epitopes validated by at least one in-vitro functional assay in the literature.

In the present study, we evaluated a heterologous prime–boost i.n. vaccination strategy using ChAd and BAd vectors expressing: S1 alone (Ad-S1), S1 with full-length N and M (Ad-S1 + N + M), S1 with multiepitope N and M constructs (Ad-S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M), or multiepitope N and M alone (Ad-Epi/N + Epi/M) as this approach is biologically and translationally relevant in the context of widespread prior exposure to ChAd-based SARS-CoV-2 vaccines in human populations. All vaccine constructs incorporated AIP-C5 and Exin21 to enhance immune potency. We assessed antigen-specific humoral and cellular immune responses and evaluated protective efficacy following SARS-CoV-2 challenge in K18-hACE2 mice. Our results demonstrate that combining S1 with conserved N and M epitopes via an i.n. Ad platform induces robust mucosal and systemic immunity and provides effective protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Materials and Methods

Cells and Viruses

BAd type 3 E1B–expressing bovine–human hybrid clone 5 (BHH/F5) [29], and BHH/F5-I-SceI (BHH/F5 cells expressing I-SceI endonuclease), [30] cells were used for rescue, propagation, and titration of BAd vectors [30]. Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells (CRL-1573; ATCC) were used for rescue and propagation of chimpanzee adenovirus type 7 (ChAd) vectors, while bovine–human hybrid clone 2 C (BHH2C) cells were used for ChAd vector titration [30–32]. VeroE6-ACE2-TMPRSS-2 and Vero CCL-81.4 cells (ATCC, USA) were used for propagation and titration of SARS-CoV-2 variants. All cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Corning) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone), gentamicin (50 µg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and penicillin (100 U/mL)–streptomycin (100 µg/mL; Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

The following SARS-CoV-2 isolates were obtained from BEI Resources: hCoV-19/USA-WA1/2020 (Wuhan lineage; NR-52281), hCoV-19/USA/MI-UM-10,052,670,540/2023 (Omicron BA.2.86; NR-59638), and hCoV-19/USA/MD-HP20874/2021 (Omicron B.1.1.529; NR-56481). Viruses were propagated in VeroE6-ACE2-TMPRSS-2 or Vero CCL-81.4 cells and titrated by tissue culture infectious dose 50% (TCID₅₀) assay.

Generation of Recombinant Adenoviral Vectors

The amino acid sequences of the SARS-CoV-2 S1, M, and N proteins from Omicron BA.2.86 (GenBank: XDF74463.1) were retrieved from NCBI. Gene constructs encoding S1 alone, S1 fused with full-length N and M (S1 + N + M), or S1 fused with multiepitope N and M constructs (S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M) were designed with GPGPG linkers between domains.

Experimentally validated CD4⁺ T-cell, CD8⁺ T-cell, and B-cell epitopes from the SARS-CoV-2 N and M proteins (Table 1) were selected from the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) [33] and further analyzed for conservation across multiple VOCs using the IEDB Epitope Conservancy Tool (sequence identity > 90%). Selected epitopes were connected using GPGPG and AYY linkers to generate Epi/N + Epi/M constructs with or without the S1 subunit.

CD4+, CD8+ T-cell and B-cell epitopes of M and N

All gene cassettes included the autophagy-inducing peptide AIP-C5 (AAQAAVVRFQEAANKQKQELD) and the Exin21 Cis-acting motif (QPRFAAA) positioned at the 5′ end and separated by a P2A cleavage site [28]. Constructs were codon-optimized for rodents and synthesized commercially (GenScript). All transgenes were under control of the human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) immediate-early promoter and bovine growth hormone (BGH) polyadenylation signal.

Replication-defective BAd vectors were generated by I-SceI–mediated release of the infectious genome following transfection into BHH/F5-I-SceI cells [13, 34]. BAd vectors were amplified in BHH/F5 cells, purified by cesium chloride density gradient ultracentrifugation, and titrated by plaque assay [35].

ChAd vectors were generated analogously using HEK293-I-SceI cells. ChAd-ΔE1E3 served as an empty-vector control. All ChAd vectors were amplified in HEK293 cells, purified by cesium chloride gradient ultracentrifugation, and titrated in BHH2C cells [35].

Immunoblotting

HEK293 cells were infected with ChAd vectors and BHH/F5 cells with BAd vectors at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 2 PFU/cell. Forty-eight h post-infection, cell lysates were prepared and subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblot analysis [34]. Membranes were probed with rabbit anti-S1 (1:5,000; BEI NR-52947), rabbit anti-N (1:5,000; GeneTex GTX135357), or rabbit anti-M (1:3,000; GeneTex GTX636246) antibodies. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were used for detection. β-actin was detected using HRP-conjugated antibody (1:5,000; Thermo Fisher Scientific; MA5-15739-HRP) as a loading control.

RNA Isolation and Reverse-transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR)

Cells infected with BAd or ChAd vectors expressing Epi/N + Epi/M or S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M (MOI = 2 PFU/cell) were harvested 48 h post-infection. Total RNA was extracted using the Monarch Spin RNA Isolation Kit (New England Biolabs; T2110S). RT-PCR assay was performed using Epi/N + Epi/M-specific primers as described previously [13]. Same RNA samples and primers were used for PCR assay to confirm the absence of genomic DNA contamination.

Immunogenicity Studies in BALB/c Mice

All animal experiments were approved by the Purdue University Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBC) and Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and conducted under BSL-2 conditions. Eight-week-old female BALB/c mice (Jackson Laboratory) were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine and inoculated i.n. with 20 µL (10 µL per nostril) of vaccine formulation.

The first vaccine dose was 1 × 108 PFU of ChAd [ChAd-ΔE1E3 (empty vector), ChAd-S1, ChAd-S1 + N + M, ChAd-S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M, or ChAd-Epi/N + Epi/M]. At four weeks post-prime immunization, animals were boosted with 3 × 107 PFU of BAd [BAd-ΔE1E3 (empty vector), BAd-S1, BAd-S1 + N + M, BAd-S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M, or BAd-Epi/N + Epi/M] vectors. The PBS-inoculated group served as a negative control. At 4 weeks post-booster, serum samples and lung washes were collected to monitor antigen-specific antibody titers by ELISA. In addition, splenocytes, MLN cells, and lung MN cells were collected to evaluate CMI responses by IFN-γ ELISpot assay.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

ELISA was performed as described previously [36]. Plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with recombinant SARS-CoV-2 S (BEI NR-58988; 1 µg/mL), N (Sino Biological; 2 µg/mL), or M (ProSci; 2 µg/mL) proteins. Plates were blocked with PBS containing 2% BSA, followed by incubation with serially diluted serum or lung wash samples. HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG, IgG1, IgG2a, or IgA secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) were used for detection. Color development was achieved using TMB substrate, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Enzyme-Linked Immunospot (ELISpot) Assays

Spleen, mediastinal lymph nodes, and lungs were harvested and placed onto a Falcon®70 μm cell strainer (white, nylon mesh; Corning, Life Sciences) fitted on a 50 mL tube. Organs were gently homogenized using the rubber end of a 3 mL syringe plunger with circular grinding motions in the presence of 5% RPMI until only connective tissue remnants remained. Cell suspensions were collected into fresh tubes, and the strainer was rinsed with RPMI to maximize cell recovery.

Samples were centrifuged at 1,800 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, and supernatants were discarded. Cell pellets from spleen and mediastinal lymph nodes were treated with ACK lysis buffer (Invitrogen, eBioscience, Waltham, MA, USA) (3 mL per spleen and 1 mL per lymph node) for 5 min at room temperature to lyse red blood cells, followed by dilution with 15 mL of 5% RPMI. Lung tissues were minced, resuspended in PBS, and layered over lymphocyte separation medium (Corning, NY, USA) followed by centrifugation at 800 × g for 20 min with no brake. The mononuclear cell layer was collected, washed, and resuspended in RPMI.

Spleen and lymph node cells were centrifuged again at 1,800 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in 3 mL (spleen) or 1 mL (lymph node) of 5% RPMI. Viable cells were counted using a hemocytometer under a light microscope. Typical yields were 45–60 × 10⁶ splenocytes, 1.5–2 × 10⁶ mediastinal lymph node cells, and comparable lung mononuclear cell numbers depending on tissue recovery. For ELISpot assays, 5 × 10⁵ splenocytes, 2.5 × 10⁵ mediastinal lymph node cells, and 1 × 10⁵ lung mononuclear cells were plated per well.

IFN-γ, IL-4, and IL-10 ELISpot assays were performed as described [37] using Mabtech reagents (Mabtech, Cincinnati, OH, USA). Cells were stimulated with 5 µg/mL S1 peptide (GPKKSTNL) or CD8⁺ peptide pools derived from the N or M proteins used in the construct described in Table 1 for 48 h at 37 °C. Spot-forming units (SFU) were enumerated using an AID iSpot reader (Autoimmun Diagnostika GmbH, Strassberg, Germany).

Virus Neutralization (VN) Assay

VN assays were performed as described previously [38]. Heat-inactivated sera were serially diluted and incubated with 100 TCID₅₀ of SARS-CoV-2 variants [hCoV-19/USA-WA1/2020 (Wuhan lineage), NR-52281; hCoV-19/USA/MI-UM-10052670540/2023 (Lineage BA.2.86, Omicron), NR-59638; and hCoV-19/USA/MD-HP20874/2021 (lineage B.1.1.529, Omicron), NR-56481], prior to infection of VeroE6-ACE2-TMPRSS-2 cells. Cytopathic effects were assessed after 72 h, and VN titers were defined as the reciprocal of the highest serum dilution that prevented CPE in ≥ 2 of 3 wells.

K18-hACE2 Mouse Protection Studies

Four-six weeks old K18-hACE2 transgenic mice (6 animals/group) from Jackson Laboratory were vaccinated i.n. following the same prime and boost regimen as described above for immunogenicity study. All immunizations were performed under BSL2 conditions.

Four weeks post-boost all groups were challenged with 2 × 10⁵ TCID₅₀ of SARS-CoV-2 (hCoV-19/USA/MI-UM-10052670540/2023, Lineage BA.2.86, Omicron) under BSL-3 conditions. Lung tissues were harvested five days post-challenge for viral titration, RNA quantification, and immunohistopathology.

Lung Viral Load and Genome Copy Quantification

Lung homogenates were titrated on VeroE6-ACE2-TMPRSS-2 cells to determine TCID₅₀ using the Reed–Muench method [13]. Viral RNA was extracted using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), and genome copy numbers were determined by qRT-PCR targeting the SARS-CoV-2 N gene. Results were expressed as log₁₀ genome equivalents per 150 ng RNA.

Histopathology, Immunocytochemistry, and Imaging Digital Quantification

Lung tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, routinely processed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4–5 μm onto charged glass slides. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathologic evaluation. All slides were examined by a board-certified veterinary clinical pathologist (A.P.S.) blinded to treatment groups. Lesions were scored semi-quantitatively, including assessments of interstitial pneumonia, alveolar inflammatory infiltrates, edema, perivascular and peribronchiolar cuffing, and vascular involvement.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections using validated protocols optimized for murine tissues (Purdue University Histopathology Research Laboratory). Primary antibodies included CD3 (rabbit polyclonal, Agilent A0452) for T lymphocytes, CD20 (rabbit polyclonal, Invitrogen PA5-16701) for B lymphocytes, Syndecan-1/CD138 (rabbit polyclonal, Abcam ab128936) for plasma cells, and Ly6G (rat monoclonal, Invitrogen 14-5931-81) for neutrophils. Antigen retrieval conditions and antibody dilutions were optimized individually. Detection was performed using species-appropriate polymer-based horseradish peroxidase systems with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen and hematoxylin counterstain.

Slides were digitized using an Aperio whole-slide scanner (Leica Biosystems). Quantitative image analysis was conducted using ImageScope software. Immune-cell–positive staining was quantified across entire lung sections and normalized to the total number of nucleated cells to account for differences in tissue cellularity and regional inflammation. Data are reported as percent-positive cells rather than absolute cell counts.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10. Differences between groups were assessed using non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests with Dunn’s multiple comparison test unless indicated otherwise in figure legends. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Data are presented as median values with interquartile ranges.

Results

Preparation and Characterization of BAd and ChAd Vectors Expressing SARS-CoV-2 Antigens and Epitopes

Replication-defective BAd and ChAd vectors were generated to evaluate a heterologous prime–boost vaccination strategy. Recombinant vectors expressing the SARS-CoV-2 S1 subunit (BAd-S1 or ChAd-S1), S1 together with full-length nucleocapsid (N) and membrane (M) proteins (BAd-S1 + N + M or ChAd-S1 + N + M), S1 combined with multiepitope constructs derived from N and M (BAd-S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M or ChAd-S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M), or the multiepitope construct alone (BAd-Epi/N + Epi/M or ChAd-Epi/N + Epi/M) were generated (Fig. 1A). The design and sequences of the epitope constructs are summarized in Fig. 1A; Table 1. All transgenes incorporated AIP-C5 and the Exin21 regulatory sequence, were driven by the CMV promoter, terminated by the BGH polyadenylation signal, and cloned into the E1 region of the Ad genome. Empty vectors lacking transgenes (BAd-ΔE1E3 and ChAd-ΔE1E3) served as negative controls.

Correct insertion of each gene cassette was verified by restriction enzyme analysis and sequencing (data not shown). Transgene expression was assessed by infecting BHH/F5 cells with BAd vectors or HEK293 cells with ChAd vectors, followed by immunoblot analysis of cell lysates using antigen-specific antibodies. Immunoblotting with an S-specific antibody revealed bands at approximately 140, 180, and 160 kDa corresponding to S1, S1 + N + M, and S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M fusion proteins, respectively, in both BAd- and ChAd-infected cells (Fig. 1B, top panel). Immunoblotting with M-specific and N-specific antibodies confirmed expression of the S1 + N + M fusion protein at approximately 180 kDa (Fig. 1B, middle and lower panels). No specific bands were detected in mock-infected or empty-vector-infected cells (Figs. 1B).

Expression of the multiepitope constructs was further confirmed at the transcript level. RT-PCR analysis of RNA isolated from vector-infected cells revealed bands of approximately 330 bp corresponding to the Epi/N + Epi/M transcript in cells infected with BAd-S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M, ChAd-S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M, BAd-Epi/N + Epi/M, or ChAd-Epi/N + Epi/M (Fig. 1C), confirming successful transcription of the epitope cassette.

Heterologous Prime–Boost Immunization Induces Robust Antigen-specific Humoral Immune Responses

The immunization regimen is depicted in Fig. 1D. Mice were primed i.n. with PBS, ChAd-ΔE1E3, ChAd-S1, ChAd-S1 + N + M, ChAd-S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M, or ChAd-Epi/N + Epi/M and boosted four weeks later with the corresponding BAd vectors. Four weeks post-boost, serum and lung wash samples were collected to assess antigen-specific antibody responses.

High levels of S1-specific IgG, IgG1, IgG2a, and IgA antibodies were detected in both serum and lung washes from mice immunized with Ad-S1, Ad-S1 + N + M, or Ad-S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M (Fig. 2A). In contrast, Ad-Epi/N + Epi/M, Ad-ΔE1E3, and PBS control groups showed no detectable S1-specific antibody responses above background.

Vaccination Induces Minimal IL-4 and IL-10 Immunosuppressive Responses

IL-4 and IL-10 are immunomodulatory cytokines associated with Th2-skewed responses and immune suppression [39]. In the context of adaptive immune response, they play a role in skewing a Th-2 response associated with a decreased immune response in both magnitude and quality [40–42]. To evaluate potential immunosuppressive effects, N- and M-specific IL-4- and IL-10-producing splenocytes were quantified by ELISpot. Except for the Ad-S1 + N + M group, all vaccination groups exhibited low levels of IL-4 and IL-10 responses comparable to control groups (Fig. 4). Ad-S1 + N + M induced the highest levels of N- and M-specific IL-4 and IL-10 production, whereas epitope-based formulations, particularly Ad-Epi/N + Epi/M, maintained a predominantly Th1-biased immune profile with minimal immunosuppressive cytokine production.

Mice vaccinated with the empty-vector control (Ad-ΔE1E3) demonstrated a mild increase in perivascular and peribronchiolar lymphocytes, forming small cuffing aggregates around pulmonary vessels while maintaining intact alveolar structure. All antigen-vaccinated groups exhibited mild to moderate organized lymphocytic inflammation, predominantly in a perivascular and peribronchiolar pattern with occasional extension into adjacent interstitial spaces. The infiltrates consisted of small, mature lymphocytes without evidence of necrosis, vasculitis, or significant alveolar injury (Fig. 7A-P). Discussion

The ongoing emergence of SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern (VOCs) and the persistence of breakthrough infections following vaccination continue to drive the urgent need for next-generation COVID-19 vaccines capable of conferring durable and broadly protective immunity [43, 44]. An ideal vaccine strategy should not only prevent severe disease but also limit viral transmission and maintain effectiveness against both current and future VOCs without reliance on frequent antigenic updates or repeated booster doses [45–47]. To address these challenges, multiple complementary approaches have been explored, including targeting conserved viral antigens, inducing mucosal immunity through alternative routes of vaccine delivery, and incorporating immunomodulatory features that enhance specific arms of immune responses [13, 48, 49].

Among SARS-CoV-2 structural proteins, nucleocapsid (N) and membrane (M) represent attractive vaccine antigens due to their relative sequence conservation and strong immunogenicity compared with the S protein [14, 50]. A multiepitope vaccine design incorporating conserved CD4⁺ T-cell, CD8⁺ T-cell, and B-cell epitopes has the potential to maximize antigen presentation through both MHC class I and II pathways while simultaneously engaging humoral and cell-mediated immunity (CMI). Importantly, epitope-based strategies may also mitigate the immune suppression associated with full-length antigen expression, which has been reported for certain SARS-CoV-2 proteins [13, 51, 52]. To construct a rational multiepitope vaccine, experimentally validated epitopes from the N and M proteins were selected using the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB), prioritizing epitopes with demonstrated immunogenicity and high conservation across SARS-CoV-2 lineages and VOCs [33].

The use of a heterologous prime–boost vaccination regimen further enhances vaccine-elicited immune responses by overcoming vector-specific immunity that can limit homologous boosting. In this study, we employed ChAd7 as a priming vector, followed by BAd3 for boosting. ChAd7 has been extensively evaluated as a vaccine platform for several viral pathogens, including influenza, HIV, respiratory syncytial virus, and Zika viruses, and is characterized by low human seroprevalence, a favorable safety profile, and robust induction of systemic and mucosal immunity when administered i.n [53–58]. Similarly, BAd vectors have been developed for vaccines against influenza, tuberculosis, and SARS-CoV-2 and demonstrate several advantages over HAd vectors, including reduced interference from preexisting immunity, sustained transgene expression, high immunogenicity at lower doses, and strong tropism for mucosal epithelial cells due to their use of α(2,3)- and α(2,6)-linked sialic acid receptors [20, 22, 26, 27, 29, 59]. Together, these properties make ChAd/BAd heterologous prime–boost delivery particularly well suited for i.n. vaccination.

Given the crucial role of T-cell–mediated immunity in controlling SARS-CoV-2 replication and eliminating infected cells, we incorporated the autophagy-inducing peptide AIP-C5 to potentiate antigen-specific CMI responses, consistent with earlier studies demonstrating its immunoenhancing properties [38, 60]. In addition, incorporation of the Exin21 cis-regulatory sequence was intended to enhance transgene stability, translation efficiency, and antigen expression, thereby improving the magnitude and quality of vaccine-induced immune responses.

Our findings demonstrate that i.n. heterologous prime–boost vaccination with ChAd and BAd vectors expressing a multiepitope N + M construct (Epi/N + Epi/M), either alone or in combination with the S1 subunit of Spike, elicits robust antigen-specific humoral and cellular immunity in mice. All vaccinated groups exhibited strong IFN-γ secretion, indicative of Th1-biased cellular immune responses, as well as high levels of antigen-specific antibodies in both serum and lung washes, highlighting the induction of systemic and mucosal immunity. Although validated CD4⁺ T cell epitopes were included in the construct to support CD8⁺ T cell and humoral responses, direct CD4⁺ T cell characterization was not performed in this study, as the primary focus was on cytotoxic immunity. However, the observed antibody and neutralizing responses indirectly support functional CD4⁺ T cell help. While vaccination with Ad-Epi/N + Epi/M alone elicited relatively lower levels of N- and M-specific humoral responses compared to formulations including S1, the addition of S1 (Ad-S1 + Epi/N + Epi/M) markedly enhanced antibody responses against N and M, suggesting a synergistic role for S1 in promoting B-cell activation and antibody maturation.

Consistent with the inclusion of S1 as a key neutralization target, all S1-containing vaccine formulations induced substantial virus-neutralizing antibody (NAb) titers against SARS-CoV-2 variants. Neutralization potency varied by strain, with the highest titers observed against the homologous vaccine strain (Omicron BA.2.86) and lower titers against the ancestral WA1/2020 strain, reflecting antigenic divergence between variants. Importantly, i.n. prime–boost vaccination conferred strong protection against SARS-CoV-2 challenge in K18-hACE2 mice. Vaccination groups containing S1 demonstrated efficient protection, evidenced by undetectable lung viral titers and absence of viral genome copies at four days post-challenge. In contrast, Ad-Epi/N + Epi/M vaccination resulted in significant but incomplete reduction of lung viral loads, indicating partial protective efficacy. The absence of detectable NAb responses in this group suggests that T-cell responses likely contributed substantially to protection, while a contribution from non-neutralizing antibody responses cannot be excluded.

These findings are particularly relevant considering our previous studies demonstrating that co-delivery of full-length N and M antigens was associated with elevated IL-4 and IL-10 responses and diminished protective efficacy, indicative of immune suppression [13]. In contrast, the epitope-based Epi/N + Epi/M construct elicited strong Th1-biased cellular immunity with minimal IL-4 and IL-10 production and conferred measurable protection. Interestingly, while earlier co-delivery of full-length S alleviated immune suppression associated with N and M, vaccination with Ad-S1 + N + M in the present study elicited moderate IL-4 and IL-10 responses. This observation suggests a potential immunomodulatory role for the S2 subunit or differences in antigen processing and presentation between full-length multivalent vectors and fusion protein–based antigen design [14, 61].

Lung histopathology and immunohistochemical analyses provided further insight into the protective immune landscape induced by our vaccine constructs. Vaccinated animals demonstrated predominantly CD3⁺ T cell driven perivascular infiltration with minimal neutrophilic involvement and preserved alveolar architecture, consistent with a coordinated recall adaptive response rather than excessive inflammatory pathology [62, 63]. The absence of necrosis, vasculitis, or interstitial damage supports the conclusion that vaccine-induced cellular immunity facilitated rapid viral containment without promoting immunopathology.

In contrast, the higher NLR observed in challenged PBS controls reflects an innate-dominant inflammatory response, which has been associated with early SARS-CoV-2 driven pulmonary inflammation and poorer viral control [64, 65]. The lymphocyte-predominant environment in vaccinated groups therefore suggests a more regulated and antigen-specific antiviral response.

Mild perivascular lymphocytic aggregates were observed in the lungs of mice receiving the empty vectors (Ad-ΔE1E3), likely reflecting low-level vector-driven immune activation [66]. In contrast, antigen-expressing constructs induced stronger, well-organized T cell infiltration, consistent with antigen-specific cellular immunity supporting viral control. These findings illustrate the dual role of Ad vectors: limited innate/vector-specific stimulation and potent adaptive, antigen-driven responses. Detailed phenotyping of infiltrates using CD4, CD8, and functional markers (e.g., Th1, Th2, Th17) could define helper and cytotoxic T cell polarization, providing mechanistic insight into the quality and balance of vaccine-induced immunity.

Collectively, our findings support a vaccine design strategy that integrates S-mediated neutralization with conserved epitope-driven T cell immunity delivered via a mucosal route. By combining cross-variant antigenic targets with heterologous Ad delivery, this approach broadens antiviral immunity beyond S and promotes localized pulmonary immune surveillance. For future clinical translation, we anticipate the use of a mucosal atomization device or nasal spray to ensure adenoviral vectors contact the nasal epithelium and nasopharynx. This method maximizes transduction of local mucosal cells to induce a strong immune response, as the fine, atomized mist is primarily retained within the nasal cavity and nasopharyngeal mucosa with significant lung deposition and closely mimicking the intended human intranasal immunization route.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that i.n. heterologous prime–boost vaccination using ChAd and BAd vectors expressing conserved N and M epitopes, particularly when combined with the S1 subunit of S, induces robust mucosal and systemic humoral and cellular immune responses and confers effective protection against SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.2.86 challenge in K18-hACE2 mice. These findings support the feasibility of a multiepitope, mucosal vaccination strategy to broaden protective immunity beyond S, potentially reducing the need for frequent vaccine reformulation. This strategy offers a promising framework for next-generation COVID-19 vaccines designed to maintain effectiveness against current and future SARS-CoV-2 variants. Future studies are warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms of protection mediated by Epi/N + Epi/M-based vaccination, assess the durability of the induced immune responses, and evaluate translational potential in additional preclinical and clinical settings.
