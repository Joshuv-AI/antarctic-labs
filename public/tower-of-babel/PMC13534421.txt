Hybrid transformer–fuzzy framework for interpretable sentiment classification in deepfake social media content
Authors: Ritu Gauraha, Ayush Kumar Agrawal, Parul Dubey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the increasing prevalence of deepfake content across social media, there is a growing challenge to trust online—especially when it comes to analyzing user attitude towards manipulative pieces of text. However, with millions of tweets generated every day, separating genuine sentiments from artificial narratives is a challenging area in sentiment analysis research. While previous models are often highly accurate, they depend on feature engineering and act like a black-box system, compromising interpretability and generalizability. This underscores the importance of having frameworks that are robust but also explainable. In the study, we evaluate the proposed model using TweepFake dataset which is publicly available with human and AI-generated tweets. Our study adopted the TweepFake dataset, which contains 25,572 tweets equally distributed between human and AI generated. Its balanced structure guarantees that the different methods of deepfake sentiment detection are assessed fairly. We present a hybrid method, which combines semantic richness (captured through Transformer-based contextual embeddings) with human-readable explanations based on fuzzy membership rules embedded in a Fuzzy Rule-Based System (FRBS). Its novelty is in integrating state-of-the-art contextual modeling with symbolic reasoning for interpretability. Under 10-fold cross-validation, performance was evaluated through Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, Jaccard Coefficient & MCC and Inference time. Experimental results demonstrate that the new framework has 97.0% accuracy and F1-score, with stable performance over epochs and more explainability compared to baseline models. The model outperforms the RoBERTa baseline, achieving a 1.6% improvement in accuracy.Keywords: Deepfake detection, Sentiment analysis, Transformer, Fuzzy rule-based system, Explainable AISubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Recently, deepfake text has generated unprecedented amounts of worry in digital communication 1. The report said that millions of social media users across the globe skim daily through content featuring potentially fake or distorted information1,2. On Twitter/X alone, more than 500 million tweets are sent each day, and a growing percentage of these feature deepfake content from AI powered fake news to mimicry based propaganda3. Recent studies indicate that nearly 41% of social media users have trouble discerning real posts from manipulated ones, and existing automated sentiment classification models achieve highly variable success with accuracy scores between 85% (traditional ML models) and 93% (BiLSTM, CNN-based methods). Given these statistics, there is an immediate demand for reliable and interpretable sentiment analysis techniques for deepfake-driven discussion4,5.

Deepfake text generation uses sophisticated generative language models, such as GPT-2 and GPT-3, based on transformer architectures to closely replicate human writing style6. Even though such technologies can have great utility in benign applications like automatic content generation, chatbots, and accessibility tools, they also support harmful use cases like fake news propagation, impersonation and influence campaigns. In previous works have been classified into three categories, namely lexicon-based approach (TextBlob, VADER, AFINN), traditional machine learning models (SVM, LR, DT), and deep learning techniques (CNN, LSTM, BiLSTM, RNN). While such model-based approaches show reasonable performance, they can often lack interpretability which is crucial in socially sensitive tasks, such as predicting and explaining sentiment towards deep fake content7,8.

Classical machine learning and deep learning models have been widely applied in traditional artificial intelligence-based sentiment analysis methods. Classical machine learning methods, namely logistic regression, support vector machines (SVM) and random forests, were first applied with handcrafted features such as bag-of-words (BoW), term frequency–inverse document frequency (TF-IDF), or lexicon-based indicators9. With the rapid development of deep learning, recurrent neural network (RNN), convolutional neural network (CNN) and long short-term memory (LSTM) models have made good use of sequential dependencies and context of semantics in text9,10. Although such techniques led to notable performance on many benchmarks, they often needed heavy hand-crafted feature engineering works and raised concerns on their applicability especially for some important areas (e.g., the misinformation and deepfake detection), where interpretability and robustness are highly desired11,12.

The rise of a large-scale transformer model like BERT and RoBERTa transformers architecture has revolutionize the sentiment analysis, since these models yields contextual embedding mechanism by self-attention13–16. In contrast to sequential models, transformers can model bidirectional dependencies, hence making them more suitable to capture fine-grained sentiment expressions and context drifts17–19. The language model that is pre-trained on sentiment datasets and then fine-tuned for classification have been showed to outperform a number of different metrics than the classical approach of performance from deep learning models20,21. Other recent transformer-based approaches also adopt interpretability and domain knowledge methods, such as multi-task learning or domain-sensitive fine-tuning targeted to the performance and responsible AI issue22. Yet, in spite of their great performance, transformer models are fundamentally a black-box, which makes explanations difficult for high-stake applications like fake tweet detection.

Some hybrids between fuzzy and deep or machine learning models have been introduced in the past, but their design rationale and application domain are quite different from those addressed here. For example, Sweidan et al.22 Fine-tune manually extracted aspect-level sentiment features from neural models using a fuzzy-ontology layer, with their fuzzy reasoning over manual defined aspect terms but not contextual sentence embeddings17. A solution combining fuzzy SVM with a CNN feature extraction phase is also proposed (at the classifier level) in order to manage uncertainty during polarity classification18. Meanwhile, some studies integrate the word-embedding features along with membership functions for generic sentiment classification task.

Unlike these methods, our approach directly employs fuzzy reasoning on high-dimensional Transformer-based contextual embeddings to interpret deep semantic representations instead of surface or handcrafted features. What’s more, the available fuzzy–deep hybrids are designed for general sentiment analysis not covering synthetic or AI-generated text in depth. We are unaware of any prior research that marries RoBERTa embeddings up with a compact linguistic FRBS for explainable sentiment analysis in deepfake (AI-generated) tweets, featuring distinctive sources of the “difficult-to-read” problem: variation supplied by the generator combined with controlled language style and artificial emotion.

The base paper used a feature-based fusion from transfer learning outputs (such as embeddings) and hancrafted features like TF-IDF, then applied majority voting among classifiers23. This approach had a very high reported accuracy 98.9%; however, it used weakly labeled data from lexicon tools such as TextBlob, VADER, and AFINN which leads to noisy labeling of the dataset. Besides, the over-reliance on hand-designed feature engineering affects generalization to other fraud scenarios and lack of interpretability methods make it less appropriate in responsible AI settings. These limitations motivate our proposed hybrid Transformer–FRBS model that stresses on semantic learning, interpretability, and thorough evaluation in balanced datasets. Sentiment classification for deepfake tweets suffers from three major challenges:

Contextual Understanding Deficit: Traditional models fail to fully capture long-range dependencies and nuanced expressions such as sarcasm or irony.

Lack of Explainability: Deep learning black-box models, despite high performance, cannot justify their predictions in human-interpretable ways.

To address these shortcomings, in this paper, a Hybrid Transformer–Fuzzy framework is proposed. Transformer models (like BERT, RoBERTa) are used to produce powerful contextual embeddings and a Fuzzy Rule-Based System (FRBS) generates interpretable rules for explaining prediction decisions. The novelty consists in the combination of state-of-the-art contextual learning and symbolic reasoning, thus uniting predictive power with interpretability. This two-path model leads to better classification performance and is also consistent with the principles of Responsible AI, which provides stakeholders with more confidence and understanding. Methodology

To this end, we propose a framework that combines Transformer encoder for context based embedding generation and the FRBS for interpretable sentiment classification. RoBERTa produces context-sensitive embeddings capturing semantic coherence, syntactic construction, sentiment orientation and discourse-level relationships. With respect to the deepfake text, fake tweets from time-killing synthesis commonly manifest abnormally central themes, repetitive lexical structures, or overly smooth sentence conversion. In embedding space these patterns are reflected in sharper polarity projections, which the FRBS accounts for with their µₚₒₛ and µₙₑg membership degrees. Data preprocessing

Retweets are pre-processed by stripping the URLs, hashtags, emojis, user mentions, stopwords and punctuation. Lowercasing and lemmatization are applied to normalize the text. The cleaned text is tokenized as input sequences for the Transformer model. Pre-processing is very important in stabilizing the contextual space used to obtain RoBERTa embeddings. Comparative experiments between raw and cleaned tweets demonstrated 11–14% less embedding variance in the cross-validated folds with cleaned tweets. This better uniformity allows the FRBS to be applied on more structured semantic projections with an increased rule coverage as well as interpretability.

All tweets were converted into lower case and preprocessed by removing URLs, users references, hashtags, emojis, extra punctuation marks and non-UTF characters. Common garbage of any tweet for twitter like characters, repeating symbols was also normalized. Missing values and empty tweets were discarded. Stop words were not removed as Transformer models need full context. Tokenization was conducted using the RoBERTa byte-pair encoder and a maximum sequence length of 128 tokens.

The dataset was split into a development set (90%) and a held-out test set (10%). The robustness of this model was further proven by its ICC tested in 10-fold cross-validation on the development set. The results shown in the last block are run on average of folds and corroborated with the raw test samples. The raw dataset comprises 25,836 tweets. After preprocessing, including duplicates deletion, corrupted lines and empty texts removal, we get 23,572 useful tweets. This is our end dataset for modeling. The balanced one was used to avoid the bias of models towards majority class, and be able to compare interpretability measures in both human-generated and AI generated classes. In cases of imbalanced deepfake content in real-world application, we will consider performance under imbalance experiment and how to augment the dataset.

Transformer-based embedding generation

The preprocessed tokens are fed into a pretrained Transformer (e.g., BERT, RoBERTa) to generate the contextual embeddings. The long-range dependencies, the subtle semantic differences as well as the contextual shift of polarity which complex traditional RNN or CNN-based models might miss to some extent are embedded in its rich representation. In the case of every sequence of tokens, the encoder generates corresponding hidden vectors for all layers. A sentence-level representation is then obtained through average pooling or the [CLS] token, which acts as feature vector for sentiment prediction.

Cross-generator generalization protocol

In order to investigate whether the RoBERTa–FRBS model is robust against out-of-distribution examples apart from in-distribution samples, we performed a pan-generator generalization test with generator labels available in the TweepFake dataset. TweepFake contains synthetic tweets generated from four different generative models: Markov, RNN, LSTM and GPT-2.

In each experiment, one type of generator was used as the target distribution. The model has been trained on the rest and was tested only on the held-out generator. This leave-one-generator-out rotation tests the extent to which a model is generalising to new deepfake styles and linguistic patterns. We consider in general the results of both RoBERTa-base and our proposed RoBERTa-FRBS under this protocol to see if the noisy rule layer improves or maintains robustness under other generative styles.

Fuzzy rule-based reasoning

Fuzzy Rule-Based Systems (FRBS) are a method of decision-making that replicates the process used by humans to arrive at a decision. Rather than simply {yes, no} logic, it deals with degrees of truth (e.g., somewhat true or very false)25–27. It follows a predefined list of IF–THEN rules to reach conclusions, and it is a suitable technique for handling problems with imprecise/imperfect boundaries28–31. The embedding vector is converted into linguistic fuzzy variables (e.g., “positiveness,” “negativeness”). Unlike the rigid class boundaries of a hard-clas- sifier, sentiment categories are assigned degrees of membership by fuzzy sets. For instance, a tweet can be member of Positive set with degree 0.78 and the Negative set with degree 0.22-like membership value. Decision and explainability

Explainable AI (XAI) is characterized as the artificial intelligence methods and techniques that are implemented to make the decision-making process of such systems transparent, interpretable and understandable to human users32,33. It acts as the bridge between complex machine learning models and human trust by granting an understandable justification for predictions and actions34,35. The output sentiment decision is calculated by max–membership criterion or weighted defuzzification. Furthermore, other than the label, it also outputs fuzzy membership values (e.g., 0.78 Positive, 0.22 Negative), for telling explainability. This hybrid approach allows for state-of-the-art accuracy from Transformer embeddings as well as interpretability due to fuzzy rules, which solves the black-box problem of deep learning.

Interpretability evaluation

To measure the transparency of this proposed framework, we calculated the classical interpretability parameters for fuzzy systems. The structure of the FRBSs is nothing more than R = 2 rules with a single linguistic antecedent, being a system of very low structural complexity. The percentage of the average rule coverage is 92.7%; this tells us that for most samples, at least one rule fires with a reciprocal degree ≥ 0.7. Moreover, the degree of c-score is more than 95%, which means rule firing is stable between samples. This sparse rule base stands in stark contrast with high-dimensional token-level explanations generated by post-hoc methods of LIME or SHAP as mentioned earlier, confirming that the Transformer–FRBS framework we design yields global, stable and human- understandable interpretability. To illustrate how the proposed system identifies synthetic text, we analyze one human-written and one AI-generated tweet. For each tweet, we present the RoBERTa embedding projection, fuzzy membership degrees (µₚₒₛ and µₙₑg), fired rules, and the final prediction.

The fake tweet example exhibits unnaturally high topical coherence and uniformly structured phrasing, which results in a stronger µₚₒₛ projection despite neutral content. The real tweet shows natural stylistic variation and contextual noise, which is reflected in balanced membership values.

These examples demonstrate how the hybrid model captures subtle “artificiality” through contextual embedding patterns.

While the performance gain over RoBERTa (+ 1.6% accuracy) is statistically significant (Wilcoxon p = 0.006), the primary advantage of FRBS lies in its heuristic interpretability. In place of relying on a high-dimensional softmax layer, FRBS map contextual embedding projections into two explicit linguistic rules. It maps the operating dynamics of embedding spaces to human-interpretable decision boundaries, thereby making deepfake detection more reliable and interpretable.

Comparison with post-hoc XAI methods

To contextualize the interpretability of the proposed RoBERTa–FRBS model, we compared it qualitatively with widely used post-hoc explanation techniques including LIME, SHAP, and Transformer attention. Figure 6 presents a four-dimension qualitative comparison following established interpretability taxonomies in the XAI literature, covering: (i) explanation dimensionality, (ii) stability under input perturbations, (iii) human interpretability effort, and (iv) global versus local coverage. The scores shown in Fig. 6 do not represent numerical outputs of the models but are conceptual ratings grounded in empirical observations reported in prior XAI studies, which is a standard approach when comparing heterogeneous explanation formats (token-level vs. rule-based vs. attention-based).

Comparison of explanation dimensionality, stability, human interpretability, and global coverage across different XAI methods.

LIME and SHAP generate local, high-dimensional, tokenlevel attributions that can be difficult to interpret without significant cognitive load. Interpretations from them usually focus on 8–25 tokens per example and may easily change with slight perturbation in text. Explanations are frequently not stable and may fail to correctly justify the model. On the contrary, our framework RoBERTa–FRBS generates global sparse and linguistically meaningful rules with the great interpretability work savings and explanation stability.

Although the absolute performance gain of the proposed RoBERTa–FRBS model over the RoBERTa-base classifier (95.4% → 97.0%) appears modest at + 1.6%, the improvement is statistically significant. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test computed across 10 cross-validation folds yields p = 0.006, confirming that the observed gain is unlikely due to random variation. From a practical standpoint, a 1.6% increase in accuracy on a dataset containing more than 25,000 tweets translates into several hundred additional deepfake-related posts being correctly classified. For social media platforms that rely on automated systems to filter, prioritize, or flag harmful synthetic content, this magnitude of improvement represents a substantial operational advantage.

In addition to the measurable accuracy improvement, the hybrid framework introduces global interpretability through fuzzy linguistic rules, a capability absent in pure Transformer-based models. Thus, the RoBERTa–FRBS model offers both statistically validated performance benefits and valuable transparency for real-world deployment.

Limitations and future scope

This study has certain limitations. First, the evaluation is performed on the TweepFake dataset using texts generated by certain models and may not extrapolate well to newer or stronger text generators. Second, since the dataset is dominated by English tweets, it is not clear whether the model works well for multilingual or crosscultural scenarios. Third, the fine-tuning stage of the transformer model is computationally expensive, and may incur scalability difficulties on low-resource domains. Lastly, even though the FRBS and SHAP are powerful for enabling explainability, the explanations themselves lay mostly at rules and feature level but fall short of conveying deeper semantic or user-friendly narratives. While the primary evaluation in this work is on TweepFake, we also conducted cross generator generalization experiments by rotating an held out generators namely GPT2, RNN etc. Our experiments show that our model generalizes reasonably well to unseen synthetic text styles, being able to achieve + 1.5% to +1.7% test-set accuracy over RoBERTa-base in the out-of-distribution setting.

In the future, we will further validate and generalize these observations to external sentiment datasets and newer text generators such as GPT-3. 5/4, Mixtral, LLaMA-series) to additionally confirm UDA robustness over domain-shift. These limitations can be addressed in future work by using larger and more diverse datasets, across multiple languages, platforms, and generative models. Lightweight transformer versions like DistilBERT can be investigated in order to minimize computational cost as well as real world applicability. Also, the fusion of fuzzy reasoning with natural-language explanations would make more intuitive and user-friendly explanations. Finally, expanding the framework into a real-time monitoring system would be capable of capturing and analyzing content around deepfake in times of emergencies including elections and crises.

In our future work, we plan to further generalize the Transformer-FRBS model for the inference of multimodal deepfake using audio-text or image-text pairs. We will investigate domain adaptation methods to enhance robustness against new generative models such as GPT-3. 5/4 and LLaMA. Further, more advanced explainability techniques like semantic rule extraction and causal attribution methods will be incorporated to further increase the interpretability.

Conclusion

In this paper, a Hybrid Transformer-Fuzzy Framework was introduced for the sentiment classification of deepfake tweets by leveraging the compelling contextual capacity of Transformer models and interpretative power of Fuzzy Rule-Based Systems. On the TweepFake dataset (25,572 tweets), our model obtained 97.0% accuracy & F1-score results with stable performance in folds/epochs. Although not quite at state-of-the-art accuracy levels of feature-engineered baselines, the task approach has several clear advantages: no dependence on noisy lexicons, less need for feature engineering, robustness to noise and human-readable explanations. This shift is a move towards Responsible AI, where transparency and trust is as important as predictiveness. For extensions in the future, we may investigate multimodal signals, domain adaptation and advanced explainability.

To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to combine fuzzy linguistic rules with contextualized Transformer representations for deepfake tweet sentiment prediction. Although in this work we will mainly concentrate on the TweepFake dataset, TweepFake itself contains naturally occurring multiple generator families (Markov, RNN, LSTM, GPT-2), allowing us to carry out internal evaluation with a form of substantial generative diversity. The cross-generator experiments validate that the proposed architecture RoBERTa–FRBS generalises well on to unseen generative styles, though it’s worth noting that future work could further complement these results with more extensive datasets and new large-scale generators to completely widen the range of generalisation description.
