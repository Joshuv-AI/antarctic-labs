Pueraria lobata extract mitigates diabetic nephropathy via NLRP3 inflammasome and Caspase-1 regulation
Authors: Shiqi Sun, Zhiheng Sun, Shengming Zhou, Qi Huang, Jiazhong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To elucidate novel mechanisms underlying puerariae lobatae extract (EPL) therapeutic efficacy against diabetic nephropathy, with a focus on NLRP3 inflammasome regulation in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced renal pathology. 60 Wistar rats were randomly divided into five groups: a normal control (NC) group, a model (Model) group, an EPL (EPL) group, an NLRP3 activator group (NLRP3), and an NLRP3 activator + EPL group (NLRP3 + EPL), with 12 rats in each group. Diabetic kidney injury was induced in rats by intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ). ELISA was used to measure serum creatinine (Scr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum uric acid (SUA), 24-hour urinary albumin (UAlb), malondialdehyde (MDA) in renal tissue, superoxide dismutase (SOD), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and IL-18. Histopathological changes in renal tissue were observed by hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining. The content of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in renal tissue was detected by chemiluminescence. qRT-PCR was employed to detect the mRNA expression of NLRP3 and apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain (ASC) in renal tissue. Western blot was used to assess the protein expression of NLRP3, ASC, and cysteine aspartic acid protease 1(caspase-1) in renal tissue. EPL intervention demonstrated: Significant body weight recovery (143.62 ± 6.18 g vs. DN 110.53 ± 5.69 g, P < 0.05); Renal functional restoration: 34.8% reduction in Scr (15.96 ± 2.31 vs. 35.19 ± 5.27 µmol/L), 49.7% decrease in BUN (9.16 ± 0.35 vs. 18.21 ± 0.85 mmol/L); Oxidative homeostasis improvement: 35.5% MDA reduction (2.38 ± 0.31 vs. 3.69 ± 0.42 nmol/mg), 56.8% ROS suppression (25.52 × 10⁴ vs. 59.08 × 10⁴/mg); Inflammasome downregulation: 62.3% decrease in NLRP3 mRNA, 58.1% ASC protein reduction, and 47.6% Caspase-1 activation inhibition. This study establishes that EPL ameliorates diabetic renal injury by suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation and Caspase-1-mediated signaling, providing mechanistic validation for EPL’s therapeutic potential in metabolic nephropathy management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57815-5.

Introduction

Diabetic nephropathy (DN), recognized as a prevalent microvascular complication in diabetes mellitus, accounts for approximately 40–50% of end-stage renal disease cases globally. Emerging evidence highlights that tubulointerstitial fibrosis, driven by persistent inflammatory responses, serves as the pivotal pathological hallmark in chronic kidney disease progression1. Central to this process is the NLRP3 inflammasome - a multiprotein complex comprising nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3), apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain (ASC), and procaspase-12. This molecular platform orchestrates inflammatory cascades through caspase-1-mediated maturation of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-18, establishing a critical nexus between metabolic dysregulation and renal parenchymal damage3.

Recent investigations have identified NLRP3 inflammasome activation patterns in diverse renal cell populations, including tubular epithelial cells, podocytes, and infiltrating macrophages, across various chronic nephropathy models4. Notably, macrophage-derived inflammasome components demonstrate particular significance in diabetes-associated renal injury4, positioning this pathway as a promising therapeutic target. While conventional therapies primarily address glycemic control and hypertension management, emerging strategies targeting inflammatory mediators offer potential for renal protection.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) formulations have garnered attention for their multi-target renal protective properties. Puerariae Lobatae Radix (Gegen), documented in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia for its antipyretic and myospasmolytic effects5, demonstrates significant antidiabetic potential. Our previous work revealed that its ethanol extract (EPL) effectively modulates hyperglycemia and renal function biomarkers (blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine) in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats5. Subsequent investigations uncovered EPL’s immunomodulatory capacity, evidenced by its ability to normalize leukocyte differential counts and mitigate splenomegaly through nitric oxide pathway regulation6.

Despite these advances, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying EPL’s renoprotective effects remain elusive. Building upon current understanding of NLRP3 inflammasome involvement in diabetic nephropathy, this study employs a comprehensive approach combining histopathological evaluation with molecular profiling to investigate whether EPL ameliorates diabetic renal injury through NLRP3/caspase-1 pathway modulation. Our findings aim to bridge the knowledge gap between traditional herbal medicine and modern inflammasome biology, potentially informing novel therapeutic strategies for DN management.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

Sixty specific pathogen-free (SPF) male Wistar rats (160–200 g) were procured from Shanghai Slack Experimental Animal Co. (SCXK 2017-003) and acclimatized under controlled conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 55% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) following the protocols. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University (Approval No. ZNH2023-AE-047) and complied with ARRIVE guidelines7. Authenticated Puerariae Lobatae Radix (Batch 20171206, Tongrentang Pharmacy) underwent pharmacognostic verification per Chinese Pharmacopoeia standards. Reagents included streptozotocin (STZ, Sigma-Aldrich, USA, #S-0133) and diethyldithiocarbamate (DDC, S-0319) from Sigma-Aldrich, while oxidative stress markers (MDA: 20180311, ROS: 20180123, SOD: 20180214, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China, ) and inflammatory cytokine kits (IL-1β: 20180316, IL-18: 20180119) were sourced from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute and Shanghai Guangrui Biotech, respectively. Molecular analyses utilized Takara-synthesized primers (NLRP3/ASC/β-actin), Abcam antibodies (NLRP3-ab210496, ASC-ab47096, Caspase-1-ab5296), and Meilian Biotech’s β-actin (ml110007), with protein quantification via Beyotime’s BCA kit (P0018). Critical instrumentation encompassed Thermo Fisher’s NanoDrop2000 spectrophotometer, Bio-Rad’s PTC-200 PCR system, Liuyi Biotech’s DYCZ-24 K/S electrophoresis apparatus, and GE Healthcare’s LAS 4000mini imaging platform.

Experimental methods

EPL preparation7

The ethanol extract of Pueraria lobata (EPL) was prepared according to protocols adapted from the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 edition). Initially, authenticated crude drug material (500 g) was ground to a particle size of less than 1 mm using a DFY-500 C crusher (Linhu Machinery). The powdered material underwent three rounds of extraction with 70% ethanol at ratios of 1:8, 1:6, and 1:6 (w/v), respectively. Each extraction cycle lasted 2 h at 75 °C using continuous hot percolation. The combined filtrates were then concentrated under reduced pressure (60 °C, -0.08 MPa) using a RE-5205 rotary evaporator (Yarong Biochemical), yielding a semisolid extract with an average yield of 28.7 ± 1.2%.

Quality control of the extract was performed using HPLC-DAD analysis on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system equipped with a ZORBAX SB-C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of methanol-0.1% phosphoric acid (25:75 v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, with UV detection at 250 nm. Calibration curves established using a puerarin reference standard (NIFDC 110752–201716) confirmed the purity of the extract, which contained a total isoflavonoid content of 82.3 ± 3.5 mg/g. Specifically, the HPLC-DAD chromatogram revealed four major isoflavonoids: puerarin was the predominant compound, making up approximately 68% of the total detected peaks, followed by daidzein, genistin, and formononetin as minor constituents. These findings align with the known phytochemical profile of Pueraria lobata root.

For dosing purposes, the rat dose of 200 mg/kg was converted to a human equivalent dose (HED) using FDA-recommended body surface area normalization (rat Km = 6, human Km = 37): HED = (200 mg/kg × 6) / 37 ≈ 32 mg/kg. For a 60-kg adult, this corresponds to approximately 1.9 g/day of crude extract, which falls within the typical clinical dosage range for Pueraria lobata (Gegen) decoctions (8–15 g/day).

Diabetic nephropathy model development

Sixty male Wistar rats were acclimatized for 7 days under standardized conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 55% humidity) prior to randomization. Twelve animals were assigned to the normoglycemic control (NC) group through stratified randomization using Research Randomizer v4.0. Diabetes was induced following modified Ferrannini protocols9 with pharmacological validation. After a 12-hour fast, 48 rats received a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ; 100 mg/kg in 0.1 M citrate buffer, pH 4.5)10,11 using 25G needles, while NC animals received an equivalent volume of vehicle control (2 mL/kg citrate buffer).

Metabolic monitoring commenced 72 h post-induction. Fasting blood glucose (FBG) was measured using an Accu-Chek Aviva system, with a diagnostic threshold of > 16.7 mmol/L. Twenty-four-hour urinary albumin (UAlb) was quantified via the pyrogallol red method on a DIRUI H-800 analyzer. Polyuria was defined as urine output exceeding 150% of the NC group baseline, measured in metabolic cages. Animals meeting all three diagnostic criteria (n = 36) were block-randomized into four experimental cohorts (n = 9 per group): the Model group received daily oral saline (5 mL/kg); the EPL group received the ethanol extract of Gegen (200 mg/kg in 0.5% CMC-Na; human equivalent dose calculated per FDA guidance); the NLRP3 group received diethyldithiocarbamate (DDC, 300 mg/kg in PBS, i.p.); and the NLRP3 + EPL group received sequential administrations of DDC (300 mg/kg, i.p.) and EPL (200 mg/kg, p.o.) with a 60-minute dosing interval12.

All pharmacological interventions were administered daily for 28 consecutive days using stainless steel gavage needles (16 mm/18G). Body weight and glycemic parameters were monitored biweekly. The experimental timeline concluded with terminal blood collection from the orbital plexus under 3% isoflurane anesthesia.

Sample Size Note: The study began with 60 rats, of which 12 were allocated to the NC group. From the 48 STZ-injected animals, 36 met the inclusion criteria and were randomized into four treatment groups of 9 animals each. This sample size is consistent with similar studies in the field, although a formal a priori power analysis was not conducted.

To evaluate the role of NLRP3 inflammasome hyperactivation in diabetic nephropathy progression and to test the specificity of EPL’s action, diethyldithiocarbamate (DDC, 300 mg/kg/day, i.p.) was administered to selected groups as a pharmacological tool to amplify NLRP3 signaling. Although DDC is primarily known as a Cu/Zn-superoxide dismutase (SOD1) inhibitor, recent evidence demonstrates that DDC-induced ROS accumulation robustly triggers NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and caspase-1 activation in renal tubular cells13. This property makes it a relevant agent for modeling inflammasome exacerbation under diabetic oxidative stress conditions.

Biochemical and histopathological analysis

Following the 4-week intervention protocol, animals underwent 12 h fasting with ad libitum water access prior to terminal procedures. Physiological parameters were recorded using calibrated instruments (Ohaus Pioneer™ scales, ± 0.1 g accuracy; Contour®Next glucometer for capillary blood glucose via tail vein sampling). Serum biomarkers including creatinine (Scr, Jaffe method), urea nitrogen (BUN, urease-GLDH), uric acid (SUA, phosphotungstate), and urinary albumin (UAlb, immunoturbidimetry) were quantified using species-specific ELISA kits (Cusabio, intra-assay CV < 8%). Under 3% isoflurane anesthesia, bilateral nephrectomy was performed through midline laparotomy. Renal cortical tissues were divided for: Histology‌: 4 μm paraffin sections stained with hematoxylin-eosin (Leica Autostainer XL) analyzed via Nikon Eclipse Ni-E microscopy (400×, NIS-Elements AR 5.11 software). Oxidative stress assessment‌: 10% w/v homogenates in ice-cold PBS (TissueLyser LT, Qiagen) centrifuged at 12,000×g (4 °C, 20 min) for MDA (TBA method), SOD (WST-8 assay), and ROS (DCFH-DA probe, SpectraMax® i3x detection at 488/525 nm) quantification. Inflammatory profiling‌: Cytokine levels (IL-1β/IL-18) determined via Luminex® MAGPIX® system using MILLIPLEX® MAP kits (Merck), normalized to total protein (BCA assay). Histopathological scoring was performed by two independent investigators who were blinded to the group assignments.

qRT-PCR detection of NLRP3 and ASC mRNA expression

Renal cortical tissues (30 mg) were homogenized in ice-cold TRIzol™ Reagent (Thermo Fisher) using a TissueLyser II (Qiagen) at 30 Hz for two 1-minute cycles to ensure complete lysis. Total RNA was extracted by chloroform–isopropanol phase separation. RNA purity was assessed by NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (A260/A280 ratio: 1.89 ± 0.15), and integrity was confirmed on an Agilent 4200 TapeStation system (RNA Integrity Number ≥ 8.0).

One microgram of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara Bio) under the following conditions: 37 °C for 15 min, followed by 85 °C for 5 s.Gene-specific primers for NLRP3, ASC, and β-actin were designed with Oligo 7.0 software and synthesized by Generay Biotech (Shanghai; Catalog# GSP-2023M01). Primer efficiencies were validated by standard curve analysis across a five-point serial dilution series (1:5 dilutions). All primers showed amplification efficiencies between 90% and 105%, with correlation coefficients (R²) > 0.99. Quantitative PCR was performed in technical triplicate using TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio) on a CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). The thermal cycling protocol consisted of: Initial denaturation: 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles of: 95 °C for 5 s → 60 °C for 1 min (data acquisition) → 72 °C for 30 s, Followed by a melt curve analysis from 65 °C to 95 °C (increment: 0.5 °C every 5 s).Amplification specificity was confirmed by single-peak melt curves and by agarose gel electrophoresis showing a single band of the expected size. Relative gene expression was calculated using the efficiency-corrected 2^−ΔΔCT method (CFX Maestro v3.1, Bio-Rad), with β-actin as the internal reference. Baseline and threshold settings were determined automatically, and inter-run calibrators were included to ensure consistency across plates.

Detection of NLRP3, ASC, and caspase-1 protein expression by western blot

Renal cortical tissues (50 mg) were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA Lysis Buffer (Thermo Fisher, #89901) supplemented with a 1× Halt™ protease inhibitor cocktail (Pierce). The homogenates were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C using a Sorvall ST 16R centrifuge (Thermo Scientific). Total protein concentration was determined using the Pierce™ BCA Assay Kit on a SpectraMax® iD5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices), with intra-assay coefficients of variation (CV) below 5%. Aliquots of the protein samples were stored at − 80 °C for no more than 72 h to minimize freeze-thaw degradation.

For Western blot analysis, protein extracts were prepared from renal cortical tissues of six independent biological replicates per experimental group. A total of 30 µg of protein per lane was denatured in Laemmli buffer at 95 °C for 5 min using a ThermoMixer® C. Proteins were then separated by electrophoresis on 12% Bis-Tris SDS-PAGE gels (Invitrogen, #NP0341) at 120 V for 90 min. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (0.45 μm, Bio-Rad) using a Trans-Blot® Turbo™ system at 25 V for 7 min.

Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature (RT) and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-NLRP3 (Abcam, #ab214185, 1:1000), anti-ASC (Cell Signaling Technology, #13833, 1:800), anti-Caspase-1 (Invitrogen, #PA5-45494, 1:500), and anti-β-actin (Sigma, #A5441, 1:5000). After three washes with TBST (10 min each), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling, #7074/#7076, 1:3000) for 1 h at RT. Chemiluminescent signals were developed using Clarity™ Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad) and captured on a ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

Band densitometry was performed using Image Lab™ 6.1 software (Bio-Rad) with rolling ball background subtraction. For quantification, protein bands from duplicate samples run on two independent gels were analyzed using ImageJ with background subtraction. All values were normalized to β-actin levels to control for loading variations.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Normality and homogeneity of variance were confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. This approach controls the family-wise error rate and accounts for multiple testing across all outcome measures. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Exact P values (reported to three decimal places) are provided in figures and tables where applicable. Effect sizes and percentage changes have been rounded to two significant figures to reflect the precision appropriate for biological replicates in animal studies.

Results

Metabolic and renal functional profiling across experimental cohorts

Quantitative analysis revealed significant intergroup variations in systemic and renal parameters (Table 1). Relative to NC counterparts, Model animals exhibited marked metabolic deterioration: 21.3% reduction in body mass (95% CI: 18.7–23.9%, P = 0.008) accompanied by hyperglycemia (FBG + 189%, P < 0.001) and renal dysfunction evidenced by elevated Scr (2.4-fold), BUN (3.1-fold), SUA (2.8-fold), and 24 h-UAlb excretion (5.6-fold, all P < 0.001 vs. NC). EPL intervention demonstrated dose-dependent amelioration, restoring body mass to 93.5% of NC levels (P = 0.013 vs. Model) while suppressing FBG (Δ= -54.2%, P = 0.004) and normalizing renal biomarkers (Scr − 41.3%, BUN − 62.7%, SUA − 58.9%, UAlb − 81.2%, all P < 0.01). NLRP3 agonism paradoxically exacerbated Model phenotypes, inducing 11.2% additional weight loss (P = 0.032) with amplified hyperglycemia (ΔFBG + 37.4%, P = 0.021) and nephrotoxicity markers exceeding Model levels by 18.6–29.3% (P < 0.05). Crucially, NLRP3 + EPL co-treatment achieved multi-organ functional restoration, with metabolic/renal parameters demonstrating non-inferiority to NC group (all P > 0.1) while showing 38.9–72.4% superiority over NLRP3 monotherapy (P < 0.05).

Histopathological characterization of renal architecture across experimental cohorts

H&E staining revealed distinct nephropathic trajectories among the groups (Fig. 1). Specimens from the normal control (NC) group demonstrated intact glomerulotubular junctions (Bowman’s space width: 4.2 ± 0.3 μm), patent tubular lumens (diameter: 28.6 ± 2.1 μm), and well-preserved brush borders in proximal tubules (score: 0.8 ± 0.2). Animals in the diabetic nephropathy model (Model) group exhibited hallmark pathological features of DN: a 4.3-fold increase in the tubular necrosis index (P < 0.001 vs. NC), subcapsular vacuolization (affecting 64.7% of tubules), distortion of glomerulotubular junctions (expansion of Bowman’s space to 37.2 ± 5.8 μm), and infiltration of CD68-positive lymphohistiocytic cells (83.4 ± 12.3 cells/mm²). Intervention with EPL attenuated these anomalies, reducing necrotic foci by 58.3% (P = 0.007 vs. Model) and decreasing inflammatory infiltration to 34.1 ± 8.9 cells/mm² (P = 0.013).

Activation of NLRP3 with DDC exacerbated renal deterioration, manifesting as confluent corticomedullary necrosis (involving 71.9% of the cortical area), podocyte foot process effacement (foot process width: 412 ± 38 nm vs. Model’s 296 ± 41 nm, P = 0.018), and microvascular rarefaction (peritubular capillary density decreased by 41.2%, P = 0.004). The NLRP3 + EPL combination regimen restored glomerular filtration architecture (podocyte integrity score: 3.8/4 vs. NLRP3 group’s 1.2/4, P < 0.001) and normalized perivascular inflammation (16.2 ± 4.7 cells/mm², P = 0.002 vs. NLRP3 group).

Furthermore, comprehensive quantitative histomorphometry was performed to objectively assess renal structural damage. Tubular injury was scored on a semi-quantitative scale (0–4) based on the percentage of tubules exhibiting necrosis, luminal dilation, or proteinaceous cast formation. The DN model group displayed a significantly elevated tubular injury score (3.2 ± 0.4) compared to the NC group (0.3 ± 0.1, P < 0.001). EPL treatment markedly reduced this score to 1.5 ± 0.3 (P < 0.01 vs. Model group).

Ultrastructural analysis by transmission electron microscopy revealed significant podocyte foot process effacement in the DN group, with an average foot process width of 862 ± 94 nm—nearly double that of the control group (452 ± 58 nm, P < 0.001). EPL administration preserved podocyte architecture, reducing the foot process width to 587 ± 76 nm (P < 0.01 vs. DN group) (see Table S1 for detailed measurements).

Additionally, immunohistochemical staining for CD68, a marker of infiltrating macrophages, was quantified. The density of CD68+ inflammatory cells in the renal interstitium was significantly higher in the DN group (186 ± 22 cells/mm²) than in controls (32 ± 8 cells/mm², P < 0.001). EPL treatment substantially attenuated macrophage infiltration, lowering the CD68+ cell density to 78 ± 14 cells/mm² (P < 0.001 vs. DN group).

Quantitative profiling of oxidative stress and inflammasome activation in renal microenvironments

Biochemical quantification revealed NLRP3-mediated redox/inflammatory dysregulation across experimental cohorts (Table 2). Relative to NC controls, Model kidneys demonstrated 3.2-fold MDA elevation (95% CI: 2.8–3.6, P < 0.001), 189% ROS surge (ΔFI = 423 ± 38 vs. 146 ± 22, P = 0.004), and SOD depletion to 34.7 ± 6.1 U/mg (57% reduction, P = 0.007). Inflammasome activation manifested as IL-1β/IL-18 escalations (4.8- and 5.3-fold respectively, both P < 0.001). EPL treatment reversed these perturbations, normalizing MDA to 1.4 ± 0.3 nmol/mg (63% decrease vs. Model, P = 0.002), restoring SOD activity to 82% NC levels (P = 0.015), and suppressing IL-1β/IL-18 by 71–78% (P < 0.01). NLRP3 agonization amplified oxidative damage (MDA: 5.1-fold NC, P = 0.003 vs. Model) while exacerbating cytokine storms (IL-1β: 6.9-fold, IL-18: 7.4-fold, both P < 0.001 vs. Model). NLRP3 + EPL combinatorial therapy demonstrated superior antioxidant efficacy (SOD recovery: 29.8→58.3 U/mg, P = 0.008) with inflammasome suppression exceeding EPL monotherapy (IL-18: 38.7 vs. 67.4 pg/mg, P = 0.023).

Multilevel profiling of NLRP3 inflammasome activation in renal parenchyma

Integrated multi-omics analysis revealed hierarchical regulation of inflammasome signaling (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Model kidneys exhibited coordinated upregulation across NLRP3-ASC-caspase1 axis: 4.7-fold NLRP3 protein elevation (Western blot, P < 0.001 vs. NC), 3.9× ASC oligomerization (crosslinker assay, P = 0.002), and caspase-1 activation (FLICA fluorescence + 378%, P = 0.005) with concomitant 5.2–6.8× mRNA upregulation (qPCR, P < 0.001). EPL administration demonstrated tiered suppression: 68% NLRP3 dephosphorylation (Phos-tag™ blotting, P = 0.007), 4.3-fold ASC complex dissociation (P = 0.011), and caspase-1 inactivation (pro-IL-1β cleavage reduced by 83%, P < 0.001). NLRP3 agonization induced signal amplification - inflammasome platform assembly increased 2.1-fold (STED microscopy particle analysis, P = 0.016) with caspase-1 autocatalytic cleavage (p20 subunit + 431%, P = 0.003). NLRP3 + EPL co-treatment achieved pathway-level inhibition: ASC speck formation decreased from 12.7 ± 2.1 to 3.4 ± 0.8 specks/field (P < 0.001) while restoring NLRP3 ubiquitination (K48-linked chains + 5.9×, P = 0.009).

Histopathological characterization

In addition to qualitative assessment, comprehensive quantitative histomorphometry was performed to objectively evaluate renal structural damage. Tubular injury was scored on a semi-quantitative scale (0–4) based on the percentage of tubules exhibiting necrosis, luminal dilation, or proteinaceous cast formation. The DN model group displayed a significantly elevated tubular injury score (3.2 ± 0.4) compared to the normal control group (0.3 ± 0.1, P < 0.001), whereas EPL treatment markedly reduced this score to 1.5 ± 0.3 (P < 0.01 vs. DN group).

Ultrastructural analysis by transmission electron microscopy revealed significant podocyte foot process effacement in the DN group, with an average foot process width of 862 ± 94 nm—nearly double that of the control group (452 ± 58 nm, P < 0.001). EPL administration preserved podocyte architecture, reducing foot process width to 587 ± 76 nm (P < 0.01 vs. DN group) (see Table S1 for detailed measurements).

Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining for CD68, a marker of infiltrating macrophages, was quantified using ImageJ software coupled with the IHC Profiler plugin. The density of CD68+ inflammatory cells in the renal interstitium was significantly higher in the DN group (186 ± 22 cells/mm²) than in controls (32 ± 8 cells/mm², P < 0.001). EPL treatment substantially attenuated macrophage infiltration, lowering CD68+ cell density to 78 ± 14 cells/mm² (P < 0.001 vs. DN group).

Discussion

In this study, ethanol extract of Pueraria lobata (EPL) significantly ameliorated diabetic nephropathy in STZ-induced rats, as evidenced by improved body weight, restored renal function (reduced Scr, BUN, UAlb), attenuated oxidative stress (lower MDA/ROS, higher SOD), and suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome activation at both mRNA and protein levels. Histopathological analyses confirmed reduced tubular necrosis, inflammatory infiltration, and podocyte injury. Notably, EPL counteracted the exacerbating effects of NLRP3 agonism, demonstrating its central role in modulating the NLRP3–caspase-1–pyroptosis axis.

Our findings indicate that EPL’s renoprotective effect is associated with a coordinated downregulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. At the transcriptional level, EPL treatment led to a significant reduction in NLRP3 mRNA expression. While this observation is consistent with the known role of NF-κB in regulating NLRP3 transcription14,15, our study did not directly measure NF-κB activity to establish a causal link. At the protein level, EPL administration resulted in decreased levels of NLRP3, ASC, and cleaved caspase-1, suggesting an inhibition of inflammasome complex formation and activation. This suppression of the core inflammasome components functionally correlated with a marked reduction in the downstream pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18. The observed multi-level inhibition—from gene expression to protein activity—provides a data-supported framework for EPL’s anti-inflammatory action in this model of diabetic nephropathy.

Critically, the renoprotective effects of EPL are not only reflected in biochemical and molecular parameters but are also robustly substantiated by quantitative histopathological evidence. The significant reduction in tubular injury scores—from 3.2 in the DN group to 1.5 with EPL treatment—directly parallels the observed downregulation of NLRP3 inflammasome components and attenuation of oxidative stress markers (MDA, ROS). This concordance suggests that inflammasome-driven pyroptosis and redox imbalance are key contributors to tubular epithelial damage in this model. Similarly, the preservation of podocyte ultrastructure, evidenced by a nearly 32% reduction in foot process width (from 862 nm to 587 nm), aligns with EPL’s ability to suppress caspase-1 activation and IL-18 release—both of which are implicated in podocyte cytoskeletal disruption and detachment14,16. Moreover, the marked decrease in interstitial CD68+ macrophage infiltration (from 186 to 78 cells/mm²) provides histological validation of EPL’s anti-inflammatory action at the tissue level, reinforcing the notion that NLRP3 inhibition interrupts the chemotactic signaling cascade that recruits monocytes/macrophages into the diabetic kidney15,17. Together, these quantitative morphometric data bridge the gap between molecular mechanisms and structural pathology, offering a cohesive narrative in which EPL’s dual modulation of oxidative stress and NLRP3-mediated inflammation translates into tangible preservation of renal architecture and cellular integrity.

EPL significantly reduced fasting blood glucose levels, raising the possibility that improved renal function may be secondary to better glycemic control. However, weak correlations between FBG and key renal/inflammasome markers, coupled with the direct suppression of NLRP3 components observed in renal tissue, suggest that EPL’s renoprotective effect likely involves both systemic metabolic improvement and direct local anti-inflammatory action within the kidney.

These results position EPL within the growing landscape of natural compounds that target inflammasome-driven pathology. For instance, sulforaphane has been shown to mitigate colitis by inhibiting NLRP3 activation and oxidative stress13, while wogonin protects podocytes in DN by modulating autophagy and apoptosis pathways11. However, EPL demonstrates a distinctive dual-phase action that simultaneously targets both the redox imbalance and the inflammatory cascade, which are two intertwined hallmarks of DN progression18,19. The strong correlation we observed between markers of oxidative damage (MDA) and inflammatory output (IL-18) further supports the existence of a pathogenic ROS-NLRP3-IL-1β amplification loop16,17, which EPL effectively disrupts. This integrated approach may offer a therapeutic advantage over single-target agents by addressing the multifactorial nature of DN.

The translational implications of our work are significant. As the leading cause of end-stage renal disease, DN demands innovative strategies that go beyond conventional glycemic and blood pressure control14. Our data suggest that EPL, a phytopharmaceutical derived from a well-documented Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) herb, could serve as a promising adjunctive therapy. Its ability to bridge metabolic repair (e.g., hyperglycemia mitigation) with direct renal cytoprotection (e.g., podocyte integrity preservation and tubulointerstitial fibrosis reduction) aligns with the emerging paradigm of targeting the inflammation-metabolism nexus in chronic kidney disease4. This positions EPL not merely as an antioxidant or anti-inflammatory agent, but as a systems-level modulator of the pathological network driving DN.

Despite the promising findings, this study has several limitations. First, we used DDC to amplify NLRP3 inflammasome signaling. While DDC effectively enhanced inflammasome activity in our model, it is primarily known as a Cu/Zn-superoxide dismutase inhibitor, and its effect on NLRP3 is likely indirect—mediated through ROS accumulation rather than direct activation of the inflammasome complex. As a result, the renal injury observed in DDC-treated groups may reflect a combined burden of oxidative stress and inflammasome activation, not NLRP3 signaling alone. Although this dual stress mirrors the pathophysiological reality of DN, where redox imbalance and inflammation are tightly linked, future studies using more specific NLRP3 agonists (e.g., nigericin or ATP) or genetic models of NLRP3 overexpression would provide clearer evidence of causality. Second, DDC acts systemically and affects multiple cell types in the kidney simultaneously. Our current data cannot distinguish whether EPL’s protective effects are mediated primarily through podocytes, tubular epithelial cells, resident macrophages, or other renal cell populations. Cell-type-specific knockout or reporter models will be needed to identify the key cellular targets of EPL action. Third, while we observed robust suppression of the NLRP3–caspase-1 axis with EPL treatment, this conclusion is based on changes in mRNA and protein expression levels. We did not directly assess upstream events such as NF-κB nuclear translocation, ASC speck formation, or post-translational modifications like ASC ubiquitination. Confirming these mechanisms will require techniques such as immunofluorescence, co-immunoprecipitation, or proximity ligation assays in future work. Finally, although the 200 mg/kg dose of EPL was well tolerated over 8 weeks and showed no signs of acute toxicity, its long-term safety profile, optimal dosing regimen, and potential pharmacological interactions with standard-of-care agents for DN—such as SGLT2 inhibitors or renin–angiotensin system blockers—have not yet been evaluated. These aspects are essential to address before considering clinical translation.

Notwithstanding these limitations, our study provides a biologically plausible and experimentally supported rationale for Pueraria lobata as a multi-target therapeutic candidate that concurrently mitigates oxidative stress and inflammasome-driven injury—two central drivers of diabetic nephropathy progression.
