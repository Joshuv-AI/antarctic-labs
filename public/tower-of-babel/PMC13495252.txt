Resurgence of malaria in northern Ethiopia (Tigray), 2018–2024: evidence from routine surveillance data

Background

Malaria remains a major public health challenge in Ethiopia, where both Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax are endemic [1, 2]. Between 2004 and 2018, Ethiopia achieved substantial reductions in malaria burden through expanded coverage of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), indoor residual spraying (IRS), improved diagnostics, and access to effective antimalarial treatment [1, 2]. However, recent reports indicate renewed increases in malaria incidence in several parts of the country, raising concerns regarding the sustainability of previous gains in malaria control [3, 4].

Previous studies have documented major changes in malaria epidemiology in Ethiopia over the past two decades. Taffese et al. reported substantial reductions in malaria incidence, mortality, and transmission intensity between 2001 and 2016 following the scale-up of malaria control interventions [2]. Subsequently, Teka et al. described urban malaria trends between 2014 and 2019, reporting an overall decline in urban malaria burden followed by renewed increases in some locations and highlighting the growing importance of urban malaria surveillance [5]. In Western Tigray, Kassahun et al. reported fluctuating malaria trends between 2011 and 2019 despite ongoing control efforts [6]. More recently, Zhou et al. documented a national resurgence of malaria between 2018 and 2022, with expanding hotspot areas and widespread outbreaks reported across Ethiopia [3].

Despite the importance of northern Ethiopia to the national malaria burden, contemporary regional surveillance data from Tigray beyond 2019 remain limited. Tigray was only sparsely represented in recent national assessments, leaving an important geographic and temporal gap in understanding malaria trends during a period of changing transmission dynamics. The present study addresses this gap by analyzing routine malaria surveillance data from Tigray between 2018 and 2024. By extending previous regional and national analyses, this study aimed to characterize temporal and spatial malaria trends in Tigray and assess how recent incidence patterns compare with broader malaria trends reported elsewhere in Ethiopia.

Methods

Data source and analysis

This study analyzed routinely collected malaria surveillance data from the Tigray region of northern Ethiopia between January 2018 and December 2024. Data were obtained from regional health authorities and consisted of weekly reported malaria incidence aggregated by epidemiological week. Data from accessible reporting areas were included in the analysis. Because surveillance coverage varied across the study period and reporting from some districts was incomplete, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting reported malaria incidence rather than the total regional malaria burden.

Study design

A retrospective surveillance analysis was conducted to assess temporal changes in malaria incidence. This study was descriptive in nature and was not designed to establish causal relationships.

Statistical analysis

Weekly malaria incidence was summarized by year using descriptive statistics. Differences among years were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Pairwise comparisons between the 2018–2022 baseline period and subsequent years were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests. To account for seasonality, weeks 1–27 of 2024 were compared with corresponding weeks from 2018 to 2022. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1.

Results

Malaria trends in Tigray, 2018–2024

Malaria surveillance data from accessible areas of Tigray between 2018 and 2024 demonstrated substantial interannual variability, with pronounced increases in reported incidence during 2023 and sustained high transmission into 2024 (Fig. 1). Weekly malaria incidence differed significantly across the study period (Kruskal–Wallis test, H = 152.71, p < 0.0001).

Reported malaria cases by epidemiological week in Tigray, northern Ethiopia, from 2018 to week 27 of 2024. Data illustrate a pronounced resurgence in cases in 2023 and beginning of 2024

Compared with the 2018–2022 baseline period, malaria incidence increased in 2023. Median weekly incidence rose from 2,113 cases during 2018–2022 to 5359 cases in 2023, representing a 2.5-fold increase. Weekly incidence in 2023 was significantly higher than during the baseline period (Mann–Whitney U = 7015, p < 0.0001). The highest weekly incidence was recorded in epidemiological week 46 of 2023 (10,223 cases), representing a 4.8-fold surge compared with the median weekly incidence during the 2018–2022 baseline period. Elevated transmission persisted in 2024, with a median weekly incidence of 5484 cases through epidemiological week 27. Comparison of weeks 1–27 in 2024 with the corresponding weeks during 2018–2022 confirmed significantly higher incidence in 2024 (Mann–Whitney U = 1674, p < 0.0001).

In 2023, more than 301,000 malaria cases were reported by epidemiological week 28, representing the highest cumulative burden recorded during the study period. By week 28 of 2024, 145,845 cases had already been reported, indicating continued high transmission. Although incidence varied considerably between years, seasonal increases were consistently observed during the main transmission period.

Spatial analyses revealed substantial heterogeneity across zones and woredas (districts), with a disproportionate burden concentrated in a limited number of localities (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. 1). Within most zones, a small number of woredas accounted for substantially higher case numbers than surrounding areas, indicating localized transmission hotspots rather than uniform increases across the region. For example, Kewet in the Central Zone, Asgede Tsimbla in the North West Zone, Hawzen Rural in the East Zone, Samre in the South East Zone, and Raya Chercher in the South Zone were among the highest-reporting woredas during the study period. The concentration of cases within a limited number of woredas suggests that the resurgence was not uniformly distributed across the region and highlights the importance of sub-regional surveillance for identifying transmission hotspots and targeting control efforts. Reporting coverage was incomplete in some areas, and therefore, the available data likely underestimate the total malaria burden in Tigray.

Discussion

This study provides one of the first regional descriptions of malaria incidence trends in Tigray extending beyond 2019 and addresses an important gap in the Ethiopian malaria literature. The findings complement previous reports documenting substantial reductions in malaria burden between 2001 and 2016 [2], urban malaria trends through 2019 [5], fluctuating malaria burden in Western Tigray between 2011 and 2019 [6], and the recent national resurgence of malaria reported elsewhere in Ethiopia [3, 4]. These studies indicate that malaria resurgence remains a recurring challenge during malaria control and elimination efforts in Ethiopia and that the increases observed in Tigray during 2023–2024 are consistent with broader epidemiological patterns reported nationally. Malaria resurgence has been documented repeatedly in regions approaching malaria control or elimination. A systematic review by Cohen et al. identified weakening of control programs, environmental change, population movement, and health-system limitations among the most frequently reported contributors to malaria resurgence globally [7].

The present study was not designed to identify the causes of resurgence in Tigray; however, the findings highlight the need for future investigations integrating epidemiological, climatic, entomological, and health-system data to better understand the drivers of changing malaria transmission patterns in northern Ethiopia.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The analysis relied on routinely collected surveillance data, and reporting completeness varied across woredas and over time. In addition, data from some areas were unavailable, meaning that reported incidence likely underestimates the true malaria burden in the region. Weekly reporting coverage was not uniform throughout the study period, and therefore, results should be interpreted as trends in reported malaria burden rather than precise estimates of regional incidence.

The relatively low reported incidence during 2021–2022 should also be interpreted with caution, as reduced healthcare utilization and interruptions to routine surveillance and reporting associated with the COVID-19 pandemic and the regional conflict resulting in broader disruptions to health-service delivery may have affected case detection [8]. Nevertheless, the consistency of the increase across multiple reporting areas and the magnitude of the rise observed in 2023–2024 suggest that the resurgence reflects a genuine epidemiological pattern rather than reporting variation alone.

This study was intended to provide surveillance evidence from a region that has been underrepresented in recent national assessments rather than to identify the causes of malaria resurgence. Understanding the relative contribution of climatic, environmental, operational, and vector-related factors will require more detailed longitudinal studies integrating epidemiological and entomological data.

Recent reports have highlighted the expansion of Anopheles stephensi and other emerging vector-related challenges elsewhere in Ethiopia [3, 5]. While the present study was not designed to evaluate the role of specific vector species, the observed resurgence underscores the importance of integrating epidemiological surveillance with entomological monitoring to improve understanding of changing malaria transmission patterns and support evidence-based control strategies.

Conclusion

Malaria incidence in Tigray increased significantly during 2023 and remained elevated in 2024 compared with the 2018–2022 baseline period. These findings provide important surveillance evidence from a region that has been underrepresented in recent national assessments and demonstrate substantial temporal and spatial variation in malaria burden. Continued epidemiological and entomological surveillance will be important for monitoring transmission patterns and informing targeted malaria control efforts in northern Ethiopia.
