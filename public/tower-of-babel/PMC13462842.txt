Artificial intelligence-based systems as auxiliary tools for thyroid nodule malignancy detection on ultrasound: a systematic review and meta-analysis of diagnostic accuracy
Authors: Rafael De Cicco, Francisco Araujo Dias, Victor Bandini Vieira, Rafael Souza, Tabata Briaunys Milan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI)-based systems are valuable tools in thyroid nodule diagnosis, but clinical controversy remains regarding diagnostic inconsistency and specificity. This meta-analysis evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of AI-assisted ultrasound systems for detecting malignant thyroid nodules compared to radiologists and risk stratification systems.MethodsGuided by the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study Design) framework, a systematic search across PubMed, Semantic Scholar, and OpenAlex identified 69 studies using histopathological or cytological reference standards. Quality and Risk of Bias were assessed via an AI-tailored QUADAS-2 tool. Pooled sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were calculated using bivariate random-effects meta-analysis.ResultsThe 69 studies evaluated >350,000 nodules. AI-based systems demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy: pooled sensitivity 89% [95% confidence interval (CI): 87–91%], specificity 84% (95% CI: 80–88%), and AUC 0.93 (95% CI: 0.91–0.95). In direct comparisons, AI systems achieved comparable or superior performance to experienced radiologists (AUC 0.90–0.95 vs. 0.76–0.86). AI significantly improved specificity over individual radiologists and systems like ACR TI-RADS. AI-optimized TI-RADS achieved higher specificity (70.2% vs. 49.2%, P<0.001) than standard ACR TI-RADS with similar sensitivity. Consequently, AI-assisted strategies reduced unnecessary fine-needle aspirations (FNAs) by up to 26.7 percentage points. Performance peaked for nodules <20 mm and in externally validated, multicenter settings. AI provided the greatest incremental benefit for less experienced practitioners, markedly improving interobserver agreement.ConclusionsAI systems achieve high diagnostic accuracy for thyroid nodule malignancy, demonstrating performance comparable or superior to radiologists and risk stratification frameworks. As adjunctive tools, they safely reduce unnecessary biopsies and improve diagnostic consistency. Future clinical integration must prioritize multimodal data and radiomics to maximize utility.Keywords: Artificial intelligence (AI), thyroid nodule, ultrasound, diagnostic accuracy, systematic review

Highlight box

Key findings

• Artificial intelligence (AI) systems demonstrate high diagnostic accuracy (pooled area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.93) for detecting malignant thyroid nodules on ultrasound.

• AI significantly improves diagnostic specificity (83.6% vs. 71.4%) compared to experienced radiologists, directly reducing unnecessary fine-needle aspirations.

What is known and what is new?

• Conventional ultrasound risk stratification systems (e.g., American College of Radiology Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System) suffer from high interobserver variability and frequently lead to overdiagnosis of benign nodules.

• This comprehensive meta-analysis confirms that AI functions effectively as an auxiliary decision-support tool, improving both diagnostic consistency among readers and overall specificity, particularly in challenging cases like follicular-patterned lesions.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• AI-based diagnostic systems should be integrated into clinical workflows as adjunctive tools (“second readers”).

• Future clinical implementations must prioritize AI integration with multimodal data and radiomics to maximize diagnostic utility and reduce healthcare burdens.

Introduction

The accurate diagnosis of thyroid nodules represents one of the most common clinical challenges in modern medicine, with significant implications for patient management and outcomes. The prevalence of thyroid nodules detected on ultrasonography ranges from 16% to 67% in different populations, with higher rates in studies utilizing high-frequency ultrasound and in predominantly female populations (1). Despite this high prevalence, malignancy occurs in only 7% to 15% of nodules, creating a substantial clinical dilemma: how to efficiently identify the minority of malignant lesions while avoiding unnecessary diagnostic interventions in the majority of benign nodules (2).

Current clinical practice relies on risk stratification systems such as the American College of Radiology Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (ACR TI-RADS), the European Thyroid Association (ETA) guidelines, the American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines, and the Chinese Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (C-TIRADS), among others (3-5). These systems employ standardized ultrasound features, including nodule composition, echogenicity, shape, margin characteristics, and the presence of echogenic foci, to categorize nodules into risk categories that guide management recommendations. However, significant interobserver variability exists in the interpretation of these ultrasound features, even among experienced radiologists, leading to diagnostic inconsistency and potentially suboptimal management decisions (6-8).

Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) cytology, guided by risk stratification systems, remains the reference standard for determining which nodules require pathological evaluation. However, FNA has several limitations: it is an invasive procedure carrying risks of discomfort, infection, and vascular injury; it cannot provide definitive diagnosis in all cases, with many samples classified as indeterminate (Bethesda III–V categories); and significant cytological interpretation variability exists among pathologists (9,10). These limitations result in unnecessary FNAs being performed in many patients, placing a substantial burden on healthcare systems and patients.

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly machine learning and deep learning techniques, has emerged as a potentially transformative approach to medical image analysis, including thyroid ultrasound interpretation. AI systems can process large volumes of imaging data, identify complex patterns that may be imperceptible to human observers, and provide rapid, quantitative risk assessments with high reproducibility (11-13). Several types of AI approaches have been applied to thyroid nodule diagnosis, including traditional machine learning algorithms (random forests, support vector machines, logistic regression), deep learning convolutional neural networks (CNNs), radiomics-based approaches, and hybrid frameworks combining human-extracted features with machine learning (14,15).

Although multiple individual studies have evaluated AI systems for thyroid nodule diagnosis, with generally promising results, systematic synthesis of these findings has been limited. Previous narrative reviews have provided qualitative summaries, but quantitative meta-analyses incorporating the expanding body of literature on AI diagnostic accuracy are needed to establish the current evidence base and guide clinical implementation (16,17). Additionally, heterogeneity in study designs, AI algorithms, comparator groups, and outcome measures has made interpretation of the comparative effectiveness of AI versus conventional diagnostic approaches challenging (18-20).

Furthermore, a robust evaluation of AI performance cannot rely exclusively on algorithmic differences (e.g., CNN versus traditional machine learning) (21). Critical drivers of diagnostic variance, such as data pathology, training set diversity, and domain shift across different ultrasound hardware and image quality standards, must be addressed to understand true clinical applicability (22). In modern clinical workflows, AI is best conceptualized not as a standalone diagnostic entity, but as a “goalkeeper”—an auxiliary decision-support tool designed to support human decision-making, mitigate interobserver variability, and safely reduce overdiagnosis, particularly in complex and controversial scenarios such as the evaluation of follicular-patterned lesions (23-25).

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to comprehensively evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of AI-based systems for detecting malignant thyroid nodules on ultrasound, compare their performance to standard radiologist assessment and conventional risk stratification systems, identify factors associated with superior or inferior performance, and provide evidence-based recommendations for clinical integration of AI technology into thyroid nodule management pathways. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2026-1-0126/rc).

Methods

Search strategy and study selection

A systematic search was conducted and updated through March 2026 across PubMed, Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex platforms, using the research query: “What is the diagnostic accuracy of artificial intelligence based systems for detecting malignant thyroid nodules on ultrasound compared with standard radiologist assessment or conventional risk stratification systems?” The updated search strategy ensured the capture of recent advancements in deep learning architectures, such as YOLOv11 models.

The search returned 500 articles, which were retrieved for abstract screening. Studies were screened for inclusion based on the following criteria.

Crucially, to prevent overfitting bias and ensure the reported metrics reflect true generalizable performance, data regarding AI diagnostic accuracy were strictly extracted from validation or external test sets rather than internal training sets.

Quality assessment

Study quality was assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2) tool, tailored with specific signaling questions specifically designed for AI studies. These AI-tailored signaling questions included: (I) whether the AI algorithm (index test) was strictly blinded to the pathology reference standard during the evaluation phase; (II) whether the training, tuning, and validation datasets were strictly and transparently partitioned to prevent data leakage; and (III) whether the validation sets included independent, external data to test for domain shift. Each domain was rated as low, high, or unclear risk of bias. Studies were not excluded based on quality assessment; rather, quality was considered in sensitivity analyses and heterogeneity exploration.

Statistical analysis

Pooled estimates of sensitivity, specificity, and AUC were calculated using bivariate random-effects meta-analysis models (which operate equivalently to hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic, or HSROC, models) to appropriately account for the correlation between sensitivity and specificity across studies and to adjust for threshold effects. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were calculated using the exact binomial method. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with I2>50% indicating substantial heterogeneity.

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by:

❖ Study design (retrospective vs. prospective), geographic region (Asia vs. North America vs. Europe vs. other), single-center vs. multicenter design, presence vs. absence of external validation, type of AI approach (deep learning vs. traditional machine learning), nodule size category (<10 vs. 10–20 vs. >20 mm), malignancy prevalence in study population (<30% vs. ≥30%). Meta-regression analyses were performed to examine relationships between study characteristics and diagnostic accuracy estimates.

❖ Study design (retrospective vs. prospective), geographic region (Asia vs. North America vs. Europe vs. other), single-center vs. multicenter design, presence vs. absence of external validation, type of AI approach (deep learning vs. traditional machine learning), nodule size category (<10 vs. 10–20 vs. >20 mm), malignancy prevalence in study population (<30% vs. ≥30%). Meta-regression analyses were performed to examine relationships between study characteristics and diagnostic accuracy estimates.

❖ Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot visual inspection and Begg’s test. For comparisons between AI and specific comparators, direct comparison meta-analyses were performed when sufficient data were available (at least three studies with direct comparisons). All analyses were performed using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software (Version 3.3, Biostat, Inc.) with a two-tailed significance level of P<0.05.

Diagnostic performance of AI systems

Pooled diagnostic accuracy

The meta-analysis encompassed diagnostic accuracy estimates from 45 studies with sufficient data. Pooled estimates demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy: sensitivity 89% (95% CI: 87–91%), specificity 84% (95% CI: 80–88%), and AUC 0.93 (95% CI: 0.91–0.95). These estimates indicated excellent overall diagnostic discrimination, with the AUC value exceeding the threshold for excellent test performance (AUC >0.90).

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial for sensitivity (I2=78.3%, 95% prediction interval: 76–102%) and specificity (I2=82.1%, 95% prediction interval: 71–96%), indicating that study characteristics and methodological factors significantly influenced reported diagnostic accuracy.

Range of individual study estimates

Individual study AUC values ranged widely from 0.75 to 0.986, though the majority exceeded 0.85. Sensitivity estimates ranged from 69.6% to 98.4%, and specificity from 52% to 94.5%. Superior performance was generally observed in deep learning ensemble models, multicenter studies, studies with external validation, studies from Asia, and prospective studies.

AI performance compared to radiologists

Across the included studies, AI systems demonstrated diagnostic performance that was superior or at least comparable to that of radiologists in the majority of direct comparisons, with 11 of 14 studies (78.6%) favoring AI-assisted evaluation. A pooled meta-analysis of the nine studies that provided sufficient data for direct AUC comparison confirmed this trend, showing a mean AUC of 0.922 (95% CI: 0.910–0.934) for AI systems compared with 0.842 (95% CI: 0.825–0.859) for radiologists. This corresponds to a statistically significant mean difference of 0.080 (95% CI: 0.055–0.105; P<0.0001), indicating a consistent improvement in overall diagnostic discrimination when AI is incorporated into the evaluation process (Table 4).

When examining sensitivity and specificity separately, a characteristic pattern emerged. AI systems demonstrated sensitivity that was comparable to, or slightly lower than, that of radiologists, with pooled values of 85.2% for AI versus 85.8% for human readers. In contrast, specificity was consistently and substantially higher with AI assistance. The mean specificity reached 83.6% for AI compared with 71.4% for radiologists, representing an absolute improvement of 12.2 percentage points (95% CI: 8.7–15.7%; P<0.0001). This finding is clinically meaningful, as the principal limitation of conventional ultrasound interpretation lies in the high false-positive rate driven by defensive diagnostic behavior. The observed gain in specificity therefore represents one of the most impactful contributions of AI to thyroid nodule management, with direct implications for reducing unnecessary biopsies and surgeries.

Impact of radiologist experience level

Four studies specifically examined differential performance based on radiologist experience level. These studies consistently demonstrated that AI systems provided greater incremental benefit when assisting less experienced radiologists. Junior radiologists assisted by deep learning achieved accuracy and AUC comparable to senior physicians, whereas unassisted junior radiologists achieved lower performance. S-Detect outperformed junior sonographers but showed comparable performance to senior sonographers.

AI performance compared to risk stratification systems

Direct comparisons between AI systems and ACR TI-RADS consistently demonstrated that AI provides superior or at least comparable diagnostic performance. Across eight comparative studies, the pooled mean AUC for AI systems reached 0.892 (95% CI: 0.876–0.908), whereas ACR TI-RADS achieved a mean AUC of 0.792 (95% CI: 0.774–0.810), representing a statistically significant difference of 0.100 (95% CI: 0.074–0.126; P<0.0001). These findings indicate that AI systems are capable of improving the discriminatory capacity of ultrasound evaluation beyond conventional lexicon-based risk stratification.

The benefits become even more evident when examining AI-optimized TI-RADS models. In a large multicenter validation study, AI-TI-RADS achieved an AUC of 0.762 compared with 0.679 for standard ACR TI-RADS, a highly significant improvement. The most clinically meaningful gain was observed in specificity, which increased from 49.2% with conventional TI-RADS to 70.2% with AI assistance, while sensitivity remained statistically comparable (82.2% versus 86.7%, P=0.052). Importantly, this improvement translated into tangible clinical impact. The AI-TI-RADS system successfully downgraded 743 nodules originally classified as intermediate risk, potentially avoiding 328 unnecessary FNA procedures, corresponding to a reduction of 42.3% without compromising malignancy detection.

Additional evidence supports the superiority of AI across guideline comparisons. A large study comparing the AI-SONIC platform with six ultrasound risk stratification guidelines demonstrated that AI systems achieved comparable or superior diagnostic performance relative to established frameworks, reinforcing the potential role of AI as an integrated decision-support tool in thyroid imaging (Tables 5-7).

One of the most clinically relevant consequences of AI implementation is the substantial reduction in unnecessary biopsy recommendations. Multiple studies quantified this effect across different AI platforms. The ThyNet-assisted strategy reduced FNA recommendations from 61.9% to 35.2%, representing a 26.7-percentage-point decrease while simultaneously lowering the rate of missed malignancies from 18.9% to 17.0%. Similarly, AI-TI-RADS avoided 42.3% of unnecessary FNAs through the downgrading of benign nodules. Machine learning–assisted visual approaches reduced unnecessary FNA rates from 30.0% to 4.5%, corresponding to a 25.5-percentage-point reduction. Deep learning models applied to follicular lesions decreased biopsy recommendations from 40.7% to 8.5–12.8%, while AI-based identification of benign nodules reduced FNA rates from 68.5% to 9.1%. Collectively, these findings highlight that the most transformative contribution of AI lies in improving specificity and preventing the cascade of overtreatment that has historically characterized thyroid nodule management.

AI has demonstrated a substantial capacity to reduce interobserver variability in the assessment of thyroid nodules, a limitation that has historically affected ultrasound-based risk stratification. Evidence across multiple studies consistently shows that AI-assisted interpretation enhances reproducibility and diagnostic consistency among readers. When radiologists used the Koios DS system as a decision-support tool, interobserver agreement increased significantly from a correlation coefficient of 0.622 to 0.876, indicating a marked improvement in consistency of interpretation. Similarly, AI-optimized TI-RADS achieved superior inter-rater reliability compared with conventional ACR TI-RADS, with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.861 versus 0.808, a statistically significant difference. Among the evaluated systems, AI-SONIC demonstrated the highest degree of reproducibility, achieving a Kendall’s tau coefficient of 0.995, reflecting near-perfect agreement. Overall, AI-assisted evaluation reached a mean interobserver concordance of 0.89, which corresponds to excellent agreement and clearly surpasses the variability typically observed among human readers alone, where concordance generally ranges between 0.72 and 0.85.

The incorporation of AI into thyroid ultrasound workflows has also demonstrated meaningful improvements in efficiency and workflow optimization. Several studies have shown that AI systems can markedly reduce interpretation time without compromising diagnostic accuracy. For example, the AI-SONIC platform achieved a mean diagnostic time of only 0.146 seconds per nodule, whereas radiologist interpretation typically required between 2.8 and 4.5 minutes per case, representing an approximately 18- to 40-fold reduction in reading time. Similarly, the implementation of the Koios DS system was associated with a significant decrease in reporting time, reducing the average time per case by 23.6% (P<0.001). These findings suggest that beyond improving diagnostic performance, AI has the potential to substantially increase workflow efficiency and throughput in high-volume thyroid imaging settings.

When performance was stratified by nodule size, several studies consistently demonstrated superior accuracy in smaller nodules. For nodules measuring less than 20 mm, AI systems achieved sensitivity of approximately 0.90 and specificity of 0.85, outperforming larger nodule categories. Notably, dynamic AI models showed significantly higher accuracy for subcentimeter nodules (≤0.50 cm) compared with conventional preoperative ultrasound assessment, highlighting the particular value of AI in early-stage disease detection (Tables 13-15).

Patient-related characteristics also influenced performance. Diagnostic accuracy was generally higher in patients aged 50 years or older compared with younger populations. In addition, studies with a higher proportion of female patients reported improved AI performance, likely reflecting the epidemiological distribution and imaging characteristics of thyroid disease.

AI systems demonstrated particularly meaningful advantages in diagnostically challenging scenarios. In follicular-patterned thyroid lesions, where conventional ultrasound features are notoriously limited, the AI-SONIC platform achieved an accuracy of 0.71 compared with 0.62 for the best-performing radiologist, a statistically significant improvement. Similarly, in cytologically indeterminate nodules classified as Bethesda categories III–V, the S-Detect system achieved a sensitivity of 95.9% and an overall accuracy of 88.1%, outperforming both ACR TI-RADS and C-TIRADS. Among heterogeneous TI-RADS category 4 nodules, fusion AI models achieved an AUROC of 0.880, exceeding the performance of experienced radiologists, who reached an AUROC of 0.815. Collectively, these findings reinforce the role of AI as a particularly valuable tool in scenarios where traditional ultrasound interpretation is most limited.

Discussion

This comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of 69 studies demonstrates that AI-based systems have achieved substantial diagnostic accuracy for thyroid nodule malignancy detection on ultrasound, with pooled sensitivity of 89%, specificity of 84%, and AUC of 0.93. These performance metrics indicate that AI systems have progressed beyond investigational status to become potentially clinically deployable tools.

A consistent pattern emerging from comparative studies is that AI systems demonstrate improved specificity while maintaining similar sensitivity compared to radiologists. This pattern is highly clinically favorable, as it translates to fewer false positive diagnoses requiring unnecessary FNA while identifying malignant nodules at rates comparable to human interpretation. The mechanism underlying this specificity advantage likely relates to AI’s ability to identify subtle quantitative imaging patterns associated with benignity that may escape human visual perception.

The observation that AI systems provide particularly substantial benefit when assisting junior radiologists or sonographers is of major significance for clinical implementation, particularly in healthcare systems with variable experience levels. This finding suggests an important role for AI in reducing diagnostic variability and improving the consistency of care. Implementation of AI-assisted systems could substantially “level up” diagnostic accuracy among less experienced practitioners, approaching the performance of senior radiologists.

Furthermore, the substantial reductions in unnecessary FNA procedures documented in multiple studies represent a major potential healthcare benefit. Reducing FNA rates from 61.9% to 35.2% or from 30.0% to 4.5% would dramatically decrease patient burden, reduce procedural risks, and decrease healthcare costs. These findings underscore that AI value extends beyond diagnostic accuracy to include improvements in clinical decision-making and resource allocation.

Hence, the superior performance of AI systems in specific challenging scenarios, such as follicular-patterned lesions, cytologically indeterminate nodules, and TI-RADS Category 4 lesions suggests particular value in situations where human radiologists struggle most. These challenging scenarios represent exactly the situations where improved diagnostic accuracy would have the greatest clinical impact.

Meta-regression analyses identified several methodological and design characteristics that were consistently associated with superior diagnostic performance of AI systems. Deep learning approaches, particularly ensemble models that combine multiple CNN architectures, demonstrated higher diagnostic accuracy than single-architecture CNNs or traditional machine-learning methods. Furthermore, the rapid evolution of deep learning, exemplified by recent architectures like YOLOv11, necessitates continuous updating of models and search parameters, which we acknowledge as an ongoing feature in the field. Studies conducted in multicenter environments with external validation cohorts also showed higher AUC values, reinforcing the importance of dataset diversity and generalizability. Prospective studies yielded slightly superior results compared with retrospective analyses, with reported AUC values of 0.913 versus 0.909, suggesting that real-world validation strengthens performance estimates. In addition, AI systems consistently demonstrated optimized performance for nodules smaller than 20 mm, highlighting their particular utility in early disease detection and screening contexts.

The considerable heterogeneity observed across studies reflects the rapidly evolving and methodologically diverse nature of this field. Reported between-study heterogeneity was substantial, with I2 values of 78.3% for sensitivity and 82.1% for specificity. This variability can be explained by differences in AI architecture, training data characteristics, study design, and population selection. AI performance is strongly influenced by the size, diversity, and imaging characteristics of training datasets, and malignancy prevalence varied widely among studies, ranging from approximately 10% to 60%. Some investigations evaluated unselected screening populations, whereas others focused on enriched surgical cohorts, which further contributed to variability in performance metrics. The possibility of publication bias must also be acknowledged, as studies reporting positive results may be more likely to reach publication. Second, our literature search was limited to PubMed, Semantic Scholar, and OpenAlex. While comprehensive, the omission of databases such as Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science may have introduced a degree of selection or publication bias, representing a methodological limitation of this review.

The available evidence supports several practical strategies for integrating AI into clinical workflows. The most realistic and immediately applicable model is the use of AI as a diagnostic adjunct or “second reader”, in which radiologists perform conventional interpretation and AI provides an independent risk assessment. Concordant results between human and algorithm can increase diagnostic confidence and reduce variability. AI also demonstrates clear value in optimizing existing guideline-based frameworks, particularly through the development of AI-modified risk stratification systems such as AI-optimized TI-RADS. Another important application is the support of less experienced practitioners, where AI assistance has been shown to substantially improve diagnostic accuracy and narrow the expertise gap. In high-volume practices, AI may also serve as a triage tool by identifying high-confidence benign nodules that do not require detailed radiologist review, thereby improving efficiency and workflow.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations must be considered before widespread implementation. Most available studies have been conducted in research environments using curated datasets, and real-world implementation data remain limited. Clinical deployment requires regulatory approval and certification, and important legal and ethical questions remain regarding liability when AI contributes to diagnostic decisions. Data privacy and security are additional concerns, particularly when cloud-based systems require transfer of ultrasound images to external servers. Evidence regarding cost-effectiveness is still scarce, and further economic analyses are needed. Many AI systems also remain difficult to interpret, functioning as “black boxes” with limited transparency, which may hinder clinical acceptance. Finally, the rapid pace of technological evolution means that current systems may quickly become outdated, emphasizing the need for continuous validation and updating of AI tools in clinical practice.

Future research should focus on several strategic areas to consolidate the role of AI in thyroid nodule management and facilitate its transition from research environments to routine clinical practice. First, there is a clear need for large prospective multicenter validation studies conducted across diverse healthcare systems. Such studies are essential to confirm real-world generalizability, reduce bias related to curated datasets, and establish robust external validation.

Direct head-to-head comparisons between commercially available and research-based AI systems also represent an important next step. Current evidence often evaluates individual algorithms in isolation, which limits the ability to identify the most effective platforms and to define performance benchmarks. Comparative studies would help clarify the relative strengths and limitations of competing architectures and support the development of standardized performance metrics.

Another promising direction involves the integration of AI with multimodal imaging. Combining ultrasound data with other imaging modalities, such as elastography, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or molecular imaging, may enable the development of multimodal predictive models capable of improving diagnostic accuracy and risk stratification. Beyond diagnosis, AI has the potential to expand into prognostic modeling and therapeutic decision support. Future systems may be able to predict tumor aggressiveness, guide individualized treatment selection, and support active surveillance strategies. Health economic evaluation represents another major research priority. Comprehensive cost-effectiveness analyses comparing AI-assisted and conventional diagnostic pathways are required to determine the financial sustainability and healthcare impact of widespread adoption. Parallel to these efforts, the development of clear regulatory frameworks and quality standards is essential to ensure safe implementation and continuous performance monitoring of AI-based diagnostic tools.

Finally, improving interpretability remains a critical objective. The development of explainable AI models capable of providing transparent and clinically meaningful reasoning will be fundamental to increasing clinician trust, facilitating adoption, and ensuring responsible integration into clinical decision-making.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides comprehensive evidence that AI-based systems have achieved high diagnostic accuracy for thyroid nodule malignancy detection on ultrasound, with performance comparable to or superior to experienced radiologists and established risk stratification systems. AI systems demonstrate particularly promising utility for: assisting less experienced practitioners, substantially improving their diagnostic accuracy, reducing unnecessary FNAs while maintaining malignancy detection rates, improving diagnostic consistency and reducing interobserver variability, providing rapid analysis and substantial efficiency gains, and addressing challenging diagnostic scenarios such as follicular-patterned lesions and cytologically indeterminate nodules.

As healthcare systems globally move toward value-based care, precision medicine, and optimization of patient outcomes, AI-assisted thyroid nodule diagnosis represents a compelling use case for technology integration. The evidence base is now sufficiently robust to support clinical implementation of appropriately validated AI systems as adjunctive tools within standardized thyroid nodule management pathways. However, successful translation of research findings to clinical practice requires careful attention to regulatory requirements, cost-effectiveness, data security, clinician training, and integration with existing clinical workflows.

Future prospective, multicenter studies with diverse patient populations and long-term outcome follow-up will be essential to establish the definitive clinical role of AI-assisted diagnosis in thyroid nodule management and to identify which patients and clinical scenarios benefit most from AI assistance. As evidence continues to accumulate and technology improves, AI systems are poised to become essential tools in the diagnostic armamentarium of thyroid specialists.
