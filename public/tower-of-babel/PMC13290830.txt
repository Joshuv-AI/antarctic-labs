Zinc-binding protein metallothionein 3 protects vascular smooth muscle cells from ferroptosis via blocking lysosomal degradation of GPX4
Authors: Wenxin Lin, Xin Feng, Jingjie Chen, Bo Huo, Xian Guo, Molin Yang, Ding-Sheng Jiang, Linling Yu, Yue Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractAortic dissection (AD) is a life-threatening vascular emergency characterized by medial degeneration and vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) loss. Although disruption of zinc homeostasis has been reported in patients with AD, how zinc ions and their regulatory proteins influence VSMC survival and disease progression remains unknown. In this study, single-cell analyses revealed that ferroptosis and zinc-related pathways were significantly enriched in VSMCs from patients with AD, showing a strong correlation between the two processes, and zinc levels were markedly elevated in dissected aortas. Furthermore, zinc exposure promoted ferroptosis in cultured primary human aortic smooth muscle cells (HASMCs). By integrating transcriptomic data from AD tissues and VSMC ferroptosis models, metallothionein-3 (MT3), a zinc-binding protein, was identified as a candidate regulator. Functional studies demonstrated that MT3 overexpression markedly attenuated lipid peroxidation, reduced reactive oxygen species accumulation, and protected VSMCs from ferroptotic cell death, whereas MT3 knockdown increased oxidative stress and exacerbated ferroptotic injury. Mechanistically, MT3 directly interacted with glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), enhanced its protein stability, without altering its transcriptional expression, and promoted glutathione synthesis, thereby activating the glutathione-GPX4 antioxidant defense pathway and mitigating oxidative injury. Notably, restoration of GPX4 effectively rescued the pro-ferroptotic effects of MT3 deficiency on HASMCs. These findings establish a previously unrecognized zinc-MT3-GPX4 axis as a critical determinant of VSMC ferroptosis in AD, linking zinc dysregulation to medial degeneration, and highlighting MT3 as a potential mechanistic candidate target to preserve vascular integrity and limit disease progression.Key Messages Impaired zinc homeostasis is implicated in the development of aortic dissection (AD).Zinc-binding protein metallothionein 3 (MT3) mitigates lipid peroxidation and protects vascular smooth muscle cells from ferroptosis.MT3 directly interacts with glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) to prevent its lysosomal degradation, thereby enhancing the glutathione–GPX4 antioxidant defense pathway.MT3 is a potential mechanistic candidate target for preserving vascular integrity and limiting AD progression. Background

Aortic dissection (AD) is a critical cardiovascular condition characterized by a tear in the intimal layer of the aorta, resulting in the formation of a false lumen [1]. Our previous studies have identified vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) ferroptosis as a key contributor to medial degeneration, ultimately leading to AD [2]. Ferroptosis is a non-apoptotic cell death mechanism characterized by iron-dependent membrane lipid peroxidation [3]. Intracellular iron overload triggers the Fenton reaction, exacerbating the accumulation of membrane lipid peroxides and leading to the execution of ferroptosis [3]. Compared to normal aortic tissues, dissected aortas exhibit increased iron content along with elevated levels of the lipid peroxide 4-HNE [4]. Notably, an imbalance in the redox system is another factor contributing to VSMC ferroptosis and is linked to the formation of AD. Our earlier studies revealed that antioxidant signaling pathways, including glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1), and GTP cyclohydrolase 1 (GCH1), are inhibited during VSMC ferroptosis [4]. Treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitors liproxstatin-1 or BRD4770 demonstrated a protective effect against VSMC ferroptosis in vivo and delayed AD through the activation of these pathways [2, 4]. Therefore, further exploration of the mechanisms regulating the redox pathway might provide deeper insights into VSMC ferroptosis and identify novel targets for intervention in AD.

As the essential trace elements, zinc plays a crucial role in numerous biological functions, and has been linked to aortic homeostasis. Previous studies have shown that zinc levels are significantly lower in the serum and aortae of AD patients compared to controls [5]. In addition, intraperitoneal injection of ZnSO4 inhibited NF-κB signaling pathway via A20 in VSMCs, and thereby prevent the development of abdominal aortic aneurysm in rats [6]. However, an in vivo study showed that low zinc reduced inflammation in the aortic wall and inhibited the phenotypic transition of VSMCs from a contractile to a synthetic state, ultimately alleviating the development of AD [7]. Thus, the regulatory role of zinc in aortic diseases remains controversial, underscoring the urgent need for more in-depth investigation. Salazar et al. reported that zinc promoted mitochondrial reactive oxide species (ROS) accumulation and NOX1 expression, eventually inducing senescence of VSMCs [8]. Similarly, zinc increased ROS production via ERK1/2-dependent post-transcriptional regulation and further exacerbated Angiotensin II-induced VSMC senescence [9]. However, the regulation of zinc in VSMC ferroptosis and AD remains poorly understood. In addition, whether zinc-regulated or zinc-binding proteins are involved in the regulatory process of zinc is still unclear.

Metallothionein (MT) family comprises a class of metal-binding proteins known for their exceptional antioxidant, neuroprotective, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory properties [10]. MTs primarily bind metals through the thiol groups of cysteine residues, leading to the formation of clusters with a strong affinity for metal ions, such as copper and zinc [10]. Among this family, MT3 serves as a prominent zinc-binding protein, capable of binding up to seven zinc ions, and plays a crucial role in cellular processes and diseases by modulating oxidative stress [11]. In osteoblast differentiation, MT3 was specifically upregulated and reduces reactive oxygen species production in C2C12 myoblasts [12]. Notably, MT3 is also implicated in regulated cell death processes, including apoptosis and autophagy. In acute myeloid leukemia cells, MT3 inhibited proliferation and induced apoptosis via FOXO1, thereby exerting tumor-suppressive effects [13]. Our team recently determined that MT3 promotes autophagy and proliferation in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells via the Zinc-MTF1-ATG5 axis, suggesting a potential link to pulmonary hypertension [14]. Given its involvement in oxidative stress, regulated cell death, and vascular function, it is plausible that MT3 participates in the ferroptosis of VSMCs, potentially offering new insights for intervention in AD.

In this study, we first analyzed high-throughput sequencing data derived from aortic tissues to investigate the roles of zinc in AD and VSMC ferroptosis. We then focused on the zinc regulatory molecule, MT3, and assessed its role in VSMC ferroptosis. To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we examined the redox systems following MT3 treatment, identifying GPX4 as the most significantly altered molecule. Finally, we explored the interaction between MT3 and GPX4, and demonstrated that the modulation of GPX4 effectively reversed the regulatory effects of MT3 on HASMC ferroptosis. Collectively, our findings suggest that zinc dysregulation is a significant factor in AD, and MT3 emerges as a potential mechanistic candidate target for VSMCs and AD.

Methods

HASMC culture

Primary HASMCs were isolated from normal aortic tissues obtained from donors, as previously described [4]. This process was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committees of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. Briefly, the aortas were dissected under a microscope to remove the intima and adventitia. The aortic tissues were then minced and adhered to a culture flask for 30 min. Subsequently, DME/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, SH30084.03; Hyclone) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (15,140–122; ThermoFisher Scientific) was added. The tissue blocks were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2, allowing HASMCs to migrate out after one week. Cells from passages 3 to 7 were used for further experiments.

CCK8 and LDH assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, BS350A, Biosharp). HASMCs were seeded in 96-well plates (8000 cells per well) and treated with cystine deprivation (CD) or imidazole ketone erastin (IKE, 2.5 μM, Selleck, S8877) for 16 h, after which CCK-8 reagent was added to the culture medium. After incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate photometer to calculate cell viability. The concentration range of zinc was selected based on previous studies [15–17]. The ferroptosis-specific inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, 2.5 μM) was used to confirm the mode of cell death.

The lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay was performed using the LDH Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (CK12, Dojindo). After treatment, 100 μL of LDH reaction mixture was added to each well and incubated for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate photometer, and the percentage of cell death was calculated depending on the blank and high control according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot

HASMCs and aortic tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer to extract whole cell proteins. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA assay (23,227, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore). After blocking with non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Following this, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Specific immunoreactive protein bands were detected using ECL Western blot reagents with the ChemiDoc™ XRS + system (Bio-Rad). Protein levels were normalized to those of β-actin. The antibodies used for western blotting included MT3 (12,179–1-AP, Proteintech), solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11, NB300-318, Novus Biologicals), GPX4 (ab125066, Abcam), FSP1 (HPA042309, ATLAS, GCH1 (16,081-4A12, Sigma-Aldrich), Flag (F1084, Sigma), and β-Actin (AC026, ABclonal).

Real-time PCR

Total RNA from HASMCs was extracted using Trizol (A33251, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 5 μg of each RNA sample were reverse-transcribed to cDNA using a cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (11141ES60, Yeason). Quantitative reverse transcriptase-PCR was performed using a SYBR Green master mix (11201ES08, Yeason), and mRNA levels were normalized to those of 18S rRNA. The primer sequences were as follows: forward, 5’-CTCCTGCAAGAAGAGCTGCT-3’; reverse, 5’-TTCTCTGCTTCTGCCTCAGC-3’ for MT3; forward, 5’- CTCAACACGGGAAACCTCAC −3’; reverse, 5’- CGCTCCACCAACTAAGAACG −3’ for 18S rRNA.

Immunofluorescence staining

HASMCs were seeded on coverslips for immunofluorescence staining. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 10 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline. Cells were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h and then incubated with 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE, MAB3249, R&D Systems) overnight. After washing three times with PBS, cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies, followed by nuclear staining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI, BL105A, Biosharp). Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Lipid ROS assay

The BODIPY-C11 assay was performed using a Lipid Peroxidation Kit (D3861, Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, HASMCs were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated with C11-BODIPY dye (10 μM per plate) for 30 min after treatment. Cells were then collected and washed twice with PBS to remove excess dye. Fluorescence intensity was monitored by acquiring green signals (oxidized BODIPY, 510 nm) and red signals (non-oxidized BODIPY, 591 nm) using the flow cytometer. The fluorescence ratio of green to red signals indicated lipid ROS generation.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

The lipid peroxidation assay kit (S0131M, Beyotime) was utilized to measure the MDA levels according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Following treatment, HASMCs were collected and lysed using RIPA lysis buffer containing butylated hydroxytoluene. The samples were then centrifuged to remove insoluble material. Thiobarbituric acid solution was added to each sample to form the MDA-TBA adduct, which was incubated at 100 °C for 30 min. A volume of 200 μL of supernatant from each sample was pipetted into 96-well plates to measure absorbance at wavelengths of 532/450 nm. The concentration of MDA was determined using a standard curve and normalized to the protein concentration.

Assessment of zinc content

Aortic tissues from 10 non-AD and 20 AD patients were collected to analyze zinc content. For sample preparation, a microwave-assisted acid digestion was performed using an Ethos T microwave digestion system (Milestone, Italy). Approximately 100 mg of tissue was weighed and mixed with 4.0 mL of HNO₃ (65%) and 2.0 mL of H₂O₂ (30%) in a Teflon pressure vessel. The vessel was sealed and subjected to digestion at 180 °C with a power of 1.0 kW. After cooling, the digest was transferred to a Teflon beaker and gently heated to approximately 2.0 mL at 60 °C. Finally, the solution was diluted to 10.0 mL with Milli-Q water and analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry.

Plasmid constructs and lentivirus infection

The MT3 overexpression plasmid was constructed using a phage plasmid by inserting a PCR-amplified human MT3 coding sequence fragment. The knockdown plasmid targeting MT3 was developed based on pLKO.1 plasmid. Two shRNA sequences targeting MT3 were designed and cloned into the pLKO.1 lentiviral vector to minimize off-target effects. The corresponding shRNA sequences are as follows: CGCGGACTCCTGCAAGTGCGA for MT3-sh1, AGGGATGCAAATGCACCTCCT for MT3-sh2. A silencing plasmid for GPX4 (target sequence: GTGGATGAAGATCCAACCCAA) was described in the previously study.

Lentivirus was packaged using the psPAX2-pMD2.G system. Briefly, the overexpression or knockdown plasmids, along with the two packaging plasmids psPAX2 (12,260, Addgene) and pMD2.G (12,259, Addgene), were co-transfected into HEK293T cells using polyethylenimine to produce native and recombinant lentiviruses. After 8 h of transfection, the medium was changed to remove transfection reagents and replaced with fresh media. The media containing lentivirus was harvested from the cells after 24 and 48 h of incubation and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter to eliminate impurities. HASMCs were transduced with the lentivirus using polybrene for 24 h prior to subsequent experiments.

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw count matrices from three human single-cell RNA-seq datasets (GSE213740, GSE222318, GSE226492) were processed using the Seurat package. Cells with low quality (< 800 or > 6,000 detected features, or > 10% mitochondrial gene content) were excluded. Metadata were curated and harmonized across datasets. Gene expression data were log-normalized with a scale factor of 10,000, and 3,000 highly variable features were identified using the “vst” method. The scaled data were subjected to principal component analysis for dimensionality reduction.

Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony algorithm based on sample identity. The corrected embeddings were used for neighbor graph construction, clustering, and UMAP visualization. VSMC clusters were identified based on canonical marker genes (MYH11, ACTA2, TAGLN, CNN1) and exclusion of non-VSMC markers. Differential expression analysis between experimental and control groups in VSMCs was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with thresholds of min.pct = 0.2, log2 fold change > 0.25, and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value < 0.05.

Bulk RNA-seq count data (IKE vs. IKE + BRD4770, CD vs. CD + MT3-sh1) were analyzed using the DESeq2 package in R. Ensembl gene IDs were converted to gene symbols using the clusterProfiler function bitr, and duplicated genes were collapsed by averaging expression values. Lowly expressed genes (total counts ≤ 10) were filtered out. Differential expression analysis between the experimental group and the control group was conducted using a negative binomial generalized linear model. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and genes with adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change|> 1 were considered statistically significant.

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed using the clusterProfiler package. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses were conducted with adjusted p-value < 0.05 as the significance threshold.

For correlation analysis, pathway activity scores were quantified for each RNA-seq sample using gene set variation analysis (GSVA). Sample identifiers were matched with corresponding group annotations prior to analysis. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationship between selected pathway scores across samples within each group. Correlation coefficients were calculated using rank-based methods to account for non-normality, and group-specific correlation patterns were compared between the experimental and control groups.

Coimmunoprecipitation assay

HASMCs and aortic tissues were lysed in immunoprecipitation lysis buffer and sonicated to ensure complete lysis. After centrifugation, 100 μL of the supernatant was reserved as input, while the remaining lysate was incubated with the indicated primary antibodies (Flag/GPX4) or IgG at 4 °C overnight with rotary agitation. Subsequently, magnetic beads were added to the reaction mixture, and the samples were incubated for 3 h. The beads were then washed to remove unbound proteins and eluted using SDS loading buffer, followed by boiling at 95 °C for 30 min. The resulting immunoprecipitate or cell lysate samples were subsequently used for western blotting analysis.

ROS level assessment

Intracellular ROS and zinc levels were measured as previously described. Following treatment, HASMCs were incubated with DCFH-DA (10 μM, S0033S-1, Beyotime) to assess ROS in PBS for 30 min in the dark. After PBS washing, fluorescence signals were observed by the flow cytometer.

Glutathione (GSH) assay

The level of GSH was assessed using a commercial kit (Beyotime, S0052) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, HASMCs were collected and lysed to obtain supernatants. Glutathione reductase was then used to convert oxidized glutathione (GSSG) to reduced glutathione (GSH). Finally, GSH reagent was added to the supernatants, and total glutathione levels were measured by detecting absorbance at 412 nm.

Statistics

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group differences were analyzed using Student’s t-test for two groups or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multiple groups with SPSS version 23.0 (IBM). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Zinc homeostasis is impaired in the development of AD

To investigate the pathological alterations of VSMCs during AD formation, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing analysis using published AD-related datasets (GSE213740, GSE222318, GSE226492). Guided by canonical cell type-specific markers, we identified SMCs, myeloid cells, T cells, mesenchymal stem cells, fibroblasts, natural killer cells, Schwann cells, endothelial cells, B cells, mast cells, and plasmacytes (Fig. 1A and B). Based on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of VSMCs between normal and AD groups, KEGG pathway enrichment of the SMC cluster revealed a remarkable enrichment of the ferroptosis signaling pathway, with significant changes in GPX4, SLC3A2, FTH1, and FTL (Fig. 1C and D). GO analysis further revealed enrichment not only in iron metabolism and oxidative stress pathways but also in zinc-related pathways, suggesting a potential involvement of zinc ions in AD (Fig. 1E). Notably, the “Ferroptosis” pathway showed a significant correlation not only with iron metabolism and oxidative stress but also with zinc-related pathways (Fig. 1F). Consistently, zinc content was elevated in dissected aortic tissues compared with non-AD controls (Fig. 1G). Next, we next explored the effect of zinc on VSMC ferroptosis and found that, zinc reduced cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner in VSMCs upon CD or IKE treatment (Fig. 1H and I). Moreover, the zinc chelator TPEN attenuated zinc-enhanced CD- or IKE-induced ferroptosis in VSMCs, whereas ferroptosis-specific inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 completely reversed zinc-induced VSMC cell death under these conditions (Fig. 1J and K). Collectively, the dysfunction of zinc metabolism might contribute to VSMC ferroptosis and thereby aggravate AD progress.

In our previous studies, we demonstrated that BRD4770 protected against VSMC ferroptosis and delayed AD progression[4], with GO analysis revealing significant enrichment of oxidative stress-related pathways (Fig. 1L). Notably, the pathway “response to reactive oxygen species” was enriched in both AD and VSMC ferroptosis analyses. Within this pathway, MT3 emerged as the most significantly altered gene (Fig. 1M). Analysis of clinical samples further revealed that MT3 expression was increased in aortic tissues from patients with AD compared with those from normal controls (Fig. 1N). As an ion-regulating protein associated with zinc homeostasis and oxidative stress, MT3 may play a critical role in regulating VSMC ferroptosis and thereby contribute to AD development.

MT3 overexpression alleviated CD- or IKE-induced VSMC ferroptosis

To explore the role of MT3 in VSMC ferroptosis, we overexpressed MT3 in HASMCs via lentivirus infection (Fig. 2A). In CD- or IKE-induced ferroptosis, the overexpression of MT3 increased cell viability, in line with the observation under the microscope (Fig. 2B-D). Similarly, LDH assay indicated that MT3 mitigated cell death upon CD or IKE treatment (Fig. 2E and F). Moreover, the fluorescent intensity of C11-BODIPY, a probe that is used to monitor the cellular lipid peroxidation levels, was decreased in MT3 overexpressed HASMCs upon CD or IKE treatment (Fig. 2G and 2H). Similarly, the overexpression of MT3 decreased the content of lipid peroxide 4-HNE and MDA in HASMC ferroptosis (Fig. 2I-K). Collectively, these results indicated that MT3 overexpression protected against VSMC ferroptosis and lipid peroxidation.

Knockdown of MT3 expression exacerbated lipid peroxidation and VSMC ferroptosis

To further confirm these findings, we constructed two MT3 knockdown plasmids for subsequent experiments to avoid off-target effects (Fig. 3A). As expected, MT3 knockdown decreased cell viability of HASMCs upon CD or IKE treatment (Fig. 3B-D). Similarly, MT3 knockdown increased proportion of cell death measured by LDH assay (Fig. 3E and F). Compared to PLKO.1 group, MT3 knockdown groups markedly increased oxidized C11-BODIPY and aggravated the accumulation lipid peroxide MDA and 4-HNE (Fig. 3G−K). Notably, the enhanced cell death and lipid peroxidation caused by MT3 knockdown following CD or IKE treatment were markedly attenuated by Ferrostatin-1 (Fig. 3B, C, G and H), indicating that the effect of MT3 on cell death is ferroptosis-specific. Taken together, MT3 knockdown showed a promotion effect on VSMC ferroptosis and lipid peroxidation.

MT3 inhibited oxidative stress via activating GSH synthesis

To investigate the underlying mechanisms of MT3 in ferroptosis regulation, we conducted transcriptomic analysis between PLKO.1 and MT3-sh1 groups following CD treatment. Compared with controls, 470 upregulated and 440 downregulated DEGs were identified in the MT3-sh1 group (Fig. 4A). Biological process analysis revealed significant enrichment of oxidative stress-related pathways (Fig. 4B), further supporting the role of MT3 in regulating redox homeostasis. Consistently, ROS levels measured by DCFH-DA were decreased in MT3 overexpressing HASMCs upon CD or IKE treatment (Fig. 4C and 4D). Next, the core molecules in common ferroptosis-related pathways (GSH-GPX4, FSP1-CoQ10, and GCH1-BH4 pathways) were detected. Notably, GPX4 expression was upregulated following MT3 overexpression but downregulated upon MT3 knockdown under both CD- and IKE-induced ferroptosis (Fig. 4E-H). The GSH-GPX4 pathway is the earliest identified anti-ferroptotic mechanism, responsible for cystine uptake and GSH synthesis and the detoxification of lipid hydroperoxides. Thus, we further found that MT3 overexpression restored GSH depletion during ferroptosis, whereas MT3 silencing exacerbated GSH loss (Fig. 4J-M). Collectively, these findings indicate that MT3 regulates GSH metabolism to prevent VSMC ferroptosis through modulation of GPX4.

MT3 inhibited lysosomal degradation of GPX4 to exert antiferroptotic effects

To further investigate the regulatory effect of MT3 on GPX4, we examined GPX4 mRNA expression and found no significant difference between MT3-overexpressing cells and controls (Fig. 5A). Thus, a direct interaction between MT3 and GPX4 is suggested, as demonstrated by co-immunoprecipitation in VSMCs and aortic tissues (Fig. 5B and C). Furthermore, chloroquine, the autophagy-lysosome pathway inhibitor, but not the ubiquitin–proteasome inhibitor MG132, reversed GPX4 downregulation induced by MT3 silencing, indicating that MT3 inhibited GPX4 degradation via the autophagy-lysosome pathway (Fig. 5D and E). Functionally, only GPX4 knockdown significantly reduced the protective effect of MT3 overexpression on VSMC viability during ferroptosis (Fig. 5F and G), whereas GPX4 overexpression rescued MT3-silenced cells from ferroptotic death (Fig. 6A-D). Consistently, GPX4 overexpression abolished MT3 knockdown-induced lipid peroxidation, assessed by the C11-BODIPY and 4-HNE assays (Fig. 6E and F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MT3 modulates GSH metabolism by directly interacting with GPX4, thereby reducing lipid peroxidation and VSMC ferroptosis.

Discussion

VSMC ferroptosis has been recognized as a critical pathogenic mechanism in AD. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that zinc homeostasis plays an important role in AD, with elevated zinc levels observed in dissected aortas compared with normal tissues. Moreover, zinc-associated molecule MT3, was found to attenuate CD- or IKE-induced lipid peroxidation and VSMC ferroptosis. Furthermore, MT3 directly bound GPX4, and chloroquine rescued GPX4 loss upon MT3 silencing. Finally, modulation of GPX4 reversed the regulatory effect of MT3 on VSMC ferroptosis. Together, these findings demonstrate that MT3 interacts with GPX4 to prevent its degradation, thereby activating the GSH-GPX4 pathway and protecting against VSMC ferroptosis (Fig. 7).

Zinc is one indispensable trace element to maintain homeostasis and its abnormity results in the abnormity of aorta. In this study, the bioinformatics analysis revealed that zinc homeostasis was impaired during AD formation, and the related pathways were significant changed. Moreover, the aorta from AD patients showed an increased zinc content, compared that in normal. In BAPN-induced mouse AD model, a low zinc diet reduced the infiltration of macrophages, inflammatory cytokines release, and phenotype switch of VSMCs, and eventually improved AD and rupture [7]. However, a previous study reported decreased zinc levels in both serum and tissue samples from patients with AD compared with controls [5]. The discrepancy with our findings may be attributable to differences in disease stage, different pathogenic mechanisms or in population selection. Nonetheless, the disruption of zinc homeostasis might play an important role in AD pathogenesis, as both excessive and insufficient zinc levels can accelerate disease progression. Notably, in CD- or IKE-induced VSMC ferroptosis, zinc aggravated cell death, showing a promotion on lipid oxidation. In the previous study, the regulation of zinc on oxidative stress and ferroptosis is well reported. Zinc deficiency has been shown to enhance glycolysis and activate lactate-mediated SREBP1/SCD1 signaling, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [17]. However, a few studies showed the opposite effect of zinc on ferroptosis. In contusion spinal cord injury, zinc improved the accumulation of oxidation products and inhibited spinal cord ferroptosis by activating NRF2/GPX4 pathway, eventually reducing cavities and tissue edema [18]. Similarly, zinc protected against ferroptosis and apoptosis via suppressing MAPK signaling pathway, and thereby improved BPA-induced oxidative stress and male reproductive damage [19]. Collectively, the regulation of ferroptosis by zinc is complex and may depend on zinc concentration, cell types, and diseases. Our results indicated that zinc promoted VSMC ferroptosis, which might be one of the mechanisms underlying AD formation.

As the member of MT family, MT3 maintains the homeostasis of copper and zinc in cells, and is best-known function as the control redox reaction. In osteoclastogenesis, MT3 loss increased the content of intracellular zinc, and further decreased mitochondrial ROS production to regulate osteoclast differentiation and survival [20]. Notably, the non-ionic regulatory role of MT3 in redox homeostasis has been increasingly recognized. In this study, we found that MT3 expression was elevated in aortic tissues of patients with AD, compared with normal controls. Moreover, MT3 alleviated CD- or IKE-induced lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in VSMCs, further confirming its antioxidant properties. RNA-sequencing revealed that redox system pathways was significantly changed upon MT3 knockdown in CD-induced ferroptosis. Notably, MT3 directly interacted with GPX4 to avoid its lysosomal degradation, and further alleviated VSMC ferroptosis by maintaining the GSH/GSSG balance. Notably, other studies have also demonstrated MT3’s non-ionic regulatory properties. MT3 directly suppressed the transcriptional activity of PPARγ, resulting in a decrease in ROS levels during early adipocyte differentiation [21]. Additionally, MT3 suppressed HIF1α activation, and in turn promoted the HIF2α transcriptional program, M(IL-4) polarization and oxidative metabolism [22]. Collectively, the interaction between MT3 and GPX4 further expanded the regulatory mechanisms of MT3 on oxidative stress via non-ionic regulatory functions, highlighting its role beyond just zinc homeostasis.

GPX4 is still considered as the core molecule in ferroptosis, and functions as a GSH dependent enzyme that reduces lipid hydroperoxides to lipid alcohols [23–25]. Our previous studies confirmed that the expression of GPX4 were decreased in both dissected aortae and VSMC ferroptosis, compared to these in controls [4]. As expected, the overexpression of GPX4 alleviated CD- or IKE-induced lipid peroxidation and VSMC ferroptosis [4]. As expected, the protective roles of GPX4 in aortic diseases have been widely reported. Upon nicotine stimulation, elevated expression of miR-1909-5p augmented oxidative damage and endothelial cells ferroptosis via inhibiting GPX4 expression [26]. Additionally, the administration of antagomir targeting miR-1909-5p alleviated ferroptosis in the aortic endothelium via GPX4, and conferred a protective effect against tobacco-related AD in vivo [26]. Similarly, the overexpression of GPX4 prevented JAK1/STAT3 activation by IL-6 and protected against VSMC ferroptosis [27]. Moreover, GPX4 knock-in reduced M1 macrophages infiltration and IL-6 expression, and thereby prevented abdominal aortic expansion induced by Ang II in mice [27]. In this study, the knockdown of GPX4 reversed the protective effects of MT3 on lipid peroxidation and VSMC ferroptosis, which verified the protective role of GPX4 in ferroptosis again. Nevertheless, targeting GPX4 with small molecule compounds to delay the formation of AD still requires further evidences and clinical exploration.

The regulation of GPX4 expression remains a central focus in ferroptosis research and has been increasingly explored for therapeutic applications. Our previous studies demonstrated that the histone methyltransferase inhibitor BRD4770 enhanced GPX4 transcription through H3K9 modification, thereby protecting against VSMC ferroptosis and delaying the progression of AD [4]. In the present study, we found that MT3 directly interacted with GPX4 and prevented its lysosomal degradation, thereby reducing cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis. Notably, lysosomal degradation has emerged as another important mechanism governing GPX4 regulation. For instance, the E3 ligase TRAF6 bound to and ubiquitinated GPX4, promoting its lysosomal degradation and exerting anti-cancer effects in breast cancer by facilitating ferroptosis [28]. Conversely, the deubiquitinase OTUD4 inhibited GPX4 lysosomal degradation via RHEB, thereby suppressing ferroptosis and contributing to tumor progression [29]. Collectively, the inhibition of MT3 on lysosomal degradation of GPX4 might be a potential mechanistic candidate target for ferroptosis modulation; however, the development of specific pharmacological agents remains an urgent unmet need.

Indeed, this study has certain limitations. First, the donated normal aortic tissues lacked corresponding baseline information and therefore could not be strictly matched to AD patients in terms of age, comorbidities, or sampling site. Thus, a more strictly matched control cohort and the inclusion of essential baseline clinical characteristics for both groups are warranted to test the robustness of the observed zinc differences. Moreover, it primarily focused on in vitro research, elucidating the roles of zinc and MT3 in VSMC ferroptosis. However, in vivo studies, such as MT3 gain- or loss-of-function experiments or pharmacological modulation, are essential for further validation. Additionally, the study provided a detailed explanation of the mechanism by which MT3 regulated VSMC ferroptosis through GPX4; however, further clarification is needed regarding the underlying mechanism of zinc’s effect on VSMC ferroptosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study revealed that zinc homeostasis is linked to the formation of AD and plays a significant role in VSMC ferroptosis. Additionally, the zinc-binding protein MT3 was found to directly interact with GPX4 to prevent its lysosomal degradation, which ultimately activated GSH-GPX4 signaling pathway and protected against lipid peroxidation and VSMC ferroptosis. Our findings enhance the understanding of zinc homeostasis in relation to ferroptosis, offering novel insights for potential interventions in AD.
