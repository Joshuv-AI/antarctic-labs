Bedside Bioimpedance and Simple Functional Tests Identify Muscle Function Impairment
Predicting Overall Survival in Chemotherapy-treated Metastatic Urothelial Carcinoma:
A Retrospective Pilot Study

Objectives: To evaluate whether bedside bioimpedance analysis and simple functional tests can identify muscle function impairment associated with overall survival in chemotherapy-treated metastatic urothelial carcinoma.
Materials: Retrospective single-center observational cohort study.
Methods: We reviewed 56 consecutive patients treated with systemic chemotherapy between January 2014 and February 2021.
Skeletal muscle index was estimated using bioimpedance analysis. Handgrip strength and walking speed were assessed in
routine care. Sarcopenia-related categories were defined using sex-specific Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia thresholds.
The prespecified primary comparison was reference (normal plus pre-sarcopenia) versus muscle function impairment (dynapenia
plus sarcopenia). Overall survival was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox models with minimal age adjustment.
Results: Muscle function impairment was associated with shorter overall survival compared with the reference group. The
association remained after age adjustment. Baseline characteristics suggested greater vulnerability in the impairment group,
including lower body weight and serum albumin and higher inflammatory burden.
Conclusions: A pragmatic bedside phenotype based on bioimpedance analysis and simple functional testing may help identify
chemotherapy-treated metastatic urothelial carcinoma patients at higher risk of poor survival.
Key words: bioimpedance analysis, chemotherapy, dynapenia, metastatic urothelial carcinoma, sarcopenia

Introduction
Metastatic urothelial carcinoma (mUC) remains
a lethal malignancy despite advances in systemic
therapy. Treatment decisions in mUC are frequently
influenced by patient fitness, comorbidity, and toler-

ance to chemotherapy. However, routine clinical
assessments may not capture the multidimensional
vulnerability often described as frailty.
Sarcopenia refers to a loss of skeletal muscle mass
and function and has been linked to adverse outcomes
in multiple cancers1, 2). Importantly, contemporary

consensus frameworks emphasize that muscle function, including strength and physical performance,
is not merely a correlate of muscle mass but a
distinct and clinically meaningful domain3, 4).
Dynapenia, defined as reduced muscle strength
with preserved muscle quantity, may represent an
earlier or different pathway of functional decline5, 6).
In geriatric medicine, strength-based phenotypes
predict disability and mortality more robustly than
muscle mass alone7, 8).
In mUC, several studies have associated CTderived measures of skeletal muscle, such as psoas
area or the L3 cross-sectional muscle index, with
survival and treatment toxicity9, 10). While CT is
widely available, muscle segmentation requires
dedicated time and software and is not uniformly
incorporated into routine practice. Bioimpedance
analysis (BIA) offers a noninvasive and rapid estimate of body composition and can be performed at
the bedside or outpatient clinic11, 12). Additionally,
the reliability of InBody devices has been reported
in comparison with reference modalities such as
DXA13). When combined with simple functional
measures such as grip strength, BIA-based assessment could provide a feasible screening approach
to identify vulnerable patients3, 4).
We therefore conducted a retrospective pilot
study to evaluate whether sarcopenia-related categories assessed by BIA and grip strength are associated with overall survival in mUC patients treated
with chemotherapy. We hypothesized that categories reflecting impaired muscle function (dynapenia and sarcopenia) would demonstrate poorer
survival compared with patients with preserved
function.
Materials and Methods
Study design and setting
This retrospective observational study included
consecutive patients with metastatic urothelial
carcinoma treated with systemic chemotherapy at
Juntendo University Hospital between January
2014 and February 2021. The study was approved
by the Institutional Review Board of Juntendo
University Hospital (approval number: 15-005)
and conducted in accordance with institutional
ethical standards for clinical research involving
human participants. Written informed consent was
obtained from all participants after providing an

2

oral explanation of the study.
Participants
Eligible patients had pathologically confirmed
urothelial carcinoma with metastatic or unresectable disease and underwent baseline body composition assessment prior to or at initiation of systemic
chemotherapy. Patients without the baseline BIA
assessment or without survival follow-up data were
excluded from survival analyses.
Body composition assessment
Body composition was evaluated using a multifrequency segmental bioimpedance analyzer (InBody
770)11-13). Parameters extracted included body
weight, body mass index (BMI), total muscle mass,
and segmental muscle mass. Skeletal muscle index
(SMI) and sarcopenia-related classification were
determined from the device outputs using sex-specific thresholds aligned with the Asian Working
Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) consensus approach3).
Muscle function assessment
Handgrip strength was assessed as part of routine
clinical evaluation and classified as low or normal
using sex-specific cutoffs consistent with AWGS
recommendations3). Walking speed was also assessed
for all patients at baseline as part of routine functional evaluation. We used grip strength, walking
speed, and SMI to classify patients into the four
sarcopenia-related categories as defined in Table S1.
Definitions of sarcopenia-related categories
Dynapenia is defined as reduced muscle strength
that is not explained by low muscle quantity or
mass5, 6). Sarcopenia-related categories were defined
using sex-specific thresholds for grip strength,
walking speed, and skeletal muscle index (SMI), as
summarized in Table S1. Patients were categorized
as normal, pre-sarcopenia, dynapenia, or sarcopenia, corresponding to preserved function with
normal SMI, low SMI with preserved function, low
function without low SMI, and low SMI with low
function, respectively. For inferential analyses, the
primary comparison was prespecified as reference
(normal+pre-sarcopenia) vs muscle function impairment (dynapenia+sarcopenia) to avoid unstable
inference from very small subgroups.

Outcome and follow-up
Baseline assessment was defined as the date of
the body composition and functional evaluation
performed immediately before or at initiation of
first-line systemic chemotherapy. Overall survival
(OS) was calculated from the date of baseline
assessment to death from any cause. Patients who
were alive at last follow-up were censored on that
date.
Statistical analysis
Continuous variables are summarized as median
(interquartile range [IQR]) and compared with
the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are
summarized as counts (percentages) and compared
with Fisher’s exact test. OS was estimated using
the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with logrank testing. Cox proportional hazards regression
was used for univariate analysis and for an ageadjusted model as a minimal prespecified adjustment. Given the limited number of events and the
small sizes of dynapenia and sarcopenia groups,
extensive multivariable modeling was avoided to
reduce overfitting; exploratory models separating
dynapenia and sarcopenia are presented as sensitivity analyses. Statistical analyses were performed
using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San
Diego, CA, USA) and R, and cross-checked in
Python. This study was designed as a hypothesisgenerating pilot study; therefore, no a priori sample
size calculation was performed and the statistical
power was limited.
Results
Study cohort and classification
A total of 56 patients with metastatic urothelial
carcinoma who underwent baseline body composition assessment were included in the survival analysis. Patients were categorized into four sarcopeniarelated groups based on skeletal muscle index and
handgrip strength: normal (n = 30), pre-sarcopenia
(n = 19), dynapenia (n = 4), and sarcopenia (n = 3)
(Figure 1). For inferential analyses, we prespecified a binary grouping to improve statistical stability:
reference (normal + pre-sarcopenia) versus muscle
function impairment (dynapenia + sarcopenia).

Baseline characteristics
Baseline characteristics stratified by the prespecified binary grouping are summarized in Table 1.
Overall, patients in the muscle function impairment
group tended to be older than those in the reference
group. As expected from the classification criteria,
grip strength and several measures of body composition differed across groups. In this cohort, the
impairment group showed lower body weight and
serum albumin and tended to have higher C-reactive protein, suggesting greater vulnerability. We
also report laboratory indices related to systemic
inflammation and nutritional status, including
complete blood counts, serum albumin, and inflammatory markers when available, to provide clinical
context for disease burden and host condition.
Collectively, these findings suggest that impaired
muscle function co-occurred with features consistent with frailty-related vulnerability in this cohort.
In addition, baseline body composition and selected
laboratory indices stratified by the four sarcopeniarelated categories are shown in Figure S1 for descriptive visualization of between-group distributions.
Overall survival
During follow-up, overall survival differed between
the prespecified binary groups. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated significantly shorter OS in the
muscle function impairment group compared with
the reference group. Median OS was 80.6 months in
the reference group versus 21.0 months in the
impairment group (p = 0.0165) (Figure 2). The separation of survival curves was most pronounced

Discussion
This retrospective pilot study suggests that
sarcopenia-related phenotypes characterized by
impaired muscle function are associated with
shorter OS among chemotherapy-treated patients
with metastatic urothelial carcinoma. The association was evident using a pragmatic binary grouping
that combined dynapenia and sarcopenia and
remained significant after minimal adjustment for
age. Given the small sample size and the exploratory nature of the analyses, these findings should
be interpreted as hypothesis-generating; however,
the direction and magnitude of effect were consistent across models.
Prior work in urothelial carcinoma has mainly
evaluated CT-derived measures of skeletal muscle
quantity, such as psoas area or L3 muscle index,
and has linked low muscle mass to survival and
treatment-related toxicity9, 10). Our results complement this literature by focusing on the functional
component of sarcopenia. Contemporary consensus
frameworks emphasize that muscle strength and
physical performance are clinically meaningful
domains that do not always parallel muscle quan-

6

tity3, 4). In this context, grip strength may capture
multidimensional vulnerability that reflects frailty
and limited physiologic reserve, and may therefore
provide prognostic information beyond body composition alone3, 4). The concept of dynapenia further
supports this distinction, highlighting reduced muscle
strength as a clinically relevant phenotype that
may occur even when muscle quantity is relatively
preserved5, 6).
From a clinical perspective, the approach tested
here is feasible for routine practice. BIA can be
obtained rapidly without radiologic segmentation,
is noninvasive, and can be repeated over time,
making it suitable for outpatient or bedside assessment11, 12). InBody-based estimates have also been
reported to show acceptable agreement with reference methods such as DXA in validation studies13).
If validated in larger cohorts, incorporating function-based screening into baseline evaluation could
help identify patients who may benefit from proactive supportive care, such as nutrition counseling,
resistance exercise programs, rehabilitation interventions, and closer monitoring during chemotherapy3, 4).
Compared with CT-based sarcopenia assessment,
which typically requires muscle segmentation with
dedicated software and trained personnel time, the
bedside approach used here can be completed in
routine care without additional imaging or post-processing11, 14). This pragmatic difference may facilitate broader implementation and repeated assessment if measurement procedures are standardized
across institutions.
Several mechanisms may underlie the observed
association between muscle function impairment
and worse survival. Reduced strength can reflect
systemic inflammation, malnutrition, cancer cachexia,
and decreased physical activity, all of which can
diminish physiologic reserve and impair the ability
to tolerate cytotoxic therapy8). Laboratory measures
presented in Table 1 provide additional context for
host condition and systemic disease burden, although
the retrospective design does not allow causal inference and residual confounding is likely.
This study has strengths that support its clinical
relevance. We evaluated a pragmatic assessment
strategy using routinely obtainable measures in a
real-world cohort and prespecified a binary comparison to improve interpretability and reduce insta-

Juntendo Medical Journal, 2026

bility from very small subgroups. We also presented
exploratory analyses separating dynapenia and
sarcopenia to improve transparency, while acknowledging that these estimates are imprecise due to
limited sample size.
Several limitations should be noted. This was a
retrospective single-center study with a small
number of patients in the dynapenia and sarcopenia
categories, limiting statistical power and restricting
multivariable adjustment. Because the dynapenia
and sarcopenia subgroups were very small, this
work should be interpreted strictly as hypothesisgenerating, and its statistical power to detect
subgroup effects is limited. An additional potential
confounder is the imbalance in primary tumor site
between groups. Upper urinary tract urothelial
carcinoma and bladder cancer can differ in clinical
behavior and prognosis, and the higher proportion
of upper urinary tract tumors in the impairment
group may have contributed to the observed survival
difference15). Given the limited number of events,
we avoided extensive multivariable adjustment;
therefore, residual confounding by primary site
cannot be excluded. Cisplatin eligibility itself may
confound the association between muscle function
and survival, because cisplatin ineligibility is
commonly driven by impaired renal function, poorer
performance status, and comorbidities. In our cohort,
carboplatin-based therapy was more frequent in
the impairment group, which may reflect underlying
vulnerability rather than treatment effect alone.
BIA-derived estimates may be influenced by
hydration status and measurement conditions, underscoring the need for standardized protocols12).
Although walking speed was assessed in all patients,
measurement conditions in routine practice may
vary, which may affect classification and supports
the need for standardized assessment procedures
across institutions4). In addition, detailed information on chemotherapy regimens, dose intensity, and
treatment-related toxicity was not incorporated
into the present analysis and should be evaluated
in future studies.
Future research should validate these findings in
prospective multicenter cohorts, assess whether
BIA plus grip strength improves prognostic stratification beyond established clinical factors, and
determine whether longitudinal changes during
treatment add predictive value3, 4). Interventional

studies that test supportive strategies guided by
muscle function screening may also be warranted.
Conclusions
Muscle function impairment identified by BIA
and simple bedside tests was associated with poorer
survival in chemotherapy-treated metastatic urothelial carcinoma. These findings are hypothesis-generating and support further evaluation of feasible
frailty screening strategies in larger cohorts.
