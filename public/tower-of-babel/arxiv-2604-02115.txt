Complete Resolution of the Butler–Costello–Graham Conjecture on Monochromatic Constellations
Gang Yang, Yaping Mao

Abstract
A constellation pattern is a finite increasing rational sequence Q = [ 0 = q 0 < q 1 < ⋯ < q k = 1 ] Q=[0=q_{0}<q_{1}<\cdots<q_{k}=1] , and a Q Q -constellation in [ n ] [n] is obtained by scaling and translating a rational pattern Q Q , with key examples including arithmetic progressions. In 2010, Butler, Costello, and Graham proposed a conjecture, that is, for any constellation pattern Q Q there is a coloring pattern of [ n ] [n] that has γ ​ n 2 + o ⁡ ( n 2 ) \gamma n^{2}+o\left(n^{2}\right) monochromatic constellations, where γ \gamma is smaller than the coefficient for a random coloring. In this paper, we confirm this conjecture. As applications of this conjecture, we obtain interval-uncommon translation-invariant linear systems associated with rational constellations and a ground-state bound for deterministic arithmetic hypergraph spin systems. Keywords: Ramsey theory; Constellation; Multiplicity; Fourier perturbation; Integer coloring AMS subject classification 2020: 05D10; 11B75, 05A16

Enumerative combinatorics studies quantitative questions about finite

These examples illustrate a general theme: qualitative structural questions
often lead naturally to quantitative counting problems.

Ramsey theory is another major source of such quantitative problems. Its

contain monochromatic substructures of a prescribed type

,
20

. In
the integer setting, this philosophy includes monochromatic arithmetic

The passage from existence to counting leads to

In graph Ramsey theory this viewpoint led to the notion of common and uncommon

Ramsey multiplicity has found applications in a wide range of additive settings, including integer intervals

Butler, Costello, and Graham
[
6
]
introduced the notion of a
constellation
to unify many rational affine patterns. Here and throughout,
\mathbb{Q}
,
\mathbb{R}
, and

\mathbb{Z}
denote the sets of rational, real, and integer numbers, respectively. A

constellation pattern
is a strictly increasing rational sequence

Q=[q_{0}=0<q_{1}<\cdots<q_{k}=1],\qquad q_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}.

A
Q
-constellation in
[n]
is a homothetic copy of this pattern
whose points are integers:

\{s+q_{0}d,s+q_{1}d,\dots,s+q_{k}d\}\subseteq[n],\qquad s,d\in\mathbb{Z},\quad d\neq 0.

We call
s
and
s+d
the
endpoints
of the
Q
-constellation in

[n]
. Arithmetic progressions correspond to the choice
q_{i}=i/k
. We
say that
Q
is
symmetric
if
q_{i}+q_{k-i}=1
for all

0\leq i\leq k
, and
nonsymmetric
otherwise.

Given a two-coloring
\chi:[n]\to\{\pm 1\}
, let
M_{Q}(\chi)
denote the
number of monochromatic
Q
-constellations in
[n]
. Let
\chi_{\mathrm{rand}}

be the uniformly random two-coloring of
[n]
, in which the colors of the
points
1,\dots,n
are independent and uniformly distributed on

\{\pm 1\}
. A fixed
Q
-constellation in
[n]
has
k+1
distinct
points, and hence is monochromatic under
\chi_{\mathrm{rand}}
with probability

2\cdot 2^{-(k+1)}=2^{-k}
. Writing
D
for the least common denominator
of
q_{0},\dots,q_{k}
, the total number of
Q
-constellations in
[n]
is

n^{2}/D+O_{Q}(n)
in the nonsymmetric case and
n^{2}/(2D)+O_{Q}(n)
in the
symmetric case, as justified in Subsection
2.2
. Therefore,
\E\!\left[M_{Q}(\chi_{\mathrm{rand}})\right]=\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)n^{2}+O_{Q}(n)
,
where

\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2^{k}D},&Q\text{ is nonsymmetric},\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{2^{k+1}D},&Q\text{ is symmetric}.\end{cases}

Here and throughout,
\E
denotes expectation. We call
\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)
the random-coloring coefficient.

For three-point patterns
Q=[0,q,1]
, Butler, Costello, and Graham proved
that the random-coloring coefficient can always be improved
[
6
]
. They
left open the corresponding problem for constellation patterns with four or
more points. The two-point pattern is the only trivial exception. Indeed, if

Q=[0,1]
and one color class has size
r
, then the number of
monochromatic
Q
-constellations is

\binom{r}{2}+\binom{n-r}{2}=\frac{r^{2}+(n-r)^{2}-n}{2}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{4}-\frac{n}{2}.

Thus the random-coloring coefficient
1/4
is already optimal for

Q=[0,1]
, and no strict improvement is possible.

It is therefore natural to formulate the Butler–Costello–Graham problem as
follows.

Conjecture 1

(Butler–Costello–Graham problem,
[
6
]
)
.

For every rational constellation pattern

Q=[q_{0}=0<q_{1}<\cdots<q_{k}=1]

with
k\geq 2
, there exists a sequence of two-colorings

\chi_{n}:[n]\to\{\pm 1\}
such that

M_{Q}(\chi_{n})\leq\gamma n^{2}+o(n^{2})

for some constant
\gamma<\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)
.

Our main theorem confirms Conjecture
1
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
Q=[q_{0}=0<q_{1}<\cdots<q_{k}=1]
with
q_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
and
k\geq 2
. Then
there exist a constant
\delta_{Q}>0
and a sequence of two-colorings

\chi_{n}:[n]\to\{\pm 1\}
such that

M_{Q}(\chi_{n})\leq\bigl(\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)-\delta_{Q}\bigr)n^{2}+O_{Q}(n),

where

\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2^{k}D},&Q\text{ is nonsymmetric},\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{2^{k+1}D},&Q\text{ is symmetric},\end{cases}

and
D
is the least common denominator of
q_{0},\dots,q_{k}
.

Our proof follows a unified strategy rather than relying on isolated
constructions tailored to particular patterns. We first encode monochromatic
counts by a continuous functional
\Phi_{Q}(b)
associated with a bias function

b:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
. The key discrete-to-continuous step is a uniform
Riemann-sum estimate on each residue class modulo
D
, valid for every
Lipschitz function and every residue class, with a uniform
O(1)
remainder.
After transferring the discrete counting problem to the continuous functional,
we expand
\Phi_{Q}(\lambda g)
around the random point
b\equiv 0
. The
first nontrivial term is a quadratic form
T_{2}(g)
. We then show that the
Fourier mode
u(x)=\cos(2\pi Dx)
is a zero direction for
T_{2}
, while
its mixed bilinear term with
v(x)=\cos(2\pi(D+1)x)
is strictly positive.
Hence a small perturbation of
u
in the direction
-v
yields

T_{2}(g)<0
, and for sufficiently small
\lambda>0
gives

\Phi_{Q}(\lambda g)<2^{-k}
. A final application of the method of conditional
expectations converts the resulting biased random construction into a
deterministic coloring.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
develops
the endpoint model and the basic counting lemmas. Section
3

introduces the continuous functional, proves the discrete-to-continuous
transfer, and derives the second-order expansion at the random point.
Section
4
constructs an explicit negative direction
for the quadratic form. Section
5
converts the
biased random construction into deterministic colorings and completes the proof
of Theorem
1.1
. Section
6
presents
applications of the main result.

2
Preliminaries

Throughout this section, fix a rational pattern

Q=[q_{0}=0<q_{1}<\cdots<q_{k}=1]
, where
q_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
, and assume
k\geq 2
.
Recall that
D
is the least common denominator of
q_{0},\dots,q_{k}
, and
write
a_{i}=Dq_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq k
. Then

0=a_{0}<a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}=D
. By the minimality of
D
, we have

\gcd(a_{1},\dots,a_{k-1},D)=1
.

The next proposition shows that every
Q
-constellation in
[n]
can be
parametrized by its two endpoints
p,q
, as in (ii) below; this
parametrization will be used throughout.

Proposition 2.1
.

The following two descriptions of a
Q
-constellation in
[n]
are
equivalent:

(i)

a set of the form

\{s+q_{0}d,s+q_{1}d,\dots,s+q_{k}d\}\subseteq[n]
, where

s,d\in\mathbb{Z}
and
d\neq 0
;

(ii)

a set of the form

\left\{x_{i}(p,q)=\frac{(D-a_{i})p+a_{i}q}{D}:0\leq i\leq k\right\}\subseteq[n],

(1)

where
p,q\in[n]
,
p\neq q
, and
p\equiv q\pmod{D}
.

Proof.

Suppose first that

X=\{s+q_{0}d,s+q_{1}d,\dots,s+q_{k}d\}\subseteq[n]
, where

s,d\in\mathbb{Z}
and
d\neq 0
. Set
p=s
and
q=s+d
. Since
q_{0}=0

and
q_{k}=1
, we have
p,q\in[n]
, and
p\neq q
. It remains to show
that
p\equiv q\pmod{D}
, equivalently
D\mid d
. For every
i
, the
point
s+q_{i}d=s+a_{i}d/D
is an integer, and hence
D\mid a_{i}d
. Since

D
is the least common denominator of
q_{0},\dots,q_{k}
, we have

\gcd(a_{1},\dots,a_{k-1},D)=1
. Therefore there exist integers

c_{1},\dots,c_{k-1},m
such that

c_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+c_{k-1}a_{k-1}+mD=1
.
Multiplying by
d
, we obtain

d=c_{1}a_{1}d+\cdots+c_{k-1}a_{k-1}d+mDd,

and every term on the right-hand side is divisible by
D
. Thus

D\mid d
, so
p\equiv q\pmod{D}
. Moreover,

x_{i}(p,q)=\frac{(D-a_{i})s+a_{i}(s+d)}{D}=s+\frac{a_{i}}{D}d=s+q_{i}d

for every
i
. Hence
X
has the form in (ii).

Conversely, suppose that
p,q\in[n]
,
p\neq q
, and

p\equiv q\pmod{D}
. Put
s=p
and
d=q-p
. Then
s,d\in\mathbb{Z}
,

d\neq 0
, and
D\mid d
. For every
i
,

x_{i}(p,q)=\frac{(D-a_{i})p+a_{i}q}{D}=p+\frac{a_{i}}{D}(q-p)=s+q_{i}d.

Thus the set in (
1
) has the form in (i). Since

x_{i}(p,q)=(1-a_{i}/D)p+(a_{i}/D)q
is a convex combination of
p
and
q
,
and since
p,q\in[n]
, all its points lie in
[n]
. This proves the
equivalence.
∎

2.1
Ordered constellations

An
ordered
Q
-constellation in
[n]
is an ordered tuple

(x_{0}(p,q),x_{1}(p,q),\dots,x_{k}(p,q))
, where
x_{i}(p,q)
is defined by
(
1
),
p,q\in[n]
,
p\neq q
, and
p\equiv q\pmod{D}
. Its
underlying set is the corresponding
Q
-constellation in
[n]
. For a
coloring
\chi:[n]\to\{\pm 1\}
, let
M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(\chi)
denote the number of
monochromatic ordered
Q
-constellations in
[n]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Every ordered
Q
-constellation in
[n]
consists of
k+1
distinct
integers.

Proof.

Consider the affine map
\phi_{p,q}(t)=p+t(q-p)
. Since
p\neq q
, this
map is injective. Moreover,
x_{i}(p,q)=\phi_{p,q}(a_{i}/D)
for every
i
.
Since
a_{0},\dots,a_{k}
are distinct, the points

x_{0}(p,q),\dots,x_{k}(p,q)
are distinct.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
p\neq q
,
p^{\prime}\neq q^{\prime}
, and

\{x_{i}(p,q):0\leq i\leq k\}=\{x_{i}(p^{\prime},q^{\prime}):0\leq i\leq k\}.

Then
\{p,q\}=\{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\}
. Moreover:

(i)

if
(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})=(p,q)
, then the two pairs determine the same ordered

Q
-constellation in
[n]
;

(ii)

if
(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})=(q,p)
, then the two pairs determine the same

Q
-constellation in
[n]
if and only if
Q
is symmetric.

Consequently, every
Q
-constellation in
[n]
corresponds to exactly one
ordered
Q
-constellation in
[n]
when
Q
is nonsymmetric, and to
exactly two ordered
Q
-constellations in
[n]
when
Q
is symmetric.

Proof.

Recall that
a_{0}=0
and
a_{k}=D
. From (
1
),

x_{0}(p,q)=p
and
x_{k}(p,q)=q
. Since
0\leq a_{i}\leq D
, every

x_{i}(p,q)
lies in the closed interval with endpoints
p
and
q
. Thus

\{p,q\}
is precisely the set of the two extreme points of

\{x_{i}(p,q):0\leq i\leq k\}
. The same is true for
\{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\}
. Therefore

\{p,q\}=\{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\}
, and hence either
(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})=(p,q)
or

(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})=(q,p)
. Part (i) is immediate.

For part (ii), assume
(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})=(q,p)
. Since
p\neq q
, the
affine map
\phi_{p,q}(t)=p+t(q-p)
is injective, and

x_{i}(p,q)=\phi_{p,q}(a_{i}/D),\qquad x_{i}(q,p)=\phi_{p,q}(1-a_{i}/D).

Hence

\{x_{i}(p,q):0\leq i\leq k\}=\{x_{i}(q,p):0\leq i\leq k\}

if and only if

\{a_{i}/D:0\leq i\leq k\}=\{1-a_{i}/D:0\leq i\leq k\}
, equivalently

\{a_{0},\dots,a_{k}\}=\{D-a_{0},\dots,D-a_{k}\}
. Since

0=a_{0}<a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}=D
, this happens if and only if

a_{i}=D-a_{k-i}
for all
i
, equivalently
q_{i}+q_{k-i}=1
for all

i
. This is precisely the symmetry of
Q
.

Consequently, every
Q
-constellation in
[n]
arises from at most two ordered endpoint pairs, namely
(p,q)
and
(q,p)
. By part (ii), both pairs determine the same
Q
-constellation in
[n]
if and only if
Q
is symmetric. Hence every
Q
-constellation in
[n]
corresponds to exactly one ordered
Q
-constellation when
Q
is nonsymmetric, and to exactly two ordered
Q
-constellations in
[n]
when
Q
is symmetric.
∎

2.2
Total counts and the random coefficient

We now justify the random-coloring coefficient used in the introduction. For

r\in\{0,1,\dots,D-1\}
, let

A_{r}=\{m\in[n]:m\equiv r\pmod{D}\}
and
N_{r}=|A_{r}|
. Then

N_{r}=n/D+O_{D}(1)
, uniformly in
r
. Let
N_{Q}^{\mathrm{ord}}(n)
and
N_{Q}(n)

denote, respectively, the total numbers of ordered
Q
-constellations in

[n]
and
Q
-constellations in
[n]
.

Ordered
Q
-constellations in
[n]
are exactly ordered pairs of distinct
endpoints from a common residue class modulo
D
. Hence

N_{Q}^{\mathrm{ord}}(n)=\sum_{r=0}^{D-1}N_{r}(N_{r}-1)=\frac{n^{2}}{D}+O_{D}(n).

By Lemma
2.2
, it follows that

N_{Q}(n)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{n^{2}}{D}+O_{D}(n),&Q\text{ is nonsymmetric},\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{n^{2}}{2D}+O_{D}(n),&Q\text{ is symmetric}.\end{cases}

(2)

Recall that
\chi_{\mathrm{rand}}
is a uniformly random two-coloring of
[n]
, in
which the colors of the points
1,\dots,n
are independent and uniformly
distributed on
\{\pm 1\}
. By Lemma
2.1
, every

Q
-constellation in
[n]
has
k+1
distinct points. Therefore a fixed

Q
-constellation in
[n]
is monochromatic under
\chi_{\mathrm{rand}}
with
probability
2\cdot 2^{-(k+1)}=2^{-k}
. Thus,
\E\!\left[M_{Q}(\chi_{\mathrm{rand}})\right]=2^{-k}N_{Q}(n)
.
Combining this with (
2
) gives the random-coloring coefficient

\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2^{k}D},&Q\text{ is nonsymmetric},\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{2^{k+1}D},&Q\text{ is symmetric}.\end{cases}

3
A continuous model and the discrete-to-continuous transfer

The purpose of this section is to pass from the discrete counting problem to a
continuous bias model on
[0,1]
. The endpoint parametrization from
Section
2
suggests normalizing the endpoints

p,q\in[n]
by
x=p/n
and
y=q/n
. This leads to a continuous
functional whose value describes the limiting monochromatic probability for a
biased product coloring.

For normalized endpoints
x=p/n
and
y=q/n
, the normalized coordinate of
the
i
-th point of the corresponding
Q
-constellation is

\frac{x_{i}(p,q)}{n}=\left(1-\frac{a_{i}}{D}\right)x+\frac{a_{i}}{D}y=(1-q_{i})x+q_{i}y.

For
x,y\in[0,1]
, define the affine forms

L_{i}(x,y)=(1-q_{i})x+q_{i}y=\left(1-\frac{a_{i}}{D}\right)x+\frac{a_{i}}{D}y,\qquad 0\leq i\leq k.

Since
0\leq q_{i}\leq 1
, each
L_{i}(x,y)
lies in
[0,1]
. Thus

L_{i}(x,y)
is the normalized position of the
i
-th point of the
constellation with normalized endpoints
x
and
y
.

For any measurable
b:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
, define

\Phi_{Q}(b)=\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1+b(L_{i}(x,y))}{2}+\prod_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1-b(L_{i}(x,y))}{2}\right)\,dy\,dx.

(4)

Here
b
is a local bias: at a point
z\in[0,1]
, the probabilities of
the colors
+1
and
-1
are
(1+b(z))/2
and
(1-b(z))/2
,
respectively. Thus, for fixed normalized endpoints
(x,y)
, the two
products in (
4
) are the probabilities that all points of the
corresponding constellation are colored
+1
and
-1
, respectively.
Therefore
\Phi_{Q}(b)
is the average monochromatic probability in the
continuous endpoint model. In particular, if
b\equiv 0
, then

\Phi_{Q}(0)=2^{-k}
.

Our continuous goal is to find a bias
b
for which
\Phi_{Q}(b)<2^{-k}
.
The transfer result below will show that, when
b
is Lipschitz, such an
inequality gives a product coloring of
[n]
whose expected number of
monochromatic
Q
-constellations is below the random-coloring main term.

For each
n
, let
\chi_{n,b}
be the product coloring of
[n]
defined
by

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\chi_{n,b}(m)=1\bigr)=\frac{1+b(m/n)}{2},\qquad\mathbb{P}\bigl(\chi_{n,b}(m)=-1\bigr)=\frac{1-b(m/n)}{2},

independently for
1\leq m\leq n
, where
\mathbb{P}
denotes probability. In
particular,
\chi_{n,0}
has the same distribution as
\chi_{\mathrm{rand}}
.

3.1
Uniform residue-class Riemann sums

Having obtained the continuous average monochromatic probability
\Phi_{Q}(b)
, we now aim to connect it to the expected number of monochromatic
Q
-constellations in the discrete setting. A critical preliminary observation is that the endpoints
p,q
of a discrete
Q
-constellation are not arbitrary integer pairs, but must satisfy the congruence
p\equiv q\pmod{D}
. For this reason, standard Riemann sum approximations are not directly applicable, and we instead need a dedicated result for Riemann sums sampled over residue classes modulo
D
.

For
r\in\{0,1,\dots,D-1\}
, let

Y_{r}=\left\{\frac{r+D\beta}{n}:1\leq r+D\beta\leq n,\ \beta\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

Thus
Y_{r}
is the normalized set of integers in
[n]
congruent to
r

modulo
D
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be Lipschitz. For each

r\in\{0,1,\dots,D-1\}
, define
T_{r}(f)=\sum_{y\in Y_{r}}f(y)
. Then,
uniformly in
r
,

T_{r}(f)=\frac{n}{D}\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt+O_{f}(1).

(5)

More precisely, if
L_{f}
is a Lipschitz constant for
f
and

M_{f}=\|f\|_{\infty}
, then

\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt-\frac{D}{n}T_{r}(f)\right|\leq(L_{f}+M_{f})\frac{D}{n}

(6)

for every
r
.

Proof.

Set
h=D/n
, and let
N=|Y_{r}|
. If
N\geq 1
, write the elements of

Y_{r}
in increasing order as
y_{0}<y_{1}<\cdots<y_{N-1}
.

We first treat the cases
N=0
and
N=1
. If
N=0
, then

T_{r}(f)=0
. This can occur only when
n<D
, hence
h>1
. Therefore

\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt-hT_{r}(f)\right|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt\right|\leq M_{f}\leq(L_{f}+M_{f})h.

If
N=1
, say
Y_{r}=\{y_{0}\}
, then

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt-hf(y_{0})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}|f(t)-f(y_{0})|\,dt+|1-h|\,|f(y_{0})|

\displaystyle\leq L_{f}\int_{0}^{1}|t-y_{0}|\,dt+M_{f}|1-h|.

Since
N=1
, necessarily
n<2D
, and hence
h=D/n>1/2
. Also

\int_{0}^{1}|t-y_{0}|\,dt\leq 1/2\leq h
and
|1-h|\leq h
. Hence

\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt-hT_{r}(f)\right|\leq(L_{f}+M_{f})h.

It remains to consider the case
N\geq 2
. Since consecutive integers in a
fixed residue class modulo
D
differ by
D
, consecutive sampling points
satisfy

y_{j+1}-y_{j}=h\qquad(0\leq j\leq N-2).

(7)

We partition
[0,1]
by Voronoi cells centered at the sampling points.
Define

m_{0}=0,\qquad m_{j}=\frac{y_{j-1}+y_{j}}{2}\quad(1\leq j\leq N-1),\qquad m_{N}=1,

and set
I_{j}=[m_{j},m_{j+1}]
for
0\leq j\leq N-1
. Then

[0,1]=\bigcup_{j=0}^{N-1}I_{j}
.

For every
t\in I_{j}
, we have
|t-y_{j}|\leq h
. Indeed, for

1\leq j\leq N-2
, (
7
) gives

I_{j}=[y_{j}-h/2,y_{j}+h/2]
. For the left boundary cell,

I_{0}=[0,(y_{0}+y_{1})/2]\subseteq[y_{0}-h,y_{0}+h],

because
0<y_{0}\leq h
and
y_{1}=y_{0}+h
. The right boundary cell is
analogous, using
0\leq 1-y_{N-1}<h
.

Moreover, every interior cell has length
h
, and only the two boundary
cells can have length different from
h
. Since

|I_{0}|=y_{0}+\frac{h}{2},\qquad|I_{N-1}|=1-y_{N-1}+\frac{h}{2},

we have

\bigl||I_{0}|-h\bigr|\leq\frac{h}{2},\qquad\bigl||I_{N-1}|-h\bigr|\leq\frac{h}{2}.

Thus

\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\bigl||I_{j}|-h\bigr|\leq h.

(8)

By Lipschitz continuity,

\left|\int_{I_{j}}f(t)\,dt-|I_{j}|f(y_{j})\right|\leq\int_{I_{j}}|f(t)-f(y_{j})|\,dt\leq L_{f}h|I_{j}|.

Summing over
j
, we get

\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt-\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}|I_{j}|f(y_{j})\right|\leq L_{f}h.

(9)

Next, by (
8
) and
|f(y_{j})|\leq M_{f}
,

\left|\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}|I_{j}|f(y_{j})-h\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}f(y_{j})\right|\leq M_{f}h.

(10)

Combining (
9
) and (
10
), we
obtain

\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt-hT_{r}(f)\right|\leq(L_{f}+M_{f})h.

Since
h=D/n
, this is (
6
). Multiplying by

h^{-1}=n/D
gives (
5
).
∎

The uniformity in the residue class allows us to apply the estimate twice,
first in one endpoint variable and then in the other, without losing control of
the error term.

3.2
Two-dimensional discrete sums versus the continuous functional

Recall that
M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(\chi)
denotes the number of monochromatic ordered

Q
-constellations in
[n]
under a coloring
\chi
, while

M_{Q}(\chi)
denotes the number of monochromatic
Q
-constellations in

[n]
. For the product coloring
\chi_{n,b}
, define

M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(n;b)=\E\!\left[M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(\chi_{n,b})\right],\qquad M_{Q}(n;b)=\E\!\left[M_{Q}(\chi_{n,b})\right].

We can now establish a precise correspondence between the expected number of monochromatic
Q
-constellations in the discrete setting and the continuous functional
\Phi_{Q}(b)
, which forms the core result of the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
b:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
is Lipschitz, then

M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(n;b)=\frac{n^{2}}{D}\,\Phi_{Q}(b)+O_{Q,b}(n).

(11)

If
Q
is nonsymmetric, then

M_{Q}(n;b)=M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(n;b)=\frac{n^{2}}{D}\,\Phi_{Q}(b)+O_{Q,b}(n).

(12)

If
Q
is symmetric, then

M_{Q}(n;b)=\frac{1}{2}M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(n;b)=\frac{n^{2}}{2D}\,\Phi_{Q}(b)+O_{Q,b}(n).

(13)

Proof.

Define

H_{b}(x,y)=\prod_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1+b(L_{i}(x,y))}{2}+\prod_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1-b(L_{i}(x,y))}{2}.

Fix endpoints
p\neq q
with
p\equiv q\pmod{D}
. By
Lemma
2.1
, the points
x_{0}(p,q),\dots,x_{k}(p,q)
are
distinct, so their colors are independent. The probability that this ordered
copy is monochromatic is therefore

H_{b}\!\left(\frac{p}{n},\frac{q}{n}\right)
,
because

\frac{x_{i}(p,q)}{n}=\left(1-\frac{a_{i}}{D}\right)\frac{p}{n}+\frac{a_{i}}{D}\frac{q}{n}=L_{i}\!\left(\frac{p}{n},\frac{q}{n}\right).

Hence

M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(n;b)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq p,q\leq n\\
p\neq q,\ p\equiv q\pmod{D}\end{subarray}}H_{b}\!\left(\frac{p}{n},\frac{q}{n}\right).

Since
0\leq H_{b}(x,y)\leq 1
, adding the diagonal
p=q
changes the sum by

O(n)
. Thus

M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(n;b)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq p,q\leq n\\
p\equiv q\pmod{D}\end{subarray}}H_{b}\!\left(\frac{p}{n},\frac{q}{n}\right)+O(n).

For each residue class
r\in\{0,\dots,D-1\}
, define

S_{r}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq p,q\leq n\\
p\equiv q\equiv r\pmod{D}\end{subarray}}H_{b}\!\left(\frac{p}{n},\frac{q}{n}\right).

Then

M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(n;b)=\sum_{r=0}^{D-1}S_{r}+O(n).

(14)

We now compare each
S_{r}
with the corresponding integral. Since
b
is
Lipschitz and the maps
L_{i}
are affine, each composition
b\circ L_{i}
is
Lipschitz on
[0,1]^{2}
. Because these functions are bounded by
1
,
finite sums and products of them remain Lipschitz. Hence
H_{b}
is
Lipschitz on
[0,1]^{2}
; let
C_{Q,b}
be a Lipschitz constant for

H_{b}
.

Fix
y\in[0,1]
, and set
f_{y}(x)=H_{b}(x,y)
. The Lipschitz constant and
supremum norm of
f_{y}
are bounded independently of
y
. Applying
Lemma
3.1
to
f_{y}
, uniformly in
y
, gives for every
residue class
r

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq p\leq n\\
p\equiv r\pmod{D}\end{subarray}}H_{b}\!\left(\frac{p}{n},y\right)=\frac{n}{D}\int_{0}^{1}H_{b}(x,y)\,dx+O_{Q,b}(1).

(15)

Summing (
15
) over
y\in Y_{r}
, we obtain

S_{r}=\sum_{y\in Y_{r}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq p\leq n\\
p\equiv r\ ({\rm mod}\ D)\end{subarray}}H_{b}\!\left(\frac{p}{n},y\right)=\frac{n}{D}\sum_{y\in Y_{r}}\int_{0}^{1}H_{b}(x,y)\,dx+O_{Q,b}(|Y_{r}|).

Since
|Y_{r}|=n/D+O_{D}(1)
, this becomes

S_{r}=\frac{n}{D}\sum_{y\in Y_{r}}G(y)+O_{Q,b}(n),\qquad G(y)=\int_{0}^{1}H_{b}(x,y)\,dx.

(16)

The function
G
is Lipschitz. Indeed, for
y,y^{\prime}\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle|G(y)-G(y^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(H_{b}(x,y)-H_{b}(x,y^{\prime})\bigr)\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}|H_{b}(x,y)-H_{b}(x,y^{\prime})|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}C_{Q,b}|y-y^{\prime}|\,dx

\displaystyle=C_{Q,b}|y-y^{\prime}|.

Applying Lemma
3.1
once more, now to
G
, yields

\sum_{y\in Y_{r}}G(y)=\frac{n}{D}\int_{0}^{1}G(y)\,dy+O_{Q,b}(1).

(17)

Substituting (
17
) into (
16
) gives

S_{r}=\frac{n^{2}}{D^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}G(y)\,dy+O_{Q,b}(n)=\frac{n^{2}}{D^{2}}\Phi_{Q}(b)+O_{Q,b}(n).

Summing over
r=0,\dots,D-1
in (
14
) gives
(
11
).

Finally, Lemma
2.2
shows that ordered

Q
-constellations are in bijection with underlying
Q
-constellations in
the nonsymmetric case, while the map from ordered constellations to underlying
constellations is two-to-one in the symmetric case. This gives
(
12
) and (
13
).
∎

Thus Proposition
3.1
reduces the problem to constructing a
Lipschitz bias
b:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
with
\Phi_{Q}(b)<2^{-k}
. Any such fixed

b
gives, for all sufficiently large
n
, a product coloring whose
expected monochromatic density is strictly below the random-coloring
coefficient
\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)
.

3.3
Second variation at the random point

We next analyze the functional
\Phi_{Q}
near the unbiased point

b\equiv 0
, which corresponds to the uniformly random coloring. The aim is
to identify a perturbation direction along which
\Phi_{Q}
decreases. Fix a
measurable function
g:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
, let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
with

|\lambda|\leq 1
, and set
b_{\lambda}=\lambda g
. Then
b_{\lambda}
is an
admissible bias function. We expand
\Phi_{Q}(b_{\lambda})
as

\lambda\to 0
; the first nonconstant term is a quadratic form in
g
.
Thus a function
g
for which this quadratic form is negative will give

\Phi_{Q}(\lambda g)<2^{-k}
for all sufficiently small positive
\lambda
.

Define the quadratic form

T_{2}(g)=\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq k}\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}g(L_{i}(x,y))g(L_{j}(x,y))\,dy\,dx.

(18)

Lemma 3.2
.

For every measurable
g:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
,

\Phi_{Q}(\lambda g)=2^{-k}+2^{-k}\lambda^{2}T_{2}(g)+O_{k}(\lambda^{4}),\qquad|\lambda|\leq 1,

(19)

where the implicit constant depends only on
k
.

Proof.

Fix
(x,y)
, and write
g_{i}=g(L_{i}(x,y))
for
0\leq i\leq k
. The
integrand in (
4
) is

2^{-(k+1)}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k}(1+\lambda g_{i})+\prod_{i=0}^{k}(1-\lambda g_{i})\right).

Expanding the two products gives

\prod_{i=0}^{k}(1+\lambda g_{i})=\sum_{S\subseteq\{0,\dots,k\}}\lambda^{|S|}\prod_{i\in S}g_{i}

and

\prod_{i=0}^{k}(1-\lambda g_{i})=\sum_{S\subseteq\{0,\dots,k\}}(-1)^{|S|}\lambda^{|S|}\prod_{i\in S}g_{i}.

Adding the two expansions cancels every odd-degree term and leaves

\prod_{i=0}^{k}(1+\lambda g_{i})+\prod_{i=0}^{k}(1-\lambda g_{i})=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq\{0,\dots,k\}\\
|S|\ \mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}\lambda^{|S|}\prod_{i\in S}g_{i}.

The terms of degrees
0
and
2
are

2+2\lambda^{2}\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq k}g_{i}g_{j}.

After integration over
[0,1]^{2}
, these contribute
2^{-k}
and

2^{-k}\lambda^{2}T_{2}(g)
, respectively. The remaining terms have even degree
at least
4
. Since
|g_{i}|\leq 1
, their total contribution is bounded in
absolute value by

2^{-k}\sum_{r\geq 2}\binom{k+1}{2r}|\lambda|^{2r}\leq 2^{-k}\left(\sum_{r\geq 2}\binom{k+1}{2r}\right)|\lambda|^{4},

for
|\lambda|\leq 1
. This is
O_{k}(\lambda^{4})
, and the lemma follows.
∎

Consequently, if
T_{2}(g)<0
, then
\Phi_{Q}(\lambda g)<2^{-k}
for all
sufficiently small
\lambda>0
. Thus it remains to construct an admissible
direction
g
with negative quadratic variation.

4
An explicit negative direction

We now construct a smooth admissible direction
g
with
T_{2}(g)<0
. Set

u(x)=\cos(2\pi Dx)
and
v(x)=\cos(2\pi(D+1)x)
. The mode
u
is a
zero direction for
T_{2}
, while the mixed bilinear term between
u
and the
adjacent mode
v
has a fixed positive sign. A small perturbation of
u

in the direction
-v
will therefore give the desired negative direction.

We first show that the
D
-frequency mode is a zero direction.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have
T_{2}(u)=0
.

Proof.

For
0\leq i<j\leq k
, set

I_{i,j}=\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}u(L_{i}(x,y))u(L_{j}(x,y))\,dy\,dx.

It suffices to show that
I_{i,j}=0
for every
i<j
. Since

q_{i}=a_{i}/D
,

DL_{i}(x,y)=(D-a_{i})x+a_{i}y,

and therefore

u(L_{i}(x,y))=\cos\bigl(2\pi((D-a_{i})x+a_{i}y)\bigr),\qquad u(L_{j}(x,y))=\cos\bigl(2\pi((D-a_{j})x+a_{j}y)\bigr).

Let

A=2\pi((D-a_{i})x+a_{i}y),\qquad B=2\pi((D-a_{j})x+a_{j}y).

Using
\cos A\cos B=\frac{1}{2}\cos(A-B)+\frac{1}{2}\cos(A+B)
, we get

I_{i,j}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}\cos(A-B)\,dy\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}\cos(A+B)\,dy\,dx.

For the first integral,

A-B=2\pi\bigl((a_{j}-a_{i})x+(a_{i}-a_{j})y\bigr)
,
and the frequency vector
(a_{j}-a_{i},a_{i}-a_{j})
is nonzero because

a_{i}\neq a_{j}
. For the second integral,

A+B=2\pi\bigl((2D-a_{i}-a_{j})x+(a_{i}+a_{j})y\bigr)
,
and the frequency vector
(2D-a_{i}-a_{j},a_{i}+a_{j})
is nonzero because

j\geq 1
and hence
a_{i}+a_{j}\geq a_{j}>0
.

In each case, writing the cosine in exponential form reduces the integral to
a linear combination of terms

\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(mx+ny)}\,dy\,dx=\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}mx}\,dx\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}ny}\,dy\right),

where
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
(m,n)\neq(0,0)
. At least one of the two
one-dimensional integrals vanishes. Thus both integrals above are zero, and
therefore
I_{i,j}=0
. Summing over all
0\leq i<j\leq k
, we obtain

T_{2}(u)=0
.
∎

We next calculate the mixed correlations of
u
and
v
. Set

J(\theta)=\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\theta x}\,dx=\begin{cases}\dfrac{e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\theta}-1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\theta},&\theta\neq 0,\\[8.0pt]
1,&\theta=0.\end{cases}

(20)

Lemma 4.2
.

If
N\in\mathbb{Z}
, then for every real
\theta
,

J(\theta)J(N-\theta)=-\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi\theta)}{\pi^{2}\theta(N-\theta)},

(21)

where the right-hand side is understood by continuous extension at

\theta=0
and
\theta=N
.

Proof.

Assume first that
\theta\neq 0,N
. By (
20
),

J(\theta)J(N-\theta)=\frac{(e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\theta}-1)(e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(N-\theta)}-1)}{(2\pi\mathrm{i}\theta)(2\pi\mathrm{i}(N-\theta))}.

Since
N\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}N}=1
, and hence

e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(N-\theta)}=e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\theta}
. Therefore the
numerator equals

(e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\theta}-1)(e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\theta}-1)=2-\bigl(e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\theta}+e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\theta}\bigr)=4\sin^{2}(\pi\theta).

Since
(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}=-4\pi^{2}
, the denominator is

-4\pi^{2}\theta(N-\theta)
, and (
21
) follows. The endpoint
cases are obtained by taking limits.
∎

4.1
Mixed correlations

Let
\Re(z)
denote the real part of the complex number
z
, that is, if

z=\alpha+\beta\mathrm{i}
with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\Re(z)=\alpha
. For integers
0\leq s<t\leq D
, write

L_{s}(x,y)=\left(1-\frac{s}{D}\right)x+\frac{s}{D}y.

This agrees with the previous notation when
s=a_{i}
. Define

U_{s,t}=\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}u(L_{s}(x,y))v(L_{t}(x,y))\,dy\,dx.

Lemma 4.3
.

For every
0\leq s<t\leq D
,

\displaystyle U_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi t/D)}{2\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{(t-s+t/D)(t-s-1+t/D)}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 102.43008pt\left.-\frac{1}{(s+t+t/D)(2D-s-t+1-t/D)}\right].

(22)

In particular,
U_{s,t}>0
when
t<D
, while
U_{s,D}=0
.

Proof.

Set

A=2\pi DL_{s}(x,y),\qquad B=2\pi(D+1)L_{t}(x,y).

Then

u(L_{s})=\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}A}+e^{-\mathrm{i}A}}{2},\qquad v(L_{t})=\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}B}+e^{-\mathrm{i}B}}{2}.

Hence

U_{s,t}=\frac{1}{2}\Re\left(\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}e^{\mathrm{i}(A+B)}\,dy\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}e^{\mathrm{i}(A-B)}\,dy\,dx\right).

Write

A+B=2\pi(\beta_{+}x+\alpha_{+}y),\qquad A-B=2\pi(\beta_{-}x+\alpha_{-}y),

where

\alpha_{+}=s+t+\frac{t}{D},\qquad\beta_{+}=2D-s-t+1-\frac{t}{D},

and

\alpha_{-}=s-t-\frac{t}{D},\qquad\beta_{-}=t-s-1+\frac{t}{D}.

Then the two integrals are
J(\alpha_{+})J(\beta_{+})
and

J(\alpha_{-})J(\beta_{-})
, respectively. Moreover,

\alpha_{+}+\beta_{+}=2D+1\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\alpha_{-}+\beta_{-}=-1\in\mathbb{Z}.

By Lemma
4.2
,

J(\alpha_{+})J(\beta_{+})=-\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha_{+})}{\pi^{2}\alpha_{+}\beta_{+}},\qquad J(\alpha_{-})J(\beta_{-})=-\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha_{-})}{\pi^{2}\alpha_{-}\beta_{-}}.

Since
s,t\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha_{+})=\sin^{2}\left(\pi\frac{t}{D}\right),\qquad\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha_{-})=\sin^{2}\left(\pi\frac{t}{D}\right).

Also,

-\alpha_{-}=t-s+\frac{t}{D},\qquad\beta_{-}=t-s-1+\frac{t}{D}.

Substituting these identities gives (
4.3
).

If
t=D
, then the factor
\sin^{2}(\pi t/D)
vanishes, so

U_{s,D}=0
. Suppose now that
t<D
. Then this sine factor is strictly
positive. Set

P_{1}=t-s+\frac{t}{D},\quad Q_{1}=t-s-1+\frac{t}{D},\quad P_{2}=s+t+\frac{t}{D},\quad Q_{2}=2D-s-t+1-\frac{t}{D}.

All four quantities are positive; in particular,

Q_{1}=t-s-1+t/D\geq t/D>0
. Moreover,

P_{2}-P_{1}=2s\geq 0,\qquad Q_{2}-Q_{1}=2\left(D-t+1-\frac{t}{D}\right)>0,

because
t<D
. Hence
P_{2}Q_{2}>P_{1}Q_{1}
, and the bracketed expression in
(
4.3
) is positive. Thus
U_{s,t}>0
.
∎

Similarly, define

V_{s,t}=\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}v(L_{s}(x,y))u(L_{t}(x,y))\,dy\,dx.

Lemma 4.4
.

For every
0\leq s<t\leq D
,

\displaystyle V_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi s/D)}{2\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{(t-s-s/D)(t-s+1-s/D)}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 102.43008pt\left.-\frac{1}{(s+t+s/D)(2D-s-t+1-s/D)}\right].

(23)

In particular,
V_{s,t}>0
when
s>0
, while
V_{0,t}=0
.

Proof.

The calculation is parallel to that in Lemma
4.3
. Set

A=2\pi(D+1)L_{s}(x,y),\qquad B=2\pi DL_{t}(x,y).

Then

v(L_{s})=\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}A}+e^{-\mathrm{i}A}}{2},\qquad u(L_{t})=\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}B}+e^{-\mathrm{i}B}}{2}.

Hence

V_{s,t}=\frac{1}{2}\Re\left(\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}e^{\mathrm{i}(A+B)}\,dy\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}e^{\mathrm{i}(A-B)}\,dy\,dx\right).

Write

A+B=2\pi(\beta_{+}x+\alpha_{+}y),\qquad A-B=2\pi(\beta_{-}x+\alpha_{-}y),

where

\alpha_{+}=s+t+\frac{s}{D},\qquad\beta_{+}=2D-s-t+1-\frac{s}{D},

and

\alpha_{-}=s-t+\frac{s}{D},\qquad\beta_{-}=t-s+1-\frac{s}{D}.

Then the two integrals are
J(\alpha_{+})J(\beta_{+})
and

J(\alpha_{-})J(\beta_{-})
, respectively. Moreover,

\alpha_{+}+\beta_{+}=2D+1\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\alpha_{-}+\beta_{-}=1\in\mathbb{Z}.

By Lemma
4.2
,

J(\alpha_{+})J(\beta_{+})=-\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha_{+})}{\pi^{2}\alpha_{+}\beta_{+}},\qquad J(\alpha_{-})J(\beta_{-})=-\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha_{-})}{\pi^{2}\alpha_{-}\beta_{-}}.

Since
s\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha_{+})=\sin^{2}\left(\pi\frac{s}{D}\right),\qquad\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha_{-})=\sin^{2}\left(\pi\frac{s}{D}\right).

Also,

-\alpha_{-}=t-s-\frac{s}{D},\qquad\beta_{-}=t-s+1-\frac{s}{D}.

Substituting these identities gives (
4.4
).

If
s=0
, then the factor
\sin^{2}(\pi s/D)
vanishes, so

V_{0,t}=0
. Suppose now that
s>0
. Then this sine factor is strictly
positive. Set

P_{1}=t-s-\frac{s}{D},\quad Q_{1}=t-s+1-\frac{s}{D},\quad P_{2}=s+t+\frac{s}{D},\quad Q_{2}=2D-s-t+1-\frac{s}{D}.

All four quantities are positive; in particular,
P_{1}\geq 1-s/D>0
. Moreover,

P_{2}-P_{1}=2s+\frac{2s}{D}>0,\qquad Q_{2}-Q_{1}=2(D-t)\geq 0.

Thus
P_{2}Q_{2}>P_{1}Q_{1}
, and the bracketed expression in (
4.4
) is
positive. Hence
V_{s,t}>0
.
∎

Summing the mixed correlations gives the required positive mixed term. Define

\displaystyle\Lambda_{Q}=\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq k}

\displaystyle\Biggl(\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}u(L_{i}(x,y))v(L_{j}(x,y))\,dy\,dx

(24)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}v(L_{i}(x,y))u(L_{j}(x,y))\,dy\,dx\Biggr).

Corollary 4.1
.

We have
\Lambda_{Q}>0
.

Proof.

For
i<j
, the affine forms
L_{i}
and
L_{j}
are the forms
L_{a_{i}}

and
L_{a_{j}}
. Hence Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
give

\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}u(L_{i}(x,y))v(L_{j}(x,y))\,dy\,dx=U_{a_{i},a_{j}}\geq 0

and

\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}v(L_{i}(x,y))u(L_{j}(x,y))\,dy\,dx=V_{a_{i},a_{j}}\geq 0.

Since
k\geq 2
, there is an interior point, and hence
0<a_{1}<D
. For the
pair
(a_{0},a_{1})=(0,a_{1})
, Lemma
4.3
gives
U_{0,a_{1}}>0
, while
Lemma
4.4
gives
V_{0,a_{1}}=0
. Thus at least one term in
(
24
) is strictly positive, and all terms are nonnegative.
Therefore
\Lambda_{Q}>0
.
∎

Finally, we construct the function
g
satisfying
T_{2}(g)<0
.
We write
C^{\infty}([0,1])
for the restrictions to
[0,1]
of smooth
functions defined on an open neighborhood of
[0,1]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

There exists a function
g\in C^{\infty}([0,1])
with

g:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
such that
T_{2}(g)<0
.

Proof.

Define the symmetric bilinear form

B_{2}(f,h)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq k}\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(f(L_{i}(x,y))h(L_{j}(x,y))+h(L_{i}(x,y))f(L_{j}(x,y))\bigr)\,dy\,dx.

Then
T_{2}(f)=B_{2}(f,f)
, and by (
24
),

2B_{2}(u,v)=\Lambda_{Q}>0
. By Lemma
4.1
,
T_{2}(u)=0
. Hence,
for every
\tau>0
,

T_{2}(u-\tau v)=T_{2}(u)-2\tau B_{2}(u,v)+\tau^{2}T_{2}(v)=-\tau\Lambda_{Q}+\tau^{2}T_{2}(v).

Since
|v|\leq 1
, we have

|T_{2}(v)|\leq\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq k}\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}|v(L_{i}(x,y))v(L_{j}(x,y))|\,dy\,dx\leq\binom{k+1}{2}.

Choose

0<\tau<\frac{\Lambda_{Q}}{2\binom{k+1}{2}}.

Then

T_{2}(u-\tau v)<-\frac{\tau\Lambda_{Q}}{2}<0.

Set

g(x)=\frac{u(x)-\tau v(x)}{1+\tau}.

Since
|u|,|v|\leq 1
, we have
|g|\leq 1
. Also

g\in C^{\infty}([0,1])
. Since
T_{2}
is homogeneous of degree two,

T_{2}(g)=\frac{1}{(1+\tau)^{2}}T_{2}(u-\tau v)<0.

∎

5
From a negative direction to deterministic colorings

We now use the negative direction constructed in Proposition
4.1

to obtain a biased product coloring whose expected monochromatic count has
leading coefficient below the random-coloring coefficient. A standard
conditional-expectation argument then gives a deterministic coloring with the
same asymptotic improvement.

Fix a function
g
as in Proposition
4.1
, and set
\eta_{Q}=-T_{2}(g)>0
.
By Lemma
3.2
, there exists a constant
C_{k}>0
,
depending only on
k
, such that, for all
|\lambda|\leq 1
,

\Phi_{Q}(\lambda g)=2^{-k}-2^{-k}\eta_{Q}\lambda^{2}+R(\lambda),\qquad|R(\lambda)|\leq C_{k}\lambda^{4}.

(25)

Choose
\lambda>0
so small that

C_{k}\lambda^{2}\leq 2^{-k-1}\eta_{Q}.

(26)

For instance, one may take

0<\lambda\leq\min\left\{1,\sqrt{\frac{\eta_{Q}}{2^{k+1}C_{k}}}\right\}.

Then (
25
) and (
26
) give

\Phi_{Q}(\lambda g)\leq 2^{-k}-2^{-k}\eta_{Q}\lambda^{2}+C_{k}\lambda^{4}\leq 2^{-k}-2^{-k-1}\eta_{Q}\lambda^{2}.

Set
c_{Q}=2^{-k-1}\eta_{Q}\lambda^{2}>0
.
Thus

\Phi_{Q}(\lambda g)\leq 2^{-k}-c_{Q}.

(27)

Take
b=\lambda g
in the product coloring
\chi_{n,b}
. Since

g\in C^{\infty}([0,1])
, the function
b
is Lipschitz; since
|g|\leq 1

and
|\lambda|\leq 1
, it is an admissible bias function. By
Proposition
3.1
and (
27
),

M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(n;\lambda g)=\frac{n^{2}}{D}\Phi_{Q}(\lambda g)+O_{Q}(n)\leq\left(2^{-k}-c_{Q}\right)\frac{n^{2}}{D}+O_{Q}(n).

(28)

Here the error term is
O_{Q}(n)
, since
g
and
\lambda
are fixed once

Q
is fixed.

It remains to pass from the biased product coloring to a deterministic one. We
use the following standard form of the method of conditional expectations.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables, each taking finitely
many values, and let
F=F(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
be an integrable real-valued random
variable. Then there exists a realization
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
such that

F(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\leq\E[F].

Moreover, one may choose such a realization by fixing the variables one at a
time so that the conditional expectation never increases.

Proof.

Suppose that
x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1}
have already been chosen. The conditional
expectation

\E\!\left[F\mid X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{m-1}=x_{m-1}\right]

is a convex combination of the conditional expectations obtained by specifying
the value of
X_{m}
. Hence at least one value
x_{m}
satisfies

\E\!\left[F\mid X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{m}=x_{m}\right]\leq\E\!\left[F\mid X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{m-1}=x_{m-1}\right].

Iterating this choice for
m=1,\dots,n
, the final conditional expectation
is
F(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, and it is at most the original expectation
\E[F]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
X_{m}\in\{\pm 1\}
be the color of
m
in the biased product coloring
with bias
b=\lambda g
, and let
\chi_{X}(m)=X_{m}
. Apply
Lemma
5.1
to

F=M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(\chi_{X})
.
Since
\E[F]=M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(n;\lambda g)
, (
28
) gives a
deterministic coloring
\chi_{n}:[n]\to\{\pm 1\}
such that

M^{\mathrm{ord}}_{Q}(\chi_{n})\leq\left(2^{-k}-c_{Q}\right)\frac{n^{2}}{D}+O_{Q}(n).

(29)

If
Q
is nonsymmetric, then ordered
Q
-constellations are in bijection
with underlying
Q
-constellations by Lemma
2.2
.
Therefore

M_{Q}(\chi_{n})\leq\left(\frac{1}{2^{k}D}-\frac{c_{Q}}{D}\right)n^{2}+O_{Q}(n).

If
Q
is symmetric, then the map from ordered constellations to underlying
constellations is two-to-one. Hence

M_{Q}(\chi_{n})\leq\left(\frac{1}{2^{k+1}D}-\frac{c_{Q}}{2D}\right)n^{2}+O_{Q}(n).

Taking

\delta_{Q}=\frac{c_{Q}}{2D}>0

in both cases, and recalling the definition of
\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)
, we obtain

M_{Q}(\chi_{n})\leq\bigl(\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)-\delta_{Q}\bigr)n^{2}+O_{Q}(n).

This proves Theorem
1.1
.
∎

6
Applications

We record two consequences of Theorem
1.1
. First, it gives a
uniform family of uncommon translation-invariant linear systems in the integer
interval model. Second, it yields a ground-state bound for a deterministic
antiferromagnetic hypergraph spin system whose hyperedges are rational
constellations.

6.1
A linear-system consequence

In graph Ramsey multiplicity theory, a graph is called common if the uniformly
random edge-coloring asymptotically minimizes the number of monochromatic
copies. The Burr–Rosta conjecture asserted that all graphs are common, but
this was disproved by Thomason for
K_{4}
, with further developments by
Jagger, Šťovíček, and Thomason
[
5
,
40
,
18
]
.
Arithmetic analogues ask the same question for linear configurations under
colorings of groups or intervals. In finite-field and finite-group settings,
commonness phenomena can behave differently; for example, common and Sidorenko
single equations have been studied and classified in several settings

[
33
,
13
,
41
]
. Theorem
1.1
concerns the integer interval
model and yields a uniform family of interval-uncommon translation-invariant
systems.

Let

Q=[0=q_{0}<q_{1}<\cdots<q_{k}=1]

be a rational constellation pattern with
k\geq 2
, and let
D
be the least
common denominator of
q_{0},\dots,q_{k}
. Write
a_{i}=Dq_{i}
. Associated to

Q
is the translation-invariant linear system

\mathcal{L}_{Q}:\qquad Dx_{i}=(D-a_{i})x_{0}+a_{i}x_{k},\qquad 1\leq i\leq k-1.

Thus
\mathcal{L}_{Q}
is a system of
k-1
equations in the variables

x_{0},\dots,x_{k}
, and each equation has coefficient sum zero.

For a coloring
\chi:[n]\to\{\pm 1\}
, let

M_{\mathcal{L}_{Q}}^{\mathrm{ind}}(\chi)
denote the number of monochromatic
nonconstant indexed solutions
(x_{0},\dots,x_{k})\in[n]^{k+1}
of

\mathcal{L}_{Q}
, where nonconstant means
x_{0}\neq x_{k}
. Such a solution is
precisely an endpoint representation of a
Q
-constellation: setting

s=x_{0}
and
d=x_{k}-x_{0}\neq 0
, the equations give

x_{i}=s+q_{i}d\qquad(0\leq i\leq k).

By Lemma
2.2
, the indexed solution count and the
constellation count differ only by the reversal factor

M_{\mathcal{L}_{Q}}^{\mathrm{ind}}(\chi)=\nu_{Q}M_{Q}(\chi),\qquad\nu_{Q}=\begin{cases}1,&Q\text{ is nonsymmetric},\\
2,&Q\text{ is symmetric}.\end{cases}

Accordingly, the random-coloring coefficient for indexed nonconstant solutions
is

\Gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(\mathcal{L}_{Q})=\nu_{Q}\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)=\frac{1}{2^{k}D}.

Corollary 6.1
.

For every rational constellation pattern
Q
with
k\geq 2
, the associated
system
\mathcal{L}_{Q}
is uncommon in the integer interval model. More
precisely, there exist
\delta_{Q}^{\prime}>0
and colorings

\chi_{n}:[n]\to\{\pm 1\}
such that

M_{\mathcal{L}_{Q}}^{\mathrm{ind}}(\chi_{n})\leq\bigl(\Gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(\mathcal{L}_{Q})-\delta_{Q}^{\prime}\bigr)n^{2}+O_{Q}(n).

Proof.

By Theorem
1.1
, there are colorings
\chi_{n}
and a constant

\delta_{Q}>0
such that

M_{Q}(\chi_{n})\leq\bigl(\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)-\delta_{Q}\bigr)n^{2}+O_{Q}(n).

Multiplying by
\nu_{Q}
, we obtain

M_{\mathcal{L}_{Q}}^{\mathrm{ind}}(\chi_{n})\leq\bigl(\nu_{Q}\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)-\nu_{Q}\delta_{Q}\bigr)n^{2}+O_{Q}(n).

Since
\Gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(\mathcal{L}_{Q})=\nu_{Q}\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)
, the claim
follows with
\delta_{Q}^{\prime}=\nu_{Q}\delta_{Q}
.
∎

Thus every rational one-dimensional affine pattern with at least three points
gives an interval-uncommon translation-invariant linear system. This includes
systems encoding longer arithmetic progressions and higher-codimension
one-dimensional rational configurations.

6.2
A spin-system consequence

Spin systems and spin glasses study energy minimization problems on the
hypercube
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
; see, for example, Panchenko’s monograph on the
Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model
[
25
]
. At zero temperature,
one is interested in ground-state energy, as in the mixed
p
-spin models
studied by Auffinger and Chen
[
1
]
. Hypergraph coloring
also has a natural spin-system formulation, where the energy penalizes
monochromatic hyperedges; see, for instance, the work of Bapst, Coja-Oghlan,
and Raßmann on random hypergraph
2
-coloring

[
2
]
. In contrast with these random models, the
hypergraph below is deterministic and arithmetic: its edges are the

Q
-constellations in
[n]
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{Q}(n)
be the family of
Q
-constellations in
[n]
, and
view
\mathcal{H}_{Q}(n)=([n],\mathcal{C}_{Q}(n))

as a
(k+1)
-uniform hypergraph. A spin configuration is a vector

\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}^{[n]}
. For
e\in\mathcal{C}_{Q}(n)
, set

\delta_{e}(\sigma)=\mathbf{1}\{\sigma\text{ is constant on }e\}
,
and define the energy

E_{Q,n}(\sigma)=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{C}_{Q}(n)}\delta_{e}(\sigma)
.
Identifying
\sigma
with the corresponding two-coloring, we have

E_{Q,n}(\sigma)=M_{Q}(\sigma)
. Hence minimizing monochromatic

Q
-constellations is exactly the ground-state problem for the Hamiltonian

E_{Q,n}
.

Under the uniformly random coloring, each hyperedge is monochromatic with
probability
2^{-k}
. Hence the random-coloring main term for the energy is

2^{-k}|\mathcal{C}_{Q}(n)|=\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)n^{2}+O_{Q}(n)
.
Define the centered energy

E_{Q,n}^{\mathrm{cent}}(\sigma)=E_{Q,n}(\sigma)-2^{-k}|\mathcal{C}_{Q}(n)|
.

Theorem
1.1
gives the following ground-state bound.

Proposition 6.1
.

There exists a constant
\delta_{Q}>0
such that

\min_{\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}^{[n]}}E_{Q,n}^{\mathrm{cent}}(\sigma)\leq-\delta_{Q}n^{2}+O_{Q}(n).

Equivalently,

\min_{\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}^{[n]}}E_{Q,n}(\sigma)\leq\bigl(\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)-\delta_{Q}\bigr)n^{2}+O_{Q}(n).

Proof.

The second inequality is exactly Theorem
1.1
, written in the spin
notation. Subtracting

2^{-k}|\mathcal{C}_{Q}(n)|=\gamma_{\mathrm{rand}}(Q)n^{2}+O_{Q}(n)

gives the centered form.
∎

The centered Hamiltonian has an even-spin expansion. For a fixed hyperedge

e
,

\delta_{e}(\sigma)=\prod_{i\in e}\frac{1+\sigma_{i}}{2}+\prod_{i\in e}\frac{1-\sigma_{i}}{2}=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq e\\
|S|\ \mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\in S}\sigma_{i}.

Therefore

E_{Q,n}^{\mathrm{cent}}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{C}_{Q}(n)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq e\\
|S|\ \mathrm{even}\\
|S|\geq 2\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\in S}\sigma_{i}.

Thus the centered energy is an even multi-spin Hamiltonian with deterministic
arithmetic couplings. Proposition
6.1
says that its
ground-state energy lies below the random-coloring main term by order
n^{2}
.
This spin-system consequence is deterministic: the couplings are induced by the
arithmetic incidence structure of
Q
-constellations, rather than by a random
graph or random hypergraph.
