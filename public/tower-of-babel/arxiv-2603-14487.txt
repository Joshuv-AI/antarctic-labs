First-return time in fractional kinetics Issue: 6 Publication type: research-article
M.Marcus Dahlenburg, G.Gianni Pagnini cor2 label=e2]gpagnini@bcamath.org \orcid 0000-0001-9917-4614

Abstract
The first-return time is the time that it takes a random walker to go back to the initial position for the first time. We study the first-return time when random walkers perform fractional kinetics, specifically fractional diffusion, that is modelled within the framework of the continuous-time random walk on homogeneous space in the uncoupled formulation with Mittag–Leffler distributed waiting-times. We consider both Markovian and non-Markovian settings, as well as any kind of symmetric jump-size distributions, namely with finite or infinite variance. We show that the first-return time density is indeed independent of the jump-size distribution when it is symmetric, and therefore it is affected only by the waiting-time distribution that embodies the memory of the process. We perform our analysis in two cases: first jump then wait and first wait then jump , and we provide several exact results, including the relation between results in the Markovian and non-Markovian settings and the difference between the two cases.

1
Introduction

Fractional kinetics
[
43
,
23
]

concerns particles’ motion
whose random displacements follow
a probability density function that evolves
according to equations including fractional calculus operators.
In a sloppy way, we can say that fractional kinetics concerns
processes that follow a
non-local generalisation of the Fokker–Planck equation.
A noteworthy example is fractional diffusion
[
29
]
.

The emerging evolution equation in fractional diffusion
generalises the heat diffusion equation by replacing
the first derivative in time and/or
the second derivative in space by the corresponding fractional derivatives

[
17
]
.
As a consequence,
the underlying random walk model is not the classical random walk
leading to the Brownian motion but its generalisation
characterised by power-law distributions.
Such generalised random walk is the continuous-time random walk (CTRW)

[
37
]

that extends classical random walk by replacing a fixed time-step
with a random interval between consecutive jumps.
These random intervals are called waiting-times and,
in the basic formulation
[
37
]
,
are assumed to be i.i.d. random variables and independent
of the jump-sizes, which also are assumed to be i.i.d. random variables.
When the distribution of the waiting-times displays a power-law,
then the evolution equation of the displacement density function
contains time-fractional derivatives.
On the other side, when the
distribution of the jump-sizes displays a power-law,
then such evolution equation contains space-fractional derivatives.
In this second case, the corresponding random walk is
called Lévy flight
[
5
]
.
When the jump-size distribution displays power-law then
the variance of the jumps and that of the walker’s density function
are infinite.
When the waiting-time distribution displays a power-law and
time-fractional derivatives appear, then the process is
non-Markovian. The process is striclty Markovian when
the waiting-time distribution is exponential

[
53
,
32
]
,
by the way this constrain can be relaxed and consider as
Markovian all those processes with finite mean waiting-times
and non-Markovian those with infinite mean waiting-times.
Here, we study the first-return time when both
waiting-time and jump-size distribution may display power-law.

The first-return time is the time that it takes a random walker
to come back for the first time to the starting location.
This kind of problem has a quite important application in the
animal kingdom because it is linked to concepts as
site fidelity, breeding, social associations, optimal foraging

[
18
]
.
Mathematically, it is strongly related with the first-passage time problem

[
41
]
.
The main result in first-passage time problems is
the celebrated Sparre Andersen theorem
[
46
]
,
which concerns the survival probability on the half-line
conditioned on the walker’s initial position
for symmetric Markovian random walks with fixed time-steps
in the presence of an absorbing boundary.
Its importance lays on the fact that it states that
the conditional survival probability
is independent of the jump-size distribution
when the walker’s trajectory starts from the location
of the absorbing boundary, thus, it is universal.
As a matter of fact, we show in this paper
that this universality of first-passage time problems
is reflected also in first-return time problems.
Therefore, the results derived here are indeed
independent of the tails of the walkers’ density function
and so the process can be governed by a Gaussian or by a Lévy stable density,
as well.
On the other side, the system is affected indeed by memory effects.
Therefore, the results display different behaviour between
Markovian, namely with exponentially distributed waiting-times

[
53
,
32
]
,
and non-Markovian frameworks.

In particular,
we report here that in discrete-time random walks,
which are Markovian by construction,
when a nearest-neighbour jump-law is taken into account
then the probability mass function of the first-return time
is linearly proportional to that of the first-passage time

[
41
,
26
,
19
]
.
As it is discussed in the following, see formula (
32
),
this linear proportionality holds also
when the assumed jump-law is different of
the nearest-neighbour rule.
However, since the general framework of the CTRW is considered here,
we study in addition the specific non-Markovian setting
generated by Mittag–Leffler distributed waiting-times
and we determine and investigate also the difference
emerging between Markovian and non-Markovian formulations.

The formulation of the CTRW is dependent on the choice about
the starting instant for measuring the elapsed time.
In fact, the mesurement of the duration of the first-return time
can start synchronised with the first jump,
and we label this case as the case
first jump then wait
,
or it can start independently of the process such that
the first jump occurs after the first random waiting-time,
and we label this case as the case
first wait then jump
.
We consider both cases, because,
even if formulae relating Markovian and non-Markovian frameworks are equal,
exact results in the Markovian setting differ between the two cases,
and so in the non-Markovian setting. Moreover,
we also quantify this difference.

We derive exact results in the Markovian setting and
we provide the relation between the Markovian and the non-Markovian setting
such that non-Markovian results
for Mittag–Leffler distributed waiting-times
are also exact and fully determined.
The derivation of the result is based on the Sparre Andersen theorem
and it exploits the Laplace transform method.
In particular, we provide some preliminary notions and results in
Section
2
where the Sparre Andersen theorem
is reminded and we also briefly remind the theory of
CTRW
[
37
]
.
This allows us to state the Sparre Andersen theorem in the framework of
the CTRW.
In this section,
we also derive integral (
27
),
that is the main formula for the calculation of the remaining results.
In Section
3
, first we formulate the problem of
first-return time and later we derive exact results both in
the case
first jump then wait
and
first wait then jump
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
The Sparre Andersen theorem

Let
\mathbb{R}
be the set of real numbers,
we denote by
\mathbb{R}^{+}
and by
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}

the set of positive real numbers
and the set of non-negative real numbers, e.g.,
the half-line of spatial excursions excluded the origin

\mathbb{R}^{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}|x>0\}
and
the elapsed times

\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}=\{t\in\mathbb{R}|t\geq 0\}
.
Analogously, we denote by
\mathbb{R}^{-}
and
by
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{-}
the set of negative real numbers
and the set of non-positive real numbers. Let furthermore

\mathbb{N}
be the set of natural numbers and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
Let
S_{n}=X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}
be the sum of
n\in\mathbb{N}

i.i.d. random variables
X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then the first ladder epoch

\{\mathcal{T}=n\}=\{S_{1}\geq 0,\dots,S_{n-1}\geq 0,S_{n}<0\}

is the epoch of the first entry of the walker
into the negative semi-axis
\mathbb{R}^{-}
.
By adding a constant
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
to all terms,
we obtain a random walk with initial position at
x_{0}
.
The probability for a walker started at
S_{0}=x_{0}
to remain
in the initial half-axis after
n
steps,
that is the probability for
\mathcal{T}
to be larger than
n
,
is called survival probability conditioned on the initial position
and the site
x=0
is termed as the location of an absorbing barrier.
Thus, let the survival probability
conditioned on the initial position
be denoted by
\phi_{n}:\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to[0,1]\,,\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
,
then for a walker started at
S_{0}=x_{0}

we have
\phi_{n}(x_{0})=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}>n)
and it yields

\int_{0}^{+\infty}k(z-x_{0})\,\phi_{n}(z)\,dz=\phi_{n+1}(x_{0})\,,\quad x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\,,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,,

(1)

with initial condition
\phi_{0}(\xi)=1\,,\forall\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
,
where
k:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
is the probability density function of the
i.i.d. random variables
X_{n}
,
\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Within the framework of the CTRW,
the density function
k(x)
turns into the jump-size distribution.

Integral equation (
1
) is known as Wiener–Hopf
equation
[
12
]
.
A first connection between random walks with an absorbing barrier and
Wiener–Hopf integrals was pointed out by D.V. Lindley in 1952

[
28
]
.
For a more general relations between
Wiener–Hopf integrals and probabilistic problems,
see, e.g., reference
[
13
, Sections XII.3a and XVIII.3]
.
However, as observed by W. Feller,
the connections between first-passage problems and the
Wiener–Hopf integrals
"are not as close as they are usually made
to appear"

[
13
, Introduction to Chapter XII]
.
For more recent papers, see, e.g., references

[
21
,
15
,
34
,
36
,
3
,
35
,
7
]
.
A general solution to the Wiener–Hopf equation (
1
)
is known in the literature
with the name of Pollaczek–Spitzer formula
[
3
]
.
This name refers to a formula,
see
[
49
, theorem 5, formula (4.6)]
and

[
3
, formula (12)]
,
derived by F. Spitzer in 1957
[
49
, theorem 3, formula (3.1)]

on the basis of an auxiliary formula obtained by F. Pollaczek in 1952

[
40
, formula (8)]
but through a different method.
It is possible to show that problem (
1
) is
equivalent to a Sturm–Liouville problem with proper
boundary conditions
[
8
]
,
and this is also an alternative and easier approach
with respect to the Pollaczek–Spitzer formula for the calculation
of the survival probability.
In particular, one of the required boundary conditions
for the Sturm–Liouville system emerged to be given by the
Sparre Andersen theorem.
Moreover,
the Sparre Andersen theorem can be derived
on the basis of formula (
1
)
under the assumption that
\phi_{n+1}(\cdot)

is of bounded variation
[
14
]
.
Actually, the Sparre Andersen theorem

[
46
]
is a fundamental result
in the study of the first-passage time problems.

Originally established in 1954

[
46
]
, the Sparre Andersen theorem states

\phi_{n}(0)=2^{-2n}\binom{2n}{n}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\pi}}\,,\quad n\to\infty\,.

(2)

Thereby, the Sparre Andersen formula (
2
)
is valid for arbitrary but symmetric
jump-size distribution, i.e.,
k(\xi)=k(-\xi)
with
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
.
In other words,
the Sparre Andersen theorem
[
46
]

provides the exact survival probability
conditioned on the initial position
for symmetric Markovian random walkers starting in the same location
of the absorbing barrier and states that
it is independent of the jump-size distribution
k(x)

(whenever it is assumed to be symmetric).
See, for example, F. Spitzer
[
48
]
and
W. Feller
[
13
, Section XII.7]
.
This independency uncovers a universal nature of the Sparre Andersen theorem
that is reflected also in other results, see, e.g.,

[
4
,
24
,
25
,
36
,
10
,
38
,
27
]
.
Thus, we refer to this independency as
the
universality
of the Sparre Andersen theorem.
Sparre Andersen formula (
2
) is indeed one of the many results
emerging from a deep
corpus studiorum
on random walks
based on combinatorial arguments
by E. Sparre Andersen

[
44
,
45
,
47
,
46
]

and F. Spitzer
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
.
For more recent and general results
on the basis of probabilistic arguments, see, e.g.,
reference
[
2
]
.
The general formulation of the first-passage time problem
for processes with discrete-time is available
in the literature

[
3
,
35
]
.

2.2
From the discrete- to the continuous-time setting

The survival probability problem can be formulated also in
the continuous-time setting.
In particular, we consider the CTRW
on homogeneous and continuous space in the uncoupled formulation
as originally introduced by Montroll & Weiss in the year 1965

[
37
]
.
This means that
the i.i.d. random waiting-times and the i.i.d. random jump-sizes are,
at any epoch, independent of each other and also
of the current position and time.
Therefore, in analogy with the discrete-time setting,
the walker’s position after
n
iterations,
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
, is given by the sum of
n

i.i.d. random variables
X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
distributed according to the
jump-size distribution
k(x)
, i.e.,
S_{n}=x_{0}+X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}

where
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
is the initial position.
The actual time
t\geq 0
is given
by the sum of
n
i.i.d. random waiting-times
\tau_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}

between two consecutive jumps, i.e.,

t=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tau_{j}
with initial instant
t=0
.
Moreover, let the survival probability
conditioned on the initial position in the continuous-time setting
be denoted by
\Lambda:\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to[0,1]
.
Thus, by using the same approach adopted to compute
the walker’s density function in the CTRW theory

[
37
]
,
the conditional survival probability
\Lambda

is given by the weighted superposition of all
the possible discrete-time counterparts
\phi_{n}(\cdot)

and it results in the series

\Lambda(x_{0},t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\phi_{n}(x_{0})\,\Psi_{n}(t)\,,\quad x_{0},t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\,,

(3)

where the index
n
counts the number of occurred jumps
and
\Psi_{n}(\cdot)

is the probability to have an elapsed time
equals to
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
after
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
jumps such that

\Psi_{n}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\Psi_{n-1}(t-\tau)\psi(\tau)\,d\tau\,,\quad\Psi_{0}(t)=\Psi(t)=1-\int_{0}^{t}\psi(\tau)d\tau\,,

(4)

and
\psi:\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}

is the distribution of the waiting-times.

For the present purposes,
we pass to the Laplace domain
and we obtain for formula (
3
)

\mathcal{L}\{\Lambda(x_{0},t);s\}=\widetilde{\Lambda}(x_{0},s)=\widetilde{\Psi}(s)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\phi_{n}(x_{0})\,\left[\widetilde{\psi}(s)\right]^{n}\,,

(5)

where
\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}\{g(t);s\}:=\widetilde{g}(s)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\,{\rm e}^{-st}\,g(t)\,dt}

is the Laplace transform of a sufficiently well-behaving function
g(t)
.
Hence, after splitting formula (
5
) in

\widetilde{\Lambda}(x_{0},s)=\widetilde{\Psi}(s)\phi_{0}(x_{0})+\widetilde{\Psi}(s)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi_{n}(x_{0})\,\left[\widetilde{\psi}(s)\right]^{n}\,,

(6)

by recalling that
\phi_{0}(x_{0})=1\,,\forall\,x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\,,
and
by expressing
\phi_{n}(\cdot)
through the
Wiener–Hopf integral equation (
1
),
from formula (
6
) it results that

\widetilde{\Lambda}(x_{0},s)=\widetilde{\Psi}(s)+\widetilde{\psi}(s)\int_{0}^{+\infty}k(\xi-x_{0})\widetilde{\Lambda}(\xi,s)\,d\xi\,.

(7)

Finally, after the inverse Laplace
transformation
g(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{\widetilde{g}(s);t\}
,
the equation for the survival probability is

\Lambda(x_{0},t)=\Psi(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\psi(t-\zeta)\int_{0}^{+\infty}k(\xi-x_{0})\Lambda(\xi,\zeta)\,d\xi d\zeta\,,\quad x_{0},t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\,,

(8)

that is the analogue with continuous-time of formula (
1
),
see also references

[
7
,
8
,
42
]
,
and we recover
\Lambda(x_{0},0)=1\,,\forall\,x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\,,
because
by definition (
4
) it holds
\Psi(0)=1
.

2.3
The Sparre Andersen theorem in the framework of the CTRW

The Sparre Andersen theorem can also be put in the framework
of the uncoupled CTRW,
in particular, through formula (
3
).
In fact, if we set
x_{0}=0
in formula (
5
)
and from the Sparre Anderson formula (
2
) for the discrete-time setting
we take the expression of
\phi_{n}(0)
,
then

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Lambda}(0,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}(s)\,\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}2^{-2n}\binom{2n}{n}\,\left[\widetilde{\psi}(s)\right]^{n}

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\Psi}(s)}{\sqrt{1-\widetilde{\psi}(s)}}=\frac{\sqrt{s\,\widetilde{\Psi}(s)}}{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\sqrt{1-\widetilde{\psi}(s)}\,,

where in the second line we used the series

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-2n}\binom{2n}{n}z^{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-z}}\,,\quad|z|<1\,.

(10)

Formula (
9
) was already reported to some extent

[
1
, formula (7)]
but not formally derived, yet.
By applying the initial and final value theorems we have

\Lambda(0,0)=\lim_{s\to\infty}s\widetilde{\Lambda}(0,s)=1\,,

(11a)

\Lambda(0,\infty)=\lim_{s\to 0}s\widetilde{\Lambda}(0,s)=0\,,

(11b)

because
\widetilde{\psi}(0)=1
and
\widetilde{\psi}(\infty)=0
.
After the Laplace anti-transformation of the first line of formula
(
9
),
the Sparre Andersen theorem reads

\Lambda(0,t)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}2^{-2n}\binom{2n}{n}\,\Psi_{n}(t)\,.

(12)

For Markovian random walks,
we have that
\psi(t)=\psi_{\rm M}(t)={\rm e}^{-t}

[
53
,
32
]
, such that

\widetilde{\psi}_{\rm{M}}(s)=\widetilde{\Psi}_{\rm{M}}(s)=\frac{1}{1+s}\,.

(13)

and then formula (
9
) reads

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{\rm{M}}(0,s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s(1+s)}}\,.

(14)

Consequently, formula (
12
) for the Markovian case
gives in the original domain

[
42
]

{\Lambda_{\rm{M}}}(0,t)={\rm e}^{-t/2}\,I_{0}(t/2)\,,

(15)

where
I_{0}(\cdot)

is the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order
0

defined by the series

I_{0}(\zeta)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\zeta^{2}/4)^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\,,\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\,,

(16)

with

I_{0}(0)=1\,,\quad\left.\frac{dI_{0}(\zeta)}{d\zeta}\right|_{\zeta=0}=0\,,\quad\left.\frac{d^{2}I_{0}(\zeta)}{d\zeta^{2}}\right|_{\zeta=0}=\frac{1}{2}\,.

(17)

From
formula (
14
) we have in the long-time limit, i.e.,
s\to 0
,
that
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{\rm{M}}(0,s)\sim 1/\sqrt{s}
and after the Laplace inversion it gives

{\Lambda_{\rm{M}}}(0,t)\sim 1/\sqrt{t}
for
t\to+\infty
.
This last limit is
the continuous-time counter-part of limit (
2
).

For non-Markovian random walks,
we consider the specific model
[
20
]

\psi(t)=t^{\beta-1}E_{\beta,\beta}(-t^{\beta})\,,\quad\Psi(t)=E_{\beta,1}(-t^{\beta})=E_{\beta}(-t^{\beta})\,,

(18)

where
E_{\beta,\alpha}(z)
is the Mittag–Leffler function

[
33
, Appendix E]

E_{\beta,\alpha}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{j}}{\Gamma(\beta j+\alpha)}\,,\quad{\rm Re}\{\beta\}>0\,,\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\,,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\,,

(19)

that in the Laplace domain correspond to

\widetilde{\psi}(s)=\frac{1}{1+s^{\beta}}\,,\quad\widetilde{\Psi}(s)=\frac{s^{\beta-1}}{1+s^{\beta}}\,.

(20)

Hence, formula (
9
) reads

\widetilde{\Lambda}(0,s)=\frac{s^{\beta/2-1}}{\sqrt{1+s^{\beta}}}\,.

(21)

We observe that it holds

\widetilde{\Lambda}(0,s)=s^{\beta-1}\widetilde{\Lambda}_{\rm{M}}(0,s^{\beta})\,,

(22)

and by applying the Efros formula
[
11
,
51
]
,
i.e.,

\mathcal{L}^{-1}\!\left\{v(s)\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}[q(s)]\right\}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{W}(\zeta)\,\mathcal{L}^{-1}\!\left\{v(s){\rm e}^{-\zeta q(s)}\right\}\,d\zeta\,,

(23)

with
v(s)=s^{\beta-1}
and
q(s)=s^{\beta}
,
in the original domain we have

\Lambda(0,t)=t^{-\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\zeta/2}I_{0}(\zeta/2)M_{\beta}(\zeta/t^{\beta})\,d\zeta\,,

(24)

where
M_{\beta}(\cdot)
is the Mainardi/Wright function
defined by the series

[
33
, Appendix F]

M_{\nu}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\frac{z^{j}}{\Gamma[-\nu j+(1-\nu)]}\,,\quad 0<\nu<1\,,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\,,

(25)

whose Laplace transform is known in the literature

[
29
, (4.26)]
.
From formula (
21
) we have
that
\widetilde{\Lambda}(0,s)\sim s^{\beta/2-1}
, when
s\to 0
,
then, after the Laplace inversion it holds

\Lambda(0,t)\sim 1/t^{\beta/2}
for
t\to+\infty
.

Moreover, after reminding that the
unconditional survival probability is determined by the integral

\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\xi)\Lambda(\xi,t)\,d\xi}
,
where
\rho:\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
is the
destribution of the initial position,
then we can state

Theorem 1
.

The unconditional survival probability of a symmetric CTRW is
independent of the jump-size distribution if this
is equal to the distribution of the initial position.

Proof.

From the third line of formula (
9
) we have

\widetilde{\Psi}(s)=s\widetilde{\Lambda}^{2}(0,s)
and,
after summing and subtracting
\widetilde{\Lambda}(0,s)
, we can derive

\widetilde{\Lambda}(0,s)=\widetilde{\Psi}(s)+\widetilde{\Lambda}(0,s)\left[1-s\widetilde{\Lambda}(0,s)\right]\,,

(26)

that, after the comparison against formula (
7
) with
x_{0}=0
,
gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}k(\xi)\widetilde{\Lambda}(\xi,s)\,d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\Lambda}(0,s)}{\widetilde{\psi}(s)}\left[1-s\widetilde{\Lambda}(0,s)\right]

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\Psi}(s)}{\widetilde{\psi}(s)}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\widetilde{\psi}(s)}}-1\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{1-\widetilde{\psi}(s)}-1+\widetilde{\psi}(s)}{s\,\widetilde{\psi}(s)}\,,

that is independent of the jump-size distribution
k(x)

as a consequence of the universality of the
Sparre Andersen theorem (
2
) when plugged into
(
3
).
∎

3
First-return time for CTRW

3.1
Problem formulation and definitions

The duration of excursions of a random walker
until its first comeback to the starting position is called
first-return time (FRT).
This observable can be understood with an example from
the animal kingdom in terms of the
return of an animal to a previously occupied area
as the duration of the flight of birds
when they leave and then return to their nests.
This is a wide-spread behaviour associated
with a number of ecological processes as site fidelity,
breeding, social associations, optimal foraging
[
18
]

and successfully modelled also by fractional kinetics

[
16
,
52
]
.

The FRT for the uncouple CTRW reported at the beginning
of Subsection §
2.2

can be exactly calculated by using integral formula (
27
).
In particular, we can exactly calculate
the FRT for the fractional kinetics emerging
from a CTRW with Mittag–Leffler distributed waiting-times
(
18
).
In this Section,
we consider a random walk starting at the origin
x=0
and,
provided that the first jump away is long
\xi
,
we are interested in how much time it takes
to the random walker to pass through the origin
for the first time after the departure.
As a matter of fact,
the problem is equivalent to a first-passage time problem
that starts at the initial instant
\tau_{0}

from the random first-landing site
\xi

and the associated absorbing barrier is located in the origin
x=0
.
Actually, the first-landing site
\xi
is
distributed according to the jump-size distribution
k(\xi)

and
\tau_{0}
is the delay elapsed between the two locations
x=0
and
x=\xi
.
The CTRW formalism allows for two different formulations
of the problem:

first jump then wait
(jw), such that
\tau_{0}=0
, and

first wait then jump
(wj),
such that
\tau_{0}
is a random variable
distributed according to
\psi(t)
.
Thus, by following the literature

[
9
,
6
,
22
]
,
we define the FRT as follows:

\text{FRT}\equiv\begin{cases}\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(\xi)+\tau_{0}\,;&\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{-}\,,\\
&\\
\mathcal{T}_{r}(\xi)+\tau_{0}\,;&\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\,,\end{cases}

(28)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the first-passage time and,
in particular,
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
is the first-passage time
when the first jump away from the origin
is towards the left into the position
\xi<0
and

\mathcal{T}_{r}
when it is towards the right into the position
\xi>0
.

Let
\displaystyle{\lambda(\xi,t)=-\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial t}}

be the first-passage time density
conditioned to the starting position
\xi
and
satisfying the normalisation condition

\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda(\xi,t)\,dt=1}
,
then the density function of the first-passage time
weighted over all the possibile first-jump landing positions results to be

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}k(\xi)\lambda(\xi,t)\,d\xi

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{+\infty}k(\xi)\lambda(\xi,t)\,d\xi\,,

where the symmetry property of the jump-size distribution

k(\xi)=k(-\xi)
is used.

Thus, since
\mathcal{T}
and
\tau_{0}
are statistically independent,
we have that the density function of their sum,
namely the FRT (
28
), is

\mathcal{P}^{\rm jw}(t)=f(t)\,,\quad{\rm when}\quad\tau_{0}=0\,,

(30a)

\mathcal{P}^{\rm wj}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\psi(t-\zeta)\,\mathcal{P}^{\rm jw}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\,,\quad{\rm when}\quad\tau_{0}\sim\psi(t)\,.

(30b)

An alternative formulation is possible by starting from the
discrete-time setting with the extension to the continuous-time setting
in the same fashion of formula (
3
)

[
1
]
.
In particular, by defining the discrete-time
first-passage time density

\lambda_{n}(\xi)=\phi_{n-1}(\xi)-\phi_{n}(\xi)
,
with
n\geq 1
, and exploiting the Sparre Andersen theorem (
2
)
at
\xi=0
, we have
[
41
]

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-2(n-1)}\binom{2(n-1)}{n-1}-2^{-2n}\binom{2n}{n}

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-2(n-1)}\binom{2n}{n}\left\{\frac{n}{4n-2}-\frac{1}{4}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-2(n-1)}\binom{2n}{n}\frac{1}{2(4n-2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{-2n}}{2n-1}\binom{2n}{n}\,,

that correctly gives
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,\lambda_{n}(0)=1
.
Moreover, by following definition (
29
),
in the discrete-time setting we have

\displaystyle f_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}k(\xi)\lambda_{n}(\xi)\,d\xi

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}k(\xi)\left[\phi_{n-1}(\xi)-\phi_{n}(\xi)\right]\,d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left[\phi_{n}(0)-\phi_{n+1}(0)\right]=2\lambda_{n+1}(0)\,,\quad n\geq 1\,,

where in the third line we used (
1
), and it holds

f_{0}=0
.

3.2
The (jw)-case

We remind that in this case
\mathcal{P}^{\rm jw}(t)=f(t)

(
30a
), thus in the following we study the density function
f(t)
.

In particular,
thanks to the relation
in the Laplace domain between the
first-passage time density and the corresponding survival probability,
i.e.,
\widetilde{\lambda}(\xi,s)=1-s\,\widetilde{\Lambda}(\xi,s)
,
from (
29
) we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{+\infty}k(\xi)\widetilde{\lambda}(\xi,s)\,d\xi

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-2s\int_{0}^{\infty}k(\xi)\widetilde{\Lambda}(\xi,s)\,d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-2\,\frac{\sqrt{1-\widetilde{\psi}(s)}-1+\widetilde{\psi}(s)}{\widetilde{\psi}(s)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2-\widetilde{\psi}(s)-2\sqrt{1-\widetilde{\psi}(s)}}{\widetilde{\psi}(s)}\,,

where formula (
27
) has been used in
the third line.
It can be checked that the normalisation condition
\widetilde{f}(0)=1

is met because
\widetilde{\psi}(0)=1
.
We underline that,
since formula (
27
) from Theorem
1
has been used
for deriving (
33
),
we have the following

Remark 1
.

The density function of the FRT of a symmetric CTRW in the (jw)-case
is independent of the jump-size distribution.

Moreover, if we consider the non-Markovian setting (
20
),
from formula (
33
)
we finally obtain that the Laplace transform of
the density function of the FRT for a CTRW
(jw)-type is

\widetilde{f}(s)=1+2s^{\beta}-2\sqrt{s^{\beta}}\sqrt{s^{\beta}+1}\,.

(34)

When
\beta=1
the system is Markovian and therefore,
by denoting with
f_{\rm M}(t)
the
corresponding density of the FRT,
the following equality in the Laplace domain holds

\widetilde{f}(s)=\widetilde{f}_{\rm M}(s^{\beta})\,.

(35)

Hence, we can state

Theorem 2
.

Let
f(t)
and
f_{\rm M}(t)
be the density functions of the FRT
in the
(jw)
-case for a symmetric CTRW
with Mittag–Leffler (
18
) and
exponentially distributed waiting-times, respectively,
then it holds

f(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta^{-1/\beta}\ell_{\beta}(t/\zeta^{1/\beta})f_{\rm M}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\,,\quad 0<\beta<1\,,

(36)

where
\ell_{\beta}(t)
is the one-sided Lévy stable density
characterised by the Laplace transform
\displaystyle{{\rm e}^{-s^{\beta}}}
.

Proof.

By reminding the Efros theorem (
23
),
formula (
35
) can be inverted
by setting
v(s)=1
and
q(s)=s^{\beta}
such that we have
the integral relation (
36
).
∎

We observe that from (
36
) the Markovian case is recovered
when
\beta=1
because
\ell_{1}(t)=\delta(t-1)
.

In the Markovian case with
\beta=1
,
from (
34
) we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}_{\rm M}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+s)\,\frac{1+2s-2\sqrt{s}\sqrt{s+1}}{1+s}

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+s}+2s\left[\frac{1}{1+s}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{s(1+s)}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+s\,\left\{\frac{1}{1+s}+2s\left[\frac{1}{1+s}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{s(1+s)}}\right]\right\}\,.

Thus, by remembering the properties of the Laplace transform,
and also the Laplace transform pair (
14
)-(
15
),
we can calculate the exact result

\displaystyle f_{\rm M}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-t/2}\left[I_{0}(\zeta)-\frac{dI_{0}(\zeta)}{d\zeta}\right]_{\zeta=t/2}-{\rm e}^{-t}

(38)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{d}{dt}\left\{{\rm e}^{-t/2}\left[I_{0}(\zeta)-\frac{dI_{0}(\zeta)}{d\zeta}\right]_{\zeta=t/2}-{\rm e}^{-t}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\rm e}^{-t/2}\left[I_{0}(\zeta)-\frac{d^{2}I_{0}(\zeta)}{d\zeta^{2}}\right]_{\zeta=t/2}\,,

and then the integral formula (
36
) is fully determined.

From (
38
), by using the values given
in (
17
), we have that

f_{\rm M}(0)=\frac{1}{4}\,,

(39)

and, when plugged in (
29
),
it gives the following universal result independent of
k(\xi)

f_{\rm M}(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}k(\xi)\lambda_{\rm M}(\xi,0)\,d\xi=\frac{1}{4}\,,

(40)

in opposition to the first-passage time density that
is indeed dependent on
k(\xi)
also at
t=0
according to

\displaystyle{\lambda_{\rm M}(\xi,0)=\int_{\xi}^{\infty}k(y)dy}
.
In the non-Markovian case it holds

f(0)=+\infty\,,

(41)

in analogy with the first-passage time density
\lambda(\xi,0)
.
In fact,
after the change of variable
\zeta=(t/\chi)^{\beta}
in (
36
), we have

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta t^{\beta-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ell_{\beta}(\chi)f_{\rm M}(t^{\beta}/\chi^{\beta})\,\frac{d\chi}{\chi^{\beta}}

(42)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle C\,t^{\beta-1}\,,\quad t\to 0\,,\quad{\rm with}\quad C=\frac{\beta}{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ell_{\beta}(\chi)\,\frac{d\chi}{\chi^{\beta}}\,.

From (
34
) we can derive the asymptotic behaviour for
large elapsed times.
Actually, we have
\widetilde{f}(s)\sim 1-2s^{\beta/2}
when
s\to 0

that is consistent with the behaviour of

\displaystyle{{\rm e}^{-2s^{\beta/2}}}
for small
s
and gives

f(t)\sim\frac{1}{2^{2/\beta}}\,\ell_{\beta/2}(t/2^{2/\beta})\sim\frac{2}{t^{\beta/2+1}}\,,\quad t\to+\infty\,.

(43)

From (
43
) it follows that the mean FRT in the (jw)-case
is infinite both in Markovian and non-Markovian setting
but the right tail of the density function decreases
slower in non-Markovian systems.
Because of formula (
29
),
we have that scaling (
43
) is also the scaling
of the corresponding first-passage time density

\lambda(\xi,t)

[
22
]
,
notwithstanding the exact functions may differ.

Tools of fractional calculus can be used for studying the
integral formula (
36
). In fact,
let
J^{\mu}
, with
\mu>0
, be the Riemann–Liouville fractional integral
defined by the Laplace symbol
s^{-\mu}

[
17
]
,
then in the Laplace domain it holds

\mathcal{L}\{J^{1-\beta}f(t);s\}=\frac{\widetilde{f}(s)}{s^{1-\beta}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm e}^{-\zeta s^{\beta}}}{s^{1-\beta}}f_{\rm M}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\,,

(44)

that, by using the formula

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}\{t^{-\beta}M_{\beta}(\zeta t^{-\beta});s\}=s^{\beta-1}{\rm e}^{-\zeta s^{\beta}}}
, with
{\rm Re}\{s\}>0
,

[
29
, (4.26)]
, gives

J^{1-\beta}f(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{\beta}}M_{\beta}(\zeta/t^{\beta})f_{\rm M}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\,.

(45)

Since from the study of time-fractional diffusion equations

[
29
]

we have that
\lim_{t\to 0}t^{-\beta}M_{\beta}(\zeta/t^{\beta})=\delta(\zeta)
,
then it holds

\left.J^{1-\beta}f(t)\right|_{t=0}=f_{\rm M}(0)=\frac{1}{4}\,.

(46)

Furthermore,
we can also have the Laplace transform of the cumulative density function

\displaystyle{F^{\rm jw}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(\zeta)d\zeta}
that,
by using (
35
), leads to the equality

\widetilde{F}^{\rm jw}(s)=\frac{\widetilde{f}(s)}{s}=s^{\beta-1}\frac{\widetilde{f}_{\rm M}(s^{\beta})}{s^{\beta}}=s^{\beta-1}\widetilde{F}^{\rm jw}_{\rm M}(s^{\beta})\,.

(47)

Therefore, we can state the following

Theorem 3
.

Let
F^{\rm jw}(t)
and
F^{\rm jw}_{\rm M}(t)

be the cumulative density functions of the FRT in the
(jw)
-case
for a symmetric CTRW
with Mittag–Leffler (
18
) and
exponentially distributed waiting-times, respectively,
then it holds

F^{\rm jw}(t)=t^{-\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}M_{\beta}(\zeta/t^{\beta})F^{\rm jw}_{\rm M}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\,,\quad 0<\beta<1\,,

(48)

where
M_{\beta}(\zeta)
is the Mainardi/Wright function
defined in (
25
).

Proof.

By applying Efros formula (
23
) in analogy with
the pair (
22
) and (
24
),
we have (
48
).
∎

To conclude, we remind the formula

[
31
, (6.3)]

t^{-\beta}M_{\beta}(\zeta/t^{\beta})=t^{-\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}M_{\eta}(\zeta/y^{\eta})M_{\nu}(y/t^{\nu})\,\frac{dy}{y^{\eta}}\,,\quad\beta=\nu\eta\,,

(49)

and then
from Theorem
3
we have the following

Corollary 1
.

The relation between the cumulative density function
with anomalous parameter
\beta
and that of parameter

\eta>\beta
is:

F^{\rm jw}(t;\beta)=t^{-\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}M_{\nu}(\zeta/t^{\nu})F^{\rm jw}(\zeta;\eta)\,d\zeta\,,\quad\beta=\nu\eta\,.

(50)

Proof.

By using (
49
) in (
48
) we have (
50
).
∎

Remark 2
.

An interesting special case of formula (
50
) is obtained when
\nu=1/2
,
that is

F^{\rm jw}(t;\beta/2)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm e}^{-\zeta^{2}/(4t)}}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}F^{\rm jw}(\zeta;\beta)\,d\zeta\,,

(51)

where the identity
M_{1/2}(\zeta)={\rm e}^{-\zeta^{2}/4}/\sqrt{\pi}
has
been used.

A large number of other formulae can be obtained by making use
of the existing results for the Mainardi/Wright function, e.g.,

[
29
,
33
,
30
,
39
]
.

3.3
The (wj)-case

The FRT density function in the (wj)-case
(
30b
) can be studied in the Laplace domain.
In particular,
by using (
29
) and (
33
), from (
30b
) we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm wj}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}(s)\widetilde{f}(s)

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2-\widetilde{\psi}(s)-2\sqrt{1-\widetilde{\psi}(s)}\,.

It can be checked that the normalisation condition

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm wj}(0)=1
is met and
by applying the initial and the final value theorems it holds

\mathcal{P}^{\rm wj}(0)=\lim_{s\to+\infty}s\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm wj}(s)=0\,,

(53a)

\mathcal{P}^{\rm wj}(+\infty)=\lim_{s\to 0}s\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm wj}(s)=0\,,

(53b)

because
\widetilde{\psi}(0)=1
and
\widetilde{\psi}(+\infty)=0
.
In analogy with Remark
1
, we underline
also in this case that,
since Theorem
1
lays behind the derivation of (
52
),
we have the following

Remark 3
.

The density function of the FRT of a symmetric CTRW in the (wj)-case
is independent of the jump-size distribution.

Formulae (
53a
) and (
53b
) hold both
in the Markovian and non-Markovian case.
More explicitly, from (
20
) we have
that the density function of the FRT for a CTRW
(wj)-type is

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm wj}(s)=\frac{1+2s^{\beta}-2\sqrt{s^{\beta}}\sqrt{s^{\beta}+1}}{s^{\beta}+1}\,.

(54)

When
\beta=1
the system is Markovian and therefore,
by denoting with
\mathcal{P}_{\rm M}^{\rm wj}(t)
the
corresponding density of the FRT, in analogy with (
35
),
in the Laplace domain it holds

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm wj}(s)=\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm M}^{\rm wj}(s^{\beta})\,,

(55)

such that, by applying the Efros formula (
23
),
it can be inverted and we have

Theorem 4
.

Let
P^{\rm wj}(t)
and
P^{\rm wj}_{\rm M}(t)

be the density functions of the FRT in the
(wj)
-case
for a symmetric CTRW
with Mittag–Leffler (
18
) and
exponentially distributed waiting-times, respectively,
then it holds

\mathcal{P}^{\rm wj}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta^{-1/\beta}\ell_{\beta}(t/\zeta^{1/\beta})\,\mathcal{P}_{\rm M}^{\rm wj}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\,,\quad 0<\beta<1\,,

(56)

which recasts formula (
36
) for the (jw)-case.

In the Markovian case with
\beta=1
,
from (
54
) we have

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm wj}_{\rm M}(s)=\frac{1}{1+s}+2s\left[\frac{1}{1+s}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{s(1+s)}}\right]\,,

(57)

and, by remembering the properties of the Laplace transform
together with the Laplace transform pair (
14
)-(
15
),
we can calculate the exact result

\mathcal{P}_{\rm M}^{\rm wj}(t)={\rm e}^{-t/2}\left[I_{0}(\zeta)-\frac{dI_{0}(\zeta)}{d\zeta}\right]_{\zeta=t/2}-{\rm e}^{-t}\,,\quad\mathcal{P}_{\rm M}^{\rm wj}(0)=0\,,

(58)

such that the integral formula (
56
) is fully determined.

We can quantify the difference between the two cases (jw) and (wj).
In fact, first by comparing (
38
) and
(
58
) we have

Proposition 1
.

The relationship between the density functions of the FRT
with exponentially distributed waiting-times
in the
(wj)
- and
(jw)
-case is

\mathcal{P}_{\rm M}^{\rm wj}(t)=2\mathcal{P}_{\rm M}^{\rm jw}(t)-{\rm e}^{-t/2}\left[\frac{dI_{0}}{d\zeta}-\frac{d^{2}I_{0}}{d\zeta^{2}}\right]_{\zeta=t/2}-{\rm e}^{-t}\,.

(59)

Later, by using (
36
) and (
56
), we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\rm wj}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{P}^{\rm jw}(t)-\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta^{-1/\beta}\ell_{\beta}(t/\zeta^{1/\beta})\,{\rm e}^{-\zeta}\,d\zeta\qquad\qquad

(60)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta^{-1/\beta}\ell_{\beta}(t/\zeta^{1/\beta})\,{\rm e}^{-\zeta/2}\left[\frac{dI_{0}}{d\chi}-\frac{d^{2}I_{0}}{d\chi^{2}}\right]_{\chi=\zeta/2}\,d\zeta\,.

The second term in the r.h.s. can be solved and put in a more clear form.
In fact, by using some formula concerning
Lévy density function
[
29
, (4.26)]
,
Mainardi/Wright function
[
29
, (4.32)]

and Mittag–Leffler function
[
33
, (1.45)]
,
we have the following equalities

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta^{-1/\beta}\ell_{\beta}(t/\zeta^{1/\beta})\,{\rm e}^{-\zeta s^{\beta}}\,d\zeta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta}{t^{\beta}}M_{\beta}(\zeta/t^{\beta})\,{\rm e}^{-\zeta s^{\beta}}\,d\zeta

(61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{s^{1-\beta}}{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}M_{\beta}(y)\,{\rm e}^{-t^{\beta}s^{\beta}y}\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{s^{1-\beta}}{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}E_{\beta}(-t^{\beta}s^{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{\beta-1}E_{\beta,\beta}(-t^{\beta}s^{\beta})\,.

Thus, by remembering (
18
) and by setting
s=1
, it holds

\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta^{-1/\beta}\ell_{\beta}(t/\zeta^{1/\beta})\,{\rm e}^{-\zeta}\,d\zeta=\psi(t)\,,

(62)

such that

Proposition 2
.

The relationship between the density functions of the FRT
with Mittag–Leffler distributed waiting-times (
18
)
in the
(wj)
- and
(jw)
-case is

\displaystyle\!\!\mathcal{P}^{\rm wj}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{P}^{\rm jw}(t)-\psi(t)\qquad\qquad

(63)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta^{-1/\beta}\ell_{\beta}(t/\zeta^{1/\beta})\,{\rm e}^{-\zeta/2}\left[\frac{dI_{0}}{d\chi}-\frac{d^{2}I_{0}}{d\chi^{2}}\right]_{\chi=\zeta/2}\,d\zeta\,.

From (
54
) we can derive the asymptotic behaviour for
large elapsed times and, in analogy with (
43
),
it is

\mathcal{P}^{\rm wj}(t)\sim\frac{1}{2^{2/\beta}}\,\ell_{\beta/2}(t/2^{2/\beta})\sim\frac{2}{t^{\beta/2+1}}\,,\quad t\to+\infty\,,

(64)

from which it follows that the mean FRT also in the (wj)-case
is infinite both in Markovian
and non-Markovian setting and the right tail of the density function
decreases slower in non-Markovian systems.

The analogies between the (jw)- and the (wj)-case include
also formulae (
45
) and (
46
) that now read as follows

J^{1-\beta}\mathcal{P}^{\rm wj}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{\beta}}M_{\beta}(\zeta/t^{\beta})\mathcal{P}^{\rm wj}_{\rm M}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\,,

(65)

\left.J^{1-\beta}\mathcal{P}^{\rm wj}(t)\right|_{t=0}=\mathcal{P}^{\rm wj}_{\rm M}(0)=0\,,

(66)

as well as the analogue of Theorem
3
, that is

Theorem 5
.

Let
F^{\rm wj}(t)
and
F^{\rm wj}_{\rm M}(t)

be the cumulative density functions of the FRT in the
(jw)
-case
for a symmetric CTRW
with Mittag–Leffler (
18
) and
exponentially distributed waiting-times, respectively,
then it holds

F^{\rm wj}(t)=t^{-\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}M_{\beta}(\zeta/t^{\beta})F^{\rm wj}_{\rm M}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\,,\quad 0<\beta<1\,.

(67)

where
M_{\beta}(\zeta)
is the Mainardi/Wright function
defined in (
25
).

Proof.

Same proof of Theorem
3
.
∎

Moreover, from Theorem
5
and by using (
49
),
we have

Corollary 2
.

The relation between the cumulative density function
with anomalous parameter
\beta
and that of parameter

\eta>\beta
is:

F^{\rm wj}(t;\beta)=t^{-\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}M_{\nu}(\zeta/t^{\nu})F^{\rm wj}(\zeta;\eta)\,d\zeta\,,\quad\beta=\nu\eta\,,

which is the analogue of formula (

).

Same proof of Corollary

.
∎
