Severe Hypertriglyceridemia With Lipemia-Induced Analytical Interference
Authors: Anmolpreet Kaur, Ruhani Bali, Akash Batta, Bishav Mohan, Shitij Chaudhary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere hypertriglyceridemia can cause significant metabolic complications and spurious laboratory results, complicating clinical decision-making. Chylomicronemia syndromes, particularly multifactorial forms, are underrecognized, and may present with both pancreatitis and premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.Case SummaryA 51-year-old man with diabetes and hypertension presented with chest pain and recent hypertriglyceridemia-induced normoenzymatic pancreatitis. Ischemic electrocardiographic changes prompted coronary angiography. Preprocedural testing showed a prothrombin time reported as “infinite” (no clot detected). Serum appeared grossly lipemic (triglycerides 4,038 mg/dL) with a creamy supernatant after centrifugation. Mixing study revealed normal coagulation parameters, confirming analytical interference rather than true coagulopathy. The combination of severe hypertriglyceridemia, relatively preserved apolipoprotein B, and multisystem manifestations favored a chylomicronemia phenotype (type IV-V spectrum). Coronary angiography confirmed obstructive disease, and following triglyceride-lowering therapies, successful percutaneous coronary angioplasty was performed.DiscussionThis case highlights the multisystem impact of severe hypertriglyceridemia and the importance of recognizing lipemia-related laboratory artifacts to avoid misdiagnosis and enable timely management.Take-Home MessagesSevere hypertriglyceridemia reflects a spectrum of triglyceride-rich lipoprotein disorders, often with combined chylomicron and very-low-density lipoproteins excess due to genetic–environmental interactions. Lipemia, associated with severe hypertriglyceridemia, may interfere with routine laboratory assays, leading to spurious results posing significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

Hypertriglyceridemia is a well-recognized etiology of acute pancreatitis, with risk increasing substantially at triglycerides >1,000 mg/dL.1 Triglyceride-rich lipoprotein (TRL) disorders, particularly those involving coexistence of fasting chylomicrons and very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDLs), confer a dual risk of pancreatitis and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.2 In addition, lipemia can cause analytical interference, producing spurious laboratory results and diagnostic delays.3 The overall clinical course, diagnostic challenges, and multisystem manifestations of this case are summarized in the Visual Summary and Video 1.

History of Presentation

A 51-year-old man with a 15-year history of hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus presented with 4 days of intermittent retrosternal chest pain radiating to left arm, associated with diaphoresis. He had no prior history of ischemic heart disease. A former smoker (quit 1 month prior), he denied alcohol use and had resided in Tanzania for 3 decades working in the sunflower oil trade.

He was initially evaluated at a referring facility, where electrocardiogram was performed (Figure 1A) and loading doses of aspirin 300 mg, clopidogrel 600 mg, atorvastatin 40 mg, and enoxaparin 60 mg subcutaneously were administered. On presentation to our institution, his blood pressure was 140/80 mm Hg and heart rate was 78 beats/min. The patient was obese (body mass index 31 kg/m2). His medications included telmisartan, combination oral hypoglycemic agents (metformin, glimepiride, dapagliflozin), saroglitazar, fenofibrate, and icosapent ethyl, initiated 1 month before presentation.

Past Medical History

One month prior, the patient was hospitalized with severe epigastric pain radiating to back and nausea. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated normal pancreatic enzymes (amylase 90 U/L, lipase 119 U/L) with severe hypertriglyceridemia (>1,000 mg/dL). Additional investigations revealed hypocalcemia (5.0 mg/dL) with hypoalbuminemia (3.1 g/dL), normal parathyroid hormone levels, mild thrombocytopenia (130,000/μL), elevated C-reactive protein (29.1 mg/L), and transaminitis (aspartate aminotransferase 117 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 649 U/L).

Abdominal ultrasonography then demonstrated hepatomegaly with fatty infiltration and a diffusely enlarged, heterogeneous pancreas without cholelithiasis. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography confirmed hepatosplenomegaly and diffuse pancreatic enlargement with mild peripancreatic fat stranding and nodularity, consistent with acute interstitial pancreatitis (modified computed tomography severity index 4 of 10) (Figure 1C). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography corroborated these findings, demonstrating reduced T1 signal and T2/short tau inversion recovery (STIR) hyperintensity of pancreatic parenchyma with surrounding fat stranding, without biliary obstruction. The episode was therefore attributed to hypertriglyceridemia-induced normoenzymatic pancreatitis.

Echocardiography showed mild concentric left ventricular hypertrophy with preserved systolic and diastolic function (ejection fraction 60%). The patient was scheduled for coronary angiography; however, preprocedural coagulation testing revealed a prothrombin time (PT) reported as infinite with an unmeasurable international normalized ratio (INR). Repeat sampling yielded identical results, and vitamin K administration failed to correct the abnormality.

Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated grade 1 fatty liver without peripancreatic collections. A subsequent mixing study demonstrated normal PT/INR, excluding factor deficiency or inhibitor; suggesting analytical interference. Management and Outcome

After confirming normal coagulation parameters, coronary angiography demonstrated significant single-vessel disease, with critical stenosis (95%) of the mid left anterior descending artery (Figures 4A and 4B), a long lesion (90%) in the first diagonal branch, and nonobstructive disease in the right coronary artery. Initial management included dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and clopidogrel), high-intensity statin (atorvastatin 80 mg), and low-molecular-weight heparin following ischemic electrocardiographic changes. Anticoagulation was temporarily withheld when PT was unmeasurable but resumed after normal mixing studies confirmed analytical interference from hypertriglyceridemia. Therapeutic plasmapheresis was considered; however, the patient opted for intensive medical therapy. Targeted triglyceride-lowering therapy was initiated with fenofibrate and high-dose omega-3 fatty acids, alongside continuation of statin therapy. Serial monitoring demonstrated a progressive decline in triglycerides (4,038 mg/dL to 3480 mg/dL on day 2 and 1,461 mg/dL by day 7).

Subsequently, percutaneous coronary intervention with deployment of a 3.5 × 28 mm Synergy Megatron everolimus-eluting stent to left anterior descending artery and balloon angioplasty of the diagonal branch was performed successfully without complications (Figures 4C to 4E).

Fasting lipoprotein electrophoresis demonstrated marked elevation of VLDLs (83.3%) with reduced low-density lipoprotein (12.6%) and high-density lipoprotein (4.1%), without detectable chylomicrons or lipoprotein(a) band. Genetic testing for inherited dyslipidemia was offered but declined by the patient, and management was continued medically.

Post procedure, clopidogrel was switched to prasugrel. Ezetimibe was introduced subsequently, and at discharge, additional lipid-lowering therapy with bempedoic acid was initiated, and omega-3 fatty acids were transitioned to purified icosapent ethyl. Comprehensive lifestyle and metabolic risk factor modification, including glycemic control and weight reduction, was advised. At follow-up, patient demonstrated sustained improvement in lipid parameters with no recurrence of pancreatitis or ischemic events.

Discussion

Hypertriglyceridemia results from accumulation of TRLs, primarily chylomicrons (intestinal origin) and VLDL (hepatic origin), with serum levels determined by the balance between production and lipoprotein lipase–mediated clearance. Impairment of this pathway—due to inherited defects or secondary conditions (diabetes, obesity, hypothyroidism)—leads to progressive TRL accumulation.4 Severe hypertriglyceridemia represents a clinically significant and potentially life-threatening condition, predisposing to both acute pancreatitis and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.5 Although triglycerides are not directly atherogenic, they reflect the atherogenic burden of TRL remnant particles and elevated apolipoprotein C-III.6 In contrast, pancreatitis risk is driven by chylomicronemia; at triglycerides >1,000 mg/dL, excess chylomicrons undergo intravascular hydrolysis, releasing toxic free fatty acids that induce local inflammation and acinar injury.1

The precise classification of lipid disorder in this case is challenging, requiring careful interpretation (Figure 5, Supplemental Table 1). Despite markedly elevated triglycerides (4,038 mg/dL), the relatively modest apolipoprotein B concentration (100.8 mg/dL) suggests triglyceride enrichment per particle rather than an increased particle number, suggesting a chylomicron-predominant state with concomitant VLDL excess.2,4 The disproportionate triglyceride elevation relative to total cholesterol further supports predominance of large TRLs and aligns with a phenotype associated with increased pancreatitis risk.2,7 Although compatible with a TRL disorder, distinction between Fredrickson type IV and V cannot be definitively established.2,4 Lipoprotein electrophoresis performed externally did not demonstrate a discrete chylomicron band; however, this should be interpreted cautiously, as detection is method-dependent, influenced by sample handling and analytical techniques; particularly in marked lipemia, which may impair electrophoretic separation.3,8,9 Accordingly, overall profile is best interpreted as a severe TRL disorder with features overlapping type IV and type V phenotypes, reflecting a likely multifactorial etiology.Figure 5Phenotypic Framework for Dyslipidemia Based on Predominant Lipid ElevationDyslipidemia is initially categorized by the predominant lipid abnormality (cholesterol, mixed, or triglyceride-predominant). Severe lipemia has important analytical implications beyond lipid measurement, particularly for coagulation testing (Figure 6). Most automated coagulation assays use photo-optical clot detection based on changes in light transmission during fibrin formation.3 Marked turbidity from TRLs scatters light and interferes with optical sensing, producing falsely prolonged clotting times or failure of clot detection. Consequently, patients with severe hypertriglyceridemia may demonstrate spuriously abnormal PT-INR values despite intact hemostasis. In this case, PT was measured using a turbidimetric (photo-optical) method, likely explaining the reported “infinite” value. A normal mixing study and absence of clinical bleeding confirmed assay interference rather than true factor deficiency, consistent with pseudohypocoagulability secondary to lipemia.3,8 Therefore, when coagulation results are discordant with clinical picture in a lipemic sample, analytical interference should be suspected and confirmed by repeat testing after sample dilution or ultracentrifugation, or with mechanical clot detection methods less affected by turbidity.8 Recognition of this phenomenon prevents unnecessary transfusions or delays in urgent procedures. Management of severe hypertriglyceridemia focuses on rapid triglyceride reduction to prevent pancreatitis, followed by sustained control of atherogenic lipoproteins to mitigate long-term cardiovascular risk. Initial therapy includes strict dietary fat restriction and triglyceride-lowering pharmacotherapy such as fibrates, statins, omega 3 fatty acids, with plasmapheresis reserved for severe cases.7,10 Long-term management requires aggressive correction of secondary contributors, together with combination lipid-lowering therapy targeting both low-density lipoprotein and TRLs.2 However, in patients with persistent or recurrent severe hypertriglyceridemia despite optimized conventional therapy, emerging targeted approaches—including apolipoprotein C-III inhibition (eg, volanesorsen, olezarsen) and angiopoietin-like protein 3 inhibition (eg, evinacumab)—offer promising options by targeting key regulators of triglyceride metabolism.2,7

Conclusions

Severe hypertriglyceridemia should prompt consideration of underlying lipoprotein phenotypes, as combined chylomicron and VLDL excess carries dual risks of pancreatitis and atherosclerotic cardiovascular events. Severe lipemia may obscure laboratory interpretation and delay urgent interventions, necessitating careful clinical correlation. Prompt recognition and aggressive lipid-lowering therapy can achieve rapid metabolic control with improved outcomes.
