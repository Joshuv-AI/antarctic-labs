Cost savings of task-sharing in family planning: estimates across 128 low- and middle-income countries
Authors: Adebiyi Adesina, Meltem Odabaş, Heidi Quinn, Moazzam Ali, Elizabeth A Sully
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTask-sharing, the expansion of health service provision to a broader range of health workers, where the number of providers is expanded through training and support of additional cadres, offers a strategy to alleviate some of the human resource and financial challenges that limit low- and middle-income countries’ (LMICs) ability to deliver essential health services, including family planning (FP). In this study, we apply the World Health Organization’s (WHO) recommendations to estimate the potential cost-savings from task-sharing FP services.MethodsWe use the Adding It Up (AIU) 2024 model estimates on contraceptive services for 128 LMICs to estimate the direct costs of providing contraceptive care for six methods – condoms (male and female), oral contraceptive pills, emergency contraceptive pills (ECP), injectables, implants and intrauterine devices. We compare the costs of two scenarios – a status quo (under current provider guidelines for FP service delivery) and a task-sharing scenario (implementing WHO task-sharing guidelines for FP service delivery).ResultsImplementing WHO task-sharing recommendations within the next year would save an estimated $1.6 billion USD across all LMICs. Task-sharing reduces the direct cost of service delivery for all six methods, saving 34% for male condoms, 31% for injectables, 30% for pills and IUDs, 22% for ECPs, 12% for implants and 9% for female condoms. These cost savings were largely consistent at the global, regional, and sub-regional levels.ConclusionsWHO recommendations for task-sharing service delivery offer a critical cost-saving policy mechanism that leverages limited human and financial resources to better meet contraceptive needs while at the same time reducing health system costs. Cost estimates generated from the AIU model show that implementing WHO recommendations for task-sharing family planning services is a promising strategy for expanding access to contraceptive care across LMICs. Amidst increasing cuts to family planning financing, strategies for delivering quality services at lower costs are critical to ensuring continued access to care.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14839-x.Keywords: Task sharing, Family planning services, Contraceptive service delivery, Health system costs, Human resources for health, Cost modeling, Low- and middle-income countries

Background

Human resources remain a critical component of any health system’s goal of providing full access to essential services [1], including family planning (FP). Countries classified by the World Bank as Lower- and Middle-Income (LMICs) are confronted with significant constraints on meeting demand for health services. Over the last decade, healthcare delivery systems, and healthcare workers in particular, were expected to do more with the same or less funding – deliver services to a growing population with complex demography-based health needs, with flat-lined or reduced funding and patient-to-provider ratios [2–4]. The withdrawal of the United States Agency for International Development, the largest donor of family planning assistance, transformed a difficult situation into a dire reality [5]. One strategy that evidence increasingly indicates could alleviate some of this pressure is task-sharing. In its foundational guidance the World Health Organization (WHO) defines task-sharing as expanding health service tasks from providers with specialized training is expanded to a broader set of providers to improve efficiency and coverage while maintaining quality standards: a concept first formalized in WHO’s 2008 guidance on task-shifting with a focus on HIV/AIDS [6], and subsequently extended to family planning [7]. Under a task-sharing model, mid-level and lay health providers, such as clinical officers, midwives, nurses and community health workers (CHWs), receive training that expands their ability to deliver all or some components of service provision.

The WHO developed recommendations specific to task-sharing for FP services to guide and spur adoption across member countries [7]. These recommendations outline which cadre of providers can provide what FP method and how to maintain quality and safety of services through training, supportive supervision, and adequate supply of contraceptive commodities.

Through training, supportive supervision and strategic implementation, task-sharing FP service delivery can expand the number of FP providers and increase access, while ensuring that quality of care and patient outcomes are comparable regardless of health provider cadre [8]. Systematic reviews of task-sharing initiatives across several countries and a broad set of services such as tuberculosis care, HIV/AIDS testing and treatment, mental health services, and maternal and newborn health (MNH), have demonstrated positive return-on-investment [9–14].

Despite this growing body of evidence supporting task-sharing FP services [14–17], there remains limited data about the implications of and associated cost-savings from scaling-up implementation at the national level. This paper models the cost implications to the health system of adopting the WHO recommendations across 128 LMICs, providing global, regional and sub-regional estimates of the cost-savings that can be achieved from task-sharing FP services.

Methods

This study estimates the cost savings associated with task sharing in the provision of contraceptive care and pregnancy related and newborn care in LMICs, from a health-system perspective with provider-mediated delivery of contraceptives. The analysis utilizes the Adding It Up (AIU) 2024 model [18, 19], which estimates the need, impact and costs associated with providing sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services across 128 LMICs. The results from the AIU model are presented in reports generated every 3–4 years which inform global, regional and national policy advocacy discussions on the resources required to fulfill contraceptive, abortion as well as maternal and newborn health needs.

The AIU model generates estimates through incorporating the following categories and sources of data:

Demographic data on population, from the Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division (UNPD) World Population Prospects 2024 [20, 21];

Demographic data on population, from the Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division (UNPD) World Population Prospects 2024 [20, 21];

Contraceptive method mix from each country’s most recent method use survey, as well as overall levels of contraceptive use for the UNPD World Contraceptive Use model-based estimates are combined with estimated population data to generate estimated number of users for each contraceptive method;

Contraceptive method mix from each country’s most recent method use survey, as well as overall levels of contraceptive use for the UNPD World Contraceptive Use model-based estimates are combined with estimated population data to generate estimated number of users for each contraceptive method;

Treatment based on globally standardized protocols for providing contraceptive services;

The treatment assumptions for each service intervention are based on information from the WHO One Health Tool (OHT) [22], refined to adhere to WHO protocols, and updated based on scientific literature and expert review. These inputs consist of three direct cost categories – personnel, contraceptive commodities and consumable supplies – which combine to generate the unit cost (per person or user per year) of providing direct FP services. For each contraceptive service, treatment protocols are outlined that quantify the types and quantities of commodities and medical supplies needed to provide contraceptive services as well as personnel levels, number of minutes and cost-per-minute (based on estimated salary data) for each service.

Personnel costs data come from WHO- CHOosing Interventions that are Cost-Effective (CHOICE) salary estimates (inflated to 2024 US dollars) [23, 24]; while the inputs for commodities and medical supplies come from the Reproductive Health Supplies Coalition FP Visibility Analytics Network (RHSC-VAN) [25], United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) [26], Management Sciences for Health supplies [27], and UNICEF procurement databases [28]. Direct cost category descriptions

The estimates of all three categories are annualized by dividing the cost per person per method by the quantity needed to provide one couple-years of protection (CYP), which is based on each method’s average length of use and drawn from AIU methodology: 14 for pills and ECP, 77 for condoms, average of 7.7 for injectables, average of 3 for implants and 4.7 for IUDs [19]. The CYP factor for condoms reflects AIU’s assumption of a monthly coital frequency of 5.6 over 12 months, with an adjustment for user wastage, rather than a simple 12-month-of-use conversion. The AIU model incorporates CYP factors for each method based on the average length of use in generating annual costs, however, it accounts for method failure rates in generating estimated demographic impact, in terms of pregnancies averted.

As outlined in Table 2, delivery of all contraceptive services for this analysis includes informed choice counselling as an integral task. Salary data for tasks that are performed by nurses or midwives applies to the average salary for both cadres. Salary data for tasks performed by CHWs or assistant nurses, applies to the average salaries for all cadres (pharmacy worker/technician, pharmacist and auxiliary nurse) between the two cadres.

Our analysis focuses exclusively on the direct service delivery costs of implementing FP task-sharing. As such, we assume that the necessary concomitant investments in an enabling environment and supporting health system infrastructure have already been made to ensure quality of care and patient outcomes are equivalent, regardless of the provider’s cadres.

Analysis

The task-sharing scenario modifies personnel cadres only, as outlined in the WHO’s FP task-sharing guidelines [7]. The core of the analysis compares personnel and direct service delivery costs between two scenarios – status quo and task-sharing. The status quo scenario uses the estimated amount of time spent by health provider cadre per visit taken from WHO OHT - a global model for estimating health systems costs, which assumes service delivery at current or pre-task sharing levels. We assume that the total estimated number of users is the same for both scenarios; however, the key difference between the two scenarios is the annualized personnel cost by method. We model the annual personnel cost and resulting direct service cost for both scenarios at the country level, then produce average annual personnel and direct cost (per person) as well as the total estimates for all LMICs at subregion, region and global levels [20].

Therefore, task sharing is expected to reduce personnel costs, which in turn lowers total direct costs, as personnel expenditure constitutes a component of direct costs. We estimate personnel costs and direct costs in status quo and task sharing scenarios, in 2024 US dollars (USD), for six family planning (FP) methods. These include four short-acting reversible contraceptives (SARCs): condoms (male and female), oral contraceptive pills (OCP/pills), emergency contraceptive pills (ECPs), and injectables; and, two that are long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs): implants and intrauterine devices (IUDs).We assume FP method provision includes informed choice counselling as well as screening for STI/HIV and GBV as important components of quality and rights-based service delivery.

We calculate annual absolute total direct cost savings (in USD) by subtracting the annual total direct cost under the task-sharing scenario from that under the status quo scenario. Relative total cost savings (in %) are calculated by dividing absolute total direct cost savings by the annual total direct cost under the status quo scenario. Annual cost per person is defined as the total annual direct cost of a contraceptive method in a given scenario divided by the number of users of that method. Absolute and relative cost savings per user are calculated using the same approach as for total cost savings.

We assess regional and sub-regional cost savings by two classification types. Using the absolute cost savings results, we first assess which region and subregions account for the largest share of global and regional cost savings, respectively. Next, we use relative cost savings results to compare the costs in status quo scenario relative to its task-sharing scenario and assess cost savings for each region and subregion.

Results

Average personnel costs

First, we compared the annualized personnel cost per person for each method for both scenarios, to identify the immediate cost implications of task-sharing. As shown in Table 3, annualized personnel costs are highest for short-acting reversible contraceptives (SARCs) with a range of $10.84 per user for pills to $7.29 per ECP. The annualized personnel cost for delivering long-acting reversible contraceptive (LARC) methods was an average of $3.18 for IUDs and $4.18 for implants. When services are reconfigured to task-sharing recommendations, personnel cost savings are 44% for condoms (male and female), pills, and injectables, 39% for ECP, 33% for IUDs and 22% for implants.

Total direct costs

Task-sharing would reduce total direct costs by US $1.6 billion (31%) across all 128 LMICs. This reduction is based on the difference between the estimated $5.17 billion in service delivery costs for the status quo scenario and $3.58 billion for the task-sharing scenario. While our health systems perspective assumes provider-mediated delivery for all methods, we acknowledge that in practice all contraceptives could be accessed via self-service channels, and condoms are most commonly acquired in this way. Excluding male and female condoms, task-sharing would still yield estimated savings of US $985 million. The largest savings accrued from task-sharing SARCs - condoms (34%), injectables (31%), pills (30%), ECP (21%), with similar savings in LARCs - IUD (30%) and implants (12%). Our assessment of the regional results presented in Table 7 shows the combined impact of two key factors that drive cost savings – the total number of users in the region and having a larger proportion of SARC users. The Asia, LAC and Africa regions account for 98% of global savings (51%, 24% and 23%, respectively). Within the Asia region, the largest cost savings were from condoms (53%), pills (22%), IUD (12%) and injectables (11%). A slightly different pattern emerges for the LAC region where task-sharing saved 45% in cost of pills, 27% for condoms and 24% for injectables. In the Africa region, injectables account for the largest share of cost savings at 39%, while savings for pills and condoms account for 37% and 14%, respectively.

The results in Table 7 show aggregate savings across all regions with savings ranging from 34% for LMICs in the European region to 24% for LMICs in the Oceania region, with the LAC (32%), Africa (30%), and Asia (30%) regions falling between that range.

Cost savings by method type for the Asia and European regions follow a similar pattern with condoms accounting for the largest share of cost savings, followed by pills and IUDs. For LMICs in the Africa and Oceania regions, injectables represent the largest share of cost savings followed by pills and condoms. For LMICs in the LAC region, pills account for the largest share of cost savings, followed by condoms and injectables.

Discussion

Implications

The primary premise of task-sharing is to address the shortage of highly skilled health workers by redistributing responsibilities among different health worker cadres, thereby expanding access to services and lowering health system costs. The results show that task-sharing achieves the greatest direct cost savings for condoms, followed by injectables, pills, and IUDs, suggesting that the more tasks associated with a method’s provision can be shifted, the greater the potential cost savings. This shows task-sharing achieves cost savings across all regions, providing a strong case for implementing WHO recommendations [7]. While this analysis presents the perspective of the health system provider and assumes services are delivered by a trained health worker, it is important to note that provision of certain contraceptive care services, such as condoms and injectables, are shifting towards individual self-care. This shift towards self-care has the potential to result in further health system cost savings and expanded access, and further work could incorporate demand for self-care data where available to understand additional cost savings.

Although significant, direct cost savings are not the same magnitude as personnel cost savings because of the moderating effect of contraceptive commodities and consumable supplies costs. The higher the cost-share of those latter categories, the lower savings from personnel cost translate to direct cost. This limiting effect was most prominent for female condoms where direct cost savings were lower relative to personnel cost savings because the average cost of female condoms account for 80% ($36.23 of $45.55) of direct service cost for the status quo scenario.

While the results of this analysis focus on costs, it is important to consider the overall impact of task-sharing FP service delivery and its impact on expanding method access and choice. Country assessments of task-sharing have shown increasing impact in terms of method availability and use. A rapid program review of pilot projects in Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, Ethiopia, Ghana and Nigeria, showed that increases in LARCs and contraceptive prevalence as well as reduction in total fertility followed implementation of task-sharing strategies [30]. In-depth evaluations of Pakistan’s Lady Health Worker (LHW) program, described an 11% increase in contraceptive use in relative to areas with no LHWs (all other provider levels remaining equal) [31]. Similarly, assessments of Ethiopia’s health extension worker (HEW) program showed that areas with access to services delivered by HEWs correlated with increased contraceptive prevalence [32].

Nevertheless, there remains a critical gap in evidence of the cost-effectiveness of implementing task-sharing at the national level. A 2023 analysis of national health systems policies and treatment protocols for LARCs in seven sub-Saharan countries - Democratic Republic of Congo, India, Mali, Mozambique, Nigeria, Rwanda and Senegal - found a need to increase availability and awareness of task-sharing of FP services as well as greater clarity of scopes of practice for providers [33]. The analysis recommended strengthening health systems’ enabling environment (such as improved training, supportive supervision, increased and reliable supply of commodities), as well as better compensation mechanisms for providers [34].

Data on implant insertion and removal by CHWs have shown promising outcomes at sub-national levels in Ethiopia and Nigeria, as have data on IUD insertion and removal by nurses and midwives [32, 35, 36]. However, those successes have yet to be implemented across all regions/states in these countries. Above all, these data would provide robust evidence that decision-makers need to scale-up task-sharing initiatives, reap cost savings, lower access barriers to and deliver quality FP services.

Limitations

A primary limitation of this analysis is its reliance on global average data for personnel costs, commodity prices, and supply costs, due to the lack of comprehensive and conclusive country-level data on these specific inputs for contraceptive service delivery. Furthermore, data on the current, national-level implementation of task-sharing for FP are limited. Our review indicates that while FP task-sharing policies are described for many LMICs, evidence of their implementation is often limited to sub-national pilot studies and programs. As such, we assume that current service delivery across all LMICs remains at pre-task-sharing modalities. We also assume that initial investments have been made to foster an enabling policy and implementation environment as well as a supporting health system infrastructure to ensure quality of care and patient outcomes are the same regardless of provider cadre. Due to the wide variation in program support and health system costs associated with task-sharing [14, 16], this analysis could not account for these additional costs which may affect the cost estimates presented here. Relatedly, our health systems perspective treats all contraceptive methods as provider-mediated, in line with WHO task-sharing guidance, which is normative rather than descriptive of current practice. This assumption is a stronger simplification for condoms than for other methods, as condoms are widely accessed through over-the-counter, pharmacy, and community-based channels in many LMICs; we therefore present additional results excluding male and female condoms in the “Total direct costs” sub-section under "Results" and in Supplemental Table S5 to support transparent interpretation of the headline savings. Our review of the available literature does not show conclusive correlation between task-sharing FP service delivery and changes in method use, as such this analysis does not account for potential changes to the distribution of users across the different methods.

Conclusion

The results presented here add to the growing evidence that task-sharing can significantly reduce the cost of delivering FP services at global, regional, and sub-regional levels. Scaling task-sharing may offer substantial, sustained reductions in FP service delivery costs, particularly in settings with high SARC reliance and limited human resources. As policymakers and program managers work to strengthen health systems and service delivery following the drastic funding cuts in global health funding limited domestic government fiscal space for health and strained human resources, task-sharing offers a promising strategy for maneuvering resource constraints to meet their population’s contraceptive needs.
