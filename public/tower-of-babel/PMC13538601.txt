Interocular difference in refraction and ocular biometry in children with anisometropia
Authors: Tailiang Lu, Wei Sun, Meihua Ding, Jing Xu, Yuanyuan Hu, Yirong Wang, Jike Song, Hongsheng Bi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo examine the association between interocular difference in refraction and ocular biometry in children with anisometropia. This analysis included 91 children aged 6–16 years with anisometropia, identified from a cross-sectional, school-based study of 765 children. All children underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination, including cycloplegic biometry and refractometry. The refractive lens power (RLP) was calculated using Bennett’s equation. The mean interocular difference in SE was 2.24 ± 1.48D. Compared to the contralateral eye, the more myopic eye or less hyperopic eye exhibited a significantly longer axial length (AL), deeper anterior chamber depth (ACD), thinner lens thickness (LT) and deeper vitreous chamber depth (VCD) (all P < 0.05). No significant interocular differences were observed for corneal curvature radius (CCR), central corneal thickness (CCT) and RLP between the two eyes (all P > 0.05). When exploratory analyzing by anisometropia type, the more myopic eye or less hyperopic eye had a significantly thinner LT and less RLP than the contralateral eye in the antimetropia group. In univariate analysis, interocular difference in SE was significantly associated with interocular difference in AL (β=−0.822, P < 0.001), ACD (β=−0.401, P < 0.001 ) and VCD (β=−0.831, P < 0.001). In the multivariate regression analysis, interocular difference in SE was significantly associated with age (β = 0.144, P = 0.002), interocular difference in AL (β=−1.024, P < 0.001) and RLP (β=−0.421, P < 0.001). Interocular difference in the parameters of AL and crystalline lens are significant factors associated with anisometropia, whereas corneal parameters show no significant interocular difference between the two eyes. The role of lenticular parameters appears to be type-dependent. Exploratory analysis further revealed that the interocular difference in a compensatory lenticular pattern to axial elongation varies among different types of anisometropia.Keywords: Anisometropia, Interocular difference, Spherical equivalent, Ocular biometry, Bennett’s equationSubject terms: Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

Anisometropia is a condition characterized by a spherical equivalent (SE) difference between the two eyes of at least 1 diopter (D)1. It is typically attributed to asymmetric binocular development under identical genetic and environmental influences during childhood. In addition to these two factors, random factors may be associated with anisometropia which appears to be the destruction of binocular emmetropization3. The prevalence of anisometropia ranges from 1.6% to 3.6% among children aged 6–9 years in Australia and Singapore, and it increases with age2,4,5. This age-related increase in prevalence is considered to be associated with the onset and progression of myopia in older children2.

The refractive lens power (RLP), corneal curvature radius (CCR) and axial length (AL) appear to be significant factors relevant to refractive development. During emmetropization, the crystalline lens and cornea lost power to compensate for the axial elongation, gradually reducing hyperopic refractive error toward emmetropia. After the age of two, corneal refractive power tends to stabilize, while the refractive power of the crystalline lens begins to decline with age and starts to increase again after 13 years old6,7. If the decreasing crystalline lens power could not compensate for the axial elongation, myopia will occur7. The previous studies8,9 have consistently shown that myopic eyes had a longer AL, lower RLP, and deeper anterior chamber depth (ACD) than hyperopic eyes. Similar interocular differences in AL, RLP, and ACD have also been associated with anisometropia10. However, another unilateral high myopia study found that the highly myopic eyes showed the same lens thickness (LT) as the contralateral eyes because the lens is no longer flattened and lost power to compensate for the axial elongation due to the physiological limit of ocular growth11,12. Therefore, it remains unclear whether axial elongation in the more myopic eye is consistently accompanied by LT thinning in the context of childhood anisometropia.

Recently there have been few investigations focusing on the relationship between ocular biometric parameters and refraction in children with anisometropia, particularly regarding interocular differences in crystalline lens characteristics. Therefore, we conducted the present study to measure interocular differences in ocular biometric parameters including LT and RLP to assess their effect on interocular differences in refraction among anisometropic children from North-Western China. This study extends previous work by exploratorily examining these parameters across different anisometropia type and degree, providing deeper insights into the biometric mechanisms underlying anisometropia in children.

Methods

Study design and participants

The data was taken from a cross-sectional and school-based study conducted in Qinghai province, China, which has been described in detail previously13. This investigation enrolled a total of 765 children aged 6–16 years from two elementary schools (The Second Primary School of Menyuan County and Qingshizui Town Boarding School of Menyuan County) and one junior high school (The Third Junior High School of Menyuan County) in the Menyuan County. After excluding participants with incomplete data, 596 children had a full set of data. Of these, 91 children met the diagnostic criteria for anisometropia (interocular difference in SE of at least 1 D between the two eyes) and constituted the study population for the present analysis. Children without anisometropia were not included in this study. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, and for all participants, one parent or legal guardian signed a written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Ophthalmic examination

All participants underwent a series of ocular examinations, including measurements of uncorrected visual acuity, best corrected visual acuity, and slit-lamp examination of the anterior and posterior segment. After cycloplegia, all refractive and ocular biometric measurements were performed. For cycloplegia, the children first received one drop of 0.4% oxybuprocaine (Santen Corp., Shiga, Japan) for topical anesthesia. Two minutes later, the first of three drops of 1% cyclopentolate (Alcon, Fort Worth, Tex., USA) was instilled at 5-minute intervals. If 30 min later the pupil diameter was at least 6 mm and the pupillary light reflex was absent, cycloplegia was considered adequate. If these criteria were not met, a fourth drop of cyclopentolate was administered.

The cycloplegic refraction was measured using auto-refractometry (KR-8900, Topcon Corp., Tokyo, Japan) at least three times in each eye of all study participants, and the mean value was used for analysis. If the difference between the maximum and minimum values of the spherical and cylindrical refractive error measurements exceeds 0.5 diopters, the measurements were to be repeated. The SE was defined as the spherical refractive power plus half of the cylindrical refractive power. The ocular biometric parameters including AL, CCR, central corneal thickness (CCT), ACD, LT, and the vitreous chamber depth (VCD) were measured by optical coherence interferometry (LENSTAR LS900; Haag-Streit Co., Koeniz, Switzerland). All biometric measurements were performed five times, and the mean value of these measurements was taken for statistical analysis. If any two measurements for AL differed by more than 0.02 mm, the eye measurement had to be repeated. The mean corneal power (CP) was calculated based on the mean CCR with a refractive index of the cornea of 1.331514. The RLP was calculated based on the cycloplegic SER, CP, ACD, LT and AL using the formula of Bennett15,16.

Definitions of anisometropia and refractive status

Anisometropia was defined as an interocular difference in SE between the two eyes of at least 1 D. Based on cycloplegic SE, the refractive status was differentiated as myopia (SE ≤ − 0.5 D), emmetropia (SE > − 0.5 D to ≤ + 0.5 D) and hyperopia (SE > + 0.5 D). Hyperopic anisometropia was classified as bilateral hyperopic anisometropia (SE > + 0.5 D in both eyes) and unilateral hyperopic anisometropia (SE > − 0.5 D to ≤ + 0.5 D in one eye and SE > 0.5 D in the contralateral eye), Myopic anisometropia included bilateral myopic anisometropia(SE ≤ − 0.5 D in both eye)and unilateral myopic anisometropia (SE > − 0.5 D to ≤ + 0.5 D in one eye and SE ≤ − 0.5 D in the contralateral eye), antimetropia was defined as SE ≤ − 0.5 D in one eye and SE > 0.5D in the contralateral eye. To ensure consistency in interocular comparison between the two eyes across different refractive categories, the eye with a more negative SE was uniformly designated as the more myopic or less hyperopic eye and the contralateral eye as the more hyperopic or less myopic eye.

Data analysis

Anisometropic children with an interocular difference in SE of at least 1 D were included in statistical analysis. For each participant, the more myopic or less hyperopic eye was assigned to a group, and their contralateral eye with more hyperopia or less myopia was assigned to the other group. The statistical analysis was conducted using a commercially available software (SPSS for Windows, version 25.0). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used in the analysis of a normality test. The mean values were presented as mean and standard deviations. The refraction and ocular biometry were compared between two eyes using a paired t-test in the children with anisometropia. Due to the small sample sizes, the subgroup comparative analyses of parameters between two eyes in the antimetropia group and the hyperopic anisometropia group were exploratory. To identify factors associated with the interocular difference in SE, we assessed the standardized regression coefficient β, the non-standardized regression coefficient B and its 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Firstly, univariate analyses were performed to identify parameters associated with the interocular difference in SE. These analyses were used only for exploratory screening and descriptive purposes. Parameters that were statistically significant in the univariate analyses were then included as candidate independent variables in the multivariate regression model, with the interocular difference in SE as the dependent variable. In addition, age, sex, and the interocular differences in LT and RLP were forced into the model based on previous evidence and clinical relevance, irrespective of their statistical significance in the univariate analyses. Thus, the final multivariate model was constructed using a clinically priori-driven strategy, rather than relying solely on automatic stepwise selection. Statistical significance was considered with a 2-sided P-value of < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

Out of 765 eligible children, 596 (77.9%) individuals (288 [48.3%] girls) had a full set of data. Based on the definition of anisometropia with an interocular difference in SE of ≥ 1.00 D between two eyes, 91 children (15.3%) of them were diagnosed with anisometropia, of whom 43 (47.3%) were boys. 505 children (84.7%) with non-anisometropia were excluded in this study. In the multivariate regression analysis, interocular difference in SE was set as the dependent variable, and all parameters that showed significant associations with interocular difference in SE in the univariate analysis were entered as independent variables. Variables with a P > 0.05 were removed. Age, gender, LT and RLP are clinically recognized as important factors correlated with SE. Therefore, the variables including age, gender, the interocular differences in LT and RLP were subsequently were forced into the model. Interocular difference in VCD was excluded due to collinearity with interocular difference in AL (variance inflation factor > 10). The final model revealed that interocular difference in SE was significantly associated with age (β = 0.144, P = 0.002), interocular difference in AL (β = −1.024, P < 0.001) and interocular difference in RLP (β= −0.421, P < 0.001), whereas no significant associations were found for interocular differences in gender, ACD and interocular difference in LT (both P > 0.05) (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study of children with anisometropia, interocular differences in AL and lenticular parameters were found to be associated with anisometropia, whereas corneal parameters show no significant interocular difference. Exploratory stratified analyses by anisometropia type revealed that the asymmetry of ocular biometric components is not uniform. Specifically, an interocular difference in the crystalline lens was observed in myopic anisometropia and antimetropia, but this difference was absent in hyperopic anisometropia. These findings suggest that anisometropia should be viewed not merely as a symmetric failure of emmetropization, but as a disorder of interocular coordination in axial growth, with lens changes assuming a secondary role. This perspective advances the mechanistic understanding of childhood anisometropia and underscores the clinical importance of monitoring the parameters of AL and lens.

Our findings are broadly consistent with earlier reports17–21, which demonstrated that interocular difference in AL was primarily associated with anisometropia. Similarly, two studies on anisometropia in Chinese children22,23 found that the more myopic eye tended to have longer AL, deeper ACD and VCD, steeper corneal curvature, and thinner LT. However, in contrast to these studies, we did not find significant interocular differences in corneal curvature. Although there were interocular differences in LT, no significant difference in RLP was observed between the two eyes. These findings suggest that the mechanisms underlying ocular growth in anisometropia may differ from those involved in emmetropization or typical myopic growth in isometropic eyes. During emmetropization or myopia progression in children, lens thinning or a reduction in RLP due to equatorial stretching can compensate for axial elongation, leading to decreased hyperopic refraction and myopia progression24. In contrast, anisometropia is thought to result from an imbalance in emmetropic homeostasis, leading to asymmetric interocular growth. This process may involve minor changes in the crystalline lens, with no corresponding changes in corneal properties2,25,26.

Our findings are also consistent with longitudinal evidence reported by Tong et al.2 who observed that increases in interocular differences in SE with age were significantly correlated with asymmetric axial elongation in Singaporean school children. Their three-year cohort study further showed that the prevalence of anisometropia increased with age, and new-onset anisometropia was associated with accelerated myopic shifts in both eyes. These longitudinal data support the hypothesis that anisometropia was mainly relevant to asymmetric axial elongation during childhood. Regarding the relationship between age and anisometropia, we found that interocular difference in SE was significantly associated with age in our multivariate model. This finding suggests that older children may have greater interocular differences in refraction for the anisometropia. This may be related to the differential rate of myopic progression between the two eyes with age.

The relationship between anisometropia and interocular differences in ocular biometric parameters especially LT and RLP remains controversial. A cross-sectional study involving participants aged 40–64 years27 reported that asymmetry of AL had the strongest association with interocular differences in SE. Meanwhile, other biometric asymmetries of LT, RLP, CP, and ACD were also associated with anisometropia, suggesting that multiple factors not just AL differences play a role in its development. However, our study found anisometropia was associated with interocular differences in AL, and not significantly associated with interocular differences in CP and LT. These results are consistent with those reported by Zhang, Y. et al.21who studied children and adolescents with unilateral high myopia and observed no lens thinning in highly myopic eyes compared to the contralateral eye. The discrepancy between our findings and those of adult study27 may be partly explained by differences in study populations. Specifically, the participants in study27 were older and many had cataracts, in which lens changes differ from the physiological lens changes associated with ocular growth in children28.

Interestingly, in this study, we found that the interocular difference in RLP was not significant in univariate analysis. However, it became significantly associated with the interocular difference in SE after adjusting for other covariates in the multivariate model. This finding likely reflects confounding or suppression effects among interocular differences in ocular biometric parameters. Specifically, the interocular difference in RLP is correlated with interocular differences in SE-related structural parameters. Thus, the independent association between the interocular difference in RLP and SE may only emerge after controlling for other relevant biometric parameters.

Our exploratory stratified analysis based on anisometropia type revealed significant interocular differences in crystalline lens parameters. In the subgroup with antimetropia, the myopic eye exhibited significantly thinner LT and lower RLP compared to the hyperopic eye. This pattern is consistent with recent findings by Kirik et al.29,who demonstrated that corneal power remained unchanged between eyes, while crystalline lens power was reduced in myopic eyes, likely due to passive mechanisms associated with axial elongation. These observations suggest that each eye follows an independent growth pattern in antimetropia. In contrast, there were no significant differences in LT and RLP between the two eyes in hyperopic anisometropia. This further suggests that anisometropia can be the result of emmetropization failure in both eyes30. During normal emmetropization, the refractive power of the cornea and lens decrease to compensate for the axial elongation during ocular growth, leading to a gradual reduction in hyperopia24. However, it was demonstrated that amblyopic hyperopia eyes exhibit disproportionately the change in the lenses and corneas along with shorter AL in Cass et al.31 study, suggesting that eyes with hyperopic anisometropia, which receive abnormal and blurred visual input, may fail to maintain the necessary balance between ocular structures during emmetropization32.

Myopic anisometropia is associated with the asymmetrically accelerated myopia progression between the two eyes5. Although both eyes are exposed to the same environmental influences, asymmetric axial elongation between the two eyes may be associated with the development of anisometropia33. Interestingly, our study found that in the myopic anisometropia group, the more myopic eye not only exhibited a longer AL but also a thinner LT. yet its RLP did not differ significantly from that of the contralateral eye. The results may be related to the small degree of anisometropia. This discrepancy also suggests that the interocular difference in LT alone was insufficient to produce a detectable interocular difference in the calculated RLP. While this might be associated with unmeasured factors such as interocular difference in lens curvature or refractive index, our current data cannot support this speculation. Additionally, it is also found that no significant intraocular differences in LT or RLP were observed between the two eyes for subjects with anisometropia of less than 3D.

In cases of anisometropia, the more myopic or less hyperopic eye exhibited deeper ACD and VCD, while CCT and CCR were similar between the two eyes. These findings are consistent with previous studies34,35. In our univariate analysis, interocular differences in ACD and VCD were significantly associated with the interocular difference in SE. However, due to high collinearity between interocular differences in VCD and AL, interocular differences in VCD was excluded from the multivariate model. As a result, the independent contribution of interocular difference in VCD to SE could not be demonstrated in the multivariate analysis. It may suggest that VCD is the primary component associated with AL elongation during ocular growth.

Potential limitations of our study should be mentioned. First, our study was a cross sectional observation so that we could not examine causal relationships between interocular difference in refraction and ocular biometry. Future longitudinal studies with larger cohorts are needed to elucidate developmental characteristics of anisometropia. Second, the study included a small sample, because there were very low prevalences of hyperopic anisometropia and antimetropia for children in this cross-sectional and school-based study. It may have reduced the statistical power to detect subtle interocular differences in lens parameters. In this study, the subgroup analyses for the antimetropia and hyperopic anisometropia groups are exploratory. Future studies with larger samples are needed to validate these exploratory findings. Third, choroidal thickness was not performed in this study. Given the potential role of the choroid in ocular growth regulation and its potential as a biomarker of treatment efficacy for myopia control therapies such as optical and pharmacological interventions, the absence of choroidal data limits our understanding of whether choroidal asymmetry contributes to anisometropia or regulates its progression. Future studies that include choroidal thickness measurements are needed to validate the association between interocular difference in choroidal thickness and anisometropia. Fourth, while the present study specifically focused on children with anisometropia aged 6 to 16 years, a critical period for anisometropic development, the results cannot be generalized to the broader population. Future studies including adults are expected to validate our findings.

Conclusion

Our study indicates that interocular difference in the parameters of AL and crystalline lens are significant factors associated with anisometropia, whereas corneal parameters show no significant interocular difference between the two eyes. The role of lenticular parameters may vary depending on the type of anisometropia. Exploratory analysis revealed that a significantly thinner lens and lower RLP in more myopic eye or less hyperopic eye were observed in children with antimetropia suggesting the interocular difference in a compensatory lenticular pattern to axial elongation is absent in other type of anisometropia.
