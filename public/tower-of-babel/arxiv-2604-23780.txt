Asymptotic preserving scheme for the shallow water equations with non-flat bottom topography and Manning friction term


Abstract
. In our previous work [ 29 ] , we proposed a class of high-order asymptotic preserving (AP) finite difference weighted essentially non-oscillatory (WENO) schemes for solving the shallow water equations (SWEs) with bottom topography and Manning friction, utilizing a penalization technique inspired by [ 6 ] . Although the added weighted diffusive term enhanced stability, it increased computational cost and slowed down the convergence rate in the intermediate regime between convection and diffusion. In this paper, we extend our previous study by removing the penalization while preserving the AP property. To achieve this, we employ a high order semi-implicit implicit-explicit Runge-Kutta (SI-IMEX-RK) time discretization, coupled with the high-order WENO reconstruction for first-order derivatives and a central difference scheme for second-order spatial derivatives. This combination yields a class of fully high-order schemes. Theoretical analysis and numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed schemes retain AP, asymptotically accurate (AA) and well-balanced properties, while offering higher computational efficiency compared to our previous schemes in [ 29 ] , especially in the intermediate regime between convection and diffusion. Moreover, treating the momentum in the friction terms implicitly is essential for preserving the AP property; otherwise, the scheme fails to converge to the limiting equations. This indicates that implicit treatment of Manning friction is necessary for the stability of the method.

1.
Introduction

In computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations, explicit methods have been preferred for their simplicity, particularly in nonlinear hyperbolic systems, which shocks can be generated even if the initial conditions are continue
[
43
,
57
]
, but they suffer from stability issues when handling stiff systems, requiring small time steps. While implicit methods, though more stable, are computationally expensive
[
14
,
13
]
.

To balance efficiency and stability, implicit-explicit (IMEX) schemes emerged, allowing stiff terms to be treated implicitly and non-stiff terms explicitly
[
37
]
. These schemes are particularly effective for multiscale problems in complex multi-physics systems
[
34
]
.
For instance, one major advantage of IMEX schemes is their ability to capture the correct behavior during scale transitions, such as from convection-dominated to diffusion-dominated regimes, especially in the diffusive limit
[
9
,
51
,
63
,
65
]
, which can be difficult to address in scheme design. Such behaviour in these schemes is known as
asymptotic preserving
(AP).
The concept of AP schemes was first proposed by Jin et al. in
[
32
,
33
]
for kinetic equations and has significant applications across various physical problems
[
18
,
41
,
19
,
23
]
.
For more details, readers can refer to references
[
28
,
34
]
.

Among the various applications of IMEX schemes to shallow water equations (SWEs) with non-flat bottom topography and Manning friction, we propose two approaches that are particularly relevant in simulating these equations numerically. The first approach involves additive IMEX methods, which use a penalization technique to ensure correct convergence of solutions to the limit equation. This approach has been effective in simulating systems like kinetic equations
[
51
,
20
,
7
]
, radiation transport
[
63
,
65
]
, and other similar applications.
In
[
29
]
, the authors applied additive IMEX time discretization with a penalization strategy, combining it with high-order WENO reconstructions proposed by Jiang and Shu
[
54
,
32
]
and central difference schemes
[
8
]
for spatial discretization. The penalization technique inspired by
[
6
]
were introduced by adding a weighted nonlinear diffusive term to ensure the AP property of the scheme.
However, the choice of weights affects the stability of the scheme
[
6
,
51
]
, and incorrect weights can lead to instability. Typically, these weights approach
0
in convection-dominated regimes and
1
in diffusion-dominated regimes, but the penalization term can slow the convergence rate in intermediate states between these regimes.

To address this issue, in this paper we adopt the second approach that involves semi-implicit (SI) IMEX framework. The SI-IMEX approach eliminates the need for penalization techniques while ensuring the schemes maintain the AP property.
This approach has been introduced in
[
6
,
5
]
and it is particularly effective for hyperbolic systems with stiff terms like the shallow water equations
[
31
,
46
,
45
,
3
]
and the Euler equations
[
30
,
4
,
8
,
10
]
. Specifically, SI-IMEX schemes have been successfully applied to systems, including the all-Froude shallow water equations and all-Mach Euler equations
[
8
,
10
,
30
,
47
,
48
,
4
,
29
,
3
,
45
]
.

In summary, in this paper our main focus is to design a class of AP SI-IMEX methods for the time discretization to avoid the penalization by adding a weighted diffusive term. We will show that, by removing the penalty term, we reduce computational cost and enhance the overall efficiency of the scheme. Furthermore, we show that the scheme is also
asymptotically accurate
(AA), i.e., the scheme maintains its order of temporal accuracy for the limit equation. Finally, for the spatial discretization, we will continue to use WENO reconstructions
[
32
,
54
]
for the first-order derivatives and the central difference scheme
[
8
]
for second-order derivatives.

1.1.
Model equations and diffusive limit

The shallow water equations (SWEs) with non-flat bottom topography and Manning friction in dimensionless form are expressed as
[
12
,
29
]
:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}h+\nabla\cdot{\bf m}=0,\\[8.53581pt]
\partial_{t}{\bf m}+\nabla\cdot\left(\dfrac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{h}\right)+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla\left(\dfrac{g}{2}h^{2}\right)=-\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}gh\nabla B-\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\gamma{\bf m}.\end{array}\right.

This system can be derived from the Navier-Stokes equations, which are fundamental in describing fluid motion
[
55
,
58
,
17
,
11
,
40
]
, and they also have wide-ranging applications in fields such as oceanography, engineering, and geophysics
[
50
,
24
,
53
,
22
,
27
,
44
,
36
,
56
,
59
,
38
,
61
,
60
,
31
]
.
Here,
h
represents the water depth,
{\bf m}
is the momentum, and
{\bf u}=\frac{{\bf m}}{h}
denotes the velocity.
These variables are defined on a time-space domain
(t,{\bf x})\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega
.
The gravitational constant is denoted by
g
, and
B({\bf x})
represents the bottom topography, which is independent of time.
The term
\gamma{\bf m}
represents the Manning friction, with
\gamma=\frac{gk^{2}|{\bf m}|}{h^{\eta}}
.
Here,
|{\bf m}|
denotes the
L_{2}
norm of
{\bf m}
, and
k
is the Manning coefficient, indicating the intensity of friction exerted by the bottom on the water, a higher value of
k
implies greater friction.
The parameter
\eta
is set to
7/3
, and
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product.

As the
late-time
behavior of the system is considered, in scenarios where friction dominates, the variables
h
and
{\bf m}
undergo long-time simulations as
\varepsilon
goes to zero, driving the system into a diffusive regime
[
12
,
29
]
.
This transition is based on the Chapman-Enskog expansion
[
12
,
29
]
, where the conserved variables are expressed as follows:

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}h({\bf x},t)=h_{0}({\bf x},t)+\varepsilon h_{1}({\bf x},t)+\varepsilon^{2}h_{2}({\bf x},t)+\cdots,\\[8.53581pt]
{\bf m}({\bf x},t)={\bf m}_{0}({\bf x},t)+\varepsilon{\bf m}_{1}({\bf x},t)+\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{2}({\bf x},t)+\cdots.\end{array}\right.

Introducing the variable
H=h+B
as the surface level, its Chapman-Enskog expansion takes the form:

(1.3)

H({\bf x},t)=h_{0}({\bf x},t)+B({\bf x})+\varepsilon h_{1}({\bf x},t)+\varepsilon^{2}h_{2}({\bf x},t)+\cdots,

since the bottom topology
B(x)
is independent of time.
Substituting the expansions (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) into the system (
1.1
) and taking the limit as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
,
we obtain the leading-order terms of system (
1.1
):

(1.4a)

\partial_{t}h_{0}+\nabla\cdot{\bf m}_{0}=0,

(1.4b)

\nabla\left(\frac{g}{2}h_{0}^{2}\right)=-gh_{0}\nabla B-\gamma_{0}{\bf m}_{0},

with
\gamma_{0}=\frac{gk^{2}|{\bf m}_{0}|}{h_{0}^{\eta}}
.
Denoting
H_{0}({\bf x})=h_{0}({\bf x})+B({\bf x})
, we can rewrite equation (
1.4b
) as

(1.5)

{\bf m}_{0}=-\sqrt{\frac{h_{0}^{\eta+1}}{k^{2}}}\frac{\nabla H_{0}}{\sqrt{|\nabla H_{0}|}}.

Substituting (
1.5
) into (
1.4a
) yields the following limit system,

(1.6a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h_{0}=\nabla\cdot\left(\sqrt{\frac{h_{0}^{\eta+1}}{k^{2}}}\frac{\nabla H_{0}}{\sqrt{|\nabla H_{0}|}}\right),

(1.6b)

\displaystyle\nabla(\frac{g}{2}h_{0}^{2})=-gh_{0}\nabla B-\gamma_{0}{\bf m}_{0}.

This system can also be viewed a class of the non-stationary
p
-Laplacian, with the general form:

(1.7)

\partial_{t}h_{0}-\nabla\cdot\left(|\nabla h_{0}|^{p-2}\nabla h_{0}\right)=0,\quad p>1,

where
p=3/2
(with an additional coefficient of
\sqrt{h_{0}^{\eta+1}/k^{2}}
omitted).
More details on
p
-Laplacian can be found in
[
1
,
26
,
35
,
16
]
.
As a degenerate parabolic equation, the
p
-Laplacian imposes parabolic-type time step restrictions on explicit schemes
[
12
]
.
Next, we will show that the SI-IMEX time discretization overcomes this issue and accurately captures the correct solutions as
\varepsilon
transitions from
\mathcal{O}(1)
to
0
.

1.2.
Steady state

For systems with source terms, they tend to admit some physically steady states, and many interesting phenomena can be interpreted as small perturbations around these equilibrium states.
These make the preservation of steady states crucial in the design of numerical schemes.
For the equations (
1.1
), when system reaches a steady state, it reduces to:

(1.8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla\cdot{\bf m}=0,\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\dfrac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{h}\right)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla\left(\dfrac{g}{2}h^{2}\right)=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}gh\nabla b-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\gamma{\bf m}.\end{array}\right.

Assuming that
\mathbf{m}\equiv\mathbf{C}
is a constant vector, particularly when
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{0}
, this system reaches what is known as “lake at rest” steady state, and the system (
1.8
) simplifies to:

(1.9)

H=h+B=\text{const},\quad{\bf m}=\mathbf{0},

indicating that the system is in a state of still water. Numerical schemes designed for system (
1.1
) must ensure this “still water” is exactly maintained at the discrete level. Such schemes are referred to as well-balanced schemes.
However, standard numerical schemes often fail to meet this requirement, necessitating special modifications to achieve the well-balanced property.
The pioneering work in this area was conducted by Bermudez and Vazquez
[
2
]
. Since then, various well-balanced schemes have been developed across different numerical frameworks, including finite difference, finite volume, and discontinuous Galerkin methods
[
42
,
66
,
59
,
61
,
60
,
15
,
21
,
49
,
52
]
.
For a comprehensive review, readers may refer to
[
62
,
39
]
for more details.

In more complex scenarios where
\mathbf{C}\neq\mathbf{0}
, representing a moving equilibrium state, the design of well-balanced schemes becomes more challenging.
Thus, we focus solely on the simpler “still water” steady state described in equation (
1.9
) in this work.

).

3

, followed by concluding remarks in Section

.

2.
Numerical scheme

In this section, we aim to design a class of numerical schemes for the SWEs with bottom topology and Manning friction (
1.1
) by employing SI-IMEX time discretizaiton.
The parameter
\varepsilon
is allowed to range from
\mathcal{O}(1)
to
0
.
When
\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}(1)
, the system is in the hyperbolic regime, where shocks are generally generated.
In this regime, the WENO reconstructions
[
32
,
54
]
, used to discrete the first-order derivatives, are crucial for accurately capturing these shocks.
As
\varepsilon
goes to zero, the system transitions to a friction-dominated regime governed by a nonlinear degenerate parabolic equation, often referred to as the ’p-Laplacian’
[
12
,
29
]
.
Consequently, a central difference scheme is employed to discrete the second-order derivatives
[
8
]
.
The high-order SI-IMEX time discretization enables the scheme to overcome parabolic-type time step restrictions, while the WENO reconstructions effectively mitigate the oscillations near discontinuities.
This combination results in a class of fully high-order schemes that can be shown, through both theoretical analysis and numerical experiments, to possess asymptotic preserving, asymptotically accurate and well-balanced properties.
The details of these validations will be discussed in the next section.

2.1.
IMEX time discretization

In numerical discretization strategies, spatial derivatives are typically discretized first, followed by temporal discretization.
However, in this study, we begin with temporal discretization, diverging from the traditional method-of-lines approach.
We first develop a first-order asymptotic preserving SI-IMEX scheme, then extend it to high-order schemes using high-order IMEX-RK methods.
The detailed procedures are as follows:

2.1.1.
First order SI-IMEX time discretization

From (
1.1
) and variable
H=h+B
, the first-order SI-IMEX scheme is given as follows:

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\frac{h^{n+1}-h^{n}}{\Delta t}+\nabla\cdot{\bf m}^{n+1}=0,

(2.1b)

\displaystyle\frac{{\bf m}^{n+1}-{\bf m}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{h}\right)^{n}=-\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon^{2}}gh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1}-\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{gk^{2}}{(h^{n})^{\eta}}\left|{\bf m}^{n+1}\right|{\bf m}^{n+1},

Here,
h^{n}
and
{\bf m}^{n}
represent the solutions at time
t^{n}
, treated explicitly in the temporal discretization. Meanwhile,
h^{n+1}
and
{\bf m}^{n+1}
, the solutions at time
t^{n+1}
, are treated implicitly and require solving.

To solve the system (
2.1
), we first determine the momentum
{\bf m}^{n+1}
from the second sub-equation (
2.1b
) of the system as follows:

(2.2)

{\bf m}^{n+1}={\bf m}^{n}-\Delta t\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{h}\right)^{n}-\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon^{2}}gh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1}-\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{gk^{2}}{(h^{n})^{\eta}}\left|{\bf m}^{n+1}\right|{\bf m}^{n+1},

Notably, Eq. (
2.2
) is nonlinear in terms of
{\bf m}^{n+1}
.
To solve it, we first isolate the term involving
{\bf m}^{n+1}
by moving the friction term to the left:

(2.3)

\left(1+\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,\frac{gk^{2}\left|{\bf m}^{n+1}\right|}{(h^{n})^{\eta}}\right){\bf m}^{n+1}={\bf m}_{\star}-\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon^{2}}gh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1},

with

(2.4)

{\bf m}^{\star}={\bf m}^{n}-\Delta t\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{h}\right)^{n}.

Next, taking the
L_{2}
norm of both sides of equation (
2.3
) leads to a quadratic equation for
|{\bf m}^{n+1}|
:

(2.5)

\left(1+\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,\frac{gk^{2}\left|{\bf m}^{n+1}\right|}{(h^{n})^{\eta}}\right)\left|{\bf m}^{n+1}\right|=\left|{\bf m}_{\star}-\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon^{2}}gh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1}\right|.

It is obviously that Eq. (
2.5
) has two real roots, and we select the positive root to ensure non-negative of the form:

(2.6)

\left|{\bf m}^{n+1}\right|=\frac{-\varepsilon^{2}+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{4}+\frac{4\Delta tgk^{2}}{(h^{n})^{\eta}}|\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}-\Delta tgh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1}|}}{\frac{2\Delta tgk^{2}}{(h^{n})^{\eta}}}.

Substituting Eq. (
2.6
) into Eq. (
2.3
) yields the value of
{\bf m}^{n+1}
:

(2.7)

{\bf m}^{n+1}=\frac{2(\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}-\Delta tgh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1})}{\varepsilon^{2}+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{4}+\frac{4\Delta tgk^{2}}{(h^{n})^{\eta}}|\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}-\Delta tgh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1}|}}.

We now substitute equation (
2.7
) into equation (
2.1a
), resulting in the following update for
h^{n+1}
:

(2.8)

h^{n+1}=h^{n}-\Delta t\nabla\cdot\left\{\frac{2(\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}-\Delta tgh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1})}{\varepsilon^{2}+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{4}+\frac{4\Delta tgk^{2}}{(h^{n})^{\eta}}|\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}-\Delta tgh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1}|}}\right\}.

The scheme (
2.8
) can be proven to converge to the following form:

(2.9)

h^{n+1}_{0}=h^{n}_{0}+\Delta t^{2}\nabla\cdot\left\{\sqrt{\frac{(h^{n}_{0})^{\eta+1}}{k^{2}}}\frac{\nabla H^{n+1}_{0}}{\sqrt{|\nabla H^{n+1}_{0}|}}\right\},

demonstrating that scheme (
2.8
) is a first-order scheme for the diffusive limit (
1.6
). Therefore, the scheme maintains the asymptotic preserving property as
\varepsilon
goes to
0
, with detailed validation provided in Section
3
.
While if we treat the
L_{2}
norm of momentum
|{\bf m}|
explicitly, i.e.,
|{\bf m}^{n}|
explicitly, it will break the AP property of the scheme, as discussed in Remark
2.1
.

Now, we want to solve Eq. (
2.8
) to obtain the value of
h^{n+1}
, and then substitute it into (
2.7
) to update the value of
{\bf m}^{n+1}
. This process completes the update of the numerical solutions at time
t^{n+1}
.
Since
H^{n+1}=h^{n+1}+B
depends linearly on
h^{n+1}
, Eq. (
2.8
) can be viewed as a nonlinear equation in terms of
h^{n+1}
.
We solve this equation using Picard iteration, a method successfully employed in our previous work
[
29
]
, and we only give a brief review here.
Additionally, when the system reaches or is near a steady state, where
\nabla H\approx\mathbf{0}
, this results in
\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}^{\star}-\Delta tgh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1}\approx\mathbf{0}
.
To maintain equilibrium state, consistent treatment of
\nabla H
is required in both numerator and denominator of Eq. (
2.8
).
If not, as
\varepsilon
is small enough and the denominator approaches zero simultaneously, it may lead to a breakdown of the scheme.

Therefore, the iterations proceed as follows:

(1)

Check for Steady State:
Calculate
||\nabla H||_{1}
to verify whether the system has reached a steady state. If
||\nabla H||_{1}<\delta
(where
\delta
is a small tolerance, e.g.,
\delta=10^{-9}
unless otherwise specified), the scheme is terminated and we set
h^{n+1}=h^{n}
to ensure the well-balanced property of the scheme. Here,
||\nabla H||_{1}
is the
L_{1}
norm of
\nabla H
.

(2)

Continue the Iteration:
the iteration continues with the following step,

(2.10)

h^{n+1,k+1}=h^{n}-\Delta t\nabla\cdot\left\{\frac{2(\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}-\Delta tgh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1,k+1})}{\varepsilon^{2}+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{4}+\frac{4\Delta tgk^{2}}{(h^{n})^{\eta}}|\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}-\Delta tgh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1,k}|}}\right\},\quad k\geq 0,

with
h^{n+1,0}=h^{n}
. The iteration will stop until
||h^{n+1,k+1}-h^{n+1,k}||_{1}<\delta
, where
\delta
is the same constant as mentioned above. We then set
h^{n+1}=h^{n+1,k+1}
.

Now that we have updated the value of
h^{n+1}
, we proceed to solve for
{\bf m}^{n+1}
using Eq. (
2.7
). The formatting is now complete.

Remark 2.1
.

The implicit treatment of the
L_{2}
norm of momentum
|{\bf m}^{n+1}|
in the Manning friction term is necessary. Otherwise, if we treat it explicitly, as shown below

(2.11)

{\bf m}^{n+1}={\bf m}^{n}-\Delta t\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{h}\right)^{n}-\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon^{2}}gh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1}-\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon^{2}}\gamma^{n}{\bf m}^{n+1},

with
\gamma^{n}=\frac{gk^{2}}{(h^{n})^{\eta}}|{\bf m}^{n}|
. Solving this equation yields

(2.12)

{\bf m}^{n+1}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}+\Delta t\gamma^{n}}\left\{{\bf m}^{n}-\Delta t\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{h}\right)^{n}-\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon^{2}}gh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1}\right\}.

Now, substituting Eq. (
2.12
) into Eq. (
2.1a
), we obtain:

(2.13)

h^{n+1}=h^{\star}+\Delta t^{2}\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}+\Delta t\gamma^{n}}gh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1}\right),

with
h^{\star}=h^{n}-\Delta t\nabla\cdot\left\{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}+\Delta t\gamma^{n}}\left[{\bf m}^{n}-\nabla\cdot\left(\dfrac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{h}\right)^{n}\right]\right\}
.
As
\varepsilon
goes to zero, Eq. (
2.13
) converges to

(2.14)

h^{n+1}=h^{n}+\Delta t\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{gh^{n}\nabla H^{n+1}}{\gamma^{n}}\right),

it is obvious that Eq. (
2.14
) cannot converge to the limiting equation (
1.6
) as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, meaning that the scheme does not have the asymptotic preserving property in the diffusive limit. And the necessity of implicit treatment of the
L_{2}
norm of momentum
|{\bf m}^{n+1}|
in the friction term for maintaining the asymptotic preserving property is further demonstrated through numerical experiments.

2.1.2.
High order SI-IMEX-RK scheme

In this section, we extend the first-order SI-IMEX scheme (
2.1
) to high-order SI-IMEX-RK schemes by employing the high-order semi-implicit approach presented in
[
5
]
.

We start to consider the general class of autonomous problems of the form

(2.15)

u^{\prime}=F(u,u),\quad u(t_{0})=u_{0}.

where
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a sufficiently regular mapping.

We observe that we can rewrite system (
1.1
) with
H=h+B
, as a partitioned one

(2.16)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{du_{E}}{dt}=F(u_{E},u_{I}),\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle\frac{du_{I}}{dt}=F(u_{E},u_{I}),\end{array}\right.

with initial conditions
u_{E}(t)=u_{I}(t)=u_{0}
where

F(u_{E},u_{I})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\nabla m_{I}\\[5.69054pt]
-\nabla\cdot\left(\dfrac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{h}\right)_{E}-\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}gh_{E}\nabla H_{I}-\dfrac{gk^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}(h_{E})^{\eta}}|{\bf m}_{I}|{\bf m}_{I}\end{array}\right)

with
u_{E}=(hu_{E},{\bf m}_{E})
and
u_{I}=(h_{I},{\bf m}_{I})
and
H_{I}=h_{I}+B
.

In such case the solution of system (
2.16
) satisfies
u(t)=u^{*}(t)
for any
t\geq t_{0}
and is also a solution of Eq.(
2.15
). System (
2.16
) is a particular case of partitioned system
[
25
]
, with an additional computational cost since we double the number of variables. Let us remark that the duplication of the unknowns in (
2.16
) does not take place if appropriate choices of the IMEX-RK scheme are considered
[
5
]
.

Now we are ready to introduce the semi-implicit strategy for solving problems of the form (
2.16
). This strategy involves treating the first variable
u^{*}
explicitly and the second variable
u
implicitly. In order to do that we shall adopt high-order IMEX R-K schemes and the coefficients of the method are usually represented in a double Butcher
tableau

[
13
]
as follows

(2.17)

\begin{array}[]{c|c}\tilde{c}&\tilde{A}\\
\hline\cr\\

\bar{K}_{i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\nabla m_{I}\\[5.69054pt]

(2.26a)

2.2.
High order spatial discretizations

For the spatial discretization strategies, we employ the WENO reconstrctions as proposed by Jiang and Shu
[
32
,
54
]
, combined with a central difference scheme
[
8
]
.
This choice is motivated by the need to accommodate validations for the parameter
\varepsilon
, which can vary from
\mathcal{O}(1)
to
0
.
This flexiblity enables the system to transition from a hyperbolic to a diffusive regime.
Shocks occurred when
\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}(1)
, while a nonlinear parabolic ‘p-Laplacian’ emerges as
\varepsilon
goes to zero.
Thus, WENO reconstrction, utilized for first-order derivatives in scheme (
2.25
) and interal stages (
2.26
) and (
2.27
), effectively controls the oscillations near discontinuities, ensuring the stability of the scheme.
Here, we implement three different types of reconstrctions, that is, WENO with Lax-Friedrichs (LF) splitting
\nabla_{LW}
, WENO reconstrction with non-viscous
\nabla_{W}
and well-balanced WENO reconstrction
\nabla_{W}^{WB}
.
To ensure the well-balanced property of the scheme, we make two minor modifications. First, in the flux splitting for the conservation of water depth
h
, we replaced the water depth
h
with the surface level
H
,

(2.28)

{\bf m}^{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left({\bf m}\pm\Lambda H\right),\quad\text{with}\quad\Lambda=\max_{{{\bf u},h}}\{|{\bf u}|+\min(1,1/\varepsilon)\sqrt{gh}\},

which prevents additional errors are introduced by the viscous term at equilibrium state.
Second, as the system achieves steady state, the gradient of pressure will be balanced by bottom topology. Thus, we split the bottom topology into two parts:

(2.29)

gh\nabla B=-gH\nabla B+\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}gB^{2}\right),

and we discrete both
\nabla B
and
\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}gB^{2}\right)
terms in the same manner as
\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}\right)
using discretizaiton
\nabla_{W}^{WB}
, ensuring that

(2.30)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{W}^{WB}\left(\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}gH\nabla_{W}^{WB}B-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{W}^{WB}\left(\frac{1}{2}gB^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{W}^{WB}\left(\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}+gHB-\frac{1}{2}gB^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{W}^{WB}\left(\frac{1}{2}gH^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

which indicates that the equilibrium state is maintained exactly at discrete level.

While for the diffusive limit, the high-order centeral difference scheme is utilized to approximate second-order derivative terms.
These combinations are enssential for accurately capturing shocks in the compressible regime and constructing a high-order solver for limiting equations (
1.6
), making the schemes suitable for a wide range of
\varepsilon
while preserving the well-balanced property.

Remark 2.2
.

Stiffness is encountered during the update of
{\bf m}_{E}^{(i)}
in Eq. (
2.26b
), primarily due to the amplification of round-off errors, which scale with
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}})
.
Although a well-balanced technique is employed to balance the gradient of pressure, bottom topography, and Manning friction term in the momentum flux, this amplification ultimately leads to the breakdown of the scheme.
In contrast, no such stiffness occurrs during the update of
{\bf m}_{I}^{(i)}
in Eq. (
2.27b
).
To address this, we focus on updating the implicit solution
{\bf m}_{I}^{(i)}
. As long as the diagonal elements
a_{ii}\ (i=1,2,\cdots s)
of the implicit RK table are non-zero, the
i
-th layer flux can be updated as follows:

(2.31)

\mathcal{F}_{{\bf m}}^{(i)}=\frac{{\bf m}^{n}-{\bf m}_{I}^{(i)}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}a_{ij}\Delta t\mathcal{F}_{{\bf m}}^{(j)}}{a_{ii}\Delta t},

where

(2.32)

\mathcal{F}_{{\bf m}}^{(j)}=\left\{\nabla_{LW}\cdot\left(\dfrac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{h}\right)_{E}+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{W}^{WB}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}gh^{2}\right)_{I}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}gH_{I}\nabla_{W}^{WB}B-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{W}^{WB}\left(\frac{1}{2}gB^{2}\right)+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\dfrac{gk^{2}}{(h_{I})^{\eta}}|{\bf m}_{I}|{\bf m}_{I}\right\}^{(j)},

This update of the flux successfully eliminates the stiffness from the scheme.

Algorithm flowchart

{\bf m}_{I}^{(i)}=\frac{2\left(\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta tgh_{E}^{(i)}\nabla H_{I}^{(i)}\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{4}+\frac{4a_{ii}\Delta tgk^{2}}{(h_{E}^{(i)})^{\eta}}|\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta tgh_{E}^{(i)}\nabla H_{I}^{(i)}|}},

where

{\bf m}_{\star}^{(i)}={\bf m}^{n}-\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}{a}_{ij}\mathcal{F}_{{\bf m}}^{(j)}-a_{ii}\Delta t\nabla_{LW}\cdot\left(\frac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{h}\right)_{E}^{(i)}.

Next, substituting Eq. (
2.36

2.35a
) yields

(2.37)

h^{(i)}_{I}=h_{\star}^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta t\nabla\cdot\left\{\frac{2\left(\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta tgh^{(i)}_{E}\nabla H^{(i)}_{I}\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{4}+\frac{4a_{ii}\Delta tgk^{2}}{(h_{E}^{(i)})^{\eta}}|\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta tgh_{E}^{(i)}\nabla H_{I}^{(i)}|}}\right\}.

with

We will check whether the steady state is achieved; if not, a Picard iteration will be utilized in the following form:

(2.38)

h^{(i),k+1}_{I}=h_{\star}^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta t\nabla\cdot\left\{\frac{2\left(\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta tgh^{(i)}_{E}\nabla H^{(i),k+1}_{I}\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{4}+\frac{4a_{ii}\Delta tgk^{2}}{(h_{E}^{(i)})^{\eta}}|\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta tgh_{E}^{(i)}\nabla H_{I}^{(i),k}|}}\right\},\forall k\geq 0,

until

is satisfied, and we set

Here, the second-order derivatives terms can be viewed as

, this coefficient is nonlinear dependent on
H
, and we will employ the high-order central difference schemes to discretize it.

After solving
h^{(i)}_{I}
, update the
{\bf m}^{(i)}_{I}
as follows:

(2.39)

{\bf m}_{I}^{(i)}=\frac{2\left\{\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta t\nabla_{W}^{WB}\left(\frac{1}{2}gh_{I}^{2}\right)^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta tgH_{I}^{(i)}\nabla_{W}^{WB}B+a_{ii}\Delta t\nabla_{W}^{WB}\left(\frac{1}{2}gB^{2}\right)\right\}}{\varepsilon^{2}+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{4}+\frac{4a_{ii}\Delta tgk^{2}}{(h_{I}^{(i)})^{\eta}}|\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta t\nabla_{W}^{WB}\left(\frac{1}{2}gh_{I}^{2}\right)^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta tgH_{I}^{(i)}\nabla_{W}^{WB}B+a_{ii}\Delta t\nabla_{W}^{WB}\left(\frac{1}{2}gB^{2}\right)|}}.

To prevent this, we implement a cut-off criterion: if condition

\Big|\varepsilon^{2}{\bf m}_{\star}^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta t\nabla_{W}^{WB}\left(\frac{1}{2}gh_{I}^{2}\right)^{(i)}-a_{ii}\Delta tgH_{I}^{(i)}\nabla_{W}^{WB}B+a_{ii}\Delta t\nabla_{W}^{WB}\left(\frac{1}{2}gB^{2}\right)\Big|<\xi,

and

unless otherwise specified), we set

to ensure the system remains in a steady state.

3.
Asymptotic preserving (AP) and Asymptotically Accurate (AA) property

In this section, we aim to validate the asymptotic preserving (AP) property of the firs-order scheme (
2.1
), as well as the asymptotically accurate (AA) property of the high-order schemes (
2.25
)-(
2.27
).
We focus on the AP and AA analysis on time discretization, while keeping the space continuous.
Additionally, we assume that all variables can be expressed in the following Chapman-Enskog form:

(3.1)

h^{n}({\bf x})=h_{0}^{n}({\bf x})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),\quad{\bf m}^{n}({\bf x})={\bf m}_{0}^{n}({\bf x})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),

with
H^{n}_{0}({\bf x})=h_{0}^{n}({\bf x})+B({\bf x})
, and the leading-order terms, satisfy the limit equation (
1.5
):

(3.2)

{\bf m}_{0}^{n}=-\left(\sqrt{\frac{h_{0}^{\eta+1}}{k^{2}}}\frac{\nabla H_{0}}{\sqrt{|\nabla H_{0}|}}\right)^{n},

where
h^{n}({\bf x})=h({\bf x},t^{n})
and
{\bf m}^{n}({\bf x})={\bf m}({\bf x},t^{n})
.
We assume that the initial data are well-prepared in the sense of (
1.2
), i.e.,
h^{n}({\bf x})=h({\bf x},t^{n})
and
{\bf m}^{n}({\bf x})={\bf m}({\bf x},t^{n})
admit the decomposition (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) at
n=0
.

3.1.
AP property

We now present the AP property of the first-order scheme (
2.1
) and provide the complete proof.

Theorem 3.1
.

The first-order scheme (
2.1
) is AP in the sense that as
\varepsilon\to 0
the limit scheme is a consistent approximation of the limit equation (
1.6
) as long as the initial conditions are well-prepared.

Proof.

This property can be proven via mathematical induction. As
\varepsilon
approaches zero, the scheme (
2.1
) with the modification (
2.2
) simplifies to:

(3.3a)

\displaystyle\frac{h^{n+1}_{0}-h^{n}_{0}}{\Delta t}+\nabla\cdot{\bf m}^{n+1}_{0}=0,

(3.3b)

\displaystyle gh^{n}_{0}\nabla H^{n+1}_{0}=-\frac{gk^{2}}{(h^{n}_{0})^{\eta}}|{\bf m}^{n+1}_{0}|{\bf m}^{n+1}_{0}.

Solving equation (
3.3b
), we find:

(3.4)

{\bf m}^{n+1}_{0}=-\sqrt{\frac{(h^{n}_{0})^{\eta+1}}{k^{2}}}\frac{\nabla H^{n+1}_{0}}{\sqrt{|\nabla H^{n+1}_{0}|}},

then, substituting Eq. (
3.4
) into Eq. (
3.3a
) yields:

(3.5)

\frac{h^{n+1}_{0}-h^{n}_{0}}{\Delta t}=\nabla\cdot\left(\sqrt{\frac{(h^{n}_{0})^{\eta+1}}{k^{2}}}\frac{\nabla H^{n+1}_{0}}{\sqrt{|\nabla H^{n+1}_{0}|}}\right),

which is a consistent first-order scheme for the limit equation (
1.6a
). Given well-prepared initial conditions, the AP property holds for all subsequent time steps
t^{n}
.
∎

3.2.
AA property

The AP property guarantees only the consistency of the scheme, but in general the AP property does not guarantee the high order accuracy of SI-IMEX schemes as
\varepsilon\to 0
and the order of accuracy may degrade. Here we now proceed to prove the AA property of high-order SI-IMEX-RK schemes (
2.25
)-(
2.27
) in order to maintain the accuracy as
\varepsilon\to 0
. In what follows, we recognize that the SA condition is crucial to guarantee the AA property of our SI-IMEX-RK scheme.

Theorem 3.2
.

Consider an SI-IMEX-RK scheme (
2.25
)-(
2.26
)-(
2.27
) of order

. Assume that the IMEX-RK method is SA and the initial conditions are well-prepared. Let us denote by

the numerical solutions obtained after one time step. Denote
U^{exa}({\bf x},t)=(h^{exa},{\bf m}^{exa})^{T}
as the exact solutions of the limit equation (
1.6
). Then after one step, if the errors between the numerical and exact solutions satisfies the following estimate:

(3.6)

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}U^{1}({\bf x};\varepsilon)=U^{exa}({\bf x},\Delta t)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{p+1}),

then the schemes possess the AA property.

Proof.

This property can also be proven by mathematical induction for time stages.
The strategy is as follows. From the AP property of the first order scheme, assuming that conditions (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) hold at time step
t^{n}
, we can get scheme (
3.4
) and (

Therefore, we get:

(1)

for the values

as

in (

(3.7a)

\displaystyle h^{(i)}_{E,0}=h^{n}_{0}-\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\tilde{a}_{ij}\nabla\cdot{\bf m}^{(j)}_{I,0},

(3.7b)

\displaystyle{\bf m}^{(i)}_{E,0}={\bf m}^{n}_{0}-\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\tilde{a}_{ij}\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{{\bf m}_{0}\otimes{\bf m}_{0}}{h_{0}}\right)_{E},

with

(3.8)

{\bf m}^{(j)}_{I,0}=-\left(\sqrt{\frac{\left(h_{E,0}\right)^{\eta+1}}{k^{2}}}\frac{\nabla H_{I,0}}{\sqrt{|\nabla H_{I,0}|}}\right)^{(j)}.

Then, substituting Eq. (

For the values

|{\bf m}|

In contrast, the SI-S2 scheme fails to simulate the solutions as
\varepsilon
goes zero, indicating that it lacks the AP property.
This suggests that the nonlinearity in the schemes designed for SWEs with non-flat bottom topography and Manning friction (
1.1

|{\bf m}|
is indeed necessary.

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose two types of high-order semi-implicit asymptotic preserving finite difference WENO schemes for the shallow water equations with bottom topology and Manning friction term (
1.1
).
The objective is to develop a highly efficient method that avoids the use of weighted diffusive terms, as proposed in
[
29
]
.
Theoretical analysis and numerical tests demonstrated that the SI-S1 scheme has the AP and AA, and well-balanced properties.
Unfortunately, the SI-S2 scheme fails to converge to the limiting equations (
1.6
) as
\varepsilon
approaches zero, indicating that the implicit treatment of the Manning friction is necessary for the stability of the scheme.
Furthermore, comparing to the first-order asymptotic preserving scheme proposed in
[
12
]
, our schemes not only avoid the severe time step restrictions as
\varepsilon\to 0
but also offer higher resolution, as demonstrated by the numerical experiments.
Furthermore, the schemes developed in this work are more efficient than those proposed in our previous work
[
29
]
, essentially in the intermediate state.
