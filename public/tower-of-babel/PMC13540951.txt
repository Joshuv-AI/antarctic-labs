Detection of β-lactam and chloramphenicol resistance genes in Gram-negative bacteria isolated from Clarias gariepinus in selected aquaculture farms
Authors: Yusuf Abdurrahman, Ayodele Timilehin Adesoji, Ado A
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) and their corresponding antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) are of global public health concern. Documented information on the epidemiology and spread of ARB and ARGs among fish farms in Katsina State, Nigeria is scarce. This study isolated and characterized selected ARB and ARGs from Gram-negative bacteria in fish from aquaculture farms within Katsina State, Nigeria.MethodologyHundred apparently healthy C. gariepinus fish samples were randomly collected from five (A, B, C, D, E) aquaculture farms. Gram-negative bacteria were isolated from samples’ intestines using selective culture media. Isolates were putatively identified using biochemical characterization. Susceptibility of isolates to antimicrobial agents were determined by disk diffusion method while resistance to at least one antibiotic agent in three classes of antibiotics were categorized as Multi-Drug Resistant (MDR). MDR isolates’ identities were further confirmed by Vitek-2 compact system. Beta-lactam and chloramphenicol resistance genes were amplified among all MDR isolates using PCR amplification.ResultsForty-Nine Gram-negative bacteria were isolated. Proteus spp was the most prevalent among samples from farms A (12.24%), D (8.16%) and E (12.24%) while E. coli was highest (8.16%) on farm C. Among the isolates, higher resistance to each of chloramphenicol (48.9%) and cefoxitin (36.7%) compared to gentamicin (2%) and ciprofloxacin (8.2%) was observed. Five (10.20%) MDR isolates were identified while vitek-2 identified Proteus mirabilis (2/5, 40%) and Sphingomonas paucimobilis (2/5, 40%) as the most prevalent MDR bacteria. ARGs detected include: beta-lactam (blaTEM (4/5), blaSHV (3/5), blaCTX-M (1/5)) and chloramphenicol (Cat1 (1/5)) resistance genes.ConclusionPresence of ARGs among Gram-negative bacteria from this study highlights a potential public health concern. Hence, urgent need to monitor and control antibiotics use within aquaculture to mitigate the spread of ARB and ARG to consumers through the food chain is urgently important within the study localities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05737-9.Keywords: Gram-negative bacteria, Beta-lactam, Resistance, Genes, Fish

Introduction

A significant number of the aquaculture sector is made up of freshwater fish farming, especially in developing countries where it contributes to the local economy and provides food and livelihood [1]. A wide variety of freshwater fish have been cultivated, with the three main species (i.e. tilapia, carp and catfish) contributing to worldwide aquaculture production [1]. Fish makes up over 17% of the animal protein eaten in Africa [2]. Fish is an excellent source of vital fatty acids, specifically omega-3 fatty acids, which the human body is unable to produce, and has minimal saturated fat content. Fish are known to contain low fat and low cholesterol and are easily digestible making them suitable to the infants, children, and elderly [3].

Fish kept in settings with a high organic load and dense stocking density is subjected to intensive activities, which can lead to the growth and dissemination of harmful bacteria [4, 5]. Thereby, resulting to bacterial disease-related issues and financial losses during fish cultivation. Clarias gariepinus, a fish species with significant economic value, is the most widely produced in Nigeria [6]. Fish and fish products are recognized as potential vehicles for transmission of human pathogenic microorganisms, including Salmonella, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella, and other Gram-negative bacteria, posing considerable public health risks [7]. Inappropriate handling and inadequately prepared fish could lead to human vulnerability to bacteria diseases. Many bacterial species that have been recovered from fish, include Salmonella, Listeria, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella, and most enteric bacteria. This can be indicators of multisource pollution [7].

However, since fish farming has become more intensive, there is a greater chance of bacterial illnesses in fish farms, which can lead to financial losses [4, 5]. Antibiotics have been used globally as aquaculture drugs or feed mixes to control fish diseases. On the one hand, antibiotics protect fish from infectious diseases to some extent [8], however, the wide application or improper use of antibiotics creates problems of bacterial resistance, promoting the occurrence of antibiotic resistant bacteria (ARB) and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in various aquatic environments. Antibiotic pollution and the resulting contamination by ARB and ARGs have aroused wide concern [9]. ARGs contamination and their distribution in microbial populations throughout the biosphere is the result of continuous selection by the heavy anthropogenic use of antibiotics over the past few decades [10]. The high market demand for aquaculture products means that antibiotics are used in farmed products more than in humans, leading to increasing antibiotic resistance in pathogens [11]. ARGs and ARB could pose a threat to human health when ingested by humans via aquaculture products [12].

The excessive use of antibiotics in aquaculture has resulted in the emergence or development of bacterial resistance [13]. Schar et al. [14] reported that more than nine ARB species were detected in tilapia fish farm that include Cyanobacteria (26.5%), Proteobacteria (26.4%), Actinobacteria (11.0%), Bacteroidetes (9.8%), Planctomycetes (8.3%), Verrucomicrobia (6.1%), Chloroflexi (2.1%), Chlorobi (1.7%), Firmicutes (1.0%), and others (4%). The ARB species in water varied from one location to another [15]. Bacterial resistance is associated with carried ARGs, which are commonly detected in the environment or among organisms in direct contact with the environment as an emerging contaminant [14]. To date, the research on ARGs in aquatic environments has mainly focused on sulfa drugs (e.g., trimethoprim), tetracycline, quinolones, and macrolides classes of antibiotics [14]. But there is paucity of documented information on the surveillance of ARBs and ARGs from aquaculture in Katsina state, Nigeria. Therefore, this study sought to genotypically detect beta-lactamase and chloramphenicol resistance genes among Enterobacteriaceae isolated from fish samples from selected fish ponds in Katsina state, Nigeria.

Materials and methods

Study locations

The study was conducted in five selected fish farm that includes A, B, C, D and E within Katsina municipal, Katsina State, Nigeria. Katsina State is situated in the North-west region of Nigeria and shares common borders with Zamfara, Kaduna, Kano and Jigawa. The geographical location of the State is 12°59′20N 07° 36′ 03″ E, the exact position of the sampling location is provided in Fig. 1.

Map of Katsina metropolis showing sample site locations as A, B, C, D and E representing Zannun, Sauki, Sardauna, Batagarawa and Ajiwa, respectively, all within Katsina, Katsina State, Nigeria

Interview schedule

In this study, a total of five farmers (i.e. one per fish farm) were recruited from the five selected fish farms for this scheduled interview based on their availability and approved consent to participate in this study. A brief verbal interview guide was developed specifically for this study to gather information about antibiotics use, antibiotics classes, and history of disease outbreaks from the different fish farms. The interview guide is provided in a supplementary material section (S1).

Sample collection

Between May and June, 2023, a total of 100 Clarias gariepinus weighing an average of 58.23 g were purchased from five selected privately owned commercial aquaculture farms while harvesting using scoop nets was carried out during routine farm harvesting using scoop nets with the assistance of farm personnel with permission from farm managers. Thereafter, twenty (20) fish samples were randomly chosen per farm within the sampling period as described by Adeshina et al. [16]. During fish samples processing in the laboratory no euthanasia procedures were performed because the fish samples had been harvested and were already dead prior to transportation to the laboratory. The fish were then quickly sealed in a sterile polythene bag containing ice and transported aseptically as described by Kim et al. [17] to the Department of Microbiology, Federal University Dutsin-Ma for bacteriological analysis.

Sample processing, bacteria isolation and characterization

Before bacterial isolation, the external surface of each dead fish sample was disinfected with 70% ethanol. Using a sterile surgical blade, the fish were aseptically dissected to expose the segment of intestine, which was subsequently slit open and swabbed to sample the intestine’s contents. The swabs were streaked directly onto sterile MacConkey agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

Following incubation, distinct colonies were selected based on their morphological characteristics, including shape, size, color, and pigmentation. Selected colonies were sub-cultured onto freshly prepared nutrient agar plates by re-streaking to obtain pure isolates. Pure colonies were subsequently transferred onto nutrient agar slants and stored at 4 °C for biochemical characterization. Isolates were identified based on colonial morphology, Gram staining and biochemical reactions following standard procedures [18].

Antibiotics susceptibility testing and selection of multi-drug resistance of bacterial isolates

Using Mueller Hinton agar and the Kirby Bauer disk diffusion method [19] pure bacterial isolates were subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST). Overnight grown culture was standardized to 0.5 MacFarland standard using DensiCHEK Plus instrument (Biomerieux, USA), then streaked on freshly prepared sterile Mueller Hinton agar plates using sterile wire loop. Thereafter, antibiotic disks including cefoxitin (30 µg), tetracycline (10 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), and gentamicin (30 µg) (Bioanalyse, Turkey) were aseptically placed on the inoculated Mueller–Hinton agar plates using sterile forceps. The plates were subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h, after which diameter of zones of inhibition were measured using standard meter rule in millimeters and interpreted as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant based on the breakpoint criteria recommended by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [20]. Isolates exhibiting resistance to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR), as defined by Magiorakos et al. [21]

Identification and characterization of selected Multidrug Resistant (MDR) bacterial isolates using Vitek—2-compact system

In order to confirm the identities of the selected MDR bacteria isolates using vitek-2-compact system, each selected MDR bacterial isolate was grown overnight, then suspended in 3 ml of normal saline in a polystyrene test tube. The suspension density was adjusted to a McFarland standard of 0.5–0.6 using a DensiCHEK Plus instrument (Biomerieux, USA). Thereafter, the gram-negative bacteria identification card tube was placed into the polystyrene test tube containing three milliliters of ready-made bacterial suspensions on the cassette. The test tube was then inserted into the Vitek machine, where the card wells were automatically filled. The filled and sealed cards were inserted into the Vitek 2 reader-incubation chamber and incubated at 370C for 18–24 h, afterward, the bacteria identity was recorded based on percentage similarity (Biomerieux, USA).

Detection of antibiotic resistance genes from the selected MDR isolates

Using the heat lysis procedure, the isolates' DNA were extracted and utilized as templates for the PCR amplification according to Ahmed et al. [22]. Beta-lactam (blaTEM, blaSHV, and blaCTX-M) as well as the chloramphenicol (cat1) resistance genes were amplified using primers described in Table 1. The protocol for the PCR amplification include: 15 μl reaction that contained 1 μl of the DNA template, 4.5 μl of PCR-grade water, 7.5 μl of PCR mastermix, and 1 μl of each of the forward and reverse primers. The reaction was conducted in a thermocycler (Bio-Rad, USA) under the following reaction conditions: 15 min of initial denaturation at 95 °C, 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, and 45 s of annealing and extension at 72 °C. This was followed by a final elongation step of 72 for seven minutes, all PCR amplification conditions remained constant across all genes except for the annealing temperature, which varied by gene as described in Table 1. Each PCR run included a positive control (Escherichia coli ATCC 35218) and a negative control (sterile distilled water in place of template DNA) to ensure the validity and reliability of the amplification. Following amplification, PCR products were separated by size using gel electrophoresis and visualized under UV light in a gel documentation system.

Discussion

Antibiotic resistance has been shown to be significantly exacerbated by the wide spread use of antibiotics in fish farming, for both therapeutic and prophylactic purposes [26]. Moreover, information gathered from farm managers in this study via verbal interviews (Table 2) indicated that antibiotics were used for either therapeutic or preventive purposes in majority of the sampled farms. The overuse and persistence of antibiotics in fish farms may have created conditions that select for and amplify MDR bacteria through environmental persistence, gene transfer, and ecological pressures. This may have resulted to the isolation of antibiotic resistant bacteria from this study as suggested by Tian et al. [26].

Five distinct bacterial species were isolated and identified in this study. Proteus spp. was the most commonly observed followed by E. coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae (Table 3). This observation differed from the work of Wamala et al. [27], who isolated different bacteria genera that included Aeromonas hydrophila, Aeromonas sobria, Edwardsiella tarda, Flavobacterium spp., and Streptococcus spp. in Oreochromis niloticus (Nile tilapia) and Clarias gariepinus (African catfish) in Uganda. However, similar to the report of Usman et al. [28], who isolated and identified 5 genera of Enterobacteriaceae among C. gariepinus from selected fish farms in Kano and Proteus mirabilis was the most prevalent. The observed variations between this study and previous studies by Wamala et al. [27] in bacterial genera could be attributed to various factors such as distinct fish species, culture conditions, geographic regions, and sampling and isolation techniques [29].

Wanja et al. [30] also reported high prevalence of Proteus mirabilis in samples from fish farm and source pond water in Kirinyaga County, Kenya, which they linked to the ponds' fertilization with poultry litter. The isolation of potentially pathogenic bacteria, including Salmonella spp., Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Escherichia coli, from C. gariepinus in this study is of considerable public health concern. These organisms are known to cause significant mortalities in fish, particularly under unfavorable environmental conditions associated with intensive aquaculture practices, and their presence in fish intended for human consumption poses a serious public health risk [31].

The high rate of resistance to both chloramphenicol (49%) recorded in this study is similar to findings of Agbeko et al. [32], who reported that a large proportion of bacterial isolates from fish farms in Ghana showing high resistance to beta-lactam (55.1%) and chloramphenicol (57%) antibiotics.

All farm managers, with the exception of farm B (Sauki Farm), acknowledged the use of antibiotics (Table 2). All bacteria isolated from these farms exhibited varying degrees of antibiotics resistance. This may be as a result of previous exposure of these bacteria to similar antibiotics. The use of antibiotics like tetracycline, cefoxitin and chloramphenicol in fish feed was the reason for the development of resistant bacteria, according to a study carried out at certain fish farms in Ghana's Ashanti area [33]. Since antibiotics are widely available over-the-counter in Nigeria, misuse and in appropriate disposal of these drugs are frequent [34]. According to some research, there are varied amounts of antibiotic residues in waste water and landfills [35], which has been connected to the potential introduction of antibiotics into aquatic habitats. This may also be the reason for the isolation of antibiotics resistant bacteria from fish samples from these farms, residue of antibiotics from nearby farm lands may have been washed into the fish ponds.

In this study, five (5) multi-drug resistant (MDR) bacteria from 49 isolates, including strains resistant to cefoxitin and chloramphenicol that were chosen for genotyping of antibiotics resistance genes. All of the isolates exhibited resistance to either of the antibiotics tested. The comparatively lower percentage resistance to gentamycin may be due to its lower use in Nigeria [36]. The detection of multidrug-resistant bacterial strains in the present study is consistent with the findings of Amian et al. [37], who reported the occurrence of MDR bacteria in aquaculture farms in Côte d'Ivoire. The increase in MDR is a common issue in West Africa as a result of the abusive and uncontrolled use of antibiotics for medical, veterinary, and food production [38], which may possibly account for the findings of this study. The MDR bacterial species isolated in this study, including Proteus spp. and E. coli, have been previously documented as causative agents of opportunistic infections in humans, particularly in immunocompromised individuals [39]. The detection of these bacteria carrying antimicrobial resistance genes in fish intended for human consumption is of significant public health concern, as they represent a potential reservoir for the transmission of resistant strains to humans through the food chain. The presence of beta-lactam and chloramphenicol resistance genes further underscores the risk, as these genetic determinants can be horizontally transferred to other bacteria in both aquatic environments and the human gut, potentially compromising the effectiveness of commonly used antimicrobial agents in clinical settings [40]. These microorganisms do, however, have implications for public health. In Ondo State, Nigeria, some of the bacteria species obtained in this study were also identified from healthy Clarias gariepinus [41]. Furthermore, studies have documented the presence of harmful bacteria in freshwater aquaculture environments in Clarias gariepinus [41, 42]. Because these bacteria have been linked to a variety of illnesses, their presence in fish puts fish consumers at risk.

The beta-lactam resistance genes blaTEM and blaSHV were detected in Proteus mirabilis, Escherichia coli, and Sphingomonas paucimobilis, consistent with the observed phenotypic resistance of these isolates to cefoxitin, a beta-lactam antibiotic [43]. This is similar to findings of Agbeko et al. [32] who investigated molecular determinants of antimicrobial resistance in fish farms in Ghana and detected blaTEM genes among Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Proteus mirabilis, alongside phenotypic resistance to beta-lactam antibiotics. BlaTEM and blaSHV were co-detected in Proteus mirabilis (Farms A and D) and Sphingomonas paucimobilis (Farm B), suggesting co-carriage of multiple beta-lactamase-encoding genes. Similarly, Singh et al. [44] also reported the co-occurrence of blaTEM, blaSHV, and blaCTX-M genes among Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Escherichia coli isolated from ampicillin-resistant strains in Portuguese fish farms. Collectively, these findings corroborate the widespread distribution of beta-lactam resistance genes among Gram-negative bacteria in aquaculture settings across different geographic regions, further supporting the public health significance of the resistance profiles observed in the present study.

In contrast, blaCTX-M was detected exclusively only in Escherichia coli from Farm A [45], consistent with the well-documented association between E. coli and extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) production [43]. The detection of blaCTX-M exclusively in E. coli in this study, is consistent with E. coli’s recognized role as the primary reservoir of extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) genes among Enterobacteriaceae [43]. Although previous study such as Zhang et al., [45] has reported occurrence of blaCTX-M in Proteus mirabilis and its clinical significance as opportunistic pathogen, blaCTX-M was not detected among MDR Proteus mirabilis isolates from the current study. Though the broad distribution of blaCTX-M across gram-negative bacteria nonetheless underscores its contribution to multidrug resistance and its potential implications for both hospital-acquired and community infections.

The cat1 gene, which mediates resistance to chloramphenicol, was detected in only one isolate, despite phenotypic resistance to chloramphenicol being observed across multiple MDR isolates in this study. This discrepancy between genotypic and phenotypic resistance may be explained by alternative resistance mechanisms not investigated in this study, including other cat gene variants such as cat2 and cat3, efflux pump-mediated resistance encoded by genes such as cmlA or floR, or alterations in outer membrane permeability that reduce intracellular drug accumulation [46]. This finding represents a limitation of the current study and highlights the need for broader molecular screening such as Whole genome Sequence (WGS) to fully elucidate the chloramphenicol resistance mechanisms and other resistance genes present among these isolates.

This study found that the Gram- negative bacteria (Escherichia coli, Spingomonas paucimobilis, and Proteus mirabilis) possessed at least one of the tested beta-lactamase resistance genes, with blaTEM being the most prevalent. Customers' health, financial security, and social well-being are greatly endangered by the presence of these resistance genes among bacterial isolates from cat fish in fish farms from this study, which has implications for public health as there is capacity for these resistance genes to spread to other bacteria if they are present on mobile genetic elements [47, 48].

The public health significance of Proteus mirabilis carrying antimicrobial resistance genes cannot be overlooked. Previous studies have documented Proteus mirabilis as an important opportunistic pathogen capable of causing urinary tract infections, diarrhoea, and keratitis in humans, with resistance to commonly used antimicrobial agents such as chloramphenicol known to complicate treatment outcomes and increase associated mortality [49]. While the present study did not evaluate the pathogenic potential of the isolated strains directly, the detection of cat1 and other resistance genes in Proteus mirabilis from fish intended for human consumption warrants serious public health attention.

Conclusion

This study showed that fish from these farms in Katsina State harbor bacteria resistant to commonly used antibiotics in these farms' localities. The study further confirmed the presence of beta-lactam resistance genes, specifically blaTEM and blaSHV, among the characterized MDR bacterial isolates. The high prevalence of these genes across multiple bacterial species is of particular concern, as their movement to other bacteria in aquatic environments and along the food chain could be facilitated if present on mobile genetic elements like plasmids, insertion sequences, transposons, integrons and gene cassettes. The potential dissemination of these resistance determinants to human pathogens through fish consumption, therefore, represents a significant public health threat, particularly in communities where C. gariepinus constitutes a major dietary protein source. Improving education and awareness among fish farmers, encouraging ethical antibiotic use, and implementing stronger regulations are essential measures for reducing the hazards posed by antibiotic resistance in aquaculture environments. Interventions to reduce antibiotic use in fish farming, as well as studies to determine the precise mechanism by which these genes causing antibiotic resistance are transmitted from one organism to another within these fish ponds, are recommended.
