Coloring-allowed Invariants and the 4-phases Functions of Knotoids
Haocong Chen, Jiacheng An, Fengling Li

Abstract
. In recent years, numerous polynomial invariants of knotoids have been constructed, some of which are defined with the signs of the crossings. In this paper, the coloring-allowed invariants of planar knotoids, which is a class of planar knotoid invariants defined with the coloring number are introduced. We demonstrate several basic properties of the coloring number and some examples of coloring-allowed invariants, among which the 4-phases functions are discussed in detail, including their invariance and properties.



\displaystyle Keywords:
Planar knotoid; Gauss diagram; coloring number; coloring-allowed invariant; 4-phases function

1
Introduction

Knotoids can be viewed as knots which are not "closed". The concept of knotoid was first proposed by Turaev in
1
in 2012. A knotoid diagram is a generic immersion of an oriented unit interval
\displaystyle[0,1]
into an oriented surface, with over-under information assigned at double points. Two knotoid diagrams on a surface are equivalent if and only if they can be transformed into each other by a finite number of Reidemeister moves and isotopies on this surface. The corresponding equivalence classes are called knotoids in the surface. See
1
for a detailed introduction.

The theory of knotoids can be regarded as a generalization of classical knot theory, and many invariants of knots can be extended to knotoids. Various studies on invariants of knotoids have been presented so far. Gügümcü and Kauffman generalized knotoids to virtual knotoids by introducing virtual crossings and constructed several new invariants in
7
. In
17
, Gügümcü and Nelson considered biquandle colorings for knotoids in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\displaystyle S^{2}
, and they constructed several coloring invariants for knotoids derived as enhancements of the biquandle counting invariant. In
22
, Gügümcü and Kauffman constructed quantum invariants for planar Morse knotoid diagrams. A generalization of quantum knot invariants to a knotoid setting was given by Moltmaker in
21
. In
18
, Moltmaker and van der Veen showed how invariants of knotoids generally give rise to well-behaved measures of how much an open curve is knotted, and improved the classification of planar knotoids with up to five crossings with these invariants. In
19
, Elhamdadi, Moltmaker and Saito defined several new planar knotoid invariants based on quandles which are able to detect planarity. Miyazawa defined a multi-variable polynomial invariant for knotoids and linkoids, which is an enhancement of the bracket polynomial for knotoids introduced by Turaev in
20
.

In recent years, numerous polynomial invariants of classical or virtual knots and knotoids have been constructed, among which some are defined via Gauss diagrams or the weight of the crossings. For instance, Kauffman defined the affine index polynomial of a knotoid diagram by

\displaystyle P_{D}(t)=\sum_{c\in C(D)}\sgn(c)(t^{d(c)}-1)
,

where
\displaystyle d(c)
is the degree of a chord in the Gauss diagram of
\displaystyle D
, which is referred to as the weight of a crossing in
12
.
In
15
, Kim, Im and Lee introduced the index polynomial of knotoids and the
\displaystyle n
th polynomial invariant of virtual knotoids by means of Gauss diagrams.
The index polynomial
\displaystyle F_{D}(t)
of a knotoid diagram
\displaystyle D
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle F_{D}(t)=\sum_{c\in C(D)}\sgn(c)(t^{i(c)}-1)
.

Feng and Li gave a new two-variable invariant of knotoids via Gauss diagrams in
8
, which is defined as:

\displaystyle F_{D}(u,v)=\sum_{c\in C(D)}\sgn(c)(u^{\widetilde{h}_{c}(v)}-1)
.

Feng, Li and Vesnin proposed a new invariant with 3 variables of knotoids in
9
, which is defined by

\displaystyle H_{D}(x,y,v)=\sum_{c\in C(D)}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sgn(c)(x^{g_{c}^{n}(v)}-1)y^{n}
.

The sign of a crossing
\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\sgn(c)
appears in each of the invariants mentioned above. The effect of an
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}
move on these invariants has to be taken into consideration when one constructs them. To avoid this, we will try to replace it by the so-called coloring number
\displaystyle u(c)
, which can be considered as a modification to the weight of a crossing. The notion of the weight of a crossing has been introduced in
7
,
10
and
12
, and the coloring number has been introduced in
3
and
4
, where it is still called the "weight" of a crossing. Kauffman has proved in
12
that the value of the weight at each crossing in an arbitrary classical knot diagram is 0, and so is the coloring number. Hence it is futile to construct invariants of classical knots with the coloring number as a factor of the coefficient of each term, since one can only obtain trivial invariants by this approach. Therefore we consider invariants of knotoids defined with the coloring number, namely the so-called coloring-allowed invariants of knotoids. As an example of coloring-allowed invariants, the 4-phases functions will be discussed in detail.

In this paper, some basic notions are given in Section 2. In Section 3, we illustrate the definition and properties of coloring numbers, after which the definition of coloring-allowed invariants and some examples will be given. Four coloring-allowed invariants which look quite similar, namely the 4-phases functions, are defined in Section 4, and their invariance is verified in this section. Discussions related to the properties of the 4-phases invariants are presented in the last part of Section 4.

The degree of

By the definition of

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{|d(c)|}=\left\{0,1,\cdots,|d(c)|-1\right\}

With the function
\displaystyle\phi_{c}

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle g_{c}(v)=\sum_{e\in r(c)}\sgn(e)v^{\phi_{c}(d(e))}-\sum_{e\in l(c)}\sgn(e)v^{\phi_{c}(-d(e))}

By the definition of
\displaystyle\phi_{c}

\displaystyle v^{|d(c)|}=v^{0}=1

\displaystyle g_{c}(v)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}[v,v^{-1}]/(v^{|d(c)|}-1)

\displaystyle v=1

\displaystyle|d(c)|=1

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} g_{c}(v)=\sum_{e\in r(c)}\sgn(e)-\sum_{e\in l(c)}\sgn(e)=\sum_{e\in r(c)}\sgn(e)+\sum_{e\in l(c)}\left(-\sgn(e)\right)\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\sgn(e)

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle-\sgn(e)

\displaystyle e

Another useful fact about the function

\displaystyle v^{\phi_{c}(-k)}\cdot v^{\phi_{c}(k)}=v^{\phi_{c}(-k)+\phi_{c}(k)}=v^{0}=1

\displaystyle v^{\phi_{c}(-k)}=v^{-\phi_{c}(k)}

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle D
be a planar knotoid diagram, then

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{c\in C(D)}u(c)=0
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle D_{1}
and
\displaystyle D_{2}
be 2 planar knotoid diagrams which differ by a forbidden move. Suppose that
\displaystyle c_{0}
is the crossing generated by the forbidden move. Without loss of generality, we may assume
\displaystyle c_{0}\in D_{1}
. Then the degree of a chord which passes through
\displaystyle c_{0}
adds 1 or subtracts 1, depending on its direction and the sign of
\displaystyle c_{0}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum\limits_{c\in r^{+}(c_{0})}d_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in r^{+}(c_{0})}d_{D_{2}}(c)&=\sum\limits_{c\in r^{+}(c_{0})}(-\sgn(c_{0}))=-\sgn(c_{0})|r^{+}(c_{0})|,\\
\sum\limits_{c\in r^{-}(c_{0})}d_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in r^{-}(c_{0})}d_{D_{2}}(c)&=\sum\limits_{c\in r^{-}(c_{0})}(-\sgn(c_{0}))=-\sgn(c_{0})|r^{-}(c_{0})|,\\
\sum\limits_{c\in l^{+}(c_{0})}d_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in l^{+}(c_{0})}d_{D_{2}}(c)&=\sum\limits_{c\in l^{+}(c_{0})}\sgn(c_{0})=\sgn(c_{0})|l^{+}(c_{0})|,\\
\sum\limits_{c\in l^{-}(c_{0})}d_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in l^{-}(c_{0})}d_{D_{2}}(c)&=\sum\limits_{c\in l^{-}(c_{0})}\sgn(c_{0})=\sgn(c_{0})|l^{-}(c_{0})|.\\
\end{aligned}

\displaystyle c_{0}
in
\displaystyle G(D_{1})

Moreover, by Proposition
3.1
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum\limits_{c\in r^{+}(c_{0})}u_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in r^{+}(c_{0})}u_{D_{2}}(c)&=-\sgn(c_{0})|r^{+}(c_{0})|,\\
\sum\limits_{c\in r^{-}(c_{0})}u_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in r^{-}(c_{0})}u_{D_{2}}(c)&=\sgn(c_{0})|r^{-}(c_{0})|,\\
\sum\limits_{c\in l^{+}(c_{0})}u_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in l^{+}(c_{0})}u_{D_{2}}(c)&=\sgn(c_{0})|l^{+}(c_{0})|,\\
\sum\limits_{c\in l^{-}(c_{0})}u_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in l^{-}(c_{0})}u_{D_{2}}(c)&=-\sgn(c_{0})|l^{-}(c_{0})|.\\
\end{aligned}

It is evident that
\displaystyle u_{D_{1}}(c)=u_{D_{2}}(c)
for a chord
\displaystyle c
which does not cross
\displaystyle c_{0}
. As a result,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\sum\limits_{c\in C(D_{1})}u_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in C(D_{2})}u_{D_{2}}(c)-u(c_{0})\\
=&\sum\limits_{c\in C(D_{2})}u_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in C(D_{2})}u_{D_{2}}(c)\\
=&\left(\sum\limits_{c\in r^{+}(c_{0})}u_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in r^{+}(c_{0})}u_{D_{2}}(c)\right)+\left(\sum\limits_{c\in r^{-}(c_{0})}u_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in r^{-}(c_{0})}u_{D_{2}}(c)\right)\\
+&\left(\sum\limits_{c\in l^{+}(c_{0})}u_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in l^{+}(c_{0})}u_{D_{2}}(c)\right)+\left(\sum\limits_{c\in l^{-}(c_{0})}u_{D_{1}}(c)-\sum\limits_{c\in l^{-}(c_{0})}u_{D_{2}}(c)\right)\\
=&-\sgn(c_{0})|r^{+}(c_{0})|+\sgn(c_{0})|r^{-}(c_{0})|+\sgn(c_{0})|l^{+}(c_{0})|-\sgn(c_{0})|l^{-}(c_{0})|\\
=&-\sgn(c_{0})\left(|r^{+}(c_{0})|-|r^{-}(c_{0})|-|l^{+}(c_{0})|+|l^{-}(c_{0})|\right)\\
=&-\sgn(c_{0})d(c_{0})\\
=&-u(c_{0}).\end{aligned}

Hence

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{c\in C(D_{1})}u_{D_{1}}(c)=\sum\limits_{c\in C(D_{2})}u_{D_{2}}(c)
.

Then the conclusion holds obviously by the formula above, since
\displaystyle D
can be deformed into a trivial knotoid diagram by a finite sequence of forbidden moves, and there is no crossing in a trivial knotoid diagram. This completes the proof of Proposition 3.2.

Our aim is to construct invariants of knotoids of the form

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{c\in C(D)}u(c)f_{c}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
,

where
\displaystyle f_{c}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
is a function defined at each crossing
\displaystyle c\in C(D)
. The definition given below can be used in this kind of invariants to construct more invariants.

Definition 3.1
.

(Coloring-allowed function)
Let
\displaystyle D
be a knotoid diagram. Suppose that
\displaystyle f_{c}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
is an
\displaystyle n
-variable function defined at each crossing
\displaystyle c\in C(D)
,
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the function
\displaystyle f_{c}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
is called a
coloring-allowed function
if it satisfies the conditions below:

(1)For an arbitrary
\displaystyle\Omega_{2}a
move operated on
\displaystyle D
,

\displaystyle f_{c_{1}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=f_{c_{2}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
,

where
\displaystyle c_{1}
and
\displaystyle c_{2}
are the crossings generated by the
\displaystyle\Omega_{2}a
move;

(2)For an arbitrary
\displaystyle\Omega_{3}a
move operated on
\displaystyle D
,

\displaystyle f_{c_{i},D}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=f_{c_{i},D^{\prime}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})

for
\displaystyle i=1,2,3
, where
\displaystyle c_{1}
,
\displaystyle c_{2}
and
\displaystyle c_{3}
are the crossings related to the
\displaystyle\Omega_{3}a
move,
\displaystyle D^{\prime}
is the knotoid diagram obtained by
\displaystyle D
after the
\displaystyle\Omega_{3}a
move;

(3)
\displaystyle f_{c}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
preserves for an arbitrary crossing
\displaystyle c
which is not involved in an
\displaystyle\Omega
-move.

With the definition of coloring-allowed functions, we can define the coloring-allowed invariants of knotoids.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle D
be a planar knotoid diagram,
\displaystyle f_{c}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
be a coloring-allowed function, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{D}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=\sum\limits_{c\in C(D)}u(c)f_{c}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})

is an invariant of
\displaystyle D
. Such an invariant is called a
coloring-allowed invariant
of knotoids.

Proof.

In
2
, Polyak proved that all oriented versions of Reidemeister moves for knot and link diagrams can be generated by a set consisting of only 4 oriented Reidemeister moves. Hence it suffices to show that
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{D}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
is invariant under the oriented Reidemeister moves
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}a
,
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}b
,

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}a
and
\displaystyle\Omega_{3}a
of planar knotoid diagrams.

Suppose that
\displaystyle D
and
\displaystyle D_{1}
are two oriented knotoid diagrams which are related by an
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}a
or
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}b
move, and
\displaystyle c_{0}
is the new crossing in
\displaystyle D_{1}
generated by this move. By Lemma
3.1
(1) and Definition
3.1
(3), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{D_{1}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})-\mathcal{A}_{D}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=u(c_{0})f_{c_{0}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=0
.

Hence
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{D}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
remains invariant under
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}a
and
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}b
moves.

Suppose that
\displaystyle D
and
\displaystyle D_{2}
are two oriented knotoid diagrams which are related by an
\displaystyle\Omega_{2}a
move, and
\displaystyle c_{1}
,
\displaystyle c_{2}
are the new crossings in
\displaystyle D_{2}
generated by this move. By Lemma
3.1
(2) and Definition
3.1
(1)(3),

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{A}_{D_{2}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})-\mathcal{A}_{D}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=u(c_{1})f_{c_{1}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})+u(c_{2})f_{c_{2}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=0.\end{aligned}

Thus
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{D}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
remains invariant under
\displaystyle\Omega_{2}a
moves.

Suppose that
\displaystyle D
and
\displaystyle D_{3}
are two oriented knotoid diagrams which are related by an
\displaystyle\Omega_{3}a
move,
\displaystyle c_{1}
,
\displaystyle c_{2}
and
\displaystyle c_{3}
are the crossings related to this move. It follows from Lemma
3.1
(3) and Definition
3.1
(2)(3) that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{A}_{D_{3}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})-\mathcal{A}_{D}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{3}u_{D_{3}}(c_{i})f_{c_{i},D_{3}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})-\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{3}u_{D}(c_{i})f_{c_{i},D}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=0.\end{aligned}

Therefore
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{D}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
remains invariant under
\displaystyle\Omega_{3}a
moves.
∎

In this section, we will introduce 4 coloring-alowed invariants, which are called the 4-phases functions, and illustrate several properties of them.

Definition 4.1. (The 4-phases functions)
Let
\displaystyle D
be a planar knotoid diagram and
\displaystyle c\in C(D)
.

Let
\displaystyle f_{c}^{B}(x,v)=x^{g_{c}(v)}
, then the
\displaystyle B
-function
of
\displaystyle D
is defined by

\displaystyle B_{D}(x,v)=\mathcal{A}_{D}^{B}(x,v)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{c\in C(D)}u(c)f_{c}^{B}(x,v)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{c\in C(D)}u(c)x^{g_{c}(v)}
.

Let
\displaystyle f_{c}^{C}(x,v)=x^{h_{c}(v)}
, then the
\displaystyle C
-function
of
\displaystyle D
is defined by

\displaystyle C_{D}(x,v)=\mathcal{A}_{D}^{C}(x,v)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{c\in C(D)}u(c)f_{c}^{C}(x,v)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{c\in C(D)}u(c)x^{h_{c}(v)}
.

Let
\displaystyle f_{c}^{\widetilde{B}}(x,v)=x^{\widetilde{g}_{c}(v)}
, then the
\displaystyle\widetilde{B}
-function
of
\displaystyle D
is defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}_{D}(x,v)=\mathcal{A}_{D}^{\widetilde{B}}(x,v)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{c\in C(D)}u(c)f_{c}^{\widetilde{B}}(x,v)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{c\in C(D)}u(c)x^{\widetilde{g}_{c}(v)}
.

Let
\displaystyle f_{c}^{\widetilde{C}}(x,v)=x^{\widetilde{h}_{c}(v)}
, then the
\displaystyle\widetilde{C}
-function
of
\displaystyle D
is defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{D}(x,v)=\mathcal{A}_{D}^{\widetilde{C}}(x,v)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{c\in C(D)}u(c)f_{c}^{\widetilde{C}}(x,v)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{c\in C(D)}u(c)x^{\widetilde{h}_{c}(v)}
.

The number of crossings in a knotoid diagram is finite, hence there are only finite non-zero terms in the 4 series above, which ensures the convergence of them. These functions given above are collectively referred to as the
4-phases functions
.

.

Let
\displaystyle D
be a planar knotoid diagram, then the 4-phases functions
\displaystyle B_{D}(x,v)
,
\displaystyle C_{D}(x,v)
,

and
\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{D}(x,v)

\displaystyle D
.

Proof.

Here we prove the invariance of
\displaystyle B_{D}(x,v)
as an instance, since the invariance of
\displaystyle C_{D}(x,v)
,
\displaystyle\widetilde{B}_{D}(x,v)
and
\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{D}(x,v)
can be verified analogously. By Theorem
3.1
, it suffices to show that
\displaystyle x^{g_{c}(v)}
is a coloring-allowed function. Thereby a proof showing that
\displaystyle g_{c}(v)
satisfies (1), (2) and (3) in Definition

\displaystyle u(c)

\displaystyle\sgn(c)
of such a crossing
\displaystyle c
preserves as well. Then by Proposition
3.1
,
\displaystyle d(c)
preserves during an
\displaystyle\Omega
-move. As a consequence, for 2 such crossings
\displaystyle c
and
\displaystyle e
which satisfies
\displaystyle c\cap e\neq\varnothing
for the corresponding chords in the Gauss diagram,
\displaystyle e
contributes equally to
\displaystyle g_{c,D}(v)
and
\displaystyle g_{c,D^{\prime}}(v)
. Therefore we only need to consider the effect of the crossings involved in an
\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle g_{c}(v)
.

. The invariance of

.
\displaystyle\forall c_{1}\in C(D_{1}),c_{2}\in C(D_{2})
, the endpoints of
\displaystyle c_{2}
lie on one side of the chord
\displaystyle c_{1}
in
\displaystyle G(D_{1}D_{2})
, hence
\displaystyle c_{2}
contributes 0 to
\displaystyle d_{D_{1}D_{2}}(c_{1})
, which gives
\displaystyle d_{D_{1}D_{2}}(c_{1})=d_{D_{1}}(c_{1})
. Moreover,
\displaystyle c_{1}\cap c_{2}=\varnothing
for the chords
\displaystyle c_{1}
and
\displaystyle c_{2}
in
\displaystyle G(D_{1}D_{2})
, thus
\displaystyle c_{2}
contributes 0 to
\displaystyle g_{c_{1},D_{1}D_{2}}(v)
as well, which implies
\displaystyle g_{c_{1},D_{1}D_{2}}(v)=g_{c_{1},D_{1}}(v)
. Analogously,
\displaystyle g_{c_{2},D_{1}D_{2}}(v)=g_{c_{2},D_{2}}(v)
. In a word,
\displaystyle g_{c,D_{1}D_{2}}(v)=g_{c,D_{i}}(v)
for each crossing
\displaystyle c
in
\displaystyle D_{i}
,
\displaystyle i=1,2
. Finally we obtain
\displaystyle B_{D_{1}D_{2}}(x,v)=B_{D_{1}}(x,v)+B_{D_{2}}(x,v)
.

∎

In Example
4.1
, we have seen that
\displaystyle C_{D}(x,v)=0
. Indeed, among the 4-phases functions,
\displaystyle C_{D}(x,v)
is more likely to be trivial in some cases. The corollary given below indicates that it is not a coincidence.

Two planar knotoid diagrams are said to be homotopic if one can be deformed into the other by a finite sequence of crossing changes.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle D_{1}
and
\displaystyle D_{2}
be two homotopic planar knotoid diagrams. Then
\displaystyle C_{D_{1}}(x,v)=C_{D_{2}}(x,v)
.

Proof.

By the hypothesis,
\displaystyle D_{2}
can be obtained from
\displaystyle D_{1}
by a finite sequence of crossing changes, hence we may assume that
\displaystyle D_{1}
and
\displaystyle D_{2}
are two planar knotoid diagrams which differ by exactly one crossing change at a crossing
\displaystyle c_{0}
, and the conclusion follows by an induction on the original number of crossing changes.

The Gauss diagrams corresponding to
\displaystyle D_{1}
and
\displaystyle D_{2}
are shown in Figure
22
. By the figure it is easy to see that
\displaystyle\rho_{D_{1}}(c_{0})=\rho_{D_{2}}(c_{0})
,
\displaystyle\lambda_{D_{1}}(c_{0})=\lambda_{D_{2}}(c_{0})
and
\displaystyle d_{D_{1}}(c)=d_{D_{2}}(c)
, which indicates that
\displaystyle c
contributes equally to
\displaystyle h_{c_{0},D_{1}}(v)
and
\displaystyle h_{c_{0},D_{2}}(v)
for an arbitrary crossing
\displaystyle c
with
\displaystyle c_{0}\cap c\neq\varnothing
for the corresponding chords in the Gauss diagrams, hence
\displaystyle h_{c_{0},D_{1}}(v)=h_{c_{0},D_{2}}(v)
. Moreover, for such a crossing
\displaystyle c
, since
\displaystyle\sgn_{D_{2}}(c_{0})=-\sgn_{D_{1}}(c_{0})
,
\displaystyle d_{D_{2}}(c_{0})=-d_{D_{1}}(c_{0})
and the fact that the directions of the chord
\displaystyle c_{0}
in
\displaystyle G(D_{1})
and
\displaystyle G(D_{2})

are opposite, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} h_{c,D_{1}}(v)-h_{c,D_{2}}(v)&=\varepsilon\sgn_{D_{1}}(c_{0})v^{\phi_{c}(\varepsilon d_{D_{1}}(c_{0}))}-\left(-\varepsilon\sgn_{D_{2}}(c_{0})v^{\phi_{c}(-\varepsilon d_{D_{2}}(c_{0}))}\right)\\
&=\varepsilon\sgn_{D_{1}}(c_{0})v^{\phi_{c}(\varepsilon d_{D_{1}}(c_{0}))}-\varepsilon\sgn_{D_{1}}(c_{0})v^{\phi_{c}(\varepsilon d_{D_{1}}(c_{0}))}=0,\end{aligned}

where
\displaystyle\varepsilon\in\left\{-1,1\right\}
depends on the direction of
\displaystyle c_{0}
. By the illustration above, obviously
\displaystyle C_{D_{1}}(x,v)=C_{D_{2}}(x,v)
.

∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\displaystyle D
be a planar knotoid diagram. If
\displaystyle D
is homotopic to a trivial knotoid diagram, then
\displaystyle C_{D}(x,v)=0
.

Corollary
4.3
follows from the fact that there is no crossing in a trivial knotoid diagram. In this way we can see that
\displaystyle C_{D}(x,v)
vanishes at least for those knotoid diagrams which are homotopic to a trivial knotoid diagram. Therefore it is natural to ask if this is true for all planar knotoid diagrams, hence the question given below arises naturally:

Question.
Is it true for every planar knotoid diagram
\displaystyle D
that
\displaystyle C_{D}(x,v)=0
?

However, this question has not been solved yet, since we are not familiar with the homotopy classes of planar knotoid diagrams which have not been studied thoroughly until now, and it is quite tough to decide whether
\displaystyle C_{D}(x,v)
vanishes or not for an arbitrary planar knotoid diagram which is not homotopic to a trivial knotoid diagram without a clear picture on the homotopy classes of planar knotoid diagrams. We are looking forward to an answer to this question.
