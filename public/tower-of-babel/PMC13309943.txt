Biomarker-guided immunomodulation in septic shock: navigating controversies and therapeutic implications for Critical Care
Authors: Yuxiu Dai, Hengyang Liu, Dingwen Xu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor

The recent investigation by Roquetaillade et al. provides compelling evidence that soluble Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid Cells-1 (sTREM-1) serves as a robust predictor of 90-day mortality and major adverse kidney events (MAKE) in ICU patients [1]. Notably, its predictive performance rivals that of complex multiparametric machine learning models, suggesting that a single, biologically informative biomarker may suffice for risk stratification. While these findings reinforce the role of innate immune activation in critical illness, they also intersect with ongoing controversies regarding biomarker standardization, therapeutic translation, and dynamic monitoring, all of which are of paramount importance to both clinicians and critical care nurses.

First, the lack of a harmonized cutoff value for sTREM-1 remains a fundamental barrier to clinical implementation. Roquetaillade et al. propose thresholds of approximately 1360 pg/mL for mortality and 1420 pg/mL for MAKE [1]. However, these values contrast sharply with earlier sepsis studies that identified 400 pg/mL as a prognostic threshold [2, 3], and with post-hoc analyses of the ASTONISH trial suggesting 532 pg/mL or even 1050 pg/mL as optimal for selecting candidates for nangibotide therapy [2, 4]. This discrepancy is not merely statistical; it poses a direct challenge to critical care practice. For bedside clinicians and nurses responsible for early goal-directed therapy, a four-fold difference in threshold translates into vastly different risk assignments and resource allocation. The variability likely stems from assay heterogeneity (Elecsys vs. ELISA), differences in sample handling, and the inflammatory milieu of specific populations (e.g., surgical vs. medical ICU patients). Without standardized assays and universally accepted cutoffs, integrating sTREM-1 into routine ICU panels remains premature.

Second, the translational promise of sTREM-1 as a companion diagnostic for TREM-1 inhibition is clouded by conflicting trial data. While nangibotide showed signals of benefit in patients with sTREM-1 ≥ 532 pg/mL, the pivotal ASTONISH trial failed to meet its primary endpoint of SOFA score improvement at the predefined 400 pg/mL threshold [2]. This raises a crucial question for critical care providers: Does a high sTREM-1 level merely identify the sickest patients, or does it specifically identify those whose pathology is driven by TREM-1 hyperactivation? Roquetaillade et al.’s finding that sTREM-1 predicts MAKE further complicates the picture [1]. It suggests a crosstalk between systemic inflammation and renal microvascular dysfunction. For nursing staff managing fluid balance and renal replacement therapy, understanding whether sTREM-1 reflects irreversible organ damage or a modifiable pathway is essential for tailoring interventions. Future interventional trials must clarify whether targeting TREM-1 improves not just survival, but functional outcomes like renal recovery.

Third, the field is grappling with the tension between static single-point measurements and the dynamic nature of critical illness. Roquetaillade et al. utilized a single admission sTREM-1 measurement. While convenient, this static approach fails to capture the evolving trajectory of immune dysregulation. Contemporary reviews emphasize that longitudinal biomarker trajectories, as opposed to isolated snapshots, better predict patient course [5]. For instance, a rising sTREM-1 trend over 48 h may signal refractory inflammation requiring escalation of care, whereas a declining trend might justify de-escalation of vasopressors or weaning of support. Incorporating dynamic sTREM-1 kinetics into predictive models, alongside other novel markers of sepsis-associated AKI [6], represents a frontier for precision nursing and medical management. Furthermore, the distinction between early and late AKI subphenotypes [7] demands that we move beyond static “snapshots” of biomarkers toward continuous, real-time monitoring systems.

Finally, the generalizability of sTREM-1 across diverse ICU populations warrants scrutiny. While Roquetaillade et al.validated their findings in the MARS cohort, the broader literature reveals that sTREM-1 elevations are not uniform across all infections or organ failures [3]. Moreover, sophisticated deep-learning models like ORAKLE demonstrate that time-series data can outperform static biomarkers in predicting MAKE [8]. This suggests that while sTREM-1 is a powerful piece of the puzzle, it may need to be integrated into multimodal platforms rather than used in isolation.

Collectively, these controversies do not diminish the importance of Roquetaillade et al.’s findings but rather highlight that sTREM-1 sits at the intersection of immune phenotyping, precision trial design, and dynamic prognostication. Resolving these debates will require harmonized assays, prospective mechanism-targeted trials, and integration of static and longitudinal frameworks.
