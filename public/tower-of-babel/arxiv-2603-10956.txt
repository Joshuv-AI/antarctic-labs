Linear Readout of Neural Manifolds with Continuous Variables
Will Slatton, Chi-Ning Chou, SueYeon Chung

Abstract
Brains and artificial neural networks compute with continuous variables such as object position or stimulus orientation. However, the complex variability in neural responses makes it difficult to link internal representational structure to task performance. We develop a statistical-mechanical theory of regression capacity that relates linear decoding efficiency of continuous variables to geometric properties of neural manifolds. Our theory handles complex neural variability and applies to real data, revealing increasing capacity for decoding object position and size along the monkey visual stream.



I
Introduction

Navigating through space requires estimating continuous variables like spatial position from highly variable sensory inputs. For a neural population to usefully represent position, it must enable a downstream readout to decode this information while filtering out irrelevant stimulus features. A similar requirement extends to neural representations of any continuous variables.

Classic neuroscience experiments have revealed neurons tuned to continuous variables, including stimulus orientation in visual cortex
26
, spatial location in the hippocampus
23
;
34
, head direction
41
, and movement direction
22
. Moving beyond single neuron tuning statistics
39
, recent advances in multi-neuron recordings have uncovered population-level structures shaped by mixed selectivity
36
and correlated variability
3
. These observations call for a theoretical framework that links the geometry of high-dimensional population codes to decoding efficiency of continuous variables.

Recently, neural manifolds—collections of neural population responses grouped by task-relevant variables—have emerged as a powerful abstraction for capturing such complex, structured variability
11
. Manifold capacity theory
12
;
43
;
8
, a recent theoretical framework using methods from statistical physics, quantifies how well a downstream neuron can distinguish discrete categories (e.g., pictures of cats versus pictures of dogs) based on the geometry of manifold-like neural activity patterns—also known as neural manifolds. This framework has been applied to both biological
(
20
;
44
;
35
;
25
)
and artificial neural networks
(
14
;
30
;
40
;
16
;
28
;
27
;
10
;
9
)
, revealing how the shape and arrangement of neural manifolds determine the ability of downstream neurons to perform classification.

However, many tasks involve continuous variables (regression) rather than discrete classes (classification). Unlike for classification, no general theory connects manifold geometry to regression performance. Existing machine learning approaches often use strong parametric assumptions, offering little geometric insight into how population variability affects linear decodability

;

;

;

In this letter, we extend manifold capacity theory to regression. We develop a statistical mechanical theory for
regression capacity
—a measure of linear readout performance for continuous variables (see
Fig. 1
)—and derive explicit formulas applicable to both synthetic models and data. For synthetic models such as spherical manifolds, we find closed-form solutions that link manifold geometry to readout performance. For example, regression capacity increases as the dimensionality and radius of the manifolds decrease.
We then apply our framework to a visual neuroscience dataset
29
, demonstrating that representations of continuous object features become more efficient along the macaque ventral stream (
Sec. III.2
). Our framework lays the foundation for identifying the geometric principles that govern the linear decodability of continuous variables in complex, high-dimensional datasets from neuroscience and machine learning.

II
Theoretical framework

Consider decoding a continuous variable (e.g., stimulus orientation;
Fig. 1
a) based on the activity of
N
neurons. We consider
P
discrete levels of the continuous variable, with labels
\{y^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
. The collection of neural population responses mapped to a given bin, each a vector in the
N
-dimensional activity space, forms a manifold
\mathcal{M}^{\mu}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

By analogy to the classification theory
12
;
8
, we define the system’s
load
as the ratio
P/N
. As the load increases, the system becomes
“less linearly regressible”
—that is, the volume of admissible linear readout weights
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
decreases. This motivates defining the regression capacity
\alpha
of the system as
the maximal load under which a linear regressor still exists
.
In the following, we study Gaussian mean-field models
12
;
43
;
8
for analytical characterization and instance-based models
8
for data analysis.

II.1
Mean-field theory for analytical study

In the
Gaussian mean-field models

12
;
43
;
8
, we are interested in the thermodynamic limit (
P,N\to\infty
) to enable analytical characterization. We parameterize manifolds as
\mathcal{M}^{\mu}=\{\mathbf{x}^{\mu}=\mathbf{u}^{\mu}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{D}s^{\mu}_{i}\mathbf{u}^{\mu}_{i}\,:\,\mathbf{s}^{\mu}\in\mathcal{S}\}
where
\mathbf{u}^{\mu}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the center of the
\mu
-th manifold,
\mathbf{u}^{\mu}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}^{\mu}_{D}
is a basis for the intrinsic part of the manifold, and
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a
D
-dimensional convex set defining the manifold shape. For example, when the manifold is a
D
-dimensional L2 sphere with radius
R
, then
\mathcal{S}=\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\,:\,\|\mathbf{s}\|_{2}=R\}
.

The manifolds in the mean-field model are randomly embedded in an ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with the embedding directions playing the role of quenched disorder in spin glass theory. The manifold bases
\mathbf{U}=\{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\mu}\}_{\mu,i}
are sampled from a correlated Gaussian distribution

p(\mathbf{U})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{N}{2}\sum_{\mu,\nu,i,j,\ell}(\Sigma^{-1})^{\mu,i}_{\nu,j}u^{\mu}_{i,\ell}u^{\nu}_{j,\ell}\right)

(1)

where
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{P(D+1)\times P(D+1)}
is a covariance tensor,
\mu,\nu
index the
P
manifolds,
i,j
index the
D
intrinsic coordinates within the
\mu
-th and
\nu
-th manifold, and
\ell
indexes the
N
neurons.
Finally, the manifold labels
\mathbf{y}=\{y^{\mu}\}
are sampled from a distribution. While our capacity formula applies to arbitrary label distributions, in
Sec. III.1
we focus on the case where the labels are drawn from a Gaussian distribution, which further simplifies the formula for geometric analysis.

To analytically study the regression capacity in the mean-field model, we consider the
Gardner volume

21
, which quantifies the volume of linear readout vectors
\bm{w}
that achieve regression error within a tolerance
\varepsilon\geq 0
:

Z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}d\mathbf{w}\prod_{\mu,\mathbf{x}^{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}^{\mu}}\Theta(\varepsilon-|\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\mu}-y^{\mu}|).

(2)

The Heaviside function
\Theta(\cdot)
enforces
|\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\mu}-y^{\mu}|\leq\varepsilon
, where
\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\mu}
denotes the inner product between the two vectors and is the decoded label for
\mathbf{x}^{\mu}
and
y^{\mu}
the target label. This approach follows earlier statistical mechanical theories of classification manifold capacity
12
;
43
;
32
;
8
and has been used in related classification settings
5
;
38
;
15
;
13
.

\overline{\log Z}

\alpha_{\textsf{mf}}(\varepsilon)=\lim_{P\to\infty}P\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{t},\bm{y}}[F_{\textsf{mf}}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{y})]\right)^{-1},

(3)

where
F_{\textsf{mf}}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{y})=\min_{\bm{w}^{+}\in\mathcal{A}_{\textsf{mf}}(\bm{y})^{+}}\|\bm{t}-\bm{w}^{+}\|^{2}
is a field related to the projection of
\mathbf{t}
to a convex cone
\mathcal{A}_{\textsf{mf}}(\bm{y})^{+}=\{\tau\bm{w}:\bm{w}\in\mathcal{A}_{\textsf{mf}}(\bm{y}),\tau\geq 0\}
with
\mathcal{A}_{\textsf{mf}}(\bm{y})=\bigcap_{\mu=1}^{P}\bigcap_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\left\{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{P(D+1)}:\left|\mathbf{w}\cdot((\Sigma^{1/2})^{\mu}\mathbf{s})-y^{\mu}\right|\leq\varepsilon\right\}
being the set of all admissible linear regressor vectors. Here
(\Sigma^{1/2})^{\mu}
denotes the
\mu
-th column block of the square root of the covariance matrix
\Sigma^{1/2}
. See the Supplementary Materials (SM) Section SII
1
for details. The mean-field formula Eq. (
3
) enables analytical calculation of regression capacity for the synthetic models studied in
Sec. III
.

II.2
Instance-based theory for data analysis

In the
instance-based model

8
for data analysis, the number of experimental conditions
P
and recorded units
N
are finite. Let
\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\mu},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{M}^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be the neural activity vectors for the
\mu
-th experimental condition (e.g.,
M
trials with the same stimulus angle
\theta^{\mu}
but with other stimulus features potentially varying). The neural manifold for this condition is the set
\mathcal{M}^{\mu}:=\{\mathbf{x}^{\mu}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
and it has some label
y^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
(e.g.,
y^{\mu}=\theta^{\mu}
for decoding stimulus angle). See
Sec. III.2
for an example on a monkey vision dataset.

We define instance-based regression capacity as
P/N_{\textsf{crit}}
, where
N_{\textsf{crit}}
is the
critical dimension
—the smallest dimension
N_{\mathrm{proj}}
such that, after random projection to an
N_{\mathrm{proj}}
-dimensional subspace, at least one admissible linear readout exists with probability
\geq 1/2
(see
Fig. 2
b). We derive a closed-form estimator for this instance-based capacity:

\alpha_{\textsf{ib}}(\varepsilon)=P\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{t}}[F_{\textsf{ib}}(\mathbf{t})]\right)^{-1},

(4)

where
F_{\textsf{ib}}(\mathbf{t})=\min_{\bm{w}^{+}\in\mathcal{A}_{\textsf{ib}}^{+}}\|\bm{t}-\bm{w}^{+}\|^{2}
is a field related to the projection of
\mathbf{t}
to a convex cone
\mathcal{A}_{\textsf{ib}}^{+}
generated by set of admissible readout weights
\mathcal{A}_{\textsf{ib}}=\bigcap_{\mu=1}^{P}\bigcap_{\bm{x}^{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}^{\mu}}\left\{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\left|\bm{w}^{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\mu}-y^{\mu}\right|\leq\varepsilon\right\}
.
That is,
\mathcal{A}_{\textsf{ib}}^{+}=\{\tau\bm{w}:\bm{w}\in\mathcal{A}_{\textsf{ib}},\tau\geq 0\}
.
We defer the details and other extensions (e.g., introducing a bias term in the linear regression) to SM Section SI
1
. An example of applying the instance-based capacity to real dataset is given in
Sec. III.2
. We remark that the capacity formula Eq. (
4
) can be empirically estimated in quadratic time (in
N,P
) via a standard QP solver.

III
Examples

We now apply our regression capacity framework to obtain closed-form capacity expressions for a handful of synthetic manifold models and interpret the results in
Sec. III.1
. Next, we use instanced-base regression capacity to study a neuroscience dataset in
Sec. III.2
.

III.1
Applications to synthetic manifolds

We start by using the mean-field formula Eq. (
3
) to compute capacity for synthetic data models and study how their geometric properties (e.g., correlations, radius, and dimensionality) influence capacity.

Point-like manifolds

First we consider
P
manifolds, each consisting of a single point. The
P
points are sampled i.i.d. from
\mathbf{x}^{\mu}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,r^{2}I_{N}/N)
with i.i.d. labels
y^{\mu}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
(see
Fig. 3
a). The parameters
r,\sigma
quantify the overall scale of the points and labels respectively. Starting with the mean-field formula Eq. (
3
), we calculate the regression capacity of these uncorrelated points:

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon)=2\min_{\tau\geq 0}\int_{b/a}^{\infty}\mathcal{D}z(az-b)^{2},

(5)

where
\mathcal{D}z
is the standard Gaussian measure,
a=\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}/r^{2}}
,
b=\varepsilon\tau/r
(derived in SM
1
Sec. III), and
\tau
is a scale factor naturally appears from the derivation.

We note that: (i)
\alpha(0)=1
, consistent with the fact that
P\leq N
linearly independent points can be mapped to arbitrary labels; (ii)
\alpha(\varepsilon)
increases monotonically with tolerance; (iii) varying
r
or simultaneously scaling
\varepsilon
and
\sigma
leaves capacity unchanged. Thus, the regression capacity of uncorrelated points depends only on the rescaled parameter
\varepsilon_{\textsf{equiv}}:=\varepsilon/\sigma
, which we call the
equivalent tolerance
(the tolerance of an equivalent model with
\sigma=1
).

Spherical manifolds

We next introduce structured variability in the form of low-rank spherical manifolds. We consider
D
-dimensional spherical shapes
\mathcal{S}=\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}:\|\mathbf{s}\|_{2}=1\}
, so that the
\mu
-th manifold is
\mathcal{M}^{\mu}=\left\{\mathbf{u}_{0}^{\mu}+\sum_{i=1}^{D}s_{i}^{\mu}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\mu}:s^{\mu}\in\mathcal{S}\right\}
.
The correlations among manifold centers
\mathbf{u}_{0}^{\mu}
and axes
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\mu}
(
i>0
) are modeled by a correlation tensor
\Sigma^{\nu,j}_{\mu,i}
. We sample
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\mu}
as
N
-dimensional Gaussians with
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\langle\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\mu},\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\nu}\rangle]=\Sigma_{\nu,j}^{\mu,i}
where

\Sigma_{\mu,i}^{\nu,j}=\begin{cases}r^{2}[(1-\psi)\delta_{\mu\nu}+\psi]&\textrm{if }i=j=0,\\
R^{2}[(1-\gamma)\delta_{\mu\nu}+\gamma]&\textrm{if }i=j>0,\\
0&\textrm{else,}\end{cases}

and sample correlated Gaussian labels
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda)
with covariance matrix
\Lambda_{\mu}^{\nu}=\sigma^{2}[(1-\rho)\delta_{\mu\nu}+\rho]
. The parameters
r,R,\sigma
scale the norm of the manifold centers, the size of the spherical variability around each manifold center, and the labels respectively. The parameters
\psi,\rho,\gamma\in[0,1)
control the respective strengths of the center, label, and axis correlations (
Fig. 4
a).

We analytically solve the regression capacity of this model of spherical manifolds:

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon)=2\min_{\tau\geq 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\;\chi_{D}(x)g(x,\tau,\varepsilon),

(6)

where
g
can be written in closed-form (see SM
1
) and
\chi_{D}(\cdot)
is the probability density function of the chi distribution with
D
degrees of freedom (see Sec. IV of SM
1
for a derivation). The inner function
g(x,\tau,\varepsilon)
is complex, but it is invariant under several parameter changes. For example,
(\varepsilon,\sigma,\tau)\leftrightarrow(a\varepsilon,a\sigma,\tau/a)
: Invariance to multiplying tolerance, label scale, and readout scale (equivalent to
dividing

\tau
) by some constant. See SM
1
for a complete list of symmetries in this model. From the symmetries of
g(x,\tau,\varepsilon)
, it follows that the capacity depends only on the
asymptotically equivalent tolerance

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{equiv}}:=\varepsilon/(\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho})
, the
asymptotically equivalent radius

R_{\mathrm{equiv}}:=R\sqrt{1-\gamma}/(r\sqrt{1-\psi})
, and the sphere dimension
D
. We numerically verify this in
Fig. 4
.

III.2
Application to neuroscience data

Finally, we demonstrate that our regression capacity theory can be applied to quantify the linear decodability of continuous latent variables from real data with complex variability. We apply our method to electrophysiological recordings from the macaque ventral stream
29
, and study representations of object pose parameters (e.g., the relative size, horizontal position, and vertical position) of a foreground object on a variable background (
Fig. 5
a). Conceptually, a successful decoder for one latent variable must filter out the neural variability induced by intrinsic noise and the encoding of all the other stimulus features. Previous work quantified decodability of object pose parameters from the same dataset by the conventional method of training a linear decoder on the full-dimensional data and evaluating its cross-validated generalization error
24
. Our results in
Fig. 5
b qualitatively match the standard decoding analysis in finding monotonic improvement in decodability from raw image pixels to area V4 to area IT for all three pose parameters. Furthermore, unlike generalization error-based metrics (e.g., support vector regression
19
;
7
) used in previous work
24
, the numerical value of our regression capacity measure can be directly interpreted as the number of neurons a downstream reader needs to decode the target variable to a desired accuracy
\varepsilon
.

One aspect of manifold construction worth noting is that we estimate capacity separately for each top-level object class in the dataset (e.g., “animal” and “fruit”), since objects belonging to these very different categories are unlikely to share a meaningful notion of absolute rotation angle. The values in
Fig. 5
b are averaged across top-level categories.

IV
Discussion

In this Letter, we introduced a theoretical framework for quantifying linear decodability of continuous variables encoded in neural population manifolds, extending manifold capacity theory from classification tasks to regression problems. Previous studies of estimation and fine discrimination from neural population activity focused on statistical properties of noise
39
;
17
;
3
, often assuming Gaussian or Poisson distributions. However, neural activity often exhibits more complex structured variability from coding nuisance variables (e.g., background features), which significantly impacts readout performance
39
;
31
;
18
;
11
. Characterizing such complex structures has been challenging due to limited methods capable of capturing their geometry
11
.

By generalizing the previous manifold capacity theory from discrete to continuous labels, we systematically relate structured variability to decoding efficiency for continuous variables. We explicitly link geometric properties of neural manifolds—such as dimensionality, radius, and correlation structures—to their regression capacities, showing that uniform correlations among manifolds and their labels effectively just rescale the data (
Sec. III.1
).

We also show how to non-parametrically define and estimate regression capacity, so that it can be directly quantified in real neuroscience datasets. We apply our framework to recordings from primate visual cortex to validate its utility beyond synthetic scenarios, finding increases in linear decodability of object pose parameters along the ventral stream consistent with past analysis
24
. These findings support the view that neural networks, whether biological or artificial, hierarchically refine representations to optimize downstream decoding.

Our framework opens several directions for technical and applied extensions.
On the applied side, our methods offer a principled approach for analyzing neural data across sensory modalities, extending prior work in the classification setting
8
to a wide range of continuous-variable tasks, including navigation and estimation problems in perception and motor control. In neuroscience, this enables quantitative comparisons of representational efficiency across brain areas or behavioral conditions, even in the presence of structured noise and nuisance variability. In machine learning, it provides a geometric lens to characterize how deep networks organize task-relevant continuous variables—across layers or throughout learning.

Instance-Based Regression Capacity Theory

In this section, we provide details for the instance-based regression capacity as introduced in
Sec. II.2
in the main text. For a given high-dimensional neural data with
N

P
manifolds (where both
N,P

SI.1
Definitions of simulation capacity and useful mathematical concepts

Suppose we have
P
fixed manifolds
\{\mathcal{M}^{\mu}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
that are embedded in a shared
N
-dimensional state space and that have some target labels
\mathbf{y}=\{y^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
for regression. As we focus on linear regression on top of these manifolds, without loss of generality we can treat these manifolds as convex sets. These manifolds may be correlated in any arbitrary way with each other and with the labels and may all be uniquely shaped. For an error tolerance
\varepsilon\geq 0
for which an admissible readout
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
exists. By
admissible
, we mean that

|\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\mu}-y^{\mu}|\leq\varepsilon\textrm{ for all }\mu\in\{1,\dots,P\}\textrm{ and }\mathbf{x}^{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}^{\mu}.

For any dimension
N_{\mathrm{proj}}
with
0\leq N_{\mathrm{proj}}\leq N
, we can randomly project the data manifolds down to
\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{proj}}}
and check whether it remains regressible (i.e., an admissible weight exists). Let
p(N_{\mathrm{proj}})
be the probability of the data still being regressible when projected to
\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{proj}}}
. Since
p(0)=0
and
p(N)=1

SI.2
Formulas for critical dimension and instance-based capacity

Now, we are ready to present our mathematical formulas for critical dimension and simulation capacity (

Definition 2

(Capacity formula)
.

Let
P

\{\mathcal{M}^{\mu}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}

\alpha(\varepsilon)

\alpha_{\textsf{ib}}(\varepsilon):=\frac{P}{N_{\textsf{ib}}(\varepsilon)}\,.

and
\alpha_{\textsf{ib}}(\varepsilon)
converge to
N_{\textsf{crit}}(\varepsilon)
and
\alpha_{\mathrm{simulation}}(\varepsilon)

(Formulas for critical dimension and instance-based capacity)

SI.3
Details for the convergence of capacity formula

In this subsection, we prove the concentration of critical dimension (and hence capacity) by a large deviation theory in convex geometry. The definition of statistical dimension and the key theorem are paraphrased below (we specialize Theorem I of
2

Definition 3
.

We call a subset

\lambda\mathbf{x}\in C

\delta(C)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{t}}\|\Pi_{C}(\mathbf{t})\|^{2},

where
\mathbf{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{N})

\Pi_{C}(\mathbf{t})=\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}\in C}\|\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{x}\|
is the projection of
\mathbf{t}
onto the convex cone
C
.

As a consequence of some basic facts about the statistical dimension given in Proposition 3.1 of
2

\delta(C)=N-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{t}}\min_{\mathbf{x}\in C}\|\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
, which we will take as the definition of the statistical dimension in our proof of Theorem 1. We will also use the fact from the same Proposition 3.1 that statistical dimension agrees with linear-algebraic dimension on subspaces.

Next, we restate the approximate conic kinematic formula—which establishes a large deviation theory for the intersection of two randomly-oriented convex cones—from

Lemma 1

(Approximate conic kinematic formula,
(

)

Let

\mathbb{R}^{N}

, The following then hold:

(i)

(ii)

.

SII
Mean-Field Regression Capacity Theory for General Manifolds with Second-Order Correlations

In this section, we provide details for the mean-field regression capacity using a generative model of data manifolds with second-order correlations and random labels as introduced in
Sec. II.1
in the main text. Our mean-field model is an extension from Gardner’s formulation of perceptron capacity for point-like neural representations
21
and the recent manifold capacity theory for classification task
12
;
43
;
8
. The main purpose of mean-field theory in this paper is to provide a framework to derive closed-form formulas for the capacity of synthetic manifold models, such as point-like (see
Sec. SIII
) and sphere-like (see
Sec. SIV
) manifolds.

SII.1
Definitions for a mean-field manifold model

Fix some
N,P\in\mathbb{N}
, which will be sent to infinity with constant ratio (i.e.,
P/N=O(1)
).
We consider
D
-dimensional manifolds of a given (WLOG, convex) shape
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D+1}
, where we denote the components of
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{S}
by
(s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{D})
and assume that
s_{0}=1
for all
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{S}
. To sample
P
manifolds embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we will sample an embedding matrix
Q^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(D+1)}
for each manifold index
\mu\in\{1,\dots,P\}
and then define the
\mu
-th manifold to be
\mathcal{M}^{\mu}=Q^{\mu}\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Denote the columns of
Q^{\mu}
by

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{0}&\mathbf{q}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{q}_{D}\end{pmatrix}\,.

Our assumption about the form of
\mathcal{S}
leads to a natural center-axis decomposition where we think of
r\mathbf{q}_{0}
as the embedded manifold’s centroid and

\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{D}s_{i}\mathbf{q}_{i}:\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{S}\right\}

as the variation around that centroid, with
\{\mathbf{q}_{i}\,:\,i>0\}
being the
D
axes on which that variation lives. To model correlations between the manifolds, we define the matrices
Q^{\mu}
to be zero-mean Gaussian matrices with some covariance structure

\mathbb{E}Q^{\mu}_{ki}Q^{\nu}_{lj}=\frac{\delta_{kl}}{N}\Sigma_{\mu i}^{\nu j}\,,

determined by some positive-definite covariance tensor
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{P(D+1)\times P(D+1)}
. This leads to

\mathbf{q}^{\mu}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{q}^{\nu}_{j}\overset{\mathcal{P}}{\to}\Sigma_{\mu i}^{\nu j}\textrm{ as }N\to\infty\,,

where
\overset{\mathcal{P}}{\to}
denotes convergence in probability. The entries
\Sigma_{\mu 0}^{\nu 0}
of this tensor determine the
“center-center”
correlations, the entries
\Sigma_{\mu i}^{\nu j}
with
i,j>0
determine the
“axis-axis”
correlations, and finally the entries
\Sigma_{\mu 0}^{\nu i}
with
i>0
determine the
“center-axis”
correlations. All of this follows
43
, which studied the capacity of essentially the same data model for classification.

Finally, to define the regression capacity for our mean-field manifold model, we need to analyze the probability of
(\{\mathcal{M}^{\mu}\},\mathbf{y})
being linearly regressible. Concretely, for every
\varepsilon>0
, consider

p_{N,P}(\varepsilon,\Sigma,\mathbf{y})=\mathbb{P}_{Q}(\exists\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\forall\mu,\forall\mathbf{x}^{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}^{\mu},\ |\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\mu}-y^{\mu}|\leq\varepsilon)\,,

where the manifolds
\mathcal{M}^{\mu}
are randomly sampled by the above procedure. We will show that
p_{N,P}
displays a sharp phase transition indexed by the load
\alpha=P/N
in the proportional limit (
P,N\to\infty
with
\alpha
constant).

To start, we will study the capacity of this model given fixed labels
\{y^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
, and afterwards we will generalize to random labels.

as random projections of the deterministic manifolds
\{\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu}\}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{M}^{\mu}=G^{\mu}\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu}
where
G^{\mu}
is the
\mu
-th column block of
G
).

Step 2: Invoke
Theorem 1
for random projection model.

Now that we have established an equivalent random projection model for the mean-field manifold model, we are able to invoke
Theorem 1
and show the concentration of linear regressibility of the latter. Concretely, for every
\eta>0
, we have

(i)

If
\frac{N}{P(D+1)}\leq\frac{N_{\textsf{mf}}(\varepsilon)}{P(D+1)}-\sqrt{\frac{8\log(4/\eta)}{P(D+1)}}
, then
\mathbb{P}_{G}(\mathcal{A}(G)\neq\varnothing)\leq\eta
;

(ii)

If
\frac{N}{P(D+1)}\geq\frac{N_{\textsf{mf}}(\varepsilon)}{P(D+1)}+\sqrt{\frac{8\log(4/\eta)}{P(D+1)}}
, then
\mathbb{P}_{G}(\mathcal{A}(G)\neq\varnothing)\geq 1-\eta
,

where

N_{\textsf{mf}}(\varepsilon)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{t}}\left[\min_{\mathbf{w}^{+}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\|\bm{t}-\mathbf{w}^{+}\|^{2}\right]

and

\mathcal{A}(M)=\left\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{P(D+1)}\,:\,|\mathbf{w}\cdot(M\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\mu})-y^{\mu}|\leq\varepsilon,\ \forall\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\mu}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu},\ \forall\mu\right\}\,.

Finally, notice that
\mathcal{A}_{\textsf{mf}}=\mathcal{A}(I_{P(D+1)})
,
\alpha_{\textsf{mf}}^{-1}(\varepsilon)=N_{\textsf{mf}}(\varepsilon)/P
, and
p_{N,P}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{y})=\mathbb{P}_{G}(\mathcal{A}(G))
, this completes the proof for
Theorem 2
.
∎

SII.3
Formula for random-label mean-field manifolds

In
Sec. SII.2
, we derived a formula (
Eq. S1
) for the manifold capacity of our mean-field model with
fixed
labels. However, the toy models we study in later sections also include randomness in the labels. To incorporate this extra disorder, we consider the probability

p_{N,P}(\varepsilon,\Sigma)=\mathbb{P}_{Q,\mathbf{y}}\left(\exists\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\forall\mu,\forall\mathbf{x}^{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}^{\mu},|\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\mu}-y^{\mu}|\leq\varepsilon\right)\,,

of an admissible readout
\bm{w}
existing for our model, where this probability is now also evaluated over some distribution of labels. We can write
p_{N,P}(\varepsilon,\Sigma)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}p_{N,P}(\varepsilon,\Sigma,\mathbf{y})
in terms of the probability of an admissible readout existing for our fixed-label model. To show that
p_{N,P}(\varepsilon,\Sigma)
exhibits a sharp phase transition at some capacity
\alpha=P/N
in the proportional limit
P,N\to\infty
, it is sufficient to show that the label-conditioned model
p_{N,P}(\varepsilon,\Sigma,\mathbf{y})
exhibits a phase transition at the same
\alpha
for almost all labels
\mathbf{y}
.

To prove this, it in turn suffices to show that the label-conditioned capacity estimator

\alpha_{\textsf{mf}}(\varepsilon,P,\mathbf{y})^{-1}=\frac{1}{P}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{t}}\left[\min_{\mathbf{w}^{+}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\|\bm{t}-\mathbf{w}^{+}\|^{2}\right]

from
Theorem 2
concentrates around its mean
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\alpha_{\textsf{mf}}(\varepsilon,P,\mathbf{y})^{-1}
as
P\to\infty
. If this concentration holds, the capacity of the mean-field model
with random labels
is then well-defined (i.e., a sharp phase transition exists) and is given by

\alpha^{-1}_{\textsf{mf}}(\varepsilon)=\lim_{P\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}[\alpha_{\textsf{mf}}(\varepsilon,P,\bm{y})^{-1}]\,.

(S2)

We will show that this concentration holds for the toy models studied in the following sections.

SIII
Regression Capacity of Random Point-Like Manifolds

Our mean-field theory for regression capacity (
Sec. SII
) enables analytical study of synthetic manifolds. In this section, we focus on deriving closed-form formulas for the regression capacity of random point-like manifolds with different correlation structures. Later in
Sec. SIV.2
, we will study the regression capacity of a data model with spherical manifolds which are correlated in multiple ways.

SIII.1
Capacity of Uncorrelated Points

We start with studying the capacity of uncorrelated random
points
with uncorrelated target labels.

SIII.1.1
Our toy model for uncorrelated points and its regression capacity

To sample
P
points
\{\bm{x}^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with expected square-norm
\mathbb{E}\|\bm{x}^{\mu}\|^{2}=r^{2}
set by some
r>0
, we define

x^{\mu}_{k}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{r^{2}}{N}\right)\textrm{ for all }\mu\in\{1,\dots,P\}\textrm{ and }k\in\{1,\dots,N\}\,.

This scaling ensures that
\mathbb{E}\bm{x}^{\mu}\cdot\bm{x}^{\nu}=r^{2}\delta_{\mu\nu}
. We sample the target labels
\{y_{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
for each point i.i.d. from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
with some label variance
\sigma^{2}>0
. In the notation of
Sec. SII.1
, we have manifold shape
\mathcal{S}=\{1\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D+1}
with dimension
D=0
, a correlation tensor
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{P(D+1)\times P(D+1)}
with entries
\Sigma_{\mu 0}^{\nu 0}=r^{2}\delta_{\mu\nu}
, and label correlation
H\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times P}
with entries
H_{\mu\nu}=\sigma^{2}\delta_{\mu\nu}
.

Here, we use the formula for random-label mean-field manifolds (
Eq. S2
) to derive a simple capacity formula for the toy model described above:

Theorem 3

(Formula for uncorrelated points)
.

The uncorrelated points model described above has capacity given by

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,r,\sigma)=\min_{\tau\geq 0}2\int_{b/a}^{\infty}\mathcal{D}z(az-b)^{2}\,,

(S3)

where
\mathcal{D}z
denotes the standard Gaussian measure and

a=\sqrt{1+\frac{\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}{r^{2}}}
,
b=\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{r}
.

We interpret this formula and numerically validate it in the main text. The derivation details for
Theorem 3
is provided in the following subsubsection for the completeness of presentation.

SIII.1.2
Derivation details for
Theorem 3

As described in
Sec. SII.3
, we will first study the label-conditioned capacity estimator

\alpha_{\textsf{mf}}(\varepsilon,P,\bm{y})^{-1}=\frac{1}{P}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{t}}\left[\min_{\mathbf{w}^{+}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\|\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{w}^{+}\|^{2}\right]\,,

which we can rewrite as
\alpha_{\textsf{mf}}(\varepsilon,P,\bm{y})^{-1}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{t}}\min_{\tau\geq 0}f(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{y},\tau)
in terms of the field
f(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{y},\tau)=\frac{1}{P}\min_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{y})}\|\mathbf{t}-\tau\mathbf{w}\|^{2}
and constraint set
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{y})=\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}:|rw^{\mu}-y^{\mu}|\leq\varepsilon\}
.
For the uncorrelated case, this field factorizes into
P
terms:

f(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{y},\tau)=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}w^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}\\
|rw^{\mu}-y^{\mu}|\leq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}(t^{\mu}-\tau w^{\mu})^{2}\,.

We can make a change of variables to
h^{\mu}=rw^{\mu}-y^{\mu}
and write

f(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{y},\tau)=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}h^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}\\
|h^{\mu}|\leq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\left(\left(t^{\mu}-\frac{\tau}{r}y^{\mu}\right)-\frac{\tau}{r}h^{\mu}\right)^{2}\,.

Observe that
f(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{y},\tau)
is identically distributed to

g(\mathbf{z},\tau)=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}h^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}\\
|h^{\mu}|\leq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\left(az^{\mu}-\frac{\tau}{r}h^{\mu}\right)^{2}

with
\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{P})
and
a=\sqrt{1+\frac{\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}{r^{2}}}
. Each term of the above sum is now i.i.d., so by the law of large numbers
g(\mathbf{z},\tau)
concentrates to the deterministic limit

\tilde{g}(\tau)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\min_{|h|\leq\varepsilon}\left(az-\frac{\tau}{r}h\right)^{2}=2\int_{b/a}^{\infty}\mathcal{D}z(az-b)^{2}\,,

as
P\to\infty
, where
b=\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{r}
. Thus,
f(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{y},\tau)
also concentrates around
\tilde{g}(\tau)
as
P\to\infty
. It follows that the label-conditioned capacity
\alpha_{\textsf{mf}}(\varepsilon,P,\bm{y})^{-1}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{t}}\min_{\tau\geq 0}f(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{y},\tau)
concentrates around
\min_{\tau\geq 0}\tilde{g}(\tau)
as
P\to\infty
, which is therefore the capacity of this uncorrelated points model as argued in
Sec. SII.3
, which is what we wanted to show in
Theorem 3
.

SIII.2
Capacity of Correlated Points

Next, we introduce uniform correlations between the points
\{\bm{x}^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
, controlled by a parameter
\psi\in[0,1)
, and the labels
\{y^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
, controlled by a parameter
\rho\in[0,1)
. We show in this subsection that the capacity of this model reduces to the capacity of the uncorrelated points model with rescaled parameters.

SIII.2.1
Our toy model for correlated points and its regression capacity

In the notation of
Sec. SII
, we define the data correlation tensor by
\Sigma_{\mu 0}^{\nu 0}=r^{2}[(1-\psi)\delta_{\mu\nu}+\psi]
and the label correlation matrix by
H_{\mu\nu}=\sigma^{2}[(1-\rho)\delta_{\mu\nu}+\rho]
.

Here, we derive a simple capacity formula for the toy model described above by reducing the capacity formula for a correlated point model to an equivalent uncorrelated point model.

Theorem 4

(Formula for correlated points)
.

Given the model for correlated points described above. Let
\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,r,\sigma,\rho,\psi,P)
be the capacity formula from
Theorem 2
for this model, we have that
\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,r,\sigma,\rho,\psi,P)\xrightarrow{P\to\infty}\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,r,\sigma,\rho,\psi)
with

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,r,\sigma,\rho,\psi)=\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,r\sqrt{1-\psi},\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho})=2\int_{b/a}^{\infty}\mathcal{D}z(az-b)^{2}

(S4)

where
\mathcal{D}z
denotes the standard Gaussian measure and

a=\sqrt{1+\frac{(1-\rho)\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}{(1-\psi)r^{2}}}
,
b=\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{(1-\psi)r}
.

We interpret this formula and numerically validate it in the main text. The derivation details for
Theorem 4
is provided in the following subsubsection for the completeness of presentation.

SIII.2.2
Derivation details for
Theorem 4

For a given label tolerance
\varepsilon>0
,
Theorem 2
gives us a capacity estimator

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon)=\min_{\tau\geq 0}\frac{1}{P}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{t},\bm{y}}\min_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{B}(\bm{y})}\left\|\bm{t}-\tau\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{u}\right\|^{2}\,,

where

\mathcal{B}(\bm{y})=\left\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}:|u_{\mu}-y_{\mu}|\leq\varepsilon\textrm{ for all }\mu\in\{1,\dots,P\}\right\}\,.

As in the previous section, our goal is to compute

\lim_{P\to\infty}\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,P).

Similarly to the previous section, we can define a shifted variable
\bm{v}=\bm{u}-\bm{y}
so that our estimator becomes

\displaystyle\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{P}\min_{\tau\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{t},\bm{y}}\min_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{B}(0)}\left\|\bm{t}-\tau\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{v}+\bm{y})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{P}\min_{\tau\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{t},\bm{y}}\min_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{B}(0)}\left\|\left(\bm{t}-\tau\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{y}\right)-\tau\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{v}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{P}\min_{\tau\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\min_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{B}(0)}\left\|\Omega^{1/2}\bm{z}-\tau\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{v}\right\|^{2},

where
\Omega=I+\tau^{2}\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}H\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, so that we have combined
\bm{t}
and
\bm{y}
into a single random vector.

The key to analyzing this model is to decompose the correlation matrices into their eigencomponents, with one eigenspace spanned by the vector
\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{P}
, where
\bm{1}=(1,1,\dots,1)
, and another
(P-1)
-dimensional eigenspace orthogonal to this vector. In particular, we have:

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=r^{2}\left[(1-\psi)I+\psi\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=r^{2}(1-\psi)\Pi+r^{2}(1-\psi+P\psi)\left[\frac{1}{P}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\right],

and

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\left[(1-\rho)I+\rho\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}(1-\rho)\Pi+\sigma^{2}(1-\rho+P\rho)\left[\frac{1}{P}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\right],

where
\Pi=I-\frac{1}{P}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r\sqrt{1-\psi}}\Pi+\frac{1}{r\sqrt{1-\psi+P\psi}}\left[\frac{1}{P}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\right],

and

\displaystyle\Omega^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho)}{r^{2}(1-\psi)}}\Pi+\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho+P\rho)}{r^{2}(1-\psi+P\psi)}}\left[\frac{1}{P}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\right].

We can write our capacity estimator as:

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,P)=\min_{\tau\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}f_{\tau}(\bm{z}),

where we split the field
f_{\tau}(\bm{z})
into two terms corresponding to the two eigenspaces of the correlation matrices:

\displaystyle f_{\tau}(\bm{z})=\min_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{B}(0)}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{P}\left\|\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho)}{r^{2}(1-\psi)}}\Pi\bm{z}-\frac{\tau}{r\sqrt{1-\psi}}\Pi\bm{v}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{P}

\displaystyle\left\|\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho+P\rho)}{r^{2}(1-\psi+P\psi)}}\left[\frac{1}{P}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\right]\bm{z}-\frac{\tau}{r\sqrt{1-\psi+P\psi}}\left[\frac{1}{P}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\right]\bm{v}\right\|^{2}.

Notice that we can rewrite the second term above as

\left(\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho+P\rho)}{r^{2}(1-\psi+P\psi)}}\frac{1}{P}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{z}-\frac{\tau}{Pr\sqrt{1-\psi+P\psi}}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{v}^{*}\right)^{2},

(S5)

where
\bm{v}^{*}
is a minimizing
\bm{v}
. Since
\bm{z}
is just a standard Gaussian vector, the law of large numbers gives

\frac{1}{P}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{z}\overset{P}{\to}0\,,

where
\overset{P}{\to}
denotes convergence in probability. Since

\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho+P\rho)}{r^{2}(1-\psi+P\psi)}}=O(1)\,,

we have

\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho+P\rho)}{r^{2}(1-\psi+P\psi)}}\frac{1}{P}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{z}\overset{P}{\to}0\,.

As for the second term, we must always have
|\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{v}^{*}/P|\leq\varepsilon
because
\bm{v}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(0)
. Thus,

\left|\frac{\tau}{Pr\sqrt{1-\psi+P\psi}}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{v}^{*}\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{r\sqrt{1-\psi+P\psi}}\to 0\,,

so the second term in Eq. (
S5
) and therefore all of Eq. (
S5
) converges in probability to zero,
regardless of what the optimal
\bm{v}^{*}
actually is
. For the purpose of computing the asymptotic capacity of our model, we can therefore replace
f_{\tau}(\bm{z})
with the simpler field

\tilde{f}_{\tau}(\bm{z})=\min_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{B}(0)}\frac{1}{P}\left\|\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho)}{r^{2}(1-\psi)}}\Pi\bm{z}-\frac{\tau}{r\sqrt{1-\psi}}\Pi\bm{v}\right\|^{2}\,.

Except for the interspersed projection matrices
\Pi
, this looks a lot like the capacity estimator for the
uncorrelated
points model studied in the previous section with rescaled parameters
r_{\mathrm{equiv}}=r\sqrt{1-\psi}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{equiv}}=\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho}
. Indeed, we will show that the asymptotic capacity of our correlated points model is equal to the capacity of this uncorrelated model. The key step is to show that
\bar{v}=\frac{1}{P}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{v}^{*}
converges to zero as
P\to\infty
. This is true for the uncorrelated case (
\psi,\rho=0
) by the law of large numbers since all the components
\{v_{\mu}^{*}\}
are i.i.d., but it takes a little work to show that this also holds for the correlated case. Since we will reuse the same argument later in
Sec. SIV.2
, we abstract it into a proposition that we prove at the end of this section:

Proposition 1
.

Let
\bm{v}^{*}(\bm{z})
be a minimizer of
\|\Pi(a\bm{z}-\bm{v})\|
subject to
\bm{v}\in B
, where
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
is a standard Gaussian vector and
B=\prod_{\mu=1}^{P}[-\varepsilon^{\mu},\varepsilon^{\mu}]
for some sequence
\{\varepsilon^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
satisfying
0\leq\varepsilon^{\mu}\leq\varepsilon
for all
\mu
and some fixed
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Then

\mathbb{P}_{z}(|\bar{v}^{*}(z)|>\eta)\leq\exp(-C\eta^2(1 - \erf(\varepsilon/|a|))^2 P/a^2)

where
\bar{x}:=\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{x}/P
and
C>0
is a universal constant. In particular, for any fixed
\varepsilon
and
a
,
\bar{v}
converges in probability to
0
as
P\to\infty
.

This proposition can be applied to the minimizer
\bm{v}=\bm{v}^{*}(\bm{z})
of the field
\tilde{f}_{\tau}(\bm{z})
to show that
\bar{v}\to 0
as
P\to\infty
.
The capacity for the
uncorrelated
points model with
r_{\mathrm{equiv}}=r\sqrt{1-\psi}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{equiv}}=\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho}
can be written as

\alpha^{-1}_{\mathrm{uncorr}}(\varepsilon,P)=\min_{\tau\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}g_{\tau}(\bm{z})\,,

where

g_{\tau}(\bm{z})=\min_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{B}(0)}\frac{1}{P}\|a\bm{z}-b\bm{v}\|^{2}\,.

We can split this into eigencomponents like before:

g_{\tau}(\bm{z})=\min_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{B}(0)}\frac{1}{P}\|\Pi(a\bm{z}-b\bm{v})\|^{2}+(a\bar{z}-b\bar{v})^{2}\,.

Since
\bar{z}\overset{P}{\to}0
as
P\to\infty
, we can equivalently consider the field

\min_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{B}(0)}\frac{1}{P}\|a\Pi\bm{z}-b\Pi\bm{v}\|^{2}+(b\bar{v})^{2}\,.

The second term is minimized when
\bar{v}=0
. As remarked above, we know that minimizing the first term alone (as in the field
\tilde{f}_{\tau}(\bm{z})
) leads to
\bar{v}\overset{P}{\to}0
as
P\to\infty
anyway. Thus,
g_{\tau}(\bm{z})
and
\tilde{f}_{\tau}(\bm{z})
are asymptotically equivalent, so the capacity of our correlated model is equal to the capacity of the uncorrelated points model with rescaled centroid norm
r_{\mathrm{equiv}}=r\sqrt{1-\psi}
and label scale
\sigma_{\mathrm{equiv}}=\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho}
.

We use the following lemma to prove
Proposition 1
:

Lemma 3

(Concentration for Lipschitz functions)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{P}\to\mathbb{R}
. Suppose
f(\cdot)
is
L
-Lipschitz. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{P})}(|f(\mathbf{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{t}^{\prime}}f(\mathbf{t}^{\prime})|>\Delta)\leq\exp\left(-C\cdot\frac{\Delta^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)

for every
\Delta>0
.

The proof for the above lemma can be found in standard textbooks such as Theorem V.1 in
33
and is often attributed to Maurey and Pisier.

SIII.2.3
Proof of
Proposition 1

We can study
\bm{v}^{*}(\bm{z})
using the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(\bm{v},\bm{\lambda}_{+},\bm{\lambda}_{-})=\frac{1}{2}\|\Pi(a\bm{z}-\bm{v})\|^{2}+\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\lambda^{\mu}_{+}(x^{\mu}-\varepsilon^{\mu})+\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\lambda^{\mu}_{-}(-x^{\mu}-\varepsilon^{\mu}).

The KKT conditions characterizing
\bm{v}=\bm{v}^{*}(\bm{z})
are:

•

Stationarity:
\Pi(\bm{v}-a\bm{z})+\bm{\lambda}=0
;

•

Feasibility:
\bm{v}\in B
;

•

Complementary slackness:
\lambda^{\mu}\neq 0\Rightarrow v^{\mu}=\operatorname{sgn}(\lambda^{\mu})\varepsilon^{\mu}
,

where we define
\bm{\lambda}=\bm{\lambda}_{+}-\bm{\lambda}_{-}
. Recalling that
\Pi\bm{x}=\bm{x}-\bar{x}\bf{1}
, these conditions imply

v^{\mu}=\max(-\varepsilon^{\mu},\min(\varepsilon^{\mu},\bar{v}+a[\Pi\bm{z}]^{\mu}))\textrm{ for all }\mu\,.

This leads to a self-consistent equation for
\bar{v}
:

\bar{v}=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\max(-\varepsilon^{\mu},\min(\varepsilon^{\mu},\bar{v}+a[\Pi\mathbf{z}]^{\mu}))=\frac{\bm{1}^{\top}}{P}\operatorname{proj}_{B}(\bar{v}\bm{1}+a\Pi\bm{z})\,.

Define

f(\bar{v},\bm{z})=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\max(-\varepsilon^{\mu},\min(\varepsilon^{\mu},\bar{v}+a[\Pi\mathbf{z}]^{\mu}))=\frac{\bm{1}^{\top}}{P}\operatorname{proj}_{B}(\bar{v}\bm{1}+a\Pi\bm{z})

to be the right-hand size of the self-consistent equation, so that
\bar{v}=f(\bar{v},\mathbf{z})
for the optimal
\mathbf{v}
. Since
\Pi
is 1-Lipschitz, the map
\mathbf{z}\mapsto\bar{v}\bm{1}+a\Pi\mathbf{z}
is
a
-Lipschitz. Projection onto any closed, convex set (here
B
) is 1-Lipschitz, so the composition of these maps
\mathbf{z}\mapsto\mathrm{proj}_{B}(\bar{v}+a\Pi\mathbf{z})
is still
a
-Lipschitz. Since
\|\bm{1}/P\|=1/\sqrt{P}
, it follows that
f(\bar{v},\mathbf{z})
is
(a/\sqrt{P})
-Lipschitz in
\mathbf{z}
. We can now invoke our concentration inequality
Lemma 3
for Lipschitz functions to obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(|f(\bar{v},\bm{z})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}f(\bar{v},\bm{z})|>\Delta\right)\leq\exp\left(-C\Delta^{2}P/a^{2}\right)

for some universal constant
C>0
. This essentially gives us a
deterministic
self-consistent equation
\bar{v}\approx\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}f(\bar{v},\bm{z})
that
\bar{v}
must satisfy for the optimal
\mathbf{v}
, and we will show below that this implies
\bar{v}
concentrates at 0 as
P\to\infty
. Specifically, we can write

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}f(\bar{v},\bm{z})=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\max(-\varepsilon^{\mu},\min(\varepsilon^{\mu},\bar{v}+a[\Pi\bm{z}]^{\mu}))\,.

Noting that
a[\Pi z]^{\mu}
is Gaussian with standard deviation
\tilde{a}=a\sqrt{1-1/P}
, we can write

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\max(-\varepsilon^{\mu},\min(\varepsilon^{\mu},\bar{v}+a[\Pi z]^{\mu}))=\mathbb{E}_{g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\max(-\varepsilon^{\mu},\min(\varepsilon^{\mu},\bar{v}+\tilde{a}g))\,,

which can be explicitly computed. In particular, one can show that

\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon^{\mu}}\mathbb{E}_{g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\max(-\varepsilon^{\mu},\min(\varepsilon^{\mu},\bar{v}+\tilde{a}g))=\frac{1}{2}\erf\left(\frac{\bar{v}+\varepsilon^{\mu}}{\tilde{a}\sqrt{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\erf\left(\frac{\bar{v}-\varepsilon^{\mu}}{\tilde{a}\sqrt{2}}\right)\,.

First, consider the case where
\bar{v}\geq 0
. The above derivative w.r.t.
\varepsilon^{\mu}
is positive, and since
\varepsilon^{\mu}\leq\varepsilon
, we have

0\leq\mathbb{E}\max(-\varepsilon^{\mu},\min(\varepsilon^{\mu},\bar{v}+a[\Pi\bm{z}]^{\mu}))\leq\mathbb{E}\max(-\varepsilon,\min(\varepsilon,\bar{v}+a[\Pi\bm{z}]^{\mu}))\,.

for all
\mu
. Similarly, when
\bar{v}\leq 0
, the above derivative w.r.t.
\varepsilon^{\mu}
is negative, and we get

\mathbb{E}\max(-\varepsilon,\min(\varepsilon,\bar{v}+a[\Pi\bm{z}]^{\mu}))\leq\mathbb{E}\max(-\varepsilon^{\mu},\min(\varepsilon^{\mu},\bar{v}+a[\Pi\bm{z}]^{\mu}))\leq 0\,.

It follows that, for any
\bar{v}
,

|\mathbb{E}f(\bar{v},\bm{z})|\leq|\mathbb{E}\tilde{f}(\bar{v},\bm{z})|
, where we define

\tilde{f}(\bar{v},\bm{z})=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\max(-\varepsilon,\min(\varepsilon,\bar{v}+a[\Pi\bm{z}]^{\mu}))\,.

Since the terms of this sum are now identically distributed, we get

\mathbb{E}\tilde{f}(\bar{v},\bm{z})=\mathbb{E}_{g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\max(-\varepsilon,\min(\varepsilon,\bar{v}+\tilde{a}g))\,.

One can compute

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{v}}\mathbb{E}\tilde{f}(\bar{v},\bm{z})=-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{\bar{v}-\varepsilon}{|\tilde{a}|\sqrt{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{\bar{v}+\varepsilon}{|\tilde{a}|\sqrt{2}}\right)\,,

(where the derivative is now w.r.t.
\bar{v}
), which satisfies

0\leq\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{v}}\mathbb{E}\tilde{f}(\bar{v},\bm{z})\leq\erf\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{|\tilde{a}|\sqrt{2}}\right)<1\textrm{ for all }\bar{v}\,.

Noting also that
\mathbb{E}\tilde{f}(0,\mathbf{z})=0
by symmetry, it follows that
|\mathbb{E}f(\bar{v},\bm{z})|\leq|\mathbb{E}\tilde{f}(\bar{v},\bm{z})|\leq|\bar{v}|\cdot\erf(\varepsilon/(|\tilde a| \sqrt{2}))
. WLOG, we may assume
P\geq 2
so that
|\tilde{a}|\geq|a|/\sqrt{2}
, and we get
|\mathbb{E}f(\bar{v},\bm{z})|\leq|\bar{v}|\cdot\operatorname{erf}(\varepsilon/|a|)<1
. Define
\Delta=\eta(1-\erf(\varepsilon/|a|))>0
for the previous concentration inequality and suppose
|f(\bar{v},\bm{z})-\mathbb{E}f(\bar{v},\bm{z})|\leq\Delta
. Then for the optimal
\mathbf{v}
,

|\bar{v}|=|f(\bar{v},\bm{z})|\leq|f(\bar{v},\bm{z})-\mathbb{E}f(\bar{v},\bm{z})|+|\mathbb{E}f(\bar{v},\bm{z})|\leq\Delta+|\bar{v}|\cdot\erf(\varepsilon/|a|)\,,

where the first equality is from our original self-consistent equation
\bar{v}=f(\bar{v},\mathbf{z})
for the optimal
\mathbf{v}
. This in turn implies
|\bar{v}|\leq\eta
. Finally, from our concentration inequality,

\mathbb{P}(|\bar{v}^{*}(\bm{z})|>\eta)\leq\exp(-C \eta^2 (1 - \erf(\varepsilon/|a|))^2 P/a^2)\,.

SIV
Regression Capacity of Random Sphere-Like Manifolds

To investigate how the geometric properties of manifolds connect to their regression capacity, we extend the random point-like manifold model in
Sec. SIII
to random sphere-like manifolds with various latent geometric parameters including manifold dimension
D
, manifold radius
R
, correlation
\psi
between manifold centers, correlation
\rho
between manifold label, and correlation
\gamma
between manifold internal axes. We derive closed-form formula (
Theorem 5
and
Theorem 6
) for the regression capacity in terms of these latent parameters.

SIV.1
Capacity of Uncorrelated Spheres

We introduce structured variability to our data. In particular, we consider
D
-dimensional spherical manifolds with radius
R
, where
D
is fixed and
N,P\to\infty
. For simplicity, we will consider the uncorrelated case first. In the following section, we will consider a model of correlated spheres and reduce it to the uncorrelated spheres model, much like we reduce the correlated points model to uncorrelated points.

SIV.1.1
Our toy model for uncorrelated spheres and its regression capacity

To sample
P
manifolds
\{\mathcal{M}^{\mu}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we first sample their centroids
\{\mathbf{x}^{\mu}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
with mean square-norm
\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}^{\mu}_{0}\|^{2}=r^{2}
set by some
r>0
. For each manifold
\mu\in\{1,2,\dots,P\}
, we also sample
D
i.i.d. axis vectors
\{\mathbf{x}^{\mu}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\}_{i=1}^{D}
with mean square-norm
\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mu}\|^{2}=R^{2}
set by some
R>0
. More concretely, the centroids are sampled from
x^{\mu}_{0k}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{r^{2}}{N}\right)
for all
\mu\in\{1,\dots,P\}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and the axes are sampled from
x^{\mu}_{ik}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}\left(0,R^{2}/N\right)
for all
\mu\in\{1,\dots,P\},i\in\{1,\dots,D\}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
This scaling ensures that
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\nu}=r^{2}\delta_{\mu\nu}
, and
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\nu}=R^{2}\delta_{\mu\nu}\delta_{ij}
.
We sample the target labels
\{y^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
for each point i.i.d. from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
with some label variance
\sigma^{2}>0
. In the notation of
Sec. SII
, we have manifold shape

\mathcal{S}=\left\{(1,s_{1},\dots,s_{D}):\sum_{i=1}^{D}s_{i}^{2}=1\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D+1}

with dimension
D
, a correlation tensor
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{P(D+1)\times P(D+1)}
with entries

\Sigma_{\mu i}^{\nu j}=\begin{cases}r^{2}\delta_{\mu\nu}&\textrm{if i = j = 0},\\
R^{2}\delta_{\mu\nu}&\textrm{if i = j > 0},\end{cases}

and label correlation
H\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times P}
with entries
H_{\mu\nu}=\sigma^{2}\delta_{\mu\nu}
.

Here, we derive a simple capacity formula for the toy model described above by reducing the capacity formula for a correlated point model to an equivalent uncorrelated point model.

Theorem 5

(Formula for uncorrelated spheres)
.

Given the model for uncorrelated spheres described above. Let
\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,r,R,D,\sigma,P)
be the capacity formula from
Theorem 2
for this model, we have that
\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,r,R,D,\sigma,P)\xrightarrow{P\to\infty}\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,r,R,D,\sigma)
with

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,r,R,D,\sigma)=2\min_{\tau\geq 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\;\chi_{D}(x)g(x,\tau,\varepsilon,r,R,\sigma)

(S6)

where
\chi_{D}(x)=\frac{x^{D-1}e^{-x^{2}/2}}{2^{D/2-1}\Gamma(D/2)}\mathbb{I}(x\geq 0)
denotes the PDF of the
\chi
distribution with
D
degrees of freedom, and

\displaystyle g(x,\tau,\varepsilon,r,R,\sigma)

\displaystyle=h\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}{r^{2}}},\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{r},\frac{r^{2}x/R+\varepsilon\tau}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{r^{2}x/R+\varepsilon\tau}{\sqrt{2(r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2})}}\right)

\displaystyle+h\left(\frac{\sqrt{r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}}{\sqrt{R^{2}+r^{2}}},\frac{\varepsilon\tau-Rx}{\sqrt{R^{2}+r^{2}}},\max\left\{\frac{\varepsilon\tau-Rx}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}},\frac{r^{2}x/R-r^{2}\varepsilon\tau/R^{2}}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle-h\left(\frac{\sqrt{r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}}{\sqrt{R^{2}+r^{2}}},\frac{\varepsilon\tau-Rx}{\sqrt{R^{2}+r^{2}}},\frac{r^{2}x/R+\varepsilon\tau}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle+h\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}{r^{2}}},0,0\right)

\displaystyle-h\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}{r^{2}}},0,\max\left\{0,\frac{r^{2}x/R-r^{2}\varepsilon\tau/R^{2}}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left(x-\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{R}\right)^{2}\operatorname{erf}\left(\max\left\{0,\frac{r^{2}x/R-r^{2}\varepsilon\tau/R^{2}}{\sqrt{2(r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2})}}\right\}\right)

where

\displaystyle h(a,b,c)

\displaystyle=\int_{c}^{\infty}\mathcal{D}z(az-b)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{a(ac-2b)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{2}\right)+\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{2}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{c}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\,.

We interpret this formula and numerically validate it in the main text. The derivation details for
Theorem 5
is provided in the following subsubsection for the completeness of presentation.

SIV.1.2
Derivation details for
Theorem 5

For a given error tolerance
\varepsilon>0
and for each
P
,
Theorem 2
gives us a capacity estimator

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,P)=\frac{1}{P}\min_{\tau\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{t},\bm{y}}\min_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{B}(\bm{y})}\left\|\bm{t}-\tau\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{u}\right\|^{2}\,.

Our goal in this section will be to show that the limit

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon)=\lim_{P\to\infty}\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,P)

exists and compute it. Then we use
Theorem 2
to conclude that
\alpha(\varepsilon)
is the asymptotic capacity of our uncorrelated points model. For our specific model, we can simplify the above capacity estimator to:

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,P)=\min_{\tau\geq 0}\frac{1}{P}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{t},\bm{y}}\min_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{B}(\bm{y})}\left\|\bm{t}_{0}-\frac{\tau}{r}\bm{u}_{0}\right\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{D}\left\|\bm{t}_{i}-\frac{\tau}{R}\bm{u}_{i}\right\|^{2},

where

\mathcal{B}(\bm{y})=\left\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{P(D+1)}:|u^{\mu}_{0}-y_{\mu}|\leq\varepsilon-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{D}(u^{\mu}_{i})^{2}}\textrm{ for all }\mu\in\{1,\dots,P\}\right\}.

As in the uncorrelated points case, since the distributions of
\bm{t}
and
\bm{y}
as well as the constraint set
\mathcal{B}(\bm{y})
all factorize across the manifolds, we can immediately reduce our capacity estimator to the
P=1
case:

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,P)=\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,1)=\min_{\tau\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{t},y}\min_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{B}(y)}\left(t_{0}-\frac{\tau}{r}u_{0}\right)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{D}\left\|t_{i}-\frac{\tau}{R}u_{i}\right\|^{2},

where now the components
\{t_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}_{i=0}^{D}
and
\{u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}_{i=0}^{D}
are scalars and the constraint set is just

\mathcal{B}(y)=\left\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}:|u_{0}-y|\leq\varepsilon-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{D}u_{i}^{2}}\right\}.

As in previous sections, we make a change of variables

v_{i}=\begin{cases}u_{0}-y&\textrm{if }i=0,\\
u_{i}&\textrm{if }i>0,\end{cases}

so that capacity becomes

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon)=\min_{\tau\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{t},y}\min_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{B}(0)}\left(t_{0}-\frac{\tau}{r}(v_{0}+y)\right)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{D}\left\|t_{i}-\frac{\tau}{R}v_{i}\right\|^{2}.

As before, we can now combine
\bm{t}
and
\bm{y}
so that our estimator becomes

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon)=\min_{\tau\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{g}}f_{\tau}(\bm{g}),

where we define the field
f_{\tau}(\bm{g})
by

f_{\tau}(\bm{g})=\min_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{B}(0)}\left(\tau g_{0}-\frac{\tau}{r}v_{0}\right)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{D}\left\|\tau g_{i}-\frac{\tau}{R}v_{i}\right\|^{2},

and where
\bm{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{D+1}
is a mean-zero Gaussian vector with covariance

\mathbb{E}g_{i}g_{j}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{r^{2}}&\textrm{if }i=j=0,\\
\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}&\textrm{if }i=j>0.\end{cases}

Let us make the further change of variables

w_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{v_{0}}{r}&\textrm{if }i=0,\\
\frac{v_{i}}{R}&\textrm{if }i>0,\end{cases}

so that

f_{\tau}(\bm{g})=\tau^{2}\min_{\bm{w}\in\mathcal{A}}\|\bm{g}-\bm{w}\|^{2},

where

\mathcal{A}=\left\{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{D+1}:r|w_{0}|\leq\varepsilon-R\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{D}w_{i}^{2}}\right\}.

The minimization problem in
f_{\tau}(\bm{g})
is just a projection of
\bm{g}
onto the set
\mathcal{A}
. For
D=1
, we can visualize this solution set
\mathcal{A}
to get some intuition for how projection behaves:

Figure S2:
Visualization of the solution set
\mathcal{A}
.

The diamond-shaped gray convex body in the center is the solution set
\mathcal{A}
. We have split the space
\mathbb{R}^{D+1}
into six different regimes based on how the projection behaves when
\bm{g}
is in each region of space. For example, if
\bm{g}
is in the cone-shaped
“embedded (+)”
regime, then the nearest solution
\bm{w}\in\mathcal{A}
is always the point
\frac{\varepsilon}{r}\vec{e}_{0}
(where
\{\vec{e}_{i}\}
is the standard basis for
\mathbb{R}^{D+1}
). To compute
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{g}}f_{\tau}(\bm{g})
, we split into these six cases, which we now formally define: Going back to the general case of arbitrary
D
, we can write down a Lagrangian characterizing the optimal
W
in the definition of
f_{\tau}(\bm{g})
:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{w},\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{g}-\bm{w}\|^{2}+\lambda^{+}(rw_{0}-\varepsilon+R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|)+\lambda^{-}(-rw_{0}-\varepsilon+R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|),

setting
\bm{\tilde{w}}=(w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{D})
and
\bm{\tilde{g}}=(g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{D})
for convenience. Recall that the subgradient of the function
\bm{x}\mapsto\|\bm{x}\|
at the point
\bm{x}=0
consists of all vectors
\bm{v}
with
\|\bm{v}\|\leq 1
. The KKT conditions for this Lagrangian are then:

•

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{0}}=w_{0}-g_{0}+(\lambda^{+}-\lambda^{-})r=0
(stationarity w.r.t
w_{0}
),

•

Either
\bm{\tilde{w}}\neq 0
and
\nabla_{\bm{\tilde{w}}}\mathcal{L}=\bm{\tilde{w}}-\bm{\tilde{g}}+(\lambda^{+}+\lambda^{-})R\frac{\bm{\tilde{w}}}{\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|}=0
, or
\bm{\tilde{w}}=0
and
\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|\leq(\lambda^{+}+\lambda^{-})R
(stationarity w.r.t.
\bm{\tilde{W}}
),

•

If
\lambda^{+}>0
, then
rw_{0}=\varepsilon-R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|
(complementary slackness of upper bound),

•

If
\lambda^{-}>0
, then
-rw_{0}=\varepsilon-R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|
(complementary slackness of lower bound),

plus primal feasibility
\bm{w}\in\mathcal{A}
and dual feasibility
\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-}\geq 0
. We now split into six cases based on which of the variables
\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-},\bm{\tilde{w}}
are zero, characterize the values of
\bm{g}
corresponding to each case (Equations C1-C6), and obtain closed-form expressions for
f_{\tau}(\bm{g})
in each case (Equations F1-F6):

Case 1:
“Interior regime”
when
\lambda^{+}=\lambda^{-}=0

In this regime, the stationarity conditions immediately yields
\bm{w}=\bm{g}
, so

f_{\tau}(\bm{g})=0

(F1)

Since
\bm{g}=\bm{w}\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

|g_{0}|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{r}-\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{r}.

(C1)

Case 2:
“Embedded (+) regime”
when
\lambda^{+}>0,\lambda^{-}=0,\bm{\tilde{w}}=0

In this regime, complementary slackness tells us
rw_{0}=\varepsilon-R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|
, and since
\bm{\tilde{w}}=0
,
w_{0}=\varepsilon/r
. The stationarity conditions yield
g_{0}=w_{0}+\lambda^{+}r
and
\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|\leq\lambda^{+}R
. Combining these, we arrive at

g_{0}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{r}+\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}.

(C2)

Also, we can write

f_{\tau}(\bm{g})=\tau^{2}\left(g_{0}-\frac{\varepsilon}{r}\right)^{2}+\tau^{2}\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|^{2}.

(F2)

Case 3:
“Embedded (-) regime”
when
\lambda^{+}=0,\lambda^{-}>0,\bm{\tilde{w}}=0

In this regime, complementary slackness tells us
-rw_{0}=\varepsilon-R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|
, and since
\bm{\tilde{w}}=0
,
w_{0}=-\varepsilon/r
. The stationarity conditions yield
g_{0}=w_{0}-\lambda^{-}r
and
\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|\leq\lambda^{-}R
. Combining these, we arrive at

g_{0}\leq-\frac{\varepsilon}{r}-\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}.

(C3)

Also, we can write

f_{\tau}(\bm{g})=\tau^{2}\left(g_{0}+\frac{\varepsilon}{r}\right)^{2}+\tau^{2}\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|^{2}.

(F3)

Case 4:
“Touching (+) regime”
when
\lambda^{+}>0,\lambda^{-}=0,\bm{\tilde{w}}\neq 0

In this regime, stationarity gives
g_{0}=w_{0}+\lambda^{+}r\geq w_{0}
. Complementary slackness gives
w_{0}=\frac{\varepsilon}{r}-\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|}{r}
. From stationarity,
\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|=\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|-\lambda^{+}R\leq\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|
. Combining these, we get

g_{0}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{r}-\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{r}.

Stationarity also gives
R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|=R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|+\lambda^{+}R^{2}
. From complementary slackness, we also know
R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|=\varepsilon-rw_{0}
. Combining these,
R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|=\varepsilon-rw_{0}+\lambda^{+}R^{2}
. From stationarity, we know
rw_{0}=rg_{0}-\lambda^{+}r^{2}
. Together this yields
R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|=\varepsilon-rg_{0}+\lambda^{+}[r^{2}+R^{2}]
. We can then solve for
\lambda^{+}
:

\lambda^{+}=\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|-\varepsilon+rg_{0}}{R^{2}+r^{2}}.

From stationarity, we know
\lambda^{+}=\frac{\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}-\frac{\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|}{R}\leq\frac{\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}
. Thus,

\lambda^{+}=\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|-\varepsilon+rg_{0}}{R^{2}+r^{2}}\leq\frac{\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}.

This inequality rearranges to give

g_{0}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{r}+\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}.

From stationarity, we have
\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|=\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|+\lambda^{+}R
. From complementary slackness,
\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|=\frac{\varepsilon}{R}-\frac{rw_{0}}{R}
. Combining these,
\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|=\frac{\varepsilon}{R}-\frac{rw_{0}}{R}+\lambda^{+}R
. Since we have
\lambda^{+}=\frac{g_{0}-w_{0}}{r}
from stationarity, it follows that

\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|=\frac{\varepsilon}{R}-\frac{rw_{0}}{R}+\frac{Rg_{0}}{r}-\frac{Rw_{0}}{r}.

Since
|rw_{0}|\leq\varepsilon-R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|
from primal feasibility, certainly
\varepsilon-R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|\geq 0
, so from slackness we have
w_{0}\geq 0
. Combining this with the above inequality,

\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{R}+\frac{Rg_{0}}{r},

which rearranges to

g_{0}\geq\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}-\frac{r\varepsilon}{R^{2}}.

Combining all these results, we arrive at

\max\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{r}-\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{r},\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}-\frac{r\varepsilon}{R^{2}}\right\}\leq g_{0}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{r}+\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}.

(C4)

From stationarity,
\bm{\tilde{g}}-\bm{\tilde{w}}=\lambda^{+}R\frac{\bm{\tilde{w}}}{\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|}
, so
\|\bm{\tilde{g}}-\bm{\tilde{w}}\|^{2}=(\lambda^{+})^{2}R^{2}
. Also from stationarity,
g_{0}-w_{0}=\lambda^{+}r
, so
(g_{0}-w_{0})^{2}=(\lambda^{+})^{2}r^{2}
. Combining this with our equation for
\lambda^{+}
above, we get

f_{\tau}(\bm{g})=\tau^{2}\frac{(R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|-\varepsilon+rg_{0})^{2}}{R^{2}+r^{2}}.

(F4)

Case 5:
“Touching (-) regime”
when
\lambda^{+}=0,\lambda^{-}>0,\bm{\tilde{w}}\neq 0

In this regime, stationarity gives
g_{0}=w_{0}-\lambda^{-}r\leq w_{0}
. Complementary slackness gives
w_{0}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{r}+\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|}{r}
. From stationarity,
\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|=\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|-\lambda^{-}R\leq\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|
. Combining these, we get

g_{0}\leq-\frac{\varepsilon}{r}+\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{r}.

Stationarity also gives
R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|=R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|+\lambda^{-}R^{2}
. From complementary slackness, we also know
R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|=\varepsilon+rw_{0}
. Combining these,
R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|=\varepsilon+rw_{0}+\lambda^{+}R^{2}
. From stationarity, we know
rw_{0}=rg_{0}+\lambda^{-}r^{2}
. Together this yields
R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|=\varepsilon+rg_{0}+\lambda^{-}[r^{2}+R^{2}]
. We can then solve for
\lambda^{-}
:

\lambda^{-}=\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|-\varepsilon-rg_{0}}{R^{2}+r^{2}}.

From stationarity, we know
\lambda^{-}=\frac{\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}-\frac{\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|}{R}\leq\frac{\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}
. Thus,

\lambda^{-}=\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|-\varepsilon-rg_{0}}{R^{2}+r^{2}}\leq\frac{\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}.

This inequality rearranges to give

g_{0}\geq-\frac{\varepsilon}{r}-\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}.

From stationarity, we have
\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|=\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|+\lambda^{-}R
. From complementary slackness,
\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|=\frac{\varepsilon}{R}+\frac{rw_{0}}{R}
. Combining these,
\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|=\frac{\varepsilon}{R}+\frac{rw_{0}}{R}+\lambda^{-}R
. Since we have
\lambda^{-}=\frac{-g_{0}+w_{0}}{r}
from stationarity, it follows that

\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|=\frac{\varepsilon}{R}+\frac{rw_{0}}{R}-\frac{Rg_{0}}{r}+\frac{Rw_{0}}{r}.

Since
|rw_{0}|\leq\varepsilon-R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|
from primal feasibility, certainly
\varepsilon-R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|\geq 0
, so from slackness we have
w_{0}\leq 0
. Combining this with the above inequality,

\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{R}-\frac{Rg_{0}}{r},

which rearranges to

g_{0}\leq-\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}+\frac{r\varepsilon}{R^{2}}.

Combining all these results, we arrive at

-\frac{\varepsilon}{r}-\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}\leq g_{0}\leq\min\left\{-\frac{\varepsilon}{r}+\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{r},-\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}+\frac{r\varepsilon}{R^{2}}\right\}.

(C5)

From stationarity,
\bm{\tilde{g}}-\bm{\tilde{w}}=\lambda^{-}R\frac{\bm{\tilde{w}}}{\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|}
, so
\|\bm{\tilde{g}}-\bm{\tilde{w}}\|^{2}=(\lambda^{-})^{2}R^{2}
. Also from stationarity,
g_{0}-w_{0}=-\lambda^{-}r
, so
(g_{0}-w_{0})^{2}=(\lambda^{-})^{2}r^{2}
. Combining this with our equation for
\lambda^{-}
above, we get

f_{\tau}(\bm{g})=\tau^{2}\frac{(R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|-\varepsilon-rg_{0})^{2}}{R^{2}+r^{2}}.

(F5)

Case 6:
“Sandwiched regime”
when
\lambda^{+}>0,\lambda^{-}>0

In this regime, complementary slackness implies
both

rw_{0}=\varepsilon-R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|
and
-rw_{0}=\varepsilon-R\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|
. Thus,
w_{0}=0
and
\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|=\varepsilon/R
. Since
\bm{\tilde{w}}\neq 0
, stationarity gives
\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|=\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|+(\lambda^{+}+\lambda^{-})R
. Since
\|\bm{\tilde{w}}\|=\varepsilon/r
, this implies
(\lambda^{+}+\lambda^{-})R=\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|-\varepsilon/R
. From stationarity again, we have
g_{0}=w_{0}+(\lambda^{+}-\lambda^{-})r
, and since
w_{0}=0
, we have
|g_{0}|=|\lambda^{+}-\lambda^{-}|r\leq(\lambda^{+}+\lambda^{-})r
. Combining these,

|g_{0}|\leq\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}-\frac{r\varepsilon}{R^{2}}.

(C6)

Since
w_{0}=0
,
(g_{0}-w_{0})^{2}=g_{0}^{2}
. From stationarity, we have
\|\bm{\tilde{g}}-\bm{\tilde{w}}\|^{2}=(\lambda^{+}+\lambda^{-})^{2}R^{2}
. We showed above that
(\lambda^{+}+\lambda^{-})R=\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|-\varepsilon/R
, so

f_{\tau}(\bm{g})=\tau^{2}g_{0}^{2}+\tau^{2}\left(\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|-\frac{\varepsilon}{R}\right)^{2}.

(F6)

Finally, observe that equations C1-C6 are mutually exclusive (ignoring overlap of probability zero). Thus, conditions C1-C6 completely characterize the six regimes we identified. Using the explicit formulae for
f_{\tau}(\bm{g})
in terms of

and
\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|
we derived above for each regime, we can write the average
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{g}}f_{\tau}(\bm{g})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{G}f_{\tau}(\bm{g})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[0\middle||g_{0}|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{r}-\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{G}}\|}{r}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\tau^{2}\left(g_{0}-\frac{\varepsilon}{r}\right)^{2}+\tau^{2}\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|^{2}\middle|g_{0}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{r}+\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\tau^{2}\left(g_{0}+\frac{\varepsilon}{r}\right)^{2}+\tau^{2}\|\bm{\tilde{G}}\|^{2}\middle|g_{0}\leq-\frac{\varepsilon}{r}-\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\tau^{2}\frac{(R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|-\varepsilon+rg_{0})^{2}}{R^{2}+r^{2}}\middle|\max\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{r}-\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{r},\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}-\frac{r\varepsilon}{R^{2}}\right\}\leq g_{0}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{r}+\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\tau^{2}\frac{(R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|-\varepsilon-rg_{0})^{2}}{R^{2}+r^{2}}\middle|-\frac{\varepsilon}{r}-\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}\leq g_{0}\leq\min\left\{-\frac{\varepsilon}{r}+\frac{R\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{r},-\frac{r\|\bm{\tilde{g}}\|}{R}+\frac{r\varepsilon}{R^{2}}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{g}}f_{\tau}(\bm{g})

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\chi_{D}(x)\int_{\frac{r^{2}x/R+\varepsilon\tau}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}}}^{\infty}\mathcal{D}z\;\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}{r^{2}}}z-\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{r}\right)^{2}+x^{2}

\displaystyle+2\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\chi_{D}(x)\int_{\max\left\{\frac{\varepsilon\tau-Rx}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}},\frac{r^{2}x/R-r^{2}\varepsilon\tau/R^{2}}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}}\right\}}^{\frac{r^{2}x/R+\varepsilon\tau}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}}}\mathcal{D}z\;\frac{(Rx-\varepsilon\tau+\sqrt{r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}z)^{2}}{R^{2}+r^{2}}

\displaystyle+2\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\chi_{D}(x)\int_{0}^{\max\left\{0,\frac{r^{2}x/R-r^{2}\varepsilon\tau/R^{2}}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}}\right\}}\mathcal{D}z\;\sqrt{1+\frac{\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}{r^{2}}}z^{2}+\left(x-\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{R}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{r^{2}x/R+\varepsilon\tau}{\sqrt{2(r^{2}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2})}}\right)

SIV.2
Correlated Spheres Reduce to Uncorrelated Spheres

Next, we introduce uniform correlations to our spheres model. As in our correlated points model, we have parameters
\psi\in[0,1)
and
\rho\in[0,1)
that control the correlations among the sphere centers and labels, respectively. Additionally, we introduce another correlation
\gamma\in[0,1)
among the

SIV.2.1
Our toy model for correlated spheres and its regression capacity

In the notation of Theorem 5, we define the data correlation tensor by

\Sigma_{\mu i}^{\nu j}=\begin{cases}r^{2}[(1-\psi)\delta_{\mu\nu}+\psi]&\textrm{if }i=j=0,\\

Here, we derive a simple capacity formula for the toy model described above by reducing the capacity formula for a correlated point model to an equivalent uncorrelated point model.

.

\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,r,R,D,\sigma,\rho,\psi,\gamma)=\alpha^{-1}(\varepsilon,r\sqrt{1-\psi},R\sqrt{\gamma},D,\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho})\,.

\{\mathcal{M}^{\mu}\},\{y^{\mu}\}
to be

\alpha_{\mathrm{simulation},\mathrm{bias}}(\varepsilon)=\frac{P}{N_{\mathrm{simulation},\mathrm{bias}}^{*}(\varepsilon)}\,.

This definition is conceptually no different than the bias-free case. We ask how many dimensions
N_{\mathrm{proj}}
of our data we need to retain under a random projection so that an admissible linear readout
with some adaptive bias that may differ for each random projection
exists with high probability. Like in our derivation of the bias-free instanced-based capacity estimator, we start by rewriting
p_{\mathrm{bias}}(N_{\mathrm{proj}})
in terms of the probability that a random subspace
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
of dimension
N_{\mathrm{proj}}
intersects some closed, convex cone (in particular, this is analogous to
Lemma 2
):

Rather than working directly with
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{bias}}(M)
, the key here is to study the set of weights
\bm{w}
for which some bias exists to make it into an admissible readout. In particular, we define
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{bias}}(M)=\{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{proj}}}:\exists b\in\mathbb{R},(\bm{w},b)\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{bias}}(M)\}
. Now
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{bias}}(M)\neq\varnothing
is equivalent to
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{bias}}(M)\neq\varnothing
, so we can write

p_{\mathrm{bias}}(N_{\mathrm{proj}})=\mathbb{P}_{\Pi^{(N_{\mathrm{proj}})}}\left(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{bias}}(\Pi^{(N_{\mathrm{proj}})})\neq\varnothing\right).

In turn,
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{bias}}(\Pi^{(N_{\mathrm{proj}})})\neq\varnothing
is equivalent to
\operatorname{im}(\Pi^{(N_{\mathrm{proj}})})\cap\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{bias}}\neq\{0\}
, where like before we define
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{bias}}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{bias}}(I_{N})
. Since the subspace
\operatorname{im}(\Pi^{(N_{\mathrm{proj}})})
is a uniformly random
N_{\mathrm{proj}}
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we can replace it with the identically distributed subspace
QD
, where
Q
is a uniformly random member of
O(N)
and
D
is some fixed
N_{\mathrm{proj}}
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Finally, the condition
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{bias}}\cap QD\neq\{0\}
is equivalent to
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{bias}}^{+}\cap QD\neq\{0\}
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{bias}}^{+}
is the cone generated by
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{bias}}
. Thus,

p_{\mathrm{bias}}(N_{\mathrm{proj}})=\mathbb{P}_{Q}\left(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{+}_{\mathrm{bias}}\cap QD\neq\{0\}\right),

and just like in the bias-free case, we have succeeded in rewriting
p_{\mathrm{bias}}
as the probability of a closed, convex cone
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{bias}}^{+}
having non-trivial intersection with a randomly-oriented subspace. We can therefore invoke the approximate conic kinematic formula
Lemma 1
to derive the following bias-enabled capacity estimator (analogous to
Definition 2
):

Definition 5

(Capacity formula; bias-enabled)
.

Let
P
manifolds
\{\mathcal{M}^{\mu}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
and labels
\{y^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}\}_{\mu=1}^{P}
be given as above. For any tolerance
\varepsilon\geq 0
, define an estimator
N^{*}_{\mathrm{bias}}(\varepsilon)
for the critical dimension
N^{*}_{\mathrm{bias}}
as

N^{*}_{\mathrm{bias}}(\varepsilon):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{t}}\left[\min_{\mathbf{w}^{+}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}_{\mathrm{bias}}}\|\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{w}^{+}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\,,

where
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{bias}}=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{bias}}(I_{N})
is the set of regression weights for the original data
such that some bias exists to make it into an admissible readout
and
S^{+}:=\{\lambda\mathbf{s}:\mathbf{s}\in S,\lambda\geq 0\}
denotes the cone generated by a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Define an estimator
\alpha_{\mathrm{bias}}(\varepsilon)
for simulation capacity
\alpha_{\mathrm{simulation}}(\varepsilon)
as

\alpha_{\mathrm{bias}}(\varepsilon):=\frac{P}{N_{\mathrm{bias}}^{*}(\varepsilon)}\,.

One can similarly incorporate an adaptive bias to our mean-field notion of regression capacity (originally defined in
Sec. SII
). For all of the synthetic data models we studied in the previous sections, one can show that the adaptive bias
b
concentrates to a value of zero and therefore capacity is unaffected by the addition of a bias. This is essentially a consequence of the label distributions we studied being symmetric around 0.
