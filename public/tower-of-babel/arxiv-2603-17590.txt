Abstract
. Suppose X X is an RCD ⁡ ( K , N ) \mathrm{RCD}(K,N) space with K ∈ ℝ K\in\mathbb{R} and N ∈ ( 1 , ∞ ) N\in(1,\infty) . We obtain a characterisation of the Newtonian-Sobolev space N 1 , 2 ​ ( X ) N^{1,2}(X) in terms of a quantity which measures to what extent a function is locally (across all scales and locations) well-approximated by harmonic functions. A similar characterisation is obtained which further takes into account the local oscillations of the approximating harmonic functions. The first characterisation is new even when X = ℝ n X=\mathbb{R}^{n} ; the second characterisation is a version of Dorronsoro’s Theorem in RCD spaces and gives a new proof of (a special case) of this theorem in Euclidean space.

1.
Introduction

It is well known that every Lipschitz function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable at
\mathcal{L}^{n}
-a.e. point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\mathcal{L}^{n}
denotes the
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure – this is Rademacher’s Theorem. While this gives strong structural information about
f
at infinitesimal scales, it says nothing about the structure of
f
at coarse scales. A quantitative version of Rademacher’s Theorem, which does provide structural information at coarse scales, was proven by Dorronsoro
[
Dor85
]
. The following is a special case of Dorronosoro’s Theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then
f\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if and only if

(1.1)

\displaystyle\Omega(f)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Omega_{f}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{dxdr}{r}<\infty.

Here,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\Omega_{f}(x,r)=\inf_{A}\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}\left(\frac{f-A}{r}\right)^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where the infimum is taken over all affine functions
A\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. In this case, it follows that
\|f\|_{W^{1,2}(X)}^{2}\sim\|f\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}+\Omega(f)
.

In words, Dorronsoro’s Theorem says that a function is in
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if and only if it is
sufficiently well-approximated
by affine functions across all scales and location, this being quantified by condition (
1.1
). The main result in
[
Dor85
]
is more general than stated above, providing similar characterisations of fractional potential spaces, however, Theorem
1.1
has proven effective in a variety of applications.

A key application of Theorem
1.1
is to the theory of
uniformly rectifiable sets
. These sets are a quantitative analogue of rectifiable sets and were first introduced and developed by David and Semmes
[
DS91
,
DS93
]
. For example, Theorem
1.1
is used in
[
DS91
]
to show that uniformly rectifiable sets satisfy the so-called
strong geometric lemma
(a quantitative tangent condition) and in
[
Azz21
]
to show that subsets of Euclidean space supporting a Poincaré inequality are uniformly rectifiable. Dorronsoro’s Theorem is an example of
quantitative differentiation
i.e., a result which provides information regarding how soon a function reaches a certain threshold of regularity. Such results have been used
[
CN13a
,
CN13b
]
to study partial regularity theory in geometric analysis and nonlinear PDE, in
[
Li14
]
to study quantitative non-embeddability of Carnot groups, and in
[
CKN11
]
to study the sparsest cut problem from theoretical computer science.

The purpose of this paper is to prove a version of Dorronsoro’s Theorem in the class of metric measure spaces satisfying the
Riemann curvature dimension
condition
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
– a generalisation of the class of Riemannian manifolds with lower bounds on the Ricci curvature. For the relevant background and historical notes on
\mathrm{RCD}
spaces, we direct the reader to the surveys
[
Amb18
,
Gig23
]
. For now, we note that the
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
condition is a Riemannian counterpart to the
curvature dimension
condition CD
(K,N)
introduced independently in the works
[
LV09
]
and
[
Stu06a
,
Stu06b
]
. While the
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
and CD
(K,N)
conditions are both notions of having Ricci curvature bounded below, the
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
condition differs from the CD
(K,N)
condition in that it prevents Finsler-type geometry. Classical examples of
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
spaces are Riemannian manifolds with Ricci curvature bounded below, Ricci limit spaces (see
[
CC97
]
), and Alexandrov spaces with (sectional) curvature bounded below (see
[
Pet11
,
ZZ10
]
). Such spaces are not necessarily locally Euclidean, can have both non-unique and non-Euclidean tangents, and can admit conical singularities.

Any space which satisfies the
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
condition with
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in(1,\infty)
satisfies a Rademacher-type theorem. Indeed, every such space is locally doubling and, by
[
Raj12a
,
Raj12b
]
, admits a local Poincaré inequality in the sense of Heinonen and Koskela
[
HK98
]
, see (
2.15
). The seminal work of Cheeger
[
Che99
]
then implies that every Lipschitz function is
infinitesimally generalised linear
at almost every point, meaning, every blow-up of the function at such a point is harmonic (defined via minimisation of a certain Dirichlet energy) with constant energy density, see
[
Che99
]
for the precise definition.

A quantitative version of the above result has appeared in
[
Che12
,
Che23
]
, where it is shown that any Lipschitz function is well-approximated by harmonic functions at
most
locations and scales. Here,
most
is quantified by a certain
weak Carleson condition
. A different quantitative differentiation result for
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
spaces, also in terms of a weak-type Carleson condition, was proved in
[
HVV24
]
as a consequence of its (quantitative) rectifiability properties. These quantitative results differ from Theorem
1.1
in the sense that they only give control over the measure of the scales and locations which exhibit bad behaviour, they do not provide integrability of the coefficients which measure the approximations.

Main results

In Theorem
A
below we characterise the Newtonian-Sobolev space
N^{1,2}(X)
(see
[
Sha00
]
) in the case that
X
is an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space. We obtain this characterisation, in the spirit of Theorem
1.1
, by considering a square function which measures the deviation from a certain model class. Of course, affine functions do not make sense in such generality, so we define a new coefficient
H_{f}^{\mathrm{osc}}
which measures local approximations by harmonic functions while also taking into account the oscillation of the gradient of the approximating function.

More precisely, suppose
(X,d,\mu)
is an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space. For a function
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
and a ball
B(x,r)\subset X,
define

(1.3)

\displaystyle H_{f}^{\mathrm{osc}}(x,r)^{2}=\inf_{h}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}\left(\frac{f-h}{r}\right)^{2}+\left(g_{h}-\langle g_{h}\rangle_{B(x,r)}\right)^{2}\,d\mu,

where the infimum is taken over all functions
h
which are
harmonic
(see (
2.32
)) in
B(x,r)
,
g_{h}
is the
minimal weak upper gradient
for
h
in
B(x,r)
(see (
2.10
)) and
\langle g_{h}\rangle_{B(x,r)}
is the average of
g_{h}
over
B(x,r)
. As previously mentioned, harmonicity is defined via minimisation of a certain Dirichlet energy; the minimal upper gradient is a metric analogue of
|\nabla h|.

The definition of
H_{f}^{\mathrm{osc}}
is motivated by the following fact – a function
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is affine if and only if it is harmonic and satisfies
g_{h}\equiv c
i.e., if and only if it is generalised linear (see
[
Che12
]
). It is tempting to seek a version of Theorem
1.1
which characterises the Sobolev space in terms of approximations by generalised linear functions. However, in such generality, one cannot expect the existence of any non-constant generalised linear function. It turns out that the quantity (
1.3
) is a good replacement for
approximating by generalised linear functions
without assuming the existence of such objects, as demonstrated below.

Theorem A
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space with
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in(1,\infty)
, assume that
X
is bounded in the case that
K<0
, and let
f\in L^{2}(X).
Then,
f\in N^{1,2}(X)
if and only if

(1.4)

\displaystyle H^{\emph{osc}}(f)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)}\int_{X}H_{f}^{\mathrm{osc}}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{d\mu dr}{r}<\infty.

In this case, we have

(1.5)

\displaystyle\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\sim\left\|\frac{f-\langle f\rangle_{X}}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)}\right\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}+H^{\emph{osc}}(f),

where the implicit constants depend only on
N
in the case that
K\geq 0
, and only on
N,K
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)
in the case that
K<0
. Here,
\nabla
is the canonical choice of gradient operator on
X
, see Section
2
.

We actually prove a version of Theorem
A
with
H_{f}^{\mathrm{osc}}
replaced by another quantity
H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}
defined in terms of the Hessian on
\mathrm{RCD}
spaces (see Definition
3.2
). Theorem
A
is an immediate consequence of this more general statement. We have stated Theorem
A
in terms of
H_{f}^{\mathrm{osc}}
since this makes sense in more general metric spaces, for example, in spaces supporting a Poincaré inequality. When
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, while it is not clear whether
H_{f}^{\mathrm{osc}}(x,r)
is comparable to
\Omega_{f}(x,r)
, it does hold that that
H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}(x,r)\sim\Omega_{f}(x,r)
(Lemma
3.4
). Thus, the proof presented here for Theorem
A
gives a new proof of Theorem
1.1
in that setting.

It turns out that the oscillation term in (
1.3
) is not necessary to characterise
N^{1,2}(X)
in terms of harmonic approximations. Indeed, we define a further coefficient
H_{f}
as in (
1.3
) except without the oscillatory term (Definition
3.1
). Then, we show the following.

Theorem B
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space with
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in(1,\infty)
, assume that
X
is bounded in the case that
K<0
, and let
f\in L^{2}(X).
Then,
f\in N^{1,2}(X)
if and only if

(1.6)

\displaystyle H(f)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)}\int_{X}H_{f}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{dxdr}{r}<\infty.

In this case, we have

(1.7)

\displaystyle\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\sim\left\|\frac{f-\langle f\rangle_{X}}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)}\right\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}+H(f),

where the implicit constants depend only on
N
in the case that
K\geq 0
, and only on
N,K
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)
in the case that
K<0
. Here,
\nabla
is the canonical choice of gradient operator on
X
, see Section
2
.

For any
f\in N^{1,2}(X)
, condition (
1.6
) is immediate from Theorem
A
. Thus, the main point of Theorem
B
is that (
1.6
) is enough to guarantee
f\in N^{1,2}(X).
Combining both Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
, one sees that having good harmonic approximations as in (
1.6
) self-improves to having good harmonic approximations with controlled oscillation as in (
1.4
). In the Euclidean case, (
1.6
) self-improves to having good affine approximations in the sense of (
1.1
). The fact that (
1.6
) implies
f\in N^{1,2}(X)
is new even in the Euclidean case. To summarise, whenever the coefficients make sense, Theorems
1.1
,
A
and
B
imply

(1.8)

\displaystyle\Omega(f)\sim H^{\text{osc}}(f)\sim H(f).

It is natural here to compare Theorem
B
with
[
AMV12
, Theorem 1.1]
(in the case
p=2
). There, the authors prove a Dorronsoro-type estimate for functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
in terms of a square function which quantifies the size of
|f(x)-\langle f\rangle_{B(x,r)}|
across all scales and locations. Heuristically, this is measuring how far
f
is from a harmonic function by measuring to what extent is satisfies the mean value property.

Finally, along with our main results concerning
\mathrm{RCD}
spaces, we prove a quantitative differentiability result for Sobolev functions on doubling metric measure spaces supporting a Poincaré inequality. By
[
Che99
]
, one can assign for each
f\in N^{1,2}(X)
a Cheeger derivative
Df
. This differential structure comes with a canonical Cheeger energy and a notion of Cheeger harmonic functions with respect to this energy. Thus, one can define a coefficient
H_{f,D}
which quantifies how far
f
is from a Cheeger harmonic function. The coefficient
H_{f}
from above is in fact just
H_{\nabla,f}
with
\nabla
the canonical choice of gradient operator on
\mathrm{RCD}
spaces. In that case, it turns out that the space of Cheeger harmonic functions with respect to
\nabla
is exactly the space of harmonic functions considered in
[
BB11
]
.

Our result in this setting is the following. We have stated a local version which implies the corresponding global estimate (like this stated in Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
) after taking
R\to\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)
.

Theorem C
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a doubling metric measure space which supports a weak
(1,2)
-Poincaré inequality, let
D
be a Cheeger derivative on
X
and let
f\in N^{1,2}(X)
. Then, for each ball
B=B(z,R)
,

(1.9)

\displaystyle\hskip 15.00002pt\left\|\frac{f-\langle f\rangle_{B}}{R}\right\|_{L^{2}(B)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B}H_{f,D}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{d\mu dr}{r}\lesssim\|Df\|_{L^{2}(AB)}^{2},

where
A\geq 1
is a constant multiple of the dilation constant in the weak Poincaré inequality and the implicit constant depends only on the doubling constant and the constants appearing in the Poincaré inequality.

Additional remarks and further questions

Besides those results mentioned in the previous section, quantitative differentiation has been developed in a number of other non-Euclidean settings, see
[
AS14
,
Hof95
,
HN19
]
. While still Euclidean, we also mention the main result of
[
AMV23
]
, which is a version of Theorem
1.1
for functions defined on uniformly rectifiable subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The phrase
Dorronsoro’s Theorem
is often associated with one of the inequalities in Theorem
A
, namely,
H^{\text{osc}}(f)\lesssim\|\nabla f\|^{2}.
There are now a number of different proofs of this inequality, each relying in some way on Euclidean structure or complex techniques. For example, the original proof due to Dorronsoro and the much shorter proof due Azzam
[
Azz16
]
rely critically on the Fourier transform; the proof due to Hytönen and Naor
[
HN19
]
relies on heat flow techniques; the starting point to Orponen’s proof
[
Orp21
]
is a difficult theorem of Jones
[
Jon90
]
. The proof here relies only on basic properties of harmonic functions and basic inequalities related to them (e.g., Caccioppoli), and is inspired by the methods presented in
[
Che12
]
. As far as the author is aware, the converse inequality is found only in the original paper of Dorronsoro. Here, we adapt some of the heat flow techniques from
[
HN19
]
to given an alternative proof of this inequality which works in
\mathrm{RCD}
spaces.

Our main result characterises
N^{1,2}(X)
only, while Dorronsoro’s original paper characterises (among other things) all
N^{1,p}(X)
with
1<p<\infty.
The most obvious question is whether a version of Theorem
A
can be shown for
p\neq 2.
Finally, every bounded Ahlfors regular
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space supports a weak Poincaré inequality and is uniformly rectifiable (see
[
HVV24
]
for this last point). It is natural to ask whether Theorem
A
can be shown for
X
supporting a weak Poincaré inequality or being uniformly rectifiable.

Upper gradients and Newtonian-Sobolev spaces

Upper gradients were first introduced in
[
HK98
]
. A non-negative Borel function
g
on a metric space
(X,d)
is said to be an
upper gradient
of a real-valued function
u
if the inequality

(2.10)

\displaystyle|u(\gamma(a))-u(\gamma(b))|\leq\int_{\gamma}g\,ds

holds for every rectifiable curve
\gamma\colon[a,b]\to X
. Here, the integral of
g
on the right-hand side is computed with respect to the arc length measure along
\gamma
induced by the metric
d
.

Following
[
Sha00
]
, we define the Newtonian-Sobolev space
N^{1,p}(X)
as the collection of all function
f\in L^{p}(X)
which admit an upper gradient
g\in L^{p}(X).

A semi-norm on
N^{1,p}(X)
is provided by

(2.11)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{N^{1,p}}^{p}=\|u\|_{L^{p}}^{p}+\inf_{g}\|g\|_{L^{p}}^{p},

where the infimum is taken over all upper gradients
g
of
u
. By the result in
[
Sha00
]
and an observation of Hajłasz
[
Haj03
]
in the case
p=1
, there exists a unique minimal
g_{u}
, called the
minimal weak upper gradient
, which is an upper gradient along
p
-almost every rectifiable curve and for which

(2.12)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{N^{1,p}}^{p}=\|u\|_{L^{p}}^{p}+\|g_{u}\|_{L^{p}}^{p}.

Here,
p
-almost every
is defined with respect to the
p
-modulus

\text{Mod}_{p}
(see
[
Hei01
]
) and
minimal
means that
g_{u}\leq g

\mu
-a.e. for any other
g
which is an upper gradient along
p
-almost every rectifiable curve.

Given a set
E\subseteq X,
define the Newtonian space with zero boundary values as

(2.13)

\displaystyle N_{0}^{1,p}(E)\coloneqq\{u\in N^{1,p}(X)\colon u=0\ p\mbox{-quasi-everywhere in }X\setminus E\}.

Here,
p
-quasi-everywhere
is defined with respect to a certain
p
-capacity in
X
, see for instance
[
Sha00
]
. Finally, for an open set
\Omega\subseteq X
, let

(2.14)

\displaystyle N^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)\coloneqq\{u\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)\colon\eta u\in N^{1,2}(X)\mbox{ for all }\eta\in{\rm LIP}_{c}(\Omega)\}.

Poincaré inequalities

Following
[
HK98
]
, we say a metric measure space supports a
weak
(q,p)
-Poincaré inequality
if there are constants
A_{\mathrm{PI}},C_{\mathrm{PI}}\geq 1
such that

(2.15)

\displaystyle\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B}|u-\langle u\rangle_{B}|^{q}\,d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq C_{\mathrm{PI}}r_{B}\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{A_{\mathrm{PI}}B}g^{p}_{u}\,d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

for all balls
B
in
X
and all integrable functions
u\colon B\to\mathbb{R}
. Here, and throughout the remainder of the article,

\langle u\rangle_{B}\coloneqq\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B}u\,d\mu\coloneqq\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\int_{B}u\,d\mu

denotes the average integral of
u
over
B
.

By Hölder’s inequality, it is easily seen that a weak Poincaré inequality is stronger for larger values of
q
and smaller values of
p
. If the underlying space is doubling, a weak
(1,p)
-Poincaré inequality is known to self-improve. The following result, see
[
HK95
, Theorem 5.1]
, demonstrates this.

Theorem 2.6
.

Suppose
(X,d,\mu)
is a doubling metric measure space which supports a weak
(1,p)
-Poincaré inequality. Then,
X
supports a weak
(q,p)
-Poincaré inequality for some
q>p.
In particular,
X
supports a weak
(p,p)
-Poincaré inequality.

The following result gives us a way to glue Sobolev functions with zero boundary values on disjoint open sets.

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose
(X,d,\mu)
is a doubling metric measure space which supports a weak
(1,p)
-Poincaré inequality, and
f\in N^{1,2}(X).
Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of disjoint open sets in
X
and
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of functions on
X
such that
\phi_{i}\in N^{1,2}_{0}(U_{i})
and
\|g_{\phi_{i}}\|_{L^{2}(U_{i})}\leq C\|g_{f}\|_{L^{2}(U_{i})}
for each
i\in I.
Then,
f^{\prime}\coloneqq f+\sum_{i\in I}\phi_{i}\in N^{1,2}(X).

Proof.

Since
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{supp}(\phi_{i})\subseteq U_{i}
and
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a disjoint collection of sets, one has that
f^{\prime}\in L^{2}(X)
. Thus, we only need to check that
f^{\prime}
has an upper gradient in
L^{2}(X)
. By
[
HKST15
, Lemma 6.2.2]
, it suffices to check that there exists
g\in L^{2}(X)
which is an upper gradient for
f^{\prime}
along
p
-almost every rectifiable curve.

We consider the function

(2.16)

\displaystyle g\coloneqq g_{f}+\sum_{i\in I}g_{\phi_{i}}.

Since
\phi_{i}\in N_{0}^{1,2}(U_{i})
, its minimal weak upper gradient
g_{\phi_{i}}
is zero
\mu
-a.e. in
X\setminus U_{i}
. Then, since the
U_{i}
are disjoint, the estimates
\|g_{\phi_{i}}\|_{L^{2}(U_{i})}\leq C\|g_{f}\|_{L^{2}(U_{i})}
for
i\in I
imply
\|g\|_{L^{2}(X)}\lesssim\|g_{f}\|_{L^{2}(X)}
i.e.,
g\in L^{2}(X)
. Let
\Gamma
be the collection of rectifiable curves in
X
. Let
\Gamma_{f}
be the rectifiable curves along which
g_{f}
fails to be an upper gradient for
f
. Define
\Gamma_{\phi_{i}}
similarly. Then, let

(2.17)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma\setminus\left(\Gamma_{f}\cup\bigcup_{i\in I}\Gamma_{i}\right).

By countable sub-additivity of the
p
-modulus (
[
Hei01
]
),
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma
has full
p
-modulus. Moreover, since for each
\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}
and
i\in I
,
g_{f}
is upper gradient for
f
along
\gamma
and
g_{\phi_{i}}
is upper gradient for
\phi_{i}
along
\gamma
, it is easy to check that
g
is an upper gradient for
f^{\prime}
along
\gamma
. Thus,
g
is an upper gradient for
f^{\prime}
along
p
-almost every rectifiable curve.
∎

The following Sobolev-type inequality is proved in
[
Bjö02
]
(see Proposition 3.1), based on arguments given in
[
KS01
, Lemma 2.8]
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Suppose
(X,d,\mu)
is a doubling metric measure space which supports a weak
(q,p)
-Poincaré inequality. There exists
C>0
, depending only on the doubling and PI constants, such that, if
B
is a ball in
X
with
0<r_{B}<(1/3)\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}X
and
u\in N_{0}^{1,p}(B)
, then

(2.18)

\displaystyle\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{B}|u|^{q}\,d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq Cr\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{B}g_{u}^{p}\,d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Derivatives and Cheeger harmonic functions

In
[
Che99
]
, Cheeger studied differentiability properties of Lipschitz functions on doubling metric measure spaces which support a weak Poincaré inequality. He proves the following remarkable generalisation of Rademacher’s Theorem in this setting.

Theorem 2.9
.

Suppose
(X,d,\mu)
is a doubling metric measure space which supports a weak
(1,p)
-Poincaré inequality. There exists a countable collection of Borel sets
U_{i}
and Lipschitz functions
\varphi_{i}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
N
depends only on the doubling and PI constants, satisfying the following. First,

(2.19)

\displaystyle\mu\left(X\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}U_{i}\right)=0.

Second, for each Lipschitz
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
and almost every
x_{0}\in U_{i}
, there exists a unique
Df(x_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
(the derivative of
f
at
x_{0}
) such that

(2.20)

\displaystyle\limsup_{X\ni x\to x_{0}}\frac{|f(x)-f(x_{0})-Df(x_{0})\cdot(\varphi_{i}(x)-\varphi_{i}(x_{0}))|}{d(x,x_{0})}=0.

The operator
D
can be extended to all functions in the Newtonian-Sobolev class
N^{1,p}(X)
(see
[
Che99
, Theorem 4.47]
). This extension is linear, satisfies Leibniz rule, and for each
u\in N^{1,p}(X),
one has

(2.21)

\displaystyle|Du|\sim g_{u}\quad\mu\mbox{-a.e.,}

where
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 2.10
.

An operator
D\colon N^{1,2}(X)\to H
, for some finite-dimensional Hilbert space
H
, is said to be a
Cheeger derivative
if it is linear, satisfies the Leibniz rule and, for each
u\in N^{1,2}(X),

(2.22)

\displaystyle\|Du\|\sim g_{u}\quad\mu\mbox{-a.e.,}

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the norm on
H
induced by the inner product.

In general, Cheeger derivatives are not uniquely determined. However, on certain spaces (e.g., Euclidean and
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
spaces), there are canonical choices of such operators. Below we define the class of Cheeger harmonic functions. Keep in mind that the collection of such functions depends on the choice of Cheeger derivative. Let us fix for the remainder of the section a Cheeger derivative
D\colon N^{1,2}(X)\to H
. We will let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote the inner product on
H
and
\|\cdot\|
denote the norm induced by the inner product.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
U\subseteq X
be open. A function
u\in N_{\rm loc}^{1,2}(U)
is said to be
Cheeger harmonic
in
U
if

(2.23)

\displaystyle\int_{U}\|Du\|^{2}\,d\mu\leq\int_{U}\|D(u+\phi)\|^{2}\,d\mu\quad\mbox{ for all }\phi\in N^{1,2}_{0}(U).

Equivalently, if

(2.24)

\displaystyle\int_{U}\langle Du,D\phi\rangle\,d\mu=0\quad\mbox{ for all }\phi\in N^{1,2}_{0}(U).

Definition 2.12
.

Let
U\subseteq X
be open and
f\in N^{1,2}(U)
. A function
u\in N^{1,2}(U)
is said to be a
solution to the Dirichlet problem for
f
in the weak sense of traces
, if
u
is Cheeger harmonic in
U
and
u=f+\phi
for some
\phi\in N^{1,2}_{0}(U).

The existence of such solutions to the Dirichlet problem is a consequence of the following.

Theorem 2.13

(
[
Che99
, Theorem 7.12]
)
.

Suppose
(X,d,\mu)
is a doubling metric measure space which supports a weak
(1,2)
-Poincaré inequality. For each open
U\subset X
and
f\in N^{1,2}(U)
, there exists a solution to the Dirichlet problem for
f
in the weak sense of traces.

Cheeger harmonic functions on metric spaces satisfy a Caccioppoli inequality. The Euclidean proof (see, for example,
[
GM12
, Theorem 4.1]
) works in this setting, but we include it for completeness.

Lemma 2.14
.

Suppose
(X,d,\mu)
is a doubling metric measure space which supports a
(1,2)
-Poincaré inequality,
U\subseteq X
is open, and
u\in N^{1,2}_{\rm loc}(U)
is Cheeger harmonic in
U
. Then, for every ball
B
such that
2B\subseteq U
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

(2.25)

\displaystyle\int_{B}\|Du\|^{2}\,d\mu\lesssim_{C_{\mu},C_{\mathrm{PI}}}\frac{1}{r_{B}^{2}}\int_{2B}|u-\lambda|^{2}\,d\mu.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume
r_{B}=1.
Let
\eta\colon X\to[0,1]
be a Lipschitz function such that
\eta\equiv 1
on
B
,
\eta\equiv 0
on
X\setminus 2B
, and
|D\eta|\lesssim 1.
Such a function is easy to construct by the McShane-Whitney extension theorem (the absolute value of the derivative of a Lipschitz function is bounded by the Lipschitz constant). Setting
\phi=(u-\lambda)\eta^{2}
in (
2.24
) and applying the Leibniz rule gives

(2.26)

\displaystyle\int_{U}\|Du\|^{2}\eta^{2}\,d\mu+\int_{U}2(u-\lambda)\eta\langle Du,D\eta\rangle\,d\mu=0.

Using the fact that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{supp}(\eta)\subseteq 2B\subseteq U
and applying Hölder’s inequality, we get

(2.27)

\displaystyle\int_{2B}\|Du\|^{2}\eta^{2}\,d\mu

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{2B}|u-\lambda|\eta\|Du\|\|D\eta\|\,d\mu

(2.28)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{2B}\|Du\|^{2}\eta^{2}\,d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{2B}\|D\eta\|^{2}|u-\lambda|^{2}\,d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Thus, since
\eta\equiv 1
on
B
,

(2.29)

\displaystyle\int_{B}\|Du\|^{2}\,d\mu\leq\int_{2B}\|Du\|^{2}\eta^{2}\,d\mu\lesssim\int_{2B}\|D\eta\|^{2}|u-\lambda|^{2}\,d\mu\lesssim\int_{2B}|u-\lambda|^{2}\,d\mu,

as required.
∎

RCD spaces

In this section, we cover the main properties of RCD spaces needed in the proofs of Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
. As mentioned in the introduction, we direct the reader to the surveys
[
Amb18
,
Gig23
]
and references therein for a thorough introduction to the theory (of which we barely scratch the surface).

The main feature of RCD spaces that enables us to prove Theorem
A
is the existence of a Hessian (acting on sufficiently smooth functions), developed in
[
Gig18
]
. We utilize the fact that, when scaled correctly, the local
L^{2}
-norm of
|\mathrm{Hess}(f)|
is comparable to the local
L^{2}
-oscillation of
|\nabla f|,
see Corollary
2.21
and Lemma
2.22
. Before getting to this, we give a brief overview of the gradient (
\nabla
), Laplacian (
\Delta
) and Hessian (
\mathrm{Hess}
) on RCD spaces, and their relevant properties. Since we will not work directly with the definition, we will skip some of the details. See
[
Gig18
]
for the full details.

Sobolev spaces

We are interested in how the aforementioned objects act on Sobolev functions. The Sobolev space utilised in
[
Gig18
]
, denoted there by
W^{1,2}(X)
, is the one developed in
[
AGS14
]
and is stated in terms of
test plans
. Similarly to the Newtonian spaces, associated to each
f\in W^{1,2}(X)
is a
minimal weak upper gradient
denoted by
|Df|_{w}
. It turns out that the space
W^{1,2}(X)
coincides with the Newtonian space in the following sense (see
[
EBS24
]
):

(1)

If
f\in N^{1,2}(X),
then
f\in W^{1,2}(X)
and
g_{f}=|Df|_{w}
almost everywhere.

(2)

If
f\in W^{1,2}(X)
, then
f
has a Borel representative
\overline{f}\in N^{1,2}(X)
with
g_{\overline{f}}=|Df|_{w}
almost everywhere.

Thus, when quoting the various necessary results from the RCD literature, we will equivalently state things in terms of
N^{1,2}(X)
.

Gradient operator

A defining feature of RCD spaces is the assumption that
N^{1,2}(X)
is a Hilbert space. This condition, referred to in
[
Gig18
]
as
infinitesimally Hilbertian
, was introduced in
[
Gig15
]
. Assuming this, there is a unique pair
(L^{0}(TX),\nabla)
, where
L^{0}(TX)
is an
L^{0}
-normed module (see
[
Gig18
]
) and
\nabla\colon N^{1,2}(X)\to L^{0}(TX)
, called the
gradient operator
, is a linear and continuous map such that

(2.30)

\displaystyle|\nabla f|=g_{f}\quad\mbox{almost everywhere}.

The gradient operator satisfies the Leibniz rule, chain rule, and the locality property, meaning,

(2.31)

\displaystyle\nabla f=\nabla g\mbox{ almost everywhere in }\{f=g\}.

Furthermore, the assumption of infinitesimally Hilbertian implies that
L^{0}(TX)
is a
Hilbert module
and can be equipped with a canonical inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle.
In particular, the gradient operator
\nabla
defines a Cheeger derivative in the sense of Definition
2.10
.

Laplace operator and harmonic functions

Let
U\subseteq X
be open and let
D(\Delta,U)
denote the space of all functions
f\in N^{1,2}_{\rm loc}(U)
for which there exists
h\in L^{2}(U)
satisfying

(2.32)

\displaystyle\int hg\,d\mu=-\int\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle\,d\mu\quad\mbox{for all $g\in N^{1,2}_{0}(U).$}

The function
h
is called the
Laplacian of
f
and is denoted by
\Delta f.
We will set
D(\Delta)\coloneqq D(\Delta,X)
. An element
f\in D(\Delta,U)
is said to be
harmonic in
U
if
\Delta f=0
. Note that this is equivalent to saying that
f
is Cheeger harmonic in
U
(Definition
2.11
) with respect to the differential
\nabla
.

Remark 2.15
.

Since
|\nabla u|=g_{u}

\mu
-a.e. (see
[
AGS13
]
), being harmonic in the above sense is the same as being harmonic in the sense considered in the book
[
BB11
]
.
In the case that
X=\mathbb{R}^{n},
it follows from (
2.30
) that there exists a linear isometry
T
from
L^{0}(TX)
to the space of vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\nabla_{\text{Euc}}f=T(\nabla f)
for each
f\in N^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, see
[
Gig18
, Remark 2.2.4]
. In particular, the space of harmonic functions described above coincides with the usual space of harmonic functions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The global Hessian

The notion of Hessian in RCD spaces was developed in
[
Gig18
]
. The definition is rather involved and, as such, we refer to the aforementioned paper for the full details. We provide a summary below.

The space of test functions on
X
is

(2.33)

\displaystyle\mbox{Test}(X)\coloneqq\{f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)\cap\mbox{LIP}(X)\cap D(\Delta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Delta f\in N^{1,2}(X)\}.

For each
f\in\mbox{Test}(X),
there is a mapping
\mathrm{Hess}(f)\colon[L^{0}(TX)]^{2}\to L^{0}(\mu)
which is bilinear, symmetric and continuous. This induces a continuous linear map
\mathrm{Hess}(f)\colon L^{0}(TX)\to L^{0}(TX)
via the formula

(2.34)

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{Hess}(f)(v),w\rangle=\mathrm{Hess}(f)(v,w),\quad w\in L^{0}(TX).

It follows from
[
Gig18
, Equation 3.3.34]
that

(2.35)

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Hess}(f)(\nabla f)=\nabla|\nabla f|^{2}=2|\nabla f|\nabla|\nabla f|.

Furthermore (see
[
Gig18
, Section 3.2]
), there is a function
|\cdot|_{\mathrm{HS}}\colon L^{0}(TX)\to L^{0}(\mu)
with the property that, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
,

(2.36)

\displaystyle|\cdot|_{\mathrm{HS}}(x)\mbox{ defines a norm on }L^{0}(TX).

The function
|\cdot|_{\mathrm{HS}}
is called the
Hilbert-Schmidt norm
. It satisfies

(2.37)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Hess}(f)(v)|\leq|\mathrm{Hess}(f)|_{\mathrm{HS}}|v|,\quad v\in L^{0}(TX)

Finally, the mapping

(2.38)

\displaystyle f\mapsto\mathrm{Hess}(f)\mbox{ is linear}.

The local Hessian

In general, we would like access to a Hessian for functions which are
locally in
\mathrm{Test}(X)
. One can make this precise as follows. For an open set
\Omega\subseteq X
, let

(2.39)

\displaystyle\text{Test}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)=\{f\in\text{LIP}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)\cap D(\Delta,\Omega)\colon\Delta f\in N^{1,2}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)\}.

If
\eta\in\text{Test}(X)
with
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{supp}(\eta)\subset\subset\Omega,
and
u\in\text{Test}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
then
\eta u\in\text{Test}(X)
. Thus, for any
f\in\text{Test}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
we can define a function
|\mathrm{Hess}(f)|_{\mathrm{HS}}\in L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
as

(2.40)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Hess}(f)|_{\mathrm{HS}}\coloneqq|\mathrm{Hess}(\eta f)|_{\mathrm{HS}},\ \mu\mbox{-a.e. in }\Omega^{\prime}

for every
\eta\in\text{Test}(X)
with compact support in
\Omega
such that
\eta=1
on
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega.
This is well-defined due to the locality property of the Hessian.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq X
. For each
f,g\in\mathrm{Test}_{\rm{loc}}(\Omega)
we have

(2.41)

\displaystyle\left||\mathrm{Hess}(f)|_{\mathrm{HS}}-|\mathrm{Hess}(g)|_{\mathrm{HS}}\right|\leq|\mathrm{Hess}(f-g)|_{\mathrm{HS}}\quad\mu\mbox{-a.e. in }\Omega.

Proof.

Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
and fix some
\eta\in\mathrm{Test}(X)
with compact support in
\Omega
which satisfies
\eta=1
on
\Omega^{\prime}
. It follows from (
2.36
), (
2.40
), the reverse triangle inequality and the linearity of the map
h\mapsto\mathrm{Hess}(h)
that

(2.42)

\displaystyle\left||\mathrm{Hess}(f)|_{\mathrm{HS}}-|\mathrm{Hess}(g)|_{\mathrm{HS}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left||\mathrm{Hess}(\eta f)|_{\mathrm{HS}}-|\mathrm{Hess}(\eta g)|_{\mathrm{HS}}\right|

(2.43)

\displaystyle\leq|\mathrm{Hess}(\eta(f-g))|_{\mathrm{HS}}=|\mathrm{Hess}(f-g)|_{\mathrm{HS}}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega^{\prime}
. Since
\Omega^{\prime}
was arbitrary, this completes the proof.
∎

Main results

We now have everything needed to state the main results of this section. Let us fix a metric measure space
(X,d,\mu)
which is
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
for some
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in(1,\infty)
.

Remark 2.17
.

In general, the dependencies in the following results are going to vary depending on whether
K\geq 0
or
K<0
. To encode this dichotomy more efficiently, let us define

(2.44)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}(X)=\begin{cases}N&\mbox{ if }K\geq 0;\\
(K,N,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X))&\mbox{ if }K<0.\end{cases}

It follows from the Bishop–Gromov
inequality in
{\rm CD}(K,N)

[
Stu06b
, Theorem 2.3]
that

(2.45)

(X,d,\mu)
is doubling with constant depending only on
\mathfrak{c}(X)
.

It also supports a
(1,2)
-Poincaré inequality.

Theorem 2.18

(
[
Raj12a
,
Raj12b
]
)
.

The space
(X,d,\mu)
supports a weak
(1,2)
-Poincaré inequality with Poincaré constant
C_{\mathrm{PI}}
depending only
\mathfrak{c}(X)
and dilatation constant given by
A_{\mathrm{PI}}=2
.

The next result guarantees the existence of bump functions with bounds on the gradient and Laplacian.

Proposition 2.19

(
[
MN19
, Lemma 3.1]
)
.

For every
x\in X
,
R>0
and
0<r<R
, there exists a Lipschitz function
\psi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(1)

0\leq\psi\leq 1
on
X
,
\psi\equiv 1
on
B(x,r)
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{supp}(\psi)\subset B(x,2r)
;

(2)

r^{2}|\Delta\psi|+r|\nabla\psi|\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}1.

The space also supports a Bochner-type inequality, from which we will derive a Caccioppoli-type estimate for the Hessian of a harmonic function (Corollary
2.21
).

Theorem 2.20

(
[
Gig18
, Theorem 3.3.8]
)
.

Given
u\in\mathrm{Test}(X)
, it holds that

(2.46)

\displaystyle\int_{X}-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla|\nabla u|^{2},\nabla\phi\rangle\,d\mu\geq\int_{X}\left(|\mathrm{Hess}(u)|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}+\langle\nabla u,\nabla\Delta u\rangle+K|\nabla u|^{2}\right)\phi\,d\mu

for all
\phi\in L^{\infty}(X)\cap N^{1,2}(X).

Corollary 2.21
.

Let
\Omega\subset X
be open. For any
h\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
which is harmonic in
\Omega
and any ball
B
such that
5B\subseteq\Omega
one has

(2.47)

\displaystyle\int_{B}|r_{B}\mathrm{Hess}(h)|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\,d\mu\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}\int_{2B}|\nabla h|^{2}\,d\mu.

Proof.

Fix a function
h
and a ball
B
as above. By definition,
h\in D(\Delta,\Omega)
, which implies that
h\in\mbox{LIP}(4B)
by
[
Jia14
, Theorem 1.1]
. By Proposition
2.19
, there exists a cut-off function
\theta\in\mathrm{Test}(X)
such that
\theta\equiv 1
on
3B
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{supp}(\theta)\subseteq 4B.
It is easy to check that
u\coloneqq\theta h\in\mathrm{Test}(X)
and
u\equiv h
on
3B
. Applying Proposition
2.19
again, there exists a cut-off function
\phi
such that
\phi\equiv 1
on
B
,
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{supp}(\phi)\subseteq 2B
and
r_{B}^{2}|\Delta\phi|\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}1.
Note that
\langle\nabla u,\nabla\Delta u\rangle\phi\equiv 0
on
X
since
h
(and hence
u
) is Cheeger harmonic on
2B
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{supp}(\phi)\subset 2B.
Applying Theorem
2.20
and the integration by parts formula (
2.32
) for
\phi
, we now have

(2.48)

\displaystyle\int_{B}|\mathrm{Hess}(h)|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\,d\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{B}|\mathrm{Hess}(u)|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\,d\mu\leq\int_{X}|\mathrm{Hess}(u)|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\phi\,d\mu

(2.49)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{X}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\Delta\phi-K|\nabla u|^{2}\phi\,d\mu

(2.50)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}r_{B}^{-2}\int_{2B}|\nabla u|^{2}\,d\mu=r_{B}^{-2}\int_{2B}|\nabla h|^{2}\,d\mu,

which completes the proof.
∎

We finish by checking a weak Poincaré inequality for
|\nabla f|
in terms of
\mathrm{Hess}(f)
.

Lemma 2.22
.

For each ball
B
in
X
and each
f\in\mathrm{Test}(X)
we have

(2.51)

\displaystyle\int_{B}||\nabla f|-\langle|\nabla f|\rangle_{B}|^{2}\,d\mu\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}\int_{4B}r_{B}^{2}|\mathrm{Hess}(f)|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\,d\mu.

Proof.

It follows from Theorem
2.6
and Theorem
2.18
that
(X,d,\mu)
supports a weak
(2,2)
-Poincaré inequality with constant
C_{\mathrm{PI}}^{\prime}
depending only on
\mathfrak{c}(X)
and dilation constant equal to
4
. This and (
2.30
) now imply

(2.52)

\displaystyle\int_{B}||\nabla f|-\langle|\nabla f|\rangle_{B}|^{2}\,d\mu\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}\int_{4B}|\nabla|\nabla f||^{2}\,d\mu.

By locality,
|\nabla|\nabla f||=0
almost everywhere in
\{|\nabla f|=0\}
. By equation (
2.37
) and (
2.35
),
|\nabla|\nabla f||\leq|\mathrm{Hess}(f)|_{\mathrm{HS}}
almost everywhere in
\{|\nabla f|>0\}
. In particular, following on from the previous inequality, we have

(2.53)

\displaystyle\int_{B}||\nabla f|-\langle|\nabla f|\rangle_{B}|^{2}\,d\mu\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}\int_{4B}|\mathrm{Hess}(f)|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}\,d\mu,

as required.
∎

3.
Properties of the
H
-coefficients

Here, we define and study the quantities
H_{f,D}
and
H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}
(recall the definition of
H_{f}^{\mathrm{osc}}
from the introduction). The first quantity gives a way to measure the local distance to the class of Cheeger harmonic functions, the second quantity gives a way to measure the local distance to the class of harmonic functions with controlled Hessian.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a doubling metric measure space which supports a weak
(1,2)
-Poincaré inequality, and let
D
be a Cheeger derivative on
X
. For each
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R},

x\in X
and
0<r<\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X),
define

(3.1)

\displaystyle H_{f,D}(x,r)^{2}\coloneqq\inf_{h}H_{f,D}(x,r;h)^{2}\coloneqq\inf_{h}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}\left(\frac{f-h}{r}\right)^{2}\,d\mu,

where the infimum is taken over all function
h\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
which are Cheeger harmonic (with respect to
D
) in
B(x,r)
. If
X
is an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space, we will write
H_{f}\coloneqq H_{\nabla,f}.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space. For each
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R},

x\in X
and
0<r<\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X),
define

(3.2)

\displaystyle H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}(x,r)^{2}\coloneqq\inf_{h}H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}(x,r;h)=\inf_{h}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}\left(\frac{f-h}{r}\right)^{2}+r^{2}|\mathrm{Hess}(h)|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\,d\mu,

where the infimum is taken over all function
h\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
which are harmonic in
B(x,r)
.

Remark 3.3
.

In the case that
B=B(x,r)
we will on occasion write
H_{f,D}(B)
and
H_{f,D}(B;h)
to denote the quantities
H_{f,D}(x,r)
and
H_{f,D}(x,r;h)
. We will adopt a similar convention with the coefficients
H_{f}
and
H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}
.

If
X
is an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space for some
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in(1,\infty)
(and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)<\infty
in the case that
K<0
), it is immediate from Lemma
2.22
that

(3.3)

\displaystyle H_{f}(x,r)\leq H_{f}^{\mathrm{osc}}(x,r)\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}(x,4r)

for each
x\in X
and
0<r<\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X).
The following tells us that, in
\mathbb{R}^{n},
the usual Dorronsoro coefficient
\Omega_{f}
is comparable to
H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

0<r<\infty
and
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\Omega_{f}(x,r)\leq H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}(x,r)\lesssim\Omega_{f}(x,r).

Proof.

The fact that
H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}\leq\Omega
is immediate from the definitions since any affine function
A
is harmonic and
|\mathrm{Hess}(A)|_{\mathrm{HS}}=0
. Here,
|\cdot|_{\mathrm{HS}}
is the usual Hilbert-Schmidt norm of a matrix. For the second inequality, fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
0<r<\infty
and
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}.
Let
\varepsilon>0
and
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be harmonic in
B(x,r)
such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}\left(\frac{f-h}{2r}\right)^{2}+|r\mathrm{Hess}(h)|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}\,d\mu\leq H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}(x,r)+\varepsilon.

Define an affine function
A\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(3.6)

\displaystyle A(y)=h(x)+\langle\nabla h\rangle_{B(x,r)}\cdot(y-x).

Then,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\Omega_{f}(x,r)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}\left(\frac{f-A}{2r}\right)^{2}\,dy

(3.8)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}\left(\frac{f-h}{2r}\right)^{2}\,dy+\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}\left(\frac{h-A}{2r}\right)^{2}\,dy,

where in the second inequality we used the fact that for
a,b\geq 0
we have
(a+b)^{2}\leq 4(a^{2}+b^{2}).
Since
A
and
h
are both harmonic in
B(x,r)
, the mean value property implies
\langle h-A\rangle_{B(x,r)}=h(x)-A(x)=0.
Thus, after applying the usual Poincaré inequality (recalling that
\nabla A=\langle\nabla h\rangle_{B(x,r)})
we have

(3.9)

\displaystyle\Omega_{f}(x,r)^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}\left(\frac{f-h}{2r}\right)^{2}\,dy+\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}|\nabla h-\langle\nabla h\rangle_{B(x,r)}|^{2}\,dy.

Applying the Poincaré inequality again, we get

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}|\nabla h-\langle\nabla h\rangle_{B(x,r)}|^{2}\,dy

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}|\partial_{x_{i}}h-\langle\partial_{x_{i}}h\rangle_{B(x,r)}|^{2}\,dy

(3.11)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}|r\nabla\partial_{x_{i}}h|^{2}\,dy=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}|r\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}h|^{2}\,dy

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}|r\mathrm{Hess}(h)|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}\,dy.

This, together with (
3.5
) and (
3.9
), implies
\Omega_{f}(x,r)\lesssim H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}(x,r)+\varepsilon.
Since
\varepsilon
was arbitrary, this completes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}(B)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{C_{\mu}^{k}r_{B^{\prime}}}{r_{B}}\right)^{2}\left(H_{f}^{\mathrm{RCD}}(B^{\prime})^{2}+\varepsilon\right).

4.

H
-coefficients

In this section, we prove Theorem
C
. We also show how Theorem

implies the
only if

B
. Throughout,
X=(X,d,\mu)
denotes a fixed doubling metric measure space supporting a weak
(1,2)
-Poincaré inequality, and
D
denotes a Cheeger derivative on

f\in N^{1,2}(X).
For brevity, we will simply denote

(4.1)

\displaystyle H\coloneqq H_{f,D}.

It follows directly from the weak
(2,2)
-Poincaré inequality (see Theorem
2.18
and Theorem
2.6
) that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{f-f_{B(z,R)}}{R}\right\|_{L^{2}(B(z,R))}^{2}\lesssim\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(B(z,A_{\mathrm{PI}}R)))}^{2}

for all

\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle AB_{Q}\subseteq B(z,10A\rho^{k}+R)\subseteq 20A\rho^{-1}B(z,R).

.

Let
\mathcal{D}

(4.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}(Q_{0})}H(3B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)\lesssim_{C_{\mu},C_{\mathrm{PI}},A_{\mathrm{PI}}}\|Df\|_{L^{2}(3A_{\mathrm{PI}}B_{Q_{0}})}^{2}.

We start by showing the following

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a system of Christ-David cubes on
X
. For each
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{D}

(4.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}(Q_{0})}H(Q)^{2}\mu(Q)\lesssim_{C_{\mu},C_{\mathrm{PI}}}\|Df\|_{L^{2}(Q_{0})}^{2}.

Remark 4.3
.

The main difference between Proposition

and Lemma
4.2

H_{f}(Q)

At the end of the section, we will show how to obtain Proposition

Fix a system of Christ-David cubes
\mathcal{D}
on

Q_{0}\in\mathcal{D}.
By scaling we may assume

(so that
\ell(Q_{0})=5
). For

let

(4.6)

\displaystyle h^{Q}=f+\phi^{Q}\in N^{1,2}(Q)

, where

(recall from Theorem
2.13

. Thus, our main focus will be on proving the following.

Lemma 4.4
.

We have

(4.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}(Q_{0})}H(Q;h^{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)\lesssim\|Df\|_{L^{2}(Q_{0})}^{2}.

We begin the proof of Lemma
4.4
in earnest. Let
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
denote the set of non-negative integers. Define, for each integer
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},
a function
f_{k}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
by

(4.8)

\displaystyle f_{k}=f+\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}(Q_{0})}\phi^{Q}.

A basic property of minimal weak upper gradients is that
g_{u+v}\leq g_{u}+g_{v}
and
g_{-u}=g_{u}
(see
[

.

For each integer
M\geq 1
we have

(5.27)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{M}\|\varrho_{k}g_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{0})}\lesssim_{C_{\mu},C_{\mathrm{PI}}}\|\nabla f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(3A_{\mathrm{PI}}B_{Q_{0}})}.

Proof.

First, by Lemma
5.8
,

(5.28)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{M}\|\varrho_{k}g_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{0})}&\lesssim\sum_{k=k^{*}-1}^{M-1}\|\varrho_{k}(g_{k+1}-g_{k})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{0})}\\
&=\sum_{k=k^{*}-1}^{M-1}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k+1}(Q_{0})}\|\varrho_{k}(g_{k+1}-g_{k})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q)}.\end{split}

Fix some
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k+1}
and let
Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
such that
Q\subseteq Q^{\prime}.
Suppose to begin with that
k\geq k^{*}
. In this case, recall from (
5.8
) and (
5.9
) that
g_{k+1}\mathds{1}_{Q}=|\mathrm{Hess}(l_{Q})|_{\mathrm{HS}}\mathds{1}_{Q}
, where
l_{Q}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is harmonic on
R_{Q}\supseteq 10B_{Q}.
Similarly,
g_{k}\mathds{1}_{Q^{\prime}}=|\mathrm{Hess}(l_{Q^{\prime}})|_{\mathrm{HS}}\mathds{1}_{Q^{\prime}}
for some function
l_{Q^{\prime}}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
which is harmonic on
R_{Q^{\prime}}\supseteq 10B_{Q^{\prime}}\supseteq 10B_{Q}
. By applying, Lemma
2.14
, Lemma
2.16
, Corollary
2.21
and (
5.8
), we have

(5.29)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\varrho_{k}(g_{k+1}-g_{k})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q)}&=\|\varrho_{k}|\mathrm{Hess}(l_{Q}-l_{Q^{\prime}})|_{\mathrm{HS}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q)}\\
&\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X),C_{\mu},C_{\mathrm{PI}}}\left\|\frac{l_{Q}-l_{Q^{\prime}}}{\varrho_{k}}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(4B_{Q})}\\
&\lesssim\left\|\frac{f-l_{Q}}{\varrho_{k+1}}\right\|_{L^{2}(4B_{Q})}^{2}+\left\|\frac{f-l_{Q^{\prime}}}{\varrho_{k}}\right\|_{L^{2}(4B_{Q})}^{2}\\
&\lesssim H_{f}(R_{Q})^{2}\mu(R_{Q})+H_{f}(R_{Q^{\prime}})^{2}\mu(R_{Q^{\prime}}).\end{split}

If
k=k^{*}-1
, a similar argument implies

(5.30)

\displaystyle\|\varrho_{0}(g_{k^{*}}-g_{k^{*}-1})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q)}\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X),C_{\mu},C_{\mathrm{PI}}}H_{f}(R_{Q})^{2}\mu(R_{Q})+\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(3A_{\mathrm{PI}}B_{Q_{0}})}^{2}.

Indeed, the only modification one needs to make is the final estimate in (
5.29
). Recall that for
Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{k^{*}-1}
, we have
l_{Q^{\prime}}\equiv\langle f\rangle_{3B_{Q^{\prime}}}
. It then follows from the Poincaré inequality and the fact that
4B_{Q}\subseteq 3B_{Q^{\prime}}
that

(5.31)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{f-l_{Q^{\prime}}}{\varrho_{k}}\right\|_{L^{2}(4B_{Q})}^{2}\leq\left\|\frac{f-l_{Q^{\prime}}}{\varrho_{k}}\right\|_{L^{2}(3B_{Q^{\prime}})}^{2}\lesssim_{C_{\mathrm{PI}}}\|\nabla f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(3A_{\mathrm{PI}}B_{Q_{0}})}.

Returning to (
5.28
), by applying (
5.29
), (
5.30
), and Lemma
5.5
, we now have

(5.32)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{M}\|\varrho_{k}g_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{0})}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(3A_{\mathrm{PI}}B_{Q_{0}})}^{2}+\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{M}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}(Q_{0})}H_{f}(R_{Q})^{2}\mu(R_{Q})

(5.33)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}\|\nabla f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(3A_{\mathrm{PI}}B_{Q_{0}})},

which finishes the proof.
∎

6.
Estimating the gradient

In this section, we prove the remaining implication of Theorem
B
. Namely, the norm of the gradient can be controlled by the square-sum of the
H_{f}
coefficients. Recall, this also implies the remaining implication in Theorem
A
. Fix a space
(X,d,\mu)
that is
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space for some
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in(1,\infty)
, and assume that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)<\infty
in the case that
K<0
. Fix also some
f\in L^{2}(X)
for the remainder of the section.

Again, we would like to discretise via Lemma
3.6
. Suppose for the moment that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)<\infty
, and let
k^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}
be the smallest integer such that
30\rho^{k^{*}}\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)/2
. For use later, we note that minimality implies

(6.1)

\displaystyle(\rho/6)\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)<5\rho^{k^{*}}.

With this choice of
k^{*}
, if
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k^{*}}
, then
6B_{Q}
is contained in a ball of radius
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)/2
. Moreover, there are at most finitely many (depending on
C_{\mu}
) cubes in
\mathcal{D}_{k}
, which translates to the fact that there are only finitely many corresponding balls. Thus, by Lemma
3.6
(2),

(6.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{\infty}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}H_{f}(6B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)\lesssim_{C_{\mu}}\int_{0}^{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)}\int_{X}H_{f}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{d\mu dr}{r}.

If
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)=\infty
, the same estimate follows immediately from Lemma
3.6
(2) with
k^{*}=-\infty
. Hence, it suffices to prove the following.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a system of Christ-David cubes on
X
and let
k^{*}
be as above. If

(6.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{\infty}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}H_{f}(6B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)<\infty,

then
f\in N^{1,2}(X).
In this case,

(6.4)

\displaystyle\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\leq C_{1}\left\|\frac{f-\langle f\rangle_{X}}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(X)}+C_{2}\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{\infty}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}H_{f}(6B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)

for some absolute constant
C_{1}
and some constant
C_{2}
depending only on
\mathfrak{c}(X)
, as in (
2.44
).

To prove Proposition
6.1
, we will make use of various properties of the heat semigroup. Consider the operator
-\Delta
which is a non-negative definite self-adjoint operator on
X
. The
heat semigroup
associated to
-\Delta
is the family
\{H_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
of operators from
L^{2}(X)
into itself given by
H_{t}\coloneqq e^{\Delta t}
. It satisfies the following list of properties (see, for example,
[
FOT11
]
).

(H1)

For each
s,t>0
,

(6.5)

\displaystyle H_{s+t}=H_{s}H_{t}.

(H2)

For each
g\in L^{2}(X)
,

(6.6)

\displaystyle\|H_{t}g-g\|_{L^{2}(X)}\to 0\mbox{ as }t\to 0.

(H3)

For each
t>0
, if
\|\cdot\|_{2\to 2}
denotes the operator norm from
L^{2}
to
L^{2}
, then

(6.7)

\displaystyle\|H_{t}\|_{2\to 2}\leq 1\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\|\nabla H_{t}\|_{2\to 2}\leq ct^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(H4)

For each
t>0
and
g\in L^{2}(X)
,

(6.8)

\displaystyle H_{t}g\in D(\Delta)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\partial_{t}H_{t}g=\Delta H_{t}g.

(H5)

For any continuous
\phi\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
,
\phi(-\Delta)
is non-negative definite self-adjoint operator which commutes with
H_{t}
,
t>0
.

It will be useful to note that, by (
2.32
) and the fact that
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}
is non-negative self-adjoint, we have

(6.9)

\displaystyle\|\nabla g\|^{2}=\langle-\Delta g,g\rangle=\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}g\|^{2}

for all
g\in D(\Delta)
.

Finally, we will need the following lemma, which is essentially contained in
[
HN19
]
(see Equations (55)–(58) there). We include the short proof for completeness.

Lemma 6.2
.

For every
g\in L^{2}(X)
we have

(6.10)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\sqrt{t}\nabla H_{t}g\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\sqrt{t}\nabla H_{t}(H_{3t}-H_{t})g\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}.

Proof.

For brevity, let us write
\|\cdot\|\coloneqq\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(X)}
. By (
H3
) and (
6.9
), we know that
\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}H_{t}g\|\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. Thus, we can write

(6.11)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}H_{t}g

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(H_{2^{k+1}t}g-H_{2^{k+2}t}g\right)

(6.12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}H_{2^{k}t}\left(H_{2^{k}t}-H_{3\cdot 2^{k}t}\right)g.

Applying this, equation (
6.9
), the triangle inequality (in
L^{2}((0,\infty),dt/t;L^{2}(X,\mathbb{R}))
), and making a change of variables
s=2^{\frac{k}{2}}
, we have

(6.13)

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\sqrt{t}\nabla H_{t}g\|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\sqrt{t}\nabla H_{2^{k}t}\left(H_{3\cdot 2^{k}t}-H_{2^{k}t}\right)g\|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(6.14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}2^{-\frac{k}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\sqrt{t}\nabla H_{t}\left(H_{3t}-H_{t}\right)g\|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(6.15)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\sqrt{t}\nabla H_{t}\left(H_{3t}-H_{t}\right)g\|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

as required.
∎

With these properties established, let us return to the proof of Proposition
6.1
. First, (
H3
) implies that
H_{s}f\in N^{1,2}(X)
for all
s>0
. Then, since the mapping
g\mapsto\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}(X)}
is lower semicontinuous with respect to convergence in
L^{2}
(see
[
Che99
, Theorem 2.5]
), and
H_{s}f\to f
in
L^{2}
as
s\to 0
(recall (
H2
)), it suffices to show the following.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a system of Christ-David cubes on
X
and let
k^{*}
be as above. We have

(6.16)

\displaystyle\|\nabla H_{s}f\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\leq C_{1}\left\|\frac{f-\langle f\rangle_{X}}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(X)}+C_{2}\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{\infty}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}H_{f}(6B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)

for some absolute constant
C_{1}
and some constant
C_{2}
depending only on
\mathfrak{c}(X)
i.e., both are independent of
s
.

The proof of Lemma
6.3
will be carried out below. Let us fix a system of Christ-David cubes
\mathcal{D}
and let us fix some
s>0
. We start by showing the following.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\alpha\coloneqq(\rho/6)
. We have

(6.17)

\displaystyle\|\nabla H_{s}f\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lesssim\left\|\frac{f-\langle f\rangle_{X}}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(X)}+\int_{0}^{\alpha\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)}\left\|\frac{H_{2t_{2}}f-f}{t}\right\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}.

Proof.

For brevity, let us denote
f_{s}\coloneqq H_{s}f
. Let
\{E_{\lambda}\colon\lambda>0\}
be the unique spectral family of projection operators on
L^{2}(X)
associated to
-\Delta
, and consider the spectral representation
-\Delta=\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda\,dE_{\lambda}.
Since
f_{s}\in D(\Delta)
, we have by (
6.9
), standard identities in spectral theory, and the identity
\lambda=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{2}e^{-2\lambda t}\,dt
, that

(6.18)

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{s}\|^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda\,d\langle E_{\lambda}f_{s},f_{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{2}e^{-2\lambda t}\,d\langle E_{\lambda}f_{s},f_{s}\rangle dt

(6.19)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\nabla H_{t}g_{s}\|^{2}\,dt,

where
g_{s}\coloneqq(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}f_{s}
and
\int G(x)dF(x)
is the Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral. Multiplying and dividing by
t
, and applying Lemma
6.2
and (
6.9
), we get

(6.20)

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{s}\|^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\sqrt{t}\nabla H_{t}g_{s}\|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\sqrt{t}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}H_{t}\left(H_{3t}-H_{t}\right)g_{s}\|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}.

Applying the first estimate in (
H3
) (on three separate occasions), commuting
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}
with
H_{t},
applying (
6.9
) and making a change of variable
t=s^{2},
this gives

(6.21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\nabla f_{s}\|^{2}&\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\|H_{3t}g_{s}-H_{t}g_{s}\|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\nabla H_{t}(H_{2t}f_{s}-f_{s})\|^{2}\frac{dt}{t}\\
&\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\|\frac{H_{2t^{2}}f_{s}-f_{s}}{t}\right\|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\|\frac{H_{2t^{2}}f-f}{t}\right\|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}.\end{split}

If
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)=\infty
, then the above inequality is exactly (
6.17
) and we are done. Suppose instead that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)<\infty
. By doubling, we know
\mu(X)<\infty
. Thus,
\langle f\rangle_{X}\in L^{2}(X)
. Since constant functions are invariant under
H_{t}
for each
t>0
, we can estimate using (
H3
) to get

(6.22)

\displaystyle\|H_{2t^{2}}f-f\|\leq\|H_{2t^{2}}f-H_{2t^{2}}\langle f\rangle_{X}\|+\|f-\langle f\rangle_{X}\|\leq 2\|f-\langle f\rangle_{X}\|.

Plugging this into (
6.21
), the final term is bounded from above by a constant multiple of

(6.23)

\displaystyle\|f-\langle f\rangle_{X}\|^{2}\int_{\alpha\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t^{3}}+\int_{0}^{\alpha\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)}\left\|\frac{H_{2t^{2}}f-f}{t}\right\|^{2},

which is comparable to the right-hand side of (
6.17
).
∎

The first term on the right-hand side of (
6.17
) is exactly the first term appearing on the right-hand side of (
6.16
). Thus, our goal for the remainder of the section is to estimate the second term on right-hand side of (
6.17
). We begin by breaking the integral into two pieces.

Define, for each
k\geq k^{*},
a function
p_{k}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
as follows. First, by applying Proposition
2.19
, construct a partition of unity
\{\theta_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}
satisfying the properties

(6.24)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{}}{supp}(\theta_{Q})\subseteq 3B_{Q},\ |\nabla\theta_{Q}(y)|\lesssim\ell(Q)^{-1},\ |\Delta\theta_{Q}(y)|\lesssim\ell(Q)^{-2},\mbox{ and }\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\theta_{Q}(y)=1

for all
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
and
y\in X
, where the implicit constants depend on
\mathfrak{c}(X)
. For
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k},
let
h_{Q}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
be a function which is harmonic on
6B_{Q}
and satisfies

(6.25)

\displaystyle H_{f}(6B_{Q};h_{Q})\lesssim H_{f}(6B_{Q}).

Then, define
p_{k}
by

(6.26)

\displaystyle p_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\theta_{Q}h_{Q}.

Let
\varrho_{k}
be as in (
4.12
), and recall that this is chosen so that
\ell(Q)=\varrho_{k}
for each
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
. By (
6.1
) and our choice of
\alpha
from Lemma
6.4
, we know that
\alpha\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)\leq\varrho_{k^{*}}
. This and (
H3
) imply

(6.27)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\alpha\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(X)}\left\|\frac{H_{2t_{2}}f-f}{t}\right\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}

(6.28)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt\lesssim\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{\infty}\int_{\varrho_{k+1}}^{\varrho_{k}}\left\|\frac{f-p_{k}}{t}\right\|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}+\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{\infty}\int_{\varrho_{k+1}}^{\varrho_{k}}\left\|\frac{H_{2t^{2}}p_{k}-p_{k}}{t}\right\|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\eqqcolon I_{1}+I_{2}.

Lemma 6.5
.

We have

(6.29)

\displaystyle I_{1}\lesssim_{C_{\mu}}\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{\infty}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}H_{f}(6B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q).

Proof.

Fix some
k\geq k^{*}
and
x\in Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
for the moment. Since
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{supp}(\theta_{R})\subseteq 3B_{R}
and
\{\theta_{R}\}_{R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}
is a partition of unity, we have

(6.30)

\displaystyle f(x)-p_{k}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\\
Q\cap 3B_{R}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\theta_{R}(x)(f(x)-h_{R}(x)).

Thus, by applying Lemma
2.2
and the inequality

(6.31)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\right)^{2}\leq N^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}^{2},\quad N\in\mathbb{N},

we get

(6.32)

\displaystyle|f(x)-p_{k}(x)|^{2}\lesssim_{C_{\mu}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\\
Q\cap 3B_{R}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}|f(x)-h_{R}(x)|^{2}.

In particular, recalling the definition of
H_{f},
for every
t\in(\varrho_{k+1},\varrho_{k})
we have

(6.33)

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\left(\frac{f-p_{k}}{t}\right)^{2}\,d\mu

\displaystyle\lesssim_{C_{\mu}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\\
Q\cap 3B_{R}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}H_{f}(3B_{R};h_{R})^{2}\mu(R).

Using this estimate, along with Lemma
2.2
and our choice of
h_{Q}
gives

(6.34)

\displaystyle I_{1}\lesssim_{C_{\mu}}\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{\infty}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\\
Q\cap 3B_{R}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}H_{f}(3B_{R};h_{Q})^{2}\mu(R)\lesssim_{C_{\mu}}\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{\infty}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}H_{f}(6B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q).

∎

The next lemma finishes the proof of Lemma
6.3
.

Lemma 6.6
.

We have

(6.35)

\displaystyle I_{2}\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{\infty}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}H_{f}(6B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q).

Proof.

Recall from (
H4
) that
\partial_{u}H_{u}=\Delta H_{u}
for each
u>0
. Thus, we can write

(6.36)

\displaystyle I_{2}=\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{\infty}\int_{\varrho_{k+1}}^{\varrho_{k}}\left\|\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{2t^{2}}\partial_{u}H_{u}p_{k}\,du\right\|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}=\sum_{k=k^{*}}^{\infty}\int_{\varrho_{k+1}}^{\varrho_{k}}\left\|\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{2t^{2}}\Delta H_{u}p_{k}\,du\right\|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}.

By Hölder’s inequality, using that
\Delta
and
H_{u}
commute, and
\|H_{u}\|_{2\to 2}\leq 1,
we find that for any
k\geq k^{*}
and
t\in(\varrho_{k+1},\varrho_{k}),

(6.37)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{2t^{2}}\Delta H_{u}p_{k}\,du\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{X}t^{2}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{0}^{2t^{2}}|H_{u}\Delta p_{k}|^{2}\,dud\mu\lesssim\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{0}^{2t^{2}}\|\varrho_{k}H_{u}\Delta p_{k}\|^{2}\,du

(6.38)

\displaystyle\leq\|\varrho_{k}\Delta p_{k}\|^{2}.

We will show in the remainder of the proof that

(6.39)

\displaystyle\|\varrho_{k}\Delta p_{k}\|^{2}=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\int_{Q}|\varrho_{k}\Delta p_{k}|^{2}\,d\mu\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}H_{f}(6B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q).

Fix some
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
for the moment. Since
\{\theta_{R}\}_{R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}
is a partition of unity, it follows that

(6.40)

\displaystyle h_{Q}-p_{k}=\sum_{R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\theta_{R}\left(h_{Q}-h_{R}\right).

In particular, applying the chain rule for
\Delta
and recalling that the function
h_{Q}
and
h_{R}
are harmonic on
6B_{Q}
and
6B_{R},
respectively, if

then

(6.41)

\displaystyle\Delta p_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\Delta(h_{Q}-p_{k})(x)

(6.42)

\displaystyle=\sum_{R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\left[\Delta\theta_{R}(x)(h_{Q}-h_{R})(x)+\nabla\theta_{R}(x)\cdot\nabla(h_{Q}-h_{R})(x)\right].

Using the bounds on the gradient and Laplacian of the cut-off functions, and estimating as we did in (
6.32
) (by applying Lemma
2.2
and (
6.31
)), we have

(6.43)

\displaystyle|\Delta p_{k}|^{2}\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X),C_{\mu}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\\
Q\cap 3B_{R}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{h_{Q}-h_{R}}{\varrho_{k}^{2}}\right|^{2}+\left|\frac{\nabla h_{Q}-\nabla h_{R}}{\varrho_{k}}\right|^{2}\quad\mbox{ on }Q.

Since
h_{Q}
and
h_{R}
are harmonic on
6B_{Q}
and
6B_{R}
, respectively, and
Q\cap 3B_{R}\subseteq 3B_{Q}\cap 3B_{R},
we may apply Caccioppoli’s inequality (Lemma
2.14
) to obtain

(6.44)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{Q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\\
Q\cap 3B_{R}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}|\nabla h_{Q}-\nabla h_{R}|^{2}\,d\mu&\lesssim_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\\
Q\cap 3B_{R}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\int_{6B_{Q}\cap 6B_{R}}\left(\frac{h_{Q}-h_{R}}{\varrho_{k}}\right)^{2}\,d\mu\\
&\lesssim_{C_{\mu}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\\
Q\cap 3B_{R}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\int_{6B_{R}}\left(\frac{f-h_{R}}{\ell(R)}\right)^{2}\,d\mu\\
&\lesssim_{C_{\mu}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\\
Q\cap 3B_{R}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}H_{f}(6B_{R})^{2}\mu(R),\end{split}

where in the second inequality we added and subtracted
f
, and applied the triangle inequality. Combining (
6.43
) and (

2.2
and using our choice of

\displaystyle\|\varrho_{k}\Delta p_{k}\|^{2}
