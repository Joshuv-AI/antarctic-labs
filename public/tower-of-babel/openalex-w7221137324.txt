Abstract
This theoretical article examines the Kantian theory of the concept, as reconstructed by Robert Hanna, and proposes
from the standpoint of Cognitive Psychiatry an inversion of its explanatory order. The objective is to show that concept,
object, unity, representation, sign, rule and understanding are late results of cognition and cannot serve as its original
conditions. The method is a qualitative conceptual essay supported by a narrative review of classical sources and of
studies published between 2021 and 2026. Hanna’s formulation is decomposed into six theses, each confronted with a
genetic reconstruction that begins in the lived and praxic processuality in which organism and world are already causally
coupled. The results describe a sequence that runs from praxis through interindividual coupling and synchronisation to
objectification and formalisation, and only then reaches signification, concept, representation and propositional
language. To be told to another person, lived experience has to be chewed into communicable units, which requires a
model of the listener’s mind and a model of one’s own; both derive from the mother, the first verbal accompanist of the
infant’s praxes, whose accompaniment, once internalised, helps to constitute the secondary self. The vocalisation “ai”
in pain illustrates functional communication prior to semiosis: the sign arises when a stabilised praxis becomes
decoupled from its original situation and able to evoke it. Recent developmental studies converge with this ordering. It
is concluded that form does not found process; process progressively founds form, which calls for a genetic
epistemology of pre-objectal, pre-formal and pre-semiotic processualities.
Keywords: Knowledge; Concept Formation; Language Development; Communication; Psychiatry.

1. Introduction
Modern epistemology usually opens its analysis with items that are already formed: the representation, the concept, the
rule, the judgement, the proposition. Kant’s theory of the concept is the most carefully built version of this starting point, and
Robert Hanna’s reconstruction states it in a single sentence: “every concept is the result of a specific generative mental operation,
or rule-governed act, of synthesis derived from the understanding, applied to a finite set of characteristics, each of which is
originally abstracted from the perceptual representations indicative of objects” (Hanna, 2001/2004, p. 193, our translation from
the Brazilian edition).
The sentence concentrates several presuppositions of transcendental epistemology: the precedence of the mental, the
generative function of the understanding, the existence of rules prior to the concept, the synthesis of a manifold, the representation
of objects that are already constituted and the abstraction of characteristics from those representations. They are Kant’s own
commitments. In the Critique of Pure Reason concepts rest on functions, understood as the unity of the act of ordering different
representations under a common one, and the understanding is described as the faculty of concepts, of rules and of judging (Kant,
1781/1998, A68–69/B93–94, A126). In the Jäsche Logic the form of a concept is produced by three logical acts of the
understanding, namely comparison, reflection and abstraction (Kant, 1800/1992, §6).
A different picture has been assembled over the last century by the study of development and of embodied cognition.
Piaget (1936) described a sensorimotor intelligence that precedes representation and proposed that epistemology should be
genetic (Piaget, 1970). Vygotsky (1934/1986) traced inner speech to the internalisation of social speech. Trevarthen (1979)
documented a primary intersubjectivity in the first months of life, and Bruner (1983) showed how the caregiver’s routines frame
the child’s entry into language. Merleau-Ponty (1945), Gibson (1979), Varela et al. (1991) and Clark (1997), each in a different
vocabulary, placed perception and cognition in the dealings of a body with its surroundings before any inner picture of the world.
Studies from the last five years add detail to this picture: infants learn that their vocalisations change what adults do months
before they have words (Elmlinger et al., 2023), turn-taking in the proto-conversation of mother and infant is accompanied by
interpersonal neural synchrony (Nguyen et al., 2023), mothers’ verbs follow what the infant is doing at that moment (West et al.,
2022), and in thirteen languages caregivers simplify their speech when they answer a child’s immature vocalisations (Elmlinger
et al., 2025). These results are seldom brought to bear on the epistemological question raised by Hanna’s sentence, namely what

The thesis presented here, formulated within Cognitive Psychiatry, proposes to invert almost the whole of that
explanatory order. The concept does not begin in a generative mental operation. It begins in a lived and praxic processuality in
which organism and world are already causally coupled. Objectification comes later, as do formalisation, signification and the
semiotic function. The understanding, in the sense of a faculty that handles representations and applies rules, is later still. In the
most primitive genesis of communication there is no need to postulate concepts, mental objects, representations or signs: a bodily
praxis can become coupled to a vocal praxis, the vocalisation of one individual can reorganise the praxis of another, and two
processualities can synchronise before either of them symbolically “means” the other. The elementary example is the vocalisation
“ai”, the Portuguese counterpart of “ouch”, in the face of pain, which shows that functional communication is possible before
semiosis: at first the listener does not have to relate a word to a representation of “pain” and can simply synchronise his or her
action with the processuality of the speaker.
The genetic architecture proposed runs as follows: processuality → lived experience and praxis → coupling →
interindividual synchronisation → recurrence → vocal accompaniment → internalisation of the accompaniment → selfreflection → objectification → formalisation → signification → concept → representation → propositional language. The middle
of this sequence is occupied by communication: the mother’s verbal accompaniment of the infant’s praxes, its internalisation as
secondary self, and the operations by which lived experience is chewed for a listener. If concept, object, unity, representation,
sign, rule and understanding are late results, an epistemology that starts from them risks turning products of cognition into its
original conditions. The consequences are therefore epistemological and not only psychological.
The aim of this article is to examine, thesis by thesis, the Kantian formulation reconstructed by Hanna, to set out the
foundations of a cognitive-praxic refoundation of epistemology in which form is the outcome of process, and to verify how far
this ordering converges with literature published in the last five years. The proposal does not intend to abolish the formal. It
relocates it genetically: form ceases to be foundation and becomes processual sedimentation.

2. Methodology
This is a qualitative study of a theoretical and conceptual nature (Pereira et al., 2018), conducted as an argumentative
essay supported by a narrative review of the literature. The object of analysis is the Kantian theory of the concept in the
formulation given by Hanna (2001/2004), read against the primary texts (Kant, 1781/1998, 1800/1992). The analysis followed
three steps. First, the formulation was decomposed into its constituent theses. Second, each thesis was confronted with a genetic
reconstruction developed within Cognitive Psychiatry, which asks what must already have taken place in the organism and
between organisms for the item named by the thesis (mental operation, rule, synthesis, understanding, characteristic,
representation of an object) to be available. Third, the resulting ordering was compared with classical works on development,
embodiment and language and with recent empirical and theoretical studies.
For the third step, a non-systematic search was carried out in October 2026 in PubMed/PubMed Central, PhilPapers and
publisher and institutional repository pages, with combinations of the terms concept formation, Kant, enactivism, embodied
cognition, interpersonal synchrony, proto-conversation, infant vocal development, caregiver speech, interjections, pain
vocalisation, inner speech and causal learning. Peer-reviewed articles published between 2021 and 2026, in English, that bore
directly on one of the steps of the proposed sequence were included; texts without identifiable authorship or without peer review
were excluded. The search was updated during the revision of the manuscript, with priority given to studies published from 2024
onwards. Twenty recent studies were retained, eleven of them from 2024 to 2026, and are cited at the points of the argument on
which they bear. Because the study is theoretical and involves no human participants or identifiable data, submission to a research

3. Results and Discussion
3.1 The Kantian text as a point of rupture
Hanna’s sentence can be decomposed into six theses: (i) the concept results from a mental operation; (ii) this operation
is generative; (iii) it is specific; (iv) it is governed by rules; (v) it consists in a synthesis derived from the understanding; (vi) it
acts upon characteristics previously abstracted from perceptual representations of objects. The criticism developed here holds
that all six begin the analysis too late. They are not necessarily false as a retrospective description of an adult cognition that is
already formalised. They become problematic when they are used to explain the genesis of the concept itself.
The distinction matters because the Jäsche Logic presents comparison, reflection and abstraction as the acts by which
concepts are generated as to their form (Kant, 1800/1992), and the question of whether comparison already requires the concept
it is supposed to produce is still debated among Kant scholars (Erlewein, 2026; Wang, 2021). Erlewein (2026) presents it as a
trilemma: the account of concept formation seems bound to move in a circle, to regress or to break off. The difficulty noted
inside the Kantian tradition is the one the genetic reading takes as its point of departure: the items invoked to explain the concept
are themselves of conceptual grain.
The exposition follows the genetic sequence and not the order of the sentence. Section 3.2 deals with the first three
theses. Sections 3.3 to 3.7 describe what the sentence leaves out: the communicative operations by which experience acquires
form, the mother’s accompaniment in which they originate and the secondary self that carries them out. Sections 3.8 and 3.9
return to theses (iv) and (v), sections 3.10 and 3.11 examine language and the sign, and section 3.12 takes up thesis (vi). The last
sections assemble the architecture, its consequences and its theses.
3.2 Before the mental operation: lived experience and praxis
The first disagreement concerns the expression “mental operation”. Before an object concept is formed there is no need
to suppose a mental operation already organised over representations. What exists at first is an organism in a situation. It acts, is
struck, changes posture, moves, avoids, approaches, vocalises, perceives and responds. If an animal chases me, before I construct
conceptually that “a lion chased me” there is visual movement, a roar, a threat, autonomic change, flight, a change of direction,
fear and bodily orientation. The starting point is lived and praxic, and it is neither formal nor representational. The mind does
not begin by inspecting an internal representation of an object, because the organism is already inside a processuality.
This starting point is the one described by the classical literature on sensorimotor intelligence and embodied perception
(Clark, 1997; Gibson, 1979; Merleau-Ponty, 1945; Piaget, 1936; Varela et al., 1991), and recent work pushes it further back.
Ciaunica et al. (2021) observe that the human organism first develops inside another human body, so that its earliest regulation
is a co-embodiment and a co-homeostasis and the minimal self is relational from the outset. Di Paolo (2021) argues that the
enactive approach requires bodies and minds to be treated as open and unfinished processes of becoming, and not as entities
given in advance. Both positions describe organism and world as coupled before anything is represented.
The expression “generative mental operation” raises a second difficulty. It may suggest an active mind that deliberately
produces a concept. In the theory presented here a large part of the initial process is automatic, reflex, neurophysiological, nondeliberate and non-conscious. The subject does not think “I shall now synthesise my perceptions in order to generate the concept
of lion”; that would be an intellectualist reconstruction of the process. Conceptualisation emerges from processes that long
precede deliberate thought.

3.3 Communicative volition and the need to chew experience
Although the process is largely automatic, there is a moment at which the will gains importance: I want to say something
to someone. This communicative wish changes the processuality deeply. I now have to look at what happened to me, select
elements, summarise, cut, order and adjust my expression to the person I am addressing. Formalisation therefore does not arise
only from a supposed internal need of the understanding. It is strongly driven by a relational need, that of making my experience
accessible to another person. The volitional element is specific only as to its communicative destination. What happens
cognitively afterwards, the selection, retrieval, association and ordering, depends largely on automatisms that are already
sedimented. The process is a mixture of communicative volition and cognitive-neurophysiological automatisms, and at no point
is it a voluntary construction supervised in full.
A lived experience is too rich to be transmitted whole. In the chase everything happened at once: the shape that moved,
the sound, the tightening of the body, the ground under the feet, the fear. None of it came divided. Speech can only say one thing
after another. Vygotsky (1934/1986) gave the difference an image: thought is a cloud and speech is the rain of words that falls
from it, so that what is present all at once in thought has to be unfolded in succession when it is spoken. The same holds, with
greater reason, for lived experience that has not yet become thought. To tell it I have to turn it into blocks, cuts, units and
sequences. It has to be, metaphorically, chewed.
Chewing is made of the operations already named. To cut is to break the flow at certain points and detach pieces from
it: the animal, the roar, the running. To select is to leave most of what was lived unsaid and to keep what can carry the episode
for someone who was not there. To summarise is to let one piece stand for many, so that “it chased me” covers a long stretch of
turns, stumbles and glances over the shoulder. To order is to place the pieces in a line, with a before and an after, although in the
event several of them were simultaneous. To adjust is to choose, for each piece, the size and the wording that this particular
listener can take in. None of these operations is found in the event. The chase had no joints at which it asked to be cut. The joints
are introduced by the need to tell it.
This chewing has two simultaneous poles, the event as lived and the capacity of the interlocutor. Food is chewed for a
mouth, and experience is chewed for a listener. The same episode is cut in one way for a child, in another for a hunter who knows
the animal, and in yet another for someone who was at my side and only needs to hear “it came back”. I do not simply describe
“the object”. I produce a communicable version of my experience with it, and the version changes with the person who receives
it.
The role of language changes accordingly. Language does not serve only to express concepts that are ready beforehand.
It takes part in the objectification of experience. When I have to explain that “it was brown”, “it had a mane”, “it came fast”, “it
roared” and “it tried to reach me”, I am converting a processual experience into communicable units. Communication forces
cutting, objectification and formalisation. To communicate is not simply to transmit form; communicating helps to produce form.
Borghi (2022) reaches a compatible conclusion from the study of concepts: all concepts are grounded in sensorimotor processes,
and the more abstract ones depend to a greater degree on linguistic and social interaction for their acquisition and use, so that
other people are part of how the concept comes to exist. A more recent review of this line of work brings together the evidence
that social interaction takes part both in the formation and in the use of abstract concepts (Borghi et al., 2025). Merleau-Ponty

3.4 To speak to the other I have to model the other’s mind
When I communicate I cannot simply pour out my experience. I have to ask implicitly what my interlocutor knows,
what he or she can recognise, which terms are familiar, how much needs explaining and what can be left out. The operation
resembles what is now called theory of mind: modelling cognitively what the other is able to understand.
The model does real work in the formation of what is said. If I judge that my listener has never seen a lion, the word
“lion” is useless and I have to go down to “a big animal, like a cat, with hair around its head”. If I judge that the listener knows
the animal well, the single word is enough and the detail would be noise. The grain at which experience is cut is therefore fixed
from outside, by an estimate of another person’s knowledge. The estimate is revised as the exchange goes on. A frown, a question
or a silence tells me that the piece was too big or too small, and I chew again. Communication is shaped by the expectation about
the interlocutor’s cognitive capacity and corrected by the interlocutor’s response.
Tomasello (1999) describes the developmental ground of this operation. Towards the end of the first year the child
comes to treat other persons as intentional agents like itself, enters scenes of joint attention with them and learns words inside
those scenes. A word learned in this way carries a perspective. The same animal can be called lion, animal, beast or danger, and
to choose one of these is to choose how the listener is asked to take the situation. The linguistic form is perspectival because it
was made for someone. The adjustment to the other begins even earlier, before there is anything to say: by five months infants
expect their vocalisations to change what the adult does and vocalise more when the expected response fails (Elmlinger et al.,
2023). The model of the listener’s knowledge that the adult speaker exercises has its first form in this practical anticipation of
the listener’s conduct.
This has a consequence for the theory of the concept. A form produced for one listener is still tied to that listener. But I
tell the episode again, to other people who know other things, and each telling demands a new cut. What survives the succession
of listeners, what can be said to any of them and recognised by all, is the part of the experience that no longer depends on who
is listening. The stable, re-identifiable and shareable pole that the tradition calls the object is reached along this path. It is what
remains constant across interlocutors, and the modelling of other minds is one of the operations that extract it.
3.5 A theory of mind of myself
A second operation is less discussed. To explain to someone what happened to me I must make my own lived experience
accessible to myself. I have to reconstruct what I felt, what I did, what I saw, what frightened me, what came before and what
came after. Communication thus involves a kind of theory of mind applied to oneself, an autotheory of mind.
The operation is needed because lived experience is not transparent to the one who lives it. While I was running I was
not observing my fear. I was afraid, and the fear was in the legs. The processuality was fully there, and it was not there as
something looked at. To tell it I have to go back over it from another position, that of someone who asks what happened, and
from that position my own experience has to be modelled much as the mind of another person is modelled: by signs, by
reconstruction, with gaps and with error. I conclude that I was frightened from what I did. I settle that the roar came before the

Two things follow. The first is that telling requires a doubling. There is the one who lived and the one who reports, and
the second treats the first almost as a third person. The questions I put to my own experience are the questions a listener would
put: what was it, what did it do, what did you do, what happened then. The autotheory of mind is the theory of mind turned
round. It borrows the interlocutor’s place and looks at oneself from there. The second is that the two models work together. I cut
my experience according to what the listener can take in, and I find out what there is to cut by questioning myself as the listener
would. Self-knowledge of this kind is a product of the effort to be understood.
The literature on inner speech describes this turning round. Vygotsky (1934/1986) observed that speech for oneself is
abbreviated, almost reduced to predicates, because the one who speaks and the one who listens share the whole context, and that
speech for another has to be unfolded in proportion to what the other lacks. When I prepare to tell what happened to me I pass
from the first regime to the second: I treat myself as a listener who needs the unfolded version. Fernyhough and Borghi (2023)
review evidence that inner speech ranges from condensed to expanded forms, keeps the give and take of dialogue and gives the
subject a distance from itself that serves reasoning and self-regulation. Borghi (2022) adds that with the more abstract concepts
the subject monitors its inner knowledge for longer and then turns to other people to settle the meaning. Tomasello (1999) places
the origin of this reflexive capacity in the child’s internalisation of the perspective that adults take on the child’s own thinking.
In all these accounts the access of the subject to itself passes through the form of an exchange with someone else.
3.6 The mother as first verbal accompanist of praxes
The adult capacities described in the three preceding sections, to chew experience, to model the listener and to model
oneself, do not appear suddenly. In the present hypothesis they have an ontogenetic history, and the history begins with someone
else doing all three on the child’s behalf.
From very early the mother, or whoever occupies her place in the care of the child, accompanies the infant’s gestures,
postures, affects, discomforts, pleasures, actions, apparent intentions and movements, and accompanies these praxes with words,
intonations, rhythms, interjections, namings and punctuations. The baby lives, and she accompanies the living with vocalisation.
This repeated association creates a fundamental matrix. She is the first verbal accompanist of the child’s praxes.
The scene is ordinary. The baby stretches towards something and the mother says “you want it… there, you got it”. The
baby startles and she says “oh, that was loud”. It pushes the spoon away: “no more, all done”. It falls, and before the crying has
taken shape she has already said “ai, that hurt”. In each case a vocal form is laid alongside a praxis while the praxis is under way
or has just ended. The baby did not ask for a description and is not yet able to use one. What it receives is a second contour,
made of sound, that runs parallel to its own movement and affect, with the same onset, the same peak and the same end.
Three features of this accompaniment matter for the theory. It is contingent in time: the vocalisation arrives with the
praxis, and it is this coincidence, repeated, that couples the two. It is rhythmic and intonational before it is lexical: the rise of the
voice follows the rise of the effort, the falling “there” marks the completion, and the infant can follow the contour long before it
can follow the words. And it punctuates: by beginning and ending where the action begins and ends, the voice marks off one
stretch of the flow from the next. The first cuts in the child’s experience are made from outside, by a voice that says where
something started and where it finished.

mothers treat the infant’s movements from the start as if they were meant. A longitudinal study of 104 dyads, observed at four,
six, nine and twelve months, found that the context of play shapes these early exchanges and that book-sharing favours vocal
turn-taking (Laudańska et al., 2025). Much of what is said in these exchanges consists of interjections, which Dingemanse (2024)
shows to be working tools of interaction. Wittgenstein (1953, §244) had seen the essential point in the case of pain: the child
hurts itself and cries, and the adults talk to it and teach it exclamations and, later, sentences.
What the mother does in these scenes is what the adult speaker will later do alone. She chews. She breaks the infant’s
continuous activity into stretches that receive a word, she picks out of everything the infant is doing the part she names, and she
offers it in a size the infant can take in: a single word, repeated, with an exaggerated contour. The metaphor is exact here. As
with food, the first chewing is done by another mouth. The reduction in size has been measured: in 1,586 transcripts from thirteen
languages, caregivers’ speech was simpler when it answered a child’s immature vocalisations than at other moments, with fewer
different words, shorter utterances and more utterances of a single word (Elmlinger et al., 2025). She also models the infant’s
mind. The apparent intentions she puts into words (“you want it”) are a theory of what the child is after, held by her before the
child can hold any theory of itself. And she adjusts continually to what the infant shows of having followed. The infant’s
experience thus reaches the infant already partly cut, selected and named by a person who has a model of it.
The pattern has a precedent in the body. Ciaunica et al. (2021) describe the earliest regulation of the organism as cohomeostasis, carried out with and through another body before it is carried out alone. The verbal accompaniment of praxes
repeats the arrangement on another plane: the infant’s experience is punctuated by another before it is punctuated by the infant.
Later this accompaniment can be internalised. The subject comes to do with itself what at first someone else did with
it. The external function, “someone accompanies my experience with language”, gradually becomes “I accompany my own
experience with language”. The intermediate step can be heard. The young child playing alone says aloud what it is doing, in the
mother’s words and often in her intonation. Vygotsky (1934/1986) studied this speech for oneself and noted that at first it comes
with the action or at its end, as a commentary, that it then moves towards the beginning of the action and starts to direct it, and
that it finally goes silent as inner speech. The child has taken over the role of accompanist. The possibility of narrating oneself
would thus have an interpersonal genealogy: an external caregiver accompanies praxis with vocalisation, the association recurs,
it is internalised, and the result is an internal accompaniment of one’s own praxes. To have the mother inside oneself is the
condition for telling oneself what is happening.
There is an evident proximity here with Vygotsky’s idea of the internalisation of social speech, which the current
literature on inner speech broadly supports (Fernyhough & Borghi, 2023). Vygotsky helps to understand the internalisation of
language. The present theory intends to go further back and asks about the earlier phase in which vocalisation does not yet need
to be fully signifying language. Before social speech that is semantically constituted there would be vocal-motor praxic couplings
that are not yet properly semiotic. The infant receives the mother’s first accompaniments as sound that goes with its action, and
not yet as statements about it. The coupling is established at this level, and only because it is already established can the same
vocal forms later be taken as meaning what they had accompanied.
In the vocabulary of Cognitive Psychiatry, the internalised accompaniment is related to the constitution of the secondary
self, to which the next section is devoted.

3.7 The secondary self as a communicative acquisition
Cognitive Psychiatry distinguishes two levels of the self. The primary self is the organism that feels, acts, orients itself,
wants, persists, remembers, expects, avoids and seeks without having to say “I am doing this”. It simply does. It is lived before
it is thematised, and it is the self of section 3.2, the one who runs from the lion. The secondary self is the level at which the
organism reacts to its own reactions. It perceives, accompanies, describes and reorganises the activity of the primary self. It is
the self that looks at itself acting, and it is what makes possible deliberate thought, self-observation, explicit planning, the
narration of oneself and thought about thought.
Nothing in the primary self demands the second level. An organism can deal with the world with great precision and
never turn back on its own dealing. The nervous system provides the possibility, since a reaction can be registered and become
the input of another reaction. The possibility, however, does not settle from what position the first reaction will be looked at, nor
with what instrument it will be held. The hypothesis of this article is that the position is first occupied by someone else.
Before the infant reacts to its own reactions, the mother reacts to them. The infant stretches and she answers the
stretching; it startles and she answers the startle. Each of her accompaniments is a reaction to a reaction, and it has the very
functions that will later define the secondary self: it perceives the infant’s praxis, accompanies it, describes it and, by soothing,
encouraging or stopping, reorganises it. In the first months the circuit of the secondary self therefore already exists, but it runs
through two organisms. The primary activity is in the child and the return upon it is in the mother. It is the arrangement already
met in bodily regulation (Ciaunica et al., 2021) and in the exchanges of the second month (Trevarthen, 1979): a function
distributed between two organisms before it belongs to one. The infant registers this return early, as the anticipation described
in section 3.4 shows (Elmlinger et al., 2023).
The secondary self is formed when this return changes place. At first there is organism and praxis. Then the other reacts
to me, names my actions and gives my experience back to me in words. I internalise this perspective, and a layer arises in which
I can see myself as the other saw me. Three moments can be distinguished in the passage. In the first the return is external: it is
the mother who says what I am doing. In the second the return is already mine, but it is still spoken aloud and still in her voice:
it is the speech for oneself of the child at play, described in section 3.6 (Vygotsky, 1934/1986). In the third the return has become
silent. It is what the adult knows as inner speech, and as the plain fact of noticing what one is doing. Recent developmental
theory also places a level of this kind late. In the agency-based model of Tomasello (2024), a second-order tier that monitors and
controls the child’s own executive processes emerges only after three to four years of age, and the flexibility of thought that
results is traced to the child’s efforts to coordinate different social perspectives in activities carried out with others.
What is internalised is a position more than a stock of words: the position of someone who watches me act and says
what I am doing (Tomasello, 1999). This is why the phrase “to see myself as the other saw me” should be taken literally. The
secondary self looks at the primary self from outside, and the outside it looks from is the place the mother occupied. Two features
of adult self-observation are explained in this way. It typically has the form of an exchange, with someone who asks and someone
who answers, and the literature on inner speech describes this dialogic form and the distance from oneself that it provides
(Fernyhough & Borghi, 2023). And it cuts experience at the joints at which experience was first cut by another voice: I notice in
myself the wanting, the hurting, the being afraid and the having finished that were once noticed in me and named.
This does not make the secondary self a purely social product. The capacity of a nervous system to register its own
reactions and to react to them is a biological condition, and a return upon one’s own activity that does not use words is
conceivable. Adults in fact differ widely in how much inner speech they report, from almost constant to almost none (Nedergaard
& Lupyan, 2024). What the communicative history supplies is the place from which the primary self is looked at and the vocal
forms with which what is seen is cut and held. The internalised perspective of the other is therefore one constitutive element of

the secondary self, and the secondary self is, for that reason, also a communicative acquisition.
The secondary self is the agent of the operations described in sections 3.3 to 3.5. To chew experience for a listener,
someone has to turn back on what was lived and cut it. To hold an autotheory of mind, someone has to take his or her own
processuality as something to be looked at. That someone is the secondary self, and the questions it puts to experience (what was

was watching it live. Self-observation is already socially formed. When as an adult I ask “what happened to me?”, I am not
performing a purely individual and original operation. I am using a capacity that was built relationally. In a certain sense I carry
the interlocutor inside me, and the communicative function has become reflexive.
Two consequences for the Kantian picture follow. The first concerns apperception. Kant places at the summit of the
understanding an “I think” that must be able to accompany all my representations (Kant, 1781/1998, B131). The verb is the one
used here for the mother. In the genetic reading, the accompaniment that Kant states as a formal condition has a history: before
an “I think” could accompany my representations, a “you want it”, a “you fell” and a “that hurt”, said by someone else,
accompanied my praxes. The unity that the reflective I brings to experience is the late and internal form of a company that was
first external. The second consequence concerns the subject of knowledge. The subject that classical epistemology places at the
start, the one who has representations and judges them, is the secondary self. If it is formed as described, the subject pole of the
relation between subject and object is as late as the object pole, and both are made in the same history: the world is objectified
by being told to a listener, and I am objectified to myself by taking the listener’s place.
This gives the early relationship a deep epistemological role. The mother enters before the formal concept. Besides
teaching names, she helps the organism to accompany, cut out, give rhythm to and punctuate its own experience, and this external
punctuation, once it has become internal, is the reflective self with which the philosophical tradition begins its analysis.
3.8 Regularity before rule
With the communicative operations and their ontogenesis in place, the remaining terms of Hanna’s sentence can be
taken in turn. The expression “rule-governed act” supposes a cognitive architecture in which rules already exist and direct the
formation of the concept. This follows from the role Kant assigns to the understanding as the faculty of rules. The present theory
contests that priority. Before the formal rule there is processual regularity.
There is something like a rule at the origin, though not in Kant’s sense. It consists of regularities that are far more
primitive: something happens and then produces a consequence; what has occurred does not simply un-occur; time does not run
back; an action alters a situation; processes accumulate transformations; certain organisations grow in complexity; certain
causalities have a direction. At first these are regularities of lived processuality, and only later rules of the understanding.
The true rule is praxic before it is logical. The organism learns in its own doing that action leads to consequence. It does
not need to formulate a principle of causality. It does, undergoes, anticipates and corrects. Regularity is incorporated
pragmatically before it is formalised. The order is: regularity of the world → praxis → incorporation → expectation → rule that
can later be made explicit. It is not: transcendental rule → organisation of experience. Once a regularity is stabilised it can be
stated as a rule, which makes the rule itself a late product and inverts Kant once more.
Developmental research on causal cognition describes the same order. In their review, Goddu and Gopnik (2024) place
the origin of causal understanding in the child’s learning to control its own movements and to act effectively on the environment,
followed by targeted intervention, and they treat depersonalised and decontextualised causal representations, that is, objective
and general ones, as what distinguishes mature human causal understanding. Explicit causal rules come at the end of a history
of doing (Piaget, 1936).

The hypothesis goes beyond simple empiricism. Besides saying that causality is learned by repetition, it holds that the

the idea, developed within Cognitive Psychiatry, of a processual form of intuition that is broader than space and time alone.
3.9 Synthesis, unity and understanding
The next term is “synthesis”. To synthesise one must suppose elements A, B and C that are afterwards brought together.
That division may itself be later. Before it there may simply be a processual flow. The separation into colour, movement, sound,
threat and object is already the result of later cuts. At the beginning, then, there may be no manifold plus synthesis. There is a
processuality from which manifold and unity will later be extracted.
Synthesis and unity can be retrospective artefacts. Once LION has been formed we can reconstruct it as mane plus tail
plus roar plus four legs, and it then seems that the mind synthesised these characteristics. We may, however, be analysing in
retrospect a product that is already formalised. Synthesis can be a later logical description and not the original genetic operation.
The same holds for unity. Kant repeatedly needs unity: unification, synthetic unity, unity of apperception (whose genetic
counterpart was indicated in section 3.7), unity of the concept. If we do not start from a formal manifold, we do not need to start
from a formal unity that gathers it. Unity can emerge afterwards. Integrative processes produce unity; unity is not what makes
integration possible. This is a relevant criticism of the Kantian architecture, in which formalism creates the problem that unity is
then called on to solve. If I start from separate elements, I have to explain how they come to be joined, and then synthesis, unity
and a unifying I appear. If the point of origin is processual and has not yet been cut up, the problem may arise only because the
theory formalised too early. Unity would be in part the solution to a problem produced by the formalist premise.
Hanna says that the synthesis is “derived from the understanding”. Cognitively this raises a difficulty. The Kantian
understanding is the faculty of concepts, the faculty of rules and the faculty of judging (Kant, 1781/1998). These are functional
descriptions. They do not explain genetically how the faculty emerges, how its operations are constituted or why it takes those
forms. The risk is that the name of the function is turned into the explanation of the function. Who synthesises? The
understanding. And what is the understanding? That which synthesises according to rules. An explanatory circle becomes
possible. The circularity that commentators discuss within Kant’s account of empirical concept formation, in which the
comparison of representations appears to need the very concept it should yield (Erlewein, 2026; Wang, 2021), is a local form of
this general problem.
Conceptual activity can precede the formally articulated understanding. Primitive communication is again the simplest
example. A baby can vocalise, respond, synchronise, anticipate and adjust, and there is no need to attribute to it a fully formed
Kantian understanding. There is communicative and proto-conceptual activity before the understanding in the formal and
reflective sense. If “understanding” names the capacity to recognise concepts, operate rules, judge and combine, it may itself be
a late level built on more elementary processes, a result of sedimentations, and it should not be used without explanation as the
origin of concepts.
A metaphor sums this up: transcendental epistemology begins on the upper floor. Kant describes with mastery what is
found there: concept, judgement, rule, synthesis, unity. The present theory asks how the building reached that height, and holds
that its construction cannot be explained with the materials of the upper floor alone.
3.10 The cry of pain: communication before signification
The interjection “ai”, announced in the Introduction and heard in the mother’s voice in section 3.6, is decisive for the
argument. Someone steps on a thorn. There is pain, withdrawal, contraction and a vocalisation: “ai!”. The theoretical error would

be to write at once that “ai” means PAIN. That presupposes signifier, signified, representation and semiotic function. None of
this is needed. We can start only with an algesic-motor praxis coupled to a vocal praxis.
One praxis can be coupled to another without representing it. This is one of the central points of the theory of language
proposed here. The vocalisation does not need to represent the pain. It can simply accompany it. The two processes become
coupled, and at first nothing requires a meaning. In the passage already cited, Wittgenstein (1953, §244) suggests that words for
sensations are tied to the primitive and natural expressions of sensation and come to be used in their place, so that the verbal
expression of pain replaces the cry and does not describe it.
The listener does not need to decode either. Another subject hears “ai!” and may look, interrupt what he or she was
doing, approach, draw back or help. The traditional description would be: heard a sign, interpreted “pain”, responded. A more
economical description is available: the vocalisation of the other reorganises my praxis. This is interindividual synchronisation.
The possibility breaks a very old association, that of language with sign and of sign with meaning. The thesis is that
there can be functional communication before signification. The communication in question is coordination of conduct, still far
from mature propositional communication. Before semiotics there is synchronisation: a speaker in process A produces
vocalisation V, and a listener modifies process B. If this recurs, A, V and B become a relatively stable configuration. No symbol
is needed at the start.
This is a strong hypothesis: language may have a pre-semiotic phase. Mature language is obviously semiotic. Its
ontogenesis, and perhaps its phylogenesis, may have involved stages in which vocalisations were first coordinations, alerts,
adjustments, rhythms, summonses and responses. Representational signification would have emerged later.
Several recent findings are consistent with it. In a comparison of interjections from 131 languages with nonlinguistic
vocalisations, Ponsonnet et al. (2024) found that pain is the only affective experience among those tested whose vowel signature,
open a-like vowels and wide falling diphthongs such as “ai”, is preserved from the nonlinguistic cry to the interjection across
languages. The word for pain keeps the acoustic shape of the bodily event it accompanies, which is what one would expect if the
vocal praxis was coupled to the algesic praxis before it became a lexical item. Dingemanse (2024) reviews evidence that
interjections, long treated as marginal, are tools for conducting interaction and help children break into language. The
developmental findings discussed in sections 3.4 and 3.6 point the same way: the infant’s expectation, at five months, that its
vocalisations will alter what adults do (Elmlinger et al., 2023) and the relation between vocal turn-taking and neural synchrony
at four to six months (Nguyen et al., 2023). A longitudinal study adds that caregivers’ contingent responses to babbling at five
months predict vocal turn-taking at ten months, and that the growth of prelinguistic vocalising and turn-taking over this period
predicts the child’s early language (Zhang et al., 2024). In these studies vocalisation coordinates conduct between two organisms
well before it can be said to stand for anything. On the philosophical side, Bar-On (2021) argues that the search for precursors
of linguistic communication needs an intermediate notion of pragmatics, broader than the Gricean one built on communicative
intentions, so that pragmatic phenomena can be recognised where speaker meaning is absent.
3.11 Decoupling and the birth of the sign
The sign is born of decoupling. When a vocalisation can occur without the original situation, a decisive transformation
takes place. “Ai” can be said while remembering the pain, imitating someone, telling a story or warning of a danger. The
vocalisation is then no longer only coupled to the present situation. It can evoke it. The path is coupling → stabilisation →
decoupling → evocation, and it is here that the semiotic function properly begins. Deacon (1997) described a comparable passage
when he derived symbolic reference from indexical relations that become progressively detached from the immediate context.
In this theory a sign is a stabilised praxic form that has acquired the capacity to function decoupled from the original

Meaning does not at first cause efficacy; efficacy prepares meaning. In the traditional model the word works because it

everything or a sign for everything.
3.12 Object, representation and characteristic as late products
The last part of Hanna’s formulation speaks of “perceptual representations indicative of objects”. The expression
presupposes objects, representations of objects and characteristics of those objects. These are exactly what should be explained.
In the perspective proposed here the cognitive object is late, and experience does not begin with ready-made objects.
Representation is late as well. Before there is a “representation of the lion” there is being chased, hearing, seeing,
running, feeling fear and orienting oneself. The concept of representation may be imposing a photograph on what was originally
a processual film. A large part of modern epistemology works with a similar scheme, the old theory of the ready-made object:
external object → internal representation → concept. The present thesis proposes another: processuality of organism and world
→ lived experience and praxis → communicative reflexivity → objectification → form → representation and concept. The
inversion is substantial, and it agrees with the critique of representation carried out by ecological and enactive approaches to
perception (Clark, 1997; Gibson, 1979; Varela et al., 1991). Fuchs (2026) reaches a similar point from phenomenology and
enactivism: putting experience into words partly undoes the unity of the lived situation, the separation of subject and object is
secondary and becomes fixed through language, and a noun such as “table” names at first a practical dealing with the thing, from
which the object is isolated only afterwards.
Early representation creates artificial problems. Once the representation of objects is presupposed, questions follow:
how do we classify these representations, how do we abstract what is common, how do we synthesise characteristics, how do
we guarantee identity, how do we arrive at unity? Several of these problems may have been created by the initial supposition
that delimited object representations were already there.
The object concept can emerge from processualities, lived experiences, praxes, partial coagulations and communication.
To abstract characteristics from objects therefore does not explain the first conceptualisation. It is an operation that becomes
possible later. The characteristic is itself a late product. To speak of mane, tail, brown or four legs already presupposes that
experience has been segmented. A characteristic is not necessarily a raw datum of perception. It is also a conquest of
formalisation, obtained in the chewing of experience described in section 3.3, and so the Kantian account as presented by Hanna
begins with yet another late product.
Before the characteristic there is lived difference. Before “brown” exists as an explicit characteristic there is chromatic
experience. Before “fast” there is the experience of displacement and temporality. Before “furry” there is a certain tactile and
visual experience. Before “aggressive” there is a relational configuration that is lived. The order is lived experience →
differentiation → stabilisation → characteristic, and not simply a characteristic that is already present followed by abstraction.
A more suitable word than “generation” may be sedimentation. The concept is sedimentation and not fabrication. It

3.14 Consequences for epistemology
These results modify epistemology itself. Knowledge ceases to be only a relation of subject to object. The basic structure
becomes triangular: world, subject and other subject. Communication is not added to knowledge afterwards. It takes part in its
formalisation (Tomasello, 1999).
Objectivity and communication come close to each other again. Communication is a matrix of objectivity: when I have
to make my lived experience communicable, I must take from it something stable, re-identifiable and shareable, which is what
stays constant from one listener to the next (section 3.4), and this favours the formation of form. Form thus has a genealogy and
is a communicational product. It does not fall ready-made from the understanding; we produce it because we need to recognise,
repeat, communicate and compare. Form is processuality stabilised for cognitive circulation.
A philosophy may reply that this is psychology and not logic. That separation is exactly what is in question. Formal
validity can remain independent of psychological facts. The genesis of the forms that logic uses is nonetheless a real cognitive
question, and to refuse the investigation for fear of psychologism does not solve the problem. It only excludes it by method, and
a genetic epistemology undertakes to lift that exclusion (Piaget, 1970).
To naturalise is not to reduce. No one is claiming that concepts are neurons or that truth is a brain state. The claim is
that concepts have a history of constitution that takes place in real organisms. Studying that history leaves logical validity intact
and explains how an organism becomes capable of logic.
Cognitive Psychiatry occupies this interval. Between neurophysiology and formal epistemology there is a layer made
of lived cognition, praxis, conceptualisation, self, inner language and communication. The layer has often been divided among
psychology, linguistics, philosophy and neuroscience. Cognitive Psychiatry proposes to treat it as one continuous process.
3.15 The theses of the refoundation
The argument can be condensed into ten theses, on the concept, language, the sign, the understanding, the rule, synthesis,
representation, objectification, form and epistemology. They are stated in Table 3.

Topic

Thesis

Concept

The concept is not originally the product of a specific mental operation of rule-governed synthesis. It
is a late formal sedimentation of lived, praxic and communicative processualities, formed on

Language

Language does not necessarily arise when a vocalisation represents an object. It can begin in praxic

The understanding should not be postulated as an original faculty that produces concepts. It can itself
be understood as a higher level of stabilisation of praxic, communicative, reflective and conceptual
capacities constituted earlier.

Rule

The explicit cognitive rule is later than processual regularity. The organism does not begin by
applying rules to the world; through its praxis it incorporates regularities of a world in which it was
itself constituted.

3.16 Convergence with recent literature and limits
The recent studies cited throughout this section were not designed to test the theory, and none of them, taken alone,
establishes it. Their value lies in convergence. Work from different fields and with different methods places bodily coupling
before representation (Ciaunica et al., 2021; Di Paolo, 2021), practical causal competence before objective causal knowledge
(Goddu & Gopnik, 2024), vocal coordination before words (Elmlinger et al., 2023; Laudańska et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2023;
Zhang et al., 2024), the mother’s verbal accompaniment of action before the child’s own narration (Elmlinger et al., 2025;
Fernyhough & Borghi, 2023; West et al., 2022), the bodily cry before the interjection (Dingemanse, 2024; Ponsonnet et al.,
2024), social interaction within concept formation (Borghi, 2022; Borghi et al., 2025; Fuchs, 2026) and a second-order level of
self-regulation that appears late and in shared activity (Tomasello, 2024). The ordering is the one proposed here.
Two limits should be stated. The pre-semiotic phase of language is a theoretical hypothesis: the available data show that
vocal coordination is early and effective, and they do not by themselves show that it is free of any proto-meaning. And the
phylogenetic extension of the sequence is offered as a possibility, since the evidence discussed is ontogenetic. Both points
indicate where longitudinal and comparative studies could confirm or correct the model.

4. Final Considerations
Hanna’s sentence concentrates the traditional architecture: the concept as mental product, generation, rule, synthesis,
understanding, characteristics, representations and objects. The criticism developed here is that each of these categories has an
earlier history. Before the mental operation there is lived experience. Before deliberate generation there is neurophysiological
automatism. Before the rule there is processual regularity. Before synthesis there is a flow not yet cut up. Before the
understanding there are praxis and interaction. Before the characteristic there is lived difference. Before representation there is
causal participation. Before the internal object there is external processuality. Before the sign there is functional vocalisation.
Before signification there is coupling. Before symbolic language there is synchronisation. And before the subject is able to
narrate itself there is another person who accompanied its experience and who was progressively internalised.
This reconstruction changes epistemology deeply. The concept ceases to be the starting point and becomes a result. The
understanding ceases to be first cause and becomes late organisation. The reflective subject ceases to be an original endowment
and becomes the internalised place of an interlocutor. The rule no longer imposes form on experience and becomes the explicit
statement of regularities that were lived beforehand. Language no longer arises from the relation of sign to meaning and acquires

The inversion can be condensed in one sentence: we do not begin by representing the world; we begin by taking part in

usually begins its analysis with the last terms of this sequence. The proposal of Cognitive Psychiatry is to reconstruct the natural

of the concept. It is a broader change, from the epistemology of ready-made form to an epistemology of the genesis of form.
