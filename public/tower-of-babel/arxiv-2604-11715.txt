Koopman Representations for Non-Vanishing Time Intervals: An Optimization Approach and Sampling Effects
Younghwan Cho, Richard Sowers

Abstract
Koopman operator theory is a key tool in data assimilation of complex dynamical systems, with the potential to be applied to multimodal data. We formulate the problem of learning Koopman eigenfunctions from observations at arbitrary, possibly non-vanishing, time intervals as an optimization problem. Analysis of the formulation reveals aliasing induced by oscillatory dynamics and the sampling pattern, making an inherent identifiability limit explicit. The analysis also uncovers phase alignment near the true Koopman frequency, which creates a steep loss valley and demands careful optimization. We further show that irregular sampling can break aliasing and lead to phase cancellation. Numerical results demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed method under large regular time intervals compared to generator extended dynamic mode decomposition, and support the idea that irregular sampling can help recover the true Koopman spectrum.



I
Introduction

The seminal idea of Koopman
[
13
]
maps flows of possibly nonlinear dynamics to a linear operator on a function space without loss of information; the tradeoff is that, generically, this space is infinite-dimensional. Under certain regularity conditions, the linear evolution of mapped flows admits a spectral decomposition for a broad family of dynamics
[
13
]
, motivating significant interest in applying Koopman decompositions to complex dynamical systems
[
18
,
3
,
15
,
1
]
. Since the Koopman operator is infinite-dimensional, finite-dimensional approximations are of great importance. However, an arbitrary finite-dimensional subspace is not necessarily Koopman-invariant. A lack of invariance can produce spurious spectral objects and only locally accurate predictions
[
19
,
2
]
. To address the aforementioned, recent studies
[
8
,
14
,
10
,
7
]
work with subspaces spanned by Koopman eigenfunctions, which are Koopman-invariant by construction, rather than arbitrary dictionaries of functions
[
23
,
12
]
.

Our interest, broadly, is applying Koopman decompositions to
multimodal
data. For example, sensor data of human gait
[
11
]
contains multiple gait events with temporal irregularity, e.g., left and right heel strike are not exactly periodic due to subjects’ heterogeneity or the morbidity of neurodegenerative disorder
[
16
]
. Loop detectors detect traffic at fixed but potentially irregular locations
[
9
]
. As such, we have various interesting phenomena hidden in the complex data, and our particular interest is to learn Koopman eigenfunctions with observations of irregular time intervals. Several studies
[
14
,
6
]
address Koopman eigenfunction learning under irregular sampling. In
[
14
]
, local Koopman eigenfunctions are constructed on non-recurrent sets, and these functions are extended to the rest of the state space via interpolation. On the other hand,
[
6
]
searches for eigenfunctions directly over the entire space by minimizing the Koopman invariance residual, and its robustness has been demonstrated empirically under different sampling scenarios.

In this paper, we propose an extension of
[
6
]
to complex Koopman spectral objects. We formulate the problem of learning Koopman eigenfunction as an optimization task that operates directly on state observations at arbitrary times.
Analysis of the loss landscape reveals that (1) the formulation explicitly reflects aliasing induced by the sampling pattern and that (2) phase alignment near the true frequency creates a steep valley. The identifiability limit is not directly exposed in convex formulations such as EDMD
[
23
]
, which may nonetheless be affected by the same information loss. Third, we show analytically and numerically that irregular sampling can break aliasing and improve the identifiability of Koopman spectral objects. We expect that our formulation and its analysis provide general insights to optimization of Koopman-based methods.

II
Preliminaries

Koopman operator theory allows us to analyze continuous-time flows on some Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
With a Lipschitz continuous or continuously differentiable vector field
\mathsf{V}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
, first define the flow map
\{\Phi_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
as
the solution of

\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}+\int_{s=0}^{t}\mathsf{V}(\Phi_{s}(\mathbf{x}))ds\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(1)

For arbitrary map (or “observable”)
f:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{C}
, one can define the Koopman group
\{\mathcal{K}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
as follows:

\left(\mathcal{K}_{t}f\right)(\mathbf{x})\overset{\text{def}}{=}f(\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}))=(f\circ\Phi_{t})(\mathbf{x})\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}.

The main result of Koopman
[
13
]
was that in certain settings (restriction of
\mathcal{K}_{t}
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
and unitary
\mathcal{K}_{t}
), we can construct spectral decompositions of
\{\mathcal{K}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
. In other words, there exist pairs
(\mathfrak{e},\lambda)
of functions
\mathfrak{e}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{C}
(eigenfunctions) and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
(eigenvalues) such that
\left(\mathcal{K}_{t}\mathfrak{e}\right)(\mathbf{x})=e^{\lambda t}\mathfrak{e}(\mathbf{x}),\kern 5.0pt\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
or, equivalently,

\mathfrak{e}(\Phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}))=e^{\lambda t}\mathfrak{e}(\mathbf{x})\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}

(2)

The Koopman decomposition states that, under the correct hypothesis, the collection of eigenfunctions spans the collection of all square-integrable functions in a proper sense.
The decomposition provides insights into nonlinear dynamics, including reduced order modeling
[
17
]
and invariant and periodic structures of the state space
[
4
]
.

III
Problem formulation

Suppose that we want to approximate a Koopman decomposition using data
\{\mathbf{x}(t_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
. If we would have a Koopman eigenpair
(\mathfrak{e},\lambda)
, we would have

\mathfrak{e}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n+1}))=e^{\lambda(t_{n+1}-t_{n})}\mathfrak{e}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n}))

for all
n\in\{1,2\dots N\}
, or, equivalently,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\mathfrak{e}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n+1}))-e^{\lambda(t_{n+1}-t_{n})}\mathfrak{e}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n}))\right|^{2}=0.

(3)

More generally, if
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{C}
, define

\mathcal{L}(\phi,\lambda)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\phi(\mathbf{x}(t_{n+1}))-e^{\lambda(t_{n+1}-t_{n})}\phi(\mathbf{x}(t_{n}))\right|_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}

(where
|\cdot|_{\mathbb{C}}
is modulus in
\mathbb{C}
)
then
\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{e},\lambda)=0
if
(\mathfrak{e},\lambda)
is a Koopman eigenpair. Conversely, if
\mathcal{L}(\phi,\lambda)=0
and there are “enough”
t_{n}
’s,
(\phi,\lambda)
should be “close” to an eigenpair (we will not make this precise).

Numerically, we might fix a space
V
of maps from
\mathbb{R}^{D}
to
\mathbb{C}
and minimize

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi\in V\\
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{L}(\phi,\lambda).

Given theoretical knowledge that there is a Koopman decomposition, this minimization problem should be close to zero if
V
is “rich” enough.

Fix some (linearly independent) collection functions
\{g_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, each of the
g_{m}
’s being a map from
\mathbb{R}^{D}
to
\mathbb{C}
. Define
\mathbf{G}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{C}^{M}
as

\mathbf{G}(x)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\begin{pmatrix}g_{1}(x)\\
g_{2}(x)\\
\vdots\\
g_{M}(x)\end{pmatrix}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. The span of
\{g_{1},g_{2}\dots g_{M}\}
is then

\left\{\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G}:\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}\right\}

where
\dagger
is a conjugate transpose.
We are then interested in the minimization problem

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}\\
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G},\lambda)

Let us write out
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G},\lambda)
. Expanding the modulus squared and averaging over
n
, we obtain

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G},\lambda)=\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{C}(\lambda)\mathbf{a}

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n+1}))-e^{\lambda(t_{n+1}-t_{n})}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n}))\right)

(4)

\displaystyle\times\left(\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n+1}))-e^{\lambda(t_{n+1}-t_{n})}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n}))\right)^{\dagger}.

Write time intervals as
\Delta t_{n}=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
, and
\lambda=\alpha+i\beta\in\mathbb{C}
.
We can now solve the optimization problem to find the approximation of
(\mathfrak{e},\lambda)
as follows:

\min_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R},\,\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{C}^{M},\,\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}=1}\mathcal{L}\left(\alpha,\beta,\mathbf{a}\right).

(5)

First, we deduce useful basic properties of (
5
). In (
5
), one can show that
\mathbf{C}(\alpha,\beta)
is Hermitian positive semi-definite. Moreover,
\mathbf{C}(\alpha,\beta)
is analytic function of
\alpha,\beta
, and the loss of (
5
) is symmetric w.r.t.
\beta
. Given some
\alpha,\beta
, the optimal solution of (
5
) is the smallest eigenvalue-eigenvector pair of
\mathbf{C}(\alpha,\beta)
. Since
\mathbf{C}(\alpha,\beta)
is Hermitian positive semi-definite, the constrained minimum over
\mathbf{a}
is obtained at the eigenvector corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{C}(\alpha,\beta)
.

We have two key linear operators in (
5
): the Koopman operator and the
\mathbf{C}(\alpha,\beta)
depending on data and the Koopman eigenvalue, whose spectra determine the regularity of (
5
). We avoid assumptions on the Koopman spectrum, as the underlying dynamics are often inaccessible.
Without further assumptions, (
5
) learns/approximates a point spectrum of an unknown Koopman operator.
\mathbf{C}(\alpha,\beta)
has a real and non-negative spectrum. We will assume that the spectrum of
\mathbf{C}(\alpha,\beta)
is simple and support this with that the occurrence of non-simple Hermitian matrices is non-generic
[
22
]
. Assuming the simple spectrum
\mathbf{C}(\alpha,\beta)
gives us a better regularity on the eigenvalue map, i.e., a map from a Hermitian matrix to its smallest eigenvalue.
This will enable us to design an optimization scheme based on the first-order information of the loss function. Denote by
\lambda_{M}:H(M)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
the smallest-eigenvalue map and by
u_{M}:H(M)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{M}
the associated unit-eigenvector map on the space of
M
-dimensional Hermitian matrices. Then, we can write (
5
) as the following form:

\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}{\min}\lambda_{M}\left(\mathbf{C}(\alpha,\beta)\right).

(6)

For brevity, we write
\lambda_{M}(\alpha,\beta)
for
\lambda_{M}(\mathbf{C}(\alpha,\beta))
, and similarly for
u_{M}
. We have the following result that can be directly applied to (
6
).

Proposition 1

Assume
\mathbf{C}(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
is
C^{1}
for all
(\alpha,\beta)
.
Suppose that at
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
the eigenvalue
\lambda_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
is simple, i.e.,
\mathbf{C}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
has no repeated eigenvalues.
Let
u_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
be a unit eigenvector associated with
\lambda_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})u_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\,u_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}),

\displaystyle u_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})^{\dagger}u_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})

\displaystyle=1.

Then
\lambda_{M}
is differentiable at
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
and its gradient with respect to
(\alpha,\beta)
satisfies the following:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\lambda_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\lambda_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\end{bmatrix}

(7)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}u_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\mathbf{C}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\right)u_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\\[6.0pt]
u_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\mathbf{C}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\right)u_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\end{bmatrix}.

Proof:

Using the one-parameter perturbation result of the eigenvalue
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
and the associated eigenvector in
[
22
]
or
[
20
]
, we take derivatives of
\mathbf{C}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})u_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})=\lambda_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\,u_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
for both sides. The results follows by using
u_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})^{\dagger}u_{M}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})=1
to organize the terms.
∎

IV
Loss landscape analysis

Having established differentiability, we analyze the structure of the loss landscape from global to local properties to understand the optimization problem.
By regular sampling, we refer to periodic sampling with a fixed, possibly non-vanishing sampling time interval. Our first observation is that the loss function of (
5
) exhibits a periodic structure in the
\beta
-axis under regular sampling.

Proposition 2

Assume
\Delta t_{n}=\delta>0,\kern 5.0pt\forall n\in[N].

Fix a feasible coefficient vector
\mathbf{a}
, and define

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta,\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle\phi_{n}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n})),

\displaystyle\psi_{n}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{def}}{=}e^{\alpha\delta}\phi_{n}\bar{\phi}_{n+1}.

Then, for every fixed
\alpha
, the map
\beta\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)
is periodic with period
2\pi/\delta
:

\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta+\nicefrac{{2\pi}}{{\delta}})=\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta),\qquad\forall\beta\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof:

By definition, the loss can be written as

\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\phi_{n+1}-e^{(\alpha+i\beta)\Delta t_{n}}\phi_{n}\right|^{2}.

Expanding (
4
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n+1}))\mathbf{G}^{T}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n+1}))

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{2\Re(\lambda)\Delta t_{n}}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n}))\mathbf{G}^{T}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n}))

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Re\left\{e^{\lambda\Delta t_{n}}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\times\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n+1}))\mathbf{G}^{T}(\mathbf{x}(t_{n}))

We multiply
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}
on the left and
\mathbf{a}
on the right. Using the definition of
\psi_{n}
,
\phi_{n}
and

\Delta t_{n}=\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big(|\phi_{n+1}|^{2}+|e^{(\alpha+i\beta)\delta}\phi_{n}|^{2}

\displaystyle-2\Re\!\big(\phi_{n+1}\overline{e^{(\alpha+i\beta)\delta}\phi_{n}}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(|\phi_{n+1}|^{2}+e^{2\alpha\delta}|\phi_{n}|^{2}-2\Re\!\left(e^{i\beta\delta}e^{\alpha\delta}\phi_{n}\bar{\phi}_{n+1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(|\phi_{n+1}|^{2}+e^{2\alpha\delta}|\phi_{n}|^{2}\right)-\frac{2}{N}\Re\!\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{i\beta\delta}\psi_{n}\right).

Then, for any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, it is clear that
\mathcal{L}\!(\alpha,\beta+\nicefrac{{2\pi}}{{\delta}})=\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)
. Therefore,
\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)
is periodic in
\beta
with period
2\pi/\delta
.
∎The result shows that coarse sampling induces stronger aliasing in the frequency direction. More precisely, predicted frequencies separated by integer multiples of
2\pi/\delta
are indistinguishable to the loss. In contrast, finer sampling increases the period in the
\beta
-direction and makes the optimization landscape less affected by phase aliasing. This interpretation is consistent with
the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem
. That is, for exact identification of a signal with a frequency band of
[-\beta_{M},\beta_{M}]
, the sampling rate needs to satisfy
\frac{2\pi}{\delta}>2\beta_{M}

[
21
]
. In this study, we do not assume a frequency band for the true dynamics, as we are machine-learning the system of interest. Hence, the smaller
\delta
is, the more amenable our problem is to numerical methods. In practice, expert knowledge can be used to propose a plausible bandwidth a priori to partially resolve the identification issue.

Using
Proposition

1
, the partial derivatives of

\lambda_{M}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)
are

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\lambda_{M}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)}{\partial\alpha}&=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Delta t_{n}e^{\alpha\Delta t_{n}}\Big(e^{\alpha\Delta t_{n}}|\phi_{n}|^{2}\\
&-\Re{(e^{i\beta\Delta t_{n}}\phi_{n}\bar{\phi}_{n+1})}\Big).\\
\frac{\partial\lambda_{M}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)}{\partial\beta}&=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Im{(\Delta t_{n}\psi_{n}e^{i\beta\Delta t_{n}})}.\end{split}

(8)

Expanding
\psi_{n}
and writing each
\phi_{n}
in polar form,

\phi_{n}=|\phi_{n}|\,e^{i\arg\phi_{n}}
, we obtain

\begin{split}&\frac{\partial\lambda_{M}(\alpha,\beta)}{\partial\beta}\\
&=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Im\!\Big(\Delta t_{n}\,|\psi_{n}|\,e^{i(\beta\Delta t_{n}+\arg\phi_{n}-\arg\phi_{n+1})}\Big)\\[6.0pt]
&=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Im\!\Big(\Delta t_{n}\,e^{(\alpha+\alpha^{*})\Delta t_{n}}\,|\phi_{n}|^{2}\,e^{i(\beta-\beta^{*})\Delta t_{n}+i2\pi k_{n}}\Big)\\[6.0pt]
&=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Delta t_{n}\,e^{(\alpha+\alpha^{*})\Delta t_{n}}\,|\phi_{n}|^{2}\,\sin\!\left((\beta-\beta^{*})\Delta t_{n}\right),\end{split}

where
\beta^{*}
denotes the true Koopman frequency. Koopman invariance implies
\phi_{n+1}=e^{(\alpha^{*}+i\beta^{*})\Delta t_{n}}\phi_{n}
and it follows that
\arg\phi_{n+1}=\arg\phi_{n}+\beta^{*}\Delta t_{n}+2\pi k_{n}
, where
k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
is the unique integer such that
\arg\phi_{n+1}\in(-\pi,\pi]
.

Let us denote the prediction error as
\Delta\beta=\beta-\beta^{*}
. When
\Delta\beta\approx 0
we have the approximation

\sin\!\left(\Delta\beta\Delta t_{n}\right)\approx\Delta\beta\Delta t_{n},

so the summands become exponential and quadratic in
\Delta t_{n}
, and their phases align, resulting in a large slope of the loss function. Thus, locally, non-vanishing time intervals make the magnitude of the gradient large, requiring a careful step-size selection near an optimum. Also, the magnitude of the predicted frequency
\beta
does not influence the local slope near the optimal solution, but is instead determined by the absolute error
\Delta\beta
. When the prediction error is large, the summand becomes highly oscillatory as a function of
\Delta t_{n}
, leading to phase cancellation across samples. However, this cancellation does not occur under regular sampling.

Under
non-vanishing regular sampling
, the loss landscape exhibits two distinct effects. Globally, the periodicity in
\beta
creates aliasing. The aliasing reflects a natural identifiability limit imposed by the sampling pattern. This ambiguity is not observed in the widely-adopted formulation of the EDMD family that solves a least-square problem. Since EDMD assumes a fixed time step, the same information loss is present, but potentially manifests as bias in the approximated operator rather than as periodicity in a loss landscape. Locally, phase alignment induces a steep valley near the true
\beta
, making optimization sensitive to step-size selection. Irregular sampling also produces this local geometry. This is consistent with the observation from
[
6
]
that a high precision line search method is effective in learning Koopman eigenvalues.

Irregular sampling
have additional effects. First, temporal irregularity breaks the periodicity in
Proposition

2
responsible for aliasing. This implies that we have better identifiability under irregular sampling times. This raises the possibility of learning high-frequency signals using a sampling rate lower than the one implied by the Shannon sampling theorem via irregular sampling, as in compressed sensing
[
5
]
.
Secondly, irregular sampling disrupts phase alignment in computing partial derivatives of
\beta
when
\Delta\beta
is non-vanishing. Hence, we would expect a gentler slope of loss landscape when our predictive error in
\beta
is large. A mixture of these effects will construct a loss landscape of the problem. We leave further analysis to future work.

V
Numerical Results

We test the proposed formulation under large time intervals and under high-frequency dynamics. The latter is designed to demonstrate the benefits of irregular sampling. In both experiments, we compare the spectrum learned from our new formulation with the one from gEDMD
[
12
]
. gEDMD approximates a Koopman semigroup generator via Galerkin method. We solve the problem (
6
) with a hybrid search over
(\alpha,\beta)
using a standard line search method and gradient descent with iterative eigen-decompositions, which is a modification of the numerical method from
[
6
]
.

V-A

Nonlinear system under coarse sampling

Consider the nonlinear system from
[
12
]

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1}(t)=\gamma x_{1}(t),

(9)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2}(t)=\delta\left(x_{2}(t)-x_{1}^{2}(t)\right),

(10)

where the eigenfunction capturing the nonlinear effect is
\mathfrak{e}(x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t))=\frac{2\gamma-\delta}{\delta}x_{2}(t)+x_{1}^{2}(t)
with associated eigenvalue
\lambda=\delta
. For the experiment, we set
(\delta,\gamma)=(-0.7,-0.8)
and use monomials up to order 4 without a constant term to reconstruct Koopman eigenfunctions. We generate 20 different trajectories from uniformly distributed initial states in
[-2,2]\times[-2,2]
. Then we sample data points from trajectories obtained from numerical integration with different time intervals
\{0.05,0.25,0.5\}
. Initial guesses
(\alpha,\beta)
are chosen from
[-3,1]\times[-1,1]
. The learned spectrum is shown in
Figure

1
.

V-B

High frequency dynamics and the effect of sampling

We test the proposed formulation with the harmonic oscillator:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=-\omega x_{2}(t)

(11)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\omega x_{1}(t).

(12)

The true Koopman eigenvalues are
i\omega
and
-i\omega
, and the system has a conserved Hamiltonian
0.5(x_{1}^{2}(t)+x_{2}^{2}(t))
at
\lambda=0
.
We let
\omega=50
and set

. We test the formulation under both regular and irregular sampling: over a time horizon of length 4, a time interval of

(\alpha,\beta)
are chosen from

6
) in
\beta
is shown in

For both cases of regular sampling, we observe periodicity and symmetry at
\beta=0
. However, for irregular sampling, only symmetry is observed. Consistent with our analysis, irregularity makes true Koopman frequencies more identifiable for numerical methods using first-order information. The learned spectrum reflects these properties, as shown in

VI
Conclusions

We formulate the problem of learning Koopman eigenfunctions from arbitrarily sampled observations as an optimization problem, extending
[
6
]
. The analysis reveals aliasing that reflects the identifiability limit, and also phase alignment effects. We test the formulation on canonical systems and show that it is robust, with greater precision than gEDMD in reconstructing the Koopman spectrum. The numerical results also support the idea that irregular sampling can improve the effectiveness of numerical methods. We expect that our results can inform optimization of Koopman-based methods, and broaden the applicability of Koopman operator theory to complex data.
