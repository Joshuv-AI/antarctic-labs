When LLM Judge Scores Look Good but Best-of-N Decisions Fail
Eddie Landesberg

Abstract
Large language models are often used as judges to score candidate responses, then validated with a single global metric such as correlation with reference labels. This can be misleading when the real deployment task is best-of- n n selection within a prompt. In a 5,000-prompt best-of-4 benchmark from Chatbot Arena, a judge with moderate global correlation ( r = 0.47 r=0.47 ) captures only 21.0% of the improvement that perfect selection would achieve over random choice. The gap arises because global agreement is driven largely by prompt-level baseline effects, while selection depends on within-prompt ranking: within-prompt correlation is only r within = 0.27 r_{\text{within}}=0.27 , and coarse pointwise scoring creates ties in 67% of pairwise comparisons. In a matched-pair best-of-2 audit, explicit pairwise judging recovers much of this lost signal, raising recovery from 21.1% to 61.2%. For judge-based selection, the relevant audit should report within-prompt signal, tie rates, and recovery/top-1 accuracy, not global agreement alone.

If you use judge scores for reranking or best-of-
n

The root cause is straightforward: global metrics mix prompt-level baseline agreement with within-prompt ranking. Selection needs the second, but global correlation is often dominated by the first. Coarse score bins make this worse: about 67% of pairwise comparisons are ties (
Figure

The same mismatch can appear with binary pairwise labels too;

Prompt difficulty is just one instance of this mechanism; any context-level factor that moves all candidates together can inflate global agreement. We treat the core question as one of
within-prompt decision validity
: can a judge identify the better candidate for a specific prompt?

Scope.

Our main results come from a large cross-policy benchmark (5,000 prompts). We also report a smaller within-policy fresh-draw pilot (24 prompts) to match the common deployment setup. The analysis is limited to one-step, fixed-policy proxy use: given a fixed judge, does proxy-based selection improve within-prompt decisions? Repeated optimization under policy shift is outside scope.

Contributions.

We make four practical contributions:

1.

Decision-centric audit
: We measure recovery and top-1 accuracy, not only global agreement.

2.

Within-vs-between decomposition
: We separate baseline agreement from true within-prompt ranking signal.

3.

Tie mechanism and pairwise audit
: We show how coarse pointwise scores create ties and test pairwise judging as a head-to-head fix.

4.

Cross-judge replication and deployment thresholds
: We replicate the pattern across judge families and translate within-prompt signal into rough recovery targets.

2
Setting and Task

We study best-of-
n
selection: given a prompt
x
and
n
candidate responses, select the response that maximizes oracle utility. Our strongest empirical claim is about fixed-judge inference-time reranking: sample several candidates for one prompt, score them, and pick one. Verifier-style search is directly analogous. RLHF-style training is only a mechanistic implication here, since prompt-conditional reward differences drive optimization
[
8
,
29
,
31
]
; we do not measure training dynamics directly. This deployment focus is increasingly reflected in evaluation benchmarks as well: recent work argues that listwise judge evaluation better matches optimization scenarios than pairwise-only meta-evaluation
[
39
]
.

Our main analysis uses a cross-policy candidate set (responses from different policies). In Appendix
B
, we also report a within-policy fresh-draw audit (multiple samples from one generator), which matches common deployment practice. The core requirement is the same in both settings: strong within-prompt ranking signal.

Best-of-
n
is useful when candidate quality varies meaningfully within a prompt and the selector can detect those differences. It is less useful when candidates are nearly identical, judge signal is weak or tie-heavy, or latency budgets force single-shot responses. This paper focuses on that boundary.

For one prompt, suppose four candidates have oracle quality 0.90, 0.70, 0.69, and 0.68. Oracle best-of-4 picks 0.90. If the judge ties all four at the top, deployed selection is effectively random, with expected quality about 0.74. The global dataset-level correlation can still look good if this same judge tracks easy prompts well. Best-of-
n
utility is determined by this local within-prompt separation, not by aggregate agreement alone.

Let
x\in\mathcal{X}
denote a prompt and
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
index candidate responses (from either different policies or multiple draws from one policy). Each response receives a judge score
S_{x,i}\in[0,100]
(normalized to
[0,1]
for analysis) and a reference label
O_{x,i}\in[0,1]
.

The decision problem is to select candidate
i^{*}=\argmax_{i}S_{x,i}
(random tie-break among equal maxima) and evaluate by oracle utility
O_{x,i^{*}}
.

We compare three selection strategies:

•

Oracle-optimal:
\argmax_{i}O_{x,i}
(best achievable)

•

Random: uniform selection (baseline)

•

Judge-greedy:
\argmax_{i}S_{x,i}
(deployed policy)

Best-of-
n
relies on relative ordering within prompt, not absolute calibration. A judge can look reasonable on average yet still fail at the one decision the deployment actually needs.

3
Metrics: From “Looks Correlated” to “Decision Useful”

Readers do not need every symbol in this section to follow the paper. The main path uses four quantities: global
r
(the usual sanity check), within-prompt
r
(the key signal diagnostic), recovery, and top-1 accuracy. The remaining metrics are supporting diagnostics.

3.1
Headline Metric (What People Usually Report)

Global correlation.

r=\Corr(S,O)
across all (prompt, candidate) pairs.
This standard sanity check mostly reflects agreement on context-level baseline effects and can exhibit Simpson/ecological-fallacy effects
[
34
,
33
]
. It does
not
directly answer whether the judge picks the right response
within a prompt
, because it conflates between-context and within-prompt association.

3.2
Decision Validity (What Optimization Needs)

For deployment, recovery and top-1 accuracy are the two end metrics. The other quantities in this section help diagnose why those numbers are high or low.

Top-1 accuracy (PCS
n
).

\Prob(\argmax_{i}S_{x,i}=\argmax_{i}O_{x,i})
with random tie-breaking for tied maxima, corresponding to probability of correct selection (PCS) in ranking-and-selection terms
[
3
]
.

Plain-language: how often the judge picks the oracle-best candidate.

Recovery rate.

\text{Recovery}=\frac{\E[O_{\text{judge}}]-\E[O_{\text{random}}]}{\E[O_{\text{oracle}}]-\E[O_{\text{random}}]}

(1)

Recovery
=0\%
means judge is no better than random;
100\%
means judge matches oracle.

Pairwise sign agreement.

\Prob(\text{sign}(\Delta S)=\text{sign}(\Delta O)\mid\Delta S\neq 0,\Delta O\neq 0)

Within-prompt Kendall
\tau
.

A rank-based complement to sign agreement:

\bar{\tau}_{\text{within}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x=1}^{N}\tau_{x}^{(b)}

(2)

where
\tau_{x}^{(b)}
is Kendall’s
\tau
-b for prompt
x

\bar{\tau}_{\text{within}}
captures whether the judge recovers the
full ranking
within each prompt.

We treat pairwise sign agreement and Kendall
\tau
as supporting diagnostics, not as standalone deployment targets. If you only remember one question from this section, it is: does the judge improve the final selection decision over random choice?

3.3
Supporting Tie Diagnostics

With discrete judge outputs, ties dominate. We therefore report both conditional agreement (
p_{\text{nt}}
on non-tied pairs) and tie-aware agreement (
p_{\text{eff}}
, counting unresolved ties as 50%). In practice,
p_{\text{nt}}
can look reasonable while
p_{\text{eff}}
remains near chance when tie rates are high.

Why tie-aware metrics matter.

If the judge often gives identical top scores, deployment falls back to random tie-breaking. Tie-aware metrics make this explicit.

3.4
Within-Between Decomposition

To formalize the gap between global agreement and local choice, we decompose scores into prompt effects and residuals:

\displaystyle S_{x,i}

\displaystyle=\mu^{S}_{x}+\varepsilon^{S}_{x,i}

(3)

\displaystyle O_{x,i}

\displaystyle=\mu^{O}_{x}+\varepsilon^{O}_{x,i}

(4)

where
\mu^{S}_{x}
and
\mu^{O}_{x}
are context-level means (capturing baseline effects; in this dataset, prompt-level effects), and
\varepsilon^{S}_{x,i}
and
\varepsilon^{O}_{x,i}
are candidate-specific deviations. Global
r
reflects correlation of both
\mu
(baseline effects) and
\varepsilon
(candidate quality). Optimization depends only on
\varepsilon
.

Binary/pairwise version (same mechanism).

The same logic holds when labels are binary choices rather than continuous scores. Aggregate agreement still splits into a between-context term and a within-context term. Let
J_{x,a,b},O_{x,a,b}\in\{0,1\}
indicate whether candidate
a
is preferred to
b
for prompt
x
by the judge and oracle, respectively. By the law of total covariance:

\Cov(J,O)=\Cov(\E[J\mid X],\E[O\mid X])+\E\!\left[\Cov(J,O\mid X)\right].

(5)

The first term is between-context coupling (baseline effects); the second is within-context directional signal.
Similarly, for a binary oracle:

\Var(O)=\Var\!\left(\Pr(O=1\mid X)\right)+\E\!\left[\Pr(O=1\mid X)\left(1-\Pr(O=1\mid X)\right)\right].

(6)

So even with binary labels, aggregate agreement can look strong when context-level baselines dominate, while within-prompt ordering remains weak.

Intuition.

Suppose many prompts are “easy” (one option is obviously better): judge and oracle agree there, inflating global agreement. But best-of-
n
utility is determined by “hard” prompts where candidates are close. If judge signal is weak on those hard prompts, decision quality stays low despite good aggregate numbers.

Data and Experimental Protocol

Dataset.

We analyze a 5,000-prompt sample drawn from Chatbot Arena prompts and response rows released through our upstream evaluation pipeline, with protocol context from the MT-Bench/Arena line
[
43
,
6
]
. For each prompt we have five candidate responses: two from the same Llama-3.3-70B helpful policy (one serves as a duplicate control), one from the same 70B model with a different system prompt, one from a larger Llama-405B helpful policy, and one deliberately bad 70B control. Our main best-of-4 analysis uses the four non-trivial candidates; the bad control is reserved for regime-sensitivity and control analyses. Each released response row carries an already-normalized reference score,
metadata.oracle_label

\in[0,1]
. This is the upstream response-level comparison target bundled with the released rows; in this paper, we take that released scalar as given and use it directly as
O_{x,i}
, with no additional Bradley–Terry refit, vote reconstruction, or post-hoc relabeling.

Model specifications.

The main 5,000-prompt analysis uses a fixed judge snapshot, GPT-5 (gpt-5-2025-08-07), with temperature 0 and 0–100 outputs. We then run a separate multi-judge audit on a shared 500-prompt sample (GPT-5.2, Claude Sonnet 4, GPT-4.1-mini, Gemini-2.5-flash, and Llama-3.3-70B; Section
6.1
), using per-judge complete-case filtering when a score is missing. The main benchmark’s reference labels are the stored upstream response-level scores described above. Evaluation prompts are in Appendix
O
.

Terminology.

To keep notation compact, we use
oracle
as shorthand for the reference signal in the experiment at hand: these stored response-level labels in the main benchmark, objective correctness on PPE-MATH, and GPT-5.2 labels in the appendix LLM-to-LLM and fresh-draw pilots.

Judge characteristics.

The judge emits only
\sim
20 unique score values (coarse discretization), resulting in a 66.5% tie rate on similar-candidate comparisons.

Statistical inference.

All confidence intervals are 95% via cluster bootstrap with prompt as the unit of resampling (1,000 resamples). This accounts for within-prompt dependence across candidates.

5.3
Tie Structure Is a Major Bottleneck

The judge emits only
\sim
20 unique values. This coarse discretization means most pairwise comparisons within a prompt produce identical scores. We distinguish pairwise ties (67%: any two responses receive identical scores) from top-1 margin ties (99%: the highest-scoring response ties with at least one other). The latter is more severe because best-of-
n
selection requires a unique winner. With 20 score bins and 4 candidates, ties at the top are nearly universal. On tied pairs, the judge cannot express direction; selection among tied options is effectively random.

5.4
Does Pairwise Judging Help?

A natural hypothesis is that the 66.5% tie rate reflects quantization, not fundamental inability to discriminate. Perhaps forcing the judge to make explicit pairwise comparisons (“Which is better, A or B?”) would recover the lost signal, consistent with recent pairwise ranking evidence for LLM evaluators
[
30
]
. At the same time, pairwise comparison is not universally safer: recent meta-evaluation work shows that comparative formats can also amplify evaluator biases depending on the task and prompt design
[
17
]
.

We test this on 7,000 stratified pairs using the same fixed judge snapshot (GPT-5, gpt-5-2025-08-07), comparing pointwise-derived preferences (from independent 0–100 scores) against an explicit pairwise prompt that allowed
A
,
B
, or
TIE
and also elicited a 1–5 confidence score.

The recovery rates in this subsection (Table

Accuracy increased monotonically with oracle gap size (from 66.3% on small gaps to 94.1% on large gaps), so the gains from reducing abstention outweighed occasional pairwise errors.

Discussion

7.1
Scope of the Evidence

This paper makes an evaluation-method contribution focused on within-prompt decision validity (directional validity). Aggregate judge agreement can overstate decision usefulness for best-of-
n
, and the paper provides diagnostics that directly target deployment decisions (within-prompt coupling, tie-aware metrics, recovery, and PCS).

The mechanism is broader than prompt difficulty alone: any context-level baseline effect that moves judge and oracle together can inflate global agreement without improving within-prompt choices (e.g., difficulty, topic/domain, response style/length, or candidate-set composition).

Our main evidence is the 5,000-prompt cross-policy benchmark. We also include a deployment-matched within-policy fresh-draw pilot (24 prompts) in Appendix
B
; treat that pilot as directional evidence rather than a definitive estimate.

The analysis covers the one-step, fixed-policy case of proxy use: given a fixed judge, does proxy-based selection improve within-prompt decisions? Repeated optimization pressure under policy shift is outside scope.

Some loss is strategy-dependent: in our setup, coarse pointwise scoring creates heavy ties, and explicit pairwise judging can recover substantial head-to-head signal (
Table

4
). But that gain is not universal: under strict best-of-4 round-robin with explicit token accounting, full pairwise underperforms pointwise for both a smaller judge (GPT-4.1-mini) and a stronger judge pilot (GPT-5) (
Table

16
). The core level-vs-decision gap is not eliminated by changing labeling style: it persists on PPE-MATH (objective binary correctness labels;
Table

12
), on external datasets (
Table

15
), and across judge families (
Table

8
). Labeling strategy is therefore a strong moderator, not a complete explanation.

7.2
Out of Scope

We measured offline selection with fixed judges. We did not run RLHF training loops or measure learning dynamics over updates. Any RLHF statement in this paper is therefore a motivation-by-analogy claim: if reward models in RLHF pipelines
[
8
,
29
]
have similar variance structure (high context-level baseline signal, weak within-prompt directional signal), optimization would inherit the same signal-quality bottleneck.

The evidence directly covers inference-time best-of-
n
and reranking decisions with fixed judges. Verifier pipelines that score many candidates and pick one are directly analogous. PPO-style RLHF and DPO-like preference optimization are mechanistically adjacent: learning depends on prompt-conditional reward differences, so weak within-prompt reward signal implies noisier or slower policy improvement even if global RM agreement is high
[
8
,
29
,
31
]
. Full training-loop verification remains future work.

7.3

For system-level tasks (ranking whole models, tracking leaderboard shifts), aggregate metrics can be the right target
[

6

25
]
. Recent meta-evaluation work likewise distinguishes system-ranking quality from instance-level best-output choice
[

7.5
Practical Checklist

1.

State the decision target. For system-level benchmarking, global metrics may be sufficient. For within-prompt selection, they are not.

2.

Report within-prompt metrics. Include
r_{\text{within}}
, tie rates, recovery, and PCS, not just global
r
.

3.

Audit in the hard regime. Use near-neighbor candidate sets that match deployment.

4.

Audit pairwise before deployment. In matched-pair best-of-2, pairwise sharply reduced ties and improved recovery; in a strict budgeted best-of-4 round-robin setting, it did not (
Tables

4
and

). The recent literature is similarly mixed: pairwise can improve comparison quality, but can also amplify evaluator biases
[

.

5.

Use explicit uncertainty for routing. Margin alone is weak here; CI width or resampling disagreement is stronger.

6.

Use explicit thresholds. In this regime,

).

7.6
Limitations and Falsification

•

Our main setting uses one judge scoring interface with coarse discretization (
\sim
20 bins).

•

The within-policy fresh-draw experiment is a small pilot (
n=24
prompts).

•

The stored reference labels are still proxies for “true” quality.

A counterexample would be a judge with similar global correlation but much higher within-prompt coupling and recovery under the same deployment-matched candidate regime.

8
Conclusion

Aggregate validity metrics can substantially overstate an LLM judge’s decision usefulness for within-prompt optimization. A judge can look acceptable on a single global metric and still make poor best-of-
n
choices. In our main cross-policy setting, a judge with
r=0.47
reaches only 21.0% recovery (
Table

2
). In our within-policy fresh-draw pilot (common deployment pattern), global agreement is
r_{\text{global}}=0.70
, but PCS
4
is only 27.4% and recovery is only 2.2% (
Table

14
).

As secondary extensions beyond the main decision-validity result, we provide concrete tools: within-between decomposition, tie-aware directional metrics, and candidate-similarity sensitivity audits. In our matched-pair best-of-2 audit, pairwise judging sharply reduces quantization ties in head-to-head comparisons, but strict best-of-4 budgeted audits show those gains are not automatic (
Table

16
). Teams still need deployment-matched best-of-
n
validation. Margin-only routing (“query the oracle when margin is small”) is weak in this dataset. Explicit uncertainty works better: confidence-interval width or resampling disagreement correlates with gain (
r\approx 0.26
) and captures 26% of optimal routing value.

This means the observed failure is not only a pointwise-scoring artifact: label strategy can improve performance materially, but the level-vs-decision gap can still persist under alternative labeling regimes.

This paper addresses one-step fixed-policy proxy use (selection/reranking with a fixed judge). Repeated optimization under policy shift is outside scope and left to future work.

Practitioners should report decision-centric metrics, audit in the hard regime that matches deployment, and use explicit uncertainty only when its extra cost is justified. A practical threshold from our results is that best-of-4 recovery becomes meaningfully useful around
r_{\text{within}}\approx 0.4
in this regime (
Table

6
). For low-stakes settings, random oracle allocation remains a strong baseline.

This result extends current judge evaluation practice rather than rejecting it: global metrics remain useful for system-level benchmarking, while optimization use cases need directional validation.

C.2
Why Margin Fails: The Cancellation Identity

Expected gain from querying the oracle decomposes as:

\E[\text{gain}\mid W]=\Pr(\text{judge wrong}\mid W)\times\E[\text{gap}\mid\text{wrong},W].

(7)

For margin to predict gain, it must correlate with at least one factor. We find:

1.

Higher margin
\to
judge slightly more often correct
(
r=+0.063
): a weak effect.

2.

Higher margin
\to
lower oracle-best value
(
r=-0.134
): some “confident” prompts are just uniformly low-quality prompts where all candidates are mediocre.

When the judge is wrong on a high-margin prompt, the loss is often larger. That larger loss roughly cancels the lower error rate, yielding
\text{Corr}(\text{margin},\text{gain})=-0.033
.

The U-shaped pattern.

\E[\text{gain}\mid\text{margin}]
is approximately U-shaped: highest at the extremes (ties and very high margins), lowest in the middle. This reflects a mixture of regimes:

•

Easy prompts
: clear winner, judge confident and correct
\to
high margin, low gain.

•

Hard prompts
: all mediocre, judge latches onto a heuristic
\to
high margin, but if wrong, large gap.

•

Ambiguous prompts
: no strong signal
\to
low margin, moderate gap.

Margin alone cannot separate “confident because easy” from “confident but wrong on a hard prompt.”

C.4
Practical Recommendations

1.

Don’t route on margin alone.
The intuition “query when uncertain” fails because margin conflates uncertainty with prompt difficulty.

2.

Add a difficulty proxy.
Combining margin with mean score (or similar) partially disentangles the signal.

3.

Consider improving the sensor.
Better uncertainty estimates may come from:

•

Multiple judge samples (disagreement as uncertainty)

•

Ensemble of different judges

•

Continuous scores instead of 20-bin quantization

4.

Random allocation is a strong baseline.

Key insight.

Confidence and oracle value are different targets. The judge may be somewhat calibrated about “am I right?” but poorly calibrated about “how much would oracle intervention help?” Margin mostly tracks the first, not the second.

Recommendation.

When routing value justifies extra cost, CI-width routing is much better than margin routing. Point estimates hide uncertainty that can be recovered with explicit elicitation or resampling.

Appendix K
Formal Results: When Correlation Fails to Predict Recovery

We formalize two key insights: (1) global correlation does not identify recovery, and (2) optimal routing depends on a quantity that may be unpredictable from observables.

K.1
Non-Identifiability of Recovery from Global Correlation

Proposition 1

(Non-Identifiability)
.

For any global correlation
r\in(0,1)
and any recovery targets
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in[0,1]
with
\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}
, there exist data-generating processes
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
such that:

1.

\mathrm{Corr}_{P_{1}}(S,O)=\mathrm{Corr}_{P_{2}}(S,O)=r

2.

\mathrm{Recovery}(P_{1})=\rho_{1}
and
\mathrm{Recovery}(P_{2})=\rho_{2}

Proof sketch.

Consider the multilevel decomposition:

\displaystyle S_{x,i}

\displaystyle=\mu^{S}_{x}+\varepsilon^{S}_{x,i},

(9)

\displaystyle O_{x,i}

\displaystyle=\mu^{O}_{x}+\varepsilon^{O}_{x,i}.

(10)

Global correlation decomposes as:

\mathrm{Corr}(S,O)=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(\mu^{S},\mu^{O})+\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon^{S},\varepsilon^{O})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(S)\cdot\mathrm{Var}(O)}}.

Recovery depends on within-prompt ranking, which is governed by
\varepsilon^{S},\varepsilon^{O}
and tie structure. By varying the ratio of between-prompt to within-prompt variance while holding the weighted sum constant, we can construct processes with identical global
r
but arbitrarily different within-prompt coupling
r_{\text{within}}=\mathrm{Corr}(\varepsilon^{S},\varepsilon^{O})
.

For example, let
P_{1}
have high between-prompt correlation but zero within-prompt coupling (recovery
\approx 0
), and let
P_{2}
have moderate both (high recovery). Both can achieve the same global
r
through appropriate variance scaling.
∎

Implication.

Global correlation alone cannot predict decision utility. Audits must separately measure within-prompt metrics.

K.2
Optimal Routing and Its Limits

Consider the routing problem: given budget
b
, choose query probability
\pi(W)
to maximize:

\max_{\pi:\E[\pi(W)]\leq b}\E\big[Y_{1}+\pi(W)\cdot(Y_{2}-Y_{1})\big],

where
Y_{1}
is oracle value of judge-selected response and
Y_{2}
is oracle-best value.

Proposition 2

(Optimal Routing)
.

Define
\Delta(W)=\E[Y_{2}-Y_{1}\mid W]
. The optimal router is:

\pi^{*}(W)=\mathbf{1}\{\Delta(W)\geq\tau\},

where threshold
\tau
is chosen to satisfy the budget constraint
\E[\pi^{*}(W)]=b
.

Proof.

The objective is linear in
\pi(W)
:

\E[Y_{1}]+\E[\pi(W)\cdot\Delta(W)].

Maximizing
\E[\pi(W)\cdot\Delta(W)]
subject to
\E[\pi(W)]\leq b
is a knapsack problem. The greedy solution queries prompts in decreasing order of
\Delta(W)
until budget is exhausted, which is equivalent to thresholding.
∎

Corollary 3

(Routing Bound)
.

Let
\sigma^{2}_{\Delta}=\mathrm{Var}(\Delta(W))
. The maximum improvement of any router over random allocation is:

V(\pi^{*})-V(\pi_{\text{rand}})\leq\sqrt{\sigma^{2}_{\Delta}}\cdot\sqrt{b(1-b)}.

If
\sigma^{2}_{\Delta}\approx 0
, all routers achieve approximately the same value as random allocation.

Application to our setting.

We observe
\mathrm{Var}(\Delta(W))/\mathrm{Var}(Y_{2}-Y_{1})\approx 0.03
when
W
includes margin and level. This means observables explain only 3% of gain variance, severely limiting any router’s improvement over random.

K.3
The Value-of-Information Calibration Gap

Define two calibration properties:

Definition 1

(Confidence Calibration)
.

A judge is
confidence-calibrated
if
\Pr(\text{correct}\mid\text{margin}=m)
is monotone increasing in
m
.

Definition 2

(VOI Calibration)
.

A judge is
VOI-calibrated
(value-of-information calibrated) if
\E[\text{gain}\mid\text{margin}=m]
is monotone decreasing in
m
.

Proposition 4

(Calibration Gap)
.

Confidence calibration does not imply VOI calibration. Specifically, if margin correlates with prompt difficulty (harder prompts
\to
higher margin), then a confidence-calibrated judge can fail VOI calibration.

Proof.

By the decomposition:

\E[\text{gain}\mid m]=\Pr(\text{wrong}\mid m)\times\E[\text{gap}\mid\text{wrong},m].

Confidence calibration ensures
\Pr(\text{wrong}\mid m)
decreases with
m
. But if
\E[\text{gap}\mid\text{wrong},m]
increases with
m
(because high-margin prompts are harder, leaving more room for improvement), the product can be non-monotone or even increasing.

In our data:
\mathrm{Corr}(\text{margin},\text{correct})=+0.063
(confidence-calibrated), but
\mathrm{Corr}(\text{margin},\text{gap}\mid\text{wrong})=+0.03
(harder prompts), yielding
\mathrm{Corr}(\text{margin},\text{gain})=-0.033\approx 0
.
∎

Implication.

A judge can “know when it is uncertain” (confidence calibration) without “knowing when querying the oracle helps” (VOI calibration). These are distinct properties, and margin captures the former but not the latter.

Appendix L
Formal Statement: Level vs Directional Validity

We formalize the distinction between level validity (addressed by CJE) and directional validity (this paper).

Decomposition.

Let
S_{x,i}
denote the judge score and
O_{x,i}
the oracle label for prompt
x
and candidate
i
:

\displaystyle S_{x,i}

\displaystyle=\mu^{S}_{x}+\varepsilon^{S}_{x,i}\quad\text{(Judge: prompt mean + candidate deviation)}

(11)

\displaystyle O_{x,i}

\displaystyle=\mu^{O}_{x}+\varepsilon^{O}_{x,i}\quad\text{(Oracle: prompt mean + candidate deviation)}

(12)

Global correlation

conflates two sources:

•

\mathrm{Corr}(\mu^{S},\mu^{O})
(agreement on prompt difficulty; between-prompt)

•

\mathrm{Corr}(\varepsilon^{S},\varepsilon^{O})
(agreement on within-prompt quality; within-prompt)

Definition 3

(Level Validity)
.

A judge satisfies level validity if
\E_{\pi}[f(S)]=\E_{\pi}[Y]
for some calibration function
f
. That is, calibrated judge scores yield unbiased policy value estimates.

Definition 4

(Directional Validity)
.

A judge satisfies directional validity if
\mathrm{Corr}(\varepsilon^{S}_{x,i},\varepsilon^{O}_{x,i})>\tau
for some threshold
\tau
. That is, within-prompt rankings are correct with sufficient reliability.

Theorem 5

(Independence of Validity Levels)
.

Level validity and directional validity are independent properties:

1.

A judge can satisfy level validity without directional validity: averaging over prompts cancels within-prompt errors, yielding unbiased policy estimates despite poor instance-level decisions.

2.

A judge can satisfy directional validity without level validity: correct within-prompt rankings do not imply calibrated absolute scores.

Why Averaging Helps Policy Estimation But Not Instance Selection.

For policy-level estimation:

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{V}(\pi))=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{x}\mathrm{Var}(S_{x,\pi(x)})=\frac{1}{n}\times\text{Average}(\mathrm{Var}(S_{x,i}))\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty

This is the standard
\sqrt{n}
convergence. Even with large individual variance, the policy mean converges.

For instance-level selection at a single prompt
x
:

P(\text{correct}\mid x)=f(r_{\text{within}},\text{tie rate})

There is no averaging: each prompt is a single decision. The full within-prompt noise directly determines decision quality.

Appendix M
Monotone Calibration Preserves Rankings

A natural question is whether score calibration could repair directional validity failures. The following lemma formalizes why monotone calibration methods, including the isotonic calibration used by CJE, cannot improve within-prompt rankings.

Lemma 6

(Monotone Calibration Preserves Argmax)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be strictly monotone increasing. For any set of scores
\{S_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
:

\arg\max_{i}f(S_{i})=\arg\max_{i}S_{i}

except for tie-breaking when
S_{i}=S_{j}
for some
i\neq j
.

Proof.

Suppose
i^{*}=\arg\max_{i}S_{i}
, so
S_{i^{*}}\geq S_{j}
for all
j
. By strict monotonicity of
f
:

•

If
S_{i^{*}}>S_{j}
, then
f(S_{i^{*}})>f(S_{j})

•

If
S_{i^{*}}=S_{j}
, then
f(S_{i^{*}})=f(S_{j})
(tie preserved)

Thus
f(S_{i^{*}})\geq f(S_{j})
for all
j
, with equality only when
S_{i^{*}}=S_{j}
.
∎

Implications.

This lemma has three key consequences:

1.

CJE compatibility:
CJE’s
monotone-only
isotonic calibration is monotone in the scalar score by construction. Lemma
6
explains why this mode achieves level validity without affecting within-prompt rankings: calibration corrects scale but preserves ordering. CJE’s two-stage calibration can incorporate response-varying covariates (e.g., length) and may re-rank candidates through the learned index; this can affect directional behavior, but is not guaranteed to improve it.

2.

Directional validity is orthogonal:
No monotone transformation of judge scores can improve within-prompt accuracy. Directional validity must come from the judge’s
raw ranking ability
, not post-hoc calibration.

3.

Ties as the exception:
The only case where calibration could affect rankings is tie-breaking. With 67% tie rates, this exception covers the majority of decisions, but random tie-breaking cannot systematically improve accuracy.

This formalizes a key insight:
level and directional validity are addressed by fundamentally different mechanisms
. Level validity can be repaired via calibration; directional validity cannot.

Setup.

Within a prompt with
n

Oracle utilities
O_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
i.i.d.

i.i.d.

\E[O_{i^{*}}]=\rho\cdot\E[\max_{i}O_{i}]

If the random baseline has

\text{Recovery}=\frac{\E[O_{i^{*}}]}{\E[\max_{i}O_{i}]}=\rho

In the idealized Gaussian/no-tie world, expected best-of-

Efficient Influence Functions for Decision Value Estimation

We develop the semiparametric efficiency theory for estimating decision value under partial oracle labeling
[
37
]
. This provides: (i) doubly robust estimators with valid confidence intervals, and (ii) a principled derivation of optimal oracle allocation. We test whether margin-based routing approximates the optimal design; empirically, it does not (
Appendix

D
).

Q.1
Setup and Notation

For each prompt
x
, we observe
K
candidate responses with judge scores
S_{1:K}
and (potentially missing) oracle utilities
O_{1:K}
. Let
W=(x,S_{1:K},\text{features})
denote the fully observed covariates.

Oracle labels are expensive, so we only observe them when queried:

•

R\in\{0,1\}
: indicator for whether oracle was queried for this prompt

•

\pi(W)=\Pr(R=1\mid W)
: query probability (known by design or modeled)

Observed data.

(W,R,R\cdot O_{1:K})
for
n
prompts.

Assumption (MAR by design).

R\perp O_{1:K}\mid W
. This holds when oracle allocation depends only on observed features (e.g., judge scores, margin).

Q.2
Decision Value Functional

A
selector

\delta:W\mapsto\{1,\ldots,K\}
maps observed features to a chosen candidate. Examples:

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{judge}}(W)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{i}S_{i}\quad\text{(judge-greedy)}

(13)

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{rand}}(W)

\displaystyle\sim\text{Uniform}\{1,\ldots,K\}\quad\text{(random)}

(14)

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{oracle}}(W)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{i}O_{i}\quad\text{(oracle-optimal, counterfactual)}

(15)

The
decision value
of selector
\delta
is:

V(\delta)=\E[O_{\delta(W)}].

(16)

This is the expected oracle utility when using
\delta
for selection, exactly what best-of-
n
optimizes.

Q.3
Efficient Influence Function for
V(\delta)

Let
m_{\delta}(W)=\E[O_{\delta(W)}\mid W]
be the conditional expectation of oracle utility given covariates.

Proposition 7

(EIF for Decision Value)
.

Under the nonparametric MAR model, the efficient influence function for
V(\delta)
is:

\phi_{\delta}(Z)=\frac{R}{\pi(W)}\Big(O_{\delta(W)}-m_{\delta}(W)\Big)+m_{\delta}(W)-V(\delta).

(17)

This is the standard missing-data EIF instantiated on the decision functional.

Doubly robust estimator.

The AIPW estimator
[
1
,
37
]
based on this EIF is:

\widehat{V}(\delta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left[\widehat{m}_{\delta}(W_{t})+\frac{R_{t}}{\widehat{\pi}(W_{t})}\Big(O_{\delta(W_{t})}-\widehat{m}_{\delta}(W_{t})\Big)\right].

(18)

Properties.

•

Double robustness
: Consistent if either
\widehat{\pi}
or
\widehat{m}_{\delta}
is correctly specified.

•

Semiparametric efficiency
: Achieves the efficiency bound when both nuisances are estimated at
o(n^{-1/4})
rates.

•

ML compatibility
: Cross-fitting yields
\sqrt{n}
-consistent inference with flexible ML estimators
[
5
]
.

Q.4
EIF for Recovery Rate

Recovery is defined as:

\text{Rec}=\frac{V(\delta_{\text{judge}})-V(\delta_{\text{rand}})}{V(\delta_{\text{oracle}})-V(\delta_{\text{rand}})}=\frac{\psi_{1}-\psi_{0}}{\psi_{2}-\psi_{0}}=:g(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2}).

(19)

Each
\psi_{k}=V(\delta_{k})
has EIF
\phi_{k}
of the form above. By the delta method, the EIF for recovery is:

\phi_{\text{Rec}}=\frac{\partial g}{\partial\psi_{0}}\phi_{0}+\frac{\partial g}{\partial\psi_{1}}\phi_{1}+\frac{\partial g}{\partial\psi_{2}}\phi_{2},

(20)

where the partial derivatives are:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\psi_{2}-\psi_{0}},

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\psi_{1}-\psi_{0}}{(\psi_{2}-\psi_{0})^{2}},

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial\psi_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}}{(\psi_{2}-\psi_{0})^{2}}.

(23)

This yields efficient estimation and asymptotically valid confidence intervals for recovery under oracle subsampling.

Q.5
Optimal Proxy Oracle Allocation

Consider estimating
\psi=\E[f(W,O)]
with the DR estimator. The asymptotic variance depends on the oracle query probability
\pi(\cdot)
.

Proposition 8

(Variance-Minimizing Design)
.

Subject to budget constraint
\E[\pi(W)]\leq b
, the variance-minimizing query probability is:

\pi^{*}(W)\propto\sqrt{\Var(f(W,O)\mid W)},

(24)

truncated to
[0,1]
with a Lagrange multiplier to satisfy the budget.

This is the Neyman allocation principle applied to decision evaluation
[
28
]
.

Connection to margin-based routing.

For
f(W,O)=O_{\delta_{\text{judge}}(W)}
, we might hypothesize that the conditional variance
\Var(O_{\delta(W)}\mid W)
is larger when:

•

The judge margin is small (uncertain which candidate is best)

•

Multiple candidates have similar judge scores (tie or near-tie)

If this held, margin-based routing would approximate the EIF-optimal design. However, this connection is
empirical, not guaranteed
: it requires that judge uncertainty actually predicts oracle outcome variance.

Empirical validation required.

Whether margin-based allocation achieves variance reduction depends on the data-generating process. In
Appendix

D
, we test this hypothesis and find that in our dataset,
\text{Corr}(\text{margin},\text{oracle variance})=0.162
(positive, not negative). Margin-based allocation does not reduce variance; oracle-optimal allocation (which uses the true conditional variance) achieves 45–77% reduction. This highlights the gap between theoretical optimality and practical proxies.

Q.6
Non-Regularity of the Optimal Selector

We emphasize that the above theory applies to evaluation of
fixed
selectors
\delta
, not inference on the
optimal
selector
\delta^{*}=\argmax_{\delta}V(\delta)
.

Inference on
\sup_{\delta\in\mathcal{D}}V(\delta)
or the argmax policy is
non-regular
in fully nonparametric models unless:

•

The policy class
\mathcal{D}
is restricted (finite or parametric)

•

A margin condition holds (no near-ties in oracle value)

•

The objective is smoothed (softmax instead of argmax)

For this reason, we focus on evaluating the
given
judge-based selector rather than claiming to find or do inference on an optimal routing rule.
