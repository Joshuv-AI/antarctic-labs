A message to Africa as Nigeria hosts the 5th Global High-Level Ministerial Conference on antimicrobial resistance
Authors: Godwin Pius Ohemu, Babafela Awosile, Kome Otokunefor, Chizaram Onyeaghala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The world is learning that no single nation can address the challenges posed by antimicrobial resistance (AMR) alone. For the first time, Africa is set to host the 5th Global High-Level Ministerial Conference on AMR, with Nigeria selected as the host country. For a continent where AMR causes more mortality per capita than anywhere else in the world, this is more than a diplomatic milestone; it is a call to action. This perspective examines what the conference aims to achieve, why Africa’s AMR challenge demands a distinctly African response, and the seven priority areas African governments, communities, and health systems must urgently address to turn this historic moment into lasting change.

Introduction

Across the world, there is a growing realization that some public health threats are too complex, too interconnected, and too devastating to be addressed by siloed national responses, no matter how wealthy a nation might be. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one such threat.

Resistant pathogens do not respect borders. They do not need immigration clearance to enter a new environment. Misuse of antibiotics in one country’s livestock sector can give rise to resistance genes that can be introduced into another country within 48 h, through international travel, global trade, and environmental pathways [1].

This reality gave rise to the Global High-Level Ministerial Conference on AMR. First convened in the Netherlands in 2014, the conference is a global collaborative platform that accelerates political commitments and coordinated actions to tackle the challenges of AMR. It has become a recurring global forum where ministries of health, agriculture, environment, and finance from different nations converge alongside United Nations (UN) agencies, researchers, civil society, and the private sector to translate global political declarations into cross-sectoral and cross-regional action.

Abuja, Nigeria, has been selected to host the 5th Global High-Level Ministerial Conference on AMR. This will be the first time this pivotal convening is held on African soil. For a region bearing the world’s highest per capita AMR mortality burden, this is more than a diplomatic achievement. Sub-Saharan Africa records 23.7 AMR-attributable deaths per 100,000 people, substantially higher than estimates for high-income regions like North America [2]. The Nigeria Center for Disease Control and Prevention (NCDC) reports that AMR causes 64,500 deaths annually, contributing to a 7% loss of gross domestic product (GDP) and an 11% decline in livestock productivity [3, 4]. A 2024 Africa CDC report highlighted that, without collective action, AMR-associated deaths on the continent could quadruple to 4.1 million per year by 2050 [5].

Hosting the 5th Global High-Level Ministerial Conference on AMR gives Africa something it rarely has in global health diplomacy: convening power. But the deeper question is what Africa will do with it. What makes this conference significant is not where it is held, but whether Africa will arrive with a coherent, continent-owned agenda that moves beyond receiving commitments to shaping them, and beyond political declarations to full-scale implementation.

Addressing this requires confronting a difficult truth: Africa’s AMR challenge has its own face, and it does not look like Europe’s or America’s. It is structurally different, shaped by pressures from antibiotic overuse and underuse, weak regulatory systems, environmental contamination, community-level transmission dynamics, and health systems stretched thin by limited resources. Deep socioeconomic determinants, including poverty, food insecurity, and economic vulnerability, compound these pressures. These factors drive communities toward antibiotic use as a rapid, affordable response to illness, even in the absence of a confirmed bacterial infection. In low-resource communities, when clean water is scarce and waste management is unreliable, infection rates rise, antibiotic demand increases, and resistant bacteria find fertile ground to grow. Any response that does not account for this complexity will fall short.

This perspective is written with the deliberate intention of contributing to an African-led Abuja discourse on what the continent must demand, commit to, and build toward, both pre- and post-conference. After the conference, this perspective challenges African leaders to be the authors of their own AMR futures, to be accountable to their own populations for the commitments made in, and to measure progress against the seven priority areas outlined here.

What Africa must rise to

High-income countries have invested heavily in enforceable regulations, national stewardship programmes, integrated surveillance, and stringent agricultural standards. While progress has been uneven and targets remain out of reach in many settings, these investments have produced measurable gains in specific resistance indicators that low- and middle-income countries have yet to replicate at scale [6]. Although socioeconomic contexts differ and similar investments may not automatically yield comparable results in African settings, these gains have hinged on strengthening institutional capacity, regulatory infrastructure, and health system functionality. Africa must chart its own path toward the same destination. But the route is different and, in many respects, more complex.

The most urgent priority for Africa is regulation and enforcement. But regulation and enforcement do not operate in a vacuum. They depend on a functioning health system, available diagnostics, and reliable pharmaceutical supply chains. Strengthening enforcement without these conditions risks restricting legitimate access to essential antimicrobials and driving people toward even more informal and unregulated channels. Across much of Africa, antibiotics remain available over the counter without a prescription, and national surveillance data, where it exists, rarely informs real-time policy decisions [7]. Strengthening and enforcing pharmaceutical regulations to ensure equitable access to medicines, aligning national AMR surveillance with WHO’s Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS), investing in water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure, and embedding stewardship within both public and private health facilities are not nice-to-haves; they are the essential pillars of an effective AMR response.

But regulation and enforcement alone address only part of the picture. AMR in Africa is also an environmental issue. Pharmaceutical effluents, hospital wastewater, agricultural runoff, and inadequate solid waste disposal systems create environmental reservoirs where resistance genes can accumulate and spread, often in the same waterways communities rely on for drinking, bathing, fishing, and irrigation [8].

Simultaneously, deficits in infection prevention and control (IPC) and in water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure mean that preventable infections continue to drive antibiotic demand [9]. These are not secondary concerns to be addressed later, after regulatory frameworks are in place; they are co-determinants of antibiotic demand and the spread of resistance and must be tackled side-by-side. In agricultural systems, for instance, unregulated antibiotic use in livestock and aquaculture, often as growth promoters rather than therapeutic agents, closes the One Health loop in ways that human health policy alone cannot address. Many smallholder farmers rely on antibiotics to compensate for the absence or inadequacy of veterinary services or biosecurity infrastructure. Africa’s AMR response must span these domains and address the underlying production pressures as a single, integrated challenge, rather than as parallel programmes managed by separate ministries.

This integration is where Africa’s lived experience of One Health becomes a genuine asset rather than a burden. In many African communities, people, animals, crops, and water sources share the same physical and economic space. This proximity creates AMR transmission pathways that high-income countries rarely encounter at the same scale. But it also means that well-designed community-level interventions can address multiple transmission routes simultaneously.

Africa’s dense networks of trusted intermediaries, including community health workers, traditional healers, religious leaders in churches and mosques, women’s groups, and local councils, provide a platform for behaviour change and awareness that no formal health system can replicate. Cultural practices around self-medication, antibiotic sharing, and illness management are deeply embedded and require engagement rather than condemnation. When faith and community leaders speak about responsible antibiotic use, they reach households that surveillance systems may never see.

Africa is also the world’s youngest continent, a fact that is both a challenge and a profound strategic opportunity. Integrating AMR literacy into school curricula, nurturing youth-led advocacy networks, and investing in the next generation of African AMR researchers, epidemiologists, and innovators must be explicit. There must be real commitments, with funding behind them, not good intentions buried in closing statements. Young Africans are already engaging with AMR through science communication, community health volunteering, and digital advocacy. This energy requires institutional support and recognition [10].

Underlying all of this is the need for robust biosecurity at the intersection of human health, animal production, food safety, and environmental management. From live animal markets and cross-border livestock corridors to informal slaughterhouses and peri-urban wetlands, Africa’s biosecurity infrastructure must be strengthened and harmonized across national boundaries [11]. The Africa CDC and the African Union’s Inter-African Bureau for Animal Resources are well-positioned to lead this continental coordination. Still, member states must provide the political leadership and financing to make it operational. Sub-regional bodies, including the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS), the West African Health Organization (WAHO), and the Southern African Development Community (SADC), also have a critical role to play in mobilizing resources and incentives, harmonizing surveillance standards, and coordinating responses across neighboring countries that share borders, ecosystems, and transmission pathways.

Finally, none of the above is sustainable without political commitment and reliable domestic financing. The 2024 Jeddah Commitments recognized the need for sustainable financing for AMR and for equitable access to antimicrobials and diagnostics. Yet the gap between commitment and implementation remains wide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [12].

In Abuja, African governments must arrive not merely as participants in a global process but as advocates for a specific outcome: an Abuja declaration with measurable targets, named accountabilities, and independent review mechanisms. Beyond the conference hall, the work is legislative and budgetary. African nations must commit to ensuring that AMR has a line item in national budgets, a place in national law, and a champion in every cabinet. African governments must increase domestic investment in AMR infrastructure. Surveillance systems cannot be built on goodwill alone. This conference is an opportunity for African nations to make public commitments to their populations.

Conclusion

The 5th Global High-Level Ministerial Conference on AMR arrives on African soil at a moment of genuine possibility. Africa is not coming to Abuja as a supplicant; she comes as an equal and important global player, with the most complex AMR landscape and, within that complexity, some of the most instructive lessons for the world. Winning the fight against AMR in Africa and all over the world will require regulation and stewardship, environmental action, IPC and WASH investment, community engagement, youth mobilization, biosecurity, and political will, pursued not as a checklist but as a single, coherent strategy. Abuja is the platform. What Africa chooses to build on it will define the continent’s contribution to one of this century’s defining public health challenges.
