Divergent responses of soil organic and inorganic carbon driven by land use during coastal reclamation
Authors: Jiahao Zhai, Lijie Pu, Sihua Huang, Jiawei Tao, Chaosheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coastal reclamation fundamentally transforms natural tidal flat ecosystems into managed terrestrial landscapes, initiating profound shifts in soil physicochemical properties and carbon dynamics; understanding how reclamation history and land use jointly regulate soil carbon fraction dynamics is essential for evaluating carbon sequestration potential in rapidly expanding reclaimed coastlines. Here, we show that reclamation induces progressive desalination, surface dealkalization, nutrient enrichment, and texture refinement; soil organic carbon increases markedly across reclamation stages, exhibiting strong surface accumulation under woodland and cropland, with mineral-associated organic carbon as the dominant fraction and organic carbon increasingly stable; soil inorganic carbon remains stable during early reclamation but declines markedly in surface layers of long-term reclaimed terrestrial soils while remaining high in aquaculture systems; and organic carbon fractions are primarily associated with nutrient availability and clay content whereas inorganic carbon dynamics are closely linked to soil pH and salinity.

Introduction

Coastal zones are among the most dynamic and productive ecosystems on Earth, providing a wide range of ecological services, including carbon sequestration, nutrient cycling, and food production1–3. However, rapid population growth and economic development have driven extensive coastal reclamation worldwide, particularly in East and Southeast Asia (e.g., the east coast of China)4–6. Large areas of natural tidal flats have been converted into cropland, aquaculture ponds, and other land use types, profoundly altering soil properties and biogeochemical processes7–9. Understanding how coastal reclamation and subsequent land use affect soil physicochemical properties and carbon dynamics is, therefore, critical for evaluating ecosystem functioning and carbon sequestration potential in reclaimed coastal regions.

Soil carbon is a fundamental regulator of soil fertility, structural stability, and ecosystem sustainability10,11. While soil carbon is universally composed of organic (SOC) and inorganic (SIC) forms, the SIC pool often represents a significantly larger and more dominant portion of the total carbon (TC) stocks in coastal regions than in many other terrestrial ecosystems3,12. These two pools exhibit divergent sensitivities to environmental shifts and land use management. Furthermore, SOC is not a homogeneous reservoir but consists of functionally distinct fractions with contrasting turnover rates. Particulate organic carbon (POC) is a labile fraction that responds rapidly to vegetation inputs and soil disturbance, whereas mineral-associated organic carbon (MAOC) constitutes a more stable pool protected by associations with soil minerals13,14. This mineral protection confers long-term stability through multiple mechanisms: physical inaccessibility of organic matter occluded within soil aggregates or adsorbed onto mineral surfaces restricts microbial access; chemical bonding between organic molecules and reactive mineral surfaces, such as iron and aluminum oxides, forms organo-mineral complexes resistant to enzymatic degradation; and the resulting reduction in decomposer activity substantially prolongs carbon residence times compared to unprotected particulate fractions15,16. Given the preponderance of SIC and the functional diversity of SOC, disentangling their respective responses to reclamation is essential for accurately predicting long-term carbon stabilization in coastal landscapes.

Coastal reclamation initiates a series of pedogenic and biogeochemical changes, including desalination, soil structure development, and shifts in redox conditions, which collectively influence soil physicochemical properties such as salinity, pH, texture, and nutrient availability17–19. These changes typically evolve over timescales of decades to centuries, leading to pronounced differences between unreclaimed tidal flats, recently reclaimed soils, and long-term reclaimed soils20–22. Previous studies have shown that reclamation age can strongly affect total SOC and SIC stocks; however, results remain inconsistent due to differences in climate, parent material, management practices, and land use types22,23. In particular, how reclamation history interacts with land use to regulate soil carbon fractions remains poorly understood.

Land use following reclamation further modifies soil carbon dynamics through variations in vegetation type, organic matter inputs, hydrological conditions, and management intensity24,25. Croplands planted with cereals or oil crops, aquaculture ponds subjected to periodic flooding and sediment disturbance, forests with perennial vegetation cover, and abandoned or barren lands can exhibit markedly different soil physicochemical environments. These land use specific effects may be amplified or constrained by reclamation history, as soils at different reclamation stages differ in salinity levels, mineral composition, and structural development26,27. Nevertheless, most existing studies have examined either the effects of reclamation age or land use in isolation, with limited attention to their interactive effects. In particular, few studies have simultaneously compared unreclaimed tidal flats, recently reclaimed areas, and long-term reclaimed lands under multiple land use types while explicitly considering soil carbon fraction into POC and MAOC, together with SIC22,28. Such an integrated approach is crucial for identifying the mechanisms controlling soil carbon accumulation and stabilization during long-term coastal reclamation. A clearer understanding of these processes will also inform land use planning and management strategies aimed at enhancing soil quality and carbon sequestration in reclaimed coastal regions.

Therefore, the objectives of this study were to (1) characterize the soil physicochemical properties across unreclaimed, recently reclaimed, and long-term reclaimed coastal soils under different land use types; (2) quantify SOC, SIC, and key SOC fractions (POC and MAOC) along reclamation stages; (3) evaluate the interactive effects of reclamation history and land use on soil carbon pools and fraction distribution; and (4) provide science-based recommendations for sustainable carbon management in coastal reclaimed lands. We hypothesized that reclamation history and land use jointly regulate soil carbon dynamics, with SOC and SIC exhibiting distinct response patterns across reclamation stages. Given the global extent of coastal reclamation and the urgent need for nature-based climate solutions, elucidating the mechanisms by which reclamation history and land use regulate soil carbon fraction dynamics will provide the scientific foundation for evidence-based land use planning and carbon management policies in reclaimed coastal ecosystems, with direct implications for achieving national and international climate change mitigation targets.

Results

Reclamation progressively reduces salinity and induces surface dealkalization in terrestrial soils

Soil pH exhibited distinct patterns across the reclamation chronosequence and among land use types (Fig. 1a). In terms of temporal evolution, soil pH increased significantly from the tidal flat to the newly reclaimed area, particularly in the barren land where pH remained at high levels (8.51–8.78) throughout the profile. As the reclamation period extended to the old reclaimed area, although the overall soil remained alkaline, a distinct “surface dealkalization” pattern emerged. Soil salinity exhibited a highly significant downward trend with the increasing duration of reclamation chronosequence (Fig. 1b). The pristine tidal flats possessed the highest salinity content, characterized by a typical surface accumulation pattern. During the newly reclaimed stage, the impacts of different land use types on salinity became sharply differentiated: barren land maintained high-salinity surface accumulation similar to tidal flats, though deep-soil salinity decreased notably. In contrast, salinity in wheat and rapeseed fields within the newly reclaimed areas significantly dropped to below 0.28 and 0.10 g kg−1, respectively, reaching the desalination levels of old reclaimed areas ahead of schedule; this highlights the strong accelerative effect of agricultural development on initial desalination. Meanwhile, salinity in newly reclaimed aquaculture ponds was markedly higher than that in wheat and rapeseed fields. In the old reclaimed stage, salinity across most plots entered a stable low-value phase, achieving full profile desalination; however, the salinity content in aquaculture ponds during this stage remained significantly higher than that of other concurrent land use types.

Soil nutrient dynamics show contrasting responses to reclamation age and land use type

Total nitrogen (TN) exhibited a strong reclamation chronosequence effect and surface enrichment characteristics (Fig. 2a). Temporally, as the tidal flat evolved into the newly reclaimed area, TN remained at low levels, except for a slight increase in the surface layers of wheat (0.57 g kg−1) and rapeseed lands (0.62 g kg−1). Upon reaching the old reclaimed area, TN concentrations increased significantly, particularly in the 0–20 cm layer, where values for woodland and wheat peaked at approximately 1.66 and 1.42 g kg−1, respectively. Vertically, the old reclaimed area displayed a distinct “surface-accumulation” pattern, with surface TN being significantly higher than that in deeper layers (40–60 cm). In contrast, TN in the tidal flat and newly reclaimed barren land was uniformly distributed across the profile, reflecting the significant role of aquaculture, cropping, and forest cover in promoting surface TN accumulation over time. Total phosphorus (TP) content showed an overall increasing trend with the duration of reclamation, though the trend was not uniformly progressive across all land use types (Fig. 2b). The tidal flat exhibited the lowest TP levels (0.42–0.46 g kg−1). TP increased notably in barren land and wheat land, while aquaculture remained comparable to the tidal flat, suggesting that the early elevation of TP was not solely attributable to crop cultivation. In the old reclaimed area, TP levels across most land use types were significantly higher than those in the newly reclaimed area and tidal flat, with surface (0–20 cm) enrichment becoming more prevalent. Notably, old reclaimed wheat land showed the highest surface TP. Compared to TN and TP, Total potassium (TK) exhibited less fluctuation across reclamation years and land use types, generally remaining within the range of 11.02–18.42 g kg−1 (Fig. 2c). TK distribution in the tidal flat and newly reclaimed area was relatively stable. In the old reclaimed area, except for a notable increase in surface TK in wheat fields (approaching 18.42 g kg−1), TK levels in other land use types changed limitedly compared to the newly reclaimed area. Available nitrogen (AN) content exhibited a significant stepwise enrichment pattern with increasing reclamation years (Fig. 3a). Along the temporal gradient, AN levels remained generally low from the tidal flat to the newly reclaimed area, except for a slight increase in the surface layer of rapeseed land (44.70 mg kg−1). Upon entering the old reclaimed area, AN concentrations underwent a marked increase, and the vertical distribution shifted from uniform to a strong “surface-accumulation” pattern. Specifically, surface AN in woodland and rapeseed land peaked at approximately 95.45 and 84.15 mg kg−1, respectively. In the newly reclaimed area, the influence of agricultural land use (especially rapeseed) on AN was already evident, although concentrations remained significantly lower than those in the old reclaimed area. The evolution of available phosphorus (AP) content was highly dependent on land use types (Fig. 3b). The tidal flat exhibited extremely low AP levels (2.79–3.39 mg kg−1). Within the newly reclaimed area, significant differentiation was observed among land use types: AP levels in the surface layers of barren land and aquaculture land were significantly higher than those in wheat and rapeseed lands. In the old reclaimed area, overall AP levels further increased with more pronounced vertical differentiation. Woodland exhibited the highest surface AP (35.29 mg kg−1), while aquaculture land also maintained high AP levels (19.52 mg kg−1) during this stage. The trend for available potassium (AK) was markedly different from that of AN and AP (Fig. 3c). Temporally, the tidal flat showed the highest AK content (351.28–414.13 mg kg−1), which significantly decreased with increasing reclamation years. Within the newly reclaimed area, land use types caused major differences in the rate of AK decline: barren and aquaculture lands maintained high AK levels (around 182.51–299.63 mg kg−1), whereas AK in wheat and rapeseed lands dropped sharply to 89.89–114.63 mg kg−1. In the newly reclaimed area, significant differentiation emerged among land use types: sand content in barren land dropped sharply to below 19.22%, while aquaculture land remained at high levels (41.46–45.66%) similar to the tidal flat. Silt was the dominant fraction of soil texture (44.18%–72.43%) (Fig. 4b). Along the temporal gradient, the proportion of silt increased significantly from the tidal flat to the newly reclaimed area, with barren land exhibiting extremely high silt levels (66.41%–72.43%) across the profile. Within the newly reclaimed area, silt content in wheat and rapeseed lands was already significantly higher than in the tidal flat, reaching old-reclaimed-area levels ahead of time, while aquaculture land remained lower. In the old reclaimed area, silt content varied considerably across land use types, peaking in the woodland surface layer (70.20%). Vertical distribution of silt was relatively uniform across most land use types in the old reclaimed area, with the exception of wheat and aquaculture.

Clay content showed an initial increase followed by stabilization over the reclamation chronosequence (Fig. 4c). The tidal flat had the lowest clay levels (<7.66%). Within the newly reclaimed area, differentiation was prominent: surface clay in barren, wheat, and rapeseed lands increased rapidly to 12.81%–15.30%, significantly higher than in aquaculture land (5.86%). By the old reclaimed area stage, woodland surface clay remained high (15.31%), while other cultivated areas stabilized between 6.28% and 11.13%. Notably, old reclaimed woodland exhibited a distinct surface-enrichment pattern, with clay content in the surface layer (0–20 cm) significantly exceeding that in the 20–60 cm depth.

Along the temporal gradient, the tidal flat and newly reclaimed barren land showed the lowest SOC levels (1.03–2.18 g kg−1) with uniform profile distribution. By the old reclaimed area stage, SOC experienced marked growth and a strong “surface-accumulation” pattern. The evolution of SIC followed a starkly different pattern compared to SOC (Fig. 5b). Temporally, the tidal flat and newly reclaimed area maintained generally high SIC levels (7.78–11.03 g kg−1) with relatively stable distribution across soil layers. Within the newly reclaimed area, aquaculture land exhibited the highest SIC level (9.80–11.03 g kg−1). However, upon reaching the old reclaimed area, SIC content decreased significantly, with a pronounced depletion occurring in the surface layer (0–20 cm). In old reclaimed wheat, woodland, and faba bean lands, surface SIC dropped to its lowest values (1.72–4.52 g kg−1), displaying a pattern that increased with soil depth. In contrast, aquaculture in the old reclaimed area maintained relatively high SIC levels (9.38–9.95 g kg−1).

TC exhibited distinct patterns across reclamation stages and land use types (Fig. 5c). Among long-term reclaimed land use types, only aquaculture systems showed TC levels significantly higher than those of the unreclaimed tidal flat. Old reclaimed woodland exhibited strong surface TC enrichment but reduced TC in deeper layers, reflecting the divergent vertical distributions of SOC and SIC. In cultivated lands, SOC gains were insufficient to fully offset SIC losses, resulting in a slight but modest decline in TC relative to pre-reclamation levels.

Reclamation drives a progressive shift toward more stable soil organic carbon pools

POC content exhibited a clear accumulation trend with increasing reclamation years and was significantly driven by land use types (Fig. 6a). From a temporal perspective, POC levels in pristine tidal flats were extremely low (0.56–0.92 g kg−1) with a uniform profile distribution. Upon entering the newly reclaimed areas, land use practices initiated a preliminary divergence in POC: topsoil (0–20 cm). POC in wheat and rapeseed fields accumulated most rapidly, significantly surpassing contemporaneous barren and aquaculture lands. In the old reclaimed areas, POC levels increased further and displayed strong surface enrichment. MAOC content showed leapfrog growth along the temporal gradient (Fig. 6b). MAOC levels in pristine tidal flats and newly reclaimed barren lands were minimal, with stable vertical distributions. Within the newly reclaimed zone, divergent characteristics became prominent: topsoil MAOC in wheat and rapeseed fields was significantly higher than in barren land. In old reclaimed areas, MAOC became the primary component of the soil carbon pool growth. Substantial land use differentiation was observed: topsoil MAOC in woodlands surged to approximately 11.83 g kg−1, roughly three times that of contemporaneous faba bean and rapeseed fields. Furthermore, aquaculture lands in the old zone exhibited a unique vertical distribution, with MAOC accumulation in deep layers (40–60 cm) significantly exceeding that of other land use types.

The POC:MAOC ratio exhibited a significant overall downward trend with increasing reclamation duration (Fig. 6c). The highest ratios were observed in pristine tidal flats (topsoil > 0.73). In newly reclaimed areas, the ratio declined to 0.25–0.55 as the accumulation rate of MAOC began to outpace that of POC. Differentiation intensified within the old reclaimed zone: the full profile ratios for woodlands and aquaculture lands dropped to their lowest and most stable levels (0.25–0.36), reflecting a transition toward a stable organic carbon structure dominated by mineral-protected states. In contrast, faba bean fields in the old zone maintained a higher POC:MAOC ratio in deeper layers, suggesting that their deep-layer carbon pools remain more active than those of other contemporaneous land use types. The POC:MAOC ratio significantly and progressively declined during the reclamation process (P < 0.001), from 0.61 in unreclaimed tidal flats to 0.42 in the newly reclaimed area, and further to 0.33 in the old reclaimed area. This persistent reduction in the ratio demonstrates that increasing reclamation age leads to enhanced SOC stability (Supplementary Table 1, 2).

Discussion

Pedogenic evolution induced by coastal reclamation and its consequences for soil physicochemical properties

Coastal reclamation represents a fundamental transformation from marine sedimentary environments to terrestrial soil systems, initiating a cascade of pedogenic, hydrological, and biogeochemical processes29,30. Globally, approximately 16% of the world’s tidal flats were lost between 1984 and 2016, with the most severe losses concentrated in Asia31. In China alone, anthropogenic reclamation of coastal wetlands caused a carbon loss of approximately 3720 Gg C between 1980 and 202032, underscoring the scale and pace at which coastal marine environments are being converted to managed terrestrial landscapes and highlighting the importance of understanding the biogeochemical consequences of such transformations. The present study reveals that reclamation history exerts a dominant control over soil physicochemical properties, while land use further modulates these trajectories, jointly shaping soil carbon dynamics. One of the most pronounced reclamation-induced changes observed in this study is the progressive decline in soil salinity. Pristine tidal flats exhibited typical surface salt accumulation due to frequent tidal inundation and evaporation. Following reclamation, hydrological isolation from seawater combined with freshwater irrigation and drainage facilitated rapid salt leaching, especially in cultivated lands. The accelerated desalination in newly reclaimed wheat and rapeseed fields highlights the critical role of agricultural management in modifying soil chemical environments. Similar patterns have been reported in other reclaimed coastal regions of eastern China, where irrigation intensity and cropping systems strongly influence the rate of soil desalination33,34. This acceleration likely results from a combination of interacting mechanisms. During the early stages of reclamation, soil amelioration measures commonly applied in this region, including the application of imported topsoil, biochar amendments, and the installation of subsurface drainage pipes, substantially reduce soil salinity by improving soil structure, enhancing water infiltration, and facilitating salt leaching. The establishment of crop canopies further alters evapotranspiration partitioning by reducing direct soil evaporation, thereby suppressing the upward capillary movement of saline groundwater that drives surface salt accumulation in bare lands35. Tillage operations disrupt soil capillary continuity and promote macropore formation, facilitating downward salt leaching under natural rainfall36,37. While the relative contributions of these individual processes were not directly quantified in the present study, their combined effects are consistent with the significantly lower salinity levels observed in wheat and rapeseed fields compared to contemporaneous barren and aquaculture lands. It should be noted, however, that accelerated desalination represents an ecological trade-off: while it facilitates rapid soil development and agricultural productivity, the progressive loss of saline conditions simultaneously threatens halophytic vegetation communities and salt-tolerant ecosystems that depend on the maintenance of specific salinity regimes, with potential consequences for coastal biodiversity and the ecosystem services these habitats provide38. In contrast, aquaculture lands maintained relatively high salinity throughout the reclamation chronosequence, reflecting periodic seawater input and limited freshwater flushing. This persistent salinity not only differentiates aquaculture soils from terrestrial land uses but also has far-reaching implications for nutrient availability and carbon stabilization.

Soil pH dynamics further illustrate the progressive terrestrialization of reclaimed soils. The initial alkalization observed in newly reclaimed soils likely results from the dominance of carbonate-rich marine sediments and reduced leaching intensity during early reclamation33. Over longer timescales, however, cultivated and forested soils in the old reclaimed area exhibited clear surface dealkalization. This phenomenon can be attributed to several interacting processes, including organic matter accumulation, root respiration, enhanced CO₂ production from microbial decomposition, and the nitrification of ammonium-based nitrogen derived from the routine application of urea and compound fertilizers in cultivated fields of the study area18,27. The absence of pronounced vertical pH stratification in aquaculture soils underscores the buffering effect of alkaline water and carbonate inputs, reinforcing the strong land use control on soil chemical evolution39.

Changes in soil texture along the reclamation gradient further indicate active pedogenesis40. The gradual enrichment of silt and clay fractions in reclaimed soils reflects sediment redistribution, aggregation, and selective erosion processes, as well as anthropogenic disturbance41. The rapid decline in sand content observed in newly reclaimed barren land is attributable to a combination of tillage-induced fine particle redistribution, cessation of tidal coarse sediment supply following hydrological isolation, and continuous fine particle accumulation through atmospheric deposition and surface runoff over more than a decade since reclamation. The subsequent stabilization of clay content in old reclaimed soils reflects the attainment of a new pedogenic equilibrium where fine particle inputs are balanced by increasing retention capacity through organo-mineral associations. Notably, surface clay content in old reclaimed woodland was significantly higher than in other land use types, suggesting that long-term vegetation cover enhances clay retention through root system stabilization of aggregates, strengthened organo-mineral associations, and reduced surface erosion under canopy protection. The absence of significant vertical variation in clay content across other land use types argues against illuviation as a dominant process, supporting a model in which clay accumulation is driven primarily by selective surface deposition and biological-physical processes rather than downward clay translocation. Fine particles, enriched in illite-dominated phyllosilicate clay minerals and pedogenic iron and aluminum oxides42,43, provide a larger specific surface area and more reactive mineral sites essential for nutrient retention and organo-mineral complex formation. Consequently, texture evolution constitutes a critical physical foundation for subsequent SOC stabilization.

Biogeochemical mechanisms underlying nutrient enrichment and depletion across land use types

The surface TN enrichment observed across old reclaimed land use types reflects fundamentally different nitrogen cycling pathways that warrant separate consideration. In woodland soils, continuous litterfall and root turnover provide sustained organic nitrogen inputs, while reduced physical disturbance promotes nitrogen retention through microbial immobilization and organo-mineral associations44. In croplands, repeated fertilization and crop residue incorporation drive surface TN enrichment, with root-derived nitrogen contributing to subsurface pools45,46. In aquaculture systems, by contrast, TN dynamics are governed by fundamentally different mechanisms: feed inputs represent an allochthonous nitrogen source that is partially mineralized in the water column and deposited as organic-rich sediments, while the prevalence of anaerobic conditions promotes denitrification losses that partially offset nitrogen accumulation47. These contrasting mechanisms explain why surface TN enrichment was less pronounced in aquaculture lands compared to woodland and cropland systems, and underscore the importance of treating terrestrial and aquatic land use types as biogeochemically distinct systems when interpreting nutrient dynamics in reclaimed coastal soils. The surface TK enrichment observed in old reclaimed wheat fields is attributable to the combined effects of long-term fertilization practices and root cycling. Farmer interviews conducted in the study area confirmed that wheat cultivation relies primarily on ternary nitrogen-phosphorus-potassium compound fertilizers supplemented by nitrogen-dominant fertilizers such as urea, with potassium introduced as a constituent of compound fertilizers rather than as a standalone input. The cumulative effect of repeated compound fertilizer applications over more than 70 years of cultivation, combined with the redistribution of potassium from deeper soil layers to the surface through root uptake and crop residue decomposition48, has driven progressive surface TK accumulation. Notably, potassium inputs via compound fertilizer are relatively modest compared to nitrogen inputs, suggesting that this enrichment reflects long-term cumulative management effects rather than intensive potassium-specific fertilization.

The higher AP concentrations observed in newly reclaimed barren land and aquaculture surface soils compared to croplands likely reflect the contrasting interplay between phosphorus sorption, plant uptake, and land use-specific inputs. In croplands, active root systems facilitate phosphorus uptake, while tillage-induced disturbance enhances contact between soluble phosphorus and reactive mineral surfaces, promoting sorption onto iron and aluminum oxides and collectively reducing AP49. In barren lands, the absence of vegetation allows phosphorus released from weathering and atmospheric deposition to accumulate without biological removal, resulting in elevated AP concentrations. In aquaculture systems, feed-derived phosphorus inputs and organic matter decomposition continuously release soluble phosphorus into surface sediments50, with accumulation further promoted by the reducing conditions that limit phosphorus re-adsorption onto mineral surfaces, thereby maintaining high AP levels. The striking decline in AK from tidal flats to reclaimed areas likely reflects a combination of mechanisms rather than a single process. Marine-derived soluble and exchangeable potassium in tidal flat soils represents a substantial legacy input accumulated through long-term tidal inundation, which is progressively depleted following reclamation through rainfall-driven leaching and crop uptake and removal in cultivated systems. However, a dilution effect may also contribute to the apparent AK concentration decline: increasing organic matter accumulation and textural refinement along the reclamation gradient increase total soil mass per unit volume22, potentially reducing AK concentration per unit weight even without absolute potassium loss. The accelerated AK decline in wheat and rapeseed fields compared to barren and aquaculture lands supports the dominant role of crop removal in driving potassium depletion under intensive cultivation51, superimposed on this background dilution effect.

Mechanistic interpretation of divergent soil organic and inorganic carbon trajectories

A central finding of this study is the fundamentally divergent responses of SOC and SIC to reclamation history and land use. While SOC exhibited continuous accumulation with reclamation age, SIC followed an opposite trend, particularly in surface layers of long-term reclaimed terrestrial soils. SOC accumulation reflects a shift from carbon-poor marine sediments to biologically active terrestrial systems. In tidal flats and newly reclaimed barren lands, SOC levels remained uniformly low throughout the soil profile, indicating limited organic inputs and strong environmental constraints on biological activity. With the introduction of vegetation and agricultural practices, SOC accumulation began rapidly, especially in surface layers52. The pronounced surface enrichment observed in old reclaimed soils highlights the cumulative effects of long-term plant residue input, root turnover, and microbial processing. Land use strongly modulated SOC dynamics. Woodland soils exhibited the highest SOC levels, consistent with continuous litter inputs, reduced disturbance, and extensive root systems53. Croplands showed moderate SOC accumulation, reflecting a balance between organic inputs and tillage-induced mineralization54. Aquaculture soils, despite receiving organic inputs from feed and sediments, accumulated SOC more slowly, likely due to anaerobic conditions that promote methanogenesis and limit physical protection mechanisms55; the associated methane production represents an unquantified greenhouse gas flux that could partially offset the carbon storage benefits of organic matter accumulation in these systems.

In contrast to SOC, SIC remained relatively stable during early reclamation stages but declined sharply in old reclaimed croplands and woodlands. This decline was most pronounced in surface layers, suggesting active carbonate dissolution and leaching. Decreasing pH, enhanced biological CO₂ production, and intensified water percolation collectively favor the dissolution of pedogenic and lithogenic carbonates56. However, dissolved inorganic carbon generated through carbonate dissolution is not necessarily lost entirely from the system; a portion may reprecipitate as secondary carbonates in deeper soil horizons beyond the 60 cm sampling depth, where pH conditions become more favorable, or may be exported to groundwater and drainage networks. Similar SIC depletion patterns have been documented in long-term cultivated calcareous soils, where agricultural intensification accelerates carbonate loss57. Notably, aquaculture lands maintained high SIC levels even in the old reclaimed area. Persistent alkalinity, elevated salinity, and continuous carbonate inputs create favorable conditions for carbonate preservation or re-precipitation58. Of particular note, field investigation confirmed that aquaculture ponds in the study area receive routine lime (calcium oxide) applications as a standard pond management practice, at rates of 50–100 kg per 667 m² during pond preparation and additional applications during the aquaculture season when water acidity increases or pathogen levels rise. These repeated lime inputs represent a continuous exogenous source of inorganic carbon that accumulates as calcium carbonate in pond sediments, directly counteracting the SIC depletion observed under terrestrial land use types and contributing to the consistently high SIC concentrations and low variability observed across the reclamation chronosequence. The statistical association between AK and SIC identified in multivariate analyses further corroborates this interpretation: AK in these soils reflects the marine ionic legacy rather than agronomic inputs, and its co-variation with salinity indicates that both variables serve as integrated indicators of the ionic environment that governs carbonate stability. The progressive decline of AK alongside salinity with reclamation age thus mirrors the weakening of the alkaline, high ionic-strength conditions that protect SIC from dissolution. These findings underscore that SIC dynamics in reclaimed coastal soils are governed primarily by hydrological regime and chemical environment rather than reclamation age alone. The inverse trends of SOC and SIC highlight an important trade-off during long-term reclamation: the transition toward SOC-rich terrestrial ecosystems may occur at the expense of inorganic carbon reservoirs, particularly under intensive land use.

The contrasting patterns between SOC accumulation and SIC depletion under different land use types translate into distinct net carbon balances. Aquaculture systems achieve a net TC gain through the simultaneous retention of SIC and gradual accumulation of SOC, while old reclaimed woodland exhibits strong surface TC enrichment but subsurface depletion. In cultivated lands, the partial compensation of SIC losses by SOC gains results in a modest net TC deficit, indicating that long-term cropland management restructures rather than dramatically depletes the soil carbon pool. These divergent net carbon balances underscore the necessity of jointly evaluating SOC and SIC dynamics when assessing the carbon sequestration potential of reclaimed coastal soils.

From labile inputs to stable pools: mechanisms of fractional soil organic carbon stabilization

Partitioning SOC into POC and MAOC provides critical insight into the mechanisms of carbon accumulation and stabilization during reclamation59. POC, representing a labile and relatively unprotected fraction, responded rapidly to land-use change, whereas MAOC reflected longer-term stabilization processes. In pristine tidal flats and newly reclaimed barren lands, POC levels were extremely low and uniformly distributed, reflecting minimal organic inputs and limited aggregation26,60. The introduction of cropping systems initiated a rapid increase in surface POC, particularly in wheat and rapeseed fields, likely associated with fresh plant residues and root-derived carbon. However, POC accumulation remained relatively modest even in old reclaimed soils, suggesting high turnover rates and vulnerability to disturbance61. MAOC exhibited a pronounced “leapfrog” growth pattern along the reclamation gradient. While MAOC levels were low during early reclamation stages, they increased dramatically in old reclaimed soils, becoming the dominant contributor to SOC accumulation. This pattern highlights the progressive enhancement of organo–mineral interactions as soils mature, likely associated with increasing clay content, improved aggregation, and higher nutrient availability62.

Woodland soils showed exceptionally high MAOC accumulation, indicating efficient conversion of organic inputs into stable mineral-associated forms. Minimal soil disturbance and continuous litter supply likely promote microbial processing pathways that favor MAOC formation. Croplands exhibited intermediate MAOC levels, whereas aquaculture soils displayed unique vertical patterns, with MAOC accumulation extending into deeper layers. This deep accumulation likely reflects the combined contribution of multiple processes: sediment burial associated with periodic pond drainage and dredging operations, which physically incorporates surface organic matter into deeper layers63; downward transport of dissolved organic matter under prolonged flooded conditions, facilitating organo-mineral associations at depth64; and in situ production of microbially derived organic compounds in deeper anoxic layers, where anaerobic decomposition generates low-molecular-weight organic products that readily associate with reactive mineral surfaces65. The anoxic conditions prevalent in aquaculture pond sediments may further stabilize this deep MAOC by suppressing oxidative decomposition, thereby promoting long-term carbon preservation in subsurface horizons66. The declining POC:MAOC ratio along the reclamation chronosequence signifies a structural shift toward more stable SOC pools. Such a transition implies increasing resistance of SOC to microbial decomposition and environmental perturbations, marking an advanced stage of soil development. Nevertheless, higher POC:MAOC ratios in certain cropping systems, particularly at depth, suggest that management practices continue to influence carbon stability even in long-term reclaimed soils.

Implications for carbon sequestration management in reclaimed coastal ecosystems

The findings of this study have important implications for carbon management in reclaimed coastal regions. Long-term reclamation combined with appropriate land use can substantially enhance SOC storage, particularly through the accumulation of stable MAOC. The peak SOC value of 14.80 g kg−1 observed in the 0–20 cm layer of old reclaimed woodland is ecologically meaningful when placed in a regional and global context. The average forest SOC content integrated across the whole soil profile in eastern China is 17.5 g kg⁻¹[ 67. In subtropical forests of Jiangsu Province, 0–20 cm surface soils of 70-year-old natural secondary broadleaf forests typically exceed 50 g kg−1, whereas 70-year-old planted coniferous forests accumulate around 40 g kg−1 68. At the global scale, total SOC content in forest topsoils ranges from 35 g kg−1 in sub/tropical zones to 52 g kg−1 in Mediterranean regions69. Our reclaimed woodland surface SOC values are therefore substantially lower than those of inland natural and planted forests of comparable age in the same province68, indicating that woodland on reclaimed coastal land remains markedly below the SOC levels of inland forest ecosystems even after seven decades of development. This persistent gap likely reflects the legacy effects of the carbon-poor marine sedimentary parent material and the comparatively short timeframe over which pedogenic and biological processes have operated since reclamation. Nevertheless, the SOC accumulation observed in old reclaimed woodland represents meaningful recovery toward natural forest baselines, highlighting both the considerable carbon sequestration achieved and the substantial remaining potential for further carbon storage in reclaimed coastal ecosystems.

However, the observed SIC depletion in terrestrial reclaimed soils suggests that SOC gains may be partially offset by SIC losses. From a climate mitigation perspective, the net carbon balance of reclamation depends on the relative magnitudes and turnover times of these pools. Management practices that enhance organic inputs while minimizing disturbance are likely to maximize SOC stabilization and MAOC formation. Woodland establishment is the most effective land use strategy for promoting long-term carbon sequestration in reclaimed coastal soils, given its capacity to sustain continuous organic inputs through litterfall and root turnover while maintaining the low-disturbance conditions that favor organo-mineral stabilization. In cultivated systems, reduced tillage and straw incorporation are recommended to minimize aggregate disruption and increase organic carbon inputs, thereby promoting the progressive shift from POC toward the more stable MAOC pool observed in long-term reclaimed croplands. To minimize SIC loss, management should prioritize practices that buffer surface soil acidification, including moderating the application of acidifying nitrogen fertilizers and maintaining adequate organic matter inputs to limit biological CO₂ production from excessive microbial activity. In aquaculture systems, the continuation of routine lime applications not only serves water quality management purposes but also inadvertently contributes to SIC conservation, partially offsetting the inorganic carbon losses observed in terrestrial land use types. Taken together, these management strategies highlight the importance of land use planning that simultaneously considers both SOC and SIC to maximize the net carbon sequestration potential of reclaimed coastal ecosystems.

Limitations and outlook

This study also has several limitations. The validity of the space-for-time substitution approach adopted in this study is supported by evidence of parent material homogeneity across the chronosequence. The study area is located in the transitional zone between the influence domains of the former Yellow River and the Yangtze River along the Nantong coastal zone in the northern Yangtze River Delta, where tidal flat sediments are consistently dominated by silt derived from the mixed supply of both river systems under a uniform tidal regime70–72. Over centennial timescales, the sediment composition in this region has shown a gradual and directionally consistent refinement rather than discrete provenance shifts, suggesting that the mineralogical and geochemical character of the parent material has remained broadly stable across the chronosequence. Furthermore, TK content remained remarkably consistent across the unreclaimed tidal flat and areas reclaimed for more than 70 years, providing strong internal geochemical evidence for parent material homogeneity across the sampling sites. These lines of evidence collectively provide a solid foundation for the space-for-time substitution approach. Nevertheless, this approach cannot fully capture historical changes in management practices, and the findings should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Owing to the high soil moisture content in deeper layers of coastal reclaimed areas, water was encountered at a certain sampling depth, preventing the collection of deeper soil samples. As a result, carbon stocks below 60 cm remain unquantified in this study, which may lead to an underestimation of TC storage, particularly in aquaculture systems where organic-rich sediment burial under flooded conditions and deep MAOC accumulation may represent a substantial additional carbon pool. Furthermore, the extent to which SIC depleted from surface layers is redistributed to deeper horizons through carbonate reprecipitation versus exported to groundwater cannot be assessed within the 60 cm sampling depth constraint, introducing uncertainty in the interpretation of net inorganic carbon losses under long-term terrestrial land use. These depth-related limitations should be considered when extrapolating the conclusions of this study to whole-profile carbon budgets. It should also be noted that MAOC was calculated as the difference between SOC and POC rather than measured directly, which assumes complete particulate organic matter recovery during fractionation and may introduce uncertainty in soils with strong aggregate stability. In addition, direct measurements of carbonate mineralogy and carbon fluxes would help to further elucidate the dynamic changes of marine sediment-derived SIC. Future studies integrating isotopic techniques, mineralogical analyses, and long-term monitoring are needed to better quantify carbon turnover and to disentangle the biological and geochemical controls on carbon stabilization in reclaimed coastal ecosystems.

Methods

Study area

The study area is located in Rudong County, Jiangsu Province (120°42’–121°22′ E, 32°12′–32°36′ N), situated on the northern wing of the Yangtze River Delta along the characteristic silty coastal zone of the South Yellow Sea (Supplementary Fig. 1). This region features a northern subtropical monsoon climate with a mean annual temperature of 15.0 °C and an average annual precipitation of 1055 mm. The warm and humid monsoon climate, characterized by concentrated summer precipitation and mild winters, is conducive to active pedogenesis, biological SOC accumulation, and carbonate weathering in surface soil layers, providing a favorable thermal and hydrological background for the soil carbon dynamics investigated in this study. As a quintessential example of large-scale coastal development and land use transition in China, Rudong possesses extensive tidal flats and a reclamation history spanning over 70 years, providing a highly representative spatiotemporal sequence (space-for-time substitution) for investigating coastal ecosystem evolution.

The land use evolution in Rudong is characterized by large-scale and phased coastal reclamation. Since the 1950s, the region has undergone a dynamic developmental process progressing from inland toward the coastline. The reclamation practice typically commences with the construction of sea dikes to intercept tidal inundation, followed by desalination through freshwater irrigation and drainage, which gradually transforms pristine saline tidal flats into highly managed anthropogenic ecosystems. Throughout this evolutionary process, the regional environment has undergone a profound transition from marine sediments to terrestrial agricultural soils19,21. With the progression of soil desalination post-reclamation, a diversified land use pattern has emerged in the study area8,23. In agricultural zones, production is dominated by dryland crops such as wheat, rapeseed, and faba bean. Concurrently, substantial coastal sections have been developed into aquaculture ponds, establishing distinct water management and nutrient cycling regimes compared to terrestrial croplands. This incremental transition from marine sediments to various land use types makes the Rudong reclamation area a critical window for exploring the evolutionary patterns of coastal soils under anthropogenic intervention.

Soil sample collection and laboratory analysis

Soil samples were collected from April to May 2025. Three representative areas were selected based on historical records and field reconnaissance to represent a chronological sequence of reclamation, including the unreclaimed area, the newly reclaimed area, and the old reclaimed area. Reclamation in the study region occurred in discrete episodes rather than as a continuous process, with major reclamation periods in the 1950s, 1970s, 1980s, and 2000s. Areas reclaimed in the 1970s and 1980s were excluded as their temporal proximity may not reflect meaningful differences in soil carbon pool development. Consequently, the newly reclaimed area has a reclamation history of less than 20 years, while the old reclaimed area has been reclaimed for more than 70 years, representing distinct stages of reclamation history with sufficient temporal separation to capture meaningful differences in soil carbon pool development. The unreclaimed area consists of pristine tidal flats. The newly reclaimed area is dominated by unused barren land (approximately 60% of the area), with aquaculture ponds accounting for approximately 30%, and wheat and rapeseed fields occupying the remaining approximately 10%, based on field reconnaissance. Here, barren land refers to uncultivated land with no vegetation or only sparse weed cover, temporarily unused due to high salinity and alkalinity during the early stages of reclamation. This land use composition reflects the early stage of agricultural development in newly reclaimed coastal areas, where large portions of land remain uncultivated pending further soil amelioration. Consequently, four land use types, including wheat, rapeseed, aquaculture, and barren land, were sampled in this area. In contrast, the old reclaimed area is characterized by intensive human utilization with no remaining barren land. The cultivated land primarily supports wheat, rapeseed, and faba beans, with numerous aquaculture ponds and some artificial woodland also present. Accordingly, five land use types, including wheat, rapeseed, faba beans, aquaculture, and woodland, were sampled in the old reclaimed area. For each land use type, three sampling plots were randomly selected and distributed across the landscape with a minimum distance of 500 m between plots of the same land use type, ensuring spatial independence and avoiding pseudo-replication. Different land use types were allowed to be spatially proximate to one another, so that observed differences in soil carbon pools could be more reliably attributed to land use type rather than other environmental gradients such as topography or hydrology. In each plot, five replicate subsamples were collected and mixed to form a single composite sample. For aquaculture ponds, soil samples were collected directly from within the pond sediments during the seasonal drainage period, when pond water had been drained as part of routine aquaculture management practices. Soil samples were collected to a depth of 60 cm and segmented into three layers, specifically 0–20 cm, 20–40 cm, and 40–60 cm. A total of 90 soil samples were obtained for subsequent analysis.

Soil pH was determined using a glass electrode method, and soil salinity was measured by the residue drying–gravimetric method. TC and TN contents were directly analyzed using a carbon–nitrogen analyzer. TP was determined using the acid digestion–molybdenum antimony colorimetric method, and TK was measured by the acid digestion–inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). AN was determined by the alkali diffusion method. AP was extracted using a sodium bicarbonate/sodium fluoride–hydrochloric acid solution and determined by the molybdenum antimony colorimetric method. AK was extracted with ammonium acetate and determined by ICP-OES. According to Stokes’ law, soil particle-size distribution and the percentage of each particle fraction were determined using a soil hydrometer. SOC was measured by the external-heating potassium dichromate oxidation–volumetric method. SIC was calculated as the difference between TC and SOC. POC was extracted with sodium hexametaphosphate and determined using the external-heating potassium dichromate oxidation method. MAOC was calculated as the difference between SOC and POC73–75. Further experimental details are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

Preliminary data processing was performed using Microsoft Excel 2021. Differences in soil properties and carbon fractions among the ten treatment groups (combinations of reclamation stage and land use type) were analyzed using a general linear model with treatment group as a fixed factor, followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. The ten treatment groups represented the nested structure of land use types within three reclamation stages (unreclaimed, newly reclaimed, and old reclaimed). One-way ANOVA was used to test the effect of soil depth (three levels: 0–20 cm, 20–40 cm, and 40–60 cm) within each treatment group. Prior to analysis, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. In most cases, the assumptions were met (P > 0.05). For data with minor violations of variance homogeneity, no data transformation was applied; instead, the analyses relied on the robustness of ANOVA under a strictly balanced design (n = 3 per group), and results were cross-checked to ensure that conclusions were not sensitive to minor assumption violations. All statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics 26. Pearson correlation, RDA and RF regression analysis were performed to assess the relationships between soil physicochemical properties and carbon fractions. All figures were plotted using Origin 2023 and R 4.5.1, and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. In R, data manipulation was performed using the readxl and dplyr packages; multivariate ordination analyses were conducted using the vegan package; random forest analysis was performed using the randomForest package; and visualizations were generated using ggplot2, with figure layout managed using cowplot and grid.
