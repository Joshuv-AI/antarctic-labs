Partial radicular versus coronal pulpotomy using chitosan and platelet-rich fibrin in vital permanent molars with irreversible pulpitis, with or without associated apical periodontitis: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Neelam Mittal, Harakh Chand Baranwal, Deepti Singh, Chanda Dhakad, Ayushi Kejriwal, Shelly Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Recent developments in biologically oriented dentistry have encouraged preservation of pulpal tissue even in cases traditionally managed with root canal therapy (RCT). Evidence suggests that in many teeth diagnosed with irreversible pulpitis, the inflammatory process may not involve the entire pulp, allowing retention of viable radicular tissue through conservative procedures such as pulpotomy. Partial radicular pulpotomy is a conservative approach that requires further clinical validation.Aim:To comparatively evaluate the clinical and radiographic outcomes of coronal and partial radicular pulpotomy using chitosan and platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) in vital permanent molars presenting with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, with or without apical periodontitis.Subjects and Methods:Sixty-eight patients aged 15–35 years with cariously exposed mature permanent molars were randomly allocated into four groups: coronal pulpotomy with chitosan, coronal pulpotomy with PRF, partial radicular pulpotomy with chitosan, and partial radicular pulpotomy with PRF. All procedures were performed according to the standardized protocols, using Biodentine as the sealing material. A Visual Analog Scale was used to assess postoperative pain. Radiographic healing was evaluated using intraoral periapical radiographs and cone-beam computed tomography at subsequent follow-ups of 3, 6, and 12 months.Statistical Evaluation:To compare outcomes between groups, the Mann–Whitney U-test was applied, while survival outcomes were determined through Kaplan–Meier analysis.Results:All groups showed a significant reduction in postoperative pain and radiographic healing. The overall 12-month survival rate was 88.2%, with the highest rate in the partial radicular pulpotomy with PRF group (94.1%), but there was no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05).Conclusions:Coronal and partial radicular pulpotomy demonstrated favorable outcomes. Partial radicular pulpotomy with chitosan or PRF may represent a reliable, minimally invasive alternative to conventional RCT.

INTRODUCTION

Dental caries is a major global oral health problem and the leading cause of pulpal inflammation in permanent teeth. Deep carious lesions often result in pulpal exposure, which has traditionally been treated by root canal therapy (RCT) based on the belief that irreversible pulpitis cannot heal.[1] However, current evidence shows that pulpal inflammation is often localized and that the dental pulp retains healing potential when bacterial contamination is controlled.[23]

Vital pulp therapy (VPT) is recognized as a biologically driven method designed to conserve the living pulp tissue within the tooth. Coronal pulpotomy removes inflamed coronal pulp when maintaining radicular pulp vitality, whereas partial radicular pulpotomy allows deeper removal of inflamed tissue when required.[45] Bioactive scaffolds such as chitosan and platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) promote pulpal healing through antimicrobial activity, angiogenesis, and growth factor release, while Biodentine provides an effective seal and stimulates reparative dentin formation.[6789]

Histological evidence suggests that inflammatory changes in irreversible pulpitis are often confined to the coronal pulp, while the radicular pulp remains viable. Based on this principle, the EndoLight concept, proposed by Wolters, recommends pulpotomy procedures that preserve healthy radicular pulp.[10] If hemostasis is achieved, full pulpotomy is performed; however, persistent bleeding may require partial radicular pulpotomy.[41011]

Clinical evidence supporting this concept has been provided by Shah et al., who demonstrated comparable 12-month outcomes between deep (partial radicular) and full pulpotomy among mature teeth exhibiting signs of irreversible pulpitis.[11] Similarly, Baranwal et al. reported equivalent clinical and radiographic outcomes of full versus partial radicular pulpotomy using Biodentine.[4] These findings support selective pulp removal as a biologically sound approach for preserving pulp vitality.

Therefore, this randomized controlled clinical trial compared coronal and partial radicular pulpotomy using chitosan and PRF in permanent molars presenting with irreversible pulpitis, with or without apical periodontitis.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Ethical clearance (ECR/526/Inst/UP/2014/RR-20) and CTRI registration (CTRI/2024/10/075830) were secured before initiating this randomized clinical study, which was performed in the Department of Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics.

After careful explanation of the treatment protocols involved, informed consent was taken before commencing the treatment.

Patients with cariously exposed mature permanent molars exhibiting symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, with or without associated apical periodontitis, were selected and divided into four groups: coronal pulpotomy using chitosan group, coronal pulpotomy using PRF group, partial radicular pulpotomy using chitosan group, and partial radicular pulpotomy using PRF group.

Patients aged 15–35 years with vital mature permanent molars exhibiting irreversible pulpitis, irrespective of the presence of apical periodontitis, presenting with spontaneous dull pain partially relieved by analgesics and exhibiting a strong, prolonged response to thermal testing, were included.

Nonrestorable teeth, negative thermal response, sinus tract or swelling, uncontrolled bleeding after pulpotomy within 5 min, insufficient bleeding indicating necrotic pulp, and medically compromised patients were excluded.

Sample size calculation

After conducting a literature review, we found that no specific studies are available related to partial radicular pulpotomy. Therefore, the sample size was determined as 12 participants per group in accordance with Julious SA’s rule-of-thumb for pilot studies.

Due to the chances of drop-out and patient compliance, the sample size was rounded off to 17 per group [Chart 1].

Study design

In vivo interventional randomized clinical trial with parallel groups, using the chit method for allocation.

Selected patients were divided among four groups:

Group 1: Coronal pulpotomy using chitosan

Local anesthesia was achieved with 2% lignocaine containing 1:80,000 epinephrine (ICPA Health Products Ltd, India), followed by placement of a rubber dam (Coltene, Switzerland) for isolation. The tooth surface was decontaminated using 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (Parcan, Septodont, USA). Carious dentin was then removed under irrigation using a slow-speed round bur, after which access to the pulp chamber was established with a sterile round bur in a high-speed handpiece under coolant. The coronal pulp was amputated to the level of the canal orifices. Bleeding was controlled using a cotton pellet soaked in 3% sodium hypochlorite.

Chitosan powder (BFCLAB) was mixed with propylene glycol and applied over the pulp tissue in a 2 mm layer, followed by placement of Biodentine (Septodont, USA) over it in a 3–4 mm thickness, which was allowed to set for 12 min. A layer of light-curing glass ionomer restorative (Ketac N100, 3M ESPE US) was subsequently placed, followed by composite (Tetric N, Ceram, Ivoclar) restoration.

Group 2: Coronal pulpotomy using platelet-rich fibrin

The same procedure was followed as in Group 1. Instead of chitosan, freshly prepared PRF obtained from the patient’s blood (centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min) was applied over the pulp tissue in a thickness of 2–3 mm, followed by Biodentine, glass ionomer cement, and composite restoration.

Group 3: Partial radicular pulpotomy using chitosan

After coronal pulp removal, 3–4 mm of radicular pulp was removed using an LN bur (Dentsply, USA). Hemostasis was achieved with sodium hypochlorite-soaked cotton on a reamer. Chitosan paste was placed into the canal till orifices using a root canal plugger, followed by Biodentine and final restoration.

Group 4: Partial radicular pulpotomy using platelet-rich fibrin

The procedure was identical to Group 3, except that PRF was placed in the canal orifices instead of chitosan.

Pain was evaluated on days 1, 7, and 30. Intraoral periapical (IOPA) radiographs were taken at 3-, 6-, and 12-month intervals, and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) was evaluated at 12 months [Figures 1 and 2].

RESULTS

Among the 68 participants initially enrolled, 10 participants did not complete follow-up, and eight cases failed; therefore, a total of 50 teeth were analyzed at 3-, 6-, and 12-month follow-up intervals. The cases were allocated into four groups based on the treatment modality: coronal pulpotomy with chitosan, coronal pulpotomy with PRF, partial radicular pulpotomy with chitosan, and partial radicular pulpotomy with PRF. Clinical and radiographic outcomes were assessed using Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores, IOPA PAI (by Orstavik), CBCT PAI ≤3 (by Estrela et al.), and root canal calcification. Nonparametric comparison between groups was carried out using the Mann–Whitney U-test, while categorical variables were examined with Fisher’s exact test, and survival analyses were evaluated through Kaplan–Meier estimation [Tables 1a-c and 2a and b].

No statistically significant variation was observed among the groups with respect to age or gender distribution. Intergroup comparison showed no statistically significant difference in VAS pain scores at any follow-up interval, although a progressive decline in mean pain scores was observed over time in all groups [Table 1a]. Radiographic evaluation demonstrated progressive reduction in PAI scores during follow-up, with a statistically significant intergroup difference observed only at the 3-month interval, while other time points remained statistically nonsignificant [Tables 1b and 1c].

The incidence of root canal calcification increased gradually during the follow-up period across all groups, without any statistically significant intergroup difference [Table 2a]. The overall survival rate was 88.2%, with the highest survival observed in Group 4 (94.1%), followed by Group 2 and Group 3 (88.2% each) and Group 1 (82.4%), although survival outcomes were statistically comparable among all groups [Table 2b].

DISCUSSION

VPT has undergone a significant paradigm shift in recent years, expanding from a procedure traditionally indicated for reversible pulpitis to a biologically based treatment approach applicable even in teeth diagnosed with irreversible pulpitis and early apical periodontitis.[1213] Advances in bioactive materials and regenerative scaffolds have strengthened the concept that preservation of the dentin–pulp complex can be a predictable and conservative alternative to conventional RCT.[671415] Histological investigations by Ricucci et al. demonstrated a strong association between clinical symptoms and pulp histology, showing that inflammation in irreversible pulpitis is often confined primarily to the coronal pulp. In contrast, the radicular pulp remains vital and capable of healing.[16]

The present randomized clinical trial assessed the clinical and radiographic outcomes associated with coronal and partial radicular pulpotomy using biologic scaffolds – chitosan and PRF – with Biodentine as the sealing material in vital permanent molars presenting with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis with or without apical periodontitis. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis [Table 2b] revealed an overall survival rate of 88.2% at 1-year follow-up, supporting pulpotomy as a viable vitality-preserving treatment for such teeth.

When outcomes were evaluated according to treatment technique, partial radicular pulpotomy demonstrated slightly higher survival percentages than coronal pulpotomy. This trend may be explained by the deeper removal of inflamed pulp tissue (approximately 3–4 mm beyond the canal orifice), which can reduce inflammatory load and facilitate improved hemorrhage control.[41117] Similar favorable outcomes for partial radicular pulpotomy performed in vital mature permanent teeth having signs of irreversible pulpitis have been reported by Baranwal et al., who demonstrated encouraging clinical and radiographic outcomes using Biodentine.[4]

Scaffold materials also appeared to influence survival trends in the present study. The PRF groups demonstrated the highest survival percentages, followed by the chitosan groups. PRF is an autologous scaffold rich in platelets and growth factors that promotes angiogenesis, cellular migration, and tissue regeneration.[61819] Chitosan also showed favorable outcomes due to its antimicrobial properties, biocompatibility, and hemostatic ability, supporting cellular attachment and organized tissue repair, consistent with findings reported in previous regenerative endodontic studies.[92021]

Biodentine served as the pulpotomy medicament in all groups and likely contributed to the favorable survival outcomes observed. Its bioactivity, sealing ability, and ability to stimulate reparative dentin formation have been widely documented.[582223] Clinical investigations by Taha et al. demonstrated encouraging clinical and radiographic success of Biodentine-based pulpotomy in fully developed permanent teeth diagnosed with irreversible pulpitis, further supporting its use in VPT.[24]

Radiographic evaluation using both IOPA and CBCT-based periapical indices demonstrated progressive healing of periapical changes across all groups, including teeth presenting with preoperative apical radiolucency [Tables 1b and 1c].

Root canal calcification is a common finding after VPT and generally reflects ongoing odontoblastic activity and partial restoration of pulp vitality, rather than treatment failure. However, it may become clinically challenging when it progresses to pulp canal obliteration.[425]

Within the limitations of this study, both coronal pulpotomy and partial radicular pulpotomy demonstrated favorable survival outcomes, indicating that these procedures represent reliable and conservative treatment options for vital permanent molars with irreversible pulpitis and periapical changes when performed under strict aseptic conditions with appropriate biomaterials.

Limitations of the study

Key constraints of this investigation include the modest sample size, its execution at a single clinical setting, and the relatively short 12-month follow-up period. Pulpal status was determined using clinical criteria without histological confirmation. Variability in scaffold characteristics, subjective pain assessment using VAS, possible radiographic interpretation bias, and lack of scaffold radiopacity may also have influenced outcome evaluation.

CONCLUSIONS

Both coronal pulpotomy and partial radicular pulpotomy showed favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes in vital permanent molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, with or without apical periodontitis.
