Abstract
This conceptual study aims to develop Guided Uncertainty, an applied behavioral framework for public service
journeys in which an important outcome remains uncertain while the person continues navigating the process under
meaningful guidance from a person, team, organization, or system. Methodologically, the article integrates literature
on administrative burden, behavioral public administration, uncertainty management, service experience, and journey
design; examines documented U.S. cases involving the Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) and
Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) as analytical tests of fit and boundaries; and compares the
framework with existing behavioral and simplification approaches. The resulting framework distinguishes Outcome
Uncertainty from Journey Uncertainty, proposes Emotional Phases as an analytical unit distinct from operational
steps, and organizes intervention through four connected movements: Understand, Design, Guide, and Learn. Guided
Uncertainty links cognitive and emotional conditions to plausible behavioral and operational risks and uses this
diagnosis to structure preventive responses in process, information, communication, and guidance. The study
concludes that public organizations may reduce avoidable uncertainty about the journey and mitigate experiential,
behavioral, and operational consequences even when substantive uncertainty must remain, without changing decision
criteria or implying a favorable outcome. The article also formulates testable propositions and a research agenda for
empirical development across public service contexts.
Keywords: Administrative burden; Behavioral public administration; Public service delivery; Uncertainty; Guided
Uncertainty.

1. Introduction
A public service can be legally and operationally legitimate and still place a person in a journey that is difficult to
navigate. Someone who depends on a decision regarding a retirement benefit must live with uncertainty about access to a
source of income that is important to their life while navigating a process that may be complex and prolonged. The agency
cannot legitimately guarantee approval before completing its analysis, but it can substantially influence how that person
experiences the journey. During periods of high personal importance, waiting, and low control, anxiety, insecurity, urgency, or
frustration may coexist with the need to understand requests, gather evidence, respond correctly, and decide when to act or
seek help. If the information received is insufficient, ambiguous, late, or inconsistent—including during service interactions—
the person may misinterpret what is required, submit inadequate or incomplete documentation, miss a necessary action, or
repeatedly seek clarification. The result may be a cumulative sequence: error, new requirement, rework, more waiting, and
intensification of the same conditions that already made the journey difficult—potentially culminating, in some cases, in more
serious avoidable consequences such as substantial delay, abandonment, or loss of access to a benefit because of failures that
occurred during the journey itself.
The same logic appears in other high-stakes journeys. A professional who depends on the issuance or renewal of a
license to continue working may face increasing pressure as time passes without a decision or without sufficient information to
understand progress and prepare appropriately. A student whose enrollment or ability to remain at a university depends on
financial aid may face similar insecurity in the presence of delays, silence, or uninformative updates. The point is not that these
people will necessarily react in the same way, but that foreseeable cognitive and emotional conditions can alter how
information is interpreted and actions are performed. Depending on how process, communication, and guidance were designed,
these conditions may be mitigated or aggravated.
This makes preventive design of the process and guidance—considering the cognitive and emotional conditions
reasonably expected throughout the journey—a central part of the problem, not merely a service detail. This guidance may be
embedded in the process itself, digital systems, automated communications, and human interactions, which together or
separately influence what the person understands, expects, and does throughout the journey. When human interaction exists,
prepared professionals can play a particularly important role by anticipating foreseeable questions and risks, providing highquality information at the appropriate time, confirming understanding, preserving continuity, and calibrating guidance without
interfering with the substantive decision. For the public organization, this capacity may mean fewer inadequate or incomplete

The central problem examined in this article, therefore, is not simply satisfaction or emotional comfort. It is how
cognitive and emotional conditions associated with uncertainty interact with process, information, and guidance and, through
that interaction, may produce consequences for both the user and the public organization’s ability to execute its processes

Guided Uncertainty builds on established foundations in behavioral science, service design, uncertainty research, and
public administration and organizes them for a specific domain: journeys in which an outcome of significant personal or
professional importance remains materially uncertain while the person must continue navigating the process under meaningful
guidance from a person, team, organization, or system. In this domain, the framework uses Outcome Uncertainty
diagnostically—what remains unknown about the substantive outcome or its timing—and Journey Uncertainty—what remains
unclear about navigation, such as the meaning of events, expectations, duration, next steps, required actions, or confirmation of
completion. The literature already recognizes forms of uncertainty associated with process and outcome (Viglia et al., 2019;
Ulaga & Kohli, 2018); the contribution proposed here lies in integrating this distinction with Emotional Phases, prospective
risk, preventive design, continuous guidance, and learning.
The framework further proposes that the human experience should not be forced into the same segmentation as the
organization’s operational flow. An Emotional Phase is an analytical period characterized by a materially similar cognitiveemotional configuration that is relevant to behavior and guidance. It may span several operational steps, and a significant
change in the person’s state may occur even when the formal process has not advanced. Waiting is the clearest example:
operationally, nothing may change; psychologically and behaviorally, the journey may change substantially.
Guided Uncertainty organizes the work into four connected movements. Understand identifies the journey’s
Emotional Phases, expected cognitive and emotional conditions, plausible drivers, forms of uncertainty, and associated
behavioral and operational risks. Design translates this diagnosis into preventive changes to process, information, expectations,
communication, preparation, interaction, or other elements, seeking to anticipate risks before they become observable error or
friction. Guide addresses how the person is actually guided through the journey as uncertainty unfolds, through the systems,
communications, channels, and human interactions that constitute that guidance. It examines information quality and timing,
proactivity, confirmation of understanding, continuity, handoffs, degraded trust, intensified conditions, and, when relevant
human contact exists, the actions of the professionals responsible for guidance.
Learn compares expectations with evidence, analytically measures outcomes at multiple levels, documents null and
adverse results, and revises the diagnosis or framework when the metrics indicate that need.
The research question is: How do cognitive and emotional conditions associated with uncertainty shape behavioral
and operational friction throughout public service journeys, and how can these risks be systematically identified and
addressed? This conceptual study aims to develop Guided Uncertainty, an applied behavioral framework for public service
journeys in which an important outcome remains uncertain while the person continues navigating the process under
meaningful guidance from a person, team, organization, or system.
This article proposes three contributions. First, it specifies an uncertainty-centered problem structure within public
service delivery, connecting the established literature on administrative burden and behavioral insights to a narrower and more
complex class of journeys in which significant uncertainty persists. Second, it proposes Emotional Phases as an analytical unit
distinct from operational steps, allowing diagnosis to follow material changes in cognitive-emotional conditions rather than
only the agency’s workflow. Third, it extends diagnosis and intervention design to continuous guidance during execution,

2. Methodology
2.1 Conceptual development and analytical approach
This study combines documentary research based on official U.S. public-service sources, specifically documented
evidence concerning Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) and Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program
(SNAP), with a qualitative narrative review of relevant literature. Narrative reviews allow the integration and interpretive
synthesis of theoretically relevant sources without the exhaustive and reproducible selection procedures characteristic of
systematic reviews (Fernandes et al., 2023; Ogassavara et al., 2025). Consistent with this approach, the literature was selected
for its direct relevance to the mechanisms and conceptual relationships examined in the study, while official and peer-reviewed
evidence from U.S. public services was used to examine the framework’s fit and boundaries. The methodological structure
supports the development of an applied behavioral framework for public service journeys (Risemberg et al., 2026).
This article adopts a conceptual framework-development approach. Guided Uncertainty is examined and articulated as
a framework for a bounded class of journeys in which an important outcome remains uncertain while the person must continue
navigating the process under meaningful guidance. Relevant literature from administrative burden, behavioral public
administration, uncertainty management, service research, procedural justice, and journey-oriented design is used to ground
specific mechanisms, examine conceptual relationships, and position the framework relative to existing knowledge. The
objective is not to report a new randomized trial or observational study, but to specify the problem domain, define analytical
constructs, organize relationships among those constructs, and derive propositions that can be examined empirically. The
framework is therefore evaluated in this article through conceptual coherence, correspondence with documented service
problems, comparison with related approaches, and the formulation of observable implications for future testing.
The analytical process proceeded in three connected steps. First, the article identified recurring problems in public
service journeys in which an important outcome or its timing remains outside the user’s control while the user must continue
interpreting information and performing required actions. Second, the framework’s constructs and relationships were examined
against relevant literature and documented public-service evidence to clarify their theoretical grounding, areas of overlap, and
distinctions from existing approaches. Third, these elements were organized into an applied architecture connecting diagnosis,
preventive design, guidance during execution, and learning from observed outcomes.
2.2 Literature and comparative foundations
The literature used in the conceptual development was selected for its direct relevance to the framework’s proposed
mechanisms rather than as a systematic review intended to exhaust all scholarship in each field. Administrative burden
research provides the principal public-administration foundation for learning, compliance, and psychological costs. Behavioral
and psychological research informs appraisal, emotion, perceived control, responsiveness, and procedural experience. Service
and uncertainty research supports distinctions among forms of uncertainty and the role of process and communication. Publicsector behavioral toolkits, behavioral journey mapping, sludge audits, human-centered design, and related approaches are
examined comparatively to identify areas of overlap and the specific analytical problem that Guided Uncertainty seeks to
address.

2.3 Selection and use of U.S. public service cases
Documented U.S. public service cases were selected as analytical illustrations because they contain observable
combinations of uncertainty, administrative requirements, communication or navigation problems, user action, and operational
consequences. The 2024 Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) implementation and multiple Supplemental
Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) interventions provide evidence from governmental or peer-reviewed sources that
permits examination of both fit and boundaries. These cases are not presented as empirical tests of Guided Uncertainty and the
article does not attribute their observed outcomes to the framework. Instead, they are used to examine whether the proposed
distinctions can organize documented problems without displacing technical, legal, capacity, eligibility, or other causal
mechanisms outside the framework’s specific contribution.
The resulting framework is assessed through testable propositions and a multilevel measurement logic that
distinguishes experiential, behavioral, operational, and substantive outcomes. This design preserves a clear boundary between
conceptual development and empirical validation: the article specifies what should be measured and which relationships should
be tested, while independent applications and future studies are required to estimate effects, replicability, transferability, and
incremental value.

3. Results
3.1 Administrative burden and public service journeys
Research on administrative burden provides both a conceptual foundation and an important boundary for Guided
Uncertainty. Moynihan et al. (2015) define administrative burden as learning, compliance, and psychological costs experienced
in citizen-state interactions. Learning costs include the resources required to discover a program, understand eligibility,
interpret requirements, and determine how to proceed. Compliance costs include the time, effort, documentation, and actions
required to satisfy administrative requirements. Psychological costs include stress, stigma, loss of autonomy, cognitive load,
and other adverse experiences associated with interacting with government. Herd and Moynihan (2018) show that these costs
can influence access to programs and the practical availability of rights and benefits.
These categories may interact within the same journey. A request for additional documentation resulting, for example,
from a misunderstood instruction or from a requirement that was not adequately anticipated in the structure of the journey may
combine learning costs, new compliance work, and psychological strain; if this combination makes it harder for the person to
understand or respond and guidance is insufficient to help them correctly perform what is required, a legitimate requirement
may generate an incorrect submission, another requirement, rework, and more waiting.
Guided Uncertainty does not propose a replacement taxonomy: it asks how these conditions evolve when uncertainty
is material, where they create execution risk, and how process, information, and guidance can be proactively structured to
reduce these risks, support understanding and proper execution of required actions, and avoid errors, repeated contacts, and
rework that affect both the user and the organization responsible for the service.
Evidence that bureaucratic encounters can generate distinct emotional responses reinforces the need to take this
question seriously. Hattke et al. (2020) experimentally examined emotional responses to excessive bureaucracy and found that
administrative burden and delay elicited negative emotions, especially confusion, frustration, and anger. Their laboratory
design used 136 participants and deliberately prioritized internal validity, so the results should not be mechanically generalized
to U.S. public services or every administrative interaction. The contribution is narrower but important: emotion in citizen-state
interaction is an empirically investigable phenomenon, not merely a rhetorical description of “poor user experience.”
The existence of a legitimate administrative requirement does not, by itself, determine how that requirement will be

This distinction separates Guided Uncertainty from a general burden-reduction agenda. Some burden is produced
primarily by unnecessary paperwork, duplicate data collection, poor digital design, or excessive procedural requirements.
These problems may be addressed more directly through process simplification, digitization, sludge reduction, legal reform, or
investment in capacity. Guided Uncertainty becomes relevant when its domain conditions are present and cognitive or
emotional conditions related to uncertainty plausibly contribute to behavioral or operational risk.
The same distinction matters for evaluation. User experience, behavior, operational performance, and substantive
outcomes may be related without moving together. A clearer status message may reduce repeated calls without changing
eligibility; a redesigned uploader may reduce denials for incomplete information without increasing participation. Guided
Uncertainty treats this separation as a methodological requirement: satisfaction is not a proxy for operational effectiveness, and
a favorable substantive outcome is not a proxy for good guidance. The question is whether the target risk changed in the
predicted direction and whether the evidence supports the proposed mechanism.
3.2 U.S. cases as tests of fit and boundaries
Documented cases in the United States make it possible to examine how the distinctions proposed by Guided
Uncertainty appear in observable public service delivery problems and to identify the limits of its application. The Free
Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) and Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) are analyzed as cases
of fit and boundary, allowing friction related to experience, behavior, and journey execution to be distinguished from
predominantly technical, legal, or capacity problems.
3.2.1 FAFSA: A pattern of high fit within a broader implementation failure
The 2024 implementation of the redesigned Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) provides a useful realworld boundary test. The U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO) found that the launch was delayed by three months
and affected by technical problems that prevented some students from starting or completing the application. Among first-time
applicants, submissions were about 9% below the prior cycle as of August 25, 2024. During the first five months of
implementation, 4.0 million of 5.4 million calls to the Department of Education call center—74%—went unanswered. GAO
also found that applicants did not always receive timely information about processing delays or how to navigate technical
problems; some were told to “try again later,” while resolution sometimes took months. GAO concluded that the lack of timely
support and clear communication contributed to applicant confusion (GAO, 2024).
A significant portion of the documented FAFSA problems resulted from technical failures and capacity constraints
that require their own solutions. For Guided Uncertainty, the complementary question is how people are guided through the
journey while those conditions persist and an important outcome remains uncertain.
It is precisely in this dimension that part of the documented FAFSA problems aligns with the framework. Students
faced an important outcome—access to financial aid and the ability to make enrollment decisions. Relevant uncertainty about

appropriate forms of communication, guidance, and escalation. Correcting the technical failure still requires the corresponding
technical solution; the framework acts on the behavioral and operational problems that may arise or intensify while the user
navigates the situation.
3.2.2 SNAP: Incomplete action, process redesign, and divergence of outcomes
Administration of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) provides an even cleaner example because
the behavioral and operational targets are directly observable. In a 2024 U.S. Office of Evaluation Sciences (OES) evaluation
involving 33,574 online applicants in a large southern state, applicants had to provide verification documents and complete an
interview. Failure to complete required steps could result in denial for incomplete information rather than a determination of
substantive ineligibility. OES observed that applicants could face cognitively demanding password requirements and
uncertainty about whether documents had already been submitted. A text-message campaign linking to a simplified, mobilefriendly document uploader reduced denials for incomplete eligibility information by 1.1 percentage points, although it did not
produce a detectable increase in overall SNAP participation (OES, 2024).
This result is particularly relevant to Guided Uncertainty because it demonstrates why Learn must separate outcome
classes. The intervention changed an operational process-related outcome—fewer denials for incomplete information—without
significantly changing the final participation rate. A framework that measured only participation could fail to detect a
meaningful process improvement; a framework that treated the reduction in procedural denial as evidence of increased benefit
receipt would overstate the result. Guided Uncertainty explicitly requires this separation.
The OES case also illustrates the difference between substantive and navigational uncertainty: the eligibility decision
remained outside the applicant’s control, while questions about login, submission, and confirmation of documents were
potentially modifiable. The intervention acted on the journey, not on eligibility.
A separate randomized field experiment involving approximately 65,000 SNAP applicants in Los Angeles identified
the initial interview as a major administrative barrier. Offering access to flexible interviews initiated by applicants themselves
increased approvals by 6 percentage points, doubled early approvals, and increased long-term participation by more than 2
percentage points (Giannella et al., 2024). This experiment demonstrates that features of administrative design can materially
affect user behavior and the procedural outcomes of a service journey. For Guided Uncertainty, the finding is particularly
relevant because it shows that how a requirement is structured and executed can influence whether people are able to progress

3.3 Guided Uncertainty: domain, constructs, and architecture
3.3.1 Domain conditions
Guided Uncertainty is not intended for every public interaction or every administrative burden. Three conditions
define the domain.
First, the outcome has significant personal or professional importance for the Guided User, the person whose journey
is being analyzed. Stakes may be financial, medical, legal, occupational, educational, familial, or otherwise materially relevant.
Routine low-impact transactions may be inconvenient without warranting the framework.
Second, a relevant part of the outcome or its timing is outside the Guided User’s control. The person may influence
the outcome through preparation, documentation, choices, or timely action, but cannot determine it unilaterally.
Third, meaningful guidance exists throughout the journey. A person, team, organization, or system plays a relevant
role in how the Guided User understands, prepares for, and progresses through the process. Guidance may be human, digital,
or hybrid; guidance is a function of the journey, not necessarily a human role.
Once these conditions are met, the intensity of application may vary according to the journey’s duration, complexity,
number of actors, waiting, handoffs, and behavioral risk.
3.3.2 Guided User, Guiding Professional, and Decision Authority
The Guided User is the primary person or population whose journey is the object of analysis. Other people may
participate materially without becoming the primary Guided User.
Guidance refers to the function of guiding the Guided User through the journey, influencing how the user understands,
prepares for, and progresses through the process. This function may be performed by people, digital systems, processes, or
combinations of these mechanisms. When guidance is performed by a professional, Guided Uncertainty uses the term Guiding
Professional to designate this human role.
Decision Authority is the person, unit, organization, or other structure responsible for determining the substantive
outcome when an identifiable decision-making authority exists. It may be the same person as the Guiding Professional, another
person or unit within the same organization, or an external entity.

3.3.3 Outcome Uncertainty and Journey Uncertainty
Outcome Uncertainty concerns uncertainty about the substantive outcome or when it will occur: Will the benefit be
approved? Will the license be granted? When will a determination be issued? Part of Outcome Uncertainty legitimately
remains until evidence is analyzed or an external event occurs.
Journey Uncertainty concerns uncertainty experienced while navigating the path: What is happening? Why was this
requested? Is this delay normal? What does this status mean? What happens next? How long may this step take? What action is
required? Was the document received?
The distinction guides different responses. Avoidable Journey Uncertainty may be reduced through improvements to
process, information, communication, or guidance. When Outcome Uncertainty must remain, process, preparation, and
guidance can still, in some cases, reduce its cognitive and emotional impact and the likelihood that its effects produce errors,
incomplete actions, repeated contacts, abandonment, or rework.
3.3.4 Emotional Phases
Operational processes are usually segmented according to the work: application, review, request for evidence,
interview, decision, appeal, renewal. Guided Uncertainty adds a different analytical segmentation.
An Emotional Phase is a portion of the journey in which the Guided User can reasonably be expected to experience a
materially similar cognitive-emotional configuration that is relevant to behavior and guidance. The phase changes when that
configuration changes significantly, not simply when the organization advances to a new operational step.
Several operational steps may occur within an Emotional Phase because the Guided User experiences similar
emotions, concerns, and uncertainties. A single operational step—especially waiting—may contain several Emotional Phases
as expectations, perceived control, concern, or trust change over time.

As Figure 1 illustrates, Emotional Phases and operational stages represent different dimensions of the same journey.
An operational stage may contain multiple Emotional Phases, while an Emotional Phase may extend across more than one
operational step. This distinction allows changes in the user’s experience and associated risks to be examined even when the
formal process remains unchanged.
3.3.5 Diagnostic chain
The diagnostic chain connects Emotional Phase, cognitive or emotional condition, plausible driver, behavioral risk,
and observable consequences. For example, prolonged waiting may be associated with anxiety and low perceived control;
plausible drivers may include high stakes and little information about timing; and risks may include repeated checking,
escalation, disengagement, or missing a later action.
Identified risks may be prioritized by considering their plausibility, the potential severity of the consequence, the
frequency or population exposed, and the possibility of intervention. This prioritization helps focus Design on the risks whose
prevention offers the greatest value to the journey and the operation.
The chain is probabilistic: the objective is not to assume that a condition causes a particular behavior, but to identify
plausible risks in advance to guide the design of a journey capable of responding preventively to those risks.
Figure 2 summarizes the four connected movements through which Guided Uncertainty translates this diagnostic
logic into preventive intervention, execution, measurement, and continuous learning.

3.3.6 Understand
Understand maps the journey from the Guided User’s perspective. It identifies operational moments, Emotional
Phases, expected cognitive-emotional conditions, plausible drivers, forms of uncertainty, behavioral risks, and potential
consequences. The mapping should reflect actual guidance—human, digital, or hybrid—and, when relevant, plausible
disruptions to normal flow.
Understand requires evidence discipline. Expected states may be investigated through interviews, complaints,
administrative data, contact reasons, practical observation, surveys, prior cases, frontline professional experience, or existing
research. When available, journey data—such as abandonment, errors, resubmissions, repeated contacts, time between steps,
and escalation patterns—can help locate where risks concentrate and which phases require deeper investigation. Observed
evidence must be separated from inference.
The framework’s replicability depends on criteria clear enough for different evaluators to independently delimit the
same journey, identify the main Emotional Phases, record the evidence used, and reach sufficiently consistent priority
diagnoses.
3.3.7 Design
Design converts diagnosis into preventive intervention involving process structure, sequence, information,
expectations, timing, preparation, communication, interaction, and legitimate opportunities for action. Instead of waiting for
anxiety, confusion, urgency, or distrust to become visible, Design prepares the journey in advance for reasonably foreseeable
conditions in each Emotional Phase.
This includes designing for plausible adverse conditions before they need to be individually identified, provided the
intervention remains appropriate for other users. When relevant disruptions to flow are also foreseeable, Design can prepare
contingency responses—such as communication, expected actions, responsibilities, and escalation criteria—to limit secondary

More communication is not automatically better. Additional information can increase cognitive load or ambiguity;

Design also distinguishes between reducing avoidable uncertainty and mitigating the effects of uncertainty that must
remain.
3.3.8 Guide
Guide is the execution layer: it transforms preventive design into concrete guidance as the journey unfolds. This
function may be performed by people, digital systems, or hybrid architectures.
A technically adequate intervention can fail through inconsistent execution. Inadequate information may lead to
incorrect interpretation or action; handoffs may break continuity; and intensified conditions may require guidance different
from that anticipated in the standard flow. When a broader disruption occurs, Guide executes and adapts the previously
designed contingency response.
Guide includes proactivity, information quality, continuity, calibration, and escalation. Calibration adapts the form of
guidance to the needs of the journey or user without changing substantive criteria applicable to the case. Guidance can
anticipate foreseeable questions and risks instead of waiting for the problem to grow throughout the user’s journey. When
relevant human interaction exists, this may require listening, more humanized communication, greater responsiveness, or
escalation as the situation requires.
This logic requires organizational preparation. People, systems, and channels responsible for guidance need to execute
the objectives of the intervention consistently, including across handoffs.
Trust is contextual. In an advisory relationship, it may involve perceived competence and alignment; in a neutral
authority, it may involve perceptions of fairness, consistency, transparency, and respect.
3.3.9 Learn
Learn converts application into evidence. It compares expected and observed patterns, evaluates whether interventions
changed target risks or outcomes, documents limitations, unintended effects, and implementation failures, and feeds findings
back into the framework. When guidance occurs through multiple channels or a contingency response, Learn should evaluate,
when feasible, where execution worked, failed, or required escalation.
Findings may lead not only to adjustment of the intervention but also to revision of the Emotional Phases, drivers, and
risks originally identified, making Learn a stage for updating the diagnosis itself.
Measurement and data analysis should occur at multiple levels when feasible and begin from a baseline appropriate to
the problem being analyzed. Experience measures may assess clarity, control, trust—including trust in the organization or
institution when relevant—burden, or relevant emotional states; behavioral measures may include completion, response, error,
or abandonment; and operational measures may include contacts, escalation, service time, rework, or throughput. Joint analysis
of these measures makes it possible to examine not only whether change occurred, but where it occurred and whether the
observed pattern is consistent with the predicted mechanism.
Success may also occur when Outcome Uncertainty remains but its cognitive or emotional impact decreases, or when
those effects no longer produce certain behavioral or operational consequences.

3.4 Applying Guided Uncertainty to service scenarios
Guided Uncertainty can be applied at different points in a public service journey where uncertainty, cognitive and
emotional conditions, and execution requirements interact. Its application is not limited to waiting periods or requests for
additional information and may span entry into and preparation for a process, administrative checkpoints, transitions between
channels or units, disruptions to expected flow, and communication after a decision. The common element is not a specific
process step, but the presence of the framework’s domain conditions and risks that can be anticipated or mitigated through
changes in journey design or guidance.
3.4.1 Essential service journeys under uncertainty
Many public services involve outcomes of great importance to people’s lives—access to benefits, licenses,
authorizations, education, assistance, and other services—without the outcome or its timing being entirely under the user’s
control. Uncertainty can therefore be a legitimate feature of the journey itself: the agency needs to analyze information, apply
criteria, perform checks, or wait for steps that cannot be replaced by an immediate-outcome experience.
Guided Uncertainty proposes that these journeys be designed not only around the steps the organization must execute,
but also around the conditions under which the person will need to navigate them. This involves anticipating how uncertainty,
waiting, stakes, and changes in cognitive and emotional conditions may affect interpretation and behavior; reducing
unnecessary Journey Uncertainty; preparing the person for what can be anticipated; and structuring guidance for uncertainty
that legitimately remains.
The potential benefit is bilateral. For the user, a journey that is more understandable and predictable in its execution
may reduce confusion, errors, unnecessary effort, and, in some cases, the cognitive and emotional impact of uncertainty. For
the public organization, the same interventions may improve the quality of journey execution: more complete information,
fewer procedural errors, repeated contacts, corrections, and rework, better use of operational capacity, and, in certain contexts,
greater trust in the institution and in how the process is conducted. The objective is not to treat the citizen as a customer who
must be satisfied regardless of the outcome, but to structure a journey in which the user and the organization can perform their
respective roles more effectively while the outcome remains uncertain for the user.
3.4.2 Preparation and execution of critical actions
Many public service journeys require the person to correctly perform actions on which progress depends:
understanding requirements, gathering documentation, completing forms, attending interviews or assessments, responding to
additional requests, and meeting deadlines. In these situations, conditions such as anxiety, cognitive overload, urgency, low
perceived control, or confusion may interact with process complexity and increase the risk of error, omission, or incomplete
action.
Guided Uncertainty structures the early identification of where these risks may concentrate and the design of the
journey to respond to them before they produce consequences. This may involve not only making instructions clearer, but
adjusting sequence, timing, preparation, expectations, confirmations, and guidance to the identified risk. A request for

3.4.3 Waiting, transitions, and journey continuity
Waiting periods, handoffs between units or channels, and steps with no visible progress present a different problem.
Reducing processing time and improving how a person navigates a necessary wait are related but distinct objectives. The
process may remain operationally unchanged while the person’s experience changes as time passes, expectations go unmet, or
new decisions need to be made. This divergence between operational process and experience is precisely one reason for
separating Emotional Phases from the formal steps of the journey.
A transfer between units, for example, may be administratively correct and still generate uncertainty about who is
handling the case, whether prior information has been preserved, and what the next step will be. Likewise, the same period of
silence may initially be interpreted as normal processing but later raise questions about delay, missing information, or whether
action is required.
In these contexts, avoidable Journey Uncertainty can be reduced through better information about status, next steps,
responsibilities, expectations, and meaningful progress milestones, such as confirmation that information was received or that
no additional action is currently required. Repeated generic updates may provide communication without materially reducing
Journey Uncertainty. When Outcome Uncertainty or the wait itself cannot legitimately be eliminated, guidance may still, in
some cases, reduce their cognitive and emotional effects and prevent them from turning into repeated contacts, escalations,
abandonment, premature actions, or other journey costs. Guidance may occur through professionals, portals, notifications,
automated messages, or combinations of these mechanisms, preserving continuity even when different actors participate in the
process.
3.4.4 Flow disruptions and contingency responses
Journeys may also be affected by system failures, extraordinary delays, channel or infrastructure unavailability,
organizational errors, or other disruptions that divert the process from its expected flow. The primary cause may require a
technical, operational, or institutional response outside the scope of Guided Uncertainty, but how the human journey moves
through the disruption remains a design and guidance problem.
The temporary unavailability of a document-submission channel, for example, may lead users to resubmit the same
material through different means, generating duplication, uncertainty about which submission is valid, and additional work for
the organization.
When relevant types of disruption can be reasonably anticipated, Guided Uncertainty allows contingency responses to
be incorporated into journey design: what information should be provided, what the user should or should not do, how
expectations should be recalibrated, when new communications should occur, and when the situation requires escalation. The
objective is to prevent an initial disruption from producing a secondary chain of confusion, duplicate attempts, inadequate
submissions, repeated contacts, loss of trust, and rework. Applying GU in this context does not replace correction of the
original failure; it seeks to prevent its effects on the journey from becoming greater than they need to be.

In many public services, the organization responsible for guiding the user also participates in the substantive decision

the final decision remains uncertain or is unfavorable. Guided Uncertainty therefore allows human experience and operational
efficiency to be treated as potentially related outcomes without confusing them with the merits of the substantive decision.
3.5 Testable propositions
The propositions below translate central Guided Uncertainty mechanisms into relationships that can be examined
empirically.
Proposition 1: Emotional Phases may add diagnostic value beyond operational steps.
In compatible journeys, segmentation by Emotional Phases may allow identification of relevant cognitive, emotional,
or behavioral variations that are not fully captured by operational steps and that may guide diagnosis or intervention design.
Proposition 2: Reducing modifiable Journey Uncertainty may influence experience, execution, and, in certain
journeys, the substantive outcome.
When Journey Uncertainty arises from modifiable characteristics of process, information, expectations, timing,
communication, or guidance, reducing it may improve understanding, preparation, trust, or execution and, through that
pathway, influence experiential, behavioral, or operational outcomes. In journeys in which the Guided User’s behavior or
performance constitutes a legitimate part of the decision-making process, these effects may also contribute to the substantive
outcome.
Proposition 3: Guidance may mitigate the effects of Outcome Uncertainty even when it remains.
When Outcome Uncertainty cannot legitimately be eliminated, appropriately structured process and guidance may
reduce its cognitive or emotional impact and/or prevent those effects from becoming harmful behavioral or operational
consequences.
Proposition 4: Distinct cognitive-emotional conditions may require different responses to the same operational
situation.
In the same operational situation, different cognitive or emotional conditions and their drivers may produce distinct
risks. Responses oriented to these differences may produce different outcomes from a uniform intervention based only on the
operational step or observed problem.
Proposition 5: Improvements in one outcome dimension do not imply equivalent improvements in others.
An intervention may improve operational outcomes without eliminating negative emotion, reduce the impact of an
emotion without eliminating it, or improve experience without changing operational performance. Changes in one class of

accommodation is also appropriate and does not introduce material harm for users who do not experience the condition, it may
reduce behavioral or operational risks without depending on prior individual diagnosis.

4. Discussion
4.1 Behavioral and psychological foundations
Guided Uncertainty integrates mechanisms informed by established literatures, which provide theoretical grounding
for its main components and help situate its contribution in relation to existing knowledge.
4.1.1 Appraisal, stress, and coping
The transactional model of stress proposed by Lazarus and Folkman (1984) emphasizes appraisal: people evaluate
what is at stake and whether the resources available are sufficient to deal with the demands they face. This perspective is
relevant to public service journeys because the same objective uncertainty may have different psychological meaning
depending on stakes, information, perceived resources, prior experience, and control. A waiting period may be tolerable when
its purpose and expected duration are understood, but threatening when the outcome is important and the user cannot interpret
what the delay means.
Guided Uncertainty therefore does not treat uncertainty as synonymous with anxiety. It treats uncertainty as a
condition that may be appraised in different ways and associated with multiple states, including concern, hope, frustration,
confusion, distrust, relief, or relative stability.
4.1.2 Emotion in bureaucratic interactions
Hattke et al. (2020) provide direct evidence from public administration that bureaucratic conditions can elicit distinct
emotions. In their laboratory experiment, administrative burden and delay generated significant negative emotional responses,
especially confusion, frustration, and anger. As a laboratory study, its results provide evidence regarding the occurrence and
differentiation of these emotional responses without determining how they manifest in every administrative journey.
For Guided Uncertainty, the importance of Hattke et al. is not to prove a specific Emotional Phase or establish that
emotion causes repeated contact or error. The study shows that emotional response to bureaucratic procedures is empirically
measurable and that different administrative conditions can produce distinguishable affective reactions. The framework
extends this problem temporally by asking whether relevant cognitive-emotional configurations change throughout a journey
and whether those changes provide incremental diagnostic value beyond operational steps.

4.1.3 Uncertainty management
Brashers’s (2001) uncertainty management perspective is important because it rejects the premise that uncertainty is
always negative or that reducing it is always the appropriate objective. People may seek, avoid, maintain, or reinterpret
uncertainty depending on context. A public agency therefore should not manufacture certainty where none exists, and an
uncertainty-centered framework should not define success as making the user certain about an outcome that cannot be known.
Viglia et al. (2019), in the context of services and payment decisions, distinguish uncertainty related to the service
process and outcome; Ulaga and Kohli (2018), in the context of business-to-business solutions, distinguish need, process, and
outcome uncertainty. Guided Uncertainty develops this family of distinctions at the journey level and assigns it a specific
diagnostic function. Journey Uncertainty concerns what the person does not understand about navigating the path: what is
happening, what an event means, what to expect, timing, next steps, required actions, or whether an action was completed.
Outcome Uncertainty concerns the substantive outcome or its timing. The framework uses the distinction to decide what can be
reduced through design and guidance and what must be navigated without false assurance.
Research on waiting further supports distinguishing objective duration from the experience of waiting. Expectations,
information, and perceived control can influence how waiting is experienced even when its actual duration does not change
(Hui & Zhou, 1996; Nie, 2000; Taylor, 1994).
4.1.4 Perceived control and legitimate participation
Langer and Rodin (1976) showed that meaningful choice and personal responsibility can affect participation and wellbeing in an institutional setting. Guided Uncertainty does not generalize this finding into a universal prescription to “give users
control.” In many public service contexts, the user cannot control the decision and should not be led to believe otherwise.
The relevant principle is legitimate perceived control. Where real choices or actions exist, they should be visible and
usable. This may include knowing what information can be prepared, when action is required, which channel can be used, what
the person can monitor, and what genuinely remains outside their influence. The distinction matters because false control can
be as harmful as opacity.
4.1.5 Responsiveness and service encounters
Service research demonstrates that direct encounters influence how people evaluate an organization. Bitner (1990), for
example, showed that employee responses and explanations can shape evaluations of service encounters. Perceived Partner
Responsiveness, originating in research on intimacy and close relationships (Reis & Shaver, 1988; Laurenceau et al., 1998), is
more distant from citizen-state interaction and should not be transplanted as though the relationships were equivalent. Its
narrower relevance is that people form judgments about whether the other party understands, validates, and responds
appropriately to their concerns.
For Guided Uncertainty, these literatures support an applied proposition: in important and uncertain journeys, the
quality of guidance may affect interpretation, trust, and execution. This guidance is a function of the journey and may be
performed by systems, automated communications, professionals, or combinations of these mechanisms. When human
interaction occurs, correct and timely information, anticipation of questions, confirmation of understanding, continuity, and
responses proportionate to intensified conditions may mitigate risks that late, generic, or inconsistent guidance would
aggravate. In digital environments, the same logic requires examining whether interfaces, notifications, status messages,
emails, confirmations, next steps, and escalation mechanisms provide guidance appropriate to the anticipated conditions and
risks. In the public sector, this does not require a personal bond or alignment with the user’s desired outcome; trust may mean

4.1.6 Procedural justice
Research on procedural justice provides the strongest theoretical basis for this distinction in authority or neutrality
environments. Tyler’s (1990) work shows that evaluations of legal and governmental authorities depend not only on favorable
outcomes but also on characteristics of the process. Voice, neutrality, respect, and trustworthiness are recurring elements in
judgments of procedural justice.
This is directly relevant when the organization responsible for guidance also participates in the Decision Authority—
the person, unit, or structure responsible for the substantive decision about the outcome—or when that authority is located
within the organization itself. Guided Uncertainty separates the role of guiding the user from the role of determining the
substantive outcome, even when both responsibilities are within the same organization. The separation avoids a conceptual
error: good guidance does not require the agency to favor the outcome desired by the user.
A licensing authority may apply a criterion neutrally while also explaining requirements, preserving continuity,
communicating respectfully, and making the process understandable. Procedural justice provides a theoretical basis for
explaining why these characteristics may matter independently of substantive victory. This perspective is particularly relevant
to Guided Uncertainty because it helps explain how the quality of guidance and procedural experience may influence the user’s
relationship with the process even when the organization cannot control or anticipate the substantive outcome.
Taken together, these literatures provide theoretical grounding for central Guided Uncertainty mechanisms and for
how the framework connects uncertainty, cognitive and emotional conditions, preventive design, guidance, and behavioral and
operational consequences throughout the journey.
4.2 Related approaches and comparative positioning
The closest comparison set for Guided Uncertainty is not a single competing framework. It is a family of approaches
that address important parts of the problem: administrative burden analysis, behavioral insights, behavioral journey mapping,
sludge audits, service design, and structured behavioral intervention toolkits.
4.2.1 U.S. Department of Labor behavioral interventions
The U.S. Department of Labor Behavioral Interventions (DOL-BI) initiative’s Practitioner’s Playbook describes a sixstep process—Understand, Diagnose, Design, Support, Test, and Learn—for identifying behavioral barriers, developing
interventions, supporting their implementation, testing them, and incorporating results into program learning (Darling et al.,
2017). DOL-BI provides a relevant example of how behavioral principles can be translated into a structured sequence of
diagnosis, intervention, and learning within public programs.
Guided Uncertainty organizes a distinct and more delimited problem: important service journeys in which material
uncertainty remains outside the user’s control while a person, organization, or system plays a significant role in guidance.
Within this domain, GU distinguishes Outcome Uncertainty from Journey Uncertainty, analyzes how cognitive and emotional
conditions evolve across Emotional Phases that need not coincide with operational steps, and uses this diagnosis to proactively
structure process, information, communication, and guidance.
This difference also appears in the Guide movement. In Guided Uncertainty, guidance during unresolved uncertainty
is part of the framework’s architecture itself and may occur through the process, digital systems, automated communications,
professional action, or hybrid combinations. The comparison with DOL-BI therefore situates GU within a tradition of

4.2.2 Behavioral journey mapping
Office of Evaluation Sciences (OES) work with the Social Security Administration’s Ticket to Work program

Guided Uncertainty adds a distinct analytical layer by also organizing human experience through Emotional Phases,
defined by relevant changes in the cognitive and emotional conditions expected throughout the journey. These phases are
mapped independently of operational structure: an Emotional Phase may span several process steps, while a relevant change in
the person’s experience may occur even when the formal process remains at the same step or shows no visible progress. This
separation makes it possible to examine how different cognitive-emotional configurations may produce distinct risks within the
same operational structure and guide preventive responses specific to those conditions. Instead of depending on individual
identification of these conditions during execution, the framework allows reasonably foreseeable adverse conditions in each
phase and their associated behavioral and operational risks to be anticipated, incorporating preventive responses into the design
of process, information, communication, preparation, and guidance.
This logic also extends to journey execution. The Guide movement carries preventive design into the systems,
communications, human interactions, or hybrid combinations through which the person is actually guided while uncertainty
remains, and Learn connects observed outcomes to refinement of phases, risks, and interventions.
4.2.3 Sludge audits and administrative simplification
Sludge audits identify unjustified frictions that make actions or processes unnecessarily difficult. Guidance from the
Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) describes sludge audits as structured behavioral science
approaches for identifying, quantifying, and preventing unjustified frictions and psychological burdens in public services
(OECD, 2024). Sludge reduction shares with Guided Uncertainty’s Design movement the objective of identifying and reducing
avoidable friction, but begins from a different analytical problem.
Guided Uncertainty focuses especially on situations in which reducing friction does not eliminate the journey’s central
uncertainty. A benefit applicant may receive a clear notice and a simple form and still remain legitimately uncertain about
eligibility. A license applicant may understand all requirements and still face a review with an uncertain outcome. Guided
Uncertainty asks what should be reduced, what remains uncertain, and how process, information, and guidance should be
structured around the uncertainty that remains—including when a disruption to normal flow intensifies these conditions and
requires a previously prepared response.
4.2.4 OECD BASIC
The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) BASIC Toolkit provides a general process
for applying behavioral insights to public-sector problems, including behavioral analysis, development of intervention
strategies, testing, and implementation (OECD, 2019). It offers a broad structure for transforming behavioral knowledge into
interventions applicable to different public policy and service problems.
Guided Uncertainty operates in a more specific domain, but its difference is not limited to scope. In journeys in which

4.2.5 Service process and outcome uncertainty

This integration guides two distinct responses: reducing modifiable Journey Uncertainty and, when Outcome
Uncertainty must remain, structuring the journey to mitigate its cognitive-emotional effects and associated behavioral or
operational risks.
4.2.6 Administrative checkpoints and human-centered design
Kim, Herd, Jilke, Moynihan, and Rodden (2025) connect administrative burden and human-centered design through
the concept of administrative checkpoints—mandatory administrative requirements that a person must satisfy to advance
through a process. In a field experiment with 1,554 Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) applicants, textmessage reminders combined with greater flexibility in interview scheduling increased interview completion by approximately
10 percentage points and benefit approval by 6 to 7 percentage points compared with the group that received only the
traditional mailed reminder. Interviews in this group were also completed, on average, 3 to 4 days earlier, while reducing
learning costs and increasing applicants’ confidence in their ability to manage the process.
These findings are particularly relevant to Guided Uncertainty because they demonstrate that how an administrative
requirement is structured, communicated, and embedded in the journey can affect behavior, execution, and perceived
experience. Guided Uncertainty broadens the analytical focus by examining how these requirements are navigated under
cognitive and emotional conditions that may change while uncertainty persists and how risks associated with those conditions
can be anticipated in journey design and guidance.
The distinction between administrative checkpoint and Emotional Phase is central to this relationship. A checkpoint
identifies an administrative requirement necessary for the person to advance; an Emotional Phase identifies a period of the
journey characterized by a cognitive and emotional configuration sufficiently similar to guide diagnosis and intervention. The
same Emotional Phase may span several checkpoints, while a phase change may occur without any new checkpoint or
operational advance. The two concepts therefore segment different dimensions of the same journey: checkpoints help locate
what the process requires; Emotional Phases help explain the conditions under which the person navigates those requirements
and which risks can be anticipated as a result.

4.2.8 Comparative positioning
The approaches examined in this section provide important antecedents for different components of Guided
Uncertainty, but the framework articulates them within its own diagnostic and intervention logic. In journeys marked by
relevant uncertainty, GU distinguishes uncertainty associated with the outcome from uncertainty produced by navigating the
process itself; segments the journey into Emotional Phases independent of operational steps; and uses the conditions identified
in each phase to anticipate behavioral and operational risks before they necessarily manifest.
This diagnosis guides preventive design of process, information, communication, preparation, and guidance for
reasonably foreseeable adverse conditions in each phase. The intervention may seek to reduce avoidable Journey Uncertainty
and, when Outcome Uncertainty must remain, reduce its cognitive and emotional impact and the likelihood that its effects
produce errors, incomplete actions, repeated contacts, abandonment, rework, or other execution problems. Guide carries this
design into the ongoing journey through digital, human, or hybrid means, while Learn uses observed outcomes to revise
diagnosis, design, and guidance. The contribution proposed by Guided Uncertainty therefore lies not only in the domain to
which it applies, but in the architecture through which conditions under uncertainty are anticipated, translated into risks, and
used to proactively structure the journey before and during execution.

4.3 Interpretation of the framework’s contribution
The central argument of this article is that, in compatible journeys, cognitive and emotional conditions associated with
uncertainty may interact with process, information, and guidance in ways that simultaneously affect user experience and the
agency’s operational execution.
First, administrative burden can be analyzed dynamically. Learning, compliance, and psychological costs may change
throughout the journey; Emotional Phases offer a testable hypothesis for capturing this variation and guiding preventive
responses.
Second, not all uncertainty should be treated as friction to be eliminated. Part of Outcome Uncertainty may be
legitimate and still have modifiable cognitive and emotional effects. The organization may not make the outcome more
predictable, but it may, in some cases, reduce anxiety, insecurity, confusion, or loss of control and prevent these conditions
from producing avoidable consequences for the user and the operation.
Third, guidance deserves to be treated as an operational component. It may be executed by people, systems, or hybrid
architectures. Inadequate guidance may generate error, a new requirement, rework, and additional waiting; proactive and
coherent guidance may reduce the probability that these effects accumulate throughout the journey. The same preventive logic
may be prepared for relevant flow disruptions without turning GU into a general incident-management framework.
Fourth, measurement should distinguish outcome classes. The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP)
uploader illustrates the point: it reduced denials for incomplete information without significantly increasing participation.
Likewise, reducing the impact of uncertainty may constitute a relevant outcome even when Outcome Uncertainty itself
remains.
4.4 Validation, limitations, and research agenda
Guided Uncertainty was developed and refined through practical experience in real-world service contexts. Its
principal mechanisms are consistent with established findings across relevant behavioral, administrative, and service
literatures, while the framework brings these mechanisms into a distinct architecture centered on uncertainty throughout the
service journey. Continued application and empirical research can further document its effects across different service contexts
and examine how these effects vary across journey conditions.
Future research can examine the replicability of Guided Uncertainty through applications that document the journey,
diagnosis, interventions, and outcomes and compare the consistency of diagnoses produced by different evaluators. An
Implementation Guide under development seeks to operationalize the framework’s principal constructs and application criteria
to support greater consistency across applications. Combining administrative, behavioral, and experience evidence may also
strengthen measurement of the conditions and effects identified by the framework.
Beyond the framework’s aggregate performance, future research may examine the incremental value of its distinctive
components, including Emotional Phases, Journey Uncertainty, preventive Design, and Guide.
The Guided Uncertainty research agenda includes questions about generalization, mechanisms, implementation, and
measurement. The practical experiences that contributed to its development provide an initial empirical basis that can be
expanded through independent applications in different contexts.
A central line of inquiry is the incremental value of the proposed constructs: to what extent Emotional Phases reveal
relevant information beyond operational steps and Journey Uncertainty adds diagnostic capacity to existing process-uncertainty
constructs. It is also important to understand how different cognitive and emotional conditions—such as anxiety, confusion,
distrust, low perceived control, and cognitive overload—interact with different drivers, risks, and contexts.

Empirical priorities include inter-rater reliability; incremental value of Emotional Phases and Journey Uncertainty;
comparison between preventive interventions and reactive responses; effects of guidance while Outcome Uncertainty remains;
human, digital, and hybrid guidance; and independent applications across different public services.

5. Final Considerations
Public service journeys often contain uncertainty that cannot legitimately be eliminated. This does not mean that its
cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and operational effects are equally inevitable.
Guided Uncertainty proposes an architecture for identifying how cognitive and emotional conditions change
throughout these journeys, anticipating risks, distinguishing avoidable uncertainty from uncertainty that must remain,
proactively structuring process and guidance, and learning from observed outcomes. This guidance may be human, digital, or
hybrid and, when relevant, include preparation for plausible disruptions.
An agency may be unable to anticipate whether a benefit will be approved or when a decision will be issued, but it
can improve information, preparation, expectations, legitimate perceived control, and guidance; reduce, in some cases, the
impact of uncertainty; and decrease errors, incomplete actions, repeated contacts, escalations, and rework. This can
simultaneously benefit the user and the operational efficiency of the service.
The central proposition is testable: in compatible journeys, how cognitive and emotional conditions under uncertainty
are anticipated, accommodated by design, and guided during execution may influence human experience and journey
performance even when the substantive outcome remains outside the user’s control.
