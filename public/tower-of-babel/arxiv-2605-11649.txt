Homothetical surfaces with constant mean curvature in hyperbolic space
Rafael Belli, Rafael López

Abstract
. We classify all homothetical surfaces with constant mean curvature H H in the hyperbolic space ℍ 3 \mathbb{H}^{3} . Using the upper half-space model with standard coordinates ( x , y , z ) (x,y,z) , these surfaces are defined by the relation z = ϕ ⁡ ( x ) ​ ψ ​ ( y ) z=\phi(x)\psi(y) , where ϕ \phi and ψ \psi are smooth functions of one variable. We demonstrate that any such surface is necessarily parabolic, meaning that either ϕ \phi or ψ \psi is a constant function. Our results cover the minimal case ( H = 0 H=0 ), the case H 2 ≠ 1 H^{2}\neq 1 , and the critical case H 2 = 1 H^{2}=1 , thereby extending the existing classification of parabolic surfaces in hyperbolic space.

1.
Introduction and statement of the main result

Let
\mathbb{H}^{3}
be the
3
-dimensional hyperbolic space, and consider the
upper half-space model of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, that is,

(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3},g_{\mathbb{H}})
, where
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:z>0\}
is endowed with the metric

g_{\mathbb{H}}=\frac{1}{z^{2}}g_{\mathbb{E}},

and
g_{\mathbb{E}}=dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}
is the Euclidean metric of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Using this model, we give the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

A surface
S
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
is said to be homothetical if it is locally parametrized by

(1)

z=\phi(x)\psi(y),

where
\phi\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\psi\colon J\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are
positive smooth functions of one variable.

We emphasize that the definition of a homothetical surface adopted here is model-dependent, as it relies on the Cartesian coordinates of the upper half-space model. However, this choice is motivated by the computational transparency of the metric in these coordinates. The model serves as an optimal workspace where the interplay between Euclidean homotheties and hyperbolic isometries is most evident, facilitating a clearer classification of the surfaces under study.

This becomes clear with the first examples of
homothetical surfaces, where one of the functions,
\phi
or
\psi
, is constant. In such cases,
S
is a parabolic surface in the sense
that it is invariant
under a one-parameter family of parabolic isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. For
example, suppose that
\psi
is constant and consider the one-parameter group
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
of parabolic isometries
P_{t}
, where
P_{t}(x,y,z)=(x,y+t,z)
. Then
S
is invariant under
\mathcal{P}
, that is,

P_{t}(S)=S
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. A simple example of a homothetical surface is a
horosphere
z=m
, where
m>0
is a constant. In this case, we can choose
\phi(x)=1
and
\psi(y)=m

for all
x,y
.

In this paper, we investigate homothetical surfaces with constant mean
curvature
H
. Note that if
\phi
or
\psi
is constant, then the equation
H=c
reduces to
a second-order ODE. Indeed, suppose
\psi
is constant,
\psi(y)=m>0
. Then
S
is parametrized by

\Phi(x,y)=(x,y,m\phi(x))
. A straightforward computation shows that the equation
H=c

can be written as

\phi\phi^{\prime\prime}+2(1+\phi^{\prime 2})-2H(1+\phi^{\prime 2})^{3/2}=0.

The classification of the parabolic surfaces with constant mean
curvature was carried out by Gomes
[
6
]
(see also,
[
4
,
7
]
; cf. Fig.
1
. Regarding translation surfaces in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, that is,
surfaces written as
z=\phi(x)+\psi(y)
, the second author classified
those with zero mean curvature
[
11
]
.

The purpose of this paper is to provide a full classification of the
homothetical surfaces of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with constant mean curvature.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
S
be a homothetical surface in
\mathbb{H}^{3}

parametrized by (
1
). If
S
has constant mean curvature, then either

\phi
or
\psi
is a constant function.

To provide context for this result, we refer to the literature on
homothetical surfaces with constant curvature in other ambient
spaces. The first approach to homothetical surfaces appeared
in
[
15
]
, where minimal, non-degenerate homothetical surfaces in
the Lorentz-Minkowski space were classified. Minimal homothetical
hypersurfaces have been investigated in both Euclidean and semi-Euclidean spaces (
[
10
,
16
]
). In particular, planes and helicoids are
the only homothetical minimal surfaces of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. If we assume that the
mean curvature is constant, the only homothetical surface is the
right-cylinder
[
8
]
. Assuming that the Gauss curvature is constant,
the classification of homothetical surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
was completed in
[
13
]
(see also

[
9
]
in the context of separable surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
). The study of homothetical surfaces with constant
curvature has been extended to other ambient spaces:
[
1
,
2
,
14
]
.

The proof of Theorem

is divided into different cases according

(Section

(Section

2.
Preliminares

In this section, we derive a suitable expression for the mean curvature of a homothetical surface.
It is well known that the mean curvature
H
of a surface in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
when the upper halfspace model is adopted, is related to its Euclidean mean curvature
H_{e}
by the formula

(2)

H=zH_{e}+N_{3},

where
N=(N_{1},N_{2},N_{3})
is the Euclidean unit normal vector to the surface
[
12
, p. 191]
.

As defined in the introduction, a homothetical surface is given by
z=\phi(x)\psi(y)
. To facilitate the computations, we treat these surfaces as a particular class of separable surfaces. Indeed, by applying the logarithm, we can represent a homothetical surface in the implicit form

f(x)+g(y)+h(z)=0,

where

(3)

\phi(x)=e^{-f(x)},\quad\psi(y)=e^{-g(y)},\quad h(z)=\log(z).

Note that in this specific case, the function
h
is fixed as
h(z)=\log(z)
, which implies
z=e^{-f(x)-g(y)}
. Since the claim of Theorem
1.2
is that either
\phi
or
\psi
is constant, this is equivalent to proving that either
f
or
g
is constant. The proof will proceed by contradiction, assuming that neither
f
nor
g
is a constant function.

To generalize this approach, consider three smooth functions
f:I_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
,
g:I_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
h:I_{3}\to\mathbb{R}
. We define a separable surface
S
as the set

S=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{H}^{3}\colon f(x)+g(y)+h(z)=0\}.

If
F(x,y,z)=f(x)+g(y)+h(z)
, the Euclidean unit normal vector field is

N=\frac{\nabla F}{|\nabla F|}=\frac{\left(f^{\prime},g^{\prime},h^{\prime}\right)}{\sqrt{f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2}+h^{\prime 2}}}

The Euclidean mean curvature
H_{e}
is given by

H_{e}=-\text{div}\left(\frac{\nabla F}{|\nabla F|}\right)=\frac{\nabla^{2}F(\nabla F,\nabla F)-|\nabla F|^{2}\Delta F}{2|\nabla F|^{3}}.

Since the Hessian
\nabla^{2}F
is diagonal, the above expression simplifies to

H_{e}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(g^{\prime 2}+h^{\prime 2})f^{\prime\prime}+(f^{\prime 2}+h^{\prime 2})g^{\prime\prime}+(f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2})h^{\prime\prime}}{(f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2}+h^{\prime 2})^{3/2}}.

Substituting these into (
2
), we obtain the hyperbolic mean curvature:

H=-\frac{z}{2}\frac{(g^{\prime 2}+h^{\prime 2})f^{\prime\prime}+(f^{\prime 2}+h^{\prime 2})g^{\prime\prime}+(f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2})h^{\prime\prime}}{(f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2}+h^{\prime 2})^{3/2}}+\frac{h^{\prime}}{(f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2}+h^{\prime 2})^{1/2}}.

Let us denote

u=f(x),\quad v=g(y),\quad w=h(z).

From the expression for
h(z)
in (
3
), we have

z=e^{w}.

Since
f
and
g
are assume to be non-constant, we can introduce new functions of the variables
u
,
v
and
w
defined by

X(u)=f^{\prime 2},\quad Y(v)=g^{\prime 2},\quad Z(w)=h^{\prime 2}=e^{-2w}.

In particular, we have

X^{\prime}=2f^{\prime\prime},\quad Y^{\prime}=2g^{\prime\prime},\quad Z^{\prime}=2h^{\prime\prime}=-2e^{-2w}.

The equation for the mean curvature then becomes

(4)

(Y+e^{-2w})X^{\prime}+(X+e^{-2w})Y^{\prime}-2(X+Y)e^{-2w}-4e^{-2w}(X+Y+e^{-2w})=-4He^{-w}(X+Y+e^{-2w})^{3/2}.

Throughout this paper, we will differentiate equations involving functions of
u,v
, and
w
. Since these variables are constrained by
u+v+w=0
, we employ the following lemma:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
Q(u,v,w)
be a smooth function. If
Q(u,v,w)=0
on the plane
\Pi=\{(u,v,w):u+v+w=0\}
, then the following holds on
\Pi
:

Q_{u}=Q_{v}=Q_{w},

where
Q_{u},Q_{v},Q_{w}
denote the partial derivatives of
Q
.

3.
Case
H=0

Equation (
4
) can be written as a polynomial equation in
P:=e^{-w}
given by

(5)

A_{0}(u,v)+A_{2}(u,v)P^{2}-4P^{4}=0,

where

(6)

\begin{split}A_{0}&=X^{\prime}Y+XY^{\prime},\\
A_{2}&=-6(X+Y)+X^{\prime}+Y^{\prime}.\end{split}

Note that the leading coefficient associated with
P^{4}
is constant. Although the
coefficients
A_{0}
and
A_{2}
depend on the variables
u
and
v
, they actually depend only on on
w=-(u+v)
owing to Vieta’s formulas, which relate the roots of a polynomial to its coefficients.
Viewing Eq. (
5
) as a polynomial in
e^{-2w}
, we then have two roots
r_{1}(w)
and
r_{2}(w)
satisfying

A_{0}=-4r_{1}(w)r_{2}(w),

A_{2}=4(r_{1}(w)+r_{2}(w)).

Applying Lemma
2.1
to
A_{0}
and
A_{2}
, we obtain
(A_{0})_{u}-(A_{0})_{v}=0
and
(A_{2})_{u}-(A_{2})_{v}=0
, which can be written as

(7)

\displaystyle X^{\prime\prime}Y-XY^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(8)

\displaystyle X^{\prime\prime}-6X^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y^{\prime\prime}-6Y^{\prime}.

From (
7
), there exists a constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, such that

(9)

\frac{X^{\prime\prime}}{X}=\lambda=\frac{Y^{\prime\prime}}{Y}.

(1)

Case
\lambda=0
. From (
9
), the functions
X
and
Y
are linear, but (
8
) implies that the slopes coincide for both. Thus, there exist constants
m,a_{1},b_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

X(u)=mu+a_{1},\quad Y(v)=mv+b_{1}.

Substituting these into (
5
), we obtain

m(a_{1}+b_{1}-mw)-2\left(3(a_{1}+b_{1})-m-3mw\right)e^{-2w}-4e^{-4w}=0.

Since the functions
\{1,w,we^{-2w},e^{-2w},e^{-4w}\}
are linearly
independent, we arrive at a contradiction.

(2)

Case
\lambda=m^{2}>0
. In this case,

X(u)=a_{1}\cosh(mu)+a_{2}\sinh(mu),

Y(v)=b_{1}\cosh(mv)+b_{2}\sinh(mv),

with
a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. Substituting these into (
8
), we obtain

(10)

\begin{split}0&=(a_{2}m-6a_{1})\sinh(mu)+(a_{1}m-6a_{2})\cosh(mu)\\
&+(6b_{1}-b_{2}m)\sinh(mv)+(6b_{2}-b_{1}m)\cosh(mv).\end{split}

It follows that the coefficients must vanish. Since not all
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
can vanish,
we deduce
m^{2}=36
, implying
m=\pm 6
. Assuming
m=6
(a similar argument holds if
m=-6
), from (
10
), we have
a_{2}=a_{1}
and
b_{2}=b_{1}
. Now (
5
)
becomes

12a_{1}b_{1}e^{-6w}-4e^{-4w}=0,

which is a contradiction.

(3)

Case
\lambda=-m^{2}<0
. In this case,

X(u)=a_{1}\cos(mu)+a_{2}\sin(mu),

Y(v)=b_{1}\cos(mv)+b_{2}\sin(mv),

with
a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. Substituting into (
8
), we obtain

\begin{split}0&=(-a_{2}m+6a_{1})\sin(mu)-(a_{1}m+6a_{2})\cos(mu)\\
&+(-6b_{1}+b_{2}m)\sin(mv)+(6b_{2}+b_{1}m)\cos(mv).\end{split}

Then, all coefficients must vanish. As in the case
\lambda=m^{2}
, we deduce that all
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are zero, yielding a contradiction.

4.
Case
H\not=0
and
H^{2}\not=1

Suppose
H\not=0
. Squaring (
4

A_{0}(u,v)+A_{2}(u,v)e^{-2w}+A_{4}(u,v)e^{-4w}+A_{6}(u,v)e^{-6w}+16(1-H^{2})e^{-8w}=0,

(12)

\begin{split}A_{0}&=(YX^{\prime}+XY^{\prime})^{2},\\
A_{2}&=-2(24H^{2}(X^{2}Y+XY^{2})+8H^{2}(X^{3}+Y^{3})+(XY^{\prime}+X^{\prime}Y)(6(X+Y)-X^{\prime}-Y^{\prime})),\\

Since

A_{0}

.
Applying Lemma (
2.1
) to the coefficient

X^{\prime\prime}Y-XY^{\prime\prime}=0.

Thus, there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

\frac{X^{\prime\prime}}{X}=\lambda=\frac{Y^{\prime\prime}}{Y}.

(1)

Case
\lambda=0
. In this case, there exist constants
a_{i},b_{i}
such that

X(u)=a_{1}u+a_{2},\quad Y(v)=b_{1}v+b_{2}.

Applying the lemma again to the coefficient
A_{6}
, we have

0=6(H^{2}-1)(X^{\prime}-Y^{\prime})+X^{\prime\prime}-Y^{\prime\prime}.

Substituting
X
and
Y

. Substituting these back into the initial equation (

w^{3}e^{-2w},w^{2}e^{-2w},w^{2}e^{-4w},w^{2},we^{-2w},we^{-4w},we^{-6w},w,e^{-2w},e^{-4w},\\
e^{-6w},e^{-8w},1
. Since these functions are linearly independent, all coefficients must vanish. However, the coefficient of

is

, which leads to a contradiction.

(2)

Case
\lambda=m^{2}>0
. In this case, there exist
a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

X(u)=a_{1}\cosh(mu)+a_{2}\sinh(mu),

We now consider the coefficient

of (

and

All coefficients must vanish. From

\begin{split}A_{3}&=6a_{2}mH^{2}(3a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}),\\

(3)

Case
\lambda=m^{2}<0
. Similarly, there exist

5.
Case

Equation (
11

A_{0}(u,v)+A_{2}(u,v)e^{-2w}+A_{4}(u,v)e^{-4w}+A_{6}(u,v)e^{-6w}=0,

(14)

\begin{split}A_{0}&=-(YX^{\prime}+XY^{\prime})^{2},\\

.

.

Thus, there exists some neighborhood
V_{3}\subset V_{2}
on which
3Y^{\prime\prime}+2a_{2}Y^{\prime}-a_{1}\neq 0
.

Case 3:

From (
34
), suppose
2\lambda Y^{\prime\prime}+\lambda a_{2}Y^{\prime}+a_{1}a_{2}Y=0

in
V_{3}
. This implies,

Y^{\prime\prime}=-\frac{a_{2}}{2}Y^{\prime}-\frac{a_{1}a_{2}}{2\lambda}Y.

In particular,
a_{2}\not=0
. Using this in (
18
), we have almost everywhere in
V_{3}
:

Y^{\prime}=\frac{-a_{1}a_{2}Y^{2}-2\lambda b_{1}Y}{\lambda((a_{2}+2b_{2})Y+2\lambda)},

and consequently,

(42)

Y^{\prime\prime}=-\frac{a_{2}Y((a_{1}-b_{1})\lambda+a_{1}b_{2}Y)}{\lambda(2\lambda+(a_{2}+2b_{2})Y)}.

Substituting both equalities into (
17
), we obtain a polynomial equation
\sum_{n=0}^{3}C_{n}Y^{n}=0
, where the
C_{n}
are polynomials in
X
and
X^{\prime}
. Thus, all
C_{n}
must vanish. The coefficient
C_{0}
is

C_{0}=4\lambda^{3}(a_{1}X+\lambda X^{\prime})(X^{\prime}(a_{2}X+\lambda)+b_{1}X).

(1):

Subcase
a_{1}X+\lambda X^{\prime}=0
identically. Then
X^{\prime}=-\frac{a_{1}}{\lambda}X
. Substituting this into the other coefficients, we get

\begin{split}C_{1}&=2a_{2}\lambda^{2}(a_{1}+b_{1})^{2}X^{2},\\
C_{2}&=4a_{1}a_{2}(a_{1}+b_{1})(a_{2}+b_{2})\lambda X^{2},\\
C_{3}&=2a_{1}^{2}a_{2}(a_{2}+b_{2})^{2}X^{2}.\end{split}

From the expression of
Y^{\prime\prime}
in (
42
), we have
a_{2}\not=0
, and
a_{1}
and
b_{1}
cannot both be zero. Thus we deduce
b_{1}=-a_{1}
and
b_{2}=-a_{2}
. On the other hand, solving
a_{1}X+\lambda X^{\prime}=0
yields
X(u)=m_{1}e^{-\frac{a_{1}}{\lambda}u}
. From the expression of
X^{\prime\prime}
in (
18
), we get
a_{2}=-\frac{a_{1}}{\lambda}
. Using this information for the function
Y
, we obtain

Y^{\prime}=\frac{a_{1}}{\lambda}Y
, which gives
Y(v)=m_{2}e^{\frac{a_{1}}{\lambda}v}
. This implies
YX^{\prime}+XY^{\prime}=0
, which is a contradiction.

(2):

Subcase
a_{1}X+\lambda X^{\prime}\not=0
and
X^{\prime}(a_{2}X+\lambda)+b_{1}X=0
identically. The arguments are similar as in the previous item and we omit the details. We have
X^{\prime}=-\frac{b_{1}}{\lambda+a_{2}X}X
. In particular
b_{1}\not=0
. Substituting in
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
and
C_{3}
, we obtain polynomial equations on
X
, whose coefficients must vanish. Hence, we find
b_{1}=a_{1}
,
b_{2}=0
. Finally,
Y^{\prime\prime}=0
in (
42
), which is a contradiction by the Claim L.

Therefore, there must exist some neighborhood
V_{4}\subset V_{3}
on which
2\lambda Y^{\prime\prime}+\lambda a_{2}Y^{\prime}+a_{1}a_{2}Y\neq 0
.

Case 4:

From (
35
), suppose
a_{2}Y^{\prime}-2a_{1}=0
in
V_{4}
. This implies that
Y
is
linear, a case already discarded.

Consequently, there exists a neighborhood
V_{5}\subset V_{4}
on which
a_{2}Y^{\prime}-2a_{1}\neq 0
. In this neighborhood, the polynomial
p
must be irreducible by construction. Thus, the polynomial

(43)

\begin{split}q(X,X^{\prime}):&=\frac{p(X,X^{\prime})}{p_{6}}=(\frac{p_{3}}{p_{6}}X+1)\Big(X^{\prime 2}+(\frac{p_{1}}{p_{6}}X^{2}+\frac{p_{4}}{p_{6}}X+\frac{p_{7}}{p_{6}})X^{\prime}\\
&+\frac{p_{2}}{p_{6}}X^{2}+\frac{p_{5}}{p_{6}}X\Big)\end{split}

is also irreducible for all
v\in V_{5}
.

Taking two values
v_{1},v_{2}\in V_{5}
, we obtain two irreducible polynomials
q_{1},q_{2}
of the same degree with the same root
(X,X^{\prime})
for all
u\in U_{0}
. By Lemma
5.1
, either
(X,X^{\prime})
assumes finitely many values (which implies
X
is constant, a contradiction) or there exists a function
\phi
such that
q_{1}=\phi q_{2}
. Since both polynomials have the same constant term with respect to
X^{\prime}
, it follows that
\phi=1
and thus
q_{1}=q_{2}
. By the arbitrariness of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
we conclude that all coefficients of
q
in (
43
)are constant for all
v\in V_{5}
. That is, by this Lemma, either
X
and
X^{\prime}
are constants in
U_{0}
, which
is a contradiction, or all of the coefficients
\frac{p_{j}}{p_{6}}
are
constants in
V_{5}
. In particular,
\frac{p_{7}}{p_{6}}=2Y^{\prime}
must be constant in
V_{5}
, which implies
Y^{\prime\prime}=0
, a contradiction by Claim L. Therefore, we conclude that the
case
\lambda\neq 0
is impossible.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
for the case
H^{2}=1
.
