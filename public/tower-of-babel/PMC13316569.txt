Milk-derived extracellular vesicles loaded with miR-146a-5p as a novel therapeutic strategy for allergic airway inflammation
Authors: Chan-Gu Li, Qi Sun, Tian Tian, Zi-Cong Wu, Ying-Chun Xie, Xiao-Qing Liu, Zhi-Rou Zhou, Long-Xin Huang, Xiao-Hui Deng, Si-Lin Yu, Qing-Ling Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious studies have demonstrated that miR-146a-5p can effectively inhibit the function of group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), which play a pivotal role in the initiation of allergic airway inflammation (AAI), thereby alleviating AAI. However, the application of miR-146a-5p faces challenges such as susceptibility to degradation and difficulty in crossing the cellular membrane. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), which naturally transport proteins and nucleic acids, have been proposed as promising RNA delivery vehicles. EVs can protect microRNAs (miRNAs) from degradation and facilitate their penetration across cellular membranes. However, the lack of scalable sources of EVs remains a challenge for their mass production, particularly in clinical application. Bovine milk has emerged as an advantageous source for the large-scale production of EVs because of its low cost and easy accessibility. Moreover, bovine milk-derived EVs (mEVs) have shown low immunogenicity and outstanding cross-species biosafety. In this study, we present the manufacturing methods and immune regulatory effects of mEVs loaded with miR-146a-5p (miR146a-5p-mEVs). Our findings indicated that mEVs exhibited outstanding biosafety and did not elicit significant systemic toxicity. Importantly, miR146a-5p-mEVs exhibited significant immunoregulatory functions in AAI, suggesting that miR146a-5p-mEVs could serve as a novel strategy for the treatment of allergic airway disease.Keywords: MT: delivery strategies, milk-derived extracellular vesicles, airway allergic inflammation, group 2 innate lymphoid cells, miRNA delivery

Introduction

Allergic airway inflammation (AAI) is a prevalent chronic airway inflammation affecting more than 40% of people in the world.1,2 AAI, including allergic rhinitis (AR) and asthma, often results in diminished quality of life due to symptoms such as sneezing and nasal congestion3 and thereby has emerged as a critical public health issue that necessitates urgent attention on a global scale.4,5,6 However, even with guideline-directed treatment, a substantial proportion of patients do not achieve adequate symptom control7 and the efficacy of allergen immunotherapy remains relatively low while adverse reactions may occur.8 Consequently, there is an urgent need for research into novel therapies for AAI.

T helper 2 cells (Th2) are traditionally regarded as the primary orchestrators of AAI.9 However, recent studies have highlighted the dominant role of group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) in initiating and maintaining AAI.10,11 ILC2s can be activated by epithelial cell-derived cytokines such as interleukin 25 (IL-25) and IL-33, leading to an increase in type 2 cytokines, including IL-5, IL-9, and IL-13.12,13,14 Additionally, ILC2s can facilitate the differentiation of naive CD4+ T cells into Th2 cells.15,16,17 We previously demonstrated that ILC2s are also associated with dendritic cell (DC) subsets: myeloid DCs promote ILC2 activity, and plasmacytoid DCs suppress ILC2 responses.18 In an AAI model, ILC2s accumulate in the lungs, lymph nodes, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), comprising more than 50% of the total IL-5+/IL-13+ cells in airway.10 In summary, ILC2s are pivotal in AAI and thus are promising targets for therapeutic intervention.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small noncoding RNAs with lengths of approximately 21–22 nucleotides. miRNAs have been shown to act as posttranscriptional regulators of various biological procedures via targeting specific mRNAs.19,20 miRNA-based therapy has emerged as a promising treatment for many diseases,20 including AAI. Among these, miR-146a-5p, a member of the highly conserved miR-146 family, has been identified as a key regulator of the immune system.21 Additionally, miR-146a-5p downregulates the expression of interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 1 (IRAK1) and TNF receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) by targeting their mRNAs.21,22,23 IRAK1 and TRAF6 are critical components of the classical nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathway and play important roles in inflammation. In the context of AAI, IL-33 and IL-25 are key activators of ILC2s. Upon binding to their respective receptors on ILC2s, these cytokines trigger downstream signaling cascades: IL-33 engages the ST2 receptor, recruiting MyD88 and IRAK1 to activate the MAPK pathway, or activates the NF-κB pathway via TRAF6; IL-25 directly recruits TRAF6 through its receptor to activate downstream signaling.12,24 Activation of these pathways induces GATA3 phosphorylation, promoting ILC2 activation and type 2 cytokine production. IRAK1 and TRAF6, which are the direct targets of miR-146a-5p, are central to these ILC2-activating pathways, positioning miR-146a-5p as a key negative regulator of ILC2-driven inflammation. Consistent with this, previous studies have demonstrated that miR-146a-5p inhibits the function of activated ILC2s and reduces the severity of AAI.22,23,25 Our own research has shown that mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived small extracellular vesicles (EVs) inhibit ILC2 function via miR-146a-5p and alleviate AAI.26

Nevertheless, the application of naked miRNAs faces significant challenges, such as inherent instability, poor cellular uptake,27,28 and the potential to trigger innate immune responses. The immune system recognizes single- and double-stranded RNA via Toll-like receptors (TLR3, TLR7, and TLR8) in endosomes and cytoplasmic receptors such as RIG-I and MDA5. For example, tumor-secreted miR-21 and miR-29a activate TLR8 in macrophages, while the let-7 family stimulates TLR7 in various immune and neural cells. Such immunostimulatory activity can cause unintended inflammation, limiting the safety and efficacy of naked miRNA therapeutics, as exemplified by the miR-34 mimic MRX34, whose clinical development was terminated due to severe immune-related adverse reactions.29 Currently, some synthetic carriers have been designed for the delivery of miRNAs, including liposomes, polymers, and other nanoparticles. These carriers can offer protection for these molecules28,30,31; however, their therapeutic efficacy has always been hindered by immunogenicity and the following severe inflammation.32,33 The immunogenicity of synthetic carriers represents a major barrier to their clinical translation. Studies have shown that lipid nanoparticle-induced inflammation is primarily driven by their lipid components, rather than the nucleic acid cargo itself.32 Even empty lipid nanoparticels (LNP) can induce robust immune responses.

Mechanistically, endosomal escape of LNP causes endosomal membrane damage, which is sensed by cytosolic galectins, leading to NLRP3 inflammasome activation and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α.32 LNP-induced inflammation can worsen pre-existing inflammation by more than 10-fold.34

EVs are nano-sized vesicles naturally secreted by a variety of cells, and these vesicles can be found in numerous body fluids of mammals, including blood, tears, milk, and urine. EVs inherently deliver proteins or nucleic acids,30,35 thereby making them natural drug vectors. Our previous studies have demonstrated that EVs derived from MSCs can effectively deliver miRNAs to alleviate AAI.26 But, the translation of MSC-EVs faces challenges such as limited yield and high costs. While EVs can be isolated from multiple sources, EVs derived from bovine milk (mEVs) stand out because of their high yield, remarkable cost effectiveness, and scalable production.36,37 mEVs possess the capacity to transport miRNAs and preserve their integrity,38 and their utility as delivery vehicles for therapeutic small RNA molecules, such as miRNAs and siRNAs, has been increasingly demonstrated in multiple studies.39 For instance, Meng et al. recently reported the successful delivery of miR-146a via mEVs, achieving cardioprotective effects in their experimental model.40 Furthermore, mEVs have been proven to exhibit low immunogenicity and biocompatibility.37,41 Additional studies have confirmed that mEVs can be taken up by human macrophages,42 indicating that mEVs can be internalized by living cells from different species. These characteristics make mEVs highly promising candidates for miRNA delivery. In this study, we first focused on the biocompatibility of mEVs. Following the loading of miR-146a-5p into mEVs, we aimed to investigate the effects of mEVs loaded with miR-146a-5p on ILC2s from patients with AR and mice with ILC2-dominant AAI.

Results

Purification and characterization of mEVs

After the removal of fat and casein from bovine milk, we obtained whey that was rich in mEVs. The mEVs were subsequently isolated using tangential flow filtration (Figure 1A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the presence of mEVs, which exhibited diameters of approximately 100 nm and displayed a characteristic lipid bilayer structure (Figure 1B). Further analysis using nanoparticle tracking analysis revealed that the mean diameter of the mEVs was approximately 120 nm, with a narrow size distribution, thereby confirming the uniformity of these vesicle particles (Figure 1C). Additionally, the average zeta potential of mEVs was approximately −8.79 mV (Figure 1D), as expected for naturally occurring EVs. Zeta potential is a key parameter for assessing the colloidal stability and surface charge of EVs. The negative surface charge of naturally occurring EVs, including those derived from milk and cell culture supernatants, is primarily attributed to their membrane composition, such as phosphatidylserine43 rich in sialic acid. Using western blotting, we further confirmed that mEVs were positive for conventional small EV-specific markers of CD9, CD63, CD81, Alix, and TSG101 (Figure 1E) and negative for the endoplasmic reticulum protein calnexin (Figure S1). In conclusion, the characterization of mEVs in relation to size, morphology, zeta potential, and specific markers collectively indicated that our mEV preparations were composed of canonical exosomes.

mEVs exhibit good biocompatibility

To assess the biocompatibility of mEVs, we first utilized airway epithelial cells BEAS-2B to evaluate the effects of various concentrations of mEVs on cell viability. Airway epithelial cells were treated with 5 gradient concentrations of mEVs (1 × 1010, 2 × 1010, 5 × 1010, 1 × 1011, and 1 × 1012 particles/mL) for the durations of 48 and 72 h, with a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)-treated group serving as the control. CCK8 assay was then employed to evaluate cell viability following treatment with mEVs. The results indicated that there was no significant decrease in cell viability after mEV treatment for 48 h (Figure 2A). To further confirm the biocompatibility of mEVs across different cell types, we extended our assessment to intestinal epithelial cells. Consistent with the findings in BEAS-2B cells, treatment with mEVs for up to 72 h did not induce any significant cytotoxicity in intestinal epithelial cells (Figure S2). These results collectively demonstrated that mEVs are non-toxic to both airway and intestinal epithelial cells, supporting their favorable safety profile for potential therapeutic applications.

Subsequently, we conducted an in vivo biosafety assessment using a mouse model. After intravenously administering mEVs at three different doses (1 × 1010, 1 × 1011, and 1 × 1012 mEVs) (Figure 2B), we evaluated several biochemical parameters indicative of organ function. These included markers of liver health, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT); markers of renal function, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (Cr); and markers associated with muscle or cardiac tissue integrity, including creatine kinase (CK) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). Additionally, we evaluated the changes in body weight and the morphological change of major organs. The results indicated that the levels of AST, ALT, BUN, and CK did not exhibit significant alterations after exposure to different dosages of mEVs; however, Cr and LDH levels displayed a downward trend after administration (Figure 2C). Furthermore, there were no notable declines in body weight (Figure 2D) or prominent changes in the morphology of major organs following multiple administrations of mEVs (Figure S3. Collectively, these results suggest that repeated administration of mEVs does not induce significant toxicity in mouse models, suggesting their potential as safe drug carriers for miRNA delivery.

Preparation and characterization of miR146a-5p-mEVs

The loading of miR-146a-5p mimics into mEVs was achieved through an extrusion approach (Figure 3A). Firstly, miR146a-5p-mEVs were characterized using TEM analyses, and the results revealed that both mEVs and miR146a-5p-mEVs exhibited vesicle structures with full integrity (Figure 3B). After the extrusion process, the diameter of mEVs loaded with miR-146a-5p was approximately 100 nm, which was slightly smaller than that of unmodified mEVs (120 nm) (Figure 3C). The average zeta potential for both mEVs and miR146a-5p-mEVs was negative, recorded at −8.79 mV for mEVs and −20.86 mV for miR146a-5p-mEVs (Figure 3D). This observed difference may be attributed to the incorporation of negatively charged miRNAs. The results of western blot analyses confirmed the presence of the exosomal marker proteins Alix, TSG101, CD63, CD9, and CD81 in both types of mEVs (Figure 3E). These findings suggest that the mEVs loaded with miR-146a-5p retain their structural integrity and essential characteristics as EVs.

To evaluate the loading efficiency, we synthesized fluorescent CY3-labeled miR-146a-5p and used a standard curve correlating the fluorescence intensity with miR-146a-5p concentration to calculate the encapsulation efficiency (Figure S4). The results revealed that the loading efficiency was approximately 38.37%, whereas the efficiency of co-incubation was only 2.01% (Figure 4A). To further confirm the encapsulation of miR-146a-5p by mEVs, we performed agarose gel electrophoresis. As shown in Figure 4B, unencapsulated CY3-labeled miR-146a-5p migrated within an electric field, forming fluorescence bands at the expected positions. Conversely, CY3-miR-146a-5p encapsulated by mEVs exhibited significantly diminished migration ability and localized accumulation at the starting point of the lane. Additionally, co-incubation of mEVs and miR-146a-5p did not affect its normal migration ability. Nanoflow cytometry analysis revealed that approximately 92.5% of the mEVs were loaded with CY5-miR-146a-5p (Figure 4C), suggesting a widespread distribution of miR-146a-5p across most mEVs, rather than their concentration in a limited subset. These findings collectively demonstrated successful loading of miR-146a-5p into mEVs.

Subsequently, we assessed the stability of miR-146a-5p over time. Within 48 h, unencapsulated miR-146a-5p was degraded to 50%, whereas 80% of sub-capsulated miRNAs remained intact after 7 days (Figure 4D). This result indicates that the loading into mEVs has a significant anti-degradation effect on miR-146a-5p.

Unencapsulated miRNAs present challenges for penetrating cell membranes due to their inherent negative charge and hydrophilicity. Consequently, the effective delivery of miRNAs into cells necessitates the use of specialized delivery carriers. To evaluate whether mEVs can successfully transport miR-146a-5p into target cells, we employed CY3-labeled miR-146a-5p and used flow cytometry analysis to assess the delivery efficiency of miR146a-5p-mEVs. As a control, we utilized Lipofectamine 3000, a widely used transfection reagent. The results indicated that mEVs loaded with miR-146a-5p achieved efficient cellular delivery, yielding an uptake efficiency of approximately 90%. In stark contrast, free miR-146a-5p alone or in simple combination with mEVs exhibited an uptake efficiency of approximately 10%. Furthermore, the performance of Lipofectamine 3000 was notably inferior to that of mEVs, demonstrating an uptake efficiency of approximately 30% (Figures 4E and 4F). Additionally, traditional transfection reagents are often associated with significant cytotoxicity, which substantially reduces cell viability. In comparison, mEVs did not exhibit any discernible adverse effects on cell viability and showed commendable biocompatibility (Figure 4G).

miR146a-5p-mEVs inhibited human ILC2 function from patients with allergic rhinitis in vitro

To ascertain whether mEVs can be internalized by ILC2s, we added DiD-labeled mEVs into the culture medium of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in vitro. The result of flow cytometry analyses demonstrated that human ILC2s significantly internalized mEVs after a 24-h co-culture period (Figures 5A and 5B). Overall, these results indicated that ILC2s were able to take up mEVs.

To evaluate the effects of miR146a-5p-mEVs on ILC2 function, human PBMCs from patients with allergic rhinitis were treated with IL-2/25/33 in the presence or absence of miR146a-5p-mEVs. We applied flow cytometry analysis to investigate the effect of miR146a-5p-mEVs on the frequency of activated ILC2s. The high frequency of IL-13+ ILC2s in response to IL-25 and IL-33 stimulation was significantly reversed after the application of miR146a-5p-mEVs. Although NC-miRNA-mEVs showed a similar trend in reducing the frequency of IL-13+ ILC2s, there was no statistically significant difference. Furthermore, unencapsulated miR146a-5p did not produce any notable changes in the population of IL-13+ ILC2s (Figure 5C). The results of ELISA indicated that stimulation with IL-2/25/IL-33 significantly elevated the levels of IL-5, IL-9, and IL-13. Notably, high concentrations of these type 2 inflammatory cytokines were markedly attenuated by the application of miR146a-5p-mEVs. The NC-miRNA-mEVs, which were loaded with nonsense miRNA sequences, showed a trend of reducing type 2 inflammatory cytokine levels, but there was no statistically significant difference compared with the inflammation stimulation group. Additionally, treatment with unencapsulated miR146a-5p exhibited no observable effect (Figure 5D). The observed trends in both IL-13+ ILC2 frequencies and levels of type 2 cytokines were consistent throughout our analyses. Collectively, these findings suggest that miR146a-5p-EVs have an inhibitory effect on the activation of ILC2s induced by IL-2/25/33 in vitro.

miR146a-5p-mEVs significantly reduced ILC2-dominant AAI in mice

To further explore the immunoregulatory effect of miR146a-5p-mEVs on ILC2-dominant AAI, we established an ILC2-dominant asthma mouse model via intratracheal administration of IL-33 (Figure S5A), which represents acute inflammation characterized by a significant increase in the frequency of ILC2s in lung tissue (Figures S5B and S5C). We then intravenously administered miR146a-5p-mEVs to assess their immunomodulatory function on ILC2-dominant AAI. Here, in IL-33-induced AAI, a pronounced peribronchial infiltration of inflammatory cells was observed, which was significantly attenuated by treatment with miR146a-5p-mEVs (Figures 6B and 6D). A parallel reduction was noted in goblet cell hyperplasia and mucus secretion within the airway epithelium (Figures 6C and 6E). These ameliorative effects were specifically attributable to the mEV-mediated delivery of miR-146a-5p, as control groups treated with either NC-miRNA-mEVs or the unencapsulated miR-146a-5p showed no significant improvement in inflammatory cell infiltration or goblet cell hyperplasia compared with the disease model.

miR146a-5p-mEVs reduced inflammatory cells and the levels of ILC2s and Th2-related cytokines

Upon analysis of inflammatory cells in BALF, a marked increase in eosinophil infiltration was observed in the ILC2-driven airway inflammation model. Treatment with miR146a-5p-mEVs significantly lowered the counts of eosinophils (Figures 7A and 7B). This therapeutic effect was absent in the NC-miRNA-mEVs and unencapsulated miR-146a-5p groups, where the levels of eosinophils remained significantly elevated, confirming that the observed suppression of inflammation is contingent upon the targeted delivery of functional miR-146a-5p by mEVs. Similarly, neutrophils were also increased in this ILC2-driven model. Treatment with miR146a-5p-mEVs effectively reversed this effect (Figures 7A and 7C), but the suppressive effect on neutrophils was less pronounced than that on eosinophils. The differential efficacy may be attributed to the fact that the ILC2-driven airway inflammation is primarily characterized by Th2-type cytokine-mediated eosinophilic inflammation. IL-33 stimulation significantly elevated the frequency of ILC2s, whereas treatment with miR146a-5p-mEVs effectively reversed this effect (Figures 7D and 7E). In contrast, mEVs loaded with nonsensical sequences or unencapsulated miR-146a-5p exhibited no significant inhibitory effect on ILC2s. Additionally, we observed notable reductions in the levels of type 2 cytokines, including IL-5 and IL-13, in the BALF after treatment with miR146a-5p-mEVs (Figures 7F and 7G). It is noteworthy that the levels of IL-5 and IL-13 showed further increase in the presence of free miR-146a-5p, which may be attributed to immune system activation triggered by the unencapsulated miRNAs (Figures 7F and 7G). In total, our findings demonstrated the therapeutic effects of miR146a-5p-mEVs on ILC2-dominant asthma-related inflammation in mice.

miR-146a-5p delivered by mEVs directly targets the 3ʹ UTRs of TRAF6 and IRAK1 and suppresses their expression

To determine whether miR-146a-5p delivered by mEVs is functionally active and capable of loading into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) to regulate target genes, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays. HEK293T cells were transfected with firefly luciferase reporter plasmids containing the wild-type or mutant 3ʹ untranslated regions (3ʹ UTRs) of IRAK1 or TRAF6, together with a Renilla luciferase normalization control, followed by the treatment with PBS, miR-146a-5p-mEVs, NC-miRNA-mEVs, or liposome-mediated miR-146a-5p mimic transfection for 24 h.

As shown in Figures 8A and 8C, miR-146a-5p-mEVs significantly reduced the luciferase activity by approximately 70%–80% for both IRAK1-3ʹ UTR and TRAF6-3ʹ UTR reporters compared with untreated controls. NC-miRNA-mEVs showed a modest reduction of approximately 20%–30%, likely attributable to intrinsic immunomodulatory effects of endogenous miRNAs carried by mEVs themselves. Importantly, mutation of the miR-146a-5p seed-binding sequences within the 3ʹ UTRs completely abrogated the luciferase suppression in all treatment groups (Figures 8B and 8D), confirming sequence-specific targeting. Liposome-mediated miR-146a-5p mimic transfection achieved comparable suppression efficiency to miR-146a-5p-mEVs, demonstrating that mEV-mediated delivery is equally effective as the commercial transfection reagent.

To further validate that miR-146a-5p delivered by mEVs functionally regulates its endogenous target genes, we examined IRAK1 and TRAF6 expression at both mRNA and protein levels in human non-CD4+ PBMCs. As shown in Figures 8E and 8F, the western blot analysis demonstrated that the treatment with miR-146a-5p-mEVs for 48 h significantly reduced IRAK1 and TRAF6 levels compared with control groups, whereas no significant changes were observed at the 24-h time point (Figure S7C). In contrast, as shown in Figures S7A and S7B, quantitative real-time PCR (real-time qPCR) analysis revealed that miR-146a-5p-mEV treatment led to a modest increase in IRAK1 and TRAF6 mRNA levels relative to controls, which may be due to an intrinsic negative feedback regulatory mechanism in the cells.

Collectively, these data provide direct evidence that mEV-delivered miR-146a-5p is functionally active. It specifically binds to the 3ʹ UTRs of its canonical target genes IRAK1 and TRAF6, leading to post-transcriptional suppression of their protein expression. These findings confirmed that mEVs effectively deliver functional miR-146a-5p that operates through the authentic miRNA pathway.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully established a method for purifying mEVs from bovine milk and demonstrated their potential as an efficient, biocompatible, and scalable delivery system for miR-146a-5p in the treatment of ILC2-driven AAI. Our findings not only validate the therapeutic efficacy of miR-146a-5p in suppressing ILC2 function but also address the critical challenges associated with miRNA-based therapies, including instability in biological environments, poor cellular uptake, and the lack of safe and scalable delivery platforms.

We confirmed that mEVs isolated from bovine milk exhibit typical exosomal characteristics—including size, morphology, zeta potential, and the presence of specific biomarkers44—ensuring their structural and biological characteristics as natural nanocarriers.

mEVs demonstrated excellent biocompatibility both in vitro and in vivo, with no evidence of cytotoxicity or organ damage, even after repeated systemic administration. This safety profile positions mEVs as a superior alternative to conventional lipid nanoparticles, which often possess immunogenicity and may induce severe inflammation.32 Although Cr and LDH levels displayed a downward trend after mEV administration, clinically, elevations of these markers carry far greater significance than decreases, as increases typically reflect tissue damage or organ dysfunction. LDH is a non-specific marker of cellular damage whose primary clinical relevance lies in elevated levels indicating tissue injury.45 Minor decreases in LDH, in the absence of other clinical signs, are not typically associated with pathological conditions and are of limited clinical significance. Similarly, isolated mild reductions in serum Cr are generally not considered clinically meaningful, as Cr levels can decline due to physiological factors such as reduced muscle mass.46 Notably, no significant weight loss was observed in the mEV-treated groups compared with controls, and mice maintained normal vitality throughout the experimental period.

Beyond their role as biosafe carriers, mEVs possess intrinsic immunomodulatory properties. Several studies have reported that mEVs themselves can exert anti-inflammatory effects in various disease models and promote cognitive development by crossing the blood-brain barrier.47,48 A key consideration when using mEVs as delivery vehicles is the potential biological impact of their endogenous cargo. mEVs naturally contain immunomodulatory miRNAs (e.g., miR-148a and miR-21) and proteins such as TGF-β, which could theoretically influence inflammatory responses.48,49,50 In our ILC2-dominant AAI model, this raised the question of whether the therapeutic effects of miR-146a-5p-loaded mEVs might be confounded by these intrinsic components. To address this, we included a negative control group treated with the mEVs loaded with negative control miRNA (“NC-miRNA-mEVs”). Notably, while this group exhibited a mild trend toward reduced inflammation, the effect did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, miR-146a-5p-loaded mEVs produced robust and significant therapeutic benefits. This observation suggests that although endogenous mEVs cargo may exert low-level background activity, it is insufficient to account for the potent effects observed with miR-146a-5p loading. These findings underscore that the primary therapeutic efficacy in our model is attributable to the exogenously loaded miR-146a-5p, rather than the intrinsic components of the mEVs themselves.

Furthermore, our data have shown that compared with EVs derived from MSCs, which are widely investigated, mEVs offer outstanding advantages in terms of source availability, scalability, and cost effectiveness (Figure S8), which make them more suitable for large-scale clinical applications.

Recently, Meng et al. reported the delivery of miR-146a via milk-derived exosomes in a model of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, demonstrating anti-inflammatory effects through inhibition of the IRAK1/TRAF6/NF-κB pathway.40 While both studies have utilized mEVs for miR-146a-5p delivery, our work targets ILC2-dominant AAI. We provide direct evidence that miR-146a-5p delivered by mEVs suppresses ILC2 activation and type 2 cytokine production. Thus, while both studies support the utility of mEVs for miR-146a-5p delivery, our work extends this concept to a new disease context and provides novel mechanistic insights into ILC2 regulation.

In our study, we identified that miR-146a-5p-loaded mEVs effectively suppressed ILC2 activation and reduced the production of type 2 cytokines in human PBMCs from AR patients. These in vitro findings were further confirmed in an ILC2-dominant murine model of AAI, where systemic administration of miR-146a-5p-mEVs significantly attenuated inflammatory cell infiltration, goblet cell hyperplasia, and eosinophil accumulation in the airways. Importantly, these therapeutic effects were specific to mEVs delivering functional miR-146a-5p, as control treatments—including empty mEVs, nonsense miRNA-loaded mEVs, or unencapsulated miR-146a-5p—failed to elicit significant improvements. Notably, the inability of unencapsulated miR-146a-5p to exert therapeutic effects—and in some cases, its potential to exacerbate inflammatory responses—highlights the necessity of a protective and delivery-competent carrier.

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the anti-inflammatory effects of miR-146a-5p-loaded mEVs, we examined its canonical target genes, IRAK1 and TRAF6, in non-CD4+ PBMCs. Treatment with miR-146a-5p-mEVs for 48 h significantly reduced IRAK1 and TRAF6 levels relative to controls, whereas no change was observed at 24 h, suggesting that sustained exposure is required for effective target suppression.

Notably, qPCR analysis revealed a modest increase in IRAK1 and TRAF6 mRNA levels following treatment. While this may seem counterintuitive, prolonged protein suppression may trigger compensatory transcriptional upregulation. Furthermore, miRNAs primarily act at the post-transcriptional level, mostly promoting translational inhibition or mRNA degradation, and effective silencing can occur without substantial mRNA decay. Therefore, the reduction in protein expression, rather than the mRNA levels, provides the most direct evidence that miR-146a-5p delivered by mEVs is functionally active. Importantly, dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that mEV-delivered miR-146a-5p directly binds to the 3ʹ UTRs of IRAK1 and TRAF6, providing definitive evidence that the observed anti-inflammatory effects are mediated through the authentic miRNA pathway (RISC) loading and post-transcriptional repression. Collectively, these data demonstrate that miR-146a-5p-loaded mEVs exert their anti-inflammatory effects, at least in part, through post-transcriptional suppression of IRAK1 and TRAF6.

Our results further reinforce the importance of ILC2s as a therapeutic target in AAI. While Th2 cells have long been considered the primary orchestrators of allergic reactions, accumulating evidence indicates that ILC2s play a more direct and dominant role in initiating and maintaining inflammatory responses, particularly through their rapid response to epithelial-derived cytokines such as IL-33 and IL-25.15 The effective suppression of ILC2 activation and subsequent inflammatory cascades by miR-146a-5p-mEVs in our study mechanistically support the therapeutic value of targeting the ILC2 pathway.

In conclusion, our study provides evidence that mEVs serve as an ideal delivery vehicle for miR-146a-5p, enabling efficient and targeted modulation of ILC2-driven immune responses in allergic airway disease. By addressing key limitations of existing miRNA delivery systems, this platform not only enhances the stability and bioavailability of miR-146a-5p but also offers a clinically feasible strategy for the treatment of type 2-inflammatory conditions.

Conclusion

In summary, mEVs were employed as a delivery vehicle for miR-146a-5p. The resulting mEVs exhibited typical exosome characteristics and demonstrated favorable biosafety profiles. Administration of miR-146a-5p-loaded mEVs significantly suppressed the function of ILC2s under allergic inflammatory conditions, reduced the expression of type 2 cytokines, and attenuated both eosinophil infiltration and goblet cell hyperplasia, thereby alleviating AAI. Overall, this study presents a safe and cost-effective strategy using mEVs to deliver miR-146a-5p for the treatment of AAI, offering novel insights into potential therapeutic approaches for this condition.

Materials and methods

Isolation of mEVs from bovine milk

Fresh bovine milk was purchased from the Guangzhou Dairy Cattle Research Institute, and casein and fat were removed as previously reported.41 Firstly, bovine milk was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C to obtain skimmed milk. Hydrochloride (HCl) was then added to the skimmed milk to adjust the pH to 4.6, at which caseins precipitate and can be easily removed. Clear whey solution containing EVs was obtained after the removal of precipitated casein through centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 20 min and serial filtration using membranes with pore sizes of 0.8, 0.45, and 0.22 μm. Subsequently, EVs were isolated using tangential flow filtration.

Zeta potential measurement

To assess the surface charge and colloidal stability of the isolated mEVs, zeta potential was measured. Zeta potential, also termed electrokinetic potential, is the potential at the slipping plane of a particle moving under an electric field, reflecting the net charge at the particle’s surface.51 Measurements were performed using an Anton Paar/LITESIZER 500 (Graz, Austria) at 25°C. Samples were diluted in PBS to achieve an appropriate count rate, and each measurement was repeated in triplicate.

Biosafety of mEVs in vitro

For the cytotoxicity assay, the impact of mEVs on cell viability was evaluated using a CCK-8 assay. The BEAS-2B airway epithelial cell line (ATCC) and intestinal epithelial cells NCM460 were chosen for this study. The cells were seeded into 96-well culture plates at a density of 10,000 cells/mL and incubated overnight to allow for complete adherence. Subsequently, the cells were treated with various concentrations of mEVs for 48 and 72 h. Following this, the cells were washed with PBS and further incubated with 100 μL of fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent for an additional 1 h. The absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, Vermont, USA) at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Biocompatibility of mEVs in vivo

C57BL/6 mice (purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.) were used to further evaluate the potential systemic toxicity of mEVs. The mice were administrated various doses of mEVs. To assess the systemic effect of mEVs, we excised the major organs for histological analysis. Organic sections of the organs were stained with hematoxylin and eosin to evaluate morphological changes. Additionally, plasma samples were collected to assess biochemical indicators. The animals were maintained in a specific pathogen-free environment in the Animal Experimental Center of North Campus, Sun Yat-sen University. All procedures involving animals performed in the study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University.

Preparation of mEVs loaded with miR-146a-5p

The loading of miR-146a-5p into mEVs was accomplished through extrusion method (Figure 3A). The concentration of mEVs was adjusted to 1 × 1012 particles/mL. miR-146a-5p mimics (purchased from Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) were mixed with mEVs at a final concentration of 400 nM. The mixture was then transferred to the syringe of liposome extruder (Avanti). Following repeated extrusion for 10 cycles, the resulting mixture including both encapsulated and unencapsulated miR-146a-5p was subjected to a 50 kDa ultrafiltration filter (Millipore, Sigma, America), where unencapsulated miR-146a-5p was removed via centrifugation.

Analysis of loading efficiency

To quantify the amount of miR-146a-5p loaded into mEVs, miR-146a-5p was labeled with CY5 or CY3. We generated a linear correlation standard curve relating the fluorescence intensity to the concentration of miR-146a-5p. The encapsulation efficiency was calculated using the following formula:

Loading efficiency (%) = (The amount of loaded miRNA)/(The initial amount of miRNA) × 100%

Flow NanoAnalyzer (NanoFCM) was used to further quantify the proportion of mEVs loaded with FAM fluorophore-labeled miR-146a-5p.

Characterization of mEVs and miR146a-5p-mEVs

The size and concentration of mEVs and miR146a-5p-mEVs were analyzed by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NanoSight NS300; Malvern, UK), and the zeta potential was measured using dynamic light scattering (Litesizer 500; Anton Paar, Austria). The morphology of both mEVs and miR146a-5p-mEVs was assessed following staining with 2% phosphotungstic acid, utilizing a transmission electron microscope (H-7650, HITACHI, Japan) at an operating voltage of 100 kV.

Western blot analysis

Biomarkers of both mEVs and miR146a-5p-mEVs and the negative marker of mEVs were detected with western blotting, as previously reported. Briefly, the protein concentrations of both mEVs and miR146a-5p-mEVs were quantified via the BCA protein assay (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, 25 μg of protein was mixed with SDS loading buffer and denatured at 95°C for 10 min. The samples were then separated via 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred from the gel to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk at room temperature for 1 h, followed by incubation with specific primary antibodies at 4°C for 14 h. After being washed for 3 times with TBST buffer (Tris 1 M; NaCl, 5 M; and Tween 20, 0.05%; pH 8.0), the membranes were further incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies on a shaker for 1 h at room temperature. Following another set of three washes with TBST buffer, the membranes were incubated with Enhanced Chemiluminescence Plus (Millipore Corporation, USA) for 5 s. Finally, images were captured using an imaging analysis system (BIO-RAD, ChemiDoc Touch, Hercules, CA, USA).

In vitro delivery efficiency of miR146a-5p-mEVs

BEAS-2B cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL into 24-well culture plates and incubated overnight to facilitate complete adherence. Subsequently, the cells were treated with miR146a-5p-mEVs, unencapsulated miR-146a-5p, and miR-146a-5p mixed with Lipo3000 (each group contained 40 pmol CY3-miR-146a-5p). After a 6-h incubation period (protected from light), the cells were collected, and the proportions of CY3-miR-146a-5p+ cells were determined using flow cytometry.

Isolation and treatment of PBMCs, Lineage− cells, and ILC2s

For the evaluation of ILC2 function in vitro, human PBMCs from patients with allergic rhinitis were isolated using Ficoll-Paque PREMIUM density gradient centrifugation following the manufacturer’s instructions (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, England, UK). All procedures involving humans were performed in accordance with the Institutional Ethical Committee for Clinical Research and Animal Trials of The First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. Lineage− cells in the PBMCs were purified using the Lineage Cell Depletion Kit (Miltenyi Biotec GmbH, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). To assess the immunoregulatory effects of miR146a-5p-mEVs on ILC2s, PBMCs and Lineage− cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 106 cells per well in 1 mL RPMI 1640, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were treated with PBS as a negative control or stimulated with recombinant human (rh) IL-2 (50 ng/mL), rhIL-25 (10 ng/mL), and rhIL-33 (10 ng/mL), serving as positive controls. Subsequently, these stimulated cells were further treated with PBS, miR146a-5p-mEVs (containing 50 nM miR-146a-5p), scramble miRNA-mEVs (containing 50 nM miRNA), or unencapsulated miR-146a-5p for a duration of 3 days. Supernatants were then collected for the subsequent analysis of IL-5, IL-9, and IL-13 levels. Additionally, the cells were collected for flow cytometry analysis of IL-13+ ILC2s.

Mouse model of ILC2-dominant eosinophilic airway inflammation and mEV administration

Female C57BL/6 mice aged 4–6 weeks were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. The animals were maintained in a specific pathogen-free environment in the Animal Experimental Center of North Campus, Sun Yat-sen University. All procedures involving animals performed in the study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University.

The ILC2-dominant eosinophilic airway inflammation mouse model was established, as previously described.26 The mice were treated intratracheally with PBS as a negative control or intratracheally stimulated with 500 ng murine IL-33 (BioLegend) diluted in 20 μL PBS per mouse on days 0, 1, and 3, which served as the positive control. To investigate the therapeutic effects of miR146a-5p-EVs, the stimulated mice were administered intravenously with PBS, miR146a-5p-mEVs (containing 5 nmol of miR-146a-5p), scramble miRNA-mEVs (containing 5 nmol of scramble miRNA) or unencapsulated miR-146a-5p two hours prior to stimulation. All the mice were sacrificed on day 5.

BALF was collected by performing lung lavage with 1 mL of pre-cooled PBS 3 times and then centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min. Following centrifugation, supernatants were obtained for the detection of Th2-related cytokines using ELISA kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Diego, CA, USA), while pellets were utilized for flow cytometry analyses of eosinophil cells. The left lobe of each lung sample was collected for histopathologic evaluation, and the remaining lung tissues were minced and digested to obtain single-cell suspension for the following flow cytometry analyses of ILC2s.

The evaluation of ILC2 levels from patients with AR

For flow cytometry analysis of ILC2s in human PBMCs, the cells were stained with Lineage markers (CD2, CD3, CD14, CD16, CD19, CD56, CD235a, and FceR1)-FITC, CRTH2-PE, and CD127-PE-Cy7. The gating strategies used for flow cytometry analyses of ILC2s in PBMCs are shown as Figure S9. In analyses of IL-13+ ILC2s, PBMCs were first stimulated with phorbol myristate acetate (50 ng/mL, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), ionomycin (1,000 ng/mL, St. Louis, Sigma, MO, USA), and monensin (1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 5 h and then stained with anti-IL-13-PB450 in addition to the above antibodies for gating ILC2s.

The evaluation of inflammatory cells in mice

For analyses of eosinophils and neutrophils in mouse BALF, the pellets were stained with antibodies against CD45-FITC, CD11b-APC-Cy7, CD64-PE, Ly-6G-Alexa 700, and Siglec-F-Alexa 647 and analyzed based on gating strategies, as previously reported.52

For analyses of ILC2s in mouse lung tissues, single-cell suspensions of lung tissue were stained with antibodies to CD45-APC-Cy7, Lineage+ markers (CD3, CD45R, CD11b, TER-119, and Ly-G6)-FITC, CD90.2-PE, ST2-PerCP-eFluor 710, and CD127-PE-Cy7 according to the gating strategies, as previously reported.53,54

After staining, the samples were analyzed on a CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Hercules, CA, USA).

ELISA for the levels of Th2-related cytokines

The levels of Th2-related cytokines in human cell-culture supernatants and mouse BALF samples were quantified by ELISA kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Diego, CA, USA). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Real-time qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from non-CD4+ PBMCs, using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using the Evo M-MLV RT premix (AG, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Real-time qPCR was carried out using SYBR Green Pro Taq HS qPCR Master Mix (AG, China) on an Applied BiosystemsTM QuantStudioTM (Invitrogen, USA). The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to β-actin as an internal control. The primer sequences used were as follows: IRAK1 forward: 5′-ACGCTGACCTGGAGTGGACTG-3′, reverse: 5′-GAAGCCGTTCTGAGCACAGTAGC-3′; TRAF6 forward: 5′-CCCAATGCCAGCGTCCCTTC-3′, reverse: 5′-GCAGCACAGTTGTCACAAGAAACC-3′; β-actin forward: 5′-AGAGCTACGAGCTGCCTGAC-3′, reverse: 5′-AGCACTGTGTTGGCGTACAG-3′.

Western blot analysis of IRAK1 and TRAF6

Non-CD4+ PBMCs were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (20 μg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against IRAK1 (CST, 1:1000), TRAF6 (CST, 1:1000), and GAPDH (CST, 1:2000). After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (CST, 1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Biosharp, China) and quantified using ImageJ software.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The wild-type and mutant 3ʹ UTRs of human IRAK1 and TRAF6 containing the putative miR-146a-5p-binding sites were synthesized and cloned into the pmirGLO Dual-Luciferase miRNA Target Expression Vector (Genepharma, China). HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells per well, 24 h prior to transfection. The cells were co-transfected with 800 ng of the wild-type or mutant reporter plasmid using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). Six hours after transfection, the medium was replaced, and the cells were treated with miR146a-5p-mEVs (2 × 1012 particles/mL), NC-miRNA-mEVs (2 × 1012 particles/mL), or liposome-mediated miR-146a-5p mimic (50 nM) for 24 h (RNAiPro Transfection Reagent, Mikx, China). Untreated cells served as the negative control. Cells were then harvested, and firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured consecutively using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Genepharma, China) on a Thermo Fisher/Varioskan LUX. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity for each well.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software and SPSS. All values are expressed as the mean ± SD or mean ± SEM for each group. Statistical significance was assessed by using two-tailed Student’s t test for single comparisons or one-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set as follows: ∗, p < 0.05; ∗∗, p < 0.01; ∗∗∗, p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗, p < 0.0001; ns, not significant.
