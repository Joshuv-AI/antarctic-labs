Standardised, telemonitored titration of guideline-directed medical therapy in heart failure is associated with faster optimisation and improved persistence compared to standard of care
Authors: Hevar Hamah Saed, Daniel Winstedt Thomas, Antros Louca, Araz Rawshani, Karin Odefjord, Rami Genead, Charlotta Ljungman, Helen Sjöland, Niklas Bergh, Tomas Mellberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRapid initiation and up-titration of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) are critical for improving outcomes in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), yet initiation is frequently delayed and target doses are often not achieved in routine clinical care. We assessed whether a predefined standardised home-based titration strategy supported by telemonitoring enhances GDMT optimisation compared with standard of care.MethodsThis study compared the TELEmonitored FAst Standardized Titration for Efficient Response in Heart Failure with reduced ejection fraction (TELEFASTER-HF) cohort (n=60) that underwent standardised, home-based GDMT titration with a contemporaneous standard-of-care cohort (n=65). Groups were balanced using inverse probability of treatment weighting. Outcomes included time to optimal medical therapy (OMT), comparative attainment of GDMT doses at 8 weeks and GDMT persistence up to 12 months after the study index.ResultsThe time to OMT was substantially shorter in TELEFASTER-HF patients compared with the standard-of-care cohort (median 48 vs 321 days, HR 16.1, 95% CI 8.3 to 31.2, p<0.001). Compared with standard-of care, TELEFASTER-HF care was associated with higher GDMT dose categories and achievement of higher rates of GDMT target doses across all drug classes needing titration at all follow-up time points. Exploratory analyses showed no heart failure hospitalisations in the TELEFASTER-HF group during 12 months from index compared with 26 events in the control cohort.ConclusionsA standardised, telemonitored, home-based GDMT titration strategy was associated with faster, more comprehensive and more durable optimisation of GDMT in patients with HFrEF compared with standard of care. This approach may help address persistent GDMT implementation gaps.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Protocol-driven, remote and visit-independent GDMT titration models remain insufficiently studied in routine clinical practice and have rarely been compared with standard care. Introduction

Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) imposes a substantial burden on healthcare systems and remains a leading cause of hospitalisation. Recurrent admissions are strongly associated with adverse prognosis, particularly during the vulnerable phase immediately following a heart failure (HF) hospitalisation or episode of clinical deterioration.13 Early initiation of quadruple guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), followed by rapid up-titration to target doses, is essential for improving outcomes in HFrEF.4 5 Despite progress, timely and systematic implementation of GDMT remains challenging in routine HF care worldwide.610 Multiple factors have been proposed, but clinical inertia among healthcare professionals has been consistently identified as a key contributor to delayed GDMT initiation and titration.11 12 Several strategies have been explored to address this implementation gap. Digital tools may support clinical decision-making and improve patient education regarding GDMT and its expected benefits.13 14 Such tools can also facilitate remote monitoring of vital signs, enable more efficient resource allocation and promote a more inclusive, patient-centred model of care.15 16 Digitally and remotely supported titration strategies have been successfully applied in home-based settings.13 17 18 However, several studies indicate that, irrespective of monitoring approach or care setting, more streamlined and standardised initiation and titration of GDMT are associated with improved dosing trajectories.4 19 20 Many of these approaches rely on frequent in-person or digital visits and involve multiple clinical decision points during titration, limiting scalability and real-world implementation. In the TELEmonitored FAst Standardized Titration for Efficient Response in Heart Failure with reduced ejection fraction (TELEFASTER-HF) study, we demonstrated that GDMT titration can be fully standardised and performed by patients themselves at home with rapid attainment of high GDMT doses. Safety was ensured through home-based monitoring (HBM) linked directly to the HF clinic and frequent laboratory testing.21

In the present study, we evaluated this standardised, remote titration strategy against contemporary standard of care during the same time period as the TELEFASTER-HF trial. We hypothesised that protocol-driven titration without scheduled clinical encounters would be more effective in achieving optimal medical therapy (OMT) and higher GDMT dose attainment compared with usual care.

Methods

Study design and cohorts

This study compared the prospective TELEFASTER-HF cohort with a contemporaneous control cohort. The TELEFASTER-HF cohort consisted of 60 patients enrolled between December 2022 and February 2024 from three cardiology and internal medicine clinics across the Region Västra Götaland of Sweden. Eligible participants had received an HFrEF diagnosis within the preceding 3 months, including patients hospitalised for decompensated HFrEF and stable patients referred to the outpatient HF clinic.21 Participants in the TELEFASTER-HF study titrated GDMT at home using standardised protocols, without scheduled in-person visits, supported by telemonitoring of vital signs (daily measurements of blood pressure, pulse rate and weight) and regular laboratory testing for safety. Participants completed weekly symptom questionnaires and had direct access to HF nurses through an in-app chat function. Blood samples were obtained at baseline and every second week during the active titration phase. Medication titration followed predefined treatment algorithms based on baseline therapy, with early initiation of sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and stepwise up-titration of beta-blockers (BB), renin-angiotensin system inhibitors/angiotensin-receptor-neprilisyn inhibitor (RASi/ARNI) and mineral receptor antagonist (MRA) therapy until target doses, OMT or clinically relevant treatment-limiting factors were reached. A protocol-specified telephone consultation with a cardiologist was conducted at 8 weeks, followed by an in-person close-out visit at 6 months. Apart from these scheduled contacts, no routine clinic visits or telephone consultations were planned during the titration phase. However, participants could be contacted by the HF team or referred for additional clinical assessment whenever deemed necessary based on symptoms, telemonitoring data, laboratory results or other safety considerations. Detailed descriptions of the intervention and titration algorithms have been published previously.21 To compare with real-world data, we established a control cohort with HFrEF. This cohort was created through a retrospective chart review of patients with HFrEF who were treated at three cardiology clinics at the Sahlgrenska University (SU) hospital (SU hospital/Sahlgrenska, SU hospital/Östra and SU hospital/Mölndal) during 2023, the period when the majority of patients in the TELEFASTER-HF study were included. Of the three hospitals, two included patients in the TELEFASTER-HF study. In the control cohort, only two patients had been screened for participation in TELEFASTER-HF but were not enrolled, one due to unwillingness to participate and the other due to technical issues with the Smartphone. The initial control cohort was generated through an electronic extraction of all hospitalisations and outpatient visits carrying an HFrEF diagnosis code (ICD-10 I50.1a) across the participating hospitals during the study period. This process yielded 1093 hospitalisations and outpatient visits corresponding to 396 unique individuals who were subsequently reviewed against the predefined eligibility criteria. No additional selection based on clinic, physician, treatment response, follow-up intensity or data availability was performed. The purpose of the control cohort was not to represent an unselected HFrEF population, but to create a comparator cohort reflecting the clinical eligibility criteria of TELEFASTER-HF. Exclusion criteria included an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <45 mL/min, current treatment with BB or RASi at >50% of the target dose, or contraindications to any of the four GDMT HF drug classes (BB, RASi/ARNI, MRA and SGLT2i). A total of 70 patients were identified with newly diagnosed HFrEF (within 3 months after diagnosis, the same inclusion criterion as in TELEFASTER-HF), or hospitalisation for decompensated HFrEF. Three patients were excluded from analysis due to death prior to the pseudo index date (see below for definition of pseudo index date), and one due to confirmed AL-amyloidosis with severe autonomous dysfunction, which was detected in the final cohort control before analysis. One patient was excluded due to follow-up in primary care (figure 1). Cognitive impairment was assessed through structured medical record review in the control cohort and was not identified in eligible patients. TELEFASTER-HF participants were required to be capable of independent HBM as part of study enrolment. All patients in this study (TELEFASTER-HF and control cohort) were treated and followed within cardiology specialist care. Standard care was delivered in accordance with the regional HF management programme at the participating hospitals. Patients with newly diagnosed HFrEF were referred to dedicated multidisciplinary HF clinics staffed by cardiologists, specialised HF nurses and physiotherapists. Patients hospitalised for acute HF were routinely scheduled for outpatient follow-up within 2–3 weeks after discharge. For patients in the outpatient HF clinic, key quality indicators include HF clinic follow-up within 30 days, initiation of first-line HF therapy within 30 days and physiotherapist assessment within 90 days. All eligible patients were followed according to local clinical practice, with repeated visits or telephone contacts aimed at optimising and titrating GDMT. Although the care pathway targets rapid initiation and optimisation of therapy, the frequency and timing of follow-up contacts were determined by routine clinical practice and available healthcare resources. The same clinical eligibility criteria were prespecified for both cohorts; however, eligibility assessment necessarily reflected the prospective enrolment framework in TELEFASTER-HF and the retrospective identification process in the control cohort. In the control cohort, dose eligibility was assessed at the time of heart-failure diagnosis, whereas in TELEFASTER-HF it was assessed at study inclusion. This difference in assessment timing should be considered when interpreting baseline dose distributions at index/pseudo-index. Because TELEFASTER-HF patients were included a median of 21.5 days after HF diagnosis, comparative analyses were anchored to this timepoint. This adjusted time point is referred to as a pseudo-index, representing a landmark timepoint corresponding to the median interval from diagnosis to study inclusion in the TELEFASTER-HF cohort. All longitudinal analyses were performed from this aligned index to estimate differences in subsequent GDMT optimisation between groups. As a consequence of this alignment strategy, some patients in the control cohort had achieved higher GDMT doses at pseudo-index than TELEFASTER-HF patients at index. The rationale for introducing a pseudo-index was to reduce time-related bias and avoid contamination of titration conditions between groups, as logistical and organisational factors in usual care (eg, diagnostic workflows, inter-clinic referral delays or administrative processes) may systematically postpone treatment initiation compared with the TELEFASTER-HF strategy, in which patients were proactively identified immediately after echocardiographic confirmation. The analytical approach, therefore, estimates differences in GDMT optimisation after alignment of follow-up timepoints, rather than differences in treatment initiation immediately at diagnosis.

Selection of the control cohort. A total of 1093 hospitalisations or outpatient visits with an I50.1a diagnosis were identified at three different hospitals, comprising 396 unique patients. Patients with heart failure (HF) duration >3 months, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <45 mL/min/1.73 m² or treatment with beta-blockers (BB) or renin–angiotensin system inhibitors (RASi) at >50% of the target dose at HF diagnosis were excluded. The final control cohort consisted of 65 patients.

After completion of the 180-day TELEFASTER-HF study, no further protocol-driven follow-up was undertaken. In the present analysis, longitudinal data for both TELEFASTER-HF patients and controls were obtained through electronic health record (EHR) review at index/pseudo-index, 8 weeks, 6 months and 12 months. For the retrospective control cohort, laboratory and clinical data were extracted using a predefined window of ±14 days around each study timepoint; observations outside this window were considered missing.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the time to reach OMT, defined as the target doses or the highest dosing of all GDMT classes achieved and attained for at least 14 days. This was clinically assessed by the treating trial cardiologist in the TELEFASTER-HF study. In the control cohort, OMT status was assessed through a structured review of the medical record using the prespecified criteria. If a patient had not reached target doses for all GDMT classes and no documentation could be found indicating that the patient had achieved OMT or was unable to tolerate further titration, this was recorded as the patient having not achieved OMT in the analysis.

Key secondary endpoints included dose attainment in each drug class over time (no therapy, 1=>0%–<50% of GDMT target dose, 2=≥50%–<100% of GDMT target dose, 3=100% of GDMT target dose) for each core drug class (BB, RASi/ARNI, MRA and SGLT2i) and comparative analysis at 8 weeks with supportive analysis at 6 and 12 months. Further key secondary endpoints included target dose in all four GDMT drug classes with comparative analysis at 8 weeks and supportive analysis at 6 and 12 months. Supportive secondary endpoint was analysis of any-dose implementation of all four GDMT drug classes at these timepoints. Exploratory endpoints included simple GDMT score,22 assessed at baseline, 8 weeks, 6 months and 12 months, as well as the number of HF-related readmissions, total all-cause readmissions and change in eGFR from baseline to 12 months.

Statistical methods

Baseline characteristics are presented as medians with IQRs for continuous variables and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Differences between the intervention and control groups prior to weighting were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Analyses used a doubly robust approach targeting the average treatment effect, combining stabilised inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) with covariate-adjusted outcome regression.23 24 Missing baseline data were addressed using multiple imputations by chained equations (m=10). Given the low proportion of missingness, imputations were aggregated into a single consensus dataset to ensure stable propensity score estimation. The propensity score was estimated via logistic regression using 24 baseline covariates, including demographics, clinical characteristics, comorbidities, vitals and laboratory measures. Covariates for the propensity score model were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and their potential association with both treatment allocation and treatment optimisation. Stabilised weights were trimmed at the 5th and 95th percentiles to optimise the trade-off between bias reduction and variance. Covariate balance was evaluated using weighted standardised mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute value <0.10 indicating negligible imbalance, supported by visual diagnostics (Love plots, onlinesupplemental figures 1 2). Primary and secondary outcomes were evaluated using appropriate survey-weighted generalised linear models: quasibinomial regression for binary outcomes, weighted proportional odds models for ordinal dose categories and quasipoisson or exact Poisson regression for readmission counts. Time-to-event data were analysed using weighted Cox proportional hazards models. Survivor analysis logic was applied, treating post-mortality timepoints as missing. To account for residual imbalance and ensure double robustness, all outcome regression models were adjusted for age and baseline eGFR. Bias-reduced logistic regression (Firth’s method) was used to handle separation for outcomes where no patients achieved the endpoint in either arm. To assess robustness against unmeasured confounding, E-values were calculated for the primary outcome’s point estimate and lower 95% CI limit. All analyses were conducted using a two-tailed significance level of α=0.05. Analyses were performed in R (V.4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using appropriate packages for propensity score weighting, balance diagnostics and survey-weighted modelling.

Primary outcome

Time to OMT was shorter in the TELEFASTER-HF cohort (median 48 days vs 321 days, HR 16.1, 95% CI 8.3 to 31.2, p<0.001), with all patients achieving OMT within 1 year compared with 54% of controls (figure 2). Among the 36 control patients classified as not having achieved OMT due to the absence of documentation of completed titration, OMT or clinical barriers to further titration, none had reached target doses across all four GDMT classes, and only four were receiving >50% of target dose across all four classes.

Time to optimal medical therapy (OMT) from baseline to 12 months, stratified by study group. Time to OMT was substantially shorter in the TELEmonitored FAst Standardized Titration for Efficient Response in Heart Failure with reduced ejection fraction (TELEFASTER-HF) group compared with controls (median 48 vs 321 days; HR 16.1, 95% CI 8.3 to 31.2, p<0.001). By day 90, 93% of TELEFASTER-HF participants had achieved OMT compared with 8% of controls. At 12 months, all TELEFASTER-HF participants had achieved OMT, compared with 54% of controls.

Key secondary outcomes

Across all post-index/pseudo-index time points, participants in the TELEFASTER-HF cohort achieved substantially higher rates of target dose attainment in BB, RASi/ARNI and MRA compared with standard care. Group-wise distributions of GDMT dose categories at all prespecified time points are shown in figure 3, with complete numerical results reported in online supplemental table 1. At 8 weeks, TELEFASTER-HF was associated with substantially higher achieved dose categories compared with standard care. The odds of being in a higher dose category were markedly increased for BB (OR 9.4, 95% CI 3.8 to 22.8), RASi (OR 22.9, 95% CI 8.7 to 60) and MRAs (OR 16.2, 95% CI 6 to 43.9), all p<0.001. For SGLT2i, the point estimate was directionally consistent but did not reach statistical significance (OR 8.8, 95% CI 0.6 to 140, p=0.123) (table 2). Dose distributions at 6 and 12 months demonstrated sustained separation between cohorts (online supplemental table 2).

Supportive secondary outcome

At 8 weeks, TELEFASTER-HF participants were more likely to receive at least one dose of all four GDMT drug classes compared with standard care (98% vs 75%; OR 8.3, 95% CI 1.4 to 48.4, p=0.02), figure 4. Differences between cohorts were attenuated at 6 and 12 months, reflecting high implementation rates in both cohorts, with no statistically significant between-cohort differences at later timepoints.

Exploratory outcomes

Simple GDMT score differed between cohorts at both 8 weeks and 12 months, favouring TELEFASTER-HF (8 weeks: median 8 vs 7, OR 8, p<0.001; 12 months: median 8 vs 7, OR 3.2, p=0.01). The change in eGFR from baseline to 12 months was similar between cohorts (median −3 vs −1.6 mL/min/1.73 m², estimated difference −2.4 mL/min/1.73 m², p=0.40). No HF hospitalisations occurred in the TELEFASTER-HF cohort during 12 months of follow-up, compared with 26 events in the control cohort (incidence rate ratio[IRR] 0, 95% CI 0 to 0.2; p<0.001). All-cause readmissions were also lower in the TELEFASTER-HF cohort (IRR 0.6, 95% CI 0.4 to 1; p=0.03 (online supplemental table 3).

Discussion

In this comparative analysis, a standardised, home-based GDMT titration pathway was associated with markedly faster optimisation of GDMT compared with contemporary standard of care. The intervention was also associated with higher achieved dose categories and more sustained achievement of target doses of BB, RASi and MRAs. Half of patients in the TELEFASTER-HF cohort achieved target doses across all classes at 12 months, compared with only 13% in the control cohort. Although this study was not designed to assess safety outcomes, the observed improvements were achieved without evidence of renal harm. These findings address a persistent implementation gap in HFrEF care.

Although GDMT at target doses confers substantial prognostic benefit, real-world attainment remains low, often due to fragmented care pathways, reliance on in-clinic decision points and clinical inertia. The tolerability and effectiveness of streamlining fast initiation of GDMT quadruple therapy and rapid titration have been shown in a large randomised controlled trial as well as in smaller studies and real-world cohorts.4 19 20 Several studies have also introduced HBM to enable GDMT titration at home.17 18 All of these studies rely on protocols with extensive clinical controls, either in-clinic or by digital contacts. By reducing reliance on routine in-person clinical encounters during the titration phase and replacing them with protocol-driven dose escalation supported by HBM, TELEFASTER-HF enables rapid optimisation during the early, high-risk phase following diagnosis or clinical worsening, when treatment effects are greatest.

Three design features of the intervention may explain the observed effectiveness. First, rapid and simultaneous titration allowed comprehensive optimisation within a median of 48 days, substantially faster than reported in most prior digital or clinic-based strategies. Second, standardised protocols reduced clinician-level variability and mitigated the well-described decline in titration activity beyond the first 1–2 months of care.8 Third, home-based delivery minimised access barriers while maintaining safety through remote monitoring of vital signs and laboratory parameters.

Compared with prior studies, this study adds complementary evidence on the implementation of GDMT titration in routine care. STRONG-HF demonstrated the benefits of early, intensive optimisation following hospitalisation but did not evaluate long-term dose persistence beyond the early post-discharge phase. Nurse-led telemonitoring strategies without fully standardised protocols have reported more modest optimisation rates.25 Trials such as Medly-Titrate and ADMINISTER demonstrated short-term feasibility of digital GDMT optimisation, but long-term persistence beyond the intervention period has not been systematically demonstrated.13 18 26 In this context, the present study extends existing work by describing both early optimisation and sustained GDMT dose persistence through 12 months in a weighted real-world comparison. Regarding the implementation of GDMT across all four drug classes at any dose, we observed a between-cohort difference at 8 weeks. This difference was not sustained at later time points, suggesting that contemporary clinical practice may be increasingly attentive to initiating all four GDMT classes, at least at low doses. This is encouraging, as the early implementation of all four drug classes, even at modest doses, has been shown to be prognostically important.27 Standardised, protocol-driven approaches to GDMT titration may represent a feasible strategy to facilitate earlier and more consistent optimisation of therapy in patients with HFrEF. In the present study, HBM supported rapid dose escalation and high rates of dose attainment without scheduled in-person visits, suggesting that clinically meaningful optimisation can be achieved without reliance on frequent in-person visits. Although not designed or powered to evaluate clinical endpoints, the absence of HF hospitalisations and fewer all-cause hospitalisations in the intervention group during the first year is notable and aligns with emerging evidence linking comprehensive GDMT optimisation to improved outcomes.4 28 This should be interpreted cautiously, as baseline differences in age, hospitalisation status at diagnosis, frailty and other unmeasured factors may have contributed to the observed between-cohort differences. These findings remain exploratory but support further evaluation in adequately powered prospective randomised trials. If confirmed, this strategy could represent a scalable approach to improving guideline implementation, aligned with current ESC digital health priorities.14

Study limitations

Several limitations merit consideration. The non-randomised design and modest sample size limit causal inference and generalisability. The control cohort was older and more often hospitalised at heart-failure diagnosis, suggesting a potentially more vulnerable population. This may partly explain differences in hospitalisation outcomes, and these findings should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. However, inpatient versus outpatient status at diagnosis does not necessarily reflect the feasibility of GDMT titration; prior studies such as STRONG-HF suggest that hospitalised patients in a vulnerable phase can tolerate and benefit from rapid up-titration. Furthermore, no meaningful between-cohort differences in renal function or blood pressure were observed that would be expected to limit titration. Age may nevertheless play a role, as clinicians often have less robust evidence to guide GDMT initiation and up-titration in very elderly patients. Although age was included in the IPTW model and balance improved substantially after weighting, formal measures of frailty were not available. Therefore, residual confounding related to unmeasured differences in frailty between cohorts cannot be excluded. NT-proBNP was not included because measurements were not obtained at comparable time points between cohorts, with sampling in TELEFASTER-HF performed at the study index rather than at diagnosis, and corresponding diagnostic values were not consistently available in controls. Eligibility assessment at different timepoints between cohorts may have resulted in baseline differences in achieved GDMT dose at index/pseudo-index, which should be considered when interpreting dose-related secondary analyses. Despite the use of a pseudo-index date and IPTW adjustment to improve comparability between cohorts, the hybrid prospective-retrospective design remains susceptible to selection bias, information bias and residual time-related bias. Medication adherence beyond the intervention period could not be systematically assessed for the TELEFASTER-HF cohort, and GDMT assessment in the control cohort relied on EHR data. Furthermore, OMT was prospectively adjudicated during TELEFASTER-HF but retrospectively reconstructed from EHRs in the control cohort, creating the potential for differential outcome misclassification. Adjudication of OMT in the retrospective cohort was performed by a single reviewer, which may increase the risk of misclassification despite the use of standardised criteria. The use of a retrospectively assembled control cohort is subject to several additional limitations. Despite rigorous adjustment, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and increasing the number of covariates in the IPTW model may reduce precision and model stability. Moreover, retrospective data are inherently susceptible to incomplete capture of clinical decision-making, titration attempts and treatment-limiting factors, which can lead to misclassification of both exposures and outcomes. Nevertheless, the use of a carefully selected control cohort, longitudinal analyses of dose trajectories and robust weighting strategies strengthens the internal validity of the comparative findings.

Conclusions

A protocol-driven, home-based GDMT titration strategy was associated with rapid, comprehensive and durable optimisation of HFrEF therapy compared with standard care.
