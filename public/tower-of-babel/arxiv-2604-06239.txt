1.
Introduction

Let

(1)

D_{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}^{2}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2(n-k)}{n-k}\qquad(n\geq 0)

be the Domb numbers (OEIS A002895). Their ordinary generating function

\mathcal{A}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 0}D_{n}z^{n}

is the unique holomorphic solution at
z=0
of the third-order differential equation

(2)

\bigl[\theta^{3}-2z(2\theta+1)(5\theta^{2}+5\theta+2)+64z^{2}(\theta+1)^{3}\bigr]y=0,\qquad\theta=z\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}.

This sequence lies at the center of several Apéry-like phenomena: it admits hypergeometric and modular parametrizations, appears in the theory of random walks and Bessel moments, and it is exactly the sequence used by Cohen in his normalization of the Ramanujan Machine conjecture Z2 for
\zeta(3)

[
2
,
3
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.

The Apéry-limit

(3)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{B_{n}}{D_{n}}=\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3)

for the rational companion sequence
B_{n}
is listed in Almkvist–van Enckevort–van Straten–Zudilin
[
1
]
as a known value, but that paper gives neither a proof nor a bibliographic pointer for the Domb case. On the other hand, Cohen’s Domb sum

(4)

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{64^{n}}{n^{3}D_{n}D_{n-1}}=\frac{56}{3}\zeta(3)

appears in
[
3
]
in experimental form. The purpose of the present note is to fill this gap and to deduce from it the Ramanujan Machine continued fraction Z2.

The main results are the following.

Theorem 1.1

(Domb Apéry-limit)
.

Let
\{D_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be given by (
1
). Let
\{B_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}\subset\mathbb{Q}
be defined by

(5)

(n+1)^{3}u_{n+1}=2(2n+1)(5n^{2}+5n+2)u_{n}-64n^{3}u_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq 1),

with
B_{0}=0
and
B_{1}=1
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{B_{n}}{D_{n}}=\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3).

Theorem 1.2

(Domb sum)
.

One has

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{64^{n}}{n^{3}D_{n}D_{n-1}}=\frac{56}{3}\zeta(3).

Theorem 1.3

(Ramanujan Machine conjecture Z2)
.

The continued fraction

2+\cfrac{-16\cdot 1^{6}}{36+\cfrac{-16\cdot 2^{6}}{160+\cfrac{-16\cdot 3^{6}}{434+\ddots}}}

converges and its value is

\frac{12}{7\zeta(3)}.

Equivalently,

\PCF\bigl((2n+1)(5n^{2}+5n+2),-16n^{6}\bigr)=\frac{12}{7\zeta(3)}.

The proof of

1.1
occupies §§

. Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
then follow by finite-difference and continuant algebra.

2.
Domb recurrence, companion sequence, and continuants

The Domb numbers satisfy the well-known three-term recurrence

(6)

(n+1)^{3}D_{n+1}=2(2n+1)(5n^{2}+5n+2)D_{n}-64n^{3}D_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq 1),

with
D_{0}=1
and
D_{1}=4
; equivalently,

(7)

(n+2)^{3}D_{n+2}-(2n+3)(10n^{2}+30n+24)D_{n+1}+64(n+1)^{3}D_{n}=0\qquad(n\geq 0).

See, for instance,
[
4
, Table 2, case
6(B)
]
.

Let
\{B_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}\subset\mathbb{Q}
be the unique sequence satisfying the same recurrence (
5
) and the initial values
B_{0}=0
,
B_{1}=1
.

Lemma 2.1

(discrete Wronskian)
.

Define

W_{n}:=D_{n}B_{n-1}-D_{n-1}B_{n}\qquad(n\geq 1).

Then

W_{n}=-\frac{64^{n-1}}{n^{3}}\qquad(n\geq 1).

Consequently,

(8)

\frac{B_{n}}{D_{n}}-\frac{B_{n-1}}{D_{n-1}}=\frac{64^{n-1}}{n^{3}D_{n}D_{n-1}}\qquad(n\geq 1).

Proof.

From (
5
) and (
6
) we obtain

\displaystyle W_{n+1}

\displaystyle=D_{n+1}B_{n}-D_{n}B_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n+1)^{3}}\Bigl(2(2n+1)(5n^{2}+5n+2)D_{n}-64n^{3}D_{n-1}\Bigr)B_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{(n+1)^{3}}D_{n}\Bigl(2(2n+1)(5n^{2}+5n+2)B_{n}-64n^{3}B_{n-1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{64n^{3}}{(n+1)^{3}}\,(D_{n}B_{n-1}-D_{n-1}B_{n})=\frac{64n^{3}}{(n+1)^{3}}W_{n}.

Since
W_{1}=D_{1}B_{0}-D_{0}B_{1}=-1
, induction gives the stated formula. Dividing by
D_{n}D_{n-1}
yields (
8
).
∎

Corollary 2.2

(finite telescoping identity)
.

For every
N\geq 1
,

(9)

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{64^{n}}{n^{3}D_{n}D_{n-1}}=64\frac{B_{N}}{D_{N}}.

Proof.

Sum (
8
) from
n=1
to
n=N
and use
B_{0}=0
.
∎

Proof of
theorem

1.2
assuming
theorem

1.1
.

Let
N\to\infty
in (
9
). By
theorem

1.1
the right-hand side tends to

64\cdot\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3)=\frac{56}{3}\zeta(3).

∎

We next connect the Domb recurrence to the Ramanujan Machine continued fraction Z2.

Proposition 2.3

(continuants and Domb normalization)
.

Let

a_{0}:=2,\qquad a_{n}:=(2n+1)(5n^{2}+5n+2)\quad(n\geq 1),\qquad b_{n}:=-16n^{6}.

Define continuants by

P_{-1}=1,\quad P_{0}=2,\qquad Q_{-1}=0,\quad Q_{0}=1,

(10)

U_{n}=a_{n}U_{n-1}+b_{n}U_{n-2}\qquad(n\geq 1),

where
U_{n}=P_{n}
or
U_{n}=Q_{n}
. Then

(11)

P_{n}=\frac{(n+1)!^{3}}{2^{n+1}}D_{n+1},\qquad Q_{n}=\frac{(n+1)!^{3}}{2^{n}}B_{n+1}\qquad(n\geq 0).

Hence

(12)

\frac{P_{n}}{Q_{n}}=\frac{D_{n+1}}{2B_{n+1}}.

Proof.

Set

\widetilde{P}_{n}:=\frac{(n+1)!^{3}}{2^{n+1}}D_{n+1}.

Using (
6
) with index
n
we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+1)!^{3}}{2^{n+1}}D_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+1)!^{3}}{2^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{2(2n+1)(5n^{2}+5n+2)D_{n}-64n^{3}D_{n-1}}{(n+1)^{3}}

\displaystyle=(2n+1)(5n^{2}+5n+2)\frac{n!^{3}}{2^{n}}D_{n}-16n^{6}\frac{(n-1)!^{3}}{2^{n-1}}D_{n-1}

\displaystyle=a_{n}\widetilde{P}_{n-1}+b_{n}\widetilde{P}_{n-2}

for
n\geq 1
. Also
\widetilde{P}_{0}=2
and
\widetilde{P}_{1}=56=a_{1}\widetilde{P}_{0}+b_{1}P_{-1}
, so
\widetilde{P}_{n}=P_{n}
by uniqueness for (
10
).

The same calculation with
B_{n}
in place of
D_{n}
gives

\widetilde{Q}_{n}:=\frac{(n+1)!^{3}}{2^{n}}B_{n+1}

satisfying (
10
). Here
\widetilde{Q}_{0}=1
and
\widetilde{Q}_{1}=36=a_{1}
, hence
\widetilde{Q}_{n}=Q_{n}
. Formula (
12
) follows immediately.
∎

Proof of
theorem

1.3
assuming
theorem

1.1
.

By construction,
P_{n}/Q_{n}
is the
n
th convergent of the continued fraction in
theorem

1.3
. From (
12
) and
theorem

1.1
we get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{P_{n}}{Q_{n}}=\frac{1}{2\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}B_{n+1}/D_{n+1}}=\frac{1}{2\cdot\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3)}=\frac{12}{7\zeta(3)}.\qed

3.
Modular parametrization and the Eichler integral

We write

\mathcal{A}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 0}D_{n}z^{n},\qquad\mathcal{B}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 0}B_{n}z^{n}.

By construction,
\mathcal{A}
satisfies (
2
), while
\mathcal{B}
satisfies the inhomogeneous equation

(13)

\bigl[\theta^{3}-2z(2\theta+1)(5\theta^{2}+5\theta+2)+64z^{2}(\theta+1)^{3}\bigr]\mathcal{B}=z.

In ordinary differential form these become

\displaystyle z^{2}(4z-1)(16z-1)y^{\prime\prime\prime}+3z(128z^{2}-30z+1)y^{\prime\prime}

(14)

\displaystyle\qquad+(448z^{2}-68z+1)y^{\prime}+4(16z-1)y=0,

for
y=\mathcal{A}
, and the same equation with right-hand side
z
for
y=\mathcal{B}
.

The modular parametrization is the level-
6
eta-product identity (Chan–Zudilin; Zhou)

(15)

\xi(\tau):=-\frac{\eta(2\tau)^{6}\eta(6\tau)^{6}}{\eta(\tau)^{6}\eta(3\tau)^{6}},\qquad A(\tau):=\frac{\eta(\tau)^{4}\eta(3\tau)^{4}}{\eta(2\tau)^{2}\eta(6\tau)^{2}},

with

(16)

A(\tau)=\mathcal{A}(\xi(\tau))=\sum_{n\geq 0}D_{n}\,\xi(\tau)^{n}.

See
[
9
, (3.2)–(3.7)]
; compare also
[
2
]
.

The Atkin–Lehner involution relevant here is

(17)

W(\tau):=\frac{3\tau-2}{6\tau-3},\qquad W=\begin{pmatrix}3&-2\\
6&-3\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det W=3,\qquad W^{2}=-3I.

We use the usual slash operator for matrices of positive determinant:

(f|_{k}M)(\tau):=(\det M)^{k/2}(c\tau+d)^{-k}f\!\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\right),\qquad M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}.

From
[
9
, (3.4), (3.7)]
we have

(18)

\xi(W\tau)=\xi(\tau),\qquad A(W\tau)=-\frac{(6\tau-3)^{2}}{3}A(\tau),

that is,

(19)

A|_{2}W=-A.

Define

(20)

E(\tau):=\frac{\mathcal{B}(\xi(\tau))}{A(\tau)}.

The next proposition identifies
E
with a weight
-2
Eichler integral.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

(21)

g(\tau):=\frac{E_{4}(\tau)-E_{4}(2\tau)-9E_{4}(3\tau)+9E_{4}(6\tau)}{240}=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}q^{n},\qquad q=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\tau},

where

(22)

a_{n}=\sigma_{3}(n)-\sigma_{3}(n/2)-9\sigma_{3}(n/3)+9\sigma_{3}(n/6).

Then

(23)

E(\tau)=-\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n}}{n^{3}}q^{n},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad D^{3}E=-g,

where
D=q\,\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}q=(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{-1}\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\tau
.

Proof.

Apply the third-order variation-of-constants construction of Yang
[
8
, Lemmas 1–2]
to the ordinary inhomogeneous equation (
14
) for
\mathcal{B}
. Since the leading coefficient in (
14
) is
z^{2}(4z-1)(16z-1)
, this gives

(24)

D^{3}E(\tau)=\Phi(\tau):=\left(\frac{D\xi(\tau)}{\xi(\tau)}\right)^{3}\frac{\xi(\tau)}{A(\tau)(1-4\xi(\tau))(1-16\xi(\tau))}.

Because
(D\xi)/\xi
has weight
2
,
A
has weight
2
, and
\xi
is modular of weight
0
, the function
\Phi
is a modular form of weight
4
on
\Gamma_{0}(6)
.

A direct
q
-expansion from (
15
) gives

\Phi(\tau)=-q-8q^{2}-19q^{3}-64q^{4}+O(q^{5}),

while from (
21
) one has

-g(\tau)=-q-8q^{2}-19q^{3}-64q^{4}+O(q^{5}).

Thus
\Phi+g\in M_{4}(\Gamma_{0}(6))
has vanishing Fourier coefficients through
q^{4}
. Since
[\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma_{0}(6)]=12
, the Sturm bound for weight
4
and level
6
is
\lfloor 4\cdot 12/12\rfloor=4
; hence, by Sturm’s theorem
[
5
, Theorem 3.13]
,
\Phi=-g
. This proves (
23
).
∎

We record the
L
-function of
g
and its alternating twist.

Proposition 3.2
.

The Dirichlet series of
g
is

(25)

L(g,s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n}}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)\zeta(s-3)(1-2^{-s})(1-3^{2-s}).

Hence

(26)

L(g,3)=-\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3).

Define the alternating twist

(27)

L^{*}(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{n}a_{n}}{n^{s}}.

Then

(28)

L^{*}(s)=-\zeta(s)\zeta(s-3)(1-2^{-s})(1-2^{4-s})(1-3^{2-s}).

In particular,

(29)

L^{*}(3)=-\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3),\qquad L^{*}(2)=0,\qquad L^{*}(1)=\frac{7}{4\pi^{2}}\zeta(3).

Moreover, for every
s\in\mathbb{C}
,

(30)

\Gamma(s)\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^{-s}L^{*}(s+3)=\Gamma(-s-2)\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^{s+2}L^{*}(1-s).

Proof.

Equation (
25
) follows immediately from (
22
) and the classical identity

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\sigma_{3}(n)}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)\zeta(s-3).

Substituting
s=3
yields (
26
) because
\zeta(0)=-1/2
.

For the twist, write
n=2^{r}m
with
m
odd. Since (
22
) implies

a_{2^{r}m}=8^{r}a_{m}\qquad(m\text{ odd},\ r\geq 0),

we get

\displaystyle L^{*}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\text{ odd}}\frac{a_{m}}{m^{s}}\left(-1+\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{8^{r}}{2^{rs}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\text{ odd}}\frac{a_{m}}{m^{s}}\left(-1+\frac{2^{3-s}}{1-2^{3-s}}\right)=-\frac{1-2^{4-s}}{1-2^{3-s}}\sum_{m\text{ odd}}\frac{a_{m}}{m^{s}}.

On the other hand, removing the
2
-Euler factor from (
25
) gives

\sum_{m\text{ odd}}\frac{a_{m}}{m^{s}}=(1-2^{3-s})L(g,s).

Combining these formulas with (
25
) proves (
28
). The values in (
29
) follow immediately, using the standard identity

\zeta^{\prime}(-2)=-\frac{\zeta(3)}{4\pi^{2}}.

Finally, (
30
) is a direct consequence of (
28
) and the functional equation of the Riemann zeta function. Indeed,

\displaystyle\Gamma(s)\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^{-s}L^{*}(s+3)

\displaystyle\qquad=-\Gamma(s)\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^{-s}\zeta(s+3)\zeta(s)(1-2^{-s-3})(1-2^{1-s})(1-3^{-s-1})

and the two zeta functional equations transform the right-hand side into

\Gamma(-s-2)\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^{s+2}L^{*}(1-s).\qed

4.
Local analysis at the dominant singularity

Let

(31)

\tau_{*}:=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\sqrt{3}}.

Then
\tau_{*}
is the unique point of the upper half-plane satisfying

(32)

3\tau^{2}-3\tau+1=0,

and
W(\tau_{*})=\tau_{*}
. By
[
9
, (3.11)]
,

(33)

\xi(\tau_{*})=\frac{1}{16}.

Thus the dominant singularity of
\mathcal{A}(z)
corresponds to the order-
2
elliptic fixed point
\tau_{*}
. Write

z_{0}:=\frac{1}{16},\qquad\varepsilon:=1-16z.

Proposition 4.1
.

The point
z_{0}=1/16
is a regular singular point of (
14
), and its local exponents are

0,\qquad\frac{1}{2},\qquad 1.

Consequently, near
z_{0}
one has expansions

(34)

\mathcal{A}(z)=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\varepsilon^{1/2}+O(\varepsilon),\qquad\mathcal{B}(z)=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\varepsilon^{1/2}+O(\varepsilon)

for suitable constants
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\beta_{0},\beta_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

In (
14
) the coefficient of
y^{\prime\prime\prime}
has a simple zero at
z_{0}
, so
z_{0}
is a regular singular point. Substitute
z=(1-\varepsilon)/16
and
y=\varepsilon^{r}
. Since
\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}z=-16\,\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\varepsilon
, the coefficient of
\varepsilon^{r-2}
in (
14
) is

-6r(2r-1)(r-1).

Hence the indicial equation is
r(2r-1)(r-1)=0
, and the exponents are exactly
0
,
1/2
, and
1
.

Now
A(\tau)=\mathcal{A}(\xi(\tau))
and
\mathcal{B}(\xi(\tau))
are holomorphic in
\tau
near
\tau_{*}
. Since
\tau_{*}
is an elliptic point of order
2
for the genus-zero group generated by
\Gamma_{0}(6)
and
W
, the Hauptmodul
\xi
has ramification index
2
at
\tau_{*}
; equivalently,

(35)

\xi(\tau)-\frac{1}{16}=c(\tau-\tau_{*})^{2}+O\bigl((\tau-\tau_{*})^{3}\bigr)\qquad(c\neq 0).

Therefore
\varepsilon^{1/2}
is a holomorphic local parameter in
\tau
, and the expansions (
34
) follow.
∎

Lemma 4.2

(transfer to coefficients)
.

The coefficients of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
satisfy

D_{n}\sim-\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\,16^{n}n^{-3/2},\qquad B_{n}\sim-\frac{\beta_{1}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\,16^{n}n^{-3/2},

and hence

(36)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{B_{n}}{D_{n}}=\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}.

Proof.

By
proposition

4.1
, both functions admit local expansions of the form

F(z)=c_{0}+c_{1}\bigl(1-z/z_{0}\bigr)^{1/2}+O(1-z/z_{0})\qquad(z_{0}=1/16).

Since there is no other singularity on
|z|=z_{0}
, the Flajolet–Odlyzko transfer theorem applies; see
[
6
, Theorem VI.1]
. It gives

[z^{n}]F(z)\sim-\frac{c_{1}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\,z_{0}^{-n}n^{-3/2}.

Applying this to
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
yields the stated asymptotics and (
36
).
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

One has

(37)

\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}=\frac{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathcal{B}(\xi(\tau))\big|_{\tau=\tau_{*}}}{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}A(\tau)\big|_{\tau=\tau_{*}}}.

Proof.

Let
u=\tau-\tau_{*}
. By (
35
) we have

\varepsilon^{1/2}=\lambda u+O(u^{2})\qquad(\lambda\neq 0).

Hence (
34
) becomes

A(\tau)=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\lambda u+O(u^{2}),\qquad\mathcal{B}(\xi(\tau))=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\lambda u+O(u^{2}).

Differentiating at
u=0
gives

A^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=\alpha_{1}\lambda,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathcal{B}(\xi(\tau))\Big|_{\tau=\tau_{*}}=\beta_{1}\lambda,

which proves (
37
).
∎

5.
Atkin–Lehner transformation law

We first compute the action of
W
on the Eisenstein combination
g
.

Lemma 5.1
.

With
W
as in (
17
), one has

E_{4}(\tau)|_{4}W=9E_{4}(3\tau),\qquad E_{4}(2\tau)|_{4}W=9E_{4}(6\tau),

E_{4}(3\tau)|_{4}W=\frac{1}{9}E_{4}(\tau),\qquad E_{4}(6\tau)|_{4}W=\frac{1}{9}E_{4}(2\tau).

Consequently,

(38)

g|_{4}W=-g.

Proof.

Factor

W=\begin{pmatrix}3&-2\\
6&-3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2\\
2&-3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\begin{pmatrix}1&-2\\
2&-3\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
E_{4}
is modular on
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
,

E_{4}|_{4}W=(E_{4}|_{4}\begin{pmatrix}1&-2\\
2&-3\end{pmatrix})\Big|_{4}\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=E_{4}\Big|_{4}\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=9E_{4}(3\tau).

Similarly,

\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}W=\begin{pmatrix}6&-4\\
6&-3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-4\\
1&-3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}6&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

so

E_{4}(2\tau)|_{4}W=2^{-2}E_{4}\Big|_{4}\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}W=2^{-2}E_{4}\Big|_{4}\begin{pmatrix}1&-4\\
1&-3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}6&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=9E_{4}(6\tau).

Now
W^{2}=-3I
, and for even weights
k
one has
f|_{k}(-3I)=f
, so
|_{k}W
is an involution. Applying
|_{4}W
to the first two formulas gives the other two. Substituting these identities into (
21
) yields (
38
).
∎

The next lemma is the special case of Bol’s identity that we need.

Lemma 5.2
.

For every holomorphic function
F
on
\mathfrak{H}
,

(39)

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{3}}\left(\frac{(6\tau-3)^{2}}{3}F(W\tau)\right)=\frac{9}{(6\tau-3)^{4}}F^{(3)}(W\tau).

Equivalently,

D^{3}(\left.F\right|_{-2}W)=\left.D^{3}F\right|_{4}W.

Proof.

A direct differentiation suffices. Since

W^{\prime}(\tau)=\frac{3}{(6\tau-3)^{2}},\qquad W^{\prime\prime}(\tau)=-\frac{36}{(6\tau-3)^{3}},\qquad W^{\prime\prime\prime}(\tau)=\frac{648}{(6\tau-3)^{4}},

repeated application of the chain rule shows that all terms involving
F
,
F^{\prime}
,
F^{\prime\prime}
cancel in the third derivative, leaving exactly (
39
). Dividing by
(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{3}
gives the
D
-version.
∎

We now determine the period polynomial of

.

Proposition 5.3

(Atkin–Lehner law for

)
.

One has

(40)

(\left.E\right|_{-2}W)(\tau)+E(\tau)=\frac{7}{6}\zeta(3)\,(3\tau^{2}-3\tau+1)=-4L(g,3)\,(3\tau^{2}-3\tau+1).

Proof.

Set

H(\tau):=(\left.E\right|_{-2}W)(\tau)+E(\tau).

By
lemmas

5.2
,
5.1
and
3.1
,

D^{3}H=(\left.D^{3}E\right|_{4}W)+D^{3}E=-(g|_{4}W)-g=0.

Therefore
H
is a polynomial of degree at most
2
.

To determine it, restrict to the
W
-invariant geodesic

(41)

\tau(y):=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\mathrm{i}y}{2\sqrt{3}}\qquad(y>0).

Then
W\tau(y)=\tau(1/y)
and, by (
23
),

(42)

F(y):=E(\tau(y))=-\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{n}a_{n}}{n^{3}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi ny}{\sqrt{3}}\right).

Mellin inversion gives, for
c>1
,

(43)

F(y)=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{(c)}\Gamma(s)\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^{-s}L^{*}(s+3)y^{-s}\,\mathrm{d}s.

By (
30
), the integrand is invariant under
s\mapsto-s-2
. Using this symmetry, shifting the contour, and collecting residues at
s=0,-2
(there is no residue at
s=-1
because
L^{*}(2)=0
) yields

(44)

F(y)-y^{2}F(1/y)=\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3)(1-y^{2}).

Indeed,

\Res_{s=0}=L^{*}(3)=-\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3),

while

\Res_{s=-2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^{2}L^{*}(1)y^{2}=\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3)y^{2}.

Now, on the geodesic (
41
),

(\left.E\right|_{-2}W)(\tau(y))=\frac{(6\tau(y)-3)^{2}}{3}E(W\tau(y))=-y^{2}F(1/y),

so (
44
) is exactly

H(\tau(y))=\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3)(1-y^{2}).

Finally,

3\tau(y)^{2}-3\tau(y)+1=\frac{1-y^{2}}{4},

whence

H(\tau(y))=\frac{7}{6}\zeta(3)\,(3\tau(y)^{2}-3\tau(y)+1).

Since both sides are polynomials of degree at most
2
in
\tau
and they agree on the infinite set
\{\tau(y):y>0\}
, identity (
40
) follows.
∎

6.
Proof of the Domb Apéry-limit

We first differentiate the transformation law at the fixed point
\tau_{*}
. Since

3\tau_{*}^{2}-3\tau_{*}+1=0,\qquad 6\tau_{*}-3=\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3},\qquad W^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=-1,

differentiating (
40
) at
\tau=\tau_{*}
gives

(45)

E(\tau_{*})+\frac{E^{\prime}(\tau_{*})}{2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}}=\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3).

Indeed, differentiating the left-hand side of (
40
) yields

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}(\left.E\right|_{-2}W)(\tau_{*})+E^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=4\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}\,E(\tau_{*})+E^{\prime}(\tau_{*})+E^{\prime}(\tau_{*}),

which equals
4\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}\,E(\tau_{*})+2E^{\prime}(\tau_{*})
; differentiating the right-hand side gives

\frac{7}{6}\zeta(3)(6\tau_{*}-3)=\frac{7}{6}\zeta(3)\,\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}.

After division by
4\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}
we obtain (
45
).

The same differentiation applied to (
19
) yields the derivative of
A
.

Lemma 6.1
.

At the fixed point
\tau_{*}
one has

(46)

A^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}\,A(\tau_{*}).

Proof.

Equation (
19
) is equivalent to

A(W\tau)=-\frac{(6\tau-3)^{2}}{3}A(\tau).

Differentiate and evaluate at
\tau_{*}
. Since
W(\tau_{*})=\tau_{*}
and
W^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=-1
, we obtain

-A^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=-4(6\tau_{*}-3)A(\tau_{*})-\frac{(6\tau_{*}-3)^{2}}{3}A^{\prime}(\tau_{*}).

Because
(6\tau_{*}-3)^{2}=-3
, the last term equals
+A^{\prime}(\tau_{*})
, and the identity simplifies to

2A^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=4(6\tau_{*}-3)A(\tau_{*})=4\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}\,A(\tau_{*}),

which is (
46
).
∎

We now complete the proof.

Proof of
theorem

1.1
.

From (
20
) we have

\mathcal{B}(\xi(\tau))=A(\tau)E(\tau).

Differentiating at
\tau_{*}
and using
lemma

6.1
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathcal{B}(\xi(\tau))\big|_{\tau=\tau_{*}}}{A^{\prime}(\tau_{*})}

\displaystyle=\frac{A^{\prime}(\tau_{*})E(\tau_{*})+A(\tau_{*})E^{\prime}(\tau_{*})}{A^{\prime}(\tau_{*})}

\displaystyle=E(\tau_{*})+\frac{E^{\prime}(\tau_{*})}{2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}}.

By (
45
) the right-hand side equals
\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3)
. Therefore, by
lemma

4.3
,

\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}=\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3).

Finally,
lemma

4.2
gives

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{B_{n}}{D_{n}}=\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}=\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3),

as claimed.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

The proof shows that the relevant constant is
not
the naive CM-value
E(\tau_{*})
. Instead, it is the linear coefficient of the Eichler integral at the order-
2
elliptic point, namely

E(\tau_{*})+\frac{E^{\prime}(\tau_{*})}{2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}}=\frac{7}{24}\zeta(3).

This is the correct replacement for the false identity
E(\tau_{*})=-L(g,3)
.
