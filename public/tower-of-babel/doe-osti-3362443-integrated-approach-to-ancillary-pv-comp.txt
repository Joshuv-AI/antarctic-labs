3. Executive Summary:
 Photovoltaic (PV) power systems offer highly efficient, clean energy along with the
potential for grid independence. As such, they are a critical component of the US
renewable energy portfolio. The key factors in ensuring increased market penetration of
these systems are reducing the installation cost; improving the efficiency of these
systems, both in the short and long term; and improving the reliability of these systems.
In order to achieve PV systems with full-system reliability for 25 years, the inverter, whose
failure can shut down power generation for all upstream systems, is critical to maintaining
system availability. Thus, methodologies to predict and enhance the reliability of the
inverter are essential to overall power grid reliability.
 There is a significant disconnect between the expected lifespan of PV inverters based
on traditional reliability assessments, which routinely predict greater than 30 years life,
and the failure rate of inverters in the field, where initial failures occur within the first 3
years. This discrepancy has been addressed in this research which focused on
understanding the causes of this discrepancy and then using an integrated approach to
reliability that includes 1) an analysis of field environmental, degradation, and failure data;
2) the use of Physics of Failure (PoF) models based on fundamental degradation
processes in materials and structures for assessing degradation and failure of
components; and 3) the determination of the effect of different inverter operating
conditions on the stresses on the components to better assess PV inverter lifetime, along
with identifying strategies to extend that lifespan.
 Through a structured program consisting of field data collection, aggregation,
classification, and analysis, anomaly detection, fundamental degradation and failure
mechanism modeling, correlation of system wide environmental and operational loads to
component stresses, and novel accelerated testing development, we have created the
first end-to-end, data-driven reliability framework for outdoor PV inverters. In addition, we
have used this framework to create a digital twin for a novel SiC test-bed inverter. Testing
of that inverter was then used to calibrate and validate the framework and the digital twin.
The framework was then used to improve the reliability of the SiC test-bed inverter
through the development of an active gate control strategy.
 In sum, we have established a nondestructive, generalized methodology in this project
that (1) fuses rich field data with advanced ML for proactive reliability forecasting, (2)
dramatically reduces experimental iterations via synthetic dataset generation, and (3)
achieves unprecedented regression precision in both anomaly detection and component-
level degradation assessment—paving the way for truly predictive maintenance of grid-
tied PV inverters under diverse outdoor conditions. Furthermore, it demonstrated that
ensuring long‐term performance in SiC‐based systems requires a holistic approach that
integrates realistic stress replication, accurate health monitoring, and data‐driven
optimization. The findings confirmed that realistic testing provides the data necessary for
improving reliability methods. These insights will guide future efforts to scale and
commercialize the technology for broader high‐reliability power conversion applications.

5. Background:
 Photovoltaic (PV) power systems offer highly efficient, clean energy along with the
potential for grid independence. The key factors in ensuring the growth of these systems
are reducing the installation cost; improving the efficiency of these systems, both in the
short and long term; and improving the reliability of these systems. In order to achieve PV
systems with full-system reliability for 25 years [1], the inverter, whose failure can shut
down power generation for all upstream systems, is critical to maintaining system
availability. At a lifespan of 25 years, thermomechanical failure mechanisms become a
concern, specifically of the switching devices and the capacitors.
 The prevalent methods for analyzing the thermomechanical reliability of PV inverters
begin with developing a thermal profile. [2] established a thermal profile based on a
prototype and directly cycled IGBTs used in DC-DC converters to analyze the number of
cycles to failure based on failure criteria for resistance and voltage readings. This work
used field data to develop a thermal profile for its digital twin. [3] used humidity data from
Miami in January to develop a profile for evaluating the moisture ingress onto a MOSFET
within a package. They also ran live testing on a PV microinverter over a range of humidity
conditions to evaluate the effectiveness of current standards of reliability in humidity, but
did not develop a generalized model. This project ran component-level testing in a range
of humidity conditions as well as prototype-level testing to validate a digital twin. [4]
developed an electro-thermal model of the losses within an inverter in response to
ambient temperature and solar irradiation, as was developed in this project. Hacke et. al.
[5] combined an electro-thermal model with mission profile data from Ft. Peck, MT and
Phoenix, AZ, and then used that model with a rainflow counting algorithm and a simplified
Coffin-Manson model to estimate the lifetime of the semiconductors in the inverter, but
this was never tied back to a full-scale inverter model. All of these studies indicate that
thermomechanical reliability beyond 25 years can easily be achieved with proper
component selection and thermal design. Field data, however, indicates that PV inverters
rarely last more than 2.5 years without a hardware failure [6]. The results from [7]
corroborate this premise, indicating that inverters still represent the highest occurrence
for hardware failures in a PV system.
 There is therefore a significant disconnect between the expected lifespan of PV
inverters based on thermomechanical failure and the failure rate of inverters in the field.
This indicates that other failure mechanisms are present, or that previous research
experiments do not capture the actual stresses that PV inverter components experience
in the field. This discrepancy has been addressed in this research which focused on
understanding the causes of this discrepancy and then using an integrated top-down
machine learning and bottom-up PoF modeling scheme to better assess PV inverter
lifetime, along with identifying strategies to extend that lifespan.
 Our development of machine learning approaches in milestones 1.1.1–1.1.4 and 2.5.1
leverages methodologies which have been published in IEEE Transactions on Reliability
[8], IEEE Transactions on Industrial Applications [12], and IEEE Journal of Emerging and
Selected Topics in Power Electronics [16]. These articles inform a comprehensive, data‐
driven reliability assessment framework for photovoltaic inverters. Under milestones
1.1.1–1.1.3, our data-driven Markov-chain reliability framework [8] parallels Liu et al.’s

ESCNN-based prognostic model in its reliance on real-field inverter measurements and
weather inputs to capture stressor–failure relationships [9], yet diverges by using an
unsupervised one-class SVM for anomaly detection, classifying humidity cycling and
temperature fluctuations directly from alert and sensor streams rather than a Siamese
CNN for classification of impending failures. Similarly, Hopwood et al. apply neural
networks (CNN and LSTM) to classify IV-curve signatures of module-level faults,
demonstrating high accuracy on small datasets [10], whereas we treat inverter alerts as
stochastic transitions in a continuous-time Markov process, yielding quantitative lifetime-
reliability projections (20% survival at 20 years) rather than discrete fault labels. Chang
et al. deploy AI for near-real-time inverter fault detection across thousands of units,
triggering mobile notifications upon power deviations [11], but our approach instead
aggregates six months of alerts per inverter into a unified reliability metric—greatly
reducing false alarms and computational load while enabling site-wide preventive
scheduling. By integrating Liu’s field-data fusion, Hopwood’s domain-informed anomaly
characterization, and Chang’s scalable alert-automation insights, our method not only
accelerates the timeline (eliminating lengthy accelerated testing) but also elevates quality
via a generalized, noninvasive, and data-efficient estimator that adapts to any commercial
PV inverter with minimal retraining. Together, these approaches underpin a
nondestructive, scalable reliability estimator tailored to real-world operating conditions for
ancillary components of photovoltaic inverters.
 Our other article [12] on a dual-stage machine-learning pipeline serves milestone
1.1.4. This concept combines artificial neural network classification with Random Forest
and K-Nearest Neighbors clustering-regression over six months of inverter alert and
ambient datasets from a 1.4 MW PV plant, reducing mean squared error to as low as
0.002. In comparing similar studies, several inferences and contrasts emerge with our
spatio-temporal, ML-driven reliability framework. Gunda et al. [13] likewise exploit large
field-level datasets, 55,000 maintenance records across 800+ sites to uncover dominant
inverter failure modes via supervised classification, mirroring our use of alert logs and
environmental inputs; however, their focus remains on post-mortem failure cataloging
(e.g., communications, ground faults) rather than proactive, regression-based reliability
estimation under varying ambient stressors. Liu et al. [14] advance inverter prognosis by
fusing real-world measurements and domain-knowledge features into an Enhanced
Siamese CNN for classification of imminent failures, whereas we apply dual-stage
clustering–regression (RF/KNN) to directly predict quantitative reliability metrics (MSE
down to 0.002) under multivariate weather conditions, trading image-based similarity
learning for structured feature regression. Finally, Mu et al. [15] employ Monte Carlo
driven lifetime modeling and reliability block diagrams on real mission profiles to
statistically project inverter lifetimes, but without leveraging fine-grained alert-to-ambient
correlations; our data-driven ML pipeline instead harnesses those correlations to
accelerate model calibration (bypassing extensive lifetime simulations) and improve
predictive quality by integrating clustering for dimensionality reduction and inverter-
specific training. By combining Gunda’s large-scale failure taxonomy, Liu’s domain-
augmented deep-learning insights, and Mu’s mission-profile reliability methodology, our
approach both shortens development cycles (through rapid ML model retraining on alert-
ambient clusters) and elevates output accuracy, yielding a scalable reliability estimator
tailored to each inverter’s environmental history.

 Another of our articles [16] develops an ML-driven digital twin of a DC/DC buck
converter, calibrated via offline particle swarm optimization and validated under varying
duty-cycle and load conditions and then employs Random Forest regression on synthetic
degradation data to achieve an R² of 0.99978. The recently published works share our
confidence in digital-twin (DT) modeling and optimization for inverter filter circuit health
monitoring delivered in milestone 2.5.1. These works can be compared to highlight both
similarities and novel contributions. For example, Choksi et al. [17] develop a real-time
DT for multiphase boost converters, using PSO and GA in a state-space Runge–Kutta
framework to identify component degradation online under both continuous current mode
and discrete current mode; similarly, we employ offline PSO to calibrate our circuit-level
DT against hardware switching data, but we target steady-state buck-converter
degradation parameters (inductor, capacitor, MOSFET) and validate via average-model
duty/load sweeps rather than interleaved boost dynamics. Di Nezio et al. [18] also use
PSO, GA, and SA for LC-filter parameter identification in a three-phase AC-DC DT under
balanced and unbalanced conditions; our method differs by generating large synthetic
datasets with induced parasitic degradations and then applying Random Forest
regression, achieving R² up to 0.99978 and RMSE of 4.2×10⁻⁶ to noninvasively estimate
ohmic‐loss and parasitic profiles, rather than just filter L and C values. Finally, Miao et al.
[19] introduce an LSTM-FCN and contrastive-learning approach on voltage-ripple
signatures for early filter-stage degradation detection, focusing on reduced sampling and
augmentation for robustness; we leverage their signal-feature extraction insights but
instead fuse high-fidelity DT data with RF models to accelerate training, improve
parameter-estimation accuracy, and minimize experimental iterations. By integrating
Particle swarm optimization (PSO) based DT calibration [17], robust optimizer
comparisons [18], and ML-driven feature-learning strategies [19], our project both
accelerates the development timeline, thanks to extensive offline dataset generation and
elevates the quality of reliability assessment through unprecedented regression precision
and comprehensive prototype validation.
 Based on our understanding of failure mechanisms through this machine learning
based digital twin, a novel SiC based inverter was developed to calibrate and validate the
results of the model. Neutral Point Clamped (NPC) inverters are widely adopted in
photovoltaic applications for their efficiency and reliability, but conventional Si-based NPC
designs face switching loss limitations at higher frequencies [20-22]. The introduction of
SiC MOSFETs enables a hybrid active NPC (H-ANPC) architecture that leverages the
high-speed switching and low-loss characteristics of SiC devices while retaining cost-
effective Si IGBTs [23] [C5]. In this topology, high-frequency switching is assigned to the
SiC MOSFETs, while Si IGBTs operate at low frequency, enabling hybrid freewheeling to
reduce conduction losses [24]. This configuration, while cost-effective, imposes
concentrated thermal and electrical stress on the SiC devices, making them the dominant
lifetime-limiting components [25-27].
 This new inverter was used to shed further light on why conventional estimates of
lifetime are not supported by field data. Conventional power device lifetime assessment
relies on power cycling tests (PCT), where devices are repeatedly heated and cooled to
induce wear-out. Although well established, PCT typically applies thermal cycling under
controlled laboratory conditions, neglecting the combined electrical and thermal

 Page 7 of 61

conditions present in real inverters [27] [28]. As a result, PCT-based lifetime predictions
can significantly deviate from field performance. In contrast, converter-specific
accelerated testing methods have been proposed to replicate operational stress
environments more accurately [29] [30], while still neglecting the interactions with other
components in the converter. Building on these advances, the Inverter-like Accelerated
Test (IAT) methodology [31] developed in this work employs an operational H-ANPC
prototype as the stress platform, applying electrothermal stress representative of real-
world operation. By capturing realistic temperature fluctuations and coupled stress
interactions, IAT produces lifetime models that better reflect practical service conditions
than conventional PCT.
 Active thermal control (ATC) strategies, such as cooling‐based control [32], PWM‐
based thermal mitigation [33], and adaptive gate driver tuning [34], have been extensively
explored to mitigate thermo‐mechanical fatigue by reducing junction temperature swings.
While these approaches can be effective for thermal cycling mitigation, they generally
operate through adjustments to a single control variable and often apply such changes
uniformly across operating conditions. This can introduce trade‐offs in efficiency or output
quality, particularly in applications requiring high performance under dynamic load
profiles. Furthermore, many ATC techniques [35], [36] overlook other significant
degradation mechanisms, such as gate oxide wear‐out, which is known to be a dominant
lifetime‐limiting factor for SiC MOSFETs. To address these limitations, unlike traditional
offline prognostic models [37], this work introduced and experimentally validated a
methodology that acts on multiple degradation modes in a coordinated manner. This
approach integrates real‐time device condition insights with targeted operational
adjustments, enabling more comprehensive stress management and extending device
lifetime while maintaining converter performance.
 In conclusion, the new approaches developed in this study complement and build
on related studies of PV inverter reliability, but provide a more complete understanding of
the actual failure dynamics in PV inverters. They do so by integrating a combination of
top-down machine learning analysis of field data, a bottom-up PoF modeling of failure
mechanisms, and the development of test-bed SiC inverter and novel accelerated testing
protocols that better simulate actual field stresses.

 6. Project Objectives:
 Solar photovoltaics are a critical component of the US renewable energy portfolio.
As such, methodologies to predict the reliability of the string inverters that dominate the
conversion and distribution of solar energy, and an evaluation of approaches to enhance
the operating lifetime of those inverters are essential to overall power grid reliability. In
addition, improved PV inverter reliability facilitates the development of more affordable
and more reliable solar PV systems that increase the penetration of renewable energy
generation. The major goal of this project was to develop an integrated approach to
reliability that includes 1) an analysis of field environmental, degradation, and failure data;
2) the use of Physics of Failure (PoF) models for assessing degradation and failure of
components; and 3) the determination of the effect of different inverter operating
conditions on the stresses on the components. The related enabling goal was to build a

 Page 8 of 61

test-bed inverter and to create a digital twin for that test bed inverter. Both of these goals
occurred in the first budget period (i.e. the first two years). The third goal, accomplished
in budget period 2 (i.e the last two years), is to incorporate reliability assessment into the
digital twin and validate it using novel accelerated tests that are more representative of
actual PV inverter operational stresses.
 The first two goals have been accomplished through a series of tasks and
milestones which are detailed below. In task 1.1, field data was mined using data
analytics. Field failures were identified and classified into likely failure mechanisms and
failure site groups, and anomalies were detected with known failure mechanisms. In task
1.2, stress defining algorithms were developed to convert a range of environmental and
operational exposure conditions, including effects of grid dynamics, to electrical,
chemical, mechanical, and thermal stresses acting on critical components in the inverter.
In addition, PoF algorithms were identified that could be used to calculate the level of
degradation and the time to failure for critical components under these stresses for one
or more failure mechanisms. These algorithms were calibrated and validated for
capacitors and switching devices through experimentation. In task 1.3, a scaled-power,
test-bed inverter was created for monitoring system performance before and after aging,
along with a digital twin for this test-bed inverter. In task 1.4, a survey was developed
and informal discussions at conferences were conducted to gauge industry input to
establish the technical and commercial value of the technology. This satisfied the two
Go-No Go milestones for the first budget period which were the (GNG 1A) Prediction of
Test Bed Inverter Performance by Digital Twin Model and (GNG 1B) Level of Outreach.
The third goal was accomplished in the second budget period in two main tasks. In Task
2.5, the classification methods and machine learning approaches to field failure data
analytics were fully implemented. The digital twin was modified to use deep learning
techniques to 1) identify which failure mechanism(s) match best to the observed data; 2)
minimize the error in the prediction vs. the observed performance; and 3) use this
information to retrain the classification engine so it makes a better upfront selection of
likely failure mechanism and site when the same operational and environmental
conditions are encountered again (Milestone 2.5.1). In Task 2.6, stress models, and
Physics-of-Failure models for degraded performance or failure of the switching
components were incorporated into the digital twin to determine the level of degraded
performance or time to failure of the test-bed inverter that are related to the performance
of the inverter as a whole. (Milestone 2.6.1) This degraded performance was validated
using accelerated aging test results (Milestone 2.6.2) on the test bed inverter. Based on
the feedback collected, an approach was developed to transition the digital twin
technology to PV manufacturers. The end-of-project technical goal were thus met of both
the development of a digital twin model validated against field data and test-bed inverter
generated accelerated and operational testing data, and an approach for transferring the
digital twin technology to PV manufacturers. The full milestone chart is included as
Appendix A.

7. Project Results and Discussion:
 As mentioned above the major goal of this project was to develop an approach to PV
inverter reliability assessment and improvement that includes a top-down analysis of field
data, a bottom-up reliability calculation based on fundamental component degradation
mechanisms and a determination of the effect of different inverter operating conditions on
the stresses on the components. This was accomplished by achieving the milestones
detailed below.

TASK 1.1: Collect and analyze field test and operational data

Milestone 1.1.1: Historic performance and field failure data collected.
 Field data was collected for outdoor inverter failure analysis and classification.
After reviewing the data, we proposed a top-down approach (Fig. 1) to enhance reliability
assessment. The data included inverter electrical input/output, weather sensor readings,
alerts, alarms, and maintenance records from four Florida PV sites with various micro-
and string inverters from multiple vendors, plus power quality data at the common
coupling point of one site (Fig. 2).

In addition, performance and reliability data were collected from fielded Solectria inverters
to allow us to generate a ranking of degradation and failure events, across the thousands
of inverters on the company’s remote access network.

 Fig. 2: FIU’s data flow architecture and some datasets snapshots

 The primary causes of inverter failures/field problems were identified based on
Solectria’s records of component issues. Based on this data, key failure mechanisms
were grouped into three categories: 1) degradation of failure-prone components in the
inverter, which include contactors, cables and connectors, IGBTs, current sensors, film
and ceramic capacitors, fans, HV DC switches, and opto-devices; 2) installation mistakes,
including gasket leaks and improper sealing of the conduit allowing moisture to enter the
inverter, and loose connections due to improper torque; 3) firmware bugs, including
issues with control and communications software; and 4) grid events, such as lightning
strikes and voltage spikes. Based on this, failure-prone components were selected for
further investigation in modeling and experiments.

Milestone 1.1.2: Characterization, inference, and classification methods developed
and implemented.

 For this milestone, we developed data reduction and analysis techniques to
transform raw data from Milestone 1.1.1 into actionable failure information. We first
filtered out non-inverter alerts such as "Power Meter Check," "Weather Station Alert,"
"Gateway heartbeat," "Device heartbeat," and "Device communication." Power quality
data from the FIU PV plant’s PCC was analyzed using wavelet packet decomposition to
detect grid anomalies during mode changes by comparing healthy and stressed inverter
signatures (Fig. 3). Similar methods classified inverter anomalies linked to physical
degradation, using prior knowledge of performance changes caused by failures like
corrosion or attachment fatigue. A CART algorithm was applied to classify inverter DC
current and voltage dependability under varying conditions such as temperature,
irradiance, humidity, and precipitation, revealing useful correlations for data classification.

                                         Page 11 of 61

 Fig. 3: Classification on failed inverter alert, correlation and time dependency.

 The inverter-specific alerts and alarms dataset was analyzed for correlations with
weather and power data using Point-Biserial and Logistic Regression for power data
(voltage, frequency at PCC) and Pearson Correlation for weather data. This approach
enabled robust classification of anomalies in inverter electrical data and PCC power-
quality metrics. The One-Class Support Vector Machine (OCSVM) exhibited the strongest
boundary discrimination among the unsupervised models evaluated. As shown in Fig.
5(a), the OCSVM cleanly preserves the dense nominal operating manifold while
accurately isolating off-manifold behavior across the entire irradiance span (0–1000
W/m²). The detected anomalies include low-irradiance startup irregularities, mid-
irradiance off-band deviations, and high-irradiance stress responses, all of which
correspond to known inverter field behaviors. Quantitatively, the OCSVM achieved an
anomaly-detection recall in the range of 0.82–0.90, precision of 0.70–0.80, and a ROC–
AUC of 0.88–0.93, consistent with the visual separation between nominal (blue) and
anomalous (red) operating points. These results confirm that the OCSVM provides a well-
calibrated and sensitive representation of inverter operating health throughout daily
irradiance variation. In contrast, Isolation Forest and an LSTM-based time-series model
exhibited substantially weaker performance. Isolation Forest detected only ~10% of
anomalies overall (recall ≈ 0.10), with detections concentrated almost exclusively at the
highest and lowest irradiance conditions (Fig. 4(a), (b)). This selective detection is
quantitatively inadequate because inverter failures can occur at any irradiance level. The
LSTM model showed large validation errors (validation RMSE > 2.5 x training RMSE) and
unstable temporal predictions for the electrical time-series data (Fig. 4(c)).
 By mapping OCSVM-detected anomalies to field measurements and alarm logs,
we classified failure root causes at both the inverter-system level and major subsystem
level (e.g., semiconductor switch cooling degradation). Overall, the characterization,
inference, and classification framework developed here provides a quantitative link
between inverter failures and environmental as well as operational stressors, including
weather effects [8]

Milestone 1.1.3: Detection of Anomalies with Known Failure Mechanisms.

 In this step, we combined data-driven identification from Milestone 1.1.1, anomaly
classification from Milestone 1.1.2, and prior knowledge of physical degradation to
correlate detected anomalies with known failure mechanisms. Low AC power production
in some inverters under similar conditions was noted. Historical data from replaced failed
inverters, including flags and warnings, were analyzed to compare anomaly distributions
before and after failure, aiming to link causes with data outliers.
Earlier CART analysis showed inverter current strongly correlates with irradiance,
prompting investigation of alerts and fault codes related to stressors using Isolation Forest
and LSTM machine learning methods (Fig. 4(a), 4(b), 4(c) to detect anomalies in low-
performing inverters. Failure mechanisms start from anomalous data crossing thresholds,
triggering alerts and faults. Findings indicate alert frequency per inverter inversely relates
to health, with stress showing cyclical patterns over time. This motivated analysis of
irradiance impact on current anomalies and failure patterns. Isolation Forests identified
clustered anomalies in inverter input current but missed broader anomalous behavior
across the profile.

 One Class SVM was applied to identify logical anomaly points across the entire
data envelope (Fig. 5a). The time stamps of anomalies in inverter input and output current
showed strong correlation with inverter alerts, which represent internal stresses crossing
thresholds. The frequency and density of alerts relate directly to degradation from failure
mechanisms, making anomaly time stamps key indicators of failures. This method
produced “time-bound” and “inverter-specific” Anomaly-Alert pairs, tested on ten low-
performing inverters not previously replaced, confirming failure mechanism patterns. The
analysis narrowed fourteen inverter alerts down to two major alert sets linked to distinct
failure mechanisms. Manufacturer manuals and service records identified these as
“inverter interference alert” and “inverter insulation resistance” (Fig. 5b), associated with
high temperature and moisture ingress, respectively. All fault patterns within the inverter
were mapped for further component degradation studies.

 Building on the correlation between failure mechanisms and alerts, we extended
the analysis to estimate inverter lifetime. An inverter that stops producing power or is
unavailable is considered unreliable. By segregating alerts into critical and non-critical
types—where critical alerts occur less frequently but persist longer, causing prolonged
failure states, and non-critical alerts reset automatically over time—we modeled inverter
health using a four-state continuous Markov process (Fig. 6a). Using alerts from 46
inverters, we created a state transition matrix showing the “all healthy” state declining
exponentially to 20% reliability after about 20 years (Fig. 6b). Non-critical alerts are
frequent during early operation but decrease over time, while critical alerts steadily
increase, reflecting semi-permanent or permanent failures requiring service. Non-critical
alerts mostly stem from installation errors or temporary weather effects. This study offers
a system-level understanding of inverter reliability by integrating anomaly-alert data,
service histories, technical reports, and weather correlations.

 This correlation enables the use of scientifically derived degradation models to
assess the extent of degradation when an anomaly is detected, determine if the anomaly
is an early failure indicator, and estimate the system’s remaining life. The goal is to identify
root causes of anomalies well before the last 20% of system life. In parallel, a component

parts—IGBT, DC link capacitor, and ventilation fan—considered most vulnerable. Results

Temperature-Humidity-Bias Testing of Snubber Capacitors: In order to generate data for
the capacitor lifetime models, we developed a plan for a combined thermal, humidity, and
bias (THB) test. We acquired 20 capacitors from a vendor used by Solectria for their
snubber capacitors, which were placed in a combined temperature-humidity chamber at
85°C and 85% RH and subject to at DC bias of 750 V. These conditions were selected to
match the conditions used by Solectria in its own testing. The tests run by Solectria,
however, terminate at 1000 hours and do not provide detailed information about
degradation. This test ran until there was clear physical failure or until the capacitors could
no longer be charged.

Figure 24 shows the circuit diagram for the THB test rig. Here there is a power supply
which continuously provides 750 V, the capacitors in parallel, two 3 MΩ resistors, and a
normally open relay. The relay is placed on the door of the test chamber, and serves as
a safety interlock which disconnects the power supply from the circuit whenever the
chamber door is opened. The resistors serve to accommodate the current limitations of
the power supply. The power supply can provide a maximum current of 1 mA, which was
sufficient for this test because the capacitors become an open circuit once charged. In
the charging phase of operation, the circuit resistance is 1.5 MΩ. The right branch
becomes open once the capacitors fully charge, at which point current only flows through
R2 at 0.25 mA. When the power supply is turned off or the relay opens, the capacitor
bank discharges through the two resistors. With this design, the time constant is 41
seconds for charging and 82 seconds for discharging.

 The location and extent of cratering in the IGBTs that failed in power cycling tells a
great deal about the dominant failure mechanism. Cratering in the die is a catastrophic
failure, which, without further detail can result from one of three failure mechanisms. The
first to consider is dielectric breakdown from voltage overstress. Voltage overstress
craters typically have a small radius, are located on the active surface of the die, and
penetrate the depth of the die. Fig. 32 shows a classic example of failure from voltage
overstress [49]. This mechanism can be rejected because the craters seen in the study
have a wide radius and continue through the die attach.

 Figure 32: Die level view of typical over-voltage cratering [49]

 The second mechanism to consider is thermal runaway. Thermal runaway results from
resistance increase due to degradation of the interconnects. This can be seen from cracks
or porosity of the die attach or from heel cracking, lifting, or delamination of the emitter
wirebond. This mechanism can be rejected because images of the decapsulated devices
show no cracking or delamination of the emitter wires, and cross-section analysis shows
no degradation of the die attach.

 The third possible mechanism is overcurrent. Overcurrent craters are centered on the
active surface of the die near or underneath the emitter wirebond, and can burn through
the die and solder. This matches the damage seen in Fig. 31a and 31b. Furthermore, Fig.
M8c shows significant damage to the gate wirebond, and even intermittent failure of the

                                       Page 30 of 61

gate wirebond can lead to multiple events which precipitate overcurrent, such as shoot-
through. This is the concluded failure mechanism.

Milestone 1.3.2: Prediction of test-bed inverter performance by digital-twin model
 A digital twin of the H-ANPC inverter was developed to enable real-time monitoring
of device health, supported by a Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), based parameter
estimation routine [50][51]. The model captures the inverter’s behavior in different
switching states, with key parameters being the on-state voltages of the SiC MOSFETs
(VDS-ON ) and Si IGBTs (VCE-ON), which directly relate to device degradation.
 Since direct measurement of these voltages during operation is challenging in a
high-voltage, noisy environment [52] [53], PSO was employed to iteratively tune the digital
twin parameters until the predicted inductor current and output voltage matched
measured values from the physical inverter. The method was first validated offline using
oscilloscope-acquired waveforms, where PSO accurately tracked the increase in on-state
voltage with load current, and Figure 36(a) shows a representative case. The algorithm
was then implemented in real time on the inverter’s DSP, running in the second core of
the controller to provide continuous parameter updates without interfering with converter
operation. Figure 36(b) compares these real-time estimates with high-resolution
oscilloscope measurements, confirming the accuracy of the online approach.
 This parameter estimation framework enables continuous tracking of device
degradation and supports integration with lifetime consumption models derived from
accelerated testing. Combining observed degradation trends with predictive modeling
provides a foundation for implementing reliability enhancement strategies.

TASK 1.4: Establish technical and commercial value of technology
Milestone 1.4.1: Complete industry survey for impact and value of work.
Survey questions were developed to determine the impact and value of this work to the
PV industry and inverter manufacturers in particular. These included the following:

 1) What does “digital twin” mean to you?
 2) What features would you like to see in a digital twin (e.g. anomaly detection,
 predictive modeling)?
 3) How would your company use it (e.g. design, condition-based maintenance)?
 4) What ROI do you think you could gain from predictive modeling and how much are
 you willing to pay for this feature?

Conversations were then held with key players in the solar inverter community at the
NREL PV Reliability Workshop and the 2024 PV Inverter Reliability Workshop in Denver,
CO. While the number of individuals surveyed was not sufficient for a formal collection
and anonymization of survey results, some key findings on the direction and value of the
effort were identified. These included the following:

1) A digital twin should provide a full simulation of the actual physical system and
should be able to mimic the performance of the actual system under different operational
and environmental conditions.
2) The key feature that was required was anomaly detection. There was less interest
in having it mimic performance of a degraded physical system, as long as it could detect
via performance differences that the physical system had degraded and needed repair.
3) Manufacturing companies would be interested in using it to detect manufacturing
defects, while utilities would be more interested in having identify the need for repair of
fielded systems.

                                                                              Page 34 of 61

4) The ROI was hard to determine as the system would be used to identify and repair
problems before the system failed. The repair costs were known but the avoided costs
of loss of the system were less easy to quantify. Also, since the utility and not the
manufacturer would reap the savings from fewer field failures, the amount the
manufacturers would pay to create the digital twin would depend upon the added value it
provided their customer (the utility), which would require customer discovery activities on
the part of the manufacturers.

This information was then used to focus the development of the tool in budget period 2
toward anomaly detection and remaining life prediction in a known physical system and
in developing an approach to transition the digital twin technology for production and
marketing at PV manufacturers.

Milestone 2.5.1: Incorporation of (field sensor, and inverter data driven) deep
learning elements as part of the digital twin.

 The identified failure mechanisms were combined with field sensor data, failure
records, and location-specific weather data to develop deep learning models capable of
forecasting failure likelihoods and pinpointing potential stress points. These forecasts rely
on known failure curves. The models use feature vectors including inverter age, weather
variables, failure alerts, input/output currents, grid PCC data, and inverter output power.
Model outputs are finely tuned, with learning ensured through established machine
learning training processes.

ML-enhanced Optimization Model for Inverter Filter Component Digital Twin:

 Fig. 37: Inverter prototype parameter extraction in Digital Model by Particle Swarm
 Optimization.

 A machine learning approach to enhance PV inverter reliability assessment by
predicting thermal losses caused by weather-dependent parasitic resistances in inverter
filter components was developed (Fig. 37). Environmental factors like temperature and
humidity affect inverter performance, causing gradual degradation—primarily in the
equivalent series resistance of capacitors and inductors, with significant capacitance loss
over time. To tackle this, a hybrid method combining hardware testing, digital modeling,
and optimization was developed to extract key parameters and monitor component
degradation efficiently. A major contribution is a machine learning model that estimates
thermal losses and predicts filter degradation. Using a degraded inverter dataset, various

                                         Page 36 of 61

models were trained, with random forests showing strong performance in degradation
assessment, offering a reliable tool for predictive maintenance and fault detection. To
validate this approach, an H-Bridge inverter prototype was designed and tested using
MATLAB/Simulink switching models. Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) was employed
to iteratively extract and refine inverter filter parameters (L, C, rL, rC) by minimizing a
predefined cost function (Fig. 37), ensuring accurate degradation modeling for real-time
monitoring and proactive maintenance. The PSO-based digital model continuously
adapts to changing environmental conditions. Parameter variation plots (Fig. 38) show
iterative improvement, while performance alignment plots (Fig. 39) confirm close
agreement between PSO-optimized outputs and experimental waveforms, validating the
model’s accuracy for real-time monitoring and lifetime prediction.

TASK 2.6: Validation of Digital Twin Using Accelerated Testing
 In this task, the reliability‐enhancement methodology for SiC MOSFETs in H‐
ANPC inverters was experimentally validated to meet the project objectives. The work
built on earlier efforts in device health estimation, accelerated testing, and lifetime
modeling, integrating them into a practical strategy aimed at reducing dominant stress
mechanisms and slowing degradation in real operating environments. The approach was
evaluated under realistic electrothermal loading using the IAT platform, ensuring that
stress interactions representative of field conditions were captured during testing. Multiple
operating scenarios were executed to assess performance, reliability, and consistency of
the method, with results confirming its ability to maintain device stress within desired limits
while preserving inverter functionality. The methodology demonstrated transferability to
other high‐power converter applications where extending component lifetime is critical.
Milestone 2.6.1: Accelerated testing to validate inverter (i.e. H-ANPC inverter)
degradation under electrical operating stresses
 The IAT was performed on SiC MOSFETs in the H-ANPC inverter and
benchmarked against conventional PCT [31]. In the IAT setup, a single phase of the H-
ANPC prototype was operated continuously under high-stress conditions to accelerate
aging. Figure 44(a) shows the hardware setup for IAT. Unlike PCT, the IAT subjects the
devices to combined voltage switching stress and thermal cycling representative of real
inverter operation. Real-time measurements included VDS-ON for SiC MOSFET and VCE-
ON for Si IGBT during switching, and case temperatures, enabling continuous tracking of
conduction and switching degradation.
 On-state voltage tracking revealed that IAT-aged SiC MOSFETs exhibited more
moderate parametric shifts than those aged under PCT. After equivalent aging, the IAT
devices retained full blocking voltage, showed no premature current saturation, and
maintained robust output characteristics. Threshold voltage shift was comparable,
indicating gate oxide stress, but without the severe package-related degradation (bond
wire lift-off, solder fatigue) observed in PCT, which caused large RDS-ON increases and
breakdown voltage collapse.
 Using IAT data, Bayerer’s lifetime model (1) was fitted for the SiC MOSFET, and
mission profile lifetime predictions were performed for a Seattle-like climate. Figure 44(b)
compares the unreliability functions from IAT- and PCT-derived models. In both climates,
the IAT model predicted a ~32% shorter B10 life than the PCT model, indicating that PCT
can significantly overestimate field lifetime. Since IAT replicates operational stress
interactions more accurately, its derived models provide a more realistic basis for
converter reliability evaluation, reducing the risk of overestimating lifetime in design.

Milestone 2.6.3: Completion of post-test inverter including digital twin test-bed
inverter improvement
Based on the digital twin, the inverter was improved through the development of an active
gate voltage control (AGVC). The AGVC scheme (AGVCS) begins with the H-ANPC
inverter operating at its default gate voltage. Upon detecting a peak load current (IL,Peak),
the multiplexer switches from input 1 to input k, applying the selected gate voltage (Vgi)
to the SiC MOSFETs. Key parameters such as the junction temperature swing (ΔTj),
mean junction temperature (Tj,mean), peak load current (IL,Peak), and DC bus voltage (VDC)
are monitored and used to evaluate the MOSFET’s lifetime using the IAT model.

Simultaneously, the TDDB model is employed to project the lifetime based on Vgi, ΔTj,
and Tj,mean. The maximum lifetime value LMAXi derived from these models is stored for
each gate voltage setting. This process is repeated for all predetermined gate voltages,
resulting in an array of lifetime values.

The optimal gate voltage (Vgo) is then determined by selecting the gate voltage
corresponding to the maximum lifetime value in the array. The system continuously
adjusts this optimal gate voltage as operational conditions, such as peak load current,
change.

                                        Page 44 of 61

The Vgi are sourced from the SiC MOSFET’s datasheet, with potential values ranging
from the minimum to the maximum gate voltages (e.g., 15V to 24V). The control scheme
evaluates all possible combinations of gate voltages, including values like 15V, 20V, 24V,
and intermediate ranges (15V to 24V), to find the optimal setting for different load
conditions. Once the optimal gate voltage is identified, it is applied to the H-ANPC
inverter's experimental hardware to ensure optimal performance across various scenarios
as shown in Figure 49.

 Fig. 49: Control flowchart for SiC MOSFET lifetime optimization.

This control scheme offers a sophisticated approach to balancing the conflicting results
of TDDB and IAT tests. By dynamically adjusting the gate voltage according to the
inverter’s operational conditions, the scheme optimizes the lifetime of SiC MOSFETs in
the H-ANPC inverter. This control strategy enhances system performance under diverse
usage scenarios, ensuring robust and reliable operation over an extended period.
Accelerated aging experiments using the IAT platform have been completed to compare
operation with the AGVCS against fixed gate-voltage operation under accelerated stress.
Four representative test conditions, summarized in Figure 50, show that for every case
the SiC MOSFET in the H-ANPC inverter operated with AGVCS reaches the 15%
increase in RDS_ON degradation criterion at significantly higher cycle counts than the
corresponding fixed-gate-voltage tests, indicating a clear extension of device lifetime
under realistic inverter stress. The IAT platform thus provides an effective, application-
relevant environment for evaluating the control strategy, and the results demonstrate that
targeted gate-voltage modulation is a practical means to enhance converter reliability.
These findings confirm that the implemented gate-voltage optimization strategy is
effective in practice and that final validation of the concept under IAT conditions for the
H-ANPC inverter has been successfully completed.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
 Under this award we delivered the first end-to-end, data-driven reliability framework
for outdoor PV inverters—spanning historic field-data aggregation, anomaly
classification, mechanism identification, stochastic lifetime modeling, and digital-twin
calibration. Key accomplishments include:
 • Comprehensive Field Data Assembly: Collected multi-site inverter electrical,
 environmental, alert, and maintenance records, enabling a top-down reliability
 perspective rather than piecemeal accelerated tests.
 • Robust Anomaly Classification: Developed filtering, wavelet-based power-
 quality analysis, CART, Isolation Forest, LSTM, and one-class SVM methods to
 link alerts and sensor anomalies to temperature- and humidity-driven failure
 modes—achieving inverter-specific “anomaly–alert” pairs that directly reflect
 degradation.
 • Stochastic Lifetime Projection: Formulated a four-state continuous-time Markov
 model from six months of alert data over 46 inverters, projecting a 20 percent
 survival probability at 20 years. This quantitatively establishes alert-frequency
 thresholds for preventive service.
 • Deep-Learning Weather Model & Dual-Stage ML Pipeline: Developed LSTM-
 based humidity forecasting and a two-stage classification/regression chain (ANN
 classification followed by RF/KNN regression) on six months of alert-ambient
 data, achieving sub-percent MSE (down to 0.002) for real-time power-availability
 predictions.
 • Digital Twin Model Development: Constructed and PSO-calibrated a circuit-level
 digital twin of the inverter’s filter components (L, C, rL, rC) using H-bridge prototype
 data. By generating synthetic degradation profiles in Simulink, we trained Random
 Forest regressors that deliver R² ≈ 0.9998 and RMSE ≈ 4.2 × 10⁻⁶ for parasitic
 resistance estimation—enabling noninvasive, on-the-fly health monitoring.
 • Innovative reliability testing: Developed the IAT to replicate realistic stresses for
 SiC MOSFETs in an H-ANPC inverter, showing that conventional PCT can
 overestimate lifetime. IAT enables more representative lifetime modeling for
 converter design.
 • H-ANPC prototype and testbed: Built and validated a scaled hybrid Si/SiC H-
 ANPC inverter as an instrumented platform for accelerated aging experiments and
 control validation.
 • Digital twin and health monitoring: Implemented a digital twin with online
 estimation of degradation indicators, eliminating the need for extra sensors while
 maintaining accuracy through validated electrothermal models.
 • Reliability Enhancement: Developed and validated a lifetime‐extension strategy
 for SiC MOSFETs in H‐ANPC inverters, demonstrating stress reduction and
 lifetime improvement through inverter‐like accelerated testing. Final validation
 under IAT has been completed.
 • Degradation mechanism insights: Post-test analysis indicated that PCT and IAT
 can trigger different dominant failure mechanisms, highlighting the importance of
 targeting relevant stresses for accelerated tests.

                                         Page 47 of 61

 • Conducting failure analysis and modeling: Conducted failure analysis of failed
 samples from field use and accelerated testing, and developed models for failure
 of the components by the identified fundamental degradation mechanisms.
Challenges & Lessons Learned:
  • Data Integration: Harmonizing heterogeneous alert formats and sensor streams
 required extensive normalization and manual labeling, underscoring the need for
 standardized inverter-data schemas.
  • Model Generalization: Early deep-learning models (LSTM, SPRT) overfit sparse
 alert classes; shifting to one-class SVM and ensemble methods improved
 robustness.
  • Scope Refinement: Initial ambitions to parallelize dozens of ML methods led to
 complexity; focusing on the highest-impact techniques (SVM for anomaly
 detection, Markov for lifetime, RF for regression, and PSO-tuned DT) streamlined
 development without compromising performance.
• Challenges of Stress Testing for Capacitors: Capacitance was virtually unchanged
  by testing indicating that steady bias is not sufficient to cause full corrosion. The main
  effect of stress testing in humidity was on ESR.
• Effect of Humidity on Life Cycle Testing in Power Devices: The lifetime reduction
 caused by humidity was found to vary based on electrical/thermal stress. This
 complication can be mitigated by putting the devices in the chamber unpowered for
 long enough to allow complete moisture ingress prior to any cycling.
In sum, we established a nondestructive, generalized methodology in this project that (1)
fuses rich field data with advanced ML for proactive reliability forecasting, (2) dramatically
reduces experimental iterations via synthetic dataset generation, and (3) achieves
unprecedented regression precision in both anomaly detection and component-level
degradation assessment—paving the way for truly predictive maintenance of grid-tied PV
inverters under diverse outdoor conditions.
