The impact of intensive trauma-focused treatment on adults with PTSD and ASD traits: a pre-post intervention study
Authors: Suze J Te Hennepe, Eline M Voorendonk, Ad de Jongh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) often co-occurs with elevated autism spectrum disorder (ASD) traits, potentially affecting treatment response. Empirical data on the impact of ASD traits on the effectiveness of trauma-focused therapy remain scarce.Objective: To investigate the treatment response following intensive trauma-focused therapy in patients with PTSD, comparing outcomes between individuals with high versus low comorbid ASD traits.Methods: 175 patients with PTSD underwent an intensive trauma-focused treatment programme that integrated prolonged exposure, EMDR therapy, psychoeducation and physical activities. Participants were categorised into a high ASD trait group (n = 70) and a low ASD trait group (n = 105) based on scores on the AQ-50. PTSD symptoms were measured pre- and post-treatment using the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 (CAPS-5). A mixed model repeated measures ANOVA was used to analyze the interaction between time and group on CAPS-5 scores.Results: Both groups showed significant reduction in PTSD symptoms following treatment, with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 2.89 in the low ASD group and 3.07 in the high ASD group). Overall, 83% of patients lost their PTSD diagnosis post-treatment. A significant decrease in AQ-50 scores was observed in the high ASD traits group with a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = .52), while 31% of these patients shifted to the low ASD traits group after treatment. Reliable clinical improvement in PTSD symptoms was observed in 93% of patients with high ASD traits and 90% of those with low ASD traits. No adverse events or treatment dropouts occurred.Conclusions: Intensive trauma-focused treatment was found effective and safe for patients with PTSD, including those with elevated ASD traits. The results suggest that presence of high ASD traits does not preclude substantial treatment gains or loss of diagnosis, and underscore the relevance of inclusive, tailored trauma-focused therapy approaches for neurodiverse populations.KEYWORDS: PTSD, autism spectrum disorder, intensive trauma-focused treatment, EMDR, exposure therapy

Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is prevalent cross-nationally, with 3.9% in the total sample and 5.6% among trauma-exposed individuals (Koenen et al., 2017). PTSD is characterised by the symptomology of intrusive re-experience, avoidance, hyperarousal, and marked negative changes in mood and cognition after exposure to traumatic events, defined as exposure to actual or threatened death, severe injury, or sexual violence (American Psychiatric Association (APA) 2013).

Individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) have been found to be at elevated risk of developing PTSD (Li et al., 2024; Rumball et al., 2020). ASD is a neurodevelopmental condition characterised by impairments in reciprocal social interaction and social communication across multiple settings, as well as restricted and repetitive behaviours and interests (APA, 2013). The worldwide population prevalence of individuals diagnosed with ASD is estimated at 0.6% with variation between regions, such as 1% in America and 0.5% in Europe (Salari et al., 2022). The percentage of individuals with PTSD and comorbid ASD remains unclear, and the treatment options for this specific group of patients remain unexplored.

While prevalence research on ASD in PTSD is lacking, few studies explored PTSD susceptibility in ASD patients. A study comparing exposure to potentially traumatic life events found higher rates of probable PTSD in adults with ASD (32%) compared to those without ASD (4%; Haruvi-Lamdan et al., 2020). Individuals with ASD reported more PTSD symptoms, particularly re-experiencing and hyperarousal, than adults without ASD did. Moreover, a recent cohort study examining the risk of acute stress disorder and PTSD in individuals with ASD established that youth with ASD have a more than 20-fold increased risk of developing acute stress disorder and PTSD (Li et al., 2024). Furthermore, adults with ASD experience a broad spectrum of traumatic life events, with over 40% exhibiting probable PTSD in the past month, and over 60% reporting probable PTSD at some point in their lives (Rumball et al., 2020). Hence, individuals with ASD seem vulnerable to the development of PTSD.

In line with the lack of prevalence studies, research on the treatment of PTSD and ASD symptoms in individuals with PTSD and comorbid ASD is limited. Preliminary findings from some case reports suggest that a trauma-focused treatment approach (i.e. eye movement desensitization and reprocessing [EMDR] therapy) for PTSD is feasible and potentially effective for children and adults with ASD (Barol & Seubert, 2010; Mevissen et al., 2011). A significant reduction in PTSD symptoms, psychological distress, and autistic features in adults with ASD was found in a controlled study that described the feasibility and efficacy of EMDR therapy for trauma-related symptoms in adults with ASD (Lobregt-van Buuren et al., 2019). A possible explanation for the observed reduction of ASD traits is that symptoms ascribed to phenotypic features of ASD may in fact be symptoms of PTSD, a phenomenon termed ‘diagnostic overshadowing’. For example, hyperarousal as a consequence of exposure to trauma may be interpreted as an autistic feature similar to hyperreactivity to sensory stimuli. There may also be shared underlying mechanisms for PTSD and ASD, including neurological abnormalities associated with both disorders, as well as cognitive and behavioural mechanisms, such as increased rumination, cognitive rigidity, avoidance, anger, and aggression (Haruvi-Lamdan et al., 2018). Taken together, the findings from these limited investigations highlight the importance of developing more knowledge of trauma-focused treatment for adults with PTSD and comorbid ASD traits, particularly because an effect on ASD traits was also observed.

Concerning the treatment of PTSD, EMDR therapy and trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy are preferred methods (U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense, 2023). Mounting evidence suggests that intensive trauma-focused treatment that includes prolonged exposure (PE), EMDR therapy, psychoeducation, and physical activity is effective for patients with PTSD and Complex PTSD (Van Woudenberg et al., 2018; Voorendonk et al., 2023). While previous research has demonstrated the effectiveness of low-intensity EMDR therapy for individuals with PTSD and comorbid ASD traits, these promising findings require replication across different treatment modalities and contexts to establish their robustness and generalizability. The observation that trauma-focused treatment appears effective for this subgroup, including effects on both PTSD and ASD symptoms, remains insufficiently established across diverse therapeutic approaches and therefore warrants further investigation in different treatment settings. Knowledge about the possible effects of intensive trauma-focused treatment on ASD traits would also contribute to the transdiagnostic approach to ASD to achieve effective and efficient treatment for adults with PTSD and comorbid ASD.

The current study aims to explore the safety, feasibility, and effects of an intensive trauma-focused treatment programme, lasting four days, for people with PTSD and comorbid ASD traits. We hypothesised that the severity of PTSD symptoms would be significantly reduced from pre- to post-treatment, both in individuals with low and high ASD traits (indexed with the Autism-Spectrum Quotient [AQ-50]). Second, we predicted that ASD traits of individuals with PTSD and high ASD traits would decrease significantly from pre- to post-treatment. Finally, we tested the hypothesis that an intensive trauma-focused treatment programme would be safe in terms of dropout and negative reactions to the intervention in individuals with PTSD and high ASD traits.

Methods

Participants

This pre-post study included participants from PSYTREC, a specialised intensive trauma-focused PTSD treatment centre. Participants underwent a hybrid four-day (two days onsite, two online) programme, with no additional treatments except stable pharmacotherapy. Inclusion criteria comprised being at least 18 years old, proficient in Dutch and diagnosed with PTSD according to the DSM-5 (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2013). Treatment occurred between May and November 2023. Measurements were provided as part of routine treatment outcome measurements; no (financial) compensation was offered. Ethical exemption was granted by the Medical Ethics Review Committee of the VU Medical Centre (registered with the US Office for Human Research Protections (OHRP) as IRB00002991, FWA number FWA00017598).

Procedure

The inclusion criteria were verified during the diagnostic phase. Traumatic experiences (LEC-5), PTSD severity (CAPS-5), and psychiatric comorbidities (MINI-S) were assessed by a clinician. The patients were invited to undergo treatment if they met the inclusion criteria. Informed consent was obtained for inclusion in this study. ASD traits (AQ-50) and PTSD symptoms (PCL-5) were measured between the two intake sessions using a self-report questionnaire. After a baseline phase that coincided with the waiting time to start treatment, ranging from 2 to 17 weeks, with an average of 7.5 weeks, patients started the treatment. Two weeks after the last treatment day, patients were invited to undergo an online post-treatment assessment of PTSD symptoms (CAPS-5). ASD traits (AQ-50) and PTSD symptoms were also measured two (PCL-5) weeks after the last treatment day using a self-report questionnaire.

Treatment

All participants received a four-day hybrid treatment over a period of one week. The first two days took place at the clinic location and provided face-to-face treatment. The following two days took place remotely through online therapy. The treatment consisted of a combination of two daily individual prolonged exposure (PE) sessions and two eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR) therapy sessions (60 min each). These treatments were highly protocolized and provided by therapists with a master’s degree in clinical psychology who were trained in PE and EMDR therapy. Therapist rotation was used (Van Minnen et al., 2018). In addition, participants engaged in two daily sessions (60 min each) of physical activity and received brief group psychoeducation in the morning and evening. Treatment dropout was defined as discontinuation of therapy before completing all scheduled sessions, regardless of the reason.

The modified protocol used for PE (Foa et al., 2007) consisted of a structured combination of imaginal exposure and in vivo exposure. Patients were instructed to describe their traumatic memories as vividly and in as much detail as possible, out loud, in the present tense. In vivo materials, such as sound fragments, pictures, or video clips, were added during imaginal exposure to test specific harm expectancies or maximise fear activation.

For EMDR therapy the standard protocol was used (De Jongh & Ten Broeke, 2019; Shapiro, 2018). The version of EMDR therapy used was EMDR 2.0 (see Matthijssen et al., 2021). This therapy variant uses the same (standard EMDR) protocol but utilises a higher dose with regard to the working memory load than regular EMDR therapy. Treatment-interfering anticipatory fear was addressed using the flashforward protocol (Logie & De Jongh, 2014).

After the trauma-focused PE and EMDR sessions, patients participated twice a day in a physical activity programme for one hour. The physical activity programme included psychomotor therapy and exercise with low- and high-intensity activities such as mountain biking, boxing, hiking, and yoga. Psychoeducation consisted of digital information about PTSD topics and completing assignments in a digital personal workbook.

Assessment instruments

The demographic characteristics of the treatment sample were gathered prior to the start of treatment using self-report demographic questionnaires at the treatment centre.

The Dutch translation of the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ-50) has been used to assess ASD traits (Hoekstra et al., 2008). The AQ-50 is a brief, self-administered instrument for measuring the degree to which an adult with normal intelligence has traits associated with the autistic spectrum (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001). This questionnaire contains 50 questions related to social skills, attention switching, attention to detail, communication, and imagination. Each item has four possible responses (‘definitely agree’, ‘slightly agree’, ‘slightly disagree’, ‘definitely disagree’). Respondents are instructed to select one response per item. Items are scored dichotomously (i.e. collapsing ‘definitely agree’ and ‘slightly agree’ into ‘agree’ and similarly for ‘disagree’), with one point being assigned for each response characteristic of ASD (score = 1 if agree [definitely and slightly] and score = 0 if disagree [slightly and definitely]). The total AQ-50 scores range from 0 to 50, with higher scores indicating more autistic traits. In this study, a cut-off point of 26 was used to minimise false-negative results (Woodbury-Smith et al., 2005). The AQ-50 appears to be a reasonable predictor of who will receive an ASD diagnosis in clinical practice (Woodbury-Smith et al., 2005). Therefore, this instrument appears to be suitable in clinical practice for screening (Woodbury-Smith et al., 2005) and research purposes (Hurst et al., 2007). In the current sample, internal consistency of the AQ-50 was good at both pre-treatment and post-treatment (α = .84).

The Clinician-Administered PTSD Symptom Scale for DSM-5 (CAPS-5; Weathers et al., 2018; Dutch version; Boeschoten et al., 2018) was used to assess PTSD diagnosis and symptom severity. The CAPS-5 is a widely used clinician-administered structured interview to assess PTSD symptoms, according to the classification system of the DSM-5. This is considered to be the gold standard for assessing PTSD (Weathers et al., 2018). The CAPS-5 symptom items have a 5-point severity scale (0 = absent, 1 = mild/subthreshold, 2 = moderate/threshold, 3 = severe, 4 = extreme), with a total score ranging from 0 to 80. The SEV2 rule, in combination with the DSM-5 algorithm, was used to diagnose PTSD (Boeschoten et al., 2018; Weathers et al., 2013). The Dutch version of the CAPS-5 has been found to be a valid and reliable measure with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.90 and a high inter-rater reliability of 0.98 (Boeschoten et al., 2018). In the current sample, internal consistency of the CAPS-5 was acceptable at pre-treatment (α = .69) and high at post-treatment (α = .92).

The PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) was used as a self-report measure of PTSD symptom severity. The PCL-5 consists of 20 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = not at all to 4 = extremely), with total scores ranging from 0 to 80. Higher scores indicate greater PTSD symptom severity. In the current sample, internal consistency of the PCL-5 was high at pre-treatment (α = .88) and very high at post-treatment (α = .97).

The Life Event Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5) (Weathers et al., 2013) was used to assess traumatic exposure. This 17-item self-report measure assesses the number and type of traumatic events experienced by a person during their lifetime. These items encompass several possible traumatic events (e.g. natural disasters, physical violence, sexual assault, exposure to toxic substances, and combat). In the present study, the Dutch translation of the LEC-5 was used (Boeschoten et al., 2014). The original LEC has been found to be a reliable measure of traumatic events (Gray et al., 2004).

For the assessment of comorbid psychiatric disorders and suicide risks, the Dutch version of the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview Screen (MINI-S; Overbeek & Schruers, 2019) was administered. The MINI-S is a brief, structured diagnostic interview based on the MINI for DSM-5, designed for rapid yet reliable screening of the most prevalent psychiatric disorders. Research supports the MINI and its short forms as instruments with satisfactory psychometric properties, including good reliability and validity in both clinical and research contexts (Sheehan et al., 1998; Van Vliet & De Beurs, 2007).

Statistical analysis

Assuming a moderate effect size of trauma-focused treatment on PTSD symptoms, an a priori power calculation showed that a minimum sample size of 98 participants was required to detect a significant difference in our primary analyses (G*Power 3.1.9.4; ANOVA, repeated measures, between factors with two groups and two measurement points, α = .05, f = .25, power = .80). Considering the possibility of dropout, technical measurement failure and missing data, we aimed at a total of 120 participants. Participants were divided into two groups: one group consisted of patients who scored low on autistic traits according to the AQ-50 at pre-treatment (low ASD traits) and the other who scored high on autistic traits according to the AQ-50 (high ASD traits) at pre-treatment. A cut-off value of 26 was used to classify and divide the patients into two groups (Woodbury-Smith et al., 2005). Sensitivity analyses compared baseline characteristics of the attrition group (n = 107; missing post-treatment data) with those of the included group (N = 175), focusing on scores on CAPS-5 and AQ-50, gender, type of trauma and age.

A mixed model repeated measures ANOVA was conducted to test the presence of an interaction effect between time (pre- to post-treatment) and group (low vs. high ASD traits) on the CAPS-5 and PCL-5 scores. Moreover, an additional sensitivity analysis including all participants with available CAPS-5 data (n = 273) was conducted using the same mixed model repeated measures ANOVA with time and group as factors. A paired t-test was performed to compare the pre- and post-treatment AQ-50 scores of the patients with high ASD traits. For each test, the level of significance for all statistical analyses was set at α = 0.05. A reliable change index (RCI) was calculated for the CAPS-5 based on a test-retest reliability of 0.73 and the pre-treatment standard deviation; symptom improvement was defined as a CAPS-5 decrease of ≥10.06 points. Chi-square tests were used to examine group differences in loss of diagnosis and in reliable improvement rates between the high and low ASD trait groups. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.

Loss of PTSD diagnosis

Overall, 146 patients (83%) lost their PTSD diagnosis post-treatment based on the CAPS-5. Specifically, 91 patients (87%) in the low ASD trait group and 55 patients (79%) in the high ASD trait group no longer met the diagnostic criteria for PTSD after treatment. There was no statistically significant difference between the low and high ASD trait groups in the proportion of patients who lost their PTSD diagnosis after treatment (χ²(1) = 1.99, p = .158).

Change in AQ-50 scores in the high ASD trait group (n = 70)

A significant decrease from pre- to post-treatment was found in the AQ-50 scores of the patients with high ASD traits (t(69) = 2.98, p = .004). As many as 31% of the patients who were in the high ASD trait group before treatment (n = 22) could be classified in the low ASD trait group after treatment.

Patient safety and adverse events

Therapists and staff continuously monitored the occurrence of adverse events and attrition throughout the study period. No adverse events were recorded during the study period, and no participants dropped out during treatment. Additionally, none of the patients, including those with high ASD traits, exhibited any clinically reliable worsening of their PTSD symptoms. Of the 70 patients with high ASD traits, 65 (92.9%) demonstrated a reliable improvement in CAPS-5 scores and 5 (7.1%) exhibited no change. In the group of patients with low ASD traits, 94 patients (89.5%) showed reliable improvement and 11 (10.5%) showed no change. There was no significant difference in reliable improvement rates between patients with high ASD traits and those with low ASD traits, χ²(1) = 0.56, p = .429.

Discussion

This study examined the safety, feasibility, and effectiveness of intensive trauma-focused treatment for individuals with PTSD and varying levels of ASD traits. The findings demonstrate that the therapy programme was associated with large and statistically significant reductions in PTSD symptoms across both groups. Importantly, 83% of all participants no longer met criteria for PTSD post-treatment, with similarly high loss of diagnosis rates in both the low (87%) and high (79%) ASD trait groups. In addition, nearly one-third of individuals initially classified with high ASD traits transitioned to the low-trait range after treatment. The intervention was well tolerated: there were no treatment dropouts, no adverse events, and no reliable symptom worsening during the study period.

The absence of a significant interaction between time and ASD traits on PTSD symptom reduction suggests that ASD traits do not reduce the effectiveness of trauma-focused treatment. This is a critical finding as it indicates that individuals with high ASD traits can benefit from the same therapeutic methods generally used for PTSD. Consequently, clinicians may not need to significantly modify core trauma-focused therapies when treating patients with both PTSD and ASD traits, thereby simplifying treatment planning and execution in clinical settings. Furthermore, the significant reduction in AQ-50 scores among those with high ASD traits is a notable finding, suggesting that trauma-focused therapy may also reduce ASD symptoms, at least as reported by individuals themselves. The fact that 31% of individuals in the high ASD trait group transitioned to the low ASD trait group post-treatment raises the possibility that trauma-focused therapy could address certain ASD-related challenges, such as social withdrawal and rigidity, which might be worsened by PTSD. This potential dual benefit emphasises the adaptability of trauma-focused interventions and warrants further investigation of their broader therapeutic effects. Most importantly, the results supported the safety of the treatment, which is especially reassuring for clinicians concerned about the intensity of such programmes, particularly for patients with complex conditions such as comorbid PTSD and ASD traits.

To this end, our findings are consistent with those of earlier studies by Lobregt-van Buuren et al. (2019) and Leuning et al. (2023), both of which reported a reduction in ASD symptom severity. Lobregt-van Buuren et al. observed significant effects on the total self-report scores of the Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS) in adults with ASD and PTSD, while Leuning’s study found no decrease in self-reported ASD symptoms, but did observe a reduction in caregiver-reported ASD symptoms on the SRS-A from baseline to follow-up. Additionally, Leuning’s study showed a significant reduction in self-reported stress on the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) and found that 52% of adolescents had significant improvements in global clinical functioning, as rated by an independent child psychiatrist using the Clinical Global Impression Improvement scale. A potential explanation for these findings could be that the clinical presentation of autistic symptoms diminishes when individuals with ASD experience reduced trauma-related stress and psychological distress such as somatisation, depression, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms. It is plausible that psychosocial factors, including exposure to adverse events and trauma, exacerbate the expression of core autistic traits. This explanation partially aligns with previous studies (Berg et al., 2016; Brenner et al., 2018; Roberts et al., 2015).

An important strength of the present study is that all patients received an identical treatment protocol consisting of two fully standardised, evidence-based trauma-focused therapies. Every patient was administered the same dose, both in terms of the number of therapy sessions and their duration. Second, the heterogeneous sample of patients with PTSD was reflected by their exposure to a wide variety of multiple traumas and high comorbidity rates, which optimises the generalisation of our findings. Third, treatment dropout was remarkably low (0%) and adherence to treatment protocols was strong. A further strength is that treatment effects were consistently observed across both clinician-rated (CAPS-5) and self-reported (PCL-5) PTSD outcomes, thereby supporting the robustness of the findings across different assessment methods. The study also has some limitations. First, although the present study included a comparison between individuals with low and high ASD trait levels, it used an uncontrolled pre-post design without a non-treatment or alternative treatment control group. This limits causal inferences regarding the observed treatment effects. Second, no long-term follow-up assessments were performed. Previous studies investigating intensive trauma treatment show that at 6-month follow-up, outcomes remain largely comparable to the immediate post-treatment results, although some studies report a slight relapse on either CAPS-5 or PCL-5 scores. Nevertheless, in all cases a substantial improvement compared to pre-treatment is sustained (Van Woudenberg et al., 2018; Voorendonk et al., 2023). In addition, adults with ASD and a history of adverse events who received a maximum of eight sessions of EMDR therapy added to treatment as usual reported a significant reduction in symptoms of post-traumatic stress and autistic features. These positive results were maintained at the end of the follow-up phase after 6–8 weeks (Lobregt-van Buuren et al., 2019). However, given the absence of long-term follow-up in the present study, conclusions regarding the durability of treatment effects should be drawn with caution.

Third, self-reporting using the AQ-50 remains a valuable tool for psychological assessment because of its practicality and direct insight into individuals’ subjective experiences. However, it also emphasises the importance of being mindful of potential biases and limitations, suggesting that self-reporting should be used along with other methods for a more comprehensive assessment (Osberg, 1989). In this study, the AQ-50 was used to assess ASD traits. Reliance on self-report measures, such as the AQ-50, for assessing ASD traits may introduce bias or inaccuracies, particularly in a population with social communication difficulties. The AQ-50 shows high sensitivity in detecting autism traits, but suffers from low specificity, resulting in many false positives. This limitation is exacerbated by comorbid conditions such as generalised anxiety disorder, which can inflate AQ scores (Ashwood et al., 2016). A fourth limitation concerns missing AQ-50 post-treatment data. Although a subset of participants (n = 14) did not receive the questionnaire and therefore could not complete it, missingness may also be related to the length and self-report nature of the AQ-50, which could have affected completion rates. Importantly, sensitivity analyses including all participants with available CAPS-5 data yielded a similar pattern of results, suggesting that missing AQ-50 post-treatment data did not meaningfully affect the study findings. A fifth limitation is that over 80% of the participants in this study were female, which raises the question of whether the findings can be generalised across sexes. A significant association has been reported between elevated autistic traits and an increased risk of childhood abuse, trauma victimisation, and PTSD symptoms in adult women (Roberts et al., 2015). Women in the highest quintiles of autistic traits experienced significantly higher rates of sexual, physical, and emotional abuse, and exhibited elevated odds of PTSD, partly mediated by childhood abuse exposure. Finally, limited information about follow-up attrition reduces transparency and may affect result generalizability.

The clinical implications of our findings are significant. First, the success of this intensive trauma-focused treatment in reducing PTSD symptoms across different levels of ASD traits suggests that such programmes could be a valuable option for patients needing rapid intervention. Given the high percentage of patients who no longer met the criteria for PTSD following treatment, this approach could also contribute to a more efficient use of mental health resources, offering substantial benefits in a condensed time frame. Second, the reduction in self-reported ASD traits following treatment may open new pathways for addressing the broader needs of individuals with PTSD and high ASD traits. Clinicians should consider integrating trauma-focused therapy into a comprehensive treatment plan, not only to target PTSD, but also to potentially alleviate some ASD-related difficulties, thereby improving overall functioning and quality of life. Finally, the demonstrated safety and minimal dropout in this study suggest that intensive trauma-focused programmes could be extended to a broader population, including those with complex comorbidities. This may inspire the development of similar programmes in various settings, such as outpatient clinics and community mental health centres, making this effective treatment more accessible to a wider range of individuals. Importantly, findings related to ASD traits should be interpreted in terms of screening-based trait differences (AQ-50) rather than diagnostic ASD classifications.

This study supports the effectiveness and safety of intensive trauma-focused treatment for individuals with PTSD and elevated ASD traits. The treatment not only reduced PTSD symptoms but was also associated with a reduction in self-reported ASD traits, without adverse effects. The absence of negative reactions or significant symptom worsening supports the viability of implementing intensive trauma-focused programmes in individuals with complex and overlapping symptom profiles that include elevated ASD trait levels. Future research should focus on exploring the long-term effects of these treatments, investigating the mechanisms underlying changes in ASD trait measures and examining clinical outcomes in diagnostically confirmed ASD populations to determine generalizability to this group.
