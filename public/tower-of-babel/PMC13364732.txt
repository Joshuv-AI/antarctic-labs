Real-World Application of a Machine Learning-Based Early Recurrence Model for Guiding Adjuvant Chemotherapy Use in Patients with Gallbladder Cancer
Authors: Won-Gun Yun, Youngmin Han, Yoon Soo Chae, Young Jae Cho, Hye-Sol Jung, Joon Seong Park, Jin-Young Jang, Wooil Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsAlthough recent randomized controlled trials have reported the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy for resected biliary tract cancer, discrepancies remain between recommendations and real-world practice. Therefore, we aimed to assess the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy in patients with gallbladder cancer and used machine learning-based risk stratification to predict recurrence to avoid unnecessary chemotherapy.MethodsPatients who underwent surgery between 2005 and 2022 and were histologically diagnosed with stage 2 or above gallbladder cancer were included. The patients were stratified by risk of early recurrence according to the machine learning-based algorithm suggested by Catalano and colleagues.ResultsAmong 395 patients, 204 (51.6%) and 191 (48.4%) were determined to have a low and high risk of early recurrence, respectively. Although the 5-year overall survival rates were not significantly different between the adjuvant chemotherapy and surveillance groups (87.2% vs 83.3%; p=0.233) in the low-risk patients, the adjuvant chemotherapy was associated with a significantly higher 5-year overall survival rate in the high-risk patients (52.1% vs 37.8%; p=0.003).ConclusionsMachine learning-based prediction of early recurrence is helpful in selecting patients who may benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy. These findings may help reduce medical expenses by avoiding unnecessary adjuvant chemotherapy in patients with low risk of early recurrence.Keywords: Gallbladder neoplasms; Chemotherapy, adjuvant; Recurrence; Machine learning algorithms

INTRODUCTION

Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is an aggressive biliary tumor with a poor prognosis.1 Surgery remains the only curative option, but only 30% to 40% of GBC patients are initially diagnosed as localized.2-4 Thus, most studies have focused on chemotherapy regimens for inoperable and metastatic GBC, and optimal surgery for localized GBC. Despite R0 resection, 30% to 65% of patients relapse, with over half developing distant metastases.5,6 High relapse rates suggest a sizable systemic component, and a potential role for adjuvant chemotherapy in localized GBC.

The role of adjuvant chemotherapy in GBC remains debated. After the introduction of the BILCAP trial, which compared adjuvant capecitabine with surveillance, guidelines began recommending 6 months of capecitabine for all resected biliary tract cancer.7-10 The ASCOT trial showed that 6 months of S-1 improved short-term survival outcomes over surveillance in resected biliary tract cancer.11 However, GBC-specific subgroup analyses showed no significant results in either clinical trial. There have also been several studies reporting no survival benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy in biliary tract cancer.12,13 In the PRODIGE 12-ACCORD 18 trial, which compared adjuvant gemcitabine plus oxaliplatin with surveillance in patients with biliary tract cancer, the surveillance group showed significantly better survival outcomes than the adjuvant chemotherapy group in GBC patients.13 These conflicting results stem from trials including all biliary tract cancer patients regardless of the disease site due to low incidence in Western countries. However, clinical and molecular characteristics, including surgeries and prognoses, vary greatly by disease site.14-16 These inconsistent results also suggest not all resected GBC patients benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy. Yet, current clinical guidelines recommend adjuvant chemotherapy for all GBC patients. Therefore, using a large, resected GBC cohort from a highly prevalent region, we evaluated adjuvant chemotherapy in GBC and used machine learning-based risk stratification for early recurrence, aiming to avoid unnecessary chemotherapy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Patient cohort

This single-center retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB number: H-2502-098-1613). This study was conducted in accordance with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and its later versions. The requirement for informed consent was waived. Consecutive patients who underwent surgery and were histologically diagnosed with GBC between 2005 and 2022 were included. Exclusion criteria were: pathological Tis or T1N0 stage (n=108), no lymph node dissection (n=72), history of other cancers within 5 years (n=42), those with a histology other than adenocarcinoma (n=23), those with insufficient clinical information (n=20), and those who died within 3 months of surgery (n=9). Recent clinical trials exploring the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy have excluded patients with pathological Tis or T1N0 stage disease because of their exceptionally good prognosis. Additionally, because lymph node metastasis is a poor prognostic factor, patients who did not undergo lymph node dissection were excluded to ensure clear indications for adjuvant chemotherapy. Finally, 395 patients were included (Supplementary Fig. 1).

2. Preoperative evaluation

Contrast-enhanced biliary protocol computed tomography was routinely performed for initial evaluation. Based on surgeon’s assessment, magnetic resonance, high-resolution ultrasound, endoscopic ultrasound, or positron emission tomography could be used for clinical staging and surgical planning.

3. Surgical procedures

Standard treatment for suspected GBC with T2 or higher category was cholecystectomy with regional lymph node dissection (±wedge resection of liver). Nodes along the common hepatic artery, porta hepatis and those located at the posterosuperior part of the pancreatic head were typically included in the scope of lymph node dissection per the Korean practical guidelines for the surgical treatment of GBC.17 For suspected T1b stage, surgeons decided whether to perform regional lymph node dissection or wedge resection of the liver by comprehensively considering the clinical characteristics including life expectancy, general condition, and preoperative images. Extrahepatic bile duct resection was performed only when intraoperative frozen evaluation demonstrated cancer involvement at the cystic duct stump. Combined organ resection, including vascular resection or major hepatectomy, was performed in exceptional cases with extensive disease burden.

4. Postoperative evaluation and adjuvant treatment

Contrast-enhanced biliary protocol computed tomography was conducted on day 4 after surgery to evaluate surgical morbidity. Major complications were defined as Clavien-Dindo grade 3 or higher.18 After discharge, patients were followed up with tumor markers and contrast-enhanced biliary protocol computed tomography. Recurrence was defined as radiological evidence of recurrent disease. Recurrence-free survival was defined as the time from the date of surgery to the first recurrence, death, or last hospital visit. Overall survival was calculated as the interval from the date of surgery to death or the last hospital visit.

After discharge, 6 months of adjuvant chemotherapy was recommended for patients with T2 or higher category or lymph node metastasis. In patients with lymph node metastasis and microscopically positive resection margin, adjuvant radiotherapy was additionally recommended. The final decision on whether to proceed with adjuvant treatment was made after multidisciplinary meetings and discussions with the patients regarding adverse effects and treatment feasibility. Chemotherapy regimens were divided into 5-fluorouracil-based (5-fluorouracil, 5-fluorouracil/leucovorin, capecitabine, and S-1) and gemcitabine-based (gemcitabine, gemcitabine/cisplatin, gemcitabine/oxaliplatin) regimens. Radiotherapy was administered as concurrent chemoradiation therapy (CCRT), either as a split or continuous course.19 A split course of radiotherapy was administered at a total dose of 40 Gy at 2 Gy per fraction. After receiving 20 Gy for 2 weeks, the patients were allowed to rest for 2 weeks. A continuous course was administered at a median dose of 50.4 Gy at 1.8–2.0 Gy per fraction. Fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy was administered as a radiosensitizer during both courses. During adjuvant treatment, contrast-enhanced abdominal and pelvic computed tomography and tumor marker tests were performed every 3 months.

5. Definitions

Clinicopathological data were collected. Surgical specimens were assessed by experienced gastrointestinal pathologists. The pathology report included staging according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer, differentiation grade, resection margin status, and the presence of microvascular and perineural invasion. Recently, Catalano and colleagues reported that machine learning-based prediction of early recurrence after resection of GBC may help stratify patients.20 Their model development cohort was composed of a multi-institutional database including data from major Western and Eastern hepatobiliary centers. Random forest models with 11 clinical characteristics including age, gender, surgical extent, T and N stages, resection margins, microvascular invasion, and perineural invasion were applied. To ascertain whether this model could be used to pinpoint subgroups that would benefit from adjuvant treatment, our patients were classified into low- and high-risk groups for early recurrence by an online calculator (https://catalano-giovanni.shinyapps.io/GallbladderER).20

6. Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are reported as counts with percentages, and continuous variables are expressed as median values with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables are compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, and continuous variables are compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Survival was estimated from Kaplan-Meier curves, and the log-rank test was used to assess differences in survival. To identify independent risk factors for recurrence, Cox proportional hazards regression models were used, and the results are presented as hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals. All factors deemed clinically relevant by literature and clinical experience were included in the univariate models. Variables with p<0.25 in the univariate models were included in the multivariate models. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Additional combined organ resections were performed in 22 patients (5.6%). Lymph node metastases were identified in 160 patients (40.5%) and R0 resection was achieved in 379 (95.9%). Adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy were administered to 235 (59.5%) and 157 patients (39.7%), respectively. Among patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy, most (91.5%) received 5-fluorouracil-based chemotherapy regimens.

2. Efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy

The 5-year overall survival rates were not significantly different between the adjuvant chemotherapy and surveillance groups (67.7% vs 65.2%; p=0.197) (Fig. 1A). Even when the adjuvant chemotherapy group was divided by chemotherapy regimen, neither the 5-fluorouracil-based regimen nor the gemcitabine-based regimen improved overall survival compared with the surveillance strategy (p=0.350) (Fig. 1B). However, when patients were divided by treatment eras (1: 2005–2009, 2: 2010–2018, 3: 2019–2022), those who received treatment later tended to have better survival outcomes (p=0.017) (Fig. 1C). This implies that effective post-recurrence systemic treatment may be more accessible to the late-period cohort.

Kaplan-Meier estimates of overall survival according to the treatment strategy (A), chemotherapy regimen (B), and the treatment eras (C) in the entire cohort. 5-FU, 5-fluorouracil; GEM, gemcitabine.

Survival analyses according to treatment strategy were performed in patients stratified by pathological T or N category to identify subgroups that could or could not benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy. There were no differences in the survival outcomes according to the receipt of chemotherapy across all T stages. In T2 stage, the 5-year overall survival rates were comparable between the adjuvant chemotherapy and surveillance groups (73.8% vs 72.9%; p=0.263) (Supplementary Fig. 2A). In T3 stage, there were no significant differences in the 5-year overall survival rates between the adjuvant chemotherapy and surveillance groups (49.6% vs 32.2%; p=0.131) (Supplementary Fig. 2B). The 5-year overall survival rate in patients with T4 stage was not significantly different between the adjuvant chemotherapy and surveillance groups (33.3% vs 25.0%; p=0.230) (Supplementary Fig. 2C).

After stratification according to lymph node metastasis status, the chemotherapy group demonstrated better survival in both subgroups. Among the patients without lymph node metastases, the adjuvant chemotherapy group had a significantly higher 5-year overall survival rate than the surveillance group (86.5% vs 78.0%; p=0.040) (Supplementary Fig. 3A). In patients with lymph node metastases, adjuvant chemotherapy was associated with a higher 5-year overall survival rate than the surveillance strategy (48.8% vs 36.3%; p=0.010) (Supplementary Fig. 3B). These findings indicate that the pathological stage alone is not sufficient to identify subgroups that could or could not benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy.

3. Predictive factors for recurrence timing

The BILCAP trial demonstrated adjuvant chemotherapy appears to have little impact on local or distant recurrence rate.8 Bridgewater and colleagues reported that delaying the date of recurrence is the primary advantage of adjuvant chemotherapy. Therefore, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to explore the predictive factors for recurrence timing (Supplementary Table 1). Multivariate analyses revealed that age, surgical extent, T category, lymph node metastasis, and differentiation grade were associated with the timing of recurrence. Additionally, univariate models identified that preoperative jaundice, microscopically involved margins, and the presence of microvascular and perineural invasion were also associated with early recurrence. These findings indicate that more clinical characteristics than pathological stage alone are required to predict recurrence timing and identify subgroups that could or could not benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy.

Additionally, we ranked the significance of variables influencing recurrence using the random forest model, which has been employed by Catalano and colleagues (Supplementary Table 1). Most of the variables identified as significant in multivariate Cox regression analyses were also ranked high in the random forest model, suggesting that the model's internal logic aligns with the actual risk factors observed in this specific population.

4. Clinical characteristics according to the machine-learning algorithm

The clinical characteristics of the risk groups are presented in Table 2. There were no significant differences in baseline demographics including age, sex, and body mass index between the two groups. High-risk patients underwent extensive surgery, including bile duct or additional combined organ resection, more frequently than low-risk patients. However, the major complication rate was not significantly different between the two groups (8.3% vs 12.0%; p=0.292). High-risk patients had higher pathological stages, microscopically involved margins, moderate/poor differentiation, and microvascular and perineural invasion more frequently than low-risk patients. Adjuvant chemotherapy was administered more frequently to high-risk patients (67.0%) than low-risk patients (52.5%, p=0.004), and a similar trend was observed for adjuvant radiotherapy (51.3% vs 28.9%; p<0.001).

The presence or absence of local and distant recurrence by adjuvant treatment and risk stratification for early recurrence was explored, and the descriptive results are reported in Supplementary Table 2. In both the adjuvant chemotherapy and surveillance groups, high-risk patients were more likely to have any form of recurrence than low-risk patients. Adjuvant chemotherapy did not appear to have any impact on the local recurrence rate in low-risk (6/107, 5.6%) or high-risk (8/63, 12.7%) patients. Additionally, adjuvant chemotherapy appeared to have little impact on the distant recurrence rate in either low-risk (5/107, 4.7%) or high-risk (14/63, 22.2%) patients.

5. Efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy based on a machine learning algorithm

The machine learning-based algorithm successfully stratified patients by overall and recurrence-free survival. The 5-year overall survival rate was 47.5% in patients predicted to experience early recurrence versus 85.2% in those not predicted to experience early recurrence (p<0.001). Similarly, the 5-year recurrence-free survival rate was 40.4% versus 81.2%, respectively (p<0.001) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 4A). Additionally, among patients with pathological stage 2, low-risk patients showed a significantly higher 5-year overall survival and recurrence-free survival rates than high-risk patients. This finding suggests that machine learning-defined risk groups have added discriminatory value over conventional staging (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Kaplan-Meier estimates of overall survival according to the predicted risk for early recurrence in the entire cohort (A) and according to the treatment strategy in patients not predicted (B) and predicted (C) to experience early recurrence.

To evaluate whether this risk stratification may be helpful in identifying patients for adjuvant chemotherapy, survival analyses according to the treatment strategy were performed in both groups. Among the low-risk patients, the 5-year overall survival rate did not differ significantly between the adjuvant chemotherapy and surveillance groups (87.2% vs 83.3%; p=0.233) (Fig. 2B). Similarly, the 5-year recurrence-free survival rate was not significantly different between the two groups (83.9% vs 78.4%; p=0.133) (Supplementary Fig. 4B). In contrast, among high-risk patients, the adjuvant chemotherapy group had a significantly higher 5-year overall survival rate than the surveillance group (52.1% vs 37.8%; p=0.003) (Fig. 2C). The 5-year recurrence-free survival rate was also significantly higher in the adjuvant chemotherapy group (44.8% vs 30.9%; p=0.002) (Supplementary Fig. 4C). For robustness, we performed the same survival analyses excluding patients who received gemcitabine-based chemotherapy. Similar to previous findings, the adjuvant chemotherapy group showed better survival outcomes than the surveillance group only in the high-risk group (Supplementary Fig. 6).

6. Optimal adjuvant treatment strategy

To explore the optimal adjuvant treatment strategy, the patients were classified into four groups: CCRT with maintenance chemotherapy, chemotherapy only, CCRT only, and surveillance. The Kaplan–Meier curves for overall survival according to the adjuvant treatment strategies are shown in Fig. 3. Among patients not predicted to experience early recurrence, the 5-year overall survival rates did not differ significantly among the four groups (84.0% vs 89.2% vs 75.0% vs 84.1%; p=0.340) (Fig. 3A). In contrast, among patients predicted to experience early recurrence, those who received adjuvant chemotherapy (CCRT with maintenance chemotherapy [52.1%] or chemotherapy only [52.7%]) had significantly higher 5-year overall survival rates than those who did not receive (CCRT only [11.7%] or surveillance [42.6%], p=0.014) (Fig. 3B). However, the 5-year overall survival rates were similar between patients treated with CCRT with maintenance chemotherapy and those treated with chemotherapy alone (p=0.970). Additionally, the 5-year overall survival rates were similar between patients treated with CCRT only and those who did not receive adjuvant treatment (p=0.308).

DISCUSSION

Although most guidelines recommend adjuvant chemotherapy for all patients with resected biliary tract cancer, supported by the positive outcomes of the BILCAP and ASCOT trials, discrepancies remain between the guidelines and real-world treatment.8,11,21,22 First, these trials included all types of biliary tract cancers despite differences in clinical and molecular features depending on disease site. Second, prespecified subgroup analyses in these trials yielded inconclusive results, likely due to limited statistical power from the small sample size. Third, because surgical extent varies among patients, targeting patients with an accurate pathological stage could be crucial. This study demonstrated that the machine learning-based early recurrence prediction algorithm suggested by Catalano et al.20 effectively identified patients unlikely to benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy. We also found that the optimal adjuvant treatment was to concentrate on systemic treatment.

Few studies have explored the efficacy of adjuvant treatment exclusively for patients with GBC, aside from those using statistical databases, such as Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results, which have a higher probability of coding errors and lack important data. Manterola et al.23 reported no strong evidence that adjuvant treatment is effective for patients with GBC. The OMEGA study, the largest cohort study on surgical outcomes of GBC, found that adjuvant chemotherapy improved overall survival only in patients with stage 3B or 4.24 Several studies have proposed pathological TNM stage-based subgroups that might benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy; however, the groupings proposed by each study varied greatly, like T3-T4, node-positive, or stage 3-4. Our findings suggest that TNM stage by itself is not sufficient to identify subgroups that might benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy. Additionally, only one-third of the OMEGA cohort received adjuvant chemotherapy, indicating definite discrepancies between real-world situations and recommendations. The clinical significance of GBC is growing as it is the most prevalent biliary tract cancer, and the number of incidental GBC cases is increasing. In this situation, algorithm-guided administration could lessen some patients’ suffering by cutting down on needless adjuvant chemotherapy, lowering social medical expenses, and directing medical resources toward those who are most in need.25

An interesting aspect of systemic treatment for biliary tract cancer is the difference in effective chemotherapy regimens between advanced settings, such as unresectable or metastatic disease, and the adjuvant setting. For over a decade, gemcitabine plus cisplatin has been the treatment of choice in advanced settings.26 Recently, in the TOPAZ-1 trial, durvalumab was added to gemcitabine plus cisplatin.27,28 However, the BCAT and PRODIGE 12-ACCORD 18 trials failed to show that adjuvant gemcitabine alone or in combination with oxaliplatin could improve survival outcomes compared with a surveillance strategy.12,13 Instead, favorable outcomes of adjuvant 5-fluorouracil-based regimens have been shown in the BILCAP and ASCOT trials.8,11 However, in the STAMP trial, patients treated with adjuvant gemcitabine plus cisplatin showed survival outcomes similar to those treated with capecitabine.29 Additionally, adjuvant chemotherapy regimens were not associated with overall survival in this study. Therefore, it is difficult to conclude whether 5-fluorouracil-based regimens are beneficial only in the adjuvant setting. Recently, the efficacy of targeted agents in advanced settings has been explored along with new discoveries of molecular targets, including HER2 amplification and NTRK fusion.30 It is anticipated that medications with more potent effects will appear in adjuvant settings in tandem with these advancements.

The efficacy of radiotherapy has not yet been explored in prospective studies. Although Ostwal et al.31 reported that both gemcitabine plus cisplatin and CCRT with maintenance capecitabine showed 1-year recurrence-free survival rates beyond the prespecified trial criteria of greater than 77%, there was no direct comparison between the two treatment strategies. Only a few retrospective studies have compared the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy and chemoradiotherapy for GBC. Kim et al.32 reported that adjuvant CCRT was associated with improved recurrence-free survival compared to chemotherapy alone but was not associated with overall survival. Some studies have reported that adjuvant CCRT is associated with better overall survival compared with chemotherapy alone.33,34 However, these studies had clear limitations, such as not evaluating details of CCRT, including the use of maintenance chemotherapy or excluding patients who did not undergo lymph node dissection. Our study indicates that the impact of CCRT is limited, and that chemotherapy may be beneficial for patients at a high risk of early recurrence. These conflicting findings highlight the need for randomized controlled trials comparing the efficacy of CCRT versus chemotherapy in adjuvant settings.

This study had some limitations. First, this was a retrospective cohort study, which may have introduced potential selection bias. Second, subgroup analyses according to resection margins or chemotherapy regimens are limited because the number of patients with microscopically positive margins or those treated with adjuvant gemcitabine-based regimens was too small.

Despite these limitations, this study has the strength of validating a machine learning-based algorithm using a high-quality resected GBC cohort with clear inclusion and exclusion criteria. This study demonstrated that patients who were not predicted to experience early recurrence did not benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy, and adding CCRT to chemotherapy did not improve overall survival compared to chemotherapy alone. However, before being applied in real-world settings, these findings should be validated by larger and prospective cohort studies.
