Integrating a “see-and-treat” approach with opportunistic human papilloma virus deoxyribonucleic acid (HPV DNA) based cervical cancer screening in India: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Sripriya Rao, Mayank Chhabra, Jagadish Kudkuli, Milan Toraskar, Aswathy G. Nath, Devu Prakash, Shweta Srivastava, Wahengbam Imo Singh, Malini Chandrasekharan, Kunal Oswal, Jaganjeet Randhawa, Venkataramanan Ramachandran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDIndia lacks an organized national cervical cancer screening program, and while the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends human papillomavirus (HPV) deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) testing as the primary modality, large-scale evidence on implementation via decentralized treatment pathways in India is limited. We evaluated the effectiveness of opportunistic high-risk HPV (hrHPV) DNA screening, assessing prevalence, genotype distribution, self-testing acceptance, and the diagnostic performance of a community-based “see-and-treat” approach.OBJECTIVETo evaluate the prevalence and genotype distribution of hrHPV and assess the diagnostic accuracy, treatment compliance, and clinical viability of a decentralized “see-and-treat” approach within a large-scale opportunistic screening program across 20 Indian states.STUDY DESIGNIn this retrospective cross-sectional study (January 2022–December 2025), 62,868 women aged 25 to 65 years were enrolled across 20 Indian states via community outreach (75.1%) and hospital-based network (24.9%). Screening utilized clinician-collected or self-collected (10.5%) samples tested via the Cobas 6800 system. hrHPV-positive women were triaged by colposcopy and managed through a decentralized “see-and-treat” approach using thermal ablation (TA) or Large Loop Excision of the Transformation Zone (LLETZ). Primary outcomes included HPV prevalence, diagnostic accuracy (AUC), and treatment compliance.RESULTSOverall HPV prevalence was 8.5% (5,354/62,868), with significant regional variation (11.3% in Delhi NCR to 4.7% in Kerala). Non-16/18 high-risk genotypes accounted for 54.6% of infections. Positivity peaked at ages 26 to 30 years (10.1%) and had a minor peak at 51 to 55 years (8.4%). The see-and-treat modality demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.95; sensitivity 0.98; specificity 0.91) with a program efficacy of 81.2% with 16.3% overtreatment and 21.1% undertreatment. Treatment compliance in the community sub-cohort was 51.3% (1,957/3,813). We identified five women with invasive cancers and 7.8% CIN III (28/357), 9.0% CIN II (32/357), and 26.3% CIN I (94/357) of the histopathology sub-group.CONCLUSIONOpportunistic hrHPV screening integrated with decentralized “see-and-treat” is a clinically robust strategy for LMICs. Significant regional heterogeneity requires tailored, state-specific policies and digital linkage-to-care frameworks to overcome compliance barriers and meet WHO 2030 cervical cancer elimination goals.

Introduction

India currently ranks third globally in terms of absolute cervical cancer incidence, with an estimated 78,499 new cases in 2024.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Despite the World Health Organization’s (WHO) global strategy to eliminate cervical cancer by 2030, India lacks a national, organized screening program, leaving most women without access to preventive care.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Projections from the Global Cancer Observatory indicate that this trend will continue, with the annual burden expected to reach approximately 2.46 million individuals by 2045.7 Human papillomavirus (HPV) is recognized as a primary contributor to the development of cervical cancer. Nearly 40 HPV strains infect the mucosa of the lower genital tract, primarily transmitted through sexual contact, and are classified based on their risk for causing cancer.2 The WHO advocates for a “90-70-90” target, requiring 70% screening coverage using high-performance HPV molecular tests twice in a lifetime, by ages 35 and 45.8 Evidence confirms that molecular high-risk HPV (hrHPV) testing is significantly more sensitive than visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) or cytology for detecting high-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia.3 The effectiveness of organized screening programs has been demonstrated in developed countries, leading to substantial decrease in invasive cervical cancer risk. There has been a shift in screening practices in regions like Europe, Australia, and the Netherlands, with HPV test-based screening programs being introduced in regular screening.9,10 Key recommendations emphasize the use of clinically validated high-performance molecular tests like hrHPV deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) tests that meet established international performance criteria, such as those defined by Meijer et al and verified through the VALGENT framework.11,12 In the absence of structured national programs, opportunistic screening integrated into public health initiatives offers a replicable model for early detection. However, the efficacy of these strategies is often mediated by complex socio-demographic disparities.13, 14, 15 In this study, we determined the prevalence and genotype distribution of hrHPV and assessed its association with demographic variables among a large-scale diverse population. We evaluated the treatment compliance and diagnostic accuracy of a decentralized “see-and-treat” approach and assessed the clinical viability of HPV self-sampling as a strategy to enhance screening reach in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cross-sectional study was designed to evaluate the effectiveness of opportunistic cervical screening using a WHO-recommended, clinically validated HPV DNA test (Cobas 6800) for detecting HPV infections and timely management of cervical pre and early stage cancers.

Study setting and participants

A total of 62,868 women underwent hrHPV DNA screening across 20 Indian states from January 2022 to December 2025. States were selected based on the existing operational footprint of the Karkinos Healthcare network and its partner organizations. Participants were identified through convenient sampling in both community-based awareness campaigns (outreach extension clinics) and existing hospital-based patient networks. Screening and HPV sample collection were undertaken with the written informed consent before the procedures. Further informed written consent was deployed for HPV-positive women before navigating them to “see-and-treat” modality. The study setting includes community extension clinics (supported by a team of clinical, nursing, and care navigators) and hospital-based screenings (facilitated by a network of clinicians). Women aged 25 to 65 years were recruited after receiving detailed information on screening procedures, risks and benefits, and provided informed consent before enrollment. To maintain the statistical integrity of the prevalence metrics for the general population and avoid data skewness, individuals with known status of HIV, commercial sex work, or incarceration were excluded. This decision was informed by our previous research identifying disproportionately high hrHPV positivity in these cohorts, which necessitates specialized clinical management outside the scope of general community screening. Formal ethical approval was granted by Karkinos Healthcare Independent Ethics Committee (KH-IEC) with a Protocol No. KH-IEC/04/2025, and HPV testing was carried out using clinician-collected and self-collection methods based on individual consent.

Screening procedure

HPV testing was carried out using clinician-collected and self-collection methods based on individual consent. Test reports were delivered via personalized messaging within 7 days of sample collection.

Recruitment strategy and cohort distribution

As this was a retrospective evaluation of an opportunistic screening program, the sample size was determined by the total participation in opportunistic HPV screening through integrated community and hospital-based services during the 4-year study period. The overall sample was stratified by screening environment, with 75.1% (N=47,202) of participants recruited via community outreach extension clinics and 24.9% (N=15,666) enrolled through hospital-based services. The primary cohort (including community and hospital recruitment) consisted of 5,354 hrHPV-positive individuals (8.5% positivity), further categorized into a community-based sub-cohort (3,813) to assess the diagnostic and positive predictive value (PPV) of the decentralized “see-and-treat” modality with the community. The study further identified a clinical management of 1,957 HPV-positive women (51.3% compliance) from community-based sub-cohort who completed advanced diagnostic and treatment protocols. Clinical management and follow-up

Hospital-based screen-positive women were directly managed by gynecologists independently, while the community-based screen-positive women were managed under a decentralized “see-and-treat” modality by a trained team of clinicians. This study only highlighted the treatments managed under see-and-treat modality within a community-based setting. Triaging was done using a colposcope followed by directed biopsies and endocervical curettages in clinically indicated cases (Type 2/3 transformation zone [TZ] and lesion extending to endocervical canal). Based on HPV results, colposcopy findings, type of TZ, extent of lesion, and Swede score, treatment was provided immediately. Hand-held Mobile ODT Colposcope, Thermal Ablator, and Portable Large Loop Excision of the Transformation Zone (LLETZ) devices were used. Thermal Ablation (TA) was performed for other hrHPV positives with Type 1 TZ and minor colposcopy findings (Swede score less than 4), while LLETZ for HPV 16 and 18 positives, Type 3 TZ, and major colposcopy findings (Swede score more than 4). These procedures were performed at the nearest clinic or hospital equipped with an operation theater facility. For clinically suspicious lesions, only punch biopsy was done in the community and further referred to the anchor center. The strategy was adopted for “closing of the clinical loop” within 2 weeks of the initial result in resource-limited settings. Hospital-based screen-positive women were directly managed by gynecologists independently following their own clinical recommendations or protocols; no SOPs were given by the study investigators. A central command center (CC) facilitated patient navigation to ensure timely linkage to care and minimize loss to follow-up. CC team-based patient follow-up, dedicated counsellor, grass-root mobilizer making three calls for every participant was strictly adhered to standard SOPs.

Data collection

Data collected included participant demographics (age, state of residence), clinical histories, and HPV DNA test results. Age was treated as a continuous variable during the data collection and subsequently categorized for statistical modelling. Follow-up data encompassed subsequent procedures, including colposcopy, pathological findings, and treatment modalities.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive, bivariate, and multivariate analysis was performed to calculate frequency, genotype distribution, and hrHPV prevalence stratified by age and geographical region and crude and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The performance of the see-and-treat modality was assessed through Pearson correlation between colposcopy and histopathology, and area under the curve (AUC) with sensitivity and specificity metrics. All analyses were performed using SPSS and R software; P values <0.05 were considered significant.

Role of funding source

The researchers exercised sole responsibility for the study design, data analysis, and manuscript preparation independent of the institutions that provided the financial resources for the program’s execution.

Demographic characteristics and HPV prevalence among 62,868 individuals with crude and adjusted odds ratio (AOR)

Independent predictors of hrHPV positivity, including crude and AOR, are summarized in Table 1. Reference categories for each variable were selected based on convenience, following an order from highest to lowest prevalence values to establish a baseline for comparative analysis. After adjusting for age, state, screening setting, and geographical location, it emerged as a considerable determinant of infection risk. Compared to the reference state (Karnataka), women residing in Delhi NCR (AOR: 1.36; 95% CI 1.23–1.51; p<.001) and other states (AOR 1.23; 95% CI 1.08–1.41; p<.002) exhibited significantly increased odds of infection. Conversely, residence in Kerala was associated with the lowest odds of positivity among the surveyed regions (AOR: 0.51; 95% CI 0.45–0.57; p<.001). Consistent with our univariate trends, increasing age served as a significant protective factor against hrHPV infection (p<.001). Using women aged 25 to 30 as the reference group, a progressive decline in risk was observed across all cohorts, with women in the 61 to 65 age group showing a 3% reduction in the odds of infection (AOR 0.97; 95% CI 0.96–0.98; p<.001). Furthermore, the screening environment influenced positivity rates; hospital-based screenings (AOR: 1.59; 95% CI 1.47–1.71; p<.001) were associated with significantly higher odds of hrHPV detection compared with community-based screenings (AOR: 0.98; 95% CI 0.98–0.99; p<.001). The overall program efficacy was 81.2%, and there was a calculated overtreatment rate of 16.3% and an undertreatment rate of 21.1%.

HPV genotype specificity and association with demographic variables

Out of the 5,354 HPV positives, HPV others (12 other high-risk types excluding 16 and 18) genotypes were most prevalent and found in 2,922 (54.6%) women, followed by HPV 16 in 1,294 (24.2%) women, HPV 18 in 527 (9.8%) women, and a combination of infection in 611 (11.4%) women. In combination of infection, HPV 16+others set is most prevalent in 373 (6.9%) women, followed by HPV 18+others in 160 (3.0%) women, HPV 16+HPV 18 in 41 (0.8%), and HPV 16+18+others in 37 (0.7%) women.

The distribution of genotypes varied significantly across demographic strata (Table 2, Figure 2). Cross-tabulated data (Table 2) provides a comprehensive breakdown of hrHPV genotype distribution categorized into HPV 16, HPV 18, HPV others, and combination of infection. A clear observation from Table 2 is that “HPV others” (non-16/18 high-risk types) genotypes predominate across nearly all sub-groups. The proportional prevalence of HPV 16 and 18 genotypes (including combination of infection) demonstrated a gradual rise from 26 to 30 years (42.9%) to 61 to 65 years (51.4%) (Table 2, Figure 2, A). Geographical analysis in Figure 2, B and Table 2 reveals significant state-specific variations; HPV 16 genotype prevalence reached its highest peaks in Madhya Pradesh (31.1%) and Andhra Pradesh (30.9%), whereas the proportional share of HPV 18 genotype is highest in Maharashtra (14.8%), Manipur (12.7%), and Andhra Pradesh (12.4%). The HPV others genotype prevalence was highest in Kerala (64.1%) and Jharkhand (59.7%). Screening outcomes and community compliance

Out of the overall HPV positives, the hospital-based HPV positives (1,541) were directly managed by clinician from where these samples were collected. While the HPV-positive community-based sub-cohort (n=3,813), 1,957 women (51.3%) complied with further see-and-treat protocol, where based on colposcopy findings, the treatment was provided (colposcopy, directed biopsies followed by TA or LLETZ) within the community with the point-of-care devices (Figure 1, B).

The colposcopy findings (n=444) highlight that 81 (18.2%) women were diagnosed with high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL), 128 (28.8%) women with low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (LSIL), 74 (16.7%) women with inflammation/cervicitis or metaplasia, and the rest, 161 (36.3%), were normal. These cases were further managed through TA in 85.7% of patients and LLETZ in 14.2%. On histopathology (n=357) findings suggest 5 (1.4%) women with features of invasive squamous cell carcinoma, 43.1% women with Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CIN III in 28 [7.8%], CIN II in 32 [9.0%], and CIN I in 94 [26.3%]), 84 (23.6) women with chronic cervicitis, and 114 (31.9%) women were normal. The confirmed cancerous cases were appropriately referred to anchor centers for treatment.

Treatment efficacy and diagnostic performance

The diagnostic triaging via colposcopy showed a strong positive correlation with definitive histopathological findings (Pearson r=0.81; 95% CI: 0.75–0.86; p<.001). The diagnostic accuracy and PPV of the see-and-treat modality were robust, with an AUC of 0.954 (Figure 3). The system demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.98 (95% CI: 0.93–1.00) and a specificity of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.81–0.97). The PPV was 0.95, while the negative predictive value (NPV) was 0.96. The AUC of 0.95 reflects high diagnostic accuracy and PPV. Performance metrics show a sensitivity of 0.98 and specificity of 0.91, supporting a program efficacy of 81.2% while maintaining a low overtreatment rate of 16.3% and an undertreatment rate of 21.1%. Overtreatment was defined as any intervention (TA/LLETZ) in cases where definitive histopathology reported normal or chronic cervicitis, or the use of LLETZ for confirmed CIN I. Undertreatment was defined as the lack of surgical intervention for confirmed CIN II, or the use of non-excisional treatment (eg, TA) for confirmed CIN III. Using these criteria, the program recorded an overtreatment rate of 16.3% and an undertreatment rate of 21.1%.

Within the sub-cohort of participants who complied with navigation protocols (n=1,957), available data from colposcopy (n=444), histopathology (n=357), and the total count of administered treatments (n=723) were evaluated to assess diagnostic accuracy and overall program efficacy (Figure 1, B).

Discussion

This retrospective study of 62,868 women represents an evaluation of opportunistic hrHPV DNA screening in India, 8.5% prevalence with a notable 54.6% of infections caused by non-16/8 genotypes (Table 2). The HPV positivity prevalence aligns with regional estimates, ranging between 5% and 15%.2,16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 The Cobas 6800 system ensures high NPV, supporting the safe extension of screening intervals. These results reinforce the superior performance of molecular testing over visual inspection or cytology in resource-limited settings. By adhering to criteria of Meijer et al and VALGENT11,12 framework, the study ensured that the molecular testing provided superior NPV, offering the long-term reassurance necessary to safely extend screening intervals to the WHO-recommended twice-per-life time strategy.8 These results reinforce the findings of anchor trials (Sankaranarayanan et al) that established the superior sensitivity of HPV molecular testing over VIA or cytology.9,10,22 Our findings identify a bimodal distribution of hrHPV positivity, characterized by a primary peak of 10.1% in younger women aged 26 to 35 years and a secondary peak of 8.4% among the perimenopausal women aged 51 to 55 years (Figure 1, A; Table 1). This secondary resurgence in later life emphasizes the necessity of continued age-specific screening to ensure early detection during the menopausal transition.

Our “see-and-treat” model (Figure 3) achieved a sensitivity of 0.98 and specificity of 0.91, meeting international clinical validation criteria11 and WHO targets for appropriate management.8 The high diagnostic precision (AUC of 0.95; Figure 3) of our triage stands in contrast to regional pilots, such as in Uzbekistan, where lower correlations between colposcopy and histopathology limited program efficacy (correlation between diagnostic colposcopy and histopathology was only 53.2%).23 Furthermore, our study (Figure 3) yielded higher diagnostic accuracy and PPV. The successful identification and clinical management of 723 individuals with precancerous cervical lesions informs that the integration of high-quality diagnostic triaging with immediate intervention (Figure 3), yielding an overall program efficacy of 81.2%, establishes a more effective clinical loop than alternative strategies prioritizing participation over the technical reliability required to meet the WHO target of 90% appropriate management. While technically robust, a critical challenge remains the 51.3% treatment compliance rate in the community-based sub-cohort. This attrition is consistent with LMIC benchmarks where follow-up reaches a 50% plateau due to socio-logistical factors.9 The observed compliance rate serves as a reminder that technical fidelity cannot overcome structural barriers. This shortfall from the WHO 90% management mandate is not a failure of decentralized modality but a result of real-world socio-logistical determinants, including domestic responsibilities and geographical accessibility. Compliance fluctuated with state-level health and economic indicators, being higher in Tamil Nadu (79.3% compliance; Health Index 64.1; literacy rate 85.5) and lower in Jharkhand (17.7% compliance; Health Index 48.2; literacy rate 76.7).24,25 To bridge this gap, future frameworks must integrate financial support, such as transportation subsidies, and digital tracking via Ayushman Bharat Health Account (ABHA) IDs.

However, to achieve WHO 2030 elimination targets, there is a considerable requirement for strengthened linkage-to-care frameworks and locally tailored interventions that bridge this gap and ensure screening effectively translates into treatment.17

Regional heterogeneity, with prevalence ranging from 11.3% in Delhi NCR to 4.7% in Kerala (Table 1, Table 2; Figure 2, B), necessitates state-specific policies rather than a uniform national strategy. The significant regional disparities observed in our study mirror the significant geographic heterogeneity documented in national registry data. Mathur’s cross-sectional analysis of 43 population-based cancer registries reveals a similar spectrum, with age-adjusted incidence rates showing nearly seven-fold variations across different Indian districts.1 Despite age functioning as a general protective factor,17 the observed resurgence of aggressive HPV 16 genotype in women aged 61 to 65 years and a secondary peak in the perimenopausal group (51–55 years; Figure 1, A) justify continued screening in older populations. Also, the age-specific screening remains vital for early detection in perimenopausal women to ensure the global target of 90% appropriate management for those with cervical precancer or cancer, respectively.8

Furthermore, the 10.5% uptake of self-sampling demonstrates its viability in bypassing socio-cultural barriers and stigma associated with pelvic exams (Table 1). This approach serves as a tool for patient empowerment in the Indian context, bypassing the lack of autonomy often experienced in seeking clinician/nurse-led care.26,27 While specific comprehensive follow-up data between self-sampling and nurse-assisted methods were not segregated, our findings align with global evidence suggesting self-sampling reduces logistical burdens in rural areas.26,28,29

Our study identifies a high prevalence of hrHPV infection among women aged 26 to 40, aligning with data from other low-resource settings.16 Although data on sexual behaviors were not collected, the high infection rate in women aged 26 to 40 suggests that lowering the screening threshold could enhance early detection.2,10 The consistency of these results across diverse settings highlights the imperative for context-specific prevention and control strategies.

In our see-and-treat protocol, TA was performed for non-16/18 hrHPV positives with Type 1 TZ and minor colposcopic findings (Swede score <4), including cases of CIN I. While we recognize this may be categorized as overtreatment, this strategy was adopted to “close the loop” within 2 weeks and safeguard participants against lesion progression in settings where longitudinal follow-up is historically challenging.30

Limitations of the study

Key limitations include the use of convenience sampling, which may underrepresent the burden in underserved or rural areas. The absence of data on sexual history and lifestyle prevents granular analysis of behavioral risks,2,10 and a 6-month follow-up period precludes assessment of long-term cancer incidence reduction.

Implications and policy advocacy

To achieve WHO 2030 targets, India must integrate high-sensitivity HPV DNA testing into national health frameworks like Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM-JAY). Our findings provide a scalable roadmap for cervical cancer elimination by demonstrating that decentralized “see-and-treat” models are technically feasible and accurate. Strengthening socio-economic support for linkage-to-care and leveraging digital identifiers for longitudinal tracking are the essential final steps to transition from opportunistic to universal screening.

Conclusions

Opportunistic hrHPV DNA-based screening using decentralized “see-and-treat” model is clinically robust, achieving high diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.95) with high sensitivity (0.98). However, the modest 51.3% treatment compliance rate emphasizes that technical excellence cannot independently overcome structural and socio-logistical barriers. To achieve WHO 2030 elimination targets, India must transition to organized screening by integrating molecular testing into national frameworks like PM-JAY, leveraging digital identifiers (ABHA IDs) for longitudinal patient tracking, and targeted socio-economic support to bridge the critical linkage-to-care gap.

Why was this study conducted?

India accounts for a significant portion of the global cervical cancer burden yet lacks a nationwide screening; therefore, study generates evidence on diagnostic accuracy and implementation of opportunistic HPV screening integrated with decentralized “see-and-treat” modalities across Indian states and demographic settings.

What are the key findings?

In 62,868 women, hrHPV prevalence was 8.5%, with non-16/18 genotypes causing 54.6% of infections; the “see-and-treat” approach demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.95, sensitivity 0.98) and decent treatment compliance rate of 51.3%. What does this study add to what is already known?

This large-scale evaluation confirms the technical feasibility of decentralized screening in resource-limited settings and demonstrates that achieving WHO 2030 targets necessitates state-specific policies and digital health integration to bridge systemic linkage-to-care gaps.
