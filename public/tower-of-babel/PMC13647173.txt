AI-driven multi-omics integration in breast cancer: clinical applications, immunotherapy prediction, and translational challenges

Zi-Yao Wang, Na Liu, Min-Bin Chen

Abstract

Breast cancer remains a major global threat to women’s health, and its marked molecular, spatial, and temporal heterogeneity continues to limit the accuracy of early diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment-response prediction. Conventional single-dimensional diagnostic and therapeutic models are often insufficient to capture the dynamic complexity of the tumor microenvironment (TME). In this context, artificial intelligence (AI) provides powerful tools for high-dimensional feature extraction, multimodal data integration, and cross-scale modeling of heterogeneous biological and clinical information. AI-enabled approaches can link microscopic molecular alterations with macroscopic imaging and pathological phenotypes, while also characterizing complex cellular interactions within the TME. This review summarizes current applications of AI-driven multimodal and multi-omics integration in breast cancer, focusing on early detection, precision diagnosis, immunotherapy-response prediction, drug-sensitivity assessment, and prognostic evaluation. We also discuss key translational challenges, including data heterogeneity, batch effects, interpretability, privacy protection, regulatory considerations, and clinical workflow integration. Overall, AI-driven multi-omics strategies offer a promising framework for improving individualized treatment selection and real-time monitoring in breast cancer, although robust prospective validation and multidisciplinary implementation are still required before routine clinical adoption. Compared with previous modality-specific reviews, this article emphasizes cross-modal evidence appraisal, clinical-maturity stratification, data-quality constraints, foundation model opportunities, and regulatory requirements for translating AI-enabled multi-omics from exploratory research to clinically auditable decision support.

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most important public health challenges affecting women worldwide (). Globally, breast cancer accounts for approximately one-third of all female malignancies, with a mortality rate representing about 15% of the total diagnosed cases. The global distribution of breast cancer is influenced by a complex interplay of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors (; ). In many countries, it also remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related death among women (). Although screening programs and improvements in surgery, endocrine therapy, and supportive care have improved overall survival (), prevention and disease control remain challenging in the context of population aging and changing lifestyles (). For patients at high risk of recurrence or metastasis, traditional single-dimensional treatment models have reached important efficacy limits, underscoring the need for more precise and individualized strategies ().

Breast cancer is not a single disease but a heterogeneous group of malignancies characterized by substantial spatial and temporal heterogeneity at the molecular level (). This marked intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) represents a major barrier to current clinical diagnosis and treatment (). At the diagnostic level, conventional pathological classification based on ER, PR, HER2, and Ki-67 is insufficient to fully capture tumor aggressiveness and invasive potential (). As a result, some early-stage patients may receive overtreatment, whereas high-risk patients with occult micrometastases may miss the optimal window for intervention (; ). Such heterogeneity directly affects the efficacy of chemotherapy and immunotherapy () and is significantly associated with 5-year survival ().

In the rapidly evolving field of cancer immunotherapy, treatment response varies substantially among patients, particularly in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) (). Because of the complexity of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), a single biomarker—such as PD-L1 expression or tumor mutational burden (TMB)—is often insufficient to accurately identify patients who are likely to benefit from therapy () and may fail to anticipate immune escape or severe immune-related adverse events (irAEs) (; ). Traditional binary classification methods do not adequately account for interactions among tumor metabolism, stromal architecture, genomic instability, and host immunity (). Therefore, the mechanisms of immune evolution in breast cancer need to be re-examined from a systems-biology perspective ().

With rapid advances in high-throughput sequencing, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), spatial transcriptomics, and radiomics, cancer research has entered an era of large-scale omics data (). However, single-omics technologies are inherently limited by their observational dimensions and may generate information silos, making it difficult to reconstruct the complex networks linking genes, metabolism, the microenvironment, and host immunity (). For multidimensional, large-scale, and highly nonlinear biological datasets, traditional statistical methods are often insufficient, whereas artificial intelligence (AI) offers powerful capabilities for high-dimensional feature extraction and cross-modal integration (). outlines the evolution from conventional single-dimensional approaches to AI-driven multi-omics integration.

AI applications in oncology depend strongly on the type of data analyzed: for structured clinical variables, classical machine-learning methods are commonly used for survival prediction and risk stratification (); for pathological and radiological images, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) can extract spatial features for lesion segmentation (; ); and for sequence data such as genomes or medical records, Transformers and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) can model long-range dependencies and support biomarker discovery. Large language models (LLMs) may further accelerate knowledge extraction and validation (; ). Through multimodal fusion, AI can reduce barriers among omics layers, map microscopic genotypes to macroscopic imaging phenotypes, and identify latent biomarker combinations (). By integrating genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, pathology, and imaging, this strategy enables comprehensive characterization of tumor mutations, gene expression, microenvironmental composition, and spatial invasion patterns (; ), thereby supporting more accurate prediction of biological behavior, prognostic risk assessment, treatment optimization, and the development of innovative therapeutic strategies ().

Existing articles have addressed AI-enabled multi-omics integration in pan-cancer research, neoadjuvant chemotherapy prediction, digital pathology, radiomics, multimodal data fusion, foundation models, and breast-cancer screening (). However, most prior work is either modality-specific, task-specific, or pan-cancer in scope. The present review complements these prior efforts by taking a breast-cancer-specific and clinically oriented evidence-appraisal perspective, with particular emphasis on evidence quality, external validation, clinical maturity, regulatory readiness, and translational feasibility across radiomics, pathomics, genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, liquid biopsy, single-cell sequencing, and spatial transcriptomics (). compares the scope of recent reviews and highlights the specific contributions of the present review.

Methods: literature search and selection strategy

This study was conducted as a structured narrative review of artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled multi-omics and multimodal research in breast cancer. PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, IEEE Xplore, and Google Scholar were searched for relevant English-language publications available up to April 2026. The search combined terms related to breast cancer, AI methods, data modalities, clinical applications, and clinical translation. Representative search terms included “breast cancer,” “artificial intelligence,” “machine learning,” “deep learning,” “multi-omics,” “multimodal data,” “radiomics,” “pathomics,” “genomics,” “transcriptomics,” “proteomics,” “metabolomics,” “liquid biopsy,” “single-cell sequencing,” “spatial transcriptomics,” “immunotherapy,” “neoadjuvant therapy,” “pathological complete response,” “drug sensitivity,” “prognosis,” “external validation,” and “clinical translation.”

Original research articles were considered when they examined AI-based analysis or integration of molecular, imaging, pathological, spatial, or clinical data in relation to breast cancer diagnosis, molecular classification, treatment-response prediction, immunotherapy stratification, drug sensitivity, prognosis, or clinical implementation. Relevant reviews and methodological studies were used to provide context regarding multimodal integration, model validation, reproducibility, and interpretability. Reporting guidelines and regulatory documents were also considered when discussing evidence quality, governance, and clinical implementation. Publications were excluded if they were unrelated to breast cancer, focused exclusively on non-oncological applications, lacked sufficient methodological information for interpretation, or did not address a clinically or biologically relevant application of AI-enabled omics or multimodal analysis.

Because this was a narrative rather than a systematic review, studies were selected for their relevance to the scope and clinical questions of the article rather than through a formal meta-analytic selection framework. Greater emphasis was placed on studies with independent external validation, multicenter cohorts, clearly defined and clinically meaningful endpoints, or clear translational relevance. Exploratory and proof-of-concept studies were also included when they represented important developments in emerging areas, particularly single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and metabolomics; however, these studies were interpreted separately from evidence supported by external, multicenter, or prospective validation.

During narrative synthesis, the maturity of the evidence was considered in relation to study design, cohort size, validation setting, endpoint relevance, reproducibility, and proximity to prospective clinical implementation. Retrospective, single-center, or internally validated studies were not interpreted as providing the same level of evidence as multicenter, externally validated, or prospective investigations. Where reported, attention was also paid to potential overfitting, population bias, model calibration, and the transparency of model development and validation.

Because the included studies differed substantially in data modality, patient population, clinical endpoint, AI architecture, and validation strategy, a quantitative meta-analysis and direct comparison of reported performance metrics were not considered appropriate. The evidence was therefore synthesized narratively, with particular attention to methodological quality, validation status, potential bias, generalizability, clinical utility, translational maturity, and barriers to implementation.

Fundamentals of AI-driven multi-omics integration strategies and algorithms

The core of multi-omics research is integration. The effective combination of structured genetic data with unstructured imaging data depends on appropriate AI model design (). Before data are entered into AI models, information from different sources requires rigorous preprocessing and feature engineering to reduce technical noise and extract clinically meaningful features ().

Batch-effect correction reduces non-biological variation generated across sequencing platforms, laboratories, and experimental batches (). Although ComBat is widely used, its performance may be limited when data from different cohorts, such as TCGA and METABRIC, are integrated (). Emerging AI approaches, including ComBat-seq for RNA-seq count data and generative adversarial network (GAN)-based denoising algorithms, are increasingly used to preserve biological signals more effectively (). Tools such as SPIRAL combine GraphSAGE with Wasserstein-distance domain adaptation to align feature distributions across batches. Missing-value imputation is another key step because multi-omics datasets often contain incomplete layers, such as absent proteomic profiles for some patients. Deep generative models, including MIWAE, and Transformer-based imputation methods can infer missing values from other omics layers and often outperform simple mean imputation (; ). Beyond batch effects and missing-value imputation, broader data-quality challenges are critical determinants of the validity of AI-enabled multi-omics models (). Inter-pathologist variability may affect diagnostic labels, tumor-region delineation, tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte assessment, and annotation masks used for pathomics model training (). Incomplete clinical metadata, including treatment sequence, dose intensity, recurrence status, follow-up duration, and subsequent lines of therapy, may introduce unmeasured confounding and compromise cross-cohort comparability (). Inconsistent definitions of clinically meaningful endpoints, such as pathological complete response, progression-free survival, disease-free survival, and overall survival, further hinder the interpretation and benchmarking of model performance across studies (). Technical heterogeneity also extends well beyond sequencing batch effects. Differences in sequencing chemistry, library preparation, tissue fixation, slide preparation, staining protocols, scanner calibration, imaging acquisition protocols, reconstruction parameters, segmentation procedures, and radiomics feature-extraction pipelines can substantially alter model inputs (). If these factors are not systematically documented, harmonized, and controlled, AI models may capture institution-specific artifacts rather than reproducible disease biology. Therefore, standardized metadata collection, predefined quality-control workflows, preregistered clinical endpoints, transparent reporting of missingness, and detailed documentation of acquisition and preprocessing parameters should be regarded as minimum requirements for robust, reproducible, and clinically translatableAI-driven multi-omics research ().

Multi-omics integration strategies: from early fusion to deep interaction

According to the stage at which data fusion occurs, AI architectures can be broadly divided into three strategies with distinct applications in breast cancer research. The first is early fusion, also known as concatenation (). This approach directly concatenates feature vectors from different omics layers into a high-dimensional input vector. For example, genomic, transcriptomic, pathological, and clinical features can be combined after feature selection to predict response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (). However, early fusion ignores distributional differences among modalities and is susceptible to the curse of dimensionality, in which high-dimensional omics data can overwhelm lower-dimensional clinical variables (). The second strategy is late fusion, or decision-level fusion, in which separate models are trained for each modality and their predictions are aggregated by voting or weighted averaging. This design is robust to missing modalities and allows each data type to be analyzed with an appropriate algorithm, such as CNNs for images and multilayer perceptrons for gene-expression data (). Its main limitation is the reduced ability to capture intermodal interactions, such as associations between specific mutations and imaging phenotypes (; ). The third strategy is intermediate, or feature-level, fusion. In this approach, modality-specific subnetworks first extract low-dimensional embeddings, which are then integrated within intermediate network layers (). Multimodal autoencoders can map different omics layers into a shared latent space for clustering or classification (; ), whereas moBRCA-net incorporates biological interaction information during feature extraction (). Tensor fusion explicitly models pairwise interactions by computing the outer product of feature vectors from different modalities () and may be useful for identifying gene–image associations ().

As bioinformatics increasingly adopts network-based approaches, graph neural networks (GNNs) and Transformers have become important algorithms for multi-omics fusion. GNNs are particularly well suited to non-Euclidean data such as patient similarity networks (PSNs). For example, MOGONET and the Multi-omics Stacked Fusion Network (MSFN) construct graphs in which nodes represent patients and edges represent omics-based similarity (; ). Through graph convolutions, GNNs propagate label information among similar patients and leverage the principle of homophily to improve classification accuracy. In breast cancer survival prediction, MSFN combines residual GCNs with CNNs and outperforms single-omics models (). GNNs can also incorporate biological prior knowledge. The GAIN-BRCA model uses miRNA–mRNA interactions and CpG–mRNA regulatory relationships to construct a graph and learn node weights. This model achieved an AUROC of 0.98 for breast cancer subtype classification, outperformed traditional methods, and identified KRAS as a key prognostic gene in the Luminal A subtype (; ).

The Transformer architecture is centered on self-attention, which dynamically weights the features most relevant to a given prediction task (). In drug-response prediction, such models can learn to prioritize gene-expression modules associated with specific imaging features (). For kinase-driven cancers such as HER2-positive breast cancer, attention mechanisms may assign greater weight to omics layers related to kinase signaling pathways (; ). Transformers can also treat genomic sequences as text-like inputs, capturing long-range genomic dependencies and improving interpretation of the functional consequences of non-coding mutations ().

Applications of AI combined with omics in the precision diagnosis of breast cancer

summarizes the relative strengths, translational maturity, and representative reported performance of major data modalities used in AI-enabled breast cancer research.

Radiomics

Radiomics-based models have shown promise, mainly in retrospective and multicohort studies, for non-invasively predicting response to immune checkpoint blockade and supporting exploratory biomarker-based patient stratification (). In mammography, deep-learning models have achieved performance comparable to radiologists for detecting breast masses and calcifications, and multimodal models that incorporate ultrasound images can further improve diagnostic sensitivity (). In MRI research, models integrating multiparametric MRI data have shown high sensitivity for both lesion segmentation and detection (). Deep-learning systems applied to mammographic X-ray images can reduce false-positive findings while maintaining radiologist-level accuracy (). In one study, the system reportedly reduced false-positive and false-negative rates by 17.8% and 5.7%, respectively. Kakileti et al. developed an AI-based thermal-imaging risk prediction framework in which the Thermalytix Risk Score (TRS) achieved an AUC of 0.89, outperforming age-based risk assessment (AUC 0.68) (). This noninvasive and radiation-free approach may be particularly valuable for women with dense breast tissue. By automatically extracting image features, radiomics can identify subtle abnormalities that are difficult to detect visually (). For example, AI can analyze mammograms to identify tumors and calcifications, supporting more efficient diagnosis (). In MRI analysis, radiomics can quantify tumor shape, texture, margins, and heterogeneity.

MRI-based radiomics has shown particular promise for predicting responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors. FDG-PET/CT-based radiomics models can predict TNBC subtypes with high sensitivity and specificity ().

Multimodal imaging integration uses AI algorithms to combine complementary imaging modalities and thereby improve predictive accuracy (). Multimodal AI systems can integrate mammography, ultrasound, and MRI data for breast cancer diagnosis (). One such system achieved an overall AUC of 0.94 and outperformed single-modality approaches. In addition, a cost-effectiveness analysis by a Singapore-based early health technology assessment suggested that AI-based risk prediction followed by selective screening of high-risk women may be cost-effective ().

Genomics

AI algorithms have improved the performance of polygenic risk scores (PRSs) for breast cancer risk assessment (). An MRI-integrated AI prediction system developed by Li et al. can noninvasively predict the final postoperative residual cancer burden (RCB) grade after two cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy and maintains stable diagnostic performance across the three major molecular subtypes of breast cancer. These findings suggest that invasive genomic biomarkers and noninvasive imaging-based models can complement each other in personalized decision-making for neoadjuvant breast cancer therapy (). When combined with clinical risk models, PRSs improved net reclassification by 5%–17% across ancestry groups. Machine-learning models also show promise for predicting BRCA1/2 mutations without direct genetic testing. Park H et al. developed a multimodal AI model integrating clinical, imaging, and genomic data to predict BRCA mutations with an AUC of 0.85 (). The model identified imaging features associated with BRCA mutations, including increased breast density and structural distortion.

AI algorithms have contributed to the identification of immunogenomic features associated with responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (). Current multi-omics machine-learning models can integrate genomic, transcriptomic, and pathological features to predict pathological complete response (pCR) to neoadjuvant therapy (). In an external validation cohort, one model achieved an AUC of 0.87, outperforming established biomarkers.

Proteomics

Proteomics provides a direct readout of cellular and organismal functional states. In early breast cancer screening, AI-assisted deep proteomics is helping to address the technical challenge posed by the broad dynamic range of blood biomarkers (). AI-driven proteomics has accelerated the discovery of novel blood-based biomarkers for breast cancer (). Lebrecht et al. further demonstrated the diagnostic potential of tear proteomics. Using surface-enhanced laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry and artificial neural networks, they identified a 20-biomarker panel with 71.19% sensitivity and 70.69% specificity, suggesting a potential noninvasive screening approach ().

Spatial transcriptomics

Recent advances in spatial transcriptomics provide a systematic framework for defining the physical localization of TME components and generating spatially resolved information relevant to treatment (). At single-cell, whole-transcriptome resolution, Spotiphy has revealed spatial and domain-specific differences in tumor–immune interactions within the breast cancer TME (). The intensity of these interactions and the activation of immune-related signaling pathways vary across tumor regions, with pathways related to cell migration and immune evasion specifically upregulated in some spatial domains (). These findings provide spatial evidence for mechanisms of immune evasion in breast cancer (). They also challenge the conventional view that the tumor core is uniformly the most immunosuppressive region, suggesting that the core may have a strong capacity to recruit CD8+ T cells (). These CD8+ T cells can penetrate the collagen matrix through COL6A3–ITGB1 ligand–receptor interactions and form a honeycomb-like immune defense structure within the tumor core. Such spatial patterns may help explain heterogeneous immunotherapy responses among patients with the same pathological subtype and may support the identification of responsive subgroups and therapeutic targets. In addition, Spotiphy identified an FSTL1+ fibroblast subpopulation whose spatial clustering pattern correlated with the efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors, suggesting a potential spatial biomarker for immunotherapy response ().

Starfysh integrates spatial transcriptomics with histological data to reveal heterogeneous tumor–immune hubs. Using deep generative modeling and archetype analysis, it characterizes tissue-specific cellular states without requiring single-cell reference data. In breast cancer, Starfysh identified spatial hubs composed of patient- and disease-specific cell populations, showing how metabolic reprogramming can shape immunosuppressive niches in aggressive disease (). Spatial single-cell transcriptomic analysis has also identified potential biomarkers for PD-1 blockade. Using Xenium technology to analyze the TME across breast cancer subtypes, investigators found CD274/CD80 interactions between macrophages and T cells and observed that effector T cells, proliferating T cells, and macrophages were located closer to tumor cells in responders. These features may serve as biomarkers for immunotherapy response.

Liquid biopsy

Traditional tissue biopsies are invasive and provide only a static snapshot from a localized tumor region (); they cannot fully capture spatial heterogeneity or dynamically monitor resistance mechanisms driven by clonal selection during treatment (). AI-integrated liquid biopsy is reshaping approaches to early screening and recurrence monitoring in breast cancer (). Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) provides a minimally invasive window into the tumor genomic landscape (). AI-assisted computational models may enhance ctDNA-based treatment monitoring by integrating longitudinal molecular signals with clinical response data. In metastatic breast cancer, shallow whole-genome sequencing of ctDNA combined with Bayesian machine-learning modeling has been shown to predict treatment response and progression-free survival, outperforming CA15-3-based assessment. In the neoadjuvant setting, serial ctDNA dynamics and AI-based quantification of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes may provide complementary information for predicting pathological response and post-treatment risk stratification (). Machine-learning methods have also identified miRNA signatures with high diagnostic accuracy; for example, an 8-miRNA panel achieved an AUC of 0.915, 82.3% accuracy, 72.2% sensitivity, and 91.5% specificity for breast cancer detection (). AI algorithms can further distinguish cancer types and predict drug efficacy, supporting personalized medicine (). summarizes representative omics and AI-enabled multimodal studies in breast cancer, including their clinical relevance, evidence status, and translational limitations.

Applications of AI in predicting immunotherapy response and drug sensitivity

Pathomics

Breast cancer prognosis assessment and drug-sensitivity prediction no longer rely solely on TNM staging. By analyzing pathological images, single-cell microenvironmental differences, spatial cell-distribution patterns, and metabolic changes, AI can support multidimensional precision prediction systems (). In BioFusionNet, the integration of histopathological, genetic, and clinical data increased the C-statistic for survival prediction to 0.77, demonstrating the value of combining AI-derived histopathological features with clinical variables (). AI models can also predict molecular subtypes directly from hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained images. BBMIL, a deep-learning algorithm developed for this purpose, predicts classical biomarkers, immunotherapy-related genomic features, and prognosis-related subtypes with high accuracy and outperforms existing methods in independent cohorts ().

Single-cell sequencing

Traditional bulk sequencing can obscure cellular heterogeneity within tumors. By combining AI with scRNA-seq, researchers can characterize differences among TME cell subpopulations and improve drug-sensitivity prediction (). PERCEPTION (Personalized Single-Cell Expression-Based Planning for Treatments in Oncology) is a transfer-learning AI model. It is first pretrained on large cell-line drug-response datasets, such as GDSC, to learn general relationships between transcriptomic features and drug sensitivity (), and is then fine-tuned with patient scRNA-seq data to address the limited availability of single-cell clinical datasets. A major strength of PERCEPTION is its ability to detect residual drug-resistant subclones. Even when a tumor appears broadly sensitive to a drug, the presence of a small resistant subclone may predict poor outcome (; ). In a breast cancer cohort, the model tracked resistance evolution during treatment and predicted treatment-failure risk for agents such as CDK4/6 inhibitors (). AI analysis has also shown that intercellular ligand–receptor communication networks are important determinants of prognosis and drug sensitivity (). For example, AI-driven clustering in TNBC identified immune-active (Clust1) and immune-exhausted (Clust2) microenvironmental subtypes ().

Spatial transcriptomics

Spatial transcriptomics adds spatial coordinates to gene-expression data. AI applications in this area mainly aim to define spatial cell-distribution patterns and clarify their influence on treatment response (). Computational analysis of spatial transcriptomic data has been used to identify therapeutic clusters in breast cancer, defined by spatially resolved predicted drug-sensitivity profiles (). Drug-sensitivity scores, such as the Beyondcell Score, correlate with radial distance from the tumor core. Peripheral tumor cells often have better blood supply and greater immune infiltration, making them more sensitive to certain therapies, whereas core-region cells are more frequently hypoxic and drug resistant (). AI models also suggest that tumor clones with identical genotypes may show markedly different drug sensitivities because of their spatial context, such as localization within ducts or stroma ().

Metabolomics

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer. In metabolomics, AI-based approaches assess treatment sensitivity by analyzing the metabolic profiles of tumor tissues (). For example, doxorubicin-resistant TNBC cells exhibit marked metabolic rewiring, with clear differences in metabolic intensity and pathway activity compared with sensitive cells. These differences include accumulation of long-chain acylcarnitines and reduction of short- and medium-chain acylcarnitines (). This pattern suggests decoupling between glycolysis and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) (), in which resistant cells incompletely degrade fatty acids and accumulate intermediate metabolites (). Such metabolic alterations can help AI models distinguish sensitive from resistant strains with high accuracy (). AI analysis also revealed that metabolite levels in sensitive cells fluctuate with drug concentration, whereas resistant cells exhibit a rigid or locked metabolic state that no longer responds dynamically to drug stimulation (). This metabolic rigidity may indicate poor prognosis (). Algorithms such as Random Forest and LightGBM (), together with multi-omics feature selection (MOFS), can be used to construct metabolite-based predictive models. AI has identified glutamate, acetylcarnitine, and choline phosphate as key predictors of drug response (). For example, choline phosphate is higher in sensitive cells but decreases as resistance develops (). These findings suggest that monitoring metabolic activity in blood or tissue may enable dynamic assessment of treatment sensitivity (). The integration of SHAP explainability with metabolomics links computational predictions to biological interpretation and improves clinical utility (). XAI-driven metabolomic machine learning may therefore improve breast cancer diagnosis while revealing metabolic dysregulation associated with disease progression ().

Technical bottlenecks and information silos in omics-based assessment of breast cancer immunotherapy

Clinical maturity should be interpreted unevenly across AI technologies. AI assistance for mammographic screening and selected digital-pathology tasks is closest to routine implementation because these workflows use standardized clinical inputs, established diagnostic endpoints, and increasingly defined quality-management processes (). In contrast, spatial transcriptomics-guided prediction, single-cell AI drug-response modeling, and multi-omics immunotherapy-response systems remain emerging research technologies (; ). These approaches provide mechanistic insight and may identify candidate biomarkers, but they generally require fresh or specialized tissue processing, complex computational pipelines, longer turnaround times, and prospective evidence that treatment decisions based on model outputs improve outcomes ().

Although single-omics technologies have substantially advanced the molecular characterization of breast cancer and the development of targeted therapies, single-dimensional approaches face important bottlenecks when applied to the complex TME and heterogeneous immune responses to immunotherapy (; ). Single-omics data therefore have intrinsic limitations. Owing to their different detection principles, each omics layer captures only part of the biological system (; ):

Genomics: whole-exome sequencing (WES) can identify TMB and specific mutations such as BRCA1/2 and PIK3CA, but static DNA-level alterations cannot be directly equated with protein expression or functional pathway activation (; ). Genomic data alone also cannot adequately reflect post-transcriptional modification and epigenetic regulation, resulting in a genotype–phenotype gap ().

Transcriptomics and Spatial Transcriptomics: There is often a time lag and a nonlinear relationship between RNA levels and the abundance of proteins that ultimately perform biological functions (). Furthermore, while spatial transcriptomics preserves information on the spatial location of cells, it currently faces challenges such as high costs, the need for improved single-cell resolution, and a lack of longitudinal dynamic monitoring capability, making it difficult to implement on a large scale in clinical cohorts ().

Proteomics: As the omics discipline closest to the agents of biological activity, proteomics has a natural advantage in identifying immune targets. However, due to technical limitations (such as the lack of signal amplification techniques similar to PCR) (), it lacks sufficient sensitivity for detecting extremely low-abundance cytokines or immunoregulatory proteins in the TME, and the data are often noisy ().

Radiomics: Radiomics enables non-invasive, macroscopic assessment of the heterogeneity of the entire tumor and its surrounding stroma (); however, it essentially involves the extraction of macroscopic physical features and lacks microscopic resolution at the molecular and cellular levels (). Furthermore, imaging features are highly susceptible to interference from different imaging devices, scanning parameters, and reconstruction algorithms, resulting in poor cross-center generalization capabilities of AI models ().

Liquid biopsy: ctDNA or circulating tumor cells (CTCs) offer non-invasive methods for dynamic monitoring (); however, their abundance is extremely low in patients with early-stage breast cancer, posing a risk of false negatives (). More importantly, molecular signals in the blood lack spatial context, making it difficult to trace the exact spatial origin of mutated clones and their interactions with local immune cells ().

Furthermore, compared to multi-omics, single-omics approaches suffer from more systemic limitations, such as the lack of a comprehensive perspective ()—analogous to a limited, unidimensional view of a complex biological system (). Tumor immunology is a complex system involving multi-level, multi-dimensional interactions. Compared to multi-omics, the central limitation of single-omics lies in its “information silo” effect (). A single omics approach cannot capture the complex biological networks involved (); that is, the efficacy of immunotherapies (such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors) depends not only on target expression but also on regulation by multiple factors, including antigen presentation, metabolic reprogramming, and angiogenesis (). A single omics approach cannot reconstruct the complete biological network spanning from “upstream gene regulation” to “downstream metabolic/phenotypic changes” (). When building AI predictive models, single-omics data often encounter performance bottlenecks due to the limited dimensionality of its features (). For example, relying solely on radiomics or genomics to predict pCR to immunotherapy in breast cancer may lead to misclassification in complex cases (such as immunological pseudo progression) (). AI analysis based on a single data modality may capture “associated” features related to prognosis rather than true “driving” biological mechanisms (; ). Cross-validation across multi-omics data—such as the alignment of imaging features with spatial transcriptomics—is the key approach to eliminating false-positive associations and identifying robust biomarkers ().

AI-driven multimodal data fusion and clinical applications

illustrates complementary combinations of omics and imaging modalities and their potential applications in AI-enabled breast cancer research. In the face of the information silos created by omics data, AI—particularly deep learning and GNNs—has demonstrated strong capabilities in high-dimensional data mapping and feature fusion (). By constructing multimodal fusion networks, AI can break down the scale barriers between the microscopic molecular, mesoscopic cellular, and macroscopic tissue levels, providing a feasible solution for assessing immunotherapy sensitivity and stratifying prognosis, a finding supported by research evidence from other types of cancer (; ).

Spatial transcriptomics combined with single-cell sequencing provides high-resolution insights into the tumor immune microenvironment

The spatial heterogeneity of immune cells in the TME is a key factor determining the response to immunotherapy in breast cancer, particularly in TNBC (). scRNA-seq offers single-cell resolution but lacks spatial coordinates, whereas spatial transcriptomics preserves tissue structure but is limited by cellular mixing at sequencing spots (). AI algorithms based on deconvolution models using graph convolutional networks or autoencoders can bridge the strengths of both approaches, specifically by mapping the characteristics of cell subpopulations defined by scRNA-seq as prior knowledge onto spatial transcriptomics data, AI can accurately infer the absolute abundance and spatial proximity interaction networks of different immune cells (such as exhausted CD8+ T cells and M2 macrophages) within each spatial location (). This multi-omics integration not only enables the precise identification of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) closely associated with immunotherapy response, but also predicts patient sensitivity to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors through spatial communication networks, thereby elevating the distinction between “cold tumors” and “hot tumors” from a simple statistical measure of abundance to the level of spatial topology ().

Proteomic-metabolomic integration reveals functional phenotypes and vulnerabilities in metabolic reprogramming

Genes and transcripts serve as biological instructions, while proteins and metabolites act as “molecular snapshots” that ultimately carry out biological processes and reflect transient phenotypes (). Breast cancer often undergoes significant metabolic reprogramming under immunotherapeutic stress—such as enhanced glycolysis or lipid metabolism abnormalities—which suppresses the function of infiltrating immune cells (). AI, leveraging machine learning frameworks such as multi-module DNNs (e.g., multi-channel variational autoencoders) or orthogonal partial least squares (OPLS), can effectively filter out noise in high-throughput proteomic and metabolomic data to extract “protein-metabolite” co-expression modules that change in concert. This integration not only aids in identifying key metabolic kinases driving immune evasion but also uncovers composite biomarker panels with high clinical accessibility in blood or tissues, providing new metabolic intervention targets for overcoming resistance to immunotherapy ().

Cross-scale prognostic prediction and improved diagnostic performance through the integration of radiomics and pathology

At the macroscopic and microscopic levels of clinical morphology, imaging modalities such as MRI and ultrasound capture the tumor’s overall blood supply, invasion boundaries, and macroscopic heterogeneity (), while digital pathology techniques such as panoramic slide images and WSI provide subcellular-level details on cell morphology, mitotic figures, and stroma-to-tumor ratios (). AI architectures based on Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) and Visual Transformers (ViT) enable deep cascaded fusion of “macroscopic imaging–microscopic pathology” features (). Studies have shown that this cross-scale multimodal AI model significantly outperforms any single-modality approach in predicting pCR and disease-free survival (DFS) following neoadjuvant therapy for breast cancer (). Furthermore, by generating high-precision multimodal risk scores, clinicians can directly plot smoother and more distinct survival curves (Kaplan-Meier survival curves) in analytical environments, enabling non-invasive, efficient preoperative individualized survival stratification and optimizing the clinical decision-making process ().

Genomics and transcriptomics: decoding the immunogenicity of neoantigens and dynamic immune evasion

Genomic sequencing approaches, such as whole-exome sequencing, can reveal the mutational substrate that may give rise to tumor neoantigens (); however, this potential must be translated into actual expression at the RNA level and effectively processed and presented on HLA molecules at the cell surface to trigger substantial T-cell killing ().AI-driven deep learning algorithms combining machine learning with attention-based DNNs perform high-dimensional joint mapping of the static DNA mutation landscape of the genome with the dynamic expression matrix of the transcriptome (). This computational framework integrates gene expression levels, mutation allele frequencies, Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) affinity, and TCR repertoire characteristics (). By leveraging this cross-omics data dimensionality reduction and feature alignment, the AI can filter out large amounts of “invalid mutation” noise—such as unexpressed or non-presentable mutations—significantly improving the accuracy of neoantigen prediction (reducing false positives), but also systematically elucidated how tumor cells achieve immune evasion through post-transcriptional modifications or loss of heterozygosity (LOH) in HLA (). The integration of this dimension represents an important computational foundation for developing personalized mRNA cancer vaccines for breast cancer and identifying potential beneficiaries of immune checkpoint inhibitors (; ). compares AI integration strategies for breast cancer multi-omics and multimodal analysis, highlighting their applications, advantages, limitations, and clinical readiness.

AI-driven multi-omics analysis for predicting immunotherapy response and drug-resistance mechanisms

The key challenge in current breast cancer immunotherapy is predicting which patients will benefit from PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors (; ). AI-based composite models may complement conventional biomarkers such as TMB and PD-L1 expression, but their role in breast cancer remains investigational and requires prospective validation (). The LORIS multimodal predictive model is an interpretable AI model based on logistic regression that integrates clinical features (age, stage), TMB, and specific genomic features. Although LORIS was developed and validated in a pan-cancer setting rather than breast cancer-specific cohorts, it illustrates how interpretable multimodal models may complement TMB and PD-L1 for ICB-response prediction. LORIS outperformed TMB alone in predicting response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). LORIS outperformed TMB, the PD-L1 tumor proportion score, and several previous machine learning models on nearly all metrics across both internal and external datasets; both TMB and the PD-L1 tumor proportion score are known biomarkers of ICB efficacy (). More importantly, as a “white-box” model, LORIS clearly demonstrates the contribution of each feature to the final risk score, significantly enhancing clinicians’ confidence in the model. In the context of neoadjuvant therapy, a multi-omics integrated machine learning model—developed by combining tumor mutation, immune cell infiltration, and pathological image features—successfully predicted the pCR rate of TNBC patients receiving immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy (). The model may help identify patients who are more likely to benefit from treatment and generate hypotheses for future combination strategies ().

The metabolic environment acts as a “regulator” of immune cell function. By analyzing metabolomics data, an AI model has uncovered the metabolic roots of immune resistance in breast cancer (). By analyzing plasma metabolomic profiles using deep learning, researchers identified a set of metabolic biomarkers comprising polyamines, glycerophosphocholine, and pentosidine. Abnormal levels of these metabolites were highly correlated with residual disease (RCB-II/III) following neoadjuvant chemotherapy. In particular, the accumulation of polyamines is known to inhibit T-cell proliferation and function (; ). The “necrotic margin” feature identified by the radiomic model is, in fact, a macroscopic manifestation of tumor hypoxia. Hypoxia leads to the activation of the HIF-1α pathway and the accumulation of adenosine, which directly suppresses the activity of cytotoxic T cells. By integrating imaging-derived hypoxia features with metabolic data, the AI model can accurately identify patient subgroups that require combined anti-hypoxia therapy (such as adenosine receptor antagonists) (). provides an overview of the links between data modalities, AI integration strategies, clinical endpoints, and requirements for clinical translation in breast cancer.

Discussion: critical appraisal and translational challenges

Although the integration of AI and multi-omics has shown substantial potential in breast cancer targeted therapy and immunotherapy research, its translation into routine clinical decision-support tools still faces major challenges across multiple domains (). At present, the translation gap is mainly related not only to technical and regulatory barriers, but also to the methodological quality, reproducibility, and generalizability of existing AI-enabled multi-omics studies (). Therefore, beyond summarizing reported model performance, it is necessary to critically evaluate cohort size, validation strategy, overfitting risk, potential bias, clinical utility, and translational readiness ().

Critical appraisal of study quality and generalizability

A key limitation of current AI-enabled multi-omics studies in breast cancer is that many models are developed using retrospective datasets, relatively small cohorts, or single-center populations. This issue is particularly important for high-dimensional omics and deep-learning models, in which the number of molecular, imaging, pathological, or spatial features may substantially exceed the number of available patients (). Under these conditions, models may achieve high performance in internal validation while learning cohort-specific noise, platform effects, or preprocessing artifacts rather than stable biological signals (). Therefore, reported performance metrics such as accuracy, AUC, or C-index should be interpreted cautiously unless they are supported by independent external validation, calibration analysis, and clinically meaningful endpoints ().

External validation and reproducibility remain inconsistent across the field.AI models often perform exceptionally well on training datasets but see a sharp drop in performance on external datasets. This is primarily due to the technical batch effect. Domain Adversarial Adaptation designs an adversarial network to force the feature extractor to learn features driven by biological differences across different datasets, thereby ignoring variations caused by technical platforms (such as Illumina and Ion Torrent). This approach has demonstrated significant improvements in robustness in breast cancer subtyping across the TCGA and METABRIC cohorts178. Next-generation AI models (such as Visual Neural Networks) map neural network layers one-to-one with biological pathways (such as the apoptosis pathway and the PI3K pathway). When the model predicts a “high risk,” it can simultaneously identify which signaling pathway is causing the abnormality, thereby directly guiding targeted drug therapy52,179.

Nevertheless, improving generalizability requires more than technical domain adaptation (). Many published models still lack transparent reporting of data preprocessing, feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, missing-data handling, and model calibration. In addition, potential sources of bias may arise from imbalanced subtype distribution, under-representation of racial or ethnic groups, differences in treatment access, and heterogeneity in endpoint definitions (). Future studies should therefore place greater emphasis on multicenter external validation, prospective testing, subgroup performance assessment, reproducible analytical pipelines, and reporting of model failure modes. These steps are essential for determining whether AI-enabled multi-omics models can generalize across institutions, populations, assay platforms, and real-world clinical workflows ().

Modality-specific limitations and translational maturity

The translational maturity of AI-enabled multi-omics integration varies substantially across data modalities (). Conventional imaging and radiomics are relatively scalable because standard clinical imaging is already widely available, but their reproducibility depends on imaging protocols, scanner type, reconstruction parameters, segmentation methods, and feature-extraction pipelines (). Digital pathology and pathomics provide rich information on tissue architecture, tumor morphology, stromal features, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and biomarker-adjacent patterns, but their generalizability may be affected by staining variability, slide scanners, tissue processing, annotation strategies, and laboratory workflows ().

Bulk genomics and transcriptomics are more clinically established for selected assays and molecular classifiers, but broad AI-based multi-omics fusion remains less mature (). High-dimensional molecular features combined with limited cohort sizes increase the risk of overfitting and unstable feature selection. Proteomics and metabolomics can provide information closer to functional tumor phenotypes, but they are often limited by high cost, preanalytical variability, platform-specific effects, and smaller validation cohorts (). Liquid biopsy offers minimally invasive and repeatable sampling for longitudinal monitoring, but AI-based liquid-biopsy models may be influenced by tumor-shedding heterogeneity, assay sensitivity, sampling time, and disease stage. Single-cell and spatial omics provide high-resolution information on cell states, spatial niches, tumor immune interactions, and treatment-resistance mechanisms, but they remain predominantly exploratory because of high cost, low throughput, analytical complexity, sparse data, and limited clinical standardization ().

The high cost of multi-omics testing, particularly spatial transcriptomics and deep metagenomics, has limited its broad adoption in routine breast cancer screening and recurrence monitoring (). Patients with advanced breast cancer may also require rapid treatment decisions before immunotherapy, whereas the turnaround time for sample collection, high-throughput sequencing, multimodal data cleaning, and generation of an AI-based prediction report may not meet clinical time constraints (). Balancing high-dimensional accuracy with clinical timeliness is therefore a practical requirement for implementation ().

From an implementation perspective, cost and turnaround time should be considered alongside model accuracy. Standardized sampling, streamlined sequencing workflows, automated quality control, and clinically interpretable reporting pipelines may help reduce delays, but these solutions require prospective evaluation before they can be incorporated into routine decision-making (; ). Therefore, the clinical value of AI-driven multi-omics should be judged not only by predictive performance but also by feasibility, reproducibility, and timeliness ().

Clinical implementation, regulation, and governance

Liability, bias, and consent also require explicit planning. AI-assisted decisions may involve shared responsibility among clinicians, hospitals, software developers, laboratories, and device manufacturers, particularly when a model recommends treatment escalation or de-escalation, or biomarker-guided therapy selection (). In practice, responsibility boundaries should be defined before deployment, including who reviews model outputs, who may override recommendations, how disagreements are documented, and how adverse decisions are audited. Bias across racial or ethnic, geographic, socioeconomic, and genetic-ancestry groups can arise from under-representation in genomic datasets, differences in screening access, local treatment patterns, and heterogeneity in clinical endpoint definitions (). Therefore, subgroup performance, calibration, uncertainty, and failure modes should be evaluated across clinically relevant populations before broad deployment. Data governance should therefore include federated or privacy-preserving learning where appropriate. It should also incorporate audit trails, data-use agreements, role-based access control, re-consent or broad consent for secondary multi-omics analysis, and patient-facing explanations of how samples, imaging data, molecular profiles and clinical records may be used to train or validate AI systems ().

Regulatory and governance considerations are equally important. From the perspective of AI-based medical devices, regulatory agencies increasingly emphasize the intended use of the model, predefined change-control plans, risk management, human factors, and real-world performance monitoring (). These expectations are consistent with lifecycle oversight rather than one-time approval, because multi-omics models may drift when sequencing platforms, imaging protocols, tissue-processing workflows, patient ancestry, treatment patterns, or endpoint definitions change. Cybersecurity should also be addressed as part of medical-device software design, labeling, and premarket documentation (). The FDA framework for AI-enabled device software and predetermined change control plans highlights that AI-enabled devices intended to be modified over time require clear boundaries for future modification, while the EMA and broader European regulatory framework similarly stresses lifecycle oversight, data quality, transparency, accountability, and human oversight, together with a risk-based approach to model use. For breast-cancer multi-omics AI, these requirements imply that a model should have a locked clinical indication, documented training data provenance, prespecified update procedures, monitoring for algorithmic drift, periodic recalibration or revalidation when input distributions shift, post-deployment performance surveillance, audit trails for model outputs, and a mechanism for clinician override. Together, these safeguards are necessary to ensure that AI-enabled breast-cancer multi-omics models remain clinically auditable, ethically responsible, and aligned with real-world clinical workflows ().

Omics data involves patient privacy, and the volume of data from a single center is often insufficient to train large deep learning models, federated learning enables collaborative model training through the exchange of model parameters without sharing raw data (; ). The ProCanFDL project demonstrates the application of federated learning to proteomics data, successfully training a cancer subtype classifier that outperforms models from individual centers while protecting privacy. This is particularly important for collecting data on rare breast cancer subtypes (). summarizes key challenges in the clinical application of AI-enabled multi-omics and potential strategies to address them.

For complex pathological and high-dimensional imaging data, explainable AI (XAI) algorithms play an important role (). In a DNN-based study of early breast cancer detection that achieved 99.2% accuracy, high accuracy alone was insufficient to ensure clinician confidence. SHAP and lime were therefore incorporated to quantify the contribution of individual features to each prediction. For example, SHAP analysis indicated that the “concave points” feature had the strongest positive influence on malignancy prediction, translating abstract model weights into interpretable morphological guidance ().

Future directions for robust clinical translation

Future development of AI-enabled multi-omics integration in breast cancer should move from performance-driven model development toward clinically robust evidence generation (). First, studies should prioritize larger and more diverse multicenter cohorts, with predefined clinical endpoints and adequate representation of molecular subtypes, disease stages, treatment settings, and demographic groups (). Second, model development should include rigorous strategies to reduce overfitting, including appropriate separation of training, validation, and test sets; nested cross-validation where feasible; transparent feature selection; regularization; calibration assessment; and independent external validation (). Third, reproducibility should be improved through standardized reporting of preprocessing pipelines, model architectures, hyperparameters, software versions, feature definitions, and missing-data handling ().

Fourth, future studies should evaluate clinical utility rather than relying only on discrimination metrics. Decision-curve analysis, calibration, subgroup analysis, prospective validation, workflow-impact assessment, cost-effectiveness analysis, and reporting of failure modes will be important for determining whether AI models can improve clinical decision-making. Fifth, privacy-preserving and collaborative learning strategies, including federated learning, may help overcome data-silo barriers while protecting patient privacy (). Finally, regulatory-grade AI models should be developed with locked intended use, transparent data provenance, lifecycle monitoring, mechanisms for clinician override, and post-deployment surveillance. These directions may help shift AI-enabled breast-cancer multi-omics integration from exploratory modeling toward clinically reliable decision support ().

Conclusion

This review summarizes progress in AI-driven multi-omics technologies for predicting immunotherapy response, elucidating resistance mechanisms, and assessing prognosis in breast cancer. It covers multimodal integration strategies involving radiomics, genomics, proteomics, spatial transcriptomics, and liquid biopsy, and discusses key barriers to clinical translation, including data privacy, batch effects, model interpretability, cost, and workflow integration.

This review contributes to the field by synthesizing evidence across four dimensions. First, it addresses an important area at the intersection of AI and breast cancer multi-omics, whereas many previous reviews have focused on single-modality AI applications or broader summaries of AI in oncology. Second, it highlights how multi-omics AI models may improve patient stratification and treatment-response prediction beyond single biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression or tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, thereby helping to identify patients most likely to benefit from immunotherapy and reducing ineffective treatment. Third, it outlines potential translation strategies, including federated learning and domain-adaptive learning, which may mitigate privacy and generalizability barriers. Fourth, it identifies persistent gaps, including insufficient interpretability, limited prospective validation, and the absence of standardized multicenter datasets, which should be prioritized in future research.

In summary, AI-driven integration of multi-omics data provides a promising framework for breast cancer diagnosis, immunotherapy-response prediction, and prognostic assessment. By combining large-scale datasets with machine-learning and foundation-model approaches, AI may reduce some sources of human error and subjective variability and support more standardized diagnostic and prognostic workflows. In clinical practice, standardized operating procedures will be needed to clarify how AI models generate diagnoses, risk predictions, and treatment recommendations. Future work should strengthen translational research by incorporating larger and more diverse datasets, improving reproducibility, conducting prospective clinical trials, and establishing regulatory frameworks for ethical and safe AI use. These efforts will be essential for integrating AI into routine breast cancer care, improving patient outcomes, and reducing clinician workload. At the same time, the field should avoid premature claims of clinical readiness: most single-cell, spatial-omics, and high-dimensional fusion models remain discovery tools until they demonstrate reproducibility, calibration, cost-effectiveness, fairness, and benefit in prospective clinical workflows.
