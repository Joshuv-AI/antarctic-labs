Proteomic analysis of dental enamel from 20 Homo naledi individuals shows no male markers
Authors: Palesa P Madupe, Alberto J Taurozzi, Claire Koenig, Ioannis Patramanis, Fazeelah Munir, Marc R Dickinson, Meaghan Mackie, Gaudry Troché, Glendon Parker, Pelagia Kyriakidou, Patrick Mahoney, Gina McFarlane, Bernhard Zipfel, Jürgen Cox, Kirsty Penkman, Lauren Schroeder, Rebecca R Ackermann, Jesper V Olsen, John Hawks, Lee Berger, Enrico Cappellini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe Rising Star cave system excavations resulted in a high number of well-preserved skeletal specimens from multiple individuals of Homo naledi, showing a high degree of morphological homogeneity, including dental variation possibly consistent with a single-sex sample. Here, we report the paleoproteomic analysis of dental enamel proteins extracted via micro-destructive acid etching from 23 H. naledi specimens belonging to a minimum of 20 individuals. After excluding the possibility of technical bias, no convincing evidence supporting the confident identification of male individuals was detected in any of the investigated samples. We also detect no variability in the recovered proteome, and we observe two amino acid substitutions: a derived one in amelogenin X compared with Homo, and an ancestral one in COL17A1, also present in Paranthropus robustus. Our results further support the homogeneity of H. naledi fossils and show how to sustainably investigate extinct hominins.Keywords: paleoproteomics, South Africa, amelogenin, sex attribution, acid etching, sex bias, Rising Star cave system

Introduction

Beginning in 2013, fossils assigned to the species Homo naledi were recovered from the Dinaledi Chamber inside the Rising Star cave system in the Cradle of Humankind, South Africa (Figures 1A–1C). Based on dental remains, this fossil record represents at least 15 individuals.2 Since then, additional H. naledi remains have been found in the same and in other chambers of the cave system, ranging from infants to older adults,3,4 further expanding the sample of individuals known for this species. Some of these remains have been dated to 335–236 ka (kilo annum/thousand years),5,6 although the estimated range for some specimens may be a wider interval (±70 ka).5 Among hominins, H. naledi is unusual for several reasons. The morphology of this species is a mosaic, combining a small brain, shoulders, and trunk similar to early hominins such as Australopithecus, with a more Homo-like face, hands, and lower limbs.2 Intriguingly, the collection is also remarkably homogeneous, with little skeletal size or morphological variation among the adult remains.2,3,7,8 A recent study of dental morphological variation also found that the homogeneity of H. naledi material may indicate a single sex, as evidenced by low size variation in teeth.9 However, the accuracy of morphology-based sex estimation can be greatly influenced by sex ratios and by the level of intersexual variability within species, factors that are difficult to establish for extinct taxa due in part to small sample sizes.10 The challenges of estimating the skeletal sex of ancient individuals, especially those in deep time, are further compounded by the degradation and incompleteness of skeletal remains.

Sex determination using ancient DNA (aDNA) has been successful for individuals with both high and low genomic coverage.11,12 However, aDNA preservation is limited by diagenesis, making this approach generally unfeasible in warmer climates across extended timescales.13,14,15 The only biomolecular solution currently viable to sex fossil specimens unamenable to aDNA analysis uses mass spectrometry (MS)-based paleoproteomic analysis of dental enamel and focuses on the detection of peptides unique to amelogenins X and Y (AMELX and AMELY).16,17,18,19,20 The sequences of human AMELX and AMELY differ for 23 diagnostic amino acid substitutions, plus a methionine (M) insertion in AMELY position 59 (Figure S1). AMELX is expressed in both male and female individuals, while ordinarily AMELY is expressed in biological males,21 at a rate that is approximately 10% that of AMELX.22

Enamel sampling for paleoproteomics requires either cutting off a fragment of the biomineralized material or, much less destructively, acid etching its surface.23,24 Proteomics has been successfully used not only to sex Holocene specimens,25,26,27 but also Pleistocene material,17,19,28 even by barely etching the enamel surface.18,20,29 While, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, no ancient biomolecule analysis of H. naledi has been published to date, here we use paleoproteomics to investigate the sex of 23 H. naledi dental enamel specimens originating from a minimum of 20 different individuals (STAR Methods section “sample selection and minimum number of individuals”; Table S1; Figure S2). The H. naledi individuals studied here are from four different areas: Dinaledi and Lesedi Chambers, Hill Antechamber, and the area designated as U.W. 110 (Figure 1C), all within the Rising Star cave system, South Africa.2,3,4,30 To sample the broadest potential range of variation in H. naledi, the material examined in this study includes specimens of different physiological ages and from all published localities within the Rising Star cave system.

Results

An initial round of analysis was performed on a set of four H. naledi specimens, U.W. 101-020, U.W. 102b-511, U.W. 101-809, and U.W. 101-886 (Figure 1C; Table S1), selected for destructive sampling in a previous work on the periodicity of enamel formation,1 while maximizing the derived scientific output, as reported here, and in future studies. We conducted intra-crystalline chiral amino acid analysis to survey protein preservation. The extent of racemization of free and total hydrolyzable amino acids (FAA and THAA, respectively) indicates that the enamel from all four H. naledi specimens behaved as a closed system retaining endogenous peptides31 (Figure S3A). U.W. 102b-511, the potential juvenile individual,1 exhibited higher D/L values in both the FAA and THAA fractions relative to the other three samples. The extent of protein degradation (assessed through AA composition, %FAA, and D to L conversion) in the H. naledi specimens is lower than in other extinct hominin tooth enamel samples where peptides have previously been recovered using MS (Figure S3B),18,19,28 supporting the feasibility of paleoproteomic analysis on these specimens. The lower level of amino acid degradation, compared with the published samples from the nearby fossil hominin site of Swartkrans, is consistent with their younger geological age.19

To test whether the minimally destructive etching methodology23,24 can be effectively used on H. naledi, the four teeth were first etched, then enamel samples weighing 28–48 mg were collected for paleoproteomic analysis based on the extraction protocol previously described.28 From each sample, 10 mg were used for single-shot tandem MS analysis, and the remaining amount was analyzed via manual offline high-pH reverse-phase fractionation,19 in an attempt to improve the depth and breadth of sequence recovery.

The two different sampling approaches yielded comparable results, with a combined total number of 12,243 peptide spectrum matches (PSMs) obtained with conventional sampling, versus 18,709 PSMs obtained when using acid etching. We observed partial sequence recovery of 6–7 proteins per sample with destructive sampling and 5–11 proteins with acid etching (Figure S4). Notably, enamelin (ENAM), AMELX, and collagen 17 alpha 1 (COL17A1) were recovered in all specimens and replicates (Figure S4). The total number of amino acid positions confidently covered with conventional sampling ranged between 451 and 494, while with etching it ranged between 502 and 747. A total of 451 amino acid positions were shared across all specimens and replicates (Table 1). Fractionation yielded a minimal improvement (Figure S5), probably because it was performed using only 18–38 mg of starting material, considerably less than what was previously used to fractionate other hominin enamel extracts (>60 mg).19

AMELX was identified by multiple partially overlapping and nonoverlapping peptide sequences affected by extended terminal hydrolysis, confidently matched to multiple MS/MS spectra displaying almost complete b and y fragment ion series (Figure S6A). The identification of AMELY-specific peptides cannot be considered reliable beyond a reasonable doubt. Specimens from individual U.W. 102b-511, still developing at death,1 showed no AMELY-specific evidence at all. Meanwhile, all the other three individuals (U.W.101-020, U.W.101-809, and U.W.101-886) displayed poor AMELY-specific PSMs with large gaps in their b and y fragment ion series, leaving the amino acids surrounding the AMELY-specific methionine residue in position 59 (M59; Figure S6B1–S6B3) uncovered, likely originating from a misidentification of those sequences by the search engine.

As no male markers were confidently identified from either the macro-destructive fragment or etching methods, and the etching method produced recoveries comparable to those obtained with conventional destructive sampling, we proceeded to etch the enamel of 19 more H. naledi teeth (STAR Methods section “peptide extraction”; Table S1; Figures S2 and S6C). No more than five specimens were processed at a time, with the intent to progressively screen all the available specimens. Only one sample (U.W.101-824) displayed a low-quality AMELY-specific PSM, with no confidently identified b and y fragment ions around the diagnostic M59 insertion (Figure S6B4).

The etching of the additional 19 H. naledi teeth (STAR Methods section “peptide extraction”; Table S1) resulted in 194 to 4774 PSMs, covering 153 to 695 total amino acid positions, depending on the sample (Table 1). Specimens U.W. 101-1915 and U.W. 101-1846 are potentially from the same individual (STAR Methods section “fossil samples and their provenance”), while the extracts from U.W. 101-1571 and U.W. 101-593, yielding very short sequence coverages of 153 and 322 amino acids, respectively (Table 1), were excluded from further analysis. The remaining 17 acid-etched specimens displayed an average amino acid sequence coverage equaling 523 positions. The combined dataset obtained from both conventional sampling and acid etching represented 20 H. naledi individuals and yielded a sum of 92,578 confidently identified PSMs from the MaxQuant software,32 while 116,264 and 145,447 PSMs were confidently identified using pFind33 and Novor.cloud,34 respectively. These PSMs partially covered between 5 and 11 proteins and an average of 577 amino acid positions (Figure S6C). A total of 441 amino acid positions were shared between all 20 H. naledi individuals, including partial protein sequences of AMELX, ENAM, and AMBN (Figure S6C).

All samples showed advanced levels of spontaneous amino acid modifications due to prolonged diagenesis, including deamidation of glutamine and asparagine, tryptophan conversion into kynurenine, and the conversion of arginine to ornithine (Figure S7). Additionally, as previously observed in other paleoanthropological material,17,18,19,20,28,35 the observed peptide length distribution was skewed toward shorter chains, indicating advanced hydrolysis (also supported by the high %FAA detected) (Figure S8). Multiple lines of evidence, therefore, support the authenticity and antiquity of the recovered peptide sequences.

The analysis of all 20 H. naledi individuals did not yield any reliable evidence supporting the identification of AMELY, even though AMELX peptides were detected at intensities that are compatible with the concomitant detection of AMELY in specimens previously assigned to male H. antecessor17 and P. robustus19 (Figure 2). This indicates that the absence of AMELY-specific markers in the mass spectrometric data was not due to a lack of sensitivity of the experimental setup or to the inherent stochasticity of the data acquisition strategy.

We estimated the probability (Pr) of positively identifying each of these individuals as female based on the relative expression and preservation of AMELX and AMELY gene products (STAR Methods section “proteomic estimation of female biological sex”). MS datasets were analyzed using PEAKS36 from all the H. naledi specimens, supplemented with 15 males from H. sapiens,27 two males from P. robustus,19 a male A. africanus,18 a male Denisovan,20 and a male H. antecessor17 for positive controls. This analysis was based on the following assumptions: (1) the ratio of AMELX to AMELY signal is comparable between H. sapiens and H. naledi, (2) the linear regression in logarithmically transformed data results in a normal and homoscedastic distribution of error, and finally, (3) the AMELY-specific sequences, otherwise consistent in Hominidae, are maintained in H. naledi. Of the 20 H. naledi individuals, 19 were confidently identified as females with a probability above 95% (Pr(Female [F]) > 0.95), while the signal from the remaining individual was consistent with a female attribution with a probability between 50% and 95% (0.5 < Pr[F] < 0.95) (Table S2). The results were in line with the analysis presented in Figure 2 and provided an additional confirmation of the absence of male specimens.

To assess inter-specific sequence variation, the assembled protein sequences from all the H. naledi individuals were aligned to their orthologs from extant and ancient hominin species. We observe a total of 18 confidently identified informative single amino acid polymorphisms (SAPs) for Hominidae (Table S3). Two of these SAPs differentiate H. naledi from present-day humans, Neanderthals, and Denisovans. One of them, with a phenylalanine (F) in AMELX position 141, was detected in 15 H. naledi individuals and is also present in Strepsirrhini and Cercopithecidae (Figure 3A). This SAP is parsimoniously interpreted as a substitution of human AMELX 141 tyrosine (Y) (canonical transcript, Ensembl: ENST00000380712.7) to F141 in H. naledi, and not as modern human AMELY 142F (canonical transcript, Ensembl: ENSG00000099721), because we found no PSM supporting the identification of the human form of AMELX carrying the Y141. All the software tools we used to match MS/MS spectra consistently corroborated this observation (Data S1). The other SAP, featuring proline (P) in the position corresponding to 635 in the H. sapiens COL17A1 ortholog (canonical transcript, Ensembl: ENST00000648076), was detected in five H. naledi individuals by both MaxQuant and pFind, but not Novor.cloud (Data S1). P635 represents the ancestral allelic state displayed in extant non-human primates (Figure 3B). In contrast, H. sapiens, Neanderthals, and Denisovans carry the derived state alanine (A) 635. This site has not been covered so far in H. antecessor, H. erectus, and A. africanus,17,18,29 while the ancestral P635 state was also observed in two P. robustus individuals.19

The recovered partial protein sequences of the 20 H. naledi individuals show no variation within the sample set. To investigate the probability of failing to identify variants (clonality) in a sample set of 20 random individuals, we simulated the same sampling scheme, using modern human genomes for comparison, as no large-scale protein data exist for modern humans. We found that the frequency at which we observed no variants in the recovered protein regions after repeatedly sampling 20 individuals from the 1,000 Genomes Project dataset37 was zero (Table S4). However, when performing the same sampling operation on panels from specific geographic areas, we observed that, for some panels with low genetic diversity at these recovered protein-coding regions, it is possible to sample no variation, but with a very low probability (Figure S9). In population panels where diversity at these recovered regions is high, such as populations from Africa, the probability of seeing no variation among 20 individuals was zero, even after resampling 1,000 times (Figure S10). Accordingly, the clonality of all the proteome sequences retrieved appears inconsistent with a sample of a large and diverse population. A less diverse population could support this result, but even then, the probability of sampling no variation in present-day human populations is low (1%–12%, depending on the population). Other explanations, such as prolonged isolation and/or inbreeding, affecting the H. naledi individuals analyzed here, may also be consistent with these results. The genealogical relationship of the individuals is unknown, although the remains come from different locations in the cave system, separated by as much as 145 m, and were not deposited at a single time,3,5 making it unlikely that they are a single-family group.

Due to the limited length of the reconstructed partial protein sequences and the number of informative sites observed, the derived phylogenetic analysis of H. naledi should be considered tentative (STAR Methods section “phylogenetic analysis”; Figure S11). In the future, a more reliable phylogenetic reconstruction could be achieved by expanding the paleoproteomic analysis to H. naledi bone and dentine, or by aDNA sequencing, if biomolecular preservation allows.

Discussion

Based on the results presented here and elsewhere,18,20,29 acid etching has been demonstrated to be a promising micro-destructive sampling approach to estimate the sex of Plio-Pleistocene dental enamel specimens, or to preliminarily screen protein recovery before proceeding with more destructive sampling strategies. Its use could make the palaeoproteomic investigation of particularly rare or precious specimens, so far incompatible with more conventional and invasive sampling practices, acceptable. While not systematically tested, enamel surface acid etching recovers slightly more PSMs than fragment cutting, in apparent contradiction with previous results.17 This could be plausible, as etching can retrieve the higher peptide diversity previously observed in maturation and erupted enamel,38 by solubilizing peptides close to the enamel surface. Similarly, when the amount of the enamel fragment removed by cutting is progressively smaller, the recoveries of the two approaches become increasingly comparable, even reaching the point when etching recovery is slightly higher. Furthermore, enamel fragment demineralization exposes ancient peptides to acid and to its peptide terminal hydrolysis action for a minimum time of approximately 48 h. On the contrary, peptides retrieved using etching are suspended in acid for less than 1 h. This could lead to lower hydrolysis and slightly to the recovery of longer and potentially more informative peptide sequences. Nonetheless, the versatility and reliability of peptide recovery by acid etching still need to be systematically validated across space and time and on larger and more diverse sample sets exposed to a wide array of diagenetic factors.

Our results show no conclusive evidence supporting amelogenin heterozygosity, nor the reliable identification of any male, in a relatively large set of H. naledi individuals that also includes U.W. 102a (nicknamed “Neo”), so far considered a likely adult male due to its relative robusticity and cranial capacity.3 While paleoproteomics has successfully identified male hominin individuals from South Africa up to two million years old,18,19 indicating that such identification is methodologically possible, the evidence provided here proves beyond a reasonable doubt that the absence of any evidence supporting the identification of male individuals in our H. naledi sample set is not due to methodological limitations. Specifically, all the H. naledi AMELX signals we measure have an intensity compatible with the reliable observation of AMELY MS/MS spectra, if they were present. This excludes the possibility that the conventional AMELY-specific peptides generally used as markers to identify male individuals went undetected below the instrumental detection limits (Figure 2 and STAR Methods section “proteomic estimation of female biological sex”). Furthermore, our positive female probability analysis indicates that 19 out of the 20 individuals are confidently identified as females, while the proteomic profile of the remaining one is consistent with a female attribution (Table S2). All these considerations parsimoniously lead us to conclude that the H. naledi specimens we analyzed do not carry the conventional male-specific AMELY markers, either because they exclusively originate from female individuals, or because of a deletion or mutation present in all the male H. naledi individuals we may have possibly sampled. Either scenario would have very interesting implications for the biology and evolution of H. naledi. The homozygosity of AMEL supports the absence of AMELY, and the identification of substitution AMELX F141, rather than the observation of AMELY-specific position F142. Together with the documented low levels of morphological variation observed in H. naledi2,7 these two distinct lines of evidence independently suggest that the H. naledi individuals currently known from the Rising Star cave system are indeed biased9 toward—and may be exclusively—females.

While our results are compatible with the hypothesis that all the specimens analyzed belong to female individuals (Table 1), it is also possible, though unlikely, that our sample set includes male H. naledi individuals whose AMELY gene was mutated or deleted. Currently, paleoproteomics can only identify male individuals by detecting peptides covering publicly available AMELY-specific hominid sequences, or minor variants of these sequences using homology search, and by discovering previously unknown sequences by de novo sequencing. However, since the H. naledi AMELX consensus sequence showed high similarity to modern humans, it would be unlikely to observe AMELY sequences substantially divergent from modern humans or other living primates. If the entire AMELY gene is deleted in H. naledi, as previously observed in a Neanderthal individual,39 then males of H. naledi would not be detectable with the approach used here, as protein profiles of males would be indistinguishable from those of females. The deletion of the entire AMELY gene has been observed in extant human populations and has no known fitness consequences.40 In modern humans, this mutation is population-dependent and very rare, occurring with a frequency ranging from 0.018% in an Austrian population41 to 8% in a Pakistani population.42 However, the rate of AMELY deletion reported in these studies should be interpreted cautiously, given sampling bias. When using large sets of high-quality genomes from the 1,000 Genomes Project and the Exome Sequencing Project,43,44 the deletion of AMELY could not be detected, leading us to conclude that the AMELY deletion is rare.45

Given a sample set of 20 distinct individuals, and assuming a population sex ratio of 1:1, the odds of finding all female individuals are extremely low and represent 0.0000954%. However, the hypothesis of a single-sex accumulation of H. naledi in the Rising Star cave is supported by a recent study9 concluding that, based on dental sizes, the currently known H. naledi fossil record could consist of individuals with a sex ratio of 9:1 or even 1:0. Single-sex accumulation could be compatible with the possibility that H. naledi adopted cultural mortuary practices, as previously proposed.46,47 This reconstruction, however, has been challenged, and further evidence may test the hypothesis of the deliberate burial of the dead by H. naledi.48 While accessing parts of the Rising Star cave system could be easier for smaller-bodied individuals, usually females among primates,49 the areas of the cave system with H. naledi remains have varied entry conditions and are a significant constraint only to larger-bodied humans today. The sample includes 10 juvenile individuals at or prior to second molar eruption, an age range for which the body size of neither sex would exceed adult female body size.

Proteomics-based single-sex bias, like the one observed in H. naledi has not been documented in ancient and modern Homo populations. The closest known dominance of female human remains, observed at the Edcoural Cave, Montemor-o-Novo (Portugal), shows a 1:0.5 (F: M) ratio and is hypothesized to be due to socio-cultural factors, possibly related to funerary practices.50 Similarly, female dominance in Iberian megalithic societies is also observed from burial sites of Panoría populations, with the highest ratio being 1:0.42 (F:M),51 due to funerary practices based on matrilineal descent. However, these examples refer to Neolithic human populations, which may have operated very differently from H. naledi or from other mid-to-late Pleistocene Homo species. Some species of non-human primates have groups with a high adult female-to-male sex ratio, so that females and juveniles may predominate within a single social group. However, none have a similarly biased sex ratio for juvenile individuals, so this kind of social structure does not fit the H. naledi evidence.

Finally, the archaic COL17A1 P635 variant we observe in 5 H. naledi individuals is derived in Denisovans, Neanderthals, and modern humans, and it has not been covered in H. erectus, from either Dmanisi (Georgia) or China, and Homo antecessor.17,29 Interestingly, though, it has been observed in two P. robustus specimens, namely SK835 and SK14132. Determining which variant is present in this and other informative positions, in other extinct hominin species, such as H. erectus and Australopithecus africanus, will help clarify the phylogenetic position of H. naledi.

Previous paleoproteomic studies of Pleistocene enamel material have largely entailed destructive sampling of important and irreplaceable samples.17,19,28,35,52 This study, and other recent ones,18,20,29 show more sustainable, minimally destructive solutions to analyze Pleistocene material using paleoproteomics.18,20,24 We show that both more and less destructive methods can produce comparable results, and that etching could be used as a screening method to survey sexual dimorphism and test protein preservation. Thanks to its minimally destructive nature, etching could even be used to investigate the sex of rare and archaeologically valuable Plio-Pleistocene hominin fossils, such as type specimens, further advancing our understanding of morphological variation in ancient taxa.

Limitations of the study

The specimens sampled in this study, much larger in number than in any other previous paleoproteomic study on Pleistocene hominins, cover all the known H. naledi individuals excavated so far, except for those for which no teeth are preserved; we cannot exclude the presence of male individuals in the latter group. We assume that the H. naledi individuals had a typical karyotype and were not affected by syndromes that can result in non-binary expression, such as Turner or Klinefelter syndrome in humans.53,54,55 Our methodology cannot detect non-binary expression of biological sex, if present in the H. naledi population we studied. The approach we described here focuses exclusively on the characterization of the enamel proteome. While more destructive, the analysis of dentine and/or bone proteomes could most probably provide a richer and more informative phylogenetic signal.

Experimental model and study participant details

Fossil Samples and their Provenance

All the specimens analyzed in this study had antimeres in order to limit the loss of morphological information (SAHRA permit ID: 3405 & 3983). The sampling strategy employed here was intended to have at least a representative sample from most sites where H. naledi is deposited within the Rising Star cave.

Sample selection and minimum number of individuals

At the initiation of this project, four H. naledi teeth were selected based upon several criteria to minimize destructive sampling of rare fossil material and to maximize the information return from several different methodologies. One key aim was preparation of thin sections of the tooth crown, in order to determine periodicity of enamel formation, directly observe histological characteristics of the crown, and for downstream analyses of developmental biology and environmental exposure. The resulting enamel periodicity data were published by Mahoney et al.1 For these purposes, the priority for the four sampled teeth was to select relatively complete crowns with minimal dental wear, that clearly represent different individuals. Permanent canines and first molars were selected to maximize comparability with the data already gathered on hypoplasia and enamel defects57,58 and to extend the coverage of enamel development from before birth to the end of canine crown formation. Additionally, it was considered desirable to represent multiple H. naledi localities within the cave system, so in addition to two juvenile individuals from the Dinaledi Chamber, one individual each from the Hill Antechamber and the Lesedi Chamber were included in this initial sample. For the purposes of individuation, sample selection relied upon previous work by Bolter et al.59 and Delezene et al.,60 as well as new observations of material that was not described in these previous studies.

U.W. 101–809 left mandibular first molar. This tooth is relatively complete and represents a late juvenile developmental stage. It was chosen because it is the likely antimere of the right first molar in the U.W. 101–377 mandible, which has been attributed to the DH7 individual61 or dental individual 4.59 This was included in Association 2 by Delezene et al.60

U.W. 101–886 right mandibular canine. This tooth is a complete crown with partially complete root, representing an early juvenile developmental stage. It is the antimere of U.W. 101–1126, and is associated with a complete set of mandibular incisors, giving it the possibility of representing an individual that is represented by several teeth within the sample. This individual must be different from DH7 represented by the U.W. 101–377 mandible, because that mandible includes an articulating right canine, U.W. 101–1014.

U.W. 101-020 left maxillary first molar. This tooth was the first specimen recovered from the Hill Antechamber. At the time this specimen was recovered, the Hill Antechamber and Dinaledi Chamber were not recognized as distinct depositional situations.2,62 Later work showed that these spaces are distinct and that passive transport from the Hill Antechamber into the Dinaledi Chamber is not feasible.4,46 U.W. 101-020 has slight occlusal wear and represents an individual of early juvenile developmental stage. It has been excluded as an antimere of any RM2 from the Dinaledi Chamber.

U.W. 102b-511 left mandibular canine. The 102b locality within the Lesedi Chamber has a mandibular fragment and five isolated teeth from a juvenile H. naledi individual.3 It is not clear whether 102b represents a different depositional space from the 102a area; however all other dental material in the Lesedi Chamber comes from adult individuals and this is the only dental sample from an individual of immature developmental stage.

The initial proteomic sampling of these four teeth demonstrated that no male-specific AMELY markers were present. Sampling four female individuals by chance within a mixed-sex sample is not, however, extremely unlikely. Because each of the four initial samples represent immature developmental stage individuals, and only one of these is clearly associated with additional mandibular and skeletal material, it is not possible to assess secondary sex characteristics from these fossils. Therefore, it was not possible to test whether the proteomic assessment of sex conflicts with any morphological data from these individuals. To test this possibility, the team decided to select an adult individual for which morphology had suggested male sex, based on body size, cranial size, and cranial morphological characteristics. Proteomic sampling of this individual and all additional specimens was carried out by the acid etching protocol and no cutting or drilling of material was performed.

LES1 right mandibular third molar. This individual from the Lesedi Chamber was assessed as the most probable male within the H. naledi sample based upon the greatest quantity of skeletal and cranial evidence.3

After sampling, the results from the LES1 tooth also showed no evidence of AMELY markers, suggesting female sex or a mutation in AMELY gene for this individual. At this stage of the research, the potential for reliable results from additional individuals using the minimally destructive approach was clear. The finding that the LES1 skeleton may represent a female individual suggested that a morphological reassessment of the entire sample was desirable, and that proteomic evidence of chromosomal sex would be essential to this reassessment.

The team adopted a strategy of identifying a series of teeth of H. naledi from all recovery areas that could be clearly established as different individuals. This survey consisted of multiple steps. The following list is presented in order of these steps, to make clear the evidence that the 23 teeth selected in the total sample represent a minimum number of 21 individuals.

First, every additional right maxillary first molar from the Dinaledi Chamber was included, with the exception of U.W. 101–999, which cannot be excluded as representing the same individual as the U.W. 101–886 mandibular canine. This added four distinct individuals to the sample set:

U.W. 101–1463 right maxillary first molar. This tooth from the Puzzle Box area of the Dinaledi Chamber is the antimere of the left maxillary M1 that is in the U.W. 101–1277 maxilla. This specimen excavated from the Puzzle Box area of the Dinaledi Chamber is attributed to the DH1 individual, which represents an adult life history stage. On the basis of cranial size and some aspects of cranial morphology, this was suggested to be a male individual.2,7 This is dental individual 6 of Bolter et al.59 and Association 7 of Delezene et al.60

U.W. 101–525 right maxillary first molar. This tooth was recovered from surface area 4SE of the Dinaledi Chamber and represents an adult individual with occlusal wear facets but no significant dentine exposure. This tooth was included in Association 5 by Delezene et al.60

U.W. 101–583 right maxillary first molar. This tooth was collected from surface area 4SH of the Dinaledi Chamber. With similar wear to U.W. 101–525, this is also inferred to be a young adult or adolescent individual.

U.W. 101–1396 right maxillary first molar. This tooth from the Puzzle Box area represents an adult life history stage. This tooth is part of Association 6 noted by Delezene et al.60

From the Hill Antechamber, three additional right maxillary first molars have been recovered and were added to the sample set. None of these three teeth were compatible in morphology or dental wear stage to the U.W. 101-020 left maxillary first molar (discussed above), which represents an individual of early juvenile developmental stage. As noted above, the spatial distinctness of the Hill Antechamber supports the hypothesis that the individuals represented in this space are different from those in the Dinaledi Chamber; additionally, the following teeth duplicate right maxillary first molars represented in the Dinaledi Chamber.

U.W. 101–344 right maxillary first molar. This tooth was recovered from the surface of the Hill Antechamber at the base of the sloping floor. It was included in Berger et al.2 prior to the recognition that the Hill Antechamber and Dinaledi Chamber are different depositional spaces. This tooth represents an adult individual.

U.W. 101–1846 right maxillary first molar. This tooth was recovered at the floor surface of the S150W150 excavation unit.2 It represents an adult individual with substantial occlusal wear including the exposure of a dentine pool on the protocone, and a refitting interproximal facet with the U.W. 101–1845 right maxillary M2.

U.W. 101–2021 right maxillary first molar. This tooth was recovered from sieving of sediments from the S100W100 excavation unit. It is an unworn first molar crown that does not match the morphology of U.W. 101-020 and cannot be an antimere of this tooth.

The U.W. 110 locality is within the fissure network extending toward the west past the Dinaledi Chamber, and a fragmented cranium of an individual attributable to the early juvenile developmental stage was recovered from this locality, including a partial maxillary dentition. This dentition includes a germ of a right maxillary first molar that demonstrates that this partial cranium is a different individual from the early juveniles sampled from the Dinaledi Chamber. A right maxillary fourth premolar germ was selected for sampling:

U.W. 110–2 right maxillary fourth premolar. As noted above this represents an early juvenile that is distinct from all Dinaledi Chamber individuals.

From the Lesedi Chamber (designated U.W. 102), a mandibular fragment of one additional adult individual with two in situ teeth has been collected, which cannot represent LES1 or the early juvenile represented by U.W. 102b-511.

U.W. 102c-589 mandibular fragment with LM1 and LM2. This individual is an adult with moderate occlusal wear on M2.

This survey brought the total number of distinct individuals to 14, using only the information from different localities and the duplication of right maxillary first molars as criteria. To add additional individuals, the survey then incorporated information on the developmental stage of teeth from the Dinaledi Chamber.

U.W. 101–1686 right mandibular deciduous second molar. This tooth is the antimere of the left mandibular deciduous second molar within the U.W. 101–1400 infant mandible, designated as dental individual 7 by Bolter et al.59 and Association 1 by Delezene et al.60 This individual represents the infant developmental stage. The individual also has a germ of a right maxillary first molar (U.W. 101–1688), but the rdm2 was assessed to be a better candidate for enamel sampling by the acid etching approach.

Four early juvenile stage individuals are represented within the Dinaledi Chamber.59 The early juvenile stage initiates at first molar eruption and ends at second molar eruption.59 At this point in the survey, only one individual in this developmental stage was included, by the permanent mandibular left canine U.W. 101–886. Two of the remaining three are younger, but still within the same developmental stage, and are documented by left mandibular deciduous canines. U.W. 101–1571 left mandibular deciduous canine. This tooth was recovered from the surface of area 4SB of the Dinaledi Chamber. U.W. 101–824 left mandibular deciduous canine. This tooth was recovered from area 4SC on the surface of the Dinaledi Chamber. It has a nearly complete root and substantial occlusal and distal wear. U.W. 101–593 right maxillary permanent second molar. This second molar germ is unworn and was recovered from the surface of area 4SJ in the Dinaledi Chamber. Method details

Biomolecular preservation

Chiral amino acid analysis was undertaken on the intra-crystalline fraction of U.W. 101-020, U.W. 101–809, U.W. 101–886 and U.W. 102b-511. The enamel specimens were processed following Dickinson et al.63 After powdering and bleaching, the specimens were divided into two fractions: one for determining the free amino acids (FAA) and one for the total hydrolysable amino acids (THAA). The concentration of the intra-crystalline amino acids and their extent of racemization (D/L value) was then quantified using RP-HPLC (Agilent 1100 series HPLC fitted with Hypersil C18 base deactivated silica column (5 μm, 250 x 3 mm) and fluorescence detector) following a modified method of Kaufman and Manley.64 D and L ratios were measured for the following amino acids: aspartic acid and asparagine (Asx); glutamic acid and glutamine (Glx); serine (Ser), alanine (Ala), valine (Val), phenylalanine (Phe), isoleucine (Ile), leucine (Leu), threonine (Thr), arginine (Arg) and tyrosine (Tyr) to provide the estimation of intra-crystalline protein degradation.

Criteria of determination of minimum number of individuals (MNI)

To determine MNI, we followed the method described in Bolter et al.59 (detailed in STAR methods section “fossil samples and their providence”). In summary, teeth checked for duplication based on type, side, wear and eruption, and age class of the teeth. As a result, we determined that MNI is 22 because U.W. 101–1846 and U.W. 101–1915 could feasibly be from the same individual.

Peptide extraction

Destructive analysis

U.W. 101-020, U.W. 101–809, U.W. 101–886 and U.W. 102b-511 were sampled via the destructive method. The sample surface was first cleaned using molecular biology-grade water. Between 10 mg and 38 mg of dental enamel was used for single shot and fractionation experiments. An enamel sample was removed from the specimen using a motorized cutting disk (Dremel), carefully avoiding sampling the dentine. The enamel subsample was partially fragmented by crushing it with a pestle and mortar before the subsample was demineralized using 500 mL (single shot) or 1.5 mL (fractionation) 10% v/v trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) for 48 h at 4˚C. Any remaining sample debris was pelleted by centrifugation at 10,000 x g for 15 mins and the supernatant was transferred to a fresh Eppendorf Protein loBind tube. During this time C18 StageTips were prepared as described in Cappellini et al.28 The StageTips were loaded (1 sample per StageTip), cleaned and eluted as described in Cappellini et al.28 An extraction blank per batch was prepared simultaneously with samples.

Etching

All H. naledi specimens were analyzed using the micro-destructive method. This protocol was modified from Stewart et al.24 Briefly, the sample surface was first cleaned using molecular biology-grade water and low dust laboratory tissue (Kimtech). 130 mL of 10% v/v TFA was placed into the cap of a 0.5 mL Eppendorf protein lo-bind tube and the sample was manually held so the surface of the sample was in contact with the acid solution for an initial 15 sec. The tube and acidic solution were discarded, a fresh one was prepared, and the sample surface that was in contact with the acid was cleaned once more, as described above. The sample was reintroduced to the new 10% TFA in the new tube, and contact was maintained for a total of 10–15 minutes with visual inspection every 3–5 minutes. These times were sample-dependent, and the aim was to avoid over-sampling and penetration of the enamel, which would result in exposing dentine to the etching solution. The acidic solution (sample extract) was removed from the 0.5 mL tube cap and placed into a fresh Protein loBind Eppendorf tube and the cap was washed with 100 mL 10% TFA and combined with the sample extract. C18 StageTip peptide concentration/cleanup was performed as described for the samples analyzed by destructive extraction above, including extraction blanks.

Peptide clean-up and fractionation

Single shot analysis was performed on all the acid etched samples and the four samples from which 10 mg of enamel was sampled for destructive analysis. For the single shot analysis, the peptides were eluted from StageTips using 30 μL of 40% acetonitrile (ACN), 60% water + 0.1% TFA, the ACN was evaporated, and the peptides were re-suspended prior to injection. The obtained extracts were concentrated to around 1 μL and reconstituted in a 10 μL solution containing 0.1% TFA, 5% ACN in water.

For the fractionated samples (18–38 mg destructive extraction), the pH of the extract was adjusted to 10 by addition of 30% Ammonium hydroxide solution (NH34OH). Following this, the sample was loaded onto a StageTip and washed with 200 μL of 20 mM NH3 in water. The elution phases were conducted as outlined below: 1- 96% 20 mM NH3 in H2O / 4% 20 mM NH3 in ACN, 2- 92% 20 mM NH3 in H2O / 8% 20 mM NH3 in ACN, 3- 88% 20 mM NH3 in H2O / 12% 20 mM NH3 in ACN, 4- 80% 20 mM NH3 in H2O / 20% 20 mM NH3 in ACN, and 5- 20% 20 mM NH3 in H2O / 80% 20 mM NH3 in ACN. The resultant extracts were evaporated to about 1 μL and reconstituted in a 10 μL mixture of 0.1% TFA, 5% ACN in water.

LC-MSMS

Peptides were separated using nanoflow liquid chromatography over 77 minutes. The mass spectrometer was an Exploris 480 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) operating data dependent acquisition (DDA) in top 10 mode.

The liquid chromatography system employed was the EasyLC 1200 system from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA), using mobile phases A (5% ACN, 0.1% formic acid in water) and B (80% ACN, 0.1% formic acid in water) with a flow rate of 250 nL/min. The loading of 4 μL occurred at a constant pressure of 500 bars. Then, two linear sections constituted the gradient 5%–30% B in 50 minutes and 30%–45% B in 10 minutes. This was followed by a 5-minute wash at 80% B and a 5-minute re-equilibration at 5% B. The separation was executed at 40°C. For the separation, an in-house pulled and packed column was used. The packing particles were ReproSil C18 beads (Dr. Maisch, Germany) with a diameter of 1.9 μm and 120 Å pores in a silica tube measuring 20 cm in length and 0.75 mm in inner diameter, equipped with an integrated electrospray ionization (ESI) tip.

The Exploris 480 mass spectrometer was employed in positive ionization mode with spray voltage at 2 kV, and ion transfer tube temperature at 275°C. In MS1, a scan was conducted across the m/z range of 350 to 1400 at an Orbitrap resolution of 120,000 at m/z 200. The subsequent MS2 experiment operated at a resolution of 60,000 within the m/z range of 100 to precursor m/z +20, utilizing 60 V collision energy at 1e-5 bar. Ions underwent fragmentation for the top 10 ions that met the criteria of intensity exceeding 2e4, a charge state between 2 and 6, and the exclusion of isotopologues. Dynamic exclusion of 20 seconds was employed.

Database search strategy

Raw data was searched using MaxQuant (V.1.6.3.4),32 pFind,33 and novor.cloud.34 A custom-built database, containing 343 entries of enamel specific proteins collected from UniProt65 corresponding to Homo sapiens, Pan, Pongo, Nomascus, Papio and Microcebus, was used. This database was supplemented with the Gigantopithecus, Homo antecessor, and Denisovan enamel proteomes and some Neanderthal enamel specific sequences.17,52 Peptide identification was performed by setting the digestion parameter to “unspecific” since aspecific digestion occurred in vivo and through hydrolysis, and the minimum length for unspecific peptides was set to 7 amino acids. In the main search, the peptide mass tolerance was 4.5 ppm, and the fragment mass tolerance was 20 ppm. For MaxQuant, the Andromeda threshold score for both unmodified and modified proteins was set to 40, to filter out peptide spectral matches (PSM) with a low-quality score and for both pFind and Novor.cloud we left the threshold default score. No fixed post translational modifications were set. Glutamine and asparagine deamidation, oxidation of methionine, oxidation of proline, phosphorylation of serine/threonine/tyrosine, ornithine conversion from asparagine, and N-terminal pyroglutamic acid from glutamic and aspartic acids were all included as possible variable modifications. Proteins included in the database of common contaminants provided by MaxQuant, pFind and Novor.cloud, for example proteinaceous laboratory reagents and human skin keratins, as well as reverse sequences were manually removed and not considered any further. Similarly, proteins detected in the laboratory blank were not further considered.

Post-translational modifications analysis

The extent of deamidation was estimated using the python script from Mackie et al.66 Other modifications were assessed by PSM counting. For a given modification, the ratio between the number of amino acids that could be modified to the number of amino acids modified was calculated. The counts were normalized by the PMS count.

Criteria for positively identifying and validating informative sites

The methodology is based on and adapted from Taurozzi et al.,67 briefly a combination of these; high scoring PSMs, a complete or almost complete y and b fragment ion series, multiple PSMs and multiple over lapping peptide sequences.

To validate potential PSMs matched to amelogenin-Y peptides, we employed a manual validation approach rather than relying exclusively on algorithmic scoring methods, which are primarily designed for modern biological samples with larger datasets. Thus, spectra used for protein sequence reconstruction were manually inspected to evaluate fragment ion coverage, with particular attention to the methionine (M) residue in SMIRPPY peptides. Additionally, experimental spectra were compared with predicted spectra (https://www.proteomicsdb.org/use/) to assess the validity of peptide identifications.

To determine whether PSMs with phenylalanine (F) at position 141 for AMELX (present in Strepsirrhini and Cercopithecidae) or at position 142 for human AmelY (canonical Ensembl transcript ENSG00000099721) were present, multiple lines of evidence were evaluated. Peptides corresponding to the human AMELX 141 tyrosine (Y) variant were not detected at this position. Noteworthy as reported by Salido et al.,22 AmelY expression is approximately 10% that of AMELX, making it improbable that AMELY variant would be detected in the absence of the corresponding AMELX peptides. In addition, a greater number of PSMs matched the amelogenin-X protein sequence compared with amelogenin-Y.

PSMs matching amelogenin-Y were excluded if the supporting evidence was ambiguous: (1) A PSM matching AmelY in specimen U.W.101-1463 contained a peptide with potential AmelY-109 arginine (R) and AmelY-110 valine (V), but this finding was supported by only a single PSM with no overlapping peptides, and the RV site was not resolved by a complete or almost complete y and b fragment ion series. (2) Specimens U.W.101-006, U.W.101-1686, U.W.101-824, and U.W.102b-511 exhibited PSMs matching AmelY at position 167 leucine (L); however, no overlapping peptides were identified, and this peptide also matched a sequence from a soil-dwelling bacterium (see BLAST: https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi#alnHdr_WP_431964074) (Figure S6 D).

Protein sequence reconstruction

Once the contaminants had been removed, the manually validated sequences were reconstructed with an in-house R68 script which can be found in this GitHub repository (https://github.com/palesamadupe-palaeoprot/PalaeoProteinSequenceReconstruction). First, the tab-separated text file "evidence.txt" from the MaxQuant32 is read into a data frame. Next, the script filters and organizes the data for each of the specified genes (ALB, AMELX, AMELY, AMBN, AHSG, AMTN, COL17A1, COL1A1, COL1A2, ENAM, ODAM, MMP20). For each gene, the script filters rows that match the gene name in the "Proteins" column. It then creates a new data frame by selecting and combining the MS/MS scan number, Experiment, Score, and Sequence columns into a single column named "id" using an underscore as a separator. After filtering and organizing the data, the script writes the organized data to a tab-separated text file for each gene (e.g., "alb_peptides.txt"). Each file contains the combined "id" column and peptide sequences. The script then generates FASTA files using the package Biostrings69 for the peptide sequences for each gene. Finally, the script aligns the peptides from each FASTA file against reference sequences using Mafft70 suite. From the aligned peptides sequences, we obtained the consensus sequence for each gene for all the H. naledi specimens.

Sampling simulations

The full dataset of the 1000 genomes43 was obtained in VCF file format.71 The dataset was subdivided into population specific vcf files, based on the population label of each individual. A copy of the complete, global population with all individuals was also maintained. All the vcf files were processed with a custom workflow based on snakemake.72 Sites that were recovered in all 20 highest coverage, H. naledi individuals were identified, resulting in a subset of 509 amino acids. The vcf file of each population was then reduced to only variants falling within the range of the coding regions corresponding to these 509 amino acids. The vcf files were further filtered to remove silent mutations and keep only variants that cause an amino acid change, based on Ensemble’s Variant Effect Predictor.73 Each filtered vcf file was sampled 1000 times, each time isolating 20 individuals and enumerating the number of variants present in the sample set along with calculating other genetic diversity metrics. The total number of times no variants were identified was divided by the 1000 repetitions, generating the probability of the observed H. naledi sampling for each population (Figure S9). All code for this analysis is available on GitHub (https://github.com/johnpatramanis/Hnld_Genetic_Diversity_Analysis) but requires the user to download and subdivide the 1000 Genomes vcf files.

Phylogenetic analysis

Sequences were divided into individual protein datasets, which were aligned using Mafft70 and then corrected for leucine and isoleucine isobaric positions as described in 17. The individual protein datasets were concatenated and converted to each of the appropriate formats for phylogenetic analysis. Multiple phylogenetic trees were generated for the concatenation of all the available proteins, using different software as demonstrated in Madupe et al.19 For the phylogenetic reconstruction using MrBayes,74 we followed the detailed instructions in Madupe et al.19 and those in Welker et al.17 for Beast275 tip-dated analysis. A set of two individuals per subspecies (or one for the consensus tree) was randomly selected from the concatenated ‘Diversity’ dataset. For the Tip dating reconstruction, the ages of each ancient individual were included in this analysis and are as follows: Altai Neanderthal – 112 ka , Chagyrskaya Neanderthal – 80 ka , Altai Denisovan – 82 ka , four P. robustus individuals – 1.9 Ma,19 a H. antecessor 900 ka individual17 and 21 of the H. naledi individuals.

Statistical estimation of female biological sex

Male sex estimation is dependent on unambiguous detection of amelogenin Y-chromosome isoform specific peptide sequences (AMELY). Absence of AMELY however can be due to two hypotheses: female sex, or failed detection of AMELY. The probability of false negative male detection will decrease as data quality increases. Female sex therefore can be expressed as a probability. This supplement outlines a statistical framework for the probabilistic estimation of female sex in H. naledi. To mitigate non-parametric and heteroscedasticity effects, this framework was developed fully in logarithmic space.

The framework addressed three factors that were needed to accommodate individual sex estimates into a probabilistic framework. Firstly, the ratio of AMELX and AMELY needed to be estimated to allow AMELY values to be modelled as a function of AMELX signal. Secondly, the lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) needed to be estimated so that the ratios could be predictable. Finally, the measurements of both the X/Y ratio and LLOQ needed to be distributed normally and be parametric, which was achieved when the measurements were transformed logarithmically. When the mean log(LLOQ) estimate was added to the mean log(X/Y) estimate the combined value described the AMELX level (logX) at which there was a 50% cumulative probability that the AMELY would be detected in the quantitative range (≤log50%Y). Since the deviation of both log(X/Y) and log(LLOQ) was normally distributed, the number of standard deviations between log50%Y and an individual logX value (Z-score) could then be used to give a cumulative probability of female sex (Pr(F)).

The lower limit of quantitation (logLLOQ) was estimated in 35 of the 75 H. naledi datasets to be 5.190 ± 0.467. To estimate the ratio of AMELX to AMELY in male enamel a total of 15 known archaeological and paleo enamel samples were processed from 5 hominin species. This included archaeological H. sapiens from Jordan (n = 10),27 a H. antecessor (n = 1)17; P. robustus (n = 2),19 A. africanus18 (n = 1), and Denisovan (n = 1)20 samples. Fourteen individuals had detectable levels for both proteins (logX, logY) with a mean ratio (logX/Y) of 1.04 ± 0.24. The logX value with a 50% probability of AMELY detection in the quantitative range was 6.23 ± 0.52. Individual estimates of both logLLOQ and log(X/Y) did not show significant departures from normality.76 Because of this Z-scores between individual logX measurements and the log50%Y could be used to accurately obtain cumulative probabilities of female sex (Pr(F)) in the absence of AMELY detection.

A total of 75 mass spectrometry datasets were analyzed in PEAKS36 from 23 H. naledi specimens and of these 68 datasets contained detectable AMELX sequence. Twenty were definitively female (Pr(F) > 0.95), two were consistent with female (Pr(F) > 0.5, < 0.95), and one was indeterminate (Pr(F) < 0.5) (Table S2). Aside from the Homo sapiens enamel sample, no unambiguous AMELY – specific peptides were detected in the sample. This analysis assumed that the ratio of AMELX to AMELY signal in Homo sapiens was maintained in H. naledi, that linear regression in logarithmically transformed data results in a normal and homoscedastic distribution of error, and that amelogenin-Y specific sequences consistent in Hominidae, were maintained in H. naledi.

The framework made both biological and statistical assumptions. Biologically, it assumed the expression levels of AMELX and AMELY in Homo sapiens apply to Homo naledi and that the respective sequences in Homo naledi were the same as in other hominins. It assumed no structural deletion of the AMELY locus. The biological assumptions were reasonable or validated elsewhere in the study. Statistically, the study assumed that the AMELX values could be used to model AMELY levels, a reasonable assumption given that both were above the LLOQ level and the relative ratio of AMELX to AMELY signal was well measured. The study also assumed that any Z-scores between individual cumulative AMELX signal values and the 50%-AMELY threshold could be used for cumulative probability estimation. The assumption was likewise validated by the Shapiro Wilk tests for normal distribution of the logarithmically transformed LLOQ value and AMELX/AMELY ratio.

Mass spectrometry datasets (.RAW format) were processed using PEAKS36 XPro (Bioinfor.com, PEAKS Studio 10.6 build 20201221), in Database mode (PEAKS-DB) with default settings except: Parent Mass Error Tolerance: 10.0 ppm, Fragment Mass Error Tolerance: 0.02 Da, Variable modifications: Oxidation (M): 15.99, deamidation (NQ): 0.98, Phosphorylation (STY): 79.97, Oxidation or Hydroxylation: 15.99. A maximum of 3 PTMS per peptide. Digestion in Unspecific mode. The reference database was a validated custom FASTA file of primate enamelome proteins, ‘Hominidae_IP_17Oct22SMIRPPY_noMice.txt’, available on the ProteomeXchange data repository (PXD054549). A decoy database was used and a 5% FDR cut-off applied to all peptide spectra matches. Resulting peptide data from the ‘>pan_troglodytes_AMELX-201’ gene product, identical to the N-terminal region of Homo sapiens ‘> NP_001133.1 amelogenin, X isoform 1 precursor [Homo sapiens]’ was copied and peptides containing the AMELX-specific “SI” motif (amino acid positions 44 and 45) were identified and collated. Matching peptide area (ion current) values were transformed logarithmically (base 10) and ranked to create a ranked peptide curve. Linear regression was applied to the middle 50% of ranked transformed peptide values, and residuals calculated for all AMELX-specific peptides. The point of negative deviation away from the regression was identified, where possible, and the rank was used obtain an estimate for LLOQ for the dataset (log10(LLOQ)). All transformed estimates of log10(LLOQ) were used to calculate a mean and standard deviation. A W-statistic was also calculated following Shapiro and Wilk,76 and compared with the Shapiro Wilk reference value to confirm that the W-statistic was greater and the hypothesis of non-normal distribution could be rejected (significance level = 0.05). The ratio of cumulative AMELX-specific peptide signals to AMELY-specific peptide signals was calculated from the datasets of all known hominin male enamel samples and logarithmically transformed (base 10). The log10(AMELY) values (logY) was subtracted from the log10(AMELX) values (logX) for each male dataset to generate a mean and standard deviation (sd). The original log10(AMELX) and the ‘logX-logY′ values were tested for non-normal distribution.76 The means for logLLOQ and ‘logX-logY′ values were summed to estimate a value of logX where there was a 50% probability of AMELY being detected at the logLLOQ (50% AMELY threshold, log50%Y) if the sample was male. The standard deviation of this threshold was calculated as the square root of the sum of the squares of the matching standard deviations (50% AMELY threshold sd, σ50%Y). The number of standard deviations (Z-score) for each dataset was calculated as the difference between individually observed logX values and the log50%Y threshold, divided by the standard deviation for the threshold (σ50%Y). The cumulative probabilities for the standard normal (Z) distribution, probability of female biological sex (Pr(F)), was calculated in Excel using the “NORM.S.DIST(“Z-score”,True)” function. This study defines definitive female biological sex (F) as Pr(F) > 0.95, contingent ‘consistent with female biological sex’ (F∗) as (0.5 < Pr(F) < 0.95), indeterminate sex (IND) as 0 ≤ Pr(F) ≤ 0.5. Definitive male sex was defined as unambiguous detection of any AMELY – specific peptide regardless of signal. Please note: All simple log notations are in base 10.

The population of peptides specific for AMELX gene product (from the >pan_troglodytes_AMELX-201 gene product) ranged from 1 to 130 peptides in the H. naledi sample. The signal of any one peptide is the product of concentration and ionization efficiency. When the ion current for each peptide was logarithmically transformed (base 10) and ranked the resulting plot (Ranked Peptide Plot, Figure S12) had a distinctive profile with three segments. At the upper end of the range, the population of peptides was a consistent subset of high signal peptides, and the ranking resulted in a curve with a sharp decline over a limited number of peptides. The major and middle portion of the curve appeared as linear, and the third segment negatively deviated away from linearity. The distribution of peptides in the linear portion of logarithmic curve reflects a distribution of peptides with a mix of ionization properties and concentrations. As the recovery of peptides decreased, the concentration and signal of this population dropped into the zone where detection of individual peptides began to fail.

This study defined the lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) as the point where signal began to fail due to non-detection (Figure S12, red marker) and fall away from the linear portion of the curve. This analysis assumed that the rate of detection failure would increase the closer the peptide signal approached the LLOD. Sampling the LLOD from peptide detection was therefore problematic, since sampling was small and stochastic effects were maximal. Sampling of LLOQ was more consistent and represented a portion of the peptide rank curve where modelling would likewise be consistent. A fixed reference point for modeling that could be estimated from each dataset. To assist in mapping the point of departure due to low concentration, the estimated LLOQ, a linear regression was calculated using the linear middle 50% of the curve (Figure S12A). The point of departure of observed peptide signals from the linear regression was sharper and easier to estimate when judging from the residual plot (Figure S12B). An overlay of ranked peptide plots illustrated that AMELX peptides detected between the LLOQ and LLOD, also occured in the linear range when concentration was increased, and ionization efficiency should not change appreciably (Figure S12C). The total estimated logLLOQ levels occurred across 2 orders of magnitude and have normal distribution (Figure S12D). LogLLOQ levels did not change at lower peptide recoveries (peptX).

The logLLOQ mean and standard deviation of 5.190 ± 0.467 was obtained from 35 of the 80 datasets where a clear point of deviation away from linearity could be discerned. The Shapiro-Wilk test did not show a significant departure from normality, W(35) = .96, p = .297.

These estimates were measured based on the sub-population of peptides that were specific for AMELX, since this was the sub-population that was informative for sex estimation. LLOQ however should be instrumental and so other peptide populations in the dataset, or all peptides, may possibly be used and theoretically be more precise since a higher density of ranked peptide signals should provide greater resolution of LLOQ, although precision of LLOQ with peptide number was not supported by Figure S12D. LLOQ estimation was also contingent on detection of at least one peptide being observed between the LLOQ and LLOD. LLOQ estimation had intrinsic errors and biases. Each estimate was based off the first peptide above the LLOQ point, an intrinsic conservative bias that theoretically would be minimized with more peptide observations, although, again, this was not supported by Figure S12D. When regressions were estimated, and the variation was symmetric and normal, 50% of the residuals will also be below the regression line. If these occurred adjacent to the LLOQ, it was difficult to resolve negative residuals from expected homoscedastic variation around a regression from residuals that result from failed peptide detection.

The underlying assumption of this framework is that the pattern of amelogenin, X isoform 1 (AMELX) specific peptides, can be used to model, or predict, the pattern of amelogenin, Y-isoform (AMELY) specific peptides. This was based on homology at the peptide level and the assumption that the ratio of expression of AMELX (NP_001133.1) and AMELY (NP_001134.1) was consistent within hominin individuals and populations, an assumption validated by these data. The ratio of AMELY to AMELX in Homo sapiens has been reported to be at a level of 10 to 1 at the transcript21,22 and 12 to 1, or 13 to 1 at the protein25,77 level. Linear regression of non-transformed values however has a non-normal distribution and therefore cannot be used to obtain a Z-score that could be used for accurate estimation of cumulative probability. This problem was addressed by conducting the analysis in logarithmic space.78

The signal of AMELX- and AMELY-specific peptides was measured in enamel from 15 male hominin individuals (archaeological H. sapiens from Jordan, n = 1027; H. antecessor, n = 117; P. robustus, n = 2,19 A. africanus, n = 118; and Denisovan, n = 120) AMELX-specific peptides were detected in at least one datafile from all individuals, with matching AMELY detected in 14 individuals, the exception being one P. robustus individual (SK835). The Shapiro-Wilk test did not show a significant departure from normality, W(14) = .93, p = .281.76

Delineation of both LLOQ and the AMELX/AMELY ratio allowed AMELY (logY) levels to be modelled as a function of AMELX peptide signal and to do so in a way that conformed to a probabilistic framework. The mean of log(LLOQ) was added to the mean of the log(AMELX/AMELY) (log(X/Y), to create a threshold value of log(AMELX) (logX) where AMELY would be predicted to be at the LLOQ. Because this value is a mean with a normal distribution, the cumulative probability that AMELY would be at the LLOQ would be 50%. The equivalency of Pr(M) and Pr(F) assumed that the Pr(F) can be modelled on the probability that AMELY would be detected above the LLOQ point for an observed AMELX value in a male individual dataset. The same probability would apply to the probability of female sex (Pr(F)) in the absence of AMELY detection.

At the practical level the definition of male was a simple binary detection of AMELY in an individual’s enamel. This difference in sex assignment criteria, that potentially may occur below the LLOQ, would be expected to impose a bias toward male sex estimation. This model also does not accommodate the sub-population of higher signal peptides in the leading part of the ranked peptide curve. This population would potentially persist above the LLOQ even as overall concentration of all peptides decreases in low and lower signal datasets. The calculated Pr(F) values are therefore an underestimate and could be considered overly conservative.

As the quality of enamelome data increases, as measured by increased logX values, the probability of a male false positive would decrease, and female true positive would increase, in the absence of any AMELY signal. Since both the log(LLOQ) and log(X/Y) values had normal distributions, the log(50%Y) threshold also would have a normal distribution. Because of this, any difference between an observed logX value and the log(50%Y) threshold, specifically the number of standard deviations between these values (Z-score), can be used to estimate cumulative probability. The standard deviation of the log(50%Y) value would equal the square root of the sum of the squared standard deviations of logLLOQ and log(X/Y).

This framework makes several biological and statistical assumptions. Biochemically it assumed that the relative expression and preservation of AMELX and AMELY gene products was consistent between other hominin species and Homo naledi. It also assumed that the sequence for AMELY in both species was stable and that there was no population-wide structural variation, AMELY deletion or recombination to the AMELX sequence, in the Y-chromosome of H. naledi.45,79 These assumptions were considered reasonable given the stability and detection of these sequences, as well as a consistent AMELX to AMELY ratio (Figure S14) in H. sapiens sub-populations and related hominin species.17,18,19,20,27

The other assumption was the choice of peptide populations to estimate LLOQ. Only peptides specific to the AMELX gene product were used since these would best match the properties of AMELY-specific peptides and were considered necessary for this framework, given that AMELX was used to predict AMELY. However, LLOQ is primarily a property of the instrument, and a higher density of peptides would maximize sampling of log peptide values around the LLOQ, potentially increasing accuracy and reducing variability. It is possible that using all amelogenin peptides, not just those specific for either of the paralogous genes, or even all peptides present in the dataset would improve LLOQ accuracy and reduce variability. Also, this study confined data analysis to peptides identified in the Database search mode of PEAKS XPro. Potential use of the subsequent PEAKS36 PTM-mode of peptide spectral matching roughly doubled the number of matches and increases overall ion current collection by about 10% to 25%. This study conducted its analysis using peptides matched using a custom reference proteome based on the limited enamel proteome with orthologous proteins across a range of primate species. The use of the custom, highly concise reference proteome increased the peptide signal 3.7-fold (p=0), the LLOQ was effectively unchanged. This was consistent with the noted increase in sensitivity when using smaller customized reference proteomes.80

The AMELX-specific peptides with the highest signals also deviate from the linear regression based off the middle 50% of the peptide rank curve. A thorough analysis of whether the disproportionate signals of this population are due to higher concentration, higher ionization efficiency, or a mixture of both has not been established. The effect of this peptide population on detected signal in the zone between the LLOQ and LLOD is also not analyzed, and more sophisticated models of AMELY prediction promise to give more precise and accurate cumulative probability estimates.

Finally, this framework assumes a normal distribution of measurements that were used to calculate estimates of logLLOQ and log(X/Y). Use of the Z-score to estimate cumulative probability of female sex assumes normal distribution, and replication of this framework on other datasets and samples should include normality tests such as the Shapiro Wilk test.
