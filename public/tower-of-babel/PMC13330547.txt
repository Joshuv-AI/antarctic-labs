Evaluation of dietary curcumin-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles on growth, blood biochemistry, gut barrier integrity and enteric gas emissions in weaned piglets
Authors: Sungyeon Chin, Yunhui Min, Mohammad Moniruzzaman, Yunji Heo, Md Meraj Ansari, Godagama Gamaarachchige Dinesh Suminda, Chang-Gi Hur, Kwang-Seob Shin, Young-Ok Son, Taesun Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Dietary curcumin-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles (SL@Cur) were evaluated in weaned piglets.•SL@Cur significantly improved body weight and upregulated the expression of myogenic genes.•SL@Cur significantly reduced serum triglyceride and total cholesterol concentrations, indicating an improvement in lipid metabolism.•SL@Cur upregulated zonula occludens-1 and claudin-2, enhancing gut barrier integrity.•SL@Cur increased beneficial gut microbiota and reduced fecal NH₃ and H2S emissions.Keywords: Curcumin, Mesoporous silica nanoparticles, Growth performance, Gut health, Enteric gas emissions

Introduction

In recent years, the livestock industry has shifted its focus beyond mere productivity toward fostering sustainable farming environments and enhancing animal welfare (Almeida et al., 2024; Gallois et al., 2009). Within the swine sector in particular, issues such as odor reduction, environmental improvement, and production efficiency have emerged as critical challenges (Moniruzzaman et al., 2021, 2023). These concerns have prompted active technological innovation and growing policy interest. Odor emissions from pig farms can significantly impair the quality of life for nearby residents, often leading to complaints and community conflicts, which in turn threaten the long-term viability of livestock operations (Lalles et al., 2021; Moniruzzaman et al., 2021). Moreover, regulatory restrictions on the use of antibiotics as growth promoters are driving a paradigm shift in conventional rearing practices (Gallois et al., 2009; Sung et al., 2025). As a result, the development of functional feed additives and improvements in housing conditions are being recognized as essential strategies for simultaneously ensuring productivity and promoting animal welfare (Liu et al., 2018; Moniruzzaman et al., 2020).

In this trend, the use of natural products based on nanotechnology is drawing attention as a functional feed additive (El-Ratel et al., 2020; Moniruzzaman et al., 2021, 2023; Qadeer et al., 2024). Curcumin is the main active compound of turmeric (Curcuma longa), a rhizome belonging to the ginger family (Zingiberaceae) (Moniruzzaman et al., 2020). Curcumin is a natural compound with physiological activities such as strong antioxidants, anti-inflammatory, and immune control and is evaluated as an effective functional material for improving animal health and improving growth rate (Moniruzzaman et al., 2020). Use of curcumin nanoparticles solve the major limitations of natural curcumin and considerably boost its medicinal effectiveness (Moniruzzaman et al., 2021, 2023). Curcumin is a strong molecule, yet on its own it has poor solubility, low absorption, quick metabolism, and low bioavailability. Nanoparticle formulations overcome these issues. To maximize the efficacy of curcumin, curcumin nanospheres (CN) was developed, which is a strategy to increase intestinal absorption and physiological activity (Moniruzzaman et al., 2021, 2023). Silica nanoparticles have structural stability and a porous structure inside, so they can effectively load and protect functional substances such as curcumin He and Shi (2011); Janjua et al. (2023); Le et al. (2021); Li et al. (2017); Man et al. (2024); O, Shea et al. (2017); Tang et al. (2012); Trewyn et al. (2008). Due to its sustained-release characteristics that the components are slowly released after safely reaching the intestine, it can maintain physiological activity for longer and is highly utilized as a delivery vehicle for oral supplementation due to its high biocompatibility (Hadisoewignyo et al., 2018; Janjua et al., 2023).

Existing studies have demonstrated the functional effects of curcumin or silica nanoparticles alone (Lu et al., 2010; Fu et al., 2013; Bashar et al., 2021; Bhagat & Singh, 2022; Chin et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2020; Michalak et al., 2022; Moniruzzaman et al., 2021; Moniruzzaman et al., 2023; Pieszka et al., 2019). For example, Moniruzzaman et al. (2021) reported that when weaning piglets were fed curcumin nanospheres as a diet, daily body weight gain and feed conversion efficiency were improved, and ammonia emissions were reduced. The same research team also confirmed significant results such as weight gain, improvement of lipid metabolism, change in intestinal morphology, and reduction of odor in a follow-up study on finishing pigs (Moniruzzaman et al., 2023). In addition, Dosoki et al. (2021) reported that the addition of silver silica complex nanoparticles (SiO2@AgNPs) to broiler diets at concentrations of up to 4 mg/kg resulted in improved growth performance without adverse effects on hematologic or oxidative stress responses. These studies support the efficacy of each nanomaterials, however, the combined treatment of curcumin and silica nanoparticles has not yet been sufficiently verified for food animal studies (Jiao et al., 2024). In particular, most of the previous studies remain at the laboratory level for therapeutic studies in terms of human welfare (Chen et al., 2018; Freidus et al., 2021; Iranshahy et al., 2023; Jambhrunkar et al., 2014; Kotcherlakota et al., 2016; Lo et al., 2021). However, from the best of our knowledge, no studies reported to evaluate curcumin-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles (SL@Cur) for growth and health improvment as well as environmental impact on livestock animals under actual farm condition.

This study comprehensively evaluated the growth performance and the effect of improving the air in the pig house by adding SL@Cur in the feed of weaned piglets. To increase the applicability of the actual livestock field, this study evaluated key factors such as growth, serum biochemistry, gut health and odor index by integrating animal welfare and environmental pollution indicators. This study presents a scientific basis for realizing sustainable livestock by verifying the practicality and environmental effectiveness of nanocomposite-based feed additives.

Materials and methods

Curcumin-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles (SL@Cur)

Curcumin-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles (SL@Cur) were obtained as a commercial product, Cumin-Soluball (Silexn Technology Co., Ltd., Busan, Republic of Korea).

Characterization of SL@Cur

Dynamic light scattering and zeta potential

The mean hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of SL@Cur nanoparticles were measured using dual-angle dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a Nano ZS analyzer (Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Malvern, UK) as described earlier (Ansari et al., 2024). Samples were placed in disposable polystyrene cuvettes, and measurements were performed in triplicate. The reported values represent the averages of three independent measurements.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM analysis was performed by depositing a drop of the SL@Cur nanoparticles solution onto a carbon-coated 300-mesh copper grid (Ted Pella Inc., CA, USA) and incubating for 20–30 min to allow adsorption as reported earlier (Ahmad et al., 2021). The grids were then dried overnight in a desiccator and visualized using a JEOL-1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Seoul, Korea) equipped with a tungsten filament at 120 kV. TEM images were captured and processed using Gatan camera software.

Field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM)

For FE-SEM analysis, SL@Cur nanoparticles were dispersed in Milli-Q water and drop-cast onto a silicon wafer, followed by overnight drying via slow evaporation as reported earlier (Ansari et al., 2025). The samples were then coated with a thin layer of gold using an auto fine coater for 30 s at 30 Pa. Nanoparticle morphology was examined using a MIRA3 FE-SEM (Tescan, Seoul, Korea) at an accelerating voltage of 5–10 kV.

Experimental design, animal and diets

A total of 36 weaned piglets (28 ± 1 days of age, 6–7 kg body weight, mixed sex) were used in a 21-day trial. Animals were randomly assigned to three groups: a control group and two treatment groups supplemented with SL@Cur at 30 or 60 mg/kg in commercial powder diets (Neopigg, Purina, Seoul, Rep. of Korea). The basal diet was a commercial weaned piglet diet available in Korea, and its nutrient composition is presented in Table 1. The actual concentrations of silica in the diets were found to be 291, 297, and 320 mg/kg for control, SL@Cur at 30 or 60 mg/kg diet, respectively (Fig. 1g). Each group included three replicates with four pigs per replicate pen (4 pigs/pen, 12 pigs/treatment). Each pig was ear tagged with number for proper identification during the sampling time. All pigs have easy access to normal drinking water and feed supply. The room temperature was maintained at 28 °Cduring the experimental period and pens related to drainage facilities. Each pen was considered the experimental unit for growth performance measurements. For tissue and serum analyses, piglets were randomly selected from each pen and used as biological replicates.

Growth and muscle related gene expressions in weaned piglets

At the end of the 21-day feeding trial, the weights of all piglets were measured individually in weight cages according to the pen number and treatment group numbers in terms of final weight, final weight gain (WG) and average daily gain (ADG). Afterwards, one piglet from each pen (12 pens) were slaughtered humanely after 21 days. The tissue samples from longissimus dorsi muscles between 12th and 13th rib spaces of each piglet were collected and instantly kept on dry ices inside the insulated box and further stored at −80 °C for RNA extraction.

Measurement of serum biochemical and antioxidant parameters

On day 21, blood samples were collected from three randomly selected pigs per treatment group via anterior vena cava puncture using clot activator tubes. Blood was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 15 min to separate the serum. Serum biochemical concentrations in terms of triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TCHO), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), glucose (GLU), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured using a serum biochemical analyzer (FUJI DRI-CHEM NX500; Fujifilm, Japan) in piglets fed the SL@Cur at 0, 30 and 60 mg/kg diet. Serum antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was assessed following the manufacturer’s instructions using a commercial SOD assay kit (Cat# ab65354, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Moreover, cortisol concentrations in the serum of piglets were measured using a cortisol kit following the manufacturer’s protocols (Cat# ab108665, Abcam, Cambridge, UK).

Histomorphology of muscle and intestinal tissues

The intestinal histological samples were prepared as described earlier(Moniruzzaman et al., 2023). Briefly, jejunal tissue samples (5 cm each) from the mid part of intestine were collected from the one slaughtered pigs from each of the pens and stored in paraformaldehyde (4%) at refrigerator temperature (4 °C) for 24 hrs. The collected tissue samples were further processed in a series of organic solvents with different concentrations using a automatic tissue processor machine (Leica TP1020, Leica Biosystems, Seoul, Rep. of Korea). The processed tissue were fixed in liquid paraffin solution in histological cassettes and then the samples sliced (5 µm thickness) using a microtome machine with sharp cutter (HistoCore BIOCUT, Leica Biosystems, Seoul, Rep. of Korea). The sliced tissue sections were further stained using Mayer’s hematoxylin (Agilent DAKO, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and eosin solutions (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) mounted with Canada balsam and covered with cover slips on the glass tissue slides. The prepared tissue sections were closely monitored using a LEICA DM 2500 microscope (Leica Biosystems, Seoul, Rep. of Korea) adjusted with a digital camera a (Tucsen Photonics Co., Ltd.) along with computer display facilities to obtain the specific images of tissue samples in terms of villi height. For the muscle histomorphology of weaned piglets, the histological samples were prepared as the same procedures described for the intestinal tissues and the images of muscle tissues were taken. The cross-sectional muscle were analyzed by Leica AiVIA AI software (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) to measured myofiber size.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) of gut barrier proteins

In case of IHC assays after 21 days, the immunohistochemical samples from jejunal parts of intestine in weaned piglets were prepared followed by previous study (Moniruzzaman et al., 2023). Briefly, intestinal tissue samples from jejunum were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated using series of alcohol solvents. To enhance the permeability of intestinal tissues, the samples were further treated with peroxidase blocking solution. For tissue antigen retrieval and blocking, 0.05% trypsin solution with 0.01% calcium chloride and 1% BAS in PBS was used, respectively. Afterwards, the respective antibodies for tight junction proteins such as 1:200 anti-ZO-1 (Cat#5406, Cell Signaling, Massachusetts, USA), 1:1000 anti-occludin (Cat#331500, invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA), 1:500 anti-claudin-1 (Cat#166338, Santa Cruz, Texas, USA) were used and incubated for overnight at 4 °C. The secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature for 1hr using with anti-mouse/rabbit secondary antibody of Dak Real Envision™ kit (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, UAS). The tissue were developed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate solution and stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Agilent DAKO, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The tissue sections were analyzed for the expressions of ZO-1, occludin and claudin-1 under a LEICA DM 2500 microscope (Tucsen Photonics Co., Ltd.) with Image J software (Image J 1.32j, National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Real-time PCR

The mRNA expression levels of genes associated with muscle tissues such as paired box 7 protein (PAX7), myosin-binding protein H (MYBPH), myogenic facor 5 (Myf5) and myogenic facor 6 (Myf6) as well as intestinal (jejunum) tight junction proteins such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), Occludin, Claudin-1 and Claudin-2 were analyzed using quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR). Total RNA was extracted from stored muscle and intestinal tissue samples (0.5 g for each tissues) with TRI Reagent (Molecular Research Center Inc., Cincinnati, OH, USA) following the user’s guideline. The concentration and purity of the RNA (OD260/280) was checked spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). The synthesized complementary DNA (cDNA) was obtained from 1 µg of total RNA using ImProm-II Reverse Transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was carried out on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with HAN qPCR Master Mix containing SYBR Green I (HanLab, Cheongju-si, Chungcheongbuk-do, Korea). The RT-qPCR (iCycler, Agilent Technologies Inc, Santa Clara, CA, USA, in Bio-Health Materials Core-Facility, Jeju National University) was carried out to analyze the relative mRNA expressions for muscle and intestinal tissues against the house-keeping gene, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GADPH) as the internal control using PowerTrack SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied biosystems, Massachusetts, USA). The final volume of each PCR sample was 10 μL consisting of 10 ng of cDNA, 0.5 μL of each 10 pmole forward and reverse primers, 5 μL of SYBR Green Master Mix and 2.7 μL of PCR grade distilled water (dH2O). The PCR conditions consisted of 40 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 20 s. Data analysis was conducted using QuantStudio™ Design & Analysis Software and calculated by employing the 2−∆∆CT method. Every PCR sample was amplified in triplicate. The gene-specific primers with forward and reverse senses, base pairs and gene bank accession numbers utilized in the present study are listed in Table 2.

Gut microbiota analysis

On day 21 of the feeding trial, aseptic samples of jejunal digesta from piglets (1 g per pig; n = 3 per treatment group) were collected and submitted to Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, Korea) for sequencing. Jejunal digesta were selected because the jejunum is a primary site of nutrient digestion and absorption, as well as of interactions between dietary components and the intestinal epithelium. Bacterial DNA was extracted and subjected to high-throughput sequencing of the V3–V4 hypervariable region (Forward 5ʹ-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3ʹ, Reverse 5ʹ-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3ʹ) of the 16S rRNA gene using the Illumina MiSeq platform (NovaSeq 6000, San Diego, CA, USA). Initial quality control involved removing adapter and primer sequences using Cutadapt (v3.2). Forward and reverse reads were subsequently trimmed to 250 base pairs and 200 base pairs, respectively. Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) were identified utilizing the DADA2 pipeline (v1.18.0), which effectively denoised low-quality and chimeric reads. Taxonomic classification of ASVs was performed using a naive Bayes classifier with a 50% confidence threshold against the NCBI 16S rRNA reference database. Downstream analyses pertaining to microbial diversity were performed using QIIME (v1.9.0). Taxonomic assignment and subsequent microbiome analyses were grounded on the ASV data.

Alpha diversity was assessed through three complementary metrics: Shannon diversity, observed amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), and Faith's phylogenetic diversity. These metrics describe within-sample diversity from distinct yet complementary perspectives: taxonomic richness, richness with evenness, and phylogenetic breadth. This approach aligns with contemporary microbiome research, in which Shannon diversity is frequently reported alongside other alpha-diversity measures, and studies often compare Shannon, observed richness, and Faith's PD. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to evaluate alpha diversity across groups. When significant differences were detected, Dunn's test with the Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied for pairwise comparisons.

Beta diversity was evaluated employing Bray-Curtis dissimilarity as the primary metric, with weighted and unweighted UniFrac providing as supplemental phylogenetic metrics. Principal coordinates analysis was performed for each distance matrix, and group differences in microbial community composition were evaluated using PERMANOVA with 999 permutations. Pairwise comparisons were conducted when the overall PERMANOVA was significant, and p-values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

Based on LEfSe, a biomarker analysis was performed in R using the lefser package (Khleborodova et al., 2024). Abundance, taxonomy, and metadata tables were processed using phyloseq (McMurdie et al., 2013), mia (Borman et al., 2026), and SummarizedExperiment (Morgan et al., 2025). After filtering out zero-abundance taxa and samples with zero abundance and total counts, relative abundances were calculated, and terminal taxonomic nodes were retained for analysis. Pairwise LEfSe comparisons were performed for Control and SL@Cur 30, as well as Control and SL@Cur 60, were conducted using Kruskal-Wallis and Wilcoxon tests with an alpha level of 0.05 and an LDA score threshold of 2.0.

Fecal gas emissions in weaned piglets

At the end of the experiment on day 21, fecal gas analysis was performed based on the method described by Moniruzzaman et al. (2021), with slight modifications. Briefly, after collecting the excreted feces for the same time from each treatment group in triplicate pens, near about 300 g of fresh feces from each pen was placed in a sealed plastic container of 2600 ml. The fecal ammonia (NH3) and hydrogen sulfide (H2S) gases were measured using a multi-gas meter (MultiRAE Lite model PGM-6208, RAE, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data were checked for normality and homogeneity of variance using Shapiro-Wilk and O’Brien tests, respectively. Statistical differences among treatment groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using OriginPro®2025 software (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Gut microbiome data were generated with RStudio package (v4.1.0).

Result

Characteristics of SL@Cur and concentration of silica in feeds

The results demonstrated that the average hydrodynamic diameter of the SL@Cur nanoparticles was 390.8 nm, with a PDI of 0.40, indicating a moderately narrow size distribution in the colloidal suspension (Fig. 1a). The zeta potential was −24.3 mV, suggesting a relatively stable nanoparticle system due to sufficient electrostatic repulsion between particles (Fig. 1b). Current–voltage relationship recorded over time during hydrodynamic size analysis that confirm the electrophoretic conditions are stable, ensuring reliable zeta potential and size measurements (Fig. 1c). Phase relationship recorded over time during particle size analysis that ensures the light scattering signal is coherent and interpretable, leading to accurate determination of particle size and distribution (Fig. 1d). Morphological characterization using TEM (Fig. 1e) and FE-SEM (Fig. 1f) further revealed SL@Cur nanoparticles exhibited a predominantly spherical morphology with a smooth surface, and the actual particle size was approximately 361.6 nm, which is slightly smaller than the hydrodynamic diameter obtained from dynamic light scattering due to the absence of the hydration layer during electron microscopy analysis. Additionally, elemental analysis indicated that the silicon (Si) concentrations in the experimental diets were 291, 297, and 320 mg/kg in the groups supplemented with 0, 30, and 60 mg/kg of SL@Cur nanoparticles, respectively, confirming the successful incorporation of silica-based nanoparticles into the formulated diets (Fig. 1g).

Effects of dietary SL@Cur on growth and muscle related gene expressions in weaned piglets

The results of the pig growth and muscle related gene expressions were presented in Table 3 and Fig. 2. At the end of 21 days feeding trial, the results of the present study showed that the final body weight in weaned piglets fed the SL@Cur at 30 and 60 mg/kg diet groups were significantly higher than the control group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2a and Table 3). However, there is no significant difference in FBW of piglets fed the SL@Cur at 30 and 60 mg/kg diet groups (p < 0.05). Furthermore, WG and ADG were found to be significantly higher in piglets fed the SL@Cur at 30 and 60 mg/kg diet groups than in the control group (Table 3) (p < 0.05). However, there were no significant differences in WG and ADG in piglets fed the SL@Cur at 30 and 60 mg/kg diet groups (p > 0.05). The histological analysis showed that muscle fiber cross-sectional area were improved in piglets fed the SL@Cur at 30 and 60 mg/kg diets compared to the control group (Fig. 2b and c). Furthermore, the muscle related mRNA gene expression such as Pax7 was significantly higher in piglets fed the SL@Cur at 60 mg/kg diet than in the SL@Cur at 30 mg/kg and control diets (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2d). However, there were no significant differences in MYBPH myogenic gene expressions in piglets fed the control as well as SL@Cur at 30 and 60 mg/kg diets (p > 0.05). Moreover, the relative mRNA gene expression levels for Myf5 and Myf6 against GADPH house-keeping gene were significantly higher in piglets fed the SL@Cur at 60 mg/kg diet than the those of piglets fed the control and SL@Cur at 30 mg/kg diets (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2f and g). However, there is no significant differences in Myf5 and Myf6 gene expressions between control and SL@Cur at 30 mg/kg diet groups (p > 0.05).

Effects of dietary SL@Cur on blood serum biochemistry in weaned piglets

The results of the serum biochemical parameters in weaned piglets after 21 days were presented in Fig. 3. The results showed that piglets fed the SL@Cur at 30 mg/kg had significantly lower TG and TCHO levels than in the control group (SL@Cur at 0 mg/kg diet) (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3a,b). On the other hand, high density lipoprotein level (HDL) in piglets fed the SL@Cur at 30 mg/kg diet group was significantly higher than in the control group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3b). However, there were no significant differences in serum TG, TCHO and HDL levels between SL@Cur at 60 mg/kg and control groups or between SL@Cur at 30 and 60 mg/kg diets (p > 0.05) (Fig. 3a,b,c). This suggest that dietary supplementation of SL@Cur could lower the body cholesterol level in weaned piglets. Serum glucose (GLU) levels in piglets decreases dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3d). Here, piglets fed the SL@Cur at 60 mg/kg diet had significantly lower GLU content than the control group (p < 0.05). Serum ALT level in piglets fed the SL@Cur at 30 mg/kg diet showed significantly lower concentrations than the control group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3e). There were no significant differences in serum AST levels among piglets fed the experimental diets (0, 30 and 60 mg/kg diets) (p > 0.05) (Fig. 3f).

Effects of dietary SL@Cur on serum antioxidant enzymes

Serum superoxide dismutase (SOD) levels in piglets fed the SL@Cur at 0, 30 and 60 mg/kg diets for 21 days showed no significant differences. These results suggest that dietary supplementation of SL@Cur did not affect the antioxidant capacity in weaned piglets (Fig. 4a). Likewise, serum cortisol levels demonstrated no significant differences in piglets fed the SL@Cur at 0, 30 and 60 mg/kg diets for 21 days (p > 0.05). These results confirmed that dietary supplementation of SL@Cur did not create any stress condition in weaned piglets that may have deleterious effect on health status of piglets (Fig. 4b).

Effects of dietary SL@Cur on gut morphology, IHC and gene expressions

The results of the gut morphology showed that piglets fed the SL@Cur at 30 and 60 mg/kg diet had no significant differences in terms of villus length and crypt depth in small intestine than the those in the control group (p > 0.05) (Fig. 5a–c). Moreover, IHC and the mRNA gene expressions for gut barrier function proteins in jejunal part of the intestine of weaned piglets were also presented (Fig. 5d–k). The tight junction protein ZO-1 content was found to be significantly higher in intestinal sections of piglets fed the 60 mg/kg SL@Cur diet than the control group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5e). However, the results showed that ZO-1 gene expression was not significantly affected in piglets fed the control, 30 and 60 mg SL@Cur /kg diets (p > 0.05) (Fig. 5h). The mRNA expression levels of occludin and claudin-1 were also not significantly affected by the experimental diets which were reflected in IHC slides of occludin and claudin-1 (p > 0.05). The results showed that the mRNA expressions of claudin-2 was significantly upregulated in piglets fed the SL@Cur at 30 mg/kg compared to the SL@Cur at 60 mg/kg and control diets (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5k). However, there is no significant difference in claudin-2 gene expression in piglets fed the control and 60 mg SL@Cur /kg diets (p > 0.05).

Effects of dietary SL@Cur on gut microbiota

The results for the intestinal microbiomes of piglets were presented in Fig. 6, which showed alterations in bacterial communities across the different dietary treatment groups. The relative abundance profiles of bacterial taxa are presented at the phylum and genus levels in Fig. 6a and b, respectively. At the phylum level, Bacillota and Bacteroidota were the predominant phyla across all treatment groups (Fig. 6a). The relative abundance of Bacillota increased progressively with increasing SL@Cur dose from 0 to 60 mg/kg, whereas Bacteroidota showed a decreasing trend. At the genus level, a large proportion of bacterial taxa remained unclassified (Fig. 6b). Among the classified genera, Clostridium exhibited a reduction in relative abundance following SL@Cur treatment. In contrast, the relative abundances of Limosilactobacillus, Lactobacillus, and Segatella increased with treatment, suggesting a dose-dependent shift in microbial community composition. Overall, SL@Cur treatment altered the relative abundance of dominant bacterial taxa, particularly by increasing Bacillota-associated genera and reducing Clostridium abundance.

Alpha diversity was determined using three complementary parameters: Shannon diversity, observed amplicon sequence variations (ASVs), and Faith's phylogenetic diversity. Alpha diversity based on the Shannon index was not significantly different among the SL@Cur 0 mg/kg, SL@Cur 30 mg/kg, and SL@Cur 60 mg/kg groups (Kruskal Wallis, p = 0.561) (Fig. 6c), implying similar within sample microbial diversity across groups. The beta diversity of Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was visualised using PCoA, with PC1 explaining 41.96% of the total variation and PC2 explaining 20.37% (Fig. 6d). According to PERMANOVA analysis, overall bacterial community composition did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.44, R2 = 0.229), indicating that between-group differences in community structure were not significant. LEfSe analysis demonstrated that SL@Cur supplementation altered the gut microbial structure of piglets in comparison to the control group, with both shared and dose specific taxonomic signatures (Fig. 6e,f). Using an LDA threshold of >2, the 30 mg/kg and 60 mg/kg treatments each produced distinct biomarker profiles instead of a simple linear shift across all taxa. Notably, s_Lactobacillus gasseri indicated the strongest discrimination in the 30 mg/kg group, with an LDA score of 4.66, suggesting that this species was a major contributor to differentiation from the control group. Because LEfSe identifies taxa that best explain between group separation, these findings suggest treatment dependent biomarker groups; however, do not establish a monotonic concentration response relationship.

Compared with the control group, the SL@Cur 30 mg/kg group was enriched in s_Lactobacillus gasseri, s_Segatella copri, s_Leyella stercorea, s_Dorea longicatena, s_Ligilactobacillus salivarius, and g_Streptococcus, whereas s_Intestinimonas timonensis, s_Lentihominibacter faecis, g_Xylanibacter, s_Muribaculum gordoncarteri, and o_Eubacteriales were reduced. In contrast, the SL@Cur 60 mg/kg group was enriched in g_Streptococcus, f_Prevotellaceae, s_Hoylesella oralis, s_Butyricicoccus porcorum, s_Dorea longicatena, s_Duncaniella dubosii, and s_Lentihominibacter faecis, while f_Tannerellaceae, s_Flintibacter butyricus, s_Sarcina ventriculi, s_Wujia chipingensis, s_Collinsella aerofaciens, c_Bacteroidia, p_Bacteroidota, s_Subdoligranulum variabile, s_Eubacterium coprostanoligenes, s_Muribaculum gordoncarteri, and o_Eubacteriales were depleted. Overall, some responses were shared across doses, including enrichment of Streptococcus and depletion of Muribaculum gordoncarteri and Eubacteriales, whereas others were dose specific, particularly enrichment of L. gasseri and L. salivarius at 30 mg/kg and enrichment of Prevotellaceae and B. porcorum at 60 mg/kg. Taken together, our findings corroborate SL@Cur's dose dependent but taxon specific impact on the piglet gut microbiota. Overall, biomarker levels are not uniformly impacted by concentration, suggesting that microbial remodelling is dose-dependent, rather than the incidence of all biomarkers is uniformly increased or reduced.

Furthermore, fecal hydrogen sulfide (H2S) gas contents in piglets fed the SL@Cur at 30 and 60 mg/kg diet were significantly lower than the control group (0 mg SL@Cur/kg diet) (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7d).

Discussion

In weaning period of pigs, there are some physiological stresses may occur such as growth retardation, decreased antioxidant activities, metabolic disorders, impaired immunity, compromised intestinal health and alterations in gut microbial diversity which ultimately affect the production efficiency of the livestock farms. For this, globally antibiotics have been using indiscriminately in diets to promote growth performance in weaning pigs. However, overwhelming use of antibiotics could increase antibiotic resistance in animals which would be more hazardous and may cause inevitable loses to livestock farms. Therefore, utilization of hazard free nanomedicine as the alternatives of antibiotics in animal feeds could enhance the health and immunity in animals. Recently, a significant number of studies have been reported on utilization of nanocurcumin in livestock specially swine health and nutrition. In addition, few studies on silica nanoparticles also reported the beneficial effects in terms of weight gain, feed efficiency, immunity, gut health and disease resistance. However, for reports on dietary supplementation of SL@Cur are very limited especially in animal nutrition and health. In previous studies, most of studies showed the insignificant effect of curcumin or nanocurcumin on the zootechnical performance of pigs, probably due to the lack of bioavailability or target delivery of curcumin. In the present study, we emphasized on the effective utilization of SL@Cur in terms of improving growth, immunity and gut health of weaned piglets based on ‘multi-omics’ approaches.

In this study, we found that the average size of the SL@Cur was 390.8 nm and the nanomaterials were spherical shapes in structure with average −24.3 mV zeta potential. Bashar et al. (2021) reported that silica nanoparticles would be 100 nm to 400 nm in diameter which is in agreement with the present study. Furthermore, Shabana et al. (2021) reported the average diameter of mesoporous silica nanoparticles loaded quercetin were 290 nm.

In our study, dietary supplementation of SL@Cur at 30 and 60 mg/kg diet showed significant improvement in final weight (FBW), weight gain (WG) and average daily gain (ADG) of weaned piglets compared to the control group. In our previous studies, we reported that dietary nanocurcumin can enhance growth performance in weaned and finishing pigs (Moniruzzaman et al., 2021, 2023). Similarly, El-Ratel et al. (2020) and Jastaniah et al. (2024) reported that dietary nano-curcumin can enhance the growth and production efficiency in growing rabbits and European seabass fish fingerlings, respectively. However, Bao et al. (2022) did not find any significant differences in largemouth bass fish supplemented with dietary nano-curcumin at different doses compared to the control diet. Furthermore, Rahmani et al. (2017) found no significant differences in broiler chicken fed the nano-curcumin supplemented (200 and 400 mg/kg) diets compared to the normal curcumin fed diet group. In a recent report, it has been demonstrated that dietary supplementation of silicate at 0.5% could enhance the body weight in weaning to finishing pigs; however, higher inclusion of silicate at 1.0% negatively affect the weight gain in pigs (Lee et al., 2020). Moreover, dietary silica nanoparticles at 2 mg/kg diet showed positive effects on growth performance in Nile tilapia compared to the higher dose at 3 mg/kg diet (Bashar et al., 2021). Mesoporous silica or its nanoform have shown to improve bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs by enabling rapid dissolution and super saturation as well as drug delivery system such as fenofibrate, quercetin or curcumin Iranshahy et al. (2023); O’Shea et al. (2017); Shabana et al. (2021). Shabana et al.(2021) reported that oral supplementation of mesoporous silica nanoparticles loaded quercetin at 100 mg/kg diet improved the health status in tilapia fish as the monogastric animal model which is in agreement with the current study in pigs for SL@Cur. However, in our experiment, feed intake was not individually recorded during the experimental period due to group housing conditions, and therefore feed conversion ratio (FCR) could not be calculated. Nevertheless, the significant increase in ADG observed in the SL@Cur-treated groups suggests improved growth performance. Future studies incorporating precise measurements of feed intake and feed efficiency will be necessary to confirm the effects of SL@Cur supplementation on feed conversion efficiency in weaned piglets. In accordance with the body weight gain, the muscle related gene expressions such as Pax7, Myf5 and Myf6 were significantly upregulated in piglets fed the SL@Cur supplemented diets. Similarly, Wang et al. (2024) reported that dietary supplementation of nano-curcumin at 0.2% can improve the myogenic gene expressions such as MSTN, Myf5 and Myf6 compared to the control diet in largemouth bass fish. The muscle histology in weaned piglets also demonstrated that muscle fibers diameters and cross-sectional area were increased in piglets fed the SL@Cur supplemented diets compared to the control diet which is in agreement with the results reported by Wang et al. (2024).

Blood serum data are important to reveal the information related to the health status in humans or animals. In the present study, piglets supplied with the SL@Cur at 30 mg/kg diet had significantly higher HDL than the control group. Moreover, piglets fed the SL@Cur at 30 mg/kg diet showed decreased total cholesterol and triglyceride levels in comparison to the control diet. These results suggested that dietary supplementation of SL@Cur at 30 mg/kg may reduce the bad lipids and increase the good lipid profile in weaned piglets. Furthermore, dietary SL@Cur at 30 mg/kg found to reduce the serum glucose and ALT levels compared to the control group. In line with the current study, Moniruzzaman et al. (2021) reported that dietary supplementation of curcumin nanosphere could reduce the serum total cholesterol level in weaned piglets. Likewise, Jastaniah et al. (2024) reported that dietary nano-curcumin at 50–70 mg/kg diet can reduce serum cholesterol, ALT and glucose which endorsed the blood serum data in the present study. Furthermore, El-Ratel et al. (2020) found that dietary nano-curcumin at 2.5 or 5 mg/kg diet could enhance the serum HDL but reduce the cholesterol, triglycerides and ALT levels compared to the control diet which also support the serum blood biochemistry data in the current study. In a study, Dosoky et al. (2021) did not find any significant differences in blood cholesterol and ALT levels on dietary supplementation of silver-silica nanoparticles in broiler chickens. However, the HDL and triglycerides levels were increased in broilers fed the silver-silica nanoparticles supplied diets (Dosoky et al., 2021). Overall, the hematological results of our study confirmed the beneficial effects of SL@Cur through improving liver health and improving blood parameters in young pigs.

Antioxidant enzymes have significant role as the oxidative stress indicating biomarkers in innate immunity after supplementing feed additives in animals such as SOD enzyme (Hossain et al., 2025). During antioxidant enzyme activities, superoxide anions (O2−) in SOD are turned into hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) by SOD. The SOD is an important antioxidant enzyme that prevent the production of hydroxyl radicals where SOD enzymes help for scavenging free radicals and protect body from the harmful effect of reactive oxygen species (ROS). On the other hand, cortisol is steroid hormone that plays an important role in body’s stress response, helping to regulate metabolism, blood glucose and inflammation (Rahman et al., 2022). In the present study, the SOD activity and cortisol concentration at both doses (30 and 60 mg/kg) were not significantly affected by the dietary supplementations of SL@Cur compared to the control group in weaned piglets. In agreement with our study, Rahman et al. (2022) found decreasing trend of SOD at higher doses of silica nanoparticles (100–300 mg/L) with increasing health status in African catfish. In addition, it was demonstrated that cortisol hormone levels increased as the doses of silica nanoparticles were increased (Rahman et al., 2022). On the contrary of our study, Jastaniah et al. (2024) reported that dietary supplementation of nano-curcumin significantly increased the SOD enzyme activities compared to the control diet in European seabass fish. The results of the present study corroborated that the administration of SL@Cur at 30 and/or 60 mg/kg diets were not harmful or stressful as well as not compromised the immunity in weaned piglets. Although SOD activity was measured in this study, other oxidative stress markers such as malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and catalase (CAT) were not evaluated. Therefore, the antioxidant effects of SL@Cur supplementation should be interpreted with caution, and further studies including multiple oxidative stress biomarkers are needed to fully elucidate its antioxidant potential.

Gut health morphology can reveal the absorption and utilization pattern of dietary additive in the intestine which reflect the zootechnical performance in animals. The small intestine of animals are consist of three parts such as duodenum, jejunum and ileum where jejunum is the most important part of small intestine as the site of nutrient absorption. Any abnormalities or morphological changes in gastrointestinal tract (GIT) may affect the growth and immunity in animals. In the present study, villi height and crypt depth in jejunam of small intestine were found to be not significantly changed in piglets supplied with SL@Cur than the control diet. The histological images confirmed that bioavailability of SL@Cur in terms of surface area of villi for absorption and utilization of SL@Cur were not compromised that reflected in growth and immunity in young pigs. In agreement with the current study, Moniruzzaman et al. (2023) found positive effects of dietary nanocurcumin in improvement of gut morphology in finishing pigs. Rahmani et al. (2018) also reported that dietary nanocurcumin at 200 mg/kg diet can enhance the jejunal tissue health in terms of enhancing villi height in broiler chicken. Furthermore, Bashar et al. (2021) postulated that dietary supplementation of silica nanoparticles at 2 mg/kg enhanced the villi width and goblet cell numbers in the intestine of Nile tilapia fish.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) is the most reliable method for understanding antigen-antibody interaction to identify specific proteins in intestinal tissue sections of animals through the immunostaining process (Moniruzzaman et al., 2023). The IHC through chromogenic reaction can detect the specific tight junction proteins in GIT by conjugating antibody with peroxidase enzymes. The GIT of animals can be consist of epithelial cellular barrier and immune functional barriers systems (Moniruzzaman et al., 2023). The tight junction proteins such as claudins, occludins and zona occludins-1 (ZO-1) are most important functional proteins that help the intestinal integrity through generating the physiological and immunological barriers in intestine (Moniruzzaman et al., 2023). However, the abnormalities in function and limited contents of tight junction proteins in intestinal tissue may harm the overall GIT function in terms of lack of intestinal permeability, digestive disorders and poor absorption of nutrients. In the present study, the chromogenic expressions of ZO-1 and claudin-1 were higher in piglets supplied with SL@Cur than the control group. Furthermore, dietary supplementation with SL@Cur upregulated the gene expression of ZO-1, occludin and claudin-1 in piglets. Notably, claudin-2 expression was also significantly increased in SL@Cur-treated groups, suggesting that SL@Cur may enhance intestinal barrier integrity while potentially improving its own bioavailability. In consistent with the present study, it has been reported that dietary curcumin nanospheres can enhance the chromogenic expressions of tight junction protein such as claudin using IHC method (Moniruzzaman et al., 2023). In agreement with the present study, dietary garlic essential oil as the phytogenic material upregulated the expressions of ZO-1 and claudin-1 genes in weaned piglets (Chen et al., 2025). Furthermore, Pu et al. (2020) reported the combined effects of benzoic acid, Bacillus coagulans and oregano oil upregulated the gene expressions of claudin-1, occludin and ZO-1 compared to the control diet in jejunal mucosa of weaned piglets.

The GIT of animals harbors a diverse microbial ecosystem comprising various bacteria and other microorganisms. The balance of microbial diversity in the intestine maintains growth, immunity, and disease resistance in organisms. A healthy and stable gut microbiome prevents the development of intestinal disorders and metabolic problems and improves growth and immunity in animals. In our previous studies, we reported that dietary curcumin nanospheres could reduce the fecal coliform bacteria (Escherichia coli) in weaned and finishing pigs (Moniruzzaman et al., 2021, 2023). In a study, Pu et al. (2020) found that combined dietary supplementation with benzoic acid, Bacillus coagulans, and oregano oil can improve and balance the gut microbial community. The dietary additive had a beneficial impact on gut health by promoting beneficial bacteria and suppressing pathogenic bacteria. This balance of bacterial populations in the cecal digesta of weaned piglets suggests that SL@Cur may alter gut microbial composition, favoring genera associated with beneficial metabolic activities. In the present study, dietary supplementation with SL@Cur improved microbial balance, as evidenced by increased diversity of beneficial bacteria. According to the Lefse biomarker analysis, SL@Cur responded dose responsively, however, not strictly linearly. There is direct evidence supporting epithelial and immunomodulatory benefits of 30 mg/kg doses in porcine systems, including enrichment of lactobacilli-associated taxa, particularly Lactobacillus gasseri and Ligilactobacillus salivarius. Among these taxa, Lactobacillus gasseri is recognized as one of the predominant intestinal lactobacilli in weaned piglets, as it stimulates the OXPHOS pathway in epithelial cells and enhances energy production. (Hu et al., 2018). Furthermore, supplementation with Ligilactobacillus salivarius has demonstrated efficacy in enhancing GIT health, modulating the microbiome, improving intestinal morphology, and increasing feed intake. Consequently, it is highly advantageous for growth and development, especially during the vital early life stage of piglets (Kinara et al., 2025). Similarly, Chen et al. (2025) reported that garlic essential oil significantly increased the abundance of Lactobacillus and markedly reduced that of E. coli. However, Lee et al. (2020) did not find significant differences in fecal Lactobacillus and E. coli contents after 16 weeks of dietary supplementation with silicate in weaning-to-finishing pigs, suggesting that microbial responses may vary depending on additive type, dose, and growth stage. In the present study, the lower dose promoted a profile associated with probiotics and lactic acid bacteria, whereas the high dose revealed a broader range of bacterial taxa and a mixed microbial shift. Besides Lactobacillus-related changes, the LEfSe analysis revealed enrichment of the family Prevotellaceae and the species Butyricicoccus porcorum, which may indicate increased representation of carbohydrate and butyrate associated taxa, since Prevotella lineage bacteria are commonly linked with complex carbohydrate utilization in pigs, and Butyricicoccus porcorum is a confirmed swine butyrate producer (Gaukroger et al., 2020; Trachsel et al., 2018). Nonetheless, simultaneous enrichment of the genus Streptococcus warrants caution, as these taxa must be supported by stronger evidence at the species or functional level (Madar Johansson et al., 2020). Interpretation of the depleted taxa should also remain cautious. Several reduced biomarkers, including s_Lentihominibacter faecis, s_Muribaculum gordoncarteri, s_Duncaniella dubosii, and o_Eubacteriales, are either poorly characterized at the species level or identified only at broad taxonomic ranks, making direct functional assignment difficult. Therefore, the observed reductions should be described as shifts in the structure of anaerobic commensals rather than a definitive loss of fiber-fermenting bacteria. Taken together, these findings support the conclusion that SL@Cur induced dose-dependent but taxon specific microbiome remodeling, with 30 mg/kg favoring lactobacilli-associated signatures and 60 mg/kg producing a broader but more heterogeneous ecological shift. This interpretation aligns with prior evidence indicating that dietary nano-ZnO supplementation increased lactic acid bacteria and total anaerobic bacteria populations, while linearly reducing intestinal E. coli levels in weaned piglets (Sun et al., 2019), indicating that feed additives have the capacity to modulate microbial communities in a manner dependent on specific taxa. Nevertheless, one limitation of the present study is the relatively small sample size used for serum biochemical and microbiome analyses (n = 3 per treatment). Although the samples were derived from independent pens, the limited number of biological replicates may reduce the statistical power to detect subtle treatment effects. Future studies with larger sample sizes will be necessary to confirm these findings.

In the present study, we found that the air quality in terms of NH3 and CO2 were not affected inside the pig farm. However, fecal noxious gases such as NH3 and H2S were significantly reduced after dietary supplementation SL@Cur compared to the control group. Likewise, in our previous studies we found that dietary supplementation of curcumin nanosphere can reduce the fecal NH3 and H2S gases in weaned piglets and finishing pigs (Moniruzzaman et al., 2021, 2023). Moreover, Lee et al. (2020) reported that dietary supplementation of silicate could reduce the fecal NH3 gas emissions; however, the researchers did not find any significant effect of silicate on reducing H2S gas in the feces of weaning to finishing pigs. Although reductions in NH₃ and H₂S emissions were observed in this study, other greenhouse gases such as methane (CH₄) were not evaluated. Therefore, the environmental implications of SL@Cur supplementation should be interpreted cautiously, and future studies measuring a broader range of gaseous emissions will be required to fully assess its environmental impact.

Conclusion

In summary, it can be corroborated that dietary supplementation of SL@Cur between 30 mg/kg and 60 mg/kg could be an effective functional feed additive in the diets of weaned piglets in terms of improving growth, blood biochemistry, antioxidant capacity, oxidative stress, gut health and microbiome as well as reducing fecal noxious gas emissions. This study has implication in swine production and health management in order to reduce indiscriminate use of antibiotics in pig feeds. In addition, dietary administration of SL@Cur in the diets of pigs would be helpful in reducing greenhouse gas emissions from the pig farms. However, further research is warranted on utilization of SL@Cur in growing to finishing pigs.
