Superelliptic Affine Lie algebras and orthogonal polynomials II
Felipe Albino dos Santos, Mikhail Neklyudov, Vyacheslav Futorny

Abstract
Let 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} be a finite-dimensional complex simple Lie algebra and r , m ≥ 2 r,m\geq 2 . The universal central extension of the superelliptic current algebra 𝔤 ⊗ A \mathfrak{g}\otimes A is 𝔤 ⊗ A ^ ≅ 𝔤 ⊗ A ⊕ ( Ω A 1 / d ​ A ) \widehat{\mathfrak{g}\otimes A}\cong\mathfrak{g}\otimes A\oplus(\Omega^{1}_{A}/dA) , where A = ℂ ⁡ [ t , t − 1 , u ] / ⟨ u m − ( 1 − 2 ​ c ​ t r + t 2 ​ r ) ⟩ A=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1},u]/\langle u^{m}-(1-2ct^{r}+t^{2r})\rangle . We compute the recursion relations governing a natural cocycle basis in Ω A 1 / d ​ A \Omega^{1}_{A}/dA and encode them by generating functions admitting closed integral expressions of superelliptic type. The 2 ​ r 2r possible choices of initial conditions are classified into four structural types; two canonical choices (types 1 and 2) produce two distinguished polynomial families. We prove that these polynomials satisfy fourth-order linear ordinary differential equations in c c , valid for all integers r , m ≥ 2 r,m\geq 2 . For the type 2 family the proof combines the Picard-Fuchs theory of the superelliptic curve u m = 1 − 2 ​ c ​ t r + t 2 ​ r u^{m}=1-2ct^{r}+t^{2r} with an algebraic identification of the explicit coefficient formulas via a rational-function identity argument. After a parity restriction and a reindexing, the resulting sequences are identified with associated ultraspherical polynomials. We show that, for each admissible n n and m ≥ 4 m\geq 4 , the corresponding fourth-order equations admit a unique polynomial solution up to scalar multiples.

Recurrence and generating functions.

From the defining relation

family of cocycle classes in
\Omega^{1}_{A}/dA
. This leads to a three-term recurrence

\{P_{k}(c)\}

We introduce generating functions

first-order differential equation in

Classification of initial conditions.

The recursion (

independent initial conditions, indexed by

j_{0}\in\{-2r,-2r+1,\ldots,-1\}
. These split into four structural types

that are linear combinations of the two canonical families; type C initial conditions
(

integrals. Two canonical initial conditions (type 1 and type 2) produce two distinguished
polynomial families,

Fourth-order equations in

Our main structural result is that the type 1 family satisfies an explicit fourth-order
linear ordinary differential equation (ODE) in the parameter
c

2.1
(i).
For the type 2 family, the same result holds for all
r\geq 2

the coefficient formulas involve a correction term

that vanishes for

and encodes the additional structure present for

The proof combines the Picard-Fuchs existence theorem for the period integral of
the superelliptic family
u^{m}=1-2cw^{r}+w^{2r}
with an algebraic identification
of the coefficients via a rational-function identity argument for each fixed
(r,m)
.
These results generalize the previously known

superelliptic situation and extend the
DJKM mechanism beyond the hyperelliptic case.

Identification and orthogonality.

After restricting to the relevant parity and performing a reindexing, both

In this paper we consider the superelliptic affine Lie algebras with the polynomial relation

1.3
Classification of all initial conditions

The recursion (

\{P_{j_{0}}:j_{0}\in\{-2r,\ldots,-1\}\}

We classify these basis solutions into four types.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let

,

, and let
j_{0}\in\{-2r,\ldots,-1\}
. The generating function

belongs to one of the following four structural types:

(A)

(Superelliptic type 1,

).
The generating function is expressed
as a regularized superelliptic integral of type
1
; this produces the family

(A’)

(Superelliptic type 2,

).
The numerator of the

, producing the superelliptic integral of
type

.

(B)

(Linear combination,
j_{0}\in\{-2r+1,\ldots,-r-1\}

For such

, the

which is a linear combination of the type

and type

for explicit constants

depending only on

These families satisfy the same ODEs as types
1
and
2
and require no separate analysis.
The special case
j_{0}=-r-1
(case

series (see Appendix
B

(C)

(Genuinely new,
j_{0}\in\{-r+1,\ldots,-1\}
).
The

coupling
introduces a term

with

(i)

j_{0}=-1
(case

(ii)

Proof.

Types (A) and (A’) follow directly from the computation of the numerator
N_{j_{0}}(z)

for
j_{0}=-2r
and
j_{0}=-r
respectively; in both cases the numerator is a monomial in
z

giving the two superelliptic integrals of Sections

and
1.2
.
For type (B), note that
j_{0}<-r

two superelliptic integrands. Equality (

For type (C), the presence of

follow by direct computation of the numerator.
∎

.

Case

Lemma 3.1
.

For each
n
, both operators
\mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{n}
and
\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{n}
preserve parity:
if

is even (resp. odd), then
\mathcal{L}^{(i)}_{n}p
is even (resp. odd).
Consequently, if
p(c)
is a polynomial solution, then its even and odd parts are polynomial
solutions as well.

Proof.

Inspecting (
2.1
) and (

), the coefficients of

and

are even polynomials in
c
, the coefficients of
P^{\prime\prime\prime}
and

P^{\prime}
are odd polynomials in
c
, and the remaining coefficient is constant in
c

Since differentiation changes parity and multiplication by an odd function changes it again,
each term in
\mathcal{L}^{(i)}_{n}p
has the same parity as

.
∎

We now record the leading action of
\mathcal{L}^{(i)}_{n}
on monomials at
\infty
.
For
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, write

\mathcal{L}^{(i)}_{n}(c^{s})=I^{(i)}_{n}(s)\,c^{s}+\text{(terms of degree $<s$)}.

The scalar
I^{(i)}_{n}(s)
is the indicial coefficient at infinity.

3.2
Type 1: degree restriction and uniqueness

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume
r\geq 2
and fix
n\in r\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
For
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
we have

\mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{n}(c^{s})=I^{(1)}_{n}(s)\,c^{s}+\text{(lower degree terms)},

where

I^{(1)}_{n}(s)=(sr+n)(sr-n+2r)(smr-mn+4mr-2m-2r)(smr+mn-2mr+2m+2r).

(3.1)

In particular, if
p(c)
is a polynomial solution of (
2.1
) of degree
d
,
then necessarily
I^{(1)}_{n}(d)=0
.

Proof.

Each coefficient in (
2.1
) has degree at most
2
in
c
, so

\mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{n}(c^{s})
is a polynomial of degree
\leq s
. Extracting the coefficient of

c^{s}
yields
I^{(1)}_{n}(s)
; the factorization (
3.1
) is obtained by collecting the
leading power
c^{s}
in (
2.1
) after substituting
P_{n}=c^{s}
.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Assume
r\geq 2
and fix
n\in r\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. If (
2.1
) admits a nonzero
polynomial solution, then its degree must be a nonnegative integer root of
I^{(1)}_{n}(s)
.
In particular, if
n\in r\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
n\geq 2r
, then
s=n/r-2
is always a root,
and it is the
only
root in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
provided

\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{m}\neq\frac{1}{2}.

(3.2)

Proof.

For
s=n/r-2
we have
sr-n+2r=0
, so
I^{(1)}_{n}(s)=0
.
The remaining roots from (

) yield candidate degrees

,
s=n/(mr)-4+2/(mr)+2/m
, and
s=-n/(mr)+2-2/(mr)-2/m
.
The first is negative for
n>0
. The third is negative as well for
n\geq 2r
.
The middle one is an integer only in the resonant case

.
Thus under (
3.2
) the only nonnegative integer root is

.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The nonresonance condition (
3.2
) excludes only the pairs

(r,m)\in\{(4,4),(3,6),(6,3)\}
among integers with

. In those three cases the
indicial polynomial (

) has an extra nonnegative integer root

, which has
opposite parity to

. The argument of Proposition

still shows
that the solution space has dimension

within each fixed parity. We do not pursue
the existence of additional polynomial solutions in the resonant cases here.

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume

,

, and the nonresonance condition (

).

Proof.

Let

. By Corollary

, any nonzero polynomial solution has

. By Lemma

we may fix a parity

and consider

∎

4
Associated ultraspherical polynomials

After shifting the indices back by
2r
, both families of polynomials

\{P_{-2r,k-2r}(c)\}
and
\{P_{-r,k-2r}(c)\}
satisfy the same
three-term recurrence at the level of the shifted index
k
.
More precisely, for

P_{k}(c)\in\bigl\{\,P_{-2r,k-2r}(c)\,;\;P_{-r,k-2r}(c)\,\bigr\},

we have, for all
k\geq 0
,

(km+m+2r)\,P_{k}(c)=2c\bigl(km+(1-r)m+r\bigr)\,P_{k-r}(c)-\bigl(k-(2r-1)\bigr)m\,P_{k-2r}(c).

(4.1)

In each family, only one parity occurs. Let
\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}
be such that

P_{k}=0
whenever
k\not\equiv\varepsilon\pmod{2}
, and define

q_{n}(c):=P_{2n+\varepsilon}(c)\qquad(n\geq 0).

Setting
k=2n+\varepsilon
in (
4.1
) yields, after a shift
n\to n-1
,

2c\bigl(2mn+m(\varepsilon+r+1)+r\bigr)\,\widetilde{q}_{n}=\bigl(2mn+m(\varepsilon+2r+1)+2r\bigr)\,\widetilde{q}_{n+1}+m(2n+\varepsilon+1)\,\widetilde{q}_{n-1},

(4.2)

where
\widetilde{q}_{n}
is a suitable reindexing of
q_{n}
. Dividing by

2(n+\nu+c_{0})
and writing

\nu:=\frac{r}{2}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{m}\Bigr),\qquad c_{0}:=\frac{\varepsilon+1}{2},

we obtain

2c\,(n+\nu+c_{0})\,\widetilde{q}_{n}=(n+c_{0})\,\widetilde{q}_{n-1}+(n+2\nu+c_{0})\,\widetilde{q}_{n+1}.

(4.3)

Equation (
4.3
) is precisely the defining three-term
recurrence for the associated ultraspherical polynomials
C_{n}^{(\nu)}(x;c_{0})

(with
c_{0}\in\{1/2,1\}
), up to normalization
[
5
]
.
In particular, the superelliptic families obtained from cases 1 and 2
are identified with associated ultraspherical polynomials with parameters

\nu=\frac{r}{2}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{m}\Bigr),\qquad c_{0}\in\Bigl\{\frac{1}{2},\,1\Bigr\}.

5
Orthogonality

Let
r\geq 2
,
m\geq 2
, and let
c_{0}=(\varepsilon+1)/2
be the shift parameter
arising in Section
4
. After parity restriction and reindexing,
the resulting sequence
\{q_{n}(c)\}
satisfies

2c\,(n+\nu+c_{0})\,q_{n}(c)=(n+c_{0})\,q_{n-1}(c)+(n+2\nu+c_{0})\,q_{n+1}(c),\qquad n\geq 0,

(5.1)

with
\nu=\tfrac{r}{2}(1+\tfrac{1}{m})>0
and
c_{0}>0
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume
r\geq 2
and
m\geq 2
. Then there exists a positive Borel measure
\mu
on
[-1,1]

such that
\{q_{n}(c)\}
is an orthogonal polynomial system in
L^{2}([-1,1],d\mu)
.
Consequently, the superelliptic families in cases 1 and 2 yield orthogonal polynomial
systems after parity restriction and the reindexing of Section
4
.

Proof.

Rewrite (
5.1
) in the form

c\,q_{n}(c)=A_{n}\,q_{n+1}(c)+B_{n}\,q_{n-1}(c),\qquad A_{n}=\frac{n+2\nu+c_{0}}{2(n+\nu+c_{0})},\quad B_{n}=\frac{n+c_{0}}{2(n+\nu+c_{0})}.

(5.2)

Since
\nu>0
and
c_{0}>0
, we have
A_{n}>0
for all
n\geq 0
and
B_{n}>0
for all
n\geq 1
.
Define a renormalized sequence
\{p_{n}\}
by
p_{n}=\gamma_{n}^{-1}q_{n}
, where
\gamma_{0}=1
and

\gamma_{n+1}=A_{n}\gamma_{n}
. Multiplying (
5.2
) by
\gamma_{n}^{-1}
yields the
Favard form

c\,p_{n}(c)=p_{n+1}(c)+a_{n}\,p_{n-1}(c),\qquad n\geq 0,

(5.3)

with

a_{n}=B_{n}A_{n-1}=\frac{(n+c_{0})(n-1+2\nu+c_{0})}{4(n+\nu+c_{0})(n-1+\nu+c_{0})}>0\quad(n\geq 1).

By Favard’s theorem, (
5.3
) implies the existence of a positive Borel measure

\mu
for which
\{p_{n}\}
is orthogonal. Since
p_{n}
differs from
q_{n}
only by a nonzero
scalar,
\{q_{n}\}
is orthogonal with respect to the same measure.
∎

Remark 5.2

(Why Favard’s theorem)
.

Although in Section
4
we identify our families with associated
ultraspherical polynomials, we derive orthogonality via Favard’s theorem for two reasons.
First, Favard yields the existence of a positive orthogonality measure
directly

from the three-term recurrence once it is put in Favard form with positive coefficients,
without requiring an explicit weight or additional analytic input. Second, this approach
is robust with respect to normalization and parameter ranges, and it applies to our
sequences as soon as the recurrence is established, independently of the subsequent
identification with a classical family. An explicit measure can be recovered from the
associated ultraspherical theory; see
[
5
]
.
