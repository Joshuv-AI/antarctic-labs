On the shape of the positivity region for a free boundary problem describing cell polarization
Sebastián Flores Sepúlveda, Barbara Niethammer, Juan J. L. Velázquez

Abstract




Abstract

In this paper we study a mass-constrained free boundary problem modeling cell polarization, in the regime where the mass is small. In the generic case of a signal with nondegenerate maxima, we prove that the solution converges locally to a global, integrable solution to an obstacle problem in the plane. We further show that the interface of the solution to the limit problem is an ellipse, the equation of which is explicit. We also study some cases where the signal has degenerate maxima, highlighting a variety of possible behaviors.

1
Introduction

Cell polarization is a crucial step in a number of biological processes, including cell division, differentiation and motility. There exists a vast biological literature studying this phenomenon, and a number of mathematical models have been proposed for its description.

In the model proposed in
[
15
]
, cell polarization is described by a bulk-surface reaction-diffusion system. In short, inactive signaling proteins diffuse in the cytosol, represented as a bounded domain
\Omega
in three-dimensional space, and can attach to the membrane, i.e. the boundary of
\Omega
, where they can become active by reacting with molecules in the exterior of the cell, represented by a “forcing” term in the reaction-diffusion system.

In a suitable scaling limit, this system may be reduced to an obstacle-type problem for the density of active signaling proteins on the membrane, with a mass constraint representing the total number of signaling proteins. The rigorous justification of this reduction as well as existence and uniqueness of steady states for the obstacle-type problem may be found in
[
15
]
and further analysis of the time-dependent problem may be found in
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
.

In this work, we study the model from
[
15
]
in the regime where the number of signaling proteins is small. We look for a detailed description of the support and the free boundary of the solutions to the obstacle-type problem posed by the model.

The problem is as follows. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a smooth bounded domain,
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
, and
g:\Gamma\rightarrow(0,1)
be a continuous function. Then the density of active signaling proteins
u
solves

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{\Gamma}u=-(1-g)\xi+\alpha g&\mathrm{on}\penalty\ \Gamma,\\
\xi\in[0,1],\penalty\ \penalty\ u\xi=1&\mathrm{on}\penalty\ \{u>0\},\\
u\geq 0,\penalty\ \displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\Gamma}u\mathrm{d}S=M,\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\Delta_{\Gamma}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator, and
g
represents a normalized chemical signal. The existence of a unique solution to (
1.1
), given
M
, is given by Theorem 3.7 in
[
15
]
. We will denote by
(u_{M},\xi_{M},\alpha_{M})
the triplet which satisfies (
1.1
). It is possible to think of the time-dependent version of problem (
1.1
) as a mass conserving parabolic obstacle problem, see
[
13
]
.

Furthermore, (
1.1
) may be written in a more compact form by using the representation formula for
\xi_{M}
:

\xi_{M}=\begin{cases}1&\text{in }\{u_{M}>0\},\\
\dfrac{\alpha_{M}g}{1-g}&\text{in }\{u_{M}=0\}.\end{cases}

therefore,
u_{M}
satisfies

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{\Gamma}u_{M}\geq((1+\alpha_{M})g-1)&\mathrm{on}\penalty\ \Gamma,\\
u_{M}\geq 0&\text{on }\Gamma,\\
-\Delta_{\Gamma}u_{M}=((1+\alpha_{M})g-1)&\mathrm{on}\penalty\ \{u_{M}>0\},\\
\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\Gamma}u_{M}\mathrm{d}S=M.\end{cases}

There is a further reformulation of the problem which highlights its nonlocality. Exploiting an explicit formula for
\alpha_{M}
(see
[
12
]
), the problem may be rewritten as

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{\Gamma}u_{M}=\left(1-\dfrac{g}{\fint_{\{u_{M}>0\}}g\mathrm{d}S}\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{u_{M}>0\}}&\mathrm{on}\penalty\ \Gamma,\\
u_{M}\geq 0&\text{on }\Gamma,\\
\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\Gamma}u_{M}\mathrm{d}S=M,\end{cases}

where
\fint_{A}g\mathrm{d}S
denotes the average over the set
A\subset\Gamma
.

We shall see that the behavior of
(u_{M},\alpha_{M})
for
M
small depends on the structure of
g
near its maximum points. In the generic case where
g
is a Morse function, we may informally state our main result as follows. As in
[
15
]
, we denote
g_{\mathrm{max}}:=\max_{\Gamma}g
and

\alpha_{0}:=\frac{1-g_{\mathrm{max}}}{g_{\mathrm{max}}}.

(1.2)

Theorem 1
.

Let
g:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be of class
C^{2}
with a finite set of nondegenerate maximum points
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, i.e.
g(p_{i})=g_{\mathrm{max}}
and
D^{2}g(p_{i})
is a negative definite bilinear form for every
i=1,\ldots,N
. Then, there exist positive numbers
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with
\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}=1
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}u_{M}\rightarrow\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\delta_{p_{i}}\hskip 8.0pt\text{ as }M\rightarrow 0,

where
\delta_{p_{i}}
is a Dirac delta centered at
p_{i}
and each
\lambda_{i}
depends only on
D^{2}g(p_{i})
. Moreover, under a suitable scaling and near a maximum point
p
, the free boundary
\partial\{u_{M}>0\}
converges to an ellipse, and is a smooth curve locally near
p
, for
M
sufficiently small.

The formula for
\lambda_{i}
is given in Theorem
4.1
.

The scaling mentioned in Theorem
1
is the consequence of a local analysis around a maximum point. In fact, we perform this analysis under the slightly more general case when
g_{\mathrm{max}}-g
is a positive function, homogeneous of degree
\gamma>0
. We obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Let
p\in\Gamma
be a maximum of
g
. Assume that there exist a neighborhood
\Theta
of
p
and functions
f,r:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that the following holds in any coordinate system in
\Theta
mapping
p
to 0:

1.

f
is of class
C^{1}
,
f(0)=0
,
f(x)>0
for any
x\neq 0
and
f
is positively
\gamma
-homogeneous for some
\gamma>0
, i.e.
f(\lambda x)=\lambda^{\gamma}f(x)
for any
\lambda\geq 0
,

2.

\frac{|r(x)|}{|x|^{\gamma}}\rightarrow 0
as
x\rightarrow 0
, where
\gamma
is the homogeneity degree of
f
,

3.

for any
x\in\Theta
,

g(x)=g_{\mathrm{max}}-f(x)+r(x).

(1.3)

Then, defining
m:=\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\Theta}u_{M}\mathrm{d}S
and

U_{m}(y)=m^{-(\gamma+2)/(\gamma+4)}u_{M}(m^{1/(\gamma+4)}y)\hskip 8.0pt\forall y\in m^{-1/(\gamma+4)}\Theta,

it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{M}-\alpha_{0}}{m^{\gamma/(\gamma+4)}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\overline{\alpha},

\displaystyle U_{m}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\overline{U}\text{ in }C^{1,\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\hskip 8.0pt\forall\delta\in(0,1),

\displaystyle\partial\{U_{m}>0\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\partial\{\overline{U}>0\}\hskip 8.0pt\text{in the Hausdorff distance.}

Moreover,
\overline{U}
is the unique solution to

\begin{cases}-\Delta\overline{U}=\left(g_{\mathrm{max}}\overline{\alpha}-\dfrac{1}{g_{\mathrm{max}}}f(y)\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{\overline{U}>0\}}&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2},\\
\overline{U}\geq 0\hskip 8.0pt\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2},\hskip 8.0pt\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\overline{U}\mathrm{d}x=1,\end{cases}

(1.4)

and has compact support.

Theorem
2
implies that the mass of
U_{M}
concentrates around the points of maximum homogeneity degree
\gamma
. Indeed, let
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
be two maximum points with homogeneity degree
\gamma_{i}
,
i=1,2
, with
\gamma_{2}>\gamma_{1}
. Then, we have

\frac{\alpha_{M}-\alpha_{0}}{m_{i}^{\gamma_{i}/(\gamma_{i}+4)}}\rightarrow\overline{\alpha}_{i}\hskip 8.0pt\text{for }i=1,2.

This implies that

\frac{m_{1}^{\gamma_{1}/(\gamma_{1}+4)}}{m_{2}^{\gamma_{2}/(\gamma_{2}+4)}}\rightarrow\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{2}}{\overline{\alpha}_{1}},

in particular, there is some
C>0
such that

C^{-1}m_{2}^{\frac{\gamma_{2}(\gamma_{1}+4)}{\gamma_{1}(\gamma_{2}+4)}}\leq m_{1}\leq Cm_{2}^{\frac{\gamma_{2}(\gamma_{1}+4)}{\gamma_{1}(\gamma_{2}+4)}}.

Thus, since
\gamma_{2}>\gamma_{1}
,
m_{1}=o(m_{2})
as
M\rightarrow 0
.

In the generic case of a Morse function, in the language of Theorem
2
, we have
f(y)=y^{\top}Ay
which is homogeneous of degree 2, hence

u_{M}(x)\sim m^{2/3}U_{m}(m^{-1/6}x)

near maximum points. Theorem
2
also implies that the masses near maximum points are all comparable, which in turn yields the existence of the
\lambda_{i}
in Theorem
1
.

Solving problem (
1.4
) explicitly for quadratic
f
, we get the limit ellipses mentioned in Theorem
1
. Ellipsoidal and paraboloidal shapes are the only possible ones in several free boundary problems related to the obstacle and Stefan problem, as was first noticed in the physical literature
[
10
,
9
]
. Classification of all global solutions in the two-dimensional obstacle problem was obtained in
[
17
]
, and the study of higher dimensional case, in the context of global solutions for the Stefan problem, was initiated in
[
3
]
. In both of these works, ellipsoidal shapes play a key role in the analysis. In the recent works
[
5
,
4
,
18
]
, a complete classification of global solutions to the elliptic obstacle problem in all dimensions is achieved. We may read our results as a classification result for bounded solutions to

-\Delta u=\left(1-x^{\top}Ax\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{u>0\}}.

Using the explicit formulas for the solution to this problem we also prove that the right-hand side does not vanish near the free boundary, allowing us to apply the regularity results for the free boundary of Caffarelli
[
2
]
for small but positive mass
M
.

As stated, Theorems
1
and
2
completely describe the generic case of Morse functions. Describing the behavior of
u_{M}
near degenerate maxima, i.e. maxima where
D^{2}g(p)
is a singular bilinear form, is a delicate issue. We pick some examples in order to illustrate the possible situations. In each case, the structure of
g
is given by a higher order Taylor expansion of
g
which, since we assume the maxima are isolated, must eventually contain a polynomial which is positive outside
0
.

First, we study an example where
f
is such that (
1.3
) holds, but the homogeneity of
f
in each variable is different. This yields a degenerate elliptic operator in the limit, so that regularity estimates cease to imply compactness of the blow-up sequences. Nevertheless, we prove the following.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
p\in\Gamma
is an isolated maximum of
g
and there exists a neighborhood
\Theta
of
p
such that, in local coordinates,

g(x)=g_{\mathrm{max}}-ax_{1}^{4}-bx_{2}^{2}+r(x),

with
a
,
b>0
and

. Then, there exists a coordinate chart
(x,\Theta)
around
p
such that

, a number
\overline{\alpha}>0
and a function
\overline{v}\in C_{c}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that the following holds. Define

U_{m}(y)=m^{-8/11}u_{M}(x^{-1}(m^{1/11}y_{1},m^{2/11}y_{2})),\hskip 8.0pt\forall y\in m^{-8/11}x(\Theta),

then

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{M}-\alpha_{0}}{m^{4/11}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\overline{\alpha},

\displaystyle U_{m}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\overline{U}\text{ strongly in }L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\hskip 8.0pt\forall p\in[1,\infty],

\displaystyle\partial_{2}U_{m}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\partial_{2}\overline{U}\text{ weakly in }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

\displaystyle\partial\{U_{m}>0\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\partial\{\overline{U}>0\}\hskip 8.0pt\text{in the Hausdorff distance.}

Moreover,
\overline{U}
is the unique solution to

\begin{cases}-\partial_{22}\overline{U}=\left(g_{\mathrm{max}}\overline{\alpha}-\dfrac{1}{g_{\mathrm{max}}}(ax_{1}^{4}+bx_{2}^{2})\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{\overline{U}>0\}}&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2},\\
\overline{U}\geq 0\hskip 8.0pt\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2},\hskip 8.0pt\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\overline{U}\mathrm{d}x=1,&\end{cases}

and has compact support.

A second example in which the hypotheses of Theorem
2
fail is given by

g(x)=g_{\mathrm{max}}-ax_{1}^{4}-bx_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}-cx_{2}^{6}+r(x)

(1.6)

around a maximum, with
a
,
b
,

. In this particular case,

may be chosen as positively 4-homogeneous, but its zero set is nontrivial. Furthermore, one could consider two different scales,
(x_{1},x_{2})\sim(\ell y_{1},\ell y_{2})
and

, which would lead, a priori, to two different possible blow-ups. We will prove that this is not the case, indeed, the following holds.

Theorem 4
.

Assume
p\in\Gamma
is a maximum of
g
such that (
1.6
) holds in a neighborhood of
p
. Define the rescaling

u_{M}(x)=m^{3/4}U_{m}(m^{-1/8}x),

Then, there exist
\overline{\alpha}>0
and a nontrivial function
\overline{U}\in W^{2,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for all

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{M}-\alpha_{0}}{m^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\overline{\alpha},

\displaystyle U_{m}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\overline{U}\text{ in }C^{1,\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\hskip 8.0pt\forall\delta\in(0,1),

Preliminaries

In what follows,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a bounded domain, whose boundary
\Gamma
is
C^{5}
regular. Recall that the Laplace Beltrami operator may be written in local coordinates as

-\Delta_{\Gamma}u=-\mathrm{div}(H(x)\nabla u(x)),

(2.1)

Let us recall equivalent formulations of the obstacle problem. All of these formulations are classical, see
[

]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a smooth bounded domain in

,

. Then, for

2.

minimizes

3.

is continuous and satisfies

\begin{cases}-\Delta u\geq f&\text{ in }\\
u\geq 0&\text{ in }\\
-\Delta u=f&\text{ in }\{u>0\}\end{cases}

in the weak sense.

The main properties of solutions to (

(Proposition 3.9 in

Preliminary computations

\displaystyle(1+\alpha_{n})g-1

\displaystyle=\beta_{n}g_{\mathrm{max}}-\left(\frac{1}{g_{\mathrm{max}}}+\beta_{n}\right)f(g_{\mathrm{max}}^{2/\gamma}\varepsilon_{n}y)+\left(\frac{1}{g_{\mathrm{max}}}+\beta_{n}\right)r(g_{\mathrm{max}}^{2/\gamma}\varepsilon_{n}y)

\displaystyle=g_{\mathrm{max}}\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}\left(\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}-\left(1+\frac{\beta_{n}}{g_{\mathrm{max}}}\right)f(y)+\left(\frac{1}{g_{\mathrm{max}}}+\beta_{n}\right)\frac{r(\varepsilon_{n}y)}{g_{\mathrm{max}}\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\right).

Note that, for
|y|\leq T
, we have

\left|\frac{\beta_{n}}{g_{\mathrm{max}}}f(y)+\left(\frac{1}{g_{\mathrm{max}}}+\beta_{n}\right)\frac{r(\varepsilon_{n}y)}{g_{\mathrm{max}}\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\right|\leq\frac{\beta_{n}}{g_{\mathrm{max}}}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{T})}+\frac{2T^{\gamma}}{g_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}}\frac{|r(\varepsilon_{n}y)|}{|\varepsilon_{n}y|^{\gamma}}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,

so that we may define

r_{n}(y)=\frac{\beta_{n}}{g_{\mathrm{max}}}f(y)+\left(\frac{1}{g_{\mathrm{max}}}+\beta_{n}\right)\frac{r(\varepsilon_{n}y)}{g_{\mathrm{max}}\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}

and we have
r_{n}\rightarrow 0
uniformly in compact sets. We will further set
g_{\mathrm{max}}=1
since the precise value of this parameter plays no role at this stage in our analysis.

Hence, the equation satisfied by
U_{n}
may be written as

-\mathrm{div}(H(g_{\mathrm{max}}^{2/\gamma}\varepsilon_{n}^{1/6}y)\nabla U_{n}(y))=\left(\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}-f(y)+r_{n}(y)\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{U_{n}>0\}}.

(3.3)

We will denote
H_{n}(y)=H(g_{\mathrm{max}}^{2/\gamma}\varepsilon_{n}y)
. By (
2.1
), for any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and any function
\varphi\in C^{2}(K)
, we have

-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\nabla\varphi)=-\Delta\varphi+o(1).

(3.4)

Finally, if
\lambda>0
is a lower bound for the ellipticity constant of
H
in
B_{R}
, then
\lambda
is also a lower bound for the ellipticity constant of
H_{n}
in
B_{R_{n}}
.

3.2
The limit problem

In view of (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), if
\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\rightarrow\overline{\alpha}
and
(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in a strong sense, its limit should satisfy

\begin{cases}-\Delta v=\left(\overline{\alpha}-f(y)\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{v>0\}},\\
v\geq 0,v\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).\end{cases}

(3.5)

We will first show that this problem has a unique solution.

Proposition 3.1
.

Given
\overline{\alpha}>0
, there exist a unique weak solution
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
to (
3.5
).

Proof.

First, let us prove uniqueness. Let
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
be two solutions to (
4.5
). The weak formulation of (
4.5
) reads

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla v_{i}\cdot\nabla(\varphi-v_{i})+(f-\overline{\alpha})(\varphi-v_{i})\mathrm{d}x\geq 0\hskip 8.0pt\forall\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\penalty\ \varphi\geq 0.

Testing
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
with each other yields

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla(v_{1}-v_{2})|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\left(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla v_{1}\cdot\nabla(v_{2}-v_{1})+\nabla v_{2}\cdot\nabla(v_{1}-v_{2})\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\left(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(f-\overline{\alpha})(v_{2}-v_{1})+(f-\overline{\alpha})(v_{1}-v_{2})\right)=0,

thus,
\nabla(v_{1}-v_{2})\equiv 0
, which means that
v_{1}-v_{2}
is constant. This implies that
v_{1}\equiv v_{2}
since both have compact support.

For the existence, we will show that solving (
3.5
) is equivalent to solving a minimization problem of the form

\min\left\{J(v):=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\nabla v|^{2}}{2}+fv\mathrm{d}y:v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\penalty\ v\geq 0,\penalty\ \intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}v=1\right\}.

(3.6)

Let us show that (
3.6
) has a minimizer. Indeed, fix any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{1})
,
\varphi\geq 0
,
\varphi\equiv 1
in
B_{1/2}
, and we have that

w=\frac{1}{\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y}\varphi

is an admissible test function.

Take a minimizing sequence
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Therefore, we have that
J(v_{n})\geq C
. In particular,
\|\nabla v_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C
. Moreover, by Nash’s inequality, we have

\|v_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{4}\leq C\|\nabla v_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\|v_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C.

Thus,
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a bounded sequence in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. By the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, there exists some
\overline{v}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and a subsequence of
(v_{n})
such that
v_{n}\rightharpoonup\overline{v}
weakly in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. In each smooth compact set we can apply the Rellich-Kondrachov theorem to extract a further subsequence such that
v_{n}\rightarrow\overline{v}
in
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We want to show that
\overline{v}
is admissible. Clearly,
\overline{v}\geq 0
, so it remains to prove that
\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\overline{v}\mathrm{d}y=1
. To verify this, note that
\nu_{n}=v_{n}\mathrm{d}x
are probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Moreover, the family
(\nu_{n})_{\{n\in\mathbb{N}\}}
is tight For any
R>0
, we have

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}fv_{n}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{R}}fv_{n}\mathrm{d}y+\intop\nolimits_{B_{R}}fv_{n}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{R}}|y|^{\gamma}f\left(\frac{y}{|y|}\right)v_{n}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq R^{\gamma}\min_{\partial B_{1}}f\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{R}}v_{n}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=R^{\gamma}\min_{\partial B_{1}}f\nu_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{R}).

Recalling that
\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(y)v_{n}\mathrm{d}y\leq J(v_{n})\leq C
we conclude

\nu_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{R})\leq\frac{C}{R^{\gamma}\min_{\partial B_{1}}f},

which implies that
(\nu_{n})
is tight. Prokhorov’s theorem
[
1
, Theorem 8.6.1]
implies that there exists a probability measure
\nu
such that
\nu_{n}\rightharpoonup\nu
in the weak sense of measures, up to a subsequence. But by testing with smooth functions with compact support, we readily verify that
\nu=\overline{v}\mathrm{d}x
. This implies that the whole sequence
(\nu_{n})
converges to
\nu
and that
\|\overline{v}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=1
, and therefore
\overline{v}
is admissible.

The weak lower semicontinuity of
u\mapsto\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
yields

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\nabla v_{n}|^{2}}{2}\mathrm{d}y\geq\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\nabla\overline{v}|^{2}}{2}\mathrm{d}y.

On the other hand, Fatou’s lemma yields

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}fv_{n}\mathrm{d}y\geq\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f\overline{v}\mathrm{d}y.

We conclude that
\overline{v}
is a minimizer of (
3.6
).

Now, we will show that the Euler-Lagrange equation of (
3.6
) is precisely (
3.5
). Take
\varphi\in C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
a nontrivial nonnegative function, and
\delta>0
. Define

v_{\delta}:=\frac{v+\delta\varphi}{\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}v+\delta\varphi\mathrm{d}y}=\frac{1}{1+\delta\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y}(v+\delta\varphi),

which is admissible for (
3.6
). By the fact that
u
is a minimizer of (
3.6
), we have
J(u_{\delta})\geq J(u)
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle J(v_{\delta})-J(v)=

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\left(1+\delta\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y\right)^{2}}|\nabla v_{\delta}|^{2}-|\nabla v|^{2}\right)+f\left(\frac{1}{1+\delta\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y}(v+\delta\varphi)-v\right)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{1}{\left(1+\delta\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y\right)^{2}}-1\right)|\nabla v|^{2}+\frac{\delta}{\left(1+\delta\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y\right)^{2}}(2\nabla v\cdot\nabla\varphi+\delta|\nabla\varphi|^{2})\right)

\displaystyle+f\left(\left(\frac{1}{1+\delta\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y}-1\right)v+\frac{\delta}{1+\delta\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y}\varphi\right)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}y.

Recalling that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\delta\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y}

\displaystyle=1-\delta\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y+o(\delta),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left(1+\delta\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=1-2\delta\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y+o(\delta),

as
\delta\rightarrow 0
, we get

\displaystyle J(v_{\delta})-J(v)=\delta\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\varphi+f\varphi\mathrm{d}y-\delta\left(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y\right)\left(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla v|^{2}-fv\mathrm{d}y\right)+o(\delta).

Dividing by
\delta
and letting
\delta\searrow 0
, we have

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\varphi+f\varphi\mathrm{d}y\geq\left(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\mathrm{d}y\right)\left(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla v|^{2}-f\mathrm{d}y\right),

which can be rewritten, defining

\overline{\alpha}:=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla v|^{2}-fv\mathrm{d}x,

(3.7)

as

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\varphi+(f-\overline{\alpha})\varphi\mathrm{d}y\geq 0\hskip 8.0pt\forall\varphi\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\penalty\ \varphi\geq 0,

which is precisely the weak formulation of (
3.5
).

Finally, define

v_{\overline{\alpha}}(y)=\overline{\alpha}^{1+2/\gamma}\overline{v}\left(\frac{y}{\overline{\alpha}^{1/\gamma}}\right),

(3.8)

and
v_{\overline{\alpha}}
solves (
3.5
).
∎

Using classical regularity estimates for elliptic equations see
[
8
, Theorem 9.11]
, we see that the solution to (
3.5
) is in
W^{2,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for any
p\in[1,\infty)
, since the right hand side of (
3.5
) is locally bounded. In particular,
v
is continuous.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
v
be the unique weak solution to (
3.5
). Then,
v
has compact support.

Proof.

Let
\kappa_{0}>0
be the constant from Lemma
2.4
, and let
R>1
be such that

\overline{\alpha}-f(x)\leq-\|v\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\kappa_{0}\hskip 8.0pt\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{R/2},

(3.9)

which exists by homogeneity and the fact that
f(x)>0
for every nonzero
x
. In particular,
v
is subharmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{R/2}
, whence

\sup_{B_{1}(x_{0})}v\leq\intop\nolimits_{B_{2}(x_{0})}v(x)\mathrm{d}x\leq\|v\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\hskip 8.0pt\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{R+1},

by the mean value property and the fact that
v\geq 0
.

For any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{R+1}
, we can apply Lemma
2.4
to
v(x)=\frac{v(x_{0}+x)}{\|v\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}
to get that
v\equiv 0
in
B_{1/2}(x_{0})
. Thus, the support of
v
is contained in
B_{R+1}
.
∎

3.3
Convergence

In this section we prove the convergence of
U_{n}
as defined in (

), first up to a subsequence, then along the whole parameter

.

Proposition 3.3
.

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}<\infty

Proof.

We argue by contradiction. Assume that, for some subsequence,
\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, there exists
\overline{r}>0
independent of
n
such that

\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon^{\gamma}}-f(y)+r_{n}(y)\geq\frac{\beta_{n}}{2\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\hskip 8.0pt\forall y\in B_{\overline{r}}.

Define the function

\varphi(y)=\frac{\beta_{n}}{16\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\left(\overline{r}^{2}-|y|^{2}\right)

which satisfies

-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\nabla\varphi)=\frac{\beta_{n}}{4\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}+o(1).

For
n
large enough,
-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\nabla U_{n})\geq-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\nabla\varphi)
in
B_{r}
, and since
U_{n}\geq 0
and
\varphi=0
on
\partial B_{\overline{r}}
, using the comparison principle we conclude
U_{n}\geq\varphi
in
B_{\overline{r}}
. In particular,

U_{n}\geq\frac{3\overline{r}^{2}\beta_{n}}{64\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\text{ in }B_{\overline{r}/2}.

Therefore,

1=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}U_{n}\mu_{n}(y)\mathrm{d}y\geq\intop\nolimits_{B_{\overline{r}/2}}U_{n}\mu_{n}(y)\mathrm{d}y\geq\frac{3\overline{r}^{2}\beta_{n}}{64\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\mu_{n}(B_{\overline{r}/2})\rightarrow\infty,

a contradiction. We conclude that
\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}
must be bounded.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

There exist
C_{0},R_{1}>0
such that
\mathrm{supp}(U_{n})\subset B_{R_{1}}
and

\|U_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C_{0}\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}},

(3.10)

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Take
v
the solution to (
3.5
) with
\overline{\alpha}=1
, and define the function

\varphi(y)=4^{2/\gamma}\left(\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\right)^{(\gamma+2)/\gamma}\Phi\left(\left(\frac{4\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\right)^{-1/\gamma}y\right).

Then,
\varphi\geq 0
and a straightforward computation shows that
\varphi
satisfies

\displaystyle-\Delta\varphi=\left(\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}-\frac{1}{4}f(y)\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{\varphi>0\}}.

By Proposition
3.2
, there is
R_{0}>0
such that
\mathrm{supp}(v)\subset B_{R_{0}}
. By definition,
\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)\subset\left(\frac{4\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\right)^{1/\gamma}B_{R_{0}}
, and by Proposition
3.3
, we may choose
C>0
such that
\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)\subset B_{CR_{0}}
for any
n
. By (
3.4
), we know that

\displaystyle-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\nabla\varphi)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}-\frac{1}{4}f(y)+o(1)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}-\frac{1}{2}f(y)

in
B_{CR_{0}}
, for
n
large. In
B_{R_{n}}\setminus B_{CR_{0}}

-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\varphi)=0\geq\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}-\frac{1}{2}f(y)
.

Recall that
\mathrm{supp}(U_{n})\subset B_{R_{n}}
for
R_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}R
, whence we have
U_{n}=\varphi=0
on
\partial B_{R_{n}}
, so that by Lemma
2.3
we have
\varphi\geq U_{n}
. In particular,

outside
B_{CR_{0}}
.

To prove (
3.2
), recall that

-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\nabla U_{n})\leq\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\penalty\ \text{ in }B_{CR_{0}}.

Thus, taking

\psi(y)=\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}((CR_{0})^{2}-|x|^{2})

we have
\psi\geq 0
and
-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\nabla\psi)=2\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}+o(1)\geq\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}
in
B_{CR_{0}}
for

large, and

on
\partial B_{CR_{0}}
. By Lemma
2.3
,

, therefore

\|U_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\frac{(CR_{0})^{2}\beta_{n}}{2\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}},

∎

Corollary 3.5
.

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}>0
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.4
, we have

1=\intop\nolimits_{B_{r_{1}}}U_{n}\mu_{n}(y)\mathrm{d}y\leq 2r_{1}^{2}\mu_{n}(B_{r_{1}})\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}},

We now turn to the convergence of the interfaces. First, take

The Hausdorff convergence of the interfaces will follow from the following proposition.

Proposition 3.6
.

Assume
\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}\rightarrow\overline{\alpha}
. For every
\delta\in(0,\overline{\alpha})
, there exists

By the fact that

Proof of Theorem
2
.

By Proposition
3.3

, we may choose a subsequence such that

Classical regularity estimates (see

Recalling that

therefore
\|\overline{U}\|_{L^{1}(B_{r_{1}})}=1

Now, the above discussion implies that

(\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{\gamma}})

To finish, we prove the Hausdorff convergence of the interfaces. Let

\partial\{v_{\overline{\alpha}\pm\delta}>0\}=\left(1\pm\frac{\delta}{\overline{\alpha}}\right)^{1/\gamma}S.

Then,
\mathrm{dist}_{H}(\{v_{\overline{\alpha}+\delta}=0\},\{v_{\overline{\alpha}-\delta}>0\})\rightarrow 0

.

By Proposition
3.6
, for any

v(x)=\left(\frac{\overline{\alpha}}{\alpha}\right)^{2/3}\overline{v}\left(\left(\frac{\alpha}{\overline{\alpha}}\right)^{1/4}x\right)

solves (
6.2
).
∎

Proposition 6.2
.

There exist
C_{0},t_{1}>0
such that
\mathrm{supp}(U_{n})\subset(-t_{1},t_{1})\times\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, there is
C>0
such that

\|U_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C_{0}\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}\hskip 8.0pt\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

(6.6)

Proof.

The proof of Proposition
3.3
may be repeated verbatim in this case, which implies that
\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}\leq C_{0}
for some
C_{0}>0
. Then,
(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
W^{2,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}
for all
p\in(1,\infty)
.

Note that, since
\mathrm{supp}(U_{n})\subset B_{\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}R}
, we have that
U_{n}
minimizes

v\mapsto\intop\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}R}}\frac{|H_{n}\nabla v|^{2}}{2}+\left(y_{1}^{4}+y_{1}^{2}y_{2}^{2}+r_{n}(x)\right)v\mathrm{dy}

among functions all nonnegative functions
v\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}R})
. Then, for
n
sufficiently large, a bump function
\varphi
for
B_{1/2}
is admissible, so that

\|\nabla U_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C_{1}.

By Nash’s inequality, we have
\|U_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C_{2}
.

Take
\overline{x}\in B_{\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}R}
such that

1-\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{4}\leq 0\hskip 8.0pt\forall x\in B_{2}(\overline{x}).

In particular,

-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\nabla U_{n})\leq\left(\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}-\frac{1}{2}(y_{1}^{4}+y_{1}^{2}y_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon_{n}^{2}y_{2}^{6})\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{U_{n}>0\}}\leq 0\hskip 8.0pt\forall x\in B_{2}(\overline{x})

By the local maximum principle (see
[
8
, Theorem 8.17]
), we have

\sup_{B_{1}(x_{0})}U_{n}\leq C\|U_{n}\|_{L^{2}(B_{2}(\overline{x}))}\leq C_{3}.

Take
t=(2C_{3}\kappa_{0}+C_{0})^{1/4}
, where
\kappa_{0}
is that of Lemma
2.4
, and compute

-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\nabla U_{n})\leq-C_{3}\kappa_{0}\mathbb{1}_{\{U_{n}>0\}}\hskip 8.0pt\forall x\in(-t,t)\times\mathbb{R}

This yields, as in the proof of Proposition
3.2
, that
U_{n}\equiv 0
for
|x_{1}|>\frac{3}{2}t=t_{1}
.

Define

\psi=\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\inf_{B_{R}}h_{11}}\left(r_{1}^{2}-y_{1}^{2}\right),

which satisfies

\displaystyle-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\nabla\psi)

\displaystyle=-h_{11}^{n}\partial_{y_{1}y_{1}}\psi-\partial_{y_{1}}\psi(\partial_{y_{1}}(h_{11}^{n})+\partial_{y_{2}}(h_{12}^{n}))

\displaystyle=\frac{2\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\inf_{B_{R}}h_{11}}h_{11}^{n}+\varepsilon_{n}\left(\frac{2\beta_{n}x_{1}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}(\partial_{1}h_{11}(\varepsilon_{n}y)+\partial_{2}h_{12}(\varepsilon_{n}y))\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}-\frac{2\beta_{n}r_{1}\|H\|_{C^{1}(B_{R})}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}\varepsilon_{n}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}

in
(-r_{1},r_{1})\times\mathbb{R}
for
n
sufficiently large. Apply Lemma
2.3
in
(-r_{1},r_{1})\times(-\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}R,\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}R)
to get
\psi\geq U_{n}
in this set. In particular,

U_{n}\leq\frac{\beta_{n}r_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\inf_{B_{R}}h_{11}}\hskip 8.0pt\text{in }B_{\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}R},

which completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 6.3
.

There exist
C_{1},C_{2},\rho_{0},n_{0}>0
such that for all
\rho>\rho_{0}
and
n\geq n_{0}
,

\mathrm{supp}(U_{n})\cap\{|x_{2}|\in(\rho,2\rho)\}\subset\left\{|x_{1}|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\rho}\right\}\cap\{|x_{2}|\in(\rho,2\rho)\},

Moreover, in this latter set,

U_{n}\leq\frac{C_{2}\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}\rho^{2}}.

Proof.

Let
\rho>0
. By the symmetry of the equation, it suffices to prove the result for
x_{2}>\rho
. Denote
Q_{\rho}=(-r_{1},r_{1})\times\left(\frac{\rho}{2},\rho\right)
. In this set, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}-(y_{1}^{4}+y_{1}^{2}y_{2}^{2})+r_{n}(y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}-\frac{1}{2}y_{1}^{2}y_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{8}y_{1}^{2},

for some
\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}\leq C_{1}
.

Denote
c_{1}=\inf_{B_{R}}h_{11}
,
c_{2}=\sup_{B_{R}}h_{22}
and define the following functions

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{c_{1}}\left(\frac{24C_{1}^{2}}{\rho^{2}}-C_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{96}x_{1}^{4}\right)&\text{ if }x_{1}^{2}\leq\frac{48C_{1}}{\rho^{2}},\\
0&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{C_{1}}{8r_{2}}\left(x_{2}-\left(\frac{\rho}{2}+\sqrt{8C_{0}r_{2}}\right)\right)^{2}&\text{ if }x_{2}-\frac{\rho}{2}\in\left(0,\sqrt{8C_{0}r_{2}}\right),\\
0&\text{ otherwise },\end{cases}

\displaystyle\varphi_{3}(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{C_{1}}{8r_{2}}\left(x_{2}-\left(3\rho-\sqrt{8C_{0}r_{2}}\right)\right)^{2}&\text{ if }3\rho-x_{2}\in\left(0,\sqrt{8C_{0}r_{2}}\right),\\
0&\text{ otherwise },\end{cases}

and
w(y_{1},y_{2})=\varphi_{1}(y_{1})+\varphi_{2}(y_{2})+\varphi_{3}(y_{2})
. We compute

\displaystyle-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\nabla\varphi_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\inf_{B_{R}}h_{11}}\left(h_{11}^{n}\left(2C_{1}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{8}y_{1}^{2}\right)+\varepsilon_{n}\left(2C_{1}y_{1}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{24}y_{1}^{3}\right)(\partial_{1}h^{n}_{11}(\varepsilon_{n}y)+\partial_{2}h^{n}_{12}(\varepsilon_{n}y))\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{\varphi_{1}>0\}}

\displaystyle\geq 2C_{1}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{8}y_{1}^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{\rho}\left(2C_{1}+\frac{1}{24}\right)\|H\|_{C^{1}(B_{R})}

\displaystyle\geq 2C_{1}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{8}y_{1}^{2}-\varepsilon_{n}\left(2C_{1}+\frac{1}{24}\right)\|H\|_{C^{1}(B_{R})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{7C_{1}}{4}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{8}y_{1}^{2}

for
n
large enough but independent of
\rho
. Moreover, for
x_{2}\in\left(\frac{\rho}{2},\frac{\rho}{2}+\sqrt{8C_{0}r2}\right)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\nabla\varphi_{2})

\displaystyle=-h_{22}^{n}\frac{C_{1}}{4r_{2}}-\varepsilon_{n}\frac{C_{1}}{4r_{2}}\left(x_{2}-\left(\frac{\rho}{2}+\sqrt{8C_{0}r_{2}}\right)\right)\left(\partial_{1}h_{1}2^{n}(\varepsilon_{n}y)+\partial_{2}h_{2}2^{n}(\varepsilon_{n}y)\right)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{C_{1}}{4}-\varepsilon_{n}\sqrt{8C_{0}r_{2}}\|H\|_{C^{1}(B_{R})}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{3C_{1}}{8},

for
n
large independently of
\rho
. The computation for
\varphi_{3}
in the range
x_{2}\in(3\rho-\sqrt{8C_{0}r_{2}},3\rho)
is analogous. We conclude

-\mathrm{div}(H_{n}\nabla w)\geq C_{1}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{8}y_{1}^{2}\hskip 8.0pt\text{ in }Q_{\rho}.

Since, by construction,
w\geq 0
in
Q_{t}
and
w\geq U_{n}
on
\partial Q_{t}
, we apply Lemma
2.3
to get
w\geq U_{n}
. Then,
U_{n}\equiv 0
if
x_{1}>\frac{\sqrt{48C_{1}}}{\rho}
, and

U_{n}\leq\frac{24C_{1}^{2}}{\rho^{2}\inf_{B_{R}}h_{11}}\hskip 8.0pt\text{ for }x_{2}\in\left(\frac{\rho}{2}+\sqrt{8C_{0}r_{2}},2\rho-\sqrt{8C_{0}r_{2}}\right).

The result follows by taking
\rho_{0}\geq 2\sqrt{8C_{0}r_{2}}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4
.

We know that
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}<\infty
. Then, the right-hand side of the equation for
U_{n}
is bounded in compact sets. Replicating the argument of the proof of Theorem
2
, there exists
\overline{U}\in W^{2,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
U_{n}\rightharpoonup\overline{U}
weakly in
W^{2,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Take
\rho_{k}=2^{k}\rho_{0}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and define

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle=(-r_{1},r_{1})\times(-\rho_{0},\rho_{0}),

\displaystyle Q_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{C}{\rho_{k}},\frac{C}{\rho_{k}}\right)\times((-\rho_{k+1},-\rho_{k})\cup(\rho_{k},\rho_{k+1})),

\displaystyle\Psi(y)

\displaystyle=T\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\rho_{k}^{2}}\mathbb{1}_{Q_{k}}

where
T
is chosen so that
C_{0}\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}\leq T
in (
6.6
). Then,
U_{n}\leq\Psi
for all
n
sufficiently large. Moreover,

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Psi=T\left(4r_{1}\rho_{0}+\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{4C(\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k})}{\rho_{k}^{3}}\right)\leq C\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{2k}}<\infty.

By the dominated convergence theorem, we have that

1=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}U_{n}\mu_{n}(y)\mathrm{d}y\rightarrow\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\overline{U}\mathrm{d}y,

which implies
\|\overline{U}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=1
.

Hence, by (
6.6
), we must have
\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}>0
. Therefore, we may choose a further subsequence such that
\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{4}}\rightarrow\overline{\alpha}>0
and
\overline{U}
solves (
6.2
).
∎

7
Miscellanea

The approach used in the present paper may be used to treat a variety of other degenerate cases. In this section, we comment on other cases and point out some limitations of this approach.

7.1
Maximum attained on a curve

The analysis performed in Section
5
may be translated to a case where
g
attains its maximum on a closed curve. We describe how to do this in the sequel. Note that while we assume certain nondegeneracy of
g
along the whole curve, one could also study degenerate cases, where some concentration phenomena (akin to the one described after Theorem
2
) is expected.

Let
\Gamma=\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and let
\gamma\subset\Gamma
be a circle in
\Gamma
. Parametrize
\gamma
by
s:[0,2\pi]\rightarrow\Gamma
and write, in coordinates,

s(t)=\left(\sin(\phi_{0})\cos\left(t\right),\sin(\phi_{0})\sin\left(t\right),\cos(\phi_{0})\right),

for
\phi_{0}\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)
. Assume that
g
attains a maximum
on
\gamma
, and that

g(\phi,t)=g_{\mathrm{max}}-a(t)(\phi-\phi_{0})^{2}+O(|\phi-\phi_{0}|^{3})\leq g_{\mathrm{max}}-\frac{a(t)}{2}(\phi-\phi_{0})^{2}

if
|\phi-\phi_{0}|\leq\frac{1}{4}
with
\inf a(t)=a_{0}>0
. Suppose, without loss of generality, that there are no other maxima for
\phi\in\left(\frac{3}{4}\phi_{0},\frac{5}{4}\phi_{0}\right)

Let
M_{n}\rightarrow 0
, and take
n
sufficiently large in order to have
u_{n}(\phi_{0}\pm\frac{\phi_{0}}{4},t)=0
for all
t\in[0,L]
, denote
\Theta\subset\Gamma
as the set delimited by the curves
\{\phi=\left(1\pm\frac{1}{4}\right)\phi_{0}\}
, and
m_{n}
the mass of
u_{n}
over
\Theta
.

Define the rescaling

u_{m}(\phi,t)=m_{n}^{4/5}U_{n}(\phi_{0}+m_{n}^{-1/5}\eta,t),

which satisfies

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\Theta}u_{m}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{2\pi}\intop\nolimits_{3\phi_{0}/4}^{5\phi_{0}/4}u_{m}(\phi,t)\sin(\phi)\mathrm{d}\phi\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{2\pi}\intop\nolimits_{-m_{n}^{-1/5}\phi_{0}/4}^{m_{n}^{-1/5}\phi_{0}/4}m_{n}U_{n}(t,\eta)\sin\left(\phi_{0}+m_{n}^{1/5}\eta\right)\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}t,

and hence

\intop\nolimits_{0}^{2\pi}\intop\nolimits_{-m_{n}^{-1/5}\phi_{0}/4}^{m_{n}^{-1/5}\phi_{0}/4}U_{n}(t,\eta)\sin\left(\phi_{0}+m_{n}^{1/5}\eta\right)\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}t=1.

In what follows,
\varepsilon_{n}:=m_{n}^{1/5}
.

Moreover, denoting

L_{n}V=\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial\eta^{2}}+\varepsilon_{n}\text{cotan}(\phi_{0}+\varepsilon_{n}\eta)\frac{\partial V}{\partial\eta}+\frac{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{\sin^{2}(\phi_{0}+\varepsilon_{n}\eta)}\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial s^{2}},

we may write (
1.1
) for
U_{n}
as

-L_{n}U_{n}=\left(\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}-a(t)\eta^{2}+r_{n}(\eta)\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{U_{n}>0\}}\hskip 8.0pt\text{ in }\left(-\frac{1}{4\varepsilon_{n}},\frac{1}{4\varepsilon_{n}}\right)\times 2\pi\mathbb{S}^{1}

In the formal limit, we have

\begin{cases}-\partial^{2}_{\eta\eta}\overline{U}=\left(\overline{\alpha}-a(t)\eta^{2}\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{\overline{U}>0\}}\hskip 8.0pt\text{ in }\mathbb{R}\times 2\pi\mathbb{S}^{1},\\
\overline{U}\geq 0,\penalty\ \penalty\ \intop\nolimits_{0}^{2\pi}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{U}(\eta,t)\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{\sin(\phi_{0})}.\end{cases}

By the argument in Section
5.1
, we readily see that the solution
(\overline{U},\overline{\alpha})
to this problem is unique and has the form

\overline{U}(\eta,t)=\begin{cases}\frac{3\overline{\alpha}^{2}}{2a(t)}-\frac{\overline{\alpha}}{2}\eta^{2}+\frac{a(t)}{12}\eta^{4}&\text{ if }\frac{a(t)\eta^{2}}{3}\leq\overline{\alpha},\\
0&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

with
\overline{\alpha}
chosen in order to satisfy the mass restriction, namely

\overline{\alpha}=\left(\frac{23\sqrt{3}\sin(\phi_{0})}{20}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{2\pi}a^{-3/2}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right)^{-2/5}.

By the assumption
a\geq a_{0}>0
,
\overline{U}
is well defined.

Note that, in this case, it is possible to compare
U_{n}
with the subsolution

\psi(\eta,t)=\frac{\beta_{n}}{4\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}(r_{0}^{2}-\eta^{2})

for
r_{0}>0
depending only on
a
and a lower bound for
\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}
. As in Proposition
5.2
, this implies
\limsup_{n}\frac{\beta_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}<\infty
.

The rest of the discusion in Section
5
may be carried out verbatim to get

U_{n}\rightarrow\overline{U}\hskip 8.0pt\text{ in }L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times 2\pi\mathbb{S}^{1})

and

\partial_{\eta}U_{n}\rightharpoonup\partial_{\eta}\overline{U}\hskip 8.0pt\text{ weakly in }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times 2\pi\mathbb{S}^{1}).

7.2
A heuristic argument for other polynomials

The analysis performed in Section
6
may be useful in other cases where the level sets of the homogeneous part of
g
lead to
L^{1}
estimates. However, the method breaks down if the homogeneous part
g_{\mathrm{max}}-g
has a zero of sufficiently high degree in a given direction.

Consider for example

g(x)=g_{\mathrm{max}}-x_{1}^{8}-x_{1}^{6}x_{2}^{2}+r(x).

This implies, as in Section
6
, that, if
x_{2}>\rho
, then

\begin{array}[]{cc}U_{m}=0,\hskip 8.0pt\forall|x_{1}|>\frac{C}{\rho^{1/3}},\text{ and }&U_{m}\leq\frac{C}{\rho^{2/3}},\hskip 8.0pt\forall|x_{1}|\leq\frac{C}{\rho^{1/3}},\end{array}

which implies that, with
Q_{k}
defined analogously as in the proof of Theorem
4
, we define

\Psi=T\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\rho_{k}^{2/3}}\mathbb{1}_{Q_{k}}

which implies

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Psi\geq C\sumop\displaylimits_{k}^{\infty}\frac{\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}}{\rho_{k}}=C\sumop\displaylimits_{k}^{\infty}1=\infty,

so that the dominated convergence theorem cannot be applied.

In a general setting, there is a simple heuristic giving way to an implicit description of the limit profile. Suppose that a higher order Taylor expansion yields

g(x)=g_{\mathrm{max}}-x_{1}^{8}-x_{1}^{6}x_{2}^{2}-x^{10}+r(x).

Note that, in this case, the homogeneous polynomial of least degree has a nontrivial zero set (i.e.
\{x_{1}=0\}
), but the higher order term forces the maximum at
x=0
to be strictly positive.

Define the coordinates

\begin{array}[]{cc}x_{1}=\ell y_{1},&x_{2}=x_{2,0}+\ell y_{2},\end{array}

where
x_{2,0},\ell>0
may depend on
\beta_{M}
. Moreover, we will require that
\ell\ll x_{2,0}
.

Using the same notation as in the previous sections, we compute

(1+\alpha_{M})g-1=\beta_{M}-x_{2,0}^{10}-\ell^{6}x_{0,2}^{2}y_{1}^{6}\left(\left(\frac{\ell}{x_{0,2}^{2}}y_{1}\right)^{2}+\left(1+\frac{\ell}{x_{0,2}}y_{2}\right)^{2}\right)+o(1).

Choose
x_{0,2}=t\beta_{M}^{1/10}
with
t\in(0,1)
fixed and
\ell=\beta_{M}^{2/15}
. Hence

(1+\alpha_{M})g-1=\beta_{M}\left(1-t^{10}-t^{2}y_{1}^{6}+o(1)\right)

this suggests the rescaling

u_{M}(x)=\beta_{M}^{19/15}U_{M}(\beta_{M}^{-2/15}x_{1},\beta_{M}^{-2/15}(x_{2}-t\beta_{M}^{1/10})),

which satisfies

-L_{M}U_{M}=\left(1-t^{10}-t^{2}y_{1}^{6}+o(1)\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{U_{M}>0\}},

with
-L_{M}\varphi\rightarrow-\Delta\varphi
for any
\varphi\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Assuming that
U_{M}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, using nondegeneracy and regularity estimates we get that the support of
U_{M}
is bounded in the
y_{1}
direction and that it converges, up to a subsequence and weakly in
W^{2,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
to a bounded solution of

-\Delta\overline{U}=\left(1-t^{10}-t^{2}y_{1}^{6}\right)\mathbb{1}_{\{\overline{U}>0\}}.

This equation may be solved explicitly. If
|t|>1
, then
\overline{U}=0
. If
|t|<1
, by a computation similar to that of Section
5
get

\overline{U}(y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{(1-t^{10})^{4/3}}{t^{2/3}}\left(\dfrac{3\sqrt[3]{7}}{8}-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{t^{1/3}y_{1}}{(1-t^{10})^{1/6}}\right)^{2}+\dfrac{1}{56}\left(\dfrac{t^{1/3}y_{1}}{(1-t^{10})^{1/6}}\right)^{8}\right)&\text{ if }\left(\dfrac{t^{1/3}y_{1}}{(1-t^{10})^{1/6}}\right)^{6}\leq 7\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Reverting back to the
x
variable yields several consequences. First, if
t
is of order
\beta_{M}^{1/10}
, then

u_{M}(0,t)\sim\frac{(1-t^{10})^{4/3}}{t^{2/3}},

for
M
sufficiently small. If
x_{2}>t\beta_{M}^{1/10}
, then

u_{M}\equiv 0\hskip 8.0pt\forall|x_{1}|\geq\frac{C}{\beta_{M}^{2/15}t^{1/3}},

which shows the asymptotics of the width of the support in the
x_{1}
direction.
