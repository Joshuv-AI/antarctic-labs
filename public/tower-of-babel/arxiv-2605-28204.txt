Abstract
In the article the m ​ m mm -entropy (an entropy of a metric measure space) introduced by C. Shannon is evaluated for an α \alpha -stable Lévy process. For α < 1 \alpha<1 the double-sided estimates of the same order are obtained for process distribution in Skorokhod space.



Dedicated to the 80-th anniversary of H. Woźniakowski

1
Introduction

1.1
mm
-entropy: definition and history

Various kinds of entropy are used for complexity evaluation of mathematical objects since Clausius classical works in termodynamics
[
3
]
.
In this article, entropy is considered as a complexity measure for metric spaces. The most known notion used for this purpose is metric entropy some times also called
\varepsilon
-entropy. Let us recall the corresponding definition.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a metric space and let
A\subset\mathcal{X}
be a totally bounded subset of
\mathcal{X}
.
Let
N(A,\varepsilon)
denote the minimal number of closed balls of radius
\varepsilon
sufficient to cover the set
A
. Then

H(A,\varepsilon):=\ln N(A,\varepsilon)

is called
metric entropy
of
A
.

The idea of using metric entropy for measuring the ”complexity” and ”massiveness” of the sets in metric spaces dates back
to the works of L.S. Pontryagin and L.G. Shnirelman from the thirties years of the XX century. A huge progress in the study of
metric entropy of various sets in functional spaces was achieved by A.N. Kolmogorov’s school, see
[
11
]
and the famous surveys
[
12
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
.

A bit later, the metric entropy found unexpected and remarkable applications in Probability Theory where R. Dudley, V.N. Sudakov and X. Fernique used it for investigation of the sample path properties of Gaussian processes, cf.
[
13
,
14
]
.

The notion of metric entropy can be naturally extended to other classes of objects. For a
metric space

(\mathcal{X},\rho)
equipped with a
finite Borel measure

P
let us define the
mm
-entropy
by

\displaystyle N^{mm}(\varepsilon,\delta)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\min\{n:\exists x_{1},x_{2}\dots,x_{n}:P(\mathcal{X}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{n}B(x_{i},\varepsilon))\leqslant\delta\},

\displaystyle H^{mm}(\varepsilon,\delta)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\ln N^{mm}(\varepsilon,\delta),

where
B(x,r)
is the closed ball of radius
r
and center
x
. Notice that this definition uses the well known fact
that a measure
P
is concentrated on a compact up to an arbitrarily small mass
[
10
]
. The index ”
mm
” stands for ”measure metric” by analogy with M. Gromov’s

mm
-spaces
[
9
]
. The quantity
H^{mm}(\cdot,\cdot)
is called
mm
-entropy
of the triplet
(\mathcal{X},\rho,P)
. See also
[
25
]
for a more detailed analysis of the structure ”distance plus measure”.

The interest to the study of
mm
-entropy is, in particular, based on the fact that it enables to define a non-trivial invariant of
mm
-spaces with respect to measure preserving isometries. For example, one can state that there is no such isomorphism of two
mm
-spaces if their
mm
-entropies are different.

Let us say some words about the curious history of this notion. In fact, it was introduced by C. Shannon in his classical work
[
17
, Appendix 7]
but passed completely unappreciated on the background of other aspects of this work which made a revolution in information transmission theory. It is enough to say that in the first Russian translation of
[
17
]
the related Appendix (as well as some others) was omitted as being of no interest.
Therefore, the notion of
mm
-entropy, unlike that of metric entropy, has been almost not studied.
We are only aware of the work
[
16
]
and some other works of the same authors where this object appears in the context of signal transmission theory. Some researchers, apparently unaware of
[
17
]
, rediscovered it.

In the literature, several well studied notions can be found that are close to
mm
-entropy as
tools for measuring an
\varepsilon
-discretization error but have an integral form. In a vast literature devoted to the dictionary based information transmission, the moments of the discretization error are used, see, for example the works of G. Luschgy and G. Pages
[
15
]
,
S. Dereich with coauthors
[
4
]
–
[
7
]
. On the other hand, A.M. Vershik used the Kantorovich distance in his works from ergodic theory
[
23
]
.

In recent years, with A.M. Vershik’s initiative, the interest to
mm
-entropy has been renewed due to potential applications in ergodic theory.
Here, one of the most natural objects of study are the distributions of random processes in the corresponding spaces of trajectories.
In this direction, A.M. Vershik and M.A. Lifshits considered in
[
24
]
the distributions of Gaussian processes. They showed
that for a wide class of Banach spaces equipped with a Gaussian measure
mm
-entropy is tightly related to the metric entropy of the corresponding dispersion ellipsoid (the unit ball of the measure’s kernel, RKHS) and to the small ball measures. Interestingly, the result of
[
24
]
is new even for the distribution of a Wiener process.

The present work is the first one where the
mm
-entropy is evaluated for an important class of
non-Gaussian
processes.

1.2
Stable Lévy processes and Skorokhod space

Let us describe the metric space and the measure that we are going to investigate.

Consider an
\alpha
-stable Lévy process

X(t),\ t\in[0,1]
with parameter
0<\alpha<1
. It is well known that these processes admit a representation

X(t)=at+\int_{\mathcal{R}}\mathds{1}_{\{s\leq t\}}uN(du,ds),

where
a\in\mathbb{R}
is a drift coefficient,
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and
N
is a Poisson random measure on

\mathcal{R}
with intensity measure
\nu(du)ds
, while the Lévy measure
\nu
has a form

\nu(du)=\Big(\mathds{1}_{\{u>0\}}\frac{d_{1}}{u^{1+\alpha}}+\mathds{1}_{\{u<0\}}\frac{d_{2}}{|u|^{1+\alpha}}\Big)du,\qquad d_{1},d_{2}\geq 0.

(1)

This integral representation essentially means that the number of jumps of the process
X
that occur on a time
interval
ds
and whose size belongs to an interval
du
is a Poisson random variable of intensity
\nu(du)ds
.
Therefore,
X
is a jump process with independent and homogeneous in time increments.
If
a=0
, i.e. there is no trend, then the process
X
is also
1/\alpha
-self-similar.

The distribution of
X
is usually considered in the
Skorokhod space
(see more details in
[
18
,
19
,
2
,
26
]
); we recall now briefly its definition.

A function
f:[0,1]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
belongs to the class of
càdlàg
functions, if it is right continuous and has all left limits, i.e. there exist the limits

\begin{gathered}1)\lim_{s\to t+}f(s)=f(t),\quad t\in[0,1),\\
2)\lim_{s\to t-}f(s),\quad t\in(0,1].\end{gathered}

Such functions form a space
\mathbb{D}[0,1]
called
Skorokhod space
.

One can define several reasonable distances on
\mathbb{D}[0,1]
. We will be interested in one of the most frequently used of them, the so called
J
-distance.
The Skorokhod’s
J
-distance
\rho
between two càdlàg functions
f,g
is defined as

\begin{gathered}\rho(f,g):=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\max\big[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|f(t)-g(\theta(t))|,\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|t-\theta(t)|\big],\end{gathered}

where
\Theta
is the class of all increasing continuous mappings
[0,1]
onto itself.

Although
\mathbb{D}[0,1]
is both a linear and a topological space, it does not belong to the class of linear topological spaces because the addition operation is not continuous in it. This fact creates certain technical difficulties for operating in it, cf. Lemma
3.1
below.

For our process
X
, as well as for other jump processes, the space
\mathbb{D}[0,1]
is a natural sample path space in a sense that there exists a version of
X
such that its sample paths
X(t),0\leqslant t\leqslant 1,
belong to
\mathbb{D}[0,1]
with probability one.
Accordingly, there is a distribution of
X
on
\mathbb{D}[0,1]
, i.e. the Borel measure
\mathcal{P}
defined as

\mathcal{P}(A):=\mathbb{P}(X(\cdot)\in A).

In the following, we consider the Skorokhod space equipped with the
J
-distance and the distribution
\mathcal{P}
of an

\alpha
-stable Lévy process
X
,
as a metric triplet
(\mathcal{X},\rho,P)
, for which the
mm
-entropy will be evaluated.

1.3
The main result

For
mm
-entropy of the just defined triplet
(\mathbb{D}[0,1],\rho,\mathcal{P})
we will prove the following double-sided estimates having the same order.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
\delta>0
there exist positive constants
B_{-},B_{+}
such that for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
it is true that

B_{-}\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,|\ln\varepsilon|\leq H^{mm}(\varepsilon,\delta)\leq B_{+}\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\,|\ln\varepsilon|.

(2)

The article is organized as follows. In Section
2
a lower bound announced in Theorem
1.1
is obtained. In Section
3
we give a sketch of the proof of the corresponding upper bound using some auxiliary results whose proofs are postponed to
Section
4
. The final section describes further possible research directions.

In the following we use some standard notations. For a finite set
A
, the
quantity
|A|
denotes the number of elements of
A
. We denote by
||x||_{\infty}
the standard
sup-norm of a function
x
on
[0,1]
.

2
The lower bound for
mm
-entropy

Let
x\in\mathbb{D}

and
\varepsilon>0
. We denote:

M_{\varepsilon}(x)
– the number of jumps of
x
with absolute value larger than
5\,\varepsilon
,

K_{\varepsilon}(x)
– the number of jumps of
x
with absolute value larger than
3\,\varepsilon
,

N_{\varepsilon}(x)
– the number of jumps of
x
with absolute value larger than
\varepsilon
.

Remark 2.1
.

If
x,y\in\mathbb{D}
are such that
\rho(x,y)\leqslant\varepsilon
, then

N_{\varepsilon}(y)\geqslant K_{\varepsilon}(x)\geqslant M_{\varepsilon}(y).

(3)

For a non-negative integer
n
let us consider the set of trajectories

\{z\in\mathbb{D}:N_{\varepsilon}(z)=n\}
,
i.e. the trajectories having exactly
n
jumps with absolute value larger than
\varepsilon
.
We will evaluate the measure of the intersection of this set with a given closed ball of radius

\varepsilon
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For every
n\geqslant 0
and every
x\in\mathbb{D}
it is true that

\mathcal{P}(B_{x}(\varepsilon)\cap\{z:N_{\varepsilon}(z)=n\})\leqslant\mathcal{P}(\{z:N_{\varepsilon}(z)=n\})\cdot n^{k}\cdot(2\varepsilon)^{k},

where
k=K_{\varepsilon}(x)
,
B_{x}(\varepsilon)
is the closed ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered at
x
.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

If the intersection of the ball
B_{x}(\varepsilon)
with
\{z:N_{\varepsilon}(z)=n\}
is empty,
then the claim is obviously true.

Assume that this intersection is non-empty. Take
z
such that

\rho(x,z)\leqslant\varepsilon
and
N_{\varepsilon}(z)=n
.

Denote by
(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{k})
the instants of jumps of the function
x
with absolute value larger than

3\varepsilon
. Let
(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n})
be the instants of jumps of the function
z

with absolute value larger than
\varepsilon
. Since
n
is fixed, we may assume that
s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}
are independent and uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
.

Since
\rho(x,z)\leqslant\varepsilon
, every jump of
x
with absolute value larger than
3\varepsilon

is associated with a jump of
z
, with absolute value larger than
\varepsilon
and the difference of time
instants of these jumps does not exceed
\varepsilon
. This means that there exists a subset of indices

m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{k}
such that

\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}|t_{i}-s_{m_{i}}|\leqslant\varepsilon.

Let us evaluate the
\mathcal{P}
-measure of such
z
. First, let us fix

\{m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{k}\}
. Since the instants
s_{m_{1}},s_{m_{2}},\dots,s_{m_{k}}
are independent and uniformly
distributed, the
\mathcal{P}
-measure of
z
such that

\quad s_{m_{i}}\in[t_{i}-\varepsilon,t_{i}+\varepsilon]\qquad i\leqslant k,

admits the bound

\mathcal{P}(\{z:N_{\varepsilon}(z)=n\})\cdot(2\varepsilon)^{k}.

It remains to recall that the number of possible subsets of indices

\{m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{k}\}
does not exceed
n^{k}
, which leads to the announced inequality.

∎

For
\varepsilon>0
let
G(\varepsilon)
denote the expected number of jumps of the process
X
on
[0,1]

with absolute value exceeding
\varepsilon
.
By the definition of
X
and the formula for its intensity measure (
1
)
one has

G(\varepsilon)=\int_{|u|>\varepsilon}\nu(du)=(d_{1}+d_{2})\,\alpha^{-1}\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}=G(1)\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}.

(4)

The random variable
M_{\varepsilon}(X)
has Poisson distribution with intensity parameter

\mu_{M,\varepsilon}:=G\big(5\varepsilon\big),

By using (
4
), this can be rewritten as

\mu_{M,\varepsilon}=G(1)\big(5\varepsilon\big)^{-\alpha}.

Let

T_{M,\varepsilon}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{D}:|M_{\varepsilon}(x)-\mu_{M,\varepsilon}|\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{6\mu_{M,\varepsilon}}{\delta}}\right\}.

By Chebyshev inequality, we have

\mathbb{P}\{X\not\in T_{M,\varepsilon}\}\leqslant\frac{\delta}{6}.

(5)

Similarly, let us define two other sets of functions and write down the corresponding inequalities:

T_{K,\varepsilon}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{D}:|K_{\varepsilon}(x)-\mu_{K,\varepsilon}|\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{6\mu_{K,\varepsilon}}{\delta}}\right\},

with
\mu_{K,\varepsilon}=G(1)\big(3\varepsilon\big)^{-\alpha}
,

\mathbb{P}\{X\not\in T_{K,\varepsilon}\}\leqslant\frac{\delta}{6},

(6)

and

T_{N,\varepsilon}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{D}:|N_{\varepsilon}(x)-\mu_{N,\varepsilon}|\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{6\mu_{N,\varepsilon}}{\delta}}\right\},

where
\mu_{N,\varepsilon}=G(1)\varepsilon^{-\alpha}
,

\mathbb{P}\{X\not\in T_{N,\varepsilon}\}\leqslant\frac{\delta}{6},

(7)

We call
x\in\mathbb{D}

typical
, if it belongs to

T_{\varepsilon}:=T_{M,\varepsilon}\cap T_{K,\varepsilon}\cap T_{N,\varepsilon}

and
untypical
otherwise.
By inequalities (
5
), (
6
), (
7
), the
\mathcal{P}
-measure of untypical functions does not exceed
\frac{\delta}{2}
.

Therefore, we need to cover a part of the set
T_{\varepsilon}
having
\mathcal{P}
-measure at least

1-\frac{3\delta}{2}
.

Let us evaluate the
\mathcal{P}
-measure of typical functions covered by a ball of radius
\varepsilon
.
Consider an arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{D}
and assume that the ball
B_{x}(\varepsilon)
contains a typical
y\in T_{\varepsilon}
.
Let
m_{\varepsilon}:=\mu_{M,\varepsilon}-\sqrt{\frac{6\mu_{M,\varepsilon}}{\delta}}
.
Since
y\in T_{\varepsilon}
, we have

M_{\varepsilon}(y)\geqslant m_{\varepsilon},

hence, by (
3
),

K_{\varepsilon}(x)\geqslant M_{\varepsilon}(y)\geqslant m_{\varepsilon}.

(8)

By Lemma
2.2
it is true that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\big(B_{x}(\varepsilon)\cap\{z:N_{\varepsilon}(z)=N_{\varepsilon}(y)\}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\big(\{z:N_{\varepsilon}(z)=N_{\varepsilon}(y)\}\big)\cdot N_{\varepsilon}(y)^{K_{\varepsilon}(x)}\cdot(2\varepsilon)^{K_{\varepsilon}(x)}

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\big(\{z:N_{\varepsilon}(z)=N_{\varepsilon}(y)\}\big)\cdot(2N_{\varepsilon}(y)\varepsilon)^{K_{\varepsilon}(x)}.

Since
y\in T_{\varepsilon}\subset T_{N,\varepsilon}
, we have

N_{\varepsilon}(y)\leqslant\mu_{N,\varepsilon}+\sqrt{\frac{6\mu_{N,\varepsilon}}{\delta}},

therefore,

N_{\varepsilon}(y)\leqslant\tilde{c}\cdot\varepsilon^{-\alpha},

where
\tilde{c}
does not depend on
\varepsilon
.
By (
8
) it follows that for small
\varepsilon
one has

\big(2N_{\varepsilon}(y)\varepsilon\big)^{K_{\varepsilon}(x)}\leqslant(2\tilde{c}\cdot\varepsilon^{1-\alpha})^{K_{\varepsilon}(x)}\leqslant(2\tilde{c}\cdot\varepsilon^{1-\alpha})^{m_{\varepsilon}}.

(10)

We stress that this bound only makes sense for
\alpha<1
, as well as its corollaries that will be derived now.
From (
9
) and (
10
),
it follows that

\mathcal{P}\big(B_{x}(\varepsilon)\cap\{z:N_{\varepsilon}(z)=N_{\varepsilon}(y)\}\big)\leqslant\mathcal{P}\big(\{z:N_{\varepsilon}(z)=N_{\varepsilon}(y)\}\big)\cdot(2\tilde{c}\cdot\varepsilon^{1-\alpha})^{m_{\varepsilon}}.

By summing up these estimates over
n\in N_{\varepsilon}(T_{\varepsilon}):=\{N_{\varepsilon}(x),x\in T_{\varepsilon}\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\big(B_{x}(\varepsilon)\cap\varepsilon\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in N_{\varepsilon}(T_{\varepsilon})}\mathcal{P}\big(B_{x}(\varepsilon)\cap\{z:N_{\varepsilon}(z)=n\}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in N_{\varepsilon}(T_{\varepsilon})}\mathcal{P}\big(\{z:N_{\varepsilon}(z)=n\}\big)\cdot(2\tilde{c}\cdot\varepsilon^{1-\alpha})^{m_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(2\tilde{c}\cdot\varepsilon^{1-\alpha})^{m_{\varepsilon}}\cdot\sum_{n\in N_{\varepsilon}(T_{\varepsilon})}\mathcal{P}\big(\{z:N_{\varepsilon}(z)=n\}\big)\leqslant(2\tilde{c}\cdot\varepsilon^{1-\alpha})^{m_{\varepsilon}}.

Therefore,

N^{mm}(\varepsilon,\delta)\geqslant\frac{1-\frac{3\delta}{2}}{(2\tilde{c}\cdot\varepsilon^{1-\alpha})^{m_{\varepsilon}}}.

Hence

\begin{gathered}H^{mm}(\varepsilon,\delta)\geqslant m_{\varepsilon}|\ln(\varepsilon^{1-\alpha})|\big(1+o(1)\big)=\mu_{M,\varepsilon}(1-\alpha)|\ln\varepsilon|\big(1+o(1)\big).\end{gathered}

(11)

Since
\mu_{M,\varepsilon}=G(1)(5\varepsilon)^{-\alpha}
,
the bound (
11
) yields

H^{mm}(\varepsilon,\delta)\geqslant B_{-}\varepsilon^{-\alpha}|\ln\varepsilon|,

for all small
\varepsilon
and some
B_{-}>0
not depending on
\varepsilon
.

3
The upper bound for
mm
-entropy

Let us split the process
X
into two parts:

X=X_{\varepsilon}+X^{\varepsilon},

(12)

where

\displaystyle X^{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle at+\int_{\mathcal{R}}\mathds{1}_{\{s\leqslant t\}}\mathds{1}_{|u|>\varepsilon}uN(du,ds),

\displaystyle X_{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}}\mathds{1}_{\{s\leqslant t\}}\mathds{1}_{|u|\leqslant\varepsilon}uN(du,ds).

The process
X^{\varepsilon}
contains the linear part and the large jumps of absolute value exceeding
\varepsilon
,
while the process
X_{\varepsilon}
contains other (small) jumps.

For a function
f
defined on
[0,1]
and a number
\Delta\geqslant 0
let us introduce
the
oscillation modulus

\omega(f,\Delta):=\sup_{|s-t|\leqslant\Delta}|f(s)-f(t)|.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{D}
it is true that

\rho(x_{1}+x_{2},y_{1}+y_{2})\leqslant\rho(x_{1},y_{1})+||x_{2}-y_{2}||_{\infty}+\omega\big(y_{2},2\rho(x_{1},y_{1})\big).

The result of
3.1
enables to handle the processes of large and small jumps separately.
The proof of this lemma is given in Section
4
.

Let us now state two basic lemmas concerning the sizes of approximating nets.
Their proofs are also given in Section
4
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
B>0
and
\delta>0
there exists a constant
K_{1}>0
such that for all small
\varepsilon

one can find a finite net
\mathcal{Y}^{\varepsilon}
of size not exceeding
\exp(K_{1}\varepsilon^{-\alpha}|\ln\varepsilon|)

and satisfying

\mathbb{P}\big(\min_{y^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\varepsilon}}\rho(X^{\varepsilon},y^{\varepsilon})>\varepsilon^{B}\big)<\delta.

(13)

Lemma 3.3
.

For all
B>2\alpha
and
\delta>0
there exists a constant
K_{2}>0
such that for all small
\varepsilon

one can find a finite net
\mathcal{Y}_{\varepsilon}
of size not exceeding
\exp(K_{2}\varepsilon^{-\alpha}|\ln\varepsilon|)
and satisfying

\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\varepsilon}}\ \omega(y,2\varepsilon^{B})\leqslant 2\varepsilon

(14)

and

\mathbb{P}\big(\min_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\varepsilon}}||X_{\varepsilon}-y||_{\infty}>3\varepsilon\big)\leqslant\delta.

Let us derive the required upper bound for
mm
-entropy from these lemmas.
Let us fix a sufficiently large
B
, namely
B>\max\{1,2\alpha\}
.

Let us consider a net
\mathcal{Y}^{\varepsilon}
from Lemma
3.2
corresponding to the parameter
B

and to the covering measure
\frac{\delta}{3}
. Define a random element
y^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\varepsilon}
by the relation

y^{\varepsilon}:=\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{\varepsilon}}\rho(X^{\varepsilon},y).

Similarly, let us consider a net
\mathcal{Y}_{\varepsilon}
from Lemma
3.3
with the same parameters
and define a random element

y_{\varepsilon}:=\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\varepsilon}}||X_{\varepsilon}-y||_{\infty}.

We will show that the net
\mathcal{Y}^{\varepsilon}+\mathcal{Y}_{\varepsilon}
approximates the process
X
with the required precision.

Assume first that the following inequalities are true

\rho(X^{\varepsilon},y^{\varepsilon})\leqslant\varepsilon^{B},\qquad||X_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}||_{\infty}\leqslant 3\,\varepsilon.

Then by Lemma
3.1
we have

\displaystyle\rho(X^{\varepsilon}+X_{\varepsilon},y^{\varepsilon}+y_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\rho(X^{\varepsilon},y^{\varepsilon})+||X_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}||_{\infty}+\omega(y_{\varepsilon},2\rho(X^{\varepsilon},y^{\varepsilon}))

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{B}+3\,\varepsilon+\omega(y_{\varepsilon},2\varepsilon^{B}).

By taking into account the inequality
B>1
and the property (
14
),
we obtain in this case

\rho(X^{\varepsilon}+X_{\varepsilon},y^{\varepsilon}+y_{\varepsilon})\leqslant\varepsilon+3\,\varepsilon+2\,\varepsilon=6\,\varepsilon.

By Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
we obtain the probabilistic estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\rho(X^{\varepsilon}+X_{\varepsilon},y^{\varepsilon}+y_{\varepsilon})>6\,\varepsilon\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\rho(X^{\varepsilon},y^{\varepsilon})>\varepsilon^{B}\big)+\mathbb{P}\big(||X_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}||_{\infty}>3\varepsilon\big)\leqslant\frac{\delta}{3}+\frac{\delta}{3}<\delta.

The size of the net
\mathcal{Y}^{\varepsilon}+\mathcal{Y}_{\varepsilon}
does not exceed

\exp(K_{1}\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}|\ln\varepsilon|+K_{2}\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}|\ln\varepsilon|):=\exp(K_{3}\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}|\ln\varepsilon|),

hence

H^{mm}\Big(6\varepsilon,\delta\Big)\leqslant K_{3}\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}|\ln\varepsilon|,

Replacement
6\,\varepsilon
to
\varepsilon
yields

H^{mm}\big(\varepsilon,\delta\big)\leqslant B_{+}\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}|\ln\varepsilon|,

which is the upper bound required in Theorem
1.1
.

4
Proofs of lemmas

4.1
Proof of Lemma
3.1

If
\rho(x_{1},y_{1})=0
, i.e.
x_{1}=y_{1}
, then the lemma’s claim follows from the trivial estimate

\rho(x_{1}+x_{2},x_{1}+y_{2})\leqslant||(x_{1}+x_{2})-(x_{1}+y_{2})||_{\infty}=||x_{2}-y_{2}||_{\infty}.

Therefore, we proceed under assumption
\rho(x_{1},y_{1})>0
.

By the definition of
\rho(x_{1},y_{1})
, for every
\zeta>0
there exists a variable change

\theta:[0,1]\mapsto[0,1]
such that

|x_{1}(t)-y_{1}(\theta(t))|\leqslant\rho(x_{1},y_{1})+\zeta,\qquad t\in[0,1],

and

|t-\theta(t)|\leqslant\rho(x_{1},y_{1})+\zeta,\qquad t\in[0,1].

It follows that under the same time change,

\displaystyle|\big(x_{1}+x_{2}\big)(t)-\big(y_{1}+y_{2}\big)(\theta(t))|\leqslant|x_{1}(t)-y_{1}(\theta(t))|+|x_{2}(t)-y_{2}(\theta(t))|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\rho(x_{1},y_{1})+\zeta+|x_{2}(t)-y_{2}(t)|+|y_{2}(t)-y_{2}(\theta(t))|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\rho(x_{1},y_{1})+\zeta+||x_{2}-y_{2}||_{\infty}+\omega\big(y_{2},\rho(x_{1},y_{1})+\zeta\big).

Since the function
\omega(y_{2},\cdot)
is non-decreasing, for
\zeta<\rho(x_{1},y_{1})
we have

Proof of Lemma
3.3

Since the integral over Poisson random measure

\int_{\mathcal{R}}\mathds{1}_{\{s\leqslant t\}}uN(du,ds)

is an

-strictly stable process, it is also a
\frac{1}{\alpha}
-self-similar process, i.e.,

\int_{\mathcal{R}}\mathds{1}_{\{s\leqslant t\}}uN(du,ds)\overset{d}{=}\varepsilon\int_{\mathcal{R}}\mathds{1}_{\{s\leqslant t\cdot\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\}}uN(du,ds).

(18)

If we consider in the identity (
18
) only the jumps of
size not exceeding
\varepsilon
, we obtain

X_{\varepsilon}(t)\overset{d}{=}\varepsilon\cdot X_{1}(t\cdot\varepsilon^{-\alpha}).

(19)

Let us construct a uniform approximation of
X_{1}(t),t\in[0,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}]
, by a random function

Y(t)
,
t\in[0,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}]
taking only integer values.

Define a sequence of stopping times
(\tau_{j})
and a function
Y
as follows.
Let
\tau_{0}:=0
, and then define
\tau_{j+1}
by
\tau_{j}
distinguishing two cases:

a)

If
X_{1}(\tau_{j})\in[k,k+\frac{1}{2}),k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
then

\tau_{j+1}:=\min\{t\geqslant\tau_{j}:X_{1}(t)\geqslant k+1\text{ or }X_{1}(t)\leqslant k-1\},

and

Y(t):=k,\qquad\tau_{j}\leqslant t<\tau_{j+1}.

b)

If
X_{1}(\tau_{j})\in[k+\frac{1}{2},k+1),k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
then

\tau_{j+1}:=\min\{t\geqslant\tau_{j}:X_{1}(t)\geqslant k+2\text{ or }X_{1}(t)\leqslant k\},

and

Y(t):=k+1,\hskip 14.22636pt\tau_{j}\leqslant t<\tau_{j+1}.

In both cases we have

|Y(t)-X_{1}(t)|\leqslant 1,\qquad\tau_{j}\leqslant t<\tau_{j+1},

therefore,

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}|Y(t)-X_{1}(t)|\leqslant 1.

(20)

Let us consider some properties of this construction.

1)

A bound on the jump size of
Y(\cdot)
.

Since the sizes of jumps of
X_{1}(\cdot)
are bounded by
1
,
in case a) we have
X_{1}(\tau_{j+1})\in[k+1,k+2)
or
X_{1}(\tau_{j+1})\in(k-2,k-1]

Therefore,

Since
Y
takes only integer values, it is true that

|Y(\tau_{j+1})-Y(\tau_{j})|\leqslant 2.

(21)

The case b) is treated similarly.

2)

A bound on the number of jumps.

Introduce auxiliary stopping times

\sigma_{j}:=\min\big\{s>0:|X_{1}(\tau_{j}+s)-X_{1}(\tau_{j})|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\big\}.

By construction,
\sigma_{j}\leqslant\tau_{j+1}-\tau_{j}
, therefore, for every integer

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{m}\leqslant 2\varepsilon^{-\alpha})\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}\leqslant 2\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}\leqslant\frac{2\varepsilon^{-\alpha}}{m}\right).

\sigma_{j}
are i.i.d. and have a common finite expectation.
Let

\varepsilon
it is true that

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A\varepsilon^{-\alpha}}\leqslant 2\varepsilon^{-\alpha})\leqslant\frac{\delta}{6},

(22)

i.e., with large probability there are at most
A\varepsilon^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,2\varepsilon^{\beta}]}|X_{1}(t)|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\right).

Since by assumption of Lemma

5.1

Our upper bound for
