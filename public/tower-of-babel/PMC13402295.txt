Divergent C2 functionalization of N-Heteroarenes via nonclassical rearomatization
Authors: Lei Huang, Huilong Zhu, Nan Wu, Du Luo, Yunsheng Xue, Zhou Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Site-selective functionalization of N-heteroarenes remains a persistent challenge due to their intrinsic aromatic stabilization and multiple reactive sites. We present a unified strategy that redirects the reactivity of N-alkenyloxy-heteroarenium salts to access two distinct product classes. Under Grignard-reagent conditions, pyridinium substrates undergo ultrafast C2-functionalization via a concerted, six-electron retro-hetero-ene rearrangement—a transformation not previously described—delivering ortho-substituted pyridines (alkyl, aryl, heteroaryl, alkynyl) in up to 93% yield. In contrast, quinolinium salts engage in a cascade involving C2‑addition, a 3,3‑sigmatropic rearrangement, cycloaddition and dehydration to furnish N‑bridged polycyclic frameworks efficiently. Notably, the reaction pathway is governed by substrate sterics; C8 substitution on quinoline switches the mechanism to the retro-hetero-ene route. The method is scalable, enables late-stage diversification of complexed scaffolds and site-selective deuterium labeling. Here we show that this strategy, supported by mechanistic and computational studies, establishes a versatile platform for heteroarene editing via nonclassical rearomatization.

Introduction

Nitrogen-containing aromatic heterocycles are indispensable motifs in bioactive molecules and functional materials. Pyridines and quinolines underpin many FDA‑approved drugs, while N‑bridged tri‑fused scaffolds such as pyrroloquinolines offer unique three‑ring frameworks with antimicrobial, anticancer, and anti‑inflammatory activity1–3 (Fig. 1a). Yet site‑selective modification of these cores is hindered by multiple electrophilic sites, often necessitating directing groups, harsh conditions, and delivering limited regioselectivity4–7. In parallel, Minisci-type radical chemistry has emerged as a directing-group-free strategy for C2-alkylation and arylation of electron-deficient N-heterocycles8,9. By employing radical precursors such as carboxylic acids, alcohols, or aryl diazonium salts, this method enables the direct coupling of diverse fragments. Nevertheless, its practical application is often constrained by strongly oxidative or acidic media, limiting functional group tolerance. Moreover, achieving high regioselectivity for C2‑arylation on unbiased pyridines remains a fundamental challenge due to competing reactivity at the C4 position, and the direct introduction of alkenyl and alkynyl motifs is particularly difficult. Pyridine N‑oxides provide an alternative activation mode, enabling ortho‑ and para‑functionalization under milder conditions10–14. However, existing N‑oxide methodologies—such as Olsson and Almqvist’s dehydrating‑agent or microwave‑assisted aryl Grignard additions, are largely confined to para‑substituted substrates and exhibit limited functional‑group tolerance15,16. Similar challenges of regioselectivity and scope persist. Meanwhile, synthetic routes to 4H‑pyrrolo[3,2,1‑ij]quinolines are scarce and substrate‑specific: Adib’s phosphine‑mediated annulation yields only four dialkyl examples17, while Wang’s gold‑catalyzed cascade is indole‑dependent18. N-alkenyloxypyridinium salts, readily synthesized through Au(I)-catalyzed addition of pyridine N-oxides to alkynes, represent a versatile class of electrophilic intermediates. Previous studies have shown that their reactivity is predominantly governed by the terminal alkene moiety, enabling an SN2′ pathway with soft nucleophiles—such as alcohols, thiols, and arenes—to deliver valuable α-functionalized alkenyloxy products, including ketones, haloketones, esters, and amides19–28. Despite this established utility, the true synthetic potential of these salts extends beyond classical alkene functionalization. Redirecting nucleophilic attack toward the electron-deficient pyridinium core would not only significantly broaden accessible molecular diversity but also defy a long-standing reactivity paradigm29–31. However, achieving permanent incorporation of a nucleophile at the C2-position remains a formidable challenge. To date, only two examples of C2‑addition have been documented, including our initial report, and in both cases the nucleophile is transient, eventually undergoing rearrangement and rearomatization that cleaves the C–Nu bond32,33 (Fig. 1b). Thus, the development of strategies to harness the innate electrophilicity of the pyridinium ring for direct and stable C–Nu bond formation constitutes a significant and unmet goal in synthetic chemistry.

Metal cations in organometallic reagents, such as Grignard reagents, often exhibit selective coordination to heteroatoms with lone pairs, thereby significantly modulating reaction pathways and enhancing regioselectivity34,35. Due to their diverse structural variations and accessibility, they have become essential tools for the introduction of functional groups into molecular frameworks. We hypothesized that, in N‑alkenyloxypyridinium salts, the highly ionic carbon nucleophile would be electrostatically attracted to the positively charged pyridinium core—favoring C2 over alkene attack—and that Mg²⁺ chelation to N and O would rigidify the intermediate to further enhance selectivity. Crucially, this C2‑selective addition forges a robust C–C bond in place of a polar C–Nu linkage, suppressing nucleophile expulsion and unlocking new aromatization cascades (Fig. 1c).

In this work, we explore the reactivity of N-alkenyloxypyridinium and N-alkenyloxyquinolinium salts with Grignard reagents, unveiling two distinct yet complementary transformation pathways (Fig. 1d). For pyridine-based salts, a rapid intramolecular retro-hetero-ene reaction enables ortho-functionalization, furnishing structurally diverse pyridine derivatives with efficiency. This pathway also extends to isoquinoline and 8‑unsubstituted quinoline analogues. In contrast, for 8-unsubstituted quinoline-based salts, we discover a cascade reaction sequence initiated by C2-selective nucleophilic attack, culminating in the formation of N-bridged tri-fused 4H-pyrrolo[3,2,1-ij]quinolines.

Results and discussion

Condition optimization

In our initial studies, N‑alkenyloxypyridinium salt (1a)—quantitatively prepared from methyl hex‑5‑ynoate and pyridine N‑oxide—was treated with 2.0 equiv. of PhMgBr at –78 °C for 2 min, delivering 3a in 88% yield alongside the uncyclized methyl ketone precursor to 4a, with < 5% of rearrangement byproduct 3a′; Warming to room temperature prior to quench yielded a mixture of 3a and cyclized 4a (Table 1, entries 1–2). These results define a, highly C2‑selective nucleophilic addition to N‑alkenyloxypyridinium salts that is uniquely followed by an intramolecular retro‑hetero‑ene rearrangement36,37. Increasing PhMgBr beyond 2 equiv. decreased the yield of 4a, suggesting other reactions were taking place. Lowering the amount to 1.5 equiv. did not significantly change the amount of 3a formed but reduced the yield of 4a (entries 3–4). Further reduction to 1.0 equiv. still afforded the C2‑alkylated pyridine 3a, though in a slightly diminished yield of 79 % (entry 5). Thus, the use of 2.0 equiv. ensures both complete conversion in the C2‑functionalization and efficient downstream transformation of the rearrangement intermediate, leading to consistently high overall yields. Raising the reaction temperature from −78 °C to −60 °C led to decreased yields of both 3a and 4a (entry 6). At −40 °C, the yield of 3a dropped to 74%, indicating the temperature sensitivity of the reaction (entry 7). Further increasing the temperature to 25 °C caused a significant drop in 3a to 53%, accompanied by a marked exothermic response and side reactions (entry 8). Examining different magnesium halide reagents, we found that substituting PhMgBr with PhMgCl resulted in a decreased yield of 3a and a modest decrease in 4a (entry 9). In contrast, using PhLi led to a dramatic reduction in 3a yield (6%) and the reaction system became particularly complex, yielding not only 4-phenylpyridine but also other byproducts (entry 10). This implies that lithium reagents are less effective than magnesium-based reagents in promoting this transformation. Control experiments underscored the essential role of the alkenyloxy unit. Replacing 1a with pyridine N‑oxide led to predominantly ring‑opened products and only trace 3a, underscoring the unique reactivity conferred by the alkenyloxy unit (entry 11). Similarly, using 1‑methoxypyridinium salts gave complex mixtures with low conversion (entry 12). By contrast, substrates bearing terminal alkenes, such as 1-((4-phenylbut-1-en-2-yl)oxy)pyridin-1-ium and 1‑(pent ‑1‑en‑2‑yloxy)pyridinium, underwent smooth conversion to 3a (entries 13–14). These results establish the terminal alkene as a necessary structural element, likely serving both as a π-acceptor for charge stabilization and a participant in the retro‑ene process. Finally, a streamlined one‑pot procedure, where 1a generated in situ was used directly, provided 3a in 83% yield, offering a practical albeit slightly lower‑yielding alternative to the stepwise isolation protocol (entry 15). Although the yields decreased slightly, the reaction demonstrated good robustness. It proceeded efficiently in an open vessel (81% yield) and tolerated technical-grade THF containing up to 0.5% water (83% yield), compared to the 88% obtained under optimized conditions (entries 16-17).

Reaction scope of retro-hetero-ene reaction

Under these optimized conditions, the reaction’s scope was thoroughly investigated (Fig. 2). We first examined the reactivity of various Grignard reagents with 1a. Both electron-donating (Me, TMS, OMe) and electron-withdrawing (Ph, CF₃, F, Cl, Br) para-substituted phenyl Grignard reagents reacted smoothly, yielding 3b–3i in good yields. Meta-substituted phenyl Grignard reagents (e.g., MeO and Cl) showed comparable efficiency to their para-substituted counterparts (3j vs. 3 f, 3k vs. 3 h). However, ortho-methoxy-substituted phenyl Grignard reagents afforded notably lower yields (3 l vs. 3 f, 3j), suggesting steric hindrance negatively impacts reaction efficiency. Beyond aryl groups, diverse heteroaryl Grignard reagents were well tolerated, delivering good yields (3m–3q). Extending to aliphatic Grignard reagents, alkynyl Grignard reagents, such as phenylethynylmagnesium bromide, afforded 3r in moderate yields, slightly lower than their aryl counterparts. Other alkyl Grignard reagents, including long-chain, short-chain, and sterically hindered variants, all participated efficiently, producing 3s–3x in acceptable yields. 4-Substituted pyridine N-oxides provided 2,4-disubstituted pyridines in good yields. Notably, 4-phenyl-substituted pyridine N-oxide gave the highest yield (3 y, 93%). Halogen-substituted derivatives (3aa, 3ab) were compatible, enabling further cross-coupling reactions. Meta-substituted pyridine N-oxides gave separable 2,3- and 2,5-disubstituted pyridines (3af, 3af’). In the case of ortho-substituted pyridine N-oxides (MeO, Ph, Me), moderate to good yields (3ag–3ai) were obtained. Additionally, ortho-heteroaryl-substituted pyridine N-oxide yielded 2,6-diarylated pyridine (3aj), expanding the synthetic utility. Encouraged by these results, we expanded the study to polycyclic N-oxides. Remarkably, isoquinoline N‑oxide also participates in this process, undergoing C1‑selective nucleophilic addition followed by a concerted retro‑hetero‑ene rearrangement to deliver 3ak. To avoid decomposition of N‑alkenyloxyquinolinium salts on silica, we employ a one‑pot sequence: Au‑catalyzed alkynylation of the N‑oxide is followed directly by addition of the Grignard reagent, obviating intermediate isolation. C8-substituted quinoliniums bearing halogen, methyl, or methoxy groups smoothly underwent the retro‑hetero‑ene process to give 3al–3ao in good yields. However, when the unsubstituted N-alkenyloxyquinolinium salt was used, notable reactivity divergence emerged, only trace amounts of 3ap were detected, and the retro-hetero-ene pathway did not occur. The reaction proceeded efficiently across randomized substrates, yielding 3aq–3aad in good yields and demonstrating broad applicability.

Reaction scope of sigma rearrangements

This unexpected reactivity prompted a systematic investigation into the behavior of these quinolinium species. Optimization of acid, temperature, metal reagent, and reagent stoichiometry led to the development of a practical two‑step, one‑pot protocol (see Supplementary Table 1). With optimized conditions in hand, we surveyed a diverse array of terminal alkynes to define the scope of the 4H-pyrrolo[3,2,1-ij]quinoline synthesis (Fig. 3). The model substrate, 5-phenylpent-1-yne, smoothly delivered 5a. Furthermore, the scope proved to be broad; For instance, 5-cyanopentyn-1-yne underwent a smooth transformation with quinoline N-oxide and phenyl Grignard reagent, affording 5b in 80% yield with the cyano group remaining intact. The structure of 5b was confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (CCDC2391609). Likewise, ester‑terminated pent‑4‑ynoate gave 5c without ester cleavage. Enyne and tosylate derivatives furnished 5d–5e, providing versatile handles for downstream diversification. Notably, 5-chloropentyne exhibited high reactivity, delivering 5 f in 82% yield. Although aryl alkynes displayed slightly lower reactivity than their aliphatic counterparts, they still afforded the desired 5g–5 h in moderate yields. Alkynes bearing electron‑donating groups furnished 5i–5j, while sulfonyl‑substituted and simple linear alkynes delivered 5k–5 l in excellent yields, underscoring the broad compatibility of this protocol. We then turned to the scope of quinoline N‑oxides. A series of monobrominated N‑oxides, with Br substituents at the 3‑, 4‑, 5‑, 6‑, and 7‑positions, smoothly engaged in the cyclocascade to afford 5o–5 s in 40–76 % yield. Both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups were well-tolerated, affording 5t–5z in moderate to good yields (up to 80%). Disubstituted halogenated quinoline N‑oxides likewise performed reliably, albeit with slightly diminished efficiency (5aa–5ad). Phenanthridine N‑oxide also participated smoothly in the cascade, delivering 5ae in 70 % yield and further underscoring the broad functional‑group compatibility of the method. With both alkyne and N‑oxide scopes defined, we next surveyed a diverse set of Grignard reagents under the optimized one‑pot conditions. Aryl Grignard reagents exhibit excellent reactivity regardless of electronic bias, with both electron-rich and electron-deficient variants furnishing 5af-5am. Bulky aryl Grignard reagents, exemplified by naphthylmagnesiumbromide, resulting 5an in 68% yield. Heteroaryl Grignard reagents, including thiophene and indole derivatives, were also compatible, affording 5ao–5aq in moderate yields. Finally, simple aliphatic Grignard reagents proved highly effective, delivering the products in uniformly excellent yields. For example, MeMgBr furnished 5ar in 86 % yield, while EtMgBr, i‑BuMgBr and n‑BuMgBr delivered 5as, 5at and 5au in 70 %, 60 % and 75 % yields, respectively. Additional aliphatic Grignard variants were also well-tolerated. Trimethylsilyl‑acetylene, phenyl, long‑chain alkyl, and cyclic-ether–substituted reagents furnished 5av–5az in good yields, and notably, cyclopropylmagnesium bromide delivered 5aaa in 81 % yield. Ethynyl and propargyl magnesium bromides smoothly delivered 5aab–5aac and 5aae in good yields. Even conjugated enyne‑type Grignard reagents (5aad) underwent the cascade without loss of efficiency, demonstrating that extended π‑systems are tolerated. Alkenyl Grignard reagents, including 2-methylprop-1-en-1-yl and vinylmagnesium bromide, delivered 5aaf-5aag further underscoring the broad applicability of this strategy.

Gram-synthesis and applications

Having investigated the substrate scope and divergent reactivity of N‑alkenyloxypyridinium and N‑alkenyloxyquinolinium salts, we next assessed the synthetic utility of these transformations. The gram‑scale synthesis and subsequent skeletal editing of 3a further underscore the synthetic potential of this approach38 (Fig. 4a). To further demonstrate the reproducibility, scalability, and operational simplicity of the strategy, a gram‑scale synthesis was performed starting from commercially available 8‑methylquinoline N‑oxide, affording product 3al in 87% yield (Fig. 4b). Additionally, gram‑scale synthesis of 4H‑pyrrolo[3,2,1‑ij]quinoline was carried out, delivering product 5aaa in 73% yield (Fig. 4c). As a further demonstration of the versatility and synthetic value of this reaction platform, we extended its use to precision isotopic labeling, a transformation typically achieved via multi-step sequences. Although alternative routes involving deprotonation-deuteration of terminal alkynes followed by hydration exist39, nevertheless, the retro-hetero-ene reaction constitutes a distinct, one-pot alternative and proved remarkably effective for the late-stage deuteriation of structurally diverse bioactive molecules, including Benzbromarone 7, Indomethacin 8, Estrone 9, Isoxepac 10, Fenofibric acid 11 and Ciprofibrate 12—all of which exhibited excellent deuterium incorporation efficiencies with good yields (Fig. 4d). Comparison with prior work

To evaluate the efficiency of our method, we conducted comparative studies with representative conventional procedures across several key transformations. First, our protocol demonstrates superior regioselectivity attributable to the concerted retro-hetero-ene mechanism. For instance, in the synthesis of 2-ethylpyridine, we achieved exclusive C2-selectivity, in sharp contrast to traditional methods that yield mixtures of C2 and C4 regioisomers40 (Fig. 5a). The synthesis of 2-vinyl-8-hydroxyquinoline, a pivotal intermediate for bioactive 8-hydroxyquinoline derivatives, further highlights our advantages. Unlike the conventional three‑step sequence starting from 2‑methyl‑8‑hydroxyquinoline (3aae), which affords average 60% yield and relies on hazardous n‑BuLi41, our route utilizes commercially available 8‑methoxyquinoline N‑oxide and improves step‑economy, replaces hazardous reagents with safer alternatives, and exhibits excellent functional‑group tolerance, offering a practical and modular pathway to diverse 8‑hydroxyquinoline scaffolds (Fig. 5b). Similarly, for the key Caerulomycin K intermediates 2-phenyl-4-methoxypyridine and 2-phenyl-4-chloropyridine, conventional Minisci-type reactions starting from 4-methoxypyridine or 4-chloropyridine give yields of only 19% and 56%7, respectively. Our protocol provides the same compounds 3aa and 3ac in 78% and 82% yields, highlighting its superior efficiency (Fig. 5c). Notably, we also applied our method to the direct late-stage functionalization of pharmaceutical molecules—a context where such transformations are often challenging (Fig. 5d). For example, bisacodyl was successfully functionalized at the pyridine ortho-position using our protocol to afford product 22 in 94% yield. In contrast, traditional Minisci conditions failed with this substrate and are generally limited to a narrow range of alkylations. Our method overcomes this limitation, enabling not only arylations (e.g., 22) but also efficient alkylations, such as the cyclohexyl-modified product 23 obtained in 68% yield. Loratadine was likewise diversified to give 24 in 78% yield. Even for the challenging ethylation at the ortho-position of the pyridine ring in loratadine, our method delivered the product 25 in 31% yield, which is superior to the 26% yield reported in the literature42. Overall, existing methods for C2-functionalization of pyridines are often highly specialized. For instance, methods for C2-alkylation typically cannot be applied for arylation or alkenylation, and vice versa. Our protocol breaks this paradigm by providing a single, unified strategy that efficiently delivers all of these substitution patterns (alkyl, aryl, alkenyl, and alkynyl) from a common intermediate. This greatly simplifies the synthetic toolbox available to chemists aiming to diversify pyridine cores at the C2 position. Moreover, reports on synthetic methods for 4H‑pyrrolo[3,2,1‑ij]quinolines are even scarcer—to the best of our knowledge, only two examples have been documented17,18, neither of which matches the broad substrate scope and high selectivity achieved by our approach, as already outlined in the introduction section.Fig. 5Comparison with prior work.a Prior method versus our method for 3aaf. b Prior method versus our method for 3aae, a precursor to bioactive 8-hydroxyquinolines. c Prior method versus our method for 3aa and 3ac, key precursors to Caerulomycin K. d Prior method versus our method versus for drugs late-stage functionalization. Mechanistic investigation

To elucidate the distinct reactivity pathways of N-alkenyloxypyridinium and N-alkenyloxyquinolinium salts upon treatment with Grignard reagents, we conducted a series of mechanistic studies, including deuterium labeling

experiments, kinetic isotope effect (KIE) measurements, control experiments, and DFT calculations.

When the reaction of 1a with PhMgBr is carried out at –78 °C and then quenched directly with D₂O, no deuterated 4a is observed (Fig. 6a, b). This finding confirms that C2–H transfer and alkene capture occur concomitantly during the retro‑hetero‑ene step—before any work‑up or external proton source can intervene. The absence of undeuterated 4a‑D under these conditions underscores the intramolecular, concerted nature of the hydrogen (or deuterium) shift and precludes the involvement of discrete, long‑lived intermediates or external protonation events. Collectively, these results strongly support a mechanistic model in which deuterium transfer and alkene capture occur in a single step, reinforcing the intramolecular nature of the transformation. Parallel KIE studies between 1a and 1a‑d₅ (kH/kD = 1.33) rule out C–H bond cleavage as the rate‑determining step (Fig. 6c). Control reactions omitting the alkenyloxy moiety furnish only ring‑opened byproducts, confirming that the retro‑hetero‑ene rearrangement is essential for both regioselectivity and efficiency (Fig. 6d). To elucidate the reaction mechanism for the formation of 4H‑pyrrolo[3,2,1‑ij]quinoline, several control and labeling experiments were performed (Fig. 7a, b). Reactions of PhMgBr with and without the Au(I) catalyst showed that Au(I) is necessary only for the formation of the alkenyloxyquinolinium intermediate. Substituent effects at the C8-position were also explored, where the presence of an 8-methoxy group completely inhibited the formation of the 4H-pyrrolo[3,2,1-ij]quinoline core, instead yielding a dearomatized product 26, indicating that the C8 hydrogen is crucial for enabling downstream transformations. Moreover, steric hindrance from bulky alkynes resulted in reduced yields of the desired 5aak, alongside an alternative C2-deprotonation pathway, leading to side products such as 27. Further investigations demonstrated that the second equivalent of the Grignard reagent plays a dual role, initially participating in nucleophilic addition and subsequently acting as a base to facilitate the cyclization step leading to pyrrole formation, as evidenced by the quantitative formation of biphenyl 28 when quenched with D₂O. A 13C‑labeling experiment provided direct evidence for the proposed sigmatropic rearrangement. 13C NMR and HRMS analysis of the resulting tricyclic product 13C‑5a unambiguously showed that the 13C‑label was retained exclusively at the C8 position. This result directly traces the fate of the alkyne carbon and confirms its incorporation into the newly formed C–C bond at C8, consistent with a 3,3‑sigmatropic rearrangement. Based on these findings, a plausible mechanism for 5 is proposed (Fig. 7c), wherein Au(I) catalysis enables initial alkyne addition, followed by protonation and formation of the N-alkenoxyquinolinium intermediate. The first equivalent of Grignard reagent then adds regioselectively at C2, triggering a concerted 3,3‑sigmatropic shift. A second Grignard equivalent serves as a base to deprotonate at C8, inducing intramolecular cyclization to form the pyrrole ring. Discussion

In conclusion, our study overcomes the limitations of previous reactions involving N-alkenyloxypyridinium salts, which were confined to peripheral editing at the terminal alkene carbon, by achieving highly selective transformations centered on the nitrogen heterocyclic core. By employing a Grignard’s reagent to induce dearomatization of the heterocyclic core, we achieve high selectivity for either a previously unknown retro-hetero-ene reaction or a 3, 3-sigmatropic rearrangement. This work not only broadens the reactivity profile and synthetic applications of these salts but also establishes a robust platform for constructing structurally complex, nitrogen-rich heterocycles. The practicality of this approach is underscored by its scalability, utility for site-selective deuterium incorporation, and efficacy in the late-stage diversification of drug-like scaffolds, offering a nonclassical re-aromatization pathway in heterocyclic synthesis.

Methods

General procedure for synthesis of ortho-substituted pyridine 3

To a flame-dried flask was added N-alkenoxypyridinium salt 1 (0.2 mmol). The flask was evacuated and backfilled with argon (two cycles), then anhydrous THF (0.4 mL) was introduced via syringe. The reaction vessel was transferred to a cryogenic reactor and cooled to −78 °C. Grignard reagent 2 (0.4 mmol, 2.0 equiv.) was added dropwise over 2 min at −78 °C. At this point, the C2-functionalization was complete. The reaction could be directly quenched at −78 °C to obtain the main product 3. Alternatively, to obtain the valued lactone byproduct 4a, the reaction mixture was warmed to ambient temperature, allowing the second equivalent of the Grignard reagent to convert the ketone ester intermediate (Int-4a; see Fig. 6b) into lactone 4a via a tandem process. The reaction was then quenched with saturated aqueous NH₄Cl (1 mL) and extracted with CH₂Cl₂. The combined organic extracts were dried over Na₂SO₄, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude residue was purified by flash chromatography on silica gel to afford the target products 3 (and 4a, if the warming procedure was applied).

General procedure for synthesis of 4H-pyrrolo[3,2,1-ij]quinolones 5

To a flame-dried flask was charged with N-oxide (0.21 mmol, 1.05 equiv.), Tf2NH (0.205 mmol, 1.03 equiv.), and HFIP (0.2 mL), and the mixture was stirred for 5 min. To this mixture were added alkyne (0.2 mmol) and PPh3AuNTf2 (2.5 mol%), and the mixture was diluted with HFIP (0.1 mL) under an argon atmosphere. The flask was sealed, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 4 h. After completion, the solvent was replaced with anhydrous THF (0.6 mL). The reaction flask was placed in a cryogenic reactor and cooled to −78 °C, and Grignard reagent 4 (0.4 mmol, 2.0 equiv.) was added dropwise over 1 min at −78 °C. The reaction was then quenched by the addition of 1 mL of saturated ammonium chloride solution. The mixture was then extracted with CH2Cl2, dried over Na2SO4, and evaporated under reduced pressure. The crude material was purified by flash chromatography on a silica gel column to afford 5.
