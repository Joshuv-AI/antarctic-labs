3. Executive Summary:

Continued growth in solar photovoltaics (PV) will rely on more utility-scale PV
deployment, which often occurs in rural areas due to high availability and low cost of
land. The extent of rural solar deployment depends on deployment costs and
permissive zoning ordinances. Not knowing whether and how solar PV will impact long-
term social and economic conditions, communities have little incentive to establish or
clarify PV zoning rules. Zoning ordinances that fail to address PV increase PV soft costs
and deployment barriers.

To reduce soft costs of and barriers to utility-scale PV and enable its widespread
deployment, our project aimed to improve knowledge flows about utility-scale solar in
rural communities. Through improving knowledge flows, we aimed to enable
communities to make more informed zoning decisions.

Our project proceeded through four major areas of work.

 Area 1: Collected data on knowledge flows, economic impacts, and trusted
 stakeholders from communities across our study region.
 Area 2: Developed computational models for estimating the economic impacts of
 utility-scale PV deployments in rural economies, and for analyzing utility-scale
 solar deployment into the future across our study region given economic impacts
 and zoning constraints
 Area 3: Tested mechanisms for disseminating knowledge on how utility-scale PV will
 affect rural economies through experiments
 Area 4: Disseminated research to stakeholders

Across our first three areas of work, our work generated new insights and methods that
are detailed in the following pages. Here we highlight some of the most significant
insights, advances, and products.
 • We created a website, https://graham.umich.edu/rural-solar, as a central
 repository for factsheets, publications, and models and their documentation.
 • We compiled the most comprehensive publicly available database of solar zoning
 ordinances, including 2,165 ordinances and covering more than 90% of
 communities across our six-state region. We found the majority (70%) of zoning
 ordinances in the Great Lakes region are silent on principal-use solar energy
 systems.
 • We advanced power system modeling tools to incorporate zoning ordinances
 and local economic impacts of utility-scale solar. We found zoning ordinances
 significantly reduce solar investment and increase system costs, and that local
 economic benefits can be significantly increased at little additional solar
 investment cost.
 • Our interviews as to how knowledge about solar flows through communities
 yielded several important insights, including that ‘anchor’ landowners or
 prominent local officials often act as knowledge brokers in communities,
 influencing how a project was perceived and whether strong opposition
 mobilized.
 • In our work on the effects of knowledge dissemination mechanisms, we focus on
 how knowledge will be “packaged” in the dissemination process with a two-round
 survey design. We find that most respondents (76%) showed at least moderately
 support for solar zoning and development with statistically significant differences
 among different stakeholder groups, states, information packaging (e.g., negative
 or positive description), and information updates (e.g., the implications of one’s
 choices), highlighting the critical role of tailored information dissemination design.
We recommend future work broaden their perspective across a larger geography and
set of energy technologies, particularly by also including wind power.

5. Background:
Continued growth in solar photovoltaics (PV) will rely on more utility-scale PV
deployment, which often occurs in rural areas due to high availability and low cost of
land (Sorensen et al. 2018). The extent of rural solar deployment depends on
deployment costs and permissive zoning ordinances (Day 2017). Our preliminary work

 Page 3 of 34

in Michigan indicated that less than a quarter of the regional zoning ordinances consider
utility-scale solar projects. Not knowing whether and how solar PV will impact long-term
social and economic conditions, communities have little incentive to establish or clarify
PV zoning rules. Zoning ordinances that fail to address PV increase PV soft costs and
deployment barriers. Our project aimed provide analysis enabling communities to make
informed zoning decisions. Our project proceeded through four major areas of work.

Area 1: Collected data on knowledge flows, economic impacts, and trusted
stakeholders from communities across our study region.
The public acceptance of utility-scale solar projects not only depends on their technical
feasibility and potential economic benefits, but also on how information about projects
more broadly flows throughout communities and who is perceived as a credible
source. In rural areas, such knowledge flows are typically shaped by local governance,
personal networks, and relationships between key stakeholders of interest. While there
is an abundance of prior research that examines public attitudes towards large-scale
renewable energy development, there is less that empirically investigates
communications processes and trusted stakeholders involved with utility-scale solar
siting, particularly in the Great Lakes region. Better understanding these dynamics is
important, as the timing, accuracy, and perceived credibility of project-specific
information can discernibly influence whether communities view solar as a positive or
negative development. In this context, citizens rely both on informal means of
communication, such as word-of-mouth conversations and social media, and more
formal sources such as local newspapers and public informational
meetings. Investigating these patterns is useful in understanding both the identity of the
messenger and the content of the message itself. Ultimately, addressing this research
gap can help inform the design of better community engagement strategies that improve
transparency, trust, and supports participation in local decision making regarding utility-
scale solar projects.

Area 2: Developed computational models for estimating the economic impacts of
utility-scale PV deployments in rural economies, and for analyzing utility-scale
solar deployment into the future across our study region given economic impacts
and zoning constraints

Our second area of work focused on computational modeling of solar PV in two areas.
First, we built computational models for estimating the economic impacts of utility-scale
PV deployments in rural economies. Prior work has focused on the socio-economic
dimensions of utility-scale solar siting, particularly the local economic impacts. On one
hand, utility-scale solar can be highly lucrative for local economies. Solar development
often provides landowners with significantly higher income through lease agreements
compared to traditional agricultural uses. For example, in Michigan, typical solar leases
offer landowners an average of $800 per acre annually for terms of 20 to 25 years,
significantly exceeding farmland rental rates, which average around $127 per acre per
year. Beyond direct landowner benefits, solar installations can generate economic
benefits for host communities, including job creation, increased property tax revenues,
and long-term economic diversification. On the other hand, the conversion of

 Page 4 of 34

agricultural lands to solar use can disrupt rural economies by affecting local agricultural
supply chains, reducing agricultural employment, and diminishing ancillary industries.
The standard regional economic model for quantifying local economic impacts of new
developments is the economic input-output (IO) model. An appropriately-specified IO
model makes up the core of the National Renewable Energy Lab’s Job and Economic
Development Impact Model (JEDI). IO-based assessments track direct, indirect, and
induced economic effects of solar projects, capturing changes in economic indicators
such as employment, earnings, gross output, and value-added contributions throughout
the project’s lifecycle. Existing literature documents positive economic outcomes of
renewable energy investments at various scales—county, state, regional, and national.
It is less common for such studies to also account for lost economic activities
associated with converting agricultural lands to solar. Rather, researchers suggest that
economic impact assessments should also account for direct effects of lost agricultural
production. This omission overestimates perceived benefits and could potentially
undermine efforts to balance solar expansion with the long-term viability of local
agricultural economies. An additional shortcoming of the current literature is that few
studies of utility-scale solar in the United States compare economic impact estimates
across multiple potential installation sites. Rather, available studies tend to focus on a
single individual project within a targeted geographic area. Accordingly, it is difficult to
generalize expected economic effects over multiple distinct studies, often employing
different key assumptions and over different regions. This gap limits our understanding
of how economic benefits of utility-scale solar could be leveraged to advance
decarbonization goals while enhancing local economic development.

Second, we advanced long-term planning models of the power system to account for
local economic impacts of and zoning constraints on solar PV deployment. With respect
to zoning ordinances, several studies agree that zoning regulations limit the availability
of land for meeting clean energy targets (Lopez et al., 2021; Mai et al., 2021; Nolon,
2015; Wu et al., 2020). Recent trends in renewable energy zoning show a rise in
restrictive ordinances for utility-scale PV originating from ordinances silent on solar and
total bans on deployment (Aidun et al., 2023). Few studies have been conducted to
quantify the specific impacts of local zoning ordinances, particularly those silent on
solar, on the cost of power system decarbonization. A study by Lopez et al. (Lopez et
al., 2021) highlights that setback requirements represent a significant obstacle to wind
deployment, leading to a 20% reduction in potential deployment across the United
States. Another study (Mai et al., 2021) demonstrates that strict land-use regulations
can impede transmission access, limit deployable capacity, and increase the cost of
electricity generated by wind systems, especially in high electrification scenarios. Lopez
et al. (Lopez et al., 2023) further emphasize the potential of zoning ordinances to
reduce solar resources by up to 38%. However, these studies survey only a limited
number of zoning ordinances at the county level and then replicate their solar provisions
across multiple jurisdictions. For instance, Lopez et al.(Lopez et al., 2022) survey 315
counties on a national level, whereas the Great Lakes region alone includes over 2,474
unique zoning jurisdictions spanning both county and township levels 29. Additionally,
these studies focus on how zoning ordinances affect the availability of land for solar
development rather than directly assessing their impact on deployment decisions

 Page 5 of 34

(Department of Energy, 2021; Lopez et al., 2021, 2023; Macknick et al., 2013; Mai et
al., 2021).

With respect to local economic impacts, the current literature fails to integrate economic
impact assessments directly into power system planning or capacity expansion models
to guide optimal utility-scale siting. Capacity expansion models typically identify the
least-cost mix of generation, storage, and transmission resources necessary to meet
electricity demand reliably under various policy constraints. These models have
improved spatial resolution to capture resource and technology cost variability, but they
generally do not incorporate local economic impacts as endogenous optimization
components. Instead, these models rely on a scenario based approach where power
system plans are developed first, and only then are separate economic impact
evaluations performed. This post-hoc process fails to identify siting configurations that
might optimize both technical and economic objectives simultaneously. Given the
growing opposition to solar siting, which has led to project delays and increased cost of
decarbonization, several studies agree that large-scale deployment of utility-scale solar
will require community support to ensure successful implementation and operation.
Developing siting strategies that benefit rural communities is essential for fostering local
acceptance and meeting both solar deployment and broader decarbonization goals.

Area 3: Tested mechanisms for disseminating knowledge on how utility-scale PV
will affect rural economies through experiments

The localized impacts of utility-scale solar projects are a crucial factor underlying
individual preferences and community acceptance. One key attribute addressed in
previous studies is the choice of project siting. Gaur et al. (2023) utilized a choice
experiment survey in Rhode Island to estimate the trade-offs people are willing to make
for a set of siting attributes, they found that residents were willing to pay $13–$49 in
monthly utility bills to avoid locating such projects on agricultural and forestry
landscapes. Moreover, studies by Larson and Krannich (2016) in Utah and Carlisle et
al. (2016) across six southern Californian counties observed reduced support among
residents for projects situated in proximity to residential areas, wildlife migration routes,
or breeding grounds. Nilson and Stedman (2022) suggested that support for large-scale
solar development in New York was associated with potential economic opportunities,
especially considering the hardships facing the agricultural communities in their study.
Similarly, Pascaris et al. (2022) found that survey respondents in Lubbock County of
Texas and Houghton County of Michigan favored projects offering economic benefits to
farmers and the local community. Crawford et al. (2022) found out the most common
concern in Michigan being the potential devaluation of residential property. Various
perceived risks for electricity supply, including intermittency, aesthetics, negative
impacts on rural and tribal culture, and community energy sovereignty, as well as risks
to wildlife, productive farmland, biodiversity, and human health, were also found to be
important factors (Roddis et al., 2020; Mulvaney, 2017; Nilson and Stedman, 2022;
Boudet, 2019). However, studies concerning the formal assessment of the trade-offs
between localized agricultural land-use impacts of utility-scale solar projects and their

 Page 6 of 34

associated economic and environmental implications were still limited in the existing
literature.

A large literature has investigated the role of different factors influencing individuals’
preferences for solar energy systems, including message-framing strategies in
advertisements (Wolske et al., 2018), financial incentives (Crago and Chernyakhovskiy,
2017), dissemination of solar energy information (Palm and Lantz, 2020), and citizens’
solar norms and environmental concerns (Kalkbrenner and Roosen, 2016; Wolske et
al., 2017). This stream of studies has primarily focused on residential solar energy.
Furthermore, a handful of qualitative studies based on interviews have outlined the
challenges hindering solar energy adoption at both residential and utility scales. These
challenges include information gaps regarding the advantages and benefits of solar
power, lack of government policy support, high upfront investment costs, land
availability, market barriers, and utilities’ ability to manage intermittent renewables
(Faiers and Neame, 2006; Jager, 2006; Rai and Sigrin, 2013; Burke et al., 2019; Frate
and Brannstrom, 2017; Schelly et al., 2021). Understanding how underlying factors and
the dissemination of information influence preferences for utility-scale solar projects will
provide valuable insights into their acceptance—or lack thereof—within local
communities.

The objective of this study is to quantify how information dissemination will affect how
individuals and communities weigh the trade-offs of agricultural land use and other
factors associated with utility-scale solar systems, such as environmental outcomes and
economic consequences, and explain how agricultural land is valued relative to other
attributes. As land use for solar projects becomes a controversial topic, there are
various information sources that emphasize different effects, some positive and some
less favorable. The literature has shown that negative, positive, and peer information
can influence preferences differently (Allcott and Knittel, 2019; Sias et al., 2023; Noll et
al., 2014). Therefore, we aim to study how preferences for solar projects are affected by
different information “packaging”, i.e., how information is framed, to what extent the
effects of information provision lasts, and information feedback might affect perceptions
and attitudes.

6. Project Objectives:

Our overall project objective was to enhance the knowledge available to key
stakeholders involved in zoning decisions, enabling communities to make informed
zoning decisions across our study region of Minnesota, Wisconsin, Illinois, Indiana,
Ohio, and Michigan. Solar zoning rules may reduce utility-scale soft costs and lower
barriers to solar adoption. By providing greater information on zoning decisions, our
project potentially enabled the simultaneous advancement of clean energy development
and community agency in the siting of utility-scale solar. To achieve our overall
objective, our project carried out several sets of activities. Leveraging the results of our
activities, we disseminated knowledge on rural economic impacts of utility-scale PV
through trusted stakeholders and empirically tested means in August 2025. Each set of
activities is discussed in turn below.

 Page 7 of 34

First, we collected data on knowledge flows, economic impacts, and trusted
stakeholders from communities across our study region. This set of activities
includes Tasks 2, 4, 8, and 10 and Milestones 1.2.1, 1.2.2, 2.8.1, 2.8.2, 1.4.1, 1.4.2,
2.10.1, GNG-1C, and GNG-1D from our Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO). Our
work in this area included data collection and interviews with a variety of community
members and leaders.
Tasks 2 and 8 and Milestones 1.2.1, 1.2.2, 2.8.1, and 2.8.2 pertain to knowledge flow
and trusted stakeholder interviews. We generated knowledge regarding (1) how
communities decided to zone solar energy, (2) what key information they needed (if
any), and (3) who gave it to them. We conducted 45 semi-structured interviews with
solar energy stakeholders spanning local officials, land owners, solar developers,
regulators, utilities, media, nonprofits, and citizens across our six state region. This
research addresses a critical gap by investigating how information about large, utility-
scale solar projects flows through rural communities. This is an understudied but vital
factor in project outcomes. Most prior work focuses narrowly on public opinion or
community acceptance, but our study integrates diverse stakeholder perspectives to
understand the networks of communication and learning, as well as the social
infrastructure of energy decision making. In this way, this work advances the knowledge
of how trust, timing, and messenger credibility shapes local-level decisions on large
solar projects.
Tasks 4 and 10 and Milestones 1.4.1, 1.4.2, 2.10.1, and GNGs 1C and 1D pertain to
economic impact interviews. We collected empirical data on local economic impacts of
solar PV projects in our study region. We also recruited local officials and landowners in
communities in the study region that currently host solar facilities to capture quantitative
and qualitative data about solar development’s economic impacts through 28 semi-
structured interviews. With this data and interviews, we aimed to answer two questions
previously unstudied. First, how are landowners using the rents they receive? Second,
how have community-wide tax or other revenues received from solar projects impacted
local services or tax burdens?

Second, we developed computational models for estimating the economic
impacts of utility-scale PV deployments in rural economies, and for analyzing
utility-scale solar deployment into the future across our study region given
economic impacts and zoning constraints. This set of activities includes Tasks 3, 5,
6, 9, 11, 12, 13, 16, and 17 and Milestones 2.12.1, 2.12.2, 2.9.1, 2.9.3, 2.11.1, 2.9.2,
3.17.1, 3.16.1, and 3.17.2 and GNGs 1A, 1B, 2A, and 2B from our SOPO.
Tasks 3 and 9 and Milestones 2.9.1, 2.9.3, and GNGs 1A and 1B pertain to collecting
and analyzing zoning ordinances across our study region. Specifically, we identified
which localities in our study region had zoning ordinances for utility-scale solar, then
collected and analyzed these ordinances. Analysis of ordinances included coding them
as permissive versus exclusionary and extracting key zoning information, e.g. setback
distances. We also aimed to publish an online public database of solar zoning
ordinances across our study region on the initial public website. The database served
as a unique resource for solar developers in siting and help zoning officials benchmark

 Page 8 of 34

their zoning ordinances against those of comparable communities. Over 90% of
ordinances were collected, read, and analyzed in each of our states, for a regional total
of 93% of ordinances. Our zoning database is the first database to collect nearly all
ordinances in a multi-state region.
Tasks 6, 12, and 16 and Milestones 2.12.1, 2.12.2, and GNG 2A pertain to economic
modelling. Specifically, we created a publicly available, referenced, and user-friendly
spreadsheet-based model to quantify the local economic impacts of utility-scale solar
deployment. To create this spreadsheet, we first established parameter profiles for a
decision support model to estimate the economic impacts of utility-scale solar PV
installation and operations on rural communities, then integrated these profiles and
economic data collected from interviews into a spreadsheet model that we built.
Parameter profiles represent an array of potential installation environments and scale,
and include cost and revenue profiles. Costs include lost productivity of agricultural land
that is converted to utility-scale solar, a unique contribution of our work. Our
spreadsheet, simulated-based model describes the expected changes in public
revenues and expenditures caused by PV development in individual communities and,
when scaled, to our study region. Our model improves on the state of the art by
including photovoltaic solar, accounting for alternative land-uses, containing specific
data on rural communities, and estimating new economic impacts, such as via non-
pecuniary and wealth direct effects and public revenues. By making our model publicly
available on our central website, our model will help communities considering solar
zoning ordinances weigh local economic impacts in their decisions.
Tasks 5, 11, 13, and 17 and Milestones 2.11.1, 2.9.2, 3.17.1, 3.16.1, 3.17.2, and GNG
2B pertain to power system modelling. Our power system modeling integrated our
unique zoning database and economic impact models (described above) into a long-
term planning (or capacity expansion) model. In so doing, we advanced capacity
expansion modelling. Prior work analyzed how zoning ordinances affected the
availability of land for solar development rather than directly assessing their impact on
deployment decisions, a gap our work addressed. And, with respect to economic
modelling, prior work had not integrated (or endogenized) economic impact
assessments directly into capacity expansion models, a gap we filled to guide optimal
utility-scale siting that balances reducing system costs with maximizing local economic
benefits. Our work therefore generated new insights into the effects of zoning
ordinances on solar growth; how solar deployment can be modified to maximize
economic benefits to host communities; and how long-term planning of power systems
can capture these important community implications.

Third, we experimentally tested mechanisms for disseminating knowledge on
how utility-scale PV will affect rural economies. This set of activities includes Tasks
14 and 18 and Milestones 2.14.1, 2.14.2, 3.18.1, GNG 2C, and EOP 3D. Specifically,
we assessed how to effectively disseminate knowledge generated from this project to
inform solar zoning decisions by communities. How solar knowledge affects zoning
decisions will depend on how it is delivered and how it is interpreted. We examined both
aspects (i.e., its delivery and interpretation) through survey experiments. The

 Page 9 of 34

experiments first identified dissemination mechanisms to be assessed. Then, target
populations for evaluating dissemination mechanisms were identified.

Our final objective is to disseminate our results to stakeholders, including
individuals responsible for zoning decisions, in order to provide them information
that will help them design and pass zoning ordinances. These activities span Task
19 and EOPs 3A, 3B, and 3C. Our dissemination took various forms. First, we created a
website to host materials generated throughout project, including our zoning database,
factsheets, and publications. We also will deliver a webinar summarizing our project
results, which will be distributed with the help of our advisory board, connections, and
interviewees. Finally, we will issue a brief survey to zoning officials across our study
region to gauge their likelihood to include solar in their zoning ordinances given the
information provided by our project.
Land use for solar projects has become a controversial topic, frequently making
headlines in news outlets and other information sources, which might highlight either
positive or negative effects. Positive coverage often emphasizes the ability to repurpose
marginal or degraded lands unsuitable for agriculture, whereas negative reporting has
highlighted the large land requirements displacing prime farmland. While the economics
literature has shown that negative, positive, and peer information can influence
preferences differently, less is known about how such information affects individual
preferences and trade-offs between land use and other attributes specific to utility-scale
solar development. So, in our research for the effects of knowledge dissemination
mechanisms, we focus on how knowledge will be “packaged” in the dissemination
process.
In preliminary feasibility exploration, we conducted analysis through in-person
interactions but found that there was very little interest in our study. We also conducted
information dissemination and data collection through traditional mail; we had low
participation interest and the limited data we received showed results similar to those of
the online format that we eventually decided to adopt.
Specifically, we tested the impacts of peer, positive, and negative information on
individuals’ preferences toward utility-scale solar projects through an information
treatment experiment in the questionnaire. Our online survey was deployed through the
Qualtrics and was administrated via Dynata in July 2024. We distributed 11,685 survey
invitations and received 3102 completed responses, implying a 26.5% response rate.
We then conducted a follow-up survey in July 2025 by distributing questionnaire to 100
initial participants who explicitly agreed to be contacted for follow-up studies. In this
follow-up survey, we further examined how providing feedback information and the
format of feedback information affect participants preferences.
The information treatments were designed with an emphasis on different categories of
information: the control group received no information, one category focused on peer
information related to peers’ adoption of regulations for utility-scale solar projects, and
the remaining categories offered information highlighting either positive or negative
effects of deploying utility-scale solar projects within local communities. Participants
were randomly assigned to one of the four versions of the questionnaire, each

                                        Page 10 of 34

emphasizing a specific aspect of information. To ensure respondents carefully read the
provided information, we included validation questions immediately after the information
content. Participants were informed that the answers to these validation questions could
be found in the preceding text and that they would not receive compensation if they
failed to answer them correctly in three attempts.
Our follow-up experiment survey research examines information persistence and
preference updating within a policy-relevant context of utility-scale solar deployment.
Using a two-round survey design with embedded information treatments and discrete
choice experiments (DCEs), we examine both the lasting effects of previously provided
information and how preference updating occurs when individualized or aggregate
feedback based on the first wave is provided in a subsequent wave. Understanding
these temporal dynamics of preference formation is critical for meaningful renewable
energy policy development. As of the writing of this report, we have distributed follow-up
survey experiments to 100 participants and expect to receive about 200 completed
follow-up responses once we complete data collection in the next 2 weeks.

7. Project Results and Discussion:
In this section, we present and discuss our project results, divided into the four activity
areas used in the prior section. In each section, we include a table that provides details
on our completion of related milestones. For the sake of brevity, we have omitted
interim milestones and instead included final milestones when multiple milestones
pertain to the same work, e.g. collection and analysis of zoning ordinances.
Area 1: Collected data on zoning ordinances, knowledge flows, economic
impacts, and trusted stakeholders from communities across our study region.
We found that local government officials often serve as central actors in the network of
knowledge flows about new utility-scale solar projects, functioning as central conduits for
both receiving information about solar projects as well as disseminating information to
their constituents. They routinely forward citizen questions to developers, help coordinate
public meetings, and share zoning and permitting information. Developers typically initiate
the earliest engagement, usually through targeted outreach to landowners and permitting
authorities, as well as communication to these local officials. However, the lack of broader
public involvement at these early stages profoundly shapes community perceptions and
trust.

Our interviews revealed that developers often delay broader public engagement until a
project is more fully scoped, which can undermine trust as citizens report feeling caught
off guard. Instead, they turn to trusted neighbors, local officials, social media, local media,
and other personal networks as key sources of solar project knowledge. In this manner,
misinformation can spread quickly. In many cases, ‘anchor’ landowners or prominent local
officials acted as knowledge brokers in communities, influencing how a project was
perceived and whether strong opposition mobilized. Across stakeholder groups,
interviewees expressed a desire for earlier, more detailed, and more transparent
communication about utility-scale solar.

                                         Page 11 of 34

Overall, this work not only maps the complex network of actors and communication
methods involved in utility-scale solar development, but it also offers guidance to
developers and policymakers seeking to improve outreach strategies and reduce friction
for future projects. These insights point to a need for more deliberate collaboration
between developers and trusted community actors in shaping knowledge flows,
especially in rural areas where communication pathways can be limited or distrusted.

Area 2: Developed computational models for estimating the economic impacts of
utility-scale PV deployments in rural economies, and for analyzing utility-scale
solar deployment into the future across our study region given economic impacts
and zoning constraints.

Our results from this area of work are detailed in two publications in review, Owusu-
Obeng et al. (2025a) and Owusu-Obeng et al. (2025b).

Owusu-Obeng et al. (2025a) find that zoning ordinances significantly constrain utility-
scale PV capacity, with varying impacts across states. Ordinances that are silent on solar
reduce regional PV capacity by 31% (940 GW), and by up to 59% (177 GW) in some
states. These silent ordinances effectively ban solar by omission. Explicit bans, either
outright or by prohibiting solar on agricultural lands, account for an additional 7%
reduction regionally, and up to 14% in certain states. Setbacks from non-participating
property lines and roads further reduce capacity by up to 6% each. When combined with
lot size requirements, total reductions from setbacks reach 16%, much smaller than the
impact of silent ordinances alone. This contrasts with prior studies that emphasize
setbacks as the primary constraint. Overall, zoning regulations, including silent
ordinances, cut total utility-scale PV potential by 54% (1.6 TW) across the region.

Owusu-Obeng et al. (2025a) further integrate the zoning-constrained PV capacity into a
capacity expansion model to assess impacts on PV investments under an 80% carbon
emissions reduction target by 2040. Three scenarios are evaluated: “ignoring zoning”,
applying existing zoning (“current zoning”), and updating silent ordinances to explicitly
allow or ban solar (expanded zoning). Under the current zoning scenario, installed utility-
scale solar capacity falls by 18% (8 GW) compared to the no-zoning case, mainly because
silent ordinances block access to cost-competitive sites with higher capacity factors. This
shortfall shifts investments to natural gas combined cycle and energy storage, which
increase by 10% (3 GW) and 8% (900 MW), respectively. However, when silent
ordinances are clarified, solar capacity nearly recovers, resulting in only a 3% reduction
relative to the no-zoning scenario. At the state level, silent ordinances drive the steepest
reductions in Michigan and Wisconsin (39–42%) and more modest declines in Illinois and
Ohio (3–16%), shifting solar investments to states like Indiana and Minnesota where silent
zoning ordinances are less prevalent.

Under Owusu-Obeng et al. (2025b), the authors analyze the key drivers of local economic
benefits, measured as value-added to the local economy, using the economic impact
model. They find that the size of the local economy is the primary driver of net impacts.
Large counties (population above 50,000), such as those in Ohio, experience up to 30%
higher economic benefits compared to small counties (population under 30,000). This is
partly attributable to a greater retention of local spending due to the presence of more

                                       Page 14 of 34

businesses and services that support solar project development. In Ohio, high property
tax revenues, driven by the state’s Payment In Lieu of Taxes (PILOT) program, further
enhance these benefits. The authors also find that incorporating the loss of agricultural
productivity from farmland conversion reduces estimated economic benefits by up to
16%. However, current economic impact models often do not explicitly account for lost
agricultural revenue.

Additionally, Owusu-Obeng et al. (2025b) integrate local economic impacts alongside
generator cost parameters into a multi-objective capacity expansion framework that
simultaneously minimizes generation and transmission investment costs while
maximizing local economic benefits at the county level. The authors find that prioritizing
community benefits within this framework shifts solar deployment toward counties with
larger economies and below-average farmland productivity. Solar investments in large
counties increase by 40% (16 GW), while investments in small and medium counties
decline. Similarly, solar deployment in counties with below-average farmland productivity
rises by 11% (8 GW), while it declines in others. A scenario prioritizing local benefits
increases cumulative economic benefits by $1 billion (11%) by 2040, with only a marginal
0.5% increase in system-wide costs. However, this reallocation redirects investments
away from Michigan and Wisconsin (down 39%) toward Ohio and Indiana (up 75%).

Area 3: Tested mechanisms for disseminating knowledge on how utility-scale PV
will affect rural economies through experiments
The type of vignette presented significantly affects individual preferences, even with
minimal exposure to peer, positive, or negative information. There are significant
disparities in how stakeholders – public officials vs. the general population, and
landowners vs. non-landowners – value different project attributes. There is preference
heterogeneity across states, particularly in the valuation of high carbon reduction, lease
payments to landowners, and the use of high-quality farmland for solar projects. Urban
and rural communities share similar preferences for various factors of utility-scale solar
projects. The estimated effects are almost the same or only differ slightly. These
heterogeneous preferences are reflected in individual’s and then communities’ land use
planning and solar zoning, such as setbacks and decommissioning bonds.
Overall, our results indicate that over three quarters of participants (77%) showed at
least moderate support for having a zoning ordinance regarding utility-scale solar
systems. The most important challenge associated with utility-scale solar projects in
local communities are high initial investment costs (for 31% of participants), land use
concerns (27%) and negative environmental outcomes (17%). Moreover, we find
preference heterogeneity across states, particularly in the valuation of high carbon
reduction, lease payments to landowners, and the use of high-quality farmland for solar
projects. For example, concerns over farmland use vary significantly, with respondents
in Illinois and Minnesota placing greater value on preserving high-quality farmland than
other states. Furthermore, while urban and rural communities share similar preferences
for various attributes of utility-scale solar projects, urban respondents place higher value
on carbon reduction, whereas rural respondents value more on lease payment to
landowners and occupied farmland quality.
Our results also suggest that the type of information presented significantly affects
individual preferences: respondents receiving positive information place higher values
                                         Page 19 of 34

on carbon benefits, while those exposed to peer or negative information require greater
compensation for projects occupying high-quality farmland. Based on a random utility
maximization framework and random parameter logit model, we find significant
heterogeneity in how stakeholders value different solar project attributes. Landowners
show greater concern for farmland quality than nonlandowners, requiring $27.41 in
monthly energy savings to accept projects on high-quality farmland relative to low-
quality farmland, whereas the general population prioritizes carbon emission reductions
and is willing to pay $5.47 and $7.16, respectively in monthly energy costs if the project
will bring medium and high carbon emission reductions relative to the low-level.
Additionally, local public officials’ willingness to pay for different solar attributes is not
statistically significant.
After implementing the first-round survey via the online Qualtrics platform in July-August
2024, we conducted the follow-up survey replicates the survey experiments but
randomizes respondents into three experimental groups: (1) a control group with no
feedback, (2) a partial feedback group shown their first-round choices, and (3) a full
feedback group shown both their prior choices and the corresponding willingness to pay
(WTP) estimates for each attribute. After the follow-up choices, respondents are asked
to self-assess the accuracy of the WTP estimates derived from their first-round choices
(e.g., whether respondents perceived their inferred WTP as "too high," "too low," or
"accurate"). Respondents’ uncertainty levels concerning their choices are incorporated
in both rounds of choices after each choice task. Based on the Round 2 choices of 31
participants, we did not find significantly more participants changed choices in the
control group and the partial feedback group, in comparison with the group who
received full feedback.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:

Area 1: Collected data on zoning ordinances, knowledge flows, economic
impacts, and trusted stakeholders from communities across our study region.

A key accomplishment of this work is the identification of local officials as the central,
trusted intermediaries in the knowledge flow ecosystem regarding utility-scale solar
development. This is noteworthy because it highlights a sometimes overlooked group as
fundamentally pivotal to both the dissemination and social acceptance of solar project
information. The study also uncovered a critical timing gap in how developers tend to
focus their early outreach efforts on landowners and permitting bodies, which can lead
to citizen perceptions of secrecy or exclusion. This sometimes results in heightened
skepticism and increased reliance on inaccurate or informal sources. These findings
suggest that earlier, more inclusive communication, perhaps facilitated through trusted
local actors, could improve community buy-in and reduce friction. While vocal opposition
groups pose challenges, we found that they were often small in number, and that the
broader community may be more open to utility-scale solar development when
information is shared in a transparent and credible manner. Taken together, these
findings offer actionable insights into how the flow, structure, and timing of knowledge
shapes the energy transition.

Area 2: Developed computational models for estimating the economic impacts of
utility-scale PV deployments in rural economies, and for analyzing utility-scale
solar deployment into the future across our study region given economic impacts
and zoning constraints.

Significant accomplishments include the collection, analysis, and publication of
thousands of zoning ordinances on our website, energyzoning.org, providing a uniquely
rich database of solar ordinances. We also generated unique insights into the effect of
zoning ordinances on the future expansion of utility-scale solar in our study region.
Specifically, we found “silent” zoning ordinances that implicitly block solar development
by failing to address its land-use play a large role in driving utility-scale PV deployment.

                                        Page 23 of 34

We found a 30% reduction in available utility-scale PV capacity (940 GW) across the
region due to zoning ordinances, with reductions reaching up to 59% (177 GW) at the
state level. This reduction results in an 18% decline in utility-scale PV investment
capacity (by 8 GW) across our study region, and stronger impacts at the state level.
Thus, we overall find that rather than explicitly restrictive ordinances, much more
common and detrimental to solar deployment are zoning ordinances that are silent on
solar development, which create a regulatory gray area, leading to unintentional barriers
to utility-scale PV deployment.

Significant accomplishments also include the first-of-a-kind endogenization of local
economic impacts of solar into a capacity expansion model. With this model, we
generated unique insights into trade-offs between solar deployment that reduces
system costs versus maximizes local economic benefits. Our analysis reveals that
relocating solar investments towards counties offering higher net economic benefits
does not increase the total cost of decarbonization. Across our region, prioritizing local
solar economic benefits increases aggregate economic impact by up to 11% (or $1
billion) while total decarbonization costs increases by less than 0.5%. This finding
challenges the conventional assumption that maximizing local economic benefits from
renewable energy projects necessitates significantly higher overall system costs.
Implicit in this finding is the critical importance that we document of incorporating the
opportunity cost of agricultural land into local economic assessments of solar
development. Local governments should recognize that these costs can offset the
perceived benefits, particularly in counties where agriculture contributes significantly to
local revenues.

Area 3: Tested mechanisms for disseminating knowledge on how utility-scale PV
will affect rural economies through experiments

Our work indicates the type of vignette presented significantly affects individual
preferences, even with minimal exposure to peer, positive, or negative information.
There are significant disparities in how stakeholders – public officials vs. the general
population, and landowners vs. non-landowners – value different project attributes.
There is preference heterogeneity across states, particularly in the valuation of high
carbon reduction, lease payments to landowners, and the use of high-quality farmland
for solar projects. Urban and rural communities share similar preferences for various
factors of utility-scale solar projects. The estimated effects are almost the same or only
differ slightly. These heterogeneous preferences are reflected in individual’s and then

9. Path Forward:

While we have completed nearly all milestones and tasks, some remaining activities
remain. We will continue work on those unrealized milestones until their completion.
Otherwise, several paths forward exist to extend our work. First, our research was
focused on solar development, but an integrated understanding of wind and solar is
                                        Page 24 of 34

essential to better understand renewables futures. This is particularly relevant to our
work on zoning and economic impacts. We hope to repeat our work above for wind
power, and use that to generate an integrated understanding of how deployment of wind
and solar can be shaped by and shape each other given zoning ordinances and local
economic impacts. We also note the importance of distinguishing between various
zoning approval mechanisms, such as by-right use, special exceptions, and conditional
uses – a differentiation that we did not have scope for. Future work should examine how
different levels of zoning permissions affect utility-scale solar (and wind) development to
provide a more comprehensive understanding of the regulatory landscape and its
impact on project deployment. Future work could also conduct a comparative analysis
across other U.S. regions, which would enable comparison between a larger set of
regional regulatory and socio-political environments on utility-scale solar development
and acceptance. Long-term tracking of communities and projects would also shed light
on manifested economic benefits and long-term evolution in community acceptance.
