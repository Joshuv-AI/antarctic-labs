 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Trip chaining, defined as the sequential linking of trips by individuals throughout a given day, provides
critical insights into daily mobility patterns and activity sequencing. Understanding these patterns has
significant implications for transportation demand forecasting, congestion management, and local
economic activity. This analysis examines trip chaining behaviors in New York State (NYS) for the years
2009 and 2017 and compares the Middle Atlantic Census Division with other U.S. regions in 2022,
utilizing data from the National Household Travel Survey (NHTS). Through demographic, geographic,
and temporal analysis, this study characterizes how populations organize travel for work, personal
errands, and social activities, providing empirical evidence of evolving trip chaining behaviors to inform
transportation planning strategies.

Workers employed in manufacturing, construction, maintenance, and farming demonstrate significantly
higher rates of direct home-work commuting patterns without intermediate trip stops. Analysis of both
2009 and 2017 data (Figure ES.1) reveals that direct return-to-home travel after work represents the
predominant pattern across all occupational categories. This pattern is particularly pronounced among
manufacturing, construction, maintenance, and farming workers (77% in 2009 and 79% in 2017 in NYS),
rates that are statistically significantly higher than those observed for clerical or professional workers
(63% in 2009 and 62% in 2017). These findings suggest reduced schedule flexibility among blue-collar
occupational categories. Comparable direct travel patterns are observed among workers unable to
telecommute (Figure ES.2) and those with dependent children, although the latter demographic exhibits
marginally elevated rates of pickup and drop-off trip activities (Figure ES.3).

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 BACKGROUND

Trip chaining, defined as the sequential linking of multiple trips within individual travel patterns,
provides critical insights into daily mobility behaviors and activity organization. This study examines
individual trip chains using data from the National Household Travel Survey (NHTS), where each chain
represents a sequence of spatially and temporally connected trip records. Trip chaining behavior reflects
the systematic organization of activities across geographic and temporal dimensions, particularly the
sequential ordering of destinations throughout daily activity patterns. Analysis of post-work trip
destinations, for instance, reveals prevalent behavioral routines and underlying mobility requirements.
These travel patterns have significant implications for transportation demand forecasting, traffic
congestion management, and commercial location planning, as they directly influence the spatial and
temporal distribution of travel flows and economic activity.

Most of the literature has focused on individual trip characteristics (Pan et al. 2025; 2024). However,
research on multi-destination journey patterns and trip-chaining methodologies has been established since
the 1980s (Thill and Thomas 1987). Subsequent investigations have examined trip chaining behaviors
across demographic segments (McGuckin and Murakami 1999), the influence of temporal and
environmental factors (Vande Walle and Steenberghen 2006; Yang et al. 2016), and interactions with
mode choice decisions (Hensher and Reyes 2000). The proliferation of comprehensive travel diary
datasets, particularly through large-scale surveys such as the NHTS, has enabled researchers to develop
more nuanced understanding of population-specific trip chaining adoption patterns and their underlying
determinants.

This analysis examines trip chaining behaviors in New York State (NYS) utilizing 2009 and 2017 NHTS
datasets. Additionally, 2022 NHTS data enables comparative analysis between the Middle Atlantic
Census Division and the rest of the United States. Through temporal comparative analysis, this study
characterizes evolving trip chaining patterns across demographic and geographic segments. The findings
provide empirical support for transportation planning applications and contextualize the heterogeneity of
travel requirements across population subgroups and temporal periods. While this research does not
evaluate specific policy interventions or propose design modifications, it presents a comprehensive
empirical characterization of intrastate trip chaining patterns and their evolution over time.

1.2 DATASET

This study used data from the NHTS, a recurring survey sponsored by the Federal Highway
Administration (FHWA) that is widely recognized as a key source for understanding travel behavior in
the United States (Federal Highway Administration 2017). The NHTS collects detailed information on
individuals’ daily travel, linked to personal, household, and vehicle characteristics, and includes variables
such as trip frequency, distance, duration, mode, and purpose.

The 2009 NHTS included 151,147 households nationwide, with 25,510 from the core national sample and
124,637 from add-on participants. Among these, 14,102 households were from NYS, which—combined
with the national sample—produced 16,165 usable NYS households. The 2017 NHTS surveyed 129,696
households nationwide, representing more than 264,000 individuals, and NYS’s add-on sample contained
17,209 households.

In 2020, FHWA launched the Next Generation NHTS (NextGen NHTS) to provide a continuous travel
monitoring program with more frequent national and regional data products. Each cycle targets at least
7,500 households to capture emerging trends. The 2022 NHTS collected responses from 7,893

 7

households, 16,997 individuals, and 31,074 trips. Due to the smaller sample size, the 2022 survey is
representative at the national and Census Division levels rather than at the state or local level (Federal
Highway Administration 2023). Nine Census Divisions are available in the data, where New York State
falls within the Middle Atlantic Division.

1.3 TRIP CHAIN DEFINITION

In this report, we define an individual’s trip chain as the sequence of trips undertaken throughout a single
day, where each trip corresponds to a record in the NHTS trip file. The primary focus of this analysis is
on activity transitions (inferred from trip purposes) and transportation mode transitions, with attention to
the following aspects:
 • Diversity of Activities. We quantify the number of distinct activity types (i.e., trip purpose
 categories) each individual engages in over the course of a day. Home is not considered as an
 activity.
 • Pre-Work Transitions. For individuals with a work trip, we investigate the activity after leaving
 home and before arriving at work, as well as the number of intermediate stops or distinct
 destinations visited.
 • Post-Work Transitions. For individuals who made a work trip during the day, we examine the
 next activity following work before returning home. We also analyze how many distinct
 destinations (places) are visited after work and before home.
 • Non-Workday Activity Transitions. For individuals who did not make a work trip on the
 assigned travel (survey) day, we identify the types of activities they engaged in and describe the
 overall structure of their daily trip chain.
 • Transportation Mode Transitions
 o We analyze the mode used after a trip made with a Privately Operated Vehicle (POV),
 excluding cases where the following trip is also via POV.
 o Similarly, we analyze the mode used after a trip made using public transit, excluding
 cases where the following trip also uses public transit.

The activity transition sequence is derived from the trip purpose field in the NHTS dataset (Table 1-1).
The mode of transportation is derived from the trip mode field in the NHTS dataset (Table 1-2). These
categories were used to define transition patterns between activities and to analyze modal transfers
associated with these transitions.

In this report, we focused on key transition points. Specifically, the first trip following or preceding key
activities such as "home" or "work."
 • Pre-Work Trip Chaining Patterns. We analyzed the share of patterns reflecting how
 individuals travel before arriving at work:
 o Home → Work → Home: The individual traveled directly from home to work without
 making any stops.
 o Home → Next activity →…→Work → …: The individual stopped for another activity
 before going to work. The “next activity” refers to the first activity after leaving home.
 • Post-Work Trip Chaining Patterns. We analyzed the share of patterns reflecting how
 individuals travel after leaving work:
 o Home → Work → Home: The individual returned directly home after work.
 o Home → Work → Next activity →… →Home: The individual conducted other activities
 after work. The “next activity” refers to the first activity after leaving work.
 • Non-Workday Activity Patterns. This includes all weekends, holidays, and vacation or off days
 for both employed and unemployed individuals. While it is possible to analyze weekends and
 other non-workdays separately, doing so often limits sample size. Therefore, we group all non-
 workdays together, defined as days when individuals made no work trips. We analyzed:
 o Home → Next activity… → …: The “next activity” refers to the first activity after
 leaving home, helping us understand typical behavior on non-workdays.
 • Transportation Mode Transition. We examined how individuals transition between modes
 during their travel:
 o …→Target mode →Next mode→ …: For a given target mode (e.g., public transport or
 personal vehicle), we identified the next mode used in the sequence when people switch
 to a different mode.

We also analyzed the number of different types of activities people engaged in on a given day. As defined
in Table 1-1, activity types include work, family and personal business, social and recreational activities,
and short stops. We grouped individuals trip pattern into the following categories:
 • No trip. Did not make any trips on the survey day.
 • One type of activity. Made one or more trips, but all for the same type of activity.
 • Two types of activities. Made trips for two distinct types of activities.
 • Three or more types of activities. Made trips for three or more different activity types.

4.1 TRIP CHAINING BY OPTION OF WFH

4.1.1 Pre-Work Trip Chaining

Workers who could WFH were generally less likely to go directly to work than those who could not,
especially in high-density areas (4,000+ people/sq mi), where the gap was statistically significant (63%
vs. 78%) in 2009 (Figure 4-1). WFH-capable workers in these dense areas were also statistically more
likely to make short stops before work (14% vs. 7%) and to engage in social and recreational activities
(9% vs. 3%). In mid-density areas (2,000–4,000), WFH workers had a higher short stop rate (14% vs.
5%) and a slightly lower direct-to-work rate, both statistically significant. In rural and low-density areas,
the differences were smaller and generally not statistically significant.

By 2017, some of these patterns persisted, but the gaps narrowed in rural and mid-density areas. In low-
density urban areas (<2,000), WFH workers were statistically less likely to go directly to work (67% vs.
79%) and more likely to make short stops (16% vs. 6%) or engage in social and recreational activities
(7% vs. 3%). In mid-density areas, WFH workers again had a higher short stop share (13% vs. 7%) and a
slightly lower direct-to-work share (75% vs. 79%), with the short stop difference being statistically
significant. In high-density areas, WFH workers were still somewhat more likely to make short stops
(10% vs. 7%), though this was not statistically significant in 2017.

4.1.2 Post-Work Trip Chaining

Workers who could WFH were consistently less likely to go directly home after work than those who
could not in 2009 (Figure 4-2). This difference was statistically significant in rural areas (51% vs. 69%),

business in all density categories, with the largest gap in rural areas (45% vs. 23%). Social and
recreational stops were also higher among WFH-capable workers in some areas, such as low-density
urban areas (13% vs. 6%), a statistically significant difference.

By 2017, the differences narrowed but still appeared in some contexts. In rural areas, direct-to-home rates
between WFH and non-WFH workers (65% vs. 68%) were not statistically significant, and the gap in
family/personal business stops also diminished (20% vs. 22%). However, in mid-density urban areas

areas still showed lower direct-to-home shares for WFH workers (56% vs. 65%), but this difference was
not statistically significant in 2017.

In both 2009 and 2017, high-density urban areas had the lowest direct-to-home shares and relatively
higher social/recreational participation for both WFH and non-WFH workers. Conversely, rural areas had
the highest direct-to-home shares for non-WFH workers, but in 2009, WFH workers in rural settings had
the most pronounced diversion toward errands.

4.1.3 Transportation Mode Transition

In 2009, rural WFH workers were statistically less likely than non-WFH workers to transition to non-
motorized modes (60% vs. 43%) and more likely to continue by POV (40% vs. 47%), though the POV
difference was not statistically significant (Figure 4-3). In low-density urban areas (<2,000 people/sq mi),
the patterns diverged sharply: WFH workers were statistically more likely to switch to POV (68% vs.
33%) and statistically less likely to walk/bike (30% vs. 65%). This suggests that in less dense settings,
WFH-capable workers using transit were more dependent on cars for onward travel.

In mid-density areas (2,000–4,000 people/sq mi) in 2009, the reverse occurred; WFH workers had a much
lower POV share (9% vs. 29%) and statistically significantly higher nonmotorized share (78% vs. 67%).
In high-density areas (4,000+ people/sq mi), nonmotorized travel dominated for both groups (82% WFH
vs. 77% non-WFH), with no statistically significant difference.

By 2017, many of these differences shifted. In rural areas, WFH workers were now statistically more
likely to use POV after transit (39% vs. 21%) and less likely to walk/bike (58% vs. 60%), reversing the
2009 pattern. In low-density urban areas, differences in POV and nonmotorized shares narrowed and were
not statistically significant.

However, in mid-density areas, a dramatic change occurred. In 2009, WFH workers overwhelmingly
walked or biked after transit, but in 2017, non-WFH workers had a much higher POV share (60% vs.
2%), a statistically significant shift. In high-density areas, nonmotorized travel remained dominant for
both groups, but WFH workers had a slightly higher share (80% vs. 77%) with no significant gap.

5.4.2 Post-Work Trip Chaining

In 2009, post-work travel behavior looked quite similar between households with and without vehicles in

 CONCLUSIONS

This analysis presents a comprehensive empirical examination of trip chaining behaviors utilizing NHTS
datasets, focusing on NYS, NYC, and comparative assessments between the Middle Atlantic Census
Division and the rest of the United States. The findings demonstrate that demographic characteristics,
including occupational category, age, and educational attainment, along with environmental factors,
systematically influence pre-work and post-work trip chaining patterns, non-work day activity sequences,
and mode transition decisions. Across all temporal periods examined, direct home→work→home travel
emerged as the predominant pattern, particularly pronounced among workers in occupations characterized
by limited schedule flexibility, including manufacturing, construction, maintenance, and farming, as well
as among households with dependent children. These results suggest that occupational constraints and
household responsibilities serve as primary determinants of simplified trip chaining behaviors.

Workers residing in high-density urban environments, particularly within NYC, demonstrated
significantly elevated rates of post-work activity engagement. This pattern aligns with established
accessibility and urban form theoretical frameworks, which demonstrate that increased density and
enhanced service proximity facilitate trip chaining behaviors (Cheng et al. 2016). In such urban contexts,
opportunities for commercial, retail, and recreational activities exhibit greater spatial concentration,
thereby reducing both travel impedance and the opportunity costs associated with intermediate stops.
These findings illustrate how built environment characteristics influence not only aggregate trip
generation rates but also the temporal sequencing and spatial organization of daily activities.
Additionally, zero-vehicle households in NYC exhibited higher propensities for post-work activity
integration, demonstrating efficient activity bundling strategies while minimizing home-based trip
origins. This observation corroborates earlier research on multipurpose urban travel patterns, which
identified that substantial proportions of household travel occur in conjunction with work-related
commuting (Hanson 1980). Conversely, zero-vehicle households in non-NYC areas of NYS showed
reduced likelihood of extending travel beyond basic commuting functions. This finding reinforces spatial
accessibility constraints documented in automobile ownership and social exclusion literature, where
limited public transit availability and reduced walkable destination density outside major metropolitan
areas constrains post-work activity participation (Gray et al. 2006).

Gender-based differences in post-work travel patterns were pronounced within NYC. Female workers
exhibited significantly higher rates of family- and personal-business-related intermediate stops following
work activities, while male workers demonstrated modest increases in leisure-oriented travel between
2009 and 2017. These empirical findings align with established literature documenting that women
engage in complex, multipurpose trip chains characterized by constrained temporal and modal resources,
whereas men demonstrate greater propensity for single-purpose travel utilizing superior transportation
modes and incurring higher associated costs (Turner and Grieco 2000). Such behavioral patterns reflect
the phenomenon of "time poverty," wherein competing household and professional obligations limit
individuals' capacity to optimize daily activity-travel patterns or extend their spatial range of activities.

Analysis of 2022 NHTS data reveals a pronounced nationwide shift toward simplified commuting
patterns, characterized by increased rates of direct home-to-work and work-to-home travel without
intermediate trip segments. While these findings suggest convergence in commuting behaviors across
demographic groups, interpretation requires methodological caution due to the reduced 2022 NHTS
sample size and the diminished proportion of respondents reporting work-related trips during their
designated survey period. These sampling limitations may introduce selection bias that could influence
the observed behavioral patterns.

Future research should expand this analytical framework through integration of comprehensive
behavioral, spatial, and temporal datasets with enhanced granularity. Examining the relationship between

 90

trip chaining behaviors and emerging mobility paradigms, including telecommuting adoption, e-
commerce penetration, micromobility integration, and innovative transit service deployment, could
elucidate how contemporary travel patterns respond to technological and societal transformations.
Enhanced spatial analysis at finer geographic scales, coupled with causal inference methodologies, would
facilitate clearer identification of mechanisms through which built environment characteristics and
accessibility measures influence trip consolidation versus direct travel decisions. Furthermore, integrating
travel behavior analysis with public health and environmental impact frameworks would yield critical
insights for transportation planners and policymakers developing sustainable mobility systems that
accommodate diverse population needs while simultaneously reducing traffic congestion. Such
interdisciplinary approaches would advance both theoretical understanding of mobility behavior and
practical applications for evidence-based transportation policy development.
