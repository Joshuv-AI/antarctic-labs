Mediating effect of health anxiety in patients with inflammatory bowel disease between disease perception and self-management behavior
Authors: Ronghua Wang, Yong Wang, Xiaojin Zhou, Jin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to examine the mediating role of health anxiety in the relationship between illness perception and self-management behavior among individuals with inflammatory bowel disease(IBD). A total of 326 patients with IBD who were treated at our hospital between June 2023, and June 2025 were enrolled. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ), the Short Health Anxiety Inventory (SHAI), and the IBD Self-Management Behavior Scale. Among the 326 participants, the mean BIPQ, SHAI, and self-management scores were 47.31 (SD = 12.71), 28.89 (SD = 8.81), and 67.79 (SD = 20.48), respectively. Self-management scores were significantly lower among patients older than 60 years and those with a disease duration of more than five years, whereas higher scores were observed among patients with a monthly income greater than 5,000 yuan (all P < 0.05). BIPQ scores were negatively correlated with self-management behavior (r = -0.660) and positively correlated with SHAI scores (r = 0.417). SHAI scores were negatively correlated with self-management behavior (r = -0.501). Mediation analysis showed that the BIPQ scores had both a direct negative association with self-management behavior (coefficient = -1.180, accounting for 82.69% of the total effect) and an indirect association mediated through health anxiety (coefficient = -0.246, accounting for 17.31% of the total effect). The 95% bias-corrected bootstrap confidence interval for the indirect effect did not include zero. Patients with IBD in this cohort exhibited predominantly negative illness perceptions, elevated health anxiety, and suboptimal self-management behaviors. The findings suggest that illness perception is associated with self-management behavior both directly and indirectly through the mediating role of health anxiety. These cross-sectional findings suggest the potential value of integrating psychological assessment and intervention into routine IBD care to improve patient self-management and long-term health outcomes. However, causal inferences cannot be drawn from the present data.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic, refractory condition characterized by inflammation and ulceration of the intestinal tract1. The clinical manifestations of IBD include recurrent abdominal pain, diarrhea, mucopurulent bloody stool, weight loss, and other symptoms, all of which seriously impair the quality of life of patients2. Self-management behavior refers to a series of health-related behaviors that patients actively adopt under the guidance of healthcare professionals to control disease symptoms, maintain disease stability, and improve their quality of life. These behaviors include regular medication adherence, dietary regulation, symptom monitoring, emotional management, regular follow-up, and other related activities3. Previous studies have shown that standardized self-management behaviors can effectively reduce the recurrence and hospitalization rates and surgical risks among patients with IBD while also improving long-term prognosis and quality of life4. However, some patients continue to experience problems, such as poor medication adherence and inadequate dietary control.

Illness perception refers to patients’ cognitive evaluations and emotional responses regarding the etiology, course, symptoms, treatment effects, and prognosis of their disease based on personal disease experience, acquisition of medical information, social support, and other factors5. Studies have shown that among patients with chronic diseases, such as diabetes and hypertension, negative illness perceptions are often associated with poor treatment adherence and insufficient self-management initiatives6. Health anxiety is a psychological state characterized by excessive concern about one’s health and irrational worry or fear related to physical discomfort7. Health anxiety not only impairs the mental health of patients with IBD but may also be associated with intestinal function through the bidirectional regulation of the brain-gut axis8.

This study is grounded in the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation (CSM), which posits that individuals form cognitive representations of their illness that subsequently guide their coping behaviors and health outcomes. According to the CSM, illness perception consists of five cognitive dimensions (identity, timeline, consequences, cause, and cure/control) and two emotional dimensions (concern and emotional response). The model further proposes that illness perceptions is associated with outcomes both directly and indirectly through emotional mediators such as anxiety. Building on this framework, we tested the following hypotheses: (1) negative illness perception is directly associated with poorer self-management behavior; (2) health anxiety mediates the relationship between illness perception and self-management behavior. Specifically, defensive functioning and emotional dysregulation may act as transdiagnostic regulators that shape how patients interpret somatic signals and engage in self-care behaviors. Recent studies suggest that deficits in emotional awareness (alexithymia) and maladaptive defense mechanisms are important upstream factors predisposing individuals to heightened health anxiety and negative illness perceptions. Therefore, investigating the pathway linking illness perception and health anxiety may provide insight into the psychosomatic processes that either facilitate or hinder effective self-management in patients with IBD.

lthough previous studies have examined the relationship between illness perception and self-management in chronic diseases such as diabetes and hypertension9, to our knowledge, no study has specifically tested the mediating role of health anxiety in the illness perception–self-management pathway among patients with IBD. This gap is particularly salient because IBD poses unique psychosomatic challenges—including the unpredictable nature of disease flares and the social stigma associated with bowel dysfunction—that may amplify health anxiety to a greater extent than in other chronic conditions. Therefore, this study aimed to test a mediation model in which health anxiety serves as a psychological mechanism linking illness perception to self-management behavior in Chinese patients with IBD. IBD poses unique psychosomatic challenges because of the unpredictable nature of disease flares and the social stigma associated with bowel dysfunction, both of which may further exacerbate health anxiety compared with other chronic conditions. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the mediating role of health anxiety in the relationship between illness perception and self-management behavior among Chinese patients with IBD. The findings may inform future hypothesis-driven intervention research aimed at improving illness perceptions, reducing health-related anxiety, and enhancing self-management behaviors in this population.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. A cross-sectional study design was used. A total of 326 patients with IBD who were treated at our hospital between June 2023 and June 2025 were enrolled.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a diagnosis of ulcerative colitis (UC) or Crohn’s disease (CD)10, confirmed by endoscopic and histopathological examinations; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) a documented disease duration of at least three months; (4) clear cognitive function and sufficient literacy to understand and complete the study questionnaires independently or with minimal investigator assistance; and (5) voluntary participation with the provision of written informed consent.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) concomitant severe digestive system diseases, including gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, primary sclerosing cholangitis, decompensated liver cirrhosis, acute pancreatitis, intestinal tuberculosis, amebic dysentery, or other conditions that could affect intestinal function or confound the study outcomes; (2) severe dysfunction of major organs, including the heart, liver, kidneys, or lungs; (3) diagnosed psychiatric disorders, cognitive impairment, or severe mental illness, including major depression, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or severe anxiety disorders diagnosed by psychiatrists. This exclusion criterion was implemented to minimize the potential confounding effects of severe pre-existing psychiatric conditions on the assessment of health anxiety and illness perceptions. However, the potential impact of this exclusion on the range and variability of health anxiety scores is discussed in the limitations section; (4) acute exacerbation of IBD accompanied by severe complications, such as severe gastrointestinal bleeding (hemoglobin decrease ≥ 20 g/L requiring blood transfusion), intestinal perforation, toxic megacolon, or septic shock; (5) intestinal surgery within the previous three months (e.g., intestinal resection or colostomy) or planned surgical treatment during the study period; (6) use of sedative-hypnotics, antipsychotics, or other medications that could affect mental status assessment within the past month; and (7) pregnancy or lactation.

All participants were recruited during routine outpatient clinic visits and did not experience acute severe disease flares requiring hospitalization at the time of assessment.

Research instruments

General information questionnaire

The general information questionnaire was designed by the researchers and included variables such as sex, age, educational level, marital status, occupation, monthly income, disease type (UC/CD), disease duration, and history of surgical treatment(defined as previous IBD-related surgery).

Brief illness perception questionnaire

The Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ) was developed by Broadbent et al.11 to assess patients’ overall cognitive and emotional perceptions of the illness. Items 1–7 and 9 were rated on a 0–10 scale. Items 1 (consequences), 3 (illness coherence, reflecting perceived uncertainty regarding the etiology), 6 (identity, reflecting difficulty in symptom recognition), 7 (concern), and 9 (emotional impact) were positively scored, with higher scores indicating more negative illness perceptions. Items 2 (timeline, reflecting perceived chronicity), 4 (treatment control), and 5 (personal control) were reverse scored before calculating the total score.

After reverse-scoring items 2, 4, and 5, all eight items were summed to produce a total score ranging from 0 to 80, with higher scores indicating a more negative or threatening perception of the illness. In the present study, Cronbach’s α coefficient for the BIPQ was 0.872, indicating good internal consistency.

Short health anxiety inventory

The Short Health Anxiety Inventory (SHAI) was developed by Salkovskis et al.12 to assess the level of health anxiety. The scale contains 18 items and uses a 4-point Likert scoring method (0 = none at all, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, and 3 = severe), yielding total scores ranging from 0 to 54.

Based on the total score, health anxiety was categorized into three levels: 0–17, indicating no or mild health anxiety; 18–34, indicating moderate health anxiety; and 35–54, indicating severe health anxiety. Higher scores indicate more severe anxiety. In the current study, Cronbach’s coefficient for the SHAI was 0.905, demonstrating excellent internal consistency.

Self-management behavior scale

The Self-Management Behavior Scale was developed by Shang et al.13. The scale consists of seven subdimensions across three overarching domains: disease management, psychological management, and life management, with 36 items. A 5-point Likert scoring method was used, with scores ranging from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“almost always”). The total score ranges from 0 to 144, with higher scores indicating better self-management.

This scale was developed and psychometrically validated in Chinese patients with IBD and demonstrated good construct validity and internal consistency in the original validation study. In the current sample, Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.934, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Data collection

Uniformly trained researchers from the Department of Gastroenterology were responsible for distributing and collecting the questionnaires. Before the survey, the researchers provided the participants with a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose, significance, content, and questionnaire completion procedures. Participants were informed that all personal information and survey data would be kept confidential. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the administration of the questionnaire.

Participants who could complete the questionnaire independently were asked to do so on their own. For participants with lower educational levels or those unable to complete the questionnaire independently, the researchers read each item aloud and completed the questionnaire according to the participants’ verbal responses to ensure the authenticity and accuracy of the data collected.After completion, the researchers checked each questionnaire on-site for completeness and logical consistency., and participants were promptly asked to supplement or correct missing or inconsistent responses to ensure data validity.No missing data were present in the final sample of 326 questionnaires, as any incomplete or inconsistent responses were identified and corrected on-site by the researchers before the participants left the clinic.

Among the 326 completed questionnaires, 261 (80.1%) were self-administered, whereas 65 (19.9%) were completed through structured interviews conducted by the researchers for participants with limited literacy skills. Independent samples t-tests showed no statistically significant differences in total BIPQ scores (P = 0.672), SHAI scores (P = 0.818), or self-management behavior scores (P = 0.543) between the two administration methods. Nevertheless, the potential for social desirability bias in the interviewer-administered subgroup should be acknowledged as a limitation of this study.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 22.0). The normality of the continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Independent-samples t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, including sex, marital status, occupation, disease type, and surgical history. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to compare three or more groups, including age, educational level, monthly income, and disease duration. When the overall F-test was statistically significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the least significant difference (LSD) test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 after correction.

Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages [n (%)], and comparisons were performed using the chi-square test. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to evaluate the associations among illness perception, health anxiety, self-management behavior, and their respective dimensions.

To examine the potential mediating role of health anxiety in the relationship between illness perception and self-management behavior, mediation analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro (Model 4) in SPSS. This model applies ordinary least squares path analysis to estimate the total, direct, and indirect effects of the variables. The unstandardized regression coefficients are reported. In the present analysis, the total BIPQ score was specified as the independent variable (X), the total SHAI score as the mediator (M), and the total self-management behavior score as the dependent variable (Y).

The bias-corrected bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples was used to generate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the indirect effect. This nonparametric resampling method is the gold standard for mediation analysis because it does not require the assumption of normality for the sampling distribution of the indirect effect, which is often skewed in small to moderate samples. A total of 5,000 resamples were generated from the original dataset with replacement, and the indirect effect was calculated for each resample to construct the empirical sampling distribution. The 95% CI was then derived from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of this distribution. This approach provides a more accurate and robust test of the mediation effect compared to traditional parametric methods such as the Sobel test. The mediation effect was considered statistically significant when the 95% CI did not include zero. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A post hoc power analysis for the mediation model was conducted using a Monte Carlo simulation based on the method proposed by Schoemann et al. (2017), with 10,000 replications. Based on the observed path coefficients (a = 0.289, b = -0.639), an R² of 0.28 for the mediator model, an R² of 0.51 for the dependent variable model, and a sample size of 326, the statistical power to detect the indirect effect was estimated to be 0.91, exceeding the conventional threshold of 0.80. These findings indicate that the study was adequately powered for primary mediation analysis.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort

A total of 380 eligible patients were approached during the recruitment period of the study. Among them, 42 declined to participate and 12 returned incomplete questionnaires, resulting in a final sample of 326 participants with a response rate of 85.79%.

Among the 326 patients with IBD, 189 (57.97%) were men and 137 (42.03%) were women. The mean age was 42.63 ± 12.58 years, ranging from 18 to 72. Regarding educational level, 43 patients (13.19%) had a primary school education or below, 85 (26.07%) had a junior high school education, 102 (31.29%) had a senior high school or technical secondary school education, and 96 (29.45%) had a college education or higher.

Regarding marital status, 258 patients(79.14%) were married, 42 (12.88%) were unmarried, and 26 (7.98%) were divorced or widowed. Regarding occupation, 195 patients (59.82%) were employed, 78 (23.93%) were retired, and 53 (16.26%) were unemployed. Monthly income was reported as < 3000 yuan in 108 patients (33.13%), 3000–5000 yuan in 125 patients (38.34%), and > 5000 yuan in 93 patients (28.53%).

Regarding disease-related characteristics, 182 patients (55.83%) had UC, and 144 (44.17%) had CD. The disease duration ranged from 3 months to 15 years, with a mean duration of 4.26 ± 3.15 years. In addition, 63 patients (19.32%) had undergone surgical treatment, whereas 263 (80.68%) had not undergone surgical treatment.The detailed results are presented in Table 1.

Mediating effect model

A mediation model was constructed with the BIPQ score as the independent variable (X), self-management behavior as the dependent variable (Y), and the SHAI score as the mediating variable (M).

Collinearity diagnostics were performed on all regression models. The variance inflation factors (VIFs) for all predictor variables were below the threshold of 10 (BIPQ total score: VIF = 1.21; SHAI score: VIF = 1.21 in the final model), indicating that multicollinearity did not significantly affect the parameter estimates.

As a sensitivity analysis, the BIPQ total score was recalculated after excluding the “concern” subscale, which was highly correlated with “emotional impact,” and the “personal control” subscale, which was highly correlated with “treatment control.” treatment control. ’ The mediation analysis was repeated using the reduced BIPQ scores. The indirect effect remained statistically significant (effect = -0.165, 95% CI: -0.253 to -0.082), supporting the robustness of the mediation model.

The results showed that higher BIPQ scores were significantly associated with lower self-management behavior scores (regression coefficient = -1.427, P < 0.001) and higher SHAI scores (regression coefficient = 0.352, P < 0.001). In addition, health anxiety was significantly and negatively associated with self-management behaviors (regression coefficient = -0.700, P < 0.001). After including the SHAI scores in the model, the BIPQ scores continued to demonstrate a significant negative association with self-management behavior (regression coefficient = -1.180). The detailed results are presented in Table 5.

Further mediation analysis was conducted after adjusting for age, monthly income, and disease duration as covariates. After adjustment, the total effect of the BIPQ scores on self-management behavior remained statistically significant. The detailed results are presented in Table 6.

In an additional sensitivity analysis, disease type (UC vs. CD) was included as a covariate in the mediation model. The indirect effect remained statistically significant (effect = -0.180, 95% CI: -0.273 to -0.087), further confirming the robustness of the mediating pathway across the disease subtypes.

Mediating effect test

The mediation analysis demonstrated that illness perception had a significant direct effect on self-management behavior (effect = -1.180), accounting for 82.69% of the total effect. In addition, health anxiety exerted a significant mediating effect on the relationship between illness perception and self-management behavior, with an indirect effect of -0.246. This indirect pathway accounted for 17.31% of the total effects. The statistical significance of the mediating effect was supported by the 95% bias-corrected bootstrap confidence interval, which did not include zero. Discussion

Consistent with the cross-sectional design of this study, all reported associations should be interpreted as correlations rather than causations. The mean BIPQ score of 47.31 observed in this Chinese cohort was comparable to, although slightly higher than, those reported in Western European IBD populations (mean score ≈ 42–45)14,15. This difference may reflect cultural variations in illness conceptualization, as collectivist cultures that emphasize family interdependence may intensify feelings of burden and loss of social role functioning, thereby contributing to more negative perceptions of illness than individualistic cultures. However, it is also possible that the relatively higher scores observed in this study were influenced by selection bias associated with recruitment from a tertiary hospital, where patients with more severe or refractory diseases were more likely to be represented.

Furthermore, the significant negative correlation between illness perception and self-management behavior (r = -0.660) was stronger than that reported in other chronic diseases, such as diabetes (r ≈ -0.35 to -0.45), suggesting that the psychological burden associated with IBD may uniquely interfere with behavioral adherence. An alternative explanation for this strong association is the possibility of a reciprocal feedback loop, whereby poor self-management reinforces negative illness perceptions. Patients who struggle with adherence may perceive their disease as less controllable, thereby worsening their cognitive appraisal of the illness. However, the cross-sectional design of the present study does not allow for testing this bidirectional relationship.

IBD is characterized by chronic recurrence, persistent symptoms, and prolonged treatment durations. Patients with IBD often experience long-term physical discomfort and are therefore prone to excessive concerns regarding disease progression and prognosis. In addition, the pathogenesis of IBD is complex, and no definitive cure is currently available for it. Patients frequently have a limited understanding of the disease etiology and insufficient confidence in treatment efficacy, resulting in a reduced sense of disease control. Moreover, declines in QoL and impairments in social functioning caused by the disease may further aggravate negative emotional experiences and strengthen negative illness perceptions16.

In the present study, the mean SHAI score among patients with IBD was 28.89 ± 8.81, which was significantly higher than the levels reported in the general population, indicating that health anxiety is particularly prominent in individuals with IBD. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies17. Patients with IBD face a long-term risk of disease recurrence, and symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, and mucopurulent bloody stools may substantially interfere with daily life and work. Consequently, patients may become excessively attentive to their physical condition, irrationally concerned about bodily discomfort, and increasingly vulnerable to health anxiety. In addition, negative illness perceptions, disease activity, and inadequate social support may exacerbate health anxiety.

The mean self-management behavior score in this study was 67.79 ± 20.48, indicating a moderate-to-low level of self-management among patients with IBD. This finding is consistent with those of previous domestic studies18.

The analysis demonstrated significant differences in self-management behaviors across age groups, with patients older than 60 years exhibiting the lowest scores. Older patients often experience age-related degenerative physiological changes, reduced intestinal functional reserve, and common comorbid chronic diseases such as well-controlled hypertension and diabetes. Importantly, our study only excluded patients with severe dysfunction of major organs (e.g., decompensated liver cirrhosis, severe heart failure), not those with mild age-related changes or well-managed comorbidities. These common age-related conditions may still limit patients’ ability to effectively engage in self-management behaviors due to reduced physical energy and functional capacity. In addition, some older adult patients experience cognitive decline, resulting in a diminished ability to understand and retain disease-related information, making it difficult to accurately implement complex self-management strategies, such as medication adherence and dietary regulation19. In contrast, younger patients generally possess stronger learning abilities, broader access to disease-related information, and higher expectations regarding quality of life, making them more willing to cooperate actively with treatment and adhere to self-management behaviors.

Patients with monthly incomes > 5,000 yuan demonstrated significantly higher self-management behavior scores than lower-income groups, reflecting the supportive role of economic status in chronic disease management. Higher-income patients may have better access to quality healthcare services, nutritious food, and rehabilitation resources, thereby reducing treatment interruptions and dietary compromises caused by financial stress. Conversely, lower-income patients may prioritize their basic living needs, making it difficult to devote sufficient time and energy to disease management. Concerns regarding medical expenses may also reduce their willingness to attend regular follow-up visits and examinations, ultimately affecting their overall self-management.

Patients with a disease duration > 5 years had lower self-management behavior scores than those with disease durations < 1 year or 1–5 years, suggesting a trend in which a longer disease duration is associated with poorer self-management. This phenomenon may be related to “treatment fatigue” which is commonly observed in chronic diseases. Because IBD is characterized by recurrent symptoms such as abdominal pain and diarrhea, long-term disease burden, and repeated treatment failures, patients’ confidence in treatment effectiveness may gradually weaken, resulting in reduced motivation for self-management, poorer medication adherence, and less rigorous dietary control20.

Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed a significant negative association between the BIPQ scores and self-management behavior. Specifically, patients with more negative illness perceptions exhibited poorer self-management behaviors. This finding is consistent with previous research21, suggesting that illness perception is an important determinant of self-management in patients with IBD. Patients with negative illness representations often perceive their condition as severe, uncontrollable, and associated with a poor prognosis. Such cognitions may serve as feelings of hopelessness and helplessness, thereby reducing treatment adherence and active participation in self-care. Consequently, these patients may be less likely to consistently engage in key management strategies, including medication adherence, dietary modifications, symptom monitoring, and physical activity22.

In contrast, patients with more adaptive illness perceptions tend to have a more accurate understanding of their disease and treatment process, a stronger sense of control, and a greater willingness to actively participate in treatment and self-management activities, resulting in better overall self-management performance. Furthermore, the correlation analysis demonstrated a significant positive relationship between the BIPQ and SHAI scores, indicating that more negative illness perceptions were associated with higher levels of health anxiety. This finding is consistent with that reported by Gigante et al.23. Excessive concern regarding disease consequences, uncertainty about disease etiology, and accompanying emotional distress may contribute to the development or exacerbation of anxiety symptoms. In addition, SHAI scores were significantly negatively correlated with self-management behavior, suggesting that higher health anxiety is associated with poorer self-management. Excessive health-related worry may consume substantial cognitive and emotional resources, thereby interfering with patients’ abilities to consistently perform structured self-management behaviors.

This study further demonstrated that illness perception was significantly and negatively associated with self-management behavior, supporting the hypothesis that more negative illness appraisals are associated with poorer self-management practices. Notably, health anxiety partially mediated this relationship, accounting for 14.08% of the total association in the adjusted model, highlighting the role of health-related worry as an important cognitive-emotional mechanism linking negative illness perceptions to behavioral outcomes. However, causal relationships cannot be established because of the cross-sectional nature of the data, causal relationships cannot be established. The observed associations suggest that illness perception is associated with self-management behavior both directly and indirectly through health anxiety.

Patients with negative illness perceptions may excessively worry about disease progression and prognosis and misinterpret normal physiological sensations as indicators of disease exacerbation. These maladaptive cognitions may induce or aggravate health anxiety. Patients with elevated health anxiety often devote excessive attention and energy to concerns about bodily discomfort and medical examinations, thereby neglecting the implementation of standardized self-management behaviors24.

The significant indirect effect of health anxiety identified in this study is consistent with contemporary psychosomatic theories that conceptualize health anxiety as a manifestation of impaired emotional regulation and maladaptive, defensive functioning. Previous studies have suggested that patients with high levels of health anxiety frequently exhibit alexithymic traits, making it difficult to distinguish emotional distress from the physical symptoms of IBD25.

From a broader psychosomatic perspective, the mediating role of health anxiety may also be interpreted within the framework of coping processes and defense mechanisms. Defensive functioning refers to largely unconscious psychological processes used to regulate emotional distress and maintain psychological stability in the context of chronic illness. When patients rely on maladaptive defenses, such as denial of emotional distress, projection of anxiety onto bodily sensations, or avoidance of illness-related information, these patterns may impair realistic illness appraisal and reduce engagement in effective self-care behaviors. In this context, health anxiety may not merely represent an isolated symptom but rather a cognitive-affective manifestation of broader defensive dysregulation.

For example, patients who unconsciously deny the psychological burden of IBD may redirect emotional distress toward excessive somatic vigilance and interpret normal gastrointestinal sensations as signs of disease worsening. This defensive displacement may create a self-reinforcing cycle in which anxiety intensifies, while the cognitive and motivational resources necessary for effective self-management gradually diminish. This interpretation is consistent with the findings reported by Martino et al.26, who demonstrated close associations between defensive functioning, illness perception, and psychological adjustment in chronic disease populations, and by Silvestro et al.27, who found that maladaptive coping styles mediate the relationship between psychological distress and health-related quality of life in patients with IBD.

Although the present study did not directly assess defense mechanisms or coping strategies, the observed mediation pathway provides indirect evidence that health anxiety is embedded within a broader system of cognitive and affective regulation. Future studies incorporating validated assessments of defensive functioning and coping strategies, such as the Defense Mechanism Rating Scales or Brief COPE, may help clarify whether these constructs serve as upstream determinants, mediators, or moderators in the illness perception–health anxiety–self-management pathway.

In addition, health anxiety may exacerbate intestinal inflammation through bidirectional regulation of the brain-gut axis, thereby contributing to recurrent symptoms and negatively influencing patients’ perceptions of the effectiveness of self-management behaviors, ultimately reducing adherence to self-management practices28,29. Therefore, in clinical practice, healthcare providers should pay close attention to patients’ illness perceptions, correct maladaptive cognitions, identify health anxiety early, and implement targeted interventions to alleviate anxiety to improve self-management ability and disease prognosis.

The present findings suggest several potential directions for future clinical intervention research, although these implications should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypotheses requiring empirical verification rather than as established clinical recommendations. First, the observed relationship between negative illness perception and poor self-management suggests that structured educational interventions delivered through individual counseling, group education, or digital platforms may help patients develop more adaptive illness representation. However, whether such interventions improve self-management behaviors requires confirmation through prospective controlled studies. Second, the mediating role of health anxiety suggests that psychological interventions specifically targeting health-related worry, such as cognitive restructuring of catastrophic interpretations and interoceptive exposure techniques, may represent promising adjunctive approaches to IBD management. However, the effectiveness of these interventions in gastroenterology settings remains to be established. Third, the finding that self-management scores were lower among older patients and those with longer disease durations suggests the possibility of “illness fatigue” or burnout in long-term IBD management. Tailored interventions, such as motivational interviewing for long-term patients and peer support programs, may warrant further investigation. Because these clinical implications are derived from cross-sectional associations, they should be considered preliminary until they are validated through interventional research.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding the causality or temporal relationships among illness perception, health anxiety, and self-management behavior. Terms such as “predictor” and “effect” used in this manuscript refer only to statistical associations within the mediation model and should not be interpreted as evidence of causality. Longitudinal and experimental studies are required to clarify the directional nature of these relationships. Second, the mediation model did not include several potentially important confounding variables, such as objective disease activity indices, social support, and coping strategies, which may have influenced the magnitude or significance of the observed mediating effects. Third, the absence of objective disease activity measures is an important limitation, as disease activity is likely to be a major determinant of both illness perception and health anxiety in patients with IBD. Without controlling for disease activity, the observed associations may partly reflect the confounding effects of an active disease status. Therefore, future studies should incorporate validated clinical disease activity indices.

Fourth, although multicollinearity diagnostics indicated acceptable variance inflation factors (all VIFs < 1.21) and sensitivity analysis excluding highly correlated subscales yielded consistent indirect effects, the high pairwise correlations observed among several BIPQ dimensions (e.g., concern and emotional impact, personal control and treatment control) suggest potential conceptual overlap that should be considered when interpreting the illness perception construct. Fifth, the multiple univariate comparisons presented in Table 2 were performed without formal correction for multiple testing; while we applied Bonferroni correction post hoc and indicated corrected significance levels in the table footnotes, the exploratory nature of these subgroup analyses should be acknowledged, and the findings await confirmation in future studies. Sixth, the exclusion of patients with formally diagnosed psychiatric disorders, including anxiety disorders, may have restricted the variability of the health anxiety scores within the sample. Although this exclusion criterion was intended to reduce confounding caused by severe psychopathology, it may also have attenuated the observed associations and limited the generalizability of the findings to the broader IBD population, in which psychiatric comorbidities are prevalent. Seventh, the use of self-reported measures may have introduced common method bias. Future studies should incorporate clinician-rated assessments and objective measures of disease activity to strengthen the methodological rigor. In addition, although all scales demonstrated acceptable-to-excellent internal consistency in the present study, the factor structure of the self-management behavior scale was not examined, and additional construct validation in diverse IBD populations is recommended. Furthermore, the subgroup with a monthly income of > 5,000 yuan was relatively small (n = 35), which may have affected the stability of comparisons involving this subgroup. Replication in larger and more socioeconomically diverse samples is required. Data collection will span from June 2023 to June 2025. Although no major changes in treatment guidelines or demographic patterns were identified during this period, minor temporal variations cannot be completely excluded and may have introduced heterogeneity into the sample.

In conclusion, attention to patients’ illness perceptions and health-related anxiety may be promising targets for improving self-management behaviors in individuals with IBD. Intervention studies are needed to determine whether modifying these psychological factors can serve as sustained improvements in self-management and long-term health outcomes.

The findings of this study also have several potentially actionable clinical implications. First, given the strong association between illness perception and self-management behavior, routine assessment using brief tools such as the BIPQ may help identify patients with highly negative illness perceptions who are at an increased risk of poor adherence. Second, the significant mediating role of health anxiety highlights the potential value of brief psychological interventions that can be feasibly implemented in gastroenterology settings. Specific approaches, including cognitive restructuring of catastrophic interpretations of abdominal symptoms, interoceptive exposure, and mindfulness-based stress reduction, may be useful in interrupting the illness perception–health anxiety pathway identified in this study. Third, the lower self-management scores observed among older patients and those with longer disease duration suggest the presence of “illness fatigue” or burnout in long-term IBD management. Accordingly, interventions may need to be tailored according to the disease stage and patient characteristics. For newly diagnosed patients, education focused on disease controllability may be especially important, whereas motivational interviewing and peer support programs may be more beneficial for patients with long-standing diseases. Finally, because this study was conducted in a single center with a culturally homogeneous sample, the generalizability of these findings and recommendations for other cultural and healthcare contexts remains uncertain. Therefore, these strategies should be further evaluated in diverse populations before broad clinical implementation. It should also be emphasized that the mediation model examined in this study reflects cross-sectional associations, rather than causal pathways. Although the identified relationships are consistent with existing psychosomatic theories, the data do not permit conclusions regarding their directionality. Therefore, the proposed clinical strategies should undergo rigorous evaluation in longitudinal and interventional studies before being adopted in routine clinical practice.

Overall, the present findings contribute to the growing body of evidence highlighting the interconnected roles of cognitive, emotional, and behavioral processes in the self-management of IBD.
