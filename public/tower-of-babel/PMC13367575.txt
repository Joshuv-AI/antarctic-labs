Development of an automated landmarking tool for the femur in dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scans using contour-based image analysis
Authors: Edward M Chu, Jonathan D Adachi, Cheryl E Quenneville
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoporosis is a skeletal disease that significantly increases fracture risk and imposes a growing public health and economic burden. Notably, hip fractures are associated with high mortality, long-term disability, and loss of independence. When evaluating osteoporosis and estimating fracture risk, DXA is commonly used to determine the BMD. To capture geometrical morphology from these DXA scans as factors in fracture risk prediction, landmarking around the femur is conducted manually around the ROI. However, this process is labor-intensive and prone to variability. This study presents the development of a fully automated femoral landmarking tool that integrates a U-Net convolutional neural network for femur contour segmentation with a geometric algorithm for consistent landmark placement. A heterogeneous dataset of 555 DXA scans was used to train and evaluate the U-Net model, achieving a pixel-wise accuracy of 97.55%, an Intersection over Union of 91.55%, and a Dice coefficient of 95.56% on the test set. When incorporated into a statistical shape and appearance modeling (SSAM) framework for fracture risk prediction, predictions using the automatically generated landmarks achieved a test AUC of 0.831 (95% CI: 0.698-0.965), compared with 0.780 (95% CI: 0.635-0.925) for manual landmarks; a paired DeLong test showed no significant difference (p = .53), indicating a comparable performance. The proposed pipeline produces anatomically relevant, reproducible landmarks, and supports fracture prediction performance similar to manual methods. It presents a scalable and objective solution for morphometric analysis in DXA imaging.Keywords: DXA imaging, femur contour, automated landmarking, bone morphology, machine learning, U-Net

Introduction

DXA is the clinical standard for assessing osteoporosis and evaluating fracture risk through BMD and a subsequent T-score that compares the BMD against a healthy adult.1 Clinically, the hip is a critical site of interest associated with 20%-30% of patients dying within the first year of a hip fracture.2,3 Although BMD is an established predictor of bone fragility, a single metric cannot fully capture individual fracture susceptibility, as a substantial proportion of fragility fractures still occur in individuals with BMD values outside the osteoporotic range.3 This limitation has prompted the use of additional structural information from DXA images, including geometric and morphological characteristics of the proximal femur that may contribute to bone strength and fracture risk.

Statistical shape and appearance modeling (SSAM) provides a framework for quantifying anatomical variation by capturing patterns of shape and image intensity across populations.4–6 Statistical shape and appearance modeling uses principal component analysis to statistically model geometric deformations and textural variations, integrating them into a unified representation. The resulting principal components (PCs) quantify the deviation of each individual’s shape and appearance from the population means. These PCs can then be provided, alongside clinical risk factors, such as BMD, age, sex, and previous fractures, as inputs to a separate predictive model for estimating hip fracture risk.7

A foundational step of traditional SSAM analysis is the placement of anatomical landmarks along the femoral contour to define bone geometry and establish point-to-point correspondence across subjects. In most implementations, these landmarks are generated through manual annotation of bone contours,8 a time-intensive process that requires trained, consistent observers. In addition to limiting scalability, manual landmark placement introduces inter- and intra-observer variability that may contribute non-biological variation to morphometric analyses.9,10 Automated landmark detection offers a potential solution to these challenges through the rapid and consistent identification of anatomical contours, especially across large imaging datasets.

Recent advances in machine learning, particularly deep learning, have shown potential for applications such as automating medical image analysis.11 Among these, the U-Net convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture has emerged as a leading method for biomedical image segmentation due to its ability to learn complex features at multiple spatial scales.12 U-Net has been successfully applied across a wide range of clinical imaging tasks, including organ segmentation in CT and MRI scans,13 brain tumor localization in MRI,14 and retinal structure segmentation in optical coherence tomography images.15 U-Net contains an encoder–decoder structure that captures both local edge details and global anatomical context, making it especially well-suited for applications such as femur contour extraction in DXA scans.12 However, applications of automated segmentation to DXA remain relatively limited, and it remains unclear if automated landmarks generated by deep learning can support downstream SSAM analyses with performance comparable to that of manually annotated datasets.

The aim of this study was to develop and evaluate a novel automated framework for femoral contour detection and landmark generation for hip DXA images. The proposed approach integrates deep learning into segmentation applications with a geometric landmarking strategy to generate consistent contour landmarks across scans. The performance of the automated method was evaluated using quantitative segmentation metrics and visual inspection, and its utility was further assessed within an SSAM for fracture risk prediction. By comparing predictive performance derived from automated and manually generated landmarks, this work aims to determine whether automated landmarking can provide a reliable and scalable alternative for SSAM of DXA images.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in 3 phases, each requiring the development of dedicated tools. In Phase A, a U-Net CNN model was applied to 555 DXA scans (72:14:14 split) from a heterogeneous dataset to generate femur contour masks. Phase B used these segmented contours as inputs to produce automated landmark coordinates. Finally, Phase C involved a comparative assessment using a subset of scans to evaluate the auto-landmarking tool’s effect on the SSAM’s performance.

Dataset composition

Five hundred and fifty-five anonymized DXA images were strategically drawn from a diverse pool of sources, including the Canadian Multicentre Osteoporosis Study (CaMos),16 the Amgen FREEDOM trial,17 and Hamilton Osteoporosis Clinic.18 The image acquisition involved scans from both Hologic Discovery DXA (Hologic Discovery A, Hologic, Inc.) and Lunar iDXA (GE Healthcare) (Table 1). Across all datasets, patients were aged 50 yr or older, with a mean age of 70.2 ± 12.54 and a mean BMD of 774.64 ± 204.87 mg/cm2. There were 112 males and 441 females in total, with 2 images missing sex labels.

Phase A: femur contour with U-Net CNN

For model development and evaluation, the dataset was stratified into training (72%), validation (14%), and test (14%) subsets. A single trained annotator was responsible for defining the “ground truth” contour in each of the femurs by manually annotating the perimeter (Figure 1). It was subsequently filled, converted to grayscale, transformed into a NumPy array, and the pixels were normalized to range from 0 to 1. The normalized array was converted back to an image and saved with the original image as a ground truth pair.

Creation of the “ground truth.” Each DXA image was given a ground truth binary mask (white or black) created by (A) manually tracing an outline around the femur and (B) subsequently filling.

Preprocessing was conducted on each image to increase the quality and consistency of the input data for the U-Net model. Histogram equalization was first applied to improve the visibility of bone structures and soft tissue boundaries. Pixel intensity values were then normalized to a range of [0, 1] using min-max normalization. All images were resized to 300 × 250 pixels for training, and the corresponding masks were resized and matched to their native image size. A Gaussian blur was added to the images to smooth jagged edges in the bone contours and reduce the risk of overfitting during training. To increase the diversity of the training dataset and improve model generalization, data augmentation techniques were applied using the Albumentations library,19 where the dataset underwent rotations (±15°), horizontal, and vertical flips.

Femur segmentation was performed using a U-Net model, a CNN architecture consisting of an encoder–decoder structure with skip connections (Figure 2). The U-Net model was chosen for its ability to capture both local and global features in the image.12 The encoder pathway was constructed with a series of double convolutional and max-pooling layers to progressively reduce the spatial dimensions of the input image while increasing the number of feature channels. Each convolutional layer was given a 3 × 3 kernel with ReLU activation, followed by a 2 × 2 max-pooling operation with a stride of 2. The decoder pathway was built mirroring the encoder but in reverse, consisting of up-sampling layers followed by double convolutional layers. Each up-sampling layer used a 2 × 2 transposed convolution to increase the spatial dimensions of the feature maps. Skip connections from the encoder pathway were concatenated with the corresponding decoder layers to preserve spatial information and improve segmentation accuracy. Finally, a 1 × 1 convolution layer was implemented to map the refined features to the desired number of output channels for binary segmentation tasks.

U-Net CNN model architecture. The input image is processed in an encoder that captures hierarchical features through down-sampling. The decoder reconstructs the spatial details through up-sampling, producing a binary mask that outlines the femur. Skip connections between encoder and decoder layers preserve fine-grained spatial information.

Gradients of the loss with respect to the parameters were computed via backpropagation and used to update weights and biases. Then, through backpropagation, the gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters was computed. The Adam optimization algorithm was used to update the parameters, and the model was trained until the loss was no longer reduced.

The U-Net CNN was implemented using the PyTorch deep learning framework.20 OpenCV was used for initial image processing tasks,21 such as histogram equalization and noise addition. The model was trained on a commercially available NVIDIA GPU with the use of CUDA acceleration for faster computation.22 The U-Net model was initially trained for 200 epochs with a batch size of 16. The Adam optimizer was used with an initial learning rate of 0.01, which was progressively reduced by 0.001 if the validation loss did not improve for 50 consecutive epochs. With the learning rate tuned, early stopping was implemented with a patience of 25 epochs by monitoring the validation loss. Training was halted when no improvement beyond a minimum delta of 0.0001 was observed.

The model performance was evaluated using the testing set based on pixel-wise accuracy (the percentage of correctly classified pixels between femur and background), Dice coefficient (the measure of overlap between predicted and ground truth masks), and Intersection over Union (IOU, the ratio of the intersection area to the union area of the predicted and ground truth masks).

To complement these quantitative metrics, a qualitative visual error analysis was conducted to assess the segmentation output in more detail. This involved overlaying color-coded pixel classifications on the original DXA images.

Phase B: automatic landmarking on the contour

The landmarking process was implemented as a structured sequence of operations designed for precision, consistency, and anatomical relevance. All right hip images were flipped to match the orientation of the left hip images. To ensure that the boundaries of the contour were smooth and continuous in preparation for further downstream processing, Gaussian blurring was applied to all masks.

The U-Net segmentation occasionally detected bright areas with undesired artifacts from the DXA images. This was resolved by first applying a binary threshold to the blurred image and then using the cv2.findContours function to detect all external contours. Among these, the contour with the largest area was selected, under the assumption that it represented the femur. This selection completely removed any undesired artifacts and allowed the model to focus solely on the anatomical structure of interest.

To establish a consistent reference frame for landmark distribution, 3 key anatomical reference points were identified on the femur contour. These included the left-most point (defined by the minimum x-coordinate of the femoral head in red) and the right-most point (the maximum x-coordinate in green) (Figure 3A). A circle was constructed with its center at the midpoint of these points, and its radius was set to half the distance between them. The lowest intersection between the femur contour and this circle served as the third anatomical reference point (in blue) and was a stable inferior anchor point on the medial side of the proximal femoral diaphysis for further positional mapping.

Examples of landmark placements on the proximal femur. (A) a binary mask with landmark placements and geometric constructs, and (B) corresponding landmarks projected on the original DXA scans. Nineteen (C) manual landmarks were annotated by a single researcher on the CaMos dataset.

The third reference point was carried horizontally over to the lateral aspect of the femur to create a fourth reference point. With these reference points identified, the femur was truncated into upper and lower regions. This focused the algorithm on the clinically significant regions of the femoral head, neck, and greater trochanter. Along this upper segment, landmarks were distributed at equal intervals based on the arc length of the contour, starting from the third (inferior medial) reference point and ending at the fourth (inferior lateral) reference point (Figure 3B). To ensure uniform spacing and spatial arrangement, the contour was resampled using linear interpolation, which smoothed out any irregularities arising from pixel-level segmentation noise.

The final set of landmarks was visualized as varying colors of dots overlaid on the original DXA image for visual inspection and verification. Their precise coordinates were exported for further analysis and prepared to be used as direct input into the SSAM tool.

Phase C: comparative assessment of automatic and manual landmark placement

To quantify the similarity between automatic and manual landmark placements, their effect on the downstream fracture prediction performance of the SSAM pipeline was evaluated. The CaMos cohort was selected for this evaluation because the SSAM framework has been previously validated on this dataset23 and its scan protocols and population characteristics were well matched to the present study. Of the 205 available CaMos scans, 189 had complete covariate data (BMD and T-score) and were retained for Phase C. This subset had a mean age of 70 ± 10 yr (mean ± SD) and comprised 97 females and 92 males. The data were split 80:20 into training and test subsets, with fracture cases proportionally allocated to each. A leave-one-out cross-validation model was used to train n times on n−1 data points, where the remaining single point was used for validation in each iteration.

Manual landmarks were generated for all CaMos DXA scans by a single researcher, using the previously established manual landmarking procedures.24 Nineteen landmarks were placed, where landmarks 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, and 19 were anatomical and the other landmarks were placed mid-distance between the anatomical landmarks for geometry (Figure 3C). Hence, 2 sets of manually and automatically landmarked images, along with their age, BMD, and T-score, were used as inputs to the SSAM to generate fracture risk scores. The area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) was assessed by generating ROC curves to depict the relationship between the true positive rate (sensitivity) and the false positive rate (1-specificity) across the classification threshold applied to the predicted fracture risk probability. Specifically, each subject’s predicted probability was compared against a 30% threshold to categorize them as high or low fracture risk. This classification threshold was selected by maximizing Youden’s J, defined as the sum of sensitivity and specificity −1. For both automated and manual test sets, the 95% CI was determined through the bootstrap method by resampling with replacement from the original data a thousand times.

Results

Training was monitored with the Weights & Biases (WandB) platform,25 logging training loss, validation loss, and the validation Dice coefficient at each epoch. Training loss is computed on the training subset and is the quantity the optimizer directly minimizes; validation loss is computed on a held-out validation subset that is not used to update model weights and therefore serves as an indicator of generalization, where a widening gap between the two would indicate overfitting. In an initial 200-epoch run (≈14 000 s), the model reached a training loss of 0.0126, a validation loss of 0.580, and a Dice coefficient of 0.954 on the validation set (Figure 4). Validation loss plateaued by approximately epoch 80, as identified through both numerical monitoring and visual assessment across multiple training runs. After this point, further training produced only marginal and fluctuating changes in all 3 metrics, with no widening train and validation gap. Specifically, the Dice coefficient, training loss, validation loss, and the qualitative appearance of the predicted contours all showed no meaningful improvement beyond this point. Early stopping was therefore set at epoch 80 in the final model.

Performance metrics of the segmentation model across 200 epochs. (A) Training loss progressively decreased and stabilized, (B) validation loss showed a similar trend, and (C) Dice-Sørensen coefficient showed a rapid increase and then stabilized. The marker on each denotes where early stopping was executed at 80 epochs based on validation performance.

The U-Net model exhibited strong performance in segmenting femur contours from DXA images, reflected in both quantitative and qualitative evaluations. On the test set, the model achieved a pixel-wise accuracy of 97.55%, an IoU score of 91.55%, and a Dice coefficient of 95.56%. These results collectively indicate a high level of precision in distinguishing femoral structures from surrounding soft tissue.

The visual inspection reinforced the numerical findings by confirming that the model’s errors were minor and largely confined to edge regions (Figure 5).

Example of visual error analysis. Visual inspection between the ground truth and the generated mask was color-coded with true positive (green), false positive (red), and false negative (blue). Three archetypes were formed from the visual analysis: (A) normal, (B) moderate error, and (C) artifact.

Within the test set (n = 80), most false pixels were detected in the femoral head and the subtrochanteric area. Three archetypes were identified based on the severity of the error in the visual analyses, particularly focusing on the femoral head detection. Artifact cases (n = 2) were identified in 2 images that contained artifacts that were not relevant to the femur. Moderate error cases (n = 16) were observed in images that had a Dice coefficient of less than 0.96, with 50% pixels of their false negatives or false positives in the femoral head (median = 0.94, IQR = 0.93-0.95). Normal cases (n = 62) showed no visible artifacts and achieved Dice coefficients at or above the 96% threshold, with false positives and false negatives confined to single-pixel or narrow boundary regions. For these cases, the U-Net model consistently captured the correct boundaries of both the FN and the intertrochanteric region across varying DXA image qualities and femur morphologies. The segmentation also appeared to be unaffected by artifacts introduced by patient positioning and varying scanner models, as evidenced by successful delineation across the test set.

The leave-one-out cross-validation analysis using manually placed landmarks yielded a test AUC of 0.780 (95% CI: 0.635-0.925; p < .001) (Figure 6). Applying the automatically generated landmarks on the same held-out dataset produced a test AUC of 0.831 (95% CI: 0.698-0.965; p < .001). A paired DeLong test on per-subject predicted probabilities from both models on the same held-out test set (n = 39) showed no statistically significant difference between the 2 approaches (ΔAUC = +0.051; z = −0.634; p = .53), supporting non-inferiority of the automated landmarking pipeline. At the Youden-optimized classification threshold, both approaches produced identical confusion matrices (accuracy 71.8%, sensitivity 64.0%, and specificity 85.7%; TP = 16, TN = 12, FP = 2, and FN = 9).

Discussion

Previous work in our lab used SSAM to capture the variability in both the shape and texture of anatomical structures across populations for the purpose of fracture risk prediction.7,24 Clinical risk factors, such as BMD, age, weight, and sex, were included as additional predictors in generating a fracture prediction score. This multivariate approach demonstrated promising results, achieving an average R2 of 0.68, compared to 0.50 for BMD alone and 0.32 for T-score (p < .05).7 Furthermore, the tool accurately classified 94% of specimens using a fracture load threshold of 3400 N, in contrast to the 38% classification accuracy using the T-score. Despite this, SSAM for hip fracture prediction requires anatomical landmarks to capture its shape, which is traditionally a manual process that limits the tool’s scalability and operational efficiency. Subsequent work in the lab explored a prototype application of CNN-based contour detection on DXA images from a subsample of longitudinal databases, including the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA) and CaMos, but stopped at the segmentation stage and did not produce landmark coordinates suitable for downstream SSAM analysis.26 The present study extends this line of work in 3 ways: (1) it couples contour segmentation with a geometric landmarking step to generate a fixed, ordered set of coordinates consumable by SSAM; (2) it trains and evaluates the pipeline on a larger and more heterogeneous dataset drawn from 3 source cohorts with different scanners; and (3) it assesses the clinical utility of the resulting landmarks through a paired comparison against manual landmarks in fracture risk prediction.

Prior automated approaches on DXA fall into 2 broad families: shape-model fitting directly to image intensities27 and learned landmark regression regularized by a statistical shape model.28–30 The BoneFinder tool exemplifies the latter, using random-forest regression-voting to place a dense set of landmarks (a mixture of anatomical reference points and evenly spaced subsets) along the proximal femur contour, with a point distribution model supporting anatomical plausibility.29 The present pipeline takes a different route. The U-Net first produces an explicit binary segmentation of the femur, and the landmark coordinates are then placed by a deterministic geometric rule along that segmented contour. Three distinctions follow. First, the learned component is a CNN trained on pixel-wise masks, rather than a random-forest ensemble trained on landmark offsets. This choice shifts the training requirement toward contour masks and allows the segmentation step to be inspected directly as an image. The use of U-Net CNN allows the present technique to be used as a tool to be integrated easily into any SSAM pipelines without the need for a large labeled dataset. Second, shape plausibility is not enforced by a pretrained shape model but is constrained only by the segmentation mask itself. This removes a regularization mechanism that can recover from locally weak image evidence, though it also avoids the shape model biasing landmark placement toward the training distribution. Third, the resulting landmark set is purely equidistant rather than anchored to anatomical features, which is well matched to the dense-correspondence requirements of SSAM but less suited to the computation of named geometric indices such as neck-shaft angle or hip axis length. Each design, therefore, carries its own trade-offs, and our choice reflects the specific goal of producing inputs for SSAM-based fracture risk prediction.

A further consideration in the design of the pipeline is that it was trained and evaluated exclusively on DXA rather than transferring a model developed for higher-resolution modalities, such as CT or MRI.31 While deep-learning segmentation of the proximal femur is well established on CT32,33 and has been applied to MRI,34 these modalities are not the ones used in routine osteoporosis assessment or large-scale population studies of bone health. DXA remains the clinical standard on both counts. Training directly on DXA ensures that the learned features reflect the contrast, projection geometry, and soft tissue overlap characteristic of this modality, rather than features learned from cross-sectional imaging that may not transfer cleanly. This alignment between training data and deployment settings is a precondition for integrating the pipeline into existing DXA-based fracture risk workflows such as SSAM.

The U-Net model produced accurate segmentations of the femoral contour, verified through both quantitative metrics and visual inspection. The quantitative metrics on the test set indicated that the U-Net model has an overall high and consistent predictive ability. Nuances in errors were further examined through visual inspection, where the analysis highlighted recurring patterns in segmentation performance. Visual inspection revealed high accuracy in tracing the FN and the intertrochanteric area, the two predominant sites of hip fracture. Clinically, approximately 50% of hip fractures occur at the FN, and about 38%-50% occur in the intertrochanteric area, which makes accurate segmentation in these regions clinically critical.35,36 As expected, most deviations occurred near the femoral head and subtrochanteric regions. The inconsistency in the femoral head seemed to be most affected by the quality of the DXA scan, such as reduced contrast, ambiguous borders, and structural complexity. These errors were especially prevalent in images, where the hip overlapped ambiguously with the pelvis in the original DXA projections.

The subtrochanteric region presented a distinct challenge, with inaccuracies likely stemming from variability in the ground truth images. Importantly, these images were collected from 3 different sources with varying scan positioning and settings, better reflecting real-world heterogeneity. While this variability could affect the amount of subtrochanteric region displayed, it also provided an opportunity to assess the model’s robustness and generalizability across diverse imaging conditions. To mitigate potential inconsistencies, the subtrochanteric region was cropped using geometric properties in the subsequent landmarking stage, where the lowest bounds of the landmark were set more consistently. Despite these localized difficulties, the model demonstrated strong performance across a diverse test set. Its encoder–decoder structure with skip connections effectively balanced fine-grained boundary detection with broader contextual understanding, demonstrating its ability to generalize across heterogeneous datasets. The use of interpolation and reference-based anchoring helped the landmark placements to remain consistent across varying image qualities and scan parameters. In the context of fracture risk modeling using SSAM, the automated landmarks achieved a test AUC of 0.831 (95% CI: 0.698-0.965), compared with 0.780 (95% CI: 0.635-0.925) for manual landmarks on the same held-out subjects. A paired DeLong test showed no statistically significant difference between the two approaches (p = .53), and both produced identical confusion matrices at the Youden-optimized threshold (accuracy 71.8%, sensitivity 64.0%, and specificity 85.7%). Taken together, these results support the non-inferiority of the automated pipeline for SSAM-based fracture risk discrimination.

Despite these findings, there are some limitations that should be acknowledged. The final fracture prediction was evaluated and compared on a single dataset (CaMos) that currently had the highest compatibility with the SSAM pipeline. While this dataset included images from multiple institutions and scanner settings, it may not fully reflect the diversity of imaging protocols, patient populations, and scanner variants found in broader clinical practice. Because the three datasets differ in scanner manufacturer, patient positioning protocols, and the extent of visible subtrochanteric shaft, contour characteristics learned from one source may not generalize perfectly to another. Based on our error analysis, the femoral head boundary would likely be the region most affected when applying the pipeline to an unseen source with a substantially different scanner or positioning protocol. Although training on a heterogeneous dataset spanning two scanner manufacturers and three clinical cohorts provides a degree of built-in robustness, we would recommend that users retrain or fine-tune the segmentation model with a representative subset from any new data source and validate the downstream SSAM performance, rather than assuming direct transferability. Relatedly, the paired comparison between manual and automated landmarks was performed on 39 subjects, producing wide confidence intervals (spanning roughly 0.13-0.14 AUC units for each method). The study was therefore adequately powered to detect non-inferiority in the observed range but not to exclude small differences between methods, and the need to replicate on larger independent test sets remains. As typical machine learning models require at least thousands of images for training, broader external validation with larger datasets is required to establish greater generalizability of the SSAM. The current landmarking strategy emphasizes spatial uniformity rather than the identification of fixed anatomical reference points. While this is advantageous for SSAM, it limits direct applicability to use cases requiring precise anatomical correspondence. An automated contouring method may also lead to novel sources of dataset variance otherwise absent in high-quality manual segmentations. With a larger dataset, this variance and its effect on the final prediction would be difficult to isolate and identify. Additionally, since the segmentation model is already trained, its predictions are deterministic at the time of inference. Therefore, the present tool is unable to produce inherent uncertainty estimates and additional development to create an explicit measure of confidence, particularly for challenging or low-quality scans, would inform clinicians to make more comprehensive decisions and increase trust in the tool’s predictions.

Overall, this study demonstrates that fully automated femoral landmarking from DXA scans, using a combination of U-Net segmentation and geometric landmarking, can achieve performance non-inferior to traditional manual approaches in the context of SSAM-based fracture risk prediction. The approach is robust across varying scan qualities, anatomical variability, and heterogeneous datasets, effectively reducing human-induced variability while maintaining anatomically relevant landmark placements. The FN and intertrochanteric area, 2 regions responsible for the majority of hip fractures, were accurately segmented and landmarked. By standardizing image-derived inputs and automating key steps in the pipeline, this work advances the scalability, reproducibility, and consistency of morphometric analysis in DXA-based fracture risk assessment.
