Self-inconsistency and attachment insecurity mediate and moderate the association between anxiety and reasons for living in Chinese college students
Authors: Lin Li, Xiaoyue Zhao, Xiaobin Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuicide remains a major public health concern, and reasons for living are an important protective factor against suicide risk. However, the processes through which anxiety relates to reasons for living are not well specified. Drawing on self-system and attachment theories, this cross-sectional study tested whether self-inconsistency and attachment avoidance mediate, and attachment anxiety moderates, the association between anxiety and reasons for living among Chinese college students. Participants were 416 undergraduates who completed measures of anxiety, reasons for living, self-inconsistency, and adult attachment. Bootstrapped regression analyses (5000 resamples) indicated that higher anxiety was associated with fewer reasons for living. These relationships were mediated by self-inconsistency and attachment avoidance, and moderated by attachment anxiety, such that the negative effect of anxiety on reasons for living was stronger at higher levels of attachment anxiety. These findings indicate that intrapersonal self-system disruption and attachment-related coping processes account for a significant portion of the association between anxiety and diminished reasons for living, underscoring the distinct contributions of attachment avoidance and attachment anxiety in college populations.Keywords: Reasons for living, Anxiety, Self-inconsistency, Attachment avoidance, Attachment anxietySubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

Suicide is a leading cause of death among individuals aged 15–29 worldwide and remains a major public health concern. College students are at a critical developmental stage marked by increasing academic, interpersonal, and identity-related demands, which may heighten psychological distress and vulnerability to crisis. Consistent with this view, prior research has documented a relatively high prevalence of suicidal ideation among college students, underscoring suicide-related risk as a salient concern in this population1–3. Although suicide research has traditionally emphasized psychopathological risk factors such as depression and anxiety, comparatively less attention has been devoted to protective psychological resources that sustain individuals during periods of crisis4. In this context, reasons for living (RFL)—positive beliefs and expectations that motivate individuals to continue living despite psychological pain—have emerged as a critical construct in suicide prevention research5–7. Compared with approaches focused solely on suicidal ideation, RFL shifts attention toward life-sustaining motivations, thereby offering clearer implications for risk identification and intervention targeting.

Anxiety is robustly associated with suicide-related behaviors and psychological crisis8–10 and may erode reasons for living by disrupting future-oriented expectations, meaning in life, and social connectedness11. However, integrative examinations of how anxiety influences reasons for living through both intrapersonal self-system processes (e.g., self-inconsistency) and interpersonal attachment-related mechanisms remain limited. Drawing on self-system and attachment theories, the present study tested a moderated parallel mediation model to clarify psychological mechanisms linking anxiety to reasons for living among college students (see Fig. 1). Self-inconsistency and attachment avoidance were specified as parallel mediators, and attachment anxiety was examined as a moderator of the direct association between anxiety and reasons for living. By integrating intrapersonal and interpersonal perspectives, this study aimed to elucidate key mechanisms underlying the relationship between anxiety and reasons for living and to inform campus mental health strategies for students with elevated anxiety and insecure attachment characteristics.

Conceptual model of current study.

The association between anxiety and reasons for living

Reasons for living reflect individuals’ positive appraisals of life meaning, future hope, interpersonal responsibility, and perceived capacity to cope with psychological pain5,12. Extensive evidence indicates that higher levels of RFL are associated with lower suicidal ideation and reduced suicide risk, highlighting their protective role in suicide prevention13–15. Anxiety has been consistently linked to elevated suicide risk and to diminished reasons for living, with these associations operating through impairments in cognitive processing and emotion regulation that are themselves linked to reduced positive evaluations of life and the future16,17. Empirical studies further suggest that individuals with higher anxiety report fewer reasons for living18,19. From a cognitive–emotional perspective, anxiety activates threat-focused attentional biases and maladaptive coping strategies, such as catastrophizing and experiential avoidance, which may weaken meaning-making processes and future-oriented motivation20,21. Accordingly, we hypothesized that anxiety would be negatively associated with reasons for living (H1).

The mediating role of self-inconsistency

Self-consistency and congruence represent the degree to which individuals’ self-concepts are integrated with lived experiences, needs, and behaviors, serving as a core indicator of self-system integration22,23. When this integration is disrupted, individuals may experience heightened internal conflict and negative self-evaluation, manifested as self-inconsistency. Elevated anxiety is associated with compromised self-system integration through its links to intensified negative self-schemas, increased discrepancies between actual and ideal selves, and avoidant coping strategies, all of which are in turn associated with heightened self-inconsistency24. In turn, greater self-inconsistency has been linked to diminished well-being, reduced meaning in life, and impaired coping under stress25,26. Such disruptions may weaken the internal psychological foundations that support reasons for living, including self-worth, goal coherence, and openness to experience27,28. Accordingly, we hypothesized that self-inconsistency would mediate the association between anxiety and reasons for living (H2).

The role of insecure attachment

Attachment theory conceptualizes attachment insecurity along two dimensions: attachment avoidance and attachment anxiety, which are associated with distinct emotion-regulation strategies and interpersonal motives29,30. Differentiating their respective roles is essential for understanding how anxiety influences reasons for living. Attachment avoidance is characterized by emotional suppression, interpersonal disengagement, and reduced reliance on others, typically enacted through deactivating strategies aimed at downregulating distress30. Although such strategies may provide short-term relief under heightened anxiety, this short-term benefit may come at the cost of longer-term social connectedness and access to emotional support, which are in turn linked to diminished meaning in life and reasons for living31,32. We therefore hypothesized that attachment avoidance would mediate the association between anxiety and reasons for living (H3).In contrast, attachment anxiety involves heightened sensitivity to relational threat, excessive reassurance seeking, and emotional amplification. Individuals high in attachment anxiety tend to engage in hyperactivating strategies, including rumination and catastrophizing, which may intensify the detrimental effects of anxiety on meaning-making and life-sustaining beliefs33,34. Accordingly, we hypothesized that attachment anxiety would moderate the direct association between anxiety and reasons for living, such that this negative association would be stronger at higher levels of attachment anxiety (H4).

Method

Participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted with undergraduates recruited from a university in China. A total of 425 questionnaires were returned; after applying prespecified exclusion criteria, 416 responses were retained for analysis (valid response rate = 97.90%). Participants were 162 men (39.00%) and 254 women (61.00%), aged 17.70 to 25.80 years (M = 21.47, SD = 0.98). Additional demographics are reported in Table 1. Questionnaires were completed in person during regular class time in a standardized classroom setting. Participants were recruited from intact classes across multiple academic disciplines to enhance sample diversity. Trained graduate students in psychology administered the survey, providing uniform verbal instructions to ensure consistency. The structured environment helped minimize potential distractions. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation, and the questionnaires were completed anonymously. Data were used solely for scientific research purposes. Ethical approval for this study was granted under approval number NWNU-2024-102,030.

Measures

Participants completed four self-report questionnaires and provided demographic information. The measures included the Self-Consistency and Congruence Scale (SCCS), the Experiences in Close Relationships scale (ECR), the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), and the Reasons for Living Inventory for Adolescents (RFL-A). All instruments were validated Chinese versions adapted following standardized cross-cultural translation and adaptation procedures35 and have demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties in Chinese college student samples.

Self-rating anxiety scale (SAS)

Anxiety symptoms were assessed with the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS)36. The SAS includes 20 items rated on a 4-point scale (1 = none or a little of the time, 4 = most or all of the time) referring to the past week. Five positively worded items were reverse scored; the remaining items were scored in the direction of anxiety. Sample items include "I feel more nervous and anxious than usual" and "I feel that everything is all right and nothing bad will happen." Item scores were summed to create a total score, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety. In the present study, the SAS demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.81), which is consistent with reliability estimates reported in previous research, supporting the appropriateness of this measure for the current sample37.

Reasons for living inventory for adolescents (RFL-A)

Reasons for living were measured using the Reasons for Living Inventory for Adolescents (RFL-A)38. The Chinese version contains 32 items rated on a 6-point scale ranging from 1 (not at all important) to 6 (extremely important). The scale comprises five subscales: Family Alliance (FA; e.g., "I feel emotionally connected to my family"), Suicide-Related Concerns (SRC; e.g., "I am afraid of suicide"), Future Optimism (FO; e.g., "I want to achieve my plans and goals in the future"), Peer Acceptance and Support (PAS; e.g., "I feel accepted and liked by my close friends"), and Self-Acceptance (SA; e.g., "I accept myself"). Higher total scores reflect stronger perceived reasons for living. The RFL-A demonstrated good internal consistency in the present sample (Cronbach’s α = 0 0.86), consistent with previous research15.

Self-consistency and congruence scale (SCCS)

Self-inconsistency was assessed with the Self-Consistency and Congruence Scale (SCCS)39,40.The SCCS consists of 35 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The Self-Consistency and Congruence Scale consists of three dimensions: Dissonance between self and experience (16 items; e.g., "People around me often feel that my views of myself are somewhat contradictory"), Flexibility of self (12 items; e.g., "Sometimes I am dissatisfied with my performance in certain areas"), and Stereotypicality of self (7 items; e.g., "Once I form an opinion about something, I am unlikely to change it"). Items in the Flexibility of self subscale are reverse scored. Subscale scores are summed to produce a total score, with higher scores indicating greater self-inconsistency and incongruence. For clarity and consistency, the total score was interpreted as an index of self-inconsistency throughout the Results and Discussion sections. The scale has good reliability and validity in Previous studies22.The SCCS demonstrated good internal consistency in the present study (Cronbach’s α = 0.82).

Experiences in close relationships (ECR)

Adult attachment was measured with the Experiences in Close Relationships scale (ECR; Chinese version)41. The ECR is a 36-item self-report measure assessing two dimensions of attachment insecurity: attachment avoidance (18 items; e.g., "I prefer not to show a partner how I feel deep down") and attachment anxiety (18 items; e.g., "I worry about being abandoned"). Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Dimension scores are calculated by averaging responses within each subscale, with higher scores indicating greater attachment insecurity. Previous studies have demonstrated good reliability for the two subscales of the Experiences in Close Relationships Scale42. In the present sample, internal consistency was acceptable for both attachment avoidance (Cronbach’s α = 0 0.82) and attachment anxiety (Cronbach’s α = 0 0.77).

Statistical analyses

SPSS 27.0 was used to assess common method bias and to conduct descriptive statistics and correlational analyses. Next, bootstrap analyses were performed using Hayes’ (2013) PROCESS macro (Version 4.1) with 5000 resamples and 95% CIs43. After controlling for gender, age, and family structure, PROCESS Model 4 was used to test the parallel mediation effects of self-inconsistency and attachment avoidance, and PROCESS Model 5 was used to test the moderating effect of attachment anxiety on the direct path from anxiety to reasons for living. To reduce the risk of multicollinearity, continuous variables were mean-centered prior to the computation of interaction terms. Regression results are reported as unstandardized coefficients with corresponding standard errors and 95% CIs.

Results

Common method deviation test

Common method bias was examined using Harman’s single-factor test. Ten factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, and the first factor accounted for 28.72% of the total variance (< 40%), suggesting no serious common method bias. Nevertheless, given the limited sensitivity of this approach, the findings should be interpreted cautiously in light of the study design.

As shown in Table 1, independent samples t-tests revealed no significant gender or family structure differences across any of the psychological variables (all p > 0.05). Among the demographic variables, age was significantly negatively correlated with reasons for living (r = − 0.108, p = 0.028) and significantly positively correlated with attachment avoidance (r = 0.153, p = 0.002).

Parallel mediation effects of self-inconsistency and attachment avoidance

A parallel mediation model tested whether self-inconsistency and attachment avoidance accounted for the association between anxiety and reasons for living, controlling for gender, age, and family structure. Anxiety was positively associated with self-inconsistency, b = 0.859, SE = 0.051, t = 16.871, p < 0.001, and with attachment avoidance, b = 0.021, SE = 0.003, t = 6.119, p < 0.001. Anxiety was negatively associated with reasons for living (total effect), b = − 1.103, SE = 0.122, t = − 9.015, p < 0.001. When both mediators were included, self-inconsistency (b = − 0.516, SE = 0.116, t = − 4.441, p < 0.001) and attachment avoidance (b = − 9.322, SE = 1.715, t = − 5.437, p < 0.001) were each negatively associated with reasons for living, and the direct effect of anxiety remained significant, b = − 0.463, SE = 0.147, t = − 3.153, p = 0.002. Bootstrapped indirect effects were significant via self-inconsistency (indirect effect = − 0.443, 95% CI [− 0.664, − 0.227]) and via attachment avoidance (indirect effect = − 0.197, 95% CI [− 0.302, − 0.109]), see Tables 3 and 4. Thus, self-inconsistency and attachment avoidance served as parallel mediators in the association between anxiety and reasons for living.

Moderating effect of attachment anxiety

To test whether attachment anxiety moderated the association between anxiety and reasons for living, the Anxiety × Attachment Anxiety interaction was entered while controlling for the mediators and covariates. The interaction term was significant (see Table 5), b = − 0.212, SE = 0.088, t = − 2.423, p = 0.016, 95% CI [− 0.384, − 0.040], indicating that the negative association between anxiety and reasons for living was stronger at higher attachment anxiety. Simple slopes analyses showed that anxiety negatively predicted reasons for living at high attachment anxiety (+ 1 SD), b = − 0.619, t = − 4.058, p < 0.001, but not at low attachment anxiety (− 1 SD), b = − 0.191, t = − 0.963, p = 0.336, see Fig. 2.

Discussion

Grounded in self-system and attachment theories, the present study tested a moderated parallel mediation model to clarify psychological mechanisms linking anxiety to reasons for living among college students. The findings showed that anxiety was negatively associated with reasons for living, that self-inconsistency and attachment avoidance operated as parallel mediators, and that attachment anxiety moderated the direct association between anxiety and reasons for living. The above findings provide complementary evidence for understanding the mechanisms underlying college students’ reasons for living, from a dual-pathway perspective that integrates intra-individual self-integration and interpersonal attachment processing. Given the cross-sectional design, the proposed mechanisms should be further examined using longitudinal and intervention-based approaches.

Anxiety and reasons for living

The observed negative association between anxiety and reasons for living suggests that elevated anxiety is associated with a reduced capacity to sustain life-affirming beliefs and the motivation to continue living. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence18, and further underscores the risk-relevant role of anxiety in shaping reasons for living among college students. From a process perspective, heightened anxiety is associated with the occupation of cognitive resources and disruptions in emotion regulation, which in turn co-occur with a greater reliance on maladaptive strategies such as rumination and experiential avoidance. Persistent attention to threat cues, combined with avoidance of internal experiences, is linked to diminished positive self-related resources, including self-worth and perceived social competence7. In addition, anxiety is often accompanied by catastrophic processing and negative outcome expectancies. These cognitive patterns are, in turn, linked to lower levels of hope, sense of purpose, and planning capacity—core components of reasons for living44. For college students engaged in identity exploration and future planning, repeated experiences of the future as uncontrollable or hopeless may destabilize life goals and perceived meaning, thereby weakening motivation to continue living45. More broadly, within cognitive–emotional frameworks, anxiety-related disruptions in attention, executive functioning, and everyday adaptation may reduce individuals’ ability to cope effectively with academic, interpersonal, and life stressors, ultimately heightening vulnerability to psychological crisis20,21,46. Together, these processes highlight anxiety reduction and the modification of maladaptive coping patterns as foundational targets for interventions aimed at strengthening reasons for living in college populations.

The mediating role of self-inconsistency

The mediating role of self-inconsistency indicates that anxiety is linked to reduced reasons for living through its association with disruptions in self-system integration, rather than solely through its relationship with intensified negative affect. Elevated anxiety may heighten internal conflict and vigilance, consuming cognitive resources needed to integrate experiences into a coherent self-concept. Under such conditions, individuals may become increasingly preoccupied with negative self-schemas and discrepancies between lived experiences and self-expectations, thereby reinforcing self-inconsistency and weakening internal coherence47. In turn, heightened self-inconsistency may erode the psychological foundations that support reasons for living. Persistent internal conflict and self-negation are closely linked to existential distress and reduced psychological resilience, limiting individuals’ capacity to sustain positive appraisals of life meaning and future value22. By contrast, a more integrated self-system allows individuals to assimilate adverse experiences without destabilizing their sense of self, facilitating meaning reconstruction and adaptive coping48,49. Related research has also supported the pathway of “anxiety–self-inconsistency–impaired meaning,” thereby providing cross-contextual theoretical support for the present findings50. Together, these findings highlight self-system integration as a central intrapersonal mechanism through which anxiety may relate to diminished reasons for living.

The mediating role of attachment avoidance

Attachment avoidance emerged as a parallel mediator, suggesting that the association between anxiety and reasons for living operates through an interpersonal pathway characterized by emotional distancing and reduced engagement with others. Heightened anxiety may reinforce avoidant coping strategies that suppress emotional expression and limit reliance on interpersonal support, thereby providing short-term relief from distress while constraining access to relational resources over time51–53. Such sustained interpersonal disengagement may gradually erode social connectedness and shared meaning, both of which are central to maintaining reasons for living54. Moreover, individuals high in attachment avoidance may disengage not only from negative emotional experiences but also from positive relational interactions, thereby limiting opportunities for emotional nourishment and meaning in life55. From the perspective of internal working models, attachment avoidance may reinforce beliefs that others are unreliable or that closeness is unsafe, perpetuating anxiety and reinforcing avoidant behavior in a self-maintaining cycle56,57. As a relatively stable interpersonal coping pattern, attachment avoidance thus represents an important indirect pathway linking anxiety to reduced reasons for living.

The moderating role of attachment anxiety

Attachment anxiety moderated the direct association between anxiety and reasons for living, such that the detrimental effect of anxiety was stronger at higher levels of attachment anxiety. This pattern suggests that heightened relational sensitivity and emotional amplification may intensify the adverse impact of anxiety on meaning-related processes. Under elevated anxiety, individuals high in attachment anxiety are more likely to engage in hyperactivating strategies, including rumination, catastrophizing, and persistent reassurance seeking, which may increase emotional exhaustion and frustration when relational needs are unmet30. As anxiety intensifies, these hyperactivating strategies may become increasingly dysregulated, undermining the capacity to derive security and meaning from interpersonal interactions. Deterioration in interaction quality and repeated experiences of perceived rejection may form part of a mutually reinforcing cycle with negative appraisals regarding self and relationships, and this constellation of factors is linked to lower levels of reasons for living42. Notably, the positive main effect of attachment anxiety observed in the present study may be tentatively interpreted as reflecting the context-dependent, short-term functionality of hyperactivation strategies58. Longitudinal evidence suggesting that heightened anxiety sensitivity may precede sustained attachment anxiety provides additional support for this amplification process59. Notably, this moderating pattern underscores functional differentiation within insecure attachment: whereas attachment avoidance primarily operates as an indirect interpersonal pathway, attachment anxiety shapes the conditions under which anxiety exerts its strongest adverse effects on reasons for living.

Conclusion

Grounded in self-system and attachment theories, the present study tested a moderated parallel mediation model to clarify psychological mechanisms linking anxiety to reasons for living among college students. The findings indicate that higher anxiety is associated with fewer perceived reasons for living, operating in part through increased self-inconsistency and attachment avoidance. Moreover, attachment anxiety amplified the negative association between anxiety and reasons for living. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the links between anxiety and reduced reasons for living are explained by two functionally distinct psychological systems: compromised self-system integration, which is reflected in self-inconsistency, and deactivating interpersonal coping strategies, which are reflected in attachment avoidance. By differentiating the mediating role of attachment avoidance from the moderating role of attachment anxiety, this study delineates functional differentiation within insecure attachment and offers an integrative framework linking intrapersonal and interpersonal mechanisms in understanding reasons for living. Clinically, the findings highlight anxiety as a salient risk indicator and suggest that interventions may benefit from simultaneously addressing self-system integration and attachment-related coping patterns, with attention to individual differences in attachment insecurity.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and limits conclusions regarding temporal ordering among anxiety, self-inconsistency, attachment processes, and reasons for living; longitudinal, cross-lagged, and experimental or intervention-based designs are needed to examine causal pathways and dynamic processes. Second, the use of a nonclinical sample from a single Chinese university constrains generalizability to clinical or high-risk populations, other cultural contexts, and different developmental stages, highlighting the need for more diverse and multi-site samples. Third, reliance on self-report measures may introduce biases related to social desirability, recall, and current affective states; future studies would benefit from multi-method and multi-informant approaches to enhance measurement robustness and ecological validity. A further limitation is the empirical overlap among key constructs. Moderate-to-high correlations—particularly between anxiety and self-inconsistency, and between self-inconsistency and attachment avoidance—suggest that self-report measures may capture shared variance reflecting underlying psychological distress rather than entirely distinct entities. These overlaps indicate that the constructs are not empirically independent, potentially pointing to a broader dimension of disengagement from self and others. Future research should clarify whether these constructs represent unique facets of a higher-order factor or demonstrate differential predictive validity. Importantly, another limitation concerns the omission of potentially relevant variables, most notably depressive symptoms, from the model. Given the substantial comorbidity between anxiety and depression, the observed effects of anxiety may be partially confounded by shared variance with depressive symptoms that was not controlled for28,60. Without such statistical control, it remains unclear whether the mediating roles of self-inconsistency and attachment avoidance reflect unique contributions of anxiety or are influenced by unmeasured depressive symptomatology. Accordingly, future research should incorporate depressive symptoms and other key covariates to more accurately disentangle the mechanisms through which anxiety affects reasons for living.
