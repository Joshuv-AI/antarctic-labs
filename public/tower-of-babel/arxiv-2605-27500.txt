Constraining Gravitational Wave Memory with Hierarchical Inference
Keefe Mitman, Maximiliano Isi, Will M. Farr

Abstract
With the multitude of gravitational wave observations that have been made in the past ten years, probing the dynamical and nonlinear nature of strong gravity is becoming more and more feasible. One promising way to test the nonlinear nature of Einstein’s theory of general relativity (GR) is through the gravitational wave null memory effect: a nonlinear prediction of GR which corresponds to initially comoving observers being permanently displaced due to a burst of gravitational radiation. Previous studies have shown that, while it is unlikely that the memory effect will be observed in a single event by the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA (LVK) detectors, evidence for memory in the population of LVK events should be attainable after ∼ \sim 2,000 gravitational wave detections. These works, however, largely relied on Bayes factors to perform their memory analyses: an approach that can depend sensitively on the analysis priors and, when naively multiplied across many events, can even favor incorrect conclusions. In this work, using the GWTC-4.0 catalog of binary black hole observations, we instead perform hierarchical Bayesian inference—which is not subject to the issues associated with Bayes factors—to measure the evidence for memory in current LVK observations. We find that we can constrain what we call the memory enhancement factor—the constant appearing in front of the contribution to the strain from the supermomentum flux—to 0.32 − 5.12 + 6.30 0.32^{+6.30}_{-5.12} (with ± \pm values denoting the 68% credible interval), consistent with its GR value of 1. We forecast that ∼ \sim 2,500 detections will be needed to constrain the memory enhancement factor away from zero at the 1 ​ σ 1\sigma level.

Methodology

III.1
Memory computation

As stated in Sec.
II
, standard LVK analyses do not use waveform models that contain the expected contribution from Eq. (
4
), which can be used as a proxy for memory. For such an analysis, the log-likelihood used to produce the public posteriors of parameters
\lambda
takes the form

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle d-R(\lambda)\,h(\lambda)\mid d-R(\lambda)\,h(\lambda)\right\rangle+C

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle d-\tilde{h}(\lambda)\mid d-\tilde{h}(\lambda)\right\rangle+C

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left\|d-\tilde{h}(\lambda)\right\|^{2}+C,

(8)

where
d
is the observed data,
h(\lambda)
is the waveform model evaluated at parameters
\lambda
,
R(\lambda)
is the detector response which includes the calibration and antenna patterns,
\tilde{h}(\lambda)\equiv R(\lambda)h(\lambda)
is the signal at the detector,

\displaystyle\langle h_{1}|h_{2}\rangle\equiv 4\mathrm{Re}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{1}^{*}(f)h_{2}(f)/S(f)\,{\rm d}f\right]

(9)

is the frequency-domain noise-weighted inner product with some noise power spectral density
S(f)
,
\left\|\cdot\right\|^{2}\equiv\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
, and
C
is a constant independent of the
\lambda

denote the residual between the data and the template, the log-likelihood simplifies to

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{2}\left\|r(\lambda)\right\|^{2}+C.

(10)

If we wish to include the missing contribution to the waveform models and thus also the effects of memory, since the memory can be computed directly from the usual waveform models using Eqs. (
6
) and (
4
), the log-likelihood becomes

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{2}\left\|r(\lambda)-\tilde{h}_{\mathrm{mem}}(\lambda)\right\|^{2}+C,

(11)

where
\tilde{h}_{\mathrm{mem}}
is
R(\lambda)h_{\mathrm{mem}}(\lambda)
for

the same

as was used in the original analysis. Crucially, this requires no new waveform model. But, if we wish to instead

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{2}\left\|r(\lambda)-A\,\tilde{h}_{\mathrm{mem}}(\lambda)\right\|^{2}+C.

Computing the posterior on
A

Rather than sampling this parameter directly, which would require reanalyzing the data from scratch, we can take advantage of the structure of Eq. (

Conveniently, Eq. (

\displaystyle\mu=\dfrac{\mathrm{Re}\left[\langle\tilde{h}_{\mathrm{mem}}(\lambda)|r(\lambda)\rangle\right]}{\left\|\tilde{h}_{\mathrm{mem}}(\lambda)\right\|^{2}},

\displaystyle\sigma=\left\|\tilde{h}_{\mathrm{mem}}(\lambda)\right\|^{-1}.

(14)

Marginalizing out the memory enhancement factor of the likelihood in Eq. (

\displaystyle\log\overline{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=\log\int\exp(\log\mathcal{L})\,{\rm d}A

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(||r(\lambda)||^{2}-\sigma^{-2}\mu^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2\pi\sigma^{-2}\right)+C.

The sample-level hyperparameters
\mu

\sigma

\lambda

\displaystyle\log w

\displaystyle=\log\overline{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda)-\log\mathcal{L}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{-2}\mu^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2\pi\sigma^{-2}\right).

(16)

With the values of

\Lambda_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{s}\dfrac{p(\lambda_{s}\mid\Lambda_{\lambda})}{W(\lambda_{s})}\int p(A\mid\lambda_{s})\,p(A\mid\Lambda_{A})\,{\rm d}A.

(22)

To further simplify this expression, we need to assume a specific population model for the memory parameter
A
.

To establish whether there is evidence for memory in the GW population as predicted by GR, we want to ascertain whether
A=1
identically for all systems, i.e., whether the population distribution for
A
is simply
p(A\mid\Lambda_{A})=\delta(A-1)
. In alternative scenarios, we might expect that there is no memory, i.e.,
p(A\mid\Lambda)=\delta(A)
, or that different systems manifest different degrees of memory relative to the default GR prediction, resulting in some arbitrary distribution of
A
with some variance.
To capture the mean and variance of the distribution of
A
, we follow Ref.
Isi et al. 2019
and set our population model for
A
to be a Gaussian of arbitrary mean
\mu_{\Lambda}
and scale
\sigma_{\Lambda}
, such that
p(A\mid\Lambda_{A})=\mathcal{N}(A\mid\mu_{\Lambda},\sigma_{\Lambda})
and
\Lambda_{A}=\{\mu_{\Lambda},\,\sigma_{\Lambda}\}
.
This model serves as a null test, by which consistency with
(\mu_{\Lambda}=1,\sigma_{\Lambda}=0)
is required for agreement with the GR expectation of
A=1
.

Under this Gaussian population model for the memory, the integral over the memory parameter
A
in Eq. (
22
) can be performed analytically, yielding

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{s}\dfrac{p(\lambda_{s}\mid\Lambda_{\lambda})}{W(\lambda_{s})}\hskip-3.0pt\int\mathcal{N}(A\mid\mu_{s},\sigma_{s})\,\mathcal{N}(A\mid\mu_{\Lambda},\sigma_{\Lambda})\,{\rm d}A

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{s}\dfrac{p(\lambda_{s}\mid\Lambda_{\lambda})}{W(\lambda_{s})}\,\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{s}\,\Big|\,\mu_{\Lambda},\sqrt{\sigma_{s}^{2}+\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}}\right),

(23)

where we used Eq. (
17
) to replace
p(A\mid\lambda_{s})
in Eq. (
22
). Equation (
III.2
) thus provides a straightforward way to evaluate the hierarchical likelihood
without sampling the latent variable
A
for each sample. Instead, each sample contributes to the likelihood through a Gaussian term that compares its sample-level memory mean
\mu_{s}
, Eq. (
13
), with the memory population mean
\mu_{\Lambda}
, with a variance that is a sum in quadrature of the sample-level memory measurement uncertainty
\sigma_{s}
, Eq. (
14
), and the overall memory population uncertainty
\sigma_{\Lambda}
.

Finally, while Eq. (
III.2
) provides the likelihood for an individual event, the log-likelihood for a catalog of some number of
N
events is, up to a constant, given by

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}(\Lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Lambda)-N\log\xi(\Lambda),

(24)

where
\xi(\Lambda)
is the detection efficiency, which encodes the detection biases
Mandel et al. 2019
.
We assume there is no selection based on the memory parameter
A
, given that we expect the memory piece to have a particularly small effect on the signal-to-noise ratio
Magee et al. 2024
.
The hyperposterior on both
\mu_{\Lambda}
and
\sigma_{\Lambda}
, obtained by sampling Eq. (
24
) together with some hyperprior
p(\mu_{\Lambda},\sigma_{\Lambda})
, encodes our best knowledge of the most likely value of
A
and the degree of variance consistent with the observed population.

For the astrophysical part of the model, i.e.,
p(\lambda\mid\Lambda_{\lambda})
, we adopt a parametric form based on the LVK analysis of GWTC-4.0 that was performed in Ref.
Abac et al. 2025b
. We factorize the BBH parameter density for the primary mass
m_{1}
, mass ratio
q=m_{2}/m_{1}
, redshift
z
, spin magnitudes
\chi_{1/2}
and spin tilts
\theta_{1/2}
. The primary mass
m_{1}\in[3,300]\,M_{\odot}
is described by a three-component mixture of a continuous broken power law and two Gaussian peaks truncated to the same support, with mixture weights drawn from a flat Dirichlet prior. The mass ratio follows the conditional power law
p(q\mid m_{1})\propto q^{\beta}
on
[3\,M_{\odot}/m_{1},\,1]
, while the redshift distribution scales as
(1+z)^{\lambda_{z}}\,\mathrm{d}V_{c}/\mathrm{d}z\,(1+z)^{-1}
for
V_{c}
the comoving volume in a Planck 2015 cosmology
Ade et al. 2016
. The cosine spin tilts
(\cos\theta_{1},\cos\theta_{2})
are jointly modeled as a mixture of an isotropic component and a product of truncated Gaussians on
[-1,1]
with shared means and widths, which follows the non-independently but identically-distributed prescription used for GWTC-4.0; each spin magnitude
\chi_{i}
is independently drawn from a Gaussian truncated to
[0,1]
with shared mean and width. We place broad uniform priors on these hyperparameters and account for potential selection effects through the rate-marginalized factor
\xi(\Lambda)^{-N}
(see Eq. (
24

Essick et al. 2025
;
LIGO Scientific Collaboration et al. 2025
. See Appendix
A
for more details.

We draw samples from the hyperposterior by using the No-U-Turn Hamiltonian Monte Carlo algorithm
Hoffman and Gelman 2014
as implemented in
NumPyro

Bingham et al. 2019
;
Phan et al. 2019

IV.2
Forecasts

To forecast the number of events that will be needed to constrain
A
away from zero, i.e., to confidently measure the memory, we will construct mock catalogs of future GW events by performing the following analysis. First, we note that from the hierarchical inference performed in Sec.
IV.1
, we have a set of hypersamples
\{\mu_{\Lambda}^{(k)},\sigma_{\Lambda}^{(k)}\}
that were inferred from the current
N_{0}=\text{152 }
observations. And, for a future catalog of size
N
, the uncertainty on both
\mu_{\Lambda}
and
\sigma_{\Lambda}
will shrink as
1/\sqrt{N}
. Therefore, for some pseudo-catalog realization indexed by
r
, we can draw a catalog-level estimate for our hyperparameters around their GR values as

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{r}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(1,s_{\mu}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,s_{\sigma^{2}}\right),

(25)

where

\displaystyle s_{\mu}

\displaystyle\equiv\Delta\mu_{0}\sqrt{\frac{N_{0}}{N}},

(26a)

\displaystyle s_{\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv\Delta\sigma_{0}^{2}\frac{N_{0}}{N}

(26b)

for
\Delta\mu_{0}
and
\Delta\sigma_{0}^{2}
the 68% half-widths computed from the current
\mu_{\Lambda}
and
\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}
posteriors. From these, we can then draw hyperposterior samples for this pseudo-catalog via

\displaystyle\mu_{\Lambda,r}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\hat{\mu}_{r},s_{\mu}\right),

(27a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\Lambda,r}^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{InvGamma}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)

(27b)

for

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\equiv 2+\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{4}}{s_{\sigma^{2}}^{2}},

(28a)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\equiv\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}(\alpha-1).

(28b)

The inverse-gamma distribution used to represent
\sigma_{\Lambda,r}^{2}
is constructed in this way so that it corresponds to a scaled inverse-

distribution whose expectation value and standard deviation match
\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}
and

and

V
Discussion

Gravitational wave memory is a key prediction of GR that is a natural target for ground-based GW detectors. It is of immense theoretical interest due to its connection to asymptotic symmetries and soft theorems and for its role in formulating a consistent theory of quantum gravity. Unfortunately, due to its low-frequency nature, it is an incredibly challenging effect to observe in individual events with the current LVK detectors. However, there is reason to believe that with enough observations, we can observe memory in a population of GW detections.

In this study, we used hierarchical Bayesian inference to measure the memory enhancement factor—the constant appearing in front of the contribution to the strain from the supermomentum flux, which is 1 in GR—across the population of GW events up to and including the recent GWTC-4.0 catalog. Unsurprisingly, we found that we can constrain this factor to
0.32^{+6.30}_{-5.12}
, which does not yield a confident measurement of the memory effect. Nonetheless, this work provides the first attempt to measure memory using hierarchical inference with a flexible model for the memory enhancement factor and thus the first analysis to successfully account for potential changes in the inferred BBH population due to memory. More generally, we also showed how to constrain the memory enhancement factor using future GWTCs and any generic waveform model, without sampling
A
, making this analysis very efficient. In slight disagreement with previous works, we find that we will likely need
\sim
2,500 BBH detections to constrain the memory enhancement factor away from zero at
1\sigma
. However, since this number is expected to possibly be reached by the fifth observing run of the LVK detectors, but certainly by the sixth observing run, we find that it is likely that we will measure the GW memory effect for the first time in a population within five to ten years.

Mitman et al. 2026a
;
Mitman et al. 2026b
under an open source license. This includes our estimates of the memory-augmented posteriors for each event.

where the factor of
(1+z)^{-1}
converts the detector-frame rate of mergers to the source frame. For this we use a flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology with
H_{0}=67.90
km/s/Mpc and
\Omega_{m}=0.3065

Ade et al. 2016

z\in[0,2.5]

A.4
Spin tilts

The cosines of the spin tilt angles,
\cos\theta_{1},\cos\theta_{2}\in[-1,1]
, are jointly modeled as a mixture between an isotropic component uniform on
[-1,1]^{2}
with density
1/4
, and a product of
[-1,1]
-truncated Gaussians with shared mean
\mu_{\theta}
and width
\sigma_{\theta}
,

\displaystyle p(\cos\theta_{1},\cos\theta_{2}\mid\Lambda_{\theta})=\;

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\mathrm{iso}}}{4}

(37)

\displaystyle\hskip-30.0pt+(1-f_{\mathrm{iso}})\prod_{i=1}^{2}\mathcal{N}_{[-1,1]}(\cos\theta_{i}\mid\mu_{\theta},\sigma_{\theta}).

This is the “non-independently but identically distributed” prescription of the GWTC-4.0
Gaussian Component Spins
model
Abac et al. 2025b
. The isotropic-mixture weight
f_{\mathrm{iso}}
allows for a sub-population of randomly oriented spins, while the shared
(\mu_{\theta},\sigma_{\theta})
tie the two component-spin orientations to a common preferred-alignment distribution.

A.5
Spin magnitudes

The two dimensionless spin magnitudes
\chi_{i}
are modeled using independent and identically distributed truncated Gaussians on
[0,1]
,

\displaystyle p(\chi_{i}\mid\mu_{\chi},\sigma_{\chi})=\mathcal{N}_{[0,1]}(\chi_{i}\mid\mu_{\chi},\sigma_{\chi})\,,\quad i\in\{1,2\},

(38)

with shared mean and width across the two components.

A.6
Selection function

Possible selection effects are accounted for through the detection efficiency
\xi(\Lambda)
in Eq. (
24
), which is evaluated as a Monte-Carlo average over the LVK Collaboration’s cumulative O1–O4a simulated-injection campaign
Essick et al. 2025
;
LIGO Scientific Collaboration et al. 2025
. Both this Monte-Carlo sum and the per-event
N
-sample sums approximating
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Lambda)
in Eq. (
III.2
) are combined with smooth penalties that suppress certain regions of the hyperparameter space where the corresponding effective sample sizes become too small for the estimator to be reliable
Tiwari 2018
;
Farr 2019
;
Essick and Farr 2022
;
Talbot and Golomb 2023
;
Heinzel and Vitale 2025
. Writing
w_{i,s}\equiv p(\lambda_{i,s}\mid\Lambda_{\lambda})/p_{\mathrm{pe}}(\lambda_{i,s})
for the importance weight of the
s
-th posterior sample of event
i
relative to its parameter-estimation prior
p_{\mathrm{pe}}
, and
w_{j}^{\mathrm{sel}}\equiv p(\lambda_{j}\mid\Lambda_{\lambda})/p_{\mathrm{inj}}(\lambda_{j})
for the analogous weight on the
j
-th of the
N_{\mathrm{draw}}
recovered injections drawn from the injection distribution
p_{\mathrm{inj}}
, the per-event and selection effective sample sizes are

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{eff},i}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\left(\sum_{s}w_{i,s}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{s}w_{i,s}^{2}},

(39)

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{eff,sel}}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\left(\sum_{j}w_{j}^{\mathrm{sel}}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{j}\left(w_{j}^{\mathrm{sel}}\right)^{2}-\left(\sum_{j}w_{j}^{\mathrm{sel}}\right)^{2}\!/N_{\mathrm{draw}}}.

(40)

We add to the log-likelihood a smooth penalty for each
N_{\mathrm{eff}}
that ramps linearly below a threshold
N_{\mathrm{crit}}
,

\displaystyle\log\Pi(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\mid N_{\mathrm{crit}})=\min\!\left(0,\;\frac{N_{\mathrm{eff}}-N_{\mathrm{crit}}}{0.05\,N_{\mathrm{crit}}}\right),

(41)

applied with
N_{\mathrm{crit}}=N_{\mathrm{obs}}
to
\min_{i}\,N_{\mathrm{eff},i}(\Lambda)
and with
N_{\mathrm{crit}}=4\,N_{\mathrm{obs}}
to
N_{\mathrm{eff,sel}}(\Lambda)
.
