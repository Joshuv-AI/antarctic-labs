Distributed Stochastic Graph Algorithms
Keren Censor-Hillel, Aditi Dudeja, George Giakkoupis

Abstract
We study stochastic graph optimization problems in a novel distributed setting. As in the standard centralized setting, a random subgraph G ∗ G^{\ast} of a known base graph G G is realized by including each edge e e independently with a known probability p e p_{e} , and we must solve an optimization problem on G ∗ G^{\ast} despite uncertainty about its edges. In the standard setting, to cope with this uncertainty, the algorithm can query any edge of G G to learn if the edge exists in G ∗ G^{\ast} , and its complexity is the number of queried edges. The distributed setting incorporates uncertainty in a natural manner, by having each vertex know only about its own edges in G ∗ G^{\ast} (and only communicate over them), and the complexity is measured by the number of synchronous communication rounds. We establish that distributed stochastic algorithms can be drastically faster than their non-stochastic counterparts and overcome known lower bounds, by showing fast distributed approximation algorithms for maximum matching, minimum vertex cover, and minimum dominating set. Specifically: • Within two rounds of single-bit messages we obtain a constant approximation for maximum matching, and a single round suffices for an O ⁡ ( log ⁡ Δ ¯ ) O(\log\bar{\Delta}) -approximation algorithm for minimum dominating set, where Δ ¯ \bar{\Delta} is the maximum expected degree of G ∗ G^{\ast} . Our most novel technical contribution is a constant approximation algorithm for minimum vertex cover which uses no communication at all. • Allowing more, but still a constant number of rounds enables better approximations: For maximum matching we provide a ( 0.68 − ϵ ) (0.68-\epsilon) -approximation and a ( 0.73 − ϵ ) (0.73-\epsilon) -approximation algorithms for general and bipartite graphs, respectively, running in poly ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) \poly(1/\epsilon) rounds; and for minimum vertex cover we give a ( 2 + ϵ ) (2+\epsilon) -approximation algorithm running in poly ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) \poly(1/\epsilon) rounds. Some of these results rely on previous works on the standard stochastic setting, namely, [Behnezhad et al., SODA 2022] and [Derakhshan and Saneian, ICALP 2025], while others require new algorithmic ideas.

This model is motivated by the observation that the traditional assumption in distributed graph algorithms that nodes do not have any information about the graph (except possibly for their set of direct neighbors), does not reflect modern networked systems, such as large-scale data centers or content delivery networks, where the underlying physical infrastructure – the base graph

The model we propose is perhaps one of the simplest and most natural abstractions for leveraging the history of a network with the goal of improving algorithmic complexity.
In practice, realization probabilities

can be viewed as predictions generated by machine learning models trained on historical traffic patterns.
Next we describe the model in detail.

Distributed Stochastic Model.

In the classic distributed models,
n
vertices in a network communicate in synchronous rounds, in each of which every vertex can send a message to each of its neighbors. When considering distributed algorithms for a stochastic setting, we follow the paradigm of the centralized stochastic setting, which allows the algorithm to preprocess the base graph.
Thus, in the distributed stochastic model, the base graph
G=(V,E)
(i.e., the network) is known to all vertices. For a graph problem

G^{\ast}

G

We demonstrate that the distributed stochastic model is remarkably powerful, allowing us to bypass lower bounds that apply to classical distributed algorithms.
Namely, we provide distributed approximation algorithms for maximum matching, minimum vertex cover, and minimum dominating set, that require a constant number of communication rounds (and sometimes no communication at all).
Achieving comparable approximations by classical distributed algorithms requires a super-constant number of communication rounds.

To obtain this result, we follow an approach from

At the core of this approach is a standard algorithm that computes a fractional matching by uniformly increasing the fractional weights of all edges;

This algorithm starts on the base graph

Adapting the approach to the distributed setting presents some technical challenges.
In particular, uniformly increasing the edge weights in the first stage of the algorithm results in prohibitively large remaining vertex degrees in the second stage.
We solve this problem by increasing the edge weights proportionally to the realization probabilities, rather than uniformly.
This ensures that vertices have constant expected remaining degrees in the realized graph, but we still need to handle separately vertices whose actual degree is super-constant.
Moreover, combining the two parts of the algorithm requires more care over the original approach in
[
19
]

We mention that paper

However, we are not aware of a way to adapt the approach given there in our setting.
The main obstacle is that the approach needs to compute a near-optimal vertex cover in a sparse subgraph of the base graph.
In our setting we can obtain the sparse subgraph, but do not know how to compute the near-optimal vertex cover efficiently.

The algorithm for bipartite graphs is identical to an algorithm given in

Each vertex from the one side of the bipartite graph proposes to a neighbor on the other side, chosen independently at randomly according to the matching probabilities from the optimal solution; and each vertex from the receiving side that gets at least one proposal accepts one of them.

Making sure that the proposal probabilities are independent, as in the bipartite case, requires some care in handling non-bipartite edges.

An Improved 3-Approximation for Vertex Cover without Communication

A result from recent independent work
[
80
]
immediately implies a

Their result assumes

Precisely,

Using the construction from

80

3
-approximate distributed stochastic algorithm without communication.
By a greedy process, the lemma constructs an ordering
\pi=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
of the vertices that satisfies the following property:
Let
M
be a random matching of
G^{\ast}
obtained by a sequential process considering the vertices in the order of

When vertex

is considered, if it is not already matched (to a vertex in
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{i-1}\}
) and has at least one unmatched neighbor in
\{v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
, then
v_{i}
is matched to a neighbor chosen uniformly at random from those options.
Let
q_{i}
be the probability that
v_{i}
is matched to a vertex in
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{i-1}\}
.
The ordering
\pi
ensures that the probability of
v_{i}
being matched to a vertex in
\{v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
is at least
R_{i}(1-2q_{i})
, where

is the probability that
v_{i}
has at least one neighbor in
\{v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{n}\}

G^{\ast}
.

Consider now the (random) vertex set

consisting of all vertices

that have at least one neighbor in
\{v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{n}\}

G^{\ast}
.
Note that

is a valid vertex cover of

because for any realized edge
(v_{i},v_{j})

is included in

Moreover,

can be constructed without communication given

Its expected size is
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|C|]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}

We can bound the expected size of matching
M
by counting its edges via their endpoints. Counting via the higher-indexed (right) endpoints yields
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|M|]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|M|]\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(1-2q_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}2q_{i}=\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|C|]-2\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|M|].

Rearranging yields
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|C|]\leq 3\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|M|]
. Since the size of any matching is a lower bound for the size of a minimum vertex cover, it follows that
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|C|]\leq 3\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|\mathrm{MVC}(G^{\ast})|]

This result does not require the assumption that all edges have the same realization probability
p

Y_{e}
and
F_{v}
given
X_{e}
,
and in the last line we used that
f_{vu}\geq 1/2
because
f_{vu}\geq f_{uv}
and
f_{vu}+f_{uv}\geq 1
.
It follows

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F_{v}\cdot Y_{v}]=\sum_{e\in E_{v}}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F_{v}\cdot Y_{e}]\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{e\in E_{v}}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[Y_{e}]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[Y_{v}].

(2)

Inequality
Equation

2
allows us to apply a property of the Poisson distribution that we show in technical
Lemma

7
, to obtain

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F_{v}]\geq\frac{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(Y_{v}\neq 0)}{3.44}

Combining this and
Equation

1
completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

7
A Property of Poisson Distribution.

Let
Y
be a Poisson random variable, and
F
be a real random variable that takes values in the interval
[0,1]
.
The two random variables are not necessarily independent.
If
\,\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F\cdot Y]\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[Y]
then

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F]\geq\frac{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(Y\neq 0)}{3.44}.

Proof.

Let
\lambda=\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[Y]
be the mean of Poisson random variable
Y
, and for
k\geq 0
, let
q_{k}
denote the probability that
Y=k
,

q_{k}=\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(Y=k)=\frac{\lambda^{k}e^{-\lambda}}{k!}.

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer and
\beta\in(0,1]
a real such that

\sum_{k\leq m}kq_{k}+\beta(m+1)q_{m+1}=\frac{\lambda}{2}.

(3)

(We will see that
m
is the median of
Y
.)
Let
F^{\ast}
be a random variable such that

F^{\ast}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $0\leq Y\leq m$}\\
1-\beta&\text{if $Y=m+1$}\\
1&\text{if $Y\geq m+2$}\\
\end{cases}

We have that
F^{\ast}
is in
[0,1]
, and we will show that
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}\cdot Y]=\frac{\lambda}{2}
. We then argue that among all random variables
F
that take values on
[0,1]
and satisfy
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F\cdot Y]\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}
,
random variable
F^{\ast}
has the smallest mean.
I.e.,
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]\leq\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F]
, for any random variable
F
that takes values on
[0,1]
and
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F\cdot Y]\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}
. Thus, to prove the lemma it suffices to show

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]\geq\frac{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(Y\neq 0)}{3.44}.

We begin by showing that
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}\cdot Y]=\frac{\lambda}{2}
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}\cdot Y]

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)(m+1)q_{m+1}+\sum_{k\geq m+2}kq_{k}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)(m+1)q_{m+1}+\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[Y]-\sum_{k\leq m+1}k\cdot q_{k}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[Y]-\frac{\lambda}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2},

where the second-last line was obtained using
Equation

3
.

Next, we explain why
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]\leq\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F]
, for any
F
that takes values on
[0,1]
and satisfies that
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F\cdot Y]\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}
:
For
y\geq 0
, let
z_{y}=\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F\cdot\mathds{1}_{Y=y}]\leq q_{y}
.
The constraint gives us that
\sum_{y}y\cdot z_{y}\geq\lambda/2
, and the goal is to minimize
\sum_{y}z_{y}
.
Since
y
is monotonically increasing, by the rearrangement inequality, the minimum for
\sum_{y}z_{y}
is attained by maximizing values of
z_{y}
for larger
y
, namely, setting
z_{y}=\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F\cdot Y]=q_{y}
, or equivalently, setting
F=1
if
y>y_{0}
, and setting
F=0
if
y<y_{0}
, for some
y_{0}
.

It remains to show

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]\geq\frac{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(Y\neq 0)}{3.44}.

First, notice that

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]=(1-\beta)q_{m+1}+\sum_{k\geq m+2}q_{k}=1-\sum_{k\leq m}q_{k}-\beta q_{m+1}.

(4)

Substituting the definition of
q_{k}
to
Equation

3
and simplifying gives

\sum_{1\leq k\leq m}\frac{\lambda^{k}e^{-\lambda}}{(k-1)!}+\beta\frac{\lambda^{m+1}e^{-\lambda}}{m!}=\frac{\lambda}{2},

thus

\sum_{k\leq m-1}q_{k}+\beta q_{m}=\frac{1}{2}.

(5)

Since
\beta\in(0,1]
, the above implies that
m
is the smallest integer such that
\sum_{k\leq m}q_{k}\geq\frac{1}{2}
, i.e.,
m

is the median of
Y
.
For every
i\geq 0
, let
\lambda_{i+1}
be the value of
\lambda
that satisfies

\sum_{k\leq i}q_{k}=\frac{1}{2};

let also
\lambda_{0}=0
.
Then, for
\lambda_{i}<\lambda\leq\lambda_{i+1}
the median is
m=i
.

We first use
Equation

5
to compute a loose bound on
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]
, which is however enough to prove the lemma for most values of
\lambda
.
Combining
Equations

4
and
5
, we obtain

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]=\frac{1}{2}-(1-\beta)q_{m}-\beta q_{m+1}=\frac{1}{2}-q_{m}+\beta(q_{m}-q_{m+1})\geq\frac{1}{2}-q_{m},

where the last inequality holds because

q_{m+1}=q_{m}\cdot\frac{\lambda}{m+1}<q_{m},

since
m\geq\lambda+\ln(2)

[
31
]
and thus
m+1>\lambda
.

For any
i\geq 1
and
\lambda_{i}<\lambda\leq\lambda_{i+1}
, the above inequality gives

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]\geq\frac{1}{2}-q_{i}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda^{i}e^{-\lambda}}{i!}\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{i^{i}e^{-i}}{i!},

since
\lambda^{i}e^{-\lambda}
is minimized for
\lambda=i
.
Note also that that term
\frac{i^{i}e^{-i}}{i!}
decreases as
i
increases.
Then, for
i\geq 4
,

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{4^{4}e^{-4}}{4!}>0.304>\frac{1}{3.3}.

Therefore the lemma holds for
\lambda>\lambda_{4}
.

For the case of
\lambda\leq\lambda_{4}
, we analyze the precise formula for
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]
.

We substitute in
Equation

4
the value of
\beta
obtained by solving
Equation

3
for
\beta
, to get

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]=1-\sum_{k\leq m}q_{k}-\frac{\lambda}{m+1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\sum_{k\leq m-1}q_{k}\right).

For
\lambda\leq\lambda_{1}
(and thus
m=0
), we have
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]=1-\lambda/2
. This gives

\frac{\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]}{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(Y\neq 0)}=\frac{1-e^{-\lambda}-\lambda/2}{1-e^{-\lambda}},

which is a decreasing function on
\lambda
, thus the minimum is at the right endpoint
\lambda_{1}
of the interval.
For
\lambda_{i}<\lambda\leq\lambda_{i+1}
, for each
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, we have verified that the function

\frac{\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[F^{\ast}]}{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(Y\neq 0)}=\frac{1-\sum_{k\leq i}q_{k}-\frac{\lambda}{i+1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\sum_{k\leq i-1}q_{k}\right)}{1-q_{0}}

is concave in each of the three intervals (the second derivative is negative), which implies the minimum lies in one of the points
\lambda_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
.
A plot of the function is shown in
Figure

1
.
Recall that
\lambda_{i+1}
is the value of
\lambda
that satisfies

\sum_{k\leq i}q_{k}=\frac{1}{2}
.
Solving that and substituting above we obtain that the global minimum is at point

\lambda_{2}=1.678347
and the minimum value is
\frac{1}{3.43068}
.
∎

\lambda
, used in the proof of
Lemma

7

3
Improved Approximation for Minimum Vertex Cover in
\poly(1/\epsilon)
Rounds

In this section we show that we can improve the approximation factor to
2+\epsilon
at the cost of not too many communication rounds, namely,
\poly(1/\epsilon)
. We give an algorithm that finds a vertex cover of
G^{\ast}
with the following properties.

See
2

Our approach is based on an algorithm from
[
19
]
, but requires several modifications, as mentioned earlier.
Throughout the algorithm we will use the following constants which we state here for convenience.
Let
0<\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{4}
, and let

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon^{3}\qquad\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{3}\qquad\varepsilon_{3}=\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{3}

\displaystyle\xi=\frac{1+\varepsilon_{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}=\frac{1+\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{3}}{\varepsilon^{3}}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{4}=\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}=2\varepsilon\qquad\varepsilon_{5}=\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})^{2}}=\varepsilon

\displaystyle\epsilon=\frac{(2+\varepsilon_{5})(1+\varepsilon_{4})}{1-\varepsilon_{5}}-2=\frac{(2+\varepsilon)(1+2\varepsilon)}{1-\varepsilon}-2\leq 10\varepsilon,

where for the last inequality we used that
\varepsilon\leq 1/4
.

Distributed Algorithm

1.

(Preprocessing Stage)

Run
Algorithm

1
on
G=(V,E)
to obtain a fractional matching
\phi=(\phi_{e})_{e\in E}
of
G
.

Algorithm

1
is similar to the algorithm in
[
19
]
, except that the fractional edge weights change proportionally to their realization probability, instead of uniformly.
For each vertex
v
, let
\phi_{v}=\sum_{e\ni v}\phi_{e}
, and let

F=\{v\in V\colon\phi_{v}=1\}.

2.

(Distributed Phase)
Let
G^{\ast}=(V,E^{\ast})
denote the realized graph. Define
\chi=(\chi_{e})_{e\in E}
as

\chi_{e}=\begin{cases}\frac{\phi_{e}}{p_{e}},&\text{if }e\in E^{\ast}\\
0,&\text{if }e\in E\setminus E^{\ast}.\end{cases}

Let
\chi_{v}=\sum_{e\ni v}\chi_{e}
, for
v\in V
. Let the set of
bad
vertices be

B=\left\{v\in V\colon\chi_{v}\geq\phi_{v}+\varepsilon_{2}\right\}.

Let
Q
be the set of all edges of
G
with no endpoints in
F\cup B
,

Q=\{e\in E\colon e\cap(F\cup B)=\emptyset\},

and let
Q^{\ast}=Q\cap E^{\ast}
. We run

2

(V,Q^{\ast})
with

and obtain
\psi=(\psi_{e})_{e\in Q^{\ast}}
. For convenience, we also define
\psi_{e}=0
, for all

.

Algorithm

2
is a discretized version of the algorithm in
[
19
]
.
The final output of the algorithm is the set

C=F\cup B\cup\left\{v\in V\mid\phi_{v}+\psi_{v}\geq 1\right\}.

Algorithm 1

WaterFilling
(
H=(V_{H},E_{H})
)

1:

For all edges
e
,
\phi_{e}\leftarrow 0
, initially

2:

Call a vertex
v

inactive
if
\phi_{v}\coloneqq\sum_{e\ni v}\phi_{e}=1
and
active
otherwise; similarly, an edge is active iff both its endpoints are active and inactive otherwise

3:

while
there exists an active edge and
\phi_{e}<\varepsilon_{1}p_{e}
for all edges
e

do

4:

Pick minimum
\delta\in(0,1)

\phi_{e}\leftarrow\phi_{e}+\delta p_{e}
for all
e
active results in at least one new inactive vertex or
\phi_{e}=\varepsilon_{1}p_{e}
for some edge
e

5:

\phi_{e}\leftarrow\phi_{e}+\delta p_{e}
for all active edges
e

6:

end

while

Algorithm 2

DistributedWaterFilling
(

For all edges
e
,
\psi_{e}\leftarrow 0

Call a vertex
v

inactive

\psi_{v}\coloneqq\sum_{e\ni v}\psi_{e}\geq 1-b_{v}

8
.

C
is a valid vertex cover, and the number of rounds of the distributed phase is
O(\xi\cdot\varepsilon_{3}^{-1})=O(1/\varepsilon^{4})
.

Proof.

Since in
Algorithm

2
Line
4
, we increase the weight on the edge by
\frac{\varepsilon_{3}}{\xi}
in each round, the total number of rounds until a vertex becomes inactive is at most
O(\xi\cdot\varepsilon_{3}^{-1})
. To see that
C
is a valid vertex cover, first observe that any edges with an end point in
F\cup B
are automatically covered by
C
. For any
e=\{u,v\}\in Q^{\ast}
,
Algorithm

2
ensures that either
\phi_{v}+\psi_{v}\geq 1
or
\phi_{u}+\psi_{u}\geq 1
. Thus, by definition of
C
, at least one of
u
or
v
are included in
C
.
∎

In the remaining of this section we bound the approximation ratio of the algorithm.

9
.

For all
e\in E
,
\phi_{e}\leq\varepsilon_{1}\cdot p_{e}
, and, in particular,
\phi_{e}=\varepsilon_{1}\cdot p_{e}
if
e\cap F=\emptyset
(i.e.,
e
is still active at the end of
Algorithm

1
).

Proof.

Let
\delta_{i}
denote the increment
\delta
that the algorithm uses in the
i
th while-loop iteration, and let
t
denote the total number of iterations.
At the end of the loop, we have for every edge
e
,

\phi_{e}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\delta_{i}\cdot p_{e}.

In particular, if
e
is still active then the above inequality holds as equality.
If at the end of the algorithm there is some active edge then by the exit condition of the while loop, and by the way
\delta
is chosen it follows that there is some active edge
e
satisfying

\phi_{e}=\varepsilon_{1}\cdot p_{e}.

It follows that
\sum_{i=1}^{t}\delta_{i}=\varepsilon_{1}
, in this case.
If there is no active edge at the end of the loop, then we similarly conclude that
\sum_{i=1}^{t}\delta_{i}\leq\varepsilon_{1}
.
In both cases the lemma follows.
∎

10
.

The maximum degree of subgraph
(V,Q^{\ast})
is at most
\xi=\frac{1+\varepsilon_{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}
.

Proof.

If
v\in F\cup B
then
v
’s degree in
(V,Q^{\ast})
is zero.
Let
v\in V\setminus(F\cup B)
.
Let
d
be
v
’s degree in
(V,Q^{\ast})
, and let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}
be its incident edges.
For each
1\leq i\leq d
, we have
e_{i}\cap F=\emptyset
, thus
Lemma

9
implies

\phi_{e_{i}}=\varepsilon_{1}\cdot p_{e_{i}}.

Then

\chi_{v}=\sum_{e\ni v}\chi_{e}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\chi_{e_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\phi_{e_{i}}}{p_{e_{i}}}=d\cdot\varepsilon_{1}.

Also, since
v\notin B
we have

\chi_{v}<\phi_{v}+\varepsilon_{2}\leq 1+\varepsilon_{2},

where the second inequality holds because
\phi
is valid fractional matching of
G
.
Combining the last two equation gives
d\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon_{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}
.
∎

The statement of the next lemma is similar to
[
19

, but several elements of the proof are different.

11
.

There is a fractional matching
y=(y_{e})_{e\in E^{\ast}}
such that

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|C|]\leq(2+\epsilon)\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|y|],

(6)

where
\epsilon\leq 10\varepsilon
.

We start with defining our
candidate
fractional matching
y
. Let
\varepsilon_{4}=\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}
. For each
e\in E^{\ast}
,

y_{e}=\left(\chi_{e}+\psi_{e}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon_{4}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{e\cap B=\emptyset}.

That is,
y_{e}=0
if
e
has an endpoint in
B
and is
\frac{\chi_{e}+\psi_{e}}{1+\varepsilon_{4}}
otherwise. We also define
y_{e}=0
if
e\in E\setminus E^{\ast}
.

12
.

The candidate fractional matching
y
is a valid fractional matching.

Proof.

For all
v\in B
, we have
y_{v}=0
, thus satisfying the fractional matching constraints trivially.
Let
v\notin B
. Then

y_{v}\leq\sum_{e\ni v}\frac{\chi_{e}+\psi_{e}}{1+\varepsilon_{4}}\leq\frac{\chi_{v}+\psi_{v}}{1+\varepsilon_{4}}.

From
Algorithm

2
, we have that
\psi_{v}\leq 1-\phi_{v}
while
v
is still active, and if
v
becomes inactive, then in the iteration when
v
first becomes inactive,
\psi_{v}
increases by at most
\frac{\varepsilon_{3}}{\xi}
times the degree of
v
in
(V,Q^{\ast})
, which is at most
\xi
, from
Lemma

10
.
Thus, at the end of
Algorithm

2
,

\psi_{v}\leq 1-\phi_{v}+\frac{\varepsilon_{3}}{\xi}\cdot\xi=1-\phi_{v}+\varepsilon_{3}.

Also
v\notin B
implies

\chi_{v}\leq\phi_{v}+\varepsilon_{2}
.
Substituting these two inequalities above gives

y_{v}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}}{1+\varepsilon_{4}}=1,

thus

∎

We define the following set
B^{+}\supseteq B
, which uses a slightly lower threshold value than
B
, namely
\phi_{v}+\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}
instead of
\phi_{v}+\varepsilon_{2}

B^{+}=\left\{v\in V\colon\chi_{v}\geq\phi_{v}+\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}\right\}.

The next lemma establishes a lower bound on
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|y|]
.

13
.

(1+\varepsilon_{2})\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|y|]\geq|\phi|+\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|\psi|]-\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|B^{+}|].

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon_{4})\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|y|]

\displaystyle=(1+\varepsilon_{4})\sum_{e\in E}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[y_{e}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}\left[\left(\chi_{e}+\psi_{e}\right)\cdot\mathds{1}_{e\cap B=\emptyset}\cdot\mathds{1}_{e\in E^{\ast}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\chi_{e}\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(e\cap B=\emptyset\,\land\,e\in E^{\ast})+\sum_{e\in E}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}\left[\psi_{e}\cdot\mathds{1}_{e\cap B=\emptyset}\cdot\mathds{1}_{e\in E^{\ast}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\chi_{e}\cdot p_{e}\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(e\cap B=\emptyset\mid e\in E^{\ast})+\sum_{e\in E}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}\left[\psi_{e}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\phi_{e}\cdot(1-\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(e\cap B\neq\emptyset\mid e\in E^{\ast}))+\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|\psi|]

\displaystyle=|\phi|-\sum_{e\in E}\phi_{e}\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(e\cap B\neq\emptyset\mid e\in E^{\ast})+\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|\psi|].

Next we will use that if
e\ni v
then
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(v\in B\mid e\in E^{\ast})\leq\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(v\in B^{+})
, because by
Lemma

9
, a single edge
e
can contribute at most
\varepsilon_{1}
to
\chi_{v}
,
in the definition of
B
and
B^{+}
.
We bound the sum in the last line above as follows,

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}\phi_{e}\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(e\cap B\neq\emptyset\mid e\in E^{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{e\ni v}\phi_{e}\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(v\in B\mid e\in E^{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{e\ni v}\phi_{e}\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(v\in B^{+})

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(v\in B^{+})\cdot\sum_{e\ni v}\phi_{e}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in V}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(v\in B^{+})\cdot 1

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|B^{+}|].

Substituting this above completes the proof.
∎

We now give an upper bound on
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|C|]
.

14
.

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|C|]\leq 2|\phi|+2\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|\psi|]+\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|B|].

Proof.

Recall
C=F\cup B\cup\{v\in V\colon\phi_{v}+\psi_{v}\geq 1\}
.
We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|C|]

\displaystyle=|F|+\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|B\setminus F|]+\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|C\setminus(F\cup B)|]

\displaystyle\leq|F|+\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|B|]+\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|C\setminus F|].

Also, from the definition of
C
it follows

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|C\setminus F|]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in V\setminus F}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[\phi_{v}+\psi_{v}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\phi_{v}-\sum_{v\in F}\phi_{v}+\sum_{v\in V}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[\psi_{v}]

\displaystyle=2|\phi|-|F|+2\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|\psi|].

Substituting this above completes the proof.
∎

Finally, we show an upper bound on
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|B^{+}|]
.
Recall
\varepsilon_{5}=\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})^{2}}
.

15
.

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|B|]\leq\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|B^{+}|]\leq\varepsilon_{5}\cdot|\phi|
.

Proof.

The first inequality is immediate from the definition of the two sets.
We prove the second inequality.
Consider any vertex
v\in V
.
Let
d
be the degree of
v
in the base graph
G
, and let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}
be its incident edges.
For
1\leq i\leq d
,

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[\chi_{e_{i}}]=\frac{\phi_{e_{i}}}{p_{e_{i}}}\cdot p_{e_{i}}=\phi_{e_{i}},

and

\Var[\chi_{e_{i}}]=\left(\frac{\phi_{e_{i}}}{p_{e_{i}}}\right)^{2}p_{e_{i}}(1-p_{e_{i}})\leq\frac{\phi_{e_{i}}^{2}}{p_{e_{i}}}\leq\varepsilon_{1}\phi_{e_{i}},

where the last inequality is obtained using
Lemma

9
.
It follows that

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[\chi_{v}]=\phi_{v},

and

\Var[\chi_{v}]=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Var[\chi_{e_{i}}]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varepsilon_{1}\phi_{e_{i}}=\varepsilon_{1}\phi_{v}.

Then the probability that vertex
v
is in
B^{+}
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(v\in B^{+})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(\chi_{v}>\phi_{v}+\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(|\chi_{v}-\phi_{v}|>\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Var[\chi_{v}]}{(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{1}\phi_{v}}{(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})^{2}}.

Finally,

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|B^{+}|]=\sum_{v\in V}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(v\in B^{+})\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{1}\cdot|\phi|}{(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})^{2}}.\qed

From
Lemmas

13
and
15
,

\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon_{4})\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|y|]

\displaystyle\geq|\phi|+\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|\psi|]-\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|B^{+}|]

18
.

The expected size of the matching

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|M|]\geq 2(1-\alpha)\cdot(1-e^{-\alpha})\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|\mathcal{M}(G^{\ast})|].

Proof.

For every vertex
v\in V
, let
\mathcal{E}_{v}
be the event that
v
is passive and receives at least one proposal.
Then

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|M|]=\sum_{v\in V}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{E}_{v}).

For every pair
u,v\in V
, let
X_{u,v}
be the indicator random variable of the event
u\in A\,\land\,uv\in\mathcal{M}(G^{\ast}_{u})
.
Note that the event
\mathcal{E}_{v}
is the same as the event

v\in P\,\land\,\sum_{u\in N_{G}(v)}X_{u,v}>0.

We have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(X_{u,v}=0)

\displaystyle=1-\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(X_{u,v}=1)

\displaystyle=1-\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(u\in A\land uv\in\mathcal{M}(G_{u}^{\ast}))

\displaystyle=1-\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(u\in A)\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(uv\in\mathcal{M}(G_{u}^{\ast}))

\displaystyle=1-\alpha\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(uv\in\mathcal{M}(G^{\ast}))

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\alpha\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(uv\in\mathcal{M}(G^{\ast}))},

where in the third line we used that
G_{u}^{\ast}
is independent of
A
by
Lemma

17
,
and in the fourth line we used that
G^{\ast}
and
G_{u}^{\ast}
have the same distribution.
Moreover, since
X_{u,v}
depends only on
S_{u}
,
G^{\ast}_{u}
,
Lemma

17
implies that the random variables
X_{u,v}
, for
u\in N_{G}(v)
, are mutually independent, and also independent of
S_{v}
.
Then for every
v\in V

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{E}_{v})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathds{P}}\!\left(v\in P\land\sum_{u\in N_{G}(v)}X_{u,v}>0\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathds{P}}\!\left(v\in P\right)\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}\!\left(\sum_{u\in N_{G}(v)}X_{u,v}>0\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\cdot\left(1-\operatorname{\mathds{P}}\!\left(\sum_{u\in N_{G}(v)}X_{u,v}=0\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\cdot\left(1-\prod_{u\in N_{G}(v)}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(X_{u,v}=0)\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha)\cdot\left(1-e^{-\alpha\cdot\sum_{u\in N_{G}(v)}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(uv\in\mathcal{M}(G^{\ast}))}\right),

where in the third line we used that the

are independent of
S_{v}

\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{E}_{v})\geq(1-\alpha)\cdot\left(1-e^{-\alpha c_{v}}\right)=(1-\alpha)\cdot c_{v}\cdot\frac{1-e^{-\alpha c_{v}}}{c_{v}}\geq(1-\alpha)\cdot c_{v}\cdot(1-e^{-\alpha}),

since the function

is decreasing and

Thus we have,

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|M|]=\sum_{v\in V}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{E}_{v})\geq(1-\alpha)\cdot(1-e^{-\alpha})\cdot\sum_{v\in V}c_{v}=2(1-\alpha)\cdot(1-e^{-\alpha})\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|\mathcal{M}(G^{\ast})|].\qed

6
Logarithmic Approximation for Minimum Dominating Set

Let
\Delta
be the maximum degree of base graph
G
and let
\bar{\Delta}
be the maximum expected degree of
G^{\ast}
,

\bar{\Delta}=\max_{v}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[\deg_{G^{\ast}}(v)].

Note that
\bar{\Delta}

is smaller than the expected maximum degree
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[\max_{v}\deg_{G^{\ast}}(v)]
.

We give an algorithm that finds a dominating set
S
of
G^{\ast}
, whose expected size is an
O(\log{\bar{\Delta}})
-approximation to that of the optimal solution
S^{\ast}
for
G^{\ast}
.

See
5

At a high level, our aim is to mimic the standard greedy algorithm (e.g.
[
60
,
72
]
) for computing a logarithmic approximation to the minimum dominating set.
The preprocessing stage computes a ranking of the vertices, such that the
i
th vertex in the ranking, denoted
v_{i}
, is the one that
in expectation
covers the largest number of vertices in
G^{\ast}
not already covered by the previous
i-1
vertices,
v_{1},\ldots,v_{i-1}
.
Then, after
G^{\ast}
is realized, each vertex chooses its neighbor in
G^{\ast}
(or itself) with the highest rank, and the set of all chosen vertices is the output of the algorithm.

Recall that the textbook analysis of the standard greedy algorithm for a deterministic graph assigns to every vertex covered by the
i
th vertex
u
in the greedy dominating set a cost of
1/d_{i}
, where
d_{i}
is the total number of vertices that
u
covers (and were not previously covered).
Then it is shown that for each vertex
v
in the optimal dominating set
S^{\ast}
, the sum of costs of all vertices adjacent to
v
and including
v
is at most
\sum_{j=1}^{\deg(v)+1}=\ln\deg(v)+O(1)
.

In our algorithm, vertices are ranked according to the expected number of vertices they cover (rather than the actual number).
This presents two challenges.
On the one hand, there are vertices for which the actual number of vertices they cover is much smaller than expected (thus they have a higher rank than they should); we call such vertices
bad
.
On the other hand, there are vertices for which the actual number of vertices they cover is much larger than expected (thus they have a lower rank than they should); we call such vertices
costly
.
We deal with bad vertices by assigning a cost of zero to the vertices they cover, and showing that the total number of such bad vertices is small (compared to the optimal dominating set size).
We deal with costly vertices by assigning a cost of 1 to the vertices they cover, and showing that the total number of vertices covered by costly vertices is small.

Below we formally present the algorithm and its analysis.

Distributed Algorithm.

1.

(
Preprocessing stage
) Order the vertices of
V
inductively, as follows. Let
V_{0}=\emptyset
and for every
i=1,\dots,n
let
V_{i}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i}\}
. Let
\tilde{G}
be a random hallucination of
G
. For every
i=1,\dots,n
and vertex
v
, let
\tilde{W}_{i}(v)
be a random variable that represents the set of vertices in
\tilde{G}
that are covered by
v
which are not already covered by vertices in
V_{i-1}
.
The probability of a vertex
u\in N_{G}(v)
to be in
\tilde{W}_{i}(v)
is

p_{uv}\cdot\prod_{w\in N_{G}(u)\cap V_{i-1}}{(1-p_{uw})}
,

since we need its edge to
v
to get sampled in
\tilde{G}
and its edges to any other vertex in
V_{i}
to not get sampled. Similarly, the probability of
v
to be in
\tilde{W}_{i}(v)
is

\prod_{w\in N_{G}(v)\cap V_{i-1}}(1-p_{vw})
.
Let
\tilde{w}_{i}(v)=\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|\tilde{W}_{i}(v)|]
.
Then

\tilde{w}_{i}(v)=\prod_{w\in N_{G}(v)\cap V_{i-1}}(1-p_{vw})+\sum_{u\in N_{G}(v)\setminus V_{i-1}}p_{uv}\cdot\prod_{w\in N_{G}(u)\cap V_{i-1}}{(1-p_{uw})}.

Let
v_{i}
be a vertex
v
that maximizes
\tilde{w}_{i}(v)
.
5
5

5

Throughout the algorithm and analysis, we break ties in any arbitrary consistent manner, say, by vertex identifiers.
We say that
i
is the rank of vertex
v_{i}
. Note that if
i<i^{\prime}
then
\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})\geq\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i^{\prime}})\geq\tilde{w}_{i^{\prime}}(v_{i^{\prime}})
.

2.

(
Communication round
) Each vertex
v
selects the vertex
u\in N_{G^{\ast}}(v)\cup\{v\}
whose rank is minimal and informs
u
that it is selected.
All selected vertices join the dominated set
S
that the algorithm outputs.

It is immediate that
S
is indeed a dominating set of
G^{\ast}
, and that the algorithm has the round and message complexity stated in
Theorem

5
.
Next we bound the approximation ratio.

For every vertex
v\in V
, let
W_{i}(v)
be the set of vertices in
G^{\ast}
that are covered by
v
which are not already covered by vertices in

V_{i-1}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{i-1}\}
, and let
w_{i}(v)=|W_{i}(v)|
.

In the following we assume without loss of generality that
\bar{\Delta}
is larger than a sufficiently large constant (as we can artificially increase
\bar{\Delta}
by adding to
G
a disjoint star graphs with
\bar{\Delta}
leaves and edge realization probabilities that are equal to 1).

25
Bad Vertices and Costly Vertices.

We say that vertex
v_{i}
is
bad
if

w_{i}(v_{i})<(\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})-8\ln\bar{\Delta})/4.

We say that
v_{i}
is
costly
if there is
i^{\prime}<i
such that

\displaystyle w_{i^{\prime}}(v_{i})>6\cdot(\lceil\tilde{w}_{i^{\prime}}(v_{i^{\prime}})\rceil+\log\bar{\Delta}),.

(7)

and denote
\nu_{i}
the smallest such
i^{\prime}
.

Let
B
and
C
denote the set of bad vertices and the set of costly vertices, respectively.

First we show that the expected number of bad vertices is small.

26
There are not Many Bad Vertices.

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|B|]\leq n/\bar{\Delta}^{2}.

Proof.

Recall that vertex
v_{i}
is bad if

w_{i}(v_{i})<(\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})-8\ln\bar{\Delta})/4
.
Suppose first that
\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})\geq 8\ln\bar{\Delta}
.
By a Chernoff bound

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(w_{i}(v_{i})<(\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})-8\ln\bar{\Delta})/4)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(w_{i}(v_{i})<\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})/4)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-(3/4)^{2}\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})/2}

\displaystyle<1/\bar{\Delta}^{2},

since
\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})\geq 8\ln\bar{\Delta}
.
If
\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})<8\ln\bar{\Delta}
, the above inequality holds trivially.
Thus, it follows that
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|B|]\leq n/\bar{\Delta}^{2}
.
∎

Next we show that costly vertices do not cover many vertices in expectation.

27
Costly Vertices do not Cover Many Vertices.

Let
d_{v}=\deg_{G^{\ast}}(v)
for any
v\in V
.

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}\left[\sum_{v\in C}d_{v}\right]\leq n/\bar{\Delta}^{3}.

Proof.

We define the set

K=\{\lceil\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})\rceil\colon 1\leq i\leq n\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil\},

and for each
k\in K
we let
j_{k}
be the smallest
i
with
\lceil\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})\rceil=k
. Note that
\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil\in K
and
j_{\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil}=1
.
Observe also that if
v_{i}\in C
then
\nu_{i}=j_{k}
for some
k\in K
.
Indeed,
\nu_{i}
cannot take a value
i^{\prime}\notin\{j_{k}\colon k\in K\}
, since otherwise for the
k
such that
\lceil\tilde{w}_{i^{\prime}}(v_{i^{\prime}})\rceil=k
, we have that
j_{k}<i^{\prime}
and
j_{k}
also satisfies
Equation

7
, as replacing
i^{\prime}
by
j_{k}
leaves the right side of inequality
Equation

7
unchanged and can only increase the left side.

To simplify notation, we define
\nu_{i}=0
if
v_{i}\notin C
, so that now each
v_{i}\in V
is associated with a value
\nu_{i}
.
We have

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}\left[\sum_{v_{i}\in C}d_{v_{i}}\right]=\sum_{v_{i}\in V}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}\left[d_{v_{i}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{v_{i}\in C}\right].

For each
v_{i}\in V
,

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}\left[d_{v_{i}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{v_{i}\in C}\right]=\sum_{k\in K\colon k\geq\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[d_{v_{i}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\nu_{i}=j_{k}}].

For any
k\in K\setminus\{\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil\}
with
k\geq\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[d_{v_{i}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\nu_{i}=j_{k}}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[d_{v_{i}}\mid\nu_{i}=j_{k}]\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(\nu_{i}=j_{k})

\displaystyle\leq 6(\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil+\log\bar{\Delta})\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(\nu_{i}=j_{k})

\displaystyle\leq 6(\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil+\log\bar{\Delta})\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(w_{j_{k}}(v_{i})>6(k+\log\bar{\Delta})),

where the first inequality holds because

\nu_{i}=j_{k}
and
k\neq\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil
imply
d_{v_{i}}=w_{1}(v_{i})\leq 6(\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil+\log\bar{\Delta})
, and the second inequality holds because

\nu_{i}=j_{k}
implies
w_{j_{k}}(v_{i})>6(k+\log\bar{\Delta})
.
Similarly, for
k=\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil
, we have
j_{k}=1
and

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[d_{v_{i}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\nu_{i}=j_{k}}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[d_{v_{i}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{d_{v_{i}}>6(\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil+\log\bar{\Delta})}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\geq 6(\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil+\log\bar{\Delta})}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(d_{v_{i}}>j)\ +\ 6(\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil+\log\bar{\Delta})\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(d_{v_{i}}\geq 6(\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil+\log\bar{\Delta})),

where the first equation holds because
\nu_{i}=j_{k}=1
implies
d_{v_{1}}=w_{1}(v_{i})>6(\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil+\log\bar{\Delta})
, and the second equation is obtained using
Lemma

28
.

Next we bound the probabilities quantities in the last two equation above.
We have
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[w_{j_{k}}(v_{i})]\leq\tilde{w}_{j_{k}}(v_{j_{k}})\leq k
, thus for any
j\geq 6(k+\log\bar{\Delta})>6\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[w_{j_{k}}(v_{i})]
, a Chernoff bound gives

\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(w_{j_{k}}(v_{i})\geq j)\leq 2^{-j}.

In particular, for
k=\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil
the above yields a bound for
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(d_{v_{i}}\geq j)
.
Substituting to the previous two equations (and performing some simple calculations) gives

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[d_{v_{i}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\nu_{i}=j_{k}}]<1/\bar{\Delta}^{4},

for both cases, for large enough
\bar{\Delta}
.

Finally, combining the above we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathds{E}}\left[\sum_{v_{i}\in C}d_{v_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{v_{i}\in V}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}\left[d_{v_{i}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{v_{i}\in C}\right].

\displaystyle=\sum_{v_{i}\in V}\sum_{k\in K\colon k\geq\tilde{w}_{i}(v_{i})}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[d_{v_{i}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\nu_{i}=j_{k}}]

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil\cdot 1/\bar{\Delta}^{4}

\displaystyle<n/\bar{\Delta}^{3}.

This completes the proof of
Lemma

27
.
∎

We can now prove the approximation guarantee stated in
Theorem

5
.

Proof of
Theorem

5
.

As mentioned earlier,
S
is a dominating set of
G^{\ast}
by construction, and the algorithm has the desired round and message complexity.
It remains to analyze the size of
S
.

Let
S^{\ast}
be an optimal solution for
G^{\ast}
.
We will show that

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|S|]=O(\log\bar{\Delta})\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|S^{\ast}|].

As in the textbook analysis of the greedy algorithm, we assign to each vertex a
cost
for covering it by
S
, but we use a slightly different cost function.
We denote by
\cost(u)
the cost for covering a given vertex
u
.
Let
v_{i}
be the vertex in
S
that covers
u
, i.e.,
v_{i}
has the minimum rank
i
among all vertices in
N_{G^{\ast}}(u)\cup\{u\}
.
Then

\cost(u)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{w_{i}(v_{i})},&\text{ if }v_{i}\notin B\\
0,&\text{ if }v_{i}\in B.\end{cases}

Then

|S|\leq|B|+\sum_{v\in V}\cost(v).

(8)

Next we lower bound
|S^{\ast}|
in terms of the costs, by distinguishing between non-costly and costly vertices.
For each vertex
v\in S^{\ast}\setminus C
, by similar reasoning as in the standard proof, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in N_{G^{\ast}}(v)\cup\{v\}}\cost(u)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq j\leq|N_{G^{\ast}}(v)|+1}\frac{1}{\max\{1,(j-8\ln\bar{\Delta})/2\}}

\displaystyle=O(\log(|N_{G^{\ast}}(v)|+1)+\log\bar{\Delta})

\displaystyle=O(\log\bar{\Delta}),

where for the last equation we used that
|N_{G^{\ast}}(v)|=O(\bar{\Delta})
since
v\notin C
.
For any vertex
v\in S^{\ast}\cap C
, we use the trivial bound

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in N_{G^{\ast}}(v)\cup\{v\}}\cost(u)

\displaystyle\leq\deg_{G^{\ast}}(v)+1.

Combining the above two cases yields

\sum_{v\in S^{\ast}}\sum_{u\in N_{G^{\ast}}(v)\cup\{v\}}\cost(u)\leq\sum_{v\in S^{\ast}\cap C}(\deg_{G^{\ast}}(v)+1)+|S^{\ast}|\cdot O(\log\bar{\Delta}).

From that and the bound on
|S|
we computed earlier in
Equation

8
, we have

\displaystyle|S|

\displaystyle\leq|B|+\sum_{v\in V}\cost(v)

\displaystyle\leq|B|+\sum_{v\in S^{\ast}}\sum_{u\in N_{G^{\ast}}(v)\cup\{v\}}\cost(u)

\displaystyle\leq|B|+\sum_{v\in S^{\ast}\cap C}(\deg_{G^{\ast}}(v)+1)+|S^{\ast}|\cdot O(\log\bar{\Delta}),

where the second inequality holds because
S^{\ast}
is a dominating set and thus must cover all vertices.
Taking the expectation and applying
Lemmas

27
and
26
gives

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|S|]\leq n/\bar{\Delta}^{2}+2n/\bar{\Delta}^{3}+\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|S^{\ast}|]\cdot O(\log\bar{\Delta}).

To complete the proof we observe that

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|S^{\ast}|]\geq\frac{n-\sum_{v\in S^{\ast}\cap C}(\deg_{G^{\ast}}(v)+1)}{6(\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil+\log\Delta)}=\Omega\left(\frac{n}{\bar{\Delta}}\right),

where the first inequality holds because all vertices not covered by costly vertices in
S^{\ast}
must be covered by vertices of degree at most
6(\lceil\bar{\Delta}\rceil+\log\Delta)
; and to obtain the second equation we apply again
Lemma

27
.
Combining the last two equations gives

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|S|]\leq\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[|S^{\ast}|]\cdot O(\log\bar{\Delta})
.
∎

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[X\cdot\mathds{1}_{X\geq\ell}]=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\geq\ell}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(X>\ell^{\prime})\ +\ \ell\cdot\operatorname{\mathds{P}}(X\geq\ell).
