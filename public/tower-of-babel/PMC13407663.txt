Starting Young: Responsive Neurostimulation in Children
Authors: Shruti Agashe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Responsive Neurostimulation in Children, Adolescents, and Young Adults-Longitudinal Effectiveness and Safety Lee ST, Patel S, Karakas C, Houck K, Trandafir C, McGinley MJ, Mohanty D, Riviello JJ, Weiner HL, Curry D, Ali I. Neurotherapeutics. 2026 Mar 19;23(2):e00865. doi: 10.1016/j.neurot.2026.e00865. Epub ahead of print. PMID: 41863185; PMCID: PMC13010426Drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) remains challenging to treat, and seizure control often fluctuates over time. Responsive neurostimulation (RNS) is increasingly used in pediatric and young adult populations, yet long-term effectiveness and safety data are limited. We evaluated longitudinal seizure control trajectories and safety of RNS in children, adolescents, and young adults treated at our center. Seizure frequency was assessed at approximately 6-month intervals from serial follow-up visits through the most recent follow-up, with each sampled visit categorized by ≥50% (all-responder, AR) or ≥90% (super-responder, SR) seizure reduction. We examined the persistence and durability of seizure control and used recurrent-event regression to identify clinical modifiers of sustained response. Responder rates improved over time, with 57% and 75% of patients achieving AR status at 24 and 47 mean months post-implantation, respectively; corresponding SR rates were 22% and 33%. Once achieved, seizure control was typically maintained: the probability of remaining an AR for 24 months was 70%, and the median proportion of follow-up with sustained response exceeded 85% across age groups. Concurrent epilepsy surgery favored maintenance of AR status (HR 1.83, p = 0.02), while syndrome-related epilepsies reduced the likelihood of sustained SR status (HR 0.30, p = 0.03). Age at implantation and responder status at 24 months both associated with long-term seizure control. Serious (0.032 events/person-year) and non-serious (0.038 events/person-year) adverse effects were infrequent. RNS provided durable benefit for most, though not all, patients, reflecting the dynamic nature of seizure control. The favorable effectiveness and safety profile support use of RNS in young patients with DRE.

Responsive neurostimulation (RNS), like most other neuromodulation modalities, has traditionally been studied with large clinical studies through an adult focal epilepsy lens: 1 or 2 seizure foci, mature cranial anatomy, and relatively stable seizure networks. The study summarized here is important because it attempts to answer 2 important questions: “Is pediatric RNS effective and safe in the real-world?”; and an even more clinically meaningful question: “How stable is seizure control over time in children, adolescents, and young adults treated with RNS?” This distinction is especially relevant in pediatric drug-resistant epilepsy, where seizure networks evolve alongside ongoing brain maturation and broader neurodevelopmental trajectories.

The central strength of the study is its longitudinal framing. Rather than relying only on seizure reduction at the most recent follow-up, the authors sampled seizure outcomes at approximately 6-month intervals and examined 2 clinically intuitive constructs: persistence and durability. Persistence was defined as maintaining a given seizure reduction threshold across consecutive sampled visits, while durability reflected the proportion of follow-up time spent at or above that response threshold after it was first achieved. Seizure control in DRE is rarely static, and the study addresses one of the major weaknesses of retrospective neuromodulation literature: the over-reliance on a single endpoint that may not represent the lived trajectory of the patient.

At approximately 24 months, 57% of patients were “all responders (AR)” defined as ≥50% seizure reduction, and 22% were “super responders (SR)” defined as ≥90% seizure reduction. By a mean of 47 months, these rates increased to 75% and 33%, respectively, with seizure freedom reported in 33% of the smaller group still represented at that time point. These findings align with the adult RNS experience, where in the majority of people, seizure reduction tends to improve gradually over time rather than reaching a static plateau early after implantation. 1 Families often ask whether improvement after RNS is “real” or, like previously tried medications, this is expected to be a “honeymoon” effect. In this study, the probability of maintaining responder status was 80% at 12 months and 70% at 24 months after first achieving responder status. Median durability after initial AR status was high across age groups: 100% in children, 87% in adolescents, and 91% in young adults. However, it also shows that a meaningful minority lose response over time. A 70% probability of maintaining ≥50% reduction at 24 months also means that approximately 30% may not maintain that level of benefit. The distinction between AR and SR status is also valuable. The study found that persistence of SR status was less robust: only 14% maintained SR status across 4 consecutive sampled visits, and the probability of maintaining SR status at 24 months after first achieving it was 31%. Similarly, seizure freedom was uncommon and often transient, with only 3 of 37 patients maintaining seizure freedom across 4 consecutive visits. Taken together, these findings suggest that RNS can produce substantial and durable seizure reduction, but sustained seizure freedom remains uncommon. The most important finding may not be the responder rate itself, but the observation that once meaningful seizure reduction was achieved, it was often maintained. These findings are exceptionally relevant to “real-world” counseling in the pediatric population as they transition to adolescence and adulthood.

The findings in the study on syndromic epilepsies are particularly relevant. In these patients, seizure burden is often embedded within a broader disorder of neurodevelopment, cognition, behavior, and network dysfunction. In this cohort, syndrome-related epilepsies included Lennox-Gastaut Syndrome, tuberous sclerosis Complex, febrile Infection-related epilepsy syndrome, and septo-optic dysplasia, affecting 11 of 56 patients overall: 3/16 children, 4/24 adolescents, and 4/16 young adults. Syndrome-related epilepsy was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of sustained SR status, with an HR of 0.30 for maintaining ≥90% seizure reduction (95% CI 0.11-0.88, P = .03). However sustained AR status appeared achievable and durable across the cohort: 73% of patients maintained ≥50% seizure reduction for at least 1 sampled visit, 62% for 2 consecutive visits, and 54% for 4 consecutive visits; after first achieving AR status, the probability of maintaining it was 80% at 12 months and 70% at 24 months. For children with syndromic epilepsy, this degree of sustained seizure reduction may still reduce hospitalizations, injuries, medication escalation, caregiver burden, and developmental disruption, even when seizure freedom or super-response is unlikely.

The breadth of targeting is both a strength and a limitation. The cohort included cortical-cortical, thalamocortical, and bithalamic active lead configurations, with targets spanning frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, insular, centromedian, anterior nucleus, and pulvinar regions. This reflects real-world pediatric epilepsy practice, where patients often do not fit clean adult trial categories. Many have multifocal epilepsy, malformations of cortical development, developmental lesions, or network-level epilepsies. However, this heterogeneity limits mechanistic interpretation. A cortical-cortical regional stimulation strategy for a focal cortical dysplasia is not the same intervention as centromedian thalamic stimulation for a broader network epilepsy. 2 The overall responder rate is useful for counseling, but target-specific conclusions require larger multicenter datasets with stratification by epilepsy type, lead location, stimulation pathway, and electrographic phenotype.

Device implantation itself, particularly depth lead placement in epileptogenic or network-relevant structures, can transiently alter seizures commonly termed as a microlesional effect. In the present study, SR and seizure-free states were not always sustained, raising the possibility that some early improvements could reflect implantation effects rather than chronic stimulation alone. This is not a criticism unique to this study; it is a persistent challenge in all retrospective neuromodulation research.

The finding that concurrent epilepsy surgery was associated with improved maintenance of ≥50% response is clinically important but difficult to interpret. Approximately one-third of the cohort underwent some form of concurrent surgery, including laser ablation, resection, or corpus callosotomy. This raises a key causal question: did RNS drive the sustained response, or did the concurrent surgery do much of the therapeutic work? In some cases, RNS may have treated residual eloquent cortex or bilateral network nodes after partial surgical treatment. In others, the surgery may have reduced the dominant seizure generator while RNS provided adjunctive network modulation. This “hybrid” approach may be especially relevant in pediatric epilepsy, where malformations of cortical development, multilobar lesions, bilateral abnormalities, or eloquent cortex involvement often limit complete resection. 2 However, without separating staged versus simultaneous procedures, quantifying the extent of resection or ablation, and analyzing electrographic burden after surgery, it is difficult to attribute benefit specifically to RNS. Given the strength of having objective electrophysiological data with RNS, future studies can explicitly analyze time to stimulation onset, stimulation-off intervals, early post-implant seizure reduction before therapeutic stimulation, and chronic ECoG trends such as long episodes, detections, magnet swipes, and electrographic seizure burden to add information about network-level neuromodulation.

Safety is reassuring but should be interpreted cautiously. Serious adverse events were infrequent, with hemorrhage occurring in 2 adults, infection in 1 child, and stimulation-related worsening of seizures in 1 child. Non-serious stimulation-related symptoms occurred in 9%. These rates appear broadly consistent with the favorable safety profile reported in adult and pediatric RNS literature, though direct comparison is limited by cohort size, single-center design, target heterogeneity, and follow-up duration. The study would also be strengthened by neuropsychological and developmental outcomes. In adults, seizure reduction is often the primary metric. In children, that is not enough. A 50% seizure reduction may be highly meaningful if it improves school attendance, language acquisition, sleep, attention, behavior, injury risk, or caregiver burden. Pediatric RNS studies should therefore include these metrics for a more holistic picture of longitudinal responses.

The next step to build robust evidence would be multicenter prospective pediatric RNS studies that combine clinical seizure outcomes with chronic ECoG biomarkers, medication tracking, neuropsychological measures, and developmentally informed imaging. Pediatric RNS should not simply be adult RNS applied to smaller patients. It is a different therapeutic context: one in which the epileptic network, cranial anatomy, and the child's developmental trajectory are all changing over time. This study is valuable because it embraces that longitudinal reality rather than reducing outcome to a single point estimate.
