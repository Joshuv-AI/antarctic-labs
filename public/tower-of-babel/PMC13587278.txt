Postoperative rupture of bronchial stump inducing severe hemorrhage from pulmonary artery trunk: a case report
Authors: Xueqiang Wei, Hao Yang, Xi Wang, YujieLei, Xin Tang, Qihan Zi, Jianze Zhang, Jie Mao, Yizhou Peng, Yunchao Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surgical resection constitutes the cornerstone of treatment for lung cancer, a disease with high global incidence. Among postoperative complications, delayed fatal hemorrhage occurring beyond 30 days postoperatively has garnered scant attention in clinical and research settings. Notably, there are no documented cases of vascular rupture and bleeding induced by chronic mechanical friction between the bronchial stump and pulmonary artery trunk to date. Following pulmonary lobectomy, the anatomical proximity between the bronchial stump and pulmonary artery trunk creates a potential risk of delayed pulmonary artery hemorrhage secondary to bronchial stump-related pathology. Herein, we report a case of a 53-year-old male patient who presented with sudden massive hemoptysis on the 40th day after right upper lobectomy. Bronchial artery embolization was initially performed but failed to control the bleeding. Emergency thoracotomy revealed a 3-mm rupture with active bleeding in the right pulmonary artery trunk, accompanied by a 2-mm fistula at the right upper lobe bronchial stump. Intraoperatively, the vascular rupture was repaired using a pericardial patch, and both the repaired vessel and the bronchial stump were wrapped with an intercostal muscle flap, achieving successful hemostasis. The patient experienced an uneventful postoperative recovery and remained alive and well at the 3-month follow-up. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report and the accompanying clinical images.

Background

Lung cancer is the most prevalent and lethal malignancy worldwide. Optimizing its surgical management strategies has long been a core focus of clinical research. Authoritative international guidelines and numerous high-quality evidence-based studies have consistently confirmed that anatomical lobectomy combined with systematic mediastinal lymphadenectomy constitutes the standard radical procedure for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [1, 2]. Compared with sublobar resection, lobectomy significantly reduces the local recurrence rate and improves long-term survival outcomes [3–5].

With the rapid advancement of minimally invasive techniques, uniportal video-assisted thoracic surgery (uVATS) lobectomy has significantly reduced the incidence of perioperative complications and improved patient tolerance, while maintaining favorable oncological outcomes [6, 7]. Nevertheless, postoperative complications—beyond common adverse events such as persistent air leak, atelectasis, cardiac arrhythmias, and bronchopleural fistula [8, 9]—remain a critical determinant of long-term patient prognosis and survival. The underlying causes of postoperative mortality remain unclear in a subset of patients. A typical example is delayed postoperative pulmonary artery hemorrhage: this complication is notoriously difficult to detect in the early stage and is associated with devastating consequences that can directly lead to fatal outcomes.

It is noteworthy that the anatomical relationship between the bronchial stump and adjacent pulmonary artery, together with their potential interactions, has not garnered sufficient attention in clinical and research contexts. Following lobectomy—particularly resection of the left upper lobe or right middle lobe—the bronchial stump adheres closely to the pulmonary artery, in some cases even inducing compression and friction. Driven by the dual effects of respiratory volume fluctuations and cardiac pulsation, the bronchial stump exerts micro-serrated shear stress on the pulmonary artery wall, triggering a cascade of pathophysiological events: adventitial ischemia → inflammatory necrosis → pseudoaneurysm formation → delayed rupture [10]. To date, bronchial stump friction-related pulmonary artery bleeding remains an underrecognized and underemphasized distinct clinical entity. This unique pathogenic mechanism is highly prone to misdiagnosis as bronchial artery hemorrhage in clinical practice, or even missed diagnosis entirely, potentially contributing to unexplained mortality following pulmonary resection [11].

Existing clinical evidence suggests that early hemorrhage (< 7 days) after lobectomy is predominantly attributed to inadequate management of vascular stumps or insufficient ligation, whereas delayed hemorrhage (> 30 days) is characterized by complex etiologies, diagnostic dilemmas, and poor clinical prognosis [12]. Although isolated cases of hemorrhage from pseudoaneurysms of the right lower pulmonary artery stump and bronchial artery have been documented [13], a systematic account of transmural pulmonary artery rupture induced by mechanical friction between the bronchial stump and pulmonary artery trunk remains lacking. Herein, we report a rare case of acute life-threatening hemorrhage arising from right pulmonary artery trunk rupture, caused by prolonged mechanical friction between the bronchial stump and pulmonary artery trunk, which occurred on the 40th day after right upper lobectomy. The patient was successfully managed with emergency thoracotomy. Combined with a systematic review of relevant literature, we propose the pathogenesis and preventive strategies for bronchial stump friction-related pulmonary artery bleeding, aiming to enhance thoracic surgeons’ awareness and vigilance regarding this highly lethal, insidious complication.

Case report

A 53-year-old male patient with a diagnosis of right upper lobe invasive adenocarcinoma was emergently admitted due to acute hemoptysis occurring 40 days after uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic right upper lobectomy. Urgent blood routine examination showed no significant increase in white blood cells and other infection indicators. Flexible bronchoscopy revealed inadequate healing of the right upper lobe bronchial stump with a 2-mm fistula formation, from which active bleeding was identified. Temporary occlusion and compression hemostasis were performed using a bronchial occlusion catheter, and bronchial artery embolization was concurrently attempted to control the hemorrhage. However, these minimally invasive interventions failed to achieve definitive hemostasis, prompting an emergency thoracotomy.

Emergency exploratory thoracotomy revealed dense adhesions between the right upper lobe bronchial stump and the right main pulmonary artery. A 3-mm defect with active bleeding was identified in the RMPA wall, and a 2-mm fistulous tract was confirmed at the adhesion site between the bronchial stump and pulmonary artery, with no pleural empyema, mediastinal infection, or localized abscess. Intraoperatively, a segment of pericardium was harvested for vascular repair and used to suture the arterial defect. Additionally, an intercostal muscle flap was dissected to reinforce, repair, and encapsulate the right upper lobe bronchial stump, achieving successful hemostasis (Fig. 1). Postoperatively, the patient had an uneventful recovery, as confirmed by follow-up chest radiography, and remained in good health during the 3-month follow-up.

Discussion

Delayed massive hemorrhage following pulmonary lobectomy is a rare but life-threatening complication in thoracic surgery. The primary objective of this case report is to elucidate the unique and close anatomical relationship between the bronchial stump and pulmonary artery after lobectomy, highlighting the risk of delayed pulmonary artery hemorrhage induced by long-term mechanical friction between these two structures—a process that can lead to pulmonary artery rupture and bronchial stump injury. This risk may serve as one of the critical underlying causes of unexplained postoperative mortality in patients undergoing lobectomy. Fatal hemorrhage occurring more than 30 days after lobectomy has an extremely low detection rate. Among its etiological factors, friction-induced pulmonary arterial bleeding stems from chronic mechanical friction between the bronchial stump and main pulmonary artery trunk: each cardiac cycle or inspiration exerts a “micro-sawing” effect. Cumulative exposure to over 100,000 such cycles is sufficient to impair the tensile strength of the vascular wall [6, 14, 15], while more than 1,000,000 cumulative cycles can disrupt collagen fibers in the vascular adventitia, ultimately resulting in adventitial necrosis and transmural rupture. However, this pathological process has received scant attention to date and lacks systematic elaboration in the existing literature [16, 17].

After transecting the lobar bronchus with a linear stapler during pulmonary lobectomy, the two ends of the bronchial stump form right angles, presenting with high rigidity and sharp edges. Anatomically, such bronchial stumps lie in close apposition to the main pulmonary artery trunk—meaning that postoperatively, the rigid, sharp-edged bronchial stump repeatedly rubs against and irritates the main pulmonary artery trunk with each cardiac pulsation. This process induces arterial injury and rupture, followed by hemorrhage, a risk that is particularly prominent after left upper lobectomy or right middle lobectomy. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the intimate positional relationship between the bronchial stump and the main pulmonary artery trunk is clearly visible. Figure 2a and b further demonstrate the hazard posed by the sharp-edged stump to the pulmonary artery trunk (circled in white). Regrettably, this risk remains unrecognized among thoracic surgeons, and no targeted preventive measures have been implemented in clinical practice.

To verify this clinical concern, we conducted a survey involving 121 thoracic surgeons from 24 major thoracic surgery centers across China. The results showed that only 2.5% of the surveyed surgeons were aware of this risk, and none had adopted any preventive strategies in their clinical work. Consequently, enhancing awareness of this risk and popularizing the preventive measures we have developed are of paramount importance for ensuring postoperative safety in lobectomy patients. Therefore, we propose the novel concept of “bronchial friction-related pulmonary artery bleeding (BFRPAB)”, with the core objective of drawing more thoracic surgeons’ attention to this underrecognized postoperative risk and prompting the implementation of corresponding measures to prevent pulmonary artery rupture and hemorrhage induced by the bronchial stump—a complication that is almost invariably fatal once it occurs. Our current preventive strategy involves interposing artificial fibrous tissue materials between the bronchial stump and the pulmonary artery (as shown in Fig. 2c and d), which effectively separates the two structures, eliminates mechanical friction, and thus completely eradicates the potential hazard of BFRPAB. Additionally, spraying a fibrin glue-collagen complex to achieve biological sealing of the space between the bronchial stump and the pulmonary artery constitutes another viable prophylactic option [18, 19]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first detailed report on bronchial stump friction-related pulmonary artery bleeding. Further research and refinement are warranted to explore more effective and convenient preventive modalities and biomaterials.

This case report aims to remind thoracic surgeons to be vigilant about the risk of BFRPAB and to provide sufficient prevention for the safety of patients’ lives. However, it still has certain limitations in the completeness of the overall data as it is an emergency case. We did not collect intraoperative images of the patient undergoing right upper lobe resection in this case. The concept of BFRPAB lacks strong experimental evidence, and we will use relevant experiments and clinical evidence to confirm this later.
