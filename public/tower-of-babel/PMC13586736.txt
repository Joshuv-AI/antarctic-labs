Demographic, Household, and Regional Factors Associated With Firearm Access in a U.S. Nationally Representative Sample of Emerging Adults Aged 18–29 Years
Authors: Lisa Fedina, Xinlu Li, Todd I. Herrenkohl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
HIGHLIGHTS•A total of 23.8% of U.S. adults aged 18–29 years report household firearm access.•Access is higher among White, multiracial, male, married, Midwest/South adults.•Access varies by intersecting identity, age, and region in young adulthood.•Findings support tailored, culturally responsive injury prevention efforts.

INTRODUCTION

Firearm-related injury and mortality constitute a significant public health crisis in the U.S.1 Young adults aged 21–24 years are most impacted by firearm-related injuries in the U.S.2,3; that is, the highest rate of firearm-related injury, both fatal and nonfatal, has been observed among people aged 20–24 years, over 10 times higher than observed in those younger than 15 years or aged ≥60 years.2 Firearm-related injuries have become the leading cause of death among children and adolescents since 2016.4 According to the Centers for Disease Control’s Web-based Injury Statistics Query and Reporting System data from 2024, firearm-involved injury accounted for more than half of suicide mortality among emerging adults aged 15–24 years (55.5%) and 25–34 years (52.3%).5 These data underscore the need to understand the prevalence, nature, and context of household firearm access among emerging and young adult populations. Although much of the existing literature focuses on the broad epidemiology of firearm ownership,6, 7, 8 there is a critical need to distinguish between individual ownership and household access, because the mere presence of a firearm in the home remains the primary environmental determinant of suicide and accidental injury.1,6 Studies have found that nearly one third of adolescents have access to firearms, and more than half of those adolescents reported that they can access firearms within their household.9,10 Understanding specific patterns of firearm access across diverse demographic, regional, and household characteristics is essential for identifying which subgroups of young adults are at the greatest risk of lethal outcomes.

Current research mainly focuses on adolescents (aged 10–19 years) or relies on aggregated state-level data.8,11 Research on adolescents has found that those living in a household with access to firearms are more likely to be male, to be older, to be White, to reside in the South or Midwest, and to live in rural areas.9,11,12 Similarly, within the population aged ≥18 years, gender, race, and urbanicity also emerged as key predictors of firearm access and suicide risk.8,11,12 In studies of adults aged ≥18 years, those with firearm access are more likely to be male, White, married, and residing outside of an urban area.12,13

Few studies have examined demographic variation in firearm access among subgroups, specifically among the emerging adult population aged 18–29 years, such as by race, ethnicity, gender identity, sexual orientation, urbanicity, and U.S. region.14 Arnett (2000) characterizes emerging adulthood as a distinct developmental period (ages 18–29 years) defined by identity exploration, instability, and a unique self-focused orientation. Because this stage is marked by the highest rates of residential mobility and the peak of risk-taking behaviors, it represents a critical window where changes in social and household contexts—such as identity development, marriage, and frequent changes in residence—can significantly alter an individual’s proximity to and access to firearms.

Although some research provides data on the general adult population (those aged ≥18 years)13,15 and includes comparisons between age cohorts,16 there is a need to examine emerging adulthood as a distinct developmental period and examine how individual demographics (e.g., race, ethnicity, gender identity), household (e.g., size, home ownership status), and regional (e.g., U.S. region, urbanicity) characteristics collectively influence firearm access within this high-risk decade. Examining the intersection of these factors is necessary, because firearm access is deeply embedded in specific cultural, economic, and regional contexts. For emerging adults (aged 18–29 years), this period is marked by significant life transitions, such as moving away from a parental home, entering the workforce, or starting new families, which may introduce new patterns of firearm exposure.

To address this need, data were analyzed from a U.S. nationally representative survey of emerging adults aged 18–29 years (N=1,077) to assess firearm access in this population and its association with a range of demographic, household, and regional-level characteristics. By providing a comprehensive descriptive portrait of the factors associated with household firearm access, this study’s findings can guide universal prevention safety messaging and tailored, culturally responsive prevention strategies that address the unique social and environmental contexts of emerging adults aged 18–29 years. On the basis of prior research on household firearm access patterns among the broader U.S. adult population,13,17 it was hypothesized that emerging adults living in rural areas, identifying as White, identifying as cisgender males, and with higher incomes would report greater household access to firearms.7,13,15 It was also hypothesized that marital status and larger household sizes would be significantly associated with increased firearm access.13 Finally, it was expected that the presence of children in the home would not significantly reduce the likelihood of firearm access, because prior studies suggest that parents of young children own firearms at rates similar to those of nonparents.18,19

METHODS

Study Sample

A cross-sectional online survey was administered to a nationally representative sample of U.S. emerging adults aged 18–29 years in January 2021. Participants were recruited through AmeriSpeak using a mixed-modal recruitment strategy (i.e., address-based sampling and random digit dialing).20,21 AmeriSpeak is a probability-based survey panel designed to be representative of the U.S. household population and includes oversampling of African American, Hispanic or Latino/a, and sexual minority (gay/lesbian/bisexual/queer) participants and has been used widely in public health survey research, including firearm-related studies.22, 23, 24, 25 The eligibility criteria for inclusion in the study were English-speaking individuals aged 18–29 years residing in the U.S. Additional details on the study methods were reported elsewhere.26

A total of 6,862 participants were initially invited to the study. Of these, 1,077 participants completed the survey (survey completion rate=15.7%). The weighted household recruitment percentage was 22.7%, accounting for panel recruitment, panel retention, and survey participation. Weights were adjusted for differential probabilities of selection, eligibility, and nonresponse. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants, and all study procedures were approved by the IRBs at the University of Michigan and the University of Chicago.

Measures

A single self-report screening item to measure general household access to firearms was Do you currently have access to a firearm in your home? (yes or no), consistent with prior survey research.19,27

Self-reported demographic variables collected by the National Opinion Research Center, which maintains data on AmeriSpeak panel participants, included age group, income, education level, race, ethnicity, and marital status. Age was treated as a categorical variable divided into 4 developmental brackets for comparisons: 18–20, 21–23, 24–26, and 27–29 years. Education level included 4 groups: no high-school diploma, high-school diploma/GED, some college, and college graduate. Annual household income included 4 levels: <$19,999; $20,000–$39,999; $40,000–$84,999; and ≥$85,000. Racial and ethnic data of participants included whether participants identified as White non-Hispanic, Black/African American non-Hispanic, Hispanic or Latino/a, multiracial or other non-Hispanic, or Asian non-Hispanic. Marital status was initially assessed across 6 categories: married, widowed, divorced, separated, never married, and living with a partner, which was recoded into a dichotomous variable (married versus nonmarried) for analysis on the basis of the distribution of responses.

Additional demographics were asked among survey participants, including gender identity and sexual orientation. To assess gender identity, survey participants were asked What is your current gender identity? Response options included male, female, transgender male to female, transgender female to male, do not identify as male or female, third gender or nonbinary, or not sure. Gender identity was recoded into 3 groups on the basis of the distribution of responses: cisgender male, cisgender female, and transgender/do not identify as male or female/nonbinary/not sure. To assess sexual orientation, participants were asked Do you consider yourself to be…, and response options included heterosexual/straight, gay/lesbian/homosexual, bisexual, asexual, queer, sexual orientation not specified, or not sure. Sexual orientation was recoded into a dichotomous variable on the basis of the distribution of responses (heterosexual/straight or gay/lesbian/homosexual/bisexual/asexual/queer/sexual orientation not specified/unsure).

Household and regional variables collected by the National Opinion Research Center included home renter status, household size, number of children in the home, U.S. geographic region, household internet access, housing type, and employment status. Home renter status included 3 groups—owned or being bought by the participant or someone in the household, rented for cash, or occupied without payment of cash rent—and was recoded into a dichotomous variable to indicate home renter (rented for cash) or nonrenter (owned by the participant or someone in the household or occupied without payment of cash rent). Total household size ranged from 1 to 6 or more individuals, and the number of children (aged <18 years) residing in the home ranged from 0 to 6 or more, which were treated as continuous variables. For U.S. geographic region, participants were grouped into the 4 major U.S. Census regions: Northeast, Midwest, South, and West. Household internet access was a dichotomous variable that indicated whether participants had access to dial-up, DSL, or cable broadband at home (yes) or had no access (no). Housing type was classified into 5 distinct categories—a 1-family house detached from any other house, a 1-family house attached to 1 or more houses, a building with 2 or more apartments, a mobile home or trailer, or other (e.g., boat, RV, van)—and was recoded into 3 groups on the basis of the distribution of this variable: 1-family house, apartment building, or mobile home/trailer/other. Finally, community type or urbanicity was measured with a self-report item on the survey asking participants the following question: What best describes the type of community you live in? Response options included urban, suburban, or rural.

Statistical Analysis

Bivariate and multivariate analyses were used to assess the factors associated with firearm access. Logistic regression was used to model associations between demographic variables and firearm access to understand the combined effects of demographic, household, and regional factors associated with firearm access. Reference groups for logistic regression analyses were selected on the basis of theory and findings from prior firearm-related research to compare the findings with those of other studies. Specifically, urban residence, the Northeast region, White non-Hispanic identity, cisgender male, heterosexual orientation, less than a high-school education, and an income <$19,999 served as reference categories on the basis of prior studies identifying trends in these demographics in relation to increased or decreased access to firearms.8,11, 12, 13, 14,17, 18, 19 For age-based comparisons, the 18–20 cohort was used as the reference to evaluate shifts in access throughout the emerging adult period. All analyses were conducted using weighted data in SPSS, Version 29.

RESULTS

The overall sample was nearly evenly split by gender (50.2% cisgender women, 47.4% cisgender men, 2.3% transgender/nonbinary). Most participants identified as White (53.2%), followed by Hispanic or Latino/a (22.6%), Black (14.0%), Asian (6.5%), and multiracial or other (3.7%). A total of 18.8% of participants identified as sexual minorities. Socioeconomic and educational variables varied across the cohort; whereas 25% were college graduates, the largest educational group reported having some college (35.4%). Household income was most frequently reported in the $40,000–$84,999 range (37.9%). Geographically, the largest proportion of participants resided in the South (38.0%), followed by the West (24.6%), the Midwest (20.3%), and the Northeast (17.1%). Results from adjusted logistic regression models indicate that emerging adults from the racial/ethnic groups of Black/African American, Hispanic or Latino/a, and Asian had lower odds of firearm access than White emerging adults (AOR=0.48, 95% CI=0.28, 0.81, p=0.01; AOR=0.64, 95% CI=0.42, 0.96, p=0.03; AOR=0.33, 95% CI=0.14, 0.79, p=0.01, respectively) after controlling for other variables (age, income, education, gender, sexual orientation, employment, children in home, internet access, region, marital status, urbanicity, home renter, and home type). The odds of firearm access for sexual minority emerging adults were lower than those of heterosexual emerging adults (AOR=0.36, 95% CI=0.22, 0.59, p<0.001). For emerging adults who were married, the odds were twice those of nonmarried emerging adults (AOR=2.44, 95% CI=1.58, 3.76, p<0.001). Emerging adults in rural settings had 1.63 times higher odds of household firearm access than those in urban areas, whereas those in suburban areas had 33% lower odds of access (AOR=1.63, 95% CI=1.10, 2.43, p=0.02 and AOR=0.67, 95% CI=0.46, 0.98, p=0.04, respectively). Finally, the odds of firearm access for emerging adults in the Midwest and South were more than twice the odds of those in the Northeast (AOR=2.02, 95% CI=1.16, 3.52, p<0.01 and AOR=2.55, 95% CI=1.52, 4.28, p<0.001, respectively) (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

Although prior research has established broad demographic patterns of firearm ownership,6, 7, 8 this study extends these findings by isolating the 18–29 emerging adult population—a stage uniquely characterized by frequent residential shifts and changes in household composition that inherently alter an individual's proximity to and access to firearms. By integrating variables such as sexual orientation, marital status, and household composition and other variables frequently omitted from national firearm surveillance data, this study contributes a more intersectional understanding of firearm access during this critical developmental period.

Overall, this study found that nearly 1 in 4 (23.8%) U.S. emerging adults have household access to firearms. This was notably lower than broader national estimates of household firearm access among the general adult population aged ≥18 years (about 40%) as well as adolescents in middle school or high school (nearly one third).9,17,19 This finding suggests that household firearm access is not uniform across the lifespan but varies significantly across age cohorts into adulthood. By focusing on emerging adulthood as a distinct developmental stage, this study highlights the importance of tracking firearm access during a period of unique social and residential transition—such as the shift from a parental home to more independent living—and indicates the need for age-specific data in future public health research.

Results regarding demographic and regional differences largely align with and extend recent national data among adults ≥18 years. For example, Morral et al.13 documented persistent demographic shifts in household firearm prevalence from 1990 to 2018, noting higher rates among White and married populations. The findings from this study mirror these trends within the 18–29 age cohort, specifically identifying that married emerging adults had twice the odds of access as their nonmarried peers. Furthermore, although Anestis and colleagues15 highlighted significant firearm access among Black and American Indian/Alaska Native adults, the study’s adjusted models show that when controlling for socioeconomic factors, Black, Latino/a, and Asian emerging adults reported significantly lower odds of household access than their White counterparts. This suggests that for young adults of color, the primary risk may stem from community-level exposure rather than direct household access, a distinction that is vital for tailoring firearm violence intervention programs.

The urbanicity and geographic differences observed in this study align with and extend prior research. Existing literature has consistently identified higher proportions of household firearm access13 and related constructs, including firearm ownership,15 among rural adults than among their urban counterparts broadly within an 18 and older age population. Findings from this study extend this knowledge to the U.S. emerging adult population. By focusing solely on a cohort of emerging adults, this study’s findings provide a more granular understanding of household firearm access during a critical developmental period that has been previously underrepresented in the literature. These data suggest that the rural–urban divide in firearm access is established early in adulthood and warrants targeted, developmentally informed prevention strategies.

Regarding geographic differences, this study found that even among the highly mobile emerging adult population, more participants residing in the Midwest (29.4%) or South (27.9%) have greater firearm access than those in other U.S. Census regions. These findings align with the established literature—specifically, the work of Morral et al.,13 which identifies the Southern region as maintaining the highest prevalence of household firearm access. Notably, this study was limited to using 4 broad U.S. Census regions to categorize geographic differences, which may obscure variations in firearm access occurring at the state or local levels. For example, Anestis, Bond, and Semenza (2023) found distinct regional differences in general firearm ownership across 5 states, suggesting that national averages often mask critical state-level realities. Morral and colleagues’13 study, using state-level modeling, suggests that household firearm access rates can vary between neighboring states within the same census region. Given that emerging adulthood is a distinct developmental period marked by increased mobility and shifting social environments, the lack of state-level data limits this study’s ability to identify specific geographic hotspots or the impact of varying state-level firearm policies on this population. Future research that employs larger, nationally representative samples is needed to examine firearm access among emerging adults at the state and local levels. Such studies would be useful for understanding how local cultural norms and state-specific legislative environments influence firearm access during this critical life transition.

Hemenway et al.16 identified that although firearm ownership patterns between Black and White adults differ, the 18–29 age bracket remains a high-vulnerability window for firearm-related injury and suicide. These data complement that work by showing that sexual minority emerging adults have significantly lower odds of household access (AOR=0.36) than their heterosexual peers. By identifying these specific patterns—such as high firearm access in rural, married, and Midwestern and Southern subgroups, versus lower household access among sexual and gender minority and Asian young adults—public health practitioners can move toward pinpointed, culturally responsive means safety interventions that address the specific environmental risks of each community.28, 29, 30

Limitations

Several limitations to the study should be noted. First, the measure of household firearm access is a self-report measure and does not assess access to firearms outside the home, such as borrowing from friends or neighborhood supply. Relatedly, the measure does not distinguish between access and ownership or possession, including storage practices, which can provide greater context into emerging adults’ experiences. Miller and colleagues31 (2021) found that young adults aged 18–29 years had the lowest odds of firearm purchasing, which further underscores the likelihood that firearm access among young adults stems from various sources. Future studies are needed to address dimensions of firearm access, ownership, carrying behaviors, and storage to understand causal pathways, including outcomes related to firearm injury. Second, the researchers were unable to conduct a granular analysis of firearm access at other regional or state levels in these data; as such, this study’s broad regional approach cannot account for significant within-region heterogeneity. This limitation warrants further investigation in future large-scale studies as well as small-area studies to inform local interventions. Third, given the sensitive nature of firearm-related data, emerging adults may have under-reported access owing to social desirability bias and respondent concerns regarding privacy or the legality of possession.32 Under-reporting of firearms is a documented challenge in national surveys, where individuals in certain demographic or geographic subgroups may be more reluctant to disclose household firearm access to researchers.32 Finally, this study examines demographic and regional characteristics without accounting for the underlying social and structural determinants that often drive these subgroup differences.33,34 Future research utilizing larger samples and prospective designs is needed to disentangle the complex relationships between these structural drivers and firearm access among young adults.24

CONCLUSIONS

This study’s findings underscore the public health significance of addressing firearm access among young adults aged 18–29 years. Overall, the high prevalence of household firearm access observed in this age group has significant implications for the burden of firearm injury among emerging adults. The documented demographic and regional differences highlight the need for targeted, culturally responsive prevention strategies that account for the social and geographic contexts influencing firearm access. For example, public health community-based violence intervention programs should address structural risks in urban settings35 and provide identity-affirming mental health supports for sexual and gender minority young adults that account for their unique risk profiles36 rather than relying on generalized messaging. Prevention strategies to reduce firearm-related harm and injury should prioritize evidence-based approaches, such as safe storage campaigns, community-based violence prevention initiatives, and firearm injury and firearm-related suicide prevention programs that engage emerging adults in both urban and rural settings.37,38 By recognizing and addressing these differences, public health practitioners and policymakers can advance more equitable approaches to prevent firearm injuries and deaths among young emerging adults.
