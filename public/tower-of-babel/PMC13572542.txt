Predictive modeling of strength characteristics of sustainable concrete using artificial neural networks with luffa fiber and marble dust
Authors: S Anandaraj, S Shaniya, S Swaminathan, S Suganya, G Sindhu Priya, L Balaji, A Dhanalakshmi, Dawit Tafesse Gebreyohannes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study involves an integrated approach to predict the mechanical properties of luffa fiber and marble dust- based concrete. It employs Artificial Neural Network (ANN) for mechanical properties prediction aiming for a higher accuracy than currently available models. The composite material used marble dust in the proportion 0–40% as fine aggregate replacement and luffa fiber in the proportion 0–2% as the natural reinforcement. Experimental results implied that the composite containing 20% marble dust and 1% luffa fiber exhibited greatest mechanical characteristics- Compressive strength of 34.5 MPa, flexural strength of 6.2 MPa and split tensile strength of 4.25 MPa. This improvement was attributed to enhanced particle packing by marble dust and effective crack bridging by treated luffa fiber. A single multi-output feedforward multilayer perceptron (MLP) ANN consisting of two hidden layers of 64 and 32 neurons with ReLU activation functions and a three-neuron linear output layer was developed for simultaneously modelling the nonlinear interactions between the input variables and strength outputs. The model was trained on 70% of the dataset, with 15% for validation and 15% for testing. The ANN model was able to predict all three mechanical strength properties simultaneously with a high degree of accuracy as demonstrated by R² values of 0.89 (compressive strength), 0.94 (flexural strength), and 0.96 (split tensile strength) for the training data sets and small root mean square error (RMSE) values and negligible bias. The 100% a20 score indicated that all the samples from the predictions fell within ± 20% of the actual experimental values, demonstrating good robustness or generality. This research basically aims to address a major gap in the existing works by exploring the limited application of ANN in predicting the performance of hybrid sustainable concrete mixes incorporating both marble dust and plant-based fibers by developing a predictive model which is capable of capturing the complex interactions between multiple factors and enhancing the prediction efficiency, minimizing the dependency on extensive experimental investigations thereby promoting data driven evaluation of strength characteristics in sustainable concrete composites.Keywords: Strength prediction, Luffa fiber, Marble dust, ANNSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The rate of concrete production has quadrupled over the last three decades, reaching a rate of 26 Gt annually. Additionally, the demand for aggregates, estimated to be around 20 Gt annually, has surpassed the total amount of fossil fuel extracted globally1. However, cement and concrete contribute to a total of 7–9% of the total CO2 emissions, which comes from the manufacturing process and fuel usage2,3. Sand and aggregate mining has also been linked to the destruction of ecosystems, fueling conflicts and the emergence of a “sand crisis”4. Given that concrete is difficult to replace functionally, the approach is to reduce the content of cement, make use of by-products and wastes, and make the mix more efficient instead of replacing concrete altogether5,6. In this case, the use of industrial by-products and construction wastes is beneficial to the circular economy and can reduce the potential for global warming by 20–40%7.

Of these materials, the waste generated in the processing of marble is in large quantities and is non-biodegradable; using it in concrete can reduce the waste disposed of in landfills and can save natural sand and cement8. Research suggests that the addition of waste marble powder up to 5–10% can enhance the compressive strength by 5–12% using the filler effect, durability can be improved in some cases. However, higher percentages can reduce the strength of the concrete in an optimized manner9,10. Similarly, partial replacement of sand by marble aggregates can improve the compressive strength, flexural strength, and impact resistance of self-compacting concrete. Life cycle assessments indicate that there can be a reduction in global warming potential up to 20%, improving sustainability indices in the case of marble waste used appropriately11.

In parallel, natural fibres have emerged as a sustainable alternative for synthetic or steel fibres for crack control and toughness in cement and geopolymer concrete mixes. Literature surveys have confirmed that natural fibres such as cotton, sisal, jute, bamboo, and many more can be used to reinforce concrete successfully for eco-friendly and waste-free composites12,13. In particular, marble dust contains very fine particles of CaCO₃, which are inert fillers used to refine pores and particles for improved packing efficiency, resulting in reduced porosity and water absorption for increased density and compactness14–16. The marble dust at Optimal dosages increases the compressive strength by 10–26%, and the split tensile strength increases by 10–18% compared to control mixes17,18. Marble dust concretes also have reduced permeability, better sulphate resistance, better resistance to freeze-thaw actions, and better salt crystallization resistance due to the denser microstructure19.

On the contrary, Luffa fibres have high porosity, large internal spaces, and high-water absorption capacity, which is above 200%, thus they can function as internal curing agents, thereby increasing hydration time, reducing autogenous shrinkage up to 57%, and increasing hydration products and matrix compactness in high-strength mortar20. Alkali (NaOH) and benzoyl chloride treatments involve surface treatments that remove hemicellulose/lignin, making the surface rough and increasing the tensile strength and modulus by about 140–150%. This enhances the bond properties of the fibres, thus improving the composite properties by increasing flexural strength and tensile modulus21,22.

In this context, Artificial Neural Networks have come to be commonly employed in civil and concrete engineering as data-driven tools for modeling complex material behavior in a more efficient way than traditional regression or empirical approaches. This is because the complex properties of concrete are affected by numerous variables in nonlinear ways. Earlier work has demonstrated the effectiveness of intelligent prediction frameworks for cementitious materials; for example, nearest-neighbourhood clustering with PSO optimization has been applied to predict 28-day compressive strength of pozzolanic concrete23, fuzzy logic has been integrated with experimental push-out data to model bond strength in fiber-reinforced self-compacting concrete (FRSCC)24, and slant-shear fuzzy logic models have been used to evaluate repair-interface bond in SCC and FRSCC25. More recently, AutoML combined with DEM simulation has enabled prediction of macroscopic behaviour in cementitious composites with variable frictional parameters26. The ANN based frameworks control the cement content, SCMs, aggregates, water, admixtures, and curing to attain the desired strength while cutting down on cost and CO2 and limiting the trial-and-error processes. ANN and other ML algorithms are used to predict the modulus of elasticity, slump, shrinkage, stiffness, and durability or service life of RC structures27,28.

The objectives of the present study are to develop an ANN model for predicting the mechanical properties of marble dust and luffa fibre concrete. To develop and use an experimental data set for concrete mixes with varying levels of marble dust replacement and luffa fibre content as input to the ANN model, as used in earlier ANN models for predicting concrete strength29. The model is developed to predict three important mechanical properties of concrete simultaneously. These are Compressive strength, split tensile strength, and Flexural strength using a single multi-output model, as adopted in earlier studies on lightweight concrete, metakaolin concrete, fibre-reinforced concrete, high-performance concrete, etc30. The predicted results are compared with the corresponding experimental results for compressive, split tensile, and flexural strengths to assess the accuracy and error range in the prediction, similar to the previous ANN concrete research in which the predicted and measured values were found to be in close conformity. For the evaluation of the developed ANN model using statistical parameters such as R, R², RMSE, MAE, and MAPE for training, validation, and testing datasets, similar to the ANN-based concrete strength prediction research31,32.

Nevertheless, inspite of the extensive application of ANN and other machine learning models in concrete materials, the majority of the existing research is focused on conventional or singly modified concretes, while the specific mix of marble dust and luffa fibres is not taken into account. The majority of the research carried out with the application of ANN/ML is focused on the following types of concretes: Conventional or steel fibre concretes, often without waste mineral additions33. Or modified with marble dust or sludge with polypropylene fibres34. Or with stone or quarry dust with one type of synthetic or natural fibres35. While research on natural fibers such as jute, bamboo, coconut, and sisal with SCMs has been conducted, luffa fibers have rarely been used and the combinations are limited36. Also, there is limited research carried out using the combined input of marble dust and natural fibres under a unified framework; the investigations carried out using the input of marble dust and fibres have used synthetic fibres not bio-fibers10. Existing models rarely incorporate fibre content, waste mineral replacement level, and curing age as parameters in the prediction of sustainable fibre-reinforced mix designs; in fact, some models ignore the variables of fibre content or consider only non-waste mixes37.

Consequently, there is an evident gap in terms of research, with regards to creating ANN-based models to forecast mechanical properties of marble dust concrete with luffa fibre, with consideration of combined effects of fibre volume, marble dust replacement percentage, and curing age in a predictive modelling framework.

Materials

In the current study, traditional concrete ingredients have been used alongside marble dust, which was employed to replace fine aggregates partially. The material selection process has been conducted in such a manner that they become compatible and appropriate for M25 grade concrete. Prior to mixing, all ingredients were subjected to testing in compliance with standard procedures.

Luffa fibers

As shown in Fig. 1, Luffa fibers are natural and bio-degradable fibers, have been employed for reinforcing concrete by virtue of its tensile strength and crack resistance qualities. Being hydrophilic, luffa fibers normally absorb water and deteriorate the adhesion between fiber and matrix.

Luffa fiber.

In order to address this drawback, the fibers were chemically treated with NaOH (sodium hydroxide). The fibers of diameter 0.4 mm were taken and initially cut into uniform pieces of length 20 mm each (l/d = 50) and immersed in an aqueous NaOH solution (5 wt%) for 24 h at ambient temperature (27 ± 2 °C). The fibers after soaking is removed and washed repeatedly using distilled water until the pH is neutralized which was verified with the help of a pH paper. The neutralized fibers were then oven-dried at 60 °C for 24 h to a constant mass before use. This procedure is consistent with the protocol reported by Zidi et al.22. The water absorption of the treated luffa fibers was measured by the 24-hour immersion method and found to be approximately 215%, which is significantly higher than that of conventional synthetic fibers but was managed by pre-soaking the fibers to a saturated surface-dry (SSD) condition prior to mixing in order to minimize undue water demand from the concrete matrix. Water

Potable water free from any contaminants was utilized in the process of mixing and curing of concrete. Water quality is an important factor that affects concrete hydration and its general durability.

Experimental procedure

Mixing and casting

Concrete mixing was carried out using a mechanical mixer to ensure uniform distribution of materials. Initially, dry components including cement, fine aggregate, coarse aggregate, and marble dust were mixed thoroughly. Water was then added gradually to achieve the desired consistency.

Luffa fibers were gradually added into the mix towards the final part of the mixing process to ensure proper dispersion and to avoid fiber agglomeration.

Fresh-state properties were measured immediately after mixing in accordance with IS 1199:1959. Workability was assessed by the slump cone test. The control mix (0% marble dust, 0% fiber) exhibited a slump of 82 mm. Progressive substitution of fine aggregate by marble dust up to 40% reduced the slump to 78 mm, 68 mm, 61 mm and 54 mm owing to the increased specific surface area of marble dust. Addition of 0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5% and 2% luffa fiber reduced the slump to 72 mm, 61 mm, 45 mm, and 30 mm respectively, confirming the workability-loss trend attributed to fiber water absorption and physical obstruction of particle flow. Fresh density was measured for all mixes using the unit weight container method and ranged from 2,381 to 2,412 kg/m³. Air content was not measured separately but is implicitly reflected in the fresh density values. Slump values for all 25 mixtures are tabulated in Table 6(complete mix-design matrix).

Concrete specimens were cast in standard moulds:

Cubes (150 mm × 150 mm × 150 mm) for compressive strength.

Cubes (150 mm × 150 mm × 150 mm) for compressive strength.

Cylinders (150 mm × 300 mm) for split tensile strength.

Cylinders (150 mm × 300 mm) for split tensile strength.

Prisms (100 mm × 100 mm × 500 mm) for flexural strength.

Prisms (100 mm × 100 mm × 500 mm) for flexural strength.

The cast specimens were kept undisturbed for a period of 24 h, demoulded, and then subjected to curing in water with a temperature range of 27 ± 2 °C until the day of testing.

Mechanical testing

Mechanical properties were evaluated at curing ages of 7, 14, and 28 days. For each mix and curing age, 3 specimens were casted and tested. The average for the 3 replicate samples was reported as the indicative value for each condition. The optimum at 20% marble dust and 1% luffa fiber (M13) was confirmed statistically; the CV for compressive, flexural, and split tensile strengths at this optimum mix did not exceed 4.2%, indicating acceptable repeatability consistent with IS 456:2000 and IS 516:1959 guidelines.

Such tests help determine the bearing load properties of concrete. However, the results from the 28th day test results were used as the primary dataset for ANN training. The dataset used for ANN training was assembled in two stages to ensure both experimental fidelity and adequate sample density for reliable model generalisation. In the first stage, three replicate specimens were cast and tested for each of the 25 mix combinations at 28 days, yielding three independently measured strength values (compressive, flexural, and split tensile) per mix. Each replicate constitutes a distinct data point, preserving specimen-level variability rather than collapsing replicates into a single averaged value. In the second stage, the experimentally bounded range of strength responses for each mix was systematically expanded through linear interpolation between the minimum and maximum replicate values at uniform intermediate intervals. This interpolation was performed strictly within the experimentally established upper and lower bounds of each mix’s three measured replicates, so no extrapolation beyond the observed data envelope was introduced. The interpolated points capture the natural within-batch variability that would be expected from additional replicate testing and are physically consistent with the measured scatter quantified in Table 7. This two-stage approach resulted in a substantially enlarged dataset that retains the statistical distribution of the experimental observations while providing the sample volume necessary for stable partitioning into training, validation, and testing subsets. The coefficient of variation across replicates at 28 days is reported in Table 7 and confirms the tight repeatability (CV not exceeding 5%) that underpins the reliability of the interpolation bounds. Multi-age (7- and 14-day) results were not included in the primary ANN model because the objective was to develop a mix-design prediction tool for 28-day design strength, consistent with standard structural design practice; curing age could be added as an additional input in future extended models.

ANN-based strength prediction

For modelling the non-linear relation between the constituent materials and their strengths in case of concrete mixed with luffa fibers and marble dust, the ANN technique was employed. There is non-linearity associated with the interaction of fiber reinforcing and matrix densification because of the presence of marble dust, which cannot be modelled using linear regression. One feature of ANN models is their ability to approximate nonlinear functions, which makes them suitable for modelling multivariate data.

A single multi-output ANN model was developed to simultaneously predict all three mechanical strength properties – compressive strength, flexural strength, and split tensile strength – from the same input variables. A shared-representation multi-output architecture is adopted because the three strength properties are governed by the same underlying mix design variables and their interactions; training a unified model exploits the correlations among outputs, improves generalisation, and reduces modelling complexity compared with maintaining separate independent models. The definitive input vector for the multi-output model comprises exactly 5 variables: (1) marble dust replacement level (% by mass of fine aggregate), (2) luffa fiber content (% by mass of cement), (3) cement content (kg/m³), (4) fine aggregate content (kg/m³), and (5) coarse aggregate content (kg/m³). Curing age was fixed at 28 days for all observations and is therefore a constant, not an independent input variable. Marble dust content in kg/m³ and fiber content in kg/m³ are directly derivable from variables (1)–(3) and are therefore not entered as separate inputs to avoid redundancy.

The complete dataset (comprising 75 original replicate measurements plus interpolated points, all at 28 days) was first assembled and Min-Max normalised using statistics computed exclusively from the training subset; no statistics derived from the validation or test subsets were used in scaling, thereby preventing data leakage. The data were then split randomly at the mixture level: all data points belonging to a given mix design were assigned exclusively to one of the three partitions (training, validation, test), ensuring that information from any single mixture did not appear in more than one partition. The test set was kept entirely withheld throughout the training and hyperparameter-selection process, and was only used once -for final performance evaluation and after the model architecture and early-stopping criterion had been fixed. Flexural strength test results

Figure 6 depicts the change in flexural strength with respect to how much of each luffa fiber and marble dust mix was used. The greatest recorded flexural strength was 6.2 MPa and occurred when using 1% luffa fiber combined with 20% marble dust, which can be seen as a bright yellow peak (high value) on the response surface. This flexural strength illustrates that adding small amounts of luffa fiber will provide an increase in flexural strength through the bridging of microcracks, as well increasing the weight-carrying capacity of these samples when bending rather than breaking. As the amount of luffa fiber increases to greater than 1%, the flexural strength begins to decrease because the fibers become less well dispersed through the concrete mixture, resulting in clusters of fibers forming at different places in the resulting sample, leading to less workable concrete and the weakening of the bond between the fiber and the concrete matrix. Therefore, adding a limited quantity of luffa will increase the tensile resistance of the concrete by improving crack bridging as well as delaying crack propagation. With high percentages of luffa fiber present, the effectiveness of the concrete will be reduced because of poor workability characteristics, fibre segregation, and lack of strong interfacial bond at the matrix-fiber interface.

Conclusion

When optimal proportions of marble dust and luffa fibers are used in concrete, it contributes to greater mechanical performance and environmental benefits through the re-use of industrial waste materials as fine aggregate replacement, consistent with circular-economy principles. Formal life-cycle assessment or carbon quantification was not conducted in this study.

Using a proportion of 20% marble dust and 1% luffa fiber represented the optimum concrete mix, which resulted in compressive strengths (34.5 MPa), flexural strengths (6.2 MPa), and split tensile strengths (4.25 MPa).

Marble dust contributed to strength improvements by both filler effects and better particle packing, thus providing a denser microstructure than the control (inferred from macroscopic strength data and literature on analogous systems; microstructural confirmation via SEM or MIP was not conducted in this study); luffa fibers improved crack resistance through effective crack bridging (inferred from flexural and split-tensile response).

The single multi-output ANN developed (feedforward MLP with five inputs, two hidden layers of 64 and 32 neurons with ReLU activation, and a three-neuron linear output layer) demonstrated the ability to simultaneously capture non-linear relationships between the mix design input variables and all three mechanical strength properties of the concrete.

The single multi-output ANN developed (feedforward MLP with five inputs, two hidden layers of 64 and 32 neurons with ReLU activation, and a three-neuron linear output layer) demonstrated the ability to simultaneously capture non-linear relationships between the mix design input variables and all three mechanical strength properties of the concrete.

The ANN achieved high predictive accuracies (R² values of up to 0.96, low RMSE, bias and a20 index of 100%), indicating that the ANN is a reliable and efficient tool for predicting mechanical properties of luffa fiber–marble dust concrete within the tested M25 design space, and has potential utility in reducing the number of experimental trials needed during mix development.
