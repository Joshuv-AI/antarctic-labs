Retrieve, Schedule, Reflect: LLM Agents for Chip QoR Optimization
Yikang Ouyang, Yang Luo, Dongsheng Zuo, Yuzhe Ma

Abstract
Modern chip design requires multi-objective optimization of timing, power, and area under stringent time-to-market constraints. Although powerful optimization algorithms are integrated into EDA tools, achieving high QoR hinges on effective long-horizon scheduling, which relies heavily on manual expert intervention. To address this issue and automate chip design, we propose an agentic LLM framework that schedules chip optimizations through direct interaction with EDA tools. The agent is grounded in natural language expertise expressed as a search tree through retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). We further improve scheduling quality with Pareto-driven QoR feedback through language reflection. Experimental results show that, compared with black-box search methods such as reinforcement learning, our framework achieves 10% greater timing improvement while consuming less power and area, with more than 4 × 4\times speedup. The post-optimization QoR is also comparable to that achieved by human experts. Finally, the agent supports customized tasks expressed in natural language, enabling preferential QoR trade-offs. The code and chip design data will be publicly available at https://github.com/YiKangOY/Open-LLM-ECO .

Introduction

Figure 1
:
Chip QoR optimization as an iterative schedule–execute–reflect workflow.

Chips are the foundation of the modern digital world.
As technology nodes advance and application requirements diversify, design houses must deliver chips with high quality-of-results (QoR) under increasingly tight time-to-market requirements.
In practice, the primary QoR metrics are timing, power, and area (PPA).
Commonly used algorithms for improving PPA include gate sizing, threshold-voltage (
V_{th}
) assignment, and buffer insertion/removal
(
Pu et al. 2025
;
Lu et al. 2021
;
Lu et al. 2023b
)
.

While these algorithms are integrated into EDA tools and can be invoked easily, achieving competitive QoR still requires extensive human effort.
Due to the NP-hard nature of chip optimization
(
Ning 1994
)
, QoR improvement often saturates and may be trapped in local minima after several iterations
(
Neto et al. 2022
)
.
As a result, optimization depends on long-horizon scheduling by expert engineers, as illustrated in
Figure

1
.
Engineers determine
what
objectives to prioritize,
which
algorithms to apply, and
how
to configure options across iterations.
During the iterations, they interpret reports and logs from tools with their expertise knowledge to schedule the process.
When QoR improvement stagnates, on previous optimization traces and adjust the scheduling strategy.
Consequently, this
schedule–execute–reflect
loop is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and demands continuous human monitoring.

Prior works often treat the scheduling of QoR optimization as searching a fixed length sequence of EDA tool commands.
These efforts predominantly rely on reinforcement learning since it can optimize over black-box objectives with only numerical QoR feedbacks
(
Agnesina et al. 2020
;
Lu et al. 2023a
;
Won & Kim 2025
;
Lu et al. 2021
)
.
Despite enabling a certain level of automation, these methods face fundamental limitations that domain knowledge is not incorporated.
Because they optimize mainly from QoR feedback without capturing the natural-language expertise that engineers use to guide scheudling, the search can require long exploration and still yield unsatisfactory QoR schedules.
Moreover, supporting customized, open-ended optimization tasks remains difficult, since they rely on predefined reward functions
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
.

To address these challenges and enable efficient, high-QoR, and customizable chip optimization, we propose the first agentic large language model (LLM) framework that schedules chip design optimizations in a manner analogous to experienced engineers.
The framework operates in an iterative
retrieve–schedule–reflect
loop using multiple agents.
First, it
retrieves
relevant optimization expertise from a library formulated as a search tree, where paths encode human‑interpretable scheduling templates described in natural language.
This guides the scheduling with expert domain knowledge rather than relying solely on numerical QoR feedback.
Based on the retrieved expertise, the agent then
schedules
task objectives and strategies, and executes EDA tool algorithms with detailed options accordingly.
Furthermore, to continuously refine scheduling policies under multi-objective QoR trade-offs,
we introduce a Pareto-driven
reflection
mechanism.
The agent identifies Pareto-optimal optimization traces, summarizes effective scheduling strategies, and incorporates these reflections to improve future schedules.

Our contributions are summarized as follows.

1.

We introduce
Retrieve-Schedule-Reflect
, the first LLM multi-agent framework that automates chip QoR optimization by scheduling the iterative use of EDA tools, substantially reducing manual effort.

2.

We adopt a RAG mechanism to produce informed schedules from curated chip optimization knowledge, organized as a search tree database of expertise schedule templates.

3.

We propose a Pareto-driven reflection mechanism that improves scheduling quality by reviewing historical optimization outcomes and learning from multi-objective trade-offs.

4.

We demonstrate that our framework achieves 10% better timing, with better area and power, and more than 4
\times
speedup over reinforcement learning baselines on 8 designs, while producing QoR comparable to human engineers.

5.

Our framework supports customized optimization preferences specified directly in natural language, enabling open-ended objectives and controllable trade-offs.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Chip Optimization

Chips are optimized by EDA tools through iterative optimization algorithm usage.
Among many optimization methods, gate sizing
(
Ning 1994
;
Fishburn & Dunlop 1985
)
and buffer insertion/removal
(
Shi & Li 2005
;
Van Ginneken 1990
)
are the most prevalent algorithms.
They directly trade off timing against power/area, and can be applied at multiple stages across logic synthesis, placement, and routing.
Practical implementations of these algorithms are integrated in both academic and commercial EDA tools
(
Ajayi & Blaauw 2019
;
Synopsys 2022
)
.
However, achieving satisfactory QoR still requires extensive human intervention to determine objective priorities and algorithm configurations for optimization
(
Coudert 2002
)
.

2.2
LLM for Chip Optimization

In chip design, LLMs are commonly used as tool-using agents that read reports/logs and propose edits to flow scripts.
For example, ChipNeMo studies domain adaptation and evaluates chip-design assistants for EDA script generation and bug analysis
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
.
ChatEDA similarly proposes an LLM-powered autonomous agent that decomposes tasks and generates scripts for RTL-to-GDSII flows
(
He et al. 2023
)
.
ORFS-agent is a representative example that optimizes an RTL-to-GDS flow by tuning flow parameters with an LLM
(
Ghose et al. 2025
)
.
However, practical QoR optimization is not a single-shot search over parameters.
Engineers run many optimization iterations and decide the next optimization target, algorithms (e.g., sizing vs. buffering), and many other detailed algorithm options based on both QoR metrics and rich diagnostic information in tool logs and reports.
Our work targets this missing layer by automating the scheduling of the iterative chip optimization process, rather than optimizing one stage or tuning a fixed set of EDA tool scripts.

2.3
LLM for Task Scheduling

Many recent LLM agent systems work as schedulers that iteratively choose of actions/tools, observe intermediate results, and revise the schedule over long horizons.
ReAct illustrates this view by interleaving reasoning with action selection to update the next-step plan after each environment interaction
(
Yao et al. 2023
)
.

Two mechanisms are especially useful for improving scheduling quality.
First,
retrieval augmentation generation (RAG)
lets the agent query external knowledge to guide decisions with informative documentation and examples
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
.
Second,
reflection
reviews summaries of failures/successes and uses them to refine future schedules, improving qualities in iterative workflows
(
Shinn et al. 2023
)
.

2.4
Problem Formulation

In this paper, we aim to automate the scheduling of chip QoR optimization with an multi-agent framework.
Given a designer-defined optimization task, the framework iteratively selects an objective from power, timing, and area, and then schedules an EDA tool algorithm together with its options/parameters.
Due to QoR saturation, the agent launches new rounds of scheduling and execution iterations with reflection on feedback from previous rounds.
The optimized chip produced in the final scheduled round is used to evaluate the resulting QoR.

3
Search Tree–based Retrieval-augmented Scheduling

We adopt a Retrieval-augmented Generation (RAG) mechanism to guide agents during scheduling with domain knowledge and EDA tool manuals.
Previous work proposes modeling sequential chip optimization as a decision tree
(
Pei et al. 2023
)
to explore different optimization paths.
Inspired by this formulation, we model scheduling as a search tree (ST) whose nodes contain natural-language contents that encode engineer scheduling strategies.
At runtime, the agent retrieves relevant ST paths and uses them as guidance for scheduling decisions.

3.1
Search Tree Representation and Knowledge Store

Figure

2
illustrates an example ST constructed from expert scheduling knowledge and EDA tool manual knowledge.
To mimic designers’ selection strategy over optimization objectives, each ST node is associated with an objective
o
selected from
\mathcal{O}=\{\text{timing},\text{power},\text{area}\}
.
Each node also stores a natural-language design state description
s
, a strategy snippet
\mathcal{G}
describing how to improve objective
o
using EDA tools, and a set of tool-manual snippets
\mathcal{M}_{o}
relevant to objective
o
.
Formally, we define a node as

v\triangleq\big(o,\,s,\,\mathcal{G},\,\mathcal{M}_{o}\big),\qquad o\in\mathcal{O}.

(1)

An ST path represents a complete expert scheduling process, including design-state evolution and how designers select subsequent objectives, corresponding algorithms, and options.
We represent a path as a variable-length sequence of nodes:

p=(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{L}),\quad L\ \text{arbitrary}.

(2)

Paths have no fixed length because improving a node’s objective using
\mathcal{G}
may require multiple EDA tool calls.
As a result, a path provides guidance at multiple granularities, ranging from abstract objective ordering to detailed strategies that can be applied across scheduling iterations.
Because ST paths are expressed in natural language, they serve as guidance rather than prescriptions, leaving the agent freedom to schedule based on the actual design state at runtime.

We build the ST from academic papers and reports with engineer input so that the encoded schedules reflect realistic optimization practice rather than arbitrary sequences.
We extract all root-to-leaf paths into a database
\mathcal{D}
for retrieval.
Additional ST details and examples are provided in
Appendix

A
.

3.2
Path-based RAG

Given a natural-language query
x
and a candidate ST path
p
, we compute dense embeddings
e_{x}=\phi(x)
and
e_{p}=\phi(p)
using the same text encoder
\phi
.
We measure relevance using cosine similarity:

\mathrm{Sim}(x,p)=\frac{e_{x}\cdot e_{p}}{\lVert e_{x}\rVert\,\lVert e_{p}\rVert}.

(3)

We then retrieve the most relevant expert guidance via top-
k
similarity search:

\mathrm{TopK}(x,\mathcal{D})=\operatorname*{arg\,top\text{-}k}_{p\in\mathcal{D}}\;\mathrm{Sim}(x,p),

(4)

where
\mathcal{D}
is the ST path database.
The retrieved paths provide the agent with end-to-end scheduling templates, which the agent uses to make guided decisions and invoke EDA tools correctly.

4
Agentic Schedule–Execute–Reflect Framework

Building upon the ST-based RAG in
Section

3
, we present an agentic framework for long-horizon chip QoR optimization scheduling.
An overview is shown in
Figure

3
.
The framework consists of three agents: a scheduler, an executor, and a reflector.

Starting from an unoptimized design, the framework performs iterative schedule–execute steps within each round.
During these iterations, RAG provides state-matched expert guidance and tool-manual grounding so that scheduling decisions are informed and tool execution remains legal.
Once QoR improvement saturates within a round, the framework restarts from the unoptimized design and launches a new round, as shown in
Figure

3
(a).
Across rounds, the reflector agent summarizes feedback from historical traces and transfer it to the scheduler and executor to improve subsequent schedules.

4.1
Within-round Schedule–Execute Loop

We first describe the within-round schedule–execute iterations, shown in
Figure

3
(b).
At iteration
i
of round
r
, the scheduler selects the next objective to optimize and forms a high-level optimization strategy.
The scheduler observes the accumulated EDA reports and QoR history
R_{r}^{(0:i-1)}
together with the user-defined optimization task
\mathcal{P}
.
To represent the current optimization context in a form compatible with the ST, the scheduler summarizes the design state as a natural-language state
s^{(i)}
and forms a retrieval query:

x^{(i)}=\psi_{\mathrm{sched}}\big(\mathcal{P},\,R_{r}^{(0:i-1)},\,s^{(i)},\,i,\,\mathcal{F}^{(r-1)}_{\mathrm{sched}}\big).

(5)

Because ST nodes are described by natural-language design states (
Equation

1
), the scheduler uses
x^{(i)}
to retrieve the most relevant expert scheduling paths:

\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(i)}=\mathrm{TopK}\big(x^{(i)},\mathcal{D}\big).

(6)

The retrieved paths
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(i)}
provide end-to-end guidance on objective ordering and tool-use strategies that match the current round’s design state.

Conditioned on
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(i)}
and the scheduling reflection from the previous round
\mathcal{F}^{(r-1)}_{\mathrm{sched}}
(will be introduced in
Section

4.2
), the scheduler selects the next objective
o^{(i)}\in\mathcal{O}
and outputs strategy advice
C^{(i)}
describing how to use the EDA tool.

Next, the executor translates the high-level advice into EDA tool commands given
(\mathcal{P},o^{(i)},C^{(i)})
.
To keep command generation legal and objective-consistent, the executor forms a manual query

q^{(i)}=\psi_{\mathrm{exec}}\big(\mathcal{P},\,o^{(i)},\,C^{(i)}\big),

(7)

and retrieves objective-oriented references

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{o^{(i)}}^{(i)}=\mathrm{TopK}\big(q^{(i)},\mathcal{M}_{o^{(i)}}\big).

(8)

Conditioned on
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{o^{(i)}}^{(i)}
and the execution reflection
\mathcal{F}^{(r-1)}_{\mathrm{exec}}
, the executor generates an executable command
A^{(i)}
that implements
C^{(i)}
while following the EDA manual and prior effective tool-use patterns
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
.

Executing
A^{(i)}
in the EDA environment yields updated reports

R_{r}^{(i)}=\mathcal{E}\big(R_{r}^{(i-1)},A^{(i)}\big),

(9)

which are appended to the within-round trace and become the input for iteration
i+1
.
This loop continues until QoR improvement saturates or an iteration limit is reached.

4.2
Across-round Pareto-driven Reflection

As more optimization algorithms are applied, QoR improvement often saturates and the flow can get stuck in a local optimum
(
Coudert 2002
;
Neto et al. 2022
)
.
Engineers therefore draw feedback from previous optimization traces and restart from the unoptimized design to explore alternative schedules that can reach better QoR.
Since QoR is multi-objective, Pareto dominance is widely adopted to measure trade-offs
(
Geng et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2024
)
.
We model this practice by maintaining Pareto-optimal traces and prompting a reflector agent to generate scheduler and executor insights from the best historical outcomes, as shown in
Figure

3
(b).

4.2.1
Filtering Pareto-optimal Traces

Let
\mathcal{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{r}\}
denote the set of terminal QoR vectors from all completed rounds up to round
r
, where

\mathbf{z}_{j}\triangleq\big(z_{j,\mathrm{timing}},\,z_{j,\mathrm{power}},\,z_{j,\mathrm{area}}\big)

(10)

is extracted from the terminal report of round
j
(i.e., from
R_{j}^{(n_{j})}
).
Here, larger
z_{j,\mathrm{timing}}
indicates better timing (e.g., larger slack), while smaller
z_{j,\mathrm{power}}
and
z_{j,\mathrm{area}}
indicate better power and area.

Each terminal vector
\mathbf{z}_{j}
corresponds to a complete optimization trace
S_{j}
that records the executed EDA commands and the resulting reports:

S_{j}\triangleq\big((A_{j}^{(1)},R_{j}^{(1)}),\,(A_{j}^{(2)},R_{j}^{(2)}),\,\dots,\,(A_{j}^{(n_{j})},R_{j}^{(n_{j})})\big).

(11)

We filter the Pareto front
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{Pareto}}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
under objectives (maximize
z_{j,\mathrm{timing}}
, minimize
z_{j,\mathrm{power}}
and
z_{j,\mathrm{area}}
), and lift it back to traces:

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{Pareto}}=\{S_{j}\mid\mathbf{z}_{j}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{Pareto}}\}.

(12)

4.2.2
Scheduler/Executor Two-level Reflection

Pareto-optimal QoR is jointly determined by (i) the objective ordering decided by the scheduler and (ii) the detailed algorithms and options decided by the executor.
Accordingly, we ask the reflector to generate two complementary reflections: a scheduling reflection
\mathcal{F}^{(r)}_{\mathrm{sched}}
and an execution reflection
\mathcal{F}^{(r)}_{\mathrm{exec}}
.

The scheduling reflection
\mathcal{F}^{(r)}_{\mathrm{sched}}
extracts patterns of objective prioritization and switching that lead to Pareto-improving outcomes.
It provides guidance on when to focus on one objective versus when to pursue others, in order to satisfy the user-defined task while achieving Pareto-optimality.
The execution reflection
\mathcal{F}^{(r)}_{\mathrm{exec}}
summarizes which EDA algorithms and option choices were effective for the current design state, and highlights failed ones to avoid in subsequent runs.
Feeding
\mathcal{F}^{(r)}_{\mathrm{sched}}
to the scheduler and
\mathcal{F}^{(r)}_{\mathrm{exec}}
to the executor progressively improves long-horizon schedules across rounds.

5
Experimental Results

5.1
Experimental Setup

To evaluate the generality of our approach, we use open-source chip designs implemented in the ASAP7 PDK
(
Clark et al. 2016
)
from the ICCAD’24 dataset
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
.
We select 8 designs from this benchmark; their statistics and optimization results are summarized in
Table

1
.
Additional design statistics are provided in
Section

B.2
.

We evaluate three core quality-of-results (QoR) metrics: timing, power, and area.
Timing is measured by slack, including worst slack and total slack, where worst slack captures the most critical timing path and total slack reflects the overall severity of timing violations.
For power and area, lower values indicate better QoR.
All optimizations and report generation are performed using
Synopsys PrimeECO
, a widely used golden tool for QoR analysis and ECO optimization.
To enable open-sourcing, code segments restricted by the tool license are replaced with mock equivalents; readers with access to the license can directly substitute the corresponding commands.
All experiments are conducted on a machine with two AMD EPYC 7543 32-core CPUs and an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU.
Unless otherwise specified, we use
GPT5-mini
(2025-08-07) as the default backbone LLM for the scheduler, executor, and reflector.

To our knowledge, there is no established baseline that directly targets long-horizon
scheduling
of iterative chip optimizations with repeated tool interactions.
We therefore include a reinforcement learning (RL) baseline with A3C algorithm
(
Mnih et al. 2016a
)
used in EDA tasks
(
Bai et al. 2024
)
as a representative black-box search approach that learns to search fixed-length sequences of EDA commands from QoR feedback; training and hyperparameter details are deferred to
Section

B.3
.
In addition, we include a human baseline by asking an experienced engineer to manually schedule the optimization process for 10 rounds, each with at most 10 schedule–execute iterations and report the final-round QoR.
Our framework is implemented with the same configurations.

5.2
Optimization Result

Our primary objective and the prompt is:
Achieve timing closure by ensuring non-negative slack, while minimizing power and area
.
Following common industrial practice, timing slack is treated as a hard constraint and is prioritized first, since negative slack indicates functional failure of the design.
Once timing violations are resolved, power and area are optimized as secondary objectives under the satisfied timing constraint.
We report results in
Table

1
, comparing our method against reinforcement learning (RL) and manual scheduling by an experienced engineer.

We first evaluate our framework by post-optimization timing slacks.
Compared with both human scheduling and our agentic framework, the RL baseline exhibits substantially larger negative slack, although it leaves only 10.92% negative slacks unsolved.
Our framework improves total slack substantially, leaving only 0.23% unsolved and exceeds human baseline.
This indicates that timing violations are mitigated broadly across the design rather than being confined to a small subset of paths.

In terms of worst slack, our method delivers improvements of over 90% relative to the original designs, bringing the most critical timing paths close to or into the non-negative regime.
This level of improvement is comparable to that achieved by experienced human engineers and consistently surpasses the RL baseline, which demonstrating that our proposed framework maintains competitive critical-path repair capability while more effectively managing multiple interacting critical and near-critical paths in designs with severe timing violations.
For designs that still exhibit negative slack after optimization (“NVDLA partition m/p” and “hidden5”), our method achieves larger improvements in both worst slack and total slack relative to the original designs, indicating the strong capability in mitigating widespread and interacting timing violations.

We next compare the post-optimization power and area across all methods.
Relative to the original designs, our method reduces area by 4.6% on average, while achieving slight power improvement without sacrificing timing closure, and it consistently outperforms both the human and RL baselines.
Compared with human scheduling, our method achieves comparable or lower power and area on most designs, while significantly improving over RL, which often incurs unnecessary area and power overheads.
On several designs such as “NVDLA partition (m/p)”, our method results in slightly higher area and power than the baselines.
This behavior is expected, as these designs suffer from severe timing violations and require aggressive timing repair, where buffers insertions and upsizing cells gates introduce inevitable area and power trade-offs.
In contrast, the RL baseline tends to over-optimize tool command sequences without explicit long-horizon task coordination, leading to suboptimal trade-offs between timing repair and power/area recovery.
Overall, the results indicate that our framework effectively balances timing closure with power/area efficiency when scheduling iterative optimizations through commercial EDA tools.

6
Conclusion

Long-horizon chip QoR optimization remains a bottleneck in modern EDA workflows, not because optimization algorithms are lacking, but because effective optimization requires the scheduling of EDA tool usage with expertise chip design knowledge.
However, black-box search is inefficient since it only relies on numerical QoR feedback, without domain knowledge.
Motivated by how expert engineers schedule the optimization, we introduced
Retrieve–Schedule–Reflect
, the first agentic LLM framework that automates chip optimization by explicitly modeling scheduling as an iterative, knowledge-guided process with self-improvement through reflection.

By integrating search tree-based retrieval with Pareto-driven reflection, our framework combines domain expertise, long-horizon scheduling, and quantitative feedback to progressively refine schedule strategies.
Experiments on 8 designs using commercial EDA tools show that our approach consistently outperforms reinforcement learning baselines and matches or exceeds expert human scheduling. Moreover, the ability to directly interpret optimization preference in natural language enables flexible, user-defined multi-objective trade-offs that are difficult to achieve with conventional methods.
Ablation studies further confirm that both retrieval augmentation and multi-round reflection are essential to robust performance.

Appendix A
Scheduling Search Tree

Here we discuss the contents in the nodes of search tree and further paths.
Each node
v
in the search tree contains a corresponding objective
o
among power, timing, area.
There is also a high-level summary
s
over design state that, like “The design is suffering from severe timing violation of x% of clock period”, or “The power compared to initial design increased by x%”, etc.
It also contains a language-instructed strategy
\mathcal{G}
over the use of EDA tools like “explore various gate sizing options” or “aggressively recover power even with negative slack”.
There is also a segment of tool manual associated with it that contains the correct use of EDA tools, which we would not show for license restrictions.

The tree is built by summarizing academic papers and technical reports and are filtered by engineers to ensure the tree is valid in terms of chip optimization.
For example, there will not be the case that
v_{i}
have summary
s_{i}
of “timing violation is small” while the next node
v_{i+1}
has
s_{i+1}
denotes that “timing violation is significant” when timing optimization is performed.

By extracting nodes in the search tree into a path, we could develop scheduling template that contains possible transition of designs states
s
and corresponding strategies.
And example path is shown in the following paragraph.
It use natural language to summarize the design state changes and objectives selected during the path.
It also have a sequence of description regarding high-level usage of EDA tools like exploring different options or increasing the efforts.
The schedulers perform RAG at each schedule step so they can always retrieve the most relevant paths based on design state and QoR changes in one round of execute-schedule iterations.

Scheduling Path Template Example

Timing (Aggressive)
\rightarrow
Timing (Incremental)
\rightarrow
Power
\rightarrow
Timing (Incremental)
**
Design summary
**: The design exhibits severe timing violation, after aggressive timing optimization, there are several remaining violations.
So we perform incremental timing optimization with different algorithm.
After they are fixed, we do power recovery.
The power recover reveals new changes to incrementally optimize timing degradation brought by power optimization.

**
Target selection
**: Use an aggressive timing pass to gain slack fast, then an incremental timing pass to stabilize, then optimize power, and end with incremental timing cleanup. Switch away from timing if it becomes well-exploited with no gains.

**
Option selection
**: Aggressive timing is **
exploration-heavy
** (broad option coverage) to expose limits.
Incremental timing is **
exploitation-heavy
**, using only options shown to help and avoiding prior no-effect attempts.
Power stage starts with exploration to find which power components can be reduced safely, then exploits the best component-focused options.

**
Guidelines
**: Don’t chase truly non-fixable timing violations. If aggressive timing increases power/area, explicitly recover power afterward (and note whether area needs a later pass).
After power changes, always run a timing cleanup to repair any regressions while minimizing new side-effects.
