Health-related quality of life in patients with cervical cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis of EQ‑5D utility scores
Authors: Priyankaa V, Harshavardhan K, Sananthya Karthikeyan, Hariharan Murugadoss, Madhumitha Haridoss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cervical cancer substantially impairs HRQoL, particularly among women in socially and economically productive age groups. EQ-5D utility values are important inputs for quality-adjusted life-year estimation, cost–utility analysis, and health technology assessment; however, pooled EQ-5D estimates for cervical cancer remain limited. This PRISMA 2020-compliant systematic review and meta-analysis searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library from inception to July 2025. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251077051). Eligible studies included adults with cervical cancer reporting EQ-5D-3 L, EQ-5D-5 L, EQ-5D VAS, or EQ-5D domain-level outcomes. Random-effects meta-analysis was used to estimate pooled mean utility and VAS scores. Heterogeneity, publication bias, sensitivity analyses, subgroup analyses, prediction intervals, and meta-regression were assessed. Twenty-four studies including 281,958 participants from 15 countries were included. Seventeen studies contributed to the EQ-5D utility meta-analysis, yielding a pooled mean utility score of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.73–0.85; 95% prediction interval: 0.52–1.05; I² = 99.6%). Seventeen studies reported EQ-5D VAS scores, with a pooled mean of 71.82 (95% CI: 67.95–75.69; 95% prediction interval: 54.54–89.11; I² = 98.3%). Stage-specific estimates were inconsistent, particularly for Stage IV disease, and should be interpreted cautiously because of small sample size, potential survivor bias, and high between-study variability. Pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression were the most frequently affected EQ-5D domains. Cervical cancer is associated with reduced HRQoL. The pooled EQ-5D estimates provide useful reference values for economic evaluations and HTA, but substantial heterogeneity and wide prediction intervals indicate that local adaptation and cautious interpretation are required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58466-2.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a malignant tumor of the cervix originating from the epithelial cells, usually in the form of squamous cell carcinoma or adenocarcinoma, associated with persistent infection by high-risk human papillomavirus types. Cervical cancer commonly develops in mid-adult life and often during the peak years of social and economic productivity. It has broad individual and societal implications1–3.

Cervical cancer currently ranks as the fourth cause of cancer in women around the world, with an estimated 660,000 new cases and about 350,000 deaths in 2022. Almost 90% of these deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries which reflects the disparities in HPV vaccination, screening, and access to timely treatment2,4.Because of this inequality, the World Health Organization launched the Global Strategy for the elimination of cervical cancer as a public health problem by setting targets of 90‒70‒90 to be reached by 2030. These include 90% of girls fully vaccinated with HPV vaccine by age 15, 70% of women screened with a high-performance test at age 35 and 45, and 90% of women diagnosed with cervical cancer receiving appropriate treatment5,6. Universal health coverage (UHC) and evidence-informed priority setting using health technology assessment (HTA) are therefore precursors to the attainment of the 90-70-90 goals. HTA including cost-utility analysis will support the optimal allocation of the limited resources by comparing the costs and health gains of such interventions, such as HPV vaccination, screening strategies, and treatment pathways, which is critical for scaling services sustainably in high-burden, resource-constrained settings6,7.

In cervical cancer, health-related quality of life reflects the impact on physical, psychological, sexual, social, and functional aspects of well-being. In this context, a preference-based measurement of health-related quality of life provides health utility scores, one of the essential inputs in conducting cost-utility analyses, which estimate quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) for informing HTA bodies about value for money regarding various prevention and treatment options3,8.

Health utility scores differ with disease stage, treatment type, and disease trajectory. They are observed to decline with the advancement of disease from pre-invasive lesions to invasive and recurrent disease, as reported by studies using EQ-5D; this is mostly due to increasing pain, fatigue, and psychological distress, although smaller decrements in mobility and self-care could be seen at earlier stages8,9.

Although the existing EQ-5D-based studies have demonstrated health state utility values to decline with advancing severity of cervical cancer, the evidence base is presently fragmented, limited to small samples and distributed unevenly across countries and health system contexts. Comprehensive stage-of-disease-specific utility estimates from diverse populations of the world are crucial to ensure that economic models accurately reflect real-world disease burden, enable robust comparisons across jurisdictions, and minimize bias from uncritical transfers of utility values between settings8,10. This review therefore aimed to synthesize EQ-5D and EQ-VAS-based health state utility scores for cervical cancer in clinically relevant health states (early-stage and advanced disease). The main outcomes are pooled, stage-specific utility values which are critical inputs for cost-utility analyses and HTA models which could inform cervical cancer prevention and control policies.

Methodology

This systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA) adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration ID: CRD420251077051). The PRISMA checklist was also completed to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting of the review process (Supplementary Table 1).

Search strategy

A comprehensive systematic search was conducted to identify studies reporting EQ-5D-3 L, EQ-5D-5 L, or the EQ-5D Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) utility scores among patients with cancer cervix. The databases including PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), were searched from inception to July 19, 2025 with no initial language restrictions were applied during database searching; however, studies without an accessible English translation were excluded during screening or publication date. The search strategy was developed using the PICO framework. Population (P) included adults diagnosed with cervical cancer or its related complications. Intervention and comparator were not applicable. Outcome (O) included HRQoL assessed using EQ-5D-3 L, EQ-5D-5 L, or the EQ-5D VAS. The search strategy was constructed using search terms “Cervical Cancer” OR “Uterine Cervical Neoplasms” for population and “EQ-5D,” “EuroQol,” “utility,” and “health-related quality of life” terms for outcome. Both free text terms and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) along with Boolean operators (OR/AND/NOT) were used to enhance search sensitivity. Reference lists of all eligible studies and relevant reviews were manually searched to identify additional relevant studies if any. The detailed database-specific search strings are provided in (Supplementary Tables 2–5).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were considered eligible for inclusion if they involved adult patients aged 18 years or older diagnosed with cervical cancer and reported quantitative health-related quality of life (HRQoL) outcomes using the EQ-5D instrument, including EQ-5D-3 L, EQ-5D-5 L, and/or EQ-5D visual analogue scale (VAS). Eligible study designs included randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, and cross-sectional studies. Studies were included only when cervical cancer-specific EQ-5D utility scores, EQ-5D VAS scores, or EQ-5D domain-level outcomes were reported separately.

This review specifically focused on EQ-5D because it is a preference-based HRQoL instrument capable of generating utility values suitable for quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) estimation, cost–utility analysis, and health technology assessment. Other HRQoL instruments, including disease-specific or cancer-specific quality-of-life scales, were excluded unless EQ-5D outcomes were also reported, as these instruments do not directly provide utility values without additional mapping procedures.

Studies involving precancerous lesions, including cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), were screened during study selection. Studies focused solely on cervical cancer screening, mixed gynecological cancer populations without separately reported cervical cancer data, or studies in which EQ-5D outcomes were not reported were also excluded. Additionally, narrative reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, conference abstracts, qualitative studies, study protocols, and studies published in languages other than English without an available English translation were excluded (Supplementary Table 6).

Study selection and data extraction

A three-phase study selection procedure was implemented.

Phase 1: Primary screening. All records retrieved from the database search were imported into the Rayyan web tool, and duplicates were removed manually. Two independent reviewers (PV and HK) screened the titles and abstracts of all identified studies, and full texts were obtained for studies deemed potentially eligible.

Phase 2: Secondary screening. The full texts were reviewed independently by two reviewers (PV and HM) based on the predefined eligibility criteria. Studies that did not meet the criteria were excluded, and the reasons for exclusion were documented. A detailed list of excluded studies is provided in Supplementary Table 6.

Phase 3: Final selection. Any discrepancies between reviewers during full-text screening were resolved through discussion and consensus with the third reviewer (SK).

Data Extraction Form (DEF) was developed in Microsoft Excel 365 and standardized through data piloting for five randomly selected studies. Extracted information included study identifiers (author, year, country, journal), study design and characteristics (sample size, duration, setting), participant demographics (mean age, sex, Stages of cervical cancer, disease duration), and outcome measures (EQ-5D version, mean utility or VAS scores, and measures of variability such as SD, SE, or IQR, domains of EQ-5D). Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (VP and HK) and discrepancies were resolved through mutual consensus.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of Bias (ROB) was assessed using validated tools appropriate to the respective study designs. The Appraisal Tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS) was used for cross-sectional studies and the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias Tool (RoB-2) for Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs). The AXIS tool consists of 20 items evaluating clarity of study aims, appropriateness of study design, definition of the target population, validity and reliability of outcome measures, adequacy of data reporting, and ethical considerations, with responses categorized as “Yes,” “No,” or “Do not know.” RCTs were evaluated using RoB-2, which examines bias in five domains including the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported results. Two authors (PV and HK) independently performed the ROB assessment for all included studies, and any disagreements were resolved through consensus or arbitration by a third reviewer (HM). Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies ranged from moderate to high, reflecting acceptable internal validity. The detailed domain-specific risk-of-bias evaluations are presented in (Supplementary Fig. 1–2).

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

The primary outcome of this review was health-related quality of life among patients with cervical cancer, assessed using EQ-5D utility scores and EQ-5D VAS scores. For continuous outcomes, including EQ-5D index scores and EQ-5D VAS scores, mean values were pooled. When studies reported EQ-5D outcomes as median and interquartile range (IQR), these values were converted to mean and standard deviation (SD) using standard formulas to improve comparability across studies11,12.

For categorical outcomes, including the proportion of patients reporting problems in the EQ-5D domains of mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, pooled proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated.

A random-effects model using the DerSimonian–Laird method was used for meta-analysis because clinical, geographical, and methodological heterogeneity was expected across the included studies. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, τ², and the I² statistic. In addition to 95% CIs, 95% prediction intervals were calculated for the primary EQ-5D utility and EQ-5D VAS outcomes to estimate the likely range of values expected in future comparable settings. Pooled estimates were graphically presented using forest plots.

Sensitivity analyses were performed using a leave-one-out approach to assess the robustness of pooled estimates and to examine the influence of individual studies. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to country and EQ-5D version where data were available. Because EQ-5D-3 L and EQ-5D-5 L differ in descriptive sensitivity and valuation algorithms, EQ-5D version was considered a potential source of methodological heterogeneity. Formal mapping between EQ-5D-3 L and EQ-5D-5 L was not performed because several included studies did not provide sufficient item-level responses or value-set information required for robust crosswalk conversion.

Meta-regression was conducted to explore whether mean age influenced EQ-5D utility estimates. Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically using Egger’s regression test. As this study synthesized secondary data from previously published literature, ethical approval and informed consent were not required. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software with “meta” package.

Results

A total of 9,227 articles were retrieved from the databases using a systematic literature search, comprising PubMed (n = 2943), Embase (n = 4332), Scopus (n = 1898) and CENTRAL (n = 54) databases. Following the removal of duplicates, 6799 studies were evaluated during the initial screening phase (title and abstract review). A total of 119 of those studies were considered eligible for secondary screening (full-text review). Ninety-five articles were excluded for not fulfilling the eligibility criteria in full-text screening. Finally, a total of 24 studies were considered for inclusion in the systematic review and meta-analysis3,9,13–34. Meta-analyses were conducted for 17 studies reporting EQ-5D utility scores, 17 studies reporting EQ-5D VAS scores, and seven studies reporting domain-specific EQ-5D proportions. Overall, all the 24 studies which provided either EQ-5D utility or EQ-5D VAS or domain-specific EQ-5D proportions contributed to meta-analysis. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1).

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of 20 cross-sectional studies included in the systematic review was assessed using the AXIS tool. Out of the 20 cross-sectional studies, 12 studies provided clear explanation and data appropriately for the characteristics of the study related to the aim of the study, study design, definition of the target population, measurement of appropriate outcomes, tools used for measurement of outcomes, statistical significance, reproducibility of methods, description of basic data, description of results in the methods, justification of discussion and conclusion, ethical approval, and informed consent, the response was “Yes” in all of the studies, indicating a low risk of bias for these questions. The study’s limitations were discussed in all 20 studies. In 25% of the studies (5/20), the sample frame was drawn from a relevant population. The selection process was likely to choose participants in 25% (5/20) of the studies. The studies did not provide explanations for sample size, internal consistency of results, or appropriate information regarding non-responders, indicating a significant risk of bias in these aspects. Only 3 research studies (23%) have stated actions used to address and characterize non-responders (Supplementary Fig. 1). The RoB-2 tool was employed to evaluate the risk of bias in the four randomized controlled trials included in this review. Three trials were assessed as having a minimal risk of bias across the five principal RoB-2 domains, whereas one trial had “some concerns” as RCTs primarily contributed to VAS analyses rather than utility pooling, the potential impact on primary outcomes is likely minimal in both the randomization method and the completeness of outcome reporting (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Meta-analysis of EQ-5D utility scores and VAS

Seventeen studies with a total sample size of 279,179 participants were included in the meta-analysis of EQ-5D utility scores among patients with cervical cancer3,9,14–16,18,19,21,22,24,26–29,32–34. Using a random-effects model, the pooled mean EQ-5D utility score was 0.79 (95% CI: 0.73–0.85), indicating reduced health-related quality of life among patients with cervical cancer. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 99.6%), suggesting considerable variability in utility estimates across populations and settings. The 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.52 to 1.05, indicating that utility values in future comparable settings may vary widely. Therefore, the pooled utility estimate should be interpreted as a broad reference value rather than a directly transferable universal estimate (Fig. 2).

Seventeen studies with a total sample size of 4,741 participants reported EQ-5D VAS scores9,13,14,16–18,20–27,31–33. The pooled mean EQ-5D VAS score was 71.82 (95% CI: 67.95–75.69) using a random-effects model. This finding suggests moderate impairment in self-rated health among patients with cervical cancer. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 98.3%), and the 95% prediction interval ranged from 54.54 to 89.11, showing wide expected variation across future study populations. These findings indicate that although the pooled VAS score provides a useful summary estimate, it should be interpreted cautiously in view of the high between-study variability (Fig. 3).

Country-specific subgroup analysis demonstrated substantial variation in EQ-5D utility estimates across included countries. The highest pooled utility estimates were observed in China 0.90 (95% CI: 0.84–0.97), Ethiopia 0.89 (95% CI: 0.88–0.91), and Thailand 0.88 (95% CI: 0.85–0.90), whereas lower estimates were observed in the United States 0.78 (95% CI: 0.78–0.78) and Indonesia 0.65 (95% CI: 0.51–0.79) (Supplementary Table 7). However, these country-level differences should be interpreted cautiously because they may reflect differences in patient characteristics, disease stage distribution, healthcare access, EQ-5D version, and national value sets or tariff algorithms, rather than true differences in health status alone. Overall, substantial cross-country variability was observed, but these differences may reflect both clinical and methodological factors, including EQ-5D valuation methods.

Country-wise subgroup analysis of EQ-5D VAS scores also showed variation across settings. The highest pooled VAS scores were reported in the Netherlands 84.70 (95% CI: 82.99–86.40) and Thailand 80.60 (95% CI: 78.40–82.80), while lower values were observed in Germany 52.50 (95% CI: 44.18–60.82), Ethiopia 66.27 (95% CI: 64.96–67.57), Canada 66.50 (95% CI: 62.26–70.74), and India 67.60 (95% CI: 67.04–68.16). Indonesia and the United States showed pooled VAS estimates of 72.51 (95% CI: 67.18–77.84) and 72.70 (95% CI: 71.38–74.01), respectively. Overall, these findings suggest substantial cross-country variability in HRQoL estimates, but direct comparison between countries should be made cautiously due to differences in study design, population composition, valuation methods, and healthcare context (Supplementary Table 8).

Subgroup analysis according to EQ-5D version was conducted to explore whether the measurement version contributed to variability in utility estimates. Seven studies9,18,19,23,25,26,33 using EQ-5D-5 L showed a pooled mean utility score of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.60–0.87; 95% prediction interval: 0.26–1.21), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 99.7%) (Supplementary Fig. 4). Five studies3,14,15,21,24 using EQ-5D-3 L reported a pooled mean utility score of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.75–0.85; 95% prediction interval: 0.64–0.95), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 92.1%). Studies that did not clearly report the EQ-5D version16,22,28,29,34 yielded a pooled mean utility score of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.80–0.88), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 99.7%) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Publication bias and robustness of the pooled estimates were assessed separately for EQ-5D utility and EQ-5D VAS outcomes. For EQ-5D utility scores, visual inspection of the funnel plot did not suggest marked asymmetry. Egger’s regression test was not statistically significant (SE = 4.22, t = 0.73, df = 15; P = 0.477), indicating no statistical evidence of small-study effects for the pooled EQ-5D utility estimate (Supplementary Fig. 5). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that sequential omission of individual studies did not materially change the pooled EQ-5D utility estimate, suggesting that no single study had a disproportionate influence on the overall pooled result. In the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, omission of the largest study did not materially alter the pooled EQ-5D utility estimate (Supplementary Fig. 6). For EQ-5D VAS scores, funnel plot inspection also did not indicate marked asymmetry, and Egger’s regression test was not statistically significant (β₁ = 0.00, SE = 0.563, z = 0.00; P = 1.0000) (Supplementary Fig. 7). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that the pooled VAS estimate remained broadly consistent after removing each study sequentially, ranging from 71.00 (95% CI: 67.27–74.72; I² = 97.8%)20 to 72.80 (95% CI: 69.26–76.34; I² = 98.4%)17. These findings suggest that the pooled VAS estimate was not driven by any single study (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Meta-regression was conducted to examine whether mean age explained variation in EQ-5D utility scores across studies. Eleven studies were included in this analysis. Mean age was not significantly associated with EQ-5D utility scores, as shown by the non-significant moderator test (F = 0.11; P = 0.746) and the small regression coefficient (β = 0.0012; SE = 0.0037). Substantial residual heterogeneity remained after including mean age in the model (τ² = 0.0033; I² = 98.21%), and the test for residual heterogeneity was statistically significant (QE = 212.49; P < 0.0001). The model explained none of the between-study heterogeneity (R² = 0%).

These findings suggest that mean age did not explain the observed variability in EQ-5D utility estimates. However, this analysis should be interpreted as exploratory because only a limited number of studies reported mean age, and other important factors such as disease stage, treatment status, recurrence, comorbidities, socioeconomic status, EQ-5D version, and value-set differences could not be fully adjusted for.

Four studies involving 381 participants were included in the meta-analysis of EQ-5D utility scores among individuals with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN). The pooled EQ-5D utility score was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.91–0.97), indicating relatively preserved health-related quality of life among women with pre-cancerous cervical lesions. Individual study estimates ranged from 0.90 to 0.97. Significant heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 89.4%, p < 0.0001), and the prediction interval ranged from 0.84 to 1.04. A random-effects model was applied for pooled analysis (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Stage-specific analyses were conducted to explore variation in HRQoL across cervical cancer stages. For EQ-5D utility scores, seven studies3,9,14–16,32,33 involving 622 participants contributed data for Stage I disease, yielding a pooled mean utility score of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.80–0.93; 95% prediction interval: 0.64–1.09; I² = 91.9%) (Supplementary Fig. 10). For Stage II disease, seven studies involving 595 participants yielded a pooled utility score of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.72–0.91; 95% prediction interval: 0.50–1.13; I² = 99.5%) (Supplementary Fig. 11). For Stage III disease, six studies involving 226 participants showed a pooled utility score of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.68–0.91; 95% prediction interval: 0.42–1.17; I² = 97.6%) (Supplementary Fig. 12). For Stage IV disease, five studies involving 58 participants yielded a pooled utility score of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.78–0.94; 95% prediction interval: 0.62–1.10; I² = 70.1%) (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Overall, EQ-5D utility scores showed a modest decline from Stage I to Stage III disease. However, the Stage IV estimate was unexpectedly higher than the Stage III estimate and comparable to the Stage I estimate. Given the small sample size and substantial between-study heterogeneity, these stage-specific findings should be interpreted cautiously.

For EQ-5D VAS scores, five studies9,14,16,32,33 involving 366 participants contributed data for Stage I disease, showing the highest pooled VAS score of 79.48 (95% CI: 74.42–84.54; 95% prediction interval: 62.98–95.99; I² = 88.7%) (Supplementary Fig. 14). For Stage II disease, five studies involving 455 participants yielded a pooled VAS score of 73.57 (95% CI: 67.82–79.32; 95% prediction interval: 54.18–92.96; I² = 94.3%) (Supplementary Fig. 15). For Stage III disease, five studies involving 199 participants showed a pooled VAS score of 74.19 (95% CI: 64.09–84.30; 95% prediction interval: 39.91–108.48; I² = 96.0%) (Supplementary Fig. 16). For Stage IV disease, five studies involving 58 participants yielded a pooled VAS score of 71.37 (95% CI: 58.58–84.16; 95% prediction interval: 32.19–110.56; I² = 87.6%) (Supplementary Fig. 17).

These findings suggest that self-rated health was generally higher in early-stage disease and lower in advanced-stage disease. However, the wide confidence intervals and prediction intervals, particularly for Stage III and Stage IV, indicate substantial uncertainty. Therefore, stage-specific VAS findings should also be interpreted cautiously.

A total of seven studies reported the proportion of problems across all five EQ-5D domains among individuals with cervical cancer. We had combined individuals with slight, moderate, severe and extreme problems into one category and individuals with no problem as another category for each domain in all studies. The pooled estimated proportion of individuals reporting problems in the mobility domain was 27.0% (95% CI: 15%– 45%), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 98.3%, τ² = 1.08) (Supplementary Fig. 18). For the self-care domain, the pooled proportion was 9.0% (95% CI: 5%–16%) with very high heterogeneity (I² = 93.9%, τ² = 0.73) (Supplementary Fig. 19). In the usual activity’s domain, 31.0% (95% CI: 15.0%–53.0%) of individuals reported difficulties, also with high heterogeneity (I² = 98.6%, τ² = 1.50) (Supplementary Fig. 20). The highest proportion of individuals reporting problems was observed in the pain/discomfort domain, where the pooled mean was 55.1% (95% CI: 38.0%–71.0%, I² = 97.9%, τ² = 0.80) (Supplementary Fig. 21). Finally, in the anxiety/depression domain, the pooled proportion was 45.0% (95% CI: 36.1%–54.0%) with very high heterogeneity (I² = 93.0%, τ² = 0.20) (Supplementary Fig. 22). Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized 24 studies involving 281,958 patients from 15 countries and found that cervical cancer is associated with reduced health-related quality of life. The pooled EQ-5D utility score was 0.79, while the pooled EQ-5D VAS score was 71.82, indicating a measurable burden on both preference-based health utility and self-rated health. However, the very high heterogeneity and wide prediction intervals observed in both analyses indicate that these pooled estimates should be interpreted as broad reference values rather than directly transferable utility inputs for all settings. Although studies using EQ-5D-5 L demonstrated slightly lower pooled utility estimates than studies using EQ-5D-3 L, this difference should be interpreted cautiously because heterogeneity remained substantial within all subgroups. These findings suggest that variation in EQ-5D versions may contribute to methodological heterogeneity but does not fully explain the observed variability in utility estimates across studies. Formal mapping between EQ-5D-3 L and EQ-5D-5 L was not performed because several studies did not report sufficient item-level responses or valuation-set information required for robust crosswalk conversion.

The findings have relevance for cost–utility analysis and health technology assessment because EQ-5D utility values are commonly used for quality-adjusted life-year estimation. Nevertheless, health technology assessment is context-specific, and locally derived value sets remain preferable for decision-making whenever available. Therefore, the pooled cross-regional estimates from this review should be applied cautiously, considering local valuation methods, healthcare systems, disease-stage distribution, and population preferences.

Stage-specific analyses showed an inconsistent pattern. EQ-5D utility values appeared to decline from Stage I to Stage III, but the Stage IV estimate was unexpectedly higher than Stage III and close to the Stage I estimate. This should not be interpreted as true clinical similarity between Stage IV and early-stage disease. Rather, the Stage IV estimate is likely influenced by small sample size, survivor bias, cross-sectional sampling, timing of HRQoL assessment, and possible under-representation of severely ill patients. In contrast, EQ-5D VAS scores showed a more clinically expected pattern, with higher self-rated health in Stage I and lower values in advanced disease, although uncertainty remained high.

Domain-level analyses showed that pain/discomfort (55.1%) and anxiety/depression (44.9%) were the most frequently affected EQ-5D dimensions. This suggests that cervical cancer imposes both physical and psychological burden, while problems in self-care were less commonly reported. These findings highlight the need for supportive care approaches that address symptom control and emotional distress, alongside disease-directed treatment.

The substantial heterogeneity observed across pooled utility and VAS estimates is likely multifactorial. Differences in country, healthcare setting, disease-stage distribution, treatment status, timing of HRQoL assessment, and patient characteristics may all have contributed to between-study variability. Methodological differences in EQ-5D measurement may also be important. In the subgroup analysis, studies using EQ-5D-5 L showed a lower pooled utility estimate than those using EQ-5D-3 L; however, heterogeneity remained substantial within both subgroups. Therefore, EQ-5D version may contribute to variability, but it does not fully explain the observed differences across studies.

Country-level differences should also be interpreted cautiously because EQ-5D utilities are influenced not only by patient health status but also by the value set or tariff used to convert health states into utility scores. As a result, lower utility values in some countries may partly reflect differences in national valuation algorithms or societal preferences rather than poorer health alone. Cross-country differences in pooled EQ-5D utility values should be interpreted cautiously, as they may partly reflect variation in national EQ-5D valuation tariffs rather than true differences in patient health status alone. Most included studies did not report the tariff or value set used, limiting assessment of whether local, crosswalk, or foreign tariffs were applied; therefore, subgroup analysis according to tariff severity could not be performed reliably. Consequently, between-country differences may reflect a combination of true HRQoL variation, healthcare-system differences, and methodological variability in EQ-5D valuation approaches.

Another important consideration is the potential for healthy participant bias in survey-based or ambulatory study populations. Large population-based surveys may preferentially include patients who are well enough to participate, while patients who are hospitalized, institutionalized, severely symptomatic, or near the end of life may be under-represented. This is particularly relevant for studies with very large survey-based samples, where the included participants may not fully capture the HRQoL burden experienced by patients with advanced or clinically severe cervical cancer. As a result, pooled utility estimates may partly underestimate the true burden of disease in the sickest patient groups28.

To place our findings in context, previous literature suggests that the pooled EQ-5D utility of 0.79 observed in this review falls within the range reported for many solid tumors, but tends to be lower than utilities in early-stage breast cancer and similar to or higher than those observed in advanced ovarian and lung cancers. A broad systematic review of health state utility values by cancer stage reported that mean utilities for cervical cancer typically range from 0.63 to 0.85 in stage I, 0.50–0.76 in stage II, 0.52–0.71 in stage III and 0.18–0.77 in stage IV, with comparable stage-related declines documented for breast, colorectal and lung cancers8. In an Indian breast cancer cohort, newly diagnosed patients had substantially reduced EQ-5D-5 L utilities compared with non-cancer controls, with improvements after treatment and worsening with higher stage, mirroring the stage–utility gradient seen in cervical cancer. Multicancer EQ-5D-5 L analyses have also shown that education, stage and comorbidity are key predictors of utilities across tumour types, suggesting that contextual and socioeconomic determinants shape HRQoL in cervical as well as breast, colorectal and other cancers35.

Despite the large overall sample, the evidence base remains unevenly distributed, with many regions and health system contexts under-represented and several countries contributing only small, single-Centre studies. Data are particularly sparse for granular health states such as precancerous lesions, recurrent or metastatic disease, post-treatment survivorship, and specific treatment pathways (surgery alone, chemoradiation, targeted therapies) constraining their use in detailed economic models. Future research should prioritize longitudinal multicenter cohorts using EQ-5D-5 L and standardized, country-specific valuation sets, capturing utilities across the full cervical cancer continuum from screening and CIN to recurrence and survivorship. Studies should also systematically measure potential modifiers including comorbidities, socioeconomic status, financial toxicity, and time since diagnosis and compare generic utilities with cancer-specific HRQoL instruments to ensure that commonly affected domains such as pain, sexual function, and emotional well-being are adequately reflected for HTA8.

This review has several limitations. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed across the pooled EQ-5D utility, EQ-5D VAS, stage-specific, and domain-specific analyses. Although random-effects models were used and 95% prediction intervals were reported, the wide prediction intervals indicate that utility values may vary considerably across future settings. Therefore, the pooled estimates should be interpreted as reference values rather than universally applicable utility inputs.

Second, stage-specific estimates should be interpreted cautiously, particularly for Stage IV disease. The Stage IV utility estimate was based on a small sample size and may be influenced by survivor bias, cross-sectional sampling, timing of HRQoL assessment, and under-representation of severely ill patients. Therefore, the apparently higher Stage IV utility estimate should not be interpreted as true clinical similarity with early-stage disease.

Third, differences in EQ-5D version and value-set algorithms may have contributed to variability across studies. Although subgroup analysis by EQ-5D version was performed, formal mapping between EQ-5D-3 L and EQ-5D-5 L was not undertaken because several studies did not provide sufficient item-level or value-set information required for robust crosswalk conversion. Similarly, most included studies did not report the EQ-5D tariff or valuation set used, limiting the ability to assess the impact of country-specific valuation methods on pooled utility estimates.

Fourth, some included studies were based on survey-based or ambulatory populations, which may introduce healthy participant bias. Patients who are hospitalized, institutionalized, severely symptomatic, or near the end of life may be less likely to participate in such studies. As a result, the pooled estimates may underestimate the HRQoL burden among patients with advanced or clinically severe cervical cancer.

Finally, the analysis was based on aggregate study-level data rather than individual patient-level data. Therefore, it was not possible to fully adjust for important clinical and methodological factors such as treatment status, time since diagnosis, recurrence, comorbidities, socioeconomic status, healthcare access, EQ-5D valuation method, and disease-stage distribution.

Conversely, this review’s strengths include a prospectively registered protocol, comprehensive multi-database search without language or date restrictions, and the largest synthesis to date of EQ-5D utilities, VAS scores, and dimension-level problems in cervical cancer. Rigorous risk-of-bias assessment, extensive sensitivity analyses, meta-regression, and country- and stage-specific pooling enhance the credibility and practical relevance of the estimates for economic evaluation and policy planning.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis found that cervical cancer is associated with reduced health-related quality of life, with a pooled EQ-5D utility score of 0.79 and a pooled EQ-5D VAS score of 71.82. The EQ-5D domain-level findings showed that pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression were the most frequently affected dimensions, highlighting the physical and psychological burden experienced by patients with cervical cancer.

The pooled EQ-5D estimates provide useful reference values for cost–utility analysis, quality-adjusted life-year estimation, and health technology assessment. However, substantial heterogeneity and wide prediction intervals indicate that these values should be interpreted cautiously and adapted to local settings where possible. Stage-specific estimates, particularly for Stage IV disease, should also be interpreted carefully because of small sample size, potential survivor bias, cross-sectional sampling, and high between-study variability. Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies, standardized EQ-5D reporting, clear documentation of value sets, and robust stage-specific utility estimates to support more accurate economic evaluation and policy decision-making in cervical cancer.
