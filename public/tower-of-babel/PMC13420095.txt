The Rectus Femoris Tendon Graft for ACL Reconstruction: Predictive Anthropometry and Surgical Pearls Based on a Prospective Case Series
Authors: Bernardo Garcia Barroso, Márcio Cabral Fagundes Rêgo, Marcelo Cabral Fagundes Rêgo, Felipe Silveira Martins, Diego Ariel de Lima, Sergio Marinho de Gusmão Canuto, Camilo Partezani Helito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The rectus femoris tendon has recently gained attention as a promising soft tissue autograft for anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction. However, there is limited information regarding its graft dimensions, harvesting technique, and safety profile.Purpose:To evaluate the dimensions of the rectus femoris tendon graft in patients undergoing ACL reconstruction, identify anthropometric predictors of graft size, describe key technical steps for safe harvesting, and report intraoperative complications.Study Design:Case series; Level of evidence, 4.Methods:A total of 211 consecutive patients undergoing ACL reconstruction with rectus femoris tendon autograft were prospectively enrolled across multiple centers. Graft dimensions—length and diameter in 2- and 3-strand configurations—were recorded intraoperatively. Correlations between graft size and patient anthropometry were assessed, and a multivariable regression model was developed to predict graft length. Intraoperative complications during graft harvesting were also documented.Results:The mean graft length was 30.8 ± 3.4 cm, with 99.1% measuring ≥24 cm. Mean diameters were 8.1 ± 0.6 mm (2-strand) and 9.3 ± 0.7 mm (3-strand), with only 1.4% of 3-strand grafts measuring <8 mm. Patient height showed the strongest correlation with graft length (r = 0.563). Complications occurred in 11.4% of cases, mostly minor and during the early adoption phase of this technique.Conclusion:The rectus femoris tendon provides consistent and predictable graft dimensions suitable for multistrand ACL reconstruction, including in patients at higher risk of undersized soft tissue grafts. These morphometric findings support its potential as an autograft option and provide a basis for preoperative planning. Clinical outcomes and donor-site morbidity require evaluation in future studies.

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction is among the most frequently performed procedures in orthopaedic sports medicine. 7 Common autograft choices include hamstring tendons and bone–patellar tendon–bone (BPTB) grafts, each with distinct advantages and limitations. 20 Hamstring grafts may yield insufficient diameter—especially in smaller individuals—and diameters <8 mm have been associated with higher revision rates. 12 BPTB grafts offer strong fixation but are often linked to anterior knee pain, kneeling discomfort, and greater donor-site morbidity. 4 These limitations have led to growing interest in quadriceps tendon (QT) autografts as an alternative. 2

Anatomical studies have shown that the superficial layer of the QT is consistently formed by the rectus femoris, making it a distinct and anatomically accessible structure for graft harvesting.9,16 This layer provides a long and thick graft with predictable structure and clear dissection planes. Biomechanical analyses show that QT grafts have a larger cross-sectional area and higher tensile strength than BPTB grafts. 22 Specific studies of the rectus femoris tendon also demonstrate favorable mechanical properties, with elasticity and strength comparable with the native ACL. 24

Minimally invasive techniques to harvest the rectus femoris tendon have recently been described.17,23 These methods yield grafts long enough for double- or triple-strand configurations, support combined reconstructions,1,18 and may reduce donor-site morbidity. Early clinical outcomes using the rectus femoris tendon have been encouraging, both in primary reconstruction 19 and in revision settings. 8 Despite these advances and the apparent robustness of this graft, large-scale morphometric data on the rectus femoris tendon as an isolated graft remain scarce, and a systematic characterization of its dimensions, anthropometric predictors, and intraoperative safety profile is needed to support its broader adoption.

In this way, this study aimed to evaluate the graft dimensions of the rectus femoris tendon in patients undergoing ACL reconstruction, identify anthropometric predictors of graft size, describe key technical steps for safe harvesting, and report intraoperative complications. Our hypothesis is that the rectus femoris tendon consistently provides adequate graft dimensions for ACL reconstruction, with an acceptable intraoperative complication profile.

Methods

This was a prospective consecutive case series, approved by the local institutional review board. Informed consent was obtained. Data were collected prospectively using standardized forms, and the analysis was performed retrospectively. A total of 211 consecutive patients undergoing ACL reconstruction using a rectus femoris tendon autograft were included. Surgeries were performed by 5 subspecialty-trained knee surgeons (B.G.B., M.C.F.R., M.C.F.R., F.S.M., C.P.H.) with high volume in ACL reconstruction, following a standardized, previously described harvesting technique. 1 This study represents a consecutive series of ACL reconstructions performed during the study period in which the rectus femoris tendon graft was used as the planned graft option.

Surgical Technique and Graft Harvest

In all cases, the patient was positioned supine under spinal anesthesia. A longitudinal skin incision of approximately 3 to 4 cm was made about 3 cm proximal to the superior pole of the patella. With the knee flexed at 90° to facilitate anatomical plane identification, the rectus femoris tendon was identified and dissected bluntly from the intermediate layer, taking advantage of the natural separation by a thin layer of fat (Figure 1). Two longitudinal tendon incisions, with a distance of 10 to 12 mm between them, were made ≥3 cm proximal to the patella, a position in which the rectus femoris tendon was not merged to the vastus, and approximately 2 to 3 mm deep, avoiding as much as possible encroachment on the vastus intermedius and the joint capsule. A clamp was placed between the rectus femoris and the vastus intermedius connecting the 2 incisions (Figure 2). Medial and lateral releases, as well as separation from the intermediate lamina, were performed before graft mobilization. A distal dissection until the proximal pole of the patella was performed and the tendon was detached from this site (Figure 3). A proximal dissection of ≥7 to 8 cm was performed, up to the myotendinous transition. A rigid 8- to 9-mm tendon stripper was introduced only after confirming complete digital mobilization (Figure 4), feeling a free excursion of the tendon, similar to what is done for the removal of the semitendinosus. Before introducing the stripper, the graft was prepared with nonabsorbable sutures to facilitate controlled extraction. With the knee extended between 0° and 20°, the stripper was directed toward the anterior superior iliac spine and advanced proximally until the tendon released. After harvest, the graft diameter was measured in 2-strand and 3-strand configurations using a sizing block. A 2-strand configuration was used when graft diameter was ≥8 mm; in most cases, if the 2-strand diameter was <8 mm, the graft was converted to a 3-strand configuration to increase diameter. Both configuration selection and fixation method were left to the operating surgeon's discretion. Femoral fixation was performed using a suspensory cortical button or interference screw, and tibial fixation was performed using an interference screw.

Data Collection and Graft Measurements

Demographic data (sex, age, weight, height, body mass index [BMI]) were recorded. Intraoperative measurements included total graft length and graft diameter in 2-strand and 3-strand configurations. After harvesting and trimming performed to remove nontendinous fringes, graft length was measured using a sterile millimetric ruler. Graft diameter was measured after folding the tendon into 2-strand and 3-strand configurations using a sizing block, prior to final implantation. Data were recorded on a standardized data collection form completed intraoperatively by the surgical team. Complications related to graft harvesting—such as graft amputation, joint capsular perforation, or hematoma—were recorded systematically according to predefined criteria.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative variables were summarized using means, standard deviations, medians, and interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute and relative frequencies. Comparisons between male and female patients were performed using unpaired Student t tests. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess associations between anthropometric variables and graft dimensions. A multiple linear regression model was used to identify independent predictors of graft length. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM Corp), and tabulated in Microsoft Excel 2013. A significance level of P < .05 was adopted for all analyses.

Results

A total of 211 patients who underwent ACL reconstruction using a rectus femoris tendon autograft were included. The mean age was 29.1 ± 9.6 years. Most patients were male (n = 167; 79.1%). The mean weight was 84.0 ± 16.7 kg, height was 1.73 ± 0.10 m, and BMI was 24.2 ± 4.5 kg/m2 (Table 1).

Male patients had significantly longer grafts than female patients (31.5 ± 3.2 cm vs 28.0 ± 2.5 cm; P < .001). Although men tended to have slightly larger diameters in both configurations, these differences were not statistically significant (P = .11 and P = .07, respectively) (Table 2). Grafts <24 cm were rare in both groups (0.6% in men vs 2.3% in women). Diameters <8 mm in the 2-strand configuration were more common in female patients (34.1% vs 21.6%), but this difference was not observed in the 3-strand configuration (1.4% in total).

Graft length showed significant positive correlation with all anthropometric variables, with height showing the strongest (r = 0.563; P < .001). Weight (r = 0.360; P < .001), BMI (r = 0.227; P = .001), and age (r = 0.143; P = .04) also correlated with length, though to a lesser extent (Table 3). Graft diameters in both configurations showed weaker but significant correlations with height (r = 0.199 and r = 0.231, respectively; both P < .01), while other associations with diameter were not statistically significant.

A multiple linear regression model identified 2 independent predictors of graft length: sex and height. Male sex was associated with a 1.62-cm increase (P = .001), and each additional meter in height added 17.02 cm to graft length (P < .001), corresponding to approximately 1.7 cm of additional graft length per 10 cm of height. Together, these variables explained 34.7% of the variance (R2 = 0.347). The resulting formula was:

Estimatedgraftlength(cm)=17.02×Height(m)+1.62×Male(1or0).

For example, a man measuring 1.78 m would be expected to have a graft of approximately 32.9 cm, while a woman measuring 1.60 m would have an estimated graft length of 27.2 cm. This model may assist surgeons in anticipating graft characteristics and guiding preoperative planning.

Intraoperative complications (Figure 6) related to graft harvesting occurred in 24 patients (11.4%), while 187 procedures (88.6%) were completed without incident (Table 4). The most common issues included dissection in an incorrect anatomical plane, joint capsular perforation, and difficulty advancing the stripper due to misalignment. Specifically, capsular violation occurred in 17 cases (8.1%), and premature graft amputation occurred in 5 cases (2.4%). Notably, in all cases of early amputation, a second graft could be successfully harvested from the same rectus femoris tendon, eliminating the need for alternative grafts. Relevant anterior thigh hematoma was recorded in 2 cases (0.9%) (Table 4). Most complications occurred during the early adoption phase of this technique, underscoring the importance of technique refinement and anatomical familiarity.

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that the rectus femoris tendon provides consistent and reliable dimensions for ACL reconstruction. In our cohort of 211 patients, 98.6% reached ≥8 mm in diameter using the 3-strand configuration. In 99.1% of cases, the graft length was ≥24 cm, which allows a graft of ≥8 cm when folded in 3, sufficient to perform any technique and any type of fixation. These results confirm the suitability of the rectus femoris tendon as a robust autograft option, even in women and individuals with short stature considered at higher risk for insufficient graft size.

Graft length showed the strongest correlation with height. Weight, BMI, and age also correlated with graft length, though these weaker associations likely reflect general body size covariance rather than a direct causal relationship with tendon morphology. Based on these findings, we developed a simple exploratory predictive model using sex and height as preoperative planning aids. External validation in independent cohorts is required before broader clinical adoption.

Compared with traditional quadriceps grafts—which may involve partial- or full-thickness harvests and may include a bone block—the rectus femoris tendon consistently provides a longer harvested tendon. In our study, the mean tendon length was 30.8 cm, yielding an effective graft of approximately 15.4 cm when folded in 2 strands and 10.3 cm when folded in 3 strands, which is comparable with the 7 to 9 cm typically reported for standard quadriceps grafts.5,21 The extended harvested length allows for both double- and triple-strand configurations and enables combined anterolateral 1 and anteromedial reconstructions 18 using a single tendon, simplifying the surgical workflow. As a partial-thickness quadriceps-based harvest, recent literature suggests that this approach may yield comparable clinical outcomes with full-thickness harvests, 10 with lower risk of postoperative quadriceps strength deficits. 11

In terms of diameter, the rectus femoris tendon demonstrated consistent and reliable graft sizes. In our study, both configurations yielded clinically acceptable diameters, with mean values of 8.1 mm for the 2-strand and 9.3 mm for the 3-strand technique (Table 2), with only 1.4% of patients failing to reach 8 mm in the 3-strand configuration. These results are particularly relevant given that hamstring autograft diameter <8 mm has been associated with a 32% increased risk of aseptic revision in a series of nearly 6000 cases, 14 as well as worsening of functional scores and higher failure rates in other hamstring series.12,13 Even with 5- or 6-strand configurations, the possibility of undersized hamstring grafts remains nonnegligible. 15 Whether extra-articular reconstruction can compensate for smaller graft diameter remains under investigation.3,6

Intraoperative complications occurred in 11.4% of cases, mostly of minor significance and during the early adoption phase of this technique. Capsular violation was the most frequent event (8.1%), typically resulting from dissection in an incorrect anatomical plane or excessive depth. Premature graft amputation, which occurred in 5 cases (2.4%), was usually related to dissection too medial or lateral, incomplete release, or stripper misdirection. Notably, in all amputation cases, a second graft could be successfully harvested from the same rectus femoris tendon, avoiding the need for alternative grafts, a particularly valuable feature in multiligament reconstruction settings or when graft availability is limited. No neurovascular injuries, quadriceps ruptures, or conversions to another graft source were observed.

Several technical points deserve emphasis (Table 5). The clamp maneuver between the rectus femoris and vastus intermedius serves not only to confirm graft width but also to verify the correct dissection plane. Resistance or difficulty passing the clamp indicates a plane that is too deep. Flexible strippers should be avoided, as they may bend or fail if the tendon has not been adequately released. In cases of uncertainty regarding the stripper trajectory, direct visualization or arthroscopic confirmation can be used. The 2 most common errors are initiating dissection too close to the patella, where the tissue planes are less distinct, and advancing the stripper before complete digital mobilization, both of which significantly increase the risk of premature graft amputation.

Early clinical data on the rectus femoris tendon autograft are encouraging. Rêgo et al 19 reported outcomes from 80 primary ACL reconstructions with a mean follow-up of 18 months, achieving a mean postoperative Lysholm score of 97.1 with 100% of patients reaching the Patient Acceptable Symptom State and no graft failures. Notably, all 3 cases of graft amputation during harvest occurred within the first 10 cases, consistent with the early adoption phase effect observed in our series. In a series of 28 revision ACL reconstructions using the rectus femoris tendon with a mean follow-up of 41.7 months, Huber et al 8 reported a mean 4-fold graft diameter of 9.2 mm, significant improvement in Lachman stability testing, and low donor-site morbidity, with only 1 patient reporting prolonged harvest-site pain. A rerupture rate of 7.1% was observed, consistent with the inherently higher failure risk in revision settings. These early results support the feasibility and safety of the rectus femoris tendon autograft in both primary and revision ACL reconstruction, though longer follow-up and comparative studies remain necessary.

Limitations

This study has limitations. Although data were collected prospectively, the analysis was retrospective. Clinical outcomes such as failure rates or return to sport were not assessed. The reported graft dimensions are technique dependent and reflect a standardized approach by experienced surgeons; reproducibility in other settings or with less experienced surgeons requires further investigation. The sex distribution was imbalanced (79.1% male); this limits the statistical power of sex-based comparisons, which should be interpreted as secondary and exploratory. A formal analysis of the early adoption phase could not be performed, as cases were not chronologically indexed by surgeons across centers.

Conclusion

The rectus femoris tendon provides consistent and predictable graft dimensions suitable for multistrand ACL reconstruction, even in patients at risk for undersized grafts. Its length also supports combined reconstructions using a single tendon. These morphometric findings support its potential as a soft tissue autograft option and provide a basis for preoperative planning. Clinical outcomes and donor-site morbidity require evaluation in future studies.
