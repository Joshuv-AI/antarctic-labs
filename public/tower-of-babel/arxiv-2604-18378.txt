Fourth-order galaxy-galaxy-lensing: Theoretical framework and direct estimation
Jonathan Oel, Lucas Porth, Peter Schneider, Elena Silvestre-Rosello

Abstract
Context. Traditional galaxy-galaxy lensing is a well-established method of probing the statistical properties of the Universe’s matter and galaxy distribution. However, this measure does not carry all the statistical information, provided the matter and galaxy distribution contain non-Gaussian features. In order to study these non-Gaussianities, it is necessary to consider higher-order statistical measures. Aims. The aim of this work is to extend the analytical basis describing the statistical correlations between galaxies and shear to the fourth order, with special emphasis on the associated aperture statistics. In order to include fourth-order statistics in future analysis of the relation between mass and galaxies, we further investigate whether we can expect to detect these statistics from observations of stage IV surveys. Methods. We define the four-point correlation function (4PCF) between the shear and the positions of triplets of foreground galaxies and derive its relation to the respective trispectrum. We convert the 4PCF to aperture statistics and derive the analytical form of the respective filter function, which we then implement in a numerical integration pipeline. Furthermore, we develop a direct estimator that allows us to measure galaxy-mass aperture moments of arbitrary order on pixelized data using a Fast-Fourier-Transform (FFT) algorithm. Results. We show that the aperture measure ⟨ 𝒩 3 ​ M ap ⟩ \langle\mathcal{N}^{3}M_{\rm ap}\rangle can be calculated with sub-percent accuracy on relevant aperture scales, θ \theta , by means of numerical integration. Furthermore, we apply the FFT-based direct estimator to a mock catalog with a realistic stage IV survey setup on a sky area of 2000 ​ deg 2 2000\penalty\ \deg^{2} , and detect the connected part of ⟨ 𝒩 3 ​ M ap ⟩ ​ ( θ ) \langle\mathcal{N}^{3}M_{\rm ap}\rangle(\theta) with a signal-to-noise ratio of roughly nine on small aperture scales.

Introduction

The constraints on cosmological parameters and the validity of any cosmological model rely on the detailed investigation of the properties of the cosmic microwave background and the large-scale distribution of matter and galaxies in the Universe.
One of the well-established means to probe the statistical properties of the large-scale structure (LSS) is provided by cosmic shear
(
6
;
18
;
35
, e.g.)
, which describes the weak gravitational lensing effect caused by the inhomogeneities in the distribution of matter.
Measurements of correlations in the cosmic shear field, as probed with the observed shapes of distant background galaxies, have mostly concentrated on second-order statistics
(
2
;
45
, e.g.)
.
The further technological advancement of cosmic shear surveys goes hand in hand with the significant detection of higher-order statistics
(
5
;
27
, e.g.)
, which are crucial in order to quantify the non-Gaussian information contained in the matter and galaxy distributions.

Apart from shear correlations, one can also study the so-called galaxy-galaxy lensing (GGL) statistics which are described by the two-point correlation function between the positions of foreground “lens” galaxies with the shear field as measured from the shapes of background “source” galaxies.
First detected by
7
, measurements of GGL have been performed for numerous surveys
(
25
;
14
, e.g.)
and can be used as a complementary probe to cosmic shear analyses to retrieve cosmological information
(
1
)
.
Since galaxies are biased tracers of the underlying matter distribution, GGL quantifies the relation between the distributions of galaxies and dark matter, which can be modeled by a scale-dependent bias parameter
(
34
)
.

32
extended the notion of GGL to third-order galaxy-galaxy-galaxy lensing (G3L) statistics, first detected by
39
in the correlation between two lens galaxies and one source ellipticity in the Red-Sequence Cluster Survey
(
11
)
.
Since then, G3L has been subject to further studies in which it was shown that one can probe galaxy-mass correlations in higher order
(
37
)
, that by using a halo model of the galaxy-mass bispectrum, G3L can constrain the halo occupation distributions of galaxies
(
24
)
, and that G3L can put to test semi-analytic models of galaxy evolution
(
29
;
23
)
.

Ongoing stage IV surveys like
Euclid

(
10
)
and LSST
(
17
)
will observe cosmic shear at a new level in terms of depth and sky coverage.
Therefore, in the further advancement of weak gravitational lensing as a tool to probe the properties of the LSS, it is important to assess whether we can expect statistical measures beyond the third order to be significantly detectable.
In particular, by taking into account fourth-order correlations between galaxies and shear (G4L), we introduce a measure that is able to quantify the relation between mass and galaxies beyond the scope of GGL and G3L.

The weak lensing signal measured by GGL is sensitive to the mass environment of individual foreground galaxies, which is dominated by the properties of the galaxy dark matter halo on relatively small scales, and the general mass distribution of the group or cluster a galaxy is embedded in towards larger scales.
This allows us to study the, generally scale-dependent, bias parameter,
b
, and galaxy-mass correlation coefficient,
r
, which relate the matter power spectrum to the cross-power spectrum between galaxies and matter.
For second-order GGL, aperture statistics present a suitable means for probing these parameters
(
34
;
16
;
15
;
40
;
41
, e.g.)
.
Therefore, throughout this work, we particularly focus on fourth-order aperture statistics that quantify the G4L signal.
By considering higher-order correlations (i.e. G3L and G4L), one can further study the weak lensing signal induced by the mass environment around pairs and triplets of foreground galaxies, and therefore study the bias parameter and galaxy-mass correlation coefficient to higher order.

Higher-order galaxy-galaxy lensing signals are also sensitive to the more subtle properties of dark matter halos, like their overall shape – for instance in a lens-shear-shear correlation – and a possible misalignment between the profiles of the mass distribution and the brightness due to a preferred orientation of lens pairs, triplets, and so on
(
38
)
.
However, since these applications require more thorough lens galaxy selections, here we only focus on forecasting the detection of G4L in the context of measuring
b
and
r
.

This work focuses on the development of the G4L statistics as the extension of the second- and third-order counterparts.
First, in Sect.
2
we give a brief overview of the concepts of weak lensing that are required to study G4L.
In Sect.
3
, we then introduce the definition of the four-point correlation function (4PCF) in G4L as well as the corresponding trispectrum, and the relations between these two quantities.
Here we also consider the conversion of the 4PCF into aperture statistics and derive the corresponding formulas.
In Sect.
4
, we develop a numerical integration pipeline in order to measure the fourth-order aperture statistics
\langle\mathcal{N}^{3}M_{\rm ap}\rangle
from the measurement of the corresponding 4PCF, assuming Gaussian shear and galaxy fields.
In Sect.
5
, we present a direct estimator for general mixed aperture moments
\langle\mathcal{N}^{n}M_{\rm ap}^{m}\rangle
, based on the pixelization of the corresponding shear and galaxy fields.
In order to assess the detection significance of aperture statistics, we apply this direct estimator to simulated data with a realistic stage IV survey cosmology setup and present the results in Sect.
6
.

Aperture measures for the relation between cosmic shear and the distribution of galaxies

In this section, we briefly introduce the necessary concepts that are required to study fourth-order statistics in weak gravitational lensing. For a more detailed overview, see, e.g.

2.1
Weak lensing basics

In modern cosmology, weak gravitational lensing is an important tool for probing the matter content of the Universe.
Although causing only a rather subtle distortion of the observed image of a single distant galaxy, this effect becomes noticeable as a systematic image distortion in a larger ensemble of observed galaxy images.
The observed galaxy shapes trace the underlying complex shear,
\gamma
, that emerges from the same scalar lensing potential as the convergence,
\kappa
.
The distribution of matter as a function of the comoving distance,
\chi
, and the projected comoving coordinates,
\boldsymbol{x}=f_{K}(\chi)\penalty\ \boldsymbol{\theta}

\delta(\boldsymbol{x},\chi)=\frac{\rho(\boldsymbol{x},\chi)-\bar{\rho}(\chi)}{\bar{\rho}(\chi)}\;,

(1)

where

denotes the matter density with the spatial mean
\bar{\rho}(\chi)

The effective convergence can then be expressed as a weighted projection of the density contrast through

\kappa(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{3H_{0}^{2}\Omega_{\rm m}}{2c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\chi_{\mathrm{h}}}\mathrm{d}\chi\penalty\ g(\chi)\penalty\ f_{K}(\chi)\penalty\ \frac{\delta(\boldsymbol{x},\chi)}{a(\chi)}\;,

(2)

where we denote the matter density parameter by

(3)

with the galaxy redshift distribution

The discrete galaxy number density

allows us to define the galaxy number density contrast as

\delta_{\mathrm{g}}(\boldsymbol{x},\chi)=\frac{n(\boldsymbol{x},\chi)-\bar{n}(\chi)}{\bar{n}(\chi)}\;,

(4)

where
\bar{n}(\chi)
denotes the mean galaxy number density.
Similarly to Eq. (
2

\kappa_{\mathrm{g}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{N(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\bar{N}}{\bar{N}}=\int\mathrm{d}\chi\penalty\ p_{\mathrm{f}}(\chi)\penalty\ \delta_{\mathrm{g}}(\boldsymbol{x},\chi)\;,

(5)

where

describes the distribution of the foreground lens galaxies with projected galaxy number density

which has the mean value

2.2
Second- and third-order statistics

In GGL one studies the correlation between matter and galaxy distribution.
Using the Fourier transforms of the matter and galaxy number density contrasts,
\hat{\delta}(\boldsymbol{k},\chi)

\langle\hat{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\penalty\ \hat{\kappa}_{\mathrm{g}}(\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\prime})\rangle=(2\pi)^{2}\penalty\ \delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\boldsymbol{\ell}+\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\prime})\penalty\ P_{\kappa\mathrm{g}}(|\boldsymbol{\ell}|)

P_{\kappa\mathrm{g}}(\ell)=\frac{3H_{0}^{2}\Omega_{\rm m}}{2c^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}\chi\penalty\ \frac{g(\chi)\penalty\ p_{\mathrm{f}}(\chi)}{f_{K}(\chi)\penalty\ a(\chi)}P_{\delta\mathrm{g}}\left(\frac{\ell}{f_{K}(\chi)},\chi\right)\;,

(8)

where one assumes a flat sky and uses Limber’s equation
(
22

From weak lensing surveys, the cross-power spectrum can be probed by the correlation function

\langle\gamma_{\mathrm{t}}\rangle(\vartheta)=\langle\kappa_{\mathrm{g}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\penalty\ \gamma(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta};\varphi)\rangle,\;

(9)

where foreground galaxy positions are correlated with the rotated shear

\gamma(\boldsymbol{\theta};\varphi)=\gamma_{\mathrm{t}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\varphi)+\mathrm{i}\gamma_{\times}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\varphi)=-\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\penalty\ \mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\varphi}\;,

(10)

which is the projection of the shear,

, as measured in a cartesian reference frame, towards the direction

\boldsymbol{\vartheta}

The real and imaginary part of the rotated shear can be identified with the tangential and cross components.

Similarly to Eq. (

G_{\mathrm{gg}\gamma}(\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2},\phi)=\left\langle\kappa_{\mathrm{g}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{1})\penalty\ \kappa_{\mathrm{g}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{2})\penalty\ \gamma\left(\boldsymbol{\theta};\frac{\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}}{2}\right)\right\rangle,

where

\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{2}

This 3PCF also extends the notion of the average tangential shear
\langle\gamma_{\mathrm{t}}\rangle
to the third order and is sensitive to the excess shear measured around pairs of foreground galaxies.

2.3
Aperture measures

A useful statistical measure to study the information contained in an observed shear field is provided by the aperture mass, developed by
33

M_{\rm ap}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{0};\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\vartheta\penalty\ U_{\theta}(|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{0}|)\penalty\ \kappa(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\;,

\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{0}

Recalling that shear and convergence emerge from the same scalar potential, the aperture mass can also be expressed by a convolution of the tangential shear field with a different filter function,
Q_{\theta}

We define the complex aperture measure

\displaystyle M=M_{\rm ap}+\mathrm{i}M_{\times}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\vartheta\penalty\ Q_{\theta}(|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{0}|)\penalty\ [\gamma_{\mathrm{t}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta};\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{0})+\mathrm{i}\gamma_{\times}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta};\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{0})]

(13)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\vartheta\penalty\ Q_{\theta}(|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{0}|)\penalty\ \gamma(\boldsymbol{\vartheta};\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{0})\;,

where, provided that the filter function
U_{\theta}
is compensated, i.e.
\int\mathrm{d}\vartheta\penalty\ \vartheta\penalty\ U_{\theta}(\vartheta)=0
,
the filter function
Q_{\theta}
is given by

Q_{\theta}(\vartheta)=\frac{2}{\vartheta^{2}}\int_{0}^{\vartheta}\mathrm{d}\vartheta^{\prime}\penalty\ \vartheta^{\prime}U_{\theta}(\vartheta^{\prime})-U_{\theta}(\vartheta)\;.

(14)

The aperture scale,
\theta
, introduces a characteristic angular scale around which the filter functions contain most of their weight.
Since the quantity
M_{\times}
vanishes if the shear field is solely due to lensing
(
9
;
31
)
, it is a measure of the
B
-mode contamination of the shear field.
In turn,
M_{\rm ap}
is only sensitive to

-mode shear such that the conversion of the shear field into the complex aperture measure
M_{\rm ap}+\mathrm{i}M_{\times}
provides a discriminator of
E

B
-mode components.

In addition to the aperture mass, we define the aperture number counts,
\mathcal{N}
,
(
34
)
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{0};\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\vartheta\penalty\ U_{\theta}(|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{0}|)\penalty\ \kappa_{\mathrm{g}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})

(15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\vartheta\penalty\ U_{\theta}(|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{0}|)\penalty\ N(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\;,

where the second equality follows from

In analogy to the aperture mass, the aperture number counts vanish in the case of a constant galaxy number density

This property ensures that

is only sensitive to deviations from the mean galaxy number density, and thus provides a smoothed representation of the galaxy number density field with zero mean.
Throughout this work, we will use the filter functions

\displaystyle U_{\theta}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\theta^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\vartheta^{2}}{2\theta^{2}}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\vartheta^{2}}{2\theta^{2}}\right)\;,\quad\text{and}

(16)

\displaystyle Q_{\theta}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\vartheta^{2}}{4\pi\theta^{4}}\exp\left(-\frac{\vartheta^{2}}{2\theta^{2}}\right)\;,

first proposed by
9

Fourth-order statistics

The concepts presented in the previous section are generalized to fourth-order statistical measures between shear and galaxies, in the following.
Here we focus on the discussion of a single shear value correlated with the position of a triplet
of foreground galaxies that are biased tracers of the underlying mass distribution. For an overview of the

3.1
Trispectrum

The definition of the power spectrum via a second-order correlation function between the Fourier transforms
of the matter density and galaxy number density contrast,
\hat{\delta}
and
\hat{\delta}_{\mathrm{g}}

to the fourth order. The galaxy-galaxy-galaxy-mass trispectrum is defined by the correlator

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\delta}_{\mathrm{g}}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1})

\displaystyle\penalty\displaystyle\hat{\delta}_{\mathrm{g}}(\boldsymbol{k}_{2})\penalty\ \hat{\delta}_{\mathrm{g}}(\boldsymbol{k}_{3})\penalty\ \hat{\delta}(\boldsymbol{k}_{4})\rangle

Moreover, statistical isotropy implies that

In Eq. (

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\delta}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1})

\displaystyle\penalty\displaystyle\hat{\delta}(\boldsymbol{k}_{2})\penalty\ \hat{\delta}(\boldsymbol{k}_{3})\penalty\ \hat{\delta}(\boldsymbol{k}_{4})\rangle

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{3}\penalty\ \delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2}+\boldsymbol{k}_{3}+\boldsymbol{k}_{4})\penalty\ T_{\delta\delta\delta\delta}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\boldsymbol{k}_{2},\boldsymbol{k}_{3},\boldsymbol{k}_{4};\chi)\;.

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{ggg}\delta}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\boldsymbol{k}_{2},\boldsymbol{k}_{3};\boldsymbol{k}_{4};\chi)

In order to avoid this problem, we will consider the conversion of the 4PCF and the trispectrum to aperture statistics, in the following.

3.3
G4L aperture statistics

Here, we focus on the galaxy-galaxy-galaxy-mass aperture statistics

The conversion of the 4PCF into aperture statistics has the great advantage that it heavily reduces the size of our data vector, from millions of bins in the 4PCF to a few hundreds or a few thousands, if we use multiple different aperture scales.

This highlights the fact that the aperture statistics constitute a direct measure of the full four-point contribution to the measured 4PCF signal.
Moreover, we do not have to worry about including the lower-order corrections in the calculation of

4
Measuring the G4L aperture statistics with a numerical integration pipeline

With Eq. (
27
) and the expression for the filter function in Eq. (
55
) at hand, we are now in the position to compute
\langle\mathcal{N}^{3}M_{\rm ap}\rangle
once the 4PCF
G_{\mathrm{ggg\gamma}}
is measured for a certain configuration of bins in the
(\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2},\vartheta_{3},\phi_{12},\phi_{23})
-space.
For a numerical integration pipeline based on a Riemann sum, the computational complexity is severely affected by the high dimensionality of that parameter space.
Therefore the bin configurations on which the 4PCF is evaluated faces constraints in its resolution; this affects mainly the three radial variables
\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2}
and
\vartheta_{3}
.
In the end, the numerical integration needs to yield sufficiently accurate results under the constraint that the number of bins is strictly limited, while also running for a reasonable amount of time.
In this section, we investigate the technical details of a numerical integration routine based on a Riemann sum and assess the precision of its results.

Before we apply the numerical integration on a complete 4PCF, we first verify the correctness of the results by only taking the Gaussian part of the 4PCF into account, meaning we neglect the connected part for the time being.
This has the advantage that we can decompose the 4PCF into the respective 2PCFs, as shown in Eq. (
58
).
The 2PCFs can be easily measured with high resolution, which enables us to analyze the performance of the numerical integration pipeline in terms of accuracy.
In this special case, the results can be checked because – like the 4PCF – the fourth-order aperture statistics can also be decomposed into second-order aperture statistics (see App.
B
), which can be readily measured.

To generate theory predictions for the 2PCFs
\langle\kappa_{\mathrm{g}}\kappa_{\mathrm{g}}\rangle(\vartheta)
and
\langle\kappa_{\mathrm{g}}\gamma\rangle(\vartheta)

We assume a flat
\Lambda

\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0.3

, the amplitude of density fluctuations
\sigma_{8}=0.8

(

to compute the linear power spectrum using the halofit prescription of

4.1
Numerical integration method

For the purpose of the numerical integration, we apply a dimensionless transformation of the radial integration variables to

[\ln y_{\mathrm{min}},\ln y_{\mathrm{max}}]

\Delta_{\mathrm{ln}}

The angular variables,

\phi_{23}

\displaystyle(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3};\theta_{4})=\sum\limits_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}=1}^{N_{y}}\Delta_{\mathrm{ln}}^{3}\penalty\ y_{i_{1}}^{2}y_{i_{2}}^{2}y_{i_{3}}^{2}\theta_{1}^{2}\theta_{2}^{2}\theta_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\penalty\displaystyle A_{\mathcal{N}^{3}M}(y_{i_{1}}\theta_{1},y_{i_{2}}\theta_{2},y_{i_{3}}\theta_{3},\phi_{j_{1}},\phi_{j_{2}}|\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3};\theta_{4})\;,

4.3.1
Bin-averaged 4PCF

In the measurement of a 4PCF, there is generally more than one galaxy quadruplet configuration that falls into one specific bin of the parameter space
(\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2},\vartheta_{3},\phi_{12},\phi_{23})
.
Although all configurations have very similar radial and angular coordinates, their parameters are slightly different from those defining the bin center.
For this reason, the resulting measured 4PCF represents an average over all configurations that fall into each bin.
Consequently, we need to replace the 4PCF in Eq. (
29

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{\mathrm{ggg}\gamma}

\displaystyle(\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2},\vartheta_{3},\phi_{12},\phi_{23})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta_{\mathrm{ln}}^{3}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int_{\ln\vartheta_{i}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ln}}/2}^{\ln\vartheta_{i}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ln}}/2}\mathrm{d}\ln\vartheta_{i}^{\prime}\penalty\ \right]\penalty\ G_{\mathrm{ggg\gamma}}(\vartheta_{1}^{\prime},\vartheta_{2}^{\prime},\vartheta_{3}^{\prime},\phi_{12},\phi_{23})\;.

In our case, we integrate only the Gaussian part of the 4PCF, which is calculated from 2PCFs according to Eq. (
58

4.4
Effect of the logarithmic radial bin size

The accuracy of the integral estimator in Eq. (
29
) depends to a large part on the resolution in the radial coordinates that is applied in the measurement of the 4PCF.
In Fig.
4
, the numerical integration results are also shown for three different logarithmic radial bin sizes
\Delta_{\mathrm{ln}}
, all performed using the same integration range and the same angular bins.
With a bin size of
\Delta_{\mathrm{ln}}\approx 0.21
, we can already achieve results that agree with the expectation from the second-order aperture statistics within 2.5%.
The bin size of
\Delta_{\mathrm{ln}}\approx 0.11
can be considered the smallest value with which the estimation of the 4PCF is still feasible, and for this case the numerical integration results show only a sub-percent-level bias for all aperture scales.

Although it is computationally not feasible to sample the 4PCF with an arbitrarily high resolution, we can still consider sampling the filter function with an even smaller radial bin size
\Delta_{\mathrm{ln}}
than presented in Fig.
4
.
Similarly to the procedure with the bin-averaged 4PCF in Sect.
4.3.1
, we can subdivide every radial bin into a number of smaller bins.
By performing several test integrations, we find that the impact of the finer sampled filter function is rather small, adding only a
\sim 0.5\%
additional negative bias using a radial bin size of
\Delta_{\mathrm{ln}}\approx 0.11
for the 4PCF.

When we consider different aperture scales, we require a smaller logarithmic bin size in order to achieve the same level of precision as in the case of equal aperture scales.
In general, the more the ratio between the aperture scales deviates from unity, the finer the radial bins need to be to resolve the integrand appropriately.
This is mainly due to the fact that the contributions to the aperture statistics coming from galaxy configurations with different radial coordinates are attenuated when using different aperture scales.
In this case, the main power of the integral is concentrated in configurations with similar radial coordinates which makes it more challenging to resolve the integrand appropriately with a limited bin size.
Therefore, we cannot expect to obtain results as accurate as presented in Fig.
4
in the case of different aperture scales.

5
Estimation of aperture statistics

5.1
Integration of correlation functions

In Sect.
3.3
, we presented the mathematical framework to compute fourth-order aperture statistics by integration over the 4PCF, where one assumes a flat sky over the considered survey field.
The fact that the correlation function can be robustly measured in the presence of survey masks makes this approach preferable to apply to real data.
As was demonstrated in Sect.
4
, for Gaussian random shear and galaxy fields, the corresponding numerical integration can be performed with sufficiently high precision.
In order to detect the connected higher-order contributions to the aperture statistics, we first need a measurement of the 4PCF.
Similar to the estimator of the shear 4PCF introduced in
28
, one can construct an efficient estimator based on a multipole decomposition of the angular dependencies of the correlation function as shown in appendix
C
. However, in the following, we present an alternative, direct estimator for measuring aperture statistics that does not rely on the measurement of the 4PCF.

5.2
Direct estimator

In the direct method of estimating aperture statistics, one samples the statistics on a number of apertures with centers
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathrm{c}}_{i}\}
on a survey field.
In the following, we construct an estimator for general higher-order mixed aperture moments
\langle\mathcal{N}^{n}M_{\rm ap}^{m}\rangle
.
The formalism applied here is similar to that used for the estimators for higher-order aperture mass moments presented in
26
.

We consider an aperture centered at position
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathrm{c}}
, containing the lens galaxies
at positions
\{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}\}
and the source galaxies at positions
\{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\mathrm{S}}_{j}\}
, and allow for different aperture scales
\theta_{\mathrm{N},1},\dots,\theta_{\mathrm{N},n}
and
\theta_{\mathrm{M},1},\dots,\theta_{\mathrm{M},m}
, for both lens and source galaxies, respectively.
As a direct tracer of the shear field, we denote the complex ellipticity of the
j
-th source galaxy by
\epsilon_{j}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{t},j}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon_{\times,j}
, where
\epsilon_{\mathrm{t},j}
and
\epsilon_{\times,j}
are the tangential and cross ellipticity, respectively.
We further allow for an ellipticity weighting with
w^{\mathrm{S}}_{j}
.
The filter functions are denoted by the shorthand
U_{\theta_{\mathrm{N},k};i_{k}}=U_{\theta_{\mathrm{N},k}}(|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i_{k}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathrm{c}}|)
with
k=1,\dots,n
for the
i_{k}
-th lens galaxy, and
Q_{\theta_{\mathrm{M},l};j_{l}}=Q_{\theta_{\mathrm{M},l}}(|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\mathrm{S}}_{j_{l}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathrm{c}}|)
with
l=1,\dots,m
for the
j_{l}
-th source galaxy.
Formally, the filters do not have finite support and thus, in theory, we need to sum over all galaxies on the whole survey field to compute this estimator.
In order to avoid spurious effects due to the finite field size,
13
separated the aperture centers at least four times the maximum aperture scale from the survey boundary.
This cut-off is applicable because the filter functions
U_{\theta}
and
Q_{\theta}
, as given in Eq. (
16

A_{\mathrm{ap}}(\theta)=\pi(4\theta)^{2}
.

The direct estimator for the mixed multiscale aperture moments of
n
-th order in
\mathcal{N}
and
m

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ap}}=(\theta_{\mathrm{N},1},\dots,\theta_{\mathrm{N},n},\theta_{\mathrm{M},1}\dots,\theta_{\mathrm{M},m})

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{N}^{n}M_{\rm ap}^{m}}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathrm{c}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ap}})=\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n}A_{\mathrm{ap}}(\theta_{\mathrm{N},k})\right]\left[\prod_{l=1}^{m}A_{\mathrm{ap}}(\theta_{\mathrm{M},l})\right]

(32)

\displaystyle\times\frac{\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{n}),(j_{1},\dots,j_{m})}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n}U_{\theta_{\mathrm{N},k};i_{k}}\right]\cdot\left[\prod_{l=1}^{m}w^{\mathrm{S}}_{j_{l}}Q_{\theta_{\mathrm{M},l};j_{l}}\epsilon_{\mathrm{t},j_{l}}\right]}{\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{n}),(j_{1},\dots,j_{m})}\left[\prod_{l=1}^{m}w^{\mathrm{S}}_{j_{l}}\right]}\;,

where the indices

and

go over all lens and source galaxies in the aperture, respectively.
The index notation in the sums is defined as

\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})}=\sum\limits_{i_{1}}\sum\limits_{i_{2}\neq i_{1}}\cdots\sum\limits_{i_{1}\neq\cdots\neq i_{n}},

(33)

Following the procedure of

.
Once we have obtained the estimator in Eq. (

\langle\mathcal{N}^{n}M_{\rm ap}^{m}\rangle
statistics.

5.3
Pixelized direct estimator

The direct estimator for
\langle\mathcal{N}^{n}M_{\rm ap}^{m}\rangle
presented in the previous subsection requires a summation over all galaxies inside an aperture.
When we now consider a pixelized representation of the data, we can make use of the Fast-Fourier-Transform (FFT) to perform the convolutions involved in the process of estimating aperture statistics.
The summation over the individual galaxies is numerically more exact, while the accuracy of the method based on the FFT is tied to the resolution of the underlying grid.
However, one advantage of the pixelization is that the computational complexity of operations, like the 2-D convolution, depend only on the number of pixels and not the number of galaxies.
While an increase in the galaxy number density results in a higher computational complexity, it leaves the speed of a discrete 2-D convolution unaffected.
For pixelized data, this method constitutes an efficient way to compute aperture moments.
In order to mitigate the loss of accuracy by the pixelization, it is important to note that the side length of a pixel should be substantially smaller than the minimum aperture scale considered, such that an aperture is resolved appropriately.
The impact of the pixel size in the calculation of aperture moments is further discussed below in Sect.
5.4
.

In order to be able to apply the FFT-method to compute aperture statistics, we first need to transform the estimator in Eq. (
32
) and the formalism presented in Sect.
D.1
from sums over indivdual galaxies to sums over pixels, as presented in Sect.
D.2
.

Towards small aperture scales, the FFT-method systematically underestimates
\langle\mathcal{N}^{2}\rangle(\theta)
with deviations up to

On these small scales, the accuracy of the FFT-method is compromised by the finite pixel size.
If the pixel size is not substantially smaller than the aperture scale, the filter function changes significantly over the range of a pixel, and thus approximating the filter function by evaluating it at the pixel center induces a systematic bias in the calculation of the aperture moments.
Owing to the functional form of the filter functions, this error is expected to become larger with higher order in the aperture moments. We find that for the moments of
\mathcal{N}
the evaluation of the filter function generally has a more profound impact on the result than for the
M_{\rm ap}
-moments. This bias can be mitigated by using a smaller pixel size; however, this goes hand in hand with a larger computational complexity of the FFT-convolutions involved in the process.

During the FFT convolution, the filter functions
U_{\theta}
and
Q_{\theta}
, as well as the lens galaxy number density field and the complex shear field, are converted to harmonic space.
According to the Nyquist–Shannon samplimg theorem
(
36
)
, the pixel size
\Delta_{\mathrm{pix}}
sets an upper limit on the spatial frequency up to which these harmonic representations can be evaluated, which is
(2\Delta_{\mathrm{pix}})^{-1}
.
Any features in the lens galaxy number density and shear field with a higher spatial frequency cannot be reconstructed accurately.
However, the Fourier transforms of the filter functions
U_{\theta}
and
Q_{\theta}
are low-pass filters in harmonic space, whose attenuation of the high frequency modes of the respective field increases with the aperture scale.
This translates to a lower limit of aperture scales down to which we can confidently compute aperture statistics, estimated by
\theta>2\Delta_{\mathrm{pix}}
.
For large aperture scales the pixel size is not expected to have a significant impact on the calculation of aperture statistics, since the filter functions attenuate the signal from high frequency modes which we fail to sample correctly due to the pixelization.

The computation of the correction terms to the aperture moments,
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s},k}
and
M_{\mathrm{s},k}
with
k>1
in Eqs. (
74
) and (
76
), involves the corresponding powers of the filter functions,
U_{\theta}^{k}
and
Q_{\theta}^{k}
.
These are narrower peaked for
k>1
than for
k=1
and, in turn, they are wider in harmonic space.
Therefore, the higher the order of the aperture moment, the stronger its correction terms are affected by the pixel size which has the largest impact on the smallest aperture scales.

Furthermore, since the Fourier transforms of the filter functions
U_{\theta}
and
Q_{\theta}
are low-pass filters, their sampling is also affected by the smallest available spatial frequency.
In real space this means that we need to sample the filter functions up to sufficiently large radii.
For the smallest aperture scales in particular, this means that we have to evaluate the filter functions beyond the limit of
4\theta
up to which the filter functions contain most of their weight.
In practice, we can define a minimum radius, e.g. by setting it to
\mathrm{max}(40^{\prime},4\theta)
, up to which we sample the filter functions.

Especially for large aperture scales, the calculation of the aperture statistics is strongly affected by the selection of the apertures which we average over.
Since the aperture moments computed in neighbouring pixels are correlated, the apertures should be selected such that they are well separated.
Therefore, we have a smaller number of available apertures at large aperture scales, leading to a larger statistical uncertainty.
On the other hand, sampling the aperture centers too sparsely might also give rise to aliasing effects.

6
Detection of fourth-order aperture statistics

With the data release of stage IV weak lensing surveys, it is of particular interest to address whether we can expect a significant detection of fourth-order statistics, such as
\langle\mathcal{N}^{3}M_{\rm ap}\rangle
.
In the following, we make use of the FFT-method introduced in the last section and apply it to simulated data sets representative of the observed data expected from stage IV surveys, in order to analyze the associated signal-to-noise ratio (S/N).

6.1
Mock data

To generate more realistic mock data that results in nontrivial fourth-order correlations between the shear
and the galaxy field, we make use of the Takahashi simulation suite
(

\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0.279

7
Discussion

In this paper, we have investigated the possibility of measuring fourth-order galaxy-shear correlations in the light of current stage IV surveys.
Fourth-order statistics contain information on non-Gaussian features in the matter and galaxy distribution and will allow us to probe the relation between galaxies and the underlying mass distribution in more detail than with only second- and third-order statistics.

Throughout this work, we focused on the correlation of the tangential shear measured around triplets of lens galaxies.
We introduced G4L statistics, naturally extending the theoretical formalism of GGL and G3L. We defined the corresponding four-point correlation function (4PCF), as well as the trispectrum, and derived the relations between both quantities.
As a convenient statistical measure for the G4L signal, we chose to convert the 4PCF to aperture statistics, which also provide a local representation of the trispectrum.
This conversion is achieved by performing the five-dimensional integration over the 4PCF multiplied by a filter function whose analytical expression was derived.

In Sect.
4
, we focused on the development of a numerical integration routine in that carries out the five-dimensional integration based on a Riemann sum.
Assuming Gaussian shear and galaxy fields, we tested the implementation of the numerical integration and took advantage of the fact that we can express the expected results in terms of second-order statistics.
In this process, we mitigated the effect of the finite radial integration range on the accuracy of the numerical integration, and emulated the bin-averaging of the 4PCF as it would be done by a practical estimator.
In the case of equal aperture scales, we find that the results are recovered within a one per cent-level of accuracy when we use a logarithmic radial bin size of
\Delta_{\mathrm{ln}}\approx 0.11
, which is a feasible bin size for practical estimators of the 4PCF.
The deviations of the integration results from the expectation is almost constant over the range of the considered aperture scales,
\theta\in[2^{\prime},30^{\prime}]
.
The small bias induced by the numerical integration is well below the noise level that we expect for the fourth-order aperture statistics in the data of stage IV surveys such that it does not add a further substantial error to the measurement of the aperture statistics.

Furthermore, we extended the direct estimator for aperture mass moments of
26
to estimate galaxy-mass aperture statistics.
Originally, this estimator is based on sums over discrete individual galaxies.
In Sect.
5.3
, we developed the pixelized counterpart of this estimator.
By making use of FFTs, we could efficiently perform the convolutions involved in this process.
The results for the second-order aperture statistics obtained with the FFT-method generally agree well with the results from the
orpheus
package as well as the integrated 2PCFs.
However, for small aperture scales, the pixelization of the data induces a systematic, negative bias of a few per cent on the
\mathcal{N}
-moments, whereas this effect does not have a significant impact on the
M_{\rm ap}
-moments.
Generally, a smaller pixel size reduces this bias, but also leads to higher computational costs.

By applying the FFT-method to an ensemble of mock observations constructed from the
42
simulation suite, we measured the
\langle\mathcal{N}^{3}M_{\rm ap}\rangle
statistics as the associated ensemble average over 1632 individual realizations, that cover a full sky when combined, and estimated the corresponding S/N, where we rescaled the noise level to a sky area of
2000\penalty\ \deg^{2}
.
The simulations mimic a typical stage IV survey setup with a mean galaxy number density of
3\penalty\ \mathrm{arcmin}^{-2}
per redshift bin.
The measurement of
\langle\mathcal{N}^{3}M_{\rm ap}\rangle(\theta)
shows that the connected part, which encapsulates non-Gaussian information in the matter and galaxy distribution, dominates the entire signal up to aperture scales of roughly
6^{\prime}
, and is measured with a
\mathrm{S/N}\approx 9
on small aperture scales, steadily declining from
\sim 8^{\prime}
towards larger scales.
When we discriminate between aperture scales for sources and lenses, we find that the S/N of the
\langle\mathcal{N}^{3}M_{\rm ap}\rangle(\theta_{\mathrm{N}},\theta_{\mathrm{M}})
statistics is mostly governed by the lens aperture scale,
\theta_{\mathrm{N}}
, such that we obtain a high S/N even for large values of the source galaxy aperture scale,
\theta_{\mathrm{M}}
.
These results indicate that we can expect to detect fourth-order aperture statistics with high significance in stage IV survey data.

Similar analyses could also be performed for the remaining galaxy-shear 4PCFs and their corresponding aperture statistics.
Regarding
\langle\mathcal{N}^{2}M_{\rm ap}^{2}\rangle
and
\langle\mathcal{N}M_{\rm ap}^{3}\rangle
, we expect the detection significance to be generally
smaller than for
\langle\mathcal{N}^{3}M_{\rm ap}\rangle
, since there are multiple shear components involved in their respective 4PCFs that are subject to shape noise.
The corresponding measurements can also be performed with the FFT-based estimator that can be applied to any arbitrary mixed aperture moments.
We have shown that this estimator performs well on simulated data.
The application to real, pixelized data will most likely induce systematic biases on any measurement due to masks on the survey field that are not present in the simulated data used here.
The impact of these biases would need to be investigated by comparing the measurements of the FFT-method to the results obtained via the direct integration of the corresponding correlation function and
orpheus
, which will suffer in a similar way due to masks.

The fact that fourth-order galaxy-shear statistics will be detectable in stage IV survey data shows that they can constitute an extension to analyses on third-order statistics in order to probe the non-Gaussian features in the Universe’s matter and galaxy distribution.
Whether the consideration of fourth-order galaxy-shear statistics will add new information and may break degeneracies when added to the combined analyses of second- and third-order statistics, has to be shown in future work.
If that is the case, the high-precision observations of stage IV surveys make it possible to investigate galaxy biasing and constrain models of galaxy evolution in more detail by taking fourth-order statistics into account.
Even if completely degenerate with second- and third-order statistics, fourth-order statistics could still be used to study nuisance parameters of such models.

Given a survey consisting of
N_{\rm{sources}}
source galaxies and
N_{\rm{lens}}
lenses, and a binning scheme of radial and angular bins, the traditional estimator of the bin-averaged G4L correlation function assigns all source-lens-lens-lens quadruplets to their corresponding quadrilateral configuration bin and then averages over those:

\displaystyle\tilde{G}

{}_{\mathrm{ggg}\gamma}(\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2},\vartheta_{3},\phi_{12},\phi_{13})\equiv\frac{\tilde{G}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{ggg}\gamma}(\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2},\vartheta_{3},\phi_{12},\phi_{13})}{N(\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2},\vartheta_{3},\phi_{12},\phi_{13})}\ ,

(64)

where the
\vartheta_{i}
and the
\phi_{ij}
denote the radial and angular bins.
To achieve a worst-case quadratic time complexity of the estimator we use its representation in the multipole basis, where the angular arguments of the 4PCF are expanded in complex exponentials. Adopting the notation of
28

\tilde{G}_{\mathrm{ggg}\gamma,\mathbf{n}}
, where
\mathbf{n}\equiv\left(n_{2},n_{3}\right)

\displaystyle\tilde{G}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{ggg}\gamma}\left(\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2},\vartheta_{3},\phi_{12},\phi_{13}\right)\equiv\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{n_{2},n_{3}=-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{G}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{ggg}\gamma,\mathbf{n}}(\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2},\vartheta_{3})\ \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n_{2}\phi_{12}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n_{3}\phi_{13}}\ ,

(65)

(66)

\displaystyle W^{\mathrm{disc}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta_{i}},\vartheta)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{lens}}}w_{\mathrm{lens}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{ik}}\ \mathcal{B}(\theta_{ik}\in\vartheta)\ ,

Appendix D
Estimator for aperture statistics

D.1
Direct estimator

Starting from the estimator in Eq. (
32
) for the mixed statistics
\widehat{\mathcal{N}^{n}M_{\rm ap}^{m}}
with arbitrary order
n
in
\mathcal{N}
and
m
in
M_{\rm ap}
, we first note that this expression factorizes into the
\mathcal{N}^{n}
–part and the
M_{\rm ap}^{m}
–part, because we sum over different galaxy populations, in general.
In the following, we focus on equal aperture scales in each
\mathcal{N}
and
M_{\rm ap}
but distinguish between
\theta_{\mathrm{N}}
and
\theta_{\mathrm{M}}
.

We can decompose the sums over all galaxies inside an aperture of area
A_{\mathrm{ap}}(\theta_{\mathrm{N}})
, as given in Eq. (
32
), into sums that are linear in the number of galaxies. In the case of
n=2
, we can decompose the sum according to

\widehat{\mathcal{N}^{2}}(\theta_{\mathrm{N}})=A_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2}(\theta_{\mathrm{N}})\frac{\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}}U_{\theta_{\mathrm{N}};i_{1}}U_{\theta_{\mathrm{N}};i_{2}}}{\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}}1}=A_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2}(\theta_{\mathrm{N}})\frac{\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}}U_{\theta_{\mathrm{N}};i_{1}}U_{\theta_{\mathrm{N}};i_{2}}-\sum_{i}U_{\theta_{\mathrm{N}};i}^{2}}{n_{\mathrm{L}}(n_{\mathrm{L}}-1)}=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s},1}^{2}-\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s},2}}{1-1/n_{\mathrm{L}}}\;,

(68)

where we defined the quantity

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s},k}(\theta_{\mathrm{N}})=\frac{1}{\bar{N}_{\mathrm{L}}^{k}}\sum_{i}U_{\theta_{\mathrm{N}};i}^{k}\;,

(69)

with the lens galaxy number density
\bar{N}_{\mathrm{L}}=n_{\mathrm{L}}/A_{\mathrm{ap}}(\theta_{\mathrm{N}})
, and
n_{\mathrm{L}}
the number of lens galaxies inside the aperture.
All further higher-order
\widehat{\mathcal{N}^{n}}
statistics can be expressed in terms of the
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s},k}
with
k=1,\dots,n
.
The general
m
-th order aperture mass can be computed recursively using the complete Bell polynomials
B_{n}
as was shown in
26
. Similarly, the
n
-th order moment
\mathcal{N}^{n}
is estimated by

\widehat{\mathcal{N}^{n}}=\frac{B_{n}\left(-\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s},1},\dots,-(n-1)!\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s},n}\right)}{\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(1-k/n_{\mathrm{L}})}\;.

(70)

A crude approximation of
\widehat{\mathcal{N}^{n}}
is to set
\widehat{\mathcal{N}^{n}}\approx\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s},1}^{n}(\theta_{\mathrm{N}})
which neglects the subsequent correction terms including
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s},k}(\theta_{\mathrm{N}})
with
k>1
.
While for large aperture scales this approximation can yield sufficiently precise results, it is only a poor estimate on small scales where the impact of the correction terms becomes more significant.
Using the global number density instead of the local one inside the respective aperture as the normalization in Eq. (
69
), changes results for
\widehat{\mathcal{N}^{2}}
already by a few per-cent, because we fail to account for fluctuations in the galaxy counts – an effect most prominent for small apertures.
This is similar to what was described for the third-order aperture mass
\langle M_{\rm ap}^{3}\rangle
in
13
.
In general, we find a better fit with other methods, as shown in Sect.
5.4
, using the local number density.

For the
\widehat{M_{\rm ap}^{m}}
statistics, we adopt the notation from
26
and write

M_{\mathrm{s},k}(\theta_{\mathrm{M}})=A_{\mathrm{ap}}^{k}(\theta_{\mathrm{M}})S_{k}\frac{\sum_{i}\left(w^{\mathrm{S}}_{i}Q_{\theta_{\mathrm{M}};i}\epsilon_{\mathrm{t},i}\right)^{k}}{\sum_{i}\left(w^{\mathrm{S}}_{i}\right)^{k}},\quad\text{with}\quad S_{k}=\frac{\sum_{i}\left(w^{\mathrm{S}}_{i}\right)^{k}}{\left(\sum_{i}w^{\mathrm{S}}_{i}\right)^{k}}\;,

(71)

such that we can calculate the aperture mass moments via

\widehat{M_{\rm ap}^{m}}=\frac{B_{m}\left(-M_{\mathrm{s},1},\dots,-(m-1)!M_{\mathrm{s},m}\right)}{B_{m}\left(-S_{1},\dots,-(m-1)!S_{m}\right)}\;.

(72)

D.2
Discretization of the direct estimator

In order to estimate aperture moments from discretized data, we need to convert the sums over the individual galaxies in the definitions of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s},k}
and
M_{\mathrm{s},k}
into sums over all pixels inside an aperture.
For the following calculations, we split up the sum over the galaxies according to

\sum_{\mathrm{gal}}1=\sum_{\mathrm{pix}}\sum_{\mathrm{gal\in pix}}1=\sum_{\mathrm{pix}}n_{\mathrm{pix}}\,,

(73)

where the sum
n_{\mathrm{pix}}=\sum_{\mathrm{gal\in pix}}
denotes the number of galaxies,
n_{\mathrm{pix}}
, in the corresponding pixel obtained from the sum over all galaxies within the pixel.
With these ingredients, we can transform the definition for
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s},k}
to the pixel level by writing

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s},k}(\theta_{\mathrm{N}})=\frac{1}{\bar{N}_{\mathrm{L}}^{k}}\sum_{\mathrm{gal}}U_{\theta_{\mathrm{N}};\mathrm{gal}}^{k}\approx\frac{1}{\bar{N}_{\mathrm{L}}^{k}}\sum_{\mathrm{pix}}U_{\theta_{\mathrm{N}};\mathrm{pix}}^{k}\sum_{\mathrm{gal\in pix}}1=\frac{1}{\bar{N}_{\mathrm{L}}^{k}}\sum_{\mathrm{pix}}U_{\theta_{\mathrm{N}};\mathrm{pix}}^{k}n_{\mathrm{L,pix}}\;,

(74)

where we denote the number of lens galaxies in a pixel by
n_{\mathrm{L,pix}}
.
The
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s},k}
can thus be simply computed by summing up the product of
n_{\mathrm{L,pix}}
with the corresponding
k
-th moment of the filter function
U_{\theta_{\mathrm{N}};\mathrm{pix}}
, evaluated at the pixel center, over all pixels in an aperture.

In order to convert the
M_{\mathrm{s},k}
to the pixel level, we first define the
k
-th order ellipticity weight for a pixel as

w^{\mathrm{S}}_{k,\mathrm{pix}}=\sum_{\mathrm{gal\in pix}}\left(w^{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{gal}}\right)^{k},

(75)

which then allows us to write

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{s},k}(\theta_{\mathrm{M}})

\displaystyle=A_{\mathrm{ap}}^{k}(\theta_{\mathrm{M}})S_{k}\frac{\sum_{\mathrm{gal}}\left(w^{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{gal}}Q_{\theta_{\mathrm{M}};\mathrm{gal}}\epsilon_{\mathrm{t},\mathrm{gal}}\right)^{k}}{\sum_{\mathrm{gal}}\left(w^{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{gal}}\right)^{k}}\approx A_{\mathrm{ap}}^{k}(\theta_{\mathrm{M}})S_{k}\frac{\sum_{\mathrm{pix}}Q_{\theta_{\mathrm{M}};\mathrm{pix}}^{k}\sum_{\mathrm{gal\in pix}}\left(w^{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{gal}}\epsilon_{\mathrm{t},\mathrm{gal}}\right)^{k}}{\sum_{\mathrm{pix}}w^{\mathrm{S}}_{k,\mathrm{pix}}}=A_{\mathrm{ap}}^{k}(\theta_{\mathrm{M}})S_{k}\frac{\sum_{\mathrm{pix}}w^{\mathrm{S}}_{k,\mathrm{pix}}Q_{\theta_{\mathrm{M}};\mathrm{pix}}^{k}\epsilon_{\mathrm{t,pix},k}}{\sum_{\mathrm{pix}}w^{\mathrm{S}}_{k,\mathrm{pix}}}\;,

(76)

where we further defined
\epsilon_{\mathrm{t,pix},k}
as the mean
k
-th ellipticity moment in a pixel given by

\epsilon_{\mathrm{t,pix},k}=\frac{\sum_{\mathrm{gal\in pix}}\left(w^{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{gal}}\epsilon_{\mathrm{t,gal}}\right)^{k}}{w^{\mathrm{S}}_{k,\mathrm{pix}}}\;.

(77)

The summation over all pixels inside an aperture can be performed efficiently by convolving the discretized shear or galaxy number density field with the corresponding filter function, which is also discretized on the same pixel grid as the data.
