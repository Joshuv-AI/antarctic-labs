Association between resilience and nurses’ professional autonomy across care contexts in the post-pandemic period: a nationwide cross-sectional survey in Japan
Authors: Sambuu Ganchuluun, Akiko Kondo, Khulan Gaalan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic has placed substantial demands on nurses and may have affected their professional autonomy. However, how resilience is associated with nurses’ professional autonomy across different care contexts in the post-pandemic period remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the association between resilience and nurses’ professional autonomy when caring for patients with COVID-19 and patients without COVID-19, and to compare professional autonomy across these care contexts.DesignWe conducted a nationwide cross-sectional online survey from June to September 2024.SettingsNurses were recruited from 18 special-functioning and regional medical care support hospitals across Japan; the hospitals were randomly selected for invitation.ParticipantsA total of 483 nurses participated in the study.MethodsNurses assessed their professional autonomy in both care contexts using the validated 28-item Nurses’ Professional Autonomy Scale. Resilience was measured using the 10-item Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale. Paired t-tests were used to compare professional autonomy scores, and hierarchical linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the association between resilience and professional autonomy. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Institute of Education, Institute of Science Tokyo (formerly Tokyo Medical and Dental University) (Approval No:C2024-013).ResultsProfessional autonomy scores were significantly lower when caring for patients with COVID-19 than when caring for patients without COVID-19 (Cohen’s d = 0.33, p < 0.001). All five factors were also significantly lower in the COVID-19 care context (p < 0.001). Resilience was positively associated with professional autonomy in both care contexts. This association remained significant when caring for patients with COVID-19 even after adjusting for professional autonomy when caring for patients without COVID-19. Resilience was positively associated with all five factors of professional autonomy in both non-COVID-19 and COVID-19 care contexts (all p < 0.001). Resilience remained significantly associated with three factors of professional autonomy when caring for patients with COVID-19, Performance (β = 0.074, p = 0.037), Abstract judgment (β = 0.148, p < 0.001), and Independent judgment (β = 0.144, p < 0.001), but not with Cognition or Concrete judgment.ConclusionsResilience was associated with nurses’ professional autonomy across care contexts. These findings suggest that resilience may be particularly relevant to higher-order and self-directed aspects of professional autonomy in clinical practice. Supporting resilience may help maintain professional autonomy under both routine and high-demand conditions.

Background

Rapidly evolving modern healthcare systems require health professionals to continuously update their knowledge, skills, and competencies while maintaining occupational health and safety standards. Nurses are expected to demonstrate a high level of professional autonomy, which is essential for ensuring the quality of nursing care and safeguarding patient safety (Rouhi-Balasi et al., 2020; Supametaporn 2013; Varjus et al. 2011). Although the nursing profession has expanded globally, the worldwide nursing shortage is projected to exceed 36 million by 2030 (World Health Organization 2020). This shortage is further worsened by heavy workloads, burnout, and high turnover, which have intensified during the COVID-19 pandemic (Dragioti et al. 2022; Labrague and de Los Santos 2020)

The COVID-19 pandemic placed unprecedented pressure on nurses, leading to increased workloads, emotional strain, and psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, stress, fatigue, and burnout (Baskin and Bartlett 2021; Jeamjitvibool et al. 2022; Jose et al. 2020; Labrague and de Los Santos 2020; Nituica et al. 2021). These mental health challenges have been associated with lower professionalism, reduced quality of care, and higher turnover intentions (Kim and Kim 2021). Given the urgent need to retain the nursing workforce, both financial and nonfinancial strategies, such as supportive work environments, strong professional relationships, and professional autonomy, must be implemented to reduce nurses’ intention to leave (Hara et al. 2020; Mrayyan 2004; Yarbrough et al. 2017).

Nurses’ professional autonomy is generally defined as the authority to make decisions and the freedom to act based on professional knowledge (Skår 2010). In this study, nurses’ professional autonomy is defined based on the revised Nurses’ Professional Autonomy Scale (Ganchuluun et al. 2023), which was originally developed by Kikuchi and Harada (1997).

Kikuchi and Harada (1997) described professional autonomy as developing through the nursing process, which includes assessment, implementation, and evaluation. It is a demonstration of professional competence through independent and self-directed judgment in nursing practice. Nurses’ judgment ability can be categorized into three types: Concrete judgment based on clinical cues, Abstract judgment based on nursing models or hypotheses, and Independent judgment, which refers to the ability to select appropriate nursing methods without relying on others (Kikuchi and Harada 1997).

Higher levels of professional autonomy are associated with lower psychological distress, job stress, and intention to leave, as well as higher job satisfaction, work engagement, and patient satisfaction (Asl et al. 2022; Choi and De Gagne 2016; Hara et al. 2020; Mrayyan et al. 2024; Van Der Goot et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021). Furthermore, greater professional autonomy has been linked to reduced patient mortality (Rao et al. 2017), highlighting its importance for both staff well-being and patient outcomes.

Multiple factors are associated with nurses’ professional autonomy, which can be broadly categorized into individual and environmental factors. At the individual level, characteristics such as educational level, clinical experience, job title, and critical thinking ability are positively associated with professional autonomy (Amini et al. 2015; Choi and De Gagne 2016; Motamed-Jahromi et al. 2015). At the environmental level, workplace factors play an important role. Supportive leadership and collaborative relationships among healthcare professionals facilitate nurses’ autonomy, whereas hierarchical or non-supportive management structures may limit independent decision-making (Hara et al. 2020; Mrayyan 2004; Pursio et al. 2021). Furthermore, healthy work environments characterized by effective teamwork and balanced workloads are associated with higher levels of professional autonomy (Mrayyan 2004).

Resilience is defined by the American Psychological Association (2018) as the ability to adapt effectively to adversity while maintaining optimism for the future. It is considered a developable skill that enables nurses to cope with workplace challenges (Kester and Wei 2018). Resilience encompasses resistance, recovery, and the restoration of physical and mental well-being following adversity (Szanton and Gill 2010). Multiple factors, such as coping strategies, supportive work environments, leadership, and effective communication, contribute to resilience (Sihvola et al. 2023). Resilience is associated with better sleep quality, improved coping skills, and enhanced psychological well-being, as well as reduced burnout and post-traumatic stress disorder (Al Hadid et al. 2022; Georges et al. 2022; Jiménez-Fernández et al. 2022; Jose et al. 2020; Labrague 2021). Resilient nurses also report higher job satisfaction (Hudgins 2016; Larrabee et al. 2010), deliver higher-quality care (Labrague and de Los Santos 2021), and experience lower levels of pandemic-related fatigue, anxiety, depression, and work stress (Jeamjitvibool et al. 2022; Labrague 2021), as well as reduced turnover intentions (Jo et al. 2021; Ying et al. 2021). Furthermore, resilience is a predictor of autonomy and psychological well-being among young adults (Klainin-Yobas et al. 2021) and has been linked to professional autonomy among physicians (Fazekas et al. 2024). Although a positive association between resilience and autonomy has been reported among advanced-practice nurses (Klein et al. 2021), the relationship between resilience and professional autonomy among registered nurses remains unclear.

In Japan, nurses had significantly lower professional autonomy when caring for patients with COVID-19 than when caring for other patients (without COVID-19) during the pandemic (Ganchuluun et al. 2023). By mid-2023, COVID-19 had transitioned to a post-acute phase with emergency measures lifted, infection rates declining, and care integrated into routine hospital services (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare 2023). This evolving context provides an opportunity to examine whether differences in nurses’ professional autonomy across care settings persist in the post‑pandemic period. However, whether the differences in nurses’ professional autonomy between caring for patients with COVID-19 and patients without COVID-19 care settings persist in the post-pandemic context remains unclear. Additionally, research on how resilience is associated with nurses’ professional autonomy across these care settings is lacking. Addressing this gap is important for understanding how to support nurses’ professional autonomy in future health crises and post-crisis environments.

This study applies the job demands–resources theory to explain the relationship between resilience and nurses’ professional autonomy (Demerouti 2025). Within this framework, COVID-19 patient care is conceptualized as a high job demand that may deplete cognitive and emotional resources, leading to reduced functioning and lower professional autonomy. According to the job demands–resources model, personal resources can buffer the negative effects of job demands and support performance. In this study, resilience is considered a personal resource that helps maintain functioning under stress, and professional autonomy is conceptualized as an outcome related to job performance. Accordingly, resilience may help maintain professional autonomy by mitigating the adverse effects of job demands. This effect may be particularly relevant for aspects of professional autonomy that involve self-directed decision-making and adaptive responses under high job demands.

This study aimed to examine 1) the difference in professional autonomy when caring for patients with COVID-19 and when caring for patients without COVID-19 (non-COVID-19), and 2) the association between resilience and nurses’ professional autonomy when caring for patients with COVID-19 and when caring for patients without COVID-19. Hypotheses were developed for the second aim.

Hypothesis 1Resilience is positively associated with nurses’ professional autonomy when caring for patients without COVID-19 care settings.

Resilience is positively associated with nurses’ professional autonomy when caring for patients without COVID-19 care settings.

Hypothesis 2Resilience is positively associated with nurses’ professional autonomy when caring for patients with COVID-19.

Resilience is positively associated with nurses’ professional autonomy when caring for patients with COVID-19.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study used a nationwide, anonymous online survey. This study included nurses employed at special-functioning hospitals and regional medical care support hospitals in Japan. Special-functioning hospitals are designated by the Minister of Health, Labor and Welfare and play a key role in providing advanced medical care, developing innovative medical technologies, and conducting specialized medical training. Regional medical care support hospitals are approved by prefectural governors, and are responsible for supporting regional healthcare systems, including coordinating patient referrals, sharing medical resources, providing emergency care, and training healthcare professionals (Minister of Health, Labor and Welfare 2020). These hospitals receive government financial subsidies and have been actively involved in COVID-19 response efforts since 2020.

Hospitals were randomly selected for invitations, and nurses working in the selected hospitals were recruited for voluntary participation. The inclusion criteria were nurses who had been employed at their hospital for at least one year, had experience caring for both patients with and without COVID-19, and provided informed consent. Nurses working in administrative roles, such as in the director’s office, were excluded.

Data collection

A comprehensive list of special-functioning hospitals (n = 88) and regional medical care support hospitals (n = 700) in Japan was compiled, yielding a total of 788 hospitals. Each hospital was assigned a unique identification number, and 120 hospitals were randomly selected for invitation using Microsoft Excel. This number was determined based on previous nationwide surveys in Japan, which reported that approximately 10% of invited hospitals typically agree to participate (Ogata 2014).

The necessary sample size was calculated using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7). The primary outcome for this calculation was the difference in total professional autonomy scores between caring for patients with COVID-19 and caring for patients without COVID-19. To detect a small effect size (d = 0.2) (Faul et al. 2009; Ganchuluun et al. 2023) with a two-tailed α of 0.05 and a power of 0.95 using a paired t-test, a minimum of 327 participants was required.

To ensure sufficient sample size, we estimated that at least 5400 nurses needed to be invited, assuming a 10% individual response rate and approximately 20% missing or incomplete data. If approximately 10% of the selected hospitals (≈12 hospitals) agreed to participate and each hospital employed an average of 450 nurses, inviting 120 hospitals was considered appropriate to achieve the required sample size.

Before distributing the study materials, the first author contacted the nursing directors of the 120 selected hospitals by telephone to obtain verbal permission to mail the research announcement poster and survey instructions. Study materials were mailed only to hospitals that granted permission. Participation was voluntary, and 18 hospitals agreed to participate in the study, with approval confirmed by nursing directors via email. Across these hospitals, 12,783 nurses (excluding first-year nurses) were eligible to participate in the survey.

In each participating hospital, nursing directors instructed unit head nurses to display the research announcement posters on unit bulletin boards. The posters included information on the study purpose, voluntary participation, and informed consent, along with a QR code linking to the anonymous online questionnaire. Nurses completed the survey at their convenience using a workplace computer or personal device. Data were collected from June to September 2024

Variables and instruments

The survey consisted of four components: individual factors (13 items), environmental factors (5 items), resilience (10 items), and professional autonomy questions (28 items). Individual and environmental variables were selected based on previous studies examining their association with resilience and professional autonomy.

Individual factors included gender, age (Amini et al. 2015; Motamed-Jahromi et al. 2015) marital status, living arrangements, department/section, years of nursing experience (Amini et al. 2015; Asl et al. 2022; Kuwano et al. 2016; Motamed-Jahromi et al. 2015) job title (Teramoto 2019), educational background (Motamed-Jahromi et al. 2015), clinical ladder level (Teramoto 2019), possession of a certified nurse specialist qualification (master’s level), possession of certified nurse qualification (six months of training), number of patients with COVID-19 experienced (Ganchuluun et al. 2023), and the approximate date of the most recent COVID-19 care experience.

Environmental factors included annual income (Guo et al. 2017), work shifts (daily working hours) (Yu et al. 2019), whether the nurse received support when facing difficulties at work, sources of support (Jo et al. 2021; Russo et al. 2018; Tanaka et al. 2020), and participation in training or study sessions within the past year.

Resilience was assessed using the Japanese version of the 10-item Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (Connor and Davidson 2003). The original scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.89) and test–retest reliability (r = 0.87). The Japanese version also showed high reliability and validity (Ito et al. 2009), with internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) of 0.90 and test–retest reliability of 0.83. Permission to use the Japanese version was obtained from the original developer. Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not true at all) to 4 (true nearly all the time). Total scores range from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater resilience.

Nurses’ professional autonomy was assessed using the revised 28-item Nurses’ Professional Autonomy Scale (Ganchuluun et al. 2023). Each participant rated their professional autonomy twice using the same scale: once for caring for patients without COVID-19 and once for when caring for patients with COVID-19.

The scale consists of five factors: Cognition (nine items; Factor 1), Performance (six items, Factor 2), Concrete judgment (four items; Factor 3), Abstract judgment (six items; Factor 4), and Basic nursing judgment (two items; Factor 5). Overall, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the revised scale was 0.941, indicating high internal consistency (Ganchuluun et al. 2023). However, internal consistency for the Basic nursing judgment factor was lower (α = 0.750) than that of the other factors (α > 0.8), likely due to the small number of items. To address this limitation, the original scale by Kikuchi and Harada (1997) was reviewed. This review indicated that the Basic nursing judgment factor did not adequately capture the independent selection of nursing methods, which is a key aspect of clinical judgment. To improve conceptual coverage, five mid-level nurses and a supervising professor evaluated the items and agreed to add one new item: “I can choose my nursing method without advice from others.” This item was added to better reflect the dimension of clinical judgment.

Following this revision, the factor structure was aligned with the original conceptual framework, and Factor 5 name was changed from Basic nursing judgment to Independent judgment. The final scale consisted of 28 items across five factors (nine, six, four, six, and three items, respectively). Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 28 to 140. Higher scores indicate greater professional autonomy.

Face validity was assessed through pilot testing with five mid-level nurses who had experience caring for patients with COVID-19. Overall Cronbach’s alpha was 0.95, with all factors above 0.8 except for the Independent judgment factor (α = 0.751). Although this factor showed limited improvement, values above 0.7 are considered acceptable (Cortina 1993).

Test–retest reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient. The five nurses completed the questionnaire twice in a one-week interval. The total score yielded an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.87, indicating good reliability.

A confirmatory factor analysis was conducted using data from 483 participants. Model fit was assessed using the goodness-of-fit index, comparative fit index, Tucker–Lewis index, and root mean square error of approximation. Values of goodness-of-fit, comparative fit index, and Tucker–Lewis index of ≥0.90 were considered indicative of good fit (Collier 2020). Although these indices did not meet the conventional threshold (goodness-of-fit = 0.817, comparative fit index = 0.887, Tucker–Lewis index = 0.873), they were close to acceptable levels, and the root mean square error of approximation value (0.077) indicated a reasonable fit. The best-fitting model was obtained by adding several error covariances based on the modification indices. However, when interpreting factor-level findings, it is important to acknowledge that the factor structure of the scale is not entirely optimal (Supplementary Figure S1).

Statistical analysis

Participants’ characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. The dependent variables were the nurses’ professional autonomy total scores for when caring for patients with COVID-19 and when caring for patients without COVID-19.

The normality of the dependent variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (p > 0.05), the skewness and kurtosis values within ±2, and Q-Q plots. Although the Shapiro–Wilk test indicated non-normality for dependent variables (p < 0.05), the skewness and kurtosis values for nurses’ professional autonomy for both contexts were within acceptable limits (±2), and Q–Q plots showed no substantial deviation from the diagonal line.

Therefore, paired t-tests were used to compare professional autonomy scores between the two care contexts of caring for patients with COVID-19 and patient without COVID-19 and to calculate the effect size (Cohen’s d), allowing comparison with previous study findings (Ganchuluun et al. 2023). Analyses were conducted at the item, factor score, and total score levels within the same participants. To address the issue of multiple testing across the 28 item-level paired t-tests, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure with q = 0.05 (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995; Glickman et al. 2014). Both raw p-values and false discovery rate–adjusted p-values are reported for all item-level comparisons.

Factors associated with nurses’ professional autonomy of both contexts were first examined using bivariate analyses. Independent variables that were significantly associated with either outcome (p < 0.05) were included in the hierarchical linear regression analyses for both contexts.

For categorical variables with three or more groups (e.g., marital status, job title, educational background, clinical ladder level, time since last COVID‑19 care, shift type, annual income, and social support), one‑way analysis of variance was used. For binary variables (e.g., gender, certified nurse specialist/certified nurse qualification, living alone, working in a critical care unit, participation in clinical ladder programs, educational level [two groups], and receiving support at work), independent t-tests were applied.

Spearman’s correlation coefficients (ρ) were used for associations between autonomy scores and non‑normally distributed continuous variables (years of nursing experience, number of COVID‑19 patients experienced, number of training sessions). Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r) were used for the analysis with normally distributed continuous variables (age and resilience).

Two hierarchical linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations between resilience and nurses’ professional autonomy, one with the total professional autonomy scores when caring for patients without COVID-19 and another with scores when caring for patients with COVID‑19. In Step 1, variables that were significant in the bivariate analyses except resilience were entered. In Step 2, resilience was added to evaluate the additional variance explained (Δ Adjusted R²). In the model predicting nurses’ professional autonomy for caring patients with COVID‑19, the nurses’ professional autonomy score for patients without COVID-19 was additionally entered in Step 3 to control for assumed baseline professional autonomy.

Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor, with values below 10 considered acceptable (Karadimitriou and Marshall 2021). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS and AMOS version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Institute of Education, Institute of Science Tokyo (formerly Tokyo Medical and Dental University) (Approval No: C2024-013). Informed consent was obtained electronically at the beginning of the online questionnaire, and only participants who agreed were included in the study. Participants were informed that participation was voluntary, responses would remain anonymous, and their involvement would not affect work evaluations. As a token of appreciation, the first 300 participants who completed the survey and provided their email addresses received a 500-yen (3.2 USD) Amazon gift card.

At the item level, raw and false discovery rate–adjusted p-values are presented in Table 2. With false discovery rate adjustment, 21 of the 28 items were significant. For most of the significant items, scores were lower when caring for patients with COVID-19 than when caring for patients without COVID-19. By contrast, the item “I am able to provide nursing care calmly in an emergency” showed significantly higher scores for when caring for patients with COVID-19 than when caring for patients without COVID-19 (false discovery rate–adjusted p = 0.007); it was the only item with a negative effect size (Cohen’s d = −0.13), which belongs to Factor 2 (Performance).

The largest effect size was observed for Factor 1 (Cognition) (Cohen’s d = 0.32), whereas the smallest was for Factor 5 (Independent judgment) (Cohen’s d = 0.15). At the item level, four items showed medium effect sizes (Cohen’s d ≥ 0.30), sixteen showed small effects (d = 0.10–0.29), and seven showed minimal differences (d < 0.10).

When caring for patients without COVID-19, the highest mean score was observed for Factor 1 (Cognition), whereas the lowest was for Factor 4 (Abstract judgment). The highest-scoring items were “I can show empathic understanding of the patient’s words and actions” and “I can cooperate with other professionals” (both mean = 4.1). The lowest-scoring item was “I can use the latest scientific evidence, including nursing articles, to decide on appropriate nursing” (mean = 3.2).

When caring for patients with COVID-19, Factors 1 (Cognition), 2 (Performance), and 3 (Concrete judgment) had the highest mean scores (all means = 3.7), and Factor 4 (Abstract judgment) showed the lowest score (mean = 3.5). The highest-scoring item was “I can cooperate with other professionals” (mean = 4.0), and the lowest was “I can use the latest scientific evidence” (mean = 3.2), consistent with findings from non-COVID-19 care setting.

Bivariate analysis of associations between participant characteristics and nurses’ professional autonomy

Factors associated with nurses’ professional autonomy when caring for patients without COVID-19

Nurses’ professional autonomy when caring for patients without COVID-19 was significantly associated with years of nursing experience (p < 0.001), number of patients with COVID-19 experienced (p = 0.013), possession of a certified nurse specialist or certified nurse qualification (p < 0.001), marital status (p = 0.003), clinical ladder level (p < 0.001), educational background (p = 0.001), number of training or study sessions attended (p < 0.001), job title (p < 0.001), annual income (p < 0.001), and resilience (p < 0.001).

Factors associated with nurses’ professional autonomy when caring for patients with COVID-19

Similar patterns were observed when caring for patients with COVID-19. Professional autonomy was significantly associated with years of nursing experience (p = 0.011), number of patients with COVID-19 experienced (p = 0.002), and possession of a certified nurse specialist or certified nurse qualification (p = 0.002). Additional significant factors included clinical ladder level (p = 0.016), educational background (p = 0.020), marital status (p = 0.005), job title (p = 0.004), annual income (p = 0.041), and resilience (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that nurses’ professional autonomy was significantly lower when caring for patients with COVID-19 than when caring for patients without COVID-19, even in the post-pandemic context. Resilience was positively associated with professional autonomy in both care contexts and remained significantly associated with professional autonomy when caring for patients with COVID-19 after adjusting for professional autonomy when caring for patients without COVID-19. These findings suggest that resilience is an important factor supporting nurses’ professional autonomy across different clinical contexts. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between resilience and nurses’ professional autonomy across care contexts in a post-pandemic environment.

Compared with previous findings (Ganchuluun et al. 2023), effect sizes (Cohen’s d) across all five professional autonomy factors were smaller, indicating that the differences in professional autonomy between COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 care contexts were less pronounced in the present study. This pattern may reflect increased familiarity with care for patients with COVID-19 and greater integration of such care into routine clinical practice. Among the factors, Cognition showed the largest difference and Independent judgment the smallest, which is consistent with previous findings.

Despite advances in standardized COVID-19 treatments, predicting individual patient outcomes, including long-term effects and variability related to immune responses and emerging variants, remains challenging (Nalbandian et al. 2021). This ongoing uncertainty may continue to influence nurses’ clinical judgment.

Nurses working with patients with suspected COVID-19 experienced higher levels of anxiety and depression than those caring for patients with confirmed COVID-19 or without COVID-19, suggesting that uncertainty and insufficient protection may increase psychological burden (Doo et al. 2021). Such differences in uncertainty may influence nurses’ ability to engage in autonomous clinical decision-making under complex conditions.

Nonetheless, several items showed minimal differences between care contexts, suggesting relative stability of certain aspects of professional autonomy. Interprofessional collaboration remained consistently high, with the item “I can cooperate with other professionals” showing one of the highest mean scores in both contexts. In addition, the ability to provide nursing care calmly in emergency situations remained high in COVID-19 patient care. These findings may reflect adaptive professional functioning developed under prolonged high-demand clinical conditions, and personal resources such as resilience may have contributed to maintaining these aspects of professional autonomy. Similar findings have been reported among physicians during the COVID-19 pandemic: higher resilience was associated with maintaining perceived quality of care and adaptive professional functioning under stressful conditions (Fazekas et al. 2024).

By contrast, skills related to the use of scientific evidence consistently showed the lowest scores across both care contexts, indicating an area requiring further support and development. Resilience was significantly associated with Abstract judgment, which includes the application of theoretical knowledge and evidence-informed decision-making. This finding may suggest that resilience is particularly relevant for supporting higher-order clinical reasoning and autonomous decision-making in complex clinical situations.

Furthermore, resilience was positively associated with nurses’ professional autonomy when caring for patients without COVID-19, supporting Hypothesis 1. This association was observed across all professional autonomy factors. These findings are consistent with previous studies among advanced-practice nurses, physicians, and young adults (Fazekas et al. 2024; Klein et al. 2021).

For Hypothesis 2, resilience remained significantly associated with professional autonomy when caring for patients with COVID-19, even after adjusting for professional autonomy when caring for patients without COVID-19. This finding suggests that resilience may help sustain professional autonomy under high-demand conditions. However, this association differed across factors. Significant associations were observed for Performance, Abstract judgment, and Independent judgment but not for Cognition or Concrete judgment. This pattern may reflect differences in these competencies.

Cognition and Concrete judgment are closely related to knowledge-based understanding, prediction, and routine clinical skills, such as responding to changes in patients’ conditions, prioritizing clinical problems, and proposing care based on assessment (Kikuchi and Harada 1997). These competencies are often supported by standardized procedures and accumulated clinical experience and may therefore be less influenced by individual psychological resources, such as resilience.

By contrast, Performance, Abstract judgment, and Independent judgment rely more heavily on autonomy and self-directed action. These factors reflect the ability to think, decide, and act Independently (Kikuchi and Harada 1997). Performance involves proactive interpersonal behaviors and interprofessional coordination, and Abstract judgment includes higher-order cognitive processes, such as applying theoretical knowledge and making decisions under uncertainty. Independent judgment reflects the ability to make autonomous clinical decisions and select appropriate nursing methods without relying on others. These competencies require adaptive coping and self-directed decision-making in complex and uncertain clinical situations and thus may be more sensitive to individual differences in resilience. This interpretation is also consistent with models of clinical judgment emphasizing contextual reasoning, reflective thinking, and adaptive decision-making in complex clinical environments (Tanner 2006).

From the perspective of the job demands–resources model, COVID-19 care can be conceptualized as a high job demand characterized by uncertainty, workload, and complexity (Demerouti 2025; Demerouti et al. 2001). Resilience, as a personal resource, may buffer these demands by helping nurses maintain functioning and engagement under such conditions.

Personal resources are considered particularly important for tasks that require proactive behavior, self-regulation, and goal-directed action (Bakker and Demerouti 2017). The present findings are consistent with this framework, suggesting that resilience is especially relevant for higher-order and autonomous aspects of professional autonomy, rather than for routine or protocol-driven competencies.

Furthermore, these results suggest that resilience contributes to both coping with stress and sustaining autonomous clinical judgment. However, certain aspects of professional autonomy, particularly those involving complex cognitive processing, may remain vulnerable when job demands exceed available resources.

Limitations and future studies

This study has several limitations. First, cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine how resilience is associated with changes in nurses’ professional autonomy over time.

Second, the findings may not be generalizable across hospital settings, particularly to small or rural hospitals, as only 18 of the 120 invited institutions participated. Participating hospitals may have differed systematically from nonparticipating hospitals in terms of organizational culture, staffing patterns, leadership support, or workplace resources. In particular, hospitals with heavier workloads, limited staffing, or lower organizational support may have been less likely to participate, potentially introducing an institutional participation bias. In addition, nurses who chose to respond may have been more professionally motivated, potentially leading to an overrepresentation of individuals with higher resilience or professional autonomy.

Third, the use of self-reported questionnaires may have introduced subjectivity and response biases, including social desirability bias and self-perception bias. Participants may have overestimated their resilience or professional autonomy to provide socially acceptable responses or evaluate their professional performance more positively.

Fourth, potential recall bias should be considered. Some nurses may not have recently cared for patients without COVID-19 and may have relied on earlier experiences when responding. In addition, although the most recent timing of COVID-19 patient care was assessed, the study did not comprehensively evaluate the duration, frequency, cumulative exposure, or sequence of care experiences when caring for patients with and without COVID-19. As a result, variations in clinical exposure may have influenced responses regarding professional autonomy. Future longitudinal or time-sensitive studies are needed to clarify how differences in clinical exposure influence professional autonomy and resilience.

Finally, although the data were theoretically nested within hospitals or departments, multilevel analysis could not be conducted. Prior methodological studies suggest that approximately 20–30 clusters are required for stable multilevel estimates (Lorah 2018; Maas and Hox 2005), whereas only 18 hospitals were included in this study. In addition, hospital identifiers were removed to maintain anonymity, preventing the calculation of intraclass correlation coefficients. As a result, between-hospital variation could not be assessed, and clustering effects were not accounted for, which may have led to underestimated standard errors (Lorah 2018). Future studies should incorporate identifiable but ethically protected hospital-level data to enable multilevel modeling and better capture organizational influences.

Practical implications

This study suggests that resilience is an important factor associated with nurses’ professional autonomy across different care contexts, including high-demand situations, such as COVID-19 care. To support professional autonomy, nursing education at the undergraduate, clinical, and graduate levels may benefit from incorporating resilience-building components, such as stress-management training, reflective practice, and structured support systems.

The findings further indicate that the association between resilience and professional autonomy varies across factors, with significant associations observed for the factors of Performance, Abstract judgment, and Independent judgment but not for Cognition or Concrete judgment. This suggests that resilience may be particularly important for supporting higher-order, self-directed, and adaptive aspects of clinical practice rather than routine or protocol-based competencies.

Given that skills related to the use of scientific evidence showed consistently low scores across both care contexts, educational approaches that integrate resilience-building with evidence appraisal, evidence-informed clinical reasoning, and reflective decision-making may be particularly valuable for strengthening higher-order professional autonomy. Future studies should examine whether resilience-focused educational interventions enhance evidence-informed clinical reasoning and the ability to use scientific evidence in autonomous nursing practice.

Conclusions

Nurses’ professional autonomy was significantly lower when caring for patients with COVID-19 than when caring for patients without COVID-19. Resilience was positively associated with professional autonomy in both care contexts and remained significant even after adjusting for professional autonomy when caring for patients without COVID-19. This association differed across factors of professional autonomy. Resilience was significantly associated with Performance, Abstract judgment, and Independent judgment but not with Cognition or Concrete judgment. These findings suggest that resilience is particularly important for sustaining higher-order, self-directed, and adaptive aspects of professional autonomy in complex and uncertain clinical environments.

Overall, this study highlights resilience as a key factor supporting nurses’ professional autonomy across care contexts. Strengthening resilience through educational strategies may be a potentially useful approach for supporting nurses’ autonomous clinical practice, workforce stability, and quality of care in demanding healthcare systems.
