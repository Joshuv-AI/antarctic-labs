 ABSTRACT

 The development and deployment of molten salt reactor (MSR) technologies
 require experimental capabilities that can evaluate molten fuel salt behavior and
 structural material performance under representative irradiation conditions.
 Although modeling and separate effects testing provide important insight, there
 remains a critical lack of in-pile data that capture the coupled effects of neutron
 irradiation, temperature, salt chemistry, and time. Informed by lessons learned
 from historical MSR programs and recent international irradiation efforts, this
 report presents a structured approach to addressing existing gaps in molten salt
 irradiation testing using the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR).
 A phased irradiation strategy is presented that incrementally increases
 experimental complexity while managing cost, risk, and facility constraints. The
 framework progresses from passive, nonfueled static capsule experiments to
 fueled, instrumented, and ultimately circulating molten salt systems, providing a
 pathway for materials screening, mechanistic understanding, and qualification-
 relevant testing. The report defines a near-term Phase 1 passive capsule concept,
 associated irradiation conditions, and a conceptual post-irradiation examination
 strategy. Ongoing modeling, out-of-pile testing, and safety analysis activities are
 described to support continued capability maturation and to enable future phases
 of molten salt irradiation testing in support of MSR technology deployment.

1. INTRODUCTION
The development and deployment of molten salt reactor (MSR) technologies require experimental
capabilities that support both molten fuel salt behavior and structural material performance evaluation under
representative irradiation conditions. In light-water reactors (LWRs), materials development and fuel
performance are supported by decades of operating experience, mature qualification frameworks, and
extensive experimental infrastructure. Corrosion mechanisms, irradiation effects, and fuel performance in
LWR systems are relatively well understood and can be investigated using a combination of out-of-pile
testing, reactor surveillance data, and established in-pile experiments. In contrast, molten salt systems
present fundamentally different challenges that cannot be addressed using existing LWR-focused testing
protocols.
Historically, the most comprehensive molten salt irradiation data were generated during the development
of the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment (MSRE) at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). These efforts
were largely focused on a narrow set of materials and components (most notably, graphite and Hastelloy N)
in contact with fluoride fuel salts specifically formulated for the MSRE. Although these experiments
provided critical insights into molten salt operation and irradiation-assisted chemistry, the experimental
capabilities that supported the MSRE’s development are no longer available, and the resulting data are not
broadly applicable to current MSR designs. In particular, there are little to no combined effects data for
modern, code-qualified structural alloys such as 316H stainless steel exposed to molten salts under neutron
irradiation.
Current MSR developers are largely constrained to structural materials permitted under ASME Section III,
Division 5 for high-temperature nuclear component design in demonstration reactors [1]. These alloys were
developed and qualified for service in aqueous LWR environments, and their performance in MSR
environments remains largely untested and uncertain. At the same time, emerging MSR concepts now span
a wide range of molten fluoride and molten chloride salt chemistries, further expanding the need for
experimental data to evaluate material compatibility and irradiation-driven degradation mechanisms across
diverse operating conditions.
Although several recent in-pile molten salt experiments have been performed, these efforts are typically
limited in scope, conducted in lower-flux reactors, or constrained by short irradiation durations. At present,
there are no domestic irradiation capabilities capable of systematically supporting the generation of
irradiation data needed to evaluate molten fuel salt chemistry and structural material performance across
the range of salt formulations and candidate materials relevant to MSR development. This lack of
representative performance data presents a significant challenge for materials down selection, design
validation, and long-term qualification.
To address this gap, this report outlines a phased approach to molten fuel salt irradiation testing using the
High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) at ORNL. This effort is implemented through the Molten Salt Irradiation
Research Testbed (MSIRT), a staged and flexible experimental framework designed to progress from
simple, passive irradiation experiments toward increasingly integrated molten fuel salt tests under
representative conditions. MSIRT emphasizes risk reduction and cost effectiveness through systematic
capability development. This report describes the MSIRT framework, outlines the phased progression of
the testbed, and introduces the initial implementation, MSIRT-1, which establishes the baseline
experimental capability that subsequent fueled and instrumented phases will build upon.

2. BACKGROUND
 2.1 HISTORICAL IN-PILE MOLTEN SALT IRRADIATION
 EXPERIMENTS
Early understanding of irradiation–corrosion interactions in molten salt systems originates from in-pile
reactor experiments and integral test reactors from the MSRE and Aircraft Reactor Experiment (ARE)
Programs of the 1950s–1970s. These experiments remain the most representative demonstrations of
coupled radiation damage, fission product chemistry, salt evolution, thermal hydraulics, and material
response under reactor operating conditions.
2.1.1 Integral Reactor Demonstrations
The first large-scale demonstrations of molten salt fuel operation were performed in the ARE in 1954 and
later in the MSRE from 1965 to 1969 [2-4]. The ARE established the feasibility of operating a circulating
molten fluoride fuel, demonstrating stable reactor control and salt behavior over short operating periods.
The MSRE extended this concept to multiyear operation, validating the long-term compatibility of fuel salts
with graphite moderators and Hastelloy N structural components.
Although the MSRE successfully demonstrated fuel salt stability and materials compatibility at the system
level, it also revealed some degradation mechanisms not captured in earlier tests, such as tellurium-induced
intergranular embrittlement of Hastelloy N. This observation emphasizes the importance of fission-product-
driven chemistry and irradiation-assisted degradation mechanisms that only manifest under fully coupled,
in-pile conditions.
2.1.2 In-Pile Loop and Capsule Experiments Supporting the MSRE and
 ARE Programs
To support reactor development and material selection for the MSRE and ARE, a series of in-pile loop and
capsule experiments were conducted in test reactors. These irradiation campaigns, carried out primarily by
Oak Ridge National Laboratory in the Low Intensity Test Reactor (LITR), Materials Testing Reactor
(MTR), and Oak Ridge Research Reactor (ORR) between the mid-1950s and mid-1970s, were instrumental
in evaluating fuel salt behavior and materials compatibility prior to implementation in MSRE.
The LITR was used to conduct the earliest forced circulation molten salt loop irradiations under flowing
conditions [5]. These tests employed closed loop systems with externally driven pumps and heated salt
lines, with instrumented test sections positioned in the reactor beam ports and auxiliary components located
outside the core region. Both horizontal and vertical loop configurations were operated to evaluate corrosion
behavior and salt stability under irradiation and controlled flow. The experiments demonstrated that in-pile
circulation of fluoride fuel salts is feasible but also identified reliability challenges related to pump
performance, seal integrity, thermal control, and external penetrations.
Building on earlier LITR loop experience, forced circulation molten salt loops operated in the MTR
provided data on fuel salt behavior and alloy compatibility under irradiation [6, 7]. The loops used compact,
pump-integrated hairpin designs with a helical nose coil positioned in the high-flux region and the pump
located at the rear of the assembly. Fission gases were removed through the sump pump, and the loop was
enclosed within a water-cooled jacket for heat removal and containment. Several tests were terminated
early due to mechanical issues associated with pump seals, gas handling, and flow stability, but useful
corrosion and salt chemistry data were obtained.
Subsequently, molten salt convection loops operated in the ORR enabled longer-duration irradiation tests
with reduced mechanical complexity compared with forced circulation systems [8]. Two natural convection
loops were operated in beam hole HN-1 using graphite core sections and Hastelloy N piping to circulate
fuel salt by imposed thermal gradients rather than pumps. The loops were heavily instrumented with
thermocouples and included provisions for gas separation, salt sampling, and inventory adjustment during

operation. These experiments provided data on fuel salt chemistry evolution, graphite compatibility, and
Hastelloy N performance under irradiation but were ultimately limited by mechanical failures associated
with thermal stresses in piping and auxiliary components.
In parallel, static capsule experiments in the MTR (ORNL-MTR-47 series) investigated graphite–salt
interactions, radiolysis gas generation, and corrosion under nonflowing conditions. The earliest
experiments, MTR-47-1 and MTR-47-2, failed due to nonrigid bellowed capsule designs that were not
predicted by out-of-pile testing, highlighting the importance of accounting for irradiation-induced
mechanical and chemical effects in experiment design [9]. The first fully successful irradiation was
MTR-47-3, which demonstrated stability over a 1,000 h duration operation [10, 11]. Subsequent
experiments increased in complexity; MTR-47-4 introduced a dedicated plenum volume [12, 13],
MTR-47-5 enabled online sampling of plenum gases [14], and MTR-47-6 incorporated both active heating
and online plenum gas sampling [14]. Collectively, this progression established an experimental foundation
for understanding fuel salt ingress into graphite and demonstrated that radiolysis-driven gas generation is
most significant at temperatures below approximately 200°C, particularly during reactor shutdown and
cooldown conditions.
Following the discovery of tellurium-induced embrittlement in MSRE components, a dedicated series of
TeGen capsule irradiations was conducted in the ORR during the mid-1970s [15, 16]. The experiments
used sealed irradiation capsules containing tubular fuel pins partially filled with molten fuel salt, allowing
tellurium to be generated in situ through fission under controlled thermal and chemical conditions. Multiple
candidate structural alloys, including modified Hastelloy N compositions with niobium and titanium
additions, were tested within a single capsule to enable direct comparison under identical irradiation
conditions. Capsule designs incorporated internal thermocouples and electrical resistance heaters to control
specimen temperature near 700°C and to limit radiolysis effects during reactor shutdown. Post-irradiation
examination (PIE) focused on metallographic evaluation of intergranular cracking and chemical analysis
of both salt and alloy specimens. Although improved resistance to tellurium-induced cracking was observed
relative to baseline Hastelloy N, the termination of the broader MSR Program limited further refinement
and long-term validation of these materials.
Although these experiments provided invaluable data that directly supported MSRE and ARE development,
the LITR, MTR, and ORR facilities have since been permanently decommissioned, and the associated in-
pile molten salt irradiation capabilities no longer exist.
2.1.3 Modern In-Pile Molten Salt Irradiation Experiments
Interest in MSRs reemerged in the 2010s after a period of dormancy following the termination of the MSR
Program in 1976, leading to a new generation of in-pile experiments. In contrast to the MSRE era, which
focused primarily on fueled fluoride salts, these modern experiments encompass a broader range of salt
chemistries, including both fluoride- and chloride-based reactor salts. This shift reflects the expanded
design space of contemporary MSR concepts and the need to evaluate salt chemistry behavior and materials
compatibility across multiple reactor classes.
At the MIT Nuclear Research Reactor (MITR), sealed capsule irradiations containing 7Li-enriched FLiBe
were performed to assess materials compatibility, tritium behavior, and irradiation effects in fluoride salts
[17, 18]. The FS-1 and FS-2 experiments used static salt inventories enclosed within nickel capsules and
were operated at approximately 700°C. These tests marked the first domestic in-pile irradiation of molten
fluoride salt since the MSRE era and provided data on corrosion behavior, tritium partitioning, and salt
radiolysis under representative conditions. The experiments were limited by small salt inventories and
capsule scale, and one irradiation was terminated early due to loss of capsule integrity.
At the Ohio State University Research Reactor (OSURR), static chloride salt capsules were irradiated to
explore irradiation effects in NaCl–MgCl2 [19, 20]. The experiments, which used sealed molybdenum

capsules containing approximately 30 g of molten chloride salt and Alloy N or 316L stainless steel
specimens, were operated at 800°C for approximately 22 h at full reactor power. Irradiations were
performed in a low neutron flux dry tube position over several short operating cycles. These tests provided
the first US in-pile corrosion data for molten chloride salts and indicated that irradiation can influence
corrosion behavior, although the short irradiation duration limited quantitative assessment of underlying
mechanisms.
The SALIENT Program at the High Flux Reactor (HFR) in Petten was established to reintroduce molten
salt irradiation testing in Europe [21-24]. SALIENT-01 consisted of thorium-bearing fluoride salt samples
irradiated in open graphite crucibles housed within a sealed, inert gas environment. The experiment was
designed to investigate fission product behavior in molten fluoride salts and interactions between the salt
and nuclear-grade graphite under irradiation. The open crucible configuration resulted in direct contact
between the molten salt and graphite; the salt chemistry was influenced by graphite properties, salt
preparation history, and impurities introduced during melting, handling, and transport, limiting control of
specimen boundary conditions and redox state during irradiation. The SALIENT-03 design incorporates
welded Hastelloy N capsules containing fluoride fuel salt with controlled compositions, internal heaters to
maintain salt temperature during reactor shutdowns, and redox buffering through defined UF4 to UF3 ratios.
The experiment includes in-pile instrumentation such as thermocouples, pressure sensing for fission gas
release, and electrochemical probes to monitor salt redox conditions. SALIENT-03 is currently an ongoing
effort, with PIE planned to quantify corrosion behavior of candidate alloys and to characterize fission
product release under irradiation.
More recently, the Molten Salt Research Temperature-Controlled Irradiation Experiment (MRTI) was
conducted in the Neutron Radiography Reactor (NRAD) to irradiate an enriched uranium-bearing chloride
fuel salt [25, 26]. The experiment employed a sealed Inconel 625 capsule containing NaCl–UCl3 fuel salt
enriched to 93% 235U. Internal heaters, thermocouples, and a cover gas plenum were used to support
temperature control and monitoring during irradiation. Approximately 13 cm3 of fuel salt was irradiated for
a total of 390 h. Following irradiation, the capsule was transferred to a hot cell facility for nondestructive
PIE, including neutron radiography and precision gamma ray scanning, with additional PIE planned to
further evaluate salt behavior and material interactions.
2.1.4 Lessons Learned and Remaining Gaps
Collectively, historical and modern reactor experiments demonstrate that irradiation–corrosion synergy in
molten salts is highly sensitive to system design. Flowing loops provide realism but suffer from complexity
and failure risk, whereas static capsules offer robustness at the cost of hydrodynamic representativeness.
Radiolysis gas generation, redox evolution, and fission product transport have repeatedly driven
unanticipated degradation, emphasizing the need for active thermal control and gas management.
Despite decades of work, existing data remain fragmented across salt chemistries, materials, and irradiation
conditions. Few experiments were designed with modern PIE strategies in mind, and direct comparisons
between separate effects and fully coupled exposures are scarce. These gaps motivate new HFIR
experiments that emphasize controlled conditions, simplified but representative capsule designs, and
integrated PIE strategies to systematically interrogate irradiation–corrosion mechanisms in molten salt
environments.

2.2.1 Flux Trap Region
The flux trap region in HFIR provides the highest fast and thermal neutron fluxes available in the reactor,
achieving significant displacements per atom damage levels. It is therefore commonly used for accelerated
materials irradiation. This region includes the target rod rabbit holder (TRRH), hydraulic tube, and
peripheral target positions. Although these locations enable rapid damage accumulation, the associated
volumetric gamma and neutron heating is significant and typically avoided for fueled experiments. As a
result, applicability to molten salt irradiation is generally limited to small, static capsules containing
nonfueled salts, where heat generation, pressure, and safety basis constraints can be conservatively
managed.
The TRRH facilities support small rabbit capsules (up to 12.2 mm diameter) or full-length targets (up to
12.7 mm diameter) across 30 available positions, with two positions capable of supporting instrumentation.
These positions offer exceptional flux levels but are constrained by limited internal volume, restricted
instrumentation routing, and stringent safety basis requirements, which limit experiment complexity.
The hydraulic tube enables rapid insertion and extraction of rabbit experiments, making it well suited for
short-duration irradiations, transient exposure studies, or investigations of the onset of irradiation-assisted
corrosion. However, the single available position and lack of sustained instrumentation capability limit its
suitability for long-duration molten salt corrosion testing or studies of salt chemistry evolution.
Peripheral target positions provide the highest fast neutron flux condition in HFIR and can accommodate
either rabbit capsules or full-length targets. Six positions are available and are frequently used for materials
irradiation due to their favorable fast flux characteristics. Similar to other flux trap locations, these positions
are best suited for compact, passive experiments due to volume and instrumentation limitations.
2.2.2 Beryllium Reflector Region
The beryllium reflector region in HFIR is traditionally used for fissile material irradiation experiments as
well as instrumented materials testing that requires larger experiment volumes than are available in the flux
trap. This balance between neutron flux and available space makes the reflector region a primary location
for experiments that involve active heating, gas control, or active instrumentation.
Small removable beryllium (RB) positions are typically reserved for isotope production and are less
commonly used for materials testing. In contrast, large RB positions (approximately 43 mm diameter) are
among the most versatile locations for molten salt irradiation experiments. Eight positions are available and
can support instrumentation, heaters, gas lines, and multiple internal subcapsules. Although neutron flux
levels are lower than those in the flux trap, they remain sufficiently high to achieve relevant damage and
fuel burnup levels. Neutron flux gradients in the RB region evolve over an irradiation cycle primarily due
to the close proximity of the RB positions to the control elements. This behavior is driven by control element
withdrawal as reactor fuel burnup progresses and must be accounted for in experiment design and thermal
analysis.
From a molten salt perspective, RB positions offer favorable access to instrumentation and integration with
the materials irradiation facility (MIF) (to be discussed in the following section). However, these positions
impose stricter constraints on neutron poison inventory, requiring careful control of salt composition,
container materials, and any spectrum-tailoring features. Spectrum tailoring may be desirable, particularly
for chloride-based salts intended for fast spectrum reactor concepts, but it must be balanced against HFIR
operational requirements because increased neutron poison loading can adversely affect reactivity and
reduce achievable cycle length when designing experiments in RB locations.
The vertical experiment facilities (VXFs) provide additional irradiation options within the reflector region.
Small VXF positions (approximately 37 mm diameter; 16 total) offer less experimental volume than RB
positions and are commonly used for non-instrumented experiments and isotope production. Large VXF
positions (approximately 69 mm diameter; 6 total) provide greater experimental volume than RB facilities

February 2026 7

but operate at reduced thermal and fast neutron flux and damage rates compared with RB and flux trap
locations. As a result, VXF positions are better suited for experiments requiring larger volumes and for
which lower flux conditions or extended exposure times are acceptable, rather than for tests requiring high
damage or burnup rates. Historically, few complex instrumented experiments have been designed for VXF
locations.
In addition to standard reflector positions, HFIR includes two slant access facilities that extend from the
outer edge of the beryllium reflector through the reactor pool to an external experiment room. These
facilities consist of approximately 100 mm diameter stainless steel piping and offer the largest available
experimental volume. Although use of the slant access facilities would require significant investment,
specialized work controls, and extensive safety analysis, they represent a potential pathway for hosting
large, loop-type molten salt experiments in the future.
2.2.3 Materials Irradiation Facility
The MIF provides the infrastructure required to support fully instrumented actively controlled irradiation
experiments in HFIR [28]. For molten salt irradiation experiments, the MIF is particularly relevant for
configurations that require active temperature control, inert gas environments, and effluent gas handling.
The MIF enables temperature regulation through controlled mixtures of inert gases (e.g., helium and argon),
allowing adjustment of the effective thermal conductance within one or more experiment zones. Integrated
electrical heaters may also be incorporated to provide temperature feedback control and to maintain target
temperatures during reactor shutdowns or cooldown periods. This capability is particularly important for
minimizing uncontrolled salt chemistry changes or radiolysis-driven effects between irradiation cycles and
prior to experiment removal.
The MIF also provides a pathway for effluent gas monitoring, which is essential for molten salt irradiation
studies. Effluent monitoring supports the detection of radiolysis products and fission gas release in fueled
experiments. Integration with the HFIR closed hot off-gas system enables the safe handling of activated
gases while preserving the ability to correlate in-pile behavior with PIE. Effluent gas streams can be routed
to radiation detectors (e.g., high-purity germanium systems) for identification of activated or fission gas
species, and future integration of chemical analysis techniques (such as laser-induced breakdown
spectroscopy of the effluent [29]) may be considered.
In addition to temperature and gas control, the MIF can be configured to support advanced in-pile sensors,
such as pressure transducers or corrosion electrochemical probes, enabling online data collection and
enhanced experiment control. These capabilities enable future phases of molten salt irradiation testing that
require higher data resolution and tighter coupling between experiment operation and post-irradiation
analysis.
A key feature of the MIF is its modular and reconfigurable gas delivery and control architecture.
Historically, instrumented experiments required custom gas routing and control hardware for each
irradiation campaign, increasing cost and development time. The current MIF configuration employs
software-reconfigurable control modules and standardized gas routing systems, significantly reducing
experiment-specific redesign effort and enabling phased progression from simple to increasingly complex
instrumented capsules.

3. PHASED IRRADIATION APPROACH
The development and qualification of materials and salts for MSR applications require experimental data
that capture the coupled effects of irradiation, temperature, chemistry, and time. Given the complexity and
cost associated with in-pile molten salt experiments, a phased irradiation approach provides a structured
pathway for progressively building experimental capability while reducing technical and programmatic

8 February 2026

risk. This strategy enables both targeted separate effects testing and increasingly integrated experiments
with higher fidelity to reactor-relevant conditions. Ultimately, it provides a pathway for fuel salt
formulation development and materials qualification across a range of candidate MSR concepts.
 3.1 RATIONALE FOR A PHASED DEVELOPMENT STRATEGY
A phased approach is motivated by three primary considerations: risk reduction, learning-based
progression, and alignment with historical experience and modern reactor constraints. Historically, in-pile
molten salt experiments have demonstrated that early failures often arise from unanticipated interactions
between salt chemistry, materials behavior, and irradiation effects rather than from single, isolated
variables. Beginning with simpler experiments allows baseline behaviors to be established under well-
controlled conditions before additional complexity is introduced. Knowledge gained at each stage informs
subsequent experiment design, improving the likelihood of technical success and the interpretability of
results.
Separating individual effects (e.g., radiolysis-driven chemistry changes, redox evolution, or baseline alloy
corrosion) from fully coupled irradiation–corrosion behavior is essential for mechanistic understanding.
Passive experiments emphasize isolation and simplicity, and later instrumented experiments enable
observation of transient behavior and feedback mechanisms that cannot be captured through PIE alone.
This iterative relationship between experimentation, mechanistic insight, and materials development forms
the foundation of MSIRT being developed for implementation in HFIR (Figure 2).
This progression is also consistent with modern irradiation facility constraints. More complex experiments
require increased development time, higher fabrication cost, and greater safety basis rigor. Reserving these
resources for the most promising material systems and salt formulations identified through earlier testing
ensures efficient use of limited irradiation capacity while maximizing scientific return.

 3.2 IRRADIATION PHASES
MSIRT is structured around four broadly defined irradiation phases that progressively increase in
experiment complexity, cost, and data resolution. Collectively, these phases are intended to support fuel
salt formulation development, salt chemistry evolution under irradiation, and structural material
performance evaluation across a range of candidate MSR concepts (Figure 3).
MSIRT-1: Passive, nonfueled salt irradiation
The initial phase focuses on passive irradiation of nonfueled fluoride and chloride carrier salts in contact
with candidate structural materials. These experiments emphasize simplicity while establishing baseline
understanding of irradiation-induced salt chemistry changes, including radiolysis products, impurity

February 2026 9

evolution, and initial salt–alloy interactions under neutron irradiation at prototypic temperatures. Phase 1
provides foundational data for screening salt chemistries/structural materials and identifying dominant
irradiation-driven chemical processes while minimizing cost, fabrication complexity, and safety basis
burden.
MSIRT-2: Passive or minimally instrumented fueled salt irradiation
Phase 2 introduces fissile-bearing fuel salt compositions while maintaining largely passive or minimally
instrumented designs. These experiments extend baseline observations to fuel salt stability, fission product
generation and retention, redox control strategies, and coupled salt–material response under irradiation.
Limited instrumentation may be included to monitor thermal conditions, but the primary emphasis remains
on PIE to assess fuel salt chemistry evolution and material compatibility.
MSIRT-3: Instrumented fueled salt irradiation
Phase 3 experiments incorporate active instrumentation to enable higher-resolution, in situ observation of
experiment behavior. Capabilities such as temperature control, pressure monitoring, sweep gas systems,
and effluent gas analysis allow direct measurement of irradiation-driven salt chemistry evolution, gas
release behavior, and transient responses that cannot be captured through passive experiments alone. Due
to increased development effort and cost, these experiments are best applied to fuel salt formulations and
material systems that have demonstrated favorable performance in earlier phases.
MSIRT-4: Circulating or flowing fueled salt experiments
The final phase represents fully integrated experiments with flowing or circulating molten salt, capturing
coupled thermal hydraulic behavior, mass transport, fission product migration, and chemistry evolution
under irradiation. Although they offer the highest fidelity to MSR operating conditions, loop experiments
are inherently complex and resource intensive. Therefore, they are envisioned as longer-term capability
developments informed by data and experience gained from static capsule testing.
Altogether, this phased irradiation approach establishes a flexible experimental platform capable of
supporting both fuel salt development and structural material qualification. By spanning simple static
capsules to advanced instrumented and flowing systems, MSIRT enables cost-effective screening of
candidate fuel salt formulations, validation of salt chemistry control strategies, and targeted qualification
data of the most promising fuel salt–alloy systems. The objectives and expected data outcomes associated
with each phase are summarized in Table 2.

4. MSIRT-1 EXPERIMENT CONCEPT: PASSIVE CAPSULE DESIGN
MSIRT-1 represents the initial implementation of the phased irradiation approach and is intended to
establish a baseline capability for molten salt irradiation in HFIR. The emphasis is on simplicity, reliability,
and data quality, enabling early evaluation of effects from irradiation on salt chemistry and corrosion.
 4.1 OBJECTIVES
The primary objectives of the MSIRT-1 passive capsule experiments are as follows:
 o Quantify irradiation–corrosion behavior of candidate structural materials in contact with molten
 salts under representative temperature and neutron flux conditions.
 o Observe radiolysis phenomena, including gas generation, salt chemistry evolution, and associated
 impacts on material degradation.
 o Establish and validate HFIR-specific workflows for capsule fabrication, assembly, and post-
 irradiation handling for PIE.
These objectives are intended to generate foundational data that inform salt–alloy compatibility while
simultaneously maturing the experimental infrastructure required for fueled and instrumented testing.
 4.2 CONCEPTUAL CAPSULE DESIGN OVERVIEW
The MSIRT-1 experiment is based on a static, sealed capsule configuration designed for irradiation in the
TRRH facilities in HFIR. The design emphasizes simplicity, robustness, and compatibility with the
dimensional, thermal, and safety constraints associated with high-flux, small-volume irradiation positions.
A conceptual representation of the capsule architecture is shown in Figure 4, and the corresponding

assembly sequence is shown in Figure 5. Key design conditions and materials of construction for the
MSIRT-1 capsule are summarized in Table 3.

 Figure 4. Passive static sealed capsule architecture for MSIRT-1.

The capsule design builds directly on proven static liquid metal irradiation capsules previously deployed in
HFIR for molten tin and molten lead corrosion experiments [30, 31]. These prior experiments established
a reliable framework for containing molten media under intense neutron flux, passively achieving target
temperatures through gamma heating and thermal conduction, and recovering specimens for PIE.
Leveraging this established HFIR design heritage reduces technical risk and provides confidence that the
MSIRT-1 capsule can be safely and reliably irradiated in the TRRH positions.
The capsule consists of an aluminum alloy (Al 6061) outer housing, which provides the primary structural
boundary to the HFIR coolant. Within the housing, a molybdenum subholder serves as the internal
containment structure for the molten salt and corrosion specimens. Molybdenum is selected for its high
melting temperature, low solubility in both fluoride and chloride salts, and weldability. Its use essentially
eliminates redox-driven reactions with the salt, thereby preserving salt chemistry and providing a
chemically stable internal environment suitable for molten salt materials exposure studies.
The internal capsule volume includes a reserved plenum region within the molybdenum subholder to
accommodate thermal expansion of the molten salt and gas generation from radiolysis. The salt inventory
directly immerses the corrosion specimens under irradiation. The dedicated plenum volume provides

12                                                                                     February 2026

passive pressure management without reliance on active venting or sweep gas systems, reducing complexity
and limiting potential failure modes during irradiation.
Corrosion specimens are miniature tensile specimens [32] intentionally selected to enhance the sensitivity
to corrosion and salt chemistry evolution in static systems, allowing measurable degradation and salt–
material interaction to occur within one to two HFIR irradiation cycles despite the limited salt inventory.
Lower surface area to volume ratios (surface area of the specimen to volume of the salt) will reduce the
total salt mass required to achieve representative exposure conditions [33]. Two corrosion specimens are
incorporated per capsule to provide redundancy during PIE. In addition, the tensile specimen geometry
enables downstream mechanical testing during PIE, supporting the assessment of irradiation- and corrosion-
assisted changes in material properties.
Temperature measurement is achieved using SiC passive thermometry specimens [34], which are
positioned in line with the corrosion specimens to ensure that temperature data correspond directly to the
material exposure environment. A key design modification relative to prior molten metal capsules is the
placement of the thermometry within an isolated central well, preventing direct contact between the
thermometry and the molten salt. This isolation mitigates impurity introduction into the salt, prevents
corrosion or chemical interaction with the thermometry specimens, and preserves the integrity of post-
irradiation SiC temperature analysis. Two SiC thermometry specimens are included for redundancy.
A SiC leaf spring is incorporated between the thermometry specimens to ensure consistent mechanical
contact with the well walls, improving thermal coupling and reducing uncertainty in temperature
reconstruction during PIE. Titanium centering thimbles are used to maintain concentric alignment of the
molybdenum holder within the aluminum housing, ensuring consistent gas gap geometry and predictable
thermal behavior during irradiation.
Thermal control in MSIRT-1 is achieved entirely through passive means, consistent with TRRH constraints.
Target experiment temperatures are established by controlling the fill gas composition (He–Ar mixtures)
within the capsule, which regulates effective thermal conductance across the gas gap between the holder
and housing. In addition, the outer diameter of the molybdenum holder is varied to further tune thermal
resistance and refine the operating temperature window. Quartz insulators are positioned at the bottom of
the capsule to thermally isolate the active experiment region from the capsule base and to maintain stable
positioning of the thermometry within the well throughout irradiation.
Current design analyses target an operating temperature range of approximately 600°C–700°C, which is
representative of typical MSR operating conditions and suitable for both chloride and fluoride salt systems.
Safety analyses are planned to bound the use of candidate corrosion specimen materials, including austenitic
and ferritic stainless steels, nickel-based alloys, and graphite.
The primary focus of MSIRT-1 is nonfueled chloride-based salt systems, including NaCl–MgCl2 and NaCl–
CeCl3, selected to isolate irradiation and radiolysis effects without the added complexity of fission product
generation or additional safety basis constraints associated with fueled salts in the TRRH irradiation
facilities. This emphasis reflects practical considerations related to beryllium handling and tritium
generation associated with fluoride salts. Although 7Li enrichment can mitigate tritium production, it
introduces additional procurement complexity, cost, and lead time. Chloride salts therefore provide a lower-
barrier pathway for early capability demonstration. Pending schedule, cost, and resource availability,
selected fluoride salts (e.g., FLiBe, FLiNaK, NaF–KF–MFx) may be evaluated.

5. PIE STRATEGY
PIE provides the primary data used to interpret the effects of irradiation on corrosion and the evolution of
salt chemistry. For MSIRT-1, PIE considerations are incorporated early in the experiment design. This
approach ensures that capsule disassembly, sample recovery, and analysis can be performed efficiently and
at reasonable cost. Deliberate planning reduces the risk of avoidable handling errors, unnecessary
complexity, or loss of critical information.
 5.1 PIE OBJECTIVES
The primary objectives of the PIE activities are as follows:
 o Characterize radiolysis- and irradiation-generated gases, including identification of dominant
 species and qualitative assessment of gas production.
 o Determine irradiation-induced salt chemistry changes, including impurity accumulation and
 compositional modifications resulting from thermal and neutron exposure.
 o Quantify corrosion behavior and microstructural degradation of structural materials exposed to
 molten salts under irradiation.
 o Assess changes in mechanical properties providing insight into irradiation effects on material
 performance.
Together, these objectives enable correlation between in-pile exposure conditions and post-irradiation
material response while establishing baseline PIE methodologies that can be extended to more advanced
fueled and instrumented MSIRT experiments.
 5.2 PIE WORKFLOW
The PIE workflow for MSIRT-1 is structured to support safe handling and traceable recovery of gases,
salts, and material specimens through a staged sequence of operations, consistent with the workflow
illustrated in Figure 6. All post-irradiation measurements are explicitly benchmarked against pre-irradiation
characterization data to enable quantitative assessment of irradiation- and environment-induced changes in
salt chemistry, gas composition, and material response.

 Figure 6. Conceptual PIE workflow for the MSIRT-1 experiment.

After the capsules are irradiated, they are transferred to a hot cell facility for initial inspection and handling.
The outer aluminum capsule housing is removed to expose the molybdenum holder assembly, which is then
placed in an inert environment to limit oxidation or unintended reactions during subsequent operations. The
plenum is punctured in a controlled enclosure, and released gases are captured for analysis. Ideally,
engineering controls are developed to allow the puncturing process to occur without exposing the salt
sample to the ambient hot cell gas space. Depending on activity levels and facility capabilities, gas analysis
may be conducted directly in the hot cell using available instrumentation (e.g., mass spectrometry), or gas
samples may be collected in secondary containment for analysis outside the hot cell. As an alternative
pathway, plenum gases may be routed through a scrubber solution, with quantities of reactive radiolysis
products inferred from post-analysis of the solution. Measured gas inventories and compositions are
compared directly to pre-irradiation capsule inventories and baseline impurity levels.

February 2026                                                                                   15

Following gas recovery, the salt inventory is recovered by melting out the salt, preferably under an inert
atmosphere. Salt samples are collected and packaged for out-of-hot-cell chemical analysis, with attention
to contamination control. Recovered salt samples are subjected to chemical analysis to assess changes in
composition and impurity content following irradiation and thermal exposure. Compositional analysis may
include techniques such as inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, inductively coupled plasma
optical emission spectrometry, or laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy, as appropriate, to quantify bulk
salt chemistry, corrosion products, and radiolysis-related species if possible. Results are evaluated relative
to pre-irradiation salt characterization to isolate irradiation-driven chemical evolution.
After salt removal, the holder is further disassembled to retrieve corrosion and thermometry specimens.
Corrosion specimens are solution-cleaned to remove residual salt, and post-irradiation mass measurements
are recorded to support quantitative mass loss assessment. Where specimen activity permits, mechanical
testing may be performed either in the hot cell or after transfer to out-of-cell facilities. Recovered specimens
are then prepared for detailed materials characterization, which may include scanning electron
microscopy/energy dispersive spectroscopy, electron backscatter diffraction, and focused cross-sectional
analysis. Advanced techniques such as transmission electron microscopy or atom probe tomography may
be applied as appropriate. All post-irradiation measurements are directly compared to pre-irradiation
reference characterization to quantify irradiation-induced microstructural and property changes.

6. FUTURE WORK
Ongoing and planned activities are focused on supporting the MSIRT-1 passive capsule experiment while
establishing the analytical, fabrication, and safety analysis documentation required for subsequent phases
of molten salt irradiation testing.
Finite element analysis thermal modeling and neutronics analyses are underway to refine expected
temperature distributions, gamma heating, and neutron exposure conditions within the capsule. These
models will be used to support design verification, define acceptable operating envelopes, and inform
interpretation of PIE results.
In parallel, out-of-pile static corrosion testing will be conducted to establish baseline salt–alloy interactions
under controlled thermal conditions. These experiments provide reference data for comparison with in-pile
results and support experiment design evaluation prior to in-pile deployment.
Development of welded containment approaches is ongoing to enable reliable sealing of capsules under
dry, inert atmospheres. This effort is focused on minimizing contamination, ensuring weld integrity, and
maintaining compatibility with HFIR handling and safety requirements.
Design work is also planned on PIE disassembly fixtures to support controlled capsule sectioning, gas
capture, and sample recovery in hot cell environments. These fixtures are intended to improve repeatability,
reduce handling risk, and streamline PIE workflows.
Finally, safety analyses are being conducted to support capsule qualification for HFIR insertion. These
analyses address internal pressure, gas generation, thermal performance, material compatibility, and failure
modes, providing the technical basis required for safe irradiation of molten salt experiments in HFIR.

7. CONCLUSIONS
This report outlines a structured, phased irradiation approach for developing and qualifying materials and
fuel salts for MSR applications using HFIR. The phased framework is intentionally designed to balance

16                                                                                     February 2026

technical ambition with risk reduction, enabling progressive capability development from simple passive
experiments to more integrated and representative testing configurations.
MSIRT-1 represents the initial implementation of this strategy and focuses on passive, static molten salt
irradiation to establish baseline understanding of irradiation impact on corrosion, radiolysis effects, and
salt–material interactions. Conceptual capsule designs, handling approaches, and PIE workflows have been
defined to emphasize robustness, safety, and data quality. Collectively, these elements provide confidence
that Phase 1 experiments can be successfully executed in HFIR within existing facility constraints.
Supporting activities are actively underway to further mature the MSIRT-1 design. Thermal and neutronics
modeling efforts are being used to refine expected irradiation conditions and operating envelopes, and out-
of-pile corrosion testing provides essential baseline data for interpreting in-pile results. In parallel, design
work is progressing on containment welding, capsule assembly, and PIE disassembly fixtures to ensure
reliable fabrication and sample recovery.
A clear plan has been established for safety analysis and PIE execution, ensuring that irradiation, handling,
and examination activities are aligned with HFIR requirements and available hot cell capabilities. These
efforts not only support MSIRT-1 readiness but also lay the groundwork for subsequent phases involving
fueled and instrumented experiments.
Taken together, this phased approach provides a defensible and adaptable pathway for advancing molten
salt irradiation testing in HFIR. The program is well positioned to generate high-confidence data that
support material and fuel salt development for MSR technologies.
