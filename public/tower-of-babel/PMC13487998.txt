Balancing radiopacity and artifact generation: a quantitative evaluation of CBCT artifacts induced by resin-based restorative materials
Authors: İkbal Esra Pehlivan, Sema Kaya, Alaettin Koç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile metal-induced artifacts in cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) are well documented, quantitative data regarding the artifact intensity and spatial distribution of modern injectable composites compared with conventional resin-based materials are sparse. This in vitro study quantitatively evaluated and compared beam-hardening and blooming artifacts generated by various resin-based restorative materials, specifically investigating whether modern injectable composites produce different artifact profiles than conventional resins in CBCT imaging.MethodsNinety 3D-printed mandibular canine models with standardized distal cavities were restored using six different materials (n = 15 per group): G-ænial Anterior (GA), EQUIA Forte HT Fil (EQF), G-ænial Universal Injectable (GUI), Vittra APS Unique (VAU), Charisma Diamond ONE (CHR), and OMNICHROMA (OMN). All samples were scanned using a CS 9600 CBCT unit under identical exposure parameters (120 kV, 4.0 mA, 0.15 mm voxel size) to eliminate motion artifacts and ensure standardization. Grayscale values (GV) were measured on mesial, distal, buccal, and lingual surfaces using standardized regions of interest (ROIs) positioned adjacent to the restoration. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis tests with post-hoc corrections (α = 0.05).ResultsSignificant differences in GV were observed on the mesial (p < 0.001), distal (p < 0.001), and buccal (p = 0.004) surfaces, whereas no significant difference was found on the lingual surface (p = 0.053). The GUI and GA groups showed the highest GV, while CHR exhibited the lowest GV across all surfaces. The highest GV were recorded on the mesial surface, not on the distal surface where the restoration was placed (p < 0.001).ConclusionsDifferent resin-based restorative materials produced different grayscale values and artifact patterns under standardized CBCT conditions. The high-filler injectable composite showed artifact characteristics comparable to those of high-viscosity resin-based materials, particularly on the surface opposite to the restoration. These findings suggest that material-related factors may influence CBCT artifact expression; however, because no direct chemical or elemental analysis was performed, these interpretations should be made cautiously. In clinical situations where CBCT imaging is anticipated, clinicians should consider the potential effect of restorative material radiopacity on image interpretation while balancing the diagnostic need for radiopaque restorations in conventional radiography.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08878-3.Keywords: Cone-beam computed tomography, Dental restorations, Dental materials, Artifacts, Radiopacity, In vitro study

Background

Radiographic imaging is a fundamental component of dental diagnosis; consequently, established protocols are available to assist clinicians in selecting the most suitable imaging modalities for patients presenting with potential dental or maxillofacial pathologies [1]. Historically, the adoption of advanced imaging modalities in dentistry was constrained by high costs, limited accessibility, and radiation concerns. However, the advent of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) has overcome these barriers, enabling clinicians to utilize multiplanar imaging for the maxillofacial region [2]. CBCT is a contemporary imaging modality that utilizes a conical X-ray beam to facilitate the three-dimensional reconstruction of the craniofacial complex. A distinct advantage of this technique over conventional medical computed tomography (CT) is the significantly lower radiation dose exposure for the patient [3, 4]. Due to its high spatial resolution, CBCT is acknowledged as a precise and reliable diagnostic tool in dentistry [5, 6]. Currently, it is routinely utilized in a wide array of clinical scenarios, ranging from preoperative implant planning and the assessment of complex root canal morphology to the localization of impacted teeth and the delineation of pathological lesions [7–9]. Nevertheless, despite these extensive diagnostic capabilities, a primary disadvantage of the system is the generation of artifacts within reconstructed images, which originate from various intrinsic and extrinsic factors [10, 11].

In radiographic imaging, an ‘artifact’ is defined as any distortion or discrepancy in the reconstructed image that does not correspond to the actual anatomy of the scanned object [8]. Despite the misconception among some dental professionals that CBCT volumes are less prone to artifacts than conventional medical CT, technical factors inherent to the system specifically the cone-beam geometry and lower energy spectra inevitably introduce distinct image distortions. These artifacts typically manifest as streaks or dark bands, compromising the diagnostic integrity of the reconstructed volume [11]. Etiologically, these image distortions are classified into four primary categories: patient-related, acquisition-based, scanner-related, and cone-beam-specific artifacts [12]. In addition to these groups, inherent physical factors such as scatter radiation and image noise are also well-documented contributors to artifact formation [13].

Among this broad etiological spectrum, the beam hardening phenomenon is particularly significant in restorative dentistry. The intensity of this artifact is directly dependent on the density of the material and the atomic number of the elements used in its components [11–15]. Due to this physical principle, the majority of the literature has focused on image distortions caused by high-atomic-number metals (e.g., amalgam) and titanium implants [16, 17]. However, modern resin-based composites and glass ionomer cements are not radiolucent; they often contain radiopaque inorganic fillers such as barium, strontium, or zirconia to distinguish them from carious tissue. Research has demonstrated a linear correlation between the radiopacity of these composite materials and the intensity of artifacts they generate [18]. Consequently, depending on the ratio of these high-atomic-number fillers, it should not be overlooked that these esthetic materials can generate beam-hardening artifacts similar to metals, potentially compromising diagnostic image quality [19, 20].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to quantitatively compare the intensity and spatial distribution of beam-hardening artifacts generated by different categories of resin-based restorative materials and to determine whether modern high-filler injectable composites produce artifact patterns comparable to conventional composites under standardized CBCT conditions. A standardized in vitro experimental design was strictly employed to eliminate confounding factors inherent to clinical imaging, such as motion artifacts and anatomical variations. By maintaining identical exposure parameters across all samples, this study sought to isolate the specific impact of restorative material type on image quality, thereby guiding clinicians in selecting materials that minimize radiographic distortion and diagnostic errors.

Materials and methods

This in vitro study was approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Van Yüzüncü Yıl University, Van, Türkiye (Approval No: 2025/02–12), and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. An a priori power analysis was conducted to evaluate the adequacy of the sample size. Reference data were taken from Sancho-Puchades et al., who reported ΔGV0.5 mm (buccal) values for different implant materials [14]. Although the referenced study evaluated implant-related artifacts, it was selected because it used a comparable CBCT-based quantitative grayscale measurement approach for artifact assessment, and studies providing material-specific effect sizes for resin-based restorative materials using a similar methodology are limited. The reported mean ± SD values were: Ti4.1 = 45.9 ± 5.8, Ti3.3 = 47.7 ± 9.2, TiZr3.3 = 86.6 ± 26.2, ZrO₂ = 171.0 ± 33.2. The calculated effect size was Cohen’s f = 1.53, corresponding to a very large effect. At a significance level of α = 0.05 and 95% power, power analysis showed that only 4 samples per group would be sufficient. Although the power analysis indicated that four samples per group would be sufficient, 15 samples were included per group to improve measurement reliability and strengthen methodological robustness by reducing variability associated with specimen positioning, restoration placement, and ROI-based grayscale analysis.

CBCT scans were obtained using a CS 9600 imaging system (Carestream Dental). The scans were performed with the following parameters: 120 kV tube voltage, 4.0 mA tube current, 15.0 s exposure time, and a FOV of Ø8 × 5 cm. The voxel size was set to 150 μm, and the scan mode was standard resolution (STD). A flat filter and a 0.7 mm copper (Cu) filter were applied. The detector type was SCINTILLATOR, with detector ID NATK015 and model name 3D BOSTON. The slice thickness was 0.15 mm.

All CBCT scans were performed using a single dry human mandible, in which different restorative materials were sequentially inserted into the same standardized tooth socket. Each 3D-printed mandibular canine was individually positioned and scanned under identical geometric and exposure conditions to ensure methodological consistency. This setup allowed all 90 samples to be imaged with a single mandible, thereby eliminating intermandibular variability and enhancing reproducibility. The mandible was stabilized in a seated position to simulate clinical posture. The image area dose product was calculated as 496.22 mGy·cm². Image acquisition was performed using CS Imaging Software (version 8.0.2.8), and the datasets were exported in DICOM format using AcqNagoya without any additional preprocessing, filtering, contrast adjustment, or grayscale modification. The exported DICOM datasets were then imported into RadiAnt DICOM Viewer for standardized grayscale measurements.

To ensure that the orientation of the teeth was consistent with their natural intraoral positioning, they were placed in a dry human mandible. To simulate the surrounding soft tissue, the mandible was coated with a layer of wax before imaging. A total of 90 artificial mandibular canine phantom teeth with standardized distal cavities were used in the study. Since all phantom teeth were specifically fabricated for this study under standardized conditions, no specimens were excluded.

All 90 teeth used in the study were restored. No unrestored control group was included. Each sample had a standardized cavity (5 mm diameter, 7 mm depth) on the distal surface, prepared using 3D printing. The samples were then restored using six different restorative materials (n = 15 per material group): G-ænial Anterior (GA), EQUIA Forte HT Fil (EQF), G-ænial Universal Injectable (GUI), Vittra APS Unique (VAU), Charisma Diamond ONE (CHR), and OMNICHROMA (OMN). The overall workflow of sample preparation, restoration, specimen positioning within the mandible, and CBCT-based ROI analysis is illustrated in Fig. 1.

The surface treatments of the specimens were performed in accordance with the type of restorative material. For the resin composite groups, the cavity surfaces were etched with 37% phosphoric acid gel (3 M Scotchbond; 3 M ESPE) for 30 s, rinsed with water spray for 30 s, and gently air-dried. G-Premio Bond (GC) was then applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions and light-cured for 10 s using an Elipar FreeLight 2 device (3 M ESPE). For the glass hybrid restorative group, the material was placed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The restorative materials used in the study and their compositions are summarized in Table 1. After restoration placement, all specimens were finished and polished using orange, yellow, and white polishing discs (Sof-Lex; 3 M ESPE) at 7000 rpm, followed by rubber burs for final surface finishing. All CBCT images were obtained using the same CS 9600 system and identical exposure parameters as described above. The imaging protocol was applied under identical conditions across all samples to ensure standardized image acquisition.

In this study, high grayscale values (GV) measured in the ROIs adjacent to the restoration were interpreted as blooming artifacts and scatter radiation caused by the high-density material, while dark streaks were interpreted as beam-hardening artifacts. Each composite restoration was evaluated in sagittal sections and divided into three equal thirds along the long axis of the tooth: coronal, middle, and apical. These levels were defined as the distance between the coronal margin of the restoration and the apical limit of the cavity.

To eliminate subjectivity and ensure reproducibility of measurements, ROI placement followed a rigid geometric protocol. At each of the three predefined vertical levels, elliptical ROIs (0.6 cm²) were precisely centered on axial slices corresponding to the four anatomical surfaces of the tooth: mesial, distal, buccal, and lingual (Fig. 2). The ROI size of 0.6 cm² was determined from preliminary measurements to provide a sufficiently large, reproducible sampling area adjacent to the restoration while avoiding direct contact with the restorative material, cavity margins, and surrounding non-target structures. To prevent measurement bias due to direct beam hardening at the material interface, a standardized 1 mm safety margin was maintained between the restoration border and the proximal limit of the ROI. This spatial standardization was consistently applied across all 90 specimens by the same calibrated examiner.

Representative axial CBCT sections showing standardized ROI placement adjacent to the restoration. Elliptical ROIs were positioned on the mesial, distal, buccal, and lingual surfaces while maintaining a standardized safety margin from the restorative material

For each surface, the mean grayscale value was calculated by averaging the pixel values obtained from the three levels (coronal, middle, and apical), providing a single representative grayscale value per surface for statistical analysis. In addition to quantitative evaluation, a qualitative assessment of artifact morphology (e.g., radial streaks, shadowing, and distortion patterns) was performed by visual inspection of axial sections. All analyses were conducted using RadiAnt DICOM Viewer software (version 2025.1, 64-bit).

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 23.0). Numerical data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, while the homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. To compare the mean pixel values produced by different restorative materials (independent groups), One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was applied for normally distributed data (distal and lingual surfaces), followed by the Tukey HSD post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. For data that did not follow a normal distribution (mesial and buccal surfaces), the Kruskal-Wallis test was used, and significant differences were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction. For comparisons of different surfaces within the same material group (dependent groups), Repeated Measures ANOVA was used for parametric data, whereas the Friedman test was applied for non-parametric data. Intra-observer reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Intraobserver reliability analysis showed an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.923. Visual inspection of the axial CBCT sections revealed varying artifact patterns among the groups. The GA and GUI groups showed hyperdense streaks (blooming) and scatter effects extending towards the mesial aspect. Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to quantitatively compare the magnitude of artifacts induced by different resin-based restorative materials in CBCT imaging. The findings revealed that although all tested materials generated artifacts to varying degrees, the GA (941.31 ± 3.51 a.u. on mesial) and GUI (938.98 ± 4.90 a.u. on mesial) composite groups produced statistically significantly higher grayscale values, indicating more intense artifact formation, particularly on the mesial surface. EQF (940.78 ± 3.47 a.u. on the mesial) showed values comparable to those of GA and GUI. It is important to note that these high GV recordings in the adjacent ROIs may reflect blooming artifacts and scatter, which may be associated with differences in radiopaque filler composition and filler loading rather than the material’s bulk density alone. In contrast, the CHR composite group exhibited the lowest pixel intensities across all surfaces, with the distal surface showing the lowest value (907.76 ± 7.86 a.u.), indicating it is the material associated with the least artifact generation. These results corroborate the hypothesis that the specific chemical composition and physical characteristics of resin-based restoratives are critical determinants of radiographic image quality. Unlike medical CT, CBCT systems do not provide standardized Hounsfield Unit calibration; therefore, grayscale values are expressed in arbitrary units and may be influenced by cone-beam geometry, scatter radiation, beam hardening, and device-specific acquisition parameters.

The variations in artifact intensity observed among the materials may be partly related to differences in radiopacity, inorganic filler loading, and radiopaque filler composition. The generation of CBCT artifacts is closely associated with the radiodensity of the scanned object. Kuusisto et al. [18] demonstrated a linear relationship between artifact formation and material radiopacity by evaluating composite resins with varying concentrations of radiopaque BaAlSiO₂ fillers. They reported that artifacts became distinct when the radiopaque filler content reached 20%, with artifact intensity increasing progressively beyond this threshold. This relationship may help explain the relatively high grayscale values observed in the GA and GUI groups in the present study. However, the behavior of the GUI group should also be interpreted in relation to the specific injectable composite evaluated. In this study, the injectable composite group consisted of G-ænial Universal Injectable, a light-cured high-filler injectable resin composite containing ultra-fine barium glass particles and silica, with a manufacturer-reported filler content of 69 wt%. Hadadi et al. [21] reported that flowable composites generally exhibited lower grayscale values than amalgam or conventional composites. However, injectable and flowable composites do not represent a chemically uniform material category. In line with this consideration, Ergücü et al. [22] demonstrated considerable variation among flowable resin composites and reported that some formulations may exhibit radiopacity levels exceeding those of enamel. Therefore, the relatively high grayscale values observed in the GUI group may be related to the specific formulation and radiopaque filler system of G-ænial Universal Injectable rather than to the injectable category as a whole.

Although the observed differences in grayscale values and artifact patterns may be related to material-dependent factors such as filler loading, filler composition, and radiopaque components, the chemical composition, elemental distribution, and filler content of the tested materials were not directly analyzed in the present study. Therefore, explanations related to high-atomic-number filler particles should be interpreted as plausible mechanisms based on manufacturer-reported information and previous literature rather than as direct experimental findings of this study.

In addition to material composition, the spatial distribution of artifacts is of critical diagnostic importance. In the present study, although restorations were placed on the distal surface, the most intense artifact formation was observed on the opposing mesial surface (p < 0.001), with GA showing the highest value (941.31 ± 3.51 a.u.) and CHR the lowest (928.21 ± 4.31 a.u.) on this surface. This finding can be explained by the principle described by Schulze et al. [11] who attribute artifacts to ‘discrepancies between mathematical modeling and the actual physical imaging process’. As the X-ray beam passes through the dense distal restoration, it undergoes hardening; when this altered data is processed during 3D reconstruction, calculation errors occur, projecting the artifact onto the opposing side. Jaju et al. [23] emphasized that such artifacts can seriously degrade image quality, sometimes to the point of making them ‘diagnostically unusable’. Clinically, this creates a significant risk where the diagnostic value is compromised not necessarily at the restoration site itself, but in the adjacent anatomical structures on the opposite (mesial) side.

The in vitro findings of this study have significant clinical implications. The intense artifacts observed, particularly in the GA and GUI groups, may compromise the radiographic evaluation of adjacent anatomical structures. Cebe et al. [24] reported that artifacts generated by various restorative materials significantly impair the detection of approximal caries. Consequently, the beam-hardening streaks identified in our study pose a risk of masking secondary caries that may develop at restoration margins. Similarly, Nikbin et al. [25] emphasized that artifacts from high-density objects reduce the diagnostic accuracy for vertical root fractures. In this context, dark bands generated by high-radiopacity resin materials could mimic fracture lines, leading to false-positive diagnoses, or conversely, obscure actual fracture lines. When high-resolution CBCT protocols with smaller voxel sizes are required for endodontic applications, such as microsurgical planning or MB2 canal detection, clinicians should consider that increased spatial resolution does not necessarily eliminate material-related artifacts, and scattering from highly radiopaque restorations may still interfere with the assessment of adjacent fine anatomical structures.

This study has certain limitations inherent to its design. As an in vitro study, it could not fully simulate clinical factors such as soft-tissue scatter and patient motion artifacts. Furthermore, to evaluate the materials’ raw artifact potential, no Metal Artifact Reduction (MAR) algorithm was applied. Both Cebe et al. [24] and Nikbin et al. [25] have shown that applying MAR algorithms reduces artifacts and improves diagnostic accuracy for caries and fractures. Future studies are recommended to investigate the efficacy of current MAR algorithms in suppressing artifacts generated by the specific high-radiopacity resin composites identified in this study. A limitation of this study is the exclusion of a natural tooth control group. However, this experimental design was chosen deliberately to prioritize methodological standardization. Natural dentition exhibits significant inter- and intra-individual variations in enamel thickness and dentin mineralization, which can confound densitometric analysis. By using a standardized 3D-printed model with homogeneous density, we eliminated this biological background noise. Consequently, the significant differences observed in grayscale values can be attributed solely to the specific composition of the restorative materials rather than anatomical variations in the substrate.

A further limitation of the present study is that unrestored 3D-printed phantom teeth were not scanned as baseline controls. Therefore, the extent to which grayscale values in restored teeth deviated from the intrinsic radiodensity of the phantom tooth material could not be directly quantified. Future studies should include blank 3D-printed phantom controls to better isolate the contribution of restorative materials to CBCT grayscale alterations and artifact expression.

Clinicians should be aware that modern GUI composites with high filler content can produce significant beam-hardening artifacts that mimic the behavior of high-density materials. When evaluating CBCT images for secondary caries or marginal adaptation, especially on the surface opposite to a restoration (e.g., a mesial surface adjacent to a distal restoration), dark bands should be interpreted with caution to avoid false-positive diagnoses. In diagnostic tasks requiring high precision, selecting a restorative material with lower radiopacity, such as the filler-optimized resin identified in this study, may reduce artifact-related diagnostic interference.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that different resin-based restorative materials produced different grayscale values and artifact patterns under standardized CBCT conditions. Modern high-filler injectable composites exhibited artifact characteristics comparable to those of high-viscosity resin-based materials. These differences may be associated with material-dependent factors such as filler loading, filler composition, and radiopaque components; however, because the chemical composition and filler content of the materials were not directly analyzed, these explanations should be interpreted cautiously. While materials with lower radiopacity, such as CHR, may produce fewer artifacts and improve the visualization of adjacent structures, clinicians should balance this advantage against the diagnostic necessity of material radiopacity. Future studies incorporating direct compositional analysis are needed to clarify the relationship between restorative material composition and CBCT artifact formation.
