Design Strategies
of Cu-Based Tandem Catalysts for
Efficient Electrochemical CO2 Reduction to C2+ Products

Yu-Cheng Liu, Yu-Jhih Shen, Kang-Shun Peng, Shuo-Peng Lin, Chi Kang, Chieh Chang Chien, Min-Si Lee, Shao-Hui Hsu, Sung-Fu Hung

Abstract

Electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) offers a promising route to convert CO2 into
value-added
fuels and chemicals using renewable electricity. Cu-based catalysts
are uniquely capable of producing C2+ hydrocarbons and
oxygenates through C–C coupling, but their performance is limited
by insufficient *CO coverage, broad product distributions, competing
hydrogen evolution, and stability issues at high current densities.
Tandem catalysis addresses these challenges by integrating CO-generating
components with Cu-based C–C coupling sites. This review summarizes
recent advances in Cu-based tandem catalysts under flow-cell and industrially
relevant conditions, focusing on spatially separated systems, Ag–Cu
bimetallic interfaces, single-atom alloys, and SAC–Cu tandem
architectures. Key design principles include regulating CO generation,
transport, interfacial structure, and coupling pathways.

Introduction

Driven by the continuous
rise in atmospheric CO2 concentrations
and the growing climate impacts of greenhouse-gas emissions,
−

 converting CO2 from an emitted greenhouse gas into a
reusable carbon feedstock has become a central objective of carbon
capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technologies.
−

 Among the various CO2 conversion strategies, the electrochemical
CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) has attracted
considerable attention because it can be operated under relatively
mild temperature and pressure conditions
−

 and can be directly powered
by renewable electricity generated from solar, wind, and other sustainable
energy sources. In contrast, photocatalytic
CO2 conversion is often limited by insufficient light absorption,
inefficient charge separation, and low quantum efficiency,
,
 whereas thermocatalytic CO2 conversion generally requires
elevated temperature, high pressure, or external hydrogen sources. These advantages make electrocatalytic CO2RR
a promising platform for coupling CO2 utilization with
renewable-energy-driven chemical production.

Electrocatalytic
CO2RR can produce a wide range of reduction
products, including CO, formate, methane, methanol, ethylene, ethanol,
and n-propanol.
,
 Among these products, C1 products such as CO and formate usually involve two-electron-transfer
pathways and can therefore be produced with high selectivity over
specific catalysts.
,
 In contrast, C2+ products,
including ethylene, ethanol, and n-propanol, have attracted increasing
interests owing to their higher energy density, greater economic value,
and broad industrial relevance. Among the various CO2RR
catalysts, Cu remains the most representative catalyst capable of
producing C2+ products. Unlike Au, Ag, Sn, and Bi, which
typically favor CO or formate formation, Cu exhibits moderate *CO adsorption strength, enabling CO intermediates
to remain on the catalyst surface and participate in subsequent C–C
coupling. Consequently, Cu is one of
the few monometallic catalysts capable of electrochemically converting
CO2 into hydrocarbons and oxygenates. However, because
C2+ product formation involves multiple electron-transfer,
proton-transfer, and C–C coupling steps, Cu-based CO2RR still faces major challenges, including broad product distributions,
insufficient selectivity, and limited stability under high-current-density
operation.
−

To evaluate the industrial relevance of a CO2RR catalyst,
several key performance metrics must be considered, including Faradaic
efficiency (FE), partial current density, cell voltage, energy efficiency,
and long-term stability. FE represents the fraction of electrons consumed
to generate a target product and is therefore widely used to evaluate
product selectivity. Partial current density, defined as the product
of FE and total current density, reflects the actual production rate
of a specific product. Techno-economic analyses (TEA) have suggested
that economically viable CO2 electrolysis generally requires
high current density, high FE, low cell voltage, and sufficient operational
stability. Accordingly, an FE­(C2+) above approximately
70% and a C2+ partial current density on the order of 300
mA cm–2 are commonly regarded as important benchmarks
for approaching industrially relevant CO2RR operation.
,,

However, conventional H-cell
systems are generally unable to meet
the requirements for high-current-density operation. Because CO2 has limited solubility in aqueous electrolytes and must first
dissolve into the electrolyte before diffusing to the catalyst surface,
CO2 mass transport in H-cells often becomes a major kinetic
limitation. As a result, H-cell measurements are typically insufficient
for accurately evaluating catalyst performance under practical operating
conditions. To overcome CO2 transport limitations, recent
CO2RR research has increasingly shifted toward gas-diffusion
electrodes (GDEs) and flow-cell systems. These systems directly deliver
gaseous CO2 to the vicinity of the catalyst layer and establish
a gas–solid–liquid three-phase interface at the catalyst
surface, thereby substantially shortening the CO2 transport
distance and enabling CO2RR operation at current densities
of several hundred mA cm–2 or even ampere-level
current densities.
−

The development of flow-cell CO2RR has brought
Cu-based
C2+ production closer to industrial implementation, but
it has also introduced new challenges. Under high-current-density
operation, insufficient *CO concentration and adsorption, electrolyte flooding,
,
 and competition from the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) can significantly
affect target-product formation. These challenges indicate that improving
the intrinsic activity of Cu alone is insufficient for achieving efficient
C2+ production. Instead, integrated catalyst design is
required to regulate CO2/CO transport, surface intermediate
coverage, local reaction environments, and C–C coupling pathways.

In response to these challenges, tandem
catalyst design has emerged
as an important strategy for enhancing C2+ selectivity
and production rates in Cu-based CO2RR. This approach generally
involves integrating CO-generating components with Cu-based C–C
coupling sites, enabling CO2 reduction, CO intermediate
supply, and subsequent C–C coupling to proceed cooperatively
within a single catalytic system.
,
 Therefore,
this review focuses on recent design strategies and mechanistic insights
into Cu-based tandem catalysts under high-current-density and flow-cell
conditions, highlighting how different tandem architectures improve
CO utilization and promote C2+ product formation.

Mechanisms and Tandem Design in Cu-Based CO2RR

Cu-based CO2RR toward C2+ products involves
multiple proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) steps and a series
of complex surface-bound intermediates. As illustrated in a, CO2 is generally first activated on the catalyst surface to form the
*COOH intermediate, followed by further electron and proton transfer
to generate *CO. For C1 products such as CO, *CO can directly
desorb from the catalyst surface. In contrast, C2+ product
formation requires sufficient *CO coverage on the Cu surface, allowing
*CO intermediates to undergo subsequent C–C coupling.
,
 The most widely discussed pathway involves the dimerization of two
*CO intermediates to form *OCCO, followed by protonation to generate
key intermediates such as *OCCOH.
,
 Subsequent
hydrogenation, protonation, and electron-transfer steps then lead
to ethylene, ethanol, acetate, or other C2 products.
−

In addition to the conventional *CO–*CO
coupling pathway,
recent studies have suggested that *CO may also couple with other
C1 intermediates, such as *CHO or *CHx. The dominant reaction pathway
depends strongly on the Cu crystal facet,
,
 local reaction microenvironment,
−

 surface *CO coverage, and applied potential. Therefore, although Cu is capable of producing various C2+ products, the complexity of these reaction pathways makes precise
control over product selectivity highly challenging.
,

The unique role of Cu in CO2RR primarily originates
from its moderate adsorption strength toward key intermediates (b). Compared with Au and Ag, which bind *CO weakly and therefore
preferentially produce CO, and Sn or Bi, which tend to favor formate
formation, Cu exhibits an intermediate *CO binding strength. This
allows Cu to retain *CO intermediates on the surface without being
completely poisoned by CO, thereby enabling further *CO hydrogenation
and C–C coupling. However, this moderate adsorption strength
also imposes an intrinsic limitation on Cu-based CO2RR.
Under high-current-density operation, the *CO supply and surface *CO
coverage required to sustain efficient C–C coupling may become
insufficient. If *CO desorbs too rapidly, or if local CO2 depletion and competing HER reduce surface *CO coverage, the probability
of C–C coupling decreases, resulting in lower C2+ selectivity and increased formation of C1 products or
H2.
,−

As illustrated
in c, tandem
catalysis provides a rational design strategy by
assigning CO generation and C–C coupling to different but spatially
adjacent catalytic sites. In a typical Cu-based tandem catalyst, a
CO-generating component, such as Ag, Au, or M–N4 single-atom sites, preferentially reduces CO2 to CO or
*CO intermediates. These CO intermediates then migrate to neighboring
Cu sites through diffusion, spillover, or interfacial migration, where
they undergo further C–C coupling and deep reduction to form
C2+ products. The central objective of tandem catalysis
is therefore to overcome insufficient *CO supply on Cu by increasing
the local CO concentration and surface *CO coverage near Cu, while
minimizing the transport distance between CO-generation sites and
C–C coupling sites.

Another critical consideration in
Cu-based tandem CO2RR is the competition with the hydrogen
evolution reaction (HER),
which directly influences both carbon utilization and C–C coupling.
At the Cu surface, adsorbed *H intermediate generated during HER competes
with *CO and other carbonaceous intermediates for available catalytic
sites. Increased *H coverage can decrease the population and proximity
of adsorbed *CO species required for carbon–carbon bond formation,
thereby suppressing multicarbon production. Under commonly employed
neutral or alkaline conditions, tandem architectures can mitigate
HER through several complementary mechanisms. In particular, increasing
the local supply and coverage of CO/*CO promotes preferential occupation
of Cu surface sites by carbonaceous intermediates, thereby limiting
competitive *H adsorption and maintaining the adjacent *CO configurations
required for C–C coupling.
,
 In parallel,
hydrophobic catalyst layers and gas-diffusion interfaces can regulate
water accessibility and mitigate electrolyte flooding while preserving
efficient CO2 and CO transport pathways.
,
 The combined regulation of surface-intermediate coverage and the
local mass-transport environment therefore shifts the competition
from *H formation toward carbonaceous intermediate retention and C–C
coupling.

The competition between CO2RR and HER becomes
considerably
more complex under acidic conditions, where the high proton availability
intrinsically favors hydrogen evolution. Although acidic CO2 electrolysis offers important advantages by mitigating carbonate
formation and improving carbon utilization, the tandem enhancement
commonly observed for Ag–Cu systems under neutral or alkaline
conditions can be substantially weakened. Beyond increased competition
from *H adsorption, the acidic electrolyte can directly modify the
adsorption behavior and interfacial state of Cu. Experimental and
theoretical studies have shown that hydronium ions can weaken *CO
binding on Cu, thereby reducing the retention
and utilization efficiency of tandem- generated CO. Strongly acidic
environments can also deplete surface oxygen-containing Cu surface
species that have been associated with favorable C–C coupling. These effects indicate that proton activity
influences tandem catalysis not only through direct competition for
surface sites but also by altering the chemical state and adsorption
properties of the Cu interface.

Interestingly, recent studies
of acidic tandem CO2RR
demonstrate that the CO-generating component can perform an additional
function beyond simply supplying CO. By consuming protons and modifying
the local reaction environment, the secondary catalytic component
can establish a neutral-to-alkaline microenvironment around Cu, thereby
suppressing proton-driven HER and restoring favorable oxygen-containing
surface species favorable for C–C coupling.
,
 Furthermore, the composition and spatial distribution of Ag within
CuAg tandem catalysts can reorganize the interfacial water network
and consequently regulate the balance between *CO retention and *H
adsorption. These findings demonstrate
that HER suppression in tandem CO2RR should not be regarded
simply as minimizing H2 production. Rather, it involves
coordinated control over *CO/*H competitive adsorption, local CO coverage,
proton and water accessibility, local pH, Cu surface chemistry, and
the interfacial solvation environment. Rational regulation of these
coupled factors is therefore essential for maintaining efficient intermediate
utilization and C–C coupling across different electrolyte environments.

However, recent studies have increasingly shown that efficient
tandem catalysis cannot be achieved solely by increasing CO generation.
If the generated CO desorbs, escapes from the catalyst layer, or diffuses
into the electrolyte before participating in C–C coupling,
the overall CO utilization efficiency remains limited. Therefore,
in addition to enhancing CO/*CO availability, precise control over
CO transport, retention, adsorption, intermediate migration, and coupling
pathways on Cu surfaces has become a central consideration in the
design of Cu-based tandem catalysts.

In recent years, advances
in flow-cell and gas-diffusion-electrode
technologies have enabled Cu-based tandem catalysts to achieve substantially
improved C2+ selectivity and partial current density under
commercially relevant high-current-density conditions, highlighting
their potential for practical CO2RR applications. As summarized
schematically in , the tandem catalysts discussed in this review can be categorized
into four representative architectures according to the spatial relationship
and degree of structural integration between the CO-generating sites
and Cu: spatially separated tandem catalysts, bimetallic heterointerfaces,
single-atom alloys (SAAs), and SAC–Cu tandem systems. Although
all four architectures share the fundamentally objective of increasing
the local availability and effective utilization of CO/*CO intermediates
for subsequent C–C coupling on Cu, they accomplish this through
distinct intermediate-transfer pathways and interfacial regulatory
mechanisms.

In spatially separated systems, CO is generated
on a physically
distinct catalytic component and subsequently transported to Cu through
diffusion or spillover Consequently, the transport distance, residence
time, and retention of CO within the catalyst layer strongly influence
the probability that the generated intermediate reaches Cu before
escaping into the gas phase. Bimetallic tandem catalysts reduce this
spatial separation by establishing direct phase boundaries between
the CO-generating component and Cu/CuOx. In addition to
shortening the intermediate-transfer distance, these heterointerfaces
can induce charge redistribution, stabilization of favorable Cu oxidation
states, and modify intermediate adsorption energetics, thereby coupling
CO supply with interfacial regulation of C–C coupling.

SAAs provide a higher degree of structural integration by incorporating
isolated secondary-metal atoms directly into the Cu lattice. This
atomic-scale arrangement enables local electronic and geometric modulation
of neighboring Cu sites while maintaining contiguous Cu ensembles
required for C–C bond formation. The close proximity between
the isolated atoms and Cu further facilitates short-range inter-migration
of CO/*CO intermediates, minimizing the transport distance between
complementary catalytic sites. In SAC–Cu tandem systems, by
contrast, atomically dispersed coordination sites, such as M–N4 centers or molecular complexes, serve as independently tunable
CO-generating centers coupled to Cu. Their tandem performance therefore
depends not only on intrinsic CO-generation activity but also on potential-window
matching between the two catalytic components, effective interfacial
contact, and efficient delivery of the generated CO/*CO intermediates
to Cu.

Following transfer to Cu, the local enriched CO/*CO intermediates
can undergo conventional *CO–*CO coupling or asymmetric coupling
with hydrogenated C1 intermediates, ultimately promoting
C2+ product formation. Thus, these four tandem architectures
regulate a common reaction sequence at different structural length
scales, ranging from mesoscale CO transport in spatially separated
systems and phase-boundary-mediated interactions in bimetallic catalysts
to atomic-scale intermediate migration in SAAs and coordination-site
engineering in SAC-Cu systems. Their major limitations consequently
differ: spacially separated system are particularly susceptible to
CO loss and mass-transport limitations; bimetallic catalysts may undergo
dynamic interfacial reconstruction; SAAs require simultaneous stabilization
of isolated atoms and preservation of contiguous Cu ensembles; and
SAC-Cu systems depend strongly on coordination-site stability, potential
matching, and efficient coupling between the CO-generating sites and
Cu. This architecture-dependent perspective provides a framework for
understanding how the spatial organization of complementary catalytic
sites governs intermediate transfer and ultimately determines tandem
CO2RR performance.

Spatially Separated Tandem Catalysts

In CO2RR tandem catalytic systems, coupling Cu with
CO-producing metal catalysts, such as Ag or Au, has been widely demonstrated
as an effective strategy for enhancing C2+ product selectivity.
This improvement is generally attributed to the increased local concentration
of the key *CO intermediate near Cu surfaces, which promotes the kinetics
of C–C coupling. In an early study, Chen et al. showed that
the physical mixing of commercial Cu and Ag powders could significantly
improve C2+ production in CO2RR. In 1 M KOH
at −0.70 V versus RHE, the incorporation of Ag increased the
partial current density of C2+ products from 37 to 160
mA cm–2, indicating
that Ag-derived CO can effectively serve as a precursor intermediate
for subsequent C–C coupling on Cu.

However, these early
tandem strategies also revealed an important
limitation: simply increasing CO supply near Cu does not necessarily
lead to highly efficient C2+ formation. Because the *CO
binding strength on Cu is relatively limited, a substantial fraction
of the generated CO may desorb or escape from the catalyst layer before
participating in C–C coupling, thereby decreasing CO utilization
efficiency and limiting C–C coupling performance. Consequently,
recent designs of spatially separated tandem catalysts have gradually
shifted from merely increasing local CO concentration toward more
comprehensive CO management strategies, including CO generation, transport,
residence, adsorption, trapping, and selective conversion on Cu surfaces.

Nanowire Arrays and Hydrophobic Microenvironments
for Promoting CO Transport, Retention, and Utilization

Recent
studies have demonstrated that nanostructured arrays can improve CO
utilization efficiency within the catalyst layer by prolonging CO
residence time and increasing the probability of C–C coupling.
−

 Bian et al. designed a hierarchical Cu NWs/NC@Ag-PTFE tandem electrode
to enhance the spillover, adsorption, and C–C coupling of Ag-derived
CO. As shown in a, this electrode consists of a PTFE membrane,
an Ag layer, vertically aligned Cu nanowire arrays, and a nitrogen-doped
carbon (NC) modification layer. The Ag layer functions as the CO-generating
component, preferentially reducing CO2 to CO, whereas the
inner Cu nanowire arrays receive Ag-derived CO through spillover.
The vertically aligned and porous Cu nanowire arrays increase the
CO diffusion path and residence time within the catalyst layer, thereby
enhancing the probability that CO contacts Cu reaction sites and participates
in subsequent coupling reactions.

Beyond the physical transport advantages provided
by the nanowire
arrays, the NC modification layer further contributes to CO trapping
and intermediate stabilization. Compared
with Cu NWs@Ag-PTFE without NC modification, the Cu/NC interface in
Cu NWs/NC@Ag-PTFE strengthens *CO adsorption, enabling Ag-derived
CO to be more effectively confined near Cu reaction sites. In situ
spectroscopic analysis revealed that this hierarchical structure enhances
the signal of linear *CO intermediates, promotes H2O dissociation,
and stabilizes the key C–C coupling intermediate *OCCOH, as
schematically illustrated in b. In situ Raman analysis of *CO adsorption further
showed that Cu NWs/NC@Ag-PTFE exhibits a higher high-frequency band
(HFB) ratio relative to the low-frequency band (LFB), suggesting higher
*CO coverage or a more favorable adsorption environment for adjacent
*CO species on the Cu surface, as shown in c.
,

In terms of catalytic
performance, Cu NWs/NC@Ag-PTFE achieved a
C2+ Faradaic efficiency of 87.5% at a current density of
519.0 mA cm–2 and exhibited a high C2+/C1 ratio of 10.42, significantly outperforming Cu NWs@Ag-PTFE
and Cu@PTFE (d–f). These results indicate that the hierarchical architecture
not only increases CO supply but also improves CO utilization efficiency
on Cu. In particular, the enhanced C2+/C1 ratio
suggests that a greater fraction of CO intermediates is directed toward
C–C coupling rather than being released from the reaction system
as CO or converted into other C1 products.

In addition
to CO transport and trapping within the catalyst layer,
interfacial wettability under flow-cell conditions is another key
factor determining whether the tandem effect can be fully activated. Because CO2RR occurs at a gas–solid–liquid
three-phase interface, excessive hydrophilicity of the Ag/Cu electrode
surface or electrolyte flooding of gas-diffusion pathways can restrict
CO2 access to Ag sites, thereby suppressing Ag-mediated
CO generation. Even when Ag and Cu are spatially adjacent, insufficient
CO2 supply or disruption of the CO transfer pathway can
prevent the intrinsic Ag–Cu tandem effect from being effectively
expressed.

Interfacial wettability is a critical design parameter
in tandem
CO2 electroreduction because it governs the balance among
CO2 transport, water accessibility, electrolyte penetration,
and intermediate utilization. Importantly, wettability can be regulated
across multiple length scales, ranging from the catalyst surface and
ionomer/binder environment to gas-diffusion layer (GDL). For example,
Lee et al. employed Cu/Ag bilayer electrodes
incorporating cation- and anion-conducting ionomers and demonstrated
that optimization of the ionomer configuration and local hydrophobicity
could simultaneously regulate the interfacial pH and water content.
This tailored microenvironment suppressed the competing HER while
facilitating the conversion of tandem-generated CO toward C2+ products, highlighting the important role of ionomers in controlling
both mass transport and the local reaction environment.

Beyond
catalyst-layer engineering, Li et al. developed a hydrophobicity-graded gas-diffusion layer for
Ni single-atom/Cu tandem electrodes. The spatially tailored hydrophobicity
mitigated electrolyte flooding and maintained efficient CO2 transport, while in situ Raman spectroscopy revealed increased local
CO intermediate coverage. These effects promoted ethylene formation
and substantially improved operational stability, demonstrating that
GDL-level wettability engineering can directly regulate intermediate
availability and utilization. More recently, Park et al. systematically investigated the binder wettability
in layered Ag/Cu tandem electrodes and identified an optimal configuration
consisted of a relatively hydrophilic CO-producing layer coupled with
a hydrophobic Cu layer. These findings demonstrate that maximizing
the overall hydrophobicity of tandem electrode is not necessarily
beneficial. Instead, spatially differentiated wettability should be
rationally tailored across the functional layers to balance CO2 transport, water accessibility, electrolyte management, HER
suppression, and CO intermediate utilization, thereby promoting selective
and stable C2+ production.

Liu et al. investigated
the effect of interfacial wettability on
layered Ag/Cu electrodes in a flow cell. The results showed that the Ag/Cu electrode exhibited CO2RR performance similar to that of the Cu-only electrode, indicating
that the Ag/Cu tandem effect was suppressed under practical operating
conditions. This suppression was mainly attributed to the exposed
Ag layer, which rendered the electrode surface more susceptible to
electrolyte wetting, thereby hindering CO2 transport to
Ag sites and decreasing both CO generation and subsequent CO transfer.

To address this issue, they employed a 1-dodecanethiol (DDT) self-assembly
strategy to form a molecular hydrophobic layer on the Ag/Cu surface
through Ag–S bonding. After DDT modification, the electrode
surface became significantly more hydrophobic, effectively reducing
electrolyte intrusion and maintaining a gas-accessible Ag/Cu interface.
This ultrathin hydrophobic layer enabled more efficient CO2 transport to Ag sites and facilitated the transfer of Ag-generated
CO to the Cu surface. Because the DDT layer is extremely thin, it
improves surface hydrophobicity while avoiding excessive reactant
blocking or significant mass-transfer resistance, as illustrated in g.

Catalytically,
DDT–Ag/Cu exhibited markedly improved C2+ production
compared with unmodified Ag/Cu and Cu electrodes,
as reflected in the product distributions shown in h, i. j further shows that DDT modification increased
the ethanol-to-ethylene ratio from approximately 0.5 to approximately
1.0. This result suggests that hydrophobic surface modification not
only restores the Ag/Cu tandem effect but may also affect the C–C
coupling pathway on Cu by regulating local CO2/CO availability,
CO residence time, and the reaction microenvironment. The in situ
Raman results shown in k–m reveal the appearance of Cu–*OH after hydrophobic
modification. In addition, the HFB signal is relatively stronger on
DDT–Ag/Cu, indicating that the Cu surface may possess a higher-coverage
*CO adsorption environment after hydrophobic modification. Since HFB
is generally associated with a high-*CO-coverage state favorable for
C–C coupling, these results further suggest that the DDT hydrophobic
layer not only improves gas transport but also promotes C2+ formation by maintaining high local CO availability.

Taken
together, these studies indicate that the central challenge
in spatially separated Ag/Cu tandem catalysts is not merely to increase
CO generation, but to ensure that CO can be effectively transported,
retained, and directed toward C–C coupling on Cu under flow-cell
conditions.

Cu Facet Regulation and Asymmetric C–C
Coupling for Controlling Product Distribution

Another important
design direction for tandem catalysts is the precise regulation of
the reaction pathway of tandem-generated CO on Cu surfaces. Traditional
Ag/Cu tandem catalysts primarily aim to increase the local CO concentration
near Cu, thereby promoting *CO–*CO coupling and C2
+ product formation. However, recent studies have shown
that, when specific hydrogenated carbon intermediates are stabilized
on the Cu surface, Ag-derived CO does not necessarily participate
exclusively in the conventional *CO–*CO coupling pathway. Instead,
it can undergo asymmetric C–C coupling with *CHx species on
Cu, thereby altering the C2 product distribution and, in
particular, enhancing ethanol selectivity.
,

Luan et al. designed a layered Cu/Ag tandem catalyst composed
of Cu(111)-oriented nanosheets and Ag nanoparticles to redirect CO2RR from a hydrocarbon-dominant pathway toward oxygenate production,
as shown in a. On bare Cu(111), the reaction mainly
proceeds through successive hydrogenation, where carbon intermediates
generated from CO2 reduction are progressively hydrogenated
into *CHx species and further converted into hydrocarbons. In contrast, in the layered Cu/Ag tandem catalyst,
Ag nanoparticles act as CO-generating sites, reducing CO2 to CO and enabling CO intermediates to diffuse to the adjacent Cu(111)
surface. Meanwhile, the Cu(111) facet favors the formation of hydrogenated
carbon intermediates, such as *CH2 or *CH. When Ag-derived
CO and Cu(111)-derived *CHx species coexist, the reaction can proceed
through asymmetric *CH2–CO or *CH–CO coupling
pathways to form oxygenated C2 intermediates, thereby promoting
ethanol formation.

The electrochemical tests and product analysis
shown in b,
c demonstrate
that the reaction pathway on layered Cu/Ag indeed shifts from hydrocarbon-dominant
to oxygenate-enhanced. Compared with bare Cu, mixed CuAg, and Ag/Cu
structures, layered Cu/Ag exhibits a higher ethanol partial current
density over a wide potential range. In particular, at −1.1
V versus RHE, the oxygenates-to-hydrocarbons ratio of layered Cu/Ag
is much higher than those of the control catalysts, while the FE­(C2+) is also significantly enhanced. These results indicate
that the advantage of layered Cu/Ag lies not only in increasing overall
C2+ formation, but also in steering the C2+ product
distribution toward oxygenated products such as ethanol.

Operando
Raman spectroscopy, shown in d, provides important evidence for this altered
reaction pathway. On the layered Cu/Ag catalyst, as the applied potential
shifts negatively, signals associated with Cu–CO, *CO, *CHx
stretching, *CH2/*CH3 bending, and *OCCHx species
can be observed.
−

 This indicates that CO intermediates and hydrogenated carbon species
are simultaneously formed on the Cu surface. These spectroscopic features
support the occurrence of CO diffusion and *CHx-assisted asymmetric
coupling in layered Cu/Ag. Compared with the successive hydrogenation
pathway dominant on pure Cu(111), layered Cu/Ag enables a bifunctional
synergy in which Ag supplies CO while Cu(111) generates *CHx species,
allowing cross-coupling between *CHx and CO and thereby modifying
C2 product selectivity.

The co-feeding experiment
shown in e
further verifies the importance of *CHx–CO
asymmetric coupling for ethanol formation. When only CO was introduced, the oxygenate production rate remained
relatively limited. In contrast, when CO and CH3I were
co-fed, the oxygenate production rate increased significantly, especially
for ethanol. This result demonstrates that increasing CO availability
while simultaneously providing a source of hydrogenated carbon intermediates
can facilitate the asymmetric C–C coupling pathway. In other
words, the coexistence of Ag-derived CO and Cu(111)-derived *CHx species
is critical for achieving ethanol-selective CO2RR on layered
Cu/Ag.

The DFT calculations shown in f–h compare Langmuir–Hinshelwood
(L–H) and Eley–Rideal-type asymmetric coupling routes.
The results indicate that, in layered Cu/Ag, Ag-derived CO can couple
with Cu(111)-derived *CH or *CH2 species to form *CHCO
or *CH2CO intermediates. In particular, under more negative
potentials, the *CH2 + CO coupling pathway exhibits a higher
theoretical rate, supporting the conclusion that CO diffusion and
*CHx-assisted asymmetric coupling are key factors in improving ethanol
selectivity.

Overall, this section demonstrates that spatially
separated tandem
catalysts have evolved beyond the simple enhancement of local CO concentration.
To further improve C2+ selectivity and production rates
under industrially relevant CO2RR conditions, spatial tandem
catalyst design should integrate CO management with precise control
over the C–C coupling pathway.

Ag–Cu Bimetallic and
Single-Atom Alloys

In conventional spatially separated Ag/Cu
tandem catalysts, CO
generated on Ag sites must diffuse or spill over to Cu surfaces before
participating in C–C coupling. Consequently, the overall tandem
efficiency can be strongly influenced by interfacial distance, CO
residence time, and mass transport. By contrast, Ag–Cu bimetallic
interfaces and AgCu single-atom alloys enable closer integration of
CO generation, *CO migration, and C–C coupling within intimate
metal–metal or metal–oxide interfaces environments.
These systems can further promote C2+ formation through
electronic-structure modulation, stabilization of Cu oxidation states,
and atomic-level inter-migration of reaction intermediates.

Ag–Cu Bimetallic Catalysts: Interfacial
and Electronic-Structure Modulation for Promoting C–C Coupling

In Ag–Cu bimetallic systems, the role of Ag extends beyond
that of a simple CO supplier. Ag incorporation can increase the *CO
coverage near adjacent Cu sites, modify the electronic state of Cu,
and regulate the energetic barriers associated with CO2 activation, *CO formation, and *CO dimerization. As a result, Ag–Cu
bimetallic interfaces provide a more tightly coupled cooperative reaction
mode than conventional spatially separated tandem catalysts.

Wei et al. reported an Ag/Cu2O interfacial catalyst for
highly selective ethylene production under neutral CO2RR
conditions. In this system, Ag was introduced
onto the Cu2O surface to form a phase-separated but intimately
contacted Ag/Cu2O heterointerface, rather than a simple
physical mixture, as shown in a. Electronic-structure analysis by XPS showed that
the Cu 2p and Ag 3d signals of the Ag–Cu2O catalyst
shifted noticeably compared with those of pristine Cu2O
and Ag, indicating the formation of electron-deficient Cu species
and strong electronic interactions among Ag, Cu, and O atoms in Cu2O (b,c). This interfacial electronic modulation alters the electronic
state of Cu sites at the Ag/Cu2O interface, thereby increasing
*CO coverage around Cu active sites and promoting subsequent C–C
coupling.

In terms of catalytic performance, the optimized
Ag–Cu2O-0.10 catalyst achieved a C2+ Faradaic
efficiency
of 73.6% at 650 mA cm–2 in 3 M KCl neutral electrolyte,
with an ethylene Faradaic efficiency of 66.0% and an ethylene partial
current density of 429.1 mA cm–2 (d). Compared with pristine
Cu2O, the formation of the Ag/Cu2O interface
significantly increased the C2H4/C1 and C2+/C1 ratios, indicating that Ag incorporation
not only enhanced CO supply but also improved the efficiency of directing
CO intermediates toward C–C coupling (e). Furthermore, the DFT calculations shown
in f, g reveal
that the Ag/Cu2O interface lowers the energy barriers for
CO2 conversion to *COOH and subsequent *CO formation, while
also facilitating *CO–*CO coupling to form *OCCO. These results
suggest that the Ag/Cu2O interface actively participates
in both CO generation and C–C coupling, rather than merely
serving as a CO supplier in the tandem system.

Jiang et al.
further demonstrated the role of Ag in stabilizing
the Cu+/Cu0 interface. The Cu+/Cu0 interface is generally considered
favorable for *CO adsorption and dimerization;
−

 however, under high-current-density
CO2RR conditions, Cu+ species are readily reduced
to Cu0, leading to gradual degradation of the interfacial
structure and active sites. To address this challenge, Ag was introduced
to construct a 5.6 atom % Ag/Cu2O–Cu catalyst containing
abundant and stable Ag/Cu+/Cu0 heterointerfaces.
In this structure, Ag not only promotes *CO intermediate generation
and local *CO coverage but also stabilizes the Cu2O/Cu
active interface, allowing the Cu+/Cu0 structure
to be better preserved under high-current-density reaction conditions,
as illustrated in h.

Cu LMM Auger spectra collected before and after the
stability test
(i,j) showed
that Ag incorporation helped maintain the Cu+/Cu0 composition and suppressed excessive reduction of the Cu2O/Cu active interface during CO2RR. In situ Raman spectra
(k) further
revealed more pronounced Ag–CO-related signals during the reaction,
indicating that Ag incorporation increased *CO intermediate generation
and surface coverage. The optimized catalyst achieved a C2+ Faradaic efficiency of 76.5 ± 1.2% at an ampere-level current
density of 1.0 A cm–2 in a 1 M KOH flow cell (l). The DFT results
in m further
confirmed that the stable Ag/Cu+/Cu0 interface
modulates the free energies of key intermediates, including *COOH,
*CO, and *OCCO, thereby lowering the energy barriers associated with
*CO formation and C–C coupling and ultimately promoting C2+ product formation.

Taken together, these two studies
demonstrate that Ag incorporation
in Cu-based catalysts plays a role that extends well beyond conventional
CO supply in tandem catalysis. Ag–Cu bimetallic interfaces
provide three key effects. First, Ag sites enhance CO/*CO generation
and increase local *CO coverage. Second, electronic interactions between
Ag and Cu2O or Cu+/Cu0 interfaces
modulate the electronic structure of Cu active sites, thereby optimizing
*CO adsorption and C–C coupling energetics. Third, Ag incorporation
stabilizes Cu+/Cu0 active interfaces, improving
catalytic stability under high-current-density operation.

Ag–Cu Single-Atom Alloy Catalysts:
Promoting Intermediate Migration while Preserving Cu Active Sites

Compared with Ag/Cu bimetallic catalysts, Ag–Cu single-atom
alloys (AgCu-SAAs) provide a more refined strategy for tandem catalyst
design. In typical Ag nanoparticle-modified Cu catalysts, Ag can effectively
promote CO2-to-CO conversion and increase the local CO
concentration. However, excessive or aggregated Ag nanoparticles may
partially cover the Cu surface, thereby reducing the number of Cu
active sites available for C–C coupling. In addition, CO generated
on Ag sites often needs to desorb into the electrolyte and subsequently
readsorb onto neighboring Cu sites. This out-migration pathway can
lead to CO loss, decreasing CO utilization efficiency and C2+ selectivity. Therefore, dispersing Ag as isolated atoms within a
Cu matrix can improve Ag atom utilization, minimize blockage of Cu
active sites, and establish a shorter and more direct CO/*CO intermediate-transfer
pathway.

Du et al. proposed a cascade electrocatalyst combining
AgCu single-atom alloy sites with Ag nanoparticles, denoted as AgCu
SANP. In this design, Ag nanoparticles
primarily function as CO-generating sites by reducing CO2 to CO, whereas AgCu-SAA sites regulate neighboring Cu sites for
C–C coupling by tuning *CO adsorption through single-atom Ag
doping. The key concept of this design is not simply to increase Ag
loading, but to couple the CO-generation ability of Ag nanoparticles
with the electronic and geometric regulation of Cu sites by AgCu-SAA.
This enables CO generation and C–C coupling enhancement to
occur synergistically within the same catalytic system, as illustrated
in a.

In CO2RR flow-cell tests (b,c), AgCu SANP achieved a
C2+ Faradaic efficiency of 94 ± 4% at an operating
current density
of approximately 720 mA cm–2, significantly outperforming
Cu nanoparticles, AgCu-SAA, and AgCu nanoparticles. Notably, although
AgCu nanoparticles provide more Ag sites and enhance CO generation,
their C2+ production decreased instead. This result indicates
that excessive Ag can block or dilute Cu active sites, leaving an
insufficient number of Cu sites available for C–C coupling.
Therefore, the high C2+ selectivity of AgCu SANP originates
from an optimized balance between Ag nanoparticles and AgCu-SAA sites:
Ag nanoparticles supply local CO, while AgCu-SAA preserves and activates
Cu-based C–C coupling sites, avoiding the Cu site loss caused
by simply increasing Ag loading.

From the perspective of structural
and electronic effects, incorporating
isolated Ag atoms into Cu nanoparticles can introduce compressive
strain into neighboring Cu–Cu bonds because of the larger atomic
radius of Ag, thereby forming asymmetric Cu active sites. The DFT
calculations shown in d indicate that this asymmetric Ag–Cu coordination
optimizes *CO adsorption on Cu sites and lowers the subsequent C–C
coupling barrier. Therefore, the function of AgCu-SAA is not limited
to increasing CO supply; it can also intrinsically enhance the ability
of Cu sites to undergo *CO dimerization and C–C coupling. This
explains why AgCu SANP outperforms Cu nanoparticles, AgCu-SAA-only
catalysts, and AgCu nanoparticles.

Wang et al. further clarified
the advantage of AgCu-SAA from the
perspective of the CO intermediate migration pathway. In conventional Ag nanoparticle-modified Cu catalysts,
CO generated on Ag sites tends to desorb first into the electrolyte
because of weak CO–Ag binding, and then readsorb onto neighboring
Cu sites, forming an indirect out-migration pathway. This transfer
process can cause CO intermediate loss, increase CO Faradaic efficiency,
and decrease C2
+ selectivity. By contrast, in
AgCu-SAA, Ag atoms are embedded or atomically dispersed within the
Cu catalyst. Because no distinct blocking interface exists between
Ag and adjacent Cu sites, CO generated on Ag sites can be directly
transferred to neighboring Cu sites, forming a more efficient inter-migration
pathway, as shown in e.

In terms of catalytic performance (f), AgCu-SAA achieved a C2+ Faradaic
efficiency of 83.4% at 900 mA cm–2 and maintained
74.8% C2+ Faradaic efficiency even at a high current density
of 1100 mA cm–2. In comparison, Ag nanoparticle-modified
Cu increased CO generation but only moderately improved C2
+ selectivity, while also exhibiting a higher CO Faradaic
efficiency. This indicates that Ag nanoparticles mainly promote CO
formation but do not efficiently direct CO toward C–C coupling
on Cu. In situ Raman spectra (g) showed that AgCu-SAA exhibits more pronounced Cu–CO,
bridge-bound *CO, and low-frequency atop *CO signals than AgCu-Nano,
indicating higher *CO coverage near Cu active sites. The DFT calculations
shown in h–j
further demonstrate that, although AgCu-Nano is theoretically more
favorable for CO/*CO generation, the inter-migration pathway from
Ag sites to adjacent Cu sites is more favorable in AgCu-SAA. In contrast,
in AgCu-Nano, CO more readily desorbs into the gas phase or electrolyte,
leading to reduced intermediate utilization efficiency. This mechanistic
distinction explains the superior CO utilization and C–C coupling
capability of AgCu-SAA.

Overall, Ag–Cu tandem catalyst
design has evolved from spatially
separated CO-supply strategies toward increasingly integrated interfacial
and atomic-scale cooperative regulation. Importantly, Ag–Cu
bimetallic heterojunctions and AgCu single-atom alloys (SAAs) promote
tandem CO2RR through mechanistically distinct modes of
cooperation. In bimetallic heterojunctions, Ag and Cu/CuOx remain as distinct phases connected through a finite interface.
Their catalytic enhancement therefore relies primarily on interfacial
charge redistribution, stabilization of favorable Cu oxidation states,
modification of intermediate adsorption energetics, and the transfer
or spillover of Ag-derived CO/*CO across the Ag–Cu phase boundary.
Accordingly, the density, electronic structure, and structural stability
of the heterointerface are critical factors governing intermediate
utilization and overall tandem performance.

In contrast, AgCu
SAAs contain isolated Ag atoms atomically dispersed
within the Cu matrix rather than forming an extended Ag domains or
Ag/Cu phase boundaries. Such atomic dispersion minimizes the geometric
blockage and dilution of contiguous Cu ensembles required for C–C
coupling, while locally perturbing the electronic structure and coordination
environment of neighboring Cu atoms. The resulting asymmetric Ag–Cu
coordination and associated lattice distortion can further modulate
*CO adsorption and C–C coupling energetics. More importantly,
the direct atomic scale proximity between isolated Ag and neighboring
Cu sites enables short-range inter-migration of CO/*CO intermediates,
reducing the need for the longer-range desorption, diffusion, and
readsorption processes characteristic of spatially separated Ag/Cu
components. Thus, whereas bimetallic heterojunctions primarily exploit
phase-boundary-mediated electronic interactions and interfacial stabilization,
and intermediate spillover, AgCu SAAs emphasize atomic-scale site
proximity, preservation of Cu ensembles, local electronic regulation,
and efficient intermediate transfer.

The Ag/Cu2O
and Ag/Cu2O–Cu heterointerfaces
discussed in exemplify the former mechanism, in which Ag promotes C2+ formation through interfacial electronic modulation, increased
*CO availability, and stabilization of catalytically favorable Cu+/Cu0 active interfaces. By contrast, the AgCu-SAA
systems discussed in illustrate how isolated Ag atoms can simultaneously
regulate the properties of neighboring Cu sites and establish short
intermediate-transfer pathways without excessively occupying the Cu
ensembles required for C–C coupling. Collectively, these studies
demonstrate that efficient Ag–Cu tandem catalysis involves
more than simply maximizing CO generation. Rather, catalytic performance
depends on matching the structural scale and mode of Ag–Cu
cooperation with the required catalytic functions, including CO formation,
*CO adsorption and migration, preservation of Cu active ensembles,
interfacial or atomic-scale structural stability, and subsequent C–C
coupling kinetics.

SAC–Cu Tandem Catalysts

In addition to Ag–Cu
bimetallic interfaces and single-atom
alloy strategies, SAC–Cu tandem systems have emerged as an
attractive approach for promoting CO2RR toward C2+ products. Compared with metallic CO-generating components such as
Ag and Au, single-atom catalysts or molecular M–N4 sites offer well-defined coordination environments, high atom utilization
efficiency, and tunable CO2-to-CO activity through regulation
of the central metal, ligand structure, and support interaction.
−

However, high CO selectivity alone does not necessarily ensure
efficient tandem catalysis. The CO-generation potential window must
overlap with the potential range required for C–C coupling
on Cu, and the generated CO/*CO intermediates must be efficiently
transferred to nearby Cu sites before desorption or diffusion into
the electrolyte. Therefore, efficient SAC–Cu tandem design
requires the integrated regulation of CO generation, potential alignment,
interfacial coupling, and *CO spillover pathways.

Potential Alignment and Interfacial Coupling
in SAC–Cu Tandem Catalysts

Liu et al. first emphasized
that operating potential alignment between CO-generating sites and
Cu-based C–C coupling sites is a critical but often overlooked
factor in tandem catalysts (a). In general, Ag nanoparticles
and certain molecular catalysts exhibit optimal CO generation activity
at relatively mild cathodic potentials. However, *CO dimerization
and C2H4 formation on Cu typically require more
negative potentials. When Cu operates in the potential region most
favorable for C–C coupling, these CO-generating components
may instead favor the hydrogen evolution reaction, thereby failing
to continuously supply CO and consequently reducing tandem efficiency.

To clarify the importance of *CO coverage in C–C
coupling,
Liu et al. performed DFT calculations to analyze the activation barrier
for *OCCO formation on Cu(111), as shown in b. The results showed that increasing *CO
coverage on the Cu surface significantly lowers the activation barrier
for *CO–*CO coupling. In contrast, increased *H coverage raises
the reaction barrier (c), indicating that *H competes with *CO for adsorption sites
and suppresses C–C coupling. These findings suggest that the
key to efficient tandem catalysis is not merely increasing the overall
reaction current, but rather maintaining high *CO coverage while suppressing
HER within the potential window where Cu most effectively produces
C2H4. Accordingly, an ideal CO-generating SAC
should possess a broad CO generation potential window, allowing continuous
CO supply even at more negative potentials.

Based on this concept,
Liu et al. compared Ag nanoparticles, nickel
phthalocyanine, and Ni SACs as CO-generating components. The results
showed that Ni SACs possess a broader CO-generation potential range
and better overlap with the C–C coupling potential window of
Cu (d). In
flow-cell measurements (e,f), the Ni SAC + Cu tandem catalyst achieved a C2H4 partial current density of approximately 370 mA cm–2 and a C2H4 Faradaic efficiency
of approximately 62%. These results demonstrate that potential alignment
determines whether the CO generated by SACs can be effectively supplied
when Cu requires high *CO coverage, thereby affecting the overall
tandem efficiency and C2H4 selectivity.

In addition to potential alignment, interfacial coupling between
SACs and Cu, as well as *CO spillover, is another key factor governing
the performance of SAC–Cu tandem catalysts. Wang et al. reported
an EP-CoP/Cu tandem catalyst constructed by in situ electropolymerization,
in which cobalt porphyrin monomers were directly electropolymerized
onto a Cu gas-diffusion electrode to form a uniform electropolymerized
cobalt porphyrin polymeric network (g). Compared
with conventional physically deposited molecular catalysts,
,
 in situ electropolymerization avoids molecular aggregation and burial
of CoN4 active centers, while
maintaining nanoscale contact between CoN4 sites and Cu
nanocrystals. This intimate contact facilitates direct *CO intermediate
spillover from Co sites to the Cu surface.

In the EP-CoP/Cu
tandem system, CoN4 sites in EP-CoP
function as CO-generating components, selectively reducing CO2 to CO or *CO intermediates. The adjacent Cu nanocrystals
then serve as C–C coupling sites, converting transferred *CO
into C2+ products. As shown in h,i, this catalyst achieved a total current
density of 726 mA cm–2 at −0.9 V versus RHE
in a flow cell, with a C2+ Faradaic efficiency of 76.8%
and an ethylene Faradaic efficiency of 43%. Compared with bare Cu,
EP-CoP/Cu exhibited a higher C2+ partial current density
and a positively shifted ethylene formation onset potential, indicating
that the tightly coupled CoP/Cu tandem interface effectively enhances
*CO supply and subsequent C–C coupling kinetics.

Theoretical
calculations further revealed that the enhanced performance
of EP-CoP/Cu arises from the combined effects of interfacial electronic
structure and a *CO adsorption-energy gradient. The DFT results shown
in j,k indicate
that the CoP/Cu2O­(111) interface provides more favorable
reaction energetics for CO2-to-CO conversion. Interfacial
electron transfer stabilizes the *COOH intermediate, thereby accelerating
CO formation. Meanwhile, *CO adsorption is more favorable on Cu2O­(111) than on CoP, generating an adsorption-energy gradient
from Co sites to the Cu surface. This gradient facilitates the transfer
of *CO generated on CoN4 sites to neighboring Cu surfaces.
As the local *CO coverage on Cu increases, the free-energy barrier
for C–C coupling decreases, ultimately promoting ethylene and
other C2+ product formation. This study demonstrates that,
in molecular SAC–Cu tandem systems, high CO selectivity alone
is insufficient; constructing a tightly coupled molecular–metal
interface that promotes *CO spillover is also essential for improving
CO utilization efficiency and C2+ selectivity.

Ligand-Engineered Molecular SACs: Regulating
the Electronic Structure of Single-Atom Centers

Beyond interfacial
coupling and operating-potential alignment, the electronic structure
of the single-atom center itself is another critical factor governing
the catalytic efficiency of SAC–Cu tandem systems. Recent studies
have shown that, compared with conventional pyrolysis-derived M–N–C
single-atom catalysts, molecular SACs with well-defined coordination
structures enable more precise establishment of structure–activity
relationships and therefore serve as important model systems for elucidating
tandem CO2RR mechanisms.

Lu et al. employed thiophene-decorated
nickel porphyrins to construct a series of molecular catalysts with
well-defined Ni–N4 coordination structures, including
NiTPP, NiTThP, and NiT2ThP. This study systematically investigated
how sulfur-containing substituents influence the electronic structure
of single-atom centers and tandem CO2RR activity, as shown
in a. Compared with conventional sulfur-doped M–N–C
catalysts, in which the distribution and local structure of sulfur
are difficult to identify,
−

 these molecular models preserve
a fixed Ni–N4 coordination environment while varying
only the number of surrounding thiophene ligands. This design enables
direct investigation of ligand electronic effects on the single-atom
center.

The DFT results shown in b–d reveal that thiophene substituents
interact
strongly with the Cu(111) surface, causing sulfur-containing ligands
to preferentially approach the Cu surface and consequently modulate
the electronic structure of the central Ni atom. As the number of
thiophene substituents increases, the Ni 3d orbitals gradually shift
downward, and the Ni d-band center decreases progressively from NiTPP
to NiT2ThP. This trend exhibits a strong linear correlation
with the *COOH formation energy. Since *COOH formation is a key step
in CO2-to-CO conversion, lowering the Ni d-band center
effectively reduces the *COOH formation barrier and enhances CO-generation
activity. Reaction free-energy analysis further indicates that NiT2ThP/Cu exhibits the lowest barrier for *COOH formation while
maintaining moderate CO desorption energy, which is beneficial for
continuously supplying CO intermediates to neighboring Cu sites.

To verify this electronic-structure modulation mechanism, the authors
further employed synchrotron-based Ni K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge
structure (XANES), resonant inelastic X-ray scattering (RIXS), and
extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analyses to examine
the influence of thiophene ligands on the electronic state of the
Ni center (e–g). The results show that increasing the number of thiophene
ligands does not simply generate high-valence Ni3+ species.
Instead, thiophene substitution induces a pronounced ligand-hole character.
This ligand-hole effect modified the Ni 3d orbital occupancy, shifts
the Ni d-band center downward, and is consistent with the DFT results.
These findings indicate that ligand engineering does not merely alter
molecular geometry, but precisely regulates the electronic structure
of the single-atom center through ligand electronic interactions,
thereby improving CO2 activation capability.

h further
provides operando evidence for the stability and electronic evolution
of the Ni molecular sites during CO2RR. Operando Ni K-edge
XANES spectra collected under reaction conditions show that the absorption
edge and spectral features of NiTThP/Cu remain close to those of the
molecular Ni reference rather than shifting toward metallic Ni foil.
This indicates that the Ni center largely preserves its isolated molecular
coordination environment during CO2RR instead of being
reduced into metallic Ni species. Therefore, the enhanced tandem activity
can be attributed to ligand-regulated Ni–N4 molecular
sites, rather than the formation of Ni nanoparticles or metallic Ni
phases during operation. This result further supports the reliability
of the molecular structure–activity relationship established
using the thiophene-decorated Ni porphyrin model system.

In
addition to theoretical and X-ray spectroscopic analyses, operando
Raman spectroscopy in i provides direct evidence for tandem reaction behavior. As
the applied potential becomes more negative, signals associated with
Ni–CO and Cu-bound *CO can be simultaneously observed. Among
the investigated catalysts, NiTThP/Cu exhibits the most pronounced
*CO-related signals, indicating that molecular Ni sites can continuously
generate CO intermediates and effectively deliver them to neighboring
Cu surfaces for subsequent C–C coupling, rather than allowing
them to desorb directly into the electrolyte. This result confirms
that ligand engineering not only enhances CO-generation efficiency
but also improves CO intermediate utilization between SACs and Cu.

In flow-cell CO2RR tests (j,k), NiTThP/Cu exhibited the best tandem
catalytic performance, achieving a C2 Faradaic efficiency
of approximately 74.3% and a C2 partial current density
of approximately 445.8 mA cm–2 at high current density,
both significantly higher than those of bare Cu. Notably, although
NiT2ThP possesses the lowest *COOH formation energy, its
overall C2 performance did not continue to improve. This
observation indicates that maximizing CO-generation activity alone
does not necessarily lead to optimal tandem performance. Instead,
efficient tandem catalysis requires a balanced optimization of CO
formation, CO transfer, and subsequent C–C coupling kinetics
on Cu. Therefore, the best tandem performance originates from appropriate
electronic-structure modulation rather than simply maximizing CO-generation
activity.

Taken together, ligand engineering provides a design
strategy distinct
from conventional tuning of the central metal or support. By precisely
regulating the electronic environment around single-atom centers,
it becomes possible to establish a direct relationship among ligand
structure, electronic structure, and catalytic activity. The thiophene-induced
ligand-hole effect, Ni d-band modulation, and reduced *COOH formation
energy collectively promote CO generation and subsequent tandem C–C
coupling. This study not only establishes a structure–activity
relationship for molecular SAC–Cu tandem systems, but also
provides an important design principle for future SAC–Cu tandem
catalysts with predictable electronic structures and high C2+ selectivity.

Overall, the development of SAC–Cu tandem
catalysts has
evolved from simply introducing CO-generating sites toward the integrated
design of single-atom-center electronic structure, operating potential,
interfacial distance, and intermediate transfer. These studies collectively
indicate that efficient SAC–Cu tandem catalysis requires more
than highly selective CO-generating sites. Instead, it relies on the
integrated regulation of potential alignment, molecular–metal
interfacial coupling, single-site coordination stability, and efficient
*CO delivery to Cu for C–C coupling.

Conclusions and Perspectives

Cu-based
electrocatalysts remain the most representative catalytic
platform for CO2RR toward C2+ products because
of their unique ability to retain *CO intermediates and promote C–C
coupling. However, their practical performance is still limited by
complex reaction pathways, insufficient *CO coverage under high-current-density
operation, competing hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), and dynamic
catalyst reconstruction, which collectively restrict C2+ selectivity, production rate, and operational stability. Tandem
catalysis offers an effective strategy to address these limitations
by integrating CO-generating components with Cu-based C–C coupling
sites, thereby increasing local CO/*CO availability and promoting
multicarbon product formation.

In this review, we summarized
recent design strategies for Cu-based
tandem catalysts, including spatially separated tandem architectures,
Ag–Cu bimetallic interfaces and single-atom alloys, and SAC–Cu
tandem systems. These studies demonstrate that efficient tandem CO2RR cannot be achieved simply by increasing CO generation.
Instead, high-performance tandem catalysts require integrated regulation
of CO transport, retention, adsorption, intermediate migration, interfacial
electronic structure, operating-potential alignment, and C–C
coupling pathways. These design principles have enabled remarkable
improvements in C2+ selectivity and partial current density,
especially under flow-cell conditions.

Despite substantial advances,
Cu-based tandem CO2RR
continues to face several fundamental challenges that must be addressed
before its advantages can be translated into practical C2+ electrosynthesis. The first challenge concerns catalyst design
and differs substantially among tandem architectures. In spatially
separated systems, secondary catalytic components can efficiently
convert CO2 to CO; however, overall tandem performance
is determined not only by the rate of CO generation but also by the
ability to retain, transport, and utilize the generated CO at neighboring
Cu sites. In addition to minimizing CO loss over finite transport
distances, morphological and microenvironmental engineering can prolong
the residence time of CO within the catalyst layer and increase its
probability of adsorption and further reduction on Cu. Improving the
capture and utilization of tandem-generated CO therefore represents
an important direction for future catalyst design. Meanwhile, secondary
metal species or heteroatoms can modify the electronic structure of
Cu and regulate *H adsorption, *CO binding, and subsequent C–C
coupling.
−

 Integrating such Cu-site engineering with localized tandem CO generation
may provide an effective strategy for directing CO toward C2+ formation rather than allowing its desorption or escape from the
reaction interface.

For bimetallic tandem catalysts, the heterointerface
itself constitutes
an essential component of the active structure. Interfacial charge
redistribution, stabilization of favorable Cu+/Cu0 states, and modulation of *CO adsorption energetics can directly
influence intermediate utilization and C–C coupling. Importantly,
such interfaces may dynamically evolve under strongly reducing CO2RR conditions. Reduction-induced phase reconstruction, metal
migration, and changes in Cu oxidation state can continuously alter
the density, composition, and chemical nature of interfacial active
sites.
,
 Previous studies have further demonstrated
that interfacial composition, atomic arrangement, and structural stability
strongly influence intermediate adsorption and reaction selectivity.
−

 Future studies should therefore focus not only on constructing favorable
heterointerfaces initially, but also on understanding and controlling
their dynamic evolution under realistic operating conditions.

AgCu single-atom alloys (SAAs) present a distinct structural challenge
because their atomic-scale cooperative effects depend on simultaneously
maintaining isolated Ag sites and sufficiently large contiguous Cu
ensembles for C–C coupling. Loss of atomic Ag dispersion, surface
reconstruction, or excessive Ag incorporation may disrupt the optimized
Ag–Cu coordination environment, decrease the availability of
neighboring Cu ensembles, and diminish the short-range intermediate-transfer
advantages of the SAA architecture.
,
 SAC–Cu
tandem catalysts face analogous stability challenges associated with
isolated coordination sites, such as M–N4 centers,
whose reconstruction or aggregation during prolonged electrolysis
may alter both their CO-generation activity and their cooperative
interaction with Cu.
,
 Moreover, as Cu catalysts are
increasingly optimized for high-rate C–C coupling across broader
and more cathodic operating windows, matching the potential-dependent
CO-generation activity of the tandem component with that of Cu becomes
increasingly important. Future catalyst development should therefore
move beyond maximizing the number or intrinsic activity of CO-generating
sites and instead prioritize Cu active-site engineering, potential
matching, dynamically stable interfaces, and persistent cooperative
structures capable of sustaining efficient intermediate transfer.

The second major challenge concerns electrode and electrolyzer
configuration, which should be regarded as an integral component of
tandem-catalyst design rather than as an independent engineering consideration.
In spatially separated and layered tandem electrodes, the sequence
and thickness of the catalytic layers, catalyst loading, porosity,
ionomer/binder distribution, and wettability collectively determine
where CO is generated, how effectively it is retained, and whether
it reaches Cu before desorbing or diffusing out of the catalyst layer.
These factors become particularly important when tandem catalysts
are translated from laboratory-scale electrodes to gas-diffusion electrodes,
flow cells, and membrane-electrode assemblies. Furthermore, the electrolyte
environment can fundamentally alter tandem mechanisms by regulating
local pH, proton and water accessibility, *CO/*H competition, interfacial
water structure, and Cu surface speciation. Consequently, an architecture
optimized in alkaline or neutral flow cells may not retain the same
tandem synergy under acidic conditions or in zero-gap MEA configurations. Future development should therefore integrate
catalyst composition with electrode architecture, ionomer chemistry,
electrolyte composition, membrane selection, and gas–liquid
transport to preserve favorable intermediate-transfer pathways and
interfacial microenvironments at industrially relevant current densities.

The third challenge is the translation of tandem catalysis from
high-performing laboratory demonstrations to practical CO2 electrolysis. High C2+ Faradaic efficiency and partial
current density must ultimately be achieved without excessive cell
voltage or energy loss.
,
 Long-term stability
also remains a major bottleneck because catalyst reconstruction, loss
of active interfaces, aggregation of isolated metal sites, electrolyte
flooding,
,
 salt precipitation, and gas-diffusion-layer degradation can progressively decrease catalytic activity
and selectivity during high-current-density operation.
,
 Carbon utilization represents another critical system-level consideration, as carbonate formation, limited single-pass
CO2 conversion, and the simultaneous production of gaseous
and liquid C2+ products increase process complexity and
downstream separation requirements.
−

 Thus, the material-level advantages
of tandem catalysts should ultimately be evaluated together with their
energy efficiency, carbon utilization, and structural durability under
continuous operating conditions.

Overall, future advanced in
Cu-based tandem CO2RR will
require a transition from independently optimizing either the CO-generating
component or the Cu catalyst toward the rational design of the entire
cooperative catalytic interface and electrolyzer environment. Operando
characterization under high-current-density conditions will be particularly
important for tracking dynamic interface reconstruction, Cu oxidation-state
evolution, single-atom stability, and CO/*CO migration between neighboring
catalytic sites. In parallel, the evaluation of tandem systems should
move beyond peak Faradaic efficiency alone and include tandem-relevant
metrics such as C2+ partial current density, the C2+/CO ratio or CO-utilization efficiency, operating-potential
window, single-pass carbon efficiency, cell voltage, and long-term
preservation of the tandem interface. Establishing quantitative relationships
among catalyst architecture, interface evolution, intermediate transport,
electrolyte environment, and electrolyzer configuration will ultimately
be essential for identifying which tandem strategies provide genuine
advantages for scalable C2+ production.
