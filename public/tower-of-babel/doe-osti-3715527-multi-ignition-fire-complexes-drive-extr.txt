Objective: Multi-ignition fire complexes drive extreme fire years and impacts
The largest and fastest-spreading fires are causing the greatest impacts on people,
ecosystems, and energy systems. Yet the mechanisms driving variability and trends in large
fires remain poorly understood. Here, we investigate the causes and impacts of multi-
ignition wildfires and contrast them with single-ignition wildfires.
Approach
● We analyzed satellite thermal imagery of wildfire spread to classify fires as single-ignition
  or multi-ignition events in California and across boreal forest ecosystems in North
  America and Eurasia.
● We used a version of E3SM v3 with an ultra-high-resolution regionally refined grid to
  simulate the Dixie wildfire in California and quantify pyrocumulonimbus formation and
  its impacts on surface winds
Results/Impacts
● We show that merging of separate ignitions into multi-ignition complexes is a key
  process amplifying the destructive potential of fires across temperate and boreal
  regions
● Multi-ignition wildfires also generate stronger atmospheric feedbacks, produce more
  pyrocumulonimbus events, and strain firefighting capacity by dispersing suppression
  resources (Figure 1). E3SM is the first ESM to explicitly simulate pyrocumulonimbus fire
  dynamics.

Multi-ignition Fire Complexes Drive Extreme Fire
Years and Impacts
DOE Office of Science researchers demonstrate that merging separate
ignitions into multi-ignition complexes is a key process that amplifies the
destructive potential of wildfires.
The Science
The largest and fastest-spreading wildfires cause the greatest impacts on ecosystems, energy systems, and
human health, yet the mechanisms driving their variability remain poorly understood. A DOE-funded team
investigated the causes and impacts of "multi-ignition" wildfires—events where separate fire starts merge
into a single complex—contrasting them with single-ignition fires across California and boreal forest
ecosystems. Using a new version of the Energy Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM v3) with an ultra-high-
resolution regionally refined grid, the team simulated the Dixie wildfire to quantify the formation of
pyrocumulonimbus (pyroCb) clouds and their subsequent impacts on surface weather and winds.

The Impact
The study reveals that multi-ignition complexes are a primary driver of extreme fire years in both
temperate and boreal regions. These complexes generate significantly stronger atmospheric feedbacks
and produce a higher frequency of pyroCb events compared to single-ignition fires. Furthermore, the
analysis shows that multi-ignition fires strain firefighting capacity by dispersing suppression resources
across multiple active fronts. In this work, researchers used a new version of the E3SM model to explicitly
simulate the complex fire-atmosphere interactions that lead to pyroCb formation.

Summary
Wildfire management and risk assessment traditionally treat ignitions as independent events. However,
this research highlights that fire merging is a critical process that amplifies fire intensity and atmospheric
impacts. Using satellite thermal imagery and advanced modeling, the team found that interactions
between fire fronts can generate powerful updrafts and erratic surface winds, which further accelerate fire
spread and impede firefighting operations. For example, simulations of the Dixie fire demonstrated how
pyroCb development between fire fronts creates strong, variable wind patterns that would be missed by
lower-resolution models. These findings suggest that recognizing fire-merging dynamics is essential for
improving future wildfire predictions and developing more effective management strategies in a warming
climate.
