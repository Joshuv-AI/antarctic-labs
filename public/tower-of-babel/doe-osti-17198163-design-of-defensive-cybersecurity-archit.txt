ABSTRACT
This report presents the design of defensive cybersecurity architectures (DCSAs) for microreactors.
A DCSA is a cybersecurity design feature that places systems into security zones in a graded
approach according to the importance of the functions performed by the systems. DCSA design
efforts for advanced rectors may commence as early as the system-level design phase. This design
approach is consistent with the U.S. NRC Regulatory Guide 5.96 for advanced reactor cybersecurity
programs and enables designers to consider the effects of security-by-design (SeBD) features on
their DCSAs. Many microreactors rely on passive heat removal, fail-safe reactivity control, and
sealed cores. These features limit the cyber-accessible control surface to the balance of plant, the
supervisory controller, and the remote operations capability. This report characterizes the systems
of a microreactor, itemizes the design assumptions on which a DCSA is based, defines a strategy for
decoupling the nuclear island from the energy island, and provides a DCSA template for an
exemplar microreactor.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
A defensive cybersecurity architecture (DCSA) is a key cybersecurity design feature to prevent access
to attack pathways to those digital technologies that perform or support significant functions of
advanced reactors. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) defines a DCSA as the
“Arrangement of [digital] systems according to the design requirements, constraints and measures
that are to be imposed during the life cycle of a system, such that systems that perform identified
facility functions of significance to the safety and security of the facility and that are assigned to
computer security levels at the facility level have the required level of protection” [1]. A DCSA
applies a graded approach and implements defense-in-depth. As a result, it ensures that the
adversary must overcome multiple, diverse, and independent measures before completing an attack.
This report is the third in a series of DCSA design reports. The previous reports developed DCSA
templates for a high-temperature, gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) and a sodium-cooled fast reactor
(SFR) [2, 3]. This report applies the same approach to microreactors. The analysis is consistent with
the Tiered Cybersecurity Analysis (TCA) detailed in U.S. NRC Regulatory Guide 5.96, "Establishing
Cybersecurity Programs for Commercial Nuclear Plants Licensed Under 10 CFR Part 53" [4]. The
TCA leverages the security-by-design (SeBD) features of the plant as the foundation of cybersecurity
analysis. A DCSA designed under this approach denies the adversary access to the plant functions
needed to cause an accident sequence that is unmitigated by the plant's physical design.
Microreactors differ from the reactors treated in the previous reports in ways that affect DCSA
design. Their cores are factory-assembled, sealed, and not refueled on site. They rely on passive heat
removal and on reactivity control that fails safe on loss of power. They are designed for autonomous
or semi-autonomous operation with remote monitoring and control. As such, a microreactor has
fewer active systems than an HTGR or SFR and a smaller cyber-accessible control surface. In the
reference configuration, the primary coolant circulates by natural convection, decay heat is removed
by natural circulation of air, and the reactor protection system trips the reactor by removing power
from the reactivity control actuators. The residual control surface is concentrated in the secondary
coolant loop, the power conversion system, the distributed control system (DCS), and the remote
monitoring and control link.
At the time of publication, no microreactor has been constructed for commercial operation in the
U.S. This report examines the microreactor designs in development for which sufficient public
documentation was available. From these designs, the systems found in microreactors are described
along with their functions, their interdependencies, and their fundamental sensors and actuators.
This report also considers the decoupling of the nuclear island (NI) from the energy island (EI) as a
design strategy. Physical decoupling limits the pathways through which a disturbance on the EI
reaches the NI. Cyber decoupling extends this separation into the digital domain. No digital control
path from the EI reaches the safety systems, and data flows from the NI to the EI are unidirectional.
Security levels are assigned to functions based on their importance to plant safety. Systems that
perform multiple functions are placed in a zone based on the security level assigned to the system's
most important function. Based on the systems' functions, systems are categorized as safety-related
(SR), non-safety related with special treatment (NSRST), or non-safety related with no special
treatment (NST). Security levels are assigned based on these classifications. The resulting DCSA
template is shown in Figure 1. This DCSA template is consistent with both the RG 5.71 approach
and the RG 5.96 approach.

 9

Security level 1 consists of a zone containing the information technology (IT) network, business
systems, and engineering systems. Systems in this level have access to the Internet via a firewall and
wireless networks are permitted. Portable media and mobile devices (PMMD) are widely used within
this security level.
Security level 2 consists of three zones containing authorized document management systems, work
control systems, and the engineering historian. PMMD are used within these zones. Bidirectional
wired network communication through a firewall is permitted between security levels 1 and 2.
Security level 3 consists of several zones containing both NSRST and NST plant systems and the
supervisory control systems. The local human-machine interface (HMI) and the DCS serve as
supervisory controllers. A remote HMI located off site is also assigned to security level 3 and
connects to the supervisory control zones through firewalls at the site boundary. Any PMMD
brought from a lower security zone to a security level 3 zone must first be processed through a
portable media and mobile device scanner. Wired network communication into security level 2 from
security level 3 is permitted, but security level 2 is only permitted to send handshaking or
acknowledgement signals to security level 3.
Security level 4 consists of two zones containing the plant SR systems: the reactor protection system
and the reserve shutdown system. Analog signals are used for communication between them. A
third security zone contains the plant physical protection system. Any PMMD brought into security
level 4 must first be scanned. One-way communication enforced by a data diode is permitted from
security level 4 to security levels 3 and 2. No digital conduit enters security level 4 from any other
level, including the remote HMI.
This report was written to demonstrate DCSA design approaches and to provide a template DCSA
design for a microreactor to be available for industry use. It is important to note that the DCSA
design template and the decoupling strategy provided in this report are intended to serve as starting
points for AR designers and are not prescriptive. Further optimization may be valuable given the
unique design and performance requirements of the plant. Passive cybersecurity controls can be
assigned to each security level for the physical access, wired, wireless, and PMMD attack pathways,
as was demonstrated in the SFR report. An area for potential future work is the energy island sub-
architecture and the interface catalog between the islands, which complete the decoupling strategy.

1. INTRODUCTION
A defensive cybersecurity architecture (DCSA) is a key cybersecurity design feature to prevent access
to attack pathways to those digital technologies that perform or support significant functions of
advanced reactors (ARs). The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) defines a DCSA as the
“Arrangement of [digital] systems according to the design requirements, constraints and measures
that are to be imposed during the life cycle of a system, such that systems that perform identified
facility functions of significance to the safety and security of the facility and that are assigned to
computer security levels at the facility level have the required level of protection” [1]. A DCSA aims
to apply a graded approach and implement defense-in-depth by providing sufficient protection to
functions important to safety, security, or safeguards (3S). A DCSA needs to ensure that the
adversary must overcome multiple, diverse, and independent measures of increasing robustness
prior to successfully completing an attack.
Most nuclear power plants (NPPs) in the U.S. commercial fleet were designed, implemented, and
initially operated without considerations for cybersecurity. The absence of cybersecurity design
features resulted in cybersecurity controls being “wrapped-around” systems to prevent access of
adversaries to significant and vulnerable components of these systems. The existing fleet relies on a
combination of strict physical protection, isolation, and air-gaps to reduce cybersecurity risks to
meet U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) guidance. Air-gapped systems require strong on-
site physical protection, access control, and extensive measures to track and control the use of
portable media and mobile devices. This often results in a single large layer within the DCSA that
requires extra effort to physically protect networks and components and to manage access control.
AR designers can consider cybersecurity from the start of the design process to avoid the wrap
around security measures often applied for the existing fleet. Designers are considering effective
cybersecurity as a fundamental part of the design basis of the reactor. This provides an opportunity
to potentially reduce costs and effort in establishing effective cybersecurity programs via integration
of cybersecurity analysis with the design process.
This report was written to demonstrate DCSA design approaches and to provide a template DCSA
design for a microreactor to be available for industry use. This report is the third in a series. It is
similar in structure to the previous reports that developed DCSA designs for a high-temperature,
gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) and a sodium-cooled fast reactors (SFR) [2, 3]. Microreactors differ
from those designs in ways that impact DCSA design decisions. Their passive safety features limit
the consequences available to a cyber adversary and their small number of active systems limits the
cyber accessible control surface. Moreover, their reliance on remote monitoring and control
introduces a communication pathway that the existing fleet has not implemented or considered. It is
important to note that the DCSA design template provided in this report is intended to serve as a
starting point for AR designers and is not prescriptive. Further considerations and decisions of the
DCSA may be valuable given the unique design and performance requirement of the plant.
This report evaluates the instrumentation and control (I&C) architecture and interdependencies of a
microreactor to derive DCSA passive requirements. At the time of publication, no microreactor has
been constructed for commercial operation in the U.S. As such, we examined the microreactor
designs in development for which sufficient public documentation was available. The systems
found in these designs, their functions, and their interdependencies are described in Section 4.
The analysis of the I&C architecture and interdependencies is consistent with the Tiered
Cybersecurity Analysis (TCA) detailed in the U.S. NRC Regulatory Guide 5.96, “Establishing
Cybersecurity Programs for Commercial Nuclear Plants Licensed Under 10 CFR 53” [4]. The TCA

leverages the security-by-design (SeBD) features of the plant as the foundation of cybersecurity
analysis. A DCSA designed as part of the TCA approach denies the adversary access to the plant
functions needed to cause an accident sequence that is unmitigated by the physical design of the
plant. This report also addresses the decoupling of the nuclear island from the energy island, which
the previous DCSA SFR report identified as a topic for further analysis [3].
This report aims to develop requirements for passive DCSA measures by:
 • Describing the systems generally found in microreactor designs, their functions, their

 • Itemizing the plant design assumptions on which a microreactor DCSA design would be

 • Identify, for each system, the cyber-accessible control surface that remains available to an

 • Defining physical and cyber decoupling strategies that limit the pathways by which a
 compromise of the energy island can reach the nuclear island.

2. BACKGROUND
This section provides a conceptual overview of AR SeBD considerations, DCSAs, and defensive
cybersecurity strategies. Much of this section is quoted or based on material in the previous SFR
and HTGR DCSA report [2, 3]. Sections 2.1 is updated to reflect the U.S. NRC issuing Regulatory
Guide 5.96 as final [5]. As such, the TCA material presented is no longer pre-decisional. Section 2.3
is updated to define a semantic model of the TCA. Sections 2.3 through 2.5 are minimally quoted
from [2] because they provide essential background material on SeBD for this report. Please refer to
[2] for expanded versions of these sections.

2.1. The Tiered Cybersecurity Analysis (TCA)
Under the United States Nuclear Regulatory Commission (US NRC) Regulatory Guide 5.71 [6],
licensees of light water reactors (LWRs) have been required to broadly apply a large set of technical
and operational cybersecurity controls to all identified critical digital assets (CDAs). For ARs this
asset-centric approach places a large time and resource burden on the licensee and does not allow
the licensee the flexibility to prioritize the systems with the greatest potential for physical harm. The
U.S. NRC staff has issued 10 CFR Part 53, “Risk-Informed, Technology-Inclusive Regulatory
Framework for Commercial Nuclear Plants,” as a final rule [7]. This provides an optional,
technology-inclusive licensing framework for commercial nuclear plants.
The U.S. NRC has issued Regulatory Guide 5.96, Revision 0 “Establishing Cybersecurity Programs
for Commercial Nuclear Plants Licensed Under 10 CFR Part 53,” which finalizes the draft guide
DG-5075 referenced in the prior DCSA reports [4]. The guide describes a method acceptable to the
NRC staff for meeting the cybersecurity requirements of 10 CFR 73.110 [8]. The methodology is
referred to in this report as the Tiered Cybersecurity Analysis (TCA). The TCA is a cybersecurity
assessment methodology that aligns domestic standards, international standards, and technical
guidance to select SeBD requirements to develop defensive network architectures and apply
effective cybersecurity controls [2, 3].
The TCA is composed of three tiers as shown in Figure 2. Tier 1 is titled Design Analysis, which
focuses on evaluating SeBD features to eliminate or mitigate accident sequences caused by a cyber-
adversary. Tier 2, Denial of Access, focuses on developing passive DCSA features and passive
cybersecurity plan (CSP) controls. Finally, Tier 2 is the Denial of Task, which focuses on preventing
the adversary from conducting specific tasks needed to conduct attacks that were not eliminated by
SeBD or prevented by DCSA features. Each tier is described in more detail in the following section.
For more information on the TCA, please refer to RG 5.96 [4] or the previous DCSA reports [2, 3].

2.1.1. Tier 1 Analysis
The goal of Design Analysis is to evaluate the plant’s safety design features and determine if they can
be credited as SeBD features. Design features analyzed and verified to prevent an attack from
leading to an unacceptable consequence from a specific scenario can be credited. As a result, this
eliminates the need for a more detailed analysis of the scenario. In such cases, the design feature
eliminates or avoids the evaluated impact(s) of an attack (e.g. radiological sabotage). Alternatively,
some design features may delay or reduce an attack’s impact. These design features are valuable to
the security of the plant, but scenarios associated with these measures would still require Tier 2
analysis because the impact is not eliminated or avoided.
Tier 1 analysis is performed under the assumption that the adversary that is limited only by the
physical design features of the plant design. This adversary is assumed to have access to any digital
system, component, or network in the plant, and is assumed to be capable of implementing any
control action within the capability of the system. Tier 1 findings that a scenario is mitigated by
SeBD requirements cannot be invalidated by changes to the design basis threat because the
adversary has already been assumed to have full control over the control surface of the plant.
Supporting methodologies include Systems-Theoretic Process Analysis, analysis of the plant safety
basis, and controlled process analysis [10]. Modeling and simulation are useful tools for conducting
Tier 1 analysis [11, 12, 13, 14]

2.1.2. Tier 2 Analysis
The goal of Tier 2 analysis is “Denial of (adversary) Access” to functions (and associated systems)
important to a set of scenarios with unacceptable consequences that are not addressed in Tier 1. Tier
2 evaluates adversary attack pathways1 and identifies passive measures to deny adversary access to
system and network.
Adversary assumptions for Tier 2 include being able to achieve their objective if they gain access to
the appropriate systems. Tier 1 scenarios and safety analyses are taken as inputs and used to identify
adversary functional scenarios associated with unacceptable consequences. One method to represent
attack sequences and bound the scope of scenarios is to use traditional PRA event trees [15]. Each

 18

plant function that must operate to mitigate an accident should be considered. This analysis should
examine each system in the sequence of plant functions required for accident mitigation and identify
available pathways for an adversary. The results of Tier 2 analysis are passive or deterministic DCSA
or cybersecurity plan (CSP) elements. The analysis in this report aligns with Tier 2 analysis.

2.1.3. Tier 3 Analysis
The goal of “Denial of Task” Analysis is to provide risk-informed control measures to adversary
functional scenarios that are not mitigated by the passive DCSA and CSP elements identified in Tier
2. In Tier 3, it is assumed that the adversary has obtained the access required to achieve their
objective and control measures must be implemented to prevent the adversary from completing
their objective. Generally, a body of controls may consist of baseline controls and risk-informed
controls. Baseline controls apply broadly and provide information security assurance while risk
informed controls treat a specific identified risk. There are several methods that can be leveraged to
identify applicable risk-informed controls (e.g., combining control action modeling using Systems-
Theoretic Process Analysis and adversary sequence modeling using attack tree modeling).

2.2. Semantic Modeling of the Tiered Cybersecurity Analysis
When licensees apply the TCA using specific analysis methods and tools, they must make implicit
decisions about what information is produced at each tier and what carries forward when scenarios
remain unresolved. Without an explicit model of these elements and their relationships, there is a
risk of inconsistent application and incomplete traceability. To address this, this report presents a
semantic model of the TCA that defines the essential elements at each tier, their relationships, and
the tier-to-tier information flow, which was designed in [16]. The model enables licensees to map
their analysis artifacts to the TCA tiers and identify what information must be produced and carried
forward at each tier boundary.
A semantic model is a formal representation of domain knowledge that defines concepts as entities,
the relationships between them, and the constraints that govern their structure [17] These models
originate in database and information science, where knowledge graphs and ontology-based
approaches have been used to capture the meaning of data rather than its storage format. The
benefits of using semantic modeling are a shared vocabulary that reduces ambiguity around a
concept or a collection of data by establishing explicit structural relationships. For more information

2.4. Defensive Cybersecurity Architectures
The U.S. NRC RG 5.71 requires a site cybersecurity defensive strategy that employs defense-in-

acceptable method is a defensive architecture that establishes formal communication boundaries, or
security levels, within which defensive measures are deployed to detect, prevent, delay, mitigate, and
recover from cyberattacks. RG 5.71 gives as an example a series of concentric defensive levels of
increasing security. These levels conceptually correspond to the existing physical security areas at a
facility, such as the vital area, protected area, owner-controlled area, corporate-accessible area, and
public area [22].

Several key definitions are summarized below from NSS 17-T.
 • Facility Function: “a coordinated set of actions and processes that need to be performed at a
 nuclear facility” [1].
 • Security Level: “a designation that indicates the degree of security protection required for a

 • System: “a set of components which interact according to a design so as to perform a
 specific (active) function, in which an element of the system can be another system, called a

 • Security Zone: “a logical and/or physical grouping of digital assets that are assigned to the

2.5. Defensive Cybersecurity Strategies
DCSA requirements are associated with passive measures focusing on denial of adversary access
through the eliminating, mitigating, or controlling attack pathways. There are five commonly
accepted attack pathways:
 1. Physical Access
 2. Wired Network Connectivity
 3. Wireless Network Connectivity
 4. Portable Media and Mobile Device
 5. Supply Chain
This report excludes supply chain attack pathway due to the need to impose requirements on
external parties. These requirements are not reflected in passive DCSA elements, although active
DCSA requirements may detect supply chain compromises.
The four types of defensive strategies detailed below provide key aspects of both passive and active
defense [25, 26]. For more information on these strategies, please refer to the previous DCSA
reports [2, 3].
 1. Fortification: A defensive barrier or other reinforcement built to strengthen a function,
 system, or zone against a malicious act. Fortification may include physical barriers and
 structures such as walls, hardened doors, or fences, or technical barriers such as
 cryptographic primitives, data diodes, and network segmentation. Approaches that enhance
 fortification are system hardening such as the removal of unnecessary ports and services.
 Unnecessary system services must be removed or disabled from devices to reinforce them
 against vulnerabilities in these services [26].
 2. Chokepoint: A strategic narrow route or gateway linking one zone, area, or network, to
 another. Chokepoints may include wired conduits between zones, entry control points to
 protected areas, and access points that connect adjacent networks. Chokepoints are most
 effective when all traffic is first forced to pass through it and provide a key location to
 deploy both authentication and detection technical measures. For example, network
 intrusion detection at the only chokepoint to a zone could monitor all communications
 entering and exiting that zone [26].
 3. Area or Access Control: The selective restriction of either access or denial to a place or
 other resources. Access control aims to prevent adversarial access to either an internal area
 or components of zones and systems. Access control can be passive, such as USB port
 blockers, walls, locked doors, and filtering, or active, such as intrusion protection systems or
 transitions between operating modes [26].
 4. Deception: False impersonation or mimicry of attractive targets to influence an adversary to
 attack them in place of real targets. This strategy involves the creation of decoys and
 honeypots to trick and trap adversaries, gain valuable threat intelligence, and update active

 25

 defense measures. Deception may enable the disclosure and identification of threat actors’
 locations, names, and other information. Deception is generally not used within the existing
 fleet, but if wireless communications were to be implemented it would be beneficial to
 provide false wireless networks, among other targets, to gain situational awareness of
 potential local adversary activity. An active directory decoy could just be a dummy file
 system that notifies analysts when accessed [26].
These types of defensive strategies are combined into an overall plan for defense-in-depth to meet
the necessary requirements to ensure sufficient cybersecurity.
The U.S. NRC RG 5.71 outlines two requirements for a defensive architecture and defensive
strategies. The defensive architecture prevents access to successive layers of security. Access
prevention is generally achieved through passive features. The defensive strategy provides detection,
protection, and response. These capabilities are generally achieved through active features [22].
These two requirements correspond to successive tiers of the TCA. Tier 2 selects the passive
features that deny adversary access. Tier 3 selects the active features that detect, protect against, and
respond to attacks that are not denied at Tier 2 [4]. This resembles the U.S. NRC’s defense-in-depth
concepts as shown in Figure 10. The three defensive strategies shown within this concept can be
found in the previous DCSA reports [2, 3].

 Figure 10. U.S. NRC’s Defense-in-Depth Concept [27]

defensive strategies:
 1. Requires: The implementation of one strategy necessitates the implementation of another for
 effectiveness.
 2. Enhances: The implementation of one strategy improves the effectiveness of another.

 3. Weakens: The implementation of one strategy undermines or negates the effectiveness of

developed on implementation assumptions, which are discussed in greater detail below.

 Figure 11. Defensive Strategies and their Relationships [26].

Chokepoints require and weaken fortification. Chokepoints require fortification because the strategic
narrow route provided by a chokepoint cannot exist without a restriction of movement in the area
adjacent to the chokepoint. Consider an asset surrounded by a wall with one small gap serving as a
chokepoint to funnel all free motion through a single known pathway. The gap cannot exist without
the wall. Chokepoints weaken fortification because they present an opportunity for the adversary to
bypass the established barrier.
Fortification enhances chokepoints by increasing the difficulty of the adversary to circumvent the
chokepoint. Consider two applications of chokepoints and fortification in the physical domain: (1) a
chain-link fence with a gate and (2) a concrete wall with an identical gate. An adversary would be
able to circumvent the gate more easily in the first scenario than the second, therefore the increased
fortification provided by the concrete wall enhances the chokepoint value provided by the gate.
Access control weakens fortification. The rationale for this relationship is identical to that for the
weakening relationship between chokepoints and fortification. Access control presents an
opportunity for the adversary to bypass the established barrier. 30 Fortification enhances access
control. The rationale for this relationship is identical to that for the enhancing relationship between
fortification and chokepoints. Increases in fortification adjacent to the access control point increases
the difficult of the adversary to bypass access control.
Access control enhances a chokepoint. One purpose of a chokepoint is to slow down the spread of
the adversary through a network. Access control supports this purpose by providing a means to
verify that the individual passing through a chokepoint is authorized to do so. If an access control
measure is implemented at a chokepoint, an adversary without authorized credentials or the means
to bypass the access control measure would be stopped, thereby achieving the purpose of the
chokepoint.
Chokepoints enhance access control. An effective chokepoint limits the adversary’s ability to bypass
the access control mechanism. Consider two scenarios: (1) a guard checking badges at a 2 ft. gap in a
fence and (2) a guard checking badges at a 20 ft. gap in a fence. An adversary would be able to

 27

circumvent the gate more easily in the first scenario than in the second, therefore the increased
chokepoint efficacy provided by the smaller gap enhances the access control provided by the guard.
Note that this scenario also demonstrates the enhancing relationship between fortification and
access control. A chokepoint can also enhance access control by establishing a location where an
access control mechanism is implemented. A chokepoint is the means to define the path/location
where an individual must verify identity.
Deception enhances fortification, access control, and chokepoints. These enhancing relationships
exist because deception provides intelligence for the system defender to improve the
implementation of the defensive strategies. If adversary activity is identified in the deception
environment, the defender may be able to identify how the adversary defeated/circumvented the
fortification, chokepoint, and/or access control strategies implemented in the deception
environment, and address those vulnerabilities in the real system. The defender may also identify
what targets are valued by the adversary, and enhance the defensive strategies employed around the
legitimate targets. This strategy becomes more effective as the deception environment approaches
the adversary’s expectations of the true operational environment and as the deception environment
approaches the legitimate environment. If the deception environment is too similar to the legitimate
environment, the adversary may be able to gather offensive intelligence to be used in future attacks.

3. DECOUPLING STRATEGIES
Decoupling strategies for ARs are strongly related to DCSA design. In this report we distinguish
between physical decoupling and cyber decoupling. Physical decoupling limits the process,
electrical, and control pathways, thereby ensuring that disturbances between plant systems do not
propagate. Cyber decoupling limits the digital pathways and mitigates compromises of one system
from reaching another. The two are complementary in the TCA. Physical decoupling determines
which consequences remain available to an adversary who controls a portion of the plant. Cyber
decoupling determines the difficultly of obtaining that control.
Several AR architectures separate the plant into a nuclear island (NI) and an energy island (EI).
These islands are coupled through an intermediate thermal buffer (e.g., molten-salt energy storage).
The buffer and its associated inventory management break the real-time coupling between reactor
power and grid demand. This enables the reactor to remain at steady power while the EI absorbs
load-following and dispatch transients. From a licensing perspective, TerraPower has pursued an EI
concept focused on independence strategy intended to support risk-informed treatment of EI
systems as non-safety while still preserving NI safety. The objective is to reduce the requirements
related to nuclear quality that are imposed on conventional energy-conversion systems, while still
preserving NI safety [28, 29].
The cyber threat environment for operational technology (OT) and industrial control systems (ICS)
motivates the treatment of decoupling. Recent analyses emphasize that the civil nuclear sector faces
persistent cybersecurity threats and that OT/ICS environments require controls that preserve
integrity and availability of critical process functions [30].
The existing nuclear fleet frequently relies on perimeter-focused controls such as air-gaps, strict
physical protection, and portable media controls to meet cybersecurity expectations. While effective
as compensatory barriers, this tends to concentrate security controls at logical and physical
boundaries. This can impede modernization use-cases that ARs seek to rely on (e.g., modular
construction, reduced staffing models, and more software-defined supervisory control) [2].
Physical decoupling offers a different approach for cybersecurity applications for two reasons. First,
it narrows the set of pathways by which EI disturbances can affect the NI. Failures on the EI are
expected to be expressed to the NI primarily through a small set of bounded process interactions at
the thermal interface, such as loss of cold-salt inventory or adverse salt temperatures, rather than
through fast, closed-loop command-and-control between islands [28]. Second, it creates an
opportunity to align cyber boundaries with the process boundary. As a result, this makes the NI/EI
interface an explicit design object rather than a by-product of network integration [2].

3.1. Purpose
This report defines a cyber decoupling strategy that mirrors NI/EI physical decoupling and the
plant’s defense-in-depth (DiD) posture. The goal is to design a state in which EI-originated cyber
compromise cannot directly reach or degrade NI safety functions. In this state, EI cyber failures are
resolved through a small, analyzable set of NI-recognized initiating conditions at the thermal
interface or ideally have no consequence for the NI at all [28] [29]. As such, we emphasize design-
time DCSA patterns for Security Level 3 (SL3) and Security Level 4 (SL4) systems and zones, which
reflect the graded approach in current regulatory guidance for advanced reactors [1] [2] [31] [5].
A secondary aim is to make the decoupling argument reviewable: interface definitions, security-level
assignments, and cross-domain data flows must be traceable to functional allocations and licensing

 29

assumptions. Requirements traceability, configuration control, and management of interface
requirements are central to digital I&C programs. These practices keep the cyber boundary aligned
with the plant boundary as the design evolves [32].
The discussion applies to advanced reactors that employ an NI/EI split and that pursue a risk-
informed, performance-based licensing basis consistent with the NEI 18-04 methodology and its
NRC endorsement via RG 1.233 [27] [33]. The strategy assumes graded SSC classifications (e.g.,
Safety-Related; Non-Safety-Related with Special Treatment (NSRST); and Non-Safety, no Special
Treatment (NST)) and maps those classifications to graded cybersecurity controls and zones [27]
[31] [1] [2].
The scope is design-centric: it focuses on architectural choices (zoning, interface minimization,
directionality constraints, platform isolation) rather than operational program details. The intent is
not to replace programmatic requirements. The intent is to reduce the number and complexity of
CDAs and pathways that must be defended at the highest security levels by eliminating them in the
architecture [2]. Where standards and guides are referenced, they are used to motivate requirements
and patterns; the specific compliance posture must be established in the plant cybersecurity plan and
licensing submittals.

3.2. Cybersecurity Foundations for NI/EI Decoupling

3.2.1. Defensive Computer Security Architecture (DCSA)
A DCSA is an organizing construct that couples (i) an explicit mapping from facility functions to
digital systems and assets, with (ii) a graded set of security levels and zones, and (iii) engineered
boundaries that enforce defense in depth across those zones. In the Sandia HTGR template, the
DCSA is treated as a design feature placed “around” the functions and systems that matter most,
enabling cyber protections to be optimized alongside the reactor design rather than retrofitted [2].
A useful way to view DCSA is as a set of mutually reinforcing defensive strategies. The HTGR
report groups controls into fortification (hardening critical digital assets), chokepoints (constraining
access paths to critical systems), and anti-access/area denial (preventing unauthorized presence
within sensitive enclaves), with the combined effect producing defense in depth [2]. For NI/EI
architectures, “chokepoints” are naturally anchored at the NI/EI interface: the design goal is to
collapse cross-island connectivity into a small number of explicit, high-assurance gateways that can
be justified as necessary and sufficient.
DCSA design begins with functional decomposition and facility characterization: identify facility
functions; determine functional interdependencies, timeliness, and accuracy requirements; identify
which systems implement those functions; then assign systems and zones to security levels via
consequence-informed grading. The IAEA guidance emphasizes both graded requirements and
defense in depth “between layers” and “within a layer,” reinforcing that zoning is not only a
perimeter concept but also an internal segregation mechanism [1] [2] [31].

3.2.2. Security Levels 3 and 4
In the graded model applied in Sandia’s HTGR DCSA template, Security Level 3 (SL3) encompasses
supervisory control and plant operational systems that may include both NSRST and NST systems,
along with the main control room HMI and supervisory controllers such as an investment
protection system and a distributed control system. The template highlights that information
dependencies between supervisory controllers and subordinate systems can drive placement of

 30

otherwise lower-consequence systems into SL3 to prevent cross-zone compromise via transitive
trust. It also demonstrates a pragmatic rule: where SL3 exchanges with a lower security level,
acknowledgements may be constrained to minimal handshake semantics to prevent lower-level
influence on higher-level logic [2].
Security Level 4 (SL4) is reserved for safety-related (SR) systems as illustrated in the HTGR template
with distinct zones for the reactor protection system and the reserve shutdown system. Within that
template, SL4-to-SL3 (and SL4-to-SL2) communication is permitted only as one-way signaling
enforced by a data diode, and intra-SL4 communications are implemented via analog signaling where
practical. This reflects a broader principle for NI/EI designs: safety platforms should be architected
to operate deterministically, with the intent of minimizing externally reachable attack surface, and
with any outward data fan-out constrained to unidirectional channels [2].
For NI/EI plants, SL3 and SL4 are best interpreted as follows. SL4 comprises NI protection and
shutdown functions (e.g., reactor trip, diverse shutdown, and associated actuation logic)
implemented on safety platforms with engineered independence. SL3 comprises (a) NI operational
systems with safety significance or investment protection value (e.g., supervisory control associated
with intermediate-loop operations), and (b) EI process control for thermal storage, power
conversion, and dispatch. The architectural requirement is not only SL4 isolation, but also the
prevention of lateral movement across SL3 zones (NI-SL3 to EI-SL3) through explicit segmentation
and constrained protocols [2] [6].

3.2.3. Regulatory and Standards Context
U.S. nuclear cybersecurity guidance is centered around the high-assurance objective of protecting
digital systems associated with safety, security, and emergency preparedness functions against cyber-
attacks up to and including the design basis threat [34] [35]. RG 5.71 articulates an approach for
meeting 10 CFR 73.54 by establishing a defensive architecture and security controls tailored to ICS
environments, drawing on NIST controls and emphasizing layered protections [36, 6] [37]. Even
when advanced reactor guidance evolves beyond RG 5.71’s LWR-centric framing, its core
architectural premise—segmentation, controlled pathways, and defense in depth—remains directly
applicable to NI/EI decoupling [6].
For advanced reactors licensed under a technology-inclusive, risk-informed framework, NRC has
described a tiered analysis approach to cybersecurity and related Part 53 rulemaking materials [4]. It
establishes, implements, and maintains a cybersecurity program meeting proposed requirements for
protection of digital computer and communication systems and networks [4]. Sandia’s HTGR report
explicitly aligns its DCSA design process with this tiered approach, treating SeBD features as
foundational and using accident sequence logic (e.g., event trees) to identify where passive
architectural measures can deny adversary access to safety-significant functions.
Internationally, the IAEA’s nuclear security guidance emphasizes a graded approach, defense in
depth, and the use of computer security levels and zones as mechanisms for organizing protections
proportionate to consequence [1] [31]. The NSS online interface for NSS No. 42-G and NSS No.
17-T (Rev. 1) highlights the assignment of systems performing facility functions to levels,
specification of a DCSA, and explicit requirements for both graded protections and defense in
depth. These concepts are consistent with the DCSA patterns used here, even when specific
numeric level mappings differ between national implementations [31] [1] [2].

3.3. Cybersecurity Decoupling Strategy and Requirements (NI vs EI)

3.3.1. Strategy Objectives
The first objective is to protect against EI cyber compromise non-safety-significant by construction
by preserving separation in the cyber domain. This can be done by ensuring EI-originated cyber
effects cannot gain direct control authority over NI safety actuation, even if EI supervisory control
and dispatch systems are fully compromised. Consistent with the SeBD/DCSA premise, the
architecture must deny an adversary access to the plant functions needed to cause an accident
sequence not mitigated by the plant’s physical design [2]. Accordingly, EI-originated cyber events
must not be able to introduce new high-consequence beyond-design-basis events not enveloped by
the safety case, nor degrade NI safety functions performed by SR systems.
The second objective is to bound NI exposure to EI-originated signals by constraining EI to NI
dependencies to a small, explicitly justified measurement set whose loss, corruption, or delay is
treated conservatively [29] [2]. This objective adopts graded controls organized through levels/zones
and defense-in-depth layers and treats system interdependencies and conduits as explicit design
objects requiring specification and control [31] [1]. Practically, EI-provided measurements should be
treated as potentially untrustworthy inputs with their failure modes are mapped into bounded NI-
side initiating conditions and conservative NI responses, rather than allowing open-ended control
channels that could bypass process constraints [28] [29].
The third objective is to eliminate EI write-access to SL4 platforms by enforcing one-way (or
otherwise non-routable) communication patterns such that SL4 does not accept routable protocol
traffic from EI or other SL3 systems [2]. This follows the one-way SL4-to-lower signaling patterns
used in the Sandia HTGR DCSA template and reflects the DCSA preference for engineered
boundaries that prevent lower-level influence on higher-consequence logic [2]. Where outward
signaling from SL4 is necessary for operational awareness, exports are constrained to unidirectional
mechanisms rather than bidirectional integration, consistent with NRC guidance emphasizing
defensive architectures and controlled pathways for protected digital assets [6].
The fourth objective is to align cyber zones with physical and licensing-relevant separation by
minimizing shared infrastructure and transitive trust relationships across islands, consistent with
zone-based graded security concepts [1] [31] and with the DCSA treatment of zones and conduits as
stable, design-time artifacts that must remain robust as the design evolves [2]. Because architectural
drift is a principal failure mode for long-lived OT systems, this objective also requires decision
traceability and configuration control practices that keep interface definitions, zoning decisions, and
conduit justifications reviewable over time [32]. Where SL3 coordination is retained for non-safety
objectives (e.g., availability, equipment protection, economic dispatch), it must remain subordinate
to—and unable to erode—the NI’s unilateral protection authority implemented in SL4.

3.3.2. Functional Allocation Across Islands and Security Levels
Functional allocation is the primary lever for cyber decoupling. The conservative rule is: safety
functions are allocated exclusively to NI SL4 systems; NSRST functions and investment protection
functions that influence reactor conditions (but are not safety actuation) are allocated to NI SL3;
dispatch, economic optimization, and most EI process control remain in EI SL3 (or lower, where
justified) [2] [31] [27]. This allocation aligns with the decoupling concept in which the EI is not
credited for safety and is treated as NST in the licensing basis under the LMP-style analysis [28] [27].

 32

Within SL3, zoning must prevent both cross-island compromise and “transitive trust” compromises
where a less critical subordinate system becomes a pivot point into a supervisory control. The
HTGR DCSA template illustrates why this matters: information dependencies between supervisory
and subordinate systems can force systems upward into higher security levels unless additional
controls break the dependency or eliminate the path [2]. Translating this to NI/EI architectures
suggests separate SL3 zones for NI supervisory control and EI process control, with any shared
operations services (e.g., HMI views, historians) implemented through replication patterns that
preserve zone integrity [2] [31].

3.3.3. Cyber Decoupling Requirements
The objectives and functional allocation above are enforced through four cyber decoupling
requirements. Each requirement is stated below, followed by rational and the guidance that
supports it. The requirements are the basis for the architecture described in the following section.
 R.1. The NI/EI cyber boundary shall coincide with or be placed inside of the NI/EI process
 boundary.

All NI/EI digital interfaces should be explicitly identified, typed (analog/contact/serial/IP),
directionally constrained, and justified against a functional need [2] [6] [31]. Interface decisions
should remain under configuration control with design traceability. This prevents subsequent design
changes from reintroducing bidirectional control paths between islands [2] [32].
 R.2. No digital control path that originates from the EI reaches NI SL4 systems.

If any signals are required from EI to NI, they should be limited to a small set of measurements
credited only for non-safety control provided via hardened, single-purpose links [3,11]. Each such
signal is evaluated for potential failure scenario and its loss, corruption, or delay is handled
conservatively by NI logic [1, 2].
 R.3. Data flows from the NI to the EI are unidirectional.

Statuses, alarms, and non-safety coordination signals that traverse from the NI to the EI are one-
way only. Where digital replication is needed, hardware-enforced unidirectionality. Such as a data
diodes, is prioritized. The HTGR template explicitly uses one-way communication from SL4 to
SL3/SL2 via a data diode. The same mechanism can be used to export NI state to EI dispatch and
control functions without creating a writable return channel [2] [6].
 R.4 Cross-zone communication within SL3 is constrained to explicitly allowed protocols and
 messages semantics.

Separate SL3 zones are maintained for NI supervisory control and EI process control. Where an
SL3 zone exchanges data with another zone, acknowledgements are limited to minimal handshake
semantics so that a lower-trust zone cannot influence higher-trust logic [2]. Shared infrastructure
and transitive trust relationships across islands are minimized, consistence with zone-based graded
security concepts [31] [1]. Zone and conduit definitions are treated as design-time artifacts that
remain under configuration control as the design evolves [32].

enforceable invariants and evidence artifacts that remain under configuration control [2] [32].

3.4.1. DCSA for NE/NI Decoupling
In an NI/EI architecture, physical decoupling separates reactor power from the demand of the
energy conversion equipment. Disturbances on the EI are expressed to the reactor to the reactor
through bounded thermal-interface conditions rather than fast closed-loop control between islands
[29] [28]. Cyber decoupling extends this separation. A credible EI compromise is assumed, but its
influence on the NI is engineered to collapse into a small, analyzable set of NI-recognized
conditions. This includes the loss of EI services and abnormal thermal-interface measurements
rather than a pathway for direct digital command [29] [30]. Within the DCSA framing, decoupling is
therefore an operational property (how the plant behaves under adversary control of EI) and an
architectural property (how zone boundaries and gateways prevent transitive trust). As such, it is
not a claim about perfect prevention [2].
Threat model boundary conditions for this alignment are intentionally conservative. First,
compromise of EI SL3 networks is treated as plausible. This is consistent with the persistent and
well-resourced threat environment observed for OT and industrial control systems [2, 1].
Second, NI SL4 safety functions are assumed to remain deterministic and capable of independent
protective action without relying on any EI-originated digital service, command, or
acknowledgement [2, 1]
Third, any EI-to-NI data that cannot be eliminated is assumed to be potentially adversary-controlled
(corrupted, delayed, replayed, or denied) and thus is only used where its failure modes can be
bounded within the licensing basis and handled conservatively by NI logic [32, 27, 33]
Security levels, zones, and conduits are treated as first-class design artifacts that must be
documented with the same rigor as the plant’s physical interface definitions.
At minimum, the DCSA documentation set includes: (i) a zone diagram assigning systems and assets
to SL4 and SL3 zones with explicit placement rationale, (ii) a conduit specification for each cross-
zone path including directionality, allowed protocols, and allowed message semantics, and (iii) a
cross-island interface catalog enumerating every NI/EI signal and data flow with an owner and
justification [1].
Treating these as controlled configuration items is important because the highest-risk failure mode
for decoupling is silent re-introduction of bidirectional pathways through convenience integrations
(e.g., shared engineering workstations, shared identity services, or “temporary” maintenance
connections) [1, 38, 27].
Decoupling is enforced through five architectural invariants. Each invariant is stated below with the
guidance that supports it.

 34

 I.1. SL4 zones do not accept routable protocol traffic or remote administrative sessions from
 EI or any other SL3 zone.

This eliminates every EI-originated digital path to NI SL4 [30, 39]
 I.2. Any NI data required by the EI is exported through hardware-enforced one-way
 mechanisms.

Data diodes, hardwired replication, or equivalent mechanisms are used in place of bidirectional
network services [30, 39]
 I.3. Cross-island connectivity is reduced to a small set of explicitly defined gateways.

Each gateway has a fixed function and typed interfaces. Unmanaged routing between islands is
prohibited [30].
 I.4. Any remaining EI-to-NI signals are limited to a small measurement set that is not
 credited for safety.

These signals are implemented as hardened, single-purpose links. Their loss or corruption is
handled conservatively on the NI [28, 29].
 I.5 Any change that adds, modifies, or repurposes an NI/EI interface is treated as a design
 change.

Each such change requires traceability to the functional allocation and re-validation of the DCSA
invariants.

3.4.2. Nuclear Island (NI) DCSA Sub-Architecture
The NI DCSA must protect SL4 safety platforms while still enabling non-safety supervisory control,
investment protection, and operations support functions. It must do so without creating new paths
that can influence SL4 behavior. NI zoning is therefore driven by two competing objectives.
without creating new paths that can influence SL4 behavior. The first is to preserve SL4
determinism and independence. The second is to provide sufficient operational observability and
equipment protection without creating control authority that could inhibit or delay protective
actuation [30, 39].

3.4.2.1. Zoning and Placement
The NI is partitioned into one or more SL4 zones that implement protection and shutdown
functions and NI-SL3 zones that implement supervisory control, NSRST functions, investment
protection, and NI-local operations support [30, 29]. The SL4 zones include the reactor protection
system (RPS) and a diverse or reserve shutdown platform, with separation that limits common-cause
exposure to shared digital infrastructure [30, 32]. TheNI-SL3 zones include supervisory control
functions, investment protection logic, and NI-local operator and human machine interface (HMI)
functions that require timely NI state but are not permitted to inhibit SL4 actuation [30, 33].
Operations support functions, such as engineering workstations, maintenance tooling, and
configuration management services, are placed in a distinct NI-SL3 support enclave. This prevents
routine operational networks from becoming a stepping stone for high-privilege access [1].

3.4.2.3. Maintenance and Software Updates
Maintenance and diagnostics are the main mechanisms by which well-structured zone architectures
are unintentionally bypassed over the plant lifetime. An NI-specific maintenance lane is therefore
treated as a defined enclave with explicit entry/exit points, rather than an ad hoc practice of
connecting vendor laptops to operational networks. Where vendor access is required, it terminates
in this enclave through controlled jump hosts, multi-factor authentication (MFA), session logging,
and time-bounded enablement, and the enclave isolated from SL4 by design [6] [9] [4]. Portable
media and patch packages are introduced through a controlled transfer station that is not
simultaneously connected to multiple zones. All changes are recorded with traceable rationale to
preserve the decoupling argument as configurations evolve [39, 30, 31, 27].
SL4 platforms are kept minimally configurable and are updated only through on-site procedures
because routine remote update workflows would undermine the SL4 isolation premise. NI-SL3
systems can support more conventional patching and software lifecycle practices. However, patch
distribution must not create a persistent backchannel from enterprise IT networks or EI systems

into NI operations networks. Any update mechanism that implies bidirectional connectivity, such as
centralized endpoint management, remote domain administration, or continuous cloud reachback.,
is incompatible with the decoupling invariants and should be treated as an architecture
nonconformance rather than an operational exception [39, 30, 31, 27]

3.4.2.4. Monitoring and Response
NI SL3 security monitoring focuses on detection and containment without creating new
dependencies into SL4. Security-relevant logs, such as gateway health, firewall events, controller
diagnostics, workstation events, are aggregated in a NI-SL3 monitoring enclave and exported one-
way to lower-trust analytic environments for correlation and long-term storage. Because SL4 is
architected to be non-routable and not remotely managed, response to suspected SL4 impact is
primarily procedural—verification, surveillance testing, and replacement or reloading under physical
control—rather than remote remediation For suspected compromise in NI SL3, response
emphasizes the following actions rather than on-the-fly cyber repair while operating [39, 30, 31, 27]
 • Isolation of affected segments,
 • Transition to local or manual control modes where available,
 • And, if required, conservative plant transitions driven by SL4 protective functions.

4.1. Reactor System
This section describes the nuclear fuel, the fuel configuration, and the reactor operating modes.

4.1.1. Nuclear Fuel
Microreactor designs employ three principal fuel forms:

 40

 1. Tristructural-Isotropic (TRISO) Coated Particle Fuel: TRISO fuel consists of a fissile
 kernel encapsulated in multiple ceramic and carbon layers that provide robust fission
 product retention under normal and accident conditions.
 The following microreactor designs use TRISO fuel: eVinci [43], Project Pele [53], Kaleidos
        [45, 46], Kronos MMR [47, 48], XENITH [57], SOLO [55], Ward 250 [56], Antares R1 [41],
 and HOLOS-QUAD [44]. Both X-energy and Radiant Industries have submitted fuel
 qualification methodology reports to the U.S. NRC [64, 46], and BWXT has delivered a full
 core of TRISO fuel for the Project Pele prototype [53]. A variant of TRISO, fully ceramic
 micro-encapsulated (FCM) fuel, embeds TRISO particles in a silicon carbide matrix rather
 than a graphite matrix. The Kronos MMR design specifies FCM fuel [47, 48].
 2. Metallic Fuel: Metallic fuel uses uranium alloyed with one or more metals to form a solid
 fuel element with high thermal conductivity. The high thermal conductivity aids in effective
 heat transfer from the fuel to the coolant and keeps the centerline temperature of the fuel
 lower during operation.
 The following microreactor designs use metallic fuel: Aurora (uranium-zirconium alloy) [42]
 and MARVEL (uranium-zirconium-hydride). U-ZrH fuel provides inherent negative
 temperature reactivity feedback due to the hydrogen moderator integral to the fuel matrix
 [51].
 3. Ceramic Fuel: Ceramic fuel uses uranium dioxide (UO₂) pellets as the fissile material
 contained within metal cladding. UO₂ is a well-characterized fuel form with extensive
 operational history in commercial light water reactors and fast reactors.
 The following microreactor designs use ceramic fuel: MegaPower/SPR [52] and SEALER
 [54].
Based on the predominance of TRISO fuel in microreactor designs and pre-application activities
with the U.S. NRC, our plant design assumption is:
 A.1. The nuclear fuel is contained in TRISO particles.

The TRISO fuel particle is composed of five layers as shown in Figure 12. The layers and their
functions are summarized below, starting with the innermost layer.
 • Fuel Kernel: This layer provides fission energy, retains fission products, and controls the
 particle oxygen potential [65].
 • Buffer Carbon: This layer attenuates fission recoils, provides void volume for fission gases,
 and accommodates fuel kernel swelling [65].
 • Inner Pyrolytic Carbon: This layer prevents chlorine attack of the fuel kernel during
 manufacturing, provides structural support, and retains gaseous fission products [65].
 • Silicon Carbide: This layer is the primary load-bearing layer of the particle and retains
 fission products [65].
 • Outer Pyrolytic Carbon: This layer provides structural support and provides a fission
 product barrier [65].

 41

 Figure 12. TRISO Fuel Particle

The majority of microreactor designs specify high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) fuel,
enriched to less than 20% uranium-235. HALEU enables smaller core sizes and longer core lifetimes
compared to low-enriched uranium (LEU), which is significant for factory-assembled, transportable
designs [66, 67].
 A.2. The TRISO fuel uses a UCO or UO₂ kernel with HALEU enrichment (less than 20%
 U-235).

Assumptions A.1 and A.2 are important for the design of a DCSA because the use of TRISO
particles with a UCO fuel kernel may act as a physical robustness factor that eliminates or mitigates
the effects of a cyberattack. UCO fuel is designed to provide improved fuel performance at high
burnup, thereby enabling longer fuel cycles. UCO also eliminates CO formation and reduces internal
gas pressure [65]. Modern TRISO particles retain fission products well under extreme temperature
conditions, enabling coolant output temperatures near 1000 °C [67].

4.1.2. Nuclear Fuel Configuration
The fuel configuration in microreactors varies by heat transfer technology. Three principal
configurations are found across the designs listed in Table III:
 1. Monolithic Core with Heat Pipe Channels: A monolithic core consists of a solid metal
 body with drilled channels that separately accommodate fuel elements and heat pipes. Heat
 is conducted from the fuel through the monolith structure to the heat pipes, which passively
 transport thermal energy to the power conversion system. This configuration provides a
 structurally integral core with no loose components, which is significant for transport and
 factory assembly.
 The following microreactor designs use a monolithic core with heat pipe channels: eVinci
 [43], MegaPower/SPR [52], Antares R1 [41], and KRUSTY/Kilopower [49, 50]. The
 MegaPower concept uses a stainless steel monolith with 2,112 fuel rod channels and 1,224
 potassium heat pipe channels arranged in six symmetrical 60-degree sectors [52]. The eVinci
 design uses a monolithic solid-state integral core body with hexagonal fuel and heat pipe

 42

 channels, with metal hydride moderator [43]. A cross-section of the eVinci monolithic core
 is shown in Figure 13.

2. Prismatic Graphite Blocks with TRISO Compacts: A prismatic core consists of
 hexagonal graphite fuel blocks containing cylindrical channels for TRISO fuel compacts and
 separate channels for coolant flow. Graphite reflectors surround the fuel region to moderate
 neutrons and reduce neutron leakage.
 The following microreactor designs use prismatic graphite blocks with TRISO compacts:
   Project Pele [53], Kaleidos [45, 46], Kronos MMR [47, 48], XENITH [57], SOLO [55], Ward
 250 [56], and HOLOS-QUAD [44]. The Kaleidos design uses cylindrical TRISO compacts
 in prismatic graphite fuel blocks with an encapsulated zirconium hydride (ZrH) moderator
 [46]. A generic prismatic fuel block is shown in Figure 14.

3. Rod-Type Fuel Assemblies in Open Pool: A rod-type open pool configuration consists

 The following microreactor designs use rod-type fuel assemblies in an open pool: MARVEL

There are no current industry trends with respect to microreactor fuel configurations. The three
configurations described above reflect the diversity of heat transfer technologies under
development, and no single configuration shows a majority among the designs listed in Table III.
However, all three configurations share common physical robustness properties that are relevant to
DCSA design. As a result, our plant design assumptions are:
 A.3. The microreactor fuel configuration and sealed core design provide multiple robust
 layers of defense to protect against release of radionuclides for design-basis accidents [66,
 71].

 A.4. The microreactor fuel configuration and sealed core design provide multiple robust
 layers of defense that increase the grace period for operator action in design-basis accident
        scenarios [72, 66].

Assumption A.3 is important for the design of a DCSA because the use of multiple robust layers of
defense may act as a physical robustness factor that eliminates or mitigates the effects of a
cyberattack. These considerations may include a structurally integral core that maintains fuel
geometry under all operating modes (including transport), large temperature margins between
normal operating conditions and fuel failure thresholds, and passive heat removal pathways that do
not depend on active control systems [66, 71]. For monolithic cores, the fuel is embedded in a solid
metal matrix that physically prevents fuel relocation [43, 52]. For prismatic cores, the fuel compacts
are contained within graphite blocks inside a sealed pressure vessel [53, 45]. For rod-type cores, the
fuel rods are fixed within a sealed reactor vessel [51].
While Assumption A.4 is not directly applicable to DCSA design as part of an isolated Tier 2
analysis, it is important when considering the complete TCA process. Assumption A.4 is most
directly applicable to Denial of Task Analysis (Tier 3), which is focused on detection and
interruption of cyberattacks before consequences of concern can occur. For implementation of this
assumption in Tier 3 analysis, more specific data would need to be presented to determine timelines
for operator action. This consideration affects DCSA design because if a plant designer intends to
credit this assumption in Tier 3, then the architecture must ensure that operators have access to
sufficient data to detect the cyber-enabled accident scenario is occurring and access to the control
surface necessary to interrupt the progression of the scenario.
A defining characteristic of microreactors, regardless of fuel configuration, is that the reactor core is
factory-assembled and sealed as a single unit. Microreactors are not designed for on-site fuel loading,
fuel shuffling, or on-line refueling during their operational lifetime [59, 73]. The core is fueled at the
factory, transported to the deployment site as a sealed unit, operated for the duration of the fuel
cycle, and returned to a centralized facility for defueling [43, 53, 45]. Core lifetimes range from
approximately 3 years (Project Pele [53]) to 20 years or more (Kronos MMR [48], XENITH [57]).
Our plant design assumptions are:
 A.5. The reactor core is factory-assembled and sealed prior to transport

 A.6. On-site refueling is not performed during the operational lifetime of the microreactor

Assumptions A.5 and A.6 are important for DCSA design because they significantly reduce the
scope and complexity of the Fuel Handling and Storage System (FHSS). The elimination of on-site
refueling removes the need for fuel loading, unloading, and recirculation systems, which in turn

 45

removes the associated cyber-accessible control surfaces from the plant and reduces the attack
surface available to an adversary. These implications are discussed further in Section 4.2.

4.1.3. Reactor Operating Modes
Based on public documentation of microreactor designs and DOE/DOD microreactor deployment
programs, it is assumed that the reactor has four operating modes:
 1. Transport: The purpose of this operating mode is to safely move the reactor between the
 factory, deployment site, and centralized defueling facility. In this mode of operation, all
 shutdown elements are fully inserted, the reactor is subcritical, and the core is sealed within
 its transport container. Heat removal during transport is provided by passive means
 (conduction and natural convection) to dissipate residual decay heat from any prior
 operation [43, 53]. The Project Pele prototype is designed for transport in four standard 20-
 foot shipping containers [53], the Kaleidos microreactor fits within a single shipping
 container [45], and the Ward 250 prototype was airlifted via C-17 cargo aircraft in February
 2026 [56]. Instrumentation during transport mode is limited to monitoring temperature,
 radiation levels, and physical security of the transport package.
 2. Start-Up: The purpose of this operating mode is to achieve criticality in the core and
 transition to energy production. In this mode of operation, shutdown elements are gradually
 withdrawn and the core is slowly brought up to criticality and allowed to slowly come up to
 temperature. For heat pipe designs, the heat pipes must transition from a frozen or inactive
 state to active two-phase operation as the core temperature increases [43]. For gas-cooled
 designs, helium circulation is established and gradually increased [53, 57]. For liquid metal
 designs, natural circulation of the primary coolant is established as the core heats [51]. Start-
 up procedures must manage thermal transients to prevent thermal stress on core
 components, particularly for monolithic core designs where differential thermal expansion
 between the monolith, fuel, and heat pipes must be managed [52, 74].
 3. Energy Production (Normal Operations): In this mode of operation, the reactor operates
 at or near its rated power level. Power is adjusted by manipulating the reactivity control
 system, typically by rotating control drums [43, 45, 57] or adjusting control rod positions [42,
 54]. Thermal energy is transferred from the core to the power conversion system via the heat
 transfer system. Many microreactor designs are intended to operate autonomously or semi-
 autonomously in this mode, with the distributed control system maintaining reactor
 operations and responding to load changes without direct operator intervention [43, 45, 57].
 4. Shutdown: The purpose of this operating mode is to shut down the reactor and remove
 decay heat. In this mode of operation, shutdown elements are fully inserted and decay heat is
 removed via the residual heat removal system. For heat pipe designs, the heat pipes passively
 transport decay heat to the heat rejection system [43, 52]. For gas-cooled designs, decay heat
 may be removed by the reactor cavity cooling system (RCCS) or a startup/shutdown cooling
 system [53, 57]. For liquid metal designs, natural circulation of the coolant passively transfers
 decay heat to air-cooled heat exchangers [51, 42]. Microreactor designs emphasize passive
 decay heat removal that does not require active cooling systems or operator intervention [66,
 71].
These modes are documented in the following assumption:
 A.7. The reactor has four operating modes: transport, start-up, energy production, and
 shutdown

 46

Assumption A.7 is important for DCSA design because it informs the interdependencies between
plant systems required to achieve the functions performed during each operating mode. The
transport mode introduces requirements for monitoring and security instrumentation during a
period when the reactor is physically outside the site boundary. These interdependencies affect the
data flow requirements between DCSA zones.
Microreactors emphasize the use of autonomous or semi-autonomous operation. Many microreactor
designs are intended for deployment at remote locations without full-time, on-site operating staff.
The eVinci microreactor is designed for unmanned operation with remote monitoring. The Kaleidos
design specifies autonomous shutdown capability within 300 milliseconds [59, 73, 66, 43, 45, 57].
Based on public documentation of microreactor designs and NRC regulatory guidance on
autonomous operation, it is assumed that:
 A.8. The microreactor is designed for autonomous or semi-autonomous operation with
 remote monitoring and control

Assumption A.8 is important for DCSA design because it fundamentally affects the cybersecurity
threat model. Autonomous operation requires that the Distributed Control System (DCS) and
Reactor Protection System (RPS) function without direct human oversight during normal
operations. Moreover, remote monitoring and control introduces a communication pathway
between the microreactor site and a remote operations center that must be secured against
cyberattack. This remote communication link represents an additional attack surface that must be
addressed in the DCSA. The implications for the DCS and RPS are discussed in Sections 4.9 and
4.10, respectively.
The close integration of these operating modes is important for plant performance. The heat
transfer system’s ability to reject heat directly affects the reactor’s ability to maintain safe operating
temperatures across all four operating modes. Moreover, the interaction between the reactor core
and its primary heat transfer medium supports inherent safety features. For heat pipe designs, the
passive two-phase heat transfer cycle provides inherent load-following capability and thermal
regulation [43, 52, 74]. For liquid metal designs, thermal expansion of the coolant provides negative
reactivity feedback during transients [51, 42]. For gas-cooled designs, the large thermal mass of the
graphite moderator and reflectors provides thermal inertia that slows temperature transients [53, 45].
These inherent safety features serve as physical robustness factors that may mitigate or eliminate the
consequences of a cyberattack.

4.2. Fuel Lifecycle Management System
In traditional reactor designs, the Fuel Handling and Storage System (FHSS) fuels the reactor and
stores spent fuel. In the previous DCSA reports, the FHSS consists of a Fuel Handling System that
loads, unloads, and recirculates fuel, and a Spent Fuel Storage System that stores spent fuel in casks
or tanks at the deployment site. Microreactors do not have these dedicated systems. This is because
the reactor core is factory assembled and sealed prior to transport (Assumption A.5). Further, on-
site refueling is not performed (Assumption A.6). As a result, there are no systems at the
deployment site to load fuel into the reactor, unload spent fuel from the reactor, recirculate fuel, or
store spent fuel independently of the reactor module. All fuel loading and defueling operations occur
at the manufacturer’s factory under a separate regulatory framework [75, 76, 77, 78].

 47

The microreactor deployment lifecycle, as documented by Westinghouse for eVinci [43, 75], BWXT
for Project Pele [79], and Radiant Industries for Kaleidos [76, 80] consists of the following fuel-
related operations and is illustrated by the U.S. NRC conceptual model [81] shown in Figure 16:
 1. The reactor is assembled and fueled at the manufacturer’s factory.
 2. The fueled reactor module is transported to the deployment site in sealed shipping
 containers.
 3. The reactor module is installed at the deployment site and operated for the duration of the
 fuel cycle.
 4. At the end of life, the reactor module (with spent fuel sealed inside) is removed from the
 deployment site.
 5. The reactor module is transported to a centralized facility for defueling, refurbishment, or
 disposition.

 Figure 16. Conceptual deployment model for microreactors [81]

Each step in the deployment lifecycle is evaluated below for digital systems and cyber-accessible
control surfaces that are relevant to DCSA design. These steps are:
 1. Factory Assembly and Fueling Step: occurs entirely at the manufacturer’s factory, which
 operates under a separate regulatory framework [77]. The factory’s digital systems, including
 fuel handling equipment, quality assurance instrumentation, and fuel assembly robotics, are
 outside the scope of the deployment site DCSA. No fuel handling equipment from the
 factory accompanies the reactor module to the deployment site.
 2. Transport to Deployment Site Step: requires that the reactor module maintain
 subcriticality throughout transit. Subcriticality during transport is maintained by passive
 mechanical means rather than active digital control. For eVinci, shutdown rods are
 mechanically inserted prior to transport [43]. For Project Pele, the core geometry and
 shutdown elements are designed to maintain subcriticality under all transport conditions,
 including shock and vibration [79]. No digital system actively controls reactivity during
 transport. Instrumentation during transport is limited to monitoring temperature, radiation
 levels, shutdown element position, and physical security of the transport package. These
 sensors are read-only and have no corresponding actuators that could be manipulated to
 cause a safety-significant event. Transport of a fueled reactor to the deployment site involves

 48

 a fresh fuel source term, while transport of an operated reactor back to the factory involves
 an irradiated fuel source term with different radiological characteristics [77].
 3. Installation at Deployment Site Step: involves positioning the sealed reactor module at
 the prepared site and connecting it to the balance of plant systems. For Kaleidos, no site
 excavation is required, and the unit achieves full power within 48 hours of arrival [80]. For
 eVinci, the plant is delivered in approximately four to five ISO containers: the reactor
 container, the power conversion unit, an instrumentation and controls container, and
 miscellaneous support equipment [43]. For Project Pele, the reactor and its associated
 systems are contained in four standard 20-foot shipping containers and can be assembled
 on-site within 72 hours [53, 79]. Installation involves mechanical handling equipment
 (cranes, transport vehicles) for positioning the reactor module, but this equipment is
 temporary and is removed after installation. The transition from transport mode to start-up
 mode involves withdrawal of shutdown elements, which is performed by the Reactivity
 Control and Shutdown System (RCSS) described in Section 4.3.
 4. Removal from Deployment Site Step: is the reverse of installation. Shutdown elements are
 fully inserted by the RCSS, the reactor module is disconnected from the balance of plant
 systems, and mechanical handling equipment repositions the module into its shipping
 container. The reactor module must cool sufficiently to allow handling, with decay heat
 removed by the passive heat removal pathways described in Assumption A.11. No fuel is
 removed from the reactor module during this step.
 5. Transport to Centralized Facility Step: is operationally similar to Step 2, with the same
 transport monitoring instrumentation. The key difference is that the source term is irradiated
 fuel rather than fresh fuel, resulting in higher radiation levels and a different radiological
 profile [77]. The monitoring sensors serve the same read-only function with no
 corresponding actuators at the deployment site.
The analysis of these lifecycle steps demonstrates that the unique microreactor fuel lifecycle
management introduces no cyber-accessible actuators at the microreactor deployment site. The only
digital instrumentation associated consists of transport read-only, monitoring sensors (temperature,
radiation, shutdown element position, and physical security). This represents a significant reduction
in attack surface compared traditional designs, which require dedicated sensors for fuel tracking,
burnup measurement, and defect detection, and actuators for fuel transit, separation, and storage
cask positioning.
Spent fuel in a microreactor remains within the sealed reactor core throughout the entire
deployment lifecycle. No spent fuel is removed from the reactor module at the deployment site, and
no separate spent fuel storage facility is required on-site [43, 53, 45, 43, 76]. For eVinci, spent fuel is
returned to a Westinghouse facility for defueling or transferred to a deep geological repository for
long-term storage [43, 75]. For Project Pele, the operated reactor module is transported to
temporary storage at existing facilities such as the Radioactive Scrap and Waste Facility (RSWF) or
the Outdoor Radioactive Storage Area (ORSA) at Idaho National Laboratory, with the potential for
post-irradiation examination at the Hot Fuel Examination Facility or the Materials and Fuels
Complex [79]. For Kaleidos, no nuclear waste or infrastructure remains on the site after the reactor
module is removed [80].
The containment of spent fuel within the sealed reactor module is supported by the fuel form itself.
TRISO fuel, used by the majority of microreactor designs (Assumption A.1), provides multiple
barriers to fission product release that remain intact under normal and accident conditions [65, 67].

 49

The sealed reactor core, the reactor vessel, and the transport container provide additional barriers.
Based on this deployment lifecycle model, the fuel lifecycle management assumptions are:
 A.9. No dedicated fuel handling or spent fuel storage systems exist at the microreactor
 deployment site. All fuel loading and defueling operations are performed at the
 manufacturer’s factory.

 A.10. Spent fuel remains within the sealed reactor module at all times during the deployment
 lifecycle.

 A.11. Decay heat from spent fuel is removed by the same passive heat removal pathways
 used during shutdown.

 A.12. The sealed reactor module operates independently of other plant systems during
 shutdown and transport modes.

Assumption A.9 is important for DCSA design because it eliminates the entire fuel handling attack
surface that exists in traditional reactor designs. The SFR and HTGR FHSS subsystems require
dedicated sensors for fuel tracking, burnup measurement, and defect detection, and actuators for
fuel transit, separation, and storage cask positioning. For microreactors, none of these cyber-
accessible control surfaces exist at the deployment site. This significantly reduces the attack surface
available to an adversary and simplifies the DCSA zone architecture.
Assumption A.10 is important for DCSA design because it eliminates the need for on-site spent fuel
transfer casks, spent fuel storage facilities, and the associated monitoring and handling
instrumentation. The only fuel-related monitoring at the deployment site is radiation and
temperature monitoring of the sealed reactor module during operation and transport.
Assumption A.11 is important for DCSA design because passive decay heat removal is a non-cyber
independent protection layer (IPL) that may mitigate or eliminate the consequences of a cyberattack.
Passive decay heat removal does not depend on active cooling systems, electrical power, or operator
action. Therefore, these systems cannot be compromised by a cyberattack targeting active control
systems.
Assumption A.12 is important for DCSA design because the independence of the sealed reactor
module during shutdown and transport modes means that a cyberattack compromising other plant
systems (such as the Distributed Control System or Power Conversion System) does not affect the
ability of the reactor module to contain spent fuel.

4.3.1. Reactivity Control System (RCS)
The primary functions of the Reactivity Control System (RCS) are [43, 45, 52, 82, 83]:
 1. Control reactivity by manipulating control drum position.
 2. Achieve hot shutdown state by rotating control drums to the shutdown position.
 3. Achieve cold shutdown state by rotating control drums to the shutdown position in
 conjunction with the RSS.
 4. Rotate control drums to the shutdown position during a reactor trip.
The majority of microreactor designs use control drums rather than traditional control rods for the
RCS. In these designs, the RCS manipulates the reactivity of the core by rotating control drums to
position reflect and absorber material. Control drums are cylindrical components located in the
reflector region on the periphery of the core. Each drum has a reflector material on one portion of
its outer surface and neutral absorber material on the remaining portion as shown in Figure 17.
When the reflector material faces the core, neutrons are reflected back into the fuel region to
increase reactivity. When the absorber material faces the core, neutrons are absorbed to decrease
reactivity. By rotating the drums incrementally, operators or autonomous control systems can adjust
reactor power with high precision [43, 45, 82].

4.4. Heat Transport System (HTS)
The Heat Transport System (HTS) removes heat from the reactor core and transports the resulting
thermal energy to the Power Conversion System. The HTS forms the principal thermal interface
between the reactor system and the balance of plant, and its boundary defines the primary coolant
boundary in designs that employ a flowing primary coolant. The HTS is responsible for maintaining
the fuel and structural temperatures within their specified acceptable design limits during normal
operation, anticipated operational occurrences, and postulated accidents.
The functions of the HTS are [51, 43, 53, 75]:
 1. Remove heat from the reactor core.
 2. Transport thermal energy to the PCS.

For microreactors, the HTS is implemented in one of three configurations: a heat-pipe
configuration, a liquid-metal pool configuration, or a gas-cooled configuration. These configurations
are discussed in the following sections.

4.4.1. Heat Pipe Configuration
A heat-pipe HTS transports thermal energy from the reactor core to the Power Conversion System
(PCS) through an array of sealed, passive heat pipes. Each heat pipe contains a small inventory of an
alkali metal working fluid, sodium or potassium, inside a sealed tube [43, 52]. Heat absorbed at the
evaporator section of the pipe vaporizes the working fluid and the vapor flows to the cooler
condenser section where it releases its heat of vaporization to a heat exchanger.
Thereafter, the condensed liquid returns to the evaporator by capillary action. The transport of heat
is accomplished by phase change of the working fluid. There are no mechanical pumps, valves, or
primary loop piping in the primary heat transport path [43]. The evaporator section of each heat
pipe is embedded in the reactor core, and the condenser section is coupled to a heat exchanger that
transfers heat to the PCS working fluid. The operating principle of a heat pipe is shown in Figure 19.

4.4.2. Gas Cooled Configuration
A gas-cooled HTS transports thermal energy from the reactor core to the PCS through the forced
circulation of a pressurized inert gas, typically helium, by one or more mechanical circulators. The
helium passes through coolant channels in the prismatic graphite core, absorbs heat from the fuel,
and exits the core at elevated temperature . The heated helium then either delivers its heat to the
PCS working fluid through a primary heat exchanger or drives a turbine in a direct closed Brayton
cycle [53, 44, 88].
Helium is selected as the working fluid because of its chemical inertness, its low neutron absorption
cross-section, and the fact that it does not become activated under neutron irradiation. The
circulator is the principal active component in the primary heat transport path, and its continued
operation is required to maintain primary coolant flow during normal operation. The primary
coolant boundary is the pressure vessel that contains the helium, which operates at an elevated
pressure that ranges from approximately 7 MPa to approximately 8 MPa among the designs [53, 44,
88].

4.4.3. Liquid Metal Pool Configuration
A liquid-metal pool HTS transports thermal energy from the reactor core to the PCS through a
liquid metal coolant held in a pool within the reactor vessel. The reactor core is submerged in the
pool and heat is transferred from the core to one or more intermediate heat exchangers located
within the vessel [51]. Circulation of the primary coolant is driven by natural convection establishing
a buoyancy-driven flow that requires no mechanical pump.
An inert cover gas, such as argon, blankets the free surface of the liquid metal to prevent reaction of
the coolant with air [51]. The reactor vessel is enclosed within a guard vessel that contains the
primary coolant in the event of a leak and provides a passive path for residual heat removal [86]. The
liquid-metal pool operates at near-atmospheric pressure and heat is transferred from the
intermediate heat exchanger to the secondary coolant that serves the Power Conversion System
(PCS).

4.4.3.1. Primary Coolant Subsystem
The Primary Coolant System (PRCS) transports thermal energy from the reactor core to the
intermediate heat exchangers that form the boundary with the Secondary Coolant Subsystem (SCS)
[90]. The functions of the Primary Coolant Subsystem are [90]:
 1.  Remove heat from the reactor core region.
 2.  Transfer heat to the intermediate heat exchangers.
 3.  Transfer decay heat to the guard vessel by passive conduction.
 4.  Return the primary coolant to the core region.
 5.  Prevent leakage of the primary coolant and the cover gas through the primary coolant
 boundary.
 6. Provide structural support for the components connected to the primary coolant boundary.
The primary coolant is eutectic sodium-potassium (NaK), which offers the following benefits as a
primary coolant for liquid-metal pool microreactors [51, 90]:
 1. Low melting temperature, allowing the coolant to remain liquid at room temperature without
 preheating.
 2. High thermal conductivity.
 3. High heat transfer at moderate velocities.
 4. Low operating pressure, near atmospheric.
 5. High boiling temperature relative to the operating temperature.
 6. Low neutron absorption cross-section.
The use of sodium-potassium requires an inert cover gas and robust containment due to its
reactivity with air and water [51, 90].
The Primary Coolant Subsystem circulates sodium-potassium through a hydraulic circuit by natural
convection, with no mechanical pump in the primary heat transport path. Heated sodium-potassium
rises from the reactor core to a distribution plenum, passes through intermediate heat exchangers
arranged at the periphery of the upper vessel, and returns through downcomer pipes to the lower
plenum and back to the core. The circulation rate is established by the temperature difference
between the core and the intermediate heat exchangers. An argon cover gas blankets the free surface

of the primary coolant in the headspace above the pool, and the Primary Coolant Subsystem is
classified as safety-related [51, 90].

natural convection. A reduction in primary coolant flow generates an alarm in the control system
rather than an automatic reactor trip [83]. As such, we assume:
 A.20. The Primary Coolant Subsystem circulates the primary coolant by natural convection
 and contains no flow-control actuator in the primary heat transport path.

4.4.3.2. Secondary Coolant Subsystem
The Secondary Coolant Subsystem (SCS) receives heat from the Primary Coolant Subsystem at the
intermediate heat exchangers and transports that heat to the Power Conversion System. The
functions of the Secondary Coolant Subsystem are [90]:
 1. Extract heat from the primary coolant at the intermediate heat exchangers.

In the MARVEL 90% final design, the Secondary Coolant Subsystem circulated a eutectic gallium-
indium-tin alloy through a pipe-in-pipe natural-convection arrangement that connected the

vessel-integrated Stirling engines introduced vibration, melt and thaw damage to the engine helium
coils, limited controllability of the overall system, and radiation damage risk to the engines [89].

by the integrated Stirling engines. The Secondary Coolant Subsystem is a forced-circulation sodium-
potassium loop that transports heat from the intermediate heat exchangers to ground-mounted

4.5. Coolant Conditioning System (CCS)
The purpose of the Coolant Conditioning System (CCS) is to provide and maintain the inert cover
gas and the primary coolant inventory required by the Heat Transport System (HTS) to perform
F.HTS.1 and F.HTS.2. The functions of the CCS are [55, 44, 83, 93, 90, 94]:
 1. Supply inert cover gas to the primary coolant boundary.
 2. Supply primary coolant inventory to the Heat Transport System.
 3. Manage primary coolant boundary pressure through cover gas inventory.
 4. Filter radionuclide and chemical impurities from the cover gas vent path.
The CCS depends on the HTS configurations outlined in the previous section. The coolant
conditioning at the deployment site and its functions are determined depending on the HTS
configurations:
 • Heat-Pipe Configuration: the working fluid is sealed within the heat pipes for the life of the
 reactor at the manufacturer factory, so no primary coolant management is performed at the
 deployment site and no cover gas system is required for the primary heat transport path [43,
       49, 50, 52].
 • Gas-Cooled Configurations: the primary coolant is itself a pressurized inert gas, typically
 helium, and the CCS combines the functions of coolant supply, purification, inventory
 control, and pressure management within a single integrated system [48]. The HTR-10
 helium purification system, which comprises a copper oxide bed, a molecular sieve adsorber,
 and a low-temperature adsorber, is the most fully published architecture for gas-cooled
 coolant conditioning at a scale relevant to microreactors [94], and the HTTR program has
 published complementary helium chemistry control and leak management technology
 applicable to gas-cooled microreactor designs [95].
 • Liquid-Metal Pool Configuration: the cover gas is a separate inert gas, typically argon for
 sodium and sodium-potassium coolants, and a coolant management subsystem maintains the
 primary coolant inventory separately from the cover gas inventory. Online purification of the
 liquid-metal coolant is not a prominent feature of the publicly documented microreactor
 designs, which is consistent with the smaller coolant inventory and shorter operational life of
       microreactors [83, 93, 86, 90].
Based on the architectural pattern shared across the publicly documented liquid-metal pool and gas-
cooled microreactors, our plant design assumption is:
 A.22. The Coolant Conditioning System (CCS) consists of two subsystems: the Cover Gas
 Subsystem and the Coolant Inventory and Makeup Subsystem.

Assumption A.22 is important for the design of a DCSA because it informs the interdependencies
between the subsystems required to support F.HTS.1 and F.HTS.2 and the functions of the CCS
itself. The following sections describe the Cover Gas Subsystem and the Coolant Inventory and
Makeup Subsystem in greater detail. The MARVEL design provides the most complete public

documentation of these subsystems and is used here as the reference implementation [54, 64],

4.5.1. Cover Gas Subsystem (CGS)
The Cover Gas Subsystem (CGS) supplies the inert gas that blankets the free surface of the primary
coolant in the headspace of the reactor vessel and the related vessel spaces. In the liquid-metal pool
reference configuration the cover gas prevents the primary coolant from reacting with air or
moisture during normal operation, accommodates volumetric expansion and contraction of the
primary coolant across the operating temperature range, and provides a pressure-controlled
boundary above the free surface of the pool. In the MARVEL design the Cover Gas Subsystem
corresponds to the Inert Gas Subsystem of the Reactor Structure and supplies ultra-high-purity
argon to the primary coolant headspace, the guard vessel, and additional reactor structure spaces that
require an inert atmosphere. The functions of the Cover Gas Subsystem are [83, 90]:
 1. Supply inert cover gas to the primary coolant headspace.
 2. Maintain cover gas pressure within the design range.
 3. Vent cover gas through a filtered path.
 4. Detect cover gas leakage.
 5. Supply inert atmosphere to the guard vessel.
The CGS is classified as safety-related because it supports the integrity of the primary coolant
boundary by preventing coolant-air reactions and by maintaining the pressure conditions assumed in
the safety analysis. The argon supply is stored at the deployment site and is replenished on the
schedule established by the licensee. A filtered vent path is provided so that cover gas can be
released without releasing radionuclides that may have accumulated in the gas during operation [83,

4.5.2. Coolant Inventory and Makeup System
The Coolant Inventory and Makeup Subsystem stores, transfers, and accounts for the primary
coolant inventory of the Heat Transport System. In the liquid-metal pool reference configuration
the subsystem holds the pre-fill primary coolant in a dedicated storage vessel at the deployment site,
transfers coolant into the Heat Transport System primary boundary during initial fill and during any
inventory recovery operation, measures the quantity of coolant delivered, and drains coolant from
the primary boundary at end of life. In the MARVEL design the Coolant Inventory and Makeup
Subsystem corresponds to the Primary Coolant Management Subsystem of the Reactor Structure,
with a corresponding Secondary Coolant Management Subsystem performing the same functions
for the gallium-indium-tin alloy of the Secondary Coolant Subsystem. The functions of the Coolant
Inventory and Makeup Subsystem are [90]:

 62

 1. Store primary coolant inventory in a dedicated storage vessel.
 2. Transfer primary coolant into the Heat Transport System.
 3. Measure primary coolant inventory transferred to the Heat Transport System.
 4. Drain primary coolant from the Heat Transport System.
The Coolant Inventory and Makeup Subsystem is classified as safety-related because it supports the
integrity of the primary coolant boundary and because verification of the primary coolant inventory
cannot be performed through in-vessel measurement in the reference design [90].
The fundamental sensors and actuators necessary for Coolant Inventory and Makeup Subsystem

4.6. Residual Heat Removal System (RHRS)
The purpose of the Residual Heat Removal System (RHRS) is to remove decay heat from the
reactor core after shutdown and transport it to the ultimate heat sink. The functions of the RHRS
are [96]:
 1. Remove decay heat from the reactor core after shutdown.
 2. Transport decay heat to the ultimate heat sink.
The relationship between the RHRS and the Heat Transport System depends on the HTS
configuration. In heat-pipe configurations, the RHRS and the HTS share the same physical
components, because the heat pipes that transport heat during normal operation continue to
transport decay heat after shutdown, so the RHRS is not a physically separate system. In gas-cooled
and liquid-metal pool configurations, the RHRS is a physically separate, passive heat transport path
that operates outside the primary coolant boundary, removing heat from the exterior of the reactor
vessel or guard vessel by natural circulation of air. The three RHRS configurations correspond to the
three HTS configurations and are described in the following sections.

4.6.1. Continued Heat Pipe Operation
In heat-pipe configurations, the residual heat removal function is performed by the same heat pipes
that transport heat during normal operation. After shutdown, the heat pipes continue to transfer
heat from the core to the condenser ends by the phase-change mechanism described in Section
3.4.1, requiring no change in configuration and no actuation [43, 52]. Decay heat is rejected from the
condenser ends to a passive heat sink. The residual heat removal function is always available due to
the heat pipes being sealed and requires no realignment of valves or activation of pumps. The
following microreactor designs perform residual heat removal through continued heat-pipe
operation: eVinci [43], MegaPower/SPR [52], Antares R1 [41], and KRUSTY/Kilopower [49, 50].

4.6.2. Reactor Cavity Cooling Configuration
In gas-cooled configurations, residual heat removal is performed by a Reactor Cavity Cooling
System (RCCS), a passive system that removes decay heat from the exterior of the reactor pressure
vessel by natural circulation of air through the reactor cavity. After shutdown, decay heat is
conducted from the core through the reactor pressure vessel wall and transferred to the RCCS
cooling panels or air ducts by thermal radiation and natural convection within the cavity, and the
heated air rises and exhausts to the environment while drawing in cooler ambient air.
The RCCS operates continuously and is completely passive, with no valves or active components
required to perform its function [96]. The following microreactor designs use a reactor cavity

 63

cooling approach for residual heat removal: Project Pele [53], Kaleidos [45], Kronos MMR [47],
XENITH [57], SOLO [55], Ward 250 [56], and HOLOS-QUAD [44]. The SOLO design
implements this function through a decay heat removal system formed by an air baffle between the
Integrated Radiological Containment and the surrounding structure [55]. A representative air-based
reactor cavity cooling configuration is shown in Figure 22.

 Figure 22. Air-Based Reactor Cavity Cooling Configuration [96]

4.6.3. Reactor Vessel Auxiliary Cooling Configuration
In liquid-metal pool configurations, residual heat removal is performed by a Reactor Vessel Auxiliary
Cooling System (RVACS), a passive system that removes decay heat from the exterior of the guard
vessel by natural circulation of air. After shutdown, decay heat is transferred from the core to the
reactor vessel wall by natural circulation of the primary coolant, conducted and radiated across the
gap between the reactor vessel and the guard vessel, and removed from the exterior of the guard
vessel by naturally circulating air flowing in the gap between the guard vessel and a surrounding
baffle wall. The heated air rises and exhausts to the environment while drawing in cooler ambient
air, and the system operates continuously with no valves or active components [96]. This
configuration is the reference design for the RHRS, consistent with the liquid-metal pool
configuration established for the Heat Transport System in Assumption A.19.

 64

The following microreactor designs use a reactor vessel auxiliary cooling approach for residual heat

Reactor Structure documentation [90]. The Aurora design removes residual heat passively through a

 A.24. The Residual Heat Removal System removes decay heat by natural circulation of air
 across the exterior of the guard vessel and contains no flow-control actuator in the residual

Assumption A.24 is important for DCSA design because the absence of a flow-control actuator in

effects of a cyber-attack against the residual heat removal function. A threat actor cannot defeat the
residual heat removal function by manipulating a pump, fan, or valve, because the function is
performed entirely by passive natural circulation. The cyber-accessible control surface of the RHRS
is limited to the sensors that monitor reactor vessel, guard vessel, and air outlet temperatures.

4.7. Power Conversation System (PCS)
The purpose of the Power Conversion System (PCS) is to convert the thermal energy delivered by
the Heat Transport System into electricity. The functions of the PCS are [97]:
 1. Transfer heat from the secondary coolant to the power conversion working fluid.
 2. Convert thermal energy into electricity.
 3. Reject waste heat to the ultimate heat sink.
The PCS is generally classified as a non-safety-related, balance-of-plant system. This is because the
reactor achieves and maintains safe shutdown and removes decay heat through the Reactivity
Control and Shutdown System and the Residual Heat Removal System independently of the state of
the Power Conversion System.
Three dynamic power conversion cycles are used across the microreactor field: the Brayton cycle,
the Stirling engine, and the Rankine cycle [43, 97]. The cycle selected for a given design depends on
the reactor outlet temperature, the available cooling resources at the deployment site, and the degree
of technical maturity sought by the developer [97]. The three configurations are described below for
completeness. The distinction among them is not of primary importance for DCSA, however, the
control surface of each maps to the consequence analysis of Tier 1 and to the information
interdependencies between plant systems. This can propagate to DCSA design decisions.

4.7.1. Brayton Cycle Configuration
In a Brayton cycle configuration, the power conversion working fluid is compressed, heated by
thermal energy from the reactor, expanded through a turbine to generate electricity, and cooled
before returning to the compressor. The working fluid may be air in an open cycle, or a closed-cycle
gas such as helium, a helium-xenon mixture, or supercritical carbon dioxide. In gas-cooled
configurations the primary coolant may itself serve as the Brayton working fluid in a direct cycle,
which removes the need for an intermediate heat exchanger. The following microreactor designs use

 65

a Brayton cycle for power conversion: eVinci, which uses an open-air Brayton cycle, Project Pele,
HOLOS-QUAD, which uses four direct Brayton modules, and Aurora, which uses a supercritical
carbon dioxide Brayton cycle [44, 53, 43, 86, 97].

4.7.2. Stirling Engine Configuration
In a Stirling engine configuration, a sealed working gas is cyclically heated and cooled between a hot
end and a cold end, and the resulting expansion and contraction of the gas drives a piston that
produces mechanical work, which a linear alternator converts into electricity. The Stirling engine is a
closed, externally heated cycle that requires no working-fluid inventory exchange with the reactor
and no turbine [94]. The Stirling engine configuration is the reference design for the Power
Conversion System, consistent with the liquid-metal pool configuration established for the Heat
Transport System in Assumption A.19.
The MARVEL design provides the most complete public documentation of this configuration and
is used here as the reference implementation, consistent with the anchoring established in Section
3.4.3. In the MARVEL design, the secondary sodium-potassium loop delivers heat from the
intermediate heat exchangers to commercial off-the-shelf Stirling engines that are ground-mounted
adjacent to the reactor, and each engine produces 5 to 7 kW of electrical output. The engines were
relocated from the reactor vessel to the ground-mounted arrangement after engineering
demonstration testing identified vibration and integration concerns with the vessel-mounted
configuration [94, 89]. The ground-mounted Stirling engine configuration is shown in Figure 23.
The following microreactor designs use a Stirling engine for power conversion: MARVEL and
KRUSTY/Kilopower [94, 89, 49, 50].

4.7.3. Rankine Cycle Configuration
In a Rankine cycle configuration, thermal energy from the reactor is transferred through a steam
generator to a water and steam working fluid, the high-quality steam is passed through a turbine-
generator to produce electricity, and the turbine exhaust is condensed and pumped back through the
steam generator. The Rankine cycle has the largest operating experience of the three configurations,
but it requires substantial cooling water resources, which can constrain its use at remote
microreactor deployment sites. No microreactor design in Table II identifies the Rankine cycle as its
reference power conversion configuration, and it is described here for completeness as one of the
cycles available to microreactor developers [97].
The reference design for the Heat Transport System is the liquid-metal pool configuration
established in Assumption A.19 and the MARVEL design couples that configuration to Stirling

 A.25. The Power Conversion System is implemented as a Stirling engine configuration.

Assumption A.25 is important for the design of a DCSA because it establishes the reference Power
Conversion System configuration and informs its control surface and the interdependencies between
the Power Conversion System and the rest of the plant. Unlike the Heat Transport System and the
Residual Heat Removal System, the Power Conversion System contains an active control surface,
including the sensors and actuators that regulate engine operation and the synchronization of the
electrical output to the load. Further, the Power Conversion System is a non-safety-related, balance-
of-plant system. This implies that a cyber-attack against the Power Conversion System impacts the
availability of electrical generation rather than the safety of the reactor, which achieves safe
shutdown and decay heat removal independently of the Power Conversion System.

4.8. Functional Containment System (FCS)
The purpose of the Functional Containment System (FCS) is to retain radionuclides and control
their release to the environment under normal operation and postulated accident conditions. In
advanced reactor and microreactor designs, the containment function is implemented as a functional
containment, a performance-based set of barriers that together limit radionuclide release to within a
dose criterion, rather than as a single large pressure-retaining containment building. This approach is
established in General Design Criterion and the corresponding Advanced Reactor Design Criterion
[16]. The functions of the FCS are [55, 86]:
 1. Retain radionuclides within the functional containment barriers.
 2. Maintain the integrity of the functional containment boundary under normal and accident
 conditions.
 3. Detect and monitor the loss of functional containment integrity.
The functional containment of a microreactor comprises multiple barriers between the radionuclide
source term and the environment. These barriers typically include the fuel particle or fuel matrix, the
fuel cladding, the primary coolant boundary, and an outer radiological boundary [55, 86]. The
number and nature of the barriers depend on the fuel form and the reactor configuration. In gas-
cooled designs that use TRISO fuel, the coated fuel particle itself retains the majority of the fission

 67

products, and the silicon carbide layer of the particle acts as a miniature pressure vessel for each fuel
kernel [44].
In liquid-metal pool designs that use metallic fuel, the fuel cladding and the primary coolant
boundary serve as the principal barriers, and the guard vessel described in Section 3.6 provides an
additional radiological boundary [90]. Across the microreactor field, the outer radiological boundary
is generally a sealed, leak-tight structure rather than a vented pressure-suppression containment,
which is consistent with the compact, factory-sealed, and transportable nature of microreactor
designs [81].
The MARVEL safety analysis provides the most completely documented instantiation of these
barriers for the liquid-metal pool configuration and identifies three confinement barriers: the fuel
cladding provides the first barrier; the primary coolant system boundary, including the reactor barrel
and its downcomers, provides the second barrier by retaining the primary sodium-potassium and any
leaked fission or activation products while keeping oxygen outside the boundary; and the guard
vessel provides the third barrier. The safety analysis situates this confinement function within the
plant safety case as one of three fundamental safety functions, alongside reactivity control and core
heat removal, each performed by passive barriers and physical hardware rather than by active
systems [98]. The MARVEL fundamental safety functions and their confinement barriers are shown
in Figure 24.

 Figure 24. MARVEL Fundamental Safety Functions and Containment Barriers [98]

The SOLO design provides the most complete public documentation of a microreactor functional
containment and is used here as the reference implementation. In the SOLO design, the functional
containment is provided by an Integrated Radiological Containment (IRC). The IRC is a fully
welded and sealed pressure boundary that encloses the helium coolant system and the safety-
significant components, surrounded by a Monolith, a non-pressurized structural block that provides
biological shielding, protection against external hazards, and passive decay heat removal. The IRC
and the Monolith together implement a layered containment strategy [55]

 68

Using this approach, the IRC has no traditional containment penetrations and no active isolation
valves. This is due to the piping systems that traverse the IRC, which are constructed as leak-tight
extensions of the IRC boundary. Loss of containment integrity is detected by monitoring the helium
pressure within the IRC and by external radiation sensors, rather than by active isolation [55].
Therefore, our plant design assumption is:
 A.26. The Functional Containment System is a sealed, leak-tight boundary that retains
 radionuclides without reliance on active isolation valves.

Assumption A.26 is important for the design of a DCSA because the functional containment is a
physical robustness factor that may mitigate or eliminate the consequences of a cyber-attack. A
sealed, leak-tight functional containment retains the radionuclide source term even if other plant
systems are compromised. Further, it does not rely on active isolation valves, implying that a threat
cannot impact the containment function by manipulating a valve actuator. The cyber-accessible
control surface of the Functional Containment System is therefore limited to the sensors that
monitor containment pressure and external radiation, with no actuators in the containment function
itself. This sensor-only control surface, combined with the passive retention of the source term,
makes the functional containment a strong non-cyber independent protection layer.

4.9. Distributed Control System (DCS)
The purpose of the Distributed Control System (DCS) is to coordinate control actions across the
plant systems to maintain operations and to transition the plant between operating states. The
functions of the DCS are [66, 85, 83, 93]:
 1. Coordinate control actions between plant systems to maintain operations.
 2. Coordinate control actions between plant systems to transition the plant between operating
 states.
 3. Support autonomous or remotely supervised operation.
The DCS is a supervisory control system that uses sensor data from multiple plant systems to
coordinate actuation among those systems. A distinguishing characteristic of microreactor control is
the emphasis on autonomous or remotely supervised operation, because microreactors are designed
for deployment at remote sites with minimal or no on-site staffing. Autonomous control
technologies for microreactors are being developed and demonstrated through national laboratory
programs, and the MARVEL design includes a documented instrumentation and control system that
performs the supervisory control function [81, 85, 83, 93, 97].
The MARVEL design provides the most complete public documentation of a microreactor control
system and is used here as the reference implementation, consistent with the anchoring established
in the preceding sections. In the MARVEL design, the control system measures plant parameters
through a reactor instrumentation subsystem, evaluates control and trip criteria, and issues
commands to the reactivity control and other plant systems, while a human-machine interface allows
operators to supervise and intervene in plant operation [83]. The MARVEL systems and their
interfaces are shown in Figure 25 and the control approach of the reference design is summarized in

4.10. Reactor Protection System (RPS)
The purpose of the Reactor Protection System (RPS) is to sense conditions that require a reactor
trip and to initiate the trip independently of the Distributed Control System. The functions of the
RPS are [83, 86]:
 1. Sense design basis accident conditions.
 2. Initiate a reactor trip to prevent the release of radionuclides in response to design basis
 accident conditions.
The RPS is a safety-related system that operates independently of the Distributed Control System.
To perform its functions, the RPS detects a condition that requires intervention and initiates a
reactor trip, which removes power from the reactivity control actuators so that the control drums
rotate to the shutdown position and the shutdown elements insert into the core. The MARVEL
design provides the most complete public documentation of a microreactor protection system and is
used here as the reference implementation [83]. The trip criteria monitored by the reference design
are summarized in Table VII.

5. MICROREACTOR DCSA DESIGN
A generic microreactor DCSA template was derived from the analysis performed in the preceding
sections as an example of the application of cybersecurity considerations to the DCSA as part of a
graded approach. First, the functions identified in Section 4 are assigned to security levels according
to their importance to plant safety. Second, systems are assigned to security zones based on the
logical and physical communication requirements between the systems. Finally, remote operations
and transport modes of microreactor and their DCSA design decisions are considered.
Security levels are assigned to functions based on their importance to plant safety. Systems that
perform multiple functions are placed into a security zone based on the security level assigned to the
system's most important function. Based on the functions enumerated in Section 4, systems are
categorized as being likely to be licensed as one of the following categories for systems, structures,
and components (SSCs): safety-related (SR), non-safety related with special treatment (NSRST), or

Security level 1 consists of a zone containing the IT network, business systems, and engineering
systems. Systems in this level have access to the Internet through a firewall. Security level 1 is the
only security level where wireless networks are permitted. Portable media and mobile devices are
widely used in these systems within this security level. Systems in this zone are within the plant
exclusion area (EA) and may be contained within an area of greater physical protection such as a
limited access area (LAA).
Security level 2 consists of three zones containing authorized document management systems, work
control systems, and the engineering historian. Portable media are used within systems in these
security levels. Systems within this zone are within the plant protected area (PA). Bidirectional wired
network communication through a firewall is permitted between security levels 1 and 2.
Security level 3 consists of several zones containing both NSRST and NST plant systems and
supervisory control systems. The main control room (MCR) human-machine interface (HMI) and
DCS serve as supervisory controllers. Operators in the MCR may interface with the DCS through
local HMI on site or through the remote HMI, which is described in Section 5.1.1. The relationships
between the DCS and its subordinate systems are discussed in Section 4. Any portable media or
mobile devices brought from a lower security zone to a zone belonging to security level 3 must first
be processed through a portable media and mobile device scanner. This may be necessary for system
updates or maintenance. Systems in this level are in the MCR within the plant vital area (VA). Wired
network communication into security level 2 from security level 3 is permitted (e.g., the engineering
historian receives data from the operations historian), but security level 2 is only permitted to send
handshaking or acknowledgement signals to security level 3.
Security level 4 consists of zones containing the plant SR systems RPS and RSS. Analog signals are
used for communications from the RPS to the RSS. Similar to security level 3, any portable media or
mobile devices brought into security level 4 must first be scanned. The systems in this level are in
the instrumentation room (IR) within the plant vital area (VA). The IR is separated from the MCR.
One-way communication enforced by a data diode is permitted from security level 4 to security
levels 3 and 2.
Security level 4 also contains a zone for the Physical Protection System (PPS). Cyber-physical force-
on-force analysis of an advanced reactor facility showed that compromise of PPS detection systems,
such as cameras and fence sensors, reduces the effectiveness of response force. As a result, the PPS
is protected at the highest security level and is isolated from the plant control systems.
Performance-based analysis may require that PPS detection systems with a significant combined
effect be assigned to separate zones within security level 4, or may allow some PPS subsystems to be
assigned to lower security levels [101].

5.1.1. DCSA Consideration for Remote Monitoring
Microreactors are designed for autonomous or semi-autonomous operation with remote monitoring
and control. In this DCSA template, we only consider remote monitoring and its cybersecurity
requirements. It is shown in Figure 27 as an off-site zone within security level 3. The remote HMI
connects to the supervisory control zones through a pair of firewalls that span the site boundary.
This conduit is the only connection between the reactor site and any location outside the PA that
carries control traffic.
The remote HMI is limited to the supervisory functions. It cannot reach the RPS or RSS in security
level 4 because no digital conduit enters security level 4. Reactor trips remain hardwired and local.
Remote commands cannot bypass the interlock relays described in Section 4. Plant data required off

 77

site is delivered through the data diode path to the operations historian and then forwarded through
the supervisory firewalls.
The remote HMI must meet the same cybersecurity requirements as the local HMI. Physical
protection of the remote location is a licensee responsibility and is outside the scope of this report.
If the remote location cannot meet security level 3 physical protection requirements, the remote
HMI shall be limited to monitoring only. Additional analysis is required to determine the latency and
availability requirements of the remote conduit and the plant response when the conduit is lost.
Future work should examine whether a single remote HMI may supervise multiple microreactor
sites without creating a common conduit across otherwise independent DCSAs. Additionally, since
this template only considers remote monitoring, the data is unidirectional from the operational
historian to the remote HMI. When considering autonomous operations, future work should
examine the DCSA architecture for the bidirectional communication and what systems the remote
operating has access to.

5.1.2. DCSA Considerations for Transport Mode
Microreactors have a transport operating mode in which the sealed module is moved to or from the
deployment site with the shutdown elements secured in the fully inserted position. During transport
the module is outside the site boundary and outside the physical protection areas of the template.
The DCS, the HMI, and the balance of plant are not connected. Instrumentation is limited to the
monitoring and security instrumentation on the transport package.
The template in Figure 27 applies to the start-up, energy production, and shutdown modes. A
separate DCSA applies to transport mode. It consists of the transport monitoring instrumentation,
the security instrumentation, and the link that reports their status. No control surface exists in this
mode, because the shutdown elements are mechanically secured and the reactor is subcritical. The
transport DCSA is a monitoring architecture whose principal requirement is the integrity and
availability of the status reporting link. As such, its design is recommended for further analysis.

6. CONCLUSION
AR designs can consider cybersecurity from the start of the design process. This avoids the wrap-
around security measures applied to the existing fleet. Designers are treating effective cybersecurity
as a fundamental part of the design basis of the reactor. This provides an opportunity to reduce the
cost and effort of establishing effective cybersecurity programs through the integration of
cybersecurity analysis.
This report presents the DCSA design for a microreactor. The analysis approach is consistent with
the TCA detailed in the U.S. Regulatory Guide 5.96, “Establishing Cybersecurity Programs for
Commercial Nuclear Plants Licensed Under 10 CFR Part 53” [4]. The TCA leverages the SeBD
features of the plant as the foundation of cybersecurity analysis. A DCSA designed as part of the
TCA approach denies the adversary access to the plant functions needed to cause an accident
sequence that is unmitigated by the physical design of the plant.
The physical design of a microreactor mitigates more accident sequences than the designs treated in
the previous reports. The primary coolant circulates by natural convection. Decay heat is removed
by natural circulation of air. The functional containment is sealed and requires no active isolation.
The reactor trips on loss of power, and the reactivity control elements return to the shutdown
position by stored mechanical energy or gravity. No fuel is handled on site. These features are
physical robustness factors and non-cyber IPLs that reduce the cyber-accessible control surface of
the plant to the sensors that monitor the safety systems, the actuators of the balance of plant, the
supervisory controller, and the remote operations link. As such, the DCSA template for a
microreactor contains fewer zones when compared to the templates for an HTGR or an SFR [2, 3].
Further, the chokepoint of the template is the conduit between the site and the remote operations
center rather than a boundary within the site.
This report also addresses the decoupling of the nuclear island from the energy island. Physical
decoupling reduces the influence of the energy island on the nuclear island to a small set of bounded
initiating conditions. Cyber decoupling aligns the cyber boundary with the physical boundary and
enforces it through unidirectional exports, explicit gateways, and change control. Together they
ensure that a compromise of the energy island affects the availability of electrical generation but
does not defeat the safety functions of the plant.
This report was written to demonstrate DCSA design approaches and to provide a template DCSA
design for a microreactor to be available for industry use. It is important to note that the DCSA
design template and the decoupling strategy provided in this report are intended to serve as starting
points for AR designers and are not prescriptive. Further optimization of the DCSA design may be
valuable given the unique design and performance requirements of the plant.
Passive cybersecurity controls may be assigned to each security level for the physical access, wired,
wireless, and portable media attack pathways, in a manner similar to what was done in the SFR
report [3]. Finally, the DCSA for the transport operating mode and the DCSA for the physical
protection system of a remotely controlled, transportable reactor require dedicated analysis.
