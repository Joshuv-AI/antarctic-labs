Cognitive profiles of patients with enlarged lateral ventricles and cognitive impairment among patients with schizophrenia
Authors: Junya Matsumoto, Satsuki Ito, Chika Sumiyoshi, Yuka Yasuda, Shin Saitoh, Michiko Fujimoto, Hidenaga Yamamori, Tomiki Sumiyoshi, Ryota Hashimoto
Published: 2026

Treatment for psychiatric disorders is based on operational diagnostic criteria derived from patients' subjective experiences and behavioral observations. Some patients respond to treatment, whereas others remain treatment-resistant. Therefore, to develop more effective treatments, an objective diagnostic concept based on neurobiological features is needed. A previous large-scale data-driven study involving subcortical brain volumes identified brain biotypes associated with cognitive and social functioning across major psychiatric disorders (Okada et al., 2023). Based on these findings, a subgroup characterized by enlarged lateral ventricles (EV) and cognitive impairment (CI), termed EVCI, was proposed as a candidate neurobiological subgroup (Yasuda et al., 2025a).

The initial report of EVCI described a high prevalence of schizophrenia, electroencephalographic abnormalities, and rare pathogenic copy number variations. Subsequent case series identified additional clinical features, including white matter hyperintensities, treatment-resistant schizophrenia, and favorable response to clozapine (Yasuda et al., 2025b). EVCI was subsequently replicated (Onitsuka et al., 2026).

Lateral ventricular enlargement, often observed in patients with schizophrenia (Weinberger et al., 1979), has been associated with cognitive dysfunction (Johnstone et al., 1976) and longitudinal IQ changes (Kubota et al., 2015). In first-episode psychosis, a significant enlargement of the third ventricle, but not the lateral ventricles, has been reported, which was associated with reduced verbal memory (Costas-Carrera et al., 2024). Given these findings, the present study aimed to investigate cognitive profiles in patients with schizophrenia by comparing those who met the EVCI criteria with those who did not.

A total of 1443 participants were included (Table S1). Among 275 patients with schizophrenia, eight patients were classified as having EVCI (EVCI group), whereas 267 patients with schizophrenia without EVCI (SZ group); both groups were recruited at Osaka University Hospital. Each patient was diagnosed by at least two trained psychiatrists according to the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV), or Fifth Edition (DSM-5), based on the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM (SCID). A total of 1168 healthy controls (HCs) from the Osaka community were recruited. Part of these patients and HCs also participated in prior neuroimaging studies (Fujino et al., 2017; Matsumoto et al., 2023a; Matsumoto et al., 2023c; Nemoto et al., 2020; Okada et al., 2023; Yasuda et al., 2025a; Yasuda et al., 2025b; Yasuda et al., 2020). Detailed inclusion criteria were described in the supplementary methods. Lateral ventricular volumes were measured from 3DT1 images acquired using three scanners and three protocols (Supplementary Methods). Lateral ventricular volume was analyzed alongside intracranial volume (ICV) using FreeSurfer (Fischl, 2012). Harmonization using general linear model residuals was performed as previously described (Matsumoto et al., 2023b; Nemoto et al., 2020; Okada et al., 2023; Yasuda et al., 2025a; Yasuda et al., 2025b). For each scanner and imaging protocol, residuals of lateral ventricular volume were calculated from a regression model based on HCs, with age, sex, and intracranial volume as covariates. Residuals were converted to Z scores using HC means and standard deviations. The presence of an EV was defined as a Z score of ≥3. CI was defined as a current IQ at least 20 points lower than the premorbid IQ estimated using the Japanese Adult Reading Test (JART) as described previously.

Eighteen cognitive measures were examined, including:

(1) CI,

(2) Premorbid IQ,

(3) Current IQ,

(4–7) Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, Third Edition (WAIS-III) subindices (WAIS-VC, Verbal Comprehension; WAIS-PO, Perceptual Organization; WAIS-WM, Working Memory; and WAIS-PS, Processing Speed),

(8–12) Wechsler Memory Scale-Revised (WMS-R) subindices (WMS-VM, Verbal Memory; WMS-VMem, Visual Memory; WMS-GM, General Memory; WMS-AC, Attention/Concentration; and WMS—DR, Delayed Recall),

(13–14) Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT) Immediate and Delayed Recall,

(15–16) Verbal Fluency (VF) semantic category and letter fluency,

(17) Continuous Performance Test d' mean (CPT-D′), and.

(18) Wisconsin Card Sorting Test Categories Completed (WCST-CC),

in accordance with our previous study (Matsumoto et al., 2026).

All cognitive measures were converted to HC-based Z scores before group comparisons. Details of the standardization procedures are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Due to the fact that the assumption of homogeneity of variance was rejected via the Levene's test, Welch's one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. Post-hoc comparisons were conducted using the Games-Howell method. Because post-hoc tests were performed for 18 cognitive measures, a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of 2.8 × 10−3 (0.05/18) was applied.

The EVCI group demonstrated significant impairments compared with the HC group in 10 of the 18 cognitive measures, while the SZ group performed significantly worse than the HC group for all cognitive measures. The pattern of cognitive profile was broadly similar between the EVCI group and the SZ group (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S2). Direct comparisons between the EVCI and SZ groups indicated significantly greater impairments in CI, current IQ, and the Perceptual Organization Index of the WAIS. The observed differences in CI and current IQ between the EVCI and SZ groups may partly reflect definitional overlap rather than independent validation, because CI, operationalized in part using IQ measures, is incorporated into the definition of EVCI. Larger effect sizes of impairment for the EVCI group in comparison with the SZ group were noted across all cognitive measures. Additional analyses adjusting for CP-equivalent dose and the use of high-anticholinergic antipsychotics yielded similar results (Supplementary Results), suggesting that medication-related factors alone are unlikely to fully explain the observed cognitive impairment.

In the present study, even in the SZ group, the overall pattern of impairments across cognitive measures was similar to that observed in the EVCI group. The latter may have failed to reach statistical significance because the EVCI group comprised only eight patients. Conversely, the small sample size itself may reflect the fact that the EVCI group represents a statistically extreme subgroup, rather than simply insufficient sampling. In any case, statistical power for comparisons involving the EVCI group was limited. With the present sample size, assuming a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, only large effect sizes (approximately Cohen's d ≥ 1.0) could be reliably detected. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as evidence of no between-group difference, and these analyses should be considered exploratory. Regarding cognitive impairment, patients with schizophrenia can be classified into three cognitive subgroups: those with CI, those without CI, and those with persistently low premorbid IQ (Fujino et al., 2017; Matsumoto et al., 2023c; Weickert et al., 2000; Yasuda et al., 2020). Patients with schizophrenia who exhibit CI can be further stratified according to the presence or absence of EV, and the subgroup with both EV and CI may represent a more severe phenotype, reflecting the co-occurrence of pronounced ventricular enlargement and substantial cognitive decline.

A previous study reported that the cognitive profile of EV(+)/CI(−) patients was similar to that of EVCI patients, although cognitive impairment was milder in the former group (Matsumoto et al., 2026). On the other hand, EV(−)/CI(+) patients showed a cognitive profile similar to that of EVCI patients. Furthermore, the SZ group showed cognitive impairment profile similar to that of EV(−)/CI(+) and EV(+)/CI(−) groups in the previous study. Most cognitive Z scores in the SZ group fell between those of the EV(−)/CI(+) and EV(+)/CI(−) groups and showed a cognitive profile similar to both groups and the EVCI group. Whether the EVCI group represents a prognostic marker or simply the extreme end of ventricular enlargement and cognitive impairment associated with schizophrenia remains unclear.

This study has several limitations, including the small sample size of the EVCI group, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies in larger cohorts are needed to confirm the reproducibility of these findings.
