2. Background:
 The SMART project was conceived to address the substantial opportunity presented by
 low-grade industrial waste heat recovery. Industrial operations generate up to two-thirds of
 their wasted heat at temperatures below 225°C, a portion that conventional thermoelectric
 generators (TEGs) utilize ineffectively, with efficiency limited to 5-8%. SMART’s
 technical approach focused on the development of Johnson-Thermo-Electrochemical
 Converter (JTEC) technologies, particularly through the design and optimization of
 advanced membrane electrode assemblies (MEAs). By targeting a heat-to-power efficiency
 threshold above 25% and increasing technology readiness from TRL 3 to TRL 4, the
 project sought to overcome longstanding bottlenecks in waste heat valorization and to
 deliver scalable, commercially relevant solutions for decarbonization of industrial sectors.

 While planned work was concluded after BP1 as directed by the DOE, outcomes from this
 initial budget period provide valuable learnings and a foundation for potential future
 advancement of JTEC technologies, either through alternative funding or collaborative
 research avenues.

 3.1 Review of JTEC device operation mechanism

 Figure 1. JTEC device functional diagram and the entropy diagram.

 The JTEC solid-state heat engine operates on the Ericsson cycle using a continuous
 circulation of hydrogen gas, as illustrated in Figure 1. The process involves four distinct
 steps, with the gas passing through MEAs at low temperature for compression and high
 temperature for expansion. The cycle consists of the following stages:

  Isothermal electrochemical compression: at low temperature, the hydrogen gas
 is compressed from a low-pressure state (State 1) to a high-pressure state (State 2),
 with energy input (WL) and heat removal (QL) through a heat sink.
  Isobaric heating: the high-pressure hydrogen gas is heated up as it passes through
 a heat exchanger (State 2 to State 3).
  Isothermal electrochemical Expansion: at high temperature, the hydrogen gas
 expands from a high-pressure state (State 3) to a low-pressure state (State 4),
 generating energy output (WH) and adsorbing external heat (QH).

  Isobaric cooling: the low-pressure hydrogen gas cooled as it flows through the
 recuperative heat exchanger (State 4 to State 1).

The cycle repeats itself, with the heat addition (Q H) exceeding the heat rejection (QL). The
resulting heat energy difference (QH - QL) is equivalent to the power energy difference (WH
- WL), representing the conversion of thermal energy to electrical energy output by the heat
engine during the complete cycle. The device can continuously circulate hydrogen,
generating excess electrical energy to perform useful work and feed external electrical
loads.

The efficiency of the Ericsson cycle approaches the Carnot efficiency, which is the
maximum efficiency with which heat energy can be converted into useful work. The MEA
converts pressure potential into voltage potential. The voltage of the MEA cell is defined
by the Nernst relationship, as given in Equation 1, where VOC is the open circuit voltage, R
is the universal gas constant, T is the cell temperature, F is Faraday’s constant, and PH/PL
is the pressure differential ratio on the two sides of the MEA. As shown in this equation,
both higher temperature and larger pressure differential ratio lead to a higher open circuit
voltage.

The Nernst voltage from the hydrogen pressure differential across a MEA in JTEC devices
is typically in the range of 0.1V-0.2V. To improve energy conversion efficiency and
increase electrical output, it is important to develop high proton conductive MEAs that can
sustain higher pressure differentials.

3.2 Literature survey: potential proton conductive membranes for JTEC devices
The ideal proton conductive membrane for JTEC devices should possess the following key
characteristics:
  High proton conductivity: a minimum of 50 mS cm-1 to ensure efficient proton
 transfer.
  Electron and chemical species insulation: to prevent electrical short circuits and
 minimize H2 crossover.
  Thermal stability: to withstand operation temperature around 225°C.
  High tensile strength and pressure tolerance: to resist mechanical stress and
 pressure differentials between the two sides of the membrane.
  Easy to manufacture and handle: to facilitate large-scale. production and
 deployment.

Proton conductive materials for JTEC devices can be broadly categorized into four groups:
  Perfluoro-sulfonic acids (PFASs) and PFAS-based composites: these materials
 are known for their high proton conductivity but may not be suitable for
 intermediate temperature applications.
  Polybenzimidazoles (PBI) and PBI-based composites: PBI-based materials
 have shown promise for high-temperature applications and can be modified to
 achieve improved proton conductivity and stability.
  Other hydrocarbon-based proton exchange membranes (PEMs), such as
 SPEEK and SPPSU: these materials have been explored for their potential in

 proton conduction but may not be suitable for intermediate temperature
 applications.
  Tetravalent metal pyrophosphates: these inorganic materials have demonstrated
 high proton conductivity and could be used to develop membranes that operate at
 intermediate temperatures.

Among these categories, materials in Category b (PBI-based composites) and Category d
(tetravalent metal pyrophosphates) show promise for developing membranes that can
operate at intermediate temperatures, specifically around 225°C. Improving the properties
of these materials can lead to the development of high-performance proton conductive
membranes for JTEC devices. The following tables will provide a literature survey of
materials in Categories b and d, focusing on the latest developments and advancements in
PBI-based composites and tetravalent metal pyrophosphates. This survey aims to identify
the current state of research in these areas and highlight potential opportunities for further
development and improvement.

PBI-based materials have been explored for their potential in proton conduction, with a
focus on their application in high-temperature devices. Table 1 lists the PBI-based
materials considered in this survey, with data reported at dry conditions. Inspired by fuel
cells, PBI membranes have been used to imbibe phosphoric acid, enabling proton
conduction in the 160-230 °C range. Phosphoric acid imbibed PBI (PA-PBI) membranes
have demonstrated high proton conductivities, reaching up to 250 mS/cm, and stabilities
exceeding 1200 hours. However, standalone PBI membranes suffer from limitations such
as phosphoric acid leaching, poor tensile strength, and swelling at high temperatures (>120
°C).

 Table 1: PBI-based materials considered in this survey.

To address these issues, PBI membranes have been blended with complementary
polycationic polymers, such as quaternary polystyrene-co-polyvinylbenzyl chloride (PBI-
IP) or quaternary pyridinium polysulfone (PBI-QPPSf), which exhibit improved
performance. These blends show lower swelling in phosphoric acid, reduced phosphoric
acid leaching, and retain proton conductivities comparable to PBI. Additionally, PBI has
been blended with various oxides, including titanium, silicon, and aluminum, which
operate like composite PFSA membranes, withholding moisture and trapping phosphoric
acid at elevated temperatures. The conductivities of these PBI-based composite membranes

range from 100-300 mS/cm. Overall, PBI-based materials offer a promising solution for
JTEC devices, with potential for high proton conductivity, oxidative and thermal stability,
and cost-effectiveness. Further research and development are needed to optimize the
performance of PBI-based membranes and address the challenges associated with their use
in JTEC devices.

Table 2 shows tetravalent metal phosphates materials considered in this survey. Tetravalent
metal phosphates, also known as pyrophosphates, are a class of inorganic proton
conductors that exhibit proton conductivities in the order of 100 mS/cm. The absence of
polymeric membranes allows these materials to maintain their properties in the
intermediate temperature range, making them suitable for the development of intermediate
temperature electrochemical energy conversion devices, such as JTEC. Notably, cerium
phosphates have shown a proton conductivity of 42 mS/cm at 250 °C in their pure form,
which can be further enhanced to 140 mS/cm when doped with boron phosphate and
supported on graphene oxide. The boron phosphate also acts as an inorganic polymer
binder, adding to the material's overall performance. Similarly, tin phosphates have
demonstrated proton conduction between 17-60 mS/cm, with improved conduction upon
doping with phosphoric acid. Other inorganic materials, such as heteropolyacids like
cesium dihydrogen phosphate, have also shown promise with a conductivity of 11 mS/cm
at 240 °C.

 Table 2: Tetravalent metal phosphate considered in this survey.

Although current studies on these materials are limited to proof-of-concept development
and testing in fuel cells, their potential as proton conductors in JTEC devices is significant.
It is envisioned that incorporating tetravalent metal phosphates and heteropolyacids into
composite membranes, in combination with polymeric materials, could lead to higher
proton conductivities at JTEC-relevant temperatures. This approach could potentially
overcome the limitations of individual materials and create high-performance proton
conductive membranes for JTEC devices. Further research is needed to explore the
applicability and promise of these materials in JTEC applications and to fully realize their
potential in intermediate temperature electrochemical energy conversion devices.

3.3 JTEC Test Platform Design, Acquisition, and Commissioning
The JTEC test platform was developed through close collaboration between JTEC Energy
Inc. and GEVAR. The platform encompasses both a detailed sub-assembly, a half-cell
JTEC device, and a full assembly serving as a comprehensive test rig. The principal cell
fixture features an active area of 50 cm² (approximately 7.75 square inches, or 2.75 inches

by 2.75 inches), allowing for the evaluation of large membrane electrode assemblies
(MEAs). For flexibility, the rig includes a reducer mechanism to accommodate smaller
MEAs as required. Key design elements focused on both experimental robustness and
practical operation. Knife-edge seals were implemented between the MEAs and structural
components to maintain a secure seal, minimizing the likelihood of mechanical rupture. In
addition, the system’s internal electrical and thermal resistances were carefully optimized
to enhance current density, power density, and overall device performance. Figure 2 and
Figure 3 illustrate the JTEC subassembly and full assembly configurations.

 Figure 2. JTEC sub-assembly design package.

 Figure 3. JTEC full-assembly design package.

During Q1 of 2025, JTEC Energy fabricated and assembled the test rig, which was
subsequently installed and commissioned at GEV (Figure 4). The completed setup
integrates two 50 cm² polymer electrolyte cell fixtures connected via a heat exchanger,
supplemented with temperature controllers, pressure sensors, outflow shutoff valves, data
acquisition hardware, custom Python scripts, and a dual-channel potentiostat for real-time

current and voltage measurement. Typical operating procedures involved purging the
system with nitrogen, introducing hydrogen at controlled pressures, conducting leak
checks, and ramping the hot-side temperature to 160°C for operational thermal gradients.
Hydrogen compression cycles were initiated by applying a positive voltage (250–350 mV)
to the cold cell electrode, with pressure sensors verifying successful H₂ compression and
achieving vacuum levels down to –13.6 psig. The resulting compression currents started at
15 mA/cm² and decreased as hydrogen was depleted from the low-pressure side. Upon
reaching the desired pressure differential, open circuit potential (OCP) measurements were
taken on the high-temperature cell, demonstrating a galvanic response (OCP ~47.5 mV).

3.4 MEA Development Path I: PBI-Inorganic Composite Membranes
The development of MEAs for this project was pursued through three distinct pathways,
all led and executed by GEVAR and its partners, excluding University of Chicago
participation. First, GEVAR sourced and evaluated commercial proton-conductive
membranes—including Pemion, Celtec-P, and metaPBI—capable of operating at elevated
temperatures (150°C to 180°C), to establish a performance baseline for high-temperature
MEAs. Second, GEVAR initiated exploratory synthesis and fabrication of polymer-
inorganic composite membranes, focusing on combinations such as PBI with TiO₂, SnO₂,
and additional additives to enhance thermal and electrochemical properties. Precursors for
PBI and additive-based membranes including PBI dispersions, platinum catalyst, and
ionomers were procured and used in iterative membrane development. Third, GEVAR
advanced efforts in inorganic membrane development, collaborating closely with its
thermal spray team to fabricate and characterize metal oxide-based membranes (such as
SnO₂ and SnP₂O₇) using thermal spray processes. Due to administrative delays and early
contract termination, collaboration with University of Chicago on polymer-based MEA

development was never initiated, and no work or financial expenditures occurred from that
partner during the reporting period.

Thermal spray process selection
Effective thermal management and resistance of
the substrate are crucial considerations for this
program, as the envisioned substrate materials are
expected to be thermally sensitive to the heat
generated by the thermal spray process.
Furthermore, the selected thermal spray process
must be compatible with the feedstock material.
Given these requirements, plasma spray has been
identified as the primary thermal spray process for
evaluation. This selection is based on prior
experience, which suggests that plasma spray may
offer the most manageable heat transfer profile to
the substrate, compared to alternative combustion-
based processes such as High-Velocity Oxy-Fuel
(HVOF) or High-Velocity Air Fuel (HVAF),
which may also be suitable for depositing ceramic
coatings of interest to this program.

 injection characteristics to the plasma plume generated. Suitable particle size
 distribution for injection into a plasma spray process are typically in the 10-200µm
 range. Finer particles (<10µm) are extremely difficult to properly feed and tend to clog
 the feeding lines and/or not have sufficient momentum to penetrate the plasma plume.
 Larger particles (>200µm) tend to overshoot through the plasma plume and/or not have
 sufficient dwell time into the plasma plume to properly melt. Because the initial
 candidate materials procured are off-the-shelf products, they are not optimized for
 injection into a thermal spray process. Figure 13 shows SEM images of two of the
 powders procured so far. In these examples, the SnO2 material is composed of very fine
 primary particles loosely agglomerate, whereas the agglomerated in the Sn 2P2O7
 powder seem to be more cohesive. As expected, we found that SnO 2 powder is not
 suitable for thermal spray, but tests with Sn2P2O7 are very successful.
(2) The second risk associated with using SnO2 and Sn2P2O7 as feedstock material is their
 stability when treated by the plasma spray process. Evaporation, decomposition and
 phase stability are some of the potential drawbacks of injecting these materials into a
 plasma plume. If we are successful in feeding SnO 2 and/or Sn2P2O7 materials into the
 process, and at producing coatings in those initial trials, analysis will be performed to
 assess and characterize those potential changes.

Deposition of ceramic layers on various porous, conductive substrates suitable for use as

with a small diameter nozzle. The spray conditions were selected to reduce heat input to
the substrate, by increasing torch to work distance, and increasing torch traverse speed.
The results of this trial were promising, with complete coating coverage obtained with
minimal damage to one of the carbon GDL’s, as well as the Ni mesh. The table below
contains the results from porous GDL substrate APS trials.

 Table 4: Results from porous GDL substrate APS trials.

Following these promising results with the GDL substrates, the focus shifted to deposition
of the proton conducting membrane by thermal spray. Two powder materials were sourced
for these trials, Sn2P2O7, and SnO2. The intention was to deposit by APS a layer which
could be post treated to obtain a dense, gas impermeable membrane with high proton
conductivity.

The equipment selected for these trials was the G4 volumetric powder feeder (Uniquecoat
Technologies, Oilville, VA) and a high enthalpy plasma torch. The powder feeder was
selected for its unique feed mechanism which enables feeding of a wide range of particle
sizes and morphologies. The plasma torch was selected for its stable operation over a wide
range of plasma process conditions, as well as its injection configuration which allows high
carrier gas flows, which is useful for fluidization and conveyance of less-than-optimal
particle size and morphologies.

The SnO2 powder (9SN-601, AEE Upper Saddle River, NJ) was determined to be
unsuitable for thermal spray, and attempts to feed the powder and inject it into the plasma
plume were unsuccessful. Other commercially available SnO2 Powders were analyzed by
SEM and found to be similar in particle size and morphology. Without agglomeration by
spray drying, or other methods, these powders were not suitable, and efforts were shifted
away from the procured SnO2 powder.

Trials with Sn2P2O7 powder (SN2PPAT-02M, American Elements) were far more
successful. The powder fed well and without clogging or pulsation between 10 and
60g/minute feed rates, and Argon carrier gas flow rates between 60 and 100 scfh. These
results warranted progression to APS spray trials with this material.

Materials Characterization
To visualize and investigate the effects of different spray conditions on the resulting
thermal sprayed Sn2P2O7 coatings, scanning electron microscope (SEM) along with energy
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) were used to characterize the
coating’s surface morphology, chemical composition, and phase constitution, respectively.
Figure 14 shows an overview of the as-received feedstock Sn 2P2O7 powder and some top-
down SEM images of the APS Sn2P2O7 coatings deposited under spray conditions A to C.
The torch enthalpy for each spray conditions are superimposed on the images for easy
references and direct comparison.

 Figure 14. SEM images of as-received Sn2P2O7 powder and APS Sn2P2O7 coatings deposited under
 conditions A to C. Torch enthalpy is shown in backets for each spray condition.

The top-down SEM images reveal evident variations in surface morphology among
different spray conditions. Notably, spray conditions A and B with higher torch enthalpy
resulted in splats, indicating significant particle melting and re-solidification. In contrast,
fragments of Sn2P2O7 powder were observed for spray condition C, suggesting that the
torch enthalpy was insufficient to melt the particles and that particle impacting and
deformation was predominant leading to particle shattering into small pieces.

The different spray conditions also resulted in different material crystallinity in the thermal
sprayed Sn2P2O7 coatings, as suggested by the XRD spectra of the as-received Sn 2P2O7
powder and APS Sn2P2O7 coatings in Fig. 15. Due to significant particle melting and fast
cooling, the Sn2P2O7 coatings deposited under conditions A and B are mostly comprised
of amorphous phase (shown as a bump in the XRD spectra). In comparison, separate and
distinct XRD peaks were observed in the Sn2P2O7 coatings deposited under condition C,
confirming the coating’s crystalline nature. However, there were some shifts in the peak
intensities and peak positions when comparing with that of the as-received Sn 2P2O7
powder, indicating that phase transformation occurred during the thermal spray process.

Figure 15. XRD spectra of as-received powder and APS Sn2P2O7 coatings deposited under conditions
 A to C.

The characterization results above demonstrate that the thermal spray conditions,
particularly the torch enthalpy, have a great influence on the surface morphology and phase
constitution of the resulting Sn2P2O7 coatings. To verify these findings, further thermal
spray trials (Conditions D to F) with a gradual increase in torch enthalpy were carried out.
Figure 16 shows a summary of the XRD spectra and SEM images collected for the as-
received Sn2P2O7 powder and thermal sprayed Sn2P2O7 coatings deposited under
conditions C to F. It is clear that spray conditions with a higher torch enthalpy led to a
higher degree of particle melting and consequently more portion of amorphous phase in
the Sn2P2O7 coatings. These results suggest that it may be possible to tailor the surface
morphology (and potentially microstructure) of the Sn 2P2O7 coatings by adjusting the torch
enthalpy along with other spray parameters.

 Figure 16. XRD spectra and SEM images of as-received powder and thermal sprayed Sn 2P2O7
 coatings deposited under conditions C to F. Torch enthalpy is shown in backets for each spray
 condition.

The microstructure of thermal-sprayed Sn₂P₂O₇ coatings was systematically examined via
cross-sectional analysis on both nickel mesh and stainless steel substrates. As depicted in
Figure 17, the coatings adhered well to both substrate types, with notable differences in
morphological characteristics. On stainless steel, the Sn₂P₂O₇ coatings exhibited enhanced
uniformity across the cross-section. The layers were largely continuous, though some
isolated pinholes and minor imperfections were observed. These defects suggest that
further process optimization, such as adjustments to spray parameters, powder feed rates,
or substrate preparation, will be beneficial to achieve fully dense films. Coatings applied
to nickel mesh substrates also demonstrated continuity, confirming that the thermal spray
technique is adaptable to more porous and complex architectures. However, a higher
concentration of pinholes was noted compared to stainless steel, likely due to the open
structure and increased difficulty in achieving complete coverage over mesh geometries.

Overall, the preliminary microstructural assessments indicate that thermal-sprayed Sn₂P₂O₇
films on both substrate types are promising candidates for integration into MEAs.
Continued development and refinement of the spray process will focus on eliminating
residual porosity and optimizing film uniformity, further enhancing suitability for future
electrochemical applications.

Figure 17. Cross-sectional SEM images of APS Sn2P2O7 coatings deposited on Ni mesh and Stainless
Steel substrates.

Initial work on SnP₂O₇ coatings was undertaken to explore their viability for thermal spray
application. Unlike Sn₂P₂O₇, which is readily available commercially, SnP₂O₇ had to be
synthesized in-house. The synthesis involved combining 82.9 grams of SnO₂ and 150 mL
of 85 wt% H₃PO₄ with 60 mL of water. This mixture was heated on a hot plate at 300 °C
for 4.5 hours to produce a consistent paste. Subsequently, the resulting paste underwent
sintering at various times and temperatures to optimize phase purity and particle
morphology across multiple batches.

Analysis revealed that the primary (unit) particle size of SnP₂O₇ is in the 300–700 nm
range, while agglomerated clusters sized between 32–180 μm are favorable for effective
feeding in the thermal spray process. Initial thermal spray trials demonstrated successful
deposition of SnP₂O₇ coatings, confirming the material's compatibility with thermal spray
techniques. Figure 18 presents key characterization data: XRD patterns of the synthesized
powder, SEM-based particle size distribution, and SEM images of the resultant coatings.
Further study is required to assess the material’s performance and suitability as a potential
MEA for future applications.

To assist with composite membrane testing and associated materials discovery, a test setup
has been established, comprising a gas delivery system for nitrogen and hydrogen, which
includes a humidification and temperature control setup capable of attaining temperatures
of up to 250 °C and humidity levels ranging from zero to 100 %RH. The setup also features
an MEA test fixture (Scribner Associates) equipped with 5 and 25cm 2 flow-fields, gold
current collector plates, and an external manifolding system capable of sustaining back
pressures of up to at least 100 psi(g). Additionally, a downstream back pressure regulation
setup has been fabricated in-house, comprising a digital pressure sensor and a proportional
relief valve. A photo of the test facility can be found in Figure 19, which shows the
configuration used to test the plain ionic conductivity of novel JTEC membranes, with

Half-cell tests using commercial Celtec-P MEAs
To establish a baseline half-cell electrochemical data and decouple the various resistances
incurred during JTEC device operation on the high temperature expansion fixture, a series
of half-cell measurements were conducted using commercial MEAs obtained from BASF.
The membranes utilized were expanded PTFE reinforced para-polybenzimidazole with a
thickness of 400 µm, marketed as Celtec-P®. The electrodes consisted of carbon-based gas
diffusion layers containing a catalyst layer comprising platinum on carbon with up to ~30%
PTFE binder.

All tests were performed using hydrogen gas at the anode at a back pressure of 60 psig and
nitrogen gas at the cathode with no back pressure with the source pressure at either 5 or 60
psi(g). The gas streams were preheated to 120 °C without humidification. The MEA was
assembled with 10 mil (254 µm) Teflon gaskets and 25 cm 2 flow fields. After assembly,
the fixture was tightened to a torque of 10 Nm and heated to 160, 180, or 200°C. The cell
was flushed with nitrogen gas flowing at 0.1 slpm for 30 minutes prior to the introduction
of hydrogen into the cell environment. Figure 20 illustrates the variation of open circuit
potentials (OCP) of the JTEC half-cell, as assembled, under various process conditions.
Initially, the OCP is less than 10 mV under nitrogen. When turning on the N2, a sharp jump
in OCP was observed, from <10 mV to 89 mV for 5 psi N2 at the cathode (black line) and
to 145 mV for 60 psi N2 (blue line) at the cathode, which is attributed to the JTEC operating
principle – i.e. the high temperature expansion and transport of hydrogen through the MEA
is galvanic and can be used to generate energy. The subsequent application and release of
backpressure in the cell further enhances the OCP, as demonstrated in the figure.

  Figure 20. (a) JTEC half-cell open circuit potential over time for various operating conditions (b)
 schematic of MEA illustrating process parameters.

To derive current-voltage information from the cell, repeating chronopotentiometry
measurements were performed, wherein currents ranging from 0-3 A were drawn from the
cell, with a 60-second pause at each current level to allow the cell potential to stabilize.
The V-I plots are shown in Figure 21a, and the corresponding power density curves are
presented in Figure 21b. In the low current region, the relatively linear variation of voltage
with current indicates a highly active catalyst interface with negligible activation
overpotential. The predominant loss mechanism in the cell appears to be ohmic, as
evidenced by the relatively linear shape of the polarization curve in the medium to high
current region. This observation is consistent with hydrogen pump studies reported in the
literature, which were conducted on intermediate temperature polymer electrolyte systems,
and is recognized to be one of the key target areas for improvement under the current
program. Notably, no concentration overpotential is observed in the studied current range.
Furthermore, it is observed that increasing the temperature from 160 to 200° C decreases
the resistance of the commercial membrane. However, the minimal performance
degradation observable at 200° C is attributed to the drying of the catalyst-membrane
interface.

 equilibrated for 20 min.
(2) a linear sweep voltammetry scan at 20 mV/s was taken driving the anode electrode to
 -1 V vs. OCP, and two impedance spectra were collected between 100kHz and 0.1 Hz
 with an AC Ripple of 0.1 Arms, using DC bias current of 0 and 200 mA
(3) The measurements in (2) were repeated at an anodic differential backpressure of 60

 0 psig (c) N2 at 60 psig (d)H2 at 60 psig.

Figure 24 shows the results of crossover current measurements. While a typical crossover
measurement presents as a sharp rise in cathodic current at low voltages (<0.5V) followed
by equilibration / mass-transfer limited conditions as the voltage is increased, the PBI-SnO 2
membrane consistently showed asymptotic behavior (often presenting as a peak) at ~0.6V
followed by further increase in crossover current.

This could point either to higher gas permeation under increased driving force, by redox
activity of the MEA chemistry, or through phosphoric acid migration through the
membrane. The former of these is considered unlikely in the nitrogen scenario since, upon
the application of 60 psi(g) of backpressure to the anode (Figure 1c) from atmosphere
(Figure 1a), the nitrogen background curve showed no change in current response. The
behavior is hence, at least partially attributed to phosphoric acid migration through the
membrane, with additional experiments deemed necessary to determine the redox activity

of tin oxide in the system. It is worth noting that the Sn 2+/Sn and the Sn+/Sn reduction
potential lies within the 0.4 – 0.6 V range where the peaks are observed.

The hydrogen spectra showed highly suppressed peaks in the 0.4-0.6 V range, indicating
that the peak signal was suppressed by competing phenomena. The fact that upon the
application of 60 psig of H2 backpressure increased the current attained to -1.4 A from 0.12
(>10x increase) implies that while not true for nitrogen, increased permeation is certain in
the case of hydrogen.

Increasing the cell temperature increases crossover current as expected – however, it is
worth noting that at high backpressure, the curves appear to asymptote at 1.2 and 1.4 A
respectively at 160 and 200C, indicating the attainment of a mass transfer constraint. For
the 5 cm2 MEAs tested, the crossover current density attained is 250 mA/cm 2. Under no
differential pressure, the max crossover current recorded for the membrane is 24 mA/cm 2.
It is worth noting that commercialized HT-PEM membranes for fuel cell operation are
expected to have a crossover current of <5 mA/cm2 to not be considered internally shorting.
Further research and development work on composite membranes with PBI is necessary to
produce a conductive PEM membrane of low hydrogen crossover under differential
pressure.

3.7 Fabrication and Testing of Full Cell JTEC Device
Device-level JTEC testing was carried out using an identical pair of 50 cm 2 MEAs
comprising of a 400 m thick Celtec-P membrane sandwiched between two carbon-cloth
electrodes equipped with Pt/C catalyst layers comprising of a net Pt loading of 1 mg/cm 2
and 30% PTFE binder. Each cell was assembled with PTFE gasketing to achieve a net 20%
cell compression (pinch) and tightened to a torque of 10 Nm.
A schematic diagram of the experimental setup can be found in Figure 26. Briefly,
Hydrogen is introduced at the low-temperature cathode inlet, the effluent from which is
plumbed directly to the high temperature anode. The high temperature anode compartment
feeds via a 2-way gate-valve (V2) to the high temperature cathode, which subsequently
connects to the low-temperature cathode. This setup is an improved gas flow pattern from
what was previously reported since it involves a single point of gas inlet.

Each test begins with the cells at their respective operating temperatures with both cells
(i.e. with valve v2 open) purged thoroughly with N2 followed by the introduction of H2 at
atmospheric pressure. The equilibration of hydrogen in the MEA is monitored through
observing changes in the open circuit potential of both cells over time (for H2-saturated
electrodes at atmospheric pressure on both sides, the V 0 should be ~0 V). Once the cells
are saturated, valves v2 and v3 are closed simultaneously, then the high-pressure side of
the cell (top electrodes in fig 1) are allowed to come up to P high). Once the cell equilibrates
at this higher pressure, valve v1 is closed and the system is allowed to equilibrate to the
pressure differential until a sufficiently low dV/dt (<5mV/min) is achieved.

 Figure 26. JTEC Device schematic and cellwise governing equations.

Under these conditions, a compression voltage Vlow is applied to the low-temperature cell.
The magnitude of this voltage is varied to lock the cell at the highest attainable pressure
ratio for a given Phigh, and Tlow as dictated by the Nernst equation. For the given results,
Vlow was maintained at 25 mV, 15 mV, an 10 mV for testing at T low = r.t. and Phigh at 60,
30, and 10 psi(g). The pressure ratios obtained at each test data point are in fig 2(a) and
2(e) for Thigh = 150 and 175C respectively. Once the cell equilibrated to the new pressure
ratio, the net voltage Vnet was calculated as the difference in open circuit potentials between
the high and low temperature cells respectively. Chronoamperometry experiments were
conducted for progressively decreasing values of Vnet until 0 net voltage or potentiostat
current compliance limit was achieved.

Fig 27(a) depicts polarization curves obtained at Thigh =150C and Phigh ranging from 10 –
60 psi(g). The effect of cell performance on changing the pressure ratio was hence studied.
It can be seen clearly that higher pressure ratios correspond to higher net voltage and higher
overall power densities. The efficiencies remain largely constant at ~20% for open circuit
conditions but drop sharply once the cell is put under load – the slope of the efficiency
curve is inversely proportional to the pressure ratio, indicating that higher pressures lead
to an effective higher ‘momentum’ at modest loads.

 Figure 27. JTEC device testing results at 150C, polarization curves (a) power density curves (b)
 pressure ratios (c) efficiencies (d), and comparison with data collected at 175C, polarization curves
 (e) power density curves (f) pressure ratios (g) efficiencies (h).

 To test the performance of the cell at higher temperatures, the experiments were repeated
 175 C. IT is noticed that the decline in pressure ratio upon load (Fig. 27e) as well as the
 cell resistance under load (slope of Fig 27f) both increase substantially upon increasing the
 temperature. This indicates that the crossover of H 2 through the Celtec membrane rises and
 the conductivity of the membrane concurrently drops when the temperature increased from
 150 to 175C. These results are in accordance with Celtec’s recommended maximum
 operating temperature of 160C, accentuating the need for developing the next generation
 of intermediate temperature membranes and ionomers.
