Abstract
Terrestrial Aquatic Interfaces (TAIs), and tidal wetlands in particular, store large amounts of carbon yet
are not well represented in Earth System Models (ESMs). Predictions of carbon cycling and greenhouse
gas (GHG) emissions in tidal wetlands are highly uncertain. Eddy covariance (EC) towers provide
ecosystem-scale GHG flux data at a temporal resolution (every 30min) that is helpful for parameterizing
and improving mechanistic realism in ESMs. We propose to use a network of eddy covariance towers and
standardized ancillary data streams, along with mesocosm experiments and statistical analyses, across
diverse tidal wetlands of North America to develop and improve biogeochemical modeling at the TAI.
Our overarching objective is to improve understanding and process-based modeling of gross primary
productivity (GPP) and CH4 emission responses, both non-linear and asynchronous, to stressors including
plant inundation, disturbance, salinity and nitrogen loading.

Objective 1
In order to address Objective 1 we tested ANN partitioning across our network of sites, experimenting
with hydrologic parameters and testing for improved performance against measured nighttime NEE and
stable isotope partitioning. This work has been submitted for publication in Global Change Biology.

ANNs and other artificial intelligence (AI) approaches offer promising alternatives for partitioning NEE
in ecosystems where traditional methods face limitations (Tramontana et al., 2020). These data-driven
models can capture complex, nonlinear interactions among multiple environmental drivers without
imposing rigid functional forms, making them particularly advantageous in dynamic environments such
as wetlands and river deltas. In these systems, hydrology, water quality and lateral carbon fluxes play an
important role in shaping carbon dynamics. Such hydrological and lateral processes are rarely accounted
for in traditional partitioning models, leading to biases in estimated GPP and RECO (Forbrich & Giblin,
2015). By incorporating variables such as WTD, salinity, and water or wind velocity, ANNs, by contrast,

can integrate these multidimensional controls to better represent the coupled hydrological and
biogeochemical processes that govern carbon exchange, providing a more flexible and realistic
framework for improving flux partitioning in complex ecosystems.

To date, there has been no systematic evaluation of CO2 flux partitioning approaches in tidal wetlands.
This study addresses that gap through a cross-site comparison of traditional (daytime and nighttime) and
ANN partitioning methods across a salinity gradient spanning seven tidal wetland sites. Our first
objective is to evaluate and compare the performance of traditional and ANN-based partitioning
approaches to identify the most suitable methods for these hydrologically and biogeochemically complex
ecosystems. Our second objective is to assess whether incorporating hydrologic and water quality
variables, such as WTD, tidal range, and salinity, can improve the accuracy and robustness of flux
partitioning models. Insights from these analyses are used to elucidate the biogeochemical and physical
controls on GPP and RECO in tidal wetlands. We hypothesize that (1) salinity will emerge as a key
predictor in brackish systems, where it strongly influences both photosynthetic and respiratory activity,
and (2) partitioning performance will decline from high to low marsh environments due to the increasing
influence of tidal dynamics and lateral carbon fluxes on ecosystem-atmosphere exchanges.

Across the eight tidal wetland sites, the ANN models that included WTD and/or salinity (ANNBEST)
generally performed as well as or better than the baseline ANN models that excluded them (ANNBASE)
(Table 3). Adding WTD improved performance at half of the sites (i.e., CA-RBM, US-Dmg, US-PLM,
US-Srr) but had no effect at US-EDN and even degraded performance at others (CA-DSM, US-PLo, US-
StJ) relative to ANNBASE. Incorporating salinity generally reduced model performance, though it had no
effect at US-PLo and improved performance at US-StJ. While individual sites showed modest gains when
hydrological variables were included, these effects did not appear to follow a clear gradient related to
salinity class, elevation, or WTD.

Because flux magnitudes are greatest in summer, we focused the diel comparison on summertime periods.
Across sites, the diel patterns showed that the ANN and nighttime temperature-based methods aligned
most closely, especially at night (Figure 3). This consistency reinforced earlier results demonstrating that
both approaches captured nighttime respiration more reliably than the daytime light-response method,
which tended to overestimate RECO across both night and day. While the nighttime and daytime
approaches generally followed a typical pattern of increasing RECO during the day across all sites, the
ANN-based approach suggested a potential Kok effect (i.e., suppression of daytime leaf respiration) at
US-EDN and US-StJ. However, at US-EDN, where isotope-based partitioning was available, the isotope-
derived estimates exhibited substantial variability and did not show a clear midday dip, indicating that
evidence for Kok-effect-like suppression of respiration remained inconclusive. The daytime dip at US-
EDN may instead have been influenced by lower daytime WTD, while reduced salinity could have
contributed to the lower daytime RECO observed at US-StJ . However, given the limited duration of the
isotope campaign, additional observations are needed to determine whether these ANN-derived patterns
reflect consistent physiological processes or instead arise from artifacts associated with the model inputs.

Tidal wetlands pose inherent challenges for conventional NEE partitioning because the assumptions
underlying standard daytime and nighttime approaches break down under strong hydrological forcing.
Temperature-based nighttime methods assume a stable, monotonic relationship between respiration and
temperature (Reichstein et al., 2005), yet tidal inundation frequently alters redox conditions, water
chemistry, gas transport, substrate supply, and lateral carbon fluxes on timescales far shorter than the 15-
day parameter windows typically used for estimating respiration sensitivity. Similarly, daytime light-
response methods assume that NEE dynamics can be captured by a hyperbolic light-response curve
modified by temperature and VPD (Lasslop et al., 2010), but these formulations do not explicitly account
for modifying factors including hydrologic pulses, storage fluxes, or salinity-driven constraints that
dominate tidal systems (Forbrich & Giblin, 2015). Consistent with these limitations, our cross-site
evaluation showed that both daytime and nighttime partitioning systematically underperformed relative to
the ANN method across all marshes, with the daytime approach in particular exhibiting strong bias and
high RMSE. These results indicate that daytime light-response partitioning is not suitable for tidal
wetlands and should be avoided when estimating GPP and RECO from EC data in these environments.

In contrast, the physics-informed ANN framework of Tramontana et al. (2020) performed robustly across
the full salinity and elevation gradient. By embedding a light-use-efficiency constraint while allowing the
network to learn nonlinear interactions among radiation, temperature, VPD, and other covariates, the
ANN approach better captured nighttime NEE compared to both empirical approaches. This is consistent
with previous work showing that neural networks can match or exceed traditional partitioning schemes in
upland and wetland ecosystems (Oikawa et al., 2017; Shahan et al., 2022), and extends those findings to
hydrologically dynamic tidal marshes. Importantly, performance remained relatively high even at low-
elevation sites where tidal flooding and lateral exchange strongly influenced carbon fluxes.

Objective 2
In order to evaluate impacts of salinity, inundation and nitrate on GPP, we conducted two main analyses.
First, Graduate student Sarah Russell synthesized 5 years of data from US-Srr to evaluate interannual
variability in GPP and found that salinity was the best predictor of variability in GPP (Russell et al. 2023;
Fig. 5). This publication significantly enhances our knowledge of how salinity influences GPP and the
findings will help inform model development. PI Lisamarie Windham-Myers submitted a Research
Highlight to DOE on the paper.

(April–August) (A) salinity, (B) vapor pressure deficit, and (C) air temperature were compared to total

Second, we conducted an experimental mesocosm study. MS student Sara Tenda (mentored by PI Gough)
led the mesocosm study and collected CO2 and CH4 flux and water chemistry data from May through
September 2023, with biomass harvests in the fall. The mesocosm study consisted of 3 replicates of
control, elevated salinity, elevated nitrate, and elevated salinity x nitrate treatments distributed across 12
tidal tanks. Each tank included 6 subsample pots containing an assemblage of freshwater species, 3 at
high and 3 at low marsh elevations (Figure 6).

Figure 6. The mesocosm study included three randomized replicates of a 2x2 factorial design with
elevated salinity and nitrate treatments applied to the experimental tanks via influx from the treated tidal
reservoirs.

Results from the mesocosm generated two novel conclusions relevant to modeling wetland fluxes and
biogeochemistry: CO2 but not CH4 fluxes are strikingly resilient following pulse salinity and nitrate
additions (Figure 7), and nitrate pulses increase biomass production by stimulating invasive plant growth
(Figure 8).

Figure 7. Weekly mean NEE of CO2 and CH4 by treatment (+/- 1 SE). Letters indicated significant
differences between treatments within the same week.

Figure 8. Biomass measurements from mesocosms post treatment. Nitrate treatments significantly
increased biomass relative to control and salinity only treatments.

Thirdly, eight years of data from US-HPY and US-MRM were analyzed by graduate student Suman
Dhakal (under mentorship of PI Schafer) to assess NEE, and CH4 fluxes and in these mesohaline tidal
wetlands have shown that salinity and water table depth only played a small role in either one of these
fluxes. The restored site US-MRM showed over the span of the study period four times higher CH4
emission than US-HPY. Likewise, the restored site showed emission of CO2 most of the years while the
natural site US-HPY recorded uptake.

Objective 3
In order to evaluate impacts of salinity, inundation and nitrate on CH4 we conducted a synthesis study
across the network. This work is being led by Katrina Poppe (under mentorship of PI Knox) and is being
prepared for publication. As mentioned above, we standardized data processing (e.g. partitioning,
gapfilling etc.) to enable easier integration and analysis across sites. Preliminary analyses of eddy
covariance data across the NATURA network suggest diel patterns in CH4 emissions vary significantly
across sites, with the majority of sites exhibiting emission dynamics dominated by tide not temperature.
Highest methane emissions are observed in freshwater tidal sites with lowest emissions from polyhaline
marshes (Fig. 9).

highest emissions midday which is often attributed to temperature and photosynthetic activity (SRR,
RRC, HPY), however other sites have lower midday emissions or emissions obscured by tidal cycles
(DSM, RBM, EDN, STJ).

the diurnal tidal cycle in influencing CH4 fluxes. These coherencies are consistent with the typical tidal
flooding pattern at each site.

Objective 4
The goal of objective 4 was to incorporate processes of salinity, inundation and nitrate on CH4 into the
PEPRMT model. This was accomplished again using our network of diverse tidal wetland sites across the
US and Canada to inform model development, parameterization and validation. This paper was published
in 2024 in JGR Biogeosciences (Oikawa et al., 2024). Overall, we found that this added model
complexity allowed PEPRMT to predict over 50% of the variance in CH4 fluxes across diverse tidal
wetlands (R2=0.54; nRMSE =1.15).

Objective 5
Currently, we are comparing the MEM-PEPRMT model with the PFLOTRAN model at GCReW. First,
we parameterized the model to the GCReW chamber data, with collaboration with Genevieve Noyce and
Postdoc Abigail Lewis (Figure 13). The model performs well against chamber data. The next step is to
compare model outputs to inform future model development.

Figure 13. Preliminary PEPRMT modeling results at GCREW. Model was calibrated using continuous
soil CH4 flux measurements collected by Genevieve Noyce.
