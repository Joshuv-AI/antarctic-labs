Community-level COVID-19 vaccination promotion capacity and its association with vaccination dose per capita in China: an entropy-weighted TOPSIS and multilevel analysis
Authors: Tianshuo Zhao, Tiancheng Xie, Xiyu Zhang, Qingsong Xu, Ninghua Huang, Yaqiong Liu, Fuqiang Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop an evaluation framework for community-level capacity to promote coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccination and to quantify vaccination promotion capacity across primary care communities in different regions of China. We further examined the association between promotion capacity and COVID-19 vaccination dose per capita to inform optimization of primary immunization services in the post-pandemic era.MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted across 94 primary care communities from 10 provinces. Indicators of vaccination mobilization and service delivery from 2020 to 2022 were collected via questionnaire across four domains: health education, mass vaccination, incentives, and monitoring/reminders. External and internal covariates were collected simultaneously, including policy, ecological and socioeconomic environments. The entropy weight method and the technique for order preference by similarity to ideal solution were used to calculate a composite score index, and communities were categorized using the rank-sum ratio (RSR) method. After covariate selection via Lasso regression, multilevel mixed-effects linear regression models assessed associations between promotion capacity and dose per capita.ResultsMarked provincial differences were observed in overall vaccination promotion capacity. Provinces such as Anhui, Henan and Zhejiang had a higher proportion of high-capacity communities (25%), whereas provinces/municipalities such as Hubei, Guizhou, Shanghai, Guangxi, Shandong, Fujian and Xinjiang had a larger share of medium- and low-capacity communities. In multilevel mixed-effects linear regression models, the RSR ranking of overall vaccination promotion capacity was significantly and negatively associated with community COVID-19 vaccination dose per capita (exp[β] = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.69–0.92), indicating that communities with better (lower RSR ranking scores) capacity achieved higher dose per capita. RSR rankings of all four primary domains were negatively associated with vaccination dose per capita: health education capacity (exp[β] = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.65–0.88), mass vaccination capacity (exp[β] = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.63–0.87), vaccination incentive capacity (exp[β] = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.65–0.91), and monitoring/reminders (exp[β] = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.88–0.98). The number of policy documents issued by municipal-level authorities and studied by local staff, as well as the post-training assessment pass rate among vaccination staff, were consistently and positively associated with dose per capita in most models.ConclusionIn conclusion, the composite evaluation framework effectively captured regional differences in community vaccination promotion capacity. Higher overall capacity and better performance in all four domains were linked to higher COVID-19 vaccination dose per capita. Strengthening policy dissemination and implementation, enhancing the quality of training and assessment for vaccination staff may help build an efficient community-based vaccination promotion system, mitigate vaccine hesitancy, and increase vaccine coverage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28150-1.

Introduction

A range of vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs), including coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), continue to impose a substantial burden on population health worldwide and in China. Since it emerged in late 2019, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has spread rapidly across the globe, causing an estimated 700 million infections and 7 million deaths [1, 2]. Despite the well-documented effectiveness and safety of vaccines, vaccine hesitancy has been identified as a major barrier to achieving and sustaining high coverage and controlling VPDs. During the roll-out of COVID-19 vaccines, varying levels of hesitancy have been reported across countries [3]. A systematic review and meta-analysis covering 114 countries/territories further revealed wide regional heterogeneity that acceptance rates ranged from 93% in Niger to 15% in Cameroon in Africa; and from 92% in Tunisia to 13% in Iraq in the Middle East and North Africa. In total, only 72 of 114 countries/territories reported acceptance rates ≥ 60% [4]. In China, although vaccination coverage in the National Immunization Program is high and overall acceptance of COVID-19 vaccines is relatively favorable, pockets of hesitancy and delayed uptake persist in certain populations and areas, posing challenges for booster campaigns and the introduction of other vaccines. Social ecological theory emphasizes that behaviors are shaped by interacting influences at the micro- (individual and family), meso- (community and service system) and macro- (policy and societal) levels [5]. Public discourse, media information, advice from family members and physicians, the service capacity of primary health care (PHC) institutions and the broader policy environment can all influence both willingness to be vaccinated and access to services, thereby jointly affecting overall vaccination coverage [6–10].

In China, vaccination against COVID-19 and other VPDs was delivered primarily through PHC facilities, including community health service centers (CHC) and township health centers (THC). Existing research on vaccination uptake has predominantly examined two related but distinct sets of factors. The first is vaccine hesitancy, which concerns individual-level attitudinal barriers — including distrust, safety concerns, and misconceptions about vaccines. The second is access barriers, which refer to structural impediments to vaccine availability — such as geographic distance, supply shortages, and information gaps [11–14]. Considerably less attention has been paid to vaccination promotion capacity, a distinct construct referring to the organizational and operational capabilities of community-level PHC facilities to actively promote and deliver vaccination services, such as health education, mass vaccination activities, incentive schemes, and monitoring and reminder mechanisms. Even where community-level interventions have been implemented, their evaluation often relies on single indicators or case studies and lacks quantitative comparisons based on objective weighting and comprehensive ranking methods, seldom linking composite capacity measures to actual vaccination uptake using multilevel analytical approaches.

In this context, this study aims to assess community-level vaccination promotion capacity for COVID-19 from group and policy perspectives across multiple provinces in China, and to examine its association with community COVID-19 vaccination coverage. The goal is to provide actionable theoretical and empirical evidence to optimize PHC-based immunization services, reduce vaccine hesitancy and improve vaccination coverage for respiratory VPDs in the post-pandemic era.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a multicenter cross-sectional design and is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline for cross-sectional studies. Ten provinces were selected from China’s eastern, central, and western regions based on the National Bureau of Statistics classification. Within each province, prefecture-level cities were identified using per capita gross domestic product (GDP) data from provincial statistical yearbooks, focusing on cities within the interquartile range of GDP. From these cities, two counties or districts were selected to represent the lower-middle and upper-middle GDP strata as the survey site.

Within each selected county/district, community CHCs and THCs were randomly sampled as vaccination units using a random number table. A total of 94 vaccination units were included from 10 provinces: Zhejiang, Anhui, Shanghai, Hubei, Guangxi, Xinjiang, Shandong, Guizhou, Henan, and Fujian, comprising 44 CHCs and 50 THCs (Supplementary Table 1).

Data collection

Data were collected via an electronic structured questionnaire completed by the designated staff in each vaccination unit. Respondents reported vaccination-related activities in their communities during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022) (Supplementary Methods 1 & 2). Data quality was ensured through a two-stage verification procedure. First, logical consistency checks were performed to identify out-of-range, missing, or implausible values, including negative counts, non-numeric entries, and values exceeding physically implausible bounds. Discrepancies were reconciled through consultation with the original respondent. Second, one completed questionnaire was randomly selected from each of the ten provinces and cross-checked against original records by recontacting the original respondents or community-level responsible officers.

The indicator system for assessing community vaccination promotion capacity included four primary dimensions: health education, mass vaccination, vaccination incentives, and monitoring and reminders. Additional factors—external environment, internal environment, and individual-level characteristics—were also recorded. The external environment encompassed policy, ecological, and socioeconomic factors; the internal environment covered vaccination institution facilities, vaccination services, feedback mechanisms, and staff training; individual-level factors addressed both vaccine recipients and providers. It is worth emphasizing that all indicators in this study were collected at the community level. (Supplementary Table 2)

The primary outcome was vaccination dose per capita, defined as the cumulative number of COVID-19 vaccine doses administered during 2020–2022 divided by the average population served by the vaccination unit. This metric captures the full course of vaccination activity, including repeated doses for the same individual, and may exceed 1.0 in communities where substantial proportions of the population received multiple doses. Data on total doses administered were obtained from official immunization records maintained at each vaccination unit. Socioeconomic and health resource indicators at the county level (e.g., GDP, and area) were also included as covariates to control for potential confounders.

Entropy-weighted Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) method

To address the lack of a universally accepted system for evaluating vaccination promotion capacity, we used an objective weighting method based on information entropy to mitigate expert bias. Communities were treated as evaluation units, and a raw data matrix for communities and indicators was constructed. All indicators were positive, and no polarity adjustment was needed. We first calculated the relative proportion for each community on each indicator. The entropy weight for each indicator was then determined, with smaller entropy values indicating greater contribution to differentiating communities. In this study, the highest weights were assigned to unit price of incentive gifts (12.93%), total WeChat reading volume (10.50%) and total Weibo reading volume (10.44%), as shown in Table 1.

Indicators and entropy-weights of community vaccination promotion capacity

After calculating the weights, we used the TOPSIS method to compute a composite score for each community’s vaccination promotion capacity. Rank-sum ratio categorization method

To facilitate interpretation and policy application of the TOPSIS results, we further used the rank-sum ratio (RSR) method to categorize community vaccination promotion capacity. Communities were ordered by their composite score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{i}$$\end{document} from lowest to highest, and the corresponding ranks and cumulative frequencies were calculated. The cumulative frequency for each community was mapped to a probability unit (Probit value), which was used as the dependent variable y, with the RSR distribution value as the independent variable x in a simple linear regression model:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:y=a+b\cdot\:\mathrm{Probit}$$\end{document}

The regression analysis indicated good model fit (F = 208.338, P < 0.001), with a coefficient of determination \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}=0.693$$\end{document}. Variance inflation factors were all < 10, suggesting no substantial multicollinearity (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Based on the fitted regression, we derived critical values for categorization. Percentile cut-offs were set at 15.866% and 84.134%, corresponding to Probit values of 4 and 6, respectively, and communities were classified into three levels of promotion capacity: “low”, “medium” and “high”. The fitted RSR thresholds were approximately 0.007 and 0.139, such that RSR < 0.007 was defined as “low”, 0.007–0.139 as “medium”, and RSR > 0.139 as “high”.

Covariate selection and multilevel modeling

To manage the large number of potential predictors, we used Lasso regression to select covariates and reduce multicollinearity. The community-level COVID-19 vaccination dose per capita was treated as the outcome, and all potential predictors were entered into the Lasso model. The optimal regularization parameter λ was determined via 10-fold cross-validation, selecting the value corresponding to the minimum mean cross-validated error (λmin), with the best model obtained at λmin = 6.9 × 10− 5, log(λ)=-4.16. Variables with non-zero coefficients were included in subsequent multilevel analyses, including number of study sessions on municipal-level policy documents, annual mean temperature, annual precipitation, number of health institution staff, total annual clinic visits at the community vaccination clinic, proportion of telephone appointments, number of feedback messages, event resolution rate, number of staff attending training, post-training assessment pass rate, mean age of vaccination staff and proportion of staff with junior professional titles (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Multilevel mixed-effects linear regression models were constructed with communities at level 1 and counties and facility types (THC vs. CHC) at level 2. A random intercept for county was specified within each facility type to account for unobserved between-county heterogeneity within THCs and CHCs separately. Random-effect variance components and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) were reported separately by facility type.

To accommodate the distribution of the vaccination dose per capita outcome, a natural log transformation was applied prior to modeling. Model results were expressed as exp (β) with 95% confidence interval (95% CI). The primary exposures were the overall RSR-based ranking or category of vaccination promotion capacity, as well as the rankings of the four primary dimensions. Covariates selected by Lasso, along with county-level socioeconomic indicators, were included as fixed effects, with a significance level set at 0.05. Higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{i}$$\end{document} values and higher raw RSR values indicate stronger vaccination promotion capacity. The RSR ranking score (i.e., rank number) is inversely related to capacity, that lower rank number indicates stronger capacity. In the multilevel models, an exp(β) < 1 for the RSR ranking score therefore indicates that communities with stronger capacity achieve higher vaccination dose per capita. Data cleaning and analysis were performed using R software (version 4.4.2).

Results

Regional distribution of community vaccination promotion capacity

A total of 94 vaccination units from 10 provinces were included. The numbers of community CHC and THC varied by province. Regional differences in vaccination promotion capacity were observed across the eastern, central, and western regions, specifically in health education, mass vaccination, vaccination incentives, and monitoring and reminders (Table 2). Eastern provinces excelled in health talks, WeChat public account posts, and new media readership, with an average of 8.81 health talks, 56.91 WeChat posts, and 549.70 reads per article per community. In contrast, while the central region’s health talk frequency and new media use were slightly lower, the number of mass vaccination sessions (51.92 per community) was comparable to the east.

Description of key vaccination promotion indicators in eastern, central and western regions

Communities in the western region demonstrated strong performance in the total number of doses administered during mass vaccination and the reach of telephone and SMS reminders, with an average of 22.38 thousand doses administered and over 6,000 individuals reached by both reminder methods. However, western communities were relatively weak in implementing incentive measures and in certain new media indicators, and some areas did not implement vaccination incentives at all.

Multilevel analysis of overall vaccination promotion capacity and vaccination dose per capita

Multilevel mixed-effects linear regression models, with RSR-based ranking of overall promotion capacity as the independent variable, revealed a significant negative association between RSR rank and community COVID-19 vaccination dose per capita (exp[β] = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.69–0.92). This indicates that communities with higher promotion capacity ranks (lower RSR values) have higher vaccination dose per capita (Table 3).

Additional variables, such as the number of study sessions on municipal-level policy documents (exp[β] = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.06–1.42) and post-training assessment pass rate (exp[β] = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.21–2.08), were positively associated with vaccination dose per capita. Conversely, annual precipitation (exp[β] = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.65–0.95) and the number of feedback messages (exp[β] = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.61–0.95) were negatively associated with vaccination dose per capita.

When the overall promotion capacity category was used as the independent variable, communities with “medium-capacity" and “low-capacity" had significantly lower vaccination dose per capita compared to "high-capacity" communities (medium: exp[β] = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.48–0.52; low: exp[β] = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.38–0.40). This finding further supports the positive association between higher vaccination promotion capacity and better vaccination dose per capita.

Random-effect estimates are presented by facility type in Supplementary Table 4. For THCs, the ICC ranged from 0.234 to 0.345, and for CHCs, the ICC ranged from 0.300 to 0.313, indicating substantial between-county heterogeneity within each facility type that was appropriately accounted for by the multilevel framework.

Associations between the four primary capacity domains and vaccination dose per capita

When the RSR-based rankings of the four primary capacity dimensions (health education, mass vaccination, incentives, and monitoring/reminders) were separately entered as independent variables in multilevel models, all dimensions were significantly associated with vaccination dose per capita. Specifically, health education capacity (exp[β] = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.65–0.88), mass vaccination capacity (exp[β] = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.63–0.87), vaccination incentive capacity (exp[β] = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.65–0.91), and monitoring and reminders (exp[β] = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.88–0.98) were all positively associated with higher vaccination dose per capita (Supplementary Table 5).

In each model, policy document study sessions and post-training assessment pass rates consistently showed positive associations with vaccination dose per capita. For instance, in the health education model, the exp(β) for policy document study sessions and post-training pass rate were 1.29 (95% CI: 1.07–1.58) and 1.51 (95% CI: 1.22–1.88), respectively. Similar associations were found in the other capacity models (mass vaccination: exp[β] = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.01–1.48; vaccination incentive: exp[β] = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.06–1.70; monitoring and reminders: exp[β] = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.12–1.39). Annual precipitation and the number of feedback messages were repeatedly negatively associated with vaccination dose per capita across all models. These results suggest that policy learning, staff training, ecological factors, and service process quality are associated with vaccination dose per capita.

Discussion

Overall vaccination promotion capacity and vaccination dose per capita

Our study used multilevel models to examine the associations between the four primary dimensions of vaccination promotion capacity—health education, mass vaccination, vaccination incentives, and monitoring and reminders—and vaccination dose per capita. Previous studies have highlighted that successful vaccine rollout depends on comprehensive, community-level promotion strategies [15]. Consistent with this, our findings show that communities with stronger overall vaccination promotion capacity achieved higher COVID-19 vaccination dose per capita. These results reinforce the importance of viewing the components of vaccination promotion capacity as an integrated whole, optimizing resources across multiple dimensions [16].

Health education capacity and vaccination dose per capita

Health education capacity is a fundamental component of vaccination promotion capacity. Our results indicate that higher capacities in health education were associated with higher vaccination coverage [16]. This supports previous research that suggests combining new media and traditional channels enhances public awareness and trust in vaccines [17]. Notably, new media approaches (e.g., instant messaging app, SMS reminders) were particularly effective in younger and urban populations, while traditional formats were more impactful in rural areas and among older adults. Consistent with this, regional health education capacity patterns suggest that communication strategies should be differentiated by region. Digital platforms are most effective in eastern provinces where new media reach is highest; short-video platforms (e.g., TikTok) show potential in the central region; and traditional community-based approaches (e.g., billboards) should be prioritized in western provinces where new media usage remains limited.

Mass vaccination and incentive capacities

Mass vaccination capacity also played a significant role in improving vaccination dose per capita, echoing findings from low- and middle-income countries where mass vaccination models have been shown to improve efficiency and reduce vaccination costs [18]. Our study suggests that mass vaccination efforts are especially beneficial in areas with limited health resources. Similarly, vaccination incentives, such as small gifts or free services, were positively associated with vaccination coverage. These measures, more commonly used in economically developed regions, may also be valuable in lower-income areas [19]. Notably, evidence from a cluster-randomized evaluation with and without incentives for routine childhood immunizations in India suggests that the capacity–coverage relationship identified in this study is not unique to COVID-19 vaccination but may generalize across vaccine types and delivery contexts [20]. And the Measles-Rubella (MR) campaign supported by Gavi substantially increased MR coverage from 11% to 85% and reduced coverage inequities across socioeconomic groups, confirming that mass vaccination strategies are effective beyond the COVID-19 context [21].

Monitoring and reminders, and service quality

Stronger capacity for monitoring and reminders was associated with improved vaccination dose per capita, in line with global recommendations for robust vaccine monitoring systems. Reminder mechanisms reduce missed doses and reinforce public trust in vaccination services. Previous research has emphasized that regular reminders and efficient monitoring systems are vital for sustainable vaccination programs [22].

Policy implementation, staffing and training

Policy support and efficient implementation are associated with higher vaccination dose per capita. In our study, more frequent learning sessions on municipal-level policy documents were positively associated with vaccination dose per capita. This highlights the importance of policy dissemination and understanding in driving vaccination performance [23]. We also observed regional disparities, with eastern and central regions demonstrating more frequent policy learning and better implementation than the western regions, likely due to differences in economic development, health resource allocation, and primary healthcare system strength. Existing evidence indicates that the effectiveness of policy support in improving coverage is closely linked to the specific social context and characteristics of target populations [15]. Therefore, policy design should be more responsive to the needs of different population groups, ensuring more precise allocation of policy resources.

The community health service environment was associated with vaccination dose per capita. Previous studies have suggested that improving the community health service environment can strengthen residents’ willingness to vaccinate and their actual vaccination behavior [24]. Our study found that the number of feedback comments and issue resolution rates were negatively associated with vaccination dose per capita, suggesting that current feedback systems may be inadequate. If complaints about vaccination services are not addressed promptly, trust in vaccines can erode, reducing willingness to vaccinate [25]. Strengthening feedback and response systems is essential for enhancing vaccination uptake.

Staffing structure and professionalization also deserve attention. Higher pass rates in post-training assessments were associated with increased vaccination coverage, consistent with previous findings. For example, the Vaccination Leadership Program in the United States reported a 20% increase in coverage due to enhanced training and credentialing [26]. The professional title structure of vaccination staff also influenced coverage, highlighting the importance of optimizing workforce structures at the primary care level.

Ecological factors and regional planning of vaccination services

Ecological factors, including climatic conditions and infrastructure, were also associated with vaccination coverage [27]. In our study, annual precipitation was negatively associated with vaccination dose per capita, likely due to disruptions in vaccination activities caused by heavy rainfall. Heavy rainfall may disrupt vaccination activities, especially for outdoor clinics or during vaccine transport [28]. In areas with high precipitation, particularly with weaker infrastructure, vaccine transport and storage can face challenges, and road hazards can delay vaccine delivery and impede vaccination activities. Extreme weather events can directly interfere with vaccination efforts and service delivery [29]. This suggests that in areas with high precipitation, particularly with weaker infrastructure, actionable strategies to mitigate these disruptions: scheduling mass vaccination during drier seasons; strengthening cold-chain backup and climate-resistant storage; prepositioning supplies at district warehouses ahead of extreme weather; and deploying mobile teams with contingency routes for isolated communities. These findings underscore the need to integrate local climate data into vaccination activity planning.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, some indicators were self-reported by primary care facilities, which may introduce recall and reporting bias. Second, despite using Lasso regression to select covariates, unmeasured confounding factors, such as residents’ risk perceptions, media narratives, and vaccine supply fluctuations, may still influence the results. Third, this study did not directly track vaccination staff’s individual-level vaccine knowledge, attitudes, and willingness to recommend at the personal level; training-related indicators captured at the community level served as proxies for staff competence and may not fully reflect individual cognitive and behavioral characteristics. Fourth, the cross-sectional design and reliance on data from the COVID-19 pandemic limit our ability to infer causality, as temporal ambiguity remains and reverse causality cannot be excluded. In addition, the relatively modest sample size may affect the precision of estimates, particularly in subgroup analyses. Future research should expand the sample size, employ longitudinal designs to further refine the evaluation framework and explore the long-term associations of vaccination promotion capacity on coverage with health outcomes.

Conclusion

This study developed a composite evaluation framework for community vaccination promotion capacity based on entropy-weighted TOPSIS and RSR methods. We systematically assessed the capacity of primary communities in four key areas: health education, mass vaccination, vaccination incentives, and monitoring and reminders, which revealed significant positive associations between promotion capacity and community-level COVID-19 vaccination dose per capita. Strengthening policy implementation at the community level, improving feedback systems, and prioritizing staff training may help support higher vaccination dose per capita. Moving forward, vaccination promotion efforts should be integrated into broader public health system strengthening, with a focus on resource optimization across these key areas. Addressing regional disparities and improving the effectiveness of local health systems will be crucial in achieving higher and more equitable vaccination coverage.
