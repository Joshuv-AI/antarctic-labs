The Clinical and Surgical Landscape of Gender Affirming Vocal Care: A Scoping Review
Authors: Kayen Tang, Abdullah Bhurgri, Phoebe Larson, Abegale McDermott, Teresa Jewell, Joseph Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo comprehensively map the surgical and nonsurgical gender affirming voice literature, synthesize our understanding of the current data, and guide future research.MethodsA literature search was performed using PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, and Web of Science Core Collection databases. Following PRISMA‐ScR guidelines, independent reviewers screened databases from inception, extracting data regarding methodological design, levels of evidence, patient demographics, outcome measures, and surgical techniques.ResultsOf 3231 articles screened, 290 met inclusion criteria. Primary publication rates increased from an average of 2.7 articles/year (2000–2009) to 31.3 articles/year (2022–2024). Most studies were cross‐sectional (n = 106) or retrospective (n = 70), nonrandomized (n = 181), and lacked a comparison group (n = 92). Primary research examined mostly transgender women (n = 119), followed by combined cohorts (n = 71), transgender men (n = 32), and nonbinary/gender diverse individuals (n = 4). Median sample sizes were 20 and 18 for all primary literature and surgical primary literature, respectively. Most primary studies addressed nonsurgical interventions (n = 180), fewer examined surgical (n = 74) or combined approaches (n = 36). Among surgical papers focused on trans women (n = 57), anterior web glottoplasty (n = 46) and cricothyroid approximation (n = 21) predominated. Surgical outcomes largely emphasized acoustic measures (75.3%) or quality of life metrics (56.8%), with limited information on functional impairment.ConclusionCurrent literature is characterized by small size, lack of randomization, and no comparison groups, features consistent with lower levels of evidence. Future research may address gaps in the literature including underrepresented populations, such as trans men and nonbinary/gender diverse individuals, and investigating the impact of vocal gender dysphoria on function.

Introduction

Gender identity is an internal sense of being male, female, or gender diverse, which may or may not align with an individual's sex assigned at birth. For many transgender individuals, this incongruence can result in gender dysphoria, which may cause significant psychological distress [1]. Because voice and communication are common markers of gender perception and identity, vocal incongruence is often a primary source of dysphoria and decreased quality of life (QoL) [2]. A voice that does not align with a patient's gender identity can lead to increased discrimination and harassment, and a decreased QoL, highlighting the medical importance of gender affirming voice care (GAVC) [3, 4].

GAVC is a multidisciplinary field encompassing both nonsurgical and surgical interventions designed to help people achieve a voice that reflects their gender. Nonsurgical modalities are often the first‐line treatment and include options such as voice therapy or hormone replacement therapy [5]. While androgen therapy (testosterone) commonly lowers fundamental frequency for transmasculine individuals [6, 7], estrogen therapy does not alter laryngeal anatomy or pitch for transfeminine individuals [8]. As a result, transfeminine patients frequently rely on behavioral voice modification and, if not satisfied with nonsurgical management, feminizing laryngeal surgeries [9, 10]. Transmasculine individuals who are unhappy with their vocal profile may also seek masculinizing voice surgery or therapy [11, 12].

The volume of research dedicated to GAVC has increased, which may reflect a growing recognition of the importance of GAVC within transgender and gender diverse healthcare [13]. Given the changing landscape and interdisciplinary nature of GAVC, a clear, descriptive analysis of the current literature is required. This scoping review aims to quantitatively describe the current state of the literature regarding surgical and nonsurgical GAVC. By evaluating study methodology and the clinical outcomes used in current research, this study sought to synthesize our understanding of the current data and identify gaps to guide future research and improve interdisciplinary patient care.

Materials and Methods

This review followed the framework developed by Arksey and O'Malley, refined by Levac et al. and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews [14, 15, 16]. The study process was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR). The protocol was registered in advance with Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/YKPQ6). The following questions guided the review:

Identification of Relevant Studies

Eligibility was defined using the PICOS framework. Individuals of all ages and gender identities (trans women/men, and nonbinary and gender diverse [NGD]) seeking any GAVC modality (surgical or nonsurgical) were included. Comparison included pre‐intervention baselines, voice modification techniques, or no comparison. Outcomes encompassed acoustic measures, patient‐reported metrics, perceptual ratings, and qualitative experience. All primary and secondary research designs published from inception were eligible.

Studies were excluded if they met the following criteria: non‐English text, inaccessible full text, different publication type, minimal discussion of GAVC, and results updated in a more recent publication.

Search Strategy

A health science librarian developed a comprehensive search strategy using expert provided keywords and seed articles (Figure S1). Searches were conducted from inception to July 9th, 2025, across PubMed, Embase (Embase.com), CINAHL (EBSCOhost), and Web of Science Core Collection (SCI‐EXPANDED, SSCI, AHCI, ESCI). Following a 50‐study pilot to confirm appropriateness of inclusion criteria and databases, results were imported into Covidence systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia, for deduplication, screening, and extraction.

Study Selection

Three independent reviewers (K.T., A.B., P.L.) screened titles, abstracts, and subsequently full texts against the established eligibility criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, involving the principal investigator (J.C.).

Data Extraction and Analysis

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized template, which included: title, year, journal, first author, last author, first author occupation, first author institution, country (and state if applicable) in which the study was conducted, last author department, last author institution, study design, primary population of the study, sample size, treatment type (surgical or nonsurgical), study measures, main takeaway, and when applicable, surgical technique. Pitch elevation surgeries were grouped into four categories. Anterior glottic web formation included endoscopic techniques that shorten vocal fold length, including Wendler Glottoplasty, vocal fold shortening and retrodisplacement of the anterior commissure (VFSRAC), and techniques using suturing and injection augmentation for web formation. Cricothyroid approximation (CTA) included open techniques that fix the anterior thyroid and cricoid cartilages together to increase vocal fold tension. Laser reduction glottoplasty included endoscopic techniques that reduce vocal fold mass using laser ablation or excision, including laser‐assisted voice adjustment (LAVA). Feminization laryngoplasty included open techniques that excise the anterior thyroid cartilage and vocal folds to shorten vocal fold length. Disagreements were resolved through consensus. Extracted data were charted into Microsoft Excel.

Protocol Addendum

A.M. was added to the study to assist with data extraction and manuscript preparation. Level of Evidence (LoE), per the Oxford Centre for Evidence‐Based Medicine guidelines (OCEBM) [17], was manually extracted following the general extraction.

Study Design

The most frequently utilized study designs were cross‐sectional (n = 106) and retrospective (n = 70), followed by prospective cohorts (n = 57) and other study designs (n = 57), such as reviews or case reports. Most of the studies were clinical primary research (n = 220), followed by secondary research (n = 47), and other primary (n = 23), such as basic science, app development, or educational framework perspectives.

Level 1 (n = 9) and Level 2 evidence (n = 8) articles were less common. Most of the literature consisted of Level 3 (n = 49), Level 4 (n = 162), and Level 5 evidence (n = 62). Few studies were randomized (n = 18) and many studies included no comparison group (n = 92) (Figure 3). Median sample size for all primary and surgical primary literature, combining all populations, was 20 and 18, respectively (Table 1).

Categorization of Literature Focus

Studies were categorized based on the primary modality of GAVC evaluated. Nonsurgical focus, such as voice and hormone replacement therapy, was the most common, comprising about 62.1% of the literature (n = 180). 25.5% of articles had a surgical focus (n = 74), while 12.4% (n = 36) evaluated a combination of both surgical and nonsurgical approaches.

Studies were also categorized based on the primary study population. Of the surgical primary literature, the majority focused on trans women (70.4%), while fewer focused on a mixed cohort (14.8%), trans men (6.2%), or other (8.6%), such as cadavers, animal models, or clinicians (Table 1).

Interventions for Trans Women

For trans women, the literature demonstrated a strong focus on pitch elevation surgery. Among surgical techniques in primary literature, anterior glottic web formation, was most studied (n = 46), followed by CTA (n = 21), unspecified (n = 8), laser reduction glottoplasty (n = 5), and feminization laryngoplasty (n = 4) (Figure 4). Prior to 2010, CTA was more commonly studied, however, newer studies have focused more on anterior glottic web formation (Figure 4 and Table 1).

Interventions for Trans Men

Transmasculine voice care focused primarily on nonsurgical modalities (91.2%), such as voice and androgen therapy. Pitch lowering surgical literature includes eight studies of Type III thyroplasty, one case report each of vocal fold injection augmentation and vocal fold testosterone injection (Table 1).

Outcome Measures and Assessment Trends

Metrics used to define clinical success varied across datasets. Overall, acoustic measures, such as fundamental frequency or formants, were the most universally used, present in 67.1% of primary papers. Patient‐reported QoL metrics, such as the trans woman voice questionnaire (TWVQ) or voice handicap index (VHI), were utilized in 49.0% of articles. Perceptual measures, such as blind listener ratings or evaluations of gendered speech, were present in 42.0% of papers. Although qualitative data, such as focus groups and interviews, mentioned the impact of gender‐voice incongruence on well‐being, quantitative analysis is less common (2.8%). Mental health was investigated in only 7.8% of primary literature, varying from the use of validated psychiatric scales to psychosocial themes emerging in semi‐structured interviews (Table 1).

Discussion

While gender affirming voice treatments have existed since the 1970s [18], the volume of GAVC literature has increased rapidly over the past two decades, suggesting rising interest in this topic and greater recognition of the clinical importance of GAVC, particularly gender affirming voice surgeries. From 2000 to 2009, publication rates of primary literature averaged 2.7 articles per year, whereas the average publication rate between 2022 and 2024 grew to 31.3 articles per year (Figure 2). Over the same period of time, the World Professional Association of Transgender Health (WPATH) changed its discussion of gender affirming voice surgery from one of caution, “there are concerns about the safety and effectiveness of voice modification surgery,” in the Standards of Care 6 published in 2001 [19] to supporting voice surgery as medically necessary in the most recent Standards of Care 8 published in 2022 [20]. Furthermore, the large number and wide distribution of institutions that contributed to literature in this review, 163 institutions in total from 34 countries, suggest that GAVC research is no longer restricted to few academic centers but rather reflects a broader international clinical interest and availability.

Surgical Versus Nonsurgical Utilization

Although there has been rapid overall growth in the literature, our analysis illustrates a greater volume of studies examining nonsurgical than surgical treatments. The volume of exclusively nonsurgical studies (62.1%) is more than double the exclusively surgical studies (25.5%). This distribution roughly mirrors current patient pursuit and clinical utilization patterns, as large scale demographic data, such as the U.S. Transgender Survey, has demonstrated that voice surgery is less commonly pursued compared to voice and communication therapy (19% and 62% of respondents had or desired voice surgery or therapy, respectively) [21]. The research output in the field aligns with the clinical reality that nonsurgical interventions remain the first‐line treatment for many patients seeking voice feminization or masculinization.

Methodological Landscape and Existing Literature

While publication volume indicates growing interest and research occurring in the field, the rigor of exclusively surgical literature remains low. Much of the surgical evidence was rated as low, with the majority (55.9%) consisting of Level 4 evidence, which includes case series and uncontrolled cohorts. Higher level studies (prospective cohort, systematic analyses) were rare in exclusively surgical literature, as we found 2, 0, and 14 publications with LoE 1, 2, and 3, respectively. This finding aligns with recent literature, as a previous scoping review that focused on speech‐language pathology practices in GAVC found an abundance of lower LoE and observational studies [13]. Our study expands on these findings by demonstrating that the surgical literature has a similar methodological limitation.

Specific contributors to this lower LoE show mixed trends over the past few decades. Contributors to lower LoE include lack of randomization (77.8% of surgical studies), lack of comparison groups (46.9% of surgical studies), and relatively small sample sizes (median n = 18). The publication rate of studies without randomization continues to outpace that of randomized studies. However, the publication rate of studies without comparison groups has matched the rate of studies with in‐group and between‐group comparisons, and study sample sizes are slowly up trending (Figure 3). While incorporation of active treatment comparison designs and randomization would yield stronger evidence, ethical barriers regarding the denial or delay of GAVC likely prevent randomized controlled trials from being performed.

Disparities in Study Populations

The existing GAVC literature focuses primarily on trans women. Across the primary clinical literature extracted, over 5300 trans women were included as study subjects, compared to about 1400 trans men and slightly fewer than 800 NGD individuals. Of the primary literature, 119 studies focused exclusively on trans women, while only 32 and 4 studies exclusively investigated trans men or NGD individuals, respectively. The differences grow further when examining primary surgical studies (including mixed), as the majority of the literature focuses on pitch elevation techniques: 66 studies identified in this review compared to 10 studies of pitch lowering techniques.

The difference in the degree that each of these transgender populations has been studied raises the question of whether the research deficits are driven by a lack of clinical need or a lack of established treatment options. Historically, literature has suggested that androgen hormone therapy induces vocal virilization, potentially resulting in relatively less gender‐related vocal distress for transmasculine individuals [22]. However, recent studies demonstrate that transmasculine cohorts frequently experience significant voice‐related distress and impairment comparable to trans women, despite hormone therapy [23, 24]. It remains less clear how NGD individuals experience voice‐related distress, likely due to limited research and the high heterogeneity of their vocal goals. However, one study suggests that while they may report less impairment compared to binary transgender individuals, their vocal impairment remains elevated compared to cisgender individuals [24]. Ultimately, it is difficult to determine whether low research volume leads to decreased patient awareness and surgical interventions or if a lack of viable surgical interventions drives low research volume.

Surgical Techniques

Within surgical literature, pitch elevation techniques are quickly changing. Prior to 2010, CTA was the most frequently studied intervention. Recently, however, studies evaluating anterior glottic web formation have outpaced CTA, making it the most studied technique (46 primary studies), followed by CTA (21 primary studies) (Figure 4).

Because actual surgical volumes are difficult to track due to the use of unlisted CPT codes, publication frequency may serve as a proxy for clinical popularity. One may infer that the volume of gender affirming voice surgeries is rising rapidly, similar to research publication volumes, and that anterior glottic web formation is the dominant technique in use currently.

However, it is important to note that despite these inferred trends in clinical popularity, the decision making behind selecting pitch elevation techniques is still largely undeveloped. Both anterior glottic web formation and CTA have downsides which, anecdotally, are tolerated regardless of clinical scenario rather than optimized for individual patient care. CTA is historically associated with a low durability of effect [25]. Conversely, anterior web formation is technically challenging, requiring endoscopic suturing, exposure of the anterior commissure, and laser management [26, 27]. Anterior glottic web formation also carries risks of airway narrowing and web dehiscence during subsequent intubations, which may be particularly relevant for individuals interested in multiple future gender‐affirming procedures [28, 29]. Ultimate pitch results remain difficult to precisely control with both techniques. While select studies discuss wholesale institutional changes in techniques utilized, in other words switching from primarily using CTA to primarily Wendler [30] or primarily Wendler to primarily laser reduction glottoplasty [31] due to perception that one technique is superior, there is little to no discussion of which clinical scenarios warrant the use of one technique versus another. Given these constraints, increased studies of alternative pitch elevation techniques are prudent to help identify which clinical scenarios each technique is most suited for.

In contrast, pitch lowering surgery remains understudied. This review identified only eight primary articles on Type III relaxation thyroplasty, one article on vocal fold injection augmentation [32] and one article on vocal fold testosterone injection [33]. Considering that the foundational framework for Type III thyroplasty was described in 1974 [18], there has been little progression in optimizing surgical pitch lowering techniques for GAVC, although the recent case reports in vocal fold augmentation and vocal fold testosterone injection, published in 2022 and 2023, suggest that more interest and research in pitch lowering techniques are developing.

Outcomes Measurements

Surgical success in GAVC has predominately been evaluated with a narrow lens focused on the voice. While literature on gender affirming surgery elsewhere in the body has assessed a wide range of outcomes including complications, reoperation rates, satisfaction, esthetics, facial and other anatomic dimension measurements, psychosocial health, functional assessment, and QoL [34, 35, 36], evaluation of gender affirming voice surgery has focused primarily on voice assessment, both perceptual (34.7% of the literature) and acoustic (75.3%), as well as assessment of QoL (56.8%). While many QoL instruments, such as the TWVQ and VHI, assess features associated with general health and well‐being, such as vocal and psychosocial function, few primary studies (2.8%) quantitatively examine non‐voice specific well‐being. These include studies that utilize the Glasgow benefit inventory, satisfaction with life scale, and transgender congruence scale. Assessment of functional impairment and well‐being remains an unmet need in the current literature.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this scoping review. First, the selected search terms may not have captured all relevant literature. For example, the foundational paper for Type III thyroplasty (Isshiki, 1974) was not retrieved. We attempted to minimize this risk by collaborating with a biomedical librarian and conducting a pilot study of our search strategy, but regardless, the search was imperfect. Second, we restricted our scoping review to English papers, potentially excluding relevant international literature. We were able to extract many translated papers but acknowledge that there is untranslatable research that we could not include. Third, extracting subjective categories, such as main takeaways or assigning LoE, makes standardization challenging across extractors, requiring discussion and consensus to navigate differing interpretations.

Conclusion

While the landscape of GAVC research has experienced rapid growth over the last two decades, the current literature remains limited by lower LoE, including small sample sizes, absent comparison groups, and a lack of randomization. There is also a pronounced lack of data on trans men and NGD individuals. Additionally, there is a need to move beyond voice‐specific QoL questionnaires to evaluate the impact of vocal gender dysphoria and its interventions on functional impairments and well‐being. By adopting higher LoE, recruiting more diverse cohorts, exploring nuances of surgical techniques, and measuring outcomes beyond vocal handicap, providers can deliver more effective and individualized therapeutic and surgical care.
