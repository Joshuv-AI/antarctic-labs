Host-dependent variation in free amino acid profiles and physiological responses enhancing drought tolerance in Santalum album
Authors: Dinesh Kumar Yadav, S. B. Chavan, Lalji Singh, G. B. Rawale, Rohit Babar, Sushil S. Changan, S. C. Ekatpure, Paresh Chaukhande, Mehdi Rahimi, V. D. Kakade, A. S. Morade, Armugham Keerthika, Appanderanda Ramani Uthappa, N A Deshmukh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sandalwood (Santalum album L.), a highly valued hemi-parasitic tree, depends on host plants for nutrient acquisition, particularly under abiotic stress. However, the influence of host species on sandalwood’s free amino acid (FAA) profiles under water deficit stress remains poorly understood. This study investigated how 11 host species modulate FAA composition in sandalwood roots during water deficit stress, aiming to identify host-specific metabolic adaptations that enhance stress resilience. LC-MS/MS analysis revealed significant host-dependent variations in FAA profiles. Pongamia pinnata association led to the highest accumulation of aspartic acid (7.28 µg g− 1 FW), glutamic acid (13.87 µg g− 1 FW), and lysine (67.11 µg g− 1 FW), while Sesbania grandiflora resulted in minimal arginine (0.55 µg g− 1 FW). Acacia nilotica and Punica granatum enhanced physiological resilience, with higher chlorophyll content (5.57 µg mg− 1 FW), relative water content (62.8%), and proline levels (18.38 µg g− 1 FW). Principal component analysis (69.7% variance explained) highlighted arginine, lysine, and histidine as key contributors to metabolic shifts under stress. Hierarchical clustering further confirmed host-specific FAA patterns, with Pongamia pinnata and nitrogen-fixing hosts promoting stress-responsive amino acids. Host selection strongly influences sandalwood’s drought tolerance. Pongamia pinnata, Dalbergia sissoo, and Punica granatum enhance nitrogen metabolism, amino acid accumulation, and physiological traits under stress. These findings offer a practical, science-based strategy for improving sandalwood resilience in semi-arid regions through climate-smart host selection in agroforestry systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56713-0.

Introduction

Sandalwood (Santalum album L.) is a hemi-parasitic, evergreen tree species widely valued for its aromatic heartwood oil, often termed the “queen of essential oils” due to its diverse applications in perfumes, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, and traditional medicines1. Native to India, sandalwood is now cultivated in Southeast Asia, Australia, and other regions, driven by high global demand for its economically significant oil2. India holds approximately 9,600 km² of sandalwood forests, predominantly in Karnataka and Tamil Nadu3,4. However, the country’s share in global sandalwood production has declined sharply, from 4,000 metric tons (MT) in the 1950 s to around 500 MT by 2007, largely due to illegal harvesting, habitat degradation, and limited regeneration efforts3. In response, plantation-based sandalwood cultivation, especially on private and agricultural lands, has gained momentum.

The successful establishment of sandalwood plantations is inherently complex due to its hemi-parasitic nature and the dependency on suitable host species for nutrient acquisition and growth5. The species lacks functional root hairs and depends on specialized haustoria to derive water, nutrients, and minerals from neighbouring host plants6. These haustorial connections enable sandalwood to tap into host xylem for essential solutes, and their formation is known to increase with age in the presence of compatible hosts7. Host nutrient deprivation can lead to significant nutrient deficiencies, particularly in nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), and calcium (Ca), as well as impaired physiological development and reduced survival5.

Consequently, research has predominantly focused on the identification of suitable host species under controlled nursery conditions1,2. Over 300 plant species have been reported as potential hosts for sandalwood, especially in tropical and subtropical environments8. However, the nature and efficacy of host-parasite interactions can vary widely, with some hosts providing better nutrient and water support than others. Competition between host and sandalwood for soil resources has also been reported, adding complexity to host selection decisions in plantation systems9,10. Under abiotic stress conditions such as drought or salinity—scenarios increasingly common due to climate change—host species can play a critical role in improving sandalwood resilience by facilitating better water access, reducing evapotranspiration, and enhancing nutrient availability10.

Drought is one of the most pervasive abiotic stressors limiting the growth and productivity of tree species globally11. By 2050, nearly 50% of cultivable land is projected to be affected by water scarcity12. In this context, drought-tolerant, deep-rooted species like sandalwood offer promising options for climate-resilient agroforestry systems, especially on degraded lands13–16. Several studies have explored host–sandalwood interactions under stress conditions. Verma et al.13 found that Dalbergia sissoo and Casuarina equisetifolia were among the most supportive hosts, enhancing sandalwood performance and exhibiting strong physiological traits themselves. Sharma et al.14,17 further demonstrated that D. sissoo and Melia dubia significantly improved growth and stress tolerance of sandalwood under drought and salinity stress by maintaining water and ionic balance, whereas sandalwood without a host could not survive. Sharma et al.18 also confirmed the adaptability of D. sissoo under subtropical stress conditions, reinforcing its potential as a reliable host species.

Understanding the dynamics of free amino acids in sandalwood is vital due to their critical role in osmotic adjustment, stress signaling, and metabolic regulation under drought stress19–21. Host species with thin, watery lateral roots and high water-use efficiency can enhance the transfer of nitrogen- and carbon-rich compounds, including amino acids, through xylem exudates—especially under drought conditions22. As a hemi-parasitic species, sandalwood not only extracts water and minerals but also likely acquires organic solutes such as amino acids from its hosts via the haustorium5,19,22. Despite this, limited research has explored host-to-sandalwood amino acid transfer5,19. Among the free amino acids, compounds like aspartic acid, glutamine, serine, alanine, proline, and cysteine are easily synthesized and transported within plant tissues20. These amino acids function as osmoprotectants, antioxidants, and metabolic precursors, especially under abiotic stress19–21. The host’s role in supplying such compounds is particularly crucial for sandalwood’s growth and resilience22. However, prior studies have mostly focused on select amino acids like proline5,13,14. Notably, Kuttan et al.24 reported specific amino acid derivatives such as cis-4-hydroxy-L-proline and sym-homospermidine, while Lu et al.22 quantified total amino acids. A full-spectrum understanding of amino acid profiles in sandalwood roots under different host associations and drought conditions remains largely unexplored23. While many studies have examined the morpho-physiological effects of hosts on sandalwood under stress13,15,17,18, none have investigated root amino acid profiles in relation to different hosts—a critical gap given the role of amino acids in stress adaptation.

This study investigates how different host plants influence the free amino acid profiles in sandalwood roots under water deficit stress. While host choice is known to affect sandalwood growth, little is known about its biochemical responses, especially under drought. This is the first detailed analysis of free amino acids in sandalwood roots grown with both scientifically validated and farmer-preferred hosts like mango, citrus, pomegranate, and tamarind1,25. We hypothesize that certain hosts may enhance sandalwood’s biochemical resilience by promoting the accumulation of stress-related amino acids. The study aims to identify such hosts that can support sandalwood cultivation in drought-prone regions.

Materials and methods

Experimental details

The experiment was conducted at the ICAR-National Institute of Abiotic Stress Management, Baramati, Maharashtra, India, located in the Deccan Plateau’s semi-arid agro-ecological region (AER-6) and agro-climatic zone AZ-95, a scarcity zone26. Santalum album seedlings were brought from National Medicinal Plant Board approved Shri Dutta Chandan nursery at Shirur, Pune, while the host samples were brought from the nursery of the Forest Range Office and ADT-KVK, Baramati (MS). The experimental site usually experiences an average annual precipitation of 560 mm, whereas the temperature ranges from a low of 13.6 °C to a maximum of 38.9 °C.

The identification and selection of the 11 host plant species were carried out based on a comprehensive review of existing literature, including relevant findings from Chavan et al.1, alongside considerations of local agro-climatic suitability, farmer preferences, and the natural availability of these species in the study region. This multi-criteria approach ensured that the selected hosts were both scientifically relevant and practically applicable for Santalum album cultivation. Detailed information on the selected host species is provided in the Table 1. The experiment was designed as a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) under a completely randomized design (CRD). It comprised 11 treatments, each representing Santalum album (sandalwood) co-cultivated with a different host species: Acacia nilotica, Acacia tortilis, Sesbania grandiflora, Dalbergia sissoo, Punica granatum, Pongamia pinnata, Azadirachta indica, Cajanus cajan, Citrus lemon, Mangifera indica, and Tamarindus indica. Each treatment was replicated three times, and for each replication, data were recorded from three individual sandalwood plants (Fig. 1).

For amino acid profiling, three plant samples per treatment were used for quantification using LC-MS analysis. The experiment was conducted once, as the establishment and growth of sandalwood with host species required approximately 14 months—six months for initial establishment and eight months for sufficient physiological development. Given the long growth cycle of sandalwood and the resource-intensive nature of the experiment, it was not repeated. However, the design ensured sufficient replication and rigor for statistical analysis.

Imposition of stress

Eight-month-old sandalwood seedlings were co-cultivated with 11 different host plants aged 8–10 months. The host plants were planted in the same pots as the sandalwood seedlings, maintaining a distance of 10 cm between them. Each pot (12 kg capacity) was filled with a mixture of red soil, black soil, and farmyard manure (FYM) in a 2:1:1 ratio. The soil used in the experiment had a pH of 7.26, electrical conductivity of 0.34 dS m⁻¹, organic carbon content of 0.45%, available nitrogen of 224 kg ha− 1, available phosphorus of 4.21 kg ha− 1, and available potassium of 110.25 kg ha− 1. During the six-month establishment phase, the plants were watered daily to ensure optimal growth and haustorial connection formation. Common silvicultural practices, including the application of insecticides and fungicides, were followed whenever the symptoms of pest or disease infestation were visible.

Once haustorial connections were established, healthy and uniformly grown plants were selected to impose water deficit conditions. Water deficit stress was induced in April by withholding irrigation. The stress period was extended until visible symptoms of water deficit stress, such as yellowing, drooping, and curling of the leaves, were observed. These symptoms began to manifest approximately 20 days after the cessation of irrigation and varied slightly among the host species. After 30 days of water deficit stress, roots from sandalwood plants associated with different hosts were harvested for FAA profiling.

The progression and intensity of water deficit stress were monitored by weighing the pots regularly to quantify moisture loss. Gravimetric methods revealed that soil moisture levels in the pots decreased from an initial range of 40–42% to approximately 8% after 30 days of stress. This ensured a consistent stress application across all replicates.

Sample preparation

Root samples were carefully excised, thoroughly rinsed with double-distilled water to remove soil and debris, and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve metabolic integrity. Subsequently, the samples were stored at −80 °C for further processing. The amino acid mixture AAS18 analytical standard from Sigma was used in the current experiment for the profiling of amino acids from the sandalwood root samples. A homogeneous 1.0 g of powdered sample was weighed into a sample container containing 20 mL of acidified methanol (1% formic acid). The mixture was vortexed thoroughly for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was passed through a 0.2 μm nylon membrane filter, and 10 µL of the sample was injected into LC-MS/MS for amino acid analysis.

LC–MS/MS analysis

Amino acids in the sandalwood root samples were analyzed using an LC-MS/MS system with Sciex ExionLC UHPLC and Qtrap 6500 + mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was performed using a Kinetex C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm; 2.6 μm). The mobile phase was composed of (A) water with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid and (B) 100% acetonitrile37. The gradient program for the mobile phase was 0–0.01 min 95% A phase, 0.01–2 min 5% A phase, 2–4 min 95% A phase, 4–8 min 95% A phase, 8–9 min 5% A phase, and 9–11 min 5% A phase. A binary LC-MS/MS solvent gradient at a flow rate of 0.60 mL min-1 of LC-MS/MS solvent gradient is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. The column oven temperature was maintained at 40 °C. The injection volume was 10 µl. The flow rate was maintained at 600 µl min-1. Using the source parameters, such as ion source voltage (5500 V), nebulizer gas (60 psi = 413685 Pa), heater gas (60 psi = 413685 Pa), ion source temperature (450 °C) and curtain gas (40 psi = 275790 Pa), the analysis was carried out with electrospray ionization (ESI) at positive polarity37. Observations recorded

Physio-biochemical attributes of sandalwood after imposing drought condition

Physiological traits such as total chlorophyll content, Relative water content (RWC) membrane stability index (MSI) were estimated. Total chlorophyll content was estimated from fresh leaf tissue (50–100 mg) using the DMSO (1:1, v/v) solvent. Samples were incubated at 65 °C until complete pigment extraction, and absorbance was measured at 645 and 663 nm. Chlorophyll concentration was calculated following Arnon38 and expressed on a fresh weight basis. For RWC estimation, fully expanded leaves from the plant’s midsection were collected early in the morning between 7 and 8 AM and immediately brought to the lab in a sealed bag. RWC was estimated using a method given by Barrs and Weatherley39 whereas, MSI was estimated using a method developed by Blum and Bercon40. The total free amino acid in sandalwood leaves was estimated by the procedure given by Moore and Stein41.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were analyzed using R statistical software, version 4.3.242. Prior to conducting the statistical analysis, a normality check of the experimental data was performed using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was carried out in a CRD, through the “Doebioresearch” package. Post-hoc mean comparisons were conducted using the Minimum Significant Difference (MSD) test (p ≤ 0.05). The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to examine the variations in amino acid content in sandalwood roots under water deficit conditions. The dataset was standardized to ensure that all variables contributed equally to the analysis. PCA reduced the dimensionality of the dataset, and the first two principal components (PCs) explained the majority of the variance. The biplot was generated using FactoMineR43 and factoextra R packages44, which visually display the relationships among variables and individuals in the reduced-dimensional space. A pairwise scatterplot matrix was generated to analyze the relationships among amino acid content of sandalwood roots influenced by different host plants under water deficit conditions. The matrix visualizes the Pearson correlation coefficients (R) in the upper triangle and (R2) values for all pairwise comparisons, with regression lines added to enhance interpretation42. Regression lines were shown in the lower triangle. The analysis was performed and visualized using the R software packages tidyverse45, ggplot246, and GGally47. The values presented in the graphs and tables are the means of the three replications.

Results and discussion

Effect of host association on amino acid profile

The content of 17 amino acids studied using LC–MS/MS from root samples of sandalwood is summarized in Table 3. The total ion chromatogram (TIC) of analyzed amino acids highlighted significant variations in FAA profiles influenced by different host species (Fig. 2). The sample was analyzed by LC-MS/MS, and the peaks were compared with those of the reference compounds analyzed under the same conditions. In some cases, matrix interference peaks were observed and separated chromatographically using a mobile phase gradient. The first transition (Q1) was employed for quantification, whereas the second transition (Q3) was used for confirmation. Quantification was based on a signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio of 10 for the limit of quantification, and an S/N ratio of 3 for the limit of detection. Amino acids that were positively detected according to these criteria are listed in Table 2.

LC-MS/MS analysis of the sandalwood root sample revealed a free amino acid (FAA) profile with a contribution of 17 amino acids, including seven essential and ten non-essential amino acids. The average FAA values varied depending on the type of host and sandalwood association under water stress condition (Table 3). Alanine reached its maximum concentration in Punica granatum (10.94 µg g⁻¹ FW), followed by Pongamia pinnata (8.63 µg g⁻¹ FW), whereas comparatively lower alanine levels were observed in Sesbania grandiflora (1.75 µg g⁻¹ FW) and Acacia nilotica (2.79 µg g⁻¹ FW). Alanine serves as a critical metabolite during water stress, contributing to energy metabolism via the alanine cycle and accumulating under stress conditions, consistent with previous reports48,49. Furthermore, the arginine content in sandalwood roots was the highest (p ≤ 0.05) in the host Pongamia pinnata (24.44 µg g− 1 FW), suggesting its active role in nitrogen metabolism, polyamine synthesis, and nitric oxide-mediated stress signalling, while comparatively low arginine levels were observed in Sesbania grandiflora (0.55 µg g⁻¹ FW), and arginine was not detected in association with Cajanus cajan. These results align with prior findings that emphasize the role of arginine in nitrogen recycling and signalling under adverse conditions50,51.

Substantial increases in aspartic acid and glutamic acid were observed in sandalwood roots associated with Pongamia pinnata (7.28 and 13.87 µg g− 1 FW, respectively) (Table 2). These amino acids are pivotal for nitrogen assimilation and act as precursors of other essential metabolites52. Similarly, glycine concentration was highest with host Pongamia pinnata (0.99 µg g− 1 FW) and lowest with host Acacia nilotica (0.32 µg g− 1 FW). Elevated glycine levels further support enhanced nitrogen metabolism in this association53. Cysteine content was highest with host Azadirachta indica (0.09 µg g− 1 FW) while, lowest with host Acacia nilotica (0.07 µg g− 1 FW). However, histidine content was highest in S. album when grown with the host Pongamia pinnata (2.57 µg g− 1 FW) and lowest in the host Sesbania grandiflora (0.19 µg g− 1 FW).

Isoleucine and Leucine, both branched-chain amino acids, exhibited their highest concentrations with Sesbania grandiflora (22.80 and 18.32 µg g− 1 FW, respectively) (Table 3). These amino acids are crucial for stress tolerance and energy provision via catabolic pathways, particularly under water-limited conditions54. Methionine, detected exclusively in sandalwood grown with Pongamia pinnata, underscores its role in methylation reactions and ethylene biosynthesis. This specificity might reflect the host’s impact on lowering water potential in the rhizosphere55. Moreover, the absence of methionine in other host associations may reflect limited sulfur metabolism or host-specific nutrient constraints, warranting further investigation. The significantly higher lysine concentration in Pongamia pinnata (67.11 µg g− 1 FW) indicated its critical role in protein biosynthesis and metabolic adjustments under water stress56. Phenylalanine, a precursor for phenylpropanoid pathways and lignin biosynthesis, was most abundant in association with Punica granatum (0.88 µg g− 1 FW). This suggests that secondary metabolite production is essential for structural integrity and adaptation to stress57.

Proline, a key amino acid involved in stress physiology, varied significantly in sandalwood depending on the host species (p < 0.05), ranging from 0.86 to 18.38 µg g⁻¹ FW (Table 3). The highest levels were recorded with Acacia nilotica (18.38 µg g⁻¹ FW), followed by other leguminous hosts such as Pongamia pinnata (14.30 µg g⁻¹ FW) and Dalbergia sissoo (12.35 µg g⁻¹ FW), while the lowest was observed with Mangifera indica (0.86 µg g⁻¹ FW). This variation indicates that host-specific associations strongly influence sandalwood’s ability to cope with drought stress. Accumulation of proline in sandalwood roots under water deficit conditions was associated with decreased water and osmotic potential, consistent with its well-documented role in osmotic adjustment and protection of cellular structures in host-dependent parasitic systems5,13,17,58,59. Elevated proline levels, particularly with A. nilotica, underline its function as an osmoprotectant that not only mitigates stress but also contributes to protein synthesis, nitrogen and carbon supply, and energy utilization59–62. Previous reports of hydroxyproline-containing proteins in sandal leaves further support the role of proline metabolism in stress adaptation63.

Serine, threonine, tyrosine, and valine exhibited significant variations across host associations (Table 3). Serine, highest with Pongamia pinnata (7.66 µg g⁻¹ FW) and lowest with Acacia nilotica (1.5 µg g⁻¹ FW), contributes to photorespiration and one-carbon metabolism, aiding stress acclimation.

Marked variations in serine, threonine, tyrosine, and valine levels highlight their involvement in the metabolic pathways associated with sandalwood growth and stress resilience64, further supporting the broader role of amino acids as building blocks, metabolic precursors, and signaling molecules orchestrating plant growth, development, and stress responses65. Notably, threonine, which peaked with Punica granatum (1.21 µg g− 1 FW), appears to contribute to protein synthesis and drought tolerance66. Similarly, tyrosine was highest with host Azadirachta indica (0.32 µg g⁻¹ FW) and lowest with host Acacia nilotica (0.05 µg g− 1 FW). While, serine was most abundant in S. album when grown with host Pongamia pinnata (7.66 µg g− 1 FW) and least with host Acacia nilotica (1.5 µg g⁻¹ FW). Finally, valine content was highest in with host Punica granatum (0.71 µg g− 1 FW) and lowest with host Sesbania grandiflora (0.18 µg g− 1 FW) (Table 3).

These findings emphasize the critical role of host plants in shaping the metabolic profile of sandalwood under water-stress conditions. Host plant such as P. pinnata consistently exhibit relatively higher concentrations of specific amino acids including arginine, lysine and proline, which are essential for stress tolerance and plant growth. These findings are in agreement with those reported by Lu et al22, who observed maximum accumulation of total free amino acids in nitrogen-fixing plants compared to non-nitrogen-fixing plants. Mani et al67 documented different types of amino acids in Santalum album leaves. Pongamia pinnata and Acacia nilotica have emerged as optimal hosts, promoting higher levels of amino acids linked to nitrogen cycling, stress mitigation, and secondary metabolism. Future studies should investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms by using advanced metabolomic and transcriptomic tools. Long-term field trials are essential to assess the sustainability and productivity of specific host-sandalwood combinations under natural conditions. Insights from this research can inform agroforestry practices and optimize sandalwood cultivation for resilience and productivity in diverse environments.

Physio-biochemical attributes of Sandalwood in association with different host species

Along with amino acid profiling, some physio-biochemical parameters were also taken to support the present study. For successful establishment and production of sandalwood, understanding of host interaction is necessary because every host influence differently under abiotic stress mainly in drought condition68,69. Since different host species exhibit varied interactions that influence physio-biochemical attributes, we observed that sandalwood grown with Acacia nilotica had the highest total chlorophyll content (5.57 µg mg− 1 FW), which was statistically at par with Azadirachta indica (5.52 µg mg− 1 FW) followed by Pongamia pinnata (5.35 µg mg− 1 FW), Cajanus cajan (5.17 µg mg− 1 FW), Tamarindus indica (4.52 µg mg− 1 FW), Dalbergia sissoo (4.10 µg mg− 1 FW), Sesbania grandiflora (3.68 µg mg− 1 FW), Citrus limon (3.81 µg mg− 1 FW), Acacia tortilis (3.26 µg mg− 1 FW) and Punica granatum (2.80 µg mg− 1 FW). However, lower chlorophyll content was observed in sandalwood grown with Mangifera indica (2.67 µg mg− 1 FW) (Fig. 3).

Being a partial-root parasitic plant, sandalwood depends on host availability for water and nutrition. These resources are transferred through haustoria, supporting its biological functioning70. The present findings are in agreement with Sharma et al14., who also reported variation in chlorophyll content in sandalwood under different host associations during drought stress.

Water is a critical regulator of physiological processes and was assessed using relative water content (RWC)71. Sandalwood grown with Acacia nilotica recorded the highest RWC (62.80%), suggesting superior water retention and overall plant hydration (Fig. 4). This was followed by Azadirachta indica (60.51%), Pongamia pinnata (59.55%), Tamarindus indica (58.65%), and Cajanus cajan (58.33%). Comparatively lower RWC was observed in Citrus limon (55.82%), Acacia tortilis (55.71%), Sesbania grandiflora (54.63%), Dalbergia sissoo (54.50%), Punica granatum (52.19%), and Mangifera indica (51.97%). The higher RWC in sandalwood associated with A. nilotica may be attributed to greater proline accumulation, whereas the lowest RWC in M. indicaindicates poor hydration and potential stress susceptibility. Sharma et al14. similarly reported a decline in RWC under drought with varying host influence, and noted higher RWC with A. indica, consistent with the present study.

Significant variation was also observed for membrane stability index (MSI). The maximum MSI was recorded with Tamarindus indica (59.94%), followed by Acacia nilotica (59.13%) and Azadirachta indica (58.13%) (Fig. 5). Sandalwood grown with P. pinnata (57.35%) and C. cajan (57.35%) showed comparable values, followed by D. sissoo (56.15%) and C. limon (56.09%). Lower MSI values were found with A. tortilis (53.80%), S. grandiflora (53.30%), and P. granatum (50.86%), while the minimum was observed with M. indica (46.40%). Lower MSI, indicates increased damage to cell membranes due to the stress conditions; meaning that as drought intensifies, the cell membranes of sandalwood become more unstable and prone to disruption.

The total free amino acid content varies significantly across the different host combinations with sandalwood (Fig. 6). Higher total free amino acid was observed with Mangifera indica (15.36 mg g⁻¹ FW) which was statistically at par with Punica granatum (15.19 mg g⁻¹ FW) and Acacia tortilis (15.15 mg g⁻¹ FW). This proposes that host plants increase osmolyte accumulation, helping sandalwood cope with osmotic stress. Moderate total free amino acid recorded with Sesbania grandiflora (14.16 mg g⁻¹ FW) followed by Citrus limon (13.81 mg g⁻¹ FW), Dalbergia sissoo (13.22 mg g⁻¹ FW), Pongamia pinnata (12.52 mg g⁻¹ FW) and Cajanus cajan (12.49 mg g⁻¹ FW). Whereas lower total free amino acid was observed with Tamarindus indica (12.74 mg g⁻¹ FW) followed by Azadirachta indica (11.95 mg g⁻¹ FW) and Acacia nilotica (10.45 mg g⁻¹ FW) (Fig. 6). Free amino acids help alleviate drought effects by enhancing nitrogen uptake, aiding its reassimilation and maintaining protein balance in mulberry72,73.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) effectively discriminated sandalwood–host associations based on their free amino acid (FAA) profiles (Fig. 7). The first two principal components explained 69.7% of the total variance (Dim1 = 44.2%, Dim2 = 25.5%), providing a robust summary of variation among host combinations. The PCA clearly separated host-sandalwood combinations based on the concentrations of specific amino acids. Arginine (24.44 µg g−1 FW), lysine (67.11 µg g−1 FW), and histidine (2.57 µg g−1 FW) were the dominant contributors to Dim1, suggesting their role in nitrogen metabolism, polyamine biosynthesis, and stress signaling. These findings align with studies noting arginine’s importance in stress resilience and nitrogen metabolism in plants. In contrast, Dim2 was primarily shaped by proline, leucine, and isoleucine, which are key metabolites in osmotic adjustment and energy metabolism under drought stress. The positioning of amino acid vectors also indicated distinct functional groupings: branched-chain amino acids (isoleucine, leucine, proline) aligned along Dim2, while valine, threonine, alanine, and tyrosine dominated the negative Dim1 axis.

Hosts are grouped into three clusters, visualized using distinct point shapes and colors (circle, triangle, square) and enclosed within a confidence ellipse. These clusters represent different groupings based on similarity in amino acid profiles. As Cluster 1 (blue circles) is associated with higher levels of Isoleucine and Proline, while Cluster 2 (yellow triangles) aligns with Cysteine and Phenylalanine. Cluster 3 (grey squares) shows divergence along Dim1, possibly influenced by Valine, Tyrosine, and Alanine. Group 1 hosts (Acacia nilotica, Sesbania grandiflora) were associated with elevated levels of proline, leucine, and isoleucine, suggesting enhanced drought resilience. Group 2 hosts (Cajanus cajan, Tamarindus indica) aligned with valine, threonine, and alanine, indicating differential regulation of osmotic and energy-related pathways. Group 3 hosts displayed unique divergence along Dim1, largely driven by valine and cysteine, highlighting host-specific metabolic specialization. Overall, the PCA biplot underscores that host species significantly modulate FAA composition in sandalwood roots, shaping stress-responsive metabolic pathways. These results reinforce the role of host identity in conferring adaptive advantages under water-limited conditions.

The heatmap revealed distinct clustering patterns of free amino acid (FAA) concentrations in sandalwood roots under water deficit conditions, reflecting strong host-dependent influences (Fig. 9). Hosts such as Acacia nilotica and Acacia tortilis grouped closely, indicating comparable amino acid profiles, likely due to shared traits such as nitrogen fixation. In contrast, Pongamia pinnata formed a separate cluster characterized by higher levels of glutamic acid (13.87 µg g⁻¹ FW), proline (14.30 µg g⁻¹ FW), and lysine (67.11 µg g⁻¹ FW). These amino acids are well known for their role in osmotic adjustment and stress signaling, suggesting that P. pinnata strongly modulates stress-responsive metabolic pathways.

Amino acid-specific clustering also provided important insights. Proline, isoleucine, and leucine grouped together, indicating co-regulation and a joint contribution to osmotic regulation and energy metabolism58. Conversely, glutamic acid and glycine clustered independently, highlighting their distinct functional roles in drought response. Elevated levels of individual amino acids in associations with Punica granatum and Azadirachta indica (red shading) contrasted with the consistently lower accumulation in Mangifera indica (blue shading), demonstrating considerable biochemical diversity among hosts.

Variation in free amino acid (FAA) profiles across host associations closely mirrored differences in physiological performance (chlorophyll, RWC, and MSI), indicating strong biochemical–physiological coupling under drought stress. Hosts such as Pongamia pinnata that maintained higher chlorophyll and MSI promoted the accumulation of stress-responsive amino acids (alanine, arginine, glutamate, glycine), supporting enhanced energy metabolism and nitrogen assimilation. Elevated arginine in this association corresponded with higher RWC and membrane stability. In contrast, Sesbania grandiflora showed higher branched-chain amino acids but lower chlorophyll and MSI, suggesting a compensatory stress response. High proline levels in Acacia nilotica were associated with reduced RWC but stable MSI, reflecting osmoprotective adaptation. Overall, host-mediated amino acid reprogramming strongly influences physiological resilience of sandalwood under water deficit. These findings underscore that host identity governs both the magnitude and composition of FAA accumulation in sandalwood. Higher amino acid accumulation with A. nilotica and P. pinnata suggests superior stress adaptation, while reduced accumulation in M. indica and P. granatum indicates weaker metabolic support under drought. Such host-driven variation aligns with observed trends in RWC and MSI, confirming that amino acid metabolism mirrors overall physiological resilience. Similar host- or genotype-specific patterns of metabolic adjustment have been reported in maize under drought72,74. Overall, the clustering analysis highlights that sandalwood’s amino acid metabolism is not only host-dependent but also represents targeted biochemical responses to water deficit. This emphasizes the importance of host selection in sandalwood-based agroforestry systems to enhance stress tolerance and sustain growth under drought conditions.

This study demonstrates how different host species influence the drought resilience of sandalwood by altering its free amino acid profiles and physiological traits. By profiling free amino acids and key physiological traits under water deficit conditions, it provides clear evidence that host selection directly affects the metabolic and biochemical pathways that underpin stress resilience in this economically and ecologically important parasitic tree. The identification of Pongamia pinnata, Acacia nilotica, Punica granatum, and Dalbergia sissoo as optimal hosts offers practical insights for enhancing sandalwood survival and productivity in semi-arid and water-limited environments, which are increasingly prevalent due to climate change. While Punica granatum was primarily associated with higher total free amino acids and elevated stress-responsive metabolites, its physiological performance (chlorophyll content, RWC, and MSI) was comparatively lower under drought conditions. These findings provide a practical basis for selecting suitable hosts to improve sandalwood performance under water stress, offering valuable insights for agroforestry practitioners, researchers, and policymakers seeking climate-resilient tree–crop systems in semi-arid regions.

This study is limited by the evaluation of only 11 host species out of more than 300 known hosts of sandalwood, which may restrict the understanding of the full range of host effects on drought tolerance. Additionally, experiments conducted under controlled nursery conditions might not fully capture the complex interactions and environmental variability present in natural field settings. The focus on short-term physiological and biochemical responses, even with the addition of gene expression analysis, could overlook longer-term impacts of host interactions on sandalwood growth and survival. We acknowledge that including leaf samples for FAA estimation and additional treatments such as sandalwood alone and under irrigated conditions would have strengthened the study. Furthermore, the study primarily addressed water deficit stress, without considering other abiotic stresses such as nutrient deficiencies, waterlogging, or salinity, which also influence sandalwood cultivation. Including antioxidant parameters such as SOD, CAT, APX, and POX, alongside more comprehensive gene expression studies, and water potential and electrolyte leakage would add significant value and deepen understanding. Despite these limitations, the current efforts are highly significant as they provide one of the first insights into the probable impacts of host species in supporting sandalwood under stress through the supply of nutrients in the form of amino acids. This novel understanding strengthens the basis for selecting compatible host species to improve drought resilience in sandalwood-based agroforestry systems.

Conclusion

In response to climate change and increasing water scarcity, this study this study provides a detailed characterization of free amino acid profiles in sandalwood roots grown with different host species under water stress. It highlighted the crucial role of selecting the right host plants to improve drought tolerance by influencing amino acid metabolism and important physiological traits. Among the 11 host species tested, Pongamia pinnata, Acacia nilotica, Punica granatum, and Dalbergia sissoo were consistently associated with relatively higher accumulation of stress-responsive amino acids—such as lysine, arginine, aspartic acid, and proline—which are known to protect plants by stabilizing proteins and membranes under stress. However, the nature of the response varied among hosts. While Punica granatum was primarily associated with higher total free amino acids and elevated stress-responsive metabolites, its physiological performance (chlorophyll content, RWC, and MSI) was comparatively lower under drought conditions. In contrast, host associations with Pongamia pinnata and Dalbergia sissoo showed more consistent improvements across both biochemical and physiological parameters. These responses suggest host-dependent modulation of sandalwood metabolism under water deficit rather than definitive enhancement of drought tolerance. Although Acacia nilotica supported sandalwood growth very well, its spiny nature may limit practical use by farmers. Therefore, combining horticultural hosts like Punica granatum, which provides moderate economic benefits, with long-term forestry hosts such as Pongamia pinnata or Dalbergia sissoo offers a balanced approach for sustainable sandalwood agroforestry. Overall, this research provides a science-based, farmer-friendly framework for host selection by distinguishing between biochemical adjustment and physiological performance in semi-arid, water-limited environments. By linking host selection to biochemical and physiological improvements, it offers new directions for climate-smart agroforestry design, and extension efforts to promote sustainable sandalwood cultivation in challenging environments.
