Training in pleural interventions: not just a matter of ‘rights’?
Authors: Andrew E Stanton, Avinash Aujayeb, Anthony W Martinelli, Eihab O Bedawi, Najib M Rahman
Published: 2026

Introduction

Pleural disease management is underpinned by diagnostic and therapeutic procedural intervention, most commonly pleural aspiration and chest drain insertion. These interventions are inherently invasive and carry recognised risk, including organ puncture and death. Landmark recommendations from the National Patient Safety Agency1 drove a nationwide step change in respiratory practice by mandating the use of thoracic ultrasound (TUS) before pleural intervention for fluid, a requirement now clearly embedded within recently updated British Thoracic Society (BTS) guidance.2 Despite this, significant harm from pleural procedures continues to be reported, and the reasons for this remain incompletely understood.3 4

This persistent risk raises important questions: are pleural interventions being undertaken in the right place, at the right time, by the right people and with the right periprocedural care? An upcoming National Confidential Enquiry into Patient Outcome and Death study is expected to provide valuable insight into these issues. In this editorial, however, we focus specifically on who the ‘right’ people may be to perform pleural procedures and how the concepts of ‘rights’ to train in and undertake these interventions should be framed in modern practice.

At the most basic level, the ‘right’ person to perform any invasive procedure is an individual who has received appropriate training, demonstrated procedural competence and possesses the clinical judgement required to decide whether an intervention is indicated in the first place. In the context of pleural disease, this raises two closely related questions: who is the right person to undertake pleural procedures and who should have the right to be trained and entrusted to perform them?

Currently, so-called ‘primary’ pleural procedures such as aspirations and intercostal drain insertion are frequently undertaken by non-respiratory clinicians working in acute, emergency and critical care settings. Historically, training in these procedures was supported by workplace exposure and embedded within generalist curricula. However, the landscape of pleural practice has evolved substantially in recent years. We argue that this evolution necessitates a re-examination of who should have the right to train in pleural procedures and how training curricula should adapt to ensure that this right is aligned with contemporary practice and patient safety.

A number of practice-changing clinical trials have informed contemporary BTS pleural disease guidelines, supporting increasingly ambulatory pathways and conservative approaches to the management of pleural effusion and pneumothorax.2 In selected patients, no intervention for pneumothorax is supported, while therapeutic intervention decisions are now guided primarily by symptoms and patient preference rather than radiological appearance alone. The adage ‘just because we can, doesn’t mean we should’ is particularly pertinent in pleural medicine. As a result of this evidence-driven shift, patients requiring pleural intervention are increasingly managed within specialist elective pleural services rather than general medical inpatient or emergency settings. This has improved procedural delivery, efficacy of care and substantially improved patient experience but has also concentrated training opportunities within specialist respiratory services, creating growing tension with the requirements of the current Internal Medicine (IM) curriculum.

In this journal, Jajbhay et al recently reported results from a survey of UK general medical registrars and respiratory consultants assessing procedural competence and confidence in chest drain insertion, alongside attitudes to out-of-hours pleural procedures.5 The term ‘out-of-hours working’ was not explicitly defined and this carries different operational meanings across National Health Service trusts. We would consider this term to mean when there is not immediately available (ie, on-site) a core body of staff across a range of seniorities within both acute (including critical care) and non-acute areas (ie, general/specialty ward) to provide both pleural intervention and support for any complications. In practice, this means times where there is simply ‘on-call’ non-resident staff. This would generally mean later evening and overnight 7 days a week but not necessarily parts of Saturday and Sunday where there may be a senior led and delivered specialty service.

Although respiratory trainees were included in the Jajbhay data, they represented a minority of respondents (66/455, 14.5%). While the survey population was weighted towards London, the findings reflect concerns that resonate nationally. Although the majority of non-respiratory trainees (68.1%) reported direct observation of procedural skills procedural sign-off for intercostal drain insertion for pneumothorax, confidence was markedly lower: fewer than half (47%) felt confident performing the procedure with supervision, and only a small minority (11%) felt confident to do so independently. Notably, many non-respiratory trainees (53%) perceived an expectation that they would be required to insert chest drains with indirect supervision, despite this not being explicitly mandated within the IM curriculum. This view was shared by a substantial proportion (41%) of respiratory consultants, highlighting a clear disconnect between curriculum requirements and service expectations of those working in general medicine. Alongside this perception of poorly confident registrars being expected to perform such procedures, the majority (69%) had not performed a pleural procedure within the preceding year, representing a significant patient safety concern.

Against this backdrop of evolving care pathways, limited procedural exposure and clear concern about capability and accountability for pleural procedures in the general medical workforce, we argue that training curricula must adapt. In doing so, it is necessary to reconsider how the ‘right’ to train in and perform pleural procedures is defined. Currently, the IM Stage 1 curriculum requires trainees to demonstrate competence in diagnostic pleural aspiration for fluid and pleural aspiration for pneumothorax (to ‘be able to decompress a large symptomatic pneumothorax’) unsupervised by end of IMY2. In addition, competency must be demonstrated in a ‘skills lab’ or via ‘satisfactory supervised practice’ for intercostal chest drain insertion.6 In contrast, there is no explicit requirement for IM trainees to demonstrate any level of capability in therapeutic aspiration of a pleural effusion. These inconsistencies warrant careful re-evaluation in light of contemporary pleural practice and patient safety priorities.

Diagnostic pleural aspiration - the case for change

The BTS have recently published the first ever training standard for pleural procedures.7 This document aims to harmonise training across professional groups and clinical settings, and defines the specific procedural capabilities required at different Capability in Practice levels, aligned to existing curricula but with important refinements. At the core of independent practice is not technical proficiency alone, but the ability to make an appropriate decision to intervene. Accordingly, the training standard explicitly requires individuals to demonstrate capability to act as an ‘intervention decision maker’ in order to be entrusted with independent practice.

IM Stage 1 trainees should be trained in the assessment and management of pleural disease: in particular, including the ability to identify when pleural intervention may be indicated and, critically, when it is not. Therefore, they need to be able to identify when to refer on to an appropriately competent operator, but do not need to be the independent ‘intervention decision makers’ themselves. That right should be appropriately reserved for clinicians with the requisite procedure volume and decision-making expertise.

Importantly, pleural procedures for fluid should only be undertaken with the procedure operator having personally conducted or directly witnessed an ultrasound assessment of the effusion. Remote or delegated ‘X marks the spot’ approaches are not acceptable and are associated with similar complication rates to not using ultrasound.8 IM trainees are not required to develop TUS capability within the current curriculum and, as such, cannot reasonably be expected to perform pleural aspiration independently, especially in out-of-hours settings where the risks are inherently higher. TUS is a vital tool in assessment of a pleural effusion, providing more information than simply guiding intervention, and so a clinician who lacks US competencies cannot be expected to be able to comprehensively assess and manage a patient with pleural effusion. Where urgent diagnostic aspiration is genuinely indicated—for example, in suspected pleural infection—trainees in IM are not required to develop any degree of thoracic ultrasound capability and thus cannot perform the procedure with any degree of independence.

Taken together, we therefore argue that there is no justification need to stipulate higher capability than requirement to practice with direct supervision for pleural aspiration in the IMY curriculum.

Predictable counterarguments arise, such as scenarios in which a diagnostic pleural aspiration is perceived to be required out of hours, or where ultrasound is provided by a radiologist unable to supervise the procedure itself. These are however issues of service configuration rather than training deficits. Training large numbers of clinicians to perform a procedure they will rarely practice is neither a proportionate nor an effective response to infrequent service pressures. There are clear parallels with other specialities and how similar invasive procedures have (appropriately) moved out of the general medicine curriculum as their practice has evolved. Some of the authors shudder at the memory of temporary cardiac pacing wires and sigmoidoscopy being deemed ‘essential’ General Internal Medicine competencies; procedures such as liver biopsy and pericardiocentesis have similarly transitioned to specialist-delivered interventions, supported by specialty-specific training in imaging and procedural decision making. To our knowledge, these changes have not been associated with adverse patient consequences, and we believe the same will hold true for pleural interventions. Rare out-of-hours needs can and should be mitigated through service-level solutions, while the right to train in and independently perform pleural procedures should rest with clinicians who practice them regularly within appropriate specialist frameworks.

Therapeutic pleural aspiration (fluid)

Therapeutic pleural effusion aspiration is undertaken primarily for symptomatic relief rather than prognostic benefit and, like diagnostic procedures, is increasingly managed in an ambulatory or outpatient pathways within specialist pleural services. Where hospital admission is required, these interventions are most commonly performed during working hours by respiratory or pleural teams with appropriate expertise and support.

Concerns are often raised regarding the acutely breathless patient with a large pleural effusion on the acute medical take. However, even when therapeutic aspiration is likely to be indicated, it is uncommon for this to represent a true medical emergency. In the vast majority of cases, pleural effusions can be managed safely with conservative measures awaiting specialist review. Only in rare circumstances—such as significant haemodynamic compromise or exceptionally high oxygen requirements—is immediate intervention necessary. This distinction is important when considering training requirements and procedural rights. Again, the authors’ position is that the infrequent need for urgent therapeutic aspiration does not justify routine entrustment of non-specialist clinicians. Instead, the safer and more proportionate response here is that care pathways should be designed to ensure that patients are supported in a place of safety until timely intervention can be provided by appropriately trained operators.9

Pneumothorax

A key consideration in this debate is the management of large, symptomatic pneumothorax with haemodynamic instability (‘tension pneumothorax’). Much of the rationale for retaining pleural procedures within the IM curriculum relates to the need for IM trainees to be equipped to manage this rare, but potentially life-threatening emergency situation, particularly in remote or less well-staffed hospitals. We recognise and accept this concern. However, in contemporary hospital practice, the clinicians best equipped to manage peri-arrest physiology—including tension pneumothorax—are typically senior doctors in anaesthesia, intensive care, or emergency medicine, who are available 24 hours a day and trained specifically in the management of time-critical physiological emergencies. These clinicians possess not only the technical skills required for emergency decompression, but also the broader expertise necessary to manage the accompanying airway, ventilatory and haemodynamic instability.

From a patient safety perspective, the ‘right’ response to suspected tension pneumothorax is therefore not universal procedural competence across the general medical workforce, but rapid recognition, escalation and access to the most appropriate senior support. IM trainees should be trained to identify tension physiology promptly, initiate immediate escalation pathways and support emergency management, rather than being routinely entrusted with independent pleural intervention.

This distinction is important. While there may be extreme and rare circumstances in which no alternative senior support is immediately available, such scenarios should not drive routine curricular requirements. Designing training around exceptional failure modes risks normalising unsafe expectations and diluting procedural competence. Instead, hospitals should be expected to have robust, clearly defined escalation pathways that ensure rapid access to clinicians with the appropriate skills to manage life-threatening pneumothorax at any time of day.

In this context, it is appropriate for IM trainees to be familiar with the principles of emergency decompression through theoretical and simulated training, but not to hold the right to independent procedural practice. We would argue that having been taught how to perform an elective diagnostic pleural aspiration does not transfer across to competence in decompression of a tension pneumothorax, which is how it has sometimes been justified for inclusion in the IMS1 curriculum. The latter is a challenging condition for even experienced operators to intervene in and represents a very different technical procedure and anatomy to consider. Definitive management of tension pneumothorax, including intercostal chest drain insertion, should remain the responsibility of clinicians who perform these procedures regularly and within appropriate governance frameworks.

Wider capability considerations

Trainees who progress into specialty training in respiratory medicine will, by necessity, develop higher levels of capability in both pleural aspiration and chest drain insertion. However, it is clear that for those IM trainees entering other specialties, it will be near impossible to maintain capability in these potentially high-risk procedures. This raises legitimate concerns about whether continued curricular requirements for independent procedural capability outside respiratory medicine remain appropriate or safe, and warrants careful re-evaluation.

In this editorial, we have focused primarily on training expectations during the early years of general IM. However, similar principles apply to higher specialty training. While clinicians training in intensive care medicine and, in certain settings, acute IM will require capability in intercostal chest drain insertion, this requirement cannot be justified on the basis of specialty designation alone. The same concerns regarding skill decay and patient safety apply wherever procedural exposure is infrequent. We also need to ensure within respiratory training programmes mechanisms are clearly in place to support maintenance of capability in pleural procedures and to maintain any individual’s ‘right’ to perform these.

The right to be trained in and independently perform pleural procedures should be contingent on regular procedural volume, embedded supervision and clear governance to support maintenance of competence, in keeping with the BTS training standards. Where pleural interventions are a routine component of practice, this is justified; where they are not, extending procedural training risks creating nominal competence without sufficient exposure to ensure safety. Training should therefore be targeted to services that deliver pleural procedures frequently, prompting reconsideration of which invasive skills belong within generalist vs specialist practice.

Historical precedent supports this distinction. There are few, if any, non-cardiology medical registrars who would now consider insertion of a temporary pacing wire to fall within the scope of general IM practice. Such procedures are appropriately delivered exclusively by specialists with dedicated training, experience and governance frameworks. Data from the recent BTS Pleural Services Organisational audit would support the notion that pleural procedures are increasingly analogous in both complexity and risk profile. The question of out-of-hours pleural intervention should be addressed through service configuration and escalation pathways, rather than by extending independent procedural training to clinicians who cannot reasonably maintain competence.

The data from Jajbhay et al highlights that lack of confidence in chest drain insertion reflects limitations in service design rather than deficiencies in training. Services require clear pathways that define when pleural intervention is indicated, who should perform it and how appropriately trained operators can be accessed in a timely manner. We recognise the inherent challenges across the varied nature of secondary care services in delivering such pathways, but this should be achievable through collaborative approaches between respiratory and acute/critical care services and where appropriate regional networks. In 2026, it would be inconceivable for a patient presenting with an acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction not to be directed immediately to an interventional cardiologist, with the requisite procedural and decision-making expertise.

Ultimately, this debate should be anchored to a simple and familiar clinical principle: when an invasive pleural intervention is required, most clinicians would wish it to be performed by the most experienced and appropriately trained operator available. Aligning training curricula and service delivery with this expectation is not about professional boundaries, but about meeting the standard of care we would all want for our own relatives.
