Current practices of index hospitalization anticoagulant use in pediatric patients with acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Abbey Elsbernd, Natalie Grills, James W. Antoon, Kathryn E. Kyler, Lucas E. Orth, Timothy J. Savage, Matt Hall, Jennifer L. Goldman, Shannon L. Carpenter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Thrombosis in pediatric patients with AML is associated with longer hospital stays, greater costs, and greater in-hospital mortality.•Approaches to anticoagulation vary greatly across institutions in terms of use and anticoagulant choice.

Introduction

Malignancy is a well-established risk factor for the development of thrombosis.1 Virchow triad dictates 3 factors that contribute to the development of arterial and venous thrombosis, including hematologic stasis, endothelial injury, and an inflammatory hypercoagulable state, with any and all of these possible and reported in the setting of hematologic malignancies. These risk factors can be exacerbated by the clinical course and treatment for cancer, including the presence of a central venous line, infections, and prolonged hospital courses.2, 3, 4 The development of major thrombotic events, including deep venous thromboembolism and pulmonary emboli, can have a significant negative impact on morbidity and mortality.

Thrombosis incidence has been reported in ∼2% to 13% of the patients treated for acute myeloid leukemia (AML).3 These data are limited by the inclusion of acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) in many studies, which confers a different thrombotic risk, and are primarily reported from adult populations.3,5,6 In pediatric populations alone, rates of 6.0% to 6.6% in patients with AML have been found.7,8

Management of thromboembolism (TE) in those with AML with anticoagulation is complicated by prolonged chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia and associated high rates of hemorrhage and bleeding complications.9 There are no standardized recommendations for either prophylactic or therapeutic anticoagulation after a venous thromboembolism or pulmonary embolism for pediatric patients with AML, with limited robust supporting evidence.10

To date, the management of TE in AML is typically based on adult recommendations. The American Society of Clinical Oncology and American Society of Hematology guidelines recommend prophylaxis for high-risk oncology patients with multiple risk factors for thrombosis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) or direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) such as apixaban or rivaroxaban, with therapeutic use of similar agents for treatment of thromboembolism.11,12 Routine prophylaxis for all patients is not recommended. Even for adults, research is still needed regarding anticoagulation management in severe thrombocytopenia and hematological malignancies. LMWH in general remains the standard recommended treatment in AML, with dosing modified based on platelet count and anticoagulation held in those with platelet counts of <25 × 109/L, although real-life practices may vary by institution.3

In the absence of pediatric guidance, we sought to understand current clinical practice and use of anticoagulation during index hospitalization in children with AML. We evaluated which patients with AML received anticoagulation and what anticoagulation was used, alongside the presence of thrombotic and bleeding events (BEs), accounting for anticoagulation use status. There is limited evidence on the use of anticoagulation in patients with AML, with even less evidence available for the pediatric population. Understanding current trends in practice will help better understand indications prompting the decision to initiate anticoagulation in patients with AML, as well as rates of BE.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective cohort study of patients with the diagnosis of AML within the Pediatric Health Information System (PHIS; Children’s Hospital Association, Lenexa, KS) database, admitted to tertiary children’s hospitals from 1 January 2016 to 31 December 2024, was performed, identifying patients aged 1 to 21 years upon admission and discharge across 49 hospitals. PHIS is an administrative and billing database of 49 geographically diverse tertiary children’s hospitals across 27 states and the District of Columbia.13 Inpatient data available through this database included demographics, daily billing data, and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), diagnoses. Hospital costs referenced in this study are estimated in PHIS using hospital/year-specific cost-to-charge ratios. The Children’s Hospital Association and participating hospitals jointly assure data quality. The Children’s Mercy Hospital Institutional Review Board granted exempt status for this study.

Patient population

The diagnosis of AML was defined by ICD-10 codes (C92.0, C92.3, C92.6, and C92.A) at discharge. Patients were only included during their index admission for treatment of AML, defined as the initial hospitalization with an AML diagnosis during the examined time frame. Patients were excluded if they had a diagnosis based on ICD-10 codes of APL (C92.4) or a diagnosis of a congenital bleeding disorder, primary thrombophilia, or other coagulation defect (D68.0, D68.1, D68.2, D68.3, D68.4, D68.5, D68.6, D68.9). The decision to exclude patients with APL was based on significant coagulopathy associated with this underlying diagnosis.14

Study definitions

Demographic data, diagnosis of TE, anticoagulation use, and BE diagnoses were extracted. Any ICD-10 code corresponding to TE was included (supplemental Table 1); any ICD-10 code corresponding to bleeding was categorized as a BE (supplemental Table 2). For the purposes of this article, cerebral sinus venous thrombosis and arterial thrombosis were separate categories from cerebral infarction. By PHIS policy, if an event occurred in >0 but <10 patients, exact numbers were not reported for privacy reasons. Evaluated anticoagulants included LMWHs, oral thrombin inhibitors, oral factor Xa inhibitors, and the vitamin K antagonist warfarin. Continuous anticoagulants, such as unfractionated heparin and bivalirudin, were excluded because of their role in complex critical care outside of the management of thrombosis. Although dosing information was unavailable, presumed prophylaxis was defined as patients receiving anticoagulation in the absence of a TE diagnosis code.

Statistical analysis

To summarize clinical and demographic characteristics of our cohort, we used frequencies with percentages for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables. Comparisons of patient characteristics were performed using the χ2 test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 3768 patients with AML were included, with 167 (4.4%) experiencing a TE (Figure 1; Table 1). Patients with TEs tended to be older than those without TEs (median, 13 years [IQR, 7-17] vs median, 10 years [IQR, 3-15]; P < .001). Anticoagulation use

Of the 3768 patients with AML, 171 (4.5%) received anticoagulation. Almost half of these patients received anticoagulation for presumed prophylaxis (83/171 [48.5%]). Of patients with a TE, only 88 (52.3%) received anticoagulation. Enoxaparin was the most frequently used anticoagulant (150/171 [87.7%]), followed by rivaroxaban (22 patients [12.9%]). Fifteen (8.8%) patients received both heparins and factor Xa inhibitors. Dalteparin, tinzaparin, betrixaban, and edoxaban were not used. BEs

A total of 750 patients (20% of the cohort) experienced ≥1 BE (Table 3). For those 750 patients, 966 individual BEs were recorded, with 154 patients experiencing bleeding at >1 location. The most common BE was ear, nose, and throat (ENT) bleeding, occurring in 433 (57.7%) patients with a BE. ENT and gastrointestinal BEs were most frequently reported concurrently, occuring in 22 patients who experienced >1 bleeding type. Discussion

Thrombosis is a significant complication in pediatric AML. Of the cohort, 4.4% of the patients developed a TE during their index hospitalization, with some patients experiencing multiple events. Thrombosis in these patients was significantly associated with longer hospital stays, longer ICU stays, increased hospital costs, and increased in-hospital mortality. Similar detrimental impacts with hospital-associated TE have been reported in general, nononcology pediatric populations.15 This may be because of the fact that ICU care, mechanical ventilation, and prolonged hospitalizations are established risk factors for TE development; simply put, TEs are more common in patients who are sick and have medically complex conditions.16,17 However, it still remains clear that TE is a detrimental complication associated with worse patient outcomes.

Our study did not identify standardized or consistent anticoagulant use during index hospitalization across the included institutions. Almost half of patients with a TE did not receive any anticoagulation at all, and nearly half of all patients with anticoagulation received it for presumed primary prophylaxis. Some institutions did not use any anticoagulation for either presumed prophylaxis or presumed therapeutic use. Enoxaparin was the most frequently used anticoagulant, followed by rivaroxaban, but only some institutions chose to use any DOAC, and some institutions did not use any anticoagulation at all. When taken as a whole, this suggests great inconsistency among institutional approaches to the use of anticoagulation for both therapeutic and prophylactic use in this population.

A total of 750 patients (20%) experienced ≥1 BE. Most patients (705/750 [94%]) with BE received no anticoagulation. Potential explanations for this finding could be that most patients did not receive anticoagulation, and that medical teams may use anticoagulation less frequently in patients who are at high risk for bleeding. Bleeding prevalence was not found to be greater among patients who received presumed prophylaxis compared to those who received presumed therapeutic anticoagulation, and those who did not receive anticoagulation, although it neared statistical significance in unadjusted analysis. Further investigation would be beneficial to understand the bleeding risk and the overall safety of use.

The management of cancer-related thrombosis is challenging in general, with AML posing a unique challenge. Several risk factors (hyperleukocytosis, immobility, and infection) may pose additional risks for thrombosis development, but prolonged thrombocytopenia makes decision-making on anticoagulation management difficult.3,5,18 The literature is mixed on the role of platelet counts as a risk factor for the development of thrombosis; however, AML-associated thrombocytopenia is a well-established risk factor for hemorrhage.3,5,6,9,19 There are no clear guidelines written for the management of thrombosis in pediatric AML, which adds significant context to the clinical practice variability found in this study.

Literature specifically addressing anticoagulation use in pediatric cancer has been primarily explored in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) or other malignancies. In ALL specifically, more research has been performed because of the association of thrombosis with asparaginase and steroids used during standard-of-care therapy. LMWH has been used as the most common agent for therapeutic anticoagulation in pediatric ALL.20 Several studies have evaluated indications and strategies for venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis in pediatric ALL and have generally identified a reduction in thrombosis with LMWH.21, 22, 23 Patient accrual has been a barrier to the development of robust studies in this realm.

DOACs such as apixaban and rivaroxaban are under active investigation for use in pediatric cancer. A subgroup analysis from the EINSTEIN-Jr phase 3 study examining pediatric patients with thromboembolism and cancer (including both solid and hematologic malignancies) identified safe and efficacious use of the DOAC rivaroxaban in a limited cohort; frequent interruptions in anticoagulation were noted because of chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia.24 The PREVAPIX-ALL trial, a phase 3, open-label, randomized, controlled trial evaluating the use of apixaban for VTE prophylaxis, did not identify clinical benefit for universal administration but did identify an increase in nonmajor bleeding.25 Subgroup analysis found statistically significant VTE risk reduction without increased bleeding in children with ALL and obesity, identifying a specific benefit in a high-risk population.26 When taken as a whole, results suggested consideration of DOACs for safe use in high-risk patients with notable potential benefit.

In the absence of pediatric data for AML, most research addressing thromboses and the use of anticoagulation in AML comes from adult literature. For these patients, generally TEs were not found to affect overall survival, with the exclusion of pulmonary emboli, compared to the reported worse in-hospital mortality found in our study.5,6,27,28 However, these data should be interpreted with caution, as it is likely that patients who are more ill and have complex conditions are likely to have increased thrombosis risk. In adult literature, guidelines are present for the consideration of prophylaxis and/or treatment anticoagulation in patients with satisfactory platelet counts, but the more detailed and practical implementation of anticoagulation varies.29

There are several limitations to consider for this study. This study relies on the use of ICD diagnosis codes for the identification of thromboembolism and BE in PHIS, which has not been carefully validated for sensitivity and specificity. Although other studies have used similar methodology for the identification of thrombosis and BE, accuracy is affected by billing information.16,17 In ideal circumstances, further validation of the diagnosis with documentation by a physician, confirmed radiographic evidence of a TE, multiple combined or persistent use of ICD codes, or use of an anticoagulant prescription would be beneficial to increase the validity and positive predictive value of our definition.30, 31, 32 We were unable to collect this additional clinical information in PHIS. We additionally elected not to validate with anticoagulant prescriptions as we suspected not all patients with AML would receive anticoagulation, as seen by the results of this study.

Weight-based dosing information per kilogram was not available for the anticoagulants used; thus, the determination of therapeutic dosing was inferred based on the presence of a TE diagnosis, with prophylaxis presumed if no TE was identified. Individual-level laboratory values, including hemoglobin and platelet counts, were not available in the database for analysis. Specific information relating to each patient’s individual course over time was unavailable for review, which may influence the decision to hold anticoagulation; “cause and effect” could not be evaluated as it relates to the timing of TE, BE, and anticoagulation initiation/discontinuation. We could also not determine if BEs were fatal. Because of the absence of hemoglobin values and mortality associated with bleeding, we were unable to use standardized definitions of bleeding, such as the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis definitions, which rely, in part, on this information.33,34

Finally, patients with APL, primary bleeding disorders, and thrombophilias were excluded. Patients with APL were excluded, given the significant differences in expected hospital course, bleeding and thrombosis risk, and differing chemotherapy regimens used.14 Patients with primary bleeding disorders and thrombophilias were excluded to remove confounding factors for greater generalizability of this population. Additional study with the inclusion of these subgroups could be helpful to better understand the differences in bleeding and thrombosis risk among these populations.

Prospective research and robust clinical trials are needed for further recommendations in pediatric patients for all cancer-associated thrombosis and for the determination of the best use for prophylactic and therapeutic anticoagulation in high-risk patient subgroups. We suspect that when determining strategies for anticoagulation in AML and in cancer-related thromboses in pediatric patients, there is unlikely to be a satisfactory universal approach, and each decision will likely require careful consideration of patient-related factors. Further insight is needed into strategies for making clinical decisions regarding the careful balancing act of hemostasis in cancer-related thrombosis.
