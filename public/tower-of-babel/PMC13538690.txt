Exploration of the auricle morphology of young adults from 10 countries
Authors: Rukiye Sumeyye Bakici, Necati Emre Sahin, Seyma Toy, Zulal Oner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare auricle morphology across individuals from 10 countries. A total of 1,351 auricle photographs from 676 student volunteers (367 males, 309 females) at Karabük University were analyzed. Participants originated from Türkiye, Chad, Morocco, Gabon, Iran, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Senegal, Syria, and Jordan/Palestine. Morphological parameters included external auricle shape, helix form, antihelix, triangular fossa, antitragus, incisura intertragica, tragus, earlobe shape, earlobe–cheek attachment, and Darwin’s tubercle. The participants were aged 16–30 years, with a mean age of 20.9 ± 3.3 years. Participants were aged 16–30 years (mean 20.9 ± 3.3). External auricle shape differed significantly by country (χ2 = 185.764, df = 54, p < 0.001), with round shapes predominant in Chad, triangular in Morocco and Kazakhstan, bagel in Gabon and Senegal, and oval in Türkiye, Uzbekistan, and Syria. Helix form also differed significantly (χ2 = 178.928, df = 63, p < 0.001). This study provides a standardized comparative framework of auricular morphology across multiple populations, offering a reference dataset that may support forensic identification, anthropological research, and clinical applications such as auricular reconstruction.

Introduction

The face is of great importance in forensic science and forensic anthropology. The most frequently examined structure in the facial area is the auricle (the external, visible part of the ear, sometimes also called the pinna)1. Determining auricle morphometry is crucial for individual identification, sex estimation, and age determination in forensic science2,3. Features such as the auricle’s size, shape, position, and symmetry significantly impact the overall facial aesthetics4.

The auricle may become deformed due to trauma, surgical resection, or congenital malformations5. Therefore, morphometric measurements of the auricle are of great importance for plastic auricle reconstruction and facial rejuvenation surgeries6. In addition, anthropometric data are always needed to define a specific population and to design hearing aids and auricle prostheses suitable for use by that population7.

The auricle’s shape, size, and orientation are as unique as a fingerprint, and it is important from an aesthetic perspective that it has a natural, beautiful shape7. As otoplasty surgery becomes increasingly popular, knowing the standard dimensions of the auricle is crucial8. The morphometry of the auricle has been found to vary by age, sex, and ethnicity, and to exhibit significant differences between the right and left auricles of the same individual6,9. In addition, males’ auricles are reported to be larger than females’ auricles and to increase in both length and width with age throughout life10,11.

Previous studies have demonstrated that auricle morphology varies according to age, sex, and population; however, most have been limited to single populations. In this context, the present study aims to provide a comparative evaluation of auricular morphoscopic features across individuals from different countries under standardized conditions.

Materials and methods

Study setting and participants

This prospective study was approved by the Local Non-Interventional Ethics Committee at Karabük University (Reference No: 726/2022). It examined a dataset of 1,351 auricle photographs from 676 student volunteers (367 males and 309 females) at Karabük University. Participants had no history of auricle surgery or deformity. One of the right earlobes was excluded from this study because it underwent corrective surgery. The participants were from Türkiye, Chad, Morocco, Gabon, Iran, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Senegal, Syria, Palestine, and Jordan (Table 1). Due to demographic overlap, individuals from Palestine and Jordan were evaluated as a combined “Palestine/Jordan” group. All participants provided written and verbal informed consent before enrolment in this study.

Data collection

All auricle photographs were acquired using an Android phone camera (Redmi Note 7; Xiaomi, Beijing, China) with a 48-megapixel AI dual camera. The participants were seated upright, with their eyes looking straight ahead and their zygomatic arch parallel to the floor. In this position, photographs of the auricle were taken from a standard side profile at a distance of 15 cm, using a millimeter ruler to ensure they were in the same plane as the face.

The following auricle parameters were examined: external auricle shape (triangular, rectangular, oval, round, bagel, pentagon, or upper triangle–lower round; Figs. 1 and 2), helix form (normal rolled, wide covering scapha, flat, concave margin, folded upper, folded lower, notch, or folded upper–lower flat; Fig. 3), antihelix form (strong, medium, or weak), triangular fossa (deep, medium, or shallow), antitragus form (slight, medium, or prominent), earlobe–cheek attachment (attached, partially-free, or free; Fig. 4), incisura intertragica shape (round, Horseshoe, V-shape, or deep U-shape), tragus shape (long, round, single knob, w-knob, or triangular round; Fig. 5), earlobe shape (triangular, rectangular, round, oval, receding upward, W, or parabolic), and Darwin’s tubercle (absent, nodosity, enlargement, or projection; Fig. 6; Table 2). To ensure the objectivity and reproducibility of the morphological assessments, a detailed scoring key was established. This includes a descriptive table of all analyzed parameters (Table 2) and a revised set of labelled figures (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6) that illustrate the representative characteristics of each scoring category. The classification used in this research is modeled after Rani et al.12. All parameters were classified jointly by two anatomists. To ensure the maximum reliability of the morphological identifications, a dual-observer consensus method was employed, following the protocol described by Rani (2020). Each morphoscopic trait was evaluated simultaneously by two researchers. Instead of independent scoring, observations were discussed in real-time, and a score was assigned only when a mutual consensus was reached between both observers. This simultaneous evaluation process was adopted to minimize subjectivity and ensure that all morphological variations were interpreted consistently throughout the study. Information about the participants’ country of origin, age, sex, and surgical history was entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed using SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical data, including sexual dimorphism and the distribution of morphological parameters by country, were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test. Due to the presence of multiple categories and cells with low expected frequencies, Monte Carlo (MC) simulation (based on 10,000 iterations) was employed to obtain robust and exact p-values, hereafter denoted as pmc. To measure the strength of the associations, Cramér’s V was calculated and interpreted as an effect size indicator based on the benchmarks proposed by Kim (2017)13. Additionally, to assess potential laterality, symmetry between the left and right-side measurements was evaluated using the McNemar-Bowker test, while the level of agreement between sides was determined using Cohen’s Kappa (κ) coefficient. Furthermore, adjusted standardized residuals were examined as a post-hoc procedure to identify specific groups contributing to statistically significant differences (p < 0.05). To investigate the morphological structure of ear shapes across different countries, both hierarchical and non-hierarchical (K-means) clustering techniques were employed. Initially, hierarchical clustering using Ward’s method with squared Euclidean distance was performed to identify the optimal number of clusters, which was subsequently refined and validated through K-means clustering analysis. The contribution and significance of each morphological trait to the resulting cluster profiles were evaluated using one-way ANOVA, with a significance threshold set at (p < 0.001).

Results

The 676 participants were aged 16–30 years, with a mean age of 20.9 ± 3.3 years. A total of 1,351 auricles (675 right and 676 left) were evaluated morphologically. To examine the internal consistency of the sex distribution across the ten countries, adjusted standardized residuals were calculated as a post-hoc test. The analysis revealed significant disproportions in three country groups, with adjusted residuals of 2.5 (Morocco), 2.6 (Uzbekistan), and 4.2 (Jordan/Palestine). A higher proportion of males was observed in Uzbekistan and Jordan/Palestine, whereas a higher proportion of females was observed in Morocco (χ2 = 30.660, df = 9, p < 0.001, V = 0.151, Table 3).

The external auricle shape did not differ significantly by sex (p > 0.05). However, helix forms did differ significantly by sex (χ2 = 125.875, df = 7, p < 0.001, V = 0.305), with males predominantly exhibiting normal rolled, concave margin, and folded lower helix forms, and females predominantly exhibiting folded upper and folded upper–lower flat helix forms. The antihelix form also differed significantly by sex (χ2 = 31.566, df = 2, p < 0.001, V = 0.153), being strong in males and medium in females.

The triangular fossa did not differ significantly by sex (p > 0.05). The incisura intertragica was predominantly round in males, whereas a horseshoe shape was more common in females (χ2 = 17.912, df = 3, p < 0.001, V = 0.115). Tragus shape also differed significantly by sex (χ2 = 33.496, df = 4, p < 0.001, V = 0.157), with a long form more common in males and a single-knob form more common in females. Moreover, antitragus form differed significantly by sex (χ2 = 8.589, df = 2, p = 0.014, V = 0.080), with it only being classified as prominent in females.

Earlobe–cheek attachment did not differ significantly by sex (p > 0.05). However, earlobe shape differed significantly by sex (χ2 = 24.898, df = 6, p < 0.001, V = 0.136), being predominantly rectangular and oval in males, and receding upward, W-shaped, and parabolic in females. Moreover, Darwin’s tubercle differed significantly by sex (χ2 = 11.859, df = 3, p = 0.008, V = 0.094), with absence more common in males and enlargement more common in females.

External auricle shape differed significantly by country (χ2 = 185.764, df = 54, pmc < 0.001, V = 0.151; Table 4). Round shapes were most common in participants from Chad, triangular shapes in Morocco and Kazakhstan, bagel shapes in Gabon and Senegal, and oval shapes in Türkiye, Uzbekistan, and Syria.

Multivariate statistical analysis techniques were used to identify structural similarities and differences among morphological shape groups of countries. The analysis process was structured with a two-stage approach. In the first stage, Hierarchical Cluster Analysis was applied to the initially defined parameter set. The Ward Method, based on variance minimization, was chosen as the clustering algorithm, and Squared Euclidean Distance was used for distance measurement. Based on the hierarchical analysis outputs, variables with low explanatory power for morphological variance were eliminated, and the focus was placed on the 6 key parameters with the highest discriminative power. In the second stage, the dendrogram structure and inter-cluster distance coefficients obtained from the hierarchical analysis were examined. Considering the breakpoints and morphological continuities in the dataset, it was determined that the most stable and interpretable structural integrity was achieved in the 5-cluster solution model.

Discussion

This study examined the morphological characteristics of the auricle in 676 young adults (1,351 auricles) from 10 countries studying at Karabük University. The findings showed that auricle morphology differed significantly by sex and country of origin. Significant sexual dimorphism was observed, particularly in helix form, antihelix form, tragus shape, earlobe shape, and Darwin’s tubercle. In addition, external auricle shape, helix form, antihelix form, incisura intertragica shape, Darwin’s tubercle, and earlobe morphology exhibited distinct distribution patterns across countries. These results indicate that the auricle varies not only between individuals but also across populations. Therefore, beyond contributing to the anthropological literature, these findings may serve as a useful reference for forensic identification and reconstructive applications.

Helix morphology is one of the most striking variations in auricle anatomy, differing significantly by both sex and ethnicity. Krishan et al.14 reported that the normal rolled helix is the most common form, but rates vary across populations. Indeed, Farkas15 reported a rate of 76–79% in Americans, Singh and Purkait16 reported a rate of 56%–60% in the central region of India, and Krishan et al.14 reported a rate of 44%–51% in North India. In our study, the normal rolled form was more prominent in males, whereas the upper folded and upper folded-lower flat forms were more prominent in females. This result is consistent with Krishan et al.14, who emphasised a higher prevalence of broad-covered scapha form in females compared to males, suggesting that sexual dimorphism in helix morphology may exhibit similar trends across different populations. In terms of ethnicity, the normal rolled form was predominant among participants from Chad, the wide scapha form was predominant among those from Senegal and Gabon, the concave margin form was predominant among those from Türkiye and Morocco, and the folded upper form was predominant among those from Syria (p < 0.001). These differences confirm that the helix form varies among populations, as noted by Singh and Purkait16 and Krishan et al.14, and suggest that the auricle is a strong distinguishing feature for both biometric identification and anthropological characterization.

Significant differences in antihelix form by both sex and country further highlight the complex structure of auricle morphology, which is shaped by genetic, environmental, and developmental factors. In this study, a strong antihelix form was more common in males, whereas a medium form was more common in females. This pattern is consistent with the geometric morphometric analysis by Ocakoğlu et al.17, which demonstrated sexual dimorphism in the helix and antihelix regions. These findings suggest that structural characteristics such as antihelix depth and cartilage thickness may be influenced by hormonal or developmental factors. In addition, the observed differences across countries indicate that population-related factors also contribute to variation in antihelix morphology. Perrone et al.18 reported similar ethnic variation in antihelix prominence among Brazilian children. In contrast, a study of young Turkish adults found no significant differences in antihelix angle across ethnic groups, although it noted considerable interindividual variation19. Taken together, these findings support the role of the antihelix as a variable anatomical feature with potential relevance in anthropological and forensic assessments.

In our study, significant differences in earlobe shape by both sex and country reveal population-based diversity and marked sexual dimorphism in external auricle morphology. The higher prevalence of rectangular and oval earlobe shapes in males and upward-curved, W-shaped, and parabolic earlobe shapes in females is consistent with the findings of Sezgin and Ersoy20, who reported that helical and earlobe shapes provided up to 88% accuracy in sex prediction in a Turkish cohort. Acharya et al.21 reported that the arched earlobe type is more common in males and the triangular earlobe type in females in the South Indian population; however, they emphasized that metric variables, such as earlobe width, are not equally determinative across populations. Similarly, Hiware et al.22 reported that earlobe width was significantly greater in females than in males in a Saudi Arabian cohort, although earlobe height did not differ by sex. In a North Indian sample, Krishan et al.14 reported that the arched earlobe type was the most common form in both males and females, while the square and triangular earlobe types were found at lower frequencies. In our study, the predominance of more prominent, wider earlobe shapes (rectangular and oval) in males and of thinner, upward-curving earlobe shapes (receding upward, W-shaped, and parabolic) in females aligns with these general trends.

Auricle morphology differed significantly across countries; complete earlobe–cheek attachment was more common among participants from Gabon, Kazakhstan, and Senegal, while free earlobes were more common among those from Türkiye and Syria, indicating the sensitivity of auricle morphology to genetic and environmental factors. The significant differences in earlobe morphology among countries reveal that this structure is a strong indicator of ethnic, geographic, and genetic diversity. In Kenya, free earlobes were predominant in Kenyan Indians, while earlobe–cheek attachment was predominant in indigenous Kenyans23. Similarly, high rates of earlobe–cheek attachment have been reported in the Indian subcontinent (65%)24. A similar trend was observed among Koreans (61.2%)25. In contrast, free earlobes were more common in the Kalabari tribe in Nigeria (59.4%)26. A multiethnic GWAS study revealed the genetic basis of these variations, showing that earlobe attachment is polygenic and underlied by different genetic loci across populations27. Morphometric data also show that earlobe length and width vary by ethnicity, and these differences may serve as an important marker in forensic identification, reconstructive surgery, and anthropological research14.

Auricular morphology may also provide significant insights into the geographical origins of individuals, particularly in high-traffic settings such as international airports. Furthermore, in cases of severe facial trauma where the ears remain preserved, or in mass casualty incidents within cosmopolitan populations, these features can offer valuable markers regarding an individual’s ancestry or population background. Such data is also pertinent for nations experiencing high migration—driven by factors like international education—and in contexts of conflict-related mass fatalities28,29. Based on the analyses conducted, it was concluded that individuals from Senegal and Jordan/Palestine could be identified by earlobe morphology, individuals from Chad, Gabon, and Jordan/Palestine by external ear shape, and individuals from Türkiye by helix and tragus shape.

In our study, Darwin’s tubercle differed by both sex and country, suggesting that this structure is not only an anatomical variation but also a reflection of genetic and evolutionary factors. While our findings show that Darwin’s tubercle is absent more often in males and its enlargement is more common in females, Krishan et al.14 reported that the nodosity type is common in both sexes and is more common in males than in females. This difference suggests that the distribution of morphological types is influenced by ethnic and geographic factors, identifying Darwin’s tubercle as a valuable anthropological marker in terms of sexual dimorphism and inter-population variation.

The main strength of this study lies in its comparative design, which evaluates auricle morphology across multiple populations within a standardized environment and a relatively narrow age range. The large sample size and the use of a detailed morphological classification system enhance the robustness of the findings. In addition, this study introduces several less commonly described morphological subtypes, contributing to the existing classification framework of auricle anatomy.

However, this study has some limitations. First, the sample may be subject to selection bias, as it consists of students from a single university and may not fully represent the general population. Second, the cross-sectional design does not allow for evaluation of temporal changes or environmental influences. Despite these limitations, the findings provide a valuable reference dataset for anthropological and forensic applications.
