Continuous metabolic syndrome score and cardiometabolic multimorbidity risk in three nationwide cohorts
Authors: Wei Zhang, Guangsheng Zhai, Xinshu Wu, Cui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the prospective association between the continuous metabolic syndrome score (cMetS) and incident cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) across diverse populations. This prospective multicohort study included 7,114 participants from China (CHARLS), 3,400 from the United States (HRS), and 7,380 from the United Kingdom (ELSA), all aged ≥ 45 years and free of CMM at baseline. cMetS was derived from waist circumference, triglycerides, HDL-C, mean arterial pressure, and fasting glucose. Associations were assessed using Cox models, restricted cubic splines, and pooled random-effects meta-analysis. Over a median follow-up of 7, 4, and 6 years in CHARLS, HRS, and ELSA, 574, 170, and 317 incident CMM cases were documented, respectively. After full adjustment, the highest (vs. lowest) cMetS tertile was associated with significantly increased CMM risk (HR = 4.27 in CHARLS, 1.77 in HRS, 1.54 in ELSA). The pooled HR per 1-SD increase in cMetS was 1.35 (95% CI 1.17–1.56). Dose–response relationships were nonlinear in CHARLS but linear in HRS and ELSA. Associations were robust in subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Higher cMetS is consistently associated with increased CMM risk among middle-aged and older adults in three nations, suggesting its potential value for risk stratification, pending further validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-63590-0.Keywords: Continuous metabolic syndrome score, Cardiometabolic multimorbidity, Multicountry, Prospective cohort, Risk predictionSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cardiology, Diseases, Endocrinology, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

The global population is aging amidst a rising epidemic of chronic non-communicable diseases1. A key challenge is cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM). CMM refers to the coexistence of two or more cardiometabolic conditions including type 2 diabetes, stroke, and heart disease2. It results in synergistic health deterioration, reduced quality of life, and increased healthcare costs. The existence of CMM increases the risk of death more than that of a single illness3,4. It is important to develop effective strategies for the early identification of high-risk population for prevention.

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of interrelated abnormalities, including abdominal obesity, elevated blood pressure, impaired fasting glucose, and dyslipidemia5. The traditional means of diagnosing MetS is dichotomous, usually whether present or absent. The traditional dichotomous definition may overlook individuals with subtle, subclinical dysfunction6. The continuous metabolic syndrome score (cMetS) goes one step further. Rather than treating the core MetS components separately, cMetS combines them into a single quantitative variable providing a more detailed measure of the extent of overall metabolic derangement7. The cMetS preserves the full gradient of metabolic dysfunction, whereas binary MetS collapses all positive individuals into a single category, discarding information on the degree of abnormality8. For example, an individual with five components just above the threshold is indistinguishable from another with five components far above the threshold under the binary definition, yet their prognoses may differ substantially6. Furthermore, cMetS can capture risk in individuals who do not meet the full binary criteria. Thus, cMetS provides a more nuanced risk stratification by reflecting the cumulative burden of metabolic abnormalities rather than a simple presence/absence classification6.

Research shows that cMetS is superior to the binary MetS in predicting incident cardiovascular disease (CVD) and type 2 diabetes9. While earlier studies have linked cMetS to single disease endpoints, its association with CMM risk remains poorly understood. The generalizability of this association across diverse cohorts, especially those from different genetic, lifestyle, and healthcare contexts, remains to be established. To address these research gaps, a prospective analysis was conducted using three nationally representative longitudinal cohorts of middle-aged and older adults: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), the US Health and Retirement Study (HRS), and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA). This multicountry design enables a thorough investigation of the cMetS-CMM relationship across different contextual settings. The objectives of this study were to evaluate the prospective association of cMetS with incident CMM in each cohort, to perform pooled meta-analysis, to assess the dose–response relationship, to evaluate the consistency of the association in key subgroups and to assess the robustness of the findings in sensitivity analyses.

Methods

Study populations

This multicohort study recruited participants from three prospective cohort studies, namely CHARLS, HRS and ELSA: CHARLS is a nationally representative survey of Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years, for which Wave 1 (2011–2012) was used as the baseline survey in the present study, and data from the subsequent Wave 2 (2013) to Wave 4 (2018) were utilized to identify incident cases of CMM; HRS is a nationally representative longitudinal study of Americans aged over 50 years, with Wave 13 (2016) data serving as the baseline in this study and follow-up conducted via Wave 14 (2018) and Wave 15 (2020); ELSA is a longitudinal study of representative English individuals aged ≥ 50 years, for which Wave 6 (2016) was adopted as the baseline in the current study, with follow-up covering Wave 7 (2018), Wave 8 (2020) and Wave 9 (2022).

Eligible participants were aged at least 45 years at the baseline, had complete information available for cMetS calculation, and were free of CMM at baseline. Individuals with no available follow-up data were excluded from the study. Figure 1 shows the detailed selection process. The final analytical samples comprised 7,114, 3,400 and 7,380 CHARLS, HRS and ELSA participants, respectively. All methods in this study involving human participants were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the ethical standards laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki. The CHARLS was approved by the Peking University Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-11,015). The ELSA was approved by the London Multicentre Research Ethics Committee (MREC/01/2/91). The HRS was reviewed and approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Assessment of cMetS

The cMetS was calculated using a standardized Z-score method, consistent with prior studies10. Z-scores were first computed for the five components: waist circumference (WC), triglycerides (TG), mean arterial pressure (MAP), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), and the inverse of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). Since HDL-C exerts a protective effect, its Z-score was multiplied by -1 to align its directional trend with that of the other components. The cMetS was calculated by adding all five z-scores. This method follows the well‑established Z‑score summation approach described in previous studies8,10. A higher cMetS indicates a higher degree of metabolic dysfunction. For categorical analysis, participants were grouped into cohort-specific cMetS tertiles (Tertile 1 to Tertile 3).

Ascertainment of CMM

The outcome was incident CMM, defined as the first recorded diagnosis of two or more of the following three conditions during follow-up: diabetes, heart disease, or stroke. To ensure consistent ascertainment of diabetes, heart disease, and stroke across CHARLS, HRS, and ELSA, we followed the harmonization framework established by the RAND (Research ANd Development) Corporation Center for the Study of Aging11. For each condition, we used a broad disease category rather than specific sub‑types to maximize cross‑cohort comparability12.

Diabetes was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: (1) self‑reported physician diagnosis; (2) use of antidiabetic medication (oral hypoglycemics or insulin); (3) fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; or (4) glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%. Heart disease was defined as either a self‑reported physician diagnosis of coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, or heart failure, or the use of cardiac medications. Stroke was defined as self‑reported physician diagnosis of stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic) or use of stroke medications. Medication use was harmonized using general treatment variables rather than specific drug classes, consistent with the RAND harmonization approach.

Covariates

The covariates included age, sex, residence, education level, marital status. Educational level was classified into three groups: middle school or below, high school or vocational school, and college or above. Smoking status was classified as current smoker and non-smoker. Similarly, drinking status was classified as current drinker and non-drinker. Clinical measures included body mass index (BMI); dyslipidemia (based on self-report); and C-reactive protein (CRP).

Handling of missing variables

The distribution of missing data across the study variables is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. In the primary analysis, missing covariate data (residence, education, marital status, smoking, drinking, BMI, dyslipidemia, and CRP) were handled using multiple imputation (MI). This methodology was selected to avoid reductions in sample size and generate more robust statistical inferences12. Specifically, we used the mice package in R. We generated 20 imputed datasets with 50 iterations each. Predictive mean matching (PMM) was used for continuous variables; logistic regression for binary variables; and polytomous logistic regression for categorical variables with > 2 levels. Convergence was assessed by visual inspection of trace plots; no non‑convergence was observed. Cox proportional hazards models were then fitted to each imputed dataset, and the estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rules (mice::pool). For the sensitivity analysis assessing missing exposure data, we applied the same MI procedure to the five cMetS components (WC, TG, MAP, FPG, and HDL‑C).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized by cMetS tertiles. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard error; categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Differences across tertiles were tested using analysis of variance for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Time-to-event analyses were conducted with Cox proportional hazards regression models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the cMetS-incident CMM association. Follow-up time was calculated from the baseline interview to the first diagnosis of CMM, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period, whichever occurred first. Three models were fitted: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 additionally adjusted for smoking status, drinking status, and BMI; Model 3 further adjusted for residence, educational level, marital status, dyslipidemia, and CRP. Nonlinear relationships were explored using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with 4 knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the cMetS distribution, as implemented by the rcsplot function from the plotRCS package.

Subgroup analyses were performed using stratification of the fully adjusted model (Model 3) according to age (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years), sex, smoking status, drinking status, and dyslipidemia. The Wald test was used to assess interactions between cMetS and each subgroup variable by examining the significance of the interaction term in the Cox proportional hazards model. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to test robustness: (1) redefining CMM as the presence of ≥ 1 (instead of ≥ 2) of the three cardiometabolic component diseases (heart disease, stroke, and diabetes); (2) further adjusting Model 3 for the use of lipid-lowering therapy; (3) performing a complete-case analysis excluding participants with any missing covariate data; (4) performing multiple imputation for the five cMetS components as a sensitivity analysis (see Handling of missing variables for details); (5) performing inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) to address potential selection bias due to missing CMM follow‑up in CHARLS and HRS (ELSA had no missing follow‑up). A random-effects meta-analysis was used to pool the cohort-specific HRs per 1-standard deviation (SD) increase in cMetS and by cMetS tertiles, with heterogeneity assessed using the I2 statistic. To evaluate the incremental predictive value of cMetS relative to the conventional binary MetS definition, we compared three models: (1) the basic model (covariate‑adjusted base model), (2) the basic model plus binary MetS, and (3) the basic model plus cMetS. Model performance was assessed using the C‑index, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI)13. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The proportional hazards assumption was tested for each Cox model using Schoenfeld residuals. A global test P value > 0.05 was considered to indicate no violation. All models satisfied the assumption (global P > 0.05; Supplementary Table S1).

Participants in the highest cMetS tertile consistently exhibited higher BMI values across all cohorts compared to the lowest tertile (all P < 0.001). Similarly, CRP level and the prevalence of dyslipidemia increased significantly with rising cMetS (all P < 0.001). Notably, in CHARLS, higher cMetS was associated with a lower proportion of rural residents (Tertile 3: 81.5% vs. Tertile 1: 88.9%, P < 0.001) and higher education level (college or above: Tertile 3: 3.2% vs. Tertile 1: 2.1%, P = 0.002). A lower percentage of current drinkers was observed in the highest cMetS tertile (Tertile 3: 37.4% vs. Tertile 1: 40.9%, P = 0.006). In the HRS cohort, higher cMetS was associated with a significantly higher prevalence of current smokers (Tertile 3: 57.2% vs. Tertile 1: 50.8%, P = 0.008) and a lower proportion of participants with college or above education level (Tertile 3: 47.4% vs. Tertile 1: 58.4%, P < 0.001). A lower percentage of current drinkers was observed in the highest cMetS tertile (Tertile 3: 57.2% vs. Tertile 1: 64.5%, P < 0.001). In the ELSA cohort, the highest cMetS tertile had a significantly lower proportion of participants with college or above education (Tertile 3: 18.6% vs. Tertile 1: 22.2%, P = 0.014). A higher percentage of current smokers was observed in the highest cMetS tertile (Tertile 3: 64.5% vs. Tertile 1: 57.9%, P < 0.001).

Association between cMetS and Incident CMM

Over a median follow-up of approximately 7 years (CHARLS), 4 years (HRS), and 6 years (ELSA), a total of 574, 170, and 317 incident CMM cases were documented, respectively.

Table 2 summarizes the Cox proportional hazards regression results. In the crude model, compared to the lowest cMetS tertile, the highest tertile was associated with markedly elevated CMM risks: CHARLS HR = 5.16 (95% CI 4.00–6.65), HRS HR = 2.38 (95% CI 1.62–3.50), and ELSA HR = 2.50 (95% CI 1.86–3.37). After sequential adjustment for potential confounders, the associations remained statistically significant, though attenuated. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), which accounted for age, sex, smoking status, drinking status, BMI, education level, marital status, residence, dyslipidemia, and CRP, the hazard ratios for Tertile 3 versus Tertile 1 were 4.27 (95% CI 3.30–5.52) in CHARLS, 1.77 (95% CI 1.13–2.77) in HRS, and 1.54 (95% CI 1.09–2.17) in ELSA. A significant dose–response relationship was observed across tertiles (P value for trend < 0.001 for CHARLS, 0.029 for HRS, and 0.001 for ELSA, fully adjusted models). When cMetS was treated as a continuous variable per 1-SD increment, the fully adjusted HRs were 1.51 (95% CI 1.43–1.60) in CHARLS, 1.29 (95% CI 1.11–1.52) in HRS, and 1.16 (95% CI 1.02–1.33) in ELSA, indicating a 51%, 29%, and 16% increased risk of CMM, respectively.

Pooled meta-analysis

The results of the random-effects meta-analysis pooling the three cohorts are shown in Table 3. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), the pooled HR for CMM comparing the highest to the lowest cMetS tertile was 2.44 (95% CI 1.41–4.21, I2 = 93.1%). The pooled HR per 1-SD increase in cMetS was 1.35 (95% CI 1.17–1.56, I2 = 86.5%). Significant heterogeneity was observed, reflecting differences in effect magnitudes across cohorts. Given the high I2 values (≥ 86.5%), the pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution, and the heterogeneity underscores the influence of population-specific factors.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses are detailed in Fig. 3. In the CHARLS cohort, the positive association between cMetS and CMM risk was consistent across subgroups defined by age, sex, smoking status, drinking status, and dyslipidemia, all P < 0.001. Significant interactions were found with drinking status (P for interaction = 0.029) and dyslipidemia (P for interaction = 0.021). In ELSA, the positive association was consistently observed in the examined subgroups (female, drinker, dyslipidemia), with no statistically significant interactions detected (all P for interaction > 0.05). In HRS, the positive association was likewise consistent across the examined subgroups (age ≥ 60, sex, smoking status, drinking status, dyslipidemia), and no statistically significant interactions were observed (all P for interaction > 0.05).

Sensitivity analyses

The results remained robust in multiple sensitivity analyses. (1) When CMM was redefined as ≥ 1 cardiometabolic disease, the associations between higher cMetS and increased CMM risk persisted in all three cohorts (Supplementary Table S2). (2) Further adjustment for relevant medications (lipid-lowering agents) in Model 3 attenuated the associations slightly, particularly in HRS, but the overall positive trends remained, especially in CHARLS and ELSA (Supplementary Table S3). (3) The complete-case analysis yielded results largely consistent with the primary analysis based on multiply imputed data (Supplementary Table S4). (4) Sensitivity analysis using MI for the five cMetS components yielded results consistent in direction with the primary analysis. As shown in Supplementary Table S5, the adjusted HRs for the highest vs lowest cMetS tertile were 4.36 (3.44–5.53) in CHARLS, 2.25 (1.64–3.10) in HRS, and 1.30 (0.89–1.89) in ELSA. All point estimates remained positive, and for CHARLS and HRS the associations remained statistically significant. These findings support the robustness of our conclusions to missing exposure data.

Assessment of potential bias due to missing CMM follow‑up

As shown in Supplementary Table S6, participants excluded due to missing CMM follow‑up differed from those included in both CHARLS and HRS, confirming that exclusion was not random. In CHARLS, excluded participants were older and had higher blood pressure and glucose; in HRS, they were younger and had higher triglycerides. To adjust for potential selection bias, we performed IPCW analysis. As shown in Supplementary Table S7, the IPCW‑weighted hazard ratios were nearly identical to the unweighted estimates: in CHARLS, the IPCW‑weighted HR for the highest vs lowest cMetS tertile was 4.05 (95% CI 3.11–5.28) vs 4.27 in the unweighted analysis; in HRS, the corresponding values were 2.01 (1.25–3.25) vs 1.77 (1.13–2.77). These results confirm that potential selection bias due to missing follow‑up did not materially affect our conclusions (ELSA had no missing CMM follow‑up; see Fig. 1).

Predictive performance

The incremental predictive value of cMetS for CMM risk was evaluated by comparing three models: basic model, basic model + binary MetS, and basic model + cMetS. As shown in Supplementary Table S8, adding cMetS to the basic model improved the C‑index from 0.664 to 0.728 in CHARLS, from 0.651 to 0.670 in HRS, and from 0.738 to 0.754 in ELSA. In CHARLS, cMetS significantly improved risk reclassification (NRI = 0.167, 95% CI 0.089–0.243); in ELSA, it significantly improved integrated discrimination (IDI = 0.091, 95% CI 0.047–0.131). Direct comparison between the cMetS and binary MetS models showed that cMetS provided superior risk reclassification in CHARLS (NRI = 0.106, 95% CI 0.020–0.187) and better integrated discrimination in ELSA (IDI = 0.061, 95% CI 0.031–0.096), while in HRS no significant difference was observed (C‑index comparison P = 0.393) (likely due to smaller sample size and fewer events). Overall, the continuous score demonstrated incremental predictive value over the binary definition in two of the three cohorts.

Discussion

This multicohort study, based on three nationally representative prospective cohorts from China, the United States and the United Kingdom, provides prospective evidence for a positive association between cMetS and CMM in middle-aged and older adults. The key findings of this study are as follows: (1) higher cMetS was significantly and positively associated with an elevated hazard of incident CMM across all three cohorts; (2) this association remained consistent after pooling the three cohorts and performing a pooled meta-analysis; (3) the shape of the dose–response relationship varied—markedly nonlinear in the CHARLS cohort but linear in the HRS and ELSA cohorts; (4) consistent results were observed across most demographic and lifestyle subgroups, and multiple sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings. These findings support the utility of cMetS as a tool for identifying high-risk individuals for the development of CMM across diverse populations.

Consistency across populations: validating a core pathophysiological pathway

It is hypothesized that cMetS is involved in the key pathophysiological mechanisms underlying CMM onset.

Specifically, the components of cMetS share a common pathological foundation: insulin resistance (IR) and chronic low-grade inflammation14. Abdominal obesity initiates this cascade by releasing free fatty acids and pro-inflammatory cytokines from dysfunctional adipocytes, directly impairing endothelial function15. IR contributes to hyperglycemia by reducing glucose uptake and enhancing hepatic gluconeogenesis16. Moreover, IR escalates hypertension by retaining sodium and stimulating the sympathetic nervous system17. It also triggers atherogenic dyslipidemia by generating small, dense low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particles18 and impairing the function of HDL-C19.

Furthermore, cMetS quantifies the vicious cycle of synergistic amplification among these components—consistent with the interrelated abnormalities of MetS including abdominal obesity, elevated blood pressure, impaired fasting glucose and dyslipidemia5. Hypertensive stress on the vascular endothelium exacerbates atherosclerosis20. Meanwhile, hyperglycemia promotes the production of advanced glycation end products, which further damage the vascular system and induce excessive oxidative stress21. Endothelial dysfunction is an important link that leads to systemic atherosclerosis. Ultimately, cumulative metabolic burden leads to multisystem organ damage. Vascular endothelial dysfunction and a prothrombotic state22,23, driven by inflammatory infiltration24, deposition of oxidized LDL25, result in concurrent atherosclerosis in coronary and cerebrovascular beds, thereby contributing to heart disease and stroke26. At the same time, the endocrine pancreas is impaired, with chronic inflammation and glucolipotoxicity causing β-cell dysfunction and type 2 diabetes27. Additionally, hemodynamic disturbance and metabolic injury directly induce adverse cardiac remodeling, thus increasing the risk of heart failure28.

Therefore, a high cMetS score indicates an ongoing multisystem pathological attack, involving overlapping pathogenic pathways in the vasculature, pancreas and heart; this elevates the likelihood of simultaneous onset of these cardiometabolic diseases, which constitutes the core feature of CMM29.

Clinical implications

The clinical and public health potential of cMetS predictive value across populations is significant. These findings suggest that cMetS may be considered for risk assessment for adults aged 45 years and above. The advantage of cMetS over binary MetS lies in its ability to retain the full spectrum of metabolic risk. While binary MetS identifies individuals crossing a diagnostic threshold, cMetS quantifies the degree of deviation from the population mean, enabling detection of subclinical metabolic derangement that may still confer elevated CMM risk. This is particularly relevant for early prevention, as individuals with intermediate cMetS levels—who would be classified as ‘MetS‑negative’ under binary criteria—may benefit from targeted lifestyle interventions before overt disease develops. Analyses of predictive performance further validate this utility: the incorporation of cMetS into the baseline model significantly improved the C-index and yielded a markedly positive NRI value. The pronounced nonlinear and accelerating risk trend observed in the CHARLS cohort identifies a clear inflection point, beyond which the risk of CMM rises sharply. This finding allows for the accurate screening of high-risk individuals in this population who require priority and intensive preventive interventions—including close clinical monitoring, personalized lifestyle intervention, and early targeted metabolic management. In contrast, the linear dose–response relationships in the HRS and ELSA cohorts support the implementation of gradual, population-wide preventive strategies, such as universal health education on healthy diet and physical activity, and routine metabolic parameter screening for middle-aged and older adults. For all individuals with elevated cMetS scores regardless of cohort, aggressive lifestyle modifications—including calorie-controlled diets, regular aerobic exercise, and weight management—may be considered as the foundational targeted intervention measure.

Heterogeneity and differences

Study findings demonstrated clear consistency in the direction of the association, while substantial heterogeneity was observed in effect sizes and dose–response curve shapes. There are many factors that could contribute that are not mutually exclusive. Variances in follow-up duration, age distribution and baseline health profile are pertinent. The more established healthcare systems and aggressive management of individual risk factors (e.g., dyslipidemia, hypertension) in the US and UK may attenuate the progression from metabolic dysfunction to clinical endpoints, potentially explaining the weaker association observed in these cohorts compared to China. The organ damage caused by metabolic dysfunction can be modulated by dietary, and other lifestyle factors. The linear dose–response relationship found in HRS and ELSA may be due to the management of metabolic risk in these populations, something which may occur relatively uniformly across the spectrum. Notably, this heterogeneity does not undermine the core study finding of a consistent positive association between cMetS and CMM risk. Nevertheless, given the substantial heterogeneity observed (I2 up to 93.1%), the pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution, and direct cross-population generalizability of the absolute risk estimates may be limited. The variation in settings suggests that, regardless of the similar biological link, the absolute risk gradients and intervention threshold targets could vary according to the population settings.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the use of three large, prospective, nationally representative cohorts. Furthermore, cMetS and CMM definitions were harmonized across all cohorts, with comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were extensive and confirmed robustness. Finally, there was a formal assessment of predictive utility.

Limitations should be acknowledged. First, some disease outcomes relied on self-report, though supplemented by blood test results and medication use data. Second, another possibility is residual confounding from other unmeasured factors, such as diet quality, physical activity intensity. Third, the generalizability to younger populations or other ethnicities requires further study. Fourth, our cMetS calculation assigned equal weight to all five components, which may not reflect their true relative contributions to CMM risk; however, this simple Z‑score summation method follows a well‑established approach that has been widely validated for metabolic syndrome prediction10,30, and was chosen to maximize cross‑cohort comparability. Future studies could explore alternative weighting schemes. Finally, as this is an observational study with a prospective cohort design, definitive causal inferences cannot be drawn, although the prospective design strengthens the temporal plausibility of the observed associations.

Conclusion

This prospective multicountry cohort study demonstrates that a higher continuous metabolic syndrome score is consistently and independently associated with an increased risk of developing cardiometabolic multimorbidity among middle-aged and older adults in China, the United States, and the United Kingdom. Further external validation, calibration, and clinical-impact studies are needed before continuous metabolic syndrome score can be recommended for routine clinical risk assessment. These findings may help inform future studies evaluating whether risk-based preventive strategies improve clinical outcomes.
