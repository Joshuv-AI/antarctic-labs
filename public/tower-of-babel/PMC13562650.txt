Expression of miR-155, miR-223, miR-29b, miR-142-3p, and miR-142-5p in kidney allografts with acute tubular necrosis, rejection phenotypes, and BK-polyomavirus nephropathy
Authors: Neva Bezeljak, Nika Kojc, Živa Pipan Tkalec, Miha Arnol, Emanuela Boštjančič, Željka Večerić-Haler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpecific tissue microRNA (miRNA) profiles may reflect biologically relevant pathways of kidney allograft injury and rejection. We analyzed the intragraft expression of five preselected miRNAs (miR-29b-3p, miR-142-3p, miR-142-5p, miR-155-5p and miR-223-3p) using qPCR in biopsies from patients with antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR; n = 26), T cell–mediated rejection (TCMR; n = 14), BK polyomavirus nephropathy (BKVN; n = 15), acute tubular necrosis (ATN; n = 8), and controls (CTRL; n = 20). miR-155-5p, miR-223-3p, and miR-142-5p/3p were significantly up-regulated in ABMR (p < 0.01), TCMR (p ≤ 0.012), and BKVN (p ≤ 0.001) compared with CTRL, while miR-223-3p levels in ATN were comparable to CTRL. These immune-related miRNAs correlated with serum creatinine, proteinuria, Banff inflammation scores (t, i, ti; all p ≤ 0.001), and transcriptomic markers of rejection and tissue injury derived from molecular analysis of biopsy samples (all p < 0.01). miR-155-5p showed the strongest and most consistent association with rejection-related parameters, whereas miR-29b-3p correlated with donor characteristics and chronic fibrosis. In classification analyses, individual miRNAs displayed moderate discrimination, with miR-223-3p performing best, while a combined multi-miRNA model markedly improved separation of rejecting and non-rejecting phenotypes (AUC = 0.954 after BKVN exclusion). Intragraft miRNA expression was associated with selected inflammatory and immune-mediated features of allograft dysfunction and may provide supportive biological information alongside histopathological and molecular assessment in future studies. However, these exploratory findings require validation in larger, independent cohorts, including paired blood or urine samples, before any diagnostic or biomarker role can be established.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59410-0.Keywords: miRNA, Kidney transplantation, Antibody-mediated rejection, Acute tubular necrosis, BK polyomavirus nephropathy, T cell-mediated rejectionSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Genetics, Medical research, Molecular biology, Nephrology

Introduction

Kidney allograft dysfunction arises from diverse causes, including rejection, ischemic injury, infections, recurrence of original kidney disease, drug toxicity, and others. Early events such as ischemia-reperfusion injury can trigger immune activation and lead to progressive tissue damage, often before clinical signs become apparent1.

Currently available clinical markers of allograft injury, such as serum creatinine and proteinuria, lack sensitivity for early or subclinical dysfunction and provide limited information on the underlying cause of injury. Light microscopy remains the diagnostic cornerstone of kidney allograft pathology; however, selected biopsies remain difficult to classify, particularly when inflammatory and injury-related lesions overlap. Interstitial inflammation, tubulitis, peritubular capillaritis, and acute tubular injury may occur in rejection, viral injury, ischemia-reperfusion injury, or combinations of these processes. Therefore, there is a need for complementary molecular approaches that do not replace histopathology, but refine the biological interpretation of allograft injury. In this context, tissue-based molecular profiling can identify candidate markers that reflect intragraft inflammation, immune activation, ischemic or viral injury, and chronic remodeling, providing a rational basis for future validation in blood or urine.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as promising candidates in this context. These small, non-coding RNA molecules regulate gene expression and are involved in key biological processes, such as immunity, inflammation, and fibrosis2. Their stability, detectability in body fluids, and tissue specificity make them attractive for biomarker development3–6. However, serum or urinary miRNA levels may not fully reflect intragraft activity, highlighting the need to first validate their relevance at the tissue level.

In this study, we analyzed the intragraft expression of five miRNAs (miR-29b-3p, miR-142-3p, miR-142-5p, miR-155-5p, and miR-223-3p) in kidney transplant biopsies with well-characterized pathological diagnoses. By correlating miRNA expression with histological Banff lesions and transcriptomic rejection classifiers, we aimed to determine whether these miRNAs reflect key injury-related processes in the allograft, and whether they may serve as candidate tissue markers for future validation in minimally invasive sample types.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort

Of 83 kidney transplant recipients included in the analysis, 57 underwent indication and 26 surveillance allograft biopsies. As expected, surveillance biopsies were almost exclusively performed in the control group (95%), while biopsies in the other diagnostic groups were largely indication-based, prompted by clinical findings such as allograft dysfunction, new-onset or increasing proteinuria, or the occurrence of donor-specific antibodies (DSAs). The study cohort comprised five diagnostic groups: antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR; n = 26), T cell–mediated rejection (TCMR; n = 14), BK polyomavirus nephropathy (BKVN; n = 15), acute tubular necrosis (ATN; n = 8), and controls (CTRL; n = 20). The overall median time from transplantation to biopsy was 398 days (range 7–5519), with substantial variation across diagnostic groups. The longest interval was observed in the ABMR group (median 3609 days), and the shortest in patients with ATN (median 18.5 days).

We next examined the intragraft expression patterns of selected microRNAs and explored their associations with donor and recipient factors, clinical indicators of graft function, histopathological Banff lesion scores, and molecular rejection or injury classifiers.

Tissue miRNA expression and association with allograft pathology

We first analyzed the expression of five selected miRNAs (miR-29b-3p, miR-142-3p, miR-142-5p, miR-155-5p, and miR-223-3p) between the different study groups ABMR, ATN, BKVN, and TCMR in comparison to CTRL. No significant differences in miR-29b-3p expression were observed among the groups. In contrast, miR-142-3p, miR-142-5p, and miR-155-5p were consistently upregulated in all four pathological groups relative to CTRL. Interestingly, miR-223-3p was up-regulated in ABMR, BKVN, and TCMR, but not in ATN. In ATN, miR-223-3p expression was similar to CTRL. Results are summarized in Fig. 1.

Differential expression of selected miRNAs across diagnostic groups. (a) Expression levels of five selected miRNAs (miR-29b-3p, miR-142-3p, miR-142-5p, miR-155-5p, and miR-223-3p) were compared between antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR), acute tubular necrosis (ATN), BK polyomavirus nephropathy (BKVN), and T cell-mediated rejection (TCMR) relative to the control group (CTRL). (b) Summary table of statistically significant pairwise comparisons with corresponding p-values.

Specific miRNA profiles distinguished between rejecting (TCMR and ABMR clustered) and non-rejecting (CTRL, ATN, BKVN) pathological states. Rejecting versus non-rejecting injury phenotypes were separated by expression of miR-142-3p (p = 0.031), miR-155-5p (p = 0.014), and miR-223-3p (p < 0.001), with miR-142-5p (p = 0.066) showing borderline significance. When patients with BKVN were excluded, all investigated miRNAs except miR-29b-3p discriminated between rejecting and non-rejecting cases (p = 0.003 for miR-142-3p, p = 0.006 for miR-142-5p, p = 0.007 for miR-155-5p, and p < 0.001 for miR-223-3p). However, the examined miRNAs did not significantly distinguish ABMR from TCMR and did not reliably discriminate rejection from BKVN. This limitation is important because both TCMR and BKVN may show interstitial inflammation and tubulitis, and the observed miRNA patterns appear to reflect shared immune-inflammatory activation rather than disease-specific signatures.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis (Fig. 2) confirmed that the selected miRNAs differentiated rejection phenotypes (ABMR, TCMR) from non-rejection states (CTRL, ATN, BKVN), although they did not discriminate between specific types of rejection. Among individual markers, miR-223-3p demonstrated the highest discriminative accuracy, whereas miR-155-5p and miR-142-3p achieved moderate performance. Because BKVN did not display a distinct miRNA pattern in earlier analyses, we repeated the ROC analysis after excluding this group. Under these conditions, all tested miRNAs demonstrated stronger discrimination, and miR-142-5p additionally reached statistical significance. A combined model including all four miRNAs further enhanced diagnostic performance. When all phenotypes were analyzed together, the model yielded an AUC of 0.781 (95% CI 0.675–0.887), while exclusion of BKVN resulted in markedly improved discrimination (AUC 0.954; 95% CI 0.907–1.000).

Correlations of tissue miRNA expression and recipient-related clinical parameters

We next assessed the intragraft expression levels of selected miRNAs and investigated their associations with established clinical indicators of allograft pathology (e.g., kidney function, proteinuria).

Most notably, miR-142-5p (p < 0.001), miR-142-3p (p < 0.001), miR-155-5p (p < 0.001), and miR-223-3p (p = 0.027) positively correlated with serum creatinine, and negatively with eGFR (p < 0.001 for miR-142-5p, p < 0.001 for miR-142-3p, p < 0.001 for miR-155-5p, p = 0.035 for miR-223-3p). The same miRNAs also positively correlated with proteinuria (p < 0.001 for miR-142-5p, p < 0.001 for miR-142-3p, p < 0.001 for miR-155-5p, p = 0.002 for miR-223-3p). In contrast, miR-29b-3p showed no significant association with allograft function or proteinuria at the time of kidney graft biopsy (p = 0.299 for serum creatinine, p = 0.522 for eGFR, p = 0.180 for proteinuria). Correlations of tissue miRNA expression and donor-related clinical parameters

Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant associations between donor and recipient parameters and the expression levels of selected intragraft miRNAs.

miR-29b-3p is significantly associated with donor type and some anthropometric features. Namely, miR-29b-3p expression was significantly higher in kidney allografts from DBD donors (rs = − 0.386, p = 0.002), and, inversely, lower in those from living donors. In addition, miR-29b-3p correlated positively with female donor sex (rs = − 0.293, p = 0.022) and inversely with donor height (rs= 0.359, p = 0.004). However, these associations should be interpreted as exploratory and may reflect donor-size or donor-type related differences in nephron mass, ischemic exposure, or chronic remodeling rather than a direct sex- or height-specific biological effect.

miR-155-5p expression is inversely associated with donor quality indices. A consistent negative correlation was observed between miR-155-5p and several indicators of donor risk, including donor age (rs= 0.332, p = 0.005), expanded criteria donor status (rs= 0.255, p = 0.034), KDRI (rs = 0.309, p = 0.011), and KDPI (rs= 0.327, p = 0.007).

miR-223-3p correlates with pre-transplant dialysis duration. A weak but statistically significant negative correlation was detected between miR-223-3p and duration of dialysis prior to transplantation (rs= 0.241, p = 0.048).

miR-142 family shows minimal association with donor or recipient parameters. Both miR-142-5p and miR-142-3p showed weak negative correlations with recipient age and donor sex, though these did not reach statistical significance.

Expression of miR-142-5p showed significant correlations with several histopathological features of kidney allograft biopsies. Higher miR-142-5p levels were associated with tubulitis (t, p = 0.001), interstitial inflammation (i, p < 0.001), and total inflammation (ti, p < 0.001). A weaker, but still significant, positive correlation was observed with tubular atrophy (ct, p = 0.029). No significant associations were found with peritubular capillaritis (ptc), vascular lesions (v, cv), C4d deposition, or electron microscopy findings of basement membrane multilayering (BMML-EM).

Expression of miR-142-3p was moderately associated with histological markers of intragraft inflammation. Higher levels of miR-142-3p correlated positively with tubulitis (t; p < 0.001), interstitial inflammation (i; p < 0.001), and total inflammation (ti; p < 0.001). A weaker positive correlation was also observed with peritubular capillaritis (ptc; p = 0.046). No significant correlations were found with vascular lesions or transplant glomerulopathy. Among chronic changes, miR-142-3p showed no clear association with chronic interstitial or tubular atrophy scores.

Expression of miR-29b-3p did not show significant correlations with most histopathological parameters. A modest negative correlation was detected with interstitial inflammation (i; p = 0.025), whereas correlations with other parameters were not significant.

Expression of miR-155-5p was moderately to strongly associated with active inflammatory lesions in kidney allografts. Higher levels of miR-155-5p correlated with tubulitis (t; p < 0.001), interstitial inflammation (i; p < 0.001), and total inflammation (ti; p < 0.001). It also correlated with Polyomavirus load score, but correlation was not statistically significant (pvl; p = 0.106). No significant correlation was observed with peritubular capillaritis (ptc; p = 0.059) or vascular changes. Weaker or non-significant associations were observed for chronic histopathological lesions, including interstitial fibrosis (ci; p = 0.083) and tubular atrophy (ct; p = 0.073) and i-IFTA (p = 0.031).

Expression of miR-223-3p was correlated with several histopathological indicators of intragraft injury. Higher levels of miR-223-3p correlated with tubulitis (t; p = 0.003), interstitial inflammation (i; p = 0.001), total inflammation (ti; p < 0.001), peritubular capillaritis (ptc; p = 0.001), chronic interstitial fibrosis (ci; p = 0.045), and tubular atrophy (ct; p = 0.037). miR-223-3p also correlated positively with Polyomavirus load score, but correlation was not statistically significant (pvl; p = 0.553), composite Banff inflammation scores (i-IFTA; p = 0.018), and BMML-EM (p = 0.017).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the expression of five miRNAs (miR-29b-3p, miR-142-3p, miR-142-5p, miR-155-5p, and miR-223-3p) in kidney allograft biopsies from patients with distinct injury phenotypes (ABMR, TCMR, ATN, and BKVN) in comparison with control biopsies. We aimed to evaluate their potential as tissue biomarkers of alloimmune injury and to explore their associations with histopathological and molecular features of kidney allograft pathology.

Tissue expression of miR-142-5p, miR-142-3p, miR-155-5p, and miR-223-3p correlated with kidney dysfunction (higher serum creatinine, lower eGFR, increased proteinuria). Among these, miR-155-5p showed the most consistent associations with both functional and histopatological injury. Donor and recipient characteristics also influenced miRNA expression. miR-29b-3p levels were higher in DBD donor kidneys and associated with female sex and lower donor height. Because these variables may be interrelated with donor type, kidney size, ischemic injury, and nephron mass, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of a direct anthropometric effect. Conversely, miR-155-5p showed inverse associations with donor-related risk factors such as older age, ECD status, and higher KDRI/KDPI, possibly suggesting altered immune responsiveness in marginal grafts. Together, these observations show that donor and recipient factors may influence intragraft miRNA profiles and should be considered in biomarker development.

Having established the influence of donor and recipient characteristics, we next examined the role of individual miRNAs with established relevance in kidney transplantation. miR-223 was among the first miRNAs linked to kidney allograft rejection. Anglicheau et al.7 reported its upregulation in biopsies and peripheral blood mononuclear cells during acute rejection, and subsequent studies confirmed its ability to distinguish immune- from non-immune-mediated injury. More recently, Nagy et al.8 demonstrated elevated plasma miR-223 together with miR-155 and miR-21 in biopsy-proven ABMR, while the BIOMARGIN study showed consistent upregulation of miR-223-3p in biopsies with microvascular inflammation, correlating with rejection severity9. In our cohort, miR-223-3p was significantly upregulated in ABMR, BKVN, and TCMR, but not in ATN, indicating a closer link to immune-mediated rather than ischemic injury and a potential role in differentiating ABMR from ATN in sensitized patients with delayed graft function10. We observed associations with several Banff lesion scores (t, i, ti, ptc, g, ci, ct) and with MMDx classifiers, particularly peritubular capillaritis and transplant glomerulopathy probabilities. Our findings suggest that miR-223-3p may reflect intragraft inflammatory and microvascular processes and could contribute supplementary information for distinguishing rejection from ischemic injury. This was further supported by ROC analysis, where miR-223-3p showed the strongest individual discriminatory capacity among the tested miRNAs.

miR-155 is a key regulator of immune responses, modulating T-cell activation, cytokine production, and macrophage polarization. In line with previous reports of its upregulation in blood, urine, and biopsy samples during acute rejection8,10–14, we observed strong upregulation of miR-155-5p across all diagnostic groups compared to controls. In our cohort, tissue expression correlated with inflammatory lesions (t, i, ti), Banff chronic indices (ci, ct, i-IFTA), and MMDx-derived classifiers, including the rejection score, TCMR and ABMR scores, and probabilistic ptc, i, and t scores. These findings support miR-155-5p as a marker of immune activation, although its ability to separate rejection from other causes of graft dysfunction was only moderate. This suggests that miR-155-5p primarily reflects general immune activation rather than phenotype-specific injury.

miR-142-3p and miR-142-5p, derived from the same precursor, have both been linked to alloimmune injury. Previous studies associated miR-142-3p with acute rejection and chronic injury, including IF/TA15–18, while miR-142-5p was reported as upregulated in chronic ABMR and capable of distinguishing rejecting from non-rejecting biopsies7,19. In our cohort, both isoforms were elevated across pathological groups compared with controls. miR-142-3p correlated mainly with inflammatory lesions (t, i, ti), suggesting involvement in acute alloimmune activation, whereas miR-142-5p additionally showed associations with chronic indices (ct) and broad MMDx-derived injury scores, pointing to roles in both acute and chronic processes. In terms of diagnostic performance, miR-142-3p demonstrated only moderate ability to separate rejection from non-rejection states, while miR-142-5p contributed to the performance of the combined model.

The miR-29 family is well known for its antifibrotic and antiinflammatory effects achieved through suppression of extracellular matrix genes and TGF-β signaling20,21. In transplantation, reduced miR-29b has been associated with chronic dysfunction and ABMR7, while increased expression in ATN may indicate tissue repair responses22. In our cohort, miR-29b-3p showed no significant differences between diagnostic groups and no consistent associations with graft function or rejection scores, aside from a modest correlation with interstitial inflammation. Beforementioned donor-related associations further support the view that miR-29b-3p may reflect donor- or tissue-related factors rather than acute rejection, but this interpretation remains exploratory and requires validation.

An important aspect of our study was the integration of miRNA profiling with MMDx-derived transcriptomic classifiers. We observed that miR-142-3p, miR-142-5p, miR-155-5p, and miR-223-3p were consistently associated with injury-related scores, with the strongest links to rejection and inflammation, consistent with their known immunological functions. In contrast, miR-29b-3p did not correlate with MMDx classifiers, further distinguishing its role from rejection-related miRNAs. Of note, miR-223-3p emerged as particularly relevant, because it was not elevated in ATN but was consistently upregulated in rejection phenotypes, suggesting potential value in differentiating immune-mediated injury from ischemic acute tubular injury in selected clinical contexts, such as delayed graft function in sensitized recipients. Importantly, when evaluated in combination, these miRNAs substantially improved discrimination between rejecting and non-rejecting phenotypes, suggesting the value of multiplex miRNA models as a possible complement to histopathology and transcriptomic assessment. At the same time, our data highlight that BKVN represents an autonomous disease landscape: neither individual host-derived miRNAs nor current MMDx approaches could reliably differentiate BKVN from rejection, reflecting the obvious biological overlap between viral injury and alloimmune activation.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, this was a historic single-center cohort with a relatively small number of patients, which may limit statistical power and generalizability. Second, we evaluated only tissue miRNA expression, which requires invasive biopsies and does not directly validate the use of these miRNAs as non-invasive biomarkers. Paired serum and urine analyses will therefore be required to determine whether the tissue-level findings can be translated into clinically useful minimally invasive assays. Third, due to the observational and cross-sectional design, as well as the type of statistical analyses performed, causal relationships between miRNA expression, rejection episodes, and chronic injury progression cannot be inferred. Fourth, diagnostically challenging phenotypes such as borderline lesions, mixed rejection, and biopsies with simultaneous rejection and BKVN were not included as predefined groups. Their exclusion allowed clearer comparison of major diagnostic categories, but it limits conclusions regarding the added diagnostic value of miRNA profiling in precisely those settings where histopathology is most challenging. Fifth, no functional experiments or longitudinal pre-/post-treatment biopsies were performed, therefore, the results should be interpreted as associative and hypothesis-generating.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that distinct intragraft miRNA profiles reflect underlying immune activation and tissue injury. miR-155-5p, miR-142-3p, miR-142-5p, and miR-223-3p emerged as indicators of rejection and inflammation, whereas miR-29b-3p appeared more closely linked to donor characteristics and chronic remodeling. Notably, miR-223-3p was not elevated in ATN but was consistently upregulated in rejection phenotypes, suggesting potential utility in settings where ischemic and immune-mediated injury are difficult to distinguish. Moreover, the combined use of multiple miRNAs substantially improved discrimination between rejecting and non-rejecting phenotypes, underscoring the added value of multiplex approaches. Integration of miRNA profiling with histopathological and molecular assessment may, after further validation, provide complementary biological information and aid in clinical decision-making However, the miRNA panel did not reliably distinguish ABMR from TCMR or rejection from BKVN. These data support the biological relevance of intragraft miRNA profiling as a complementary research tool for understanding allograft pathology, while its diagnostic and non-invasive biomarker utility requires validation in larger, prospective cohorts with challenging phenotypes and paired blood or urine samples.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and histopathological evaluation

This observational historic cohort study was conducted at the Centre for Kidney Transplantation at the Department of Nephrology, University Medical Centre Ljubljana, in collaboration with the Institute of Pathology, Faculty of Medicine, University of Ljubljana. The study was approved by the National Medical Ethics Committee of the Republic of Slovenia on March 16, 2021 (approval No. 0120 − 91/2021/7), and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Eligible participants were kidney transplant recipients who had undergone an indication or surveillance allograft biopsy between 2013 and 2023, with available formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks and complete clinical and laboratory data documented in the institutional electronic medical record system. Biopsies were identified from the central renal pathology archive of the Institute of Pathology. Cases with overlapping features (e.g., mixed rejection, recurrent glomerulonephritis) or insufficient clinicopathological data were excluded. All patients provided informed consent at the time of biopsy authorizing the use of their kidney tissue for molecular analyses and permitting the use of anonymized clinical information for research and scientific publication.

Histopathological evaluation followed Banff 2019 classification criteria23. Individual Banff lesion scores were semi-quantitatively assessed, and histopathological diagnoses were established by consensus of renal pathologists and nephrologists at joint clinicopathological case reviews. Lesion scoring included standardized grading of inflammatory, vascular, and chronicity parameters, with C4d immunohistochemistry performed in all cases. Polyomavirus BK load (BK load) was assessed immunohistochemically.

Inclusion criteria for the five predefined diagnostic entities comprise a rigorous, multi-step approach: (i) comprehensive evaluation of histopathological parameters according to the Banff 2019 classification, (ii) integration of clinical and pathological findings to ensure diagnostic consistency, and (iii) thorough follow-up by attending nephrologists to monitor therapeutic response, which was used as an additional measure to validate the accuracy and reproducibility of histopathological diagnoses. From 2020 onward, when tissue quality and volume permitted, molecular profiling with the Molecular Microscope Diagnostic System (MMDx; One Lambda, Thermo Fisher) was performed to complement histopathological classification.

In total, 83 kidney transplant recipients fulfilled the inclusion criteria and were categorized into five diagnostic entities:

Antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR; n = 26): defined by microvascular inflammation, C4d positivity, and/or endothelial antibody interaction, and the presence of donor-specific antibodies (DSAs).

T cell–mediated rejection (TCMR; n = 14) based on the extent of interstitial inflammation and tubulitis, with or without vascular involvement, in the absence of DSAs.

BK polyomavirus nephropathy (BKVN; n = 15) diagnosed by viral cytopathic changes in tubular epithelial cells, positive SV40 immunohistochemistry, detectable polyomavirus BK viremia, and absence of rejection features.

Acute tubular necrosis (ATN; n = 8) characterized by acute tubular injury without histopathological or molecular signs of rejection or viral infection.

Controls (CTRL; n = 20) biopsies from recipients with stable allograft function within at least 3 months before biopsy and no significant histopathological abnormalities.

Banff lesion definitions and thresholds for each diagnostic category (ABMR, TCMR, BKVN, ATN, and controls) are summarized in Supplementary Table 123.

Clinical and laboratory data collection

Clinical, laboratory, immunological, and donor-related data were collected from the institutional electronic medical records. For each patient, the following variables were recorded at the time of biopsy: time since transplantation, cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD), serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, mL/min/1.73 m²) calculated using the CKD-EPI formula, estimated protein excretion rate (ePER, g/day), presence and class of donor-specific antibodies (DSAs), and type of biopsy (indication vs. surveillance). Data on induction and maintenance immunosuppressive therapy were also extracted. Donor characteristics included donor type (DBD – donation after brain death, or living donor), donor age, sex, body mass index (BMI), expanded criteria donor (ECD) status, Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI), Kidney Donor Risk Index (KDRI), the number of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) mismatches, and ischemia times. In patients with BKVN, plasma BK viremia and peak BK viral load within three months were additionally recorded.

Molecular profiling using the molecular microscope diagnostic system (MMDx)

In a subset of biopsies (n = 60), molecular diagnostics were performed using the Molecular Microscope Diagnostic System (MMDx; One Lambda, Thermo Fisher Scientific). MMDx was applied when biopsy tissue volume permitted safe allocation of a portion of the core for transcriptomic analysis. The platform combines microarray-based gene expression profiling with machine learning algorithms trained on a large, annotated reference set of kidney allograft biopsies24.

Total RNA was extracted from FFPE or fresh biopsy tissue, hybridized to the Affymetrix PrimeView array, and analyzed centrally at the Alberta Transplant Applied Genomics Centre (University of Alberta, Canada). The resulting output includes quantitative scores for global injury, parenchymal scarring, T cell–mediated and antibody-mediated rejection, as well as probabilistic classifiers for archetypal histopathological lesions and composite diagnostic categories24.

For statistical analysis, we extracted the following parameters from the MMDx report listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Selection of candidate miRNAs

A panel of five candidate miRNAs was selected using a targeted, hypothesis-driven strategy based on published evidence, biological plausibility, and our previous studies in kidney transplantation. In our earlier serum-based study, miR-155 and miR-223 were upregulated in patients with ABMR compared with controls, although their expression was not clearly associated with graft function. Furthermore, our previous tissue miRNA expression profiling study identified differential miRNA patterns across ATN, rejection phenotypes, and BKVN, including the five miRNAs selected for further validation in the present study12,25,26. The chosen molecules (miR-155-5p, miR-223-3p, miR-142-3p, miR-142-5p, and miR-29b-3p) represent key regulators of both immune and fibrotic pathways relevant to allograft injury.

Their biological roles have been described as follows:

miR-155-5p, which plays a central role in adaptive immune responses, particularly in T cell differentiation, and has been implicated in both acute rejection and chronic allograft dysfunction through its proinflammatory and profibrotic activity4,7,27,28.

miR-223-3p, which modulates innate immune responses and has been associated with protection against excessive inflammation and promotion of graft tolerance29.

miR-142-3p and miR-142-5p, both highly expressed in hematopoietic cells, involved in immune cell differentiation and regulation of T cell–mediated responses, with relevance to rejection, interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy (IFTA), and acute tubular injury19,30.

miR-29b-3p, a member of the miR-29 family, involved in the regulation of extracellular matrix turnover and fibrogenesis, processes central to chronic graft injury and interstitial scarring31.

Analysis of miRNA tissue expression

Isolation of RNA

Tissue samples were cut into 10-µm-thick samples from FFPE tissue blocks. For the isolation procedure, three to four 10-µm-thick sections were used. Total RNA isolation was performed using an AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 1 mL of Xylene (Merck, Kenloworth, NJ, USA) was added for deparaffinization and RNA was eluted in 30 µL of nuclease-free water. The concentration was measured using NanoDrop-1000 and Qubit by High-sensitivity RNA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Reverse transcription into cDNA

Using the miRCURY LNA RT Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), reverse transcription (RT) was subsequently conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions, including the addition of the spike-in UniSp6 (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) to verify RT technical success. Reaction was performed in a 10 µl reaction master mix containing 10 ng of total RNA. 2 µl RT buffer (2X) and 1 µl of enzyme reverse transcriptase. The reaction was run at 42 °C for 60 min, 85 °C for 5 min and 4 °C forever.

Selection of reference genes

The set of reference genes (RGs) for normalizing miRNA expression in renal tissue samples was selected from the previously published miRNA screening26. Briefly, we analyzed the expression of RGs based on two criteria: standard deviations between all sample pools less than 0.5 and no statistically significant differences in expression between the independent groups. Three RG miRNAs with the smallest standard deviation between pools, namely let-7e-3p, miR-19b-3p, and miR-20a-5p, were chosen based on above criteria.

Quantification of the selected miRNAs, RGs, and control miRNAs

The cDNA obtained from RNA isolated from tissue samples was diluted 60-fold, and 3 µl was used in 10 µl qPCR reaction master mix, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (5 µl miRCURY LNA miRNA SybrGreen 2X, 1 µl miRCURY LNA miRNA PCR Assays 10X, 1 µl ddH2O). All qPCR reactions were performed in duplicate on the QuantStudio 7Pro (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Foster City, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The signal was collected at the endpoint of every cycle. To ascertain the specificity of the qPCR products, melting curve analysis was conducted using a ramping rate of 0.075 °C/1s in the 60–95 °C range. The primers used for quantification of five selected miRNAs (miR-29b-3p, miR-142-3p, miR-142-5p, miR-155-5p, and miR-223-3p) and the RGs used are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. Expression was normalized to the geometric mean of three RG miRNAs: let-7e-3p, miR-19b-3p, and miR-20a-5p. Prior to qPCR, the amplification efficiency for each analyzed miRNA, including RGs, was determined using pool of RNA of each analyzed group of patients: ABMR, ATN, BKVN, CTRL, and TCMR. Pools of RNAs were reverse transcribed, and after their 4-fold dilutions, each dilution was used in a triplicate qPCR reaction.

Statistical analysis

ΔCq was calculated to quantify the expression of individual miRNA gene relative to the geometric expression mean of RGs32. The Mann-Whitney test was employed to analyze differences in ΔCq and in clinical and histologic characteristics. The Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (Spearman’s rho) was used for all correlations, including calculation of statistical power and estimation of needed sample size. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis included the most promising miRNAs. For the combined ROC model, predicted probabilities were generated using binary logistic regression including miR-142-3p, miR-142-5p, miR-155-5p, and miR-223-3p as predictors. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS analytical software (IBM SPSS statistics, version 27.0, Armonk, NY, USA) with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
