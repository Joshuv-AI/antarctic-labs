Bridging classical and martingale Schrödinger bridges
Julio Backhoff, Mathias Beiglböck, Giorgia Bifronte, Armand Ley

Abstract
. We investigate the martingale Schrödinger bridge, recently introduced by Nutz and Wiesel as a distinguished martingale transport plan between two probability measures in convex order. We show that this construction extends naturally to arbitrary dimension and admits several equivalent characterizations. In particular, we identify its continuous-time counterpart as the continuous martingale with prescribed marginals that minimizes a weighted quadratic energy measuring the deviation from Brownian motion. In the irreducible case, we prove that this continuous martingale Schrödinger bridge coincides with the Föllmer martingale, that is, with the Doob martingale associated to a suitable Föllmer process. More generally, we relate the martingale Schrödinger bridge to a variational problem over base measures and to the dual formulation of the corresponding weak optimal transport problem, thereby clarifying its connection with the classical Schrödinger bridge. Keywords: entropic transport, martingale transport, Schrödinger problem, Schrödinger bridge, Föllmer process, Gibbs density, filtering Mathematics Subject Classification (2010): Primary 60G42, 60G44; Secondary 91G20.

Introduction

We consider martingale transport between probability measures

By Strassen’s theorem

Besides its intrinsic mathematical interest, this is also highly relevant for applications, for instance in mathematical finance (see e.g.

Interestingly, most known constructions are essentially limited to dimension

Our main object is the

1.1.
Overview of five main characterizations of
m^{\text{SB}}

For this overview, we restrict to the case where the entropic martingale transport problem (
P
) is finite and
(\mu,\nu)
is irreducible. In this regime, the five characterizations below are equivalent whenever the relevant dual maximizers exist. (
P
) belongs to the broader class of weak martingale transport problems, where dual attainment is a delicate problem, see
[
15
,
16
,
20

. Remarkably, Hasenbichler, Pammer, and Schrott (
[

in the irreducible case under mild regularity assumptions on the cost function which are satisfied by the entropy.
To keep the presentation concise, we suppress the precise integrability and regularity assumptions and focus only on the structural picture.

1.1.1.
Entropic MOT characterization

We begin with the discrete-time martingale Schrödinger problem

(
P
)

\displaystyle\inf_{m\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)}H(m\mid\mu\otimes\nu),

where

denotes the relative entropy, i.e. equals
\int\log\frac{dQ}{dR}\,dQ
if

and

We call the unique minimizer
m^{\text{SB}}
of this problem the martingale Schrödinger bridge.
We note that, if
H(\nu|\gamma)<+\infty
, then taking the reference measure to be
\mu.\gamma
, having first marginal
\mu
and independent Gaussian increments, changes the objective only by a constant and can therefore also be used to define

, see Lemma
4.11

1.1.2.
Gibbs form of the density through a dual WOT problem

Problem (
P
) admits a dual formulation, as already noted by Nutz and Wiesel
[
48
]
in the one-dimensional case. We rather view Problem (

) as a weak transport problem in the sense of
[

]
; see (

[

,

,

in particular the existence of optimizers is treated in

,

]
. Dual attainment (either in the Nutz-Wiesel form or in the weak dual (

implies that

is the martingale Schrödinger bridge if and only if it has a density of the form

(1.1)

\frac{dm}{d\mu\otimes\nu}(x,y)=\exp\big(\varphi(x)+\psi(y)-h(x)\cdot(y-x)\big).

This is the martingale analogue of the usual exponential structure in the classical Schrödinger problem.
Here the term
h(x)\cdot(y-x)
plays the role of a Lagrange multiplier enforcing the martingale constraint.

1.1.3.
Continuous-time minimizer

A continuous-time martingale Schrödinger bridge is obtained by minimizing a weighted quadratic energy among continuous martingales with prescribed marginals.
Consider

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}M_{0}\sim\mu,\;M_{1}\sim\nu,\\
M_{t}=M_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,dB_{s}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|\sigma_{t}-I_{d}|_{\text{HS}}^{2}}{1-t}\,dt\Big].

If

The converse direction is more interesting.
Starting from the static optimizer

Specifically, for

Mixing the family

1.1.4.
Föllmer martingale characterization

Given probability measures
\bar{\mu},\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, consider the classical dynamic Schrödinger problem

(1.2)

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{0}\sim\bar{\mu},\;X_{1}\sim\nu,X_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{s}\,ds+B_{t}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}|\beta_{t}|^{2}\,dt\Big].

Its optimizer
(X^{\text{SB}}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is called the Schrödinger bridge, or Föllmer process, between
\bar{\mu}
and
\nu
.
We will use the name
Föllmer martingale
for the associated Doob martingale

M_{t}:=\mathbb{E}[X^{\text{SB}}_{1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}].

Remarkably, every Föllmer martingale is the continuous-time martingale Schrödinger bridge between its own marginals.
Conversely, if
(\mu,\nu)
is irreducible, there exists a unique Föllmer martingale satisfying
M_{0}\sim\mu
and
M_{1}\sim\nu
.
The Föllmer martingale is a (time-inhomogenous) Markov martingale that can be seen as a deterministic transformation of a Schrödinger bridge.
Specifically, we have

M_{t}=f_{t}(X^{\text{SB}}_{t}),\,\,f_{t}(z):=\mathbb{E}[X^{\text{SB}}_{1}\mid X^{\text{SB}}_{t}=z]=z+(1-t)\,\nabla_{z}\log P_{1-t}(d\nu/d\gamma\cdot e^{\psi}),

where
\bar{f},g
are the Schrödinger potentials of the Schrödinger bridge and
P_{t}
denotes the heat semigroup.

The link between the continuous-time and discrete-time formulations is rooted in the representation of relative entropy via adapted drifts, going back to the work of Föllmer
[
29
,
30
]
. In continuous time, entropy minimization admits a stochastic control representation in terms of the Föllmer drift, whose quadratic energy coincides with relative entropy, as made explicit by Lehec
[
44
]
. This identity, further developed by Eldan, Mikulincer, and Shenfeld
[
28
,
27
]
, provides a dynamic formulation of entropy that naturally bridges to the discrete-time entropic transport problems.

1.1.5.
Föllmer coupling and the base measure via
MCov
-conjugation

The Föllmer martingale construction can also be formulated naturally at the discrete-time level.
To this end, define for
\mu,\eta,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the max-covariance functional and its entropic counterpart

(1.3)

\displaystyle\textnormal{MCov}(\mu,\eta)=\sup_{m\in\mathsf{Cpl}(\mu,\eta)}\int\bar{x}\cdot y\,dm,\quad\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\eta)=\sup_{\pi\in\mathsf{Cpl}(\eta,\nu)}\Big\{\int\bar{x}\cdot y\,d\pi-H(\pi\mid\eta\otimes\nu)\Big\}.

While the optimizer of
MCov
is precisely the classical quadratic-cost optimizer, that is, the Brenier map, the optimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{\nu}
is the classical Schrödinger coupling
\pi^{\text{SB}}
between
\eta
and
\nu
, equivalently the endpoint law of the dynamic optimizer in (
1.2
).
In particular,

(1.4)

\displaystyle m_{\pi^{\text{SB}}}:=((\bar{x},y)\mapsto(T(\bar{x}),y))_{\#}\pi^{\text{SB}},\quad T(\bar{x})=\operatorname{bary}(\pi^{\text{SB}}_{\bar{x}})

is the static martingale Schrödinger bridge between its marginals.
We call
\bar{\mu}
the base measure for the martingale Schrödinger bridge between
\mu
and
\nu
if
m_{\pi^{\text{SB}}}\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
for
\pi^{\text{SB}}
optimal for
\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\bar{\mu})
, and hence
m_{\pi^{\text{SB}}}=m^{\text{SB}}
.
Strikingly, the base measure
\bar{\mu}
can be characterized variationally through the
MCov
-conjugate of
\mathcal{E}_{\nu}
:

(VP)

\displaystyle\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\big\{\textnormal{MCov}(\mu,\eta)-\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\eta)\big\}.

Any optimizer
\eta=\bar{\mu}
yields the base measure whose Föllmer coupling with
\nu
induces the martingale Schrödinger bridge from
\mu
to
\nu
.
Thus
\bar{\mu}
appears as the optimal intermediate law through which the martingale Schrödinger problem factors into a martingale part and a classical Schrödinger part.

This variational structure becomes more transparent after lifting to the Lions space, see
[
12
,
50
,
17
]
.
Indeed, the functionals
\eta\mapsto\textnormal{MCov}(\mu,\eta)
and
\eta\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\eta)
admit liftings to functionals on
L^{2}
, and in that lifted space the above
MCov
-conjugation becomes an actual convex conjugation.
This viewpoint also motivates the first-order conditions appearing in Section
3
below.
At an optimizer
\bar{\mu}
of (
VP
), one expects (see
[
17
, Section 2]
) that the Wasserstein gradients satisfy

\textstyle\frac{\delta}{\delta\bar{\mu}}\textnormal{MCov}(\mu,\bar{\mu})=\frac{\delta}{\delta\bar{\mu}}\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\bar{\mu})

up to an additive constant.
Equivalently, for any lift
\bar{X}\sim\bar{\mu}
, the corresponding lifted gradients coincide at
\bar{X}
.
The left-hand side comes from the martingale transport part, while the right-hand side is encoded by the Schrödinger potentials of the classical bridge from
\bar{\mu}
to
\nu
.
This first-order condition is the key mechanism by which the optimal base measure
\bar{\mu}
is linked to the Gibbs structure of
m^{\text{SB}}
.

1.2.
How the five characterizations fit together

At a heuristic level, the whole structure is determined once the base measure
\bar{\mu}
is known.
Indeed, let
\bar{f},g
be the Schrödinger potentials of the classical Schrödinger bridge
\pi^{\text{SB}}
between
\bar{\mu}
and
\nu
.
Then
\pi^{\text{SB}}
is given in Gibbs form by

\pi^{\text{SB}}(d\bar{x},dy)=\bar{\mu}(d\bar{x})\pi^{\text{SB}}_{\bar{x}}(dy),\qquad\frac{d\pi^{\text{SB}}_{\bar{x}}}{d\nu}(y)=\frac{e^{\bar{x}\cdot y+\psi(y)}}{\int e^{\bar{x}\cdot z+\psi(z)}\,\nu(dz)}=e^{\bar{x}\cdot y+\psi(y)+\bar{\varphi}(\bar{x})}.

Hence its barycentric projection is explicit:

T(\bar{x}):=\operatorname{bary}(\pi^{\text{SB}}_{\bar{x}})=\int y\,\pi^{\text{SB}}_{\bar{x}}(dy)=\nabla_{\bar{x}}\log\int e^{\bar{x}\cdot z+g(z)}\,\nu(dz).

The role of the base measure is precisely that
T_{\#}\bar{\mu}=\mu
.
Once this holds, the martingale Schrödinger bridge is obtained simply by barycentric projection of the classical Schrödinger coupling,

m^{\text{SB}}=(T,\id)_{\#}\pi^{\text{SB}}.

In particular, the static Gibbs form of
m^{\text{SB}}
is just the image of the Gibbs form of
\pi^{\text{SB}}
. As we prove below
T
is invertible, which yields exactly a density of the form (
1.1
).

The resulting dictionary can be summarized by the following picture:

\bar{\mu}
\pi^{\text{SB}}
\in\mathsf{Cpl}(\bar{\mu},\nu)
\mu
m^{\text{SB}}
\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
\text{solve }SP(\bar{\mu},\nu)
T(\bar{x})=\operatorname{bary}(\pi^{\text{SB}}_{\bar{x}})
(T,\id)_{\#}
h=T^{-1}
x\mapsto m^{\text{SB}}_{x}=\pi^{\text{SB}}_{h(x)}

The continuous-time objects are then equally explicit.
The same potential
\psi
determines the Föllmer process
X^{\text{SB}}
from
\bar{\mu}
to
\nu
, and the associated Föllmer martingale is its Doob transform

M_{t}=\mathbb{E}[X^{\text{SB}}_{1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]=f_{t}(X^{\text{SB}}_{t}),\,\,f_{t}(z):=z+(1-t)\,\nabla_{z}\log P_{1-t}(d\nu/d\gamma\cdot e^{\psi}).

Thus, once
\bar{\mu}
and
g
are identified, the Föllmer coupling, the martingale Schrödinger bridge, its Gibbs density, and its continuous-time realization all follow in a rather explicit way.

1.3.
Behavior under scaling and connection to filtering

A key structural feature of the Föllmer martingale is its simple dependence on the volatility of the reference Brownian motion.
Write
m^{\text{SB}}(dx,dy)=\mu(dx)m^{\text{SB}}_{x}(dy)
for the disintegration of the (static) martingale Schrödinger bridge and let
\sigma>0
.
For each
x
, consider the Föllmer process from
\delta_{x}
to
m^{\text{SB}}_{x}
relative to the reference process
\sigma B
, and denote by
M^{\sigma}=(M_{t}^{\sigma})_{t\in[0,1]}
the corresponding Doob martingale.
Thus
M^{\sigma}
is the Föllmer martingale associated with
m^{\text{SB}}
and the reference volatility
\sigma
.
The dependence on
\sigma
becomes particularly transparent after passing to a filtering representation which is of independent interest:

Let
(X,Y)\sim m^{\text{SB}}
, let
W
be an independent Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Here
Y-X
is interpreted as a hidden (static) signal, which one tries to estimate from the observation process

R_{s}:=s(Y-X)+W_{s},\qquad s\geq 0.

The optimal estimator w.r.t. mean squared error is given by the associated filtered mean (‘filtering process’)

Z_{s}:=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X,(R_{r})_{0\leq r\leq s}],\qquad s\geq 0.

We will show in
Theorem

5.1
that via the deterministic time change
s\in[0,+\infty]\mapsto\frac{\sigma^{2}s}{1+\sigma^{2}s}\in[0,1]
, the filtering process
Z
is precisely the canonical form of the Föllmer martingale, independent of the choice of
\sigma
. Thus all Föllmer martingales associated with the same data
\mu,\nu
, but different reference volatilities, are in fact one and the same process written in different time scales.
The small-noise and large-noise regimes correspond simply to zooming into the initial and terminal layers, respectively.

1.4.
Relation to Henry-Labordère’s Schrödinger martingale

It is also instructive to compare our construction with the
Schrödinger martingale
introduced by Henry-Labordère in
[
40
]
.
The two approaches are closely related in spirit, but they solve different optimization problems and should not be identified in general.

Henry-Labordère’s starting point is a fixed prior martingale model on path space.
Given such a prior law
P^{0}
, one considers calibrated martingale laws
P
and minimizes the path-space relative entropy

\inf_{P\in\mathcal{M}_{\rm cal}}H(P\mid P^{0}),\qquad H(P\mid P^{0}):=E^{P}\!\left[\log\frac{dP}{dP^{0}}\right].

Thus the prior path law is part of the input, and the resulting Schrödinger martingale depends essentially on this choice of reference model. The change of measure w.r.t. the reference model translates into a change of drift for the volatility process. This approach has proved fruitful, for instance in the applications by Guyon
[
36
]
.

Our construction is different in a fundamental way.
The primary object in the present paper is the static martingale coupling
m^{\text{SB}}
, selected intrinsically by the endpoint optimization problem (
P
).
Only after this static step do we pass to continuous time and associate with
m^{\text{SB}}
its canonical Föllmer martingale.
Accordingly, our continuous-time martingale is canonical once
(\mu,\nu)
are fixed, whereas Henry-Labordère’s Schrödinger martingale is prior-dependent.

The two frameworks overlap only at the philosophical level of the underlying Schrödinger mechanism.
This becomes most transparent in the Dirac-start case.
If one fixes
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a terminal law
\rho
, then both approaches naturally involve a deformation of Brownian motion started from
\delta_{x}
and constrained by the terminal behavior. However Henry-Labordère minimizes an entropy functional while our approach minimizes a weighted energy. We refer to the articles
[
4
,
5
,
26
]
for recent alternative approaches and Carmona and Xu
[
21
]
for a precursor.

2.
Preliminaries and equality of values for the discrete-time problems

This work is concerned with the relation between multiple optimization problems. The main one is

(P)

\displaystyle\inf_{m\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)}H(m|\mu\otimes\nu),

which is clearly equivalent to
\inf_{m\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)}\int H(m_{x}|\nu)\mu(dx)

As we will see, this problem has a related continuous-time counterpart

(
P^{cont}

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}M_{0}\sim\mu,\,M_{1}\sim\nu,\\

On the dual side, we will encounter the problem

(D)

\displaystyle\sup_{\psi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{\int\psi d\nu+\int\sup_{h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[h\cdot x-\log\int\exp(h\cdot y+\psi(y))\nu(dy)\right]\mu(dx)\right\},

(VP)

\displaystyle\sup_{\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\bar{\mu},\nu)}\left[H(\pi|\bar{\mu}\otimes\nu)-\int\bar{x}\cdot y\,\pi(d\bar{x},dy)\right]+\textnormal{MCov}(\bar{\mu},\mu)\right\},

3.
Implications of attainment

In this section, we show that attainment of (

Assumption 3.1

Assume:

(H1)

The measure
\nu
has finite exponential moments, that is,

\displaystyle\int e^{q|y|}\,\nu(dy)<\infty\qquad\text{for all }q\in\mathbb{R}_{+};

(H2)

The measure
\nu
is not concentrated on any hyperplane.

Observe that Assumption (H2) is essentially non-restrictive and only serves to avoid artificial dimensionality. More precisely, if

is a linear subspace of

is convex non-negative and

, which yields

. This means that, for
\mu
-a.e.

. Thus

is concentrated on

. By choosing

Remark 3.2
.

Before turning to the proofs, we comment on the question of existence and uniqueness of optimizers for the problems (
P
) and (
D
).

(1)

First, observe that attainment of (
P
) is straightforward. If the value of (
P
) is

, then any martingale transport plan trivially attains it. If the value is finite, then existence of a minimizer follows from the lower semicontinuity of the entropy together with the compactness of
\Pi_{M}(\mu,\nu)
. Moreover, by strict convexity of

, the minimizer is unique.

(2)

The question of the existence of an optimizer for the problems (
D
) is more delicate and will not be addressed in this article. In an ongoing work, we develop a Sinkhorn-type algorithm and prove its convergence, which in turn ensures the existence of solutions to both the dual problem and the variational problem.

Regarding uniqueness, first note that the dual functional
\mathcal{D}
is invariant by affine shift: for all admissible
\psi

\mathcal{D}(\psi+a+b\cdot\id)=\mathcal{D}(\psi),\quad\text{for all }a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Up to this invariance, the optimizer of

is unique. This follows from the fact that
\mathcal{D}
is strictly convex up to affine shift: for all
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
admissible

\mathcal{D}((1-t)\psi_{1}+t\psi_{2})\leq(1-t)\mathcal{D}(\psi_{1})+t\mathcal{D}(\psi_{2}),\quad\text{for all }t\in]0,1[,

and equality holds if and only if

is affine. The of concavity relies on applying the Hölder inequality to the exponential term to get concavity, while the identification of the affine shift as the only degree of freedom follows from the characterization of the equality case in the Hölder inequality. Uniqueness of an optimizer for (

4.1.
A cost-preserving bijection between martingale-drifted processes and admissible martingales

Fix

We formalize and extend this observation by proving that these operations define a cost-preserving correspondence between the set of martingale drifts and the set of admissible martingales (from
\delta_{x}
to

).

Theorem 4.3
.

The map

\Phi:\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{drift}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{mart}}

\Phi(X)_{t}=\mathbb{E}\big[X_{1}\mid\sigma(\{B_{s},s\in[0,t]\})\big],\qquad t\in[0,1],

is a bijection between
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{drift}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{mart}}
.
Its inverse

is given by

\Phi^{-1}(M)_{t}=x+B_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}u_{s}ds,

where, if the dynamic of

Moreover, the costs are preserved: for all

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{mart}}\big(\Phi(X)\big)=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{drift}}(X),\qquad\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{drift}}\big(\Phi^{-1}(M)\big)=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{mart}}(M).

Remark 4.4

We write

Proof.

From drifted to martingale:

By stochastic Fubini, we have

4.2.
On the Föllmer process of Schrödinger bridges

Define

\Omega=\mathcal{C}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
, and let

4.2.1.
Schrödinger bridge problem

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
m_{x}
be a probability measure with barycentre
x
. Denote by
\mathbb{W}^{x}
the law of a standard Brownian motion started at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The continuous-time Schrödinger (Bridge) problem is, in this particular case, defined as

(SB)

\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega),{X_{0}}_{\#}\mathbb{Q}=\delta_{x},{X_{1}}_{\#}\mathbb{Q}=m_{x}}H(\mathbb{Q}|\mathbb{W}^{x}).

The following is well-known and direct to prove:

Proposition 4.7
.

Assume
H(m_{x}|\gamma_{x})<+\infty
. Then the problem (
SB
) admits a unique solution, called Schrödinger bridge from
x
to
m_{x}
and denoted
\mathbb{Q}^{x}
. Moreover,

(4.6)

\frac{d\mathbb{Q}^{x}}{d\mathbb{W}^{x}}(\omega)=\rho_{x}(\omega_{1}),\qquad\text{where }\rho_{x}:=\frac{dm_{x}}{d\gamma_{x}},

and it holds

H(\mathbb{Q}^{x}|\mathbb{R}^{x})=H(m_{x}|\gamma_{x}).

In fact the term ’Schrödinger bridge’ is more typically employed for the solution of (
SB
) when the initial distribution is not a Dirac, while in the Dirac case the terminology ’h-transform’ or ’conditioned Brownian motion’ is often preferred in the classical literature. For simplicity, we only use the former terminology.

4.2.2.
Föllmer drift of the Schrödinger brige

Given a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
absolutely continuous with respect to the standard Wiener measure
\mathbb{W}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, Föllmer proved the existence of a progressively measurable process
(u_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}^{d})
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that:

(1)

For all
t\geq 0
and
\omega\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\int_{0}^{t}|u_{s}(\omega)|^{2}ds<+\infty
;

(2)

\operatorname{Law}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X-U)=\mathbb{W}
where
U
is defined by
U_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}u_{s}\,ds
for all
t\geq 0
;

(3)

H(\mathbb{Q}\mid\mathbb{W})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}|u_{t}(\omega)|^{2}dt
.

The process
(u_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is called the
Föllmer drift
associated with
\mathbb{Q}
, while
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{Q},X)
is referred to as the
Föllmer process
of
\mathbb{Q}
(or associated to
\mathbb{Q}
).

In the following, we compute the Föllmer drift when
\mathbb{Q}
is the Schrödinger bridge from
\delta_{x}
to
m_{x}
, as well as the volatility of the associated Doob martingale. The expression of the Föllmer drift is classical in the literature on h-transforms, but see Lehec’s
[
45
, Theorem 12]
for a recent reference. An analogue computation of the volatility of the Doob martingale associated to the Föllmer drift of the Schrödinger bridge can be found in Eldan and Mikulincer’s
[
28
]
. For the sake of completeness, we provide the proof in the appendix.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
m_{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
absolutely continuous with respect to
\gamma_{x}
, and define
\rho_{x}=\frac{dm_{x}}{d\gamma_{x}}.
As in the previous subsection
\mathbb{Q}_{x}
denotes the Schrödinger bridge from
\delta_{x}
to
m_{x}.
Let
(B_{t}^{x})_{t\in[0,1]}
be the process defined by

(4.7)

B_{t}^{x}=X_{t}-x-\int_{0}^{t}u_{s}^{x}(X_{s})ds,

where

u_{s}^{x}(z):=\nabla\log(\gamma^{1-t}*\rho_{x})(z).

Then:

(1)

The tuple
X^{x}:=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{Q}^{x},(B_{t}^{x})_{t\in[0,1]},X)
belongs to
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{drift}}
and

(4.8)

\mathcal{C}_{\textrm{drift}}(X^{x}):=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}|u_{t}^{x}|^{2}dt\right)=H(\mathbb{Q}^{x}|\mathbb{W}^{x})=H(m_{x}|\gamma_{x}).

This shows that
(u_{t}^{x})_{t\geq 0}
is the Föllmer drift of
\mathbb{Q}_{x}.

(2)

It holds

\operatorname{Law}_{\mathbb{Q}^{x}}(X_{1}|X_{t}=z)=\frac{\rho_{x}(y)\phi_{1-t}(y-z)}{\int\rho_{x}(\tilde{y})\phi_{1-t}(\tilde{y}-z)d\tilde{y}}dy.

(3)

The Doob martingale

canonically associated to

, i.e.,

M_{t}^{x}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{x}}[X_{1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x}],\qquad\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x}=\sigma(\{B_{s}^{x}~;~0\leq s\leq t\}),

satisfies

(4.9)

M_{t}^{x}=X_{t}^{x}+(1-t)u_{t}^{x}(X_{t}^{x})\quad\text{and}\quad dM_{t}^{x}=\sigma_{t}^{x}(M_{t}^{x})dB_{t}^{x},

where
\sigma_{t}^{x}:=I+(1-t)\nabla^{2}\log(\gamma^{1-t}*\rho_{x}).
Moreover,

(4.10)

H(m_{x}|\gamma_{x})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|\sigma_{t}^{x}-I|^{2}}{1-t}dt\right).

Remark 4.9

Moreover, the previous inequality is an equality if and only if

Applying Lemma

Lemma 4.11

\eqref{eq:introP}+H(\nu|\gamma)=\eqref{eq:introP_G}+m_{2}(\mu).

Let

\frac{d\mu\otimes\nu}{d\mu\otimes\gamma}(x,y)=\frac{d\nu}{d\gamma}(y)

\frac{dm}{d\mu\otimes\gamma}(x,y)=\frac{dm}{d\mu\otimes\nu}(x,y)\frac{d\nu}{d\gamma}(y)

If

\displaystyle H(m|\mu\otimes\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int\log\left(\frac{dm}{d\mu\cdot\gamma}\right)+\log\left(\frac{d\mu\cdot\gamma}{\mu\otimes\gamma}\right)dm

\displaystyle=H(m|\mu\cdot\gamma)+\int x\cdot y-\frac{x^{2}}{2}dm(x,y)=H(m|\mu\cdot\gamma)+m_{2}(\mu).

5.
Behaviour under scaling and connection to filtering

We extend the discussion given in Part
1.3
of the Introduction.
Here
m\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
, we write
m(dx,dy)=\mu(dx)m_{x}(dy)
, and let
\sigma>0
.
For each
x
, consider the Föllmer process from
\delta_{x}
to
m_{x}
relative to the reference process
\sigma B
, and denote by
M^{\sigma}=(M_{t}^{\sigma})_{t\in[0,1]}
the corresponding Doob martingale.
Thus
M^{\sigma}
is the Föllmer martingale associated with the coupling
m
and the reference volatility
\sigma
.

Let
(X,Y)\sim m^{\text{SB}}
, let
W
be an independent Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Here
Y-X
represents a hidden (static) signal, which one tries to estimate from the observation process

R_{s}:=s(Y-X)+W_{s},\qquad s\geq 0.

The optimal estimator is

Z_{s}:=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X,(R_{r})_{0\leq r\leq s}],\qquad s\geq 0.

We have:

Theorem 5.1

(Scaling and filtering representation)
.

Consider the deterministic time change

\tau_{\sigma}(s):=\frac{\sigma^{2}s}{1+\sigma^{2}s},

s\geq 0.

Then
Z=(Z_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is a continuous Markov martingale, and for every
\sigma>0
,

(M^{\sigma}_{\tau_{\sigma}(s)})_{s\geq 0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(Z_{s})_{s\geq 0}.

In particular, the law of the time-changed process
(M^{\sigma}_{\tau_{\sigma}(s)})_{s\geq 0}
does not depend on
\sigma
.

Proof.

Since both constructions are fiberwise in the disintegration
m(dx,dy)=\mu(dx)m_{x}(dy)
, it suffices to prove the statement conditionally on
X=x
, that is, for the case
\mu=\delta_{x}
and terminal law
m_{x}
. The general result then follows by integrating over
x\sim\mu
.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
Y\sim m_{x}
, and let
\widetilde{X}^{\sigma}
be the Föllmer process from
\delta_{x}
to
m_{x}
relative to the reference process
\sigma B
. Its associated Doob martingale is
M_{t}^{\sigma}=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid(\widetilde{X}_{u}^{\sigma})_{0\leq u\leq t}]
. Conditionally on
Y=y
, the process
\widetilde{X}^{\sigma}
is a Brownian bridge from
x
to
y
with volatility
\sigma
. Hence, on a suitable probability space, one may write

\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\sigma}=x+t(Y-x)+\sigma\beta_{t},\qquad 0\leq t<1,

where
\beta
is a standard Brownian bridge independent of
Y
. Using the classical representation
\beta_{t}=(1-t)\widetilde{W}_{t/(1-t)}
for a Brownian motion
\widetilde{W}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this becomes

\widetilde{X}_{\tau_{\sigma}(s)}^{\sigma}=x+\tau_{\sigma}(s)(Y-x)+\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma^{2}s}\widetilde{W}_{\sigma^{2}s}.

Now define
W_{s}:=\sigma^{-1}\widetilde{W}_{\sigma^{2}s}
, so that
W
is again a Brownian motion by Brownian scaling, and set
R_{s}:=s(Y-x)+W_{s}
. Then, for every
s\geq 0
,

R_{s}=\frac{1+\sigma^{2}s}{\sigma^{2}}\bigl(\widetilde{X}_{\tau_{\sigma}(s)}^{\sigma}-x\bigr).

Conversely, if
t<1
and
s=t/(\sigma^{2}(1-t))
, then

\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\sigma}=x+\sigma^{2}(1-t)\,R_{\,t/(\sigma^{2}(1-t))}.

Thus the paths
(R_{r})_{0\leq r\leq s}
and
(\widetilde{X}_{u}^{\sigma})_{0\leq u\leq\tau_{\sigma}(s)}
determine each other deterministically, and hence generate the same
\sigma
-field. Therefore

M_{\tau_{\sigma}(s)}^{\sigma}=\mathbb{E}\big[Y\mid(\widetilde{X}_{u}^{\sigma})_{0\leq u\leq\tau_{\sigma}(s)}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[Y\mid(R_{r})_{0\leq r\leq s}\big]=:Z_{s}

for every
s\geq 0
. In particular, we have the pathwise identity

(M^{\sigma}_{\tau_{\sigma}(s)})_{s\geq 0}=(Z_{s})_{s\geq 0}\qquad\text{a.s.}

∎

Thus all Föllmer martingales associated with the same coupling
m
, but different reference volatilities, are in fact one and the same process written in different time scales. The theorem also suggests that the canonical time scale for the Föllmer martingale is not the original interval
[0,1]
, but the information time
s\in[0,\infty)
of the filtering problem.
Indeed, since

\frac{R_{s}}{s}=(Y-X)+\frac{W_{s}}{s},

time
s
corresponds to observing
Y-X
through Gaussian noise of covariance
s^{-1}I_{d}
.
In this sense,
1/s
is the effective observation noise variance, and
s
is the natural parameter governing the decay of the posterior covariance.

The filtering representation also indicates a special property of the martingale Schrödinger bridge among general martingale couplings.
For a general coupling
m(dx,dy)=\mu(dx)m_{x}(dy)
, the natural state variable of the associated filter is not merely the conditional mean

Z_{s}=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X,(R_{r})_{0\leq r\leq s}],

but the full posterior law

\Pi_{s}:=\operatorname{Law}\big(Y\mid X,(R_{r})_{0\leq r\leq s}\big),

which takes values in the infinite-dimensional space
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
In general, the future evolution is determined by
\Pi_{s}
, not by its mean alone.

By contrast, for the martingale Schrödinger bridge the conditional laws form a common exponential family, as reflected in the Gibbs structure (
1.1
).
It is therefore natural to expect, at least in dimension one, that
m^{\text{SB}}
is the unique martingale coupling for which the associated filtering problem closes with a one-dimensional sufficient statistic, equivalently, for which the current value of the martingale is already a sufficient state variable for the future.

This heuristic is closely related to the Pitman–Koopman–Darmois principle, according to which finite-dimensional sufficient statistics are characteristic of exponential families.
From this perspective, the martingale Schrödinger bridge should be viewed not only as the entropic optimizer among martingale couplings, but also as the unique coupling for which the hidden terminal information can be encoded by a one-dimensional Markovian statistic.

We close this part with a particular, one-dimensional case, where the Föllmer martingale admits a surprisingly explicit form:

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\mu=\delta_{1/2}
and
\nu=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1}
, and let
M=(M_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
be the corresponding Föllmer martingale for the reference Brownian motion
B
.
Set

Z_{s}:=M_{s/(1+s)},\qquad s\geq 0.

Then there exist a Bernoulli random variable
Y
with
\mathcal{P}(Y=1)=\mathcal{P}(Y=0)=\frac{1}{2}
and an independent Brownian motion
W
such that, with

R_{s}:=s\Bigl(Y-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)+W_{s},\qquad s\geq 0,

one has

Z_{s}=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}^{R}]=\mathcal{P}(Y=1\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}^{R})=\frac{1}{1+e^{-R_{s}}},\qquad s\geq 0,

where
\mathcal{F}_{s}^{R}:=\sigma(R_{r}:0\leq r\leq s)
.
Moreover,
Z
is the unique weak solution of the filtering SDE

dZ_{s}=Z_{s}(1-Z_{s})\,dB_{s},\qquad Z_{0}=\frac{1}{2}.

Proof.

Since
\mu=\delta_{1/2}
and
\nu=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1}
, there is only one martingale coupling between
\mu
and
\nu
, namely
X\equiv\frac{1}{2}
and
Y\sim\nu
. By Theorem
5.1
, after the time change
t=s/(1+s)
, the Föllmer martingale admits the filtering representation

Z_{s}=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}^{R}],\qquad R_{s}=s\Bigl(Y-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)+W_{s},

for a Brownian motion
W
independent of
Y
.

We now compute this conditional expectation explicitly. Conditionally on
Y
, the process
R
is a Brownian motion with constant drift:

R_{s}=\begin{cases}\frac{s}{2}+W_{s},&Y=1,\\[2.84526pt]
-\frac{s}{2}+W_{s},&Y=0.\end{cases}

Hence, by the Cameron–Martin formula, the likelihoods on
\mathcal{F}_{s}^{R}
corresponding to the two possible values of

are

L_{s}^{\pm}:=\exp\!\Big(\pm\frac{1}{2}R_{s}-\frac{1}{8}s\Big).

Since the prior probabilities are equal, Bayes’ formula yields

\mathcal{P}(Y=1\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}^{R})=\frac{L_{s}^{+}}{L_{s}^{+}+L_{s}^{-}}=\frac{e^{R_{s}/2-s/8}}{e^{R_{s}/2-s/8}+e^{-R_{s}/2-s/8}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-R_{s}}}.

As
Y\in\{0,1\}
, its conditional expectation coincides with the conditional probability of the event
\{Y=1\}
, and therefore

Z_{s}=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}^{R}]=\frac{1}{1+e^{-R_{s}}}.

To derive the SDE, define the innovation process

I_{s}:=R_{s}-\int_{0}^{s}\Bigl(Z_{u}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)\,du.

By standard filtering theory,
I
is a Brownian motion with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{s}^{R})_{s\geq 0}
, and

dR_{s}=\Bigl(Z_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)\,ds+dI_{s}.

Now write
Z_{s}=\Lambda(R_{s})
with
\Lambda(r):=(1+e^{-r})^{-1}
. Then

\Lambda^{\prime}(r)=\Lambda(r)\bigl(1-\Lambda(r)\bigr),\qquad\Lambda^{\prime\prime}(r)=\Lambda(r)\bigl(1-\Lambda(r)\bigr)\bigl(1-2\Lambda(r)\bigr).

Applying Itô’s formula,

dZ_{s}=\Lambda^{\prime}(R_{s})\,dR_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{\prime\prime}(R_{s})\,ds.

Substituting
dR_{s}
and
Z_{s}=\Lambda(R_{s})
, we get

dZ_{s}=Z_{s}(1-Z_{s})\Bigl[\Bigl(Z_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)\,ds+dI_{s}\Bigr]+\frac{1}{2}Z_{s}(1-Z_{s})(1-2Z_{s})\,ds.

The drift terms cancel, leaving

dZ_{s}=Z_{s}(1-Z_{s})\,dI_{s}.

Renaming the Brownian motion
I
as
B
, this gives the claimed SDE.
∎

6.2.
Three-Point Discrete Marginals

We next consider a discrete counterpart of the Gaussian example, in which both marginals are supported on three points. In contrast with the Gaussian case, the minimizers of (
P
) and (
PB
) no longer coincide. Nevertheless, they turn out to be numerically very close. We make this precise by first parametrizing the set
\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
, then characterizing separately the entropy minimizer and the Bass minimizer, and finally comparing them on a concrete example.

Let

\mu=p_{1}\delta_{-1}+q_{1}\delta_{0}+r_{1}\delta_{1},\qquad\nu=p_{2}\delta_{-2}+q_{2}\delta_{0}+r_{2}\delta_{2},

where

r_{1}=1-p_{1}-q_{1},\qquad r_{2}=1-p_{2}-q_{2},

and assume throughout that all atoms have positive mass. We identify any coupling

\pi\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)

with its
3\times 3
matrix
(\pi_{x,y})
, whose rows are indexed by

x\in\{-1,0,1\}
and columns by
y\in\{-2,0,2\}
.

A convenient parametrization is obtained by setting

u\coloneq\pi_{-1,-2},\qquad v\coloneq\pi_{0,-2},\qquad w\coloneq\pi_{1,-2}=p_{2}-u-v.

Imposing the marginal constraints together with the martingale constraints, one finds that every admissible martingale coupling is necessarily of the form

(6.4)

\pi(u,v)=\begin{pmatrix}u&\frac{3p_{1}}{2}-2u&u-\frac{p_{1}}{2}\\[4.0pt]
v&q_{1}-2v&v\\[4.0pt]
w&\frac{r_{1}}{2}-2w&w+\frac{r_{1}}{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad w=p_{2}-u-v.

Thus
\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
is a two-dimensional affine family. The nonnegativity constraints
\pi_{x,y}\geq 0
are equivalent to

\frac{p_{1}}{2}\leq u\leq\frac{3p_{1}}{4},\qquad 0\leq v\leq\frac{q_{1}}{2},\qquad p_{2}-\frac{r_{1}}{4}\leq u+v\leq p_{2}.

We denote by
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
the corresponding feasible polygon in the
(u,v)
-plane. Hence both optimization problems reduce to minimization over the compact convex set
\mathcal{S}
.

We first consider the martingale Schrödinger problem (
P
). Since
\mu
and
\nu
have full support, every
\pi\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
satisfies
\pi\ll\mu\otimes\nu
, and therefore

H(\pi\mid\mu\otimes\nu)<\infty.

Minimizing

H(\pi\mid\mu\otimes\nu)=\sum_{x,y}\pi_{x,y}\log\!\Big(\frac{\pi_{x,y}}{\mu_{x}\nu_{y}}\Big)

over
\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
is equivalent to minimizing

J(\pi)\coloneq\sum_{x,y}\pi_{x,y}\log\pi_{x,y}.

Accordingly, using the parametrization
\pi=\pi(u,v)
from (
6.4
), we define

F(u,v)\coloneq J(\pi(u,v)),\qquad(u,v)\in\mathcal{S}.

Since
a\mapsto a\log a
is strictly convex on
(0,\infty)
and
(u,v)\mapsto\pi(u,v)
is affine, the function
F
is strictly convex on
\mathcal{S}
. Therefore the entropy minimizer is unique.

Under the irreducibility assumptions on
(\mu,\nu)
,
the optimizer
m^{E}
admits the exponential representation
(
3.11
), and in particular all its entries are strictly positive. Equivalently, if

m^{E}=\pi(u^{E},v^{E}),

then
(u^{E},v^{E})
lies in the interior of
\mathcal{S}
, so it is characterized by the first-order condition

\nabla F(u^{E},v^{E})=0.

A direct computation yields the system

(6.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} u(2u-p_{1})\,(r_{1}-4w)^{2}&=(3p_{1}-4u)^{2}\,w\,(r_{1}+2w),\\[4.0pt]
v^{2}\,(r_{1}-4w)^{2}&=2\,(q_{1}-2v)^{2}\,w\,(r_{1}+2w),\end{aligned}\right.\qquad w=p_{2}-u-v.

We next turn to the Bass problem (
PB
). Denoting its optimizer by

m^{B}=\pi(u^{B},v^{B}),

the corresponding first-order conditions are

(6.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{u}{p_{1}}\Big)-\Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{3}{2}-\frac{u}{p_{1}}\Big)&=\Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{w}{r_{1}}\Big)-\Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{w}{r_{1}}\Big),\\[6.0pt]
\Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{v}{q_{1}}\Big)-\Phi^{-1}\!\Big(1-\frac{v}{q_{1}}\Big)&=\Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{w}{r_{1}}\Big)-\Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{w}{r_{1}}\Big),\end{aligned}\right.\qquad w=p_{2}-u-v,

where
\Phi^{-1}
denotes the standard normal quantile function.

Thus the two optimizers are characterized by the systems (
6.5
) and (
6.6
). We now illustrate the comparison on a concrete example, with parameters chosen so as to accentuate the discrepancy between the two optimizers. The outcome is that, even after such a tuning, the two optimizers remain remarkably close.

For

\mu=0.40\,\delta_{-1}+0.46\,\delta_{0}+0.14\,\delta_{1},\qquad\nu=0.43\,\delta_{-2}+0.27\,\delta_{0}+0.30\,\delta_{2},

the corresponding entropy and Bass optimizers are

m^{E}=\begin{pmatrix}0.25123&0.09755&0.05123\\
0.16085&0.13831&0.16085\\
0.01793&0.03414&0.08793\end{pmatrix},\qquad m^{B}=\begin{pmatrix}0.25229&0.09543&0.05229\\
0.15941&0.14117&0.15941\\
0.01830&0.03340&0.08830\end{pmatrix},

and

(u^{E}-u^{B},\;v^{E}-v^{B})=(-1.06,\;1.43)\times 10^{-3}.

Hence
m^{E}\neq m^{B}
, although the discrepancy between the two optimizers is very small in this example.

A computational Lemma

Lemma A.1
.

Fix
s\in]0,1[
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let

\phi_{s}:w\mapsto(2\pi s)^{-d/2}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{|w|^{2}}{2s}\Big)
be the density of
\gamma^{s}
. Consider
m_{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
m_{x}\ll\gamma_{x}
, and define

\rho_{x}=\frac{dm_{x}}{d\gamma_{x}}
.
Then
\rho_{x}*\phi_{s}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

(A.1)

\nabla_{z}\log(\rho_{x}*\phi_{s})(z)=\frac{1}{s}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{q_{s}^{x,z}}[Y]-z\Big)

and

(A.2)

\nabla_{z}^{2}\log(\rho_{x}*\phi_{s})(z)=\frac{1}{s^{2}}Cov_{q_{s}^{x,z}}(Y)-\frac{I}{s},

where

(A.3)

q_{s}^{x,z}(dy)=\frac{\phi_{s}(z-y)\,\rho_{x}(y)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{s}(z-\tilde{y})\,\rho_{x}(\tilde{y})\,d\tilde{y}}dy.

Proof.

Set
I=\rho_{x}*\phi_{s}
. As
\rho_{x}(y)dy=\frac{dm_{x}}{d\gamma_{x}}(y)dy=\frac{dm_{x}}{dy}(y)\frac{dy}{d\gamma_{x}}(y)dy=\frac{dy}{d\gamma_{x}}(y)m_{x}(dy)
,

I(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{s}(z-y)\,\rho_{x}(y)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(y,z)\,m_{x}(dy),

with

\displaystyle K(y,z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\phi_{s}(z-y)}{\phi_{1}(y-x)}=s^{-d/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{|z-y|^{2}}{2s}+\frac{|y-x|^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=s^{-d/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1-s}{2s}|y|^{2}+\Big(\frac{z}{s}-x\Big)\!\cdot y-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2s}+\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}\right)

Therefore, for every compact set
K
and
p>1
, there exists
C_{K,p}>0
such that

(A.4)

\sup_{(x,y)\in K\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ |y|^{p}\,K(y,z)\ \leq\ C_{K,p}.

As
m_{x}
is a probability measure,
I
takes finite values. Next,

\nabla_{z}K(z,y)=-\frac{1}{s}(z-y)K(z,y),

so

|\nabla_{z}K(z,y)|\leq\frac{1}{s}\left(C_{K,0}\max_{z\in K}|z|+C_{K,1}\right).

Therefore, the dominated convergence theorem applies, and gives

\displaystyle\nabla I(z)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{s}(z-y)K(z,y)m_{x}(dy)=-\frac{1}{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(z-y)\phi_{s}(y-z)\rho_{x}(y)dy.

Defining
J(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\,\phi_{s}(z-y)\rho_{x}(y)\,dy
,
this rewrites

(A.5)

\nabla I(z)=\frac{1}{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(y-z)\phi_{s}(z-y)\rho_{x}(y)\,dy=\frac{1}{s}\big(J(z)-zI(z)\big),

Therefore,

\nabla\log I(z)=\frac{\nabla I(z)}{I(z)}=\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{J(z)}{I(z)}-z\right)=\frac{1}{s}\left(\mathbb{E}_{q^{x,z}_{s}}(Y)-z\right),

which proves Equation (
A.1
). Similarly, writing
J=(J_{1},\dots,J_{n})
and
F(z,y)=\phi_{s}(z-y)\rho_{x}(y)
, we have

\partial_{i}J_{j}(z)=\frac{1}{s}\int y_{j}(y_{i}-z_{i})F(z,y)dy=\frac{1}{s}\int y_{i}y_{j}F(z,y)dy-\frac{1}{s}z_{i}J_{j}(z).

By Equation (
A.5
), it holds
\partial_{i}I(z)=\frac{1}{s}\big(J_{i}(z)-z_{i}I(z)\big)
, so

\displaystyle{\partial_{i}J_{j}(z)I(z)-\partial_{i}I(z)J_{j}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{I(z)}{s}\int y_{i}y_{j}F(z,y)dy-\frac{1}{s}J_{j}(z)J_{i}(z),

so

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\log I(z)

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}\left(z\mapsto\frac{1}{s}\left(J_{j}(z)/I(z)-z_{j}\right)\right)=\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{\partial_{i}J_{j}(z)I(z)-\partial_{i}I(z)J_{j}(z)}{I(z)^{2}}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s^{2}}\left(\frac{\int y_{i}y_{j}F(z,y)dy}{\int F(z,y)dy}-\frac{\int y_{i}F(z,y)dy}{\int F(z,y)dy}\frac{\int y_{j}F(z,y)dy}{\int F(z,y)dy}\right)-\frac{1}{s}.

which concludes the proof of Equation (
A.2

Appendix B
From drifts to martingale drifts

This appendix section is devoted to the proof of Remark
4.5
.

Proposition B.1
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space equipped with a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
B
generating a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
. Let
X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
be a drift from
\delta_{x}
to
m_{x}
, in the sense that there exists a progressively measurable process
(u_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
such that:

X_{t}=x+B_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}u_{s}ds,

\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\|u_{t}\|^{2}dt\right)<+\infty\quad\text{and}\quad X_{1}\sim m_{x}.

Then, the Doob martingale
M
associated to
X
belongs to
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{mart}}(\delta_{x},m_{x})
, and
\tilde{X}:=\Phi^{-1}(\tilde{M})
satisfies

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{drift}}(\tilde{X})\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\|u_{t}\|^{2}dt\right).

Moreover, the above inequality is an equality if and only
X=\tilde{X}
almost surely.

Proof.

As
u\in L^{2}_{{\textnormal{prog}}}(\Omega\times[0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\xi:=\int_{0}^{1}u_{s}ds
is centred, there exists
\alpha\in L^{2}_{{\textnormal{prog}}}(\Omega\times[0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\xi=\int_{0}^{1}\alpha_{s}dB_{s}.

For all
t\in[0,1[
, define our candidate drift
\tilde{u}
by

\tilde{u}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\alpha_{s}}{1-s}dB_{s},\quad t\in[0,1[.

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the Hilbert space
L^{2}_{{\textnormal{prog}}}(\Omega\times[0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
endowed its scalar

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}:(v,w)\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}v_{t}\cdot w_{t}dt\right).

The technical core of the proof consist in proving that
\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{H}
. To do this, define

I:\mathcal{H}\to L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad Iv=\int_{0}^{1}v_{t}\,dt,

and

\mathcal{A}_{\xi}=\Bigl\{u\in\mathcal{H}:\ Iu=\xi\Bigr\}.

As
u\in\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
by assumption,
\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
is non-empty. By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\mathbb{E}\Bigl|\int_{0}^{1}v_{t}\,dt\Bigr|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}|v_{t}|^{2}\,dt=\|v\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathcal{A}_{\xi},

so
I
is linear continuous and
\mathcal{A}_{\xi}=I^{-1}(\{\xi\})
is a closed affine subspace of
\mathcal{H}
. Therefore, by the Hilbert projection theorem,
\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
admits a unique element
u^{\ast}
of minimal norm. To show that
u^{*}
belongs to
\ker I^{\perp}
, let
v\in\ker I
. As
u^{*}+\lambda v\in\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
for all
\lambda
, the map
\lambda\mapsto\|u^{\ast}+\lambda v\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

has a minimum at
\lambda=0
. Differentiating at
0
gives
\langle u^{\ast},v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=0,
which proves
u^{\ast}\perp\ker I.
Denoting by
I^{*}:L^{2}\to\mathcal{H}
the adjoint operator of
I
, by the standard Hilbert space identity

(\ker I)^{\perp}=\overline{\mathrm{Im}(I^{\ast})},

there exists a sequence
(\eta^{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})

such that
I^{\ast}\eta^{n}\to u^{\ast}
in
\mathcal{H}
. As for all
u\in H
and
\eta\in K
,

\displaystyle\langle Iu,\eta\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\int_{0}^{1}u_{t}\,dt\Big)\cdot\eta\Big]=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[u_{t}\cdot\eta]\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\big[u_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\eta\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\big]dt=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}u_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\eta|\mathcal{F}_{t}]dt,

it holds

(I^{*}\eta)_{t}=\mathbb{E}\left[\eta|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right],\quad t\in[0,1].

Therefore, for all
\eta\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
, it holds

\mathbb{E}(II^{*}\eta)=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}I^{*}\eta_{t}dt\right)=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}(I^{*}\eta_{t})dt=\mathbb{E}(\eta).

As
I^{*}\eta\to u^{*}
and
I
is continuous,
II^{*}\eta^{n}\to Iu^{*}=\xi
and

\mathbb{E}(\eta^{n})=\mathbb{E}(II^{*}\eta^{n})\to\mathbb{E}(\xi)=0.

So, up to substituting
\eta^{n}
by
\eta^{n}-\mathbb{E}(\eta^{n})
, we can assume that the
\eta^{n}
are centred. The martingale representation theorem then yields a process

\beta^{n}\in L^{2}_{{\textnormal{prog}}}(\Omega\times[0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
such that

\eta^{n}=\int_{0}^{1}\beta^{n}_{s}\,dB_{s}.

Consequently,

(I^{\ast}\eta^{n})_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\eta^{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]=\int_{0}^{t}\beta^{n}_{s}\,dB_{s},\qquad t\in[0,1].

By stochastic Fubini,

I(I^{\ast}\eta^{n})=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[\eta^{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]dt=\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{t}\beta^{n}_{s}\,dB_{s}\Bigr)\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\beta^{n}_{s}\,dB_{s}.

Continuity of
I
then yields
I(I^{\ast}\eta^{n})\to Iu^{\ast}=\xi
, that is

\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\beta^{n}_{s}\,dB_{s}\to\int_{0}^{1}\alpha_{s}dB_{s}\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}).

By Itô isometry,

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big|(1-s)\beta^{n}_{s}-\alpha_{s}\big|^{2}\right]\,ds\to 0,

that is,

(1-s)\beta^{n}_{s}\to\alpha_{s}\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}).

Hence, for any fixed
t<1
,

\beta^{n}_{s}1_{[0,t]}(s)\to\frac{\alpha_{s}}{1-s}1_{[0,t]}(s)\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,1]).

Applying Itô isometry again, we get

(B.1)

\int_{0}^{t}\beta^{n}_{s}\,dB_{s}\to\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\alpha_{s}}{1-s}\,dB_{s}\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}).

On the other hand, from

I^{\ast}\eta^{n}\to u^{\ast}\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{H},

we have

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big|(I^{\ast}\eta^{n})_{t}-u^{\ast}_{t}\big|^{2}\right]\,dt\to 0.

By extraction, there exists a subsequence
(n_{k})
such that for a.e.
t\in[0,1]
,

(I^{\ast}\eta^{n_{k}})_{t}\to u^{\ast}_{t}\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}).

For such
t
, combined with Equation (
B.1
), it holds

u^{\ast}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\alpha_{s}}{1-s}\,dB_{s}=\tilde{u}_{t}\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}).

As
u^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\|\tilde{u}_{t}\|^{2}dt\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\|u_{t}^{*}\|^{2}dt\right)<+\infty,

the drift
\tilde{u}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}
. Moreover,
\tilde{u}=u^{*}
in
H
. Since
u^{*}\in\ker I^{\perp}
and
Iu^{*}=Iu
, it holds

\|u\|=\|u^{*}-u+u^{*}\|=\|u^{*}-u\|+\|u^{*}\|\geq\|u^{*}\|,

with equality if and only if
u=u^{*}
in
\mathcal{H}
. Now, define the process
\tilde{X}
by

\tilde{X}_{t}=x+B_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{u}_{s}ds,\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

As
Iu=I\tilde{u}=\xi
, we get
X_{1}=\tilde{X}_{1}
. Since
\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{H}
and
\tilde{u}
is a martingale,
\tilde{X}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{drift}}(\delta_{x},m_{x})
and

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{drift}}(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\|\tilde{u}_{t}\|^{2}dt\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\|u_{t}\|^{2}dt\right).

Moreover the above inequality is an equality if and only if
u=\tilde{u}
in
\mathcal{H}
, that is, if and only if
X=\tilde{X}
almost surely. As
X_{1}=\tilde{X}_{1}
,
\tilde{X}
and
X
have the same Doob martingale, which rewrite
\Phi(\tilde{X})=M
and concludes.
∎
