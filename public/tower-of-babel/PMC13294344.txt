Capacity building for genomic surveillance of mpox and other emerging diseases in resource-limited settings within the African Great Lakes region

ABSTRACT
Genomic surveillance has become an indispensable tool for the identification of pathogens and tracking of transmission chains. Building upon the global sequencing and surveillance infrastructure developed and expanded during the COVID-19 pandemic, these capacities are now being adapted to track other pathogens in low- and middle-income countries which remain disproportionately affected by infectious diseases. This is evident in the recent and unprecedented spread of mpox virus in regions experiencing multiple concurrent infectious disease outbreaks, highlighting the need for broad, adaptable diagnostic detection and sequencing capacity. In this Perspective, we describe the applications, insights, and challenges encountered during ongoing capacity building efforts for the characterization of the mpox outbreak and other emerging pathogens in the African Great Lakes Region.

Background

Since the Ebola virus epidemic in Sierra Leone in 2015, nanopore sequencing has proven to be a powerful tool for rapid mobile whole-genome sequencing to support the public health response to emerging pathogens worldwide, including in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)1. During the COVID-19 pandemic, genomics was applied at an unprecedented scale for global surveillance, monitoring spillover events, characterizing epidemic strains and detecting new variants with possible phenotypic changes. The real-time application of this genomic surveillance technique facilitated input for immediate risk assessments2. However, the capacity to perform genomic surveillance at scale is mainly present in high-income countries. For example, 17% of all sequences available on GISAID (global repository for viral sequences) are derived from the United Kingdom, compared to just 1% from the African continent, most of which originate from a limited number of African countries. If the population per capita is taken into account, with a population of 69.2 million people in the UK versus 1.4 billion people in the African continent, this becomes more significant. Furthermore, based on data from GISAID, when expressed as a number of sequences per capita, the current density of SARS-CoV-2 sequences on the African continent is approximately 0.01%, compared to 1.01% in North America, 0.97% in Europe, 0.11% in South America, 0.68% in Oceania and 0.03% in Asia (Fig. 1, Table S1). Although this discrepancy is staggering, it reflects not only technical gaps but also different priorities, as well as broader structural and socioeconomic challenges inherent to the region. Nevertheless, sequencing capacity has improved in recent years due to investments during the COVID-19 pandemic2. However, with the end of these investments and the reduction in funding for global health by the US and other countries, there is a real risk that this progress will come to a halt in the coming years. Future challenges are both securing long-term investments and sustaining routine capacity for genomic surveillance of emerging viruses and local training following large scale outbreaks of a specific disease triggering a global public health response3,4. For sustainable capacity building, the acquired diagnostic and sequencing capacity could be redirected towards other disease programs and (emerging) pathogens, such as HIV, mpox virus (MPXV) (Figure S1), Rift Valley fever virus (RVFV), dengue virus, measles virus, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Vibrio cholerae, and yellow fever virus amongst others. In the Great Lakes region of Africa, the interaction between humans, animals and the environment is constant, necessitating integrated genomic surveillance within a One Health approach. The need for broader application of established core capacity was also advocated following the West African Ebola outbreak (2013-2016)5. During this outbreak response, real-time sequencing was established as part of the coordinated laboratory effort to support public health responses. However, no longer-term program was considered at the time5. In this Perspective, we discuss our experiences building diagnostic and sequencing capacity within ongoing projects funded through the Global Health European and Developing Countries Clinical Trials Partnership 3 (Global Health EDCTP3) (https://www.globalsurveillance.eu/projects/great-life; https://juakivu.org/)6,7. Within this Perspective, we focus on the following Great Lake countries: Burundi, the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), Rwanda and Tanzania. The African Great Lakes region includes a series of lakes and surrounding land area in and around the East African Rift. This region is characterized not only by shared geography but also by considerable human mobility and trade via the central corridor transport network. The African Great Lakes region is also one of the most densely populated and biodiverse areas in Africa, with significant levels of inter- and cross-border movements that can facilitate the emergence and spread of pathogens, making genomic surveillance and diagnosis a public health priority. We describe the insights from several capacity building trainings of local scientists to apply genomic surveillance since January of 2024 (Table 1). In GREAT-LIFE6, broad sequencing training was provided to enable the ability to test for any disease x through generic protocols. This led to the first detection and genomic characterisation of clade Ib MPXV8 during the outbreak in South Kivu expanding within and beyond the DRC. This is further studied in the newly funded JUA-KIVU project7 that aims to address other specific questions identified by the research team. This project resulted in the release of the first clade Ib sequences from the DRC and Burundi, insights into co-infections, and development of novel diagnostic PCR assays6,7. Where possible, data is shared on public databases along with the protocols (https://www.globalsurveillance.eu/projects/great-life/great-life-protocols). Health infrastructure

The studies conducted so far through our collaborations, established in the GREATLIFE consortium and strengthened after the formation of the JUA KIVU consortium, addressed questions related to ongoing outbreaks in remote areas, some of which had extremely limited resources6,7. The initial sampling of suspected mpox cases began around a local hospital in Kamituga in late 2023 that initially had no diagnostic capacity and limited infrastructure for sample collection and storage. Therefore, local staff first required training in safe sampling techniques and samples had to be transported to the national reference lab to perform diagnostic testing. Sample quality is essential for conventional diagnostics and particularly sequencing-based assays. However, in our experience, poor infrastructure and challenges in maintaining cold chains often compromised sample integrity during transport. We have seen that double-stranded DNA viruses such as mpox virus are generally stable and the absence of a continuous cold chain for multiple days in a variety of sample types still allowed for near to complete whole genome sequencing, but also that sample preservatives are critical for RNA viruses such as RVFV, which are less stable9. An additional challenge is the need to preserve and store a portion of the sample material for further characterization in early phases of an outbreak or whenever a change in epidemiology is observed. For this, storage of the original material in appropriate virus transport medium was required to allow for pathogen isolation and follow-up phenotypic characterization. Initial storage and transport of untreated samples from cholera patients resulted in low culture success rates for whole‑genome sequencing, further highlighting the need for appropriate transport media (e.g., Cary‑Blair for V. cholerae) or improvised alternatives such as sealed moist filter‑paper spots during outbreaks10. Furthermore, we piloted two approaches, Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate (SDS) cards and activated granular charcoal, which showed great promise in enabling sequencing without the presence of a cold chain. The acquisition of a centralized -20 °C freezer for (acute and prospective) sample and reagent storage also played a crucial role in prospective workshops.

Developing molecular testing capacity

Conventional molecular diagnostic assays are not only the basis for surveillance but also for follow-up genomic tracking by guiding sample selection for sequencing. Globally, real‑time PCR is the most common diagnostic tool for detection of viral pathogens and is considered the gold standard11.

A current default is the use of molecular point-of-care (POC) platforms which enable automated diagnosis of mpox in community settings and can be applied in cases with limited trained personnel or when a real-time PCR thermal cycler or reagents are not available. Following the declaration of the second mpox related public health emergency of international concern (PHEIC) by the WHO in August 2024, POC platforms were widely distributed among support initiatives (e.g., USAID programs [currently paused] and Africa CDC)12. POC platforms were also deployed previously during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic and during tuberculosis programmes13. The expansion of POC networks to all health districts with basic stock of test cartridges is often advocated as a cornerstone of pandemic preparedness. However, whilst important during an outbreak, the deployment of these automated platforms is not a long-term solution for diagnostic capacity building. Typically, molecular POC diagnostics are limited to certain targets, difficult to adapt and upscale, and are expensive compared to real-time PCR. Currently, the only fully automated POC system for MPXV diagnostics is the GeneXpert Mpox (Cepheid)12. However, it has not yet been adapted to differentiate between different co-circulating lineages, a modification that was achieved within a matter of weeks with open real-time PCR platforms within our consortium14. The limitation to specific targets also proved problematic during the 2026 Ebola outbreak in the DRC, where the GeneXpert assay could not detect the causative Bundibugyo virus15. Finally, as these POC platforms require minimal training of local personnel, they do not help to support training efforts and strengthening of laboratory capacity development, which is a critical pillar of preparedness.

In contrast to these POC platforms, real‑time PCR assays on open platforms are the default choice in diagnostic centres in other parts of the world, allowing for rapid, versatile, specific, scalable, cost‑effective and time‑effective testing, provided the capacity is available. However, a key challenge for deploying non‑commercial real‑time PCR platforms on site was obtaining positive controls as DNA from different MPXV clades for assay validation may not be accessible to every country or laboratory and shipping such controls requires export licenses, which are time‑consuming to obtain and can delay responses during unfolding outbreaks. Similar regulatory hurdles are also encountered with synthetic plasmids that are subject to genetic engineering and/or select agent regulations, which may prevent their distribution.

Several technological improvements for the rapid detection of emerging pathogens, such as CRISPR and LAMP assays, are currently being explored16,17. These assays could combine the sensitivity and agnostic nature of real-time PCRs with the ease of use of POC platforms, without the requirement of advanced equipment. However, they can face similar challenges with regards to supply chains and costs. Furthermore, these tests still need to be put to the test and validated in field settings before they can be used as an alternative diagnostics assay to the current gold standard.

Sequencing, cost efficiency, supply chains, and data analysis

There have been successful efforts to build capacity in decentralized settings using low-cost sequencing devices which do not require high throughput to be cost-effective1,18. During our capacity building initiatives, the MinION sequencing device generated data in real-time which facilitated immediate support to the outbreak response. Furthermore, being a low-power device powered through a laptop, the MinION remained operational even during prolonged power cuts. The ability to rapidly generate reliable data is a crucial aspect of capacity building for outbreak support. Finally, its mobility facilitated on-site training and application in resource limited settings. These features differ from larger benchtop sequencing platforms which depend on high throughput for cost efficiency and therefore are often limited to reference laboratories13.

As well as different sequencing platforms, several different sequencing approaches exist for the characterization of microbial pathogens1,13. While metagenomic and capture-based approaches are agnostic and can characterize viral and bacterial co-infections directly from a sample, they are more laborious, prone to sample-to-sample contamination, expensive, and challenging to analyse compared to whole-genome sequencing. Culture based bacterial and amplicon based viral whole-genome sequencing are cost‑effective, straightforward and sensitive, enabling rapid data generation during outbreaks19,20. Furthermore, their underlying principles and data‑analysis workflows can (once established) be transferred to other emerging pathogens.

One of the challenges for performing local sequencing and diagnostic assays is a limited supply chain, characterized by a small number of centralized suppliers. Specific local challenges experienced were the availability of real-time PCR thermal cyclers (ideally including backup power battery), a supply chain for plastics, reagents, maintenance, core laboratory equipment, and trained personnel. Currently, for instance, Nanopore sequencing consumables coverage remains limited in many LMICs. Supplies can be shipped to international airports and distributed among local collaborators. However, this adds further costs and delays at customs can compromise the availability and functionality of consumables and fragile enzymes. A potential solution could be investment or subsidization of local production and centralized storage facilities to reduce dependency on international logistics and improve supply stability. However, such efforts require substantial investment and sustained consumable demand within these regions, making this a complex and long-term undertaking. Another option is the substitution of certain reagents with alternative or commonly stocked enzymes which can be implemented into existing sequencing workflows21. Based on our experiences, the training focus shifted from initial wet-lab foundations (with sequencing now performed locally) towards increasing emphasis on bioinformatic skills. Here the application of Graphical User Interface (GUI) based workflows (Table 1) can help more users participate in analysis compared with purely command-line approaches. It is important that local scientists can perform bioinformatic analysis. Some initiatives, such as PHA4GE, VEME and the ARTIC network [https://artic.network/], provide manuals for bioinformatic pipelines for genomic pathogen surveillance, as data analysis often lacks standardization and can be complex for institutions without (and even with) bioinformatics expertise. These programs are comprehensive and it will take time, as well as the continuous availability, standardization and support of free, open-source software with GUIs, for them to be applied widely across LMICs. The generation of consensus sequences and assemblies on site from raw data through pipelines also helps to reduce bottlenecks caused by transferring large amounts of raw data over slow or unstable internet connections22. Simultaneously, this facilitates the use of internet-based tools such as Nextclade, timely submission to public databases (e.g. GenBank, Pathoplexus and GISAID) and sharing of preliminary analyses on discussion forums (e.g. https://virological.org/).

Personal reflections, future directions, and concluding remarks

Genomic pathogen surveillance has emerged as a powerful tool to monitor viral spread and evolution, identify transmission patterns and guide public health response. Building and strengthening local capacity within LMICs is vital to achieve this globally. Several collaborative initiatives are ongoing; however they are often ad-hoc, responding to emergencies. To support more sustainable and long-term capacity building, longer programs and collaborations are required that can support local capacity and readiness even when funding is limited. This includes time to train local scientists in embedded outbreak research, to set up protocols with pre-approval to allow rapid research response and to develop scientific papers which are important to lay the foundation to maximize public health impact. A clear definition and priority list of the research and diagnostic questions (prevalence studies, surveillance, outbreak investigation, etc.), followed by selection of the appropriate sample types, collection strategies (sensitivity, preservation strategies etc.) and respective metadata structure are essential and should be planned as early as possible.

Another key question is how much sequencing is necessary during the different stages of an outbreak and how to sustain surveillance beyond initial investments. Most African mpox genomic surveillance conducted since the second PHEIC took place where capacity already existed, often in central reference labs which can be quite far from the field sites. Standardization of wet and dry lab protocols among regional, national, and international laboratories will ensure quality control and aid further collaborations and support. Cost-effective sequencing approaches and platforms with low initial acquisition costs are key (particularly outside of national reference centres) and currently best satisfied by utilizing whole-genome sequencing and Nanopore sequencing. Further improvements to reagent availability such as local supply chains and exploration of the substitution of reagents from the current protocols with alternatives would contribute to a greater operational independence. Local production of these reagents would also contribute to this but would require large scale investments. Consideration that infrastructure may be weak must also be taken into account during sample transport and may be mitigated with cold chain independent solutions such as chemical sample preservation methods and utilization of solar energy. Furthermore, power cuts should be expected during assays and mitigated with a backup battery and low-power workflows. Local bioinformatics capacity is essential for independent pathogen surveillance and for shortening the interval between sampling and public health decision-making. Building this capacity requires investments in regional training programs, educational frameworks, secure and privacy preserving data sharing protocols as well as hands-on analytical experience. Recent improvements in internet infrastructure offer new opportunities for remote training and technical support. At the same time, the use of user-friendly GUI-based bioinformatics pipelines (Table 1) and local collaborative networks is essential for enabling data sharing and decentralized analysis.

LMICs remain disproportionately affected by infectious disease risks and regional collaborative capacity building efforts are beneficial to expand genomic surveillance accessibility in these settings. Recognizing current capacity differences and highlighting the challenges in LMICs is crucial to help expand and sustain genomic surveillance, thereby strengthening national, regional and global decision‑making.
