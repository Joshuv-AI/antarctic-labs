The balancing act of app creation: Navigating the user-centred design of an industry-developed app for adolescents with menstrual pain
Authors: Michelle M Gagnon, Alexandra R Brilz, Harini S Gathaatharan, Nicole M Alberts, Jennifer L Gordon, Alexandria Pavelich, Tracie Risling, Jennifer N Stinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMenstrual pain affects over half of menstruating adolescents, and there is a desire for accessible, adjunct treatments, such as behavioural health interventions in mobile health (mHealth) formats. To date, no mHealth apps for adolescents with menstrual pain have been designed with youth. Although user-centred design is best practice, many challenges can arise, particularly when working with industry partners in the design process. We report on the development and usability testing phase of an industry-developed app for adolescents with menstrual pain, along with exploration of challenges and emerging recommendations throughout the process. The user-centred design process was composed of three phases. In Phase 1 we identified an industry partner and established a patient advisory committee. In Phase 2, adolescents (N = 22) reviewed iteratively developed versions of app design and content using a think-aloud procedure, followed by a semi-structured interview. The app was well-received by adolescents, although in each phase, feedback related to (1) visual design, (2) interface design, (3) content, and (4) app features was observed. In Phase 3, research team members and a patient advisor reflected on the process of working with industry. Identified reflections were thematically analysed and an overarching theme of Integration of Research, Industry, Institutional, and Funding Needs was generated, with four subthemes: timeline considerations; genuine patient involvement; financial constraints; and establishing mutual understanding. We provide a checklist of recommendations for working with youth and industry in app design and practical considerations when working with industry partners and adolescent stakeholders are discussed.Keywords: User-centred design, Dysmenorrhea, Patient engagement, Behavioural health interventions, mhealth, Mobile apps, Females

Introduction

Menstrual pain is a pressing health concern that affects between 50 and 92% of adolescents who menstruate (Armour et al., 2020). Unmanaged menstrual pain can result in considerable health detriments, including poorer quality of life (Mizuta et al., 2023), mental health symptoms (Zhao et al., 2021; Cameron et al., 2025) and long-term risk of chronic pain (Li et al., 2021; Reid-McCann et al., 2025). Although pharmacological treatments for menstrual pain remain the first-line treatment, a subset of adolescents do not find these effective (Wall et al., 2025) and are interested in non-pharmacological pain management options (Gagnon et al., 2024). Among adolescents, challenges in menstrual pain management are further compounded by stigma (Johnston-Robledo and Chrisler, 2020), dismissal of menstrual pain as normal, invalidation of requests for intervention when help-seeking (Wall et al., 2025), and lower menstrual literacy (Reid-McCann et al., 2025; Munro et al., 2024). There is a need for interventions for menstrual pain that address the unique needs and preferences of adolescents.

Behavioural health interventions are guided by a biopsychosocial understanding of health and illness (Barajas et al., 2021; Wade and Halligan, 2017) and are a valuable alternative or adjunct to pharmacological approaches. Moreover, behavioural health interventions lend themselves well to mobile health (mHealth) formats. Mhealth and behavioural health interventions have offered important improvements to care across many adolescent pain concerns, including chronic pain (Palermo et al., 2016) and post-operative pain (Murray et al., 2022). Menstrual pain, in contrast to other types of pain, has lagged in its conceptualization as a biopsychosocial phenomenon (Gagnon et al., 2022), despite this model being well-established as the gold-standard in pain care (Gatchel et al., 2007). Consequently, although behavioural and mHealth interventions, such as mindfulness- and acceptance-based approaches, are gaining traction as effective treatments for pain (Zoogman et al., 2015; Laundy et al., 2021), there is limited research supporting such approaches in the management of menstrual pain specifically (Gagnon et al., 2022).

Adolescents have widespread access to smartphones, particularly in Western countries (Statistics Canada, 2021), and engage with health-related apps. In 2018, nearly 50% of adolescents in the United States aged 14–17 had used a health-related app (Rideout et al., 2018), with similar rates reported in other countries (Gulec and Smahel, 2022). Adolescents and adults commonly use menstrual tracking apps and wearables for the monitoring of menses and associated symptoms (Trépanier et al., 2023; Earle et al., 2021; Wartella et al., 2015), pointing to interest in digital tools to manage menstrual experiences. Although a handful of tracking apps incorporate pain content, this content is poor quality, not evidence-based, and often requires a paid subscription which decreases accessibility (Trépanier et al., 2023). Non-pharmacological and psychoeducational mHealth interventions for adolescent menstrual pain that consider the specific needs of adolescents are lacking. Through a preliminary needs assessment, we identified that adolescents who menstruate displayed interest in using app-based approaches for the management of menstrual pain (Gagnon et al., 2024).

Despite the potential benefit of mHealth interventions, many health-related apps fail to be implemented, with poor usability being a primary driver of limited uptake (Giebel et al., 2023). App design that integrates users in the design process is integral to closing the research-practice gap (Ferrucci et al., 2021) and increases app satisfaction, uptake, and efficacy (Chan et al., 2011; Wolpin and Stewart, 2011; McCurdie et al., 2012). User-centred design (UCD) is one such design approach. Through methods such as focus groups, participatory design sessions, and usability testing, end-user input is gathered and incorporated throughout the process, from design to testing (Schnall et al., 2016). UCD can be particularly powerful in supporting equity-deserving groups (i.e., groups facing barriers to access, resources, or opportunities, owing to systemic inequities and discrimination (Canadian Institutes of Health Research, 2025)), such as adolescents with menstrual pain experiencing dismissal and stigmatization due to the women's health and reproductive nature of their concerns, which can be further exacerbated by their age and gender. Involvement of these youth in design would aid to reflect the realities of their experience and their needs.

Although UCD may be “best practice” in mHealth research, the process is often complex, and there is limited guidance on how to prevent or navigate arising challenges. The complexities of engaging stakeholders across sectors (e.g., industry, research institutions, patients) with diverse priorities can create constraints. Internal pressures within sectors can also impact decisions. Much of the literature on navigating challenges in UCD is aimed at app developers (e.g. Salah et al., 2014; Marti and Bannon, 2009). The perspectives of research teams and patient partners working with industry partners have been underrepresented in these discussion, with most work focusing on pre-design phases (Voorheis et al., 2024). Equity-deserving groups have been particularly overlooked in this discussion, although a subset of papers have centred on older adults (Tong et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). Exploring perspectives of those working with and those from groups in historically stigmatized areas of health, such as adolescents menstrual pain, can provide valuable guidance for researchers undertaking similar work.

Our primary purpose was to describe our UCD and results from usability testing of an app-based behavioural health intervention for adolescents with menstrual pain. Our secondary purpose was to discuss encountered challenges of, and provide recommendations for, UCD in multi-stakeholder groups that incorporate industry, researchers, and adolescent patient advisors. A small number of studies have involved needs assessments from a user-centred perspective to inform future menstruation-related app development (Gagnon et al., 2024; Apsey et al., 2024), while one group compared usability of a menstrual calendar across adolescents and adults (Özcan et al., 2024), however, to our knowledge, this is the first paper reporting usability testing of a mHealth intervention in menstrual pain for adolescents. We focus this paper specifically on the design and usability testing phases of a mindfulness-based and psychoeducational app for adolescents. This period represents the timeframe where the bulk of interaction with the industry partner occurred.

Methods

This work was embedded within established UCD frameworks (Gulliksen et al., 2003). Having already worked with users to define the context and requirements (Gagnon et al., 2024), we engaged in iterative stages of design and usability test. We describe three-phases (see Fig. 1). Phase 1 includes findings from our initial onboarding and consultation with the industry partner, and direct engagement with patient advisors. Phase 2 involves three rounds of usability testing and user feedback. In Phase 3, we report our reflections on the process and recommendations for UCD with adolescents and industry partners.

Summary of Phases.

Phase 1: Patient advisor and industry partner engagement

Phase 1 involved simultaneous processes. Adolescent patient advisors with menstrual pain were recruited. Patient advisors were seen as members of the research team. They reviewed and gave feedback on all materials and prototypes, informed prioritization of needs, and engaged in decision-making throughout the entire design process. We recruited nine patient advisors aged 13 to 18 years (M = 15.89, SD = 1.90). Recruitment largely occurred via Meta advertisements (i.e., Facebook and Instagram) targeting adolescents and parents of adolescents. Ads were also placed in community centres and community associations throughout the country, with focus on reaching areas of diverse economic and cultural status. Parental consent to participate was obtained for patient advisors under the age of 18.

Concurrently, identification of an industry partner occurred following internal institutional processes and policies. In July 2022, the first meeting was held with the industry partner (referred to as the “app developer” from here forth). This meeting involved a high-level discussion of the purpose and priorities of the app. Collated findings from a prior scoping review (Trépanier et al., 2023) and needs assessment (Gagnon et al., 2024) were share with the app developer to provide preliminary priorities. The app developer planned to approach development using agile development (Abrahamsson et al., 2017) and held a kickoff meeting in August 2022 where Phase 1 materials (see 2.1.1) were shared. Following this meeting, materials were reviewed by the research team members, including patient advisors. Preferences and concerns were collated and prioritized. Prioritized feedback was shared with the app developer and used to inform Phase 2 protypes.

Phase 1: Materials

User stories

User stories are short descriptions of features or functionalities from the perspective of the user. These were identified by the app developer based on review of preliminary priorities for the app and discussion with the research team. The app developer required that we categorize each story as a “must have,” “should have,” could have,” “won't have” based on app goals and budget.

Mood board

Mood boards are a collection of images, colours, and fonts showing potential app aesthetic and visual features to users. They allow for input and feedback prior to committing to an aesthetic in the development stage. The mood board consisted of a photography option (e.g., nature-based photography, photography of classrooms); and illustrations of scenery (e.g., lakes, mountains), people (e.g., individuals meditating), and abstract geometric shapes. Two colour theming options were provided: 1) light purple background with dark blue/purple writing, and 2) dark purple background with light pink and purple writing.

Low-fidelity wireframes

Low-fidelity wireframes demonstrate how screen elements and navigation might appear throughout the app. The app developer provided images of 20 proposed app pages. These included a proposed home page, psychoeducational content pages, pain rating pages, and personalized app-use statistics pages (see supplementary materials).

Phase 2: Iterative development and usability testing

In Phase 2, app content and prototype development occurred and were tested in three rounds of usability testing with participants. Consistent with UCD, rounds were iterative, such that feedback from each round was prioritized and integrated into materials presented in subsequent rounds.

Participants

Phase 2 was approved by the University of Saskatchewan Research Ethics Board (BEH 3492). Three separate group of 6–8 adolescents, aged 13–17 and experiencing pain with menstruation, were recruited. Members of the patient advisor group were not eligible to serve as study participants. Additional inclusion criteria included 1) being English speaking, 2) living in Canada, and 3) having parental consent to participate. In the final round, an additional eligibility criterion was added of having an Apple device, as the final version of the app was developed for Apple iOS. Recruitment occurred through paid Meta ads that included a link to a form in which to provide a contact email. Four ads were posted for seven days each. Two ads were posted in September 2022 for the Round 1 testing, one ad was posted in October 2022 for Round 2 testing, and a third ad was posted March 2023 for Round 3 testing. Across the full study period, 220 individuals (210 youth and 10 parents) expressed interest and were contacted by the research team. Adolescents and a parent/guardian who remained interested met with a member of the research team via Zoom to review the purpose of the study. Adolescent assent and parental consent were obtained for participants wishing to enroll. Participating adolescents received $50 CAD Amazon gift card or cash as compensation.

App content and prototype development

App content

App content was developed by the research team. The content included psychoeducational materials and mindfulness activities, with these focus areas identified in prior work (Gagnon et al., 2024). To develop mindfulness activities, a preliminary list of categories (e.g., emotions, pain) with potential meditation activities and audio meditations was generated and reviewed by the research team and patient advisors. Similarly, psychoeducation topics were generated within the broad areas of “mindfulness education” and “period pain education.” Once a content plan was established, the planned content was divided between two authors responsible for content development (M.G. and K.W.). For each planned activity (i.e., meditation script, activity, or educational content), a primary content developer generated the content. The secondary content developer then provided feedback on the drafted content. The content was then revised by the primary content developer and re-reviewed by the second content developer.

App prototypes

In Rounds 1 and 2, the app developer provided click-through high fidelity prototypes of the app, shared with the research team via Figma (Figma Inc, 2022). The prototypes allowed the viewers to review the design and intended navigation of the app. The prototypes were organized into three main tabs: Home, Education, and Insights. The Home tab was the main landing page and contained the mindfulness activities. Psychoeducational content was found in the Education section, and tracking and statistics in the Insights section. Prototypes in Rounds 1 and 2 included placeholder text.

In Round 3, a functional, iOS version of the app integrating both design and content was tested. The app was named Mindful Monthlies based on participant and advisor feedback and was organized into four tabs: Home, About, Education, and Insights. In line with earlier prototypes, the Home tab was the main landing page for the app and contained the mindfulness sessions. These sessions were arranged into five categories based on: Period Pain, Big Emotions, Self-Care, Daily Stressors, Being Present. Mindfulness activities (i.e., audio, text, video, external website link) were located under each category. Mindfulness meditations were professionally recorded and imported into the app. Before and after each activity, users were prompted to rate their pain from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain possible). Upon completion of an activity, any decrease in pain rating was shown on the screen along with a positive and motivational affirmation. The About tab explained how to use the app and listed the research team. The Education tab included subsections explaining mindfulness (e.g., what is mindfulness, research supporting mindfulness) and periods (e.g., what is period pain, pain management), as well as external links to resources on these topics. The Insights tab included two reports: 1) average pain reduction, based on an average of pre-post activity ratings during the past month; 2) quality-of-life tracker, where adolescents indicated they had experiences changes in period pain, mood, anxiety, stress, sleep, and relationships.

Usability testing session procedure

Each of the three versions of the protype/app were tested through a round of usability testing. A usability testing session was scheduled with each participant within a round. Sessions were led by a research assistant on Zoom and were recorded. In Rounds 1 and 2, participants accessed the prototype through a link and engaged screen sharing while interacting with the prototype. In Round 2, two prototypes with the same functionality but different aesthetics were provided to determine which was preferred by participants (supplementary Fig. 1). As content was not yet embedded in the Rounds 1 and 2 prototypes, content testing occurred separately via screen sharing of word documents. Participants reviewed the planned content categories in each of the mindfulness and education sections, as well as two sample paragraphs of education content. Sample mindfulness audios (n = 2) were recorded and played on an iPhone 11. In Round 3, participants downloaded TestFlight (Apple Inc, 2023), a third-party app for beta testing of in-production apps, and reviewed the app directly from their phone.

Each usability testing session included two components: 1) participant review of protype/app and content using the think-aloud method (Fonteyn et al., 1993), and 2) semi-structured interview. The think-aloud method is a commonly used approach often considered best practice in usability testing (Boren and Ramey, 2000; Hornbæk, 2010) (Fonteyn et al., 1993) where participants share immediate reactions and thoughts while exploring a product. One of two research assistants (A.B. or A.P) led the usability sessions. Each research assistant received training in the think-aloud method from the first author that involved review of materials on the method and a practice session where feedback was provided. To ensure consistency in implementation of the think-aloud method across usability sessions, an instruction script was developed and read to each participant.

A semi-structured Usability Testing Interview Guide was developed to evaluate the protype/app and content in each round (see supplementary materials). The interview comprised the second portion of the usability testing session. An interview was chosen as narrative responses can help provide greater depth of understanding of usability issues (Maramba et al., 2019). The guide was developed by team members with expertise in usability testing and content expertise in menstrual pain and mindfulness. Questions drew from established dimensions of usability (Davis, 1989; International Organization for Standardization, 2019; International Organization for Standardization, 2018) and queried perceptions of app features and design (e.g., features liked/disliked, visual appeal) and content (e.g., ease of understanding, usefulness of content). Participants also provided feedback on potential app names and responded to demographic questions.

Data analysis and feedback

Participant demographics were summarized using frequencies. Audio recordings of the usability sessions were transcribed and a semantic-level thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2022; Braun and Clarke, 2006) was used to examine the session data. This approach was selected given that the goal was to focus on the manifest content of the data rather than to examine underlying meanings and concepts. One researcher (M.G.) reviewed the transcripts to become familiar with the data and then generated initial codes. Codes were then categorized into themes. To ensure trustworthiness of the analyses and coding structure, a second researcher (A.B.) independently reviewed a subset of transcripts. Both coders then met to discuss and refine the coding structure until consensus was reached.

After each round of testing, prioritization of changes were identified by the research team and patient advisors. Patient advisors provided input on what they perceived as most important to users and helped interpret participant feedback, as needed. Prioritized items were reviewed with the app developer and decisions regarding inclusion were made based on feasibility and cost. Content suggestions were addressed by the research team.

Phase 3: Team reflection and lessons learned

Throughout the phases of this study, challenges and lessons were flagged and recorded. Meetings minutes with the app developer were reviewed to identify positive outcomes of meetings along with areas that required additional problem solving. Team members involved in various phases generated a list of lessons learned. Additionally, a patient advisor (H.G.) provided their thoughts and reflections on the process. Common themes were identified through discussion and consensus and then reviewed by the larger research team for final approval.

Results

Phase 1

Prioritization of user stories based on research team and patient advisor consultation is summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Patient advisors were open to nature-based photography or an illustration-based mood boards but were overwhelmingly opposed to photography that reflected real locations such as school. They also requested broader diversity in the images of people (e.g., inclusion of gender diversity, visible minorities). They preferred the dark background with light writing.

Patient advisor feedback on the low-fidelity wireframe related to content and wording of app components. Adolescents suggested positive affirmations (e.g., you did it!) rather than “next” to indicate task completion. They approved of the insights that track mindfulness and pain statistics but were concern that not having an immediate pain reduction would be discouraging. They requested additional charts and visual representation of their data.

Based on the feedback, a decision was made with the app developer to focus on nature-based imagery with illustrations added throughout, and the preferred darker theme was selected. Additional visualization of data was deemed outside of the project budget. Positive feedback and presentation of pain data that would be encouraging to adolescents was prioritized as a “must have” item.

Phase 2

Round 1 testing

Demographic characteristics of participants involved in all rounds of usability testing are in Table 1. Coding of the Round 1 think-aloud transcripts for prototype testing resulted in the generation of four overarching categories: (1) Visual design, (2) Interface design, (3) Content, and (4) Features. Each category had subcategories. Categories and example feedback are available in Supplementary Table 2. Regarding Visual Design, participants approved of the simplicity of the design and layout but did not like the colour of the prototype. They requested more images, bigger font and bolding, and more breaks between text. Participants commented frequently on the pain scales, but preferences varied. For the Interface Design, participants liked the navigation, but wanted account personalization options (e.g., night mode, themes). Several areas of navigation confusion were identified. Subsets of participants identified minor preferences, such as a scroll bar on the home page and the ability to sort favourites. The main menu on the bottom of the screen was seen favourably. Comments about Features were generally related to what participants liked (e.g., pain ratings, favourites), but participants recommended the addition of a feedback button. Content comments related to aspects of content that participants saw favourably. Participants liked the education component – particularly that it was not limited to period education – access to external resources, text and audio meditations, and session descriptions.

Think-aloud responses related to sample content were diverse but positive. Specific themes could not be developed due to diversity of feedback and many statements of liking content without further elaboration (“it looks good”). Round 2 testing

The same four categories identified in Round 1 think-aloud transcripts (i.e., visual design, interface design, feature, content) were observed, supporting the stability of the coding structure and that saturation had been reached across themes. More positive comments and fewer recommendations for changes were identified by Round 2 participants (Supplementary Table 2).

Regarding Interface Design, addition of profiles and personalization features were requested, but navigation and organization of content, particularly the mindfulness activities were positively discussed. Participants liked the simplicity of the Visual Design and spoke positively of the colours and backgrounds of both proposed protypes. The nature images were preferred over abstract illustrations. Feature suggestions related to the addition of gamification, push notifications, filtering and favouriting of activities, period tracking, and a community discussion board. Existing features in the app, particularly insights, were viewed positively. Regarding the Content, participants noted that they liked the Education section and the meditation content areas, although they made suggestions to add yoga, bilateral beats, and descriptions next to meditation content areas.

Interview responses aligned with this feedback. All participants found the navigation to be easy. Most and least liked components are summarized in Supplementary Table 4. Suggested additions included gamification, profiles, and avatars; as well as the ability to filter sessions, a more defined home page, instructions on how to use the app, period tracking, the inclusion of a community, and yoga videos.

Two key ideas emerged in Round 2 content coding. First, participants found explanations of ‘normal’ versus ‘concerning’ pain as helpful and aided in not normalizing their extreme pain. Second, participants found reported statistics in the Education section helpful to understanding their pain in a larger context. Overall, participants described the content as easy to read, although the addition of pronunciations and definitions was suggested. Participants recommended using more bullet points, images, or pages to break up the text. Participants described the meditation audio as well-paced and an appropriate length. They responded positively to the female voice, which they described as soothing and calming. All adolescents requested options to have music or nature sounds as background in the meditations.

Round 3 testing

As occurred in Rounds 1 and 2, the generated coding structure led to the same four categories (i.e., visual design, interface design, feature, content). Feedback for the app was positive across the four categories, with minor suggestions for change (Supplementary Table 2). Interface Design pointed to a well-organized and easy to navigate app. Two participants suggested further division of the home page. Regarding the Visual Design¸ colour was consistently spoken about positively. Further suggestions for bolding font and adding bullets and breaks in text were made. Perceptions of images were discrepant: several participants approved, some wanted more, and some suggested changes to images.

Most comments related to Content were positive. This was the first time participants had access to the entirety of app content. Mindfulness activity feedback suggested it was relatable and relevant. Audio activities were positively received, with several participants reporting feeling more relaxed following the meditation and all comments about the audio voice were positive. Participants recommended adding an ending sound (e.g., bell) to the mindfulness audios. Participants were divided about the pain focus of the meditations, with some preferring there be no focus on pain. Non-audio meditation activities were described as clear and several participants noted that the step-by-step instructions were valuable. The education was described as engaging, useful, and validating. Notably, several participants identified that the references to published research were important and that the About page listing the research team added credibility to the app. Lastly, regarding Features, feedback related to the need for more tracking options and visualization of app use history. Likes and dislikes are summarized in Supplementary Table 5. All participants described the navigation as easy (7/7) and the content as easy to understand (7/7).

The research team and patient advisors prioritized all feedback; however, the app developer requested changes be limited to functionality issues and textual errors (e.g., typos). In this stage, legal statements and an end-user agreement were also added to the app. Sample images from the final version of the app are provided in Fig. 2.

Phase 3: Team reflections

An overarching core singular theme was identified from our reflective discussions, meeting minutes, and notes. This theme was labelled Integration of Research, Industry, Institutional, and Funding Needs. Overall, progress occurred smoothly when all stakeholders (i.e., research team, patient advisors, app developer, research institutions, granting agency) were aligned in their goals and expectations. Challenges occurred when individual priorities conflicted across stakeholder groups, such as when the app developer needed to prioritize timelines for their multiple projects and our team needed to prioritize soliciting user feedback. Similarly, when workstyles aligned across groups, there was good momentum and communication; and when approaches diverged, there was friction. For instance, research team members were more accustomed to providing detailed descriptions and asking for clarifications in emails, whereas members of the app team preferred to keep emails short and discuss questions in meetings. Institutional and funding needs added a layer of complexity due to administrative demands and processes for managing research funds. Four subthemes were generated within this overarching theme. These included: 1) timeline considerations; 2) genuine patient involvement; 3) financial constraints; and 4) establishing mutual understanding (Table 2).

Regarding timeline considerations, it became evident that our team's timelines for gathering feedback were misaligned with the app developer's expectation. The app developer employed a commonly used “sprint” approach, (Badriyah et al., 2018) which required quick decision-making. Early on, feedback was requested within 3 to 5 business days. Given our need to engage patient advisors and adolescent participants, our team could not gather feedback that quickly. While waiting for our feedback, the app developer would switch to other projects, and we would be required to wait until focus could return to our project. We relied heavily on patient advisors when quick decisions were needed that could not be gathered through data collection.

We found that our expectations for genuine patient involvement and integration of user feedback were not aligned with those of the app developer. Our team emphasized that if we did not address user feedback, our app would not succeed. Indeed, on many occasions, the app developer and our team incorrectly predicted adolescent preferences. As the work progressed, the app developer and our team worked creatively to address adolescents' requests within the limitations of the project. For instance, although period tracking was not added, external tracking options were added to the Education section.

Despite challenges conveying the importance of patient feedback to the app developer, our patient advisor (H. G.) described feeling like a valued team member. This helped her not feel discouraged from providing further feedback when her suggestions were not incorporated. Of note, patient advisors were not involved in meetings with the app developer as these occurred during school hours. Our patient advisor described uncertainty as to whether they would have felt comfortable speaking with the developers had that been an option. Their young age was identified as one reason, along with feelings of being underqualified to comment of the work of professional app developers. Our advisor reported feeling more comfortable and willing to share honest feedback with the researcher with whom she had a built a relationship.

The first two subthemes were tied to financial constraints. The app developer needed to allocate resources to multiple ongoing projects. Many of the user requests would have significantly impacted the app developer's timeline on other projects or were too complex to implement in a cost-effective way. Our challenges with budget were substantial and began at the grant development stage wherein the company that provided a quote for the grant application was not ultimately the company used. Our app developer quoted the same scope of work and timeline at a cost that was 1.5 times more than the original quote. Although possible within our budget, this increase created less flexibility to address unanticipated feedback and resulting timeline adjustments. The patient advisor's ability to understand financial constraints throughout the process became essential; it allowed them to engage more deeply in prioritization that balanced user preferences with project limitations. Our advisor noted that with their understanding of financial constraints, they preferred providing feedback on static images rather than a functional app, as it felt like there were fewer consequences to suggesting changes.

Our method of communicating as researchers differed from that of the industry team, highlighting the need to establish a mutual understanding. Throughout the process we struggled with misunderstandings on both the researcher and app developer side. We struggled to understand when the app developer required approval on components and when an approval become a final decision that could not be changed based on user feedback. The app developer was less familiar with research requirements such as participant confidentiality, which required troubleshooting around how to provide participants access to the app without disclosing their identity to the app developer.

Discussion

Mindful Monthlies is a novel app developed to help adolescents manage menstrual pain. In this study, we designed the app and conducted usability testing. To our knowledge, this is the first mhealth intervention designed with and for adolescents with menstrual pain. Strengths of our study were the UCD, iterative process, and inclusion of patient advisors. We also documented the process and examined considerations when working with youth and industry partners. Our findings underscore challenges of multi-stakeholder app development. Based on our experiences, we present recommendations and have developed a checklist for research teams collaborating with adolescents and industry partners in app development.

Few health-related apps for teens have been developed with adolescents (Majeed-Ariss et al., 2015) with no user-informed apps in the area of adolescent menstrual pain (Trépanier et al., 2023). Results from our usability cycles (Phase 2) indicated that adolescents positively appraised the usability of the interface, design, and content of our app. Although we experienced challenges with inconsistent goals across stakeholders as reported in Phase 3, which has been identified as a risk for poor user satisfaction (Tong et al., 2022), our app was ultimately well-received by adolescents. It is possible that our approach to UCD allowed for optimization of quantity of feedback, such that sufficient feedback was received from adolescents at integral timepoints, allowing the app to address user needs. For instance, we incorporated nine patient advisors group throughout the entire process, while also recruiting 22 participants to provide feedback on iterations of the app. Fewer changes were requested by adolescents as the development progressed, pointing to improvements with each iteration, and aligning with recommendations that two to three usability cycles are sufficient for app refinement (Jibb et al., 2017; Macefield, 2009).

As is common in app design, some requested additions were not possible. Requested such as gamification and personalized profiles were cost prohibitive and outside the scope of the project. Such components aimed at increasing engagement are commonly requested by adolescents in app development (Kenny et al., 2016), and should be considered in future iterations of our app.

We identified several considerations when working with industry to develop apps for adolescent end-users. Although several of these recommendations are consistent with best practice for app development and UCD, the additional challenges of balancing adolescent and industry needs warrant further focus. We discuss these considerations below and developed an accompanying checklist (Fig. 3).

Checklist of considerations when working with adolescents and industry partners in app development.

As a condition disproportionately affecting girls/women and gender-diverse individuals, failing to address menstrual pain leads to health disparities for these groups. Throughout our process, the content area itself was rarely a focus of discussion with the app developers. We are unclear whether perceptions of stigma or embarrassment commonly associated with the topic of menstruation would have been experienced if the adolescents and app developer had connected directly; however, our team's efforts to build trusting relationships with patient advisors was noted as valuable. We ensured that the selected app developer had mhealth experience and an openness to this topic to minimize potential stigma-related concerns.

Our efforts to decrease marginalization were largely focused on ensuring that adolescent voices were incorporated. Most notably, the research team took on a role as advocates for adolescent needs throughout the process. Prior work has emphasized understanding the power imbalance among stakeholders and prioritizing end-user needs (Noorbergen et al., 2021; Birnie et al., 2025). Best practice in patient-engaged research would suggest that patients should have been in meetings with the app developer, and challenges of not having end-users and app developers connect have been discussed by others (Tong et al., 2022). Scheduling limitations were a major motivator for separate meetings, as the app developer and research team met during work hours, which overlapped with school hours. To include the adolescent voice in discussions, we needed to be creative. This included bringing quotes from adolescents to the app developer, using frequencies to highlight that views were held by multiple adolescents, and emphasizing that requests came from adolescent feedback, not the researchers' preference.

There may be value when establishing contracts with industry partners for an adolescent-focused app to exploring availability to meet outside business hours. Nevertheless, our patient partner expressed hesitation speaking directly to the app developer, even if schedules permitted. This hesitation appeared linked to age and feeling underqualified. Balancing patient partner comfort with best practice remains essential. Early discussions to understand advisors' willingness to engage with app developers and needs to support their role as advisors may be beneficial. If hesitation in sharing perspectives with app developers or feelings of intimidation are present, there may be value to providing training to patient advisors to assist them in preparing for and navigating discussions with industry. Recruiting individuals open to interacting with industry may also be an option, but may overlook needs of certain groups.

Clarifying mutual understanding of roles, timelines, budget, and language between the research team and the app developer is necessary and these conversations need to come early. We would have benefitted from upfront discussion with the app developer around how decisions and changes were prioritized. Cost, ultimately, was the primary driver for all decisions, but could not always be predicted until the work was completed. We learned the importance of ensuring that the app developer understood the limitations of research funding (e.g., grant timeline, fixed budget). Moreover, there are many approaches to user integration in design which may lead to disconnects when speaking with app developers, especially developers with prior research involvement; past experiences may be used as a benchmark for how the process should unfold. Collaboration with researchers equipped to develop apps or mHealth centres embedded within university infrastructure are an alternative to industry collaborations. This approach may facilitate familiarity with research processes and funding requirements, and may offer opportunities for more flexibility in the use of funding (e.g., move at pace of research team, prioritize user feedback) (Chettri et al., 2022).

Limitations

Although efforts were made to recruit diverse youth, we had limited contribution from gender diverse individuals. The process was conducted in Canada and although our participants and patient advisors were culturally diverse, a Western lens was brought to this project. Additionally, integration of formalized equity approaches in health design could have augmented our equity focus (Voorheis et al., 2024). Similarly, we did not use standardized usability questions (e.g., Nielsen, 1994) and doing so may have improved our usability findings. Additionally, participants were not provided all content until Round 3 of testing and earlier review of content may have resulted in further adjustments. Our app was only developed for Apple iOS due to cost limitations, which limited who could participate in Round 3 testing. Lastly, we acknowledge that our team's approach and the approach of our industry partner are likely unique, and that any alternative combination of researcher and industry members may have led to different outcomes.

Conclusion

Using UCD, we worked with an industry partner to design an app for adolescents with menstrual pain. Our findings point to a well-received app that is user friendly and relevant for Western adolescents with menstrual pain. Lessons from our process reflections underscore the need for early conversations among researchers and industry partners for a mutual understanding of expectations, and opportunities for engagement of adolescents through multiple avenues in this type of mHealth UCD.
