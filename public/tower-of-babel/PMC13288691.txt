Novel approach to the codesign of a tailored intervention bundle: applying theory with pragmatism
Authors: Surakshya Pokharel, Onyebuchi Omodon, Douglas Woodhouse, Leah Ferrie, Diane Duncan, Ashi Mehta, Anshula Ambasta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTheory-based design of tailored intervention bundles to address low-value care is an evolving science. Codesign approaches are increasingly being used to engage end-users in implementation projects. This manuscript outlines a pragmatic, theory-informed approach to codesigning an intervention bundle with physicians to address laboratory test overuse in hospitals.MethodsWe used a qualitative approach to collect data during virtual audit and group feedback sessions based on the Calgary Audit and Feedback Framework between January 2021 and June 2021 across four hospitals in Calgary, Canada. Sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed, and detailed field notes were taken. In introductory sessions, participants identified barriers, facilitators, and intervention ideas for laboratory test overuse in hospitals. In follow-up sessions, they contextualised ideas using a patient-journey map and prioritised them with a matrix. We used the Theoretical Domains Framework to analyse barriers and facilitators and applied behaviour change techniques (BCT) to map physician-generated interventions to theory-based implementation strategies.ResultsWe conducted twelve sessions with 83 physicians representing family medicine and internal medicine specialties. Our pragmatic codesign approach with physicians emphasised assessing clinical stability when considering ordering practices and generated intervention ideas focused on stable patients. Physician-generated intervention ideas were mapped to the BCT taxonomy to inform the development of a tailored behavioural change intervention bundle to reduce overutilisation of laboratory testing in hospitals, consisting of (1) policy changes, (2) order system modifications, (3) education, and (4) audit and feedback.ConclusionsOur approach allowed the recognition of context-specific barriers and facilitators, engaged end-users to codevelop intervention ideas that addressed the identified barriers, and led to the development of a tailored multicomponent intervention bundle. This approach emulated effective application of BCTs to systematically identified barriers and facilitators. Further research is required to adapt this approach to other contexts to determine generalisability and to evaluate the effectiveness of this intervention bundle.Keywords: Implementation science, Healthcare quality improvement, Safety culture, Qualitative research

Background

Designing effective behaviour change interventions requires identifying barriers, selecting context-specific intervention components, applying theory and encouraging meaningful end-user engagement.1,5 Meaningful end-user engagement is defined as ‘an explicit and measurable responsibility’,6 such as contributing to the development of an intervention plan itself. Codesign approaches support this by positioning end-users as active collaborators, who help to codesign the intervention, thereby ensuring that the intervention aligns with their needs.7 Despite the benefits of codesign approaches, end-user engagement often occurs retrospectively when intervention plans are already developed.8 Although codesign frameworks exist in the literature that emphasise shared decision-making, incorporate user experiences and develop improved services,9 there is a gap in understanding how we can effectively engage end-users prospectively.

Behaviour change interventions are coordinated sets of activities designed to shift specified patterns of behaviours and are composed of behaviour change techniques (BCTs)—the smallest replicable part of an intervention that retains its proposed mechanism of change.10 11 BCTs can be applied individually or in combination with other BCTs11 but differences may exist between the BCTs used in implementation versus de-implementation interventions.12,15 De-implementation interventions aim to reduce the frequency of low-value clinical behaviours,16 such as the over-ordering of routine laboratory tests in hospitals, regardless of clinical indication.17,19 Overuse of laboratory tests causes patient harm including hospital-acquired anaemia leading to longer hospital stays and increased mortality;20 21 adds to healthcare providers’ cognitive load with the need to review high volumes of test results and wastes healthcare resources.21,24 The challenge of laboratory test overuse presents an opportunity to apply BCTs to design an intervention, beginning with identification of barriers and facilitators of appropriate test use in collaboration with physicians using a theoretically driven validated framework,1,525 like the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF). The TDF synthesises constructs from various behaviour change theories into 14 key domains,31,33 accounting for both individual behaviours and environmental factors, thus making it useful in de-implementation efforts.13 Furthermore, linking TDF domains to specific BCTs31,35 using the Theory And Technique Tool (TATT) (https://theoryandtechniquetool.humanbehaviourchange.org/tool)36 allows for the development of tailored behaviour change interventions that are grounded in theory and evidence.

We collaborated with physicians using a pragmatic codesign process to codevelop a tailored intervention bundle targeting laboratory test overuse in hospitals. This manuscript outlines our codesign process and demonstrates, through a comprehensive analysis of identified barriers and facilitators using the TDF and by linking BCTs to barriers via the TATT,36 that the codesigned intervention ideas align with a theory-based approach to intervention bundle design.

Methods

Study design

We followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines37 (online supplemental Additional File 1) to provide rigour to this study. Our study was conducted in the context of a concurrent multisite quality improvement project to reduce the repetitive use of routine laboratory testing for patients under internal medicine and family medicine (hospitalists) physicians across four adult hospitals in Calgary.38 Physicians received system-generated reports to their secure institutional email account, showing their daily average utilisation of routine laboratory tests relative to their specialty-specific and site-specific peers,38 and an additional recruitment email, inviting them to participate in an hour-long peer-facilitated virtual audit and group feedback session to discuss their reports. A second updated report was emailed in 2 months, followed by another recruitment email requesting physicians to participate in a follow-up 1-hour virtual session. The sessions were site-specific and conducted on Zoom.39 The sessions included physician participants, at least one physician facilitator (AA, female internist; DW, male hospitalist) and at least two other team members.

Codesign sessions

The sessions were guided by O’Cathain’s ‘Cocreation, Coproduction, and Codesign Partnership Approach’4 to intervention development and embedded within facilitated audit and group feedback sessions based on the Calgary Audit and Feedback Framework (CAFF).40 41 The CAFF is theoretically and behaviourally grounded, drawing on implementation science frameworks, educational feedback theory, and behaviour change principles to structure discussions that translate insights into clinician action.41 The CAFF and O’Cathain’s approach informed session prompts, facilitation and prioritisation exercises, ensuring that participant input was actively mapped to theory-informed constructs and translated into actionable operational changes. Table 1 provides an overview of the structure and content of these sessions. Mentimeter,42 a web-based polling and visualisation tool, was used by participants in introductory sessions, to record their ideas of barriers to and facilitators for appropriate laboratory test ordering. Based on the concepts discussed in the initial sessions, we presented participants with a patient-journey process map (figure 1) that differentiated unstable and stable patients during the follow-up sessions. The patient-journey process map prompted participants to reflect on laboratory testing practices throughout hospitalisation and prioritise the proposed ideas using a visual prioritisation matrix (figure 2). Participants chose to develop strategies focusing on stable patients due to variation in laboratory needs across hospitalisation trajectories of unstable patients. Miro,43 a web-based group-brainstorming platform, was used to visually map ideas. All sessions were audio-recorded, and detailed field notes were taken.

Data analysis

OO and LF listened to and familiarised themselves with the session recordings, which were transcribed simultaneously using Microsoft Office44 transcription, reviewed to correct transcription errors, anonymised and coded in NVivo 12.45 Transcripts were not returned to participants but were reviewed by our physician facilitators. Transcripts, Mentimeter42 responses, Zoom39 chat and field notes were coded as ‘barriers’, ‘facilitators’ and ‘generated intervention ideas’ by LF and OO. OO, SP, and AA then organised each barrier and facilitator according to the TDF and generated belief statements at each domain with supporting quotes, which were then reviewed by the research team. A specific belief reflects how a domain influences behaviour, and a belief statement highlights common barriers and facilitators across participants. The data was then categorised into broader themes representing barriers and facilitators to appropriate use of routine laboratory tests by OO, AA, and SP using the TATT36 to find links between the TDF domains of identified barriers and facilitators, generated intervention ideas, and associated BCTs and their mechanisms of action (MoA) within the TDF domains. Participant-generated intervention ideas were mapped onto the listed BCT components and consecutively linked to the specific TDF domains related to identified barriers. The TATT36 uses colour-coded cells to show the strength of links between BCTs and TDF domains; only strongly linked BCTs were selected. We then grouped intervention ideas into a behaviour change bundle using four categories from a published systematic review46 on strategies to reduce laboratory test overuse.

Results

We conducted 12 virtual codesign sessions (eight introductory and four follow-up) including 83 unique internist or hospitalist participants. Each session included between two and twenty-five participants. The recording quality of sessions seven and nine was inadequate for complete transcription; thus, we only coded available field notes and portions of transcripts. In addition to barriers and facilitators, an additional concept that emerged from the initial discussions was the concept of differentiating stable and unstable patients since opportunities to reduce low-value testing are most appropriate in stable patients “… if you don't know they [patients] are stable, sometimes you just end up ordering labs for everybody just to like get the lay of the land… even if they didn't need them” (P1, Session 2). We used this information to structure prioritisation of intervention ideas in the follow-up sessions.

Identification of barriers and facilitators

The identified barriers and facilitators were organised according to the relevant TDF domains, accompanied by belief statements and illustrative quotes for each relevant domain (online supplemental Additional File 2). Based on the table data, we identified overall themes representing six barriers and four facilitators which we discuss below with the associated TDF domains italicised in brackets. Codevelopment of behaviour change intervention bundle with physicians

Participant-generated intervention ideas were collated and summarised on a prioritisation matrix where participants ranked all ideas as potentially of ‘High Benefit’ (figure 2). We further mapped the intervention ideas to key domains for specific barriers and identified 10 BCTs: (1) pros and cons, (2) comparative imagining of future outcomes, (3) social support (unspecified), (4) demonstration of behaviour, (5) avoidance/reducing exposure to cues for the behaviour, (6) conserving mental resources, (7) instruction on how to perform behaviour, (8) restructuring the social environment, (9) prompts/cues and (10) feedback on behaviour. Participant-generated ideas aligned with the analysed barriers and facilitators (table 2), demonstrating that our approach had allowed participants to intuitively move through the phases of identifying barriers and facilitators and collaboratively developing targeted behaviour change interventions. We then organised the individual ideas into a behaviour change intervention bundle (figure 3) comprising four categories: (1) policy changes, (2) modification of EMR systems, (3) education, (4) audit and feedback.

Discussion

We have described a novel and pragmatic approach to the codesign of a de-implementation intervention bundle and demonstrated that the codesign process emulates a validated theory-based approach of intervention design.31,34 Our description of a pragmatic codesign approach with end-users that incorporates the previously described steps of (1) defining the target behaviour, (2) using a theoretical framework to identify barriers and facilitators to desired change, (3) applying BCTs to overcome identified barriers and (4) using these learnings to develop an intervention bundle that can be implemented and tested in a spread and scaled fashion1 30 (currently underway)47 is new. Furthermore, the novelty of our study lies in the structured, theory-informed integration of codesign methods in real-world clinical settings, an approach shown to improve the relevance and applicability of research outputs by embedding perspectives of provider groups within theoretically guided processes.7 Structured codesign approaches that integrate research evidence with provider group insights and behavioural theory enhance the rigour of intervention development and demonstrate the potential for meaningful translation into practice, aligning with growing evidence in the codesign and implementation science literature.48 The intervention ideas we arrived at through the codesign process reflect the ones identified in a systematic review,46 thus demonstrating the strength of our approach.

The prospective integration of codesign with end-users starting at the early stages of intervention development resulted in tailored implementation strategies that were context-specific and responsive to physicians’ needs, encouraging early participant buy-in. Prior research has demonstrated the importance of incorporating feedback from end-users to improve the feasibility and acceptability of interventions.1 This proactive input contrasts with retrospective input, which can delay implementation impact.8 Engaging end-users early helps to incentivise, enable, and sustain the adoption of the intervention plan, increasing the relevance and practicality of the intervention in real-world settings.49 Our approach aligns with key principles of codesign such as inclusiveness, genuine participation, ownership, responsiveness, iterative participation, and outcome-focused development.50

While prior research has explored reasons for inappropriate utilisation of laboratory tests among inpatients,51 52 factors that promote appropriate utilisation of tests53 54 and the effectiveness of interventions to reduce laboratory care,46 55 56 engaging physicians early in codesign enabled us to identify novel and contextual elements. For example, while education is a known facilitator of appropriate test ordering,4655,60 our participants emphasised the need for targeted education (eg focused on the consequences of over-ordering) and noted residents may over-order to show their clinical knowledge, thus impacting physician metrics. This highlights a bidirectional influence and the importance of promoting appropriate test use across all career stages. While other studies have often described system design changes to reduce ordering,51 our participants additionally proposed ideas such as differentiating stable versus unstable patients, aligning and reconciling test ordering with care transitions, improving EMR interfaces to include prior test information and allowing lab add-ons to previously drawn blood samples, and providing visual tools to support patient education and to improve communication of test rationale and results, aligning with the importance of patient-centred communication and shared decision-making.61 In addition to previously described health system barriers that contribute to low-value care,62,64 our study surfaces novel insights into the contribution of structural factors like frequent patient care handovers and the importance of considering patient stability for intervention design. These insights may be generalisable to overuse in other areas, such as imaging, where similar barriers—time pressure, system design, and patient expectations—are also seen.63

Our study adds to the limited evidence base on the use of theoretical frameworks in de-implementation strategies13 and demonstrates how the BCT taxonomy can be applied to de-implementation interventions supporting clinicians’ behaviour change. Among the BCTs we proposed, restructuring the social environment has not previously been reported in implementation interventions but shows strong associations with successful behaviour change in de-implementation interventions.16 This BCT may encounter resistance and require additional resources and leadership to be effective.16 We recommend this BCT to address barriers related to health system organisation, in alignment with the policy change strategies included in our intervention bundle, and add to the evidence for the importance of the social environment in de-implementation studies. In embedding this study within the facilitated audit and group feedback session of an existing project, we demonstrate how system improvement projects can inform continuous improvement of intervention bundles by collaborating with end-users.

Limitations

Participants were recruited from two specialties, and not all members within these specialties participated in the sessions. The study was limited to one city, which restricts the generalisability of findings to other geographic regions and patient populations. However, the perspectives of many physicians were captured, lending strength to the findings. Although we used an expert-developed online tool36 to explore evidence for possible BCT and MoA links, these empirical links remain limited and further research is warranted to understand the underlying mechanisms of behaviour change.35 Furthermore, the study focused on de-implementing in-hospital laboratory tests; further research is needed to assess the approach’s generalisability to other topics. While at the time of the study, laboratory ordering was primarily done by attending physicians and residents, advanced practice providers (eg nurse practitioners or physician assistants) are increasingly playing a larger role and should be engaged in subsequent iterations of intervention design, development, and refinement. We considered physician engagement an intentional and necessary first phase in facilitating planned behaviour change. However, laboratory test overuse is embedded within complex adaptive systems, encompassing organisational culture, accountability structures, and workflow processes that interact with individual ordering behaviours.64,66 Accordingly, it is essential to engage collaborators across the broader healthcare system to inform and refine intervention strategies. Nevertheless, the proposed intervention ideas incorporate strategies with demonstrated effectiveness in reducing laboratory test overutilisation.46

In subsequent phases, we have engaged a diverse group of collaborators, including nurses, health authority representatives, laboratory technicians, patients, and patient partners, to refine our interventional ideas and to ensure alignment with the local context.67 As next steps, our team is working with collaborators across multiple hospitals to design, develop, and implement context-specific intervention bundles.47 67 Insights from physician participants directly informed real-world modifications in several hospitals, including the ability to add tests to previously drawn bloodwork, automated reminders for repeat orders, and a new limit restricting routine lab orders to 3 days. This is one of many examples of the changes that have emerged from this current study, and future research will assess the cumulative impact of these modifications and evaluate their impact.

Conclusions

We have described a novel and practical approach to codesigning an intervention bundle with end-user engagement and demonstrated systematically how that process simulates the theory-based development of a behaviour change intervention bundle based on implementation science theory. Future work should apply this approach in other settings, incorporate it into continuous improvement cycles that effectively engage end-users, and evaluate the impact of codesigned interventions.
