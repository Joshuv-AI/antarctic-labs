Analysis and numerical simulations of a landfast ice model
Felix Brandt, Carolin Mehlmann

Abstract
. In this manuscript, we consider a common modeling framework for Arctic landfast ice based on the work of Lemieux et al. [ 27 ] , which is designed for use in large-scale climate models. This approach extends the classical viscous–plastic sea-ice model introduced by Hibler [ 18 ] , which remains the most used model for simulating large-scale sea-ice dynamics in climate science. In particular, landfast ice refers to sea-ice that is attached to the coastline or grounded and therefore exhibits nearly vanishing motion. We present a rigorous analytical and numerical study of this landfast ice model. The main analytical contributions are the local strong well-posedness, the global strong well-posedness in the absence of external forces and for initial data close to constant equilibrium solutions, and the existence of time-periodic solutions. Complementing the analysis, we perform numerical simulations that illustrate key qualitative differences between landfast ice and classical viscous–plastic sea-ice models. In particular, the simulations reveal the formation of stationary equilibrium states characterized by vanishing ice velocity. These observations are consistent with the global-in-time existence result close to equilibria established in Theorem 4.1 as well as the time-periodic result in Theorem 5.2 . The combined analytical and numerical results provide new insight into the structure, stability, and long-term behavior of landfast ice dynamics.

1.
Introduction

Sea-ice in polar regions plays a central role in mediating the exchange of heat and momentum between the ocean and the atmosphere, thereby influencing the climate system as a whole
[
43
]
.
The sea-ice cover typically consists of several regimes, which can be divided into landfast ice, pack ice, and the marginal ice zone.
In contrast to pack ice, which is transported by winds and ocean currents, landfast ice (also referred to as fastice in the sequel) remains stationary along coastlines or over shallow seabeds
[
31
]
.
In contrast, the marginal ice zone marks the transition from the pack ice regime to the open ocean, ice floes dynamically interact with each other
[
44
]
.

In this manuscript, we consider a common framework based on the work of Lemieux et al.
[
27
]
to represent Arctic landfast ice in large-scale climate models.
This approach to model Arctic landfast ice is based on a modification of the viscous–plastic sea-ice model introduced by Hibler
[
18
]
.
The model of Hibler remains the standard for simulating large-scale sea-ice dynamics in climate models
[
4
]
, where sea-ice is typically represented as a continuous viscous–plastic (VP) material.
Recent modeling studies
[
27
,
23
]
introduced a basal stress parameterization together with a modification of sea-ice tensile strength within the VP framework to account for anchoring effects in shallow regions.
This combination has been shown to successfully reproduce the spatial distribution of fastice in several Arctic coastal areas.

In this paper, for the first time, we present a rigorous analysis of a suitable regularization of the landfast ice model introduced by Lemieux et al.
[
27
]
.
In fact, we show that for suitable initial data that especially lie in
\mathrm{C}^{1}
in space, the regularized landfast ice model admits a unique, local-in-time, strong solution.
Moreover, for initial data close to constant equilibria, and in the absence of external forcing terms, we show the global strong well-posedness, and we discuss the existence of time-periodic strong solutions for small time-periodic forcing terms.
In addition, we provide numerical simulations, revealing the difference of landfast ice with viscous-plastic sea-ice
[
18
]
.

The investigation of sea-ice has attracted a lot of attention in recent years in both, the numerical and mathematical analysis.
For numerical studies in the context of Hibler’s viscous-plastic sea-ice model, we refer, e.g., to the works
[
48
,
28
,
21
,
34
,
41
,
33
,
47
,
42
]
.
An elastic-viscous-plastic (EVP) sea-ice model was introduced in
[
20
]
in order to reduce the computational costs.
Concerning numerical studies of
landfast ice
, recently, a hybrid particle-continuum method for the simulation of landfast ice by means of subgrid iceberg interaction has been developed in
[
32
]
.

The
rigorous
mathematical analysis of sea-ice started only very recently.
In
[
9
]
, local strong well-posedness and global strong well-posedness close to constant equilibria for a fully parabolic regularized version of Hibler’s model were obtained.
At the same time, the local strong well-posedness to a different regularization was established in
[
29
]
.
A Lagrangian approach paved the way for the existence of a local-in-time strong solution to Hibler’s parabolic-hyperbolic model in
[
8
]
, emphasizing the hyperbolicity of the balance laws as in
[
29
]
while employing a weaker regularization of the stress tensor as in
[
9
]
.
Motivated by
[
20
]
, the global well-posedness of the EVP sea-ice model with additional Voigt-regularization was shown in
[
6
]
.
Well-posedness for different regularizations of Hibler’s model was also studied in
[
12
,
11
,
30
]
.
The existence and uniqueness of a weak solution to the momentum equation in Hibler’s model with local cut-off was established in
[
16
]
.
In
[
14
]
, by introducing a broader solution concept, the authors showed the existence of energy-driven solutions to the momentum balance in Hibler’s sea-ice model, especially addressing the singular limit in the stress tensor.
The analysis from
[
9
]
was also extended to the situation of time-periodic external forcing terms with applications to time-periodic wind forces
[
10
]
, see also
[
7
, Sec. 7.2]
, and the analysis of a coupled atmosphere-sea-ice-ocean model
[
3
]
.

To the best knowledge of the authors, the present article is the first one addressing the rigorous analysis of landfast ice.
For the analysis of a related problem on shallow grounded ice sheets, we refer to
[
36
]
.

We now describe the approach in the present paper in more detail.
In order to tackle the well-posedness, we first introduce the so-called
fastice operator
.
Compared to the analysis in
[
9
]
, we need to account for the adjusted rheology that differs from the one in Hibler’s viscous-plastic model by the tensile strength as specified in (
2.3
).
We then employ theory for parabolic boundary value problems
[
15
]
based on ellipticity properties of this differential operator as well as its
\mathrm{L}^{q}
-realization subject to Dirichlet boundary conditions.
This leads to the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity of the
\mathrm{L}^{q}
-realization of the fastice operator and then also the linearized operator matrix associated with the landfast ice model.
For the well-posedness, we employ quasilinear methods based on maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity theory
[
1
,
38
]
.
In particular, this requires us to establish nonlinear estimates of the basal stress
f_{\mathrm{b}}
that is introduced in (
2.6
).
Invoking time weights, we are able to lower the regularity of the initial data and exploit instantaneous regularization of the solution.

With regard to the global well-posedness close to equilibria, we make use of the generalized principle of linearized stability
[
40
]
to deal with the fact that zero lies in the spectrum of the operator matrix due to the Neumann Laplacian operators in the regularized balance laws.
We especially manage to handle the case of tensile strength parameter
k_{\mathrm{t}}\equiv 1
, which induces a smallness condition on the regularization parameter in the rheology.

For the existence of time-periodic solutions, we capitalize on the Arendt-Bu theorem
[
2
]
, providing a characterization of the so-called maximal periodic
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity in terms of maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity and a spectral condition on the semigroup.
At this stage, to handle the lack of invertibility, we introduce a new ground space, where for the
h
- and
A
-component, we consider the space of functions with spatial average zero.
In this case, we also discuss the situation of
k_{\mathrm{t}}\equiv 1
.

This article is organized as follows.
In
Section 2
, we introduce the governing equations of the landfast ice model from
[
27
]
.

Section 3
is dedicated to the local-in-time strong well-posedness of the regularized landfast ice model as asserted in
Theorem 3.1
, and to the required underlying linear theory and nonlinear estimates.
In addition, we discuss the situation of hyperbolic balance laws, i.e., the case without parabolic regularization in the equations for the mean ice thickness and the ice concentration.

Section 4
is concerned with the global strong well-posedness close to equilibrium solutions in the absence of external forcing terms, while in
Section 5
, we prove the existence of a time-periodic strong solution.
Finally, in
Section 6
, we present the numerical simulations, revealing key differences between landfast ice and viscous-plastic sea-ice, and reflecting the global existence result close to equilibria in
Theorem 4.1
and the existence of a time-periodic solution for sufficiently small time-periodic wind forces as shown in
Theorem 5.2
.
The required analytical tools such as theory for parabolic boundary value problems, quasilinear existence theory, the generalized principle of linearized theory, and the Arendt-Bu theorem are collected in
Appendix A
.

2.
Governing equations of the landfast ice model

Let
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded domain with boundary
\partial G
of class
\mathrm{C}^{2}
and
(0,T)
, with
0<T\leq\infty
, the time interval on which we consider the sea-ice evolution.
By
x
,
y
,
t
, we denote the horizontal spatial coordinates and the time, respectively.
The sea-ice motion is described by the following three variables: the sea-ice concentration
A=A(x,y,t)\colon G\times(0,T)\to[0,1]
(the fraction of a grid cell covered with ice), the mean ice thickness
h=h(x,y,t)\colon G\times(0,T)\to[0,\infty)
, and the sea-ice velocity
\bm{v}=\bm{v}(x,y,t)\colon G\times(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The sea-ice concentration and sea-ice thickness evolve over time via transport equations, where we include artificial diffusion terms to simplify the analysis in a first step.
We will also elaborate on the possibility of omitting these diffusion terms in a second step, see
Subsection 3.3
.
For
d_{\mathrm{h}}
,
d_{\mathrm{A}}>0
, the regularized balance laws are given by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h+\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{v}h)

\displaystyle=d_{\mathrm{h}}\Delta h,

\displaystyle\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times(0,T),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A+\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{v}A)

\displaystyle=d_{\mathrm{A}}\Delta A,

\displaystyle\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times(0,T),

while the velocity is determined from the momentum equation

(2.2)

\rho h\bigl(\partial_{t}\bm{v}+(\bm{v}\cdot\nabla)\bm{v}\bigr)=f_{\mathrm{c}}+f_{\mathrm{sh}}+f_{\mathrm{o}}+f_{\mathrm{a}}+f_{\mathrm{b}}+f_{\sigma},\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times(0,T).

where
\rho>0
is the (constant) sea-ice density.
The forcing terms on the right-hand side of (
2.2
) are introduced in the sequel.
Note that the convective term
(\bm{v}\cdot\nabla)\bm{v}
will be neglected in the numerical simulations in
Section 6
.
In the above, the term

f_{\sigma}=\mathrm{div}\,\bm{\sigma}

represents the sea-ice rheology, which models the relation of the internal sea-ice stresses
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{v},A,h)
and the strain rates

\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\bm{v}+\nabla\bm{v}^{\top})=\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})I\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2},

where
I\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
denotes the identity matrix.
Following Lemieux et al.
[
27
]
, we consider a modified version of the viscous-plastic rheology to model landfast ice.
To make the notation more compact, we introduce the rheology in terms of the trace and deviatoric parts of the strain rate tensor, i.e.,

(2.3)

\bm{\sigma}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\zeta\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}(\bm{v})+\zeta(\tr(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}(\bm{v})))I-\frac{P^{\prime}}{2}I,

The viscosity
\zeta
and the pressure term
P^{\prime}
are given by

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{P+T}{2\max(\Delta_{P}(\bm{v}),\Delta_{\min})},\kern 5.0pt\text{where}\kern 5.0pt\Delta_{P}(\bm{v})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}:\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}\kern 5.0pt\text{as well as}\kern 5.0pt\Delta_{\min}=2\times 10^{-9},\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle P^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq P-T.

In the above, the “
:
” in
\Delta_{P}(\bm{v})
denotes the Frobenius inner product between tensors, and
e\geq 1
is the ratio of the main axes of the elliptic yield curve.
Following
[
23
]
, the ice strength
P
and the tensile strength
T
are modeled as

(2.4)

P=h\,P^{*}\exp(-c^{*}(1-A))-T,\qquad T=k_{\mathrm{t}}h\,P^{*}\exp(-c^{*}(1-A))=k_{\mathrm{t}}P,

where
P^{*}>0
is an ice strength parameter,
c^{*}>0
is an ice concentration parameter, and
k_{\mathrm{t}}\in[0,1]
is the isotropic tensile strength parameter.
As we will see below, the case
k_{\mathrm{t}}\equiv 1
poses additional analytical difficulties for the long-term dynamics as well as the existence of time-periodic solutions.

To ensure a smooth transition between the viscous closure,
\Delta_{\min}
, and plastic regimes,
\Delta_{P}(\bm{v})
, we follow
[
22
,
34
]
and define the regularized strain rate invariant: by

(2.5)

\Delta(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})\coloneqq\sqrt{\Delta_{P}(\bm{v})+\Delta_{\min}^{2}}.

The atmospheric
f_{\mathrm{a}}
and oceanic drag
f_{\mathrm{o}}
are modeled as

f_{\mathrm{a}}=C_{\mathrm{a}}\rho_{\mathrm{a}}\|\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}\|_{2}\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0ptf_{\mathrm{o}}=C_{\mathrm{o}}\rho_{\mathrm{o}}\|\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{\mathrm{o}}\|_{2}(\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{\mathrm{o}}),

where
C_{\mathrm{a}}
,
C_{\mathrm{o}}>0
are the oceanic and atmospheric drag coefficients,
\rho_{\mathrm{a}}
,
\rho_{\mathrm{o}}>0
are the oceanic and atmospheric densities, and
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}
,
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{o}}
represent the velocity fields of near surface atmospheric and oceanic flows, respectively.

The forces due to the Coriolis force
f_{\mathrm{c}}
and the changing sea surface height,
f_{\mathrm{sh}}
, are given in their common form by

f_{\mathrm{c}}=-\rho hf\mathbf{k}\times\bm{v}\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0ptf_{\mathrm{sh}}=-\rho hg\nabla H_{\mathrm{d}},

with Coriolis parameter
C_{\mathrm{cor}}>0
, the unit upward pointing unit vector
\mathbf{k}
, the gravity constant
g
, and the surface height
H_{\mathrm{d}}
.
We follow a common approach and approximate the changing sea-surface height by

f_{\mathrm{sh}}\approx C_{\mathrm{cor}}\bm{k}\times\bm{v}_{\mathrm{o}}.

The basal stress
f_{\mathrm{b}}
is given by

(2.6)

f_{\mathrm{b}}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }h\leq h_{\mathrm{crit}},\\
k_{2}\left(\frac{-\bm{v}}{|\bm{v}|+v_{0}}\right)(h-h_{\mathrm{crit}})\exp\left(-\alpha_{\mathrm{b}}(1-A)\right),&\text{if }h>h_{\mathrm{crit}},\end{cases}

where
h
is the mean thickness in a grid cell (or volume per unit area),
h_{\mathrm{crit}}
the critical mean thickness,
k_{2}
a free parameter that determines the maximum basal stress,
A
the ice concentration,
v_{0}
a small velocity parameter, and
\alpha_{\mathrm{b}}
the basal stress ice concentration parameter.
The inclusion of the basal drag,
f_{\mathrm{b}}
in (
2.2
) indicates that the basal stress is zero when
h\leq h_{\mathrm{crit}}
, or, in other words, that the parameterized ridge is not deep enough to reach the seafloor.

3.
Linear theory and local well-posedness

In this section, we address the local-in-time strong well-posedness of the landfast ice model as introduced in
Section 2
.
After stating the corresponding result,
Theorem 3.1
, in
Subsection 3.1
, we address the linear theory by showing that the differential operator associated with the (regularized) updated strain rate stress tensor from (
2.3
), see also (
2.5
) for the regularization, admits maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-
\mathrm{L}^{q}
-regularity, paving the way for the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity of the complete linearized system.
In
Subsection 3.2
, we then establish suitable nonlinear estimates, where we especially focus on the basal stress
f_{\mathrm{b}}
, and we then use quasilinear methods, see
Appendix A
, to deduce the local well-posedness result.
We finally discuss the local strong well-posedness of the system without parabolic regularization in the balance laws in
Subsection 3.3
.

We start by making precise the functional analytic setting.
In fact, the ground space
\mathrm{X}_{0}
and regularity space
\mathrm{X}_{1}
are given by

(3.1)

\mathrm{X}_{0}\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{q}(G)^{2}\times\mathrm{L}^{q}(G)\times\mathrm{L}^{q}(G)\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{X}_{1}\coloneqq\mathrm{W}^{2,q}(G)^{2}\cap\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,q}(G)^{2}\times\mathrm{W}_{\mathrm{N}}^{2,q}(G)\times\mathrm{W}_{\mathrm{N}}^{2,q}(G),

where
\mathrm{W}_{\mathrm{N}}^{2,q}(G)\coloneqq\{f\in\mathrm{W}^{2,q}(G):\partial_{\nu}f=0\text{ on }\partial G\}
.
For
p
,
q\in(1,\infty)
and
\mu\in(\frac{1}{p},1]
, we also introduce the time trace space
\mathrm{X}_{\gamma,\mu}=(\mathrm{X}_{0},\mathrm{X}_{1})_{\mu-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}},p}
.
If

(3.2)

\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}<\mu,

this space takes the form

\mathrm{X}_{\gamma,\mu}=\mathrm{B}_{qp,\mathrm{D}}^{2(\mu-\frac{1}{p})}(G)^{2}\times\mathrm{B}_{qp,\mathrm{N}}^{2(\mu-\frac{1}{p})}(G)\times\mathrm{B}_{qp,\mathrm{N}}^{2(\mu-\frac{1}{p})}(G),

where the subscripts
D
and
N
indicate Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions, respectively.
Note that (
3.2
) ensures that the trace and the normal derivative are well-defined.
Indeed, if
p
,
q
and
\mu
satisfy (
3.2
), it follows from classical theory, see, e.g.,
[
45
, Thm.4.6.1]
, that there exists
\alpha>0
sufficiently small such that

\mathrm{X}_{\gamma,\mu}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{C}^{1,\alpha}(\overline{G})^{4}.

As we will also see in
Subsection 3.1
, the reason for making this assumption on
p
,
q
and
\mu
is to guarantee that the coefficients of the differential operator are Hölder-continuous, enabling us to prove the boundedness of the
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus and thus also the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity of the fastice operator.

To make sure that the
h
and
A
take values in their physically reasonable ranges, and that the mean ice thickness stays uniformly bounded from below, we define the open subset
V_{\mu}\subset\mathrm{X}_{\gamma,\mu}
by

(3.3)

V_{\mu}\coloneqq\{\bm{u}=(\bm{v},h,A)\in\mathrm{X}_{\gamma,\mu}:h>\kappa\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0ptA\in(0,1+\delta)\}.

Here
\delta>0
was introduced to allow for

, which corresponds to the case of thick ice in the complete control area.
In the sequel, we also use
\bm{u}=(\bm{v},h,A)
to denote the principal variable.

We provide the complete system of equations below.
As we assume that
h\geq\kappa
for a sufficiently small parameter
\kappa>0
, we may divide the momentum equation by the ice mass
\rho h
, leading to

(3.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\bm{v}+(\bm{v}\cdot\nabla)\bm{v}&=\frac{1}{\rho h}\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{\sigma})-C_{\mathrm{cor}}\bm{k}\times(\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{\mathrm{o}})\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{\rho h}\bigl(f_{\mathrm{a}}+f_{\mathrm{o}}(\bm{v})+f_{\mathrm{b}}(\bm{v},h,A)\bigr),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{t}h+\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{v}h)&=d_{\mathrm{h}}\Delta h,&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{t}A+\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{v}A)&=d_{\mathrm{A}}\Delta A,&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times(0,T),\\
\bm{v}=0,\kern 5.0pt\partial_{\nu}h&=\partial_{\nu}A=0,&&\kern 5.0pt\text{on}\kern 5.0pt\partial G\times(0,T),\\

Before stating the first main result of this paper, we invoke the concept of time weights.
In fact, for a time interval
(0,T)
, where

, and for
\mathrm{X}_{i}
,

\mathrm{L}_{\mu}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{i})\coloneqq\left\{f\colon(0,T)\to\mathrm{X}_{i}:t^{1-\mu}f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{i})\right\},\kern 5.0pt\text{with}\kern 5.0pt\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\mu}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{i})}\coloneqq\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|t^{1-\mu}f\|_{\mathrm{X}_{i}}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

\displaystyle\mathrm{W}_{\mu}^{1,p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{0})

\bm{u}_{2}\in\overline{B}_{\mathrm{X}_{\gamma,\mu}}(\bm{u}_{0},r)

\|\bm{u}(\cdot,\bm{u}_{1})-\bm{u}(\cdot,\bm{u}_{2})\|_{\mathbb{E}_{1,\mu}(0,T^{\prime})}\leq c\cdot\|\bm{u}_{1}-\bm{u}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{X}_{\gamma,\mu}},

i.e., the solution
\bm{u}
depends continuously on the initial data.

(2)

For every
\tau\in(0,T^{\prime})
, we find that

\bm{u}\in\mathbb{E}_{1}(\tau,T^{\prime})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{1,1}(\tau,T^{\prime})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{C}([\tau,T^{\prime}];\mathrm{X}_{\gamma}),

so the solution regularizes instantaneously.

(3)

If we assume that
\bm{v}_{a}
,
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{o}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2p}(0,\infty;\mathrm{L}^{2q}(G)^{2})
, then the solution
\bm{u}
exists on a maximal time interval
J(\bm{u}_{0})=[0,t_{+}(\bm{u}_{0}))
that is characterized by the following alternatives:

(a)

global existence, so
t_{+}(\bm{u}_{0})=\infty
,

(b)

\lim_{t\to t_{+}(\bm{u}_{0})}\dist_{\mathrm{X}_{\gamma,\mu}}(\bm{u}(t),\partial V_{\mu})=0
, or

(c)

the limit
\lim_{t\to t_{+}(\bm{u}_{0})}\bm{u}(t)
does not exist in
\mathrm{X}_{\gamma,\mu}
.

3.1.
Linear theory

In this subsection, we address the linear theory.
A key step here is to establish suitable ellipticity properties of the operator associated with the internal ice stress in the landfast ice.
Note that due to the tensile strength
T
as introduced in (
2.4
), it is different from Hibler’s sea-ice model.

For convenience, we briefly recall the regularized stress tensor as introduced in
Section 2
.
By a slight abuse of notation, we still denote the viscosity with the regularized strain rate
\Delta(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})
from (
2.5
) by
\zeta
, i.e.,

\zeta=\frac{\tilde{P}}{2\Delta(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})},\kern 5.0pt\text{where}\kern 5.0pt\tilde{P}\coloneqq P+T=(1+k_{\mathrm{t}})hP^{*}\exp(-c^{*}(1-A)).

The corresponding regularized stress tensor then takes the form

\bm{\sigma}=\frac{1}{2}\zeta\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}(\bm{v})+\zeta(\tr(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}(\bm{v})))I-\frac{P^{\prime}}{2}I,\kern 5.0pt\text{where}\kern 5.0ptP^{\prime}\coloneqq P-T=(1-k_{\mathrm{t}})hP^{*}\exp(-c^{*}(1-A)).

In order to deduce the shape of the quasilinear second order operator arising from
\mathrm{div}\,\bm{\sigma}
, we introduce a matrix
\mathbb{S}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
such that

\mathbb{S}\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}=\begin{pmatrix}\left(1+\frac{1}{e^{2}}\right)\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{11}+\left(1-\frac{1}{e^{2}}\right)\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{22}&\frac{1}{e^{2}}\left(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{12}+\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{21}\right)\\
\frac{1}{e^{2}}\left(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{12}+\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{21}\right)&\left(1-\frac{1}{e^{2}}\right)\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{11}+\left(1+\frac{1}{e^{2}}\right)\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

Upon identifying
\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
with the vector
(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{11},\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{12},\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{21},\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{22})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
, the action of
\mathbb{S}
to
\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}
amounts to the multiplication by the matrix

\mathbb{S}=\left(\mathbb{S}_{ij}^{kl}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}1+\frac{1}{e^{2}}&0&0&1-\frac{1}{e^{2}}\\
0&\frac{1}{e^{2}}&\frac{1}{e^{2}}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{e^{2}}&\frac{1}{e^{2}}&0\\
1-\frac{1}{e^{2}}&0&0&1+\frac{1}{e^{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

With
\tilde{S}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}},\tilde{P})\coloneqq\frac{\tilde{P}}{2}\frac{\mathbb{S}\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}}{\Delta(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})}
, the stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
admits the representation
\bm{\sigma}=\tilde{S}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}},\tilde{P})-\frac{P^{\prime}}{2}I
.
The
fastice operator

\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}
is then defined by

\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}\bm{v}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\rho h}\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{S}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}},\tilde{P})=\frac{1}{\rho h}\mathrm{div}\,\left(\frac{\tilde{P}}{2}\frac{\mathbb{S}\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}}{\Delta(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})}\right).

Straightforward calculations reveal that

(\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}\bm{v})_{i}\coloneqq-\sum\limits_{j,k,l=1}^{2}\frac{\tilde{P}}{2\rho h}\frac{1}{\Delta(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})}\left(\mathbb{S}_{ij}^{kl}-\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})}(\mathbb{S}\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})_{ik}(\mathbb{S}\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})_{jl}\right)\mathrm{D}_{k}\mathrm{D}_{l}\bm{v}_{j}+\frac{1}{2\rho h\Delta(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{2}(\partial_{j}\tilde{P})(\mathbb{S}\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})_{ij},

where
i=1,2
and
\mathrm{D}_{m}=-\mathrm{i}\partial_{m}
.
The coefficients of the principal part of
\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}
are given by

(3.5)

a_{ij}^{kl}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}},\tilde{P})\coloneqq-\frac{\tilde{P}}{2\rho h}\frac{1}{\Delta(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})}\left(\mathbb{S}_{ij}^{kl}-\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})}(\mathbb{S}\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})_{ik}(\mathbb{S}\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})_{jl}\right).

For
\bm{u}_{0}=(\bm{v}_{0},h_{0},A_{0})\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\overline{G})^{2}\times\mathrm{C}(\overline{G})\times\mathrm{C}(\overline{G})
with
h_{0}\geq\kappa
, the
linearized fastice operator
takes the form

(3.6)

\displaystyle{}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,l=1}^{2}a_{ij}^{kl}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}(\bm{v}_{0}),\tilde{P}(h_{0},A_{0}))\mathrm{D}_{k}\mathrm{D}_{l}\bm{v}_{j}+\frac{1}{2\rho h_{0}\Delta(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}(\bm{v}_{0}))}\sum_{j=1}^{2}(\partial_{j}\tilde{P}(h_{0},A_{0}))(\mathbb{S}\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}(\bm{v}))_{ij}.

We now analyze the linearized fastice operator
\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}(\bm{u}_{0})
from (
3.6
).
In the light of
Appendix A
on the boundedness of the
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus of the
\mathrm{L}^{q}
-realization and
Appendix A
on the Lopatinskii-Shapiro condition, the task is to establish the strong normal ellipticity as made precise in
Appendix A
.
This is what we will address in the following.
We start with the strong ellipticity and the parameter-ellipticity.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\bm{u}_{0}\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\overline{G})^{2}\times\mathrm{C}(\overline{G})\times\mathrm{C}(\overline{G})
be such that
h_{0}\geq\kappa
.
Then for all
x\in\overline{G}
, the principal part of the negative linearized fastice operator
-\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}(\bm{u}_{0})
as introduced in (
3.6
) is strongly elliptic and parameter-elliptic of angle
\phi_{-\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}(\bm{u}_{0})}=0
.

Proof.

First, we recall the principal part of
-\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}(\bm{u}_{0})
given by
-\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}_{\#}(x,\xi)=\sum_{k,l=1}^{2}-a_{ij}^{kl}(x)\xi_{k}\xi_{l}
, where the coefficients
a_{ij}^{kl}
were introduced in (
3.5
).
It can be verified that the
a_{ij}^{kl}
satisfy the symmetries

(3.7)

a_{ij}^{kl}=a_{ji}^{lk}=a_{kl}^{ij}=a_{kj}^{il}=a_{il}^{kj}=a_{lk}^{ji}.

Thus, the symbol of the principal part can be represented as

\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}_{\#}(x,\xi)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}^{11}\xi_{1}^{2}+2a_{11}^{12}\xi_{1}\xi_{2}+a_{11}^{22}\xi_{2}^{2}&a_{11}^{12}\xi_{1}^{2}+(a_{12}^{12}+a_{11}^{22})\xi_{1}\xi_{2}+a_{12}^{22}\xi_{2}^{2}\\
a_{11}^{12}\xi_{1}^{2}+(a_{12}^{12}+a_{11}^{22})\xi_{1}\xi_{2}+a_{12}^{22}\xi_{2}^{2}&a_{11}^{22}\xi_{1}^{2}+2a_{12}^{22}\xi_{1}\xi_{2}+a_{22}^{22}\xi_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

To simplify notation, for
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
, we introduce
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{I}}\coloneqq\bm{d}_{11}+\bm{d}_{22}
,
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{II}}\coloneqq\bm{d}_{11}-\bm{d}_{22}
and
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{III}}\coloneqq\frac{\bm{d}_{12}+\bm{d}_{21}}{2}
.
As in
[
9
, (4.4) and (4.5)]
, we then obtain

(3.8)

\bm{d}^{\top}\mathbb{S}\bm{d}=\bm{d}_{\mathrm{I}}^{2}+\frac{1}{e^{2}}(\bm{d}_{\mathrm{II}}^{2}+4\bm{d}_{\mathrm{III}}^{2})\eqqcolon\triangle^{2}(\bm{d})\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt(\bm{d}^{\top}(\mathbb{S}\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}))^{2}\leq\triangle^{2}(\bm{d})\triangle^{2}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}).

By virtue of
h_{0}\geq\kappa>0
, we find that
\frac{\tilde{P}(h_{0},A_{0})}{2\rho h_{0}\Delta^{3}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})}
is real-valued, bounded, continuous, and bounded from below by a constant
c>0
, so the previous estimate leads to

(3.9)

\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{2}-a_{ij}^{kl}\bm{d}_{ik}\bm{d}_{jl}=\frac{\tilde{P}(h_{0},A_{0})}{2\rho h_{0}\Delta^{3}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})}\bigl(\Delta^{2}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})\bm{d}^{\top}\mathbb{S}\bm{d}-(\bm{d}^{\top}\mathbb{S}\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}})^{2}\bigr)\geq c\Delta_{\min}^{2}\Delta^{2}(\bm{d}).

We now capitalize on (
3.9
) to show the strong ellipticity.
Let
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that
|\xi|=|\eta|=1
, and for
i=1,2
, define
\eta_{i}\coloneqq x_{i}+\mathrm{i}y_{i}
.
Thanks to the symmetries of the coefficients (
3.7
), and using the estimate from (
3.9
) joint with
|\xi|=1
and
|\eta|=1
, which imply
\triangle^{2}(\xi\otimes x)=(\xi\cdot x)^{2}+\frac{1}{e^{2}}|x|^{2}
and
\triangle^{2}(\xi\otimes x)+\triangle^{2}(\xi\otimes y)\geq\frac{1}{e^{2}}

\displaystyle\re(-\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}_{\#}(x,\xi)\eta|\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{2}-a_{ij}^{kl}(\xi\otimes x)_{jl}(\xi\otimes x)_{ik}-a_{ij}^{kl}(\xi\otimes y)_{jl}(\xi\otimes y)_{ik}

\displaystyle\geq c\Delta_{\min}^{2}\bigl((\triangle^{2}(\xi\otimes x)+\triangle^{2}(\xi\otimes y)\bigr)\geq\frac{c\Delta_{\min}^{2}}{e^{2}}.

This shows that
-\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}(\bm{u}_{0})
is strongly elliptic.
By the symmetry of
-\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}_{\#}
, we get
\sigma(-\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}_{\#}(x,\xi))\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
for all

and

Thus,

is parameter-elliptic with
\phi_{-\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{FI}}(\bm{u}_{0})}=0
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\bm{u}_{0}\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\overline{G})^{2}\times\mathrm{C}(\overline{G})\times\mathrm{C}(\overline{G})
be such that

\xi

\displaystyle\re\left(\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{2}-a_{ij}^{kl}(\bm{u}_{0})(\xi_{l}u_{j}-\nu_{l}v_{j})\overline{(\xi_{k}u_{i}-\nu_{k}v_{i})}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0,\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\re\left(\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{2}-a_{ij}^{kl}(\bm{u}_{0})(\xi_{l}u_{j}-\nu_{l}v_{j})\overline{(\xi_{k}u_{i}-\nu_{k}v_{i})}\right)

\displaystyle>0,\kern 5.0pt\text{if}\kern 5.0pt\im(u|v)\neq 0.

Appendix A

Proof.

We consider
x\in\partial G
,
\xi
,
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

|\xi|=|\nu|=1

(\xi|\nu)=0

u

In the sequel, we also write

The symmetries of the

With regard to the second part, we focus on the case of equality with zero in the preceding estimate and infer that

(3.10)

\im(u|v)=\tilde{x}_{1}y_{1}-x_{1}\tilde{y}_{1}+\tilde{x}_{2}y_{2}-x_{2}\tilde{y}_{2}=0.

By

\xi_{2}\neq 0

\xi_{1}\neq 0

Then
(\xi|\nu)=0

\nu_{2}\neq 0

By (

x_{1}=\frac{\nu_{1}}{\xi_{1}}\tilde{x}_{1}

\tilde{x}_{2}=\frac{\xi_{2}}{\nu_{2}}x_{2}

An insertion into (

∎

This subsection is dedicated to proving estimates of the nonlinear terms, and to showing the local strong well-posedness result,

.

3.3.
Remarks on the parabolic-hyperbolic setting

In this section, we briefly elaborate on the situation of the parabolic-hyperbolic setting as also addressed in
[
8
]
, see also
[
29
]
for a different regularization of the stress tensor.

The model without parabolic regularization in the balance laws is given by

(3.18)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\bm{v}+(\bm{v}\cdot\nabla)\bm{v}&=\frac{1}{\rho h}\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{\sigma})-C_{\mathrm{cor}}\bm{k}\times(\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{\mathrm{o}})\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{\rho h}\bigl(f_{\mathrm{a}}+f_{\mathrm{o}}(\bm{v})+f_{\mathrm{b}}(\bm{v},h,A)\bigr),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{t}h+\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{v}h)&=0,&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{t}A+\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{v}A)&=0,&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times(0,T),\\
\bm{v}&=0,&&\kern 5.0pt\text{on}\kern 5.0pt\partial G\times(0,T),\\
\bm{v}(0)=\bm{v}_{0},\kern 5.0pth(0)&=h_{0},\kern 5.0ptA(0)=A_{0},&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG.\end{aligned}\right.

In contrast to the model in (
3.4
), no boundary conditions on

and

are imposed.
The functional analytic setting suited for this problem is as follows:
We define the ground space

and the regularity space

.
In this context, we also introduce the trace space
\mathrm{Y}_{\gamma}=(\mathrm{Y}_{0},\mathrm{Y}_{1})_{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}},p}=\mathrm{B}_{qp,\mathrm{D}}^{2-\nicefrac{{2}}{{p}}}(G)^{2}\times\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(G)\times\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(G)
, where the subscript
D
represents Dirichlet boundary conditions in the situation

.
We remark that this is the threshold of the regularity parameter in the Besov space
\mathrm{B}_{qp}^{2-\nicefrac{{2}}{{p}}}(G)
such that the trace is defined in a strong sense, see, e.g.,
[
45
, Sec. 2.9]
.
Similarly as for the fully parabolic problem, for
\delta>0
sufficiently small, we define the open set
V\subset\mathrm{Y}_{\gamma}
by

(3.19)

V\coloneqq\{\bm{u}=(\bm{v},h,A)\in\mathrm{X}_{\gamma}:h>\kappa\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0ptA\in(0,1+\delta)\}.

We now state the local strong well-posedness result for (
3.18
).

Theorem 3.8
.

Consider
p
,
q\in(1,\infty)
such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}<\frac{1}{2}
, and let
\bm{u}_{0}=(\bm{v}_{0},h_{0},A_{0})\in V
, where

has been defined in (
3.19
).
Moreover, assume that the wind and ocean velocities
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}
and
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{o}}
fulfill
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}
,
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{o}}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2q}(G)^{2})
for all

.
Then there exists

such that (
3.18
) has a unique solution
\bm{u}=(\bm{v},h,A)\in\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(0,T^{\prime};\mathrm{Y}_{0})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T^{\prime};\mathrm{Y}_{1})

Instead of providing the complete proof of

, we sketch the main steps, which are similar to those in
[

]
upon invoking the maximal regularity of

as established in

The first step is to transform the problem to Lagrangian coordinates to circumvent the hyperbolic effects in the balance laws.
Thus, we invoke the characteristics

solving

\partial_{t}X(t,\bm{y})=\bm{v}(t,X(t,\bm{y})),\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptt>0,\kern 5.0ptX(0,\bm{y})=\bm{y},\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0pt\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

the inverse of

.
We then introduce the variables in Lagrangian coordinates
\tilde{\bm{v}}(t,\bm{y})\coloneqq\bm{v}(t,X(t,\bm{y}))
,
\tilde{h}(t,\bm{y})\coloneqq h(t,X(t,\bm{y}))
, and

For

and

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\tilde{\bm{v}}&=\tilde{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{FI}}(\tilde{\bm{u}})\tilde{\bm{v}}-\tilde{B}(\tilde{\bm{u}})\binom{\tilde{h}}{\tilde{A}}-C_{\mathrm{cor}}\bm{k}\times(\tilde{\bm{v}}-\bm{v}_{\mathrm{o}})\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{\rho\tilde{h}}\bigl(f_{\mathrm{a}}+f_{\mathrm{o}}(\tilde{\bm{v}})+f_{\mathrm{b}}(\tilde{\bm{v}},\tilde{h},\tilde{A})\bigr),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times(0,T),\\

Proposition 3.9
.

Suppose that
p
,

satisfy

Then there is

so that (

\tilde{\bm{u}}=(\tilde{\bm{v}},\tilde{h},\tilde{A})\in\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(0,T;\mathrm{Y}_{0})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{Y}_{1})\cap\mathrm{C}([0,T];V)

4.
Global well-posedness close to equilibria

In this section, we investigate the global strong well-posedness of the landfast ice model close to equilibria, and in the absence of external forcing terms.
The corresponding result can be found in
Theorem 4.1
.
The idea is to use the generalized principle of linearized stability as recalled in
Appendix A
.
With regard to the rheology, we distinguish the cases
k_{\mathrm{t}}\in[0,1)
and
k_{\mathrm{t}}\equiv 1
.
In fact, in the former case, in contrast to
[
9
, Thm. 2.3]
, we manage to show the result without assumption on the regularization parameter

, while in the latter case, such a smallness condition seems indispensable.

More precisely, we focus on the situation when

.
The resulting simplified landfast ice problem is given by

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\bm{v}+(\bm{v}\cdot\nabla)\bm{v}&=\frac{1}{\rho h}\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{\sigma}),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times(0,T),\\

\partial_{t}A+\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{v}A)&=d_{\mathrm{A}}\Delta A,&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times(0,T),\\
\bm{v}=0,\kern 5.0pt\partial_{\nu}h&=\partial_{\nu}A=0,&&\kern 5.0pt\text{on}\kern 5.0pt\partial G\times(0,T),\\
\bm{v}(0)=\bm{v}_{0},\kern 5.0pth(0)&=h_{0},\kern 5.0ptA(0)=A_{0},&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG.\end{aligned}\right.

We focus here on the unweighted case, i.e., for
p
,
q\in(1,\infty)
such that

(4.2)

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}<\frac{1}{2},

we consider the trace space

(4.3)

\mathrm{X}_{\gamma}\coloneqq\mathrm{X}_{\gamma,1}=\mathrm{B}_{qp,\mathrm{D}}^{2-\frac{2}{p}}(G)^{2}\times\mathrm{B}_{qp,\mathrm{N}}^{2-\frac{2}{p}}(G)\times\mathrm{B}_{qp,\mathrm{N}}^{2-\frac{2}{p}}(G),

where we recall that the subscripts

and

indicate Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions, respectively.
Analogously, we invoke the open set
V\coloneqq V_{1}
as introduced in (
3.3
).

The simplified right-hand side

capturing the lower-order nonlinearities is given by

-\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{v}h)\\
-\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{v}A)\end{pmatrix}.

Recalling the operator matrix

from (
3.14

\bm{u}=(\bm{v},h,A)

4.4
), or, equivalently, to (
4.1
), i.e.,

.

Consider

,

5.
Time-periodic solutions to the landfast ice model

In this section, we discuss the existence of a time-periodic strong solution to the landfast ice model in the presence of time-periodic forcing terms as stated in
Theorem 5.2
.
A particular application is the presence of time-periodic wind forces as in the simulations shown in
Figure 3
.
The main idea here is to use the Arendt-Bu theorem, see
Appendix A
.
A key difficulty is that in view of the Neumann Laplacian operators in the balance laws, the linearized operator matrix is not invertible.
We thus consider time-periodic solutions as deviations of equilibrium solutions and introduce a modified functional analytic framework involving spaces of functions with average zero as in the proof of
Section 4
.

For a fixed time period
T>0
, and for suitable
T
-periodic external forcing terms
f_{\mathrm{ice}}
,
f_{\mathrm{h}}
as well as

, the landfast ice model in the periodic setting is given by

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\bm{v}+(\bm{v}\cdot\nabla)\bm{v}&=\frac{1}{\rho h}\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{\sigma})-C_{\mathrm{cor}}\bm{k}\times(\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{\mathrm{o}})\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{\rho h}\bigl(f_{\mathrm{a}}+f_{\mathrm{o}}(\bm{v})+f_{\mathrm{b}}(\bm{v},h,A)\bigr)+f_{\mathrm{ice}},&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times\mathbb{R},\\
\partial_{t}h+\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{v}h)&=d_{\mathrm{h}}\Delta h+f_{\mathrm{h}},&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0ptG\times\mathbb{R},\\

\bm{v}=0,\kern 5.0pt\partial_{\nu}h&=\partial_{\nu}A=0,&&\kern 5.0pt\text{on}\kern 5.0pt\partial G\times\mathbb{R},\\
\bm{v}(t)=\bm{v}(t+T),\kern 5.0pth(t)&=h(t+T),\kern 5.0ptA(t)=A(t+T),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptt\in\mathbb{R}.\end{aligned}\right.

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\tilde{\bm{v}}-\frac{1}{\rho(\tilde{h}+h_{*})}\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{\sigma})&=-(\tilde{\bm{v}}\cdot\nabla)\tilde{\bm{v}}-C_{\mathrm{cor}}\bm{k}\times(\tilde{\bm{v}}-\bm{v}_{\mathrm{o}})+\frac{1}{\rho(\tilde{h}+h_{*})}\bigl(f_{\mathrm{a}}\\

We are now in position to prove the main result in this section on the existence of a time-periodic strong solution to the landfast ice model subject to time-periodic forcing terms.

Proof of
Theorem 5.2
.

The proof is based on an application of the abstract result

.

Section 5
implies that the linearized operator

has maximal

and satisfies

Appendix A

In

Numerical simulations

In this section, we examine the influence of tensile strength and basal drag parameterizations on the formation of landfast ice and compare this extended model to the classical viscous–plastic formulation used to describe pack ice.
Moreover, we compare the numerical simulations with the analytical results obtained in the previous sections.

In

is the case of constant (and thus time-periodic) wind forcing and its relation to the analytical result.

The case of a domain of the form as

.
The domain is initially fully covered by sea-ice with varying thickness given by

A(x,y,0)=1,\quad h(x,y,0)=2.5\,\mathrm{m}-\sin\!\left(\frac{x}{512\,\mathrm{km}}\pi\right).

6.1.
A landfast-ice extension of the viscous–plastic sea-ice model

In the standard viscous–plastic sea-ice model (without the basal drag term (
2.6
) and with tensile strength
T=0
in (
2.3
)), the ice is transported in the direction of the wind, leading to a reduction of ice concentration along the left boundary of the domain (see Panel I in
Figure 1
).

In contrast, in the landfast ice configuration, i.e., when the viscous–plastic rheology is augmented by tensile strength (
2.3
) and the momentum equation by a basal drag term (
2.6
), the ice remains stationary near the left boundary (coast). This behavior results from the grounding condition (
h>h_{\mathrm{crit}}
) and the increased resistance of the ice to tensile stresses.

Consequently, an opening in the ice cover forms in the left part of the domain, where the sea-ice concentration drops to nearly zero (see Panel III in
Figure 1
). Such openings, known as polynyas, typically arise under offshore wind forcing, as considered here. To the right of the polynya, the sea-ice concentration increases again and accumulates due to the imposed Dirichlet boundary conditions.

In the standard viscous–plastic model, the ice thickness is transported with the wind in the same way as the ice concentration (see Panel II in
Figure 1
). As a result, the thickness decreases toward the left boundary and accumulates along the right boundary of the domain. The slight decrease in thickness visible in the center of the domain can be attributed to the sinusoidal variation in the initial condition.

In the landfast ice configuration (Panel IV in
Figure 1
), the ice thickness remains anchored near the left boundary, decreases within the opening (polynya), exhibits variations toward the center due to the imposed initial condition, and accumulates again at the right boundary. Compared to the standard viscous–plastic model, the accumulated thickness at the right boundary is smaller in the landfast ice case (compare Panels II and IV in
Figure 1
). This behavior can be attributed to the increased stiffness of the ice in the landfast ice configuration.

This behavior of the sea-ice concentration and thickness is also reflected in the sea-ice velocity field: the velocity vanishes in a strip along the left boundary, increases toward the center of the domain, and decreases again toward the right boundary (see Panel II and Panel VI in
Figure 1
). Owing to the enhanced resistance to tensile stresses and an additional basal drag in the landfast ice formulation, the ice becomes effectively stiffer and accumulates over a broader region along the right boundary compared to the standard viscous–plastic model. This is evident from the wider near-zero velocity region in Panel IV relative to Panel II.

Appendix A
Boundary value problems and quasilinear theory

The purpose of this section is to recall several analytical preliminaries for convenience of the reader.
These concepts comprise parabolic boundary value problems, quasilinear existence theory, the generalized principle of linearized stability for the stability analysis of equilibria, and existence theory for time-periodic evolution equations based on the Arendt-Bu theorem.

First, we recall some theory related to elliptic differential operators as well as the implications for the associated
\mathrm{L}^{q}

For
x\in\Omega

with principal part
\mathcal{A}_{\#}(x,\mathrm{D})
and associated symbol
\mathcal{A}_{\#}(x,\xi)

(A.1)

Definition A.1

Definition A.2

First, we invoke the notion of maximal periodic
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity.

\mathrm{X}_{0}
