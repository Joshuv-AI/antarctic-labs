Urinary sodium, table salt use and incidence of late-onset asthma in females and males: evidence from a large prospective cohort
Authors: Selma Kronsteiner-Gicevic, Alysha S. Thompson, Martina Gaggl, Aedín Cassidy, Nicola P. Bondonno, Charmaine J.M. Lim, Robab Breyer-Kohansal, Tilman Kühn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe contribution of dietary sodium to asthma development remains unclear, particularly for adult-onset disease. We examined associations between estimated 24-h urinary sodium excretion, table salt use and incident asthma in a large prospective cohort.MethodsWe analysed 420 041 adults aged 40–69 years without prior asthma. Incident asthma was ascertained through linked health records. Urinary sodium was estimated using the INTERSALT equation from spot urine samples. Frequency of adding salt at the table was self-reported. Associations were evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models with adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle and clinical factors.ResultsOver a median 10.9-year follow-up, 11 927 participants developed asthma. Compared with the lowest quintile, the highest quintile of estimated urinary sodium was associated with increased asthma incidence (hazard ratio (HR) 1.50, 95% CI 1.41–1.59). Associations were stronger in females (HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.44–1.68) than males (HR 1.28, 95% CI 1.17–1.39; p-heterogeneity=0.0011) and in individuals with low eosinophil counts <300 cells·µL−1 (HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.43–1.66) compared with those ≥300 cells·µL−1 (HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.10–1.40; p-heterogeneity=0.0017). Associations were also stronger in never-smokers (HR 1.64, 95% CI 1.51–1.79) than former (HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.33–1.60) or current smokers (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.00–1.36; p-heterogeneity=0.0008). Frequent table salt use (“always” versus “never/rarely”) was associated with higher asthma incidence (HR 1.28, 95% CI 1.19–1.37), with no meaningful subgroup differences.ConclusionsHigher estimated urinary sodium excretion and frequent table salt use were associated with greater asthma incidence. Stronger associations in women and individuals with low eosinophil counts suggest potential phenotype-specific susceptibility. Randomised studies are warranted to test whether sodium reduction lowers asthma risk in these subgroups.

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways, affecting over 262 million people in 2019 and causing an estimated 461 000 deaths annually [1]. In the UK alone, approximately 5.4 million individuals live with asthma, with more than 1000 deaths each year [2, 3]. Asthma is a heterogeneous condition involving distinct inflammatory pathways, commonly described as type 2 (T2)-associated and non-T2 inflammatory patterns, although these do not fully align with clinical phenotypes in all cases [4, 5]. T2-associated asthma is typically characterised by eosinophilic airway inflammation and elevated biomarkers such as blood or sputum eosinophils and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO), particularly when assessed using repeated measurements [2]. These pathways are linked to cytokines including interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13 in early-onset disease and IL-5 in later-onset asthma [5]. In contrast, non-T2 asthma encompasses a heterogeneous group of phenotypes often associated with neutrophilic or paucigranulocytic airway inflammation, IL-17-related pathways [6, 7], more severe disease and reduced corticosteroid responsiveness [8, 9].

Asthma incidence is influenced by genetic predisposition, early life exposures, allergies, environmental pollutants, occupational hazards and lifestyle factors such as diet [10–13] and obesity [14]. Early-onset asthma is more common in boys [3], whereas late-onset asthma disproportionally affects females [1, 15]. Sex hormones are thought to contribute to this disparity, as testosterone suppresses innate lymphoid cell type 2 activity [16, 17], while oestrogen has been associated with increased asthma severity [18]. Given the global asthma burden and the limited treatment responsiveness of some phenotypes, particularly non-T2 asthma [1, 8–10, 19], identifying modifiable risk factors for primary prevention remains a public health priority.

Dietary salt has been long hypothesised to play a role in asthma pathophysiology. Early ecological and survey studies linked high table salt purchases to asthma mortality [20] and high urinary sodium excretion to bronchial hyperreactivity [21], although findings were inconsistent [20–24]. More recently, experimental and animal studies [25–28] have suggested that high sodium exposure may disrupt immune homeostasis by promoting pro-inflammatory pathways and supressing anti-inflammatory responses [26, 29]. Elevated sodium concentrations have been shown to enhance T-helper (Th) 2 cell activity and cytokine production in human Th cells [27], modulate epithelial sodium channels involved in airway responsiveness [30] and promote Th17 differentiation [28]. Evidence from animal models further suggests potential sex-specific effects; in female mice, a high-salt diet induced allergic airway inflammation, altered T-cell profiles, upregulated stress-related genes and disrupted the gut–lung microbiome [25, 31]. While these findings provide biological plausibility, their direct relevance to habitual dietary sodium intake and asthma development in human remains to be established.

Measuring sodium exposure in large population-based studies poses methodological challenges [32]. Although repeated 24-h urinary sodium excretion is the gold standard, its high participant burden [33] limits feasibility in large studies. Spot urine samples, combined with predictive equations such as Tanaka and INTERSALT [34, 35], provide reasonable estimates of 24-h urinary sodium despite substantial intra-individual variability. Self-reported dietary salt intake is prone to misreporting [36–38], but simple behavioural measures, such as the frequency of adding salt to food at table, have been shown to correlate with the total sodium intake estimated from repeated dietary recalls [39], offering a scalable and practical proxy for population studies.

In this study, we evaluated the associations of INTERSALT-estimated 24-h urinary sodium excretion and frequency of adding salt to food at the table with asthma incidence among adults in a large prospective cohort. In ancillary analyses, we examined whether these associations varied by sex, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), history of respiratory allergies and blood eosinophil count.

Methods

Study design and population

Data utilised in this analysis were derived from the UK Biobank [40], a comprehensive, population-based prospective cohort study of UK adults. Our study cohort comprised 502 236 participants with data available for analysis (supplementary figure S1). We excluded 60 122 participants with asthma (both from hospital data and self-reported) and 3075 participants with pre-existing diagnosis of kidney disease, heart failure or adrenal insufficiency. Additionally, 1025 participants were excluded due to missing data on salt addition to food (either missing or “prefer not to answer”) or age, and 18 998 due to missing urinary sodium or potassium data, or BMI, which was necessary for the calculation of the INTERSALT [41] formulae.

Consequently, the final analytic sample for examining associations between salt addition to food and asthma comprised 438 014 participants, while the analyses involving urinary sodium were restricted to 420 041 participants. The UK Biobank study received ethical approval from the National Health Service (NHS) North West Multicentre Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent at the time of recruitment.

Assessment of exposures, outcomes and covariates

24-h urinary sodium was estimated from spot urine samples collected at baseline using INTERSALT equations [41]. The INTERSALT formulae were chosen as they are sex-specific, include Western population-specific intercepts [34] and incorporate urinary potassium [42] as a critical factor influencing sodium excretion. The frequency of salt use at the table was captured through a baseline touchscreen questionnaire administered between 2006 and 2010 (UK Biobank Data-Field 1478). Participants responded to the question “Do you add salt to your food? (Do not include salt used in cooking)”, with the following response options: “never/rarely”, “sometimes”, “usually”, “always” and “prefer not to answer”. Agreement in exposure classification between the self-reported frequency of adding salt to food at table and the INTERSALT formulae-estimated 24-h urinary sodium in quartiles was evaluated using Kappa statistics (supplementary figure S2).

Asthma was defined using the International Classification of Disease 10 code (J45), based on a combination of self-reports and hospital admission data. UK Biobank does not provide standardised clinical diagnostic testing for asthma (e.g. bronchodilator reversibility or bronchial challenge), and baseline spirometry was performed without bronchodilator and therefore not used to define asthma. Sociodemographic (sex, age, ethnicity, education level, Townsend deprivation index) and lifestyle (diet, smoking, alcohol use, physical activity) covariates were obtained from the touchscreen questionnaire. Information on medication use was obtained through verbal interview, while a combination of the touchscreen questionnaire, verbal interview and hospital inpatient data provided a history of allergies. Serum eosinophil count was measured from blood samples and asthma polygenic risk scores (PRSs) were derived from genome-wide association study data [43]. BMI was calculated from the measured weight and height, all obtained at baseline. Season was derived from the month of the baseline assessment and entered as a four-level categorical variable (winter: December–February; spring: March–May; summer: June–August; autumn: September–November) in urinary sodium models. Supplementary table S1 contains more information on covariate coding and categorisation.

Statistical analysis

We assessed associations between estimated 24-h urinary sodium and asthma incidence using Cox proportional hazards models with age as the time scale. Age at baseline was used as age at study entry and age at exit as age at asthma diagnosis, death or latest follow-up (whichever occurred first). The proportionality assumption was evaluated by plotting Schoenfeld residuals and we observed no evidence of violation (supplementary figure S3). Follow-up for hospital inpatient data was available until 31 October 2022 for England (Hospital Episode Statistics for England), 31 August 2022 for Scotland (Scottish Morbidity Records) and 31 May 2022 for Wales (Patient Episode Database for Wales). Models were first adjusted for sex and ethnicity (White, Mixed, Asian or Asian British, Black or Black British, other, missing) and stratified by the UK region, with multivariable models further adjusted for smoking status (never, former, current, missing), pack-years (continuous), passive smoking (in home/outside home combined: yes, no), residential air pollution (NO2) (continuous), residential air pollution (particulate matter ≤2.5 µm in diameter) (continuous), occupational pollution exposure/high-risk job (yes, no), education level (low, medium, high), Townsend deprivation index (in quintiles, missing), alcohol consumption (<1 g·day−1, 1–7 g·day−1, 8–15 g·day−1, ≥16 g·day−1, missing), physical activity (in metabolic equivalent task h·week−1, categorised into tertiles, missing), use of diuretics (yes, no), nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (yes, no), antihypertensives (yes, no), diabetes drugs (yes, no), corticosteroids (yes, no), history of respiratory allergy (yes, no), cups of coffee per day (tertiles), cups of tea per day (tertiles), glasses of water per day (continuous) and season (winter, spring, summer, autumn, missing). Models assessing the association with adding salt were also adjusted for BMI (<18.5, ≥18.5–<25, ≥25–<30, ≥30 kg·m−2, missing) and dietary factors at baseline (frequency of consumption of beef, processed meat, pork was grouped into “red meat intake”, while fresh fruit, salad/raw vegetables and cooked vegetables were combined into “fruit and vegetable intake”, both categorised into tertiles). Urinary sodium models were not adjusted for the BMI as it was already included in the INTERSALT formulae. Linear trend tests were conducted by modelling the estimated 24-h urinary sodium and the adding salt to food frequency as continuous variables.

In a secondary analysis, we evaluated heterogeneity of associations by sex (male, female), smoking status (never, former, current smoker), BMI (<25, 25–29.9, ≥30 kg·m−2), asthma PRS (tertiles), history of respiratory allergies (hay fever, allergic rhinitis, allergy to house dust mite) (yes, no) and serum eosinophil count (<300, ≥300 cells·μL−1) [44] by fitting separate models, followed by Cochran's Q test for heterogeneity. Blood eosinophil count was used to explore heterogeneity of associations as a pragmatic systemic marker associated with T2-related inflammation; however, it represents an indirect and imperfect proxy of airway inflammatory endotypes, particularly in adult-onset asthma and was not used to classify the inflammatory phenotype of incident asthma. Finally, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we evaluated baseline correlations of both urinary sodium and the frequency of adding salt to food at the table with the indicators of lung function (forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC) and FEV1 % pred) and correlations of both exposures with the serum eosinophil count. Next, we evaluated associations of the estimated 24-h urinary sodium excretion quintiles with the odds of having asthma at baseline in all participants (before excluding prevalent asthma cases) in sex, age and ethnicity-adjusted logistic regression models. Finally, to assess robustness to potential disease misclassification and population heterogeneity, we repeated all analyses after excluding participants with prevalent COPD (both self-reported cases and hospital admissions) and in analyses restricted to White participants.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc). All statistical tests were two-sided and p<0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Study sample characteristics

Over the 10.9 years of follow-up (table 1 and supplementary table S2), 11 927 incident asthma cases were recorded in 420 041 participants. At baseline, participants in the highest quintile (Q5) of 24-hour urinary sodium excretion were more physically active, had a slightly higher BMI, were more likely to be passive smokers and to use diuretics, NSAIDs, antihypertensive or diabetes medicines compared to the participants in the lowest quintile (Q1). The mean±sd urinary sodium excretion in Q5 was 3780±790 mg·day−1 versus 2253±530 mg·day−1 in Q1. Baseline characteristics of participants with a high (≥300 cells·µL−1) versus nonhigh (<300 cells·µL−1) eosinophil count were generally similar. The exceptions were sex (55% females in <300 cells·µL−1 versus 43% in the ≥300 cells·µL−1 group), smoking status (44% current/former smokers in the <300 cells·µL−1 group versus 51% in the ≥300 cells·µL−1 group) and history of respiratory allergy (20% in in the <300 cells·µL−1 group versus 26% in the ≥300 cells·µL−1 group). Mean±sd eosinophil count was 130±65 cells·µL−1 in the <300 cells·µL−1 group and 404±179 cells·µL−1 in the ≥300 cells·µL−1 group.

Urinary sodium and asthma incidence

In multivariable-adjusted models (table 2), higher estimated 24-h urinary sodium excretion was associated with increased asthma incidence in a graded manner. Compared with participants in the lowest quintile (Q1), hazard ratios (HRs) increased progressively across higher quintiles, with HRs of 1.03 (95% CI 0.97–1.09) in Q2, 1.07 (95% CI 1.01–1.14) in Q3, 1.18 (95% CI 1.12–1.25) in Q4 and 1.50 (95% CI 1.41–1.59) in Q5 (p-trend <0.0001) in the pooled sample of males and females, indicating a dose–response relationship without evidence of a clear threshold effect. In sex-stratified analyses, associations were stronger among females (HR comparing Q5 versus Q1 of 1.55, 95% CI 1.44–1.68; p-trend <0.0001) than males (HR 1.28, 95% CI 1.17–1.39; p-trend <0.0001), with statistically significant effect modification by sex (p-heterogeneity=0.0011).

Frequency of adding salt to food and asthma incidence

In multivariable-adjusted models (table 3), more frequent discretionary salt use was associated with higher asthma incidence in a graded manner. Compared with participants who reported never or rarely adding salt to food, HRs increased progressively across categories of salt use, with HRs of 1.04 (95% CI 1.00–1.08) for “sometimes”, 1.10 (95% CI 1.04–1.16) for “usually” and 1.28 (95% CI 1.19–1.37) for “always” adding salt (p-trend <0.0001) in the pooled sample, indicating a dose–response relationship without evidence of a distinct threshold effect. In sex-stratified analyses, similar graded associations were observed in males and females. Although point estimates were slightly higher among females, formal tests did not indicate significant effect modification by sex (p-heterogeneity=0.75).

Ancillary analyses

Stratified analysis of estimated 24-h urinary sodium and asthma incidence (table 4) revealed heterogeneity by eosinophil count, smoking status and BMI. Associations were stronger among participants with eosinophil levels <300 cells·µL−1 (HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.43–1.66) than among those with eosinophil levels ≥300 cells·µL−1 (HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.10–1.40; p-heterogeneity=0.0017). Similarly, associations were strongest among never-smokers (HR 1.64, 95% CI 1.51–1.79), intermediate among former smokers (HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.33–1.60) and weakest among current smokers (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.00–1.37; p-heterogeneity=0.0008). Associations were also lower in magnitude in higher BMI categories (HR 1.33, 95% CI 1.11–1.60 for BMI <25 kg·m−2; HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.15–1.41 for BMI 25–29.9 kg·m−2; and HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.90–1.18 for BMI ≥30 kg·m−2; p-heterogeneity=0.02). In contrast, for frequency of adding salt to food, statistically significant heterogeneity was observed only by BMI, with stronger associations among participants with lower BMI and attenuation among obese participants (always versus never/rarely: HR 1.47, 95% CI 1.27–1.71 for BMI <25 kg·m−2; HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.12–1.42 for BMI 25–29.9 kg·m−2; and HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.02–1.31 for BMI ≥30 kg·m−2; p-heterogeneity=0.03) (supplementary table S3). Associations between sodium exposure and asthma incidence were observed across all PRS tertiles and among participants with and without a history of respiratory allergy, with no consistent evidence of effect modification by genetic risk or allergy status (table 4 and supplementary table S3).

Discussion

Within this large, population-based study of adult females and males, we identified novel evidence of a positive association between salt intake and a late-onset asthma incidence. Linear dose–response associations were observed for both urinary sodium excretion and the frequency of adding salt to food at the table, after adjustment for a number of sociodemographic, behavioural, health and environmental factors. Although the observed associations were statistically robust and moderate in magnitude, they are unlikely to be clinically actionable at the individual level. However, even modest relative risks may be relevant at the population level given the high prevalence of both sodium exposure and adult-onset asthma. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as supporting sodium intake as a dominant or singular determinant of asthma risk, but rather as one potentially contributing factor within a multifactorial disease process.

To place these findings in a biological context, we considered potential mechanisms linking systemic sodium handling to airway function. Altered renal sodium handling, potentially mediated by systemic regulatory factors such as endogenous Na+/K+-ATPase inhibitors, may influence airway smooth muscle cellular ion handling without implying changes in plasma sodium concentration [45, 46]. Such regulatory signalling can Favor increased intracellular sodium accumulation in airway smooth muscle cells. Elevated intracellular sodium may impair forward-mode Na+/Ca2+ exchange or promote reverse-mode Na+/Ca2+ exchanger activity, leading to intracellular calcium accumulation and increased smooth muscle contractility [47]. This suggests a plausible mechanistic link between renal sodium handling and airway physiology. This renal–pulmonary axis may represent an underappreciated contributor to airway hyperreactivity in conditions associated with altered sodium balance.

In stratified analyses, associations between urinary sodium and asthma incidence were more pronounced among females, participants with lower baseline blood eosinophil counts, never-smokers and nonobese individuals, and were lower in magnitude among males, participants with higher eosinophil counts, past or current smokers and obese individuals. The parallel attenuation patterns across BMI and smoking categories suggest that strong competing risk factors, such as obesity or active smoking, may reduce the relative contribution of sodium exposure to asthma risk in adult populations. These findings do not imply absence of association in higher-risk groups, but rather indicate that sodium exposure may play a proportionally greater role among individuals without dominant inflammatory or metabolic risk factors. Given the observational design and limitations of exposure assessment, subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis-generating. Analyses stratified by asthma PRS did not reveal a consistent pattern of effect modification for either estimated urinary sodium excretion or discretionary salt use. Although higher sodium exposure was associated with increased asthma incidence within each PRS tertile, effect estimates were broadly similar and confidence intervals overlapped. This lack of a clear gradient is not unexpected, as current asthma PRSs capture only a modest proportion of disease susceptibility and may not reflect pathways relevant to sodium-related mechanisms [48]. Overall, these findings suggest that associations between sodium exposure and asthma risk are largely independent of common genetic risk and should be interpreted as exploratory.

Few studies have examined the association between urinary sodium and asthma incidence in large adult populations, and evidence for discretionary salt is similarly limited. A recent population-based study among adults with a history of allergies [25] reported associations between urinary sodium and allergic asthma in women but not in men. Direct comparison with earlier studies is challenging due to differences in study design and a predominant focus on asthma treatment rather than prevention [49, 50]. Small pilot trials have reported symptomatic improvement with sodium restriction among individuals with asthma [21, 51, 52], but these findings derive from selected patient populations and short-term interventions.

Sex differences observed in our study may reflect underlying physiological or hormonal factors. Females generally have smaller lungs and airways, which may increase susceptibility to airway narrowing, and experimental studies suggest that oestrogen can modulate cytokines such as IL-4 [18, 53] and IL-17A [54], key mediators of T2 and non-T2 inflammatory pathways. Nonetheless, these mechanisms derive largely from experimental models and their relevance to population-level dietary sodium exposure is unclear. We also cannot exclude the possibility that sex-related differences in spot urine concentration contributed to observed heterogeneity.

The stronger associations observed among participants with lower baseline blood eosinophil counts warrant cautious interpretation. Experimental studies have primarily linked high sodium exposure to T2-related immune pathways; however, such models may not fully capture the heterogeneity of adult-onset asthma in population-based settings. Blood eosinophil count represents an indirect and imperfect systemic proxy of airway inflammatory endotypes and does not fully distinguish T2 from non-T2 mechanisms. The observed heterogeneity by eosinophil level may therefore reflect differences in susceptibility or phenotype composition rather than a direct contradiction of experimental findings. These results underscore the need for future studies incorporating airway-specific biomarkers. Baseline cross-sectional analyses showing associations between urinary sodium, asthma prevalence, lung function and eosinophil counts were intended to provide contextual support for the prospective findings rather than to infer biological mechanisms. Together with the weaker associations observed among current smokers, these findings suggest that sodium exposure may have a more pronounced relative impact in individuals without strong competing sources of airway inflammation [55].

Among the strengths of this study are the large sample size, substantial number of incident asthma cases and the extensive covariate data enabling adjustment for multiple potential confounders and detailed subgroup analyses. The use of both biomarker-based sodium estimates and a behavioural measure of salt use provided converging evidence supporting the robustness of our findings. Restricting analyses to participants free of asthma at baseline strengthened temporal interpretation, although causal inference cannot be established. Several limitations should be noted. The observational design precludes causal inference and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Estimation of 24-h urinary sodium excretion from spot urine using predictive equations, including INTERSALT, provides only an approximation and is subject to imprecision and population-specific differences. In addition, urinary sodium was measured once at baseline and day-to-day variability and long-term changes in intake may have introduced nondifferential misclassification, likely attenuating associations. Nevertheless, results were directionally consistent when using adding-salt-to-food frequency as the exposure, indicating robustness across biomarker-based and behavioural exposure measures. Generalisability may be limited, as UK Biobank participants are older and healthier than the general population and dietary sodium sources reflect country-specific culinary practices and regulatory environments; consequently, absolute intake levels may differ across settings, although biomarker-based assessment enhances cross-setting comparability. Comprehensive dietary data were unavailable at baseline, preventing quantification of sodium intake from foods. Characterisation of airway inflammatory endotypes ideally requires repeated measurements and airway-specific biomarkers such as FENO or sputum cytology, whereas our analyses relied on a single baseline blood eosinophil measure, which represents an indirect and imperfect systemic marker and does not fully capture T2 airway inflammation, particularly in adult-onset asthma. Asthma ascertainment was based on self-reported physician diagnosis and linkage to healthcare records, without confirmation by standardised diagnostic testing, such as bronchodilator reversibility or bronchial challenge testing, which may have introduced nondifferential outcome misclassification. Finally, reliance on hospital and death registries for case ascertainment may bias case identification toward more severe disease.

Conclusion

In this large prospective cohort of 420 041 adults, higher dietary sodium intake, reflected by elevated urinary sodium excretion and more frequent discretionary salt use, was associated with increased asthma incidence. Associations appeared more evident among females, participants with lower baseline blood eosinophil counts and never-smokers, and lower in magnitude among individuals with obesity and current smoking. Although effect sizes were moderate and causality cannot be inferred, these findings support the hypothesis that sodium exposure may contribute to asthma risk within a multifactorial disease framework, with its relative importance varying across phenotypic and lifestyle contexts. Randomised trials and mechanistic studies are needed to determine whether sodium reduction can reduce asthma risk in specific populations.
