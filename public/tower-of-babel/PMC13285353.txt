Move Fast and Strengthen Justice: A framework for responsible technology adoption
Authors: Sarah P. Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Forensic science is undergoing rapid technological expansion driven by advances in artificial intelligence, automation, and data-intensive investigative tools. While these innovations offer significant promise, their adoption has frequently outpaced the development of governance structures necessary to ensure scientific validity, procedural justice, and public trust—a pattern familiar in technology adoption within the criminal legal system. This paper applies the Gartner Hype Cycle as an analytic framework to examine the risks associated with implementing forensic science technologies before appropriate safeguards are in place. It argues that minimizing harm, promoting integrity, and sustaining public confidence require proactive adoption practices that integrate scientific rigor, justice-centered considerations, and mechanisms for institutional repair.

Introduction

Forensic science service providers (FSSPs) worldwide, just as all other industries, are experiencing an unprecedented surge in technological innovation—particularly in artificial intelligence, automation, and data-driven investigative tools. Hardly a day passes without a new claim about what an algorithm can accomplish or a prediction about which forensic science tasks are on the brink of automation. This moment of scientific possibility carries extraordinary responsibility. Powerful technologies are powerful because they can transform criminal investigations, but they can also amplify risk, inequity, and error if implemented prematurely or without proper safeguards.

Technology adoption is inherently complex for FSSPs which often operate under significant resource constraints and within intricate workflows that are highly sensitive to disruption. For accredited laboratories, the introduction of a new method or tool requires extensive preparation, including validation studies, performance assessments, and the development or revision of standard operating procedures. Most importantly, the consequences of getting it wrong are profound as real lives and liberty are at stake.

FSSPs also face pressure from law enforcement, policymakers, and the public to adopt frontier technologies in the name of public safety. While deliberate preparation can reduce the risk of serious adverse outcomes, excessive delay may hinder a laboratory's ability to benefit from promising scientific advances. Caught between public safety imperatives on one side and the risk of wrongful convictions on the other – what is a forensic science leader to do?

The Gartner Hype Cycle framework

The challenges associated with technology adoption are not a new phenomenon. Jackie Fenn, a consultant with a firm specializing in guiding large organizations and government agencies through emerging technology adoption, observed recurring patterns across industries when institutions attempted to implement technologies that were immature or not ready for operational use. Her observations ultimately informed the development of a management decision-making tool that became synonymous with her company: the Gartner Hype Cycle [2].

What is the Gartner Hype Cycle?

The Gartner Hype Cycle is not a description of the evolution of the technology itself, but rather, it describes the expectations of users and public expectations over the course of technology implementation. Applying the Gartner Hype Cycle to a historic technology

An illustrative example of the hype cycle using a historical technology is the advent of forensic anthropometry. Simon Cole wrote extensively about the development and institutionalization of forensic anthropometry in the late 19th century [4]. A French police officer named Alphonse Bertillon developed what became known as the Bertillon system, a series of measurements that included head dimensions, limb lengths, stature, and ear length, designed to identify individuals who repeatedly came into contact with the criminal legal system. Prior to this innovation trigger, identification typically relied upon sifting through an alphabetized stack of photographs or by eyewitnesses.

Bertillon's first identification was documented in 1883 and reportedly identified 241 individuals in its first year. For an era lacking modern communications tools, Bertillonage spread like wildfire. Within a decade, this system was adopted by law enforcement agencies around the world, approaching its peak of inflated expectations. Two primary issues plagued identification by Bertillonage. First, it is now clear that anthropometric measurements were not fit-for-purpose for the monumental task of identification. Second, as the system dispersed, its application often diverged from the rigor and standardization envisioned by its creator.

The consequent trough of disillusionment is frequently illustrated using the “Will West case.” According to FBI lore, a man named Will West was processed at a federal prison in Leavenworth, Kansas, in 1903, when his Bertillon measurements were found to be identical to a man named William West, who was already serving time at Leavenworth. Although the two men shared similar physical characteristics, they possessed conclusively different fingerprints. Historical investigation has shown this story to be a composite rather than a precise factual account [4], but anthropometry nevertheless gave way to fingerprint comparison during the early 20th century.

Although Bertillonage itself did not endure, the system contributed important lessons regarding data standardization, criminal recordkeeping, and bureaucratic organization. These principles, rather than the method itself, can be understood as moving along the slope of enlightenment. They ultimately reached a plateau of productivity in the form of standardized criminal records and biometric information systems that underpin modern investigative practices.

It is important to situate Bertillonage in its broader historical context. The system emerged at a time when body measurements were used as a way to differentiate populations as “savage” versus “civilized” [5] or to support innate criminality [6]. These pseudoscientific efforts to connect phenotype with behavior are now recognized as part of the eugenic tradition. In practice, anthropometry often resulted in differentiation and surveillance when deployed disproportionately against vulnerable or marginalized populations [7], a dynamic that closely parallels concerns raised about contemporary biometric technologies [8].

Applying the Gartner Hype Cycle to current technology

The Gartner Hype Cycle can also be applied to contemporary forensic science technologies. One of the defining features of modern forensic science is DNA technology. John Butler has extensively documented the scientific and institutional evolution of forensic DNA technology, a staple of criminal investigations worldwide [9].

The innovation trigger occurred in 1984, when Sir Alec Jeffreys discovered DNA fingerprinting, followed by its first application in a criminal case in 1986. Although fingerprint comparison was well established, DNA testing transcended its limitations by transforming forensic identification from surface traces to a biological signature. DNA was rapidly adopted and soon described in legal, policy, and media discourse as the “gold standard” of forensic identification, frequently treated as synonymous with infallibility [10,11]. This narrative of certainty obscured significant technical, interpretive, and operational challenges, contributing to the peak of inflated expectations in the 1990s.

Despite the foundational scientific strength of DNA testing, its early implementation occurred in the absence of uniform standards and robust quality management systems. Accreditation and quality assurance were not yet routine requirements, and practice varied widely across laboratories. As a result, miscarriages of justice emerged alongside several high-profile laboratory failures. In response to these concerns, the National Academy of Sciences issued two reports during the 1990s that explicitly addressed the limitations, risks, and sources of error in forensic DNA testing, and laid the groundwork for the development of best practices and quality assurance frameworks [12,13].

The discovery of serious errors and misconduct in DNA testing at the Houston Police Department Crime Laboratory, culminating in the exoneration of Josiah Sutton [14], marked a clear descent into the trough of disillusionment in the early 2000s. The technical foundation for recovery had been laid earlier: the FBI Laboratory established the thirteen core CODIS Short Tandem Repeat (STR) loci in 1997, and the National DNA Index System became operational in 1998 [9,15]. The slope of enlightenment followed in the mid-2000s, driven less by the technology itself than by the expansion of accreditation and quality assurance standards, together with growing recognition that even powerful technologies require governance, transparency, validation, and oversight. These developments ultimately contributed to a plateau of productivity in the 2010s, characterized by the routine, standardized, and accredited use of STR-based forensic DNA analysis across jurisdictions [15].

Problems with adopting immature technologies

The challenges associated with technology adoption can be usefully framed using the Gartner Hype Cycle. The examples discussed here draw primarily from the United States experience. This focus is not intended to suggest that the U.S. approach is superior, but rather reflects the extent to which forensic science systems in the United States have provided criminal justice professionals repeated opportunities to learn—often through failure.

By examining these missteps and their consequences, this perspective aims to surface lessons that may be useful to forensic science leaders elsewhere. Sharing these experiences is intended to support more informed decision-making and to help other jurisdictions anticipate and avoid challenges that have proven costly within the U.S. system.

As we look at where we are today, the pace of innovation has become both a strength and a strain. Three challenges to technology adoption today include: (1) Rapid speed of innovation triggers, (2) Inflated expectations of presumptive and investigative technologies, and (3) being pushed into the trough by operational urgency.

Rapid speed of innovation triggers

The rapid pace of technological innovation has created significant governance challenges. In the United States, most criminal justice oversight occurs at the state level rather than the federal level. Consequently, criminal justice reforms like postconviction DNA access and eyewitness identification reforms cannot achieve nationwide implementation without the passage of legislation in all fifty states. This decentralized structure necessarily creates serious advocacy burdens. In theory, a state-by-state, technology-by-technology approach to governance could function effectively if new tools were introduced sequentially, allowing regulatory and policy efforts to conclude before the next innovation emerged (Fig. 2). Under those conditions, policymakers could assess risks, develop safeguards, and implement oversight structures before widespread deployment. In reality, technology policymaking is neither linear nor orderly. Multiple tools can be released simultaneously, each at a different stage of technical maturity and accompanied by varying levels of hype. This convergence of rapid innovation, uneven readiness, public and institutional pressure, and lengthy advocacy timelines overwhelms traditional regulatory pathways and complicates efforts to align governance with technological adoption.Fig. 2Illustration of expected versus actual pace of technology triggers.

For data-rich technologies involving machine learning or artificial intelligence, these challenges are compounded by the absence of a comprehensive national data governance framework in the United States comparable to the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Without a unifying legal structure to guide how data are collected, analyzed, used, retained, or expunged, oversight of data-intensive technologies is left to a fragmented patchwork of state statutes and regulations, if legislation exists at all. The result is a highly uneven legal landscape in which safeguards vary widely by jurisdiction and frequently lag behind technological implementation.

The experience with forensic investigative genetic genealogy (FIGG) illustrates this problem. In Maryland, it took two years to pass legislation regulating law enforcement use of forensic investigative genetic genealogy (FIGG). As of 2025, only Maryland [16], Montana [17], and Utah [18] have stand-alone statutes specifically regulating law enforcement use of FIGG.

Inflated expectations of presumptive and investigative technologies

Inflated expectations play a significant role in shaping how presumptive and investigative technologies spread and how intractable the political and law enforcement demand for their adoption intensifies. This dynamic has proven particularly problematic in the context of technologies intended for preliminary screening or investigative use.

Presumptive and investigative tools are not inherently flawed. When deployed for their intended purpose and accompanied by appropriate safeguards, such technologies can serve a valuable role in guiding investigations. In the United States, however, these tools are frequently used in ways that are not fit-for-purpose. Rather than serving as preliminary indicators or investigative leads, they often become the sole evidentiary basis for charging decisions and case outcomes. Several structural dynamics contribute to this pattern.

First, in the absence of statutory requirements, regulatory frameworks, or procurement standards, many technologies remain effectively unregulated. At the state level in particular, the absence of affirmative requirements often functions as permission for unrestricted use. Without scientific oversight or mandatory standards, law enforcement agencies may adopt technologies based primarily on vendor claims or cost considerations, rather than on demonstrated performance, validation, or risk assessment. As a result, agencies may acquire tools that lack adequate safeguards, documentation, or governance structures prior to implementation.

Second, the primary formal constraint on forensic science technologies in the United States tends to be legal admissibility. If evidence is expected to be introduced at trial, it may be subject to judicial scrutiny and admissibility constraints. However, technologies used solely in the investigative phase often operate outside this constraint. These tools can nonetheless shape the trajectory of a case. This is particularly consequential in a system characterized by an exceptionally high rate of guilty pleas. Approximately 97 percent of federal and state criminal cases in the United States are resolved through plea agreements rather than trials [19]. As a result, individuals may encounter only presumptive or investigative evidence before they must decide whether to take a guilty plea. Notably, approximately 24 percent of individuals listed in the National Registry of Exonerations initially entered guilty pleas despite their innocence, underscoring the risks associated with overreliance on unconfirmed evidence [20].

One illustrative example is the widespread use of colorimetric field drug tests. Unlike laboratory-based confirmatory methods, these tests typically consist of single-use pouches designed to detect specific substances, such as cocaine or fentanyl. Officers often carry only a limited selection of these pouches, meaning that the test performed is constrained by what is available or expected rather than by an open-ended inquiry into substance identity. These tests are frequently deployed during roadside stops, which research has shown disproportionately affect Black drivers compared to white drivers [21].

Colorimetric field drug tests are not fit-for-purpose for confirming the identity of a controlled substance. Nevertheless, their results have been used to support arrests and prosecutions [22]. In Texas, a prosecutor identified cases in which individuals pleaded guilty based on field drug test results, only for subsequent laboratory analysis to reveal that no controlled substance was present [23]. A review of cases between 2004 and 2015 found that, among 301 instances in which laboratory testing did not match field test results, 74 percent involved substances that were not controlled substances at all. Further analysis by the Quattrone Center for the Fair Administration of Justice estimated that approximately 773,000 arrests in the United States may be attributable to the use of field drug tests without confirmatory laboratory testing [24]. Detained individuals may choose to plead guilty rather than fight the charges, even if they are innocent. Their findings also indicate that many law enforcement agencies rely exclusively on these presumptive tests, without routine follow-up confirmation.

Together, these dynamics illustrate how inflated expectations, coupled with regulatory gaps and operational pressures, can lead presumptive technologies to function as de facto determinative evidence, well beyond their intended role.

Engagement with law enforcement agencies regarding the use of field drug tests consistently reveals a recurring justification for their continued reliance. When stakeholders are asked why presumptive results cannot be followed by confirmatory laboratory testing, the response often points to crime laboratories, already burdened by significant backlogs and which cannot absorb additional case volume [22,25]. Sending all suspected drug evidence for confirmation would further overwhelm their limited capacity.

This dynamic exposes a fundamental tension within the system. High case volume and limited laboratory resources combine to create conditions in which expediency acceptably substitutes for evidentiary rigor. Operational urgency becomes the driver of decision-making, even when it can undermine accuracy and fairness. The result creates a danger zone in which the pressure to process cases quickly prevents individuals from receiving reliable testing and meaningful access to justice, and in which presumptive tools are asked to carry far more weight than they were ever designed to bear.

Pushed into the trough by operational urgency

Operational urgency can accelerate technologies into the trough of disillusionment when errors, wrongful arrests, or wrongful convictions occur. One prominent example in the United States involves the use of facial recognition technology (FRT).

In January 2020, Robert Williams was wrongfully arrested in Detroit for a retail theft that occurred in 2018 [26]. His arrest, carried out in front of his children, was widely reported and became a focal point in national discussions about the risks associated with the use of facial recognition in criminal investigations.

Subsequent reporting revealed multiple points of failure. The probe image obtained from surveillance footage was low quality and blurry, and bore little resemblance to Mr. Williams. Despite this obvious problem, no substantive investigation occurred beyond the facial recognition search and a subsequent identification from a photo lineup that included Mr. Williams’ photograph.

Critically, investigators placed excessive trust in the technology itself. Rather than independently assessing the similarity between the probe image and Mr. Williams’ photograph, or undertaking corroborating investigative work, the facial recognition result was treated as authoritative. While the generation of a candidate list is not inherently problematic, such lists are probabilistic, may reflect underlying biases, and are intended to be evaluated by trained practitioners using structured analytical processes and quality controls. In this case, those safeguards were absent. Because facial recognition was used as an investigative rather than an evidentiary tool, no formal requirements for validation, documentation, or review constrained its use.

Notably, just one month prior to Mr. Williams' arrest, the National Institute of Standards and Technology published a major study examining demographic differentials in facial recognition performance [27]. That report documented variations in accuracy associated with race, gender, and age, and emphasized the importance of algorithm quality, image capture standards, and training data to system performance. It is unlikely that this research reached the investigators involved in Mr. Williams’ case. Even if it had, would they have understood its lessons? Disseminating scientific findings to practitioners in real time remains a persistent challenge, and investigative technologies are often deployed with an implicit presumption of objectivity that can obscure known limitations.

The consequences of this failure were far-reaching. Mr. Williams’ arrest fueled the ongoing decline in public trust and promoted legislative action across the United States. Longstanding concerns raised by advocacy organizations seeking to restrict or ban FRT were suddenly grounded in concrete harm. His case was followed by numerous media accounts of wrongful arrests linked to facial recognition [28] which notably substantiated the demographic problems linked with FRT algorithms. These developments have fueled an ongoing debate between advocates calling for safeguards to address demonstrable risks of misidentification and proponents of the technology who view legislative responses as overly restrictive [29].

This pattern illustrates a recurring consequence of deploying powerful technologies without adequate governance. Public trust is a critical component of successful technology adoption [30]. When that trust collapses, technologies risk not only restriction but wholesale rejection, even in circumstances where more measured safeguards might have preserved their responsible and effective use [31].

Building enlightenment through enhancing integrity

Preventing technologies from precipitous falls from the peak of inflated expectations requires intentional movement along the slope of enlightenment. That transition does not occur automatically and must be built deliberately and systematically through fostering activities that enhance integrity.

The zone of integrity

In the forensic science context, integrity is not a singular concept but one that operates across technical, legal, and institutional domains. Building enlightenment requires parallel and sustained commitments to science, justice, and repair. Ideally, these commitments are proactively established at the point of technology introduction rather than retroactively in response to failure (See Fig. 3).Fig. 3Building zones of integrity to mitigate hype cycle risks.

As technologies mature, errors and limitations will inevitably surface. The objective is not to deny or conceal these shortcomings, but to incorporate them into governance structures that temper unrealistic expectations before harm occurs. By lowering the height of the initial peak, systems can reduce the risk of dramatic collapse while strengthening resilience, accountability, and trust over time.

The central question, then, is how forensic science systems commit to understanding the limitations of emerging technologies and how they can respond when errors emerge. Whether those moments become opportunities for learning or catalysts for disillusionment depends on the presence or absence of mechanisms that promote transparency, accountability, and institutional repair. Rather than waiting for the inevitable disillusionment stage, building integrity into the technology adoption process requires early and deliberate efforts to embed these values [32].

Science

Forensic science professionals are well acquainted with the foundational role that science plays in building integrity. Those engaged in research, validation, and peer review understand the importance of rigorous methodology, performance testing, and continual evaluation. Because public trust in technology is fostered by dialogue, distributive fairness, procedural fairness, lawfulness, and effectiveness [32], trustworthy forensic science practice must rest on scientific principles of transparency, validation, standardization, documentation, disclosure, and quality management.

While generating scientific evidence about the capabilities and limitations of new technologies is essential to advancing enlightenment, the timing of that evidence matters. Producing robust validation and performance data at the front end of the adoption process, before technologies are widely deployed, can help prevent the formation of unearned or inflated expectations. Early scientific scrutiny therefore serves not only an evidentiary function, but also a governance function, by shaping realistic understanding and responsible use.

Justice

Justice systems differ significantly across jurisdictions, shaped by distinct legal traditions, cultural values, and institutional structures. Practices that are routine in one country may be unfamiliar or even impermissible in another. Some systems emphasize individual rights and adversarial protections, while others prioritize communitarian values or administrative efficiency. In some jurisdictions, safeguards are embedded automatically; in others, they must be actively asserted.

These differences are not inherently right or wrong. They reflect the realities of a diverse global landscape in which forensic science operates within, and must adapt to, varying legal and cultural contexts. Recognizing this diversity is essential when considering how scientific standards intersect with justice outcomes across systems.

Justice considerations also require engagement with ethical frameworks that cannot be evaluated in the abstract. Ethical judgments about technology use are deeply informed by social and historical context. In many countries, including the United States, histories of inequitable treatment and systemic disparities affecting vulnerable populations shape how technologies are implemented, experienced, and perceived. These contexts must be taken into account when implementing forensic tools, particularly those that may amplify existing inequities.

Court systems and laws that govern the treatment of evidence also vary widely across the globe. One illustrative example is the use of evaluative activity-level reasoning in DNA interpretation. This approach is well established in parts of Europe and has been integrated into judicial practice in several European jurisdictions. In contrast, its adoption in the United States remains contested. A recent law review article argued that while the underlying scientific reasoning may be sound, the application of evaluative activity-level reasoning may be impractical within the constraints of U.S. evidentiary rules [33]. This debate underscores how differences in legal frameworks, not scientific validity alone, shape whether and how forensic science methodologies can be responsibly implemented.

Repair

Technology scholarship has traditionally emphasized innovation, often overlooking the equally critical work of repair. Scholars in science and technology studies have developed a body of scholarship that centers repair as an essential, ongoing practice rather than a discrete response to failure. In this literature, repair reflects the values of a society as it exercises choices in how systems are maintained, fixed, and restored when breakdowns inevitably occur [[34], [35], [36]].

Breakdowns are not exceptional in science or forensic practice ⎯ they are routine. DNA samples may become contaminated, evidence may be misplaced, storage units may lose power, analytical judgments may be mistaken, or an examiner may misstate findings during courtroom testimony. The critical question is therefore not whether errors occur, but whether institutions respond and how they do so. A society's approach to repair reveals its underlying values: whether it prioritizes transparency, accountability, and learning, or instead favors concealment and expediency.

Applied to forensic science, a culture of repair requires mechanisms that encourage acknowledgement of error, corrective action, institutional learning, and restoration to the people harmed by the breakdown. In the U.S. context, the author has advanced the duty to correct and notify as a critical form of repair, one that recognizes the affirmative obligation to disclose errors that may affect past cases directly to the persons harmed [37]. Public oversight can further serve as a repair mechanism, reinforcing accountability while promoting continuous improvement rather than punitive blame. Community technology oversight boards offer an opportunity to bring stakeholders together to establish safe systems for technology implementation and respond when unexpected outcomes occur [38]. Together, these approaches situate repair not as a failure of technology, but as a measure of institutional integrity and resilience.

A hopeful example

After examining multiple instances in which inflated expectations and operational urgency contributed to failure, it is important to highlight an example in which governance, transparency, and repair were deliberately embedded into the forensic science system. In the United States, one such example can be found in the forensic science governance activities in the State of Texas.

Texas has historically been associated with a high number of wrongful convictions [20]. In response, a bipartisan group of legislators enacted a series of reforms designed both to prevent wrongful convictions and to provide meaningful responses when errors occur. Among the most consequential of these reforms in forensic science was the creation of the Texas Forensic Science Commission, an independent oversight body with a mandate to investigate and oversee forensic science issues [39,40].

The Commission has undertaken work that remains uncommon in other U.S. jurisdictions and proactively and systemically builds integrity into forensic science practice. Notably, following the publication of research by the National Institute of Standards and Technology examining inconclusive decisions and error rates in forensic science [41], the Commission established a statewide collaboration among forensic firearms practitioners and stakeholders to understand and implement its recommendations [42]. This group is currently developing a single, standardized method for firearms analysis to facilitate the systematic collection of performance and validation data. Given the size and diversity of forensic science service providers in Texas, achieving consensus across laboratories represents a significant accomplishment. This effort will enable the collection of validation data and quality metrics that would not be possible under fragmented governance approaches.

Texas has also advanced justice through transparency-focused reforms. The state enacted an enhanced discovery law [43] and operationalized that commitment through the creation of a centralized electronic portal for sharing evidence [44]. This infrastructure reduces the risk of inadvertent nondisclosure, ensures consistent access for defense counsel, and supports procedural justice. The Commission also convened a Rapid DNA working group composed of a broad range of stakeholders1 to guide the implementation of Rapid DNA technologies. The working group will make recommendations regarding evidence types for use with the technology as well as guidance on interagency collaboration. By convening a broad group of stakeholders, the working group facilitated discussions regarding appropriate use cases, data handling practices, and safeguards necessary to protect both scientific integrity and privacy rights. In many respects, this effort functions as a community technology oversight process, bringing diverse perspectives together to guide responsible implementation.

Finally, the Commission has established a robust system for handling public complaints and promoting laboratory self-disclosures of serious quality incidents. Research demonstrated a notable trend: as laboratory self-disclosures increased over time, public complaints declined [45]. This shift reflects growing trust, not only in the oversight body, but also in laboratories’ willingness to come forward when errors occur.

That trust proved critical when population data errors were identified in information distributed by the FBI for DNA interpretation. In response, Texas undertook a comprehensive, statewide DNA mixture review [46]. Although resource-intensive and lengthy, the review created an enduring mechanism through which individuals with potentially affected cases could seek evaluation. This response exemplifies repair as an institutional practice: one that prioritizes accountability, learning, and justice over expediency, and that helps sustain public trust over time.

Conclusion

As forensic science leaders evaluate the adoption of new technologies, three strategic objectives should guide implementation: moderating the peak of inflated expectations, elevating the trough of disillusionment, and deliberately managing the trajectory toward a sustainable plateau of productivity. Achieving this balance requires integrity operationalized through sustained commitments to science, justice, and repair.

Throughout this discussion, integrity has emerged as the connective tissue between technological capability and institutional trust. Scientific rigor, including validation, standards, and quality management, is essential but not sufficient on its own. Justice considerations shaped by legal frameworks, cultural values, and procedural safeguards determine how technologies affect individuals and communities. Repair mechanisms, including transparency, error correction, and oversight, ensure that systems remain resilient and accountable when failures inevitably occur.

As forensic science managers consider the implementation of machine learning and artificial intelligence, critical decisions will need to be made regarding the levels of automation and degree of human involvement as technologies are implemented [47]. These decisions raise important questions: which applications of a given technology are likely to sustain a healthy Gartner Hype Cycle, and which risk sharp ascent followed by collapse? Will technologies be deployed in ways that promote controlled, evidence-based adoption or will their implementation invite extreme volatility? What systems of repair exist to respond when errors inevitably occur? Guarding against extreme swings will be a central leadership responsibility for forensic science leaders.

Across the INTERPOL conference, a consistent theme emerged: safeguards matter. Nearly every presentation addressed validation, governance, transparency, or oversight. This shared emphasis reflects a growing recognition that progress in forensic science depends not on speed alone, but on careful deliberation. Integrity, applied early and maintained continuously, is what protects practitioners, institutions, and the public from the consequences of inflated expectations.

As the INTERPOL meeting takes place in France, it is fitting to close with the words of a French scientist whose life exemplified both perseverance and rigor. Marie Curie observed that “the way of progress is neither swift nor easy.” That insight remains deeply relevant. If implementing new technology feels effortless, it may be a signal that critical questions have been left unasked. The challenges, debates, and concerns that accompany responsible adoption are not signs of failure, they are evidence of meaningful progress.

The work of building forensic science systems that are scientifically sound, just, and trustworthy is demanding by design. It is precisely this careful, sometimes uncomfortable work that enables technology to serve society in a just, equitable, and sustainable way.
