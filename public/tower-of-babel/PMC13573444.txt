Decoding candidemia in critically ill patients: unsupervised clustering identifies three unique phenotypes
Authors: Florian Reizine, Juliette Henry, Luc Desmedt, Christophe Camus, Valentin Coirier, François Arrive, Estelle Burban, Gabriel Eustache, Tony Belleville, Antoine Marc, Jolan Malherbe, Pierre Bouju, Paul Jaubert, Olivier Lesieur, Maxime Leclerc, Pierre Fillâtre, Aurélien Frérou, Renaud Prével, Nolwenn Mainguy, Anne Cady, Florent Morio, Solène Le Gal, Estelle Perraud, Laurence Delhaes, Sébastien Imbert, Helene Guegan, Julie Bonhomme, Marc Pihet, Clarisse Dupin, Agathe Delbove, Damien Du Cheyron, Yoann Launey, Cécile Aubron, Jean-Pierre Gangneux

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCandidemia displays significant clinical heterogeneity in critically ill patients. This study aimed to identify distinct clinical phenotypes and to assess their association with 90-day mortality.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study of 492 intensive care unit (ICU) patients with candidemia from 16 French ICUs (2015–2023). We performed a factor analysis of mixed data (FAMD) incorporating both categorical and continuous baseline variables, followed by hierarchical clustering on principal components (HCPC). Survival analysis was performed with Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsOverall, 90-day mortality for the 492 patients (median age: 64 years, 69.1% male) with candidemia was 62.6%. Three different phenotypes emerged from FAMD followed by HCPC: Phenotype 1 (n = 70, 14.2%) comprised patients with severe immunosuppression, mostly due to hematological malignancies (82.9%), and high severity scores (SAPS II:70); Phenotype 2 (n = 223, 45.3%) corresponded to elderly cirrhotic patients (19.3%) with early-onset digestive candidemia; Phenotype 3 (n = 199, 40.5%) comprised younger patients with lower severity scores and catheter-related candidemia. Mortality differed significantly between phenotypes: 72.9% (Phenotype 1), 70.4% (Phenotype 2), and 50.3% (Phenotype 3) (p < 0.001). Independent predictors of mortality included age (aHR: 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00-1.02; p = 0.003), cirrhosis (aHR: 1.90, 95% CI: 1.39-2.60; p < 0.001), SAPS II (aHR: 1.01, 95% CI: 1.01-1.02; p < 0.001), echinocandin use (aHR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.39-0.63; p < 0.001) and proven catheter-related candidemia (protective; aHR: 0.48, 95% CI: 0.33-0.69; p < 0.001). Immunodepression was not associated with mortality.ConclusionUnsupervised clustering identified three clinically different candidemia phenotypes with different outcomes. Introduction

Candidemia remains one of the most severe bloodstream infections encountered in critically ill patients. It is associated with persistently high mortality despite major advances in intensive care medicine and the availability of effective antifungal therapies for more than two decades. Recent large European cohorts and international guidelines continue to report a mortality ranging from 30% to over 50% in patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs), highlighting the current clinical burden of this infection [1–5].

In addition to its general severity, ICU-acquired candidemia is characterized by marked clinical heterogeneity. Patients differ widely in terms of age, comorbid conditions, immune status, severity of acute illness, source of infection, and timing of onset during the ICU stay. Host-related factors appear to play a key role in determining outcomes [3, 6]. However, current management strategies and most clinical studies still rely on simplified classifications of candidemia severity, which struggle to account for this heterogeneity in critically ill populations.

Several attempts have been made to distinguish “uncomplicated” from “complicated” candidemia, to guide treatment duration and intensity. Such frameworks may be applicable to selected non-ICU patients, but their relevance in critically ill patients remains limited. In the ICU setting, conventional multivariable approaches based on predefined risk factors have displayed only modest discrimination, and the dichotomous classification of candidemia severity does not reflect the continuum of host vulnerability and organ dysfunction encountered in daily practice [7].

We recently reported results for the French multicenter CandidICU cohort of 492 ICU patients with candidemia, revealing a 90-day mortality of 62.6% and a substantial burden of complications, including secondary localizations (mostly vascular, ocular and cardiac) [6]. In this cohort, mortality was higher in patients not screened for secondary localizations, highlighting the need for more detailed patient characterization, over and above traditional risk stratification.

In this context, data-driven approaches based on unsupervised statistical methods provide an opportunity to characterize the heterogeneity of ICU-acquired candidemia in more detail. By integrating multiple clinical, biological, and microbiological variables without a priori assumptions, clustering techniques can identify distinct clinical phenotypes that are not apparent in traditional analytical strategies. Such approaches have recently improved risk stratification and guided precision medicine strategies in other heterogeneous ICU syndromes but they have yet to be applied to candidemia in critically ill patients [8, 9]. The aim of this ancillary post-hoc analysis of the CandidICU cohort was, therefore, to identify distinct clinical phenotypes of candidemia by unsupervised clustering and to assess their association with 90-day mortality.

Methods

Study design

This ancillary study was based on data from the CandidICU cohort study, a retrospective, multicenter observational study that included adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted to one of 16 ICUs between January 2015 and January 2023 with at least one episode of candidemia [6].

The CandidICU study was designed to describe the epidemiology, clinical features, management, and outcomes of candidemia in critically ill patients. The protocol was approved by the local ethics committee (Comité d’éthique CHU Rennes, No. 23.99) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written information was provided to all survivors.

Definition and data collection

At each center, data from electronic health records were collected retrospectively on a standardized case report form; they were then reviewed centrally to ensure quality and consistency.

Candidemia was defined as at least one blood culture positive for a Candida species. ICU-acquired candidemia was defined as a positive blood culture obtained ≥ 48 h after ICU admission.

The general characteristics of the patients analyzed included age, sex, obesity (body mass index > 30 kg/m2), chronic kidney disease, cirrhosis, diabetes, and long-term corticosteroid use (defined as > 7 days at a dose ≥ 1 mg/kg/day [prednisone equivalent] or > 3 months at a lower dose). The underlying cause of immunosuppression was classified as solid organ transplantation (SOT), hematologic malignancy, solid tumor (ST), immune-mediated inflammatory disease, HIV infection, or neutropenia.

The initial laboratory results and management variables recorded included prior antifungal use during the last three months; the times from hospital admission and from ICU admission to candidemia onset; the surgical reason for ICU admission; and the presence of sepsis or septic shock at ICU admission according to Sepsis-3 criteria [10]. Lactate concentration, leukocyte count, and temperature at ICU admission were recorded, together with the need for intubation within 24 h of ICU admission (defined as early intubation). This variable was included as a marker of acute severity. Illness severity was assessed with Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II) [11]. The microbiological data recorded included Candida species along with susceptibility to fluconazole or echinocandins. Candida species were categorized into a simplified variable by grouping rare species (prevalence < 2%) into an "other" category, while retaining C. albicans, C. glabrata, C. tropicalis, C. krusei, and C. parapsilosis as individual categories to ensure statistical stability. The source of candidemia was determined by the treating physicians based on clinical symptoms, microbiological samples, and radiological findings, after exclusion of other evident sources. Digestive source was defined by the presence of intra-abdominal infection based on clinical symptoms (abdominal pain, peritonism), microbiological documentation (positive cultures from peritoneal fluid or intra-abdominal samples), radiological findings (intra-abdominal collections, pneumoperitoneum, gastrointestinal perforation), or recent abdominal surgery. Respiratory source was defined by clinical symptoms of pneumonia, isolation of Candida from bronchoalveolar lavage or protected respiratory samples at significant thresholds, and radiological findings (new infiltrates, consolidations, cavitary lesions), in the absence of another identifiable source. Treatment-related variables were collected including time to antifungal therapy, echinocandin use and effective antifungal treatment (defined as appropriate therapy based on species susceptibility and antifungal susceptibility testing for fluconazole and echinocandins). While sources of infection (including catheter-related candidemia) were recorded, detailed data on catheter removal or other source control procedures were not systematically available for the entire cohort and could not be included in the multivariable analysis.

The outcome variables included survival to day 90 (D90) after candidemia onset, duration of stay in the ICU and in the hospital, need for and duration of mechanical ventilation, occurrence of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) during ICU stay, development of acute kidney injury (AKI), the identification of a candidemia-related secondary localization and the need for renal replacement therapy (RRT). The detailed definitions of secondary localizations have been previously reported in the primary publication of the CandidICU cohort [6].

Study objectives

The aim of this ancillary study was to delineate distinct phenotypes of candidemia by unsupervised clustering methods, and to assess the relationship of these phenotypes to clinical outcomes, including D90 mortality.

The secondary objectives were to identify factors independently associated with 90-day (D90) mortality in ICU patients with candidemia and to compare baseline characteristics and outcomes between the patients who did and did not survive to D90.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as medians (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. For comparisons between groups, we used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables and the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared as a function of survival at day 90. Missing data were handled using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE), generating five imputed datasets to account for uncertainty due to missing values. Only the available baseline missing-at-random variables were used for imputation.

Associations with 90-day mortality were first explored by univariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, with the retention of variables with p < 0.20 for inclusion in multivariable models.

Backward stepwise selection based on p-values was then applied to derive the final Cox model, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The proportional hazards assumption was verified with Schoenfeld residuals. To assess robustness and potential collinearity, we performed two sensitivity analyses. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for variables retained in the final Cox model (values < 3 considered acceptable). We also conducted LASSO regression with tenfold cross-validation. Variables selected under the optimal penalization parameter were entered into a standard Cox model to obtain interpretable hazard ratios.

For characterization of the heterogeneity of candidemia presentations, we conducted an unsupervised clustering analysis combining factor analysis of mixed data (FAMD) and hierarchical clustering on principal components (HCPC) as previously described [8, 9, 12]. The choice of FAMD was driven by the mixed structure of our dataset, which included both continuous and categorical variables. FAMD simultaneously performs principal component analysis for continuous variables and multiple correspondence analysis for categorical variables, allowing balanced contributions of both data types within a unified dimension-reduction framework. This approach avoids arbitrary transformation of categorical variables, which would have been required for distance-based methods such as k-means. All variables included in the clustering procedure were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and previously identified determinants of candidemia severity and outcome in ICU patients, including findings from the original CandidICU study and recent international guidelines [6]. These comprised baseline host characteristics (e.g., age, comorbidities, immunosuppression status), severity parameters at ICU admission (including SAPS II score, lactate concentration, and sepsis/septic shock), timing of candidemia, microbiological features (source of infection and simplified Candida species category), and prior antifungal exposure. Outcome variables and post-baseline therapeutic variables were intentionally excluded to avoid outcome-driven clustering. Variables with near-zero variance, identified using the nearZeroVar function (i.e., SOT and HIV infection), were excluded from the clustering analysis to ensure model stability. Their baseline frequencies are nonetheless reported in the results and they were retained in descriptive and outcome analyses.

Cluster stability was assessed with internal validation indices (mean silhouette width, Calinski–Harabasz index, Davies–Bouldin index). Robustness was further evaluated by calculating the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) comparing HCPC-derived partitions with k-means clustering. To assess internal validity and cluster stability, we performed resampling-based validation. From the original cohort (N = 492), we generated 500 subsamples, each containing 80% of patients randomly selected without replacement. For each subsample, we repeated the entire clustering procedure (FAMD followed by HCPC). Cluster assignments from each subsample were compared to the original cluster assignments using the ARI, with values closer to 1 indicating stronger agreement. The mean ARI and its 95% CI across all iterations were reported.

Clusters were visualized on the first two FAMD dimensions with 95% confidence ellipses, and on a two-dimensional t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) map. Baseline and outcome variables were compared between clusters to assess external validity.

Statistical analyses were performed with R 4.1.1 statistical software. All tests were two-tailed and values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. This study was designed in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [13].

Results

Study population

We included a total of 492 critically ill patients with blood culture-proven candidemia in this study. The baseline characteristics of these patients at ICU admission are summarized in Table 1. Median age was 64 years (IQR: 52–71 years), and 69.1% of the patients were male. The observed comorbid conditions included obesity in 23.5%, diabetes in 21.5%, cirrhosis in 12.3%, and chronic renal failure in 6.5% of the patients. The underlying causes of immunosuppression were hematological malignancies in 16.3%, ST in 22.8%, neutropenia in 10.6%, immune-mediated inflammatory disease in 6.5%, and long-term corticosteroid therapy in 11.0%. At ICU admission, 79.5% of the patients presented with sepsis or septic shock, and the median SAPS II score was 56 (IQR: 42-73). Median time from hospital admission to candidemia onset was 12 days (IQR: 5-23 days), and median time from ICU admission to candidemia was 4 days (IQR: 0-12 days).

Cluster analysis

Unsupervised clustering analysis with a combination of FAMD and HCPC identified three clinically different phenotypes in the 492 patients analyzed (Tables 1–2). Visual inspection of the FAMD scatterplot showed that the three clusters were well separated overall, albeit with a partial overlap at the margins, reflecting the continuum of clinical presentations in critically ill patients with candidemia (Fig. 1). In addition, we explored potential non-linear relationships and validated cluster consistency by performing t-SNE on the FAMD coordinates (Supplementary Fig. 1). The t-SNE projection did not preserve global distances like FAMD, but it did confirm the three-cluster structure in a non-linear embedding space, with patients grouped together by assigned phenotype. This complementary visualization demonstrates the robustness of the identified phenotypes to different dimensionality reduction approaches.

Internal validation supported the robustness of this three-cluster solution. The mean silhouette width was 0.20 (Cluster 1: 0.30; Cluster 2: 0.23; Cluster 3: 0.13), indicating moderate separation, the Calinski–Harabasz index was 106.8 and the Davies–Bouldin index was 1.65, both these results being consistent with acceptable cluster quality. Comparison of HCPC-derived clusters with k-means partitions applied to the same FAMD components showed perfect agreement (ARI = 1.00), confirming identical patient allocation across algorithmic approaches. In contrast, clustering applied to t-SNE coordinates showed lower concordance (ARI = 0.45), consistent with the visualization-oriented nature of this non-linear technique. In addition, internal validation through resampling (500 iterations, 80% subsamples) confirmed the robustness of the three-cluster solution, with a mean ARI of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.28–0.90), indicating substantial stability. This suggests that the identified phenotypes reflect reproducible clinical patterns rather than artifacts of the specific cohort composition.

The Phenotype 1 (n = 70) group included hematological malignancies in 58 (82.9%) patients and neutropenia in 47 patients (67.1%); 68 (97.1%) patients had hematological disease, neutropenia or both. These patients also had high SAPS II scores (median: 70 [54-84]), higher temperatures at ICU admission (median 38.35 °C [37.50-39.27 °C]) and low blood leukocyte counts (median: 0.45 [0.10-5.60] × 10⁹/L). The Phenotype 2 (n = 223) group included older patients (median age: 66 [55-72] years) with cirrhosis in 43 (19.3%), high blood lactate concentrations at admission (median: 4.10 [2.26-7.22] mmol/L), candidemia that occurred earlier during ICU stay (median: 3.0 [0.0-8.0] days) and was mostly digestive (148 [66.4%]), with intubation within 24 h in 169 (75.8%) patients and frequent surgical ICU admission (38.6%), suggesting that many of these patients may have undergone abdominal surgery. This is supported by the predominance of digestive source of candidemia (66.4%) in this phenotype. The patients in the Phenotype 3 (n = 199) group were younger with lower SAPS II scores (median: 43 [32-52]), candidemia was a later onset (median: 9.0 [1.0-19.5] days), and were more likely to have catheter-related (61 [30.7%] proven, 39 [19.6%] suspected) or cutaneous (13 [6.5%]) sources of infection. Candida albicans was the predominant species detected in all phenotypes, but the distributions of other species differed between phenotypes: C. krusei was more frequent in Phenotype 1 (10.0% vs. 3.4% overall), C. glabrata in Phenotype 2 (24.2% vs. 15.4% overall), and C. parapsilosis in Phenotype 3 (8.0% vs. 4.3% overall) (p < 0.001 for inter-phenotype comparisons) (Table 1). Regarding the detailed other cause of ICU admission, Phenotype 1 and 2 were predominantly composed of patients admitted for shock, while Phenotype 3 included a mix of medical admissions, with a notable proportion of patients admitted for respiratory failure (28%) (Supplementary Table 1).

Mortality at day 90 was 51/70 (72.9%) for phenotype 1, 157/223 (70.4%) for phenotype 2, and 100/199 (50.3%) for phenotype 3 (p < 0.001). Acute kidney injury occurred in 55 (78.6%), 187 (84.2%) and 129 (65.2%) patients with phenotypes 1, 2 and 3, respectively (p < 0.001); renal replacement therapy was reported in 31 (44.3%), 121 (54.3%) and 76 (38.4%) patients, respectively (p = 0.005); mechanical ventilation was reported in 57 (81.4%), 206 (92.4%) and 152 (76.4%) patients, respectively (p < 0.001); and ventilator-associated pneumonia was reported in 20 (28.6%), 40 (18.1%) and 64 (32.7%) patients, respectively (p = 0.002). In total, 376 patients were screened for secondary localizations, which were detected in 18/50 (36.0%) patients with phenotype 1, 31/154 (20.1%) patients with phenotype 2, and 33/163 (20.2%) patients with phenotype 3 (p = 0.045). The duration of ICU stay was 13 [6-28], 13 [7-24] and 23 [9-45] days, respectively, and the duration of hospital stay was 31 [18-58], 23 [12-46] and 47 [25-86] days, respectively (p < 0.001 for both) (Table 2). In addition, day-90 ICU-free survival was greater for phenotype 3 (0 [0-46], 0 [0-62] and 19 [0-72] days, respectively, p = 0.001) (Table 2).

Kaplan–Meier survival curves up to day 90 for the three phenotypes revealed that Phenotype 3 patients had significantly longer survival than those in Phenotype 1 or 2 (log-rank test: p < 0.001; Fig. 2). Pairwise comparisons using Cox proportional hazards models showed that mortality was significantly lower in Phenotype 3 compared to Phenotype 1 (HR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.42-0.82; p = 0.002) and compared to Phenotype 2 (HR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.41-0.81; p = 0.001). No significant difference was observed between Phenotypes 1 and 2 (HR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.75-1.41; p = 0.862).

Patient characteristics according to 90-day survival

Overall, 308 patients (62.6%) died within 90 days of candidemia onset. The non-survivors were older (median: 65 [55-72] years vs. 60 [49-69] years; p < 0.001), more frequently cirrhotic (15.6% vs. 6.5%; p = 0.005), neutropenic (13.0% vs. 6.5%; p = 0.035), and suffering from hematological malignancies (20.1% vs. 9.8%; p = 0.004) than the survivors (n = 184). SOT was more common among survivors (6.5% vs. 2.3%; p = 0.034) (Supplementary Table 2).

Non-survivors had higher SAPS II scores (median: 61 [49-77] vs. 46 [35-61]; p < 0.001), higher blood lactate concentrations at ICU admission (median: 2.7 [1.7-5.2] vs. 2.2 [1.4-4.1] mmol/L; p = 0.002), lower blood leukocyte counts (median: 10.94 [4.30-19.18] vs. 12.97 [6.38-19.87] 10⁹/L; p = 0.029), and were more frequently intubated within 24 h of ICU admission (91.2% vs. 72.8%; p < 0.001).

The time from ICU admission to candidemia onset was shorter in non-survivors than in survivors (median: 3 days [0-11] vs. 5 days [1-12], respectively; p = 0.003). A digestive source of candidemia was more frequent among non-survivors (50.6% vs. 35.3%), whereas proven catheter-related candidemia was more frequent among survivors (26.6% vs. 11.4%) (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 2).

Survival analysis

Survival analysis (Table 3) revealed that older age (HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01-1.03; p < 0.001), obesity (HR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.01-1.68; p = 0.039), cirrhosis (HR: 1.80, 95% CI: 1.32-2.46; p < 0.001), hematological malignancies (HR: 1.44, 95% CI: 1.09-1.90; p = 0.011), higher SAPS II score (HR: 1.02 per point, 95% CI: 1.01-1.02; p < 0.001), higher lactate concentration at ICU admission (HR: 1.04 per mmol/L, 95% CI: 1.01-1.07; p = 0.002) were significantly associated with 90-day mortality. Conversely, proven catheter-related candidemia was associated with a markedly lower risk of death (HR: 0.41, 95% CI: 0.28-0.59; p < 0.001) as was a surgical reason for ICU admission (HR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.54-0.91; p = 0.007). Among treatment-related variables, echinocandin use was strongly associated with reduced mortality (HR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.38-0.62; p < 0.001), as was effective initial antifungal treatment (HR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.49-0.89; p = 0.007).

In adjusted survival analysis, six variables remained independent predictors of 90-day mortality after backward stepwise selection: age (aHR: 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00-1.02; p = 0.003), cirrhosis (aHR: 1.90, 95% CI: 1.39-2.60; p < 0.001), SAPS II score (aHR: 1.01 per point, 95% CI: 1.01-1.02; p < 0.001), proven catheter-related candidemia (aHR: 0.48, 95% CI: 0.33-0.69; p < 0.001), surgical reason for ICU admission (aHR: 0.67, 95% CI: 0.51-0.87; p = 0.003), and echinocandin use (aHR: 0.49, 95% CI: 0.39-0.63; p < 0.001) (Table 3). All VIFs were below 1.2, indicating no concerning collinearity. In LASSO analysis, all variables from the primary model were retained under the optimal lambda.min criterion. Hazard ratios from the LASSO-based Cox model were nearly identical to the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 3), confirming model stability.

Discussion

In this post-hoc analysis of the CandidICU multicenter cohort, we used unsupervised machine learning to delineate various clinical phenotypes of candidemia in critically ill patients. Using FAMD followed by HCPC, we identified three distinct phenotypes with markedly different clinical profiles and outcomes.

Risk stratification for mortality in ICU-acquired candidemia remains challenging. In their extensive literature review, Reinhold et al. proposed descriptions of complicated and non-complicated candidemia to facilitate the adaptation of patient management [7]. The post-hoc analysis of the CandidICU study presented here made it possible to identify risk factors in a real-life cohort of patients from 16 French ICUs recruited in various ways (medical and surgical ICUs, from university and general hospitals), representative of the huge diversity of patients with candidemia. The initial competing risk analysis identified SAPSII score and the time for which blood cultures remained positive as independent risk factors for complicated candidemia with a secondary localization. Noteworthy, the 76.4% screening rate for secondary localizations reflects real-life practice across 16 French ICUs, where screening decisions were influenced by early mortality, debated recommendations, examination availability, and clinician discretion, as previously described [6]. Building on these previous findings, our unsupervised approach identified three clinically different phenotypes with divergent outcomes, facilitating the transition from complication-based classification to host-centered phenotyping.

Phenotype 3 had significantly lower relative mortality (50.3% vs. 70.4-72.9%, p < 0.001), though 50% absolute mortality remains substantial. This phenotype was characterized by lower illness severity, a higher rate of catheter-related infections, and more Candida parapsilosis. These features suggest that source control via catheter removal may reduce the need for prolonged antifungal therapy. While not ‘low-risk’ in absolute terms, the significant mortality difference compared to other phenotypes raises questions about whether current standard protocols are equally appropriate for all patients. The profile of Phenotype 3, characterized by lower severity and a higher rate of catheter-related infections, could be of interest for future investigations into de-escalation strategies, although this hypothesis requires formal testing in dedicated trials. The high mortality in Phenotype 1 despite standard therapy highlights the need for alternative approaches. While combinations of antifungal drugs with immunotherapy have shown promise in Aspergillus infections, their role in candidemia remains unexplored and warrants investigation [14–16]. For the clinician, identifying a patient fitting Phenotype 1 (severe immunosuppression with hematological malignancy and neutropenia) should prompt a low threshold for screening secondary localizations (present in 36%), consideration of antifungal prophylaxis review, a precise Candida species identification and antifungal susceptibility testing, and close monitoring for complications. Moreover, several clinical features classically associated with candidemia risk warrant discussion in relation to our phenotypes. The high proportion of surgical ICU admissions in Phenotype 2, combined with digestive sources, suggests that abdominal surgery may characterize this group. Phenotype 3 shows enrichment for catheter-related candidemia and C. parapsilosis. However, detailed data on catheter characteristics and surgical specifics were unavailable. Moreover, despite having the highest proportion of proven catheter-related candidemia (a factor independently associated with lower mortality) the persistent 50% mortality in Phenotype 3 reflects several realities: first, source identification does not guarantee timely source control, and some cases are diagnosed too late for effective catheter removal; second, even ‘controllable’ sources of candidemia carry inherent risk in critically ill patients; third, it is important to note that catheter-related infections, while overrepresented in this phenotype, represent only a minority of cases (30.7% proven, 19.6% suspected), with the remaining patients having other sources of infection that may carry higher intrinsic risk. The protective effect of catheter-related origin reduces mortality compared to other sources but does not eliminate it, explaining the 50% mortality in this group while still representing a 20% absolute risk reduction compared to other phenotypes. The mechanisms underlying this protective effect, while plausible, remain hypothetical and warrant proper investigation in future dedicated studies designed specifically to address the role of source control timing and biofilm disruption in catheter-related candidemia. Beyond source-related considerations, the higher proportion of respiratory source in Phenotype 1 (14.3%) likely reflects the susceptibility of severely immunocompromised patients to deep-seated fungal infections, including Candida pneumonia. However, respiratory foci are difficult to definitively establish in critically ill patients and may represent colonization or secondary hematogenous seeding rather than primary infection. Noteworthy, in our cohort, echinocandin use emerged as a strong independent protective factor, with a 51% relative reduction in mortality after adjustment for other covariates. This finding aligns with current guidelines recommending echinocandins as first-line therapy for critically ill patients with candidemia. Furthermore, the earlier onset of candidemia in Phenotypes 1 and 2 likely reflects rapid translocation in immunosuppressed patients and early postoperative complications, while later onset in Phenotype 3 is consistent with time-dependent risk factors such as prolonged catheterization. However, immortality bias may also contribute, as higher early mortality in Phenotypes 1 and 2 limits their opportunity to develop late-onset candidemia.

Although Phenotypes 1 and 3 had similar age distributions, the survival advantage of Phenotype 3 reflects its lower illness severity, higher rate of controllable catheter-related infections, and distinct microbiological profile-factors that outweigh the prognostic impact of age in this population. For Phenotype 3, the predominance of catheter-related infection and C. parapsilosis should encourage prompt catheter removal when possible, and may justify consideration of earlier step-down to fluconazole once susceptibility is confirmed because of the recent and increased risk of emergence of resistance [17].

Interestingly, we identified two phenotypes among patients with candidemia who included some immunocompromised individuals. Phenotype 1 patients had very severe disease and high mortality (up to 72.9%), and were characterized by profound immunosuppression, predominantly hematological malignancies and neutropenia. Fluconazole-resistant species, such as C. krusei, were slightly more frequent in this cluster, likely due to prophylaxis-mediated selection. Phenotype 2, the largest group (n = 223, 45.3%), was primarily defined by older age, a high burden of cirrhosis (19.3%), and frequent postoperative ICU admissions (38.6%). While 106 patients (47.5%) in this group had some form of immunosuppression (mostly solid tumors), the majority were not immunocompromised. These patients presented with apparently moderate but significant disease severity (median SAPS II 66), but one of the main findings of this study was that patients with this phenotype have a 90-day mortality as high as that in patients with strong immunosuppression (70.4%), with a high frequency of complications necessitating organ support, such as mechanical ventilation (92.4%) and renal replacement therapy (54.3%). A digestive source of Candida predominated and the prevalence of Candida glabrata was high in the group of patients with this phenotype, justifying echinocandin treatment and an optimization of the management of associated organ failures. These risk factors are consistent with those identified in a recent single-center patients including 1,985 patients, 416 of whom had cirrhosis, and 37 of whom developed candidemia, including 16 with cirrhosis, resulting in a prevalence of candidemia of 91 per 1,000 ICU patients with cirrhosis) [18]. In this study, cirrhosis emerged as an independent risk factor for candidemia in the ICU, with a severity proportional to Child–Pugh score [18]. The impairment of both innate and adaptive immune responses observed during hepatic failure may increase the risk of candidemia and contribute to higher mortality [19, 20]. In Phenotype 2, the combination of digestive source, cirrhosis, and high prevalence of C. glabrata (24.2%) reinforces the importance of echinocandin therapy, combined with screening for potential echinocandin in vitro resistance (or molecular characterization of FKS gene mutations), and aggressive surgical source control, as well as anticipation of organ support needs given the high rates of mechanical ventilation (92.4%) and renal replacement therapy (54.3%).

Our phenotype-based stratification has several therapeutic implications. Strengthening host immunity (potentially through treatment with a combination of checkpoint inhibitors and antifungal drugs) may be beneficial in the high-mortality Phenotypes 1 and 2, although prospective validation is required to confirm this.

More broadly, the time may have come to rethink the design of candidemia trials. Most trials currently enroll all patients with a positive blood culture. Future studies should instead focus on more homogeneous groups defined by clinical phenotype. For example, patients with Phenotype 3 (characterized by lower severity and higher survival rates) may represent an appropriate subgroup for future non-inferiority trials testing shorter antifungal courses or newer agents such as rezafungine and fosmanogepix, given their more favorable risk profile compared to other phenotypes.

Conversely, research on the high-risk Phenotypes 1 and 2 (these groups include immunocompromised and cirrhotic patients) could explore different questions. These studies could investigate combination therapies, assess immunomodulatory strategies, and refine phenotype-specific management to address the distinctive pathophysiology and high mortality of these phenotypes.

The use of FAMD followed by HCPC enabled integration of heterogeneous clinical and microbiological variables within a unified analytical framework, without arbitrary transformation of categorical variables. Although alternative clustering approaches exist, this framework was well suited to the mixed data structure and exploratory phenotyping objective of our cohort. Along these lines, the perfect agreement between HCPC and k-means clustering supports the robustness of the identified phenotypes, whereas lower concordance with clustering on t-SNE coordinates reflects the visualization-oriented nature of this non-linear method. This targeted, phenotype-driven approach would generate evidence more directly applicable to specific patient profiles, accelerating progress toward personalized care in candidemia. Actually, the data-driven phenotyping approach used in this study complements traditional clinical reasoning by providing quantitative thresholds, revealing unexpected microbiological associations (such as the clustering of C. glabrata with Phenotype 2 and C. parapsilosis with Phenotype 3), and generating testable hypotheses for future therapeutic trials. By deriving phenotypes solely from baseline characteristics, this data-driven approach provides an unbiased description of patient heterogeneity. The protective effect of both catheter-related candidemia and surgical ICU admission likely reflects the common mechanism of effective source control (whether through catheter removal, drainage of intra-abdominal collections, or surgical debridement). These findings reinforce current recommendations emphasizing prompt source control in eligible patients and highlight the prognostic importance of this intervention. In addition, closer postoperative monitoring may facilitate earlier diagnosis and treatment initiation. These findings suggest that the survival advantage observed in surgical patients may be mediated by timely and effective source control strategies, beyond catheter removal alone.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design entails a risk of residual confounding and potential selection bias, despite the multicenter and consecutive nature of the cohort. Second, the clustering process necessarily depends on the variables available in the dataset; relevant host or infection-related factors not captured in the CandiICU study may have influenced phenotype assignment [6]. Third, although the multicenter and consecutive nature of the cohort enhances representativeness, the model was derived and evaluated within the same population without external validation. Bootstrap resampling confirmed internal robustness (mean ARI 0.74), but generalizability to other settings remains to be established. External validation in independent cohorts is essential before clinical implementation. The clustering process is inherently dependent on available variables; relevant factors not captured may have influenced phenotype assignment. The modest silhouette score (0.20) highlights partial overlap between groups, expected in complex syndromes like candidemia but warranting cautious interpretation. Similarly, we must also acknowledge that the therapeutic implications suggested by our phenotypes, while clinically plausible, remain hypothetical and require prospective validation. These phenotypes reflect observed patterns that align with clinical impressions but should not yet guide routine clinical decisions. Future interventional studies are needed to determine whether phenotype-guided management improves outcomes. Fourth, treatment variables (including the timing and choice of antifungal therapy and source control procedures) were not incorporated into the clustering analysis to prevent circularity, thereby limiting our ability to draw conclusions regarding phenotype-specific therapeutic responses. Finally, concerning our survival analysis, although stepwise selection has recognized limitations, the consistency between stepwise and LASSO approaches, together with low VIFs (< 1.2), supports the robustness of the identified mortality predictors. Moreover, long-term immunosuppressive therapies other than corticosteroids were not systematically recorded, and cumulative corticosteroid doses could not be precisely quantified due to the retrospective design. Similarly, although data on prior antifungal exposure were complete in our dataset, we acknowledge that retrospective ascertainment of this information is inherently subject to potential misclassification, as medication histories may not have been perfectly available for all patients, particularly those transferred from other institutions (a limitation common to all retrospective studies). Furthermore, solid organ transplantation and HIV infection were infrequent in our cohort and were excluded from clustering analyses to avoid instability related to near-zero variance variables, although these patients were retained in descriptive and outcome analyses. Additionally, because our study focused on clinical phenotypes and outcomes in patients with established candidemia, we did not collect detailed information on catheter characteristics (duration, tunneling, total parenteral nutrition use) or on the specific nature of abdominal surgery. These factors, while important for understanding the pathophysiology of candidemia acquisition, were outside the scope of our investigation and represent an important direction for future research. Similarly, while we captured echinocandin use and effective antifungal therapy, detailed data on catheter removal or other source control procedures were not systematically available for the entire cohort and could not be included in the multivariable analysis. These factors should be addressed in future prospective studies. Finally, although data on prior antifungal exposure were complete in our dataset, we acknowledge that retrospective ascertainment of this information is inherently subject to potential misclassification, as medication histories may not have been perfectly available for all patients, particularly those transferred from other institutions (a limitation common to all retrospective studies).

In conclusion, in this multicenter cohort of ICU patients, unsupervised clustering suggests that candidemia may encompass distinctive clinical phenotypes with different host characteristics, microbiological profiles, and markedly different outcomes. Further validation is needed, but this phenotype-based approach could provide a framework complementary to traditional classifications, potentially making it possible to address the clinical heterogeneity observed in critically ill patients with bloodstream Candida infections.
