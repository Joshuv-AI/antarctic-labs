ABSTRACT
Ion sources and ion beams often contain ions of more than one charge-to-mass ratio, due to the
presence of multiple ionization states and/or multiple atomic and molecular ion species in the
source. We show that, under certain conditions, analysis of these systems can be simplified using
the notion of “equivalent equilibrium ion distributions” (EEIDs). These distributions have identical
ion trajectories, space-charge distributions, and electric-field distributions, but can be composed
of ion species with arbitrary charge-to-mass ratios. We use this result to obtain multi-species
generalizations for the perveance of an ion diode, which relates the diode voltage to the extracted
beam current, and for the perveance of a drifting ion beam, which determines the axial length-scale
for beam expansion due to the self-electric field. Applied to a quasi-neutral plasma ion source
containing cold ions and hot electrons, we find that the location of the beam-plasma interface
(meniscus), where ions are extracted from the plasma, depends on the electron density and ion
energy per unit charge at the interface, independent of the ion composition. For certain types of
numerical simulations, EEIDs allow one to calculate the beam optics of an intense multi-species ion
source by doing a simulation for a single ion species with an artificial charge-to-mass ratio chosen to
minimize computation time. We illustrate these results using the 3D particle-in-cell, finite-element
code Aleph. The simulations show how ions in a plasma source self-consistently accelerate to the
supersonic speed needed for steady, radially expanding flow. Multi-species equilibria are subject
to streaming instabilities because, while the different species have the same kinetic energy per unit
charge, their velocities scale with the square root of the charge-to-mass ratio. The simulations show
that a strong ion streaming instability can occur in a multi-species plasma ion source.

1. INTRODUCTION

Many types of sources are used to generate ion beams. Techniques have been developed to obtain
free ions starting from the solid, liquid, or gas state of the desired atomic or molecular species
at STP (standard temperature and pressure) [1]. In the case of gases, ions are typically produced
through bulk electrical breakdown of an initially neutral working gas [2], while ions of metallic
elements can be produced by creating a “vacuum arc” between electrodes where the cathode (and
sometimes the anode) is made from the particular material of interest [3]. These sources and the
beams they produce often contain ions of more than one charge-to-mass ratio, due to the presence of
multiple ionization states and/or multiple atomic and molecular ion species in the source. In many
applications, one particular ion species and charge-to-mass ratio is desired and other ion species are
considered to be contaminants that may degrade performance. In this report, we examine sources
where the ions are subject only to long-range electrostatic forces, neglecting short-range collisions
involving the ion species. Such collisions are, of course, essential to produce ions from neutral
materials, but we assume that electron-ion and ion-neutral collisions have a negligible effect on
the ion motion in the region of interest. We show that the distribution of space-charge and electric
field generated by a single-ion-species solution to the time-independent Vlasov-Poisson equations
(where a steady-state distribution of electrons may also be present) can be realized by any other
positively-charged species (or mixture thereof) when certain conditions are met. We use the term
“equivalent equilibrium ion distributions” (EEIDs) to denote these interchangeable ion distributions.
The speedup of numerical simulations of ion sources and ion beams enabled by EEIDs has been in
use since 2012, but the physical justification has not previously been documented.
This report is organized as follows. In Sec. 2, we obtain the conditions under which ion species
are interchangeable in an electrostatic equilibrium, first using a particle trajectory (“Lagrangian”)
treatment, and then using the Vlasov-Poisson equations for the single-particle distribution function.
Section 3 applies the results of Sec. 2 to two non-neutral ion equilibria: space-charge-limited
emission of ions from the surface of an electrode and ion-beam transport in an accelerator. We
obtain multi-species generalizations for the perveance of an ion diode and for the perveance of a
drifting ion beam. In Sec. 4, we apply the results to the current-density matching condition for
ion extraction from a plasma ion source. Section 5 illustrates the results using particle-in-cell
simulations of an ion source with an ion extraction aperture and accelerating gap. A “reference”
simulation of an ion-source equilibrium is carried out with an ion charge-to-mass ratio chosen to
minimize computation time. We then verify that EEIDs derived from this equilibrium have the same
ion trajectories in both the quasi-neutral and non-neutral regions. We find that the EEID containing
two ion species exhibits a strong ion streaming instability. Section 6 gives our conclusions.

2. ELECTROSTATIC ION EQUILIBRIA

We start by considering a steady-state ion-electron plasma within a finite volume Ω bounded
by a closed surface Γ. A steady-state source of particles is provided either by ionization of a
constant neutral-particle density within Ω, or by injection of ions and electrons through a subset
of Γ. A particle sink is realized by removing particles that strike internal solid boundaries and
external solid or “open” boundaries. An example of this type of system is the plasma source/ion
diode shown in Fig. 2-1, where Ω is the shaded region that includes both the plasma and the ion
diode. We assume that the electrode attached to the ionization power supply maintains a constant
electron temperature that is sufficient to ionize the neutral background gas. We further assume

4. ION DIODE WITH A PLASMA SOURCE

Sections 3.1 and 3.2 dealt with ideal space-charge-limited sources capable of emitting arbitrarily
large current densities. In a plasma-source ion diode [1, 3], however, the ion current that can be
extracted is limited by the rate at which ions are created in the source. This leads to a matching
condition for steady-state operation. In this section, we give the single-species matching condition
and then generalize it to the multi-species case.

4.1. Matching condition for a single-species ion source

The extraction region of a plasma-source ion diode is essentially a plasma sheath where, through
the use of an appropriate electrode configuration and voltages, the potential continues to decrease as
one moves away from the surface of the plasma, accelerating the ions to an energy much larger than
the energy at which they enter the plasma sheath (see Fig. 2-1). The potential completely confines
the plasma electrons in the direction normal to the surface of the plasma. The interface between
the quasi-neutral plasma and the non-neutral ion beam is often called the plasma meniscus because
of its typical curved shape in 2- or 3-dimensional geometry. This curvature can be exploited to

5. NUMERICAL SIMULATIONS OF EEIDS

5.1. Semi-implicit algorithm

In this section we illustrate the results of Sec. 4 with numerical simulations of the plasma ion source
and ion diode shown in Fig. 5-1. We used the electrostatic, finite-element, particle-in-cell (PIC) code
Aleph [22] to carry out the simulations. We generated tetrahedral-cell meshes using Cubit [23], and
visualized the results using ParaView [24]. In Aleph, particle positions and velocities are advanced
in time using an explicit time-centered leap-frog scheme. The electrostatic potential is represented
using linear finite elements, and the electric field is therefore spatially constant in each cell. This
combination yields an energy-conserving field-particle interaction as the timestep Δ𝑡 → 0, and
mitigates numerical heating caused by aliasing in “momentum-conserving” algorithms [25, 26]. To
avoid having to resolve the electron plasma frequency and the electron Debye length (except where
necessary), we followed the “semi-implicit” prescription given by Barnes [27], which combines
the particle push just described with the modified Poisson equation found in “direct-implicit” PIC
algorithms [28]:
 𝜌
                                          −∇ · (1 + 𝜒) ∇𝜙 =                                        (5.1)
 𝜖0
where 𝜒 = 𝐶4 𝜔2𝑝𝑒 Δ𝑡 2 is the “implicit susceptibility” due to electrons, 𝜔 𝑝𝑒 is the electron plasma
frequency, and 𝐶 is a numerical constant. The term 𝐶𝜔2𝑝𝑒 Δ𝑡 2 /4 arises from the acceleration of
electrons in the electric field due to local charge-imbalance between ions and electrons. This field
causes the local electrons to move in the direction that tends to restore charge-neutrality. In one
timestep, the electrons move a distance proportional to 𝑒∇𝜙Δ𝑡 2 /𝑚 𝑒 , and when the resulting density
perturbation is summed over these electrons it appears as the susceptibility term, 𝜒, in Eq. (5.1).
It is an implicit term because the 𝜙 used to calculate the density perturbation is evaluated at the
same time as the 𝜙 being solved for in the Poisson equation. In Ref. [27], 𝐶 is treated as an
arbitrary constant, unlike the direct-implicit formulation, where 𝐶 also appears (as the parameter
𝛽 ≡ 𝐶/4) in the particle advance algorithm and is restricted to 0 < 𝛽 ≲ 1 [28]. For 𝐶 > 1, a
stable algorithm is obtained that is not subject to the explicit-PIC stability condition Δ𝑡 ≲ 2/𝜔 𝑝𝑒
(see Appendix A.1). The algorithm was implemented in Aleph by the author in collaboration with
D. Barnes and has been used for electrostatic simulations of dense quasi-neutral plasmas where
electron plasma oscillations do not play a significant role in the dynamics.

5.5. Particle noise reduction

In carrying out the simulations in Secs. 5.3 and 5.4, we took advantage of noise reduction made
possible by the semi-implicit algorithm (Sec. 5.1). In the plasma creation and expansion regions of
Fig. 5-1, we set the coefficient 𝐶 in Eq. (5.1) to a value of 1800. As shown in Appendix A.2, this
suppresses short-wavelength components of the potential that are generated by density fluctuations
arising from the discrete-particle noise endemic to the PIC method, while preserving the correct
long-wavelength components of the potential. In the aperture and accelerating gap, we used 𝐶 = 18,
which we found to be small enough to resolve the potential variation at the plasma meniscus.

physically real. When we repeated the two-species calculation in Fig. 5-8 using 𝐶 = 180 instead

6. CONCLUSIONS

We have shown that, under certain conditions, ions in a given single-ion-species electrostatic
equilibrium can be replaced by an ion species with a different charge-to-mass ratio, or by a mixture
of species, while preserving the ion trajectories, space-charge distribution, and electrostatic field.
We refer to these related equilibria as equivalent equilibrium ion distributions (EEIDs). The EEID
conditions were derived using both particle-trajectory and Vlasov-Poisson formulations.
The EEID formalism was applied to several common ion-beam problems. For non-neutral equilib-
ria, multi-species generalizations were obtained for the space-charge-limited current and perveance
of an ion diode, and for the generalized perveance that determines ion-beam expansion due to
space-charge. These results show how the generalized perveance depends on the ion composition
through the species current fractions and charge-to-mass ratios.
For an ion diode with a quasi-neutral plasma source, the analysis showed that the matching condition
at the plasma meniscus can be written in terms of the electron density and ion energy per unit charge
at the sheath edge, independent of the ion composition. Likewise, the generalized perveance of the
extracted ion beam can be written in terms of the electron density and ion energy per unit charge
at the sheath edge.
Particle-in-cell simulations with Aleph were used to illustrate these results. Exact EEIDs con-
structed from a reference single-species equilibrium produced the same meniscus shape, ion tra-
jectories, and extracted beam behavior for both single-species and two-species cases. Approximate
EEIDs were demonstrated for cases in which the electron production rate was not the same as in
the reference case, but was set equal to the ion production rate for the ion species or mixture of ion
species that replaced the ions in the reference simulation. The difference in the results was largely
confined to the sheath regions.
A practical consequence is that steady-state beam optics for a single- or multi-species ion source can
be obtained from a numerically convenient single-species simulation with an artificial charge-to-
mass ratio chosen to minimize computation time. The results also show, however, that multi-species
EEIDs can be susceptible to ion streaming instabilities because the different species have different
drift velocities even when they have the same kinetic energy per unit charge. The simulations
presented show such an instability in a two-species plasma, producing a large perturbation in the
density of the minority species.

 oscillations do not need to be resolved in the simulations in Sec. 5, but their existence
 imposes a numerical stability constraint on the timestep: 𝜔 𝑝𝑒 Δ𝑡 ≲ 2 (see Appendix A.1).
 The semi-implicit algorithm used for the simulations (Sec. 5.1) avoids this constraint. In our
 simulations, the timestep was instead limited by the need to resolve electrons being reflected
 at the plasma meniscus.
  2. |𝑣𝑖 |Δ𝑡/Δ𝑥 and |𝑣 𝑒 |Δ𝑡/Δ𝑥, where |𝑣 𝑖 |, |𝑣 𝑒 | are averages of the magnitude of the particle
 velocities in a cell (Fig. B-2). These metrics measure the accuracy of the integration of the
     particle orbits. The value of |𝑣 𝑖 |Δ𝑡/Δ𝑥 significantly increases in the accelerating gap because
 of the large increase in the ion speed. The plot in Fig. B-2 exaggerates the effect because Δ𝑥 is
 approximated by the cube root of the tetrahedral cell volume, but the cells in the accelerating
 gap are elongated in the axial direction (see Figs. 5-3 and 5-4)
  3. Δ𝑥/𝜆 𝐷𝑒 (Fig. B-3): This measures how large the cells are compared to the local electron
 Debye length. In explicit, momentum-conserving algorithms, Δ𝑥/𝜆 𝐷𝑒 ≲ 1 is needed to
 avoid numerical heating of the electrons. This is a severe constraint in a dense plasma since
 √
 𝜆 𝐷𝑒 ∝ 1/ 𝑛𝑒 . The algorithm used for the simulations allows Δ𝑥/𝜆 𝐷𝑒 ≫ 1 while avoiding
 numerical heating. In the densest part of the plasma, we have Δ𝑥/𝜆 𝐷𝑒 ≈ 20. At the meniscus,
 however, Δ𝑥 needs to be of order 𝜆 𝐷𝑒 or smaller to obtain the correct location of the plasma
 surface.
  4. Δ𝑥/𝜆 𝐷𝑒 , where 𝜆 𝐷𝑒 is the modified Debye length in Eq. A.6 (Fig. B-4). The semi-implicit
 algorithm artificially increases the physical Debye length. This is useful for reducing particle
 noise (see Appendix A.2). Using a coefficient 𝐶 = 1800 in the plasma creation and expansion
 regions yields the low-noise potential profile in Fig. 5-13 and ion phase-space in Fig. 5-16,
 while preserving the physical ion acceleration due to the small excess of positive charge in
 the plasma. Near the meniscus, however, the spatial scale over which the electric potential
 varies is the physical Debye length, so that large values of 𝐶 cannot be used. We found that
 simulations with 𝐶 = 18 (as in Fig. B-4(b)) and 𝐶 = 4 give essentially the same meniscus
 location and downstream ion trajectories. As shown in Sec. 5.6, instabilities with wavelengths
 on the order of the physical Debye length can occur, and these are not properly resolved if 𝐶
 is too large.
