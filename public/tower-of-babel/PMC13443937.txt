Explainable generative 3D design driven by social media semantics
Authors: Zhenyu Wang, Karen Sato, Jianmin Wang, Preben Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough social media extensively captures user preferences and aesthetic intentions, its highly unstructured and emotionally expressive semantics have long been regarded as noise that is difficult to translate into reliable generative cues. Here we show that social media semantics contain stable patterns that can be systematically abstracted and validated, and that these patterns exert interpretable effects on the structural quality and semantic consistency of three-dimensional (3D) generation. Using 60,500 real-world social media posts, we validate this finding through CoX-3D, an explainable generative framework that introduces an Explainable Mediation Layer to bridge macroscopic social computing with microscopic physical rendering. Incorporating these stable semantic patterns reduces structural reconstruction error by over 50% and improves semantic consistency, measured by CLIP similarity, by 13.1%. Semantic activation and attribution analyses further reveal differentiated roles of semantic factors in geometric and stylistic decisions. These results demonstrate that social media semantics constitute a systematically modelable and interpretable cognitive resource, establishing a generalizable paradigm for socially informed generative 3D design.Subject terms: Mechanical engineering, Software

Introduction

Social media is reshaping human expression and preference formation at an unprecedented scale1. Hundreds of millions of users continuously articulate their immediate responses to product form, style, material, and usage experience through text, images and emotionally expressive language2,3. These user-generated contents (UGCs) are no longer regarded solely as sources of informational noise, but are increasingly recognised as containing latent semantic signals that reflect user preferences and collective patterns of social cognition4. In the early stages of design, where human aesthetic judgment and creative decision-making play a central role, a key challenge for data-driven design and human–AI collaborative design lies in transforming these highly dynamic and widely distributed social semantics into structured knowledge that can inform design decisions.

However, social media semantics are not equivalent to actionable design requirements. Compared with traditional survey or interview data, expressions in UGC are often driven by emotion, social motivation, and individual experience, resulting in semantics that are highly unstructured, noisy and weakly constrained5. When social media content is directly used as input for generative design, short-term emotions or incidental preferences can be easily misinterpreted as stable requirements, which undermines the credibility of design decisions and the reliability of generated outcomes in practical applications6. A central challenge, therefore, lies in identifying stable and interpretable demand cues that transcend individual emotional expression within high-noise and weakly constrained social semantics, and in transforming these cues into controllable drivers for design generation.

Meanwhile, generative artificial intelligence has substantially lowered the technical barriers to 3D modelling and content creation. Text-to-3D7 and image-to-3D8 approaches have been widely adopted in various domains9–12, enabling the rapid generation of high-quality 3D assets13. However, existing 3D generation pipelines largely operate under an end-to-end, black-box optimisation paradigm. In such workflows, textual prompts are implicitly mapped to latent geometric spaces, leaving the translation of semantic intent into physical geometry and texturing fundamentally opaque14,15. Most current 3D texture generation approaches also operate primarily at the RGB colour level, struggling to support physically based rendering (PBR) attributes required for industrially viable workflows16. This prompt-and-pray mechanism severely limits their applicability in professional design contexts, where traceability and systematic controllability are paramount.

The fundamental technical bottleneck, therefore, is not a lack of generative capacity, but the absence of an explainable mediation Layer that can explicitly govern the transition from unstructured human semantics to physically-based 3D parameters.

Recent studies suggest that introducing explainable mechanisms and human-in-the-loop strategies into complex generative tasks can improve model credibility and user trust17,18. Nevertheless, a unified and explainable generative framework that can support real design decision-making is still lacking. In practical design contexts19,20, three fundamental questions remain unresolved: (RQ1) how stable design-relevant semantics can be extracted from large-scale social media data; (RQ2) how these semantics operate during the 3D generation process; and (RQ3) whether semantic or parameter adjustments lead to predictable changes in geometry and material properties.

This methodological gap is particularly pronounced in early-stage design, where user preferences and aesthetic judgments play a dominant role21,22. To systematically address these challenges, we propose CoX-3D, a co-explainable 3D design framework driven by social media semantics. Rather than treating social media content as direct design requirements, CoX-3D conceptualises social media semantics as demand cues and employs systematic semantic modelling and consistency validation to identify patterns that recur stably across users and contexts within large-scale user-generated data. These demand cues function as an interpretable mediation layer that guides subsequent 3D generation and optimisation, establishing analyzable and verifiable links between semantic modelling and geometric synthesis. The definition and abstraction of these demand cues are grounded in systematic user insight and formative studies (see Supplementary Section 2). To ensure that the extracted semantic patterns are interpretable and stable across samples, rather than reflecting isolated individual expressions.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, CoX-3D establishes a closed-loop framework that connects social semantics to 3D asset generation. Multimodal social media data are first integrated into a unified semantic space and then transformed into structured design semantic representations through a large language model (LLM) guided topic abstraction and cross-modal alignment. These semantics are subsequently mapped to the 3D generation process, where semantic attribution and activation analyses quantify the relative contributions of different semantic factors to decisions regarding form, style and material.

As a result, CoX-3D not only generates 3D assets but also renders the influence of design semantics on 3D outcomes traceable, analyzable, and open to intervention during the generation process. By transforming social media semantics from uncontrollable external prompts into analyzable, verifiable, and adjustable design drivers, CoX-3D offers a general paradigm for human–AI collaborative design. This study extends the applicability of generative 3D design to real-world scenarios driven by social semantics, providing a new computational perspective on how user preferences shape design outcomes.

Results

Social media semantics can be abstracted into stable and scalable demand cues

Addressing RQ1, we first examine whether social media semantics exhibit stable and scalable structural properties under real-world large-scale data conditions, such that they can be systematically abstracted into interpretable demand cues rather than forming a loose aggregation of noise and sporadic expressions. To this end, we constructed a semantic dataset comprising over 60,500 real-world multimodal social media samples and conducted a systematic comparison of 23 representative text representation and topic modelling approaches. Their clustering performance was evaluated at three data scales of 10k, 30k and 50k samples, respectively (see Supplementary Table S10).

Clustering quality was characterised using four complementary metrics. The Silhouette score23 (SS) reflects within-cluster semantic coherence. The Calinski–Harabasz index24 (CHI) measures inter-cluster separation. The Davies–Bouldin index25 (DBI) captures cluster compactness. The Dunn index26 (DI) provides an overall assessment of structural quality. It is important to note that this analysis does not aim to identify a single model that optimises any individual metric. Instead, it is designed to reveal the overall evolutionary trends and stability characteristics of social media semantic structures as the data scale increases.

As shown in Fig. 2a, increasing the data scale from 10k to 50k leads to progressively clearer and more stable clustering structures. This observation suggests that large-scale social media semantics are not merely an accumulation of noise, but rather reveal latent structures that can be modelled. Among the evaluated methods, ELLM, which incorporates LLMs for semantic filtering and abstraction using GPT-4o in combination with BERTopic, consistently achieves high and stable structural quality across all three data scales. Its performance improves monotonically with increasing data volume, indicating stronger robustness to scale expansion.

Further sensitivity analysis across clustering granularities ranging from K = 5–25 shows that ELLM exhibits relatively small fluctuations in structural metrics under different topic resolutions (Fig. 2b). In contrast, several baseline methods display pronounced instability as the number of clusters increases, suggesting higher sensitivity of their semantic structures to granularity changes27. These results indicate that the semantic structures modelled by ELLM remain stable not only during scale expansion but also under changes in abstraction granularity (see Supplementary Section 6.1). Visualisation of the semantic network structure (Fig. 2c) further shows that excessively fine clustering leads to semantic fragmentation, whereas overly coarse clustering results in excessive semantic merging and abstraction. Intermediate granularity achieves a more appropriate balance between semantic discrimination and global coherence28,29. This observation further suggests that, under appropriate abstraction conditions, social media semantics can form latent representations that are both structurally coherent and semantically consistent30,31.

These results demonstrate from the perspectives of scale expansion and parameter robustness that social media semantics under real-world large-scale conditions do not constitute random noise. Instead, they exhibit stable, scalable and interpretable latent structures32,33. By incorporating LLMs for semantic filtering and abstraction, ELLM systematically transforms highly unstructured social media expressions into interpretable demand cues. These cues provide a reliable semantic foundation for subsequent design semantic mapping, controllable 3D generation, and human–AI collaborative design. In addition, we also validated the efficacy of ELLM through ablation experiments (see Supplementary Section 6.2).

Demand cues map onto interpretable design attribute spaces

After establishing that social media semantics exhibit a stable structural organisation under large-scale data conditions, we further address a critical question: whether the abstracted demand cues can be translated, at the design level, into interpretable and quantifiable attributes with consistent semantic meaning, rather than remaining as opaque high-dimensional embeddings. To this end, we construct an attribute space consisting of twelve design semantic dimensions derived from high-quality social media semantic samples.

Specifically, these dimensions include form and shape, style attributes, material and texture, colour semantics, structure and craftsmanship, functional requirements, ergonomics and UX, emotional and value needs, quality and reliability, sustainability, semantic coherence and mesh complexity (Fig. 3a). Importantly, these dimensions are not freely generated by the model. Instead, they form an interpretable and theory-informed attribute system grounded in formative studies and systematic design semantic analysis. As such, this attribute space serves as a semantic mediation layer that bridges large-scale social media semantics and downstream design decision-making, enabling transparent reasoning and controllable generative outcomes.

Global and local feature attribution analyses based on the Shapley (SHAP)34 additive explanations framework reveal substantial differences in the contributions of distinct design semantic dimensions to generative quality prediction (Fig. 3b). At the local feature level, form and shape, style attributes, and material and texture exhibit higher mean contribution values across the full sample set, accompanied by broader distribution ranges. This pattern indicates that these dimensions play a more stable and dominant role in users’ perceptual evaluation and subjective assessment of generative quality.

In contrast, semantic coherence, sustainability and mesh complexity demonstrate relatively lower average contributions at the global level. Nevertheless, under specific design instances and contextual conditions, these dimensions still show clear and directionally consistent effects, highlighting their regulatory importance in localised design decision-making and semantic control processes. Further global attribution analysis indicates that form and shape, material and texture, and high-level semantic factors constitute the primary driving forces influencing overall 3D generative quality evaluation.

Further SHAP dependence analysis reveals response patterns between variations in design semantic feature values and predicted generation quality (Fig. 3c). The results show that different design semantic dimensions exhibit distinct response profiles. Some dimensions display approximately monotonic relationships, whereas others demonstrate pronounced non-linear or threshold effects. These response patterns recur across diverse samples, indicating stable statistical structures rather than incidental effects driven by individual cases (see Supplementary Section 8).

Overall, these results demonstrate that demand cues abstracted from social media are neither arbitrary labels nor black-box embeddings. Instead, they can be systematically mapped to a set of design attribute dimensions with explicit semantic meaning. These dimensions exhibit stable yet differentiated influence patterns in generation quality prediction, thereby providing an interpretable design semantic foundation for subsequent semantic-to-geometric mapping, controllable generation and human–AI collaborative design.

Demand-aware semantic–geometric alignment improves perceived 3D design quality

This section addresses RQ2. Rather than focusing on whether a 3D model can be generated, we examine whether explicitly aligning the generation process with user demand semantics leads to results that more closely resemble real design outcomes in terms of design consistency, geometric stability and overall perceptual quality.

To evaluate whether these semantic advantages translate into practical improvements at the level of 3D generation, we conducted systematic comparisons between CoX-3D and several representative 3D generation methods across two canonical tasks, text-to-3D and image-to-3D (Supplementary Table S13). Quantitative results show that CoX-3D achieves higher semantic alignment, measured using CLIP-based metrics, in both tasks, while exhibiting substantially lower deviations across multiple geometric distribution measures such as kernel distance (KD) and Fréchet distance (FD). These findings indicate that explicitly incorporating demand semantics into the geometric generation process constitutes a primary factor driving performance improvements, rather than reliance on more complex generative architectures or more aggressive post-processing strategies. This advantage is particularly pronounced in the image-to-3D setting. Whereas baseline methods often exhibit trade-offs between semantic adherence and geometric stability, CoX-3D maintains semantic consistency while substantially suppressing geometric drift, resulting in generation behaviour that better aligns with design expectations.

These quantitative trends are corroborated by qualitative results (Figs. 4 and 5). Under generation tasks involving complex geometries, strong stylistic constraints, or high levels of detail, 3D assets produced by CoX-3D exhibit greater stability in overall design consistency. Specifically, CoX-3D outperforms baseline methods in global structural coherence, including stable proportions and intact primary forms, local geometric consistency without collapse or abnormal deformation of key components, and texture continuity with material distributions that remain aligned with stylistic semantics.

Notably, the advantages of CoX-3D extend beyond single-pass generation outcomes. In iterative generation and structural editing tasks driven by social media semantics (Fig. 4d, e), CoX-3D supports component-level insertion, removal and replacement of 3D assets, while consistently maintaining semantic and geometric coherence across multiple iterations. By contrast, most baseline methods exhibit cumulative geometric degradation after repeated editing operations. Supplementary experiments (see Supplementary Section 7.2) further show that semantic guidance effectively constrains the direction of geometric optimisation during local structural editing and stylistic adjustment, allowing generated results to remain coherent at the design level.

User subjective evaluation results further support these findings (Fig. 5c). Across multiple dimensions, including semantic consistency, geometric integrity, detail quality and overall preference, CoX-3D receives higher and more evenly distributed ratings, with this advantage remaining consistent across text-to-3D, image-to-3D and social-media-to-3D settings. This comparability is not reflected in increased visual complexity, but rather in the overall coherence between semantic intent and geometric realisation.

Overall, these results demonstrate that explicitly incorporating stable demand semantics into the 3D generation process, and constraining it through explainable semantic–geometric alignment mechanisms, systematically enhances the perceptual quality and structural consistency of 3D design outcomes.

Explainable feedback enhances human–AI collaboration and perceived design quality

As supplementary evidence for RQ2, beyond the quantitative and qualitative results reported above, we conducted a user study with 30 participants to systematically examine how explainable feedback influences the generation process stability, subjective understanding efficiency, and interaction behaviour in realistic co-design scenarios. Rather than evaluating outcomes solely based on final visual quality, the experiment incorporated findings from formative studies and focused on process-level changes in human–AI collaboration. To support this research, we developed a dedicated front-end evaluation and interaction system (See Fig. S1 in Supplementary Section 2).

From an optimisation perspective, CoX-3D exhibits faster and more stable convergence behaviour in multimodal generation tasks (Fig. 6a). Compared with baseline methods, fluctuations in training loss are substantially reduced in the middle and later stages, without evident performance degradation. These observations suggest that semantic constraints lead to more consistent geometric optimisation, providing a stable procedural foundation for interactive generation and iterative design refinement rather than an incidental advantage of single-pass outputs.

We further conducted systematic comparisons across text-to-3D, image-to-3D, and social-media-to-3D generation scenarios. The results show that incorporating ELLM-based social media semantic modelling improves both subjective and objective evaluations across methods in the social-media-to-3D setting, with CoX-3D maintaining more favourable and stable distributions across multiple metrics (see Supplementary Section 9). This indicates that abstracted and filtered social media semantics do not act as noise, but instead function as an effective mediation layer linking the generation process with user-perceived outcomes. Ablation analysis further reveals the relative contributions of different information sources during the collaborative process (Fig. 6b). Among the top-performing methods, removing social media semantic input leads to a substantially larger decrease in overall collaboration scores than removing textual or visual inputs, indicating that social media semantics play a key role in aligning generated results with user understanding, whereas reliance on a single modality is more likely to induce judgement instability.

At the user level, the subjective evaluation results obtained from a custom multi-view comparison protocol35 and an image-quality ranking36 task indicate that explainable feedback significantly improved participants’ ability to understand differences between generated results, identify quality distinctions, and complete design decisions (Fig. 6c). Specifically, the multi-view comparison interface and the ranking-based evaluation procedure reduced cognitive load by providing clearer spatial organisation and more distinguishable visual encodings, thereby improving overall usability. When combined with semantic activation visualisations and localised attention cues, participants were able to map visual variations in generated results to corresponding semantic factors more directly. This shifted evaluation and adjustment from trial-and-error exploration toward semantically informed decision-making, thereby reducing random exploratory behaviour.

Interaction behaviour analysis shows that explainable feedback mechanisms significantly alter operational patterns in human–AI collaboration (Fig. 6d). Explainable feedback guides users to make targeted, localised adjustments aimed at achieving specific outcomes. In contrast, unguided exploration results in broad and scattered interactions. Compared with conditions without explanation, users are more inclined to perform localised adjustments guided by explicit semantic–geometric correspondences, rather than resorting to wholesale replacement or repeated regeneration. Co-occurrence analysis between user demands and design deficiencies further reveals stable structural patterns (Fig. 6e). For example, excessive mesh complexity frequently co-occurs with reduced editability and increased cost perception, while user demands are often expressed as multi-attribute combinations involving form, material, and style, reflecting the compound nature of decision-making in real design contexts.

Taken together, explainable feedback not only enhances stability and controllability in the generation process but also reshapes how users understand generated outcomes and form trust. By explicitly integrating semantic information into the interaction loop, CoX-3D strengthens the effectiveness of human–AI collaborative design at the behavioural level, providing empirical support for the application of explainable generative systems in real-world design practice.

Component-wise ablation reveals complementary functional roles

This section addresses RQ3 through ablation experiments designed to elucidate the functional roles of key modules in CoX-3D and their coordination during the semantic-to-geometric generation process. Under a unified experimental setup, we systematically ablated the core components of CoX-3D and evaluated their effects in both text-to-3D and image-to-3D tasks (Supplementary Table S12).

The results show that all ablated variants exhibit consistent performance degradation relative to the complete model, with highly similar degradation patterns observed across both tasks. This observation indicates that the performance advantages of CoX-3D do not arise from any single module, but instead emerge from coordinated interactions among multiple components, each fulfilling a stable and distinguishable functional role during generation.

ELLM

Removing the high-level semantic modelling module leads to a pronounced reduction in semantic alignment, as measured by CLIP, accompanied by clear deterioration across multiple geometric distribution metrics. This result indicates that abstracted and filtered social media semantics are not optional inputs, but instead constitute a fundamental prerequisite for cross-modal constraint and stable generation. In the absence of this module, generated outputs are more prone to semantic drift, and geometric structures respond less consistently to semantic variations, preventing the formation of predictable geometric adjustment trajectories.

CLIP and SDS

Among all ablation settings, removing either CLIP guidance or score distillation sampling (SDS) optimisation results in the most pronounced overall performance degradation, characterised by simultaneous declines in semantic alignment and geometric stability. This finding suggests that these two components jointly support the core functions of semantic–geometric alignment and stable geometric convergence during generation. When either component is absent, the model struggles to maintain a balance between semantic fidelity and geometric plausibility, and semantic variations fail to translate into directionally consistent geometric responses.

Mesh-VAE-PBR

Removing the Mesh-VAE-PBR module or its KL regularisation primarily affects generation distribution and geometric stability, while exerting a comparatively limited direct impact on semantic alignment. This indicates that these modules are not the primary drivers of semantic expression, but instead act by constraining latent space structure and geometric distributions, thereby suppressing anomalous forms and structural degradation. In doing so, they provide stable geometric support for semantically guided generation, allowing semantic modulation to be reflected in geometry in a controlled manner.

Grad-CAM and SHAP

Notably, removing explainability modules has a limited effect on single-pass quantitative metrics, but leads to systematic reductions in distributional stability during multi-iteration generation and structural editing. This observation indicates that the primary role of explainability modules is not to improve one-off generation performance, but to support sustained alignment between semantic feedback and human–AI collaboration. This support enables semantic adjustments to remain directionally consistent across interactions, preventing the accumulation of geometric degradation.

These results demonstrate that CoX-3D achieves stable and predictable mappings from semantic variation to geometric response through coordinated module interaction rather than simple module stacking, thereby establishing a methodological foundation for controllable human–AI collaborative design.

Discussion

This study introduces a 3D generation framework grounded in social media semantics and, more importantly, clarifies how social semantics can be systematically modelled, constrained, and transformed into explainable design decision mechanisms within generative design. Across methodological design, generative mechanisms and human–AI collaboration, CoX-3D exhibits fundamental differences from existing end-to-end generation approaches, highlighting the central role of semantic structure in stable generation and collaborative design.

Methodologically, the core contribution of the CoX-3D framework transcends the mere integration of existing neural models; rather, it introduces an architectural paradigm shift in generative design. By establishing an explainable mediation layer, CoX-3D successfully bridges the longstanding gap between macro-level social computing and micro-level physical rendering. Instead of relying on blind gradient descent driven by opaque prompt embeddings, our framework orchestrates SDS and cross-modal alignment as a semantically-constrained directional optimisation process. This architecture effectively transforms generative 3D modelling from an unpredictable, trial-and-error black box into a transparent, verifiable and mechanistic human–AI collaborative closed loop.

At the semantic modelling level, the results show that social media semantics do not constitute a fragmented or purely noisy information source but can instead be stably organised into a set of design attribute dimensions under large-scale modelling conditions37. Furthermore, our controlled ablation study (see Supplementary Fig. S6) reveals a fundamental distinction: while generic descriptive prompts—simulating standard vision-language datasets—merely capture objective physical facts, our socially informed demand cues successfully encapsulate subjective design intent and affective preferences. This confirms that the nuanced affective and stylistic dimensions uniquely inherent to social media data act as essential constraints for achieving highly plausible 3D geometries and context-aware PBR texturing. Consequently, this socially driven mediation yields significantly higher CLIP similarity scores, transcending the generative capabilities of conventional text-to-3D pipelines.

To ensure the validity of these semantic drivers, the 12 design dimensions were not predefined but algorithmically derived from 60,500 real-world samples via a bottom-up pipeline (see the Coding dictionary in Table S2 and the end-to-end derivation pipeline in Supplementary Fig. S3). Generative validation confirms that these emergent dimensions possess orthogonal controllability over 3D geometry and texturing, thereby bridging the gap between abstract social media affect and concrete physically-based rendering parameters38.

At the level of generative mechanisms, the results further indicate that linear combinations of attributes do not determine design quality but are jointly constrained by nonlinear effective ranges associated with multiple attribute categories39. Attributes related to appearance and style often exhibit threshold-like responses, whereas geometric coherence and engineering plausibility follow a balance-range mechanism. Structural validity acts as a baseline constraint, whose absence consistently degrades overall quality, while its perceptual gains become limited once a minimum requirement is met. This mechanism explains the quality instability and semantic drift frequently observed in generative design. By explicitly modelling semantic–geometric constraints, CoX-3D enables semantic adjustments to induce directionally consistent and predictable geometric and visual responses, thereby improving the stability of the generation process.

From an application and human–AI collaboration perspective, our framework redefines the functional boundaries of explainable AI in generative design40. While tools like Grad-CAM and SHAP are traditionally confined to post-hoc analysis, CoX-3D integrates them into a closed human–AI collaborative loop. In this paradigm, a post-hoc diagnostic at iteration t directly informs the semantic intervention for iteration t + 1, effectively transforming post-hoc explanations into system-level mechanistic controls (detailed in Supplementary Section 8). By explicitly visualising semantic activations and localised geometric attention regions, model feedback allows the design process to shift from passive result selection to understanding-driven collaborative reasoning41. This semantic mediation layer substantially reduces users’ cognitive burden, positioning the model closer to a collaborative design tool rather than a replacement for designers.

Regarding the trade-off between specialised semantic modules and general-purpose LLMs, this study adopts a dedicated social semantic modelling module (ELLM) rather than directly relying on general models, such as ChatGPT, primarily due to considerations of stability, controllability and engineering deployment. A dedicated semantic module can be more closely aligned with design task requirements and provide more stable and explainable semantic constraints for the generation process42.

Regarding limitations and future directions, several issues warrant further investigation. Social media corpora may inherently contain biases or noise, and their distributional characteristics may affect the generalisability of semantic modelling. In addition, the current 3D generation process leaves room for improvement in computational cost and optimisation efficiency, particularly for high-resolution meshes and complex materials43,44. Furthermore, integrating CoX-3D into practical engineering design workflows necessitates resolving compatibility issues with existing CAD toolchains. As a viable engineering application pathway, CoX-3D may be employed as a generative module during the front-end semantic and morphological exploration phase to rapidly produce initial three-dimensional form proposals. Subsequently, these generated outputs can undergo scale adjustment, structural decomposition and internal reconstruction within mainstream engineering design software, such as CATIA or 3ds Max, employing a hybrid semi-reverse and semi-forward modelling approach. This workflow holds promise for reducing the costs associated with early modelling and iterative revisions without altering the existing engineering software ecosystem, thereby enhancing design iteration efficiency and secondary editing flexibility45,46. It should be further noted that the datasets used in the present study are primarily collected through web scraping from online social media platforms and lack explicit informed consent mechanisms, which to some extent constrain a systematic evaluation of model fairness and potential biases47.

Methods

CoX-3D dataset construction

To support systematic modelling and cross-modal alignment of social media semantics in explainable 3D generation tasks, we constructed a large-scale multimodal social media dataset, referred to as the CoX-3D dataset. The dataset was collected from major Chinese social media platforms and includes textual, visual and temporal information. It is designed to capture the diversity, noise characteristics and temporal evolution of semantic expressions in UGC within real-world design contexts.

Each data sample consists of three components. (1) Textual content that reflects demand cues, aesthetic preferences, and evaluative descriptions related to a product or concept. (2) Associated image that represents visual examples discussed or referenced by users. (3) Publication timestamp used to model temporal dynamics and trends in semantic expression. Textual data were processed through tokenization, noise removal and semantic normalisation to reduce the influence of colloquial expressions and emotional noise. Visual data were resized to a unified resolution and processed using the segment anything model (SAM) to separate foreground subjects from background regions, thereby reducing background interference48. Temporal information was encoded as relative time features to support subsequent analyses of semantic stability and trend alignment.

After multi-stage data filtering and consistency validation, a total of 60,500 high-quality text–image pairs were retained. Based on topic stability analysis, these samples were organised into twelve semantic topic clusters that remain stable across different dataset scales. These clusters were further mapped to explainable design feature dimensions derived from SHAP analysis, representing key design attributes, such as form, style, material properties and semantic consistency. This dataset serves as a unified data foundation for semantic modelling, cross-modal alignment, and 3D generation experiments within the CoX-3D framework, enabling the evaluation of model performance and generalisation under social media-driven design scenarios (see Supplementary Section 3).

CoX-3D framework overview

CoX-3D consists of two coordinated core modules, namely an explainable 3D large language model (ELLM) and a Mesh-VAE-PBR-based 3D generation module (Fig. 7). The framework aims to transform unstructured and emotionally expressed social media semantics into explainable and controllable intermediate design semantic representations, which are then used to guide constrained 3D asset generation and optimisation.

Architecture of the CoX-3D method.

a Semantic-driven overall generation mechanism. The system takes social media user-generated content (UGC) as input and employs a large language model-guided semantic modelling module, referred to as the ELLM, to abstract and structure high-noise social semantics into an explainable design semantic prior. This semantic mediation layer is explicitly introduced at the initialisation stage of generation and acts as a stable constraint during subsequent optimisation of 3D geometry and material properties, rather than controlling generation through implicit prompting. b 3D generation under semantic constraints and cross-scenario adaptation. Under the cross-modal semantic alignment and constraints provided by ELLM, the mesh-VAE-PBR module jointly generates and optimises 3D geometry and PBR material representations. This process does not rely on predefined object categories or structural templates and can be adapted to multiple generation paradigms and design tasks, demonstrating the generality and scalability of the framework. c Semantic modelling and alignment mechanism of ELLM. Social media semantics are first processed through BERTopic for topic modelling and noise suppression, and are then refined through large language model-based semantic filtering and prompt alignment. This process maps unstructured social expressions into a structured design semantic space, providing stable and analyzable semantic constraints for 3D generation. d Mesh-VAE-PBR and the explainable optimisation loop. Under the constraints imposed by SDS and CLIP-based semantic alignment, the model performs semantically guided joint generation of 3D geometry and material properties, with geometry and texture optimised through latent variables in a differentiable framework. Grad-CAM reveals semantic attention patterns over local geometric regions, while SHAP quantifies the global contribution of individual semantic factors to the overall generation outcome. Together, these mechanisms form a dual-level explainable feedback loop linking semantics and geometry, enabling iterative human–AI-in-the-loop optimisation.

Hyperparameter settings

All experiments were conducted on three NVIDIA A5000 GPUs, each with 24 GB of memory. Hyperparameter configurations of CoX-3D cover both the BERTopic-LLM-based social media semantic modelling module and the Mesh-VAE-PBR 3D asset generation module. All hyperparameters were tuned on the validation set and then fixed across both the main experiments and ablation studies.

During the semantic modelling phase, text is mapped to 768-dimensional semantic embeddings via a frozen pre-trained sentence vector encoder. Topic discovery employs the BERTopic framework, where UMAP’s neighbourhood size is set to 15 and minimum distance to 0.1. Clustering is performed using HDBSCAN with a minimum cluster size of 30 and a minimum sample size of 10. Topic keywords were extracted using a TF–IDF-based topic representation strategy, following classical term-weighting principles and recent BERTopic-based topic modelling practice49,59,60. For each topic, the top 10 keywords with the highest topic-level TF–IDF weights were retained. The LLM serves solely as a semantic controller during inference, with relevant topic filtering constrained by KL divergence, weighted at λKL=0.5.

During the 3D generation phase, the Mesh-VAE-PBR module is initialised from a pre-trained model. Both geometric and material latent dimensions are set to 128, with the latent space regularisation weight set to β=1×104. 3D optimisation is guided by SDS, with diffusion sampling set to 50 steps and a NeRF learning rate of 1 × 10−³. This is combined with a CLIP semantic consistency constraint (weighted at 1.0). Geometric regularisation comprises Laplacian smoothing and normal continuity loss, weighted at 0.1 and 0.05, respectively. Beyond these modules, all other network components remain frozen throughout experimentation to prevent interference from non-critical parameter updates (see Supplementary Section 5.3).

Data split

To prevent semantic information leakage and mitigate temporal bias, data splitting followed both topic independence and temporal consistency principles. The complete dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 7:2:1. Samples within the same semantic theme cluster are not assigned to different subsets, thereby preventing information leakage at the thematic level. Furthermore, temporal constraints are imposed on the data partitioning, ensuring that test set samples collectively occur later than those in the training and validation sets. This guarantees that the model encounters a temporally unseen semantic distribution of social media content during testing, thereby providing a more authentic assessment of its generalisation capabilities regarding emerging design semantics and societal trends. All subsets fully preserve multimodal information, including text, images and timestamps for end-to-end semantic modelling, cross-modal alignment and 3D generation experiments.

Evaluation metrics

To systematically evaluate CoX-3D’s performance across its two core modules, social media semantic modelling (ELLM) and 3D asset generation (mesh-VAE-PBR), we employed quantitative and qualitative assessment metrics tailored to distinct task characteristics (see Supplementary Section 5). This metric system comprehensively characterises the model’s performance in authentic social media semantic-driven design scenarios, with evaluation dimensions encompassing structural quality, semantic consistency, and distribution stability, among other aspects.

Social media semantic modelling evaluation (ELLM): we treat topic discovery as an unsupervised clustering problem, employing the SS23, CHI24, DBI25 and DI26 to evaluate clustering performance across different methods. These metrics assess intra-class compactness, inter-class separation and overall structural quality.

3D asset generation stage (mesh-VAE-PBR): we evaluate performance across three dimensions: semantic consistency, generation quality and distribution stability61. Semantic consistency is measured via the CLIP score62; generation quality and distributional divergence are assessed using the FD63, computed based on Inception64, DINOv2 and geometric features65 respectively; distributional stability is further evaluated through KD66 to assess higher-order statistical consistency between the generated sample distribution and the reference distribution (see Supplementary Section 5.2B).

Adopted baselines

To systematically evaluate CoX-3D’s performance in social media semantic modelling and 3D asset generation tasks, we selected a diverse set of representative methods spanning different modelling paradigms and technical approaches as comparative baselines. All baseline methods employed publicly available implementations or official models and were run under uniform data partitioning, input conditions and evaluation metrics to ensure fairness and reproducibility of comparisons.

Semantic modelling and topic clustering baselines encompass: traditional statistical and linear models (TF-IDF50, LDA67, PLSA68 and Naive Bayes69), distributed word vector models (Word2Vec70, GloVe71, FastText72 and ELMo73), sequence modelling approaches (RNN74, LSTM75, Seq2Seq+Attention76), Transformer architecture models (BERT77, BERTopic, RoBERTa78), LLMs (GPT-279, Mistral80, Qwen-1.581 and Doubao82), and multimodal LLMs (GPT-4o, BLIP-283, LLaMA-3.184 and Gemini-1.585).

The 3D asset generation baseline encompasses multiple mainstream text/image-to-3D approaches, including DreamGaussian86, Magic3D87, LN3Diff88, Point-E89, Make-It-3D90, Shap-E91 and LGM92, covering diverse technical pathways such as diffusion-based, implicit representation, and direct 3D generation. All methods were evaluated under identical input conditions to ensure comparability of generated results at both semantic and geometric levels (see Supplementary Section 5.2A).
