Evaluating clinical psychology doctoral students’ competence in delivering CBT for chronic pain
Authors: Mary Beth McCullough, Gail N. Kemp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic pain affects approximately 20% of adults in the United States and is associated with significant psychological and functional impairment. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is an evidence-based intervention for chronic pain, but there is a shortage of trained providers. The Chronic Pain Management Program (CPMP) was developed to train clinical psychology doctoral students in delivering CBT for chronic pain. This study evaluates the effectiveness of the CPMP in improving trainees’ knowledge, clinical experience, and perceptions of their competency in providing CBT-based interventions for chronic pain.MethodsSixteen doctoral students completed the CBT for Chronic Pain Competence Scale (CCPCS) before and after participating in the CPMP. The CCPCS, adapted from an existing CBT competence measure, assessed self-reported knowledge, experience, and perceptions related to CBT for chronic pain. Internal consistency was examined using Cronbach’s alpha, and paired-samples t-tests were conducted to evaluate pre-to-post training changes.ResultsThe CCPCS demonstrated strong internal consistency across all subscales. Paired-samples t-tests revealed significant improvements in self-reported knowledge (Cohen’s d = -2.85) and experience (Cohen’s d = -3.05), suggesting the CPMP effectively enhanced trainees’ competence in delivering CBT for chronic pain. However, perceptions of CBT’s effectiveness did not significantly change, indicating that attitudes toward CBT may be more resistant to change or that the perception subscale requires refinement.ConclusionsThe CPMP successfully increased trainees’ views on their knowledge and experience in CBT for chronic pain, reinforcing the value of structured, interdisciplinary training programs in addressing shortages in psychological treatment of pain management. Future research should further examine the long-term impact of the CPMP on trainee knowledge and skill in delivering CBT for chronic pain and refine assessment tools to better capture changes in clinical attitudes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09757-9.

Background

Chronic pain, defined as pain persisting for three months or longer, affects approximately 20% of adults in the United States, impacting an estimated 51.6 million individuals [1]. Among them, 6.9% experience pain severe enough to significantly limit daily activities [1]. The prevalence of chronic pain has been rising in recent years. According to a study by the National Institutes of Health, the incidence of new cases of chronic pain surpasses that of several other common conditions, including diabetes, depression, and hypertension [2]. Chronic pain comes with a high price tag for individuals and society with costs ranging from $560 billion to $635 billion for health care, lost work productivity, and reduced wages, with treatment-related costs surpassing that of cancer and heart disease [3]. Chronic pain is associated with an increased risk of depression, anxiety, substance use disorders, and suicide, underscoring the need for effective treatment strategies [4, 5].

The gold-standard approach to chronic pain management is an integrated model that incorporates physical therapy, pharmacological interventions, and psychological treatment [6]. Psychological interventions play a critical role in chronic pain management, as psychological factors influence patients’ engagement in medical and rehabilitative care, as well as their overall quality of life [6]. However, access to psychological treatment remains a significant barrier for many individuals with chronic pain due to a shortage of trained providers. Across the United States, there is a deficit of behavioral health professionals with specialized training in chronic pain treatment, making it difficult to meet the growing demand for services [7]. This shortage is particularly pronounced in Georgia, the setting of this study, where the ratio of behavioral health providers to residents ranks among the lowest in the nation. According to the Mental Health America organization’s State of Mental Health in America survey, Georgia ranks 48th in the nation for behavioral health provider availability, with approximately 600 individuals for every one behavioral health professional [8]. Both rural and urban counties across the state reflect this critical shortage, resulting in substantial gaps in service provision and preventative care.

Cognitive behavioral therapy for chronic pain

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) has been widely recognized as an effective psychological intervention for chronic pain [9]. It has been shown to reduce pain intensity, improve functional outcomes, and mitigate maladaptive cognitions that contribute to pain persistence and associated distress. Additionally, CBT plays a crucial role in reducing reliance on opioid medications, which is particularly important given the ongoing opioid crisis [10]. The core components of CBT for chronic pain include: (1) psychoeducation on the connection between thoughts, emotions, behavior, and chronic pain; (2) recognizing and tracking thoughts, emotions, and behaviors to understand their impact on pain in specific situations; (3) understanding pain theories (e.g., Gate Control Theory); (4) utilizing relaxation strategies and physical coping techniques; (5) identifying unhelpful automatic thoughts related to pain; (6) replacing automatic thoughts with more adaptive, realistic coping thoughts; (7) applying stress management techniques for pain management; (8) implementing physical activity and pacing strategies; (9) developing assertiveness skills; (10) improving sleep hygiene and understanding its relationship to chronic pain; and (11) engaging in pleasant activity scheduling [11].

While CBT is often a core component of clinical psychology doctoral training, specialized instruction in its application for chronic pain is often limited. As a result, many psychology trainees may enter clinical practice without the specific skills needed to effectively address the unique challenges associated with chronic pain management. Expanding training opportunities for clinical psychology doctoral students in CBT for chronic pain could help alleviate the current provider shortage and improve patient health outcomes.

The Chronic Pain Management Program (CPMP)

To address this gap, the Chronic Pain Management Program (CPMP) was developed at a university in Georgia to train clinical psychology doctoral students in the delivery of CBT for chronic pain. The CPMP provides a structured program that trains students with specialized skills in chronic pain treatment. Each year, the program enrolls nine doctoral students, who gain hands-on experience delivering group-based, CBT interventions to individuals in the community experiencing chronic pain.

The CPMP curriculum takes an interdisciplinary approach, incorporating multiple training components to enhance students’ competence in delivering CBT for chronic pain. Key elements include: (1) workshops covering the neuroscience of pain, Socratic questioning in CBT, group therapy dynamics, and practical applications of CBT for chronic pain; (2) a seminar series featuring experts from physical therapy, pharmacy, and medicine who specialize in chronic pain treatment; (3) hands-on experience through shadowing a physical therapist in a clinical setting; and (4) weekly group supervision, where students review therapy session recordings, receive structured feedback, and refine their clinical skills. The goal of these training components is to enhance students’ ability to deliver cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) to individuals in the community experiencing chronic pain.

Study objectives

Given the need for specialized training in CBT for chronic pain, this study evaluated the effectiveness of the CPMP in enhancing trainees’ perceived competency, defined as their knowledge, clinical experience, and perceptions of their ability to deliver CBT-based interventions. The first aim was to develop and examine the psychometric properties of the CBT for Chronic Pain Competence Scale (CCPCS) to assess trainee competency in this area. Given the lack of an existing measure to assess CBT competency specific to chronic pain, the CCPCS was adapted from a validated general CBT competency scale [12]. The second aim was to assess the effectiveness of CPMP in enhancing trainee competency using the CCPCS. This was examined by comparing pre- and post-program differences in trainees’ self-reported knowledge, experience, and perceptions of competency in delivering CBT for chronic pain.

This study contributes to the field by assessing the impact of a training program designed to provide clinical psychology doctoral students with the skills necessary to deliver CBT for chronic pain. Additionally, the development of the CCPCS provides a standardized measure to evaluate training outcomes, offering a model for other programs seeking to enhance their pain management training efforts.

Methods

Participants

The study sample consisted of 16 clinical psychology doctoral students who successfully completed the CPMP, representing 88% of the 18 students invited to participate in the study. Two cohorts of students have participated in the CPMP over the course of two years, with each cohort consisting of 9 students. In the first cohort, 7 out of 9 students participated; in the second cohort, all 9 students participated. The majority were in their second year of doctoral training (n = 9), followed by third-year (n = 4) and fourth-year students (n = 3). To be eligible, participants were required to have completed the one-year CPMP, ensuring that all trainees had received the same structured CBT training for chronic pain. Data were collected between August 2023 and May 2025 and included two consecutive cohorts of the CPMP. Participants were recruited from a clinical psychology doctoral program at a university in Georgia.

Materials

To evaluate CPMP trainees’ perceived competency in delivering CBT for chronic pain, the CBT for Chronic Pain Competence Scale (CCPCS) was developed through adaptation of the CBT Competence Scale, a validated measure of general CBT delivery [12]. While the original structure was retained, including the three subscales (Knowledge, Experience, and Perception) and Likert-type scoring system, modifications were necessary to ensure its relevance to chronic pain treatment in a clinical context.

The original CBT Competence Scale was designed to assess the delivery of CBT in school-based settings with students. To align the measure with chronic pain management in clinical practice, items were modified to reflect CBT interventions for patients with chronic pain. For instance, the original item “Teaching students to notice and measure their thoughts, emotions, and behavior in the context of a given situation” was revised to “Teaching patients to notice and measure their thoughts, emotions, behavior, and how it impacts their pain in the context of a given situation.” In addition to modifying existing items, several new items were introduced to better assess CBT techniques specific to chronic pain management. These included items addressing psychoeducation on the connection between thoughts, emotions, behavior, and pain, training on pain theories such as Gate Control Theory, rationale for physical activity pacing, and the role of sleep hygiene in chronic pain management. Items were reviewed and revised by two licensed clinical psychologists with content expertise in CBT and assessment.

For the Perceptions subscale, the numbering of the original questions remained unchanged, but wording was modified to reflect the chronic pain context. For instance, the original scale included the item, “CBT, if used appropriately, will improve the average clinical outcomes of most students,” which was revised to “CBT, if used appropriately, will improve the average clinical outcomes of most patients with chronic pain.” These modifications ensured that the CCPCS appropriately reflected the clinical context in which trainees would be applying CBT principles.

Knowledge subscale

Participants rated their self-perceived expertise in 11 core CBT techniques using a 5-point Likert-type scale (0 = Very low; 4 = Very high). This domain assesses familiarity with key CBT strategies, including psychoeducation on pain mechanisms, cognitive restructuring, relaxation techniques, stress management, behavioral activation, and sleep hygiene. Higher scores indicate greater perceived expertise in applying CBT for chronic pain.

Experience subscale

Participants reported how frequently they implemented the same 11 CBT strategies in clinical practice using a 5-point Likert-type scale (0 = Never; 4 = Always). This domain measures the extent of real-world application of CBT techniques, with higher scores reflecting greater perceived comfort and experience in delivering CBT for chronic pain.

Perception subscale

Participants rated their agreement with six statements about the effectiveness of CBT for chronic pain on a 3-point Likert-type scale (1 = Disagree, 2 = Agree, 3 = Don’t Know). These items assessed beliefs about CBT’s efficacy, applicability across diverse populations, and feasibility in clinical practice. Following the CBT Competence Scale scoring approach, responses were recoded into a binary format: positive perceptions = 1, negative or uncertain responses = 0. Reverse coding was applied to Items 3 and 5 [12]. The final scale ranged from 0 to 4, with higher scores reflecting a more positive perception of CBT in chronic pain management.

Procedure

Participants completed the CCPCS at two time points to assess changes in perceived competency over the course of the program. Because the CCPCS was still under development during the first year of the CPMP, the initial CPMP cohort (n = 7) completed both pre- and post-assessments retrospectively at a single time point using a retrospective pretest method [13]. In contrast, the second cohort (n = 9) completed the CCPCS at two separate time points: before and after participating in the program. To examine potential differences related to assessment methodology, independent samples t-tests were conducted comparing cohorts on pre- and post-program scores across all subscales. No significant differences were observed (all p’s > 0.05), suggesting that cohort differences did not meaningfully influence the observed outcomes. Given the absence of significant differences between cohorts and the small sample size, cohorts were combined for primary analyses to preserve statistical power; however, findings should be interpreted with caution given differences in assessment methodology.

The pre-program assessment measured baseline knowledge, experience, and perceptions of CBT for chronic pain, while the post-program assessment evaluated changes following completion of the CPMP. Paired pre–post analyses were conducted to assess these changes. In addition, the psychometric properties of the CCPCS were examined, including internal consistency and inter-item correlations. Given the small sample size, analyses were limited to preliminary reliability and item-level statistics, and more advanced psychometric procedures (e.g., factor analysis) were not conducted. Together, these analyses were used to evaluate the effectiveness of the CPMP in improving trainee competency in CBT for chronic pain over time. Although the sample size is below recommended thresholds for full psychometric validation, it is consistent with prior pilot studies evaluating questionnaire reliability [14]. Accordingly, the present study should be considered a preliminary evaluation of the CCPCS, with future research needed to establish its psychometric properties in larger samples.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, as well as the ethical principles outlined in the American Psychological Association Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct. Ethical approval was obtained from the Mercer University Institutional Review Board (IRB Approval #H24-05037). Participation was voluntary, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Participants were informed that their responses would be kept confidential, reported only in aggregate form, and would have no impact on their academic standing or evaluation.

Results

Psychometric properties

The internal consistency of the CBT for Chronic Pain Competence Scale (CCPCS) was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and inter-item correlations. Reliability was examined for the overall CCPCS (28 items) as well as for each subscale (Knowledge, Experience, and Perception) at both the pre-program and post-program assessments. Across time points, the CCPCS demonstrated strong reliability.

Pre-program assessment

At the pre-program assessment, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.931 for the overall CCPCS, indicating excellent internal consistency. Each subscale also demonstrated high reliability, with Knowledge (α = 0.907, 11 items), Experience (α = 0.899, 11 items), and Perception (α = 0.717, 6 items).

Inter-item correlations for the Knowledge subscale ranged from 0.004 to 0.895 (M = 0.45), suggesting adequate consistency without excessive redundancy. The lowest correlations were associated with Knowledge Item 3, which assessed teaching patients about pain theories, such as Gate Control Theory. A similar pattern was observed for the Experience subscale, where inter-item correlations ranged from 0.12 to 0.79 (M = 0.46). The lowest correlations were found for Item 6 (teaching patients to replace automatic thoughts with coping thoughts) and Item 10 (teaching about sleep hygiene and its relationship to chronic pain).

For the Perception subscale, inter-item correlations ranged from 0.00 to 0.73 (M = 0.38). The weakest correlations were associated with Question 5, which stated that CBT is too complicated for most patients to understand and was reverse-coded. Additionally, low correlations were observed between Questions 4 and 6, which assessed the appropriateness of CBT for research versus real-world settings and its applicability across diverse populations.

Post-program assessment

At the post-program assessment, Cronbach’s alpha remained high for the overall CCPCS (α = 0.928), as well as for the Knowledge (α = 0.890), and Experience (α = 0.910) subscales. Although the Perception subscale was lower (α = 0.780), it demonstrated adequate internal consistency.

Inter-item correlations for the Knowledge subscale ranged from 0.024 to 0.870 (M = 0.44). The lowest correlations were linked to Knowledge Item 1, which assessed psychoeducation on the connection between thoughts, emotions, behavior, and chronic pain. The Experience subscale showed a similar pattern, with inter-item correlations ranging from 0.11 to 0.88 (M = 0.51). The lowest correlations were found for Item 2, which assessed teaching patients to track their thoughts, emotions, and behaviors in relation to pain, and Item 3, which measured teaching patients about pain theories.

For the Perception subscale, inter-item correlations ranged from 0.00 to 0.82 (M = 0.50). The weakest correlation remained between Questions 4 and 6, consistent with pre-program findings.

These results support the internal consistency of the CCPCS and its subscales while identifying individual items that may require further refinement. The inter-item correlations confirm that the scales measure related constructs without excessive redundancy. Evaluation of training effectiveness

A series of paired-samples t-tests were conducted to examine pre-to-post program differences in Knowledge, Experience, and Perception scores.

Results indicated a significant increase in Knowledge scores from pre-program (M = 1.39, SD = 0.74) to post-program (M = 3.41, SD = 0.42), t(15) = -11.38, p < .001, with a mean difference of -2.02 (95% CI [-2.39, -1.64]). The effect size (Cohen’s d = -2.85) indicates a large effect. Similarly, Experience scores increased significantly from pre-program (M = 1.24, SD = 0.63) to post-program (M = 3.43, SD = 0.47), t(15) = -12.18, p < .001, with a mean difference of -2.18 (95% CI [-2.56, -1.80]). The effect size (Cohen’s d = -3.05) also reflects a large effect.

In contrast, Perception scores showed no statistically significant change from pre-program (M = 3.12, SD = 1.02) to post-program (M = 3.20, SD = 1.03), t(15) = -0.44, p = .669, with a mean difference of -0.08 (95% CI [-0.49, 0.32]). The effect size (Cohen’s d = -0.11) suggests a negligible effect.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the psychometric properties and results of the CCPCS as a measure of the effectiveness of the Chronic Pain Management Program (CPMP) in enhancing clinical psychology doctoral students’ self-reported knowledge, experience, and perceptions of competency in delivering Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) for chronic pain. The internal consistency was strong for the CCPCS overall and for the Knowledge and Experience subscales. The internal consistency for the Perception subscale was weaker, but adequate.

Examining the results of the scale highlighted significant improvements in perceived knowledge and experience for clinical psychology doctoral trainees, suggesting that the program successfully enhanced trainees’ understanding and application of CBT techniques. However, no significant change was observed in perceptions of CBT’s effectiveness, raising questions about how attitudes toward CBT for chronic pain develop and whether they can be meaningfully shifted through short-term training. This finding may also reflect psychometric limitations, as the perception subscale demonstrated lower internal consistency relative to the other subscales. Although internal consistency for the pre- and post-program perception scales was within an acceptable range, the measure may still lack the precision needed to detect meaningful changes in attitudes.

The observed improvements in self-reported knowledge and experience align with prior research indicating that structured training programs enhance competence in psychological interventions [15–17]. Given the strong effect sizes in both domains (Cohen’s d = -2.85 for knowledge; d = -3.05 for experience), the CPMP appears to be an effective training model for developing competencies in chronic pain treatment. These findings are particularly relevant given the ongoing behavioral health provider shortage in the U.S., as there is an urgent need for more clinicians trained in evidence-based pain management strategies [7]. By incorporating didactic instruction, interdisciplinary collaboration, and supervised clinical practice, the CPMP offers a comprehensive learning experience that may help bridge existing gaps in doctoral training related to pain management.

Despite these strengths, the lack of significant change in perceptions of CBT’s effectiveness raises important considerations. One possible explanation is that students entered the program with already well-established beliefs about CBT’s role in pain management, resulting in limited variability and a potential ceiling effect that reduced the likelihood of detecting change. Additionally, many participants were enrolled in a clinical psychology doctoral program in which CBT is a foundational component of training, suggesting that prior exposure to CBT principles may have contributed to relatively stable attitudes over time. Another consideration is whether the perception subscale effectively captured changes in beliefs. Several items (Questions 4, 5, and 6) were identified as problematic, accounting for half of the scale and potentially limiting its sensitivity to detect change. More broadly, the subscale consisted of a small number of items and demonstrated comparatively lower internal consistency, which may have further constrained its ability to detect meaningful changes in attitudes. It is also possible that attitudes toward the effectiveness of CBT for chronic pain are less malleable than knowledge- and skill-based competencies, particularly over a relatively short training period. Future iterations of this measure should consider revising the Perception subscale, incorporating more comprehensive assessments of treatment attitudes, and exploring whether longer-term or more immersive clinical experiences are necessary to influence trainees’ beliefs about treatment effectiveness.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the small sample size (N = 16) limits the generalizability of the results. While the observed effect sizes were large, a larger sample would provide greater statistical power and allow for a more nuanced analysis of individual differences in learning outcomes. Second, competency was assessed using self-report measures, which may not accurately reflect actual clinical proficiency. The CCPCS captures trainees’ perceived knowledge, experience, and beliefs about CBT for chronic pain; however, self-assessment is subject to bias and may not align with objective performance [18]. As such, improvements observed in this study may reflect increased confidence or perceived competence rather than demonstrable changes in clinical skill. Notably, prior research with clinical psychology doctoral trainees suggests variability in the accuracy of self-reported competence relative to supervisor ratings, with less discrepancy typically observed among mid-level trainees [19]. Given that participants in the present study were primarily at an intermediate stage of training, self-reported competency may be somewhat more aligned with external evaluations, although this cannot be confirmed without objective measures. Future research should incorporate more objective and multimethod assessments of competency, such as supervisor ratings, structured observational evaluations, or behavioral coding of recorded sessions, to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of trainees’ clinical abilities and strengthen conclusions regarding training effectiveness.

Relatedly, the present study provides limited evidence of the CCPCS’s construct validity. Although the scale was adapted from an existing validated CBT competence measure, the current evaluation focused primarily on its use as an outcome measure to assess change over time. Psychometric analyses were therefore limited to internal consistency and inter-item correlations. In accordance with statistical conventions, more advanced procedures, such as exploratory factor analysis, were not conducted due to sample size limitations [20]. Additionally, the measure was not validated against external criteria (e.g., observational ratings or supervisor evaluations), limiting conclusions about its validity as an assessment of clinical skill. The Perception subscale, in particular, consisted of a brief six-item measure, which may not fully capture the complexity of trainees’ attitudes toward CBT effectiveness and may have limited sensitivity to detect change. Accordingly, the CCPCS should be considered a preliminary measure, and future research is needed to establish its factor structure and broader construct validity in larger samples.

An additional limitation relates to the use of mixed assessment approaches across cohorts. The first cohort completed a retrospective pretest design, whereas the second cohort completed a traditional pre-post assessment. Retrospective pretest designs may be subject to response-shift bias [13], potentially inflating perceived improvement. Although no significant differences were observed between cohorts on pre- or post-program scores, these assessment approaches are not equivalent and may differentially influence reported change. To preserve statistical power given the small sample size, cohorts were combined for primary analyses; however, future research should utilize consistent assessment methods across cohorts and examine potential cohort-specific effects in larger samples. Future research should consider more comprehensive assessment tools, including qualitative methods or longer scales that capture both explicit and implicit beliefs about CBT for chronic pain.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence that the CPMP effectively enhances self-reported knowledge and clinical experience in delivering CBT for chronic pain among doctoral trainees. These findings highlight the value of structured, interdisciplinary training programs in preparing clinicians to address growing workforce shortages in pain management. Notably, the lack of significant change in perceptions of CBT’s effectiveness suggests that attitudes toward treatment modalities may require different or extended interventions, such as prolonged clinical exposure or mentorship experiences.

Given the strong effect sizes for knowledge and experience gains, programs like the CPMP may serve as a model for integrating pain psychology training into doctoral curricula. Future research should explore longitudinal training effects, incorporate larger samples, utilize alternative measures of perception, and employ more objective competency assessments to further refine and enhance the effectiveness of CBT training for chronic pain management.
