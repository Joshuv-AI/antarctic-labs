Why Depth Matters in Parallelizable Sequence Models: A Lie Algebraic View
Gyuryang Heo, Timothy Ngotiaoco, Kazuki Irie, Samuel J. Gershman, Bernardo Sabatini

Abstract
Scalable sequence models, such as Transformer variants and structured state-space models, often trade expressivity power for sequence-level parallelism, which enables efficient training. Here we examine the bounds on error and how error scales when models operate outside of their expressivity regimes using a Lie-algebraic control perspective. Our theory formulates a correspondence between the depth of a sequence model and the tower of Lie algebra extensions. Echoing recent theoretical studies, we characterize the Lie-algebraic class of constant-depth sequence models and their corresponding expressivity bounds. Furthermore, we analytically derive an approximation error bound and show that error diminishes exponentially as the depth increases, consistent with the strong empirical performance of these models. We validate our theoretical predictions using experiments on symbolic word and continuous-valued state-tracking problems.

Most sequence problems, from symbolic processing tasks like natural language, mathematics, and coding, to the understanding of physical-world dynamics, are fundamentally
order-sensitive
.
Yet, highly scalable sequence models often achieve parallelism via

Notably,

This strong structural bias has inspired the study of the expressivity limits of such models
(

In particular, recent theoretical endeavors have identified various reasoning problems and state-tracking problems
(

;
46
;

that are provably unsolvable by constant-depth transformers or diagonal structured state-space models.

Despite these theoretical bounds, deep scalable sequence models remain the most empirically successful architectures
for real-world large-scale tasks, as shown in large language models.
This discrepancy motivates a quantitative question:

How badly does a model operate when applied to tasks it provably cannot solve exactly?

Quantifying this gap matters in practice, and therefore computationally more efficient models
would be preferable if the error remains to be tolerable.
Although prior work such as
49

69

the error-expressivity scaling laws have not, to our knowledge, been clearly derived.

2.1
Lie groups and Lie algebras

A
Lie group

G
is a smooth manifold equipped with a group structure under smooth multiplication.
Its tangent space at the identity characterizes an associated
Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}
.
Generally, a Lie algebra is a vector space
\mathfrak{g}
equipped with a bilinear and antisymmetric Lie bracket operation
[\cdot,\cdot]:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
that satisfies the Jacobi identity:

[[X,Y],Z]=[X,[Y,Z]]-[Y,[X,Z]],\quad X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{g}.

A canonical example of the Lie bracket is a matrix commutator.
For matrices

\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
the Lie bracket corresponds to the matrix commutator
[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}]=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}
.

Given a vector space spanned by a collection of vector fields
\mathcal{F}:=\{F_{i}\}
, the smallest Lie algebra is denoted as
\text{Lie}(\mathcal{F})

(
34
)
.
Unless otherwise mentioned, Lie algebras are finite-dimensional over
\mathbb{R}
.

Classes of Lie algebras

Here we classify Lie algebras based on their
degree
of order sensitivity.
The
derived
series of
\mathfrak{g}
is the inductive sequence of commutator ideals:

\mathfrak{g}^{(0)}=\mathfrak{g},\quad\mathfrak{g}^{(i)}=[\mathfrak{g}^{(i-1)},\mathfrak{g}^{(i-1)}].

If
\mathfrak{g}^{(j)}=0
with the smallest finite number
j
,
\mathfrak{g}
is classified as a
solvable
Lie algebra with derived length
j
.
Similarly, the
lower central
series of
\mathfrak{g}
is a sequence of ideals spanned by nested commutators:

\mathfrak{g}^{0}=\mathfrak{g},\quad\mathfrak{g}^{i}=[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}^{i-1}].

If
\mathfrak{g}^{c}=0
with the smallest finite number
c
,
\mathfrak{g}
is
nilpotent
with class
c
.
An
abelian
Lie algebra is the special case with
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]=0

(
30
)
.

These Lie algebras are related by a structural hierarchy.
Abelian Lie algebras are nilpotent, and all nilpotent Lie algebras are solvable.
A solvable Lie algebra can be constructed by a

tower
of abelian algebra extensions (
Remark

A.6
).
Extension is formally introduced in
Section

A.3
.

Most practical applications of artificial neural networks leverage deep layered structures with additional pointwise computation (e.g. MLP) between layers. To extend our theory, here we discuss dynamical systems with deep layered structure

(Deep SSM)

The

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{0}(x(t))h_{0}(t)+\mathbf{b}_{0}(x(t))

2.5
Word problems

Here we briefly introduce
word problems
, one of the standard test beds for sequence model expressivity.
We also use it in the experimental
Section

4

For a formal introduction, we refer to
Definition

C.2
.

Informally, a word problem is defined for a finitely generated group

5
5

5

More generally, word problems can be defined for a monoid or group given a finitely generating alphabet set.

(
43
;
46
)
.
We treat elements of the generating set as
alphabet symbols
,
and the product of a sequence of these symbols as a
word
.
Under the group composition rule, each word evaluates to a group element.
The word problem asks whether one can evaluate arbitrary words to the corresponding group elements.

From a computational perspective, the word problem can be viewed as a symbolic state-tracking task.
As symbols arrive sequentially, the model must update an internal state according to the group multiplication rule
and determine the resulting group element.

Let us provide a tangible example.
Consider each
alphabet symbol
as a
primitive action
, such as permutation, rotation, or a state transition operation (
Figure

1
).
A
word
is then a sequence of
action commands
evaluated by composing primitive actions in the given order.
A sequence of matrix multiplication, or a rollout in a Markov decision process (MDP) with action-conditioned state transition, provide examples of word evaluation.

The algebraic complexity of word problems is therefore rich and well-defined.
When the chosen finite group is abelian, the corresponding word problem is inherently order-symmetric.
In contrast, swapping alphabet orders in non-abelian groups can change evaluation,
introducing order-sensitivity.

Remark 3.9

(Conjecture on restricted SSMs)
.

As shown in
Theorem

3.2
, restricted SSMs do not substantially extend expressivity beyond abelian SSMs. Although restricted SSMs can be order-sensitive (

2.2
), their strong structural constraints prevent them from simulating general SSMs with a single layer.
Motivated by this observation, we conjecture that the advantage of restricted layers over abelian layers is limited. In particular, we conjecture the existence of solvable
\mathbf{S}_{\mathfrak{g}}
whose derived length is

4.1
Length Generalization on Solvable Word Problems

Here we evaluate performance on symbolic state-tracking problems, formalized as word problems (WPs) with chosen finite groups.
We selected word problems for each class and trained sequence models to confirm each models’ algebraic capacity using various depths.

Following prior work
(
46
;
60
)
, we formulate a word problem as a sequence labeling task.
Each sequence consists of tokens with each token representing a group element.
At each position in a sequence to be read from left to right, we have a target label/token corresponding to the group element obtained by composing symbols in the sequence so far.
The model is trained to predict the corresponding target label at each position.
Further experimental details can be found in Appendix
D.1
.

We first evaluate the length generalization performance by training models on sequences of length 128 and testing them on length up to 256.
Results are shown in Table
2
; and we break down the results as follows.

Abelian WPs.

We first evaluate models on abelian WPs as a smoke test.
Empirically, echoing prior work, we observe that transformer failed to model the simplest abelian word problems,
C_{2}
.
Similarly, positive diagonal only (denoted as +-only) GLA and Mamba did not perform well in
C_{2}
.
In contrast, Signed Mamba can solve
C_{2}
, while failing on
C_{3}
; and AUSSM succeeded at both
C_{2}
and
C_{3}
.

Nilpotent and Solvable WPs.

We further evaluate models on more challenging WPs.
Theoretically,
D_{8}
,
H_{3}
, and
S_{3}
requires at least two, while
S_{4}
requires three, layers of
abelian
SSMs.
Note that sequence models correspond to
restricted
SSMs, and may solve
S_{4}
in two layers. However, we conjectured that this would not be the case
Remark

3.9
.

Consistent with the prediction, none of the models except DeltaProduct successfully performed the task with a single layer.
With two layers, we observe that the inductive bias significantly influences model behavior.
Signed Mamba successfully learned
D_{8}
, a nilpotent task,
but only achieves partial generalization on the solvable task
S_{3}
.
In contrast, AUSSM fails to learn a generalizable solution for
non-abelian word problems under our experimental setting.

It remains an open question what hinders the performance of deeper models.
Still, several factors may offer some insights.
Our theory constructs on real arithmetic whereas empirical computation is on finite precision; the impact of finite precision is beyond the scope of the current work.
Moreover, without further constraints, how gradient-based optimization shapes the solution is also unclear.
This points to the
learnability
issue:
the practical learnability is influenced by the low-level inductive biases of
each model (i.e., the specific choice of state update rules), which are tangential to the expressivity theory.
For example, we empirically observe that, although Signed Mamba has a more restricted eigenvalue range than AUSSM,
empirically it performed better in the
2

5
Discussion

The expressivity of parallelizable sequence models is limited at constant depth;
yet, deep parallelizable models achieve strong empirical performance on complex tasks.
Our work provides a Lie-algebraic perspective on this discrepancy.

Our theory connects the depth of sequence models to an abstract, algebraic extension theory.
The connection provides an optimistic message: although the algebraic obstruction may preclude an
exact
simulation, deep architectures compensate to rapidly minimize the error. This conclusion is supported by our experiments. Across symbolic and physical tasks, we observe that increasing depth systematically reduces error on tasks outside each model’s theoretical expressivity range. Our observations suggest depth as a structural mechanism that mitigates complex order sensitivity.
This also motivates parallelizable models with adaptive depth
(
19
;
16
;
17
;
15
;
64
)

Together, our study clarifies expressivity bounds of sequence models and how the bound is mitigated by approximation, providing a practical guideline for model selection.

5.1
Future directions

Our results naturally suggest several future directions.
One immediate direction is examining the impact of positional encoding (PE).
Although additive PE is unlikely to unlock expressivity,
an advanced form of multiplicative PE does boost the expressivity of commuting sequence models
(
43
;
73
)
.
Understanding, or constructing, such mechanisms through algebraic structure
is a promising and practical avenue of research.

Orthogonally, our theory assumes real arithmetic and may be sensitive to the use of finite precision in real-world applications. On the one hand, exponentially vanishing error may fall below numerical resolution, potentially blurring the algebraic obstruction. On the other hand, finite precision may constrain learnability of the system under gradient-based optimization, which we have not studied. How finite precision interacts with our findings and impacts expressivity needs to be deeply considered.

Lastly, improvements in training algorithms may improve the learnability of sequence models, allowing more extensive testing of the bounds derived here.

6
Conclusion

Scalable sequence models achieve parallelism by imposing order symmetry, which limits their ability to
exactly
solve many sequence problems. We make this performance gap tangible and measurable by Lie theory and connect the depth of sequence models to algebraic extension theory. Our theory shows that, even when exact simulation is not possible, the order-sensitive approximation error vanishes exponentially with depth.
Experiments on symbolic word problems and 3D rotation state tracking task supports our theory, while also exposing practical challenges such as the instability during training of deep models. Together, we provide an algebraic perspective for understanding depth-dependent error-expressivity in parallelizable sequence models.

In this section, we introduce further mathematical preliminaries
as necessary to provide self-contained proofs of our theorems.
We omit rigorous proofs for these observations,
and interested readers are directed to related textbooks and research papers

.
We open the section with the Lie group and Lie group action to motivate our Lie-algebraic approach.

A.1
Lie groups and Lie algebras

A set
G
is a semigroup when
G
is closed under an associative binary operation
\cdot:G\times G\to G
, usually called a ”multiplication” operation.
If
\exists e\in G
such that
e\cdot g=g\cdot e=g
for
\forall g\in G
,
G
is a monoid.
Moreover,
G
is a group if for
\forall g\in G
there exists an element
g^{-1}\in G
such that
g^{-1}\cdot g=g\cdot g^{-1}=e
.

Lie group and Lie group action

A Lie group
G
is a smooth manifold equipped with a group structure under smooth multiplication.
For
x,y\in G
let us denote the left translation
L_{x}(y)=xy
.
From the smoothness of the multiplication,

L_{x}:G\to G
is a diffeomorphism of the underlying manifold
\mathcal{G}=G
.
In other words, translation can be thought of
G

acting
on
\mathcal{G}
.
The notion of Lie group
action
generally follows:

Definition A.1

(Lie group action)
.

Given a Lie group
G
and a smooth manifold
\mathcal{M}
,
a (left) Lie group action is a smooth map

\Gamma:G\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
that satisfies

\Gamma(e,h)=h
and
\Gamma(xy,h)=\Gamma(x,\Gamma(y,h))

for an identity
e\in G
, any two elements
x,y\in G
,
and a point on manifold
h\in\mathcal{M}
.

Following, we define the
orbit
of
h
denoted as
\text{Orb}_{G}(h):=\{\Gamma(g,h)\mid g\in G\}
.

Stabilizer
, or an isotropy subgroup, is a subgroup of
G
where
\text{Stab}_{G}(h):=\{g\in G\mid\Gamma(g,h)=h\}
.

While
Definition

A.1
straightforwardly describes how
\Gamma
operates on
\mathcal{M}
,
it is often advantageous to consider
\Gamma
as inducing a diffeomorphism on
\mathcal{M}
.
We can naturally introduce a Lie group homomorphism

\Pi:G\to\text{Diff}(\mathcal{M})
such that
\Pi(g)=\Gamma(g,\cdot)
,
where
\text{Diff}(\mathcal{M})
denotes collection of diffeomorphisms of
\mathcal{M}
.

Lie bracket and Lie algebra

Now one may consider a smooth vector field
V:G\to TG

and their collection
\mathfrak{X}(G)
.
Given the differential of the left translation at point
y
,

(dL_{x})_{y}:T_{y}G\to T_{L_{x}(y)}G=T_{xy}G,

the pushforward
(L_{x})_{*}V\in\mathfrak{X}(G)
is well-defined
(
66
;
42
)
.
Then,
V
is said to be left-invariant when
(L_{x})_{*}V=V
.
In other words, a left-invariant vector field
V

satisfies
\bigl((L_{x})_{*}V\bigr)_{xy}=(dL_{x})_{y}V_{y}=V_{xy}
for
\forall x,y\in G

and completely determined by its evaluation at identity, as
V_{x}=V_{xe}=(dL_{x})_{e}V_{e}
.
Thus, a collection of left-invariant vector fields,
\mathfrak{X}_{L}(G)
, has a one-to-one correspondence with
T_{e}G
.

As a tangent vector is defined by how it differentiates smooth functions,
a smooth vector field on
\mathcal{M}

is commonly identified as the derivation
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})\to C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
.
Following Leibniz rule,
the Lie bracket naturally arises to satisfy the derivation property
(
66
)
.
For
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
,

\displaystyle XY(fg)

\displaystyle=X((Yf)g+fYg)

\displaystyle=(XYf)g+(Yf)(Xg)+(Xf)(Yg)+f(XYg)

\displaystyle\neq(XYf)g+f(XYg).

Observe the extra term
(Yf)(Xg)+(Xf)(Yg)
obstructs the derivation property.
One can subtract
YX(fg)
to cancel out the extra term,
and naturally
(XY-YX)
is again a derivation of
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
.

Definition A.2

(Lie bracket of vector fields)
.

Given two smooth vector fields
X,Y:M\to TM
on an open subset
M
of a smooth manifold
\mathcal{M}
,
their Lie bracket is defined as following:

[X,Y]f=(XY-YX)f=X(Yf)-Y(Xf),\quad\forall f\in C^{\infty}(M).

(8)

\mathfrak{X}_{L}(G)
is closed under the Lie bracket.
Now we are ready to associate the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
to the Lie group
G
:

Definition A.3

(Lie algebra associated to Lie group)
.

For a Lie group
G
, we identify the vector space of Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}:=T_{e}G
.
For any
X\in\mathfrak{g}
, assign a unique
X^{L}\in\mathfrak{X}_{L}(G)
that satisfies
X^{L}(y)=(dL_{y})_{e}X
for
y\in G
.
We define the Lie bracket operation on
\mathfrak{g}
such that for
X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}
,

[X,Y]:=[X^{L},Y^{L}](e).

Then a map
\pi^{L}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{X}_{L}(G)
,
\pi^{L}(X)=X^{L}
is a Lie algebra isomorphism.

Naturally, Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
can also be identified as a collection of the left-invariant, first-order differential operators on
G
.
Note the Lie bracket operation depends on the identification: with
\mathfrak{g}\cong T_{e}G
,
[X,Y]\in T_{e}G
.
On the other hand, identifying
\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathfrak{X}_{L}(G)
produces
[X,Y]\in\mathfrak{X}_{L}G
.
The identification should be clear from the context.

We can analogously define the right translation on Lie group
R_{x}(y)=yx
.
The differential of the right translation at point y gives
(dR_{x})_{y}:T_{y}G\to T_{R_{x}(y)}G=T_{yx}G
,
and we denote the collection of right-invariant vector fields as
\mathfrak{X}_{R}(G)
.
Notationally, one can prove that
[X^{L},Y^{L}](e)=-[X^{R},Y^{R}](e)

(
42
)
.
In that,
\pi^{R}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{X}_{R}G
,
\pi^{R}(X)=X^{R}
swaps the Lie bracket sign.

Bridge to geometry

As a concrete example, consider the open set
M
in
Equation

8
with local coordinate charts
x=(x^{1},\cdots,x^{n})

around point
p\in M
.
Then we can rewrite

and similarly for

Given the local coordinate, Lie brackets get a tangible interpretation.
One can exhaust the vector field Lie brackets with coordinate,

\displaystyle[X,Y]=\sum_{c=1}^{n}[X,Y]^{c}\partial_{c},\quad[X,Y]^{c}=X(Y^{c})-Y(X^{c}),

where
[X,Y]^{c}

[X,Y]\in\mathfrak{X}(M)

Evaluated at

[X,Y]^{c}(p)=X_{p}(Y^{c})-Y_{p}(X^{c})

Then from the differential it is transparent:

[X,Y]^{c}(p)=X_{p}(Y^{c})-Y_{p}(X^{c})=(dY^{c})_{p}(X_{p})-(dX^{c})_{p}(Y_{p}).

One may interpret above as a directional derivative of a smooth coordinate function

A.3
Lie algebra extension

Analogous to the notion of the group extension, Lie algebra extension formalizes

Here we introduce the notion of Lie algebra extension

Definition A.5

(Lie algebra extension)
.

The Lie algebra
\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}
is an extension of base Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}
by (finite-dimensional) kernel Lie algebra
\mathfrak{a}
if there exists a short exact sequence of Lie algebras:

0\to\mathfrak{a}\xrightarrow{i}\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}\xrightarrow{\theta}\mathfrak{h}\to 0.

(14)

Redundantly,

is a Lie algebra monomorphism,
while
\theta:\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}\to\mathfrak{h}
is a Lie algebra epimorphism.
The short exact sequence satisfies
\text{im}\;i=\ker\theta
,
thus
i(\mathfrak{a})
is an ideal in
\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}
and
\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}/i(\mathfrak{a})\cong\mathfrak{h}
.
Following common convention, we identify
\mathfrak{a}
with
i(\mathfrak{a})
without confusion.

Remark A.6

(Tower of abelian extension)
.

A solvable Lie algebra with derived series
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}^{(0)}\supset\mathfrak{g}^{(1)}\supset\cdots\supset\mathfrak{g}^{(k)}=\{0\}

is an extension:

0\to\mathfrak{g}^{(n)}\to\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}^{(n)}\to 0.

When
n=k-1
,
\mathfrak{g}^{(k-1)}
is an abelian Lie (sub-)algebra and

\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}^{(k-1)}
is a solvable Lie algebra of derived length
k-1
.
In iteration, we recover a
k
-length
tower
of abelian Lie algebra extension.

Central extension and beyond

If all extension belongs to the split extension class, our discussion would be much easier.
However note that the linear section

Among non-split extension, central extension is another key class of the Lie algebra extension.

Definition A.8

(Central extension)
.

In
Equation

14
let
\mathfrak{a}
be abelian.

[(X,u),(Y,v)]=([X,Y],\omega(X,Y)).

When

Taken together, the Lie bracket on general Lie algebra extension follows:

[(X,u),(Y,v)]_{\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}}=[[X,Y]_{\mathfrak{h}},[u,v]_{\mathfrak{a}}+\rho(X)v-\rho(Y)u+\omega(X,Y)].

Fortunately, following studies have extended the universal embedding theorem

(

)

Appendix B
Control theory basics

B.1
Abstract control systems

Here we discuss abstract notions of controlled dynamical systems
(
34
)
.
On a real smooth finite-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{M}
, we consider a time-dependent tangent vector field:

F:[0,\tau]\times\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{X}\to T\mathcal{M},

which is locally Lipschitz in
h\in\mathcal{M}
and measurable in time
t\in[0,\tau]
.
In this context, we call
\mathcal{M}
the
state space
and
\mathcal{X}
the
input space
.
Then, with a fixed input path
x:[0,T]\to\mathcal{X}
we obtain the controlled ODE:

\dot{h}(t)=F_{x}(t,h(t)),\;F_{x}(t,h(t)):=F(t,h(t),x(t)),

(18)

assuming the path is locally bounded and integrable
(
34
)
.
We call a tuple
S=(\mathcal{M},F,\mathcal{X})
a
controlled dynamical system
.
Lastly, we say the system is initialized when
h(0)
is fixed.
Restricting
\mathcal{M}
to be on a Euclidean state space realizes SSM.

B.2
Simulation

Here we formally introduce the notion of simulation
(
63
;
31
;
41
)
.

Definition B.1

(Simulation)
.

Let two initialized
S_{0}
,
S_{1}
share an input space.
Consider a smooth map
\varphi:\mathcal{M}_{0}\to\mathcal{M}_{1}

such that
\varphi(h_{0}(t))=h_{1}(t)
for any admissible inputs.
If
\varphi
is a surjective submersion, we say
S_{0}

simulates

S_{1}
.
Moreover, if
\varphi
is (locally) diffeomorphic, we say
S_{0}
is equivalent to
S_{1}
.

Note that if
S_{0}
simulates
S_{1}
and
\dim S_{0}=\dim S_{0}
,
then
\varphi
is diffeomorphic.
Therefore, equivalence implies simulation.

For SSMs,
\varphi
is restricted to be full-rank linear map.
Following, diffeomorphic
\varphi
is invertible linear map.
Arbitrary smooth
\varphi
would typically leave the SSM model class
(
41
;
65
)
.
When restricted to SSMs, we define simulation error correspondingly.

Definition B.2

(SSM Simulation error on horizon)
.

Consider initialized
\mathbf{S}_{0}
,
\mathbf{S}_{1}
with
n_{0}=\dim\mathbf{S}_{0}\geq\dim\mathbf{S}_{1}=n_{1}
.
Let
\mathcal{X}_{ad}
be shared admissible input path space, with

U:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}_{ad}\mid x:[0,T]\to\mathcal{X},\|x\|\leq M\}

be measure bounded paths over chosen interval
[0,T]
.
Then, we define simulation error:

\Delta_{h}:=\inf_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{0}}}\sup_{x\in U}\bigl\|\mathbf{P}h_{0}(T)-h_{1}(T)\bigr\|,

(19)

where
\mathbf{P}
is a full-rank linear map.

Note that
\mathbf{P}
can be replaced with invertible linear map
\mathbf{T}

by state augmentation on
\mathbf{S}_{1}
, which leads to the standard state-space equivalence relation
in linear parameter-varying (LPV) system theory
(
65
)
.

B.3
Minimality

To understand whether one system simulates the other, we need a notion of minimality.
With a concept of Lie group action, minimality comes in a straightforward manner
(
36
;
41
)
.

Consider
5
-tuple
(\mathcal{M},F,\mathcal{X}_{ad},\mathcal{Y},g)

and its evolution operator
\Psi
.
Consider a collection
S=\{\Psi_{x}\}
for
x\in\mathcal{X}_{ad}
,
then one can notice

is a semigroup.
Although any
\Psi_{x}(t,0)
represent as an invertible matrix,
the existence of admissible input for
\Psi_{x^{\prime}}=\Psi_{x}^{-1}
is not guaranteed.
With completion, we denote a group
G
be the smallest subgroup of
\text{Diff}(\mathcal{M})
containing

.

For a chosen point
h\in\mathcal{M}
, consider a collection
S(h)=\{\Psi(h):\Psi\in S\}
and
G(h)=\{\Psi(h):\Psi\in G\}
.
It is akin to the orbit of Lie group action:
intuitively,
S(h)
is the set of accessible points on
\mathcal{M}
under admissible inputs.
We say the system is (weakly)
controllable
at point
h
when
S(h)=\mathcal{M}
.
When satisfied, the condition implies
G(h)=\mathcal{M}
.

Now consider the output map.
Given any two points
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
, denote
y_{1}(t)=g(\Psi(t,0)(h_{1}))
and
y_{2}(t)
correspondingly.
Then
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are said to be indistinguishable when
y_{1}(t)=y_{2}(t)
for any admissible inputs.
The system is
observable
when
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
being indistinguishable implies
h_{1}=h_{2}
.
Collectively, we say the system
(\mathcal{M},F,\mathcal{X}_{ad},\mathcal{Y},g)
is
minimal

when both (weakly) controllable and observable
(
41
)
.

Remark B.3
.

Often, the attention is restricted to open subset

however small.
Without global Lie group action and orbits, one can characterize the system with Lie algebras.
For example, weak controllability is replaced by

(
61
)
.

One may easily notice the notion of minimality in LTI system is a special case of the above.
We can similarly induce notion of minimality for SSMs,
commonly studied as a (quasi-)LPV system
(
65
)
.

B.4
Magnus series and path signature

The Magnus series provides Lie-algebraic expansion of the state-transition matrix.
As various evolution operators in physical applications are indeed a integral curve on a Lie group,
the Magnus expansion (or its truncation) serves as a useful tool in numerical approximation problems
(
34
)
.
Given a system
\mathbf{S}
, we explicitly write down a few first terms:

\displaystyle\Phi(t,0)

\displaystyle=\exp\Omega(t),\quad\Omega(t)=\sum\Omega_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{A}(t_{1})\;dt_{1}

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\;dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\;dt_{2}[\mathbf{A}(t_{1}),\mathbf{A}(t_{2})]

\displaystyle\Omega_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\int_{0}^{t}\;dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\;dt_{2}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\;dt_{3}\Bigl([\mathbf{A}(t_{1}),[\mathbf{A}(t_{2}),\mathbf{A}(t_{3})]]+[\mathbf{A}(t_{3}),[\mathbf{A}(t_{2}),\mathbf{A}(t_{1})]]\Bigr)

\displaystyle\cdots

Observe how the lower central series contributes to each order of the Magnus series.
Specifically, the
i
-th order Magnus term involves a linear combination of elements in the
(i-1)
-th member of the lower central series,
\mathfrak{g}^{i-1}
.
Consequently, if
\mathfrak{g}
is a class
c
nilpotent Lie algebra, then the Magnus expansion of
\mathbf{S}_{\mathfrak{g}}
truncates at order
c
. As a special case, abelian
\mathfrak{g}
is the class
1
nilpotent Lie algebra, all commutators of
\mathbf{S}_{\mathfrak{g}}
vanish and we have truncation at order
1
.

Recent progress in continuous-time neural network modeling leverages
rough path theory and path signature
(
49
;
68
;
69
)
.
Path signature is deeply related to the Magnus series
(
12
;
13
)
.
For linear CDE, the path signature can be expressed as
a tensor exponential of the Magnus expansion
(
23
)
.
In other words, the Magnus expansion is a representation of
log-signature

(
48
;
68
)

when input variation is bounded.
Log-signature underscores the algebraic property of input path,
while the Magnus expansion prioritizes state transition.
The study of the Magnus series is relatively mature and often provides both theoretical and computational benefits
when the path signature admits the Magnus expansion
(
23
)
.

Here we formally introduce the word problem for a finite monoid and related concepts.

Definition C.2

(Word problem
(
46
)
)
.

Consider a finite alphabet set
\mathcal{A}
.
We denote the free monoid on
\mathcal{A}
as
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
, and call its elements as
words
.
Also consider a finite monoid
M
generated by an image of alphabet map
\boldsymbol{\varphi}:\mathcal{A}\to M
.
Then, there exists a unique monoid homomorphism
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}:\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\to M
such that:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(a)=\boldsymbol{\varphi}(a),\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{t})=\boldsymbol{\varphi}(a_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}(a_{2})\cdot\ldots\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}(a_{t})\in M,

for alphabets
a,a_{1},\cdots,a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
.
The word problem for monoid is a decision problem such that for two arbitrary words
u,v\in\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
we decide whether
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(u)=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(v)
.

As noted in
46
, the word problem in sequence modeling requires one to learn the word evaluation,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
.
Learning
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
is equivalent to expressing the correct state transition operation when a new input arrives.
The decision of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(u)=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(v)
then immediately follows. Without confusion, we often identify
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(a)\in M
with an alphabet
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Definition C.3

(Lyndon words
(
2
)
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be totally ordered.
For any words, we introduce the
lexicographical
order on
\mathcal{A}^{*}

a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{i}<b_{1}b_{2}\ldots b_{j}\quad\text{if}\begin{cases}a_{1}=b_{1},a_{2}=b_{2},\ldots,a_{n}=b_{n},a_{n+1}<b_{n+1}\text{ for some }n\geq 0\\
\text{or}\\
a_{1}=b_{1},\ldots,a_{i}=b_{i}\text{ and }i<j.\end{cases}

(35)

Then, a word
w=a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{i}
is
Lyndon
if it is smaller than all of its cyclic permutations:

a_{1}\ldots a_{n-1}a_{n}\ldots a_{i}<a_{n}\ldots a_{i}a_{1}\ldots a_{n-1}\quad\forall n\in\{2,\ldots,i\}.

and we call
a_{1}\ldots a_{n-1}
and
a_{n}\ldots a_{i}
as
prefix
and
suffix
of the word, respectively.

Proof.

Following
Definition

C.2
,
the Chen-Fox-Lyndon theorem states that any words have a unique

factorization in Lyndon words
(
10
;
45
;
2
)
.
Let us denote a set of Lyndon words on
\mathcal{A}
as
L
, and its subset up to length
T
as

.
For any word
w
,

w=l_{1}l_{2}\ldots l_{k},\quad l_{1}\geq l_{2}\cdots\geq l_{k},\quad l_{i}\in L.

Notice that the canonical factorization of a Lyndon word is trivially itself.
Instead, Lyndon word has a
costandard
factorization. For any Lyndon word

,

l=l_{1}l_{2},\quad l_{1},l_{2}\in L,

where
l_{2}
is the longest proper Lyndon suffix of
l
.

Clearly, the canonical factorization provides the key insight in our proof.
Intuitively, when word length is bounded at
T
,
it suffices to ”memorize” Lyndon words and their composition up to length
T
to solve the problem.

It is known that
L
provides a basis of
free
Lie algebra on
\mathcal{A}

(
2
;
68
)
.
Let
\mathfrak{L}
be a free Lie algebra generated by a finite set
\{e_{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
,
where each element is labeled by an alphabet.
The bracket rule is given:

\text{b}[a]=e_{a}\in\mathfrak{L}\text{ for }a\in\mathcal{A},\quad\text{b}[l]=[\text{b}[l_{1}],\text{b}[l_{2}]],

(36)

with
l=l_{1}l_{2}
be the costandard factorization.
For a simple example, consider a Lyndon word

with costandard factorization

Appendix D
Experimental Details

Here we provide further experimental details.
Upon acceptance, we will release all our code used to produce our experimental results on a public GitHub repository.
We conducted all our experiments using a single GPU (A100 or H100) and no run lasted longer than three hours.

D.1
Word problems

Task formulation.

We encode each of the word problems used in Table
2
as a causal sequence labeling problem.
Each word problem has a finite set of group elements.
A task sequence is constructed by assigning a unique index to each group element in the corresponding word problem.
Given a sequence of indexed transformations, the task is to predict, at each position in the sequence, the class label corresponding to the group element obtained by composing the transformations in sequence.
Training and test data sets are constructed by randomly sampling such sequences.

In practice, the corresponding word problem sequence is prepended with an artificial

beginning
of sequence (BOS) token.
Conventionally, we assign the same BOS token as the target.
BOS token allows the model to
burn in
an initial state before processing the actual problem sequence.

In our preliminary studies, we also tried to explicitly learn the initial states of the sequence models (for GLA and Mamba variants) as part of the trainable parameters.
As we did not find this to be helpful, all our experiments are conducted without learning the initial states.
Modes are trained to make prediction at each position in the sequence, up to the maximum length of the data.

A sequence-level prediction is only counted as correct if the model successfully predicts all the elements in the sequence correctly.

Model configurations.

Models are separately trained on each word problem. Given that each word problem has a different number of group element, their base vocabulary size is different for each case (namely, 2 for parity/
C_{2}
, 3 for
C_{3}
, 8 for
D_{8}
and
H_{3}
, 6 for
S_{3}
, 24 for
S_{4}
, and 60 for
A_{5}
).
In addition to these base vocabulary, additional special tokens such as the padding token, including BOS, are allocated.
Such a choice is conventional for most modern sequence model implementations, which we follow;
none of them is required in our tasks except BOS.

The numbers reported in Table
2
are the best results from
training all hyper-parameters configuration listed in Table
3

for three random seeds each for 100 epochs.
We monitor length generalization performance, and stop early
if the model achieves 100% sequence-level prediction accuracy.
This corresponds to the standard evaluation protocol on word problems
(
46
;
60
)
, and serves as an operational definition of a model that is unsuccessful at performing a certain task.
For all tasks except the simplest
C_{2}
, we use 500k training sequences, and evaluation is conducted on 1000 test sequences.
As a technical side note, Deltaproduct achieved 100% generalization accuracy with only 100K training sequences for all the tasks; nevertheless, to potentially help other models, we increased it to 500K.

We use sequence length of 128 for training and 256 for testing.

Implementation.

We use
flash-linear-attention

(
76
)
to implement transformer, GLA, and DeltaProduct models.
We use public code from the respective papers for signed Mamba
(
26

and AUSSM
(

Instead of signed Mamba, one could also use complex or signed diagonals for GLAs in theory.
However, GLA’s efficient implementations of the chunk-wise training algorithm assume positive diagonals as also noted by
26

Therefore, we focus on signed Mamba only.

Note that the AUSSM implementation requires us to disable mixed precision for compatibility with the use of complex numbers.
Furthermore, the AUSSM backward kernel assumes sequence length to be a multiple of 8,
