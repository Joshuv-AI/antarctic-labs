Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction With Modified Remnant‐Preserving Technique: A Sleeve‐Like Membrane Suturing Method for the Tibial Remnant
Authors: Fengjun Ge, Shuang Cong, Weibin Yan, Ruifeng Fang, Cheng Li, Guangxin Huang, Jianying Pan, Denghui Xie, Chun Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction with modified remnant‐preserving technique refers to an approach adopted during surgery to preserve the tibial remnant of ACL. In arthroscopic ACL reconstruction, the ACL remnant and excess synovium are removed to obtain a broader surgical field of view typically. However, it is shown that preserving the tibial remnant and fixing it around the graft can protect the graft and promote vascularization and ligamentization. This technical note describes the modified remnant‐preserving technique for the ACL reconstruction, in which the suture was used to fix the sleeve‐like tibial remnant around the graft to promote vascularization and ligamentization of the graft. This technique is proven simple, effective, and reproducible and can be learned quickly by surgeons.

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is an important stable structure of the knee joint and ACL rupture is a common sports injury. It's the core structure that maintains the stability between the tibia and femur, and its main function is to prevent displacement, rotation, and hyperextension of the knee joint. ACL tears account for more than 50% of all knee joint injuries. In the United States, there are approximately 200,000 to 250,000 cases of ACL injuries each year, the majority of which are young people participating in competitive sports. 1 A variety of internal and external risk factors for ACL rupture have been identified, which include anatomical variations, neuromuscular defects, biomechanical abnormalities, movement patterns and hormonal states. 2

Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) is a commonly used surgical method for treating ACL tears, with the main goal of restoring the patient's mobility and delaying joint aging. This surgery replaces the damaged ACL by transplanting a new ligament, such as autologous tendons, allogeneic tendons, and artificial ligaments. 3 Since Mayo Robson reported the first case of ACLR in 1895, this surgery has made great progress. Although this surgery has excellent clinical effects, there are many problems in its implementation, which include poor tendon‐bone union, slow ligamentization process, and high incidence of rerupture. 4

To increase the success rate of ACLR, J.H. Ahn proposed the ACL remnant‐preserving reconstruction technique. 5 Traditional surgical methods completely remove the ACL remnant to obtain a broader surgical field of view and prevent postoperative Cyclops syndrome. 6 However, studies have shown that preserving the ACL remnant does not affect the postoperative knee joint function of patients. 7 In addition, research has shown that ACL disability preservation and reconstruction techniques can improve the proprioceptive function of the knee joint, provide good blood supply, prevent synovial fluid from seeping into the tunnel, and prevent tunnel dilation. 8 , 9 Preserving the ACL remnant in ACLR can accelerate the vascularization and ligamentization of the graft and the union of tendon‐bone in the early postoperative period.

Based on previous studies, this article describes a modified remnant‐preserving technique for ACLR. Current research has not yet indicated which method of ACL remnant‐preserving reconstruction can better promote graft ligamentization and the union of tendon‐bone. 10 The modified remnant‐preserving technique we describe retains the tibial stump of ACL and fixes it onto the graft. The advantage of this technique is that it retains and fixes the ACL stump onto the graft through a simple and repeatable operation. The ACL remnant is preserved and sutured around the graft in a sleeve‐like manner to provide protection for the graft. However, no studies have shown that this technique has a significant effect on promoting ligamentization and tendon‐bone union of the graft in the middle and late postoperative period. In this article, we detail the technique of using tendon sutures to fix the ACL remnant in a sleeve‐like manner around the graft.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Preoperative Preparation

The surgical technique is presented in Video 1. The patient is positioned supine on the operating table, with a well‐inflated tourniquet applied to the proximal end of the same‐side thigh. The surgical area is prepared and draped in a standard sterile fashion, and the arthroscope, electric reamer, and other equipment are connected. After anesthesia and before the surgery begins, physical examination is performed, such as the Lachman test, anterior drawer test, or pivot shift test, to assess and confirm the presence of ACL rupture.

Arthroscopic Stage

Standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals are constructed using a surgical knife. The patellofemoral joint, medial and lateral compartments, and femoral notch are then inspected arthroscopically. During this process, the strength of the remaining ACL fibers is tested with a probe to confirm the degree of ACL tear. The ACL remnant is repaired and redundant synovium in the joint cavity is cleared with an electric reamer. The presence of meniscal tears is assessed by observation and a probe, and repairs are performed if necessary. The anterior portion of the medial wall of the lateral femoral condyle is contoured to restore its normal arch, to ensure no impingement between the ACL graft and the intercondylar fossa during knee extension.

Graft Preparation

In this technique, the semitendinosus and gracilis tendons are used as autografts for ACL reconstruction. A 5 cm oblique incision is made at 2 cm medial tibial tuberosity, and the sartorius fascia is exposed through blunt dissection. After locating the semitendinosus and gracilis tendons, the sartorius fascia is incised parallel to the semitendinosus tendon. The tendons are separated from the sartorius fascia and the tibial insertions are separated. The tendons are harvested using a tendon extractor.

The harvested tendons are cleaned of residual muscle fibers on the tendon preparation table. The graft is fixed to the femur using a titanium suspension plate (RIGIDLOOP system, 15 mm, Depuy, Shanghai). The tendons are sutured at their proximal ends with a continuous whipstitch and fixed on the loop of RIGIDLOOP. The two tendons are passed over the loop to form a 6‐strand graft (Figure 1A). The graft diameter is measured, and a drill of the same diameter will be used to create the femoral tunnel. The depth of the femoral tunnel will be measured, and a mark will be made on the graft after femoral tunnel construction (Figure 1B), which provide guidance when the graft is pulled from the tibia to the femur. The tendon suture is passed through the loop of RIGIDLOOP for suturing the ACL remnant (Figure 1C).

Femoral Tunnel Construction

The femoral end of the ACL remnant is cleared using an electric reamer and radiofrequency plasma knife to fully expose the medial wall of the lateral femoral condyle. The lateral femoral intercondylar ridge is identified, and the intersection of the lateral intercondylar ridge and the bifurcate ridge is a key landmark for locating the femoral tunnel opening. In nearly isometric ACL reconstruction, the femoral tunnel is located on the lateral intercondylar ridge, 2 to 3 mm proximal to the bifurcate ridge. If the lateral intercondylar ridge is absent, the isometric femoral tunnel is located on the extension line of the posterior femoral cortex. The femoral locator is used to position the isometric point, and a long guide pin (2.4 mm) is drilled in. The locator is removed, and a 4.5‐mm hollow drill is used to penetrate the lateral femoral cortex. The depth of the femoral tunnel is measured with a depth gauge. An 8‐mm length of the 4.5‐mm diameter part is retained at the tunnel exit, and the rest of the tunnel is enlarged with a hollow drill of the same diameter as the graft to create a bottleneck‐like stepped tunnel.

Tibial Tunnel Construction

The ACL tibial remnant is retracted to observe the anteromedial attachment of the lateral meniscus. The center of the tibial remnant is located approximately 2.0 mm medial to the anteromedial attachment of the lateral meniscus. The tip of the tibial locator is placed 1.0 mm posterior to the center of the original ACL tibial remnant, and a short guide pin (2.4 mm) is drilled in. The tibial locator is removed, and a drill of the same diameter as the graft is used to create the tibial tunnel.

Graft Insertion and Fixation

Under arthroscopic guidance, a long guide pin with a tail thread is used to pull a double‐strand tendon suture through the femoral tunnel, with the tail end retrieved from the tibial tunnel exit using a grasper for graft traction.

A tendon suture is passed through the ACL remnant with suture hook (Figure 2A), and a polydioxanone (PDS) II suture is introduced (Figure 2B). The PDS II suture is retrieved using a grasper (Figure 2C), and by pulling on the PDS II suture, the ACL remnant can be retracted to expose the tibial tunnel opening, which is convenient for graft traction.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

All patients follow a standardized postoperative rehabilitation protocol. For isolated ACL reconstruction, the knee is placed in a hinged knee brace locked in full extension during walking and sleep for 6 weeks. Partial weight‐bearing with crutches is allowed from postoperative day 3, with the knee range of motion limited to 0° to 90° of flexion for the first 2 weeks, gradually increasing to maximum flexion. If quadriceps control is good at 4 weeks postoperatively, crutches can be discarded, and full weight‐bearing can be gradually increased. The brace can be stopped at 6 weeks postoperatively. If a meniscal repair is performed simultaneously, partial weight‐bearing is delayed until 1 to 2 weeks postoperatively, depending on the meniscal injury. Around 12 weeks postoperatively, patients can run and perform sport‐specific agility maneuvers, while also engaging in open‐chain quadriceps strengthening exercises. Full return to sports, including those requiring sudden stops and turns, usually occurs at 6 months postoperatively, depending on quadriceps recovery and patient readiness.

Postoperative Outcomes

The early postoperative period imaging results showed that the ligamentization of the ACL graft with modified remnant‐preserving technique was higher than that with nonresidual preservation reconstruction graft. Magnetic resonance imaging had shown that the SNQ of ACL graft with modified remnant‐preserving technique was higher than that with nonresidual preservation reconstruction in the early postoperative period. It was found no significant difference in the average SNQ value of the grafts at 3 months postoperatively, possibly because the grafts had not yet begun to remodel or were just at the initial stage. And it has also shown that graft maturity begins to increase gradually at 3 months postoperatively, peaks at 6 months, and then gradually decreases. 11

DISCUSSION

This article describes a simple and reproducible method for preserving the original ACL tibial remnant and fixing it onto the graft during ACL reconstruction. The advantages of this technique are that while maintaining the basic position of the tibial and femoral tunnels in isometric ACL reconstruction, the original ACL tibial remnant is suspended and fixed on the loop of the RIGIDLOOP, allowing it to wrap around the graft in a sleeve‐like manner (Table 1).

This article summarizes some pearls and pitfalls for this surgical technique (Table 2). One critical step is to pass a PDS II suture through the tibial remnant, which can be used to drag the remnant to obtain a better surgical view. When fixing the tibial remnant, the PDS II suture is also used to introduce the tendon suture. Another critical step is to pass a tendon suture on the loop of the RIGIDLOOP, which is used to fix the tibial remnant with tension on the surface of the graft. Haviv et al. 12 found that within 3 months after ACL rupture, the ACL remnant does not form scar tissue, but later, scar tissue forms at the femoral notch and eventually adheres to the posterior cruciate ligament. Therefore, the ACLR should be performed within 3 months after injury. To prevent the tibial remnant from degenerating and atrophying into scar tissue too early, it is crucial to fix the ACL remnant on the loop of the RIGIDLOOP with a tendon suture to maintain it under tension.

When performing this technique, there are some considerations. First, preserving the tibial remnant may affect the positioning of the tibial tunnel. During the surgery, the tip of the tibial locator is placed 1 mm posterior to the center of the original tibial remnant. 13 , 14 However, as the tibial remnant may block the arthroscopic view, inexperienced surgeons may place the tibial tunnel at the center of the tibial remnant and injure the remnant, leading to failure in preserving the tibial remnant. Another common mistake is that the tendon suture may slip off the ACL remnant during the knot‐tying process. This is because the residual tissue of the ACL is in a loose and friable state after the ACL tears. If the suture passes through a loose area of ACL remnant, it may dislodge from the ACL remnant during knot‐tying due to excessive tension. A probe should be used to examine the ACL remnant to assess the condition of the tissue to avoid this mistake before using the suture passer. Any loose and friable tissue should be removed using a planer blade.

The ACLR with modified remnant‐preserving technique, which wraps the ACL remnant around the graft in a sleeve‐like membrane suturing method, provides effective protection to the graft. However, because the ACL remnant and part of the synovial tissue are preserved, the risk of post‐operative knee stiffness is increased. 15 Therefore, patients must strictly follow the rehabilitation plan to prevent knee stiffness and limited range of motion. 16 Additionally, further research is needed to evaluate the long‐term clinical outcomes of this technique and to compare it with other technical solutions.
