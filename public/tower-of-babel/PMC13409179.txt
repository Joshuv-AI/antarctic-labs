Forest soils: A species-rich habitat for Cylindrocladiella
Authors: W.W. Zhou, H.B. Dong, H.X. Zhou, P.W. Crous, M.J. Wingfield, S.F. Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Species of Cylindrocladiella are soil-borne fungi that are distributed in temperate, sub-tropical and tropical regions of the world. Very little is known regarding the biology of these fungi that are mostly considered as saprobes, but there have also been reports of pathogenicity in some cases. Prior to this study, 46 species of Cylindrocladiella were known based on DNA sequence comparisons and phylogenetic analyses. Only seven of those species have been reported from China, some of which were associated with diseases of woody plants. In this study, soil samples were collected from natural forests and plantations in eight provinces (autonomous regions) of northeastern, central, southeastern, southwestern, and southern regions of China. A total of 914 soil samples were analysed, resulting in 407 Cylindrocladiella isolates. They were identified based on morphological characteristics and phylogenetic analyses of DNA sequence data for the tef1, tub2, his3, and ITS regions. This resulted in the identification of eight known, and 22 novel species. Cylindrocladiella spp. tended to be widely distributed in forest soils across a wide range of climatic zones. The species diversity in warm areas was higher than in cooler areas. Of the 68 species now recognised and verified based on DNA data, these fungi are known from more than 25 countries, of which 57 species were isolated from soils, 18 from plant tissues, and nine from both soils and plant tissues. Species with wide geographic distributions including putative pathogens such as, Cy. peruviana and Cy. parva that have been found on multiple plant species, while others are confined to more limited niches. The exceptionally large number of new species found in this study suggests that Cylindrocladiella species have been poorly sampled and that many more are likely to be found in warm and humid environments. Overall, this study provides a foundation for future investigations aimed at clarifying the evolutionary relationships, ecology, and pathology of this interesting and little understood genus.Citation: Zhou WW, Dong HB, Zhou HX, Crous PW, Wingfield MJ, Chen SF (2026). INTRODUCTION

The hyphomycete genus Cylindrocladiella (Cy.) (Hypocreales, Nectriaceae) was established in 1982 to accommodate five cylindrocladium-like species, Cylindrocladiella camelliae, Cy. infestans, Cy. novae-zelandiae, Cy. parva, and Cy. peruviana (Boesewinkel 1982). While peripherally similar, Cylindrocladiella spp. produce conidia and conidiophores that are morphologically different to those in the better-known genus Cylindrocladium (asexual morph of Calonectria) (Boesewinkel 1982). Peerally (1991) argued for the separation of Cylindrocladium species with small conidia in the new genus Cylindrocladiella. This was supported by subsequent molecular studies confirming the generic status of Cylindrocladiella (Victor et al. 1998, Schoch et al. 2000).

Species of Cylindrocladiella are distinguished morphologically from Cylindrocladium by small (< 20 μm in length), 1-septate conidia, aseptate, thick-walled stipe extensions, penicillate and/or subverticillate symmetrically branched conidiophores, slimy conidial masses, and Nectricladiella sexual morphs. In contrast, Cylindrocladium, has larger (> 20 μm in length) and 1 to multi-septate conidia, thin-walled, septate stipe extensions, only penicillate conidiophores, conidial masses in parallel clusters, and Calonectria sexual morphs (Boesewinkel 1982, Crous & Wingfield 1993, Crous et al. 1994, Crous 2002, Lombard et al. 2012, 2017).

Species of Cylindrocladiella are generally considered as saprobes or in some cases pathogens occurring in the soil or on various forestry, agricultural, and horticultural plants (Crous 2002, van Coller et al. 2005, Erper et al. 2013, Aiello et al. 2020, Ye et al. 2021). Cylindrocladiella spp. have been shown to infect at least 72 plant species in 38 families (https://fungi.ars.usda.gov) including the Arecaceae, Fabaceae, Myrtaceae, Pinaceae and Proteaceae (https://fungi.ars.usda.gov/). Symptoms associated with infections by these fungi include root rot, stem wilt, damping off, shoot die-back, leaf spot, and cutting rot (Crous et al. 1991, Crous 2002, van Coller et al. 2005, Erper et al. 2013, Armengol & Gramaje 2016, Aiello et al. 2020, Akgül et al. 2022).

Species of Cylindrocladiella are widely distributed across a range of climatic zones including temperate, sub-tropical and tropical regions of the world (Crous 2002, Lombard et al. 2012, 2017, Pham et al. 2018, Liu & Chen 2024). Of these, 46 species have been identified based on DNA sequence comparisons (Inderbitzin et al. 2012, Lombard et al. 2012, 2017, Crous et al. 2017, 2019, Pham et al. 2018, Tibpromma et al. 2018, Marin-Felix et al. 2019, Liu & Chen 2024). Cylindrocladiella species have been reported from all continents except Antarctica and have been recorded from 25 countries, with a relatively large number known from Australia (13), Thailand (11), South Africa (10), China (7), and New Zealand (6) (https://fungi.ars.usda.gov).

Species of Cylindrocladiella are soil-borne fungi. Of the 46 species verified based on DNA sequence comparisons, 35 were isolated from soil (Lombard et al. 2012, 2017, Pham et al. 2018, Marin-Felix et al. 2019, Liu & Chen 2024). In China, seven species, namely Cy. infestans, Cy. lageniformis, Cy. longistipitata, Cy. peruviana, Cy. viticola, Cy. xishuangbannaensis, and Cy. yunnanensis have been identified based on DNA sequence data (Tibpromma et al. 2018, Schoch et al. 2000, Ye et al. 2021, Wang et al. 2023, Zhou et al. 2023, Liu & Chen 2024) and three of these species, Cy. infestans, Cy. longistipitata and Cy. yunnanensis, were isolated from soils (Schoch et al. 2000, Liu & Chen 2024). Cylindrocladiella xishuangbannaensis was isolated from Pandanus sp. (Tibpromma et al. 2018). Cylindrocladiella lageniformis, Cy. peruviana and Cy. viticola from roots, rootstocks, or trunks of Vitis vinifera (Ye et al. 2021, Wang et al. 2023, Zhou et al. 2023).

The aim of this study was to consider the diversity of Cylindrocladiella species in soils from natural forests and commercial plantations over a wide geographic range in China. Following recent approaches, the isolated fungi were identified based on phylogenetic analyses as well as morphological characteristics. The results were compared with knowledge of Cylindrocladiella species globally, to understand their geographic distributions and substrate characteristics.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample collection and isolations

Soils samples were collected from 38 forests in eight provinces (regions) of China, located in different climatic zones (Figs 1, 2). These sites included forests at 38 sites in Heilongjiang (6), Henan (2), Xizang (15), Fujian (5), Guangxi (1), Yunnan (1), Hong Kong (2), and Hainan (6). Details including geographic region, climate, GPS coordinates, and altitude of these 38 sites are provided in Table 1. Samples from Hong Kong were collected in March 2014, from Guangxi in June 2021, and those from the other six provinces between October 2023 and November 2024.

Soil samples were collected from natural forests in Heilongjiang, Henan, Xizang, Yunnan, Hong Kong, and Hainan, and from Eucalyptus urophylla × E. grandis plantations in Fujian, Guangxi, and Hainan (Fig. 1). Both the natural forests and plantations typically had thick layers of leaf litter. This was removed before collecting the soil samples. The upper 0–20 cm of the humid soil profile was collected. Between seven and 72 soil samples (Table 1) were collected randomly at each site. Samples were placed in plastic bags to maintain moisture and transported to the laboratory for baiting and isolation of Cylindrocladiella spp.

Soil samples from each plastic bag were transferred to a plastic sample isolation cup (4.4 cm diam., 5.5 cm in height, and 80 mL in volume; Chengdu Rich Science Industry Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China). They were then moistened by spraying with distilled water and mixed well using a sterilized bamboo stick. Surface-disinfested Medicago sativa (alfalfa) seeds and Saccharum officinarum (sugarcane) leaf fragments were scattered onto the soil surfaces as baits. The soils and baits were incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 7 d. After incubation, the samples were inspected using dissection microscope for the presence of conidiophores on the baits having typical morphological characteristics of Cylindrocladiella (Crous 2002). Conidial masses were transferred using a sterilized needle to 2 % malt extract agar (MEA) containing 16 g malt extract (Qingdao Hope Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China) and 16 g agar (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) per 800 mL of water. After incubation at 25 °C for 6–8 h, germinating conidia were transferred individually onto fresh MEA plates and incubated at 25 °C for 5–10 d to obtain single-conidium cultures.

All single-conidium cultures were deposited in the Culture Collection (CSFF) of the Forest Pathogen Center at the College of Forestry of the Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University, Fuzhou, Fujian Province, China. Representative isolates are also maintained in the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Centre (CGMCC), Beijing, China. Dried specimens were deposited in the Mycological Fungarium of the Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (HMAS), Beijing, China.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing

All Cylindrocladiella isolates obtained in this study were identified based on DNA sequence data. Mycelium of each isolate was collected from axenic cultures grown on MEA for 7 d using a sterilized scalpel transferred into 2 mL Eppendorf tubes. Genomic DNA was extracted from the mycelium of every isolate using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method described by Van Burik et al. (1998). After adding 30 μL TE buffer (1 M Tris-HCl and 0.5 M EDTA, pH 8.0), the extracted DNA was dissolved and 2.5 μL RNase (10 mg/ mL) was added to degrade the RNA. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h and the DNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop Lite Plus spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). All extracted DNA samples were diluted to approximately 100 ng/uL using DNase/RNase-free ddH2O (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and stored at −20 °C for further use.

Based on the results of previous research, the translation elongation factor 1-alpha (tef1), β-tubulin (tub2), partial regions of histone H3 (his3), and internal transcribed spacer regions and intervening 5.8S nrRNA (ITS) of these four regions were considered as reliable DNA barcodes to distinguish species in Cylindrocladiella (Lombard et al. 2012, 2017, Pham et al. 2018, Liu & Chen 2024). The primer pairs EF1-728F (Carbone & Kohn 1999)/EF-2 (O’Donnell et al. 1998), T1 (O’Donnell & Cigelnik 1997)/CYLTUB1R (Crous et al. 2004b), CYLH3F/CYLH3R (Crous et al. 2004b), and ITS1F (Gardes & Bruns 1993)/ITS4 (White et al. 1990) were used to amplify the fragments of tef1, tub2, his3, and ITS regions, respectively. The amplification reactions were conducted as described by Liu et al. (2020).

Amplification products of all Cylindrocladiella isolates were sequenced in both directions using the same primers used for PCR amplification. The PCR products were sequenced by the Beijing Genomics Institute located in Guangzhou, China. All obtained sequences were edited and assembled using MEGA v. 7.0 software (Kumar et al. 2016). All sequences obtained in this study were deposited in GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov).

The tef1 and tub2 regions were sequenced for all Cylindrocladiella isolates, and used in a standard nucleotide BLAST search in the NCBI database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) for preliminary species identification. Representative isolates for all the genotypes determined by tef1 and tub2 sequences were selected for PCR amplification and sequencing of additional regions of his3 and ITS.

Multigene phylogenetic analyses

The tef1, tub2, his3, and ITS regions of all representative Cylindrocladiella isolates sequenced in this study were used in the phylogenetic analyses. In addition, these gene regions for representative isolates, including the ex-type isolates, of all published Cylindrocladiella species were obtained from GenBank and used in the phylogenetic analyses. The sequence datasets were aligned using the online version of MAFFT v. 7 (Katoh & Standley 2013) (http://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/server) with an FFT–NS–i strategy (slow; interactive refinement method). The aligned datasets were manually curated in MEGA v. 7.0 software (Kumar et al. 2016). Sequence data for two isolates of Calonectria pauciramosa (CBS 137243 and CBS 138824) were used as outgroups (Liu et al. 2020, Liu & Chen 2024).

For phylogenetic inference, sequence datasets for each of the four gene regions and a concatenated dataset were used. PhyML v. 3.0 (Guindon & Gascuel 2003) was used to perform maximum likelihood (ML) analyses. The alignments of sequence data for final analyses were deposited in TreeBASE (http://treebase.org). The analyses were performed with the default GTR substitution matrix. In PhyML, the bootstrap supports for ML analyses were estimated using 1000 replicates to assess the reliability of the inferred phylogenies. Phylogenetic trees were viewed using MEGA v. 7.0 (Kumar et al. 2016). Based on phylogenetic analyses and sequence comparisons, the Cylindrocladiella isolates obtained in this study were identified.

The individual Cylindrocladiella species were recognised and identified based on concordance of four gene genealogies. Three criteria were applied in determining species boundaries based on phylogenetic analyses and sequence comparisons. These were (i) in the single gene phylogenetic trees, the isolate formed a distinct lineage that differentiated it from other species in at least two of the four individual gene regions, and where these groupings did not contradict those of the other loci; (ii) in the combined tree based on the four-loci concatenated dataset, the isolate formed an independent lineage supported by high bootstrap values; (iii) the isolate had a certain number (close to or greater than 0.5 % of the total number of base of the single gene sequence) of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that differentiated it from its phylogenetically closest relatives in each of the two gene regions mentioned in criterion (i); and the isolate had a certain number (greater than 0.5 % of the total number of base of four-loci sequences) of SNPs that differentiated it from its phylogenetically closest relatives. Putatively novel species recognised based on the phylogenetic analyses were further confirmed utilising morphological and/or ecological characteristics.

Morphology

Representative isolates of the novel species identified based on DNA sequence comparisons were used for morphological characterisation. To induce the asexual morphs, mycelium plugs from single conidium cultures were transferred onto synthetic nutrient-poor agar (SNA; Nirenberg 1981), and incubated at 25 °C for 4–7 d. Pieces of autoclaved carnation leaves were added to the SNA plates to induce the asexual morphs. Asexual Cylindrocladiella structures were mounted in 85 % lactic acid, and examined using a Zeiss Axio Imager.A2 compound microscope (Carl Zeiss Ltd., Germany).

To induce the sexual morphs, isolates of each novel species were crossed with each other in all possible combinations on minimum salt agar (MN; Guerber & Correll 2001). Sterile toothpicks were placed on the surface of the media (Guerber & Correll 2001). Isolates were crossed with themselves as controls. All the crosses were incubated at 25 °C for 2–8 wk. When isolate combinations produced perithecia extruding viable ascospores, the crosses were considered as successful. In the case of sexual structures, the perithecia were transferred to Leica Biosystems tissue freezing medium (Leica Biosystems Nussloch, Germany), which was frozen at −20 °C for 10 min. Vertical sections (8 μm thick) were cut through the perithecia using a CryoStar NX50 microtome (Thermo Scientific, Walldorf, Germany) at −20 °C and examined under a Zeiss Axio Imager.A2 compound microscope. The 8-µm-thick sections through the perithecia were mounted in 85 % lactic acid and 3 % KOH to evaluate changes in colour.

For each taxonomically informative structure (such as the length and width of conidia, and ascospores), fifty measurements were determined. Minimum, maximum and average (mean) measurements are presented as (minimum–) (average – standard deviation) – (average + standard deviation) (–maximum). Only the extremes are presented for other fungal structures.

To determine the optimal growth temperature for each of the novel species, 5-mm-diam. mycelial plugs of representative isolates were taken from the actively growing margins of 7-d-old cultures, and transferred to the centres of 90-mm-diam. Petri dishes containing fresh MEA. These were incubated at temperatures ranging from 5 °C to 35 °C at 5 °C intervals, and five replicate plates per temperature per isolate. Colony diameters were measured after 6–7 d. Colony morphology and colours were described using the colour charts of Rayner (1970) using 7-d-old cultures on MEA incubated at 25 °C in the dark in the incubator. All descriptions and associated metadata were deposited in MycoBank (Crous et al. 2004a, www.mycobank.org).

Species diversity and distribution

In order to gain insights into their biology, data pertaining all Cylindrocladiella spp. isolated in this study were considered. Parameters for comparison included number of isolates of each species, geographic occurrence and species number/isolate number (SN/IN) for each province.

Global diversity and distribution

All available data regarding species diversity, areas of occurrence, substrates and hosts were assembled from previously published studies for species that have been identified based on DNA sequence data. These data were compared with those arising from the present study to provide a global overview of occurrence of the various species, the substrate characteristics of all those species, and the soil/plant distribution characteristics of the widely distributed species.

RESULTS

Sample collection and isolations

Nine hundred and fourteen soil samples were collected from 38 sites in forests of the eight provinces selected for this study. Cylindrocladiella spp. were isolated from 97 of these samples from 20 sites (Table 1). Between two and eight single-conidium cultures were obtained from each sample with each taken from a different position on the bait. This resulted in a total of 407 isolates for further study (Tables 1, S1).

Multigene phylogenetic analyses

The tef1 and tub2 gene regions were amplified for all 407 isolates (Table S1). The genotype of each isolate was determined based on the tef1 and tub2 gene sequences. This resulted in 54, 45, and 69 genotypes for tef1, tub2, and a combination of tef1 and tub2 sequences, respectively. Based on these 69 genotypes, and taking the sampling sites into consideration, 196 isolates were selected to amplify the his3 and ITS regions. Sequence data for all of these isolates, which represented the 89 genotypes determined from the sequence data for the tef1, tub2, his3, and ITS regions, were used in phylogenetic analyses (Table 2). The amplicons generated for the tef1, tub2, his3, and ITS regions of the Cylindrocladiella spp. studied were approximately 480, 560, 505, and 560 bp, respectively.

Sequences of 84 isolates, ex-type or other strains, representing 46 Cylindrocladiella species that have been described based on tef1/tub2/his3/ITS sequences data were retrieved from GenBank (Table 3). Phylogenetic analyses based on the four individual regions and the concatenated dataset for the four regions were conducted using the ML method. The aligned sequences of each region of tef1, tub2, his3, ITS, as well as the combined sequences of four regions were deposited in TreeBASE (No. 32659). The ML tree based on the data for the four combined loci showed that the 196 isolates clustered in 30 groups, which were labelled as Groups 1–30 (Fig. 4). Based on the three criteria applied in determining species boundaries described previously, we treated the 30 groups emerging from the phylogenetic analyses as distinct taxa (Figs 4, S1–S4). Of these, we recognised 22 as novel taxa. These novel taxa resided in Groups 2–4, 6–9, 11–16, 18–22, 26, 28–30 that fulfilled the three criteria that were applied to define novel species. Isolates in Groups 1, 5, 10, 17, 23, 24, 25, and 27 did not fulfil all three criteria for novel species clarification, and these groups were identified as representing Cy. longistipitata, Cy. horticola, Cy. brevistipitata, Cy. reginae, Cy. parvispora, Cy. obpyriformis, Cy. peruviana, and Cy. longiphialidica, respectively.

After identification of the 196 isolates based on sequence comparisons and phylogenetic analyses of tef1/tub2/his3/ITS sequences, the remaining 211 isolates collected in this study were identified based only on tef1/tub2 sequences (Table S1). In total, 68 Cylindrocladiella species were identified in this study of which 22 represented novel taxa. These 68 Cylindrocladiella species grouped in at least six species complexes (SC). They included Cy. infestans SC (29 species), Cy. lageniformis SC (seven species), Cy. parva SC (eight species), Cy. novae-zelandiae SC (five species), Cy. camelliae SC (16 species), and Cy. ledongensis SC (two species) (Fig. 4). Cylindrocladiella postalofficium formed an independent phylogenetic lineage. Species in the Cy. lageniformis complex formed several independent clades with long tree lengths, which indicated that several complexes could be present, but this can only be clarified once more taxa are found to better define this complex (Fig. 4).

Morphology

Asexual structures were common on SNA in all 22 novel species defined based on the phylogenetic analyses studied, and only isolate CSFF 31406 residing in phylogenetic Group 9 isolates (Fig. 4) formed perithecia with viable ascospores, that were produced within 6 wk. The species was shown to be homothallic based on the fact that perithecia formed in a single conidial isolate. The novel species recognised in this study (Fig. 4) are described below.

Taxonomy

Cylindrocladiella curvata W.W. Zhou & S.F. Chen, sp. nov. MB 863802. Fig. 5.

Etymology: From Latin curvatus (curved). Name refers to the curved vesicles in this fungus.

Sexual morph not observed. Conidiophores dimorphic, penicillate and subverticillate, mononematous and hyaline. Penicillate conidiophores comprising a stipe, a penicillate arrangement of fertile branches, a stipe extension and a terminal vesicle; stipe septate, hyaline, smooth, 76.5–119.5 × 4–6.5 μm (av. = 95.5 × 5.5 μm); stipe extension aseptate, straight, 89.5–173 μm (av. = 127 μm) long, thick-walled with one basal septum, terminating in thin-walled, elongated, curved cylindrical to lanceolate vesicles, 3–5 μm (av. = 4 μm) wide. Penicillate conidiogenous apparatus with primary branches aseptate, 9–20 × 2.5–4.5 μm (av. = 15.5 × 3 μm), secondary branches aseptate, 9.5–13.5 × 2–3.5 μm (av. = 11 × 3 μm), each terminal branch producing 3–9 phialides; phialides cylindrical to cymbiform to doliiform, hyaline, aseptate, 8.5–18 × 2.5–3.5 μm (av. = 11.5 × 2.5 μm), apex with minute periclinal thickening and collarette. Subverticillate conidiophores in moderate numbers, comprising of a septate stipe, primary and secondary branches terminating in 1–5 phialides; primary branches straight, hyaline, 0–1-septate, 23–50 × 2.5–4.5 μm (av. = 32 × 3.5 μm); secondary branches straight, hyaline, aseptate, 18–49 × 2.5–4 μm (av. = 26.5 × 3 μm); phialides cylindrical to cymbiform, hyaline, aseptate, 15–36.5 × 2.5–3.5 μm (av. = 24.5 × 2.5 μm), apex with minute periclinal thickening and collarette. Conidia cylindrical, rounded at both ends, straight, 1-septate, (10.5–)12–14(–14.5) × 1.5–2.5 μm (av. = 13 × 2 μm), frequently slightly flattened at the base, held in asymmetrical clusters by colourless slime.

Culture characteristics: Colonies white to buff on the surface and pale luteous to salmon in reverse on MEA after 6 d; smooth margins; extensive aerial mycelium in the middle and the margins; chlamydospores not observed. Optimal growth temperature at 25 °C, no growth at 5 °C and 35 °C; after 6 d, colonies at 10 °C, 15 °C, 20 °C, 25 °C and 30 °C reached 13.3 mm, 29.3 mm, 56.0 mm, 76.0 mm and 52.3 mm, respectively.

Ecology and distribution: Cylindrocladiella curvata was isolated from soil in a natural forest in Hainan Province in southern China. This area is consistently humid throughout the year. It is one of the few tropical rainforest areas in China.

Typus: China, Hainan, Ledong County, Jianfeng Town, 18°44′25.2636″N, 108°51′30.6540″E, 815.92 m.a.s.l., from soil in a natural forest, Nov. 2024, S.F. Chen et al. (holotype HMAS 354482, fungarium specimen preserved as dried culture, culture ex-type CSFF 31458 = CGMCC 3.29902).

Additional material examined: China, Hainan, Ledong County, Jianfeng Town, 18°44′25.2636″N, 108°51′30.6540″E, 815.92 m.a.s.l., from soil in a natural forest, Nov. 2024, S.F. Chen et al., HMAS 354481, culture CSFF 31457 = CGMCC 3.29901; ibid., cultures CSFF 31456 and CSFF 31459.

Notes: Cylindrocladiella curvata is phylogenetically closely related to Cy. montana. Morphologically, the stipes of Cy. curvata (av. = 95.5 × 5.5 μm) are longer than those in Cy. montana (av. = 70.5 × 4.5 μm); the stipe extensions in Cy. curvata (av. = 127 μm) are longer than those of Cy. montana (av. = 90.5 μm); the conidia of Cy. curvata (av. = 13 × 2 μm) are narrower than those of Cy. montana (av. = 13 × 2.5 μm). Furthermore, the ex-type isolates of Cy. curvata (CSFF 31458) and Cy. montana (CSFF 31436) have 15 unique nucleotides for two loci: tub2, 8 bp; his3, 7 bp.

Cylindrocladiella cylindrica W.W. Zhou & S.F. Chen, sp. nov. MB 863803. Fig. 6.

Etymology: Name refers to the cylindrical vesicles in this fungus.

Sexual morph not observed. Conidiophores monomorphic, penicillate, mononematous and hyaline. Penicillate conidiophores comprising a stipe, a penicillate arrangement of fertile branches, a stipe extension and a terminal vesicle; stipe septate, hyaline, smooth, 55–154 × 3–5.5 μm (av. = 93.5 × 4 μm); stipe extension aseptate, straight, 47.5–132 μm (av. = 89 μm) long, thick-walled with one basal septum, terminating in thin-walled, elongated, cylindrical to lanceolate vesicles, 3–4.5 μm (av. = 4 μm) wide. Penicillate conidiogenous apparatus with primary branches aseptate, 11–24.5 × 2.5–4 μm (av. = 15 × 3 μm), secondary branches aseptate, 9–16.5 × 2.5–3.5 μm (av. = 12.5 × 3 μm), each terminal branch producing 2–9 phialides; phialides cylindrical to cymbiform, hyaline, aseptate, 8–15.5 × 2.5–3.5 μm (av. = 12 × 3 μm), apex with minute periclinal thickening and collarette. Subverticillate conidiophores not observed. Conidia cylindrical, rounded at both ends, straight, 1-septate, (11–)12.5–15.5(–17.5) × 1.5–2.5(–3.5) μm (av. = 14 × 2 μm), frequently slightly flattened at the base, held in asymmetrical clusters by colourless slime.

Culture characteristics: Colonies white to buff on the surface and salmon to luteous in reverse on MEA after 6 d; smooth margins; extensive aerial mycelium in the middle and the margins; chlamydospores moderate throughout medium, arranged in chains. Optimal growth temperature at 25 °C, no growth at 5 °C and 35 °C; after 6 d, colonies at 10 °C, 15 °C, 20 °C, 25 °C and 30 °C reached 15.0 mm, 33.5 mm, 59.5 mm, 74.0 mm and 50.0 mm, respectively.

Ecology and distribution: Cylindrocladiella cylindrica was isolated from natural forest soil in Xizang in western China. This area is in a subtropical zone, and located at the edge of the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau.

Typus: China, Xizang, Linzhi, Motuo County, Motuo Town, 29°17′1.158″N, 95°15′44.046″E, 804.59 m.a.s.l., from soil in a natural forest, Oct. 2023, S.F. Chen et al. (holotype HMAS 354484, fungarium specimen preserved as dried culture, culture ex-type CSFF 26376 = CGMCC 3.29904).

Additional material examined: China, Xizang, Linzhi, Motuo County, Motuo Town, 29°17′1.158″N, 95°15′44.046″E, 804.59 m.a.s.l., from soil in a natural forest, Oct. 2023, S.F. Chen et al., HMAS 354485, culture CSFF 26377 = CGMCC 3.29905.

Notes: Cylindrocladiella cylindrica is phylogenetically closely related to Cy. montana. The two species are morphologically distinct with subverticillate conidiophores present in Cy. montana, but not in Cy. cylindrica. The stipes of Cy. cylindrica (av. = 93.5 × 4 μm) are also longer than those of Cy. montana (av. = 70.5 × 4.5 μm) and the conidia of Cy. cylindrica (av. = 14 × 2 μm) are longer but narrower than those of Cy. montana (av. = 13 × 2.5 μm). Furthermore, the ex-type isolates of Cy. cylindrica (CSFF 26376) and Cy. montana (CSFF 31436) have 13 unique nucleotides for three loci: tub2, 5 bp; his3, 7 bp; ITS, 1 bp.

Cylindrocladiella dengfengensis W.W. Zhou & S.F. Chen, sp. nov. MB 863804. Fig. 7.

Etymology: Named for the locality Dengfeng where the fungus was isolated for the first time.

Species diversity and distribution

For the 30 Cylindrocladiella species identified in this study, Cy. longistipitata (82 isolates) accounted for 20.1 % of the isolates. This was followed by the newly described species Cy. temperata (62 isolates, 15.2 %), Cy. henanensis (59 isolates, 14.5 %), Cy. heilongjiangensis (31 isolates, 7.6 %), Cy. parviconidia (24 isolates, 5.9 %), Cy. fafuana (21 isolates, 5.2 %) (Fig. 3). Cylindrocladiella longistipitata, Cy. temperata, Cy. henanensis were thus, the most prevalent species (Fig. 3).

Cylindrocladiella species were isolated from between one and three provinces. Cylindrocladiella fafuana (21 isolates; Fujian, Hainan, and Hong Kong) was the only species found in three provinces. Cylindrocladiella montata (4 isolates; Hainan, and Xizang), Cy. peruviana (8 isolates; Fujian, and Guangxi), Cy. temperata (62 isolates; Heilongjiang, and Henan), Cy. tropicalis (8 isolates; Hainan, and Yunnan), and Cy. turangicola (4 isolates; Fujian, and Hainan) were obtained from two provinces. Each of the other 24 species was found in only one province, including Cy. longistipitata, which was the most dominant species.

Based on the values of species number/isolate number (SN/IN) for each of the eight provinces sampled, the results showed that the value for Hong Kong (0.67, two species of three isolates) was highest. This was followed by Yunnan (0.54, seven species of 13 isolates), Fujian (0.24, four species of 17 isolates), Hainan (0.14, 10 species of 69 isolates), and Guangxi (0.14, one species of seven isolates). The SN/IN values of Xizang (0.058, seven species of 120 isolates), Heilongjiang (0.043, two species of 47 isolates), and Henan (0.031, four species of 131 isolates) are relatively low. The results showed that the SN/IN values for the temperate zone (Heilongjiang in the cold temperate zone, and Henan in the warm temperate zone) were lower than those for the subtropical (Xizang, Fujian, Guangxi, Yunnan, and Hong Kong) and tropical zones (Hainan). Twenty-five Cylindrocladiella spp. were obtained from subtropical/tropical zones, and only five species were isolated from a temperate zone. No species were isolated from both the temperate zone and subtropical/tropical zones.

Global diversity and distribution

Including the 22 novel species identified and described in this study, there are now 68 Cylindrocladiella spp. recognised and supported by DNA sequence data. These species have been found on all continents except Antarctica (Table S2, Fig. 27). They have mainly been isolated from regions of the world having tropical or sub-tropical climates. Countries from which a relatively large number of species are known include China (35), Australia (13), Thailand (11), South Africa (10), followed by New Zealand (6) and Vietnam (6) (Table S2, Fig. 27).

Cylindrocladiella species have been isolated from soils, plants, leaf litter, and ants. Of the 68 recognised species, 57 have been found in soils, 18 have been isolated from plant tissues, and nine species have been recorded from both soils and plant tissues. Two species have been collected only from leaf litter, and one species (Cy. peruviana) was isolated from ants, soils, and plant tissues (Table S2, Fig. 27).

Five species, Cy. peruviana (13), Cy. lageniformis (6), Cy. parva (6), Cy. infestans (5), and Cy. stellenboschensis (5) have been recorded from at least five different countries (Table S2, Fig. 27). With the exception of Cy. parva, the other four species were all isolated from both soils and plant tissues. Cylindrocladiella peruviana, and Cy. parva were isolated from relatively large number of plant species in different regions. Cylindrocladiella peruviana was isolated from the tissues of eight plant species (residing in eight families) in eight different countries, and Cy. parva from the tissues of six plant species (six families) in six countries (Table S2). Other than plant tissues, Cy. peruviana was also isolated from soils in six countries (Table S2). Vitis vinifera and species of Eucalyptus are the plants from which the greatest number Cylindrocladiella species have been isolated. Those isolated from V. vinifera include Cy. lageniformis, Cy. parva, Cy. peruviana, Cy. pseudoparva, Cy. viticola, and Cy. vitis, and those from Eucalyptus species include Cy. camelliae, Cy. lageniformis, Cy. lanceolata, and Cy. pseudohawaiiensis (Table S2). These Cylindrocladiella species were mainly isolated from V. vinifera roots, and from Eucalyptus cuttings in nurseries (Table S2).

DISCUSSION

A total of 407 Cylindrocladiella isolates were obtained from soils collected from natural forests and plantations in eight provinces of China. Multilocus phylogenetic inference and morphological characteristics led to the recognition of 30 species. These included Cy. brevistipitata, Cy. horticola, Cy. longiphialidica, Cy. longistipitata, Cy. obpyriformis, Cy. parvispora, Cy. peruviana and Cy. reginae, as well as 22 novel species that are described here.

Results of this study increase the global number of Cylindrocladiella spp. identified based on DNA sequence comparisons to 68. Including the results of this study, 35 Cylindrocladiella species are now recognised from China, of which seven were previously known (Tibpromma et al. 2018, Ye et al. 2021, Wang et al. 2023, Zhou et al. 2023, Liu & Chen 2024). With the exception of Cy. longistipitata and Cy. peruviana, which were known in China (Wang et al. 2023, Liu & Chen 2024), six previously described species found in this study are all new records for the country.

Consistent with the results in previous studies (Lombard et al. 2012, 2017, Pham et al. 2018, Liu & Chen 2024), sequence data for the tef1, tub2, his3, and ITS regions were used in this study to distinguish the new Cylindrocladiella species. These four regions have also been useful in studies identifying other genera in the Nectriaceae, e.g. Gliocladiopsis, Neonectria, and Dactylonectria (Gordillo & Decock 2019, Ye et al. 2021). The criteria based on phylogenetic analyses and sequence comparisons used in this and previous studies (Liu et al. 2020, 2024, Liu & Chen 2024) and provided a sound basis to define species boundaries in Cylindrocladiella.

Vesicle and conidial characteristics are considered as the most relevant morphological features to distinguish species of Cylindrocladiella, and this is also true for its allied genus Calonectria (Crous 2002, Lombard et al. 2012, 2017, Pham et al. 2018, Liu et al. 2020, 2026, Liu & Chen 2024). Results of this and previous studies have highlighted the fact that the morphology of vesicles, and conidia, while useful, overlap among species of Cylindrocladiella (Lombard et al. 2012, 2017, Liu & Chen 2024).

Phylogenetic analyses indicated that all Cylindrocladiella species can be defined in six species complexes, including the Cy. ledongensis complex defined in this study. Importantly, these species could not be distinguished based only on the morphology of their vesicles and conidia. While species in some complexes had common morphological characteristics, such as the vesicles of species in the Cy. ledongensis complex being consistently cylindrical, those in the Cy. parva species complex are consistently diverse in shape (Boesewinkel 1982, Lombard et al. 2012, 2017). Interestingly, the conidia of species in the Cy. ledongensis complex (average length close to or longer than 20 µm) are also longer than species in the other five species complexes.

An important part of this study was to consider the diversity and areas of occurrence of Cylindrocladiella species both in China and globally. The species collected in this study were from forest soils collected in different regions that have distinctly different climates. Analyses of the results showed clear evidence that the distribution of Cylindrocladiella species is influenced by climate. It was particularly interesting that species found in the warmer areas were never found in the temperate zones and vice versa, which could indicate that species of Cylindrocladiella have a particular preference or specialization for specific climate types, and that the geographic distribution of these species is affected by temperature and rainfall. The global distribution of Cylindrocladiella spp. observed in this study showed that the majority (83 %) of Cylindrocladiella species were isolated from soils, 26 % of the species from plant tissues, and 13 % were isolated from both soils and plant tissues. Results indicated that some apparently plant pathogenic species are widely distributed in different geographic locations, occurring in both soils and a number of diverse plant hosts. It is highly likely that additional species will be found in the future and as studies focus specifically on diseased roots of plants in different parts of the world.

Although this study focussed on isolations from soils, some Cylindrocladiella species have been isolated from diseased plant tissues (van Coller et al. 2005, Agustí-Brisach et al. 2012, Koike et al. 2016, Aiello et al. 2020, Ye et al. 2021, Akgül et al. 2022). These include various crop plants grown in China (Ye et al. 2021, Wang et al. 2023), Italy (Aiello et al. 2020), New Zealand (Jones et al. 2012), South Africa (van Coller et al. 2005), Spain (Agustí-Brisach et al. 2012, Capote et al. 2022), Turkey (Erper et al. 2013, Akgül et al. 2022), and the United States (Koike et al. 2016). Inoculation experiments have also shown that species such as Cy. lageniformis (Koike et al. 2016, Ye et al. 2021), Cy. parva (Agustí-Brisach et al. 2012, Erper et al. 2013), Cy. peruviana (Agustí-Brisach et al. 2012, Aiello et al. 2020, Akgül et al. 2022, Wang et al. 2023), and Cy. variabilis (Capote et al. 2022), were pathogenic when tested on seedlings or branches of the plants from which they had been isolated. It was thus of interest that the apparently pathogenic Cy. lageniformis and Cy. peruviana were found in this study or have been isolated previously in China (Ye et al. 2021, Wang et al. 2023).

Overall, the results of this study have shown that Cylindrocladiella species are widely distributed in Chinese forest soils and across a wide range of climates. The remarkably high number of new species found highlights the fact that these fungi have been poorly collected in the country and that many more species are likely to emerge as sampling is intensified. It is also likely that the same will be true for other areas of the world and particular those with tropical to subtropical climates and where soils have high levels of humidity. There is evidence to suggest that Cylindrocladiella species can be pathogens and future studies should consider the ability of those species found in this study to infect plants. But the question of pathogenicity of these fungi remains obscure and is an area of research that is likely to yield interesting and important results in the future.
