Commutator Estimates for Low-Temperature Fermi Gases
Jacky J. Chong, Laurent Lafleche, Jinyeop Lee, Chiara Saffirio

Abstract
. We investigate the semiclassical regularity of thermal equilibria in the presence of a harmonic potential at low temperature; that is, we obtain the asymptotic behavior of the Schatten norms of commutators of the one-body operators associated with these equilibria and the position and momentum operators. We also obtain upper bounds in the magnetic field case for the Fock–Darwin Hamiltonian. Our estimates, in particular, allow us to observe several regimes depending on the joint behavior of the Planck constant, the temperature, and the strength of the magnetic field.

1.
Introduction

A system of non-interacting fermions in thermal equilibrium is fully characterized by the temperature
T
, the chemical potential
\mu
, and the single-particle Hamiltonian
\mathsf{H}
. The Fermi–Dirac distribution

F_{\beta,\mu}(E)=(e^{\beta(E-\mu)}+1)^{-1},\qquad\beta=1/(k_{B}T)\,,

where we set the Boltzmann constant
k_{B}=1
, determines the occupation of each single-particle eigenstate of
\mathsf{H}
. Equivalently, the associated one-particle density operator is given by

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}=F_{\beta,\mu}(\mathsf{H})\,.

The zero-temperature limit of the Fermi–Dirac distribution is the indicator function

F_{\infty,\mu}(E):=\mathds{1}_{E\leq\mu}\,.

In this case, the associated one-particle density operator
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\infty,\mu}
is the projection onto the eigenspace associated with the non-positive eigenvalues of
\mathsf{H}-\mu
.

The aim of this paper is to investigate the semiclassical regularity of the operator
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}
, the size of the Schatten norms of its commutators with the position and momentum operators, for different regimes of the parameters
\beta>0
,
\mu
, and the Planck constant

h=2\pi\,\hbar\,.

To obtain sharp asymptotics in terms of all these parameters, we focus on the specific example of the harmonic oscillator

\mathsf{H}=-\hbar^{2}\Delta+\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}.

The Fermi–Dirac distribution also induces a classical function on phase space
\mathbb{R}^{2d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}_{x}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\xi}
defined by

f_{\beta,\mu}(x,\xi)=F_{\beta,\mu}(H(x,\xi))\,,

where

H(x,\xi)=\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}

denotes the classical Hamiltonian. Together, these objects determine all thermodynamic and statistical properties of the gas.

We also study the case where there is a magnetic field. In this case, one restricts to
d=3
and replaces
\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert^{2}
by
\left\lvert\xi-A\right\rvert^{2}
for the kinetic energy part of the classical Hamiltonian, where
A=A(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the vector potential; that is, the magnetic field is given by

B=\curl A\,.

We will restrict ourselves to the case of a constant, but possibly strong, magnetic field of the form
B=2\,b\,e_{3}
, with direction
e_{3}=(0,0,1)
and strength
2\,b
. The corresponding quantum Hamiltonian is given by

\mathsf{H}_{A}=\left\lvert i\hbar\nabla+A\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}.

1.1.
Notation

The distribution
f_{\beta,\mu}
is the classical physics analogue of the quantum state
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}
. It is well-known from the Weyl law (see e.g.
[
8
,
39
]
, or
[
37
]
for the optimal rate of convergence) that

h^{d}\tr\!\left({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\right)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{\beta,\mu}\quad\text{ when }\hbar\to 0

and this holds for very general Hamiltonian operators. More generally, to link the density operators of quantum mechanics and the phase-space distributions of classical statistical mechanics, one can introduce the Wigner transform, which associates to any Hilbert–Schmidt operator
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the function on the phase space

f_{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}(x,\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-i\,y\cdot\xi/\hbar}\,{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(x+\tfrac{y}{2},x-\tfrac{y}{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\,.

The phase-space Weyl law then tells more precisely that

f_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}}\to f_{\beta,\mu}\quad\text{ when }\hbar\to 0\,.

In this paper, we want to understand the quantum analogue of the regularity of phase space distributions, which can be measured in terms of Sobolev norms, i.e. in terms of Lebesgue norms of gradients. Observing that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}=h^{d}\tr\!\left({\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right)
and

(1)

\left\lVert f_{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}=\left(h^{d}\tr\!\left(\left\lvert{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\rvert^{2}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where the absolute value of an operator
A
is defined by
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=\sqrt{A^{*}A}
, it is convenient to introduce scaled Schatten norms

(2)

\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}=\left(h^{d}\tr\!\left(\left\lvert{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\rvert^{p}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

as the quantum analog of the classical phase space Lebesgue norms. On the other hand, the quantum analog of gradients in phase space can be defined as

(3)

\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}:=\left[{\nabla,{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right]\quad\text{ and }\quad\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}:=\left[{\tfrac{x}{i\hbar},{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right],

in accordance with the correspondence principle of quantum mechanics, which suggests to replace Poisson brackets by commutators of operators.
The quantum gradients defined in (
3
) are compatible with the Wigner transform in the sense that
f_{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}=\nabla_{x}f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
and
f_{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}=\nabla_{\xi}f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
. In the classical case, we denote by
z=(x,\xi)
the phase-space variable. Then
\nabla_{z}f=(\nabla_{x}f,\nabla_{\xi}f)
. Paralleling the classical case, we consider the vector-valued operator

\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!z}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}:=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu},\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\bigr)\,.

It satisfies
\lvert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!z}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\rvert^{2}=\lvert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\rvert^{2}
, and measures how far the state is from commuting with the position and momentum observables. With these definitions, in analogy with the classical Sobolev spaces, we can then introduce the homogeneous quantum Sobolev norms

(4)

\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\rVert_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{1,p}}:=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!z}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}.

They satisfy (see e.g.
[
27
, Proposition A.2]
)

(5)

\begin{split}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\rVert_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{1,p}}^{p}&\approx_{d,p}\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{d}\left(\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi_{\mathrm{j}}}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}^{p}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x_{\mathrm{j}}}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}^{p}\right),\\
\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\rVert_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{1,\infty}}&\approx_{d,p}\sup_{\mathrm{j}\in\{1,\dots,d\}}\bigl\{\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi_{\mathrm{j}}}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}},\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x_{\mathrm{j}}}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\bigr\}\,,\end{split}

where we adopt the notation
A\approx_{\lambda}B
if there exist constants
c_{\lambda},C_{\lambda}>0
, dependent on some parameter
\lambda
, such that
c_{\lambda}\,B\leq A\leq C_{\lambda}\,B
. They also satisfy the quantum analog of the Sobolev inequalities
[
27
]
.

The boundedness of the norms (
4
) independently of
\hbar
implies that the state represented by the operator
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
has a semiclassical structure in the following sense. Assume there exists
C>0
, independent of
\hbar
, such that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\leq C
. By Definition (
3
), we get
\left\lVert\left[{x,{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right]\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\leq C\,\hbar
, thus suggesting that, for
\hbar
small, the operator
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
almost commutes with the position operator
x
.

Generally, for
p\in[1,\infty)
, we refer to the understanding of the size of
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!z}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}
in terms of
\hbar
as the
semiclassical regularity
of the state
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
, and say that
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
is semiclassically regular if there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
\hbar
such that

(6)

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!z}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\leq C\,.

Since the Wigner transform is an isometry from
\mathcal{L}^{2}
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, it follows that
\left\lVert\nabla_{z}f_{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!z}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}
, hence a relation between the semiclassical regularity of
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
and the regularity properties of its Wigner transform
f_{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}
. While equilibrium states at positive temperature with regular potentials are semiclassically regular (see e.g.
[
12
]
), this is not the case at zero temperature, i.e.
\beta=\infty
, in which case Inequality (
6
) can only hold for
p=1
and the Wigner transform can only be in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
for
s<1/2
(see
[
28
]
). More precisely, for states of the form
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\mathds{1}_{-\hbar^{2}\Delta+V\leq 0}
with
V
slightly locally regular, confining and with a non-trivial negative part, the optimal semiclassical regularity estimates are of the form
[
18
,
10
]

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!z}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\leq C\,\hbar^{-1/p^{\prime}}\quad\text{ with }\quad\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1-\frac{1}{p}

the Hölder conjugate of
p
. The purpose of this work is to show that suitable asymptotic regimes involving
\hbar
and
\beta
may nevertheless give rise to nontrivial and interesting semiclassical regularity behavior.

1.2.
Main results

Our main result for the harmonic oscillator is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
F(r)=(1+e^{\beta(r-\mu)})^{-1}
and the corresponding one-particle density operator
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}:=F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2})
, with
\mathbf{z}=(x,\boldsymbol{p})
. Fix
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
p\in[1,\infty)
.
If
\hbar\in(0,1]
and
\beta\hbar\leq 1
, then

(7)

\big\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!z}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\approx_{d,p}\beta^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{d}{p}}\,e^{\beta(\mu-d\hbar)}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\mu<d\hbar\}}\\
+\beta^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{d}{p}}\left((\beta\left(\mu-d\hbar+\hbar\right))^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{d-1}{p}}+1\right)\mathds{1}_{\{\mu\geq d\hbar\}}\,.

If
\hbar\in(0,1]
and
\beta\hbar\geq 1
, then

(8)

\big\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!z}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\approx_{d,p}\hbar^{\frac{d}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\,e^{\beta(\mu-(d+2)\hbar)}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\mu<d\hbar\}}\\
+\left(\mu-d\hbar+\hbar\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{d-1}{p}}\hbar^{-1/p^{\prime}}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\mu\geq d\hbar\}}\,.

Remark 1.2
.

In particular, if
\mu=1
,
\hbar\leq 1
and the temperature is low, i.e.
T=1/\beta\to 0
, then there are two cases. If
\beta\hbar\geq 1
, i.e.
T\leq\hbar
, and then

\big\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!z}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\approx_{d,p}\hbar^{-1/p^{\prime}}

which is the same behavior as in the zero temperature case investigated in
[
18
,
2
,
28
,
10
]
. This is equivalent to

h^{d}\tr\!\left(\lvert[x,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}]\rvert^{p}+\lvert[\hbar\nabla,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}]\rvert^{p}\right)\approx_{d,p}\hbar\,.

When
\beta\hbar\leq 1
, i.e.
T\geq\hbar
, one obtains instead

\big\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!z}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\approx_{d,p}\left\langle\beta\right\rangle^{1/p^{\prime}},

where
\left\langle\beta\right\rangle:=\sqrt{1+\beta^{2}}
. That is, in this case, the size of the quantum gradients is comparable to that of the classical ones. This is equivalent to

h^{d}\tr\!\left(\lvert[x,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}]\rvert^{p}+\lvert[\hbar\nabla,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}]\rvert^{p}\right)\approx_{d,p}\left\langle\beta\right\rangle^{p-1}\hbar^{p}.

In particular, if
\beta\hbar\to 0
, that is if the temperature does not decay too fast to
0
in comparison to
\hbar
, then the size of these commutators is smaller than the zero temperature case, although
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}
converges to a zero temperature state.

Remark 1.3
.

We could treat the general case of fermions with spin by introducing a spin degeneracy factor. That is, we write

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}=g_{\textnormal{s}}\,F_{\beta,\mu}(\mathsf{H})\,,

where
g_{\textnormal{s}}=2
for spin-
\frac{1}{2}
particles (e.g., electrons). Since this factor does not affect the essential analysis, we set
g_{\textnormal{s}}=1
in what follows.

Our main result for the harmonic oscillator with magnetic field reads as follows. Here we use the notation
A\lesssim B
to indicate that there exists a universal constant
C>0
independent of the parameters such that
A\leq B
. We will also later write
A\lesssim_{\lambda}B
if the constant depends on the parameter
\lambda
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
F(r)=(1+e^{\beta(r-\mu)})^{-1}
with
\beta,\mu>0
and the corresponding 3 dimensional one-particle density operator
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}:=F(\mathsf{H}_{A})
, with
\mathsf{H}_{A}=\left\lvert i\hbar\nabla+A\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}
and the vector potential
A=b\left(-x_{2},x_{1},0\right)
for some
b\in\mathbb{R}
. Fix
\mu\geq\left(2\left\langle b\right\rangle+1\right)\hbar
, then, for any
p\in[1,\infty]
, we have the estimates

\displaystyle\big\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}+\left\langle b\right\rangle\big\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\langle b\right\rangle\left\langle\beta\mu\right\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2}{p}}\beta^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{p}}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\beta\hbar\left\langle b\right\rangle\leq 1

\displaystyle\lesssim M_{p}(\beta\hbar,b)\,\mu^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2}{p}}\,\hbar^{-1/p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\beta\hbar\left\langle b\right\rangle\geq 1

with

\displaystyle M_{p}(\beta\hbar,b)

\displaystyle=\left\langle b\right\rangle^{\frac{1}{p}}+(\beta\hbar)^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\left\langle b\right\rangle^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\left\langle b\right\rangle^{-1}\leq\beta\hbar\leq 1

\displaystyle=\beta\hbar+\left\langle b\right\rangle^{\frac{1}{p}}+(\beta\hbar\left\langle b\right\rangle)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\text{ if }1\leq\beta\hbar\leq\left\langle b\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\beta\hbar\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\left\langle b\right\rangle^{\max(\frac{1}{p},\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\left\langle b\right\rangle\leq\beta\hbar\,.

Remark 1.5
.

The result seems to be sharp when
\beta\hbar\left\langle b\right\rangle\lesssim 1
, since one recovers the estimate from the classical case (Proposition
3.1
) and from the non-magnetic case (Theorem
1.1
) when
b\leq 1
. This condition can be seen as a generalization of the condition
T\geq\hbar
from the case without magnetic field, and can be interpreted as the fact that
T
should be larger than the gap between eigenvalues. In the case with magnetic field, as the proof shows, the Hamiltonian is indeed the sum of three oscillators, one of which has eigenvalues spacing of
\left\langle b\right\rangle\hbar
. In all the cases, we obtain that the quantum norms are bounded by the classical ones in the sense that

\big\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}+\left\langle b\right\rangle\big\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\lesssim\left\langle b\right\rangle\left\langle\beta\mu\right\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2}{p}}\beta^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{p}}.

Remark 1.6
.

In the large magnetic field regime regime
b\hbar\to 1
considered for instance in
[
33
,
34
,
36
]
, if
\mu=1
and
\beta\geq 1

\big\lVert[\boldsymbol{p},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}]\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\lesssim\beta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\qquad\big\lVert[x,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}]\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\lesssim\beta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\hbar\,,

so the commutators with
x
are small, but not the commutators with
\boldsymbol{p}
.

Remark 1.7
.

The hypothesis
\left(2\left\langle b\right\rangle+1\right)\hbar\leq\mu
in Theorem
1.4
is imposed to simplify the exposition and is equivalent to telling that
\mathsf{H}_{A}-\mu
has negative eigenvalues. In the other cases, the commutators will be much smaller.

In the zero temperature case, we have the following result, which coincides with taking
\beta\hbar=1
in the previous theorem.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{A}=\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{H}_{A}\leq\mu}
with
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. Then for any
p\in[1,\infty]

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{A}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2}{p}}\left\langle b\right\rangle^{\max(\frac{1}{p},\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})-1}\hbar^{-1/p^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{A}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2}{p}}\left\langle b\right\rangle^{\max(\frac{1}{p},\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}\hbar^{-1/p^{\prime}}.

where
\mu\geq\left(2\left\langle b\right\rangle+1\right)\hbar
, or else
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{A}=0
.

Remark 1.9
.

If
\mu=1
and
b
satisfies
b\hbar\leq 1
, then the above theorem gives in terms of commutators

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left[{x,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{A}}\right]\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\langle b\right\rangle^{\max(\frac{1}{p},\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})-1}\hbar^{1/p},

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left[{\boldsymbol{p},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{A}}\right]\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\langle b\right\rangle^{\max(\frac{1}{p},\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}\hbar^{1/p}.

Similar estimates were recently obtained in
[
3
, Theorem 1]
for the case of the trace norm for more general external and vector potentials; more precisely, they obtained the following, for
b\hbar\leq 1

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left[{x,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{A}}\right]\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\langle b\right\rangle\hbar

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left[{\boldsymbol{p}-A,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{A}}\right]\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\langle b\right\rangle^{2}\hbar\,.

Hence, in the case of constant magnetic field with a harmonic trapping potential considered in this work, Theorem
1.8
improves on the
b
-dependency, as it gives

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left[{x,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{A}}\right]\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\hbar

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left[{\boldsymbol{p}-A,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{A}}\right]\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\langle b\right\rangle\hbar\,.

1.3.
Motivation and known results

The semiclassical regularity discussed above, for which bounds are established in Theorem
1.1
, has recently played a central role in several areas of mathematics, ranging from the analysis of many-body quantum systems to semiclassical analysis and Weyl-type laws. In particular, in
[
7
]
the mean-field approximation of the fermionic many-body evolution via the Hartree–Fock equation was established for pure states, i.e., for states whose one-particle density matrix
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
is a rank-one projection, corresponding to the zero-temperature regime
\beta=\infty
. This result was later extended in
[
4
,
13
]
to mixed states associated with finite positive temperature
\beta>0
.
In both
[
7
,
4
]
, the initial data for the Hartree–Fock equation were assumed to satisfy the semiclassical condition (
6
) with
p=1
, and for all
p\in[1,\infty]
in
[
13
]
. This approach was subsequently generalized to the pseudo-relativistic Hartree–Fock equation in
[
6
]
, and to the Pauli–Fierz Hamiltonian approximated by the Maxwell–Schrödinger equation in
[
31
]
, where the semiclassical structure of the initial state again constituted a crucial assumption.

In
[
3
]
, the authors derive the Hartree–Fock equation with a constant magnetic field. The key ingredient in their proof is the semiclassical structure and its dependence on the strength of the magnetic field (see Remark
1.9
). We note that
the semiclassical structure also plays an important role in
[
36
]
, in which, motivated by the study of the quantum hall effect, a strong magnetic field is considered. For this reason, we allow the strength of the magnetic field to be large.

More recently, the works
[
19
,
20
]
addressed the approximation of the many-body fermionic Schrödinger evolution with relativistic dispersion by the semi-relativistic Hartree–Fock equation for extended gases. In this framework, a local semiclassical structure was introduced. In particular, the proof in
[
20
]
crucially relies on the approximation of pure states by mixed states in the limit
\beta\to\infty
. This motivates our study of different asymptotic regimes in
\beta
and
\hbar
, with the aim of quantifying the loss of regularity or of semiclassical structure of mixed states as they approach pure states.

The above-mentioned semiclassical regularity has also played an important role in the semiclassical analysis of the dynamics of mean-field equations of Hartree type. See, for instance
[
5
]
where the Hartree–Fock equation is approximated by the Vlasov equation,
[
32
]
where the Maxwell–Schrödinger equation is approximated by the Vlasov–Maxwell system,
[
14
]
where the Bogoliubov–de Gennes equations is approximated by a system of coupled Vlasov equations. Moreover, semiclassical regularity also gives a control on the size of quantum Wasserstein pseudo-metrics
[
23
,
26
]
, which are a useful tool in proving the joint mean-field and semiclassical limits
[
21
,
22
,
25
,
11
]
.

Another application is the study of the semiclassical limit of the equilibria themselves, and in particular, at zero temperature, the Weyl law and determinantal processes. In
[
16
,
10
,
9
]
, semiclassical regularity estimates are used to obtain quantitative rates of convergence of spectral functions in the semiclassical limit.

All the above mentioned topics motivate the study of Schatten norms of commutators of equilibria. The study of the zero temperature case has been carried out in
[
18
,
2
,
28
,
10
]
, and, for constant magnetic fields, in
[
3
]
. We believe that our work will help in understanding the optimal size of the commutators in all these regimes.

1.4.
Structure of the paper

The article is organized as follows:
In Section
2
, we investigate the semiclassical regularity of the harmonic oscillator without a magnetic field.
We begin in Section
2.1
by analyzing the classical mechanical problem, to be compared with the quantum analog considered in Section
2.2
.

In Section
3
, we consider the magnetic harmonic oscillator.
Section
3.1
derives both classical upper and lower bounds for the phase space gradients in the presence of a uniform magnetic field.
In Section
3.2
, we introduce the algebraic framework for the quantum harmonic oscillator with a magnetic field. Finally, Section
3.3
is devoted to the upper bound for the magnetic commutators.

2.
Size of Commutators: Harmonic Oscillator

For
\beta>0
, we define the phase space distribution

(9)

f_{\beta,\mu}(z)=F(\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2})\quad\text{ with }\quad F(r)=(1+e^{\beta\left(r-\mu\right)})^{-1},

where
z=(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is the phase space variable.

2.1.
Classical case

In this section, we look at the classical situation, that is, the case of the gradient of the classical phase space distribution. This will serve as a guide for the quantum case.

Lemma 2.1
.

The position density
n_{\beta,\mu}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{\beta,\mu}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi
satisfies

n_{\beta,\mu}(x)\approx_{d}\left[\left((\mu-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}}+\beta^{-\frac{d}{2}}\right)\mathds{1}_{\{\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}\leq\mu\}}+\beta^{-\frac{d}{2}}\,e^{-\beta(\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}-\mu)}\mathds{1}_{\{\mu<\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}\}}\right],

and the total mass is given by

(10)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{\beta,\mu}\approx_{d}\left[\left(\mu^{d}+\beta^{-d}\right)\mathds{1}_{\{\mu\geq 0\}}+\beta^{-d}\,e^{\beta\mu}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\mu<0\}}\right].

In particular, the total mass is finite and bounded uniformly in
\beta
for
\beta\geq 1
.

Proof.

A change of variables yields

\displaystyle n_{\beta,\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{t^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{1+e^{t-\beta\left(\mu-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}\right)}}=:\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\cF_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Bigl(\beta\,\bigl(\mu-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}\bigr)\Bigr),

where
\cF_{\alpha}(\nu)
is the Fermi–Dirac integral of order
\alpha
. Similarly,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{\beta,\mu}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}n_{\beta,\mu}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta}\right)^{d}\cF_{d-1}(\beta\mu)\,.

Using the standard pointwise estimates for the Fermi–Dirac integral (see, e.g.,
[
24
, Lemma 18]
), we obtain the desired result.
∎

Observe that

F^{\prime}(r)=\frac{-\beta\,e^{\beta\left(r-\mu\right)}}{\left(1+e^{\beta\left(r-\mu\right)}\right)^{2}}=-\beta\,F(r)\left(1-F(r)\right).

By a straightforward computation, the
L^{p}
norms are given by

(11)

\left\lVert\nabla_{z}f_{\beta,\mu}\right\rVert_{L^{p}}^{p}=\frac{2^{p}\pi^{d}\beta^{p/2-d}}{\Gamma(d)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{d+p/2-1}\frac{e^{p(t-\beta\mu)}}{(1+e^{t-\beta\mu})^{2p}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

and

(12)

\left\lVert f_{\beta,\mu}\left(1-f_{\beta,\mu}\right)\right\rVert_{L^{p}}^{p}=\frac{\pi^{d}}{\Gamma(d)\,\beta^{d}}\int_{0}^{\;\infty}t^{d-1}\frac{e^{p(t-\beta\mu)}}{(1+e^{t-\beta\mu})^{2p}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,.

Hence, it suffices to study integrals of the form

I_{p,c}(\nu):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(c)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{c-1}\frac{e^{p(t-\nu)}}{(1+e^{t-\nu})^{2p}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,,

where
p,c\geq 1
and
\nu=\beta\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. In fact, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
p,c\geq 1
,

I_{p,c}(\nu)\approx_{p,c}\left\langle\nu\right\rangle^{c-1}\mathds{1}_{\{\nu\geq 0\}}+e^{p\nu}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\nu<0\}}\,.

This gives the following behavior for Lebesgue norms of the gradient of
f_{\beta,\mu}
and the difference between the function and its square.

Proposition 2.3
.

For all
\beta>0
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\left\lVert\nabla_{z}f_{\beta,\mu}\right\rVert_{L^{p}}\approx_{d,p}\beta^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{d}{p}}\Bigl(\left\langle\beta\mu\right\rangle^{\frac{d-1}{p}+\frac{1}{2}}\mathds{1}_{\{\mu\geq 0\}}+e^{\beta\mu}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\mu<0\}}\Bigr)

and

\left\lVert f_{\beta,\mu}\left(1-f_{\beta,\mu}\right)\right\rVert_{L^{p}}\approx_{d,p}\beta^{-\frac{d}{p}}\Bigl(\left\langle\beta\mu\right\rangle^{\frac{d-1}{p}}\mathds{1}_{\{\mu\geq 0\}}+e^{\beta\mu}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\mu<0\}}\Bigr)\,.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

First, consider the case
\nu\leq 0
. Since
e^{t-\nu}<1+e^{t-\nu}\leq 2\,e^{t-\nu}
, we have

I\geq\frac{e^{p\nu}}{2^{2p}\Gamma(c)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{c-1}e^{-pt}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=2^{-2p}\,p^{-c}\,e^{p\nu}\quad\text{and}\quad I\leq p^{-c}\,e^{p\nu}\,.

Thus,
I\approx_{p,c}e^{p\nu}
for all
\nu\leq 0
. For
\nu>0
, we use the inequality

\frac{1}{4}\,e^{-\left\lvert s-\nu\right\rvert}\leq\frac{e^{s-\nu}}{(1+e^{s-\nu})^{2}}\leq e^{-\left\lvert s-\nu\right\rvert}\,,

which yields the bound

I\approx_{p}\frac{1}{\Gamma(c)}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{c-1}e^{-p\left\lvert s-\nu\right\rvert}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{\Gamma(c)}\int_{-\nu}^{\infty}(t+\nu)^{c-1}e^{-p\left\lvert t\right\rvert}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,,

after the change of variable
t=s-\nu
. Using the inequality
(a+b)^{c}\leq 2^{c}\left(a^{c}+b^{c}\right)
for
a,b,c>0
, we obtain

\frac{1}{\Gamma(c)}\int_{-\nu}^{\infty}(t+\nu)^{c-1}e^{-p\left\lvert t\right\rvert}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{2}{\Gamma(c)}\int_{0}^{\infty}(t+\nu)^{c-1}e^{-pt}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
\leq\frac{2^{c}}{\Gamma(c)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(t^{c-1}+\nu^{c-1}\bigr)e^{-pt}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=2^{c}\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{c}}+\frac{\nu^{c-1}}{p\Gamma(c)}\biggr).

Conversely, using
(a+b)^{c}\geq 2^{-1}(a^{c}+b^{c})
, we have

\frac{1}{\Gamma(c)}\int_{-\nu}^{\infty}(t+\nu)^{c-1}e^{-p\left\lvert t\right\rvert}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\geq\frac{1}{\Gamma(c)}\int_{0}^{\infty}(t+\nu)^{c-1}e^{-pt}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
\geq\frac{1}{2\Gamma(c)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(t^{c-1}+\nu^{c-1}\bigr)e^{-pt}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{c}}+\frac{\nu^{c-1}}{p\Gamma(c)}\biggr).

Combining these bounds gives the desired result.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

From Proposition
2.3
, one can recover the norms of the gradients with respect to
x
or
\xi
. Indeed, for instance, the gradient with respect to
x
, by polar decomposition, writing
z=r\,u=r\left(u_{x},u_{\xi}\right)
with
u\in\mathds{S}^{2d-1}
, it holds

\left\lVert\nabla_{x}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}}^{p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\lvert 2\,x\,F^{\prime}(\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2})\rvert^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathds{S}^{2d-1}}\left\lvert 2\,u_{x}\right\rvert^{p}\lvert F^{\prime}(r^{2})\rvert^{p}\,r^{2d+p-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\,.

One can compute the integral on the sphere by using that it is the derivative of the integral on the ball of size
R
at
R=1
, that is

\int_{\mathds{S}^{2d-1}}\left\lvert u_{x}\right\rvert^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=J^{\prime}(1)\quad\text{ with }\quad J(R)=\int_{B_{R}}\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\,.

The integral of
J(R)
can be computed using the Fubini theorem to integrate in
\xi
before integrating in
x
, a polar change of variable and a formula for the Beta function, giving

J(R)=\frac{\omega_{d}}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{p}\left(R^{2}-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}\right)_{+}^{d/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\omega_{d}\,\omega_{2d+p}}{\omega_{d+p}}\frac{R^{2d+p}}{2d+p}

where
\omega_{d}=\frac{2\,\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}
is the measure of
\mathds{S}^{d-1}
when
d
is an integer. This yields finally

(13)

\left\lVert\nabla_{\xi}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}}=\left\lVert\nabla_{x}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}}=C_{d,p}\left\lVert\nabla_{z}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}}

with
C_{d,p}^{p}=\frac{\omega_{d}\,\omega_{2d+p}}{\omega_{2d}\,\omega_{d+p}}
.

2.2.
Quantum case

We now proceed to the quantum case, for which the quantity
\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2}
is replaced by the harmonic oscillator. More precisely, let
\mathbf{z}=(x,\boldsymbol{p})
where
\boldsymbol{p}=-i\hbar\nabla
, then the harmonic oscillator is given by

\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}=-\hbar^{2}\Delta+\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert\boldsymbol{p}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}.

The associated creation and annihilation operators are given by

a=x+i\,\boldsymbol{p}\quad\text{ and }\quad a^{*}=x-i\,\boldsymbol{p}\,.

They satisfy
\left[{a,a^{*}}\right]=a\cdot a^{*}-a^{*}\cdot a=2d\hbar
and
\left\lvert a\right\rvert^{2}:=a^{*}\cdot a=\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}-d\hbar
, that is

\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert a\right\rvert^{2}+d\hbar\,.

With this, it is a standard result that the eigenvalues of
\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}
are of the form
\lambda_{n}=(2n+d)\hbar
with multiplicity

(14)

N_{n}:=\bigl|\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{d}=n\}\bigr|=\binom{n+d-1}{d-1}.

We consider
F(r)=(1+e^{\beta(r-\mu)})^{-1}
and the corresponding one-particle density operator

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}:=F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2})\,.

Its quantum Sobolev norm satisfies

\big\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!z}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\approx_{p}\big\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}+\big\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\approx_{p}\frac{1}{\hbar}\big\lVert[a,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}]\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\,,

which follows from Formula (
5
), the triangle inequality for Schatten norms, and the identities

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i\hbar}\left[{a,A}\right]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}A-\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}A\,,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i\hbar}\left[{a^{\ast},A}\right]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}A+\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}A\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}A

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i\hbar}\left[{a-a^{\ast},A}\right],

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}A

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i\hbar}\left[{a+a^{\ast},A}\right],

and the fact that
\left[{a^{*},A}\right]=-\left[{a,A}\right]^{*}
provided
A
is self-adjoint.

Lemma 2.5
.

The Schatten norm of the commutator of
a
and the density operator
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}
satisfies

S_{p}^{p}:=\frac{1}{\hbar^{p}}\left\lVert\left[{a,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}}\right]\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}^{p}=\frac{h^{d}}{\hbar^{p}}\tr\!\left(\left\lvert F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2})-F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}-2\hbar)\right\rvert^{p}\left(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}-d\hbar\right)^{p/2}\right).

Expressing
\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}
in the eigenfunction basis, this yields

(15)

S_{p}^{p}=\,\frac{2\pi}{\hbar^{p-1}}\sum_{n\geq 0}N_{n}\,h^{d-1}\left\lvert F(\lambda_{n})-F(\lambda_{n-1})\right\rvert^{p}\left(\lambda_{n}-d\hbar\right)^{\frac{p}{2}},

where we define
\lambda_{-1}=\left(d-2\right)\hbar
.

Proof.

The creation operator satisfies

(16)

\left[{a,\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}}\right]=2\hbar\,a\,.

By functional calculus and Equation (
16
), this implies

a\,F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2})=F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}+2\hbar)\,a\quad\text{ and }\quad F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2})\,a=a\,F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}-2\hbar)\,,

where the second identity follows by replacing
F(y)
with
F(y-2\hbar)
. Using these identities yields

\left[{a,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}}\right]=\left[{a,F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2})}\right]=a\left(F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2})-F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}-2\hbar)\right).

Then, applying the commutation relation of
a
, we get

\left\lvert\left[{a,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}}\right]\right\rvert^{2}=\left(\overline{F}(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2})-\overline{F}(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}-2\hbar)\right)\left(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}-d\hbar\right)\left(F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2})-F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}-2\hbar)\right)

from which we deduce that

\left\lvert\left[{a,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}}\right]\right\rvert=\left\lvert F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2})-F(\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}-2\hbar)\right\rvert\sqrt{\left\lvert\mathbf{z}\right\rvert^{2}-d\hbar}\,.

The result follows by taking the trace of the power
p
of this identity.
∎

To estimate the right-hand side of Identity (
15
), let us state the following lemma which is a discrete analog of Lemma
2.2
. Again, one can think of
\nu
as representing the value of
\beta\,\mu
, or more precisely
\nu=\beta\left(\mu-d\hbar\right)\in\mathbb{R}
. On the other hand, there is a new semiclassical parameter
\eta=\beta\,\hbar>0
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Fix
p,c\geq 1
and
M>0
, and define

S_{p,c}(\eta,\nu):=\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\frac{e^{p\left(\eta n-\nu\right)}}{\left(1+e^{\eta n-\nu}\right)^{p}\left(1+e^{\eta\left(n-1\right)-\nu}\right)^{p}}\left(\eta n\right)^{c-1}\eta\,.

Then, if the parameter
\eta>0
satisfies
\eta\leq M
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

S_{p,c}(\eta,\nu)\approx_{p,c,M}\bigl(1+\nu^{c-1}+\eta^{c-1}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\{\nu\geq 0\}}+e^{p\nu}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\nu<0\}}\,.

In particular, we recover the classical result when
\eta=\beta\,\hbar
is sufficiently small.

Proof.

In the case
\nu<0
, we use
e^{\eta n-\nu}<1+e^{\eta n-\nu}\leq 2\,e^{\eta n-\nu}
, which yields

S_{p,c}(\eta,\nu)\approx_{p}\eta^{c}\operatorname{Li}_{-c+1}(e^{-\eta p})\,e^{p\nu},

where
\operatorname{Li}_{s}(z)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}z^{n}\,n^{-s}
is the polylogarithm of order
s
provided
\left\lvert z\right\rvert<1
. Hence, it suffices to consider
\eta^{c}\operatorname{Li}_{-c+1}(e^{-\eta p})
.

The case
c=1
reduces to studying the geometric series, which yields

\eta\operatorname{Li}_{0}(e^{-\eta p})=\frac{1}{p}\frac{\eta p}{e^{\eta p}-1}\approx_{p,M}1\,.

Hence, it suffices to consider
c>1
. Applying the Abel–Plana formula (see, e.g.,
[
35
, Section 8.3]
), we get

\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}e^{-\eta pn}\,n^{c-1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\eta px}\,x^{c-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+2\int^{\infty}_{0}x^{c-1}\frac{\sin\Bigl(\eta px-\frac{\pi(c-1)}{2}\Bigr)}{e^{2\pi x}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,.

This yields

\eta^{c}\operatorname{Li}_{-c+1}(e^{-\eta p})=\frac{\Gamma(c)}{p^{c}}+2\int^{\infty}_{0}t^{c-1}\frac{\cos(p\,t-\frac{\pi c}{2})}{e^{2\pi t/\eta}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,.

Then it follows that

(17)

\left\lvert\eta^{c}\operatorname{Li}_{-c+1}(e^{-\eta p})-\frac{\Gamma(c)}{p^{c}}\right\rvert\leq 2\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{t^{c-1}}{e^{2\pi t/\eta}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\zeta(c)\Gamma(c)}{2^{c-1}\pi^{c}}\,\eta^{c}.

In the case
\nu>0
, we split the sum into three parts:

(18)

S_{p,c}=\Biggl(\sum_{n:\,n\leq\left\lfloor{\nu/\eta}\right\rfloor}+\sum_{n:\,n>\lceil\nu/\eta\rceil}+\sum_{\lfloor\nu/\eta\rfloor<n\leq\lceil\nu/\eta\rceil}\Biggr)(\cdots)=:\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}+\Sigma_{3}\,.

Since
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
can be treated similarly, we shall focus on
\Sigma_{2}
. Again, note that

\Sigma_{2}\approx_{p}\eta^{c}\sum_{n:\,n>n_{0}}e^{-(\eta n-\nu)p}\,n^{c-1},

where
n_{0}:=\lceil\nu/\eta\rceil
. Rewriting the sum yields

\Sigma_{2}\approx_{p}\eta^{c}e^{-\delta p}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-\eta pk}\left(n_{0}+k\right)^{c-1},

where
\delta:=\eta\,n_{0}-\nu\in[0,\eta)
.

When
c=1
, we again have a geometric series, which yields

\Sigma_{2}\approx_{p}\frac{e^{-\delta p}}{p}\,\frac{\eta p}{e^{\eta p}-1}\approx_{p,M}1\,.

In the case
c>1
, we apply the Abel–Plana formula to get

(19)

\eta^{c}\,e^{-\delta p}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-\eta pk}\left(n_{0}+k\right)^{c-1}=e^{p\nu}\,\frac{\Gamma(c,\eta\,p\left(n_{0}+1\right))}{p^{c}}\\
+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\eta^{c}\,e^{-(\delta+\eta)p}\left(n_{0}+1\right)^{c-1}\\
+2\,e^{-\eta p}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\bigl(\eta^{2}\left(n_{0}+1\right)^{2}+t^{2}\bigr)^{(c-1)/2}\sin\!\left(p\,t-\left(c-1\right)\arctan\frac{t}{\nu+\delta+\eta}\right)}{e^{2\pi t/\eta}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,.

Note that we have the estimate

\frac{e^{p\nu}}{p^{c}}\left\lvert\Gamma(c,p\,\nu)-\Gamma(c,p\left(\nu+\delta+\eta\right))\right\rvert=\,\int^{\delta+\eta}_{0}(\nu+t)^{c-1}e^{-pt}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\lesssim\eta\,\nu^{c-1}+\eta^{c},

which means

e^{p\nu}\,\frac{\Gamma(c,\eta\,p\left(n_{0}+1\right))}{p^{c}}=\frac{e^{p\nu}}{p^{c}}\,\Gamma(c,p\,\nu)+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\eta\,\nu^{c-1}+\eta^{c}\bigr)\,.

To estimate the last term of Equation (
19
), we split the integral as
J_{1}+J_{2}:=\bigl(\int_{t<\eta}+\int_{t>\eta}\bigr)(\cdots)
. For the second integral, we have the bound

J_{2}\lesssim e^{-\eta p}\int^{\infty}_{\eta}\frac{\big[(\nu+\delta+\eta)^{2}+t^{2}\big]^{(c-1)/2}}{e^{2\pi t/\eta}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
\lesssim e^{-\eta p}\left(\eta\,\nu^{c-1}\int^{\infty}_{1}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}{e^{2\pi u}-1}+\eta^{c}\int^{\infty}_{1}\frac{u^{c-1}}{e^{2\pi u}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\right)\lesssim e^{-\eta p}\,\bigl(\eta\,\nu^{c-1}+\eta^{c}\bigr)\,.

Lastly, for the
J_{1}
term, we have

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-\eta p}\,\nu^{c-1}\int^{\eta}_{0}\frac{\left\lvert\sin\!\left(p\,t-\left(c-1\right)\arctan\frac{t}{\nu+\delta+\eta}\right)\right\rvert}{e^{2\pi t/\eta}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-\eta p}\,\nu^{c-1}\int^{\eta}_{0}\frac{\left\lvert p\,t-\left(c-1\right)\arctan\frac{t}{\nu+\delta+\eta}\right\rvert}{e^{2\pi t/\eta}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-\eta p}\,\nu^{c-1}\left(p+\frac{c-1}{\eta}\right)\int^{\eta}_{0}\frac{t}{e^{2\pi t/\eta}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\lesssim_{M,c}\eta\,\nu^{c-1}e^{-\eta p}.

For the interface term
\Sigma_{3}
, we have

(20)

\Sigma_{3}=\frac{e^{\delta p}}{\left(1+e^{\delta}\right)^{p}\left(1+e^{\delta-\eta}\right)^{p}}\left(\nu+\delta\right)^{c-1}\eta\,.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Let us also state the following lemma for
\eta=\beta\hbar
large, which will be useful for the case of very small temperatures.

Lemma 2.7
.

Fix
p\geq 1
and
c\geq 1
. Suppose the parameters
\eta\geq 1
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy
\eta\gg\left\lvert\nu\right\rvert
, then we have

S_{p,c}(\eta,\nu)\approx_{p,c}\eta^{c}\,e^{-(\eta-\nu)p}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\nu<0\}}+\eta^{c}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\nu\geq 0\}}\,.

If the parameters satisfy
1\leq\eta\leq\left\lvert\nu\right\rvert
, then we have

S_{p,c}(\eta,\nu)\approx_{p,c}\eta^{c}\,e^{-(\eta-\nu)p}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\nu<0\}}+\eta\,\nu^{c-1}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\nu\geq 0\}}\,.

Proof.

Suppose
\nu<0
and consider
c>1
(we leave the case
c=1
to the reader), we have

S\approx_{p}\eta^{c}\operatorname{Li}_{-c+1}(e^{-\eta p})\,e^{p\nu}\,,

where
\operatorname{Li}_{-c+1}(e^{-\eta p})\approx_{c}e^{-\eta p}
for
\eta\geq 1
.
Indeed, since we have that

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}e^{-\eta pn}n^{c-1}=

\displaystyle\,e^{-\eta pn}\left(1+\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}e^{-\eta pn}(n+1)^{c-1}\right)

\displaystyle\approx_{c}

\displaystyle\,e^{-\eta pn}\left(1+\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}e^{-\eta pn}n^{c-1}+\frac{e^{-\eta p}}{1-e^{-\eta p}}\right)

which yields

\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}e^{-\eta pn}n^{c-1}\approx_{c}\frac{e^{-\eta p}}{1-e^{-\eta p}}\left(1+\frac{e^{-\eta p}}{1-e^{-\eta p}}\right)\approx e^{-\eta p}.

The argument is independent of the size of
\left\lvert\nu\right\rvert
.

Next, assume
\nu>0
. In the case
\eta\gg\nu
, i.e.,
\lceil\nu/\eta\rceil=1
, then we have that

S\approx_{p}\eta^{c}+\eta^{c}\,e^{-p(\eta-\nu)}\sum^{\infty}_{n=2}e^{-\eta p(n-2)}n^{c-1}\approx_{p,c}\eta^{c}\,.

Now, consider the case
\eta\leq\nu
. We split the sum as in (
18
). In the case of the interface term
\Sigma_{3}
, we see from Equation (
20
) that

\Sigma_{3}\approx_{p}\left(\nu+\delta\right)^{c-1}\eta\,.

For the term
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
, we will handle them as above. It suffices to consider
\Sigma_{2}
. Notice, we have

\Sigma_{2}\approx_{p}\eta^{c}\,e^{-\delta p}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-\eta pk}\left(n_{0}+k\right)^{c-1}\approx_{p}e^{-\left(\eta+\delta\right)p}\left(\nu+\delta\right)^{c-1}\eta\,,

which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Combining all the previous lemmas, we now have all the tools to prove the main result of this section.

Proposition 2.8
.

Fix
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
p\geq 1
. Assuming
\hbar\in(0,1]
and
\beta\hbar\leq 1
,
then we have

(21)

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!z}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,\mu}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\approx_{d,p}\beta^{-\frac{d}{p}+\frac{1}{2}}\,e^{\beta(\mu-d\hbar)}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\mu<d\hbar\}}\\
+\Bigl(\left(\mu-(d-1)\hbar\right)^{\frac{d-1}{p}+\frac{1}{2}}\beta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}+1\Bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\{\mu\geq d\hbar\}}\,.

Proof.

Note that from Equation (
14
) one can deduce, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the following

\frac{(n+1)^{d-1}}{\left(d-1\right)!}\leq N_{n}\leq\frac{(n+\frac{d}{2})^{d-1}}{\left(d-1\right)!}\,,

which can be further rewritten in terms of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}=\left(2n+d\right)\hbar
as

(22)

\omega_{2d}\left(\lambda_{n}-(d-2)\hbar\right)^{d-1}\leq 2\pi\,N_{n}\,h^{d-1}\leq\omega_{2d}\,\lambda_{n}^{d-1}.

where
\omega_{d}=\bigl|\mathds{S}^{d-1}\bigr|
. Consequently, we obtain from Identity (
15
) and (
22
) the lower bound

\displaystyle S_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\omega_{2d}}{\hbar^{p-1}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\left\lvert F(\lambda_{n})-F(\lambda_{n}-2\hbar)\right\rvert^{p}\left(\lambda_{n}-d\hbar\right)^{d+\frac{p}{2}-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\ +\biggl(\frac{1-e^{-2\beta\hbar}}{\beta\hbar}\biggr)^{p}\beta^{-d+\frac{p}{2}}\,e^{p\beta(\mu-d\hbar)}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\mu<d\hbar\}}\,.

Similarly, for the upper bound, we have that

\displaystyle S_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d}\frac{\omega_{2d}}{\hbar^{p-1}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\left\lvert F(\lambda_{n})-F(\lambda_{n}-2\hbar)\right\rvert^{p}\left(\lambda_{n}-d\hbar\right)^{d+\frac{p}{2}-1}

\displaystyle\qquad+\hbar^{d-1}\left(\frac{1-e^{-2\beta\hbar}}{\beta\hbar}\right)^{p}\beta^{-1+\frac{p}{2}}\left(\left(\beta\left(\mu-d\hbar\right)\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}+(\beta\hbar)^{\frac{p}{2}}+1\right)\mathds{1}_{\{\mu\geq d\hbar\}}\,.

This completes the proof of the proposition.

3.
Size of Commutators: with Magnetic Field

3.1.
Classical case

Consider the classical magnetic harmonic Hamiltonian

(24)

H_{A}(x,\xi):=\left\lvert\xi-A\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2},

where
A=A(x)=(A_{1}(x),\ldots,A_{d}(x))
is a spatially-dependent vector potential. The magnetic field

B=\curl A

is not uniform but independent of time. When
A=0
, we recover
H=H_{0}=\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2}
.

Let
f_{\beta,\mu}^{A}(z)=F(H_{A})
and
f_{\beta,\mu}(z)=F(H)
as above with
z=(x,\xi)
. Then by a simple change of variable

(25)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{\beta,\mu}^{A}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{\beta,\mu}\,,

which we already estimated in Equation (
10
). Similarly a simple change of variable together with the fact that
\nabla_{\xi}f_{\beta,\mu}=2\,\xi\,F^{\prime}(\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2})
gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left\lvert\nabla_{\xi}f_{\beta,\mu}^{A}\right\rvert^{p}=2^{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert^{p}\left\lvert F^{\prime}(\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2})\right\rvert^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left\lvert\nabla_{\xi}f_{\beta,\mu}\right\rvert^{p}

and by Equation (
13
),

\left\lVert\nabla_{\xi}f_{\beta,\mu}\right\rVert_{L^{p}}=C_{d,p}\left\lVert\nabla_{z}f_{\beta,\mu}\right\rVert_{L^{p}},

which we estimated in Proposition
2.3
. On the other hand,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left\lvert\nabla_{x}f_{\beta,\mu}^{A}\right\rvert^{p}=2^{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left\lvert x-\nabla A(x)\xi\right\rvert^{p}\left\lvert F^{\prime}(\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2})\right\rvert^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\,.

This implies that

(26)

\left\lVert\nabla_{x}f_{\beta,\mu}^{A}\right\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq\left\lVert\left\langle\nabla A\right\rangle\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert\nabla_{z}f_{\beta,\mu}\right\rVert_{L^{p}},

where
\left\langle x\right\rangle=\sqrt{1+\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}}
are the Japanese brackets.

One can be more precise in the case of a uniform three-dimensional magnetic field,
B=(0,0,2b)
with
b\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. One can take

(27)

A=\tfrac{1}{2}\,B\times x=b\left(-x_{2},x_{1},0\right)

to get the following estimates, which show that the previous inequality (
26
) is sharp.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
A
be given by the above formula (
27
) in dimension
d=3
. Then for any
p\in[1,\infty]
and
\beta>0
, it holds

\left\lVert\nabla_{x}f_{\beta,\mu}^{A}\right\rVert_{L^{p}}\approx_{p}\left\langle b\right\rangle\left\lVert\nabla_{z}f_{\beta,\mu}\right\rVert_{L^{p}}

and in the particular case
p=2
,

\left\lVert\nabla_{x}f_{\beta,\mu}^{A}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\tfrac{3+2\,b^{2}}{6}\left\lVert\nabla_{z}f_{\beta,\mu}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Remark 3.2
.

For the size of
\left\lVert\nabla_{z}f_{\beta,\mu}\right\rVert_{L^{p}}
, recall Proposition
2.3
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Since, by definition of
A
, it holds

\nabla A=b\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\oplus 0\,,

one deduces that

x-\nabla A(x)\xi=x+b\,\xi_{12}^{\perp}\,,

with
\xi_{12}^{\perp}=(-\xi_{2},\xi_{1},0)
. Therefore, by a polar change of coordinates and since
\nabla_{z}f_{\beta,\mu}=2\,z\,F^{\prime}(\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2})
, one obtains

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left\lvert\nabla_{x}f_{\beta,\mu}^{A}\right\rvert^{p}=2^{p}\,I_{p}(b)\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2d-1+p}\left\lvert F^{\prime}(r^{2})\right\rvert^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=\frac{I_{p}(b)}{\omega_{2d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert\nabla_{z}f_{\beta,\mu}\right\rvert^{p}

with
\omega_{2d}=\lvert\mathds{S}^{2d-1}\rvert
and

I_{p}(b):=\int_{\mathds{S}^{2d-1}}\left\lvert x+b\,\xi^{\perp}_{12}\right\rvert^{p}\sigma(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi)\,,

where
\sigma
is the Lebesgue measure on the sphere
\mathds{S}^{2d-1}
. When
p=2
, expanding the square and using that integrals of odd functions on the sphere vanish, one obtains directly by symmetry between all the coordinates

I_{2}(b)=\int_{\mathds{S}^{2d-1}}\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}+b^{2}\left\lvert\xi_{12}\right\rvert^{2}\sigma(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi)=\tfrac{3+2\,b^{2}}{6}\,\omega_{2d}\,.

When
p\neq 2
, by diagonalizing the symmetric matrix associated with the quadratic form, there exist orthogonal coordinates
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{6})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
such that

\lvert x+b\,\xi^{\perp}_{12}\rvert^{2}=(b^{2}+1)\,(y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2})+y_{3}^{2}\,,

which, since
(a+b)^{c}\approx_{c}a^{c}+b^{c}
, implies

\displaystyle I_{p}(b)

\displaystyle=\,\int_{\mathbb{S}^{5}}((b^{2}+1)\,(y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2})+y_{3}^{2})^{p/2}\sigma(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\approx_{p}\,(b^{2}+1)^{p/2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{5}}\left(y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{p/2}\sigma(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)+\int_{\mathds{S}^{5}}\left\lvert y_{3}\right\rvert^{p}\sigma(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)\,,

that is, we have

I_{p}(b)\approx_{p}(b^{2}+1)^{p/2}=\left\langle b\right\rangle^{p}\,.

This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

3.2.
Quantum case

The magnetic harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian in 3D (also called the Fock–Darwin Hamiltonian
[
17
,
15
]
, and studied by Landau in the case without confining potential
[
29
,
30
]
) is the Hamiltonian

\mathsf{H}_{A}=\left\lvert{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2},

with unit mass and charge,
where
{\boldsymbol{v}}=({\boldsymbol{v}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{3})
is the velocity or kinematic momentum, defined by

{\boldsymbol{v}}:=\boldsymbol{p}-A\quad\text{ with }\quad\boldsymbol{p}=-i\hbar\nabla

and
\left\lvert{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rvert^{2}
is the magnetic Laplacian.

We restrict ourselves to the case of the uniform magnetic field
B=2\,b\,e_{3}
, with the vector potential
A=\tfrac{1}{2}\,B\times x
. Here,
b
is the magnetic field strength.
In this case, while one still gets
\left[{x,{\boldsymbol{v}}}\right]=\left[{x,\boldsymbol{p}}\right]=i\hbar\,\mathrm{Id}
, we observe that

\left[{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2}}\right]=2i\,b\hbar,\qquad\left[{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{3}}\right]=\left[{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{3}}\right]=0\,.

To find the spectrum, we separate the transverse directions
x_{12}=(x_{1},x_{2})
from the longitudinal direction
x_{3}
, writing
x=(x_{12},x_{3})
. The Hamiltonian then decouples into commuting observables

\mathsf{H}_{A}=\left\lvert{\boldsymbol{v}}_{12}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert x_{12}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert\boldsymbol{p}_{3}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert x_{3}\right\rvert^{2}=:\mathsf{H}_{\perp}+\mathsf{H}_{\parallel}\,.

The longitudinal part
\mathsf{H}_{\parallel}
is a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator, while the transverse part
\mathsf{H}_{\perp}
is treated using complex coordinates.

To handle
\mathsf{H}_{\perp}
, we further decouple the operator into two commuting observables

\mathsf{H}_{\perp}=\left\lvert\boldsymbol{p}_{12}\right\rvert^{2}+(b^{2}+1)\left\lvert x_{12}\right\rvert^{2}+2\,b\left(x_{1}\,\boldsymbol{p}_{2}-x_{2}\,\boldsymbol{p}_{1}\right)=\mathsf{H}_{\perp,\,\textnormal{osc}}+2\,b\,\mathsf{L}_{\parallel}\,,

that is, a two-dimensional magnetic oscillator
\mathsf{H}_{\perp,\,\textnormal{osc}}
and the third component of the angular momentum
\mathsf{L}_{\parallel}
. Then, following the standard approach (see, e.g.
[
1
,
38
]
) we replace
x_{12}
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{12}
by the operators
w:=x_{1}+i\,x_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{w}:=\boldsymbol{p}_{1}-i\,\boldsymbol{p}_{2}
which are such that
\left\lvert w\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert x_{12}\right\rvert^{2}
and
\left\lvert\boldsymbol{p}_{w}\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert\boldsymbol{p}_{12}\right\rvert^{2}
.

We define the scaled cyclotron and guiding center operators

a_{1}=\left\langle b\right\rangle w+i\,\boldsymbol{p}_{w}^{*}\quad\text{ and }\quad a_{2}=\left\langle b\right\rangle w^{*}+i\,\boldsymbol{p}_{w}\,.

It can be readily checked that these operators satisfy the commutation relations

(28)

\left[{a_{1},a_{1}^{*}}\right]=\left[{a_{2},a_{2}^{*}}\right]=4\left\langle b\right\rangle\hbar\quad\text{ and }\quad\left[{a_{1},a_{2}}\right]=\left[{a_{1},a_{2}^{*}}\right]=0\,.

They also satisfy

\left\lvert a_{1}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert a_{2}\right\rvert^{2}=2\left\langle b\right\rangle^{2}\left\lvert x_{12}\right\rvert^{2}+2\left\lvert\boldsymbol{p}_{12}\right\rvert^{2}-4\left\langle b\right\rangle\hbar\quad\text{ and }\quad\left\lvert a_{2}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert a_{1}\right\rvert^{2}=4\left\langle b\right\rangle\mathsf{L}_{\parallel}\,.

Finally, defining
a_{3}=x_{3}+i\,\boldsymbol{p}_{3}
, the annihilation operator associated to the longitudinal Hamiltonian, the full Hamiltonian can be written as a convex combination of harmonic oscillators

\mathsf{H}_{A}=\frac{1}{2\left\langle b\right\rangle}\left(\left(\left\langle b\right\rangle-b\right)\left\lvert a_{1}\right\rvert^{2}+\left(\left\langle b\right\rangle+b\right)\left\lvert a_{2}\right\rvert^{2}\right)+\left\lvert a_{3}\right\rvert^{2}+\left(2\left\langle b\right\rangle+1\right)\hbar\,.

Note that
[a_{1},a^{\sharp}_{3}]=[a_{2},a^{\sharp}_{3}]=0
where
a^{\sharp}
denotes either the raising or lowering operator. Furthermore, in terms of its lowest eigenvalue
\Lambda_{0}
and the effective angular frequency
\Omega
, defined by

\Lambda_{0}:=\left(2\left\langle b\right\rangle+1\right)\hbar\quad\text{ and }\quad\Omega:=b/\langle b\rangle\in(0,1)\,,

the Hamiltonian can be written

\mathsf{H}_{A}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\left(1-\Omega\right)\left\lvert a_{1}\right\rvert^{2}+\left(1+\Omega\right)\left\lvert a_{2}\right\rvert^{2}\right)+\left\lvert a_{3}\right\rvert^{2}+\Lambda_{0}\,.

From the commutation relation, the eigenvalues of
\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert^{2}
are of the form
\alpha_{j}\,n
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with

(29)

\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=4\left\langle b\right\rangle\hbar\quad\text{ and }\quad\alpha_{3}=\hbar\,.

Therefore, the eigenvalues of
\mathsf{H}_{A}
are

(30)

\Lambda_{n}=\lambda_{1}n_{1}+\lambda_{2}n_{2}+\lambda_{3}n_{3}+\Lambda_{0}=\lambda\cdot n+\Lambda_{0}\,,

where
n=(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
and
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
given by

(31)

\lambda_{1}:=2\left(\left\langle b\right\rangle-b\right)\hbar,\quad\lambda_{2}:=2\left(\left\langle b\right\rangle+b\right)\hbar,\quad\lambda_{3}:=\hbar\,.

Moreover, for simplicity, we may choose
b
so that the eigenvalues have multiplicity one; for instance, taking
b=q\sqrt{2}
with
q\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
suffices.

3.3.
Upper Bounds for the Size of Commutators

As in the case without a magnetic field, we estimate the quantum gradients by first estimating the commutators with the creation and annihilation operators.

In the direction of the magnetic field, in which the Hamiltonian is just a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator, this allows use to use the usual relations
x_{3}=\frac{a_{3}+a_{3}^{*}}{2}
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{3}=\frac{a_{3}-a_{3}^{*}}{2i}
. To express
x_{12}
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{12}
in terms of the creation and annihilation operators defined above, observe that

(32)

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{1}+a_{1}^{*}+a_{2}+a_{2}^{*}}{4\left\langle b\right\rangle}\,,\qquad\qquad x_{2}=\frac{a_{1}-a_{1}^{*}+a_{2}^{*}-a_{2}}{4\left\langle b\right\rangle\,i}\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{1}-a_{1}^{*}+a_{2}-a_{2}^{*}}{4\,i}\,,\qquad\qquad\boldsymbol{p}_{2}=\frac{-a_{1}-a_{1}^{*}+a_{2}+a_{2}^{*}}{4}\,.

Moreover, since
{\boldsymbol{v}}=\boldsymbol{p}-A
with
A=b\left(-x_{2},x_{1},0\right)
, we can also recover
{\boldsymbol{v}}
from the formulas

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\,i}\Big(\left(1-\Omega\right)\left(a_{1}-a_{1}^{*}\right)+\left(1+\Omega\right)\left(a_{2}-a_{2}^{*}\right)\Big)\,,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big(\left(1+\Omega\right)\left(a_{2}+a_{2}^{*}\right)-\left(1-\Omega\right)\left(a_{1}-a_{1}^{*}\right)\Big)\,.

From the commutation relations (
28
) of
\{a_{j}\}_{j=1,2,3}
, one deduces that for any bounded function
F
and any
j\in\set{1,2,3}

a_{j}\,F(\mathsf{H}_{A})=F(\mathsf{H}_{A}+\lambda_{j})\,a_{j}\,.

By the same argument as for the case without magnetic field in Lemma
2.5
, this yields

\big\lVert[a_{j},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\beta,A}]\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}^{p}=h^{d}\tr\!\left(\left\lvert F(\mathsf{H}_{A})-F(\mathsf{H}_{A}-\lambda_{j})\right\rvert^{p}\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert^{p}\right).
