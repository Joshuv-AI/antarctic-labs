Predicting the strength of waste aggregate concrete blocks using novel hybrid machine learning models and graphical user interface deployment
Authors: Md Arifuzzaman, S M Shishir Ahmed, Md Abdul Khaled, Sameen Yasar Siddiq, Prajnat Barua, Sourov Paul, Md Mahamudul Hasan, A K M Azad, Muhammad Ali Martuza, Ayed Eid Alluqmani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConcrete blocks made from waste aggregates have become a promising way to reduce waste and conserve natural resources while still offering good mechanical performance in both solid and hollow concrete blocks. This is especially relevant as more sustainable construction projects increasingly use recycled and alternative materials. This research develops a series of novel hybrid machine learning (ML) models to accurately predict compressive strength, using a dataset of 544 concrete samples from various sources. The six novel hybrid ML frameworks are designed as Hybrid Stacked Ensemble (HSE), Hybrid Residual Learning (HRL), Hybrid Weighted Ensemble (HWE), Hybrid Meta-Learning (HML), Hybrid Bayesian Stacking (HBS), and Hybrid Feature Fusion (HFF). Results show that novel Hybrid Bayesian Stacking (HBS) algorithms deliver excellent predictive accuracy across all evaluation metrics, with (R2 = 0.998, RMSE = 0.665) during training and (R2 = 0.987, RMSE = 1.836) during testing. Furthermore, Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analyses identified important input features and their effects on compressive strength. A graphical user interface (GUI) was developed to make predictive models accessible for practical engineeringKeywords: Concrete block, Waste aggregate, Hybrid Bayesian stacking ensemble, SHAP interpretability, Individual conditional expectation, Compressive strength prediction, Sustainable construction, GUI deploymentSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Concrete is one of the most extensively utilized construction materials worldwide, with global production exceeding 30 billion tons annually1. Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC), the main ingredient in concrete, is harmful to the environment due to its production. About 5 to 8 percent of the world’s CO2 emissions come from making cement. By 2060, production could reach 4 to 6 billion tons per year, which would make climate change even worse2–4. This damage to the environment has made people call for long-lasting options to making concrete, like using waste materials and extra cementitious materials (SCMs) instead of cement and aggregates in some cases5.

Recycled concrete aggregates (RCA) derived from construction and demolition waste have been widely studied for use in concrete. Recent studies have shown that the proper treatment and handling of RCA can reduce its water absorption to about 3.0%. This means that it can be used to replace up to 50% of natural aggregates without losing much strength6,7. But because mortar is stuck to RCA particles, they tend to be more porous and absorb more water than natural aggregates. This means that the mix design needs to be carefully optimized8. Rice husk ash (RHA) is made by burning agricultural waste under controlled conditions at temperatures between 550 and 700 °C. It has very pozzolanic properties and has shown promise in making concrete last longer and be stronger when used as a partial cement replacement at amounts of 10–30%9–11. In the same way, fly ash (FA) from thermal power plants has pozzolanic action that helps build strength over time and makes things last longer12. Studies have demonstrated optimal RHA replacement levels of 10–20% to maximize compressive strength, with higher replacement rates leading to reduced strength due to insufficient calcium hydroxide availability13.

Hollow concrete blocks (HCBs), which constitute a significant portion of masonry construction materials, offer substantial potential for incorporating waste materials. Some industry wastes, like fly ash, blast furnace slag, silica fume, and steel scrap, and some agricultural wastes, like rice husk ash, bagasse ash, and coconut shells, have been looked at as possible alternatives to cement and aggregates in the production of concrete14–18. The utilization of these waste materials not only addresses disposal challenges but also contributes to energy conservation, natural resource preservation, and reduced raw material costs19. Fly ash, characterized by spherical particles and pozzolanic properties, improves workability and long-term strength development when used at replacement levels of 20–30%20. Additionally, EMV-fabricated RAC improved compressive strength and elasticity, delayed damage progression, and showed distinct compressive and tensile size-effect behaviours21. Ground granulated blast furnace slag has latent hydraulic qualities and has been shown to make concrete last longer and be less likely to break down when exposed to sulfate22. Studies on hollow concrete blocks with 100% RCA replacement have demonstrated structural soundness and compliance with international standards when properly designed23.

Despite the demonstrated viability of incorporating waste materials into concrete, accurately predicting compressive strength remains a critical challenge24,25. Traditional empirical models, based primarily on the water-cement ratio and curing age, fail to adequately capture the complex interactions among multiple mixture components, particularly when dealing with heterogeneous waste materials26. The traditional way of checking strength involves curing for 28 days, then performing destructive tests. This takes a lot of time, work, and money. Because of these problems, it is hard for leftover materials to be widely used in concrete production. This is because engineers need accurate predictive tools to assess concrete quality and develop the best mix design27,28.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) methods have become strong alternatives to old empirical models for guessing the mechanical properties of concrete29–32. Studies have shown that different machine learning models used to predict the strength of concrete have coefficient of determination (R2) values higher than 0.9533,34. However, individual ML models possess inherent limitations: ANNs are susceptible to overfitting and require extensive hyperparameter tuning, decision tree-based models exhibit instability with data variations, and linear models cannot adequately represent complex nonlinear interactions35,36. To overcome the limitations of single models, ensemble learning approaches have gained prominence in recent literature37. Stacking ensemble learning, which uses a meta-learner to combine predictions from multiple base models in the best possible way, has shown strong promise for real-world applications. Using random forest, gradient boosting decision trees, XGBoost, and LightGBM as base learners and linear regression as the meta-learner38. Li and Song39 showed that the stacking ensemble model did a much better job of predicting the strength of rice husk ash concrete than individual methods. Similarly, research on ultra-high-performance concrete has shown that stacking regressors combining XGBoost, CatBoost, LightGBM, and Extra Trees can achieve R2 values exceeding 0.9840,41.

Building on ensemble learning foundations, hybrid machine learning approaches integrate multiple methodologies, including different algorithm families, feature engineering techniques, and optimization strategies, to create sophisticated predictive frameworks. Bayesian optimization combined with stacked ensemble learning has been shown to enhance model performance through systematic hyperparameter tuning significantly42,43. Meta-learning approaches, which learn from predictions of multiple diverse models, have demonstrated exceptional performance with R2 values exceeding 0.93 for structural engineering applications44. Residual learning frameworks, where a secondary model learns to predict the errors of a base model, offer advantages in scenarios with systematic prediction biases45. Feature fusion strategies that combine predictions from models trained on different feature representations have shown improved performance through multi-view learning46. Despite these advances, comprehensive investigations comparing multiple hybrid architectures specifically for hollow concrete blocks with waste aggregates remain limited in the literature.

Literature review

Over the past ten years, the use of machine learning methods to predict the compressive strength of concrete has grown by a significant amount. This is because computers have become faster, and more experimental data is available. In the beginning, artificial neural networks (ANNs) were mostly used because they could describe complicated, nonlinear relationships between input parameters and outputs47,48. Subsequent research by Ni et al.49 established that multilayer perceptron (MLP) architectures with properly configured hidden layers could achieve mean absolute percentage errors below 10% for diverse concrete mixtures.

Recently, tree-based ensemble methods have become more popular because they are reliable, easy to understand, and perform well across a wide range of real-world situations. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), which was created by Chen and Guestrin50, has proven to be very good at predicting the strength of concrete. Feng et al.51 showed that XGBoost could get R2 values higher than 0.96 for high-performance concrete while giving feature importance scores that were in line with principles of concrete science. Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) and Categorical Boosting (CatBoost) represent recent innovations that offer improved computational efficiency and native handling of categorical variables52,53. Comparative studies have shown that these gradient boosting variants consistently outperform traditional algorithms such as linear regression, decision trees, and support vector machines for concrete applications54,55.

Research by Zhang et al.56 established that RF models could effectively capture the complex interactions among mixture components, aggregates, and curing conditions, achieving prediction accuracies comparable to neural networks while significantly reducing training time. Support vector regression (SVR), while less commonly employed than tree-based methods, has shown competitive performance for datasets with moderate sample sizes, particularly when combined with appropriate kernel functions and hyperparameter optimization57,58. Li and Song39 created a stacking ensemble model for rice husk ash concrete that uses RF, GBDT, XGBoost, and LightGBM as base learners and linear regression as the meta-learner. This model performs much better than individual models. Similar success has been reported for ultra-high-performance concrete, where stacking ensembles combining XGBoost, CatBoost, LightGBM, and Extra Trees achieved R2 values exceeding 0.9859.

Bayesian optimization has become an effective method for systematic hyperparameter tuning in ensemble models60. Conventional hyperparameter optimization techniques, such as grid search and random search, become computationally impractical for ensemble models with numerous hyperparameters. Bayesian and PSO optimisation tackle this issue by developing a probabilistic surrogate model of the objective function and employing acquisition functions to direct the search towards favourable hyperparameter configurations61,62. Recent research has demonstrated that Bayesian-optimised stacking ensembles can achieve superior performance compared to manually tuned or grid-searched alternatives while requiring significantly fewer function evaluations63,64. Meta-learning approaches extend the ensemble learning concept by training models to learn from the learning process itself65. In the context of concrete strength prediction, meta-learning involves combining multiple base models trained on different feature representations or data subsets, with a meta-model learning to weight their contributions based on input characteristics optimally66. Research on steel fiber-reinforced concrete has shown that meta-learning architectures can achieve mean absolute errors below 0.4 MPa while providing enhanced generalization to unseen mixture designs67.

Residual learning frameworks, inspired by deep learning architectures such as ResNet, have been adapted for concrete strength prediction with promising results68,69. The core concept involves training a primary model to make initial predictions, followed by a secondary model that learns to predict the residuals (prediction errors) of the primary model70. The final prediction combines both models, effectively enabling progressive refinement of estimates. Feature fusion strategies represent another innovative hybrid approach that combines predictions from models trained on different feature representations71. The diverse perspectives captured by different feature representations can be combined through weighted averaging or meta-learning to produce more robust predictions72,73. Recent applications of SHAP to concrete strength prediction have demonstrated that ML models learn relationships consistent with established principles of concrete science74. Similarly, research on recycled aggregate concrete has used SHAP to confirm that the percentage of aggregate replacement exhibits nonlinear effects, with optimal ranges varying by waste material type75,76.

Research gaps

This research systematically addresses these gaps by (1) focusing specifically on hollow concrete blocks with comprehensive waste material incorporation; (2) developing and comparing six distinct novel hybrid ML architectures with complementary strengths; (3) utilizing a comprehensive dataset spanning multiple waste types including recycled aggregates, industrial by-products, and agricultural wastes; (4) integrating Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) and SHAP interpretability analysis throughout the modeling process; (5) employing rigorous validation protocols including k-fold cross-validation and comprehensive performance metrics; and (6) developing a practical GUI application for industrial deployment. The combination of advanced hybrid modeling, domain-specific focus, comprehensive interpretability analysis, and practical deployment tools positions this research to make significant contributions toward sustainable, data-driven concrete technology.

Research significance

The present research is crucial because it establishes a rigorous, data-driven system for accurately predicting the compressive strength of sustainable hollow and solid concrete blocks made from waste aggregates. This directly supports both the goals of structural safety and environmental protection. The tool uses standard mixture parameters, such as coarse aggregate (CA), fine aggregate (FA), water–cement ratio (W/C), binder content, and curing age, as input features. Additionally, the main methodological contribution is the development of six advanced novel hybrid machine learning frameworks that combine different base learners and meta-learners. These novel hybrid models perform very well, especially the Hybrid Bayesian Stacking (HBS) method, which achieves a coefficient of determination close to 1. This shows that advanced ensemble strategies can yield near-experimental accuracy when estimating compressive strength.

Beyond predictive accuracy, incorporating SHAP and ICE analyses provides crucial interpretability, enabling engineers to identify the most influential parameters, such as coarse aggregate (CA), fine aggregate (FA), and curing age, thereby facilitating knowledge-driven engineering decisions. Additionally, a graphical user interface (GUI) makes these models practically deployable, bridging the gap between advanced computational intelligence and day-to-day structural engineering practice. Furthermore, this research not only provides immediate practical benefits but also lays a strong foundation for future work on code development, reliability assessment, and the use of hybrid ML techniques for other performance indicators, such as the durability, shrinkage, or thermal properties of environmentally friendly concrete blocks.

Materials and methods

Database

The database used in this study was compiled from experimental results reported in 19 independent, peer-reviewed journal articles on hollow and solid concrete blocks containing waste materials as partial replacements for cement, fine aggregate, or coarse aggregate, published between 2009 and 2025, as listed in Appendix. In this study, a total of 544 data points were collected from experiments and existing studies to predict the compressive strength of HCB that uses waste materials as aggregates and binders. In these experiments, different mix designs have been created using various recyclable materials. Binder, fine aggregate, and coarse aggregate were replaced with 21 types of recyclable materials to conduct the experiments. The highest 93 of these data used rice husk ash as the replacement material. Banana leaf ash was used in 60% of the data, ranking as the second-most-substituted material. The main objective of this study is to identify the variables that have most strongly influenced the mechanical performance of these blocks.

Selection criteria

To ensure the consistency and reliability of the compiled database, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied during data collection. Only studies reporting complete mixture proportions and experimentally measured compressive strength values were included. The selected studies were required to have essential input variables, including recycled aggregate content, water-to-cement ratio, binder content, fine aggregate content, coarse aggregate content, and curing age. Studies with incomplete mix design information, missing compressive strength values, duplicate datasets, inconsistent units, or unclear testing procedures are excluded from the database.

All data collected from different studies have been standardized to consistent engineering units prior to model development. The compressive strength values are converted into MPa, while material quantities are converted into kg/m3. Duplicate records have been carefully checked and removed to avoid bias during model training. Data consistency and numerical ranges were also verified via statistical screening prior to preprocessing.

Compression testing standards and block specifications

The concrete blocks used in the database have been experimentally tested in the original studies using standard compressive strength testing procedures. Most of the selected experiments followed ASTM C140, ASTM C39, BS EN 772, or equivalent international standards for determining compressive strength. The database includes both hollow and solid concrete blocks with varying dimensions and geometries depending on the original experimental programs. In most studies, load application during compression testing was performed under monotonic axial loading conditions using calibrated compression testing machines.

Although minor variations existed among experimental studies in specimen dimensions and curing environments, only studies that followed acceptable standard testing methodologies have been incorporated into the database to maintain data consistency and reliability.

Concrete block

A robust mix of cement, sand, and coarse aggregates is used to make concrete blocks. The ratio of cement to sand to aggregate is usually 1:3:6 or 1:2:4, depending on the required block strength. The mix is designed to have a low water-to-cement ratio to ensure greater stability and longer-term durability.

Blocks are rectangular pieces of material with controlled dimensions. Its density can range from light to heavy depending on the construction purpose. Concrete blocks are more effective at retaining heat and sound than regular clay bricks, particularly because they are light or hollow. Additionally, they resist fire more effectively. Concrete blocks are significant because they speed up the construction process, reduce mortar joints, and produce masonry of the same quality. They are commonly used for walls that bear forces and those that don’t, as well as for partitions, boundary walls, and other masonry elements in housing and infrastructure projects.

Hollow concrete block

Hollow blocks use similar concrete mixes, but they are often made with lower-density aggregates or partial replacements to make them lighter and better at retaining heat. They are most often used as non-load-bearing structures, so their compressive strength is typically lower.

They have large voids inside, usually occupying more than 25% of the volume, which makes them lighter and better at insulating against sound and heat, but they are a little less durable than solid blocks. Bulk density can be either normal-weight or lightweight, making it easier to handle and reducing the dead load on structures. Hollow blocks are essential for quickly and cheaply building non-load-bearing and medium-load-bearing walls, infill walls in frames, and partitions. When additional structural capacity is needed, such as in reinforced masonry walls, columns, or shear walls, their cavities can be filled with grout or reinforced.

Solid concrete block

Solid blocks have a denser, often richer concrete mix that gives them higher compressive strengths. It’s usually left out with big holes, so the entire section adds to strength and mass per unit. Physically, solid, dense blocks are often used in pavements or structures that need to withstand high pressure. Their compressive strengths are usually higher than those of hollow concrete blocks. Because they are denser, they don’t insulate heat as well as lightweight or hollow blocks. Solid blocks are crucial in places where large loads must be supported and strength is required, such as foundations, retaining walls, basement walls, and heavily loaded bearing walls. They are also used for paving, columns, and other structural masonry applications where strength and impact resistance are more important than weight loss. Parametric study

The bivariate relationships between compressive strength and each input parameter are examined through scatter plots with linear best-fit trajectories, supplemented by corresponding frequency histograms and kernel density estimation (KDE) curves, as presented in Fig. 3. CA content exhibited the most pronounced positive association with compressive strength, with the scatter plot revealing a clear ascending trend across the CA range of 0–1470 kg/m3, corroborating the well-established role of skeletal aggregate in enhancing the load-bearing matrix of masonry blocks. Binder content similarly showed a discernible positive trend, with compressive strength values progressively exceeding 200 kg/m3. The corresponding bimodal KDE, exhibiting dual density peaks near 90 and 325 kg/m3, reflects the heterogeneous binder proportioning strategies documented in the consolidated literature. Curing age manifested a positive monotonic trend consistent with continued hydration-driven strength gain, with the histogram revealing a pronounced concentration of specimens at the standard testing ages of 7, 14, and 28 days, confirming the conventional curing protocols adopted by contributing studies. Conversely, the W/C ratio displayed a negative linear trajectory with high-strength specimens (> 50 MPa) clustered at W/C values between 0.40 and 0.45, and a bimodal frequency distribution peaking near 0.45 and 0.60, indicative of two distinct mix design philosophies within the database. FA content showed a modest inverse trend with compressive strength, with most high-FA specimens registering strengths below 10 MPa, whereas recycled aggregate exhibited a comparatively weak and diffuse negative association, attributable to the interfacial transition zone weakening commonly associated with elevated substitution levels.

Data preprocessing

The integrity and quality of input data fundamentally determine the reliability and accuracy of machine learning predictions. Raw experimental data from multiple research sources inevitably contain inconsistencies, missing values, and formatting irregularities that must be systematically addressed before model development. This study implements a comprehensive preprocessing pipeline to transform heterogeneous concrete mixture data into a standardized format suitable for hybrid ensemble learning algorithms. All preprocessing transformations are stored in serialized pipeline objects to ensure identical processing of new data during model deployment. This systematic preprocessing framework establishes a robust foundation for the subsequent development of hybrid ensemble models and ensures that machine learning algorithms receive clean, consistent, and properly scaled inputs, maximizing predictive accuracy while maintaining physical interpretability.

Hybrid stacked ensemble (HSE)

The Hybrid Stacked Ensemble represents a two-level hierarchical learning architecture that combines heterogeneous base learners through a meta-learning layer. The HSE architecture comprises three base learners selected for their algorithmic diversity and proven performance in regression tasks. Hybrid Bayesian stacking (HBS)

The Hybrid Bayesian Stacking architecture implements a probabilistic meta-learning framework that quantifies prediction uncertainty while combining base learner outputs. Unlike deterministic meta-learners (Ridge, ordinary least squares), Bayesian Ridge regression maintains probability distributions over meta-learner coefficients, providing principled uncertainty quantification for ensemble predictions. Hybrid feature fusion (HFF)

The Hybrid Feature Fusion architecture transforms the original feature space via dimensionality reduction before ensemble learning, yielding a richer representation that combines original features with principal components capturing dominant directions of variance. This approach addresses potential redundancy in the original feature set while preserving essential information through principled dimensionality reduction.

Feature fusion begins with standardization of all nine input features to zero mean and unit variance, ensuring that PCA identifies variance directions based on correlation structure rather than measurement scales. Model training and hyperparameter optimization

All six hybrid models were trained using a systematic protocol designed to maximize predictive accuracy, prevent overfitting, and ensure reproducibility. The training set was used exclusively for model fitting, the validation set for hyperparameter selection and early stopping, and the test set for final performance evaluation, as reported in the results.

Hyperparameter optimization employed grid search with fivefold cross-validation on the training set. For each hyperparameter configuration, the training set was divided into 5 folds, and models were trained on 4 folds while evaluating on the held-out fold. This process was repeated 5 times with different held-out folds, and the average validation performance was used to determine the score for each configuration. Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis

A model-interpretability method called SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) analysis describes the direction and magnitude of each input feature’s contribution to a machine learning prediction. Each feature is assigned a numerical value by SHAP that indicates how much it influences a prediction relative to a baseline, such as the model’s average prediction. When a feature has a positive SHAP value, the predicted output increases; when it has a negative value, the predicted output decreases.

Figure 11 illustrates the SHAP summary plot, which shows how each input feature affects the predicted compressive strength of all the concrete block samples. The horizontal line of each point indicates the SHAP value, which shows how much that feature changes the predicted strength relative to the baseline. Points on the right correspond to positive SHAP values, suggesting that specific feature values push the prediction towards higher compressive strength, whereas points on the left correspond to negative SHAP values, indicating a reduction in strength. The vertical ordering of features reflects their overall importance, with the most influential variables positioned at the top. The color gradient encodes the actual feature value for each instance, enabling simultaneous visualization of both directions of effect. Higher CA contents significantly increase predicted strength in this SHAP summary plot, while lower CA slightly decreases it. This is undoubtedly the most significant feature. At higher values, the effect of FA is weaker and generally positive. Strength can usually be improved by longer curing ages, although this effect is only moderate. Strength with negative SHAP values tends to decrease with a higher W/C, while it gradually rises with a lower W/C. Although it has a smaller effect than CA, a higher binder content certainly improves strength. Subsequently, in this dataset, predicted strength typically increases according to elevated RA content. Furthermore, this helps confirm that the learned relationships align with accepted concrete technology principles, such as the beneficial effects of proper binder and curing and the detrimental effects of excessive water content on compressive strength.

Individual conditional expectation (ICE) plot analysis

Heterogeneous feature effects on predicted compressive strength were visualized using ICE analysis, in which each blue curve shows the prediction trajectory of a single sample as a single feature is varied while all other features are held constant. The average effect (partial dependence) across all samples is shown by the red line, which demonstrates the various ways in which material parameters affect concrete strength. The content of FA varied greatly among samples and showed complex, non-monotonic behavior (Fig. 16a,b). Three distinct zones were identified: predictions remained stable with little variation (5–15 MPa) at low FA content (150–700 kg/m3); individual curves in a transition zone (700–900 kg/m3) showed dramatic divergence, with some samples reaching 90 MPa and others declining; and curves converged toward varied but stable strength levels beyond 900 kg/m3. Initial stability at 9.5 MPa, a spike to 15.5 MPa at about 750 kg/m3, a decline to 14 MPa at moderate levels, and a recovery to 15.5 MPa at maximum FA content were the characteristic patterns of the average effect. This variability shows that the effects of FA are highly context-dependent, varying with mixture proportions, and illustrates its various functions in microstructure development, workability control, and void filling.

ICE analysis results for the top three input features.

The most striking threshold behavior was shown by CA content (Fig. 16c,d). All samples showed consistently low strength (2–7 MPa) with horizontal ICE curves below 700 kg/m3. Nearly vertical transitions took place at 700–1000 kg/m3 as samples split into different strength classes (5–90 MPa). A systematic decrease in strength was noted above 1000 kg/m3. Conclusion

This study successfully developed and evaluated six novel hybrid machine learning frameworks for predicting the compressive strength of concrete blocks (HCB) incorporating waste aggregates. A total of six key input parameters were evaluated, with 544 data samples for compressive strength prediction, all sourced from previous studies. Hyperparameter optimization and rigorous cross-validation were conducted to ensure robust model performance. Eighty per cent of these resources were meant for training, whereas twenty per cent were meant for testing. Using advanced ensemble models, including Hybrid Stacked Ensemble (HSE), Hybrid Residual Learning (HRL), Hybrid Weighted Ensemble (HWE), Hybrid Meta-Learning (HML), Hybrid Bayesian Stacking (HBS), and Hybrid Feature Fusion (HFF). Each hybrid model combined multiple base learners (Random Forest, XGBoost, Artificial Neural Networks) with sophisticated meta-learning approaches. Among the models, the novel Hybrid Bayesian Stacking (HBS) and Hybrid Stacked Ensemble (HSE) achieved superior performance, demonstrating excellent generalization between training and testing phases, with R2 values exceeding 0.985.

Furthermore, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), ICE Plots, and a Graphical User Interface (GUI) were used to analyze the contributions and interactions of input materials to the prediction of compressive strength. Lastly, nine evaluation criteria were used to check how well the models worked and how accurate they were. Based on these results, subsequent findings can be derived from the current study.

From the comprehensive analysis, it is observed that concrete block compressive strength is significantly influenced by multiple variables, with CA (0.66) and binder content (0.39) demonstrating the strongest positive correlation. Conversely, FA and W/C have negative impacts, particularly in high-strength regions, while curing age predominantly contributes positively to sustainable concrete production when properly proportioned in mixture designs.

To further integrate these advanced prediction models into a practical engineering tool, a graphical user interface (GUI) was developed, allowing designers to estimate compressive strength openly, honestly, and accurately.

The successful integration of novel hybrid machine learning architectures with comprehensive interpretability analysis addresses a critical gap in traditional black-box prediction methods. The SHAP-based feature importance analysis not only validates model predictions against established concrete technology principles but also provides transparent decision-making support essential for regulatory compliance and engineering practice.

Limitations and future studies

The methodologies, tools, and insights presented in this study provide a robust framework for future research in computational materials science and offer practical instruments to accelerate the development and adoption of eco-friendly concrete technologies in the global construction industry, ultimately reducing environmental impact and enhancing resource efficiency in the built environment.

Moreover, this study achieved exceptional predictive accuracy and model interpretability, and several promising avenues for future research emerge. Expanding the dataset to include additional geographic regions, diverse waste-material sources, and varied environmental curing conditions would further enhance the model’s generalizability across global construction contexts. Integration of additional input features, such as aggregate particle morphology, chemical composition parameters, mineralogical characteristics, and microstructural properties, could provide even deeper insights into the mechanisms of strength development. Investigation of hybrid models to predict other critical concrete properties beyond compressive strength, including tensile and flexural strengths, durability indicators such as permeability and chloride resistance, and long-term performance metrics, would extend the utility of these methodologies. The GUI tool has not undergone formal user acceptance testing or integration with structural design workflows, limiting its utility to illustrative demonstrations. Development of multi-objective optimisation frameworks that simultaneously consider strength, cost, and environmental impact could provide holistic solutions for sustainable concrete design
