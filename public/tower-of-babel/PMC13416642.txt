Is the St. Gallen Algorithm for proximal humerus fractures associated with improved outcomes?
Authors: Angela M Moosmann, Andres Marin, Philippe Vial, Attila Güleryüz, Joseph M Schwab, Moritz Tannast
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProximal humerus fractures are the fourth most common type of fracture in elderly humans, seventh most common overall, and their incidence is rising. In 2019, the St. Gallen Algorithm (SGA) for treatment of proximal humerus fractures was proposed to improve treatment outcomes and reduce secondary surgeries. To date, no external validation of this algorithm has been reported.MethodsWe performed a retrospective validation study with patients who sustained an isolated proximal humerus fracture between January 2010 and December 2020 at our institution. We calculated the prevalence of patients following the SGA and compared failure rates and Subjective Shoulder Value scores between those following the SGA and not. We also evaluated the entire cohort for predictors of treatment failure.ResultsThe prevalence of patients receiving treatment based on the SGA at our institution was 43% (n = 316). The overall failure rate was 13% (94/730 patients) at a mean follow-up of 22 months. The failure rate was 11% (36/316 patients) for the SGA group and 14% (58/414 patients) for the non-SGA group (P value = .3). Subjective Shoulder Value scores were 89 ± 12 in the SGA group (n = 33) and 90 ± 13 in the non-SGA group (n = 52; P = .7). Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no difference in survivorship between following and not following the SGA. Univariate analysis identified algorithm pathway 10 and Neer fracture type 3 as risk factors for failure. Multivariable analysis suggested a potential association between adherence to the SGA and reduced failure risk; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the borderline statistical significance and limitations of the regression model.ConclusionFollowing the SGA produced similar results to not following the SGA in terms of failure rate, survivorship, and clinical outcome scores. However, multivariate analysis identifies following the SGA as protective against failure. The findings of this study provide supportive but not definitive evidence for the clinical utility of the SGA as a decision-making aid in the treatment of proximal humerus fractures.Keywords: Proximal humerus fracture, St. Gallen Algorithm, Deltoid Tuberosity Index, Hertel HGLS classification, Neer classification, Surgical treatment, Conservative treatment

Proximal humerus fractures are one of the most frequent fractures, especially in the elderly.2 As the worldwide population ages, their incidence will rise.1 Most fractures are minimally displaced and can be treated conservatively.3,4,6,10 Surgical treatment includes open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF; Fig. 1, A and B), hemiarthroplasty, and reverse shoulder arthroplasty (Fig. 1, C and D).5,8,9 Recent studies have not demonstrated a clear benefit of surgical treatment compared to a conservative approach.7

Surgical treatment modalities for a proximal humerus fracture. (A) ORIF with a one-third tubular plate. (B) ORIF with a PHILOS locking plate. (C) Hemiarthroplasty. (D) Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA). ORIF, open reduction and internal fixation.

The St. Gallen Algorithm (SGA) was introduced in 2019 to combine fracture characteristics, radiographic measurements, patient demographics, and activity level into the decision-making process for the most appropriate treatment. The algorithm is structured as a decision tree with 2 main pathways based on patient age (<65 years and ≥65 years; Fig. 2, A and B11). Within each pathway, patients are further stratified according to fracture morphology, bone quality, and displacement patterns, resulting in specific treatment recommendations ranging from conservative management to ORIF or arthroplasty.

The St. Gallen Algorithm.11 (A) Decision tree for patients under the age of 65 years. (B) Decision tree for patients equal or older the age of 65 years. Fracture characteristics as well as patient-centered aspects are used to allocate each patient to a specific subgroup from 1 to 17.

No independent validation has been reported for this treatment algorithm. At our hospital, the SGA was not actively implemented during the study period, and treatment decisions were made at the discretion of the treating surgeon. Consequently, any agreement between the actual treatment and the algorithm recommendation occurred unintentionally rather than as a result of deliberate adherence. This allows for a retrospective observational comparison but must be considered when interpreting the results.

One method to perform an external validation of the SGA is to compare the outcomes of patients at our institution whose treatment mirrored the SGA recommendation, with the outcomes of patients who did not.

Our study evaluates the original 2019 version of the SGA, as this was the only version available during the majority of the study period (2010–2020). A revised version of the algorithm was subsequently published following internal validation (Spross et al, The Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery 2021), and future external validation studies should assess this updated model.

Materials and methods

The present study is an institutional review board–approved (2023-00008), single-center, retrospective validation study. We reviewed all patients sustaining an isolated proximal humerus fracture who presented to our institution between January 2010 and December 2020. This resulted in 1,178 patients for further review (Fig. 3). We excluded patients who had an unhealed fracture with a follow-up of less than 12 months (n = 114), polytrauma (n = 228), insufficient/incomplete imaging (n = 32), patients younger than 18 years of age (n = 64), pathologic fractures (n = 6), and fractures initially presenting after more than 14 days (n = 4). After applying these criteria, 730 patients were available for complete analysis (Fig. 3).

Using our institutional database, one of the authors (A.M.M.) collected the following parameters: age, gender, and autonomy/activity level. Based on this data, the recommended SGA treatment pathway was identified and recorded for each patient. It is important to emphasize that this classification was performed retrospectively and that treatment decisions were not guided by the SGA. Therefore, cases classified as following the SGA reflect incidental agreement between surgeon-selected treatment and algorithm recommendation rather than intentional adherence.

Patients whose treatment agreed with the recommendation were allocated to the “SGA” group (n = 316), while those whose treatments differed were allocated to the “non-SGA” group (n = 414). Demographics for these 2 groups are shown in Table II. All patient records were further reviewed to determine if they required secondary unplanned surgery within 12 months after initiation of treatment which was defined as treatment failure. This definition was applied consistently for calculation of overall failure rates and for Kaplan–Meier survivorship analysis.

In addition to the binary classification (SGA vs. non-SGA), we performed a subgroup analysis to identify the specific algorithm pathways assigned to each patient and to characterize the type of deviation when treatment did not follow the recommended pathway. Deviations were categorized according to whether the actual treatment was more or less invasive than recommended (eg, ORIF instead of reverse shoulder arthroplasty, conservative treatment instead of surgery, or arthroplasty instead of fixation).

Once this patient list was identified, all patients with an existing Swiss address who were not listed as “deceased” in our electronic medical record were sent a SSV questionnaire via mail (n = 504). Of those, 82 (16%) complete questionnaires were returned.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables, normal distribution was tested with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. To determine differences in overall failure rates between the 2 groups, we performed a Chi-square test. The probability of secondary unplanned surgery within 12 months following initiation of treatment was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier survival method with a log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed to determine independent predictive factors for failure. Risk factors were age, gender, laterality, DTI, Neer classification, recommended algorithm pathway, actual treatment, and whether the algorithm was followed. All statistical analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Outcomes of treatments

The overall failure rate was 13% (94/730 patients) at a mean follow-up of 22 months. The failure rate for the SGA group was 11% (36/316 patients) and for the non-SGA group was 14% (58/414 patients; P value = .3) (Table III). Kaplan–Meier survivorship with unplanned secondary surgery as the end point showed a 12-month survival rate of 87% (95% confidence interval [CI] 82 – 91%) for the SGA group versus 85% (95% CI 81 – 89%) for the non-SGA group (P value = .2; Fig. 6). SSV scores were 89 ± 12 in the SGA group (n = 33) and 90 ± 13 in the non-SGA group (n = 52; P = .7). Of the 730 patients included in the final analysis, 504 were eligible for follow-up contact. A total of 82 completed questionnaires were returned, corresponding to a response rate of 16% among contacted patients and 11% relative to the entire study cohort. Given this low response rate, the SSV results must be interpreted with caution due to potential response bias and limited representativeness.

Predictive factors for outcome failure

Univariate Cox regression predictive factors for failure were algorithm pathway 10 (hazard ratio [HR] 11.1; 95% CI 1.00-124; P value = .050) and Neer fracture type 3 (HR 2.72; 95% CI 1.07-6.94; P value = .036) (Table IV). Multivariate Cox regression using age, DTI, gender, side, Neer class, SGA pathway, and group (SGA versus non-SGA) as the model factors identified being in the SGA group as an independent, protective factor against failure (HR 0.61; 95% CI 0.37-1.00; P value = .050) (Table V).

Summary

Although crude failure rates and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed no statistically significant difference between groups, multivariate Cox regression analysis—adjusting for age, fracture type, DTI, gender, side, and assigned pathway—identified adherence to the SGA as an independent protective factor against failure (HR 0.61; 95% CI 0.37–1.00; P = .050).

Discussion

The introduction of the SGA in 2019 aimed to establish a patient-centered approach to treatment of proximal humerus fractures. No external validation using an independent cohort has been published. Our study aimed to assess the validity of this algorithm in a high-volume trauma center. It is important to emphasize that this external validation was performed using the original 2019 version of the SGA. The algorithm was revised after internal validation and published in 2021. From a methodological perspective, external validation should first be conducted on the initially proposed model before evaluating revised versions that incorporate outcome-based refinements. Future investigations should therefore assess the performance of the updated algorithm in independent cohorts.

The prevalence of patients following the SGA recommendations at our institution was 43%. Most of these patients were treated prior to the publication of the SGA in 2019, which can partially explain our accordance with the SGA.

The additional subgroup analysis revealed that deviations from the algorithm were not random but occurred predominantly in specific clinical constellations. In elderly patients, surgeons occasionally preferred fixation over arthroplasty, whereas in selected younger patients with complex fracture configurations, arthroplasty was chosen despite algorithmic recommendation for reconstruction. These findings underline the influence of surgeon judgment and individual patient factors in real-world decision-making.

The failure rates as well as the Kaplan–Meier Survivorship are similar in the SGA-group versus the non-SGA group both in our study as well as in the original paper.11

Although multivariate analysis suggested a protective association of adherence to the SGA, the primary unadjusted outcomes—including failure rates, Kaplan–Meier survivorship, and SSV scores—did not show statistically significant differences between groups. Furthermore, the multivariable Cox model demonstrated wide CIs for several covariates, reflecting limited event numbers within specific algorithm pathways and potential model instability. Therefore, the observed association between SGA adherence and reduced failure risk should be interpreted with considerable caution and regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence of a protective effect.

Despite the overall failure rate, post-operative SSV shows an overall similar subjective satisfaction in SGA group versus the non-SGA group.

Limitations

Our study has limitations. The retrospective design of this study represents an inherent limitation, as treatment allocation was not randomized and may have been influenced by surgeon preference and patient-specific factors. While this reflects real-world clinical practice, it introduces potential confounding that cannot be fully controlled despite multivariate adjustment.

Consequently, the classification into SGA and non-SGA groups was performed retrospectively, and agreement with the algorithm occurred incidentally rather than intentionally. This distinction is essential, as the present study does not represent a prospective implementation of the algorithm but rather an observational comparison reflecting real-world practice.

To achieve a different outcome, further validation of the SGA using specific surgical techniques or specific treatment pathways should be used.

Another limitation of this study is that treatment failure was defined as unplanned secondary surgery within 12 months, representing a clear and objective end point but capturing only 1 aspect of clinical outcome. This definition does not account for functional limitations or patient satisfaction. To complement this, patient-reported outcomes were assessed using the SSV. However, the number of responses was limited (82/730; 11%), introducing a substantial risk of response bias and limiting the generalizability of these findings. Therefore, both the definition of failure and the interpretation of patient-reported outcomes should be considered with caution.

Reasons were address changes; no volunteer response or the patient has deceased. While multivariate analysis suggested a reduced relative risk of secondary surgery, the statistical significance was borderline and the regression model showed evidence of limited stability. Accordingly, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. A multicenter, prospective controlled study would provide additional robust analysis of the algorithm. Future studies should focus on optimizing the number of pathways in the algorithm, as well as reducing the rate of secondary surgeries.

Conclusion

In this retrospective external cohort, adherence to the original 2019 SGA resulted in comparable crude failure rates and clinical outcomes when compared to non–algorithm-based treatment. While multivariate analysis suggested a reduced relative risk of secondary surgery, this finding must be interpreted with caution. Overall, our results provide supportive but not definitive evidence for the clinical utility of the SGA. Prospective and multicenter studies are warranted to confirm these findings.
