Computerized CTG self-monitoring versus standard Doppler assessment in late-onset fetal growth restriction (cosmos): a pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Urszula Nowacka, Tadeusz Issat, Karolina Krajewska, Agnieszka Norwa, Natalia Mazanowska, Joanna Kowalczyk-Buss, Szymon Kozlowski, Marta Kuchcinska, Piotr Laudański, Artur Jakimiuk, Agata Majewska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeLate-onset fetal growth restriction (FGR) presents clinical management challenges, often requiring frequent in-hospital fetal surveillance. Telemedicine-based computerized cardiotocography (cCTG) performed at home may offer a viable remote monitoring addition.MethodsThis single-center, open-label, a pilot randomized controlled pragmatic trial (COSMOS) was conducted in Warsaw, Poland, between 2022 and 2025. A total of 120 women with late-onset FGR, defined according to the Delphi criteria, were randomly assigned (1:1) to either a cCTG-based monitoring approach with protocol-mandated safety Doppler assessments or to standard hospital-based Doppler surveillance. The primary outcome was neonatal condition at birth (Apgar score at 5 min, umbilical artery pH). Secondary outcomes included emergency cesarean section for fetal compromise, maternal anxiety, measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7), number of antenatal visits, and a composite of adverse neonatal outcomes. The study protocol was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05034861).ResultsThere were no significant differences between groups in Apgar scores, umbilical cord arterial pH, or need for neonatal resuscitation. The rate of emergency cesarean delivery was lower in the cCTG-based group. Women in the cCTG-based group also reported significantly lower anxiety levels at delivery and required fewer antenatal visits, with no increase in adverse neonatal outcomes.ConclusionsHome-based computerized CTG within a pragmatic real-world framework appears feasible in pregnancies complicated by late-onset FGR. These findings support the potential of this approach to reduce hospital-based monitoring, maternal anxiety and in-person visits; however, these results should be considered hypothesis-generating and not indicative of clinical effectiveness.Clinical trials registrationDate 13.08.2021 NCT Number NCT05034861Keywords: Fetal growth restriction, Cardiotocography, Pregnancy complications, Placental insufficiency, Anxiety disorders

What does this study add to the clinical work?

Home-based computerized CTG provides a safe addition to hospital Doppler surveillance in late-onset fetal growth restriction. It reduces maternal anxiety and clinic visits while maintaining neonatal safety, addressing the challenge of limited access to specialized perinatal care.

Introduction

Fetal growth restriction (FGR) is one of the most formidable challenges in antenatal care. Although multiple factors may impair fetal growth potential, uteroplacental insufficiency remains the leading cause of abnormal growth in otherwise healthy fetuses [1]. Both FGR and small for gestational age (SGA) significantly contribute to stillbirth in the late third trimester [2]. Growth restriction is defined as a condition in which a fetus is unable to reach its genetic growth potential, and despite established diagnostic criteria, it is best viewed as a continuum rather than rigid categories [3]. Postnatally, affected infants have increased risk of morbidity and unfavorable developmental outcomes [4]. There are two FGR types, differing in prevalence, pathophysiology and ultrasound patterns [1, 5]. Although early FGR (< 32 weeks) carries higher perinatal mortality, late-onset FGR (≥ 32 weeks) is more common and more difficult to detect [6, 7].

Early and late-onset fetal growth restriction is diagnosed based on criteria defined by the Delphi consensus [8, 9]. Surveillance tools include growth velocity, customized charts, Doppler velocimetry, biophysical profile, biomarkers, and cardiotocography (CTG), with short-term variation (STV) measured by computerized CTG (cCTG) [1]. Reduced STV reflects autonomic dysfunction linked to FGR-associated hypoxemia, offering an advantage over conventional CTG interpretation [10].

TRUFFLE (Trial of Randomized Umbilical and Fetal Flow in Europe) and GRIT (Growth Restriction Intervention Trial) studies were randomized controlled trials assessing the timing of delivery in early FGR [11–13]. TRUFFLE study demonstrated that co-monitoring with ductus venosus (DV) Doppler velocimetry and cCTG, combined with protocol-driven delivery, improves outcomes [14]. In contrast to early-onset, late-onset FGR is mildly related to placental lesions, and Doppler findings often fail to predict adverse outcomes [15]. The cerebroplacental ratio (CPR) may improve risk assessment, whereas the biophysical profile is not useful in late FGR [16–18]. Current monitoring prioritizes the middle cerebral artery pulsatility index (MCA-PI), umbilical artery pulsatility index (UA-PI) and their ratio[1]. To date, the only randomized interventional trial focused on FGR at or near term is the DIGITAT study (Disproportionate Intrauterine Growth Intervention Trial At Term) [19].

Management at our center follows the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) and national guidelines [1, 20]. Standard care for late-onset FGR with positive end-diastolic flow (EDF) includes fortnightly estimated fetal weight (EFW) assessment and at least weekly Doppler surveillance, with delivery at 37 weeks unless earlier intervention is indicated.

Given the time-consuming nature of frequent Doppler assessments, we hypothesized that a home-based cCTG monitoring approach may reduce hospital visits while maintaining effective fetal surveillance. This pragmatic study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of home-based cCTG in the management of late-onset FGR, as a complement to current standard care, with potential to inform future strategies aiming to reduce frequency of hospital-based Doppler assessments.

Methods

The COSMOS trial (COmputerized CTG Self-MOnitoring versus Standard Doppler assessment in Late-onset FGR) was a single-center, open-label, two-arm randomized controlled trial conducted between 2022 and 2025 at a tertiary perinatal care center in Warsaw, Poland. The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Institute of Mother and Child (approval no. 50/2021, issued on 24 June 2021. The study protocol followed SPIRIT guidelines and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05034861). All participants reviewed the study protocol and provided written informed consent prior to participation.

The inclusion criteria for the study were: age ≥ 18 years, singleton pregnancy, gestational age between 32 + 0 and 36 + 6 weeks of gestation, late-onset FGR diagnosed according to Delphi consensus criteria and demonstrating positive EDF in the umbilical artery. Exclusion criteria included major fetal anomalies, abnormal genetic testing results (if available), indications for imminent delivery (≤ 48 h after randomization), confirmed congenital infection, or preterm prelabor rupture of membranes. Participants were randomized 1:1 using a secure, web-based randomization system (Sealed Envelope). Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and clinical staff was not feasible.

A hospital “visit”, alongside a safety assessment only, included maternal blood pressure measurement, ultrasound with Doppler of UA, MCA, DV, and uterine arteries, amniotic fluid assessment using the deepest vertical pocket (DVP), ≥ 30 min of computerized cardiotocography (cCTG), estimated fetal weight (EFW) every 14 days, and review of any ordered biochemical tests. The sonographers performing the examinations (UN, KK, JKB) were trained and hold the Diploma in Fetal Medicine awarded by the Fetal Medicine Foundation. A computerized CTG (cCTG) (Philips Avalon FM30 CTG; Philips Healthcare, Eindhoven, the Netherlands) was performed once weekly during hospital visit. Women in the standard care arm (Doppler group) received biweekly EFW assessments and weekly Doppler evaluation, including UA-PI, MCA-PI, DV PI, and uterine arteries pulsatility index (UtA PI). Women in the intervention arm (cCTG-based monitoring group with protocol-mandated safety Doppler assessments) underwent in-hospital assessment of EFW and Doppler parameters every 2 weeks, with additional alternate-week Doppler examinations performed by an independent operator to ensure continuous weekly monitoring (as a “safety-net”). No extra interventions were performed based on these visits, and their inclusion was in accordance with ISUOG recommendations and national internal guidelines. However, these were not counted as study visits.

Participants received a home-based cCTG device (Carebits®, Warsaw, Poland; ISO 13485) and.

Performed recordings at least twice weekly for at ≥ 30 min. Women could initiate extra recordings in case of reduced fetal movements, but were instructed to report to the maternity assessment unit for clinical evaluation. All cCTG traces were reviewed remotely by trained midwives or obstetricians, with immediate feedback in the event of abnormal findings. The detailed report for a normal trace was available in the application within 30 min after completion of the CTG recording. Both during the hospital evaluation and the home-based CTG, the interpreters used the Dawes–Redman criteria modified by the STV values specified in the study protocol (STV < 3.5 ms before 34 weeks or < 4.5 ms after 34 weeks). Whenever the criteria were not met, participants from both groups were advised to present to the emergency department. According to institutional recommendations elective delivery was scheduled at 37 weeks of gestation, unless cCTG or Doppler findings indicated earlier intervention. The primary outcome was neonatal condition at birth, assessed by umbilical arterial cord blood pH. The prespecified analysis defined umbilical arterial pH as a categorical outcome (proportion of neonates with pH < 7.15). Due to the very low number of events, an additional post hoc analysis was performed using pH as a continuous variable. Secondary outcomes included maternal anxiety assessed with the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7) at recruitment and delivery, the total number of antenatal visits (excluding those performed for safety-only purposes), the rate of emergency cesarean section due to presumed fetal compromise and neonatal outcomes: 5 min Apgar score < 8 and composite adverse neonatal outcome (need for ventilation, neonatal intensive care unit admission (NICU), phototherapy, intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) grade ≥ II, hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), periventricular leukomalacia (PVL), respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), neonatal sepsis, necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), anemia requiring transfusion, or fetal/neonatal death. Analyses were performed using a modified intention-to-treat approach, including all randomized participants with available outcome data. Participants were excluded from specific analyses if outcome data were unavailable, including cases of loss to follow-up, delivery outside the study center, or post-randomization events precluding outcome assessment. Denominators varied across outcomes, because some analyses were restricted to participants eligible for the specific outcome. No imputation of missing data was performed.

Delivery in both groups was indicated in cases of persistent fetal heart rate decelerations, biophysical profile score ≤ 4, severe maternal complications, cCTG STV < 3.5 ms before 34 weeks or < 4.5 ms after 34 weeks, absent or reversed end-diastolic flow (AEDF/REDF) in the umbilical artery, or Doppler/EFW findings meeting predefined thresholds at or beyond 36 weeks. Amniotic fluid volume was not included in the protocol, as it is not part of the ISUOG Delphi Consensus. Nevertheless, none of the fetuses had a deepest vertical pocket < 2 cm.

Sample size calculation

The minimum required sample size was calculated based on a subanalysis of the TRUFFLE trial [21]. Assuming a two-sided alpha of 0.05 and a power of 80% (beta = 0.20), and accounting for a 10% dropout rate, the final estimated sample size was 256 participants.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using StataCorp. 2025. Stata Statistical Software: Release 19. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC. For continuous variables, normality of distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (with Lilliefors correction) and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with normal distribution were analyzed using the independent samples t test; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess the association between selected predictors and outcomes, multivariable logistic regression models were performed. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. The models were adjusted for clinically relevant maternal characteristics, including maternal age, body mass index (BMI), and parity. Due to the relatively small number of outcome events, the number of covariates included in the models was limited to these variables to reduce the risk of model overfitting. Sensitivity analyses restricted to complete cases were performed to assess the potential impact of missing outcomes. A per-protocol analysis excluding participants with safety Doppler was not feasible, as all participants underwent at least one such assessment in accordance with the study safety protocol.

Results

Due to the suspension of funding for the continuation of the study by the device provider, we were forced to terminate the study before reaching the planned sample size. A total of 120 participants were ultimately randomized, with 63 allocated to the cCTG-based group and 57 to the Doppler group. Outcomes were available for 59 participants in the cCTG-based group and 55 in the Doppler group. The remaining participants were lost to follow-up or they delivered in other hospitals, and outcome data were, therefore, unavailable. Secondary outcomes

The number of visits was significantly higher in the Doppler group (p = 0.01). All participants in the cCTG-based group underwent at least one protocol-mandated safety Doppler assessment. The median number of Doppler examinations per participant was 2 (IQR 1–3). Cesarean section due to presumed fetal compromise was significantly less frequent in the cCTG-based compared to the Doppler group (aOR 0.24, 95% CI 0.06–0.99). There were no significant differences between groups regarding Apgar score < 8 at 5 min (p = 0.59) and in NICU admission rates (aOR 1.92, 95% CI 0.53–6.94). (Table 3).

At the time of randomization, the proportion of participants who met the threshold for at least mild anxiety (GAD-7 ≥ 5) was similarly high across groups (p = 0.85). However, by the end of the study, a significantly greater proportion of participants in one group exceeded the anxiety threshold (p = 0.02). Discussion

The findings of the COSMOS trial provide compelling evidence that home-based cCTG is a safe and effective addition to Doppler-based hospital surveillance in pregnancies complicated by late-onset FGR. This randomized controlled trial showed comparable neonatal safety between the groups, alongside benefits related to maternal well-being and healthcare resource utilization. The absence of significant differences in neonatal condition or serious neonatal complications indicates that telemonitored cCTG may provide a safety level comparable to current standards of care. However, due to the very low number of events for the prespecified categorical pH outcome, additional post hoc analysis using continuous pH values was performed, and these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Although a lower rate of emergency cesarean delivery was observed in the CTG group, this result should be interpreted cautiously, as the study did not reach its intended sample size and external factors may have influenced outcomes. Thus, the difference likely reflects random variability rather than a true effect of the monitoring method. In general, presumed fetal compromise is a common feature of deliveries complicated by placentation disorders, yet predicting it remains challenging. However, predictive tools and placentation markers used in routine clinical practice may help address this challenge, as placental dysfunction is a potential surrogate of spontaneous birth and presumed fetal compromise. The risk for PE at 12 and 36 weeks was found predictive of both these events which could help stratify risk and guide the selection of appropriate medical management strategies to maximize the chances of an optimal outcome [22, 23]. Moreover, screening at 36 weeks allows prediction of cases of fetal compromise requiring cesarean delivery during induction of labor, which occurs in a large proportion of late-onset FGR cases, and, therefore, has a direct impact on clinical practice [24]. Because this study began in 2021 and the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio was measured before delivery in only a minority of patients, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn. Nevertheless, with its expanding use in clinical practice, this index may emerge as a widely applicable tool for risk assessment and for guiding decisions regarding the timing and mode of delivery.

Women in the cCTG arm reported significantly lower anxiety scores at delivery, suggesting that the ability to self-monitor may offer psychological reassurance. The reduced number of antenatal visits in the intervention group highlights a key logistical and economic advantage of remote monitoring, particularly relevant in areas with limited access to tertiary care or during public health emergencies [25].

A major strength of this study is its randomized and pragmatic design addressing late-onset FGR, an area in which clinical practice remains largely guided by expert opinion. COSMOS provides valuable evidence and reflects advances in telemedicine, supporting a modern, patient-centered approach that may enhance accessibility and continuity of care. However, the study has limitations, including the reduced sample size, which weakens the statistical power of the findings. As a result, the study was likely underpowered to detect small but clinically relevant differences, and only relatively large effect sizes could be reliably identified;, therefore, non-significant results should be interpreted with caution. There is also a theoretical risk of missing early signs of circulatory centralization, especially if upcoming TRUFFLE 2 results confirm that delivery in late-onset FGR should occur once centralization is detected. Nevertheless, centralization typically develops over several days and is usually detectable within the 14 day monitoring interval; further analysis on this aspect is planned.

Potential issues related to device setup and patient compliance must also be acknowledged. All participants completed at least two recordings per week, and no major technical difficulties were reported after initial training. The use of different devices for home-based and outpatient recordings may have introduced minor variability in signal quality.

Weekly Doppler surveillance places a considerable burden on healthcare systems and on patients residing far from tertiary facilities. While Doppler is widely available, the skills required to accurately perform and interpret assessments are not universal. A U.S. survey of 72 final-year MFM fellows found that only 58% felt comfortable performing umbilical artery Doppler examinations, illustrating existing gaps in expertise [26]. In resource-limited settings, substituting part of Doppler-based surveillance with CTG may be a practical alternative, supporting fetal monitoring where access to MFM specialists is limited and aligning with increasing technological demands in prenatal care [27].

These findings are consistent with literature indicating that Doppler abnormalities in late-onset FGR have limited predictive value [8, 15]. Evidence from the DIGITAT trial suggested that timely delivery may be more critical than the specific monitoring method [19]. While TRUFFLE emphasized combined Doppler and cCTG in early FGR, it left uncertainty regarding late-onset disease, which COSMOS begins to address.

Existing data on remote CTG suggest it may support fetal safety while improving patient satisfaction and reducing costs [28]. A study from the Netherlands comparing home telemonitoring with hospital admission in high-risk pregnancies reported fewer admissions, cost savings, and higher patient satisfaction [27]. A Danish study of over 400 intermediate- and high-risk pregnancies showed that fetal telemonitoring may be as safe as hospital-based care, with cost reductions of 40–50% [29]. Perinatal outcomes in our cohort were comparable to those reported in a multicenter study from 2023, with similar rates of emergency cesarean delivery and related neonatal indicators [30]. In this context, our results suggest that remote cCTG monitoring is a feasible approach to the management of late-onset FGR and may complement conventional in-hospital surveillance. However, as an underpowered pilot study with incomplete follow-up, these findings should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating. Larger studies are required to evaluate clinical outcomes and the potential impact of telemonitored cCTG on healthcare and patient experience.
