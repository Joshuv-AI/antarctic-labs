Machine Learning Transferability for Malware Detection
César Vieira, João Vitorino, Eva Maia, Isabel Praça

Abstract
Malware continues to be a predominant operational risk for organizations, especially when obfuscation techniques are used to evade detection. Despite the ongoing efforts in the development of Machine Learning (ML) detection approaches, there is still a lack of feature compatibility in public datasets. This limits generalization when facing distribution shifts, as well as transferability to different datasets. This study evaluates the suitability of different data preprocessing approaches for the detection of Portable Executable (PE) files with ML models. The preprocessing pipeline unifies EMBERv2 (2,381-dim) features datasets, trains paired models under two training setups: EMBER + BODMAS (EB) and EMBER + BODMAS + ERMDS (EBR) . Regarding model evaluation, both EB and EBR models are tested against TRITIUM, INFERNO and SOREL-20M. ERMDS is also used for testing for the EB setup. The findings indicate that compact boosting static detectors are applicable for on-host use, but require a careful analysis of how PE obfuscation techniques affect the feature distributions of training datasets and during model inference.

Introduction

Malware is defined as software that intentionally compromises the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of information systems, thereby enabling attackers to engage in extortion, disruption and espionage activities
[
19
]
.
From 2023 to 2025, the
European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA)
identified the Public Administration as the most frequently targeted by malware within the
European Union (EU)
, with ransomware and data-related intrusions as the main attack vectors
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
.
These intrusions are indicative of trends that have been observed in the context of specialized attack ecosystems, which include
Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS)
and access-brokered access
[
8
]
.

In order to prevent the execution of malicious programs, organizations can make use of both signature and anomaly-based approaches.
Signature-based detection approaches are capable of achieving high operational speeds and low
False Positive Rate (FPR)
by comparing the hash of a given binary with a database of known malware sample hashes or matching file content against a set of known byte or string patterns, such as YARA rules
[
13
]
.

On the other hand, anomaly-based detection approaches increasingly rely on
Machine Learning (ML)
to learn a profile of benign host behavior or a discriminative boundary from static features, which requires hyperparameter optimization.
Methods include supervised, semi-supervised and unsupervised families, ranging from gradient-boosted trees and feed-forward networks to one-class and reconstruction-based approaches
[
9
]
.

However, despite the growing usage of
ML
in anomaly-based approaches, their application to real-world
Portable Executable (PE)
files still faces challenges.
The limited compatibility and standardization of the utilized features across public datasets hinders the reproducibility and transferability of detection pipelines
[
15
]
.
Moreover, models trained on one dataset may generalize poorly to others due to distributional mismatch and evolving attacker tooling, which may cause concept drift over time, requiring periodic model retraining and recalibration of feature distributions and data preprocessing approaches
[
4
]
.

This study evaluates different data preprocessing approaches for the detection of
PE
files with ML models.
A static detection pipeline is built on
EMBER
-v2 features with two training setups:
EMBER + BODMAS
and
EMBER + BODMAS + ERMDS
.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)
or
XGBoost Feature Selection (XGBFS)
are then applied to the unified datasets, producing feature vectors of 128/256/384 dimensions where FLAML tuned model pairs (
LightGBM
,
XGBoost
,
Extra Trees
,
Random Forest
) are then trained against the aforementioned reduced vector dimensions.
Evaluation covers in-distribution test splits and cross-dataset inference on
SOREL-20M
, TRITIUM, INFERNO (and
ERMDS
when withheld), reporting F1,
AUC
and
TPR
@1%/0.1%
FPR
to assess generalization, obfuscation robustness and drift sensitivity.

Section

Section
4

Finally, section
5

Related Work

To choose the most relevant datasets and data preprocessing approaches, it is important to analyze recent literature regarding static Windows malware detection. Table

provides a summary of relevant scientific papers, covering the key milestones and more recent developments.

Historically, signature-based detection on Windows has relied on deterministic content rules that require minimal computation.
However, such systems are vulnerable to semantics-preserving changes, packing, polymorphism and other forms of obfuscation, which limit the coverage of novel and zero-day malware.
Motivated by these limitations, Schultz et al.
[
22
]
and Kolter & Maloof
[
12

proposed approaches that relied on the extraction of features such as imported functions, strings and byte n-grams, which were then processed by
ML

and boosted decision trees.

Subsequently, Saxe & Berlin
[
21
]
replaced manual feature selection with automatic representations derived from two-dimensional byte-entropy histograms of entire binaries.
This approach captured spatial correlations and entropy variations within executable content, improving scalability, robustness to obfuscation and higher classification accuracy compared to manually engineered n-gram features, marking an early transition toward deep-learning static malware analysis.

The earliest major benchmark for static malware classification was Microsoft BIG 2015
[
18
]
, which provided over 500 GB of labeled Windows
Portable Executable
binaries grouped into nine malware families.
The competition instigated the first comparative studies between traditional feature-engineered models and emerging deep-learning methods for family classification.
The solution that was found to be most effective by Wang et al.
[
24
]
used an ensemble of
Random Forest (RF)
and Gradient Boosting on engineered byte and assembly-level statistics.

The publication of
Elastic Malware Benchmark for Empowering Researchers (EMBER)

[
2
]
contributed to the advancement of static malware classification by introducing a fully open and standardized dataset for static malware detection.
The dataset includes 1.1 million
PE
files, encoded into 2381-dimensional feature vectors, capturing
PE
header fields, section entropies, imports and string statistics through feature hashing.
The
Light Gradient-Boosting Machine (LightGBM)
baseline achieved an
Area Under the Curve (AUC)
of 0.999, with a 92.9%
TPR
@0.1%
FPR
, surpassing the byte-sequence
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)
MalConv model, which achieved an
AUC
of 0.998.
The authors further updated the dataset with the introduction of the 2018 version, which included samples from 2018 and expanded the representation to a 2381-feature vector.

Further work also saw improvements on the baseline model, where Rahali et al.
[
17
]
proposed an adaptation of
LightGBM
incorporating a custom logistic loss function in order to account for the cost of misclassifying samples.
When evaluated against the standard
LightGBM
, their approach achieved a lower classification error rate and demonstrated more stable convergence behaviour.

To address the limitations in scope and timeframe imposed by
EMBER
,
SOphos-REversingLabs 20 Million (SOREL-20M)

[
10
]
introduced a dataset containing approximately 20 million
PE
samples.
The latter takes advantage of the
EMBER
-v2 feature standard and uses temporal splits for training, validation and testing.
The models applied to assess the dataset were
LightGBM
and a multi-task
Feed-Forward Neural Network (FFNN)
inspired by the ALOHA approach
[
20
]
.
The results of the experiment yielded an
\approx
0.998
AUC
and
TPR
@0.1%
FPR
above 90%.
Subsequent studies, including Austin B. et al.
[
3
]
, have employed AutoML-driven hyperparameter optimization in the context of
FFNN
architectures that have been trained on both
EMBER
and
SOREL-20M
.
The study reported improvements in the low
TPR
regime across both datasets, reaching a
TPR
0.1%
FPR
of 0.956 on
EMBER
and 0.965 on
SOREL-20M
.

Recent research highlights the persistent challenge of concept drift, defined as the evolution of malware features at a rate faster than static detectors can adapt.
The Rapidrift framework, proposed by Manikandaraja et al.
[
14
]
, is based on this premise and introduces a methodology capable of analyzing concept drift at a more granular level, quantifying the degradation of model performance across temporal partitions and adversarial shifts. In the same study, the authors released two new datasets, TRITIUM and INFERNO, designed to capture distinct aspects of real-world malware evolution. TRITIUM is a product of naturally occurring Windows
PE
samples collected from operational environments, whereas INFERNO was constructed using popular cybersecurity tools that allow the creation and modification of malicious payloads.
Furthermore, Choudhary A. et al.
[
5
]
explored ensemble and gradient boosting classifiers along dimensionality-reduction techniques such as
Principal Component Analysis
and
XGBoost Feature Selection
over
EMBER
, BODMAS and
Evaluating Robustness of Learning-based Malware Detection Systems (ERMDS)
. The study obtained an F1 score of
\approx
0.9773 and
\approx
0.9958
AUC
score, while simultaneously reducing vector dimensionality by nearly 85%.

In summary, an analysis of current literature reveals an inability to deliver detectors capable of generalizing across heterogeneous systems, feature pipelines and temporal shifts, with obfuscation often degrading performance outside the training domain.
The analysis of existing studies reveals a predominant tendency to evaluate primarily on self-curated datasets and feature variants, which imposes limitations on the comparison of results and complicates cross-dataset generalization and robustness to packing, polymorphism and concept drift.

3
Methodology

This section describes the work pipeline for both approaches and the datasets used throughout the study.
The performed experiments included two different training datasets,
EB
and
EBR
, and two different feature reduction processes,
PCA
and
XGBFS
. These were used to train
LightGBM
,
XGBoost
,
Extra Trees
and
Random Forest
model pairs, with hyperparameters tuned via FLAML and selected on the corresponding validation split of each unified dataset.
The experiments were performed on a Windows Desktop with an Intel i7-12700KF, a NVIDIA 5070 TI and 32 GB of RAM, occupying
\approx
200 GB of disk space.

3.2
Data Processing

The design and implementation of the presented processing pipeline closely follow the methodology proposed by Choudhary et al.
[
5
]
, with some adaptations regarding dataset usage.
As outlined in Section
3.1
, all datasets are structured in accordance with the
EMBER
-v2 feature standard
[
2
]
.
The workflow, illustrated in Figure
1
, begins with the dataset-specific preprocessing steps, which include vectorization, the removal of missing samples and the unification of sub-sets.

Figure 1:
Data preprocessing and model evaluation workflow.

Subsequently, a unified training dataset consisting of the
EB
is produced and model training splits the combined training and validation pools into two equal partitions.
The remaining datasets are intended exclusively for the purpose of evaluating the models robustness against obfuscated specific attacks and large data in post-training inference evaluation.

Following the consolidation of the dataset, Robust Scaling is applied in order to mitigate the influence of outliers in the data.
This scaling method centers features around their median and scales by the
InterQuartile Range (IQR)
, providing resilience against the potentially distorting effect of extreme values. This robust normalization is particularly suitable when datasets contain skewed distributions, as it preserves the primary data structure while stabilizing the learning process
[
1
]
.
Afterwards, MinMax Scaling is applied, rescaling each feature to a fixed interval by linearly transforming all values into an interval between 0 and 1.
This guarantees consistent input ranges, facilitating stable optimization and fair feature comparison in downstream models.
All scaling parameters are determined exclusively from the training set, ensuring strict data segregation across validation and test phases.

Consequently, the feature vectors undergo dimensionality reduction using supervised feature selection via
XGBFS
, where features are ranked according to their importance gain and unsupervised
PCA
, which projects data into orthogonal components, thus preserving maximal variance.
For each technique it was produced reduced feature subsets of 128, 256 and 384 dimensions.

3.3
Model Training

For each tree classifier used, namely
XGBoost
,
LightGBM
,
Extra Trees
and
Random Forest
, two independent instances were trained, one per partition and tune each instance with FLAML
[
23
]
to optimize its hyperparameters on its own partition.
This yields a pair of tuned models per classifier that have seen separate training data and undergone separate searches.

At inference the two instances of a given classifier were combined by weighted soft voting.
Let
x
be a sample while
p_{1}(x)
and
p_{2}(x)
are the probability outputs for each instance, the final score is calculated as in Equation
1
:

\hat{p}(x;w)=w\cdot p_{1}(x)+(1-w)\cdot p_{2}(x),w\in[0,1]

(1)

where
w
increments in steps of 0.1.
The weight pairs that achieve the highest validation performance are kept for testing and all robustness evaluations.

For each reduced feature set, F1-score,
AUC
and
TPR
@0.1% and 1%
FPR
were used to assess models efficiency.
This choice for evaluation metrics is justified since F1-Score serves as an harmonic mean of precision and recall at a chosen decision threshold (in the case of this study, best_f1).

AUC
helps understand if the model is actually learning from the features, while
TPR
at a threshold allows for an estimation of the model performance in real environments, since malware only constitutes a small percentage of sample in these cases.

Results and Discussion

EB
and
EBR
, but also with the TRITIUM, INFERNO and
ERMDS
datasets.
The transferability of the
ML
models is assessed using multiple evaluation metrics, such as F1-Score,
AUC
and
TPR
@1% and 0.1%
FPR
.
SOREL-20M
, on the other hand, was only tested using the test split due to limitations in computational resources.

Regarding the

Operating-point results confirm this, where at 1%

The F1 of 98.27% indicates well-balanced precision and recall.

5
Conclusions

The present study investigated host-based detection on Windows operating systems using static
EMBER
-v2 features and two training regimes, namely
EB
and
EBR
.
The pipeline made use of robust and min–max scaling, reduced dimensionality to 128, 256 and 384 using
PCA
or
XGBFS
, trained model pairs for four tree estimators with FLAML-tuned hyperparameters and evaluated model performance with F1,
AUC
and
TPR
at 1% and 0.1%
FPR
on a unified test splits and other
EMBER
-v2 datasets.

The results of this study indicate that boosting-based estimators are the most reliable, especially when paired with
XGBFS
at 384 dimensions, which consistently outperforms
PCA
at matched sizes.
Model generalization remained sensitive to the training mixture.
Specifically,
EB
models performance degraded substantially when tested against
ERMDS
, whereas including
ERMDS
in the training set reduced generalization to
SOREL-20M
relative to
EB
.
This pattern is consistent with a distributional mismatch between
ERMDS
and the remaining
EMBER
-v2 datasets.
The
EB
configuration was particularly vulnerable to obfuscation-heavy samples when
ERMDS
was treated as external data, which motivates further analysis of how obfuscation affects feature distributions.

Future work will include additional models proposed in the literature which include additional
ML
models and more complex deep learning models in order to assess robustness to obfuscation and concept drift, focusing on cross-dataset generalization.
Overall, it is important to continue the research efforts into the analysis of how feature representation, training data composition and design choices affect model generalization, especially at low
FPR
.
