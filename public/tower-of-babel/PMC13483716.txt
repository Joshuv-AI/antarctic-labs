Glycoproteomic and genetic analysis of N-glycosylation of complement component C3 reveals immune pathway regulation
Authors: Dinko Šoić, Najda Rudman, Oliver C. Grant, Frano Vučković, Azra Frkatović-Hodžić, Eliza Gazaway, Flemming Pociot, James F. Wilson, Ozren Polašek, Robert J. Woods, Olga Gornik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-translational modifications represent a critical layer of protein regulation, yet their genetic control and population variation remain poorly characterized. Here we present a comprehensive analysis integrating population-scale glycoproteomics with genome-wide association study (GWAS) to uncover genetic regulatory networks controlling N-glycosylation of complement component C3.ResultsThrough LC–MS glycopeptide analysis of 816 Croatian adults, we characterize site-specific N-glycosylation at two C3 sites (N63 and N917), confirming an unusual phenotype consisting of exclusive high-mannose structures rather than complex glycans typical of secreted proteins. GWAS identifies six genetic loci significantly associated with C3 N-glycosylation patterns, including genes encoding proteins involved in maintenance of the protein secretory pathway, proteins involved in the complement pathway or regulation of complement, and regulators of gene expression. Using colocalization analysis, we discover shared causal variants between C3 glycosylation and immune diseases, particularly rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease, suggesting glycosylation as a mechanistic link between genetic variation and disease susceptibility. C3 N-glycoprofiling reveals significant associations with sex, age, and metabolic parameters, indicating integration of genetic and environmental factors. Structural modeling provides mechanistic insights, revealing how protein architecture constrains glycan processing and enables functional glycan-mediated interactions.ConclusionsOur multiomic approach establishes a framework for understanding how genetic variation shapes post-translational modifications at population scale, demonstrating C3 N-glycosylation as both a genetically and environmentally regulated checkpoint in complement activation with implications for precision medicine approaches in immune and metabolic diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04153-4.

Background

N-glycosylation is a highly conserved co- and post-translational protein modification in which complex oligosaccharide structures, called N-glycans, are covalently added to asparagine (Asn/N) residues. N-glycosylation has emerged in recent years as a powerful diagnostic tool and a key functional effector of many glycoproteins. The process of N-glycan attachment and maturation involves the action of many glycosyltransferases and glycosidases, and is regulated by a huge number of different genes—not only those intuitively related to the process itself. The process can end in three main N-glycan types: a) high mannose, that are formed in the early course of the process and have multiple mannose units attached to glycan core; b) complex, that are produced by trimming off mannose from the high mannose glycans and then elaborating with different monosaccharide units (N-acetylglucosamine, mannose, galactose, sialic acid, fucose…); and c) hybrid, which are glycans that are formed during the transition from high mannose to complex. Each glycoprotein can have one to many glycosylation sites that may be occupied by different N-glycan structures, resulting in many glycoforms of the same protein, integrating genetic, epigenetic and environmental information [1, 2]. Today it is known that N-glycans have protective roles for the protein as well as influence protein functions in many physiological and pathophysiological processes.

Glycans are one of the main regulators of antibody effector function [3, 4] and many other features of the immune system [5, 6], and have also been implicated as critical regulators of complement-mediated inflammation [7, 8]. For a long time, glycans were underexplored due to their structural complexity [9], while recent advances in analytical techniques have enabled large-scale studies that have identified glycans as biomarkers and even active mediators in various diseases [10–14].

In our previous work on children with new-onset type 1 diabetes and their healthy siblings from the Danish Registry of Childhood and Adolescent Diabetes (DanDiabKids) [15], as well as Croatian adults with type 1 diabetes complications [16], we established C3 N-glycosylation as a potential novel biomarker of both autoimmunity and metabolic dysregulation. Additionally, the C3 gene was identified as a novel genetic regulator of plasma protein N-glycosylation [17], with the associated genetic variants having been previously linked to type 1 diabetes risk and complement activation [18, 19].

C3 is a central protein in all complement activation pathways that are in control of the host defence against microorganisms and the removal of self and non-self targets, among other important immune functions [20]. So far, only a few studies have addressed C3 protein glycosylation, and our recent study on children with type 1 diabetes was the only to examine C3 N-glycosylation in a larger cohort of participants [21]. We demonstrated that C3 contains three consensus N-glycosylation sequons, of which only two (N63 and N917) are occupied by N-glycans [22]. The third site remains unoccupied, though the structural or functional basis for this differential site occupancy has not been fully elucidated. Notably, both occupied sites bear exclusively high-mannose structures [22], an uncommon occurrence for plasma glycoproteins, which predominantly carry complex N-glycans. Indeed, while the majority of complement proteins are synthesized in hepatocytes (C3 has a 4.5% contribution from the kidney [23]), only C3 and C4 carry high mannose glycans [22, 24]. We, and others, have even reported the unexpected presence of the monoglucosylated high mannose N-linked glycan on secreted C3. The presence of this glycan type on a mature functional protein is highly unusual as it normally represents a retention signal for the calnexin-calreticulin quality control pathway in the endoplasmic reticulum, and should trigger protein degradation [21, 22], indicating that this particular N-glycan may play an important role in C3 function. Given that the vast majority of complement proteins originate from the same tissue, this indicates that the unique N-glycosylation of the C3 protein may be attributed to local protein structure rather than a distinct, cell-specific pathway.

Native C3 is an inactive glycoprotein that becomes activated through cleavage-induced conformational changes, exposing its thioester-containing domain (TED). The activated form, C3b, covalently binds target surfaces via the TED, amplifying complement activation, promoting phagocytosis, and stimulating adaptive immune responses [25]. Previous research suggested that N-glycans at the N63 glycosylation site may be involved in the binding of inactivated C3b (iC3b, which functions as an opsonin) to the complement receptor 3 (CR3, also known as Mac-1 or integrin αMβ2) [26]. CR3 is expressed on various myeloid cells including macrophages, monocytes, and neutrophils, where it mediates phagocytosis of iC3b-opsonized particles and modulates immune cell trafficking and activation [27]. The N63 glycans are situated in the MG1 domain of C3b, which directly interacts with the thioester-containing domain (TED). Together with MG4 and MG5, this domain establishes the correct orientation of TED necessary for covalent attachment to pathogen surfaces. Importantly, the crystal structure of the iC3b-CR3 complex reveals that N63 is positioned at the protein–protein interface, suggesting that glycan structures at this site could directly influence binding affinity and downstream immune responses [26]. The N917 glycosylation site is located within a domain involved in pathogen binding and, critically, lies proximal to the interaction interface between C3b and factor B [25]. Factor B binding to C3b is essential for formation of the alternative pathway C3 convertase (C3bBb), which amplifies complement activation by cleaving additional C3 molecules. The strategic positioning of N917 near this functionally critical interface raises the possibility that glycan structures at this site could modulate convertase assembly, stability, or regulation, thereby influencing the magnitude of complement amplification.

Given the potential importance of C3 N-glycosylation in complement activation and immune function, comprehensive characterization of its regulation and population variation is needed. Here, we present the first population-scale, site-specific analysis of C3 N-glycosylation in a general adult population of European ancestry from the Croatian island Korčula. Our study integrates glycoproteomics with genome-wide association analysis to understand the genetic architecture underlying C3 glycoform variation, and examines associations with demographic, anthropometric, and metabolic traits to characterize environmental influences. Through colocalization analysis with immune disease genome-wide association study (GWAS), we explore whether C3 glycosylation may represent a mechanistic link between genetic variation and disease susceptibility. Finally, we employ molecular dynamics simulations to provide structural insights into glycan processing constraints and model glycan-mediated interactions at the iC3b-CR3 interface.

Results

C3 N-glycopeptide analysis

Recently published high-throughput glycoproteomic workflow which includes protein enrichment and MS analysis of complement component C3 was utilized to examine C3 N-glycosylation profiles in the adult population of the habitants from the island of Korčula (Croatia). In total, blood plasma samples from 816 general adult population participants from the Croatian island Korčula aged 18–109 were included in the study. The study population consisted predominantly of females (66.5%, median age 52 years) compared to males (33.5%, median age 55 years), with participants’ biochemical parameters, including lipid profile and glucose metabolism markers, all within reference intervals (Additional file 1: Table S1). N-glycosylation analysis was done at the glycopeptide level, enabling precise identification of glycan compositions at specific amino acid residues within the C3 protein. As in our previous study, two occupied N-glycosylation sites were identified, but due to improved sample preparation protocol additional three glycoforms were detected on the site C3_N917 (Table 1). Hence, we identified three glycoforms on the C3_N63 N-glycosylation site and six on the C3_N917, resulting in two N-glycosylation clusters and nine glycopeptides per sample (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Relative abundances of the glycopeptides found on each glycosylation site are presented in Fig. 1, and the total results of the C3 N-glycosylation analysis can be found in Additional file 2: Table S2. C3 N-glycosylation site occupancy

The N-glycosylation macroheterogeneity of complement component C3, i.e. the level of occupancy of a particular N-glycosylation site with N-glycans was studied using a label-free approach. After digestion of the samples and their subsequent deglycosylation, signal intensities of the deglycosylated peptides were compared to those of the non-glycosylated peptides. Control samples which weren’t treated with PNGase F were included to account for spontaneous deamidation. For both N-glycosylation sites, no non-glycosylated peptides were present (Additional file 1: Fig. S2), indicating that C3 N-glycosylation sites are probably completely occupied with N-glycans. Extracted signals for C3 N-glycosylation site occupancy analysis can be seen in Additional file 1: Table S3.

Effects of sex and age on the C3 N-glycome

As N-glycans are known to be modulated by both sex and age, their effects on the C3 N-glycome were studied through regression analysis of all measured glycoforms. After the adjustment for multiple testing, statistically significant associations were observed between sex and eight of the nine C3 glycoforms, while age showed significant correlations with six glycoforms (Fig. 2, Additional file 1: Table S4). The strongest associations with sex were found on C3_N917 site with Man8 glycoform (R = −0.372, p.adj = 6.84 × 10–27) being more abundant in females, and Man9 and GlcMan9 glycoforms being more prevalent among males (Additional file 1: Table S5). Interestingly, similar trend of higher abundance of unprocessed glycan structures (i.e. those with more mannose units) in younger and middle-aged males can also be observed on C3_N63 site, with Man7 structure having the strongest correlation (R = 0.207, p.adj = 6.89 × 10–9). Compared to sex-related associations, age proved to have smaller effect on C3 N-glycome. Nevertheless, the effect proved to be statistically significant for all of the glycoforms on C3_N63 site, with the strongest association actually being on C3_N917 site (glycoform GlcMan9 with R = 0.271, p.adj = 2.13 × 10–14) (Additional file 1: Table S5). Associations of C3 N-glycome with biochemical and anthropomorphic traits

Given the established influence of biochemical and anthropometric parameters, particularly those related to metabolic health, on N-glycosylation of plasma glycoproteins, we conducted regression analyses incorporating various traits (listed in Additional file 1: Table S6) to examine whether similar relationships exist for C3 glycosylation. As most biochemical and physiological traits are strongly correlated with age and sex, both parameters were included as covariates in subsequent analyses. Regression analyses unveiled strong associations between multiple C3 glycoforms and both biochemical (including glucose, triglycerides, uric acid, etc.) and clinical parameters (such as weight or waist, hip, head and brachial circumference, etc.) (Additional file 1: Fig. S3, Additional file 3: Table S7). The most prominent associations identified were between C3 N-glycome profiles and BMI, with six out of nine C3 glycoforms being significantly associated with the parameter. Compellingly, on both N-glycosylation sites higher BMI was linked to a similar trend towards higher abundance of unprocessed glycan structures with more mannose units. However, it should be noted that various metabolic health indicators (glucose, triglycerides, HbA1c, blood pressure, etc.) displayed correlation patterns with C3 N-glycome akin to those observed for BMI. Even prevalence of diabetes showed similar trend of connection, although the association with C3 N-glycome was weaker compared to that of corresponding biochemical traits. Given the widespread correlation of biochemical and physiological traits with BMI, regression analysis was repeated, but this time with BMI included as an additional covariate together with sex and age (Fig. 3, Additional file 4: Table S8). Expectedly, most of the associations with parameters obviously correlated with BMI (such as weight, waist and hip circumference, etc.) were lost. Therefore, fewer associations reached statistical significance. Furthermore, observed effects appeared to be relatively weaker compared to the analysis with only age and sex included as covariates. Even so, the direction of associations between C3 glycoforms and metabolic traits remained consistent with the unadjusted analysis: less-processed glycan structures with more mannose residues continued to associate with unfavourable metabolic profiles, with the strongest associations observed for triglycerides, uric acid, glucose, HbA1c, and cholesterol. Intriguingly, association of C3_N63_Man5 with diabetes prevalence proved to be even stronger after the introduced correction for BMI. Discovery and replication GWAS

We performed a discovery GWAS of C3 N-glycosylation in a cohort of European descent from Croatian island Korčula (N = 801). The association between HRC-imputed SNPs and nine C3 N-glycan phenotypes was studied under the assumption of an additive linear model. The results are presented in Table 2. We identified 6 genome-wide significant loci associated with C3 N-glycosylation with candidate genes including MAN1C1 (mannosidase alpha class 1 C member 1), TMED5 (transmembrane emp24 domain-containing protein 5), DR1 (Down-Regulator Of Transcription 1), CFH/CFHR1/CFHR2/CFHR3/CFHR4 (complement factor H and complement factor H -related protein 1, 2, 3 and 4), TXNDC11 (thioredoxin domain-containing protein), FUT5 (4-galactosyl-N-acetylglucosaminide 3-alpha-L-fucosyltransferase), AC104532.2 (long non-coding RNA), and C3 (complement component C3). Regional plots showing the glycan-associated genetic regions and displaying magnitude of association for each SNP, the pairwise linkage disequilibrium (LD) pattern with the most strongly associated SNP, and positions of SNPs relative to nearby genes are presented in Additional file 1: Fig. S4-S9. Our results suggest both cis- and trans-acting regulation of N-glycans at N917 glycosylation site, implicating C3 locus itself in addition to TXNDC11, TMED5/DR1 and CFH/CFHR loci. Both transcriptional and post-translational effects are also suggested by implication of both MAN1C1 and FUT5/AC104532.2 loci in the regulation of N-glycans at N63 glycosylation site. eQTLgen colocalization analysis identified colocalization of variants associated with C3 glycosylation with TMED5, DR1, and TXNDC11 gene expression (Fig. 4 and Additional file 1: Table S9). To validate the robustness of our findings and to assess generalizability across different populations, we performed replication analysis in an independent cohort of European descent from the Danish Registry of Childhood and Adolescent Diabetes [15]. The results of the replication analysis are presented in Additional file 1: Table S10. Out of three loci for which genotype data were available in the smaller replication cohort, two loci were replicated. In unaffected children (N = 82) the association of the C3 locus and C3 N-glycosylation patterns was replicated (rs2230203 with C3_N917_Man9 p = 5.41 × 10–4, beta = −0.661; with C3_N917_Man8 p = 1.45 × 10–3, beta = 0.613), whereas in affected children (N = 59) association was nominally significant (rs1047286 with C3_N917_GlcMan9 p = 3.03 × 10–2, beta = −0.506; with C3_N917_Man8 p = 2.11 × 10–2, beta = 0.553). The association and effect direction of the CFH/CFHR1/CFHR2/CFHR3/CFHR4 locus was also replicated (rs1065489 with C3_N917_Man8 p = 1.58 × 10–2, beta = −0.497). Collectively, the identified loci suggest several distinct biological processes in the regulation of C3 glycosylation: direct glycan processing (MAN1C1), early ER quality control and mannose trimming (TXNDC11), protein trafficking through the secretory pathway mediated by glycans (TMED5), complement pathway function and regulation (C3, CFH/CFHR1-4), and transcriptional regulation of glycan biosynthesis (DR1,AC104532.2).

Functional consequences

Bearing in mind that missense variants may directly impact the protein function and thus affect the phenotype, functional consequences of associated variants were estimated by using Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) v97 by Ensembl [28]. For TMED5 and CFHR3 potential pathogenicity of variants was detected by both SIFT and Polyphen-2, whereas only Polyphen-2 detected potential pathogenicity for CFH. For C3 and TXNDC11, the variants were predicted to result in benign change. C3 glycoprocessing enzyme accessibility analysis

ER resident mannosidase I (ERManI) efficiently cleaves a single mannose from the B-arm of the glycan to generate Man8. A crystal structure of ERManI bound to Man9, was employed to calculate the degree to which ERManI could access the two N-glycosites on C3. The percentage of time that the N-glycan was accessible for processing was calculated by superimposing ERManI onto each glycosylation site via aligning the ERManI-bound Man9. After superimposition, any snapshots that resulted in significant atomic overlaps between ERManI and C3 were deemed to be inaccessible to the enzyme. Both sites on C3 displayed very low degrees of accessibility to ERManI (N63 < 2%, N917 ~ 0%), indicating that these sites would be relatively slowly processed by ERManI. In the case of N63, even the shapes that were accessible resulted in close contacts between the loops of Leu54-His60 and Leu756-Ile764 with the aligned ERManI, further decreasing the likelihood of efficient enzyme processing.

Discussion

This study provides an integrated analysis of complement C3 N-glycosylation, combining site-specific glycopeptide quantification in a population cohort with genome-wide association analysis to characterize both environmental and genetic determinants, along with their functional and disease-related implications. Having previously characterized C3 glycosylation aberrations in type 1 diabetes, we now present the first large-scale analysis of C3 N-glycosylation in a general adult population. Our study of 816 European adults from the Croatian island Korčula confirms that C3 N-glycosylation sites are fully occupied with exclusively high-mannose structures, including unusual monoglucosylated glycans (Fig. 1, Table 1), setting C3 apart from other complement and plasma glycoproteins. We further identify associations between C3 glycoform distributions and metabolic traits, age, and sex (Figs. 2 and 3, Additional file 1: Table S6, Additional file 3: Table S7), suggesting that C3 glycosylation reflects physiological state. Finally, we map six genetic loci influencing C3 glycosylation (Table 2), implicating glycan processing enzymes, protein secretory pathway components, regulators of gene transcription, and complement proteins and regulators, with disease colocalization analysis revealing connections to rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease (Fig. 5). Below, we discuss each of these findings in turn, relating them to known C3 biology and their potential clinical significance.

The N-glycan repertoire of C3 represents the earliest, simplest forms in N-glycan biosynthetic pathway, as these glycans are normally further processed by endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi enzymes resulting in complex or hybrid structures on mature glycoproteins [24, 32, 33]. Our Concanavalin A-based enrichment approach was selected because prior studies using orthogonal methods have established that C3 carries exclusively high-mannose glycans. While ConA exhibits preferential affinity for high-mannose structures, it also binds the trimannosyl core present in complex and hybrid glycans [34], and if such structures existed on C3, at least partial detection would be expected. Our structural modelling provides a mechanistic explanation for this exclusivity: both glycosylation sites are buried within the protein and inaccessible to Golgi-resident processing enzymes (Fig. 6). Typically, the monoglucosylated structure indicates protein misfolding and serves as a retention signal for the refolding processes within the calnexin-calreticulin quality control pathway in the ER [35, 36]. Nevertheless, in the case of C3, our study showed that around 5% of its total glycan part corresponds to GlcMan9 glycan even in its functional state, an unprecedented occurrence for a glycoprotein from a healthy individual [22]. As these features sharply contrast with even other complement components, which are predominantly synthesized in hepatocytes like C3, retention of high mannose and monoglucosylated glycans likely originates from C3’s unique protein structure rather than a distinct biosynthetic pathway. Previous studies, complemented by our structural modelling analysis of site accessibility in this paper, suggest that C3 N-glycosylation sites are buried within the protein, potentially shielding them from further processing by Golgi enzymes [37–39]. However, other plasma glycoproteins (such as Fc region of IgG) [40, 41] also have buried glycosylation sites but fail to retain monoglucosylated forms, indicating that unique glycosylation of C3 may involve additional factors beyond site accessibility. Our site occupancy analysis confirmed that C3 glycosite occupancy is absolute, as already inferred in previous reports [21, 42] and established that the genetic and phenotypic associations observed in this study reflect variation in glycoform distribution rather than differential site occupancy.

This study provides a mechanistic explanation for C3’s unique glycosylation pattern through MD simulations and enzyme accessibility analysis. The accessibility analysis of the ERManI enzyme, which catalyses the processing of high mannose Man9 glycan into Man8, indicated that the local 3D structure of C3 hinders processing of the glycans into mature glycoforms, and provided a structural rationale for why only high mannose glycans were observed. Both sites on C3 were inaccessible to ERManI, indicating that these sites would be relatively under processed. In the case of N63, even the shapes that were accessible resulted in close contacts between specific protein loops and the aligned ERManI. The degree to which the N917 was encapsulated by the surrounding protein structure (Fig. 6) could also explain how monoglucosylated forms, which are normally retained in the ER as part of the calnexin-calreticulin cycle, might have evaded cleavage and detection of the terminal glucose residue once the protein was folded. The retention of monoglucosylated structures, rather than representing a peculiarity of protein processing, may constitute an evolutionarily conserved regulatory mechanism that enables modulation of C3’s activity through subtle conformational changes affecting glycan accessibility.

Site N917 is proximal to the site where the interaction between C3b (the active form of C3) and factor B takes place, which is very important for further reactions of the complement system [25]. As for the second glycosylation site, a study by Fernandez et al. [26] suggested that N-glycans at N63 on the C3 protein may be involved in the binding of iC3b, which functions as an opsonin, to the CR3 integrin receptor found on different immune cells that have been implicated in various immune cell interactions and trafficking [27]. Their model only included glycans on iC3b, and inferred possible proximity-based interactions with CR3 glycans. Based on our structural modelling results, where glycans were modelled on both iC3b and CR3, the glycans do appear to play an important role in the interaction, since the iC3b-CR3 co-complex greatly reduces the conformational freedom of both glycans at either site, which were found to interact with each other regardless of the type of glycan on N224 (Fig. 7). The 3D structure of a glycosylated form of the iC3b-CR3 co-complex generated here suggests that the C3 glycan at N63 and the CR3 glycan at N224 may directly interact with each other and the underlying protein surface during recognition. If correct, this would imply that the glycoforms present at these sites could influence the strength of the interaction and have consequences for CR3 function. Given that three different glycans (Man7, Man6, Man5) are present at C3_N63, these structural variations could modulate iC3b-CR3 binding strength and consequent immune functions. Importantly, another study showed that binding of specific mannose glycan-binding protein, conglutinin, inhibits iC3b-CR3 complex, which further confirms the importance of glycans for this interaction [43].

Having established the structural basis for C3’s unusual glycosylation, we next consider how these glycoforms vary with physiological state. Our population-scale analysis revealed remarkable stability of C3 glycosylation patterns under physiological conditions, suggesting evolutionary pressure to maintain specific glycoform distributions and providing a robust baseline for evaluating disease-associated changes. Although we found interindividual variation to be relatively limited, C3 N-glycome proved to be affected both by sex and age. Sex-dependent variations in C3 glycosylation were particularly pronounced, with the most substantial differences observed on the C3_N917 site. Higher abundance of Man8 in females, contrasted with the prevalence of Man9 and GlcMan9 structures in males, suggests a sex-specific regulation of C3 glycan processing. Interestingly, this pattern of less processed glycan structures being more prevalent in males is also evident at the C3_N63 site, particularly among younger and middle-aged males. The observed changes in C3 N-glycosylation could potentially contribute to sex-specific differences in complement activation and regulation of immune responses, which in turn might at least partly explain the strong sex bias of certain autoimmune diseases [44]. Moreover, the influence of sex hormones on immune function is well-documented, with estrogens generally enhancing humoral immunity, while androgens and progesterone often acting immunosuppressive [45]. The sex-dependent variations in C3 glycosylation we observed might be influenced by these hormonal differences, with C3 N-glycome possibly being the intermediate for hormonal fine tuning of the complement cascade. Sex-specific glycosylation patterns have previously been reported for IgG [46, 47] and AGP [48], suggesting a systemically regulated mechanism of immune response modulation through protein glycosylation. Quantitatively, sex explains less than 5% of variance in the strongest associated glycan traits for IgG, whereas it accounts for over 10% of variance in C3 and AGP glycan traits. This more pronounced sex-specific glycosylation pattern in C3 and AGP, compared to IgG, likely reflects their roles as acute phase proteins in inflammatory conditions known to exhibit significant sex differences [45].

Regarding age, its effect proved to be less pronounced than that of sex. Notably, the strongest age association was an increase in the monoglucosylated structure. Age-dependent accumulation of this quality control-associated glycan could reflect age-associated changes in immune function, potentially explaining the increased susceptibility to infections and autoimmune disorders observed in older populations [49]. These age-related changes in C3 glycosylation, although modest, present an interesting contrast to the well-documented pronounced effects of aging on IgG glycosylation, where a decrease in galactosylation and increase in bisecting GlcNAc are consistently observed with advancing age. AGP similarly demonstrates limited age-related changes in its glycosylation profile. Quantitatively, age explains over 40% of the variance in the strongest associated glycan traits for IgG [46, 47], whereas it accounts for less than 10% and 5% [48] of the variance in C3 and AGP glycan traits, respectively. While all three proteins exhibit age-related glycosylation changes, these marked differences in magnitude likely reflect their distinct roles in immunity—with IgG glycosylation being more dynamically regulated throughout the lifespan, while C3 and AGP glycosylation maintain relative stability, perhaps due to their crucial roles as upstream mediators in complement activation and acute phase response.

Beyond these environmental and demographic influences, our GWAS revealed substantial genetic control of C3 glycosylation. We revealed six loci significantly associated with C3 glycosylation (Table 2) including genes that encode (1) glycan processing enzymes, (2) proteins involved in maintenance of the protein secretory pathway, (3) proteins involved in the complement pathway or regulation of complement, and (4) regulators of gene expression. Among identified variants, deleterious and possibly damaging variants were those within genes whose protein products are involved in complement regulation and maintenance of the protein secretory pathway. Genetic associations with C3 N-glycans at N917 glycosylation site suggest both local and distal regulatory mechanisms, thus implicating both cis-acting effects and trans-acting regulatory loci: variants at the C3 locus itself directly influence glycosylation of the encoded C3 protein at the N917 site, potentially through effects on protein folding, secretion kinetics, or steric accessibility of glycosylation site, while the remaining loci (TXNDC11, TMED5/DR1, and CFH/CFHR) may modulate C3 glycosylation distally through glycan processing, protein secretory pathway glycan-mediated maintenance, complement pathway interactions or gene transcription regulation. Implication of trans-acting regulatory DR1 locus in C3 N917 N-glycosylation suggests coordinated C3 glycosylation regulation at both transcriptional and post-translational levels. A limitation of our study is that the discovery cohort derives from an island population (Korčula, Croatia), which may not fully represent the genetic diversity of broader European or global populations. However, isolated populations offer increased power for genetic discovery due to reduced genetic and environmental heterogeneity, and this biobank has yielded reproducible findings across multiple complex trait studies [50–52]. We should also acknowledge that the replication cohort was smaller and age-mismatched (children/adolescents vs. adults), limiting statistical power. Furthermore, the use of Immunochip genotyping data in the replication cohort restricted our ability to test all discovered loci; specifically, MAN1C1, TMED5/DR1, and FUT5/AC104532.2 could not be assessed due to incomplete coverage of these genomic regions. Nevertheless, the replication of key associations in an independent Danish cohort and biological plausibility of discovered loci supports the generalizability of our findings across European populations, though further validation in diverse ancestries remains needed.

The most significant genetic associations have been identified with the C3 genetic locus. Two SNPs (rs1047286 and rs2230199, which are in high LD and thus often in population inherited together) that cause amino acid changes in the C3 protein were associated with N-glycan proportions at C3_N917 site. At that site there are 6 different N-glycans, ordered according to how early they form in the glycosylation pathway: GlcMan9, Man9, Man8, Man7, Man6, Man5. SNP rs1047286 causes the cyclic to acyclic amino acid change of Pro to Leu at position 292 in the C3 protein, whereas the SNP rs2230199 causes the change of charged Arg to neutral Gly at position 80. For the N917 glycosylation site our results show that proportions of C3 glycans that are the least processed and earliest in the glycosylation pathway decrease, and the proportions of C3 glycans that are more processed and later in the glycosylation pathway increase in individuals who have rarer amino acid variants. It is possible that these rarer amino acids variants change the C3 protein conformation, thus leading to easier approach to N917 glycosylation site for glycosyltransferases and further glycan processing. However, our structural analysis indicates that the N-glycans of both N63 and N917 site of C3 are distal from residues 80 and 292, suggesting that correlation of the above-mentioned SNPs with the degree of glycan processing observed at N917 site is not a result of changes to processing enzyme site accessibility. One of the possible explanations could be that glycosidases have an increased affinity for N917 site when C3 bears rarer amino acid variants, especially when considering that Pro is a cyclic hydrophobic amino acid and is mostly in the interior of the protein, whereas Arg is charged.

Beyond the C3 locus, our analysis uncovered five additional loci significantly associated with C3 glycosylation. Variants in TXNDC11, encoding a transmembrane protein localized in the ER, were strongly associated with Man9 and Man8 glycoforms at the N917 site. TXNDC11 functions alongside ER degradation, enhancing α-mannosidase-like protein (EDEM2) in glycoprotein endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation (gpERAD), and EDEM2-TXNDC11 complex has been shown to convert Man9 to Man8 in vitro [53, 54]. Our findings suggest that alterations in TXNDC11 expression may modulate Man9 to Man8 early glycan processing step, potentially affecting the retention or exposure of high mannose glycans on C3. This locus also demonstrated strong genetic colocalization with rheumatoid arthritis (PP4 = 91%), highlighting potential role of C3_N917_Man9 glycan in rheumatoid arthritis development. Variants in TMED5 locus, encoding a member of the p24 protein family involved in protein trafficking between the ER and Golgi, were associated with Man7 glycoforms at N917. TMED5 plays a pivotal role in maintaining the secretory pathway’s integrity, ensuring the proper trafficking of glycoproteins like C3. It has also been shown that TMED proteins have a significant role in the unconventional protein secretion (UPS) that avoids the ER-to-Golgi route, with some of these proteins directed to UPS by binding to their high mannose glycans [55–57]. One possible explanation for the existence of solely high mannose and even monoglucosylated glycans on C3 could be trafficking through unconventional secretion pathways that bypass Golgi processing, where complex glycan synthesis normally occurs. TMED proteins, including TMED5 which we identified in our GWAS, have been implicated in directing certain proteins to unconventional secretion routes based on their high mannose glycan content, as demonstrated for proteins like CFTR and pendrin [55]. If C3 partially utilizes such alternative trafficking routes, it would escape the Golgi-resident mannosidases and glycosyltransferases that normally process high mannose structures into complex glycans. Importantly, identified missense variant within TMED5 was estimated in our analysis as deleterious and possibly damaging, suggesting that it may directly impact the TMED5 protein structure and subsequently function. The same locus also implicates DR1, a down-regulator of gene transcription [58], suggesting that glycosylation control may involve coordinated regulation at both the transcriptional and post-translational levels. Notably, this locus demonstrated genetic overlap with inflammatory bowel disease (PP4 = 65%), implicating TMED5/DR1-mediated glycosylation changes in the pathogenesis of gut-related immune disorders. Our analysis identified associations between Man8 glycoform at N917 and variants within CFH/CFHR1/CFHR2/CFHR3/CFHR4 region, genes encoding complement factor H (FH) and its related proteins (FHR). FH inhibits complement alternative pathway, whereas FHR proteins balance complement activation by competing with factor H for binding sites on C3b [59, 60]. Identified missense variant within CFH and CFHR3 were estimated in our analysis as deleterious or possibly damaging, suggesting their possible effect on structure and consequently function of these complement regulators. Importantly, missense variants that have been identified in our study within both C3 and CFH have been previously associated with increased complement activation through alternative pathway [19], thus suggesting that variations in complement genes may modulate complement regulation indirectly by influencing glycan processing or presentation on C3, further linking complement glycosylation to immune system function. The MAN1C1 locus, encoding a mannosidase critical for glycan processing [61], was associated with Man6 and Man7 glycoforms at the N63 site. This locus emphasizes the direct role of glycan processing enzymes in shaping C3 glycosylation. Variants within FUT5/AC104532.2 locus were associated with glycoforms at the N63 site, specifically influencing the relative abundances of Man5 and Man6. FUT5 encodes an enzyme involved in the formation of complex glycans [62]; however, these complex structures were not found on the C3 protein. The most significant association for this locus was with variant located upstream of the long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) AC104532.2. The correlation between lncRNAs and glycosylation has been previously reported; interestingly, levels of lncNEAT1 has been negatively correlated with expression level of another alpha 1,2-mannosidase, MAN1A1 [63]. This suggests that lncRNA variants may affect upstream glycan biosynthetic pathways, indirectly altering C3 glycoform distributions.

The association between C3_N63_Man5 and diabetes prevalence observed in our study, which notably strengthened after BMI correction directly parallels our previous observations in type 1 diabetes of significantly reduced Man5GlcNAc₂ at the N63 site in recent-onset type 1 diabetes patients from the Danish registry. The convergence of C3_N63_Man5 as a diabetes-associated glycoform suggests that the degree of C3 mannose trimming may be mechanistically relevant to diabetes pathophysiology. Man5GlcNAc₂ represents the most extensively processed high-mannose structure at site N63, generated through sequential ER and Golgi α-mannosidase activity. Reduced Man5GlcNAc₂ abundance, reflecting incomplete mannose trimming, has been proposed as a marker of ER stress, which is characteristic of both pancreatic β-cells under metabolic pressure and renal tubular cells in diabetic nephropathy. Notably, the variant rs1047286, which showed the strongest genetic association with C3 glycosylation herein in the general adult population of the Croatian island Korčula, was a novel association at genome-wide significance with plasma oligomannose N-glycans in recent-onset type 1 diabetes patients from the Danish registry [17] and has also been suggested to have a role in type 2 diabetes combined with coronary artery disease in a Chinese population [64]. Additionally, C3 rs2230199, which was also identified in our study to regulate C3 N-glycosylation and which is in high LD with rs1047286 (r2 = 0.82), was previously shown to be associated with an increased risk of type 1 diabetes development in a study population comprising participants from Finland, Sweden, Germany and the United States [18], further supporting the relevance of this finding in diabetes. This cross-ancestry, cross-diabetes-type replication underscores the potential importance of C3 glycosylation as a molecular link between complement function and diabetes susceptibility. Our colocalization analysis further revealed shared genetic architecture between C3 glycosylation and other immune-mediated conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis at the TXNDC11 locus and inflammatory bowel disease at the TMED5/DR1 locus (Fig. 5). Given that type 1 diabetes shares autoimmune etiology with these conditions and that complement dysregulation has been implicated in all three diseases, these findings suggest that C3 glycosylation may represent a broader intermediate phenotype connecting genetic variation to inflammatory and autoimmune disease risk through modulation of complement activity. It should be noted that these remarks are based on association and colocalization analyses and whether C3 glycosylation acts as a causal mediator or a correlated biomarker of shared regulatory architecture at these loci remains to be determined through future functional studies.

Conclusions

This study provides the first population-scale, site-specific analysis of complement C3 N-glycosylation, showing that its unusual high-mannose phenotype is shaped by both genetic variation and physiological factors including sex, age, and metabolic status. Genetic colocalization links C3 glycoforms to rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease, positioning C3 N-glycosylation as a candidate mechanistic intermediate between genetic variation and immune diseases. More broadly, our work identifies C3 N-glycosylation as a genetically and environmentally regulated checkpoint in complement activation with implications for precision medicine approaches in immune and metabolic diseases.

Methods

Study participants

This study used fasting plasma samples collected in 2007 from the island of Korčula’s (Croatia) adult population obtained from the “10,001 Dalmatians” biobank [65], used in numerous studies [50, 66, 67]. Participants were from the town of Korčula and the villages Lumbarda, Žrnovo, and Račišće. All participants were aged 18 or above and provided informed consent prior to their involvement. The study protocol received approval from the Ethical Committee of the Medical School, University of Zagreb, as well as the Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee for Scotland, and adhered strictly to applicable ethical guidelines. To ensure optimal sample quality, fasting plasma samples were collected and promptly processed on-site, and then stored at −80 °C.

Experimental design

This research took an observational approach, employing convenience sampling as the sampling method. The sample size was not predetermined using statistical calculations but rather based on participant availability. Samples were processed in batches using 96-well plates. The experimental design was predetermined and incorporated sex and age information through blocking. Each plate contained technical replicates of a standard plasma sample and blank samples for quality control and batch correction. The standard plasma sample used for the analysis was generated by pooling samples from the cohort. Moreover, approximately 10% of the samples on each plate were duplicates to evaluate method variability. Sample analysis was conducted blindly, and all samples were analysed with a uniform set of techniques.

C3 glycoprotein enrichment and digestion

C3 was enriched from a 10 μl starting volume of human plasma using a slightly optimized version of a protocol described previously [21]. Briefly, plasma samples were diluted in binding buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, 0.5 M NaCl, 1 mM MnCl2, 1 mM CaCl2, pH 7.4) and loaded onto a conditioned Concanavalin A (Con A)-Sepharose 4B (Global Life Sciences Solutions) resin slurry. After a 2 h incubation at 4 °C, the lectin matrix was washed with binding buffer and bound glycoproteins were eluted with 200 mM methyl α-D-mannopyranoside (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.1 M acetic acid [(Merck KGaA), pH 3.0]. The washing steps were carried out on a vacuum manifold (Pall Corp), while the elution step was done by low-speed centrifugation. The resulting eluates were promptly dried using a SpeedVac Vacuum Concentrator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) until subsequent digestion. Dried samples were resuspended in 15% (v/v) 2-propanol (Merck KGaA) in 0.1 M ammonium acetate (Acros Organics) and denatured by incubation at 60 °C for 10 min [52]. Endoproteinase Glu-C (Sigma-Aldrich) was added in a 1:50 protease:protein ratio (w/w) to digest the samples for 18 h at 37 °C.

Regarding the site occupancy analysis, dried samples were resuspended in 15% (v/v) 2-propanol (Merck KGaA) in 0.1 M ammonium bicarbonate (Sigma-Aldrich) and denatured by incubation at 60 °C for 10 min [52], after which endoproteinase Glu-C (Sigma-Aldrich) was added in a 1:25 protease:protein ratio (w/w) to digest the samples for 18 h at 37 °C.

Glycopeptide enrichment using HILIC-SPE

After the digestion, glycopeptide enrichment was done using Chromabond HILIC silica beads (Macherey–Nagel GmbH & Co) [68]. First, a 50 mg/ml suspension of HILIC beads was prepared in 0.1% (v/v) TFA (Sigma-Aldrich) on a magnetic stirrer. Afterwards, 5 mg of the suspension was added to each well of the Orochem OF1100 filter plate (Orochem Technologies Inc). Prior to sample application, all wells underwent two rounds of prewashing with 0.1% TFA (v/v), after which they were equilibrated with 0.1% TFA in 85% acetonitrile (ACN) (v/v) (VWR International). The samples were diluted with 0.1% TFA in ACN to achieve a final concentration of 85% ACN and were then applied to the conditioned beads. After that, unbound impurities were washed away with 0.1% TFA in 85% ACN (v/v). Washing steps were carried out on a vacuum manifold (Pall Corp). Enriched glycopeptides were eluted with 0.1% TFA into a clean skirted PCR plate through low-speed centrifugation. Finally, the eluates were dried down using a SpeedVac Vacuum Concentrator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stored at − 20 °C until analysis.

Cleanup step for the site occupancy analysis is described below under a separate section.

LC–ESI–MS analysis of enriched glycopeptides

Glycopeptide analysis was conducted using an ACQUITY H-class ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (Waters) coupled to a Synapt G2-Si ESI-QTOF (Waters) mass spectrometer. The instruments were under the control of MassLynx v.4.1 software (Waters). Dried glycopeptides were dissolved in 50 μl of ultrapure water and injected onto ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (130 Å, 1.7 µm, 1 mm × 150 mm, Waters). Column temperature was maintained at 30 °C, and the flow rate was set at 0.4 ml/min, with 0.1% FA (v/v) (Sigma Aldrich) as solvent A, and 0.1% FA in ACN (v/v) as solvent B. Separation method used a linear gradient of 12–19% of solvent B in a 16-min run, after which a washing step with 80% of solvent B was performed for 3 min, and equilibration step with 12% of solvent B for additional 6 min, resulting in a total running time of 25 min.

The mass spectrometer was operated in a positive ion mode with conditions set as follows: capillary voltage 3 kV, sampling cone voltage 40 V, source temperature 120 °C, desolvation temperature 350 °C, desolvation gas flow 600 l/h. Mass spectra were recorded from 500 to 2500 m/z at a frequency of 1 Hz.

LC–ESI–MS/MS method for site occupancy analysis is described below under a separate section.

Glycoproteomic data extraction and processing

Due to the slightly optimized sample preparation protocol, we were able to find three additional C3 glycoforms which remained undetected in our previous work [21]. Therefore, for data extraction we defined two chromatographic peak clusters: one with 3, and another with 6 glycoforms. It should be noted that all glycan assignments represent compositional identifications based on intact mass and fragmentation experiments, interpreted within the context of the well-established high-mannose processing pathway. Glycopeptide quantification was performed using MS1 intact masses. Glycan structures at both sites were previously confirmed by MS/MS fragmentation analysis [21]. Automated relative quantification of the MS data was performed using LaCy Tools operating under a free software license (version 1.0.1, b.9), as described previously [69]. Before glycoproteomic data quantification, original data files were converted to mzXML open data format using ProteoWizard MSConvert tool (version 3.0.19208). Chromatograms were aligned based on the retention times of the five most abundant glycopeptide signals. Targeted peak integration encompassed doubly, triply, quadruply, and quintuply charged species, with summed spectra integration ensuring coverage of at least 99% of the theoretical isotopic pattern. Quality control parameters including mass accuracy, deviation from the theoretical isotopic pattern, and signal-to-noise ratio, were automatically computed for each analyte in every sample. Data quality assessment relied on average quality control values calculated across all analysed samples, considering each glycoform and charge state separately. Inclusion criteria were defined as follows: mass accuracy below 30 ppm, isotopic pattern quality below 25%, and signal-to-noise ratio exceeding 15. For every glycoform, only charge states meeting these three criteria were summed and quantified. Finally, summed extracted data was normalized per glycosylation site, revealing the relative abundance of each glycoform.

Glycoproteomic statistical data analysis

Extracted LC–MS data underwent batch correction to mitigate experimental variation. To address the non-symmetric glycoform distributions and to accommodate the multiplicative aspect of batch effects, measurements were first log-transformed. Utilizing the ComBat method from the R package “sva” [70], batch correction was conducted on the log-transformed measurements, considering technical variation (plate number) as a batch covariate. The estimated batch effects were then subtracted from the log-transformed measurements, resulting in measurements that were adjusted for experimental noise.

To examine associations between glycoforms and biochemical or physiological traits, either a general linear regression model or Pearson correlation test was employed, depending on the specific analysis. When conducting regression models, age and sex (and BMI, if applicable) were included as additional covariates, unless age and sex were the primary variables of interest. Before regression analysis, glycoform variables, as well as quantitative biochemical and physiological traits, were transformed to a standard normal distribution (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) using an inverse transformation of ranks to achieve normality (implemented using the “GenABEL” R package [71], specifically the rntransform() function). By employing rank-transformed variables, the standardized variances of the transformed glycoform variables facilitated comparability of estimated effects across different cohorts. In Pearson correlation tests, glycoform measurements were adjusted for age, sex, and BMI (if applicable) before conducting the correlation analysis. The false discovery rate was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, implemented with the p.adjust() function using the “BH” method. All data analysis and visualization were performed using the R programming language (version 4.0.2).

C3 N-glycosylation site occupancy analysis

Site occupancy analysis of C3 glycoprotein was done using human plasma standard made from pooled plasma of the participants from the studied population. Four technical replicates of the PNGase F treated samples were analysed, together with four untreated samples which were used as a control for spontaneous deamidation. Sample preparation as well as LC–MS method needed to be modified to properly analyse non-glycosylated and deglycosylated peptides. Regarding the sample preparation, Glu-C digestion was stopped with 10-min incubation at 65 °C, after which 5U of PNGase F (Promega Corporation) was added to the samples and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Peptide cleanup step was performed using Chromabond C18 silica beads (Macherey–Nagel GmbH & Co), following similar procedure described for glycopeptide enrichment on HILIC beads. Briefly, a 50 mg/ml suspension of C18 beads was prepared in 0.1% (v/v) TFA (Sigma-Aldrich) on a magnetic stirrer, and 5 mg of the suspension was added to each well of the Orochem OF1100 filter plate (Orochem Technologies Inc). Prior to sample application, all wells underwent two rounds of prewashing with 0.1% TFA (v/v) in 80% ACN, after which they were equilibrated with 0.1% TFA (v/v). The samples were diluted with 0.1% TFA (v/v) ten times and were applied to the conditioned beads. Impurities were washed away with 0.1% TFA (v/v), and the peptides eluted with 0.1% TFA in 80% ACN into a clean skirted PCR plate. Finally, the eluates were dried down using a SpeedVac Vacuum Concentrator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stored at − 20 °C until analysis.

LC–ESI–MS/MS method for site occupancy analysis was performed on the same system used for analysis of the enriched glycopeptides. However, to properly analyse the non-glycosylated and deglycosylated peptides, LC conditions were changed. Separation method used a linear gradient of 4–50% of solvent B (0.1% TFA (v/v) in ACN) in a 60-min run, after which a washing step with 80% of solvent B was performed for 3 min, and equilibration step with 96% of solvent A (0.1% TFA (v/v)) for additional 7 min, resulting in a total running time of 70 min. Also, in order to confirm the deglycosylated and non-glycosylated peptides, a MS/MS spectra were also recorded, with collision energy ramp used for the fragmentation (LM CE Ramp Start 15 V, LM CE Ramp End 30 V, HM CE Ramp Start 60 V, HM CE Ramp End 80 V).

Data was extracted in the same way as for the Korčula cohort, with the inclusion of both non-glycosylated and deamidated peptide per glycosylation site. Summed extracted data was compared between PNGase F treated and untreated samples.

Discovery GWAS

GWAS was performed in 801 individuals from the general population of the Croatian island Korčula on Haplotype Reference Consortium (HRC) imputed genotypes, assuming an additive linear model of association (genome build: GRCh37). Genotyping platforms used were Illumina HumanHap s370CNV DUO/QUAD Phase 1 and Illumina HumanOmniExpress Exome. ID call rate was set to > 97%, SNP call rate to > 98%, HWE p-value > 1e-6, and minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 1%. SHAPEIT2/Sanger software was used for imputation of genetic data. Linear mixed model was fit to adjust for age and sex, while also accounting for the genomic kinship. This step was performed using polygenic function in GenABEL R package [71], followed by testing the association between SNPs and glycan traits using the RegScan software [72]. 99% of variation in 9 glycan traits was explained with 5 principal components, thus this number was used to further adjust the genome-wide significance threshold (5 × 10–8/5 = 1 × 10–8). FUMA v1.6.1 [73] was used for GWAS loci definition and results exploration. SNP2GENE function in FUMA first identified SNPs which passed the genome-wide significance threshold, further filtering the independent SNPs based on the LD threshold (r2 < 0.6). LD estimates were based on 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European reference panel [74]. For each independent significant SNP, SNP2GENE defined the boundaries of the genomic locus based on the extent of LD (r2 > 0.6) and MAF > 0.01. The maximum distance of 250 kb was used to merge LD blocks into a single genomic locus. SNPs with r2 ≥ 0.6 with the independent significant SNPs were included in further annotations and gene prioritisation.

Replication analysis

Following the discovery GWAS, replication analysis was performed to validate genome-wide significant results from the discovery phase. For this purpose, out of nine C3 N-glycopeptides determined in the discovery cohort, data for six C3 N-glycopeptides (Man5, Man6 and Man7 on C3_N63, and GlcMan9, Man9 and Man8 on N917) was available, which we previously obtained with LC–MS from the 84 unaffected and 61 affected children [21] from the cohort of European descent from the Danish Registry of Childhood and Adolescent Diabetes (DanDiabKids) [15]. In the replication cohort genotype data, which we previously determined [17], were available for three loci (1: 196674982–196910030, 16: 11764329–11841539, 19: 6710782–6724340) out of six that passed genome-wide significance in the discovery cohort. Genotype data for 19: 6710782–6724340 locus (top SNP rs1047286) were available in both affected (N = 59) and unaffected children (N = 82), whereas for other two loci genotype data were only available for affected children (N = 59). The significance threshold was set to p < 0.05/3 = 1.7 × 10–2, where 3 is the number of loci available for testing in the replication cohort. If in the replication cohort the same top SNP as in the discovery cohort was not available, SNP in LD (R2 > 0.7) with the top SNP identified in the discovery cohort was used. Association was defined as replicated if effect direction was consistent between the discovery and replication analyses (glycopeptide either increasing or decreasing with the same allele). List of SNPs that were tested in the replication cohort is presented in the Additional file 1: Table S13.

Gene prioritization

Candidate genes for each locus were prioritized based on genomic position (closest gene based on ANNOVAR annotations), functional consequence-based prioritisation (genes whose missense variants were associated with C3 N-glycosylation), colocalization of SNP associations with gene expression in whole blood and with C3 N-glycosylation (pleiotropy with gene expression) and biological relevance for glycosylation processes.

Functional consequences

SIFT and Polyphen-2 algorithms were used to estimate possible functional consequences of associated variants, as applied in the Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) by Ensembl [28]. SIFT gives a score and a qualitative prediction (score < 0.05 is termed deleterious, otherwise tolerated). Polyphen-2 also provides a score and a qualitative prediction (score > 0.05 predicted as possibly damaging, score > 0.91 as probably damaging, otherwise benign or unknown).

Colocalization with gene expression in whole blood and with diseases

Approximate Bayes Factor (ABF) method [75] was used to determine the colocalization of C3 glycome GWAS signals with gene expression in whole blood, as applied in the coloc package [76] in R [77]. For this purpose, whole blood eQTL data was acquired from the publicly available eQTLgen dataset [78]. Tissue-specific eQTL data (liver eQTL, GTEx liver dataset) was not used due to low number of SNPs in the associated regions to be able to accurately determine whether there is colocalization.

Colocalization with diseases was performed by using GWAS summary statistics from the GWAS catalogue (accessed in March 2023) of relevant (newest or largest) GWASs on type 2 diabetes and autoimmune diseases that were chosen to cover each of the three autoimmune disease groups: gastrointestinal tract diseases, rheumatic and systemic diseases, and allergic diseases [29]. The full list of diseases in colocalization analysis and links for summary statistics download is available in Additional file 5: Table S14. Five posterior probabilities are given as the output of the method, referring to each of the five hypotheses: H0) there is no association with either of the two traits, H1) association with trait 1, but not with trait 2, H2) association with trait 2, but not with trait 1, H3) association with both trait 1 and trait 2, but two independent causal variants and H4) association with both trait 1 and trait 2 and one shared causal variant. For colocalization (and thus strong support for the gene prioritization), and as evidence of high confidence for pleiotropy between C3 glycosylation and diseases or gene expression the threshold of 65% for PP4 (probability of the shared underlying causal variant for both traits) was used. It is important to highlight that high PP4 designates at least one shared causal variant and low PP4 does not mean that two phenotypes do not share one, especially if the PP3 (probability of both phenotypes being associated with the region but having different causal variants) is high.

3D modelling and molecular dynamics simulations

The glycoprotein builder available within gmml2 (https://github.com/GLYCAM-Web/gmml2 version 0.0.1) was used to add a Man9 glycan to the N63 and N917 sites in a crystal structure of C3 (PDBID: 2A73 [79]). The structures were prepared for energy minimization and MD simulation with the tleap module of AmberTools [80]. An octahedral box of Tip5p waters [81] was placed around the glycoprotein with an 8 Å edge buffer and counterions to neutralize the system. GLYCAM_06j-1 force field [82] was used for the glycans and AMBERff19SB force field [83] for the protein and ions. Five replicates of the initial C3 structure were then subjected to MD simulations using an established 9-step equilibration protocol [84] with a Langevin thermostat [85] and a production time of 1 µs, resulting in 5 µs of cumulative simulation.

The glycoprotein builder was also used to add a Man6 glycan to the N63 of iC3b and both a Man9 and biantennary N-glycan to the N224 of CR3 (H-chain) in a crystal structure of their co-complex (PDBID: 7AKK [26]).

MD trajectory processing and analysis

The cpptraj [86] module of AmberTools was used to extract 1,000 snapshots at regular 1 ns intervals from each MD simulation trajectory as pdb files. The ERManI accessibility analysis was performed as described previously [87]. Briefly, UCSF Chimera [88] v1.9 was used to align the crystal structure of ERManI (PDBID: 5KIJ [89]) onto each N-glycan via the ring atoms of the common Manβ1-4GlcNAc moiety. The number of overlapping backbone atoms were counted and snapshots with > 5 overlapping atoms were deemed inaccessible to the enzyme.
