Serge Cantat, Romain Dujardin

Abstract
. We investigate the multiplier rigidity problem for polynomial automorphisms of 𝐂 2 \mathbf{C}^{2} . A first result states that a complex Hénon map of given degree is determined up to finitely many choices by its multiplier spectrum, or more generally by the unstable multipliers of its saddle periodic points. This is the counterpart in this setting of a classical result of McMullen for one-dimensional rational maps. For compositions of Hénon maps, the same rigidity holds provided the multi-degree and the multi-Jacobian are fixed. As in McMullen’s theorem, this follows from the nonexistence of stable algebraic families in the corresponding parameter space. This in turn relies on precise asymptotic bounds for the Lyapunov exponents of the maximal entropy measure along diverging families.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview of the problem

The multiplier rigidity problem
asks whether a dynamical system in a certain class is determined by the
multipliers (or eigenvalues) of its periodic orbits. It is part of a more general landscape
of spectral rigidity problems, which has many different incarnations in geometry and dynamics (see
Wilkinson’s recent monograph
[
39
]
for an overview).

In the dynamics of one-dimensional rational functions, multipliers are often viewed as algebraic
functions on moduli spaces and used to describe their geometry; (local)
multiplier rigidity means that these functions define (local) coordinates.
This point of view was popularized by Milnor
[
31
,
32
]

and Silverman
[
36
]
(the term “multiplier spectrum” apparently first appeared in

[
33
]
).
In this setting, parameter spaces are finite-dimensional. Therefore,
compared to classical spectral rigidity problems in dynamics, the results and
methods have a more algebraic flavor. The local multiplier rigidity problem
was settled in an early paper of McMullen
[
30
]
.
Still, despite recent breakthroughs by Ji and Xie
[
26
,
27
]

and Huguin
[
24
]
, the complete characterization of pairs of isospectral
rational maps remains open.

In this paper, we address similar questions for polynomial automorphisms of
\mathbf{C}^{2}
.
In this context, the multiplier rigidity problem and some of its variants
were already considered in the foundational paper of Friedland and Milnor
[
22
]
;
and results on the global analytic rigidity of polynomial automorphisms
were recently obtained in
[
7
,
10
,
11
]
.

1.2.
Classification of polynomial automorphisms

In order to state our results, we first need to recall a few basic facts.
We denote by
\mathsf{Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{2})
the group of polynomial automorphisms of
\mathbf{C}^{2}
.
The dynamical degree of
f\in\mathsf{Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{2})
is

(1.1)

\lambda_{1}(f):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\deg(f^{n})^{1/n}\in[1,\infty).

This number turns out to be a positive integer and is invariant under conjugacy.
If
\lambda_{1}(f)=1
,
f
is said
elementary
and its dynamics is easy to analyze.
When
\lambda_{1}(f)>1
, we say that
f
is
loxodromic
. A
Hénon map

h\colon\mathbf{C}^{2}\to\mathbf{C}^{2}
is a polynomial automorphism of the form

(1.2)

h(x,y)=(ay+p(x),x)

for some
a\in\mathbf{C}^{*}
and some
p\in\mathbf{C}[x]
of degree
\geq 2
. Hénon maps are the primary examples of loxodromic automorphisms:
\lambda_{1}(h)=\deg(p)
.

According to
[
22
]
, if
f
is loxodromic there exists a sequence of integers
(d_{k},\ldots,d_{1})\in\mathbf{N}_{\geq 2}^{k}
, a sequence of non-zero complex numbers

(a_{k},\ldots,a_{1})\in(\mathbf{C}^{*})^{k}
and a sequence of monic and centered polynomials

p=(p_{k},\ldots,p_{1})\in\mathbf{C}[x]^{k}
of respective degrees
d_{i}
such that
f
is conjugate to the composition

g=h_{k}\circ\cdots\circ h_{1}
,
where
h_{i}(x,y)=(a_{i}y+p_{i}(x),x)
. We refer to the decomposition
g=h_{k}\circ\cdots\circ h_{1}
as a
Friedland-Milnor normal form
of
f
.
The Jacobians of
f
and
g
are both equal to
\jac(g)=(-1)^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}
.
The degree of
g
is
d=\prod_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}
, and
\deg(g^{n})=\deg(g)^{n}
, hence
\lambda_{1}(f)=\lambda_{1}(g)=d
.
In particular, if
\lambda_{1}(f)
is a prime number then
k=1
and
f

is conjugate to a monic and centered Hénon map.
By definition,

\mathbf{d}=(d_{k},\ldots,d_{1})
is the
multidegree
of
g
and
\mathbf{a}=(a_{k},\ldots,a_{1})
is its
multi-Jacobian
.
We let
\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d}}

(resp.
\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{a}}
)
be the space of compositions of
k
monic and centered Hénon mappings of
multidegree
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbf{N}_{\geq 2}^{k}
(resp. and multi-Jacobian
\mathbf{a}
).
As an algebraic variety,
\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d}}
is isomorphic to
(\mathbf{C}^{*})^{k}\times\mathbf{C}^{d_{1}-1}\times\ldots\mathbf{C}^{d_{k}-1}
. For
k=1
we
simply write
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\mathbf{d}}=\mathcal{H}^{1}_{d}
.

The normal form is not unique, but the defect of uniqueness is well understood: it is unique up to permutation of the factors
h_{i}
and a diagonal action of the group

\mathbb{U}_{d-1}
of
(d-1)^{\rm th}

roots of unity (see §
5.3
or
[
22
]
for details).
Thus, coming back to
f
,
the multidegree and the multi-Jacobian define conjugacy invariants only up to a finite group action.

1.3.
Multiplier rigidity

If
z
is a periodic point of (exact) period
n
of
f\colon\mathbf{C}^{2}\to\mathbf{C}^{2}
,
the
multipliers

\lambda_{1}(z)
,
\lambda_{2}(z)
of
z

are the (unordered) eigenvalues of
Df^{n}_{z}
.
A convenient way to encode the multipliers is by the

trace

\tr(Df^{n}_{z})
and the Jacobian
\jac(f^{n})
,
which satisfy

(1.3)

\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=\tr(Df^{n}_{z})\;\text{ and } \;\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}=\jac(f^{n}).

If
z
is of saddle type, the eigenvalues can be distinguished and are denoted by

\lambda^{s}(z)
and
\lambda^{u}(z)
, with

\left|\lambda^{s}(z)\right|<1<\left|\lambda^{u}(z)\right|
.
The
multiplier spectrum

(resp. the
trace spectrum
) of
f
is the set of all multipliers (resp. traces) of all periodic cycles of
f
, organized by their periods. The
unstable multiplier spectrum

is the set of unstable eigenvalues
\lambda^{u}
of saddle periodic cycles, organized by their periods.
We refer to §
3.2
for precise definitions.

Theorem A
.

A complex Hénon map
f(x,y)=(ay+p(x),x)

is determined
up to finitely many choices by its trace spectrum (resp. its unstable multiplier spectrum).

In particular any complex Hénon map
f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{d}

has the following local multiplier rigidity property:
any
f
sufficiently close to
f_{0}
with the same trace
(resp. unstable multiplier) spectrum is equal to
f_{0}
.
An interesting point in the theorem is that the knowledge of the Jacobian
is not necessary for this spectral characterization.

Let
S(f_{0})
be the number of maps
f\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{d}
with the same multiplier (or trace) spectrum as
f_{0}
.
Theorem
A
says that
S(f_{0})
is finite. In fact,

S
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{d}
and
S(f_{0})=1
for a generic
f_{0}
(see
Theorem
3.7
and Theorem
4.2
). Thus,
a generic Hénon map is actually
determined up to conjugacy
by its multiplier spectrum. It is an open problem whether this holds for every loxodromic automorphism
(cf.
[
27
, Conjecture 1.7]
for one-dimensional rational maps).

By Noetherianity,
for every
d
there exists
P=P(d)

such that a complex Hénon map
f
is determined
up to finitely many choices by the traces of its periodic points up to period

P
(see Theorem
3.7
). Friedland and Milnor proved that for
d=2
or 3, the knowledge of the
traces of the
fixed
points together with the Jacobian determines the
conjugacy class of
f
(see
[
22
, §7]
).
For the analogous problem of the determination of a polynomial
p:\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{C}
by its periodic point multipliers, Huguin showed in
[
24
]
that
P=2
is sufficient.
We will give in Section
4
a quick proof of Theorem
A
when the
Jacobian is different from
-1
(i.e.
a\neq 1
): it is
based on Huguin’s theorem, using the specific formula defining the Hénon map
f
, and it shows that
P=2
is enough for these maps as well.
On the other hand the family
(f_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\mathbf{C}}
defined by

(1.4)

f_{\lambda}(x,y)=(y+(x^{2}-\lambda^{2})^{2},x)

is a non-trivial family of Hénon maps of Jacobian
-1

whose periodic data is constant in period 1 and 2 (see Example
4.3
).
This shows that Huguin’s method cannot be applied to this setting.
We will present a more conceptual
(and complicated) proof of Theorem
A
, which
works in Jacobian
-1
and
yields the following result.

Theorem B
.

The conjugacy class of a loxodromic automorphism is determined, up to finitely many possibilities, by: (a) its multidegree, (b) its multi-jacobian, and (c)
its trace spectrum (resp. its unstable multiplier spectrum).

Note that in Theorem
B
the multi-Jacobian is fixed.
We can also address the
C^{1}
rigidity problem
suggested by Friedland-Milnor in
[
22
, Thm 7.5]
, as well as the global
real-analytic rigidity result of
[
11
, Thm 7.1]
.

Theorem C
.

The
C^{1}
conjugacy class of any complex Hénon map
f_{0}
of given degree (resp. any composition
f_{0}
of Hénon maps
with given multidegree and multi-Jacobian) is finite.
In particular in
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{1}_{d}
or
\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{a}}
the following rigidity property holds: for
f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
,
if
f\in\mathcal{H}
is
C^{1}
conjugate to
f_{0}
in a neighborhood of its Julia set
and is sufficiently close to
f_{0}
, then
f=f_{0}
.

1.4.
Stable algebraic families

Following a beautiful idea of
McMullen
[
30
]
,
the results above
will follow from a global structural stability statement.

Theorem D
.

Any stable irreducible algebraic family of complex Hénon maps is trivial. Likewise, any stable
irreducible algebraic family of compositions of Hénon maps with fixed multi-Jacobian is trivial.

By an
algebraic family of (compositions of) Hénon maps
, we mean an algebraic variety together with an algebraic map
\Lambda\to\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d}}
for some
k\geq 1
and
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbf{N}^{k}_{\geq 2}
.
The family is
trivial
if its image is reduced to a point. Most of the time, a family can be identified with its image in
\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d}}
.
To derive Theorems
A
,
B
and
C
from
Theorem
D
, we argue as in
[
30
]
:
given
f_{0}
in
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{1}_{d}
or
\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{a}}
,
we consider the set
\Lambda(f_{0})\subset\mathcal{H}

of mappings with the same trace (resp. unstable multiplier)
spectrum and show that it is a stable algebraic family. For the trace spectrum,
\Lambda(f_{0})
is obviously algebraic and
the difficulty is to prove the stability, because we do not specify the Jacobian.
Conversely, for the unstable multiplier spectrum, stability is not an issue, what is delicate
is that
\Lambda(f_{0})
is only a priori a countable
intersection of semi-algebraic subsets.
The details are in Proposition
3.3
.
Actually for the unstable multipliers there is an additional finiteness issue that
can only be resolved after Theorem
E
is established (see §
6.3
).

Note that the word ‘stability’ has not been defined yet.
In first approximation, this refers to the notion of
weak stability defined by Lyubich and the second author in
[
14
]
,
however a subtlety arises whether we authorize a small set of periodic points to bifurcate or not. This issue is
analyzed in §
2.2
.

1.5.
Divergence of Lyapunov exponents

The starting point of the proof of Theorem
D
is that a non-trivial stable algebraic family
\Lambda

is unbounded in parameter space, so it should exhibit some degeneration phenomena.
We analyze these degenerations by taking inspiration from the
theory of polynomials in one variable. In that case, there is
a simple approach to Theorem
D
based on the Lyapunov
exponent of the equilibrium measure.

Indeed, let
\mathcal{P}_{d}=\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbf{C})
be the space of affine conjugacy classes of polynomials of degree
d
in one complex variable.
Branner and Hubbard
[
9
]

have first studied the asymptotic properties of the dynamics when
p
tends to infinity in

\mathcal{P}_{d}
(other relevant sources for us include
[
12
,
24
]
).
Define the
maximal escape rate
of a polynomial
p
by

(1.5)

M(p)=\max\left\{G_{p}(c)\;;\;\ p^{\prime}(c)=0\right\},

where
G_{p}(z)=\lim_{n\to\infty}d^{-n}\log^{+}\left|p^{n}(z)\right|
is the dynamical Green function.
The quantity
M(p)
is invariant under conjugacy so it defines a function on
\mathcal{P}_{d}
.
It is shown in
[
9
]
that
M
defines a proper map
\mathcal{P}_{d}\to\mathbf{R}_{+}

and provides a good measurement of the amount of degeneracy of
p
.
Most importantly for us, the Manning-Przytycki formula
[
29
,
35
]

(1.6)

\chi(\mu_{p})=\log d+\sum_{p^{\prime}(c)=0}G_{p}(c)

for the Lyapunov exponent

\chi(\mu_{p})

of the equilibrium measure
\mu_{p}=\Delta G_{p}

implies that

(1.7)

\log d+M(p)\leq\chi(\mu_{p})\leq\log d+(d-1)M(p).

In a stable family the multipliers of repelling points move holomorphically and take values in
\mathbf{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, thus if the family is irreducible and algebraic they must remain constant. On the other hand, by the equidistribution theory of periodic points,
for large
n
the Lyapunov exponent
\frac{1}{n}\log\left|\lambda^{u}\right|
of a typical point of period
n

is close to
\chi(\mu_{p})
. These two facts and the lower estimate
in (
1.7
) imply that
M(p)
is bounded on the family. Since

M
is proper on
\mathcal{P}_{d}
, we conclude that a stable algebraic family must be
bounded in the affine variety
\mathcal{P}_{d}
, hence a finite set.

Remark 1.1
.

Huguin’s approach in
[
24
]
is based on an
estimate similar to the lower bound in (
1.7
)
for the Lyapunov exponents of periodic points of period
1
and
2

(see
[
24
, Cor A.1]
). This estimate from one variable dynamics
plays an important role in Section
4
, where we treat Hénon maps with
a\neq 1
.
The example in (
1.4
) shows that the corresponding
result is not true in
\mathsf{Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{2})
.

In Section
6
we design a two-dimensional version of this argument.
If
f=h_{k}\circ\cdots\circ h_{1}
is a composition of monic and centered Hénon maps as in
§
1.2
(with possibly
k=1
),
we define

(1.8)

M(f)=\max(M(p_{i}),i=1,\ldots,k).

(Because of the
\mathbb{U}_{d-1}
action alluded to in §
1.2
,
M(f)
is not invariant under conjugacy, we refer to §
5.3
for a discussion and a remedy.)
According to
[
4
]
,
f
admits a canonical equilibrium measure
\mu_{f}
, whose Lyapunov exponents
satisfy

(1.9)

\chi^{-}(\mu_{f})\leq-\log d<0<\log d\leq\chi^{+}(\mu_{f}).

In
[
5
]
, Bedford and Smillie provide an exact expression for
\chi^{\pm}(\mu_{f})
which is
analogous to the Manning-Przytycki Formula (
1.6
) and involves the delicate
notion of unstable critical point (see §
6.1.5
for a presentation).
We use it to obtain the following theorem.

Theorem E
.

Let
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbf{N}_{\geq 2}^{k}
be a multidegree and
\mathbf{a}\in(\mathbf{C}^{*})^{k}
be a multi-Jacobian, for some
k\geq 1
.
There exists positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}

depending only on
(\mathbf{d},\mathbf{a})

such that

C_{1}M(f)\leq\chi^{+}(\mu_{f})\leq C_{2}M(f),

for all
f
in
\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{a}}
with
M(f)\geq 1
.

From the relation
\chi^{+}(\mu_{f})+\chi^{-}(\mu_{f})=\log\left|\jac(f)\right|
,
a similar result holds for
\chi^{-}(\mu_{f})
.
Our estimate is actually much more precise, and allows the
multi-Jacobian to diverge at a controlled rate with respect to
M(f)
: see Propositions
6.1
and
6.10
.
The proof is perturbative in nature (even if we allow for pretty large perturbations),
and ultimately relies on the one-dimensional estimates of
[
9
]
, together with
a geometric analysis of unstable critical points in the spirit of
[
17
]
.

To deduce Theorem
D
from Theorem
E
, consider a stable
algebraic family
\Lambda
. As for polynomial maps, we observe that
the multipliers of saddle periodic points must remain constant along each component of
\Lambda
.
In particular the Jacobian is constant as well (this explains why there is no assumption on the Jacobian in the Hénon case).
Then, Theorem
E
implies that
M(f)
must be bounded along the family

\Lambda
, which
implies that
\Lambda
is a finite set.

Thus, to drop the assumption on the multi-Jacobian in Theorem
D
, we should prove a version of Theorem
E
in which the ‘constant multi-Jacobian’ assumption is replaced by ‘constant Jacobian’.
It is unclear to us whether such a statement holds.
What is clear is that our method breaks down
without such an assumption (see Example
6.15
).
A related issue, which appears in
[
9
]
, is
the compactness of the connectedness locus.
In
\mathcal{P}_{d}
this follows from the fact that connectedness of the Julia set is equivalent to
M(p)=0
.
Likewise,
Theorem
E
together with the results of
[
6
]
and
[
16
]
imply that
the connectedness locus is compact in
\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{a}}
for fixed
\mathbf{a}

(see Corollary
6.20
).
On the other hand this result is not true for variable Jacobian
(see §
2.3
)
: this outlines a difference between the one-dimensional and the two-dimensional situations.

The asymptotic behavior of the Lyapunov exponent
along algebraic families (or along degenerating sequences)
is an important topic in the dynamics of rational
functions (see e.g.,
[
20
,
28
,
19
,
21
,
18
]
and the references therein)
and groups of Möbius transformations
(see e.g.,
[
34
,
13
]
). This asymptotic behavior can be studied with techniques from non-Archimedean degenerations: such techniques are not yet available for automorphisms of
\mathbf{C}^{2}

and would constitute a natural continuation of this work.

2.
Stability theory of loxodromic automorphisms

2.1.
Dynamics of loxodromic automorphisms

We use the standard dynamical objects associated to a loxodromic automorphism
f\colon\mathbf{C}^{2}\to\mathbf{C}^{2}
,
as summarized in
[
6
, §1]
or
[
14
, §2.1]
, with the same notation.
In particular we will consider the Green functions
G^{\pm}
, the equilibrium measure
\mu
, and the “small Julia set”
J^{\varstar}=\supp(\mu)
. Most often we work with families, and the dependence in
f
is underlined: we write
G^{\pm}_{f}
,
\mu_{f}
,
K_{f}
,
\ldots
, or
G^{\pm}_{\lambda}
,
\mu_{\lambda}
,
K_{\lambda}
,
\ldots
, when
f
depends on a parameter
\lambda
.

Let
d\geq 2
be the dynamical degree of
f
.
We denote by
\Fix(f)
the set of fixed points of
f
and by
\Per_{n}(f)
the set of periodic points of exact period
n
.
So
\Fix(f^{n})
is the sets
\Per_{k}(f)
for
k
dividing
n
.
It was shown in
[
22
, Thm 3.1]
that all periodic points are isolated and
\#\Fix(f^{n})=d^{n}
, counting multiplicities.
An exact expression for
\#\Per_{n}(f)
can be obtained by the Möbius inversion formula.
Since
\Per_{n}(f)=\Fix(f^{n})\setminus\bigcup_{k\leq n/2}\Fix(f^{k})
, we get

\#\Per_{n}(f)\sim d^{n}

as
n\to\infty
.
We denote by
\SPer_{n}(f)
the set of saddle periodic points of exact period
n
and by
\SPer(f)
the set of all saddle periodic points.
We already introduced the notation
\lambda^{s/u}(p)
in §
1.3

for the stable/unstable multipliers of
p\in\SPer_{n}(f)
.
We will denote by
\chi^{s/u}
the corresponding Lyapunov exponents, that is

\chi^{s/u}(p)=\frac{1}{n}\log\left|\lambda^{s/u}(p)\right|.

If
\mathrm{P}
is a set of periodic points, we write
\mathrm{P}_{n}
for
\mathrm{P}\cap\Per_{n}(f)
.
We say that
\mathrm{P}
is of asymptotic density
1
if
\#\mathrm{P}_{n}\sim d^{n}
as
n\to\infty
,
and of positive upper density if
\limsup_{n\to\infty}d^{-n}\#\mathrm{P}_{n}>0
.

The equidistribution theorem obtained by Bedford, Lyubich and Smillie in
[
2
]

asserts that
\SPer_{n}(f)
is of asymptotic density
1
and
that saddle periodic points are asymptotically equidistributed towards the equilibrium measure
\mu_{f}
.
The following reinforcement of this result was obtained in
[
11
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f\in\mathsf{Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{2})
be a loxodromic automorphism of dynamical degree
d
.
There exists a set
\SPer^{+}(f)\subset\SPer(f)

of saddle periodic points of asymptotic density 1 such that
if
(p_{n})
is an arbitrary sequence of periodic points with
p_{n}\in\Per_{n}(f)
, then

(2.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\delta_{f^{k}(p_{n})}=\mu_{f}\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\chi^{u}(p_{n})=\chi^{+}(\mu_{f}).

As a consequence, if
\mathrm{P}
is a set of saddle periodic points with
positive upper density, there is a sequence
(p_{n_{j}})_{j\geq 0}
with

p_{n_{j}}\in\mathrm{P}\cap\Per_{n_{j}}(f)
such that the two properties in (
2.1
)
hold for
p_{n_{j}}
as
j
goes to
+\infty
.

2.2.
J^{\varstar}
-stability

Let
(f_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
be a holomorphic family of loxodromic automorphisms of
\mathbf{C}^{2}
of dynamical degree
d
.
We assume in this section that
\Lambda
is connected.
Abusing notation, we often identify the parameter in
\Lambda
with the corresponding map
f_{\lambda}\in\mathsf{Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{2})
and simply write “
f\in\Lambda
”.
When a parameter
{\lambda_{0}}\in\Lambda
is used as a base point we often set
f_{0}=f_{\lambda_{0}}
.

A notion of stability was developed for such families in
[
14
]
.
It can be expressed in several ways, which we now describe.
Recall that by the implicit function theorem, any saddle periodic point
can be followed holomorphically along
\Lambda
until one of its multipliers hits the value
1

3.2.
Trace and multiplier spectra

Let
q
be a positive integer and let
\mathbf{C}^{(q)}
denote the symmetric product of
q

\mathbf{C}
that is,
\mathbf{C}^{q}/{\sf{Sym}}_{q}
where the symmetric group acts by permutations. Points of
\mathbf{C}^{(q)}
will be called

(of length
q
). Thus, a multiset is a finite set
\left\{x_{1},\ldots x_{q}\right\}
in which the elements
x_{i}\in\mathbf{C}
are repeated according to their multiplicity; equivalently, it is a divisor
\sum_{j}n_{j}z_{j}
supported by some points
z_{j}\in\mathbf{C}
with multiplicities
n_{j}\geq 1
such that
\sum_{j}n_{j}=q
.
The elementary symmetric functions provide an isomorphism
\iota\colon\mathbf{C}^{(q)}\to\mathbf{C}^{q}
.

Let us fix some integer
n\geq 1
. On the variety
\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d}}\times\mathbf{C}^{2}
, the function

\tr_{n}\colon(f,z)\mapsto\tr(D_{z}f^{n})

is regular. By restriction, it induces a regular function
\tr_{n}\colon\widehat{\Per}_{n}\to\mathbf{C}
, and we can
then define a regular map
\Trace_{n}\colon\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d}}\to\mathbf{C}^{(p_{n})}
such that

\Trace_{n}(f)=\left\{\tr(D_{z}f^{n})\;;\;z\in\Per_{n}^{*}(f)\right\},

viewed as a multiset of length
p_{n}
.
We define the
trace spectrum
and the
multiplier spectrum
of
f
by

(3.1)

\displaystyle\Trace(f)

\displaystyle=\left(\Trace_{n}(f)\right)_{n\geq 1}\in\prod_{n\geq 1}\mathbf{C}^{(p_{n})}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\Mult(f)

\displaystyle=(\jac(f),\Trace(f))\in\mathbf{C}^{*}\times\prod_{n\geq 1}\mathbf{C}^{(p_{n})}.

Since
\jac(f)^{n}=\det(D_{z}f^{n})
for every fixed point of
f^{n}
, the multiplier spectrum encodes the conjugacy invariants
(\tr,\det)
of
D_{z}f^{n}
, whose knowledge is equivalent to that of the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\}\in\mathbf{C}^{(2)}
, at all points of formal period

, for every

. The use of the formal period instead of the exact period is meant to guarantee good analytic and algebraic behavior for these quantities.

For saddle points, we directly consider the unstable multiplier and define

\SMult^{u}_{n}(f)=\left\{\lambda^{u}(z)\;;\;z\in\SPer_{n}(f)\right\}\in\mathbf{C}^{\#\SPer_{n}(f))},

\SMult^{u}(f)=\left(\SMult^{u}_{n}(f)\right)_{n\geq 1}\text{ and }\SMult(f)=({\jac(f)},{\SMult^{u}(f)}).

(Since

depends sharply on
f
, we do not claim that
\SMult^{u}_{n}(f)
depends in a regular or analytic way on

.)

Remark 3.2
.

Since the number of periodic (resp. saddle periodic)
points of period

grows like

,
the dynamical degree of a loxodromic automorphism is tautologically determined by
its multiplier (resp. unstable multiplier) spectrum.

\left\{f\in\Lambda\;;\;\forall n\geq 1,\Trace_{n}(f)\subset E_{n}\right\}

is finite. Likewise the set

\left\{f\in\Lambda\;;\;\forall n\geq 1,\SMult^{u}_{n}(f)\subset E_{n}\right\}

is discrete.

Proof for the trace spectrum.

For
n\geq 1
, set

(3.3)

\Sigma_{n}=\left\{\lambda\in\Lambda\;;\;\Trace_{n}(f_{\lambda})\subset E_{n}\right\}\,\text{ and }\,\Sigma=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\Sigma_{n}.

These are algebraic subsets of
\Lambda
because each of the maps
\Trace_{n}
is regular.
To conclude that
\Sigma
is finite, we fix an irreducible component of
\Sigma
, still denoted by
\Sigma
for simplicity, and we show that
(f_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Sigma}
is stable.
We follow the same strategy as for
[
11
, Lem. 8.5]
,
except that we work with traces instead of multipliers.

Fix
\lambda_{0}\in\Sigma
and set
f_{0}=f_{\lambda_{0}}
. Fix
\alpha>0
such that
\chi^{+}(\mu_{f_{0}})\geq\log^{+}\left|\jac(f_{0})\right|+2\alpha
. Let
\mathcal{N}
be an open ball
around
\lambda_{0}
in which
\log\left|\jac(f_{\lambda})\right|-\log^{+}\left|\jac(f_{0})\right|<\alpha/4
.
Theorem
2.1
provides
a set
\SPer^{+}(f_{0})
of saddle points of asymptotic density 1, such that
\chi^{u}(z_{0})\geq\chi^{+}(\mu_{f_{0}})-\alpha
for
every
z_{0}\in\SPer^{+}(f_{0})
.
Let us show that points in

\SPer^{+}(f_{0})
of sufficiently large period
persist as saddle points for all parameters in
\mathcal{N}\cap\Sigma
.

Fix
z_{0}\in\SPer^{+}(f_{0})
, of period
n
. We have

(3.4)

\left|\tr(D_{z_{0}}f_{0}^{n})\right|\geq\left|\lambda^{u}(z_{0})\right|-1\geq\exp\left(n(\log^{+}\left|\jac(f_{0})\right|+\alpha)\right)-1.

Let
z_{\lambda}
be the analytic continuation of
z_{0}
as a saddle point, for
\lambda
in some connected
neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(z_{0})
of
\lambda_{0}
in
\Sigma
(that depends a priori on
z_{0}
).
Along
\Sigma
,

\Trace_{n}(f_{\lambda})
is contained in the finite set
E_{n}
, so

\lambda\mapsto\tr(D_{z_{\lambda}}f_{\lambda}^{n})
is constant on

\mathcal{N}(z_{0})
, equal to
\tr(D_{z_{0}}f_{0}^{n})
. Thus for
\lambda\in\mathcal{N}(z_{0})
we have
\left|\lambda^{u}(z_{\lambda})-\tr(D_{z_{0}}f_{0}^{n})\right|<1
, and then

(3.5)

\left|\lambda^{u}(z_{\lambda})\right|\geq\left|\tr(D_{z_{0}}f_{0}^{n})\right|-1\geq\exp\left(n(\log^{+}\left|\jac(f_{0})\right|+\alpha)\right)-2.

Fix
n_{0}
so large that for
n\geq n_{0}
,

(3.6)

\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\exp\left(n(\log^{+}\left|\jac(f_{0})\right|+\alpha)\right)-2\right)\geq\log^{+}\left|\jac(f_{0})\right|+\alpha/2.

Then
\chi^{u}(z_{\lambda})\geq\alpha/2
for all
z_{0}\in\SPer^{+}(f_{0})
of period
\geq n_{0}
, and the definition of
\mathcal{N}
gives

(3.7)

\chi^{s}(z_{\lambda})=\log\left|\jac(f_{\lambda})\right|-\chi^{u}(z_{\lambda})\leq\log\left|\jac(f_{\lambda})\right|-\log^{+}\left|\jac(f_{0})\right|-\alpha/2\leq-\alpha/4.

Thus, the analytic continuation
z_{\lambda}
of
z_{0}
remains a saddle periodic point when

\lambda\in\mathcal{N}\cap\Sigma
, with the uniform estimates

\chi^{s}(z_{\lambda})\leq-\alpha/4\text{ and }\chi^{u}(z_{\lambda})\geq\alpha/2.

Thus, no bifurcation is possible and we can take
\mathcal{N}(z_{0})=\mathcal{N}\cap\Sigma
, as was to be shown.

This argument shows that
(f_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Sigma}
is locally stable of type (II),
hence also of type (III). To enhance this to global stability,
we follow an idea used for
[
11
, Prop. 8.6]
.
We will show that the Jacobian must be constant on
\Sigma
, so that we can choose
\mathcal{N}=\Sigma
.
By Remark
2.5
,
(f_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Sigma}
is globally stable of type (VI):
all periodic points can be followed, but not necessarily as saddles. By way of contradiction, assume that

\lambda\mapsto\jac(f_{\lambda})
is not constant on
\Sigma
and fix a parameter
\lambda_{1}\in\Sigma
and

\alpha>0
such that
\log\left|\jac(f_{\lambda_{1}})\right|\geq\log\left|\jac(f_{\lambda_{0}})\right|+2\alpha
,
as well as a path
\gamma
in
\Sigma
joining
\lambda_{0}
to
\lambda_{1}
.
Note that
\chi^{+}(\mu_{f_{0}})\leq\log\left|\jac(f_{\lambda_{0}})\right|
, hence

\log\left|\jac(f_{\lambda_{1}})\right|\geq\chi^{+}(\mu_{f_{0}})+2\alpha
. Fix
a set
\SPer^{+}(f_{0})
of saddle points of asymptotic density
1
, such that
\chi^{u}(z_{0})\leq\chi^{+}(\mu_{f_{0}})+\alpha
for
every
z_{0}\in\SPer^{+}(f_{0})
.
For such a
z_{0}
we have

(3.8)

\left|\tr(D_{z_{0}}f_{\lambda_{0}}^{n})\right|=\left|\lambda^{u}(z_{0})+\lambda^{s}(z_{0})\right|\leq\left|\lambda^{u}(z_{0})\right|+1\leq\exp\left(n(\chi^{+}(\mu_{f_{0}})+\alpha)\right)+1.

Let us follow
z_{0}
along the path
\gamma
up to some periodic point
z_{1}
. By assumption the trace stays constant so
\left|\tr(D_{z_{1}}f_{\lambda_{1}}^{n})\right|
satisfies the inequality (
3.8

z_{1}
is a saddle point for
f_{\lambda_{1}}

we infer

(3.9)

\displaystyle\left|\jac(f_{\lambda_{1}})\right|^{n}\leq\left|\lambda^{u}(z_{1})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\tr(D_{z_{1}}f_{\lambda_{1}}^{n})\right|+1

\displaystyle=\left|\tr(D_{z_{0}}f_{\lambda_{0}}^{n})\right|+1\leq\exp\left(n(\chi^{+}(\mu_{f_{0}})+\alpha)\right)+2.

But
\log\left|\jac(f_{\lambda_{1}})\right|\geq\chi^{+}(\mu_{f_{0}})+2\alpha
so if

is large enough we get a contradiction. Therefore, as we follow any point of
\SPer^{+}(f_{0})
of sufficiently large period along

, which contradicts
the fact that
\SPer^{+}(f_{0})
has asymptotic density

. So
the Jacobian is constant on
\Sigma
, and we are done.
∎

Proof for the unstable multiplier spectrum.

This is more delicate because the sets

\Sigma^{u}_{n}:=\left\{\lambda\in\Lambda\;;\;\SMult^{u}_{n}(f_{\lambda})\subset E_{n}\right\}\text{ and }\;\Sigma^{u}:=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\Sigma_{n}^{u}

at

Theorem
2.1

\SPer^{+}(f_{0})

Consider the subset of

.

(1)

The proof shows that

(2)

The verification is left to the reader.

Further results: uniformity and arbitrary fields

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be either of

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{d}
or
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{a}}^{k}
for some

\mathbf{d}\in\mathbf{N}^{k}_{\geq 2}
and
\mathbf{a}\in(\mathbf{C}^{*})^{k}
. There exists
P
and
N
depending only on
\mathbf{d}
such that for every
f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
, the set

\left\{f\in\mathcal{H}\;;\;\ \Trace_{n}(g)=\Trace_{n}(f_{0})\,\text{ for every }n\leq P\right\}

has cardinality at most
N
.
Moreover, this result holds for polynomial automorphisms of
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbf{K})
, for any algebraically closed field
\mathbf{K}
of characteristic zero.

Proof.

We first work over
\mathbf{C}
. Assume
k\geq 2
, and
denote by
\mathbf{a}(f)
the multi-Jacobian of
f\in\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d}}
.
Introduce a sequence of algebraic varieties

S_{n}=\left\{(f,g)\in\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d}}\times\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\mathbf{d}}\;;\;\forall\ell\leq n,\ \Trace_{\ell}(f)=\Trace_{\ell}(g)\text{ and }\mathbf{a}(f)=\mathbf{a}(g)\right\}.

Since this sequence is non-increasing (
S_{n+1}\subset S_{n}
)
there is an index
P\geq 1
such that
S_{n}=S_{P}
for all
n\geq P
.
The first projection
\pi\colon S_{P}\to\mathcal{H}
is a surjective morphism and by Theorem
3.5
all its fibers are finite. Thus, there is an integer
N
such that every fiber of
\pi
contains at most
N
elements, as was to be shown.

For
k=1
the argument is the same by considering

S_{n}=\left\{(f,g)\in\mathcal{H}_{d}^{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{d}^{1}\;;\;\forall\ell\leq n,\ \Trace_{\ell}(f)=\Trace_{\ell}(g)\right\}.

Now we work over

\mathbf{K}
.
The Friedland-Milnor classification holds in this case as well,
and we consider
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{K})
to be either of

\mathcal{H}_{d}^{1}(\mathbf{K})
or
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{a}}^{k}(\mathbf{K})

Let

and

be the integers defined in the previous paragraph (for the field

Pick distinct elements

of
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{K})

such that
\Trace_{n}(f_{m})=\Trace_{n}(f_{1})

and

.
Let
\mathbf{L}_{0}\subset\mathbf{K}
be the

and let

Example 3.8
.

Let
{\mathbf{K}}
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic

,
such as
\overline{{\mathbf{F}}_{p}}
.
Consider the additive automorphism of the affine plane
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{K}}
defined by

(3.12)

f(x,y)=(ay+sx^{p},x)

where
a\in{\mathbf{K}}^{*}
is fixed and
s\in{\mathbf{K}}^{*}
is a parameter.
Then, the differential

does not depend on
(x,y)
,
its trace is
0
and its determinant is

. If
\alpha
is a square root of

This shows that Theorems

4.
Hénon maps and Huguin’s theorem

This section provides a direct proof, based on
[
24
, Thm A]
, of the next two theorems.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(f_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
be an irreducible algebraic family
of complex Hénon maps,
such that
\jac(f_{\lambda})\not\equiv-1
. If it is stable of type (I), then it is trivial.

This is really a result about type (I) stability since, as we will see, it deals specifically with points of period
1
and
2
.
So even for Hénon maps with Jacobian different from
-1
, this result is weaker than
Theorem
D
(see the comment about the chain of implications after Proposition
2.2
).
On the other hand, by Remark
3.4
(1), for Hénon maps of Jacobian different from
-1
, Theorem
4.1
can be used instead of Theorem
D
to get Theorem
C
and the unstable multiplier version of
Theorem
A
. An amusing consequence is that global considerations about periodic points of
periods
1
and
2
in
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{d}
lead to local unstable multiplier rigidity (resp.
C^{1}
rigidity on
J^{\varstar}
),
even for maps whose points of periods 1 and 2 are all attracting.

Theorem 4.2
.

A complex Hénon map
f
of given degree
whose Jacobian is different from
-1
is determined up to finitely many choices by
the multipliers of its periodic points of period
1
and
2
(or equivalently by
\jac(f)
,
\Trace_{1}(f)
and
\Trace_{2}(f)
). That is, for any
f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{d}
such that
\jac(f_{0})\neq-1
the set

\Sigma_{2}(f_{0})=\left\{f\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{d}\;;\;\jac(f)=\jac(f_{0}),\Trace_{1}(f)=\Trace_{1}(f_{0}),\ \Trace_{2}(f)=\Trace_{2}(f_{0})\right\}

is finite.
Furthermore, for
f_{0}
in a dense Zariski open subset of
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{d}
,
\Sigma_{2}(f_{0})=\left\{f_{0}\right\}
.

These two theorems stem from a computational miracle which does not hold for
Jacobian
-1
. The following example shows that
Theorem
4.2
does not carry over to that case.

Example 4.3
.

The general (reduced) form of a Hénon map of Jacobian
-1
is

f(x,y)=(y+p(x),x)
, with
p
monic and centered. The equation

(y_{0}+p(x_{0}),x_{0})=(x_{0},y_{0})
for fixed points is equivalent to

(4.1)

x_{0}=y_{0}\;{\text{ and }} \;p(x_{0})=0

and the trace of the differential at such a fixed point equals
p^{\prime}(x_{0})
. For fixed points of
f^{2}
we obtain

(4.2)

\begin{cases}x_{0}+p(y_{0}+p(x_{0}))=x_{0}\\
y_{0}+p(x_{0})=y_{0}\end{cases}{\text{ or equivalently }} \begin{cases}p(x_{0})=0\\
p(y_{0})=0\end{cases}.

Among these solutions, points of period 2 are those for which
x_{0}\neq y_{0}
. The computation of the differential gives

(4.3)

\tr(D_{(x_{0},y_{0})}f^{2})=p^{\prime}(x_{0})p^{\prime}(y_{0})+2.

Let us choose for instance
p_{\lambda}(x)=(x-\lambda)^{2}(x+\lambda)^{2}
. Then all solutions of

p_{\lambda}(x_{0})=0
satisfy

p^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x_{0})=0
, and we conclude that the family

f_{\lambda}(x,y)=(y+(x^{2}-\lambda^{2})^{2},x)
violates the conclusions of Theorem
4.2
. Note that
Theorem 7.1 of
[
22
]
asserts that Hénon maps of degree
2
and
3
are determined by the multipliers of their fixed points, therefore
4
is the smallest possible degree for such an example. ∎

We now proceed to the proof of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
(f_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
be an algebraic family of loxodromic automorphisms, with
\Lambda
irreducible.
If it is stable of type (I), then
the stable and unstable multipliers of all saddle periodic points, as well as the jacobian

\jac(f_{\lambda})

Proposition 4.5

Let

Proof.

This relies on an explicit calculation. Write

\frac{1}{1-a}p(x_{0})=x_{0}\;{\text{ and }} \;y_{0}=x_{0},

For points of period

5.1.
Crossed and Hénon-like mappings

5.1.1.
Definitions

In this paper by definition a bidisk is a domain
\mathbb{B}\subset\mathbf{C}^{2}
that is
biholomorphic to a standard open bidisk
D_{1}\times D_{2}
. It comes equipped with two projections
\pi_{1}:\mathbb{B}\to D_{1}
and
\pi_{2}:\mathbb{B}\to D_{2}
, respectively called vertical and horizontal.
We always assume that the biholomorphism, hence also the projections extend holomorphically to some neighborhood of
\overline{\mathbb{B}}
in
\mathbf{C}^{2}
.
The vertical and horizontal boundaries are
\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}=\pi_{1}^{-1}(\partial D_{1})
and
\partial^{h}\mathbb{B}=\pi_{2}^{-1}(\partial D_{2})
, respectively.
An object (subvariety, subdomain, current) is
vertical
(resp.
horizontal
) if it
is disjoint from a neighborhood of
\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}
(resp.
\partial^{h}\mathbb{B}
).
We use a slightly different convention for lines: by definition, a
horizontal line
(resp.
vertical line
) is a fiber of
\pi_{2}

(resp.
\pi_{1}
). Since the
\pi_{i}
are typically not linear, those lines are usually not lines in the usual sense in
\mathbf{C}^{2}
.

Any horizontal submanifold
V
in
\mathbb{B}
has a
degree

\deg(V)
,
defined as the degree of the branched covering
\pi_{1}:V\to D_{1}
.
Likewise, any horizontal closed positive current
T
has a
slice mass

\sm(T)
,
which is the mass of the positive measure
T\wedge[L^{v}]
, where
L^{v}
is any vertical line.
If
T=[V]
is the integration current on a horizontal manifold, then
\sm(T)=\deg(V)
.
Finally, if
S
(resp.
T
) is a horizontal (resp. vertical) closed positive current,
the measure
T\wedge S
is well-defined and its total mass satisfies
{\left\|T\wedge S\right\|}=\sm(T)\sm(S)
.
We refer to
[
15
]
for these results. By convention, a horizontal disk
\Delta
is a horizontal submanifold biholomorphic to a disk; in particular
\partial\Delta\subset\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}
.

If
\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
are two bidisks, a
crossed mapping

(f,\mathbb{B},\mathbb{B}^{\prime})
is the data of an injective holomorphic map defined in a neighborhood of
\overline{\mathbb{B}}
, such that (i)
f(\mathbb{B})\cap\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
,
(ii)
f(\partial^{v}\mathbb{B})\cap\overline{\mathbb{B}^{\prime}}=\emptyset
, and (iii)
f(\overline{\mathbb{B}})\cap\partial\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\subset\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
.
We abuse notation and simply write

f\colon\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
even if by definition
f(\mathbb{B})
is not contained in
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
.
Crossed mappings were introduced in
[
23
]
, however this definition is from
[
15
]
. The degree of a crossed mapping is the degree of the horizontal curve
f(L^{h})\cap\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
, where
L^{h}
is any horizontal line in
\mathbb{B}
.
If
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
we say that

is a
Hénon-like map
.

5.1.2.
Unramified maps

The following considerations are new (see Ishii
[
25
]
for related ideas).

Definition 5.1
.

A crossed mapping
f\colon\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
is
unramified over
\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
if
for every horizontal line
L^{h}
in
\mathbb{B}
,

(i)

f(L^{h})
is transverse to
\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
, and

(ii)

f(L^{h})\cap\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
is a disjoint
union of holomorphic disks.

An equivalent formulation of (i) is that
there is a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}

of
\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
such that for every
L^{h}
,
\pi_{1}\arrowvert_{f(L^{h})\cap\mathcal{N}}

is an unramified covering onto its image, hence the terminology.

Remark 5.2
.

In
[
38
]
Wermer constructed a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbf{C}^{2}
such that
\Omega
is biholomorphic to a bidisk and
\Omega\cap(\mathbf{C}\times\left\{0\right\})
is not polynomially convex.
This suggest that there might exist crossed maps
(f,\mathbb{B},\mathbb{B}^{\prime})

with a horizontal line that satisfies (i) but fails to satisfy (ii).
An easily checkable sufficient condition for (ii) is the following:

the natural projection
\pi_{2}^{\prime}:\mathbb{B}\to D_{2}^{\prime}
extends holomorphically to
f(\mathbb{B})
.

It holds for the unit bidisk and more generally
when
\pi_{2}^{\prime}
extends to a globally defined holomorphic map
\mathbf{C}^{2}\to\mathbf{C}
.
Indeed in this case
\left\{z\in L^{h},\ {\pi_{2}^{\prime}\circ f(z)}\in D^{\prime}_{2}\right\}
is a
union of disks by the maximum principle,
and
L^{h}\cap f^{-1}(\mathbb{B}^{\prime})
is the union of the disks among those whose image intersects
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
.
For completeness let us mention a more intrinsic version.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbf{C}^{2}
be a Runge domain. If

Proof.

Write

, where the
\gamma_{i}
are smooth Jordan curves.
Let
D_{i}
be the Jordan domain in
D
bounded by
\gamma_{i}
.
We claim that the

are disjoint and

.
To prove this, observe that by Wermer’s theorem
[
37
]
, the polynomial hull

or a Riemann surface bordered by

Finally,

, and contains points outside

∎

5.1.3.
Iteration vs. cut-off iteration

When considering a Hénon-like map
f\colon\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{B}
, it is understood in
[
15
]
that
iteration means cut-off iteration, that is,
the only orbits that we consider are those that never leave
\mathbb{B}
. In other words, we only iterate
f\arrowvert_{f^{-1}(\mathbb{B})}
(often denoted
f\arrowvert_{\mathbb{B}}
).
Now suppose that the Hénon-like map
f
is the restriction of a global holomorphic diffeomorphism
\tilde{f}
of
\mathbf{C}^{2}
. Then, some orbits of
\tilde{f}
might leave
\mathbb{B}
before entering it again.
We say that

f

is iterated properly
if
{\tilde{f}}^{k}({\tilde{f}}(\mathbb{B})\setminus\mathbb{B})\cap\mathbb{B}=\emptyset
for every
k\geq 0
. This implies the corresponding property for negative iterates. For instance, if
\tilde{f}
is a Hénon map and
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{D}(0,R)^{2}
is a sufficiently large bidisk, orbits leaving
\mathbb{B}
must escape to infinity, and this property holds.

Likewise, if
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are two Hénon-like maps in
\mathbb{B}
, which are restrictions of globally defined mappings
\tilde{f}_{i}
, their composition in the sense of Hénon-like maps is
f_{2}\arrowvert_{\mathbb{B}}\circ f_{1}\arrowvert_{\mathbb{B}}
, which may differ from
(\tilde{f}_{2}\circ\tilde{f}_{1})\arrowvert_{\mathbb{B}}
.
This happens precisely when
\tilde{f}_{2}(\tilde{f}_{1}(\mathbb{B})\setminus\mathbb{B})\cap\mathbb{B}\neq\emptyset
.
As above, we say that
f_{2}
and
f_{1}

are composed properly
if

\tilde{f}_{2}(\tilde{f}_{1}(\mathbb{B})\setminus\mathbb{B})\cap\mathbb{B}=\emptyset
, in which case
f_{2}\arrowvert_{\mathbb{B}}\circ f_{1}\arrowvert_{\mathbb{B}}=(\tilde{f}_{2}\circ\tilde{f}_{1})\arrowvert_{\mathbb{B}}
.
This definition can be generalized to compositions of arbitrary length.

In what follows, we will use the same notation for
f
and its extension
\tilde{f}
, when such an extension exists.

5.2.
Estimates for polynomials in one variable

Recall that
\mathcal{P}_{d}=\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbf{C})
is the complex variety of conjugacy classes of polynomials of degree
d
in one complex variable under the action of the affine group.
We view it as the quotient of the space

\mathcal{P}_{d}^{\rm cm}\simeq\mathbf{C}^{d-1}
of monic and centered polynomials
p(z)=z^{d}+\sum_{j=0}^{d-2}a_{j}z^{j}
by the group of
(d-1)^{\rm th}
roots of unity, acting by

p\mapsto\beta^{-1}p(\beta z)
.
For us, most of the time, it will be enough to work on
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{\rm cm}
.

5.2.1.
The maximal escape rate

Recall from Equation (
1.5
) that the maximal escape rate is defined by

M(p)=\max\left\{G_{p}(c)\;;\;p^{\prime}(c)=0\right\}.

We will be interested in the regime where
M(p)
is large.
As explained in
[
9
]
, the asymptotic geometry of
p
depends on
M(p)
. To see this,
we define

(5.1)

R_{p}=e^{M(p)};

as we will see, it provides a good measure of the size (or norm) of
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{\rm cm}
and of the characteristic scale of its Julia set.
For instance, the sublevel sets
\left\{G_{p}<t\right\}
are connected precisely for
t>M(p)
.

5.2.2.
The Böttcher coordinate

The Böttcher coordinate
\varphi_{p}
is defined on the open set

(5.2)

{\mathcal{U}}_{p}=\left\{z\in\mathbf{C};\;G_{p}(z)>M(p)\right\}

and provides a holomorphic diffeomorphism from
{\mathcal{U}}_{p}
to
\mathbf{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}_{R_{p}}}

such that

(5.3)

G_{p}(z)=\log\left|\varphi_{p}(z)\right|\quad\forall z\in{\mathcal{U}}_{p}.

The reciprocal diffeomorphism will be denoted
\psi_{p}\colon\mathbf{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}_{R_{p}}}\to{\mathcal{U}}_{p}
.
Both
\varphi_{p}
and
\psi_{p}
are tangent to the identity at infinity:
\varphi_{p}(z)=z+O(1/z)

and
\psi_{p}(w)=w+O(1/w)
.

Recall the distorsion estimates of Koebe:
if
g\colon\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\to\mathbf{C}
is a univalent function such that
g(0)=0
and
g^{\prime}(0)=1
, then

(5.4)

\frac{\left|u\right|}{(1+\left|u\right|/\rho)^{2}} \leq\left|g(u)\right|\leq\frac{\left|u\right|}{(1-\left|u\right|/\rho)^{2}}.

Applying these estimates to
u\mapsto 1/\psi_{p}(1/u)
and making
the change of variable
\psi_{p}(w)=z
, we obtain

(5.5)

\frac{1}{(1+R_{p}/\left|\varphi_{p}(z)\right|)^{2}}\leq\frac{\left|\varphi_{p}(z)\right|}{\left|z\right|}\leq\frac{1}{(1-R_{p}/\left|\varphi_{p}(z)\right|)^{2}}.

This proves the following lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

The Böttcher coordinate

5.3.

Let us complete the discussion of §

\displaystyle:=s_{1}^{-1}\circ g\circ s_{1}=\tilde{h}_{k}\circ\cdots\circ\tilde{h}_{1},\text{ with }\tilde{h}_{i}=s_{i+1}^{-1}\circ h_{i}\circ s_{i}.

Friedland and Milnor proved that an element

\chi^{+}(\mu_{f})=\log\left|a\right|+\left|\chi^{-}(\mu_{f})\right|=\log\left|a\right|+\chi^{+}(\mu_{f^{-1}})

reduces the problem to finding good estimates for the Lyapunov exponent
\chi^{+}(\mu_{f^{-1}})
of
f^{-1}

with respect to
\mu_{f^{-1}}=\mu_{f}
.
But
f^{-1}(x,y)=(y,\frac{1}{a}(x-p(y)))
is conjugate to the normal form

(\frac{1}{a}y+q(x),x)
, where
q(x)=-\frac{1}{a\alpha}p(\alpha x)
for
\alpha^{d-1}=-a
.
This automorphism
(x,y)\mapsto(\frac{1}{a}y+q(x),x)
is a small, invertible perturbation of the one-variable map
x\mapsto q(x)
.
As such, it is eligible to the techniques of
[
17
, §3]
.
In this paper
q
is considered to be fixed,
but the argument can easily be adapted to the case of a variable polynomial
q

and yields
\chi^{+}(f)=\log\left|a\right|+\chi^{+}(q)+o(1)
.
Depending on the order of magnitude of
\left|a\right|
,
q
diverges in
\mathcal{P}_{d}
or not.
For instance, suppose
\left|a\right|\geq cR_{p}^{\beta}
and
\beta
is large enough (
\beta>(d-1)^{2}/2
suffices). Then from the bounds (
5.8
) and (
5.13
)
it is easy to see that
q(x)\to x^{d}
as
R\to\infty
. Then Theorem 1.3 in
[
17
]
implies that
\chi^{+}(\mu_{f^{-1}})\to\log d
, hence

\chi^{+}(\mu_{f})=\log\left|a\right|+\log d+o(1)
.

With these ingredients in hand, we can hope for a complete description of the behavior of
\chi^{\pm}(\mu_{f})
at infinity in
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{d}
.

6.1.2.
Estimates on
G_{f}
and
\varphi_{f}

Since in the following we only work with the forward Green and Böttcher functions
G^{+}_{f}

and
\varphi^{+}_{f}
, for notational lightness we remove the superscript
+
.
It is well known that when
f
is fixed,
\left|x\right|
is large, and
\left|y\right|\leq\left|x\right|
, then
G_{f}(x,y)=\log\left|x\right|+O(1)
and

\varphi_{f}(x,y)=x+O(1)
.
We need to understand how large
x
must be to get such an estimate when
f
varies in the space of Hénon maps.
Under an appropriate bound on the Jacobian, it
turns out that the right scale is determined by
R_{f}
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Suppose

\left|a\right|\leq R_{f}^{d-1}
. Then
\varphi_{f}
is well-defined in the domain defined by
\left|y\right|\leq\left|x\right|
and
\left|x\right|\geq 12dR_{f}
, and in this domain we have

\left|\frac{\varphi_{f}(x,y)}{x}-1\right|<2\frac{R_{f}}{\left|x\right|}\quad\text{ and }\quad\left|G_{f}(x,y)-\log\left|x\right|\right|<2\frac{R_{f}}{\left|x\right|}.

Proof.

Denote
(x_{n},y_{n})=f^{n}(x,y)
. We come back to the construction of
\varphi_{f}
: recall that

\varphi_{f}(x,y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}(\pi_{1}\circ f^{n}(x,y))^{1/d^{n}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}^{1/d^{n}}
as
n
goes to
+\infty
, for an appropriate choice of
(d^{n})^{\rm th}
-root. For later use in Proposition
6.12
we split the proof in two steps.

Step 1.–
Estimation of
x_{k}
.

Consider

(6.3)

\theta(x,y)=\frac{x_{1}}{x^{d}}-1=\frac{\pi_{1}\circ f(x,y)}{x^{d}}-1=\frac{p(x)+ay}{x^{d}}-1.

Since
\left|x\right|\geq 12dR_{f}
and
\left|a\right|\leq R_{f}^{d-1}
, Lemma
5.7
implies that

(6.4)

\displaystyle\left|\theta(x,y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(12d+2\right)\frac{R_{f}^{2}}{\left|x\right|^{2}}+\frac{R_{f}^{d-1}}{\left|x\right|^{d-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left((12d+2)\frac{R_{f}}{\left|x\right|}+1\right)\frac{R_{f}}{\left|x\right|}\leq\left(2+\frac{1}{6d}\right)\frac{R_{f}}{\left|x\right|}.

In particular,
\left|\theta(x,y)\right|\leq 1/11
, so that

(6.5)

\left|x_{1}\right|\geq(10/11)\left|x\right|^{d}\geq 10d\left|x\right|\geq 20\left|x\right|.

This implies that the domain defined by
\left|y\right|\leq\left|x\right|
and
\left|x\right|\geq 12dR_{f}
is stable,
that
\left|x_{k}\right|\geq(10d)^{k}\left|x\right|\geq 20^{k}\left|x\right|
, and that
\left|\theta(x_{k},y_{k})\right|\leq 1/11
for every
k\geq 0
.

Step 2.–
Estimation of
\varphi_{f}
. From now on we only use the bound
\left|\theta(x,y)\right|\leq 1/6
.

Set
\varphi_{0}(x,y)=x
and for
n\geq 1
,

(6.6)

\varphi_{n}(x,y)=x\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1+\theta(f^{k}(x,y))\right)^{1/d^{k+1}}

where
(\cdot)^{1/d^{n}}
is the principal branch of the
(d^{n})
-th root.
This makes sense because

\left|\theta(f^{k}(x,y))\right|\leq 1/6
for all
k\geq 0
.
Therefore the infinite product converges and
\varphi_{f}(x,y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{n}(x,y)
is well-defined.
To estimate this product we will use the following elementary inequalities, where
\alpha=1.1

(6.7)

\displaystyle\left|\exp(v)-1\right|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\left|v\right|\text{ for }\left|v\right|\leq 1/6,

(6.8)

\displaystyle\left|\log(1+u)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\left|u\right|\text{ for }\left|u\right|\leq 1/6.

(To prove these upper bounds, one simply expands
\exp(v)
and
\log(1-u)
in power series). Let us write

\left(1+\theta(f^{k}(x,y))\right)^{1/d^{k+1}}=1+u_{k}.

Then, the Inequalities (
6.7
) and (
6.8
) give

(6.9)

\displaystyle\left|u_{k}\right|=\left|\exp\left(\frac{1}{d^{k+1}}\log\left(1+\theta(f^{k}(x,y))\right)\right)-1\right|\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{d^{k+1}}\left|\theta\circ f^{k}(x,y)\right|;

and from Equation (
6.4
) and the lower bound
\left|x_{k}\right|\geq 20^{k}\left|x\right|
, we derive

(6.10)

\displaystyle\left|u_{k}\right|\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{d}\left(2+\frac{1}{6d}\right)\frac{1}{(20d)^{k}}\frac{R_{f}}{\left|x\right|}.

Now, the modulus of
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\log(1+u_{k})
is bounded from above by
\alpha\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left|u_{k}\right|
, and from the last upper bound this is less than
1/6
.
Thus, inequality (
6.7
) and the definition of
\varphi_{n}
in (
6.6
) give

(6.11)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\varphi_{n}(x,y)}{x}-1\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\exp\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\log(1+u_{k})\right)-1\right|\leq\alpha^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left|u_{k}\right|

(6.12)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha^{4}}{d}\left(2+\frac{1}{6d}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{(20d)^{k}}\right)\frac{R_{f}}{\left|x\right|}\leq\frac{8}{5}\frac{R_{f}}{\left|x\right|}.

The desired bound on
\varphi_{f}
follows by letting
n
go to
\infty
.
The corresponding estimate for
G_{f}
is immediate by writing

(6.13)

\left|G_{f}(x,y)-\log\left|x\right|\right|=\left|\log\left|\frac{\varphi_{f}(x,y)}{x}\right|\right|\leq{\max(\left|\log(1+u)\right|,\left|\log(1-u)\right|)},

where
u=\left|{\varphi_{f}(x,y)}/{x}-1\right|
is bounded by
(8/5)(1/12d)\leq 1/15

by (
6.12
), hence

\max(\left|\log(1\pm u)\right|)\leq\alpha{u}
, and the inequality
8\alpha/5<2
completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 6.3
.

Keep notation as above, and suppose as before that
\left|a\right|\leq R_{f}^{d-1}
.
Let

V^{+}=\left\{(x,y)\;;\;\left|x\right|>\max(\left|y\right|,12dR_{f})+4R_{f}\right\}.

Then for every

\left\{\varphi_{f}(x,y)=\varphi_{f}(x_{0},y_{0})\right\}

, whose slope over the disk

\left\{(x,y)\;;\;\left|x\right|>\max(\left|y\right|,12dR_{f})+6R_{f}\right\}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
6.2
, we have

\left|\varphi_{f}(x,y)-x\right|<2R_{f}
when
\left|x\right|\geq\max(\left|y\right|,12dR_{f})
.
Let
(x_{0},y_{0})
be an element of
V^{+}
and set

,
which satisfies
\left|x^{\varstar}\right|\geq\left|x_{0}\right|-2R_{f}>(12d+2)R_{f}

Set

.
The disk
\mathbb{D}(0,R^{\varstar})

contains
\mathbb{D}(0,12dR_{f})
.
Fix

in
\mathbb{D}(0,R^{\varstar})

and consider the function

for

.
For such a

can be applied to

.

6.1.3.
Crossed mappings

Let
\mathbb{B}_{1}=\mathbb{D}(0,10R_{f})^{2}
.
It is an easy consequence of Lemma
5.5

that for
\left|a\right|\leq R_{f}^{d-1}
,

is a Hénon-like map in
\mathbb{B}_{1}
. Here is a sharper result:

Lemma 6.4
.

With notation as above, if
\left|a\right|\leq R_{f}^{d-1}
then
f
is a crossed map of degree
d
from

\mathbb{B}_{1}
to
\mathbb{B}_{3}:=\mathbb{D}(0,6^{d}R_{f}^{d})\times\mathbb{D}(0,10R_{f})
.

Remark 6.5
.

As the proof will show, the conclusion of the lemma hold if
R_{f}
is replaced by any larger radius
R
. This will be used in Proposition
6.10
.

Proof.

We have to show that
(i)
f(\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}_{1})\cap\overline{\mathbb{B}_{3}}=\emptyset
,
(ii)
f(\mathbb{B}_{1})\cap\partial\mathbb{B}_{3}\subset\partial^{v}(\mathbb{B}_{3})
,
and (iii)
f(\mathbb{B}_{1})\cap{\mathbb{B}_{3}}\neq\emptyset
. Recall that
R_{f}=R_{p}
.

For the first condition, we observe that
(x,y)\in\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}_{1}
means

\left|y\right|\leq 10R_{f}=\left|x\right|
.
The lower bound of Lemma
5.5
gives

\left|\varphi_{p}(x)\right|\geq 7R_{f}
, so
\left|\varphi_{p}(p(x))\right|\geq(7R_{f})^{d}
; then, the upper bound gives

\left|p(x)\right|\geq(7R_{f})^{d}-2R_{f}
. Therefore

(6.14)

\left|p(x)+ay\right|\geq(7R_{f})^{d}-2R_{f}-10R_{f}^{d}\geq(7^{d}-12)R_{f}^{d}>6^{d}R_{f}^{d}.

To establish property (ii), suppose
(x,y)\in\mathbb{B}_{1}
is such that
f(x,y)\in\partial\mathbb{B}_{3}
. Property (i) implies
\left|x\right|<10R_{f}
,
so that
\left|\pi_{2}\circ f(x,y)\right|=\left|x\right|<10R_{f}
, that is,
f(x,y)\in\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}_{3}
.

For property (iii), consider the horizontal line
L=\{(x,0)\;;\;\left|x\right|\leq 10R_{f}\}\subset\mathbb{B}_{1}
.
Its image by
f
is parametrized by
x\mapsto(p(x),x)
, and the estimate obtained for
p(x)
above in proving (i) shows that
the first projection
x\mapsto p(x)
realizes a branched covering of degree
d
from
\mathbb{D}(0,10R_{f})

to a domain containing
\mathbb{D}(0,6^{d}R_{f}^{d})
. This proves (iii) and the fact that the crossed mapping

f
is of degree
d
.
∎

Proof of the upper estimate (

).

It is shown in
[

, Thm A.2]
that if
\varphi_{f}
is well defined on a neighborhood of

, then

(the infimum of such

We will show that for

Set

Lemma
6.4

show that

in

In particular,

Example 6.15

A typical situation where the assumptions of Proposition

Appendix A
Maps with few multipliers

Definition A.1
.

A loxodromic automorphism
f
is
exceptional
if there exists
a complex number
\kappa
such that
\lambda^{u}(p)=\kappa^{n}

for every
p\in\SPer_{n}(f)
and every
n\geq 1
.

This concept arises naturally in many rigidity issues
(see e.g.
[
1
]
or
[
11
, §2]
for our context).
We saw in Example
3.8
that analogues of
exceptional automorphisms exist in positive characteristic, but
we are not aware of any example in characteristic
0
. Partial non-existence results for exceptional
automorphisms have been obtained in
[
1
, Prop. 6.1]
and
[
11
, Prop. 8.9]
.

Theorem A.2
.

If
(f_{\lambda})
is an algebraic family of loxodromic polynomial automorphisms of
\mathbf{C}^{2}
in which any stable irreducible algebraic family is trivial, then the set of exceptional maps is discrete in
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subset\Lambda
be the set of exceptional maps. Put

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}=\left\{\lambda\in\Lambda\,;\,\forall m\leq n,\,\forall p\in\SPer_{m},\,\forall q\in\SPer_{n},\,(\lambda^{u}(p))^{n}=(\lambda^{u}(q))^{m}\right\}

and
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
,
so that
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
contains
\mathcal{E}
. Assume by way of contradiction that

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
is not discrete and pick an accumulation point

f_{0}
. For
n\geq 1
, let
V_{n}
be a neighborhood of
f_{0}
in which all saddle points of period at most
n
persist.
Then
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\cap V_{n}
is an analytic subset of positive dimension
in
V_{n}
; more precisely, it is a
union of components of the intersection of
V_{n}
with some algebraic subvariety of
\Lambda
. Reducing
V_{n}
if necessary we may assume that (i)
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\cap V_{n}
admits a unique connected component containing
f_{0}
and (ii)
V_{n+1}\subset V_{n}
. Let
n_{1}
be such that the germ of

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
at
f_{0}
is constant for
n\geq n_{1}
.

We claim that
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n_{1}}\cap V_{n_{1}}
is a stable family of type (I). Indeed, if

n\geq n_{1}
and
f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\cap V_{n}
, then
for every
p=p(f)\in\SPer_{n}(f)
and every
q=q(f)\in\SPer_{n_{1}}(f)
, we have a
relation of the form
\lambda^{u}(p)^{n_{1}}=\lambda^{u}(q)^{n}
. Since
f
has constant Jacobian, we have
\lambda^{s}(p)^{n_{1}}=\lambda^{s}(q)^{n}
as well.
Now,
q
can be
continued holomorphically as a saddle along any path in

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n_{1}}\cap V_{n_{1}}
, and
the persistence of the relations
\lambda^{u}(p)^{n_{1}}=\lambda^{u}(q)^{n}
and

\lambda^{s}(p)^{n_{1}}=\lambda^{s}(q)^{n}
show that
p
can be followed
as a saddle point along that path. Therefore
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n_{1}}\cap V_{n_{1}}
is
a stable family, as claimed.

By definition
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n_{1}}
,
and we have shown that all relations defining
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
persist along
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n_{1}}\cap V_{n_{1}}
,
thus
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\cap V_{n_{1}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n_{1}}\cap V_{n_{1}}
.
This reasoning shows that
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
is an analytic subset in
\Lambda
, which
is locally a union of local components of an algebraic variety. Thus, by analytic continuation, we conclude that any global analytic component of
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
is a
stable algebraic family, and the existence of a positive dimensional component contradicts our assumption. Therefore
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
is a discrete set.
∎
