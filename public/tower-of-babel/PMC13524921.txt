Extension of overlap measures for Multi-Class Biomarker Evaluation in Alzheimer's Disease
Authors: Victor Miguel Sierra Marquina, Maria del Carmen Pardo, Alba Maria Franco-Pereira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn a progressive disease such as the Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the availability of suitable biomarkers tracking the stages of its progression could markedly accelerate drug development by providing an earlier indication of drug efficacy. Investigators use diagnostic tests to classify disease stages into probable Alzheimer’s disease, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and normal cognitive aging. In this paper, we focus on developing a proper statistical overlap measure-based method to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of tests with three diagnostic categories. Parametric and non-parametric approaches for the estimation of the overlap measure (OVL) are presented as well as their bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs). The performance of these estimations and its CIs are evaluated through simulations. Furthermore, it is compared with the Volume Under the ROC Surface (VUS), the most common measure to assess the accuracy of tests with three ordinal diagnostic categories. A neuropsychological data set from a longitudinal cohort study for the detection of biomarkers for identifying stages of Alzheimer’s disease is discussed.Keywords: Diagnostic accuracy, Volume under the ROC surface (VUS), Overlap measure (OVL), Alzheimer’s diseaseSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that leads to the death of brain cells that cannot be replaced once lost. While the use of biomarkers in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) research has expanded significantly, current clinical and ethical guidelines–such as those outlined by the National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) and neurological societies–limit the use of biological biomarker-based methods for presymptomatic diagnosis in routine clinical practice. The development of in-vivo biomarkers has moved the diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease from the dementia stage towards the prodromal stage and has introduced the potential for preclinical diagnosis (i.e., before symptom onset). These developments are relevant for the testing of potential therapies for secondary prevention of Alzheimer’s disease1. In the context of current diagnostic practices, challenges such as the high cost and limited accessibility of advanced biomarkers (e.g. PET imaging, CSF assays), as well as diagnostic uncertainty in cases with overlapping clinical features (e.g. MCI vs early AD), remain significant.

While there are many proposals for diagnostic testing of binary markers (healthy vs. diseased), there is a lack of research and statistical tools for three-population scenarios that can limit disease prevention and early detection efforts. The transitional stage in the AD process is critical to detect not only because it is indicative of more serious disease development in the future but also because this is the time for family caregivers to discuss care options and family future planning while the patient can still take part in making decisions2. In this paper, we will consider mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and/or early stage Alzheimer’s disease as a transition stage between the cognitive changes of normal aging and the more serious problems caused by Alzheimer’s disease (AD).

When it is critical to classify individuals into three groups (i.e. normal, early disease stage, fully developed disease stage), Scurfield3 introduced the ROC surface as a natural generalization of the ROC curve. Mossman4 proposed a polyhedral ROC surface and the volume under the surface as a summary measure of the diagnostic accuracy. Dreiseitl et al.5 further provided a non-parametric estimate and the associated variance for the volume under a polyhedral ROC surface with probabilistic rating data. The ROC surface for multi-class diagnostic problems, in a non-parametric context, was proposed by Nakas and Yiannoutsos6. Reference 7 also discussed the nonparametric estimation of a single VUS. Both parametric and nonparametric inferences on the VUS have been developed2,8,9). Kang and Tian10 offered an extensive study comparing possible parametric and non-parametric approaches for the estimation of VUS in terms of bias and root mean square error.

The VUS is an extension of the total area under the ROC curve (AUC). However, the drawbacks of the AUC as a measure of a biomarker’s diagnostic accuracy to distinguish between two disease states are known and many other alternative summary indices have been proposed Franco Pereira et al.15. Recently, overlapping coefficients (OVL) are widely used in many fields, for example authors in Mizuno et al.11 compared pharmacokinetic parameters of the same drug for two ethnically different regions; the niche overlap of two species, which uses up to n different resources, is addressed by Mueller and Altenberg12; the proportion of devices that have a similar range of failure time has been analyzed by Al-Saleh and Samawi13. Pardo and Franco-Pereira14 explored the advantages of the overlap measures over the ROC summary indices to assess the accuracy of a medical diagnostic test in the binary case. The overlap measures have some desirable properties such as invariance to monotone increasing transformations as the ROC summary indices. AUC does not capture shape differences whereas OVL has the advantage of being able to detect differences in both location and shape between the underlying distributions of diseased and healthy samples. Moreover, AUC yields improper values when it is 0.5 or lower, which occurs when the mean score for diseased individuals is less than the mean score for healthy individuals. Therefore, the overlap measures have been proved to be better than the ROC summary indices for assessing diagnostic tests, not only because they perform well when the ROC summary indices fail but also because they are comparable or even superior when the latter are effective26 and Alcaraz-Peñalba et al.16 addressed the need for robust methods to construct confidence intervals for OVL when dealing with two-class problem by proposing both parametric and non-parametric approaches and recommended different cutoff values for classifying differentiation quality.

Given the advantages of OVL in the two-class case, the aim of this study is to extend and investigate the OVL as a measure of a biomarker’s ability to distinguish among three disease groups. In Sect. "VUS and OVL measures", we provide the motivation for the use of OVL and highlight its advantages in relation to the VUS. Several parametric and non-parametric estimation approaches are proposed for this measure, along with the construction of bootstrap-based confidence intervals (CIs). Additionally, we introduce inferential methods for OVL to compare two biomarkers, as well as its extension to multiclass classification problems. We then apply our methodology to estimate the diagnostic accuracy of AD biomarkers to classify from MCI to AD. A simulation study is conducted to compare OVL estimation procedures in terms of bias and root mean squared error (RMSE), and to assess the performance of the CI estimates with respect to achieving the nominal coverage probability. Furthermore, we compare the power of OVL with that of VUS in identifying potential biomarkers. We conclude with a discussion of our findings and provide recommendations for the appropriate use of OVL.

The drawbacks of VUS are analogous to those associated with the AUC in the binary disease classification context. First, VUS relies on the stochastic order assumption which may not hold for many diseases. In fact, when this assumption is violated, the VUS should not be used, as it can be as low as 1/6, even if the underlying biomarker has perfect discriminatory ability. Second, VUS is sensitive to the number of crossings between the class-conditional density functions. For a detailed discussion, see Samawi et al.17.

which corresponds to the sum of the three error probabilities–namely, the probabilities of failing to correctly classify an observation into its true class. From a probabilistic standpoint, the OVL quantifies the likelihood that a randomly drawn observation from each group (e.g. cognitively normal, MCI, AD) lies in a region where its group is least distinguishable from the others. This measure captures the degree of uncertainty or ambiguity in group separation based on biomarker distributions, without relying on predefined thresholds or classification rules.

The OVL measure has a limitation in extreme scenarios where the biomarker distributions for the first two classes are nearly indistinguishable, while the third class is clearly separated. In such cases, OVL may misleadingly indicate strong overall discriminatory ability, despite the biomarker’s inability to differentiate between the first two classes. This highlights the need for caution when interpreting low OVL values as evidence of a promising biomarker. A necessary follow-up step is to perform pairwise comparisons to assess whether adequate separation exists between each pair of classes. This can be readily achieved using the two-class version of OVL26. In fact, Nakas and Yiannoutsos6 recommended examining pairwise comparisons using ROC curves after computing the VUS value, as this approach provides additional insight into the differences among the three groups. Once a diagnostic test has been proven accurate enough for practical use, a cutoff value (or values) for screening subjects must be determined. As discussed in Sect. 3.4 of Nakas34, both VUS and OVL are useful measures for evaluating the discrimination ability of the marker under study but not to select the cut-off points that may be used for clinical decision-making. A review of several methods for optimal cut-point selection can be found in Hassanzad and Hajian-Tilaki21.

The OVL manifold

Evaluating the overall discrimination capacity of a marker for multi-class classification problems is a challenging task. A seminal work on optimal ROC manifolds was promulgated by Scurfield25. Clinical workflow for the application of the overlap measure

To bridge the gap between methodological development and practical application, a structured clinical workflow for the implementation of the overlap measure OVL is proposed in Fig. 1. This framework is designed to support clinicians in evaluating the discriminatory ability of biomarkers in a practical, interpretable, and threshold-free manner across a wide range of diagnostic settings, without requiring prior alignment or transformation of biomarker direction.

Clinical workflow for applying the OVL in biomarker-based diagnosis.

In contrast to alternative measures such as the VUS, which require a preliminary alignment of biomarker direction (e.g. ensuring a consistent ordering across diagnostic groups), the OVL can be directly computed from the observed data without additional preprocessing steps. This property simplifies its application in practice and reduces the risk of misinterpretation due to incorrect data transformations.

The workflow reflects the typical steps followed in clinical practice, from data collection to biomarker evaluation and decision support. When low overlap is observed, additional pairwise comparisons can be performed to identify specific limitations in discriminatory performance. Unlike alternative approaches such as VUS, the OVL does not require prior alignment of biomarker direction, facilitating its direct application in practice.

The process begins with the collection and preprocessing of patient biomarker data, including clinical, biological, or imaging-derived variables. Next, individuals are classified into clinically meaningful diagnostic categories defined according to the problem under study. These groups may correspond to different disease stages, severity levels, or distinct clinical conditions, and provide the basis for evaluating the separation between distributions.

The OVL is then computed to quantify the degree of overlap among the distributions associated with each diagnostic group. This can be achieved using either parametric approaches, assuming specific distributional forms (optionally supported by transformations such as Box-Cox), or non-parametric methods based on kernel density estimation. The resulting OVL value represents the extent to which the groups are not distinguishable. To support biomarker selection, OVL values can be compared across multiple candidate biomarkers. Those exhibiting lower overlap are preferred, as they provide greater diagnostic discrimination. Interpretation of the OVL is straightforward: lower values indicate reduced overlap between groups and therefore better discriminatory performance of the biomarker, whereas higher values reflect increased ambiguity in classification. The interpretation of OVL values in the three-class setting follows guidelines analogous to the two-class case, as given in Franco-Pereira et al.26 For practical interpretation, we propose the following thresholds: OVL values between 0.8 and 1.0 indicatepoor discriminatory ability, values between 0.5 and 0.8 indicate moderate discrimination, values between 0.3 and 0.5indicate good discrimination, values between 0.1 and 0.3 indicate very good discrimination, and values below 0.1 indicateexcellent discrimination.. These thresholds provide a practical and intuitive framework for assessing the overall discriminatory ability of a biomarker. In practice, when the OVL is low, additional pairwise analyses are recommended to avoid situations in which only a category exhibits good separation. This complementary approach provides further insight into the discriminatory performance of the biomarker and supports more informed clinical interpretation. In addition, OVL can be visually supported through graphical representations such as histograms or density plots, enhancing interpretability.

Therefore, the results derived from OVL analysis can be incorporated as a complementary tool in clinical decision-making. While not intended to replace established approaches such as the volume under the ROC surface (VUS), OVL provides a robust and easily interpretable alternative, particularly in scenarios where the underlying assumptions required by VUS are not satisfied. Its low computational cost and intuitive probabilistic interpretation make it especially suitable for integration into clinical workflows and decision-support systems across diverse medical domains.

To illustrate the practical implementation of this framework, we consider real data collected by the Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center at the University of Washington27. Several neuropsychological biomarkers with different discriminatory profiles are analyzed. The results show that, in scenarios where the assumptions underlying the VUS are reasonably satisfied, both VUS and OVL lead to consistent conclusions. However, in more challenging situations, where the stochastic ordering of the distributions is not clearly fulfilled, OVL remains directly applicable and provides a more reliable and transparent assessment of group separability.

Application to a real data

Alzheimer’s is the most common degenerative dementia, affecting approximately 47% of the population aged 85 or older. Neuropsychometric tests have been used to detect Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) compared to normal aging and Alzheimer’s. The database we have considered includes individuals from a longitudinal cohort of the Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center at the University of Washington (ADRC)27 and is available in the GitHub repository28. The severity of each individual’s disease was classified according to the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR). The dataset contains 14 neuropsychometric markers. These markers were measured in 118 individuals aged 75, divided into three diagnostic categories: the non-demented or healthy group (, N = 45) with CDR = 0; the MCI group (, N = 44) with CDR = 0.5; and the Alzheimer’s group (, N = 29) with CDR = 1.

Temporal function (ktemp), visual retention (zbentd), and language performance as assessed by the Boston Naming Test (zboston) capture complementary cognitive domains that are differentially affected in the early stages of Alzheimer’s disease. The temporal factor (ktemp) primarily reflects functions subserved by the medial temporal lobe, including the hippocampus, a region critically involved in episodic memory and one of the earliest sites of Alzheimer-related neurodegeneration driven by tau pathology. Visual retention (zbentd), derived from the Benton Visual Retention Test, assesses visuospatial memory and perceptual organization, which are associated with temporo-parietal networks and may reveal subtle deficits not captured by verbal memory tasks. The Boston Naming Test (zboston) evaluates lexical retrieval and semantic memory, functions linked to temporal neocortical regions that become increasingly affected as the disease progresses. Clinically, the combination of these markers provides a multidimensional characterization of cognitive decline, capturing early hippocampal dysfunction (ktemp), non-verbal memory impairment (zbentd), and emerging language deficits (zboston). Biologically, their alterations are consistent with the spatial progression of Alzheimer’s pathology, from medial temporal structures to broader cortical areas, supporting their joint relevance for improving early detection and discrimination between normal aging, Mild Cognitive Impairment, and Alzheimer’s disease.

The variable ktemp reflects memory-related cognitive processes, which are known to be critically affected in neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease. In fact, previous analyses such as Luo and Xiong29 have identified ktemp as a highly effective biomarker for discriminating among cognitively normal individuals, patients with MCI, and patients with Alzheimer’s disease, providing a VUS value of 0.75. In that work, the assumption of normality was also assessed and found to be reasonable for this biomarker. Consistent with this evidence, the distributions of ktemp tend to follow a clear ordering across diagnostic groups (see Fig. S1 in the Supplementary Information), leading to similar conclusions when evaluated using both VUS and OVL. Accordingly, for this biomarker we report parametric estimators under the normality assumption.

Comparison of the size and power of the OVL and VUS estimators

Concluding remarks

This work introduces the Overlap Measure (OVL) as a novel approach to evaluating the discriminatory power of biomarkers in neurodegenerative disease diagnosis. By quantifying the probability that each group’s observations fall in regions where their distribution is least distinct from others, the OVL offers a probabilistically grounded and threshold-free measure of classification ambiguity. While not intended to replace current diag- nostic tools like VUS (Volume Under the ROC Surface), the OVL can serve as a practical adjunct rather than a replacement. Importantly, OVL can be applied in a broader range of scenarios, including cases in which the assumptions required for VUS are not satisfied, allowing the evaluation of biomarkers that would otherwise be difficult to assess. We acknowledge the importance of aligning with current clinical and ethical standards for AD diagnosis. We recognize that clinical integration of OVL would require development of user-friendly software, clinician training, and prospective validation. Nevertheless, the OVL’s low computational cost and interpretability make it a promising candidate for integration into decision-support systems in neurology.

The volume under the ROC surface (VUS) can be regarded as a natural extension of the area under the ROC curve (AUC) to three-class classification problems and has been widely used for evaluating diagnostic markers in this setting. However, our results highlight several important limitations when applying VUS in practice. First, under ideal conditions -namely, equal variances across groups and clear stochastic ordering- VUS provides a reasonable global summary of discrimination. Second, when these assumptions are violated, as is often the case in real clinical data (e.g. heterogeneous variability between normal aging, Mild Cognitive Impairment, and Alzheimer’s disease), the performance of VUS deteriorates substantially. In particular, our simulation results (Tables 14, 17, and S8) show that its statistical power can drop to values close to the nominal significance level (≈ 0.05), indicating a failure to detect truly informative biomarkers. Third, this limitation has direct practical implications: reliance on VUS may lead to the incorrect dismissal of clinically relevant markers, especially those exhibiting overlapping but still meaningful distributions across groups.

In contrast, the proposed OVL, inspired by earlier two-class formulations14,17, remains well-defined and robust under heteroscedasticity and non-ordered distributions. From a methodological perspective, OVL provides a direct quantification of the shared probability mass among the three groups, offering a more stable and interpretable measure of discrimination in complex scenarios. From a practical standpoint, this translates into improved sensitivity for identifying biomarkers that retain clinical value despite partial overlap between diagnostic categories. Across most simulated scenarios, OVL demonstrates higher statistical power than VUS, and even in cases where VUS performs slightly better, the differences are marginal and do not outweigh its instability in more realistic settings. Importantly, the use of OVL reduces the risk of overlooking potentially useful, less invasive, or cost-effective biomarkers that could support early detection and clinical decision-making. While neither VUS nor OVL directly provides optimal classification thresholds, the improved reliability of OVL makes it a more suitable preliminary tool within diagnostic evaluation pipelines, complementing existing threshold-selection methods (e.g. Hassanzad and Hajian-Tilaki21) and enhancing the overall assessment of candidate biomarkers in real-world applications.

Parametric and non-parametric estimations of OVL have been proposed. Bootstrap confidence intervals were also proposed and evaluated in terms of attaining the nominal coverage probability based on a simulation study.

Future research involves the development of more accurate methods for confidence intervals construction for OVL in a three-class disease setting, to account for possible effects of covariates on the OVL and to study deeply the OVL measure for comparing 2 or more biomarkers as well as the extension to the k-classes problem.
