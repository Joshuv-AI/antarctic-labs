Training dentists in tobacco cessation counselling in Manila, Philippines: a mixed-methods study with implications for practice and education
Authors: Priyanka Ramesh Jain, Annalie DeLemos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTobacco use remains a leading public health challenge in the Philippines, contributing substantially to oral and systemic diseases. Dental professionals are well placed to deliver tobacco cessation counselling (TCC), yet its integration into routine practice remains limited. Evidence on theory-informed training interventions for dental professionals in low- and middle-income countries is scarce. This study evaluated the effectiveness of a structured training intervention on dentists’ capability, opportunity and motivation to deliver TCC within dental settings.MethodsA convergent mixed-methods design was used. 30 practising dentists involved in clinical teaching and patient care were purposively recruited from 14 dental schools in Manila, Philippines. Participants completed an 8-hour face-to-face training based on WHO-endorsed brief tobacco intervention models (5A’s/5R’s) and motivational interviewing. Quantitative data were collected using pretraining and immediate post-training surveys assessing knowledge, preparedness, attitudes and perceived barriers. Data were analysed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Qualitative data were collected through semistructured interviews with 11 participants within 4 weeks of training. Eight transcripts were included in the final analysis and analysed thematically using the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation–Behaviour (COM-B) framework. Findings were integrated during interpretation using a side-by-side comparison mapped to COM-B domains.ResultsPost-training scores showed statistically significant improvements across all domains (all p<0.05). Awareness/knowledge increased by 60% and preparedness/skills by 64%. Qualitative findings indicated enhanced counselling capability, improved awareness of referral pathways (opportunity) and stronger professional motivation. However, system-level barriers, such as limited referral infrastructure and time constraints, persisted.ConclusionsA brief, structured training intervention significantly improved Filipino dentists’ capability, opportunity and motivation to deliver TCC. However, sustained implementation will require integration into dental curricula, establishment of clear referral pathways and broader health system support. These findings provide context-specific evidence to inform the next phase of the research programme, which focuses on policy integration and curriculum reform.Keywords: Epidemiologic Research Design; Health Personnel; Public Health Practice; Qualitative Research; Education, Medical

Introduction

Tobacco use remains a leading preventable cause of premature morbidity and mortality worldwide and is projected to account for approximately 8.7 million deaths annually by 2030.1 It increases the risk of respiratory and cardiovascular disease2 and is causally associated with oral cancers, such as leukoplakia3 and oral squamous cell carcinoma,4 periodontal disease, higher rates of tooth loss5 6 and impaired wound healing.7 8 These effects make tobacco use directly relevant to dental practice, positioning dental professionals as key contributors to cessation efforts.

In the Philippines, tobacco use remains a major public health challenge, with approximately one in five adults identified as current users (19.5%), with a higher prevalence among males (34.7%) than females (4.2%).9 Combustible cigarettes remain the most commonly used product,10 with increasing use and experimentation with electronic cigarettes, particularly among adolescents and young adults.11 These trends underscore the need for health systems to adapt cessation strategies across a wider range of healthcare settings, including oral health services.

While national tobacco control efforts in the country have focused on population-level tobacco control measures such as tobacco taxation, smoke-free policies, graphic health warnings and a national Quitline,12 13 cessation support at the point of care remains less developed. Recent Department of Health (DOH) administrative orders call for integrating tobacco cessation services across health programmes, including oral health, but offer limited operational guidance on dentists’ roles in routine cessation delivery.14 In dental settings, integration of tobacco cessation counselling (TCC) is further constrained by uncertainty regarding dentists’ role in tobacco cessation, low awareness and utilisation of referral pathways, and competing clinical demands that restrict time for preventive counselling.

Dental professionals are well placed to deliver brief TCC because of their repeated contact with patients, opportunities for early identification of tobacco-related oral disease and ability to provide brief preventive advice during routine care.15,17 However, tobacco-related education within dental training is often delivered in a predominantly theoretical manner, with limited emphasis on practical counselling skills, structured intervention models and referral pathways.18 Within the Philippine context, these limitations are compounded by unclear expectations regarding dentists’ roles in cessation support, competing clinical demands and limited integration of cessation training into undergraduate curricula.

Despite growing recognition of the importance of behavioural approaches in implementation research, few studies in dental settings have applied comprehensive frameworks to examine how training interventions influence clinicians’ capability, opportunity and motivation to deliver TCC, particularly within low- and middle-income settings.19,22 To address this gap, the present study evaluates a structured TCC training intervention for dental educators in Manila using the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation–Behaviour (COM-B) model.21 The COM-B framework was selected because it enables examination of both individual behavioural capability and the contextual factors influencing implementation within clinical practice. Using a mixed-methods approach, the study explores measurable post-training changes alongside organisational and system-level influences affecting implementation within dental settings.

This study forms part of a broader three-phase research programme aimed at strengthening TCC among dental professionals in the Philippines (figure 1). Phase 1 identified gaps in knowledge, counselling skills, confidence, referral awareness and institutional support, while the current phase focuses on evaluating a structured capacity-building intervention. Findings from this phase will inform phase 3 work on policy integration and curriculum reform, with potential relevance for other low- and middle-income settings.

Materials and methods

Study design

A convergent mixed-methods design with a dominant quantitative strand was employed. The study combined quantitative pre–post surveys with qualitative semistructured interviews conducted within 4 weeks of the intervention. Quantitative and qualitative data were analysed separately and integrated at the interpretation stage to provide a comprehensive understanding of changes in knowledge, preparedness and contextual factors influencing implementation. Integration was performed using a side-by-side comparison of quantitative and qualitative findings mapped to the COM-B domains.

Sampling and recruitment

Formal invitations were sent to the deans of 14 dental schools in Manila, Philippines, requesting the nomination of faculty members. Eligible participants were dental professionals/educators involved in clinical teaching, community dentistry or patient care, as these roles were considered most relevant to integrating TCC into practice and education. Participants were purposively selected from nominated candidates based on predefined eligibility criteria, including active involvement in clinical or teaching roles and willingness to participate in both training and evaluation activities. A total of 30 participants were included (based on the logistical capacity of the pilot training programme), representing multiple institutions to capture diverse academic and clinical perspectives.

This sample size was considered appropriate for an exploratory pilot intervention designed to assess short-term changes in knowledge, preparedness and perceived barriers following training.23 All participants completed the preintervention and postintervention surveys, and a subset participated in follow-up qualitative interviews. Intervention: brief tobacco intervention training

The intervention consisted of an 8-hour face-to-face training programme delivered by subject experts from the Lung Center of the Philippines in collaboration with the DOH. The training combined WHO-endorsed brief intervention models (5A’s/5R’s) with motivational interviewing.24 Eight modules were delivered using a combination of lectures, video-based role-plays, facilitated discussions and hands-on activities. Training content covered tobacco use epidemiology and oral health at global and national levels; behavioural models and motivational interviewing; practical application of the 5A’s and 5R’s frameworks; communication skills and role-play; and referral systems and follow-up, including linkage to DOH cessation services such as the national Quitline. Pharmacotherapy content focused on basic principles of nicotine replacement therapy, patient education and appropriate referral, consistent with dentists’ scope of practice in the Philippines. The training did not authorise prescribing.

The training was accredited as a continuing professional development activity, and participants received certification on completion. As participation was linked to Continuing Professional Development (CPD) credits, this may have influenced participant motivation and engagement, potentially affecting self-reported outcomes.

Data collection

Quantitative data

A structured questionnaire adapted from a previously published instrument and modified for the Philippine context was used.25 Additional items captured sociodemographic characteristics. The questionnaire was pilot tested for clarity, feasibility and contextual relevance, with minor wording revisions made to reduce ambiguity. The questionnaire was informed by the COM-B framework.21 Items assessing knowledge of tobacco cessation guidelines and counselling approaches reflected capability, while perceived skills and confidence aligned with motivation. Factors relating to time constraints, referral pathways, institutional support and patient receptivity captured opportunity. This framework guided both instrument development and interpretation.

The final instrument comprised 30 items across six domains: awareness of TCC; awareness and knowledge; preparedness and skills to provide counselling; perceived barriers to counselling; and recommendations for improvement. An additional domain assessed perceived barriers specific to the training intervention (brief tobacco intervention training). Responses were measured using a 7-point Likert scale (from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree’).

Pretraining surveys were administered immediately before the training, and post-training surveys were administered immediately after completion on the same day. Identical content was used across electronic and paper formats to maximise participation and accommodate varying access preferences. Content validity was established through expert review by three public health dentistry and clinical practice specialists. Pilot testing demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.83).

Participant confidentiality was maintained throughout.

Qualitative data

Semistructured interviews of approximately 30 min were conducted within 4 weeks of training to explore how the training influenced participants’ TCC behaviours, guided by the COM-B framework. All 30 participants were invited to participate in interviews, and 11 consented.

Interviews were conducted by trained researchers with experience in qualitative health research. The interview guide was developed collaboratively by the research team and DOH experts, reviewed by public health specialists and pilot tested for clarity. It included open-ended questions and probes addressing capability, opportunity, motivation, perceived barriers to implementation and suggestions for improving future training (onlinesupplemental materials 1 2). Interviews were conducted within 4 weeks of training completion at locations convenient to participants. Sample sufficiency was determined based on thematic saturation and the concept of information power, where the focused study aim, a relatively homogenous participant group and application of the COM-B framework enabled in-depth analysis despite a smaller number of transcripts.26 27

Data analysis

All participants provided written informed consent before data collection, including consent for audio recording of interviews.

Quantitative analysis

Quantitative data were analysed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM) and checked for completeness. Missing data were handled using pairwise deletion. Pre-post differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, appropriate for paired ordinal data from non-normally distributed samples. Descriptive statistics were reported as means and SDs, with CIs presented where appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05, and effect sizes were calculated to support interpretation.

Given the small sample size and exploratory design, analyses focused on overall patterns rather than multivariable models. Potential confounders, including years of clinical experience, prior tobacco cessation training and practice setting, were examined descriptively but not adjusted for statistically. The potential influence of residual or unmeasured confounding was acknowledged as a limitation. Participant data were stored confidentially using unique study codes without direct identifiers.

Qualitative analysis

Interviews were transcribed verbatim and deidentified. Participants were assigned unique study codes (P1–P11), which were used consistently across analysis and reporting. Audio files and transcripts were stored on password-protected devices accessible only to the research team, and only deidentified quotations were reported. Of the 11 interviews conducted, eight transcripts were included in the final analysis based on predefined criteria of completeness, clarity and analytical relevance. While sample size was guided by thematic saturation, the number of transcripts analysed was smaller than that reported in some methodological studies, which may limit the breadth of thematic coverage.

Data were analysed using a hybrid inductive-deductive thematic approach, supported by NVivo software and guided by the COM-B framework. Transcripts and open-ended responses were initially coded inductively to identify meaningful units of text. Codes were subsequently grouped into themes and deductively mapped to the COM-B domains of Capability, Opportunity and Motivation. Coding was conducted independently by two researchers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus. Data saturation was reached when no new themes emerged. Representative quotations were selected to illustrate key findings.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study.

Qualitative results

Qualitative analysis identified recurring patterns across participants, highlighting how gains in individual capability and motivation following training were shaped and constrained by contextual and system-level factors influencing opportunities for implementation. Themes are presented below within the COM-B framework.

Capability (COM-B): knowledge, skills and perceived preparedness

Participants described increased understanding of brief intervention models, particularly the 5A’s framework, and greater clarity regarding how counselling could be incorporated into routine dental care. Several participants (n=6/8) reported that the training provided their first structured exposure to TCC.

Participants with limited prior exposure to cessation counselling reported substantial gains in preparedness, while those with prior experience noted that the training helped clarify and improve application of existing knowledge.

P2: Before attending, I had no idea how I would do it. Right after the training, of course, I have a little bit of an idea of how we should approach it.

P3: I am more confident to give counselling.

P1: This is new to me. Now I know the process.

The training was commonly described as understandable and relevant to dental practice. Interactive components, particularly role-plays, case discussions and practical demonstrations, were frequently cited as helpful for developing communication skills:

P3: The training was easy to understand, especially the role-playing part.

Some participants also commented on elements of the training that affected learning, including the difficulty of the pretest and the format of the lectures.

P5: The pre-test was difficult, and I think the lecture could be made more engaging.

Despite these gains, some participants described feeling unsure about how to respond when patients were resistant or how to continue conversations beyond an initial discussion, suggesting a need for further skills development.

P5: Sometimes I’m not sure how to continue when the patient is not interested.

Opportunity: system-level constraints shaping behavioural translation

Participants consistently described a gap (n=6/8) between feeling ready to deliver TCC and being able to do so within the realities of everyday dental practice. Although training increased confidence and motivation, opportunities to act were often constrained by organisational factors.

Time pressure, competing clinical priorities and the limited integration of preventive counselling into routine workflows were commonly identified as barriers. Participants (n=5/8) described practical challenges such as not having enough time during consultations, the absence of structured workflows and limited system-level support for brief counselling, all of which made it difficult to sustain cessation support in everyday practice. Some suggested that short, standardised prompts could make counselling more feasible within routine care, underscoring the importance of organisational design in enabling practice.

P8: Even if I want to counsel, there’s nowhere to refer the patient after that.

At the same time, participants reported increased awareness of available cessation services following training, including referral options linked to national programmes:

P4: I am now aware that DOH is reaching out to people who wish to quit smoking.

P7: I now refer patients to the Lung Center of the Philippines and inform them about nicotine patches.

These findings help explain the increase in quantitative scores related to perceived barriers, reflecting greater awareness of system-level constraints following training rather than a worsening of practice conditions.

Motivation: professional role identity, advocacy and moderated counselling intentions

Participants described a strengthened sense of professional responsibility to address tobacco use as part of comprehensive oral healthcare (n=6/8). Motivation was reflected primarily in professional identity and expressed intentions to engage in counselling and advocacy.

P1: It is our advocacy.

Motivation was also reflected in participants’ intentions to advocate for TCC beyond individual clinical encounters. Dentists described interest in promoting training, integrating cessation content into dental education and encouraging colleagues to engage in similar programmes:

P4: I want to teach smoking cessation counselling to my students.

P8: I encourage my peers to attend the same training.

Despite this increased motivational readiness, participants expressed concerns about patient receptivity to cessation advice, particularly in contexts where tobacco use was socially normalised or not perceived as relevant to dental care. Some participants reported hesitation in initiating counselling when they anticipated that patients might not view tobacco cessation as relevant to dental care:

P2: I know it’s important, but sometimes patients don’t see it as part of dental care.

Integration of findings

Qualitative findings provide context for the quantitative results by explaining how improvements in capability and motivation were shaped by real-world practice conditions. Increases in knowledge and preparedness correspond to enhanced capability, while the observed rise in perceived barriers reflects greater recognition of contextual and system-level constraints following training rather than an actual increase in barriers.

For example, increased reporting of barriers in quantitative findings was explained by qualitative accounts highlighting time constraints, workflow limitations and restricted referral pathways. Although motivation to provide TCC increased, its translation into routine practice remained dependent on organisational support and structural factors.

Discussion

This mixed-methods evaluation examined the effectiveness of a structured TCC training on dental educators’ knowledge, preparedness and motivation to deliver cessation support. Quantitative findings demonstrated improvements in self-reported capability-related domains, while qualitative findings provided insight into how contextual factors influenced the translation of these gains into practice. Increased reporting of barriers reflects greater recognition of real-world constraints following training, rather than a deterioration in implementation conditions.

In particular, training improved dentists’ understanding of brief intervention frameworks, notably the 5A’s, and enhanced perceived preparedness to initiate cessation discussions. However, participants’ accounts suggest that translating these gains into consistent practice remained contingent on time availability, institutional support and the integration of TCC within existing service delivery structures. Improvements were observed across participants; however, subgroup differences could not be assessed due to the exploratory design and sample size.

These findings are consistent with international evidence demonstrating that dental professionals can play a critical role in tobacco cessation when equipped with practical counselling skills.28,33 However, similar to studies in Malaysia, India and the UK, this study shows that competing clinical priorities and organisational constraints limit consistent implementation.34,36 The persistence of these barriers across diverse settings suggests that the gap between training and practice is structural rather than solely related to individual competence.

The improvements in preparedness and counselling confidence observed in this study align with these reports. Continuous emphasis on the incorporation of tobacco cessation training at the undergraduate level and after completion of the dental degree is important.37

These findings are consistent with evidence suggesting that improvements in provider capability alone may be insufficient to sustain behavioural change without supportive systems.38 Although brief interventions delivered by dental professionals can increase quit attempts, patient outcomes are strengthened when counselling is supported by pharmacotherapy access and structured referral pathways.30,32 Participants’ accounts of limited referral options and restricted access to nicotine replacement therapy reflect these broader implementation challenges.

In the Philippine context, these structural challenges are further shaped by cultural and social factors. Tobacco use is embedded within social norms, gender roles and economic conditions that influence patient readiness to engage with cessation advice. Previous studies have highlighted the role of cultural and social context in shaping health communication and behaviour change, including in tobacco cessation interventions.39,41 In this study, participants reported that linking cessation advice to visible oral health conditions was an effective strategy to initiate discussions with patients, consistent with evidence suggesting that clinically relevant oral health concerns may enhance engagement with cessation interventions.30 However, concerns about patient receptivity and potential impacts on rapport underscore the need for culturally sensitive communication approaches that align cessation advice with patient priorities.

Nevertheless, concerns persisted regarding providers’ perceptions of patient receptivity and the potential impact on rapport, particularly where patients prioritised immediate procedural treatment. Although participants expressed interest in advocating for TCC within their institutions or incorporating cessation content into teaching, such efforts were often constrained by limited institutional prioritisation and the absence of formal curricular integration. Consistent with prior studies, the absence of formal curricular integration and organisational endorsement may limit the sustainability and scalability of training gains.42,45

Strengths and limitations

This study highlights practical lessons for implementing tobacco cessation training in dental settings. Mixed-methods findings showed that while training improved knowledge and preparedness, awareness of system-level constraints influenced feasibility, underscoring the need to pair skills-based training with institutional support and referral pathways.

However, several limitations should be considered. The sample size was small and based on purposive recruitment of dental educators from institutions in Manila, which may limit generalisability to other settings, including rural and private practice contexts. Outcomes were based on short-term, self-reported measures collected immediately after training and may be influenced by social desirability bias. The study did not assess behavioural changes in clinical practice or patient-level outcomes. In addition, the qualitative sample was limited, which may restrict the diversity of perspectives captured. Finally, information on tobacco cessation education in dental training was based on participant self-report rather than formal curriculum review. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Implications for practice, policy and education

This study makes several important contributions. First, it provides context-specific evidence from a low- and middle-income country on the effectiveness of a theory-informed tobacco cessation training intervention for dental professionals. Second, by applying the COM-B framework within a mixed-methods design, it offers a more comprehensive understanding of how capability, opportunity and motivation interact in real-world settings. Third, it demonstrates that increased awareness of barriers following training is not a negative outcome, but a critical step towards identifying implementation gaps and informing system-level solutions.

A coordinated, system-oriented approach is required to integrate TCC into dental settings. Three priority actions emerge.

At the system level, strengthening referral pathways and access to cessation services is essential to support continuity of care beyond brief counselling. This aligns with national priorities outlined in the Philippine National Tobacco Prevention and Control Strategy 2030.46

At the practice level, embedding brief, structured counselling prompts into routine workflows may improve feasibility in time-constrained clinical environments.

At the education level, integrating competency-based tobacco cessation training into undergraduate curricula and continuing professional development is critical to building sustained capacity.

Together, these findings underscore that training alone is insufficient. Effective implementation depends on aligning individual capability with supportive organisational structures and health system policies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a structured, theory-informed training intervention can improve dental professionals’ knowledge, preparedness and motivation to deliver TCC. However, these gains do not automatically translate into sustained practice. Implementation is shaped by system-level constraints, including time pressures, limited referral pathways and broader organisational and cultural factors.

Strengthening the role of dental professionals in tobacco control will require moving beyond training alone towards integrated approaches that align education, clinical practice and health system support. Addressing these structural barriers is essential to translating provider readiness into meaningful, sustained contributions to tobacco cessation in low- and middle-income settings.
