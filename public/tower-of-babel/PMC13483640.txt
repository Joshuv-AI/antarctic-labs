Genomic prediction in dolichos bean (Lablab purpureus L. Sweet) breeding: choice of the type of training population and selection generation
Authors: Mugali Pundalik Kalpana, Chandrappa Anilkumar, Channabasava, Gonal Basanagouda, Chindi Basavaraj Siddu, Sampangi Ramesh, Dinesh Sindhu, Hosakoti Satish, Munegowda Kemparaju, K. Madhusudan, V Spoorthi, Rath Bidwan, Balthikumeru Vishwanatha Sinchana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dolichos bean is a nutritionally rich yet underutilized orphan legume crop. Conventional phenotype-based genetic improvement has been slow; therefore, integrating genomic selection (GS) into breeding programs is essential to accelerate genetic gain. Our objective in this study was to optimize the factors influencing GS success in dolichos bean by identifying: (i) the most effective GS model, (ii) an appropriate training population (TP), and (iii) the ideal selection generation for fresh pod yield. Four populations with distinct genetic structures were evaluated for fresh pod yield and genotyped using 413 SSR markers distributed across 11 linkage groups. The TPs were evaluated across five environments (locations and/or years), whereas the breeding populations (BPs) were evaluated over two seasons. Four GS models were trained on two differentially structured TPs and validated on two breeding populations. The optimal generation for genomic prediction was assessed by comparing an early-generation segregating population (F2:3) with an advanced-generation population (F5:6). Our results indicate that the RKHS model showed the highest predictive ability (i.e. correlation between observed and predicted fresh pod yield) compared to the other three models. We found that a closely structured Recombinant Inbred Line (RIL) population is the most effective for training genomic prediction models. Furthermore, predictive abilities were comparable between the F2:3 and F5:6 generations. We conclude that predicting fresh pod yield in early segregating generations using an RKHS model trained on a RIL population is highly effective strategy for implementing GS in dolichos bean improvement programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09139-9.

Introduction

Dolichos bean (Lablab purpureus L. Sweet), popularly called as field bean or hyacinth bean, and is an important vegetable legume since ancient times [1]. It is grown extensively in semi-arid rainfed production systems in India and African countries [1]. The crop is believed to originate in the Indian subcontinent and spread to other parts of the world [2]. The crop is an autogamous diploid with 11 pairs of chromosomes with a genome size of 376 Mb [3, 4]. In southern India, it is one of the major sources of protein to the millions of people, especially those depend on the vegetarian diet as the source of energy [1]. While fresh pods or dry pods are the harvestable and marketable economic products, fresh beans and dry seeds are consumable products. The fresh beans are used as vegetable. A unique fragrance from the fresh pods at the harvestable maturity is preferred by consumers [5]. Either fresh beans or dry seeds proven source of nutrients such as protein, fat, carbohydrates, and micronutrients including calcium, iron, and phosphorous for many rural poor in India and African countries [1]. Since the crop is domesticated and conventionally grown in semi-arid rainfed conditions, it has the capacity to withstand water stress conditions [6]. Despite its multi-purpose usage and climatic resilience, the crop is underutilized due to its low yielding ability. Enhancing its production potential by developing improved cultivars is expected to contribute to nutritional security as well as livelihoods of millions of people/farmers in India and Africa [1].

Conventional phenotype-based selection, especially, in early segregating generation for traits such as grain yield has been less effective in dolichos bean. This attributed to its complex inheritance driven by significant influence of environment and cross-over genotype-environment interactions on the trait expression [7, 8]. Of late, the DNA marker-assisted breeding approaches are becoming more popular in many crops due to their environment neutrality, crop stage non-specificity, simple inheritance and ease of assay [9]. Use of DNA molecular markers as surrogates of complex characters helps improve pace and precision of breeding any crop, including dolichos bean. However, there is a paucity of DNA marker resources in dolichos bean [10]. Nevertheless, simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers are being used in small-scale laboratories for dolichos bean breeding research [7, 10–12]. These SSR markers could be utilized in marker-assisted selection (MAS) for introgressing major effect quantitative trait loci (QTL) controlling the target traits. However, as is well documented, the MAS is less effective for quantitative traits that are controlled by large number of minor effect QTL [13]. Therefore, a predictive strategy that utilizes genome-wide marker information to select the most desirable candidate genotypes for a target trait has been suggested as a most effective to improve quantitative traits [14]. Genomic selection (GS) is such a strategy that uses genome-wide markers to precisely predict the performance of most desirable candidate genotypes.

GS involves the phenotyping and genotyping of a set of genotypes called the training population (TP), on which the statistical models are trained to predict the phenotype of another set of genotypes called the testing or breeding population (BP) which are only genotyped and not phenotyped [15, 16]. The marker effects estimated in the TP and genotypic information of BPs are used to predict the performance of candidates in BP [17]. The GS approach is proven successful in many crops, including rice [18–20], maize [16, 21], wheat [22–24], etc., for improving genetic gain. The use of GS in the improvement of underutilized crops like dolichos bean is still in infancy. Considering the small genome size [25] and low effective recombination driven by self-pollination in dolichos bean, a low density of markers is effective to implement GS.

To enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of GS, it is necessary to optimize the parameters of genomic prediction. Efforts have been made to optimize GS in other legume crops such as soybean [26–29], chickpea [30, 31], etc. However, such studies are seldom attempted in dolichos bean. In these premises, the present investigation was envisaged with following objectives: (i) to understand the importance of genetic structure of TP and (ii) to compare genomic prediction ability at early and advanced generation of BP.

Materials and methods

Experimental genetic material

The experimental genetic material consisted of four populations which include (i) a set of 96 germplasm accessions, (ii) F5:6 recombinant inbred lines (RIL), (iii) F2:3 population derived from HA 4 × HA 5 and (iv) F2:3 population derived from HA 10 − 8 × HA 5. The germplasm population was consisted of 96 accessions; most of them are collected from dolichos bean growing regions of India and a few accessions from Kenya. This population covers most genetic diversity of dolichos bean that exists in India (Supplementary Table S1). This germplasm population was used as training population-I (TP-1) in the present investigation. The second population, a RIL population, consisting of 135 lines developed from a bi-parental cross between two varieties (HA-4 and HA-5) released from the University of Agricultural Sciences (UAS), Bangalore, India. This population was used as training population – II (TP-II). Among the other two populations, one F2:3 population was derived from crosses between HA-4 and HA-5, and the second F2:3 population was derived from HA-10-8 and HA-5. These two F2:3 populations were used as breeding population-I (BP-I) and breeding population-II (BP-II), respectively. Each BP consisted of 144 progeny families, summed up to a total of 288 lines in the breeding population. The parents of BPs and the parents of the RIL population were also included in TP-I. The genetic material used in the study was maintained at the Department of Genetics and Plant Breeding, University of Agricultural Sciences, Bangalore and, the material is available to public for research purpose through material transfer agreement. The steps in the development of BP-I, BP-II and TP-II is outlined in Fig. 1.

Phenotyping experimental populations

The experimental populations were evaluated over years or locations. The TP-I was evaluated in a complete block design with two replications across five years in rainy seasons from 2012 to 2019 at the experimental plots of the UAS, Bangalore, India. The TP-II was evaluated across five locations, namely (i) College of Agriculture, Bangalore, Karnataka, India, (ii) College of Agriculture, Chintamani, Karnataka, India, (iii) Krishi Vigyan Kendra, Hadonahalli, Karnataka, India, (iv) College of Agriculture, Hassan, Karnataka, India, and (v) Farmer’s field, Belagavi, Karnataka, India. The TP-II was evaluated twice (rainy and post-rainy seasons) at the College of Agriculture, Bangalore. Thus, TP-II was assessed in a total of six environments in an incomplete block design with two replications. BP-I and BP-II populations were evaluated at the experimental plots of the UAS, Bangalore, India, during the rainy and post-rainy seasons of 2022. The progeny families of BP-I and BP-II were evaluated in an incomplete block design with two replications. The seeds of each individual in all four populations were sown in a 3 m length rows separated by 0.60 m space between rows. The seeded rows were thinned after 15 days of planting to maintain 0.2 m of space between plants within the row. To raise healthy crop, recommended agronomic and plant protection practices were followed in all experiments. The fresh, fully filled pods were harvested from randomly sampled 10 plants in each line avoiding border plants. The fresh pod yield was recorded at two pickings, and the cumulative pod yield from two pickings was recorded as pod yield per plant (g plant− 1).

Genotyping the experimental populations

The germplasm accessions and RIL populations were genetically homozygous and homogenous. Therefore, a representative plant per line was labeled to collect leaf samples for genomic DNA extraction. In the case of F2:3 populations (BPs), the cryopreserved DNA samples of respective F2 plants were used for genotyping. The genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves of 15-day-old seedlings using Cetyl-tri-methyl ammonium bromide (CTAB) method [32]. The DNA samples were quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer following manufacturer guidelines. A total of 591 new simple sequence repeat (SSR)-based markers developed from genomic sequences of a popular variety, HA 4, as detailed in Spoorthi et al. [10]were used to genotype the populations. The germplasm population was genotyped with all the 591 SSR markers. Of those, 413 markers amplified in all the accessions of the population. The genomic regions of the accessions complementary to the primer sequences of the markers were amplified using an Applied Biosystems/Veriti 96-well thermocycler. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) mixture was prepared by adding 2 µL of 10 ng/ µL template DNA, 4 µL of ready PCR mixture from Amplicon®, and 8 µL of nuclease-free double distilled water to make the total reaction mixture volume to 14 µL. The PCR program was set with 94 °C for one minute as the initial denaturation temperature, followed by 34 cycles of amplification with 94 °C for two minutes of denaturation, primer annealing at a primer-specific temperature for one minute, 72 °C for three minutes of extension, followed by 72 °C for 10 min of final extension. The amplicons were separated on a 3.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. The amplicons were visualized under the gel photo-documentation system (Bio-Imaging system) and recorded as binary data, which was used for further analysis.

The polymorphism survey indicated 140, 140 and 83 SSR markers polymorphic between the parents of TP-II, BP-I and BP-II. Hence, RIL population and BP-I were genotyped with 140 markers and BP-II was genotyped with 83 markers. The parents of TP-II, BP-I and BP-II populations were members of the germplasm population, were also genotyped with 413 markers. The genotype information of populations other than TP-I was imputed on the basis of conditional probabilities of markers in a bi-parental population, which considers the estimated recombination rate between nearest non-missing markers [33]. For TP-II, BP-I, and BP-II populations, which were derived from biparental crosses, the parental genotypes across the 413 SSR markers were known. Progeny genotypes were inferred based on observed parental allele segregation and recombination patterns across loci, following expected Mendelian inheritance. This approach ensured a consistent marker framework across populations, as all individuals were scored at the same set of loci. This standardization enabled the use of marker effects estimated from TP-I for prediction in the biparental populations without requiring additional imputation of marker sets across datasets.

Genomic prediction

The genomic prediction was performed using four dolichos bean populations in the current investigation. The BLUE values for fresh pod yield estimated across location or over years were used as phenotype, and binary marker information from all populations was used as genotype data for genomic prediction. It is now well established that the use of different models need not necessarily change the outcome; however, finding a better model to use in practical breeding has its own advantages, both technically and practically [36]. Therefore, we used four models in the present investigation. These include three Bayesian models, namely (i) Bayes A [15], (ii) Bayes B [37], and (iii) Bayesian ridge regression (BRR) model [38], and (iv) Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces Regression (RKHS) [39], a semi-parametric model. These were chosen based on our previous findings showing comparable performance under low marker densities [40]. The basic model considered for building different models is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Y=X\beta\:+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}{Z}_{k}{\gamma\:}_{k}+\epsilon\:$$\end{document}

Where, Y is a vector of predicted fresh pod yield, X is matrix of genotype marker information, β is vector of fixed effects, m is marker numbers, Zk is genotype indicator variable, γk is vector of marker effects and ε is vector of residuals.

Genomic predictions were assessed with two core ideas: first, to identify most appropriate TP where germplasm population (TP-I) and RIL population (TP-II) were used as training populations and two early generation populations were used as BP. Second, to identify most appropriate generation (early / advanced) for genomic prediction where germplasm was used as TP and F2:3 and RIL populations of the same cross as BP. All the genomic prediction analyses were performed using BGLR package [41] in R software. Identification of most appropriate training population

The most appropriate TP was identified by performing independent validations across two BPs. The TP-I consisted of a genetically diverse germplasm collection; the parents of the BPs were included in this set to ensure genetic relatedness between the training and testing sets. The TP-II was a more closely related RIL population, comprising full-sibs derived from a single cross. Specifically, TP-II and BP-I share both parents, while BP-II shares at least one common parent with both TP-II and BP-I.

Predictive abilities (as Pearson correlation coefficient between predicted and observed fresh pod yield per plant) within each TP were initially estimated using a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) approach. In this method, each individual is sequentially used as the testing genotype while the remaining individuals serve as the training set, repeating the process for all accessions in the population. As LOOCV inherently uses all available data without random partitioning, no additional stratification was applied; however, the populations used were biparental or structured populations with limited population stratification. Subsequently, independent validations were performed by applying models trained on TP-I and TP-II to predict the performance of BP-I and BP-II. All analyses were conducted using 100 repeated validation cycles (model runs) to assess prediction stability and model performance. For the Bayesian models, 5,000 MCMC iterations were performed with a burn-in of 500 iterations and a thinning interval of 5. Differences in predictive abilities between models and training populations were initially assessed using a paired t-test. However, given the potential dependence among prediction accuracies, results were interpreted cautiously.

Appropriate generation for genomic prediction

It is known that the selection efficiency is promising after fixation of alleles in the accessions. However, effective early generation selection can save resources and time and accelerate the breeding process [42]. Hence, predictive abilities at early generation and advanced generations of breeding cycles were tested and compared. The TP-II and BP-I share the same parents; the TP-II was a stable and fixed generation, while the BP-I was a segregating early generation. Parents of two breeding populations were included in the germplasm population (TP-I). Therefore, TP-I was used as a training population, TP-II was used as a prediction population in advanced generation prediction, and BP-I was used for prediction in early generation. The analysis in both cases was performed in 100 iterations to allow model convergence and stable predictions. The predictive abilities in both breeding populations were compared and tested using a simple ‘t’ statistic. The results were interpreted in relation to the most appropriate generation for genomic prediction.

Results

Populations and phenotype data

The accessions or lines in TPs (TP-I: germplasm population and TP-II: RIL population) and BPs (two F2:3 populations) differed significantly for fresh pod yield. The test environments also contributed significantly to the variation in fresh pod yield of the entries (Tables 1 and 2). The entries also displayed significant genotype × environment interactions in both TP and BP for fresh pod yield (Tables 1 and 2). The BLUE values estimated across replications over environments showed normal distribution with negligible skewness and kurtosis (Fig. 3). The average fresh pod yield in the RIL population was slightly greater than fresh pod yield in two BPs. The heritability of fresh pod yield in experimental populations ranged between 0.39 (germplasm population) and 0.59 (RIL population) (Table 3).

Predictive abilities within training populations

The predictive abilities within training populations were estimated following a leave-one-out cross-validation approach. The average predictive abilities within TP-I ranged between − 0.12 for the Bayes A model and 0.25 for the RKHS model. For TP-II, the average predictive abilities ranged between 0.17 for Bayes B and 0.36 for the RKHS model. Overall, the estimates of predictive abilities of RKHS were relatively greater than those of other three models. The estimates of predictive abilities of TP-II were relatively better compared to TP-I for all the models (Fig. 4). Based on Bayes A model, the two training populations differed significantly with greater predictive ability in TP-II. On the other hand, though the training populations differed with respect to predictive ability based on BRR and RKHS models with relatively higher predictive abilities in the TP-II population, the difference was non-significant. However, the average predictive abilities of Bayes B model were comparable between the two training populations.

Appropriate training population

The estimates of predictive abilities of the two TPs were considered to identify the most appropriate TP. The TP, with higher predictive abilities in both cross- and independent validation, was considered as the most appropriate. In the present study, the estimates of predictive abilities in TP-II (RIL population) were relatively greater than those in TP-I (germplasm population) in cross-validation approach (Fig. 4). The results were validated in two breeding populations. The estimates of predictive abilities in both the BPs were significantly higher when TP-II was used as a training population (Figs. 5 and 6). The estimates of predictive abilities based on all the models (except BRR) using TP-I and TP-II were significant when they were independently validated in BP-I (Fig. 5). Among models, RKHS predicted the fresh pod yield better than any other model. Similarly, the estimates of predictive abilities from TP-II were significantly greater than those in TP-I when they were validated in BP-II (Fig. 6). As in BP-I, the estimates of predictive abilities on the RKHS model were significantly better than any other model in BP-II. These results from cross- and independent validations suggest that the TP-II (RIL population) is more appropriate training population for implementing genomic selection in dolichos bean.

Appropriate generation for genomic prediction

The estimates of predictive abilities in the RIL population were comparatively higher than those in the F2:3 population in Bayes B and RKHS models and non-significant in Bayes A and BRR. The difference in predictive abilities was significant only at 5% probability when estimated using Bayes B and RKHS models (Fig. 7). Though predictive abilities differed statistically between RIL and F2:3 population, the differences are narrow to consider them as practically significant. These results provide compelling evidence to infer that GS is most appropriate and effective for early generation selection in dolichos bean breeding.

Discussion

The results suggest that implementing genomic selection at an early segregating generation, using a model trained on a closely related population such as a RIL, is most effective for fresh pod yield in dolichos bean improvement. The predictive abilities in the two training populations indicate that the use of highly related populations, such as full-sibs or RIL populations, improves predictive ability more than less related populations. Implementing genomic selection at the early generations of the breeding cycle is beneficial to saving substantial time and resources. These results have a direct influence on dolichos bean breeding and the breeding of similar legume crops.

In independent validation, the predictive abilities were superior when the full-sib RIL population (TP-II) was used as the training population rather than the less related TP-I. The observed difference in predictive ability can be attributed to variation in population genetic parameters as well as differences in genetic relationships between the populations. RIL populations derived from biparental crosses typically exhibit more uniform allele frequency distributions, extended linkage disequilibrium (LD), and consistent linkage phases between markers and underlying quantitative trait loci, which facilitate more accurate estimation and transferability of marker effects. In contrast, germplasm populations are characterized by greater genetic diversity, variable allele frequencies, and more rapid LD decay, which can reduce the stability of marker–trait associations and consequently lower prediction accuracy [14, 43]. Furthermore, individuals within TP-I related with an average kinship of 0.22 and the average genomic relationship between TP-I and the biparental populations was lower (0.08) compared to TP-II (0.32), indicating closer genetic relatedness of TP-II with the validation populations, which likely contributed to its higher prediction accuracy. These results suggest that the use of highly related populations as training populations helps improve the predictive abilities in dolichos bean. The marker effects estimated from closed training populations confound with less noise [44]. When used on related test populations, marker effects precisely predict the performance of candidates. Based on our results, we opine that a related RIL population should be the choice for a training population in predicting a quantitative trait such as fresh pod yield in dolichos bean.

The higher prediction accuracy observed in the RIL-based training population is consistent with trends reported across multiple crops, where related or structured populations outperform diverse germplasm panels. For instance, studies in soybean have shown that RIL populations achieve higher prediction accuracy than cultivar panels for seed yield traits [45]. Similar results were reported in barley, where connected half-sibs were used as training populations, which improved predictive ability [46]. This pattern reflects the importance of genetic relatedness and LD structure in determining genomic prediction efficiency. Compared to well-studied crops, the prediction accuracies observed in dolichos bean are within a comparable range under similar training population designs, suggesting that GS can be effectively implemented in this species. However, as an under-researched orphan crop, dolichos bean presents additional challenges, including limited genomic resources, smaller and less structured populations, which may constrain prediction accuracy and model optimization. These findings highlight the importance of optimizing training population design and resource development to improve GS efficiency in such crops.

The generation for implementing genomic selection is crucial. Implementing genomic selection at an early generation of the breeding cycle helps in the proper allocation of resources [42]. Our results indicate that selection at an early generation (i.e., the F2 generation) was comparable with selection in advanced generations. Hence, early-generation selection is effective for fresh pod yield in dolichos bean. In a wheat breeding program, it was proven that early-generation selection is effective [47], which is consistent with our results. Selection at an early generation helps in the elimination of inferior entries and the advancement of potential candidate individuals with efficient use of resources. Alternatively, resources can be utilized to increase the progeny size before selection which allows for more transgressive segregants for the trait.

In general, the predictive abilities for fresh pod yield in both training populations were relatively low across models. This may be attributed in part to the low heritability of the trait, given that trait heritability plays a crucial role in improving predictive abilities for quantitative traits [17, 44, 48]. Another important factor influencing predictive ability is marker density. In the present investigation, SSR markers were used due to the current unavailability of genotyping arrays for dolichos bean and the substantial resources and bioinformatic standardization required for high-throughput approaches such as genotyping-by-sequencing or whole-genome resequencing. These approaches often require careful harmonization of marker datasets across populations due to variation in marker discovery and missing data patterns. The number of SSR markers used was limited to 413. However, these markers were distributed across the genome, and were sufficient to enable genomic selection for dry seed yield in dolichos bean [40]. The germplasm collection showed moderate relatedness (average kinship = 0.22), which, together with the expected LD in this self-pollinated crop, supports the use of a moderate number of SSR markers for capturing marker–trait associations. Similarly, 135 SSR markers in the prediction of oil content in oil palm [49] and 200 random fragment length polymorphisms (RFLPs) in the prediction of grain yield in maize [16] resulted in relatively high prediction accuracy. The genome sizes of maize and oil palm are much larger than that of dolichos bean. The effectiveness of a lower number of markers encourages other breeders to use low density markers in implementing genomic selection in orphan crops. Hence, the marker numbers used in the present study are justifiable, and poor heritability of trait in the populations is the cause for lower predictive abilities.

Relatively smaller population sizes in both training and testing sets can influence the predictive ability of genomic selection models. However, previous studies in dolichos bean have shown that reasonable prediction accuracies can still be achieved with low to moderate population sizes, particularly when the training and testing populations are closely related [40]. Similarly, in rice, a self-pollinated crop, studies using varying population densities have demonstrated that a training population size of approximately 150 individuals is sufficient to achieve acceptable predictive ability [50]. Furthermore, the present study focused on a single trait, fresh pod yield, which is a key economic trait in dolichos bean. Inclusion of multiple traits and the implementation of multi-trait models may further improve prediction accuracy [20]. Additionally, incorporating multiple traits along with multi-environment trials will be essential to enhance the robustness and broader applicability of genomic selection models in dolichos bean.

Among the models tested in the present study, the predictive abilities based on RKHS were high enough for implementation of GS in a highly self-pollinated crop like dolichos bean. The RKHS uses a relationship matrix as a component of the model, which makes it a better model for prediction [51]. The predictive abilities of TP-II (RIL population) were comparatively better than those of TP-I (the germplasm population) across all the models. These results are not surprising, considering that predictive ability increases as genetic relatedness among the entries in a training population increases [52]. This is because, while TP-I consisted of germplasm lines that are not related by descent, TP-II consisted of full-sibs derived from highly related parents that shared large genomic regions derived from the two parents.

Orphan legume crop breeding faces significant genomic and financial resource constraints. Integrating genomic selection into existing workflows offers a way to overcome the limits of traditional phenotype-based selection. By utilizing related populations and low-density marker panels, breeders can implement selection as early as the F2 generation. This approach achieves higher genetic gains and ensures more efficient resource allocation. Overall, this study provides a validated framework for dolichos bean and similar legume species. It paves the way for the rapid development of high-yielding varieties.
