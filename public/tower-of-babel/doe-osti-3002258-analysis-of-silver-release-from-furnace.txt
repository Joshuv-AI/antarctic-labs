 ABSTRACT

High temperature testing of intact TRISO particles previously irradiated in the AGR-5/6/7 experiment
was performed in the Furnace for Irradiated TRISO Testing (FITT) to directly confirm diffusive release
of silver and europium from intact TRISO particles. Testing was conducted from 1,100–1,600C for
exposure times up to 100 h to directly confirm silver through layer release below safety testing
temperatures. The FITT analysis showed highest levels of silver release at 1,300–1,400C which confirms
athermal release behaviors previous observed in step-wise and transient safety tests. Additionally, release
was non-uniform with some particles releasing a majority of their inventory while others did not appear to
release silver under identical testing conditions, which was consistent with historic observations. An
assessment of the effective silver diffusion coefficient in the SiC layer was conducted and indicated
maximum values in the 1,300–1,400C range. The magnitude of the calculated diffusion coefficients also
exceeded currently accepted diffusion coefficients. The release behavior of europium from intact particles
was also analyzed in FITT for at 1,450–1,550C for 500 h to 750 h. Direct confirmation of europium
release below safety testing temperatures was confirmed absent contributions from matrix release. The
analysis indicated europium release follows a general Arrhenius behavior and suggests general uniform
release and indicates irradiation conditions influence observed release response. Calculated diffusion
kinetics agree well with historic experiments.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) particle fuel is a proposed fuel form for high temperature gas reactors.
TRISO particles rely primarily on a SiC layer to retain metallic fission products not retained in the kernel.
The Advanced Gas Reactor Fuel Development and Qualification (AGR) Program [1, 2] has shown that
the SiC layer performs well at retaining fission products and mitigates fission product release through out
of pile testing up to 1,800C. However, silver, europium, and strontium have been observed to be released
through intact particles both in pile and during out of pile safety testing at elevated temperatures [3]. The
release of 110mAg is of interest due to its potential impact on reactor maintenance through increased
radiation fields around pumps, heat exchangers, piping, and other equipment related to maintenance
concerns. Mechanisms for fission product release such as grain boundary diffusion have been proposed
and appear to be supported by out of pile safety testing results from AGR-1 at 1,800C due to comparison
of variants with differing SiC microstructures [2].

Overall release of relevant radionuclides is generally determined by destructive post-irradiation
examination (PIE). For as-irradiated compacts, the general process constitutes capsule component
analysis, assessment of radionuclides present outside of intact particles via deconsolidation-leach-burn-
leach, and determination of the remaining inventory in intact particles through the use of the Irradiated
Microsphere Gamma Analyzer (IMGA) [4, 1]. While the overall release is confidently assessed,
calculating effective diffusion coefficients is difficult due to the complex irradiation history consisting of
variable thermal histories and temperature gradients across compacts, resulting in Multiphysics simulation
efforts which show some disagreement with experimental results [5]. For diffusive release assessment
during safety testing in the Core Conduction Cooldown Test Facility (CCCTF) at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL) or the Fuel Accident Condition Simulator (FACS) furnace at Idaho National
Laboratory (INL) release is determined from whole compacts through time-dependent sampling of
volatile fission products plated out on a cold finger. Safety tests in CCCTF and FACS are primarily
targeted to explore particle failures at relevant and margin accident and margin temperatures. Confident
determination of diffusive release from safety testing presents challenges as through layer release is
masked by release of radionuclides present in the matrix after irradiation. For carbide compound formers
such as europium and strontium, this results in delayed transport through the carbon-based compact

 1

matrix and graphite holder, further masking potential through layer release and is further complicated by
transport through the graphite compact holders [6]. Quantification of release fractions rely on using a
correction factor based on temperature of interest to account for the concentration of relevant fission
products plated out on furnace internals. The FITT system was deployed to allow for direct measurement
of release measuring an individual particle’s inventory of radionuclides before and after exposure to
temperature. By adopting this approach complications due to matrix release contributions and use of
correction factors to quantify release from intact particles is avoided. The FITT system also allows for
longer duration exposures over multiple temperature ranges as access to the safety testing systems (FACS
and CCCTF) is limited due to their need for AGR primary mission assessment of particle failure
performance [7].

The results reported herein reflect two separate thrusts and build upon efforts to establish FITT for
diffusive release studies [7, 8]. The first thrust is the exploration of 110mAg release to understand the
temperature dependence of silver release and estimation of effective diffusion coefficients of silver in the
SiC layer. Silver represents a particular challenge, as out of pile safety tests show non-Arrhenius release
behaviors. Stepwise and transient tests in FACS and CCCTF [8, 7] from AGR-1 and AGR-2 compacts
show increased release rates as temperature decreases from 1,600C through ~1,000C. Isochronal testing
at 100 h in FITT over 1,100–1,600C was conduct on individual particles to identify the temperature of
maximum release as indicated in the step-wise and transient safety tests. Again, FITT serves to isolate
release to individual particles and removing contributions from release of the inventory in the compact
matrix reflecting a direct observation of through-layer release. Select conditions were also targeted for
sequential reheat testing where individual particles were exposed to discreet time steps (25 h) up to 100 h
to understand time dependent release behavior. The second thrust was exploring europium release. Both
europium and strontium are observed to be release from intact particles particularly at high time-average
volume average (TAVA) temperature irradiation conditions [9, 10, 11]. A series of FITT tests were
conducted at 1,450–1,550C for durations up to 750 h. These test conditions vary from silver as europium
diffusion is slower than silver and therefore requires higher temperatures and longer test times to observe.
While strontium is of interest and relevant to the source term it is not evaluated in this study as strontium
does not have a measurable gamma-signature, and the individual particle inventory is not readily
measurable. Europium is expected to behave similarly to strontium as such the europium response may
provide insight to strontium release.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL OBJECTIVES AND METHODS

The isochronal and sequential reheat testing results presented in this report expand on previous FITT
thermal exposure tests by exploring the release of 110mAg and 154Eu in an inert argon atmosphere at
varying temperatures and durations [7]. Each FITT test consisted of ten particles from AGR-5/6/7
compacts exposed to various temperatures in an inert argon gas atmosphere. Particles were selected from
as-irradiated AGR-5/6/7 Compacts 2-2-1, 3-6-3, and 5-1-3. Irradiation and fabrication information of
these compacts is shown in Table 1. These compacts reflect a range in in pile irradiation conditions [12].

Individual particles for FITT exposures were selected after they had been electrolytically deconsolidated
in nitric acid, as described in previous reporting [13]. After deconsolidation, the liberated particles were
separated from matrix residue by using a stainless-steel sieve rack and repeatedly rinsing the material.
Cleaned particles were transferred to the IMGA and individually analyzed using an HPGe gamma
detector. The IMGA individual particle analysis is described in Section 2.1. After IMGA examination,
~10% of the particles were retained and the remaining ~90% were returned to the main hot cells for burn-
leach analysis. Particles for FITT testing were selected from the 10% remaining particles. These were
initially counted at the IMGA in individual vials utilizing the system’s vial handling mode to maintain

 2

particle identity but in some instances were later counted at the Vertical Counting System (VCS) to
increase particle counting throughput. The VCS individual particle analysis is described in Section 2.3.
After gamma counting, the particles were tested in the FITT system, as described in Section 2.1. Particle
identity was maintained, and the posttest particles were counted again posttest in the IMGA or VCS
systems to produce before and after direct comparison for relevant fission products to determine how
heating tests affected fission product release.

The investigation of europium release used an alternative test matrix because it requires higher

experimental failures led to consumption of available AGR-5/6/7 Compact 2-2-1 particles for FITT
efforts. As-irradiated AGR-5/6/7 Compacts 3-6-3 and 5-1-3 were selected for europium release study with

capture insight on irradiation conditions and possible impact on europium release, however, FITT5-18

potential release variation overtime. The FITT5-14 1,500C for 750 h total elapsed time test of AGR-
5/6/7 Compact 3-6-3 did not survive testing due to oxygen ingress. This leaves only the 1,450C
sequential reheating tests available for analysis of time resolved release.

2.1 FITT EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

The FITT allows high temperature testing of samples under inert testing or oxidation testing. The furnace
set up and the testing conditions followed the inert testing process describe herein. First, the system was
assembled as shown in Figure 1, and a bakeout run performed to condition the alumina tube and drive out
impurities [7]. For the bakeout, the temperature was set to 20 C above the target test temperature and
held at temperature for approximately 48 h. Ten irradiated TRISO particles were loaded in a graphite
holder with individual wells for the particles arranged in groups of two, three, and five particles so that
each particle’s identity is maintained throughout the test. The holder consists of a graphite bolt, graphite
holder, thin SiC washer, thick graphite washer, and graphite nut to contain the particles in a single unit.
The assembled holder, individual components, and furnace assembly are shown in Figure 1. The particles
and graphite holder were then placed inside of an zirconia cup. The cup was placed inside the alumina
tube, then the system shown in Figure 1a was loaded through the top of a Rapid Temp Furnace (1708FL)
by CM Furnaces. After being attached to inlet and outlet gas lines, vacuum was pulled on the system to
check for leaks and then purged with argon. This cycle was performed three times to prevent residual
oxygen. Following FITT5-13 test failing for oxygen intrusion, steps were taken to mitigate oxygen
entering the system. These steps include installing a Large Oxygen Trap (BOT-2) from Agilent to the
inlet side of the airflow, replacing manual flow controllers with an Alicat mass flow controller, upgrading
the existing nitrile O-ring around the welded quick connect assembly to a higher temperature resistant
Fluorocarbon O-ring, and adding alumina support blocks to reduce loads on the gas lines.

During the test, the temperature was ramped at 5C/min to avoid inducing particle failures. After the test,

normalized against 137Cs since cesium is a well-retained fission product in intact particles, where
normalization was conducted to account for differing kernel sizes and fission product production during
irradiation [7]. The measured versus calculated (M/C) 110mAg activity ratio was also reported using
MCNP depletion calculations [4, 5].

2.2 IMGA EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

In depth description of the IMGA system and operation can be found in previous reports [19]. The
particles or vials are stored in a carousel, where motion control slides and a vacuum generator are used to
pick up particles or vials and position them in front of an HPGe detector for gamma spectrometry. The
system operates in either particle mode or vial mode. Particle mode is used to scan particles for a short
(~2 min) count time and sort them into bins in the carousel based on fission product retention. This mode
allows for a high throughput but does not maintain particle identity, the exception being particles with

 6

abnormal gamma signatures which are isolated for follow on analysis [19]. It is used to count all the
particles in a deconsolidated fuel compact and identify potentially failed particles. Vial mode involves
placing individual particles in glass vials, which maintain particle identity. This mode has a lower
throughput but can measure lower activity or shorter-half-life fission products, such as 110mAg and 154Eu,
when longer count times are used. Particles used for FITT testing were initially counted in vial mode on
the timescale of hours and then loaded out of the hot cells for testing. After the graphite holder used for
testing was disassembled, the particles were loaded back into the IMGA hot cell for a post-testing
analysis (also using vial mode). For particles that were subjected to sequential reheat testing, this process
was repeated to gather measurement data between all reheating stages.

The activities were decay corrected to one day after end of irradiation and were corrected for detector
efficiency, background, and particle self-shielding. Resulting activities in becquerels were normalized to
calculated and ratioed against normalized 137Cs activity values (M/C), which provide information on
fission product retention and account for variations in kernel size that can contribute to measured activity
variations not related to retention. Equation (1) shows the calculation performed for the M/C value of

2.3 VCS EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

The VCS consists of a HPGe detector set inside a leaded enclosure in a low radiation background area.
Figure 2 shows a schematic of the VCS setup. Inside the leaded enclosure is a custom stand, which allows
sample holder plates to be staged at desired distances from the detector. This distance is determined based
on the activity of the sample and the dead time of the detector. Typically, greater than 10,000 counts are
targeted for each peak of interest to have confidence in quantifying activities, which determines the total
amount of time each sample analyzed. Point sources consisting of 137Cs, 152Eu, and 241Am are used to
perform energy and efficiency calibrations. To mirror the counting conditions of the real particle samples,
the point sources are placed on top of a sectioned glass v-vial. The particles were counted sequentially at
a distance of 60 cm from the detector. Initially, the spectrum collected is only adjusted by energy and
efficiency calibrations with decay, background, and self-shielding corrections are applied to the data in
post processing.

2.4 DIFFUSION SIMULATIONS

Values of predicted release fractions versus silver or europium diffusion coefficients in SiC were
generated using 1D diffusion profiles through the TRISO particle layers and the ratio of the pre- and
posttest 110mAg and 154Eu activity within the particle using the BISON platform. BISON is a finite element
preconditioned Jacobian free Newton Krelov fuel performance code developed and maintained by Idaho
National Laboratory [20]. BISON is built on MOOSE [21], which creates a generalized Multiphysics
framework and in turn utilizes LIBMESH [22] and PETSc [23].

The simulation consisted of the AGR-5/6/7 compact average fuel temperature and power histories for
relevant compacts examined [16] followed by the temperature ramp to the FITT test conditions. This
provides the distribution of silver and europium within the particle at the beginning of the test and end of
test. The diffusion coefficients for silver and europium in all TRISO particle components (kernel, buffer,
pyrocarbon layers) except the SiC layer were set according to recommendations in IAEA-TECDOC-978
[24]. However, for europium diffusion, the diffusion coefficient in the kernel and pyrocarbon layers was
set to recommendations from Hayashi et al. [25]. A more detailed description of fission product diffusion
simulations in BISON leveraged here can be found in Refs. [20, 26].

Values of predicted release fractions versus silver or europium diffusion coefficients in SiC, were
interpolated to estimate the diffusion coefficient of silver or europium in each particle based on measured
release fraction, e.g., change in directly measured decay corrected activity pre- and posttest. The
simulations were performed in this fashion to attempt to account for the initial distribution of silver or
europium and multiple diffusion pathways. Example profiles for AGR-5/6/7 Compact 3-6-3 europium
pre- and posttest are shown in Figure 3.

A possible bias towards “high” 110mAg inventory particles being more susceptible to release was noted
based on direct activity assessment and the 110mAg M/C assessment. Retained 110mAg was sensitive to in
pile irradiation conditions with “high” retention particles are expected to have experience lower in pile
irradiation temperatures [1, 28]. This likely impacts the distribution and availability of silver within
particles for diffusive transport. For the out of pile tests, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the greater
starting activity reflects a stronger driving force for diffusion, e.g., a larger concentration gradient, while
the higher activity may also support better counting statistics and an ability to differentiate release more
confidently. Because all the tested particles came from the same batch, it appears that variables such as
grain size would not be a primary driver for the observed variation assuming that particles produced in the
same batch have similar microstructures [29]. However, grain boundary diffusion is known to be a
primary diffusion pathway for Ag in SiC [30].

3.4 ESTIMATED SILVER DIFFUSION COEFFICIENTS PER PARTICLE

As described in Section 2.4, the diffusion coefficient of each particle was estimated using 1D spherical
diffusion through a TRISO particle and the ratio of the pre- and posttest 110mAg activity within the
particle. The irradiation was simulated to set the initial test condition, then the temperature was ramped to
the test temperature. The diffusion coefficients through the other components in the particle (kernel,
buffer, pyrocarbon) were set according to recommendations in IAEA-TECDOC-978 [24]. Thus, an array
of simulations with various diffusion coefficients during the FITT thermal exposures period for

 22

isochronal and reheated particles was performed, and the diffusion coefficient of the SiC layer was
correlated to the temperature of exposure and directly related to the magnitude of measured 110mAg release
during FITT exposures.

The estimated diffusion coefficients for silver in SiC are listed in Table 15 for particles from isochronal
testing that pass the 68% confidence interval and pass the 95% confidence interval. Figure 15 visualizes
the distribution of calculated diffusion versus inverse temperature and shows that there was a maximum
in the average calculated diffusion coefficient around 1,300C to 1,400C from the isochronal testing
reflecting the observation of maximum per particle release. The estimated diffusion coefficients exceed
those recommended by the IAEA-TECDOC-978 [24]. For diffusion behavior following typical Arrhenius
behavior, the diffusion kinetics are represented by the relationship D = Doexp(-Q/kT), where, D is the
temperature dependent effective diffusion coefficient (m2/s), Do is a pre-exponential term (m2/s), Q is an
activation energy (kJ/mol), T is temperature, and k is the universal gas constant. A plot of D versus 1/T
would reflect a linear trend with the pre-exponential term (Do) as the x-axis intercept and activation
energy (Q) related to the slope. The IAEA-TECDOC-978 data follows a linear trend reflecting Arrhenius
diffusion, however, the silver diffusion isochronal data reflects an athermal relationship, indicating the
diffusion response did not follow typical Arrhenius diffusion kinetics but was consistent with safety
testing observations via transient and step-wise testing of a maximum release response below 1600C [2,
3, 9].

It was hypothesized that the availability of europium to diffusive through the system was likely impacted
by multiple variables including irradiation temperature and kernel/buffer response. Considering
irradiation temperature, higher irradiation temperatures likely support excess diffusive release from the
kernel during irradiation, as such, europium may be present in the buffer and inner pyrolytic carbon layers
of the particle and therefore have a shorter diffusion pathway required to be released from the particle for
higher temperature irradiation conditions. Similarly variable irradiation induced dimension changes of the
buffer and kernel structure are observed during irradiation [1]. Common responses are uniform
densification of the buffer around the kernel leaving a gap between buffer and inner pyrolytic carbon with
a local region of contact [32] and buffer fracture with and without kernel extrusion where a larger surface
area is present to support direct solid-state diffusion of fission products and actinides to the SiC layer
compared to the uniform buffer densification condition [1]. Analysis of particle cross-sections after safety
testing in the CCCTF show a direct correlation between these particles types (fractured buffer versus
uniform buffer densification) and fission product pile up at the inner pyrolytic carbon/SiC interface,
where fractured buffer particles commonly show a uniform distribution of fission product and actinide
pile-up around the circumference of the inner pyrolytic carbon/SiC interface and particles with uniform
densification show fission product pile-up primarily adjacent to the buffer/IPyC connection region [1].
These variable fission product and actinide distributions at the inner pyrolytic carbon/SiC may reflect
differences in diffusion pathways between particles with different internal layer structures. Additional
analysis is needed to confirm any correlation between internal particle layer structure after irradiation,
154Eu M/C, and impact on FITT release observations.

An assessment of the diffusion kinetics was further explored as the europium diffusion behavior for
AGR-5/6/7 Compact 3-6-3 reflects general Arrhenius behavior. Figure 24 shows the calculated diffusion
coefficients for europium in SiC as a function of inverse temperature for this work, along with legacy data

UCO TRISO. Typical Arrhenius diffusion behavior follows the form D = Doexp(-Q/kT), where, D is the

1/T (shown in Figure 24) allows for determination of the pre-exponential term (Do) and activation energy
(Q). The diffusion kinetics terms, Q and Do were calculated for particles passing 68% and 95%
confidence test (Table 33) using AGR-5/6/7 Compact 3-6-3 data (1,550C AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3
data excluded).

 5. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

5.1 SILVER RELEASE

Direct confirmation of silver release below typical safety testing temperature was confirmed via FITT
analysis. Measuring the direct change in activity of a particle pretest and posttest isolated release from
contributions that may mask diffusive release from integral compact safety testing. Particles were
examined from AGR-5/6/7 Compact 2-2-1 for silver release and showed a maximum in release on a per
particle basis at 1,300–1,400C and a maximum calculated diffusion coefficient for silver in SiC at the
same temperatures, 1,300–1,400C. This effort builds off initial FITT examinations of silver release from
AGR-2 Compact 5-4-2 (3.14 × 1025 n/m2, 12.03% FIMA, and 1,071C time-average volume-average
(TAVA) temperature) which were testing in FITT between 1,100C and 1,300C and indicated 110mAg
release at temperatures below typical safety testing temperatures [7]. the non-Arrhenius, athermal
diffusion behavior corroborates the increase in silver release observed during transient test in FACS at
INL [2] and CCCTF at ORNL [9] and step-wise testing in CCCTF at ORNL [8] as temperatures moved
below 1,600C. The cause of the athermal release was not determined by FITT analysis, further effort
would be required to determine the responsible mechanism and was outside of the scope of this effort.

The 110mAg release was non-uniform with particles from the same tests releasing 0–100% of their
inventory. This response was consistent with historic observations from out of pile test conducted by
Bullock which showed similar non-uniform release response [18]. There was no obvious trend regarding
initial inventory, however, isochronal testing indicated higher 110mAg M/C particles more frequently
passed the 68% and 95% confidence test, though this was not supported by sequential heating tests.
Additional results would be needed to support this; however, it was speculated that this bias could be
resultant from either better counting statistics (higher activity) or regarding diffusion, more available
silver for diffusion reflecting a potential stronger concentration gradient to drive diffusion. While grain
boundary diffusion was shown to be the dominant diffusion mechanism at 1800C safety tests from
AGR-1, grain boundary diffusion was likely not responsible for the non-uniform release in the FITT
assessment as all particles came from the same fabrication batch which showed uniform SiC
microstructure [29].

The assessment of sequential heating tests indicated most silver was release during the initial 50 h
exposure. This led to decreasing magnitude in average calculated diffusion coefficients for silver in SiC
over the subsequent sequential heating steps. This suggests that the calculated diffusion coefficients at
100 h may reflect an underestimation of the diffusivity early in the exposures. The sequential tests also
showed a maximum in calculated diffusion coefficients for silver in SiC at 1,400C. The isochronal tests
showed similar calculated diffusion coefficients for silver in SiC at 1,300C (D = 1.621.83E-14 m2/s,
95% confidence test) and 1,400C (D = 1.671.42E-14 m2/s 95% confidence test). The additional
information from sequential testing suggests 1,400C may reflect the maximum in diffusive release.

Computational modeling efforts predicting silver release have at times shown disagreement with release
from experimental results [33, 34]. This suggests the commonly used diffusion coefficients may not
accurately reflect diffusive release during irradiation at temperatures relevant to those examined via FITT.
The diffusion coefficients calculated for silver in SiC were determined from out of pile testing, however,
if a similar response was active during irradiation it may account for some of the disagreement between

 42

Multiphysics release calculations and experiments. Further effort would be required to simulate release
based on the diffusion coefficients calculated form FITT results.

5.2 EUROPIUM RELEASE

Indication of through layer release has been shown during safety testing of AGR compacts but
confirmation of release below 1,800C safety tests have been difficult due to slow diffusion through
graphitic compact matrix and graphite holders during testing and contributions from europium present
outside of the particles after irradiation. Europium release was assessed via FITT, exploring isochronal
testing at 1,450–1,550C for 500 h and sequential reheat testing at 1,450C from 500–750 h.
Confirmation of out of pile through layer release was determined directly by measuring the change in
154Eu activity before and after testing. Europium release was confirmed at temperatures as low as 1,450C

by isolating release measurements to direct measurement of particles, removing contributions from matrix
release and uptake in graphite holders.

The observed release was generally uniform absent three outlier particles at 1,500C from AGR-5/6/7
Compact 3-6-3 testing. The uniform release was consistent with previous efforts reported by Bullock [18]
on UCO TRISO particles exposed to temperature in out of pile experiments. The magnitude of the
diffusion coefficients for europium in SiC calculated from the FITT effort were similar to the diffusion
coefficients for europium in SiC reported in the Bullock work giving credibility to both tests. No clear
correlation was observed between particles with different starting inventories (154Eu M/C) further
supporting uniform release behaviors. The outlier particles observed at 1,500C 500 h test from Compact
3-6-3 were confirmed to be intact particles. The deviation from uniform release was not confirmed during
this reported testing. A working hypothesis is that irradiation induced structural changes of the buffer and
kernel may impact the diffusive release pathways based on destructive analysis of previously safety tested
particles where intact buffer particles and fractured buffer particles show differences in fission product
pileup at the IPyC/SiC interface after safety testing [1]. Future work would be required to support this
hypothesis. Like the 110mAg FITT assessment, all particles were from identical particle fabrication routes
with similar SiC microstructure [29]. While grain boundary diffusion is expected to be a contributing
diffusion mechanism, no systematic exploration of microstructural effects was explored therefore no
direct insight on the responsible mechanism for release can be determined from the FITT analysis.

The reported diffusion kinetics for europium in SiC during diffusive release from intact TRISO particles
from AGR-5/6/7 Compact 3-6-3 particles supports an Arrhenius behavior for europium diffusive release
and indicates a difference in response for particles exposed to different irradiation conditions. Influence of
irradiation conditions on diffusive release was prominent when comparing the response of “low”
temperature AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 particle tests with the “high” temperature AGR-5/6/7 Compact 3-
6-3 particle test. The AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 particles despite being exposed to higher temperature
(1,550C 500 h) in FITT had limited confidence in release showing only one particle passing the 68%
confidence test with 154Eu release of 2.7±1.9% compared to “high” temperature AGR-5/6/7 Compact
3-6-3 exposed to 1,500C, 500 h (average 154Eu release of 23.61±22.12%). The primary differences
between these results being in pile irradiation conditions experienced by the different compacts. Direct
comparisons between AGR-5/6/7 Compacts 3-6-3 and 5-1-3 were planned as part of this testing but were
not successful due to experimental failures. Direct confirmation of the root-cause of the difference in
response is outside of the scope of this effort; however, it is speculated that europium distribution within
the particle at the end of irradiation may influence release. This may be supported by the initial 154Eu M/C
analysis where AGR-5/6/7 Compact 3-6-3 particles showed indication of existing through layer release
compared to AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 particles (higher averaged starting 154Eu M/C), however no clear
bias was confirmed when exploring the broad range in 154Eu from AGR-5/6/7 Compact 3-6-3. Other

 43

possible variables may be related to SiC irradiation damage, where both compacts experienced a
difference in irradiation temperature and fast fluence (3.391025 n/m2 for AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3
compared to 5.471025 n/m2 for AGR-5/6/7 Compact 3-6-3), which may impact the resultant SiC defect
structure. Future work would improve on the understanding of the impact of in pile conditions on 154Eu
release. Additional confidence of the calculated diffusion kinetics and observed differences between
diffusion observed in “low” temperature AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 and “high” temperature AGR-5/6/7
Compact 3-6-3 would also be obtained.

The calculated diffusion coefficients for europium in SiC and diffusion kinetics (Do = 2.4610-9 m2/s, Q =
201 kJ/mol) established from the 1,450C and 1,500C AGR-5/6/7 Compact 3-6-3 particles that passed
the 95% confidence test show good agreement with historic work and likely represent a conservative
bound for 154Eu release. The conservative bound assessment was due to the identification of elevated
release relative to the diffusion coefficients for europium in SiC calculated for the “low” temperature
AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 and historic Bullock [18] work, also irradiated at lower temperature, having
lower calculated diffusion coefficients. Refinement of the determined diffusion kinetics would occur if
additional testing were conducted to establish more confidence in the temperature dependence as only two
temperatures were used to establish diffusion kinetics for AGR-5/6/7 Compact 3-6-3.
