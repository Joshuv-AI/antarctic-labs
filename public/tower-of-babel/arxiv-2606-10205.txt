Cell-cell adhesion and multiphase Hele–Shaw problem as the singular limit of a Keller–Segel system
Jiwoong Jang, Antoine Mellet

Abstract
We investigate a singular limit of a system of Patlak–Keller–Segel (PKS) equations modeling the evolution of multiple interacting species. Our primary motivation is the Differential Adhesion Hypothesis (DAH), introduced by Malcolm Steinberg in 1962, which posits that cell populations self-organize by minimizing adhesion energy, in a manner analogous to fluids minimizing surface tension. Our starting point is a continuum model describing the evolution of the density distributions of N N distinct species, representing different cell types. These species interact through attractive nonlocal forces governed by interaction kernels of similar form but different strengths (the N × N N\times N matrix of interaction coefficients encodes the key properties of the system). These attractive forces are balanced by a nonlinear pressure, depending on the total density and strong enough to prevent concentration. In the limit of short-range interactions, we establish sufficient conditions on the interaction matrix for cell sorting to take place (i.e., the spontaneous separation of the different species). We then prove a general Γ \Gamma -convergence result for the associated energy functional, showing convergence to an interfacial energy where the surface tension coefficients are determined by a geodesic problem. A detailed analysis of this problem allows us to identify regimes in which engulfment occurs (more adhesive species cluster together and are surrounded by less adhesive ones) which is a key feature of the DAH. In the second part of the paper, we analyze the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the PKS system in the combined long-time and short-range interaction limit. Under a standard energy convergence assumption, we prove the convergence to a multiphase Hele–Shaw problem with surface tension (and contact angle conditions at triple junctions).



Key words.
Chemotaxis, Singular limit, Cell-cell adhesion, Hele–Shaw flow, Multiphase flows.

Introduction

1.1
The multispecies PKS model

Several recent works have investigated the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
of the rescaled Patlak–Keller–Segel (PKS) model for chemotaxis:

\begin{cases}\varepsilon\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta\rho^{m}+\alpha\operatorname{div}(\rho\nabla\phi)=0,&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,\infty),\\
\sigma\phi-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\phi=\rho.&\text{in }\Omega.\end{cases}

(1.1)

When
m>2
, the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
leads to phase separation, that is,
\rho^{\varepsilon}(x,t)\to\theta\chi_{E(t)}
for some constant
\theta>0
(depending only on
m
and the coefficients
\sigma>0
and
\alpha>0
) and the limiting set
E(t)
can be proved to evolve according to the Hele–Shaw free boundary problem with surface tension (see
[
6
,
11
,
17
,
23
,
15
]
).

Our goal is to extend this analysis to a system of coupled Patlak–Keller–Segel equations describing multiple species interacting through chemotaxis and cross-diffusion.
For each
i=1,\dots,N
, we denote by
\rho_{i}(x,t)
the density distribution of the
i
-th species of living organisms (hereafter referred to as “cells”). Its evolution is described by:

\varepsilon\partial_{t}\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\operatorname{div}\left(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\nabla f_{m}^{\prime}\left(\rho_{1}^{\varepsilon}+\dots+\rho_{N}^{\varepsilon}\right)\right)+\operatorname{div}\left(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{ij}\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}_{j}\right)\right)=0\qquad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,\infty),

(1.2)

where
\phi^{\varepsilon}_{j}(\cdot,t)
denotes the chemotaxis potential associated to the
j
-th species, solution of

\sigma\phi^{\varepsilon}_{j}-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\phi^{\varepsilon}_{j}=\rho^{\varepsilon}_{j}\qquad\text{in }\Omega,\quad j=1,\dots N.

(1.3)

The evolution of the density of the
i
-th species is thus coupled to that of the other species through two mechanisms. First, the cross-diffusion term models the tendency of cells to move away from crowded regions via Darcy’s law. It depends on the total density

\rho^{\varepsilon}:=\rho^{\varepsilon}_{1}+\cdots+\rho_{N}^{\varepsilon}
through the simple power-law pressure:

f_{m}(s)=\frac{s^{m}}{m-1},\qquad m>2,

(1.4)

so that when
N=1
, this model reduces to (
1.1
).
Second,
the terms
+\alpha_{ij}\operatorname{div}(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\phi_{j}^{\varepsilon})
describe the interactions between the
i
-th and
j
-th species. In the context of chemotaxis, the potentials
\phi_{j}^{\varepsilon}
are often interpreted as the density of some chemoattractant (produced by the
j
-th cells), solution of the elliptic equation (
1.3
).
We can write
\phi_{j}^{\varepsilon}(x)=\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)\rho_{j}^{\varepsilon}(y)\,dy

for some Green function
G
independent of
j
. In particular the chemotaxis mechanisms are identical and the properties of the system depend on the coefficients
\alpha_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
which represent the strength of the interactions between the
i
-th and
j
-th cells. These interactions can be attractive (
\alpha_{ij}>0
) or repulsive (
\alpha_{ij}<0
)
but we will always assume that self-interactions are attractive (
\alpha_{ii}>0
, see Assumptions
1.2
and
1.2
below).
A key role in our analysis is played by the interaction coefficients matrix

A:=(\alpha_{ij})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}.

Finally, the system is set on a fixed bounded domain
\Omega
and supplemented with Neumann boundary conditions and initial conditions:

\left(-\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\nabla f_{m}^{\prime}\left(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{1}+\cdots+\rho_{N}^{\varepsilon}\right)+\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{ij}\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}_{j}\right)\right)\cdot n=0\qquad\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,\infty),

\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}_{i}\cdot n=0\qquad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

and

\rho^{\varepsilon}_{i}(x,0)=\rho^{\varepsilon}_{i,in}(x)\qquad\text{in }\Omega\qquad\text{for each }i=1,\cdots,N.

These conditions imply in particular that (
1.2
) preserves the mass of each species so we have

\int_{\Omega}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{i}(x,t)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{i,in}(x)\,dx=:m_{i}\qquad\forall t>0.

(1.5)

The primary objective of this paper is to show that differences in the interaction coefficients
\alpha_{ij}
lead to cell sorting and to emergent collective behavior (large-scale patterns arising from simple pairwise interactions).
This will be done by studying the singular limit
\varepsilon\to 0
of the system (
1.2
)-(
1.3
), which can be interpreted as a long-time/large-scale asymptotic limit: the system (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) can be obtained via a rescaling
(x,t)\mapsto(\varepsilon x,\varepsilon^{3}t)
assuming that the microscopic domain is large (so that
\Omega
has size of order
1
after the rescaling) and that the cell populations are large (so that the

are of order
1
after this rescaling).
We refer to Section
2.1
for further details about the role of

in (
1.2
)-(
1.3
).

Our first step is to identify sufficient conditions on the interaction matrix
A=(\alpha_{ij})
under which
the system evolves from overlapping density distributions
\rho_{i}
to segregated regions
E_{i}(t)
where
\rho_{i}>0
and
\rho_{j}=0
for

(see Assumptions
1.2
-
1.2
below).
We then perform an asymptotic analysis of the associated energy functional and prove that it
\Gamma
- converges to an interfacial energy functional where the surface tension coefficients are determined by the length of some geodesics in the phase plane (for an appropriate metric).

This result validates the Differential Adhesion Hypothesis (DAH), introduced by Malcolm Steinberg in 1962
[
31
,
30
,
32
,
33
]
, which states that
cells in a tissue behave like molecules in a fluid: they move and adhere to each other with different strengths and these differences drive the system toward a configuration that minimizes an interfacial surface energy.
Our analysis rigorously connects the system (
1.2
)-(
1.3
)
to the regimes predicted by the DAH: mixing, complete sorting, partial engulfment, and full engulfment. In particular, a careful analysis of the auxiliary geodesic problem allows us to identify conditions on
A
that lead to engulfment (which is a state where more adhesive species cluster together and are surrounded by less adhesive ones).

Finally, we derive an effective evolution equation for the segregated regions
E_{i}(t)
:
Building on recent results for the one-species model

,
17
,
23
]

we prove that, in the sharp-interface limit

, the evolution of the sets

is governed by the multiphase Hele–Shaw free boundary problem with surface tension, thereby providing a continuum description of the dynamics driven by adhesion.

The emergence of collective behavior in multicellular systems as a result of cell-cell interactions is an actively studied topic. Our work is particularly motivated by
[
8
]
, where a Cahn-Hilliard approximation is used to derive a local continuum description of adhesion-driven dynamics. In contrast, we retain here the nonlocal structure of the underlying PKS-type model.

Main results

The PKS system (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) is classically the gradient flow of the energy

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{1}^{\varepsilon},\dots,\rho_{N}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f_{m}(\rho_{1}^{\varepsilon}+\cdots+\rho_{N}^{\varepsilon})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\alpha_{ij}\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\phi_{j}^{\varepsilon}\,dx

In what follows, it will be convenient to use the vector notation

\overrightarrow{\rho}:=({\rho_{1}},\dots,{\rho_{N}})^{T}\,:\,\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N}

We also introduce the function

f(\overrightarrow{\rho})=\begin{cases}f_{m}(\rho_{1}+\cdots+\rho_{N})&\mbox{ if }\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geqslant 0}\\

With these notations, the energy (

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f(\overrightarrow{\rho})-\frac{1}{2}(\overrightarrow{\rho})^{T}A\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}\,dx

\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}:=(\phi_{1}^{\varepsilon},\cdots,\phi_{N}^{\varepsilon})^{T}

solution of (

We thus set

\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f(\overrightarrow{\rho}(x))+\overrightarrow{a}\cdot\overrightarrow{\rho}(x)-\frac{1}{2}\overrightarrow{\rho}^{T}(x)A\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,dx\qquad&\text{if }\int_{\Omega}\overrightarrow{\rho}\,dx=\overrightarrow{m},\\

\chi_{E_{i}}v_{i}\cdot n=0&\textit{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,\infty),\\
\chi_{E_{i}(0)}=\chi_{E_{i,in}}&\textit{in }\Omega.\end{cases}

(1.19)

Moreover, the energy dissipation holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{0}(\overrightarrow{\rho}(t))+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\rho_{i}|v_{i}|^{2}\,dxdt\leqslant\mathcal{E}^{0}(\overrightarrow{\rho}_{in})\qquad\text{for any }t>0.

(iii)

The pressure

\displaystyle p^{\varepsilon}:=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\rho^{\varepsilon}f_{m}^{\prime}(\rho^{\varepsilon})+\overrightarrow{a}\cdot\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}-(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon})^{T}A\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}\right)

converges to a function
p(x,t)
weakly* in
L^{2}_{loc}(0,\infty;(C^{s}(\Omega))^{*})
for any
s\in(0,1)
.

(iv)

If the following energy convergence is satisfied:

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}(t))\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{E}^{0}(\overrightarrow{\rho}(t))\,dt,

(1.20)

then the evolution of the system is described by the multiphase Hele–Shaw free boundary problem with surface tension in the following sense:
The total flux
j:=\rho_{1}v_{1}+\cdots+\rho_{N}v_{N}
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}j\cdot\xi-p\operatorname{div}(\xi)\,dx\,dt=-\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i,j=0}^{N}\sigma_{ij}\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}(\xi)-\nu_{ij}\otimes\nu_{ij}:D\xi)\,d\mu_{ij}\,dt,

(1.21)

for every test vector field
\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T);\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
\xi\cdot n\equiv 0
on
\partial\Omega

where, for
i,j=0,\cdots,N
,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \sigma_{ij}&\text{ is the surface tension coefficient given by \eqref{eq:metric-coefficient}},\\
\mu_{ij}&\text{ denotes the surface measure }d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\lfloor_{\partial^{*}E_{i}\cap\partial^{*}E_{j}\cap\Omega},\\
\nu_{ij}&\text{ denotes the measure theoretic unit normal vector of }\partial^{*}E_{i}\cap\partial^{*}E_{j}\cap\Omega.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Assuming that we have a smooth solution of (
1.19
)-(
1.21
), we can describe more explicitly the evolution of the interfaces
\partial E_{i}
:
We denote by
\nu_{i}
the outward unit normal vector of
\Sigma_{i}:=\partial E_{i}\cap\Omega
and by
\nu_{ij}
the unit normal vector of
\Sigma_{ij}:=\partial E_{i}\cap\partial E_{j}\cap\Omega
directed from
E_{i}
to
E_{j}
.

The continuity equation then leads to the conditions

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{div}\,(v_{i})&=0\text{ in }E_{i}(t),\\
v_{i}\cdot n&=0\text{ on }E_{i}(t)\cap\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.22)

together with the condition

V_{ij}=v_{i}\cdot\nu_{ij}=v_{j}\cdot\nu_{ij},

where
V_{ij}
is the normal speed of
\Sigma_{ij}
in the direction of
\nu_{ij}
.

Next, we turn to Equation (
1.21
): We first note that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}p\,\mathrm{div}\,(\xi)\,dx=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\int_{E_{i}}p\,\mathrm{div}\,(\xi)\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{N}\int_{\Sigma_{ij}}[p]_{\Sigma_{ij}}\xi\cdot\nu_{ij}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}-\int_{\Omega}\nabla p\cdot\xi\,dx,

where
[p]_{\Sigma_{ij}}(x)
denotes the jump of
p
across
\Sigma_{ij}
,
so that (
1.21
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(j+\nabla p)\cdot\xi\,dx\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{N}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Sigma_{ij}}[p]\bigg|_{\Sigma_{ij}}\xi\cdot\nu_{ij}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\,dt

(1.23)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=-\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i,j=0}^{N}\sigma_{ij}\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}(\xi)-\nu_{ij}\otimes\nu_{ij}:D\xi)\,d\mu_{ij}\,dt

(1.24)

This implies that
j=\theta_{i}v_{i}=-\nabla p
in
E_{i}(t)
and
using the classical formula

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{ij}}\mathrm{div}\,(\xi)-\nu_{ij}\otimes\nu_{ij}:D\xi\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}=\int_{\Sigma{ij}}\kappa_{ij}(\xi\cdot\nu_{ij})\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}+\int_{\partial\Sigma_{ij}}\xi\cdot\widetilde{n}_{ij}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}

where
\kappa_{ij}:=\mathrm{div}\,_{\Gamma_{ij}}(\nu_{ij})
is the mean curvature and
\widetilde{n}_{ij}
is the unit conormal vector on
\partial\Gamma_{ij}
,
we obtain the following multiphase Hele-Shaw free boundary problem with surface tension:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta p&=0\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{ in }E_{i}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial p}{\partial n}=0\qquad\text{ on }\partial\Omega\cap E_{i}(t),\\
[p]_{\Sigma_{ij}}&=2\sigma_{ij}\kappa_{ij}\,\,\,\,\,\,\qquad\text{ on }\Sigma_{ij},\\
V_{ij}=-\theta_{i}^{-1}\left[\frac{\partial p}{\partial\nu_{ij}}\right]^{-}&=-\theta_{j}^{-1}\left[\frac{\partial p}{\partial\nu_{ij}}\right]^{+}\text{on }\Sigma_{ij},\\
\sigma_{ij}\widetilde{n}_{ij}+\sigma_{jk}\widetilde{n}_{jk}+\sigma_{ki}\widetilde{n}_{ki}&=0\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{ on }\Sigma_{ijk},\\
\widetilde{n}_{ij}&=n\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{ in }\partial\Sigma_{ij}\cap\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.25)

where
\Sigma_{ijk}:=\partial E_{i}\cap\partial E_{j}\cap\partial E_{k}\cap\Omega
denotes the triple junction.

We point out that the fourth equation in (
1.25
) is a well-known formulation of the Herring contact angle condition at triple junctions (see (
2.2
)), while the last equation of (
1.25
) indicates that the interfaces meet
\partial\Omega
orthogonally.

2
Motivations and related works

2.1
A system of interacting species

The system (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) is a particular system for interacting populations.
In a more general context, it might be natural to associate to each species a different volume density
\mu_{i}>0
and a different mobility coefficient
d_{i}>0
leading to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho_{i}-d_{i}\operatorname{div}(\rho_{i}\nabla f^{\prime}(\rho))+\operatorname{div}\left(\rho_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\left(K_{ij}*\rho_{j}\right)\right)=0,\\
\sigma_{ij}K_{ij}-\Delta K_{ij}=\chi_{ij}\delta_{0},\end{cases}

where
\sigma_{ij},\chi_{ij}>0
are possibly different adhesion coefficients and the pressure depends on the total volume

\rho:=\mu_{1}\rho_{1}+\cdots+\mu_{N}\rho_{N}.

The change of variables,
\overline{\rho}_{i}(x,t)=\mu_{i}\rho_{i}(x,d_{i}t)
and
\overline{K}_{ij}=\frac{1}{d_{i}\mu_{j}\chi_{ij}}K_{ij}
leads back to a system with
\mu_{i}=1
,
d_{i}=1
, and
\chi_{ij}=1
.
On the other hand, we stress the fact that our analysis requires that

{K}_{ij}=\alpha_{ij}G\qquad\mbox{ where $G$ solves }\sigma G-\Delta G=\delta,

meaning that all interactions, while of different strength, result from the same underlying mechanism.
This allows us to compare the self/cross and attraction/repulsion effects simply by comparing the interaction coefficients
\alpha_{ij}
.

Our particular choice of kernel
G
is classical in the context of chemotaxis. While our results are expected to hold for more general kernel
G
, this choice plays a fundamental role in this paper.
Rigorously justifying the result (especially Theorem
1.1
) for more general
G
is an open and challenging problem.

The introduction of the small parameter
\varepsilon
in the system can then be justified as follows:
The formation of sharp interfaces in such system is observed when a large population is observed at appropriate scale.
More precisely, we consider a very large total number of cells, which we parametrize by

\int_{\Omega}\rho_{i}\,dx=m_{i}\varepsilon^{-d}\qquad\text{for some small parameter }\varepsilon\ll 1.

The rescalings
x\mapsto\varepsilon x
and
t\mapsto\varepsilon^{3}t
lead to
\int_{\Omega}\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\,dx=m_{i}
and

\displaystyle\varepsilon\partial_{t}\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\operatorname{div}(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\nabla f_{m}^{\prime}(\rho^{\varepsilon}))+\operatorname{div}\left(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\left(K^{\varepsilon}_{ij}*\rho_{j}^{\varepsilon}\right)\right)=0,

(2.1)

where

K_{ij}^{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-d}K_{ij}(\varepsilon^{-1}x).

With this rescaling and particular choice of
G
, we are able to rigorously identify the asymptotic dynamics and interpret our results in the context of the celebrated Differential Adhesion Hypothesis (DAH) which we discuss in further details below.

2.2
The Differential Adhesion Hypothesis

The Differential Adhesion Hypothesis (DAH), first formulated by Steinberg
[
31
,
30
,
32
,
33
]
, suggests that equilibrium configurations are solely determined by the relative strength of adhesion between the different species of cells via the interfacial energy given by (for two species):

\sigma_{01}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\Sigma_{01})+\sigma_{02}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\Sigma_{02})+\sigma_{12}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\Sigma_{12}).

As a result, the DAH leads to different types of equilibria: mixing, complete sorting, partial and full engulfment (see Figure
2
).

A major contribution of this work is the rigorous derivation of this interfacial energy from the nonlocal interaction model (
1.2
)-(
1.3
).
This derivation leads in particular to the formula (
1.13
)-(
1.14
) that relates the adhesion coefficient
\sigma_{ij}
to the strength of the attraction between the
i
-th and
j
-th species
\alpha_{ij}
.
Although this formula is hardly explicit, the coefficients
\sigma_{ij}
can be computed numerically and we establish (see Theorem
1.2
) rigorously the appearance of the different regimes predicted by the DAH depending on the cross-interaction coefficient.
In Appendix
B
we briefly present some numerical results that illustrate the transition between these regimes depending on the strength of the cross-interactions.

Figure 2
:
The four configurations of cell-cell adhesion from the DAH.

(I) Mixing corresponds to a situation where the two types of cell are not distinguishable energetically (same interaction coefficients) leading in particular to
\sigma_{12}=0
. In our analysis, this case is ruled out by Assumption
1.2
(two such cell populations would be treated as one).

(II) In the complete sorting case, different species avoid interfacial contact with each other (and thus evolve to balls with prescribed volume).
This occurs when

\sigma_{12}=\sigma_{01}+\sigma_{02}

and the interfacial energy reduces to

\displaystyle 2(\sigma_{01}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial^{*}E_{1}\cap\Omega)+\sigma_{02}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial^{*}E_{2}\cap\Omega)).

We show that this energy appears when considering non-interacting species (i.e., with cross-interaction coefficient
\alpha_{12}=0
), see Theorem
1.2
-(i).

(III) Partial engulfment results from cross-interaction that is not exceedingly high, leading cell B (the less cohesive cell) to engulf cell A (the more cohesive one) partially.
It occurs when the adhesion coefficients
\sigma_{ij}
are positive and satisfy strict triangle inequalities

\sigma_{ij}<\sigma_{ik}+\sigma_{kj}\qquad\text{for any permutation }(i,j,k)=(0,1,2).

Equilibrium states are characterized by nontangential contact angles at any triple junctions.
The angles are prescribed by the coefficients
\sigma_{ij}
’s appearing in the energy via the Herring contact angle condition

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(\theta_{0})}{\sigma_{12}}=\frac{\sin(\theta_{1})}{\sigma_{02}}=\frac{\sin(\theta_{2})}{\sigma_{01}}.

(2.2)

Partial engulfment can be viewed as an intermediate state between complete sorting and full engulfment.
Theorem
1.2
-(ii) proves that this regime appears in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
when
the cross-interaction coefficient
\alpha_{12}
is positive but small.

(IV) Finally, full engulfment happens when cell B envelopes cell A completely.
It occurs when we have equality in the triangle inequality:

\sigma_{01}=\sigma_{02}+\sigma_{21}

and we will establish this equality for large enough cross-interaction coefficient

\alpha_{22}\leqslant\alpha_{12}\leqslant\sqrt{\alpha_{11}\alpha_{22}}
- see Theorem
1.2
-(iii). This results coincides with the results of
[
8
,
28
]
.

We mention that the interfacial energy does not favor a particular placement of the engulfed cells A, although
numerical simulation results
[
8
,
28
]
suggest that cells A tend to stay at the center as in Figure
2
(IV). This symmetry is also expected to happen in our framework for a fixed (but small)
\varepsilon>0
as the nonlocal effects should lead to a unique and radially symmetric minimizer.

2.3
Brief review of the literature

This paper is at the intersection of several important topics: the PKS model and free boundary problems, multiphase flows, and cell-cell adhesion.
There is a rich body of literature devoted to single phase problems (the classical Cahn–Hilliard and Allen–Cahn equations, Muskat, Hele–Shaw and Mullins–Sekerka flows with surface tension, etc.).
Since our focus is on the multi-phase framework, we will not review these work extensively here.

In the case
N=1
, connections between PKS type models and free boundary problems
have been explored in various settings. It was first shown in
[
17
,
16
]
that the PKS model with the incompressible constraint
\rho\leqslant 1
leads to the formation of sharp interfaces that evolve according to the (one-phase) Hele–Shaw free boundary problems with surface tension when
\varepsilon
tends to zero.
This result was extended in
[
23
]
to nonlinear diffusion with
m>2
.
We also refer to
[
21
,
29
,
14
]
for further work in this direction on the parabolic-parabolic and elliptic-parabolic PKS model (this last one leads to volume-preserving mean curvature flow).
These results rely on the
\Gamma
-convergence of the associated energy to the perimeter functional, in a way that generalizes the classical Modica–Mortola
[
26
]
convergence result for the Allen–Cahn energy.

In the multiphase setting
N>1
,
[
3
]
generalized the Modica–Mortola result to multi-well potentials and
proved the
\Gamma
-convergence to the multiphase perimeter functional.
In fact,
[
3
]
relies on a suitable generalization of the Modica–Mortola idea in vector form
which we use in our analysis as well (see Theorem
1.1
and Section
4.3
).
The properties of the limiting multiphase perimeter functional are an important topic in the calculus of variation. The lower semicontinuity of such functionals (including anisotropic framework) is investigated in
[
1
]
.
A sufficient condition for the lower semicontinuity is also established in
[
27
]
, which shows that in the isotropic case (as is the case for (
1.12
)), the lower semicontinuity is equivalent to the triangle inequality (
1.16
).

Using the
\Gamma
-convergence result of
[
3
]
,
[
19
]
shows that the vectorial Allen–Cahn equation approximates the multiphase mean curvature flow, whose convergence is proved under the same energy convergence assumption (
1.20
).
In the same spirit, in
[
18
]
, the multiphase version of the
thresholding schemes of
[
7
]
(see also
[
24
,
25
]
)
is proved to converge to the multiphase mean curvature flow.

We note that in
[
7
,
18
,
24
,
25
]
the heat content energy is used to approximate the perimeter functional. Using the same idea,
[
13
]
introduces a diffuse approximation of the Muskat problem with surface tension (modeling the flow of two immiscible fluids, like oil and water, through a porous medium) as well as its multiphase problem. This viewpoint of
[
13
]
allows to construct weak solutions and to prove the rigorous convergence to the Muskat problem under the energy assumption (
1.20
).

Cell-cell adhesion is a classical topic and remains an active area of research. The Differential
Adhesion Hypothesis (DAH) was formulated by Malcolm Steinberg more than fifty years ago

[
31
,
30
,
32
,
33
]
, motivated by earlier adhesion-based experiments
[
12
,
35
]
. In this
series of papers, Steinberg identified four qualitative regimes of equilibrium configurations, which
we describe in Figure
2
.
The analogy between immiscible fluids with distinct surface
tensions and cell populations with different adhesion strengths, together with the hypothesis that
cells reorganize to minimize a total adhesion energy in a manner similar to immiscible fluids, has
motivated extensive theoretical and experimental studies; see, for instance,
[
9
,
10
]
and
the references therein.

Among the many mathematical models proposed for cell-cell adhesion (local or nonlocal, continuum or discrete), the ones most directly related to our work are the continuum formulations used in
[
9
,
5
]
. In particular,
[
9
]
studies a local continuum model obtained via a Cahn–Hilliard-type approximation and provides both mathematical analysis and numerical simulations (see also
[
8
,
28
]
for additional simulation results). We refer to the bibliography of
[
9
]
for further references on adhesion-driven sorting and related modeling approaches. While our motivation is similar, we work in a different regime: we retain a genuinely nonlocal interaction structure (i.e., we do not pass through a Cahn–Hilliard approximation), and nonlinear diffusion effects with
m>2
play a central role in our results.

3.2
The multi-well structure of the energy

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on
the fact that the energy
\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}
can be written as the sum of a potential energy (with multiple wells) and a transition energy.
The computation below generalizes to multispecies the computation used in
[
17
,
23
]

Lemma 3.1
.

Define the function
W\,:\,\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
by

W(\overrightarrow{\rho}):=\displaystyle f(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\overrightarrow{a}\cdot\overrightarrow{\rho}-\frac{1}{2\sigma}\overrightarrow{\rho}^{T}A\overrightarrow{\rho}.

(3.1)

Then, the energy (
1.11
)
can be rewritten in the form

\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(\overrightarrow{\rho})\,dx+\frac{1}{2\sigma\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}(\overrightarrow{\rho}-\sigma\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})^{T}A(\overrightarrow{\rho}-\sigma\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})\,dx+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})^{T}A\nabla\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}\,dx

(3.2)

for all
\overrightarrow{\rho}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geqslant 0}
such that
\int_{\Omega}\overrightarrow{\rho}\,dx=\overrightarrow{m}
and with
\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}
solution of (
1.10
).

Proof.

We recall that

\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\overrightarrow{a}\cdot\overrightarrow{\rho}-\frac{1}{2}(\overrightarrow{\rho})^{T}A\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}\,dx

and we write

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}-\frac{1}{2}(\overrightarrow{\rho})^{T}A(\sigma\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-\frac{1}{2}(\overrightarrow{\rho})^{T}A(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\frac{1}{2}(\overrightarrow{\rho}-\sigma\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})^{T}A(\overrightarrow{\rho}-\sigma\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}(\sigma\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})^{T}A(\overrightarrow{\rho}-\sigma\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})\,dx.

Using Equation (
1.3
), the last term in this equality becomes

\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}(\sigma\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})^{T}A(\overrightarrow{\rho}-\sigma\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})\,\,dx=-\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}(\sigma\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})^{T}A(\Delta\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})\,\,dx=\sigma\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})^{T}A(\nabla\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})\,\,dx.

Putting these equalities together, we get:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\overrightarrow{a}\cdot\overrightarrow{\rho}-\frac{1}{2\sigma}(\overrightarrow{\rho})^{T}A\overrightarrow{\rho}\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2\sigma\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}(\overrightarrow{\rho}-\sigma\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})^{T}A(\overrightarrow{\rho}-\sigma\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})\,dx+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})^{T}A\nabla\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}\,dx

(3.3)

and the result follows.
∎

+\infty&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad\mbox{ where }a_{\alpha}:=\frac{m-2}{m-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\alpha\right)^{\frac{m-1}{m-2}}.

(3.4)

Similarly, using Assumptions
1.2
and
1.2

Proposition 3.2
.

Under Assumptions

3.1
) satisfies:

\displaystyle W(\overrightarrow{\rho})\geqslant 0\quad\forall\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\quad\text{and}\quad\{W(\overrightarrow{\rho})=0\}=\{0,\theta_{1}\overrightarrow{e_{1}},\cdots,\theta_{N}\overrightarrow{e_{N}}\}.

(3.5)

where
\theta_{i}=\left(\frac{1}{2\sigma}\alpha_{ii}\right)^{\frac{1}{m-2}}
.

Proof.

Along the axis
\{u\overrightarrow{e_{i}}\,|\,u\in\mathbb{R}\}
,
W
coincides with the double-well potential (
3.4
), that is

W(u\overrightarrow{e_{i}})=w_{\alpha_{ii}}(u).

It follows from the comment above that
W
is non-negative and only vanishes at the points
0,\theta_{1}\overrightarrow{e_{1}},\cdots,\theta_{N}\overrightarrow{e_{N}}
along the axes.

To complete the proof, it only remains to show that
W

does not have any zeroes that are not on one of the axes.
This will be done by proving that
W
cannot have a local minimizer in
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geqslant 0}\setminus\left(\cup_{i=1}^{N}\{\rho_{i}\overrightarrow{e_{i}}\,|\,\rho_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\}\right)
.

Suppose, by contradiction, that
W
has a local minimizer
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geqslant 0}\setminus\left(\cup_{i=1}^{N}\{\rho_{i}\overrightarrow{e_{i}}\,|\,\rho_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\}\right)
. Since
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}
is not on an axis, at least two of its coordinates
\rho_{i}^{0}
and
\rho_{j}^{0}
must be positive.

Case 1.
Assume that
\alpha_{ii}\neq\alpha_{jj}
.

The function

\displaystyle W_{0}\,:\,(\rho_{i},\rho_{j})\longmapsto W\big(\rho_{1}^{0},\ldots,\rho_{i-1}^{0},\rho_{i},\rho_{i+1}^{0},\ldots,\rho_{j-1}^{0},\rho_{j},\rho_{j+1}^{0},\ldots,\rho_{N}^{0}\big)

has a minimum at
(\rho_{i}^{0},\rho_{j}^{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{>0}
, so its Hessian must have nonnegative determinant. This Hessian is given by:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hessian}(\rho_{i}^{0},\rho_{j}^{0})=\frac{1}{\sigma}\begin{bmatrix}S-\alpha_{ii}&S-\alpha_{ij}\\
S-\alpha_{ji}&S-\alpha_{jj}\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{where }S:=\sigma f_{m}^{\prime\prime}\left(\sum_{i}\rho_{i}^{0}\right)>0.

We have
\det(\mathrm{Hessian}(\rho_{i}^{0},\rho_{j}^{0}))=\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\left(2\alpha_{ij}-(\alpha_{ii}+\alpha_{jj})\right)S
.
Assumption
1.2
implies
2\alpha_{ij}\leqslant 2\sqrt{\alpha_{ii}\alpha_{jj}}<\alpha_{ii}+\alpha_{jj}

(this last inequality is only strict when
\alpha_{ii}\neq\alpha_{jj}
), which together with the fact that
S>0
implies
\det(\mathrm{Hessian}(\rho_{i}^{0},\rho_{j}^{0}))<0
, a contradiction.

Case 2.
Assume that
\alpha_{ii}=\alpha_{jj}
(in that case, we have
\theta_{i}=\theta_{j}
and
a_{i}=a_{j}
).

For
s\in(-\delta,\delta)
with
\delta>0
sufficiently small, we define

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\rho}^{s}=\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}+s(\overrightarrow{e_{i}}-\overrightarrow{e_{j}}).

Then, for all
s\in(-\delta,\delta)
, we have
\rho_{1}^{0}+\cdots+\rho_{N}^{0}=\rho_{1}^{s}+\cdots+\rho_{N}^{s}
and
\overrightarrow{a}\cdot\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}=\overrightarrow{a}\cdot\overrightarrow{\rho}^{s}
(since
a_{i}=a_{j}
).
It follows that the first two terms of
W({\overrightarrow{\rho}}^{s})=f(\rho^{s})+\overrightarrow{a}\cdot{\overrightarrow{\rho}}^{s}-\frac{1}{2\sigma}{(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{s})}^{T}A{\overrightarrow{\rho}}^{s}
do not change with
s
. The assumption that
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}
is a local minimizer of
W
thus implies that the function

\displaystyle g(s):=\frac{1}{2}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{s})^{T}A\overrightarrow{\rho}^{s}\qquad\text{for }s\in(-\delta,\delta)

attains a local maximum at
s=0
. Consequently, we must have

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}(0)=(\overrightarrow{e_{i}}-\overrightarrow{e_{j}})^{T}A(\overrightarrow{e_{i}}-\overrightarrow{e_{j}})\leqslant 0.

Since
A
is positive semidefinite, this implies that
A(\overrightarrow{e_{i}}-\overrightarrow{e_{j}})=0
, which contradicts the assumption
1.2
.
∎

3.3
Connection to the multiphase Allen–Cahn functional

The assumption that
A
is symmetric and positive semidefinite (Assumption
1.2
) implies in particular that all the terms in (
3.2
) are non-negative.
This assumption also implies that
A
can be factored as
A=Q^{T}Q
for some square matrix
Q
(such as the symmetric square root
A^{1/2}
or the upper/lower Cholesky matrices - any such matrix
Q
can be used and we fix one from now on). This allows us to write:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\frac{1}{2\sigma}|Q(\sigma\overrightarrow{\phi^{\varepsilon}}-\overrightarrow{\rho})|^{2}\,dx+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}|Q\nabla\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\frac{1}{2\sigma}|\sigma\overrightarrow{\psi^{\varepsilon}}-Q\overrightarrow{\rho}|^{2}\,dx+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx,\qquad\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}=Q\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}.

(3.6)

Motivated by
[
17
,
23
]
, we now introduce the function
h:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
defined by

h\left(\overrightarrow{\psi}\right):=\inf_{\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{W(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\frac{1}{2\sigma}\left|\sigma\overrightarrow{\psi}-Q\overrightarrow{\rho}\right|^{2}\right\},

(3.7)

which has the multi-well structure inherited from (
3.5
):
It is clear from the definition that
h
is non-negative and that
h\left(\overrightarrow{\psi}\right)=0
if and only if
\sigma\overrightarrow{\psi}=\theta_{i}Q\overrightarrow{e_{i}}
(for which we can take
\overrightarrow{\rho}=\theta_{i}\overrightarrow{e_{i}}
in the infimum).
We thus introduce the phase points

\overrightarrow{\alpha_{0}}:=\overrightarrow{0},\qquad\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}:=\frac{1}{\sigma}\theta_{i}Q\overrightarrow{e_{i}}\quad\mbox{ for }i=1,\cdots,N.

(3.8)

Assumption
1.2
implies that these points are distinct:

Proposition 3.3
.

Under Assumption
1.2
, we have

\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}=\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\overrightarrow{e_{i}}=A\overrightarrow{e_{j}}.

In particular, Assumption
1.2
implies that
\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}\neq\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}}
for all
i\neq j\in\{0,\cdots,N\}
.

The potential
\overrightarrow{\psi}\mapsto h(\overrightarrow{\psi})
defined by (
3.7
) satisfies

\displaystyle h\geqslant 0\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{N}\quad\text{and}\quad\{\overrightarrow{\psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,|\,h(\overrightarrow{\psi})=0\}=\{\overrightarrow{\alpha_{0}},\overrightarrow{\alpha_{1}},\cdots,\overrightarrow{\alpha_{N}}\}.

(3.9)

Proof.

Since
Q\overrightarrow{x}=\overrightarrow{0}
if and only if
A\overrightarrow{x}=\overrightarrow{0}
, we have that

\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}=\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}}\Longleftrightarrow\theta_{i}A\overrightarrow{e_{i}}=\theta_{j}A\overrightarrow{e_{j}}.

Next, we note that the equality
\theta_{i}A\overrightarrow{e_{i}}=\theta_{j}A\overrightarrow{e_{j}}
is equivalent to
\theta_{i}\alpha_{ki}=\theta_{j}\alpha_{kj}
for all
k=1,\dots N
.
Taking
k=i
and
k=j
, we get
\theta_{i}\alpha_{ii}=\theta_{j}\alpha_{ij}
and
\theta_{i}\alpha_{ji}=\theta_{j}\alpha_{jj}
. Recalling that
A
is symmetric, we deduce

\theta_{i}^{2}\alpha_{ii}=\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\alpha_{ij}=\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\alpha_{ji}=\theta_{j}^{2}\alpha_{jj}.

Since
\theta_{k}=\left(\frac{\chi}{2\sigma}\alpha_{kk}\right)^{\frac{1}{m-2}}
, this implies that
\alpha_{ii}=\alpha_{jj}
and thus that
\theta_{i}=\theta_{j}
and
\alpha_{ki}=\alpha_{kj}
for all
k=1,\dots N
. This proves that

\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}=\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A\overrightarrow{e_{i}}=A\overrightarrow{e_{j}}.

To prove the other implication, we note similarly that the equality
A\overrightarrow{e_{i}}=A\overrightarrow{e_{j}}
is equivalent to
\alpha_{ki}=\alpha_{kj}
for all
k=1,\dots N
and taking
k=i
and
k=j
, we get
\alpha_{ii}=\alpha_{ij}=\alpha_{ji}=\alpha_{jj}
and so
\theta_{i}=\theta_{j}
.
it follows that
\theta_{i}A\overrightarrow{e_{i}}=\theta_{j}A\overrightarrow{e_{j}}
which complete the proof.
∎

The formula (
3.3

)
yield:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho})\geqslant\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}),\qquad\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}=Q\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon},

(3.10)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\psi}):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}h(\overrightarrow{\psi})\,dx+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\overrightarrow{\psi}|^{2}\,dx&\text{if }\overrightarrow{\psi}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{N},\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

This functional is a Allen–Cahn type functional with the multi-well structure defined by

Furthermore, the mass constraint on

naturally implies the constraint

.
The asymptotic behavior of

:

Theorem 3.4

(​​​
[

The energy

\Gamma

The surface tension coefficients are given by

and the function

\displaystyle d(\overrightarrow{\zeta_{0}},\overrightarrow{\zeta_{1}}):=\inf\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{h(\gamma(t))}|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|\,dt\,|\,\text{$\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{N}$ is piecewise $C^{1}$},\,\gamma(0)=\overrightarrow{\zeta_{0}},\,\gamma(1)=\overrightarrow{\zeta_{1}}\right\}.

The limiting energy (

Using the alternate formula (

In view of (

The challenge is thus to construct a recovery sequence for the limsup property.

4
The
\Gamma
-convergence of the energy

We take
\sigma=1
for simplicity henceforth in this paper (the general case follows similarly).

4.1
Auxiliary results

Following
[
23
]
, we derive several formula that will be used in the proof of the
\Gamma
-convergence result.
First,
we denote by

f^{*}(\overrightarrow{v}):=\sup_{\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{\overrightarrow{\rho}\cdot\overrightarrow{v}-f(\overrightarrow{\rho})\right\}
the Legendre transform of
f
.
We note that
f
can be written as a function of one variable as follows:

f(\overrightarrow{\rho})=g(\rho_{1}+\cdots\rho_{N})
with
g(s)=\frac{s^{m}}{m-1}
. The definition of the Legendre transform then yields
f^{*}(\overrightarrow{v})=g^{*}(\max\{v_{1},\dots,v_{N}\})
and so (since
g^{*}(p)=\left(\frac{m-1}{m}\max\{p,0\}\right)^{\frac{m}{m-1}}
):

f^{*}(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})=\left(\frac{m-1}{m}\max\{v_{1},\dots,v_{N},0\}\right)^{\frac{m}{m-1}}.

At a point
\overrightarrow{v}
where there is a unique index
i
such that
v_{i}=\max\{v_{1},\dots,v_{N},0\}
, we have
{f^{*}}^{\prime}(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})=\left(\frac{m-1}{m}\max\{v_{1},\dots,v_{N},0\}\right)^{\frac{1}{m-1}}\overrightarrow{e_{i}}
. Otherwise, with

I(\overrightarrow{v}):=\{i=1,\cdots,N\,|\,v_{i}=\max\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{N},0\}\}
, we find

\partial f^{*}(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})=\left\{\left(\frac{m-1}{m}\max\{v_{1},\dots,v_{N},0\}\right)^{\frac{1}{m-1}}\sum_{i\in I(\overrightarrow{v})}\nu_{i}\overrightarrow{e_{i}}\,\Big|\,\nu_{i}\in[0,1]\text{ and }\sum_{i\in I(\overrightarrow{v})}\nu_{i}=1\right\}.

(4.1)

With these notations, we can prove the following result:

Lemma 4.1
.

The following statements hold:

(i)

The function
h
defined by (
3.7
) can also be written as:

\displaystyle h(\overrightarrow{\psi})=\frac{1}{2}|\overrightarrow{\psi}|^{2}-f^{*}(Q^{T}\overrightarrow{\psi}-\overrightarrow{a}).

(4.2)

(ii)

For a given
\overrightarrow{\psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we have

h(\overrightarrow{\psi})=W(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\frac{1}{2}|\overrightarrow{\psi}-Q\overrightarrow{\rho}|^{2}\iff Q^{T}\overrightarrow{\psi}-\overrightarrow{a}\in\partial f(\overrightarrow{\rho})\iff\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\partial f^{*}(Q^{T}\overrightarrow{\psi}-\overrightarrow{a}).

When
\overrightarrow{\psi}=Q\overrightarrow{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, these are also equivalent to

A\overrightarrow{\phi}-\overrightarrow{a}\in\partial f(\overrightarrow{\rho})

and

\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\partial f^{*}(A\overrightarrow{\phi}-\overrightarrow{a})
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\partial f^{*}(\theta_{i}A\overrightarrow{e_{i}}-\overrightarrow{a})=\{\theta_{i}{\overrightarrow{e_{i}}}\}

for all
i=0,\cdots,N
(with
\theta_{0}\overrightarrow{e_{0}}:=\overrightarrow{0}
).

(iii)

After re-numbering the species so that
\alpha_{11}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\alpha_{NN}
, we have: Given
i,j=1,\cdots,N
with
j\geqslant i
, there holds
\alpha_{jj}\phi_{j}-a_{j}\geqslant\alpha_{ji}\phi_{j}-a_{i}\text{ for any }\phi_{j}\in[0,\theta_{j}].
As a consequence, for any
j=1,\cdots,N
and
\phi_{j}\in[0,\theta_{j}]
, there exists
\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\partial f^{*}(\phi_{j}A\overrightarrow{e_{j}}-\overrightarrow{a})
such that

\overrightarrow{\rho}\cdot\overrightarrow{e_{i}}=0\quad\mbox{ for all }i=1,\cdots,j-1

(in other words, there is a vector in the subdifferential
\partial f^{*}(\phi_{j}A\overrightarrow{e_{j}}-\overrightarrow{a})
whose
first
j-1
components are zero).

Proof.

(i)

We compute

\displaystyle h(\overrightarrow{\psi})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{W(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\frac{1}{2}|\overrightarrow{\psi}-Q\overrightarrow{\rho}|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{f(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\overrightarrow{a}\cdot\overrightarrow{\rho}-\frac{1}{2}|Q\overrightarrow{\rho}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\overrightarrow{\psi}|^{2}-\overrightarrow{\psi}^{T}Q\overrightarrow{\rho}+\frac{1}{2}|Q\overrightarrow{\rho}|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}|\overrightarrow{\psi}|^{2}-\left((Q^{T}\overrightarrow{\psi}-\overrightarrow{a})\cdot\overrightarrow{\rho}-f(\overrightarrow{\rho})\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|\overrightarrow{\psi}|^{2}-\sup_{\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{(Q^{T}\overrightarrow{\psi}-\overrightarrow{a})\cdot\overrightarrow{\rho}-f(\overrightarrow{\rho})\right\}=\frac{1}{2}|\overrightarrow{\psi}|^{2}-f^{*}(Q^{T}\overrightarrow{\psi}-\overrightarrow{a}),

(4.3)

which yields the first result.

(ii)

Next, the computation above shows that the infimum in the definition of
h
is attained at
\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
if and only if the supremum in (
4.3
) is attained at the same
\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Classically, this is equivalent to
Q^{T}\overrightarrow{\psi}-\overrightarrow{a}\in\partial f(\overrightarrow{\rho})
. The last equivalence is a standard property of the Legendre transform.

Finally, since we have
h(\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}})=0=W(\theta_{i}\overrightarrow{e_{i}})+\frac{1}{2}|\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}-\theta_{i}Q\overrightarrow{e_{i}}|^{2}
, the argument above implies that

\theta_{i}{\overrightarrow{e_{i}}}\in\partial f^{*}(\theta_{i}A\overrightarrow{e_{i}}-\overrightarrow{a})
.

Conversely, let
\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\partial f^{*}(\theta_{i}A\overrightarrow{e_{i}}-\overrightarrow{a})
. Then we must have
h(\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}})=0=W(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\frac{1}{2}|\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}-Q\overrightarrow{\rho}|^{2}
.
But this means that
W(\overrightarrow{\rho})=0
and
Q\overrightarrow{\rho}=\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}
. The first condition implies that

\overrightarrow{\rho}=\theta_{i}\overrightarrow{e_{j}}
for some
j=0,\cdots,N
, and the second one then gives
Q\theta_{i}\overrightarrow{e_{j}}=\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}
which in view of Proposition
3.3

implies
\overrightarrow{\rho}=\theta_{i}\overrightarrow{e_{i}}
(this is an important consequence of our Assumption
1.2
).

(iii)

Given two indices
j>i
, we first prove that

\alpha_{jj}\phi_{j}-a_{j}\geqslant\alpha_{ji}\phi_{j}-a_{i}\qquad\text{ for all }\phi_{j}\in[0,\theta_{j}].

(4.4)

By interpolation, it is enough to show that (
4.4
) holds when
\phi_{j}=0
and
\phi_{j}=\theta_{j}
: when
\phi_{j}=0
the inequality follows from the ordering
\alpha_{jj}\leqslant\alpha_{ii}
and the definition of
a_{i}
. When
\phi_{j}=\theta_{j}
is it a consequence of (ii) (that is,
\theta_{j}{\overrightarrow{e_{j}}}\in\partial f^{*}(\theta_{j}A\overrightarrow{e_{j}}-\overrightarrow{a})
) and the form of the subgradient
\partial f^{*}
(
4.1
).

Next, denoting
\overrightarrow{v}=\phi_{j}A\overrightarrow{e_{j}}-\overrightarrow{a}
, we have
v_{i}=\phi_{j}\alpha_{ij}-a_{i}
, so (
4.4
) implies that

v_{i}\leqslant v_{j}
for all
i<j
. We deduce that there exists
k\geqslant j
such that
v_{k}=\max\{v_{1},\dots,v_{N},0\}
. The definition (
4.1
) implies that

\left(\frac{m-1}{m}v_{k}\right)^{\frac{1}{m-1}}\overrightarrow{e_{k}}\in\partial f^{*}(\phi_{j}A\overrightarrow{e_{j}}-\overrightarrow{a}).

We can thus choose
\overrightarrow{\rho}=\left(\frac{m-1}{m}v_{k}\right)^{\frac{1}{m-1}}\overrightarrow{e_{k}}
, which has the desired property.

∎

We now introduce the following relaxations of the energy functional:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\frac{1}{2}|Q\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}-Q\overrightarrow{\rho}|^{2}\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla Q\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx&\text{if }\overrightarrow{\rho}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{N},\\
\infty&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(4.5)

with
\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}
solution of (
1.10
) (
so
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho})=\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho})
whenever
\int_{\Omega}\overrightarrow{\rho}\,dx=\overrightarrow{m}
)
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho},\overrightarrow{\psi}):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\frac{1}{2}|\overrightarrow{\psi}-Q\overrightarrow{\rho}|^{2}\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\overrightarrow{\psi}|^{2}\,dx\quad\text{for }(\overrightarrow{\rho},\overrightarrow{\psi})\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{N}\times H^{1}(\Omega)^{N}

\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}
solution of (

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\psi}):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}h(\overrightarrow{\psi})\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\overrightarrow{\psi}|^{2}\,dx&\text{if }\overrightarrow{\psi}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{N},\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We then have the following result:

Proposition 4.2
.

The following statements hold:

(i)

For all
\overrightarrow{\rho}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{N}
, it holds

\displaystyle\min_{\overrightarrow{\psi}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}\mathcal{G}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho},\overrightarrow{\psi})=\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}).

The minimum is attained when
\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}=Q\phi^{\varepsilon}
with
\phi^{\varepsilon}
solution of (
1.10
).

(ii)

For all
\overrightarrow{\psi}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
, it holds

\displaystyle\min_{\overrightarrow{\rho}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{N}}\mathcal{G}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho},\overrightarrow{\psi})=\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\psi}).

The minimum is attained at
\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\partial f^{*}(Q^{T}\overrightarrow{\psi}-\overrightarrow{a})
.

(iii)

There exists a constant
C
such that: If
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{1},\overrightarrow{\rho}^{2}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{N}
have disjoint supports with

\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{supp}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{1}),\mathrm{supp}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{2}))>\delta

for some
\delta>0
, then

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{1}+\overrightarrow{\rho}^{2})\leqslant\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{1})+\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{2})+C\|\overrightarrow{\rho}^{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{N}}\|\overrightarrow{\rho}^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{N}}e^{-\frac{\delta}{C\varepsilon}}

for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
small enough.

Proof.

(i)

Write

\displaystyle\min_{\overrightarrow{\psi}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}\mathcal{G}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho},\overrightarrow{\psi})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(\overrightarrow{\rho})\,dx+\min_{\overrightarrow{\psi}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\overrightarrow{\psi}-Q\overrightarrow{\rho}|^{2}\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\overrightarrow{\psi}|^{2}\,dx\right\}.

It is now classical that the minimization problem from the second term is attained for
\overrightarrow{\psi}
(variational) solution of

\begin{cases}\overrightarrow{\psi}-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\overrightarrow{\psi}=Q\overrightarrow{\rho}&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
\nabla\overrightarrow{\psi}\cdot n=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

The result follows.

(ii)

The conclusion follows from Lemma
4.1
and from the fact that the form
\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\partial f^{*}(Q^{T}\overrightarrow{\psi}-\overrightarrow{a})
guarantees
\overrightarrow{\rho}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geqslant 0})
.

(iii)

We note that (
4.5
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\overrightarrow{a}\cdot\overrightarrow{\rho}-\frac{1}{2}\overrightarrow{\rho}^{T}A\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}\,dx

(via the same computation as in the derivation of (
3.2
)). Since
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{1}
and
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{2}
have disjoint supports, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{1}+\overrightarrow{\rho}^{2})-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{1})-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}{\overrightarrow{\rho}^{1}}^{T}A\overrightarrow{\phi^{2}}^{\varepsilon}+{\overrightarrow{\rho}^{2}}^{T}A\overrightarrow{\phi^{1}}^{\varepsilon}\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{M}{\varepsilon}\max_{i,j=1,\cdots,N}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)\rho^{1}_{j}(x)\rho^{2}_{j}(y)\,dxdy

where
M:=\max\{|\alpha_{ij}|\,;\,i,j=1,\cdots,N\}
and

is the green kernel of the equation (

) (so that
\phi_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)\rho_{i}(y)\,dy
). The result then follows from the assumption
\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{supp}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{1}),\mathrm{supp}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{2}))>\delta
and the classical fact that

\displaystyle G(x,y)\leqslant Ce^{-\frac{|x-y|}{C\varepsilon}}

for some
C>0
large enough (depending only on
\Omega
).

∎

4.2
The liminf property

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
1.1
-(i), which follows from the inequality (
3.10
) and the
\Gamma
-convergence of the functional
\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}
given by Theorem
3.4
.
We recall the main steps below, as they will be useful later on, and refer to
[
3
]
for details about the proofs.

First, an important role is played by the functions
\varphi_{i}\,:\,\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
defined for
i=0,\cdots,N
by

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}:=\max\left\{2d(\cdot,\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}),M\right\}

where
d
is the distance function defined by (
3.12
)
and
M>0
is a fixed constant larger than
2d(\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}},\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}})+1
for any
i,j=0,\cdots,N
.
The following result is the key to adapting the well-known Modica–Mortola trick to this multiphase setting:

Proposition 4.3

(​​​
[
3
, Proposition 2.1, (2.2)]
)
.

For any
i=0,\cdots,N
, the function
\varphi_{i}
is bounded and Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. For any
\overrightarrow{\psi}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N})
, it holds that
\varphi_{i}\circ\overrightarrow{\psi}\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
and that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\left|\nabla(\varphi_{i}\circ\overrightarrow{\psi})\right|\,dx\leqslant\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\sqrt{2h(\overrightarrow{\psi}(x))}\left|\nabla\overrightarrow{\psi}(x)\right|\,dx

for any open subsets
\Omega^{\prime}
of
\Omega
.

With this result in hand, we can prove the liminf property:
Consider a sequence
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}
which converges to
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}
strongly in
L^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. If
\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon})=+\infty
, the statement trivially holds, so we assume that
\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon})<+\infty
and fix
M>0
and a subsequence (still denoted by
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}
) along which

\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon})\leqslant M.

(4.6)

In particular, we have
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geqslant 0}
a.e. on
\Omega
and
\int_{\Omega}\overrightarrow{\rho}\,dx=\overrightarrow{m}
. Up to another sequence, we can further assume that
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}
converges to
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}
a.e. on
\Omega
, and therefore that
W(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon})\to W(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0})
a.e. on
\Omega
. Since
\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon})\leqslant M
, we have

\int_{\Omega}W(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon})\,dx\leqslant\varepsilon\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon})\leqslant M\varepsilon,

and Fatou lemma implies that
W(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0})=0
a.e. on
\Omega
. Consequently, we have
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}\chi_{E_{i}}\overrightarrow{e_{i}}
for some family of subsets
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of
\Omega
and so
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}\in\mathcal{F}(A,\overrightarrow{m}
).

Denote
\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}
solution to (
1.10
) and
\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}:=Q\overrightarrow{\phi^{\varepsilon}}
.
For any collection of open sets
\{U_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
such that
U_{i}\cap U_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
, Proposition
4.3
and (
3.10
) implies

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{N}\left|\nabla(\varphi_{i}\circ\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon})\right|(U_{i})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{N}\int_{U_{i}}\sqrt{2h(\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}(x))}\left|\nabla\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}(x)\right|\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{2h(\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}(x))}\left|\nabla\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}(x)\right|\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}h(\overrightarrow{\psi^{\varepsilon}})\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx\leqslant\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}).

(4.7)

By taking the supremum over all such collections,
we obtain
2
2

2

For two regular nonnegative Borel measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
\Omega
, the supremum
\mu\vee\nu
is defined by

\displaystyle(\mu\vee\nu)(U)=\sup\{\mu(U^{\prime})+\nu(U^{\prime\prime}):\ U^{\prime}\cap U^{\prime\prime}=\varnothing,\,U^{\prime}\cup U^{\prime\prime}\subset U,\,U^{\prime}\text{ and }U^{\prime\prime}\text{ are open sets in }\Omega\}.

It is the smallest regular measure which is greater than or equal to
\mu
and
\nu
on any Borel subsets of
\Omega
.

\displaystyle\bigvee_{i=0}^{N}\left|\nabla(\varphi_{i}\circ\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon})\right|(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\{U_{i}\}}\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{N}\left|\nabla(\varphi_{i}\circ\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon})\right|(U_{i})\right\}\leqslant\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}).

(4.8)

We can then check that
\varphi_{i}\circ\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}
converges to
\varphi_{i}\circ Q\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}
strongly in

. Indeed, the bound on the energy (

) implies

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{i}\circ\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{i}\circ Q\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\varphi_{i}\circ Q\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{i}\circ Q\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\varphi_{i}\circ Q\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{i}\circ Q\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\left\|Q\left(\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}-\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}-\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\sqrt{M\varepsilon}+\|\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}-\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\right)\to 0\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

The lower semicontinuity of the BV norm then yields

\displaystyle\left|\nabla(\varphi_{i}\circ Q\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0})\right|(U)\leqslant\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}|\nabla(\varphi_{i}\circ\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon})|(U)

(4.9)

for all open subsets

, and therefore

\displaystyle\bigvee_{i=0}^{N}\left|\nabla(\varphi_{i}\circ Q\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0})\right|(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{N}\left|\nabla(\varphi_{i}\circ Q\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0})\right|(U_{i})\,|\,U_{i}\subset\Omega\text{ is open and }U_{i}\cap U_{j}=\emptyset\text{ for $i\neq j$}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bigvee_{i=0}^{N}\left|\nabla(\varphi_{i}\circ\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon})\right|(\Omega).

We deduce (using (
4.8

\displaystyle\bigvee_{i=0}^{N}\left|\nabla(\varphi_{i}\circ Q\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0})\right|(\Omega)\leqslant\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bigvee_{i=0}^{N}\left|\nabla(\varphi_{i}\circ\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon})\right|(\Omega)\leqslant\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}).

Finally, we recall that
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}\chi_{E_{i}}\overrightarrow{e_{i}}
and (
4.2
) and (

) imply that
\varphi_{i}\circ Q\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}\in BV(\Omega)
for all

. We thus have
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}\in\mathcal{F}(\Omega,\overrightarrow{m})
. Finally,
[

gives

\bigvee_{i=0}^{N}\left|\nabla(\varphi_{i}\circ Q\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0})\right|(\Omega)=\sum_{i,j=0}^{N}d(\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}},\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}})\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial^{*}E_{i}\cap\partial^{*}E_{j}\cap\Omega)

(4.10)

which completes the proof.

4.3
The limsup property

We now prove Theorem

-(ii), namely the existence of a recovery sequence.

Proof of Theorem

-(ii).

Fix
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega)^{N}
. If
\mathcal{E}^{0}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0})=+\infty
, the statement holds trivially, so we assume that
\mathcal{E}^{0}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0})<+\infty
. We can then write

for a partition

of

By

As in

Step 1:
Construct a sequence

1.

2.

for all

(4.13)

3.

In particular

for some constant

4.

The transition region

satisfies:

(4.15)

for some constant

With this recovery sequence at hand, we now define the corresponding density distribution using (

\widetilde{\overrightarrow{\rho}}^{\varepsilon,0}\in\partial f^{*}(Q^{T}\overrightarrow{\psi^{\varepsilon,0}}-\overrightarrow{a})

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}\left(\widetilde{\overrightarrow{\rho}}^{\varepsilon,0}\right)\leqslant\mathcal{G}^{\varepsilon}\left(\widetilde{\overrightarrow{\rho}}^{\varepsilon,0},\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon,0}\right)=\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}\left(\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon,0}\right).

We immediately note that Lemma

We now need to show that we can modify this sequence
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon,0}
to get the correct mass constraint. This will be done in two steps: First, we show that we can replace
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon,0}

Step 2:
Construct a sequence

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}\left(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon,0}\right)\leqslant\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}\left(\widetilde{\overrightarrow{\rho}}^{\varepsilon,0}\right)+C\varepsilon.

(4.28)

We thus have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\eta_{j}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,dx\leqslant C_{4}\varepsilon^{1+\frac{1}{2d}}\qquad\mbox{ for all }j=2,\dots,N.

(4.29)

and we can change the mass defect
\overline{m}_{j}^{\varepsilon}
,
j=2,\dots,N
to

\displaystyle\overline{m}_{j}^{\prime\varepsilon}:=\overline{m}_{j}^{\varepsilon}-\int_{B_{1}}\eta_{j}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,dx.

In view of (
4.18
) and (
4.29
), we still have

\displaystyle C_{0}^{-1}\varepsilon/2\leqslant\overline{m}_{j}^{\prime\varepsilon}\leqslant C_{0}\varepsilon

(4.30)

as long as
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
is small enough.

We can now proceed in the same way to construct the corrector
\overrightarrow{\eta}^{\varepsilon}
in
B_{2}
. It should satisfy

\overrightarrow{\eta}^{\varepsilon}(x)\in\partial f^{*}(\chi_{2}^{\varepsilon,s_{2}}(x)Q^{T}\overrightarrow{\alpha_{2}}-\overrightarrow{a})\qquad\forall x\in B_{2}.

(4.31)

and for the same reasons as above, it is possible to choose
s_{2}\in[0,r_{2}]
so that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{2}}\eta_{2}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,dx={\overline{m}^{\prime}}_{2}^{\varepsilon}.

This implies that

\int_{B_{1}\cup B_{2}}\eta_{2}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,dx=\overline{m}_{2}^{\varepsilon}.

Crucially, this corrector might have non trivial components
\eta_{j}(x)
for
j=3,\dots,N
in the ring

\{s_{2}\leqslant|x-x_{2}|\leqslant s_{2}+\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2d}}\}
,
but thanks to (
4.21
) and Lemma
4.1
-(iii), we can choose
\overrightarrow{\eta}^{\varepsilon}(x)
satisfying (
4.31
) such that
\eta_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x)=0
in
B_{2}
. We thus still have

\int_{B_{1}\cup B_{2}}\eta_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,dx=\overline{m}_{1}^{\varepsilon}.

This is the key remark that allows us to adjust the mass of the second component while keeping the mass of the first component constant.

We can now iterate this process, taking
\varepsilon
a bit smaller at each step and constructing a corrector
\overrightarrow{\eta}^{\varepsilon}(x)
satisfying in particular

\mathrm{Supp}\;\eta_{i}^{\varepsilon}\subset\cup_{j\leqslant i}B_{j},\quad\int_{\Omega}\eta_{i}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,dx=\overline{m}_{i}^{\varepsilon}.

The function

\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}=\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon,0}+\overrightarrow{\eta}^{\varepsilon}

satisfies

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geqslant 0}),\,\int_{\Omega}\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}\,dx=\overrightarrow{m},\text{ and }\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}\text{ converges to $\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}$ strongly in }L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N}).

(4.32)

Furthermore, since the
\chi_{i}^{\varepsilon,s_{i}}(x)
have disjoint support, we can write:

\overrightarrow{\eta}^{\varepsilon}(x)\in\partial f^{*}(Q^{T}\overrightarrow{\chi}^{\varepsilon}(x)-\overrightarrow{a})\qquad\forall x\in B_{1}

where

\overrightarrow{\chi}^{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}^{\varepsilon,s_{i}}(x)\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}.

Step 3:
Estimate the contribution of the corrector to the energy.

Define

\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}=\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon,0}+\overrightarrow{\chi}^{\varepsilon}.

We note that the two term have disjoint supports so we can write:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon})=\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}\left(\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon,0}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{B_{j}}h(\overrightarrow{\chi}^{\varepsilon})\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{B_{j}}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\overrightarrow{\chi}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx\right).

(4.33)

Since
\overrightarrow{\chi}^{\varepsilon}=\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}}
in
B_{s_{j}}(x_{j})
, the function
h(\overrightarrow{\chi}^{\varepsilon})
is supported in the ring
\{s_{j}\leqslant|x-x_{1}|\leqslant s_{j}+\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2d}}\}
and so
(using (
4.28
)):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{B_{j}}h(\overrightarrow{\chi}^{\varepsilon})\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{\varepsilon}|\{s_{j}\leqslant|x-x_{1}|\leqslant s_{j}+\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2d}}\}|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\varepsilon^{-1}\omega_{d}\left(\left(r_{j}+\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2d}}\right)^{d}-r_{j}^{d}\right)\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2d}}\to 0\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

(4.34)

Similarly,
\nabla\overrightarrow{\chi}^{\varepsilon}
is supported in that same ring and satisfies
|\nabla\overrightarrow{\chi}^{\varepsilon}(x)|\leqslant\varepsilon^{-\frac{3}{2d}}
so that

\displaystyle\varepsilon\int_{B_{j}}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\overrightarrow{\chi}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx\leqslant C\varepsilon\,\omega_{d}\left(\left(r_{j}+\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2d}}\right)^{d}-r_{j}^{d}\right)\,\varepsilon^{-\frac{3}{2d}}\leqslant C\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{d}}\to 0\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0

(4.35)

for
d\geqslant 2
. Combining (
4.12
), (
4.33
), (
4.3
), and (
4.35
), we obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon})\leqslant\mathcal{E}^{0}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{0}).

(4.36)

For
j=1,\cdots,N
, denote by
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon,j}
defined by
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon,j}=\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}
on the
j
-th ball
B_{j}
and by zero outside
B_{j}
. Similarly, for
j=1,\cdots,N
, denote by
\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon,j}
defined by
\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon,j}=\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}
on the
j
-th ball
B_{j}
and by zero outside
B_{j}
. As (
4.11
) holds with
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon,j}
and
\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon,j}
for each

, we have, by Proposition
4.2
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}\left(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon,j}\right)\leqslant\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}\left(\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon,j}\right)\qquad\text{for each }j=1,\cdots,N.

(4.37)

Therefore, combining (
4.20
), (
4.24
), (
4.32
), (
4.37
), and successive applications of Proposition
4.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon})=\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{N}\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon,j}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{N}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\varepsilon}\left(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon,j}\right)+NCe^{-\frac{\delta_{0}}{C\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{N}\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}\left(\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon,j}\right)+C\varepsilon\delta^{-1}+NCe^{-\frac{\delta_{0}}{C\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}\left(\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}\right)+C\varepsilon\delta^{-1}+NCe^{-\frac{\delta_{0}}{C\varepsilon}}.

Taking the limit supremum as
\varepsilon\to 0
to the above and using (
4.36
) finish the proof.
∎

5
Applications to cell-cell adhesion and the DAH: Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section, we establish some key properties of the limiting interfacial energy
\mathcal{E}^{0}
depending on the interaction coefficients
\alpha_{ij}
.
We recall that the adhesion coefficients appearing in
\mathcal{E}^{0}
depend on the
\alpha_{ij}
via the formula(
3.11
) which we recall here for convenience:

\sigma_{ij}:=d(\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}},\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}}),\qquad i,j=0,\dots N

where the function
d:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to[0,\infty]
is the distance defined by (
3.12
) and also recalled here:

\displaystyle d(\overrightarrow{\zeta_{0}},\overrightarrow{\zeta_{1}}):=\inf\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{h(\gamma(t))}|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|\,dt\,|\,\text{$\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{N}$ is piecewise $C^{1}$},\,\gamma(0)=\overrightarrow{\zeta_{0}},\,\gamma(1)=\overrightarrow{\zeta_{1}}\right\}

for any
\overrightarrow{\zeta_{0}},\overrightarrow{\zeta_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
In what follows, we will refer as geodesic the curve that realizes the infimum in this definition (assuming it exists. the rigorous arguments will rely on almost geodesics).

As detailed in Section
2.2
, the properties of
\mathcal{E}^{0}
are related to cases of equality in the triangle inequality

d(\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}},\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}})\leqslant d(\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}},\overrightarrow{\alpha_{k}})+d(\overrightarrow{\alpha_{k}},\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}})

which we explore in this section.

5.1
Non-interacting species

We start with a simple particular case in which cells of different species do not interact (
\alpha_{ij}=0
if
i\neq j
). In that case we can prove the following proposition which implies Theorem
1.2
-(i) when
N=2
:

Proposition 5.1
.

If
A
is diagonal, then
d(\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}},\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}})=d(\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}},0)+d(0,\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}})
that is:

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{i0}+\sigma_{0j}\qquad\text{for any }i,j=1,\cdots,N.

Therefore, for
\overrightarrow{\rho}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}\chi_{E_{i}}\overrightarrow{e_{i}}\in\mathcal{F}(A,\overrightarrow{m})
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{0}(\overrightarrow{\rho})=\sum_{i,j=0}^{N}\sigma_{ij}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial^{*}E_{i}\cap\partial^{*}E_{j}\cap\Omega)=2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{0i}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial^{*}E_{i}\cap\Omega).

In particular, if
\overrightarrow{\rho}
minimizes
\mathcal{E}^{0}
, then each
E_{i}
minimizes the energy
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial^{*}E_{i}\cap\Omega)
, which leads to surfaces with constant mean-curvature that intersects the fixed boundary
\Omega
with normal contact angle.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Since
A
is diagonal, the matrix

is the diagonal matrix with entries
\alpha_{jj}^{1/2}
and we have

\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}=\theta_{i}\alpha_{ii}^{1/2}\overrightarrow{e_{i}}.

In particular, we can write:

\displaystyle h(\overrightarrow{\psi})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{W(\overrightarrow{\rho})+\frac{1}{2}|\overrightarrow{\psi}-Q\overrightarrow{\rho}|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{f\left(\sum_{j}\rho_{j}\right)+\sum_{j}a_{j}\rho_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\alpha_{jj}\rho_{j}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}(\psi_{j}-\alpha_{jj}^{1/2}\rho_{j})^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf_{\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{\sum_{j}f\left(\rho_{j}\right)+\sum_{j}a_{j}\rho_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\alpha_{jj}\rho_{j}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}(\psi_{j}-\alpha_{jj}^{1/2}\rho_{j})^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{j}h(\psi_{j}\overrightarrow{e_{j}})

(5.1)

where we used the fact that

when

is given by (
1.9
).

We can now use this inequality to show that the geodesic (that is the curve that realizes the minimum in the definition of

(

))
from
\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}
to
\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}}
is the curve
\gamma_{0}
which is the piecewise straight line along the axes from
\overrightarrow{\alpha_{i}}
to
0
and then from
0
to
\overrightarrow{\alpha_{j}}
:

Fix
i=1
and
j=2
(without loss of generality) and let

\overrightarrow{\psi}=(\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{N}):[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an arbitrary piecewise

curve joining
\overrightarrow{\alpha_{1}}
to
\overrightarrow{\alpha_{2}}
, that is

\overrightarrow{\psi}(0)=(\theta_{1}\alpha_{11}^{1/2},0,\dots 0),\qquad\overrightarrow{\psi}(1)=(0,\theta_{2}\alpha_{22}^{1/2},0,\dots 0).

(5.2)

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (

), we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{h(\overrightarrow{\psi})}|\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\prime}|\,dt

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{N}h(\psi_{k}\overrightarrow{e_{k}})}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1,2}\psi_{k}^{\prime 2}}\,dt

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{h(\psi_{k}\overrightarrow{e_{k}})}|\psi_{k}^{\prime}|\,dt.

6
Convergence of the gradient flow

We keep

(6.1)

6.1
Phase separation: Theorem
1.3
(i)-(ii)

We begin by recording uniform estimates that are needed in the proof:

Lemma 6.1
.

The following hold, where
C_{M}>0
is a suitable constant that is large depending only on

.

(i)

\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}(t))\leqslant M
for all

.

(ii)

\|\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{m}(\Omega))}\leqslant C_{M}
for

.

(iii)

\|j_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}\left(0,\infty;L^{\frac{2m}{m+1}}(\Omega)\right)}\leqslant C_{M}
for
i=1,\cdots,N
.

(iv)

\|\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}(t)-\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}(s)\|_{W^{-1,\frac{2m}{m+1}}(\Omega)}\leqslant C_{M}\sqrt{t-s},
for all
0\leqslant s\leqslant t.

Proof.

Recall that the energy inequality (Theorem
A.1
-(v)) yields:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}(t))+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|j_{i}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{\rho^{\varepsilon}_{i}}\,dxdt\leqslant M.

(6.2)

(i)

This is a direct consequence of (
6.2
).

(ii)

From the form
f=f_{m}
with

, the coercivity property holds in the sense that there are constants

such that

\displaystyle W(\overrightarrow{\rho})\geqslant c|\overrightarrow{\rho}|^{m}\qquad\text{whenever }|\overrightarrow{\rho}|\geqslant C\text{ for }\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geqslant 0}.

The statement (iii) follows from the coercivity property and the estimate that
\int_{\Omega}W(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}(t))\,dx\leqslant\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}(t))\leqslant M
for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
t>0
.

(iii)

By Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\|j_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\frac{2m}{m+1}}(\Omega)}\leqslant\|(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon})^{1/2}\|_{L^{2m}(\Omega)}\left\|\frac{j_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon})^{1/2}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

which implies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|j_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\frac{2m}{m+1}}(\Omega)}^{2}\,dt\leqslant C_{M}\qquad\text{for each }i=1,\cdots,N.

(iv)

We first note that for
\psi\in W^{1,\frac{2m}{m-1}}(\Omega)
, we have, by (iii),

\displaystyle\left\|\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{m}(\Omega)}^{1/2}\left\||\nabla\psi|^{2}\right\|_{L^{\frac{m}{m-1}}(\Omega)}^{1/2}\leqslant C\left\|\nabla\psi\right\|_{L^{\frac{2m}{m-1}}(\Omega)}.

From the continuity equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{i}+\mathrm{div}\,(j^{\varepsilon}_{i})=0,

we see that for any
\psi\in W^{1,\frac{2m}{m-1}}(\Omega)
and
0\leqslant s\leqslant t
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\left(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}(x,t)-\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}(x,s)\right)\psi(x)\,dx\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}j_{i}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|j_{i}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}}\,dx\,dt\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dx\,dt\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|\nabla\psi\|_{L^{\frac{2m}{m-1}}(\Omega)}\sqrt{t-s},

which implies the statement (v). Here, (
6.2
) is used in the last inequality.

∎

.

Proof.

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}(\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon})^{T}A\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}\operatorname{div}(\xi)\,dx-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}(\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon})^{T}A(\nabla\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi)\,dx=\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\mathrm{div}\,{\xi}\,dx-\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon})^{T}\nabla\overrightarrow{\psi}^{\varepsilon}:\nabla\xi\,dx

Proof of Theorem

\overrightarrow{\rho}^{n}\in\mathcal{K}
by

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\tau,0}=\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\varepsilon}_{in},\quad\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\tau,n}\in\operatorname{argmin}\left\{\frac{1}{2\tau}W_{2}^{2}(\overrightarrow{\rho},\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\tau,n-1})+\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho})\,:\,\overrightarrow{\rho}\in\mathcal{K}\right\}\quad\text{for }n\geqslant 0.

The velocity field is then built from
T_{i}
, the unique optimal transport map from
\rho_{i}^{n}
to
\rho_{i}^{n-1}
, as follows:

\displaystyle v_{i}^{\tau,n}(x):=\frac{x-T_{i}(x)}{\tau}.

We can define the pressure
p_{i}^{\tau,n}
satisfying
p^{\tau,n}\in\partial f(\rho^{\tau,n})
where
\rho^{\tau,n}=\rho^{\tau,n}_{1}+\cdots+\rho^{\tau,n}_{N}
and

\displaystyle\rho_{i}^{\tau,n}v_{i}^{\tau,n}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{ij}\rho_{i}^{\tau,n}\nabla\phi_{j}^{\tau,n}-\rho_{i}^{\tau,n}\nabla p^{\tau,n}\right),

where
\phi_{j}^{\tau,n}
is the solution to (
1.3
) with
\rho_{j}^{\tau,n}
in place of
\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}
. We refer to
[
16
, Section 2]
for the pressure.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 0}
, define the piecewise constant functions by

\begin{cases}\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\tau}(t)&:=\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\tau,n}\quad\text{for }t\in[n\tau,(n+1)\tau),\\
\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\tau}(t)&:=\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\tau,n}\quad\text{for }t\in[n\tau,(n+1)\tau),\\
\,p^{\tau}(t)&:=p^{\tau,n}\quad\,\text{ for }t\in[n\tau,(n+1)\tau),\text{ and}\\
\,v_{i}^{\tau}(t)&:=v^{\tau,n}\quad\,\text{ for }t\in[n\tau,(n+1)\tau),\,i=1,\cdots,N.\end{cases}

We have the following convergence theorem.

Theorem A.2
.

Given
T>0
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and initial data
\overrightarrow{\rho}_{in}
with
\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\overrightarrow{\rho}_{in})<+\infty
, there is a sequence
\tau\to 0
such that the following hold.

(i)

The vector
\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\tau}
converges to
\overrightarrow{\rho}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{K})\cap C^{1/2}(0,T;W_{0}^{-1,\frac{2m}{m-1}}(\Omega)^{N})
locally uniformly in
t>0
with respect to the Wasserstein distance.

(ii)

The vector
\overrightarrow{\phi}^{\tau}
converges to
\overrightarrow{\phi}
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,2}(\Omega)^{N})
.

(iii)

The flux
j_{i}^{\tau}:=\rho_{i}^{\tau}v_{i}^{\tau}
converges to
j_{i}
weakly
∗
in
L^{\frac{2m}{m-1}}(\Omega\times(0,T))
for each
i=1,\cdots,N
. Moreover, for each
i=1,\cdots,N
, there exists
v_{i}\in L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T);d\rho_{i})
such that
j_{i}=\rho_{i}v_{i}
.

(iv)

The field
\rho_{i}^{\tau}\nabla p^{\tau}
converges to
\rho_{i}\overrightarrow{\varphi}
weakly
∗
in
L^{\frac{2m}{m-1}}(\Omega\times(0,T))
for each
i=1,\cdots,N
.

(v)

Finally, the limits
\overrightarrow{\rho},\,\overrightarrow{\phi},\,\overrightarrow{\varphi},\,v_{i}
solve the equations (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) upto time
T>0
in the sense of Theorem
A.1
(i)-(iv). Also, the dissipation as stated in Theorem
A.1
(v) holds.

Appendix C
Some properties of BV functions

We recall the following classical result (see for instance
[
2
,
17
]
):

Proposition C.1
.

Let
f_{k}
be a sequence of functions such that
f_{k}\to f
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
when
k\to\infty
.
Then

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)|\nabla f_{k}|\geqslant\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)|\nabla f|

for all
\zeta\in C(\Omega)
with
\zeta\geqslant 0
.
Furthermore, if

|\nabla f_{k}|(\Omega)\to|\nabla f|(\Omega)
,
then

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)|\nabla f_{k}|=\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)|\nabla f|\qquad\mbox{ for all $\zeta\in C(\Omega)$}.

A similar result (with a similar proof) holds for the supremum of measures:

Proposition C.2
.

Let
f_{k}^{1}
and
f_{k}^{2}
be two sequences of functions such that
f_{k}^{i}\to f^{i}
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
as
k\to\infty
for
i=1,2
. Let
\mu_{k}=|\nabla f_{k}^{1}|\vee|\nabla f_{k}^{2}|
and
\mu=|\nabla f^{1}|\vee|\nabla f^{2}|
be the corresponding supremum measures. Then

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)\,d\mu_{k}\geqslant\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)\,d\mu\qquad\text{for all $\zeta\in C(\Omega)$ with $\zeta\geqslant 0$. }

Furthermore, if
\mu_{k}(\Omega)\to\mu(\Omega)
, then

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)\,d\mu_{k}=\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)\,d\mu\qquad\textit{for all }\zeta\in C(\Omega).
