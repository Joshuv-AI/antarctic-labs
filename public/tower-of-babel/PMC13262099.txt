From childhood to adult trauma and negative birth experiences: distal and proximal factors associated with childbirth-related posttraumatic stress symptoms
Authors: Meike K Blecker, Esther Ivanka Grätsch, Elisabeth Conrad, Hannah Klusmann, Sinha Engel, Stephanie Haering, Caroline Meyer, Noemi Luecking, Christine Knaevelsrud, Sarah Schumacher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Childbirth-related posttraumatic stress symptoms (CB-PTSS) have been recognized as a serious postpartum complication. Although childhood maltreatment (CM) is a well-established vulnerability factor for stress-related mental disorders, including posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), its role in CB-PTSS remains largely unexplored.Objective:This study aimed to investigate the association between CM and CB-PTSS at 12 months postpartum, while also considering other major risk factors for CB-PTSS, such as adult trauma and subjective birth experiences (SBEs). In exploratory analyses, we tested whether CM differentially predicted childbirth-related PTSD symptom clusters (intrusion, avoidance, hyperarousal).Methods:N = 111 individuals provided retrospective information on CM and SBEs, as well as information on current CB-PTSS via online questionnaires at 12 months postpartum. Adult trauma was assessed as part of a clinical interview with a trauma checklist. Hierarchical regression models examined whether adult trauma, SBEs, and CM predicted CB-PTSS.Results:Both adult trauma (p = .018) and SBEs predicted higher levels of CB-PTSS (p < .001). Beyond those variables, CM was a significant predictor of CB-PTSS (p = .016). Specifically, CM significantly predicted higher levels of childbirth-related intrusion (p < .01), but not avoidance (p = .576) or hyperarousal symptoms (p = .078).Conclusion:CM may represent a key antepartum vulnerability factor for CB-PTSS, underscoring the far-reaching impact of CM on peripartum mental health. At the same time, more proximal experiences, such as negative SBEs, may critically impact the risk for adverse postpartum outcomes. Further research is needed to elucidate the complex interplay between CM, SBEs, and CB-PTSS, while interdisciplinary, trauma-informed strategies are crucial to prevent and treat CB-PTSS.KEYWORDS: Childhood maltreatment, childbirth-related posttraumatic stress, traumatic birth, subjective birth experiences, CB-PTSD

Introduction

Following a traumatic event, women develop posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) more often than men (Haering, Schulze, et al., 2024; Olff, 2017; Olff et al., 2007). This is because women are more affected by risk factors for PTSD, including exposure to high-impact events such as sexual abuse (Benjet et al., 2016; Kessler et al., 2017), as well as more intense immediate stress reactions (Christiansen & Hansen, 2015; Haering, Meyer, et al., 2024). Notably, sex-specific risk factors for PTSD, i.e. those inherently associated with one specific sex, remain largely understudied (Haering, Meyer, et al., 2024). For example, traumatic childbirth and resulting childbirth-related PTSD (CB-PTSD) have long been neglected, despite the finding that about 25% of birth experiences can be considered traumatic (Blecker et al., 2025a; Weigl et al., 2021). As a result, about 5% of postpartum individuals meet the diagnostic criteria for PTSD (Heyne et al., 2022), and even more (approx. 12%) develop some childbirth-related posttraumatic stress symptoms (CB-PTSS; Heyne et al., 2022). CB-PTSS can have various adverse consequences for both the affected individuals and their families, including difficulties in the socio-emotional child development (Garthus-Niegel et al., 2017), mother-infant bonding (Devita et al., 2025), decreased relationship satisfaction (Garthus-Niegel et al., 2018), and avoidance of future pregnancies (Gottvall & Waldenström, 2002).

To prevent these potential sequelae of CB-PTSS, developing targeted prevention strategies is essential. This requires a comprehensive understanding of underlying aetiological factors. Ayers et al. (2016) summarized early research into a meta-analytically derived diathesis-stress model for CB-PTSS which has been confirmed and expanded by more recent work (see, for example, Dekel et al., 2019; Kress et al., 2021). This framework distinguishes between characteristics present before or during pregnancy (vulnerability factors), birth-related characteristics (risk factors), and postpartum factors that may further increase risk. Risk factors include subjective and objective birth characteristics, such as dissociation and operative birth, while (mental) health complications during pregnancy, such as depression, and a history of PTSD were identified as important vulnerability factors. Additional risk factors, expanding Ayers et al.’s (2016) model, include primiparity (Simpson et al., 2018) and unplanned pregnancies (Andersen et al., 2012). Most consistently, negative subjective birth experiences (SBEs) have been confirmed a risk factor for CB-PTSS (e.g. Garthus-Niegel et al., 2013; Handelzalts et al., 2025; König et al., 2016; Kress et al., 2021), underscoring the importance of the subjective appraisal of birth experiences (Jagodnik et al., 2024). Notably, many of the identified factors are pregnancy- and birth-related, indicating that factors with close temporal proximity to symptom development may be particularly influential in the aetiology of CB-PTSS. While proximal risk factors may inform immediate intervention during the peripartum, characterizing more distal factors, i.e. vulnerabilities preceding pregnancy, may help determine risk groups for whom targeted, early prevention is necessary. Thus, differentiating predictors by their temporal proximity to CB-PTSS onset (proximal vs. distal) may contribute to a more refined aetiological understanding. To date, however, distal factors have received less attention in CB-PTSS research. In line with findings outside of the realm of childbirth (Liu et al., 2017), some evidence indicates that prior exposure to trauma – as a distal factor – increases risk for CB-PTSS (Ayers et al., 2016). Preliminary evidence further suggests a cumulative, building block effect, whereby a greater number of traumatic events (trauma load) predicts greater risk for CB-PTSS (Steudte-Schmiedgen et al., 2023). However, most previous studies have focused on adult trauma.

Interestingly, the effect of early trauma, such as childhood maltreatment (CM), on CB-PTSS remains largely understudied, despite extensive evidence that CM, disrupting long-term stress regulation, is a major transdiagnostic risk factor across the lifespan (McLaughlin et al., 2020). CM includes all forms of emotional, physical, and sexual abuse and emotional and physical neglect of children and youth that result in actual or potential harm to their survival, health, development, or dignity (WHO, 2019). Epidemiological and meta-analytic data suggest that CM affects up to one-third of children globally (Stoltenborgh et al., 2015) and in Germany (Witt et al., 2017). CM has been associated with an increased risk of stress-related mental disorders, including affective, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive, substance use, and posttraumatic stress disorders, as well as comorbidities (McLaughlin et al., 2010; Teicher et al., 2022). Moreover, individuals with a history of CM frequently experience greater psychosocial burden, such as lower educational attainment, economic disparities, and intimate partner violence (Gilbert et al., 2009; Li et al., 2019), that may continue to exert negative effects during the peripartum period (Hitzler et al., 2022a). This may contribute to the increased vulnerability of CM survivors to more chronic courses and a higher risk of recurrence of mental disorders and less favourable outcomes following psychotherapeutic treatment (Nanni et al., 2012), with evidence suggesting that this effect may also be relevant during and after pregnancy (Ammerman et al., 2016; Grote et al., 2012). The peripartum period represents a time of profound physiological and psychosocial changes (Mulder et al., 2002; Szpunar & Parry, 2017). It is therefore a time of increased susceptibility for CM-related sequelae (Finy & Christian, 2018), such as perinatal depression and anxiety (Choi & Sikkema, 2016; Racine et al., 2020). Initial evidence suggests survivors of childhood sexual abuse experience more trauma during the peripartum and show elevated levels of general PTSD (Mackle et al., 2023; Wosu et al., 2015). At the same time, a range of pregnancy- and birth-related factors are particularly distressing for individuals with CM (Christie et al., 2017). A review of qualitative studies on peripartum experiences among CM survivors suggests that feeling passively exposed to birth rather than being an active participant, being disregarded by care providers, and a general lack of control makes birth not only highly threatening, but can also reactivate CM-related memories (Chamberlain et al., 2019). Additionally, in a recent study, CM was associated with dissociation during childbirth, which in turn was linked to PTSD symptoms postpartum (Brenner et al., 2024). Together, among CM survivors, these factors may increase the likelihood of a traumatic birth experience and may impair emotional processing of the birth experience, thereby elevating the risk for CB-PTSS. However, research exploring the specific effects of CM on childbirth-related-PTSS is scarce, with only one study to date explicitly examining this link (Steudte-Schmiedgen et al., 2023). This study found no significant association between CM and CB-PTSS at eight weeks postpartum, potentially due to the low prevalence of both CM and CB-PTSS in this sample.

In sum, while it is plausible that individuals with a history of CM are at increased risk for CB-PTSS, there is still a lack of studies exploring this link. Early identification of highly vulnerable individuals is key to reducing CB-PTSS. Therefore, CM, as a distal, antepartum vulnerability factor, may serve as an ideal target for early preventive interventions during pregnancy. Notably, earlier work on CM and both general and CB-PTSD has mostly considered the early postpartum period, potentially underestimating the impact of CM due to the influence of more proximal and transient risk factors such as rapid hormonal changes postpartum. Moreover, given the increased psychosocial stress in individuals with CM, they might be at particular risk for persistent CB-PTSS trajectories. Thus, a later assessment of CB-PTSS seems essential to fully capture the potential enduring impact of CM on postpartum mental health. Moreover, low levels of CB-PTSS in earlier studies might have obscured relevant risk factors, underscoring the need to focus on high-risk samples. Additionally, no study has yet considered CM as a distal factor alongside SBE, the strongest proximal risk factor for CB-PTSS. To develop targeted intervention strategies, considering both distal (vulnerability) and proximal (risk) factors seems essential. Lastly, most studies rely on total CB-PTSS scores, thereby limiting insight into potentially distinct risk factors associated with each symptom cluster.

To address these gaps, this study aimed to investigate CM as a unique, distal factor for CB-PTSS at 12 months postpartum, accounting for adult trauma load and subjective birth experiences in a sample with unintended pregnancies. To further deepen aetiological insight, we examined both overall CB-PTSS and the different symptom clusters: childbirth-related intrusion, avoidance, and hyperarousal symptoms. First, we expected individuals with CM to report higher levels of CB-PTSS (H1). Second, we aimed at replicating the predictive role of adult trauma load (H2.1) and negative subjective birth experiences for CB-PTSS (H2.2), while controlling for common risk factors. Third, we hypothesized that CM predicts CB-PTSS beyond common risk factors, adult trauma load, and subjective birth experiences for CB-PTSS (H2.3). Lastly, we explored whether CM also predicts elevated childbirth-related intrusion, avoidance, and hyperarousal symptoms (H3) beyond adult trauma load, SBEs, and common risk factors.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study used data from a sub-study of the multisite project ‘ELSA – Experiences and Living Conditions of Women with an Unintended Pregnancy – Counseling and Care Services’. For the sub-study ‘ELSA@FUBerlin’, individuals1 with an unintended pregnancy that was either terminated with an elective abortion or carried to term within the past 12 months were eligible. To detect effects of CM, we targeted 50% CM exposure in the sample (for more details about the study design, see preregistration: https://osf.io/vcs7d/overview). Participants were recruited between September 2021 and February 2023, primarily through paid advertisements on social media platforms. Other recruitment pathways included an opt-in through the larger, representative ELSA study and collaborations with stakeholders such as healthcare providers.

This study focuses on the N = 111 participants who carried their pregnancy to term (see Supplement, Figure S1.1). Following an online screening questionnaire, eligibility was verified in a phone call, and eligible individuals were scheduled for a 12-month postpartum assessment. This assessment comprised a semi-structured clinical interview and online questionnaires. The clinical interview was conducted via telephone by trained research assistants. Participants provided written consent at enrolment and received 50€ for participating.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) age < 18 years, (2) unintended pregnancy resulting from sexual assault, (3) a subsequent pregnancy following the index unintended pregnancy, and (4) chronic or long-term health conditions (e.g. systemic chronic diseases) or medications (e.g. glucocorticoids) known to affect the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis regulation, as another part of the study focused on HPA axis functioning. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Hochschule Fulda (Nr.:3.1.9.2).

Measurements

Sociodemographic, birth- and postpartum-related characteristics

Sociodemographic characteristics were assessed either in the screening survey (e.g. education, citizenship, parity) or in the 12-month postpartum online questionnaire (e.g. age). Education was defined as the highest completed professional qualification (high school diploma, vocational training, academic degree or similar). Furthermore, birth- and postpartum-related characteristics (e.g. mode of birth, breastfeeding status) were assessed in the 12-months postpartum online questionnaire.

Psychological measures

Childbirth-related posttraumatic stress symptoms were assessed with the German version of Impact of Events Scale Revised (IES-R; Maercker & Schützwohl, 1998; Weiss & Marmar, 1997). The IES-R measures three PTSD symptom clusters (intrusion, avoidance, hyperarousal), based on the ICD-10 criteria. To allow for a specific measure of childbirth-related PTSS, we adapted all items to refer to childbirth as the traumatic event. Participants completed the IES-R for the index birth, i.e. the most recent birth. The IES-R comprises 22 items, with a scale ranging from not at all (0) to often (5). We used the sum score as a measure for overall CB-PTSS (range: 0–110) as well as the sum scores of each subscale (ranges: intrusion: 0–35, avoidance: 0–40, hyperarousal: 0–35). In our sample, internal consistency was good (Cronbach’s alpha: α = 0.86).

Childhood maltreatment was measured with the German version of the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ; Bernstein et al., 2003; Wingenfeld et al., 2010). The CTQ assesses five dimensions of childhood maltreatment: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect, each measured by five items. Participants rated their experiences on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never true) to 5 (very often true). For each subscale, cut-off scores can be applied to reflect CM severity on four levels: none, minimal, moderate, or severe CM exposure (Häuser et al., 2011). Based on Witt et al.’s approach (2017), participants who scored above the cut-off for moderate exposure on at least one subscale were categorized as having experienced moderate-to-severe CM; everyone else was categorized as having experienced none-to-minimal CM (cut-offs for moderate exposure: > 12 for emotional abuse, > 9 for physical abuse, > 7 for sexual abuse, > 14 for emotional neglect, and > 9 for physical neglect; see: Häuser et al., 2011). Internal consistency for the CTQ subscales was acceptable to excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.72–0.95).

Trauma exposure during adulthood was assessed in the clinical interview with a modified version of the Munich Composite International Diagnostic Interview trauma checklist (M-CIDI/DIA-X; Hoyer et al., 2020). To acknowledge events during labor, birth or shortly after birth as a potentially traumatic event, i.e. childbirth-related trauma, we added two items to the trauma checklist, based on the wording of the DSM-5 trauma criterion: one to reflect experiences of serious injury or (threatened) death of the birthing individual, and a second to reflect experiences of serious injury or (threatened) death of the child. In line with the DSM-5 criterion, childbirth-related trauma could refer to directly experiencing a traumatic birth, as well as learning about or witnessing a traumatic birth experienced by others. We labelled the birth relevant to the current study (to which the CB-PTSS refer) as the index birth. For all events, participants indicated which trauma categories they had experienced and at what age. To obtain a measure for the number of traumatic experiences during adulthood, i.e. trauma load, we calculated the total number of all trauma categories, including childbirth-related trauma, experienced after the age of 18.

Subjective birth (SBEs) was measured via a single item question (‘How was your overall birth experience?’) which has repeatedly been shown to have a high predictive value for CB-PTSS (Garthus-Niegel et al., 2013; Kress et al., 2021). The question was rated on a numeric scale, ranging from 0 (very negative) to 10 (very positive). Hence, higher values represent a more positive birth experience.

Past major depressive episodes (MDEs), with onset during or prior to pregnancy, were assessed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 – Clinician Version (SCID-CV; Beesdo-Baum et al., 2019; First et al., 2016). If participants reported suicidality, a licenced psychotherapist conducted a follow-up assessment to evaluate suicide risk. When indicated, appropriate support and referrals to psychiatric services were provided.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed with R Studio (version: 4.4.1). For the CTQ, one item was missing for two participants. We imputed these missing values through predictive mean matching (Hmisc; Harrell, 2025). For n = 10 (9.01%) participants, no data was available regarding the SBEs item. We replaced it using a random forest imputation algorithm (MissForest; Stekhoven & Bühlmann, 2012).

To test for differences between participants with moderate-severe vs. none-minimal CM for all study variables, including CB-PTSS (H1), we used Fisher’s test for categorical and Welch t-tests for continuous variables.

For all following analyses, categorical variables were dichotomized and dummy-coded. Before the main analyses, we calculated Spearman Rank correlations between the main study variables. Then, to determine the predictive effect of SBEs, trauma load and CM on CB-PTSS, we conducted hierarchical multiple regression models. We included age, education, past MDEs, mode of birth, primiparity, and any childbirth-related trauma directly experienced by the participants in the past (i.e. prior to the index birth) as common risk factors (Andersen et al., 2012; Ayers et al., 2016). These variables were entered in the first step of the hierarchical regression model. Trauma load was added in the second step (H2.1), followed by SBEs in the third step (H2.2), and CM in the fourth step (H2.3), which included all previously entered variables. The same steps were performed for childbirth-related intrusion, avoidance, and hyperarousal symptoms as separate outcome variables. Focusing on H3, we report the last step of the regression (for steps 1 –3, see Supplement 3). Due to violations of regression assumptions, i.e. lack of normality and homoscedasticity of residuals, we applied bootstrapping resampling procedures with 2,000 iterations. Bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were used to determine the significance of the predictors. The significance level was set at p ≤ .05. The R code used for the analyses is openly available at https://osf.io/mb9qa/overview.

Results

Sample characteristics

Of our N = 111 participants, n = 62 (55.9%) reported none-minimal CM, and n = 49 (44.1%) reported moderate-severe CM. The most frequent trauma types in our sample were accidental/violent death or suicide (n = 26, 23.4%), followed by sexual assault (n = 30, 27%) and childbirth-related trauma (n = 34, 30.6%). 14 (12.6%) participants reported that the index birth was traumatic. Participants with moderate-severe CM reported significantly lower levels of education, more frequently at least one past MDE, and, in adulthood, a higher trauma load, as well as more frequent experiences of sexual assault and accidental/violent death or suicide (see Table 1 for details).

Correlational analysis

Spearman correlations showed small, significant correlations of trauma load with higher overall childbirth-related posttraumatic stress, intrusion, and hyperarousal (rs’s = .19–24, all p’s < .05), but not avoidance symptoms. Negative subjective birth experiences were significantly associated with overall CB-PTSS, avoidance, and hyperarousal (rs’s = −.29 to −64, p’s < .001 to < .01), but not intrusion symptoms. Furthermore, CM was significantly associated with overall childbirth-related posttraumatic stress, intrusion, and hyperarousal (rs’s = .24–28, p’s < .01 to < .05), but not avoidance symptoms. For details, see Supplement 2.

Main analysis

Predictive association between childhood maltreatment and overall childbirth-related posttraumatic stress symptoms

In Step 1 of the hierarchical regression model, having a low level of education (β = −0.21, p = .04) and being primiparous (β = 0.28, p = .034) were significant predictors of high levels of CB-PTSS. In Step 2 (H2.1), a high trauma load (β = 0.27, p = .018), and in Step 3 (H2.2), negative SBEs (β = −0.46, p < .001), each significantly predicted CB-PTSS, explaining an additional 5.4% and 15.1% of the variance, respectively. As hypothesized (H2.3), step 4 showed that – while controlling for common risk factors, trauma load, and SBEs – CM significantly predicted elevated CB-PTSS, β = 0.23, p = .016. Adding CM to the model significantly increased the explained variance (ΔR²adj = .036), with the final model accounting for 32.2% of the variance in CB-PTSS (see Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to advance our aetiological understanding of CB-PTSS by investigating CM experiences alongside established risk factors, including adult trauma load and SBEs. To our knowledge, it is among the first to examine CM in the context of CB-PTSS, highlighting its relevance during the peripartum period (for a review, see: Christie et al., 2017). Individuals with moderate-severe CM reported significantly higher overall CB-PTSS at 12 months postpartum. Although CM was a significant predictor beyond trauma load and SBEs, SBEs remained a strong predictor for CB-PTSS, emphasizing the need for an improved understanding of how distal and proximal factors may interact. Exploratory analyses revealed that CM uniquely predicted childbirth-related intrusion, but not avoidance and hyperarousal symptoms.

As hypothesized, exposure to CM was associated with elevated CB-PTSS at 12 months postpartum, demonstrating for the first time the negative effect of CM on childbirth-related PTSS. This finding broadens the range of peripartum mental conditions for which CM survivors may be particularly vulnerable (Choi & Sikkema, 2016; Souch et al., 2022). Beyond distressing peripartum experiences, the transition to parenthood may represent a critical vulnerability period for CM survivors: CM is associated with negative views of parenting, ambivalence about oneself as a parent, and more negative perceptions of child behaviour (Christie et al., 2017), which may intensify the impact of negative birth experiences. Furthermore, reduced postpartum social support among CM survivors (Hitzler et al., 2022b) may limit opportunities for external buffering of postpartum demands. Overall, this could significantly hinder emotional adjustment after birth, raising stress levels and thus increasing the risk for CB-PTSS. Notably, contrary to preliminary evidence suggesting that CB-PTSS may decrease after about 6 months postpartum, CB-PTSS was more pronounced in our sample at 12 months postpartum, particularly among CM survivors, compared to CB-PTSS in other samples measured at earlier postpartum timepoints (Steudte-Schmiedgen et al., 2023). While longitudinal research on trajectories of CB-PTSS and differential predictors remains scarce, our results tentatively suggest that CM survivors may be particularly vulnerable to persistent symptom courses, emphasizing the importance of early detection of such at-risk groups. Focusing on PTSS with childbirth as the index trauma is critical because it differs fundamentally from other traumatic events (Horesh et al., 2021). Because it is usually viewed as a positive life event, a traumatic childbirth constitutes a profound violation of expectations, which may impede the processing and integration of the birth experience. Moreover, it is a predictable yet partly uncontrollable event that usually involves intense physical sensations and intimate contact with obstetric staff. This makes childbirth not only a potential source of subsequent traumatization for survivors of CM but also offers a unique opportunity for prevention. For instance, trauma-informed obstetric care could contribute to identifying individuals with a history of trauma (through early screening), and shared decision-making during pregnancy and childbirth may strengthen birthing individuals’ sense of control (Benton et al., 2024). Integrating such approaches into perinatal health care may not only reduce the risk for CB-PTSS, but also address other obstetric disparities that trauma survivors face (Owens et al., 2022).

Supporting our hypotheses, adult trauma load, SBEs, and CM significantly predicted CB-PTSS beyond common risk factors. When all predictors were included simultaneously, however, adult trauma load was no longer significant, whereas SBEs emerged as the strongest predictor, followed by CM. These findings indicate that both distal (childhood) and proximal (peripartum) experiences contribute to CB-PTSS, consistent with the diathesis-stress model by Ayers et al. (2016). Importantly, our results highlight childhood (vs. adulthood) trauma as a central, yet underexplored vulnerability factor. Extensive evidence shows that early adversity sensitizes the stress response systems (e.g. McLaughlin et al., 2010), leading to less effective coping with subsequent stressors and therefore, greater risk for stress-related psychopathology, including PTSD, and, potentially, CB-PTSS. It should also be noted that prior PTSD symptoms, which we did not measure, may also explain the effect of trauma history. For example, Grekin et al. (2021) found an indirect effect of trauma history through prenatal PTSD symptoms on CB-PTSS. Furthermore, while in our sample, trauma during adulthood was less influential than CM, specific trauma types (e.g. interpersonal) may interact with both CM and birth experiences to predict elevated CB-PTSS (Liu et al., 2017). Future CB-PTSS research would therefore benefit from a comprehensive assessment of trauma history and preexisting PTSD symptoms that may escalate into a clinical diagnosis after childbirth. This may contribute to identifying pathways for secondary prevention, for example, trauma-focused therapy for CM survivors with PTSD during pregnancy (Becker-Sadzio et al., 2020).

Our finding that SBEs were the strongest predictor for CB-PTSS confirms the critical role of proximal experiences, compared to CM, for peripartum mental health (Bergunde et al., 2024; Fairbrother et al., 2025). Importantly, negative SBEs have also been linked to postpartum depression and impaired bonding (Coo et al., 2023; Seefeld et al., 2023), underscoring their importance for postpartum (family) mental health. Building on previous research in community samples (e.g. Kress et al., 2021), our study demonstrates that SBEs are also a key predictor in a sample with unintended pregnancies – a particularly vulnerable group for adverse postpartum mental health (Abajobir et al., 2016). Although CM survivors in our sample did not report more negative SBEs, prior research suggests that negative experiences during birth, such as the lack of support during birth, may be especially detrimental for trauma-exposed individuals (Ford & Ayers, 2011). Negative SBEs may also include interpersonal difficulties with care providers, such as insufficient communication and lack of informed consent (Chamberlain et al., 2019; Harris & Ayers, 2012), which may be particularly distressing for individuals with CM. Clinically, continuous support during labor and birth has been shown not only to improve SBEs but also to positively affect obstetric outcomes (Bohren et al., 2017). Overall, considering the scarcity of evidence-based interventions specifically targeting perinatal populations with a history of trauma (Jones et al., 2023), elucidating mechanisms linking CM to potential negative SBEs and to CB-PTSS is a crucial next step for developing effective prevention strategies. For example, difficulties in emotion regulation (Klusmann et al., 2025; Vogel et al., 2025), altered stress responses (Blecker et al., 2025b; Steudte-Schmiedgen et al., 2023), and low social support (Shiffman et al., 2023) have been documented as important pathways of CM in the postpartum period.

Our exploratory analysis revealed a distinct pattern of predictors for CB-PTSS clusters: CM significantly predicted childbirth-related intrusion, but not avoidance or hyperarousal symptoms, while SBEs did not predict intrusion but rather avoidance and hyperarousal symptoms. Most previous CB-PTSS studies relied on total symptom severity and did not differentiate between PTSD symptom clusters, making our study the first to highlight CM as a predictor for childbirth-related intrusions. Intrusive memories are considered the hallmark symptom of PTSD; therefore, identifying intrusion-specific predictors appears particularly important for aetiological models of CB-PTSS. Notably, a recent study found higher levels of re-experiencing symptoms in individuals with childbirth as the index trauma compared to other traumatic events (Harrison et al., 2021), suggesting that symptoms of intrusion may indeed be highly relevant to CB-PTSS. Given that childbirth involves intense sensory experiences, it may give rise to heightened intrusive memories, particularly among individuals with CM histories. For instance, increased stress sensitivity in individuals with a history of CM (e.g. McLaughlin et al., 2010) may enhance emotional and physiological reactivity to postpartum trauma-related cues, such as bodily sensations during breastfeeding, thereby potentially eliciting intrusive symptoms. While explanations for why SBEs, but not CM, predicted higher childbirth-related avoidance and hyperarousal symptoms remain speculative, several aspects warrant consideration. First, in line with cognitive models of PTSD (Ehlers & Clark, 2000), a strongly negative subjective appraisal of childbirth may promote avoidance of reminders, explaining the significant association between SBEs and avoidance symptoms in our sample. Second, hyperarousal symptoms may be less clearly pathological in postpartum individuals; for example, increased vigilance towards the baby may be adaptive, and arousal may also reflect proximal stressors such as acute child illness. Third, the hyperarousal cluster includes several sleep-related items not adapted to the postpartum context, limiting the ability to capture clinically significant sleep disturbances. Together, these factors may complicate the identification of distal vulnerabilities, such as CM. Future research is needed to replicate these findings and to clarify whether the symptom clusters are indeed associated with distinct risk factors. In addition, examining whether CM survivors indeed show different CB-PTSS profiles (compared to non-maltreated individuals) may help to inform tailored intervention strategies. For example, interventions targeting intrusive memories, such as visuospatial tasks, appear to be a promising, easily implementable approach to reduce CB-PTSD risk (Deforges et al., 2023) and may be particularly important for CM survivors.

Finally, our finding that, overall, one in three participants experienced a childbirth-related trauma in their lifetime – making it the most common traumatic event – underlines the need to recognize childbirth as a potentially traumatic experience. Yet, widely used trauma checklists such as the life event checklist (Weathers et al., 2013) still do not include it. This calls for a more gender-informed perspective in psychotraumatology (Haering, Meyer, et al., 2024), which eventually can inform clinical guidelines and promote trauma-informed obstetric care.

This study has several limitations. First, CB-PTSS was assessed via self-report questionnaires rather than clinical interviews, which may limit diagnostic accuracy. Second, the retrospective assessment of CM and SBEs may introduce recall bias. However, Danese and Widom (2020) showed that subjective rather than objective reports of CM are accurate predictors of adult psychopathology, highlighting the relevance of subjective narratives and experiences. Third, CB-PTSS was assessed regardless of whether the index birth was experienced as a traumatic event. Notably, there is an ongoing debate about how well the current DSM trauma criterion captures childbirth-related trauma (Leinweber et al., 2022), and many studies measure CB-PTSS without assessing it (e.g. Kress et al., 2021). However, to reflect the DSM (or ICD) PTSD diagnosis, future studies may benefit from focusing on samples meeting the trauma criterion. Lastly, recruitment primarily via social media may limit generalizability due to potential selection bias.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important insights into the relationships between CM and CB-PTSS, particularly across its symptom clusters. Furthermore, while several studies in the context of postpartum mental health assessed PTSS symptoms after childbirth broadly (i.e. linked to any event; e.g. Brenner et al., 2024), the present study anchored symptoms to prior childbirth and adapted the IES-R items to reflect CB-PTSS. Furthermore, conducting this research among individuals with unintended pregnancy, this study addresses existing diversity gaps in peripartum research, which most commonly investigates samples ‘with planned or wanted singleton pregnancy’ (Garthus-Niegel et al., 2022, p. 1). At the same time, the exclusive focus on unintended pregnancies limits our ability to determine whether pregnancy intention constitutes an independent risk dimension in the context of CB-PTSS. Future prospective research, including pregnancy intention as a distinct predictor, is therefore needed to clarify whether it contributes to CB-PTSS risk beyond other psychosocial vulnerabilities such as age or income.

Conclusion

CM has well-documented, enduring effects on multiple health outcomes, including stress-related disorders such as PTSD, yet its role remains insufficiently studied in the context of childbirth. Our study is the first to show that CM predicted higher overall childbirth-related PTSS and intrusions at 12 months postpartum, extending previous findings on the heightened vulnerability of CM survivors for adverse peripartum outcomes. We also highlighted negative SBEs as a strong predictor for CB-PTSS in a high-risk group with unintended pregnancies. Understanding the interplay between distal (CM) and proximal (SBEs) risk factors is essential for effective prevention strategies. Interdisciplinary efforts to reduce CB-PTSS are urgently needed, eventually contributing to the long-term mental health of postpartum individuals and mitigating transgenerational effects.
