Ensuring Data Freshness in Multi-Rate Task Chains Scheduling
1 st José Luis Conradi Hoffmann 2 nd Antônio Augusto Fröhlich

Abstract
In safety-critical autonomous systems, data freshness presents a fundamental design challenge. While the Logical Execution Time (LET) paradigm ensures compositional determinism, it often does so at the cost of injected latency, degrading the phase margin of high-frequency control loops. Furthermore, mapping heterogeneous, multi-rate sensor fusion requirements onto rigid task-centric schedules typically implies in resource-inefficient oversampling. This paper proposes a Task-based scheduling framework extended with data freshness constraints. Unlike traditional models, scheduling decisions are driven by the lifespan of data. We introduce task offset based on the data freshness constraint to order data production in a Just-in-Time (JIT) fashion: the completion of the production of data with strictest data freshness constraint is delayed to the instant its consumers will be ready to use it. This allows for flexible task release offsets. We introduce a formal methodology to decompose Data Dependency Graphs into Dominant Paths by tracing the strictest data freshness constraints backward from the actuators. Based on this decomposition, we propose a Consensus Offset Search algorithm that synchronizes shared producers and private predecessors. This approach enforces end-to-end data freshness without the artificial latency of LET buffering. We formally prove that this offset-based alignment preserves the 100% schedulability capacity of Global EDF, ensuring data freshness while eliminating the computational overhead of redundant sampling.



I
Introduction

The paradigm shift towards high-level autonomous driving demands software architectures capable of managing distributed critical applications while guaranteeing rigorous safety constraints. Modern automotive systems rely on distributed multi-core Electronic Control Units (ECUs) managed by standards such as AUTOSAR to abstract functional logic from hardware topology
[
1
]
. The correctness of these systems depends not only on the logical result of computation but also on the time at which these results are produced
[
6
]
. The foundational
Time-Triggered Architecture (TTA)
by Kopetz established that a global time base is a prerequisite for composability in distributed real-time systems, simplifying error detection and guaranteeing timeliness
[
15
]
. Today, these concepts underpin safety certification under ISO 26262, which relies on the Fault Tolerant Time Interval (FTTI)—the maximum time span between a fault and the system’s safe reaction
[
13
]
. Consequently, the timing behavior of the task chains that process sensor data becomes as critical as the logical correctness of the control algorithms themselves.

To address temporal interference in these architectures, the Logical Execution Time (LET) paradigm has emerged as a dominant design pattern
[
12
,
14
]
. By decoupling communication from execution, LET ensures that task input/output timing is independent of platform speed. Recent advancements have extended this to distributed networks via System-Level LET (SL-LET)
[
8
]
, and proposed algebraic approaches to collapse task chains into zero-jitter logical tasks
[
4
]
. However, achieving this determinism often forces a trade-off. To guarantee that end-to-end latency remains within the FTTI while adhering to fixed LET intervals, designers typically over-dimension resources or accept stale data, effectively trading phase margins for scheduling predictability. For fast dynamic systems, such as a Lane Keeping Assist (LKA), using the freshest data is often more critical for maintaining stability than eliminating jitter; if lateral position data is old due to LET buffering, the steering controller calculates a reaction for a road curvature the vehicle has already passed, potentially leading to oscillation.

To address this trade-off, we propose a fundamental inversion of the design model: rather than permitting the scheduler to execute tasks as soon as possible (ASAP), release times are constrained by the data freshness. We introduce a task offset assignment based on data freshness implied by task dependency. Unlike traditional real-time analysis where tight deadlines dictate high priority, we argue that tasks with large data freshness windows (high latency tolerance) should be assigned earlier offsets. This ensures that high-latency operations are processed in advance, effectively anchoring the timeline. Conversely, tasks with strict data freshness constraints (short data freshness) are assigned later offsets, delaying their execution until the precise moment their data is required.

In this work, we formalize this logic to synchronize heterogeneous sensor-to-actuator task chains. By analyzing the task dependency graph backwards from the actuation sink, we establish release offsets based on data freshness. This pre-fetching of slow data (e.g., Camera) ensures that highly dynamic, tighter data freshness, data (e.g., IMU) is sampled Just-In-Time (JIT). Once the offsets are applied, the runtime scheduler manages execution using standard Earliest Deadline First (EDF). This hybrid approach eliminates the ”stale data” problem caused by uncoordinated early execution without the artificial latency overhead of Logical Execution Time (LET) buffering.

II
Related Works

The scheduling of safety-critical automotive systems has traditionally been approached through the lens of task-centric deadlines. This section reviews the evolution from classical hard real-time scheduling to modern data-centric paradigms, highlighting the shift from task schedulability to end-to-end data freshness in Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs).

II-A

Predictability in Task Chains

The foundation of hard real-time systems lies in guaranteeing that critical tasks meet their deadlines under worst-case conditions. Seminal work by Burns on Fixed Priority Scheduling (FPS) established rigorous Response Time Analysis (RTA) for single-core
[
5
]
and distributed CAN-based systems
[
20
]
. To address composability, Kopetz introduced the Time-Triggered Architecture (TTA)
[
15
]
, advocating for a global time base to decouple communication from processing.

However, modern autonomous systems have evolved into complex, multi-rate Cause-Effect Chains. Becker
et al.

[
2
]
proposed a comprehensive end-to-end timing analysis for such chains, enabling analysis based on
Worst Case-Execution Time (WCET)
estimates even without detailed platform knowledge. While effective for verifying register-based communication, this model faces challenges in modern architectures where hidden latencies introduced by security algorithms, transient network loads, and complex AI data dependencies significantly impact system performance.

Sun
et al.

[
19
]
advanced RTA for DAG tasks with arbitrary release patterns. Li
et al.

[
16
]
reviewed several techniques for end-to-end latency analysis, concluding that current solutions still underpins interplay and merging of multiple chains and that optimized prioritization and period assignment are still an open challenge.

Recent research into Conditional DAGs has addressed the complexity of intra-task priority assignment to minimize response times on multiprocessors
[
11
]
and Response-Time Analysis for conditional branches in tasks
[
17
]
. While these methods optimize for
execution urgency
, they do not inherently account for
data freshness
.

II-B

Logical Execution Time (LET) and Task Chains

To reconcile preemption flexibility with temporal determinism, the Logical Execution Time (LET) paradigm
[
12
]
fixes task input/output timing. This concept, standardized in AUTOSAR
[
1
]
, has been the subject of intense optimization.

Bini
et al.

[
4
]
proposed an algebraic framework to collapse periodic LET chains into single zero-jitter logical tasks. While this eliminates jitter, it enforces rigid structural latencies. Addressing multi-core contention, Wang
et al.

[
21
]
highlights that uncontrolled jitter is a major threat to data integrity, providing a linear-time complexity analysis on jitter at read and write events. Our solution uses a Consensus Search to fix static offsets, effectively damping the jitter propagation analyzed in their work. The data freshness constraint is used to build a schedule where the temporal slack is strategically placed at the start, making the system naturally robust to the propagation effects.

II-C

Task Dependency and Data Freshness

Palencia et al.
[
18
]
established that offsets can be used to mitigate the pessimism of Response Time Analysis (RTA) in transactions, their approach remains task-centric, focusing on processor interference. Our work extends the utility of offsets into the semantic domain, using them to align the data freshness windows of heterogeneous data streams. We demonstrate that by Construction, specifically through the offset assignment based on data freshness, we can ensure data freshness while maintaining the capacity preservation that Palencia’s analysis seeks to verify.

Recent literature has increasingly focused on semantic metrics of data freshness, distinguishing between simple precedence constraints and actual temporal properties like
Data Age
and
Reaction Time
. Gohari
et al.

[
9
]
has provided a rigorous analysis for Worst-Case Data Age in multi-rate DAGs under timing uncertainty. However, their framework remains primarily analytical, quantifying the data freshness degradation inherent in existing schedules.

Günzel et al.
[
10
]
highlights the increasing complexity of automotive timing analysis, where heterogeneous communication mechanisms (e.g., LET, Implicit Communication) introduce varying levels of structural latency into cause-effect chains.

Existing frameworks for DAGs primarily focus on bounding the Worst-Case Data Age (WCDA) under standard scheduling policies where tasks are released As Soon As Possible (ASAP). In these models, data age accumulates passively while fresh data waits in output buffers for consumer activation. While these works provide rigorous
analysis
methods to bound the age of a given schedule, they generally assume the schedule is driven by standard execution priorities (e.g., EDF or Rate Monotonic). Our work differs by shifting the focus from
analysis
to
construction
. Rather than accepting the data freshness degradation inherent in ASAP scheduling, we employ a
Start-Time Optimization
strategy to actively inject delays, ensuring data freshness alignment by design.

While frameworks like Zero-Jitter LET
[
4
]
achieve synchronization by buffering (delaying) the
output
, our method achieves synchronization by delaying the
input
(the sampling instant) of the faster tasks. This allows the system to handle high-latency dependencies (like vision systems) by starting them early, while the critical path (dominant dependency) is scheduled to execute only when the high-latency data is nearing availability, thereby ensuring data freshness at the moment of fusion.

To illustrate the difference, consider a fusion task
\tau_{fus}
that requires data from a high-latency Camera task (
\tau_{cam},C_{cam}=10ms
) and a low-latency IMU task (
\tau_{imu},C_{imu}=1ms
).

•

Standard Approach (Analysis):
Both tasks are released at
t=0
.
\tau_{imu}
finishes at
t=1
, while
\tau_{cam}
finishes at
t=10
. The fusion occurs at
t=10
. Consequently, the IMU data sits in the output buffer for
\Delta t=9ms
, resulting in a data age of
10ms
at the point of fusion.

•

Proposed Approach (Construction):
We apply a
Start Offset
to the dominant task.
\tau_{cam}
starts at
t=0
(due to its large data freshness window/latency). However,
\tau_{imu}
is offset to start at
t=9
. Both tasks finish exactly at
t=10
. The IMU data age at fusion is minimal (
1ms
), eliminating the
9ms
of ”stale” waiting time.

Our approach shifts from analyzing the age of execution flows to constructing flows that minimize waiting times by design.

III-B

Scheduling Constraints and Operational Assumptions

To ensure the tractability of the proposed Start-Time Optimization, we define the following baseline constraints and operational assumptions regarding the platform and data semantics.

III-B
1
Baseline Constraints

These constraints define the rigid boundaries of the problem of scheduling while ensuring data freshness:

•

C1. Unconstrained Sensor Release:

Source tasks (Sensors) are not bound by precedence constraints. They may be scheduled at any offset
\Phi_{i}
within the period
T_{i}
to optimize the alignment with downstream consumers.

•

C2. Data Freshness Constraint:

Let
s_{j,m}
denote the
Start Time
of the
m
-th job of consumer task
\tau_{j}
. Let
r_{i,last}
denote the
Release Time
of the most recent job from predecessor
\tau_{i}
that has completed and is available to
\tau_{j}
before
s_{j,m}
. The data freshness constraint requires:

\forall\tau_{i}\in pred(\tau_{j}):\quad s_{j,m}-r_{i,last}\leq E_{ji}

(6)

If this condition is violated for any input stream (i.e., the data age exceeds the allowed data freshness
E_{ji}
), task
\tau_{j}
cannot execute safely.

•

C3. Sensing-Time Anchoring:

The age of a sample is strictly relative to its
production instant
(sensing or creation time
r_{i,k}
), not its transmission start or reception time. Network delays (
L_{ij}
) consume the data freshness budget
E_{ji}
rather than extending it.

III-B
2
System Assumptions

We adopt the following assumptions to abstract the hardware/software interface:

•

A1. Localized Data Freshness (Non-Transitive Data Age):

We assume the data freshness attribute is reset at each processing stage. The age of a derived sample produced by
\tau_{j}
is relative to
\tau_{j}
’s execution time, not the original sensor’s timestamp.

Rationale:
While the
input
constraint (C2) ensures the chain’s temporal integrity up to that point, the
output
data freshness represents the useful life of the computation result itself (e.g., an object list is fresh for
50ms
after computation, provided the input image was fresh when computed).

•

A2. Atomic Execution-Communication:

We model the Sense/Transform and Communicate phases as atomic non-preemptible units. A task
\tau_{i}
completes its computation
C_{i}
and immediately flushes data to the network. This simplifies the interference model by treating the WCET and Communication Latency as a contiguous block in the worst-case analysis.

•

A3. Deterministic Network Latency:

We assume a directly connected network topology where the transmission time for a message from node
Node_{i}
to
Node_{j}
is constant and deterministic, given by
L_{ij}
. This assumption holds for time-sensitive networks (TSN) or switched Ethernet with static forwarding tables.

•

A4. Actuation-Driven Phasing:

The system timeline is anchored to the actuation requirements. We assume the first actuation dictates the periodicity start (
t=0
).

Implication:
The initial period is treated as a safe ”booting phase” where the system is permitted to pre-load the pipeline. This allows high-latency data (where
E_{ji}>T_{i}
) to be sampled in the negative timeline (pre-boot) or during the first cycle, ensuring that steady-state data freshness constraints are met from the very first effective actuation.

III-C

Data Freshness Constraint

We adopt the following definition of data age for multi-rate chains. For a producer job
\tau_{i,k}
released at
r_{i,k}
and a consumer job
\tau_{j,m}
that begins execution at
s_{j,m}
, the instantaneous age
\Delta_{age}
of the data at the moment of consumption is defined as the time elapsed since the producer’s release:

\Delta_{age}(i,j)=s_{j,m}-r_{i,k}

(7)

The system is considered schedulable only if, for all active dependencies
(\tau_{i},\tau_{j})\in\mathcal{E}
, the age at consumption does not exceed the producer’s freshness threshold:

\max_{\forall k,m}(s_{j,m}-r_{i,k})\leq E_{ji}

(8)

The objective of the proposed Start-Time Optimization is to compute the vector
\mathbf{\Phi}=\{\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{n}\}
such that tasks with tight
E_{ji}
are delayed (increasing
\Phi_{i}
) to execute Just-In-Time (JIT) for their consumers, thereby minimizing the holding time component of
\Delta_{age}
.

IV
Adapting Start Offset to Optimize Data Freshness in Task Chains

Traditional real-time scheduling focuses on task urgency, where the most constrained tasks (shortest deadlines) are prioritized to execute as early as possible (ASAP). However, in sensor-fusion chains, ASAP execution is often the primary driver of data staleness. When a high-frequency sensor with strict data freshness completes early, it must wait for high-latency support tasks (e.g., vision processing) to complete before fusion can occur. During this synchronization gap, the fresh data ages, often exceeding its data freshness bound before it is ever consumed.

To address this, we propose a shift toward
Just-In-Time
(JIT) scheduling. By calculating static release offsets (
\Phi
) based on signal expiry rather than task urgency, we can anchor the execution of the entire chain to the slowest bottleneck and delay the sampling of fast sensors. This ensures that all inputs arrive at the fusion node at the peak of their data freshness.

IV-C

Demand-Driven Period Derivation

In contrast to traditional sensing-driven models, we employ a
Demand-Driven
approach. The temporal attributes of a producer task
\tau_{i}
are not arbitrary; they are derived constraints dictated by the requirements of its consumer set
succ(\tau_{i})
. We define the derivation rules for the producer’s period
T_{i}
and required data freshness
E_{ji}
as follows:

1. Linear Dependency (1:1 Case):

Consider a simple chain where task
\tau_{i}
produces data solely for a single consumer
\tau_{j}
. The producer must adapt its rate to match the consumption rhythm of the consumer.

•

Derived Period:
To guarantee fresh data availability for every execution of the consumer without wasteful oversampling, the producer inherits the consumer’s demands, either producing a new sample at the consumer rate (i.e.,
T_{j}\geq E_{ji}
) or according to the data freshness constraint (i.e., when
T_{j}<E_{ji}
).

T_{i}=\max(T_{j},E_{ji})

(11)

2. Shared Producer / Divergent Flow (1:N Case):

Consider a Producer
\tau_{i}
that publishes data to a set of multiple consumers
succ(\tau_{i})=\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{k}\}
. The producer must adapt its period to properly attend
all
of its consumers simultaneously.

•

Derived Period (Harmonicity):
To maintain synchronization with multiple consumers, the producer must operate at a rate that is harmonically compatible with the entire consumer set:

T_{i}=\text{GCD}\left(\{\max(T_{c},E_{ci})\mid\forall\tau_{c}\in succ(\tau_{i})\}\right)

(12)

Remark 2
.

This backward propagation of constraints ensures that the Actuator (the end of a data path) drives the timing definition. The Actuator’s period
T_{head}
is determined by the physical plant dynamics, and this rhythm propagates upstream to the sensors.

\tau_{k}
in the path is the critical predecessor of the node
\tau_{k-1}
(i.e.,
\tau_{k}=previous(\tau_{k-1})
).

IV-E

Task Prioritization - Case 1: Linear Task Chain

For a linear task chain
\mathcal{P}
executing on a single processor, the explicit modeling of data dependencies can be simplified. We reduce the precedence-constrained scheduling problem to a standard
Earliest Deadline First (EDF)
problem by deriving
Effective Deadlines
(
D^{eff}
) for each task.

In a linear chain, a predecessor
\tau_{i}
must finish early enough to allow all subsequent tasks
\{\tau_{i+1},\dots,\tau_{N}\}
to execute and communicate before the global chain deadline. Therefore, the absolute deadline of a task is not its period
T
, but a stricter constraint derived from the demand of its successors.

Let the task chain consist of
N
tasks, where
\tau_{N}
is the chain head (Actuator) and
\tau_{1}
is the source (Sensor).

1. Base Case (Chain Head):

The actuator must complete by the end of the period.

D^{eff}_{N}=T

(14)

2. Recursive Step (Upstream Propagation):

For any task
\tau_{i}
(
i<N
), the deadline is the effective deadline of its successor
\tau_{i+1}
minus the execution time of the successor and the communication latency between them.

D^{eff}_{i}=D^{eff}_{i+1}-C_{i+1}-L_{i,i+1}

(15)

3. General Closed Form:

Substituting recursively, the effective deadline for any task
\tau_{i}
is the global period reduced by the cumulative worst-case execution and transmission time of the downstream path:

D^{eff}_{i}=T-\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\left(C_{j}+L_{j-1,j}\right)

(16)

By assigning these derived deadlines, we guarantee that:

D^{eff}_{1}<D^{eff}_{2}<\dots<D^{eff}_{N}

(17)

When scheduled under EDF:

•

At the start of the period (
t=0
), all tasks are released.

•

The scheduler selects the task with the smallest deadline, which is always the source
\tau_{1}
.

•

Once
\tau_{1}
completes,
\tau_{2}
becomes the runnable task with the earliest deadline.

•

This naturally enforces the topological order
\tau_{1}\to\tau_{N}
purely through deadline priorities, satisfying the dependency constraints without explicit synchronization primitives.

Remark 3

(Data Freshness Check)
.

If the data freshness constraint
E_{i+1~i}
is tighter than the structural slack, the deadline must be further clamped. The generalized effective deadline is:

D^{eff}_{i}=\min\left(D^{eff}_{i+1}-C_{i+1}-L_{i,i+1},\quad r_{i}+E_{i+1~i}\right)

(18)

However, in valid linear designs, the structural deadline derived above usually satisfies the data freshness requirements of well-formed control loops.

IV-E
1
Schedulability in Global EDF

Standard Global EDF proofs rely on the property that the scheduler is
work-conserving
(a core is never idle if a task is ready). A suspended task is ”open” but not ”ready,” creating an anomaly where the processor idles despite pending workload.

Consequence:
It is proven that self-suspensions induce a ”jitter” effect that is harder to schedule than pure computation. The standard G-EDF schedulability test fails because:

\text{Demand}(t)+\text{SuspensionGap}(t)>\text{Supply}(t)

(19)

The parameter
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
denotes a continuous time interval. For any task
\tau_{i}
, the demand
Demand(\tau_{i},t)
is the sum of the execution times of all jobs of
\tau_{i}
whose arrival times and absolute deadlines lie within
[0,t]
, while
Supply(t)=m\cdot t
.

The total system demand over interval
t
is then:

\sum_{\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{T}}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{t-\Phi_{i}}{T_{i}}\right\rfloor+1\right)\cdot C_{i}+\text{SuspensionGap}(t)

(20)

Where the
SuspensionGap
is the sum of all idle intervals on
m
cores where no critical task can be scheduled due to data freshness-imposed offsets.

To restore the validity of the EDF proof, we must move from ”Independent Task Analysis” to
Transaction-Based Analysis
using the offsets
\Phi_{k}
defined previously. Note that this is only a logical artifact to evaluate schedulability of the system considering worst-case analysis, and the real system will not suffer from these offsets and will idle the CPU instead.

By enforcing the release offset
\Phi_{cons}=C_{prod}+L_{cons,prod}
, we ensure that:

1.

Independence Restoration:
When
\tau_{cons}
is finally released at
t=\Phi_{cons}
, the data is already available.
\tau_{cons}
never waits.
C^{\prime}_{cons}=C_{cons}
.

2.

Validity of EDF:
The demand of
\tau_{cons}
is shifted in time. It contributes to the system load only over the interval
[\Phi_{cons},D_{cons}]
.

The schedulability check must be updated to an
Offset-Aware Test
:

\forall t>0,\quad\sum_{\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{T}}\max\left(0,\left\lfloor\frac{t-\Phi_{i}}{T}\right\rfloor+1\right)\cdot C_{i}\leq m\cdot t

(21)

where
m
is the number of processors. The test must hold for all
t>0
to ensure the system is schedulable under all conditions. In practice,
t
is checked at task arrivals and deadlines up to the Least Common Multiple (LCM) of all task periods. The term
\lfloor\frac{t-\Phi_{i}}{T}\rfloor+1
counts how many instances (jobs) of task
\tau_{i}
have been released within the window
t
, accounting for the delay introduced by the offset
\Phi_{i}
. This formula correctly accounts for the fact that downstream tasks cannot demand CPU time at the beginning of the period.

IV-F

Task Prioritization - Case 2: Handling Non-Dominant Branches

When a consumer task
\tau_{cons}
requires synchronized data from multiple predecessor branches (e.g., sensor fusion), the schedule must ensure that
all
input data remains valid at the specific instant of consumption
f_{cons,k}
(a new sample of
cons
is produced).

This problem is universally solved by identifying the system’s temporal
Anchor
and aligning all other branches relative to it. This approach applies equally to single-core (ordering) and multi-core (offset) scenarios.

The instant of consumption
f_{cons,k}
is determined by the ”Bottleneck Branch”—the predecessor path with the longest physical latency (execution + transmission). The consumer cannot start until this slowest path completes, therefore, we can adjust the start of other branches based on this anchor time.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of all predecessor tasks feeding
\tau_{cons}
. The anchor time relative to the period start is:

f_{cons,k}=\max_{\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{P}}\left(\Phi_{i}+C_{i}+L_{i,cons}\right)+C_{cons}

(22)

where
\Phi_{i}
is the release offset of predecessor
\tau_{i}
.

Once the anchor
f_{cons,k}
is established, we calculate the required timing for every other predecessor
\tau_{j}
to ensure its data does not expire before
f_{cons,k}
, given as the
Latest Safe Start Time (
LST_{j}
)
:

LST_{j}=f_{cons,k}-E_{cons~j}

(23)

The scheduler’s goal is to align the actual start time
s_{j}
as close to
LST_{j}
as possible (
s_{j}\approx LST_{j}
). The gap between the earliest possible start (
0
) and the required start (
LST
) represents the
Temporal Slack
.

How we utilize this slack depends on the hardware resource constraints, but the mathematical target (
LST
) remains constant.

IV-F
1
Case 2.1: Global Scheduling (Multi-Core)

•

Mechanism:

Explicit Offset Injection.

•

Since cores are available in parallel, we simply delay the release of the fast/strict task
\tau_{j}
until the last safe moment to minimize data age and buffer residence.

\Phi_{j}=LST_{j}

(24)

•

Result:
The task sleeps during the slack time, saving system bandwidth or allowing lower-priority background tasks to run. The effective deadline of these task is then set to
D^{eff}_{j}=f_{cons,k}
, the anchor.

IV-F
2
Case 2.2: Serial Scheduling (Single-Core)

•

Mechanism:

Effective Deadline Ordering.

•

To ensure the most fragile data (tight data freshness) is generated last (immediately before consumption) we treat the data freshness constraints as a
Virtual Dependency Chain
.

•

We conceptually place the ”Strict” task
\tau_{strict}
as the immediate predecessor of the Consumer, and the ”Loose” task
\tau_{loose}
as a predecessor of the Strict task (
\tau_{loose}\to\ldots\to\tau_{strict}\to\tau_{cons}
).

•

Deadline Derivation:
Using our recursive effective deadline definition (
D_{i}=D_{next}-C_{next}
), the task with the tighter data freshness (closer to consumption) inherits a
larger effective deadline
than the loose task.

•

Result:
Since
D^{eff}_{loose}<D^{eff}_{strict}
, an EDF scheduler naturally selects the Loose task first. It executes earlier in the frame, allowing the Strict task to execute later (closer to the deadline), thereby minimizing the age of the strict data at the moment of consumption.

Remark 4
.

In both cases, the
Offset-Aware Schedulability Test
validates the system.

•

For Multi-Core,
\Phi_{j}
reduces the interference window, and when combined with the effective deadline set to the start of the next task, allows for ordering even in G-EDF.

•

For Single-Core, the ordering ensures that no task effectively ”blocks” another beyond the allowable data freshness window.

IV-G

We consider a system where a shared producer
\tau_{S}
feeds two or more consumer chains.

•

Path A (Strict):
Requires
\tau_{S}
to push data very late in the frame (high freshness).

•

Path B (Standard):
One or more ”normal” chain that typically executes early.

If we set the offset of the shared task
\Phi_{S}
to satisfy Path A,
\tau_{S}
finishes at time
t_{late}
.
However, Path B (Consumer
\tau_{B}
) is scheduled by default at
t=0
.

\text{Issue: }\Phi_{B}(0)<\Phi_{S}+C_{S}(t_{late})

(25)

\tau_{B}
attempts to execute before
\tau_{S}
has produced the current data. It would read the
previous

Age\approx T+t_{late}
).

The problem can be interpreted as:
”find a Static Global Offset (
\Phi_{shared}
) that satisfies heterogeneous consumer constraints”
. By definition,
T_{shared}=GCD(T_{1},T_{2},...)

j

j+1
satisfies Path B within the same hyperperiod. Going for a ”Per-Job Offset” consists of applying a per-job offset if a match isn’t possible. To keep the proposal EDF proof valid, we must frame this not as ”variable offsets” but as a Multi-Task Decomposition over the hyperperiod. If
\Phi

H/T

To avoid the ”Domino Effect” of shifting offsets while maintaining EDF periodicity, we propose a recursive tightening algorithm. Let
Succ(\tau_{S})

\tau_{S}

\tau_{S}

\Phi_{S}
that satisfies the data freshness of all paths. To maintain EDF periodicity, we avoid job-specific offsets by applying
Static Offset Propagation
.

For each consumer

be the required start time of
C_{i}
. The valid finish time for
\tau_{S}
relative to

f_{S}\in[f_{cons,i}-E_{iS},f_{cons,i}-L_{iS}-C_{i}]

Rollback and Adjustment Strategy

If an instance of
\tau_{S}
fails the data freshness check for
C_{i}
, the offset
\Phi_{S}
must be adjusted. If
f_{S}<f_{cons,i}-E_{iS}

\Phi_{S}

Consistency Preservation (Non-Breakage Analysis)

Let
\tau_{S}
be a shared producer that feeds data to a consumer
\tau_{cons}

\Phi_{S}

C_{cons}

Phase-Shift Invariance
.

If
\Phi_{S}

\tau_{cons}

V
Proof of Schedulability Capacity Preservation

This section presents the proof that our offset based solution preserves EDF Schedulability Capacity, which is done through demand bound function and properly handling the
work-conserving property
when tackling self-suspension of tasks.

V-A

Theorem: Asynchronous Dominance

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n}\}
be a set of periodic tasks with implicit deadlines
D_{i}=T_{i}
. The introduction of static offsets
\Phi_{i}\geq 0
to satisfy data freshness constraints does not reduce the maximum schedulable utilization bound of the system under Global EDF.

V-B

Proof by Demand Bound Function (DBF)

The Demand Bound Function
DBF(\tau_{i},t)
defines the maximum execution requirement of task
\tau_{i}
in any interval of length
t
. For a synchronous task (
\Phi_{i}=0
):

DBF_{sync}(\tau_{i},t)=\max\left(0,\left\lfloor\frac{t-D_{i}}{T_{i}}\right\rfloor+1\right)\cdot C_{i}

(28)

When an offset
\Phi_{i}
is introduced, the first job is delayed, shifting the demand curve to the right:

DBF_{async}(\tau_{i},t)=\max\left(0,\left\lfloor\frac{t-(\Phi_{i}+D_{i})}{T_{i}}\right\rfloor+1\right)\cdot C_{i}

(29)

Step 1: Interval Dominance.

For any
t>0
, since
\Phi_{i}\geq 0
, it follows that:

\left\lfloor\frac{t-(\Phi_{i}+D_{i})}{T_{i}}\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor\frac{t-D_{i}}{T_{i}}\right\rfloor

(30)

Consequently,
DBF_{async}(t)\leq DBF_{sync}(t)
for all intervals. The total demand on the
m
processors is strictly less than or equal to the synchronous case, which is the worst-case scenario for EDF schedulability.

Step 2: Utilization Invariance.

The long-term capacity requirement is governed by utilization
U=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{DBF(t)}{t}
. Since:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{(\lfloor\frac{t-\Phi_{i}-D_{i}}{T_{i}}\rfloor+1)C_{i}}{t}=\frac{C_{i}}{T_{i}}

(31)

The total system utilization
U=\sum\frac{C_{i}}{T_{i}}
remains unchanged.

V-C

Handling the ”Self-Suspension” Artifact

One may argue that the offset
\Phi_{i}
acts as a self-suspension, which is known to degrade EDF bounds. However, in our
Static Offset Propagation
,
\Phi_{i}
is not a dynamic suspension but a
release delay
.

•

Work-Conserving Property:
Under G-EDF, the scheduler remains work-conserving. If core
m_{j}
is idle during
[0,\Phi_{i}]
, it is available to any other ready task.

•

Idle Time vs. Capacity:
The ”Suspension Gap” only impacts capacity if
D_{i}
is shortened. Because we preserve the relative deadline
D^{\prime}_{i}=T_{i}
(by shifting both release and deadline), the task density
\delta_{i}=C_{i}/D_{i}
is invariant.

V-D

Proof Conclusion

Since
DBF_{async}(t)\leq DBF_{sync}(t)
and
\sum\frac{C_{i}}{D_{i}}
remains constant, any task set that is schedulable at
U\leq m
without offsets remains schedulable with our data freshness-aware offsets. The capacity
1.0\times m
is preserved.

VI
Conclusion

The evolution of real-time systems has shifted from ensuring task completion within strict deadlines to guaranteeing the data freshness of data across complex, multi-rate chains. This paper addressed the inherent ”freshness-for-determinism” trade-off found in current automotive standards. While the Logical Execution Time (LET) paradigm provides structural determinism, it does so by injecting artificial latencies through output buffering. Conversely, standard As-Soon-As-Possible (ASAP) scheduling, while computationally efficient, suffers from data aging in multi-rate sensor fusion where fast data waits for high-latency dependencies.

We have proposed Scheduling based on
Data Freshness
, an inversion of the traditional priority logic of real-time systems. By employing offset assignment based on data freshness, our constructive methodology assigns earlier static offsets to high-latency tasks, effectively ”anchoring” the timeline to accommodate slow sensor processing (e.g., vision systems). By delaying the sampling instants (offsets) of data freshness-critical tasks rather than buffering their outputs, we achieve
Just-In-Time (JIT)
execution, which aligns data production with consumption and minimizes data age at the point of fusion. To resolve temporal conflicts in shared task structures, we introduced a
Consensus Search
backtracking algorithm that ensures global data freshness across heterogeneous consumer paths without requiring job-specific scheduling. Furthermore, we formally proved that this offset-based alignment
preserves the 100% capacity of Global EDF
, demonstrating that data freshness can be guaranteed without sacrificing CPU utilization bounds.

Our work differentiates itself from the current state-of-the-art by moving from
analysis
to
construction
. While recent literature
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
17
]
provides rigorous methods to bound the Worst-Case Data Age (WCDA) and Worst-Case Response Time (WCRT) of existing schedules, our framework actively injects offsets to eliminate the waiting time inherent in ASAP execution. Furthermore, unlike LET frameworks
[
4
,
21
]
that achieve predictability by delaying the availability of already-computed data, our method delays the sampling instant. This preserves the phase margin of high-frequency control loops by ensuring that the fusion task consumes the freshest possible data snapshot at the precise moment its slowest dependency becomes available.
