Acid Generation In Lithium Ion Batteries-
Role of electrolyte oxidation and difluorophosphoric acid generation in crossover and
capacity fade in Lithium Ion batteries.

Reprinted with permission from ACS Energy Lett. 2021, 6, 3788–3792. American Chemical Society.

(a) Schematic diagram of the difluorophosphoric acid (F2PO2H) generation on the cathode and
it’s subsequent crossover reactions. (b) 19F NMR spectra of the electrolyte of the cycled cells
showing the increasing concentration of acid with extended cycling

The Science
Cathode materials that can be cycled to a high voltage has long been an interest to lithium ion
battery community because they can increase the energy density of the batteries. But they have
poor cycling performance, especially at high temperatures. Reason for this capacity fade has
frequently been reported to be transition metal ion dissolution from the cathodes and their
subsequent deposition on the anode destroying the anodes’ protective solid electrolyte interphase
(SEI). However, the mechanism for transition metals degrading the anode SEI components is
unclear.. On the other hand, oxidative decomposition of the electrolyte results in the generation
of various acidic species including glycolic acid, formic acid, PF5, HF and OPF3. These species
can be involved in cross over reactions which could facilitate the decomposition of the anode
SEI components leading to capacity fade. This investigation focusses on the generation of the
strong acid difluorophosphoric acid (F2PO2H) and has been quantified for two different cathode
materials. There is an excellent correlation between the concentration F2PO2H, SEI degradation
and the capacity loss of the cells.

The Impact
Understanding contributing factors for capacity fade in high voltage LIB such as acidic species
generation during the cell cycling will enable the Lithium ion battery community to develop
methods to improve the capacity retention in high voltage cathodes. These methods may include
developing electrolyte formulations or metal oxide surface films that can inhibit the oxidation of
LiPF6 to generate F2PO2H leading to improved capacity retention.

Summary
Transition metal ion dissolution was long thought to be the reason for poor cycling performance
in Lithium ion batteries cycled to a high voltage, but since the anode SEI is electrochemically
insulating, the mechanism for transition metal mediated SEI decomposition in unclear. Our
results show that acidic species such as F2PO2H generation on the cathode side and their
subsequent crossover reactions with the SEI components damaging it could more likely be a
major factor to capacity fade.
