Loss of Reduction and Clavicle Fractures Commonly Occur in Patients Undergoing Acromioclavicular Joint Reconstruction and Female Sex Is Associated With Higher Fracture Risk
Authors: Scott Fong, Arthur Saroyan, Rajiv S Vasudevan, Michael S Lee, Louise A Atadja, Peter F Monahan, Jay Moran, Kevin Girardi, Jacob D Kodra, Jonathan Salandra, Andrew E Jimenez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo use a large, multi‐institutional database to characterize the outcomes and complication profile after primary acromioclavicular (AC) joint reconstruction and to evaluate potential sex‐related differences in outcomes and complications.MethodsThe TriNetX national database was queried for patients undergoing AC joint reconstruction between January 2015 and October 2022. Ninety‐day and 2‐year outcomes including shoulder‐related complications, secondary surgeries, and medical resource use were evaluated. Propensity‐matched analysis assessed the association between sex and these outcomes, reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A 2‐year Kaplan‐Meier analysis compared revision probability between men and women.ResultsIn patients undergoing primary AC joint reconstruction, the revision rate was 3.9%, with loss of reduction in 13.4% and distal clavicle fractures in 15.7% within 2 years of surgery. On sex‐based analysis, women had higher reported rates of postoperative antidepressant use (15.8% vs 11.2%; OR 1.495, 95% CI 1.048‐2.132, P = .026), opioid prescriptions (52.7% vs 46.4%; OR 1.289, 95% CI 1.014‐1.637, P = .038), and clavicle fractures within both 90 days (19.6% vs 12.8%; OR 1.647, 95% CI 1.185‐2.294, P = .003) and 2 years (22.2% vs 16.0%; OR 1.493, 95% CI 1.098‐2.032, P = .010) postoperatively. Men had higher reported rates of loss of reduction within 90 days (14.2% vs 9.7%; OR 1.538, 95% CI 1.057‐2.237, P = .024), which was not significantly different within 2 years postoperatively.ConclusionsAfter primary AC joint reconstruction, loss of reduction (2 years, 13.4%) and distal clavicle fractures (2 years, 15.7%) were frequently reported complications in the postoperative period, with a 3.9% revision reconstruction rate within 2 years. Women reported higher rates of distal clavicle fracture in the postoperative period, and the high fracture rate in the female cohort (90 days, 19.6%) highlights a potentially under‐recognized clinical consideration.Level of EvidenceLevel III, retrospective cohort study.

Acromioclavicular (AC) joint separations are most common in young male athletes (between 20 and 30 years old), engaging in high‐intensity contact sports (e.g., football, rugby, hockey, and wrestling). 1 , 2 The mechanism of injury is often a direct blow or fall onto the superior shoulder in adduction, resulting in a force on the AC joint that damages or disrupts the AC and coracoclavicular ligaments. 2 Surgical reconstruction of the AC joint is often considered for patients who sustained high‐grade injuries with disruption of both the AC and coracoclavicular ligaments and significant displacement with instability in the coronal and/or sagittal plane (Rockwood type III‐VI injuries). 3

Several surgical techniques have been developed for reconstructing the AC joint, with no overall superior method shown in the literature. 4 , 5 Either open or arthroscopic methods can be used with the method of fixation falling into 1 or more of 3 categories: nonbiological fixation (e.g., suture, endobutton, synthetic graft, and Bosworth screw), biological fixation (allograft or autograft), or ligament/tendon transfer (Weaver‐Dunn procedure). 4 Nonbiological (suture) fixation can be supplemented with biological augmentation, often with semitendinosus allograft or autograft. 6 Additionally, acromial hook plates can be used but may require additional surgery for symptomatic hardware removal owing to the high rate of subacromial impingement and acromial osteolysis. 7 , 8

High complication and failure rates in patients undergoing AC joint reconstruction have been reported in the literature. A 2018 meta‐analysis of 1704 patients undergoing AC joint reconstruction reported a 20.8% loss of reduction and 14.2% overall pooled complication rate. 5 A more recent 2023 study by Chen et al. reported a failure rate of 25% and a pooled complication rate of 35%. 9 Studies have shown no difference in overall complication rates between open and arthroscopic techniques, although a higher rate of clavicle/coracoid fractures was observed with open surgery. 5 Other relatively common complications reported in the literature are infection, persistent pain at the AC joint, and adhesive capsulitis. 9

Most of the current literature reporting on the outcomes after AC joint reconstruction have evaluated relatively small cohorts with single‐institution analyses. Furthermore, there is a paucity of literature on outcomes among women undergoing AC joint reconstructions. The purposes of this study were to use a large, multi‐institutional database to characterize the outcomes and complication profile after primary AC joint reconstruction and to evaluate potential sex‐related differences in outcomes and complications. We hypothesized that overall revision reconstruction and complication rates after AC reconstruction would be low. Additionally, based on prior clinical evidence reporting higher reoperation rates among female patients after AC reconstruction, we hypothesized that female patients may show higher subsequent reconstruction compared with male patients. 10

METHODS

Study Design and Data Collection

This study was performed using TriNetX US Collaborative Network database (TriNetX, Cambridge, MA). The database uses anonymized electronic medical records from 68 health care organizations across the United States and was thus exempt from Institutional Review Board approval. All outcomes, procedures, and medications were identified using the International Classification of Disease, 10th revision, Clinical Modification, Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes, and Veteran Affairs National Formulary codes. Because of the absence of laterality modifiers within the database, the laterality of each procedure could not be reliably determined. The TriNetX database was queried in November 2024, and patients were eligible for inclusion if they underwent primary AC joint reconstruction between January 2015 and October 2022, identified using CPT codes 23552 and 23550. Patients without documentation of these procedures within the specified study period were not included.

To characterize the 2‐year complication profile and outcomes of primary AC joint reconstruction, the index event was defined for this study as the patient's first AC joint reconstruction, including both open and arthroscopic procedures. Postoperative outcomes were then queried within 90 days and 2 years of surgery and subdivided into 3 categories: shoulder‐related complications, secondary surgeries, and medical resource use. To protect patient privacy, outcomes with less than 10 patients are not reported in TriNetX and are reported as “≤10” instead. A full list of postoperative outcomes and CPT, International Classification of Disease, 10th revision, Clinical Modification, or Veteran Affairs codes used for their identification are listed in the Supporting Information: Appendix 1.

Subgroup Analysis: Male Versus Female Cohort

The initial AC joint reconstruction cohort was then subdivided into a male and female cohort. The 2 cohorts were 1:1 propensity matched based on age and race as well as the following comorbidities recorded at any time point prior to surgery: hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, tobacco use, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, obesity, osteoporosis, coagulation defects, opioid‐related disorders, anxiety disorders, depressive episode or major depressive disorder, and alcohol‐related disorders. The cohorts were also matched based on or prior medication prescriptions such as prior opioid analgesic or antidepressant prescriptions. All comorbidities are detailed in the Supporting Information: Appendix 2. Shoulder‐related complications, secondary surgeries, medical resource use, and medical complications were evaluated at 90‐day and 2‐year timeframes (Supporting Information: Appendix 1).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the analytic software on the TriNetX platform. Age was recorded as mean ± standard deviation, and all other variables were recorded as number (%). Continuous metrics were assessed using Mann‐Whitney U‐tests, whereas categorical metrics were evaluated with χ 2 tests. The sex‐based subgroup analysis was conducted using a propensity score matching analysis, which used a 1:1 nearest neighbor greedy matching approach and a caliper width of 0.25. Covariate balance after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences, with values <0.1 considered adequate balance in accordance with prior literature. 11 , 12 All postoperative outcomes were assessed using odds ratios (ORs) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and P value. A P value of <.05 was indicated as statistically significant. The tables present ORs and 95% CIs with men as the reference group, whereas the text also presents reciprocal ORs where appropriate for ease of interpretation from the female reference perspective. 13 Further, a 2‐year survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan‐Meier method to evaluate the probability of revision AC joint reconstruction in men versus women. Patients were included up to the time of their last recorded encounter in the database.

RESULTS

Demographics of the AC Joint Reconstruction Cohort

There were 3349 patients who underwent AC reconstruction between January 2015 and October 2022. The mean age of the primary AC joint reconstruction was 45.7 ± 15.6, 621 (18.54%) were women, and 2441 (72.9%) were White. Comorbidities of patients in this cohort are detailed in the Supporting Information: Appendix 2. Sixteen percent of the patients had a medical history of hypertension, 8% had a history of alcohol‐related disorders, and 66% had been prescribed prior opioid analgesics previously.

Postoperative Outcomes

Within 90 days after primary AC joint reconstruction, loss of reduction was reported to occur at a rate of 11.6% with 4.00% reporting 100% to 200% displacement postoperatively. Further, 13.4% of patients sustained a distal clavicle fracture within 90 days of surgery. Additionally, within 90 days postoperatively, 43.2% of patients were prescribed opioid analgesics. Male Versus Female Subgroup Analysis

After propensity score matching, the cohorts were balanced with 537 patients in the male group and 537 patients in the female group (Supporting Information: Appendix 3). The mean age of patients was 43.2 years in men and 42.6 years in women. After propensity match, there were no significant discrepancies in comorbidities between the male and female patient cohorts.

Complications and Adverse Medical Events

Complications and adverse medical events were evaluated at 90 days post‐AC joint reconstruction (Table 3). At 90 days, deep vein thrombosis rate was similar between the 2 cohorts (≤1.9% in both men and women, OR 1.000, 95% CI 0.413‐2.423, P = 1.000). Pulmonary embolism was reported to occur only in the female cohort at a rate of ≤1.9% (P = .001). Surgical site infection rates were also low across cohorts at 1.9% for men and 2.0% for women with no significant difference (P = .826).

Postoperative Medical Resource Use

Medical resource use rates within the 90‐day period after AC joint reconstruction are detailed in Table 3. Rates of emergency department visits and inpatient readmissions were not significantly different between male and female cohorts. Opioid prescription rates were significantly higher for women (52.7% vs 46.4%, OR 1.289, 95% CI 1.014‐1.637, P = .038). There was also a statistically significantly higher rate of antidepressant medication use postoperatively among women compared with men (15.8% vs 11.2%, OR 1.495, 95% CI 1.048‐2.132, P = .026).

Shoulder‐Related Complications

Shoulder‐related complications were assessed within 90 days and 2 years after primary AC joint reconstruction and reported in Tables 3 and 4, respectively. Within 90 days after surgery, male patients exhibited higher rates of loss of reduction compared with female patients (14.2% vs 9.7%, OR 1.538, 95% CI 1.057‐2.237, P = .024). Within 90 days after primary AC joint reconstruction, women also reported significantly higher rates of clavicle fractures (19.6% vs 12.8%, OR 1.647, 95% CI 1.185‐2.294, P = .003). Other complications, including coracoid fractures, brachial plexus injury, adhesive capsulitis, axillary nerve injury, and hardware complications, showed no statistically significant differences between groups.

Secondary Surgery

Rates of revision surgery were evaluated at 2 years postoperatively (Table 4). Female patients had similar rates of revision AC joint reconstruction compared with male patients (5.96% vs 3.7%, OR 0.610, 95% CI 0.345‐1.082, P = .088). Total shoulder arthroplasties were infrequent among both cohorts at ≤1.862% for both men and women, with no statistically significant difference in rates between the 2 groups. Arthroscopic Mumford procedures were also infrequent at ≤1.862% for men and 0% for women (P = .001). DISCUSSION

This study found that the overall 2‐year rate of secondary surgeries after primary AC joint reconstruction was low; however, reports of loss of reduction (2 years, 13.4%) and distal clavicle fractures (2 years, 15.7%) were frequent in the postoperative period. On sex‐based analysis, the primary finding was a markedly elevated rate of reported distal clavicle fractures among female patients (90 days, 19.6%; 2 years, 22.2%), with significantly higher fracture rates compared with men after primary AC reconstruction. Further, men exhibited a higher risk of early (≤90 days) loss of reduction that did not persist at 2 years. Lastly, women had higher rates of postoperative antidepressant and opioid prescription within both the 90‐day and 2‐year timelines after primary AC joint reconstruction.

The reported AC reconstruction revision rates in the literature vary, including a pooled rate of 7.6% at a mean follow‐up of 34.3 months reported by a meta‐analysis by Gowd et al., although comparison is limited by methodological differences. 5 Revision rates may also vary depending on the reconstruction techniques used. A recent systematic review of 34 studies with a minimum 2‐year follow‐up reported reoperation rates ranging from 1.2% to 5.4%, depending on the reconstruction technique used (e.g., free tendon graft, modified Weaver‐Dunn, and hook plate techniques), with synthetic ligament devices showing the lowest reoperation rates. 14 Although the current study was unable to stratify outcomes by different techniques, future studies with sufficient procedural granularity to enable technique‐specific, sex‐stratified comparisons can help identify optimal surgical approaches that minimize loss of reduction and distal clavicle fractures.

The loss of reduction rate after AC reconstruction has been variably reported in the literature, with substantial heterogeneity in methodology including outcome definitions, imaging protocol, and methods of data capture. In their case series of 279 patients, Chen et al. noted that a loss of reduction of greater than 50% based on immediate and final postoperative radiographs was observed in 25% of their patients. 9 Similarly, in their systematic review of 460 cases of AC joint reconstructions, Pill et al. noted that 96 (20.9%) patients showed radiographic loss of reduction. 15 Loss of reduction may contribute to clinically significant postoperative symptoms, as greater postoperative displacement has been associated with worse visual analog scale and subjective pain scores. 16 , 17 The loss of reduction rate observed in our cohort (2 years, 13.4%) was identified using diagnostic coding retrieved in the postoperative period rather than confirmed clinically with radiographic assessment, reflecting significant differences in outcome ascertainment compared with prior studies in the literature. A comparable degree of heterogeneity in outcome reporting is observed for distal clavicle fractures, which were reported in 15.7% of patients within the 2‐year postoperative period in the present study. Prior literature has described a wide range of postoperative clavicle fracture rates, with pooled estimates of coracoid and clavicle fractures reported at 5.7% in a meta‐analysis by Gowd et al. and individual clinical studies documenting distal clavicle fracture rates as high as 18%. 5 , 14 , 18 , 19

The observed sex‐based differences in early postoperative loss of reduction may reflect biomechanical factors that influence construct stability during initial postoperative healing. Early postoperative loss of reduction is often attributed to “windscreen‐wiper” micromotion of the fixation, which can erode stability and widen clavicular tunnels. 20 , 21 In men, greater musculature and heavier skeletal structures in the shoulder girdle may impose higher loads on the fixation, increasing the risk of construct destabilization during the early healing phase compared with women. 22 Unfortunately, the mechanism of injury could not be extracted from our dataset, so the causes of the early postoperative reseparation or distal clavicle fractures are unclear. Future studies may seek to clarify the specific risk factors and mechanisms for these early complications.

The increased distal clavicle fractures reported in the postoperative period among female patients may partially stem from anatomical differences between men and women. For example, Renfree et al., in their cadaveric study, revealed that men have 5 mm of additional anteroposterior width of the distal clavicle compared with women. 23 Furthermore, an ex vivo assessment by Cirovic et al. revealed that women exhibit increased cortical porosity and reduced bone thickness in the anterior subregion of the distal clavicle, potentially predisposing them to higher fracture risk. 24 Clinically, Wang et al., in their database study, also reported that women were more likely to undergo revision surgery and distal clavicle excision after AC joint reconstruction. 10 Although surgical techniques which may decrease fracture risk should be prioritized in all patients given the high clavicular fracture rates, surgeons and patients should be aware of higher risk in women. Minimizing both tunnel diameter and number of clavicular tunnels may offer theoretical protection against fractures. Supporting this, Gowd et al., in their meta‐analysis, found that tendon graft fixation requiring larger tunnel diameters was associated with significantly increased fracture risk. 5 Similarly, in their biomechanical analysis, Spiegl et al. revealed that a 6.0 mm clavicular tunnel with hamstring tendon graft resulted in inferior clavicular strength compared with a cortical fixation button technique using a 2.4 mm tunnel. 25 Our findings highlight a markedly elevated risk of postoperative clavicular fracture among female patients, which emphasizes the need for further research to identify surgical techniques that may mitigate this risk.

Lastly, the increased short‐term opioid prescriptions in women aligns with previous studies showing worsened postoperative pain and greater narcotic use after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair in women compared with men. 26 , 27 Furthermore, although there is limited evidence comparing postoperative antidepressant prescriptions between men and women after orthopaedic procedures, antidepressant use is generally more prevalent among female patients. 28 , 29 Although this study controlled for depression as well as prior antidepressant prescriptions, it is conceivable that primary AC joint reconstruction itself may trigger need for additional antidepressant prescriptions in some patients, potentially because of psychological stressors or postoperative pain.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we were unable to differentiate between open and arthroscopic AC joint reconstruction techniques as both procedures may be billed using the same CPT code. This also means that we were unable to identify the specific technique used, potential graft choice, or number of tunnels. Second, data from the TriNetX database cannot be confirmed clinically, which may introduce errors and confound results. Third, we were unable to verify laterality of the procedures queried via TriNetX, which necessitates careful interpretation of study results. Fourth, we were unable to extract granular data which may be confounding factors such as concomitant injuries, mechanism of injury, or detailed radiographic findings of separation including the grade of the injury. Fifth, concomitant procedures performed at the time of the index AC joint reconstruction, such as distal clavicle excision (Mumford), could not be reliably identified and were therefore not evaluated in this study. Sixth, the propensity score matching incorporated extensive clinically relevant covariates selected a priori to minimize confounding; however, this may have reduced the number of matched patients and reflects trade‐offs inherent to the chosen matching parameters, including caliper width. Additionally, although comorbidities were included in the propensity matching process, their independent associations with outcomes such as fracture, loss of fixation, or failure were not evaluated and may have influenced observed complication rates. Seventh, loss of reduction was identified based on postoperative diagnostic coding (S43.109, S43.12) rather than direct radiographic assessment. Eighth, despite relatively high reported rates of loss of reduction and distal clavicle fracture, few patients underwent revision AC joint reconstruction, suggesting that many of these complications may have either been managed nonoperatively or with procedures not captured by revision‐specific CPT codes, including nonspecific or unlisted procedure codes (e.g., CPT 29999). Further, the specific management of postoperative distal clavicle fractures could not be reliably determined using claims data.

CONCLUSIONS

After primary AC joint reconstruction, loss of reduction (2 years, 13.4%) and distal clavicle fractures (2 years, 15.7%) were frequently reported complications in the postoperative period, with a 3.9% revision reconstruction rate within 2 years. Women reported higher rates of distal clavicle fracture in the postoperative period, and the high fracture rate in the female cohort (90 days, 19.6%) highlights a potentially under‐recognized clinical consideration.
