Analyzing and Improving Diffusion Models for Time-Series Data Imputation: A Proximal Recursion Perspective
Zhichao Chen, Hao Wang, Fangyikang Wang, Licheng Pan, Zhengnan Li, Yunfei Teng, Haoxuan Li, Zhouchen Lin

Abstract
Diffusion models (DMs) have shown promise for Time-Series Data Imputation (TSDI); however, their performance remains inconsistent in complex scenarios. We attribute this to two primary obstacles: (1) non-stationary temporal dynamics, which can bias the inference trajectory and lead to outlier-sensitive imputations; and (2) objective inconsistency, since imputation favors accurate pointwise recovery whereas DMs are inherently trained to generate diverse samples. To better understand these issues, we analyze DM-based TSDI process through a proximal-operator perspective and uncover that an implicit Wasserstein distance regularization inherent in the process hinders the model’s ability to counteract non-stationarity and dissipative regularizer, thereby amplifying diversity at the expense of fidelity. Building on this insight, we propose a novel framework called SPIRIT ( S ¯ \underline{\text{S}} emi- P ¯ \underline{\text{P}} rox i ¯ \underline{\text{i}} mal Transport R ¯ \underline{\text{R}} egularized time-series I ¯ \underline{\text{I}} mpu t ¯ \underline{\text{t}} ation). Specifically, we introduce entropy-induced Bregman divergence to relax the mass preserving constraint in the Wasserstein distance, formulate the semi-proximal transport (SPT) discrepancy, and theoretically prove the robustness of SPT against non-stationarity. Subsequently, we remove the dissipative structure and derive the complete SPIRIT workflow, with SPT serving as the proximal operator. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed SPIRIT approach.

Recently, diffusion models (DMs) have been widely adopted for TSDI task due to their excellent performance for data generation tasks
[

Given partially observed time-series data, DMs define a forward diffusion (noising) process that gradually perturbs the data with Gaussian noise, producing a sequence of increasingly noisy variables. A neural network is introduced to estimate the score function, and TSDI can be reformulated as sampling from the conditional distribution by solving the reverse-time stochastic differential equation, which imputes the missing values via progressively denoises the variables from the initial noise. Initiated by

Contributions:
The main contributions of this manuscript can be summarized as follows:

1.

We cast DM-based TSDI as an optimization problem proximal term and pinpoint two key bottlenecks: (i) limited robustness to non-stationarity induced by the Wasserstein distance, and (ii) imputation inaccuracy caused by the dissipative regularizer.

2.

We relax the Wasserstein distance via the generalized Bregman divergence and propose a semi-proximal transport discrepancy; we further provide theoretical analysis on robustness to non-stationarity.

3.

We remove the dissipative regularizer and re-derive a TSDI procedure under the proximal optimization framework with the semi-proximal transport discrepancy, yielding a new DM-based TSDI termed SPIRIT.

2.1
Problem Formulation

Suppose
\mathbf{X}^{\text{ideal}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times T\times D}
denotes the fully observed time series with
N
pieces of data, and each datum is consists of
T
chronologically ordered observations and
D
features. Missing entries are encoded by a binary mask
\mathbf{M}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times T\times D}
, where
\mathbf{M}_{n,t,d}=1
if the entry
\mathbf{X}^{\text{ideal}}_{n,t,d}
is missing and
\mathbf{M}_{n,t,d}=0
otherwise. The observed data matrix
\mathbf{X}^{\text{obs}}
is then given by
\mathbf{X}^{\text{obs}}=\mathbf{X}^{\text{ideal}}\odot(1-\mathbf{M})+\texttt{NaN}\odot\mathbf{M}
, where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product and “
NaN
” represents unobserved entries. The goal of time-series imputation is to construct an imputed data matrix
\mathbf{X}^{\text{imp}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times T\times D}
from
\mathbf{X}^{\text{obs}}
such that
\mathbf{X}^{\text{imp}}\odot\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{X}^{\text{ideal}}\odot(1-\mathbf{M})\approx\mathbf{X}^{\text{ideal}}
. For
\mathbf{M}_{n,t,d}=0
, we have
\mathbf{X}^{\text{imp}}\odot(1-\mathbf{M})=\mathbf{X}^{\text{obs}}\odot(1-\mathbf{M})
. On this basis, we denote probability density functions (PDFs) by
p(\cdot)
; for example, the PDF of the fully observed time series is written as
p(\mathbf{X}^{\text{ideal}})
. During imputation, the model induces an empirical distribution over the imputed samples, which we denote by
q(\mathbf{X}^{\text{imp}})
. Concretely, we represent
q
as a Dirac delta measure,
q(\mathbf{X}^{\text{imp}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}_{n}}
, where
\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}_{n}}
is the Dirac measure concentrated at the
n
-th imputed observation
\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}_{n}
. Since time-series data are indexed along the temporal axis and the SDE underlying DMs is also time-defined, we use
\tau
and
t
to denote the time indices, and
T
and
\mathrm{T}
to denote the terminal time indices of the dataset and the DM.

2.2
Proximal Operator and Proximal Recursion

Given a proper, lower semi-continuous convex function
g:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, the proximal operator
\text{prox}_{\varepsilon g}(\cdot)
with step size
\varepsilon
is given by:

\text{prox}_{\varepsilon g}({\mathbf{x}})=\mathop{\arg\min}_{{\mathbf{y}}}g({\mathbf{y}})+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(1)

Crucially,
Equation

1
balances minimizing
g
against a “proximal term”,
\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}
, which enforces the solution
{\mathbf{y}}
to remain in the neighborhood of
{\mathbf{x}}
with a strength controlled by the coefficient
\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}

[
50
]
, and the process that iteratively repeating
Equation

1
is named proximal recursion
[
11
,
10
]
.

2.3
Wasserstein Distance

Let
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
be the set of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
with finite second moment. For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
, the
2
-Wasserstein distance
[
62
]
is defined by the following “optimal transport” (OT) problem:

\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\pi({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}),

(2)

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
, i.e., joint distributions on

\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
.

2.4
Dissipative Structure in Stochastic Dynamics

In continuous-time stochastic dynamics
[
55
]
, the evolution of a state
{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}
is modeled by

\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}=b({\mathbf{x}}_{\tau},\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau+\sigma({\mathbf{x}}_{\tau},\tau)\mathrm{d}W_{\tau},

(3)

where
b({\mathbf{x}}_{\tau},\tau)
is the drift term,
\sigma({\mathbf{x}}_{\tau},\tau)
is the volatility term, and
\mathrm{d}W_{\tau}
denotes the Wiener process. From the perspective of non-equilibrium stochastic dynamics, both the deterministic drift
b({\mathbf{x}}_{\tau},\tau)=-{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}
and the stochastic diffusion
\mathrm{d}W_{\tau}
contribute to the dissipative behavior
[
49
]
of the system: the
-{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}
pulls the state back toward the origin energy dissipation, while the Wiener-driven diffusion
\mathrm{d}W_{\tau}
spreads the state distribution to prevent collapse.

First, the data are highly
non-stationary
: for example, in the Electricity dataset, consumption patterns differ substantially across weekdays and holidays, while in the Weather dataset, climatological patterns vary markedly across seasons. Second, the task demands stringent pointwise
accuracy
, as exemplified by mean squared error (MSE) and mean absolute error (MAE). To better illustrate how these challenges interact with the diffusion framework, we present the following two toy case studies.

3.2
Analysis DMs In the Context of TSDI

In the context of DM-based TSDI, suppose that we have learned the conditional score function
\nabla\log p({\mathbf{x}}^{\mathrm{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathrm{obs}})
. The imputation procedure is then carried out by simulating the following SDE from
\tau=\mathrm{T}
to
\tau=0

[
61
,
15
]
:

\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}=[f({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}},\tau)-g^{2}\nabla\log{p({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{obs}})}]\mathrm{d}\tau+g\mathrm{d}W_{\tau},

(4)

where
f({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}},\tau)
and
g
are the predefined drift and diffusion coefficients, respectively. In particular, we merely update the place where
\mathbf{M}_{n,t,d}=1
.

However, it should be pointed out that directly simulating
Equation

4
can be suboptimal for TSDI. To support this claim, we first state the following proposition:

Proposition 3.1
.

The imputation process for DMs can be formulated as iteratively solving the following optimization problem in a proximal operator form:

\displaystyle\mathop{\inf}_{q^{\prime}}\indent-\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}

\displaystyle[\log{p({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{obs}})}]+\frac{1}{2\eta}\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(q^{\prime},q)+\phi({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}),

(5)

where we abbreviate the candidate distribution (distribution for current iteration)
q^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}})
and the base distribution (distribution for previous iteration)
q({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}})
as
q^{\prime}
and
q
, respectively. The term
\frac{1}{\eta}
is a predefined positive term determined by the noise schedule of DMs, and the term
\phi({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}})
denotes the dissipative structure-related term, which depends on the underlying SDE and is specified as follows:

•

Variance Preserving SDE (VP-SDE):

\phi({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}[\log{q^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}})}]-\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}[\|{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}\|_{2}^{2}]
, and
\eta=\beta(\tau)
.

•

Variance Exploding SDE (VE-SDE):
\phi({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}[\log{q^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}})}]
, and
\eta=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}(\tau)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}
.

From the proposition above, it is evident that directly deploying DMs for TSDI tasks faces two critical challenges. First, the proximal term induced by the squared 2-Wasserstein distance
\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(q^{\prime},q)
restricts the model’s flexibility, resulting in a lack of robustness toward non-stationary dynamics. Second, the proximal term induced by the dissipative regularizer
\phi({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}})
injects entropy that encourages over-diverse outputs, potentially degrading the deterministic accuracy required for TSDI task.

The key to addressing the first issue is to devise a novel discrepancy metric that mitigates the impact of non-stationarity. Building on this, we aim to remove the dissipative structure and re-derive an alternative inference procedure that ensures accurate imputation. The subsequent two subsections therefore focus on these two aspects.

3.3
Semi-Proximal Transport Framework

To address the limitations of vanilla OT framework, which is sensitivity to outliers due to strict mass conservation in non-stationary environments, we propose the SPT framework. Unlike standard OT, which forces a complete match between distributions, SPT relaxes the hard constraint on the target marginal by introducing a divergence penalty.

Formally, we define the SPT discrepancy as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu)}\int\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+D_{\psi}(\pi_{\mathbf{y}},\nu),

(6)

where
\Pi(\mu)
denotes the set of couplings with the first marginal fixed to
\mu
, and
\pi_{\mathbf{y}}
denotes the second marginal of
\pi
. The term
D_{\psi}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the generalized Bregman divergence defined as follows
[
5
]
:

D_{\psi}(\rho,\nu)\coloneqq\psi(\rho)-\psi(\nu)-\langle\delta_{\nu}\psi(\nu),\rho-\nu\rangle,

(7)

where
\delta_{\nu}\psi(\nu)
is the first variation of
\psi(\nu)
with-respect-to
\nu
, and
\psi:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is a strictly convex functional termed Bregman potential
[
4
,
6
]
. While the framework admits general potentials, in this work, we specifically adopt the entropic potential
\psi(\mu)\coloneqq\int\mu({\mathbf{x}})[\log\mu({\mathbf{x}})-1]\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}
. We select this specific potential for its geometric properties. Specifically, its gradient map acts as a mirror map that ensures that the iterations naturally remain within the space of positive measures (i.e.,
\rho\geq 0
) throughout the optimization
[
29
,
59
,
56
]
, which guaranties the well-definedness of the transport plan and thus stabilizes the subsequent iterative updates.

By replacing the rigorous constraint
\pi_{\mathbf{y}}=\nu
with the soft penalty
D_{\psi}(\pi_{\mathbf{y}},\nu)
, the SPT framework transforms the vanilla OT framework into a selective matching process. When the cost of transporting mass to an outlier is excessively high, the optimization prefers to incur the penalty rather than distorting the transport plan, thereby ensuring robustness against non-stationary scenario. Based on Lemma 1 in
[
26
]
and Theorem C.2 in
[
65
]
, we state the following lemma to demonstrate the outlier robustness of the proposed SPT discrepancy:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, and consider a contaminated target distribution
\tilde{\nu}=(1-\zeta)\nu+\zeta\delta_{\mathbf{z}},\zeta\in(0,1)
, where
\delta_{\mathbf{z}}
denotes a Dirac mass at the outlier location
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. The Wasserstein distance has the following lower bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle(\mu,\tilde{\nu})\geq\zeta\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,{\nu})

(8)

\displaystyle+(1-\zeta)[\|\mathbf{y}^{*}-\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}-g(\mathbf{y}^{*})+\int{h(\mathbf{x})\mu(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}],

for some
\mathbf{y}^{*}
belonging to the support of
\nu
, and where
f
and
g
are optimal dual potentials for
\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)
. Meanwhile, the SPT discrepancy with Bregman potential
\psi(\nu)\coloneqq\int\nu(\mathbf{y})[\log\nu(\mathbf{y})-1]\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}
under target contamination admits the bound as follows:

\mathbb{S}(\mu,\tilde{\nu})\leq(1-\zeta)\mathbb{S}(\mu,\nu)+\zeta(1-e^{-D(\mathbf{z})})+C(\zeta),

(9)

where
D(\mathbf{z})\coloneqq\int\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\mu(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
is the average distance of
\mathbf{z}
and samples from
\nu
, and
C(\zeta)
is a constant defined as
C(\zeta)\coloneqq(1-\zeta)\log\frac{1}{1-\zeta}-\zeta\log\zeta
.

This theoretical advantage is visually corroborated in the case study shown in
Figure

1
. As demonstrated in
Figure

1(c)
, while standard OT incorrectly pairs distinct modes due to forced matching, the SPT framework successfully ignores transient noise and correctly aligns the stable modes, effectively handling the non-stationary property.

3.4
Diversity-Eliminated Functional for Imputation

Based on the theoretical results in
Sections

3.2
and
3.3
, we design the following objective functional to “improve” the DM-based TSDI task:

\displaystyle\mathop{\inf}_{q^{\prime}}\indent-\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}[\log p

\displaystyle({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{obs}})]+\frac{1}{2\eta}\mathbb{S}(q^{\prime},q)

(10)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}[\|\nabla\log{p({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{obs}})}\|_{2}^{2}],

where we eliminate the dissipative structure-related term
\phi({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})
, which encourages the diversity of the imputation results. Based on this, we add the gradient norm regularization of the corresponding
\nabla\log{p({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{obs}})}

\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}[\|\nabla\log{p({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{obs}})}\|_{2}^{2}]
, which seeks to finding the point that has saddle point such that we can accelerate the imputation process. As such, we formulate the following theorem to demonstrate the sufficient condition that reduces the functional given by
Equation

10
:

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume
p({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{obs}})
is once continuously differentiable and
\nabla\log p({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{obs}})
is square-integrable under the measures considered. Let
\psi(\rho)=\int\rho({\mathbf{x}})[\log\rho({\mathbf{x}})-1]\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}
. Represent
q^{\prime}
by an empirical measure
q^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\delta_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}
with
w_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}=1
. The descent directions
\boldsymbol{T}
for updating the locations
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and logarithmic weights
\{\log{w_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, which yield an approximate solution to
Equation

10
, are given as follows:

•

Location direction
, where
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are updated by:

\boldsymbol{T}_{{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})=\nabla\log p({\mathbf{x}}^{\mathrm{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathrm{obs}}).

(11)

•

Weight direction
, where logarithmic weights
\{\log{w_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are updated by:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}_{w}({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}})=-2

\displaystyle\|\nabla\log p({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{obs}})\|_{2}^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle+2\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\log p({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{obs}})\|_{2}^{2}].

Even though
Proposition

3.3
provides an update direction for the log-weights
\{\log w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, directly applying this direction does not automatically guarantee that the resulting weights remain feasible, i.e.,
w_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}=1
. To alleviate this issue, we introduce the following proposition to ensure a well-defined iteration process for

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\eta>0
and
\boldsymbol{T}_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
. Define the intermediate log-weights:
\log\widehat{{w}}_{i}^{(k+1)}\coloneqq\log{{w}}_{i}^{(k)}+\eta\boldsymbol{T}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})
, the corresponding normalized weights can be obtained by the following equation:

{{w}}_{i}^{(k+1)}=\frac{{\widehat{{w}}_{i}^{(k+1)}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{D}{\widehat{{w}}_{j}^{(k+1)}}}.

(13)

So far, within the proximal-recursion framework, we have addressed robustness to non-stationarity and the inaccuracy induced by the two proximal terms namely Wasserstein distance and dissipative regularizer in DM-based TSDI task.

3.5
Overall Workflow

Although
Section

3.4
proposes a novel imputation process under the proximal recursion framework, the overall SPIRIT workflow is not yet explicitly summarized.
To complete the workflow, we still need a principled way to learn the conditional score

\nabla\log p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}\mid{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})
.

However, compared with previous DM-based TSDI approaches, where the score function is obtained by bridging missing and observed data using a predefined SDE and can be learned via score matching, our method modifies the objective functional and thus cannot directly adopt the same learning strategy. Therefore, we introduce the following proposition to enable the learning of
\nabla\log p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}\mid{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})

Proposition 3.5
.

For learning the score network
s_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})
, the following two objectives are equivalent:

\displaystyle\mathop{\arg\min}_{s_{\theta}}\|s_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})-\nabla\log{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})}\|_{2}^{2}

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\arg\min}_{s_{\theta}}\|s_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})-\nabla\log{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}\|_{2}^{2}.

Following
Proposition

3.5
, it suffices to learn the marginal score
\nabla\log p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})
.
Moreover, given an initial imputation
{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}
, the right-hand side of

Equation

14
can be learned via the denoising score matching
[
63
]
given in
Equation

15
. The justification of this equivalence is provided in
Section

.

\mathop{\arg\min}_{s_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\sigma}(\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}[\|s_{\theta}(\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}^{\text{imp}})-\nabla\log q_{\sigma}(\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})\|_{2}^{2}],

(15)

where
\sigma
is variance scale,
\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}^{\text{imp}}
is obtained by
\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}^{\text{imp}}={\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}+\epsilon,\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})

4.2
Overall Performance

Table

1

Table

1
:

•

Efficacy of existing TSDI approaches:
Existing TSDI methods demonstrate strong performance. In particular, discriminative approaches such as PatchTST and TimesNet achieve highly competitive results, ranking first or second in 4 out of 14 cases. These models effectively capture temporal patterns in the data and leverage them for TSDI. Meanwhile, methods specifically designed for TSDI, such as CSDI and PSW-I, also perform comparably, achieving first or second best in 10 out of 14 cases.

•

Mass relaxation matters:
Across a range of methods, we observe that vanilla OT-based TSDI approaches, including CSDI, Glocal (from the proximal-term perspective introduced in
Section

3.2
), Sinkhorn, and TDM, do not outperform relaxed transportation-based approaches such as PSW-I and SPIRIT. We attribute this phenomenon to the sensitivity of vanilla OT formulations to the non-stationarity inherent in time-series data, which ultimately leads to suboptimal imputation accuracy.

•

Necessity of dissipative-structure elimination:
Although CSDI achieves competitive results on most of the datasets, we observe that it can fail in certain datasets, for example, Illness and Traffic. In addition, Glocal and GP-VAE also exhibit consistently weaker performance. This phenomenon suggests that for TSDI, which is an accuracy-oriented task, removing the dissipative-structure term is necessary to ensure reliable performance.

•

Efficacy of the proposed SPIRIT method:
SPIRIT retains the strengths of generative TSDI methods while mitigating their limitations in handling non-stationarity. Overall, SPIRIT achieves the best or second-best performance in 10 out of 14 cases, demonstrating strong effectiveness and practical potential in real-world applications.

4.3
Ablation Studies

In this subsection, we present the ablation study. Our SPIRIT approach has two main contributions: (1) introducing the SPT discrepancy as the proximal term, and (2) removing terms associated with the dissipative structure. Notably, the dissipative structure induced by the VP-SDE is not appropriate in our setting because the conditional distribution defined by the DDPM underlying SDE is incompatible with the SPIRIT framework. We therefore focus on using
\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}[\log q^{\prime}]
to represent the dissipative structure. The corresponding results are reported in
Table

2
.

From
Table

2
, we observe consistent performance drops when ablating either the SPT proximal term or the
\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}[\log q^{\prime}]
component. This indicates that both introducing SPT and eliminating the dissipative-structure term are important to SPIRIT’s overall performance. Moreover, the relative impact differs across datasets. On ETT-h1 and ETT-h2, removing SPT leads to a smaller degradation than removing
\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}[\log q^{\prime}]
, whereas on ETT-m1 and ETT-m2 the performance drop from removing SPT is larger. This suggests that on larger-scale datasets such as ETT-m1 and ETT-m2, the SPT component contributes more substantially to accuracy improvements, potentially because larger datasets are more likely to contain outliers. This finding highlights the importance of introducing SPT. Moreover, it provides empirical support for
Lemma

3.2
and further motivates the use of the entropy-induced generalized Bregman divergence. Finally, when both components are removed simultaneously, SPIRIT exhibits the most severe degradation, further confirming that SPT and dissipative-structure elimination are complementary and jointly underpin SPIRIT’s superior performance.

6
Conclusions

In this manuscript, we analyze and improve DM-based TSDI through the lens of proximal-term regularization. We first reformulate the DM-based TSDI procedure as a proximal-recursion process and identify two proximal components that hinder performance: the Wasserstein-distance term and the dissipative-structure term. The former leads to limited robustness under non-stationary features, while the latter tends to encourage diverse imputations rather than accurate reconstructions. To address these issues, we replace the Wasserstein distance with a generalized Bregman divergence induced by an entropy functional, which relaxes the overly restrictive constraint imposed by the Wasserstein metric. We further remove the dissipative-structure term. Based on these modifications, we propose a new DM-based TSDI framework, termed SPIRIT. Extensive experiments validate the effectiveness of SPIRIT.

Additional Background Knowledge

In this subsection, we demonstrate the detailed background knowledge we use in the derivation of our main theoretical results in our manuscript.

Differential Equation:

Suppose we have the following SDE, which is known as the Itô process
[
82
]
:

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=f({\mathbf{x}},\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau+g(\tau)\mathrm{d}W_{\tau}.

(16)

Denote the marginal distribution of
{\mathbf{x}}
at time
\tau
as
q_{\tau}({\mathbf{x}})
. It can be observed that the
q_{\tau}({\mathbf{x}})
satisfies the following partial differential equation (PDE), which is known as the Fokker-Planck equation
[
55
]
:

\frac{\partial q_{\tau}({\mathbf{x}})}{\partial\tau}=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot[f(\mathbf{x},\tau)q_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})]+\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot[g^{2}(\tau)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}q_{\tau}({\mathbf{x}})].

(17)

A classical solution to
Equation

17
requires
q_{\tau}({\mathbf{x}})
to be differentiable in both
\tau
and
{\mathbf{x}}
. To address this issue, rather than working with classical (smooth) solutions of
Equation

17
, we consider a weak, measure-valued representation of
q_{\tau}({\mathbf{x}})
using a finite set of particles
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}

[
39
]
:

q_{\tau}({\mathbf{x}})\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}.

(18)

As such, for each particles, we solve the PDE defined by
Equation

17
using the following ordinary differential equation (ODE), which merely requires changing the spatial position of
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and provides a weak solution to (
17
)
[
23
]
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=f({\mathbf{x}}_{i},\tau)-\frac{1}{2}g^{2}(\tau)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log q_{\tau}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}).

(19)

While the standard Itô process evolves the distribution
q_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})
by transporting the particle locations
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, an alternative approach is to steer the density by adjusting the particle weights. Specifically, we formulate the empirical approximation of
q_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})
as a weighted sum of Dirac measures:

q_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{i}},\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}=1,\quad w_{i}\geq 0}_{[w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}]\in\Delta^{N-1}}.

(20)

In this framework, the shape of
q_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})
is controlled by the time-varying weights
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
while the particle locations remain fixed. The evolution of the probability density is governed by the following integro-differential equation:

\frac{\partial q_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\tau}=[\int g_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})q_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-g_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})]q_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}),

(21)

where
g_{\tau}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the scalar function driving the reweighting process. Consequently, the continuous time dynamics for the individual weight
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
follow the ODE:

\frac{\mathrm{d}w_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=w_{i}[\int g_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})q_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-g_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{i})],\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

(22)

Using the fact that
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\log w_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\dfrac{1}{w_{i}}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}w_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}
,
Equation

22
can be further reformulated as follows:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\log w_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=[\int g_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})q_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-g_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{i})],\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

(23)

Notably,
Equation

23
is the normalized equation for
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and its un-normalized counterpart can be given as follows:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\log w_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=-g_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

(24)

Wasserstein Distance and Its Dynamic Formulation:

Let
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
denote the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
with finite second moments, i.e.,
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})\coloneqq\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{D})|\int\|{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu({\mathbf{x}})<\infty\}
, where
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
denotes the set of all probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Considering any two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
, we define the
p
-Wasserstein distance as follows
[
62
]
:

\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{(q_{\tau},v_{\tau})}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}\|v_{\tau}({\mathbf{x}})\|^{2}q_{\tau}({\mathbf{x}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{d}\tau,

\boldsymbol{T}^{\star}({\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq{\mathbf{x}}+\eta v_{\tau}^{*}({\mathbf{x}}),

we can reformulate the

(29)

\int\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\|^{2}_{2}\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})=(1-\zeta)\int\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\pi^{\star}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})+\zeta\int\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{z}}\|^{2}\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}).

(59)

Using the convexity of
p\mapsto D_{\psi}(p,q)
in its first argument for fixed
q
, we have

D_{\psi}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathbf{y}},\tilde{\nu}_{\varphi})\leq(1-\zeta)D_{\psi}(\pi^{\star}_{\mathbf{y}},\tilde{\nu}_{\varphi})+\zeta D_{\psi}(\delta_{\mathbf{z}},\tilde{\nu}_{\varphi}).

(60)

Moreover, since
\mathbf{z}
is outlier, it is justified to introduce the assumption that
\nu(\{\mathbf{z}\})\approx 0
. Hence, we have:

D_{\psi}(\delta_{\mathbf{z}},\tilde{\nu}_{\varphi})=-\log(\zeta\varphi).

(61)

Since
\tilde{\nu}_{\varphi}\geq(1-\zeta)\nu
as measures, we obtain the following result when
\pi^{\star}_{\mathbf{y}}\ll\nu
(to promise the well-definess for the computation of Bregman divergence):

D_{\psi}(\pi^{\star}_{\mathbf{y}},\tilde{\nu}_{\varphi})=\int\pi^{\star}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y})\log\frac{\pi^{\star}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y})}{\tilde{\nu}_{\varphi}(\mathbf{y})}\leq\int\pi^{\star}_{\mathbf{y}}\log\frac{\pi^{\star}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y})}{(1-\zeta)\nu(\mathbf{y})}=D_{\psi}(\pi^{\star}_{\mathbf{y}},\nu)+\log\frac{1}{1-\zeta}.

(62)

Collecting terms and using the feasible coupling
\widehat{\pi}
, we obtain, for any
\varphi\geq 0
, we get the following inequality:

\mathbb{S}(\mu,\tilde{\nu})\leq(1-\zeta)\mathbb{S}(\mu,\nu)+\zeta[\varphi D(\mathbf{z})+(\varphi\log\varphi-\varphi+1)]+C(\zeta),

(63)

where
D(\mathbf{z}):=\int\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|^{2}\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})
, and

C(\zeta)\coloneqq(1-\zeta)\log\frac{1}{1-\zeta}-\zeta\log\zeta
is a constant (for fixed
\zeta
) independent of
\varphi
.

Finally, since
Equation

63
holds for any
\varphi\geq 0
, we minimize the
\varphi
-dependent term.
Define

e(\varphi):=\varphi D(\mathbf{z})+(\varphi\log\varphi-\varphi+1),

(64)

Then take the first-order condition for the optimization problem, we have:

\frac{\mathrm{d}e(\varphi)}{\mathrm{d}\varphi}=D(\mathbf{z})+\log\varphi=0\Rightarrow\varphi^{\star}=\exp(-D(\mathbf{z})).

(65)

On this basis, consider the second-order condition, we have:

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}e(\varphi)}{\mathrm{d}\varphi^{2}}=\frac{1}{\varphi}>0.

(66)

Thus, we have the following result:

\inf_{\varphi\geq 0}\quad e(\varphi)=D(z)e^{-D(z)}-d(z)e^{-D(z)}-e^{-D(z)}+1=1-e^{-D(z)}.

(67)

Plugging
Equation

67
into
Equation

63
yields the desired bound.
∎

(91)

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
, using

\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{i}}(w_{i}\log\frac{w_{i}}{w_{i}^{(k)}})=\log\frac{w_{i}}{w_{i}^{(k)}}+1,\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{i}}(-\sum_{j}w_{j})=-1,

(92)

we get the following result:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{i}}=\boldsymbol{T}_{w_{i}}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})-\frac{1}{\eta}(\log\frac{w_{i}}{w_{i}^{(k)}}+1-1)+\lambda+\gamma_{i}=\boldsymbol{T}_{w_{i}}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})-\frac{1}{\eta}\log\frac{w_{i}}{w_{i}^{(k)}}+\lambda+\gamma_{i}.

(93)

Setting

93
to
0
, we have the following result:

0=\boldsymbol{T}_{w_{i}}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})-\frac{1}{\eta}\log\frac{w_{i}}{w_{i}^{(k)}}+\lambda+\gamma_{i}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\log\frac{w_{i}}{w_{i}^{(k)}}=\eta(\boldsymbol{T}_{w_{i}}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})+\lambda+\gamma_{i}).

(94)

For the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker condition
[
8
]
, we have the following conditions termed “complementary slackness” condition:

w_{i}\geq 0,\quad\gamma_{i}\geq 0,\quad\gamma_{i}w_{i}=0,\quad\sum_{i}w_{i}=1.

(95)

Since
w_{i}
can be given as follows based on

w_{i}=w_{i}^{(k)}\exp[\eta(\boldsymbol{T}_{w_{i}}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})+\lambda+\gamma_{i})],

(96)

we know that
\gamma_{i}=0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
. Then, we have:

w_{i}=w_{i}^{(k)}\exp[\eta(\boldsymbol{T}_{w_{i}}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})+\lambda)].

(97)

On this basis, using the fact that:

\widehat{w}_{i}^{(k+1)}\coloneqq w_{i}^{(k)}\exp(\eta\boldsymbol{T}_{w_{i}}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})),

(98)

we can further reformulate

(99)

Imposing
\sum_{i=1}^{D}w_{i}^{(k+1)}=1

can be reformulated as follows:

\exp(\eta\lambda)=\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{D}\widehat{w}_{j}^{(k+1)}}.

(100)

Finally, based on

,
100
and
99

B.5
Derivation of Proposition
3.5
and Related Discussions

Proposition
.

The following two learning objective are identical for score network
s_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})
learning:

\displaystyle\mathop{\arg\min}_{s_{\theta}}\|s_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})-\nabla\log{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{s_{\theta}}\|s_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})-\nabla\log{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}\|_{2}^{2}

(101)

Proof.

The key is proving that learning
\nabla\log{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}
is identity to learning
\nabla\log{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})}
. To address this issue, we have the following result:

(102)

On this basis, the score function of

can be written as

\displaystyle\nabla\log p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})\overset{\text{(i)}}{=}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})}[\log{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})}]}{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})}[p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})\nabla\log{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})}]}{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}\overset{\text{(ii)}}{=}\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}[\nabla\log{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})}],

(103)

where “(i)” follows from differentiating under the integral sign:

\displaystyle\nabla\log p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})=\frac{\nabla p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}=\frac{\nabla\int p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}}}{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}=\frac{\int p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})\nabla p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}}}{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})}\left[\nabla p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})\right]}{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})},

(104)

and “(ii)” uses Bayes’ rule
p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})=\dfrac{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})}{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})}\left[p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}}\log p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})\right]}{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}}\log p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}}}{p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}

\displaystyle\int p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}}\log p({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}^{\text{obs}}}.

Taking

B.6
Discussions of the Convergence Property for SPIRIT Framework

In
Section

3.5
, we note that the “Score Learning” stage admits convergence guarantees from standard optimization theory under mild regularity conditions (e.g.,
[
7
]
). For the “Recursive Imputation” stage, define the energy
\mathcal{J}({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}[\log p({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}\mid{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{obs}})]+\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}[\left\|\nabla\log p({\mathbf{x}}^{\text{imp}}\mid{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{obs}})\right\|_{2}^{2}].
Assuming
\mathcal{J}
is lower bounded and smooth, its value decreases monotonically along the imputation iterates in the continuous-time limit
\eta\to 0
, and the dynamics converge to a stationary point. In this subsection, we further analyze the convergence properties of both the “Score Learning” and “Recursive Imputation” stages.

Convergence Analysis of “Score Learning”.

At first, we define the
\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}
as follows:

\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{q_{\sigma}(\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})}[\|s_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})-\nabla\log q_{\sigma}(\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}^{\text{imp}}|{\mathbf{x}^{\text{imp}}})\|_{2}^{2}]

(117)

For the following mild assumptions, we get the convergence promising of the “Score Learning” stage:

1.

Assumption 1 (Lower bound):

\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta)\geq\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta^{\star})\geq 0>-\infty
for all
\theta
.

2.

Assumption 2 (
L
-smooth):

\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta)
is
L
-Lipschitz:
\|\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta^{\prime})\|\leq L\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|\quad\forall\theta,\theta^{\prime}.

The “Lower bound” condition holds since
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{DSM}}(\theta)
is an expectation of a squared
\ell_{2}
error and thus
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{DSM}}(\theta)\geq 0
. The “
L
-smoothness” condition (i.e., Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{DSM}}
) is a standard assumption in gradient-descent convergence analyses, for example
[
2
]
. A sufficient (but not necessary) set of conditions for
L
-smoothness is to impose appropriate boundedness/spectral-norm constraints on the network so that
\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{DSM}}
becomes Lipschitz; gradient clipping is a practical heuristic that controls update magnitudes but does not imply
L
-smoothness.

Under Assumptions 1 and 2, for learning rate
lr\in(0,\frac{2}{L})
, the gradient descent iterates satisfy:

\|\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})\|\to 0\quad\text{as }e\to\infty.

(118)

Because
\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta)
is
L
-smooth, we have the following result for any

\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta)+\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta),\theta^{\prime}-\theta\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|^{2}.

(119)

Apply this with
\theta=\theta_{e}
and
\theta^{\prime}=\theta_{e+1}=\theta_{e}-lr\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e+1})

(120)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})+\left\langle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e}),-lr\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|-\xi\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})-lr\|\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})\|^{2}+\frac{L\times lr^{2}}{2}\|\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})-lr(1-\frac{L\times lr}{2})\|\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})\|^{2}.

Since

, we define the coefficient

as follows:

c\coloneqq lr(1-\frac{L\times lr}{2})>0.

(121)

Therefore, we get the following results:

\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})-\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e+1})\geq c\|\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})\|^{2}.

(122)

Summing from

to

\sum_{e=0}^{{\mathcal{E}_{\text{score}}}-1}c\|\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})\|^{2}\leq\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})-\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{\mathrm{T}})\leq\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})-\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta^{\star}).

(123)

Letting

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})\|^{2}<\infty.

(124)

A series of nonnegative terms is finite only if the terms go to zero, hence

\lim_{e\to\infty}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSM}}(\theta_{e})\|\to 0.

(125)

This proves convergence to a stationary point.

\mathcal{J}[q^{\prime}_{\tau}]
is non-increasing and the dynamics converge to a stationary solution characterized by the following derivation:

\bigl\|\nabla_{x}\delta_{q^{\prime}_{\tau}}\mathcal{J}[q^{\prime}_{\tau}]\bigr\|_{2}^{2}=0\Rightarrow\nabla_{x}\delta_{q^{\prime}_{\tau}}\mathcal{J}[q^{\prime}_{\tau}]=0\Rightarrow\delta_{q^{\prime}_{\tau}}\mathcal{J}[q^{\prime}_{\tau}]\equiv C,

(131)

where the last implication follows because a function with zero spatial gradient is a constant, and
C<\infty
is a constant. Therefore, we establish convergence of the “Recursive Imputation” stage in the continuous-time limit.

Appendix E
Discussions on Limitations & Future Research Directions

In our study, although SPIRIT achieves promising performance on TSDI, several limitations remain and motivate future research:

•

Periodic structure and temporal dependence:
Following common practice, we treat multivariate time
[
58
,
57
]
. While effective, this design does not explicitly model temporal autocorrelation and periodic patterns. Future work could incorporate frequency-domain diffusion models
[
19
]
, or design a proximal term using PSW
[
65
]
that better preserves periodic structure.

•

Limited uncertainty quantification:
To prioritize accurate imputation, our derivation reduces the dissipative component, which may diminish sample diversity and weaken uncertainty estimates. A promising direction is to adopt multi-objective optimization
[
28
]
to balance accuracy and diversity, enabling reliable uncertainty quantification without sacrificing imputation quality.

•

Downstream-task awareness:
We focus on TSDI
per se
. Future work should investigate how to integrate SPIRIT with downstream models, for example, forecasting
[
53
]
, to improve robustness under missing-data scenarios, potentially via joint training or end-to-end task-aware objectives.

•

Beyond MCAR to MNAR settings:
Our current formulation primarily targets missing completely at random (MCAR). In many real applications, missingness depends on the (unobserved) values or the data-collection mechanism (MNAR). Extending SPIRIT to MNAR settings may require explicitly modeling the missingness mechanism
[
33
]
and incorporating distributionally robust optimization
[
34
]
to improve reliability under mechanism shift.

•

Alternative score-learning strategies:
In this work, we adopt denoise score matching to learn the score function
[
63
]
. DSM requires computing gradients with respect to the inputs, which incurs additional backpropagation overhead and leads to higher runtime as the data scale increases. Future work could explore alternative score-learning objectives or architectures that reduce input-gradient computation.
