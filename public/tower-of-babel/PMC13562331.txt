Predicting anatomical difficulty in robot-assisted rectal resection using a novel anatomic landmark-based grading system: a prospective observational study
Authors: K. Jinno, F. Pfeffer, B. S. Nedrebø, H. M. Forsmo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to develop an anatomic landmark-based ordinal grading system for surgical difficulty in robot-assisted rectal resection and to assess its preoperative predictability, with the potential to support preoperative assessment of surgical difficulty.MethodsWe divided the procedure into two phases: mesocolon dissection and mesorectal dissection. Surgical difficulty was defined using an anatomy-based ordinal grading system (grades 1–3) for each phase. A cohort of 158 patients undergoing robot-assisted rectal resection was included. Regression analysis was performed for each phase using preoperative variables to select predictors. Pooled tenfold cross-validation was used for model validation.ResultsFor the mesocolic phase, a four-predictor model (body mass index, sex, lung disease, and age) achieved moderate performance (accuracy 0.648; AUC (≤ 1 vs > 1) 0.787; AUC (≤ 2 vs 3) 0.831). For the mesorectal phase, a three-predictor model (body mass index, sex, and neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy) achieved good predictive performance (accuracy 0.697; AUC (≤ 1 vs > 1) 0.927; AUC (≤ 2 vs 3) 0.850). The final equations were implemented in a ready-to-use calculator to facilitate clinical application.ConclusionThis study introduces a novel, phase-specific grading system that allows preoperative estimation of anatomical difficulty in robot-assisted rectal resection. The model is consistent with established preoperative risk factors. By utilizing standard preoperative data, this tool may assist surgeons in anticipating operative complexity and tailoring their surgical approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10151-026-03368-8.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most common malignancy worldwide, with rectal cancer accounting for approximately 38% of all cases, corresponding to 729,702 cases in 2022 [1]. Since the introduction of total mesorectal excision (TME), local recurrence rates have declined markedly from 25–40% to 5–10%, making TME the standard procedure for rectal cancer surgeries [2–4]. Laparoscopic rectal resection has gained popularity due to several advantages over the open approach, including reduced intraoperative blood loss and shorter hospital stays [5, 6]. However, this technique involves a longer learning curve, requiring a higher case volume to reach proficiency because of the narrow pelvic cavity [7]. Robot-assisted rectal resection was introduced to overcome these limitations, offering three-dimensional visualization, improved ergonomics, and enhanced maneuverability compared to conventional laparoscopy [8].

Despite these advancements, rectal cancer surgery remains technically challenging. Previous studies have identified predictors influencing surgical difficulty of rectal resections, including body mass index (BMI), tumor height, sex, preoperative chemoradiotherapy, and magnetic resonance (MR) pelvimetries [9–15]. Scoring systems, such as those proposed by Escal et al. [10] and DeAngelis et al. [11], have shown promise but vary in predictive accuracy and kappa values. Most studies include MR pelvimetry parameters as predictors, though specific parameters vary between articles. This study aims to establish a simple definition of difficulty in rectal resection and a robust system to predict surgical difficulties based on preoperative data.

Materials and methods

This prospective observational study included 158 robot-assisted rectal resections performed between 2021 and 2023 at Haukeland University Hospital, Bergen, Norway. All operations were performed using the da Vinci Xi Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Patients with histologically proven adenocarcinoma of the rectum whose operations were performed by our three most experienced surgeons (with over 20 years of clinical experience and over 1 year of experience with robot-assisted rectal resections) were included. Operations with poor video quality, high rectal cancer without the need to open peritoneal fold for R0 resections, and operations converted to laparotomy were excluded. Data collection included demographic, clinical, radiographic, and intraoperative variables. Total mesorectal dissection (TME) is defined as dissection with an intact mesorectum. Partial mesorectal excision (PME) is defined as resection of part of the mesorectum. All the operation videos were analyzed by a single surgeon.

The study is reported in line with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Surgical technique

At the Department of Surgery, Haukeland University Hospital, our standardized approach for rectal resection is performed using the da Vinci Xi Surgical System. The procedure begins with the identification of the inferior mesenteric vein (IMV) and mobilization of the mesocolon from the medial aspect within Toldt’s fascia. Mobilization of the splenic flexure is performed selectively, based on an intraoperative assessment of sigmoid redundancy.

Subsequently, the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) is identified, and the mesocolon is dissected caudally. High ligation of the IMA is performed at the D3 level. Following medial dissection, the mesocolon is fully mobilized via a lateral approach. The robotic system is then undocked and redocked to facilitate pelvic dissection. In female patients, uterine suspension is routinely performed to optimize visibility of the surgical field.

Mesorectal dissection is carried out to the planned resection level, achieving either PME or TME. The rectum is divided using a linear stapler. Specimen extraction is performed via a Pfannenstiel incision. The proximal resection margin is defined using indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence angiography to ensure adequate perfusion. After the anvil is secured and the extraction incision is closed, bowel continuity is restored using a circular stapler.

Definitions of difficulty

There have been several attempts to define the difficulty of rectal resections. Escal et al. [10] defined difficulty based on several intraoperative and postoperative factors. Veenhof et al. included surgeons’ intraoperative subjective evaluation ranging from 1 to 10 [9]. Sun et al. used specimen quality and surgeons’ evaluation [14]. While Yamaoka et al. [13] and Yuval et al. [15] used dissection time.

In this study, we developed a simplified anatomy-based difficulty grading system. Specific anatomical features that are known to affect operative difficulty were used to define grades. As the mesocolic and mesorectal phases involve distinct anatomical challenges, we graded them separately.

Mesocolon difficulty grade

The mesocolon grading system was designed to reflect technical difficulty during medial-to-lateral mobilization based on the quality and stability of exposure. Visualization of the IMV and the degree of retraction required were used as practical indicators of anatomical accessibility and working space (Table 1, Fig. 1). Mesorectum difficulty grade

Difficulty grading for the mesorectal phase was based on the exposure of the anterior mesorectum. As pelvic space is a key determinant of technical difficulty, grading was performed after opening the peritoneal fold and initiating distal dissection (approximately 1 cm), while one robotic arm lifted the peritoneum. Analysis of video

Haukeland University Hospital uses the Sectra system (Sectra AB, Linköping, Sweden) which records all the robot-assisted operations. Grading was performed by a single experienced surgeon.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics® version 30. Cross-validation and diagnostic metrics were generated through syntax-based routines and custom macros.

Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical data were expressed as numbers and percentages.

Ordinal logistic regression was applied for both univariate and multivariate analyses. Variables with P ≤ 0.05 after univariate analysis were included in multivariate analysis.

Candidate predictors were selected based on univariable associations (p ≤ 0.20) for the large model or using a multivariable regression model with a significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05 for the reduced model.

No external validation dataset was available. Internal validity was therefore assessed through pooled tenfold cross-validation. Model performance was evaluated by accuracy and threshold AUCs. We then chose the final model based on parsimony after model validation.

Additional details regarding model specification, convergence, and repeated pooled cross-validation procedures are provided in Supplementary File S1.

In accordance with Norwegian and institutional regulations, the study was registered as a clinical audit (eprotokoll, REK 683464).

Multivariable analysis of mesorectal difficulty grade

We compared a large model including all the predictors with p ≤ 0.20 with univariable analysis (BMI, T stage, EMVI, ASA, hemoglobin, heart disease, albumin, sex, neoadjuvant therapy) to a compact three-predictor model (p ≤ 0.05) from multivariable analysis (BMI, sex, and neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy). Using pooled tenfold cross-validation, the three-predictor model performed better than the larger model and was selected for parsimony. Its validated performance was accuracy 0.682 and threshold AUCs: AUC (≤ 1 vs > 1) 0.920 and AUC (≤ 2 vs 3) 0.839 (Table 12). The model showed good predictive ability. Among patients with both mesocolon and mesorectal grades available (n = 122), discordance between the two grades was observed in 45.1% of cases.

The final multivariable models were expressed as ordinal logistic regression equations (Eqs. 1 and 2), allowing calculation of the probability of each difficulty grade for an individual patient. To facilitate clinical use, the equations were implemented in an Excel-based calculator requiring only routinely available preoperative variables. The calculator provides predicted probabilities for each difficulty grade and identifies the most likely grade for the mesocolic and mesorectal phases.

The calculator is provided as an Excel-based supplementary file (Supplementary File S2), allowing for independent use and inspection of the model.

Discussion

Preoperative estimation of surgical difficulty may support operative planning by identifying cases that may require increased technical focus, experienced assistance, or appropriate allocation of operative time. While previous studies have proposed various models, definitions of difficulty are often complex or subjective. In this study, we introduce a simplified, anatomy-based grading system based on objective anatomical landmarks. The procedure was divided into mesocolon and mesorectal phases, as these represent distinct anatomical challenges that influence surgical difficulty.

We developed separate models for the mesocolic and the mesorectal phases. For mesocolic dissection, BMI, sex, lung disease, and age were significant predictors. Higher BMI, male sex, and advanced age were associated with higher difficulty grades.

The model showed moderate predictive performance. We hypothesize that a tendency for less intravisceral fat and a wider pelvic bone structure in the female body provides better operative space [16]. Interestingly, lung disease (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, COPD) emerged as a predictor. COPD is not a known risk factor for operative difficulty but is a known risk factor for complications [17]. Patients with COPD may have altered diaphragmatic dynamics which could influence operative exposure despite pneumoperitoneum. In addition, COPD has been associated with constipation and abdominal distension [18], which may contribute to voluminous small bowel and reduced working space during mesocolic dissection, although these mechanisms remain speculative. The number of cases in this study is also limited. Higher age was related to higher grades in the study. Causes are likely multifactorial.

For mesorectal dissection, sex, BMI, and neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy were included in the final model. Male sex, higher BMI, and receipt of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy were related to higher difficulty grades. The model showed good predictive performance. Male patients typically possess a narrower pelvis and bulkier mesorectum, explaining the increased difficulty [19]. BMI was a significant predictor for higher difficulty in rectal resection in most articles [9, 10, 12, 15]. Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy likely contributes to difficulty through two mechanisms: it is indicated in locally advanced tumors, and radiation-induced inflammation and fibrosis leads to bulkier, rigid tissue disturbing dissection planes.

Differences between mesocolon and mesorectal grades were observed in 45.1% of cases; however, this comparison is descriptive and does not account for the ordinal nature of the grading system. Therefore, the clinical significance of such discordance remains uncertain.

For difficulty predicting, most articles use dichotomous outcomes. MRI pelvimetry has been associated with operative difficulty, but its clinical utility is limited by variability, complexity, and lack of standardization. The present model aims to provide a simpler, clinically applicable alternative using routinely available variables. Our study used three ordinal grades with simple predictors and still demonstrated moderate-to-good predictive performance. The grades are relatively objective and can be applied universally since they are based on anatomy.

The developed models are incorporated into calculators, which can be easily accessed to provide estimated anatomical difficulty grades. We suggest using this easily available preoperative analysis for tailored preparation, including operator selection and equipment allocation, to enhance surgical efficiency and outcomes.

This study has several limitations. Grading was performed by a single experienced surgeon, and interobserver variability was not assessed. Although the grading system is based on clearly defined anatomical landmarks, formal evaluation of reproducibility across multiple assessors is needed. In addition, a number of cases were excluded because of insufficient video quality and conversion to laparotomy, which may introduce selection bias, although these exclusions were considered largely technical and limited in number. Finally, the proposed grading system was not validated against objective operative outcomes such as operative time or specimen quality. Future studies should evaluate these associations and provide external validation.

Conclusion

This study introduces a simple and effective model to predict difficulty in robot-assisted rectal resections. Our attempt to define difficulty using anatomy-based grades reflects well-known preoperative risk factors. By utilizing readily available preoperative data, it offers a valuable tool for supporting surgical planning and preoperative assessment.
