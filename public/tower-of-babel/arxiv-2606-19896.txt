Optimal and Adaptive Bayesian Sampling for Non-Linear Parameter Estimation under White Noise Corresponding author: lennart.bosch@uni-ulm.de
Lennart H. Bosch, Martin B. Plenio

Abstract




1. Introduction

Non-linear model fitting is a common problem in various fields of science and engineering aiming to estimate unknown parameters from a given set of data samples.
In correspondence with the expected signal model, the exact design of the experiment and sampling scheme may greatly impact the overall achievable precision.
Signal samples acquired at high signal amplitudes relative to the noise floor are intuitively more informative for parameter estimation than samples acquired in signal regions, where the signal is almost completely buried in noise.
For various non-linear models, however, correlations between samples are crucial for high precision and the optimal sampling constitutes a highly non-trivial configuration.
Systematic approaches to finding the sampling schemes that are expected to yield the highest possible accuracy using the available resources, such as time or number of samples, are collectively referred to as experimental design optimization
[
1
,
2
]
.
Which regions of the sampling space exhibit characteristic behavior is typically governed by the true values of underlying model parameters and, consequently, so will the optimal design.
This further motivates the use of adaptive sampling approaches that dynamically update the knowledge of parameter values during the data acquisition and reallocate sampling or further respective resources to improve the estimation precision
[
3
]
.

Experimental design optimization has been successfully employed
across a wide range of disciplines, including sensor placement optimization
[
4
]
, study design in psychology
[
5
,
6
]
, and sampling strategy optimization
[
1
]
, where the appropriate optimality criterion fundamentally depends on the research question and parameters of interest.
Over recent years, the techniques have also increasingly been applied to experiments involving solid-state spin sensors such as the nitrogen-vacancy (NV) center, featuring applications like magnetometry
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
, nuclear spin sensing
[
11
]
, charge state detection
[
12
]
, and relaxometry
[
13
]
.
In above mentioned quantum sensor setups, efficient sampling schemes improve the expected precision by focusing on relevant sampling regions, for example, frequency intervals including resonances or time periods before the signal decay by decoherence.

A vast number of optimality criteria are derived from the Fisher information matrix
[
1
,
2
]
, which quantifies the information that an observable random variable carries about unknown model parameters.
An alternative class of approaches utilizes the principles of Bayesian parameter estimation where the expected information gain constitutes a natural figure of merit
[
14
,
15
]
.
Within this framework, adaptive schemes emerge naturally through principles of Bayesian updating
[
14
,
16
,
3
,
15
]
.
The computation of the expected information gain, however, typically requires the evaluation of nested integrals, such that practical implementations often rely on numerical approximations or stochastic methods
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
.

This work considers a signal scenario in which linear signal parameters are eliminated from the estimation procedure through marginalization assuming white Gaussian noise and discusses the consequences for optimal experimental designs derived from the expected information gain.
To this end, we make heavy use of the Laplace approximation to simplify one of the integrals in the expected information gain
[
17
,
18
]
and perform a series expansion of the remaining expression.
Leading expansion terms yield a very compact expression that facilitates analytical results for exponentially decaying signals and admits compact implementations of adaptive sampling schemes.
Because exponentially decaying signals are a common occurrence in different fields of science, optimal sampling schemes have been discussed in the corresponding literature
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
The results obtained from the approach presented in this work is capable of reproducing the known results using a more accessible description, complements them with numerical experiments, and expands on the discussions through the adaptive schemes.
Application of above described methods to NV center experiments requires operation under conditions that render the independent white noise a sufficiently accurate model of the noise.
This is achieved either through large photon counting statistics or the use of an ensemble of NV centers
[
9
,
10
,
13
]
.

2. Theory

Model and Analytical Marginalization

The class of signals considered in this work assumes a model
f(t;\theta,\vec{a})
which constitutes a linear superposition with weights
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
of non-linear basis functions
\varphi_{k}(t;\theta)
with
k=1,\dots,m
that depend explicitly on time and a set of parameters
\theta
.
Taking
n
samples at discrete points in time
t_{j}
with
j=1,\dots,n
, the model compactly reads

\displaystyle\vec{f}(\vec{a},\theta)=\mathbf{\Phi}(\theta)\cdot\vec{a}\,,

(1)

where the matrix
\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is composed of column vectors of the non-linear basis functions
\varphi_{k}(t;\theta)
evaluated for all
t_{j}
.
The observed data is modeled as random samples from the likelihood distribution
P\big(\vec{y}|\theta,\vec{a}\big)
, which is conditioned on the signal model through the associated parameters and resembles the nature of the noise.
Further discussions focus on the most widespread noise model of additive white noise for which the likelihood distribution reads

\displaystyle P{}\left(\vec{y}|\theta,\vec{a}\right)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{n/2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert\vec{y}-\mathbf{\Phi}\cdot\vec{a}\right\rVert^{2}\right\}\,.

(2)

Equivalently, the data samples are described as sum of the true signal and background noise as

\displaystyle\vec{y}=\cdot+\vec{\varepsilon}\,,

(3)

with elements of
\vec{\varepsilon}
being identically independently distributed and hats indicate the use of true parameter values.

For a variety of problems, parameter estimates can efficiently be obtained from the maximum of the likelihood in
eq.

2
with respect to
\vec{a}
and
\theta

[
25
]
.
In contrast, the Bayesian approach aims to derive parameter estimates from the posterior distribution
P{}\left(\theta,\vec{a}|\vec{y}\right)
, instead.
Starting from the noise model, and imposing a prior distribution
P(\theta,\vec{a})
for the parameters to estimate, the posterior distribution is obtained via Bayes theorem
[
26
]

\displaystyle P{}\left(\theta,\vec{a}|\vec{y}\right)=\frac{P{}\left(\vec{y}|\theta,\vec{a}\right)\cdot P(\theta,\vec{a})}{P(\vec{y})}\,.

(4)

The prior distribution on the data
P(\vec{y})
can merely be understood as normalization constant for the posterior.
Parameter estimates are then often inferred from the mean of the posterior distribution and some measure of the width provides uncertainty estimates
For a detailed discussion of the implications and consequences, we shall refer to the book by Kruschke
[
27
, Ch.2]
.

Treating problems of spectral estimation, for example, the linear parameters
\vec{a}
are of negligible interest, and following the example of Bretthorst
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
, can be marginalized over to obtain a posterior distribution in the non-linear parameters only as

\displaystyle P{}\left(\theta|\vec{y}\right)=\intop\nolimits\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}P{}\left(\theta,\vec{a}|\vec{y}\right)\mathrm{d}\vec{a}\,.

(5)

To express insignificant prior knowledge of
\vec{a}
, a natural choice for the prior in
\vec{a}
is given by a normal prior distribution centered around
0
.
Choosing the distribution’s width
\tau
for each element of
\vec{a}
sufficiently large such that the prior covers the true values, the prior acts as a gentle regularization without distorting influence of the data.
Thus, the prior distribution explicitly reads

\displaystyle P(\theta,\vec{a})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\tau^{2})^{m/2}}\exp\{-\frac{\left\lVert\vec{a}\right\rVert^{2}}{2\tau^{2}}\}\cdot P(\theta)\,.

(6)

Above choice for the prior allows for analytical evaluation of the marginalization in
eq.

5
and, omitting terms that do not explicitly depend on
\theta
, yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\ln P{}\left(\theta|\vec{y}\right)&\varpropto\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\vec{y}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{\Phi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}+\xi^{2}\mathbb{I})^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})\vec{y}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\ln\{\det(\mathbf{\Phi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}+\xi^{2}\mathbb{I})\}-\ln P(\theta)\\
&=:F(\theta;\vec{y})\,,\end{split}

(7)

with
\xi:=\sigma/\tau
1
1

1

Please note that the introduction of
\xi
as a plain substitution is only possible if the distribution is explicitly defined as distribution in
\theta
only and not also in
\tau
and
\sigma
.
.
The evaluation of
eq.

7
requires
\mathbf{\Phi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}+\xi^{2}\mathbb{I}
to be non-singular.
While large
\xi
is technically sufficient for regularization, a safe criterion is
n>m
, i.e., there are more data samples than basis functions, and
\mathbf{\Phi}
has full rank, meaning that basis functions are sufficiently orthogonal.
Throughout the rest of this work, constant (full) rank is assumed for
\mathbf{\Phi}{}
for all values of
\theta
considered, such that local continuity for
F(\theta;\vec{y}{})
in
\theta
is guaranteed.
The expression for the marginal posterior in
eq.

7
is employed in similar form in sparse classification as well as regression
[
31
]
and recently gained traction in spectral estimation
[
32
,
33
]
.
In the following section we present systematic expansions of estimators and the covariance which ultimately give rise to an objective function for the optimization of sampling schemes.

The explicit computation of the posterior mean for generic
\mathbf{\Phi}
is tedious and not expected to yield analytically tractable results.
Therefore, the remainder of this work follows the example of maximum-likelihood and focuses on the maximum a-posteriori (MAP) estimator derived from the maximum of
P{}\left(\theta|\vec{y}\right)
, instead.
Equivalently, estimators are derived from the minimum
-\log P{}\left(\theta|\vec{y}\right)
denoted by
F(\theta;\vec{y})
in
eq.

7
.
Likewise, this work focuses on uncertainty estimation from the posterior variance instead of quantiles or credibility intervals to allow for a compact analytical treatment.
Because the computation of the moments of the distribution can be cumbersome, nevertheless, upcoming discussions make use of the Laplacian approximation and approximate the variance by the inverse of the distribution’s curvature at the maximum.

Figure

1
(b) schematically demonstrates the approximation of
F(\theta;\vec{y})
up to quadratic order around the maximum and shows the effect on the probability distribution after taking the exponential in
fig.

1
(c).

Above described procedures are straightforward to conduct numerically for a specific realization of the noise
\vec{\varepsilon}
.
Because
F(\theta;\vec{y})
explicitly depends on
\vec{y}
and the noise realization, so will the estimate, lifting it to a random variable.
From a theoretical point of view, the estimate is expected to approach a deterministic noiseless estimate of
\theta
governed by the local minimum of the noiseless objective function
F(\theta;)
upon continuous reduction of the noise variance.
Slowly turning the noise contribution up, again, the estimate will experience a correction that explicitly depends on the exact realization of
\vec{\varepsilon}
.
To model this behavior theoretically, the estimate is expanded into

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}=\theta_{0}+\delta\theta\,,

(8)

where
\theta_{0}
denotes the noiseless estimate and
\delta\theta
describes the correction explicitly depending on
\vec{\varepsilon}
.
Assuming a local optimum, the defining equation for
\theta_{0}
and
\delta\theta
constitutes
\partial_{\theta}F(\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}};\vec{y})=0
.
To separate out the effect of
\delta\theta
, expand the implicit definition around
\theta_{0}
up to linear order in
\delta\theta
as

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{\theta}F(\theta_{0};\vec{y})+\partial_{\theta}^{2}F(\theta_{0};\vec{y})\cdot\delta\theta+\mathcal{O}\big(\delta\theta^{2}\big)\,.

(9)

All individual terms of the expansion are required to vanish for the equation to be satisfied.
Expanding
\delta\theta
further in powers of the noise as
\delta\theta=\delta\theta^{(1)}+\delta\theta^{(2)}+\dots
and separating the terms in
eq.

9
by powers of
\varepsilon
(indicated by the superscript) yields a system of equations for expansion terms
\delta\theta^{(j)}
upon setting the corresponding coefficients equal to zero.
For demonstrational purposes, we shall note here that the resulting equations for the deterministic and linear order read

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}F^{(0)}(\theta_{0};\vec{y})

\displaystyle=0

(10a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}F^{(1)}(\theta_{0};\vec{y})+\partial^{2}_{\theta}F^{(0)}(\theta_{0};\vec{y})\cdot\delta\theta^{(1)}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(10b)

that now allow to solve
eq.

10a
for
\theta_{0}
and recursively obtain the linear correction
\delta\theta^{(1)}
from
eq.

10b
.
Higher orders are obtained from the corresponding extension of above equations.
Ref.
[
34
]
employed a similar approach to derive variance estimates of the estimator.

Recalling the posterior’s logarithm from
eq.

3
and introducing
\mathbf{M}=\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{\Phi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}+\xi^{2}\mathbb{I})^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
, the objective function from
eq.

7
expands into powers of
\vec{\varepsilon}
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}F(\theta;\vec{y})&=\\
\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}&\Big\{\vec{\varepsilon}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}\vec{\varepsilon}+\vec{\varepsilon}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}+^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}\vec{\varepsilon}+{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}\Big\}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\ln\{\det(\mathbf{\Phi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}+\xi^{2}\mathbb{I})\}-\ln P(\theta)\,,\end{split}

(11)

and one can read off the individual contributions required for
eqs.

10a
and
10b
.
For problems with multiple free parameters compactly described by multidimensional
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the expansion up to linear order requires the introduction of the gradient and Hessian to end up at the defining equations

\displaystyle\nabla F^{(0)}(\theta_{0};\vec{y})

\displaystyle=0

(12a)

\displaystyle\nabla F^{(1)}(\theta_{0};\vec{y})+\mathbf{H}^{(0)}(\theta_{0};\vec{y})\cdot\delta\theta^{(1)}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(12b)

with elements of the matrix
\mathbf{H}
given by
[\mathbf{H}]_{jk}=\partial_{j}\partial_{k}F(\theta_{0};\vec{y})
and
\partial_{j}
denotes the derivative with respect to the
j
-th component of
\theta
.
Higher-order corrections from the expansion in
eq.

9
require the introduction of higher-order tensors and, therefore, quickly become tedious to evaluate.

The Hessian used to approximate the posterior distribution and whose inverse provides an estimate of the covariance similarly can be separated into a noiseless expression and corrections depending on the realization of
\vec{\varepsilon}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}};\vec{y})=\mathbf{H}_{0}+\delta\mathbf{H}\,.

(13)

To identify the contributions, expand the Hessian at the MAP estimator in powers of corrections as

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}};\vec{y})=\mathbf{H}(\theta_{0};\vec{y})+\nabla\mathbf{H}(\theta_{0};\vec{y})\cdot\delta\theta+\dots\,,

(14)

where the term
\nabla\mathbf{H}\cdot\delta\theta
has to be understood as
\sum_{j}\partial_{j}\mathbf{H}(\theta_{0};\vec{y})\delta\theta_{j}
.
Similar as for
eqs.

10a
and
10b
, the expansion terms can be sorted by powers of
\vec{\varepsilon}
to read off the expressions for
\mathbf{H}_{0}
and the contribution to
\delta\mathbf{H}
linear in
\vec{\varepsilon}
.
For a posterior of the form in
eq.

7
, the noiseless term is obtained upon setting
\vec{\varepsilon}
in
\vec{y}
to zero and plugging in
\theta_{0}
for the non-linear parameter.

For the analysis of the scaling behavior of noiseless contribution and corrections, limit the discussion to a scenario under which
\mathbf{\Phi}\mathbf{\Phi}+^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\xi^{2}\mathbb{I}
is far from singular around
\theta_{0}
(
\mathbf{\Phi}
has full column-rank and
n>m
) and
\mathbf{\Phi}
is assumed at least twice continuously differentiable in a local environment around
\theta_{0}
, such that also
F(\theta_{0};\vec{y})
is twice continuously differentiable.
In addition, the sampling space is required to be bounded as
t_{\mathrm{l}}\leq\{t_{j}\}\leq t_{\mathrm{u}}
.
Because
\theta_{0}
is chosen such that
F(\theta_{0};)
exhibits a local maximum,
\mathbf{H}_{0}
is positive definite.

\mathbf{H}_{0}
is then expected to asymptotically approach the Fisher information and, therefore, grows linearly with
n
.
The correction term is decomposable into a sum of random variables each constructed from elements of
\vec{\varepsilon}
and powers thereof, such that by the central limit theorem, it will exhibit a
\sqrt{n}
scaling with the number of samples.
Thus, with increasing number of samples, the deterministic contribution increasingly dominates the overall Hessian and becomes an increasingly accurate approximation.
Lifting the constraint on the sampling space to be bounded, one can construct scenarios in which
\mathbf{H}_{0}
achieves super-linear scaling, for example, for the uniform sampling of an oscillating function with constant amplitude
[
35
]
.

The scaling behavior of the covariance matrix as inverse of
H(\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}};\vec{y})
follows from the expansion

\displaystyle(\mathbf{H}_{0}+\delta\mathbf{H})^{-1}=\mathbf{H}_{0}^{-1}-\mathbf{H}_{0}^{-1}\cdot\delta\mathbf{H}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{0}^{-1}+\dots\,.

(15)

Recalling the scaling of
\mathbf{H}_{0}
and
\delta\mathbf{H}
, the leading order term scales as
1/n
with the next-to-leading order term being suppressed as
1/n^{3/2}
.
Therefore, the leading order term serves as a suitable approximation of the covariance in the case of moderate noise and a reasonable number of samples.

\mathbf{H}_{0}

Optimal and Iterative Sampling

For a specific realization of the data, the posterior distribution’s information content is quantified using the Shannon entropy
[
36
,
16
]
as

\displaystyle S(\Omega;\vec{y})=-\intop\nolimits P{}\left(\theta|\vec{y}\right)\ln\big(P{}\left(\theta|\vec{y}\right)\big)\mathrm{d}\theta\,.

(16)

After invoking the Laplace approximation, the entropy reduces to

\displaystyle S(\Omega;\vec{y})=-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det(\mathbf{H}(\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}};\vec{y}))+\frac{p}{2}(1+\ln(2\pi))\,,

(17)

where
p
denotes the dimension of
\theta
.
Using the separation of terms in the Hessian from
eq.

13
and invoking Fredholm’s determinant
[
37
]
yields

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \ln\det(\mathbf{H}_{0}+\delta\mathbf{H})&=\ln\det(\mathbf{H}_{0})+\ln\det(1+\mathbf{H}_{0}^{-1}\delta\mathbf{H})\\
=\ln\det(\mathbf{H}_{0})&+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\Tr\big((\mathbf{H}_{0}^{-1}\delta\mathbf{H})^{k}\big)\,.\end{aligned}

(18)

Above series expansion exhibits a convergence radius of one and, therefore, puts an upper bound on the allowed value for
|\mathbf{H}_{0}^{-1}\delta\mathbf{H}|
for the equality to hold.
A strict condition is provided by

\displaystyle\|\delta\mathbf{H}\|\ll\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathbf{H}_{0})\,,

(19)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the operator norm
2
2

2

Because
\delta\mathbf{H}
is assumed to be symmetric, its operator norm is identical to the absolute value of its largest eigenvalue.
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}
is the smallest eigenvalue.
While the scaling of
\mathbf{H}_{0}
and
\delta\mathbf{H}
with
n
discussed in the previous section is generally favorable for the convergence, the suppression of correction terms to the entropy may fail locally if any of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}_{0}
deviates from the expected scaling, directly transpiring to
\det(\mathbf{H}_{0})
.

Assuming
eq.

19
to hold and feeding the expansion in
eq.

18
back into
eq.

17
allows to compute the expected information entropy as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Expect_{\vec{y}}S(\Omega;\vec{y})=-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det(\mathbf{H}_{0})+&\frac{p}{2}(1+\ln(2\pi))\\
&+\mathcal{O}\big(\sigma^{2}/n\|\|^{2}\big)\,,\end{aligned}

(20)

For above expansion of
\Expect_{\vec{y}}S(\Omega;\vec{y})
to yield valid results, a fixed non-linear dimension
p
, fixed number of basis functions
m
, and full column rank of the design matrix
\mathbf{\Phi}
such that
\mathbf{\Phi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}
is far from singular are required.
Similar as for the analysis following
eqs.

13
and
14
, under additional assumptions about
\theta_{0}
being an identifiable parameter value and bounded or otherwise controlled leverage of the sampling design, the leading order hessian obeys

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{0}=\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{n\|\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big)

and the leading order zero-mean noise correction follows

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{H}=\mathcal{O}_{P}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{n}\|\|}{\sigma}\Big)\,.

The expectation value then eliminates contributions linear in the noise the lowest-order surviving term in the combination of both of the above scalings in
\mathbf{H}_{0}^{-1}\delta\mathbf{H}
finally exhibits the scaling indicated in
eq.

20
.
As rule of thumb, the approximation by the noiseless hessian term is accurate either for a small number of samples as long as either the SNR is significantly larger than
1
or
n
is sufficiently large to compensate for the lack of SNR.

In addition to the statistical corrections, the expression for the expected information entropy in
eq.

20
deviates from the true value due to the Laplace approximation.
The scaling behavior of the deviation can be obtained by comparison of higher order terms in the expansion of the posterior’s logarithm and is expected to scale linearly in
\sigma/\|\|\sqrt{n}

[
38
]
.
While this error may dominate over the statistical corrections in some scenarios, the total error is still expected to be suppressed polynomially with increasing number of samples.

The optimization of the sampling scheme, or more generally the experimental design aims to maximize the information entropy which may be approximated by the leading-order term in
eq.

20
.
The monotonicity of the logarithm allows to further strip the expression to the maximization problem

\displaystyle\max_{\{t_{j}\}\in[t_{a},t_{b}]}\det\mathbf{H}_{0}\,.

(21)

Above expression weighs each sample equally and, thus, optimizes the sampling scheme for a predefined number of samples.

For selected scenarios there exists an analytic optimum while for the general case one has to resort to numerical optimization, typically including some parameter bounds or further constraints on relative distances between sampling times.
The extension of the objective function to scenarios of varying time-cost per sample requires the distinction between destructive measurements and non-destructive measurements.
The first class requires reinitialization of the system after each sample and the time cost of the experiment corresponds to the sum of each individual sampling times plus the time for read-out and initialization.
The independence of these experiments typically guarantees statistical independence of the noise terms even for samples taken at identical time difference to the respective system initialization.
The second class allows to sample at contiguous time points from the same signal and the time-cost amounts to the time required until the last sample is recorded.
Here the sampling rate is typically limited by the measurement device which may exceed the noise bandwidth and the assumption about statistical independence could break down.
In example A of
section

3
, we explore a modification of the objective function to account for repetition of identical non-destructive uniform sampling.
A generalization of the objective function as stated in
eq.

21
to weigh sampling cost individually, however, requires a fundamentally different approach to quantification of the precision and goes beyond the scope of this work.
Since the expression for the information entropy features involved maps that combine information from different sampling times, reasonable heuristic regularization terms to complement the objective function in
eq.

21
are highly non-trivial.
The similarity of the problem stated in
eq.

21
to the D-optimality criterion in the literature on optimal experimental design
[
22
]
is no coincidence but a consequence of
\mathbf{H}_{0}
approaching the Fisher information matrix in the limit of dominant likelihood (vast amount of data or uninformative prior) and, thus, solutions of the corresponding optimization problem are expected to align.

For all data following the relation in
eq.

1
, the value of
\det(\mathbf{H}_{0})
implicitly depends on the true value of the underlying signal parameters through its dependence on
\theta_{0}
.
This dependence can be explained intuitively using an example of a decaying signal with stationary noise floor.
The optimal sampling scheme requires knowledge of the signal life time to avoid regions after complete decay of the signal as these samples evidently carry no usable information.
While this dependence is easily handled in a theoretical study, it resembles an issue for the practical implementation of any optimized sampling scheme if little to no knowledge about the true parameters is available.
The problem gives rise to adaptive design optimization methods as reviewed in
[
3
]
, for which an experiment starts with a rough estimate of the parameters or even with random sampling and the parameter estimate and the experimental configuration is adjusted once data has arrived.

Figure

1
(d) schematically shows a formalization of this concept in which one or more samples are recorded alternatingly with updates of the parameter estimates and adjusting the sampling strategy until desired precision or a sampling threshold is satisfied.

Focusing on the case of one sample at a time, the ideal strategy is to choose the sampling time such that it has the highest positive impact on entropy taking into account all previous samples.
The arrival of a new sample with value
\gamma
is described by the transformation of design matrix and data vector as

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}\to\mathbf{\Phi}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\Phi}\\
\vec{\varphi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\end{pmatrix}\,,\ \text{and}\ \vec{y}\to\vec{y}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\vec{y}\\
\gamma\end{pmatrix}\,,

(22)

where
\vec{\varphi}
describes the basis functions evaluated at the next sampling time.
Inserting the expansion from
eq.

18
into
eq.

17
, the change in information entropy reads
[
15
]

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S(\Omega;\vec{y})&-S(\Omega;\vec{y}^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{2}\big(\ln\det(\mathbf{H}_{0})-\ln\det(\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime})\big)\\
&-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{2k}\Tr\big((\mathbf{H}_{0}^{-1}\delta\mathbf{H})^{k}-(\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime-1}\delta\mathbf{H}^{\prime})^{k}\big)\end{aligned}

(23)

Consequently, leading order terms of the expected information gain upon adding a sample come down to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\big[S(\Omega;\vec{y})&-S(\Omega;\vec{y}^{\prime})\big]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\ln\Big(\frac{\det(\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime})}{\det(\mathbf{H}_{0})}\Big)+\mathcal{O}_{P}\big(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\|\|\big)\,,\end{aligned}

(24)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}
denotes the expectation value over the newly acquired sample and
\mathcal{O}_{P}
describes the scaling of statistical correction terms originating from previous samples.
Corrections from second moments coming from
\mathbb{E}\gamma
scale as
\sigma^{2}/n\|\|^{2}
and are, therefore, not explicitly mentioned here.
Iteratively finding the next sample based on the greatest benefit to the estimation given all previous samples then comes down to maximization of
\det(\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime})
on some interval
[t_{a},t_{b}]
, where basis functions in
\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}
are evaluated using the current best parameter estimates for
\theta
.
For practical purposes often
t_{a}=0
and
t_{b}<\infty
and the optimum for various scenarios can very well be located on the interval boundary.
The use of the best parameter estimates in place of
\theta_{0}
technically results in the evaluation of
\mathbf{H}_{0}+\delta\mathbf{H}
instead of
\mathbf{H}_{0}
and, therefore, accounts for parts of the random corrections.
For demonstration purposes,
fig.

section

.
In addition to the expected information entropy computed from the Laplace approximation,
fig.

(a) also features the expected information entropy computed via numerical integration with the ratio of solid and dashed curves evaluated at corresponding optima in
fig.

For statistically independent samples, the noiseless Hessian typically separates into contributions from different samples and if the change constitutes a rank-1 update of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}=\mathbf{H}_{0}+\vec{v}\cdot\vec{v}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\,,

(25)

the change in entropy simplifies to
[

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\big[S(\Omega;\vec{y})&-S(\Omega;\vec{y}^{\prime})\big]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\ln\big(1+\vec{v}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{-1}\vec{v}\big)+\mathcal{O}_{P}\big(\sigma/n\|\|\big)\,.\end{aligned}

(26)

When the leading order Hessian is used as approximation to compute the estimation precision, finding the next sample comes down to a maximization of the leading term in
eq.

26
with respect to the new sampling times, which solely appears in
\vec{v}
through
\vec{\varphi}
.
The non-linear dependence of
\vec{v}
on the next sampling time, however, leaves no options but standard numerical optimization routines.
3
3

3

Knowledge of the eigenvector of
\mathbf{H}_{0}^{-1}
to the largest eigenvalue permits the reformulation as non-linear least-squares problem, but this eigenvector is generally also unknown, requiring extra computational effort before the actual optimization.

The impact of an additional data sample on the posterior’s maximum is typically smaller than the posterior’s current width and decreases with the total number of samples.
In a practical setting, the parameter update is easiest computed using a trust region-based batch optimization routine with the previous estimator as starting point.
Because the numerical optimizer is called repeatedly on barely changing data, the convergence criteria can be chosen rather loosely to reduce the number of iterations per parameter update and speed up the overall procedure.
As demonstrated in
appendix

A
, the independent statistic nature of samples admits the logarithm of the posterior distribution in the asymptotic regime to separate as

\displaystyle F(\theta;\vec{y}^{\prime})=F(\theta;\vec{y})+\Delta(\theta;\vec{y}^{\prime})\,,

(27)

with
\Delta
denoting the contribution imposed by the additional sample.
Assuming the optimizer
\theta_{0}
before the update known such that
\partial_{j}F(\theta_{0};\vec{y})=0
, the (Quasi-)Newton update step reduces to

\displaystyle\theta_{0}\to\theta_{0}-\big(\mathbf{H}^{\prime}(\theta_{0})\big)^{-1}\cdot\nabla\Delta(\theta_{0};\vec{y}^{\prime})\,.

(28)

The expression for
\mathbf{H}^{\prime}
can here easily be approximated using the leading order hessian
\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}
after updating the best guess of
\vec{a}
used to approximate the true values taking into account the newly acquired sample.
While above expression may be interesting for an analytical investigation, the well-known Levenberg-Marquardt
[

\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}^{\prime}\vec{y}^{\prime}
evaluated at
\theta_{0}
, only.

While a change in the sampling strategy may influence the rate of convergence of the estimator with increasing number of samples, the asymptotic bias remains unaffected.
Because the iterative approach may also distribute samples uniformly it is capable of matching the precision of uniform sampling.
There are some scenarios in which it is apparent, that above presented greedy algorithm for increasing number of samples asymptotically approaches the global optimal value with respect to all sampling times, e.g., in example A of

In the general case, however, greedy optimization may fail due to the same reasons as gradient-based optimizers, such as local optima, aliasing, and poor initial estimates.

Asymptotic Limit Posterior

The marginal distribution in
eq.

7
represents an involved expression for a wide variety of basis functions and prior distributions on
\theta
.
Some simple general statements are possible, nevertheless, when the posterior is treated in the context of what is henceforth referred to as the asymptotic limit.
The asymptotic limit describes the case of large signal to noise ratio
\|\|/\sigma
or a large number of data samples or both.
While these assumptions above sound like a stringent criterion, they typically are satisfied whenever a human observer can identify a signal pattern in noisy data with the bare eye.
4
4

4

Being able to see a signal or pattern in the data is typically a minimal requirement to convince researchers of the existence of a signal.
Performing parameter estimation using a signal whose existence is uncertain in most cases yields unsatisfying results and we do our best to avoid this regime.

Under these conditions, it is also sufficient to assume an uninformative prior on both,
\vec{a}
and
\theta
.
The expression for the posterior is then well approximated by dropping terms independent of
\vec{y}
in

\displaystyle-\ln P{}\left(\theta|\vec{y}\right)\approx\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\vec{y}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{\Phi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi})^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})\vec{y}\,.

(29)

This expression can be compactly rewritten by noting that
\mathbf{P}(\theta)=\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{\Phi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi})^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
describes the projector onto the range of
\mathbf{\Phi}
and introducing the orthogonal projection
\mathbf{P}_{\perp}=\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{P}
such that

\displaystyle-\ln P{}\left(\theta|\vec{y}\right)\approx\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mathbf{P}_{\perp}\vec{y}\|^{2}=:F_{\mathrm{as}}(\theta;\vec{y})\,.

(30)

Above expression is well-known as the Variable Projection objective function
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
which is obtained upon solving the LS problem to estimate
\vec{a}
assuming fixed
\theta
and plugging the result back into the objective function for the non-linear LS problem.
Its emergence from marginalization of a normal distribution has been noted in the literature and is not trivial but a consequence of the Gaussian structure of the posterior with respect to the linear parameters
[
44
]
.

For further insights into the estimation behavior of
F_{\mathrm{as}}
, the expression for
F_{\mathrm{as}}
from
eq.

30
is used to evaluate the defining equation
eq.

12a
to find

\displaystyle 0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}\partial_{j}F_{\mathrm{as}}^{(0)}(\theta_{0})=\frac{2}{2\sigma^{2}}(\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}(\theta_{0}))^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\partial_{j}\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}(\theta_{0}))\,.

(31)

The projection
\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}(\theta_{0})
trivially yields zero if
\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}
is constructed using
\mathbf{\Phi}(\theta_{0})=
.
Therefore, above equation is satisfied if
\theta_{0}
is chosen at the true value and the estimator proves consistent to leading order under identifiability and regularity assumptions and asymptotically approaches the true value.
Matrix elements of the leading order Hessian
\mathbf{H}_{0}
for

required to compute the correction to the estimator as in

12b
and the leading order covariance matrix in

reduce as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{k}\partial_{j}F_{\mathrm{as}}^{(0)}()&=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\partial_{k}\big[(\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}())^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\partial_{j}\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}())\big]\\
\hfil\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(\partial_{k}\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}())^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\partial_{j}\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}())\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(\underbrace{\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}()}_{=0})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\partial_{k}\partial_{j}\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}())\end{split}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(\partial_{k}\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}())^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\partial_{j}\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}())\,.\end{aligned}

(32)

Rewriting the derivative of
\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}

[

]
evaluated for the true parameters simplifies above expression further to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{j}\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}()\vec{a}&=-\big[\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}\partial_{j}\mathbf{\Phi}(){{}^{\dagger}}+{(\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}\partial_{j}\mathbf{\Phi}()^{\dagger})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}\big]\\
&=-\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}\partial_{j}\mathbf{\Phi}\ \Big\rvert_{\theta=}\,.\end{split}

(33)

The final expression then compactly reads

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{0}=\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{L}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}

(34)

with
\mathbf{L}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\big(\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}\partial_{1},\ \mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}\partial_{2},\dots\big)
.

To gain some understanding of the nature of above Hessian, consider a scenario of one-dimensional
\theta
and a single basis function
\vec{\varphi}
.
The only element of the Hessian matrix then reduces to

\displaystyle h_{1}=\frac{{}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\|\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}\partial_{j}\vec{\varphi}()\|^{2}\,.

(35)

The vector whose norm squared determines the precision explicitly reads

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}\partial_{j}\vec{\varphi}()=\Big[\partial_{j}\vec{\varphi}-\frac{\vec{\varphi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\partial_{j}\vec{\varphi}}{\vec{\varphi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\vec{\varphi}}\vec{\varphi}\Big]_{\theta=}

(36)

and describes the component of

orthogonal to
\vec{\varphi}
as, e.g., obtained from the Gram-Schmidt procedure.
In conclusion, the precision is governed by how much the derivative diverts the basis function from the basis function itself.
Generalized to multiple basis functions, the precision is given by the projection of the weighted derivative
\partial_{j}\mathbf{\Phi}()
onto the orthogonal range of
\mathbf{\Phi}
.

The evaluation of the leading order expression for the Hessian in
eq.

requires knowledge of the true parameter values for
\theta
and
\vec{a}
, admitting theoretical studies of the optimal sampling approach.
With increasing amount of data, however, estimates of
\theta
and
\vec{a}
derived from numerical optimization of

and the linear LS problem, respectively, are sufficiently accurate such that

evaluated with the estimates yields an accurate approximation of the Hessian for uncertainty estimation.
Substitution of the ordinary LS estimates for
\vec{a}
from
\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}\vec{y}
recovers the well-known approximation to the Hessian matrix used in the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm
[
40
]

As demonstrated in
Appendix

separates as in

given by

\displaystyle\big[\vec{v}\big]_{j}=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{1+\vec{u}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\vec{u}}}\begin{pmatrix}\vec{u}\\
-1\end{pmatrix}^{\intercal}\cdot\vec{x}_{j}^{\prime}\,,

(37)

where
\vec{u}=(\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\vec{\varphi}
and
\vec{x}_{j}^{\prime}=\partial_{j}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\prime}()
.
The full matrix element of the leading order Hessian then reads

\displaystyle\big[\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}\big]_{ij}=\big[\mathbf{H}_{0}\big]_{ij}+\frac{(\vec{u}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\vec{x}_{i}-\eta_{i})(\vec{u}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\vec{x}_{j}-\eta_{j})}{\sigma^{2}(\vec{u}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\vec{u}+1)}\,,

(38)

where

While it is possible to isolate the contribution from the previous samples, some of the information is still explicitly contained in the update through
\mathbf{\Phi}

This behavior explicitly distinguishes the procedure described here from Bayesian Updating for conjugate priors, where information in accumulated representation combined with the new data suffices to fully define the posterior after the update.

Uniform Sampling

The derivation of approximate expression for the estimation uncertainty for frequency and decay rate of an oscillating signal can be simplified upon making the transition to natural units of time.
Explicitly, the parameters
\omega

Starting from

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{\Phi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&\mathbf{\Phi}=\\
&\sum\limits_{t=0}^{n-1}\begin{pmatrix}\cos^{2}(\omega t)\mathrm{e}^{-2\kappa t}&\cos(\omega t)\sin(\omega t)\mathrm{e}^{-2\kappa t}\\
\cos(\omega t)\sin(\omega t)\mathrm{e}^{-2\kappa t}&\sin^{2}(\omega t)\mathrm{e}^{-2\kappa t}\end{pmatrix}\end{split}

For any reasonable experimental setup that allows to unequivocally identify an oscillating signal as shown in

Non-Uniform Sampling and the Iterative Approach

Because of the periodic nature of oscillating signals, the exact distribution of the samples for selected configurations greatly impacts the estimation performance.
In the most severe case, all samples coincide with roots of the signal and the observer hardly observes any signal variation at all.
The matrix
\mathbf{\Phi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}
becomes near singular and the precision quantified by the Hessian evaluates to values barely larger than zero.
On the other hand, small variation around the signal roots typically exhibit the greatest change in the samples because of the gradient at these locations being maximal.
The discussions following
eqs.

35
and
36
, thus, hint at samples at these locations playing an important role for the optimal sampling.
Therefore, the optimal sample distribution is highly nontrivial even for known frequency and phase and general statements are hardly possible.
While estimation performance for stationary oscillating signals always benefits from covering a larger overall time interval
[
35
]
, sampling of exponentially decaying signals is expected to exhibit a sweet spot, again.
Using the determinant of the Hessian matrix to quantify the expected performance and recalling the formula for the determinant of a
2\times 2
matrix, the optimization is expected to yield a sampling scheme that maximizes the precision of individual parameters while keeping the correlations minimal.

Multiple frequencies

The approach presented in this work can readily be generalized to multiple different frequencies and decay rates, albeit quickly increasing the complexity in the general case.
The literature concluded that for large separation of individual frequencies the basis functions are approximately orthogonal and the estimation procedure separates
[
35
]
.
This argument readily applies also to exponentially decaying signals as long as the basis functions are sufficiently orthogonal taking into account the exponential decay
[
46
]
.
For uniform sampling, this requirement is safely achieved if the two signal components achieve a phase shift of at least
\pi
before either of the signals vanish, compactly expressed through

\displaystyle|_{1}-_{2}|/\max(_{1},_{2})\gtrsim\pi\,.

Above condition is practically satisfied as soon as the resonances can clearly be identified as separate frequencies after discrete Fourier transformation.
Using the posterior volume measure for precision quantization, the iterative procedure suppresses correlation between different parameters and, therefore, is expected to optimize the orthogonality under discrete sampling.
The exact strategy for this procedure is highly non-trivial and may vastly vary for exact parameter values present.
It is not obvious if non-uniform sampling is more efficient in establishing orthogonality in the sense of requiring fewer samples.
Numerical experiments demonstrate that optimized sampling, again, weighs different signal regions differently, but do not reveal any trivial strategy
[
24
]
.

4. Discussion

Approximative evaluation of the information for two examples demonstrates and quantifies the potential improvement over uniform sampling of
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}20\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
.
The explicit dependence of the sampling schemes of true underlying signal parameters is addressed through the application of adaptive sampling schemes, achieving improvements over uniform sampling on similar improvements levels.
The overall scaling laws in the reduction of estimation uncertainty with increasing number of samples remain untouched.

Some of the results presented with the examples in
section

3
coincide with results obtained from optimal experimental design based on the Fisher information matrix
[
20
,
22
,
23
]
, thereby confirming the validity.
The Bayesian approach employed in this work, in contrast, makes use of the Shannon entropy for the quantification of estimation quality and allows for extension by additional prior distributions on the non-linear parameters.
While the principles of adaptive design optimization have been extensively discussed in the literature, to knowledge of the authors there is no explicit example that applies the ansatz to marginal distributions and addresses the consequences.

The schemes presented in this work focus on scenarios in which the cost of each sample is weighted equally.
There are various extensions of the objective function as in
eq.

21
possible to account for varying cost, e.g., for samples that require longer waiting times until the measurement.
One of these options has been explored in example A, where the noise parameter
\sigma
has been adjusted to account for repetition of sampling at the same locations.
Compact formulations for individual weighting of samples in the general, however, require further efforts that are outside the scope of this work.

The simplification undertaken in
eq.

30
under what we called the asymptotic limit represents a strong approximation that renders inaccurate in scenarios of very poor statistics, i.e., number of samples on the order of free model parameters and low SNR, and Markov-Chain Monte-Carlo sampling is the preferred choice for an accurate representation of the posterior distribution.
In scenarios that permit the use of adaptive schemes, however, the acquisition of samples continuously improves the accuracy of the approximation, eventually moving into the asymptotic limit.
For the examples considered in this work, in particular, the approximation turns out simple and effective.

Uniform sampling generally has the advantage of requiring little to zero prior knowledge about the expected signal structure and the obtained data is particularly accessible for interpretation by human researchers.
The strong dependence of optimized sampling schemes require a predefined signal model and some prior knowledge about the expected parameter values, such that they are most effective for routine experimental setups that are repeated various times.
Therefore, the framework and derived schemes in this work are, for example, particularly relevant to calibration procedures and experiments with a high repetition rate of similar experiments for reliable statistics, such as for anomaly detection.

Appendix A
Update of the Asymptotic Posterior Distribution and Parameter Estimates

In the fundamental Bayesian update scheme assuming statistically independent data, the posterior distribution is updated upon multiplication by the likelihood for new observations.
From the form of the posterior in
eq.

7
it becomes apparent, that despite statistically independent observations, the posterior does not separate into previous and new samples after marginalization any more.
Formally, the addition of a new data sample is described by the transformation

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}\to\mathbf{\Phi}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\Phi}\\
\vec{\varphi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\end{pmatrix}\ \ \text{ and }\ \vec{y}\to\vec{y}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\vec{y}\\
\gamma\end{pmatrix}\,,

(A.1)

respectively, where
\vec{\varphi}
describes the basis functions evaluated at the new time point and
\gamma
is the newly acquired sample.

To compute the impact onto the orthogonal projector
\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}^{\prime}
, recall that the projector
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}
is computed using the updated
\mathbf{\Phi}^{\prime}
explicitly as

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{\prime}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{\prime}\big(\mathbf{\Phi}^{\prime\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\prime}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\prime\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\,.

(A.2)

Noting that the update to
\mathbf{\Phi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}
can be written as a rank-1 update, application of the Sherman-Morrison formula
[
47
]
yields

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \big(\mathbf{\Phi}^{\prime\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\prime}\big)^{-1}&=\big(\mathbf{\Phi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}+\vec{\varphi}\vec{\varphi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\big)^{-1}\\
&=\big(\mathbf{\Phi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}\big)^{-1}-\frac{(\mathbf{\Phi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi})^{-1}\vec{\varphi}\vec{\varphi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\big(\mathbf{\Phi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}\big)^{-1}}{1+\vec{\varphi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\big(\mathbf{\Phi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}\big)^{-1}\vec{\varphi}}\,.\end{aligned}

(A.3)

Introducing the vector

\displaystyle\vec{u}:=\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{\Phi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi})^{-1}\vec{\varphi}

(A.4)

and the scalar

\displaystyle\alpha:=\vec{\varphi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{\Phi}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi})^{-1}\vec{\varphi}\,,

(A.5)

the projector from
eq.

A.2
may be rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{P}^{\prime}&=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{P}&\vec{u}\\
\vec{u}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&\alpha\end{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}\vec{u}\vec{u}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&\alpha\vec{u}\\
\alpha\vec{u}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&\alpha^{2}\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{P}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}-\vec{u}\vec{u}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&\vec{u}\\
\vec{u}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\,.\end{aligned}

(A.6)

The orthogonal projector from
\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}^{\prime}=\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{P}^{\prime}
then likewise separates into previous and new contributions as

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}\vec{u}\vec{u}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&-\vec{u}\\
-\vec{u}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(A.7)

To compute the value of
F_{\mathrm{as}}\varpropto\|\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}^{\prime}\vec{y}^{\prime}\|^{2}
after the update, note that the product of the two terms contribution to above
\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}^{\prime}
yields zero, such that the final result separates into a contribution from previous samples and an additional term.
Algebraic simplification and noting that
\vec{u}=(\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\vec{\phi}
ultimately yields

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}^{\prime}\vec{y}^{\prime}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}\vec{y}\|^{2}+\frac{\big(\vec{\phi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}\vec{y}-\gamma\big)^{2}}{1+\alpha}\,.

(A.8)

The addition of a new sample extends the previous value by the squared distance between the best guess for the signal value and the sample value
\gamma
weighted by
1/(1+\alpha)
.
Thus, applied explicitly to the posterior distribution in the asymptotic limit yields

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{as}}(\omega;\vec{y}^{\prime})=F_{\mathrm{as}}(\omega;\vec{y})+\Delta(\omega;\vec{y}^{\prime})\,,

(A.9)

with
\Delta
representing the second term of
eq.

A.8
multiplied by
1/2\sigma^{2}
.
Assuming the posterior distribution to be governed by the exponential of above expression, the distribution separates into a product of the previous distribution and an update term.
Previously eliminated linear parameters
\vec{a}
take the role of latent variables that relate all individual data samples, such that the update term itself explicitly depends on all previously observed samples and, therefore, does not follow the ideal case for Bayesian updating.
The requirement to keep book of all previous observations to compute the update to the posterior makes plainly recomputing the posterior distribution taking into account all new samples the preferred method in many practical scenarios.

To compute the update to the leading order Hessian, recall from
eq.

34
that the contribution to the matrix elements of the Hessian at location of the local maximum featuring the sampling points is computed from

\displaystyle(\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}\vec{x}_{j})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\cdot(\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}\vec{x}_{k})\,,

(A.10)

with
\vec{x}_{j}=\partial_{j}\mathbf{\Phi}
.
The addition of a sample adds
\eta_{j}=(\partial_{j}\vec{\varphi})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\vec{a}
as additional component to
\vec{x}_{j}
and transforms
\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}
to
\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}^{\prime}
as in
eq.

A.7
.
Now noting that the symmetric projector satisfies
(\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}^{\prime})^{2}=\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}^{\prime}
and recalling that
\alpha
can be rewritten as
\alpha=\vec{u}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\vec{u}
, the insertion of
\mathbf{P}{}_{\perp}^{\prime}
into
eq.

A.10
leads to the expression in
eq.

38
of the main text.

Optimal Sampling Configuration for the Exponentially Decaying Signal

The optimal sampling configuration for an exponentially decaying signal is provided with the global maximum of
\phi
with

\displaystyle\phi(\{t_{j}\})=S_{2}-S_{1}^{2}/S_{0}

(C.11)

where
S_{l}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}^{l}\mathrm{e}^{-2\kappa t_{j}}
and
t_{j}\geq 0
for

Let the function
\phi
be as in
eq.

C.11
.
The maximum of
\phi
is achieved with each

assuming either of
t=0
and

\displaystyle t^{\ast}=\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{S_{1}}{S_{0}}\,.

Note that
\phi
is continuous in each variable
t_{j}\geq 0
and bounded for finite

.
To argue that
\phi
exhibits a global maximum on a compact set

t_{j}
but the one with index

and show that there exists a point

for which

assume a value larger than for taking

Separating out contributions from

(C.13)

where the tilde indicates quantities evaluated using only all remaining

Using above expression, one finds

and assumes

Because
