Introduction

Traditionally, logic is concerned with relations between
statements
—declarative sentences such as “Alice is a student” or “all students passed the test”. Inquisitive logic
(
6
;
14
)
is an approach that aims to extend the scope of logic to cover not only statements, but also
questions
—interrogative sentences such as “whether Alice is a student” and “which students passed the test”.
This extension is interesting for several reasons: it allows us to regard interesting logical relations such as
answerhood
and
dependency
as facets of the key logical notion of
entailment
, and to deploy the tools traditionally developed for entailment to study these notions
(
12
;
14
)
; it makes it possible to give a uniform account of the contribution of connectives and quantifiers across statements and questions
(
6
)
; it enables us to perform inferences with formulas denoting information
types
, rather than specific pieces of information
(
13
;
32
)
; it allows us to define modal operators that can be applied to questions, enabling a general account of knowledge ascriptions as well as a modal account of inquisitive attitudes such as
wondering

(
9
)
.
Since its development, inquisitive logic has found many applications in different domains, including natural language semantics
(
16
;
1
;
38
;
39
;
10
;
3
;
37
, see, among many others,)
, cognitive psychology
(
28
;
29
;
30
)
, formal epistemology
(
15
)
and decision theory
(
17
)
.

Syntactically, inquisitive logics are typically obtained by taking an existing logic of statements, and extending it with question-forming operators. Semantically, they are interpreted in standard possible-world models for intensional logics. In order to give a uniform account of statements and questions, however, the basic semantic notion of
truth at a world
is replaced by a notion of
support at an information state
, where an information state (also called simply a
state
) is modeled as a set of possible worlds. For a statement, to be supported at a state is to be true at each world in the state; for a question, to be supported is to be
settled
in the state, i.e., to have an answer.

1

The move from single worlds to sets of worlds mirrors a parallel move made in the framework of
team semantics

(
24
)
, where variable assignments are replaced by sets of assignments. Team semantics has been used to define a variety of logics of dependence and independence, such as dependence logic
(
40
)
, inclusion logic
(
20
)
, and independence logic
(
21
)
. The similarity of the two semantic frameworks accounts for deep and systematic connections between inquisitive logics and team-based logics
(
42
;
41
;
12
;
14
;
7
, for discussion see, among others,)
.

The two most widely studied inquisitive logic systems are
inquisitive propositional logic
,
InqB
, and
inquisitive first-order logic
,
InqBQ

(
14
, for an introduction, see)
. The former extends classical propositional logic with a question-forming connective

\!{\geqslant}

, called
inquisitive disjunction
. Thus, in this system one has, in addition to formulas regimenting statements like “it’s raining” (
r
) and “it’s snowing” (
s
), also formulas regimenting the questions “whether it’s raining” (
r\,\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\!{\geqslant}$}\,\neg r
, abbreviated
?r
) and “whether it’s raining or snowing” (
r\,\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\!{\geqslant}$}\,s
). In a similar vein, the first-order system
InqBQ
extends classical first-order logic with

\!{\geqslant}

and with an inquisitive existential quantifier,
\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}
. In this system one has, in addition to standard first-order formulas like
\forall xPx
regimenting statements like “all students passed the test”, also formulas like
?\forall xPx
,
\forall x?Px
, and
\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}xPx
regimenting, respectively, the questions “whether all students passed the test”, “which students passed the test”, and “who is one student who passed the test”. Entailments in this logic capture interesting logical relations between types of information: for instance, the fact that the formulas
\exists xPx
and
\forall x?Px
jointly entail
\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}xPx
captures the fact that, given the information that an object with a certain property exists and a specification of which objects have that property, one is then in a position to give an example of an object with the relevant property.

Inquisitive propositional logic is by now very well-understood: an axiomatization is known
(
8
)
, as well as several other proof systems
(
19
;
4
;
13
;
31
)
;
InqB
has been systematically related to intermediate logics
(
11
;
8
)
, and weakenings based on non-classical logics of statements have been studied extensively
(
33
;
34
;
35
;
7
)
; algebraic approaches have been developed
(
2
;
36
)
, and properties such as structural completeness and interpolation have been investigated
(
25
;
18
)
.

By contrast, ever since its first introduction in
11
, inquisitive first-order logic has remained rather mysterious, even with regards to its fundamental meta-theoretic properties. In particular, in spite of many investigations, the following fundamental questions are long-standing open problems:

•

Open Question 1: Entailment-compactness

Is
InqBQ
-entailment compact, in the sense that whenever a conclusion
\psi
follows from a set of premises
\Phi
, it also follows from a finite subset
\Phi_{0}\subseteq\Phi
?

•

Open Question 2: Recursive enumerability

Is the set of validities of
InqBQ
recursively enumerable? Equivalently, does
InqBQ
admit a recursive axiomatization?

We may summarize the situation by saying that, at present, we do not know whether
InqBQ
is first-order-logic-like or second-order-logic-like with respect to its fundamental meta-theoretic properties.

Positive answers to the two questions above have been obtained for two important fragments of
InqBQ
: the
clant
(classical antecedent) fragment, where antecedents of implications are restricted to classical first-order formulas
(
23
)
; and the
rex
(restricted existential) fragment, where the inquisitive existential can appear only within the antecedent of an implication
(
5
)
. For the whole system
InqBQ
, however, the above questions are still open.

In this paper, we provide an answer to these long-standing open questions. We show that
InqBQ
is
not
entailment compact and
not
recursively axiomatizable. In other words,
InqBQ
is second-order-like with respect to its meta-theoretic properties.

Our proof strategy is simple. Building on ideas we explored in recent unpublished work
(
27
)
, we identify a sentence
\eta
that, relative to a certain class of “full” models, expresses the finiteness of the domain. While the class of full information models is not definable in
InqBQ
, its complement is: in other words, there is a sentence
\theta
capturing non-fullness. We can then produce a violation of entailment-compactness as follows: consider the set
\Phi
containing
\eta
along with each first-order sentence
\chi_{n}
expressing that there are at least
n
objects for
n\in\mathbb{N}
; this set can only be satisfied in a model which is not full, and so it entails
\theta
; but each finite subset
\Phi_{0}\subseteq\Phi
can be satisfied in a full model, and so it does not entail
\theta
.

Using the formulas
\eta
and
\theta
, we can also settle Open Question 2 by reducing the problem of first-order logic validity over finite structures to the problem of validity in
InqBQ
: a first-order sentence
\alpha
is valid over the set of finite structures if and only if the sentence
\eta\to\alpha\,\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\!{\geqslant}$}\,\theta
is valid in
InqBQ
(more precisely, in the version of
InqBQ
with rigid identity, as we will explain). Since the set of first-order validities over finite models is not recursively enumerable, neither is the set of
InqBQ
-validities.

Our paper thus settles the two main open problems in the area of inquisitive first-order logic, while also opening new interesting ones; in addition, our proof strategy provides ideas that may well find broader applicability in the study of this logic.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we cover the necessary background on
InqBQ
. In Section
3
we show that
InqBQ
is not entailment-compact, settling Open Question 1. In Section
4
we show that
InqBQ
-validities are not recursively enumerable, settling Open Question 2. Section
5
summarizes our results and discusses further research directions.

2
Background

This section provides the necessary background on
InqBQ
. For a more detailed introduction to this logic, and for proofs of the results, we refer to
14
.

Syntax.

Just as in standard predicate logic, a signature
\Sigma
for
InqBQ
consists of a set of predicate symbols and a set of function symbols, each with a corresponding arity
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Function symbols, however, come in two kinds:
rigid
and
non-rigid
. Function symbols of arity 0 are called
constant symbols
. The set of
terms
is defined inductively from first-order variables and function symbols in the usual way.

Formulas of
InqBQ
are given by the following BNF definition, where
P
is an
n
-ary predicate symbol and
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
as well as
t,t^{\prime}
are terms:

\varphi\;:=\;P(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\mid(t=t^{\prime})\mid\bot\mid(\varphi\land\varphi)\mid(\varphi\,\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\!{\geqslant}$}\,\varphi)\mid(\varphi\to\varphi)\mid\forall x\varphi\mid\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}x\varphi

Formulas that do not contain

\!{\geqslant}

or
\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}
are called
classical formulas
. The set of classical formulas can be identified with the language of standard first-order predicate logic, with a particular choice of primitives. The remaining operators can be defined in the following standard way:

•

\neg\varphi:=\varphi\to\bot

•

\top:=\neg\bot

•

(\varphi\lor\psi):=\neg(\neg\varphi\land\neg\psi)

•

\exists x\varphi:=\neg\forall x\neg\varphi

As usual, we abbreviate
\neg(t=t^{\prime})
as
(t\neq t^{\prime})
. Following standard practice in inquisitive logic, a unary question-mark operator is defined as follows:

?\varphi:=(\varphi\,\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\!{\geqslant}$}\,\neg\varphi)

We use
\varphi,\psi,\chi
as meta-variables ranging over all formulas of
InqBQ
, and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
as meta-variables ranging over classical formulas.

Models.

A
first-order information model
is a quadruple
M=(W,D,I,\sim)
where:

•

W
, the
universe
, is a non-empty set whose elements are called
worlds
;

•

D
, the
domain
, is a non-empty set whose elements are called
individuals
;

•

I
equips each world
w
with an interpretation function
I_{w}
, which assigns

–

to each
n
-ary predicate symbol
P
, an
n
-ary relation
I_{w}(P)\subseteq D^{n}
;

–

to each
n
-ary function symbol
f
, an
n
-ary function
I_{w}(f):D^{n}\to D
.

For convenience, we also write
P_{w}
or
f_{w}
instead of
I_{w}(P)
and
I_{w}(f)
.

•

\sim
equips each world
w
with an equivalence relation
\sim_{w}\subseteq D\times D
which is a congruence relative to the interpretations of the relation and function symbols, i.e., such that if
d_{1}\sim_{w}d_{1}^{\prime},\;\dots\;,d_{n}\sim_{w}d_{n}^{\prime}
then:

–

for any
n
-ary predicate
R
:
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})\in R_{w}\iff(d_{1}^{\prime},\dots,d_{n}^{\prime})\in R_{w}
;

–

for any
n
-ary function symbol
f
:
f(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})\sim_{w}f(d_{1}^{\prime},\dots,d_{n}^{\prime})
.

Note that to each world
w
in such a model
M
we can assign a standard relational structure
\mathcal{M}_{w}=(D/{\sim_{w}},I_{w}^{\sim})
for the signature
\Sigma
, whose domain is the quotient of
D
relative to the local equivalence relation
\sim_{w}
, and those interpretation function
I_{w}^{\sim}
is the one induced on the quotient by
I_{w}
.

Models where the equivalence relation
\sim_{w}
is simply the identity relation
id_{D}
for each world are called id-models. In an id-model, the relational structure
\mathcal{M}_{w}
associated with a world is simply the pair
(D,I_{w})
; an id-model can thus be seen simply as a family
(\mathcal{M}_{w})_{w\in W}
of standard relational structures over a common domain.

Semantics.

An
information state
(or simply a
state
, for short) in a model
M=(W,D,I,\sim)
is a set of worlds
s\subseteq W
. As usual, an assignment is a function from the set of first-order variables into
D
. If
g
is an assignment,
x
is a variable, and
d\in D
, we denote by
g[x\mapsto d]
the assignment that maps
x
to
d
and coincides with
g
on the remaining variables. The denotation of a term
t
relative to a world
w
and an assignment
g
, denoted
[t]_{w}^{g}
, is defined inductively by

[x]_{w}^{g}=g(x),\qquad\qquad[f(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})]_{w}^{g}=f_{w}([t_{1}]_{w}^{g},\dots,[t_{1}]_{w}^{g}).

As discussed in the introduction, the semantics of
InqBQ
is given in terms of a relation of
support
which is assessed relative to information states. Given a first-order information model
M=(W,D,I,\sim)
, a state
s\subseteq W
, an assignment
g
from the set of variables into
D
, we define support by the following clauses:

•

M,s\models_{g}P(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\iff\forall w\in s:([t_{1}]_{w}^{g},\dots,[t_{n}]_{w}^{g})\in P_{w}

•

M,s\models_{g}t=t^{\prime}\iff\forall w\in s:[t]_{w}^{g}\sim_{w}[t^{\prime}]_{w}^{g}

•

M,s\models_{g}\bot\iff s=\emptyset

•

M,s\models_{g}\varphi\land\psi\iff M,s\models_{g}\varphi
and
M,s\models_{g}\psi

•

M,s\models_{g}\varphi\,\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\!{\geqslant}$}\,\psi\iff M,s\models_{g}\varphi
or
M,s\models_{g}\psi

•

M,s\models_{g}\varphi\to\psi\iff\forall t\subseteq s:M,t\models_{g}\varphi
implies
M,t\models_{g}\psi

•

M,s\models_{g}\forall x\varphi\iff\forall d\in D:M,s\models_{g[x\mapsto d]}\varphi

•

M,s\models_{g}\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}x\varphi\iff\exists d\in D:M,s\models_{g[x\mapsto d]}\varphi

We write simply
M\models_{g}\varphi
to mean
M,W\models_{g}\varphi
.

The support relation is persistent, i.e., if
M,s\models_{g}\varphi
and
t\subseteq s
, then
M,t\models_{g}\varphi
. Moreover, the empty state trivially support any formula. As usual, the choice of the assignment
g
does not matter in the case of sentences and closed terms, and we can thus omit reference to it.

Entailment, validity, and equivalence.

The logical notions of entailment, validity, and equivalence are defined in the obvious way:

•

A set of formulas
\Phi

entails
a formula
\psi
, denoted
\Phi\models\psi
, if for any model
M
, state
s
and assignment
g
, if
M,s\models_{g}\varphi
for all
\varphi\in\Phi
, then
M,s\models_{g}\psi
.

•

A formula
\psi
is
valid
, denoted
\models\psi
, if it is entailed by the empty set, i.e., if it is supported relative to every model, state, and assignment.

•

Two formulas
\varphi
and
\psi
are
equivalent
, denoted
\varphi\equiv\psi
, if for every model
M
, state
s
, and assignment
g
we have
M,s\models_{g}\varphi\iff M,s\models_{g}\psi
.

Corresponding notions of id-entailment (
\Phi\models_{\text{id}}\psi
), id-validity (
\Phi\models_{\text{id}}\psi
), and id-equivalence (
\varphi\equiv_{\text{id}}\psi
) are defined by restricting the class of models to id-models, i.e., models where ‘=’ is interpreted as the identity relation on the domain.

Truth and conservativity.

Although the primitive semantic notion for
InqBQ
is support at a state, a notion of truth at a world is recovered by defining truth at a world
w
as support with respect to the corresponding singleton
\{w\}
:

M,w\models_{g}\varphi\overset{\text{def}}{\iff}M,\{w\}\models_{g}\varphi

The following proposition ensures that, for classical formulas, the resulting truth-conditions coincide with the familiar ones.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
M
be a model for
InqBQ
,
w
a world, and
g
an assignment. For any classical formula
\alpha
we have

M,w\models_{g}\alpha\iff\mathcal{M}_{w}\models_{g}\alpha

where the expression on the right denotes satisfaction in standard Tarskian semantics.

For some formulas
\varphi
, support simply boils down to global truth, in the sense that for every model
M
, state
s
, and assignment
g
we have

M,s\models_{g}\varphi\iff\forall w\in s:M,w\models_{g}\varphi

If this is the case, we say that the formula
\varphi
is truth-conditional. The following proposition ensures that, up to equivalence, the truth-conditional formulas of
InqBQ
are exactly the classical formulas.

Proposition 2.2
.

A formula of
InqBQ
is truth-conditional if and only if it is equivalent to a classical formula. In particular, classical formulas are truth-conditional.

Together, propositions
2.1
and
2.2
imply that with respect to classical formulas, our semantics is essentially equivalent to standard Tarskian semantics. As a consequence, for classical formulas entailment simply amounts to entailment is classical first-order logic.

Proposition 2.3

(Conservativity)
.

If
\Gamma
is a set of classical formulas and
\alpha
is a classical formula, we have

\Gamma\models\alpha\iff\Gamma\models_{\textsf{FOL}}\alpha

where
\models_{\textsf{FOL}}
denotes entailment in classical first-order logic.

Questions.

In contrast to classical formulas, formulas involving the inquisitive operators

\!{\geqslant}

and
\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}
are typically not truth-conditional. For them, support at a state does not reduce to a local condition on the individual worlds in the state, but is instead a global matter. In this section, we introduce some particular examples of such formulas that play a key role in the following.

Example 2.4

(Mention-all questions)
.

Let
P
be an
n
-ary predicate and let
\overline{x}
be a sequence of
n
variables. The sentence
\forall\overline{x}?P(\overline{x})
is supported in a state iff
P
has the same extension at each world in the state:

M,s\models_{g}\forall\overline{x}?P(\overline{x})\iff\forall w,w^{\prime}\in s:P_{w}=P_{w^{\prime}}

Intuitively,
\forall\overline{x}?P(\overline{x})
captures the question “which objects (or tuples) satisfy
P
”, a
mention-all
question that asks for a complete specification of the extension of
P
.

In particular, the formula
\forall x\forall y?(x=y)
expresses the rigidity of equality, i.e., fact that the equality predicate ‘=’ is interpreted in the same way at each world in the evaluation state. This formula is obviously valid in id-models, where ‘=’ is interpreted rigidly as the identity relation, but not in the general case. In fact, the following result says that this principle fully characterizes the logic of id-models relative to the logic of all first-order information models: a claim of id-entailment is equivalent to a claim of general entailment with the extra premise that equality is rigid
(
14
, for a proof, see Proposition 5.5.31 in)
.

Proposition 2.5

(General entailment and id-entailment)
.

For any set of formulas
\Phi
and any formula
\psi
:

\Phi\models_{\text{id}}\psi\iff\Phi,\forall x\forall y?(x=y)\models\psi

This connection will play a key role below: we will prove our key results in the simpler setting of id-models, and then extend them to the general case by means of this connection.

Example 2.6

(Mention-some questions)
.

Let
P
be an
n
-ary predicate and let
\overline{x}
be a sequence of
n
variables. The sentence
\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}\overline{x}P(\overline{x})
is supported in a state iff some particular tuple
\overline{d}
satisfies
P
in every world in the state:

M,s\models_{g}\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}\overline{x}P(\overline{x})\iff\exists\overline{d}\in D^{n}\forall w\in s:\overline{d}\in P_{w}

Intuitively,
\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}xP(\overline{x})
captures the question “what is an example of an object (or tuple) satisfying
P
”, a so-called
mention-some
question that asks for an instance of
P
.

Example 2.7

(Identification questions)
.

For a term
t
, define corresponding formulas
\lambda t
and
\mu t
as follows:

\lambda t\;:=\;\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}x(x=t)\qquad\qquad\mu t\;:=\;\forall x?(x=t)

where
x
is an arbitrarily chosen variable not occurring in
t
.
2
2

2

This definition leaves
\lambda t
partly unspecified. This underspecification is irrelevant for our purposes, but for the sake of definiteness, we could assume that variables are ordered as
x_{0},x_{1},\dots
, and
\lambda t
is defined by using the
least
variable not occurring in
t
.

Relative to id-models, these formulas are equivalent: they are both supported in a state if the interpretation of
t
is constant throughout the state. More formally, if
M
is an id-model,
s
a state, and
g
an assignment, we have:

M,s\models_{g}\lambda t\iff M,s\models_{g}\mu t\iff\forall w,w^{\prime}\in s:[t]_{w}^{g}=[t]_{w^{\prime}}^{g}

Intuitively,
\lambda t
and
\mu t
are two ways of capturing the identification question “what is
t
”, which asks to pin down the denotation of the term.

Dependencies.

InqBQ
provides us with a systematic way of expressing dependencies as implications among questions: intuitively, if
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
are formulas representing the questions
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
respectively, then the implication
\varphi\to\varphi^{\prime}
is supported in a state in case, relative to that state, any answer to
Q
yields a corresponding answer to
Q^{\prime}
. We introduce a special case of dependence formulas that plays a key role below.

If
\overline{t}=t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
is a sequence of terms and
t^{\prime}
is a term, we use the following abbreviation, adopted from Dependence Logic:

=\hskip-2.84544pt(\overline{t},t^{\prime})\quad:=\quad\lambda t_{1}\land\dots\land\lambda t_{n}\to\lambda t^{\prime}

In the context of an id-model
M
, the above formula is supported in a state
s
if, throughout
s
, the value of
t^{\prime}
is functionally determined by the values of
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
. More formally, we have

\displaystyle M,s\models_{g}\,=\hskip-2.84544pt(\overline{t},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall w,w^{\prime}\in s:[\,\overline{t}\,]_{w}^{g}=[\,\overline{t}\,]_{w^{\prime}}^{g}\text{ implies }[t^{\prime}]_{w}^{g}=[t^{\prime}]_{w^{\prime}}^{g}

where
[\,\overline{t}\,]_{w}^{g}=([t_{1}]_{w}^{g}\dots,[t_{n}]_{w}^{g})
.
Note that, since the formulas
\lambda t
and
\mu t
are equivalent relative to id-models, the same result could also have been obtained by defining
=\hskip-2.84544pt(\overline{t},t^{\prime})
as
\mu t_{1}\land\dots\land\mu t_{n}\to\mu t^{\prime}
.
3
3

3

This is relevant since the definition of
=\hskip-2.84544pt(\overline{t},t^{\prime})
by means of
\mu
, unlike the one that uses
\lambda
, yields a formula belonging to a known well-behaved fragment of
InqBQ
, namely, the rex fragment
5
.

3
InqBQ
is not entailment-compact

In this section, we settle Open Question 1, proving that
InqBQ
is
not
entailment-compact. We first restrict to id-models, where the predicate ‘=’ is interpreted as the identity relation at every world, and then extend the result to all models for
InqBQ
.

Consider a signature
\Sigma
containing only two non-rigid constant symbols,
a
and
b
, and consider an id-model
M
for this signature with set of worlds
W
and domain
D
. Relative to each world
w
, the interpretation function assigns to each constant an extension,
a_{w}\in D
and
b_{w}\in D
. Now to an information state
s\subseteq W
we can associate a corresponding binary relation
R_{s}\subseteq D^{2}
defined by

R_{s}=\{(a_{w},b_{w})\mid w\in s\}.

We say that the model
M=(W,D,I)
is
full
in case
R_{W}=D^{2}
.

A fact that will be useful below is that to each domain
D\neq\emptyset
we can associate a corresponding canonical full id-model
M_{D}
, defined in the following way.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a
D\neq\emptyset
, the canonical id-model for the signature
\Sigma
based on
D
is the id-model
M_{D}
whose universe is
W_{D}=D^{2}
, whose domain is
D
, and whose interpretation function is such that for a world
w=(d,e)
we have
a_{w}=d
and
b_{w}=e
.

In this model, for each state
s\subseteq W_{K}=D^{2}
we have
R_{s}=s
. In particular,
R_{W_{K}}=W_{K}=D^{2}
, showing that
M_{D}
is a full model.

We first prove that, relative to the class of full id-models,
InqBQ
can define the finiteness of the domain. More precisely, we prove that relative to this class of models, the finiteness of the domain is captured by the following
InqBQ
-sentence:

\eta\;:=\;(=\hskip-2.84544pt(a,b)\,\wedge=\hskip-2.84544pt(b,a)\land\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}x(x\neq b)\;\rightarrow\;\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}x(x\neq a)).

Lemma 3.2
.

If
M
is a full id-model with domain
D
:
M\models\eta\iff D
is finite.

4

Recall that
M\models\eta
abbreviates
M,W\models\eta
, where
W
is the universe of
M
.

Proof.

Suppose
M
is a full id-model with domain
D
. We prove that
M\not\models\eta\iff D
is infinite. By definition,
M\not\models\eta
iff there is an
s\subseteq W
that supports the antecedent of
\eta
but not the consequent. As we discussed above, this state is associated with a relation
R_{s}=\{(a_{w},b_{w})\mid w\in s\}
. Spelling out the semantics, we find that:

•

M,s\models{=\hskip-2.84544pt(a,b)}\iff R_{s}
is a function;

•

M,s\models{=\hskip-2.84544pt(b,a)}\iff R_{s}
is injective;

•

M,s\not\models\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}x(x\neq a)\iff\text{dom}(R_{s})=D
;

•

M,s\models\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}x(x\neq b)\iff\text{ran}(R_{s})\neq D
.

So, a state
s
falsifies the conditional
\eta
just in case the associated relation

is an injective function from
D
to

which is not surjective. Since
M

for some state

. So, in a full model, a state falsifying the conditional exists iff there exists an injective function defined on

which is not surjective, which holds iff

The fullness of an id-model cannot be defined in
InqBQ
, due to persistency: support for
InqBQ
-sentences is preserved when we move from a model
M
to a sub-model
M^{\prime}
obtained by restricting the set of worlds to a subset
W^{\prime}\subseteq W
. However, the property of fullness is
not
preserved by this operation: if
M
is full, the submodel
M^{\prime}
is not necessarily full. Rather, the property of fullness is preserved in the opposite direction, namely, upwards: if the sub-model
M^{\prime}
is full, then the entire model
M
is full. But this means that the complementary property of
non-fullness
is downward-persistent. We might thus expect it to be definable in
InqBQ
. And indeed, it is.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any id-model
M
,
M\text{ is full}\iff M\not\models\theta
, where:

\theta\;:=\;\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}x\mathord{\exists\hskip-3.99994pt\exists}y\neg(x=a\land y=b)

Proof.

We have the following:

\displaystyle M\models\theta

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\exists d,e\in D\forall w\in W:(d,e)\neq(a_{w},b_{w})

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\exists d,e\in D:(d,e)\not\in R_{W}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle R_{W}\neq D^{2}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle M\text{ is not full}

∎

Using the formulas
\eta
and
\theta
, we can show the failure of compactness for id-entailment.

Theorem 3.4
.

InqBQ
is not entailment-compact relative to id-models. That is, there is a set of formulas
\Phi\cup\{\psi\}
such that
\Phi\models_{\text{id}}\psi
, but
\Phi_{0}\not\models_{\text{id}}\psi
for all finite
\Phi_{0}\subseteq\Phi
.

Proof.

Let the sentences
\eta
and
\theta
be defined as above. Consider for each

saying that

contains at least
n

. We claim that we have the following counterexample to the compactness of id-entailment:

1.

X,\eta\models_{\text{id}}\theta

2.

X_{0},\eta\not\models_{\text{id}}\theta
for all finite
X_{0}\subseteq X
.

To prove item 1, need to show that for all id-models
M
,

(M\models\chi_{n}\text{ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$) and }M\models\eta\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;M\models\theta

which is equivalent to:

(M\models\chi_{n}\text{ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$) and }M\not\models\theta\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;M\not\models\eta

So, suppose
M\models\chi_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
M\not\models\theta
. By propositions
2.1
and
2.2
we have
M\models\chi_{n}\iff\#D\geq n
. Since
M\models\chi_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

is infinite. Since
M\not\models\theta
, Lemma
3.3
ensures that
M
is full. So,
M
is a full id-model with an infinite domain. By Lemma
3.2
, it follows that
M\not\models\eta
, as required.

To prove item 2, consider any finite
X_{0}\subseteq X
. Let
k
be the greatest

such that
\chi_{n}\in X_{0}
(if
X_{0}=\emptyset
we may let

). Let
D
be a set with

elements and let

be the canonical full id-model based on

, as given by Definition

. We have:

•

M_{D}\models\chi_{n}
for all

, since by definition

•

M_{D}\models\eta
by Lemma

is full and

is finite;

•

M_{D}\not\models\theta

M_{D}
is full.

So,

.

Using the connection between id-entailment and general entailment in
InqBQ
(Proposition
2.5
), we can also extend our result to general entailment.

Theorem 3.5
.

InqBQ
is not entailment-compact. That is, there is a set of formulas
\Phi\cup\{\psi\}
such that
\Phi\models\psi
, but
\Phi_{0}\not\models\psi
for all finite
\Phi_{0}\subseteq\Phi
.

Proof.

Suppose for a contradiction that entailment in
InqBQ
were compact. Then id-entailment would be compact as well, since using Proposition
2.5
and the supposed compactness of
InqBQ
-entailment we would have:

\displaystyle\Phi\models_{\text{id}}\psi

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\Phi,\forall x\forall y?(x=y)\models\psi

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\text{for some finite }\Phi_{0}\subseteq\Phi:\Phi_{0},\forall x\forall y?(x=y)\models\psi

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\text{for some finite }\Phi_{0}\subseteq\Phi:\Phi_{0}\models_{\text{id}}\psi

But this would contradict the previous theorem.
∎

We thus established an answer to Open Question 1: inquisitive first-order logic is not entailment-compact.

This result precludes the possibility of a strongly complete proof system for
InqBQ
(if such a system existed, every entailment would be witnessed by a proof, which would use only a finite set of premises, implying entailment-compactness). We will now see that a weakly complete proof system for
InqBQ
is not possible, either.

4
Recursive enumerability

In this section, we settle Open Question 2, proving that the set of
InqBQ
validities is
not
recursively enumerable.

Towards this result, we generalize the notion of canonical full id-model given in Definition
3.1
in the following way.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(D,I)
be a relational structure in a signature
\Sigma
, and let
a,b
be constant symbols which are not in
\Sigma
. The
canonical full id-model based on
\mathcal{M}
is the id-model
M_{\mathcal{M}}
over the extended signature
\Sigma\cup\{a,b\}
defined as follows: the universe is
W_{D}=D^{2}
; the domain is
D
; and the interpretation function
I^{+}
is defined so that for each world
w=(d,e)
, we have:

•

a_{w}=d

•

b_{w}=e

•

\sigma_{w}=I(\sigma)
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma

In words, only the interpretation of the fresh constant symbols
a
and
b
changes from world to world, while the interpretation of the symbols in
\Sigma
is constant and coincides with their interpretation in the original model
\mathcal{M}
.

Clearly, the model
M_{\mathcal{M}}
is full. Moreover, for each world
w
, consider the local structure
\mathcal{M}_{w}=(D,I^{+}_{w})
induced by
M_{\mathcal{M}}
on
w
: the reduct of this structure to the signature
\Sigma
is simply the original model
\mathcal{M}=(D,I)
. Using this fact, we can show that for classical sentences
\alpha
in the original signature
\Sigma
, support in
M_{\mathcal{M}}
coincides with truth in
\mathcal{M}
under standard Tarskian semantics.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any classical sentence
\alpha
in the signature
\Sigma
we have:

M_{\mathcal{M}}\models\alpha\iff\mathcal{M}\models\alpha

Proof.

We have:

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{M}}\models\alpha

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{M}},W_{D}\models\alpha

(definition)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall w\in W_{D}:M_{\mathcal{M}},w\models\alpha

(Prop.
2.2
)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall w\in W_{D}:\mathcal{M}_{w}\models\alpha

(Prop.
2.1
)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\models\alpha

(
\mathcal{M}_{w}\upharpoonright\Sigma=\mathcal{M}
)

∎

Let us say that a first-order sentence is
finitely valid
in first-order logic if it is valid over finite structures. We can now establish a connection between finite validity in first-order logic and id-validity as well as general validity in
InqBQ
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any first-order sentence
\alpha
in a signature
\Sigma
not containing the constants
a
and
b
, the following are equivalent:

1.

\alpha
is finitely valid in first-order logic

2.

\eta\to\alpha\,\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\!{\geqslant}$}\,\theta
is id-valid in

where
\eta,\theta
are defined as Section
3

3.

\rho\land\eta\to\alpha\,\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\!{\geqslant}$}\,\theta
is valid in
InqBQ
where
\rho:=\forall x\forall y?(x=y)

Proof.

1\Rightarrow 2
.

Suppose
\eta\to\alpha\,\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\!{\geqslant}$}\,\theta
is not id-valid in

. Then there is an id-model
M=(W,D,I)
such that
M\models\eta
but
M\not\models\alpha
and
M\not\models\theta
. Since

is full by Lemma

M
is full and

. Since

and

there is a world

, this means that for the relational structure

we have

. So,
\mathcal{M}_{w}
is a finite relational structure falsifying

Suppose

By Proposition

We can now use this connection to establish a negative answer to Open Question 2.

Theorem 4.4
.

The sets of validities and id-validities in
InqBQ
are not r.e.

Proof.

The previous lemma shows that the problem of finite validity for first-order sentences reduces to the problem of id-validity in
InqBQ
through the computable mapping
\alpha\mapsto{(\eta\to\alpha\,\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\!{\geqslant}$}\,\theta)}
, and to the problem of general validity via the computable mapping
\alpha\mapsto{(\rho\land\eta\to\alpha\,\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\!{\geqslant}$}\,\theta)}
. Since the set of finitely valid first-order sentences is not r.e., neither are the sets of id-validities and general validities in
InqBQ
.
∎

5
Conclusion and open problems

Two fundamental questions about inquisitive first-order logic,
InqBQ
, have been open since this logic was first introduced in
11
: whether entailment in this logic satisfies compactness, and whether the validities are recursively enumerable, thus allowing for a recursive axiomatization. We have settled both questions in the negative: in
InqBQ
, entailment is not compact, and the set of validities is not recursively enumerable.

While settling these long-standing questions, our results also open some new ones. In light of our negative answer to the questions above, work on “tame” fragments of
InqBQ
—fragments that
are
entailment-compact and recursively enumerable—becomes especially significant. As mentioned in the introduction, at present two interesting tame fragments are known: the clant (classical antecedent) fragment
(
22
)
, which restricts antecedents of implications to classical formulas, and the rex (restricted existential) fragment
(
5
)
, which restricts occurrences of the inquisitive existential quantifier to conditional antecedents. As one should expect, one of the formulas that played a key role in our proofs, namely, the formula
\eta
, does not belong to either of these fragments: it is an implication with an inquisitive antecedent, and an inquisitive existential quantifier in the consequent. One notable thing about
\eta
is that it uses left-nested implications (in the definition, these are hidden within the dependence formulas
=\hskip-2.84544pt(a,b)
and
=\hskip-2.84544pt(b,a)
, which in our setting are shorthands for the inquisitive implications
(\lambda a\to\lambda b)
and
(\lambda b\to\lambda a)
). A natural question is whether by disallowing left-nesting of implications, or even just by restricting it to classical formulas, one obtains a tame fragment. Existing work on the so-called NNIL fragment of intuitionistic logic
(
26
)
might be relevant here. Other natural questions in this area are whether it is possible to find a tame fragment of
InqBQ
that includes both the clant fragment and the rex fragment, and whether it is possible to find any
maximal
tame fragment of
InqBQ
(up to logical equivalence).

It would also be interesting to ask to what extent our results can be generalized to non-classical versions of inquisitive first-order logic. For now, the only such logic that has been explored in some detail is the intuitionistic version of inquisitive first-order logic
(
32
)
. For this logic, the problems of compactness and recursive enumerability are currently open. It seems likely that the ideas in this paper can be adapted to settle these problems in the negative.

Finally, one significant problem about
InqBQ
posed in
14
is left open by our results: is every invalidity of
InqBQ
refutable in a model that has a countable universe and a countable domain? In other words, is the logic of countable models identical to the logic of arbitrary models? It would be interesting to explore whether ideas similar to those we used in our proofs can help with this problem and other related problems concerning potential Löwenheim-Skolem-like properties of
InqBQ
.
